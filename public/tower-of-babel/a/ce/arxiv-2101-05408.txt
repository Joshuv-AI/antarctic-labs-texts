The full approximation storage multigrid scheme: A 1D finite element example Thanks: Version 3. This note is expository, and submission for publication is not foreseen. Thanks to Matt Knepley for thoughtful comments.
Ed Bueler

Abstract
. This note describes the full approximation storage (FAS) multigrid scheme for an easy one-dimensional nonlinear boundary value problem discretized by a simple finite element (FE) scheme. We apply both FAS V-cycles and F-cycles, with a nonlinear Gauss-Seidel smoother, to solve the finite-dimensional problem. The mathematics of the FAS restriction and prolongation operators, in the FE case, are explained. A self-contained Python program implements the scheme. Optimal performance, i.e. work proportional to the number of unknowns, is demonstrated for both kinds of cycles, including convergence nearly to discretization error in a single F-cycle.



1.
Introduction

We consider the full approximation storage (FAS) scheme, originally described by Brandt
[
4
]
, for an easy nonlinear elliptic equation. Like other multigrid schemes it exhibits optimal solver complexity
[
9
]
when correctly applied, as we demonstrate at the end. Helpful write-ups of FAS can be found in well-known textbooks
[
5
,
7
,
20
]
, but we describe the scheme from a finite element point of view, compatible with the multigrid approaches used for obstacle problems
[
13
]
for example, and we provide an easy-to-digest Python implementation.

Our problem is an ordinary differential equation (ODE) boundary value problem, the nonlinear Liouville-Bratu equation
[
6
,
16
]
:

(1)

-u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda\,e^{u}=g,\hskip 20.00003ptu(0)=u(1)=0.

In this problem
\lambda
is a real constant,
g(x)
is given, and we seek
u(x)
. This equation arises in the theory of combustion
[
12
]
and the stability of stars.

Our goal is to solve (
1
) in optimal
O(m)
time on a mesh of
m
elements. A Python implementation of FAS,
fas1.py
in directory
fas/py/
,

github.com/bueler/fas-intro
and look in the
fas/py/
directory.
accomplishes such optimal-time solutions both by V-cycle and F-cycle strategies (section
6
), and this note serves as its documentation. While optimal-time solutions of 1D problems are not unusual, FAS and other multigrid strategies for many nonlinear 2D and 3D partial differential equations (PDEs) are also optimal. This makes them the highest-performing class of solver algorithms for such problems.

By default the program
fas1.py
solves (
1
) with
g=0
. A runtime option
-mms
, the “method of manufactured solutions”
[
9
]
, facilitates testing by specifying a problem with known exact solution and nonzero source term. In detail, the solution is
u(x)=\sin(3\pi x)
, and by differentiation
g(x)=9\pi^{2}\sin(3\pi x)-\lambda e^{\sin(3\pi x)}
.

2.
The finite element method

To solve the problem using the finite element (FE) method
[
3
,
9
,
10
]
, we rewrite (
1
) in weak form. Let
F
be the nonlinear operator

(2)

F(u)[v]=\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)-\lambda e^{u(x)}v(x)\,dx,

acting on
u
and
v
from the space of functions
\mathcal{H}=H_{0}^{1}[0,1]
, a Sobolev space
[
11
]
. (These functions have zero boundary values and one square-integrable derivative.) Note
F(u)[v]
is linear in
v
but not in
u
. We also define a linear functional built from the right-hand function
g
in (
1
):

(3)

\ell[v]=\left<g,v\right>=\int_{0}^{1}g(x)v(x)dx.

Both
F(u)[\cdot]
and
\ell[\cdot]
are (continuous) linear functionals, acting on functions
v
in
\mathcal{H}
, thus they are in the dual space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. One derives the weak form

(4)

F(u)[v]=\ell[v]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $v$ in $\mathcal{H}$}

by multiplying equation (
1
) by a test function
v
and integrating by parts. From now on we address problem (
4
), despite its abstract form.

In an FE context a clear separation is desirable between functions, like the solution
u
, and the equations themselves, which are, essentially, functionals. As in linear algebra, where one indexes the equations by row indices, (
4
) states the “
v
th equation”; the equations are indexed by the test functions. The FE method will reduce the problem to a finite number of unknowns by writing
u
in a basis of a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. One gets finitely-many equations by using test functions
v
from the same basis.

We apply the simplest possible mesh setup, namely an equally-spaced mesh on
[0,1]
of
m
elements (subintervals) of lengths
h=1/m
. The interior nodes (points) are
x_{p}=ph
for
p=1,\dots,m-1
. This mesh supports a finite-dimensional vector subspace of
\mathcal{H}
:

(5)

\mathcal{S}^{h}=\left\{v(x)\,\big|\,v\text{ is continuous, linear on each subinterval, and }v(0)=v(1)=0\right\}.

This space has a basis of “hat” functions
\{\psi_{p}(x)\}
, one for each interior node (Figure
1
). Such a hat function
\psi_{p}
is defined by two properties:
\psi_{p}
is in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
and
\psi_{p}(x_{q})=\delta_{pq}
for all
q
. Note that the
L^{2}
norm of
\psi_{p}
depends on the mesh resolution
h
, and that
\left<\psi_{p},\psi_{q}\right>\neq 0
for three indices
q=p-1,p,p+1
. Thus this basis of hat functions, while well-conditioned, is not orthonormal.

Figure 1.
A piecewise-linear hat function
\psi_{p}(x)
lives at each interior node
x_{p}
.

The numerical solution
u^{h}
has the expansion

(6)

u^{h}(x)=\sum_{p=1}^{m-1}u[p]\psi_{p}(x)

with coefficients
u[p]
equal to the point values
u^{h}(x_{p})
. That is, because the hat functions form a “nodal basis”
[
10
]
,
u^{h}
may be represented as a vector
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
either by its coefficients in the basis
\{\psi_{p}\}
or by its point values:

(7)

\mathbf{u}=\{u[p]\}=\{u^{h}(x_{p})\}.

The FE approximation
F^{h}
of the nonlinear operator
F
in (
2
) acts on functions in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
. Its values
F^{h}(w^{h})[\psi_{p}]
are easily computed if the transcendental integral is approximated, for example by using the trapezoid rule, as follows. Noting that the support of
\psi_{p}(x)
is
[x_{p-1},x_{p+1}]
, and that the derivative of
\psi_{p}
is
\pm 1/h
, we have:

(8)

\displaystyle F(w^{h})[\psi_{p}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(w^{h})^{\prime}(x)\psi_{p}^{\prime}(x)-\lambda e^{w^{h}(x)}\psi_{p}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{p-1}}^{x_{p+1}}(w^{h})^{\prime}(x)(\pm 1/h)\,dx-\lambda\int_{x_{p-1}}^{x_{p+1}}e^{w^{h}(x)}\psi_{p}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\approx h\left(\frac{w[p]-w[p-1]}{h}-\frac{w[p+1]-w[p]}{h}\right)-h\lambda e^{w[p]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\left(2w[p]-w[p-1]-w[p+1]\right)-h\lambda e^{w[p]}

\displaystyle=F^{h}(w^{h})[\psi_{p}]

Note that
F^{h}
is a rescaled version of a well-known
O(h^{2})
finite difference expression. Function
FF()
in
fas1.py
computes this formula.

Now consider the right-hand-side functional
\ell[v]
in (
4
), which we will approximate by
\ell^{h}[v]
acting on
\mathcal{S}^{h}
. We again apply the trapezoid rule to compute the integral
\left<g,\psi_{p}\right>
, and we get the simple formula

(9)

\ell^{h}[\psi_{p}]=h\,g(x_{p}).

The linear functional
\ell^{h}
and the function
g
are different objects, though they only differ by a factor of the mesh size
h
.

The finite element weak form can now be stated:

(10)

F^{h}(u^{h})[v]=\ell^{h}[v]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all }v\text{ in }\mathcal{S}^{h}.

To numerically solve (
10
) we will seek an iterate
w^{h}
so that the
residual

(11)

r^{h}(w^{h})[v]=\ell^{h}[v]-F^{h}(w^{h})[v]

is small for all
v
in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
. Again
r^{h}(w^{h})
is a linear functional acting on functions in
\mathcal{S}_{h}
, so it suffices to apply it to a basis of test functions
v=\psi_{p}
:

(12)

r^{h}(w^{h})[\psi_{p}]=\ell^{h}[\psi_{p}]-\frac{1}{h}\left(2w[p]-w[p-1]-w[p+1]\right)+h\lambda e^{w[p]}.

Solving the finite-dimensional nonlinear system, i.e. the FE approximation of (
4
), is equivalent to finding
w^{h}
in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
so that
r^{h}(w^{h})[\psi_{p}]=0
for
p=1,\dots,m-1
.

A function in
fas1.py
computes (
12
) for any source functional
\ell^{h}
. On the original mesh, soon to be called the “fine mesh”, we will use formula (
9
). However, the FAS algorithm (sections
4
and
6
) is a systematic way to introduce a new source functional on each coarser mesh.

The function
u^{h}(x)
in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
, equivalently
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
given by (
7
), exactly solves a finite-dimensional nonlinear system (
10
). In practice, however, at each stage we only possess an iterate
w^{h}(x)
, for which the “algebraic error”

(13)

e^{h}=w^{h}-u^{h}

might be small. On the other hand,
u^{h}
is not the continuum solution either; the “discretization error”
u^{h}-u
, where
u
is the exact solution of the continuum problem (
4
), is also nonzero in general. The theory of an FE method will show that discretization error goes to zero as
h\to 0
, at a particular rate determined by the FE space and the smoothness of the continuum problem
[
10
]
, but such a theory assumes we have exactly-solved the finite-dimensional system, i.e. that we possess
u^{h}
itself. The full “numerical error” is the difference
w^{h}-u
, and we have

(14)

\|w^{h}-u\|\leq\|w^{h}-u^{h}\|+\|u^{h}-u\|.

In other words, the numerical error, which we want to control, is bounded by the algebraic error plus the discretization error.

In the
-mms
case of
fas1.py
, where the exact solution
u
of the continuum problem is known, the numerical error norm
\|w^{h}-u\|
is computable. Normally we cannot access
u
or
u^{h}
directly, and only the residual norm
\|r^{h}(w^{h})\|
is computable, but the norm of the algebraic error is controlled to within a matrix condition number by the residual norm.

3.
The nonlinear Gauss-Seidel iteration

Next we describe an iteration which will, if carried far enough, solve the finite-dimensional nonlinear system (
10
) to desired accuracy. This is the nonlinear Gauss-Seidel (NGS) iteration
[
7
]
, also called Gauss-Seidel-Newton
[
5
]
. It updates the iterate
w^{h}
by changing each point value
w^{h}(x_{p})
to make the residual at that point zero. That is, NGS solves the problem

(15)

\phi(c)=r^{h}(w^{h}+c\psi_{p})[\psi_{p}]=0

for a scalar
c
. Once
c
is found we update the point value (coefficient):

(16)

w^{h}\leftarrow w^{h}+c\psi_{p},

equivalently
w[p]\leftarrow w[p]+c
.

In the linear Gauss-Seidel iteration
[
14
]
,
w[p]
is updated in a certain nodal ordering, using current values
w[q]
when evaluating the residual in (
15
). However, as the residual is made zero at one point it is no longer zero at the previous points. Gauss-Seidel-type methods are called “successive”
[
13
]
or “multiplicative”
[
9
]
corrections. “Additive” corrections, of which the Jacobi iteration
[
14
]
is the best known, are also possible, but they are somewhat less efficient. Our program only runs in serial, and the parallelizability of the Jacobi iteration cannot be exploited.

Solving the scalar problem
\phi(c)=0
cannot be done exactly for a transcendental problem like (
1
). Instead we will use a fixed number of Newton iterations
[
9
, Chapter 4]
to generate a (scalar) sequence
\{c_{k}\}
converging to
c
. Starting from
c_{0}=0
we compute

(17)

\phi^{\prime}(c_{k})\,s_{k}=-\phi(c_{k}),\hskip 20.00003ptc_{k+1}=c_{k}+s_{k},

for
k=0,1,\dots
. From (
12
) we have

\displaystyle\phi(c)

\displaystyle=\ell^{h}[\psi_{p}]-\frac{1}{h}\left(2(w[p]+c)-w[p-1]-w[p+1]\right)+h\lambda e^{w[p]+c},

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h}+h\lambda e^{w[p]+c}.

The vast majority of the work of our FAS algorithms will be in evaluating these expressions.

The NGS method sweeps through the mesh, zeroing
\phi(c)
at successive nodes
x_{p}
, in increasing
p
order, as in the following pseudocode which modifies
w^{h}
in-place:

ngssweep
(w^{h},\ell^{h},\textnormal{{niters}}=2)
:

r(w^{h})[v]:=\ell^{h}[v]-F^{h}(w^{h})[v]

for
p=1,\dots,m-1

\phi(c):=r^{h}(w^{h}+c\psi_{p})[\psi_{p}]

c=0

for
k=1,\dots,
niters

c\leftarrow c-\phi(c)/\phi^{\prime}(c)

w[p]\leftarrow w[p]+c

For FAS algorithms (next section) we also define
ngssweep-back
with decreasing node order
p=m-1,\dots,1
. Function
ngssweep()
in
fas1.py
computes either order.

For a linear differential equation the Gauss-Seidel iteration is known to converge subject to matrix assumptions which correspond to ellipticity of the original problem
[
14
, for example]
. We expect that for weak nonlinearities, e.g. small
\lambda
in (
1
), our method will converge as a solution method for (
10
), and we will demonstrate that this occurs in practice (section
7
). However, one observes that, after substantial progress in the first few sweeps during which the residual becomes very smooth, NGS stagnates. Following Brandt
[
4
,
5
]
, who asserts that such a stalling scheme must be wrong, we adopt the multigrid approach next.

4.
The FAS equation for two levels

The fundamental goal of any multigrid scheme is to do a minimal amount of work (smoothing) on a given mesh and then switch to a less expensive coarser mesh to do the rest of the work. By transferring (restricting) a version of the problem to the coarser mesh one can nearly solve for the error. The coarse-mesh approximation of the error is then added-back (prolonged) to correct the solution on the finer mesh.

Thus the multigrid
full approximation storage
(FAS) scheme
[
4
,
7
]
must include the following elements:

(i)

a hierarchy of meshes, with restriction and prolongation operators between levels,

(ii)

a “smoother” for each level, and

(iii)

a meaningful way to transfer the problem to a coarser mesh.

Regarding (i), we describe only two levels at first, but deep mesh hierarchies are used in section
6
. Here our coarse mesh has spacing
2h
and
m/2
elements (subintervals); all quantities on the coarse mesh have superscript “
2h
”. The program
fas1.py
only refines by factors of two, but the ideas generalize for other refinement factors.

For (ii), a small fixed number of NGS sweeps is our smoother. Each sweep, algorithm
ngssweep
above, is an
O(m)
operation with a small constant. The constant is determined by the number of Newton iterations and the expense of evaluating nonlinearities at each point, e.g.
\lambda e^{u}
in (
1
). A few NGS sweeps produce smooth fine-mesh residual
r^{h}(w^{h})
and algebraic error
e^{h}=w^{h}-u^{h}
, but these fields are not necessarily small. Using more sweeps of NGS would eventually make the error small, and solve problem (
10
), but inefficiently in the sense that many sweeps would be needed, generally giving an
O(m^{q})
method for
q\gg 1
. However, a coarser mesh will see the coarse-mesh interpolant of the fine-mesh residual as less smooth, so then NGS can quickly eliminate a large fraction of the error. Descending to yet coarser meshes, in a V-cycle as described in section
6
, leads to a coarsest mesh on which the error can be essentially eliminated by applying NGS at only a few interior points. (In the default settings for
fas1.py
, the coarsest mesh has two subintervals and one interior point.)

For item (iii), what is the coarse-mesh version of the problem? To derive this equation, which is Brandt’s FAS equation
[
4
]
, we start from the FE weak form (
10
). The fine-mesh solution
u^{h}
is generally unknown. For an iterate
w^{h}
we subtract
F^{h}(w^{h})[v]
from both sides of (
10
) to get a residual (
11
) on the right:

(18)

F^{h}(u^{h})[v]-F^{h}(w^{h})[v]=r^{h}(w^{h})[v].

This is not yet the FAS equation, but three key observations apply to (
18
):

•

Both
w^{h}
and
r^{h}(w^{h})
are known and/or computable.

•

If the smoother has been applied then
e^{h}=w^{h}-u^{h}
and
r^{h}(w^{h})
are smooth.

•

If
F^{h}
were linear in
w^{h}
then we could rewrite (
18
) in terms of the error:

\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003ptF^{h}(e^{h})[v]=-r^{h}(w^{h})[v],\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{\emph{if $F^{h}$ is linear}})

equivalently
A\mathbf{e}=-\mathbf{r}
in matrix form.

Based on these observations, Brandt proposed a new nonlinear equation for the coarse mesh. It modifies (
18
) by replacing terms using restriction operators on the computable quantities and by re-discretizing the nonlinear operator into
F^{2h}
acting on
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
. Because the problem is nonlinear we must store the coarse-mesh solution, namely
u^{2h}
in
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
, not just the error. Denoting the restriction operators by
R^{\prime}
and
R
, which are addressed in the next section, the following is Brandt’s FAS equation:

(19)

F^{2h}(u^{2h})[v]-F^{2h}(Rw^{h})[v]=R^{\prime}(r^{h}(w^{h}))[v],

for all
v
in
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
. We can simplify the appearance by defining a linear functional

(20)

\ell^{2h}[v]=R^{\prime}(r^{h}(w^{h}))[v]+F^{2h}(Rw^{h})[v]

so (
19
) becomes

(21)

F^{2h}(u^{2h})[v]=\ell^{2h}[v].

The key idea behind the FAS equation (
21
), which has the same form as the fine-mesh weak form (
10
), is that the smoothness of the error and residual have allowed us to accurately coarsen the problem. Note that if
w^{h}=u^{h}
, that is, if
w^{h}
is the exact solution to the fine-mesh problem (
10
), then
r^{h}(w^{h})=0
so
\ell^{2h}
simplifies to
F^{2h}(Rw^{h})[v]
, and the solution of (
21
) would be
u^{2h}=Rw^{h}
by well-posedness.

In stating a two-level FAS method we will suppose (
21
) is solved exactly, so
u^{2h}
and the coarse-mesh error
u^{2h}-Rw^{h}
are known. We then update the fine-mesh iterate by adding a fine-mesh version of the error:

(22)

w^{h}\leftarrow w^{h}+P(u^{2h}-Rw^{h})

Here
P
is a prolongation operator (next section); it extends a function in
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
to
\mathcal{S}^{h}
. Supposing that the smoother and the restriction/prolongation operators
R^{\prime},R,P
are all determined, formulas (
21
), (
20
), and (
22
) define the following in-place algorithm in which
F^{h}
and
F^{2h}
denote discretizations of
F
:

fas-twolevel
(w^{h},\ell^{h},\textnormal{{down}}=1,\textnormal{{up}}=1)
:

for
j=1,\dots,
down

ngssweep
(w^{h},\ell^{h})

\ell^{2h}[v]:=R^{\prime}(\ell^{h}-F^{h}(w^{h}))[v]+F^{2h}(Rw^{h})[v]

w^{2h}=\textnormal{{copy}}(Rw^{h})

coarsesolve
(w^{2h},\ell^{2h})

w^{h}\leftarrow w^{h}+P(w^{2h}-Rw^{h})

for
j=1,\dots,
up

ngssweep-back
(w^{h},\ell^{h})

Note that we allow smoothing before and after the coarse-mesh correction.

While it is common in linear multigrid
[
7
,
9
,
20
]
to apply a direct solver like LU decomposition as the coarse-mesh solver, our problem is nonlinear and no finite-time direct solver is available. Instead we do a fixed number of NGS sweeps:

coarsesolve
(w,\ell,\textnormal{{coarse}}=1)
:

for
j=1,\dots,
coarse

ngssweep
(w,\ell)

In order to implement FAS we must define the action of operators
R^{\prime}
,
R
, and
P
used in (
20
) and (
22
),which is done next. In section
6
we will define an FAS V-cycle by replacing
coarsesolve
with recursive application of the FAS coarsening step.

5.
Restriction and prolongation operators

To explain the two restriction operators
R^{\prime}
and
R
in (
21
), and the prolongation
P
in (
22
), first note that functions
w^{h}
in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
are distinct objects from linear functionals like the residual
r^{h}(w^{h})
. Denoting such linear functionals by
(\mathcal{S}^{h})^{\prime}
, the three operators are distinguished by their domain and range spaces:

(23)

\displaystyle R^{\prime}

\displaystyle:(\mathcal{S}^{h})^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{S}^{2h})^{\prime},

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle:\mathcal{S}^{h}\to\mathcal{S}^{2h},

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle:\mathcal{S}^{2h}\to\mathcal{S}^{h}.

On the other hand, both functions in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
and linear functionals in
(\mathcal{S}^{h})^{\prime}
are representable by vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
. One stores a function
w^{h}
via coefficients
w[p]
with respect to an expansion in the hat function basis
\{\psi_{p}\}
, as in (
6
) for example, while one stores a functional
\ell^{h}
by its values
\ell^{h}[\psi_{p}]
. Though it makes sense to represent
w^{h}
as a column vector and
\ell^{h}
as a row vector
[
19
]
, in Python we will use “flat” one-dimensional NumPy arrays
[
15
]
for both purposes. For our problem an iterate
w^{h}
has zero boundary values, and likewise
\ell^{h}
acts on functions having zero boundary values, thus only interior hat functions are needed in these representations.

But how do
R^{\prime}
,
R
, and
P
actually operate? The key calculation relates the coarse-mesh hat functions
\psi_{q}^{2h}(x)
to the fine mesh hats
\psi_{p}^{h}(x)
:

(24)

\psi_{q}^{2h}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q-1}^{h}(x)+\psi_{2q}^{h}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q+1}^{h}(x),

for
q=1,2,\dots,m/2-1
. (We are assuming
m
is even.) See Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
Formula (
24
) writes a coarse-mesh hat function
\psi_{q}^{2h}(x)
(solid) as a linear combination of fine-mesh hats
\psi_{p}^{h}(x)
(dotted).

First consider the prolongation
P
. Because a piecewise-linear function on the coarse mesh is also a piecewise-linear function on the fine mesh, in this FE context
P
is defined as the injection of
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
into
\mathcal{S}^{h}
, without changing the function. If
w^{2h}(x)=\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}w[q]\psi_{q}^{2h}(x)
then (
24
) computes
Pw^{2h}
in terms of fine-mesh hat functions:

(25)

\displaystyle(Pw^{2h})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}w[q]\left(\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q-1}^{h}(x)+\psi_{2q}^{h}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q+1}^{h}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}w[1]\psi_{1}^{h}(x)+w[1]\psi_{2}^{h}(x)+\left(\frac{1}{2}w[1]+\frac{1}{2}w[2]\right)\psi_{3}^{h}(x)+w[2]\psi_{4}^{h}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\left(\frac{1}{2}w[2]+\frac{1}{2}w[3]\right)\psi_{5}^{h}(x)+\dots+w[m/2\!-\!1]\psi_{m-2}^{h}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\frac{1}{2}w[m/2\!-\!1]\psi_{m-1}^{h}(x)

As a matrix,
P:\mathbb{R}^{m/2-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
acts on vectors; it has
m/2-1
columns and
m-1
rows:

(26)

P=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&&&\\
1&&&\\
1/2&1/2&&\\
&1&&\\
&1/2&1/2&\\
&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}

The columns of
P
are linearly-independent and sum to 2 by (
24
). The row sums equal one except for the first and last rows.

Next, the restriction
R^{\prime}
acts on fine-mesh linear functionals
\ell:\mathcal{S}^{h}\to\mathbb{R}
. It is called “canonical restriction”
[
13
]
because the output functional
R^{\prime}\ell:\mathcal{S}^{2h}\to\mathbb{R}
acts on coarse-mesh functions in the same way
\ell
itself acts on those functions. Defining
R^{\prime}
involves no choices. We may state this using
P
: for
v
in
\mathcal{S}^{2h}
,

(27)

(R^{\prime}\ell)[v]=\ell[Pv].

As noted earlier,
\ell
in
\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
has entries
\ell[\psi_{p}^{h}]
. One computes the values of
R^{\prime}\ell
using (
24
):

(28)

\displaystyle(R^{\prime}\ell)[\psi_{q}^{2h}]

\displaystyle=\ell[\psi_{q}^{2h}]=\ell\left[\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q-1}^{h}+\psi_{2q}^{h}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2q+1}^{h}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ell[\psi_{2q-1}^{h}]+\ell[\psi_{2q}^{h}]+\frac{1}{2}\ell[\psi_{2q+1}^{h}].

As a matrix,
R^{\prime}
is the transpose of
P
, with
m/2-1
rows and
m-1
columns:

(29)

R^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1&1/2&&&\\
&&1/2&1&1/2&\\
&&&&1/2&\\
&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}

Finally we consider the restriction
R:\mathcal{S}^{h}\to\mathcal{S}^{2h}
acting on functions, a more interesting map which loses information. (By contrast,
P
and
R^{\prime}
preserve their input, without loss, via reinterpretation on the output mesh.) Consider a fine-mesh function
w^{h}=\sum_{p=1}^{m-1}w[p]\psi_{p}^{h}
. The result
Rw^{h}
should be linear across those fine-mesh nodes which are not in the coarse mesh, so values at in-between nodes are not recoverable.

There are three well-known choices for the restriction
R
:

•

R_{\text{pr}}
is defined as projection, by the property

(30)

\left<R_{\text{pr}}w^{h},v\right>=\left<w^{h},v\right>

for all
v\in\mathcal{S}^{2h}
. Computing the entries of
R_{\text{pr}}
requires solving a linear system. To do so we define invertible, sparse, symmetric mass matrices
Q_{jk}^{h}=\left<\psi_{j}^{h},\psi_{k}^{h}\right>
for the fine mesh and
Q_{jk}^{2h}=\left<\psi_{j}^{2h},\psi_{k}^{2h}\right>
for the coarse
[
10
]
. Then one solves a matrix equation for
R_{\text{pr}}
:

(31)

Q^{2h}R_{\text{pr}}=R^{\prime}Q^{h},

or equivalently
R_{\text{pr}}=(Q^{2h})^{-1}R^{\prime}Q^{h}
. Equation (
31
) is justified by using
v=\psi_{s}^{2h}
in definition (
30
), and then applying (
24
), as follows. Write
z=R_{\text{pr}}w^{h}=\sum_{q=1}^{M-1}z[q]\psi_{q}^{2h}
and expand both sides:

\displaystyle\left<z,\psi_{s}^{2h}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<w^{h},\psi_{s}^{2h}\right>

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}z[q]\left<\psi_{q}^{2h},\psi_{s}^{2h}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{m-1}w[p]\left<\psi_{p}^{h},\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2s-1}^{h}+\psi_{2s}^{h}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{2s+1}^{h}\right>

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}Q_{sq}^{2h}z[q]

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}Q_{2s-1,p}+Q_{2s,p}+\frac{1}{2}Q_{2s+1,p}\right)w[p]

\displaystyle(Q^{2h}R_{\text{pr}}w^{h})[s]

\displaystyle=(R^{\prime}Q^{h}w^{h})[s]

(Note
w^{h}
in
\mathcal{S}^{h}
and index
s
are arbitrary.) In 1D the mass matrices
Q^{2h},Q^{h}
are tridiagonal, thus each column of
R_{\text{pr}}
can be found by solving equation (
31
) using an
O(m)
algorithm
[
19
]
. While this is implementable, and computable by hand in this case, the alternatives below are easier to implement.

•

R_{\text{in}}
is defined as pointwise injection. Supposing
w^{h}=\sum_{p=1}^{m-1}w[p]\psi_{p}^{h}
,

(32)

R_{\text{in}}w^{h}=\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}w[2q]\psi_{q}^{2h},

so
(R_{\text{in}}w^{h})(x_{q})=w^{h}(x_{q})=w[2q]
for each point
x_{q}
. In other words, to compute
R_{\text{in}}w^{h}
we simply drop the nodal values at those fine-mesh nodes which are not in the coarse mesh. As a matrix this is

(33)

R_{\text{in}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&&&&&\\
&&0&1&&&\\
&&&&0&1&\\
&&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}.

This restriction is very simple but it can lose track of the magnitude of
w^{h}
, or badly mis-represent it,
if
the input is not smooth. For example, sampling a sawtooth function at the coarse-mesh nodes would capture only the peaks or only the troughs.

•

R_{\text{fw}}
, the “full-weighting” restriction
[
7
]
, averages nodal values onto the coarse mesh:

(34)

R_{\text{fw}}w^{h}=\sum_{q=1}^{m/2-1}\left(\frac{1}{4}w[2q-1]+\frac{1}{2}w[2q]+\frac{1}{4}w[2q+1]\right)\psi_{q}^{2h}.

This computes each coarse-mesh nodal value of
z=R_{\text{fw}}w^{h}
as a weighted average of the value of
w^{h}
at the three closest fine-mesh nodes. The matrix is a multiple of the canonical restriction matrix in (
29
):

(35)

R_{\text{fw}}=\begin{bmatrix}1/4&1/2&1/4&&&\\
&&1/4&1/2&1/4&\\
&&&&1/4&\\
&&&&&\ddots\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}R^{\prime}.

Which restriction do we choose? Because of their simplicity, we will implement and compare
R_{\text{in}}
and
R_{\text{fw}}
in
fas1.py
.

6.
Cycles

The main principles of the FAS scheme are already contained in the
fas-twolevel
algorithm in section
4
, from which it is a small step to solve the coarse-mesh problem by the same scheme, creating a so-called “V-cycle”. To define this precisely we need an indexed hierarchy of mesh levels. Start with a coarsest mesh with
m_{0}
elements of length
h_{0}=1/m_{0}
. (By default in
fas1.py
we have
m_{0}=2
.) For
k=1,\dots,K
we refine by factors of two so that the
k

m_{k}=2^{k}m_{0}
elements of length
h_{k}=h_{0}/2^{k}
. The final
K
th mesh is now called the “fine mesh”. Instead of the superscripts
h
and
2h
used in section
4
, a “
k

On this hierarchy an FAS V-cycle is the following in-place recursive algorithm:

Our Python code

However, we can add a different multilevel idea to get an even better cycle. It is based on the observation that an iterative equation solver, linear or nonlinear, often depends critically on the quality of its initial iterate. Indeed, choosing initial iterate
w^{K}=0
and calling
fas-solver
may not yield a convergent method. However, one finds in practice that coarse meshes are more forgiving with respect to the initial iterate than are finer meshes. The new idea is to start on the coarsest mesh in the hierarchy, where a blind guess like
w^{0}=0
is most likely to succeed, and then work upward through the levels. At each mesh level one computes an initial iterate by prolongation of a nearly-converged iterate on the previous level, and then one does a V-cycle. At the finest mesh level we may do repeated V-cycles.

The resulting algorithm is called an FAS multigrid “F-cycle” because the pattern in Figure
3
(right) looks vaguely like an “F” on its back:

fas-fcycle
(K)
:

w^{0}=0

\ell^{0}[v]=\left<g,v\right>

coarsesolve
(w^{0},\ell^{0})

for
k=1,\dots,K

w^{k}=\hat{P}w^{k-1}

\ell^{k}[v]=\left<g,v\right>

fas-vcycle
(k,w^{k},\ell^{k})

return
w^{K}

This algorithm is also called a “full multigrid” (FMG) cycle
[
5
,
7
]
, but the meaning of “full” is fundamentally different in FAS versus FMG terminology.

One may run
fas-fcycle
to generate the initial iterate for
fas-solver
. However, as seen in section
8
the result of one F-cycle is already a very good solution.

It is important to avoid the introduction of high frequencies as one generates the first iterate on the finer mesh. Thus a coarse-mesh solution is prolonged on to the next level by a possibly-different operator:

(36)

w^{k}=\hat{P}w^{k-1}

It is common for a better interpolation scheme to be used for
\hat{P}
than for
P

[
20
]
. Our choice for
\hat{P}
first applies
P
to generate a fine-mesh function, but this is followed by sweeping once through the
new
fine-mesh nodes and applying NGS there without altering values at the coarse-mesh nodes. This
\hat{P}
is half of a smoother, and counted as such; see section
8
.

8.
Performance

Having verified our method, our first performance test compares three solver algorithms:

•

fas-fcycle
, defined in section
6
.

•

fas-solver
, which does V-cycles, also defined in section
6
.

•

ngsonly
in section
7
.

The two FAS pseudocodes actually represent many different algorithms according to the different options. While making no attempt to systematically-explore the parameter space, we observe that 7 to 12 V(1,1) cycles suffice to approach discretization error in the
-mms
problem. For F-cycles we must choose how many V-cycles to take once the finest level is reached, and 2 or 3 usually suffice. Experimentation in minimizing the work units (below), while maintaining convergence, yields a choice of three V(1,0) cycles.

A standard way to compare multigrid-type solvers uses the concept of a
work unit
(WU). One WU is the number of operations needed to do a smoother sweep on the finest mesh, which takes
O(m)
arithmetic (floating point) operations. For WUs in a 1D multilevel scheme, a smoother sweep on the second-finest mesh is
\frac{1}{2}

[
7
]
which depends on the number of levels

. For simplicity we do not count the arithmetic work in restriction and prolongation, other than in the enhanced prolongation
\hat{P}
in (
36
), which uses
\frac{1}{2}
WU when passing to the finest mesh. Also we ignore non-arithmetic work entirely, for example vector copies.

Consider the

\displaystyle\text{WU}\big(\text{{F-cycle$+Z\times$V(1,0)}}\big)

\displaystyle\approx 3+2Z

\displaystyle\text{WU}\big(\text{{$Z$ V(1,1) cycles}}\big)

\displaystyle\approx 4Z

\displaystyle\text{WU}\big(\text{{$Z$ NGS sweeps}}\big)

\displaystyle=Z

9.
Extensions

Our program
fas1.py
is deliberately basic in many senses. Here are three possible extensions which the reader might want to implement:

I.

The default value of the parameter
\lambda
in (
1
) is
-lam 1.0
, but one can check that the
g=0
problem becomes unstable at a critical value
\lambda_{c}\approx 3.5
. Interestingly, the solution changes very little as
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{c}
; things are boring until failure occurs. (The most-common numerical symptom is overflow of
e^{u}
.) Equation (
1
) is a very simple model for combustion of a chemical mixture, and this instability corresponds to a chemical explosion
[
12
]
. However, finding
\lambda_{c}
precisely is not easy because
fas1.py
always initializes at the distant location
w^{0}=0
. The behavior of FAS F-cycles is especially nontrivial near the critical
\lambda
because the critical value is different on coarse grids. (And apparently sometimes smaller!) A better strategy for solutions near the critical value, and for parameter studies generally, is “continuation”. For example, one might use a saved fine-mesh solution as the initial value in a run with a slightly-different
\lambda
value. The new run would only use V-cycles.

II.

Equation (
1
) is a “semilinear” ODE because its nonlinearity occurs in the zeroth-derivative term
[
11
]
. One might instead solve a “quasilinear” equation where the nonlinearity is in the coefficient to the highest-order derivative. For example, one might try a
p
-Laplacian
[
11
]
extension to the Liouville-Bratu equation:

(37)

-\left(|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-\lambda e^{u}=g.

This equation is the same as (
1
) when
p=2
, but for other values
p
in
(1,\infty)
the solution is less well-behaved because the coefficient of
u^{\prime\prime}
can degenerate or explode. (A literature at least exists for the corresponding Poisson-like problem with
\lambda=0

[
2
,
9
]
.) A basic technique is to regularize the leading coefficient with a numerical parameter
\epsilon>0
: replace
|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}
with
\left(|u^{\prime}|^{2}+\epsilon\right)^{(p-2)/2}
. With such a change, continuation (item
I
) is also recommended.

III.

The most significant extension of
fas1.py
would be to “merely” change from 1D to 2D or 3D. That is, to change from solving ODEs to solving elliptic PDEs like
-\nabla^{2}u-\lambda e^{u}=g
, where
\nabla^{2}
is the Laplacian operator. However, doing this in the style of
fas1.py
, using only NumPy vectors for infrastructure, is not recommended. Instead, it would be wise to apply an FE library like Firedrake
[
18
]
or Fenics
[
17
]
, on top of an advanced solver library like PETSc
[
1
,
9
]
. Such libraries imply a substantial learning curve, and their support for FAS multigrid methods is incomplete, but they allow experimentation with higher-order FE spaces and many other benefits.

10.
Conclusion

Regarding the performance of the tested solvers, we summarize as follows:

On any mesh of
m
elements, problem (
10
) can be solved nearly to the discretization error of our piecewise-linear FE method by using a single FAS F-cycle, or a few FAS V-cycles. The work of these methods is
O(m)
with a small constant; they are optimal. The faster F-cycle gives textbook multigrid efficiency. These facts holds for all
m
up until rounding errors overwhelm the discretization at around
m=10^{6}
. By contrast, single-level NGS requires rapidly-increasing numbers of sweeps; its work scales as
O(m^{q})
for
q\gg 1
.
