New extremal inclusions and their applications to two-phase composites
Liping Liu, Richard James, Perry Leo

Abstract




Draft: August 17, 2010

Article submitted to Archive for Rational Mechanics and Analysis.

Abstract

In this paper, we find a class of special inclusions that
have the same property with respect to second order linear
partial differential equations as holds for
ellipsoids. That is, in the simplest case and in physical terms,
constant magnetization of the inclusion implies constant
magnetic field on the inclusion.
The special inclusions are found as solutions of a simple variational inequality.
This variational inequality allows us to
prescribe the connectivity and periodicity properties of
the inclusions. For example we find periodic arrays of inclusions in two and three dimensions
for which constant magnetization of the inclusions implies constant
magnetic field on the inclusions.
The volume fraction of the inclusions can be any number between zero and one. We find
such inclusions with any finite number of components
and components that are multiply connected.
These special inclusions enjoy many useful
properties with respect to homogenization and energy
minimization. For example, we use them to give new results
on a) the effective properties of two-phase composites and
b) optimal bounds and optimal microstructures for
two-phase composites.

Introduction

Poisson

[
48
]
found a remarkable property of ellipsoids: given
a uniformly magnetized ellipsoid, the
induced magnetic field is uniform inside the
ellipsoid. Explicit expressions for this field were
obtained by
Maxwell

[
42
]
.

Eshelby

[
13
,
14
]
exploited a
similar result in the linear theory of elasticity.
Eshelby’s solution asserts that a uniform eigenstrain on an
ellipsoidal subregion in an infinite elastic medium induces
uniform stress inside the ellipsoid (see also,
Mura

[
46
]
).
In this paper we find other shapes besides ellipsoids
with these properties.

The relevant problem can be formulated as
the following system of partial differential equations
for
{\bf v}:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{m}
:

\displaystyle{\rm div}[{\bf L}\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf P}\chi_{\Omega}]=0\qquad{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\;{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n},

(1-1)

understood in the sense of distributions. Here,

{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{m\times n}
, the tensor
{\bf L}:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{m\times n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{m\times n}

is assumed to be symmetric and positive semi-definite,
and
\chi_{\Omega}
is the characteristic function of
\Omega\subset{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
.

\Omega
is called the inclusion.
In the application to ferromagnetism,
m=1
,
\nabla{\bf v}
is the magnetic field,

{\bf P}
is the magnetization, and
{\bf L}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. In applications
to electrostatics,
m=1
,
\nabla{\bf v}
is the electric field,

{\bf P}
is the polarization, and
{\bf L}
is
the permittivity tensor of free space.
In the application to linearized elasticity,
m=n
,
\nabla{\bf v}
is the displacement
gradient,
{\bf P}
is the eigenstress and
{\bf L}
is the elasticity tensor.

In the theory of composites, in fracture mechanics and in the modeling of phase transformations
a related problem, called the
inhomogeneous

Eshelby inclusion problem, appears often.
The governing equations for this problem are

\displaystyle{\rm div}[{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x})+{\bf F}\chi_{\Omega})]=0\qquad{{\rm\;on\;}}\;{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n},

(1-2)

where

is the eigenstrain and the elasticity
tensor

{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{\bf L}_{1}&\qquad{\bf x}\in\Omega,\\
{\bf L}_{0}&\qquad{\bf x}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}\setminus\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1-3)

The inhomogeneous Eshelby inclusion problem concerns two
different elastic materials, one inside the inclusion, and
an imposed eigenstrain
{\bf F}
on the inclusion.

Eshelby

[
13
]
realized that
under suitable mild hypotheses on the elasticity tensors
the homogeneous problem (
1-1

1-2
) provided that

for the homogeneous problem (
1-1

is

\Omega

and (

to obtain optimal bounds for composites.

The requirement that a solution
{\bf v}

of problem (
1-1
) satisfy
\nabla{\bf v}=constant
on
\Omega
places strong restrictions on the region

\Omega

regions with this property in the periodic and
other cases, in all dimensions
n\geq 2
, including cases in which
\Omega
is
multiply connected.

Since
\nabla{\bf v}
being constant on

\Omega
leads to great simplification, ellipsoids play a central
role in the theory of composites (
Christensen
[
9
]
; Milton
[
44
]
),
in micromechanics (
Mura
[
46
]
) and in experimental measurements

[

). The uniformity of the induced
field can also be used to simplify
free energy minimization problems that arise
in theories of ferroelectric and magnetostrictive materials

[

]
;

[

]
; Liu, James & Leo
[

]
),
and this was our original motivation for developing
the theory of E-inclusions. Roughly speaking, even though these
are non-convex variational problems, the special properties
of ellipsoids are used to show that certain weak limits of
minimizing sequences are uniform, and this allows one to find,
and also to minimize, the relaxed energy.
Two or more ellipsoids
do not enjoy this special property. Thus
in many of these applications, only one
ellipsoid can be present in the model, and therefore the
results apply either to isolated ellipsoids or to
composites in the dilute limit. In most if not all of these
cases it is not an ellipsoid
per se
that is being used
but only its property of having uniform field, when it
is uniformly magnetized. Many authors have speculated on the
possibility that other regions may have this

Mura
[
47
]
), and
that, if so, their analysis would also apply to those regions.

Besides ellipsoids, examples of E-inclusions

gave a concise derivation of the

These results on the status of E-inclusions as optimal

E-inclusions have a more fundamental relation to the
underlying equations than merely as a method of

.

In Section
2
we present a general method for constructing E-inclusions. They
are found as solutions of a simple variational inequality with a piecewise
quadratic obstacle. The region where the
minimizer touches the obstacle defines
the E-inclusion. The existence and regularity of minimizers for this variational inequality
are adapted from the textbooks of

[

]
; Friedman
[
16
]
.
We further show that periodic E-inclusions corresponding
to a single negative-semidefinite matrix (

) can be
constructed in all dimensions and with any volume fraction.
The lattice vectors defining the periodicity can be arbitrarily prescribed.
In two dimensions, one family of these periodic
E-inclusions specialize to the Vigdergauz microstructure. By varying
the piecewise quadratic obstacle in the variational inequality,
a variety of more general E-inclusions can be produced.
For example, we can construct periodic E-inclusions with multiply connected components.
In Section
3
we present a numerical scheme for calculating periodic E-inclusions
and various examples of the calculated periodic E-inclusions.
In Section
4.1
we solve the periodic Eshelby inclusion problem and explicitly
calculate the effective properties of two-phase composites with periodic
E-inclusion structures.
In Section
4.2

and are shown to be attained by the periodic E-inclusions.
In Section
5
we introduce a generalized concept of E-inclusions
called sequential E-inclusions in terms of gradient Young measure.
We finish with a summary of our results.

2.2

To construct nonperiodic E-inclusions in
{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n},\,n\geq 3

the variational inequality

\displaystyle G_{r}(u_{r})=\inf_{u\in K_{r}}\bigg\{G_{r}(u)\equiv\int_{B_{r}}\frac{\;1}{\;2}|\nabla u|^{2}d{\bf x}\bigg\},

(2-27)

where

\displaystyle K_{r}=\{v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(B_{r})\,:\;v\geq\phi\}.

(2-28)

We use this variational inequality to find

and the coincident set,
and then we pass to the limit

Let

be quadratic functions on
{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}

{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}

As before, we say that

(i)

(ii)

, and

(iii)

is bounded from above on
{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}

From the basic theory

[
6

;

[

Theorem 2.4

Since

also satisfies the complementarity conditions

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{r}\geq 0,\;\;u_{r}\geq\phi,\;\;{{\rm\;\;and\;\;}}\;\;-\Delta u_{r}(u_{r}-\phi)=0\;\;\;{{\rm a.e.}}{{\rm\;on\;}}\;B_{r}.\qquad

In particular, letting

be the coincident set,

\displaystyle\nabla\nabla u_{r}=\nabla\nabla\phi\qquad\;\;{{\rm a.e.}}\ {\rm on}\;\Omega_{r}.

(2-31)

The limit of the minimizers

of problem (

Let

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{\infty}\geq 0,\;u_{\infty}\geq\phi,\;{{\rm\;\;and\;\;}}\;-\Delta u_{\infty}(u_{\infty}-\phi)=0\;\;{{\rm a.e.}}\;{{\rm\;on\;}}\;B_{R}.\qquad

We claim that

are disjoint and open. It follows from

\displaystyle\Delta u_{\infty}=0\;\;

\displaystyle{{\rm a.e.}}{{\rm\;on\;}}

Theorem 2.5

3
Examples of periodic E-inclusions

We now consider various examples of periodic E-inclusions. From the
discussion above, periodic E-inclusions constructed by
Theorem
2.2
can be specified by a Bravais lattice

{\cal L}
, the quantity
f>0
and a periodic piecewise
quadratic obstacle
\phi_{per}
.
It is worthwhile noticing that from the comparison theorem (see e.g.
Friedman

[
16
]
, page 26),
periodic E-inclusions corresponding to a fixed obstacle
satisfy
\Omega_{per}^{f_{1}}\subset\Omega_{per}^{f_{2}}

if
f_{2}>f_{1}>0
. Also the interior of any periodic E-inclusion
cannot contain the singular points of the obstacle on which

\nabla\nabla\phi_{per}
is unbounded in distributional sense.
By varying the obstacle
\phi_{per}
and
f
,
a large class of periodic E-inclusions can be constructed in any
dimension
n\geq 2
.
We show a few examples below.

The first example is a simple lamination. Let
{\bf n}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
be a
unit vector,
f>0
,
a<0
, and
h_{per}(x)=\max\{\frac{\;1}{\;2}a(x+\nu)^{2}:\;\nu\in{Z\hskip-4.8ptZ}\}
for
x\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}
.
Consider the obstacle

\displaystyle\phi_{per}({\bf x})=h_{per}({\bf x}\cdot{\bf n}).

By the method given above this is a
periodic E-inclusion corresponding to
{\bf Q}_{1}=a{\bf n}\otimes{\bf n}
and
{\bf Q}_{2}=f{\bf n}\otimes{\bf n}
with volume fractions
f/(f-a)
and
-a/(f-a)
, respectively,
see Fig.
1
. In another words a simple lamination
is a periodic E-inclusion.

We now present some numerical examples.
The coated spheres (
Hashin & Shtrikman
[
23
]
) and confocal ellipsoids
(
Milton
[
43
]
), familiar from homogenization theory,
can be constructed as a periodic E-inclusions.
The example in Fig.
2
is computed by using the obstacle

\displaystyle\phi_{per}({\bf x})=\max\{0,\frac{\;1}{\;2}({\bf x}+{\bf r})\cdot{\bf Q}_{1}({\bf x}+{\bf r})+h_{1}:\;{\bf r}\in{\cal L}\},

where
h_{1}>0
,

are appropriately chosen so that the graph
of the obstacle consists of isolated “mountains” emerging out of
a horizontal “sea”. So, if

is large enough,
the minimizer

contacts the mountains around the peaks
and the sea but is detached from the rim of singular points of
\phi_{per}
.
It can be proved that the coincident set in each unit cell is separated by two
confocal ellipsoids, by noticing the Newtonian potential of a homogeneous solid ellipsoid
is not only quadratic inside the ellipsoid, but also quadratic outside the ellipsoid on
the equipotential surface which is a confocal ellipsoid, see
Kellogg
[
29
]
.
On the other hand, if

is very small or the obstacles
of (

);
see also

[

for an analytic derivation of the

[
19
]
.
First let us consider the variational problem (
2-1
) with
the constraint
u\geq\phi_{per}
neglected. Clearly the Euler-Lagrange equation of
this variational problem is the Poisson equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u=f&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\;Y,\\
\mbox{periodic boundary conditions}&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\;\partial Y,\end{cases}

which, according to the finite element method (see e.g.
Kwon & Bang
[
35
]
), can be
discretized as

\displaystyle{\hat{K}}{\hat{u}}={\hat{f}}.

(3-1)

Here
{\hat{u}}
, a column vector, denotes the values of the potential
u
at the nodal points
in the finite element model,

{\hat{K}}
and
{\hat{f}}
are usually called the
stiffness matrix
and
loads
, respectively.
Now let us take into account the discretized constraint
{\hat{u}}\geq\hat{\phi}_{per}
, where

\hat{\phi}_{per}
are the values of the obstacle
\phi_{per}
at the nodal points.
The discrete version of the variational inequality (
2-1
) becomes
the following quadratic programming problem:

\displaystyle\min\{\hat{G}({\hat{u}})=-\frac{\;1}{\;2}{\hat{u}}\cdot{\hat{K}}{\hat{u}}+{\hat{f}}\cdot{\hat{u}}:\;{\hat{u}}\geq\hat{\phi}_{per}\},

(3-2)

which can be easily solved using standard solvers. The following computations use
a uniform mesh with around
10^{5}
nodal points. The iterations are terminated when
the relative difference between the values
\hat{G}({\hat{u}})
of two consecutive iterations
is less than
10^{-10}
. With these parameters, the iterations converge within
a few minutes on a personal computer. The resulting periodic E-inclusion includes all
nodal points on which
|{\hat{u}}-\hat{\phi}|
is less than
a\times 10^{-3}
, where
a

is at the order of
1
.

The numerical scheme is verified by comparing the results with the analytic
solutions for the Vigdergauz structures in two dimensions with a square unit cell
and with
{\bf Q}=-{{\rm\,diag}}(1,1)
. The volume fractions were chosen to be,
from inward to outward,
0.06,\;0.12
,
0.18,\;0.26
,

0.34,\;0.43
,
0.55,\;0.67
.
In Fig.

between the analytical shapes and our calculated shapes.
As is well-known from the Vigdergauz construction, E-inclusions are asymptotic to a
circle at small volume fraction and to the unit square at volume fractions approaching one.

4
Applications

In this section, we use periodic E-inclusions to
solve problems for two-phase composites.
From Definition
1
a periodic
E-inclusion is associated to matrices
{\mathbb{K}}
and volume fractions
\Theta
.
In general periodic E-inclusions having
N^{\prime}\leq N
distinct
matrices in
{\mathbb{K}}
can be used to solve problems for
(N^{\prime}+1)
-phase composites.
For two-phase composites we need only periodic E-inclusions with

{\mathbb{K}}=\{{\bf Q},{\bf Q},\dots,{\bf Q}\}
.
Applications of periodic E-inclusions to multi-phase composites
are presented in a separate publication (
Liu
[
37
]
).

4.1
Periodic Eshelby inclusion problems and effective
properties of two-phase composites

Our first observation is that some effective properties of
composites having one phase made with periodic E-inclusions can be
easily calculated. Let
{\mathbb{L}}
be the collection of all symmetric
tensors
{\bf L}:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
which are either
positive definite or elasticity tensors. We consider a periodic
two-phase composite defined by

\displaystyle{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\bf L}_{1}\in{\mathbb{L}}&\qquad{\bf x}\in\Omega,\\
{\bf L}_{0}\in{\mathbb{L}}&\qquad{\bf x}\in Y\setminus\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

where the notation is as above,
\Omega\subset Y
is measurable,
and
Y\subset{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
is an open unit cell.

Consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle J_{\ast}({\bf L},\,{\bf F},\,\Omega)=\min\bigg\{\frac{\;1}{\;2}{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})\cdot{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})d{\bf x}:\qquad

(4-4)

\displaystyle{\bf v}\in W^{1,2}_{per}(Y;{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n})\bigg\}.

Physically, in the case of linearized elasticity

J_{\ast}({\bf L},\,{\bf F},\,\Omega)
is the elastic
energy density induced by an applied average strain
{\bf F}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
.
The effective tensor
{\bf L}^{e}(\Omega)
is defined as (
Christensen
[
9
]
)

\displaystyle\frac{\;1}{\;2}{\bf F}\cdot{\bf L}^{e}(\Omega){\bf F}=J_{\ast}({\bf L},\,{\bf F},\,\Omega)\qquad\forall\,{\bf F}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}.

(4-5)

From standard arguments in the calculus of variations (
Evans
[
15
]
), a minimizer
of problem (
4-4
) exists and necessarily satisfies
the Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm div}\big[{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})\big]=0\qquad&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;Y,\\
\mbox{periodic boundary conditions}&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\partial Y.\\
\end{cases}

(4-6)

We are interested in calculating the effective tensor
{\bf L}^{e}(\Omega)
.
Problem (
4-6
) is referred to as
the
inhomogeneous
Eshelby inclusion problem
in a periodic setting (cf., equation (
1-2
)).

The relation between periodic E-inclusions and problem (
4-6
)
can be uncovered by adapting a well-known argument of
Eshelby
[
13
]
.
We begin with the
homogeneous
Eshelby inclusion problem,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm div}\big[{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf P}\chi_{\Omega}\big]=0\qquad&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;Y,\\
\mbox{periodic boundary conditions}&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\partial Y,\\
\end{cases}

(4-7)

where
{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
is given and
{\bf v}\in W^{1,2}_{per}(Y,{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n})
is the unknown.
Equation (
4-7
) is understood in the sense of distributions.
Below, we
sometimes write
{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})
to emphasize the (linear) dependence of

{\bf v}
on
{\bf P}
.
Further, motivated by the convenient property of ellipsoids employed by Eshelby,
we assume that
\Omega
and
{\bf P}
are such that there is a solution
{\bf v}
of
the homogeneous problem (
4-7
)
satisfying

\displaystyle\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})=-(1-\theta){\bf R}{\bf P}\;

\displaystyle{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\Omega,

(4-8)

where
\theta=|\Omega|/|Y|
is the volume fraction of the inclusion,
and the linear mapping

\displaystyle{\bf R}{\bf P}=\frac{-1}{1-\theta}\,{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{\Omega}\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})d{\bf x}

(4-9)

is symmetric and depends on
\Omega
(for the symmetry, see (
4-37
)).
From equations (
4-7
) and (
4-9
), it follows that

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})\cdot{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})d{\bf x}=\theta(1-\theta){\bf P}\cdot{\bf R}{\bf P}\geq 0\;\;\forall\;{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}.\qquad

(4-10)

Together with equation (
4-8
)
and following the Eshelby’s argument,
we now observe that a solution of problem (
4-7
)
also solves problem (
4-6
) under restrictions given below.
To see this, let us formally rewrite equations (
4-7
) and (
4-6
) in a less concise form as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm div}[{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}]=0&{{\rm\;in\;}}\;Y\setminus\Omega,\\
{\rm div}[{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}]=0&{{\rm\;in\;}}\;\Omega,\\
{{{[\![}}}{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf P}\chi_{\Omega}{{{]\!]}}}{\bf n}=0&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\partial\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm div}[{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}]=0&{{\rm\;in\;}}\;Y\setminus\Omega,\\
{\rm div}[{\bf L}_{1}\nabla{\bf v}]=0&{{\rm\;in\;}}\;\Omega,\\
{{{[\![}}}{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F}){{{]\!]}}}{\bf n}=0&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\partial\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

respectively,
where
{{{[\![}}}\cdot{{{]\!]}}}
denotes the jump across the
\partial\Omega
.
By matching the jump conditions in (
4.1
)
and (
4.1
), direct calculations show that if
{\bf v}

satisfies all equations in (
4.1
) and
equation (
4-8
), then
{\bf v}
also satisfies all
equations in (
4.1
) for
{\bf F}
satisfying

\displaystyle{{\vartriangle}\bf L}{\bf F}=(1-\theta){{\vartriangle}\bf L}{\bf R}{\bf P}-{\bf P},

(4-19)

where

{{\vartriangle}\bf L}={\bf L}_{0}-{\bf L}_{1}
. Properly interpreted, this formal argument
can be made rigorous. More specifically, the weak form of
(
4-7
) is

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}({\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf P}\chi_{\Omega})\cdot\nabla{\bf w}d{\bf x}=0\qquad\forall\;{\bf w}\in W^{1,2}_{per}(Y;{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}).

(4-20)

By equations
(
4-8
) and (
4-19
), equation (
4-20
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}[{\bf L}_{0}\nabla{\bf v}-{{\vartriangle}\bf L}(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})\chi_{\Omega}]\cdot\nabla{\bf w}d{\bf x}=0\qquad\forall\;{\bf w}\in W^{1,2}_{per}(Y;{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}),

which is exactly the weak form of (
4-6
).
Also, the energy of the inhomogeneous problem (
4-6
) can be conveniently written as

\displaystyle 2J_{\ast}({\bf L},{\bf F},\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})\cdot{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})d{\bf x}

(4-21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}{\bf F}\cdot{\bf L}({\bf x},\Omega)(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}{\bf F}\cdot{\bf L}_{0}{\bf F}d{\bf x}-{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}{\bf F}\cdot{{\vartriangle}\bf L}(\nabla{\bf v}+{\bf F})\chi_{\Omega}d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf F}\cdot{\bf L}_{0}{\bf F}+\theta{\bf P}\cdot{\bf F},

where

{\bf F}
and
{\bf P}
are related by equation (
4-19
).

As emphasized above, equation (
4-8
)
is not true unless the inclusion
\Omega

is very special. We now show that periodic E-inclusions given by Theorem
2.3

are indeed such special inclusions in many interesting situations. First we explain
the relation between the scalar and vector-valued problems.

Lemma 1

Let

u\in W^{2,2}_{per}(Y)
be a distributional solution of problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u=\theta-\chi_{\Omega}&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;Y,\\
\mbox{\rm periodic boundary conditions}&{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\partial Y.\\
\end{cases}

(4-22)

Denote by
\delta_{ij}
(
i,j=1,\cdots,n
) the components of the identity
matrix
{\bf I}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
.
If
{\bf L}_{0}\in{\mathbb{L}}
, and

\displaystyle({\bf L}_{0})_{piqj}=\mu_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{pq}+\mu_{2}\delta_{pj}\delta_{iq}+\lambda\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq},

(4-23)

then

\displaystyle{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})=\frac{{\bf P}\nabla u({\bf x})}{\lambda+\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}

(4-24)

solves problem (
4-7
) for
{\bf P}={\bf I}
. If
\mu_{2}+\lambda=0

then
{\bf v}
defined by (
4-24
) solves
problem (
4-7
) for every
{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
.

Proof

Note that
{\bf L}_{0}\in{\mathbb{L}}
implies the constants
\mu_{1}
,

\mu_{2}
and
\lambda
necessarily satisfy

\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}
,
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}>0
and

\lambda>-\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{n}
. Since
({\bf L}_{0})_{piqj}=\mu_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{pq}+\mu_{2}\delta_{pj}\delta_{iq}+\lambda\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}
, equation (
4-7
) can be
formally written as

\displaystyle\;\mu_{1}({\bf v})_{p,ii}+(\mu_{2}+\lambda)({\bf v})_{q,qp}+({\bf P})_{pi}(\chi_{\Omega})_{,i}=0.

(4-25)

It is easy to verify by
direct calculation that
{\bf v}
defined in equation (
4-24
)
satisfies equation (
4-25
) if
{\bf P}={\bf I}
. If
\mu_{2}+\lambda=0
then
{\bf v}
satisfying (
4-24
) also satisfies
equation (
4-25
) for all
{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times m}
. This
formal calculation can be made rigorous since solutions of
equation (
4-22
) are in
W^{2,2}_{per}(Y)
.

Now we note that if
\Omega
is
a periodic E-inclusion specified by
equation (
2-26
) with
{\bf Q}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
(cf., (
2-25
)),
the second equation in (
2-26
)
and equation (
4-24
) imply that for any

{\bf P}\in\{a{\bf I}:\;a\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}\}
,

\displaystyle\nabla{\bf v}({\bf x},{\bf P})=-\frac{(1-\theta)}{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\lambda}{\bf P}{\bf Q}\;\qquad\;{{\rm\;on\;}}\;\;\Omega

(4-26)

and

\displaystyle{\bf R}{\bf P}=\frac{{\bf P}{\bf Q}}{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\lambda}.

(4-27)

If
\mu_{2}+\lambda=0
, equations (
4-26
) and (
4-27
) hold for
all
{\bf P}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}
by Lemma
1
. Therefore, under the conditions
specified in Lemma
1
, if
\Omega
is a periodic E-inclusion then the
homogeneous Eshelby problem can be used to solve the inhomogeneous
Eshelby problem.

In applications to elasticity it is typically of interest to solve
the inhomogeneous Eshelby inclusion problem (
4-6
) for given
elasticity tensors
{\bf L}_{1}

5
Summary and discussion

We have shown the existence of special inclusions for which
the overdetermined problems (
1-5
)-(
1-6
)
and (
1-7
)-(
1-8
) admit a solution.
They are constructed as the coincident set of a simple
variational inequality with respect to piecewise quadratic obstacles.
These structures are called
E-inclusions
based on
their analogy with ellipsoids and their extremal properties
for energy minimization problems in homogenization theory.
Important restrictions on the parameters which characterize a periodic E-inclusion, namely,
the matrices
{\mathbb{K}}
and volume fractions
\Theta
,
have also been derived, see equation (
2-23
).
Numerical studies have revealed the diversity of periodic
and nonperiodic E-inclusions.

It is of interest in the above to know what are the restrictions
on the symmetric matrices
{\mathbb{K}}=({\bf Q}_{1},\dots,{\bf Q}_{N})

and volume fractions
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N})
for which we
can find periodic E-inclusions.
Let
u\in W^{2,2}_{per}(Y)
be a solution of (
1-7
)
associated to a periodic E-inclusion. Using
L^{p}
estimates for the Laplace operator
we see that
u
is in fact bounded in
W^{2,p}_{per}(Y)
for any

1\leq p<\infty
since
\Delta u
is bounded in
L^{\infty}_{per}(Y)

(
Gilbarg & Trudinger

[
18
]
, page 235).
Then we can rescale it and get a sequence
u^{(k)}({\bf x})=(1/k^{2})u(k{\bf x})\chi_{D}({\bf x})
for an open bounded domain
D
.
The corresponding sequence of gradients
{\bf v}^{(k)}({\bf x})=\nabla u^{(k)}({\bf x})=(1/k)\nabla u(k{\bf x})

is bounded in
W^{1,p}(D,{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n})
for any
1\leq p<\infty
. The
study of the gradient Young measure of the sequence
{\bf v}^{(k)}
gives
natural restrictions on
{\mathbb{K}}
and volume fractions
\Theta
. For this and
other purposes it is useful to define the concept of a sequential E-inclusion.
A
sequential E-inclusion
is a homogeneous gradient Young measure that
is generated by a sequence bounded in
W^{1,p}(D)

for any
1\leq p<\infty
, has zero center of mass,
and satisfies

\displaystyle\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\delta_{{\bf Q}_{i}}+\theta_{0}\mu,

(5-1)

where
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N}\geq 0
,

\theta_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\geq 0
,
\theta_{0}p_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}{{\rm Tr}}({\bf Q}_{i})
,
and
\mu
is a probability measure satisfying
supp
\,\mu\subset\{X\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}_{sym}:{{\rm Tr}}(X)=p_{0}\}
.
These conditions are all satisfied by the gradient Young measure generated by the
rescaled sequence
{\bf v}^{(k)}=\nabla u^{(k)}
. In
particular, the Dirac masses at
{\bf Q}_{i}
arise from the periodic E-inclusions and the
condition
{{\rm Tr}}(X)=p_{0}
arises from the Poisson equation
\Delta u=p_{0}

on the complementary set. From the basic relation between gradient Young measures and
quasiconvex functions (
Kinderlehrer & Pedregal
[
32
,
33
]

),
we have that

\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}\psi(X)\,d\nu(X)\geq\psi(0)

(5-2)

for all quasiconvex functions
\psi:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}
.
The notion of quasiconvexity used here is the one appropriate
for second gradient (
Šverák
[
52
]
). In particular,
we can show that, by similar arguments as in
Allaire & Kohn
[
1
]
,
equation (
5-2
) holds if
\psi
is quadratic and
is rank-one convex for symmetric rank-one
matrices.

We now show that
the previous restrictions (
2-23
)
on
{\mathbb{K}}
and
\Theta
also follow from equation (
5-2
).
For any
X\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}_{sym}
, consider the quadratic function

\psi(X)={\bf m}\cdot({{\rm Tr}}(X)X-X^{2}){\bf m}

for some
{\bf m}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
. Direct calculations reveal that

\psi(X+\lambda{\bf n}\otimes{\bf n})=\psi(X)+\lambda({\bf m}\cdot X{\bf m}|{\bf n}|^{2}+{{\rm Tr}}(X)({\bf n}\cdot{\bf m})^{2}-2({\bf n}\cdot{\bf m}){\bf n}\cdot X{\bf m})
is an affine
function of
\lambda
for any
{\bf n},\,{\bf m}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
and therefore is
convex on symmetric rank-one matrices. (In fact,
\psi
is a null Lagrangian
in this second gradient context.)
An application of (
5-2
) to
\pm\psi
shows that for a sequential
E-inclusion (
5-1
),

\displaystyle 0=\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}({{\rm Tr}}(X)X-X^{2})d\nu(X)\hskip 142.26378pt

(5-3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}({{\rm Tr}}({\bf Q}_{i}){\bf Q}_{i}-{\bf Q}_{i}^{2})+\theta_{0}\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}({{\rm Tr}}(X)X-X^{2})d\mu(X).

Since the center of mass of
\nu
is zero, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}{{\rm Tr}}(X)Xd\mu(X)=p_{0}\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}Xd\mu(X)=p_{0}[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}{\bf Q}_{i}]/\theta_{0}.

(5-4)

The last term in (

) can be bounded using Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}X^{2}d\mu(X)\geq\bigg[\int_{{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}}Xd\mu(X)\bigg]^{2}=[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}{\bf Q}_{i}]^{2}/\theta_{0}^{2}.

(5-5)

Substituting equations (

) and (

) into (
5-3
), we
obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\theta_{0}{{\rm Tr}}({\bf Q}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{j}{{\rm Tr}}({\bf Q}_{j})]\theta_{i}{\bf Q}_{i}\geq\theta_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}{\bf Q}_{i}^{2}+[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}{\bf Q}_{i}]^{2},

which is identical to equation (

). Of course, there are many other
quasiconvex functions that could be used in equation (

) that would
evidently give further restrictions on the

and

.

As shown in recent work on optimal bounds for multiphase composites (
Liu
[
37
]
),
the concept of a sequential E-inclusion is useful to characterize the
microstructures that attain the Hashin-Shtrikman bounds. The result in its strongest
form states that if
{\bf L}_{1},\dots,{\bf L}_{N}
satisfies
{\cal R}({\bf L}_{i}-{\bf L}_{0})\supset{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n\times n}_{sym},\ i\neq 0
(see Theorem
4.2
),
a microstructure attains the Hashin-Shtrikman bounds if and only
if it is a sequential E-inclusion.

E-inclusions can also be used to solve
problems on the effective behavior of nonlinear composites.
For instance, let us consider
a periodic two-phase nonlinear composite with effective
properties defined by

\displaystyle I^{e}({\bf e}_{0})=\min_{w\in W^{1,2}_{per}(Y)}{\int\!\!\!\!\!\!-}_{Y}I(\nabla w+{\bf e}_{0},{\bf x})d{\bf x},

(5-6)

where
{\bf e}_{0}\in{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}
is the applied average field, and
the energy function
I:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}\times{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}
is given by

\displaystyle I({\bf e},{\bf x})=\begin{cases}I_{1}({\bf e})&{{\rm if}}\;{\bf x}\in\Omega,\\
{\bf e}\cdot{\bf I}{\bf e}&{{\rm if}}\;{\bf x}\in Y\setminus\Omega.\\
\end{cases}

Here
I_{1}:{I\hskip-3.0ptR}^{n}\to{I\hskip-3.0ptR}
, describing the
nonlinear phase on
\Omega
, is a strictly convex but not necessarily quadratic function,
and the identity matrix
{\bf I}
describes the linear phase on
Y\setminus\Omega
.
If
\Omega
is a periodic E-inclusion with matrix
{\bf Q}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
and volume fraction

\theta
(cf., (
2-26
)), the minimization problem (
5-6
)
is explicitly solvable in terms of a linear combination of functions satisfying
(
2-26
) in spite of the nonlinearity of
I_{1}({\bf e}_{0})
. The
details will be presented in a future publication. Essentially,
the fact that
\nabla w
is constant on
\Omega
reduces the nonlinear part of the
problem to an algebraic equation. This algebraic equation
may be solvable in any nonlinear theory for which linear boundary conditions
gives a linear solution. It is then only a matter of showing that
these boundary conditions can be satisfied.
This observation (for ellipsoids) goes back to

Hill
[
25
]
.

Structures that look a lot like E-inclusions are apparently seen in
nature. For instance, Fig.
12
is a dark-field electron micrograph
of Ni
3
Ge precipitates in late stage coarsening of binary Ni-Ge alloys.
The experimental conditions are described in
Kim & Ardell

[
31
]
. The
transformation strain is dilatational in this case and the transformation is
cubic to cubic.
Precipitates in this and other coarsening nickel-based superalloys form
approximately periodic arrays and have shapes like those seen in Fig.
7
.
Like periodic E-inclusions, these precipitates become more cuboidal at higher volume
fraction. In late stage coarsening it is accepted that the
minimization of elastic energy, both with respect to fields as well as shapes,
governs the evolution of microstructure (
Jou, Leo & Lowengrub
[
26
]
;
Thornton, Akaiwa & Voorhees
[
50
]
). In
addition, interfacial energy plays a role in the evolution,
but its influence is less important at the later stages of coarsening.
E-inclusions are likely to be favored in the evolution of precipitates in these
alloys since they are optimal shapes with respect to elastic energy, as shown
in Section
4
. To make this possible connection between E-inclusions and
Ni
3
Ge precipitates quantitative, one should find an estimate of the effect
of the presence of interfacial energy on shape. Also, one would also need to check
that the elasticity tensor of the matrix phase exceeds that of the precipitate
(or generalize our results) and generalize our results to allow a
cubic matrix phase.

Finally we remark that the analogy between ellipsoids
and periodic E-inclusions is not perfect in the sense
that
\nabla{\bf v}
is uniform on the ellipsoid in problem (
1-1
) for
any

matrix
{\bf P}
, whereas periodic E-inclusions have this property only
for the matrix
{\bf P}={\bf I}
, unless the tensor
{\bf L}_{0}
has a
special form. Nevertheless, we anticipate periodic E-inclusions can
find wide applications in the theories of micromechanics, composites
and fracture mechanics as does the ubiquitous
Eshelby’s
solution
in these fields.
