Uncertainty Calibration in Bayesian Neural Networks via Distance-Aware Priors
Gianluca Detommaso, Alberto Gasparin, Andrew Wilson, Cedric Archambeau

Abstract
As we move away from the data, the predictive uncertainty should increase, since a great variety of explanations are consistent with the little available information. We introduce Distance-Aware Prior (DAP) calibration, a method to correct overconfidence of Bayesian deep learning models outside of the training domain. We define DAPs as prior distributions over the model parameters that depend on the inputs through a measure of their distance from the training set. DAP calibration is agnostic to the posterior inference method, and it can be performed as a post-processing step. We demonstrate its effectiveness against several baselines in a variety of classification and regression problems, including benchmarks designed to test the quality of predictive distributions away from the data.



1
Introduction

Uncertainty quantification is crucial in a variety of real-world applications, such as human decision making in medicine, risk assessment in automatic vehicles, anomaly detection in fraud and cybersecurity. Uncertainty is often compartmentalized into aleatoric and epistemic components
[
17
,
29
,
13
]
. Aleatoric uncertainty characterizes the intrinsic stochasticity in the data given the data generating process. Epistemic uncertainty describes our inability to identify the data generating process because of lack of information
[
13
]
. Aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible because, given a data generating process, the noise in the data cannot be decreased. Epistemic uncertainty can be reduced by either better modelling or by collecting more data. In general, we expect epistemic uncertainty to be small close to the training set, where data is available, and large outside of the training domain, when data is either not available or very sparse. We refer to these two situations as in-distribution and out-of-distribution (OOD). Deep learning models tend to overfit the training data, erroneously providing the same uncertainty regardless of where we wish to make a prediction
[
11
]
.

In Bayesian neural networks, one places a prior over parameters, inducing a prior over functions that represents epistemic uncertainty. The most commonly used prior is an independent Gaussian prior over parameters
[
43
]
. In practice issues with approximate inference can have a significant effect on the ability of these approaches to represent growing uncertainty away from the data
[
28
,
8
,
48
]
. Moreover, even with high quality inference, these priors lead to poor predictive distribution in OOD settings
[
14
]
.

In the classic framework, model parameters and input data are assumed to be independent
a priori

[
43
]
, failing to represent our honest beliefs that the uncertainty should grow as we move away from the data. However, representing these beliefs is particularly desirable in OOD settings, where the data provides none or scarce information about the model parameters, and the prior distribution is the main determinant of the epistemic uncertainty.

For this reason, we drop the independence assumption between model parameters and inputs, and propose uncertainty calibration via
distance-aware priors
(DAPs). These priors over the model parameters induce larger model uncertainty at inputs further from the training data. Since the prior distribution has a strong influence on the predictive distribution in parts of the input space where data is sparse or not available, we can calibrate the input-dependent increase in prior uncertainty to remedy overconfidence OOD. As we do not impose any restriction on the target data or likelihood function, DAP calibration can be performed in both classification and regression problems. Furthermore, DAP calibration is agnostic to the posterior inference method
[
22
,
9
,
45
,
28
,
34
,
50
]
, and it can be performed as a post-processing step.

We remark that although DAP calibration only targets OOD uncertainty, it can be seamlessly combined with methods better tailored to calibrate in-domain uncertainty, such as temperature scaling
[
35
,
11
,
21
,
23
,
41
,
16
]
. Nevertheless, throughout this paper we will exclusively focus on OOD uncertainty calibration, in order to avoid confounding results.

We can summarize the contributions of this work as follows. (1) We propose a novel method to calibrate OOD uncertainty estimates of Bayesian neural network models. (2) We introduce a simple distance-aware Gaussian prior, capturing the dependence of the model parameters to the inputs through a parametrized distance. (3) We consider a distance measure based on neural network feature mapping and (4) introduce a calibration loss function to achieve a desired level of uncertainty OOD, in both classification and regression problems. (5) We propose an importance sampling procedure that allows to perform calibration as a post-processing step. (6) We study the effectiveness of DAP calibration over several experiments, and against several methods for OOD uncertainty estimation.

2
Related Work

The concept of distant-aware uncertainty is native in Gaussian Processes (GPs)
[
42
]
, where a kernel captures a measure of distance between pairs of inputs. Modern approaches combine Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernels with deep feature extractors, i.e. deep neural networks that transform the input space in order to obtain a better fit of the data
[
4
]
. This approach is commonly referred to as Deep Kernel Learning (DKL)
[
46
,
44
]
, and has recently inspired a variety of methods for deterministic uncertainty estimation. SNGP
[
26
]
is one such method, which builds upon DKL proposing to approximate a GP via Random Fourier Features (RFFs)
[
36
]
. In the same work the authors propose a theoretical analysis of the uncertainty estimation problem, identifying input distance awareness as a key property for the task. In practice, they show how requiring deep learning models to satisfy a bi-Lipschitz condition (enforced via Spectral Normalization (SN)
[
31
]
) can actually make the model’s predictive uncertainty input distance aware.
[
1
]
adopts a similar approach to the one described in SNGP, where SN is used along with residual networks to enforce the bi-Lipschitz condition, while a variational inducing point GP approximation is used instead of RFFs.

In Deep Uncertainty Quantification (DUQ)
[
40
]
, another single-forward pass method, the bi-Lipschitz constraint is enforced via a two-sided gradient penalty
[
10
]
, while predictions are made via a RBF network which measures the distance between the transformed test input and some class centroids. This limits its scope to classification only. These methods are competitive with Deep Ensembles on multiple OOD benchmarks, but still requires changes in the training procedure and, as such, cannot be applied as a post-processing step. A simpler alternative that addresses this issue is Deep Deterministic Uncertainty (DDU)
[
32
]
, where Gaussian Discriminant Analysis is fit after model training and later used for characterizing epistemic uncertainty, while the softmax predictive entropy is used for aleatoric uncertainty. Similarly to
[
40
,
26
,
1
]
, this method performs best when SN is used in the feature extractor, outperforming both SNGP and DUQ on popular OOD classification benchmarks.

In this work, rather than introducing a distance-aware output function, we define distance-aware prior distributions in order to obtain uncertainty estimates that grow as we move away from the training data. Like DDU, DAP calibration does not require any change to neither the deep learning model, nor the posterior inference procedure.

3
Distance-aware priors

We assume to be given a training data set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of inputs
x_{i}
and targets
y_{i}

We denote collections of training inputs and targets by
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
, respectively. After the training data is observed, we are interested in quantifying the uncertainty of validation targets
y_{j}^{*}
, given validation inputs
x_{j}^{*}
, for
j=1,\dots,N^{*}
. We do so by looking at statistics defined over the predictive density
p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, which is obtained by integrating out the parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
of the deep learning model:

p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}}p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*})\,p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta,

(1)

where
p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*})
denotes the test likelihood function and
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
the posterior distribution.

In (
1
), we followed the typical assumption that the test target is independent of the training data given the test input and the model parameters
[
2
,
12
]
, and hence we can write
p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as
p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*})
. The idea behind this assumption is that the parameters
\theta
capture all the information in the training set necessary to characterize
y_{j}^{*}
.

A second common assumption is that the prior distribution of the model parameters
\theta
is independent of the inputs
\boldsymbol{x}
and
x_{j}^{*}
. In scenarios where the interpretation of the model parameters is explainable, this assumption may be justified by the lack of a clear causal relation from inputs to parameters. In deep learning models, on the other hand, the model parameters have no clear interpretation, hence the independence assumption is dictated by practical reasons rather than a principled understanding of the dependency structure. In fact, in order to compensate for the consequences of this independence assumption, input data are often normalized and outliers removed, with the goal of making parameters “compatible” with all the inputs passed to the model, instead of letting the distribution of the model parameters depend on the actual inputs. While practical, from an epistemic (model) uncertainty point of view this assumption may be harmful, as it is taking away from the model parameters the possibility to express more or less uncertainty depending on the inputs. For example, it is reasonable to assume that the model should be able to express
a priori
more uncertainty far away from the training inputs, and less close by.

For these reasons, we do not assume that the model parameters are independent of the inputs, and will construct prior distributions
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{\xi})
that express more uncertainty when arbitrary collections of inputs
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are far from the training inputs, and less otherwise. We will refer to this type of distributions as
distance-aware priors
. Figure
1
shows the directed graphical model, where the blue arrows correspond to the dependencies that we account for, in contrast with the standard assumption described earlier.

\boldsymbol{x}
x_{j}^{*}
\theta
\boldsymbol{y}
y_{j}^{*}

Figure 1
:
Unlike standard priors in deep learning, we assume not only the targets
y
but also the model parameters
\theta
explicitly depend on the data inputs
x
, as indicated by the blue arrows.

It also makes clear that in this framework there is a difference between the posterior density
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
that appears in (
1
) and
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, since
\theta
and
x_{j}^{*}
are not independent given the training data
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
.

3.1
Independent and joint predictions are not the same

We note that because of the blue arrows in Figure
1
, in this framework there is a difference between predicting independently and jointly: the joint predictive
p(\boldsymbol{y}^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and the product of the independent predictive distributions
p(y_{j}^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
are not the same, since
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
and
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
are different.

The right distribution to use depends on the application. If we are interested in a certain prediction given that we are observing a certain set of inputs (e.g. predicting the score of a student, given that scores are normalized over the results of the whole class), the joint predictive may be the right distribution to use. Otherwise, if we are interested in simultaneous independent predictions, we would rather use the independent predictive distribution. Throughout this paper we use the individual predictive, since we would argue that the latter situation is more commonly desired in machine learning applications.

3.2
Distance-aware priors

We define distance-aware priors as distributions over the model parameters that depend on the inputs through a measure of their distance from the training inputs. In this work we use the term
distance
to indicate set-to-set distance, typically from an arbitrary set of inputs
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to the training set
\boldsymbol{x}
. We require a distance function to be always non-negative, and to satisfy the triangular inequality. Notice that we drop the symmetry assumption since it is not needed for the purpose of this work. We use the notation
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
to indicate a distance from
\boldsymbol{\xi}
to
\boldsymbol{x}
parametrized by
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\phi}}
, where for sake of notation we leave the argument
\boldsymbol{x}
implicit.

While there are many possible ways to define a distance-aware prior, throughout this paper we will consider the following distance-aware Gaussian prior:

p(\theta|\boldsymbol{\xi})=\text{Normal}(\theta\,|0,\,(\sigma_{0}+d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))^{2}I_{n_{\theta}}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is an arbitrary collection of inputs,
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a standard deviation parameter,
I_{n_{\theta}}
is an identity matrix of size
n_{\theta}
and
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a distance metric parametrized by
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\phi}}
.

The definition of distance-aware Gaussian in (
2
) could be easily extended by considering an array of distance
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{\theta}}
instead of a scalar quantity, which would allow to rescale the prior variance independently for each component of
\theta
. However, throughout this paper we will not consider this option, since a scalar distance is practically easier to calibrate over a low-dimensional parameter
\phi
.

3.3
Requiring the distance to be zero at training inputs

We further require that
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, i.e. the distance evaluated at the collection of training inputs must be 0. It follows that, at training time, the distance-aware Gaussian prior introduced in
3.2
boils down to a classic Gaussian prior, i.e.
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})=\text{Normal}(\theta|0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
,
which does not depend on the distance nor on its parameters
\phi
. Furthermore, by Bayes’ theorem we can write

p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=p(\boldsymbol{y}|\theta,\boldsymbol{x})p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})/p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x}).

The posterior
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is not distance dependent since none of the terms on the right hand side depend on distance. As such, the latter corresponds to the posterior of standard frameworks, and it can be inferred via classic posterior inference methods
[
33
,
3
,
27
,
22
,
9
]
. The difference between classic priors and distance-aware priors arises at prediction time, when we need to evaluate the posterior
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. In fact, by Bayes’ theorem this posterior can be expressed in terms of the prior
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
, which in turn depends on the distance
d_{\phi}([x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}])
, that is generally non-zero.

3.4
Choice of distance in distance-aware priors

In this paper we consider a distance

d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=g(\phi)d_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

(3)

where
g(\cdot)
is a scalar positive function of
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
, while
d_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is a distance independent of
\phi
. We address
d_{0}(\cdot)
as
pre-distance
, since it corresponds to the distance
d_{\phi}(\cdot)
before the scaling
g(\phi)
has been applied. The choice in (
3
) is practically compelling for calibration over
\phi
, since after the pre-distance
d_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is pre-computed, the distance
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
can be cheaply evaluated for different values of
\phi
. The function
g(\phi)
acts as a scaling factor, which regulates the strength of the distance in the variance of the distance-aware prior, for the specific collection of inputs
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

We require the scaling function
g(\phi)
to be a smooth function with positive image defined on the reals. This condition allows us to optimize with respect to
\phi
in an unbounded domain. In this paper we simply choose
g(\phi)=e^{\phi}
, but other functions, e.g. a softplus or a scaled sigmoid, are also valid choices. We take the parameter
\phi
to be a scalar to promote scalability and robustness; other choices are possible.

As pre-distance, we consider a projected nearest neighbor asymmetric distance given by

d_{0}(\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\max_{k}\min_{i=1\dots,N}\|\mathcal{P}(\xi_{k})-\mathcal{P}(x_{i})\|_{2},

(4)

where
\xi_{k}
refers to the inputs in the collection
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
is a projection operator that transforms the input space into a low-dimensional latent space.
The projector
\mathcal{P}
can be defined in several ways, for example via a variational autoencoder (VAE)
[
19
]
, random kitchen sinks
[
36
]
, or as a deep feature extractor, i.e. as the forward pass of a trained deep learning model, up to the last hidden layer
[
4
]
. Because at posterior inference time we already train a deep learning model from input to output, throughout this paper we exploit the latter solution as a projector. We remark that the pre-distance in (
4
) is strongly related to the Hausdorff distance
[
38
]
, which, unlike (
4
), is symmetric. Since the symmetry property is not needed for the purpose of this paper, we use (
4
) because cheaper to compute.

It is important to notice that, since we are interested in independent predictions, as discussed in Section
3.1
, we only ever need to evaluate the distance at collections
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}=[x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}]
. From (
4
), it is immediate to see that
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, satisfying the property required in Section
3.2
, which makes the posterior
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
independent of the distance. On the other hand, we observe that

d_{\phi}([x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}])=d_{\phi}(x_{j}^{*})=\min_{i=1\dots,N}\|\mathcal{P}(x_{j}^{*})-\mathcal{P}(x_{i})\|_{2}.

Hence the maximum operator in (
4
) is never needed, and it could be formally replaced by any other function over the index
k
, e.g. a sum operator.

4
Estimation and calibration of predictive uncertainty

In this section we introduce importance sampling as a convenient method to estimate statistics with respect to the predictive
p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. Furthermore, we introduce a calibration strategy to correct overconfidence OOD as a post-processing step.

4.1
Importance sampling to compute statistics over the posterior predictive distribution

As discussed in Section
3.1
, we are interested in generating independent predictions for each of the inputs
x_{j}^{*}
. In order to do so, one may write the predictive distribution in (
1
) as an expectation with respect to the posterior
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, and estimate it via a Monte Carlo integration. However, since for each
j
we require an individual posterior inference approximation, inference can become quickly infeasible. Furthermore, as we will see in Section
4.2
, in order to calibrate the distance
d_{\phi}([x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}])
in the distance-aware priors, we might want predictive estimates for several values of
\phi
, making this approach even more computationally intensive.

In order to circumvent all these problems at once, we propose to use importance sampling, where we adopt the posterior
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as importance distribution. We note that the latter posterior does not depend on
x_{j}^{*}
, nor does it depend on
\phi
: as in Section
3.3
, we required
d_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
. We then rewrite the predictive in (
1
) as

p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}[p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*})w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})],

(5)

where we refer to
w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as the
posterior weight
defined by

w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}=\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}.

(6)

We provide details in Appendix
A
. Hence, we can infer the posterior
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
a single time, generate samples from it, and estimate the predictive in (
5
) via Monte Carlo for each
x_{j}^{*}
and each
\phi
.

More generally, we can use this approach to estimate the posterior expectation of any statistics
f(\theta)
:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}[f(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}[f(\theta)w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})].

(7)

If
f(\theta):=p(y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*})
, we get
the predictive distribution in (
5
), while if
f(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{j}^{*}|\theta,x_{j}^{*}}[Y_{j}^{*}]
, we get the predictive mean. Other statistics such as predictive variance and entropy, as well as their decomposition into aleatoric and epistemic components, can be derived analogously.

We remark once more that while it might appear unfamiliar that the posterior
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
depends on the validation input
x_{j}^{*}
, this dependence is due to the model dependencies highlighted in blue in Figure
1
, for which
x_{j}^{*}
and
\theta
are not independent even when
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are observed. Similarly, the marginal
p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
also depends on
x_{j}^{*}
, since
\boldsymbol{y}
and
x_{j}^{*}
are not independent when
\theta
is not observed. This phenomenon has a natural analogy with the
observer effect
in physics
[
7
]
, stating that the outcome of a system can depend on the point of view of who is observing it, and be affected by the act of observing itself. In our framework, the system is the probabilistic model, and the point of view corresponds to the index
j
, for which an input
x_{j}
is observed.

4.1.1
Marginal ratio estimation and the Gaussian posterior scenario

The computation of the posterior weight in (
6
) involves the estimation of the marginal ratio, i.e. the ratio between
p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
and
p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
. Each of these marginals can be estimated via Monte Carlo integration:

p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x}}[p(\boldsymbol{y}|\theta,\boldsymbol{x})]
and
p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}}[p(\boldsymbol{y}|\theta,\boldsymbol{x})]
.
One alternative, for which a derivation is provided in Appendix
B
, is to observe that

\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}\right]^{-1},

which can be again approximated via Monte Carlo with samples from the posterior
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. If we furthermore approximate the posterior as a Gaussian distribution, which is often the case in several popular Bayesian inference methods, e.g.
[
3
,
27
]
, then the marginal can be estimated in closed-form. Indeed, given the Gaussian distance-aware prior definition in (
2
), suppose that the posterior distribution
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is approximated as a diagonal Gaussian with mean
\mu=[\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n_{\theta}}]
and variance
\sigma^{2}=[\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma^{2}_{n_{\theta}}]
. In addition, let us assume that
\max_{i}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}<\sigma_{0}^{2}
, meaning that the data information contracts the prior variance over all directions of the parameter space. Then we have

\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i=1}^{n_{\theta}}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\beta_{i,j}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{i,j}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right),

with
d_{j}:=d([x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}])
,
\beta_{i,j}^{-2}:=\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}
and
\gamma_{j}^{-2}:=\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{(\sigma_{0}+d_{j}^{2})}
. See Appendix (
B
).

4.2
Calibrating the distance as a post-processing step of training

We have introduced distance-aware priors in order to correct overconfidence of predictions OOD. We do so by increasing the prior variance according to the distance of the validation inputs from the training set. However, for the total predictive uncertainty to be reasonable, the distance needs to be calibrated. Since we use a parametric distance
d_{\phi}(\cdot)
, specifically as in (
3
), we calibrate the distance by picking
\phi
such that we reach the minimum of a certain calibration loss.

There are many plausible choices for the calibration loss. For example, given a validation data set, one could maximize a sum over
j
of the test log-marginal likelihood, i.e.
\sum_{j}\log p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*})
, in order to find the parameter
\phi
for which the model best fits the validation data. A similar choice may involve maximizing the summed log-predictive
\sum_{j}\log p(y_{j}^{*}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, where the marginalization is with respect to the posterior rather than the prior. Alternatively, one could choose an empirical Bayes approach
[
30
]
. In this paper we choose the following loss function, which appeared to work best across a variety of experiments:

\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\frac{1}{N^{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{*}}\|\mathcal{U}_{\phi}(x_{j}^{*})-\gamma\|^{2}.

(8)

The loss in (
8
) forces a certain measure of uncertainty
\mathcal{U}_{\phi}(x_{j}^{*})
to be as close as possible to a certain
\gamma
at a set of validation inputs
x_{j}^{*}
, for
j=1,\dots,N^{*}
. For example, in a classification case, we may choose
\mathcal{U}_{\phi}(x_{j}^{*})
to be the predictive mean evaluated at some OOD inputs
x_{j}^{*}
, and
\gamma=\left[\tfrac{1}{m},\dots,\tfrac{1}{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m
is the number of classes. This loss steers the model towards a state of total uncertainty at the given inputs, counteracting overconfidence OOD. In regression, we may choose
\mathcal{U}_{\phi}(x_{j}^{*})
to be the epistemic variance evaluated at some OOD inputs
x_{j}^{*}
, and
\gamma
to be some value based on a quantile of the epistemic variances estimated at the training inputs. This strategy drives the epistemic variance to be large OOD compared to the variance in-domain, which again counteracts the overconfidence issue.

The OOD inputs
x_{j}^{*}
might be available for a certain test case, in which case they might as well be directly used. If these are not available, a possible strategy is to generate them from an arbitrary distribution, with a domain just outside of the training domain. With low-dimensional inputs, this is convenient, as one may be able to visualize where the training domain is located, and simulate OOD inputs accordingly. When this is less clear, a possible drawback is that if the generated inputs are very far from the training data, even after calibrating with (
8
) the predictive uncertainty might still be too small in the nearest of the training domain. In order to avoid this issue, in high-dimensions we prefer to take a set of validation inputs on which we either misclassify, or we misregress above a certain error threshold. Intuitively, inputs on which we predict badly are often the furthest from the training domain, which we will confirm experimentally in Section
5
.

5
Experiments

In this section, we first demonstrate the effect of DAP calibration on low-dimensional toy examples, in order to visualize its effect on OOD uncertainty estimates. Then we test DAP calibration on UCI Gap
[
8
]
, a set of benchmarks designed to test the quality of predictive distributions away from the data. Finally, we study the ability of our method to discriminate in-distribution and OOD data, on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR10. We compare its performance against several baselines.

5.1
Toy examples

We start with two low-dimensional toy examples. The first example is a standard two-moons two-dimensional data set for classification, extensively studied in several works
[
26
,
40
]
. Details about the experimental setup are given in Appendix
C
. We focus on the epistemic variance, as it best characterizes uncertainty OOD. The left panel in Figure
2
shows the epistemic component of the predictive variance produced via Automatic Differentiation Variational Inference (ADVI)
[

6
Conclusion and future directions

In this work we introduced a type of OOD uncertainty calibration based on distance-aware priors (DAPs). These prior distributions assume that model parameters depend on the inputs through a measure of their distance from the training data. DAP calibration can be done as a post-processing step of posterior inference, and it can be easily integrated with other popular calibration techniques. We performed extensive evaluation on both classification and regression tasks, demonstrating competitive performance against several other methods that aim to estimate OOD uncertainty. We believe DAP can be used as a practical option to decrease the issue of overconfidence in out-of-distribution settings. We expect this approach to have broad impact, since in virtually any real-world problem our test points are not drawn from exactly the same distribution as our training points, and we must have carefully calibrated uncertainty for decision making in these settings.

A natural extension of this work is to couple DAP calibration with other methods for in-domain calibration, such as Platt scaling
[
35
]
. Furthermore, it would be interesting to investigate the impact of feature-collapse
[
40
,
26
,
32
,
1
]
when using neural networks as the projector in the distance computation in
DAP. Another important direction is to improve the importance sampling approach by employing variance reduction strategies that can mitigate its known instability issues
[
39
]
.

Appendix A
The posterior weight

The relation between the distance-aware prior distribution
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
and the posterior distribution
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
in (
1
) is given by Bayes’ theorem:

p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\theta,\boldsymbol{x})p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})},

where
p(\boldsymbol{y}|\theta,\boldsymbol{x})
is the training likelihood,
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
is the distance-aware prior and
p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
is the marginal training likelihood, i.e. the evidence. Then, by using Bayes’ theorem on both
p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, we get

\displaystyle w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle:=\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}=\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}.

Appendix B
The marginal ratio in the Gaussian scenario

We first note that the expected value of
w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
with respect to
p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})
is one:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}[w(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})]=\int\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta=\int p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta=1.

Given the expression of posterior weight (see Appendix
A
), this implies

\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}\right]^{-1}.

Secondly, let us assume

\displaystyle p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle:=N(\theta|0,(\sigma_{0}+d_{j})^{2}I_{n_{\theta}}),

\displaystyle p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle:=N(\theta|0,\sigma_{0}^{2}I_{n_{\theta}}),

\displaystyle p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle:=N(\theta|\mu,\text{diag}(\sigma^{2})),

where
d_{j}:=d([x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}])
, and
\text{diag}(\sigma^{2})
is a diagonal matrix with
\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{\theta}}
on the diagonal. We have

\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{(\sigma_{0}+d_{j})^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right)\right)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\exp\left(\frac{\|\theta\|^{2}}{2\gamma_{j}^{2}}\right),

with

\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}:=\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\sigma_{0}+d_{j})^{2}}.

Let us define
\beta_{i,j}^{-2}:=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}
, and assume
\beta_{i,j}^{2}>0
. We will show later when this condition is true. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\frac{p(\theta|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\theta|\boldsymbol{x})}\right]=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{\theta}/2}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}-2\theta_{i}\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right)\,d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{\theta}/2}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}\right)\theta_{i}^{2}-2\theta_{i}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right)\,d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{\theta}/2}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta_{i,j}^{-2}\left(\theta_{i}^{2}-2\theta_{i}\beta_{i,j}^{2}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\beta_{i,j}^{4}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\right)-\beta_{i,j}^{2}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}+\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right)\,d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{\theta}/2}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{i,j}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right)\int\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\beta_{i,j}^{2}}\left(\theta_{i}-\beta_{i,j}^{2}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\,d\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{\beta_{i,j}}{\sigma_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{i,j}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right).

Because of the identity shown above, we have

\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}|x_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{x})}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}+d_{j}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{n_{\theta}}\prod_{i}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\beta_{i,j}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{i,j}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)\right).

Let us now define
\bar{\sigma}^{2}:=\max(\sigma_{i}^{2})
. We show that if
\bar{\sigma}^{2}<\sigma_{0}^{2}
, then
\beta_{i,j}^{2}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,n_{\theta}
. Let us start by the thesis:

\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}>0\quad\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n_{\theta},

which is verified if and only if

\gamma_{j}^{2}>\max(\sigma_{i}^{2})=\bar{\sigma}^{2}.

By definition of
\gamma_{j}^{2}
, this is true if and only if

\sigma_{0}^{2}(\sigma_{0}+d_{j})^{2}>\bar{\sigma}^{2}(d_{j}^{2}+2d_{j}\sigma_{0}).

By developing the products, we get

\sigma_{0}^{4}+\sigma_{0}^{2}d_{j}^{2}+2\sigma_{0}^{3}d_{j}>\bar{\sigma}^{2}d_{j}^{2}+2\sigma_{0}\bar{\sigma}^{2}d_{j}.

Finally, by factorization, we have

(\sigma_{0}^{2}-\bar{\sigma}^{2})d_{j}^{2}+2\sigma_{0}(\sigma_{0}^{2}-\bar{\sigma}^{2})d_{j}+\sigma_{0}^{4}>0,

which is clearly always positive given the assumption
\bar{\sigma}^{2}<\sigma_{0}^{2}
.

Toy regression.

We model the target distribution via a Gaussian, where mean and standard deviations are modelled via independent MLPs. We use a standard Gaussian over all model parameters. Since only the parameters of the mean model directly contribute to the epistemic uncertainty, we do DAP calibration only on them, that is we define a DAP that increases the prior variance with a distance only for the components of
\theta

\gamma=10q_{95}

Classification experiments.

We employed a two-layer MLP with 30 hidden layers and tanh activations for the MNIST experiments. We trained the model for 50 epochs with SGD with momentum of 0.9, a learning rate of

The network was trained for 200 epochs without early stopping and batch size of 128 decaying the learning rate by a factor of 0.2 every 60 epochs. For ODIN and DDU we used MAP as the pretrained feature extractor. In DAP, we also used the MAP model to transform the inputs before feeding them to the distance function in order to ease the comparison with the other methods. For ODIN we chose the temperature parameter
