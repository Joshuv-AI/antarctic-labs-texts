Enumeration of partitions modulo 4
Aditya Khanna

Abstract
. The number of standard Young tableaux possible of shape corresponding to a partition λ \lambda is called the dimension of the partition and is denoted by f λ f^{\lambda} . Partitions with odd dimensions were enumerated by McKay and were further classified by Macdonald. Let a i ​ ( n ) a_{i}(n) be the number of partitions of n n with dimension congruent to i i modulo 4. In this paper, we refine Macdonald’s and McKay’s results by calculating a 1 ​ ( n ) a_{1}(n) and a 3 ​ ( n ) a_{3}(n) when n n has no consecutive 1s in its binary expansion or when the sum of binary digits of n n is 2 and giving a recursive formula to compute a 2 ​ ( n ) a_{2}(n) for all n n .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Foreword

A partition of
n
is a tuple of natural numbers,
\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
, with non-increasing entries that add up to
n
. The
dimension of the partition
\lambda
, denoted by
f^{\lambda}
, is the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
(ref.
Section

2.1
) and can be calculated by the famous hook-length formula (
Remark

21
). Denote by
m_{p}(n)
, the number of partitions whose dimensions are not divisible by a given natural number,
p
.

Macdonald, in his landmark paper
[
Mac71
]
, gave a complete answer for
m_{p}(n)
which can be understood quite elegantly using the
p
-core tower approach (ref.
Section

6
). The case for
p=2
has a wonderful form. For
n=2^{k_{1}}+\ldots+2^{k_{\ell}}
with
k_{1}>\ldots>k_{\ell}
, we have,

m_{2}(n)=2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}},

which is entirely dependent on the binary expansion of
n
.

Note that
m_{2}(n)
enumerates partitions of
n
which have an odd dimension. We call these
odd partitions
and will denote their count by
a(n):=m_{2}(n)
.

Recently, there has been some interest in extending Macdonald’s results. In their unpublished notes, Amrutha P and T. Geetha
[
AG22
]
tackle the problem of computing
m_{2^{k}}(n)
. They provide general recursive results and find
m_{4}(2^{\ell})
and
m_{8}(2^{\ell})
. As we shall see, the enumeration results for
m_{4}(n)
follow from our analysis of “sparse numbers”, i.e., numbers with no consecutive 1s in their binary expansions.

This study of partitions has a representation theoretic context as partitions of
n
index the irreducible representations of the symmetric group,
S_{n}
. There are results regarding the behaviour of irreducible representations corresponding to odd partitions under restriction (
[
Gia+16
]
,
[
APS16
]
). There are results (
[
Pel20
]
,
[
GPS20
]
) which show that the density of odd character values (not just degrees) goes to zero. Although, they give an idea about the asymptotic behaviour of odd partitions, they do not explore the modular properties further. Some papers (
[
OZ21
]
,
[
Las08
]
) give explicit results for character values of symmetric groups but the formulae do not aid us in enumeration.

We aim to fill this gap by providing explicit enumeration results which extend the odd dimensional partition enumeration formula of Macdonald. We present our results now and data for our quantity of interest,
\delta
, can be found
here
.

1.2.
Main Results

Let
a_{i}(n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
with dimension congruent to
i
modulo 4. Then the number of odd partitions,
a(n)=m_{2}(n)=a_{1}(n)+a_{3}(n)
. We compute the values of
a_{1}(n)
and
a_{3}(n)
for some specific values of
n
. Define
\delta(n):=a_{1}(n)-a_{3}(n)
. In this paper, we present the following main theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
n,m,R\in\mathbb{N}
with
R\geq 2
and
m>0
. Suppose,
n=2^{R}+m
with
2^{R-1}>m
. Then we have

\delta(n)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n\text{ is even}\\
4\delta(m),&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Equivalently, for
k_{1}>\ldots>k_{\ell}
such that
k_{1}>k_{2}+1
, we have

a_{1}(2^{k_{1}}+\ldots+2^{k_{\ell}})=\begin{cases}2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1},&\text{if }k_{\ell}>0\text{}\\
4a_{1}(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{l}2^{k_{i}})+(2^{k_{1}-1}-2)2^{k_{2}+\ldots+k_{\ell}},&\text{if }k_{\ell}=0\text{},\end{cases}

and

a_{3}(2^{k_{1}}+\ldots+2^{k_{\ell}})=\begin{cases}2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1},&\text{if }k_{\ell}>0\text{}\\
4a_{3}(\sum\limits_{i=2}^{l}2^{k_{i}})+(2^{k_{1}-1}-2)2^{k_{2}+\ldots+k_{\ell}},&\text{if }k_{\ell}=0\text{}\end{cases}

We call a positive integer
sparse
if it does not have any consecutive ones in its binary expansion, i.e.,
k_{i}>k_{i+1}+1
in our notation. For instance,
(42)_{10}=(101010)_{2}
is sparse. This case has the following pleasing corollary:

Corollary 2
.

If
n
is a sparse number, then

\delta(n)=\begin{cases}2\text{,}&\text{if }n=2\\
0,&\text{if }n>2\text{ is even}\\
4^{\nu(n)-1},&\text{if }n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

where
\nu(n)
denotes the number of 1s (or equivalently the sum of digits) in the binary expansion of
n
. Let
\ell\geq 2
, then for
k_{1}>\ldots>k_{\ell}\geq 0
with
k_{i}>k_{i+1}+1
for all
i
, we have

a_{1}(2^{k_{1}}+\ldots+2^{k_{\ell}})=\begin{cases}2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1},&\text{if }k_{\ell}>0\\
2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1}+4^{\ell-2},&\text{if }k_{\ell}=0\text{},\end{cases}

and

a_{3}(2^{k_{1}}+\ldots+2^{k_{\ell}})=\begin{cases}2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1},&\text{if }k_{\ell}>0\\
2^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}-1}-4^{\ell-2},&\text{if }k_{\ell}=0\text{},\end{cases}

Note that we recover explicit and recursion formulae for
a_{1}(n)
and
a_{3}(n)
by using
\delta(n)=a_{1}(n)-a_{3}(n)
and
a(n)=a_{1}(n)+a_{3}(n)
.

In the case where the binary expansion is
\texttt{1100}\ldots
, we have the following result:

Theorem 3
.

Let
n=2^{R}+2^{R-1}
with
R\geq 1
, then

\delta(n)=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }n=3\\
8,&\text{if }n=6\\
0,&else.\end{cases}

Explicitly, we have

a_{1}(2^{R}+2^{R-1})=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }R=1\\
8,&\text{if }R=2\\
4^{R-1},&else,\end{cases}

and

a_{3}(2^{R}+2^{R-1})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }R=1\\
0,&\text{if }R=2\\
4^{R-1},&else.\end{cases}

For
a_{2}(n)
, which counts even dimensional partitions with
f^{\lambda}
not divisible by 4, we have recursive results for all natural numbers and closed form results for sparse
n
:

Theorem 4
.

Let
n=2^{R}+m
such that
m<2^{R}
. Then we have

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\binom{2^{R-1}}{2}\cdot a(m),&\text{ if }m<2^{R-1}\\
2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\left(\binom{2^{R-1}}{3}+2^{R-1}\right)\cdot\displaystyle{\frac{a(m)}{2^{R-1}}},&\text{ if }2^{R-1}\leq m<2^{R}.\end{cases}

Corollary 5
.

When
n
is sparse, we have

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{a(n)}{8}(n-2\nu(n))},&\text{if }n\text{ is even}\\
a_{2}(n-1),&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

2.4.

For any finite

2.6.
Od function

Notation 27
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
v_{2}(n)
denote the largest power of 2 that divides

.

Definition 28
.

Let
\Od:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
be defined as follows

\Od(n)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }n/2^{v_{2}(n)}\equiv 1\mod 4\\
-1,&\text{if }n/2^{v_{2}(n)}\equiv 3\mod 4.\end{cases}

The
\Od

n
and outputs it modulo 4.

Lemma 29
.

For all
m,n\geq 1
, we have
\Od(mn)=\Od(m)\Od(n)
.

Proof.

The largest odd number dividing
mn
is the product of the largest odd numbers dividing
m
and
n
respectively. Taking modulo 4 over multiplication gives the result.
∎

It also follows that if
k
divides
n
, then
\Od(n/k)=\Od(n)\Od(k)
.

Notation 30

(Binary expansion)
.

Let
n\in\N
.

(1)

Let
n=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}b_{i}2^{i}
such that
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. We write
n=b_{k}\ldots b_{0}
if
b_{k}=1
and
b_{i}=0
for

. This is the

n
.

(2)

Let
\bin(n):=\{i\mid b_{i}=1\}
, i.e., the “positions” of 1s in the binary expansion of
n
.

(3)

Denote the sum of digits by
\nu(n):=\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}b_{i}
.

(4)

Let

, i.e., the sum of the leftmost two digits of

.

Remark 31
.

Let
n
be as above. Denote

. Then,
\Od(n)\equiv b_{j+1}b_{j}\mod 4
. This corresponds to taking the binary expansion of

3.
The Workhorse Formula

In this section, we construct a relationship between partitions and their
2^{R}
-parents, which will allow us to analyse their dimensional behaviour modulo 4. We first define a statistic of consequence inspired by
[
DW89
]
:

Definition 32
.

For a natural number
n
(with binary expansion
b_{k}\ldots b_{0}
), let the number of pairs of consecutive 1s (disjoint or overlapping) be denoted by
D(n)
. Notationally,
D(n)
is the number of
i
such that the product
b_{i}\cdot b_{i+1}=1
.

Example 33
.

We have
D(7)=2
,
D(42)=0
and
D(367)=4
.

We start with the following lemma:

Lemma 34
.

For any natural number
n
, we have

\Od(n!)=(-1)^{D(n)+\nu(\lfloor n/4\rfloor)},

where
\nu(n)
is the number of 1s in the binary expansion of
n
as in
30
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the integral part.

Proof.

By
29
, we have

\Od(n!)=\prod\limits_{r=1}^{n}\Od(r)=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\Od(r)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\Od(r).

The above rearrangement allows us to use the following two facts to obtain a recursion. Firstly,
\Od(2r)=\Od(r)
which gives

\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\Od(r)=\Od(\lfloor n/2\rfloor!).

We have

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\Od(r)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\in 4\mathbb{N}+1\end{subarray}}\Od(r)\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\in 4\mathbb{N}-1\end{subarray}}\Od(r)

\displaystyle=1\cdot\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r\leq n\\
r\in 4\mathbb{N}-1\end{subarray}}(-1).

As there are exactly
\lfloor(n+1)/4\rfloor
terms of the arithmetic progression
3,7,11,\ldots
less than or equal to
n
, we get

\Od(n!)=(-1)^{\lfloor(n+1)/4\rfloor}\Od(\lfloor n/2\rfloor!).

Let
n
have the binary expansion
b_{k}\ldots b_{0}
as in
30
. By using
\lfloor\frac{b_{\ell}\ldots b_{0}}{2}\rfloor=b_{\ell}\ldots b_{1}
and iteratively simplifying
\Od(\bullet)
,we can rewrite our expression as

\Od(n!)=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{0}+1}{4}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{1}+1}{4}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{2}+1}{4}\rfloor+\ldots+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}b_{k-1}+1}{4}\rfloor}\Od(b_{k}!).

Notice that
\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{j+1}b_{j}+1}{4}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{j+1}b_{j}}{4}\rfloor+1
if and only if both
b_{j}
and
b_{j+1}
are 1. For all other cases, we have the equality,
\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{j+1}b_{j}+1}{4}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{j+1}b_{j}}{4}\rfloor
.

The inequality occurs exactly
D(n)
times, giving us

\Od(n!)=(-1)^{D(n)}(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{0}}{4}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}\ldots b_{1}}{4}\rfloor+\ldots+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}b_{k-1}b_{k-2}}{4}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{b_{k}b_{k-1}}{4}\rfloor}\Od(b_{k}!).

We have
\lfloor\frac{b_{\ell}\ldots b_{0}}{4}\rfloor=b_{\ell}\ldots b_{2}
. Also, as
b_{k}\in\{0,1\}
,
\Od(b_{k}!)=1
. Thus, we get

\Od(n!)=(-1)^{D(n)}(-1)^{{b_{k}\ldots b_{2}}+{b_{k}\ldots b_{3}}+\ldots+b_{k}+0}.

When
(-1)
is raised to a number, the parity of the number entirely decides the result, thus
(-1)^{b_{k}\ldots b_{j}}=(-1)^{b_{j}}
. Using this, we obtain,

\displaystyle\Od(n!)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{D(n)+b_{2}+b_{3}+\ldots+b_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{D(n)+\nu(\lfloor n/4\rfloor)}.

∎

Before stating the main formula, we define an important quantity:

Definition 35
.

Let
\lambda
be a
2^{R}
-parent of
\mu

be the affected hook-length (

Now, we have the arsenal to approach the hook-length formula and apply our
\Od

Proposition 36

(Workhorse formula)
.

Let

with

. Let
\mu\vdash m
and
\lambda

2^{R}
-parent of
\mu

\Od(f^{\lambda})=(-1)^{s_{2}(n)+s_{2}(h^{\lambda}_{\mu})+\eta^{\lambda}_{\mu}}\Od(f^{\mu}),

where
\eta^{\lambda}_{\mu}
is as defined above and

is as in

Proof.

By applying
\Od
on

\lambda

\Od(f^{\lambda})=(-1)^{D(n)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor)}\prod\limits_{h\in H(\lambda)}(-1)^{D(h)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{h}{4}\rfloor)}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x>y\\
x,y\in H(\lambda)\end{subarray}}\Od(|x-y|).

Suppose,
|H(\mu)^{+r}|=|H(\lambda)|
for some

. Then, we obtain,

\Od(f^{\mu})=(-1)^{D(m)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor)}\prod\limits_{h\in H(\mu)^{+r}}(-1)^{D(h)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{h}{4}\rfloor)}\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x>y\\
x,y\in H(\mu)^{+r}\end{subarray}}\Od(|x-y|).

Let
n

b_{R}\ldots b_{0}
. Then,

if and only if

\nu(\lfloor m/4\rfloor)=\nu(\lfloor n/4\rfloor)-1
. These two facts, give us

(-1)^{D(n)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor)-D(m)-\nu(\lfloor\frac{m}{4}\rfloor)}=(-1)^{1+b_{R-1}}=(-1)^{s_{2}(n)}.

We have that
h^{\lambda}_{\mu}\in H(\lambda)\backslash H(\mu)^{+r}
whereas
h^{\lambda}_{\mu}-2^{R}\in H(\mu)^{+r}\backslash H(\lambda)
. All the other elements of the sets

and

are same. Carrying out a similar computation as above, we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{\prod\limits_{h\in H(\lambda)}(-1)^{D(h)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{h}{4}\rfloor)}}{\prod\limits_{h\in H(\mu)^{+r}}(-1)^{D(h)+\nu(\lfloor\frac{h}{4}\rfloor)}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{D(h^{\lambda}_{\mu})+\nu(\lfloor\frac{h^{\lambda}_{\mu}}{4}\rfloor)-{D(h^{\lambda}_{\mu}-2^{R})-\nu(\lfloor\frac{h^{\lambda}_{\mu}-2^{R}}{4}\rfloor)}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s_{2}(h^{\lambda}_{\mu})}.

Extending this argument to the remaining product and using

∎

Lemma 37

For any natural number

(1)

Let

Proposition 41

2^{R}
-parent of

We consider the ratio

(3)

Definition 44

Example 45

The number

Proposition 53

. Thus, there is an injective map

given by

. Further, if

and

, then

and
r_{h}
have the same parity. We now break the sum into two parts depending on whether

belongs to
H(\lambda_{[r]})
, equivalently, whether
\mathbb{I}_{H(\lambda_{[r_{h}]})}(2^{R-1})=1
.

\displaystyle S_{\p_{2}^{\uparrow}(\mu)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|H(\mu)|-1}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{h}\\
h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}+\mathbb{I}_{H(\lambda_{[r_{h}]})}(2^{R-1})}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{other }r\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r+\mathbb{I}_{H(\lambda_{[r]})}(2^{R-1})}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|H(\mu)|-1}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}+1}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{other }r\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\right)

We add and subtract
\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}}
which gives us nice summations.

\displaystyle S_{\p_{2}^{\uparrow}(\mu)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|H(\mu)|-1}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}+1}-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{other }r\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|H(\mu)|-1}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}\left((-1)^{r_{h}+1}-(-1)^{r_{h}}\right)+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{2^{R-1}}(-1)^{r}\right)

The sum
\sum\limits_{r=1}^{2^{R-1}}(-1)^{r}
undergoes full cancellation and gives us zero.

\displaystyle S_{\p_{2}^{\uparrow}(\mu)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{|H(\mu)|-1}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}\left((-1)^{r_{h}+1}\right)+0\right)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{|H(\mu)|}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r_{h}}\right)

As
h
and
r_{h}
have the same parity,

\displaystyle S_{\p_{2}^{\uparrow}(\mu)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{|H(\mu)|}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H(\mu)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{h}\right)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{|H(\mu)|}\g(H(\mu)).

∎

Lemma 61
.

Let
\lambda
be an odd partition of

and
X
be its
\beta
-set. Then, we have

\g(X)=\begin{cases}1-(-1)^{n},&\text{if }|X|\text{ is even}\\
(-1)^{n},&\text{if }|X|\text{ is odd}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall that if
\lambda\in\p(\mu)
, then there exists an

such that

for

Using the fact that

and

have the same parity, we can deduce that
\g(H(\lambda))=\g(H(\mu)^{+s})
.
As

, one can show that all odd entries become even and all even entries odd with an added even entry

\g(X^{+1})=1-\g(X)

\g(X^{+s})=\begin{cases}\g(X),&\text{if }s\text{ is even}\\
1-\g(X),&\text{if }s\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

We can now work our way down hook-by-hook (taking successive

-cores) and calculate

by finding
\g(H(\alpha))
where
\alpha=\varnothing
when

is even and

when

is odd. Applying the above relation repeatedly, we get,

\g(H(\lambda))=\begin{cases}\g(H(\alpha)),&\text{if }|H(\lambda)|-|H(\alpha)|\text{ is even}\\
1-\g(H(\alpha)),&\text{if }|H(\lambda)|-|H(\alpha)|\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

Intuitively, the quantity

counts the total number of

Trivially,
\g(H(\varnothing))=0
. If

\g(H(\lambda))=\begin{cases}0&|H(\lambda)|\text{ is even}\\
1&|H(\lambda)|\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

On the other hand, we have
\g(\{1\})=-1
and if

\g(H(\lambda))=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }|H(\lambda)|\text{ is even.}\\
-1,&\text{if }|H(\lambda)|\text{ is odd}\par\end{cases}

The claim follows immediately from this computation.

We wish to extend the results of the previous section beyond the sparse case. Although, a general formula remains elusive; we can take confident steps towards it. We consider all numbers whose binary expansions are of the form

. In notation, we consider

with

We use similar methods as we did in the previous section. Recall that for

We also state a corollary of

Corollary 64

Let

for some

Lemma 65
.

With the above notation, we have

S_{\p_{1}(\mu)}(\mu)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }b\text{ is even}\\
2,&\text{if }b\text{ is odd}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

As in the proof of

, define the bijection

Firstly, consider

in this case is equal to 0, which gives us

On the other hand, for

. In this case,

\displaystyle S_{\p_{1}(\mu)}({\mu})

Notice that as

, the parity of

Now, we move on to the results for Type II
2^{R}

Lemma 66
.

With the above notation, we have

As before, let

We now consider partitions whose dimensions are congruent to 2 modulo 4, i.e.,

To tackle the problem of enumeration, we use the machinery of

Recall that

(

-cores. It is well-known (refer Proposition 3.7 of

Notation 67
.

We simplify the notation by writing

We recall the construction of 2-core towers as presented in

For

•

Label the root node with
\core_{2}(\lambda)=\core_{2}(\lambda^{(\varnothing)})
.

•

If the length of the unique path from the root node to a node

is

is in the

i^{\mathrm{th}}

•

Every node in the

i^{\mathrm{th}}

(i+1)^{\text{st}}
row. Define a recursive labelling as follows: if the label of some node,

, in the

i^{\mathrm{th}}

\core_{2}(\lambda^{(b)})
for some binary string

, then the two nodes in the

and

respectively.

This tree is known as the 2-core tower of

We state a well-known classification result for 2-cores.

Lemma 69
.

A partition
\lambda
is a 2-core if and only if
\lambda=(n,n-1,\ldots 1)
for some
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a 2-core. If
x\in H(\lambda)
, then
x-2\in H(\lambda)
otherwise there will be a 2-hook. If
x
is even, then
x,x-2,\ldots,2\in H(\lambda)
. As
2\in H(\lambda)
, there is a 2-hook, so
H(\lambda)
cannot contain any even entries. If
x
is odd, then it must contain all consecutive odd numbers from 1 to
x
, which corresponds to the fact that
\lambda=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)
where
n=\frac{x+1}{2}
. The converse is straightforward to check.
∎

Notation 70
.

Let
\T_{k}(w)
denote the number of solutions
\left(\mu_{[1]},\ldots,\mu_{[2^{k}]}\right)
to

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2^{k}}|\mu_{[i]}|=w,

where each
\mu_{[i]}
is a 2-core.

Lemma 71
.

With the above notation, we have,

\T_{k}(w)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }w=0\\
2^{k},&\text{if }w=1\\
\binom{2^{k}}{2},&\text{if }w=2\\
\binom{2^{k}}{3}+2^{k},&\text{if }w=3.\end{cases}

Proof.

When
w=0
, the only solution is
\mu_{[i]}=\varnothing
for all
i
. Thus,
\T_{k}(0)=1
.

When
w=1
, we have
2^{k}
possible options for
\mu_{[i]}=(1)
as
1\leq i\leq 2^{k}
which gives
\T_{k}(1)=2^{k}
.

For
w=2
, we must choose
\mu_{[i]}=\mu_{[j]}=(1)
, for some
i
and
j
, and the rest
\varnothing
. Thus,
\T_{k}(2)=\binom{2^{k}}{2}
.

There does exist a 2-core of size 3, which is
(2,1)
. If
w=3
, we have two options, either we choose
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}
such that
\mu_{[i_{1}]}=\mu_{[i_{2}]}=\mu_{[i_{3}]}=(1)
or
j
such that
\mu_{[j]}=(2,1)
. For the former, we get
\binom{2^{k}}{3}
ways and the latter can be done in
2^{k}
ways, thus giving
\T_{k}(3)=\binom{2^{k}}{3}+2^{k}
.
∎

Definition 72

(Weight in a 2-core tower)
.

The
weight of the

\lambda

is given by

w_{k}(\lambda):=\sum\limits_{b\in\{0,1\}^{k}}|\core_{2}(\lambda^{(b)})|,

where we define
w_{0}(\lambda)=|\core_{2}(\lambda)|
.

We state some important properties of 2-core towers and how they relate to dimensions. For the subsequent discussion, we will let
n=\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}b_{i}2^{i}
, where
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Further, recall that
\bin(n)=\{i\mid b_{i}=1\}
.

Notation 73
.

Let
\bin^{\prime}(n)=\{i>0\mid b_{i}=1\}
.

Note the strict inequality in the definition. Furthermore, we have the relation,
\bin^{\prime}(n)=\bin(n)\backslash\{0\}
.

Proposition 74
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition of
n
. We write
w_{i}:=w_{i}(\lambda)
. In this case, the following hold:

(1)

\lambda
is an odd partition if and only
w_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
.

(2)

v_{2}(f^{\lambda})=1
if and only if for some
R\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
, we have
w_{R-1}=b_{R-1}+2
,
w_{R}=0
and
w_{i}=b_{i}
otherwise.

Proof.

The proof of (1) can be found in section 4 of
[
Mac71
]
. Also, through section 4 of
[
Mac71
]
, we know that there exists a sequence of non-negative integers
(z_{i})_{i\geq 0}
with
z_{0}=0
such that

w_{i}+z_{i}=b_{i}+2z_{i+1}.

From Section 3 of
[
Mac71
]
, we know that if
v_{2}(f^{\lambda})=1
, then we have
\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}w_{i}=\sum\limits_{i\geq 0}b_{i}+1
. Combining these, we get that we must have
z_{i}=1
for exactly one
i>0
and zero for the rest. Putting
z_{k}=1
gives us the equations,

\displaystyle w_{k-1}

\displaystyle=b_{k-1}+2

\displaystyle w_{k}+1

\displaystyle=b_{k}.

Notice that the second equation above tells us that
z_{k}=1
is possible only when
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
. Thus,
w_{k}=0
and
w_{k-1}=b_{k-1}+2
. The rest is clear as
z_{i}=z_{i+1}=0
.
∎

Notation 75
.

Fix an
n
with binary expansion
b_{i}
. For
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
, define a sequence of non-negative integers,
\mathbf{w}_{k}(n):=(w^{k}_{i}(n))_{i\geq 0}
with the following properties:

(1)

w^{k}_{k-1}(n)=b_{k-1}+2
,

(2)

w^{k}_{k}(n)=0
, and

(3)

w^{k}_{i}(n)=b_{i}
for all other values of
i
.

Let
\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n)):=\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))
.

Note that the above product is the number of ways of constructing 2-core towers corresponding to partitions
\lambda
such that
w_{i}(\lambda)=w^{k}_{i}(n)
, for some
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
, and
v_{2}(f^{\lambda})=1
.

Proof of
4
.

Fix an
n
. By the above discussion (
74
), we have that the number of partitions with dimensions congruent to 2 modulo 4,
a_{2}(n)
, is given by

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n)).

Denote the binary expansion of
n
with
b_{i}
as before. If we suppose
n=2^{R}+m
with
m<2^{R}
, then
b_{R}=1
and
b_{i}=0
for
i>R
. Note that
\bin^{\prime}(n)=\bin^{\prime}(m)\backslash\{R\}
. So, except for
k=R
, we have
\mathbf{w}_{k}(n)=\mathbf{w}_{k}(m)
. Now, we can break the sum up as,

a_{2}(n)=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))\right)+\T(\mathbf{w}_{R}(n)).

For the term on the left of the plus sign, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)\backslash\{R\}}\T_{R}(w^{k}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=2^{R}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(n))\qquad{\color[rgb]{1,0.47,0.62}\text{(As }w_{R}^{k}(n)=b_{R}=1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{R}}{\T_{R}(0)}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\T_{R}(0)\prod\limits_{i\neq R}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(m))\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0.47,0.62}\text{(As }\mathbf{w}_{k}(n)=\mathbf{w}_{k}(m))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{R}}{\T_{R}(0)}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(m)}\prod\limits_{i\geq 0}\T_{i}(w^{k}_{i}(m))

\displaystyle=2^{R}a_{2}(m).

Now, we deal with the term on the right of the plus sign. We see that

\displaystyle\T(\mathbf{w}_{R}(n))

\displaystyle=\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R,R-1}\T_{i}(w^{R}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))\prod\limits_{i\neq R,R-1}\T_{i}(b_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\T_{R-1}(w^{R}_{R-1}(n))\T_{R}(w^{R}_{R}(n))}{\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1})\T_{R}(b_{R})}a(n).

\frac{\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1}+2)}{2^{R}\T_{R-1}(b_{R-1})}a(n).

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2^{R}\cdot a_{2}(m)+\binom{2^{R-1}}{2}\cdot a(m),&\text{ if }m<2^{R-1}\\

The first case corresponds to

and the second one corresponds to

In the sparse case, this theorem takes a nice form as evident in
5
. Although, we can prove the corollary using the recursive relations in
3
, we choose to do perform direct computations as it is more illuminating.

Proof of
5
.

Let
n
be a sparse number, i.e., it has no consecutive 1s in its binary expansion. The summation as before is:

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\T(\mathbf{w}_{k}(n))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\T_{k-1}(w^{k}_{k-1}(n))\T_{k}(w^{k}_{k}(n))}{\T_{k-1}(b_{k-1})\T_{k}(b_{k})}a(n).

As
n
is sparse, for
k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)
, we have
b_{k-1}=0
. Further,
w^{k}_{k}(n)=0
and
w^{k}_{k-1}(n)=b_{k-1}+2=2
.

This gives us the following summation,

a_{2}(n)=\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\T_{k-1}(2)}{\T_{k}(1)}a(n).

By using the results from
71
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle a_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=a(n)\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{\binom{2^{k-1}}{2}}{2^{k}}

\displaystyle=a(n)\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}\frac{(2^{k-1})(2^{k-1}-1)}{2\cdot 2^{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a(n)}{8}\sum\limits_{k\in\bin^{\prime}(n)}2^{k}-2.

If
n
is even, then this summation becomes
n-2\nu(n)
. If
n
is odd, the summation becomes
(n-1)+2(\nu(n)-1)=(n-1)+2(\nu(n-1))
. This gives us the final answer as,

a_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{a(n)}{8}(n-2\nu(n))},&\text{if }n\text{ is even}\\
a_{2}(n-1),&\text{if }n\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

∎

Closing Remarks and Open Problems

It is natural to ask why the case of
n=2^{R}+m
for
2^{R-1}<m<2^{R}
is so difficult that it evades our methods. As we saw before, the values of
S_{\p(\mu)}(\mu)
depend only on
|\mu|
(
Section

4
), thus, independent of the partition
\mu
chosen as our
2^{R}
-core. In the remaining case, we do not have this luxury. The values of
S_{\p(\mu)}(\mu)
do depend on the chosen
2^{R}
-core
\mu
. For instance, consider two partitions of 21,
\mu=(17,3,1)
and
\nu=(12,3,3,2,1)
. Considering these as
32
-cores, one can check that
S_{\p(\mu)}(\mu)=8
and
S_{\p(\nu)}(\nu)=0
.

The difference between the case we tackle in the paper and the unsolved one differs in a few ways. Most importantly, in
60
, when
2^{R-1}<m<2^{R}
the set
D_{[1,2^{R-1}]}
is a strict subset of
[1,2^{R-1}]
. The injective map defined in the lemma is no longer valid and calculation now depends on the elements of
H(\mu)
. One runs into a similar problem in
63
and also has to consider whether
3\cdot 2^{R-1}\in H(\mu)
. Even the computation in
53
starts depending on the entries of
H(\mu)
.

In the
2^{R-1}<m<2^{R}
case, one can see that for
\mu\vdash m
it might be necessary to consider the further
2^{R-1}
core. So, one may have to look at Type I/II parents of Type I/II parents thus greatly increasing the cases.

Furthermore, we saw that
\delta(n)
was a power of 4 for sparse numbers. If one looks through the initial run of the
data
, one may form the hypothesis that every non-zero
\delta(n)
is a power of 2, which soon is falsified as
\delta(118)=-384
which is not a power of 2 and not even positive. Thus, for non-sparse numbers, the values of
\delta(n)
begin to deviate from “nice” values. The following run from 122 to 127 shows how egregious this deviation can get.

Along with this roadblock, we present some relevant problems that remain unsolved.

(1)

What is
\delta(n)
in the case when
n=2^{R}+m
for
2^{R-1}<m<2^{R}
?

(2)

Is it possible to provide a reasonable bound for
