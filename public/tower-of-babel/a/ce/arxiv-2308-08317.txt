Sufficientness postulates for measure-valued Pólya urn sequences
Hristo Sariev, Mladen Savov

Abstract
In a recent paper, the authors studied the distribution properties of a class of exchangeable processes, called measure-valued Pólya sequences (MVPS), which arise as the observation process in a generalized urn sampling scheme. Here we present several results in the form of “sufficientness” postulates that characterize their predictive distributions. In particular, we show that exchangeable MVPSs are the unique exchangeable models whose predictive distributions are a mixture of the marginal distribution and the average of a probability kernel evaluated at past observation. When the latter coincides with the empirical measure, we recover a well-known result for the exchangeable model with a Dirichlet process prior. In addition, we provide a “pure” sufficientness postulate for exchangeable MVPSs that does not assume a particular analytic form for the predictive distributions. Two other sufficientness postulates consider the case when the state space is finite.



Keywords:

Pólya sequences, Urn models, Predictive distributions, Bayesian nonparametrics, Exchangeability, Sufficientness postulate

Introduction

The exchangeable model with a Dirichlet process prior is a cornerstone of Bayesian nonparametric analysis and has inspired much of the subsequent work in the area (for an overview, see
[
22
]
). One remarkable feature of the Dirichlet process, due to
[
26
]
and later
[
23
]
, is that it is the unique prior for which the predictive distributions of the underlying exchangeable process are a mixture of the marginal distribution and the empirical measure. More specifically, let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of random variables taking values in some standard space, say
\mathbb{X}=[0,1]
, and suppose that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is exchangeable, i.e. the order of
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
is probabilistically irrelevant (refer to Section
2
for a rigorous treatment). Denote by
\nu(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot\,)
the marginal distribution of the process. If for each
n=1,2,\ldots
the predictive distribution of
X_{n+1}
given
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
is the convex combination

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=(1-a_{n})\nu(\cdot)+a_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(\cdot),

(1.1)

for some constant
a_{n}\in(0,1)
, where
\delta_{x}
is the unit mass at
x
, then Theorem 1 in
[
23
]
implies that
a_{n}=n/(n+\theta)
, with
\theta=a_{1}^{-1}-1
, and
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has a Dirichlet process prior with parameters
(\theta,\nu)
. The well-known Blackwell-MacQueen urn model
[
7
]
assumes (
1.1
) directly with
a_{n}=n/(n+\theta)
.

Predictive constructions of the type (
1.1
) have several attractive features. For one, they provide a simple and efficient way to simulate the sequence
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
. Indeed, (
1.1
) leads to a recursive procedure

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\theta+n-1}{\theta+n}\,\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n-1})+\frac{1}{\theta+n}\,\delta_{X_{i}}(\cdot),

which allows for fast computations and limited storage of information for streaming data (see
[
11
, Section 5]
for more details on recursive algorithms and predictions). On the other hand, the system of predictive distributions
(\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}))_{n\geq 0}
determines the law of the process
\mathbb{P}((X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)\in\cdot\,)
without the need for prior elicitation. Such a predictive approach to Bayesian modeling, focused on observable quantities instead of unknown parameters, is deeply rooted in the philosophical foundations of Bayesian analysis
[
10
,
11
]
, and has recently enjoyed renewed interest, see e.g.
[
6
,
9
,
11
]
.

A particular class of predictive characterizations, to which (
1.1
) belongs (see below), are the so-called postulates of “sufficientness”, dating back to the writings of Cambridge philosopher W. E. Johnson
[
19
]
(for more historical details, see
[
29
]
and
[
14
, Chapter 5]
). Unlike the usual notions of sufficiency, see e.g.
[
12
, Section 5]
, sufficientness postulates provide conditions on the functional form of the predictive distributions arising from natural judgments on the inductive process of learning. In other words, model specification is based on general assessments about the form and type of sample information used to make predictions. As an example, the original Johnson’s sufficientness postulate assumes an exchangeable multinomial model on
\mathbb{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
with
k
types such that, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
, the probability of observing type
j\in\mathbb{X}
on the
(n+1)
th trial

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=j|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=f_{n,j}(N_{n,j})

(1.2)

depends on the sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
only by the number of times
N_{n,j}
that
j
has been observed in the past. By Theorem 2.1 in
[
29
]
, any exchangeable process with predictive distributions (
1.2
) has a Dirichlet distribution prior (see also Proposition
3.1
in Section
3
). Similar considerations about the relevance of different pieces of sample information have led to sufficientness postulates for the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet process
[
31
]
, Gibbs-type priors
[
2
]
and feature-sampling models
[
8
]
. Sufficientness postulates have also been proposed for neutral to the right priors
[
28
]
and recurrent Markov exchangeable sequences
[
30
]
.

Within this paradigm, the predictive rule (
1.1
) can be shown to be motivated by the same principle behind (
1.2
), extended to more general state spaces
\mathbb{X}
, without necessarily assuming that the number of observed types is limited or known in advance. To that end, let
K_{n}
and
X_{1}^{*},\ldots,X_{K_{n}}^{*}
denote, respectively, the number and the distinct values of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
in
order of appearance
. Following
[
31
]
, we assume that the probability of observing the
j
th value to appear

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=X_{j}^{*}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=p_{n}(N_{n,j})

(1.3)

is a function of the number of times it has been previously observed, while the probability of observing (discovering) a new type depends only on the sample size,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\notin\{X_{1}^{*},\ldots,X_{K_{n}}^{*}\}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=r_{n}.

(1.4)

Note that unlike (
1.2
), the function
p_{n}
does not change depending on
j
, which is a consequence of the framework, since type
j
is not known to exist in advance
[
31
, p.381, Footnote 5]
. Moreover, the original proposal by
[
31
]
allows
r_{n}=r_{n}(K_{n})
to depend further on the number of distinct values, leading to a general characterization result for the two-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet process. On the other hand, the model (
1.3
)-(
1.4
) describes an (exchangeable) random partition process (in fact, the Chinese restaurant process and the related Ewens’ partition structure
[
31
, p.380, Remarks]
), but does not discuss the mechanism of ‘labeling’
X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},\ldots
. Assuming that their values are assigned independently, from a
diffuse
distribution
\nu
, i.e.
\nu(\{x\})=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
, we can express (
1.3
)-(
1.4
) jointly as

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n}(N_{n,j})\delta_{X_{j}^{*}}(\cdot)+r_{n}\nu(\cdot).

(1.5)

Exchangeable processes with predictive distributions of the kind (
1.5
) belong to the class of
species sampling sequences

[
16
,
25
]
and are closely related to the theory of exchangeable random partitions (for an excellent overview, see
[
15
, Chapter 14]
). It follows from (
1.5
) that, for any
k=1,\ldots,n
and all counts
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}=n
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{n}(n_{j})+r_{n}=1;

thus, assuming that every partition of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
by type has a positive probability of occurring, we can easily deduce that

p_{n}(n_{j})=(1-r_{n})\frac{n_{j}}{n}.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=(1-r_{n})\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{n}}N_{n,j}\delta_{X_{j}^{*}}(\cdot)+r_{n}\nu(\cdot)=(1-r_{n})\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(\cdot)+r_{n}\nu(\cdot),

suggesting that the analytic form in (
1.1
) arises from a “pure” sufficientness principle.

An important limitation of (
1.1
) and species sampling models, in general, is that they are designed to work with categorical data, taking into account only the frequencies of the observed types. In this work, we study an extension of (
1.1
) by replacing
\delta_{x}
with a general probability measure
R_{x}
, so that

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=(1-a_{n})\nu(\cdot)+a_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(\cdot),

(1.6)

for some constant
a_{n}\in(0,1)
. Depending on the particular
R
, the predictive rule (
1.6
) can more efficiently exploit sample information coming from continuous data by considering, for example, where each observation falls inside
\mathbb{X}
. In fact, for diffuse
R
and
\nu
, all
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
will be distinct, so notions like random partition and species discovery become practically meaningless. Our goal is to characterize the class of exchangeable processes that are governed by (
1.6
), and thus motivate their use in Bayesian nonparametric analysis from an inductive learning perspective.

Notice that when
a_{n}=n/(n+\theta)
for some
\theta>0
, equation (
1.6
) becomes

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\theta\nu(\cdot)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(\cdot)}{\theta+n},

and, following
[
27
]
, the process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a member of the class of
measure-valued Pólya sequences
(MVPS). We might expect from (
1.1
), where
R_{x}=\delta_{x}
, that the only exchangeable processes having predictive rules (
1.6
) will be exchangeable MVPSs, in which case
[
27
]
provides a complete description of their prior distributions. In particular, it would follow from Theorem 3.13 in
[
27
]
that when
\mathbb{X}
is countable or
R
is dominated by
\nu
, the exchangeable sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has a Dirichlet process mixture prior (see Section
2.2
). Our main result, Theorem
3.1
, states that this is indeed the case, but the proof is fundamentally different from that for (
1.1
), making extensive use of the measure-valued nature of (
1.6
) as the theory of random partitions is generally not available.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we introduce MVPSs and provide notation that will be used later on. Theorem
3.1
is stated in Section
3
along with a “pure” sufficientness postulate for exchangeable MVPSs that does not assume a particular analytic form for the predictive distributions, as does (
1.6
). In addition, we provide two other sufficientness postulates that generalize (
1.2
) for the case when
\mathbb{X}
is finite. Proofs are postponed to Section
4
.

The model

2.1
Preliminaries

From now on,
\mathbb{X}
is a complete separable metric space endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{X}
, which from standard results is countably generated. All random quantities are defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{H},\mathbb{P})
unless otherwise specified. For all unexplained measure-theoretic details, we refer to
[
20
]
.

Given two arbitrary measures
\mu,\pi
on
\mathbb{X}
, we say that
\mu
is
absolutely continuous
with respect to
\pi
, denoted
\mu\ll\pi
, if
\mu(A)=0
whenever
\pi(A)=0
for any
A\in\mathcal{X}
, and we say that
\mu
and
\pi
are
mutually singular
, denoted
\mu\perp\pi
, if there exists
S\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mu(S)=0=\pi(S^{c})
.

A

\mathbb{X}
is a function
R:\mathbb{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
(i)
the map
x\mapsto R(x,B)\equiv R_{x}(B)
is
\mathcal{X}
-measurable, for all
B\in\mathcal{X}
; and
(ii)

R_{x}
is a measure on
\mathbb{X}
. In addition,
R
is said to be
finite
if
R_{x}(\mathbb{X})<\infty
, and is called a
probability kernel
if
R_{x}(\mathbb{X})=1
, for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{X}
, and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{X}
. A probability kernel
R
on
\mathbb{X}
is said to be a
regular version of the conditional distribution (r.c.d.) for
\nu
given
\mathcal{G}
, which for emphasis we will denote by

R_{x}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})(x)\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-almost every (a.e.) }x,

if
(i)
the map
x\mapsto R_{x}(B)
is
\mathcal{G}
-measurable, for all
B\in\mathcal{X}
; and
(ii)

\int_{A}R_{x}(B)\nu(dx)=\nu(A\cap B)
, for all
A\in\mathcal{G}
and
B\in\mathcal{X}
. The assumptions on
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
guarantee that a r.c.d. for
\nu
given
\mathcal{G}
exists and is unique up to a

-null set, and all conditional distributions must be understood as regular versions. Moreover, a regular version
R
of

is called
a.e. proper
if there exists
F\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\nu(F)=1
and

R_{x}(A)=\delta_{x}(A)\qquad\mbox{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}\mbox{ and }x\in F.

Following
[
4

, an a.e. proper r.c.d. for
\nu
given
\mathcal{G}
exists if and only if
\mathcal{G}
is
countably generated (c.g.) under
\nu
, in the sense that there exists
C\in\mathcal{G}
such that

and
\mathcal{G}\cap C
is c.g.

An
\mathbb{X}
-valued sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of random variables is said to be

if, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
and all permutations

of
\{1,\ldots,n\}

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\overset{d}{=}(X_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma(n)}).

In that case, by de Finetti representation theorem for exchangeable sequences, see e.g.
[
1
, Section 3]
, there exists a random probability measure
\tilde{P}
on
\mathbb{X}
, called the
directing random measure
of the sequence, such that, given
\tilde{P}
, the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are conditionally independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to
\tilde{P}
,

\displaystyle X_{n}\mid\tilde{P}

\displaystyle\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}

\displaystyle\tilde{P}

\displaystyle\tilde{P}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle Q

where
Q
is the (nonparameteric)
prior
distribution of
\tilde{P}
. Moreover,
\tilde{P}
is the almost sure (a.s.) weak limit of the empirical measure

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{P}\qquad\mbox{a.s.},

and the predictive distributions of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{P}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

The most popular prior distribution in Bayesian nonparametric analysis is arguably the
Dirichlet process
,
\mbox{DP}(\theta,\nu)
, which is parameterized by a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{X}
and a constant
\theta>0
. We recall that
\tilde{P}\sim\mbox{DP}(\theta,\nu)
if, for every finite partition
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
\mathbb{X}
, the random vector
(\tilde{P}(B_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{P}(B_{n}))
has a Dirichlet distribution with parameters
(\theta\nu(B_{1}),\ldots,\theta\nu(B_{n}))
. Equivalently, see e.g.
[
15
, Theorem 4.12]
, the Dirichlet process is the distribution of an a.s. discrete random probability measure with so-called stick-breaking weights,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}V_{j}\delta_{Z_{j}}\sim\mbox{DP}(\theta\nu),

(2.1)

where
V_{j}=W_{j}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}W_{i}
, and
W_{1},W_{2},\ldots\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\textnormal{Beta}(1,\theta)
and
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\nu
are independent random variables.

Exchangeable processes having directing random measures
\tilde{P}
as in (
2.1
), for arbitrary sequences of non-negative random weights
(V_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and independent atoms
(Z_{j})_{j\geq 1}
coming from a diffuse distribution
\nu
, are called (proper)
species sampling sequences
and have predictive distributions of the form (
1.5
) for more general functions
p_{n}
and
r_{n}
, see
[
16
,
25
]
for a complete characterization. Because of the discrete nature of their
\tilde{P}
and the fact that the atoms
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
are a.s. all distinct, species sampling models are, by Kingman’s theory, in one-to-one correspondence with exchangeable random partitions, up to a specification of
\nu
(for more information, we refer to
[
25
]
and
[
15
, Chapter 14]
). In fact, the exchangeable partition generated by a random sample from a Dirichlet process (
2.1
) with diffuse
\nu
is the well-known Chinese restaurant process.

2.2
Exchangeable MVPS

Following
[
27
]
, we call any sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{X}
-valued random variables a
measure-valued Pólya (urn) sequence
with parameters
\theta,\nu
and
R
, denoted MVPS
(\theta,\nu,R)
, if
\nu(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot\,)
and, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\theta\nu(\cdot)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(\cdot)}{\theta+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(\mathbb{X})},

(2.2)

where
R
is a finite transition kernel on
\mathbb{X}
, called the
reinforcement kernel
of the process,
\nu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{X}
, known as the
base measure
, and
\theta>0
is a positive constant. When
R_{x}(\mathbb{X})=m
for
\nu
-a.e.
x
and some
m>0
, both
R
and the corresponding MVPS are said to be
balanced
.

The predictive structure (
2.2
) admits a physical interpretation in terms of a generalized urn sampling scheme as follows. Suppose initially that we have an urn whose contents are described by the measure
\theta\nu
in the sense that, for each
B\in\mathcal{X}
, the quantity
\theta\nu(B)
records the total
mass
of balls whose colors lie in
B
. At time
n=1
, a ball is drawn at random from the urn with probability distribution
\nu
. Depending on its color,
X_{1}
, we reinforce the urn with additional balls according to the measure
R_{X_{1}}
, so that the updated composition becomes
\theta\nu+R_{X_{1}}
. At each subsequent time
n>1
, a ball with color
X_{n}
is drawn with probability proportional to
\theta\nu+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}R_{X_{i}}
, and the contents of the urn get “reinforced” by
R_{X_{n}}
. The model with
|\mathbb{X}|=k
and
R_{x}=\delta_{x}
, i.e. we only add one additional ball of the observed color, corresponds to the classical
k
-color Pólya urn scheme and measure-valued Pólya urn processes have been proposed by
[
3
,
13
,
18
,
24
]
as extensions to infinite colors and more general reinforcement mechanisms. The case where the observation process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is exchangeable has been investigated by
[
27
]
, and the main results from that paper are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorems 3.2, 3.7 and 3.9 in
[
27
]
)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable, but not i.i.d. MVPS with parameters
(\theta,\nu,R)
and directing random measure
\tilde{P}
. Then

(a)

R
is balanced and there exists a (c.g. under
\nu
) sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{X}
such that, normalized,
R
is a r.c.d. for
\nu

\mathcal{G}
,

\frac{R_{x}(\cdot)}{R_{x}(\mathbb{X})}=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})(x)\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-a.e. }x;

(b)

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
converges a.s. in total variation, as

, to
\tilde{P}
;

(c)

\tilde{P}
is equal in law to

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}V_{j}\frac{R_{Z_{j}}(\cdot)}{R_{Z_{j}}(\mathbb{X})},

(2.3)

where
(V_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(Z_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are given in (
2.1
).

Independently from the theory on measure-valued urn processes, Berti et al.
[
5
]
introduced the
kernel based Dirichlet sequence
, which is a balanced MVPS with reinforcement kernel
R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})
, for some sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{X}
. In

provides a converse statement and shows that, under exchangeability,

need not be assumed to be balanced a priori. In fact, it follows from Theorem

that any exchangeable MVPS
(\theta,\nu,R)
can be reparameterized as a balanced exchangeable MVPS

for some probability kernel
\tilde{R}
and constant

, so that

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\tilde{\theta}+n}\nu(\cdot)+\frac{n}{\tilde{\theta}+n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{R}_{X_{i}}(\cdot).

(2.4)

Therefore, exchangeable MVPSs satisfy the sufficientness postulate (
1.6
), and in Section
3
we show that the opposite statement is also true. On the other hand, by Theorem
2.1
(c)
, the directing random measure
\tilde{P}
of any MVPS inherits its properties directly from the reinforcement kernel
\tilde{R}
, so that
\tilde{P}
is a.s. diffuse and/or absolutely continuous with respect to some reference probability measure whenever
\tilde{R}
is. This is in stark contrast to the discrete nature of the Dirichlet process (
2.1
) and species sampling models, in general, all while keeping the same simple form of the predictive distributions (
2.4
).

Theorem
2.1
can be refined when
\mathbb{X}

3
Results

Theorem
3.1
, together with the discussion around (
2.4
), states that the family of exchangeable MVPSs coincides with the class of exchangeable processes satisfying the sufficientness postulate (
1.6

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable process with marginal distribution
\nu
and predictive distributions (
1.6
), that is, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=(1-a_{n})\nu(\cdot)+a_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(\cdot),

(3.1)

where
R
is a probability kernel on
\mathbb{X}
, and
a_{n}\in(0,1)
. Then
a_{n}=n/(a_{1}^{-1}-1+n)
for all
n\geq 1
. Moreover,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with parameters
(a_{1}^{-1}-1,\nu,R)
.

Remark 3.1
.

Since
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in Theorem
3.1
is an exchangeable MVPS, then Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
provide additional guidance for the choice of the parameter
R
. In particular, there exists a (c.g. under
\nu)
sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{X}
such that
R
is a r.c.d. for
\nu
given
\mathcal{G}
,

R_{x}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})(x)\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-a.e. }x.

Therefore, in a Bayesian nonparametric setting, when we make the initial assessment that our exchangeable model is characterized by (
3.1
), instead of choosing a specific probability kernel
R
, we actually have to decide on a predefined body of knowledge in the form of the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
, making the model “essentially” parameterized by
\mathcal{G}
. If, in addition,
R
is dominated by
\nu
, the above amounts to a decision on a particular partition of
\mathbb{X}
(see Examples
3.1
and
3.2
).

Example 3.1

(Mixture model)
.

Let
\theta>0
and
\nu,P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
be probability measures on
\mathbb{X}
such that
P_{k}(D_{k})=1
,
k\geq 1
, where
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{X}
forms a countable partition of
\mathbb{X}
. For each
x\in\mathbb{X}
, we define

P_{(x)}(\cdot):=P_{k}(\cdot)\qquad\mbox{if and only if}\qquad x\in D_{k}.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable MVPS with predictive distributions

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\theta}{\theta+n}\nu(\cdot)+\frac{n}{\theta+n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{(X_{i})}(\cdot).

By Theorem 3.14 in
[
27
]
, the reinforcement kernel of any MVPS admits a Lebesgue decomposition with respect to a universal set, so in the present example, there exist sets
C,S\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\nu(C)=1
,

P_{(x)}\perp\nu\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{(x)}(S)=1,\qquad\mbox{for every }x\in C\cap S,

and

P_{(x)}\ll\nu\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{(x)}(S^{c})=1,\qquad\mbox{for every }x\in C\cap S^{c}.

Let
\{D_{j_{1}},D_{j_{2}},\ldots\}
be the smallest cover of
C\cap S^{c}
extracted from
\{D_{1},D_{2},\ldots\}
. Since
P_{(x)}\equiv P_{k}
when
x\in D_{k}
, then
P_{(x)}\ll\nu
and
P_{(x)}(\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}})=1
, for all
x\in\bigcup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}
. Furthermore,
\nu(D_{j_{i}})>0
, for each
i=1,2,\ldots
, and

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\bigr)\geq\frac{\theta}{\theta+n}\nu(\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}),

which implies that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\infty
and, from the conditional Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}\mbox{ i.o.})=1
. It follows that the subsequence of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
restricted on
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}
is itself an exchangeable MVPS with parameters
\theta^{*}=\theta\nu(\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}})
,
\nu^{*}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\,|\cup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}})
and
R_{x}=P_{(x)}
, for
x\in\bigcup_{i\geq 1}D_{j_{i}}
, so by Theorem
2.2
, as applied to the subsequence, we get

P_{j_{i}}(\cdot)=P_{(x)}(\cdot)=\nu^{*}(\cdot\mid D_{j_{i}})=\nu(\cdot\mid D_{j_{i}}),

using some
x\in D_{j_{i}}
as reference. The singular part of the reinforcement kernel has no such simple representation, yet by Theorem
2.1
it is a r.c.d. for
\nu
on
S
.

If
\nu(S)=0
or, equivalently,
P_{k}\ll\nu
for all
k\geq 1
, then

P_{(x)}(\cdot)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\nu(\cdot\mid D_{k})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}}(x)\qquad\mbox{for every }x\in\mathbb{X},

and, from Theorem
2.2
,

\displaystyle X_{n}\mid\tilde{P}

\displaystyle\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\tilde{P}(D_{k})P_{k}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\bigl(\tilde{P}(D_{k})\bigr)_{k\geq 1}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mbox{DP}(\theta,\nu)

Such models are commonly used for density estimation, see e.g.
[
15
, Chapter 5]
, and exchangeable MVPSs with dominated reinforcement kernels offer a particular set of model choices. For example, when
|\mathbb{X}|<\infty
and
\nu(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\mathbb{X}|}
,
A\in\mathcal{X}
, is uniform on
\mathbb{X}
, the directing random measure of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
becomes

\sum_{k\geq 1}\tilde{P}(D_{k})\frac{|\,\cdot\,\cap\,D_{k}|}{|D_{k}|},

which is a random histogram with non-uniform widths given by the
|D_{k}|
’s. At the other extreme, when
\nu(dx)=f(x)dx
is absolutely continuous (with respect to the Lebesgue measure) on
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}
, the directing random measure of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is itself absolutely continuous with random density

\sum_{k\geq 1}\tilde{P}(D_{k})f_{k}(x),

where
f_{k}(x)=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}}(x)f(x)}{\int_{D_{k}}f(y)dy}
,
x\in\mathbb{X}
, is the section of
f
over
D_{k}
, normalized.

Example 3.2
.

(
k
-color urn model)
Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable MVPS
(\theta,\nu,R)
on
\mathbb{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, where, without loss of generality, we assume that
R
is a probability kernel (see Section
2.2
). By Theorem
2.2
, there exists a partition
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of
\mathbb{X}
, for some
1\leq m\leq k
, such that

R_{x}(\cdot)=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\nu(\cdot\mid D_{l})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D_{l}}(x)\qquad\mbox{for every }x\in\mathbb{X}.

(3.2)

It follows that, with
\nu
and
\theta
fixed, the total number of exchangeable
k
-color MVPSs is equal to the number of possible partitions of
\mathbb{X}
, also known as Bell’s number, which is given by the formula

B_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{i=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-i}\binom{j}{i}i^{k},

with
B_{2}=2
,
B_{3}=5
,
B_{4}=15
, etc. The classical
k
-color Pólya urn model corresponds to the case with the finest partition
D_{j}=\{j\}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
, while we obtain an i.i.d. sequence when
D_{1}=\mathbb{X}
. On the other hand, intermediate cases can be appealing from an application point of view and can be used, for example, in a clinical trial setting to target both fixed asymptotic allocations (within the
D_{j}
’s) and random asymptotic allocations (across the
D_{j}
’s), simultaneously.

3.1
“Pure” sufficientness postulate for MVPSs

Recall from the introduction that (
1.1
) is equivalent to (
1.3
)-(
1.4
), up to a relabeling of the observations. In this section, we investigate whether exchangeable MVPSs can be characterized by statements like those in (
1.3
)-(
1.4
), which one might argue reflect a “pure” sufficientness postulate since they do not assume a particular analytic form for the predictive distributions. Based on the discussion in Remark
3.1
, such a result should be stated in terms of the sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
for which
R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})
. Here, we follow the approach by
[
11
, Proposition 3.3]
(see also Example
3.3
), who give an alternative formulation of (
1.3
)-(
1.4
) via predictive sufficiency. To that end, we introduce, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
and every
A\in\mathcal{X}
, the notation

N_{n,A}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{i}).

We also define, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
,

\mathcal{G}_{n}:=X_{n}^{-1}(\mathcal{G}).

The next theorem states that an exchangeable process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with reference to some c.g. under
\nu
sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{X}
if
(i)
the probability that
X_{n+1}
belongs to a set
A\in\mathcal{G}
depends only on the number of observations
N_{n,A}
in
A
; and
(ii)
, given that it belongs to
\mathcal{G}
, the random variable
X_{n+1}
is conditionally independent from
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. Note that condition
(i)
becomes vacuous when
\mathcal{G}
is a simple
\sigma
-algebra generated by a single subset
A\in\mathcal{X}
(see also Remark 1 in
[
29
]
and the discussion after Proposition
3.1
). For that purpose, we introduce the notion of a
\nu
-essentially not simple

\sigma
-algebra, which is a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
containing a finite partition
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
of
\mathbb{X}
with
|k|>2
elements such that at least three of them have positive
\nu
-probability,
\#\{j:\nu(B_{j})>0\}>2
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable process with marginal distribution
\nu
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{X}
on
\mathbb{X}
, which is c.g. under
\nu
and
\nu
-essentially not simple. If for each
n=1,2,\ldots
it holds

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|N_{n,A})\qquad\mbox{for all }A\in\mathcal{G},

(3.3)

and

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n+1})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\qquad\mbox{for all }B\in\mathcal{X},

(3.4)

then
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with reinforcement kernel
R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})
. The converse statement is also true.

Example 3.3

(Dirichlet process)
.

The current example essentially repeats Proposition 3.3 in
[
11
]
. Suppose in Theorem
3.2
that we have
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{X}
, which is c.g. by assumption. Then (
3.4
) is trivially true, so any exchangeable process
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfying

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|N_{n,A})\qquad\mbox{for all }A\in\mathcal{X},

is an MVPS with reinforcement kernel

R_{x}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{X})(x)=\delta_{x}(\cdot)\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-a.e. }x,

and, following
[
7
]
, has a Dirichlet process prior.

Example 3.4

(Mixture model)
.

In Theorem
3.2
, suppose that
\mathcal{G}=\sigma(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots)
, where
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{X}
forms a countable partition of
\mathbb{X}
. Then (
3.3
)-(
3.4
) can be equivalently formulated as

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in D_{k}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in D_{k}|N_{n,D_{k}})\qquad\mbox{for each }k=1,2,\ldots,

and, with a slight abuse of notation,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},X_{n+1}\in D_{k})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|X_{n+1}\in D_{k})\qquad\mbox{for all }B\in\mathcal{X}.

In that case,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with reinforcement kernel

R_{x}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\sigma(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots))(x)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\nu(\cdot\mid D_{k})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D_{k}}(x)\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-a.e. }x,

so that
R_{x}\ll\nu
and, from Theorem
2.2
,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has a Dirichlet process mixture prior.

Example 3.5

(Dirichlet invariant process)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}
. Define

\mathcal{G}:=\{A\in\mathcal{X}:-A=A\}\equiv\{A\cup(-A),A\in\mathcal{X}\},

where
-A=\{-x,x\in A\}
. Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable process such that, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
and every
A\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|N_{n,A}+N_{n,-A}),

(3.5)

and

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in-A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}).

(3.6)

If we denote by
\nu
the marginal distribution of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we get
\nu(-A)=\nu(A)
from (
3.6
), for every
A\in\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, (
3.5
) implies (
3.3
) when
A\in\mathcal{G}
, since
-A=A
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},A\in\mathcal{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{G}
. Since
\nu
is invariant with respect to
\mathcal{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\nu(A\cap B)=\frac{\nu(A\cap B)+\nu((-A)\cap B)}{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})\cdot\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})+\mathbbm{1}_{-A}(X_{n+1})\bigr)\Bigr];

thus,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})=\frac{\delta_{X_{n+1}}(A)+\delta_{-X_{n+1}}(A)}{2}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

If, in addition,
A\cap(-A)=\emptyset
, then
\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A\cup(-A)}(X_{n+1})
a.s. and, from (
3.5
) and (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{A}

\displaystyle(X_{n+1})\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\cdot\mathbbm{P}(X_{n+1}\in A\cap B|N_{n,A\cap B}+N_{n,(-A)\cap B})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\cdot\mathbbm{P}(X_{n+1}\in(A\cup(-A))\cap B|N_{n,(A\cup(-A))\cap B})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{A\cup(-A)}(X_{n+1})\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in A_{n}|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})\bigr].

By varying
A\in\mathcal{C}:=\{(-b,-a],(a,b]:a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+},a<b\}\cup\{\emptyset\}
and using the fact that
\mathcal{C}
forms a
\pi
-class generating
\mathcal{X}=\sigma(\mathcal{C})
, we can conclude that
X_{n+1}
and
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
. Hence,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
3.2
and is therefore an exchangeable MVPS with reinforcement kernel

R_{x}(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})(x)=\frac{\delta_{x}(\cdot)+\delta_{-x}(\cdot)}{2}\qquad\mbox{for }\nu\mbox{-a.e. }x.

This model has been studied by
[
5
]
, who show that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has a so-called Dirichlet invariant process prior (for more information, see Examples 3 and 17 in
[
5
]
).

3.2
k-color MVPS

In this section, we prove a generalization of Johnson’s sufficientness postulate (
1.2
) in the context of the
k
-color urn model from Example

. Although some of the results can be deduced from previous sections, for completeness we present a separate proof in the spirit of
[

Let
\mathbb{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and
\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a partition of
\mathbb{X}
, for some
1\leq m\leq k
. For each
j\in\mathbb{X}
, let us denote by
m(j)
the index
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
j\in D_{l}
, and let us define

N_{0,l}:=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad N_{n,l}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{l}}(X_{i}),\qquad\mbox{for }n\geq 1,l=1,\ldots,m.

If
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an exchangeable MVPS with reinforcement kernel given by (
3.2
), then for each
j=1,\ldots,k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=j|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\nu(\{j\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu(\{j\}|D_{m(j)})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D_{m(j)}}(X_{i})}{\theta+n}=\frac{\theta\nu(\{j\})+\nu(\{j\}|D_{m(j)})\cdot N_{n,m(j)}}{\theta+n},

that is, the probability of observing type

on the
(n+1)
th trial depends on the sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
only by the number of times that each type in
D_{m(j)}
has been observed in the past. This fact motivates the next proposition, which extends Johnson’s sufficientness postulate (

) by considering a grouping of types according to
D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}
.

For ease of notation, we will denote

\mathbb{P}_{0}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot\;)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{n}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}),\quad\mbox{for }n=1,2,\ldots

In addition, we will work under the assumption that any sequence of types has a positive probability of being observed,

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{n})>0\qquad\mbox{for all }(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{X}^{n}\mbox{ and }n=1,2,\ldots

(A)

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}
form a partition of
\mathbb{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, with
3\leq m\leq k
, and let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable sequence of
\mathbb{X}
-valued random variables satisfying (
A
). Using the established notation, if for each
j=1,\ldots,k
and

, it holds

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\{j\})=f_{n,j}(N_{n,m(j)}),

for some functions
f_{n,j}:\{0,\ldots,n\}\rightarrow[0,1]
, then there exist
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}>0
such that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with constant
\bar{w}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}
, base measure
\nu(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}}\delta_{j}(\cdot)
and reinforcement kernel

R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\sigma(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m})).

If
m=1
in Proposition
3.1
, it is easy to see that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is i.i.d. with marginal distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
. When

, however, additional assumptions on

are needed to obtain the stated characterization result (see the remark after the proof of Proposition

in Section

). A different approach, following the work of Hill and coauthors

]
, is presented in the next proposition, which is more in line with Proposition

and covers the case

. Hill et al.’s
[

]

, for
j=1,\ldots,k
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}
form a partition of
\mathbb{X}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
, with
1\leq m\leq k
, and let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an exchangeable, but not i.i.d. sequence of
\mathbb{X}
-valued random variables satisfying (

). Fix

, and define

\bar{w}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\bar{w}_{l}:=\sum_{j\in D_{l}}w_{j},\quad\mbox{for }l=1,\ldots,m.

Using the established notation, if for each
j=1,\ldots,k
and
n=0,1,\ldots
, it holds

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\{j\})=f_{j}\biggl(\frac{\bar{w}_{m(j)}+N_{n,m(j)}}{\bar{w}+n}\biggr),

for some functions
f_{j}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
, then
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with constant
\bar{w}
, base measure
\nu(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}}\delta_{j}(\cdot)
and reinforcement kernel

R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\sigma(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m})).

4
Proofs

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem
3.1
will be proven in several steps. We start by deriving some relations between
\nu
,
R
and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as a consequence of exchangeability.

Step 1.
Let
A,B\in\mathcal{X}
. It follows from (
3.1
) and
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in dx)=\nu(dx)
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X_{2}\in B)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in B|X_{1}=x)\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\bigl\{(1-a_{1})\nu(B)+a_{1}R_{x}(B)\bigr\}\nu(dx)=(1-a_{1})\nu(A)\nu(B)+a_{1}\int_{A}R_{x}(B)\nu(dx).

Since
(X_{1},X_{2})\overset{d}{=}(X_{2},X_{1})
by exchangeability, then

\displaystyle(1-a_{1})\nu(A)\nu(B)+a_{1}\int_{A}R_{x}(B)\nu(dx)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A,X_{2}\in B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B,X_{2}\in A)=(1-a_{1})\nu(A)\nu(B)+a_{1}\int_{B}R_{x}(A)\nu(dx);

therefore,
\int_{x\in A}\int_{y\in B}R_{x}(dy)\nu(dx)=\int_{x\in B}\int_{y\in A}R_{x}(dy)\nu(dx)
or, as measures on
\mathbb{X}^{2}
,

R_{x}(dy)\nu(dx)=R_{y}(dx)\nu(dy).

(4.1)

Next, we have for
\nu
-a.e.
x

Step 2.

Case 1:
\nu(D)>0
.

By definition, for every

\nu(B_{x})\neq R_{x}(B_{x})
. It follows from (

-a.e.

Sub-Case 1a:

,

r(x,y)r(y,z)=r(x,z)r(z,y).

(4.14)

Recall that, for every
x\in D
, there exists
B_{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\nu(B_{x})\neq R_{x}(B)
. It follows from (
4.14
) that, for
\nu
-a.e.
x
in
D
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{X}}R_{y}(B_{x})R_{x}(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in\mathbb{X}}\int_{z\in B_{x}}r(x,y)r(y,z)\nu(dz)\nu(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in B_{x}}\int_{z\in\mathbb{X}}r(x,y)r(y,z)\nu(dz)\nu(dy)=\int_{B_{x}}R_{y}(\mathbb{X})R_{x}(dy)=R_{x}(B_{x}).

On the other hand, by (
4.2
), for
\nu
-a.e.
x
in
D
,

\int_{\mathbb{X}}R_{y}(B_{x})R_{x}(dy)=(1-c^{*})R_{x}(B_{x})+c^{*}\nu(B_{x});

therefore,
R_{x}(B_{x})=\nu(B_{x})
, absurd. As a result,
c^{*}=0
, which implies as in the previous sub-case that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with parameters
(a^{-1}-1,\nu,R)
.

Case 2:
\nu(D)=0
.

If
\nu(D)=0
, then
R_{x}\equiv\nu
for
\nu
-a.e.
x
. Since
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in\cdot\,)=\nu(\cdot)
, it follows for every
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B_{1},X_{2}\in B_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in B_{2}|X_{1}=x)\nu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\bigl\{(1-a_{1})\nu(B_{2})+a_{1}R_{x}(B_{2})\bigr\}\nu(dx)=\nu(B_{1})\nu(B_{2});

thus,
\mathbb{P}_{(X_{1},X_{2})}(\cdot)=(\nu\times\nu)(\cdot)
. By induction, for every
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in B_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\nu(B_{i});

therefore,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is i.i.d. with marginal distribution
\nu
, and thus trivially an MVPS with parameters
(a^{-1}-1,\nu,R)
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Suppose that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies (
3.3
)-(
3.4
). By assumption,
\mathcal{G}
is c.g. under
\nu
, so there exists (see
[
4
, p.649]
) an a.e. proper regular version
R
of
\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})
, in the sense that, for some
F\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\nu(F)=1
, it holds

R_{x}(A)=\delta_{x}(A)\qquad\mbox{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}\mbox{ and }x\in F.

Moreover, since
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is identically distributed with marginal distribution
\nu
, we have
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in F,\ldots,X_{n}\in F)=1
, for each
n=1,2,\ldots
, which implies that, on a set of probability one,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(A)\qquad\mbox{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}.

(4.15)

Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\in\mathcal{G}
form a partition of
\mathbb{X}
with
k>2
and
\#\{j:\nu(B_{j})>0\}>2
. It follows from (
3.3
) that the sequence
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
on the level of
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
satisfies Johnson’s sufficientness postulate (
1.2
), so from Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.1 in
[
29
]
, there exist
\alpha_{B_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{B_{k}}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{B_{i}}>0
and, for each
j=1,\ldots,k
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B_{j}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\alpha_{B_{j}}+N_{n,B_{j}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{B_{i}}+n}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

In particular,
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B_{j})=\alpha_{B_{j}}\bigl/\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{B_{i}}
, from where
a_{B_{j}}=\nu(B_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{B_{i}}
. Moreover,
\theta:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{B_{i}}
does not depend on the particular partition chosen. Indeed, let
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be another partition of
\mathbb{X}
with
m>2
and
\#\{l:\nu(C_{l})>0\}>2
. If
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\}
is finer than
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
and, say,
B_{j}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{L}C_{j_{l}}
, where
\nu(B_{j})>0
, we obtain, using the results in
[
29
]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\theta\nu(B_{j})+N_{n,B_{j}}}{\theta+n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in C_{j_{l}}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\nu(C_{j_{l}})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}}+N_{n,C_{j_{l}}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}}+n}=\frac{\nu(B_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}}+N_{n,B_{j}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}}+n}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

After simplification, we get
n(\theta-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}})\nu(B_{j})=(\theta-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}})N_{n,B_{j}}
a.s., for all
n=1,2,\ldots
, which implies that
\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{C_{i}}
. In any other case, we can consider the partition
\{B_{i}\cap C_{l},i=1,\ldots,k,l=1,\ldots,m\}
and conclude that the three partitions produce the same constant.

Let
A\in\mathcal{G}
. Arguing through the partition
\{A\cap B_{j},A^{c}\cap B_{j},j=1,\ldots,k\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\theta\nu(A\cap B_{j})+N_{n,A\cap B_{j}}}{\theta+n}=\frac{\theta\nu(A)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(A)}{\theta+n}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Using that
\mathcal{G}
is c.g. under
\nu
, we obtain that, as measures on
\mathbb{X}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in dx|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\frac{\theta\nu(A)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(dx)}{\theta+n}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

(4.16)

Let
A\in\mathcal{G}
and
B\in\mathcal{X}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\nu(A\cap B)=\int_{A}R_{x}(B)\nu(dx)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})\cdot R_{X_{n+1}}(B)],

and, since
\omega\mapsto R_{X_{n+1}(\omega)}(B)
is
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
-measurable,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})=R_{X_{n+1}}(B)\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Applying (
4.16
), we obtain, on a set of probability one,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\mathcal{G}_{n+1})|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[R_{X_{n+1}}(B)|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}R_{x}(B)\biggl\{\frac{\theta\nu(dx)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(dx)}{\theta+n}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\int_{\mathbb{X}}R_{x}(B)\nu(dx)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{X}}R_{x}(B)\delta_{X_{i}}(dx)}{\theta+n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\nu(B)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(B)}{\theta+n}.

Note that the above result does not contradict (
4.16
) when
B\in\mathcal{G}
because of (
4.15
). Therefore, we can conclude that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with parameters
(\theta,\nu,R)
.

Conversely, suppose that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an exchangeable MVPS with reinforcement kernel
R(\cdot)=\nu(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})
. Recall from Theorem
2.1
and the discussion around (
2.4
) that any exchangeable MVPS admits such a representation. Moreover, we may assume that
R
is a.e. proper, without loss of generality. Arguing as in (
4.15
), we obtain, for all
A\in\mathcal{G}
, on a set of probability one,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\nu(A)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(A)}{\theta+n}=\frac{\theta\nu(A)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(A)}{\theta+n}=\frac{\theta\nu(A)+N_{n,A}}{\theta+n};

thus,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies (
3.3
). Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},B\in\mathcal{X}
and
A\in\mathcal{G}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{A}

\displaystyle(X_{n+1})\mathbbm{1}_{B}(X_{n+1})]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in A\cap B|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\bigr]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\frac{\theta\nu(A\cap B)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(A\cap B)}{\theta+n}\biggr]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\frac{\theta\nu(A\cap B)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{X_{i}}(B)\delta_{X_{i}}(A)}{\theta+n}\biggr]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\int_{A}R_{x}(B)\biggl\{\frac{\theta\nu(dx)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}(dx)}{\theta+n}\biggr\}\biggr]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})R_{X_{n+1}}(B)|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(X_{1})\cdots\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})\cdot R_{X_{n+1}}(B)]

\displaystyle\,=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in A_{n}|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})\cdot R_{X_{n+1}}(B)\bigr]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in A_{n}|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A}(X_{n+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in B|\mathcal{G}_{n+1})\bigr],

where
(a)
follows from
R_{x}(A\cap B)=\nu(A\cap B|\mathcal{G})(x)=\nu(B|\mathcal{G})(x)\delta_{x}(A)=R_{x}(B)\delta_{x}(A)
, for
\nu
-a.e.
x
, since
A\in\mathcal{G}
; and
(b)
follows by taking
A_{1}=\cdots=A_{n}=\mathbb{X}
and noticing that
R_{X_{n+1}}(B)
is
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
-measurable. Therefore,
X_{n+1}
and
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let us define, for every
n\geq 0
,
l=1,\ldots,m
and
n_{l}=0,1,\ldots,n
, the quantity

g_{n,l}(n_{l}):=\sum_{j\in D_{l}}f_{n,j}(n_{l}).

It follows from (
A
) that
f_{n,j}(n_{l})>0
, and so
g_{n,l}(n_{l})>0
. Recall that
m(j)
denotes, for each
j=1,\ldots,k
, the index
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
j\in D_{l}
. Define
Y_{n}:=m(X_{n})
, for
n\geq 1
. Then
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an exchangeable process with predictive distributions

\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}=l|Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})=P_{n}(D_{l})=g_{n,l}(N_{n,l})\qquad\mbox{a.s., for }l=1,\ldots,m.

(4.17)

Therefore,
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies Johnson’s sufficientness postulate (
1.2
), so from Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.1 in
[
29
]
, there exist
\bar{w}_{1},\ldots,\bar{w}_{m}>0
such that

g_{n,l}(n_{l})=\frac{\bar{w}_{l}+n_{l}}{\bar{w}+n},

(4.18)

where
\bar{w}:=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\bar{w}_{l}
; thus, in particular,

\mathbb{P}_{0}(D_{l})=\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}=l)=g_{0,l}(0)=\frac{\bar{w}_{l}}{\bar{w}}\qquad\mbox{for }l=1,\ldots,m.

(4.19)

Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
be such that
D_{m(j)}=\{j\}
. Then
f_{n,j}(n_{m(j)})=g_{n,m(j)}(n_{m(j)})
, so from (
4.18
)-(
4.19
),

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\{j\})=g_{n,m(j)}(N_{n,m(j)})=\frac{\bar{w}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{j\})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{m(j)}}(X_{i})}{\bar{w}+n}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

(4.20)

Let
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
be such that
m(i)=m(j)
. Fix
n\geq 0
, where for
n=0
the expressions involving
\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are meant as unconditional statements. It follows for every
h=1,2,\ldots
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=j,X_{n+2}\notin D_{m(j)}

\displaystyle,\ldots,X_{n+h+1}\notin D_{m(j)}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=f_{n,j}(N_{n,m(j)})\prod_{s=1}^{h}\bigl(1-g_{n+s,m(j)}(N_{n,m(j)}+1)\bigr)\quad\mbox{a.s.},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\notin D_{m(j)},\ldots,X_{n+h}\notin

\displaystyle D_{m(j)},X_{n+h+1}=j|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=f_{n+h,j}(N_{n,m(j)})\prod_{s=0}^{h-1}\bigl(1-g_{n+s,m(j)}(N_{n,m(j)})\bigr)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

By exchangeability, the two expressions are equal, so we obtain

\frac{f_{n,j}(N_{n,m(j)})}{f_{n+h,j}(N_{n,m(j)})}=\frac{\prod_{s=0}^{h-1}\bigl(1-g_{n+s,m(j)}(N_{n,m(j)})\bigr)}{\prod_{s=1}^{h}\bigl(1-g_{n+s,m(j)}(N_{n,m(j)}+1)\bigr)}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}

Since the right-hand side depends on
j
only through
m(j)
, the same ratio
\frac{f_{n,i}}{f_{n+h,i}}
for
i
is equal to the same quantity; therefore, with
n_{m(j)}\equiv n_{m(i)}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
fixed,

\frac{f_{n,i}(n_{m(i)})}{f_{n,j}(n_{m(j)})}=\frac{f_{n+h,i}(n_{m(i)})}{f_{n+h,j}(n_{m(j)})}\qquad\mbox{for every }h=1,2,\ldots

(4.21)

On the other hand, for every
h=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\notin D_{m(j)},\ldots,X_{n}

\displaystyle\notin D_{m(j)},X_{n+1}=j,X_{n+2}\in D_{m(j)},\ldots,X_{2n+1}\in D_{m(j)})

\displaystyle=f_{n,j}(0)\prod_{s=0}^{n-1}\bigl(1-g_{s,m(j)}(0)\bigr)\prod_{s=1}^{n}g_{n+s,m(j)}(s)\qquad\mbox{a.s.},

and, applying the permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,2n+1\}
that swaps the indices
(1,\ldots,h)
with
(n+2,\ldots,n+h+1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{\sigma(1)}\notin D_{m(j)},

\displaystyle\ldots,X_{\sigma(n)}\notin D_{m(j)},X_{\sigma(n+1)}=j,\ldots,X_{\sigma(2n+1)}\in D_{m(j)})

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =f_{n,j}(h)&\prod_{s=0}^{h-1}g_{s,m(j)}(s)\prod_{s=0}^{n-h-1}\bigl(1-g_{h+s,m(j)}(h)\bigr)\\
&\times\prod_{s=1}^{h}\bigl(1-g_{n+s,m(j)}(h)\bigr)\prod_{s=1}^{n-h}g_{n+h+s,m(j)}(h+s-1)\quad\mbox{a.s.}\end{aligned}

Since
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{2n+1})\overset{d}{=}(X_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,X_{\sigma(2n+1)})
by exchangeability, we obtain, as in (
4.21
), with
n
fixed,

\frac{f_{n,i}(0)}{f_{n,j}(0)}=\frac{f_{n,i}(h)}{f_{n,j}(h)}\qquad\mbox{for every }h=1,\ldots,n.

(4.22)

It follows from (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) that, for any such pair
i,j
, the ratio below is independent of
n
and
n_{m(j)}\equiv n_{m(i)}
,

\frac{f_{n,i}(n_{m(i)})}{f_{n,j}(n_{m(j)})}=:d_{i,j}.

Summing over all
i
in
D_{m(j)}
and letting
w_{j}:=\frac{\bar{w}_{m(j)}}{\sum_{i\in D_{m(j)}}d_{i,j}}
, gives

f_{n,j}(n_{m(j)})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in D_{m(j)}}d_{i,j}}g_{n,m(j)}(n_{m(j)})=\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}_{m(j)}}g_{n,m(j)}(n_{m(j)});

thus, in particular, from (
4.19
),

\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{j\})=f_{0,j}(0)=\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}_{m(j)}}g_{0,m(j)}(0)=\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}}.

In that case,
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{j\}|D_{m(j)})=\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}_{m(j)}}
; therefore, using (
4.18
),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}_{n}(\{j\})=f_{n,j}(N_{n,m(j)})&=\frac{w_{j}}{\bar{w}_{m(j)}}\frac{\bar{w}_{m(j)}+N_{n,m(j)}}{\bar{w}+n}\\
&=\frac{\bar{w}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{j\})+\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{j\}|D_{m(j)})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{m(j)}}(X_{i})}{\bar{w}+n}\qquad\mbox{a.s.}\end{aligned}

(4.23)

We can now conclude from (
4.20
) and (
4.23
) that, as measures on
\mathbb{X}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}\in dy\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{w}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{0}(dy)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{0}(dy\mid D_{l})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{D_{l}}(X_{i})}{\bar{w}+n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{w}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{0}(dy)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{0}(dy\mid\sigma(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}))(X_{i})}{\bar{w}+n}\quad\mbox{a.s.},

which implies that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is an MVPS with parameters
\bigl(\bar{w},\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}(\cdot|\sigma(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}))\bigr)
.
∎
