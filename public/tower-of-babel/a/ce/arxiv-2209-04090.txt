Quantiles, Ranks and Signs in Metric Spaces
Hang Liu, Xueqin Wang, Jin Zhu, Heping Zhang

Abstract
Non-Euclidean data become more prevalent in practice, necessitating the development of a framework for statistical inference analogous to that for Euclidean data. Quantile is one of the most important concepts in traditional statistical inference; we introduce the counterpart, both locally and globally, for data objects in metric spaces. This is realized by expanding upon the metric distribution function proposed by Wang et al. 2021 . Rank and sign are defined at local and global levels as a natural consequence of the center-outward ordering of metric spaces brought about by the local and global quantiles. The theoretical properties are established, such as the root- n n consistency and uniform consistency of the local and global empirical quantiles and the distribution-freeness of ranks and signs. The empirical metric median, which is defined here as the 0th empirical global metric quantile, is proven to be resistant to contamination by means of both theoretical and numerical approaches. Quantiles have been shown to be valuable through extensive simulations in a number of metric spaces. Moreover, we introduce a family of fast rank-based independence tests for a generic metric space. Monte Carlo experiments show good finite-sample performance of the test.



Keywords:
Metric space, Local metric quantile, Global metric quantile, Metric rank, Metric sign

1
Introduction

Data in non-Euclidean spaces can be found in various fields. Examples include directional data
(
Marinucci et al. 2008
;
Marinucci & Peccati 2011
)
, symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices
(
Smith et al. 2013
)
, samples of probability distribution functions in Wasserstein spaces
(
Petersen et al. 2021
)
, to name only a few. In order to perform nonparametric statistical inference and analysis for such data, we need to develop fundamental concepts, including quantiles, ranks, and signs for non-Euclidean data.

Unlike the real line, however, there is no canonical ordering in non-Euclidean spaces, which makes
it is challenging to develop necessary and important concepts, methods, and theories.
To tackle this issue, various types of depths have been proposed to introduce ordering in non-Euclidean spaces, including the angular Tukey depth
(
Liu & Singh 1992
)
and distance-based depth
(
Pandolfo et al. 2018
)
both for spherical data, the intrinsic zonoid depth for SPD matrices
(
Chau et al. 2019
)
, the halfspace depth for curve data
(
De Micheaux et al. 2021
)
, and so on. These notions of depths, however, focus only on a specific metric space. However, relevant literature on general metric spaces is scarce. By defining halfspace through the underlying distance metric,
Dai et al. 2022
proposed a notion of depth at a point as the least probability measure of the halfspaces containing this point. Like the halfspace depth in the Euclidean space, the depth of
Dai et al. 2022
is computationally inefficient. Another type of depth is the depth profile proposed by
Dubey et al. 2022
, referring to the distribution of the distances between a point and the other elements in the metric space.

Different from
Dai et al. 2022
and
Dubey et al. 2022
, where depths are defined but concepts of quantiles are missing, this paper proposes novel concepts of quantiles from both the local and global perspectives for general metric spaces. Our definition is based on the metric distribution function (MDF), which is the probability measure of a closed ball characterized by two points in metric space
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
. According to the MDF levels, our local quantiles at a specific location in the metric space are defined.
In order to define quantiles from a global perspective, we take a quantity resulting from aggregating the local information of the MDF for all points in the metric space and define the global quantiles based on the levels of the distribution function of this quantity. The empirical versions of the local and global quantiles are defined as the discrete sample versions of their population counterparts. Observing the local and global quantiles induces center-outward orderings in the metric space locally and globally, respectively, we therefore define the local and global ranks and signs based on their empirical quantile levels.

In addition to attractive interpretation, as described previously, our novel concepts of the local and global quantiles, ranks, and signs also enjoy a number of desirable theoretical properties. For example, the empirical local and global quantiles are root-
n
and uniformly consistent; the local and global ranks and signs are distribution-free. Compared with the depths in
Dai et al. 2022
and
Dubey et al. 2022
, our empirical global quantiles are easy to implement in practice since the computation is straightforward, does not involve any optimization process, and is computationally efficient with complexity
O(n^{2}\log(n))
when the global quantiles are computed for all the
n
sample points. As illustrated via numerical study in Section
6
, distributions in various metric spaces are well-characterized by the global quantiles, and the empirical quantiles have good finite-sample performance, manifesting the reasonableness of our novel concepts. Moreover, theoretical analysis of the breakdown point and simulation study under various metric spaces show that our
empirical metric median
, defined as the
0
th empirical global metric quantile, is more robust against contamination than the
metric halfspace median
of
Dai et al. 2022
and the
transport median
of
Dubey et al. 2022
.

The global ranks and signs in this paper naturally allow us to define rank-based tests for a generic metric space, which have seldom been considered in the literature on rank-based statistical inference. As an illustration, in Section
5
, we consider a class of rank-based two-sample independence tests, which are computationally efficient with a complexity of
O(n^{2}\log(n))
. Monte Carlo experiments show the consistency of our test and good finite-sample performance even under heavy-tailed distributions.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
briefly introduces the MDF, its empirical version, and the asymptotic properties in
Wang et al. 2021
, which are the building blocks of the main concepts of this paper. Sections
3
and
4
, from the local and global perspectives, respectively, define the metric quantile, rank, and sign, and establish the asymptotic results, including the root-
n
and uniform consistency, of the empirical metric quantile. Moreover, computation aspects and robustness of the global metric quantile are discussed in Section
4
. Section
5
proposes the metric rank-based independence test. Section
6
illustrates the concepts of the global metric quantile and its empirical version via Monte Carlo experiments under various metric spaces. Numerical analysis comparing the robustness of the empirical metric median and the counterparts of
Dai et al. 2022
and
Dubey et al. 2022
is also included in Section
6
. The finite-sample performance of the metric rank-based independence test is investigated via Monte Carlo experiments in Section
6
.
Section
7
concludes the paper and provides some perspectives for future research. All proofs, some preliminaries, simulation details, additional results, and a discussion of the computation algorithm are collected in Appendices.

2
Preliminaries

Wang et al. 2021
proposed the concept of the
metric distribution function
(MDF) in a metric space, serving as a quasi-distribution in metric space. In this section, we briefly introduce the MDF and its empirical version, which are important ingredients for defining quantiles in the metric space.

2.1
Metric distribution function

An ordered pair
({\cal M},d)
is called
metric space
if
{\cal M}
is a set and
d
is a metric or distance on
{\cal M}
(see Appendix
for some examples of non-Euclidean metric spaces that have wide applications in various fields). Throughout the paper, we denote by
\bar{B}({\bf u},r):=\{{\bf v}:d({\bf u},{\bf v})\leq r\}
the closed ball with the center
{\bf u}
and radius
r\geq 0
on
({\cal M},d)
, and by
B({\bf u},r):=\{{\bf v}:d({\bf u},{\bf v})<r\}
the corresponding open ball.

Denote by
\mu
a probability measure associated with a random variable
{\bf X}\in{\cal M}
.
Let

\delta({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf X}):=I(d({\bf u},{\bf X})\leq d({\bf u},{\bf v})),

where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function. The metric distribution function of
\mu
on
{\cal M}
measures the probability that
{\bf X}\in{\cal M}
is contained in
\bar{B}({\bf u},r)
and is defined as

F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})\coloneqq\mu(\bar{B}({\bf u},{\bf r}))={\rm E}(\delta({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf X}))

for
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in{\cal M}.

Different from the traditional distribution function in an Euclidean space, which is a function of one variable and is defined from a global perspective,
F_{\mu}^{\cal M}
is a probability distribution function defined locally (at
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
) for the closed ball
\bar{B}({\bf u},r)\subset{\cal M}
characterized by two variables
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}
.

2.2
Empirical metric distribution function

Based on the definition of the MDF, its empirical version can naturally be defined as follows. Let
{\bf X}^{(n)}\coloneqq\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. sample generated from the probability measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}
. For
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in{\cal M}
, the empirical metric distribution function (EMDF) is the empirical probability measure of the sample being contained in
\bar{B}({\bf u},r)
. It is defined as

F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf X}_{i}).

(1)

Similar to the empirical distribution function on the real line,
F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}
also satisfies the Glivenko-Cantelli and Donsker asymptotic, which are important properties for conducting nonparametric statistical inference in a metric space and for establishing asymptotics of the quantiles proposed in this paper. See Section
in the Supplementary material for details of the Glivenko-Cantelli and Donsker properties. The establishment of the two properties of the EMDF relies on the following assumptions imposed in
Wang et al. 2021
.

Assumption 1

\mu
on
{\cal M}
satisfies that

n^{-1}{\rm E}_{{\bf X}}[\log({\rm card}({\cal F}({\bf X}^{(n)})))]\rightarrow 0
,
where
{\rm card}
denotes the cardinality of a set and
{\cal F}({\bf X}^{(n)})\coloneqq\{h({\bf X}_{1}),\ldots,h({\bf X}_{n})|h\in{\cal F}\}
.

Assumption 2

The collection of the indicator functions of closed balls on
{\cal M}
, i.e.
{\cal F}\coloneqq\{\delta({\bf u},{\bf v},\cdot):{\bf u},{\bf v}\in{\cal M}\}
, is a VC class
1

van der Vaart & Wellner 1996
for the definition.
with a finite VC-dimension.

3
Local metric quantile and its empirical version

3.1
Local metric quantile

Once we have the definition of the MDF for the metric space
({\cal M},d)
, the quantile can naturally be defined according to the level of the MDF.
Note that, in contrast to the traditional distribution function in an Euclidean space, the MDF is a probability distribution function defined locally at
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
; hence, the quantile in Definition
1
is also defined from a local perspective for each
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
. It is a measure of outlyingness with respect to a fixed point in the metric space.

Definition 1

Given a point
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
, the
\tau
th quantile (
\tau\in[0,1]
) of
\mu
on
{\cal M}
is

q^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf v}\in{\cal M}:F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})=\tau\}
,
the
\tau
th quantile region is
\bar{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf v}\in{\cal M}:F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})\leq\tau\}
,
and the
\tau
th inner quantile region is

\tilde{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf v}\in{\cal M}:F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})<\tau\}
.

Throughout, we make the following mild assumption on
\mu
, which entails some desirable properties of the quantiles such as nestedness and
\sqrt{n}
-consistency of the empirical local metric quantile defined in Section
3.2
.

Assumption 3

\mu
is absolutely continuous with density function
f
, which is positive and continuous everywhere on
{\cal M}
.

In analog to the quantile regions on the real line, the quantile regions in Definition
1
enjoy the following property of nestedness. It implies that the quantile
q^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)
induces a center-outward ordering of nested regions, with
{\bf u}
being the center. See Section
Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 1

If Assumption
3
holds, then for
0\leq\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}\leq 1
and any
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
,
\bar{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau_{1})\subsetneq\bar{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau_{2})
.

3.2
Empirical local metric quantile

Given an i.i.d. sample
{\bf X}^{(n)}\coloneqq\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\}
generated from the probability measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}
, similar to Definition
1
, we can naturally define the empirical version of the local metric quantile based on the EMDF. It measures the outlyingness of the sample with respect to a fixed point in the metric space.

Definition 2

Given a point
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
, the
\tau
th empirical quantile (
\tau\in[0,1]
) of
\mu
on
{\cal M}
is

q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\coloneqq\begin{cases}{\bf u},&\tau\in[0,\frac{1}{n})\\
\argmin_{{\bf X}_{i}\in{\bf X}^{(n)}}F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{i})\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{i})\geq\tau,&\tau\in[\frac{1}{n},1].\end{cases}

Remark 1

Note that the
\tau
th population quantile in Definition
1
yields a set of contours in
{\cal M}
, whereas the empirical version, with probability one, is a point in
{\cal M}
due to the absolute continuity of
\mu
. The
\tau
th sample quantile may alternatively be defined as a set of points through factorizing
n
, which would yield a set of contours in
{\cal M}
after performing interpolation. Specifically, by factorization of
n=n_{1}n_{2}+n_{0}
, where
n_{1},n_{2}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and
n_{0}
, as the multiplicity of the
0
th sample quantile, is a small number, one can define the
\tau
th (
\tau\geq 1/n_{2}
) sample quantile as a set of
n_{1}
points according to the ordering of
F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,n
. This approach is equivalent to defining
\tau
th sample quantile as the set of
[\tau,\tau+n_{1}/n)
th sample quantiles in Definition
2
. This type of definition, however, usually requires a relatively large
n
(especially when the dimension is large) to obtain a regular contour. In the context of Euclidean space, defining the sample contour through the factorization of
n
can be found in
Hallin et al. 2021
. A similar remark also applies to the global empirical metric quantile defined in Section
4.2
.

Proposition
2
states that the
\tau
th empirical local metric quantile, with probability one, lies in a small neighborhood (of order
n^{-1/2}
) of the
\tau
th local quantile contours, which is generated by the difference of two nested inner quantile regions. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 2

For any
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
, when
\tau\in[0,\frac{1}{n})
,
q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)=q^{\cal M}({\bf u},0)
; when
\tau\in[\frac{1}{n},1]
, under Assumptions
2
and
3
, with probability one,

q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\subset\tilde{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{G}_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)))\setminus\tilde{q}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{G}_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau))),

where
\mathbb{G}_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v}):=\sqrt{n}(F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})-F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})),\,{\bf u},{\bf v}\in{\cal M}
.

The following result, moreover, states the uniform consistency (in terms of
{\bf u}
and
\tau
) of the empirical local metric quantile. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 3

If Assumption
1
holds, then

\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\underset{{\bf u}\in{\cal M},\tau\in[0,1]}{\sup}{\rm P}(q_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)\not\subset q^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau))=0.

3.3
Local metric ranks and signs

On a real line, the distribution function, as a monotonic increasing map from
\mathbb{R}
to
[0,1]
, introduces the left-to-right order relation. Its empirical version, also as a monotonic increasing map from the i.i.d. sample to the regular grid on
[0,1]
, naturally allows us to define ranks of a sample by multiplication of the sample size
n
(or
n+1
). In our setting of a general metric space, when a point
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
is given, we can similarly define ranks locally by the local empirical distribution function. Moreover, signs can also be defined locally according to the sign of
F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})n/(n+1)-1/2,1\leq j\leq n.

Definition 3

Let
\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. sample generated from the probability measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}
. Then given a point
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
, the local metric rank of
{\bf X}_{j},1\leq j\leq n
is

R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j}):=nF_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j},{\bf X}_{i})

and the local metric sign of
{\bf X}_{j}
is

S_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j}):={\rm sign}\left(\frac{nF_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)={\rm sign}\left(\frac{R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

An important property that makes ranks and signs successful tools for statistical inference is their distribution-freeness. Similar to their counterparts on the real line, this property also holds for our local metric ranks and signs. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 4

Let
\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. sample generated from the probability measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}
. Then given a point
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
,

(i)

F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}^{(n)}):=(F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{1}),F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{2}),\ldots,F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{n}))
is uniformly distributed over the
n!
permutations of
(1/n,2/n,\ldots,1)
;

(ii)

R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}^{(n)}):=(R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{1}),\ldots,R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{n}))
is uniformly distributed over the
n!
permutations of
(1,\ldots,n)
;

(iii)

{\rm P}(S_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})=1)=\frac{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}{n}={\rm P}(S_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})=-1)
and
{\rm P}(S_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})=0)=1-\frac{2\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}{n}
.

3.4
Isometry-invariance of the local concepts

Now, we discuss the equivariance properties of the distribution functions, local quantiles, ranks, and signs. Let
({\cal M},d)
and
({\cal M}^{\prime},d^{\prime})
be two metric spaces, and

T:{\cal M}\rightarrow{\cal M}^{\prime}
be an isometry; that is,

d^{\prime}(T{\bf u},T{\bf v})=d({\bf u},{\bf v}),\forall{\bf u},{\bf v}\in{\cal M}.

Proposition
5
states that the distribution function, empirical version, local ranks, and signs are invariant under an isometric transformation. The local quantile and its empirical version are also invariant to an isometric transformation. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 5

Let
\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}\}
be i.i.d. samples generated from the probability measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}
. Then

(i)

F_{\mu}^{{\cal M}^{\prime}}(T{\bf u},T{\bf v})=F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})
and
F_{\mu,n}^{{\cal M}^{\prime}}(T{\bf u},T{\bf v})=F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},{\bf v})

(ii)

q^{{\cal M}^{\prime}}(T{\bf u},\tau)=Tq^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)
and
q_{n}^{\cal M^{\prime}}(T{\bf u},\tau)=Tq_{n}^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)

(iii)

R_{\mu,n}^{{\cal M}^{\prime};{\rm loc}}(T{\bf u},T{\bf X}_{j})=R_{\mu,n}^{{\cal M};{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})
and
S_{\mu,n}^{{\cal M}^{\prime};{\rm loc}}(T{\bf u},T{\bf X}_{j})=S_{\mu,n}^{{\cal M};{\rm loc}}({\bf u},{\bf X}_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.

4
Global metric quantile and its empirical version

4.1
Global metric quantile

In contrast to the local metric quantile in Section
3
, this section defines the quantile from a global perspective. Noticing that
F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf X},{\bf u})
provides probability information for
{\bf u}
locally at
{\bf X}
, to define the global quantile, we consider aggregating the local information of
F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf X},{\bf u})
for all points
{\bf X}\in{\cal M}
. To this end, define
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})\coloneqq{\rm E}(F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf X},{\bf u}))
which, intuitively, provides an averaged “population ranking” information of
{\bf u}
when evaluating at each point in the metric space; in the terminology of sociology, it measures how “popular”
{\bf u}
is when voted by all
{\bf X}\in{\cal M}
. As a simple illustration of the intuition, we simulate i.i.d. samples from the standard spherical Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
(see Section
in the Supplementary material for details of the vMF distribution). We compute
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u}):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf X}_{i},{\bf u})
, with
n=1000
, as an estimate of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
. In Figure
1
(the left/right panel is for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
/
\mathcal{S}^{2}
), values of
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
of some points are marked in red. Notice that, in Figure
1
,
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
increases as
{\bf u}
moves away from the center. This motivates our definition of global metric quantile, which essentially relies on the ordering of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
for each
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
. Letting
L\coloneqq\underset{{\bf u}\in{\cal M}}{\min}J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
and
U\coloneqq\underset{{\bf u}\in{\cal M}}{\max}J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
, we make the following assumption.

Plots of samples generated from the standard spherical Gaussian distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(left panel) and the von Mises-Fisher distribution in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
(right panel), with estimates of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
marked in red at some points.

Assumption 4

L\neq U
.

We remark that in the case that
L=U
, the global quantiles may simply be defined as the local quantiles
q^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)
by choosing any point
{\bf u}
on
{\cal M}
, since
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
is uniformly distributed over
{\cal M}
and any
{\bf u}
would provide the same global information. For instance, when
{\cal M}
is the unit sphere and
{\bf X}
is uniformly distributed, one may choose any
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
as the
0
th global quantile and define the
\tau
th global quantile as
q^{\cal M}({\bf u},\tau)
.

Since
\mu
is absolutely continuous with positive density
f
everywhere in
{\cal M}
,
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
has a positive density
f_{J}
everywhere on
[L,U]
. Denoting by
F_{J}
the distribution function of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})\in[L,U]
, then
F_{J}
is a strict monotonic increasing function of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}
. We define the global quantiles as follows.

Definition 4

The
\tau
th global quantile (
\tau\in[0,1]
) of
\mu
on
{\cal M}
is

Q^{\cal M}(\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf u}\in{\cal M}:F_{J}(J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))=\tau\}
,
the
\tau
th quantile region is
\bar{Q}^{\cal M}(\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf u}\in{\cal M}:F_{J}(J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))\leq\tau\}
,
and the
\tau
th inner quantile region is

\tilde{Q}^{\cal M}(\tau)\coloneqq\{{\bf u}\in{\cal M}:F_{J}(J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))<\tau\}.

Similar to the local quantile regions, the global quantile regions are also nested, and their intersection is
0
th global quantile region, which is a compact set of
{\bf u}\in{\cal M}
that minimizes
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
. This minimizer is called
metric median
, which may be unique. For example, when
{\cal M}=\mathcal{S}^{p}
and
\mu
is rotationally symmetric with respect to the pole, the metric median is a parallel or a collection of parallels. Proposition
6
states the nestedness property of the global quantile regions. Therefore, the global quantiles induce a center-outward ordering for the metric space
{\cal M}
. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 6

If Assumptions
3
and
4
hold, then for
0\leq\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}\leq 1
,
\bar{Q}^{\cal M}(\tau_{1})\subsetneq\bar{Q}^{\cal M}(\tau_{2})
.

4.2
Empirical global metric quantile

Let
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf X}_{i},{\bf u})
, which can be considered as an approximation to
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})\coloneqq{\rm E}(F_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf X},{\bf u}))
, and denote by

F_{J_{n}}^{(n)}(J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf X}_{i})\leq J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))

the empirical distribution function of
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
. The definition of empirical global metric quantiles relies on the ordering of
J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf X}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,n
.

Definition 5

The
\tau
th empirical global quantile (
\tau\in[0,1]
) of
\mu
on

4.3
Global metric rank and sign

The canonical ordering of the real line makes it easy to define ranks and signs in terms of the sample order. In

Definition 6

and the metric sign of
{\bf X}_{j}

S_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm Global}}({\bf X}_{j}):={\rm sign}\left(\frac{nF_{J_{n}}^{(n)}(J_{\mu,n}^{\cal M}({\bf X}_{j}))}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)={\rm sign}\left(\frac{R_{\mu,n}^{\cal M;{\rm Global}}}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

4.5
Relation with depth function

Various types of depth functions have been proposed in order to tackle the problem of non-canonical ordering in multi-dimensional Euclidean spaces and non-Euclidean spaces, including halfspace depth
(
Tukey 1975
)
, simplicial depth
(
Liu 1990
)
, spatial depth
(
Serfling 2002
)
, to name just a few, in Euclidean space, and halfspace depth
(
Dai et al. 2022
)
and depth profiles
(
Dubey et al. 2022
)
in non-Euclidean spaces. In the context of Euclidean spaces,
Zuo & Serfling 2000
formally defined the statistical depth function which should possess four properties:
(i)
affine invariance with respect to some linear transformation;
(ii)
the depth function is maximized at the center;
(iii)
the depth function decreases monotonically along any fixed ray through the center;
(iv)
the depth function approaches zero as the point approaches infinity.

As mentioned in Section
4
, our global quantiles hinge on the ordering of
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\in{\cal M}
denote a point in the metric median
Q^{\cal M}(0)
of
({\cal M},d)
. Proposition
11
states that under certain conditions of the metric space and the probability measure,
D_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u}):=1-F_{J}(J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u}))
can be interpreted as a “metric depth” function satisfying the above properties of a depth function in an Euclidean space. See Section
in the Supplementary material for its proof.

Proposition 11

The metric depth function
D_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
satisfies

(i)

D_{\mu}^{{\cal M}^{\prime}}(T{\bf u})=D_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
under an isometric transformation
T:{\cal M}\rightarrow{\cal M}^{\prime}
defined in Section
4.4
;

(ii)

D_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})
attains the maximum value at any
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\in Q^{\cal M}(0)
;

(iii)

when
({\cal M},d)
is a geodesic space and the probability measure
\mu
satisfies
J_{\mu}^{\cal M}(\gamma(t_{1}))\leq J_{\mu}^{\cal M}(\gamma(t_{2}))
for any
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq 1
, where
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow{\cal M}
is an arbitrary geodesic curve starting from
\theta
, i.e.
\gamma(0)=\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\in Q^{\cal M}(0)
, then
D_{\mu}^{\cal M}(\gamma(t_{2}))\leq D_{\mu}^{\cal M}(\gamma(t_{1}))
;

(iv)

D_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf u})=0
for any
{\bf u}\in\underset{{\bf v}\in{\cal M}}{\argmax}J_{\mu}^{\cal M}({\bf v})
.

Suppose
({\cal M},d)
is the geodesic space as in part (iii) of Proposition
11
. Let

\Gamma_{\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))}:=\{{\bf X}\in{\cal M}:d({\bf X},\gamma(t_{2}))\geq d({\bf X},\gamma(t_{1}))\}\vskip-5.69054pt

and

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))}:=\{{\bf X}\in{\cal M}:d({\bf X},\gamma(t_{2}))<d({\bf X},\gamma(t_{1}))\}\vskip-5.69054pt

according to the distance to

\int_{\Gamma_{\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))}}\mu(B({\bf X},\gamma(t_{2})\setminus B({\bf X},\gamma(t_{1})){\rm d}\mu\geq\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))}}\mu(B({\bf X},\gamma(t_{1})\setminus B({\bf X},\gamma(t_{2})){\rm d}\mu

for any geodesic curve

and any

Metric rank-based independence test

Thanks to the distribution-free property, rank-based tests have been applied quite successfully in Euclidean spaces for various statistical models, including location
(
Hodges & Lehmann 1956
)
, regression models
(
Jureckova 1971
;
Koul 1971
;
Hallin et al. 2022a
)
, autoregressive time series
(
Koul & Saleh 1993
)
,
non-linear time series
(
Mukherjee 2007
;
Liu & Mukherjee 2022
)
, to name a few. In a generic metric space, due to the lack of the concept of rank, their application has been much less considered. Based on the global metric rank defined in Section
4.3
, we are able to construct rank-based tests for a generic metric space. In this section, we illustrate this point by considering a class of rank-based tests of independence.

5.1
Test statistics

Let
{\bf X}\in({\cal M}_{1},d_{1})
and
{\bf Y}\in({\cal M}_{2},d_{2})
be two random variables with distributions satisfying Assumption
3
. The problem of interest is to test the null

\mathcal{H}_{0}:{\bf X}\,\,\text{and}\,\,{\bf Y}\,\text{are independent}

based on
n
independent copies
({\bf X}_{1},{\bf Y}_{1}),\ldots,({\bf X}_{n},{\bf Y}_{n})
of
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
. Let
R_{ni}^{{\bf X}}
and
R_{ni}^{{\bf Y}}
denote the global metric ranks of
{\bf X}_{i}
and
{\bf Y}_{i}
among
{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}
and
{\bf Y}_{1},\ldots,{\bf Y}_{n}
, respectively. The rank-based test of independence we consider is based on the classical linear rank statistic (see, e.g.,
Sidak et al. 1999
), which takes the form

T^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{1}\left(\frac{R_{ni}^{{\bf X}}}{n+1}\right)\varphi_{2}\left(\frac{R_{ni}^{{\bf Y}}}{n+1}\right),

where
\varphi_{1}:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi_{2}:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are
score functions
satisfying the following traditional assumption.

Assumption 5

The score functions
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
(i) are square-integrable, that is,
\int_{0}^{1}\varphi_{\ell}(u){\rm d}u<\infty,\ell=1,2
, and (ii) are differences of two continuous monotonic increasing functions.

Assumption
5
is quite mild and is satisfied by all square-integrable functions with bounded variation. The metric rank-based test statistic takes the form

W^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}:=(T^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}-{\rm E}(T^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}))/({\rm Var}(T^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}))^{1/2},

where the expectation and variance are taken under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
In view of Theorem 6.1.8.1 in
Sidak et al. 1999
,
W^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}
is asymptotically standard normal under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The test statistic
W^{(n)}_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}
relies on the choice of score functions
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
. Below, we provide an example of a standard score function that is widely employed in the Euclidean space (see, e.g.,
Sidak et al. 1999
for the univariate case and
Hallin et al. 2022b
for the multivariate case).

Example
(
Spearman
score). A simple choice is
\varphi_{1}(u)=u=\varphi_{2}(u)
. The corresponding linear rank statistic is

T^{(n)}_{\text{Sp}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{ni}^{{\bf X}}R_{ni}^{{\bf Y}}/(n+1)^{2}

and the test statistic

W^{(n)}_{\text{Sp}}=\frac{12}{n(n+1)\sqrt{n-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{ni}^{{\bf X}}R_{ni}^{{\bf Y}}-\frac{3(n+1)}{\sqrt{n-1}}\vskip-8.53581pt

upto a scale factor
(n-1)^{-1/2}
, coincides with the the Spearman rank correlation.

7
Conclusion and discussion

In Euclidean space, quantiles, ranks, and signs are crucial to nonparametric reasoning. They serve as the foundation for numerous well-known procedures for testing hypotheses. These test statistics are, hence, conceptually straightforward, computationally efficient, and theoretically beautiful. We introduced local and global metric quantiles, metric ranks, and metric signs in metric space as their isometry-invariant counterparts. In addition to exhibiting the desired theoretical qualities, their empirical counterparts are computationally efficient. They facilitate the application of conventional nonparametric statistical inference methods to metric spaces. Consequently, these cutting-edge concepts open new research opportunities for metric space-valued data. For instance, in metric spaces, we can create a distribution-free test of independence using metric ranks or metric signs. Quantile regression, in which the covariates and/or the response are measured in metric spaces, is an additional challenging but fascinating research topic.
