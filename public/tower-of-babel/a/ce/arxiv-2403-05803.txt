Semiparametric Inference for Regression-Discontinuity Designs
Rong J.B. Zhu, Weiwei Jiang

Abstract
Treatment effects in regression discontinuity designs (RDDs) are often estimated using local regression methods. However, global approximation methods are generally deemed inefficient. In this paper, we propose a semiparametric framework tailored for estimating treatment effects in RDDs. Our global approach conceptualizes the identification of treatment effects within RDDs as a partially linear modeling problem, with the linear component capturing the treatment effect. Furthermore, we utilize the P P -spline method to approximate the nonparametric function and develop procedures for inferring treatment effects within this framework. We demonstrate through Monte Carlo simulations that the proposed method performs well across various scenarios. Furthermore, we illustrate using real-world datasets that our global approach may result in more reliable inference.

Causal inference; Regression discontinuity design; Partially linear model;
P
-spline.

Introduction

The regression discontinuity design (RDD) was initially introduced by
Thistlewaite and Campbell 1960
to examine the influence of merit awards on students’ the future academic outcomes. Since then, it has evolved into one of the most reputable strategies for estimating treatment effects across disciplines, such as economics, political science, and medicine.
In an RDD, units receive scores, and treatment allocation is determined by whether the score exceeds a predetermined cutoff value: units scoring above the cutoff are designated to the treatment condition, while those scoring below are assigned to the control condition. This treatment assignment rule creates a discontinuity in the probability of receiving treatment, allowing researchers to learn about the treatment effect by comparing units barely above and barely below the cutoff. For comprehensive reviews of the RDD literature, refer to
Imbens and Lemieux 2008
and
Cattaneo et al. 2019
.

The local linear or quadratic approximation
(
Fan 1992
;
Fan and Gijbels 1996
)
is a commonly employed approach in RDDs
(
Hahn et al. 2001
)
. Specifically, researchers typically estimate the treatment effect locally around the cutoff point, which depends on the chosen bandwidth.
Studies have investigated optimal bandwidth selection for local linear and quadratic regression specifications
(
Imbens and Kalyanaraman 2012
;
Calonico et al. 2014
;
Calonico et al. 2015
)
.
Another approach that researchers have explored is the utilization of a global polynomial regression approach
(
Lee and Lemieux 2010
)
. In practice, researchers often employ high order (up to fifth or sixth-order) polynomials,
choosing the degree of the polynomial using statistical information criteria or cross-validation. However,
Gelman and Imbens 2019
have argued against the use of high-order global polynomial approximations, advocating instead for inference based on local low-order polynomials, such as local linear or local quadratic models.

In this article, we delve into a semiparametric inference framework for RDDs, proposing a global estimation method, which could serve as an alternative to local estimation methods.
Our primary focus is on identifying the treatment effect within the framework of a partially linear model. This entails capturing the treatment effect through the coefficient of the treatment assignment variable in the linear component of the partially linear model.
By employing
P
-spline to approximate the nonparameteric part,
we reformulate the semiparametric RDD framework into a linear mixed model regression.
Subsequently, we derive the estimate of the treatment effect within the linear mixed model regression framework.
Theoretically, we establish the asymptotic normality of the estimate under regular conditions, providing an inference procedure based on this normality.
Empirically, our experiments on simulated datasets support the assertion that our global estimation provides a viable alternative to local estimation methods.
Furthermore, our examination of real-world datasets demonstrates that our global estimation approach may yield more reliable inferences.

The rest of the article proceeds as follows. Section
2
proposes a semiparametric framework that incorporates the partially linear model to estimate treatment effects. Section
3
discusses estimation and inference procedures. Section
4
presents the empirical analysis. Section
5
showcases the performance on real datasets. Concluding remarks are provided in Section
6
. Proofs are dispersed between the main text and the appendix.

2
A Semiparametric framework for regression-discontinuity designs

2.1
Setup and assumptions

Employing the potential outcome approach, let
(Y_{i}(0),Y_{i}(1))
denote the pair of potential outcomes for individual
i=1,\cdots,n
with
w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
representing the treatment received.
There are
n_{1}
units that receive the treatment, while the remaining
n_{0}=n-n_{1}
units serve as controls.
Each individual
i
is assigned only one treatment, denoted by
w_{i}
,
resulting in the observation of a single realized outcome,
y_{i}=Y_{i}(w_{i})
.

We study the RDDs, particularly the sharp case,
where the treatment is a monotone, deterministic function of a pretreatment variable (forcing variable)
x_{i}
being on either side of a fixed and known threshold
x_{0}
, i.e.,

w_{i}=\mathbb{I}\{x_{i}\geq x_{0}\}
.
Denote the conditional expectations of the potential outcomes as

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(1)|x_{i}=x],

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(0)|x_{i}=x].

The average effect at the threshold
x_{0}
is defined as

\tau_{x_{0}}=\mu_{1}(x_{0})-\mu_{0}(x_{0}).

We impose the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

Assume that the potential outcomes are modeled as

\displaystyle Y_{i}(1)=

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}(1),

\displaystyle Y_{i}(0)=

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}(0).

Assumption 2
.

Assume that the mean functions
\mu_{1}(x)
and
\mu_{0}(x)
are continuous.

Assumption 3
.

Assume that the model errors
(\epsilon_{i}(1),\epsilon_{i}(0))_{i=1}^{n}
satisfy the following properties:

\{(\epsilon_{1}(0),\epsilon_{1}(1))^{\top},\cdots,(\epsilon_{n}(0),\epsilon_{n}(1))^{\top}\}
is
an independent and identically distributed sequence with
(\epsilon_{i}(0),\epsilon_{i}(1))^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{2})
.

Assumptions
1
&
2
are standard in RDDs
(
Imbens and Lemieux 2008
)
.
In Assumption
3
, for convenience, we assume homoscedasticity in
\epsilon_{i}(1)
and
\epsilon_{i}(0)
.
Nevertheless, this assumption can be extended to accommodate heteroscedasticity in these errors, thereby allowing for two different variances.

2.2
Semiparametric RDD framework

Now, we introduce a semiparametric framework for estimating the average effect of the treatment.
Denote
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon_{i}(1)w_{i}+\epsilon_{i}(0)(1-w_{i})
, and
define

\displaystyle g(x_{i})=\mu_{0}(x_{i})\mathbb{I}\{x_{i}<x_{0}\}+(\mu_{1}(x_{i})-\tau_{x_{0}})\mathbb{I}\{x_{i}\geq x_{0}\}.

(1)

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold.
We have that the observations are generated from the following model:

\displaystyle y_{i}=

\displaystyle\tau_{x_{0}}w_{i}+g(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}.

(2)

Moreover,
g(x)
is continuous at
x_{0}
, and

\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{n}
are independent with

\text{Var}(\epsilon_{i})=\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

Model (
2
) is a direct result from Assumption
1
and the definition of
g(x_{i})
in (
1
).

From the definition of
g(x)
,

\displaystyle g(x_{0})=

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(x_{0})-\tau_{x_{0}}=\mu_{0}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}^{+}}g(x)=

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}^{+}}\mu_{1}(x)-\tau_{x_{0}}=\mu_{0}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}^{-}}g(x)=

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}^{+}}\mu_{0}(x)=\mu_{0}(x_{0}),

where the last steps in the last two equations above are from Assumption
2
.
The three equations above imply that
g(x)
is continuous at
x_{0}
.

From Assumption
3
, we have that
\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{n}
are independent, and

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\epsilon_{i})=

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\epsilon_{i}(1)w_{i}+\epsilon_{i}(0)(1-w_{i})]=\sigma^{2}.

Therefore, Theorem
1
is proved.
∎

Theorem
1
establishes the identifiability of the treatment effect for RDDs using the partially linear model (
2
), wherein inference on the treatment effect can be construed as inference on the coefficient of
w_{i}
.

3
Semiparametric inference

3.1
P
-spline approach on modeling
g(x)

We approximate
g(x)
using
P
-splines with the following function:

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x+\cdots\beta_{p}x^{p}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}(x-\kappa_{k})_{+}^{p},

(3)

where
p
(taking values of 1 or 2) represents the degree of the spline,
(x)_{+}^{p}=x^{p}\mathbb{I}\{x\geq 0\}
, and
\kappa_{1}<\cdots<\kappa_{K}
is a set of knots over the range of
x
.
Given the knots
\boldsymbol{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{K})^{\top}
and
p
, Model (
3
) use the truncated polynomial spline basis
\{1,x,\cdots,x^{p},(x-\kappa_{1})_{+}^{p},\cdots,(x-\kappa_{K})_{+}^{p}\}
to approximate the function
g(x)
.
Denote

\mathbf{x}=(1,x,\cdots,x^{p})^{\top}
,
\mathbf{z}=((x-\kappa_{1})_{+}^{p},\cdots,(x-\kappa_{K})_{+}^{p})^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\cdots,\beta_{p})^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{K})^{\top}
.
Model (
3
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle g_{m}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}.

(4)

Provided that the knots are sufficiently spread out and the number of knots
K
is sufficiently large, the approximation above is of a high level of accuracy.
The model may encounter overfitting issues due to potential over-parameterization.
To mitigate this issue, a penalty is imposed on the magnitude of the spline parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, treating
\gamma_{k}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\gamma}^{2})
as a random effect with variance
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}

(
Ruppert et al. 2003
)
.
Empirical studies by
Ruppert 2002
suggested that the approximation bias using the
P
-spline approach is negligible in univariate nonparametric regression cases. Theoretical analysis by
Claeskens et al. 2009
demonstrated that the bias is
O(n^{-(p+1)/(2p+1)})
assuming
\lambda=O(1)
and
K=O(n^{1/(2p+3)})
, which is negligible.

Ruppert et al. 2003
provided recommendations on knot selection. The knots are frequently placed at equally spaced quantiles of the distribution of
x
, with
K
chosen to be large relative to the size of the dataset.
For univariate
x
, a typical knot choice is to have one knot every four or five observations, with a maximum number ranging from 35 to 50.

The family
\{g_{m}(x)\}
includes functions with discontinuous derivatives or discontinuities themselves. Such occurrences naturally arise in splines when a knot is placed at the discontinuity location.
Consequently, our proposed method can effectively handle functions
g
with isolated discontinuities or discontinuous derivatives.

3.2
Reformulation to linear mixed model

As the
P
-spline approach can be viewed as random-effects models
(
Ruppert 2002
)
,
it is natural to reformulate the semiparametric RDD framework to linear mixed model regression.
Define
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}
,

\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{n})^{\top}
,

\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}
,

\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{z}_{n})^{\top}
, and

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{n})^{\top}
.
We treat the data as if they came from the regression model

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\tau_{x_{0}}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{K})
and

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
.

Denoting
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\tau_{x_{0}},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
,
we estimate the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\theta}
using generalized least squares.
In the model,
the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{y}
is given by

\mathbf{V}=\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}.

We have
\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{Q}_{z}
, where

\mathbf{Q}_{z}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}+\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\gamma}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{K})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}.

Denote
\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{X})
.
Assuming known variances
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
, we estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}
using the generalized least squares estimate:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{U})^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{y}=\left(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{U}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{w}&\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{w}&\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{X}\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{y}\end{array}\right).

The estimates of
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
can be obtained
using maximum likelihood or restricted maximum likelihood
(
Patterson and Thompson 1971
)
, which are implemented by the “nlme”
(
Pinheiro et al. 2023
)
or “lme4”
(
Bates et al. 2015
)
packages in R.
Once consistent estimators
\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
for
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
are obtained, they can be inserted into (
3.2
) to obtain the feasible GLS estimator.

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{w}&\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{w}&\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{X}\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{y}\end{array}\right),

where

\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}+\hat{\sigma}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{K})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}
.

3.3
Inference

Utilizing standard results on asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimates (MLE) in mixed effects models,
we present the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the following theorem.

Denote

\rho_{1}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n_{1}/n
,

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{z}_{i}
, and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}
.
We impose the following assumption for the theorem.

Assumption 4
.

(a)
K\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(b)
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}K/n=0
.

(c)
r=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z})/K>0
.

(d)
\rho_{1}
satisfies the following inequalities and
\rho_{1}>0
and
\rho_{1}<1/(1+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z})
.

Parts (a) & (b) in Assumption
4
impose constraints on the number of knots
K
.
These conditions require that the number of knots approaches infinity as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and that the number of knots is of a smaller order than
n
(i.e.,
o(n)
).
Part (c) permits the rank of the design matrix of
\mathbf{Z}
to be smaller than
K
but of the same order.

Part (d) imposes the conditions on the ratio of treated units. Specifically,

\rho_{1}>0
requires the presence of treated units in the vicinity of the threshold. Now, our attention shifts to the condition
\rho_{1}<1/(1+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z})
.
Denoting
\mathbf{V}_{z}
as the variance of
\mathbf{z}_{i}
,
we have that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}+\mathbf{V}_{z})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}}{1+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}}.

This leads to the expression for the condition:

\rho_{1}<(1+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z})/(1+2\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}).

As
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}\rightarrow 0
, the requirement simplifies to
\rho_{1}<1
.
Conversely, as
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}\rightarrow\infty
, the requirement becomes
\rho_{1}<1/2
.
This indicates that as
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}
decreases, the requirement on the ratio of treated units becomes less strict.
The term
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}
quantifies the dispersion of the data
\mathbf{z}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, within the space defined by the inverse of the variance-covariance matrix
\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}
. A larger value of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{z}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{z}
indicates that the data points tend to cluster closer to the mean vector, suggesting less dispersion or variability in the data.

Theorem 2
.

Define

(p+2)\times(p+2)
matrix
\mathbf{J}
given by

\mathbf{J}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{U}/n.

Under the model (
5
), assuming that Assumption
4
holds,
we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})\rightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{J}^{-1}),\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(14)

Proof.

The result in (
14
) from an application of
Miller 1977
. See details in Appendix A.
∎

Theorem
2
is proven in the case where the unknown variance components are substituted by the MLE estimates.
However, in the scenario where the unknown variance components are estimated using
restricted maximum likelihood estimation, asymptotic normality still holds, as demonstrated by
Jiang 1998
. Nonetheless, further elaboration is required for a complete derivation, applying the result outlined in
Jiang 1998
.

The primary outcome of Theorem
2
is to offer inference on
\tau_{x_{0}}
.
Noting
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\hat{\tau}_{x_{0}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
,
the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\tau}_{x_{0}}
is obtained from
(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{U})^{-1}
using the block-wise inversion formula.
Applying the block-wise inversion formula to
(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{U})^{-1}
implies that
its
(1,1)
-th element is given by

V_{\tau}=\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{z}\mathbf{w}\right)^{-1}.

Therefore, Theorem
2
implies:

\displaystyle V_{\tau}^{-1/2}(\hat{\tau}_{x_{0}}-\tau_{x_{0}})\rightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(15)

The variance
V_{\tau}
is estimated as

\hat{V}_{\tau}=\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}_{z}\mathbf{w}\right)^{-1}.

Hence, an asymptotic point-wise
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\tau_{x_{0}}
is given by

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\tau}_{x_{0}}-z_{\alpha/2}\hat{V}_{\tau}^{1/2},\hat{\tau}_{x_{0}}+z_{\alpha/2}\hat{V}_{\tau}^{1/2}\right),

(16)

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the upper
\alpha/2
-quantile from a standard Gaussian distribution.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct Monte Carlo simulations to examine the finite sample performance of our proposed method.
We carry out 5000 replications, generating a random sample

\{(x_{i},\epsilon_{i})^{\top}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
with a sample size of
n=50
or 200 for each replication.
The covariate
x_{i}=2z_{i}-1
, where
z_{i}
follows a beta distribution of
\text{beta}(3,3)
, and the error term
\epsilon_{i}
follows a normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma=0.2
.

In our method’s implementation, we set the number of knots
K=20
.
We also explore the automatic way of choosing
K

, yielding similar results.
Once

is determined, the knots are placed at fixed quantiles of
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
.
We consider two values for

1

Discussion

While local estimators are commonly preferred over global estimators for RDDs, this paper demonstrates that a semiparametric inference framework provides efficient global estimation for RDDs.
We have applied
P
-spline to represent the nonparametric part, but other nonparametric methods may be efficient as well. Further studies are needed.
We have examined this inference procedure within the classical setting of sharp RDDs.
It is anticipated that the semiparametric inference framework can be adapted for use with fuzzy RDDs with appropriate modifications. Further in-depth analysis is required to explore this potential extension fully.
Another potential extension is the regression kink design
(
Card et al. 2015
)
, where
Calonico et al. 2014
and
Gelman and Imbens 2019
recommend using local quadratic approach, extending the argument presented by
Hahn et al. 2001
.
Similar to our study on RDDs, our semiparametric inference may offer an alternative to local estimators in the regression kink design.
Furthermore, exploring RDDs with covariates
(
Calonico et al. 2019
)
and RDDs with a discrete running variable
(
Kolesár and Rothe 2018
)
could be worthwhile extensions to consider.
