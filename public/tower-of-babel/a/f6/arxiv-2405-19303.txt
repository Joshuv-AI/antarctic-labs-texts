Morse theory for chromatic Delaunay triangulations
Abhinav Natarajan 1,*, Thomas Chaplin 1, Adam Brown 1, and Maria-Jose Jimenez 2,† 1 Mathematical Institute, University of Oxford, United Kingdom. 2 Universidad de Sevilla, Spain

Abstract
The chromatic alpha filtration is a generalization of the alpha filtration that can encode spatial relationships among classes of labelled point cloud data, and has applications in topological data analysis of multi-species data. In this paper we introduce the chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations, which are computationally favourable alternatives to the chromatic alpha filtration. We use generalized discrete Morse theory to show that the Čech, chromatic Delaunay–Čech, and chromatic alpha filtrations are related by simplicial collapses. Our result generalizes a result of Bauer and Edelsbrunner from the non-chromatic to the chromatic setting. We also show that the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is locally stable to perturbations of the underlying point cloud. Our results provide theoretical justification for the use of chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations in applications, and we demonstrate their computational advantage with numerical experiments.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The field of topological data analysis (TDA) is founded on the idea of interpreting datasets as geometric objects that can be studied with tools from algebraic topology.
When the dataset is a point cloud
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we build a filtration of topological spaces, typically a filtered simplicial complex, to serve as a multiscale approximation of the hypothetical space from which the data are assumed to be sampled.
We can then describe the geometry of the dataset through topological invariants of the filtration, such as
persistent homology

[
ELZ00
,
ZC05
]
, that are stable with respect to perturbations of the data.

More recent data collection techniques in fields like cancer biology, geospatial analysis, and ecology produce
chromatic
(labelled) data with interactions between the points of each colour.
In these cases one is interested not just in the spatial structure of the data as a whole, but also in the spatial relationships among subsets of the data produced by the colouring; see
Figure

1
.

Figure 1
:

The point cloud above does not have interesting topological features
a priori
, but when considering the colouring, the blue points enclose a void that is filled in by the orange points.
This spatial relationship can be captured by the chromatic alpha filtration.

One way to formalize this is to study the inclusion maps among such subsets of points.
Mathematically speaking, a
chromatic set
is given by a set of points
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a surjective function
\mu:X\to\{0,\ldots,s\}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given sets of colours
I\subset J\subset\{0,\ldots,s\}
, the spatial relationship between the set of points with colours in
I
and
J
respectively is captured by the spatial properties of the inclusion map
\mu^{-1}(I)\hookrightarrow\mu^{-1}(J)
.
Here too
X
is represented by a filtered simplicial complex.
If the subsets in the partition of
X
produced by the colouring correspond to filtered subcomplexes of the original filtration—in other words, the construction of the filtration is functorial with respect to the input dataset—then we can study the induced inclusion maps among these filtered subcomplexes.

Common choices for building a filtration in TDA are the
Čech filtration

\Cech_{\bullet}(X)
, the
Vietoris–Rips filtration

\VR_{\bullet}(X)
, and the
alpha filtration

\Alpha_{\bullet}(X)
.
The Čech and Vietoris–Rips filtrations have the necessary functoriality property—indeed,
\Cech_{\bullet}(\mu^{-1}(I))
and
\VR_{\bullet}(\mu^{-1}(I))
are sub-filtrations of
\Cech_{\bullet}(\mu^{-1}(J))
and
\VR_{\bullet}(\mu^{-1}(J))
respectively.
Unfortunately, both of these constructions are filtrations of the complete simplicial complex on the vertex set
X
, in which the number of simplices scales exponentially with the number of data points
n
.
This makes their computation impractical for large datasets.
On the other hand, if
X
is in general position, then the alpha filtration, which was first defined in
[
Ede95
]
, is a filtration of the Delaunay triangulation
\Del(X)
associated to
X

[
Del34
]
.
The number of simplices in
\Del(X)
is bounded by a polynomial in
n
of degree
\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor

[
McM70
,
Sei95
]
, and when the points of
X
are sampled independently and uniformly from the unit ball, then the expected number of simplices of
\Del(X)
is linear in
n

[
Dwy91
]
.
This makes it feasible to compute the alpha filtration when the ambient space is low-dimensional.
Moreover,
\Alpha_{r}(X)
is homotopy equivalent to
\Cech_{r}(X)
for any
r\geq 0
, so the alpha filtration captures the same topological information as the Čech filtration.
Unfortunately, alpha filtrations and the underlying Delaunay triangulations are not functorial with respect to inclusions of point clouds; that is,
\Del(\mu^{-1}(I))
is not necessarily a sub-complex of
\Del(\mu^{-1}(J))
(see
Figure

2
).
This deficiency is addressed by the
chromatic Delaunay triangulation

\Del(X,\mu)
, introduced by Biswas et al.
[
Bis+22
]
, and the corresponding
chromatic alpha filtration

\Alpha_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
, introduced by
[
Mon+25
]
.
The chromatic alpha filtration has the following properties:

1.

\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
is a deformation retract of
\Cech_{r}(X)
for any colouring
\mu
of the data.

2.

Given sets of colours
I\subset J\subset\{0,\ldots,s\}
, the chromatic alpha filtration associated to the subset of points with colours in
I
is a filtered subcomplex of the chromatic alpha filtration associated to the subset of points with colours in
J
.

3.

For a random colouring, the expected number of simplices in
\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\mu)
is
O(n^{\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil})

[
Bis+22
]
, which is far fewer than the number of simplices in the Čech filtration.

Thus, the chromatic alpha filtration interpolates between the functoriality of the Čech filtration and the sparsity of the alpha filtration, while preserving the same spatial information.
In fact, the Čech and alpha filtrations are extremal cases of chromatic alpha filtrations (see
Section

2.4
for the precise relationship).

1.2
Contributions

The properties stated above make the chromatic alpha filtration an attractive choice for quantifying spatial relations in low-dimensional data.
However, the sparsity of the filtration is partially offset by the computational complexity of producing the filtration values themselves.
To simplify computations, we introduce the
chromatic Delaunay–Čech
filtration
\DelCech_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
and the
chromatic Delaunay–Rips
filtration
\DelVR_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
.
Under a general position assumption, these are filtrations of the chromatic Delaunay triangulation where the filtration value of each simplex is given by its filtration value in the Čech and Vietoris–Rips filtration respectively (see
Section

2.6
for the precise definitions).
Previous work has already shown the effectiveness of the monochromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration in machine learning applications
[
MM23
]
.
Moreover, the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is substantially faster to compute than the chromatic alpha filtration (see
Section

6
).
Our goal in this paper is to provide theoretical justification for the use of our constructions.

[

.

Our proof of

relies on the construction of a novel generalized discrete Morse function on the chromatic Delaunay triangulation that is structurally compatible with the Čech filtration.
The details of this construction are given in
Section

.
On the other hand, our proof of
Theorem

is an adaptation of the proof in
[
BE16
]
to the chromatic setting.
To enable this adaptation, in
Section

we use tools from convex optimization to study the discrete Morse-theoretic structure of a family of filtrations which includes the Čech, chromatic alpha, and chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtrations.
We prove that for each of these filtrations, its filtration function (which assigns to each simplex its filtration value) is a generalized discrete Morse function, and we relate the combinatorial structure of the corresponding Morse gradient to the geometry of the underlying point cloud.
The Morse theoretic structure of filtration functions has been described before for the Čech filtration
[

]
and alpha filtration
[

,

]
, and these results were unified in
[

]
.
More recently, it was shown in
[

that the chromatic alpha filtration function is a generalized discrete Morse function.
Our work simultaneously extends each of these results to a larger class of filtrations.
Finally, in
Section

we prove
Theorems

and

using the results from
Sections

3
and

.

Simplicial expansions and collapses are examples of
simple homotopy equivalence
, which is a combinatorial strengthening of the notion of homotopy equivalence for CW-complexes, and which is algebraically characterized by the vanishing of Whitehead torsion
[

]
.
In light of this,
Theorems

and

immediately imply the following result.

Corollary C
.

For any
r\geq 0
, each inclusion in
(1)
is a simple homotopy equivalence, and therefore each simplicial complex in that diagram has the same simple homotopy type.

Our result does not imply that there are simplicial collapses along the first column in
(1)
; indeed, our methods cannot be extended for this case, as we explain in
Remark

5.4
.

From a computational perspective, the main consequences of our results are:

1.

The inclusion of the chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtration into the Čech filtration is a homotopy equivalence at each filtration level, which makes the chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtration a drop-in alternative to the Čech and chromatic alpha filtrations for the purpose of quantifying spatial relationships through persistent homology.

2.

The chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration, which is related to the chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtration via nested inclusions (see
Section

6
), serves to approximate the Čech filtration for persistent homology computations.

To further justify the use of the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration in practical computations, in
Section

6
we show that the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration associated to a point cloud is locally stable to perturbations of the underlying point cloud.
In fact, we show that this local stability extends to maps between chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations that are induced by inclusions of the underlying point clouds (
Proposition

6.7
).
This is a generalization of the stability result in
[
MM23
]
, in which the authors prove that the monochromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is locally stable to perturbations of the underlying point cloud.
In
Section

6
we also document the empirical runtime performance of the chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations, vis-à-vis the chromatic alpha filtration.
The numerical evidence suggests that the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is significantly faster to compute than the chromatic alpha filtration, and is therefore a viable alternative for analysing spatial relationships in low-dimensional point clouds.

1.3
Related Work

The Čech filtration originated in the work of Alexandroff and Čech in algebraic topology, and gained importance in TDA due to the work of E. Carlsson, G. Carlsson, and de Silva
[
CCD06
,
SC04
]
.
Its foundational role in TDA is justified by the fact that the Čech complex
\Cech_{r}(X)
has the same homotopy type as the union of closed balls of radius
r
centred on the points in
X
(see
Section

2.2
).
Vietoris–Rips complexes originated in the work of Vietoris
[
Vie27
]
, and were popularized in the work of Rips and Gromov
[
Gro87
]
.
While the Vietoris–Rips filtration does not have the same geometric interpretation as the Čech filtration, it still contains meaningful information about the spatial structure of
X

[
Hau96
,
Lat01
,
Cha+10
,
ALS13
]
.
Moreover, it is much faster to compute the filtration values of the Vietoris–Rips filtration, and this advantage carries over to the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration.
The chromatic alpha filtration was first defined for the special case of a bi-colouring
\mu:X\to\{0,1\}
by Reani and Bobrowski
[
RB23
]
, under the name
coupled alpha filtration
.

The chromatic alpha filtration is also related to the Delaunay bi-filtration
[
Alo+23
]
, which is defined for a function on a point cloud, in the case when the class labels are hierarchically nested.
We leave the consideration of this special case for future work.
Aside from chromatic alpha filtrations, other approaches for studying spatial relations have been previously considered.
Bauer and Edelsbrunner
[
BE16
]
generalize alpha filtrations to “selective Delaunay filtrations”.
Given point clouds
X\subset Y
, they construct filtrations
\Del_{\bullet}(Y,Y)\subset\Del_{\bullet}(X,Y)
that are pointwise homotopy equivalent to
\Cech_{\bullet}(Y)
and
\Cech_{\bullet}(X)
respectively (see
Section

4.1
for more details).
We will make use of their construction in our proofs, but we remark that the chromatic filtrations in this paper are functorial over a larger class of inclusions (for example, see
Remark

2.1
for a precise description of the functoriality of the chromatic alpha filtration).
Stolz et al.
[
Sto+23
]
analyse spatial relations using Dowker filtrations
[
Dow52
,
CM18
]
and multi-species witness filtrations.
The multi-species witness filtration is based upon the witness complex
[
DC04
]
, which can often be seen as a Delaunay triangulation with respect to an intrinsic metric on the data points
[
Sil08
]
.

Discrete Morse theory, the analogue of Morse theory for finite CW-complexes, was originally formulated by Forman
[
For98
]
in terms of real-valued functions on the set of cells of the complex.
Chari
[
Cha00
]
showed that the theory could equivalently be described in terms of pairings in the face poset of the complex.
In our work we use a generalization of discrete Morse theory, proposed by
[
Fre09
]
, that considers arbitrary intervals in the face poset instead of pairings.
Similar ideas have been considered earlier in the form of the “Cluster Lemma”
[
Her05
,
Jon03
]
.
Separately, the idea of collapsing simplicial complexes along intervals in the face poset has been considered before
[
Weg75
,
Koz00
]
.

is the nerve of the collection
\{D_{\bullet}^{\prime}(x,X)\}_{x\in X}
:

\Alpha_{r}(X)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\left\{\sigma\subset X\mid\bigcap_{x\in\sigma}D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

(7)

By construction the underlying simplicial complex of the alpha filtration has vertex set
X
.

We say that a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
empty
of
E\subset X
if it does not enclose any points of
E
.
Then the alpha filtration can be equivalently defined by

\Alpha_{r}(X)=\{\sigma\subset X\mid\text{$\sigma$ has a circumsphere of radius $r$ empty of $X$}\}.

(8)

For fixed
X
the alpha filtration defines a functor
[0,\infty]\to\mathsf{SimpComp}
.
It is straightforward to check that the union of Voronoi balls equals the union of closed balls:
\cup_{x\in X}D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X)=\cup_{x\in X}D_{r}(x)
.
Then the functorial nerve lemma (Theorem B in
[
Bau+23
]
) implies that the alpha filtration has the same persistent homotopy type as the offset filtration.

The Voronoi domains comprise a polyhedral decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
called the
Voronoi tessellation
associated to
X
.
The geometric dual to the Voronoi tessellation is called the
Delaunay mosaic
of
X
and denoted by
\Del(X)
, and is a polyhedral decomposition of the convex hull of
X
.
If the Delaunay mosaic is simplicial then we call it the
Delaunay triangulation
of
X
, and in this case it is the unique geometric realization of
\Alpha_{\infty}(X)
whose vertices coincide with
X
.
Using
Equation

8
, the Delaunay mosaic is guaranteed to be simplicial if
X
satisfies the following general position condition.

(GP1)
.

We say
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
(GP1)
if for all
0\leq k<d
, no
k+3
points of
X
lie on a common
k
-sphere.

The condition
(GP1)
is mild; for example, it is satisfied almost surely when
X
is the realization of a stationary Poisson point process (see Lemma 2.1 in
[
ENR17
]
).

2.4
Chromatic Alpha Filtration

Now we describe chromatic alpha filtrations, which generalize alpha filtrations to chromatic sets.
We follow the terminology of
[
Mon+25
]
in this section.

Let
\mu=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s})
be a colouring of
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For each
m\in\{0,\ldots,s\}
we have a collection of open sets
\{D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X_{m})\}_{x\in X_{m}}
, that is, the collection of Voronoi balls of radius
r
with respect to the set of
m
-coloured points.
The chromatic alpha filtration is defined as the nerve of the union of this collection
\cup_{m=0}^{s}\{D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X_{m})\}_{x\in X_{m}}
(see
Figures

3(b)
,
3(c)
and
3(d)
):

\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\left\{\sigma\subset X\mid\bigcap_{x\in\sigma}D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X_{\mu(x)})\neq\emptyset\right\}.

(9)

By construction, the underlying simplicial complex of the chromatic alpha filtration has vertex set
X
.
The filtration is invariant to a permutation of the labels
\{0,\ldots,s\}
and only depends on the set
\{X_{0},\ldots,X_{s}\}
.
As in the case of the alpha filtration, the functorial nerve lemma shows that the chromatic alpha filtration has the same persistent homotopy type as the offset filtration.

The alpha filtration and Čech filtrations are extremal cases of the chromatic alpha filtration.
When
\mu
gives each point the same colour (that is,
X
is monochromatic) then
X_{\mu(x)}=X
for all
x\in X
, and in this case the chromatic alpha filtration reduces to the alpha filtration.
When
\mu
gives each point a distinct colour (the
maximal colouring
) then
\dom(x,X_{\mu(x)})=\dom(x,\{x\})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and hence the Voronoi balls are just Euclidean balls:
D_{r}^{\prime}(x,X_{\mu(x)})=D_{r}(x)
.
In this case the chromatic alpha filtration is isomorphic to the Čech filtration.

The two most important properties of the chromatic alpha filtration are its sparsity and its functoriality.
For example, if
X
has
n
points and the colouring is chosen uniformly randomly, then the expected number of simplices of the chromatic alpha filtration is
O\left(n^{\left\lceil\frac{d}{2}\right\rceil}\right)
; we refer the reader to
[
Bis+22
]
for more details.
We focus here on the functoriality.

From the definition we see that the chromatic alpha filtration is functorial with respect to inclusions of points in the following sense.
Suppose
I\subset\{0,\ldots,s\}
is some subset of colours,
X_{I}\mathrel{\coloneqq}\mu^{-1}(I)
is the set of
I
-coloured points, and
\mu_{I}
is the restriction of
\mu
to
X_{I}
.
Then for each
r\geq 0
,
\Alpha_{r}(X_{I},\mu_{I})
is the full subcomplex of
\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
spanned by the vertices in
X_{I}
.

Slightly less obvious is the fact that the chromatic alpha filtration is functorial with respect to refinement of class labels.
That is, if
\mu=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s})
and
\nu=(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s^{\prime}}^{\prime})
are colourings of
X
such that
\nu\preceq\mu
, then
\Alpha_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
is a sub-filtration of
\Alpha_{\bullet}(X,\nu)
.
To see this, observe that for any
x\in X
, we have
X_{\mu(x)}\supset X^{\prime}_{\nu(x)}
and hence
\dom(x,X_{\mu(x)})\subset\dom(x,X^{\prime}_{\nu(x)})
.
Then if
\sigma\in\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
we have by definition

\displaystyle\emptyset\neq\bigcap_{x\in\sigma}D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X_{\mu(x)})\subset\bigcap_{x\in\sigma}D^{\prime}_{r}(x,X^{\prime}_{\nu(x)})

and hence
\sigma\in\Alpha_{r}(X,\nu)
.
An important consequence of this fact is that
\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
is a strong deformation retract of
\Alpha_{r}(X,\nu)
for every
r\geq 0
.
This is a consequence of Corollary 0.20 in
[
Hat02
]
applied to the inclusion
\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)\hookrightarrow\Alpha_{r}(X,\nu)
, which is a homotopy equivalence by the nerve lemma.

\Del(X,\mu)
is simplicial for all colourings
\mu

satisfies
(GP1)
for all
\mu
.
By the correspondence between stacks and spheres, this requirement can be formulated in terms of stacks.

(GP2)
.

We say
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
(GP2)
if for any colouring
\mu:X\to\{0,\ldots,s\}
, any
[s+1]

(d+s+1)
-points.

Notice that
(GP2)
is a generalization of
(GP1)
, in that
X
satisfies
(GP1)
if it satisfies
(GP2)
for the monochromatic colouring.
For our proofs we will also need a condition to ensure that the chromatic Delaunay mosaic has non-empty interior in
\mathbb{R}^{d+s}
if
X
has sufficiently many points.

Example 2.2
.

If
X
consists of 3 collinear points on the plane each having the same colour, then
\Del(X,\mu)
is a 1-dimensional simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
a=(0,0)
,
b=(0,1)
,
c=(1,0)
, and
d=(1,2)
.
Then
X=\{a,b,c,d\}
comprises the four corners of a non-rectangular trapezium in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
ab
parallel to
cd
.
Define the colouring
\mu=(\{a,b\},\{c,d\})
; then one can check that
\Del(X,\mu)
is a 2-dimensional simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The problem in the first example above is that
X
is not in general linear position, while the problem in the second example is that there are parallel linear subspaces (the lines
ab
and
cd
) generated by points in
X
.
To avoid situations like these, we will place restrictions on the linear subspaces generated by subsets of
X
.
The
lineality space
of
P\subset X
is the linear subspace
\Lin(P)
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(passing through the origin) that is parallel to the affine hull of
P
.
It is equivalently the set of linear combinations
\sum_{p\in P}\lambda_{p}p
of elements in
P
with coefficients that sum to zero:
\sum_{p\in P}\lambda_{p}=0
.

(GP3)
.

We say that
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
(GP3)
if for all
k\geq 0
, all subsets
P\subset X
with
|P|\leq d+k+1
, and any partition of
P
into

(P_{0},\ldots,P_{k})
, we have

\dim\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Lin(P_{i})\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\dim(\Lin(P_{i}))=|P|-k-1.

(14)

Remark 2.4
.

For any
P\subset X
with
|P|\leq d+k+1
and a partition of
P
into
k+1
non-empty subsets
(P_{0},\ldots,P_{k})
, basic linear algebra shows that

\displaystyle\dim\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Lin(P_{i})\right)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\dim(\Lin(P_{i}))\leq|P|-k-1.

Thus,
(GP3)
is the assertion that the inequalities in the above expression are equalities.
The second inequality is an equality if and only if each
P_{i}
is an affinely independent set, and taken over arbitrary
P
and
k=0
, this ensures that
X
is in general linear position.
The first inequality is an equality if and only if
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Lin(P_{i})
is a direct sum, which addresses the issue of parallel linear subspaces.

Example 2.5
.

A set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies
(GP3)
if and only if no three points are collinear and no two lines formed by pairs of points are parallel.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\mu:X\to\{0,\ldots,s\}
be any colouring of
X
.
Suppose
X
satisfies

.
Then the affine hull of
X^{\mu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+s}
has dimension

Proof.

We will find set of
k+1

that are affinely independent.
Choose a subset
P^{\mu}=\{x_{0}^{\mu},\ldots,x_{k}^{\mu}\}
of points in

that contains a point of every colour, i.e.,

.
Suppose there exist coefficients
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{\mu}=0
.
We will show that each
\lambda_{i}=0
, thereby showing affine independence of
P^{\mu}
.

Now notice that for each

, by construction we have

where the unit entry appears in the

th

Therefore, we must have
\sum_{\mu(x_{j})=m}\lambda_{j}=0
for each
i\in I
.
Let

.
Now

is the projection of

to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d+s}

0=\sum_{x_{i}\in P}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{\mu}\implies\sum_{i\in I}y_{i}=0.

Since
X
satisfies
(GP3)
, we have that
y_{i}=0
for each

.
Moreover,
(GP3)
also implies affine independence of each

\lambda_{i}=0
for all

.
This shows that

is an affinely independent set as desired.

2.8
Generalized Discrete Morse Theory

Let
K
be a simplicial complex.
Let
\mathcal{H}(K)
be the Hasse diagram of the co-face poset of
K
, that is, the directed graph whose vertices are simplices in
K
with a directed edge
\sigma\to\tau
if
\sigma^{(p+1)}>\tau^{(p)}
.
An
interval
in
\mathcal{H}(K)
is a collection of simplices of
K
of the form

[\tau,\sigma]=\{\eta\in K\mid\tau\leq\eta\leq\sigma\}.

(20)

A
generalized discrete vector field

V
is a partition of the vertices of
\mathcal{H}(K)
by intervals.
If
V
is a generalized discrete vector field, then a function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}
is a
generalized discrete Morse function
with
generalized discrete Morse gradient

V
if the following are true:

1.

f
is monotonic on
\mathcal{H}(K)
;

2.

For any pair
\tau\leq\sigma
,
f(\sigma)=f(\tau)
if and only if
\sigma
and
\tau
belong to a common interval in
V
.

In this case the singleton intervals in
V
are called the
critical simplices
of
V
or
f
, and we denote the set of critical simplices by
\Crit(V)
or
\Crit(f)
.
Under these definitions, a discrete Morse gradient in the sense of Forman’s original discrete Morse theory
[
For98
]
is nothing but a generalized gradient in which every interval has at most two simplices.
For simplicity, we will use the terms ‘generalized vector fields’, ‘generalized gradients’, and ‘generalized Morse functions’, with the implicit understanding that these refer to the discrete setting in this paper.
Generalized gradients are characterized by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.13
(Generalised Gradient Acyclicity Lemma).

Let
V
be a generalized vector field on a simplicial complex
K
.
Let
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
be the quotient graph induced by the equivalence relation on
K
defined by the intervals in
V
.
Then
V
is the generalized gradient of some generalized Morse function
f
(not necessarily unique) if and only if
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
is a directed acyclic graph.

Proof.

If
V
is the generalized gradient of
f
, then
f
descends to a well-defined function on
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
since it is constant on intervals in
V
.
Furthermore,
f
is strictly increasing across the edges of
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
, so
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
must be acyclic.
Conversely, if
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
is directed acyclic, let
P
be the poset defined by the reachability graph of
\mathcal{H}(K)/V
.
Choose a linear extension of
P
, and for
x\in\mathcal{H}(K)/V
let
f(x)
be the height of
x
in this linear extension.
Then
f
is a generalized Morse function on
K
whose generalized gradient is
V
.
∎

Generalized gradients are primarily useful because they can encode sequences of elementary simplicial collapses.
We have the following proposition
[
BE16
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem 2.14
(Generalised Gradient Collapsing Theorem).

Let
K
be a simplicial complex with a subcomplex
L
.
Then there is a simplicial collapse
K\searrow L
if and only if there is a generalized gradient
V
(not necessarily unique) on
K
such that
K\setminus L
is a union of non-critical intervals of
V
.

Proof.

The reverse implication is simply Theorem 2.2 in
[
BE16
]
, and we lay out a proof here for clarity.
The gradient
V
can be refined to a set
V^{\prime}
of intervals of length two as follows.
For each interval
[\tau,\sigma]
, choose an arbitrary
x\in\sigma\setminus\tau
and partition
[\tau,\sigma]
into pairs
\{\eta\setminus\{x\},\eta\cup\{x\}\}
for all
\eta\in[\tau,\sigma]
.
The set of pairs thus obtained is a genuine discrete Morse gradient (i.e., non-generalised) by the Cluster Lemma
[
Her05
,
Jon03
]
, and clearly
\Crit(V)=\Crit(V^{\prime})
.
It follows from the results in
[
For98
]
that there is a simplicial collapse
K\searrow L
, and the pairs in
V^{\prime}
correspond to the elementary collapses in the sequence of elementary collapses that define
K\searrow L
.

For the forward implication of the theorem, we induct on the number of elementary collapses in the collapsing sequence
K\searrow L
.
First suppose
K=L\sqcup\{\tau,\sigma\}
.
Then it is straightforward to check that
V=\{[\tau,\sigma]\}\cup L
is a generalized gradient on
K
with
\Crit(V)=L
.
The result then follows using induction and the Gradient Composition Lemma
[
BE16
, Lemma 5.2]
.
∎

In Forman’s original theory, the restriction of a discrete Morse function to a subcomplex is also a discrete Morse function
[
For98
, Lemma 4.1]
.
The following lemma is the analogue of that result for the generalized case, and the proof is a straightforward verification.

Lemma 2.15
(Generalised Gradient Restriction Lemma).

Let
V
be a generalized gradient on a simplicial complex
K
and let
L
be a subcomplex of
K
such that
K\setminus L
is a union of intervals of
V
.
Then the set of intervals of
V
contained in
L
is a generalized gradient on
L
which we denote as
V_{L}
, and
\Crit(V_{L})=\Crit(V)\cap L
.

The following lemma, stated in
[
BE16
]
, allows us to obtain a common refinement of a family of generalized gradients.

Lemma 2.16
(Sum Refinement Lemma).

Suppose
f:K\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:L\to\mathbb{R}
are generalized Morse functions with generalized gradients
V
and
W
respectively. Then
f+g
is a generalized Morse function on
K\cap L
whose generalized gradient is given by

Z=\{I\cap J\mid I\in V,J\in W,I\cap J\neq\emptyset\}.

(21)

2.9
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions

In
Section

4
we will show that the chromatic alpha filtration function is a generalized discrete Morse function.
A key observation is that the filtration value of each simplex
\sigma\in\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\mu)
is the solution to a convex optimization problem with affine constraints.
Such problems have solutions that are characterized by the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
[
Kar39
,
KT51
]
, which are a generalization of the method of Lagrange multipliers to non-smooth convex functions.
To state the KKT conditions we need a non-smooth analogue of the differential of a function.
If
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex function then the
sub-differential
of
f

[
MN14
, Chapter 2]
at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the closed convex set
\partial f(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

\partial f(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid f(x+h)\geq f(x)+\langle v,h\rangle\text{ for all }h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

(22)

If
f
is a smooth function then its sub-differential coincides with the ordinary gradient, i.e.,
\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}

[
MN14
, Proposition 2.36]
.

Theorem 2.17
(KKT Conditions).

Let
f
be a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
be finite disjoint sets, let
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{I}\sqcup\mathcal{J}
, and let
\{g_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
be a set of affine linear functions. Then
x_{*}
is a minimum of the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{crcll}\textnormal{minimise}&f(x)\\
\textnormal{subject to}&g_{i}(x)&\geq&0&\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{I},\\
&g_{j}(x)&=&0&\text{ for all }j\in\mathcal{J},\end{array}

if and only if there exist scalars
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that the following conditions hold:

Primal feasibility

g_{i}(x_{*})\geq 0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
and
g_{j}(x_{*})=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}
.

Stationarity

The zero vector belongs to the set
\partial f(x_{*})+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\lambda_{k}\partial g_{k}(x_{*})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\partial f
is the sub-differential of
f
and
\partial g_{k}
is the gradient of
g_{k}
.

Dual feasibility

\lambda_{i}\leq 0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Complementary slackness

\lambda_{i}g_{i}(x_{*})=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

We will also need the following well-known lemma from the theory of convex sub-differentials
[
MN14
, Theorem 2.93]
.

Lemma 2.18
.

Suppose
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
is a set of convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f(x)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\max_{1\leq k\leq m}f_{k}(x)
.
Then
\partial f(x)
is the convex hull of the union of sub-differentials of the active functions of
f
at
x
, i.e.,

\partial f(x)=\Conv\left\{\bigcup_{f_{k}(x)=f(x)}\partial f_{k}(x)\right\}.

(23)

3
Collapsing the Chromatic Delaunay Triangulation

Throughout this section, we fix a point cloud

in general position.
The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in general position, and
\mu
and
\nu
are colourings of
X
such that
\nu
is a refinement of
\mu
.
Then there is a simplicial collapse
\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\nu)\searrow\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\mu)
.

We will construct a generalized discrete Morse gradient that induces the collapse in the theorem.
We begin with a series of observations to reformulate the problem.
Since
X
is in general position, we have isomorphisms
\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\mu)\cong\Del(X,\mu)
and
\Alpha_{\infty}(X,\nu)\cong\Del(X,\nu)
as abstract simplicial complexes.
By
Remark

2.9
there is an embedding
\iota:\Del(X,\mu)\hookrightarrow\Del(X,\nu)
.
Therefore, it suffices to exhibit a simplicial collapse
\Del(X,\nu)\searrow\iota(\Del(X,\mu))
.

Next, we may assume that
\nu
is an
elementary refinement
of
\mu
, by which we mean that
\nu
is obtained from
\mu
by splitting up some class.
More precisely if
\mu=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s})
and
\nu=(X^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}})
then we may assume that
\nu\preceq\mu
and
s^{\prime}=s+1
.
This is because an arbitrary refinement
\nu\preceq\mu
can be written as a sequence of elementary refinements
\nu=\mu_{k}\preceq\ldots\preceq\mu_{0}=\mu
, whereby there is a sequence of embeddings
\Del(X,\mu)=\Del(X,\mu_{0})\hookrightarrow\ldots\hookrightarrow\Del(X,\mu_{k})=\Del(X,\nu)
.
Then the result for elementary refinements applied to this sequence yields the general result.

Since the chromatic alpha filtration is invariant to a permutation of class labels, we may also assume that
\nu
is obtained from
\mu
by splitting up specifically the last class.
In other words,
X_{i}=X_{i}^{\prime}
for

and
X_{s}=X^{\prime}_{s}\cup X^{\prime}_{s+1}
.
Then we are in the situation of
Remark

2.10
, wherein the chromatic lift
X^{\nu}=(X^{\mu})^{\zeta}
for some colouring
\zeta:X\to\{0,1\}
.
Using the definition of the chromatic Delaunay triangulation gives

\Del(X,\nu)=\Del(X^{\nu})=\Del((X^{\mu})^{\zeta})=\Del(X^{\mu},\zeta)\quad\text{and}\quad\Del(X,\mu)=\Del(X^{\mu}).

(24)

Therefore, we must show that
\Del(X^{\mu},\zeta)\searrow\iota(\Del(X^{\mu}))
, for which it suffices to show that
\Del(X,\mu)\searrow\iota(\Del(X))
whenever
\mu
is a bi-colouring of
X
.
Put together, these arguments show that

3.1
is a corollary of the following special case:

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in general position and
\mu:X\to\{0,1\}
is a bi-colouring of
X
.
Then there is a simplicial collapse
\Del(X,\mu)\searrow\iota(\Del(X))
, where
\iota:\Del(X)\hookrightarrow\Del(X,\mu)
is the embedding described in
Remark

2.9
.

The rest of the section is devoted to proving
Theorem

3.2
.
We will assume that
\mu
is a fixed bi-colouring of

.

6
Consequences for Practical Computations

In this section, we highlight the consequences of our main results for computational applications, for both the chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration.
We also prove that the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is locally stable under perturbations of chromatic sets.
In fact, we prove a more general result, which is that diagrams of chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations induced by inclusions of chromatic sets are locally stable to perturbations of the underlying point clouds.
We formulate and prove this statement using the notion of slice categories, and we refer the reader to
[
Rie17
]
for a reference on the latter.
Finally, we show the computational advantage of the chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations through numerical experiments.

6.1
Chromatic Delaunay–Čech Filtration

Given
X
in general position and
\mu
a colouring of
X
,
Theorem

B
shows that the inclusions

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{r}(X,\mu)\hookrightarrow\DelCech_{r}(X,\mu)\hookrightarrow\Cech_{r}(X)

are homotopy equivalences at every scale

.
Viewing each filtration as a functor
\mathsf{ChrSet}_{d}\times[0,\infty]\to\mathsf{SimpComp}
, and using the commutativity of

.
After composing with the homology functor, these inclusions are pointwise isomorphisms and hence together constitute a natural isomorphism.

Corollary 6.1
.

The Čech, chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic alpha filtrations have naturally isomorphic persistent homology.
More precisely,

H_{\ast}\circ\Cech
,

and

are naturally isomorphic functors
\mathsf{ChrSet}_{d}\times[0,\infty]\to\mathsf{grVec}
,
where

As mentioned in the introduction, the inclusion maps among the subfiltrations of the chromatic alpha filtration encode information about spatial relationships in the data.
If

is a chromatic set and

is a class of colours, then the inclusion map

This type of inclusion has some drawbacks: it effectively only captures pairwise interactions (the colours in

vs the rest) which do not carry information about higher-order spatial relationships, and it is asymmetric by definition.
To circumvent these limitations, Montesano et al.
[

]
define the

, which is the subfiltration of

of simplices coloured with at most

-chromatic subfiltration into

.
More generally, let

denote the abstract simplex whose vertex set is the set of colours.
The colouring

.

One can analogously define the

6.2
Chromatic Delaunay–Rips Filtration

In general, the Čech, Vietoris–Rips and Delaunay–Rips filtrations do not share the same persistent homotopy type; see
[
MM23
]
for concrete examples.
Therefore, the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration (of which the Delaunay–Rips filtration is a special case) does not in general have the “correct” persistent homotopy type.
However, the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration does approximate the Čech filtration, in the following sense.
De Silva and Ghrist
[
SG07
]
show that if
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}}
then for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
there is an inclusion of complexes

\Cech_{t}(X)\subset\VR_{t}(X)\subset\Cech_{\delta t}(X)

(47)

from which it follows that

\DelCech_{t}(X,\mu)\subset\DelVR_{t}(X,\mu)\subset\DelCech_{\delta t}(X,\mu).

(48)

Theorem

B
shows that
\DelCech_{t}(X,\mu)\simeq\Cech_{t}(X)
, so after applying the homology functor we get a commutative diagram in which the horizontal maps are induced by inclusions.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{\ast}(\Cech_{t}(X))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{\ast}(\Cech_{\delta t}(X))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{\ast}(\DelVR_{t}(X,\mu))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{\ast}(\DelVR_{\delta t}(X,\mu))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}

(49)

The top row of the diagram, as a persistence module, is the usual persistent homology of the point cloud
X
.
The bottom row is the persistent homology calculated using the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration.
This relationship is analogous to that between the Vietoris–Rips and Čech filtrations, which is frequently cited to justify the use of the former to approximate the latter.

Another justification for the use of Vietoris–Rips filtrations is that its persistent homology is a stable descriptor; the persistent homology of the Vietoris–Rips filtration is a Lipschitz map with respect to the Gromov–Hausdorff distance on point clouds and the interleaving distance on persistence modules
[
Cha+09
]
.
Unfortunately, the (chromatic) Delaunay–Rips filtration does not enjoy such a result since perturbations of the underlying point cloud can change the Delaunay triangulation (see
[
MM23
]
for an example).
However, we show that the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration, as a mapping
(X,\mu)\mapsto\DelVR_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
, is locally stable to perturbations of the underlying point cloud, and that this local stability extends to diagrams of chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations induced by diagrams of inclusions of chromatic sets.

To begin, we relate perturbations and Vietoris–Rips filtration values.
Let
X
and
Y
be point clouds.
Given any

, the

of

\dis(f)\mathrel{\coloneqq}\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\big|\|x-x^{\prime}\|-\|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\|\big|.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\Rad_{\VR}
denote the filtration function of the Vietoris–Rips filtration.
Let

and let

be any bijection.
Then for any

|\Rad_{\VR}(\sigma)-\Rad_{\VR}(f(\sigma))|\leq\frac{1}{2}\dis(f).

Proof.

This follows immediately from the definitions since, for any

,

\displaystyle\big|\|x-y\|-\|f(x)-f(y)\|\big|\leq\dis(f)

and
\Rad_{\VR}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\max_{x,y\in\sigma}\|x-y\|
.

In order to state our stability results, we need a metric on chromatic sets.
Suppose we have

and

If there are no colour-preserving bijections from

This means that, in particular,

We use

We also need a metric on diagrams of filtrations, for which we use the interleaving distance.
Here we recall the basic definition, and we refer the reader to

If

(X,\mu)
and
(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
be chromatic sets and suppose there is a colour-preserving bijection
f:X\to X^{\prime}
that induces an isomorphism
\Del(X,\mu)\to\Del(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
of simplicial complexes.
Then
f
induces a
\left(\frac{1}{2}\dis(f)\right)
-interleaving between

\DelVR\downarrow(X,\mu)
and
\DelVR\downarrow(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
(Y,\nu)\hookrightarrow(X,\mu)
be a morphism of chromatic sets in
\mathsf{ChrSet}_{d}
, and let
Y^{\prime}=f(Y)
.
Since
f
is a colour-preserving bijection, the isomorphism it induces between
\Del(X,\mu)
and
\Del(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
restricts to an isomorphism between
\Del(Y,\nu)
and
\Del(Y^{\prime},\nu\circ f^{-1})
.
The filtration values are determined by the Vietoris–Rips filtration function so

6.3
and
6.4
ensure that
f
induces a
\left(\frac{1}{2}\dis(f)\right)
-interleaving between the filtrations
\DelVR_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
and
\DelVR_{\bullet}(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
, which restricts to an interleaving between
\DelVR_{\bullet}(Y,\nu)
and
\DelVR_{\bullet}(Y^{\prime},\nu\circ f^{-1})
.
Since both interleavings are given by the same pair of maps, they also define an interleaving between
\DelVR_{\bullet}(Y,\nu)\hookrightarrow\DelVR_{\bullet}(X,\mu)
and
\DelVR_{\bullet}(Y^{\prime},\nu\circ f^{-1})\hookrightarrow\DelVR_{\bullet}(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
as persistent objects in the functor category
[(\bullet\to\bullet),\mathsf{SimpComp}]
.
Since
(Y,\nu)
is arbitrary, the interleaving is in fact an interleaving between
\DelVR\downarrow(X,\mu)
and
\DelVR\downarrow(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
as persistent objects in
[\mathcal{P}_{s+1},\mathsf{SimpComp}]
.
∎

Previous work has shown that if
d_{C}(X,Y)
is small enough for monochromatic point clouds, then the Delaunay triangulations of
X
and
Y
are isomorphic

]
.
In the following we show that this extends to the chromatic case.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
(X,\mu)\in\mathsf{ChrSet}_{d}
be in general position.
Then there is an
\epsilon>0
such that for all
(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\in B_{C}(X,\epsilon)
, there is a colour-preserving bijection
f:X\to X^{\prime}

such that
f
induces an isomorphism
\Del(X,\mu)\to\Del(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
and
\dis(f)\leq 2d_{C}(X,X^{\prime})
.

Proof.

A consequence of the results in
[
BDG13
]
is that
there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that
if
g:X^{\mu}\to(X^{\prime})^{\mu^{\prime}}
is a bijection and
\sup_{x^{\mu}\in X^{\mu}}\|x^{\mu}-g(x^{\mu})\|<\epsilon
,
then
g
induces an isomorphism of simplicial complexes

\Del(X,\mu)\cong\Del(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
.
Since
d_{C}(X,X^{\prime})
is finite and the set of colour-preserving bijections
X\to X^{\prime}
is finite, there is a colour-preserving bijection
f:X\to X^{\prime}
such that

\|x-f(x)\|\leq d_{C}(X,X^{\prime})
for every
x\in X
.
Now
f
also induces a bijection between the lifts
f^{\mu}:X^{\mu}\to(X^{\prime})^{\mu^{\prime}}
and moreover

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{\mu}\in X^{\mu}}\|x^{\mu}-f^{\mu}(x^{\mu})\|=\sup_{x\in X}\|x-f(x)\|\leq d_{C}(X,X^{\prime}).

Therefore, so long as
d_{C}(X,X^{\prime})<\epsilon
, the bijection
f
induces an isomorphism as required.
Finally, note that a simple application of the triangle inequality shows
\dis(f)\leq 2d_{C}(X,X^{\prime})
.
∎

Plot of median time taken to compute each filtration (including the underlying triangulation) vs the number of input points.

The worst-case computational complexity for each of the chromatic alpha, chromatic Delaunay–Čech, and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations is linear in the number of simplices of the chromatic Delaunay triangulation, and the constant factor is the worst-case computational complexity to compute the filtration value for an individual simplex.
This constant factor, which depends on the ambient dimension
d
as well as the number of colours, leads to practical differences for computations with large or high-dimensional datasets where computing the filtration values can be a computational bottleneck.

The algorithm to compute the chromatic alpha filtration value
[
Mon+25
]
for a simplex consists of two main steps.
The first step is to find the stack of minimum radius passing through the simplex, which can be reduced to the problem of finding a minimum bounding ball for the simplex with the centre of the ball constrained to lie in some affine plane.
The second step is to check if the stack thus obtained is empty, which involves iterating over the points in the co-faces of the simplex.
If the stack is not empty, the simplex is assigned the minimum birth time of its co-faces, which requires the computations to proceed from highest to lowest dimensional simplices.
Computing the Čech filtration value, on the other hand, involves finding minimum bounding balls for each simplex with no constraints and no additional steps.
The Vietoris–Rips filtration value is faster to compute since it only requires the comparison of edge lengths in the simplex.
In both of our constructions, the filtration values can be computed for low-dimensional simplices without needing the values for higher-dimensional ones.
This is desirable for low-dimensional data which only has persistent homological features of low degree, where only the low-dimensional skeleton of the filtration is required.

To test the computational performance of each filtration, we run numerical experiments using three different sampling schemes:

Varying the number of points:

For 10 values of
n
chosen log-uniformly between 100 and 10000,
n
points are sampled independently uniformly from
[0,1]^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and each point is independently assigned a colour from
\{0,1\}
uniformly at random.

Varying the ambient dimension:

For each
d\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}
,
200
points are sampled independently uniformly from the unit sphere embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and each point is independently assigned a colour from
\{0,1\}
uniformly at random.

Varying the number of colours:

For each
s\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}
,
500
points are sampled independently uniformly from
[0,1]^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and each point is independently assigned a colour from
\{0,\ldots,s-1\}
uniformly at random.

We record the time taken to compute the chromatic alpha, chromatic Delaunay–Čech, and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations for these samples.
These times include the time taken to compute the underlying triangulation; we also record the time to compute the triangulation as a baseline.
The process is repeated 5 times for each set of parameters, and we record the median time.

Remark 6.8
.

For the chromatic Delaunay–Čech and chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtrations, it is possible to compute filtration values in parallel across all simplices of the filtration.
For the chromatic alpha filtration, we can compute the filtration values in parallel across all simplices of a given dimension, proceeding from high to low dimensions.
These parallelizations are implemented in
chalc
using multi-threading, but the resultant speed-up does not scale linearly with the number of allocated CPU cores since our parallel implementations are constrained by memory bandwidth.
This also makes the gain from parallelization highly hardware-dependent, so the results we show are for the single-threaded implementation only.

6.4
Summary

The practical consequences of our theoretical and experimental results can be summarized as follows:

•

For low-dimensional data (
d\leq 3
), the chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtration is slightly faster to compute than the chromatic alpha filtration and has isomorphic persistent homology.
Moreover, the two filtrations induce isomorphic maps on persistent homology.

•

The chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is significantly faster than both the chromatic alpha and chromatic Delaunay–Čech filtrations.
The persistent homology of the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is locally stable and approximates the chromatic alpha filtration.
For higher-dimensional data or for many colours, the primary constraint for computing the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration is computing the underlying chromatic Delaunay triangulation.

•

The instability of the Delaunay triangulation increases with ambient dimension
[
BDG13
]
.
Since the chromatic Delaunay triangulation can be viewed as a Delaunay triangulation of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d+s}
, one should be wary of stability issues with the chromatic Delaunay–Rips filtration when
d+s
is large.

7
Future Directions

When
\nu,\mu
are colourings of the point cloud
X
such that
\nu
is a refinement of
\mu
,
our results show that
\Alpha_{r}(X,\nu)
and
\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
have the same simple homotopy type for any
r\geq 0
.
However, whether there exists a collapse
\Alpha_{r}(X,\nu)\searrow\Alpha_{r}(X,\mu)
for each
r\geq 0
remains an open question.
Even if the answer to the question is affirmative, in general there is no generalized discrete Morse function that induces this collapse for all values of
r
simultaneously, as explained in
Remark

5.4
.
This motivates the question of defining “persistent simple homotopy type”, and whether there is a corresponding algebraic notion of a “persistent Whitehead torsion”.

In practical applications, it is sometimes useful to assign weights to the data points.
There is a notion of weighted Voronoi diagrams and weighted alpha filtrations for weighted datasets; the extension to the chromatic case has not yet been explored.
Another consideration is stability; it is desirable that the local neighbourhood of stability of the chromatic Delaunay triangulation is as large as possible.
One possibility is to extend the idea of “approximate Delaunay triangulations”, defined in
[
Sil08
]
, to the chromatic setting.
It is also open whether the stability result in
Proposition

6.7
extends to a more general metric on chromatic sets, such as the constrained Gromov–Hausdorff distance for chromatic sets defined in
[
DRZ25
]
.

As mentioned in
section

6.3
, the filtration values for our constructions can be computed for low-dimensional simplices without needing the values for higher-dimensional ones, which is relevant for low-dimensional data where only the low-dimensional skeleton of the filtration is required.
Taking full advantage of this fact requires being able to compute the low-dimensional skeleton of the chromatic Delaunay triangulation; a possible approach is to use the methods recently developed in
[
CC24
]
.
Leveraging this approach is a subject of future work.
