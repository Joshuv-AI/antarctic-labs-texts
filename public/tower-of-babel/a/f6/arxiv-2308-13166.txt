Certainty Equivalence Control-Based Heuristics in Multi-Stage Convex Stochastic Optimization Problems
Chen Yan, Alexandre Reiffers-Masson

Abstract
We examine a multi-stage stochastic optimization problem characterized by stagewise-independent, decision-dependent noises with strict constraints. The problem assumes convexity in that, following a specific relaxation, it transforms into a deterministic convex program. The relaxation process is inspired by the principle of Certainty Equivalence Control, which substitutes uncertainties with their nominal values and requires the hard constraints to be satisfied only in an expected sense. Utilizing the solutions obtained from these convex programs, we propose two universal methodologies—re-solving-based and projection-based—to formulate feasible policies relevant to the original problem. These methodologies are subsequently amalgamated to develop a hybrid policy, equipped with a tuning parameter that manages the frequency of re-solving. We derive upper bounds on the gap between the performance of these heuristic policies and the optimal one. Under the Lipschitz-type regularity of the model, these bounds are proportional to the square root of the stochastic noise variance. Assuming additional 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} -smoothness regularity, an alternative bound, proportional to the variance of the stochastic noise, can be established—providing a refinement when variances are small. Our model provides a framework for dynamic decision-making under uncertainty, encompassing classic inventory and Markovian bandit problems while embracing a broader range of stochastic optimization challenges. We demonstrate our methods using numerical experiments on a network utility maximization problem.

Introduction

Multi-stage stochastic optimization, with its intricate mix of uncertainty and decision-making across time, serves as a cornerstone in a multitude of real-world applications, including supply chain management, power systems operation, inventory control, and financial planning (
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Pflug and Pichler 2014
,
Küchler 2009
). This paper focuses on a distinct variant of this type of problem, distinguished by convexity, stagewise-independent decision-dependent noises, and stringent constraints.

To tackle the inherent complexity of the problem, we utilize a specific relaxation process that transforms it into a deterministic convex program. This process is twofold. The first part, inspired by the well-established Certainty Equivalence Control (CEC) principle, replaces uncertainties with their nominal values—typically the mean—and acts as if these were the actual values. It has been demonstrated that, under certain conditions for the linear-quadratic stochastic control problem, this approach actually results in optimal control (
Simon 1956
,
Theil 1957
,
Duchan 1974
,
Runggaldier 1981
). The second part entails relaxing stringent constraints to be met only in an expected sense. This removes all uncertainties from the equation and offers a valuable approximation in handling intricate stochastic dynamics.

The potency of the approach discussed above, within a multi-stage optimization framework, has been well demonstrated in inventory management (
Kunnumkal and Topaloglu 2008
,
Jasin and Kumar 2012
,
Cooper 2002
,
Secomandi 2008
,
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
) and Markovian bandits (
Hu and Frazier 2017
,
Zayas-Cabán et al. 2017
,
Brown and Smith 2020
,
Zhang and Frazier 2021
,
Gast et al. 2021
) contexts, where the relaxed problems emerge as linear programs. Building on these successful applications, we broaden this methodology to encompass a more extensive range of problems, where the relaxed problem can now be a convex program. We propose two universal methodologies—re-solving and projection—to devise feasible policies applicable to the original problem. We then integrate these methodologies to derive a hybrid policy, featuring a tuning parameter to effectively regulate the frequency of re-solving.

In quantifying the effectiveness of these heuristic policies, we establish upper bounds on the gap between their performance and the optimal one. Establishing these upper bounds involves a meticulous manipulation of the propagation of stochastic errors, supplemented with concentration-type inequalities. Assuming Lipschitz-type regularity in the model, these bounds are proportional to the square root of the stochastic noise variance—a first-order estimation. An alternative bound, proportional to the variance of the stochastic noise, is established under the additional
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness regularity. This second-order estimation provides a refinement when variances are small. Such regularity conditions are met on the model, given that the relaxed programs satisfy specific constraint qualifications. This analysis is facilitated by tools from parameterized optimization (
Bonnans and Shapiro 2013
and
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Chapters 4,5)
).

Related Works and Our Contributions

Our problem assumes an inherent convexity, demonstrated by the CEC-based relaxation process, transforming it into a deterministic convex program. As per our knowledge, previous applications of CEC in multi-stage stochastic optimization settings, such as inventory management or Markovian bandits mentioned previously, restrict themselves to linear programs. The inherent linearity allows for scaling within the model, with the scaling parameter governed by the system size and facilitating asymptotic limit analysis when the size grows large. In a more generalized convex setting, however, such scaling ceases to be applicable in general. Therefore, we shift the perspective to consider a single stochastic problem, presuming that the amplitude of the variance of stochasticities can be reduced—an approach similar to a density model in the linear case (see Remark
6.5
for details). Unquestionably, the absence of linearity also leads to more complex analysis and calculations.

We posit that this convexity requirement, to some extent, represents the most general framework to consider under this methodology. Several factors underpin this claim. Primarily, Jensen’s inequality applies, ensuring the value of the relaxed convex program is
larger
than the value of the optimal policy, which is typically elusive. Without this relationship, the upper bounds on the performance gap developed in this paper would become untenable (see Remark
4.1
for details). The second factor is computational: convex programs straddle the boundary between efficiently solvable and intractable problems
(
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 2001
, Lecture 5)
. The appeal of applying CEC diminishes if significant challenges already arise at the level of solving the relaxed problems.

In our model, the constraints are "hard": we cannot tolerate any violations, even minor ones. A major challenge we face is that the relaxed problem merely approximates the actual problem, and the decisions derived from it are generally not even feasible for the original problem. Thus, devising simple, efficient, and high-performing feasible policies for the original problem based on the solutions to the relaxed problems is a central theme in these CEC-based techniques. Examples include the LP-index policy (
Gast et al. 2021
) and randomized activation control policy (
Zayas-Cabán et al. 2017
) for the Markovian bandit problem, as well as the booking limit policy and bid-price policy for inventory management (
Jasin and Kumar 2013
). Another basic idea, based on re-solving and originating from Model Predictive Control (
Rakovic and Levine 2018
), is also well-documented (
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
,
Gast et al. 2022
,
Brown and Zhang 2022a
). In this paper, we extend the re-solving concept, termed the "update policy," to a more general convex setting. Simultaneously, we also introduce, to the best of our knowledge, a new concept of projecting the relaxed solution onto the feasible set, which we term the "projection policy", thereby contributing another method for such constructions.

A noteworthy feature of our model, which sets it apart from classical multi-stage stochastic optimization problems as in
Shapiro et al. 2021
,
Pflug and Pichler 2014
,
Küchler 2009
, is the introduction of decision-dependent noise. This divergence is not purely academic; instead, it is motivated by a range of theoretical and practical applications we aim to address—from Markov decision processes (
Puterman 1994
), stochastic approximation (
Kushner and Yin 2003
), computational complexity (
Papadimitriou 1985
), to reinforcement learning (
Sutton and Barto 2018
). The decision-dependent noise renders various techniques used to handle a standard multi-stage problem, such as quasi-Monte-Carlo approximations and scenario trees, not directly applicable. However, an advantage of the CEC-based heuristics lies in their ability to treat decision-dependent noise in the same manner as i.i.d. white noise, under the assumption of uniformly bounded variances, which we adopt in this paper (see (
4
)).

Our second-order estimations hinge on local
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness regularity. This characteristic has previously been noted in the simpler linear program case, as exemplified in
Jasin and Kumar 2012
,
Wu et al. 2015
,
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
for the inventory model, and in
Zhang and Frazier 2021
,
Gast et al. 2021
,
Brown and Zhang 2022a
,
Gast et al. 2023
for the Markovian bandit model. These studies introduce this additional regularity under various names such as non-degeneracy or non-singularity, with their precise definitions varying across different papers and contexts. We argue that this regularity property can be expressed as generally as in any finite-dimensional variational inequality (
Facchinei and Pang 2003
), ultimately establishing a diffeomorphism that renders the constrained parameterized optimization problem locally akin to an unconstrained, parameter-free problem within a neighborhood of the origin in an appropriately dimensioned Euclidean space. We provide a unifying discussion in Appendix
EC.3
, building on existing works such as
Robinson 1982
,
Robinson 1987
,
Dunn 1987
. We anticipate this unified viewpoint may lend further insight into this property, especially considering its ability to accelerate convergence rates in the asymptotic regime (see Section
6.3
for details).

Remark 2.2

(Discussion on the Model Assumptions)

The convexity assumption, which plays an essential role in our method, will be justified later in Remark
4.1
, after introducing the certainty equivalence control on the problem. Formulated as a multistage stochastic optimization problem, the model has
stagewise-independent
noise
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
, Chapter 3)
. It incorporates two sources of stochasticity,
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathcal{E}
, that have distinct nature and are motivated from different application scenarios, as we shall see in the examples displayed in Section
3
. Clearly the i.i.d. noise
\mathbf{W}
is a special and simplified case from the decision-dependent noise
\mathcal{E}
. Throughout the analysis of the paper we are only concerned with their first and second moments, and it turns out that this subtlety does not play an essential role in our method. Additinally, if the model lacks decision-dependent noise
\mathcal{E}
, the Sample Average Approximation, a Monte Carlo method, can be utilized to approximate the optimal solution of (
1
)
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
, Chapter 5)
. Let us also note that state augmentation can be applied by defining
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})
. Under this setup, the
\mathbf{W}
-part acts as an exogenous and uncontrollable segment of the system state
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
, as seen in, for example,
Brown and
Zhang 2022b
. While this alternative modeling does not introduce technical changes to our subsequent discussions, separating
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{W}
allows algorithms to operate over a reduced state space
(
Bertsekas 2012
, Section 1.4)
.

In terms of computational complexity, already for linear two-stage stochastic programs with fixed recourse, it is shown in
Hanasusanto et al. 2016
that it is #P-hard to find an approximate solution with sufficiently high accuracy. In the same paper, it is argued that problems with non-relatively complete recourse is even more challenging to solve
(
Hanasusanto et al. 2016
, Theorem 4)
. Intuitively, this is caused by additional implicit constraints at each time-step to ensure feasibility in the future. One common approach to dealt with this issue is to incorporate a high cost for any violation of the hard constraints. Quite often this can transform the program into an equivalent problem with relatively complete recourse, but instead the reward function will have a huge Lipschitz constant
(
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
, Section 3)
. To avoid technical difficulties we hence opt to assume that our problem has relatively complete recourse.

For a general
T
-stage program with stagewise-independent and decision-dependent noise, it is shown in
Dyer and Stougie 2006
that the problem is PSPACE-hard, by treating
T
as an input parameter. The statue for the complexity of the simpler problem with decision-independent noise is still open (e.g. with only the
\mathbf{W}
-part in our model (
1
)). It is conjectured in
Dyer and Stougie 2006
that it is PSPACE-hard as well.
\square

3.2
Network Revenue and Inventory Management

In an inventory management scenario, at each time-step
t
,
\mathbf{X}(t)
represents the amount of
n_{x}
types of resource available in storage,
\mathbf{W}(t)
represents the quantity of arrivals of
n_{w}
types of (real-valued) customer demands, and
\mathbf{U}(t)
consists of two parts: the first part
\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)
represents the amount of additional resource
j
, for
1\leq j\leq n_{x}
, ordered to replenish the storage, at a cost of
c_{j}
; the second part
\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)
represents the amount of each type
i
, for
1\leq i\leq n_{w}
, of customer demands that we actually serve at time-step
t
, hence
\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\leq\mathbf{W}(t)
. Each type
i
of customer demand, for
1\leq i\leq n_{w}
, requires a certain combination
a_{ij}
units of resources
j
to be served, for
1\leq j\leq n_{x}
, and produce the amount
r_{i}
of profit to the decision-maker. While the customer demands that are not served leave the system and are considered as lost with no extra cost. In addition, the resources that are left in the storage incur a holding cost
h_{j}
per unit of time, for
1\leq j\leq n_{x}
. So to summarize the reward can be written as

R_{t}\left(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{W}(t),\mathbf{U}(t)\right)=-\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\top}-\left(\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)-\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{A}\right)\cdot\mathbf{h}^{\top}

where
\mathbf{A}
is the matrix with entries
a_{ij}
. There is clearly also the constraint
\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)-\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{A}\geq\mathbf{0}
. Hence the optimization problem (
1
) for this inventory management problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}(1),T)=~

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\mathbf{U}[1,T]}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\top}-\left(\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)-\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{A}\right)\cdot\mathbf{h}^{\top}\right]

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbf{U}[1,T]}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(1)=\mathbf{x}(1)\ a.s.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)-\mathbf{W}(t)\leq\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle-\mathbf{X}(t)\leq\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $2\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle-\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)\leq\mathbf{0},\ -\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\leq\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t+1)=\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathbf{U}_{1}(t)-\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)\cdot\mathbf{A}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for }1\leq t\leq T-1

which is a particular case with linear rewards, linear constraints and without the
\mathcal{E}
stochastic part. This is the model that has been considered in
(
Shapiro et al. 2021
, Section 1.3.3)
as a
T
-stage stochastic optimization problem.

Many of its variants have been studied extensively in the literature. Notably, the extension of the model with customer-choice has been considered in
Kunnumkal and Topaloglu 2008
,
Bront et al. 2009
,
Jasin and Kumar 2012
, which incorporate randomness from customer demands’ resource consumptions, and can be modeled using the
\mathcal{E}
-part:
\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)
is hence replaced everywhere by
\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)+\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{U}_{2}(t))
, where
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{U}_{2}(t))
are noises with distributions depend on
\mathbf{U}_{2}(t)
. A simpler model without the possibility of replenishing the storage has been considered in
Cooper 2002
,
Secomandi 2008
,
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
, using a re-solving policy similar to the update policy considered in the current work. A thorough discussion for inventory models can be found in
Talluri et al. 2004
,
Zipkin 2000
.

3.3
Markovian Bandits and Weakly Coupled Markov Decision Processes

A bandit consists of an integer number of symmetric arms, where each arm has
n_{x}
states, that evolves as a Markov decision process (MDP). The whole bandit itself can be seen as a large MDP that consists of
N
statistically identical sub-MDPs (arms), with
N\in\mathbb{N}
being an integer. The vector
\mathbf{X}(t)
represents the number of arms of the bandit being in each of the
n_{x}
states, that sums to
N
. An arm can undertake one among a certain number
n_{u}
of actions, where each action
j
, for
1\leq j\leq n_{u}
, consumes a certain combination
c_{jk}
, for
1\leq k\leq I(t)
of resources, with a total availability of
\mathbf{b}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{I(t)}
resources. In the meantime, an arm in state
i
, for
1\leq i\leq n_{x}
, undertaking action
j
, for
1\leq j\leq n_{u}
, earns a reward
r_{ij}
for the decision-maker. The vector
\mathbf{U}(t)
represents the number of arms undertaking each action, hence it will be more convenient to treat it as a matrix of dimension
n_{x}\times n_{u}
, and naturally
\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}(t)
. The resource constraints can be written compactly as
\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}\leq\mathbf{b}(t)
, with
\mathbf{c}
being the matrix of entries
c_{jk}
; while the utility collected at time-step
t
is
\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, with
\mathbf{r}
being the matrix of entries
r_{ij}
. Denote by "
\mathrm{Multinomial}(n,\mathbf{p})
" the multinomial distribution with
n
trials and probability vector of success
\mathbf{p}
. For
1\leq i\leq n_{x}
and
1\leq j\leq n_{u}
, the Markov evolution of
\mathbf{X}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\mathrm{Multinomial}(U_{ij}(t),\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{i})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}U_{ij}(t)\cdot\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{i}}_{\phi(\mathbf{U}(t))}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\mathrm{Multinomial}(U_{ij}(t),\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}U_{ij}(t)\cdot\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{i}}_{\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{U}(t))}

where
\mathbf{P}^{(j)}
for
1\leq j\leq n_{u}
, are in total
n_{u}
transition probability matrices of size
n_{x}\times n_{x}
, and
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{U}(t))
are decision-dependent noise obtained by substituting from the multinomial distributions their mean values
\phi(\mathbf{U}(t))
, the latter being an affine function. So in summary the optimization problem for the Markovian bandit model is

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x}(1),T)=~

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\mathbf{U}[1,T]}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}\right]

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbf{U}[1,T]}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(1)=\mathbf{x}(1)\ a.s.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{b}(t)\leq\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle-\mathbf{U}(t)\leq\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle-\mathbf{U}(t)\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{0}\ a.s.\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ },

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(t+1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{u}}\mathrm{Multinomial}(U_{ij}(t),\mathbf{P}^{(j)}_{i})\ \ a.s.\ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T-1$}

(14b)

which has linear rewards, linear constraints and without the
\mathbf{W}
stochastic part. Note that in the current model
\mathbf{X}(t)
and
\mathbf{U}(t)
are discrete and integer-valued, and does not fit into the continuous-valued setting we supposed in problem (
1
). We refer to Remark
6.5
for a further discussion on this issue.

This finite horizon Markovian bandit model has been widely studied in the literature, see e.g.
Hu and Frazier 2017
,
Zayas-Cabán et al. 2017
,
Brown and Smith 2020
,
Zhang and Frazier 2021
,
Gast et al. 2021
, as well as its generalization to weakly coupled Markov decision processes
Adelman and Mersereau 2008
,
Carpentier et al. 2020
,
Gast et al. 2022
,
Brown and
Zhang 2022b
,
Brown and Zhang 2022a
. In
Papadimitriou and
Tsitsiklis 1999
, a problem of routing and scheduling in closed queueing networks, called "Network of Queues" has been studied. This problem can be reformulated into the form of (
14
), by considering each class of jobs as a state, and each server as an action, with an additional action of being idle. The constraints correspond to the classes of jobs that a particular server can serve. By treating
N
as an input parameter, the problem is shown to be EXP-complete, provided that the horizon
T
is exponential in
N
, see
(
Papadimitriou and
Tsitsiklis 1999
, Corollary 1)
and
(
Blondel and Tsitsiklis 2000
, Section 5.2)
. The simpler "Restless Bandits" problem that has a single server is proven to be PSPACE-complete
(
Papadimitriou and
Tsitsiklis 1999
, Theorem 4)
.

4
The Certainty Equivalent Control (CEC)

The heuristic policies to problem (
1
) that we shall discuss in this work, i.e. the update policy in Section
5.1
, the projection policy in Section
5.2
, are inspired from the
certainty equivalent control
(CEC). The CEC is in general a sub-optimal control that applies at each stage the control that would be optimal if some or all of the uncertain quantities were fixed at their expected values
(
Bertsekas 2012
, Chapter 6)
. There is an additional difficulty in the current situation to apply CEC, however, as we are facing a problem with
hard constraints
that depend on both the current system state
\mathbf{X}(t)
and disturbance
\mathbf{W}(t)
, the
feasibility
of an action is hence of major concern. A key point in the development of the subsequent sections is centered around how to design feasible controls after taking the expectation.

Based on CEC, we apply the following relaxation to the original problem (
1
): define
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}(t)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathbf{x}(t)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{U}(t)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathbf{u}(t)
where the expectation is taken with the whole trajectory. From the convexity requirement made in Assumption
1
, these are well-defined system states and controls. By Assumptions
2
,
3
,
4
and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}\left(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{W}(t),\mathbf{U}(t)\right)\right]\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}\left(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t)\right).

(15)

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{t,i}(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{W}(t),\mathbf{U}(t))\right]\geq g_{t,i}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t))\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ and $1\leq i\leq I(t)$}

(16)

\mathbb{E}\left[h_{t,j}(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{W}(t),\mathbf{U}(t))\right]=h_{t,j}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t))\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ and $1\leq j\leq J(t)$}

(17)

By Assumption
5
, the expectations can be interchanged with the affine function
\phi(\cdot)
in (
1e
). By Assumption
6
, the stochastic parts
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{X}(t),\mathbf{W}(t),\mathbf{U}(t))
are with zero means. All these considerations lead to the following relaxed mathematical program with decision variables
\mathbf{u}[1,T]
:

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)=~

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\mathbf{u}[1,T]}{\mathrm{max}}\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}\left(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t)\right)

(18a)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbf{u}[1,T]}}{\mathrm{max}}$}][c]{\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\mathrm{max}$}][l]{\mathrm{\kern 1.00006pts.t.}}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(1)=\mathbf{x},

(18b)

\displaystyle g_{t,i}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t))\leq 0\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ and $1\leq i\leq I(t)$},

(18c)

\displaystyle h_{t,j}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t))=0\ \ \mbox{ for $1\leq t\leq T$ and $1\leq j\leq J(t)$},

(18d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)=\phi\left(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathbf{u}(t)\right)\ \ \ \mbox{ for }1\leq t\leq T-1

(18e)

The subscript "
vel-
" in the notation
V_{\mathrm{rel}-}
is to make contrast with the later notation
V_{\mathrm{rel}+}
, emphasizing the fact that the relaxed program (
18
) is solved without the knowledge of
\mathbf{W}(1)
at time-step
1
. More generally, for each decision epoch
1\leq t\leq T
, we write
V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T+1-t)
to refer to the relaxed CEC problem at time-step
t
with current system state
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}
and time-span
[t,T]
. Remark that (
18
) is a deterministic convex program. From (
15
) and (
16
), we see that in (
18
) we are maximizing a larger objective function over a less restrictive feasible region. We hence obtain the key inequality
V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x},T)\leq V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)
.

Remark 4.1

(Necessity of the Convexity Assumption)

The convexity assumptions made in model (
1
) merit further justification. Specifically, the concavity of the reward functions
R_{t}(\cdot)
, the convexity of the inequality constraint functions
g_{t,i}(\cdot)
, and the affinity of the equality constraint functions
h_{t,j}(\cdot)
as well as the system evolution function
\phi(\cdot)
, together ensure the relaxed problem (
18
) following the CEC remains a convex program. From a computational point of view, there exist solution methods that efficiently solve every convex optimization program satisfying very mild computability restrictions; in contrast, no efficient universal solution methods for non-convex programs are known, and there are strong reasons to expect that no such methods exist, see
(
Ben-Tal and Nemirovski 2001
, Lecture 5)
for a thorough discussion. So it is reasonable to remain in the convex paradigm, as otherwise even the relaxed problem may not be tractable.

More importantly, these properties are pivotal as they guarantee that in (
18
) we are maximizing a larger objective function over a superset of the original feasible region, securing the vital inequality
V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x},T)\leq V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)
. The significance of maintaining
V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x},T)\leq V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)
lies in providing an upper-bound estimate for the
sub-optimality gap
of any heuristic policy under consideration. This relationship is encapsulated in the inequality:

sub-optimality gap

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x},T)-\mbox{value of an heuristic policy}

\displaystyle\ \leq V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)-\mbox{value of an heuristic policy}

(19)

As previously noted in Remark
2.2
, determining an exact value for
V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbf{x},T)
to evaluate the sub-optimality gap is generally a complex task. However,
V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\mathbf{x},T)
can be acquired by solving a convex program, and the value of an efficient heuristic policy can be estimated via Monte-Carlo simulation. Thus, for the remainder of this paper, we will use the right-hand side of (
4.1
) as an upper-bound estimate of the sub-optimality gap, termed an
optimality gap bound
.
\square

5.2
The Projection Policy without Re-Solving

The update policy in Algorithm
1
requires re-solving a convex program that spans the horizon
[t,T]
at each time-step
t
. This re-solving procedure adjusts the decision based on current available information, and may help to avoid the accumulation of stochastic noises. Yet, from a computational point of view it is not efficient. In this section we propose the idea of policy projection, that replaces the task of re-solving a complex convex program by computing a simpler Euclidean projection.

As before, by solving (
18
) we obtain an optimal solution
\mathbf{u}^{*}[1,T]
with the corresponding
\mathbf{x}^{*}[1,T]
. For each time-step
t
, after the realization of
\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t)
and
\mathbf{W}(t)=\mathbf{w}
, the control
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)
is in general not feasible, as it was planned with respect to
\mathbf{W}(t)=\mathbf{\overline{w}}
and
\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{x}^{*}(t)
. In other words, recall the following notation for the set of feasible controls at time-step
t
:

\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\ \big|\ g_{t,i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\leq 0,\ \ \mbox{ for }1\leq i\leq I(t);\ \ h_{t,j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})=0,\ \ \mbox{ for }1\leq j\leq J(t)\right\}

(21)

then
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)\in\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})
but in general
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)\notin\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})
. The idea is that since
\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})
is the set of all feasible actions at time-step
t
, which is a non-empty closed set by our assumption, we may apply the Euclidean projection of
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)
onto
\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})
to obtain a feasible action, which is the closest feasible action to
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)
measured by Euclidean distance. Denote by
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})}(\mathbf{u}^{*}(t))
this Euclidean projection. It is the unique solution to the following convex program
parameterized
by
(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})
:

\min_{\mathbf{u}}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{*}(t))\cdot(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{*}(t))^{\top}\mbox{ subject to }\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\mbox{ given by \eqref{eq:feasible-set-for-projection} }

(22)

We apply this projected action to the system, and repeat this procedure at each time-step
1\leq t\leq T
. This gives rise to the projection policy summarized in Algorithm

.

Motivated by this observation, we propose a hybrid algorithm that combines the strength of both policies, which works as follows: we fix a tuning parameter
\Theta>0
and keep in the memory a deterministically optimal control
\mathfrak{U}
and system trajectory
\mathfrak{X}
from the lastly-solved relaxed convex program. We compute the Euclidean projection as we do in the projection policy. If the "deviation", denoted as
\theta
, of the system to its deterministic counter-part is greater than
\Theta
, then we apply a re-solving as in the update policy. In the meantime we also update
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{X}
in the memory. This is summarized in Algorithm
3
. We remark that as compared to the projection policy, the additional computation of
\theta
in the hybrid policy is almost free.

Note that in Line 7 of Algorithm
3
, we have defined
\theta=\left\|(\mathfrak{X}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},\mathfrak{U}(t))-(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}_{\pi})\right\|
as the deviation at each time-step
t
. We believe that this may capture more information than just using
\left\|\mathfrak{U}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{\pi}\right\|
. The latter quantity plays an essential role in the theory of
error bounds
, see e.g.
Luo et al. 1996
and
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Chapter 6)
, by considering a (non-negative) residual function such as

r_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{I(t)}\left(g_{t,i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\right)_{+}+\sum_{j=1}^{J(t)}\left|h_{t,j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u})\right|

where
(\cdot)_{+}=\max\{\cdot,0\}
. This theory aims at establishing bounds of type

\tau^{\prime}\cdot r_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathfrak{U}(t))^{\gamma^{\prime}}\leq\left\|\mathfrak{U}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{\pi}\right\|=\left\|\mathfrak{U}(t)-\Pi_{\mathcal{U}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})}(\mathfrak{U}(t))\right\|\leq\tau\cdot r_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\mathfrak{U}(t))^{\gamma}

(23)

with some positive constants
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
and
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
. An immediate application of these error bounds in the implementation of the hybrid policy is the following: the exact value of
\left\|\mathfrak{U}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{\pi}\right\|
is not essential, since we only need a comparison of
\theta
with an empirically chosen threshold
\Theta
. We may very well use the easily computable residual functions in the left and right hand sides of (
23
) to approximate the quantity
\left\|\mathfrak{U}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{\pi}\right\|
for this purpose, provided that these bounds can be proven valid. This saves us from the computation of an Euclidean projection, rendering the hybrid policy even more efficient than the update policy.

6.3
Interpretation of the Optimality Gap Bounds

In this subsection, we illustrate via en elementary observation as how the additional
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness can achieve for a refinement, and justify how and when this can be called a refinement via the previously studied examples in Section
3
.

For this purpose, let
Y
be a real-valued random variable with mean value
\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=y
and a small variance, with the meaning of "small" that will be made precise in the analysis. Suppose we are interested in finding an upper bound for the quantity
\left|\mathbb{E}\left[v(Y)\right]-v(y)\right|
, with a certain real-valued
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth function
v(\cdot)
. Let
\bar{v}
be an upper bound of the function
v(\cdot)
over the support of
Y
, and we suppose that
\bar{v}<\infty
. Let us choose a small
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
y
. If we only rely on the Lipschitz-continuity of the function
v(\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[v(Y)\right]-v(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{v}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y-y\right|\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y\in[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]}\right]+2\bar{v}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(Y\notin[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq L_{v}\cdot\varepsilon+C_{1}\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}{\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]}\right)}

(27)

with
L_{v}
being the Lipschitz-constant of
v(\cdot)
in the interval
[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]
, and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants from Lemma
10.1
. On the other hand, if we use the additional
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness, we have

v(Y)=v(y)+v^{\prime}(y)\cdot(Y-y)+\frac{1}{2}v^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\tilde{y}_{Y})\cdot(Y-y)^{2}

with
\tilde{y}_{Y}
being some value depending on
Y
. So we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[v(Y)\right]-v(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H_{v}}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-y)^{2}\cdot\mathds{1}_{Y\in[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]}\right]+2\bar{v}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(Y\notin[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\frac{H_{v}}{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}+C_{1}\cdot\exp{\left(-\frac{C_{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}{\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]}\right)}

(28)

where
H_{v}
is some upper found for the function
v^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
in
[y-\varepsilon,y+\varepsilon]
. If
\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]
is much smaller than
\varepsilon^{2}
, say
\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]=\varepsilon^{3}
, then the second term in (
27
) and (
28
) is negligible as compared to their respective first terms. If in the meantime, the value of
\varepsilon
can also chosen to be small enough, the bound in (
27
) is of first-order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, while the bound in (
28
) is of second-order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
. So in summary, a sufficient condition on
\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]
and
\varepsilon
to ensure that the upper bound (
28
) is a refinement to the upper bound (
27
) is
\mathrm{var}\left[Y\right]\ll\varepsilon^{2}\ll 1
. Note that this is
not
a necessary condition and in any case, a second-order bound, if exists, always provides an alternative estimation to the optimality gap. We refer to Figure
3
for an illustration of this effect in a numerical study of the network utility maximization example.

Remark 6.5

(Scaling under Affinity)

In Examples
3.2
and
3.3
, both relaxed problems corresponding to (
13
) and (
14
) are linear programs, enabling scaling of the model size, which in turn gives rise to a reduction in variances commensurate with the scaling. For instance, in Example
3.3
, the vectors
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{U}
denote the number of bandit arms in each of the
n_{x}
states and taking each of the
n_{u}
actions respectively, totaling
N
. Consequently, the vectors
\mathbf{M}^{(N)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathbf{X}/N
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(N)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathbf{U}/N
represent the corresponding
proportions
. Given that every (in)equality defining the optimization problem (
14
) is affine, dividing each formula by
N
, while allowing
fractional
numbers of arms, transforms it into an equivalent problem involving the proportionality real-valued vectors
\mathbf{M}^{(N)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(N)}
within some simplex with appropriate dimensions. Specifically, (
14b
) transforms into
\mathbf{M}^{(N)}(t+1)=\phi(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)}(t))+\mathbf{E}^{(N)}(t)
, where
\phi(\cdot)
represents an affine function and
\mathbf{E}^{(N)}(t)
serves as a random vector constituting the stochastic component,
\mathcal{E}
. Here,
\mathbf{E}^{(N)}(t)
adheres to
\mathrm{var}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]=\mathcal{O}(1/N)
, as proven in
(
Gast et al. 2022
, Lemma 1)
. Thus, as the number of symmetric arms
N
increases, a second-order result as found in Theorems
6.2
and
6.4
delivers a faster
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
asymptotic convergence rate for the optimality gap bounds, contrasting with the
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
rate seen in Theorem
6.1
and Theorem
6.3
. In fact,
Gast et al. 2022
has demonstrated that with a perfect rounding scheme to fit the original integer-valued problem (
14
), the convergence rate can be enhanced to an even faster rate of
\exp(-\mathcal{O}(N))
.

Similarly, in Example
3.2
for the inventory model without the option of replenishing storage, a common scaling method is to multiply both the initial resource and the time horizon by a factor
N
(not necessarily integer). As shown in
Jasin and Kumar 2012
, a faster
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
asymptotic convergence rate can be achieved if the relaxed linear program satisfies a non-degenerate condition. We emphasize that these efficient approximations do not contradict the computational hardness results, e.g. those mentioned in Section
3.3
, since the scaling regimes under the two situations are different. For example, the "Restless Bandits" problem in
Papadimitriou and
Tsitsiklis 1999
under this scaling involves identifying the best
5
,
50
, or
500
arms out of
10
,
100
, or
1000
arms, respectively. By contrast, identifying a fixed quantity of
5
arms, regardless of the total number of arms, is PSPACE-complete. Also, note that in the setting of a convex program studied in this paper, such scaling is in general not possible. For situations where the amplitude of the variances are kept constant, thereby making a consideration of robust optimality more suitable, we refer to Remark
EC.1
for further discussion.

8
Conclusion and Future Works

We addressed multi-stage stochastic optimization problems that exhibit a convex structure upon applying a CEC-based relaxation. Our framework not only embraces established models from inventory management and Markovian bandits, but also adeptly manages more intricate, non-linear problems like network utility maximization. The heuristic policies we introduced offer a quantifiable performance gap in relation to the optimal ones, while also balance between performance and efficiency. As we conclude, we chart a path for several avenues of future research.

(i)

(Infinite Horizon) In situations where all the data of the model (i.e., rewards, noises, and constraints) are time-independent, we can formulate the problem under the infinite horizon with a time-averaged reward criterion. Specific models of (
1
), such as the "Restless Bandits", have already been analysed within this framework, as seen with the well-known Whittle index policy (
Whittle 1988
) and the LP-priority policy (
Verloop 2016
). A distinguishing feature of the infinite horizon case is that, when appropriately formulated, we only have a single relaxed program that describes the system’s stationary behavior - significantly simpler than the finite horizon case, which is size
T
times larger. In principle, the idea from the projection policy in this paper still applies. However, it now necessitates an additional verification of asymptotic stability to ensure optimality. In a recent paper (
Hong et al. 2023
), which studied the "Restless Bandits" model, the authors circumvented this asymptotic stability condition using a coupling technique. Exploring how to integrate their approach into the more general setting in this paper is a worthwhile endeavor.

(ii)

(The Effect of Re-Solving) The numerical experiments in Section
7
indicate that more frequent re-solving always yields improved performance in this network utility maximization model. Additionally, the analysis in Appendix
EC.4.2
hints at the update policy’s potential for increased noise immunity. Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of the effects of re-solving is far from trivial, since it requires comparing different approximation schemes for a stochastic problem. Note that this question should not be conflated with the concept of time-consistency in multi-stage stochastic programming
(
Pflug and Pichler 2014
, Chapter 5)
. Although it may seem counterintuitive, more re-solving is not always beneficial - a fact underscored by counter-examples for the inventory management model found in
Cooper 2002
,
Secomandi 2008
,
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
. We plan to explore this issue further and report our findings in a follow-up paper, utilizing a broader framework than the one presented in this paper (convexity is not needed for studying the effect of re-solving). Our numerical experiments appear to suggest that the negative impact of re-solving is related to degeneracy, as we define in Appendix
EC.3
, in conjunction with the presence of stringent constraints.

(iii)

(Discrete-Valuedness) One limitation in our model is the assumption of continuous-valued data. However, in many real-world applications, the model is intrinsically discrete-valued, such as when the state is represented by counting numbers as in the Markovian bandit model of Example
3.2
, or when the action is binary as in some variants of the inventory management model of Example
3.3
. In many cases, continuous-valuedness is more a hindrance than a help, as unless explicit analytical solutions are available, continuous functions must be discretized for numerical solving. In our context, however, it is the inverse: discrete-valuedness disrupts the desired smoothness and convexity properties essential for our CEC-based analysis. Certain situations allow us to overcome these difficulties by relaxing discrete values, e.g. by permitting fractional numbers of arms as discussed in Remark
6.5
and
Gast et al. 2021
,
Gast et al. 2022
, or by treating binary action as a probability with a value between
0
and
1
, as in the probabilistic allocation policy in
Bumpensanti and Wang 2020
.

to

\mathcal{N}_{K}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\left\{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{d}\cdot(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}\leq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{y}\in K\right\}

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{0}\in F(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{N}_{K}(\mathbf{x})

(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Section 1.1)
. Suppose that
K

K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \big|\ h_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0,\forall j=1,\dots,J\mbox{ and }g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\forall i=1,\dots,I\right\}

where
h_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are affine and
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are convex and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth. Consider a convex function

Proof 11.2

Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mbox{SOL}(K(\mathbf{p}^{*}),F(\cdot,\mathbf{p}^{*}))

\mathcal{C}^{r}

\mathcal{C}^{r}

. Using the
\mathcal{C}^{r}

To summarize the ideas in this proof:

Under LICQ and non-degeneracy, a parameterized constrained optimization problem behaves like a non-parametric (from LICQ) and unconstrained (from non-degeneracy) problem in a neighbourhood of the stationary point in question.

This observation has played a key role in our refined analysis of Theorem
6.2
and
6.4
, which guarantees respectively that the policy mappings
S^{*}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})
and
\mathrm{Proj}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})

inherit the same level of smoothness of the corresponding objective functions. In general, explicit computation of a such solution mapping is not possible, as already can be seen from the classical IFT, which guarantees the existence but not a construction of the implicit function. Nevertheless, for the particular case of WCMDP in Example
3.3
, where the relaxed problems are linear programs, such solution mapping can be efficiently computed via inverting some coefficient matrix. This has played an important role in the implementation of an improved update policy for WCMDP depicted in Algorithm
2
of
Gast et al. 2022

It is important to note that both the LICQ and non-degeneracy are seen as generic properties for a
single
problem. While a rigorous formulation of this claim is not provided in this paper, we refer to a result due to Morse in
(
Ioffe 2017

and related concepts therein for a discussion. However, the conditions required for (

6.2
are notably more stringent: they necessitate that the LICQ and non-degeneracy conditions be simultaneously satisfied in a total of

and

11.3
The Lipschitz Property

In the literature, the Lipschitz property to the solution mapping is often referred to as a sort of solution stability
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Definitions 5.2.3 and 5.2.6)
or regularity
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Theorem 5.4.12)
, with the Lipschitz constant measuring the speed of change to SOL(
K,F
) when the pair
(K,F)
undergoes small perturbations. We first state two well known constraint qualifications that are weakened from LICQ.

a)

The Mangasarian-Fromovitz Constraint Qualification (MFCQ) holds at
\mathbf{x}\in K
, if

(i)

The gradients
\left\{\nabla h_{j}(\mathbf{x}),\forall j=1,\dots,J\right\}
are linearly independent.

(ii)

There exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\nabla h_{j}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\top}=0
, for all
j=1,\dots,J
, and
\nabla g_{i}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\top}<0
, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x})
.

b)

The Constant Rank Constraint Qualification (CRCQ) holds at
\mathbf{x}\in K
if there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon)
of
\mathbf{x}
such that for every pair of index subsets
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\subset\{1,\dots,J\}
, the family of gradient vectors

\left\{\nabla g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mid i\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\right\}\cup\left\{\nabla h_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mid j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}\right\}

has the same rank for all
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon)\cap K
(which depends on
(\mathcal{I}^{\prime},\mathcal{J}^{\prime})
).

It can be shown that LICQ implies both MFCQ and CRCQ, while MFCQ and CRCQ can not be compared with each other. Also any one of these three CQ’s implies Abadie’s CQ
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Proposition 3.2.1)
.

It turns out that to ensure the Lipschitz property, with MFCQ and CRCQ together are not sufficient, and we need to find a third condition in the absence of non-degeneracy. This technical condition is called Strong Coherent Orientation Condition (SCOC), to which we introduce now. For simplicity, we remove the equality constraints defining the constraint set (by removing redundant constraints and using change of variables), and set

K(\mathbf{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \big|\ g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}\right\}

where for each
i=1,\dots,I
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}
,
g_{i}(\cdot,\mathbf{p}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are convex and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth. For each pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
with
\mathbf{x}\in\mbox{SOL}(K(\mathbf{p}),F(\cdot,\mathbf{p}))
, and for each multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, denote the Lagrangian function

L(\mathbf{x},\lambda,\mathbf{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
denote the set of multipliers
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
satisfying the KKT system (
77
). A basic result states that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
is non-empty if
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mbox{SOL}(K(\mathbf{p}^{*}),F(\cdot,\mathbf{p}^{*}))
and MFCQ holds at
\mathbf{x}^{*}

(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Proposition 3.2.1)
.

We introduce a partition of the index set
\left\{1,\dots,I\right\}
with respect to the triple
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda,\mathbf{p}^{*})
such that
\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
:

\mbox{support}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{i\mid\lambda_{i}>0=g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\}

\mbox{degenerate}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{i\mid\lambda_{i}=0=g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\}

\mbox{inactive}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{i\mid\lambda_{i}=0>g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\}

In addition, denote by
\mbox{active}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mbox{support}(\lambda)\cup\mbox{degenerate}(\lambda)
. We remark that by our second characterization of non-degeneracy,
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mbox{SOL}(K(\mathbf{p}^{*}),F(\cdot,\mathbf{p}^{*}))
is non-degenerate if and only if
\mbox{degenerate}(\lambda)=\emptyset
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{e}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
("e" for

) be the subset of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
consisting of
\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
for which
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\mid i\in\mbox{active}(\lambda)\}
are linearly independent.

For each
d\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and each
\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
, define the
directional critical set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda;\mathbf{p}^{*},d\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\big\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot d\mathbf{p}=0\ \ \forall i\in\mbox{support}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot d\mathbf{p}\leq 0\ \ \forall i\in\mbox{degenerate}(\lambda)\big\}

Proposition 5.4.7 of
Facchinei and Pang 2003
states that for each
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
, the set
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda^{\prime};\mathbf{p}^{*},d\mathbf{p})
is non-empty if and only if
\lambda^{\prime}
solves the linear program

\mbox{maximize }\ \sum_{i=1}^{I}\lambda_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})d\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mbox{ subject to }\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})

(78)

The dual of (
78
) is the linear program

\mbox{minimize }\ F(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot d\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mbox{ subject to }\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})d\mathbf{x}^{\top}+\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\cdot d\mathbf{p}^{\top}

(79)

Let
\mathcal{M}^{c}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*};d\mathbf{p})
and
\mathcal{D}^{c}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*};d\mathbf{p})
be respectively the set of solutions to (
78
) and (

) ("c" for critical). We have

\emptyset,&\mbox{if }\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*};d\mathbf{p})\backslash\mathcal{M}^{c}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*};d\mathbf{p})\end{cases}

so it is either empty or a polyhedron set defined by
\mathcal{D}^{c}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*};d\mathbf{p})
. We use the abbreviation
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathcal{I}}
for the set of vectors
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}\}
. Define the

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\big\{\mathcal{I}\subset\{1,\dots,I\}\ \big|\ \exists\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\ \mbox{such that }

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{support}(\lambda)\subset\mathcal{I}\subset\mbox{active}(\lambda)\mbox{ and }\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})\mbox{ are linearly independent}\big\}

with "

" for basis.

is non-empty and finite: non-empty since
\mathcal{M}^{e}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
, finite since there are only finitely many constraints. Let
B
be the cardinal of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
and enumerate the elements as
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})=\{\mathcal{I}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}^{B}\}

\mathcal{I}^{b}
gives rise to a unique multiplier
\lambda^{b}\in\mathcal{M}^{e}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\mathcal{M}^{e}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})

\Lambda^{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}J_{\mathbf{x}}L(\mathbf{x}^{*},\lambda^{b},\mathbf{p}^{*})&\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathcal{I}^{b}}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})^{\top}\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathcal{I}^{b}}(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}

\mathbf{x}

In this appendix, we build upon the findings in the main text, extending the discussion on the update and projection policies to a wider spectrum of policy classes. Remark 12.2

(Asymptotic Optimality and Robust Optimality)

The motivation to consider policy mappings in
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{*}
, as opposed to the broader classes
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}
, stems from the pursuit of asymptotic optimality. If the variances
\mathrm{var}\left[\mathbf{W}\right]
and
\mathrm{var}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]
can be manipulated and reduced, then as variances approach zero, the performance of the policies in these classes tends towards the optimal value. This has been illustrated in Section
6.3
. However, under circumstances where variances cannot be controlled by the decision-maker, adopting a robustness perspective may be more appropriate. Specifically, by selecting a suitable constant
\delta_{t}>0
for each time-step
1\leq t\leq T
, and finding a policy mapping in the larger class
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}
and satisfies

\inf_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}),\varepsilon_{t})}R_{t}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},S_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})\right)\geq R_{t}\left(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},S^{*}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})\right)-\delta_{t}\ \ \mbox{ for all }1\leq t\leq T

(86)

it will lead to the same conclusion of (

S[1,T]
, assuming that the sum
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{t}
does not exceed the constant
C^{\prime}_{3}
, and by treating the situation outside the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood in the same manner.

It is worth noting that solving (
86
) essentially amounts to controlling the worst-case performance within an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a deterministically optimal trajectory (the nominal one), which is a typical objective in a robust optimization problem. While stochastic optimization operates under the presumption that uncertainty has a probabilistic description, robust optimization posits a deterministic, set-based model for uncertainty (
Bertsimas et al. 2011
). This approach aims to devise a solution that remains feasible for
any
realization of uncertainty within a specified set. Interestingly, our CEC-based heuristics already exhibit a distributional robustness, as they focus solely on the first and second moments of these distributions. Therefore, as indicated in Remark
EC.1
, by choosing the uncertainty set as an
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of the deterministically optimal trajectory, solving the robust counterpart (
86
) results in the same optimality gap bounds. Robust finite-horizon Markov decision processes with finite state and action spaces have been explored in
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
, Chapter 13)
, and a robust multi-stage optimization framework is discussed in
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
, Chapter 14)
. Nevertheless, solving (
86
) remains challenging due to its infinite dimensionality. A potential solution could be considering affine solution mappings, as proposed in
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
, Chapter 14)
.
\square

The above corollary essentially posits that for the establishment of a first-order or second-order optimality gap bound, one needs only to extrapolate a

The update and projection policies proffer two universal methods for such a construction. However, an unanswered query remains: do they represent a sound choice within the corresponding policy class in terms of performance?

\varepsilon_{t+1}
-neighbourhood of
\mathbf{x}^{*}(t+1)
, for any
(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{B}\left((\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}),\varepsilon_{t}\right)
.

Since (
91
) holds for
any

S_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{\overline{w}})-\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle\leq\qquad\inf_{S_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}}\left(\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},S_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},S_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}\right)\cdot\left\|(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})-(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\right\|

(92)

We now interchange the roles of
(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})
and
(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})
above, by choosing a policy mapping
S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}
with Lipschitz constant
c_{\mathrm{lip},S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}
. This time we obtain

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w})=R_{t}\big(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w},S^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\big)+V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\phi\big(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w},S^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\big),T-t)

while

\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{\overline{w}})\geq R_{t}\big(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},S^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})\big)+V_{\mathrm{rel}-}(\phi\big(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}},S^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})\big),T-t)

Hence we deduce the following inequality in the other direction

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w})-\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{\overline{w}})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\inf_{S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}}\left(\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}\right)\cdot\left\|(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})-(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\right\|

(93)

Combining (
92
) and (
93
), we can take the absolute value and deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{\overline{w}})-\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w})\right|\leq\left\|(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})-(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\right\|\cdot

\displaystyle\max\Bigg\{\inf_{S_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}}\left(\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},S_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},S_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}\right),

\displaystyle\inf_{S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}}\left(\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w}}\right)\Bigg\}

(94)

Define
c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
as claimed in (
88
) of the corollary. From (
94
) we deduce that for all
(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}),\varepsilon_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{\overline{w}})-\hat{V}_{\mathrm{rel}+}(\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},t}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},t}\right)\cdot\left\|(\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}})-(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\right\|

(95)

So that we can choose
\overline{K}_{t-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{lip},t}+1)+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
to complete the induction step.
\square

Denote by
c_{\mathrm{update},t}
the Lipschitz constant of
S^{*}_{t}(\cdot)
, then from (
87
) and (
88
) we have
c_{\mathrm{update},t}\geq c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
. By inspecting the proof of Corollary
EC.2
, we see that a looser, and non-explicit upper bound of
K_{t}
can be constructed by replacing
c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
in (
89
) by
c_{\mathrm{update},t}
. On the other hand, with
c_{\mathrm{proj},t}
denoting the Lipschitz constant of the projection mapping
\mathrm{Proj}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},t)
, guaranteed from Assumption
6.2
, we have
c_{\mathrm{proj},t}\geq\inf_{S_{t}(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{lip}}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}}c_{\mathrm{lip},S_{t}(\cdot)}^{\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}}
. While it is in general not possible to directly compare
c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
with
c_{\mathrm{proj},t}
due to the
"
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\in\\
\mathcal{B}((\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}),\varepsilon_{t})\end{subarray}}
" part in (
88
), the point is that as long as there
exists
one Lipschitz extrapolation to a policy mapping
S^{\prime}_{t}(\cdot)
satisfying
S^{\prime}_{t}(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{u}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}(t),T+1-t,\mathbf{w}}(t)
and having a small Lipschitz constant, uniformly for all
(\mathbf{x}(t),\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbf{x}^{*}(t),\mathbf{\overline{w}}),\varepsilon_{t})
, then
c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
would be small. This consideration is
independent
of the policy that we use; while the constant
c_{\mathrm{proj},t}
depends on the specific policy (the projection policy here) taken.

As an illustration, consider the weakly coupled Markov decision processes (WCMDPs) discussed in Example
3.3
. From (
14b
), the function
\phi(\cdot)
depends merely on the control part
\mathbf{U}
, and not on
\mathbf{X}
nor
\mathbf{W}
. This implies that (
89
) can be refined to
\overline{K}_{t-1}=\overline{K}_{t}c_{\phi}\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},t}+(c_{R}+1)\cdot c_{\mathrm{lip},t}
. One can then show that
c_{\phi}\leq 1
, and, by exhibiting one Lipschitz extrapolation as in
(
Brown and Zhang 2022a
, Proposition 4.1)
, that
c_{\mathrm{lip},t}\leq 1
for
1\leq t\leq T
. It implies that the constant
C_{1}
in (
31
) of Theorem
6.1
can be chosen to grow at most quadratically with
T
(and not exponentially). In contrast, it is observed numerically in
Yan 2022
that, for a large class of policies that do not involve any re-solving (a so-called "one-pass policy"), there exists some WCMDP on which the constant
c_{\phi}\cdot c_{*,t}
is strictly larger than
1
, where
c_{*,t}
is the Lipschitz constant depending on the specific one-pass policy under consideration.

One may argue that from (
89
) the constant
K_{t}
can still explode exponentially with
t
. We believe that this is unavoidable, unless the problem admits additional structure as in Example
3.3
. This is based on the computational complexity results already mentioned in Remark
2.2
, that a
T
-stage stochastic optimization problem is drastically more difficult than a
2
-stage problem. Additionally, there are evidences from the so-called
sample complexity
on
T
-stage problems with only the i.i.d. noises
\mathbf{W}
, that supports the exponential growth, see e.g.
(
Shapiro and Nemirovski 2005
, Section 3.2)
. A lower bound in
Reaiche 2016
for a certain class of
T
-stage problems even gives a growth rate of the sample complexity with a multiplicative constant
T^{T}
.
