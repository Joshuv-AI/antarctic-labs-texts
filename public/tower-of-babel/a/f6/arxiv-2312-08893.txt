Solving Dense Linear Systems Faster Than via Preconditioning
Michał Dereziński and Jiaming Yang

Abstract
We give a stochastic optimization algorithm that solves a dense n × n n\times n real-valued linear system A ​ x = b Ax=b , returning x ~ \tilde{x} such that ‖ A ​ x ~ − b ‖ ≤ ϵ ​ ‖ b ‖ \|A\tilde{x}-b\|\leq\epsilon\|b\| in time: O ~ ​ ( ( n 2 + n ​ k ω − 1 ) ​ log ⁡ 1 / ϵ ) , \displaystyle\tilde{O}\big((n^{2}+nk^{\omega-1})\log 1/\epsilon\big), where k k is the number of singular values of A A larger than O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) times its smallest positive singular value, ω < 2.372 \omega<2.372 is the matrix multiplication exponent, and O ~ \tilde{O} hides a poly-logarithmic in n n factor. When k = O ⁡ ( n 1 − θ ) k=O(n^{1-\theta}) (namely, A A has a flat-tailed spectrum, e.g., due to noisy data or regularization), this improves on both the cost of solving the system directly, as well as on the cost of preconditioning an iterative method such as conjugate gradient. In particular, our algorithm has an O ~ ​ ( n 2 ) \tilde{O}(n^{2}) runtime when k = O ⁡ ( n 0.729 ) k=O(n^{0.729}) . We further adapt this result to sparse positive semidefinite matrices and least squares regression. Our main algorithm can be viewed as a randomized block coordinate descent method, where the key challenge is simultaneously ensuring good convergence and fast per-iteration time. We then use a Markov chain coupling argument to show that similar convergence can be attained with a cheaper sampling scheme, and accelerate the block coordinate descent update via matrix sketching.

Introduction

Solving a linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
is one of the most classical algorithmic tasks, with applications in scientific computing, engineering, data science, optimization, and more. Many classical deterministic algorithms are known for this task, from direct methods such as Gaussian elimination to iterative methods such as Conjugate Gradient. More recently, there has been much success in developing faster algorithms based on randomized techniques such as sampling and sketching for solving linear systems under specific structural conditions, e.g., graph-structured
[
ST14
,
KMP12
,
CKK+18
]
and other classes of sparse linear systems
[
XXG12
,
Gra06
]
, as well as extremely over/under-determined systems
[
Sar06
,
Woo14
]
. However, the benefits of using randomized algorithms for general dense linear systems remain unclear.

Most of the work on improving the time complexity of direct methods for solving linear systems has focused on the reduction to matrix multiplication via divide-and-conquer approaches. This has led to the complexity of
O(n^{\omega})
for solving an
n\times n
linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, where
\omega<2.371552

[
WXXZ23
]
is the current best known bound on the matrix multiplication exponent, developed over a long line of works
[
Str69
,
Pan84
,
CW87
,
Wil12
]
.

On the opposite end of the spectrum from direct methods are iterative methods, which, through a sequence of cheap iterations, construct estimates that gradually converge to the solution. These approaches are widely used in practice for solving large linear systems. One of the most famous of those methods is Conjugate Gradient (CG) / Lanczos algorithm
[
HS52
]
. This method under exact arithmetic recovers the optimal solution in
n
iterations, each costing
O(n^{2})
time for a dense system. However, CG is more commonly used as an approximation algorithm for well-conditioned systems, taking
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations to converge
\epsilon
-close when the condition number
\kappa(\mathbf{A})
(the ratio between largest and smallest positive singular values) is bounded by a constant. These dramatically opposing views of the complexity of CG can be unified through careful analysis of its dependence on the singular value distribution of
\mathbf{A}

[
SW09
]
. In particular, CG will take
k+O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations to converge, when
\mathbf{A}
has at most
k
singular values larger than
O(1)
times the smallest positive singular value, or equivalently, when
\kappa(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k})=O(1)
, where
\mathbf{A}_{k}
is
\mathbf{A}
’s best rank-
k
approximation. This leads to the overall complexity of
\tilde{O}(n^{2}k)
, where we use
\tilde{O}
to hide the logarithmic factors.

The above discussion indicates that solving linear systems with a matrix having only
k=o(n)
large singular values should be substantially easier than in the worst-case. Understanding this phenomenon is motivated by the real-world prevalence of such linear systems: either due to direct regularization (e.g.,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
), as in continuous optimization
[
BV04
]
, machine learning
[
ZDW13
]
, and statistics
[
DW18
]
, or because the input matrix is distorted by isotropic noise (e.g.,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}+\delta\mathbf{N}
, where
\mathbf{N}
is the noise), which can be caused by measurement error
[
LW11
]
, data compression
[
LGW+21
]
, and privacy enforcement
[
DR14
]
. Yet, the
\tilde{O}(n^{2}k)
complexity of CG is highly unsatisfactory, as it only provides an improvement over direct methods for small
k
, and does not lead to a near-linear time algorithm unless
k=\tilde{O}(1)
. This leads to our central question:

What is the complexity of solving an
n\times n
linear system with
k
large singular values?

Arguably the most popular strategy of accelerating the complexity of linear system solvers such as CG is via preconditioning. For instance, given access to
\mathbf{A}
’s best rank
k
approximation
\mathbf{A}_{k}
, we can easily construct a preconditioner matrix
\mathbf{M}
that allows CG to solve our problem in
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations (since
\mathbf{A}_{k}
describes
the entire ill-conditioned part of
\mathbf{A}
’s spectrum). While computing
\mathbf{A}_{k}
exactly is as expensive as directly solving the linear system, much recent work has been dedicated to fast randomized algorithms for low-rank approximation via matrix sketching. In particular, we can use power iteration
[
HMT11
]
(or Krylov subspace iteration
[
MM15
]
) to construct a low-rank approximation
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
with a spectral norm guarantee
\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}-\mathbf{A}\|_{2}=O(\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A}))
, sufficient to construct a good preconditioner for our problem. These algorithms require (repeatedly) multiplying matrix
\mathbf{A}
with an
n\times k
random matrix, leading to the runtime of
\tilde{O}(n^{2}k^{\omega-2})
. This complexity can be further refined using fast rectangular matrix multiplication
[
LG12
]
by replacing the exponent
\omega
with
\omega(1,1,\log_{n}(k))\leq\omega
. Nevertheless, for large enough
k
, the resulting preconditioning algorithm will not run in near-linear time.

. For simplicity, from now on we will present all time complexities using just the square matrix multiplication exponent

Some randomized low-rank approximation algorithms avoid power iteration and run in
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+nk^{\omega-1})
time
[
CW13

CEM+15
,
CMM17
]
, but they only guarantee a bound on the Frobenius norm error
\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{k}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}
which is worse by up to a

In summary, existing approaches suffer from an undesirable trade-off between the cost of preconditioning and the cost of solving the system, where one of those costs has to be larger than the input size by at least a
\mathrm{poly}(k)

1.1
Main Results

In this paper, we avoid the undesirable trade-off between the cost of preconditioning and the cost of solving a dense linear system with
k
large singular values, by abandoning the preconditioning step in favor of stochastic optimization.

Theorem 1.1

(Dense linear system, simplified Theorem
3.2
)
.

Given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\epsilon>0
and a constant
C=O(1)
, we can compute
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
such that
\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\|\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{b}\|
in time:

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\Big((n^{2}+nk^{\omega-1})\log 1/\epsilon\Big),

where
k
is the number of singular values of
\mathbf{A}
larger than
C
times its smallest positive singular value.

Remark 1.1
.

We note that
\tilde{O}
hides only
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
factors, and the algorithm can be implemented without the knowledge of
k
. Theorem
3.2
further extends to rectangular and inconsistent systems.

The above theorem implies that we can solve an
n\times n
linear system with
k
large singular values in near-linear time,
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
, as long as
k=O(n^{\frac{1}{\omega-1}})=O(n^{0.729})
.
Moreover, when we compare the time complexity
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+nk^{\omega-1})
against the cost of CG with low-rank preconditioning, the proposed algorithm entirely avoids the
\tilde{O}(n^{2}k^{\omega-2})
cost of power iteration, without sacrificing the complexity of the solver (up to logarithmic factors). See Table
1
for a summary of our results.

Least squares.

Our approach naturally extends to both over- and under-determined systems. In particular, Theorem
3.2
directly shows that we can solve a tall
n\times d
least squares problem
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|
with
k
large singular values, in time
\tilde{O}(nd+dk^{\omega-1})
. This can be further improved for sparse matrices, by combining our algorithm with existing sketching-based preconditioning techniques for tall least squares. Below, we use
\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})
to denote the number of non-zeros in
\mathbf{A}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Least squares, simplified Theorem
10.1
)
.

Given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
k
large singular values,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\epsilon>0
, we can compute
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
so that
\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}\leq\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\epsilon\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
in time

\tilde{O}\Big((\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+d^{2}+dk^{\omega-1})\log 1/\epsilon\Big).

Positive semidefinite systems.

An important application of our results is solving symmetric positive semidefinite (psd) linear systems, which arise from data covariances, kernel matrices, Hessian matrices, and more. While Theorem
1.1
already provides improved time complexity guarantees for dense psd systems, a careful adaptation of our algorithm allows it to exploit the sparsity structure of the problem, as well as improving the
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
factors in the bounds. Similarly to prior stochastic psd linear system solvers
[
LS13
]
, our complexity bounds depend on the following notion of sparsity,
\mathrm{nnz}^{*}(\mathbf{A})=n\cdot\max_{i}\{\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{i,:})\}\leq n^{2}
, which is proportional to
\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})
when the non-zeros are distributed somewhat uniformly. For a psd matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we define
\|x\|_{\mathbf{A}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}
.

Theorem 1.3

(PSD linear system, simplified Theorem
8.1
)
.

Given a psd matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
k
large eigenvalues,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\epsilon>0
, we can find
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
such that
\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}\|_{\mathbf{A}}\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}\|_{\mathbf{A}}
in time:

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\Big((\mathrm{nnz}^{*}(\mathbf{A})+nk^{\omega-1})\log 1/\epsilon\Big).

Our results naturally apply to regularized linear systems, such as kernel ridge regression, i.e.,
(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
for a psd matrix
\mathbf{K}
, and
l_{2}
-regularized least squares,
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
(as special cases of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.2
, respectively). In these cases, we provide the first complexity bounds that scale with the number of eigenvalues (of
\mathbf{K}
or of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
) larger than the regularization parameter
\lambda
. This is in contrast to prior works that directly exploit this explicit regularization structure
[
ACW17
,
FTU21
]
, where the time complexity scales with a notion of
\lambda
-effective dimension which can be arbitrarily larger than our
k
. Thus, remarkably, we achieve a direct runtime improvement over explicitly regularized linear system solvers, even though our algorithms are entirely oblivious to the regularization structure themselves (see Section
1.3
for discussion).

Numerical stability of our methods.

For the sake of simplicity, all our results assume the exact arithmetic (Real-RAM) model. However, we do not foresee any obstacles in establishing the numerical stability of our algorithms in a Word-RAM model with word sizes polylogarithmic in
n
, the condition number of
\mathbf{A}
, and precision
1/\epsilon
. In fact, our convergence analysis of the main algorithmic framework already allows for approximate computations (Section
7
), so that an inner solver can be employed to perform them. Thus, the question reduces to the numerical stability of the inner solver. For this, we use preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG), where the preconditioner is constructed by sketching a tall matrix (i.e., multiplying by a sparse oblivious subspace embedding), and then computing a singular value decomposition of the sketch. While the conjugate gradient method can behave differently in finite precision than in the Real-RAM model, it has been established that it still obtains essentially the same worst-case convergence rate (stated in our
Lemma

2.6
) in finite precision
[
Gre89
,
MMS18
]
.
The preconditioner can be constructed in a stable way using black-box (fast) linear algebra routines
[
DDH07
]
.

1.2
Our Techniques

A core inspiration for our algorithms is a broad family of stochastic iterative methods for solving linear systems, which includes randomized Kaczmarz
[
SV09
]
, coordinate descent
[
LL10
]
, sketch-and-project
[
GR15
]
, and many others
[
LS13
]
. Unlike CG-type methods which do a pass over the entire matrix
\mathbf{A}
in each iteration, these approaches randomly sub-sample a small number of rows/columns of
\mathbf{A}
(or construct a small sketch of
\mathbf{A}
) and use this stochastic information to perform a sublinear iterative update. There has been much success in characterizing the convergence and complexity of stochastic methods in the ultra-small sample regime (i.e., single row/column per iteration
[
SV09
]
), but the theoretical analysis of the larger sample regime has proven much more elusive. While there has been some progress in this direction over the last few years, it has come with trade-offs: meaningful characterizations of the convergence behavior have required relying on either constructing sophisticated and expensive sub-sampling distributions (such as determinantal point processes
[
RK20
,
MDK20
]
), or even more expensive dense sketching methods (such as the Gaussian sketch
[
RN21
,
DR24
]
), rendering the entire procedure computationally impractical.

In light of this context, when designing and analyzing our stochastic iterative solvers, we encountered three main bottlenecks which had to be overcome to establish any improvement over CG-based complexity guarantees for solving dense linear systems:

1.

Obtaining a sharp convergence guarantee that depends on the spectral structure of
\mathbf{A}
;

2.

Relying on a fast sub-sampling/sketching scheme;

3.

Ensuring fast computation of the iterative updates.

1. Convergence guarantee.
As a starting point in our analysis we used recent works on analyzing the convergence rate of block coordinate descent algorithms using Determinantal Point Processes (DPP) as the sub-sampling method. DPPs form a family of discrete distributions over subsets of a finite domain, e.g.,
\{1,...,n\}
, where the probabilities of each subset are proportional to a determinant of a certain sub-matrix (the uniform distribution over spanning trees of a graph is one example). Exploiting certain formulas over sums of determinants and adjugate matrices, recent works
[
RK20
,
MDK20
]
have characterized the convergence rate of block coordinate descent algorithms with DPP and k-DPP (DPP with size
k
subsets) sub-sampling in terms of the elementary symmetric polynomials (ESP) of the singular values of matrix
\mathbf{A}
. Of particular relevance to our results are the guarantees obtained by Rodomanov and Kropotov
[
RK20
]
. However, their convergence bounds by themselves are not sufficiently sharp to obtain any improvement over CG-type methods, even if we entirely ignored the remaining two bottlenecks. Thus, as a first step, we improve the convergence guarantees of
[
RK20
]
for k-DPP-based block coordinate descent by applying theory of majorization, via Schur-concavity of the ratios of ESPs, leading to a factor
k
improvement in the convergence complexity. In particular, we show that for matrices with
k
large singular values, using
\tilde{O}(k)
-sized blocks, we need
\tilde{O}(n/k)
iterations to solve a linear system.

2. Fast sub-sampling.
There has been much recent work on efficiently sampling from discrete distributions, including strongly Rayleigh measures
[
AGR16
]
such as k-DPPs. Most recently, Anari, Liu and Vuong
[
ALV22
]
provided nearly-tight complexity analysis for a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm that samples from a k-DPP (to within a small total variation distance). However, the time complexity of their algorithm is still not sufficient to produce a stochastic method that improves upon CG-type solvers for dense linear systems, as it requires performing multiple singular value decompositions of
n\times k
matrices to produce a single k-DPP sample, at the cost of at least
\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1})
per iteration. To address this, in the next step of our analysis, we show that the convergence guarantees of k-DPP sampling can be retained (up to logarithmic factors) by a much simpler and cheaper sub-sampling procedure, after preprocessing the linear system with a randomized Hadamard transform
[
Tro11
]
. We show this by coupling a hypothetical run of the MCMC algorithm from
[
ALV22
]
with our sub-sampling procedure, in such a way that the k-DPP sample will (with high probability) be contained within our sample.

3. Fast updates.
Standard block coordinate methods perform their iterations by reducing the
n\times n
linear system to a much smaller
k\times n
linear system, which is extremely under-determined, and then choose a solution with the smallest distance from the current iterate. For
k=O(1)
, this is a very simple and inexpensive update, but as
k
grows, the cost of solving the system exactly becomes prohibitively expensive. However, we can leverage the fact that, unlike the original system, this new system is extremely rectangular, which makes it much easier to construct an effective preconditioner via sketching
[
RT08
,
MSM14
,
Woo14
]
. We prove that
O(\log(n/k))
(inner) iterations of preconditioned CG can be used to solve this sub-problem to within sufficient accuracy so that the overall convergence complexity of the stochastic method remains unchanged. This improves the cost of the stochastic updates from
O(nk^{\omega-1})
to
\tilde{O}(nk+k^{\omega})
. Since we showed that
\tilde{O}(n/k)
updates are needed to converge, this leads to the final complexity result in Theorem
1.1
.

1.3
Background and Related Work

Linear system solvers.

Extensive literature has been dedicated to solving linear systems both via direct and iterative methods. The direct approaches are based on the strategy of applying a (possibly implicit) representation of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
to the vector
\mathbf{b}
. Examples of this approach include Gaussian elimination, Cholesky factorization and QR decomposition. A key breakthrough in the time complexity of direct methods came from Strassen, who showed that matrix inversion is equivalent to matrix multiplication
[
Str69
]
. This kickstarted a long line of works on fast
O(n^{\omega})
matrix multiplication
[
Pan84
]
, including the Coppersmith-Winograd method
[
CW87
]
. This is still an active area of research
[
Wil12
,
LG12
,
AW21
,
WXXZ23
]
.

There has also been much work on accelerating iterative linear system solvers, particularly those based on Krylov subspace methods, such as conjugate gradient / Lanczos. Here, most of the efforts have been concentrated on constructing effective preconditioners, for example via specialized efficient Cholesky factorizations
[
KLP+16
,
KS16
]
, or on improving their numerical stability
[
PV21
,
Nie22
]
. However, these approaches have focused on sparse and/or structured systems, e.g., those decomposable into a sum of simple components, particularly Laplacians of undirected graphs
[
Vai89
,
ST14
,
KMP12
]
, and their extensions
[
CKK+18
]
. Other examples of structured systems with efficient solvers include Hankel/Toeplitz matrices
[
KKM79
,
XXG12
]
and circulant matrices
[
Gra06
]
.

Preconditioning regularized systems.

Linear systems with an explicit regularization term form another class of structured systems that can benefit from effective preconditioning. The main examples here are: the
l_{2}
-regularized least squares task,
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
; kernel ridge regression,
(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{K}
is a psd kernel matrix; and the regularized Newton step,
(\mathbf{H}+\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{g}
, where
\mathbf{H}
is the Hessian matrix, which is symmetric but may not be psd. A long line of works have used randomized sketching, sampling, and the Nyström method
[
EAM14
,
RCR17
,
MM17
]
to construct effective low-rank preconditioners for these systems
[
ACW17
,
FTU21
]
, relying on the explicit regularizer to avoid power iteration. To construct a good preconditioner for, say, the matrix
\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
, those algorithms construct a decomposition of
\mathbf{K}
with rank at least proportional to the so-called
\lambda
-effective dimension:
d_{\lambda}={\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1})\leq n

[
EAM14
]
. The best known cost of this preconditioning step, even exploiting the structural properties of psd matrices, is at least
\tilde{O}(nd_{\lambda}^{\omega-1})

[
MM17
]
. Similarly as our parameter
k
, the
\lambda
-effective dimension exploits the flat tail of the spectrum of
\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
. In fact, it is easy to see that such a matrix may have no more than
k=2d_{\lambda}
eigenvalues larger than twice its smallest eigenvalue. However the bound cannot be reversed: for any
n
,
k
and
\lambda
, one can construct an
n\times n
psd matrix
\mathbf{K}
such that
\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
has only
k
eigenvalues larger than twice its smallest eigenvalue, and yet its
\lambda
-effective dimension is at least
n/2
, in which case, the preconditioning cost becomes
\Theta(n^{\omega})
, as large as the cost of directly solving the system. Thus, our algorithms not only recover but also improve upon the time complexity of these specialized solvers, even without explicit access to the regularization term.

Stochastic solvers.
The literature on stochastic iterative methods for solving linear systems has its origins in the Kaczmarz method
[
Kac37
]
, which performs iterative updates while cycling through the rows of the system and projecting the current iterate onto the hyperplane formed by an individual row. A randomized version of this method was introduced and analyzed by Strohmer and Vershynin
[
SV09
]
, followed up by closely related randomized coordinate descent methods for psd linear systems and least squares by Leventhal and Lewis
[
LL10
]
. This triggered much research into stochastic iterative solvers, leading to more general classes of methods that sample blocks of rows instead of a single row
[
NW13
,
NT14
,
MDK20
]
, or use matrix sketching
[
RN21
,
DR24
]
via the Sketch-and-Project framework
[
GR15
]
. Despite these efforts, it is still not fully understood when stochastic solvers are faster than deterministic (e.g., Krylov-based) iterative methods. One such result was obtained by Lee and Sidford
[
LS13
]
, who showed that the convergence rate of an accelerated stochastic coordinate descent solver exhibits better condition number dependence than conjugate gradient for psd linear systems. However, this still leads to poor performance for matrices that have an ill-conditioned top-
k
part of the spectrum, whereas our algorithms avoid this by using blocks of coordinates (rows or columns) as opposed to one coordinate at a time.

Matrix sketching and sub-sampling.
Our techniques are closely related to matrix sketching and sampling techniques commonly used in randomized linear algebra
[
HMT11
,
Woo14
,
DM16
,
MT20
,
MDM+23
]
, which is an area that has led to improved randomized approximation algorithms for tasks such as least squares regression
[
Sar06
,
RT08
,
CW13
]
, low-rank approximation
[
CEM+15
,
CMM17
,
LW20
]
,
l_{p}
regression
[
MM13
,
CP15
,
CD21
,
WW22
]
, linear programming
[
CLS21
]
and more
[
SWZ19
,
JKL+20
]
. Some of the key matrix sketching techniques we use in this work are subspace embeddings
[
CW13
,
NN13
,
MM13
,
Coh16
,
CDDR24
]
, which can be used to construct effective preconditioners for highly over- or under-determined linear systems (e.g., least squares). In particular, our fast least squares solver is an accelerated version of the sketch-to-precondition algorithm by Rokhlin and Tygert
[
RT08
]
, which, using current subspace embedding techniques, can solve this task in
\tilde{O}(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+d^{\omega})
time by sketching the tall
n\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
down to an
\tilde{O}(d)\times d
size, and then performing a QR/SVD decomposition to produce a preconditioner. When the matrix
\mathbf{A}
has only
k
large singular values, then this can be improved to
\tilde{O}(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+d^{2}k^{\omega-2})
, by using randomized low-rank SVD with power iteration
[
HMT11
]
. We instead use our stochastic solver to avoid constructing an explicit preconditioner altogether (see Table
1
for comparison). Finally, as mentioned earlier, many randomized low-rank approximation methods mentioned above, such as
[
CEM+15
,
CMM17
]
, avoid power iteration and run in linear time. However, these approaches provide a Frobenius norm error guarantee (as opposed to an approximation of all top
k
singular values), which does not translate into a strong preconditioner for a linear system.

One of the key matrix sub-sampling techniques we use (albeit mainly for our theoretical analysis, rather than as an algorithmic routine) is called determinantal point processes (DPP), also known as volume sampling. This is a family of discrete distributions which has its origins in physics
[
Mac75
]
and graph theory
[
Gue83
]
. More recently, DPPs have been used in randomized linear algebra
[
DM21
]
to construct optimal coresets for the column subset selection problem
[
DRVW06
,
GS12
,
DKM20
]
and least squares regression
[
DCMW19
]
, as well as for discrete and stochastic optimization
[
NST19
,
DLM20
,
RK20
,
MDK20
]
. Early algorithmic approaches to DPP sampling required an exact SVD of the input matrix
[
HKPV06
,
KT12
]
, but a more recent line of works showed that this can be avoided
[
Der19
,
DCV19
]
, culminating in fast Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling algorithms for DPPs
[
AGR16
,
AD20
,
ALV22
]
. Still, these methods lead to a substantial overhead in time complexity for our algorithms, which is why we do not use them explicitly except for certain special cases (namely, psd linear systems).

Problem Setup and Notation.

Throughout most of the paper, we will focus on a linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})=r
and
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
be the decreasing singular values of
\mathbf{A}
. There are two cases we consider. In the first case where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{A})
, the linear system is consistent and has one unique min-length solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
. In the second case where
\mathbf{b}\notin\mathrm{range}(\mathbf{A})
, the linear system is inconsistent and there is no exact solution, and we denote
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
as the solution of the least squares problem
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|
.
As a special case, we also consider solving a symmetric positive semidefinite definite (psd) linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we use
\mathcal{S}_{n}
to denote the psd cone.

For vector
\mathbf{v}
, we use
\|\mathbf{v}\|
to denote its Euclidean norm, and for a psd matrix
\mathbf{M}
, we denote
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbf{M}}:=\sqrt{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}}
. For matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we denote
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}
and
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}
as its operator norm and Frobenius norm respectively, and denote
\kappa(\mathbf{A})
as its condition number. We also define the averaged condition number of
\mathbf{A}
as
\bar{\kappa}(\mathbf{A}):=\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}\|\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})}}\leq\kappa(\mathbf{A})
, which arises in the analysis of our algorithms. For
1\leq k\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})
, we denote the best rank-
k
approximation of
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathbf{A}_{k}:=\argmin_{\mathrm{rank}(\hat{\mathbf{A}})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\hat{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}
, and denote
\bar{\kappa}_{k}:=\bar{\kappa}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_{k})
(i.e., the averaged condition number of the tail of the spectrum). Let
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
be the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
in decreasing order, if we assume that there exists
k
such that
\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{r}=O(1)
holds for all
i\geq k
, then we have
\bar{\kappa}_{k}=(\frac{1}{r-k}\sum_{j>k}^{r}\sigma_{j}^{2}/\sigma_{r}^{2})^{1/2}=O(1)
. For simplicity of presentation, throughout the paper we assume that
\bar{\kappa}_{k}<m^{4}
, which is a rather loose condition. For two
n\times n
psd matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, we say
\mathbf{A}\approx_{1+\epsilon}\mathbf{B}
when
\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\mathbf{A}\preceq\mathbf{B}\preceq(1+\epsilon)\mathbf{A}
where
\preceq
denotes the matrix ordering.

For
1\leq\tau\leq m
, we use
\tbinom{[m]}{\tau}
to denote the set of all the
\tau
-element subsets of
[m]=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. For each
S\in\tbinom{[m]}{\tau}
, we use
\mathbf{I}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times m}
to denote the matrix obtained from
m\times m
identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
by only retaining the rows whose indices are in
S
. For matrix
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and subset
S\in\tbinom{[m]}{\tau}
, we use
\mathbf{L}_{S,S}=\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times\tau}
to denote the principal submatrix located at the intersection of the rows and columns with indices from
S
, we also denote
\mathbf{L}_{:,S}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{L}_{S,:}=\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{L}
respectively. For a square
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{L}
, we use
\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{L})
to denote its adjugate matrix, which is an
n\times n
matrix such that
\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{L})_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\mathrm{det}(\mathbf{L}_{[n]\backslash j,[n]\backslash i})

Elementary symmetric polynomials.

We next introduce some concepts that are needed for the convergence analysis of our algorithms, starting with elementary symmetric polynomials (ESP) and their properties.

Definition 2.1

For a given vector

Lemma 2.1

Let

Given a PSD matrix with dimension

\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{L})

An important special case of

Definition 2.3

Given a PSD matrix

\mathrm{P}
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{L})

When the PSD matrix

]

Total variation distance.

In the analysis of sampling a
k
-DPP sample, we will need the following definitions and properties of total variation distance.

Definition 2.4

(Total variation distance)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures defined on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, the total variation distance between
\mu
and

\displaystyle\delta(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|.

Definition 2.5

(Coupling, see Definition 4.12 in
[
VH14
]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be two probability measures defined on
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
respectively. We say a joint distribution of random variable

over

is a coupling of
\mu

X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu

The following standard lemma states that the total variation distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is the minimum probability
that random variables
X\sim\mu
and

do not coincide.

Lemma 2.4

(Total variation, see Example 4.14 in
[
VH14
]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures defined on some

. Then, we have

\inf_{(X,Y)}\Pr\{X\neq Y\}=\delta(\mu,\nu)
,
where the infimum is taken over all couplings of
\mu
and

.

Randomized Hadamard transform.

We use the randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) as a preprocessing step in our algorithms, to ensure that a simple block coordinate sampling procedure works as well as a determinantal point process.

Definition 2.6
.

The Hadamard matrix
\mathbf{H}_{2^{k}}
of dimension

is obtained using the following recurrence relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=[1],\qquad\mathbf{H}_{2n}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{n}&\mathbf{H}_{n}\\
\mathbf{H}_{n}&-\mathbf{H}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Throughout the paper, we will drop the subscript from

Definition 2.7
.

The randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) of an

is the product
\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}
, where
\mathbf{D}
is a random

with zero rows if necessary, we may assume that

In the analysis of using randomized Hadamard transform, one key technique we use is the tail bound for a Rademacher random vector, which is defined as
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
where each
d_{i}
is an independent random sign variable that equals
1
with probability

and
-1
otherwise. We have the following tail bound lemma.

Lemma 2.5

(Rademacher tail bound, e.g.,
[
Tro11
]
)
.

Let

be a convex function on vectors with Lipschitz bound
|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
. Let
\mathbf{d}
be a Rademacher vector. Then, for all

,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{f(\mathbf{d})\geq{\mathbb{E}}[f(\mathbf{d})]+Lt\right\}\leq e^{-t^{2}/8}.

Conjugate gradient.

We have the following algorithm and convergence guarantee of using conjugate gradient method to solve positive semidefinite linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}
, where we are only given
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{b}
without knowing
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
.

Lemma 2.6

(Convergence of CG,
[
Hay18
]
)
.

Consider solving positive semidefinite linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
are given. Let
\kappa:=\kappa(\mathbf{A})
be the condition number and
\mathbf{u}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
be the min-length solution. Then, running conjugate gradient by starting from
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
will yield the following convergence result:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}_{s}-\mathbf{u}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}\leq 2\left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{s}\|\mathbf{u}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}}.

We denote the output of conjugate gradient as
\mathbf{u}_{s}=\mathrm{CG}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},s)
.

Subspace embeddings.

To construct the preconditioner in our inner iteration for solving the linear system, as well as for obtaining an improved input sparsity time algorithm for least squares, we use the following result which guarantees a
(1+\epsilon)
-subspace embedding with an optimal embedding dimension

Lemma 2.7

(Adapted from Theorem 1.4 in
[
CDDR24
]
)

Given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},\epsilon<1/2
,

where

is an embedding matrix with
\phi=O((d+\log(1/\delta))/\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~~\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|\leq\|\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|\leq(1+\epsilon)\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|.

3
Main Algorithms

In this section we state our main algorithms and main results for solving a dense linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. For the sake of completeness, and because they provide somewhat different guarantees in somewhat different regimes, we propose two algorithms.

The first algorithm (Algorithm
2
) can be viewed as a Kaczmarz-type stochastic solver, in that it sub-samples the rows of
\mathbf{A}
and projects its iterate onto the hyperplane defined by this smaller linear system. As other Kaczmarz-type methods, this algorithm converges to the optimum solution of a consistent linear system, and its convergence rate is naturally measured in the Euclidean distance from the optimum, obtaining a guarantee of the form
\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|
.

The second algorithm (Algorithm
3
) can be viewed more as a coordinate descent-type method, which sub-samples the columns of
\mathbf{A}
. This algorithm actually solves an implicit psd linear system
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{c}
, but by setting
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, we can use it to solve the
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
system via the normal equations. This approach has the advantage that when the original linear system is inconsistent, then the procedure will converge to the least squares solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
. Another difference from the Kaczmarz-type algorithm is that the convergence guarantees of Algorithm
3
are expressed as
\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\|
.

In order to ensure fast convergence with a cheap sub-sampling scheme, we first transform the linear system to an equivalent form by using the randomized Hadamard transform (RHT). The transformed system enjoys the property that the corresponding determinantal point process is nearly isotropic, which makes it easier to sample, see Section
6
. Note that our two algorithms use slightly different preprocessing steps: the Kaczmarz-type method (Algorithm
2
) applies the RHT from the left,
\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}
, because it uses row sampling; whereas the coordinate descent-type method (Algorithm
3
) applies the RHT from the right,
\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{H}
, because it uses column sampling. In either case, this transformation can be done in
\tilde{O}(mn)
time, and since the RHT is an orthogonal matrix, we can easily recover the solution to the original system.

Both algorithms use an inner solver to approximately solve the small linear system obtained via sub-sampling (the projection step). We do this by constructing a preconditioner for the smaller system and use CG as an inner solver. The construction of the preconditioner is based on a subspace embedding sketch, leveraging the fact that the small system is highly rectangular, see Algorithm
6
and Lemma
2.7
.

4.1
Expected Projection under DPP Sampling

Let
\mathbf{I}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times m}
be a sampling matrix with
\tau\leq n
. Our analysis is based on an iterative method to solve the linear system which we will refer to as
sketch-and-project
, following
[
GR15
]
(it is also known as the block Kaczmarz method):

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{b})}_{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}}.

(4.1)

Denote
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
as the min-length solution and denote
\mathbf{P}_{S}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}).

Notice that
\mathbf{P}_{S}
is a projection matrix and thus
\mathbf{P}_{S}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{S}
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
is not in the nullspace of
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}]
, we know
\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
will always not be in the nullspace of
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}].

For the detailed reason see proof of Lemma

.
Thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})^{2}](\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(1-\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}]))\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}.

In order to obtain the convergence rate of sketch-and-project, we need to lower bound the quantity
\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])
, which turns out to be quite challenging for most sampling schemes. To address this, following recent prior work
[

]
, we will consider
S
sampled according to
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})

[

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
. Directly applying their results to our setting with

, we can obtain a lower bound of order
\Omega(\frac{1}{r})
, where

is the rank of
\mathbf{A}
and we hide the dependence on

. In the following lemma, we refine the proof by providing a sharper bound on the expression derived by
[

]
, using Schur concavity of the ratios of elementary symmetric polynomials. Namely, we show that if we use a slightly larger DPP sample size

4.3
Approximate Sketch-and-Project

In the update rule of sketch-and-project (see Eq.(
4.1
)), the remaining problem is that we need to compute
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}:=(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{A}})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{b}})
in each iteration, where
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}
and
\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}
. Directly computing this quantity takes
O(n\tau^{\omega-1})
. Instead of directly computing

, we show that it is possible to

this quantity in
\tilde{O}(n\tau+\tau^{\omega})
time (see Section

). Denote
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times n}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\bar{\mathbf{b}}
, let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times\tau}
be a preconditioner such that
\tilde{\kappa}:=\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})=O(1)
holds with high probability. We first show that we can transform the problem of computing
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}
to solving the following preconditioned normal equation of the second kind:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t},~~~\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}.

Then we use CG to solve this positive semidefinite linear system over
\mathbf{u}
. Let
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{*}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}^{*}
be the optimal solution, we show that
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{*}=\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}
. By using Lemma
2.6
and doing the change of variable back from
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathbf{w}
, we show in Lemma
7.1
that after
s

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{s}-\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}\|\leq 2\left(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-1}{\tilde{\kappa}+1}\right)^{s}\|\mathbf{w}^{*}_{t}\|.

The only part left is to construct a preconditioner
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times\tau}
such that
\tilde{\kappa}=O(1)
, and we construct it by using fast oblivious subspace embedding matrix proposed by
[
CDDR24
]
.
According to Lemma
2.7
, by setting
\phi=O(\tau+\log(1/\delta))
we have that
\tilde{\kappa}=O(1)
holds with probability
1-\delta
, since we can set
\epsilon
to be a small constant. Next we analyze the time complexity: given
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, computing
\mathbf{\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
takes

\displaystyle O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{2}\log^{4}(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{\omega-1}\phi)=O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{\omega}+\tau^{\omega-1}\log(1/\delta))=O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{\omega}).

Monotonicity of Projections.

We start our analysis by making the following simple observation, which implies that in the sketch-and-project scheme, a larger set can only improve the convergence guarantee, since it leads to a “larger” projection matrix in the sense of matrix orders. Formally we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Given matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, for any subsets
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq[m]
, define projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{S_{i}}:=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}\mathbf{A}
where
\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S_{i}|\times m}
is the sampling matrix. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{S_{1}}\preceq\mathbf{P}_{S_{2}}.

Proof.

We only need to show that for any vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{S_{1}}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{S_{2}}\mathbf{x}
. Let
\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{i}}\rangle
be the subspace spanned by the rows of
\mathbf{I}_{S_{i}}\mathbf{A}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{S_{i}}\mathbf{x}=1-\mathbf{x}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S_{i}})\mathbf{x}=1-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{P}_{S_{i}}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=1-\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{i}}\rangle}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}.

Note that since
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
, we know that
\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{2}}\rangle
contains
\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{1}}\rangle
, thus we have:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{1}}\rangle}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{1}\|^{2}\geq\min_{\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathrm{span}\langle\mathbf{A}_{S_{2}}\rangle}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\|^{2}.

We conclude that
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{S_{1}}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{S_{2}}\mathbf{x}
holds for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
, as desired.
∎

According to Lemma
6.3
,
\mathbf{P}_{S_{1}}\preceq\mathbf{P}_{S_{2}}
holds for all
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
. Thus we can take expectation over the randomness of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
and have
\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{1}}])\leq\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{2}}])
holds conditioned on the event
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
.
Thus, if we can obtain a sample
\tilde{S}
that contains a
k
-DPP sample
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}
, then we know that
\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])
which guarantees a faster convergence rate for our algorithm.

6.1
Preprocessing

In this section we show that the
k
-DPP marginals become approximately isotropic (i.e., uniform) after we use the randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) as a preprocessing step. We note that prior works have shown that the RHT can uniformize the entries of a vector, or the row-norms or leverage scores of a matrix
[
Tro11
]
. However, this analysis typically relies on the fact that all of these quantities can be easily described as linear or quadratic functions of the rows of the target matrix. On the other hand, the marginals of a general fixed size DPP are not expressed with any simple formula (they can only be described with expressions that involve elementary symmetric polynomials of certain sub-matrices of the input matrix). We get around this issue by relying on the fact that
k
-DPP marginals can be described as a mixture of the marginals of simpler Projection DPPs (this fact has been previously used in
k
-DPP sampling algorithms
[
KT12
]
).

First, we show that applying the RHT as in Algorithm
2
does not affect the optimal solution of the linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
. Denote
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A},\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}
and consider the linear system
\bar{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}=\bar{\mathbf{b}}
. Since
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
, we know the optimal solution is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\bar{\mathbf{b}}=(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{x}^{*},

which is the same as the optimal solution of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
. To construct this system, we need to compute
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}
which takes
O(mn\log m)=\tilde{O}(mn)
time, this is much better than the
\tilde{O}(mnk^{\omega-2})
result in Section
6.4
. Next we show that the marginals of
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
are approximately isotropic.

Lemma 6.4

(Approximately isotropic
k
-DPP)
.

Given matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, denote
\mathbf{Q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}
as the RHT matrix and
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}
as the preprocessed matrix. Let
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
, for any
0<\delta<1
, with probability
1-\delta
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(m/\delta)}{m}}\right)^{2}~~~\text{for all}~~~j\in[m].

Proof.

Although there is no explicit expression for the marginals of general fixed size DPP, there is one specific class of fixed size DPP called Projection DPP which has simple expressions for the marginals. Moreover, sampling a
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}
is equivalent to sampling a mixture of Projection DPPs, see Lemma
2.2
. Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
be the SVD of
\mathbf{A}
and denote
\bar{\mathbf{U}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}
, then we have eigendecomposition
\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}=\bar{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\top})\bar{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}
. Denote
r=\mathrm{rank}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})
. Lemma
2.2
shows that if we first sample
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r})
where
\gamma_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+1})
are independent, and then sample
S_{\gamma}\sim\mathrm{P}
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}^{\top})
, where
\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}
is the submatrix of columns of
\bar{\mathbf{U}}
indexed by the non-zeros in
\gamma
, then conditioned on the event that
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=k
,
S_{\gamma}
is distributed the same as
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
.

The advantage of decomposing
k
-DPP into
\mathrm{P}
-DPPs is that we can explicitly express marginals of
\mathrm{P}
-DPP. Since
\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}=\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}^{\dagger}
is a projection matrix, we know that the marginals of
\mathrm{P}
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}^{\top})
can be expressed as the diagonal entries of this kernel matrix as (e.g., see
[
DM21
]
)

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\gamma}\}=(\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}^{\top})_{j,j}=\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}~~~\text{where}~~~j\in[m].

Based on this result, we can compute the marginals of
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}
by summing over the marginals of all possible P-DPPs. For
j\in[m]
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\left(\Pr\{j\in S_{\gamma}\}\cdot\Pr\{\gamma\mid\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=k\}\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}\left(\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}\cdot\Pr\{\gamma\mid\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=k\}\right).

Note that by definition we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\|\mathbf{d}^{\top}{\operatorname{diag}}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2},

where
\mathbf{d}
is the vector of Rademacher variables along the diagonal of
\mathbf{D}
. If we denote
\mathbf{E}_{j}:={\operatorname{diag}}(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{H})
and
p_{\gamma}:=\Pr\{\gamma\mid\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=k\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot\|\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot\|\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{d}^{\top}\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}\right)\mathbf{d}.

We define function
f_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{x}}
where
\mathbf{B}_{j}:=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}
. We first analyze the properties of
\mathbf{B}_{j}
. Since
\mathbf{E}_{j}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}=\mathbf{I}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot\|\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j}\|\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot\|\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\|=1

and

\displaystyle{\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{B}_{j})=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot{\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{E}_{j}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{j})=\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\cdot{\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{:,\gamma})=k\cdot\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{r}}p_{\gamma}\right)=k.

Next we analyze the properties of
f_{j}
. Note that
f_{j}
is convex, with Lipschitz constant as follows:

\displaystyle|f_{j}(\mathbf{x})-f_{j}(\mathbf{y})|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\mathbf{B}_{j}}-\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\mathbf{B}_{j}}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{\mathbf{B}_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\|\mathbf{B}_{j}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

We consider the random variable
f_{j}(\mathbf{d})
and bound the expectation as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}(\mathbf{d})]\leq({\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}(\mathbf{d})^{2}])^{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{m}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{d}]\right)^{1/2}=\left(\frac{1}{m}{\operatorname{tr}}(\mathbf{B}_{j})\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.

Then we use Lemma
2.5
on the concentration of a Lipschitz function of a Rademacher random vector, with
t=\sqrt{8\log(m/\delta)}
, and obtain

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{d}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(m/\delta)}{m}}\right\}\leq\frac{\delta}{m}.

Further notice that
\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}=f_{j}^{2}(\mathbf{d})=\frac{1}{m}\mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{B}_{j}\mathbf{d}
, thus by applying union bound we have that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(m/\delta)}{m}}\right)^{2}~~~\text{for all}~~~j\in[m].

∎

Remark 6.2
.

Since we assume that
k\geq\log m
, by setting
\delta=0.01
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log(m/\delta)}{m}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2k}{m}+\frac{16\log(m/\delta)}{m}\leq\frac{c_{0}k}{m}

for some constant
c_{0}=O(1)
. This shows that after the randomized Hadamard transform, the marginals are approximately isotropic up to a constant factor with probability
0.99
.

6.2
Successful DPP Sample

In this section we show that as long as the marginals are approximately isotropic, then with high probability, the output of Algorithm
4
is the same as a real
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
sample. The main result is Lemma
6.6
. Notice that Algorithm
4
generates new
T_{i}
’s with given size by calling the uniform sampling oracle, thus we first compute the number of calls to the oracle we need in Lemma
6.5
. Since the proof of Lemma
6.5
is a straightforward concentration argument, we defer the proof to Appendix
A
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose we have an oracle that can uniformly sample one element from
[m]
. Suppose we have
S\in\tbinom{[m]}{k}
where
k\leq m
. Let
t=O(k)
and
0<\delta<1
, then with probability
1-\delta
, after at most
O(t+\log\frac{1}{\delta})
calls to the oracle, we can obtain
T:=\cup_{j=1}^{t}\{i_{j}\}
such that
T\subseteq[m]\backslash S
.

Lemma 6.6

(Ghost coupling)
.

Conditioned on an event that holds with probability
0.99
(and only depends on RHT). For
k\geq\log m
and
\delta=m^{-O(1)}
, if we run Algorithm
4
with choice
\mathrm{call}\geq Ck
for some constant
C=O(1)
, and
\mathrm{iter}=O(\log^{3}m)
, then with probability
1-\delta
,
S_{\mathrm{iter}}
is the same as
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}
, where
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
6.4
and Remark
6.2
, with probability
0.99
we have
\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}\leq\frac{c_{0}k}{m}
holds for all
j\in[m]
for some constant
c_{0}=O(1)
, which gives

\displaystyle K:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Pr\{j\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}\leq c_{0}k.

By combining this result with Lemma
6.1
, we know that conditioned on an event that holds with probability
0.99
, Algorithm
4
produces a sample from a distribution with total variation distance
m^{-O(1)}
from the
k
-DPP distribution with
O(\log^{3}m)
iterations of up-down walk, and size of the super set needs to satisfies
|T_{i}|=O(K)
. Since we know that
K=O(k)
, the constraints become
|T_{i}|=O(k)
for all
i
. By Lemma
6.5
, in order to satisfy
|T_{i}|=t-k=O(k)
for all
i

holds with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, we need to set

\displaystyle\mathrm{call}=O(t-k+\log\frac{2\mathrm{iter}}{\delta})=O(k+\log\log m+\log\frac{1}{\delta})=O(k+\log\frac{1}{\delta}).

We set
\delta=m^{-O(1)}
and have
\log\frac{1}{\delta}=O(\log m)
, thus there exists a constant
C=O(1)
such that it suffices to set
\mathrm{call}\geq Ck
. Then we can apply a union bound and know that with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, we can obtain all
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathrm{iter}}
such that
|T_{i}|=t-k
and
T_{i}\cap S_{i-1}=\varnothing
. Finally, by using Lemma
2.4
and applying another union bound again, we know that
\Pr\{S_{\mathrm{iter}}=S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}=1-\delta
.
∎

Completing the coupling argument.

We now use the guarantee for the ghost algorithm from Lemma
6.6
to complete the proof of Lemma
6.2
from the beginning of this section.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

In Algorithm
5
, in order to calculate
\tau
which is the number of calls to the oracle we need, we look at the coupled Algorithm
4
. There are two parts that need to be computed: (i)
\mathrm{call}
, the number of calls we need to obtain each
T_{i}
and (ii)
\mathrm{iter}
, the number of iterations we need. Let
\delta=m^{-O(1)}
be the failure probability. We set
\mathrm{call}\geq Ck
for some constant
C=O(1)
and
\mathrm{iter}=O(\log^{3}m)
, then we have
\tau=\mathrm{call}\times\mathrm{iter}\geq Ck\log^{3}m
.
According to Lemma
6.6
, we know
S_{\mathrm{iter}}=S_{\mathrm{DPP}}
holds with probability
1-\delta
. Furthermore, since we almost surely have

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{iter}}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=0}^{\mathrm{iter}-1}(S_{i}\cup T_{i})=\tilde{S},

thus we know
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\subseteq\tilde{S}
holds with probability
1-\delta
. Thus we finish the proof.
∎

Now with Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
, we prove our main result as follows.

6.3
Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

For
k\geq\log m
, we set
\tau\geq Ck\log^{3}m
for some constant
C=O(1)
which is specified in the proof of Lemma
6.6
and larger than
2
. We set
k^{\prime}=\min\{\tau/\log^{3}m,r\}
and consider
\mathrm{DPP}
sample
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim k^{\prime}
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}\bar{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})
, and let
\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}
be the corresponding projection matrix. Let
\tilde{S}
be the output of Algorithm
5
with given
\tau
, and let
\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}
be the corresponding projection matrix. We define
\mathcal{E}
as the event
\{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\subseteq\tilde{S}\}
, then according to Lemma
6.2
, event
\mathcal{E}
holds with probability
1-\delta
. Thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}]=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}+{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}\mid\mathcal{E}^{c}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}\mid\mathcal{E}^{c}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}\cdot\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-\delta\cdot\mathbf{I}

where the first step follows from the law of total expectation, the second step follows since the projection matrices are PSD, the third step follows from the definition of
\mathcal{E}
and Lemma
6.3
, the fourth step follows from the law of total expectation, the fifth step follows from Lemma
6.3
and the last step follows since
\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}\geq 1-\delta
. Notice that since
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
, we know that
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}
has the same spectrum as
\mathbf{A}
and thus
\bar{\kappa}_{k}(\bar{\mathbf{A}})=\bar{\kappa}_{k}(\mathbf{A})=\bar{\kappa}_{k}
. By applying Lemma
4.1
to
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
we know that
\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])\geq\frac{k^{\prime}-k}{k^{\prime}-k-1+(r-k)\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}}
. By combining this with the above result we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq

\displaystyle~\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])-\delta

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{k^{\prime}-k}{k^{\prime}-k-1+(r-k)\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}}-\delta

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle~\frac{\tau-k\log^{3}m}{\tau-k\log^{3}m+(r-k)\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\tau-k\log^{3}m}{\tau-(\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}+1)k\log^{3}m+r\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

(6.1)

where the third step follows from
k^{\prime}\leq\tau/\log^{3}m
. We can verify that by treating
\bar{\kappa}_{k}
as a constant and considering the above expression (Eq.(

)) as a function of
k

. By using
k\leq\tau/(C\log^{3}m)
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau}{C\tau-(\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}+1)\tau+Cr\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{(C-1-\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2})\tau/r+C\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta.

We consider two cases here. If
\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\geq C-1
, then by using

we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{C\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta.

If
\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}<C-1

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{(C-1-\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2})\log^{3}m+C\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{(C-1)\log^{3}m+(C-1)\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{\tau/r}{(\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}+1)\log^{3}m}-\delta\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\delta

where we use the fact that
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\geq 1
. Finally, according to Remark
6.1
we can see that the failure probability is actually
\delta=m^{-9}
. After some calculation we know that as long as
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\leq m^{4}
, we have the following holds:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{\tilde{S}}])\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}-\frac{1}{m^{9}}\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2.5\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}m}.

∎

6.4
Comparison with Direct
k
-DPP Sampling

In this section we compare our result with the results from
[
ALV22
]
, and show an improvement of at least
O(k^{\omega-2})
. The main result of approximately sampling
k
-DPP in
[
ALV22
]
is Lemma
2.3
, however our setting is different in that we are not given the PSD matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, instead we only know
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. We can naively first compute
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
which takes
O(m^{\omega-1}n)
then use Lemma
2.3
, resulting in prohibitive
\tilde{O}(m^{\omega-1}n+mk^{\omega-1})
cost of sampling one
k
-DPP. As an alternative, we can improve this result by not computing
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
explicitly.

By slightly revising the proof in
[
ALV22
]
, we show that given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we can obtain
s
independent approximate samples from
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})
in time
\tilde{O}(mnk^{\omega-2}+snk^{\omega-1})
. To be specific there are two steps, here we give a brief proof sketch of each step without rigorous proof.

•

Preprocessing: according to
[
ALV22
]
, we first need to obtain an exact
k
-DPP sample defined on an
8k\times 8k
PSD matrix. Constructing this matrix takes
O(nk^{\omega-1})
, and sampling
k
-DPP defined by it takes
\tilde{O}(k^{\omega})
, thus it takes
\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1}+k^{\omega})=\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1})
. Then by using Theorem 34 in
[
ALV22
]
, Algorithm
1
produces marginal overestimates
q_{i}\geq\Pr\{i\in S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}
with sum
\sum_{i\in[m]}q_{i}\leq 4k
in time
\tilde{O}(\frac{m}{k}\cdot nk^{\omega-1})=\tilde{O}(mnk^{\omega-2})
. After the preprocessing step, the distribution is in approximately isotropic position.

•

Sampling: after the preprocessing step, we obtain an approximately isotropic distribution, and the next step is to get independent approximate samples. This step is relatively easy, we can directly use Lemma
6.1
and know that we can obtain approximate samples from
k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})
in time
\tilde{O}(1)\cdot\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1})=\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1})
. Here we use the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}(4k,k)=\tilde{O}(n(4k)^{\omega-1}+(4k)^{\omega})=\tilde{O}(nk^{\omega-1})
with
\mu=k
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})
is the time of sampling exact DPP.

By the above two steps, we conclude that we can obtain independent approximate
k
-DPP samples in time
\tilde{O}(mnk^{\omega-2}+snk^{\omega-1})
. In comparison, our Lemma
6.2
only needs an
\tilde{O}(mn)
preprocessing time and
O(k\log^{3}m)
calls to an uniform sampling oracle to guarantee the same failure probability, thus the total cost is only
\tilde{O}(mn+sk\log^{3}m)=\tilde{O}(mn+sk)
which is at least an
O(k^{\omega-2})
improvement.

Approximate Sketch-and-Project

In this section we detail the idea and discussion in Section
4.3
, and show how we approximate the update step of sketch-and-project by solving a preconditioned normal equation as a sub-problem. For notation convenience we use
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
to denote the ones after randomized Hadamard transform, which is the original
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}
respectively for
\mathbf{Q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}
. Specifically,
\mathbf{A}
in this section corresponds to
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
in Section
6
.

7.1
Approximate Update by Solving Sub-Problem

In the update rule of sketch-and-project (see Eq.(
4.1
)), we need to compute
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}:=(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{b})
in each iteration. We can directly compute this quantity: we first compute
\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}
in
O(n\tau)
, then compute
\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A})^{\top}
in
O(n\tau^{\omega-1})
, then compute
(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top})^{\dagger}
in
O(\tau^{\omega})
, and finally apply
\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}
then
(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top})^{\dagger}
to a vector, which takes
O(n\tau+\tau^{2})
time. These steps take
O(n\tau^{\omega-1}+\tau^{\omega})
in total, which is prohibitive for our per-iteration cost. Instead of directly computing
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}
, we show that it is possible to
approximately
compute this quantity in
\tilde{O}(n\tau+\tau^{\omega})
time, by relying on the fact that it can be transformed into a highly under-determined least squares problem, and using a variant of the sketch-to-precondition approach
[
RT08
,
MSM14
]
.

Denote
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times n}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{b}
, notice that since
\tau\leq n
, we can transform the problem of computing
(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{b})
to solving an under-determined least squares problem:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}=\arg\min\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}~~~\text{subject to}~~~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}

(7.1)

which has solution
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}
. It is equivalent to solving the second kind normal equation:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t},~~~\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}.

;

time.

\mathbf{M}\leftarrow\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}

return

\mathbf{M}

Notice that
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}
is a positive semidefinite linear system over
\mathbf{u}
, thus we can use CG to solve it. However since we cannot bound
\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})
, we need to precondition this linear system first. Notice that the subspace embedding result of Lemma
2.7
directly implies a way to construct a preconditioner. By calling Algorithm
6
and treating

as a fixed constant, we can obtain an invertible matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{*}=\arg\min\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}~~~\text{subject to}~~~\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t}

(7.2)

and similarly, we can reformulate Problem(

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{t},~~~\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}.

\mathbf{u}

Lemma 7.1

(Convergence)
.

Given
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau\times n},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau}
, let
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
be the least squares solution of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
. Given
\delta<1/2
, if the subspace embedding matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\phi\times n}
from Lemma
2.7
satisfies

1-\delta

outputs
\mathbf{M}
in time

such that
\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})=O(1)
, and solving the following system

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}},~~~\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}

(7.3)

by using CG and starting from

will yield the following convergence result:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{s}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|\leq 2\left(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-1}{\tilde{\kappa}+1}\right)^{s}\cdot\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{*}
be the min-length solution of the PSD linear system
(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{*}
as the solution of Problem(
7.3
), then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{*}=

\displaystyle~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M})^{\dagger}\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{M}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{w}^{*}

which shows that the optimal solution of Problem(
7.3
) is the same as that of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
. According to Lemma
2.7
we know that with the choice of
\phi=O(\tau+\log(1/\delta))
,
\tilde{\kappa}=O(1)
holds with probability
1-\delta
. According to Lemma
2.6
, the convergence guarantee is

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{u}_{s}-\mathbf{u}^{*})\|\leq 2\left(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-1}{\tilde{\kappa}+1}\right)^{s}\cdot\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}^{*}\|

and by doing the change of variable back from
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathbf{w}
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{s}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|\leq 2\left(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-1}{\tilde{\kappa}+1}\right)^{s}\cdot\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|.

Finally we compute the cost of constructing the preconditioner. Following Lemma
2.7
and Algorithm
6
, we first construct
\mathbf{\Phi}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
which takes
O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{2}\log^{4}(\tau/\delta))
, then compute the compact size SVD as
\mathbf{\Phi}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}=\tilde{\mathbf{U}}\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
which takes

where

, and finally we set
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{-1}
which takes
O(\tau^{2})

\displaystyle O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{2}\log^{4}(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{\omega-1}\phi)=O(n\tau\log(\tau/\delta)+\tau^{\omega})=\tilde{O}(n\tau+\tau^{\omega}).

∎

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}).

Since we assume that
\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
is not in the nullspace of
\mathbf{A}
, by induction we know
\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
will always be not in the nullspace of
\mathbf{A}
(same as the argument in proof of Lemma
4.3
in Section
7.2
). Next, we proceed with a standard convergence analysis of block coordinate descent:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*})=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})\mathbf{A}^{1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})\mathbf{A}^{1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})

where
\mathbf{P}_{S}=\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{S}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}^{1/2}
is the projection matrix according to
\mathbf{A}^{1/2}
and
S
.
Similarly, but with slight difference, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}^{1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{S})^{2}](\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(1-\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}]))\cdot\|\mathbf{A}^{1/2}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(1-\rho)\cdot\|(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}

where we denote
\rho=\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])
. According to Theorem 4 of
[
ALV22
]
we know with probability
1-n^{-10}>0.99
, the marginal probabilities are approximately isotropic. According to Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
2.4
, conditioned on this event, there is a coupling
(S,S_{\mathrm{DPP}})
where
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim\tau
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A})
, such that
S=S_{\mathrm{DPP}}
with probability
1-n^{-O(1)}
. Denote
\mathcal{E}
as the event
\{S=S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\}
, we have
\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}=1-\delta
where
\delta=n^{-O(1)}
. Similar to the proof of Lemma
4.2
in Section
6.3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}]=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}+{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}\mid\mathcal{E}^{c}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}\mid\mathcal{E}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}\mid\mathcal{E}^{c}]\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-\Pr\{\mathcal{E}^{c}\}\cdot\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}]-\delta\cdot\mathbf{I}.

Next we apply Lemma
4.1
to
\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with rank
r
, by denoting
S_{\mathrm{DPP}}\sim\tau
-
\mathrm{DPP}(\mathbf{A})
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{\tau-k}{\tau-k-1+(r-k)\bar{\kappa}_{k}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau}{(C-1)\tau+C(r-k)\bar{\kappa}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{(C-1-\bar{\kappa}_{k})\tau/r+C\bar{\kappa}_{k}}.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we consider two cases here. If
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\geq C-1
, then by using
C\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])\geq\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{C\bar{\kappa}_{k}}\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}}.

If
\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}<C-1
, then we use the assumption that
\tau\leq r
and have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])\geq\frac{(C-1)\tau/r}{(C-1-\bar{\kappa}_{k})+C\bar{\kappa}_{k}}=\frac{\tau/r}{1+\bar{\kappa}_{k}}\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}}

Finally, according to Remark
6.1
we can see that the failure probability is actually
\delta=n^{-9}
. After some calculation we know that as long as
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\leq n^{8}
, we have the following holds:

\displaystyle\rho=\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])\geq\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S_{\mathrm{DPP}}}])-\delta\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2\bar{\kappa}_{k}}-\frac{1}{n^{9}}\geq\frac{\tau/r}{3\bar{\kappa}_{k}}.

Thus we have the following convergence result:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau/r}{3\bar{\kappa}_{k}}\right)^{t}\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}.

By further choosing
t_{\max}=O(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}/\tau\cdot\log(100/\epsilon))
and denoting
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{\max}}
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|/100
. By using Markov’s inequality and taking an union bound, we have
\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
holds with probability
0.98
. Next we compute the time complexity. According to Theorem 34 in
[
ALV22
]
, the preprocessing step takes
O(\frac{n}{\tau}\log n\cdot\tau^{\omega}\log^{3}n)=O(n\tau^{\omega-1}\log^{4}n)
. As for the sampling procedure, generating each approximate
\tau
-
\mathrm{DPP}
sample takes
O(\tau^{\omega}\log^{3}n)
. Further notice that in Algorithm
7
the step of computing
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{S}\mathbf{b}
takes
O(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{S,:}))
, thus the overall time complexity is

\displaystyle~O\left(n\tau^{\omega-1}\log^{4}n+r\bar{\kappa}_{k}/\tau\cdot\log(1/\epsilon)\cdot(\max_{S:|S|=\tau}\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{S,:})+\tau^{\omega}\log^{3}n)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O\Big((\mathrm{nnz}^{*}(\mathbf{A})+n\tau^{\omega-1}\log^{4}n)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}\log 1/\epsilon\Big)

where
\mathrm{nnz}^{*}(\mathbf{A})=n\cdot\max_{i}\{\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}_{i,:})\}\leq n^{2}
.
∎

Implicit PSD Linear Systems

In this section we give a proof of Theorem
3.2
which is our second main theorem. Though there are some differences, the proof idea is similar with that of Theorem
3.1
, which is detailed in Section
4
. Here, we actually show a more general result, which is that one can use Algorithm

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{c}
, with any
\mathbf{c}
(this becomes useful for our sparse least squares solver in Section

[
LL10

GR15

\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

In the algorithm, we preprocess the
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{c}
linear system (from both left and right) with
\mathbf{Q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}
:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}^{\top})(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}^{\top})\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c},~~~\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{z}.

For notation convenience we use
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{c}
to denote the ones after randomized Hadamard transform (which is the original
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{c}
), and we will transform back to the original
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{c}
in the end of the proof. Thus we need to solve the implicit PSD linear system
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}
, where the optimal solution is
\mathbf{z}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{c}
. Denote
\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\tau}
as the column sampling matrix where
\tilde{S}
is the output of Algorithm
5
and
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\tau}
. Consider the following sketch-and-project update rule:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t+1}=\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{c}).

(9.1)

Denote
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{c}
, by right multiplying by
\mathbf{A}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{t+1}=\mathbf{y}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\dagger}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{y}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\underbrace{(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}}_{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}}.

Similarly, instead of directly computing
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}
we consider solving the sub-problem
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}=\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
. We construct the preconditioner
\mathbf{M}
similarly as Lemma
7.1
, and consider the following linear system

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{c}},~~~\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u},

then Lemma
7.1
guarantees that
\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M})=O(1)
holds with probability
1-\delta_{1}
. Denote
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
as the optimal solution, by calling
\mathrm{CG}(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top},\mathbf{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{c}},s)
and starting from
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, Lemma
2.6
gives the following convergence result:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{u}_{s}-\mathbf{u}^{*})\|\leq 2\left(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-1}{\tilde{\kappa}+1}\right)^{s}\cdot\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}^{*}\|.

where
\tilde{\kappa}=O(1)
holds with probability
1-\delta_{1}
. Define
\mathcal{E}
as the event that
\tilde{\kappa}=O(1)
, if event
\mathcal{E}
holds, then by choosing
s_{\max}=O(\log(1/\epsilon))
and changing the variable back from
\mathbf{u}
to
\mathbf{w}
we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{w}_{s_{\max}}-\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*})\|\leq\epsilon\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}\|.

Denote
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}
as the update of
exact
sketch-and-project, notice that our approximate update is
\mathbf{z}_{t+1}=\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{I}_{\tilde{S}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{s_{\max}}
. Denote
\Delta_{t}=\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}_{t+1})\|
as the matrix
\mathbf{A}
norm difference between our approximate update and exact sketch-and-project update, and we bound it as

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}_{t+1})\|=\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{w}_{s_{\max}}-\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\epsilon\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{w}_{t}^{*}\|=\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\epsilon(\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}^{*}\|+\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}^{*}\|)\leq 2\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|.

Notice that
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}
is the same as the update of exact block coordinate descent on the PSD linear system
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}
, so similarly as before, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle~(1-\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}]))\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(1-\rho)\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}.

where we denote
\rho=\lambda_{\min}^{+}({\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{P}_{S}])
. By combining the above two results we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}\|(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1})+(\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\Delta_{t}^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}+2\Delta_{t}\cdot({\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\Delta_{t}^{2}+(1-\rho)\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}+2\Delta_{t}\sqrt{1-\rho}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(\sqrt{1-\rho}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|+\Delta_{t})^{2}.

Notice that if event
\mathcal{E}
holds, then we have
\Delta_{t}\leq 2\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|
by choosing
s_{\max}=\log(1/\epsilon)
. If event
\mathcal{E}
does not hold, we use the same trick as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
and bound
\Delta_{t}
as
\Delta_{t}\leq 8\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|
. To conclude we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}[\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{E}]\cdot\Pr\{\mathcal{E}\}+{\mathbb{E}}[\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}\mid\bar{\mathcal{E}}]\cdot\Pr\{\bar{\mathcal{E}}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(1-\delta_{1})\cdot(\sqrt{1-\rho}+2\epsilon)^{2}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}+\delta_{1}\cdot(\sqrt{1-\rho}+8)^{2}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~[(\sqrt{1-\rho}+2\epsilon)^{2}+81\delta_{1}]\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}.

We set
\epsilon=\rho/8
and
\delta_{1}=\rho/324
, notice that
\sqrt{1-\rho}+\rho/4\leq\sqrt{1-\rho/2}
, thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\rho}{4}\right)\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}.

By applying Lemma
4.2
to
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, we know that by setting
\tau\geq Ck\log^{3}n
for some constant
C=O(1)
, then we have
\rho\geq\frac{\tau/r}{2.5\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}n}
. By combining this with the above result we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{t}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau/r}{10\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}n}\right)^{t}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}_{0}-\mathbf{z}^{*})\|^{2}.

Notice that this is the convergence result for solving
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{c}
for an arbitrary
\mathbf{c}
. By choosing
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, we have
\mathbf{z}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{b}
which is the least squares solution of linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{b}
we want. Finally, notice that both the matrix
\mathbf{A}
and vector
\mathbf{c}
here are after the randomized Hadamard transform, and we need to transform the variable back. Since
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
, the quantity
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}
here is the identical to the original
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
, thus we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau/r}{10\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log^{3}n}\right)^{t}\cdot\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}.

By further choosing
\tau=O(k\cdot\log^{3}n),\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
t_{\max}=O(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}/k\cdot\log(100/\epsilon))
we have
{\mathbb{E}}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{100}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}
. By applying Markov’s inequality we have
\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\epsilon\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\|^{2}
holds with probability
0.98
. Finally by using Lemma
2.7
we compute the total cost as

\displaystyle~O(mn\log n)+t_{\max}\cdot O(m\tau\log(\tau/\delta_{1})+\tau^{\omega}+m\tau s_{\max})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O(mn\log n)+r\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}/k\cdot\log(100/\epsilon)\cdot O(mk\log^{3}n\log(r\bar{\kappa}_{k})+k^{\omega}\log^{3\omega}n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O(mn\log n+(mr\log^{3}n\log(r\bar{\kappa}_{k})+rk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}n)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log 1/\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O(mn\log n+(mr\log^{4}n+rk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}n)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log 1/\epsilon)

where the last step follows from the assumption
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\leq n^{4}
and thus
\log(r\bar{\kappa}_{k})=O(\log n)
.
∎

Least Squares Regression

In this section, consider a tall least squares problem where, given an
n\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\gg d
, we want to find

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}.

Note that one could simply apply our Algorithm
3
and use Theorem
3.2
to get the complexity analysis, obtaining runtime
\tilde{O}(nd+dk^{\omega-1})
. However, in this setting, we can further improve on this approach for sparse matrices by combining it with a standard sketch-to-precondition iterative solver for tall least squares
[
RT08
]
. In this approach, one first constructs a nearly square sketch
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
of the tall matrix
\mathbf{A}
, by using a subspace embedding such as the one from Lemma
2.7
, at the cost of
\tilde{O}(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+d^{2})
. Then, one constructs a preconditioner of
\mathbf{A}
by performing an SVD (or QR) decomposition of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, at the cost of
\tilde{O}(d^{\omega})
. Now, we can simply run a preconditioned iterative solver in time
\tilde{O}(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+d^{2})
to solve the least squares task. For matrices with
k
large singular values, the preconditioner construction can be further accelerated by using approximate SVD with power iteration in time
\tilde{O}(d^{2}k^{\omega-2})
.

In this section, we show that instead of directly constructing the preconditioner from
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, we can use our Algorithm
3
as an inner solver, which will act as a preconditioner for the outer iterative method. In this case, as our outer iterative method we use preconditioned gradient descent.

Theorem 10.1

(Least squares)
.

Given matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})=r
, let
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
be the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
in decreasing order. For
\log d\leq k<r
, define
\bar{\kappa}_{k}:=(\frac{1}{r-k}\sum_{j>k}^{r}\sigma_{j}^{2}/\sigma_{r}^{2})^{1/2}
and assume for simplicity that
\bar{\kappa}_{k}\leq d^{4}
.
Given
\epsilon>0
, running Algorithm
8
with choice
\tau\geq Ck\log^{3}d
for some
C=O(1)
,
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0},s_{\max}=O(\log(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}/\tau)),t_{\max}=O(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}/k)
and

will output
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
such that with probability
0.99
we have
\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}\leq\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\epsilon\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
in time

\displaystyle O\left(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})\log(d/\epsilon)+(d^{2}\log^{4}d+dk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}d)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log 1/\epsilon\right).

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})=r
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider solving the least squares problem
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|
with (approximate) preconditioned gradient descent

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{g}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\underbrace{(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b})}_{\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}}

where
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{A}
is such that
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\approx_{1+\epsilon_{H}}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
for some constant
\epsilon_{H}
that will be specified later. By using the fact that
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})=\mathbf{g}_{t}
we have
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}}=\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}
, and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}-2(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}+\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}-2\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{g}_{t}+\|\mathbf{H}^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{g}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}-2\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}}^{2}+(1+\epsilon_{H})\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}-(1-\epsilon_{H})\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{\dagger}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}-(1-\epsilon_{H})^{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~2\epsilon_{H}\cdot\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}

By choosing
\epsilon_{H}=1/8
we have
\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}
. Suppose for now that we can construct a randomized estimate
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}]\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}
, where
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}
is the expectation conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
. Then, since we also assume that
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\approx_{1+\epsilon_{H}}\mathbf{H}
for
\epsilon_{H}=1/8
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\big]\leq\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{H}}{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}\big]\leq\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon_{H}}}{4}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}\leq\frac{1+\epsilon_{H}}{4}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\leq\frac{1}{3}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}.

By combining the above two results we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\big]=

\displaystyle~{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}+{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}-\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}+\frac{1}{3}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{\mathbf{H}}+\frac{1}{3}\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|_{\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{5}{6}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|.

To achieve this convergence result, there are two parts left that need to be done: (i) construct the preconditioner
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\approx_{1+1/8}\mathbf{H}
is a spectral approximation; and (ii) compute the randomized estimate
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\big[\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}\big]\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}
.
For the first part, we use sparse embedding matrix to construct
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{A}
. According to Lemma
2.7
we know
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\approx_{1+1/8}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
holds with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}
by choosing
\phi=O(d+\log(1/\delta_{1}))
, and the construction takes
O(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})\log(d/\delta_{1})+d^{2}\log^{4}(d/\delta_{1}))
time.

For the second part, to approximate
\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}
, we treat it as solving the linear system
(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{g}_{t}
where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\phi\times d}
for
\phi=O(d+\log(1/\delta_{1}))
and
\mathbf{g}_{t}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{b})
is given. Notice that since
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is an
(1+\frac{1}{8})
-spectral approximation of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
, we know that all the singular values of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
are constant factor approximations of the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
, thus we know
\bar{\kappa}_{k}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})
and
\bar{\kappa}_{k}(\mathbf{A})
only differs within a constant factor. We apply Algorithm
3
to
(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\phi\times d},\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{g}_{t},\mathbf{p}_{0}=\mathbf{0})
with choice
\tau\geq Ck\log^{3}d,s_{\max}=O(\log(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}/k))
and
t_{\max}=O(r\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}/k)
(note that the RHT preprocessing step from line 2 can use the same RHT across all gradient descent iterations). According to the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we know that with the right choice of constants the output
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}
will satisfy
{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\|\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}-\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}]\leq\frac{1}{4}\|\mathbf{p}_{t}^{*}\|_{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}
in time

\displaystyle O(\phi d\log d+(\phi r\log^{4}d+rk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}d)\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2})=O((d^{2}\log^{4}d+dk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}d)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}).

With the above analysis, we have the following convergence result, where the expectation is taken over the entire run of the algorithm, conditioning on successful construction and preprocessing of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
(which holds with high probability):

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{T}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\big]\leq\Big(\frac{5}{6}\Big)^{T}\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|

Thus by choosing
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
T=O(\log(1/\epsilon))
, using Markov’s inequality we can obtain that with probability 0.99,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}_{T}
will satisfy
\|\mathbf{A}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
, which in turn implies
\|\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{b}\|^{2}+\epsilon\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}
.
The overall time complexity of the procedure is:

\displaystyle~O\left(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})\log d+d^{2}\log^{4}d+(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})+\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}d^{2}\log^{4}d+\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}dk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}d)\log 1/\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~O\left(\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A})\log(d/\epsilon)+(d^{2}\log^{4}d+dk^{\omega-1}\log^{3\omega}d)\cdot\bar{\kappa}_{k}^{2}\log 1/\epsilon\right).

∎

Appendix A
Omitted Proofs

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

We first state Lemma
A.1
which is the tail bounds for sums of geometric random variables.

Lemma A.1

(Theorem 2.1 in
[
Jan18
]
)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent geometric random variables with possibly different distributions:
X_{i}\sim p_{i}
where
0<p_{i}\leq 1
. Let
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\mu:={\mathbb{E}}[X]
and
p_{*}:=\min_{i}p_{i}
. For any
\lambda\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\{X\geq\lambda\mu\}\leq e^{-p_{*}\mu(\lambda-1-\ln\lambda)}.

For
i\in[t]
, we define random variable
X_{i}
as the number of calls it take to obtain a new sample in the
i
-th step (that is, we have obtained
i-1
samples and want to get the
i
-th new sample). Then we know that
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
are independent geometric random variables with different distributions:
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Ge}(p_{i})
where
p_{i}=1-\frac{k+i-1}{m}
. We denote
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i}
and also define

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu:={\mathbb{E}}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}[X_{i}]=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\\
p_{*}:=\min_{i}p_{i}=1-\frac{k+t-1}{m}\end{cases}

Then we have

\displaystyle\mu=

\displaystyle~\frac{m}{m-k}+\frac{m}{m-k-1}+\cdots+\frac{m}{m-k-t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{1-\frac{k}{m}}+\frac{1}{1-\frac{k+1}{m}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{1-\frac{k+t-1}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~(1+\frac{2k}{m})+(1+\frac{2(k+1)}{m})+\cdots+(1+\frac{2(k+t-1)}{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~t+\frac{2kt+t(t-1)}{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~t\cdot\left(1+\frac{2k+t-1}{m}\right)

where the third step follows from
k,t\ll m
. Next we lower bound
p_{*}\mu
:

\displaystyle p_{*}\mu=

\displaystyle~\left(1-\frac{k+t-1}{m}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{k}{m}}+\frac{1}{1-\frac{k+1}{m}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{1-\frac{k+t-1}{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~t-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{t-i}{m-(k+i-1)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~t-t\cdot\frac{t-1}{m-k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\frac{2}{3}t

where the third step follows since
\frac{t-i}{m-(k+i-1)}
is decreasing in
i
and the last step follows from
k,t\ll m
. Now we can bound the tail probability by using concentration inequality of geometric distribution. We use Lemma
A.1
by setting
\lambda=2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}
and have

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{X\geq(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\mu\right\}\leq

\displaystyle~\exp(-p_{*}\mu(1+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\log(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\exp(-\frac{2t}{3}(1+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\log(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})))

In order to show that RHS is less than
\delta
, we only need to show
1+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}-\log(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\geq\frac{3}{2t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}
. We define
f(x)=1+\frac{x}{2}-\log(2+2x)
, then we know
f(x)
is decreasing for
0\leq x\leq 1
and increasing for
x>1
, thus
f_{\min}=f(1)=\frac{3}{2}-\log 4>0
. Since
\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}>0
we have
f(\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})>0
, which gives

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{X\geq(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\mu\right\}\leq\delta.

This shows that with probability at least
1-\delta
, the number of calls to the oracle is at most

\displaystyle X\leq(2+\frac{2}{t}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\cdot t\cdot\left(1+\frac{2k+t-1}{m}\right)\leq 4t+4\log\frac{1}{\delta}

which is
O(t+\log\frac{1}{\delta})
.
∎
