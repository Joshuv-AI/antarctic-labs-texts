On a probabilistic extension of the Oldenburger-Kolakoski sequence
Chloé Boisson, Damien Jamet, Irène Marcovici

Abstract
The Oldenburger-Kolakoski sequence is the only infinite sequence over the alphabet { 1 , 2 } \{1,2\} that starts with 1 and is its own run-length encoding. In the present work, we take a step back from this largely known and studied sequence by introducing some randomness in the choice of the letters written. This enables us to provide some results on the convergence of the density of 1 1 ’s in the resulting sequence. When the choice of the letters is given by an infinite sequence of i.i.d. random variables or by a Markov chain, the average densities of letters converge. Moreover, in the case of i.i.d. random variables, we are able to prove that the densities even almost surely converge.

2.3
Extension of the definition to infinite directing sequences

Now that we have started studying the notions of directing and directed sequences, a natural question arises: << Can one extend the definition of
\mathcal{O}_{T}
sequences to infinite (possibly not periodic) sequences
T
? >>
Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\star}
be an alphabet and let
T=(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
be an infinite sequence over
\mathcal{A}
.
By construction,
\mathcal{O}_{t_{1}\dots t_{n}}
is a prefix of
\mathcal{O}_{t_{1}\dots t_{n+1}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
. One thus defines
\mathcal{O}_{T}
as the limit of
\mathcal{O}_{t_{1}\dots t_{n}}
when
n
tends to infinity:
\mathcal{O}_{T}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{O}_{t_{1}\dots t_{n}}
.

The present work deals with the densities of letters in
\mathcal{O}_{T}
when
T=(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
is an infinite sequence over
\mathcal{A}
. Do these densities exist? If so, how much are they value?

We are especially interested in the case where the directing sequence is random. Let
\mathbb{T}=(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
be a sequence of random variables. By definition, the sequence directed by
\mathbb{T}
is the random sequence
\mathbb{X}=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
defined by
\mathbb{X}=\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{T}}}
.

The present paper is organized as follows. In section
3
, we consider the case where the directing sequence is made of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables over a two-letter alphabet. In section
4
, we treat the case where the random sequence
\mathbb{X}
is directed by a Markov chain.

3
Sequence directed by independent random variables

In the present section,
\mathbb{T}=(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables (i.i.d. for short) over the two-letter alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2\}
, with
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=1)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=2)=1-p
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, where
p
is a fixed parameter in
]0,1[
. The sequence
\mathbb{T}
is thus distributed according to the
product distribution

(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
. We denote
\mathbb{T}\sim((p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
be the sequence directed by
\mathbb{T}
. The sequence
\mathbb{X}
is a random sequence with
a priori
unknown distribution. Assume that one wants to compute the
n
th
letter
X_{n}
of
\mathbb{X}
, for some large integer
n
. Unless the sequence
\mathbb{T}
begins with a long succession of
1
’s (an event which has a low probability to occur), one just has to read the first terms
T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}
of
\mathbb{T}
, until knowing the length and position of the block containing
X_{n}
, and to fill that block by
1
’s with probability
p
, or by
2
’s with probability
1-p
. The resulting value of
X_{n}
obtained that way will have the desired distribution. This leads to the fact that
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}=1)=p
, and we can even use this observation to compute more precisely the value of
{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}=1)
, as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

If
\mathbb{T}\sim(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
with
p\in]0,1[
, then for any
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{(X_{n}=1)}=p(1-p^{n-2}+p^{n-1}).

Proof.

We will decompose the event
\{X_{n}=1\}
according to the partition
\{\mathcal{S}_{n,k}:k\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket\}
of
\{1,2\}^{n}
introduced in the previous section, and use the observations (
) and (
).
The following two particular cases are obvious:

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,n})=p,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,n-1})=0.

Let us now consider some
(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, with
k<n-1
. Let
k^{\prime}
be the unique integer such that
|w_{k^{\prime}-1}|<n
and
|w_{k^{\prime}}|\geq n
. Concretely, the
n
th
letter
X_{n}
is written during the
k^{\prime}
th
step, and it is equal to
T_{k^{\prime}}
. By definition of
\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, we have
k^{\prime}\geq k
. Let us show that
k^{\prime}\neq k
. We reason by contradiction and assume that
k^{\prime}=k
. Let us now look at what we know at the end of the
k-1
th
step.

•

By definition of
k^{\prime}
,
X_{n}
will be written in the next block to the right of
w_{k^{\prime}-1}
.

•

By definition of
k
, we do not know the length of this block yet.

Consequently, we do not know the length of any (empty) block to the right of
w_{k^{\prime}-1}
. It directly implies that
w_{k^{\prime}-1}
is made of 1’s (and thus, that
w_{k^{\prime}-1}=1^{k^{\prime}-1}
). It follows that
n\leq|w_{k^{\prime}}|\leq|w_{k^{\prime}-1}|+2=k^{\prime}+1
. Since
k^{\prime}=k<n-1
, we get a contradiction, which means that
k<k^{\prime}
.

The fact that
(T_{1},...,T_{n})
belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
only depends on the beginning
(T_{1},...,T_{k})
of the sequence. Recall that
\mathbb{T}
has a product distribution
(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}.
Since
k^{\prime}>k
, and
X_{n}=T_{k^{\prime}}
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=p.

Finally, we use the formula of total probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\;\mathbb{P}((T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})\times\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}((T_{1},...T_{n-1})=(1,...,1))\times\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n-1})=(1,...,1))

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}((T_{1},...T_{n-1})=(1,...,2))\times\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\,|\,(T_{1},...,T_{n-1})=(1,...,2))

\displaystyle=\big(1-(p^{n}+2(1-p)p^{n-1}+p^{n-2}(1-p)^{2})\big)\times p+p^{n}\times 1+0

\displaystyle=p(1-p^{n-2}+p^{n-1}).

∎

As a corollary, we obtain the following convergence of the proportion of
1
’s in
\mathbb{X}
.

Corollary 1
.

If
\mathbb{T}\sim(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
with
p\in]0,1[
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}(|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1})}{n}=p

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{X}
has values in
\{1,2\}
, we have
|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1}=2n-(X_{1}+...+X_{n}).
It follows that

\dfrac{\mathbb{E}(|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1})}{n}=2-\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{k})}{n}.

By Proposition
1
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1)=p
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=2)=1-p
. We deduce that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=2-p
.
By Cesàro lemma, we obtain:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}(|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1})}{n}=2-(2-p)=p.

∎

Each time we run a simulation with
\mathbb{T}\sim(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
, the frequency of
1
’s in
\mathbb{X}
seems to converge to
p
. We thus expect the sequence
{|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1}}/{n}
to converge almost surely to
p
, and not only in expectation. Since the random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
are correlated, we can not directly apply the strong law of large numbers (SLLN) to prove the almost sure convergence of
(X_{1}+...+X_{n})/n
. However, the correlations being sufficiently weak, we can apply the following stronger version of the SLLN.

Theorem 1

(Lyons
[
11
]
)
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N^{\star}}}
be a sequence of real-valued random variables such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N^{\star}},

|Y_{n}|\leq 1
and

\forall n,m\in\mathbb{N^{\star}},\ \mathbb{E}(Y_{m}Y_{n})\leq\Phi(|n-m|),\quad\mbox{ with }\Phi\geq 0\mbox{ and }\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Phi(n)}{n}<\infty.

Then
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}=0
almost surely.

In order to apply Theorem
1
, let us first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathbb{T}\sim(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
with
p\in]0,1[
, then for any
m\geq 1
and any
n\geq m+2
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\mbox{ and\lx@nobreakspace}X_{n}=1)=p\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{n,n}\cap(X_{m}=2)=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{S}_{n,n-1}\cap(X_{m}=2)=\emptyset
, we have

\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\,\cap\,X_{n}=1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\,\cap\,X_{n}=1\,\cap\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\,\cap\,X_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\mathbb{P}((T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})\times\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\ |\ X_{n}=1\,\cap\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})

First, observe that for
k\leq n-2
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=p
.
Let us now prove that

\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\ |\ X_{n}=1\,\cap\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}).

It is equivalent to proving that when
X_{m}=2
and
(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
are not incompatible,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ X_{m}=2\,\cap\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}).

Let
i
be the integer such that the letter
X_{m}
is given by
T_{i}
.
We can decompose the event
(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
into the two following cases.

1.

If
i>k
, then after reading
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k})
, we know the size of the blocks containing
X_{m}
and
X_{n}
but not their content:

\underset{\text{$w_{k}$}}{\hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{$\times\times\times\times\times\times\times\times$}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{?}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \underset{X_{m}}{\hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{2}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \underset{X_{n}}{\hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{?}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}}.

In this case, the variables giving the values of
X_{m}
and

are independent, thus the additional information that

does not affect the probability of having

2.

If

, then reading

\underset{\text{$w_{k}$ contains $X_{m}$}}{\hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{$\times\times\times\times\times\times 2\ \times$}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{?}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{?}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{??}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}\ \underset{X_{n}}{\hbox{\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt\vtop{\hbox{\kern 1.49994pt\hbox{?}\kern 1.49994pt}\kern 1.43515pt\hrule}\kern-0.4pt\vrule height=2.15277pt,width=0.4pt,depth=1.43515pt\kern-0.4pt}}.

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ X_{m}=2\,\cap\,(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=1\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})=p.

We deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\,\cap\,X_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\mathbb{P}(T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})\times p\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2\ |\ (T_{1},...,T_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k})

\displaystyle=p\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2).

We can now state the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathbb{T}\sim(p\delta_{1}+(1-p)\delta_{2})^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}^{\star}}}
with
p\in]0,1[
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1}}{n}=p\text{ almost surely.}

Proof.

In order to apply Theorem
1
, we need to center the random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, we thus introduce the random variables
\tilde{X}_{n}=X_{n}-(2-p),
in order to have
|\tilde{X}_{n}|\leq 1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{n})=0
. Now, let us exploit Lemma
1
to compute
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{X}_{m}\tilde{X}_{n})
, for
n\geq m+2
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{m}=p\,\cap\,\tilde{X}_{n}=p-1)=p\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{m}=p\,\cap\,\tilde{X}_{n}=p)=(1-p)\times\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{m}=p-1\,\cap\,\tilde{X}_{n}=p-1)=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=2)-p\,\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{m}=p-1\,\cap\,\tilde{X}_{n}=p)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=2)-(1-p)\,\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=2).

Gathering these values and using Proposition

Let us define a function

for all

and

By Theorem

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{k}=0\mbox{ almost surely}.

Consequently,
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}=2-p\mbox{ a.s.},
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{|X_{1}\dots X_{n}|_{1}}{n}=p\text{ almost surely.}

∎

6
Conclusion and discussion

Over the alphabet
\{1,a\}
, with
a\in\{2,3\}
, we have shown that in almost all the sequences directed by an infinite sequence
\mathbb{T}=(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}
of i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=1)=p\in]0,1[
and
\mathbb{P}(T_{n}=a)=1-p
, the density of
1
’s is equal to
p
.
We have also shown that the average density of
1
’s among all sequences directed by a Markov chain with transition probability
p\in]0,1[
from
1
to
a
and from
a
to 1 is equal to
1/2
.

Keane’s conjecture
[
9
]
states that this result can be extended to the deterministic case, namely when
p=1
, over the alphabet
\{1,2\}
. On the other hand, over the alphabet
\{1,3\}
this result is not extendable to the deterministic case since the density of
1
’s in
\mathcal{O}_{1,3}
is close to 0.3972.
[
1
]
.

When
\mathbb{T}
is a Markov chain, the closer its transition probability

1

\mathcal{O}_{T}
is to share a long prefix with
\mathcal{O}_{1,3}
. Therefore, the closer the transition probability

is to 1, the closer the density of 1’s in the sequence
\mathcal{O}_{T}
is to that in the sequence
\mathcal{O}_{1,3}

eventually approaches 0.5 as this prefix gets longer. See Figure
