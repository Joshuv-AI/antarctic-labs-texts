A Toeplitz-like operator with rational matrix symbol having poles on the unit circle: Matrix representation and spectral analysis
G.J. Groenewald, S. ter Horst, J. Jaftha, A.C.M. Ran

Abstract
. In this paper we consider a class of unbounded Toeplitz operators with rational matrix symbols that have poles on the unit circle and employ state space realization techniques from linear systems theory, as used in our earlier analysis in [ 11 ] of this class of operators, to study the connection with semi-infinite Toeplitz matrices and to determine the essential spectrum and resolvent set.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 47B35, 47A53; Secondary 93C05

1.
Introduction

In
[
6
]
a class of unbounded Toeplitz-like operators was introduced, whose symbols are rational functions with poles on the unit circle. The case where the symbol is matrix-valued was first studied in
[
9
]
. Let
\Omega(z)
be a rational
m\times m
matrix function with poles on the unit circle. Then the unbounded Toeplitz-like operator
T_{\Omega}\left(H^{p}_{m}\rightarrow H^{p}_{m}\right)
with symbol
\Omega(z)
from
[
9
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Dom}(T_{\Omega})=

\displaystyle\left\{f\in H^{p}_{m}:\Omega f=h+r\textrm{ where }h\in L^{p}_{m}({\mathbb{T}}),\mbox{ and }r\in{\mathrm{Rat}}_{0}^{m}({\mathbb{T}})\right\}

\displaystyle T_{\Omega}f={\mathbb{P}}h,

\displaystyle\textrm{ where }{\mathbb{P}}\textrm{ is the Riesz projection of }L^{p}_{m}({\mathbb{T}})\textrm{ onto }H^{p}_{m}.

Here, for
1<p<\infty
,
L^{p}_{m}({\mathbb{T}})
and
H^{p}_{m}
denote the spaces of vector-valued functions of length
m
with entries in the Lebesgue space
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
over the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
and with entries in the Hardy space
H^{p}
over the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
, respectively, and
{\mathrm{Rat}}_{0}^{k}({\mathbb{T}})
is the set of strictly proper rational vector-valued functions of length
k
with poles only on the unit circle.

Assuming that
\det\Omega(z)
is not uniformly zero, in
[
9
]
a Wiener-Hopf type factorization is determined for
\Omega(z)
leading to a factorization of the Toeplitz-like operator
T_{\Omega}
, from which it can be derived that
T_{\Omega}
is Fredholm if and only if
\Omega(z)
has no zeroes on
{\mathbb{T}}
. Here a zero of
\Omega(z)
corresponds to a pole of
\Omega(z)^{-1}
in which case
\det\Omega(z)
need not be zero, unlike in the case where there are no poles on
{\mathbb{T}}
. The Wiener-Hopf type factorization allows one to determine the Fredholm index, in case
\Omega(z)
has no zeroes on
{\mathbb{T}}
. However, due to the complicated structure of this Wiener-Hopf type factorization, it appears difficult to determine the dimensions of the kernel and co-kernel of
T_{\Omega}
, and hence whether
T_{\Omega}
is invertible.

Using state space representations, in
[
11
]
we determine a criterion for invertibility of
T_{\Omega}
in terms of an associated algebraic Riccati equation. In that setting, we assume that
\Omega(z)
is given by a minimal state space realization of the form

(1.1)

\Omega(z)=R_{0}+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B+\gamma(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta,

where
R_{0}
,
A,B,C
, and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are matrices of appropriate size, and
I
indicates an identity matrix of appropriate size, such that
A
has all its eigenvalues in the open unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
and
\alpha
has all its eigenvalues in the closed unit disc
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, i.e.,
A
is stable and
\alpha
is semi-stable. Note that each rational matrix function
\Omega(z)
admits such a realization. Minimality of the realization (
1.1
) means that there does not exists a realization of the same form with
A
or
\alpha
of smaller size, and implies that the poles of
\Omega(z)
in
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
correspond to the eigenvalues of
\alpha
and the poles in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash{\mathbb{D}}
correspond to the inverses of the eigenvalues of
A
(to be interpreted as
\infty
if
0
is an eigenvalue of
A
). This is equivalent to the triples
(C,A,B)
, and
(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
both being discrete-time observable and controllable, cf.,
[
12
]
, which we will abbreviate by saying that the triples
(C,A,B)
and
(\gamma,\alpha,\beta)
are minimal. In the present paper we further exploit the state space approach initiated in
[
11
]
to determine further properties of the operator
T_{\Omega}
.

As noted in Proposition 2.1 of
[
9
]
, the space of vector polynomials of size
m
is contained in the domain of
T_{\Omega}
, and so
T_{\Omega}
generates a matrix representation with respect to the standard basis in
H^{p}_{m}
, which, analogously to the scalar case (see Theorem 1.3 in
[
6
]
), has a Toeplitz structure with certain conditions on the growth of the coefficients. Using state space realization techniques, in Section
2
we show that conversely a semi-infinite Toeplitz matrix satisfying these conditions necessarily leads to a matrix symbol of the type we consider. We also discuss this Toeplitz matrix structure in the context of the Sarason class of unbounded Toeplitz operators, as proposed in the last section of
[
16
]
and further investigated in
[
14
,
15
]
.

In Section
3
we make use of the characterization of Fredholmness and invertibility of
T_{\Omega}
together with state space techniques to compute the essential spectrum and resolvent set of the operator
T_{\Omega}
. Furthermore, we express these subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
in terms of the matrices of the realization (
1.1
) and stabilizing solutions to associated Riccati equations. In the case of continuous rational matrix functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
, so that
T_{\Omega}
is bounded, using a different type of realization Fredholmness and invertibility of
T_{\Omega}
have been studied in
[
5
, Chapter XXIV]
.

Some examples that illustrate these results are given in Section
4
to conclude the paper. In particular, in one of the examples the essential spectrum turns out to be the full complex plane. This is in contrast to what happens for scalar symbols, in which case the essential spectrum is an algebraic curve in the complex plane.

2.
Semi-infinite Toeplitz matrices

Since the space
{\mathcal{P}}^{m}
of vector polynomials of size
m
is contained in the domain of
T_{\Omega}
, for any
m\times m
rational matrix function
\Omega(z)
,
T_{\Omega}
has a block matrix representation
T_{\Omega}=[t_{i,j}]_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
with
t_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times m}
, with respect to the standard block basis of
H^{p}
, and this matrix representation has a Toeplitz structure, that is
t_{i,j}=a_{i-j}
for all
i,j
. The latter follows from the fact that
T_{z^{-1}I}T_{\Omega}T_{zI}f=T_{\Omega}f
for
f\in\operatorname{Dom}(T_{\Omega})
.

Conversely, let
[T]=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i-j}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
be a semi-infinite block Toeplitz matrix, where each
a_{j}
is an
m\times m
matrix. In this section we discuss the question: Under which conditions on the entries
a_{j}
does this semi-infinite block Toeplitz matrix correspond to a block Toeplitz matrix generated by an operator
T_{\Omega}
as defined above? The results in this section also hold for the case where the entries are non-square matrices; these results can be obtained simply by adding zero-rows or zero-columns to make the matrices square. We consider the following conditions:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\textup{(i)}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\|a_{0}\|,\|a_{1}\|,\ldots$ are the Taylor coefficients of a function in $H^{p}$; and}

\displaystyle\textup{(ii)}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\|a_{-j}\|\leq K\binom{j}{M-1}$},\ j\geq 1\mbox{ for some }M\in\mathbb{N},\,K>0.

These conditions are not enough to get rationality of the symbol. To achieve that, we shall make use of the theory of state space realizations from systems theory, as outlined in, e.g.,
[
12
, Chapter 3]
. Form the block Hankel matrices

H^{+}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{k}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&\cdots&a_{k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{k}&a_{k+1}&\cdots&a_{2k-1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad H^{-}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\cdots&a_{-k}\\
a_{-2}&a_{-3}&\cdots&a_{-k-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{-k}&a_{-k-1}&\cdots&a_{-2k+1}\end{bmatrix}.

We shall also impose the condition

(2.3)

\max_{k\geq 1}{\rm rank\,}H^{+}_{k}=:n_{+}<\infty,\qquad\max_{k\geq 1}{\rm rank\,}H^{-}_{k}=:n_{-}<\infty.

The following theorem is the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.1
.

A semi-infinite block Toeplitz matrix
T=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i-j}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
with each
a_{j}
an
m\times m
matrix, is the block Toeplitz representation of an operator
T_{\Omega}
for a rational
m\times m
matrix function
\Omega(z)
, possibly with poles on
{\mathbb{T}}
, if and only if the conditions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) hold.

Proof.

Assume that the conditions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) hold. Then by the realization algorithm (cf., Section 3.4 in
[
12
]
) there is a minimal triple of matrices
(A,B,C)
such that
a_{j}=CA^{j-1}B
for
j\geq 1
, and there is a minimal triple of matrices
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
such that
a_{-j}=\gamma\alpha^{j-1}\beta
for
j\geq 1
. Then for
|z|
small enough we have

\Omega_{+}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}z^{j}=zC(I-zA)^{-1}B,

while for
|z|
large enough we have

\Omega_{-}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{-j}z^{-j}=\gamma(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta.

By condition (i) in (
2.2
) it follows that each entry in the matrix function
\Omega_{+}(z)
is in
H^{p}
, and hence
\Omega_{+}(z)\in H^{p}_{m\times m}
, which implies that
\Omega_{+}(z)
has no poles in the closed unit disc
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Then the minimality of the triple
(A,B,C)
implies that
A
has all its eigenvalues inside
{\mathbb{D}}
, and thus
A
is stable (cf., Corollary 8.14 in
[
1
]
).

According to (ii) in (
2.2
), there exist
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
K>0
such that
\|a_{-j}\|\leq K\binom{j}{M-1}
for all
j\geq 0
. This implies that for each
r>1
the function
\Omega_{-}(z)
is analytic on
|z|>r
, since
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}K\binom{j}{M-1}r^{-j}
is a convergent series (for instance by the ratio test). Hence
\Omega_{-}(z)
is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, so that
\alpha
has al its eigenvalues inside
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
and hence is semi-stable, now using minimality of
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
again in combination with Corollary 8.14 in
[
1
]
.

Thus, the function

\Omega(z)=a_{0}+zC(I-zA)^{-1}B+\gamma(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta

is a rational matrix function which possibly has poles on the unit circle, and for this
\Omega(z)
we claim that
T
coincides with the Toeplitz block matrix representation of
T_{\Omega}
. To see this, we consider the expansion of
\Omega(z)z^{n}
for
n=0,1,\ldots
, as in the proof of Lemma 4.1 of
[
11
]
. By successively using the identity

(zI-\alpha)^{-1}=z^{-1}I+z^{-1}\alpha(zI-\alpha)^{-1},

it follows that for each
w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
we have

\displaystyle\Omega(z)z^{n}w

\displaystyle=z^{n+1}C(I-zA)^{-1}Bw+a_{0}z^{n}w+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}z^{n-k-1}\gamma\alpha^{k}\beta w+\gamma\alpha^{n}(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta w

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{n+1+j}CA^{j}Bw+a_{0}z^{n}w+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}z^{n-k-1}a_{-k-1}w+\gamma\alpha^{n}(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta w

\displaystyle=z^{n}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z^{j+1}a_{j+1}+a_{0}+\sum_{l=-n}^{-1}z^{l}a_{l}\right)w+\gamma\alpha^{n}(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta w

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j-n}wz^{j}+\gamma\alpha^{n}(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta w.

Due to the eigenvalues of
\alpha
being in
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, it follows that
\gamma\alpha^{n}(zI-\alpha)^{-1}\beta w
can be written as a function in
{\mathrm{Rat}}_{0}^{m}({\mathbb{T}})
and an anti-analytic part in
L^{p}_{m}({\mathbb{T}})
, showing that the
T_{\Omega}z^{n}w
is equal to
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j-n}wz^{j}
, which confirms the claim regarding the Toeplitz block matrix representation of
T_{\Omega}
.

For the converse implication, assume
\Omega(z)
is an
m\times m
rational matrix function, which may have poles on the unit circle. An argument similar to the analogous result for the scalar case in
[
6
, Theorem 1.3]
works here as well, but we give an alternative argument using state space realizations. Note that
\Omega(z)
admits a minimal state space realization as in (
1.1
) with
A
stable and
\alpha
semi-stable. In the previous paragraph we showed that
T_{\Omega}
has a block Toeplitz matrix representation
[a_{i-j}]_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
with
a_{0}=R_{0}
,
a_{j}=CA^{j-1}B
, for
j\geq 1
, and
a_{j}=\gamma\alpha^{-j-1}\beta
for
j\leq-1
. Then
H_{k}^{+}
and
H_{k}^{-}
are the
k\times k
block Hankel matrices associated with the rational matrix functions
\Omega_{+}(z)
and
\Omega_{-}(z)
, as defined above, respectively, and hence (
2.3
) holds by the realization algorithm
[
12
, Section 3.4]
. Since
A
is stable, it is clear that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}z^{j}a_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}z^{j}CA^{j-1}B=zC(I-zA)^{-1}B\ \mbox{is in $H^{p}_{m\times m}$}.

Hence item (i) in (
2.2
) holds.

Since
a_{-j}=\gamma\alpha^{j-1}\beta
for
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\|a_{-j}\|\leq\|\gamma\|\|\beta\|\|\alpha^{j-1}\|
.
To get an upper bound on the growth of
\|\alpha^{j-1}\|
let
\alpha=SJS^{-1}
where
J
is in Jordan normal form. Then
\|\alpha^{j-1}\|\leq\|S\|\cdot\|S^{-1}\|\|J^{j-1}\|
. Let
M
be the size of the largest Jordan block in
J
corresponding to an eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of
\alpha
on the unit circle, and let
\lambda_{0}I+N
be that Jordan block. Then
\|J^{j-1}\|\leq\|(\lambda_{0}I+N)^{j-1}\|
. Moreover, using the fact that the norm of an
M\times M
matrix is bounded by
M
times the maximum absolute value of the entries in the matrix, we see that for some constant
K>0
we have

\|J^{j-1}\|\leq K\cdot\begin{pmatrix}j\\
M-1\end{pmatrix},

as the largest absolute value of an entry in
(\lambda_{0}I+N)^{j-1}
occurs in the right upper corner of the matrix. This proves the theorem.
∎

While the theorem only claims existence of an
m\times m
rational matrix function
\Omega(z)
such that the given semi-infinite block Toeplitz
T
corresponds to the block Toeplitz representation of
T_{\Omega}
, the realization algorithm of Section 3.4 in
[
12
]
used in the proof actually gives a construction of the matrices
A,B,C
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
that together with
R_{0}:=a_{0}
form a minimal state space realization of
\Omega(z)
once it is established that (
2.3
) holds.

Remark 2.2
.

We connect the result of Theorem
2.1
with the
Sarason class of unbounded Toeplitz operators
, as proposed by Sarason in the last section of
[
16
]
, and investigated further by Rosenfeld in
[
14
,
15
]
(who referred to such operators as Sarason-Toeplitz operators). The idea stems from the observation that a bounded operator
T
on
H^{2}
is a Toeplitz operator with symbol from
L^{\infty}
if and only if
S^{*}TS=T
, with
S=T_{z}
the forward shift operator on
H^{2}
, i.e., the Toeplitz operator with symbol
z\mapsto z
. To remain in the context of the present paper, we extend the definition of
[
14
,
15
]
, and say that an operator
T(H^{p}_{m}\to H^{p}_{m})
is in the
Sarason class of unbounded Toeplitz operators
(abbreviated to
Sarason class
in the sequel) whenever

(i)

T
is closed and densely defined;

(ii)

\operatorname{Dom}(T)
is
T_{zI_{m}}
-invariant;

(iii)

T_{z^{-1}I_{m}}TT_{zI_{m}}=T
;

(iv)

If
f\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
and
f(0)=0
, then
T_{z^{-1}I_{m}}f\in\operatorname{Dom}(T)
.

Here
T_{zI_{m}}
and
T_{z^{-1}I_{m}}
are the bounded Toeplitz operators with symbols
z\mapsto zI_{m}
and
z\mapsto z^{-1}I_{m}
. For the case
m=1
and
p=2
it was shown in
[
8
]
that the unbounded Toeplitz operators considered in this paper fall within the Sarason class, and it is not difficult to see that this extends to
1<p<\infty
(as already noted in
[
8
]
) and to arbitrary
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
(for items (i)–(iii) see Proposition 2.1 in
[
9
]
).

Let us consider an operator
T
in the Sarason class with the property that the constant functions are in
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
. Since
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
is
T_{zI_{m}}
-invariant by (ii), it follows that the vector polynomials of size
m
are in
\operatorname{Dom}(T)
. Hence
T
admits a block matrix representation with respect to the standard block basis of
H_{m}^{p}
, and it is easy to see that (iii) implies that the block matrix representation has Toeplitz structure, i.e.,
[T]=[a_{i-j}]_{i,j=0}^{\infty}
for matrices
a_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times m}
.

In
[
8
]
we show that the operator in the Sarason class is not uniquely determined by the symbol, since for a scalar-valued rational function
\omega
the adjoint
T_{\omega}^{*}
of the unbounded Toeplitz operator
T_{\omega}

is a restriction of the unbounded Toeplitz operator
T_{\omega^{*}}
, and thus the symbol
\omega^{*}
defines two different operators in the Sarason class, with the same symbol.

In short, for an operator in the Sarason class:

(i)

We get a matrix representation with respect to the standard basis, and this matrix representation has a Toeplitz structure.

(ii)

The Toeplitz matrix structure generates an abstract Fourier representation, and the fact that
{\mathcal{P}}^{m}\subset\operatorname{Dom}(T)
implies that the ’analytic part’ must be in
H^{p}_{m}
. Apart from that, we are not sure if there are any conditions on the matrix entries that can be obtained.

From that perspective, we can state the following
open problem
. Start with a Toeplitz matrix and assume the ’analytic part’ is in
H^{p}_{m}
. Can we then define an unbounded Toeplitz operator from that? It is clear how
T
acts on the vector polynomials of size
m
. Is there maybe a unique way to extend the operator on
{\mathcal{P}}^{m}
to one that satisfies (i)–(iv)? Even without uniqueness this would be a relevant result.

3.
Spectral analysis of
T_{\Omega}

In this section we use state space methods to determine some properties of the spectrum of
T_{\Omega}
. In the case that
\Omega(z)
is a scalar function, the spectrum was described in detail in
[
7
]
. For matrix-valued symbols the situation is much more complicated, as mentioned in the introduction. In our results, the starting point is that
\Omega(z)
is given by a state space realization of the form (
1.1
)
with
A
stable and
\alpha
semi-stable. Throughout this section, for a square matrix
M
we write
\sigma(M)
for the spectrum of
M
, i.e., the set of its eigenvalues, and
\rho(M):={\mathbb{C}}\backslash\sigma(M)
for the resolvent set of
M
.

3.1.
The essential spectrum

Recall that the essential spectrum of
T_{\Omega}
is defined by

\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})=\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon T_{\Omega}-\lambda I_{H^{p}}\mbox{ is not Fredholm}\right\}.

We can write
T_{\Omega}-\lambda I_{H^{p}}=T_{\Omega-\lambda I_{m}}
and it then follows from Theorem 1.3 in
[
9
]
that
\lambda
is in
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
precisely when
\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m}
has a zero on
{\mathbb{T}}
, that is,
(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})^{-1}
has a pole on
{\mathbb{T}}
, provided that
\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})
is not uniformly zero (in
z
for the fixed choice of
\lambda
). In particular, the essential spectrum of
T_{\Omega}
does not depend on
p
. A complication that occurs in the case of matrix symbols with poles on
{\mathbb{T}}
is that the symbol can have a pole and a zero at the same point of
{\mathbb{T}}
, so that the zeroes of
\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m}
on
{\mathbb{T}}
do not necessarily correspond to the zeroes of
\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})
on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega(z)
be a rational
m\times m
matrix function given by a minimal state space realization (
1.1
) with
A
stable and
\alpha
semi-stable.
Define the linear pencil

L(\lambda,z):=\begin{bmatrix}zA-I&0&B\\
0&\alpha-zI&\beta\\
zC&\gamma&R_{0}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix},\quad\lambda,z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Then we have the following two descriptions of
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
:

\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon\det L(\lambda,\nu)=0\mbox{ for some }\nu\in{\mathbb{T}}\},

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon\lambda\in\sigma(\Omega(\nu))\mbox{ for some }\nu\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\sigma(\alpha)\}\cup

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cup\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon\det L(\lambda,\nu)=0\mbox{ for some }\nu\in\mathbb{T}\cap\sigma(\alpha)\}.

First we deal with
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which
\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})
is uniformly zero, i.e.,
\lambda
in the set

(3.4)

E(\Omega)=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\colon\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})=0\mbox{ for all }z\in\mathbb{C}\mbox{ that are not poles of }\Omega(z)\}.

The following example shows that this set can be nonempty.

Example 3.2
.

Consider

\Omega(z)=\begin{bmatrix}2&\tfrac{1}{z-1}\\
0&2\end{bmatrix},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

for which
\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{2}
has both a pole and zero at
z=1
, for each
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
. However, we have that
\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{2})=(2-\lambda)^{2}
is constant in
z
, and in particular,
\det(\Omega(z)-2I)=0
for all
z
. Clearly, this only happens for
\lambda=2
. Hence
E(\Omega)=\{2\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
m\times m
rational matrix function
\Omega(z)
the set
E(\Omega)
consists of at most
m
points and

E(\Omega)\subset\sigma_{p}(T_{\Omega})\cap\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega}).

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda\in E(\Omega)
. Write
\Omega(z)=\frac{1}{q(z)}P(z)
, where
q(z)
is the least common multiple of all denominators of entries of
\Omega(z)
and
P(z)
is an
n\times n
matrix polynomial. Then

\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})=\frac{1}{q(z)^{m}}\det(P(z)-\lambda q(z)I_{m}).

This is zero for all
z
if and only if
\det(P(z)-\lambda q(z)I_{m})=0
for all
z
. The latter expression is an
m
-th degree polynomial in
\lambda
with coefficients which are polynomials in
z
. For
z
not a zero of
q
there are precisely
m
solutions
\lambda
, counting multiplicities. This means that
E(\Omega)
consists of at most
m
points.

According to Forney
[
3
]
, when viewing
\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m}
as a matrix over the field of rational functions in
z
, for
\lambda\in E(\Omega)
there is a basis for
\operatorname{Ker}(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})
consisting of vector polynomials. Now, if
(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})r(z)
is uniformly
0
for a nontrivial vector polynomial
r(z)
, then
r(z)\in\operatorname{Ker}(T_{\Omega}-\lambda I)
, so that
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\Omega})
. However, also
(\Omega(z)-\lambda I_{m})r(z)h(z)
is uniformly zero for any
h\in H^{p}
. Hence
rH^{p}\in\operatorname{Ker}(T_{\Omega}-\lambda I)
, so that
\operatorname{Ker}(T_{\Omega}-\lambda I)
is infinite dimensional and thus
\lambda\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
. Hence
E(\Omega)\subset\sigma_{p}(T_{\Omega})\cap\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Decompose
L(\lambda,z)
as

L(\lambda,z)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}zA-I&0&B\\
0&\alpha-zI&\beta\\
\hline\cr zC&\gamma&R_{0}-\lambda I\end{array}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{A}(z)&\widehat{B}\\
\widehat{C}(z)&\widehat{D}_{\lambda}\end{bmatrix},\quad\lambda,z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Note that
\widehat{A}(z)
is invertible if and only if
z
is not a pole of
\Omega(z)
, due to the minimality of the realization (
1.1
). In that case, we have that

\displaystyle\Omega(z)-\lambda I

\displaystyle=R_{0}-\lambda I+\begin{bmatrix}zC&\gamma\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I-zA&0\\
0&zI-\alpha\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}B\\
\beta\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=R_{0}-\lambda I-\begin{bmatrix}zC&\gamma\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}zA-I&0\\
0&\alpha-zI\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}B\\
\beta\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{D}_{\lambda}-\widehat{C}(z)\widehat{A}(z)^{-1}\widehat{B}.

Hence
\Omega(z)-\lambda I
is the Schur complement of
L(\lambda,z)
with respect to
\widehat{A}(z)
; see, e.g.,
[
1
, Section 2.2]
. It follows that, whenever
\det\widehat{A}(z)\neq 0
,
\Omega(z)-\lambda I
is invertible if and only if
L(\lambda,z)
is invertible.

Now take
\lambda_{0}\in E(\Omega)
, with
E(\Omega)
as in (
3.4
). Then for any
\nu\in{\mathbb{T}}
with
\det\widehat{A}(\nu)\neq 0
, we have that
\det(\Omega(\nu)-\lambda_{0}I)=0
(so that
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(\Omega(\nu))
) and hence
\det L(\lambda_{0},\nu)=0
, while also
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}(T_{\Omega})}
, by Lemma
3.3
. So for
\lambda_{0}\in E(\Omega)
, the statements hold.

From now on we assume
\lambda_{0}\notin E(\Omega)
. Applying the standard Schur complement inversion formula
[
1
, Section 2.2]
to
L(\lambda_{0},z)
with respect to
\widehat{A}(z)
, we have that

(3.5)

(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I)^{-1}=EL(\lambda_{0},z)^{-1}E^{*},\mbox{ with $E=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&I\end{bmatrix}$},\quad\mbox{whenever }\det L(\lambda_{0},z)\neq 0.

Note that the above formula for
(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I)^{-1}
extends to the poles of
\Omega(z)
, as long as
L(\lambda_{0},z)
is invertible. Recall that the zeroes of
\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I
are the poles of
(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I)^{-1}
, and that
T_{\Omega}-\lambda_{0}I
is not Fredholm if and only if
\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I
has a zero on
{\mathbb{T}}
, by Theorem 1.3 in
[
9
]
. Hence, to prove the first formula for
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
it suffices to show that the poles of
(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I)^{-1}
coincide with the zeroes of
\det L(\lambda_{0},z)
, because
L(\lambda_{0},z)
is a matrix polynomial in
z
.
Define

N(\lambda_{0},z)=\begin{bmatrix}L(\lambda_{0},z)&E^{*}\\
E&0\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ with $E$ as in \eqref{InvForm}}.

By (
3.5
),
(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0}I)^{-1}
is the Schur complement of
N(\lambda_{0},z)
with respect to
L(\lambda_{0},z)
. Because the pairs
(A,B)
and
(\alpha,\beta)
are controllable,
A
is stable and
\alpha
is semi-stable, the matrix

\begin{bmatrix}zA-I&0&B\\
0&zI-\alpha&\beta\end{bmatrix}

has full row rank for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. This implies that
\begin{bmatrix}L(\lambda_{0},z)&E^{*}\end{bmatrix}
also has full row rank for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. From this it follows that the Smith form of
\begin{bmatrix}L(\lambda_{0},z)&E^{*}\end{bmatrix}
is
\begin{bmatrix}I\ &0\end{bmatrix}
, so that, by Proposition 2.1 in
[
2
]
the matrix polynomials
L(\lambda_{0},z)
and
E^{*}
are right coprime.
Because the pairs
(C,A)
and
(\gamma,\alpha)
are observable,
A
is stable and
\alpha
is semi-stable, it can be seen in a similar manner that
L(\lambda_{0},z)
and
E
are left coprime. It then follows from a result of Rosenbrock
[
13
]
(see also Theorem 3.5 in
[
2
]
for a nice presentation and extension of this material), that
L(\lambda_{0},z)^{-1}
and
(\Omega(z)-\lambda_{0})^{-1}
have the same poles, which, as was explained above, coincide with the zeroes of
\det L(\lambda_{0},z)
. This completes the proof of the first description of the essential spectrum.

For the second description, we may still assume
\lambda_{0}\notin E(\Omega)
. Let
\nu\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus\sigma(\alpha)
By Schur’s determinant formula, applied to
L(\lambda_{0},\nu)
, we have

\det L(\lambda_{0},\nu)=\det(\Omega(\nu)-\lambda_{0})\det\widehat{A}(\nu)=\det(\Omega(\nu)-\lambda_{0}I)\det(\nu A-I)\det(\nu I-\alpha).

Hence
\det L(\lambda_{0},\nu)=0
if and only if
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue of
\Omega(\nu)
. That proves the second description of the essential spectrum.
∎

3.2.
The resolvent set

Next, we consider the resolvent set.
Using results from
[
11
]
, see also
[
4
]
, we can characterize invertibiliy of
T_{\Omega}-\lambda I=T_{\Omega-\lambda I}
in terms of existence of the stabilizing solution to an algebraic Riccati equation. That gives a result that makes it possible to determine whether or not
\lambda
is in the resolvent set of
T_{\Omega}
. To be precise, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be given by the realization (
1.1
). Then
\lambda\in\rho(T_{\Omega})

if and only if there is a matrix
Q
, depending on
\lambda
, such that
R_{0}-\gamma QB-\lambda I
is invertible,
Q

satisfies the algebraic Riccati equation

(3.6)

Q=\alpha QA+(\beta-\alpha QB)(R_{0}-\gamma QB-\lambda I)^{-1}(C-\gamma QA),

and such that the matrices

A_{\circ}:=A-B(R_{0}-\gamma QB-\lambda I)^{-1}(C-\gamma QA),\ \ \alpha_{\circ}:=\alpha-\left(\beta-\alpha QB\right)(R_{0}-\gamma QB-\lambda I)^{-1}\gamma

are both stable.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3
we have that
E(\Omega)\subset\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
. Hence we can restrict to
\lambda\notin E(\Omega)
, i.e.,
\det(\Omega(z)-\lambda I)
is not uniformly zero. In that case, the main result of
[
11
]
can be applied, and it follows that the operator
T_{\Omega}-\lambda I=T_{\Omega-\lambda I_{m}}
is invertible if and only if there is a solution
Q
to the algebraic Riccati equation (
3.6
) for which
A_{\circ}
and
\alpha_{\circ}
are stable.
∎

We have the following corollary, which concerns a special case.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be given by the realization (
1.1
) and suppose that
\Omega(z)
has all its poles in
{\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}}
, and the limit at infinity of
\Omega(z)
exists. Then
\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\Omega})
if and only if
\lambda\in\sigma(R_{0})
or there is a
\mu\notin\mathbb{D}
,
\mu
not a pole of
\Omega(z)
, for which
\lambda\in\sigma(\Omega(\mu))
or
\det K(\lambda,\mu)=0
for
\mu\in{\mathbb{T}}
a pole of
\Omega(z)
, where

K(\lambda,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha-zI&\beta\\
\gamma&R_{0}-\lambda I\end{bmatrix},\quad\lambda,z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Proof.

Under the assumption that
\Omega(z)
has all its poles inside
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, and the limit at infinity of
\Omega(z)
exists, the matrix
A
is zero on the zero-dimensional space. In that case the Riccati equation is vacuous, and it follows from Proposition
3.4
that
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
is in the resolvent of
T_{\Omega}
if and only if
R_{0}-\lambda I
is invertible (i.e.,
\lambda\in\rho(R_{0})
) and
\alpha_{\circ}:=\alpha-\beta(R_{0}-\lambda I)^{-1}\gamma
is stable.

Note that due to the observations above, the matrix polynomial
K(\lambda,z)
coincides with the matrix polynomial
L(\lambda,z)
in Theorem
3.1
. For
\lambda\in\rho(R_{0})
, the Schur complement of
K(\lambda,z)
with respect to
R_{0}-\lambda I
is equal to
\alpha_{\circ}-zI
. Hence in that case
\alpha_{\circ}-zI
is invertible if and only if
K(\lambda,z)
is invertible. Since
\alpha_{\circ}
is stable precisely when
\alpha_{\circ}-\mu I
is invertible for each
\mu\notin{\mathbb{D}}
, it follows that
\lambda
is in the resolvent of
T_{\Omega}
if and only if
\lambda\in\rho(R_{0})
and
\det K(\lambda,\mu)\neq 0
for all
\mu\notin{\mathbb{D}}
. Stated in the negative,
\lambda
is in
\sigma(T_{\Omega})
if and only if
\lambda\in\sigma(R_{0})
or
\det K(\lambda,\mu)=0
for some
\mu\notin{\mathbb{D}}
.

To complete the proof, note that for
\mu\notin{\mathbb{D}}
which is not a pole of
\Omega(z)
, i.e., such that
\mu\in\rho(\alpha)
, the Schur complement of
K(\lambda,\mu)
with respect to
\alpha-\mu I
is equal to

R_{0}-\lambda I+\gamma(\mu I-\alpha)^{-1}\beta=\Omega(\mu)-\lambda I.

Hence, in that case
\det K(\lambda,\mu)=0
holds if and only if
\det(\Omega(\mu)-\lambda I)=0
, i.e.,
\lambda\in\sigma(\Omega(\mu))
.
∎

4.
Examples

We conclude the paper with a number of examples. The first three examples are of scalar symbols, which allows us to compare with the results from
[
7
]
, where the spectrum of
T_{\omega}
for a scalar symbol
\omega
is described in detail. The fourth example gives a
2\times 2
matrix example, illustrating the methods outlined here for a fairly simple case. The final example illustrates the methods for a case in which
E(\Omega)
is not empty.

Example 4.1
.

We start with an easy example. Let
\omega(z)=\frac{z-(a+ib)}{z-1}=1+\frac{1-(a+ib)}{z-1}
. This symbol is also considered in Example 4.1 in
[
7
]
. For the matrices in the realization we can take
R_{0}=1
,
A=0
on the zero-dimensional space,
B=0
,
C=0
,
\alpha=1
on a one-dimensional space,
\gamma=1
,
\beta=1-a-bi
. We are in the setting of Corollary
3.5
. The algebraic Riccati equation has the solution
q=0
as a map from a zero-dimensional space to a one-dimensional space. The matrix
A_{\circ}
is also zero on the zero-dimensional space, while
\alpha_{\circ}=1-(1-a-bi)(1-\lambda)^{-1}
, for which we require
\lambda\neq 1=R_{0}
. The only further condition on invertibility is then that
|\alpha_{\circ}|<1
. So,
\lambda\in\sigma(T_{\omega})
if and only if
|\alpha_{\circ}|\geq 1
. In turn this is equivalent to
|a+bi-\lambda|\geq|1-\lambda|
. Note that this inequality is always satisfied for
\lambda=1
. For
\lambda=x+iy
we have
|a+bi-\lambda|=|1-\lambda|
if and only if
2by=a^{2}+b^{2}-1+(2-2a)x
. This is precisely the line
L
given in Example 4.1 in
[
7
]
. For this example we retrieve the results as presented there.

Example 4.2
.

Let
\omega(z)=\frac{z^{2}+1}{z-1}=z+1+\frac{2}{z-1}
.
The matrices in the realization can be taken to be
R_{0}=1,A=0,C=B=1,\alpha=1,\beta=2,\gamma=1
. Here
A
and
\alpha
are
1\times 1
matrices. The algebraic Riccati equation for
T_{\omega}-\lambda I
becomes

q=(2-q)(1-q-\lambda)^{-1}.

Further,
A_{\circ}=-(1-q-\lambda)^{-1}
and
\alpha_{\circ}=1-(2-q)(1-q-\lambda)^{-1}
. The algebraic Ricatti equation gives
\lambda=2-q-\frac{2}{q}
. Hence
1-q-\lambda=-1+\frac{2}{q}=\frac{2-q}{q}
. If follows that we should have
q\neq 2
and
q\neq 0
, but it is easily checked that
q=2
and
q=0
do not satisfy the algebraic Ricatti equation, irrespectively of the value of
\lambda
. We further get that
A_{\circ}=\frac{q}{q-2}
and
\alpha_{\circ}=1-q
. Stability of
A_{\circ}
and
\alpha_{\circ}
just means
|A_{\circ}|<1
and
|\alpha_{\circ}|<1
, which can be expressed as
\operatorname{Re}q<1
and
|1-q|<1
, respectively.

So,
\lambda
is in the resolvent set of
T_{\omega}
precisely when
\lambda=2-q-\frac{2}{q}
for a
q
that is in the open left half of the disc of radius
1
with centre at
1
.
The semi-circular part of the boundary of that set maps precisely to part of the essential spectrum, namely the part of the curve
z=x+iy
parametrized by

x=\cos(\theta),y=-\frac{\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)}{1-\cos(\theta)}
for
-\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2
.
(Recall from
[
7
]
that the essential spectrum of
T_{\omega}
is given by the curve
\omega(\mathbb{T})
.)
The spectrum is the set to the left of that, the essential spectrum has an extra ”loop” parametrized by this curve for
\pi/2<\theta<3\pi/2
, see Figure 1.

Example 4.3
.

Let
\omega(z)=\tfrac{z^{3}+3z+1}{z^{2}-1}=z+\tfrac{5}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z-1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z+1}
. (Compare Example 5.2 in
[
7
]
.) The matrices in a realization can be taken to be

R_{0}=0,\ A=0,\ B=C=1\mbox{ (all $1\times 1$), and }\alpha=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\ \beta=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\ \gamma=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{5}{2}&\tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

The algebraic Riccati equation for
T_{\omega-\lambda}
becomes, with
q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
a
2\times 1
matrix:

\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}1-q_{1}\\
1+q_{2}\end{bmatrix}(\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2}+\lambda)^{-1}.

Using this equation we can express
\lambda
in
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=-(\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2})+\tfrac{-1+q_{1}}{q_{1}},

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=-(\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2})+\tfrac{-1-q_{2}}{q_{2}}.

This also gives a relation between
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
:
\tfrac{-1+q_{1}}{q_{1}}=\tfrac{-1-q_{2}}{q_{2}}
, equivalently,

q_{2}=\tfrac{q_{1}}{1-2q_{1}}.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle A_{\circ}

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2}+\lambda)^{-1}=\tfrac{q_{1}}{q_{1}-1}=-\tfrac{q_{2}}{q_{2}+1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\circ}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1-q_{1}\\
1+q_{2}\end{bmatrix}(\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}+\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2}+\lambda)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{5}{2}&\tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{5}{2}&\tfrac{3}{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1-\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}&-\tfrac{3}{2}q_{1}\\
-\tfrac{5}{2}q_{2}&-1-\tfrac{3}{2}q_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Solving for
\lambda
completely in terms of
q_{1}
we have

\lambda=\tfrac{-1+q_{1}}{q_{1}}-\tfrac{5}{2}q_{1}-\tfrac{3}{2}\tfrac{q_{1}}{1-2q_{1}}.

Set
z=\tfrac{-1+q_{1}}{q_{1}}
, or equivalently,
q_{1}=\tfrac{-1}{z-1}
. Then one checks that

\tfrac{q_{1}}{1-2q_{1}}=-\tfrac{1}{z+1}
. It follows that

\lambda=z+\tfrac{5}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z-1}+\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z+1}=\omega(z).

Rewriting
A_{\circ}
in terms of
z
we have
A_{\circ}=\tfrac{q_{1}}{q_{1}-1}=\tfrac{1}{z}
. Stability of
A_{\circ}
is therefore equivalent to
|z|>1
. Also rewriting
\alpha_{\circ}
in terms of
z
we have

\alpha_{\circ}=\alpha_{\circ}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}1+\tfrac{5}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z-1}&\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z-1}\\
\tfrac{5}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z+1}&-1+\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{z+1}\end{bmatrix}.

We see that the resolvent set of
T_{\omega}
is the set of all
\lambda=\omega(z)
for which

|z|>1
and
\alpha_{\circ}(z)
is stable. Moreover, for
|z|\leq 1
the matrix
A_{\circ}
is not stable, so for those values of
z
we have that
\lambda=\omega(z)\in\sigma(T_{\omega})
. Notice that the map from
z
to
\lambda
is not one-to-one, so for a given
\lambda
there may be a value of
z
with
|z|>1
for which
\lambda=\omega(z)
such that
\alpha_{\circ}(z)
is stable, while there may be another value of
z
with
|z|>1
for which
\lambda=\omega(z)
such that
\alpha_{\circ}(z)
is not stable.

Our next remark is that the curve
\omega(\mathbb{T})
gives the essential spectrum (see
[
7
]
), and in each connected component of the complement of this curve the Fredholm index of the operator
T_{\omega}-\lambda I
is independent of
\lambda
. In addition, the resolvent of
T_{\omega}
is a union of connected components of the complement of the curve
\omega(\mathbb{T})
. Hence, we have to check invertibility of
T_{\omega}-\lambda I
for just one point
\lambda
in each component. See Figure 2 for the connected components.

T_{\omega}
, where
\omega(z)=\frac{z^{3}+3z+1}{z^{2}-1}

Taking
z
real we have that
\lambda=\omega(z)
is real, and the whole real line is in one connected component. Taking
z=0
gives
\lambda=-1
. For
z=0
the matrix
A_{\circ}
is not stable, so
\lambda=-1
is in the spectrum of

, and hence the whole connected component containing the real line is in the spectrum of

. Taking

we have that
\lambda=-0.1+1.8i
which is in the connected component not containing the real line and above the real line. For this value of

) and

\alpha_{\circ}(3i)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{3}{4}-\tfrac{3}{4}i&-\tfrac{3}{20}-\tfrac{9}{20}i\\[2.84526pt]

which has spectral radius
0.9572
and hence is stable. Thus, this component belongs to the resolvent of
T_{\omega}
. Taking

, we obtain that

, which is in the connected component not containing the real line and below the real line. We have

, and hence also

is the area in blue in Figure 2, while the resolvent set of

is the area in white in Figure 2.

Example 4.4
.

Let

\Omega(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{z-1}&\tfrac{z}{z+1}\\
\tfrac{z}{z-1}&\tfrac{1}{z+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{z-1}&0\\
0&\tfrac{1}{z+1}\end{bmatrix}.

Since there are no poles in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, we see that a minimal realization of the form (
1.1
) with
A
stable and
\alpha
semi-stable, has
A=0
on a zero-dimensional space,
B=0
(
0\times 2
),
C=0
(
2\times 0
), while the other matrices can be taken as

R_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\alpha=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad\beta=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=I_{2}.

This example fits in the context of Corollary
3.5
. Hence the algebraic Riccati equation (
3.6
) has
Q=0
as a solution, interpreted as a
2\times 0
matrix. The matrix
A_{\circ}
is the zero matrix on a zero-dimensional space, the matrix
\alpha_{\circ}
is given as a function of
\lambda
by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\circ}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\circ}(\lambda)=\alpha-\beta(R_{0}-\lambda I)^{-1}\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}-\lambda&1\\
1&-\lambda\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\lambda-1}&-\tfrac{1}{\lambda+1}\\
\tfrac{1}{\lambda-1}&\tfrac{1}{\lambda+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}&-\tfrac{1}{\lambda+1}\\
\tfrac{1}{\lambda-1}&-\tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}\end{bmatrix},\mbox{ for $\lambda\neq\pm 1$}.

Set
z=\tfrac{1}{\lambda}
, then one readily checks that
\alpha_{\circ}(\lambda)=-\Omega(z)=-\Omega(\tfrac{1}{\lambda})
. It follows that
T_{\Omega}-\lambda I
is invertible if and only if
-\Omega(\tfrac{1}{\lambda})
is a stable matrix. However, since
\det\Omega(z)=-1
for all
z
the matrix
\Omega(z)
cannot be stable for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. It follows that the resolvent set is empty and hence
\sigma(T_{\Omega})=\mathbb{C}
.

Next, we determine the essential spectrum of
T_{\Omega}
. The matrix pencil
L(\lambda,z)
is given by

L(\lambda,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha-zI&\beta\\
\gamma&R_{0}-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1-z&0&1&0\\
0&-1-z&0&1\\
1&-1&-\lambda&1\\
1&1&1&-\lambda\end{bmatrix}.

We obtain

\det L(\lambda,z)=\lambda^{2}(z^{2}-1)-(z^{2}-1)-2\lambda z=(z^{2}-1)(\lambda^{2}-1)-2\lambda z.

Note that for each
\lambda
, the polynomial
\det L(\lambda,z)
is never identically zero. Hence
E(\Omega)=\emptyset
.
According to Theorem
3.1
,
\lambda
is in the essential spectrum if and only if it is an eigenvalue of
\Omega(z)
for some
z\neq\pm 1
on the unit circle or
\det L(\lambda,\pm 1)=0
. Our formula for
\det L(\lambda,z)
shows that the latter occurs only when
\lambda=0
.
Since the determinant of
\Omega(z)
is
-1
and the trace of
\Omega(z)
is
\tfrac{2z}{z^{2}-1}
, the characteristic polynomial of
\Omega(z)
is given by

p_{\Omega(z)}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-\tfrac{2z}{z^{2}-1}\lambda-1=(\lambda-\tfrac{z}{z^{2}-1})^{2}-1-\tfrac{z^{2}}{(z^{2}-1)^{2}}.

Hence the characteristic equation of
\Omega(z)
becomes

(\lambda-\tfrac{z}{z^{2}-1})^{2}=1+\tfrac{z^{2}}{(z^{2}-1)^{2}}=\tfrac{z^{4}-z^{2}+1}{z^{4}-2z^{2}+1}.

Specializing to
z=e^{i\theta}
gives

(\lambda-\tfrac{e^{i\theta}}{e^{i2\theta}-1})^{2}=\tfrac{e^{i4\theta}-e^{i2\theta}+1}{e^{i4\theta}-2e^{i2\theta}+1}=\tfrac{e^{i2\theta}+e^{-i2\theta}-1}{e^{i2\theta}+e^{-i2\theta}-2}=\tfrac{1-2\cos(2\theta)}{2-2\cos(2\theta)}=\tfrac{3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)}{4\sin^{2}(\theta)}.

We obtain that for
\theta
such that
3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)\geq 0
we have

\lambda=\tfrac{e^{i\theta}}{e^{i2\theta}-1}\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)}}{2|\sin(\theta)|}=\tfrac{1}{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)}}{2|\sin(\theta)|}=-\tfrac{i}{2\sin(\theta)}\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)}}{2|\sin(\theta)|}.

Observe that these values of
\lambda
are on the unit circle. Noticing that
3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)\geq 0
precisely when
\tfrac{\pi}{6}\leq\theta\leq\tfrac{5\theta}{6}
or
\tfrac{7\pi}{6}\leq\theta\leq\tfrac{11\theta}{6}
we have that for these values of
\theta
the two values of
\lambda
trace out two curves which lie on the unit circle and connect respectively
e^{i\pi/6}
to
e^{i5\pi/6}
and
e^{i7\pi/6}
to
e^{i11\pi/6}
.

For the case where
3-4\cos^{2}(\theta)<0
the values of
\lambda
are given by

\lambda=\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sin(\theta)}\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{-3+4\cos^{2}(\theta)}}{2|\sin(\theta)|}\right)\cdot i.

The values of
\theta
for which
3-4\cos(\theta)^{2}<0
are given by
-\tfrac{\pi}{6}<\theta<\tfrac{\pi}{6}
or
\tfrac{5\pi}{6}<\theta<\tfrac{7\pi}{6}
.
One easily checks that for these values of
\theta
the two values of
\lambda
trace out the whole imaginary line, excluding
0
since
\lambda=0
would occur at
\theta=0
or
\theta=\pi
in which case
\sin(\theta)=0
. However,
\lambda=0
is in the essential spectrum because that is when
\det L(\lambda,\pm 1)=0
.

It follows that the essential spectrum of
T_{\Omega}
is the union of the imaginary line with two arcs on the unit circle. See Figure 3.

.

Example 4.5
.

We return to Example
3.2
. In that case we have

\Omega(z)=\begin{bmatrix}2&\tfrac{1}{z-1}\\
0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\
0&2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}(z-1)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that the right hand side of the above equation constitutes a minimal realization of
\Omega
. We saw before that
E(\Omega)=\{2\}
. Further,

L(\lambda,z)=\begin{bmatrix}1-z&0&1\\
1&2-\lambda&0\\
0&0&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix},

so that
\det L(\lambda,z)=(2-\lambda)^{2}(1-z)
. It follows that all
\lambda\not=2
are in the essential spectrum, and since the essential spectrum is closed, we have
\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})=\mathbb{C}
. That
2\in\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})=\mathbb{C}
also follows since
E(\Omega)\subset\sigma_{\textup{ess}}(T_{\Omega})
, by Lemma
3.3
.
