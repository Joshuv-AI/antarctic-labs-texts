Embedding Principle: a hierarchical structure of loss landscape of deep neural networks
Yaoyu Zhang, Yuqing Li, Zhongwang Zhang, Tao Luo, Zhi-Qin John Xu

Abstract
We prove a general Embedding Principle of loss landscape of deep neural networks (NNs) that unravels a hierarchical structure of the loss landscape of NNs, i.e., loss landscape of an NN contains all critical points of all the narrower NNs. This result is obtained by constructing a class of critical embeddings which map any critical point of a narrower NN to a critical point of the target NN with the same output function. By discovering a wide class of general compatible critical embeddings, we provide a gross estimate of the dimension of critical submanifolds embedded from critical points of narrower NNs. We further prove an irreversiblility property of any critical embedding that the number of negative/zero/positive eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix of a critical point may increase but never decrease as an NN becomes wider through the embedding. Using a special realization of general compatible critical embedding, we prove a stringent necessary condition for being a “truly-bad” critical point that never becomes a strict-saddle point through any critical embedding. This result implies the commonplace of strict-saddle points in wide NNs, which may be an important reason underlying the easy optimization of wide NNs widely observed in practice.

1
Introduction

The loss landscape of a deep neural network (NN) is important to both its optimization dynamics and generalization performance, hence is a key issue in deep learning theory.
It has been realized for a long time that it is important to quantify exactly how the loss landscape looks like
(
E et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2020
)
.
This problem is difficult since various visualization methods show that the NN loss landscape is very complicated
(
Li et al. 2018
;
Skorokhodov and Burtsev 2019
)
. Moreover, its non-convexity, high dimensionality and the dependence on data, model and the specific form of loss function make it very difficult to obtain a general understanding through empirical study.
Therefore, though it has been extensively studied over the years, it remains an open problem to provide a clear picture about the general structure of a DNN loss landscape, e.g., critical points/submanifolds, their output functions and other properties.

Our work is inspired by the following empirical observations. From the aspect of optimization, it is often observed that wider NNs are easier for training. This phenomenon not only holds in a neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
, where the gradient descent training can find the global minimum with a linear convergence rate
(
Du et al. 2019
;
Chizat and Bach 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2018
)
, but also happens in highly nonlinear regimes far beyond NTK
(
Luo et al. 2021b
)
. From the aspect of generalization, the puzzle that over-parameterized NNs often generalize well seems to contradict the conventional learning theory
(
Breiman 1995
;
Zhang et al.
)
. The frequency principle
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Rahaman et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
;
Luo et al. 2021a
)
shows that NNs, over-parameterized or not, tend to fit the training data by a low-frequency function, which suggests that the learned function by an NN is often of much lower complexity than the NN’s capacity. Specifically, with small initialization, e.g., in a condensed regime, weights of an NN are empirically found to condense on isolated directions resulting in an output function mimicking that of a narrower NN
(
Luo et al. 2021b
;
Maennel et al. 2018
)
. These observations raise a question that in which sense learning of a wide NN is not drastically different from a narrower NN despite potentially huge difference in their numbers of parameters. From the aspect of pruning, empirical works propose a “lottery ticket hypothesis” that a substantially smaller sub-network can achieve the same accuracy as the original large network
(
Frankle and Carbin 2018
)
. However, it is not yet clear about the mechanism of redundancy in a learned wide NN, which makes a drastic pruning possible in practice.

All above empirical observations, though relevant to different aspects of NN, are in essence pointing towards an intrinsic similarity between narrow and wide NNs.
In this work, focusing on the loss landscape, we address the following problem: What are the relations of critical points and the corresponding output functions of loss landscape among NNs with different widths. The significance of studying the critical points and their output functions of the NN loss landscape is as follows. From the optimization perspective, NN parameters trained by gradient descent provably converge to a critical point, which in general is not necessarily a global minimum or local minimum. Moreover, even for these saddle points which can be escaped, e.g., strict-saddle points
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
, they may still attract the training trajectory, contributing to the implicit regularization of NNs. We are specifically interested in output functions corresponding to critical points, named as
critical functions
for convenience. Studying these critical functions that potentially attract the learning of NN is clearly important for a deeper understanding of the learning process of an NN.

Our key finding in this work is the following general principle about critical points/functions of NN loss landscape intuitively stated as follows:

Embedding principle
: the loss landscape of an NN
contains
all critical points/functions of all the narrower NNs.

The Embedding Principle shows that any NN loss landscape contains a hierarchical structure of critical points/functions with different complexities from NNs of different widths. Specifically, it ensures existence of “simple” critical functions that can be represented by narrow NNs. Therefore, combining with the phenomenon of Frequency Principle and condensation, we conjecture that nonlinear training of NNs may be implicitly biased towards these “simple” critical functions. We will carefully look into this conjecture in our future works.

To prove the Embedding Principle, we first construct one-step critical embeddings which map any parameters of a narrow network to that of an one-neuron wider NN preserving the output function and criticality. With these embeddings, critical points of a narrow network loss landscape is mapped to
1
-d critical affine subspaces of an one-neuron wider network loss landscape with the same output function. These one-step critical embeddings are constructed by adding a null neuron or splitting an existing neuron.
By composition of one-step embeddings, any critical point of a narrow network loss landscape can be mapped to a critical point of any wider network loss landscape preserving the output function. Importantly, we further propose a wide class of general compatible critical embeddings, where one-step embeddings or their composition are its special cases. Note that, all critical points of a wide NN embedded from a critical point of a narrower NN by all possible general compatible critical embeddings, form high-dimensional critical submanifolds which in general are not affine subspaces for three-layer or deeper NNs.

The critical embeddings naturally link critical points of NNs of different widths, thus providing a means to track how properties of these critical points may change as the width of the NN increases. Using these critical embeddings as a tool, we obtain rich information about the general structure of an NN loss landscape.

We show that the degeneracy of a critical point substantially increases when it is embedded to a wider network, due to the fact that a critical point can be mapped to a high-dimensional critical submanifold through a class of critical embeddings. This degeneracy of critical points arises from the neuron redundancy of the wide NN in representing certain simple critical functions from narrower NNs, which is different from over-parameterization induced degeneracy studied in
Cooper 2021
. We also study the property of Hessian of critical points through critical embedding, e.g., the number of its negative eigenvalues, which determines whether the corresponding critical point is a strict-saddle that enables easy optimization
(
Lee et al. 2019
)
. We prove an irreversiblility of critical embedding that the number of negative eigenvalues of Hessian matrix may increase but never decrease as an NN becomes wider through critical embedding. Moreover, we introduce a notion of “truly-bad” critical point which never becomes a strict-saddle point through any critical embedding. We prove a stringent necessary condition for being a “truly-bad” critical point that requires an important ingredient of its Hessian matrix being a zero matrix. This result implies the commonplace of strict-saddle points in the high-dimensional critical submanifolds of wide NNs, which may be an important reason underlying the easy optimization of wide NNs widely observed in practice.

In summary, the following general understanding of an NN loss landscape is obtained by the embedding principle in this work:

(i) It contains a hierarchical structure of critical points/functions with different complexities from that of all narrower NNs;

(ii) Critical functions from narrower NNs in general forms a high-dimensional critical submanifold with a gross estimate of the dimension:
K+\sum_{k\in[L]}K_{l}K_{l-1}
, where
K_{l}
is the difference in neuron number in layer
l
between the target NN and the narrower NN.

(iii) If it has critical points other than the global minima that are not strict-saddle points, they mostly can become strict-saddle points in wider NNs through critical embedding, which means the optimization difficulty results from these critical points can be alleviated by simply using a wider NN.

2
Related works

This work is an comprehensive extension of our previous conference paper
Zhang et al. 2021b
, in which we study one-step embeddings and their multi-step composition. Similar results on composition embedding are studied in other works
(
Fukumizu and Amari 2000
;
Fukumizu et al. 2019
;
Simsek et al. 2021
)
.

Simple gradient-descent-based optimization on the complex loss landscape of NN
(
Skorokhodov and Burtsev 2019
;
E et al. 2020
)
often finds solutions that generalize well. Many works study the geometry of the NN loss landscape at critical points in relation to its generalization ability. For example, empirical works show that SGD
(
Keskar et al. 2017
)
and dropout
(
Zhang et al. 2021c
)
training can find a flat minimizer, which may explain why such stochastic training can find solution that generalize better.

Wu et al. 2017
further suggest that the volume of basin of attraction of good (flat) minima may dominate over that of poor (sharp) minima in practical problems.
He et al. 2019
show that at a local minimum there exist many asymmetric directions such that the loss increases abruptly along one side, and slowly along the opposite side.
Ding et al. 2019
prove that for any multi-layer network with generic input data and non-linear activation functions, sub-optimal local minima can exist, no matter how wide the network is. When the network width increases towards infinity, the loss landscape may become simpler and the training can avoid spurious valleys with high probability in an over-parameterized regime
(
Venturi et al. 2019
)
. In an extremely over-parameterized regime with a large initialization, i.e., the linear regime identified in
Luo et al. 2021b
with the NTK regime as its special case
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
, the gradient descent training can find the global minimum with a linear convergence rate
(
Luo et al. 2021b
;
Du et al. 2019
;
Chizat and Bach 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2018
)
.

The starting point of this work originates from our work in
Luo et al. 2021b
, where we identify a highly nonlinear condensed regime far beyond the NTK regime that weights condense in isolated directions during the training. Moreover, neural networks of different width often exhibit similar condensed behavior, e.g., stagnating at similar loss with almost the same output function, which is illustrated in experiments in our conference paper
Zhang et al. 2021b
. The condensation is a highly nonlinear feature learning process important to implicit regularization and generalization of NNs. The condensation transforms a large network to a network of only a few effective neurons, leading to an output function with low complexity. Such learning process is consistent with another line of research, that is, the complexity of NN output gradually increases during the training
(
Arpit et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Rahaman et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
;
Luo et al. 2021a
;
Kalimeris et al. 2019
;
Goldt et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
;
Mingard et al. 2019
;
Jin et al. 2020
)
. For example, the Frequency Principle
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2020
)
states that NNs often fit target functions from low to high frequencies during the training. A series of works study the mechanism of condensation at an initial training stage, such as for ReLU network
(
Maennel et al. 2018
;
Pellegrini and Biroli 2020
)
and network with continuously differentiable activation functions
(
Xu et al. 2021
)
.

This work in some sense serves as our attempt to uncover the theoretical structure underlying the condensation phenomenon from the perspective of loss function by proving a general Embedding Principle. In another aspect, the condensation phenomenon also confirms the value of Embedding Principle in understanding the highly nonlinear training behavior in practice.

The Embedding Principle provides a structural mechanism underlying the degeneracy as a very common property for critical points
(
Choromanska et al. 2015
;
Sagun et al. 2016
)
. Thus it complements the understanding that global minima of NNs typically form a high dimensional manifold due to over-parameterization
(
Cooper 2021
)
.

(1)

where the

\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

\bm{0}=\Big(\bm{0}_{m_{1}\times m_{0}},\bm{0}_{m_{1}\times 1},\ldots,\bm{0}_{m_{L}\times m_{L-1}},\bm{0}_{m_{L}\times 1}\Big)\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}},

2L-2

\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}:=(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L-2]},\bm{b}^{[L-2]},\bm{W}^{[L-1]},\bm{b}^{[L-1]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L-2},m_{L-1}\}}.

(2)

\bm{f}^{[0]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\bm{x}

\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}

\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\sigma(\bm{W}^{[l]}\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})+\bm{b}^{[l]}),

where
\sigma(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the activation function applied coordinate-wisely, with slight abuse of notation.
Finally, we denote

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}):=\bm{f}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}):=\bm{W}^{[L]}\bm{f}^{[L-1]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})+\bm{b}^{[L]},

(3)

and for simplicity, sometimes we may drop the subscript
\bm{\theta}

3.2
Loss function

The set of training data is denoted by
S:=\left\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
. Here we assume that there exists an unknown function
\bm{f}^{*}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
satisfying
\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})=\bm{y}_{i}
for
i\in[n]
.
The empirical risk reads as

R_{S}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}),\bm{y}_{i}):=\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x})).

(4)

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{S}
is defined for any function
h(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathbb{E}_{S}h(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\bm{x}_{i}),

and the loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
4
) is differentiable in both variables, and the derivative of
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
with respect to its first argument is denoted by
\nabla\ell(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{*})
. In this paper, we always take derivatives/gradients of
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
in its first argument with respect to any possible parameter.

For each
l\in[L]
, we define the error vectors as

\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]}:=\nabla_{\bm{f}^{[l]}}\ell,

and the feature gradients as

\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L]}:=\mathbf{1},~\text{and}~~\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\sigma^{(1)}\left(\bm{W}^{[l]}\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{b}^{[l]}\right)~\text{for}~l\in[L-1],

where we use
\sigma^{(1)}(\cdot)
for the first derivative of
\sigma(\cdot)
. Moreover, we call
\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}
the feature vectors, and we denote the collections of feature vectors, feature gradients, and error vectors
\{\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{l=1}^{L}
respectively by

\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\{\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{l=1}^{L},~\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\{\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{l=1}^{L},~\bm{Z}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\{\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{l=1}^{L}.

Moreover, using backpropagation, we can derive the following relations concerning the above quantities

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L]}

\displaystyle=\nabla\ell,

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]}

\displaystyle=(\bm{W}^{[l+1]})^{\intercal}\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l+1]}\circ\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l+1]}\right),\quad l\in[L-1],

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l]}}\ell

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]}\circ\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]}\right)(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l-1]})^{\intercal},\quad l\in[L],

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}}\ell

\displaystyle=\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]}\circ\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l]},\quad l\in[L],

where we use
\circ
for the Hadamard product

Specifically, for simplicity of gradient computation, we define another group of error vectors for

and we denote

\bm{E}_{\bm{\theta}}:=\{\bm{e}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}\}_{l=1}^{L}

Directly from relation (

3.3
Hessian

Given a scalar loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
, twice differentiable in both variables, and an activation function
\sigma(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, also twice differentiable, we denote that

\displaystyle R_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(f(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),f^{*}(\bm{x})).

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\nabla\ell(\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x}))^{\intercal}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i},

where

is the

, and

is the
i
-th element of vector
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
.
Then for the Hessian matrix
\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left((\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{ij}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{j}\right)^{\intercal}+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left((\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\right),

where
\partial_{ij}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})
is the

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(1)}_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{ij}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{j}\right)^{\intercal},

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(2)}_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left((\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\right),

(10)

and

\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{H}^{(1)}_{S}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{H}^{(2)}_{S}(\bm{\theta}).

3.4
Assumptions and conventions of notations

We begin this part by introducing several assumptions that will be used throughout this paper:

Assumption
.

(i) We choose the
L
-layer (
L\geq 2
) fully-connected deep neural networks (NNs) as our model.

(ii) Our training data is
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
.

(iii) We use the empirical loss
R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y})
.

(iv) Loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
and activation function
\sigma(\cdot)
are (weakly) differentiable. (Remark: twice differentiable is required for the computation of Hessian)

After stating out the assumptions, we would also like to introduce some conventions of notations that are frequently used in this paper in the following.

We write
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
for a fully-connected
L
-layer network with width
(m_{0},\ldots,m_{L})
, by which the tuple class of its parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
is determined whereas its activation
\sigma(\cdot)
is not provided. When
\sigma(\cdot)
is given, the output of
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
is denoted by
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})
with
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
.

Note that, if given two NNs,
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, their corresponding parameters belong to different tuple classes except when
m^{\prime}_{l}=m_{l}
for all
l\in[0:L]
. Therefore, in this work,
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})
and
R_{S}({\bm{\theta}})
may correspond to output and
loss landscape of different NNs distinguished by
\bm{\theta}
of different tuple classes.

Given two NNs,
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
with
m^{\prime}_{0}=m_{0}
,
m^{\prime}_{L}=m_{L}
, and
m^{\prime}_{l}\geq m_{l}
for any
l\in[L-1]
, then for
K=\sum_{l=1}^{L-1}(m^{\prime}_{l}-m_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we say that
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
is
K
-neuron
wider
than
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, and conversely,
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
is
K
-neuron
narrower
than
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
.

As long as we have two NNs,
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, given in the context of Definitions, Theorems, Propositions, Lemmas etc, we always assume that
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
is wider than
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, i.e.,
m^{\prime}_{0}=m_{0}
,
m^{\prime}_{L}=m_{L}
, and
m^{\prime}_{l}\geq m_{l}
for any
l\in[L-1]
. We also denote
M=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(m_{l}+1)m_{l+1}
and
M^{\prime}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(m^{\prime}_{l}+1)m^{\prime}_{l+1}
, and consequently,
M^{\prime}\geq M
. We denote the parameters of a narrower network by
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}
, and the counterpart of a wider network by
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}
. Then, given the data
S
, loss
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
and activation
\sigma(\cdot)
, the collection of critical points of narrower NN and wider NN can be found respectively and denoted by
\bm{\Theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}}:=\{\bm{\theta}|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}\}
and
\bm{\Theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}:=\{\bm{\theta}|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\bm{0}\}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}}:=\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}|\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}}\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}:=\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}|\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}\}
are denoted for the function spaces induced by critical points accordingly.

Finally, a neuron, say the
i
-th neuron in layer
l
, is termed a
null neuron
if its output is a constant independent of input
\bm{x}
for any activation, i.e.,
(\bm{f}^{[l]})_{i}(\cdot)\equiv\mathrm{Const}
for any
\sigma(\cdot)
. Otherwise, we call this neuron an
effective neuron
.

4
Embedding Principle

In this section, we prove the Embedding Principle by constructing critical embeddings. First, we define a critical embedding operation. Then, by constructing one-step critical embeddings and their composition, we prove the Embedding Principle that critical points of the loss landscape of a narrow network can be embedded to critical affine subspaces of the loss landscape of any wider network while preserving the output function. Finally, we emphasize the importance of Embedding Principle in understanding the implicit regularization and generalization of NNs.

We begin with the concepts of embedding, affine embedding and critical embedding.

Definition 1

(
Embedding and affine embedding).

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, an

embedding
is an injective operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\neq\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
for
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{2}
. In addition,
\mathcal{T}
is an
affine embedding
if
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{T}(\bm{0})
is a linear operator, i.e.,
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{\theta}_{2})=\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}+\bm{\theta}_{2})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\beta\bm{\theta})=\beta\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{\theta})
for any
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 1
.

For any given affine embedding
\mathcal{T}
, it is associated with a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{\prime}\times M}
and a vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{\prime}}
such that
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}))=\bm{A}\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{c}
, where
M=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(m_{l}+1)m_{l+1}
and
M^{\prime}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(m^{\prime}_{l}+1)m^{\prime}_{l+1}
. As noted before, we do not distinguish tuple
\bm{\theta}
from its vectorization
\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\theta})
in the following. Hence,
\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{A}\bm{\theta}+\bm{c}
.

Definition 2

(
Critical embedding).

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, a
critical embedding
is an affine embedding
\mathcal{T}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}
, which maps any set of its network parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
to that of a wider NN
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}=\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}
satisfying that: For any given data
S
, loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
, activation function
\sigma(\cdot)
,

(i)
output preserving:

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}(\bm{x})=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{x})
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(ii)
representation preserving:

\mathrm{span}\left\{\left\{\left(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}(\cdot)\right)_{j}\right\}_{j\in[m_{l}]}\cup\{1\}\right\}=\mathrm{span}\left\{\left\{\left(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\cdot)\right)_{j^{\prime}}\right\}_{j^{\prime}\in[m^{\prime}_{l}]}\cup\{1\}\right\},~~\text{for any}~l\in[L],

where
\{\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and
\{\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}\}_{l=1}^{L}
are feature vectors of
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, and
1:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the constant function, i.e.,
1(\cdot)\equiv 1
;

(iii)
criticality preserving:
If
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}
is a critical point of
R_{S}({\bm{\theta}})
, i.e.,
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}
, then
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}
is also a critical point of
R_{S}({\bm{\theta}})
, i.e.,
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\bm{0}
.

Specifically, if an embedding is a critical embedding with
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
one-neuron wider than
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
,
we call it
one-step critical embedding
.

4.1
One-step critical embedding

In this subsection, we introduce two types of one-step critical embeddings. we start with the definition of one-step null embedding. Intuitively, a one-step null embedding adds a null neuron to the NN, whose output is a constant independent of input
\bm{x}
for any activation.

Definition 3

(
One-step null embedding
)
.

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameter

\bm{\theta}=(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L]},\bm{b}^{[L]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
,
then for any
l\in[L-1]
, we define the operators
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{l,0}
applying on
\bm{\theta}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}|_{k},\quad k\neq l,~l+1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{W}^{[l]}\\
\bm{0}_{1\times m_{l-1}}\\
\end{array}}\right],\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{b}^{[l]}\\
0\\
\end{array}}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\bm{W}^{[l+1]},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right],\bm{b}^{[l+1]}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{0}_{m_{k}\times m_{k-1}},\bm{0}_{m_{k}\times 1}\right),\quad k\neq l,~l+1

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{0}_{(m_{l}+1)\times m_{l-1}},\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{0}_{m_{l}\times 1}\\
1\\
\end{array}}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,0}(\bm{\theta})|_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times{(m_{l}+1)}},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right).

We define
one-step null embedding

\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
as: For any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
,

\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=(\mathcal{T}_{l,0}+\alpha\mathcal{V}_{l,0})(\bm{\theta}).

Note that the neuron added by the above one-step null embedding has zero output weights, zero input weights and an arbitrary bias. An illustration is shown in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Illustration of one-step null embedding. The purple neuron is added with both input and output weights as zero and an arbitrary bias.

We proceed to the definition of one-step splitting embedding.

Definition 4

(
One-step splitting embedding
)
.

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameter

\bm{\theta}=(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L]},\bm{b}^{[L]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
, then for any
l\in[L-1]
and
s\in[m_{l}]
, we define the operators
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}
and
\mathcal{V}_{l,s}
applying on
\bm{\theta}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{k}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}|_{k},\quad k\neq l,l+1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{W}^{[l]}\\
\bm{W}^{[l]}_{s,[1:m_{l-1}]}\\
\end{array}}\right],\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{b}^{[l]}\\
\bm{b}^{[l]}_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\bm{W}^{[l+1]},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right],\bm{b}^{[l+1]}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{0}_{m_{k}\times m_{k-1}},\bm{0}_{m_{k}\times 1}\right),k\neq l,l+1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{l}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{0}_{(m_{l}+1)\times m_{l-1}},\bm{0}_{(m_{l}+1)\times 1}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l,s}(\bm{\theta})|_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(s-1)},-\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(m_{l}-s)},\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s}\right],\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right).

We define
one-step splitting embedding

\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
as: For any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
,

\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=(\mathcal{T}_{l,s}+\alpha\mathcal{V}_{l,s})(\bm{\theta}).

An illustration of one-step splitting method is shown in Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Illustration of one-step splitting embedding. The black neuron in the left network is split into the blue and purple neurons in the right network. The red (green) output weight of the black neuron in the left net is split into two red (green) weights in the right net with ratio
(1-\alpha)
and
\alpha
, respectively. This is also illustrated in
Zhang et al. 2021b
.

Remark 2
.

The parameters
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
correspond to a
L
-layer NN with width
(m_{0},\ldots,m_{l-1},m_{l}+1,m_{l+1},\ldots,m_{L})
since
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
are one-step embeddings.

Remark 3
.

We observe that
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
can be applied on the neural network parameter
\bm{\theta}
of any given
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
of proper depth and width, hence the domain of
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
is not limited to a specific
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
. Instead, the extended domain of
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
assembles all possible tuple class, i.e., if we denote the extended domain of
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
by
\mathcal{D}_{l,0}
, then

\mathcal{D}_{l,0}:=\bigsqcup_{l<L}\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{n_{0},\cdots,n_{L}\}},

(11)

and if we denote the extended domain of
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
by
\mathcal{D}_{l,s}
, then

\mathcal{D}_{l,s}:=\bigsqcup_{l<L,s\leq n_{l}}\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{n_{0},\cdots,n_{L}\}},

(12)

where
\bigsqcup
refers to the disjoint union of different tuple classes.

By the above remark, we may extend
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
to their extended domains, and we identify

\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}:\mathcal{D}_{l,0}\to\mathcal{D}_{l,0},~~\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}:\mathcal{D}_{l,s}\to\mathcal{D}_{l,s},

with their restrictions on
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
for some given
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
.

Theorem 1
.

One-step null embedding and one-step splitting embedding are critical embeddings.

In order to prove Theorem
1
, we need several lemmas.

Lemma 5
.

For any one-step null embedding
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
, given any
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
with
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\in\mathcal{D}_{l,0}
, we have
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}:=\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})
satisfies the following conditions: given any data
S
, loss
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
and activation
\sigma(\cdot)
, for any
l\in[L-1]
,

(i) feature vectors in
\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:
\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
, for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
and
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})^{\intercal},\sigma(\alpha)\right]^{\intercal}
;

(ii) feature gradients in
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:
\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
, for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
and
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[(\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})^{\intercal},\sigma^{(1)}(\alpha)\right]^{\intercal}
;

(iii) error vectors in
\bm{Z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:

\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
, for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
and
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]},0\right]^{\intercal}
;

(iv)
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
is injective for all
\alpha
;

(v)
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
is an affine embedding for all
\alpha
.

Lemma 6
.

For any one-step splitting embedding
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
, given any
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
with
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\in\mathcal{D}_{l,s}
, we have
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}:=\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})
satisfies the following conditions: given any data
S
, loss
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
and activation
\sigma(\cdot)
, for any
l\in[L-1]
,

(i) feature vectors in
\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:
\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
, for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
and
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})^{\intercal},(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\right]^{\intercal}
;

(ii) feature gradients in
\bm{G}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:
\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
, for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
and
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[(\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})^{\intercal},(\bm{g}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\right]^{\intercal}

(iii) error vectors in
\bm{Z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
:

\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
,

\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\left[\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]}\right)^{\intercal}_{[1:s-1]},(1-\alpha)(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s},\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]}\right)^{\intercal}_{[s+1:m_{l}]},\alpha(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\right]^{\intercal}

(iv)
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}

\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}

Directly from Lemma

.

For any one-step null embedding
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
, given any
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
with
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\in\mathcal{D}_{l,0}

and activation
\sigma(\cdot)
,

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}
, then
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\bm{0}
.

.

For any one-step splitting embedding
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
, given any
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}

, we have
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}:=\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})

S
, loss

and activation

4.2
Composition of one-step embeddings and the Embedding Principle

We first define the composition of two embeddings, which would naturally leads to the composition of arbitrary embeddings.

Definition 7

(Composition of two embeddings)
.

Suppose we have an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
, and we have two embeddings
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
satisfying

\mathcal{T}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime\prime}_{L}\}}
, with
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
maps the range of
\mathcal{T}
into
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime\prime}_{L}\}}
, where
m^{\prime\prime}_{0}=m^{\prime}_{0}=m_{0}
,
m^{\prime\prime}_{L}=m^{\prime}_{L}=m_{L}
, and for any
l\in[L-1]
,
m^{\prime\prime}_{l}\geq m^{\prime}_{l}\geq m_{l}
. Since
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}
is obviously an injective operator, then
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}
is an embedding
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime\prime}_{L}\}}
, and we term
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}
the composition of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{T}
,i.e., for any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
,

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta})).

For simplicity, for any
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},K\geq 2
, we denote hereafter
\prod_{l=1}^{K}\mathcal{T}_{l}:=\mathcal{T}_{K}\cdots\mathcal{T}_{1}
as the composition of
K
individual embeddings

Definition 8

(
K

)

Suppose we have two vectors

A
K

The composition of one-step embeddings renders a feasible method to embedding any critical point of the loss landscape of a narrow NN to a critical point with the same output function of any wider NN, therefore, we have the following Embedding Principle.

4.3
Importance of Embedding Principle

Mathematically speaking, Embedding Principle is a natural result of an embedding operation that preserves output function and criticality. However, we must emphasize the importance of stating and proving it explicitly in this work. For a long time, researchers study the loss landscape of NN from an optimization perspective focusing specifically on its property in the parameter space. Lots of works make effort in tackling problems like whether bad local minima exist, whether local minima are also global minima and whether all saddle points are strict-saddle points, etc. However, because the loss landscape of NN also has profound impact on its implicit regularization and generalization performance, it is important to look into the loss landscape from the perspective of function spaces.

Motivated by the phenomena of Frequency Principle
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Rahaman et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
;
Luo et al. 2021a
)
and condensation
(
Luo et al. 2021b
)
, we are very interested in the question of what are the critical functions of an NN loss landscape that may attract the training trajectory in the function space. Specifically, we care about whether there are “simple” critical functions in wide NNs that may implicitly regularize the training to help avoid overfitting. For example, in our experiments shown in Fig.
3
, we clearly observe that the training of a width-
500
two-layer tanh-NN in fitting
50
data points experiences two stages. At the first stage, it learns an output function close to the best fitting of the width-
1
tanh-NN and stays for a while, seemingly that it encounters a saddle point. At the second stage, it converges to an output function close to the best fitting of the width-
3
tanh-NN, which interpolates all the data points. Clearly, the complexity of the output function of the width-
500
NN gradually increases during the training, leading to a non-overfitting interpolation of data despite of possessing overfitting capability. However, before studying the universality of such training behavior clearly relevant to generalization, it is important to have a theoretical answer to whether such “simple” critical functions always exist even in very wide NNs.

General compatible critical embedding

In the following, we propose a much wider class of general compatible embeddings with above class of one-step and multi-step composition embeddings as its special cases.

, one critical point in general can be embedded to a

-dimensional critical affine subspace, which provides a lower bound to the degeneracy of embedded critical points. Precisely, in
Zhang et al. 2021b
, we prove the following theorem using composition of one-step splitting embeddings.

Theorem 20

(Theorem 2 in
Zhang et al. 2021b
)
.

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and a
K
-neuron wider
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, then for any critical point
\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{c}}_{\textnormal{narr}}=\left(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L]},\bm{b}^{[L]}\right)
satisfying
\bm{W}^{[l]}\neq\bm{0}
for each
l\in[L]
,
\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{c}}_{\textnormal{narr}}
can be critically embedded to a
K
-dimensional critical affine subspace
\mathcal{M}_{\textnormal{wide}}=\{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}\bm{\mu}_{i}|\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}
of loss landscape of
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
. Here
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}:=(\prod_{l=1}^{K}\mathcal{T}_{l_{l},s_{l}})(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})
and
\bm{\mu}_{i}:=\mathcal{T}_{l_{K},s_{K}}\cdots\mathcal{V}_{l_{i},s_{i}}\cdots\mathcal{T}_{l_{1},s_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})

for all
l\in[K]
. (The definitions of
\mathcal{V}_{l_{l},s_{l}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{l_{l},s_{l}}
can be found in Definition
4
).

In addition, in this work, as we propose above a wider class of general compatible critical embeddings, we attempt to obtain a better estimate of the dimension of critical submanifolds corresponding to any given critical function
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{c}}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
of a narrow NN. A gross estimate yields the following heuristic argument.

Heuristic argument
.

Given any total index mapping
\mathcal{I}
, the degree of freedom of all possible
\bm{\alpha}
for general compatible critical embedding
\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
to a
K
-neuron wider NN is
K+\sum_{l\in[L]}K_{l}K_{l-1}
, where
K_{l}:=m^{\prime}_{l}-m_{l}
, and
K:=\sum_{l\in[L-1]}K_{l}
.

Justification
.

By the definition of general compatible critical embedding,
\{\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and auxiliary variables
\bm{\beta}=\left\{\bm{\beta}^{[l]}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}|~l\in[L],j\in[m^{\prime}_{l}]\backslash\mathcal{I}_{l}^{-1}(0)\right\}
with
\bm{\beta}^{[L]}_{k}=\bm{1}
satisfy forward and backward compatibility conditions.

Step 1: We first observe that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(s)}\bm{\beta}_{i}^{[l]}=1
for any
s\in[m_{l}]
. Thus,
\bm{\beta}
has
K-m_{\mathrm{null}}
degrees of freedom, where
m_{\mathrm{null}}
is the number of null neurons.

Step 2: Given any such
\bm{\beta}
, we now consider the degrees of freedom for
\{\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.
Note that forward and backward conditions are not independent of one another because

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(s)}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(k)}\bm{\beta}^{[l]}_{i}\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{ij}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(k)}\bm{\beta}^{[l]}_{i}=1=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(s)}\bm{\beta}^{[l-1]}_{j}

automatically holds for the given
\bm{\beta}
. Therefore, there are
m^{\prime}_{l}m_{l-1}+m_{l}m^{\prime}_{l-1}-m_{l}m_{l-1}
independent linear equations for
m^{\prime}_{l}m^{\prime}_{l-1}
parameters in
\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}
for each layer
l
, resulting in
\sum_{l\in[L]}K_{l}K_{l-1}
degrees of freedom in total.

Step 3: Given any effective biases
\bm{B}_{*}
as auxiliary variables for all null neurons and any
\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}
satisfying forward and backward conditions,
(\bm{\alpha}_{\textnormal{b}}^{[l]})_{i}
is uniquely determined for all neurons in the wide NN. Therefore, there are
m_{\mathrm{null}}
degrees of freedom in
\bm{B}_{*}
.

In the end, the degrees of freedom in
\bm{\alpha}
is the summation of degrees of freedom in auxiliary variables
\bm{\beta}
, degrees of freedom in
\{\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}\}_{l=1}^{L}
given
\bm{\beta}
and additional degrees of freedom in
\bm{B}_{*}
for all null neurons, which add up to
K+\sum_{l\in[L]}K_{l}K_{l-1}
degrees of freedom.

Intuitively, degrees of freedom in critical embedding
\mathcal{T}
transform into dimensions of critical submanifolds, resulting in a higher estimate of degrees of degeneracy
K+\sum_{l\in[L]}K_{l}K_{l-1}
in comparision to
K
obtained by multi-step composition embedding in
Zhang et al. 2021b
. We note that the nonlinear coupling between
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
in general results in non-affine curved critical submanifolds for three-layer or deeper NNs containing the corresponding
K
-dimensional critical affine subspaces identified in Theorem
20
. A more rigorous estimate requires careful handling of the nonlinear coupling. We leave it to later works.

6.2
Irreversible transition to strict-saddle point

In this subsection, we look further into the transition between different types of critical points, e.g., local minima, saddle points, through critical embeddings. We are specifically interested in the transition of a critical point to a strict-saddle point due to its good optimization guarantee detailed in
Lee et al. 2019
. We prove the following irreversibility property for any critical embedding, which guarantees that a strict-saddle point always embedds to strict-saddle points.

Theorem 21
.

Given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and
any of its parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, for any critical embedding
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M^{\prime}}
to any wider
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
, the number of positive, zero, negative eigenvalues of
\bm{H}_{S}(\mathcal{T}(\bm{\theta}))
is no less than the counterparts of
\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta})
.

6.3
“Truly-bad” critical point

The above irreversibility property of any critical embedding provokes the following thought about a conventional bad local minimum: for any bad local minimum of a given NN, if it can become a strict-saddle point in wider NNs, then it should not be a problem as we can simply use a wider NN in practice. However, there is still a “truly-bad” situation in which a critical point may never become a strict-saddle point through any critical embedding. In the following, through proving a stringent necessary condition for such a “truly-bad” critical point defined below, we justify its rarity, hence providing a reasonable conjecture for the easy optimization of wide NNs widely observed in practice.

We denote hereafter that
\bm{A}\succeq\bm{B}
if and only if
\bm{A}-\bm{B}
is a semi-positive definite matrix, and
\bm{A}\succ\bm{B}
if and only if
\bm{A}-\bm{B}
is a strictly positive definite matrix.

Definition 22

(

Given any data

(ii) For any critical embedding

then we term this critical point a

We would like to introduce some additional notations in order to state Lemma

We denote

7
Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we prove the Embedding Principle that loss landscape of an NN
contains
all critical points/functions of all the narrower NNs. We define the critical embedding, which serves as the key tool not only to the proof of Embedding Principle but also to the study of the general geometry of loss landscape. Importantly, we discover a wide class of general compatible embedding, by which we obtain rich understanding about the critical points/submanifolds, e.g., their degree of degeneracy, their easy and irreversible transition to strict-saddle points.

The general compatible embedding proposed in this work unravels that the embedding relation from a critical point of the loss landscape of a narrow NN to critical points of the loss landscape of any wide NN is not limited by the composition of adding neurons one by one, but can be a collective operation. As a consequence, all critical points of a wide NN embedded from a critical point of a narrower NN by all possible general compatible critical embeddings, form high-dimensional critical submanifolds which in general are not affine subspaces for three-layer or deeper NNs. It is interesting and important. However, it remains a problem for the future study about whether the general compatible embeddings in certain sense are all the critical embeddings.

Embedding Principle provides an integrated view about loss landscapes of NNs with different widths. Specifically, it informs us that the existence of bad local minimum in a NN with a fixed width may not be a big deal for optimization as long as it can become a strict-saddle point in wider NNs, i.e., not a “truly-bad” critical point. In this work, we prove a stringent necessary condition for a “truly-bad” critical point. Still, it remains a problem about whether non-trivial “truly-bad” critical points indeed exist, and whether they are indeed a headache for optimization.

We emphasize that Embedding Principle provides a function space view on the critical points /submanifolds of loss landscape, which is of great importance for studying the implicit regularization and generalization of NNs. In recent years, it has been more and more clear that optimization and generalization are heavily intertwined with one another for deep learning. Therefore, one can not expect to develop a deep learning theory with optimization theory and generalization theory established separately. Yet, for a long time, the study of loss landscape focuses mainly on the parameter space in pursuit of an optimization guarantee. On the other hand, the study of generalization focuses mainly on the function space, failing to incorporate the geometry of loss landscape in parameter space which is key to the training. Now, by the Embedding Principle, we see a hierarchical structure of critical functions of different complexities of the loss landscape, which originate from loss landscape with clear optimization implication while being amenable to the analysis of complexity with clear generalization implication. Such a critical function hierarchy reflects the degree of matching between the NN architecture and data, i.e., if critical functions of narrow NNs attain low training error, then the NN architecture well matches the target function and may obtain a well generalized solution through training like in Fig.
3
. Though, more works need to be done to unravel the full details and implication of this critical function hierarchy, we believe it is an important piece and may be a key to the deep learning theory.

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{W}^{[l]}\\
\bm{W}^{[l]}_{s,[1:m_{l-1}]}\\
\end{array}}\right]\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}+\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{b}^{[l]}\\
\bm{b}^{[l]}_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right]\right)=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right].

Note that

\alpha\left[\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(s-1)},-\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(m_{l}-s)},\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right]=\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}.

Thus

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\left[\bm{W}^{[{l+1}]},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right]+\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}+\bm{b}^{[l+1]}+\alpha~\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right)=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l+1]}.

Next, by the construction of
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}
, it is clear that
\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{f}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
for any
l^{\prime}\in[l+1:L]
.

(ii) The results for feature gradients
\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}
for
l^{\prime}\in[L]
can be calculated in a similar way except by replacing
\sigma(\cdot)
with
\sigma^{(1)}(\cdot)
.

(iii) By the backpropagation and the above facts in (i), we have

\bm{z}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\nabla\ell(\bm{f}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}},\bm{y})=\nabla\ell(\bm{f}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}},\bm{y})=\bm{z}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}.

Recall the recurrence relation for
l^{\prime}\in[l+1:L-1]
, then we recursively obtain the following equality for
l^{\prime}
from
L-1
down to
l+1
:

\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=(\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}+1]})^{\intercal}\left(\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}\right)=(\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}+1]})^{\intercal}\left(\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\right)=\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l^{\prime}]}.

(41)

Next,

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\bm{W}^{[{l+1}]},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times 1}\right]+\alpha\left[\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(s-1)},-\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s},\bm{0}_{m_{l+1}\times(m_{l}-s)},\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{[1:m_{l+1}],s}\right]\right)^{\intercal}\left(\bm{z}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}\circ\bm{g}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
0\\
\end{array}}\right]+\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{0}_{(s-1)\times 1}\\
-\alpha(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{l}-s)\times 1}\\
\alpha(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\end{array}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})^{\intercal}_{[1:s-1]},(1-\alpha)(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s},(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})^{\intercal}_{[s+1:m_{l}]},\alpha(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{s}\right]^{\intercal}.

(48)

Finally,

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}

\displaystyle=\left[(\bm{W}^{[l]})^{\intercal},(\bm{W}^{[l]})_{s,[1:m_{l-1}]}^{\intercal}\right]\left(\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
0\\
\end{array}}\right]+\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{0}_{(s-1)\times 1}\\
-\alpha(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{l}-s)\times 1}\\
\alpha(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\end{array}}\right]\right)\circ\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\\
(\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=(\bm{W}^{[l]})^{\intercal}\left(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\circ\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\right)+\bm{0}_{m_{l-1}\times 1}=\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l-1]}.

(57)

This with the recurrence relation once again leads to
\bm{z}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l^{\prime}]}
for all
l^{\prime}\in[l-1]
.

(iv) If for
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{2}
, since
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}|_{k}
for
k\neq l,l+1
is the identity map, then if there exists some
k_{0}\neq l,l+1
, such that
\bm{W}_{1}^{[k_{0}]}\neq\bm{W}_{2}^{[k_{0}]}
or
\bm{b}_{1}^{[k_{0}]}\neq\bm{b}_{2}^{[k_{0}]}
, then obviously
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\neq\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
. If
k_{0}=l
or
k_{0}=l+1
, by similar reasoning,
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\neq\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
.

(v) For
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\bm{W}_{0}^{[1]},\bm{b}_{0}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}_{0}^{[L]},\bm{b}_{0}^{[L]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{l,s}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{0})=\Big(\bm{W}_{0}^{[1]},\bm{b}_{0}^{[1]},\cdots,\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{W}_{0}^{[l]}\\
({\bm{W}_{0}^{[l]}})_{s,[1:m_{l-1}]}\\
\end{array}}\right],\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{b}_{0}^{[l]}\\
({\bm{b}_{0}^{[l]}})_{s}\\
\end{array}}\right],

\displaystyle\left[(\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{0})_{[1:m_{l+1}],[1:s-1]},(1-\alpha)(\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{0})_{[1:m_{l+1}],s},(\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{0})_{[1:m_{l+1}],[s+1:m_{l}]},\alpha(\bm{W}_{0}^{[l+1]})_{[1:m_{l+1}],s}\right],

\displaystyle~~\bm{b}_{0}^{[l+1]},\cdots,\bm{W}_{0}^{[L]},\bm{b}_{0}^{[L]}\Big),

obviously
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
is a linear operator, thus
\mathcal{T}_{l,s}^{\alpha}
is an affine operator.
∎

Directly from Lemma
5
and Lemma
6
, we obtain that both one-step null embedding and one-step splitting embedding satisfy the property of output preserving and representation preserving, and all we need is to check the property of criticality preserving.

Proposition

(Proposition
1
in main text)
.

For any one-step null embedding
\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}
, given any
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameters

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}
with
\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\in\mathcal{D}_{l,0}
, we have
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}:=\mathcal{T}_{l,0}^{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})
satisfies the following conditions: given any data
S
, loss
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
and activation
\sigma(\cdot)
,
if
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}
, then
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\bm{0}
.

Proof.

Gradient of loss with respect to network parameters of each layer can be computed from
\bm{F}
,
\bm{G}
, and
\bm{Z}
as follows

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left(\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}]}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}-1]})^{\intercal}\right),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}]}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}}\right).

Then, by Lemma
5
, we have for
l^{\prime}\neq l,l+1
,

\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0},

and

\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}.

Also, for any
j\in[m_{l+1}]
,
k\in[m_{l}]
, since
(\bm{z}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}=(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l+1]})_{j}
,

(\bm{g}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}=(\bm{g}^{[l+1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{j}
,

(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{k}=(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{k}
, and
\bm{W}_{j,(m_{l}+1)}^{[l+1]}\equiv 0
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{j,k}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{j,k}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l+1]}_{j,(m_{l}+1)}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l+1]}_{j}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l+1]}_{j}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=0.

Similarly, for any
j\in[m_{l}]
,
k\in[m_{l-1}]
, since
(\bm{z}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}=(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[l]})_{j}
,

(\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}=(\bm{g}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{j}
,

(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{k}=(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{k}
, and
\bm{W}_{(m_{l}+1),k}^{[l]}\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l]}_{j,k}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l]}_{j,k}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l]}_{(m_{l}+1),k}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}_{j}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}_{j}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=0.

Moreover, by Lemma
5
, the output function
\bm{f}^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}}^{[L]}
is independent of the hyperparameter
\alpha
, and
R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})
, then since
\bm{b}^{[l]}_{(m_{l}+1)}=\alpha
, we have

\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}_{(m_{l}+1)}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=0.

Collecting all the above relations, we obtain that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\bm{0}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l]}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}]}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}}\right).

\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0},

\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}})=\nabla_{\bm{b}^{[l^{\prime}]}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}})=\bm{0}.

Theorem

(Theorem
9
in main text)
.

A
K
-step composition embedding is a critical embedding.

Proof.

We shall prove it using induction.

For
K=1
, Proposition
9
holds since both one-step null embedding and one step splitting embedding are critical embeddings.

Assume that Proposition
9
holds for
K=l-1
, we want to show that it also holds for
K=l
.

From the induction hypothesis, we only need to show that if given two critical embeddings
\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
is also a critical embedding.

(i)
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
is injective:

For
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
belonging to a same tuple class but
\bm{\theta}_{1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{2}
, since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is injective, then
\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\neq\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
. Since
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is injective, then
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\neq\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
.

(ii)
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
is an affine embedding:

We have that
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
are linear operators, then

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}):=\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0}),

we need to show that for any
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}+\bm{\theta}_{2})=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(c\bm{\theta}_{1})=c\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}).

Since

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}+\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1}+\bm{\theta}_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})-\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})\right)-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\right)+\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right)-\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})\right)-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(c\bm{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(c\bm{\theta}_{1})-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(c\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+(1-c)\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})\right)-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=c\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\right)+(1-c)\mathcal{T}_{2}\left(\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})\right)-\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=c\widetilde{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}).

(iii)
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies the property of output preserving:

Since
\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies the property of output preserving, then for any
\bm{\theta}
,
\bm{f}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
. Similarly for
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, we have
\bm{f}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}=\bm{f}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}
, hence
\bm{f}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})}=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
.

(iv)
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies the property of representation preserving:

Similar reasoning in (iii).

(v)
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
satisfies the property of criticality preserving:

Since
\bm{\theta}
is a critical point of
R_{S}({\bm{\theta}})
, so is
\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
, and
\mathcal{T}_{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
as well, we finish the proof.
∎

Lemma

(Lemma
13
in main text)
.

For any affine embedding
\mathcal{T}:\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}\to\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m^{\prime}_{0},\cdots,m^{\prime}_{L}\}}
satisfying the output preserving property,
if there exist a total index mapping
\mathcal{I}=\{\mathcal{I}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}
from
\mathrm{NN}(\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
to
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})

and auxiliary variables
\bm{\beta}=\left\{\bm{\beta}^{[l]}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}|~l\in[L],j\in[m^{\prime}_{l}]\backslash\mathcal{I}_{l}^{-1}(0)\right\}
,
such that for any given neuron belonging to

Proof.

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{W}^{[l^{\prime}]}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\left(\left(\bm{z}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}]}\circ\bm{g}^{[l^{\prime}]}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[l^{\prime}-1]})^{\intercal}\right),

Hence for

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}(\bm{e}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}^{[l]})_{i}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}^{[l-1]})_{j}

\mathcal{I}_{l-1}(j)=0

Since

Theorem

(Theorem
15
in main text)
.

General compatible embedding is a critical embedding.

Remark that we later name it as general compatible critical embedding in this work.

Proof.

We need to prove four properties one by one.

1.

\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is output preserving and representation preserving;

2.

\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is an injective operator;

3.

\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is an affine embedding;

4.

\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is criticality preserving.

(i) We prove output preserving and representation preserving by doing induction on layers.

For the first layer, i.e.,
l=1
, we have for
i\in[m^{\prime}_{1}]
,

\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{[1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{j\in[m^{\prime}_{0}]}(\bm{W}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{wide}})_{ij}\bm{x}_{j}+(\bm{b}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{wide}})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{j\in[m_{0}]}\bm{\alpha}^{[1]}_{ij}(\bm{W}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{1}(i),j}\bm{x}_{j}+(\bm{\alpha}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{b}})_{i}+(\bm{b}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{1}(i)}\right),

then for
i\notin\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(0)
, for each
j\in[m_{0}]
,
since
\bm{\alpha}^{[1]}_{ij}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{0}(j)}\bm{\alpha}_{is}=1
,
(\bm{\alpha}^{[1]}_{\textnormal{b}})_{i}=0
, hence

(\bm{f}^{[1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=(\bm{f}^{[1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{\mathcal{I}_{1}(i)}.

Otherwise, for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{1}(0)
, we have
\bm{\alpha}^{[1]}_{ij}=0
and
(\bm{\alpha}_{\textnormal{b}}^{[1]})_{i}=(\bm{b}^{[1]}_{*})_{i}
, then

\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{[1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=\sigma\left((\bm{b}^{[1]}_{*})_{i}\right),

which is a constant function playing the same role as the bias term in the next layer.

Suppose for layer
l-1
, we have for
i\notin\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(0)

(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l-1}(i)},

Then we want to show that this is also the case for layer
l
.

We obtain that

\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{j\in[m^{\prime}_{l-1}]}(\bm{W}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{wide}})_{ij}(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}+(\bm{b}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{wide}})_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{s\in[m_{l-1}]\cup\{0\}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(s)}\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{ij}(\bm{W}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i),s}(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}+(\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{b}})_{i}+(\bm{b}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\Bigg(\sum_{s\in[m_{l-1}]}(\bm{W}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i),s}(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(s)}\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{ij}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l-1}(0)}\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{ij}\sigma\left((\bm{b}^{[l-1]}_{*})_{j}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~+(\bm{\alpha}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{b}})_{i}+(\bm{b}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i)}\Bigg).

For
i\notin\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(0)
, we have

\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=\sigma\left(\sum_{s\in[m_{l-1}]}(\bm{W}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i),s}(\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{s}+(\bm{b}^{[l]}_{\textnormal{narr}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i)}\right)=(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}})_{\mathcal{I}_{l}(i)}.

Otherwise, for
i\in\mathcal{I}^{-1}_{l}(0)
, we have

\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=\sigma\left((\bm{b}^{[l]}_{*})_{i}\right).

Then, for any layer

, we have for

and for

(\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}=\sigma\left((\bm{b}^{[l]}_{*})_{i}\right).

Hence, we have proved already that
\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is output preserving and representation preserving.

(ii) We prove that
\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is injective.

If for
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},1},\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},2}\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}}

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},2}
, then there exists some

, such that
\bm{W}_{\textnormal{narr},1}^{[l]}\neq\bm{W}_{\textnormal{narr},2}^{[l]}
or
\bm{b}_{\textnormal{narr},1}^{[l]}\neq\bm{b}_{\textnormal{narr},2}^{[l]}
.

Then, if
\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}
is not injective, there exists
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},2}
, such that
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide},1}=\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide},2}
, where
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide},1}:=\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},1})

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide},2}:=\mathcal{T}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr},2})

Since there exists

\bm{W}_{\textnormal{narr},1}^{[l]}\neq\bm{W}_{\textnormal{narr},2}^{[l]}

\bm{b}_{\textnormal{narr},1}^{[l]}\neq\bm{b}_{\textnormal{narr},2}^{[l]}

, then

hence

.

For the case where

, since

for any

. Then for any

Theorem

\bm{A}^{\intercal}\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{A}\bm{\theta}+\bm{c})\bm{A}\equiv\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta}).

\bm{\theta}_{0}

negative eigenvalues.

(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}(\bm{W}^{[L]})_{i,j}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j}+(\bm{b}^{[L]})_{i},

hence we obtain that, for any

,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})^{i}}{\partial\bm{W}^{[L]}}

\displaystyle=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{0}_{(i-1)\times m_{L-1}}\\
\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j}\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{L}-i)\times m_{L-1}}\\
\end{array}}\right],~~j\in[m_{L-1}],

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})^{i}}{\partial\bm{b}^{[L]}}

\displaystyle=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{0}_{(i-1)\times 1}\\
1\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{L}-i)\times 1}\\
\end{array}}\right].

Finally, given an
\mathrm{NN}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L})
and its parameter

\bm{\theta}=(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L]},\bm{b}^{[L]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L}\}},

we remind the readers once again that the collection of parameters
\bm{\theta}
is a
2L
-tuple.
We denote that the upper bracket
[L-1]
by limiting ourselves to the first
2L-2

\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}:=(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\cdots,\bm{W}^{[L-2]},\bm{b}^{[L-2]},\bm{W}^{[L-1]},\bm{b}^{[L-1]})\in\mathrm{Tuple}_{\{m_{0},\cdots,m_{L-2},m_{L-1}\}},

(62)

and similarly, we identify
\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}
with its vectorization
\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]})\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{[L-1]}}
with
M^{[L-1]}:=\sum_{l=0}^{L-2}(m_{l}+1)m_{l+1}
.

Moreover, we observe that for
i\in[m_{L}]
,
j\in[m_{L-1}]

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\bm{W}_{i,j}^{[L]}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\left((\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\right),

hence we denote hereafter the expression by

\bm{H}^{(2),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\bm{W}_{i,j}^{[L]}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j}.

We denote further that

\bm{H}^{(1),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{ij}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}})_{j}\right)^{\intercal},

and
\bm{H}^{[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}):=\bm{H}^{(1),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{H}^{(2),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta})
.

Lemma

(Lemma

in main text)
.

Given any data
S
, loss
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)

\sigma(\cdot)

\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{c}}\in\bm{\Theta}^{\textnormal{c}}

Proof.

We consider the three-fold global splitting embedding

\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}}

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}}

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{1}:=\bm{\theta}_{2}:=\bm{\theta}_{3}:=\bm{\theta}^{[L-1]}=\left(\mathrm{vec}(\bm{W}^{[1]})^{\intercal},\cdots,\mathrm{vec}(\bm{W}^{[L-2]})^{\intercal},\mathrm{vec}(\bm{W}^{[L-1]})^{\intercal},{\bm{b}^{[1]}}^{\intercal},\cdots,{\bm{b}^{[L-1]}}^{\intercal}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{\textnormal{c}}_{\textnormal{wide}})

(63)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}},\bm{f}^{*})\bm{W}_{i,j}^{[L]}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L-1]}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L-1]}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j},

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{m_{L}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{ij}\ell(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}},\bm{f}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L-1]}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L-1]}}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}})_{j}\right)^{\intercal}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{A}_{1,1}&\bm{A}_{1,2}&\bm{A}_{1,3}\\
\bm{A}_{2,1}&\bm{A}_{2,2}&\bm{A}_{2,3}\\
\bm{A}_{3,1}&\bm{A}_{3,2}&\bm{A}_{3,3}\end{pmatrix},

with

\bm{B}_{2,1}&\bm{B}_{2,2}&\bm{B}_{2,3}\\
\bm{B}_{3,1}&\bm{B}_{3,2}&\bm{B}_{3,3}\end{pmatrix},

for
p\in[3]
and
q\in[3]
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{x})\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}\left(\bm{W}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L]}\right)_{i,j}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j},i\in[m_{L}],

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{x})\right)_{i}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{3}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{3}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{x})\right)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2m_{L-1}+1}^{3m_{L-1}}\left(\bm{W}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{[L]}\right)_{i,j}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{3}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{3}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}^{[L-1]}\right)_{j},i\in[m_{L}],

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{x})\right)_{i}

Thus for

, then there exist a nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0
associated with its unit eigenvector
\bm{v}
.

\bm{v}^{\intercal}\bm{H}^{(2),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})\bm{v}=\lambda

We observe that the matrix below is a principle submatrix of the Hessian at
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}
, i.e., by choosing the columns and rows corresponding to
\left(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2},\bm{\theta}_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}):

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}^{(1),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})&\bm{H}^{(1),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})&-\bm{H}^{(1),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})\\

\displaystyle~~+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}^{(2),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\bm{H}^{(2),[L-1]}_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{narr}}^{\textnormal{c}})&\mathbf{0}\\

, since
\bm{v}^{\intercal}\widetilde{\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}})\bm{v}=\lambda
,
then for
\bm{u}=[\bm{v}^{\intercal},-\bm{v}^{\intercal},\mathbf{0}]^{\intercal}

indicating
\bm{\theta}_{\textnormal{wide}}^{\textnormal{c}}
is also a strict-saddle point.

.

, loss

\sigma(\cdot)
