Finding a Cluster in Incomplete Data
Eduard Eiben, Robert Ganian, Iyad Kanj, Sebastian Ordyniak, Stefan Szeider

Abstract
We study two variants of the fundamental problem of finding a cluster in incomplete data. In the problems under consideration, we are given a multiset of incomplete d d -dimensional vectors over the binary domain and integers k k and r r , and the goal is to complete the missing vector entries so that the multiset of complete vectors either contains (i) a cluster of k k vectors of radius at most r r , or (ii) a cluster of k k vectors of diameter at most r r . We give tight characterizations of the parameterized complexity of the problems under consideration with respect to the parameters k k , r r , and a third parameter that captures the missing vector entries.

The

-Center

(i.e., when the cluster contains all vectors in

). Cabello et al.
[
4
]
studied the parameterized complexity of the geometric
p
-Center Problem
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Frances and Litman
[
14
]
studied the complexity of
Rad-Cluster
with
k=|M|
, in the context of computing the radius of a binary code; they referred to it as the
Covering Radius
problem and showed it to be
NP
-hard. Ga̧sieniec et al.
[
16
,
17
]
studied (the
optimization versions of)
Rad-Cluster
and
Diam-Cluster
with
k=|M|
and obtained polynomial-time algorithms
as well as lower bounds for a number of cases. They also obtained
2
-approximation algorithms for these problems by extending an earlier algorithm by Gonzalez
[
18
]
.

The
Rad-Cluster
problem restricted to the subcase of

was also extensively studied under the nomenclature
Closest String
. Li et al.
[
25
]
showed that the problem admits a polynomial time approximation scheme if the goal is to minimize
r
.
Gramm et al.
[
19
]
studied

Closest String
from the parameterized complexity perspective and showed it to be fixed-parameter tractable parameterized by

. Following this naming convention, Boucher and Ma
[
2
]
, and Bulteau and Schmid
[
3
]
studied the parameterized complexity of

under the nomenclature

. They considered several parameters, including some of the parameters under consideration in this paper. Notably, the restriction of our fixed-parameter algorithm for

parameterized by

.
Moreover, our

immediately implies an algorithm of the same running time for

, as a special case.

For incomplete data, Hermelin and Rozenberg
[
20
]
studied the parameterized complexity of

for
k=|M|
under the nomenclature
Closest String with Wildcards
problem, with respect to several parameterizations. Very recently, Koana et al.
[
21
]
revisited the earlier work of Hermelin and Rozenberg

Even more recently, the same group
[

also studied a problem related to

for

. They obtain a classical-complexity classification w.r.t. constant lower and upper bounds on the diameter and the maximum number of missing entries per row.

Vector Terminology.
Let
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
be two vectors in
\{0,1,{\small\square}\}^{d}
, where
{\small\square}
is used to represent coordinates whose value is unknown (i.e., missing entries). We denote
by
\Delta(\vec{a},\vec{b})
the set of coordinates in which

\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
are guaranteed to differ, i.e.,

\Delta(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\{\,i\;{|}\;(\vec{a}[i]=1\wedge\vec{b}[i]=0)\vee(\vec{a}[i]=0\wedge\vec{b}[i]=1)\,\}

the

between

\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
measured only between known entries, i.e.,

|\Delta(\vec{a},\vec{b})|
. Moreover, for a subset

of coordinates, where

\vec{a}

restricted to the coordinates in

There is a one-to-one correspondence between vectors in

It will be useful to represent a vector by the set of coordinates where the vector has the value
1
.
We introduce the following notation for vectors to switch between their set-representation
and vector-representation. We denote by

the set

.
We extend this notation to sets of

\{0,1,{\small\square}\}^{d}

the set

We say that a vector

is a

if

and
we say that
\vec{a}

contains

Parameterized Complexity.
In parameterized complexity
[
13
,
7
,
6
]
,
the complexity of a problem is studied not only with respect to the
input size, but also with respect to some problem parameter(s). The
core idea behind parameterized complexity is that the combinatorial
explosion resulting from the
NP
-hardness of a problem can sometimes
be confined to certain structural parameters that are small in
practical settings. We now proceed to the formal definitions.

A
parameterized problem

Q
is a subset of
\Omega^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, where
\Omega
is a fixed alphabet. Each instance of
Q
is a pair
(I,\kappa)
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
is called the
parameter
. A parameterized problem
Q
is

fixed-parameter tractable
(
FPT
)
[
13
,
7
,
6
]
, if there is an
algorithm, called an
FPT
-algorithm
, that decides whether an input
(I,\kappa)

is a member of
Q
in time
f(\kappa)\cdot|I|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
f
is a computable function and
|I|
is the input instance size. The class
FPT
denotes the class of all fixed-parameter
tractable parameterized problems.

A parameterized problem
Q

is
FPT
-reducible
to a parameterized problem
Q^{\prime}
if there is
an algorithm, called an
FPT
-reduction
, that transforms each instance
(I,\kappa)
of
Q

into an instance
(I^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
of

Q^{\prime}
in time
f(\kappa)\cdot|I|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, such that
\kappa^{\prime}\leq g(\kappa)
and
(I,\kappa)\in Q
if and
only if
(I^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})\in Q^{\prime}
, where
f
and
g
are computable
functions. By
FPT
-time
, we denote time of the form
f(\kappa)\cdot|I|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
f
is a computable function.
Based on the notion of
FPT
-reducibility, a hierarchy of
parameterized complexity,
the
W
-hierarchy

=\bigcup_{t\geq 0}{{{\mbox{{{W}}}}}}{{{[t]}}}
, where
{{{\mbox{{{W}}}}}}{{{[t]}}}\subseteq{{{\mbox{{{W}}}}}}{{{[t+1]}}}
for all
t\geq 0
, has
been introduced, in which the
0
-th level
W
[0] is the class
FPT
. The notions of hardness and completeness have been defined for each level

W
[
i
] of the
W
-hierarchy for
i\geq 1

[
7
,
6
]
. It is commonly believed that
{{{\mbox{{{W}}}}}}{{{[1]}}}\neq{\mbox{{{FPT}}}}
(see
[
7
,
6
]
). The

W
[1]-hardness has served as the main working hypothesis of fixed-parameter
intractability. The class
XP
contains parameterized problems that can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(|I|^{f(\kappa)})
, where
f
is a computable function; it
contains the class
W
[t], for
t\geq 0
, and every problem in
XP
is polynomial-time solvable when the parameters are bounded by a constant.
The class
paraNP
is the class of parameterized problems that can be solved by non-deterministic algorithms in time
f(\kappa)\cdot|I|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
f
is a computable function.
A problem is
paraNP
-hard
if it is
NP
-hard for a constant value of the parameter
[
13
]
.

A parameterized problem is
kernelizable

if there exists a polynomial-time reduction that maps an instance
(I,\kappa)
of
the problem to another instance
(I^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
such that (1)
|I^{\prime}|\leq f(\kappa)
and
\kappa^{\prime}\leq f(\kappa)
, where
f
is a computable function, and (2)
(I,\kappa)
is a
Yes
-instance
of the problem if and only if
(I^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
is. The instance

(I^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
is called the
kernel
of
I
. It is well known that a
decidable problem is
FPT
if and only if it is
kernelizable
[
7
]
.
A
polynomial kernel
is a kernel whose size can be bounded by a
polynomial in the parameter.

A
Turing kernelization
for a parameterized problem
Q
is an algorithm that, provided
with access to an oracle for
Q
, decides in polynomial time whether or not an input
(I,\kappa)

is a YES-instance of
Q
. During its
computation, the algorithm can produce polynomially-many oracle queries on input of size
f(\kappa)
, for some computable function
f
. The function
f
is referred to as the
kernel size
.
An
FPT
approximation scheme
for a parameterized
maximization problem is an algorithm that takes as input an instance

(I,\kappa)
of
Q
and
0<\epsilon\leq 1
, and produces in
FPT
-time in
\kappa+1/\epsilon
a solution to
I
that is an
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation for the instance.

3.1
Diam-Cluster-Completion
Parameterized by
k+r+\lambda(M)

We start by showing that
Diam-Cluster-Completion
parameterized by
k+r+\lambda(M)

is
FPT
. We will later show a stronger
result, namely that the same result already holds if we only
parameterize by
r+\lambda(M)
. Showing the weaker result here is
important for the following reasons: (1) we use the algorithm presented here as a subroutine in our result
for the parameterization
r+\lambda(M)
, (2) the techniques developed here can also be employed for
Rad-Cluster-Completion
, and
(3) we obtain a Turing kernel of size polynomial in
k
.

The following lemma shows how this
can be used to pre-process the instance (by identifying vectors that
cannot be in a cluster with the two vectors of maximum distance). Note
that this idea and the following lemma only deal with the case that the
solution cluster uses at least two vectors from
M\setminus D_{M}
,
where
D_{M}
is a deletion set such that every vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
has at most
\lambda(M)
missing entries. For a vector
\vec{v}
, we
denote by
\textup{ME}(\vec{v})
the set of all missing coordinates of
\vec{v}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
be two vectors in
M\setminus D_{M}
and
let
t
be an integer with
\delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\leq t\leq\delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})+2|D_{M}|
. Then any vector
\vec{m}
satisfying

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})|>t
,
|\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t
, or

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t/2
cannot be
contained in a DIAM-Cluster/RAD-Cluster containing
\vec{v}
and

\vec{u}
, and in which
\vec{v}
and

\vec{u}
are two vectors of maximum distance
t
.

Proof.

Clearly, the lemma holds for any vector
\vec{m}
satisfying either

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})|>t
or
|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})|>t
,
since we are only looking for clusters where
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}

are of distance
t

to one another and have maximum distance within
the cluster. Now let
\vec{m}
be a vector satisfying

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t/2
.
Since the completions of
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
in a solution differ in exactly
t
coordinates in

\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u})
, it follows that
\vec{m}
differs
in at least
t/2
of those coordinates from either
\vec{v}
or

\vec{u}
. It follows that
\vec{m}
differs in more than
t
coordinates

∎

The following lemma shows that the instance obtained after
identifying two vectors of maximum distance and removing the vectors

-

Lemma 3
.

Let
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
be two vectors in

and
let

be an integer with
\delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\leq t\leq\delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})+2|D_{M}|
. Let

be the set of all vectors
in

obtained after removing all vectors
\vec{m}

for which either

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})|)>t
,
|\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|)>t
, or

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t/2
. If

|M^{\prime}|>k3^{2|D_{M}|+t}
, then

contains a
DIAM-Cluster of size at least
k

also a RAD-Cluster of radius
t/2
.

Proof.

Assume that there are at least
k3^{2|D_{M}|+t}

vectors
\vec{m}
in
M^{\prime}
. Then there is a set

of

all the coordinates in

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u})|\leq t+2|D_{M}|

3^{2|D_{M}|+t}
possible assignments to these coordinates. Since

\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
are equal on all other coordinates, i.e.,
the coordinates in
[d]\setminus(\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u}))
,
we obtain that any such vector
\vec{m}
disagrees with
\vec{v}
(or

\vec{u}
)
on at most

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t/2
and we
would have removed
\vec{m}
from

M\setminus(D_{M}\cup\{\vec{v},\vec{u}\})

agree on all coordinates in
\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u})
, we can complete all of them in the same manner on

\vec{v}
is
equal to
\vec{u}
and we can complete all vectors in

by setting

\vec{v}
(or equivalently
\vec{u}
) on all other coordinates.
Let

be the set of vectors obtained from

after completing
the vectors in this manner. Then
\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})\subseteq[d]\setminus(\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u}))
; moreover,

|\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})|\leq|(\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m}))\cup(\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m}^{\prime})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m}^{\prime}))|\leq 2t/2=t
for every two vectors
\vec{m}
and
\vec{m}^{\prime}
in

. Therefore, the vectors in

form a DIAM-Cluster with diameter
at most
t
. Moreover, the vectors in
S^{\prime}
also form a RAD-Cluster with

t/2
, as witnessed by a center equal to

\vec{v}
(or equivalently
\vec{u}
) on all coordinates in

[d]\setminus(\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{v})\cup\textup{ME}(\vec{u}))
, and
equal to any vector in

on all other coordinates.
∎

We will now introduce notions required to reduce the number of coordinates.
Let
|Z(M)|
, for

, be the set of
all coordinates

such that at least two vectors in

disagree on
their

such that

.
Intuitively,

is the set of

, since all other

they can always be completed to the same value.
We are now ready to show that
Diam-Cluster-Completion
has a Turing kernel
parameterized by

.

Theorem 4
.

Diam-Cluster-Completion
parameterized by

has a Turing-kernel
containing at most
n=k3^{2\lambda(M)+r}+\lambda(M)+2
vectors, each
having at most

Proof.

We distinguish three cases: (1) the solution cluster contains at
least two vectors in

, (2) the solution cluster
contains exactly one vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
, and (3) the
solution cluster does not contain any vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
.

We start by showing the result for case (1).
Our first goal is to reduce the number of vectors in

. As a first step, we
guess two vectors
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
in
M\setminus D_{M}
that
are farthest apart in the solution cluster w.r.t. to all vectors in

M\setminus D_{M}
; this is possible since we assume that the cluster
contains at least two vectors in

.
We then guess the exact distance,

\vec{v}

and
\vec{u}
in a completion leading to the cluster. Note that

can
be any value between
\delta(\vec{v},\vec{u})
and

.
We now remove all vectors
\vec{m}

from

for which either

|\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\cap\Delta(\vec{u},\vec{m})|>t/2

Note that this is safe by of Lemma

It now follows from Lemma

.
Otherwise, we obtain that

in at most

.

In the following let

.
For the second case, i.e., the case that the solution cluster

, we first guess the
vector say
\vec{m}
in

that will be included in the solution

For the third case, i.e., the case that the solution cluster

, we first remove all vectors
in

. This leaves us with
|D_{M}|

only remains to reduce the number of coordinates. To achieve this,

Namely, we compute a set

of relevant coordinates, starting from

and

in

•

if

\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})
to

and otherwise

•

we add an arbitrary subset of exactly

to

.

Let

be the matrix obtained from

after removing all

and
(M_{D},k,r)

equivalent instances of

. This follows because the vectors
in any DIAM-Cluster for

can be completed in the same manner
on all coordinates not in

|D_{M}|

vectors each having at most
\binom{|D_{M}|}{2}(t+1)
coordinates.
∎

3.2
Diam-Cluster-Completion
Parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)

With Theorem
4
in hand, we can move on to establishing the fixed-parameter tractability of
Diam-Cluster-Completion
parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)
.
At the heart of our approach lies a new technique for analyzing the structure of vectors through sunflowers in their set representations, which we dub
iterative sunflower harvesting
.
We first preprocess the instance to establish some basic properties. We then show a general result about sunflowers that allows us to derive a succinct representation of the solution cluster—in particular, this guarantees that the hypothetical solution can be described by a bounded number of sunflower cores. Finally, we proceed to “harvesting” these sunflowers cores using a branching procedure, thus computing the solution cluster.

3.2.1
Preprocessing the Instance

Let
(M,k,r)
be an instance of
Diam-Cluster-Completion
, let

be a completion of
M
that contains a maximum size DIAM-Cluster, and fix
P^{*}
to be such a maximum size DIAM-Cluster in

. We also fix

to be a
\lambda(M)
-
deletion set. The goal of the algorithm is to find

and

.

If

, then we can use the algorithm in Theorem
4
to obtain a Turing kernel whose size is a function of

Diam-Cluster-Completion
if
FPT
parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)
, thus giving the desired result. We assume henceforth that
P^{*}
contains the completion of at least two vectors in
M\setminus D_{M}
. We can guess the subset
P_{R}
of

that will be completed to vectors in

, and restrict our attention to finding a

M\setminus D_{M}
of size
|P^{*}\setminus D_{M}|
. We will do so by enumerating all such DIAM-Cluster’s in
M\setminus D_{M}
and at the end check whether one of them, together with

, can be extended into a DIAM-Cluster.

Therefore, we will focus on what follows on finding a cluster in
M\setminus D_{M}
. We first
guess two vectors
\vec{v}
and
\vec{u}
in
M\setminus D_{M}

\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}

\vec{v}^{*}

\vec{u}^{*}

or
\vec{u}^{*}

We then normalize all vectors in

by
the all-zero vector, and for every other vector

if
\vec{w}[i]={\small\square}
, and
\vec{w}^{\prime}[i]=1

\vec{w}\in M\setminus D_{M}

\Lambda(\vec{w})
of all completions of

at distance at most

from both
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}
. Note that

M\setminus D_{M}
, where

is the dimension of the vectors in

. We then remove all vectors
\vec{w}
with

.

We will extend the notation

to

of vectors in

is the set of all completions of a vector

at distance at most

to all vectors in

. We are now ready to show that after normalizing the vectors in

, the multiset

satisfies certain structural properties that we refer to as an

-saturated subset (of

.

3.2.2
Sunflower Fields or Representing Sets by Cores of Sunflowers

In this subsection, we provide the central component for our algorithm based on
the iterative sunflower harvesting technique. Crucial to this component
is a general structural lemma that allows us to represent a family
of sets in a succinct manner in terms of sunflower cores, which we believe
to be interesting in its own right. We first state the result in its
most general form (for sets), and then show how to adapt it to
our setting.

Definition 1
.

Let
U
be a universe,
\mathcal{B}
a family of subsets of
U
and

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is an
r
-saturated
subfamily of

\mathcal{B}
(for
r\in\mathbb{N}
) if the following holds for every
t\in\mathbb{N}
and every sunflower

S\subseteq\mathcal{A}
containing at least
r+1
sets of cardinality
t
with
core
C
:
\mathcal{A}
contains every set
B\in\mathcal{B}
of cardinality
t
such that

C\subseteq B
.

Intuitively, this property states that
\mathcal{A}
contains
all sets in
\mathcal{B}
which are super-sets of cores of every sufficiently-large sunflower in
\mathcal{A}
(with sets of the same cardinality).

The connection of this set property to clusters is as follows. We will show that every maximal cluster is an
r
-saturated subset of
M^{*}
.
Since
P^{*}
contains the all-zero vector
\vec{v}^{*}
, any vector in
P^{*}
contains at most
r
ones. Fix
t\in[r]
, and consider the set of all vectors in
P^{*}
containing exactly
t
ones. The above notion will allow to draw the following assumption: if the aforementioned set of vectors is large, then it must contain a large sunflower and all the vectors in
M
whose completions share the core of this sunflower
must be
in
P
. This property will subsequently allow us to represent every hypothetical solution using a bounded number of sunflowers, as we show next.

The crucial insight now is that every
r
-saturated subfamily
containing only sets of bounded size admits a succinct representation, where we can
completely describe the set via a bounded number of
sunflower cores. This is made precise in the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two families of sets of cardinality at most

r^{\prime}
over universe
U
such that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.
If
\mathcal{A}
is an
r
-saturated subset of
\mathcal{B}
,
then there is a set
\mathcal{S}
of at most
(rr^{\prime})^{r^{\prime}}

U

such that
\mathcal{A}
is equal to the
set of all sets
B
in
\mathcal{B}
satisfying that
S\subseteq B
for some

S\in\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{A}

We show the claim by giving a bounded-depth search tree algorithm

of

, and in which each leaf node is
marked as either successful or unsuccessful. Note that the algorithm is not necessarily efficient, and all
the steps can be brute-forced. The set
\mathcal{S}
then
contains all sets associated with a successful leaf node of

.
Initially,
T
only consists of the root node
r

X_{r}:=\emptyset

T
contains a leaf that is not yet marked as either successful or unsuccessful.
Let
t
be a leaf node of
T
(that has

|X_{t}|=r^{\prime}
and

, or whether
\mathcal{A}

contains a sunflower with at least

petals and core

containing all sets

contains a sunflower with at

petals and core

. We now claim that the set

M
, since these can always be completed in the
same manner for all the vectors. Now the remaining instance is a
kernel containing at most
m=k3^{|D_{M}|+t}+2+|D_{M}|
vectors each
having at most
t(m-1)+|D_{M}|
coordinates. This completes the proof for
the case that the solution cluster contains at least two vectors in

M\setminus D_{M}
.

In the following, let
t=2r
.

For the second case, i.e., the case that the solution cluster
contains exactly one vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
, we first guess the
vector say
\vec{m}
in
M\setminus D_{M}
that will be included in the solution
cluster and then remove all other vectors from
M\setminus D_{M}
. This
already leaves us with at most
|D_{M}|+1
vectors and it only remains
to reduce the number of relevant coordinates. Because we guessed
that
\vec{m}
will be in the solution cluster, we can now safely
remove all vectors
\vec{m}^{\prime}\in M
, with

\delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})>t
. Now every remaining vector
differs from
\vec{m}
in at most
t
coordinates and hence

Z(M)\leq t(|D_{M}|+1)+|D_{M}|
, which gives us the desired kernel.

For the third case, i.e., the case that the solution cluster
contains no vectors in
M\setminus D_{M}
, we first remove all vectors
in
M\setminus D_{M}
. This leaves us with only
|D_{M}|
vectors and it
only remains to reduce the number of coordinates. To achieve this,
we compute a set of coordinates
that preserves the distance up to
t
between any pair of vectors.
Namely, we compute a set
D
of relevant coordinates starting from

D=\emptyset
by adding the following coordinates to
D

for every two distinct vectors
\vec{m}
and
\vec{m}^{\prime}
in

M
:

•

if
|\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})|\leq t
, we add

\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})
to
D
and otherwise

•

we add an arbitrary subset of at most
t+1
coordinates in

\Delta(\vec{m},\vec{m}^{\prime})
to
D
.

Let
M_{D}
be the matrix obtained from
D
after removing all
coordinates/columns in
D
.
It is now
straightforward to show that
(M,k,r)
and
(M_{D},k,r)
are
equivalent instances of
Rad-Cluster-Completion
. Since
|D|\leq\binom{|D_{M}|}{2}(t+1)
, the remaining instance has at most
|D_{M}|

vectors each having at most
\binom{|D_{M}|}{2}(t+1)
coordinates.
∎

4.2
Rad-Cluster-Completion
Parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)

While, it is relatively easy to see that
Rad-Cluster-Completion
parameterized by

r+\lambda(M)
can be solved in time
f(\lambda(M),r)n^{\mathcal{O}(r)}
,
here we provide a more efficient algorithm by
reducing the degree of the polynomial in the run-time from
\mathcal{O}(r)

to
\log r
. Moreover, our algorithm can be
applied to the
Closest String with Outliers

problem
[
3
]
.

Theorem 22
.

Rad-Cluster-Completion
can be solved in time
\mathcal{O}(|M|2^{\lambda(M)}(|M|(2^{2r}+d))^{\log r+1})

and is therefore in
XP
parameterized
r+\lambda(M)
.

Proof.

Note first that, by Observation
20
, testing whether a vector
\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is
a center of a solution cluster for
(M,k,r)
can be achieved in time

\mathcal{O}(|M|d)
. Therefore, it suffices to focus on finding a center for the cluster.

Let
(M,k,r)
be an instance of
Rad-Cluster-Completion
. If
k\leq\lambda(M)
, then we can
use the
FPT
-algorithm parameterized by
k+r+\lambda(M)
implied by
Theorem
21
. Otherwise, every RAD-Cluster of size at
least
k
contains at least one vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
.

Let
F\subseteq[d]
and let
t
be an integer, where
0\leq t\leq r
.
We say that a vector
\vec{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}

is an
(F,t)
-seed
for a center
\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
of a solution for
(M,k,r)
if it satisfies:

(C1)

\vec{c}
agrees with
\vec{v}
on all coordinates in
F
; and

(C2)

\vec{c}
differs from
\vec{v}
on at most
t
coordinates
outside of
F
.

We will show next that if
\vec{v}
is an
(F,t)
-seed for
\vec{c}
,
then either
\vec{v}
is the center of a solution for
(M,k,r)
, or
there is a vector
\vec{m}\in M
with
r<\delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\leq 2r
and a subset
C\subset D\setminus F
, where

D=\Delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})
, such that the vector
\vec{v}^{\prime}
obtained
from
\vec{v}
by complementing all coordinates in
C
is an
(F\cup D,t/2)
-seed for
\vec{c}
.

Towards showing the claim, let
\vec{v}
be an
(F,t)
-seed for some center
\vec{c}
of a solution.
Note that if
\vec{v}
is not yet
the
center of a solution, then the solution with center
\vec{c}
must contain a vector

\vec{m}\in M
such that
r<\delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})
and
\delta(\vec{m},\vec{c})\leq r
, and hence,
\delta(\vec{v},\vec{m})\leq 2r
. Let
C
be the subset of coordinates in
D\setminus F
,
on which
\vec{c}
and
\vec{v}
differ, and let
\vec{v}^{\prime}
be the
vector obtained from
\vec{v}
by complementing the values of the
coordinates in
C
. Then
\vec{c}
agrees with
\vec{v}^{\prime}
on all
coordinates in
F\cup D
. Moreover, since
\vec{v}
differs from

\vec{c}
in at most
t
coordinates, it follows that
\vec{v}^{\prime}

differs from
\vec{c}
in at most
t-|C|
coordinates. In addition,

\vec{v}^{\prime}
differs from
\vec{c}
in at most
r-(|D|-|C|)\leq|C|-1

coordinates; this is true since
\vec{c}
differs from
\vec{m}
in at least
|D|-|C|
and at
most
r
coordinates. Therefore,
\vec{v}^{\prime}
differs
from
\vec{c}
in at most
\min\{t-|C|,|C|-1\}\leq t/2
coordinates,
implying that
\vec{v}^{\prime}
is a
(F\cup D,t/2)
-seed for
\vec{c}
.

Now suppose that we are given a vector
\vec{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
that
is an
(F,t)
-seed for the center
\vec{c}
of a solution for

(M,k,r)
, and such that
\vec{v}
itself is not the center of a solution
for
(M,k,r)
. Then, the above claim shows us how to compute in time

\mathcal{O}(|M|d2^{2r})
a set
V^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of
at most
|M|2^{2r}
vectors, that contains a vector
\vec{v}^{\prime}
that is an
(F^{\prime},t/2)
-seed for
\vec{c}
for some
F^{\prime}
satisfying
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
. This
procedure can now be used within a recursive procedure

findCenter
(\vec{v},F,t)
that given

\vec{v}
,
F
, and
t
does the following. It first checks in time

\mathcal{O}(|M|d)
whether
\vec{v}
is already the center of a
solution for
(M,k,r)
. If so, it returns
\vec{v}
; otherwise it
branches on the vectors in
V^{\prime}

and calls itself recursively using the call

findCenter
(\vec{v}^{\prime},F^{\prime},t/2)
for every vector
\vec{v}^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
and
some
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
, and returns the center of a solution returned
by one of those recursive calls, or rejects if all recursive
calls reject. This way
findCenter
(\vec{v},F,t)

is guaranteed to return the center of a solution as long as

\vec{v}
is a
(F,t)
-seed for
\vec{c}
. Moreover, since the recursion depth of
findCenter
(\vec{v},F,t)
is at
most
\log t
, it runs in time
\mathcal{O}(|M|(2^{2r}+d)(|M|2^{2r})^{\log t})=\mathcal{O}((|M|(2^{2r}+d))^{\log t+1})
.

Now suppose that
(M,k,r)
has a solution with center
\vec{c}
. Since the solution contains at least one vector from
M\setminus D_{M}
, there is a vector
\vec{v}^{\prime}\in M\setminus D_{M}
and a completion

\vec{v}
of
\vec{v}^{\prime}
such that
\delta(\vec{v},\vec{c})\leq r
. The vector
\vec{v}
is a
(\emptyset,r)
-seed for
\vec{c}
and
calling
findCenter
(\vec{v},\emptyset,r)
will return a center
for a solution in time
\mathcal{O}((|M|(2^{2r}+d))^{\log t+1})
. Since every
center
\vec{c}
of a solution is within the
r
-neighborhood of
some completed vector in
M\setminus D_{M}
, we can now decide in time
\mathcal{O}(|M|2^{\lambda(M)}(|M|(2^{2r}+d))^{\log t+1})
whether

(M,k,r)
has a solution (and if so return a center) by calling

findCenter
(\vec{v},\emptyset,r)
for every completion
\vec{v}

of every vector
\vec{v}^{\prime}\in M\setminus D_{M}
.
∎

We note that the algorithm provided by
the above theorem generalizes a previous algorithm of Marx
[
27
, Lemma 3.2]
for
Closest Substring

to strings that may contain unknown characters. In particular, it lifts the concept of “generators” to strings with unknown characters by showing that
there are
\log r

vectors that can be computed efficiently and that define at most
r\log r
“important” coordinates for the center of
some solution.

4.3
FPT Approximation Scheme Parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)

In this subsection we give an algorithm that, for a given instance
(M,k,r)
of
Rad-Cluster-Completion
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, where
0<\varepsilon<1
, computes in FPT-time parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

a center of a
Rad
-cluster of size at least
(1-\varepsilon)k
, or it correctly concludes that no RAD-Cluster of size
k
exists.

The algorithm starts by performing a similar branching and pre-processing to the
FPT
algorithm for
Diam-Cluster-Completion
parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)
. Fix
M^{*}
to be a completion of
M
that contains a maximum size RAD-Cluster, let
P^{*}
be such a maximum size RAD-Cluster in
M^{*}
, and let
\vec{s}^{*}
be a center of
P^{*}
. The goal of the algorithm is to find
\vec{s}^{*}
, as given
\vec{s}^{*}
, by Observation
20
, we can decide the instance in time
\mathcal{O}(|M|d)
.

If
k<\frac{2\lambda(M)}{\varepsilon}+2
, then we use the algorithm in Theorem
21
to obtain a Turing kernel; since the vectors in the Turing kernel have their dimension bounded by a function of the parameter, we can enumerate all possible centers of the RAD-Cluster to solve every sub-instance of the Turing kernel. Now we can assume that
k\geq\frac{2\lambda(M)}{\varepsilon}+2
. We first observe that
|P^{*}\setminus D_{M}|
contains at least two vectors (recall that
D_{M}
denotes the
\lambda(M)
-deletion set in
M
).
We now guess two vectors
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
in
M\setminus D_{M}
, together with their respective completions
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{v}^{*}
, such that both
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{v}^{*}
are in
P^{*}
and are the farthest vectors apart in
P^{*}\setminus D_{M}
; fix
r_{\max}=\delta(\vec{v}^{*},\vec{u}^{*})
. Note that
r_{\max}\leq 2r
. Since there are at most
|M|^{2}
pairs of vectors in
M\setminus D_{M}
and each has at most
\lambda(M)
missing entries, there are at most
2^{2\lambda(M)}|M|^{2}
possible pairs of
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{v}^{*}
, and our algorithm enumerates all these pairs. What we describe from now on assumes that the guess of
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{v}^{*}
is correct.
We normalize all the vectors in
M
so that

\vec{v}^{*}
becomes the all-zero vector, i.e., we replace
\vec{v}^{*}
by
the all-zero vector, and for every other vector
\vec{w}\neq\vec{v}^{*}
,
we replace it with the vector
\vec{w}^{\prime}
such that
\vec{w}^{\prime}[i]=0
if

\vec{v}^{*}[i]=\vec{w}[i]
,
\vec{w}^{\prime}[i]={\small\square}
if
\vec{w}[i]={\small\square}
, and
\vec{w}^{\prime}[i]=1
, otherwise.
Finally, for each vector
\vec{w}\in M
, we can in time
r_{\max}\cdot d
, where
d
is the dimension of the vectors in
M
, check if there is a completion
\vec{w}^{*}
of
\vec{w}
such that the distance from
\vec{w}^{*}
to both
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}
is at most
r_{\max}
. This is because we can always set
\vec{w}^{*}[i]=0
for all
i\in[d]\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
such that
\vec{w}^{*}[i]={\small\square}
, since in that case
\vec{v}^{*}[i]=\vec{u}^{*}[i]=0
and we are looking for a completion that is close to both
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}
. Hence, to decide if there is such completion, we only need to decide how many coordinates of
\vec{w}^{*}
in
\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
we need to set to
0
and how many we need to set to
1
. We can now remove all vectors
\vec{w}
that cannot be completed to a vector of distance at most
r_{\max}
from both
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}
. Note here that some vectors in
P^{*}\cap D_{M}
might be further away from
\vec{v}^{*}
or
\vec{u}^{*}
and they could have been removed from
M
at this step.
However, since
k\geq\frac{2\lambda(M)}{\varepsilon}+2
, we have
\lambda(M)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}k
and, if our guess of
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{u}^{*}
is correct, we did not remove any vector from
P^{*}\setminus D_{M}
. Hence, after the preprocessing,
M
contains a RAD-Cluster with center
\vec{s}^{*}
and at least
k^{\prime}=(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k
vectors. Our goal is to find a center for a RAD-Cluster with at least
(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k^{\prime}=(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})^{2}k\geq(1-\varepsilon)k
vectors. For ease of exposition, we let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and we let
k^{\prime}\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k
be the number of vectors of
P^{*}
still in
M
.

Lemma 23
.

After the above pre-processing, in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{r_{\max}}\cdot|M|)
, we can find a center for a RAD-Cluster of size
\frac{|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}}
.

Proof.

The lemma follows rather straightforwardly from the following claim.

Claim 3
.

For every vector
\vec{w}\in M
it holds that
|\Delta(\vec{w})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|\leq r
.

Proof of Claim.

Let
\vec{w}^{*}
be a completion of
\vec{w}
such that
\vec{w}^{*}
is at distance at most
r_{\max}
from both
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{v}^{*}
. Notice that
\Delta(\vec{w})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
; hence
|\Delta(\vec{w})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|\leq|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|
, and it suffices to show that
|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|\leq r
.

Now
\vec{w}^{*}
is at distance at most
r_{\max}
from
\vec{v}^{*}
, and hence we have
\delta(\vec{w}^{*},\vec{v}^{*})\leq r_{\max}
and
\Delta(\vec{v}^{*})=\emptyset
. It follows that
|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|+|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|\leq r_{\max}.
Similarly,
\vec{w}^{*}
is at distance at most
r_{\max}
from
\vec{u}^{*}
, i.e.,
\delta(\vec{w}^{*},\vec{u}^{*})\leq r_{\max}
, and
|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|+|\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})|\leq r_{\max}.
Combining the two inequalities, we get
2|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|+|\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})|+|\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})|\leq 2r_{\max}.
Since
|\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})|+|\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{w})|=|\delta(\vec{v}^{*},\vec{u}^{*})|=r_{\max},
we have
|\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})|\leq\frac{r_{\max}}{2}\leq r.

∎

We can now branch over all possible subsets
T
of
\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
, and for each such subset, try as the center the vector
\vec{s}^{*}_{T}
such that
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{T})=T
and compute the set of all vectors at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{T}
. To prove the lemma, it remains to show that every vector in
M
can be completed to a vector at distance at most
r
from at least one of
2^{r_{\max}}
vectors
\vec{s}^{*}_{T}
.
Let
\vec{w}
be a vector in
M
that can be completed to a vector
\vec{w}^{*}
with
\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})=T
for some
T\subseteq\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
. Now let
\vec{w}^{*}
be such completion of
\vec{w}
that
\Delta(\vec{w}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})=T
and
\vec{w}^{*}[i]=0
for all
i\in[d]\setminus\Delta(\vec{u}^{*})
such that
\vec{w}[i]={\small\square}
. It follows from the claim that
\vec{w}^{*}
is at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{T}
.
∎

Therefore, we can assume henceforth that
k^{\prime}\geq\frac{|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}}
; otherwise, Lemma
23
computes a RAD-Cluster of size at least
k^{\prime}
.
Now the algorithm sets
\vec{s}^{*}_{0}=\vec{v}^{*}=\vec{0}
and the goal is to iteratively compute

\vec{s}^{*}_{1},\vec{s}^{*}_{2},\vec{s}^{*}_{3},\ldots,\vec{s}^{*}_{r^{\prime}}
,
r^{\prime}\leq r
, such that:

1.

for all
i\in[r^{\prime}]
, we have
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})=\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i-1})\cup\{c_{i}\}
for some coordinate
c_{i}\in\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i-1})
;

2.

for all
j\in[r^{\prime}-1]
, the number of vectors at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{j}
is less than
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
; and

3.

the number of vectors at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{r^{\prime}}
is at least
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
.

Let
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
be such that
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
for some
i\in[r^{\prime}-1]
. The number of vectors at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
,
i<r^{\prime}
, is less than
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
. This means that at least
\varepsilon^{\prime}k^{\prime}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}}
vectors whose completions are in
P^{*}
are at distance at least
r+1
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
. For every such vector
\vec{w}
, it is easy to see that since
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
, it must be the case that
(\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})\cap\Delta(\vec{w}))\setminus\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})
is not empty. Note that
|\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})|\leq r
, and hence there exists
c_{i+1}\in\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
such that, for at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
vectors
\vec{w}
in
M
at distance at least
r+1
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
, it holds that
c_{i+1}\in\Delta(\vec{w})
. Moreover, for every vector
\vec{w}\in M
, we have
|\Delta(\vec{w})|\leq r_{\max}
. It follows–by a straightforward counting argument–that there are at most
\frac{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}{\varepsilon}\cdot r_{\max}
coordinates
c\in[d]
such that, for at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
vectors
\vec{w}
, it holds that
c\in\Delta(\vec{w})
. Therefore, to obtain
\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1}
such that
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
, we only need to branch on one of at most
\frac{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}{\varepsilon}\cdot r_{\max}
coordinates. By exhaustively branching on the coordinates that are set to 1 in at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
many vectors in
M
, until either the number of vectors at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
is at least
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
or
i\geq r
, we get:

Theorem 24
.

Given an instance
(M,k,r)
of
Rad-Cluster-Completion
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, where
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists an
FPT

algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, parameterized by
r+\lambda(M)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, such that
\mathcal{A}
either computes a RAD-Cluster of size at least
(1-\varepsilon)k
, or correctly concludes that
M
does not contain a RAD-Cluster of size
k
.

Proof.

The initial phase of the algorithm either computes a RAD-Cluster of size at least
k
if
k<\frac{2\lambda(M)}{\varepsilon}+2
, or branches into at most
2^{2\lambda(M)}|M|^{2}
many branches. Afterwards, the algorithm pre-processes every branch such that in each branch we can either compute a center for a RAD-Cluster of size at least
k^{\prime}\geq k-\lambda(M)\geq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k
in time
\mathcal{O}(2^{r_{\max}}|M|)
by Lemma
23
, or we end up in an instance
(M^{\prime},k^{\prime},r)
such that

k^{\prime}\geq\frac{|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}}
and

|\Delta(\vec{w})|\leq r_{\max}
for all
\vec{w}\in M
.
Moreover, we can assume that there exists a maximum size cluster RAD-Cluster
P^{*}
in
M^{\prime}
with
\vec{0}\in P^{*}
. Note that
k^{\prime}\geq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})k
and a center of RAD-Cluster of size
k^{\prime}
in
M^{\prime}
can be straightforwardly transformed to a center of RAD-Cluster of size
k^{\prime}
in
M
.

From now on the algorithm is rather simple. It starts with
s^{*}_{0}=\vec{0}
. Now for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,r\}
it either concludes that
s^{*}_{i}
is a good enough center or it branches in at most
\frac{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}{\varepsilon}\cdot r_{\max}
possibilities for
s^{*}_{i+1}
with
|\Delta(s^{*}_{i+1})|=i+1
as follows.

1.

It first computes the set
P_{i}
of all vectors in
M
at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
. This can be done in
\mathcal{O}(|M|d)
time, where
d
is the dimension of the vectors in
M
.

2.

If this set has size at least
(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
, it outputs as the center the vector
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
and stops.

3.

Now, if
i=r
and we did not find a good center, then any center containing
\vec{s}^{*}_{r}
would be too far from
\vec{0}
, so the algorithm outputs
Fail
and stops.

4.

Otherwise it branches over all coordinates
c\in[d]\setminus\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})
it computes the set
W\subseteq(M\setminus P_{i})
of all vectors
\vec{w}
with distance at least
r+1
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
and
c\in\Delta(\vec{w})
.

(a)

If
|W|\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
, then the algorithm branches on letting
\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1}
be the vector such that
\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1}[c]=1
and
\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1}[j]=\vec{s}^{*}_{i}[j]
for all
j\neq c_{i}
.

(b)

If
|W|<\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
, discard the current choice of
c
.

The above algorithm is a branching algorithm, where the maximum number of branches at each node is
\frac{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}{\varepsilon}\cdot r_{\max}

and the branching depth is
r
. Otherwise, the algorithm runs in polynomial time in every branching node. Hence the total running time of the algorithm is

(\frac{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}{\varepsilon}\cdot r_{\max})^{r}\cdot|M|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. If the algorithm outputs a vector
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
, then there are at least

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})k^{\prime}
vectors in
M^{\prime}
at distance at most
r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
. On the other hand, if there is RAD-Cluster
P^{*}
of size at
least
k^{\prime}
with center
\vec{s}^{*}
such that
\vec{0}\in P^{*}
, then

\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{0})=\emptyset\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
,

|\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})|\geq r
, and for every vector
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
with

\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
we already argued that there
exists
c_{i+1}\in\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})\setminus\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})
such that for at least
\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}|M|}{2^{r_{\max}}\cdot r}
vectors
\vec{w}
in
M
at distance at least
r+1
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
it holds
c_{i+1}\in\Delta(\vec{w})
. Hence the node with
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
contains a branch with center
\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1}
such that
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i+1})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
. If
\Delta(\vec{s}^{*}_{i})\subseteq\Delta(\vec{s}^{*})
and

, then
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}=\vec{s}^{*}
and there are
k^{\prime}

r
from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
. It follows that if
(M^{\prime},k^{\prime},r)

with at least

from
\vec{s}^{*}_{i}
.

5
Concluding Remarks

We studied the parameterized complexity of two fundamental problems pertaining to incomplete data that have applications in data analytics. In most cases, we were able to provide a complete landscape of the parameterized complexity of the problems w.r.t. the parameters under consideration.
It is worth noting that all algorithmic upper bounds obtained in this paper can also be directly generalized to vectors (i.e., matrices) over a domain whose size is bounded by the parameter value by using the encoding described by Eiben et al.
[
9
]
.

Two important open questions ensue from our work, namely determining the parameterized complexity of
Rad-Cluster-Completion
w.r.t. each of the two parameterizations
k+\lambda
and
r+\lambda
. In particular, the restrictions of these two problems to complete data (i.e.,
\lambda=0
) remain open, and result in two important questions about the parameterized complexity of
Rad-Cluster
parameterized by the cluster size
k
or by the cluster radius
r
.
