Compositional dynamic modelling for causal prediction in multivariate time series
Kevin Li, Graham Tierney †, Christoph Hellmayr ‡ & Mike West #

Abstract




Abstract

Theoretical developments in sequential Bayesian analysis of multivariate dynamic models underlie new methodology for causal prediction. This extends the utility of existing models with computationally efficient methodology, enabling routine exploration of Bayesian counterfactual analyses with multiple selected time series as synthetic controls. Methodological contributions also define the concept of outcome adaptive modelling to monitor and inferentially respond to changes in experimental time series following interventions designed to explore causal effects. The benefits of sequential analyses with time-varying parameter models for causal investigations are inherited in this broader setting. A case study in commercial causal analysis– involving retail revenue outcomes related to marketing interventions– highlights the methodological advances.

Keywords:

Bayesian forecasting, Business revenue forecasting, Counterfactual prediction, Decouple/recouple, Outcome adaptive models, Synthetic controls

Introduction

Developments in time series for causal analysis increasingly emphasize multivariate outcomes and the importance of flexible models for relationships among series. We focus on the use of synthetic controls and counterfactual forecasting, where a set of time series are jointly modelled prior to an intervention and
then partitioned into post-intervention “experimental” and “control” sets. Series identified as controls are assumedly not impacted by the intervention; they thus provide some opportunity to explain post-intervention experimental outcomes by control-experimental relationships unrelated to the intervention. Other aspects of the post-intervention development of experimental series may then partly reflect causal responses. Predicting hypothetical counterfactual outcomes assuming no causal effects provides the basis for assessing this.

Bayesian methods for time series using the synthetic control approach initiated in
Brodersen et al. (2015)
and has been expanded in several directions recently
(
Menchetti and
Bojinov, 2022
;
Papadogeorgou et al., 2023
;
Antonelli and
Beck, 2023
, e.g.)
. Additional related developments have brought dynamic latent factor models into the causal domain
(
Pang
et al., 2022
, e.g.)
though with, of course, major model specification and computational burdens. Some of the challenges to these recent approaches that define our perspective relate to the needs to admit time-varying parameters, especially in patterns of dependencies over time within and between control and experimental series. A related concern is the appropriate evaluation of full uncertainties about counterfactual outcomes that a fully Bayesian analysis can provide. It is also important to focus on– and enable– sequential analysis to highlight the inherently important interests in monitoring for potential causal effects as new data arises. Further, the reliance of some previous methods on computationally intensive methods such as MCMC is a limiting factor for both model development and in terms of scalability to larger numbers of series. The recent introduction to the causal forecasting area of the traditional class of multivariate dynamic linear models (MVDLMs:
Prado
et al., 2021
, chap. 10
) in
Tierney et al. (2024a)
addressed these desiderata in a restricted setting. The restrictions require that synthetic control series be defined as exogenous, used as predictors of both control and counterfactual series pre- and post-intervention. This is very limiting and obviates a main goal here: that of using the full subset of series that are not subject to the intervention as the controls themselves. A model framework that allows this will provide flexibility in understanding– and utilizing– the predictive value of the identified control series without imposing assumptions as to how they relate to the experimental series. This will, of course, require that pre-intervention dependencies
are appropriately characterized.

We address these goals by extending the theory and resulting methodology of the MVDLM framework. A simulated example conveys core ideas, and summaries from a case study in a business application related to retail marketing interventions highlights the methodological advances.

Compositional Dynamic Modelling for Causal Prediction

Counterfactual Setting, Synthetic Controls and Analysis Goals

The setting involves an intervention time point
T
when a subset of the series are identified as experimental,
potentially impacted by the intervention. The assumption is that the remaining series are not impacted, and to be regarded as controls. With no loss of generality write
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{y}_{ct}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{et}^{\prime})
for the partition of the
q
series into the vertical stack of the
q_{c}-
vector
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
and the
q_{e}-
vector
\mathbf{y}_{et}
, where
q_{c}+q_{e}=q.
The notation reflects the causal interests with
c
denoting series that will be designated as
control and
e
experimental when
t\geq T.
We use the same notation and terminology for
t<T.
In the common observational setting, the controls are regarded as synthetic, chosen on the assumption of no impact of the intervention, and no interference (spillover) of causal effects post-intervention.
Pre-intervention modelling analyses all series jointly, characterizing potentially time-varying
cross-series dependencies. Post-intervention, analysis continues in the counterfactual setting where only
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
is observed and predictions of hypothetical unobserved, counterfactual outcomes are made to compare with the actual outcomes.

Specifically, write
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
for latent, counterfactual outcomes for
t\geq T;
this is the hypothetical development of the designated experimental series if the intervention has no effect. Pre-intervention, both
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
and
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}=\mathbf{y}_{et}
are observed, while post-intervention
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
is observed but
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
is “missing data”. Thus
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
is regarded a latent series distinct from the actual post-intervention outcomes on the
e-
series, and no information from the latter is using in model analyses for
t\geq T.

MVDLMs are workhorses of applied time series monitoring and forecasting. They have recently been applied in causal studies in settings where the
\mathbf{F}_{t}
vector has exogenous predictors chosen as synthetic controls
(
Tierney et al., 2024a
;
Tierney et al., 2024b
)
. There, predictor variables are chosen as elements of
\mathbf{F}_{t}
to reflect and partly explain relationships across subsets of series in
\mathbf{y}_{t}
based on data observed prior to the intervention time. Of those, predictors identified as controls are assumed to be unaffected by the intervention; hence, post-intervention, they enable partial correction for cross-series dependencies that are putatively unrelated to the intervention. Then, post-intervention development of the series can be interrogated for additional patterns that may plausibly be identified as causal responses. Important features of the MVDLM analysis– and core to our goals here– are that:

•

cross-series dependencies and regression effects of synthetic controls can change over time;

•

causal effects may change over the post-intervention period, requiring appropriate uncertainty characterization when predicting the counterfactual series;

•

in some applications, interests also concern causal implications for cumulative and/or aggregate effects on the experimental series, and a fully Bayesian multivariate model amenable to direct evaluation of posterior and predictive distributions is needed;

•

analysis is sequential and time-adaptive, so that post-intervention causal inferences can be monitored as control data accrues;

•

analysis is fully conjugate, theoretically precise and computationally scalable.

A main focus of the current paper is to use the full
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
as controls along with any other chosen, exogenous predictors in the
\mathbf{F}_{t}
regression vectors. However, the MVDLM theory breaks down in this setting. That theory requires full observations on all series in
\mathbf{y}_{t}
. In the counterfactual framework, the subvector
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
is unobserved for
t\geq T
so that there is no post-intervention information relevant to inferences on relevant columns of the state matrix

{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}
or for variances and covariances in
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}
related to the experimental series. At time
t=T,
the posterior for
({\bm{\Theta}}_{T},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{T}|\mathcal{D}_{T})
is no longer of the NIW form, and that is then true for all future priors and posteriors as the model evolves. Hence, generalization
of the existing MVDLM analysis is needed.

An Extended Class of Compositional MVDLMs

The compositional form of the MVDLM admits a more general class of conjugate priors. This extended theory
is general but particularly suited to the context of counterfactual modelling for causal prediction– a setting in which different levels of information arise for the
c-
marginal and
e|c-
conditional series where the standard theory breaks down.
The development exploits the conditional decoupling of these two subseries.
The compositional model is that of eqns. (
2

3

3

This is important in extending the theory to allow for different covariance patterns– and their changes over time– for state vectors in the experimental series compared to those in the control series. Some of the essential underlying theory was exploited in simpler versions of MVDLMs by

Causal Inference and Prediction

Counterfactual Dynamic Models and Causal Prediction

In the counterfactual setting of Section
3.1
,
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
is designated as the vector control series and– in our notation so far–
\mathbf{y}_{et}
the experimental. Up to time
t=T
, they are jointly observed and thereafter the latter is “missing”– the hypothetical latent counterfactual series.
The explicit notation reflects this:
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
is the hypothetical counterfactual series and
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
is the actual outcome series. That is,

\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{y}_{ct}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}^{\prime})
for
t<T
and
\mathbf{y}_{t}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{y}_{ct}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}^{\prime})
. In the general theory of Section
3.3
, this
simply relabels the
e
series according to whether and when it is observed or latent. Then,
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
is, of course, actually observed for
t\geq t
; this standard counterfactual framework ignores those outcomes in the model analysis post-intervention.
Investigating (in)consistency of the actually realized outcomes
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
with inferences and predictions from the counterfactual model is the primary causal focus. A compositional model of Section
3.3
applies over all time. The context is consistent with treating the
c,e
series exchangeably prior to the intervention time
T.
The standard MVDLM analysis applies for
t<T
with discount factors

\beta_{e}=\beta,~\delta_{e}=\delta
and a specified NIW prior at
t=0.
For
t\geq T
the evolution/predict/update steps are based on the more general
NIW-CNIW theory of compositional models. In order that resulting counterfactual inferences be related solely to the missingness of
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
, the same model structure and discount factors are maintained post-intervention.

Post-intervention, analysis generates sequentially revised forecast distributions
p(\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}|\mathcal{D}_{t-1})
yielding
1-
step ahead inferences on the
predictive causal effect

\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}-\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}.
This recognizes full uncertainty in the predictive setting. Then,
on observation of
\mathbf{y}_{ct}
, this can be trivially updated to time
t
filtered causal inference: generate a direct Monte Carlo sample for the
posterior predictive distribution of
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}-\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
by draws from
model parameters from the time
t

3.3.4
followed by draws of
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
from the

e-
model component of eqn. (
2
).

In some applications, look-ahead causal predictions are contextually important
(
Tierney et al., 2024a
, e.g.)
. Here the ability to forecast multiple steps ahead– with full predictive distributions that properly characterize uncertainties about the future development of
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t+k}
over
k{=}1,2,\ldots

Outcome Adaptive Dynamic Models and Causal Prediction

In the post-intervention period the actual outcomes
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
are observed. Much of the causal literature generally only uses these outcome, as discussed above, to contrast with the counterfactual predictions. One innovation in the current paper is go further and actively model the post-intervention development of
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
– separately from, and independently of, the counterfactual analysis. The rationale is that understanding actual post-intervention evolution can and will impact inferences and predictions under the non-counterfactual hypothesis, i.e., admitting and allowing for causal intervention effects on the experimental series. The goals are to: (i) define an
outcome adaptive model (OAM)
to run in parallel to, and explicitly independent of, the counterfactual model; (ii) to explore inferences under this OAM relative to the counterfactual model to more properly understand potential causal effects; and (iii) to continually monitor and update the OAM analysis– and update predictions based on it– so as to be able to adapt in case a future decision is made that there have indeed been “changes due to intervention”. Following such a decision, inferences and predictions relative the counterfactual “null hypothesis” can then be accessed. In applications, such a decision will have implications for moving forward with future analysis based on the OAM.

As an example, suppose a marketing intervention has a large initial effect on sales, but that it then begins to gradually decay over time. In the short term, the counterfactual evaluations of incoming data highlight the impact of the intervention, but will then be slow to signal the decay of the effect over later times. An appropriate OAM, in contrast, will more rapidly adapt to the decaying effect, being responsive to– and updated by– the incoming
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
data. This
will provide sequentially updated and practically realistic short-term predictions of the continuing decay.

The OAM defines a parallel model for
\mathbf{y}_{e_{1}t}
in the conditional MVDLM framework, in tandem with the counterfactual model for
\mathbf{y}_{e_{0}t}
and conditionally independent of
\mathbf{y}_{e0t}.
The OAM model states and volatilities evolve in time conditionally independently of those in the counterfactual model. Methodologically, this adds no new questions. The above theory of the sequential filtering and forecasting in the NIW-CNIW framework is simply cloned and used
for series
e_{1}|c
in parallel to that for series
e_{0}|c.
The independence structure means that observations on the treated experimental outcomes
e_{1}
do not at all affect
inferences and predictions in the counterfactual
e_{0}|c
model.

Appropriately treating the
c,e_{0},e_{1}
series as exchangeable prior to the intervention time
T,
the MVDLM analysis applies with discount factors

\beta_{e_{1}}=\beta_{e_{0}}=\beta
and
\delta_{e_{1}}=\delta_{e_{0}}=\delta
. This is precisely as developed for the
c
and
e_{0}|c
series in the counterfactual analysis,
now adding the parallel OAM for
e_{1}|c
. However, a key novelty is that, at the intervention time
T
alone, the OAM discount factors
\beta_{e_{1}},\delta_{e_{1}}
are reduced
to smaller values; this model intervention applies only at the specific time
T
with discount values reverting to the common values
\beta,\delta
thereafter. This is a standard statistical response to the view that the intervention
may have
a causal impact on the
e_{1}
series, and that lower discount factors at that instant will allow for more adaptation in learning under the OAM in the short term
(e.g.
West and
Harrison, 1986
;
West and
Harrison, 1989
;
West and
Harrison, 1997
, chap. 11)
.

Summary Comments

Key aspects of the compositional MVDLM framework include characterising cross-series dependencies among controls and experimental series
pre-intervention, and allowing for such dependencies to change over time. Then, causal effects may change over the post-intervention period, and it is important to properly communicate corresponding counterfactual uncertainties. A fully Bayesian multivariate model that is amenable to direct evaluation of inferences and predictions on primary counterfactual series as well as functions of them is desirable in many applications, as is sequential and time-adaptive analysis for monitoring post-intervention data. We also stress access to theoretically precise and computationally scalable methodology. These goals are addressed in this paper, with methodology that advances the utility of MVDLMs in causal settings. The theoretical development exploits compositional structure and represents an example of a decouple/recouple modelling strategy
(
West, 2020
)
. The methodology defines computationally simple, scalable Bayesian analysis for counterfactual causal inference and prediction using any number of designated series as synthetic controls. Additional developments include the use of the outcome adaptive model for post-intervention monitoring for potential use in resulting decisions about the practical relevance of inferred causal effects.

There are various potential future directions for both applying compositional MVDLMs and for new methodological extensions. Applications in various areas will require specification of series to be used as synthetic controls. In more highly multivariate settings this can build on the example here in using exploratory analysis of pre-intervention data to explore and exploit heterogeneity among series. Then, extension to causal settings where experimental series are enrolled in a study sequentially– the settings of staggered “roll-out”, a.k.a. staggered accrual of experimental series into the intervention study– is one key interest. The structure of compositional MVDLMs is amenable to such extensions, but details and questions of scalability are open for investigation. The MVDLM framework admits many choices of model structure through the
\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{G}_{t}
components that future applications may exploit. Some such choices lead to time-varying vector
autoregressions, workhorses in traditional analysis and forecasting econometrics and allied areas. Such models raise questions about extension to the causal setting. Autoregressions require recent values of some of the series used as predictors of others for the near-term future; the challenge then arises if some of these predictor series become experimental post-intervention in models for the continuing control series. Related
questions arise in settings where there is interest in alternatives to the exchangeable structure of MVDLMs with respect to both the specifications of
(\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{G}_{t})
and to cross-series dependencies. Relaxing these assumptions– such as towards simultaneous graphical
dynamic linear models
(
Gruber and
West, 2016
;
Gruber and
West, 2017
)
– will modify the framework, and open up questions of how the the compositional perspective and theory
for causal prediction will be developed.

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{ec}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}^{-1}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{e}-{\bm{\Sigma}}_{ec}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{ec}^{\prime}.

Distributional summaries:

•

matrix normal;

•

{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}\sim\textrm{IW}(n+q_{c},\mathbf{D}_{e}-\mathbf{D}_{ec}\mathbf{D}_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{ec}^{\prime}).

The
p\times q
matrix

, matrix normal with conformable matrix mean
\mathbf{M}=\textrm{E}({\bm{\Theta}}|{\bm{\Sigma}})\equiv\textrm{E}({\bm{\Theta}})
,

\mathbf{C}
and row

With

A Class of Conditional Normal-Inverse Wishart (CNIW) Distributions

With definitions, notation and partitioning as in Sections
A.1
and
B.2
,
specify additional parameters as follows:

•

a
p\times q
matrix
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{Z}_{c},\mathbf{Z}_{e})
where
\mathbf{Z}_{c}
is
p\times q_{c}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{e}
is
p\times q_{e}
;

•

a
p\times p
s.p.d. matrix
\mathbf{C}_{e}
;

•

a d.o.f.
s_{e}>0
;

•

a
q\times q
s.p.d. matrix
\mathbf{H}
whose partitioned form conformable with that of
{\bm{\Sigma}}

has s.p.d. diagonal blocks
\mathbf{H}_{c},\mathbf{H}_{e}
and lower left off-diagonal block
\mathbf{H}_{ec}
.

The resulting CNIW p.d.f. conditional on any value of
{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}
is defined in the compositional form

p({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})=p({\bm{\Theta}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})p({\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}|{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})p({\bm{\Psi}}_{e})

(9)

with conditional independencies implicit in the notation and independently of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}.

The terms here are:

•

{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{Z}_{e}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e});

•

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}|{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{H}_{ec}\mathbf{H}_{c}^{-1},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e},\mathbf{H}_{c}^{-1})
, matrix normal;

•

{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}\sim\textrm{IW}(s_{e},\mathbf{H}_{e}-\mathbf{H}_{ec}\mathbf{H}_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{ec}^{\prime}).

The notation is
({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})\sim\textrm{CNIW}(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{C}_{e},s_{e},\mathbf{H}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}).

NIW-CNIW Distributions:

Suppose
({\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})\sim\textrm{NIW}(\mathbf{M}_{c},\mathbf{C},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})
with the
p\times q
mean matrix

\mathbf{M}
partitioned conformably with
{\bm{\Sigma}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{M}=(\mathbf{M}_{c},\mathbf{M}_{e})
where
\mathbf{M}_{c}
is
p\times q_{c}
and
\mathbf{M}_{e}
is
p\times q_{e}.

It follows that the specific choices
\mathbf{Z}_{c}=\mathbf{M}_{c}
,
\mathbf{Z}_{e}=\mathbf{M}_{e}
,
\mathbf{C}_{e}=\mathbf{C}
,
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{D}
and
s_{e}=n+q_{c}
reduce the CNIW distribution to the implied distribution (conditional on
{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}
) under the NIW. That is, the implied joint distribution for
({\bm{\Theta}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
is precisely
\textrm{NIW}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{C},n,\mathbf{D}).

More generally, other choices of the CNIW parameters provide more flexibility in representing information and uncertainty about the components of
({\bm{\Theta}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
in the above partitioned forms; this allows a focus on potentially different information for the components. In general,
the resulting joint distribution over components is no longer NIW; it is referred to as a member of the class of NIW-CNIW distributions; the p.d.f. is

p({\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})p({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})
where the first term is NIW and the second the
conditional p.d.f. of eqn. (
9
).

Prior-to-Posterior Updating in MVDLMs with NIW-CNIW Priors

It turns out that the class of NIW-CNIW distributions defines conjugate priors for likelihood functions arising in MVDLMs as in of eqn. (
1
),
substantially extending the existing NIW theory. The theory is presented here in general, at one point in time and ignoring the time subscript for clarity in focusing on the basics of of prior-to-posterior updating.

The observation equation is

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}+{\bm{\nu}}^{\prime}
with
{\bm{\nu}}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{0},{\bm{\Sigma}}).
Partition
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime})^{\prime}
conformably with the partitioned
{\bm{\Sigma}}
above,
so that
\mathbf{y}_{c}
is
q_{c}\times 1
and
\mathbf{y}_{e}
is
q_{e}\times 1.
Then, standard conditional normal theory provides the p.d.f.
p(\mathbf{y}|{\bm{\Theta}},{\bm{\Sigma}})
in the compositional form

p(\mathbf{y}|{\bm{\Theta}},{\bm{\Sigma}})=p(\mathbf{y}_{c}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})p(\mathbf{y}_{e}|\mathbf{y}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})

(10)

with components

•

(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})
,

•

(\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}|\mathbf{y}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}+(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}).

Suppose now the prior for states and components of variance matrix parameters has the NIW-CNIW form. It turns out that the posterior conditional on
\mathbf{y}
is also NIW-CNIW.

Theorem 1.

Under the above model, assume the defined NIW-CNIW prior

•

({\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c})\sim\textrm{NIW}(\mathbf{M}_{c}^{*},\mathbf{C}^{*},n_{c}^{*},\mathbf{D}_{c}^{*})
,

•

({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})\sim\textrm{CNIW}(\mathbf{Z}^{*},\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*},s_{e}^{*},\mathbf{H}^{*}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})
independently of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}.

Here all prior parameters superscripted
*
are known. Then, the posterior is also NIW-CNIW, namely

•

({\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}|\mathbf{y})\sim\textrm{NIW}(\mathbf{M}_{c},\mathbf{C}_{c},n_{c},\mathbf{D}_{c})
,

•

({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|\mathbf{y},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})\sim\textrm{CNIW}(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{C}_{e},s_{e},\mathbf{H}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})
independently of
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{c}.

The proof, with details of the evaluation of the posterior parameters, follows.

Marginal NIW Model

The
c-
marginal model of eqn. (
10
) for data
\mathbf{y}_{c}
alone, coupled with the marginal NIW prior on
({\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Psi}}_{c})
, defines a traditional matrix normal, inverse Wishart set-up. Hence the posterior is NIW with parameters
(\mathbf{M}_{c},\mathbf{C}_{c},n_{c},\mathbf{D}_{c})
computed from the
usual NIW updating equations, as follows.

Define the
q_{c}-
vector point forecast
\mathbf{f}_{c}
by
\mathbf{f}_{c}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{M}_{c}^{*}
, the resulting forecast error vector

\textrm{e}_{c}=\mathbf{y}_{c}-\mathbf{f}_{c}
, and the
p-
vector of adaptive coefficients
\mathbf{A}_{c}=\mathbf{C}_{c}^{*}\mathbf{F}/v_{c}
where
v_{c}=1+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{c}^{*}\mathbf{F}
.
The posterior NIW parameters are then

\mathbf{M}_{c}=\mathbf{M}_{c}^{*}+\mathbf{A}_{c}\textrm{e}_{c}^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{C}_{c}=\mathbf{C}_{c}^{*}-\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\prime}v_{c}
,
n_{c}=n_{c}^{*}+1
and
\mathbf{D}_{c}=\mathbf{D}_{c}^{*}+\textrm{e}_{c}\textrm{e}_{c}^{\prime}/v_{c}.

CNIW Model

The
e|c-
conditional model of eqn. (
10
) for data
\mathbf{y}_{e}
alone– and assuming known values of

(\mathbf{y}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c})
– defines a conditional likelihood to update the specified CNIW prior for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}.

The observation model p.d.f.

p(\mathbf{y}_{e}|\mathbf{y}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c},{\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})
defines the conditional likelihood function for
({\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}).

Define the
q-
vector
\mathbf{z}
via
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}^{*}
and the
p-
vector

\mathbf{A}_{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}\mathbf{F}/v_{e}
with
v_{e}=1+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}\mathbf{F}.

Then the posterior CNIW parameters are given by

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Z}^{*}+\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
,

Proof of CNIW Updating.

Two steps identify (a) the conditional
posterior for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}
given
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}
, and (b) the marginal posterior for
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}.

These are, of course, conditional on
\mathbf{y},{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}
and all prior parameters.

(a) Conditional for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}
.

Define
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{e}
via
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{e}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}-(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}
. The conditional likelihood function for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}
has the form of a matrix normal arising from the dynamic regression

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{e}^{\prime}|{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})
with “synthetic” outcome
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{e}^{\prime}
. The conditional prior

{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}\sim\textrm{N}(\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})
is conjugate to this likelihood function hence the posterior is matrix normal. The parameter updates follow the general matrix normal theory used in MVDLMs, with results as follows.

Define the synthetic forecast vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}_{e}
and corresponding forecast error

\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}
via
\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}_{e}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\{\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}\}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}=\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{e}-\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}_{e}.

The standard theory shows that the conditional posterior for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}
is

\textrm{N}(\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}_{e}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e})

(11)

where
\mathbf{C}_{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}-\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{A}_{e}^{\prime}v_{e}
with
\mathbf{A}_{e}=\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}\mathbf{F}/v_{e}
and
v_{e}=1+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}\mathbf{F}.

Now,

\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}^{\prime}=\{\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}-(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}\}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\{\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}\}=\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}

where
\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}
and
\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}
.
Importantly, the terms in
{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}
cancel out in this expression for
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}^{\prime}.
Substituting for
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}^{\prime}
in
the posterior mean in eqn. (
11
) leads to the mean expressed as

\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}_{e}(\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime})=\mathbf{Z}_{e}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}

where
\mathbf{Z}_{e}=\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{c}=\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}+\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}
.
Hence the posterior mean has the CNIW form
\mathbf{Z}_{e}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}
where
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{Z}_{c},\mathbf{Z}_{e})
is
given by
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Z}^{*}+\mathbf{A}_{e}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
with
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime})=\mathbf{y}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}^{*}
.

(a) Marginal for
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}
.

Consider now the distribution of
\mathbf{y}_{e}
marginalized over the conditional matrix normal for
{\bm{\Theta}}_{e}
.
This is normal with mean vector for
\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}
given by

\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\{\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+({\bm{\Theta}}_{c}-\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}\}+(\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}){\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}+\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}

with
\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{c}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{c}^{*}
as earlier defined. Importantly, this mean vector for
\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}
does not now involve
{\bm{\Theta}}_{c}
. The corresponding
variance matrix is
v_{e}{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}
with scalar
v_{e}=1+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{C}_{e}^{*}\mathbf{F}
also earlier defined. As all quantities here are known apart from

({\bm{\Gamma}}_{e},{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}),
this normal p.d.f. results in a likelihood function for these parameters that has a matrix normal, inverse Wishart form. This likelihood function is easily seen to reduce to

|{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|^{-1/2}\textrm{etr}\{-{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}^{-1}({\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{c}\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}-{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{c}\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}+\mathbf{z}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime})/(2v_{e})\}

with
\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{e}^{\prime}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{e}^{*}
also as earlier defined. The NIW prior
has p.d.f. proportional to

|{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}|^{-(q_{e}+s_{e}^{*}/2)}\textrm{etr}\{-{\bm{\Psi}}_{e}^{-1}({\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}\mathbf{H}_{c}^{*}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}-{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}\mathbf{H}_{ec}^{*^{\prime}}-\mathbf{H}_{ec}^{*}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{e}^{\prime}+\mathbf{H}_{e}^{*})/2\}

hence the product of the likelihood and prior has the NIW form with updated d.o.f.

s_{e}=s_{e}^{*}+1,
and updated
q\times q
s.p.d. matrix
\mathbf{H}
whose diagonal blocks are
\mathbf{H}_{c}=\mathbf{H}_{c}^{*}+\mathbf{z}_{c}\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}/v_{e}
and

\mathbf{H}_{e}=\mathbf{H}_{e}^{*}+\mathbf{z}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime}/v_{e}
and with
lower left off-diagonal block
\mathbf{H}_{ec}=\mathbf{H}_{ec}^{*}+\mathbf{z}_{e}\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime}/v_{e}
. This can be simply written as
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}^{*}+\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}/v_{e}

where
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}_{e}^{\prime})
as earlier defined.

This completes the proof and delivers simple updating formulæ in the CNIW setting.
