The Difference λ \lambda -calculus: A Language for Difference Categories
Mario Alvarez-Picallo, C.-H. Luke Ong

Abstract
. Cartesian difference categories are a recent generalisation of Cartesian differential categories which introduce a notion of “infinitesimal” arrows satisfying an analogue of the Kock-Lawvere axiom, with the axioms of a Cartesian differential category being satisfied only “up to an infinitesimal perturbation”. In this work, we construct a simply-typed calculus in the spirit of the differential λ \lambda -calculus equipped with syntactic “infinitesimals” and show how its models correspond to difference λ \lambda -categories, a family of Cartesian difference categories equipped with suitably well-behaved exponentials.

1.
Introduction

A recent series of works introduced the concept of change actions and
differential maps between them
[
5
,
4
]
in
order to study settings equipped with derivative-like operations. Although the
motivating example was the eminently practical field of incremental computation,
similar structures appear in more abstract settings such as the calculus of
finite differences and Cartesian differential categories.

Of particular interest are Cartesian difference categories

[
2
]
, a well-behaved class of change action models

[
4
]
that are much closer to the strong axioms of a
Cartesian differential category
[
6
]
while remaining
general enough for interpreting discrete calculus. A Cartesian difference
category is a left additive category equipped with an “infinitesimal
extension”, an operation that sends an arrow
f
to an arrow
\varepsilon(f)

which should be understood as
f
being multiplied by an “infinitesimal”
element – infinitesimal in the sense that it verifies the Kock-Lawvere axiom
from synthetic differential geometry (we refer the reader to

[
12
]
for an introduction to SDG).

The interest of Cartesian differential categories is in part motivated by the
fact that they provide models for the differential
\lambda
-calculus

[
11
,
9
]
, which extends the

\lambda
-calculus with linear combinations of terms and an operator that
differentiates arbitrary
\lambda
-abstractions. The claim that differentiation
in the differential
\lambda
-calculus corresponds to the standard, “analytic”
notion is then justified by its interpretation in (a well-behaved class of)
Cartesian differential categories

[
7
,
14
]
.

It is reasonable to ask, then, whether there is a similar calculus that captures
the behavior of derivatives in difference categories – especially since, as it
has been shown, these subsume differential categories. The issue is far from
trivial, as many of the properties of the differential
\lambda
-calculus
crucially hinge on derivatives being linear. Through this work we provide an
affirmative answer to this question by defining untyped and simply-typed
variants for a simple calculus which extends the differential
\lambda
-calculus
with a notion of derivative more suitable to the Cartesian difference setting.

2.
Cartesian Difference Categories

The theory of Cartesian difference categories is developed and discussed at

[

{defi}

A
Cartesian left additive category
(
[
6
, Definition
1.1.1.]
)
\mathbf{C}
is a Cartesian category where every
hom-set
{\mathbf{C}}\left[{A},{B}\right]
is endowed with the structure of a
commutative monoid
({\mathbf{C}}\left[{A},{B}\right],+,0)
such that
0\circ f=0
,

(f+g)\circ h=(f\circ h)+(g\circ h)
and
\left\langle{f_{1}},{f_{2}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{g_{1}},{g_{2}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f_{1}+g_{1}},{f_{2}+g_{2}}\right\rangle

An
infinitesimal extension
(
[
2
, Definition

a choice of a monoid homomorphism

\mathbf{C}

\varepsilon(f+g)=\varepsilon(f)+\varepsilon(g)
and
\varepsilon(0)=0

require that
\varepsilon
be compatible with the Cartesian structure, in the
sense that
\varepsilon(\left\langle{f},{g}\right\rangle)=\left\langle{\varepsilon(f)},{\varepsilon(g)}\right\rangle

{defi}

A
Cartesian difference category
(
[
2

,ref=

(0)

(2)

\partial\left[{\mathbf{id}_{A}}\right]=\pi_{2}

3.
Difference
\lambda
-Categories

In order to give a semantics for the differential
\lambda
-calculus, it does
not suffice to ask for a Cartesian differential category equipped with
exponentials – the exponential structure has to be compatible with both the
additive and the differential structure, in the sense of

[
7
, Definition 4.4]
. For difference categories we
will require an identical equation, together with a condition requiring
higher-order functions to respect the infinitesimal extension.

{defi}

We remind the reader that a Cartesian left additive category is

Cartesian closed left additive

(
[
7
, Definition 4.2]
) whenever it is Cartesian
closed and satisfies
\Lambda(f+g)=\Lambda(f)+\Lambda(g),\Lambda(0)=0
.

A Cartesian difference category
\mathbf{C}
is a
difference

\lambda
-category
if it Cartesian closed left additive and satisfies the
following additional axioms:

,ref=
[
\partial\lambda
.0]
,align=left]

(1)

\partial\left[{\Lambda(f)}\right]=\Lambda\left(\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ\left\langle{\left(\pi_{1}\times\mathbf{id}\right)},{\left(\pi_{2}\times 0\right)}\right\rangle\right)

(2)

\Lambda(\varepsilon(f))=\varepsilon\left(\Lambda(f)\right)

Equivalently, let
\mathbf{sw}
denote the map

\left\langle{\left\langle{\pi_{11}},{\pi_{2}}\right\rangle},{\pi_{21}}\right\rangle:(A\times B)\times C\to(A\times C)\times B
.
Then the condition
1
can be written in terms of
\mathbf{sw}
as:

\partial\left[{\Lambda(f)}\right]\coloneqq\Lambda\left(\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ\left(\mathbf{id}\times\left\langle{\mathbf{id}},{0}\right\rangle\right)\circ\mathbf{sw}\right)

Axiom
1
is identical to its differential analogue
[
7
, Definition
4.4]
, and it follows the same broad intuition. Given
a map
f:A\times B\to C
, we usually understand the composite
\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ(\mathbf{id}_{A\times B}\times(\mathbf{id}_{A}\times 0_{B})):(A\times B)\times A\to C
as a
partial derivative of
f
with respect to its first argument. Hence, just as it
was with differential
\lambda
-categories, axiom
1
states that the
derivative of a curried function is precisely the derivative of the uncurried
function with respect to its first argument.

{exa}

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a differential
\lambda
-category. Then the trivial Cartesian
difference category obtained by setting
\varepsilon(f)=0
(as in

[
2

) is a difference

\lambda
-category. Furthermore, the Kleisli category
\mathbf{C}_{\mathsf{T}}

induced by its tangent bundle monad (as in
[
2
, Proposition
6]
) is also a difference
\lambda
-category.

{exa}

The category
\overline{\mathbf{{Ab}}}
(
[
2

5.2]
), which has Abelian groups as objects and arbitrary
functions between their carrier sets as morphisms, is a difference

\lambda

\varepsilon(f)=f
and
difference combinator
\partial\left[{f}\right](x,u)=f(x+u)-f(x)
. Given
groups
G,H

G\Rightarrow H
is the set of (set-theoretic)
functions from
G
into
H
, endowed with the group structure of
H
lifted
pointwise (that is,
(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)
). Evidently the exponential
respects the monoidal structure and the infinitesimal extension. We check that
it also verifies axiom
1

\displaystyle\partial\left[{\Lambda(f)}\right](x,u)(y)

\displaystyle=\Lambda(f)(x+u)(y)-\Lambda(f)(x)(y)

\displaystyle=f(x+u,y)-f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\Lambda(\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ(\mathbf{id}\times\left\langle{\mathbf{id}},{0}\right\rangle)\circ\mathbf{sw})(x,u)(y)

A central property of differential
\lambda
-categories is a deep correspondence
between differentiation and the evaluation map. As one would expect, the partial
derivative of the evaluation map gives one a first-class derivative operator
(see, for example,
[
7

, which provides
an interpretation for the differential substitution operator in the differential

\lambda

although its formulation is somewhat more involved.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\mathbf{C}
-morphisms
\Lambda(f):A\to(B\Rightarrow C),e:A\to B
, the
following identities hold:

(2.i)

\partial\left[{\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\Lambda(f)},{e}\right\rangle}\right]=\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\partial\left[{\Lambda(f)}\right]},{e\circ\pi_{1}}\right\rangle+\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ\left\langle{\left\langle{\pi_{1}+\varepsilon(\pi_{2})},{e\circ\pi_{1}}\right\rangle},{\left\langle{0},{\partial\left[{e}\right]}\right\rangle}\right\rangle

(2.ii)

\partial\left[{\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\Lambda(f)},{e}\right\rangle}\right]=\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\partial\left[{\Lambda(f)}\right]},{e\circ\pi_{1}+\varepsilon(\partial\left[{e}\right])}\right\rangle+\partial\left[{f}\right]\circ\left\langle{\left\langle{\pi_{1}},{e\circ\pi_{1}}\right\rangle},{\left\langle{0},{\partial\left[{e}\right]}\right\rangle}\right\rangle

As is the case in differential
\lambda
-categories, we can define a
“differential substitution” operator on the semantic side. This operator
is akin to post-composition with a partial derivative, and can be defined
as follows.

{defi}

Given morphisms
s:A\times B\rightarrow C,u:A\rightarrow B

differential composition

s\star u:A\times B\rightarrow C
by:

s\star u\coloneqq\partial\left[{s}\right]\circ\left\langle{\mathbf{id}_{A\times B}},{\left\langle{0_{A}},{u\circ\pi_{1}}\right\rangle}\right\rangle

We should understand the morphism

as the partial derivative of
s

the following useful properties (proofs of which can be found in
[

Lemma 3

Let

(3.i)

\left(\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\Lambda(f)},{e}\right\rangle\right)\star g=\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\Lambda(f\star(e\star g))},{e}\right\rangle+\mathbf{ev}\circ\left\langle{\Lambda(f)\star g},{e\circ\left\langle{\pi_{1}},{\pi_{2}+\varepsilon(g)\circ\pi_{1}}\right\rangle}\right\rangle

\Lambda(f\star e)\star g=\Lambda\Big[\Lambda^{-}(\Lambda(f)\star g)\star(e\circ(\mathbf{id}+\left\langle{0},{\varepsilon(g)}\right\rangle)))+\varepsilon(f\star e)\star(e\star g)+(f\star(e\star g))\Big]

\Lambda(f\star e)\circ\left\langle{\pi_{1}},{g^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\Lambda(\Lambda^{-}(\Lambda(f)\circ\left\langle{\pi_{1}},{g^{\prime}}\right\rangle)\star(e\circ\left\langle{\pi_{1}},{g^{\prime}}\right\rangle))

4.
An Untyped Calculus of Differences

We proceed in a manner similar to Vaux
[
16
]
in his treatment
of the algebraic
\lambda
-calculus; that is, we will first define a set of
“unrestricted” terms
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
which we will later consider up to an
equivalence relation arising from the theory of difference categories.

{defi}

The set
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
of
unrestricted terms
of
the
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
-calculus is given by the following inductive
definition (assuming, as is usual, a countably infinite set of distinct variables

x,y,z,\ldots
):

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\text{Terms: }&s,t,e&\coloneqq&x\ |\ \lambda x.t\ |\ (s\ t)\ |\ \text{D}(s)\cdot t\ |\ \varepsilon t\ |\ s+t\ |\ 0\end{array}

Since the only binder in the
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
-calculus
is the usual
\lambda
-abstraction, the definition of free and bound variables
is straightforward.

{defi}

The set of
free variables

\text{FV}(t)
of a term
t\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
is defined by induction on the structure of
t
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\text{FV}(x)&\coloneqq&\{x\}\\
\text{FV}(\lambda x.t)&\coloneqq&\text{FV}(t)\setminus\{x\}\\
\text{FV}(s\ t)&\coloneqq&\text{FV}(s)\cup\text{FV}(t)\\
\text{FV}(\text{D}(s)\cdot t)&\coloneqq&\text{FV}(s)\cup\text{FV}(t)\\
\text{FV}(\varepsilon t)&\coloneqq&\text{FV}(t)\\
\text{FV}(s+t)&\coloneqq&\text{FV}(s)\cup\text{FV}(t)\\
\text{FV}(0)&\coloneqq&\emptyset\\
\end{array}

As usual, a variable
x
is
free
in
t
whenever
x\in\text{FV}(t)
.
An occurrence of a variable
x
in some term
t
is said to be
bound

whenever it appears in some subterm
t^{\prime}
of
t
with
x\not\in\text{FV}(t)
.
Two terms are said to be
\alpha
-equivalent if they are identical up to a
renaming of all their bound variables.

In what follows, we will speak of terms only up to
\alpha
-equivalence. That
is, we consider the terms
\lambda x.x
and
\lambda y.y
to be identical
for all intents and purposes. Since this means we can rename bound variables
freely, we will assume by convention that all bound variables appearing in any
term
t\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
are different from its free variables.

4.1.
Differential Equivalence

Further to
\alpha
-equivalence, we introduce here the notion of differential
equivalence of terms. The role of this relation is, as in

[
15
]
, to enforce that the elementary algebraic properties of
sums and actions are preserved. For example, we wish to treat the terms
\lambda x.(0+\varepsilon(s+t))
and
(\lambda x.\varepsilon t)+(\lambda x.\varepsilon s)
as if they were equivalent (as it will be the case in the models).
This equivalence relation also has the role of ensuring that the axioms of a
Cartesian difference category are verified, especially regularity of
derivatives.

{defi}

A binary relation
\sim{}\subseteq\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\times\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
is
contextual
whenever it satisfies the conditions
in Figure
1

\begin{array}[]{ccc}t\sim t^{\prime}&\Rightarrow&\lambda x.t\sim\lambda x.t^{\prime}\\
t\sim t^{\prime}&\Rightarrow&\varepsilon t\sim\varepsilon t^{\prime}\\
s\sim s^{\prime}\wedge t\sim t^{\prime}&\Rightarrow&s\ t\sim s^{\prime}\ t^{\prime}\\
s\sim s^{\prime}\wedge t\sim t^{\prime}&\Rightarrow&\text{D}(s)\cdot t\sim\text{D}(s^{\prime})\cdot t^{\prime}\\
s\sim s^{\prime}\wedge t\sim t^{\prime}&\Rightarrow&s+t\sim s^{\prime}+t^{\prime}\\
\end{array}

The above conditions can be separated in a number of conceptually distinct
groups corresponding to their purpose. These are as follows:

•

The first block of equations simply states that
+,0
define a
commutative monoid and that
\varepsilon
defines a monoid homomorphism.

•

The second block of equations amounts to stating that the monoid and
infinitesimal extension structure on functions is pointwise.

•

The third block of equations implies (and is equivalent to) stating that
addition and infinitesimal extension are “linear”, in the sense that they
are equal to their own derivatives (that is,
\partial\left[{+}\right]=+\circ\pi_{2}
and

\partial\left[{\varepsilon}\right]=\varepsilon
).

•

The fourth block of equations states structural properties of the
derivative, such as the derivative conditions and the commutativity of second
derivatives. Similar equations are also present in the differential

\lambda
-calculus, where they merely state that the derivative is additive
(as opposed to regular).

Most of these equations correspond directly to properties of Cartesian
difference categories, with the only exception being the requirement that
\text{D}(s)\cdot(\varepsilon t)\sim_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon(\text{D}(s)\cdot t)
and the “duplication
of infinitesimals” in
\varepsilon\text{D}(\text{D}(s)\cdot t)\cdot e=\varepsilon^{2}\text{D}(\text{D}(s)\cdot t)\cdot e
. To understand the logic of the first equation, consider
that the term
\text{D}(\lambda x.s)\cdot\varepsilon t
should roughly correspond to

\partial\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,\varepsilon{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket})
which expands to:

\displaystyle\partial\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,\varepsilon{{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket}})

\displaystyle=\partial\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,0+\varepsilon{{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket}})

\displaystyle=\partial\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,0)+\varepsilon\partial^{2}\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,0,0,{{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket}})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\partial^{2}\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,0,0,{{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket}})

In the particular setting of Cartesian difference categories, the last term
boils down to
\varepsilon\partial\left[{{\left\llbracket{s}\right\rrbracket}}\right](x,{\left\llbracket{t}\right\rrbracket})
by axiom
6
, hence the
equality
\text{D}(s)\cdot\varepsilon t\sim_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon(\text{D}(s)\cdot t)
is justified,
allowing infinitesimal extensions to be “pulled out” of differential
application. The duplication of infinitesimals is simply a syntactic version of
Lemma
1.ii
.

{defi}

The set
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of
well-formed terms
, or simply

terms
, of the
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
-calculus is defined as the quotient
set
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\Lambda_{\varepsilon}/\sim_{\varepsilon}
. Whenever
t
is an
unrestricted term, we write
\underline{t}
to refer to the well-formed term
represented by
t
, that is to say, the
\sim_{\varepsilon}
-equivalence class of
t
.

The notion of differential equivalence allows us to ensure that our calculus
reflects the laws of the underlying models, but has the unintended
consequence that our
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
-terms are equivalence classes, rather
than purely syntactic objects. We will proceed by defining a notion of canonical
form of a term and a canonicalization algorithm which explicitly constructs
the canonical form of any given term, thus proving that
\sim_{\varepsilon}
is decidable.

{defi}

We define the sets
\text{B}_{\varepsilon}\subset\text{B}_{\varepsilon}^{+}\subset\text{B}_{\varepsilon}^{*}\subset\text{C}_{\varepsilon}^{+}\subset\text{C}_{\varepsilon}(\subset\Lambda_{\varepsilon})
of
basic
,

positive
,
additive
,
positive canonical
and

canonical
terms according to the following grammar:

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\text{Basic terms: }&s^{\textbf{b}},t^{\textbf{b}},e^{\textbf{b}}\in\text{B}_{\varepsilon}&\coloneqq&x\ |\ \lambda x.t^{\textbf{b}}\ |\ (s^{\textbf{b}}\ t^{*})\ |\ \text{D}(s^{\textbf{b}})\cdot t^{\textbf{b}}\\
\text{Positive terms: }&s^{+},t^{+},e^{+}\in\text{B}_{\varepsilon}^{+}&\coloneqq&s^{\textbf{b}}\ |\ s^{\textbf{b}}+(t^{+})\\
\text{Additive terms: }&s^{*},t^{*},e^{*}\in\text{B}_{\varepsilon}^{*}&\coloneqq&0\ |\ s^{+}\\
\text{Positive canonical terms: }&S^{+},T^{+}\in\text{C}_{\varepsilon}&\coloneqq&\varepsilon^{k}s^{\textbf{b}}\ |\ \varepsilon^{k}s^{\textbf{b}}+(S^{+})\\
\text{Canonical terms: }&S,T\in\text{C}_{\varepsilon}&\coloneqq&0\ |\ S^{+}\end{array}

We will sometimes abuse the notation and write
\underline{t^{*}}
or
\underline{t^{\textbf{b}}}
to
denote well-formed terms whose canonical form is an additive or basic term
respectively.

The above definition is somewhat technical, so a more informal description of
canonical forms should be helpful. We observe that all the rules of differential
equivalence can be oriented from left to right to obtain a rewrite system -
intuitively, this rewrite system operates by pulling all the instances of
addition and infinitesimal extension to the outermost layers of a term. Since
every syntactic construct is additive except for application, basic terms may only
contain additive terms as the arguments to a function application.

As infinitesimal extensions are themselves additive, we also want to disallow
terms such as
\varepsilon(s+t)
, instead factoring out the extension
into
\varepsilon s+\varepsilon t)
. A general canonical term

then has
the form:

T=\varepsilon^{k_{1}}t^{\textbf{b}}_{1}+(\varepsilon^{k_{2}}t_{2}+(\ldots+\varepsilon^{k_{n}}t^{\textbf{b}}_{n})\ldots)

That is to say, a canonical term is similar to a polynomial with coefficients in
the set of basic terms and a variable
\varepsilon
(but note that canonical terms
are always written in their “fully distributed” form, that is, we write

rather than
\varepsilon((s+t)+\varepsilon e)
).

We will freely abuse the notation and write
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{k_{i}}t^{\textbf{b}}_{i}

denote a general canonical term, as this form is easier to manipulate in many
cases. In particular, the canonical term

is precisely the sum of zero terms.

\lambda_{\varepsilon}

With the substitution operations we have introduced so far, we can now
proceed to give a small-step operational semantics as a reduction system.

{defi}

The

\leadsto{}\subseteq\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\times\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
is the least contextual relation satisfying the

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(\lambda x.t)\ s&\leadsto_{\beta}&{t}\left[{s}/{x}\right]\\

\end{array}

Figure 6.

We write
\leadsto^{+}
to denote the transitive closure of
\leadsto

and
\leadsto^{*}

While the one-step reduction rules for
\lambda_{\varepsilon}

they are in fact not equivalent, as our notion of differential substitution diverges substantially.

The above one-step reduction is defined as a relation from unrestricted terms to
unrestricted terms, but it is not compatible with differential equivalence. That
is to say, there may be differentially equivalent terms
t\sim_{\varepsilon}t^{\prime}
such that

t^{\prime}
can be reduced but

cannot. For example, consider the term
(\lambda x.x+0)\ 0

\beta

is, however, equivalent to

.

We could lift the one-step reduction relation to well-formed terms by setting

\underline{t}\leadsto\underline{t^{\prime}}
whenever there exist
s,s^{\prime}
such that
t\sim_{\varepsilon}s,t^{\prime}\sim_{\varepsilon}s^{\prime}
and
s\leadsto s^{\prime}
. This is not very satisfactory,
however, as it would make one-step reduction undecidable. Indeed, in order to
check whether
\underline{s}\leadsto\underline{s^{\prime}}
it would be necessary to check
whether
s\leadsto s^{\prime}
for all their (infinitely many) representatives

Another problem with this definition lies in the fact that the term
\underline{0}

(ostensibly a value which should not reduce) can also be written as
\underline{0\ t}

for any term

in one step, then according to
the previous definition so does
\underline{0\ t}

\underline{0\ t^{\prime}}
, which is
equivalent to
\underline{0}
. Hence zero reduces to itself, rather than being a normal

Fortunately the canonical form of a term

It is a well-known result that the differential

{defi}

{\left\llbracket{\cdot}\right\rrbracket}\coloneqq\mathbf{1}

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

We have defined here the difference
\lambda
-calculus, which generalises the
differential
\lambda
-calculus in exactly the same manner as Cartesian
difference categories generalise their differential counterpart. While this
calculus is of theoretical interest, it lacks most practical features, such as
iteration or conditionals, and it is not immediately obvious how to extend it
with these. It is not clear, for example, precisely when iteration combinators
are differentiable in the difference category sense.

The problem of iteration is closely related to integration, which is itself the
focus of current work on the differential side

[
8
,
13
]
. Indeed, consider a hypothetical
extension of the difference
\lambda
-calculus equipped with a type of natural
numbers (with the identity as its corresponding infinitesimal extension, that is
to say,
\varepsilon_{\mathbb{N}}=\mathbf{id}_{\mathbb{N}}
). How should an iteration operator

iter
be defined? The straightforward option would be to give it the
usual behavior, that is to say:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\textbf{iter}\ \mathbf{Z}\ z\ s&\leadsto&z\\
\textbf{iter}\ (\mathbf{S}\ n)\ z\ s&\leadsto&s\ (\textbf{iter}\ n\ z\ s)\end{array}

These reduction rules entail that every object involved must be complete, that
is to say, for every
s,t:A
, there is some
u:A
with
s\mathrel{+}{}\varepsilon(u)=t
– such an element is given by the term
((\text{D}(\lambda n.\textbf{iter}\ n\ s\ (\lambda x.t))\cdot(\mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{Z}))\ \mathbf{Z})
.

This would rule out a number of interesting models and so it seems
unsatisfactory. An alternative is to define the iteration operator by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\textbf{iter}\ \mathbf{Z}\ z\ s&\leadsto&z\\
\textbf{iter}\ (\mathbf{S}\ n)\ z\ s&\leadsto&(\textbf{iter}\ n\ z\ s)+\varepsilon(s\ (\textbf{iter}\ n\ z\ s))\end{array}

Fixed
z,s
, and defining the map
\mu(n)\coloneqq\textbf{iter}\ n\ z\ s
, its
derivative
\text{D}[\mu](n,\mathbf{S}\ \mathbf{Z})
is precisely
s(\mu(n))
. Or, in other
words, the function
\mu:\mathbb{N}\to A
is a “curve” which starts at
z

and whose derivative at a given point
n
is
s(\mu(n))
– this boils down to
stating that the curve
\mu
is an integral curve for the vector field
s

satisfying the initial condition
\mu(\mathbf{Z})=z
! Hence it may be possible to
understand iteration as a discrete counterpart of the Picard-Lindelöf theorem,
which states that such integral curves always exist (locally).

It would be of great interest to extend
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
with an interation
operator and give its semantics in terms of differential (or difference)
equations. Studying recurrence equations using the language of differential
equations is a very useful tool in discrete analysis; for example, one can treat
the recursive definition of the Fibonacci sequence as a discrete ODE and use
differential equation methods to find a closed-form solution. We believe that in
a language which frames iteration in such terms may be amenable to optimisation
by similar analytic methods.
