Abstract
Granger causality has been employed to investigate causality relations between components of stationary multiple time series. We generalize this concept by developing statistical inference for local Granger causality for multivariate locally stationary processes. Our proposed local Granger causality approach captures time-evolving causality relationships in nonstationary processes. The proposed local Granger causality is well represented in the frequency domain and estimated based on the parametric time-varying spectral density matrix using the local Whittle likelihood. Under regularity conditions, we demonstrate that the estimators converge to multivariate normal in distribution. Additionally, the test statistic for the local Granger causality is shown to be asymptotically distributed as a quadratic form of a multivariate normal distribution. The finite sample performance is confirmed with several simulation studies for multivariate time-varying autoregressive models. For practical demonstration, the proposed local Granger causality method uncovered new functional connectivity relationships between channels in brain signals. Moreover, the method was able to identify structural changes in financial data.



Keywords:

local Granger causality,
multivariate locally stationary processes,
time-varying spectral density matrix,
local Whittle likelihood, brain signals.

1
Introduction

Statistical inference for cause and effect remains at the forefront of many
studies including biology, medicine, physical systems, environmental science,
public health, policy and finance. However, there remain challenges on
inference because causality is notoriously difficult to establish.
Granger causality, proposed in
Granger 1963
and
Granger 1969
,
is a milestone of causal inference in dynamic models.
In broad terms, Granger causality from a time series
\{Y_{t}\}
to
another series
\{X_{t}\}
measures the predictive ability from the series
\{Y_{t}\}
to
\{X_{t}\}
.
If the predictive ability of
(X_{s},Y_{s})_{s<t}
on
{X_{t}}
is not different from the predictive ability of
(X_{s})_{s<t}
on
{X_{t}}
, then there is
“no Granger causal relationship”
from the series
\{Y_{t}\}
to
\{X_{t}\}
.
Thus, Granger causality analysis is important for determining whether or not a set of variables
contains useful information for improving the prediction of another set of variables.

Geweke 1982
and
Geweke 1984
considered measures of linear dependence and feedback between components of a multivariate time series
in both time and frequency domains.

Hosoya 1991
proposed a refinement of the above measures, which also has a well-defined representation in the frequency domain.
For nonstationary vector autoregressive (VAR) models,
Sims et al. 1990
considered
the Wald’s statistic for the hypothesis testing problem and elucidated its nonstandard asymptotic distribution.
Granger causality for nonstationary bivariate cointegrated processes has also been considered in
Granger and Lin 1995
, among others.
A thorough treatment of the Granger causality for multivariate time series is discussed in
Lütkepohl 2005
.

In this paper, we propose a local Granger causality measure based on the locally stationary process.
A locally stationary process has a Cramér-like representation but its
transfer function is allowed to change over time.
Formal models for the time-varying spectra of nonstationary processes
have been developed since this concept was introduced in
Priestley 1965
.
A more theoretically rigorous framework for multivariate locally stationary processes have been formulated in
Dahlhaus 2000
.
To estimate the time-varying spectral density of a locally stationary process,

Neumann and von Sachs 1997
developed a wavelet estimator based on the pre-periodogram.
The parameter estimation for an evolutionary spectral is discussed in
Dahlhaus and Giraitis 1998
.
Local inference for locally stationary time series was investigated in
Dahlhaus 2009
.
Moreover,
Dahlhaus and Polonik 2009
constructed the estimation theory for the weak convergence of the empirical spectral processes.
To the best of our knowledge, despite the recent progress on models that capture nonstationary behavior,
local Granger causality has not yet been developed.
To address this limitation, this paper undertakes the task of developing this local
concept
because many time series phenomena display Granger causality behavior
that changes over the course of time
(e.g., electroencephalograms and stock market indices).
Thus, the contribution of this paper is a rigorous framework for statistical inference
for local Granger causality.

We focus on multivariate locally stationary processes
to develop the statistical inference for local Granger causality.
Statistical inference for multivariate stationary processes
has been discussed in
Hannan 1970
,
Taniguchi and Kakizawa 2000
,
Shumway and Stoffer 2000
and references therein.

Taniguchi et al. 1996
developed a nonparametric method to test the cross-relationships between multiple time series.

Sakiyama and Taniguchi 2004
discussed the discriminant analysis for multivariate locally stationary processes
based on the likelihood ratio.

Huang et al. 2004
proposed a SLEX model to develop a discriminant scheme that can extract local features of time series.

Ombao et al. 2001
and
Ombao et al. 2005

developed models for bivariate and multivariate nonstationary data using the SLEX basis which consists
of well-localized Fourier-like waveforms.

As noted, the goal of this paper is to develop statistical inference for local Granger causality
for multivariate locally stationary processes.
In particular, local Granger causality is expressed in the frequency domain
using the foundational ideas on Granger causality
for stationary processes.
We develop a procedure for parameter estimation based on the local Whittle likelihood
and
derive the asymptotic distribution of the estimators.
Under regularity conditions,
the estimates are shown to converge to multivariate normal in distribution.
Parametric Granger causality, however, converges to normal
or a quadratic form of normal random variables,
which depends on the gradient of the causality measure.
Several simulation studies were conducted to evaluate the finite sample performance
for multivariate time-varying autoregressive models.
To illustrate the potential impact of the proposed work, we analyzed the log-returns of the financial data and
multichannel electroencephalogram (EEG) data. Using the proposed method, the local Granger causality analyses
produced new insightful results in the data analyses.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we propose the local Granger causality.
The properties of the local Granger causality are detailed immediately behind the definition.
In Section
3
, we develop statistical inference for local Granger causality
based on the local Whittle estimation for multivariate locally stationary processes.
Numerical results on finite sample performance of the estimator and the test statistic for local causality are reported in Section
4
.
In Section
5
, we apply the proposed local Granger causality to EEG data and financial data.
The proofs for the theoretical results in Section
3
are relegated to Section
A
in supplement.

2
Local Granger Causality

In this section, we introduce the
concept of local Granger causality (LGC) in the framework of locally stationary processes.
Let
\bm{X}_{t,T}=(X_{t,T}^{(1)},\dots,X_{t,T}^{(p)})^{\,\mathrm{T}}
be a sequence of
p
-dimensional multivariate stochastic processes

\bm{X}_{t,T}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{t,T}(j)\bm{\epsilon}_{t-j},

(1)

where the sequences
\{A_{t,T}(j)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy the following conditions:
there exists a positive constant
C_{A}
such that

\sup_{t,T}\lVert A_{t,T}(j)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)},

and there exists a sequence of functions
A(\cdot,j):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

\sup_{u}\lVert A(u,j)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)}
;

(ii)

\sup_{j}\Bigl\lVert A_{t,T}(j)-A\Bigl(\frac{t}{T},j\Bigr)\Bigr\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)}T^{-1}
;

(iii)

V\Bigl(\lVert A(\cdot,j)\rVert_{\infty}\Bigr)\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)}
,

where
V(f)
is the total variation of the function
f
on the interval
[0,1]
, i.e.,
V
is defined as

V(f)=\sup\Bigl\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lvert f(x_{k})-f(x_{k-1})\rvert;\,0\leq x_{0}<\cdots<x_{m}\leq 1,\,m\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr\}.

The process (
1
) is usually referred to as the multivariate locally stationary process.
We impose the following assumptions on the process (
1
) for the estimation theory later on.

Assumption 2.1
.

For the process in (
1
),
let
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
be independent and identically distributed with

E\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\bm{0}
and
E\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{\top}=K
,
where the matrix
K
exists and all elements are bounded by
C_{K}
.
Furthermore, all elements in the
r
th moment of
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
exist and bounded by
C_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{(r)}
.
There exists a finite constant
C>0
such that
C_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{(r)}<C
.

Let
m
and
M
be two positive integers such that
p=m+M
.
Suppose
\bm{X}_{t,T}=\bigl({\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}}^{\top},{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}
,

\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
has the time-varying spectral density matrix
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
with the partition

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}&\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{12}\\
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{21}&\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{22}\end{pmatrix}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}A(u,\lambda)KA(u,-\lambda)^{\top},\qquad u\in[0,1],

(2)

where
A(u,\lambda):=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}A(u,j)\exp(\,\mathrm{i}j\lambda)
.
Let
\Sigma(u)
be the one-step-ahead prediction error covariance matrix
based on the time-varying spectral density matrix
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
with the same partition.
By the Kolmogorov’s formula for multiple time series, we have

\det\Sigma(u)=\exp\Biggl(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\Bigl(\det 2\pi\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\Biggr),\qquad\text{for any $u\in[0,1]$},

(see
Hannan 1970
, p.162).

Let
H(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\overline{\text{sp}}(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)},1\leq t\leq\tau_{1};\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)},1\leq t\leq\tau_{2})

be the closed linear subspace generated by

\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)},1\leq t\leq\tau_{1};\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)},1\leq t\leq\tau_{2}\}
.
Especially, we use
H(\tau_{1},0)
and
H(0,\tau_{2})
to express the
closed linear subspace generated by

\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)},t\leq\tau_{1}\}
,

\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)},t\leq\tau_{2}\}
,
respectively.

Introducing a companion process

\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}=\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\mid H(t,t-1)\bigr),

(3)

we propose the local Granger causality measure from
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\}
to
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\}
as

{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm FGC}(u,\lambda)d\lambda,

(4)

where

{\rm FGC}(u,\lambda)=\log\frac{\lvert\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\rvert}{\Bigl\lvert\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}-2\pi\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{12}\tilde{\Sigma}(u)_{22}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{21}\Bigr\rvert}.

Here,
\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)
is the time-varying spectral density matrix of the process
\{\bigl({\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}}^{\top},{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\}

(see Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
below),
and
\tilde{\Sigma}(u)
is an
(M\times M)
-matrix

\tilde{\Sigma}(u)_{22}=\Sigma(u)_{22}-\Sigma(u)_{21}\Sigma(u)_{11}^{-1}\Sigma(u)_{12}.

The proposal of the local Granger causality (
4
) is motivated by Hosoya’s measure of causality
(a term first coined in
Granger and Lin 1995
)
in combination with the nonstationary version of Kolmogorov’s formula by
Dahlhaus 1996
.
The companion process
\{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}\}
in (
3
) is introduced in order to remove the possible effect brought by
the nonorthogonality between residuals of predictions

E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)
and
E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)
.

We now start to explain properties of the proposed local Granger causality.
Denote by
\Sigma_{t,T}
the one-step-ahead prediction error covariance matrix, i.e.,

\Sigma_{t,T}=\,\mathrm{var}\bigl[\bm{X}_{t,T}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)\bigr].

Proposition 2.1
.

The companion process
\{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}\}
is a locally stationary process with the time-varying spectral density

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{22}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\tilde{\Sigma}(u)_{22}.

(5)

Proof.

From the definition of
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}
in (
1
), we have

\bm{X}_{t,T}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)=A_{t,T}(0)\bm{\epsilon}_{t}.

By the following formula (see Lemma 2.2 in
Hosoya 1991
)

\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\mid H(t,t-1)\bigr)=\\
\bigl\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(2)}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)\bigr\}-\Sigma_{t,T,21}\Sigma_{t,T,11}^{-1}\bigl\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid H(t-1,t-1)\bigr)\bigr\},

we find that

\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}=\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{(2)}-\Sigma_{t,T,21}\Sigma_{t,T,11}^{-1}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{(1)}\\
=\begin{pmatrix}-\Sigma_{t,T,21}\Sigma_{t,T,11}^{-1}&\,\,\,\bm{I}_{M}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}.

In view of Example 2.3 (i) in
Dahlhaus 2000
,
\{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}\}
is locally stationary.
A straightforward calculation gives the expression of
\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{22}
in (
5
).
∎

Let
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(\tau)
be the closed linear subspace generated by
\{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)},1\leq t\leq\tau\}
.
The Hosoya measure is defined as

{\rm HM}^{(2\to 1)}_{t,T}:=\log\frac{\det\,\mathrm{var}\bigl[\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid H(t-1,0)\bigr)\bigr]}{\det\,\mathrm{var}\bigl[\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid\sigma\{H(t-1,0)\cup\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(t-1)\}\bigr)\bigr]}.

(6)

For any fixed

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
has a factorization

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bm{\Lambda}(u,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda})\bm{\Lambda}(u,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda})^{*},\quad z\in\mathcal{D},

(7)

(see
Rozanov 1967

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose all eigenvalues of
A(u,\lambda)A(u,-\lambda)^{\top}
are bounded from below by some constant
C>0

uniformly in
u
and
\lambda
,
and all components of
A(u,\lambda)
are differentiable in
u

\lambda

(\partial/\partial u)(\partial/\partial\lambda)A(u,\lambda)_{ab}
for
a,b\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}
.
It holds that

\lvert{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(t/T)-{\rm HM}^{(2\to 1)}_{t,T}\rvert=o_{t}(1)+O_{T}(1),

where the
o_{t}(1)
term is uniform in
T
and the
o_{T}(1)
term is uniform in
t
.

Proof.

From Proposition
2.1
, we see that
the process
\{\bigl({\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}}^{\top},{\bm{Y}_{t,T}^{(2)}}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\}
is locally stationary.
In view of Lemma 2.3 in

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)
with

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{11}=\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}
and

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{21}=\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,-\lambda)_{12}^{\top}=\Bigl(-\Sigma(u)_{21}\Sigma(u)_{11}^{-1}\quad I_{M}\Bigr)\bm{\Lambda}(u,0)\bm{\Lambda}(u,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\\
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{12}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

A direct computation shows that the process

\bigl\{\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid\sigma\{H(t-1,0)\cup\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(t-1)\}\bigr)\bigr\}

is still locally stationary and has the time-varying spectral density

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{11}-\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{12}\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{22}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{f}}(u,\lambda)_{21}.

Inspection of Theorem 3.2 in

\det\,\mathrm{var}\bigl[\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid H(t-1,0)\bigr)\bigr]=\\
\exp\Biggl(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\Bigl(\det 2\pi\bm{f}(t/T,\lambda)_{11}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\Biggr)+o_{t}(1)+o_{T}(1),

(9)

and

\det\,\mathrm{var}\bigl[\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}-E\bigl(\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}\mid\sigma\{H(t-1,0)\cup\mathcal{H}^{(2)}(t-1)\}\bigr)\bigr]\\

+o_{t}(1)+o_{T}(1).

Combining (

The local Granger causality measure can be regarded as the limit of that constructed by the Wigner-Ville spectrum.
To be specific, let
\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)
be the Wigner-Ville spectrum of the process
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}
, i.e.,

\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{Cov}\bigl(\bm{X}_{[t-s/2],T},\bm{X}_{[t+s/2],T}\Bigr)\exp(-\mathrm{i}\lambda s),

(see
Martin and Flandrin 1985
).
The measure of the Wigner-Ville spectrum now is

{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}_{t,T}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm FGC}_{t,T}(\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda,

where

{\rm FGC}_{t,T}(\lambda)=\log\frac{\lvert\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert}{\Bigl\lvert\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-2\pi\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{12}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,T,22}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{21}\Bigr\rvert}

and
\tilde{\Sigma}(u)
is an
(M\times M)
-matrix

\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,T,22}=\Sigma_{t,T,22}-\Sigma_{t,T,21}\Sigma_{t,T,11}^{-1}\Sigma_{t,T,12}.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
is uniformly Lipschiz continuous with respect to
u
and
\lambda
.
For any sequence
t/T\to u
,
We have

\Bigl\lvert{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)-{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}_{t,T}\Bigr\rvert=o(1).

Proof.

We only show that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\bigl(\log\lvert\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\rvert-\log\lvert\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=o(1),

to see the difference in the numerator. The denominator can be proved similarly.

Let us consider the scalar process
\bm{\alpha}^{*}\bm{X}_{t,T}^{(1)}

with the Wigner-Ville spectrum

f^{\bm{\alpha}}_{t,T}(\lambda):=\bm{\alpha}^{*}\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}\bm{\alpha}
for any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Comparing it with
f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)=\bm{\alpha}^{*}\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bm{\alpha}
,
by Theorem 2.2 in
Dahlhaus 1996
, we see that

\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\lvert f_{t,T}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)-f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=o(1),

which implies that

\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\lvert f_{t,T}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)-f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=o(1),

(11)

since by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\lvert f_{t,T}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)-f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\Bigl(\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\lvert f_{t,T}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)-f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\Bigr)^{1/2}.

By Taylor’s expansion, we have

\log\lvert\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert=\log\lvert\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\rvert+{\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bigr)\Bigr]\\
+o\Bigl({\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr).

(12)

Remembering that
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bm{\Lambda}(u,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda})\bm{\Lambda}(u,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda})^{*}
from (
7
),
we see that there exists an
m\times m
Hermitian matrix
\bm{B}
such that
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)^{-1}_{11}=\bm{B}^{*}\bm{B}
,
and thus

{\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bigr)\Bigr]={\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{B}\bigl(\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bigr)\bm{B}^{*}\Bigr],

(13)

which is a sum of quadratic forms
f_{t,T}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\lambda)-f^{\bm{\alpha}}(u,\lambda)
.
Applying (
11
) to (
13
) yields

\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}-\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\bigr)\Bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=o(1),

and by observing (
12
), we conclude that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\lvert\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{11}\rvert-\log\lvert\bm{f}_{t,T}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=o(1).

∎

Remark 2.4
.

The construction of a linear predictor in practice for the locally stationary process may be of interest to some readers.
It can be shown that the predictor for the locally stationary process and that for the stationary approximation
are asymptotically equivalent under adequate conditions.
In contrast, we focus on the nonstationary version of Kolmogorov’s formula found in
Dahlhaus 1996
.
We elucidated that our local causality measure, as the limit of the measure constructed by the Wigner-Ville spectrum,
is a unique measure for multivariate locally stationary processes.

3.1
Local Whittle estimation

Let
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}
be the multivariate locally stationary process defined by (
1
)
with the time-varying spectral density
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
defined by (
2
).
The starting point is local estimation
by fitting a parametric spectral density model
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)
,

\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, to
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
.

Consider the observation stretch
(\bm{X}_{1,T},\dots,\bm{X}_{T,T})
and define

\bm{I}_{T}(u,\lambda)
to be the pre-periodogram matrix

\bm{I}_{T}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{l:1\leq[uT+1/2\pm l/2]\leq T}\bm{X}_{[uT+1/2+l/2],T}\bm{X}_{[uT+1/2-l/2],T}^{\top}\exp(-\,\mathrm{i}\lambda l).

(14)

Note that the pre-periodogram
\bm{I}_{T}(u,\lambda)
was first introduced in
Neumann and von Sachs 1997
.

We define the spectral divergence
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},u)
between the parametric spectral density
and the time-varying spectral density as

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},u)=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\det\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)+{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

(15)

For any fixed
u\in[0,1]
, define
\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)
as

\bm{\theta}_{0}(u):=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},u).

(16)

Let
u_{k}:=k/T
. The sample analogue
\mathcal{L}_{T}
of the spectral divergence is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{T}(\bm{\theta},u)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\frac{1}{b_{T}}K\Bigl(\frac{u-u_{k}}{b_{T}}\Bigr)\\
\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\det\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)+{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\bm{I}_{T}(u_{k},\lambda)\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda,

(17)

and the local Whittle estimator of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)
is defined as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u):=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{T}(\bm{\theta},u).

(18)

We impose the following assumptions on the class of time-varying spectral densities
and the kernel function
K
in (
17
)
to investigate the asymptotic properties of the local Whittle estimator (
18
).

Assumption 3.1
.

(i)

The time-varying spectral density matrix
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)
is continuously differentiable
with respect to
u
for
u\in(0,1)
.

(ii)

K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a nonnegative, bounded symmetric continuous function of bounded variation
with a compact support
[-1,1]
satisfying
\int K(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1
.
Let

K_{b}(x):=\frac{1}{b}K\Bigl(\frac{x}{b}\Bigr),

where
b:=b_{T}\to 0
, as
T\to\infty
.

We now specify the regularity conditions for the parametric model
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)

and the local parameter
\bm{\theta}(u)
.
For the brevity, let
\bm{\theta}:=\bm{\theta}(u)
when
u
does not matter.

Assumption 3.2
.

(i)

For any fixed
u\in[0,1]
,
\bm{\theta}(u)\in\Theta
,
where
\Theta
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

For any fixed
u\in[0,1]
,

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(1)}(u)}\not=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(2)}(u)}

on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, if
\bm{\theta}^{(1)}(u)\not=\bm{\theta}^{(2)}(u)
.

(iii)

The parametric spectral density matrix
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)
is bounded away from 0 for each component,
and is continuously differentiable with respect to
\lambda
for

\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi)
.

(iv)

For any
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
is positive definite and
it is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}
.

(v)

For any fixed
u\in[0,1]
,

(v-a)

\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\in\Theta
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},u)
and
lies in the interior of
\Theta
.

(v-b)

the matrices

M_{I}^{u}=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Biggl[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}{\rm Tr}\Bigl\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\bm{I}_{T}(u,\lambda)\Bigr\}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\log\det\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Biggr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

and

M_{f}^{u}=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Biggl[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}{\rm Tr}\Bigl\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\Bigr\}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\log\det\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Biggr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

are both positive definite.

First, let us consider the asymptotics for the sample analog of the spectral divergence
\mathcal{L}_{T}(\bm{\theta},u)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
and
3.2
hold.
For any
u\in(0,1)
, if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
, then we have

\sqrt{Tb_{T}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{T}(\bm{\theta},u)-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},u)\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{V}^{\mathcal{L}}(u)).

as
T\to\infty
,
where

\mathbb{V}^{\mathcal{L}}(u)=4\pi\int_{-1}^{1}K(v)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Biggl(\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,t,v,w=1}^{p}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Bigl(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{rt}(\lambda_{1})\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{vw}(\lambda_{2})\tilde{\gamma}_{rtvw}(u;-\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{2})\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{2}\Biggr),

(19)

where
\tilde{\gamma}
is the fourth-order spectral density of the process.

Remark 3.2
.

Inspection of the proof of Theorem
3.1
reveals that the main order of bias is
O(T^{1/2}b_{T}^{5/2})

and the asymptotic variance is of order
O(T^{-1}b_{T}^{-1})
.
The optimal order of the bandwidth
b_{T}
can be determined by equating squared bias and variance.
Thus, we obtain the optimal order
b_{T}=O(T^{-1/3})
and the mean square error is
O(T^{-2/3})
.
This optimal order is similar to the one derived in
Künsch 1989
in the context of
statistical inference for stationary time series.

Let
M_{f,0}^{u}
be

M_{f,0}^{u}=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Biggl[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}{\rm Tr}\Bigl\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\Bigr\}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\log\det\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Biggr]_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Now we establish the asymptotic normality of the local estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
and
3.2
hold.
For any
u\in(0,1)
, if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
, then we have

\sqrt{Tb_{T}}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{V}(u)),

(20)

as
T\to\infty
, where
\mathbb{V}(u):=(M_{f,0}^{u})^{-1}\mathbb{V}^{\bm{\theta}}(u)(M_{f,0}^{u})^{-1}
and

\mathbb{V}^{\bm{\theta}}(u)_{ab}=4\pi\int_{-1}^{1}K(v)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Biggl(\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm Tr}\Bigl[\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\biggl\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\biggr\}\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\biggl\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}(\lambda)\biggr\}\Bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,t,v,w=1}^{p}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{rt}(\lambda_{1})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{b}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}^{vw}(\lambda_{2})\tilde{\gamma}_{rtvw}(u;-\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{2})\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{2}\Biggr),

(21)

(a,b=1,\dots,d)
,
where
\tilde{\gamma}
is the fourth-order spectral density of the process.

3.2
Inference for causality measures

In this subsection,
we develop the statistical inference for the local Granger causality measure (
4
)
based on the parametric model
\{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}\mid\bm{\theta}(u)\in\Theta\}
.
Denote by
\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
the parametric one-step-ahead prediction error matrix,
and
by
\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
the parametric model for the companion process (
3
).
Note that the matrix
\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
is uniquely determined by
the model
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
and the matrix
\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
(see, e.g., (
5
) and (
8
)).

Suppose
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}
,

\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}(u)}

and

\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}

have the same partition as (
2
).
To make the statistical inference feasible, we impose the following assumption
on the parametric models.

Assumption 3.3
.

For any
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
,

\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\log\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda>-\infty.

Assumption
3.3
is usually referred to as the maximal rank condition.
Under Assumption
3.3
,
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}(\lambda)
is non-degenerate a.e. and
\Sigma_{\bm{\theta}(u)}

is positive definite for any fixed
u\in[0,1]
.
Now the parametric local Granger causality for (
4
) is

{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}{\rm FGC}\bigl(\lambda;\bm{\theta}(u)\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda,

(22)

where

{\rm FGC}(\lambda;\,\bm{\theta})=\log\frac{\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert}{{\Bigl\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}-2\pi\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}\Bigr\rvert}}.

The main results are described in the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
If we have, for some
i=1,\dots,d
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}\not=0,\qquad\text{for some $\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi]$,}

(23)

uniformly in
u\in[0,1]
, and if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
,
then we have

\sqrt{Tb_{T}}\Bigl({\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})-{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\Bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{V}^{\text{GC}}(u)),

where

\mathbb{V}^{\text{GC}}(u)=\left(\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\right)^{\top}\mathbb{V}(u)\left(\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\right),

and

\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}}),\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d}}{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\right)^{\top}

with

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}(u)}(\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

There are situations when condition (
23
) may not be satisfied.
That is, for some
u\in(0,1)
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}=\bm{0},\qquad\text{a.e. $\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi]$.}

(24)

In this case, we centralize
{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})
as
{\rm CGC}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})
, i.e.,

{\rm CGC}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}}):={\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})-{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}}).

(25)

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
In addition, assume (
24
) with

\mathcal{H}(u,\lambda):=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}\not=O_{d\times d},\qquad\text{for some $\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi]$},

for
u\in(0,1)
.
Let

\mathcal{H}(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\mathcal{H}(u,\lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

(26)

Then if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
, the following result holds

2Tb_{T}{\rm CGC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr)^{\top}\mathcal{H}(u)\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr),

(27)

where the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr)
is defined in Theorem
3.3
.
In particular, if
\mathbb{V}(u)^{-1/2}\mathcal{H}(u)\mathbb{V}(u)^{-1/2}
is an idempotent matrix, then
the right hand side of (
27
)
has a chi-squared distribution
\chi^{2}_{\nu}

with the degrees of freedom

\nu={\rm Tr}(\mathbb{V}(u)^{-1/2}\mathcal{H}(u)\mathbb{V}(u)^{-1/2}).

Example 1

(Time-varying vector autoregression model)
.

Suppose the multivariate locally Gaussian stationary process (
1
) has the time-varying spectral density

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\bigl(I_{2}+A(u)\exp(\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\bigr)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{12}&\sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}\bigl(I_{2}+A(u)^{\top}\exp(-\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\bigr)^{-1},

where
A(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{11}(u)&\alpha_{12}(u)\\
\alpha_{21}(u)&\alpha_{22}(u)\end{pmatrix}

and

\alpha_{12}(u)\equiv 0
.

We adopt the following parametric spectral density
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)
for model fitting.

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Biggl(I_{2}+\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}\exp(\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\Biggr)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}\\
s_{12}&s_{22}\end{pmatrix}\Biggl(I_{2}+\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\exp(-\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\Biggr)^{-1},

where
\bm{\theta}=(a_{11},a_{12},a_{21},a_{22},s_{11},s_{12},s_{22})^{\top}
.
From the definition (
22
), we have

{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}-\log\Bigl\lvert 1-2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\Bigr\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Thus,
{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)
is

{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)=-\log\Bigl\lvert 1-2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}f_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}^{-1}\Bigr\rvert.

A straightforward computation leads to

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{a_{12}=0}=\bm{0},\qquad\text{for any $\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi]$.}

In addition, it holds that

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial a_{12}^{2}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}=\frac{2(\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}-\sigma_{12}^{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{11}^{4}}\frac{\lvert 1-\alpha_{11}(u)\exp(\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert 1-\alpha_{22}(u)\exp(\,\mathrm{i}\lambda)\rvert^{2}}>0;

and for all
\lambda\in(-\pi,\pi]
,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}=0,\quad\text{for $\theta_{i}\not=a_{12}$ or $\theta_{j}\not=a_{12}$}.

Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}=\bigl(\hat{\alpha}_{11}(u),\hat{\alpha}_{12}(u),\hat{\alpha}_{21}(u),\hat{\alpha}_{22}(u),\hat{\sigma}_{11},\hat{\sigma}_{12},\hat{\sigma}_{22}\bigr)^{\top}

be the local Whittle estimator defined in (
18
).
Applying Theorem
3.5
, we obtain

Tb_{T}\,\frac{\sigma_{11}^{4}\Bigl(\int_{-1}^{1}K(v)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Bigr)^{-1}}{\bigl(1+\alpha_{11}(u)^{2}-2\alpha_{11}(u)\alpha_{22}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}-\sigma_{12}^{2}\bigr)^{2}}{\rm CGC}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})\xrightarrow{d}\chi^{2}_{1},

(28)

since

\mathcal{H}(u)_{22}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial a_{12}^{2}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
=\frac{2\bigl(1+\alpha_{11}(u)^{2}-2\alpha_{11}(u)\alpha_{22}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}-\sigma_{12}^{2}\bigr)^{2}}{\bigl(1-\alpha_{22}(u)^{2}\bigr)\sigma_{11}^{4}}

and

\mathbb{V}(u)_{22}=\bigl(1-\alpha_{22}(u)^{2}\bigr)\int_{-1}^{1}K(v)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v.

3.3
Hypothesis testing for causality measures

We now address the hypothesis testing problem for the local measures
{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}
.
Suppose that we want to test for local causality at a particular rescaled time
u\in[0,1]
.
Define the local hypothesis
H_{0}^{(2\to 1)}
to be

H_{0}^{(2\to 1)}:{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)=c.

(29)

We consider two cases of the null hypothesis (
29
): (i)
c=0
, and (ii)
c>0
.

Let us first consider the case (i)
c=0
.
For any fixed
u\in[0,1]
, with the shorthand
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}(u)
, we have

{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log\frac{\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert}{\Bigl\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}-2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}\Bigr\rvert}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda,

and thus,
{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,\bm{\theta})=0
if and only if

\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}\rvert=\Bigl\lvert\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}-2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}\Bigr\rvert.

(30)

Here,
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{11}
and
2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}

are both Hermitian. Thus, the equality (
30
) holds if

2\pi\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{21}=O_{m\times m}.

It follows that
\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}=O_{m\times M}
,
since
\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\theta},22}\right)^{-1}
is positive definite.
Since
\mathfrak{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)_{12}=O_{m\times M}
,
it is straightforward to see that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)=\bm{0},

which is the case we considered in Theorem
3.5
.

Accordingly, for the local hypothesis
H_{0}^{(2\to 1)}:{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)=0
, we take

S^{\dagger}(u):=2T\,b_{T}\,{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})

as the test statistic. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{(2\to 1)}:{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)=0
,
if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
, it holds that

S^{\dagger}(u)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr)^{\top}\mathcal{H}(u)\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr).

where
\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbb{V}(u)\bigr)
is defined in Theorem
3.3
.

Remark 3.7
.

The matrix
\mathcal{H}(u)
in (
26
) is unknown in general,
but it is determinable from the parameterization of
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)
.
In practice, the matrix
\mathcal{H}(u)
should be replaced with its plug-in version

\mathcal{H}(u;\,\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}{\rm FGC}^{(2\to 1)}_{\bm{\theta}}(\lambda)\Bigr|_{\bm{\theta}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda

to construct an asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for
{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)
.
Instead, in some situation,
for instance, as a continuation of Example
1
, if we take

\tilde{S}^{\dagger}(u):=T\,b_{T}\,\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{11}^{4}\Bigl(\int_{-1}^{1}K(v)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Bigr)^{-1}}{\bigl(1+\hat{\alpha}_{11}(u)^{2}-2\hat{\alpha}_{11}(u)\hat{\alpha}_{22}(u)\bigr)\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{11}\hat{\sigma}_{22}-\hat{\sigma}_{12}^{2}\bigr)^{2}}{\rm CGC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})

as a sample version of the left hand side in (
28
),
then the confidence interval is
[0,\chi^{2}_{1,1-\alpha}]
, where

\chi^{2}_{1,1-\alpha}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the chi-squared distribution
with 1 degree of freedom.
It is straightforward to see that the test by
\tilde{S}^{\dagger}(u)
is consistent.
If the null hypothesis is rejected,
then the conclusion is that at level
\alpha
there is sufficient evidence to suggest that
there exists local Granger causality from one series to another at rescaled time
u\in[0,1]
.

We now move on to the second case (ii)
c>0
.
The Wald type test statistic
S^{*}(u)
is

S^{*}(u)=Tb_{T}\Bigl({\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})-c\Bigr)\\
\qquad\qquad\quad\left[\left(\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})\right)\mathbb{V}(u)\left(\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})\right)^{\top}\right]^{-1}\Bigl({\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})-c\Bigr).

(31)

The following result is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.4
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{(2\to 1)}:{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u)=c>0
,
if
b_{T}^{-1}=o\bigl(T(\log T)^{-6}\bigr)
and
b_{T}=o(T^{-1/5})
, we have

S^{*}(u)\xrightarrow{d}\chi^{2}_{d},

where
\chi^{2}_{d}
is a chi-squared distribution with the degrees of freedom
d
.

Remark 3.9
.

The covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}(u)
in (
31
) is usually unknown,
and thus, we have to construct a consistent estimator
\hat{\mathbb{V}}(u)
instead.
This can be done by following
Keenan 1987
or
Taniguchi 1982
.

4
Numerical Simulations

In this section, we investigate the finite sample performance
of our proposed local Whittle estimation and the hypothesis testing for non-causality
of multiple time series.

4.1
Finite sample performance of local Whittle estimation

Let
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}
be a multivariate locally stationary process defined by

\bm{X}_{t,T}=A\Bigl(\frac{t}{T}\Bigr)\bm{X}_{t-1,T}+\bm{\epsilon}_{t},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},I_{2}).

(32)

We consider two examples for the time-varying coefficient matrix
A(\cdot)
.
Let
A^{({\rm i})}(u)
and
A^{({\rm ii})}(u)
be

A^{({\rm i})}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&a_{12}(u)\\
0&1/2\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad A^{({\rm ii})}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}7/10&a_{12}(u)\\
0&3/10\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
a_{12}(u)
is defined as

a_{12}(u)=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{$0\leq u\leq 1/\pi$},\\

Data Analysis

In this section, we apply local Granger causality to two real datasets – EEG data and financial data.

5.1
EEG data

We provide a brief description of the data.
The EEG signals are sampled at the rate of 100 Hertz. The recordings are taken from channels
the central channels (C3, C4, Cz), parietal channels (P3, P4) and the temporal channels (T3, T4, T5)
which correspond roughly to the central, parietal and temporal brain cortical regions (See Figure

The original dataset has 32680 time points for each channel (i.e., the period of the observation is
326.8 seconds). This dataset was previously analyzed in
Ombao et al. 2005
and
Schröder and Ombao 2019
. However,
none of these two papers addressed the very important issue of causality. This is the first paper that
examined local Granger causality features in this data.

The dataset is the weekly log-returns
of the closing stock prices of two financial groups (Mitsubishi and Mizuho) in the Nikkei index.
For brevity, two financial groups are denoted by A and B here.
The weekly data are from 2006 January 1st to 2010 December 26th,
so the length of the data is
T=260
.

In our data analysis, we compute local Granger causality

{\rm GC}^{{\rm A}\to{\rm B}}(u)
and
{\rm GC}^{{\rm B}\to{\rm A}}(u)

for rescaled time

In general, Granger causality is not symmetric (e.g., the analysis of EEG data) and
we regard

\mathbb{R}^{2}

We separate local Granger causality in (

(1) from 2006 January 1st to 2008 June 29th;

We apply computational topology tools,
persistence diagram
,
persistence barcode
,

persistence landscape
,
to capture the feature of these data points of local Granger causality
(See, e.g.

The result is statistically significant at the significance level of 0.01.
It is known that there is a financial crisis between 2007 and 2008.
Through the analysis of local Granger causality,
we detected the structural change of the causality between these two financial data.
A theoretical justification of this approach will be left as future work.

6
Conclusion

The primary contribution of this paper is statistical inference for local Granger causality for multivariate time series
under the framework of multivariate locally stationary processes.
Our proposed concept of local Granger causality is a generalization of Geweke’s measure and Hosoya’s measure
- both of which were developed only for stationary processes.
Our proposed generalization is well characterized in the frequency domain and has the advantage of being able to capture time-evolving causality relationships.
We
developed a procedure for hypothesis testing for the existence of the local Granger causality
from a parametric point of view.
We demonstrate, through the analysis of real data, the efficiency and efficacy of this procedure to
find the time-evolving aspects of the local Granger causality, which could be overlooked by the existing method
for causality analysis.

In summary, we proposed a consistent method to detect the time change of the local Granger causality.
While our proposed method is nonparametric,
we note that a procedure for stationary processes was developed in
Taniguchi et al. 1996
.
This could serve as an inspiration for constructing a nonparametric method for locally stationary processes
to test for the local Granger causality, and compare the performance of both approaches.
For multiple time series, to investigate the time change of the local Granger causality
also suffers from the curse of dimensionality.
There are many remaining challenges including dimension reduction in terms of causality
between each component of multiple time series.
In addition to the Lasso method in
Tibshirani 1996
, most penalized estimation procedures
could be added to our parametric approach to shrink some minor causality between components.
A frequency-specific local causality approach will also be elucidated in our future work.

+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)^{\top}\mathcal{H}(u)\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)+o_{P}\Bigl(\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr).

Since
\nabla{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=\bm{0}
from (
24
),
we have

Tb_{T}\bigl({\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;\,{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}})-{\rm GC}^{(2\to 1)}(u;{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\bigr)=\\
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{Tb_{T}}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)^{\top}\mathcal{H}(u)\sqrt{Tb_{T}}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{T}(u)-\bm{\theta}_{0}(u)\bigr)+o_{P}(1).

We arrived at the conclusion (
27
) by the continuous mapping theorem.
∎

Appendix B
Multivariate locally stationary processes

In Section

Especially, we evaluate the absolute differences of the covariances and higher order cumulants

\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}

\{\bm{X}^{*}(u)\}
with a spectral density matrix
\bm{f}(u,\lambda)

Let

l(j):=\begin{cases}1,&\qquad\lvert j\rvert\leq 1,\\
\lvert j\rvert\log^{1+\kappa}\lvert j\rvert,&\qquad\lvert j\rvert>1,\end{cases}

for some constant
\kappa>0

Let
C
be a generic constant in Appendix, and
the following inequality is repetitively used in the proof.

\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{l(j)l(j+s)}\leq\frac{C}{l(s)}.

(45)

The following assumption corresponds to Assumption
2.1
, which is imposed for the multivariate locally stationary process
\{\bm{X}_{t,T}\}
in the main text.

Assumption B.1
.

Suppose the multivariate locally stationary process

\bm{X}_{t,T}=(X_{t,T}^{(1)},\dots,\\
X_{t,T}^{(d)},\dots,X_{t,T}^{(p)})^{\top}
has a representation

\bm{X}_{t,T}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{t,T}(j)\bm{\epsilon}_{t-j},

(46)

where the sequences
\{A_{t,T}(j)\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

\{\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}

there exists a constant

\sup_{t,T}\lVert A_{t,T}(j)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)},

(47)

and there exists a sequence of functions
A(\cdot,j):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(i)

\sup_{u}\lVert A(u,j)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)}
;

(ii)

\sup_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl\lVert A_{t,T}(j)-A\Bigl(\frac{t}{T},j\Bigr)\Bigr\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{A}

(iii)

V\Bigl(\lVert A(\cdot,j)\rVert_{\infty}\Bigr)\leq\frac{C_{A}}{l(j)}
,

where
V(f)
is the total variation of the function
f
on the interval
[0,1]

In addition,
the
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed with

E\bm{\epsilon}_{t}=\bm{0}
and
E\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}^{\top}=K
,
where the matrix
K

C_{K}
.
Furthermore, all the moments of
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
exist.
All elements in
r

C_{\bm{\epsilon}}^{(r)}<C

.

Remark B.1
.

Let us consider the time-varying spectral density matrix

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\bigl(\bm{f}(u,\lambda)_{ij}\bigr)_{i,j=1,\dots,p}
defined in (
2

\bm{f}(u,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}A(u,\lambda)KA(u,-\lambda)^{\top},

.
The autovariance function
\bm{\gamma}(u,s)

\bm{\gamma}(u,s)=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\bm{f}(u,\lambda)\exp(\,\mathrm{i}\lambda s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}A(u,j)KA(u,j+s)^{\top}.

Especially, the (a, b)-element of the matrix

\displaystyle\lvert\bm{\gamma}(u,s)_{ab}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sup_{u}\lVert A(u,j)\rVert_{\infty}\lVert K\rVert_{\infty}\sup_{u}\lVert A(u,j+s)^{\top}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{l(j)l(j+s)}

\displaystyle\frac{C}{l(s)},

(i) and the third inequality follows from (

Remark B.2

From (

Remark B.3

on discrete points

Lemma B.4

Under (

Proof.

From (

\displaystyle B_{1}+B_{2},\quad\text{(say).}

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\beta_{T}

Suppose

Remark C.4
.

The sequence
\beta_{T}
is used to alleviate the divergence of the harmonic series.
There exists a sequence

Proof.

Using the expression (

We apply Lemma

to compute the higher order cumulants.
Actually, it holds that

{\rm cum}\bigl(\xi_{T}(u^{(1)};\psi^{(1)})_{a_{1}b_{1}},\cdots,\xi_{T}(u^{(q)};\psi^{(q)})_{a_{q}b_{q}}\bigr)\\
={\rm cum}\Bigl(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\kappa_{1}}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{1},T}^{(a_{1})}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{1}-s_{1},T}^{(b_{1})},\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\kappa_{2}}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{2},T}^{(a_{2})}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{2}-s_{2},T}^{(b_{2})},\dots,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\kappa_{q}}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{q},T}^{(a_{q})}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{q}-s_{q},T}^{(b_{q})}\Bigr)\\

To compute higher order cumulants,
we have to consider all indecomposable partitions of the following table

\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{X}_{\kappa_{1},T}^{(a_{1})}&\bm{X}_{\kappa_{1}-s_{1},T}^{(b_{1})}\\

B.5

We give a representative example of a partition below.

C.2
Asymptotic Normality

Here, we show the asymptotic normality of the empirical spectral process
\xi_{T}(\psi)
in (
60
).
To this goal, we adopt the idea in
Dahlhaus and Polonik 2009

to use the Gaussian kernel as the mollifier with the property of being rapidly decreasing.
Let
G
be the Gaussian kernel, that is,

G(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\Bigr),

and
G_{b}
the mollifier

G_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}G\Bigl(\frac{x}{\beta}\Bigr),

with
\beta:=\beta_{T}\to 0
as
T\to\infty
.
From the convolution theorem,
the Fourier coefficients
\hat{\psi}^{*T}
of
\psi^{*T}:=\psi*G_{\beta}
are

\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,k)=\hat{\psi}(u,k)\hat{G}_{\beta}(k),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(77)

Remark C.5
.

The remarkable feature of this manipulation is that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,k)\rvert\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}(u,k)\rvert,

since for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\lvert\hat{G}_{\beta}(k)\rvert=\Bigl\lvert\exp\Bigl(\frac{-\beta^{2}k^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr\rvert\leq 1.

In addition, the following result holds.

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,k)\rvert=O\Bigl(\log\bigl(\beta_{T}^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr).

(78)

If we take
\beta_{T}
as
\beta_{T}=T^{-k}
for any
k\geq 1
, then we have

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,k)\rvert=O(\log T).

Proof of Remark
C.5
.

For any
1\leq i,j\leq p
, let
\psi:=\phi_{ij}\in\Psi
as in Assumption
C.1
.
Note that
\psi(u,\cdot)
is a continuous function of bounded variation.

(i)

Let
k\not=0
.
From Jordan decomposition theorem, there exists a signed measure
g_{\psi}
such that

\hat{\psi}(u,k)=\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\frac{\exp(-\,\mathrm{i}k\lambda)-1}{-\,\mathrm{i}k}g_{\psi}(u,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda),

which leads to

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}(u,k)\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\lvert k\rvert}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}V\bigl(\psi(u,\cdot)\bigr)\leq\frac{C\tau_{\infty,\rm{TV}}}{\lvert k\rvert}.

(79)

(ii)

Let
k=0
.

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}(u,0)\rvert\leq 2\pi\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\sup_{\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi]}\psi(u,\lambda)\leq 2\pi\tau_{\infty,\infty}.

(80)

Combing (
79
) and (
80
) with the relation (
77
), we obtain

\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\lvert\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,k)\rvert\leq C\Bigl(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lvert k\rvert}\exp\Bigl(\frac{-\beta^{2}k^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\log\bigl(\beta^{-1}\bigr)\Bigr).

Thus, the equation (
78
) is shown.
∎

Theorem C.6
.

Suppose Assumptions
B.1

\big(\xi_{T}(u^{(1)};\psi^{(1)})_{a_{1}b_{1}},\cdots,\xi_{T}(u^{(q)};\psi^{(q)})_{a_{q}b_{q}}\bigr)^{\top}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bm{0},(V_{jk})_{j,k=1,\dots q}\bigr),\qquad\text{as $T\to\infty$,}

where

V_{jk}=2\pi\delta(u^{(j)},u^{(k)})\\
\Biggl(\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{(j)}(v,\lambda)\overline{\psi^{(k)}(v,\lambda)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Bigr)\bm{f}(u^{(j)},\lambda)_{a_{j}a_{k}}\overline{\bm{f}(u^{(j)},\lambda)}_{b_{j}b_{k}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
+\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{(j)}(v,\lambda)\overline{\psi^{(k)}(v,-\lambda)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Bigr)\bm{f}(u^{(j)},\lambda)_{a_{j}b_{k}}\overline{\bm{f}(u^{(j)},\lambda)}_{b_{j}a_{k}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda\\
+\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\int^{\pi}_{-\pi}\Bigl(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{(j)}(v,\lambda_{1})\overline{\psi^{(k)}(v,-\lambda_{2})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\Bigr)\tilde{\gamma}_{a_{j}a_{k}b_{j}b_{k}}(u^{(j)};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda_{2}\Biggr),

where
\tilde{\gamma}
is the fourth-order spectral density of the process.

Proof.

First we show that

\,\mathrm{var}\Bigl(\xi_{T}(u;\psi)_{ab}-\xi_{T}(u;\psi^{*T})_{ab}\Bigr)\to 0,

which, in turn, shows that

\xi_{T}(u;\psi)_{ab}-\xi_{T}(u;\psi^{*T})_{ab}\rightarrow_{P}0.

(81)

As in Remark
C.5
, let
\beta_{T}=T^{-k}
for any

.
Following this choice, we have
O(\beta_{T}/b_{T})=o(1)
.

Note that

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{var}\Bigl(\xi_{T}(u;\psi)_{ab}-\xi_{T}(u;\psi^{*T})_{ab}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=Tb_{T}\,\mathrm{var}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2\pi T}

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1-T}^{T-1}\bigl\{\hat{\psi}_{T}(u-u_{k},-s)-\hat{\psi}^{*T}_{T}(u-u_{k},-s)\bigr\}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{s}}\bm{X}_{k+t,T}^{(a)}\bm{X}_{k+t+s,T}^{(b)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq b_{T}^{-1}\Bigl(\sup_{u}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert\hat{\psi}(u,-s)-\hat{\psi}^{*T}(u,-s)\rvert\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Cb_{T}^{-1}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\lvert\exp(-s^{2}\beta_{T}^{2}/2)-1\rvert^{2}}{s^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from (
77
).
Since
\lvert\exp(-s^{2}\beta_{T}^{2}/2)-1\rvert\leq\min(1,s^{2}\beta_{T}^{2}/2)
,
the order of the last term is
O(\beta_{T}/b_{T})=o(1)
.
Thus, (
81
) is shown.

Now, we only have to consider the finite distributions of
\xi_{T}(u;\psi^{*T})
.
However, from Remark
C.5
, we find that the condition (
65
) is satisfied
and thus the covariance matrix of
\xi_{T}(u;\psi^{*T})
can be expressed in the form of
(
66
).
Therefore, the proof is completed.
∎
