Logics of Dependence and Independence: The Local Variants
Erich Grädel, Phil Pützstück

Abstract
Modern logics of dependence and independence are based on team semantics, which means that formulae are evaluated not on a single assignment of values to variables, but on a set of such assignments, called a team. This leads to high expressive power, on the level of existential second-order logic. As an alternative, Baltag and van Benthem have proposed a local variant of dependence logic, called logic of functional dependence ( LFD ). While its semantics is also based on a team, the formulae are evaluated locally on just one of its assignments, and the team just serves as the supply of the possible assignments that are taken into account in the evaluation process. This logic thus relies on the modal perspective of generalized assignments semantics, and can be seen as a fragment of first-order logic. For the variant of LFD without equality, the satisfiability problem is decidable. We extend the idea of localising logics of dependence and independence in a systematic way, taking into account local variants of standard atomic dependency properties: besides dependence and independence, also inclusion, exclusion, and anonymity. We study model-theoretic and algorithmic questions of the localised logics, and also resolve some of the questions that had been left open by Baltag and van Benthem. In particular, we study decidability issues of the local logics, and prove that satisfiability of LFD with equality is undecidable. Further, we establish characterisation theorems via appropriate notions of bisimulation and study the complexity of model checking problems for these logics.

Introduction

Following work that has been initiated by Hodges
[
14
]
and
Väänänen
[
18
]
, modern logics of dependence and independence are
generally based on
team semantics
. This means that a formula
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

in such a logic is not evaluated on a single assignment
s:\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\rightarrow A
of values to
the free variables,
but for a
set
of such assignments, which is called a
team
. In these logics,
dependence and independence statements about variables, such as
“
y
depends on
x
”
or

“
x
and
y
are independent”
are considered as
atomic properties of teams
.
Besides dependence and independence atoms, further atomic team properties have been
considered: inclusion, exclusion, anonymity, conditional independence, and others.
A crucial feature of the logics with team semantics is that they manipulate second-order objects
by first-order syntax. The evaluation of a formula on a team can be considered as a dynamic process
(or game) that modifies the team while moving from the formula
through its syntax tree to the atoms or, equivalently, as an annotation of the syntax tree of the formula by teams.
The expressive power of logics with team semantics is typically on the level of powerful fragments
of existential second-order logic. More precisely, with a logic
L
with team semantics,
which syntactically extends first-order logic by certain atomic team properties, one can associate
a fragment
F\subseteq\Sigma^{1}_{1}
of existential-second-order, such that every formula

\varphi(\bar{x})\in L
(of vocabulary
\tau
) is equivalent to a
sentence

\psi\in F
(of vocabulary
\tau\cup\{T\}
, where
T
is a predicate for the team),
such that
\varphi(\bar{x})
is true in a structure
\mathfrak{M}
with a team
T
, if, and only if
the expanded structure
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is a model of
\psi
. Understanding the expressive power of a logic
with team semantics thus means to identify the fragment
F\subseteq\Sigma^{1}_{1}
such that
these equivalences hold in both directions. Here are some of the most important results of this kind:

•

Dependence logic
{\textsf{FO}}(\operatorname{dep})
and exclusion logic
{\textsf{FO}}({}\mathbin{\operatorname{|}}{})

are equivalent to the fragment of
\Sigma^{1}_{1}
-sentences in which the
predicate for the team appears only negatively
[
16
]
.

•

Inclusion logic
{\textsf{FO}}(\subseteq)
and anonymity logic
{\textsf{FO}}(\Upsilon)
are
equivalent to sentences of form
\forall\bar{x}(T\bar{x}\rightarrow\psi(T,\bar{x}))

in the posGFP-fragment of least fixed-point logic,
such that
T
occurs only positively in
\psi

[
9
]
.

•

Independence logic
{\textsf{FO}}(\perp)
and inclusion-exclusion logic

{\textsf{FO}}(\subseteq,{}\mathbin{\operatorname{|}}{})
are equivalent with full
\Sigma^{1}_{1}

(and thus can describe all NP-properties of teams)
[
8
]
.

In particular, all of these logics are much more expressive than classical
first-order logic and are of course undecidable for satisfiability.

Recently, Baltag and van Benthem
[
3
]
have proposed a different
kind of dependence logic, called logic of functional dependence
LFD
,
which can be considered as a fragment of first-order logic.
Its semantics is also based on an underlying team of assignments,
but the formulae are evaluated
locally
on just one of these assignments,
while the team serves as the supply of (or one might also say, the restriction for)
the possible assignments that are admitted in the evaluation process of the formula.
This is the modal perspective of generalized assignments semantics,
where not all possible assignments of values to variables are available but
only a given set of them; these may be considered as the possible worlds
in a Kripke style semantics, where neighbouring worlds are assignments that
agree on some subset of the variables. Thus, in an evaluation process of such
a formula, the team remains unchanged, but one moves around between different
assignments in that team. Dependence comes in by means of atoms
D_{X}y

(where
y
is a variable and
X
is a set of variables), which are true at a
given assignment
s
if all assignments
t
in the underlying team that agree
with
s
on all variables in the set
X
, also agree with
s
on
y
.
The atom
D_{X}y
can thus be read as a
local
dependence of
y
on
X

(on the assignments related to the current one by agreement on
X
),
whereas the standard dependence atom
\operatorname{dep}(X,y)
in Väänänen’s dependence logic

[
18
]
says that
y

globally
depends on
X
in the team,
in the sense that there is function which, for
all
assignments in the team,
maps the values for
X
to the value for
y
. There is a further dependence
operator in
LFD
, of form
\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi
, with the meaning that
\varphi
is true at
all assignments that agree with the current one on
X
.

Baltag and van Benthem provide a detailed study of many aspects of
LFD
.
In particular they prove that
LFD
can be embedded into classical first-order logic
(with the team of possible assignments as an additional predicate) and they view

LFD
as a
minimal
logic of functional dependence. They prove that the variant
of
LFD
without equality is decidable for satisfiability, but leave open the problem
whether this is also the case for
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
, the version with equality.
Towards the end of their paper, Baltag and van Benthem also study a local version
of
independence
, by atoms
\operatorname{Ind}_{X}(Y)
, saying that the values of the variables
in
X
at the current assignment
s
do not constrain the values of the variables
in
Y
: for any assignment
t
in the team there is one with the same
X
-values
as
s
and the same
Y
-values as
t
. Interestingly, this local notion of independence
is not symmetric:
\operatorname{Ind}_{X}(Y)
does not imply
\operatorname{Ind}_{Y}(X)
, contrary to the global
independence atoms
X\bot Y
in the independence logic of Grädel and Väänänen
[
10
]
.
Baltag and van Benthem also observe that this local independence logic is undecidable,
even without equality.

In this paper, we resolve some of the problems left open by Baltag and van Benthem.
In particular, we prove that
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
is undecidable, and we establish a
characterisation theorem via an appropriate notion of bisimulation.

Further, we study the idea of
localising
logics of dependence and
independence in a more systematic way. We consider local variants of all the
standard atomic dependency notions for teams; beyond dependence and independence,
this includes anonymity, inclusion, and exclusion. In this setting, it turns out
that anonymity is just the negation of dependence, and inclusion is the negation
of exclusion (which is not the case for the global team semantical variants of these atoms).
This also suggests to look more closely at the role of negation.
If, as in global logics of dependence and independence, we insist that formulae
are written in negation normal form, and that negation is applied to classical
atoms only, but not to atomic dependencies, then by considering local dependence
atoms
D_{X}y
and local anonymity atoms
\Upsilon_{X}y\equiv\neg D_{X}y
separately
we obtain two even “more minimal” logics than
LFD
, whose common extension coincides with
LFD
.

2
Localised variants of logics of dependence and independence

2.1
Global and local atomic properties of teams

A team
T
is a set of assignments
{s}:{\cal V}\rightarrow M
with a common domain
{\cal V}
of variables
and a common set of values
M
, typically the universe of a structure.
For a tuple
\bar{x}
of variables from
{\cal V}
we write
T(\bar{x})\coloneqq\{{s}(\bar{x})\mid{s}\in T\}

for the set of values of
\bar{x}
in the team
T
.
Whenever
{\cal V}
is finite, it is convenient to fix
some enumeration
\bar{v}
of
{\cal V}
so we can denote assignments
{s}

by their tuple of values
{\bar{{s}}}={s}(\bar{v})
.
Conversely, given some fitting tuple of values
\bar{a}
,
the notation
\bar{v}\mapsto\bar{a}
represents an assignment
{s}
with
{s}(\bar{v})=\bar{a}
.
This allows us to view
T
as a relation in the usual sense.

For the purpose of this paper, we call the standard team semantical atoms such as
dependence, independence, anonymity, inclusion and exclusion
global atoms
.
A global atom
\alpha
, over a set of variables
{\cal V}(\alpha)
defines,
for every set
M
of values, the extension
[\![\alpha]\!]^{M}
of all teams
T
such that

M\models_{T}\alpha
.
We require that the domain of
T
contains at least
{\cal V}(\alpha)
, and that the
truth of
\alpha
in
T
only depends on the variables in
{\cal V}(\alpha)
, i.e.,

M\models_{T}\alpha
if, and only if,
M\models_{T\restriction{\cal V}(\alpha)}\alpha
.
The most important global atoms are:

Dependence:

M\models_{T}\operatorname{dep}(\bar{x},y)

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{s}\in T)(\forall{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})\rightarrow{s}(y)={t}(y)
;

Inclusion:

M\models_{T}\bar{x}\subseteq\bar{y}

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{s}\in T)(\exists{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{y})
;

Exclusion:

M\models_{T}\bar{x}\mathbin{\operatorname{|}}\bar{y}

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{s}\in T)(\forall{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})\neq{t}(\bar{y})
;

Anonymity:

M\models_{T}\bar{x}\Upsilon y

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{s}\in T)(\exists{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})\land{s}(y)\neq{t}(y)
;

Independence:

M\models_{T}\bar{x}\perp\bar{y}

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{s},{t}\in T)(\exists{u}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={u}(\bar{x})\land{t}(\bar{y})={u}(\bar{y})
.

Notice that all these global atoms are defined by a universal expression of form

(\forall{s}\in T)\beta
where
\beta
is a statement about equalities and inequalities between
values of
{s}
and values of other
{t}\in T
.

A
local
team semantical atom
\beta
instead is evaluated on a local assignment
{s}

of some fixed underlying team
T
. Thus, the extension
[\![\beta]\!]^{M,T}
of
\beta

on a set
M
of values and a team
T
of assignments
{t}:{\cal V}\rightarrow M
, is
the set of all
{s}\in T
such that
(M,T)\models_{s}\beta
.
We say that
\beta
is the local variant of the global atom
\alpha
if, for any
set of values
M
and any team
T
, we have that

M\models_{T}\alpha\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (M,T)\models_{s}\beta\text{ for all }{s}\in T.

The following example gives a local variant of dependence.

Example 1
.

Consider the team over
{\cal V}=\{x,y,z\}
represented by the following table:

x

y

z

0

0

0

1

1

0

1

2

1

2

2

1

.

We say that
x
locally depends on
y
at the assignment

(1,1,0)
, because fixing
y
to be its value

in this specific assignment
,
also fixes
x
to its value in said assignment.
In other words, fixing
y
to
1
causes
x
to be fixed to 1.
As another example,
y
locally depends on
z

at the assignments
(1,2,1)
and
(2,2,1)
,
because
z=1
entails
y=2
in the above table.
However,
y
does not depend on
x
at
(1,1,0)
.

Notice that the global functional dependence
is the universal closure of this local dependence;

y
depends on
x
in the whole team if, and only if,

y
locally depends on
x
at every assignment in the team.

The local variants of the standard global dependency atoms are the following:

Dependence:

(M,T)\models_{s}D_{\bar{x}}y

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})\rightarrow{s}(y)={t}(y)
;

Inclusion:

(M,T)\models_{s}\bar{x}\in\bar{y}

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ {s}(\bar{x})\in T(\bar{y})
;

Exclusion:

(M,T)\models_{s}\bar{x}\notin\bar{y}

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ {s}(\bar{x})\notin T(\bar{y})
;

Anonymity:

(M,T)\models_{s}\Upsilon_{\bar{x}}y

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\exists{t}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})\land{s}(y)\neq{t}(y)
;

Independence:

(M,T)\models_{s}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{x}}(\bar{y})

\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\ (\forall{t}\in T)(\exists{u}\in T)\ {s}(\bar{x})={u}(\bar{x})\land{t}(\bar{y})={u}(\bar{y})
.

Notice that there are some striking differences between the properties of local and global
atoms. For the local atoms, anonymity and dependence, as well as inclusion and exclusion,
are directly related via negation, which is not the case for the global atoms.
Further, local independence is not symmetric in
\bar{x}
and
\bar{y}
.

2.2
The local dependence logic LFD

We now describe the
logic of functional dependence

LFD
, as introduced
by Baltag and van Benthem
[
3
]
, and recall the main results
they proved on this logic.
We shall then propose a slightly different presentation of local

For a tuple
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in A^{n}

we denote by
[\bar{a}]\coloneqq\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}

Given some function

f(\bar{a})\coloneqq(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{n}))

Definition 2

A type

\tau

{\cal V}

If both
\tau

A dependence model

consists of a
\tau

\mathfrak{M}
with universe

,
and a nonempty team

and co-domain

Pointed dependence models

Definition 3
.

The syntax of formulae in
{\textsf{LFD}}(\tau,{\cal V})
is given by

\varphi\quad\Coloneqq\quad R\bar{x}\quad\mid\quad D_{X}y\quad\mid\quad\lnot\varphi\quad\mid\quad\varphi\land\varphi\quad\mid\quad\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi,

R\in\tau

\bar{x}
is a tuple of variables of appropriate length,

X\subseteq{\cal V}
is
finite
, and

.
Further,
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
is the extension of
LFD
by equality atoms

. We shall also make use of the
following notations:

•

The dual of
\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi
is defined as
\mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi\coloneqq\lnot\mathsf{D}_{X}\lnot\varphi
.

•

For the special case

\QGA\varphi\coloneqq\mathsf{D}_{\varnothing}\varphi
and
\QGE\varphi\coloneqq\mathsf{E}_{\varnothing}\varphi
.

•

We use
\mathsf{D}_{\bar{x}}\varphi\coloneqq\mathsf{D}_{[\bar{x}]}\varphi

and likewise for the other quantifiers.

We refer to the
\mathsf{D}_{X}
and
\mathsf{E}_{X}
as dependence quantifiers or modalities of
LFD
,
and also call
\QGA
and
\QGE
global modalities.
As we will often deal with dependence on
sets
of variables,
we will use the notation
{s}=_{X}{t}
whenever the assignments
{s},{t}\in T
agree
on the set of variables
X\subseteq{\cal V}
, i.e.
{s}(x)={t}(x)
for all

.
Note that
{=_{\varnothing}}=T\times T
.

Definition 4
.

The semantics of
LFD
and
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
on a dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is defined by the usual rules
for atomic formulae and connectives together with

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}D_{X}y

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\qquad{t}=_{X}{s}\text{ implies }{t}=_{y}{s}\text{ for all ${t}\in T$},

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi\text{ for all ${t}\in T$ with ${t}=_{X}{s}$}.

Obviously,
\mathsf{E}_{X},\QGE,\QGA
then have the expected semantics

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi\text{ for some ${t}\in T$ with ${t}=_{X}{s}$},

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\QGE\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi\text{ for some ${t}\in T$},

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\QGA\varphi

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi\text{ for all ${t}\in T$}.

Of course,
\mathsf{D}_{X}
and
\mathsf{E}_{X}
are variants of the traditional quantifiers
\forall
and

\exists
, and we briefly want to justify the use of these new quantifiers instead of the
classical ones.
The main point is that the reasoning about free and bound variables becomes more transparent.
Indeed, it is a desirable feature that the meaning of a formula should only depend on its free
variables, in the sense that if
{s}=_{\Free(\varphi)}{t}
then it should be the case that

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi
if, and only if,
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi
.
By defining
\Free(D_{X}y)=\Free(\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi)=X
, this is easily seen to be the case.
Using a naive interpretation of traditional quantifiers, saying that
\forall y\varphi
holds
for the assignment
{s}
if, and only if
\varphi
holds for all assignments
{t}

that agree with
{s}
on all variables except
y

behaviour

that
\forall y\varphi

Indeed, it is easy to construct a team in which the formula

On the other side, Baltag and van Benthem showed that such

,
where
\bar{z}

The semantics of

Indeed, notice the similarities to the modalities

\mathsf{D}_{X}

In this sense,

.
Note that global functional dependence

We shall see that there are important differences between
LFD
and
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
.

Let

2.3
A general definition for localised logics

To consider logics based on other local atoms than the local dependency atom,
we generalise the logic
LFD
described in the last subsection.
We will use a basic sublogic
L
of relational atoms and boolean connectives.
This is extended by our local atoms and the dependence quantifiers
\mathsf{D}_{X}
and
\mathsf{E}_{X}

introduced in the last section.
The main difference is that we will allow negation only on relational atoms,
to further differentiate between different local atoms.

Now let
\Omega\subseteq\{D,\Upsilon,=,\neq,\in,\not\in,\operatorname{Ind},\dots\}

be a collection of atomic operators, which, applied to appropriate tuples
of variables from
{\cal V}
, define local atoms
\beta(\bar{x})
such as

x=y
,
x\neq y
,
D_{\bar{x}}y
,
\Upsilon_{\bar{x}}y
,
\bar{x}\in\bar{y}
,
\bar{x}\not\in\bar{y}
,
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{x}}\bar{y}
, etc.
We denote the collection of such atoms by
\Omega[{\cal V}]
.

Definition 6
.

The syntax of formulae in the local team logic
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
of type
(\tau,{\cal V}
) is given by

\varphi\quad\Coloneqq R\bar{x}\ \mid\ \neg R\bar{x}\ \mid\ \beta(\bar{x})\ \mid\ \varphi\lor\varphi\ \mid\ \varphi\land\varphi\ \mid\ \mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi\ \mid\ \mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi

where
R\in\tau
is a relation symbol,

X\subseteq{\cal V}
is finite,
\bar{x}
is a tuple of variables in
{\cal V}
of appropriate length,
and
\beta(\bar{x})\in\Omega[{\cal V}]
.
Formulae are evaluated on dependence models
(\mathfrak{M},T)
consisting of
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{M}
and a
team
T
with domain
{\cal V}
and values in
\mathfrak{M}
, locally at some assignment
{s}\in T
.
The rules to determine whether
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi
, for
\varphi\in L[\Omega]
extend
the truth definitions for
\tau

and
\neg R\bar{x}
,
and for local team atoms

by the standard rules for boolean connectives and
the rules for dependence quantifiers
\mathsf{D}_{X},\mathsf{E}_{X}
as in
\cref

The free variables of

are defined as usual.
For the other atoms,
we set
\Free(D_{X}y)=X
,

\Free(\bar{x}\in\bar{y})=[\bar{x}]
,
and
\Free(\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{x}}(\bar{y}))=[\bar{x}]
,
where the negations of these atoms have the same free variables.

We shall also use the operators
\QGA
and
\QGE
explained above.
Note that we can now state our definition that some atom
\beta
is the local
variant of a global atom
\alpha
via

M\models_{T}\alpha\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(M,T)\models\QGA\beta\quad\mathbin{:\,\Longleftrightarrow}\quad(M,T)\models_{s}\beta\text{ for all }{s}\in T.

We will thus allow ourselves to use
\alpha
in
{\textsf{L}}[\beta]
,
with the intended semantics
\alpha\equiv\QGA\beta
, e.g. we can write
\operatorname{dep}(x,y)
instead
of
\QGA D_{x}y
within
{\textsf{L}}[D]
.

Obviously, the logics
LFD
and
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
of Baltag and van Benthem
are equivalent to
{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
and
{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,=,\neq]
, respectively.

2.4
The standard translation into first-order logic

A dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)
of type
(\tau,{\cal V})
with finite
{\cal V}
,
enumerated as
{\cal V}=\{v_{1}\dots,v_{n}\}
, can be viewed as a structure of vocabulary
\tau\cup\{T\}
,
expanding
\mathfrak{M}
by a predicate (of arity
|{\cal V}|=n
) for the team.
It is not difficult to see that, for any of the logics
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
described above,
there is straightforward translation that associates with every formula

\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
of type
(\tau,{\cal V})
an equivalent formula
\varphi^{*}\in{\textsf{FO}}

of vocabulary
\tau\cup\{T\}
, which means that,
for all
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and all
{s}\in T
, we have that

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\mathfrak{M},T)\models\varphi^{*}({s}(\bar{v}))

Notice that the semantics on the left side is the semantics of

{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
whereas on the right side we have the
standard Tarski semantics of first-order logic.

For the case of
LFD
and
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
this has been called
the
standard translation
in
[
3
]
,
and it is a straightforward generalisation of the translations of modal logics into first-order logic.
The rules of the translation are trivial for
\tau
-literals and Boolean connectives.
Quantifiers are translated via relativisation to the team predicate.
Given a formula
\varphi
,
X\subseteq{\cal V}
and a tuple
\bar{z}
enumerating
{\cal V}\setminus X

(in particular,
\bar{z}
is a subtuple of
\bar{v}
), we put

(\mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi)^{*}:=\exists\bar{z}(T\bar{v}\land\varphi^{*})\quad\text{ and }\quad(\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi)^{*}:=\forall\bar{z}(T\bar{v}\rightarrow\varphi^{*}).

It remains to provide translations for the local team atoms
\beta(\bar{x})\in\Omega[{\cal V}]
.
The translation preserves their free variables.
For ease of notation, we explicitly give the translations just for atoms with two variables

v_{i},v_{j}
from
{\cal V}
. It is obvious that the translations generalise to arbitrary tuples.
Let
\bar{z},\bar{z}^{\prime}
be distinct copies of
\bar{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
.
For
j\leq n
, let
\bar{z}_{-j}
denote the tuple obtained by omitting
z_{j}
, and
let
\bar{z}[j\mapsto y]=(z_{1},\dots,z_{j-1},y,z_{j+1},\dots,z_{n})
. Then we set

\displaystyle(v_{i}\in v_{j})^{*}

\displaystyle:=\exists\bar{z}(T\bar{z}\land z_{j}=v_{i}),

\displaystyle(D_{v_{i}}v_{j})^{*}

\displaystyle:=\forall\bar{z}_{-i}\forall\bar{z}^{\prime}_{-i}((T\bar{z}[i\mapsto v_{i}]\land T\bar{z}^{\prime}[i\mapsto v_{i}])\to z_{j}=z^{\prime}_{j}),

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Ind}_{v_{i}}v_{j})^{*}

\displaystyle:=\forall\bar{z}(T\bar{z}\rightarrow\exists\bar{z}^{\prime}(T\bar{z}^{\prime}\land z^{\prime}_{i}=v_{i}\land z^{\prime}_{j}=z_{j})).

For the negations of these atoms, we consider the negation of their translation,
so for example
(v_{i}\notin v_{j})^{*}=\lnot(v_{i}\in v_{j})^{*}
.

Thus, the local logics of dependence and independence
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
can all be considered
as fragments of first-order logic. We remark that this restricted expressive power, compared to
the global logics of dependence and independence which are fragments of existential second-order logic,
is not just due to the localisation of the team atoms.
Indeed, as we have seen, the global dependency atoms are easily expressible in the local logics,
via a further universal quantification with
\QGA
. But the localisation of the dependencies,
together with the global restriction of the available assignments to a
fixed team
,
permits to evaluate these logics by first-order rules,
as opposed to the inherent second-order operations in team semantics
such as the decomposition of teams, and their Skolem extensions.
In the next sections, we explore whether this more limited expressiveness is balanced by the benefits
of better algorithmic manageability and convenient model-theoretic properties.

3
Decidability and undecidability

We now want to discuss the question which of the logics
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
are decidable for
satisfiability. An obvious road for proving this is to show that the standard translation puts
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

into a known decidable fragment of
FO
. This works in some cases, but not always, and
in particular,
LFD
seems to resist such an approach.
By different methods, Baltag and van Benthem have shown that
LFD
is decidable, and have
formulated the corresponding question for
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
as an open problem. We shall prove below that

{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
is undecidable.

3.1
Embeddings into the guarded fragment

A known decidable fragment of
FO
is the guarded fragment
GF
.
In general, guarded logics arise as a natural generalization of modal logics.
Consider the standard translation
\varphi\mapsto\varphi^{*}(x)

that identifies propositional modal logic
ML

with the modal fragment of
FO
, by rewriting modal operators as relativised
quantifiers:

(\square\varphi)^{*}=\forall y(Exy\rightarrow\varphi^{*}(y))\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\lozenge\varphi)^{*}=\exists y(Exy\land\varphi^{*})).

The guarded fragment
GF
, introduced in
[
1
]
,
generalises this idea to a much more powerful setting of first-order logic with
an arbitrary relational vocabulary and an arbitrary number of variables.
It lifts all restrictions of the modal fragment, except for the
requirement that all quantifiers must be relativised (guarded) by some atom
that contains all free variables of the quantified formula.
More formally,
GF
is the smallest fragment of relational
FO
generated
from atomic formulae by propositional connectives and guarded quantification:
if
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is a formula in
GF
and
\alpha(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is an atomic formula
that contains all free variables of
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, then also

\forall\bar{y}(\alpha(\bar{x},\bar{y})\rightarrow\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists\bar{y}(\alpha(\bar{x},\bar{y})\land\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y}))

are formulae of
GF
.

The guarded fragment preserves, and to some degree explains, many of the good
model-theoretic and algorithmic properties of modal logics.
In particular,
GF
is decidable
[
1
]
and indeed has the finite model property

[
12
]
: every satisfiable formula of
GF
has a finite model.

If we consider the standard translation of the logics
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
into
FO
, we see that
that the translations of the logical operators preserve guardedness, so the questions is
just, which of the local atoms
\beta(\bar{x})
can be rewritten as a guarded formula.
Our previous first-order translation of the local inclusion and exclusion atoms
can be rewritten as

(v_{i}\in v_{j})^{*}:=\exists\bar{z}_{-j}T\bar{z}[j\mapsto v_{i}]\quad\text{ and }\quad(v_{i}\not\in v_{j})^{*}:=\forall\bar{z}_{-j}(T\bar{z}[j\mapsto v_{i}]\rightarrow\bot)

which are guarded formulae.

Proposition 7
.

The local variant of inclusion-exclusion logic
{\textsf{L}}[=,\neq,\in,\not\in]

is a fragment of
GF
. In particular, it has the finite model property
(and is therefore decidable for satisfiability and validity).

Recall that the global inclusion-exclusion logic, instead,
has the full power of independence logic and
\Sigma^{1}_{1}
.
The standard translations of local dependence, local anonymity,
and local independence are, however, not guarded.
For local independence logic, a straightforward argument shows that even without equality,
one can enforce cartesian products within the assignment space,
leading to the expressive power of usual first-order logic.
Hence
{\textsf{L}}[\operatorname{Ind}]
is already undecidable, and can therefore not be embedded into
GF
.
The relationship of
LFD
with the guarded fragment,
or other guarded logics, is more difficult to analyse;
we shall address this issue in
\cref
sec:comp:gf.

3.2
Other decidability arguments

Proposition 8
.

{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,\neq,\notin]
is decidable for satisfiability.

Proof.

Given a dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)
we denote by
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})

the corresponding
LFD
-equivalent variable-distinguished (v.d.) model,
as described in
\cref
fact:all-dist.
We first prove via induction on
\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,\neq,\notin]
that
whenever
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi
, we also have
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\varphi
.

If
\varphi\in\{\bar{x}\neq\bar{y},\ \bar{x}\notin\bar{y}\}
and
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi
, then
we know that
\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}

as tuples of variables
,
so obviously
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\varphi
.
If otherwise
\varphi\in L[D,\Upsilon]\equiv{\textsf{LFD}}
,
this follows from
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{LFD}}}(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
.
The induction step for boolean connectives
\land
and
\lor
is clear.
Also, note that if
{s},{t}\in T
, then we have

{s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})
if and only if
{s^{\prime}}(\bar{x})={t^{\prime}}(\bar{x})
.
Hence the induction steps for the quantifiers
\mathsf{E}_{\bar{x}}\varphi
and
\mathsf{D}_{\bar{x}}\varphi

are straightforward:
If
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi
and
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
with
{t^{\prime}}=_{X}{s^{\prime}}
,
then also
{t}=_{X}{s}
, so
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{t}\varphi
. By induction hypothesis we obtain

(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{t^{\prime}}}\varphi
. Since
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
with
{t^{\prime}}=_{X}{s^{\prime}}
was arbitrary,
we obtain
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{s^{\prime}}\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi
. The induction step for
\mathsf{E}_{X}
is analogous.
This concludes the induction.

In the following, let
[\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}]=\top
if
\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}
(as tuples of variables),
and otherwise
[\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}]=\bot
.
Then, since
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
is v.d., we have

(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}[\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}].

An analogue holds for
\bar{x}\notin\bar{y}
, so that over the class of v.d. dependence models,
we have
\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}\equiv\bar{x}\notin\bar{y}\equiv[\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}]
,
and thereby
{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,\neq,\notin]\equiv{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]\equiv{\textsf{LFD}}
.
Finally, since the v.d. model is
LFD
-equivalent to the original,
we see that
LFD
-formulae are satisfiable if and only if they have a v.d. model.
Given
\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,\neq,\notin]
, let
\varphi^{\prime}
denote the
LFD
-formula
obtained from
\varphi
by replacing all occurrences of
\bar{x}\neq\bar{y}
or
\bar{x}\notin\bar{y}
.
Then

\displaystyle\varphi\text{ satisfiable}

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi\text{ for some pointed dependence model }(\mathfrak{M},T),s

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\varphi

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\varphi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\varphi^{\prime}\text{ satisfiable}.

Thus we have constructed a satisfiability-preserving reduction from

{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,\neq,\notin]
to
LFD
.
Since
LFD
is known to be decidable for satisfiability
[
3
]
, this concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Undecidability: dependence together with equality or inclusion

We now solve a main open problem from
[
3
]
by proving that

{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
is undecidable.
In fact, we will prove that
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
is undecidable
and then show how to adapt the argument to
{\textsf{L}}[D,=]
,
which is contained in
{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon,=,\neq]\equiv{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
.
The crucial reason for the undecidability is that,
in the presence of either equality or inclusion,
no analogue of
\cref
fact:all-dist can be established,
and we can use these atoms to copy values between variables,
while keeping certain other values fixed.
We demonstrate this idea for inclusion:

Example 9
.

Consider a dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)
over variables
{\cal V}=\{x,y,z\}

such that
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models xyx\subseteq xyz
, i.e.
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\QGA(xyx\in xyz)
,
meaning
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}xyx\in xyz
for all
{s}\in T
.
Then, given some
{s}\in T
with
{s}(x,y,z)=(a,b,c)
, there exists
{t}\in T

such that
{t}(x,y,z)=(a,b,a)
. In this sense, we copied the value
a
of
x

to the variable
z
, while keeping the value of the variables
x
and
y
fixed.

We recall some basic notions concerning the classical decision
problem for first-order logic.
For details we refer to
[
6
, Chapter 3.1]
.
A syntactic fragment
F\subseteq{\textsf{FO}}
is called a

conservative reduction class
if there exists a conservative
reduction
f:{\textsf{FO}}\rightarrow F
, i.e. a computable function
that preserves satisfiability and finite satisfiability in both directions.
If
F
is a conservative reduction class, then
by Trakhtenbrot’s Theorem, the satisfiability and finite satisfiability problems for

F
are undecidable.

One of the classical conservative reduction classes is
the Kahr-Class, denoted
[\forall\exists\forall,(\omega,1)]
,
which consists of the sentences of form
\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi(x,y,z)

where
\varphi
is quantifier-free, without equality, and
which may only use a single binary relation but an unbounded number of monadic ones.

We shall construct a conservative reduction from the Kahr-Class
into fragments of
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
and
{\textsf{L}}[D,=]
.
We require only: four variables,
one binary predicate and an unbounded number of monadic ones,
one dependence atom (used positively), and six inclusions/equalities (also used positively).

Theorem 10
.

The four-variable fragments of
{\textsf{L}}[=,D]
and
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
are conservative reduction classes.
In particular, the satisfiability, validity and finite satisfiability problems for
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}
are undecidable.

Proof.

We first prove the claim for
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
, and then show how to adapt the proof
to
{\textsf{L}}[D,=]
. Since the Kahr-Class is a conservative reduction class, it suffices,
by transitivity, to exhibit a conservative reduction
\psi\mapsto\psi^{*}

from
[\forall\exists\forall,(\omega,1)]
to
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
.

Notice that, for
\psi=\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi(x,y,z)
in the Kahr-Class,
the quantifier-free part
\varphi
is in the base logic
L
. We define

\psi^{*}\coloneqq\QGA\varphi(x,y,z)\land\operatorname{dep}(x,y)\land\bigwedge_{i=0}^{5}\vartheta_{i}.

The subformulae
\vartheta_{i}
allow us to copy the value of a variable
to another,
while keeping certain other variables fixed
,
as demonstrated in the example above.
This is used to enforce a cartesian product
included in
T(x,z)
for the dependence models
(\mathfrak{M},T)
,
allowing us to retrieve a classical model from them.
We require an extra variable
v
as an additional
“temporary storage” to copy values between variables.
So while we keep
\varphi
to contain only the variables
x,y,z
,
overall we will need four variables, whose order we fix to
x,y,z,v
.
The
\vartheta_{i}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\vartheta_{0}\coloneqq xx\subseteq xz\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $x$ to $z$, keeping $x$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}\coloneqq yyv\subseteq yzv\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $z$, keeping $y,v$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{2}\coloneqq xzx\subseteq xzv\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $x$ to $v$, keeping $x,z$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{3}\coloneqq yzy\subseteq yzv\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $v$, keeping $y,z$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{4}\coloneqq zzv\subseteq xzv\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $z$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{5}\coloneqq vzv\subseteq xzv\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $v$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$}).

Since
\operatorname{dep}(x,y)\equiv\QGA D_{x}y
and analogously

\bar{x}\subseteq\bar{y}\equiv\QGA(\bar{x}\in\bar{y})
it follows that
\psi^{*}
indeed
is in the four-variable fragment of
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
.

We have to prove the following two claims:

1.

A (finite) model of
\psi
induces a (finite) dependence model of
\psi^{*}
.claim]clm:cons-red:1

2.

A (finite) dependence model of
\psi^{*}
induces a (finite) model of
\psi
.claim]clm:cons-red:2

To prove
\cref
clm:cons-red:1,
assume that we have a model
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi
with universe
A
.
Thus there exists a function
f\colon A\to A
such that

{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi(a,fa,b)
for all
a,b\in A
.
We construct the dependence model
({\mathfrak{A}},T)
with team
T
given by

T\coloneqq\{(a,fa,b,c)\mid a,b,c\in A\}.

Remember that we denote assignments by their tuple of values,
so
(a,fa,b,c)
represents the assignment
(x,y,z,v)\mapsto(a,fa,b,c)
.
It is clear that
y
globally depends on
x
, i.e.
({\mathfrak{A}},T)\models\operatorname{dep}(x,y)
,
and that by the choice of
T
we also have
({\mathfrak{A}},T)\models\QGA\varphi(x,y,z)
.
The
\vartheta_{i}
are satisfied in
(\mathfrak{M},T)
, since
T(x,z,v)=A^{3}

is a cartesian product of the whole universe.
Overall, we obtain
({\mathfrak{A}},T)\models\psi^{*}
.
Clearly, if
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a finite model, then
({\mathfrak{A}},T)
is finite as well.
This completes the proof of
\cref
clm:cons-red:1.

For the converse,
\cref
clm:cons-red:2,
suppose that we have a dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)
such that
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\psi^{*}
.
Because of the global dependence
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\operatorname{dep}(x,y)
there exists a function

f\colon T(x)\to T(y)
such that
{t}(y)=f({t}(x))
for all
{t}\in T
.
Note that
T(y)\subseteq T(x)
, since
\vartheta_{1}
and
\vartheta_{4}

allow us to copy values from
y
to
z
and from there to
x
.
Hence we have
f\colon T(x)\to T(x)
, i.e. we can iterate
f
on values of
x
.
Fix some arbitrary
{s}\in T
and set
\underline{0}\coloneqq{s}(x)
, as well as

\underline{i}\coloneqq f^{i}\underline{0}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

We construct a model for
\psi
by
{\mathfrak{A}}\coloneqq\mathfrak{M}\upharpoonright A

where
A\coloneqq\{\underline{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
.
The function
f\upharpoonright A\colon A\to A
plays the role of the Skolem function
for
y
in the quantification
\forall x\exists y\forall z
of
\psi
.
We need to ensure that
\varphi(a,fa,b)
holds in
\mathfrak{M}
(and thus in
{\mathfrak{A}}
)
for all
a,b\in A
.

Since
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\QGA\varphi(x,y,z)
we know that
\mathfrak{M}\models\varphi({t}(x),f({t}(x)),{t}(z))

for all
{t}\in T
.
Hence it suffices to show that

A\times A\subseteq T(x,z).

(1)

In the following we write
*
as placeholder for not further specified elements of
\mathfrak{M}
.
The expression
{t}\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{i}}{u}
for some
{t}\in T

denotes that the existence of

{u}\in T
follows by applying the “copy-rule” which
\vartheta_{i}
represents.
Notice that for all
{t}\in T
with
{t}(x)=\underline{i}
we have

{t}(y)=f(\underline{i})=\underline{i+1}
.
In particular, keeping the value of
x
implies keeping the value of
y
.

1.

(\underline{0},\underline{0})\in T(x,z)
:

We know that
{s}
looks like
(\underline{0},\underline{1},*,*)
.
Since
{s}\in T
and

{s}=(\underline{0},\underline{1},*,*)\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{0}}(\underline{0},\underline{1},\underline{0},*)\eqqcolon{t}\qquad\qquad\text{(copy $x$ to $z$, keeping $x,y$)}

we see that
{t}\in T
with
{t}(x,z)=(\underline{0},\underline{0})
.

2.

If
(\underline{0},\underline{j})\in T(x,z)
, then also
(\underline{0},\underline{j+1})\in T(x,z)
:

By assumption we have
{t}=(\underline{0},\underline{1},\underline{j},*)\in T
.
Together with the derivation

\displaystyle{t}=\

\displaystyle(\underline{0},\underline{1},\underline{j},*)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{2}}\quad

\displaystyle(\underline{0},\underline{1},\underline{j},\underline{0})

\displaystyle(\text{copy $x$ to $v$, keeping $x,y,z$})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{4}}\quad

\displaystyle(\underline{j},\underline{j+1},\underline{j},\underline{0})

\displaystyle(\text{copy $z$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{1}}\quad

\displaystyle(*,\underline{j+1},\underline{j+1},\underline{0})

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $z$, keeping $y,v$})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{5}}\quad

\displaystyle(\underline{0},\underline{1},\underline{j+1},\underline{0})\eqqcolon{u}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $v$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$})

we obtain
{u}\in T
with
{u}(x,z)=(\underline{0},\underline{j+1})
.

3.

If
(\underline{i},\underline{j})\in T(x,z)
then also
(\underline{i+1},\underline{j})\in T(x,z)
:

By assumption we have
{t}=(\underline{i},\underline{i+1},\underline{j},*)\in T
.
Together with the derivation

\displaystyle{t}=\

\displaystyle(\underline{i},\underline{i+1},\underline{j},*)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{3}}\quad

\displaystyle(*,\underline{i+1},\underline{j},\underline{i+1})

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $v$, keeping $y,z$})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\vartheta_{5}}\quad

\displaystyle(\underline{i+1},\underline{i+2},\underline{j},\underline{i+1})\eqqcolon{u}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $v$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$})

we obtain
{u}\in T
with
{u}(x,z)=(\underline{i+1},\underline{j})
.

Now the inclusion (
1
) follows via induction.
By the above argument this proves that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi(a,fa,b)

for all
a,b\in A
and hence
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi(x,y,z)
.
Again it is clear that if
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is finite, then so is
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
This concludes the proof of
\cref
clm:cons-red:2,
showing that the four-variable fragment of
{\textsf{L}}[D,\in]
is a conservative reduction class.

For the case of
{\textsf{L}}[D,=]
, note first that for any dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T)

with
{s}\in T
and non-empty tuples of variables
\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z}
with
[\bar{y}]\subseteq[\bar{x}]
, we have

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\bar{x}\bar{y}\in\bar{x}\bar{z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\mathsf{E}_{\bar{x}\bar{y}}(\bar{y}=\bar{z})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\mathsf{E}_{\bar{x}}(\bar{y}=\bar{z}).

Thus, if
[\bar{y}]\subseteq[\bar{x}]
,
we have
\bar{x}\bar{y}\subseteq\bar{x}\bar{z}\equiv\QGA\mathsf{E}_{\bar{x}}(\bar{y}=\bar{z})
.
In our case, all inclusion atoms can be written in this form.
Hence, we can define equivalent formulae in
{\textsf{L}}[D,=]
:

\displaystyle\vartheta_{0}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{x}(x=z)\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{copy $x$ to $z$, keeping $x$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{yv}(y=z)

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $z$, keeping $y,v$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{2}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{xz}(x=v)

\displaystyle(\text{copy $x$ to $v$, keeping $x,z$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{3}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{yz}(y=v)

\displaystyle(\text{copy $y$ to $v$, keeping $y,z$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{4}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{zv}(z=x)

\displaystyle(\text{copy $z$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$}),

\displaystyle\vartheta_{5}\coloneqq\QGA\mathsf{E}_{zv}(v=x)

\displaystyle(\text{copy $v$ to $x$, keeping $z,v$}).

The proof then works in the exact same way as before.
∎

Classification by Decidability: Conclusion

Consider the lattice of the local logics using the atoms

4
Bisimulation and Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theory for LFD

We define a notion of bisimulation for local dependence logics
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

and in particular for
LFD
and
{\textsf{LFD}}^{=}

in such a way that we obtain an analogue of the classical
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Theorem in
\cref
sec:bisim:ef,
and later an analogue of van Benthems’s Theorem in
\cref
sec:comp:fo:char.
In the following, many results will require that
\Omega
and hence
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

is closed under negation.
We shall also consider infinitary variants of these logics.

4.1
Bisimulation

Definition 11

(Bisimulation)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
be two dependence models of the same
type
(\tau,{\cal V})
. A binary relation
Z\subseteq T\times T^{\prime}

is an
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimulation between
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
if for all
({s},{s^{\prime}})\in Z
:

1.

(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
agree on the atoms of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
:

(a)

For all
R\in\tau
and
\bar{x}\in{\cal V}^{\ar(R)}
we have

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}R\bar{x}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{s^{\prime}}R\bar{x}
.

(b)

For all local atoms
\beta\in\Omega[{\cal V}]

we have
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\beta\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{s^{\prime}}\beta
.

2.

(back) For all
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
and all
finite

X\subseteq{t^{\prime}}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s^{\prime}}\coloneqq\{x\in{\cal V}\mid{t^{\prime}}=_{x}{s^{\prime}}\}

there is some
{t}\in T
with
({t},{t^{\prime}})\in Z
and
{t}=_{X}{s}
.

3.

(forth) For all
{t}\in T
and all
finite

X\subseteq{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}\coloneqq\{x\in{\cal V}\mid{t}=_{x}{s}\}

there is some
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
with
({t},{t^{\prime}})\in Z
and
{t^{\prime}}=_{X}{s^{\prime}}
.

We restrict ourselves to finite sets
X\subseteq{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}

because
L
only allows finite sets within our modalities
\mathsf{D}_{X}
and
\mathsf{E}_{X}
.
Whenever
(\mathfrak{M},T),(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
and
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
are clear from context, we write

{s}\sim{s^{\prime}}
if there exists an
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimulation

Z
between
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
with
({s},{s^{\prime}})\in Z
.

Definition 12

(Ordinal approximations to bisimulation)
.

We write
{s}\sim^{0}{s^{\prime}}
and say that
{s}
and
{s^{\prime}}
are
\alpha
-bisimilar
if
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
agree on
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-atoms.
Now define
{s}\sim^{\alpha}{s^{\prime}}
for ordinals
\alpha\in\mathrm{On}
by induction;
when defining
{s}\sim^{\alpha+1}{s^{\prime}}
,
we require the conditions

•

(\alpha+1)
-back:
For all
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
and all
finite

X\subseteq{t^{\prime}}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s^{\prime}}
there exists some
{t}\in T

with
{t}\sim^{\alpha}{t^{\prime}}
and
{t}=_{X}{s}
.

•

(\alpha+1)
-forth:
For all
{t}\in T
and all
finite

X\subseteq{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}
there exists some
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}

with
{t}\sim^{\alpha}{t^{\prime}}
and
{t^{\prime}}=_{X}{s^{\prime}}
.

For limit ordinals
\lambda
,
we say that
{s}\sim^{\lambda}{s^{\prime}}
if

{s}\sim^{\alpha}{s^{\prime}}
for all
\alpha<\lambda
,
so essentially

{\sim^{\lambda}}=\bigcap_{\alpha<\lambda}{\sim^{\alpha}}.

(2)

As usual,
\alpha
-bisimilarity implies

\beta
-bisimilarity for all
\beta<\alpha
.
Furthermore, full bisimilarity is now simply given by

{\sim}=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\mathrm{On}}{\sim^{\alpha}}
.

We want to emphasize that the back and forth
conditions of our bisimulations do not require the regarded assignments
{t}
or
{t^{\prime}}

to actually agree on any variables with
{s}
or
{s^{\prime}}
respectively,
i.e.
{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}
and
{t^{\prime}}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s^{\prime}}
may be empty.
The reason for this is that
L
has the global modalities

\QGA=\mathsf{D}_{\varnothing}
and
\QGE=\mathsf{E}_{\varnothing}

and that we want bisimilarity to correspond to logical equivalence
(we say that some modality is global if its corresponding accessibility relation is the all-relation,
i.e. contains all possible pairs of objects, as is the case for
=_{\varnothing}
on teams).
As an example, let
{\cal V}
be finite and

\psi\coloneqq\QGE(Rx)\land\bigwedge_{v\in{\cal V}}\lnot\mathsf{E}_{v}Rx.

Then
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\psi
means that there is some

{t}\in T
with
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{{t}}Rx
,
but that there is no
{u}\in T
with
u=_{x}s
having this property,
and thus
{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}=\varnothing
.
Since
L
is able to witness this
{t}
, it is natural
to require a bisimilar
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
for
every

{t}\in T
,
and not just for those
{t}
that agree with
{s}
on some variable.
Likewise for the back condition.

This forces every bisimulation to be
global
,
meaning that every assignment in
T

is bisimilar to at least one assignment in
T^{\prime}
, and vice versa.
This is a common consequence of having global modalities;
in the context of, say,
ML
with an explicitly added global modality,
often denoted
{\textsf{ML}}(\forall)
, the canonical bisimulation is just the global version of
ordinary
ML
-bisimulation
[
7
]
.

Since we already defined bisimulation for infinite ordinals,
the corresponding step for our logics is to consider their infinitary variants.
We briefly give some routine definitions which we need in what follows.

Definition 13

(
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}
)
.

The infinitary extension
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}
of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
allows
conjunction and disjunction over arbitrarily large sets of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}
-formulae,
with the obvious semantics.

Definition 14

(Quantifier Rank)
.

The quantifier rank
\qr(\varphi)
of some
\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}
is a
recursively defined ordinal. We define
\qr(\beta)=0
for atoms
\beta

(including negated relational atoms and the local atoms),
as well as
\qr(\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi)=\qr(\mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi)=\qr(\varphi)+1
.
Lastly, we set

\qr\left(\bigwedge_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}\right)=\qr\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}\right)=\sup_{i\in I}\qr(\varphi_{i})
.

Definition 15

(Equivalence)
.

We use the usual symbols
\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}
(
\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\alpha}
)
for equivalence of pointed dependence models in the logic
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

(up to quantifier rank
\alpha
).
We also use the short form
{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\alpha}{s^{\prime}}
whenever
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}

are clear from context.
The infinitary case
{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}_{\infty}
is defined analogously, but we often
write
\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\infty}
instead of
\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}}
.

4.2
An Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Theorem

Given a logic, a common goal is to find a correspondence
between logical indistinguishability and behavioural
equivalence in some structural form,
often as a relation akin to bisimulation, a collection of partial isomorphisms,
or a winning strategy of certain two-player games.
For
FO
we have back-and-forth systems and Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games (cf.
[
15
, Chapter 3.3]
),
whereas for
ML
one has ordinary bisimulation
and the corresponding bisimulation games (cf.
[
5
, Chapter 2.2]
).
The following results and in particular
\cref
thm:bisim
show that the bisimulations defined above
fulfill such a role for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
whenever
\Omega
is closed under negation.

Lemma 16
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
be a dependence model of some finite type
(\tau,{\cal V})
and
\Omega
be finite
and closed under negation.
For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a formula
\chi^{k}_{s}\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})

of quantifier rank
k
that defines the

\sim^{k}
-class of
{s}
, so that for all suitable
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}

(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{s^{\prime}}\chi^{k}_{{s}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s^{\prime}}\sim^{k}{s}

Up to
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-equivalence,
the number of such
\chi^{k}_{{s}}
is finite and depends only on
\tau,{\cal V},\Omega
and
k
.

Proof.

Since
\tau,{\cal V}
and
\Omega
are finite,
there are up to equivalence only finitely many formulae

\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
with
\qr(\varphi)=0
,
which allows us to define

\chi^{0}_{{s}}\coloneqq\bigwedge\{\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})\mid\qr(\varphi)=0,\ (\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi\}.

One proceeds inductively by defining

\chi_{{s}}^{k+1}\coloneqq\varphi^{k+1}_{\text{back}}\land\varphi^{k+1}_{\text{forth}}
, where

\varphi_{\text{back}}^{k+1}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{X\subseteq{\cal V}}\mathsf{D}_{X}\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}{t}\in T\\
{t}=_{X}{s}\end{subarray}}\chi^{k}_{{t}}\quad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\quad\varphi_{\text{forth}}^{k+1}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{{t}\in T}\ \bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq{\cal V}\\[1.0pt]
{t}=_{X}{s}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{E}_{X}\chi^{k}_{{t}}.

By the induction hypothesis, these are well-defined
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
-formulae
and correspond precisely to the
(k+1)
-back and
(k+1)
-forth
conditions as given in
\cref
def:bisim-approx.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
be a dependence model of some type
(\tau,{\cal V})
and
\Omega
be closed under negation.
For every
\alpha\in\mathrm{On}
there exists a formula
\chi^{\alpha}_{s}\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})

of quantifier rank
\alpha
that defines the

\sim^{\alpha}
-class of
{s}
, so that for all suitable
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}

(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{s^{\prime}}\chi^{\alpha}_{{s}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s^{\prime}}\sim^{\alpha}{s}.

Proof.

For
\alpha=0
and successor ordinals,
we use analogous definitions to the ones above,
except that we have to explicitly consider only
finite

X\subseteq{\cal V}

as they are used within the quantifiers
\mathsf{D}_{X}
and
\mathsf{E}_{X}
.
For limit ordinals
\lambda
, we set

\chi^{\lambda}_{s}\coloneqq\bigwedge_{\alpha<\lambda}\chi^{\alpha}_{s},

which corresponds to definition of
\sim^{\lambda}
,
see
\cref
eq:approx-bisim-lim-ord in
\cref
def:bisim-approx.
Conclude via transfinite induction.
∎

Theorem 18

(Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé and Karp theorems for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be closed under negation.
If
\Omega
is
finite
and

(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
are dependence models of the same
finite
type, then

{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{k}{s^{\prime}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{k}{s^{\prime}},\qquad k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

As a consequence we obtain that under those same conditions

{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\omega}{s^{\prime}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}.

For
\Omega
not necessarily finite and arbitrary types we obtain

{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\alpha}{s^{\prime}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}}^{\alpha}{s^{\prime}},\qquad\alpha\in\mathrm{On}

and therefore

{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\infty}{s^{\prime}}.

Proof.

The proof is a routine induction.
One shows that
k
-
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimilarity entails
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-equivalence
up to quantifier rank
k
by using the characteristics of our bisimulation,
whereas the converse implication is immediate from the above lemmas.
The infinitary case is handled analogously.
∎

This allows us to show undefinability of
some property of (pointed) dependence models,
by finding two such models that are bisimilar but differ on said property.
By the above theorem, we then know that these models
are logically indistinguishable in
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
,
so the considered property cannot be defined in the respective logic.

Example 19
.

Let
\Omega=\{D,\Upsilon,=,\neq\}
.
We show that the inclusion
T(x)\subseteq T(y)
is not
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]_{\infty}
-definable.
It suffices to show this for
\tau=\varnothing
and
{\cal V}=\{x,y\}
,
because the example below can be adapted accordingly.
Consider dependence models
(\mathfrak{M},T),(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
of type
(\varnothing,\{x,y\})

with teams given by

T\coloneqq\{(a,b),(b,a)\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T^{\prime}\coloneqq\{(1,2),(2,0)\}.

Note that
T(x)\subseteq T(y)
, but
T^{\prime}(x)\not\subseteq T^{\prime}(y)
.
Now let
Z
be the binary relation on

T\times T^{\prime}
defined by

(a,b)\mathbin{Z}(1,2)
and
(b,a)\mathbin{Z}(2,0)
.
It is easy to verify that
Z
is a full
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimulation and
hence
{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\infty}{s^{\prime}}
by
\cref
thm:bisim,
for all
({s},{s^{\prime}})\in Z
.
Indeed, note that
\Upsilon_{\varnothing}x,\Upsilon_{\varnothing}y,D_{x}y,D_{y}x

and
x\neq y
hold at all assignments in both teams,
so the pairs of assignments agree on atoms.
Furthermore, in both teams we see that the two assignments
do not agree on any variables, so evidently the only choice we have
at the back and forth clauses (every assignment stands in
Z
-relation to exactly one other)
always works out.

5
A Characterisation Theorem

We now prove a characterisation theorem for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.
The theorem is an analogue of van Benthem’s Theorem, which states
that
ML
, via its standard translation into
FO
,
is precisely the bisimulation-invariant fragment of
FO
over the class of pointed Kripke structures.
It was first formulated in
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
.
We adapt a well known proof using saturated structures
by following the exposition in
[
5
, Chapter 2.6]
.
We assume that the reader is familiar with basic model-theoretic notions
such as elementary extensions and
\omega
-saturated structures.

Given a dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
of finite type
(\tau,{\cal V})
,
we have already seen that we can interpret
(\mathfrak{M},T)
as a
\tau\cup\{T\}
-structure,
where
T
is a
|{\cal V}|
-ary relation symbol; this is done by fixing an enumeration
\bar{v}
of
{\cal V}

and viewing
T
as the
|{\cal V}|
-ary relation
T(\bar{v})=\{{s}(\bar{v})\mid{s}\in T\}
over
(\mathfrak{M},T)
.
In the following, we also write
{\bar{{s}}}\coloneqq{s}(\bar{v})
for the
|{\cal V}|
-ary tuple
corresponding to
{s}\in T
. For clarity of presentation we denote the evaluation
of the first-order formula
\varphi(\bar{x})\in{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup\{T\})
in the structure
(\mathfrak{M},T)
at
the tuple
{\bar{{s}}}
under classical Tarski-semantics by
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})
.
In
\cref
subsec:trans we gave a first-order translation
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]\to{\textsf{FO}},\varphi\mapsto\varphi^{*}
such that
for all
\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
and every fitting dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
we have

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi^{*}({\bar{{s}}}).

In the following, let
(\tau,{\cal V})
be a finite type with an enumeration
\bar{v}
of
{\cal V}
,
and
\Omega\subseteq\{D,\Upsilon,=,\neq,\in,\notin,\operatorname{Ind},\cdots\}

a finite set of local atoms that is closed under negation
and to which we may extend the above first-order translation.

Definition 20
.

For dependence models
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
let

\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}((\mathfrak{M},T),{s})\coloneqq\{\varphi^{*}\mid\varphi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega],\ (\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{s}\varphi\}\subseteq{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup\{T\}).

We use
\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})
if
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is clear from context.
Note that
\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})
generally
contains formulae with free variables among
\bar{v}
, not just sentences.

Lemma 21
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
be pointed dependence models. Then

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s^{\prime}})({\bar{{s}}})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})=\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s^{\prime}})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}.

Definition 22
.

Write
n=|{\cal V}|=|\bar{v}|
and consider tuples of variables
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

and
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
.
With respect to the ordering of
{\cal V}
given by
\bar{v}
, we identify

X\subseteq{\cal V}
with a set of indices
I_{X}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Now define
\vartheta_{X}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup\{T\})
by

\vartheta_{X}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\coloneqq T\bar{x}\land T\bar{y}\land\bigwedge_{i\in I_{X}}x_{i}=y_{i}.

Lemma 23
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T)
be a dependence model,
X\subseteq{\cal V}
and
{\bar{{s}}},{\bar{{t}}}
tuples over
\mathfrak{M}
of length
|{\cal V}|
.
Write
{s},{t}
for the induced assignments with
{s}(\bar{v})={\bar{{s}}}
and
{t}(\bar{v})={\bar{{t}}}
.
Then

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\vartheta_{X}({\bar{{s}}},{\bar{{t}}})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{s},{t}\in T\text{ and }{s}=_{X}{t}.

Lemma 24
.

Let
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
be two
(\tau,{\cal V})
dependence models,
so that their corresponding
(\tau\cup\{T\})
-structures are
\omega
-saturated.
If
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}^{\omega}(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
, then already
{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

For ease of presentation we write
\sim
and
\sim^{\omega}
, omitting the subscript
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.
Let
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
be as described above.
By our Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Theorem, it follows from
{s}\sim^{\omega}{s^{\prime}}
that
{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}
. Hence it suffices to show that

Z\coloneqq\{({s},{s^{\prime}})\in T\times T^{\prime}\mid(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}\}.

is a bisimulation between
(\mathfrak{M},T)
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
.
Given
({s},{s^{\prime}})\in Z
, we clearly have
{s}\equiv^{0}_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}
,
so
{s}
and
{s^{\prime}}
agree on atoms.
We proceed by checking the forth condition.

Let
{t}\in T
and
X\subseteq{t}\operatorname{\overline{\cap}}{s}=\{x\in{\cal V}\mid{t}=_{x}{s}\}
be finite.
Set

p(\bar{y})\coloneqq\{\vartheta_{X}(\bar{y},{s^{\prime}})\}\cup\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({t})(\bar{y}).

We want to show that
p
is a type of
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
with parameters
{\bar{{s^{\prime}}}}
,
i.e. that
p
together with the first-order theory
\Th_{{\textsf{FO}}}((\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})
is satisfiable.
For a compactness argument we consider a finite
\Phi_{0}(\bar{y})\subseteq\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({t})(\bar{y})

and define

\varphi(\bar{x})\coloneqq\exists{\bar{{t^{\prime}}}}(\vartheta_{X}({\bar{{t^{\prime}}}},\bar{x})\land\bigwedge\Phi_{0}({\bar{{t^{\prime}}}})).

Now there exists some finite

\Psi_{0}\subseteq{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
with
\Phi_{0}(\bar{y})=\{\varphi^{*}(\bar{y})\mid\varphi\in\Psi_{0}\}
.
Since our translation commutes with
\land
, we obtain
(\bigwedge\Psi_{0})^{*}=\bigwedge\Phi_{0}
.
We claim that
\varphi\equiv(\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0})^{*}
.
Clearly any
(\tau\cup\{T\})
-structure can be interpreted as the corresponding
structure to a
(\tau,{\cal V})
dependence model
({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})
.
For such a
({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})
and any
a\in T^{\prime\prime}
we have

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi(\bar{a})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

there exists
\bar{b}
over
{\mathfrak{A}}
with
({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\vartheta_{X}(\bar{b},\bar{a})

and
({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\left(\bigwedge\Psi_{0}\right)^{*}(\bar{b})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

there exists
b\in T^{\prime\prime}
with
b=_{X}a

and
({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models_{b}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models_{a}\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{A}},T^{\prime\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\left(\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}\right)^{*}(\bar{a}).

Thus, as claimed, we have
\varphi\equiv\left(\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}\right)^{*}
.
From
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{{t}}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}
and
{t}=_{X}{s}
we obtain

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{{s}}\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}
.
Since
{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}
we get
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models_{{s^{\prime}}}\mathsf{E}_{X}\bigwedge\Psi_{0}

and therefore
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})
.
Hence
\{\vartheta_{X}(\bar{y},{\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})\}\cup\Phi_{0}(\bar{y})\cup\Th_{{\textsf{FO}}}((\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})
is satisfiable.
It follows by compactness that

p(\bar{y})
is a type with finitely many parameters (namely
{\bar{{s^{\prime}}}}
) over
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})
.

By
\omega
-saturatedness we obtain some tuple
{\bar{{t^{\prime}}}}
in
\mathfrak{N}

with
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}p({\bar{{t^{\prime}}}})
.
From the definition of
\vartheta_{X}
we see that

{\bar{{t^{\prime}}}}={t^{\prime}}(\bar{v})
for some
{t^{\prime}}\in T^{\prime}
with
{t^{\prime}}=_{X}{s^{\prime}}
.
Furthermore
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({t})({\bar{{t^{\prime}}}})
,
so
{t}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{t^{\prime}}
and hence
({t},{t^{\prime}})\in Z
,
which proves the forth condition.
The back condition is shown analogously.
We conclude that
Z
is a bisimulation.
∎

Theorem 25

(Expressive Completeness)
.

For any
\varphi\in{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup\{T\})
the following are equivalent:

1.

\varphi
is
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimulation-invariant,
i.e. for all
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
of type
(\tau,{\cal V})

{s}\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})\quad\text{iff}\quad(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s^{\prime}}}}).

2.

\varphi\equiv\psi^{*}
for some
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
,
so that for all
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
of type
(\tau,{\cal V})

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models_{{s}}\psi.

As this result holds a fixed
\Omega
and arbitrary finite types
(\tau,{\cal V})
,
we write
{\textsf{FO}}/{\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}}\equiv{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.

Proof.

As in the previous proof, we omit
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
from
\sim
and
\sim^{\omega}
,
and say bisimulation instead of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-bisimulation.
The implication “(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)” is clear by our Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Theorem.
Now assume that
\varphi
is bisimulation-invariant
and consider the set of its
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
-consequences:

C(\varphi)(\bar{x})\coloneqq\{\psi^{*}(\bar{x})\mid\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})\text{ and }\varphi\models\psi^{*}\}.

We claim that it suffices to show
C(\varphi)\models\varphi
.
Indeed, using compactness this yields a finite subset
C_{0}\subseteq C(\varphi)

with
C_{0}\models\varphi
and therefore
\bigwedge C_{0}\equiv\varphi
.
But
C_{0}=\{\psi^{*}\mid\psi\in\Psi\}
for some (finite)
\Psi\subseteq{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
,
so by setting
\psi=\bigwedge\Psi
,
we obtain the desired result that
\varphi\equiv\bigwedge C_{0}=\psi^{*}

for some
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
.

If
C(\varphi)
is unsatisfiable,
C(\varphi)\models\varphi
holds vacuously.
Hence let
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
be a dependence model of type
(\tau,{\cal V})
with
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}C(\varphi)({\bar{{s}}})
.
We need to show
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})
.
If
\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})\cup\{\varphi\}
were unsatisfiable,
by compactness there would be some finite
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})

such that
\Phi_{0}\cup\{\varphi\}
is unsatisfiable.
This implies
\varphi\models\lnot\bigwedge\Phi_{0}
.
Since
\Omega
is closed under negation,
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
is too,
and we obtain
\lnot\bigwedge\Phi_{0}\in C(\varphi)
.
This contradicts
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}C(\varphi)({\bar{{s}}})
and
\Phi_{0}\subseteq\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})
.

Therefore
\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})\cup\{\varphi\}
has some model
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}
.
Since
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}({s})({\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})
we obtain
{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}{s^{\prime}}
.
Now take
\omega
-saturated elementary extensions
(\mathfrak{M},T)\preceq(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T_{+})

and
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\preceq(\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+})
.
By elementary extension we have
(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T_{+}),{s}\equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}

and likewise for
(\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+}),{s^{\prime}}
. It follows that

(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T_{+}),{s}\ \equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}\ (\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\ \equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}\ (\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime}),{s^{\prime}}\ \equiv_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}\ (\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+}),{s^{\prime}}.

Our Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Theorem yields
(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T_{+}),{s}\sim^{\omega}(\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+}),{s^{\prime}}
.
But now we can apply
\cref
lemma:omega-sat to find

(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T_{+}),{s}\sim(\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+}),{s^{\prime}}
.
Since
\varphi\in{\textsf{FO}}
, we infer
(\mathfrak{N}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})

from
(\mathfrak{N},T^{\prime})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s^{\prime}}}})
by elementary extension.
Moreover,
\varphi
is bisimulation-invariant,
so we obtain
(\mathfrak{M}_{+},T^{\prime}_{+})\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})
.
Again by elementary extension, we arrive at
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})
.

With this we showed that whenever
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\varphi({\bar{{s}}})

we also have
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}C(\varphi)({\bar{{s}}})

for an arbitrary
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
of type
(\tau,{\cal V})
. Hence
C(\varphi)\models\varphi
.
We discussed above how this concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

6
Local Dependence and Guarded Fragments of
FO

We have seen that some of the considered local logics can be embedded into
GF
,
the guarded fragment of first-order logic. However, it was unclear whether this
is also the case for
LFD
, which features the local dependence atom.
We now discuss the relationship of local dependence with

GF
and other guarded fragments of
FO
.
Arguably one of the most natural generalizations of
GF
within
FO

is the clique-guarded fragment
CGF
, introduced in
[
12
]
.

Recall that the Gaifman graph of a relational
\tau
-structure
{\mathfrak{A}}

has as its universe the universe of
{\mathfrak{A}}
, and an edge between two distinct elements

a\neq b
if these coexist in some atomic fact of
{\mathfrak{A}}
,
i.e. they occur together in some
\bar{c}\in R^{{\mathfrak{A}}}
for some
R\in\tau
.
Obviously, guarded tuples in a relational structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
induce
a clique in the Gaifman graph of
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Moreover, for each finite relational
\tau
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a positive,
existential first-order formula
clique(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
which is satisfied at a tuple
\bar{a}

of some
\tau
-structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
if and only if
\bar{a}
induces a clique in the Gaifman graph of
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

CGF
is then defined in an analogous way to
GF
,
but always uses
clique
of the right arity as a guard, in the sense of

\forall\bar{y}(clique(\bar{x}\bar{y})\rightarrow\varphi(\bar{x}\bar{y}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\exists\bar{y}(clique(\bar{x}\bar{y})\land\varphi(\bar{x}\bar{y})).

Obviously
{\textsf{ML}}\subsetneq{\textsf{GF}}\subsetneq{\textsf{CGF}}\subsetneq{\textsf{FO}}
.
Guarded bisimulations between structures
{\mathfrak{A}},\bar{a}
and
{\mathfrak{B}},\bar{b}

are defined as sets
I
of partial isomorphisms between
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}

such that
(\bar{a}\mapsto\bar{b})\in I
and
I
is closed under suitable back and forth conditions.
This notion naturally extends to clique-guarded bisimulation for
CGF
, as described in
[
12
]
.
For a survey of various notions of bisimulation
and their uses for understanding expressive power,
model-theoretic and algorithmic properties of modal and guarded logics,
we refer the reader to
[
13
]
.

Other first-order translations

Apart from the standard translation discussed until now,
one can also consider other first-order translation of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.
In
[
3
]
, Baltag and van Benthem emphasized the modal perspective
of
{\textsf{LFD}}\equiv{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
, and presented a modal semantics for
LFD

over so-called standard relational models.
The semantics of local dependence and local independence
only require knowledge about structure of the team with respect to the ”agreement”-relations
=_{X}

for
X\subseteq{\cal V}
.
In this sense, one introduces abstract equivalence relations
\sim_{X}
which allow
us to abstract away the actual values of assignments while preserving the intended semantics.
A dependence model induces such a standard relational model in a straightforward way:
the new universe is the team, the relations become monadic and we add the equivalences
\sim_{x}

for
x\in{\cal V}
. Formulae in the base logic
L
are then translated as

\tr^{s}(R\bar{x})\coloneqq R_{\bar{x}}{s}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tr^{s}(\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi)\coloneqq\forall{t}\left(\bigwedge_{x\in X}{t}\sim_{x}{s}\rightarrow\tr^{t}(\varphi)\right),

where
\tr
commutes with boolean connectives. For the local atoms, we set

\tr^{s}(D_{x}y)\coloneqq\forall{t}\left({t}\sim_{x}{s}\rightarrow{t}\sim_{y}{s}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tr^{s}(\operatorname{Ind}_{x}y)\coloneqq\forall{t}\exists{u}({s}\sim_{x}{u}\land{u}\sim_{y}{t}).

If we restrict the considered class of structures to
those that are induced by dependence models under this correspondence
(so in particular they have to interpret the
\sim_{x}
as equivalence relations)
then this translation shares many of the nice characteristics of the standard translation.
Indeed, using the modal translation in the definition of
\Th_{{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]}

and setting
\vartheta_{X}({s},{t})=\bigwedge_{x\in X}{s}\sim_{x}{t}
,
the proof of the characterisation theorem in the last section
also works in this modal context, with minimal adaptions.

Expressive Incomparability

In the following we want to prove that under these translations,
local dependence is inherently incompatible with the clique-guarded fragment of first-order logic.

Let
\tau
be a relational vocabulary and
{\mathfrak{A}},{\mathfrak{B}}
two
\tau
-structures
with disjoint domains
A,B
.
Their disjoint union is the
\tau
-structure
{\mathfrak{C}}={\mathfrak{A}}\uplus{\mathfrak{B}}
with domain

C=A\uplus B
so that
{\mathfrak{C}}\upharpoonright A
and
{\mathfrak{C}}\upharpoonright B
are isomorphic
to
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}
respectively, and for all tuples
\bar{c}
over
C

which contain elements from both
A
and
B
we have
{\mathfrak{C}}\models\lnot R\bar{c}

for every
R\in\tau
.

Proposition 26
.

The relevant bisimulations for
GF
and
CGF
are compatible with
disjoint unions of bisimilar models.
Specifically, let
I
be a clique-guarded bisimulation between

{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}
, and
I^{\prime}
be one between
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{C}}
.
Then
I\cup I^{\prime}
is a clique-guarded bisimulation between
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}\uplus{\mathfrak{C}}
.
In particular, from
{\mathfrak{A}},\bar{a}\sim_{{\textsf{CGF}}}{\mathfrak{B}},\bar{b}
and

{\mathfrak{A}},\bar{a}\sim_{{\textsf{CGF}}}{\mathfrak{C}},\bar{c}
we infer

{\mathfrak{A}},\bar{a}\sim_{{\textsf{CGF}}}({\mathfrak{B}}\uplus{\mathfrak{C}}),\bar{b}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathfrak{A}},\bar{a}\sim_{{\textsf{CGF}}}({\mathfrak{B}}\uplus{\mathfrak{C}}),\bar{c}.

In this setting, if
\varphi(\bar{x})\in{\textsf{CGF}}
, then
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi(\bar{a})
holds
if and only if
({\mathfrak{B}}\uplus{\mathfrak{C}})\models\varphi(\bar{b})\land\varphi(\bar{c})
.

This highlights a small but important
difference between these guarded fragments and our logics of local dependence.
Namely, an analogue for invariance under disjoint union
cannot hold for dependence models in presence of the local dependence atom.
Indeed, we can define constancy of a variable
x
via
D_{\varnothing}x
,
which is clearly not invariant under disjoint unions.

Proposition 27
.

The standard and modal translations do not embed
{\textsf{L}}[D]
into
CGF
.
More specifically, there does not exist a
CGF
-sentence
\psi

that is equivalent to either translation of the constancy atom
D_{\varnothing}x
.

Proof.

In the context of the standard translation,
it is clear that the disjoint union of the first-order structures
corresponding to two dependence models is itself the
first-order structure corresponding to the disjoint union of the dependence models.
Unlike
D_{\varnothing}x
,
CGF
is invariant under disjoint unions,
so there cannot exist a
\psi\in{\textsf{CGF}}
with
(D_{\varnothing}x)^{*}\equiv\psi
.

The argument for the modal translation is similar, and relies on the fact
that the class of first-order structures we consider is well-behaved
with respect to disjoint union in the above sense.
∎

There are certainly also notions expressible in
GF
but not in
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.
For one, in the setting of the standard translation it becomes obvious that

{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
cannot make statements about assignments and values outside of the team;
we may have
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\exists xRx
and simultaneously
(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\QGA\lnot Rx
.
Even in the modal setting we can easily find formulae in
GF
that
are not equivalent to any
{\textsf{LFD}}\equiv{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
formula.

Proposition 28
.

Let
(\tau,{\cal V})
be a finite type with
x,y,z\in{\cal V}
and let

\varphi({s})\coloneqq\forall{t}({t}\sim_{x}{s}\ \rightarrow\ ({t}\sim_{y}{s}\lor{t}\sim_{z}{s}))\in{\textsf{GF}}^{2}.

Then there exists no
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
whose modal translation is equivalent
to
\varphi
over the considered class of first-order structures.

Proof.

We give two
(\varnothing,\{x,y,z\})
dependence models
that are
{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
-bisimilar, but where their corresponding
(modal) first-order structures disagree on
\varphi
.
These are uniquely determined by the structure of their teams.
The first team has
=_{y}
-classes
\{t_{1},s\},\{t_{2}\}
and
=_{z}
-classes
\{t_{1}\},\{s,t_{2}\}
.
The second team has
=_{y}
-classes
\{t^{\prime}_{1},s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\},\{t^{\prime}_{2},t^{\prime\prime}_{2}\}

and
=_{z}
-classes
\{t^{\prime}_{1}\},\{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}_{2}\},\{s^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime\prime}_{2}\}
.
In both teams all assignments agree on
x
.
The bisimulation relates
s
to
s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}
,
t_{1}
to
t^{\prime}_{1}
, and
t_{2}
to
t^{\prime}_{2}
and
t^{\prime\prime}_{2}
.
Then
s\sim_{{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]}s^{\prime}
but
\varphi
holds at
s
, while it does not hold at
s^{\prime}
.
The example is easily extended to larger types containing the variables
x,y,z
.
∎

Corollary 29
.

In the context of the standard and modal translations,

{\textsf{LFD}}\equiv{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
is expressively incomparable
to the guarded fragment
GF
and even the clique-guarded fragment
CGF
.

7
Model checking

In this last section we study the complexity of the model checking problem,
abbreviated
\MC({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
, for local logics
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
:
Given a formula
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
and a fitting finite pointed dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
,
we ask whether
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\models\psi
.
We consider the
combined complexity
, measured with respect to the size of all inputs.
It turns that this complexity is largely influenced
by how we encode the team
T
of
(\mathfrak{M},T)
.

For comparison, we recall that the model checking for
first-order logic (
FO
) is
Pspace
-complete in general,
but
Ptime
-complete for many interesting fragments
of
FO

[
11
, Chapter 3.1]
,
including the modal fragment
ML
, the bounded
variable fragments
{\textsf{FO}}^{k}
(for any
k\geq 2
), and also
the guarded fragment
GF

[
4
]
.

For the rest of this section
(\tau,{\cal V})
denotes a finite type,

\Omega\subseteq\{D,\Upsilon,=,\neq,\in,\notin,\operatorname{Ind},\lnot\operatorname{Ind}\}
,
is a collection of local atoms,
and we study the model checking problem of

{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
.

Before we discuss these technicalities of team encodings,
we consider the special case of full models.
We call dependence models full if their team consists of the whole
assignment space, so
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is full if
T=M^{{\cal V}}
.
Over the class of all full dependence models,
even the base logic
{\textsf{L}}={\textsf{L}}[\varnothing]
is as expressive as relational first-order logic without equality;
the quantifiers
\exists x
and
\forall x
then have the same semantics
as
\mathsf{E}_{{\cal V}_{x}}
and
\mathsf{D}_{{\cal V}_{x}}
respectively, where
{\cal V}_{x}\coloneqq{\cal V}\setminus\{x\}
.
For example, if
(\mathfrak{M},T)
is full and
{\cal V}=\{x,y\}
, then

(\mathfrak{M},T)\models^{{\textsf{FO}}}\forall x\exists yRxy\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\mathsf{D}_{y}\mathsf{E}_{x}Rxy\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\mathfrak{M},T)\models\QGA\mathsf{E}_{x}Rxy.

This was already noted for
{\textsf{LFD}}\equiv{\textsf{L}}[D,\Upsilon]
by Baltag and van Benthem in
[
3
]
.

It is therefore not surprising that when restricting attention to full dependence models,
we obtain the same lower bounds on the complexity of
\MC({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
as for first-order logic.
Denote by
\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
the restriction of model checking for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

to instances where the team is always the full team,
and write
\MCFK({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
for its
k
-variable restriction.
Thus the inputs are of the form
(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}})
where
\bar{v}
is an enumeration of
{\cal V}

and
{\bar{{s}}}={s}(\bar{v})
for
{s}\in M^{{\cal V}}
is an encoding of
the current assignment at which
\psi
should be evaluated.
By the same techniques as for first-order logic, one
immediately obtains the following hardness results.

Proposition 30
.

\MCF({\textsf{L}})
is
Pspace
-hard and
\MCFK({\textsf{L}})
is
Ptime
-hard for
k\geq 2
.
Since
L
is a sublogic of
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
, these results also hold
for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
with arbitrary
\Omega
.

Solving the model checking problem for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
.

We consider a general method for solving the model checking problem for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

that abstracts away the specifics on how the team is encoded.
We assume familiarity with alternating complexity classes
as given in
[
2
, Chapter 3]
or
[
17
, Chapters 16.2 & 19.1]
.
The model checking problem for first-order logic model can be solved
in a standard way (cf.
[
11
, Chapter 3.1]
) by an alternating algorithm
which, to determine whether
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi(\bar{a})
, requires

•

alternating space
\mathcal{O}(\log|\psi|+r\log|{\mathfrak{A}}|)
, where
r
is the maximal
number of free variables in any subformula of
\psi
, and

•

alternating time
\mathcal{O}(|\psi|\log|{\mathfrak{A}}|)
.

Together with the well-known facts that
\textsc{Alogspace}=\textsc{Ptime}
and
\textsc{Aptime}=\textsc{Pspace}

one then obtains that
\MC({\textsf{FO}}^{k})\in\textsc{Ptime},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\MC({\textsf{FO}})\in\textsc{Pspace}
.
It is straightforward to adapt this alternating algorithm to our setting.

ModelCheck(
\psi,(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
)

Input:

a formula
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
in negation normal form where

(\tau,{\cal V})
is finite, and

a finite pointed
(\tau,{\cal V})
dependence model
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}
.

if

\psi
is a literal

then

if

Algorithm 1

Alternating model checking for
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]

It implements the usual model checking game
between an existential and universal player,
played on positions
(\varphi,{t})
where
\varphi
is some subformula of
\psi

and

The algorithm
Accept

How to encode the team.

Now we come back to the discussion on how to encode the team.
A first idea might be to simply list all
assignments of the team in the input.
Call this variant
\MCL({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
.
In many cases, this encoding is rather inefficient;
given
\mathfrak{M},\psi
, a tuple
\bar{v}
enumerating
{\cal V}
and a current assignment
{s}\in T
,
encoding
T
as a list of all its assignments may cause an exponentially longer input,
as in the case of full models where
T=M^{{\cal V}}
.
As a consequence, one obtains a deceptively low complexity for
\MCL({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
.

Proposition 31
.

The alternating algorithm can be implemented to decide instances

(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}},T)
of
\MCL({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])

with alternating workspace
\mathcal{O}(\log|\psi|+\log|T|)
.
In particular, we obtain that
\MCL({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])\in\textsc{Alogspace}=\textsc{Ptime}
.

Proof.

We implement picking (existentially guessing or universally choosing)
assignments
{t}\in T
by picking a pointer to some tuple in the list representing
T
.
Such a pointer only requires
\log|T|
space,
which allows us to decide whether
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\models\beta
with alternating workspace

\mathcal{O}(\log|T|)
, for any
\beta\in\Omega
. This is clear for
\beta\in\{=,\neq\}
.
For
\beta=D_{X}y
, we universally choose
{t}\in T
with
{t}=_{X}{s}
and

Accept
if and only if
{t}=_{y}{s}
.
If
\beta=(\bar{x}\in\bar{y})
, then we existentially guess
{t}\in T

and
Accept
if and only if
{s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{y})
.
Given
\beta=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{x}}\bar{y}
, we universally choose
{u}\in T
and then
existentially guess
{t}\in T
, accepting
only in the case that
{s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{x})
and
{u}(\bar{y})={t}(\bar{y})
.
The atoms
\Upsilon,\notin,\lnot\operatorname{Ind}
are handled dually to their counterparts.

A pointer of length
\log|\psi|
suffices to specify the current subformula
\varphi
of
\psi
.
Since
\psi,\mathfrak{M}
and
T
are never modified,
the algorithm only needs to keep track of at most 3 assignments and 3 subformulae at any time.
Together with the above analysis, this proves the claim.
∎

Comparing the above to our result that
\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
is
Pspace
-hard,
we seem to have a contradiction to the common belief that
\textsc{Ptime}\neq\textsc{Pspace}
.
This is however not the case, as the two problems differ on the length of their inputs.
Indeed, there cannot exist a polynomial-time reduction from
\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])

to
\MCL({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
, because the size of the full team
M^{{\cal V}}
is exponential in the size

|\psi|+|\mathfrak{M}|+|{\cal V}|
of the input of
\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
.

Nevertheless, this disparity shows that the approach of
encoding
T
as a list may yield unsatisfactory results.
A different approach is to encode the team as a first-order formula
\varphi_{T}

over the vocabulary
\tau\cup M
where all elements of
\mathfrak{M}
are added as constants
interpreted by themselves in the corresponding expansion
\mathfrak{M}_{M}
of
\mathfrak{M}
.
We want that for all
{\bar{{t}}}\in M^{|{\cal V}|}
:

\mathfrak{M}_{M}\models\varphi_{T}({\bar{{t}}})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\text{there is some ${t}\in T$ with ${t}(\bar{v})={\bar{{t}}}$.}

Definition 32
.

We encode the team by a first-order formula as described above.
More specifically, define the problem
\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
as follows.

1.

The inputs are tuples
(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}},\varphi_{T})
where

(a)

(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}})
have the same interpretation as before,
with
\psi\in{\textsf{L}}[\Omega](\tau,{\cal V})
,
\mathfrak{M}
a
\tau
-structure with universe
M
,

\bar{v}
an enumeration of
{\cal V}
and
{\bar{{s}}}\in M^{|{\cal V}|}
encoding
a current assignment.

(b)

\varphi_{T}\in{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup M)
encodes the team
T\subseteq M^{{\cal V}}

as described above, with
\mathfrak{M}_{M}\models\varphi_{T}({\bar{{s}}})
.

2.

The task is to decide whether
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\models\psi
.

3.

The complexity is measured in the input size,
so essentially with respect to
|\psi|+|\mathfrak{M}|+|{\cal V}|+|\varphi_{T}|
.

Proposition 33
.

We can implement the alternating algorithm so that a given instance
(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}},\varphi_{T})

for
\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
is solved requiring only

1.

alternating space
\mathcal{O}(\log|\psi|+\log|\varphi_{T}|+(|{\cal V}|+r)\log|\mathfrak{M}|)
, where

r
is the maximal number of free variables in any subformula of
\varphi_{T}
, and

2.

alternating time
\mathcal{O}(|\psi|\cdot(|\psi|+|\mathfrak{M}|+(|{\cal V}|+|\varphi_{T}|)\log|\mathfrak{M}|))
.

In particular, we obtain that
\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])\in\textsc{Aptime}=\textsc{Pspace}
.

Proof.

Assignments
{t}
are encoded by their values
{\bar{{t}}}={t}(\bar{v})
,
thus taking
\mathcal{O}(A)\coloneqq\mathcal{O}(|{\cal V}|\log|\mathfrak{M}|)
space.
We implement picking assignments
{t}\in T
by picking a tuple
{\bar{{t}}}\in M^{|{\cal V}|}

and then performing first-order model checking on
(\varphi_{T},\mathfrak{M}_{M},{\bar{{t}}})
.
We know that
|\mathfrak{M}_{M}|
is polynomial in
|\mathfrak{M}|
and hence
\log|\mathfrak{M}_{M}|\in\mathcal{O}(\log|\mathfrak{M}|)
.
It follows from the complexity of first-order model checking listed in the last paragraph
that picking an assignment in this way requires
alternating space
\mathcal{O}(P_{s})\coloneqq\mathcal{O}(A+\log|\varphi_{T}|+r\log|\mathfrak{M}|)
and
alternating time
\mathcal{O}(P_{t})\coloneqq\mathcal{O}(A+|\varphi_{T}|\log|\mathfrak{M}|)
.

The space-analysis is parallel to the proof of
\cref
prop:algo-comp1.
We store a subformula
\varphi
of
\psi
as a pointer of length
\log|\psi|
.
Then
\psi,\mathfrak{M}
and
\varphi_{T}
are never modified,
and at any time we need at most 3 assignments and 3 subformulae in the workspace.
This yields the claimed alternating space-complexity
\mathcal{O}(P_{s}+\log|\psi|)
,
since we can always reuse the space required for the first-order model checking.

For the time analysis, note that checking something such as
{t}(\bar{x})={s}(\bar{y})

for two assignments
{s},{t}
in the workspace is
possible within alternating time
\mathcal{O}(A)
, the size of the assignments.

1.

If
\varphi
is a relational literal
we can evaluate whether
(\mathfrak{M},T),{s}\models\varphi

in alternating time
\mathcal{O}(|\psi|+|\mathfrak{M}|+A)
.

2.

For other local atoms
\beta\in\Omega

or dependence quantifiers,
the algorithm picks at most two new assignments
and then does a constant number of checks of the form
{s}(\bar{x})={t}(\bar{y})
.
In the case of dependence quantifiers,
it also updates the current subformula from
\mathsf{D}_{X}\varphi
or
\mathsf{E}_{X}\varphi
to
\varphi
.
This can be accomplished in alternating time
\mathcal{O}(P_{t}+|\psi|)
.

3.

Choosing some subformula at conjunctions and disjunctions takes only

\mathcal{O}(|\psi|)
time, since we just have to move our pointer within
\psi
.

Clearly we have at most
|\psi|
recursive calls,
which yields the claimed alternating time-complexity
\mathcal{O}(|\psi|(P_{t}+|\psi|+|\mathfrak{M}|))
.
∎

We now want to show an analogue of
\MC({\textsf{FO}}^{k})\in\textsc{Alogspace}=\textsc{Ptime}
,
so we define
\MCFOK({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
as the restriction
of
\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
to instances where
|{\cal V}|\leq k
.
We now want the alternating space-complexity
given in
\cref
prop:algo-comp2 to be logarithmic in the input.
Problematic is the occurrence of
r
, which describes the maximum
number of free variables in any subformula of
\varphi_{T}
,
and originates from the space-complexity of the first-order model checking
we perform when picking new assignments.
Currently, we allow arbitrary
\varphi_{T}\in{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup M)

to represent the team
T
in the input.
To obtain our wanted analogue,
we need to bound
r
by some constant for all instances of
\MCFOK({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
.

Every team
T\subseteq M^{{\cal V}}
can be encoded by an
{\textsf{FO}}(\tau\cup M)
-formula

\varphi_{T}
that uses only the variables in
{\cal V}
.
Indeed, we can just set
\varphi_{T}(\bar{x})=\bigvee_{{t}\in T}\bar{x}={t}(\bar{v})
.
This shows that it is very lenient to assume
that there exists some global bound for
r
in all instances of
\MCFOK({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
.

Definition 34
.

For

Proof.

From

Proposition 36
.

1.

\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
is
Pspace
-complete.

2.

\MCFOBK({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])
is
Ptime
-complete for all
B\geq k\geq 2
.

Proof.

Since the full team is specified by
\varphi_{M^{{\cal V}}}=\text{True}
,
we obtain the following logspace-computable reduction

\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])\to\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega]),\quad(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}})\mapsto(\psi,\mathfrak{M},\bar{v},{\bar{{s}}},\text{True}),

which shows that
\MCF({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])\leq_{\log}\MCFO({\textsf{L}}[\Omega])

Via the same reduction we can show that

.
Hence the hardness-results follow from
\cref

The rest was already discussed in
\cref

∎

This shows that if the local atoms are efficiently checkable,
the complexity of model checking
{\textsf{L}}[\Omega]
depends mostly on how one encodes the team.
Encoding the team as a list, as one would do for ordinary relations,
we obtain
Ptime
-completeness for both the finite-variable and the unconstrained variant.
Encoding the team as a first-order formula is more efficient,
and yields essentially the same complexity as that of first-order model checking;

Pspace
-complete in general, but
Ptime
-complete in restriction to
k\geq 2
variables.
