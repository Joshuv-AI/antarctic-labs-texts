Towards the Identifiability in Noisy Label Learning: A Multinomial Mixture Approach
Cuong Nguyen, Thanh-Toan Do, Gustavo Carneiro

Abstract
Learning from noisy labels (LNL) plays a crucial role in deep learning. The most promising LNL methods rely on identifying clean-label samples from a dataset with noisy annotations. Such an identification is challenging because the conventional LNL problem, which assumes a single noisy label per instance, is non-identifiable, i.e., clean labels cannot be estimated theoretically without additional heuristics. In this paper, we aim to formally investigate this identifiability issue using multinomial mixture models to determine the constraints that make the problem identifiable. Specifically, we discover that the LNL problem becomes identifiable if there are at least 2 ​ C − 1 2C-1 noisy labels per instance, where C C is the number of classes. To meet this requirement without relying on additional 2 ​ C − 2 2C-2 manual annotations per instance, we propose a method that automatically generates additional noisy labels by estimating the noisy label distribution based on nearest neighbours. These additional noisy labels enable us to apply the Expectation-Maximisation algorithm to estimate the posterior probabilities of clean labels, which are then used to train the model of interest. We empirically demonstrate that our proposed method is capable of estimating clean labels without any heuristics in several label noise benchmarks, including synthetic, web-controlled, and real-world label noises. Furthermore, our method performs competitively with many state-of-the-art methods.

Such subpar annotation, coupled with the well-known issue of deep neural networks being susceptible to overfitting to randomly-labelled data

More specifically, learning from noisy labels (LNL) aims to infer the “clean” label

Failing to address the LNL identifiability issue might lead to estimating an undesirable model which may not be useful for making predictions or drawing conclusions. Existing LNL methods, therefore, introduce additional heuristic assumptions to make the noisy label learning identifiable. Those include, but are not limited to, the small-loss criterion

Despite achieving successful results in several benchmarks, those heuristics are either sub-optimal, e.g., small-loss criterion might under-select “hard examples” (i.e., samples that are near decision boundaries) that are informative for learning, or associated with assumptions that might not always hold in practice, e.g., anchor points in

Such issues have motivated studies into the identifiability issue for the LNL problems.

In this paper, we carry out a new study on the challenging identifiability issue in the LNL problem, aiming at finding conditions that make the problem identifiable. Our contributions can be summarised as follows:

•

We provide a theoretically-rigorous formulation of the identifiability condition in the LNL problem, which concludes that at least
2C-1
noisy labels per training sample are needed to make the problem sufficiently and necessarily identifiable. Such result also implies the non-identifiability of the current setting in noisy-label learning where only a single noisy label per training sample is given.

•

We propose a practical method relying on nearest neighbours to generate several additional noisy labels per training sample to meet that condition, instead of requesting
2C-2
extra noisy labels per training sample from human annotators. The generated noisy labels are then used in the Expectation - Maximisation (EM) algorithm to infer clean labels and train the model of interest simultaneously. To our best knowledge, this is the first study working on generating additional noisy labels to make the LNL problem identifiable.

The empirical evaluation of the proposed method evaluated on several LNL benchmarks (including synthetic, web-controlled and real-world label noise problems) demonstrates that the method can successfully estimate clean labels without any heuristics. Furthermore, even though the main goal of this paper is the theoretical investigation of the identifiability condition, our method shows competitive results with several state-of-the-art techniques.

Background

Identifiability

The study of identifiability is to investigate whether the exact parameters of a model of interest may be recovered from observed data. An identifiable model means that its parameters can be uniquely determined from available data. In contrast, a non-identifiable model implies that there exists multiple sets of parameters, where each set can explain the observed data equally well. Identifiability is particularly important in statistical modelling, where statistical methods are used to infer the true set of parameters from data. Formally, the identifiability of a distribution
p(X;\theta)
over a random variable
X
, parameterised by
\theta\in\Theta
with
\Theta
denoting a parametric space, can be defined as follows:

Definition 1

p(X;\theta)
is identifiable if it satisfies:
p(X;\theta)=p(X;\theta^{\prime})\implies\theta=\theta^{\prime},\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
.

Mixture models

A mixture model of
C
distributions can be written as:

q(X)=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{c}p(X;\theta_{c})
,
where
X
is a random variable in
\mathcal{X}
,
\pi
is the mixture coefficient vector in the
(C-1)
-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta_{C-1}=\left\{\mathbf{y}:\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{C}\wedge\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=1\right\}
, and
\{p(X;\theta_{c})\}_{c=1}^{C}
is a set of
C
distributions. Compared to a single distribution, mixture models are more flexible with higher modelling capacity, and hence, widely used to provide computationally convenient representation of complex data distributions. Some of the most common ones include Gaussian-, Bernoulli- and multinomial-mixture models. And since mixture models are an instance of latent variable models, we can use the EM algorithm
[
7
]
to infer their parameters.

Identifiability issue in mixture models

One common problem in statistical inference for mixture models is the identifiability issue defined in
Definition

1
. For example, if all the
C
component distributions in a mixture model
q(X)
belong to the same parametric family, then
q(X)
is invariant under
C!
permutations by simply swapping the indices of the component distributions, a phenomenon known as
label-switching
. In practice, the identifiability issue due to
label-switching
(we will refer to this identifiability issue as label-switching from now on) is of no concern since one can impose an appropriate constraint on its parameters to obtain a unique solution. Nevertheless, parameter identifiability up to the permutation of class labels (we will refer this as identifiability in the remaining of this paper) is still a practical problem, at least in maximum likelihood for mixture models where the distribution components of such mixtures belong to certain distribution families. According to
[
37
, section 3.1]
, most mixture models supported on continuous space, e.g., Gaussian mixture models (excluding the mixture of uniform distributions), are identifiable. However, when the support space is discrete, the identifiability of such mixtures might not always hold. For example, a mixture of Poisson distribution
[
36
]
or a mixture of negative binomial distribution
[
46
]
is identifiable, while a mixture of binomial distributions is only identifiable under certain conditions
[
36
, Proposition 4]
. Another example is multinomial mixture models which is, according to
Theorem

1
, identifiable if and only if the number of samples is at least almost twice the number of mixture components.

1
[
18
, Lemma 2.2]
and
[
10

If

is a multinomial distribution, where

\rho_{c}
is the success probability vector of
C

\mathcal{M}_{N,C}=\left\{M(\mathbf{x}):M(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{c}\,\mathrm{Mult}(\mathbf{x};N,\rho_{c})\right\}

is identifiable (up to label permutation)
if and only if

.

Methodology

In this section, we address the LNL identifiability issue by formulating the noisy label learning with multinomial mixture models and imposing the identifiable condition. We then present a mitigation solution of the label-switching problem by imposing certain constraints when inferring
p(Y|X)
.

3.1
Noisy label learning as a multinomial mixture and the idenfitiability condition

The likelihood of the noisy label

presented in

1

\textstyle p\left(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i},\rho_{i}\right)=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}\,\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y};N,\rho_{ic}).

(2)

As the LNL problem can be formulated as a multinomial mixture model shown in

if and only if
there are at least
2C-1
samples of noisy label
\hat{Y}
for an instance

with
C
being the number of classes.

Given

{N=1}
. Therefore, according to

, it is non-identifiable for

. Another example is that binary classification on noisy labels, corresponding to

Remark 1

The result in
1
may seem pessimistic compared to the finding in
[
28
]
claiming that the LNL identifiability requires at least three “informative” noisy labels. Such requirement is equivalent to the need of at least three annotators per instance, where the transition matrix representing for each annotator must be full-rank. The assumption of full-rank transition matrices is implicitly dependent on the number of classes, as a larger number of classes
C
increases the difficulty to ensure that each
C
-by-
C
transition matrix is full-rank. Furthermore, the assumption lacks clarity because it is unclear how to translate a full-rank transition matrix to the property an annotator needs to have. In contrast, our result in
1
does not rely on any assumptions about noisy label “informativeness” nor full-rank transition matrices, and thus, can be more easily applied.

Hence, to address the identifiability issue in LNL, at least additional
2C-2
noisy labels per instance are needed. One naive way is to request more noisy labels per instance, e.g., via crowd-sourcing, that satisfies the identifiable condition in
1
. Such approach is, however, costly, time-consuming and poorly scalable, especially when the number of classes
C
is very large. For example, WebVision dataset
[
24
]
with
C=1,000
will require at least an addition of 1,998 noisy labels per sample, resulting in an intractable solution. To mitigate, we propose a practical method in
Section

3.2
to generate additional noisy labels to address the identifiability issue in LNL.

3.2
Practical method to address the identifiability issue in LNL

To obtain additional noisy labels per sample without additional labelling resources, we propose to approximate the noisy label distribution
p(\hat{Y}|X)
by taking the similarity between instance features into account. Our hypothesis is that instances with similar features tend to be annotated similarly. In other words, similar instances have similar noisy labels.
Thus, we can employ the single noisy label per instance available in the training dataset to approximate the noisy label distribution
p(\hat{Y}|X)
. The approximated distribution is then used to generate many noisy labels that meet the identifiability condition specified in
1
. Subsequently, the EM algorithm is employed to infer the parameter of the multinomial mixture that models the LNL problem in
Eq.

2
.

3.2.1
Approximate the noisy label distribution
p(\hat{Y}|X)

To generate additional noisy labels, we approximate the noisy label distribution of each training sample by exploiting the information of its nearest neighbours. The noisy label distribution of an instance is simultaneously derived not only from its own noisy label, but also the noisy labels of other instances whose features are similar to the instance. Specifically, the approximated distribution, denoted as
\tilde{p}(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i})
, can be written as:

\textstyle\tilde{p}(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mu\,p(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i})+(1-\mu)\sum_{j\neq i,j=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\,p(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{j}),

(3)

where:
\mu
is a hyper-parameter in
[0,1]
reflecting the trade-off between the noisy labels of the instance and other instances,
p(\hat{Y}|X)
is a multinomial mixture defined in
Eq.

2
,
K
is the number of nearest neighbour instances considered in the approximation, and
\mathbf{A}_{ij}
is a coefficient in
[0,1]
representing the similarity between
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. Note that
\sum_{j\neq i,j=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{ij}=1
.

There are several ways to find the similarity matrix
[\mathbf{A}_{ij}],\mathbf{A}_{ii}=0,i\in\{1,\ldots,M\},j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. For example, the study in
[
13
]
employs sparse subspace clustering method
[
9
]
to approximate the label distribution when learning human age from images. In this paper, we use a slightly similar but more efficient method that utilises the nearest neighbour information: locality-constrained linear coding (LLC)
[
40
]
. In particular, the coefficient
\mathbf{A}_{ij}
can be determined via the following optimisation:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}\norm{\mathbf{x}_{i} - \mathbf{B}_{i} \mathbf{A}_{i}}_{2}^{2}+\lambda\norm{\mathbf{d}_{i} \odot\mathbf{A}_{i}}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.: }\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{i}=1,\mathbf{A}_{ij}\geq 0,\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,K\},

(4)

where
\mathbf{B}_{i}
is a matrix containing
K

\mathbf{x}_{i}
(each column is a nearest-neighbour instance),
\mathbf{A}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{i1}&\mathbf{A}_{i2}&\ldots&\mathbf{A}_{iK}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is the
K
-dimensional vector representing the coding coefficients,
\odot
is the element-wise multiplication (a.k.a. Hadamard product),

\mathbf{d}_{i}=\exp(\nicefrac{\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x}_{i}, \mathbf{B}_{i})}{\sigma})
is the locality adaptor
with
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{i})
being a vector of Euclidean distances from
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to each of its nearest neighbours, and
\sigma
being used for adjusting the weight decay speed for the locality adaptor. Nevertheless, since our interest is locality, not sparsity, in our implementation, we ignore the second term in
Eq.

4
by setting
\lambda=0
.

Note that the optimisation in (
4
) is slightly different from the original LLC due to the additional constraint of non-negativity of
\mathbf{A}_{ij}
. Nevertheless, the optimisation resembles a quadratic program, and therefore, can be efficiently solved by off-the-shelf solvers, such as OSQP
[
35
]
which is available in the deep learning framework JAX (refer to
OSQP solver in JAXopt
).

Another problem when solving the optimisation in (
4
) for the coding vector
\mathbf{A}_{i}
of each instance
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is to find the nearest neighbours for an instance
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. In light of efficiently obtaining nearest neighbours, we employ FAISS
[
16
]
– a library for efficient similarity search and clustering of dense vectors written in C++ with Python wrappers and able to run on GPUs.

3.2.3
Overcome label switching

Although generating more noisy labels per sample by LLC resolves the identifiability issue to obtain a “unique” solution, we still need to deal with the
C!
permutations due to label-switching. This issue is, however, intrinsic to mixture models in general, unless we impose further constraints. In the context of LNL, the noise is often assumed to be non-dominant
[
25
,
50
,
52
]
. In other words:

\forall c,j\in\{1,\ldots,C\}:p(\hat{Y}=c|X,Y=c)\geq p(\hat{Y}=j|X,Y=c),

(8)

which means that the transition matrix is diagonally dominant. Otherwise, the concept of a class label might completely be switched to another class, causing a class mismatch with the one defined in the corresponding evaluation set.

One way to integrate the constraint in (
8
) is to design the prior parameter
\beta
to enforce the matrix
\rho_{i}

to be close to some diagonally dominant matrix, such as the identity matrix. Imposing such prior, however, increases the complexity of the objective function in
Eq.

5
, resulting in a complicated fine-tuning for
\beta
. In the implementation, we observe that simply initialising the parameter matrix
\rho_{i}
of
p(\hat{Y}|Y,X=\mathbf{x}_{i})
as a diagonally dominant matrix gives us quite accurate results. We, therefore, follow this approach as a simple way to mitigate the label-switching issue.

Related work

LNL has been studied since the 1980s with some early works focusing on the statistical point of view
[
1
,
3
]
, such as determining the number of samples to achieve certain prediction accuracy under certain types of label noise. The field has then attracted more research interest, especially in the era of deep learning where an increasing number of annotated data is required to train large deep learning models. Learning from noisy labels is inherently challenging due to the issue of identifiability. Despite its importance, the identifiability issue in LNL remains a partially addressed problem that requires the introduction of ad-hoc constraints or exploration through the use of multiple noisy labels.

Ad-hoc constraints

Many studies have implicitly or partially discussed the identifiability issue in the LNL problem and proposed practical methods designed with different heuristic criteria to make the problem identifiable. The most widely-used constraint is the
small loss criterion
where the labels of samples with small loss values are assumed to be clean
[
12
]
. Training is then carried out either on only those low-risk samples
[
12
]
or cast as a semi-supervised learning approach with those clean samples representing labelled data while the others denoting un-labelled data
[
23
]
. Although this line of research achieves remarkable results in several benchmarks, they still lack theoretical foundations that explain why the
small loss criterion
is effective. There is one recent attempt that theoretically investigates the
small loss hypothesis

[
11
]
, but the study is applicable only to the class-dependent (a.k.a. instance-independent) label noise setting that assumes the presence of “anchor points”, i.e., samples that are guaranteed to have clean labels. Other methods propose different ad-hoc constraints based on observations in matrix decomposition and geometry. For instance,
[
25
]
suggest the minimal volume of the simplex formed from the columns of transition matrices.
[
50
]
present a matrix decomposition approach and employ total variation regularisation to ensure the uniqueness of the solution.
[
6
]
impose similarity of transition matrices between samples that are close to each other.

Multiple noisy labels per instance

Learning from multiple noisy labels per instance has recently emerged as one approach to theoretically address the identifiability issue. To our best knowledge, the most relevant study in this area is the investigation of identifiability of transition matrices in noisy label learning
[
28
]
. In that paper, the authors implicitly extend the conventional 2-D transition matrix to a 3-D tensor with the third dimension representing annotators and exploit the results in 3-D array
[
21
,
22
]
to find the condition of identifiability. Similar to a study in crowd-sourcing literature
[
38
, Lemma 1]
, the authors in
[
28
]
conclude that at least 3 “informative” noisy labels per instance are needed. Although the finding is more optimistic than ours, it relies on the assumption of “informative” noisy labels, which requires a full-rank transition matrix for each annotator on each instance. Such assumption, however, lacks clarity since it is unclear how to translate the full-rankness required for a transition matrix to a property an annotator must have. Furthermore, it is unclear if the identifiability still holds when the transition matrices of two annotators are the same. In contrast, our result does not rely on any assumptions, such as the “informativeness” of noisy labels nor full-ranked transition matrices, except the iid-ness of multiple noisy labels.

6
Conclusion

This study has conducted a formal investigation into the identifiability of noisy label learning using multinomial mixture models. Specifically, we have formulated the label noise problem as a multinomial mixture model, where the clean label probability is represented as the mixture coefficient and each column in the transition matrix is represented as each multinomial component. Such modelling reveals that LNL is identifiable when there are at least
2C-1
noisy labels per sample provided; otherwise, the problem becomes non-identifiable. Hence, to address the noisy label problem, we propose a method that leverages nearest neighbours to generate additional noisy labels that satisfy the identifiability requirement. This approach enables us to estimate the clean labels using the EM algorithm. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed method performs competitively with existing SOTA methods on various challenging benchmarks, particularly in scenarios involving instance-dependent and real-world label noises.

Appendix A
EM for multinomial mixture models

This appendix presents the EM algorithm for multinomial mixture models mentioned in
Section

3.2.2
.

Recall that a mixture of multinomial distributions in the LNL problem can be written as:

p(\hat{Y}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}p(Y=c)\,p(\hat{Y}|Y=c)=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y};N,\rho_{ic}).

(
2
)

A.1
Maximum likelihood

Given
L
noisy labels
\{\hat{y}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
of an instance
X=\mathbf{x}_{i}
, the objective in terms of maximum likelihood estimation can be written as:

\max_{\pi_{i},\rho_{i}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\ln p(\hat{Y}=\mathbf{y}_{l}|X=\mathbf{x}_{i})=\max_{\pi_{i},\rho_{i}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y}=\mathbf{y}_{l};N,\rho_{ic}).

(9)

A.1.1
E-step

This step is to calculate the posterior of the latent variable
\mathbf{z}_{n}
given the data
\mathbf{x}_{n}
:

\begin{split}\gamma_{lc}&=p(Y=c|\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l},X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)},\rho_{i}^{(t)})\\
&=\frac{p(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l}|Y=c,X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\rho_{i}^{(t)})\,p(Y=c|X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}p(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l}|Y=c,X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\rho_{i}^{(t)})\,p(Y=c|X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)})}\\
&=\frac{\pi_{c}^{(t)}\,\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l};N,\rho_{ic}^{(t)})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}^{(t)}\,\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l};N,\rho_{ic}^{(t)})}.\end{split}

(10)

A.1.2
M-step

In the M-step, we maximise the following expected completed log-likelihood w.r.t.
\pi_{i}
and
\rho_{i}
:

\begin{split}Q&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l},X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)},\rho_{i}^{(t)})}\left[\ln p(\hat{Y},Y|X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i},\rho_{i})\right]\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l},X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)},\rho_{i}^{(t)})}\left[\ln p(Y|X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i})+\ln p(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l}|Y,X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\rho_{i})\right]\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y=c|\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l},X=\mathbf{x}_{i};\pi_{i}^{(t)},\rho_{i}^{(t)})}\left[\ln\pi_{ic}+\ln\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l};N,\rho_{ic})\right]\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\gamma_{nk}\left[\ln\pi_{ic}+\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{l}\ln\rho_{icc^{\prime}}+\mathrm{const.}\right].\end{split}

(11)

The Lagrangian for
\pi
can be written as:

Q_{\pi_{i}}=Q-\lambda\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}-1\right),

(12)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier. Taking derivative of the Lagrangian w.r.t.
\pi_{ic}
gives:

\partialderivative{Q_{\pi_{i}}}{\pi_{ic}}=\frac{1}{\pi_{ic}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}-\lambda.

(13)

Setting the derivative to zero and solving for
\pi_{ic}
gives:

\pi_{ic}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}.

(14)

And since
\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}=1
, one can substitute and find that
\lambda=L
. Thus:

\boxed{\pi_{ic}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}.}

(15)

Similarly, the Lagrangian of
\rho_{icc^{\prime}}
can be expressed as:

Q_{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}=Q-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\eta_{c}\left(\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}\rho_{icc^{\prime}}-1\right),

(16)

where
\eta_{c}
is the Lagrange multiplier. Taking derivative w.r.t.
\rho_{icc^{\prime}}
gives:

\partialderivative{Q_{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}}{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{lc^{\prime}}-\eta_{c}.

(17)

Setting the derivative to zero and solving for
\rho_{icc^{\prime}}
gives:

\rho_{icc^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\eta_{c}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{lc^{\prime}}.

(18)

The constraint on
\rho_{ic}
as a probability vector leads to
\eta_{c}=N\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}
. Thus:

\boxed{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{lc^{\prime}}}{N\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}}.}

(19)

A.2
Maximum a posterior (MAP)

The objective function is similar to the one in
Section

A.1
, except including the prior on
\pi_{i}
and
\rho_{i}
as follows:

\max_{\pi_{i},\rho_{i}}Q:=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_{ic}\mathrm{Mult}(\hat{Y}=\mathbf{y}_{l};N,\rho_{ic})+\ln p(\pi_{i};\alpha)+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\ln p(\rho_{ic};\beta),

(20)

where the two priors are:

\displaystyle p(\pi_{i};\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Dir}(\pi_{i};\alpha)

(21)

\displaystyle p(\rho_{ic};\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Dir}(\rho_{ic};\beta).

(22)

The E-step in this case remains unchanged from (
10
).

The derivative of the Lagrangian for
\pi
can be written as:

\partialderivative{Q_{\pi_{i}}^{\mathrm{MAP}}}{\pi_{ic}}=\frac{1}{\pi_{ic}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}+\alpha_{c}-1\right)-\lambda.

(23)

Thus:

\boxed{\pi_{ic}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}+\alpha_{c}-1}{L+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\alpha_{c}-C}.}

(24)

Similarly for
\rho_{icc^{\prime}}
:

\partialderivative{Q_{\rho}^{\mathrm{MAP}}}{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{lc^{\prime}}+\beta_{c^{\prime}}-1\right)-\eta_{c}.

(25)

Thus:

\boxed{\rho_{icc^{\prime}}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{lc^{\prime}}+\beta_{c^{\prime}}-1}{N\sum_{l=1}^{L}\gamma_{lc}+\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}\beta_{c^{\prime}}-C}.}

Appendix D
Experiment details

We use a mini-batch size of 128 training samples, perform a warm-up for 10 epochs and train for 200 epochs. The optimiser used is SGD with a momentum of 0.9 and an initial learning rate 0.01 when training without self-supervised learning and 0.001 when self-supervised learning models are used. The learning is decayed with cosine annealing with
T=1,000
. For the priors defined in (
5
), we assume both priors on the mixture coefficient (or clean label posterior) and the probability vector of the multinomial components in the transition matrix as symmetric Dirichlet distributions with
\alpha=20
and
\beta=5
. For the nearest neighbours, we first randomly sample a subset of 10,000 samples then perform nearest neighbour search and select the 100 nearest samples for LLC.
