Abstract
. A fundamental issue in the theory of time-dependent differential equations is to characterize precompact sets in Bochner spaces. We here survey the theory, starting with the classical Aubin–Lions inequality and its important extension by Dubinskiĭ. In particular, we give a simple and self-contained proof of the compactness result due to Chen, Jüngel, and Liu.

1.
Introduction

A common question in the theory of time-dependent differential equations
regards sufficient conditions for precompactness
of sets in a Bochner space
L^{p}(0,T;B)
,
where
B
is a Banach space and
(0,T)
is a time interval.
Recall that a subset
S
of a metric space is called
precompact

if every sequence in
S
has a convergent subsequence
(with a limit not necessarily in
S
).
Equivalently, the closure of
S
is compact.

The classical Aubin theorem
[
2
]
from 1963 states that
if
f_{n}
is bounded in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
where
X\subset B
is compactly embedded,
and the time derivative
\partial f_{n}/\partial t
is bounded in
L^{p}(0,T;Y)
,
where
B\subset Y
is continuously embedded,
then the sequence
(f_{n})
is relatively compact in
L^{p}(0,T;B)
.
A general discussion of this topic can be found
in the classical and widely cited paper by Simon
[
18
]
.
Later on, several generalizations and extensions have been proved.
Covering them all is beyond the scope of this paper.

A core part of Aubin’s argument is based on
what is frequently called the Aubin–Lions lemma.
We prefer to call it the Aubin–Lions
inequality

to avoid confusion with the compactness theorem
for which it is an important ingredient.
Note that some authors (e.g.,
[
4
]
)
refer to the resulting compactness theorem as the Aubin–Lions lemma.

Lemma

(Aubin–Lions inequality)
.

Let
X\subseteq B\subseteq Y
with a compact embedding of
X
in
B

and continuous embedding of
B
in
Y
.
Then for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
\eta
such that for all
v\in X

\lVert v\rVert_{B}\leq\varepsilon\lVert v\rVert_{X}+\eta\lVert v\rVert_{Y}.

The first proof can be found in Lions
[
14
, Prop. 4.1, p. 59]
,
see also
[
15
, Lemma 5.1, p. 58]
.

Remark
.

Some authors, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
3
]
,
refer to this as Ehrling’s lemma.
Also Nirenberg
[
16
]
quotes Ehrling’s result.
In Ehrling
[
7
, eq. (6)]

we find the following concrete estimate, resembling the above inequality:

(1.1)

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial D)}^{2}\leq A\big(h\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}(D)}^{2}+h^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big)

corresponding to
X=\dot{H}^{1}(D)
,
B=L^{2}(\partial D)
, and
Y=L^{2}(D)
with
h
positive.
Here,
\dot{H}
denotes a homogeneous Sobolev space.
However, these spaces do not have the above embedding properties.
Using this on the derivative yields
[
7
, eq. (7)]

\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}(\partial D)}^{2}\leq A\big(h\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{2}(D)}^{2}+h^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}(D)}^{2}\big).

Furthermore, he gets
[
7
, p. 272, line 7]

\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}(D)}^{2}\leq A\big(h\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{2}(D)}^{2}+h^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\big).

Estimate (
1.1
) appears as a “Peter–Paul” version
of the standard Sobolev estimate for traces,
namely
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial D)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1}(D)}
.
Since these estimates do not have the relevant embedding properties
and are stated for very concrete spaces,
the term “Ehrling’s lemma” does not seem justified.

Shortly after the appearance of Aubin’s paper, Dubinskiĭ

[
6
, Lemma 1, p. 229 of
[
1
]
]
1
1

1

[
1
]
contains the English translation of
[
6
]
,
see also
[
15
, Lemma 12.1, p. 141]
,
generalized the Aubin–Lions inequality in a very useful direction.
See
[
3
]
for a nice review
and correction of Dubinskiĭ’s argument.
His approach replaced the Banach space
X
with a cone
M
,
being a subset of a vector space that is closed
with respect to multiplication with nonnegative scalars,
and the norm by a non-negative homogeneous scalar function
(a
gauge
in our preferred terminology)
that only vanishes for a zero argument.
His aim was to show existence of unique weak solutions
of degenerate parabolic equations, an example being

u_{t}=\nabla\cdot\big(\lvert u\rvert^{\gamma}\nabla u\big)+h,

on a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
In order to accomplish this,
he proved the generalization of the Aubin–Lions inequality below.

We first define a gauge
[u]_{M}
on a cone
M

as a map
[\,\cdot\,]_{M}\colon M\to[0,\infty)

such that
[u]_{M}=0
if and only if
u=0
.
In addition we require
[\lambda u]_{M}=\lambda[u]_{M}
for all
\lambda\geq 0
.

Lemma

(Aubin–Lions–Dubinskiĭ inequality)
.

Let
A_{0},A_{1}
be normed spaces, and let
M
be a cone with gauge
[\,\cdot\,]_{M}
.
Assume that
M\subset A_{0}\subset A_{1}
with
M\subset A_{0}
compact
and
A_{0}\subset A_{1}
continuous embeddings, respectively.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
N(\varepsilon)

such that for all
u,v\in M
we have

\lVert u-v\rVert_{A_{0}}\leq\varepsilon([u]_{M}+[v]_{M})+N(\varepsilon)\lVert u-v\rVert_{A_{1}}.

Remark
.

Dubinskiĭ called
M

a seminormed non-negative cone
with
[\,\cdot\,]_{M}
a seminorm,
but we have used our preferred terminology instead.
Precise definitions will be given later.
He gives an example of a gauge as

[u]_{M}^{\alpha+\beta}=\int_{D}\lvert u\rvert^{\alpha}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{\beta}dx+\int_{\partial D}\lvert u\rvert^{\alpha+\beta}ds,

for
\alpha>0
,
\beta\geq 1
and
D
a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth boundary
\partial D
.
The cone
M
was the set of
u
satisfying
[u]_{M}<\infty
.
It is not closed under addition
(equivalently for cones, not convex).

Dubinskiĭ showed the following compactness result,
shown here as corrected and generalized by Barrett and Süli:

Theorem

(
[
6
, Thm. 1, p. 229]
,

[
3
, Thm. 2.1]
)
.

Let
A_{0},A_{1},M
be as in the lemma,
and
p
,
p_{1}\in[0,\infty]
with
(p,p_{1})\neq(\infty,1)
.
Further, let
\mathcal{Y}
be the set of Bochner measurable functions
(0,T)\to M

having a weak derivative
u_{t}
that is also Bochner measurable, such that

[u]_{\mathcal{Y}}:=\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}[u]_{M}^{p}\,dt\bigg)^{1/p}+\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert u_{t}\rVert_{A_{1}}^{p_{1}}\bigg)^{1/p_{1}}

(with the obvious modifications if
p=\infty
or
p_{1}=\infty
)
is finite.
Then
\mathcal{Y}\subset L^{p}((0,T);A_{0})
is a compact embedding.

Dubinskiĭ’s inequality was further generalized in
[
4
]
,
where the authors realized that one does not need the continuous embedding
A_{0}\subset A_{1}
.
More precisely they showed the following result.

Lemma
.

Let
B,Y
be Banach spaces, and let
M
be a cone in
B
with gauge
[\,\cdot\,]_{M}
.
Assume that
M\subset B
is compactly embedded.
Assume that for all
w_{n}\in B\cap Y

such that
w_{n}\to w
in
B
and
w_{n}\to 0
in
Y
we have that
w=0
.
Then we have that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
N(\varepsilon)

such that for all
u,v\in M
we have

\lVert u-v\rVert_{B}\leq\varepsilon([u]_{M}+[v]_{M})+N(\varepsilon)\lVert u-v\rVert_{Y}.

Under these conditions, they showed the following compactness result.

Theorem
.

Let
B,Y,M
be as in the lemma, and assume that
M\cap Y\neq\emptyset
.
Let
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Assume the conditions of the lemma are satisfied.
Let
U\subset L^{p}((0,T);M\cap Y)
be bounded in
L^{p}((0,T);M)
.
Furthermore, assume that translations are uniformly continuous, that is,

\lVert{\sigma_{h}u-u}\rVert_{L^{p}((0,T-h);Y)}\to 0
uniformly for
u\in U
as
h\to 0
.

Then
U
is relatively compact in
L^{p}((0,T);B)
.

Our interest in these questions arose from the obvious analogy
with precompact subsets of Lebesgue spaces.
The classical theorem of Kolmogorov–Riesz and its improvement by Sudakov
give a complete characterization of precompact subsets of Lebesgue spaces,
see
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
In the present paper, we draw on the analogy between these questions.
In particular, we shall prove versions of the results referenced above
for Bochner spaces based on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
rather than an interval
(0,T)
.
We strive for simplicity of proof and digestible and self-contained exposition.
We also suggest and use terminology somewhat different
from what is seen in the literature so far.

2.
Compactness for Bochner spaces based on Euclidean spaces

We start with a very brief overview
of the basic theory of Bochner spaces.
Consider a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
,
and a Banach space
B
.
A function
f\colon\Omega\to B
is called
Bochner measurable

if it is the
\mu
-almost everywhere limit
of a sequence of simple functions,
a simple function being one that takes only a finite number of values,
each on a measurable subset of
\Omega
with finite measure.
The Pettis measurability theorem
[
17
, Thm. 1.1]

states that
f
is Bochner measurable if, and only if,
it is weakly measurable and separably-valued.
Here,
f
is called
weakly measurable

if the composition of
f

with any bounded linear functional is measurable,
and
separably-valued
if there is a separable subspace
B^{\prime}\subseteq B

so that
f(x)\in B^{\prime}
for
\mu
-almost every
x\in\Omega
.
Pettis considered only the case where
\mu(\Omega)<\infty
,
but the
\sigma
-finite case follows easily.

The
Bochner space

L^{p}(\Omega,\mu;B)
(with
1\leq p<\infty
) consists of
all Bochner measurable functions
f

satisfying
\int_{\Omega}\lVert f\rVert^{p}\,d\mu<\infty
.
With the norm
\lVert f\rVert_{p}=\big(\int_{\Omega}\lVert f\rVert^{p}\,d\mu\big)^{1/p}
, the space

L^{p}(\Omega,\mu;B)
becomes a Banach space.

A note on notation: Whenever
f\colon\Omega\to B
,
we let
\lVert f\rVert
denote the
function

t\mapsto\lVert f(t)\rVert
.
Think of it as “pointwise norm”.
Accordingly, we never omit the subscript
p

on the
L^{p}
-norm
\lVert f\rVert_{p}
.

It turns out that the simple functions are dense in
L^{1}(\Omega,\mu;B)
.
This fact allows the definition of the
Bochner integral

\int_{\Omega}f\,d\mu
for all
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mu;B)
by continuity,
starting from the obvious definition of the integral for simple
f
.
Elementary properties like the
integral triangle inequality

\lVert\int_{\Omega}f\,d\mu\rVert\leq\int_{\Omega}\lVert f\rVert\,d\mu

are immediate consequences.

The totally bounded subsets of
L^{p}(\Omega,\mu;B)

were characterized by Diaz and Mayoral in
[
5
]
;
see
[
19
]
for an elementary proof.
This characterization is not well suited for applications in PDE theory, however.

From now on we concentrate our attention
on the Bochner spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)

(the use of Lebesgue measure is hidden in the notation).
We write
\lvert Q\rvert
for the Lebesgue measure of a set
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Our concern is with precompactness,
but let us turn our attention to a closely related concept.
Recall that a subset
S
of a metric space
X
is called
totally bounded

if for every
\varepsilon>0
,
S
can be covered by a finite number of sets,
each of diameter less than
\varepsilon
(an
\varepsilon
-cover).
Equivalently, for each
\varepsilon>0
, there is a finite subset of
S
(an
\varepsilon
-net)
so that every member of
S
is closer than
\varepsilon
to some member of the subset.
It is well known that a metric space is compact
if and only if it is complete and totally bounded.
It follows that a subset of a complete metric space
is precompact if and only if it is totally bounded.
In applications, one wants convergent subsequences,
i.e., one wants precompactness.
However, from now on we shall concentrate on total boundedness instead,
simply because that is what emerges from the proofs.

In the following definitions,

\mathcal{F}
is a subset of
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
, where
1\leq p<\infty
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

\mathcal{F}
is called

L^{p}
-equivanishing

if
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\mkern 1.0mu\lvert x\rvert>r\mkern 1.0mu]\,\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx\to 0

when
r\to\infty
, uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Here and later we employ the “Iverson bracket”
[
12
]
:
When
S
is a statement,
[\mkern 1.0muS\mkern 1.0mu]=1
if
S
is true,
and
[\mkern 1.0muS\mkern 1.0mu]=0
if
S
is false.

Definition 2.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

\mathcal{F}
is called

L^{p}
-equicontinuous

if
\lVert\sigma_{h}f-f\rVert_{p}\to 0

when
\lvert h\rvert\to 0
, uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Here
\sigma_{h}f(x)=f(x+h)
(
x
,
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

It is easily seen that a totally bounded set
is
L^{p}
-equivanishing and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous,
since it is true of any singleton set
and hence of any finite set.

Definition 2.3
.

\mathcal{F}
is called

totally bounded in the mean

if for each bounded measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\{\int_{E}f(x)\,dx\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is totally bounded in
B
.

A totally bounded set is totally bounded in the mean
because the map
f\mapsto\int_{E}f(x)\,dx
is uniformly continuous.

The following theorem is due (at least) to Aubin and Simon
in the case of functions supported on a bounded interval.
(In that case,
L^{p}
-equivanishing is of course irrelevant.)
It may also be considered a variant of the Kolmogorov–Riesz theorem
– and indeed, our proof is a straightforward adaptation
of the proof of the Kolmogorov–Riesz theorem
given in
[
9
]
.
All the compactness results below
will be proved by reducing them to this theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
B
be a Banach space,
and
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
a subset with
1\leq p<\infty
.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded if and only if
it is
L^{p}
-equivanishing,
L^{p}
-equicontinuous,
and totally bounded in the mean.

We shall prove the theorem using the following lemma.
We omit the trivial proof.
The reader can probably construct one
in less time than it would take to look it up in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
9
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space.
Assume that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some

\delta>0
, a metric space
W
​, and a mapping

\Phi\colon X\to W
so that
\Phi[X]
is totally bounded, and
whenever
x,y\in X
are such that

d\big(\Phi(x),\Phi(y)\big)<\delta
, then
d(x,y)<\varepsilon
.
Then
X
is totally bounded.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The necessity of the three conditions
was dealt with above.

Now assume that the three conditions are satisfied.
Let
\varepsilon>0
, and pick
R
and
\rho>0
so that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\mkern 1.0mu\lvert x\rvert>R\mkern 1.0mu]\,\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx<\varepsilon^{p}

(
L^{p}
-equivanishing)
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lVert f({x+h})-f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx<\varepsilon^{p}
whenever
\lvert h\rvert<\rho

(
L^{p}
-equicontinuity).

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed cube with diameter less than
\rho
,
centered at the origin.
Let
Q_{i}
,
i=1
, …,
N
, be non-overlapping
(in the sense of pairwise disjoint interiors)
translates of
Q

whose union contains

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lvert x\rvert<R\}
.
Define the map

P\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)\to B^{N},\quad(Pf)_{i}=f_{i}:=\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{Q_{i}}f\,dx.

Note that
\lvert Q_{i}\rvert=\lvert Q\rvert
, so the integral is an average over
Q_{i}
.
By total boundedness in the mean,
\{Pf\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in\mathcal{F}\}
is totally bounded.
Note that

\displaystyle\lVert f(x)-f_{i}\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle=\Big\lVert\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{Q_{i}}\big(f(x)-f(y)\big)\,dy\Big\rVert^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f(y)\rVert\,dy\Big)^{p}\leq\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f(y)\rVert^{p}\,dy

using first the integral triangle inequality and then Jensen’s inequality, and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f_{i}\rVert^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f(y)\rVert^{p}\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\int_{2Q}\lVert f(x)-f(x+h)\rVert^{p}\,dh\,dx

\displaystyle=\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{2Q}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f(x+h)\rVert^{p}\,dx\,dh

\displaystyle\leq\lvert Q\rvert^{-1}\int_{2Q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lVert f(x)-f(x+h)\rVert^{p}\,dx\,dh<2^{d}\varepsilon^{p},

at the end using the
L^{p}
-equicontinuity inequality
and the fact that
\lvert h\rvert<\rho
when
h\in 2Q
.

Now consider two functions
f
,
g\in\mathcal{F}
.
Using the
L^{p}
-equivanishing inequality, the triangle inequality in
B
,
Minkowski’s inequality, and the above estimate, we find

\displaystyle\lVert f-g\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle<2\varepsilon+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-g(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\big(\lVert f(x)-f_{i}\rVert+\lVert f_{i}-g_{i}\rVert+\lVert f_{i}(x)-g(x)\rVert\big)^{p}\,dx\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f(x)-f_{i}\rVert^{p}\,dx\bigg)^{1/p}+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert f_{i}-g_{i}\rVert^{p}\,dx\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}\leq 2\varepsilon}+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{Q_{i}}\lVert g_{i}-g(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx\bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle<(2+2^{1+d/p})\varepsilon+\bigg(\lvert Q\rvert\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert f_{i}-g_{i}\rVert^{p}\bigg)^{1/p}.

Thus, if
\big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert f_{i}-g_{i}\rVert^{p}\big)^{1/p}<\lvert Q\rvert^{-1/p}\varepsilon

then
\lVert f-g\rVert_{p}<(3+2^{1+d/p})\varepsilon
.
By Lemma
2.5
and the total boundedness of
\{Pf\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in\mathcal{F}\}
,

\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded.
∎

Directly proving that a family of functions
is totally bounded in the mean can be difficult.
However, a common scenario considers
L^{p}
-functions
with values in a compactly embedded subspace.
As mentioned in the introduction,
this has since been generalized,
replacing the subspace by a cone.
Once the usefulness of this generalization is realized,
the proof turns out to require little extra work
compared to the original setting.

We start with some definitions.
Note that we deviate from the terminology commonly seen in the literature
(indicated in parentheses below),
which we find cumbersome and somewhat confusing.

Definitions 2.6
.

A
cone
(nonnegative cone) is a nonempty subset
X
of some vector space
so that
ax\in X
whenever
a\in[0,\infty)
and
x\in X
.

A
gauge
(seminorm) on a cone
X

is a map
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\colon X\to[0,\infty)

so that
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}ax\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}=a\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}x\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}
whenever
x\in X
and
a\geq 0
,
and
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}x\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}>0
if
x\neq 0
.
A cone with a gauge is called a
gauged cone

(seminormed non-negative cone).

A gauged cone
X
in a Banach space is called
a
compactly gauged cone

(compactly embedded seminormed non-negative cone (!))
if
X_{r}:=\{x\in X\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}x\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\leq r\}
is precompact for
r=1

– and hence for all
r>0
, since
X_{r}=rX_{1}
.

Now assume that
X
is a compactly gauged cone in a Banach space
B
.
Then there is a constant
C
such that
\lVert x\rVert\leq C\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}x\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}
for any
x\in X
.
We may assume without loss of generality that
C=1
, so
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert\leq\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}
.
Define
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
to be the set of all functions

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to X

such that
f
is Bochner measurable as a function into
B
,
and
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}
is measurable as well,
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}^{p}\,dx<\infty
.
Write
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}_{p}=\big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}^{p}\,dx\big)^{1/p}
.

The measurability of
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}

does not follow automatically from Bochner measurability,
as is shown by the following simple example:
Let
B=\mathbb{C}
as a real vector space,
let
X
be the upper half plane,
let
\psi\colon(0,\pi)\to(0,1)
be a non-measurable function,
put
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}te^{i\theta}\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}=t\psi(\theta)
for
t\geq 0
and
\theta\in(0,\pi)
,
and define
f(\theta)=e^{i\theta}
for
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.

We can now state the following result.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
X
be a compactly gauged cone in a Banach space
B
,
and let
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
be bounded

(
i.e., there is a uniform bound on
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}_{p}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
)
.
Assume further that
\mathcal{F}
is
L^{p}
-equivanishing
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
.

We need a definition and some lemmas for the proof.

Definition 2.8
.

A set
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\Omega,\mu;B)
is called
uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable

if for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
r
so that

\int_{\Omega}[\mkern 1.0mu\lVert f\rVert>r\mkern 1.0mu]\,\lVert f\rVert^{p}\,d\mu<\varepsilon\qquad(f\in\mathcal{F}).

With this definition we get the following result.

Lemma 2.9
.

A totally bounded subset of
L^{p}(\Omega,\mu;B)
is uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable.

We omit the easy proof,
hinting only that it is true for a singleton set,
hence for a finite set.
Now use total boundedness to approximate the given set by a finite set.
See also
[
19
]
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
is
bounded,
L^{p}
-equivanishing,
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable.

Proof.

First, note that the set
\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert:=\{\lVert f\rVert\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in\mathcal{F}\}

is also bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous –
the latter follows from

\big\lvert\lVert f(x+h)\rVert-\lVert f(x)\rVert\big\rvert\leq\lVert f(x+h)-f(x)\rVert
. The set

\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert
is
L^{p}
-equivanishing, because
\mathcal{F}
is.
And since bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}
are totally bounded,

\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert
is totally bounded by Theorem
2.4
.
(We could also use the Kolmogorov–Riesz theorem
[
9
, Thm. 5]
here.)
By Lemma
2.9
,
\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert
is uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable,
and hence (trivially) so is
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
is uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable,
then for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
so that

\int_{D}\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx<\varepsilon

whenever
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is measurable with
\lvert D\rvert<\delta
.

Proof.

Simply write

\int_{D}\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx=\int_{D}[\mkern 1.0mu\lVert f(x)\rVert\leq r\mkern 1.0mu]\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx+\int_{D}[\mkern 1.0mu\lVert f(x)\rVert>r\mkern 1.0mu]\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx,

pick
r
so that the last integral is less than
\varepsilon/2

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
(with
D=\mathbb{R}^{d}
),
and then note that the first integral is at most
\delta r^{p}
.
Choosing
\delta=\varepsilon/(2r^{p})
yields the desired inequality.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

In light of Theorem
2.4
,
we only need to show that
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded in the mean.

First, take any
\varepsilon>0
.
Combining Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
,
there is some
\delta>0
so that
\int_{D}\lVert f(x)\rVert^{p}\,dx<\varepsilon^{p}

whenever
\lvert D\rvert<\delta
.

Write
\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}_{p}\leq M
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then
\lvert\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f(x)\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}>r\}\rvert<(M/r)^{p}
.
Hence, if
r>M/\delta^{1/p}
,
we get
\lVert[\mkern 1.0mu\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}>r\mkern 1.0mu]\,f\rVert_{p}<\varepsilon
.
That is,
\lVert f-\tilde{f}\rVert_{p}<\varepsilon
,
where we write
\tilde{f}=[\mkern 1.0mu\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\leq r\mkern 1.0mu]\,f
.

Next, note that

\tilde{f}
takes values in the totally bounded set
X_{r}
.
The convex hull
\co X_{r}
is totally bounded as well.
Consider a bounded measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The mean value
\lvert E\rvert^{-1}\int_{E}\tilde{f}\,dx

belongs to the closure
\cco X_{r}
of
\co X_{r}
,
which is also totally bounded.

From the above estimates,
the integral
\int_{E}f\,dx

has distance less than
\varepsilon
to the compact set
\lvert E\rvert\cco X_{r}
.
Since this is so for any
\varepsilon>0

(note that
r
depends on
\varepsilon>0
),
the set
\{\int_{E}f\,dx\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f\in\mathcal{F}\}

is totally bounded.
This completes the proof.
∎

The first condition of Theorem
2.7
allows us
to somewhat relax the
L^{p}
-equicontinuity condition:
We merely need to assume this condition in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};Y)
,
where
B
is continuously embedded in a Banach space
Y
.
The resulting setting can be summarized by the formula

X\Subset B\subseteq Y
,
where
X
is a compactly gauged cone in
B

and
B
is embedded in
Y
.

However, a yet more general assumption,
first identified in
[
4
]
,
avoids even the need for an embedding,
and instead has
B
and
Y
be Banach spaces embedded in some common space,
and
X\subset B\cap Y
with
X\Subset B
.
This requires one more technical assumption,
detailed in the lemma below.
The assumption clearly holds when
B
is embedded in
Y
,
and also in the very common situation where both spaces
are embedded in a common topological vector space,
such as a space of vector-valued distributions.

We will write
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
for the norm on
B
,
and
\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}
for the norm on
Y
.
Correspondingly,
we also write
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{p}
and
\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}_{p}

for the norms in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
and
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};Y)

respectively.

Lemma 2.12

(
[
4
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let
(B,\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert)
and
(Y,\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}})

be Banach spaces which are subspaces of the same
vector space,
and let
X\subset B
be a compactly gauged cone in
B
.
Assume that there is no sequence in
B\cap Y
converging in
Y
to
0

and in
B
to some non-zero vector.
Then for each
\eta>0
there exists
c>0

such that

\lVert u-v\rVert\leq\eta\big(\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}v\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\big)+c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}u-v\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\qquad\text{for all $u$, $v\in X$.}

Proof.

First, note the following reformulation of the stated sequence
condition:

Each non-zero
x\in B
has a neighborhood
U
in
B

so that
\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}
has a positive lower bound on
U\cap Y
.

(If
U\cap Y=\emptyset
, we may take that lower bound to be
+\infty
.)
It follows that

\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}
has a positive lower bound on
K\cap Y

for any compact set
K\subset B\setminus\{0\}
.

Now let
K
be the closure in
X
of the set

\{u-v\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}u,v\in X\text{ and }\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}v\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\leq 1\}
,
which is totally bounded in
B
because
\{u\in X\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\leq 1\}

is totally bounded and subtraction is uniformly continuous.
Thus
K
is compact in
B
,
and so is
L=\{w\in K\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\lVert w\rVert\geq\eta\}
.
Since
0\notin L
,

\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\,\cdot\,\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}
has a positive lower bound on
L\cap Y
,
so for a sufficiently large
c
,

\lVert w\rVert\leq\eta+c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}w\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}

for all
w\in L
, and hence for all
w\in K
.
That is,

\lVert u-v\rVert\leq\eta+c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}u-v\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\qquad\text{for all $u$, $v\in K$ with $\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}v\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\leq 1$.}

Replacing arbitrary
u
,
v\in X
by

u/\big(\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}v\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\big)
and

v/\big(\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}u\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}v\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\big)
respectively
yields the desired result.
∎

Theorem 2.13

(compare Thm.
3.4
below)
.

Let
B
and
Y
be Banach spaces which are subspaces of the same
vector space,
and let
X\subset B
be a compactly gauged cone in
B
.
Assume that there is no sequence in
B\cap Y
converging in
Y
to
0

and in
B
to some non-zero vector.
Further, assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};Y)

is bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
,

L^{p}
-equivanishing in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
,
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};Y)
,
where
1\leq p<\infty
.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
.

Proof.

We only need to prove that
\mathcal{F}

is
L^{p}
-equicontinuous in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};B)
,
and then Theorem
2.7
takes care of the rest.

To this end, let
\varepsilon>0
,
pick
\eta>0
(to be determined later), and pick a constant
c

as in the statement of Lemma
2.12
.
If
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
then

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma_{h}f-f\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\lVert f(x+h)-f(x)\big\rVert^{p}\,dx\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\eta\big(\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f(x+h)\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}+\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f(x)\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}\big)+c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\big|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu\big|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu\big|$\crcr}}}}f(x+h)-f(x)\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\big|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu\big|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu\big|$\crcr}}}}\Big)^{p}\,dx\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}_{p}+c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\sigma_{h}f-f\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}_{p}.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is bounded in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};X)
,
we can pick
\eta
to ensure that
2\eta\mathopen{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\langle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\langle$\crcr}}}f\mathclose{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\rangle$\cr$\mkern 2.0mu\rangle$\crcr}}}_{p}<\varepsilon
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
After this is done,
c
is now fixed.
If
\lvert h\rvert
is small enough, we have
c\mathopen{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}\sigma_{h}f-f\mathclose{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$|$\cr$\mkern 2.5mu|$\cr$\mkern 5.0mu|$\crcr}}}}_{p}<\varepsilon

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, since
\mathcal{F}
is
L^{p}
-equicontinuous in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};Y)
.
Thus we have
\lVert\sigma_{h}f-f\rVert<2\varepsilon
,
and the proof is complete.
∎

3.
Compactness for Bochner spaces based on bounded intervals

We have expressed our results so far
for functions defined on a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
since this seems natural.
However, in the most commonly occurring applications in PDE theory,
an interval
(0,T)
is used instead.
We can of course embed
L^{p}(0,T;B)
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R};B)

by extending functions to be zero outside
(0,T)
.
All of our notions and results survive this extension intact,
with one exception, namely, the definition of
L^{p}
-continuity
(Definition
2.2
).
The common definition for functions on
(0,T)
is as follows.
The seemingly minor, but potentially damaging, difference
is the need to restrict the interval of integration
to the part where
\sigma_{h}f(t)=f(t+h)
is defined:

Definition 3.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(0,T;B)
is called

L^{p}
-equicontinuous
if
\int_{0}^{T-h}\lVert f(t+h)-f(t)\rVert^{p}\,dt\to 0

when
0<h\to 0
, uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

If we extend the integral to all of
\mathbb{R}
,
we get Definition
2.2
.
However, in doing so, we have to contend with two extra terms

\int_{0}^{h}\lVert f\rVert^{p}\,dt
and
\int_{T-h}^{T}\lVert f\rVert^{p}\,dt
.
It turns out that these extra terms vanish in the limit
if we assume also that
\mathcal{F}
is bounded.
The following proposition allows us to apply all the compactness results
of the previous section to families of functions in
L^{p}(0,T;B)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(0,T;B)
is bounded
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous according to Definition
3.1
.
Then, if we extend each function to
\mathbb{R}

by setting it zero outside
(0,T)
,
the resulting set is
L^{p}
-equicontinuous
according to Definition
2.2
.

The proof depends on a slight detour:

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(0,T;B)

(1\leq p<\infty)
is bounded
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous,
then
\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert^{p}
is
L^{1}
-equicontinuous.

Proof.

Assume
1<p<\infty
, and let
q
be the conjugate exponent.
(The case
p=1
is immediate.)
Writing
a\vee b
for the maximum of
a
and
b
,
and assuming
0<h<T
, we estimate

\int_{0}^{T-h}\big\lvert\lVert f(t+h)\rVert^{p}-\lVert f(t)\rVert^{p}\big\rvert\,dt\\
\begin{aligned} \qquad&\leq p\int_{0}^{T-h}\big(\lVert f(t+h)\rVert\vee\lVert f(t)\rVert\big)^{p-1}\big\lvert\lVert f(t+h)\rVert-\lVert f(t)\rVert\big\rvert\,dt\\
&\leq p\Big(\int_{0}^{T-h}\big(\lVert f(t+h)\rVert\vee\lVert f(t)\rVert\big)^{(p-1)q}\,dt\Big)^{1/q}\cdot\Big(\int_{0}^{T-h}\big\lvert\lVert f(t+h)\rVert-\lVert f(t)\rVert\big\rvert^{p}\,dt\Big)^{1/p}\\
&\leq p\Big(\int_{0}^{T-h}\big(\lVert f(t+h)\rVert+\lVert f(t)\rVert\big)^{p}\,dt\Big)^{1/q}\cdot\Big(\int_{0}^{T-h}\lVert f(t+h)-f(t)\rVert^{p}\,dt\Big)^{1/p}\end{aligned}

using the Hölder inequality and noting that
(p-1)q=p
.
The first integral on the final line is uniformly bounded
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
and the desired result follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We only need to show that

\int_{0}^{h}\lVert f(t)\rVert^{p}\,dt\to 0
and

\int_{T-h}^{T}\lVert f(t)\rVert^{p}\,dt\to 0

as
0<h\to 0
, uniformly for
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
(Proving the sufficiency of this claim is left to the reader.)
We only prove the former;
the latter is shown similarly.

By Lemma
3.3
,
we may as well replace
\mathcal{F}
by
\lVert\mathcal{F}\rVert^{p}
,
and
p
by
1
;
i.e., we assume that
B=\mathbb{R}
,
p=1

and that
\mathcal{F}
consists of non-negative functions.

We shall prove the contrapositive:
Assume that
\varepsilon>0
and that for every
h>0

there is some
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
\int_{0}^{h}f\,dt\geq\varepsilon
,
and also that

\mathcal{F}
is
L^{p}
-equicontinuous according to Definition
3.1
.

We shall prove that then
\mathcal{F}
is unbounded.

To this end, pick any
\eta>0

and let
\rho\in(0,T)
be so that

\int_{0}^{T-h}\lVert f(t+h)-f(t)\rVert\,dt<\eta
whenever
0<h<\rho
.
Next pick any natural number
n
,
let
h\in(0,\rho)
with
(n+1)h<T
,
and let
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
\int_{0}^{h}f\,dt\geq\varepsilon
.
Write
f_{i}(t)=f(ih+t)
, for
t\in(0,h)
and
i=0
,
1
, …,
n
.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=n\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(n-i)\big(\lVert f_{i+1}\rVert_{1}-\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{1}\big)

\displaystyle\geq n\varepsilon-n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big\lvert\lVert f_{i+1}\rVert_{1}-\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{1}\big\rvert\geq n\varepsilon-n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lVert f_{i+1}-f_{i}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq n\varepsilon-n\int_{0}^{T-h}\lvert f(t+h)-f(t)\rvert\,dt\geq n(\varepsilon-\eta).

Thus merely picking
\eta<\varepsilon
and
n
large
(forcing
h
to be small),
we get arbitrarily large values for
\lVert f\rVert_{1}
with
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Combining Prop.
3.2
and Thm.
2.13

immediately yields the following:

Theorem 3.4

(
[
4
, Thm. 1]
)
.

Let
B
and
Y
be Banach spaces which are subspaces of the same
vector space,
and let
X\subset B
be a compactly gauged cone in
B
.
Assume that there is no sequence in
B\cap Y
converging in
Y
to
0

and in
B
to some non-zero vector.
Further, assume that
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{p}(0,T;X)\cap L^{p}(0,T;Y)

is bounded in
L^{p}(0,T;X)
,

L^{p}
-equivanishing in
L^{p}(0,T;B)
,
and
L^{p}
-equicontinuous in
L^{p}(0,T;Y)
,
where
1\leq p<\infty
.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is totally bounded in
L^{p}(0,T;B)
.
