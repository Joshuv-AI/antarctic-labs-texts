A polytopal discrete de Rham complex on manifolds, with application to the Maxwell equations
Jérôme Droniou, Marien Hanot, Todd Oliynyk

Abstract
We design in this work a discrete de Rham complex on manifolds. This complex, written in the framework of exterior calculus, has the same cohomology as the continuous de Rham complex, is of arbitrary order of accuracy and, in principle, can be designed on meshes made of generic elements (that is, elements whose boundary is the union of an arbitrary number of curved edges/faces). Notions of local (full and trimmed) polynomial spaces are developed, with compatibility requirements between polynomials on mesh entities of various dimensions. We give explicit constructions of such polynomials in 2D, for some meshes made of curved triangles or quadrangles (such meshes are easy to design in many cases, starting from a few charts describing the manifold). The discrete de Rham complex is then used to set up a scheme for the Maxwell equations on a 2D manifold without boundary, and we show that a natural discrete version of the constraint linking the electric field and the electric charge density is satisfied. Numerical examples are provided on the sphere and the torus, based on bespoke analytical solutions and meshes on each of these manifolds. Key words. Discrete de Rham complex, manifolds, exterior calculus, Maxwell equations, numerical simulations MSC2020. 65M50, 65M60, 35Q61, 78M10.

Introduction

The goal of this paper is to design a discrete version of the de Rham complex on manifolds, which can be built on meshes made of generic elements and has the same cohomology as the continuous de Rham complex. This complex can be used to design schemes for partial differential equations (PDEs) which inherit some important stability properties from the continuous models (such as energy bounds or constraint preservation).

Let
\Omega
be an
n
-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold with or without boundary. In the exterior calculus framework, the continuous de Rham complex on
\Omega
is

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{d}}

Several approaches have been employed to achieve such a discretisation. One of the most successful one is the Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC), which provides a generic framework for constructing and analysing discrete complexes
[
3
,
2
]
. However, its design – based on explicit locally polynomial and globally conforming functions – makes it only applicable to certain meshes, mostly made of triangles/tetrahedra or quadrangles/hexahedra. We also note the broader framework of Generalised Finite Element Systems
[
19
]
, but whose analysis tools are currently limited to algebraic properties of the discrete complexes, and does not provide explicit means to construct fully computable complexes on generic meshes.

The application of FEEC to computational electromagnetism on domains in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was pioneered by A. Bossavit
[
14
,
12
,
13
]
. Subsequent developments are discussed in
[
34
,
35
]
, along with references therein. Despite significant progress, to our knowledge the application of FEEC techniques in electromagnetism has so far remained restricted to flat domains in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

A recent new trend is that of polytopal methods, that is, numerical methods that are applicable on meshes made of generic polygons/polyhedra (polytopes), and have an arbitrary degree of accuracy. The Virtual Element Method (VEM)
[
8
,
7
]
and Discrete De Rham (DDR)
[
27
,
24
]
are two main examples of approaches to building
polytopal discrete complexes
(these two approaches can actually be bridged, see
[
9
]
). Such complexes have benefits over FEEC ones: the flexibility of polytopal meshes leads to seamless local mesh refinement (to better capture, e.g., stiff solutions or complex geometries), and their high-level approach (which does not require globally conforming basis functions) may lead to leaner constructions using systematic processes in their design, such as static condensation and serendipity; see
[
20
,
10
,
15
,
25
,
23
]
for examples of the efficiency of discrete polytopal complexes.

The question of stability of discretisations of PDEs also applies to models set on manifolds, and leads to the natural question of designing discrete versions of the de Rham complex on such spaces.
In the finite element setting, most studies have been done on embedded manifolds – typically, a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. An initial (extrinsic) approach is to approximate the surface by a piecewise linear surface,
on which a triangulations and finite element spaces can be easily constructed,
and to numerically approximate the PDE on that approximated surface
[
22
,
21
]
(see also
[
2
]
for FEEC and
[
30
]
for VEM).
The drawback of this simple – although completely computable – approach is its practical limitation to low-order methods because the error committed by approximating the surface limits the accuracy of the resulting scheme
(unless we have an explicit knowledge – which is rare – of the distance function in a tubular neighbourhood of the manifold).
To mitigate this issue, a second (intrinsic) approach consists in defining triangulations and finite element spaces directly on the manifold,
and in therefore discretising the PDE without the geometrical error created by approximating the manifold, see
[
4
]
and reference therein.
The challenge is then to define a notion of piecewise polynomial functions on the triangulation of the manifold.
One option is through the usage of an explicit piecewise linear manifold,
on which finite element triangulations and spaces can be trivially defined (the notion of polynomials being obvious),
and to transport these triangulations and spaces onto the physical manifold.
In
[
4
]
, it is required that this approximate manifold be contained on an open set close enough to the surface to ensure the
uniqueness, on that open set, of the orthogonal projection on the manifold;
this is actually not a requirement, as the mere existence of an homeomorphism between the physical and piecewise linear manifolds is sufficient to transport all finite element spaces
(
H^{1}
-conforming, but also
\mathbf{H}(\CURL)
- or
\mathbf{H}(\DIV)
-conforming) through pull-back
[
37
]
.
Another, strongly related, approach to construct intrinsic triangulations and finite element spaces is to assume the existence of a global explicit system of coordinates on the manifold;
this system can then be used to represent the mesh and local polynomial spaces (and amounts to the above-mentioned homeomorphism and pull-back approach).
This, however, has some limitations on the type of manifold that can be considered (a sphere, e.g., cannot be covered by a single chart).
The reference
[
5
]
describes this approach in the context of VEM,
and highlights that the usage of a single chart on a sphere generates a loss of accuracy due to the singularity of this chart at a point ; it is numerically shown that using two charts can mitigate this issue, but the design of the method does not account for the usage of multiple charts.

In all cases, the existence of a global object to represent the manifold (piecewise linear manifold approximating and/or homeomorphic to the physical manifold) can be established,
but the explicit description of this object remains elusive except in special cases,
which mainly restricts global approaches to theoretical analysis, or requires to construct computable spaces that introduce an additional geometrical error, the control of which requiring additional properties on the global object – e.g.,
that the homeomorphism is close to an isometry.
See the discussion in
[
37
]
, which designs and analyses a FEEC on manifolds.

The reason for the need of a global approach mostly lies in the requirement for finite element spaces to be subspaces of the continuous spaces (conforming approximation).
This is also visible in the abstract construction of
[
37
, Section 3-4]
where, although the design of the polynomial spaces is done locally through pullbacks,
the continuity requirements of the
smooth triangulation
ultimately makes it non-computable (except for trivial case).

Polytopal methods do not have such a requirement. Instead, their discrete spaces can be interpreted as spaces of vectors of polynomial functions on mesh entities of various dimensions
(elements, but also faces, edges, vertices);
these polynomial functions are completely unrelated with each other: for example,
polynomials on the faces do not have to be the traces of polynomials in the elements;
see
[
9
,
11
]
for DDR and VEM.
As a consequence, the restrictions – even on flat spaces – imposed on the geometry of the mesh elements by finite element methods are lifted,
and there is no issue in gluing local constructions to form a global space (no global property on the space needs to be imposed).
The trade-off is that notions of polynomials on faces and edges must be available.
This is not in itself an issue even on manifolds, as local coordinates can be used for this,
but when considering
complexes
, some compatibility conditions are necessary;
in particular, traces on faces of polynomials in elements must be polynomial on faces
(which imposes a compatibility of the local coordinates used to define polynomials).
Moreover,
trimmed
local polynomial spaces play an important role in discrete complexes
[
2
,
11
,
7
]
;
when going to polytopal complexes on manifolds, such notions of spaces must therefore also be defined,
which raises questions on intrinsic Koszul operators in local coordinates and their compatibility conditions to ensure that traces of trimmed polynomial spaces remain trimmed polynomial spaces of the same degree.

At this point, it is worthwhile noticing other approaches to solving partial differential equations on manifolds that have been developed, primarily by the General Relativity community. The oldest approach is the Regge calculus
[
31
,
40
,
43
]
, which is a lattice based method for numerically solving the Einstein equations. Related to the Regge calculus is the smooth lattice method developed in
[
18
]
. This method offer significant improvements over the Regge calculus and has been used to solve the Einstein equations on spacetime manifolds with compact spacial slices
[
16
,
17
]
.
In a different direction, an approach to solving partial differential equations, in particular the Einstein equations, on spacetime manifolds that is based on local discrete coordinate patches was developed in
[
42
]
.
More recently, the authors of the articles
[
38
,
39
,
44
]
developed a multi-cube decomposition method for solving partial differential on compact manifolds and employed this method to construct numerical solutions of the Einstein constraint equations on various compact three-manifolds. Finally, we mention the article
[
29
]
, which is most relevant to our results, where a discrete exterior calculus is employed to design a numerical scheme for solving the Einstein equations. This approach has been used to find solutions to the Einstein equations on spacetimes with compact spatial slices in
[
41
]
. All these methods, however, are limited to meshes with elements having a specific shape (tetrahedral or cubic).

In this work, we tackle the question of designing a discrete complex on generic (not necessarily embedded) manifolds, that supports meshes having generic element shapes.
We define suitable notions of local (complete and trimmed) polynomial spaces on a generic mesh of
\Omega
, tackling the issue of compatibility of traces of polynomials. Like the abstract setting of
[
37
]
, our construction relies on mappings between subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the manifold.
However, our compatibility requirement between the mappings is much milder. This added flexibility enables the construction of computable mappings.
Our construction works even when several charts are used to represent the manifold, and does not require a global homeomorphism between the manifold and a piecewise linear manifold;
it is also purely local, and we provide practical spaces on various polygonal elements.
We then use these local polynomial spaces to design,
following the construction in
[
11
]
, a discrete version of the de Rham complex of differential forms on the manifold. Some key properties of this complex are stated,
including the isomorphism of its cohomology with that of the de Rham complex, as well as its primal consistency (Theorem
19
), which is one of the main novelties of this work as it does not follow as in the regular (flat) DDR complex – in particular, it is not a consequence of an exact consistency on polynomial forms.
We derive a 2+1 formulation of the Maxwell equations on 2D manifolds, and use the DDR complex to design a scheme for these equations;
the preservation of constraint follows from the properties of the discrete complex.
The behaviour of the scheme is numerically illustrated on manufactured solutions on the sphere and the torus; to do so, we
design suitable meshes (based on two charts of each manifold) on which local polynomial spaces with compatibility conditions can be constructed.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the abstract setting required to define the DDR complex on a manifold; in particular, we identify a suitable notion of
polynomial spaces on a mesh of the manifold, that allows us to construct trimmed polynomial spaces and such that restrictions of polynomials on lower-dimensional mesh entities are also polynomials on these mesh entities. This abstract setting is used in Section
3
to build the discrete spaces and operators of the discrete de Rham complex of differential
forms on the manifold, following the design in
[
11
]
. We state the main properties (consistency, commutation, isomorphism of cohomologies) of this DDR complex. In Section
4
shows how to explicitly construct suitable polynomial spaces,
satisfying the setting of Section
2
, provided that local charts with a compatibility condition (between charts of a face and charts of its sub-faces) can be found.
Section
5
then tackles an application of the discrete de Rham complex: the electromagnetism equations on a manifold. We recall the 2+1 formulation of the Maxwell equations in differential forms, and build a scheme for this model. The properties of the DDR complex show that this scheme preserves a discrete version of the constraint on the electric field as well as the energy of the model. We finally perform numerical tests on the 2-dimensional sphere and torus, illustrating the practical behaviour and accuracy of the scheme. Three appendices conclude the paper: Appendix
A
details a practical construction of polynomial spaces on a 2D manifold mesh made of curved 3- or 4-edge elements, Appendix
B
recalls important tensor calculus constructions and derives the 2+1 formulation of the elecromagnetism equations, and Appendix
C
presents the analytical solutions used in the numerical simulations.

2
Setting

2.1
Mesh

We let
\mathcal{M}_{h}
be a mesh made of a finite collection of submanifolds of dimensions
d=0,\ldots,n
that partition
\Omega
. For such a
d
, the set
\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
collects all the submanifolds in
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of dimension
d
, which we also call
d
-cells, or cells when the dimension is not useful; “elements” refer to
n
-cells.

We assume the following properties (the first two explicitly re-state that
\mathcal{M}_{h}
partitions
\Omega
):

•

The submanifolds cover
\Omega
:
\bigcup_{d\in[0,n]}\bigcup_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}f=\Omega
.

•

The submanifolds are pairwise disjoint:

\forall f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{h},f\neq f^{\prime}\implies f\cap f^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

•

Boundaries of the submanifolds are submanifolds:

\forall d\in[1,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
\forall d^{\prime}\in[0,d-1]
,
\forall f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
f^{\prime}\cap\overline{f}\neq\emptyset\implies f^{\prime}\subset{\partial f}
.

The last property allows us to define the collection of cells on the boundary of
f
:

\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(f):=\{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\,:\,f^{\prime}\subset{\partial f}\}
.

Algebraic properties of the discrete de Rham complex designed in Section
3
do not require any other assumption on the mesh.
However, analytical properties (such as the consistency of the complex, see Section
3.3
) do depend on the regularity of the mesh, a concept that we introduce now.
In what follows, the notation
a\lesssim b
stands for
a\leq Cb
with a constant
C
that does not depend on
the parameter
h
, the dimension
d\leq n
, on the subcell
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
or on the forms considered on
f
.
The notation
a\approx b
stands for
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
.

Definition 1

(Equivalence of meshes)
.

We say that a (flat) polytopal mesh
M_{h}
(as defined in
[
26
, Definition 1.4]
)
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}_{h}
if:

•

Their same-dimensional cells can be put in bijection:

\forall d\in[0,n]
, there is a bijection
\Phi_{d}\,:\,\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\to\Delta_{d}(M_{h})
.

•

They are topologically equivalent:

\forall d\in[1,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
\Phi_{d-1}(\Delta_{d-1}(f))=\Delta_{d-1}(\Phi_{d}(f))
.

•

Their geometries are equivalent:

\forall d\in[1,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, there is a diffeomorphism

\phi_{f}\,:\,f\to\Phi_{d}(f)
satisfying:

\|\nabla\phi_{f}\|_{\infty}\approx\|\det(\nabla\phi_{f})\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{d}}\approx\|\det(\nabla\phi_{f}^{-1})\|_{\infty}^{-\frac{1}{d}}\approx\|\nabla\phi_{f}^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{-1}.

(2)

•

The geometry is regular on boundaries:

\forall d\in[2,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
\forall f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
,

\|\nabla\phi_{f}\|_{\infty}\approx\|\nabla\phi_{f^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}.

(3)

Above,
\|F\|_{\infty}
denotes the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the function
F
on its domain. Note that imposing, for example, that
\|\nabla\phi_{f}\|_{\infty}
is comparable to
\|\det(\nabla\phi_{f})\|_{\infty}^{\frac{1}{d}}
amounts to enforcing a level of “isotropy” to
f
, and (
2
) is therefore an assumption on the shape regularity of
f
.

Assumption 2

(Regular mesh sequence)
.

We assume that the mesh sequence
(\mathcal{M}_{h})_{h}
is regular in the sense that there exists an equivalent (in the sense of Definition
1
) polytopal mesh sequence
(M_{h})_{h}
which is regular in the sense of
[
26
, Definition 1.9]
.

For
d\geq 1
, we define the characteristic size of a
d
-cell

as

h_{f}:=|f|^{\frac{1}{d}}
.
Noticing that

h_{f}\approx\|\nabla\phi_{f}\|_{\infty}^{-1}h_{\Phi_{d}(f)},

(4)

we can translate the geometric bounds on regular polytopal mesh sequences into our setting.
We recall the main properties:

•

The size of neighbouring cells is comparable:

\forall d\in[2,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
\forall f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
,
h_{f}\approx h_{f^{\prime}}
.

•

The number of sub-cells in an cell boundary is bounded:

\forall d\in[1,n]
,
\forall f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
\mathrm{Card}(\Delta_{d-1}(f))\lesssim 1
.

2.2
Discrete polynomial spaces

In the rest of the paper, we use a range of concepts related to differential forms and exterior calculus. We recall in Appendix
B
some of these concepts, and we refer the interested reader to
[

To build a DDR complex on

This is not an easy concept to define as these spaces must satisfy a range of compatibility properties with the exterior derivative, the trace, etc.
In this section, we define the key axioms on these spaces, and infer further notions such as trimmed polynomial spaces.
The construction below mimics well-known properties of polynomial spaces,
exterior derivative and Koszul operator on flat spaces;
we however have to check carefully that the minimal properties we require
(the complex properties and Assumption

are indeed sufficient to deduce, in particular,
the decomposition of polynomial spaces which justifies the definition of trimmed spaces.

Assumption 3

(Homogeneous polynomials)
.

We assume the following properties, for all
0\leq d\leq n
and
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
:

(
3
.a)

There exists spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f))_{s\in\mathbb{Z},l\in\mathbb{Z}}
that form a graded decomposition
\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{l}(f)=\bigoplus_{s\leq r}\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)

such that, on each
\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)
,
\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d}
acts as an homothetie:

\forall s,l\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\exists\lambda_{s,l}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d})p=\lambda_{s,l}p
for all
p\in\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)
.
For
s<0
,
\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)=\{0\}
and we fix
\lambda_{s,l}=\lambda_{s+l,0}
, with
\lambda_{s+l,0}=0
if
s+l<0
.

(
3
.b)

The eigenvalues of
\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d}
identify the polynomial degree:

\forall d>0
,
\forall l\in[0,d]
,
\forall s,s^{\prime}\geq 0
,
\lambda_{s,l}=\lambda_{s^{\prime},l}\implies s=s^{\prime}
.
Moreover, if
s\geq 0
then
\lambda_{s,l}=0
if and only if
(s,l)=(0,0)
.

(

.c)

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)\neq\{0\}

.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
3
, for all
0\leq l\leq d
,
s\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, the eigenvalues are related by the relation
\lambda_{s,l}=\lambda_{s+l,0}
.
Moreover
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{s+1}\Lambda^{l}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l+1}(f)
and
\kappa\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l+1}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{s+1}\Lambda^{l}(f)
.

Proof.

The case
s<0
being obvious by the choice in Assumption
(.a)
, we only need to consider
s\geq 0
.
Let us first take
l=0
and let us prove by induction on
s\in\mathbb{N}
that

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{s+1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{1}(f)
and
\lambda_{s,1}=\lambda_{s+1,0}
.
Starting with
s=0
, Assumption
(.a)
gives
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}_{1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathcal{P}_{0}\Lambda^{1}(f)=\mathcal{H}_{0}\Lambda^{1}(f)
, which proves the first property for the base case. For the second property,
by Assumption
(.c)
there is
p\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\Lambda^{0}(f)
such that
\mathrm{d}p\neq 0
.
Moreover
\kappa\mathrm{d}p=\lambda_{1,0}p-\mathrm{d}\kappa p
.
Thus,

(\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d})\mathrm{d}p=\mathrm{d}\kappa\mathrm{d}p=\lambda_{1,0}\mathrm{d}p-\cancel{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}\kappa\mathrm{d}p},

and
\mathrm{d}p
is an eigenvector of
\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d}
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1,0}
.
We note that
\mathrm{d}p\in\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}\Lambda^{1}(f)
.
Therefore,
\lambda_{0,1}=\lambda_{1,0}
and the case
s=0
is proved.

Let now
s\geq 1
and assume that, for all
0\leq k<s
,
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{k+1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}\Lambda^{1}(f)
and
\lambda_{k,1}=\lambda_{k+1,0}
. We need to prove that
\lambda_{s,1}=\lambda_{s+1,0}
.
Let
p\in\mathcal{H}_{s+1}\Lambda^{0}(f)
. If
\mathrm{d}p=0
then clearly
\mathrm{d}p\in\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{1}(f)
. Otherwise, using the same arguments as for
s=0

we note that
(\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d})\mathrm{d}p=\lambda_{s+1,0}\mathrm{d}p
,
and
\mathrm{d}p\in\mathcal{P}_{s}\Lambda^{1}(f)=\bigoplus_{k\leq s}\mathcal{H}_{k}\Lambda^{1}(f)
.
Since
\mathrm{d}p
is an eigenvector of
\mathrm{d}\kappa+\kappa\mathrm{d}
(as it is not equal to

it belongs to an
\mathcal{H}_{k}\Lambda^{1}(f)
for some

\lambda_{k,1}=\lambda_{s+1,0}
.
By induction hypothesis, if

\lambda_{k,1}=\lambda_{k+1,0}\not=\lambda_{s+1,0}
(the non-equality coming from Assumption
(.b)
). Hence,

and
\lambda_{s,1}=\lambda_{s+1,0}
. In passing, we have also proved that
\mathrm{d}p\in\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{1}(f)
, so that
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{s+1}\Lambda^{0}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{1}(f)
, and the induction is therefore complete.

Another induction on

concludes the proof that
\lambda_{s,l}=\lambda_{s+l,0}
for all

The fact that

Lemma 5
.

Under Assumption
3
, for all

,

\displaystyle\kappa\,:\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)\to

\displaystyle\,\kappa\mathcal{H}_{s-1}\Lambda^{l+1}(f)\subset\mathcal{H}_{s}\Lambda^{l}(f)\,.

Proof.

We only need to prove that

The case

We therefore assume that

and take

(.a)
, we have

Lemma 6

(Decomposition of polynomial spaces)
.

Under Assumption

We first prove that

3
Discrete de Rham complex

3.1
Construction

The construction of the DDR complex is done following the approach of
[
11
]
in the flat case, with one major difference due to the fact that we are working on manifolds instead of Euclidean spaces: the Hodge star operator
\star
on
\Omega
(or on its submanifolds forming the mesh) depends on the metric of this manifold (or the induced metric on the submanifolds) – see (
53
) in Appendix
B
for its definition. As a consequence, it does not preserve polynomial spaces, since the coefficients of the metric, in the local coordinates chosen to define the notion of polynomial functions, are not necessarily constant. This leads us to consider a slightly different definition of the discrete space (
9
) and related interpolator (
10
), as well as a slightly different local discrete exterior derivative and potential (Definition
10
).

We recall that, on a given cell
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
and for any
k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
, the Hodge star operator
\star
is a mapping that associates to each
k
-differential form
\omega
on
f
a
(d-k)
-differential form
\star\omega
on
f
such that, for any
x\in f
and any
(d-k)
-alternating form
\mu
on the tangent space
T_{x}f
,

\langle(\star\omega)_{x},\mu\rangle_{g_{x}}\mathrm{vol}_{x}=\omega_{x}\wedge\mu,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{g_{x}}
is the metric-dependent inner product on
(d-k)
-alternating forms on
T_{x}f
, and
\mathrm{vol}_{x}
is the volume form on this tangent space. We also refer to (
53
) for an expression in coordinates on Lorentzian manifolds – the only difference with Riemannian manifolds being in the removal of the minus sign in front of
\det g
.

Given a polynomial degree
r\geq 0
, the discrete counterpart
\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k}
of the space
H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
,
0\leq k\leq n
, is defined as

\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k}:=\bigtimes_{d=k}^{n}\bigtimes_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})}\star^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{r}^{{-}}\Lambda^{d-k}(f).

(9)

A generic
\underline{{\omega}}_{h}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k}
is denoted by
\underline{{\omega}}_{h}=(\omega_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),\,d\in[k,n]}
with
\omega_{f}\in\star^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{r}^{{-}}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)

for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
. As commonly done in DDR constructions, we denote by
\underline{{\omega}}_{f}=(\omega_{f^{\prime}})_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d}^{\prime}(f),\,d^{\prime}\in[k,d]}
the restriction of
\underline{{\omega}}_{h}
to
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
. The same convention (replacing an index
h
with
f
) will also be used to denote restrictions of operators.

The interpolator
\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{k}:C^{0}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{\Omega})\to\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k}
is defined by projecting the traces on the polynomial spaces: for all
\omega\in C^{0}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
,

\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{k}\omega:=(\star^{-1}\pi^{{-},d-k}_{r,f}\star\tr_{f}\omega)_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),d\in[k,n]}

(10)

where
\pi^{{-},d-k}_{r,f}:L^{2}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)\to\mathcal{P}_{r}^{{-}}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)
is the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projector on the trimmed space.

Definition 10

(Local discrete exterior derivative and discrete potential)
.

Let
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
,
k\in[0,d]
and
r\geq 0
. The local discrete exterior derivative

\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\,:\,\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,f}^{k}\rightarrow\star^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
and discrete potential

P^{k}_{r,f}\,:\,\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,f}^{k}\rightarrow\star^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)
are defined inductively on the dimension of
f
as follows.
For all
\underline{{\omega}}_{f}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,f}^{k}
:

•

If
d=k
:

P^{k}_{r,f}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}:=\,\omega_{f}\in\star^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{0}(f).

(11)

•

If
d\geq k+1
:

\diamondsuit

\forall\mu_{f}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k-1}(f)
,

\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}\wedge\mu_{f}:=(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\omega_{f}\wedge\mathrm{d}\mu_{f}+\int_{\partial f}P^{k}_{r,{\partial f}}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu_{f},

(12)

where we have denoted by
P^{k}_{r,{\partial f}}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}
the piecewise function on
{\partial f}
obtained patching the functions
P^{k}_{r,f^{\prime}}\underline{{\omega}}_{f^{\prime}}
for
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
.

\diamondsuit

\forall\mu_{f}\in\kappa\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(f)
,
\forall\nu_{f}\in\kappa\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{d-k+1}(f)
,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}P^{k}_{r,f}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}\wedge(\mathrm{d}\mu_{f}+\nu_{f}):={}

\displaystyle\int_{f}\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\underline{{\omega}}_{f}\wedge\mu_{f}-\int_{\partial f}P^{k}_{r,{\partial f}}\underline{{\omega}}_{\partial f}\wedge\tr_{\partial f}\mu_{f}

(13)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{k+1}\int_{f}\omega_{f}\wedge v_{f}.

In these formulas, the orientation on the boundary
\partial f
is the one induced by
f
.

The global discrete exterior derivative is then obtained projecting the local discrete exterior derivatives on the spaces forming the component of the global discrete space (

\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{k}_{r,h}:\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k}\to\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{k+1}
is given by

\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{k}_{r,h}\underline{{\omega}}_{h}:=(\star^{-1}\pi^{{-},d-k-1}_{r,f}\star\mathrm{d}^{k}_{r,f}\underline{{\omega}}_{f})_{f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h}),d\in[k+1,n]}.

The discrete DDR sequence on
\mathcal{M}_{h}
is then

3.2
Algebraic properties

The following results are covered in the flat case in
[
11
]
. Their proofs there only rely on the relations between the Hodge star operator and integrals of differential forms (which are also satisfied in the case of manifolds), the properties of full and trimmed polynomial spaces (which are mimicked by our constructions and assumptions in Section
2.2
), and the formulas defining the local discrete exterior derivative and potential reconstruction (same formulas as in Definition
10

\star^{-1}
in (

[

directly adapt to our setting, and are therefore omitted.

3.3
Consistency

In the usual DDR construction, the initial consistency result for local discrete potentials and exterior derivatives is an exact polynomial consistency
[
11
, Theorem 15]
.
However, on manifolds, Hodge stars of polynomials are not necessarily polynomials; for this reason, it does not seem possible to obtain an exact
polynomial consistency of these local operators (see Remark
20
for more details).
Instead, we directly establish a consistency result in terms of approximation
properties of these operators when applied to interpolates of smooth differential forms – a result which is usually seen as a consequence of a polynomial consistency, but independently established here.

As the DDR construction relies on the decompositions in Definition
7
,
we must ensure that the direct sum does not degenerate as
h
goes to zero.
The setting in Section
4
gives a construction of local polynomial spaces that satisfy the following assumption.
Below,
\|\mu\|_{X}
is a shorthand for the
L^{2}
-norm of
\mu\in L^{2}\Lambda^{k}(X)
.

Assumption 17

(Local Poincaré inequality on Koszul complement, topological decomposition and discrete trace)
.

We assume the following bounds on the discrete spaces:
For all
k\geq 0
,
d\geq k
,
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
and
r\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{f}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{f}\|\mathrm{d}\mu\|_{f},

\displaystyle\quad\forall\mu\in\kappa\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{k}(f),

(17)

\displaystyle\|\alpha\|_{f}+\|\beta\|_{f}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\alpha+\beta\|_{f},

\displaystyle\quad\forall\alpha\in\kappa\mathcal{P}_{r-1}\Lambda^{k+1}(f),\quad\forall\beta\in\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}_{r+1}\Lambda^{k-1}(f),

(18)

\displaystyle\|\tr_{\partial f}\mu\|_{{\partial f}}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{f}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\mu\|_{f},

\displaystyle\quad\forall\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{k}(f).

(19)

We also assume that the discrete spaces have similar approximation properties as those of polynomials in Euclidean space (see
[

, Theorem 1.45]
).

Remark 20

(Polynomial consistency)
.

Polynomial consistency is the property:
P^{k}_{r,f}\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,f}^{k}\omega=\omega
for all
\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{r}\Lambda^{k}(f)
.
As shown in
[
11
, Section 3.5]
, the initial step for proving this property for the DDR complex on the Euclidean space consists in considering
d=k
and in writing
\star^{-1}\pi^{d-k}_{r,f}\star\omega=\star^{-1}\star\omega=\omega
; the removal of the
L^{2}
-projector
\pi^{d-k}_{r,f}
is justified since, when the metric is constant,
\star\omega
is a polynomial form of the same degree as
\omega
. However, in the context of DDR on a manifold,
\star\omega
may no longer be polynomial of the same degree as
\omega
since the
\star
operator involves the coefficients of the (non-constant) metric.
Hence, polynomial consistency for the DDR on a manifold does not seem readily accessible. This does not prevent the method, however, from having optimal primal consistency on smooth forms, as demonstrated by Theorem
19
.

Remark 21

(About the regularity requirement on
\omega
)
.

The space
H^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f;\Delta)
, with its assumed regularity of traces of functions on lower-dimensional cells, is the natural one to state the consistency estimates in Theorem
19
. Classical spaces of differential forms can easily be embedded into
H^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f;\Delta)
.

For example, letting
C^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{f})
be the space of
k
-forms that are continuous over
\overline{f}
along with all their derivatives up to order
r+1
, and considering the semi-norm

|\omega|_{C^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f)}=\max_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,|\alpha|=r+1}\|\partial^{\alpha}\omega\|_{L^{\infty}\Lambda^{k}(f)},

we have
C^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{f})\subset H^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f;\Delta)
and

|\omega|_{r+1,f,\Delta}\lesssim|f|^{1/2}|\omega|_{C^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f)}\quad\forall\omega\in C^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(\overline{f}).

Likewise, repeated uses of the continuous trace inequality

\|\tr_{{\partial f}^{\prime}}\mu\|_{{\partial f}^{\prime}}\lesssim h_{f^{\prime}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\mu\|_{f^{\prime}}+h_{f^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mu|_{{H^{1}\Lambda^{k}(f^{\prime})}},\quad\forall\mu\in H^{1}\Lambda^{k}(f^{\prime})\,,\quad\forall f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d^{\prime}}(\mathcal{M}_{h})\,,\quad\forall d^{\prime}\in[k,d]

show that
H^{r+1+d-k}\Lambda^{k}(f)\subset H^{r+1}\Lambda^{k}(f;\Delta)
with

|\omega|_{r+1,f,\Delta}\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{d-k}h_{f}^{i}|\omega|_{H^{r+1+i}\Lambda^{k}(f)}\quad\forall\omega\in H^{r+1+d-k}\Lambda^{k}(f).

4
Construction of local polynomial spaces on manifolds

The notion of polynomial depends on the choice of coordinates.
In general, a manifold cannot be covered with a single chart.
Therefore, if polynomial spaces are to be designed on a manifold, their definition needs to be coherent with (some) changes of coordinates.
We only need our polynomial spaces to be locally defined on
d
-cells,
but we also need to ensure that restrictions of polynomials to the boundary of
d
-cells are polynomials on
(d-1)
-cells
(of the same or lower degree), for all
d=1,\ldots,n
.

We prove in this section that, if Assumption
22
below holds, then suitable local polynomial spaces can be constructed.
This assumption essentially states that, when read in the coordinates chosen on a
d
-cell
f
, the subcells of
f
are linearly embedded in
f
.
Appendix
A
shows how to practically construct mappings satisfying this assumption in dimension
n=2
and for various cell shapes.
Notice that, if a cell is a polytope in all the charts of the chosen atlas on the manifold,
then Assumption
22
is trivially satisfied.
Therefore, we can automatically build a basis on cells that are flat shaped in all charts (even if the metric is not trivial).
Only cells that are not flat polytopes in one of the atlas’ charts require a special attention; these cells usually lie at the transition between two charts.

Assumption 22

(Affine compatible local coordinates)
.

For all
0\leq d\leq n
and
f\in\Delta_{d}(\mathcal{M}_{h})
, there is a
C^{2}
-diffeomorphism
I_{f}:U_{f}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{f}
. Moreover,
for all
f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{d-1}(f)
, setting
J_{f}:=(I_{f})^{-1}
and denoting by
\mathfrak{I}_{f,f^{\prime}}\,:\,f^{\prime}\to\overline{f}
the inclusion of
f^{\prime}
into
\overline{f}
,
the mapping
\mathcal{T}_{f,f^{\prime}}:=J_{f}\circ\mathfrak{I}_{f,f^{\prime}}\circ I_{f^{\prime}}:U_{f^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is affine.

This assumption essentially states that each cell has a suitable coordinate system (represented by the diffeomorphisms
I_{f}
) such that, for
each
(d-1)
-cell
f^{\prime}
of
f
, the coordinate system of
f^{\prime}
is affine in the coordinates of
f
. This key property ensures that, when defining
polynomial functions through these coordinate systems (as in Definition
23
), the restriction to
f^{\prime}
of polynomials on
f

are polynomial on
f^{\prime}
(see Lemmas
25
and
26
), which is one of the essential properties required on local polynomial spaces to build the DDR sequence (and potentially other polytopal schemes).

Appendix
A
gives a possible practical approach to construct local coordinates (that is, parametrisations) that satisfy Assumption
22
, by a hierarchical construction: starting from parametrisations of the
1
-cells (edges),
parametrisations of
2
-cells are constructed using the coordinates originating from two of its edges, in an explicit way and taking care of respecting the compatibility with the coordinates on the other edges. Note that this construction builds at the same time a flat polytopal mesh
that is equivalent to the manifold mesh in the sense of Definition
1

(provided the diffeomorphisms

2
)); hence, following the process in Appendix

and

Only the approximation properties of Assumption

In the following, we detail the construction of the polynomial spaces and the implications of Assumption

.
We denote by

Lemma 27

(Local Poincaré and trace inequalities under affine compatible local coordinates)
.

If Assumptions

and
2

The main idea is to use the general bound

For all

Application

We present here a 2+1 model for the Maxwell equations on a manifold written in the language of differential forms, and use the DDR complex to design a scheme for this model.
We refer the reader to the notations recalled in Appendix
B
and used throughout this section.

5.1
Electromagnetism in
2+1
dimensions

Following
[
33
,
32
]
,
we foliate a
3
-dimensional space-time manifold
M
by level sets of a time function
t
. Letting
1
1

1

Throughout this section, we use lower case Greek letters (
\mu,\nu,\gamma
etc.) to label space-time coordinate indices that run over
0,1,2
while lower case Latin letters (
i,j,k
etc.) will label spatial coordinate indices that run over
1,2
.

g=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}\otimes dx^{\nu}
denote a Lorentzian metric on
M
, we perform a
2+1
decomposition on

via

(g_{\mu\nu}):=\begin{pmatrix}-N^{2}+|\beta|_{\gamma}^{2}&\beta_{j}\\
\beta_{i}&\gamma_{ij}\end{pmatrix},

(39)

where
\gamma=\gamma_{ij}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j}
is the induced metric on the

spatial surfaces,

is the lapse,
\beta=\beta_{i}dx^{i}
is the shift, and we use
(x^{i})
to denote spatial coordinates on these surfaces.
We define a future pointing unit normal to the spatial surfaces by

(40)

and, in the following, we distinguish geometric objects associated with the spatial surface with a tilde; for example,

\widetilde{\mathrm{vol}}
and

{\widetilde{\sharp}}
are the volume form and
\sharp

\gamma
on the spatial surfaces. Further, we introduce the vector field

via

\partial_{t}=Nn+\beta^{{\widetilde{\sharp}}},

and note that for any adapted coordinate system
(t,x^{i})
(i.e. which satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{t}}x^{i}=0
), the vector field
\partial_{t}
will coincide with the coordinate vector field
\partial_{t}
associated to the coordinate system
(t,x^{i})
.

Letting
F=\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}\wedge dx^{\nu}
denote the electromagnetic field tensor, the Maxwell equations can be expressed in terms of

as
[
33
, Section 18.2]

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}F={}

\displaystyle 0,

(41a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\star F={}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}^{-1}\star\underline{{j}},

(41b)

where
\underline{{j}}
is the electric
3

is the permittivity of the medium, supposed constant here.
Following the usual convention in
3+1

we define the electric field
E
and the magnetic field

\displaystyle E:={}

\displaystyle-\mathsf{i}_{n}F,

(42)

\displaystyle B:={}

\displaystyle\mathsf{i}_{n}(\star F),

(43)

where we refer to (
30

\mathsf{i}_{n}
.
Notice that, in
2+1
dimensions, the magnetic field

is a scalar field.
Below, we will use
\widetilde{E}
to denote the restriction
of
E
to the spatial surfaces, that is,
\widetilde{E}(\tau)=\iota_{\tau}^{*}E
where
\iota_{\tau}
is the inclusion map of the spatial surface

. If
E
is expressed in terms of the adapted coordinate
(t,x^{i})
as

E=E_{0}dt+E_{i}dx^{i}
,
then
\widetilde{E}=E_{i}dx^{i}
and

We denote the spatial codifferential on
k

and assume that the shift vanishes, that is,
\beta=0
.
It is proved in Appendix
B
the Maxwell equations (

2+1

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{d}}(N\widetilde{E})={}

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}(B\widetilde{\mathrm{vol}}),

(44)

\displaystyle-\widetilde{\delta}\widetilde{E}={}

\displaystyle\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}(NB\widetilde{\mathrm{vol}})={}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}^{-1}\underline{{J}}+\widetilde{\star}^{-1}\partial_{t}(\widetilde{\star}\widetilde{E}),

where

is the electric charge density and

is the electric current density.

For the numerical tests, we consider a metric that is independent of time with constant lapse

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{E}={}

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}B^{\prime},

\displaystyle-\widetilde{\delta}\widetilde{E}={}

\displaystyle\mu_{0}\widetilde{J}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}\widetilde{E},

(45c)

We note that the following compatibility condition (from hereon assumed) on the source terms, following from the property

\widetilde{\delta}\,\widetilde{J}=-\partial_{t}\widetilde{\delta}\,\widetilde{E}=\partial_{t}\rho.

\widetilde{\delta}

(\widetilde{E},\widetilde{B})\in C^{1}([0,T];\Lambda^{1}(\Omega))\times C^{1}([0,T];\Lambda^{2}(\Omega))

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\mathrm{d}}\widetilde{E},v^{2}\rangle={}

\displaystyle-\langle\partial_{t}B^{\prime},v^{2}\rangle,

(47a)

\displaystyle\langle B^{\prime},\widetilde{\mathrm{d}}v^{1}\rangle={}

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{J},v^{1}\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}\widetilde{E},v^{1}\rangle.

(47b)

We note that the constraint (
45b

and setting

in (

5.2
Semi-discrete scheme

We describe here a numerical scheme based on the discrete de Rham complex for
the system (
45
). This scheme is of arbitrary order of accuracy, applicable to polygonal meshes on the chosen 2D manifold, and naturally preserves the constraint (
45b
) at the discrete level.

Recalling the definition of the discrete
L^{2}
-like inner product (Definition
11
), the (semi-discrete) scheme is built on the product space
X_{h}:=\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{1}\times\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{2}
and reads:
find
(\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h})\in C^{1}([0,T];X_{h})
such that, for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{1},\underline{{v}}_{h}^{2})\in X_{h}
and all

\displaystyle\langle\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{1}_{r,h}\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}(t),\underline{{v}}_{h}^{2}\rangle={}

\displaystyle-\langle\partial_{t}\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}(t),\underline{{v}}_{h}^{2}\rangle,

(48a)

\displaystyle\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}(t),\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{1}_{r,h}\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{1}\rangle={}

\displaystyle\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{1}\widetilde{J}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{1}\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{1}\rangle.

(48b)

We note that, in the context of the DDR complex, the discrete

are denoted without a tilde.
Owing to the properties of this complex, this scheme preserves a discrete version of the constraint.

Proposition 28

(Discrete constraint preservation)
.

Let
\rho_{h}\in C^{1}([0,T];\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{0})
be the discrete electric charge density defined by

\langle\rho_{h}(t),\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\rangle=-\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}(0),\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{0}_{r,h}\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{1}\widetilde{J}(s),\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}_{r,h}^{0}\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\rangle\,ds\qquad\forall t\in[0,T]\,,\forall\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{0}.

(49)

Under Assumptions
3
and

,
if
(\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h})
is a solution to (

), then

\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}(t),\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}^{0}_{r,h}\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\rangle=-\langle\rho_{h}(t),\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\rangle\qquad\forall t\in[0,T]\,,\forall\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{0}.

(50)

Remark 29

(On the discrete constraint preservation)
.

By the Riesz representation theorem, (
49

\rho_{h}(t)\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{0}
.
This definition only depends on the data of the model (initial value of the electric field and electric current density) and, given the relation (
46
), provides a consistent discrete version of the continuous electric charge density.

Letting
\underline{{\delta}}_{r,h}^{1}
be the adjoint of
\underline{{\mathrm{d}}}_{r,h}^{1}

\underline{{\delta}}_{r,h}^{1}\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}(t)=-\rho_{h}(t)
, which is a coherent discrete version of the continuous constraint (

\epsilon_{0}=1
here).

Proof of Proposition
28

Let
\underline{{v}}_{h}^{0}\in\underline{\boldsymbol{X}}_{r,h}^{0}
and set

Lemma 30

(Energy preservation)
.

In the absence of a current density, the solution
(\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h})
of (
48
) satisfies

\partial_{t}\left(\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}\rangle+\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}\rangle\right)=0.

(51)

Proof.

Evaluating (
48a
) and (
48b
)
with
\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}^{1}=\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}
and
\underline{v}_{h}^{2}=\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}
yields
\langle\partial_{t}\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}\rangle=0
, which implies the result.
∎

Remark 31

(Discrete energy preservation)
.

When using a time discretisation scheme preserving quadratic invariants, such as the Crank–Nicolson time stepping,
a discrete version of (
51
) can be established and shows that the total energy of the system
\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{E}}_{h}\rangle+\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}_{h}\rangle
remains constant in time.
Dissipative time-stepping, such as the implicit Euler method, lead to a decrease of the total energy.

Remark 32

(Well-posedness of the semi-discrete and fully discrete schemes)
.

The semi-discrete scheme (
48
) is a linear system of ordinary differential equations in the finite-dimensional space
X_{h}
. By the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem, for each pair of initial conditions it has a unique solution over
[0,T]
.

Regarding fully discrete versions of this scheme, when using implicit Euler or Crank–Nicolson, the scheme reduces at each time step to a square linear system of equations. The energy estimate satisfied by these schemes (see Remark
31
) provides a bound on any of their solutions; as a consequence, the system’s matrix has a trivial kernel and the system is uniquely solvable, which shows that the fully discrete schemes also have a unique solution for each chosen initial condition.

5.3
Building the mesh

Meshing manifolds while ensuring Assumption
22
is not trivial, and to our knowledge, there is no available software producing the required data.
We therefore developed our own in-house mesh generator for the two manifolds considered here, the flat torus and the sphere.
The meshing of the flat torus is a Cartesian grid on
[0,1)^{2}
using four charts to correctly match the boundary.
The meshing of the sphere is more interesting, and described now.

We restrict the two charts of the atlas to the north and south hemispheres; these charts do not overlap on an open set, as their transition occurs on a closed

\mathbb{R}^{2}

In order to correctly discretise to boundary circle, we chose to use one layer of curved cell, and then to map the inside of the disk using arbitrary flat cells.

We devised an automated mesh generation algorithm. Starting from a chosen radius

we cut the outer circle into

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(2tr)^{2}}}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right],

We present here the results of the numerical tests.
The scheme has been implemented in the Manicore C++ framework
(see
https://mlhanot.github.io/Manicore/
, version 1.0.1),
using linear algebra facilities from the Eigen3 library (see
https://eigen.tuxfamily.org

Although the framework has been reimplemented from scratch,
its design borrows from the HArDCore C++ framework implementing the DDR complexes on flat spaces

https://github.com/jdroniou/HArDCore
).
From classical results on flat spaces, we expect to see a convergence in space of order
r+1
for the
L^{2}
norm of
E
and
B
, when the exact solutions are smooth and only spatial error is accounted for.

We used a Crank-Nicolson time stepping.
In order to neglect the error originated from the time discretisation, we first used a time step of
\Delta t=10^{-5}
.
These results were compared with those obtained using the larger time step
\Delta t=10^{-3}
, and showed only a negligible difference (a relative difference of order

). Hence, at the considered time steps the spatial discretisation fully dominates, and to save computational resources we conducted all the tests below using the larger time step

The simulation are computed on the time span

\sqrt{\int_{t=0}^{2\pi}\langle\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{k}E-\underline{{E}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{I}}_{r,h}^{k}E-\underline{{E}}_{h}\rangle}.

6
Conclusion

We developed a discrete de Rham complex on manifolds, that is applicable on curved meshes. We designed a framework of full and trimmed polynomial spaces on such meshes, identifying the required assumptions that enabled us to adapt the setting of
[
11
]
to the case of manifolds. The discrete complex, based on the exterior calculus presentation of the de Rham complex, has the same cohomology as the continuous de Rham complex, and has an arbitrary order of accuracy.

We showed that if the mesh cells can be embedded into flat spaces in a way that ensures that the composition of internal and boundary mappings are linear, then the assumptions of our framework are satisfied. An explicit construction of these embeddings was provided for a range of curved 3- and 4-edge cells in 2D, which typically arise when mapping triangular/quadrangular cells through the charts of the manifold. The ideas behind this construction could in principle be used to cover other kind of cells (or 3D cells), but would require technical efforts and is the topic of interesting further research on mesh generation.

As an illustration of the usage of this discrete complex, we considered the case of the Maxwell equations written in differential forms on a sphere and on a torus. The complex property enabled us to design a scheme that preserves both the energy and the constraint associated with this model.
We constructed smooth and non-smooth solutions, and numerically assessed the efficiency of the scheme in terms of its preservation properties and accuracy.

Appendix A
Construction of suitable charts in dimension
n=2

In this appendix, we provide a few examples of constructions that satisfy Assumption
22
for various topologies.
They can be used where a curved boundary is required and combined with flat cells elsewhere.
Actually, when a cell is flat in a chart, we can simply restrict this chart to get a suitable
I_{f}
verifying this assumption.
Therefore, our method is really a generalization of the polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and we only need to create more exotic spaces on curved cells.
While there is no fully generic way to construct
I_{f}
, we give below some possible constructions in several cases.

The idea is to apply a hierarchical construction of the mappings
I_{f}
in Assumption
22
. In the 2D case we consider here, this means that we start from the diffeomorphisms
I_{f}:[0,1]\to\overline{f}
corresponding the 1-cells (edges)
f
, which are often easy to describe. Then, for each 2-cell
f
, we use the mappings
(I_{f^{\prime}})_{f^{\prime}\in\Delta_{1}(f)}
of its edges to construct a diffeomorphism
I_{f}:[0,1]^{2}\to\overline{f}
(or sometimes its inverse
J_{f}=(I_{f})^{-1}
, which is equivalent) that satisfies the compatibility condition of Assumption
22
, that is, the linearity of the composition of mappings. The initial approach to find the suitable
I_{f}
is to pick two edges of
f
connected by a vertex and try and foliate this 2-cell using the coordinates on these edges; of course, maintaining the compatibility with the other edges is where the challenge arises, and requires to adjust this first idea to the particular shape of the element by modifying the foliation as we move closer to the other edges. On principle, this approach can also be extended to other shapes and to higher dimensions, but will still require some level of case-by-case adjustment and, probably, extensive calculations in complicated geometries.

In practice, we work in coordinates, and therefore start from a given chart
J^{0}_{f}\,:\,\overline{f}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
When we mention the injection of an edge
E\in\Delta_{1}(f)
,
we are actually speaking about the composition

J^{0}_{f}\circ\mathfrak{I}_{f,E}\circ I_{E}
.
Likewise, we will not directly build maps from
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
but rather some diffeomorphism
J_{f}
between two subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The actual map that verifies Assumption
22
is
I_{f}:=(J_{f}\circ J^{0}_{f})^{-1}
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the Maxwell equations

In this section, we first recall some generic identities on tensor calculus and then show that, in the absence of shift, (

Let
g=(g_{\mu\nu})
denote the metric on the tangent bundle of a generic
n
-dimensional Lorentzian manifold,
and let
(g^{\mu\nu}):=(g_{\mu\nu})^{-1}
.
Assuming
\det g<0
, the volume form is given in coordinates by

(\mathrm{vol})_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}=\sqrt{-\det g}\ \epsilon_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}

(52)

where
\epsilon_{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}
denotes the Levi–Civita symbol.
Given a
1
-form
\alpha:=\alpha_{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}
and a vector field
b:=b^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
,
we define the sharp
\sharp
and flat
\flat
operator to raise and lower indices by:

(\alpha^{\sharp})^{\nu}:=g^{\nu\mu}\alpha_{\mu},\qquad(b^{\flat})_{\nu}:=g_{\nu\mu}b^{\mu}.

We use the Hodge star operator
\star
to identify
k
-forms with
(n-k)
-forms.
In index notation, it is defined pointwise on the linear basis of
k
-forms by

\star(\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{k}}):=\frac{\sqrt{-\det g}}{(n-k)!}\epsilon^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{k}}{}_{\nu_{k+1}\dots\nu_{n}}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu_{k+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\nu_{n}}

(53)

where

\epsilon^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{k}}{}_{\nu_{k+1}\dots\nu_{n}}:=g^{\mu_{1}\nu_{1}}\dots g^{\mu_{k}\nu_{k}}\epsilon_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}.

In general, the product of two
\epsilon
symbols is given by

\epsilon^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}\epsilon_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}=\det(g^{-1})\delta^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}},

(54)

where
\delta^{\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}}_{\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}}
is the generalized Kronecker delta
(equal to
1
if
\mu_{1}\dots\mu_{n}
is an even permutation of
\nu_{1}\dots\nu_{n}

-1
if it is an odd permutation and

otherwise).
The generalized Kronecker delta can also be used to compute wedge products in index notation.
The wedge product of two

-forms
\alpha=\alpha_{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}
and

is given by

\alpha\wedge\beta=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}\alpha_{\mu^{\prime}}\beta_{\nu^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}.

(55)

In the rest of this appendix, we specialise to the case of

dimensions and adopt the notations (
39

(g_{\mu\nu}):=\begin{pmatrix}-N^{2}+|\beta|_{\gamma}^{2}&\beta_{j}\\
\beta_{i}&\gamma_{ij}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad n:=(-N\mathrm{d}t)^{\sharp}.

The inverse matrix of
g
is given by

g^{-1}=(g^{\mu\nu})=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}&\frac{\beta_{j}}{N^{2}}\\
\frac{\beta_{i}}{N^{2}}&\gamma^{ij}-\frac{\beta_{i}\beta_{j}}{N^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

The determinant of

-form
