Quantum HodgeRank: Topology-Based Rank Aggregation on Quantum Computers
Caesnan M. G. Leditto, Angus Southwell, Behnam Tonekaboni, Muhammad Usman, Kavan Modi

Abstract
Rank aggregation techniques play a crucial role in identifying the best alternatives, but the inherent imbalance and incompleteness of real-world data pose significant challenges. The HodgeRank algorithm, which utilizes discrete exterior calculus on a graph, can overcome these issues and output a consistent global ranking of alternatives. Recently, higher-order networks have been employed to analyze complex multipartite interactions in network data. However, extending HodgeRank to ranking on higher-order networks requires computational costs that are exponential in the network dimension. To address this challenge, we develop a quantum algorithm that outputs a quantum state proportional to the HodgeRank solution. By incorporating quantum singular value transformation and tools from quantum topological data analysis, our approach achieves time complexity independent of the network dimension, avoiding the need for quantum random access memory or sparse access input models to create the boundary matrix in the higher dimensional discrete exterior calculus. We also present efficient algorithms and heuristics to extract meaningful information from the output quantum state, including an algorithm to compute the consistency of the ranking. Using our algorithm, the consistency measure estimation can potentially achieve superpolynomial quantum speedups for higher-order network data with specific structures. Beyond ranking tasks, our methods suggest that quantum computing could find fruitful applications in studying higher-order networks and discrete exterior calculus.

HodgeRank.
— We briefly describe HodgeRank and the associated mathematical problems (see
Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
for details). HodgeRank provides a global ranking of alternatives
\mathcal{V}:=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. The input to HodgeRank is the aggregated pairwise preferences of voters, modelled by an edge flow

on a comparison graph

\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}\in\mathbb{R^{|\mathcal{E}|}}

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{G}
need not be complete, as not all pairs of alternatives need to be compared directly. The value

represents the preference for alternative
i
over
j

denotes that the edge is oriented.

Given a comparison graph
\mathcal{G}
and preference data

, the pairwise ranking problem can then be formulated as a weighted
\ell_{2}
minimization problem with solution

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}_{\mathrm{G}}=\argmin_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathrm{im}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\dagger})}\left[\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}w_{ij}\left(x_{ij}-\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}([v_{i},v_{j}])\right)^{2}\right],

(1)

where
w_{ij}
represents the weight associated to the data on (oriented) edge
[v_{i},v_{j}]
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is a matrix representation of the
k
-th boundary operator of a clique complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathcal{G})
(see Appendix
I
). The space
\mathrm{im}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\dagger})
contains so-called

such that

is an analogue of the gradient of the
potential function

\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{0}_{*}
.

HodgeRank then computes the potential (or score) function

from Eq. (
1

\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{0}_{\ast}=\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}_{\mathrm{G}}

defines a global ranking of the alternatives: the score HodgeRank gives to alternative

is

, and

\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{0}(v_{i})\geq\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{0}(v_{j})
. In this Letter, we set

is given by

correspond to the best alternatives. The length of the vector
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}_{\mathrm{G}}
, relative to the length of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{1}
, is a measure of the rankability of the alternatives according to the data

.

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}.

(2)

Quantum least squares algorithms.
—The HodgeRank problem in Eq. (
1
) is a specific type of weighted least squares problem. For
k=1
, this problem can be solved via an exact classical computation with complexity
O(n^{3})

Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
.
Approximate algorithms, such as various graph Laplacian solvers

;
Spielmanm2014NearlySystems
;
Cohen2014Solving
or topological signal processing (TSP) methods

;
Yang2022SimplicialFilters
, have complexities that scale roughly quadratically in
n
, with varying dependence on the approximation parameters. In higher dimensions, computing
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{G}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}
via least squares using classical sparse linear equation solvers has worst-case scaling
\Omega(n_{k})

;
Ding2022HardnessGadgets
, where
n_{k}
is the number of
k
simplices. This follows from the fact that
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is an
n_{k}\times n_{k-1}
matrix with
(k+1)n_{k}
non-zero entries. Generalizing the TSP approach to avoid matrix multiplication by instead performing repeated matrix-vector multiplication similarly leads to (approximate) algorithms with complexity
\Omega(n_{k})
. This is problematic because the number of
k
-simplices can be as large as

, which is asymptotically

-HodgeRank problem.

Quantum computers can potentially speed up certain high-dimensional linear algebra computations. In recent years, there have been several quantum algorithms proposed for tackling least square problems

;

;

However, this algorithm and the other previously mentioned quantum least squares algorithms are of limited use for solving

constructed via quantum random access memory (QRAM) or a sparse access input model (SAIM) suffer from poor worst-case scaling due to

In the next section, we develop a quantum algorithm to solve
k
-HodgeRank on a quantum computer. Given a quantum state that encodes simplicial signal values and a (classically stored) list of edges in the clique complex, our algorithm outputs a quantum state that approximates a state proportional to Eq. (

Approximating consistency measures.
—We provide algorithms to measure the rankability of data in terms of so-called consistency and local inconsistency measures
\mathrm{R}(k)\in[0,1]
and
\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k)\in[0,1]
, generalising definitions from
Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
.
The consistency measure associated to the data
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
is the (Euclidean) length of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{G}}
relative to
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
. This can be viewed as a measure of “goodness of fit” in a least squares sense.
Similarly, the local inconsistency measure is the relative length of the projection of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
onto
\mathrm{im}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1})
, which we denote
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{C}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(k):=\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\mbox{ and }\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k):=\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{C}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.

(9)

The value
\mathrm{R}(k)
measures whether the (sets of) alternatives are globally ranked in a meaningful way based on the data, where larger values indicate high rankability. Conversely,
\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k)
measures the presence of non-transitive preference relationships in the data, that is, cases where the consensus prefers
A

B

B
over
C
, and yet

I

\mathrm{R}(k)=\left(\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right)^{1/2}
, where
\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}:=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger})^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is the projector onto
\mathrm{im}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger})
. Thus, we can approximate
\mathrm{R}(k)
using a Hadamard test, which can be accelerated further with amplitude estimation
lin2022lecture
, provided we can make an approximate PUE of the projector
\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}
. We do this using QTSP by setting
H(x,y)=p(x)=x^{2}g_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}(x^{2})
, where as before
g_{\varepsilon}
is a polynomial which
\varepsilon/(2\kappa_{k}^{2})
-approximates
1/(2\kappa_{k}^{2}x)
. It follows that
\big\|\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}-2\kappa_{k}^{2}\,p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)\big\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}/2
, where
p(x)
is degree
D=O\big(\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(n\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})\big)
(see Theorem 2 in
Leditto2023topological
).
Thus, if
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}}=\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},x^{2}g_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}(x^{2}))
is the unitary that block encodes
p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)
in the subspace defined by the ancilla in state
\ket{0}^{\otimes a}
, then

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{k}^{2}\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\bra{0}^{\otimes a}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{G}}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\ket{0}^{\otimes a}=\left(\mathrm{R}(k)\right)^{2}\pm\varepsilon^{2}/2.

(10)

The value of
\mathrm{R}(k)
can be
\varepsilon
-approximated with success probability
1-\delta
using
O(\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2})
controlled applications of
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
and
\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},x^{2}g_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}(x)\big)
.
Thus, if
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
has non-Clifford gate depth
G
,
the overall non-Clifford gate depth of this procedure is

O\left(\kappa_{k}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\left(G+n\kappa_{k}^{2}\log n\log\left(\frac{n\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right)\right)\approx\tilde{O}\left(\kappa_{k}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\left(G+n\kappa_{k}^{2}\right)\right)
,
where
\tilde{O}
hides logarithmic factors of
n
,
\varepsilon
, and
\kappa_{k}
.
In the
k=1
case, this algorithm achieves a polynomial speed-up over the exact classical algorithm, which runs in time
O(n^{3})

Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
, provided
G
is small. The total runtime of the analogous approximate computation via classical topological signal processing (TSP)
Barbarossa2020TopologicalComplexes
;
Schaub2021SignalBeyond
is
O\big(nkn_{k}\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(n\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})\big)

Yang2021FiniteComplexes
;
Yang2022SimplicialFilters
. As discussed earlier, the linear dependence on
n_{k}
leads to exponential (in
k
) worst-case scaling.

This application can serve as a quantum-accelerated litmus test to check whether performing
k
-HodgeRank classically is worthwhile without the large classical computing time required to determine
\mathrm{R}(k)
exactly. An approximation of
\mathrm{R}(k)
up to a small constant, e.g.
\varepsilon\approx 0.1
, would be indicative of whether the underlying data
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
is amenable to a global ranking via
k
-HodgeRank.

Computing the local inconsistency measure is similar. In this task we estimate
\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k)=\left(\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\Pi_{\mathrm{C}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right)^{1/2}
, where
\Pi_{\mathrm{C}}
is the projector onto
\mathrm{im}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1})
. Thus, instead of applying
p(x)
as defined above, apply a filter
H(x,y)=q(y)=y^{2}g_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}(y^{2})
such that
2\kappa_{k+1}^{2}\,q\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}/\sqrt{n}\big)
is
(\varepsilon^{2}/2)
-close to the projector
\Pi_{\mathrm{C}}
in spectral norm. Analogously to approximating the consistency score, one can use an accelerated Hadamard test that computes a
\varepsilon
-approximation to
\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k)
with a probability of success
1-\delta
using
\tilde{O}(\kappa_{k+1}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))
applications of
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
and
\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},y^{2}g_{\varepsilon^{2}/2}(y^{2})\big)
. The exact classical computation for this quantity, as given by Jiang et al.
Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
, is
O(n^{9})
in the
k=1
case. Implementing this same matrix polynomial via classical TSP methods has complexity
O\big(nkn_{k+1}\kappa_{k+1}^{2}\log(n\kappa_{k+1}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})\big)
for general
k
, and has the same exponential scaling pitfalls as computing
\mathrm{R}(k)
classically.

Relative rankings of few alternatives.
—We combine quantum
k
-HodgeRank and amplitude estimation to approximate the scores of individual alternatives. This gives a heuristic way of relatively ranking a small set of alternatives without full-state tomography or performing the full classical computation.
We give the basic description here, for more details on this application see Appendix
V
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}
be a unitary with action
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}\ket{0}=\ket{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}}
, which is simply a tensor product of
X
gates on the appropriate qubits. By calling controlled applications of quantum
k
-HodgeRank and state preparation unitaries
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}
, we can implement the Hadamard test to approximate the score
s^{k-1}_{*,i}
of each
(k-1)
-simplex
\sigma_{k-1}^{i}
.
Approximating the scores for a set of
L
alternatives up to an additive error
\varepsilon
requires
O(n)
qubits and
O\big(L\kappa_{k}^{2}\,\log(L/\delta)/(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon)\big)
calls to each of
\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon/2}(x)\big)
,
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}
. Excluding the complexity of the state preparation unitary
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
, the total
non-Clifford gate depth is
\tilde{O}(L\kappa_{k}^{4}\sqrt{n}\log(L/\delta)/\varepsilon)
.
One can relatively rank
L
alternatives
\sigma_{k-1}^{i_{1}},\dots,\sigma_{k-1}^{i_{L}}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathcal{G})
by approximating each of their scores and ranking them according to these approximations.

For small
L
(e.g.
L=o(\sqrt{n})
), this procedure is, in principle, more efficient than computing HodgeRank classically. However, this procedure is heuristic because an appropriate value for
\varepsilon
is not obvious a priori, and there may be data where
\varepsilon=O(n^{-ck})
is needed to extract useful information. For example, as
k
grows, the number of
k
-simplices may grow exponentially. Thus, the average amplitude of a basis state in
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}
might vanish exponentially in
k
(since the quantum state has unit norm). As a result, the accuracy and precision requirements of both
xg_{\varepsilon/2}(x^{2})
and the Hadamard test may need to scale exponentially with
k
to extract meaningful information from the approximate amplitudes. This would limit the general advantage gained over classical methods to polynomial scaling at best. However, there may be specific choices of clique complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathcal{G})
and
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
data where the quantum advantage is greater.

Finding good alternatives.
—
We now consider the problem of determining an approximate score for every
(k-1)
-simplex. As mentioned previously, full-state tomography can be very expensive. However, one can use tomography techniques given in
vanApeldoorn2022QuantumUnitaries
to obtain a classically stored vector
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}
which is an
\ell_{\infty}
norm approximation to
\ket{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}}
. As detailed in Appendix
VI
, one can determine the score of every
(k-1)
-simplex, up to additive error
O(\varepsilon)
, in
O(\kappa_{k}^{4}n\varepsilon^{-2})
applications of
\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x))
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
.

One can use this to find an alternative that has a score within additive error
O(\varepsilon)
of the best alternative, which for an appropriate choice of
\varepsilon
will correspond to a high-ranking alternative. This
\ell_{\infty}
norm approximation can also be used to determine whether the alternatives can be classified into subsets with similar scores within subsets and large differences in scores between subsets. However, this method for returning good alternatives is at best heuristic, as an appropriate choice of
\varepsilon
cannot be easily determined from
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
, and indeed
\varepsilon
may need to be arbitrarily small to extract meaningful information (e.g. returning an alternative whose score is “close” to the best score), as the amplitudes of the state
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}
may be arbitrarily close.

Conclusions.
—
Quantum HodgeRank and the subsequent use cases presented here provide an alternative avenue for exploring quantum advantage using tools created for quantum topological data analysis (QTDA). This provides a promising path towards solving specific classes of least squares problems, with applications to ranking and discrete exterior calculus, without the issues arising from using QRAM/SAIM to encode the matrix. Standard techniques for dequantizing quantum linear algebra algorithms
Chia2020DequantizedQSVT
;
Tang2021DequantizedQPCA
;
Gharibian2022DequantizingConjecture
more broadly, have not historically worked for QTDA due to the potentially exponential scaling of the Frobenius norm of the boundary operator. Dequantized QTDA algorithms
Apers2023simpleclassical
rely on the relationship to the problem of projecting to the null space of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}+\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}
(known as the Hodge/combinatorial Laplacian
Goldberg2002Complexes
) and estimating its rank. HodgeRank and the applications considered here do not a priori share this connection to finding the rank of the null space, and so the methods used to dequantize QTDA do not obviously apply. An interesting future direction would be determining to what extent QTSP and quantum
k
-HodgeRank can be dequantized.

As HodgeRank offers an alternative solution to find the stationary distribution of the random walk on the web page graph
Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
, the quantum HodgeRank algorithm could be considered as a variant of quantum PageRank algorithms
Garneron2012Adiabatic
;
Sanchez-Burillo2012
. We also note that while recommendation systems are related to rank aggregation problems, the problem formulation and methods used in HodgeRank differ significantly from those studied by Kerenidis and Prakash
Kerenidis2017QuantumSystem
. This distinction extends to the underlying quantum algorithms as well.

We note that in this Letter we often compare approximate quantum algorithms to exact classical methods or deterministic methods described using stock-standard matrix multiplication, inversion, and diagonalization subroutines. Comparing to a broader range of classical algorithms would be a natural next step. While other approximate methods for computing
\bm{\mathrm{s}}_{*}^{k-1}
as given in Eq. (
2
) do exist for
k=1
and some limited cases for
k=2

Cohen2014Solving
;
Black2022HodgeComplexes
;
Ding2022HardnessGadgets
, these methods do not naturally extend to general Laplacian linear equation solvers for
k\geq 2
. In general, Laplacian equations with
k=2
are as difficult to solve as general linear equations
Ding2022HardnessGadgets
, where sparse approximate solvers for linear equations with
N
nonzero coefficients and condition number
\kappa
run in time
O(\min\left\{N^{2.27159},N\kappa\right\})

Ding2022HardnessGadgets
. In the case of Laplacian equations for larger
k
,
N=n_{k}
and thus can grow exponentially in
k
, as each
k
-simplex may be adjacent to
\Omega(nk)
other
k
-simplices. This suggests, at least intuitively, that Laplacian equations for general
k
are expected to be hard to solve, even approximately.

We highlight the method used in
Ding2022HardnessGadgets
that maps general linear equations to linear equations in a boundary matrix of a simplicial complex of dimension
k=2
. This mapping allows any least square problem to be realized as a least square problem where the matrix is a boundary matrix
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{2}
of a simplicial complex. As such, one could theoretically use quantum
2
-HodgeRank to solve general linear equations. In practice, the complexity of performing the mapping itself scales linearly in the number of variables, so this would not provide more than a polynomial advantage at best. However, with this in mind, it is possible that quantum
k
-HodgeRank could be used to solve more general least square problems. In particular, if the mapping used in
Ding2022HardnessGadgets
to map general linear equations to linear equations of boundary matrices of arbitrary dimension in a more vertex-compact form, this could provide an alternative avenue for new quantum linear system solvers. A simpler intermediate step to broadening the use cases of our methods would be to characterize the boundary matrices of simplicial (or clique) complexes.
For example, if
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
is a boundary matrix of a simplicial complex, all entries of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
must be in
\{-1,0,+1\}
, and each column must have exactly
k+1
nonzero entries.
A complete characterization of these matrices would allow us to determine the class of least squares problems for which quantum
k
-HodgeRank could offer a speed-up compared to classical algorithms or QRAM/SAIM-based quantum methods.

In the framework of discrete exterior calculus
hiranithesis2003discrete
;
desbrun2005discrete
, quantum
k
-HodgeRank, and more broadly QTSP, could potentially have applications in studying the Kuramoto model of coupled oscillators
Acebron2005KuramotoReview
and its higher-order analogues
Millan2020ExplosiveComplexes
;
Carletti2023GlobalTopologicalSync
. Moreover, discretized and triangulated domains are types of higher-order networks. Hence, the algorithm could be applied to such domains to solve variants of boundary value problems in fluid dynamics
COTTER2014FEECFluids
;
Hirani2015FEECFluids
;
MOHAMED2016175FEECFluids
and plasma physics
Squire2012FEECVlasovMaxwell
;
Kraus_Kormann_Morrison_Sonnendrücker_2017GEMPIC
.

Since our algorithm relies on fast methods to prepare the initial state
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
, applications with quantum data would be desirable. One possible avenue is measuring quantum contextuality
Abramsky_2011SheafContextuality
;
Abramsky2017ContextualFraction
, as sheaves can be defined over simplicial complexes. We also anticipate that the quantum
k
-HodgeRank, and more broadly QTSP, could potentially have applications in studying multipartite interactions in complex quantum networks
Nokkala2024ComplexQNetworks
.

On the more mathematical side,
Jiang et al.
Jiang2011StatisticalTheory
note that the mathematical tools apply for higher-order networks modelled by simplicial complexes as well, as the definition and relevant properties of the boundary operators
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
hold for arbitrary
k
. Nevertheless, they do not give an operational meaning to the higher-dimensional analogues.
Finding an operational interpretation for
k
-HodgeRank could have wide-reaching applications to higher-order network modelling. It would provide more concrete applications where these quantum algorithms perform best.

I
Review on Simplicial Complexes

Here we give a brief overview of the necessary topology required to understand topological signal processing and
k
-HodgeRank; for a more detailed review of simplicial complexes and related algebraic topology see
[
58
]
.
A simplicial complex with node set
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{K}_{n}
(sometimes dropping the
n
subscript), is a set of subsets of the node/vertex set
\mathcal{V}
(these subsets are called simplices) such that (i)
\{v_{i}\}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and (ii) if
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
and
\tau\subset\sigma
, then
\tau\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
(i.e., every subset of a simplex is a simplex). A particular family of simplicial complexes that are relevant to this work is the family of clique complexes. A clique complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathcal{G})
, for some graph
\mathcal{G}
, is a simplicial complex with the same node set as
\mathcal{G}
where the set of
k
-simplices is the set of
(k-1)
-cliques in
\mathcal{G}
. The structure of
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\mathcal{G})
is thus entirely determined by the graph
\mathcal{G}
.

We give some basic terminology here that we use when discussing simplicial complexes. A simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
is called a
k
-simplex if
|\sigma|=k+1
(the
+1
follows from the idea that a
k
-dimensional simplex in the geometric sense has
k+1
vertices, e.g. a 2-dimensional simplex is a triangle which has 3 vertices). A simplex
\tau
is a
q
-face of a
k
-simplex
\sigma
if
\tau\subset\sigma
and
|\tau|=q+1
, for
q<k
. To construct the boundary operators, we also associate an orientation to each simplex, which corresponds to the parity of a permutation of its constituent elements. That is, each simplex has two orientations, which are called positive or negative. For a simplex
\sigma=\{v_{i_{0}},\dots,v_{i_{k}}\}
, we denote the oriented simplex by
\left[v_{i_{0}},\dots,v_{i_{k}}\right]
.

Simplicial complexes are a well-studied class of higher-order networks because of their extra topological structure. One particularly relevant construction from algebraic topology associated to simplicial complexes that mathematically underpins our work is the space of
k
-chains
\mathfrak{C}_{k}:=\mathfrak{C}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}
, defined as formal sums of
k
-simplices in the complex.
It is sufficient for our purposes to consider the space of
k
-chains as (isomorphic to) a real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
where the basis is the set of
k
-simplices in
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{C}_{k}:=\big\{\sum_{i=0}^{n_{k}-1}c_{i}\sigma_{k}^{i}\mid c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}
, where
n_{k}
is the number of
k
-simplices in
\mathcal{K}
. The isomorphism to
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
can be seen by mapping each simplex
\sigma_{k}^{i}
to
\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{i}
, where
\{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{i}\}_{i\in[n_{k}]}
is the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
.

The different spaces
\mathfrak{C}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
associated with different dimensions
k
are related to each other by maps known as boundary operators.
The
k
-th boundary operator
\partial_{k}:\mathfrak{C}_{k}\to\mathfrak{C}_{k-1}
is a linear map such that for
\sigma_{k}=[v_{i_{0}},\cdots,v_{i_{k}}]\in\mathfrak{C}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\,(-1)^{j}\,[v_{i_{0}},\cdots,v_{i_{j-1}},v_{i_{j+1}}\cdots,v_{i_{k}}].

(11)

Here, the vertex
v_{i_{j}}
is removed from each simplex in the sum. That is,
\partial_{k}
maps a
k
-simplex to a formal sum of its
(k-1)
-faces, which are the boundary of the
k
-simplex in a topological sense.

Since the boundary operator acts linearly on the space of
k
-chains, it can be represented by a matrix.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}}
be the matrix representation of
\partial_{k}
, using the canonical bases of
k
-simplices and
(k-1)
-simplices for
\mathfrak{C}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathfrak{C}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K})
respectively.
The entries of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
are

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\right)_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\mbox{if $i\neq j$, $\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\subset\sigma_{k}^{j}$, $\epsilon(\sigma_{k-1}^{i})\sim\epsilon(\sigma_{k}^{j})$}\\
-1,\quad&\mbox{if $i\neq j$, $\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\subset\sigma_{k}^{j}$, $\epsilon(\sigma_{k-1}^{i})\nsim\epsilon(\sigma_{k}^{j})$}\\
0,\quad&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(12)

where
\epsilon(\sigma_{k-1}^{i})\sim\epsilon(\sigma_{k}^{j})
means
\sigma_{k-1}^{i}
and
\sigma_{k}^{j}
have the same orientation and
\epsilon(\sigma_{k-1}^{i})\nsim\epsilon(\sigma_{k}^{j})
means
\sigma_{k-1}^{i}
and
\sigma_{k}^{j}
have different orientations (again referring to
[
58
]
for the full definition). The
\pm 1
entries account for the orientation of each
k
-simplex relative to its boundary
(k-1)
-faces. The adjoint (equivalently transpose since
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is real) of the boundary operator
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}
is called the coboundary operator and naturally maps from
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}}
(
\mathfrak{C}_{k-1}(\mathcal{K})
) to
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
(
\mathfrak{C}_{k}(\mathcal{K})
).

The boundary and coboundary operators are used to construct the Hodge Laplacian (matrix)
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}
, defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}:=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger},\quad&\mbox{for $k=0$}\\
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}+\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger},\quad&\mbox{for $1<k<n-2$}\\
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k},\quad&\mbox{for $k=n-1$.}\end{cases}

(13)

Furthermore,
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{\ell}:=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u}:=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}
are defined as lower and upper Hodge Laplacian operators, respectively. More details of these operators can be found in Ref.
[
58
]
. Hence, Eq. (
2
) can be expressed as
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
.

The boundary and Laplacian operators define what is known as the Hodge decomposition. This states that
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, the vector space of real numbers assigned to
k
-simplices (i.e. the space of
k
-chains), can be partitioned into three orthogonal components

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}=\mathrm{im}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\oplus\mathrm{im}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\right)\oplus\mathrm{ker}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\right).

(14)

These are respectively known as the gradient, curl, and harmonic components, generalizing the language used in low-dimensional vector calculus. For an arbitrary
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, we write
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}=\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{G}}+\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{C}}+\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{H}}
where
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{G}}
,
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{C}}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{H}}
belong to the respective subspaces. The dimension of
\mathrm{ker}(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k})
is known as the
k
-th Betti number of the simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, and is well studied in classical and quantum topological data analysis.

II
Topological signal processing (TSP) and details of quantum topological signal processing (QTSP)

We utilize a newly developed framework from signal processing called topological signal processing (TSP)
[
21
]
to design our algorithms. TSP extends the concept of signals and signal processing from discretized time domains to more topologically nuanced spaces. In the TSP framework, the signal is referred to as a simplicial signal
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
and is defined as a
k
-chain (formally, a
k
-cochain, but for our purposes these are isomorphic) on a simplicial complex
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, effectively assigning a real number to each
k
-simplex. To perform filtering operations on these signals that respect the topology of the underlying space, TSP employs a filter operator described by polynomials of lower and upper Hodge Laplacian operators
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{\ell}=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u}=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}

[
50
,
10
]
, respectively, called a simplicial filter:

\displaystyle H\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{\ell},\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u}\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{{\sum}}_{i_{\ell}=1}^{d_{\ell}}h_{i_{\ell}}^{\ell}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{\ell}\right)^{i_{\ell}}+{{\sum}}_{i_{u}=1}^{d_{u}}h_{i_{u}}^{u}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u}\right)^{i_{u}}-h_{0}\bm{\mathrm{I}}.

The aim of TSP is to design such a filter
H(x,y)
so that the filtered simplicial signal
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{fil}}:=H(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{\ell},\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u})\,\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
extracts particular information from the original signal
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
. The form of the filter is determined by its polynomial coefficients, called the simplicial filter coefficients, and depends on the task at hand. The problem of finding the coefficients is known as the filter design problem. Yang et al.
[
10
]
designed simplicial filters to project a simplicial signal
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
onto signals
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k+1}
, which are similar to gradient and curl projections that we discuss in Application 1. However, the polynomials they use to approximate the projections are quite different to what we employ in this paper, as they used least squares methods to find the filter coefficients. Nevertheless, this particular application helps illustrate the connections between the Hodge decomposition and
k
-HodgeRank.

Recently a quantum algorithm, called quantum topological signal processing (QTSP), was developed for performing TSP filtering on quantum computers. Here we briefly review the general QTSP filtering algorithm, and refer the interested reader to
[
42
]
for more details. For our purposes in this paper, we slightly generalize the definition of a simplicial filter given in their paper to accommodate the “filter” we implement in quantum
k
-HodgeRank. As in Lemma 1 of
[
42
]
, define
H(x,y):[-1,1]^{2}\to[-1,1]
as a multivariable polynomial that is the sum of

and

, each with degree at most

such that both polynomials are either both even, both odd, or one is zero. The definite parity of

is a map from
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}

for some

(that is, the output simplicial signals all correspond to simplices of the same dimension).

Instead of encoding each entry of the boundary matrices via QRAM/SAIM to implement block-encoding of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}
, a projected unitary encoding of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}
is used. This is done using what is known as the fermionic Dirac boundary operator representation

. Using its efficient application via a Clifford loader circuit as described in
[

, the PUE of

\displaystyle\Pi_{k-1}\bm{\mathrm{D}}\Pi_{k}=\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}},

(15)

where

is a

-simplex identifying function, given in

46

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}\lvert\sigma_{k}^{i}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle=\begin{cases}\lvert\sigma_{k}^{i}\rangle\lvert 1\rangle,\quad&\mbox{if $\sigma_{k}^{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}$}\\
\lvert\sigma_{k}^{i}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle,\quad&\mbox{if $\sigma_{k}^{i}\notin\mathcal{K}_{n}$}.\end{cases}

The quantum circuit for

does not depend on the dimension of the simplices, or even the simplicial complex itself. On the other hand,

is a sequence of multi-controlled

, along with inequality checks against predetermined values that depend on

We refer to this computational model as a classically controlled quantum circuit, as defined in the main body. Then, the quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) and linear combination of unitaries (LCU) methods provide a way of constructing a PUE of the simplicial filter operator
H\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n},\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}/\sqrt{n}\right)
given by

\displaystyle p\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+q\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{{\sum}}_{i_{\ell}\in[d_{\ell}]}\mathfrak{h}_{i_{\ell}}^{\ell}\left(\dfrac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{i_{\ell}}+{{\sum}}_{i_{u}\in[d_{u}]}\mathfrak{h}_{i_{u}}^{u}\left(\dfrac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{i_{u}}.

(16)

This generalizes the definition in
[
42
]

and
\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k}^{u}

\displaystyle\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},H(x,y)\big)\big(\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle^{\otimes a}\big)=\lvert 0\rangle^{\otimes a}\,H\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle+\lvert\perp\rangle,

(17)

where

is an un-normalized state satisfying

, and
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
is as defined in Eq. (

as an implementation of QSVT
[

with a function

and PUEs of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}^{\dagger}

[

,

. Upon successful postselection, the output state is given in Eq. (

and uses
n

O(n)

[

]

To complete the definition of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
given in Eq. (

-element subset of
[n]

III
Quantum HodgeRank (extended)

Figure 1:

A comparison between several quantum least square (QLS) algorithms (with or without block-encoding (BE) method) and quantum
k
-HodgeRank to solve the
k
-HodgeRank problem given an input simplicial signal state
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
. Compared to classical methods, we do not need to explicitly construct the boundary operator element-wise, nor do we need to perform classical matrix-matrix or matrix-vector multiplication. Compared to general quantum least squares algorithms, such as those given by
[
38

, quantum HodgeRank utilizes a method that constructs an operator that encodes the boundary matrix with a much smaller scaling factor, which leads to much faster computations if the clique complex has many
k
-simplices. While it is possible that the methods used in
[
38

could be extended to utilize the PUE of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
, we do not explore that in this work.

Problem 1
.

Given a simplicial signal state
\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle
that encodes a simplicial signal value
s_{i}^{k}
for each
k
-simplex
\sigma_{k}^{i}
, output a quantum state that has amplitudes proportional to Eq. (
2
).

The quantum
k
-HodgeRank algorithm is essentially an implementation of the QTSP algorithm. Quantum
k
-HodgeRank outputs, upon successful post-selection, a quantum state that encodes an approximation to the (normalised) vector in Eq. (
2
).

Let
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
be a block-encoding or a projected unitary encoding of a matrix
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
. Gilyen et al.
[
44
]
apply QSVT to create a quantum circuit that encodes
\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{+}
, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
. For simplicity, we use the slight modification of the statement as given in
[
49
]
(in the proof of their Theorem 5), which assumes
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
is Hermitian.
The idea is to implement QSVT with a polynomial
g_{\varepsilon}(x)
of degree
O\big(\kappa_{k}\log(\kappa/(\alpha\varepsilon))\big)
, where
\kappa\in(\sqrt{n}/\zeta_{\mathrm{min}},\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, such that (i)
\big|g_{\varepsilon}(x)\big|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, (ii)
\big|1/x-2\kappa g_{\varepsilon}(x)\big|\leq\varepsilon
for
x\in[-1,-1/\kappa]\cup[1/\kappa,1]
, and (iii)
g_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
. Applying
g_{\varepsilon}(x)
to
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
approximately inverts every non-zero singular value while leaving the zero values untouched. Thus, it follows that
\big\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{+}-2\kappa/\alpha\big(\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\otimes\bra{0}^{\otimes n}\big)\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\big)\big\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
.

In the
k
-HodgeRank problem, we want to implement
(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger})^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
, given a PUE of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
. This is naturally related to implementing the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}
, which is a Hermitian matrix. It immediately follows that each eigenvalue of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}
is the square of the corresponding singular value of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
. Thus, we use QSVT to implement the polynomial
xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})
. Note that (i) since
|g_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, it immediately follows that
|xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, and (ii) if
g_{\varepsilon}(x)
has degree
D
, then
xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})
has degree
2D+1
. This allows the tools developed in the proof of Theorem 5 in
[
49
]
to be applied even though
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is not Hermitian (and indeed not even necessarily square). This is discussed briefly in
[
42
]
. Recall that the action of the QTSP circuit is described in Eq. (
17
). The circuit to apply
QTSP
\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})\big)
then has non-Clifford gate depth
O\big(n\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(n)\log(\sqrt{n}\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon)\big)
, as stated in Eq. (
8
), which follows from the cost of QTSP given in Appendix
II
with
D=O\big(\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(\sqrt{n}\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon)\big)
. Recall that
\kappa_{k}
is the effective condition number of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
and let
\zeta_{\mathrm{min}}^{k}
be the smallest non-zero singular value of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
. Problem
1
can be approximately solved as described in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\mathcal{N}_{*})
. Upon correct postselection, the QTSP algorithm with input state
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
and
H(x,y)=p(x)=xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})
outputs a quantum state
\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle
that is
2\varepsilon/\left(\mathcal{N}_{\ast}-\varepsilon\right)
-close to
\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle:=1/\mathcal{N}_{\ast}\,\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\big)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle
. The cost of applying
QTSP
\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})\big)
is
O\big(\kappa_{k}^{2}n\,\log(n)\log(\sqrt{n}\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon)\big)
and the probability of successful postselection is
O(n\mathcal{N}_{\ast}^{2}/\kappa_{k}^{4})
, where
\mathcal{N}_{\ast}:=\big\|\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\big)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\big\|_{2}
.

Proof.

It follows from
[
42
]
(adapting Theorem 5 in
[
49
]
) that there exists a polynomial
p(x)=xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})
of degree
O\big(\kappa_{k}^{2}\log(\sqrt{n}\kappa_{k}^{2}/\varepsilon)\big)
satisfying
\kappa_{k}\in(\sqrt{n}/\zeta_{\mathrm{min}}^{k},\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, such that
\big|g_{\varepsilon}(x)\big|\leq 1
and
\left|xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})\right|
for all
x\in[-1,1]
and
\big|1/x-2\kappa_{k}^{2}g_{\varepsilon}(x)\big|\leq\varepsilon
for
x\in[-1,-1/\kappa_{k}^{2}]\cup[1/\kappa_{k}^{2},1]
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}-\frac{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,

(19)

recalling
\bm{\mathrm{L}}^{u}_{k-1}=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}
as defined in Appendix
I
.
Also note that
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}=\frac{\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}}{\|\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\|}
is an alternative expression for
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}
as defined in Eq. (
5
).
Thus, we can apply
QTSP
\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})\big)
to the input state and the ancilla qubits such that

\displaystyle\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2})\big)\big(\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle^{\otimes a}\big)=p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle^{\otimes a}+\lvert\perp\rangle.

(20)

Upon the successful postselection on the ancilla qubits with probability
O(\mathcal{\widetilde{N}}_{\ast}^{2})
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\ast}:=\big\|p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\big\|_{2}
, the output is

\displaystyle\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\ast}}\,p\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\ast}}\,{{\sum}}_{i\in[n_{k-1}]}\,\frac{\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}/\sqrt{n}}\,\lvert\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\rangle.

(21)

Eq. (
20
) and the assumption that
\mathcal{N}_{*}>\varepsilon
together imply that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}\geq\sqrt{n}\left(\mathcal{N}_{\ast}-\varepsilon\right)/(2\kappa_{k}^{2})
, as the reverse triangle inequality implies that

\displaystyle\left\|p\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\right\|=\left\|p\left(\frac{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right\|\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{N}_{*}-x\right|

for some
x\in(0,\varepsilon)
, which implies the desired lower bound on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}
. A similar argument implies the corresponding upper bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}\leq\sqrt{n}(\mathcal{N}_{*}+\varepsilon)/(2\kappa_{k}^{2})
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle-\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\big(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\big)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle}{\mathcal{N_{\ast}}}-\frac{p\big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\big)\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle}{\mathcal{\widetilde{N}_{\ast}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}}\left\|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}-\mathcal{N}_{*}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}}\left[\left\|\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}-p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right\|+\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{*}-\varepsilon}\left[\left\|\left(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}-\frac{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right\|+\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\mathcal{N}_{*}-\varepsilon}.

This proves the first claim of the theorem. Now we turn to the postselection probability.
The probability of successful postselection (that is, obtaining
\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle
), is
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{2}
. By the aforementioned bounds on
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}
, if
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{*}>\varepsilon
, this is
\Omega(n(\mathcal{N}_{\ast}-\varepsilon)^{2}/(2\kappa_{k}^{2})^{2})
.
∎

We remark that
\mathcal{N}_{*}
can be arbitrarily small in general. However, if the consistency measure
\mathrm{R}(k)
is bounded away from zero, one can choose
\varepsilon
that is small enough so that
\big\|\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle-\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle\big\|_{2}
does not blow up. Let
\gamma_{\mathrm{G}}
be a (potentially application-dependent) lower bound for
\mathrm{R}(k)
set such that the rankability of
\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}\rangle
is considered good (e.g.
\gamma_{\mathrm{G}}=0.1
). Then, for data
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
such that

, it follows that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\big(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\big)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\lvert\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\rangle\big\|_{2}\leq\big\|\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\big\|_{2}\mathcal{N}_{*}\leq\sqrt{n}\mathcal{N}_{*},

since the largest singular value of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}
is at most
\sqrt{n}

[
71

, Theorem

, ensures that the output after postselection is

Moreover, if we want to boost the probability of successful postselection to be

rounds of (fixed-point oblivious) amplitude amplification

is

III.1
Assumptions on the weights
w_{ij}
in the
\ell_{2}
minimization

Here we also discuss the assumption we make about
w_{ij}
in Eq. (
1
). Our quantum
k
-HodgeRank algorithm assumes that
w_{ij}=1
if
[v_{i},v_{j}]\in\mathcal{E}
and is

otherwise. This is equivalent to the condition that there exists some constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that every pair of alternatives is either ranked by zero people or
c
people. This condition is called a

case in statistical ranking
[
4
]
. The primary reason for this restriction is because the boundary and coboundary operators in terms of fermionic Dirac operators, as originally given by

and would be an interesting avenue for further exploration.

IV
Application 1: calculation of HodgeRank (in)consistency measures via accelerated Hadamard tests

Here we give more details on the approximation of the (generalized) consistency measure
\mathrm{R}(k)
and local inconsistency measure
\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{C}}(k)
, as defined in Eq. (
9
). Since the methods for computing these are nearly identical, we combine their discussion. Let
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}
be a projector onto a particular subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, viewed as the space of
k
-chains. Here
\mathrm{P}
is a placeholder for either
\mathrm{G}
(projecting onto
\mathrm{im}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)
) or
\mathrm{C}
(projecting onto
\mathrm{im}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k+1}\right)
). The orthogonality of the gradient, curl, and harmonic spaces given by the Hodge decomposition (Eq.
14
) implies that

\displaystyle\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}=\frac{1}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}\langle\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k},\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{P}}\rangle=\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{P}}\|^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}.

Thus, if we can obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation to the inner product
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
, this is a
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
-approximation to the relative length of the projection of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
onto the desired subspace. When
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{G}
, this is the consistency measure, and when
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{C}
this is the local inconsistency measure.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}}:=\mathrm{QTSP}\left(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\right)
be the projected unitary encoding of the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-approximation to the projector
\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}
(defined in Application 1 in the main text), where
\kappa_{\mathrm{G}}=\kappa_{k}
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{C}}=\kappa_{k+1}
are defined accordingly. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{QTSP}\left(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\right)\ket{0}^{\otimes a}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\ket{0}^{\otimes a}+\sum_{x\neq 0}\ket{\mathrm{Garb}_{x}}\ket{x},

where
\|\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}-\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\bra{0}^{\otimes a}\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}})\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\ket{0}^{\otimes a}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}.

Recall that if
\|\Pi-\tilde{\Pi}\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, then
\left|\langle\Pi\ket{x},\ket{y}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\Pi}\ket{x},\ket{y}\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
for all unit vectors
\ket{x},\ket{y}
. Applying this with
\ket{x}=\ket{y}=\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
implies that
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
is an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-approximation to
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
.

Importantly,
\bra{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
can be approximated via a Hadamard test to
\beta
additive error with probability at least
1-\delta
in
O(\log(1/\delta)/\beta^{2})
controlled applications of
\mathrm{QTSP}\left(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}\right)
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
. This can be improved to
O(\log(1/\delta)/\beta)
applications using amplitude estimation, specifically by performing amplitude estimation on the Grover iterate that identifies the ancilla in state
\ket{0}^{\otimes a}
. The Grover iterate itself is straightforward to implement given the state preparation unitary
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
. Knowing
\frac{1}{2\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}}\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{P}}\|^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}
to additive error
\beta
corresponds to knowing
\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{P}}\|}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|}
to additive error
\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}\sqrt{2\beta}
.
Thus, using
O(\kappa_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2})
applications of the QTSP implementation of the Hodge projector with approximation error
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}
, we can approximate
\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}_{\mathrm{P}}\|^{2}}{\|\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\|^{2}}
to error
\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Combining this with the complexity for implementing
\textsc{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},H(x,y))
as given in Eq. (
8
) (or Theorem 2 in
[
42
]
) gives the complexities stated in Application 2.

V
Application 2: finding the relative ranking of alternatives

Problem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\big\{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\big\}_{i\in[L]}\subset\mathcal{K}_{n}
be a subset of
L
distinct
(k-1)
-simplices in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Output a list of ordered scores
\big\{s^{k-1}_{*,i}\big\}_{i\in[L]}
corresponding to such alternatives.

Given
\ket{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{\ast}}
, we can implement amplitude estimation to estimate the score assigned to each
(k-1)
-simplex by
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{\ast}}
. There are many different quantum amplitude estimation algorithms, but as we are more interested in illustrating new possible avenues for applying well-established tools from QTDA rather than aiming for optimality, we stick with a relatively simple Hadamard test.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{k-1}}^{i}
be a state preparation unitary such that
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{k-1}}^{i}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\ket{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}}
(which by Eq. (
18
) is simply parallel
\bm{\mathrm{X}}
gates on the appropriate system qubits) for
\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\in\mathcal{M}
and define
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{amp}}^{i}:=\big(\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\big)^{\dagger}\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x^{2})\big)\big(\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\big)
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
. Define a Hadamard test circuit

\displaystyle\mathrm{HAD}\big(\bm{\mathrm{U}}\big):=\big(\bm{\mathrm{Had}}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{n}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\big)\bm{\mathrm{c}}\bm{\mathrm{U}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{Had}}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{n}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a}\big),

(22)

where
\bm{\mathrm{cU}}
denotes a controlled application of
\bm{\mathrm{U}}
. Then, we can estimate the rescaled amplitude
\big(\sqrt{n}/2\kappa_{k}^{2}\big)\,\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}=\bra{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}=\bra{0}^{\otimes(n+a)}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{amp}}^{i}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n+a)}

by estimating

P(0)=\big(\bra{0}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{n+a}\big)\mathrm{HAD}\big(\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{amp}}^{i}\big)\big(\ket{0}\ket{0}^{n+a}\big)
, the probability of a single shot of the Hadamard test returning the outcome
0
. The amplitude estimation requires
O(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AE}})
calls to
\mathrm{HAD}\big(\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{amp}}^{i}\big)
and its inverse, for some
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AE}}>0
, to obtain an estimate
\hat{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}
that is
\big(2\kappa_{k}^{2}/\sqrt{n}\big)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AE}}
-close to
\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}
with probability of success
1-\delta
. This leads to

\displaystyle\big|s^{k-1}_{\ast,i}-\hat{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\bm{\mathrm{L}}_{k-1}^{u}\big)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}-\frac{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\big\|_{2}+\big|\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}-\hat{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}+\big(2\kappa_{k}^{2}/\sqrt{n}\big)\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AE}}=\varepsilon,

by setting
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/2
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AE}}=\varepsilon\sqrt{n}/(4\kappa_{k}^{2})
. We perform this amplitude estimation procedure
L
times, one for each
(k-1)
-simplex in
\mathcal{M}
, to obtain
\big\{\hat{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}\big\}_{i\in[L]}
. Given
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, the above application requires
O(n)
qubits and
O\big(L\kappa_{k}^{2}\,\log(L/\delta)/(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon)\big)
calls to each of
\textsc{QTSP}\big(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon/2}(x)\big)
,
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k-1}^{i}
to obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation to each of the
L
scores with probability
1-\delta
(by the union bound over all
L
procedures). The last step is then to order the scores to create a relative ranking of the elements in
\mathcal{M}
.

This is only a heuristic because, given some data
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}
, there is no clear way to choose
\varepsilon
a priori in order to guarantee that the relative ranking of
\mathcal{M}
obtained by the above procedure is the same as the relative ranking of these simplices according to
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}
. If a pair of amplitudes in
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}
corresponding to basis states in
\mathcal{M}
differ by less than
2\varepsilon
, these two basis states may be out of order in the relative ranking. For example, suppose the amplitudes of
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
are given by the sequence
\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{n_{k-1}}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{n_{k}-1}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k-1}}},\dots\right)
, and the aim is to relatively rank the first three alternatives. If
n_{k-1}\approx\binom{n}{k}
, then
\varepsilon=O\left(\binom{n}{k}^{-1/2}\right)
is needed in order to deduce anything meaningful from the output. For this reason this application will not achieve a large (exponential), general speedup over the classical computation, even if it does well in some cases.

VI
Application 3: finding a good alternative (extended)

Problem 3
.

Given
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}
, with high probability output an ordered list of alternatives
\{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\}
according to
\{\hat{s}^{k-1}_{\ast,i}\}
that are
\varepsilon
-close to their scores
\{s^{k-1}_{\ast,i}\}
.

We tackle this problem using
\varepsilon
-
\ell_{\infty}
-norm tomography as described in
[
53
]
. We present two of their theorems and discuss their applicability to quantum
k
-HodgeRank.

Theorem 2

(Corollary 17, Proposition 19
[
53
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and let
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{j\in[d]}\alpha_{j}\ket{j}
be a quantum state with
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

O\left(\log(d/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
applications (in parallel) of a controlled state-preparation unitary for
\ket{\psi}
suffice to compute an
\varepsilon
-
\ell_{q}
-norm estimate
\tilde{\alpha}
of
\alpha
with success probability at least
1-\delta
. On the other hand, if we only wish to learn
\tilde{\alpha}
up to a global phase, it is sufficient to have
O\left(\log(d)\log(d/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}\right)
copies of
\ket{\psi}
, with the ability to perform unitary operations on each copy before measurement.

We can apply these results with the unitary
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2}))(\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{1}}^{a})
which prepares the state described in Eq. (
20
), which plays the role of
\ket{\psi}
.
The dimension of the vector
\alpha
, i.e., the amplitudes of the state
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\varepsilon}\ket{0}^{\otimes(n+a)}
, is
2^{n+a}
, where
a=O(n)
is the number of ancilla qubits used. Thus, in the language of Theorem
2
,
d=2^{O(n)}
. Eqs. (
19
) and (
21
) together imply that

\displaystyle\left|\bra{x}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)^{+}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}-\frac{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\bra{x}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{k}/\sqrt{n}\right)\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k}}\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Setting
\ket{x}=\ket{\sigma_{k-1}^{i}}
implies that
\left|\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}-s^{k-1}_{*,i}\right|\leq\varepsilon
for all
i\in[n_{k-1}]
. Now suppose we have a classical representation of
\ket{\hat{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}}
, an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-
\ell_{\infty}
norm approximation to
\ket{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}}
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{n}}{2\kappa_{k}^{2}}
. Then for each
i\in[n_{k-1}]
,

\displaystyle\left|\hat{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}-{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}\right|\leq\left|\hat{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}-\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}\right|+\left|\tilde{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}-{s}^{k-1}_{*,i}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon.

Applying Theorem
2
with
d=2^{O(n)}
and
\delta=2^{-n}
, it follows that
O(\kappa_{k}^{4}\varepsilon^{-2})
parallel, controlled applications of
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\varepsilon}
(i.e., of
\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2}))
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
) are required to learn
\ket{\hat{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}}
with probability
1-2^{-n}
, or
O(\kappa_{k}^{4}n\varepsilon^{-2})
applications
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\varepsilon}
are required to learn
\ket{\hat{\bm{\mathrm{s}}}^{k-1}_{*}}
up to a global phase with probability
1-2^{-n}
.

Knowing the value of
\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}
up to a global phase amounts to knowing the global order of alternatives but not knowing whether it is ranked best-to-worst or worst-to-best. In many cases, this would be possible to determine at a glance, by simply comparing the two extremes manually. In this case, one could get away with only using copies of
\ket{\bm{\mathrm{s}}^{k-1}_{*}}
, rather than conditional applications of
\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2}))
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
. However, due to the concrete implementation of
\mathrm{QTSP}(k,\mathcal{K}_{n},xg_{\varepsilon}(x^{2}))
, this might not offer a huge advantage unless controlled access to
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
is unavailable.

This method for determining a good alternative is only a heuristic for similar reasons that were discussed in Appendix
V
: there is no way to determine an appropriate choice of
\varepsilon
, and there are states where
\varepsilon
will have to be exponentially small in
k
to draw meaningful conclusions from this procedure. If there was some guarantee that the scores of the alternatives all differ by at least some
\varepsilon>0
that does not vanish exponentially, then one can recover the exact ranking according to
k
-HodgeRank efficiently (provided the standard assumptions about
\kappa_{k}
being small). However, this is not a reasonable assumption in general. A more relaxed assumption may be that if a small set of alternatives (
k
-simplices) have much higher scores than the rest, classifying whether alternatives lie in this set or not may be efficient.
