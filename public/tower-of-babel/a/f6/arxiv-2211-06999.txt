Orthogonal polynomials on a class of planar algebraic curves
Marco Fasondini, Sheehan Olver, Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We construct bivariate orthogonal polynomials (OPs) on algebraic curves of the form y m = ϕ ⁡ ( x ) y^{m}=\phi(x) in ℝ 2 {\mathbb{R}}^{2} where m = 1 , 2 m=1,2 and ϕ \phi is a polynomial of arbitrary degree d d , in terms of univariate semiclassical OPs. We compute connection coefficients that relate the bivariate OPs to a polynomial basis that is itself orthogonal and whose span contains the OPs as a subspace. The connection matrix is shown to be banded and the connection coefficients and Jacobi matrices for OPs of degree 0 , … , N 0,\ldots,N are computed via the Lanczos algorithm in 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ d 4 ) \mathcal{O}(Nd^{4}) operations.



1.
Introduction

In previous works, we studied orthogonal polynomials (OPs) on planar curves and on quadratic surfaces of revolution. The OPs were constructed explicitly on the wedge and the square
[
11
]
, planar quadratic curves
[
14
]
, cubic curves
[
6
]
, as well as on and inside quadratic surfaces of revolution
[
12
]
. These OPs were used in applications, such as the statistics of determinantal point processes, the computation of Stieltjes transforms, multivariate function approximation and orthogonal series
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
, the approximation of singular or nearly singular functions of one variable (including those that arise as solutions to differential equations), the explicit solution to wave equations
[
15
]
, spectral methods for partial differential equations on trapeziums, disk slices and spherical caps
[
17
,
18
]
and a spectral method for fractional integral equations
[
16
]
.

In this paper we generalise both the types of curves on which OPs are constructed as well as their method of construction, in particular to OPs defined on curves of the form
y^{m}=\phi(x)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
where
m=1,2
and
\phi
is a polynomial of arbitrary degree
d
. Unlike the previously mentioned studies, our constructions of OPs are computational, not explicit. However, on quadratic and cubic curves, our computational method reproduces the OP bases given in
[
14
,
6
]
and in section
4
we give explicit bases for a few new special cases. Our approach is first to explicitly construct a polynomial basis whose span contains the bivariate OPs as a subspace, then we cast the computation of the OPs as a numerical linear algebra problem, namely, simultaneously block-tridiagonalize two symmetric, commuting matrices, which we solve with the Lanczos algorithm. This is a very general approach that allows us to construct OPs (i) on certain algebraic curves (the topic of this paper) and also (ii) inside those curves (two-dimensional domains), (iii) their surfaces of revolution (three-dimensional regions) and (iv) associated star domains (2D and 3D regions). The construction of OPs on (ii)–(iv) will be considered elsewhere. Sparse spectral methods for PDEs is an application of the OPs we construct that is of special interest and will be pursued in future research.

2.
Univariate OPs

Throughout, we shall denote a univariate, orthonormal,
degree-
n
OP that is orthogonal with respect to the inner product

(2.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\omega}=\int f(x)g(x)\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where
\omega
is a non-negative weight function, by
p_{n}(\omega;x)
, or
p_{n}(\omega)
. We shall also make use of quasimatrix
[
19
]
notation. For example, the quasimatrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\phi w)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c}p_{0}(\phi w)&p_{1}(\phi w)&p_{2}(\phi w)&\cdots\end{array}\right),

where
\phi=\phi(x)
is polynomial that is strictly positive on the support of the weight
w
, is a ‘matrix’ with a countable infinity of columns consisting of OPs with respect to the weight
\phi w
, ordered by degrees.

We shall let
w
be a classical weight, i.e., a Jacobi, Laguerre or Hermite weight
[
4
, Ch. 18]
with support
\mathrm{supp}(w)=(-1,1),(0,\infty)
and
(-\infty,\infty)
, respectively. If all the roots of the polynomial
\phi
(with
\phi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(w)
) are at the endpoints of
\mathrm{supp}(w)
, then
\phi w
is also a classical weight, otherwise at least one of the roots of
\phi
are off
\mathrm{supp}(w)
and
\phi w
is a semiclassical weight
[
10
]
and we refer to the
p_{n}(\phi w)
as (orthonormal) semiclassical OPs.

The fundamental building blocks of our constructions of bivariate OPs will be Jacobi matrices and a ‘raising matrix’ of univariate OPs, which we shall now define. Recall that if a family of OPs, e.g.,
p_{n}(\phi w)
,
n\geq 0
, is orthonormal with respect to an inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{\phi w}
, then they satisfy a three-term recurrence of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle xp_{n}(\phi w)=\beta_{n-1}p_{n-1}(\phi w)+\alpha_{n}p_{n}(\phi w)+\beta_{n}p_{n+1}(\phi w),\qquad n\geq 0,

where
\beta_{-1}=0=p_{-1}(\phi w)
and the recurrence coefficients are

(2.4)

\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{n}(\phi w)={\langle}xp_{n}(\phi w),p_{n}(\phi w){\rangle}_{\phi w},\qquad\beta_{n}=\beta_{n}(\phi w)={\langle}xp_{n}(\phi w),p_{n+1}(\phi w){\rangle}_{\phi w}.

Since
w
is a classical weight and
\phi(x)>0
for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(w)
,
\alpha_{n}(\phi w),\beta_{n}(\phi w)\in\mathbb{R}
for
n\geq 0
.
In quasimatrix notation, (
2.3
) becomes

(2.5)

x\mathbf{P}(\phi w)=\mathbf{P}(\phi w)J(\phi w),

where
J(\phi w)
is an infinite, symmetric, tridiagonal matrix known as the Jacobi matrix of the OPs,

(2.6)

J(\phi w)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\alpha_{0}(\phi w)&\beta_{0}(\phi w)&&\\
\beta_{0}(\phi w)&\alpha_{1}(\phi w)&\beta_{1}(\phi w)&\\
&\beta_{1}(\phi w)&\alpha_{2}(\phi w)&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

We say that a (finite or infinite) matrix
A
is banded and has bandwidths
(\lambda,\mu)
,
\lambda,\mu\geq 0
if
A_{i,j}=0
for
i-j>\lambda
and
j-i>\mu
and the same applies for block-matrices (in which case
A_{i,j}
refers to block
(i,j)
of
A
). Hence,
J(\phi w)
has bandwidths
(1,1)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
\deg\phi=d
, then the OPs
\{p_{n}(w)\}
and
\{p_{n}(\phi w)\}
are related via a banded raising matrix
R
with bandwidths
(0,d)
according to

(2.7)

\mathbf{P}(w)=\mathbf{P}(\phi w)R,

where
\mathbf{P}(\phi w)
is given in (
2
) and
\mathbf{P}(w)
is defined analogously and

(2.8)

R=\left(\begin{array}[]{c c c c c c c}r_{0,0}&\cdots&\cdots&r_{0,d}&&&\\
&r_{1,1}&\cdots&\cdots&r_{1,d+1}&&\\
&&r_{2,2}&\cdots&\cdots&r_{2,d+2}&\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right),

where

(2.9)

r_{k,n}=\langle p_{n}(w),p_{k}(\phi w)\rangle_{\phi w}=\langle p_{n}(w),\phi p_{k}(\phi w)\rangle_{w}.

Proof.

Since any polynomial of degree
n
is a linear combination of the
p_{k}(\phi w)
with
0\leq k\leq n
, we have
p_{n}(w)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}r_{k,n}p_{k}(\phi w)
, where the expression (
2.9
) and the fact that
r_{k,n}=0
for
0\leq k<n-d
follow since the
p_{k}(\phi w)
and
p_{n}(w)
are (orthonormal) OPs.
∎

In quasimatrix notation, we write

(2.10)

R=\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{\phi w}=\int\mathbf{P}^{\top}(\phi w)\mathbf{P}(w)\phi w\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\mathbf{P}^{\top}(\phi w)\mathbf{P}(w)

is interpreted as an outer product of two (infinite) row vectors whose entries are functions and integration is performed entry-wise. Therefore,
\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{\phi w}
is an infinite matrix whose entries are defined as follows: Let
\bm{e}_{k}
be the standard basis vector

(2.11)

\bm{e}_{k}=\Big(\underbrace{0\>\cdots\>0}_{k\text{ zeros}}\>1\>0\>0\>\cdots\Big)^{\top}

then the
(k,n)
entry of
\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{\phi w}
for
k,n\geq 0
is (cf. (
2.9
))

(2.12)

\left(\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{\phi w}\right)_{k,n}=\int\mathbf{P}(\phi w)\bm{e}_{k}\mathbf{P}(w)\bm{e}_{n}\phi w\,dx=\langle p_{n}(w),p_{k}(\phi w)\rangle_{\phi w}=r_{k,n}.

Similarly, we can express the Jacobi matrix as
J(\phi w)=\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),x\mathbf{P}(\phi w)\rangle_{\phi w}
and since the OPs
\{p_{n}(w)\}
and
\{p_{n}(\phi w)\}
are orthonormal,
\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(\phi w)\rangle_{\phi w}=I=\langle\mathbf{P}(w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{w}
, where
I
is an infinite identity matrix.

The coefficients
r_{k,n}
of the raising matrix
R
can also be viewed as the connection coefficients relating the classical OP basis
\{p_{n}(w)\}
to the semiclassical OP basis
\{p_{n}(\phi w)\}
. Algorithms for computing connection coefficients between (mainly univariate) classical OP families with different weights are discussed in
[
13
]
. The more general problem of relating the OP families
\{p_{n}(rw)\}
and
\{p_{n}(w)\}
, where
r=r(x)
is a rational function, and determining the recurrence coefficients of the former OP family is discussed in
[
7
, sect. 2.4]
and associated algorithms are considered in
[
9
, Ch. 5]
. For our purposes (i.e., the case
r(x)=\phi(x)
), we note that one method for computing
R
and
J(\phi w)
is via the Cholesky factor
L
of the symmetric, positive definite matrix

\Phi=\langle\mathbf{P}(w),\mathbf{P}(w)\rangle_{\phi w}=\int\mathbf{P}(w)^{\top}\mathbf{P}(w)\phi w\,\mathrm{d}x=LL^{\top}

because from (
2.7
) we have that

I=\langle\mathbf{P}(\phi w),\mathbf{P}(\phi w)\rangle_{w}=\int\mathbf{P}(\phi w)^{\top}\mathbf{P}(\phi w)\phi w\,\mathrm{d}x=R^{-\top}\Phi R^{-1}=R^{-\top}LL^{\top}R^{-1}

from which we conclude that

(2.13)

R=L^{\top},\qquad J(\phi w)=R^{\top}J(w)R,

where the second identity follows from (
2.5
) and (
2.7
). Another method for computing
R
and
J(\phi w)
given
J(w)
is to use Christoffel’s theorem
[
7
, sect. 2.4]
.

The topic of this paper is also the computation of connection coefficients (and, as a byproduct, recurrence coefficients will also be computed), however we shall relate bivariate OPs (restricted to curves) to a polynomial basis that is orthogonal with respect to the
same
weight as the bivariate OPs. As we shall see, not only is our connection problem complicated by the fact that it is bivariate but also because in general the degree of a bivariate polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is different from its degree when restricted to an algebraic curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

3.
Bivariate OPs on
y=\phi
and
y^{2}=\phi

Curves defined by
p(x,y)=0
, where
p
is an irreducible bivariate quadratic or cubic polynomial, can be simplified to the form
y^{2}=\phi(x)
, with
\deg\phi\leq 2
for quadratics and
\deg\phi=3
for cubics, see
[
14
]
and
[
6
]
, respectively. Through affine transformations, non-degenerate quadratics can be reduced to either a parabola (
\phi=x
), a circle (
\phi=1-x^{2}
), or a hyperbola (
\phi=x^{2}-1
). A cubic curve in the standard form
y^{2}=\phi(x)
,
\deg\phi=3
is an elliptic curve if it has no cusps, isolated points or self-intersections; the OPs we constructed in
[
6
]
are valid on both elliptic and non-elliptic curves. In this paper we consider bivariate OPs on the following more general curves

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{m,d}=\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\begin{cases}y=\phi(x)&\text{if }m=1\\
y^{2}=\phi(x)>0&\text{if }m=2\end{cases},x\in\mathrm{supp}(w),\deg\phi=d>0\right\}

where
\mathrm{supp}(w)
denotes the support of a non-negative weight function
w(x)
; either
\mathrm{supp}(w)=(a,b)
,
(a,\infty)
or
(-\infty,\infty)
, where
-\infty<a<b<\infty
and
\phi
may vanish at
a
or
b
. The restriction
\phi>0
does not apply for
m=1
but for
m=2
, the positivity of
\phi
is necessary for the curve to be defined in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

For the special case
m=1
,
d=0
and a classical weight
w
, the classical OPs are OPs on the curves
{\gamma}_{1,0}
. The case
m=2
and
d=0
is treated in
[
14
]
. OPs on curves of the form
y^{m}=\phi
with
m>2
, which we leave for future work, will be discussed briefly in section
6
.

We consider OPs in
(x,y)
that are restricted to the curve
{\gamma}_{m,d}
and which are orthogonal with respect to the following inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}=\int_{{\gamma}_{m,d}}f(x,y)g(x,y)\omega(x,y)\mathrm{d}\sigma(x,y),

where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
is the arc length measure on
{\gamma}_{m,d}
. We incorporate the arc length into the non-negative weight function by setting
\omega(x,y)=w(x)/\sqrt{1+(y^{\prime}(x))^{2}}
so that the inner product simplifies to

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}=\begin{cases}&\displaystyle{\int}f\left(x,\phi(x)\right)g\left(x,\phi(x)\right)w(x)\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 61.17325ptm=1,\\
&\displaystyle{\int}\left[f\left(x,\sqrt{\phi(x)}\right)g\left(x,\sqrt{\phi(x)}\right)\right.\\
&\left.\>\>\>+f\left(x,-\sqrt{\phi(x)}\right)g\left(x,-\sqrt{\phi(x)}\right)\right]w(x)\mathrm{d}x,\>\>\>m=2,\end{cases}

where the integrals are over
\mathrm{supp}(w)
. The bilinear form
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}
defines an inner product on the space of polynomials of two variables restricted to the curve
{\gamma}_{m,d}
,

\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d}):={\mathbb{R}}[x,y]/{\langle}y^{m}-\phi(x){\rangle},

which denotes the space of bivariate polynomials modulo the ideal generated by
y^{m}-\phi(x)=0
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d},w)
(or
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
) be the space of bivariate OPs of degree
n
with respect to the inner product (
3.2
) defined on
\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d})
, then

(3.3)

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d},w)=\min\{n+1,\max\{d,m\}\}.

Proof.

We first consider the dimension of the space of monomials of degree
n
on the curve
{\gamma}_{m,d}
, which we denote by
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})
. Three cases arise: (i)
n\leq\max\{d,m\}-1
, (ii)
m>d,n\geq m
and (iii)
m\leq d
,
n\geq d
. Since we only let
m=1,2
and
d>0
, case (ii) only arises if
m=2
and
d=1
.

For case(i), we have that

(3.4)

{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=\displaystyle{\left\{x^{n-k}y^{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{n}},\qquad n\leq\max\{d,m\}-1,

because the monomials
x^{k}y^{n-k}
are linearly independent for
0\leq k\leq n
and thus
\dim{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=n+1
.

For case (ii),

(3.5)

{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=\left\{x^{n-k}y^{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{m-1},\qquad m>d,\qquad n\geq m,

because we can set
y^{m}=\phi(x)
and express the monomials
x^{n-k}y^{k}
with
k\geq m
in terms of the monomials in
{\mathcal{B}}_{k}({\gamma}_{m,d})
, with
0\leq k\leq n
, hence only the monomials
x^{n-k}y^{k}
with
0\leq k\leq m-1
are linearly independent and thus
\dim{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=m
.

For the final case (iii),

(3.6)

{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=\left\{x^{d-1}y^{n+1-d},x^{d-2}y^{n+2-d},\ldots,y^{n}\right\},\qquad d\geq m,\qquad n\geq d,

because we can use the fact that
y^{m}=c_{d}x^{d}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}
, with
c_{d}\neq 0
to set
x^{d}=\psi(x,y)
, where
\deg\psi<d
, and express
y^{n-k}x^{k}
with
k\geq d
in terms of lower degree monomials, therefore
\dim{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=d

Applying the Gram–Schmidt process to the basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})
in cases (i)–(iii), it follows that
\dim{\mathcal{B}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d})=\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\gamma}_{m,d},w)
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

A bivariate polynomial
p(x,y)\in\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d})
can be represented in terms of polynomials of one variable as follows,

(3.7)

p(x,y)=\begin{cases}q(x)&\text{if }m=1,\\
r(x)+ys(x)&\text{if }m=2,\end{cases}

where
q(x),r(x),s(x)
are polynomials in
x
.

Proof.

Any
p(x,y)\in\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d})
can be expressed as a linear combination of the elements of the monomial bases in (
3.4
)–(
3.6
). The result follows immediately upon setting
y=\phi(x)
(if
m=1
) or
y^{2}=\phi(x)
(if
m=2
) in the monomial expansion of
p(x,y)
.
∎

In view of this result, we introduce the “
P
-polynomials” or “
P
-basis”

(3.8)

\displaystyle P^{(m,d)}_{n,k}(x,y)=\begin{cases}p_{n}(w;x)&\text{for }m=1,k=0,n\geq 0\\
p_{n}(w;x)/\sqrt{2}&\text{for }m=2,k=0,n\geq 0\\
yp_{n-1}(\phi w;x)/\sqrt{2}&\text{for }m=2,k=1,n\geq 1\end{cases},

which clearly form a basis for
\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d})
and, moreover:

Proposition 3.3
.

The
P
-polynomials are orthonormal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}
:

\langle P^{(m,d)}_{n,k},P^{(m,d)}_{\ell,i}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}=\delta_{n,\ell}\delta_{k,i},\qquad m=1,2.

Proof.

Since the
p_{n}(w)
are orthonormal with respect to the univariate inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{w}
we have, for
m=1
,

\langle P^{(1,d)}_{n,0},P^{(1,d)}_{\ell,0}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{1,d},w}=\langle p_{n}(w),p_{\ell}(w)\rangle_{w}=\delta_{n,\ell}.

For
m=2
, three orthogonality relations need to be demonstrated. First, orthogonality between
P
-polynomials with
k=0
in (
3.8
):

\langle P^{(2,d)}_{n,0},P^{(2,d)}_{\ell,0}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{2,d},w}=\langle p_{n}(w),p_{\ell}(w)\rangle_{w}=\delta_{n,\ell}.

Next, between polynomials with
k=1
,

\langle P^{(2,d)}_{n,1},P^{(2,d)}_{\ell,1}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{2,d},w}=\langle\phi p_{n-1}(\phi w),p_{\ell-1}(\phi w)\rangle_{w}=\langle p_{n-1}(\phi w),p_{\ell-1}(\phi w)\rangle_{\phi w}=\delta_{n,\ell}.

The final orthogonality relation is due to the reflection symmetry of the curve for
m=2
:

\langle P^{(2,d)}_{n,1},P^{(2,d)}_{\ell,0}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{2,d},w}=\int\left[yp_{n-1}(\phi w)p_{\ell}(w)-yp_{n-1}(\phi w)p_{\ell}(w)\right]w\mathrm{d}x=0.

∎

3.1.
Connection coefficients

We denote the degree-
n
, orthonormal OPs on
{\gamma}_{m,d}

Y^{(m,d)}_{n,k}(x,y)

It follows from Proposition

.

3.2.
Minimal and maximal degrees of polynomials on
{\gamma}_{m,d}

If
p
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
, then we define the degree of the restriction of
p
to
{\gamma}_{m,d}
as the lowest degree of
q
among all
q
satisfying
p=q
modulo
\langle y^{m}-\phi(x)\rangle
. By construction, the monomial bases in (
3.4
)–(
3.6
) are minimal degree representations of polynomials in
\Pi({\gamma}_{m,d})
. Therefore, the degree of
p(x,y)
on the curve
{\gamma}_{m,d}
is the degree of its expansion in the monomial basis given in (
3.4
)–(
3.6
).

If
d>m
and
p(x,y)\in\Pi({\gamma}_{m,n})
, then a maximal degree representation of
p
is obtained by setting
y=\phi
(if
m=1
) or
y^{2}=\phi
(if
m=2
), in which case
p
takes the form given in (
3.7
). For example, if
m=1<d
, then
y^{n}=\phi^{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n
in
\Pi({\gamma}_{m,n})
whose maximal degree on
{\gamma}_{1,d}
is
nd
. Observe from (
3.8
) that the total degree of a
P
-polynomial
P^{(m,d)}_{n,k}
is
n
, which is also its maximal degree since it is in the form (
3.7
). Therefore, the maximal degree of
p(x,y)\in\Pi({\gamma}_{m,n})
is the total degree of its expansion in the
P
-basis. We denote the maximal degree of
p(x,y)
on
{\gamma}_{m,d}
by
\deg p
and its minimal degree by
\deg_{{\gamma}_{m,d}}(p)
. For example, if
m=1<d
, then
\deg y^{n}=nd
and
\deg_{{\gamma}_{1,d}}(y^{n})=n
and for
n>d
,
\deg P^{(m,d)}_{n,k}=n>\deg_{{\gamma}_{m,d}}(P^{(m,d)}_{n,k})
while, by definition,
\deg_{{\gamma}_{m,d}}(Y^{(m,d)}_{n,k})=n
.

5.2.
The Gram–Schmidt process viewed as a block Lanczos algorithm

Since the
Y_{n,k}^{(m,d)}
are orthonormal OPs, the

satisfy three-term recurrences of the form
[

(5.19)

\displaystyle x{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}={\mathbb{Y}}_{n-1}^{(m,d)}\left(B_{n-1,x}^{(m,d)}\right)^{\top}+{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}A_{n,x}^{(m,d)}+{\mathbb{Y}}_{n+1}^{(m,d)}B_{n,x}^{(m,d)},

and

(5.20)

\displaystyle y{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}={\mathbb{Y}}_{n-1}^{(m,d)}\left(B_{n-1,y}^{(m,d)}\right)^{\top}+{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}A_{n,y}^{(m,d)}+{\mathbb{Y}}_{n+1}^{(m,d)}B_{n,y}^{(m,d)},

for
n\geq 0
with
{\mathbb{Y}}_{-1}:=0
, where

(5.21)

\displaystyle A_{n,x}^{(m,d)}={\langle}{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)},x{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w},\quad B_{n,x}^{(m,d)}={\langle}{\mathbb{Y}}_{n+1}^{(m,d)},x{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w},

and
A_{n,y}^{(m,d)}
,

are defined similarly. Hence,

and

are symmetric.
In quasimatrix notation, the recurrences (

where

and

are the symmetric, commuting, block-tridiagonal Jacobi matrices of the OPs
\mathbf{Y}^{(m,d)}

To express the recurrences (

(5.23)

{\mathbb{Y}}_{n}^{(m,d)}=\mathbf{P}^{(m,d)}{\mathbb{C}}_{n}^{(m,d)}.

5.7.
Numerical stability and computational complexity

The recursive formulae for the connection coefficients given in section
5.5
are numerically unstable. This is to be expected since the formulae arise from the Gram–Schmidt procedure, which is well-known to be unstable
[
1
]
and the instability of the Lanczos algorithm is also well-established (see
[
7
, section 2.2.3]
and
[
3
, Supplement D]
). We shall consider the computational cost of our Lanczos (or, equivalently, Gram–Schmidt) algorithm when it is stabilized via reorthogonalization
[
1
,
3
]
(the Lanczos algorithm can also be stabilized if it is performed via Householder transformations
[
20
]
or Givens rotations
[
8
]
).

Due to the instability of the Lanczos procedure, the OPs are not exactly orthogonal in floating point arithmetic. After a new OP has been generated via a new column of the connection matrix (using the formulae in section
5.5
and Appendix
A
), we orthogonalize this OP against lower degree OPs to ensure orthogonality up to the level of roundoff errors. That is, we replace the OP
Y_{n+1,k}^{(m,d)}
with its re-orthogonalized version:

(5.87)

Y_{n+1,k}^{(m,d)}\leftarrow Y_{n+1,k}^{(m,d)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s\leq n+1\\
1\leq i\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\end{subarray}}\langle Y_{n+1,k}^{(m,d)},Y^{(m,d)}_{s,i}\rangle_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}\,Y^{(m,d)}_{s,i},

which we express in terms of the columns of the connection matrix by setting
Y_{n,k}^{(m,d)}=\mathbf{P}^{(m,d)}\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(n,k)}
:

\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(n+1,k)}\leftarrow\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(n+1,k)}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s\leq n+1\\
1\leq i\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\end{subarray}}\left[\left(\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(n+1,k)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(s,i)}\right]\mathbf{C}^{(m,d)}_{\ell(s,i)}.

We can use the results in section
5.6
and Appendix
B
(i.e., the fact that the connection matrices are banded) to show that we only need to re-orthogonalize against a constant number of lower degree OPs (as opposed to

6.
Conclusion

For the cases
m=1,2
, we have constructed bivariate OPs on the algebraic curves
y^{m}=\phi
. For
m>2
, if we generalise the
P
-polynomials

P^{(m,d)}_{n,k}=y^{k}p_{n-k}(\phi^{2k/m}w;x),\qquad k=0,\ldots,m-1,

and the inner product

\displaystyle{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\int}f\left(x,\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)g\left(x,\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)w(x)\mathrm{d}x,&m\text{ odd},\\
\displaystyle{\int}\left[f\left(x,\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)g\left(x,\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)\right.&\\
\left.\>\>\>+f\left(x,-\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)g\left(x,-\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}\right)\right]w(x)\mathrm{d}x,&m\text{ even},\end{cases}

then the
P
-polynomials are not orthogonal with respect to
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\gamma}_{m,d},w}
for
m>2
. We can nevertheless still construct the OPs on
y^{m}=\phi
as linear combinations of the
P
-polynomials. However, the computation of the connection matrix would require the Cholesky factorisation of a dense Gram matrix, which has cubic complexity, see footnote
1
. For
m=1,2
, we showed that the connection matrix is banded but for
m>2
, the connection matrix will in general be upper triangular.

Topics for future research include the development of the theory of interpolation, quadrature and orthogonal series on the curves
y^{m}=\phi
, as was done for quadratic and cubic curves in
[
14
,
6
]
. As an application, OPs on
y^{m}=\phi(x)
could be used to approximate functions with algebraic singularities of the form
\sqrt[m]{\phi(x)}
at the zeros of
\phi
, again as was done in
[
14
,
6
]
for square-root and cubic singularities. Another application of the OPs on
y^{m}=\phi
is the construction of OPs inside
y^{m}=\phi
and their associated surfaces of revolution for the purpose of spectral methods for partial differential equations.

, then
n
is odd and
C^{(2,d)}_{j,\ell(n+1,k)}=0
for

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}(j,2)=0\qquad\text{or}

\displaystyle j>1+(n-1)d\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad k=1,3,\ldots,d-4

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}(j,2)=1\qquad\text{or}

\displaystyle j>2+(n-1)d\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad k=2,4,\ldots,d-3

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}(j,2)=0\qquad\text{or}

\displaystyle j>3+(n-1)d\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad k=d-2

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}(j,2)=1\qquad\text{or}

\displaystyle j>4+(n-1)d\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad k=d-1

\displaystyle\mathrm{mod}(j,2)=0\qquad\text{or}

\displaystyle j>(n+1)d-1\qquad

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad k=d

Proof.

It follows from the cases listed in (
A.1
) whether, for a given column
\mathbf{C}^{(2,d)}_{\ell(n,k)}
, the even-indexed rows or the odd-indexed rows are zero. For example, for
0\leq n\leq d-1
, if
k
is odd, then
C^{(2,d)}_{2j,\ell(n,k)}=0
for
j\geq 1
.

To determine the upper and lower bounds on the summation indices in (
A.1
), we use the same arguments as in Proposition
5.6
, which we present here with more brevity.

For
0\leq n\leq d-2
,

(B.9)

\deg x^{n+2-k}y^{k-1}=\begin{cases}n+2-k+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)},\qquad k=1,3,\ldots,\begin{cases}n+1&n\text{ even}\\
n+2&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}\\
n+3-k+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{k-2}{2}\right)},\qquad k=2,4,\ldots,\begin{cases}n+2&n\text{ even}\\
n+1&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}.\end{cases}

For
n\geq d-1
with
\mathrm{mod}(n+1-d,2)=0
,

(B.10)

\deg x^{d-k}y^{n+1+k-d}=\begin{cases}d-k+1+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{n+k-d}{2}\right)},\qquad k=1,3,\ldots,\begin{cases}d&n\text{ even}\\
d-1&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}\\
d-k+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{n+1+k-d}{2}\right)},\qquad k=2,4,\ldots,\begin{cases}d-1&n\text{ even}\\
d&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}\end{cases}

and if
n\geq d-1
and
\mathrm{mod}(n+1-d,2)=1
, then

(B.11)

\deg x^{d-k}y^{n+1+k-d}=\begin{cases}d-k+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{n+1+k-d}{2}\right)},\qquad k=1,3,\ldots,\begin{cases}d-1&n\text{ even}\\
d&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}\\
d-k+1+\displaystyle{d\left(\frac{n+k-d}{2}\right)},\qquad k=2,4,\ldots,\begin{cases}d&n\text{ even}\\
d-1&n\text{ odd}\end{cases}.\end{cases}

By the same reasoning used in Proposition
5.6
, we conclude from (
B.9
) that if
n
is even, with
0\leq n\leq d-2
, then

(B.12)

\deg Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\begin{cases}dn/2&k=1,3,\ldots,n-1\\
2+d(n-2)/2&k=2,4,\ldots,n-2\\
3+d(n-2)/2&k=n\\
1+dn/2&k=n+1,n+2\end{cases}.

For
0\leq n\leq d-2
, it follows from (
B.9
) that

(B.13)

\displaystyle\deg_{{\gamma}_{2,d}}(P^{(2,d)}_{j,0})<n+1\quad\text{and}\quad\deg_{{\gamma}_{2,d}}(P^{(2,d)}_{j,1})<n+1\quad\text{for}\quad j<n+1.

Combining the first and second cases in (
A.1
) with (
B.12
) and (
B.13
), it follows that if
n
is even, with
0\leq n\leq d-2
, then

(B.14)

Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{dn/2}C^{(2,d)}_{2j-1,\ell(n+1,k)}P_{j,0}^{(2,d)},\qquad k=1,3,\ldots,n-1\\

(B.15)

Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{2+d(n-2)/2}C^{(2,d)}_{2j,\ell(n+1,k)}P_{j,1}^{(2,d)},\qquad k=2,4,\ldots,n-2\\

(B.16)

Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{3+d(n-2)/2}C^{(2,d)}_{2j,\ell(n+1,k)}P_{j,1}^{(2,d)},\qquad k=n\\

(B.17)

Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{1+dn/2}C^{(2,d)}_{2j-1,\ell(n+1,k)}P_{j,0}^{(2,d)},\qquad k=n+1\\

(B.18)

Y^{(2,d)}_{n+1,k}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{1+dn/2}C^{(2,d)}_{2j,\ell(n+1,k)}P_{j,1}^{(2,d)},\qquad k=n+2.

Note that in (
B.14
), the row indices of
C^{(2,d)}_{j,\ell(n+1,k)}
range from
2n+1
to
dn-1
, and hence the statements in (
B.1
) and (
B.2
) that
C^{(2,d)}_{j,\ell(n+1,k)}=0
for
j<2n+1
and
j>dn-1
. Similarly, (
B.3
)–(
B.6
) follow from, respectively, (
B.15
)–(
B.18
).

The proof that
C^{(2,d)}_{j,\ell(n+1,k)}=0
for the remaining cases is similar.
∎
