Highly-mass-loaded hot galactic winds are unstable to cool filament formation 2023 Highly-mass-loaded hot galactic winds are unstable to cool filament formation – B
Dustin D. Nguyen, Todd A. Thompson, Evan E. Schneider, & Ashley P. Tarrant

Abstract
When cool clouds are ram-pressure accelerated by a hot supersonic galactic wind, some of the clouds may be shredded by hydrodynamical instabilities and incorporated into the hot flow. Recent one-dimensional steady-state calculations show how cool cloud entrainment directly affects the bulk thermodynamics, kinematics, and observational characteristics of the hot gas. In particular, mass-loading decelerates the hot flow and changes its entropy. Here, we investigate the stability of planar and spherical mass-loaded hot supersonic flows using both perturbation analysis and three-dimensional time-dependent radiative hydrodynamical simulations. We show that mass-loading is stable over a broad range of parameters and that the 1D time-steady analytic solutions exactly reproduce the 3D time-dependent calculations, provided that the flow does not decelerate sufficiently to become subsonic. For higher values of the mass-loading, the flow develops a sonic point and becomes thermally unstable, rapidly cooling and forming elongated dense cometary filaments. We explore the mass-loading parameters required to reach a sonic point and the radiative formation of these filaments. For certain approximations, we can derive simple analytic criteria. In general a mass-loading rate similar to the initial mass outflow rate is required. In this sense, the destruction of small cool clouds by a hot flow may ultimately spontaneously generate fast cool filaments, as observed in starburst superwinds. Lastly, we find that the kinematics of filaments is sensitive to the slope of the mass-loading function. Filaments move faster than the surrounding wind if mass-loading is over long distances whereas filaments move slower than their surroundings if mass-loading is abrupt.



1
Introduction

Galactic outflows are a critical component of galaxy evolution. Winds are commonly found in star-forming galaxies at both low- and high-redshift
(
Martin 1998
;
Pettini
et al. 2001
;
Rubin et al. 2010
;
Heckman et al. 2015
;
Heckman &
Borthakur 2016
)
. They modulate star formation
(
Larson 1974
;
Heckman
et al. 1990
)
, shape the stellar mass and mass-metallicity relations
(
Dekel &
Silk 1986
;
Finlator &
Davé 2008
;
Peeples &
Shankar 2011
)
, and advect metals into the circumgalactic and intergalactic medium
(
Aguirre et al. 2001
;
Scannapieco et al. 2002
;
Tremonti
et al. 2004
;
Oppenheimer &
Davé 2006
;
Oppenheimer &
Davé 2008
)
. Evidence for high velocity cool neutral and ionized gas is observed in local starburst galaxies and high-redshift star-forming galaxies in both emission and absorption (e.g.,
Veilleux et al. 2005
;
Heckman &
Thompson 2017
and references therein).

A possible explanation for the origin of high velocity cool material seen in observations is that cool clouds are ram-pressure accelerated by a hot wind. However, low-column density cool clouds immersed in a hot supersonic wind will be shredded by hydrodynamic instabilities and mixed into the hot outflow before they can be accelerated (see
Zhang et al. 2017
for a review). The acceleration time,
t_{\mathrm{drag}}
, can be written in terms of destruction time (the “cloud crushing" timescale),
t_{\mathrm{cc}}
, as
t_{\mathrm{drag}}\sim\sqrt{\chi}\,t_{\mathrm{cc}}
, where
\chi\gg 1
is the relative over-density of the cool cloud relative to the hot wind, implying that cool clouds should be destroyed before they are accelerated
(
Klein
et al. 1994
)
. While this result has been verified in a wide range of simulations
(
Klein
et al. 1994
;
Cooper et al. 2009
;
Scannapieco
& Brüggen 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Schneider &
Robertson 2017
;
Li
et al. 2020
)
, recent work shows that for sufficiently high column densities clouds can survive and even grow through mixing at the wind-cloud interface
(
Gronke &
Oh 2018
;
Gronke &
Oh 2020
)
.

The destruction and entrainment of low column density cool clouds into the hot superwind fluid has direct consequences for the bulk thermodynamics, kinematics, and X-ray emissivity of the hot wind
(
Suchkov et al. 1996
;
Smith 1996
;
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
;
Fielding &
Bryan 2022
)
. Mass-loading (
\dot{\mu}\,\mathrm{[M_{\odot}\,yr^{-1}\,kpc^{-3}]})
increases both the hot gas temperature and its density, while decelerating the flow. Further, mass-loading increases the hot gas entropy if the gas Mach number is above a critical value of
\mathcal{M}=\sqrt{(11+\sqrt{(}46))/5}\sim 1.9
(see
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
) and decreases the entropy otherwise. Sufficiently strong mass-loading can decelerate a supersonic flow sufficiently to produce a sonic point (see below).

A critical question is whether the 1D steady-state models are stable to mass-loading, even in the idealized cases where mass-loading is treated as a simple volumetric source of cool matter.
Smith 1996
suggested a smooth transition from super- to sub-sonic flow is not possible and that a shock is expected to develop, which could potentially cause a breakdown of the time-steady solutions.
Shchekinov 1996
also argues that flows will become unstable near the sonic point, and that the instability growth rate may scale as the Mach number squared.

Here, we provide a set of analytic investigations and numerical tests meant to illuminate the stability of mass-loaded hot outflows. This is an important step in critically assessing 1D steady-state models that purport to explain aspects of the hot gas profiles
(
Suchkov et al. 1996
;
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
)
and 1D steady-state models that explicitly couple a distribution of cool clouds (with acceleration, deceleration, and growth) to the hot gas dynamics
(
Cowie
et al. 1981
;
Fielding &
Bryan 2022
)
. We wish to answer the question of whether or not mass-loaded hot flows of the type typically invoked to explain the hot gas in M82 and other local starbursts are stable or unstable, and to understand how the bulk dynamics are effected, with direct relevance for observations.

In
§ 2
, we study the linear stability of mass-loaded flows. In
§ 3
, we quantify how much mass-loading is required to decelerate a steady supersonic wind through a sonic point. In
§ 4
, we make a direct comparison between our semi-analytic 1D models and 3D hydrodynamical simulations and find that mass-loaded flows are stable provided they do not cross a sonic point. For high enough mass-loading, we find flows become thermally unstable and form dense filaments. We then discuss implications for galactic winds and multi-phase microphysics in
§ 5
. Lastly we summarize in
§ 6
and highlight future directions.

2
linear stability of mass-loaded flows

Previous works have used steady-state models to quantify how mass entrainment impacts hot outflows
(
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
;
Fielding &
Bryan 2022
)
. Here, we consider the stability of mass-loaded winds for planar
(\hat{x})
, spherical
(\hat{r})
, and arbitrary area
A(x)
flow tubes (
\hat{x}
). Throughout the analytics below, we will often take the high Mach number limit of a solution. We consider mass-loading to occur after the supersonic flow reaches its asymptotic bulk velocity, which occurs at around
\mathcal{M}\gtrsim 2
for a launched spherical wind. The physical picture we have in mind is illustrated in Figure
1
.

2.1
Planar Mass-loaded Flow

For a one-dimensional planar flow with mass-loading (
\dot{\mu}
in g cm
-3
s
-1
), the hydrodynamic equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\rho v)=\dot{\mu},

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}-\frac{v\dot{\mu}}{\rho},

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}=\frac{\dot{\mu}P}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)\frac{\rho v^{2}}{P}-\gamma\bigg)+\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}\bigg).

(3)

The steady-state equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\rho_{0}v_{0})=\dot{\mu},

(4)

\displaystyle v_{0}\frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial x}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial P_{0}}{\partial x}-\frac{v_{0}\dot{\mu}}{\rho_{0}},

(5)

\displaystyle v_{0}\frac{\partial P_{0}}{\partial x}=\frac{\dot{\mu}P_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)\frac{\rho_{0}v_{0}^{2}}{P_{0}}-\gamma\bigg)+\frac{\gamma v_{0}P_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial x}.

(6)

We test the linear stability of the planar flow using perturbation analysis. Our volumetric mass-loading rate is a function of distance, so only the velocity, density, and pressure are perturbed as

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=v(x)+\delta v\exp[-i(\omega t-kx)],

(7)

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)

\displaystyle=\rho(x)+\delta\rho\exp[-i(\omega t-kx)],

\displaystyle P(x,t)

\displaystyle=P(x)+\delta P\exp[-i(\omega t-kx)].

Substituting the above perturbations into Equations
1
,
2
, and
3
, we derive a dispersion relation for
\omega
(see Appendix
A
for full dispersion relation).
The system is unstable if
\omega
is positive and complex, and stable otherwise. Only terms linear in the perturbations
\delta v
,
\delta\rho
, and
\delta P
are kept. In the high wave number limit (
k\rightarrow\infty
) the dispersion relation is

\displaystyle k(1-\mathcal{M}^{2})^{2}v\rho^{2}+i\mathcal{M}^{2}(1+\gamma)(1-\gamma\mathcal{M}^{2})\dot{\mu}\rho/2

(8)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-(1-3\mathcal{M}^{2}(1-\mathcal{M}^{2})\rho^{2}\omega=0.

This high-
k
limit of the dispersion relation is linear in
\omega
. The imaginary component of the frequency (i.e., growth rate),
\omega_{\mathrm{load}}
, is

\omega_{\mathrm{load}}=\frac{(1+\gamma)}{2}\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}\frac{(\gamma\mathcal{M}^{4}+\mathcal{M}^{2})}{(3\mathcal{M}^{4}-4\mathcal{M}^{2}+1)},

(9)

which has poles at

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}=1/\sqrt{3}\ \mathrm{and}\ 1.

(10)

The critical points
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}
correspond to locations where instability growth and damping rates peak and is independent of
\gamma
. In Figure
2
, we plot
\omega_{\mathrm{load}}/(\dot{\mu}/\rho)
as a function of Mach number (black solid line). We see that the instability grows as the flow is decelerated through the sonic point from
\mathcal{M}>1
. However, immediately after transitioning the sonic point, the growth rate is negative indicating damping. For
\gamma=5/3
, in the highly supersonic limit
\mathcal{M}\gg 1
, the growth rate is

\omega_{\mathrm{load}}\simeq\frac{20}{27}\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}\quad(\mathcal{M}\gg 1).

(11)

Equation
11
disagrees with the growth rate derived by
Shchekinov 1996
, who found that
\omega_{\mathrm{load}}\propto\mathcal{M}^{2}
for
\mathcal{M}\gg 1
. However, in their study the volumetric mass-loading rate was a function of temperature
\dot{\mu}=\dot{\mu}(T)
which differs from our definition of mass-loading
\dot{\mu}=\dot{\mu}(r)
. For their variable
\alpha=\partial\log(\dot{\mu})/\log(T)=0
, our governing hydrodynamic equations are identical. In Appendix
B
, we discuss possible reasons for the discrepancy.

Figure 2:
The instability growth rate (Eq.
9
) divided by the mass-loading rate
\dot{\mu}/\rho
. The instability grows when
\omega_{\mathrm{load}}/\dot{\mu}/\rho>0
and is damped when
\omega_{\mathrm{load}}/\dot{\mu}/\rho<0
. When being decelerated through critical point
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}=1
, the flow is shortly unstable, but is then becomes stabilized when is in the subsonic regime. Similarly, when being accelerated through
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}=1/\sqrt{3}\simeq 0.57
, the flow is unstable, but then becomes stable. The derived growth rates indicate that flows that pass through critical points
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}=1\,\mathrm{and}\,1/\sqrt{3}
are unstable.

2.2
Spherical Mass-loaded Flow

For a spherical flow, the hydrodynamic equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial r^{2}}(\rho vr^{2})=\dot{\mu},

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}-\frac{v\dot{\mu}}{\rho},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial P}{\partial r}=\frac{\dot{\mu}P}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)\frac{\rho v^{2}}{P}-\gamma\bigg)+\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial r}\bigg).

(14)

\omega_{\mathrm{load}}=\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}\frac{(\gamma+1)}{2}\frac{\gamma\mathcal{M}^{4}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{3\mathcal{M}^{4}-4\mathcal{M}^{2}+1}-\frac{v}{r}\frac{[2(\gamma+1)\mathcal{M}^{4}-2\mathcal{M}^{2}+2]}{3\mathcal{M}^{4}-4\mathcal{M}^{2}+1}.

(15)

The derived growth carries the same frequency derived in the planar case (Eq.
9
), however there is an additional damping term that arises from spherical expansion, as expected. As in
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
, we will consider mass-loading outside of the wind-driving region. In the high Mach number limit the growth rate is

\omega_{\mathrm{load}}\simeq\frac{20}{27}\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}-\frac{16}{9}\frac{v}{r}\quad(\mathcal{M}\gg 1)

(16)

2.3
Non-spherical Mass-loaded Flow

For a time-steady one-dimensional flow undergoing expansion into area element
A(x)
, the hydrodynamic equations are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(A\rho_{0}v_{0})=\dot{\mu},

(17)

\displaystyle v_{0}\frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial x}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial P_{0}}{\partial x}-\frac{v_{0}\dot{\mu}}{\rho_{0}},

(18)

\displaystyle v_{0}\frac{\partial P_{0}}{\partial x}=\frac{\dot{\mu}P_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)\frac{\rho_{0}v_{0}^{2}}{P_{0}}-\gamma\bigg)+\frac{\gamma v_{0}P_{0}}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial x}.

(19)

Following the same procedures as described above in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, the derived growth rate in the high-
k
limit is

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{load}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}\frac{(\gamma+1)}{2}\frac{\gamma\mathcal{M}^{4}+\mathcal{M}^{2}}{3\mathcal{M}^{4}-4\mathcal{M}^{2}+1}\noindent

(20)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2v\frac{d\ln A}{dx}\frac{[2(\gamma+1)\mathcal{M}^{4}-2\mathcal{M}^{2}+2]}{3\mathcal{M}^{4}-4\mathcal{M}^{2}+1}.

(21)

For
A\propto x^{0}
, we recover the growth rate of the planar flow (Eq.
9
) and when
x\rightarrow r
and
A\propto r^{2}
, we recover the growth rate of the spherical flow (Eq.
15
). In the high Mach number limit the growth rate is

\omega_{\mathrm{load}}\simeq\frac{20}{27}\frac{\dot{\mu}}{\rho}-\frac{32}{9}v\frac{d\ln A}{dx}.

(22)

3
Required Mass-loading For Transonic Transition

In the previous section we found that the instability growth rates peak at
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{crit}}=1\ \mathrm{and}\ 1/\sqrt{3}
. We now derive the amount of mass-loading required for a supersonic flow to be decelerated through the sonic point.
First, we expand the derivative of the Mach number
d\mathcal{M}/dr
as:

\frac{d\mathcal{M}}{dr}=\frac{1}{(\gamma P/\rho)^{1/2}}\frac{dv}{dr}+\frac{v}{(\gamma P/\rho)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{2\rho}\frac{d\rho}{dr}-\frac{v}{(\gamma P/\rho)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{2P}\frac{dP}{dr}.

(23)

From the steady-state equations (Eqs.
12
,
13
, and
14
, with
\partial/\partial t=0
), we solve the three equations for the derivatives
dv/dr
,
d\rho/dr
, and
dP/dr
and substitute them above so that Equation
23
simplifies to

\frac{d\mathcal{M}}{dr}=-\frac{\mathcal{M}(2+(\gamma-1)\mathcal{M}^{2})(r\dot{\mu}(1+\mathcal{M}^{2})-4v\rho)}{4(\mathcal{M}^{2}-1)rv\rho}.

(24)

Before any mass-loading, at any radius
r
, the mass-flow rate of the spherical adiabatic wind is

\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}=4\pi r^{2}\rho_{\mathrm{hot}}v_{\mathrm{hot}}=\mathrm{constant}.

(25)

For consistency with the above equation, we denote the Mach number of the adiabatic flow (before mass-loading) as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}
. We substitute these into Equation
23
and take
\gamma=5/3
so that

\frac{d\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}}{dr}=-\frac{2\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}^{2}+3)((5\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}^{2}+3)\pi r^{3}\dot{\mu}-3\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}})}{9(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}^{2}-1)r\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}}.

(26)

We require mass-loading to be instantaneous and to occur uniformly within an infinitesimally thin shell of volume
V_{\mathrm{shell,load}}=4\pi r^{2}\Delta r
such that the total entrained mass is given by the product of
V_{\mathrm{shell,load}}\times\dot{\mu}
. The total decrease in Mach number from
r\rightarrow r+\Delta r
is then

\frac{\Delta\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}}{\Delta r}=-\frac{(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}-1)}{\Delta r}.

(27)

We set Equation
26
equal to Equation
27
and assume the flow is highly supersonic before mass-loading. We find the minimum mass-loading required to decelerate the flow through the sonic point to be

\dot{M}_{\mathrm{load,min}}=\frac{18}{5}\frac{\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}^{2}}\quad(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}\gg 1).

(28)

Equation
28
shows that the minimum mass-loading criterion decreases with Mach number. We note that this is the Mach number of the supersonic flow before mass-loading. The relevant radial scalings for an adiabatic supersonic spherical wind are
\rho_{\mathrm{hot}}\propto r^{-2}
,
v_{\mathrm{hot}}\propto r^{0}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}\propto r^{2/3}
. This means
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}\propto\rho_{\mathrm{hot}}^{-1/3}
. Restated again for clarity: higher Mach number flows for a time-steady spherical
adiabatic
wind do not imply higher bulk velocities, it merely means the flow is cooler and less dense. From momentum conservation, for fixed velocity, we would expect denser winds to require more mass-loading for equivalent deceleration. From Equation
28
, this appears to be the case as
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{load,min}}\propto\rho_{\mathrm{hot}}^{2/3}
.

Uniform feedback launches a wind with a sonic point at the edge of the starburst volume
(
Chevalier &
Clegg 1985
)
. After leaving this boundary, the flow continues to accelerate due to the steep pressure gradient up until approximately
\mathcal{M}\sim 2
(e.g., see Fig. 4 of
Nguyen et al. 2023
). For an adiabatic spherical wind with
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}=2
,

\dot{M}_{\mathrm{load,min}}\sim\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}\quad(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}=2)

(29)

which is approximately the injected mass within the wind-driving region (see Eq.
31
).

4
Simulations

In this section we describe our numerical experiments exploring the time-dependent stability of mass-loaded winds. In Section
4.1
, we first consider the stability of supersonic spherical mass-loaded flow solutions obtained from the steady-state equations by
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
. These have relatively low mass-loading in the supersonic region and we find them to be stable, as expected from the perturbation analysis. In Section
4.2
, we simulate more heavily mass-loaded flows that are sufficiently decelerated to pass through a sonic point. These solutions become radiative and undergo an instability that produces dense filaments, as sketched in Figure
1
.

In all of the simulations presented, winds are launched via SN feedback as described by
Chevalier &
Clegg 1985
(CC85). Within the starburst volume,
R
, energy and mass are uniformly deposited with rates

\dot{E}_{\mathrm{hot}}=3.1\times\alpha\times 10^{41}\times\dot{M}_{\mathrm{SFR,*}}\,\mathrm{[ergs\,s^{-1}]},

(30)

and

\dot{M}_{\mathrm{hot}}=\beta\times\dot{M}_{\mathrm{SFR}},

(31)

where
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{SFR,*}}=\dot{M}_{\mathrm{SFR}}/\mathrm{M_{\odot}\,yr^{-1}}
,
\dot{M}_{\mathrm{SFR}}\,[M_{\odot}\,yr^{-1}]
is the star-formation rate, and
\alpha
and
\beta
are the dimensionless SNe energy and mass-loading efficiencies, respectively, and we have assumed that there is one supernova per 100
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
of star formation and that each supernova releases
10^{51}\,
ergs. Beyond the injection radius
R
, the flow is supersonic
(
Chevalier &
Clegg 1985
)
. At a specified distance away from the wind-driving region
R_{\mathrm{load}}>R
, we mass-load the flow by sub-grid injection of mass, meant to represent the entrainment of cool low column density clouds into the hot supersonic flow
(
Cowie
et al. 1981
;
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
)
. We use a single power-law to parameterize the mass-loading:

\dot{\mu}=\dot{\mu}_{0}\times(\lambda/r)^{\Delta}\quad(R_{\mathrm{load}}>R),

(32)

where
\lambda\,\mathrm{[kpc]}
is an arbitrary length scale,
\Delta
is the slope of the mass-loading power-law, and
\dot{\mu}_{0}\,\mathrm{[M_{\odot}\,kpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}]}
is a constant. The power-law mass-loading is physically motivated by the
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
fit to the CGOLS IV simulation
(
Schneider et al. 2020
)
. Note that the reader should not confuse mass-loading of the type we explore here (Eq.
32
), with the mass-loading that occurs within the central driving region (
r<R
) that produces the hot outflow (Eq.
31
). We adopt the cooling curve given by
Schneider et al. 2018
, which is an analytic fit to a solar-metallicity collisional ionization equilibrium curve calculated with Cloudy
(
Ferland
et al. 2013
)
. This cooling curve assumes a temperature floor of
10^{4}
K to account for heating and photoionization from UV sources. Throughout the paper we take
\mu=0.6
for a plasma with solar metallicity.

5
Discussion

5.1
Observational implications

Thompson et al. 2016
postulated that the rapid destruction of cool clouds near the wind-driving region would lead to mass-loading of the hot phase and then a radiative instability that would lead to the reformation of cool wind material at a larger scale, in a process they termed “cool cloud transmigration." Here, we show that the mass-loading of cool gas into the hot flow combined with radiative cooling produces an instability identified in Section
4.2
. By forcing the flow through a sonic point, we show that mass-loading provides a mechanism for the cool gas to spontaneously form dense filaments and produce distributions of velocity, density, and column density (Fig.
10
) that differ markedly from the expectations of a spherical adiabatic outflow or from models that accelerate cool clouds via ram pressure or other mechanisms. This model produces dense filaments of cool material that should produce strong recombination emission (Fig.
11
), possibly explaining the prominent fast H
\alpha
-emitting filaments in the galactic outflows of ultra-luminous infrared galaxies
(
Soto et al. 2012
;
Martin et al. 2015
)
, nearby starbursts such as M82
(
Shopbell &
Bland-Hawthorn 1998
;
Lehnert
et al. 1999
;
Strickland et al. 2000
;
Strickland et al. 2004
)
and NGC 253
(
Arnaboldi et al. 1995
;
Rodríguez-Rico
et al. 2006
)
, and high-redshift star-forming galaxies
(
Shapiro
et al. 2009
;
Newman
et al. 2012
)
.

As we show in Figure
11
, when we project the emission along the line of sight, the dense filaments are prominent, with kinematics of order several hundred km s (see Figs.
6
and
12
). These velocities are qualitatively similar to the kinematics of M82’s H
\alpha
filaments (
v\sim 500-650\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
) observed by
Shopbell &
Bland-Hawthorn 1998
, who additionally find a roughly constant velocity gradient. The 1D skewers of the filaments (Fig.
15
) indicate that the filaments are also moving at roughly constant velocity for the power-law mass-loading model (see Fig.
15
, Section
Appendix A: Skewers
). Although we have carried out only a small parameter study, we find that the kinematics of the filaments depends on the spatial distribution of the mass-loading (see Fig.
10
). In other wind models, predictions for the velocity gradient
dv/dr
for the cool material are determined by other physics: e.g., ram pressure acceleration of the hot gas on the cool clouds
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Fielding et al. 2020
)
, momentum and energy exchange from between the hot and cool fluids
(
Gronke &
Oh 2020
;
Schneider et al. 2020
;
Fielding &
Bryan 2022
)
, cosmic ray acceleration
(
Ipavich 1975
;
Farber et al. 2018
;
Quataert
et al. 2022b
;
Quataert
et al. 2022a
)
, and/or radiation pressure
(
Thompson et al. 2015
;
Blackstone &
Thompson 2023
;
Menon
et al. 2023
)
. As we show in Figure
10
, despite equivalent total mass-deposition, the spatial dependency of the mass-loading function impacts filament kinematics in a non-linear manner that requires future investigation.

5.2
Stability for central vs. distributed mass-loading

The fate of a large-scale hot wind is determined by the amount of cool material is entrained.
Wang 1995
;
Silich et al. 2004
;
Thompson et al. 2016
showed that if the mass-loading in the central wind-driving region is high enough, the flow rapidly cools at
r_{\mathrm{cool}}
down to
10^{4}\,K
. This picture is qualitatively similar to cool cloud entrainment
outside
of the wind-driving region that we consider throughout this work and in
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
, however there are some important differences. First, the central mass-loading considered by
Wang 1995
;
Suchkov et al. 1996
;
Silich et al. 2004
;
Thompson et al. 2016
occurs within the wind-driving region, implying the added mass has a significant energy content and that the initial flow being mass-loaded is subsonic.
Smith 1996
found that mass-loading with additional energy injection generically leads to a stable rising Mach number solution (like that in CC85). This may be why galactic winds that undergo rapid cooling due to heavy central mass-loading were found to be stable
(
Schneider et al. 2018
)
. In this work we showed that mass-loading with no energy content can lead to instability, in agreement with the prediction of
Smith 1996
.

5.3
Implications for mixing on smaller scales

The process of mass-loading is prevalent in the microphysical interactions within multi-phase media. Recent works show that there are regions of parameter space where cool clouds can grow from a mixing cooling cycle in wind tunnel configurations
(
Gronke &
Oh 2018
;
Sparre et al. 2019
;
Sparre
et al. 2020
;
Gronke &
Oh 2020
)
, in turbulent backgrounds from coagulation
(
Gronke &
Oh 2022
)
, in tails of ram-pressure stripped galaxies
(
Tonnesen &
Bryan 2021
)
, during gravitational infall
(
Tan
et al. 2023
)
, and combinations of all of the above
(see
Faucher-Giguere
& Oh 2023
, for a review)
. Mixing occurs at the interface between the hot wind and cool cloud, where, if the cooling-efficiency at the turbulent mixing layer is sufficiently large, then mixed ‘warm’ gas can contribute to new co-moving cool gas
(
Begelman &
Fabian 1990
;
Fielding et al. 2020
;
Tan
et al. 2021
;
Abruzzo
et al. 2022
)
. Critical to this process is the transfer of momentum from the hot phase to the cool phase, which may accelerate a cloud
(
Gronke &
Oh 2020
;
Schneider et al. 2020
)
or decelerate an in-falling cloud
(
Tan
et al. 2023
)
. In the former case, the transfer of momentum requires the rapid deceleration of hot supersonic material as it mixes with the surface layer of the static cool cloud. In this work, we show that strong deceleration of the hot phase through the sonic point may promote thermal instability and formation into filaments, perhaps being an important piece of physics in understanding how hot gas mixes, cools, and condensates onto clouds on the microphysical scale.

5.4
Caveats

The simulations presented here are idealized configurations meant to explore solely the stability of mass-loaded flows. In doing so, we have neglected physics that is present in realistic galactic environments. In particular, we assume a single cooling function fit to solar composition gas that collisional ionization equilibrium, which may not be true for extended low density outflows
(
Gray
et al. 2019a
;
Gray et al. 2019b
;
Sarkar
et al. 2022
)
. We do not include conduction, which may dominate energy transport in the winds before they are mass-loaded
(
Thompson et al. 2016
)
. Furthermore, in our treatment of background UV heating, our cooling curve truncates at
T_{\mathrm{floor}}=10^{4}\,\mathrm{K}
, but the scale of cold filament formation and dynamics of hot gas changes if the gas is allowed to cool to
10\,\mathrm{K}

(
Tanner
et al. 2016
)
. This effect may have implications for the dynamics and physical size of the filaments we find here. We do not resolve the shattering scale-length
l_{\mathrm{shatter}}

(
McCourt et al. 2018
)
in any of our simulations (See Fig.
13
), implying our simulations do not include any shattering-like behavior which may be important in setting the physical size of filaments. We have assumed that the thermal and kinetic energy of the swept up cool clouds to be zero which may have implications for flow stability
(
Smith 1996
)
. Additionally, the
R_{\mathrm{load}}
we chose is more than twice the starburst radius
R
, however
Schneider et al. 2020
found mass-loading to be quite close to the wind-driving region. Moving the loading radius inwards closer to
R
implies the hot flow will be closer to the sonic point (before mass-loading), which may have implications for filament formation. Furthermore, the dynamics of the cool flows and filaments shown here may be different if the cooling length is resolved
(
Rey et al. 2023
)
. Lastly, the kinematics of the filaments are expected to be different in the presence of a gravitational potential, which we neglect here.

6
Synthesis

Mass-loading is expected to occur on a wide range of scales from hot galactic superwinds entraining clouds, to the microphysical intricacies of multi-phase mixing. Here, we considered the stability of supersonic hot galactic winds that undergo mass-loading. Our main findings are:

1.

The steady-state assumptions of mass-loaded 1D models in previous works
(
Cowie
et al. 1981
;
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
;
Fielding &
Bryan 2022
)
are valid provided the flows are not decelerated through the sonic point (Fig.
3
), in agreement with our linear stability analysis (Sec.
2
).

2.

Mass-loaded flows are unstable as they pass through critical points
\mathcal{M}=1
and
1/\sqrt{3}
(Fig.
2
). When the flow enters the subsonic regime, it is re-accelerated back to supersonic speeds through a saddle-point like transition
Williams &
Dyson 1994
. The flow leads to dense filament formation as the flow subsequently undergoes rapid bulk cooling (Fig.
5
). To our knowledge, this instability has not been seen in previous CC85-like wind calculations with radiative cooling, although it may be implicit in simulations like
Cooper et al. 2009
and
Schneider et al. 2020
where there is a complex interaction between hot and cooler phases. We explicitly show cooling is required for non-linear filament formation (Fig.
6
).

3.

Filaments are expected to produce recombination radiation. If the radiation is optically thin, the filaments projected along the line of sight are structurally coherent, and are qualitatively similar to the H
\alpha
filaments observed in the galactic winds of starburst M82 (Figs.
11
and
12
).

4.

The kinematics of the filaments depend on the spatial distribution of mass-loading, even if the total mass entrained into the hot wind is equivalent (Fig.
10
). Abrupt mass-loading leads to filaments that move slower than the surrounding wind. Distributed mass-loading over large scales, leads to filaments that move faster than the surrounding wind. We used a power-law to describe distributed mass-loading, which was also similarly used in fitting mass-loading of the hot wind
(
Nguyen &
Thompson 2021
)
for the CGOLS IV simulation
(
Schneider et al. 2020
)
.

5.

The minimum mass-loading required to decelerate a supersonic flow down to the sonic point is of order the injected mass from SNe (Eq.
28
), but depends on the spatial extent over which mass-loading occurs. This implies that if hot supersonic flows sweep up enough cool clouds, it would ultimately result in the production of fast cool clouds. We verify this criterion with 3D hydrodynamic simulations (Fig.
7
).

Appendix B
