Signatures of Cooper pair dynamics and quantum-critical superconductivity in tunable carrier bands
Zhiyu Dong, Patrick A. Lee, Leonid S. Levitov

Abstract
Different superconducting pairing mechanisms are markedly distinct in the underlying Cooper pair kinematics. Pairing interactions mediated by quantum-critical soft modes are dominated by highly collinear processes, falling into two classes: forward scattering and backscattering. In contrast, phonon mechanisms have a generic non-collinear character. We show that the type of kinematics can be identified by examining the evolution of superconductivity when tuning the Fermi surface geometry. We illustrate our approach using recently measured phase diagrams of various graphene systems. Our analysis unambiguously connects the emergence of superconductivity at “ghost crossings” of Fermi surfaces in distinct valleys to the pair kinematics of a backscattering type. Together with the observed non-monotonic behavior of superconductivity near its onset (sharp rise followed by a drop), it provides strong support for a particular quantum-critical superconductivity scenario. These findings conclusively settle the long-standing debate on the origin of superconductivity in this system and demonstrate the essential role of quantum-critical modes in superconducting pairing. Moreover, our work highlights the potential of tuning bands via ghost crossings as a promising means of boosting superconductivity.

Zhou et al. 2022
do not provide sufficient information to extract the ratio between interlayer bias
u
and displacement field
D
. However they do give useful upper and lower bounds for
u/D
.

Namely, quantum oscillation measurements
Zhou et al. 2022
reveal that: 1) the isospin-unpolarized Sym
4
phase below the isospin-polarized PIP
2
phase (shown in Fig.
2
b) has a single Fermi surface per isospin; 2) the isospin-unpolarized Sym
12
phase above PIP
2
features three distinct pockets per isospin.
We find that in order to reproduce these two observations, the value
u/D
should fall in the range:
0.057<\frac{u}{eD\cdot\rm{nm}}<0.072
. Accordingly, as a best guess, we pick a value in the middle of this window,
\frac{u}{eD\cdot\rm{nm}}=0.065
.
With this value, we derive the transition line between overlapping and non-overlapping Fermi seas, indicated as the yellow line in Fig.
2
, which closely matches the emergence point of the SC phase.

It is worth noting that several experiments have attempted to measure the
u/D
ratio for BBG, yielding vastly different values that do not fall within the range inferred from our fermiology analysis. Namely, Refs.
Zhang et al. 2009
and
Zhang et al. 2022a
find
\frac{u}{eD\cdot\rm{nm}}=0.1
and
0.033
, respectively.
Due to significant variations in values, likely due to electrostatic differences in devices, we refrained from using them directly. Instead, we selected
u/D
as described above to predict fermiology that best matches the measurements.

To restate the main result of our analysis,
in both BBG and in BBG/WSe
2
the lines that mark the emergence of ghost Fermi surface crossings match perfectly the points of the onset of superconductivity. As discussed above, the emergence of valley crossings strongly impacts the pair scattering kinematics, favoring backscattering. To the contrary, it has little impact on the density of states at the Fermi level or the e-e interaction strength. Therefore the observed behavior is difficult to understand within a conventional BCS superconductivity framework but is naturally explained by the IVC superconductivity mechanism.

This conclusion is further supported by the nonmonotonic behavior of superconductivity near the onset (a rise followed by a drop) observed in BBG/WSe
2
. This observation is explained by the enhancement of pairing at small angles
\theta
between Fermi surfaces at the ghost crossing discussed above. It is interesting to compare the nonmonotonic behavior of superconductivity in BBG/WSe
2
with the monotonic behavior observed at superconductivity onset in BBG/hBN samples.
We believe that this difference can be attributed to the constraints imposed by isospin orders and Fermiology. Specifically, the SC phase in BBG/hBN must lie outside the PIP
2
phase which is not necessarily compatible with the SC order, and below the boundary where valley crossings occur (yellow line in Fig.
2
). These constraints limit the SC phase to a narrow wedge in the phase diagram (Fig.
2
), preventing the enhancement of the SC phase at the onset of valley crossings. In comparison, in the BBG/WSe
2
these constraints are lifted. Since the “vanilla” phase lies below the phase boundary, the onset of valley crossings (solid yellow line in Fig.
3
) extends downwards and therefore does not constrain the SC phase.

Lastly, we believe that the IVC pairing revealed by our analysis is generally applicable to other observed SC phases, such as the SC
2
phase in BBG/WSe
2
. Here a conclusive analysis would require more knowledge of the isospin phase diagram, which is currently being investigated by several groups
Xie and Sarma 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
. Nonetheless, the SC
2
phase, which is a wedge embedded between different isospin orders, shows an abrupt onset which is likely related to a ghost valley crossing (see Fig.
3
).

In conclusion, the sudden appearance of SC phases coincides with the appearance of the
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
-
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
ghost Fermi surface crossings in both BBG and WSe
2
-supported BBG. This behavior suggests that quantum-critical fluctuations drive the pairing in both systems, favoring a backscattering-type pairing interaction due to the IVC order as the glue for superconductivity over other candidates like valley-polarization order
Dong et al. 2022
.
Overall, it is not compatible with conventional phonon mechanisms
Chou et al. 2021
;
Chou et al. 2022
, nor with the conventional Kohn-Luttinger mechanisms
Cea et al. 2022
;
Cea 2023
, pointing to a mechanism that involves a soft quantum-critical mode as a pairing glue.
Last but not least, it highlights tuning bands through ghost crossings as an attractive pathway to enhance superconductivity.

Model

In this section we provide details on the model used in the main text. Our single-particle Hamiltonian is identical to that in Ref.
McCann and Koshino 2013
whereas the interaction model is same as that in Ref.
Dong et al. 2022

The electron single-particle Hamiltonian for BBG is a
4\times 4
matrix expressed in the basis of
\left\{c_{A,1}^{\eta,s},c_{B,1}^{\eta,s},c_{A,2}^{\eta,s},c_{B,2}^{\eta,s}\right\}
(A and B are sublattice indices, 1 and 2 are the layer indices,
\eta=\pm 1
labels valley
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
, and

is the spin index)
McCann and Koshino 2013
:

H_{\rm BBG}=\left(\begin{matrix}u/2&v\pi^{\dagger}&-v_{4}\pi^{\dagger}&v_{3}\pi\\

-v_{4}\pi&t_{1}&-u/2+\Delta^{\prime}&v\pi^{\dagger}\\
v_{3}\pi^{\dagger}&-v_{4}\pi&v\pi&-u/2\end{matrix}\right)

(7)

where
\pi=\hbar\left(\eta k_{x}+ik_{y}\right)
,
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
are the
x
and

components of momentum measured from
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
or
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
. This Hamiltonian gives a trigonally-warped conduction band. In the isospin-polarized phase on which the SC emerges, quantum oscillations measurement shows that the Fermi sea in each isospin consists of three pockets, as shown in panels (ii) and (iii) of Fig.

c) in main text.

The electron-electron interaction in BBG is modeled as an intervalley local interaction

H_{int}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}U\sum_{l,\alpha,q,\tau,s}:\rho^{(0)}_{q,\alpha,l,\tau,s}\rho^{(0)}_{-q,\alpha^{\prime},l^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},s^{\prime}}:

where

. In the analysis below we project
H_{int}^{(0)}
onto the conduction band which is the only band relevant for SC. In the regime of
D
much larger than all other elements in Eq.(
7
), the projected interaction Hamiltonian is approximately given by

H_{int}=\frac{1}{2}U\sum_{q,\tau\tau^{\prime}ss^{\prime}}:\rho_{q,\eta,s}\rho_{-q,\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}:,\quad

(9)

Here the quantities

Appendix B
The scattering vertex function near IVC instability

In this section, we consider the Cooper pair scattering vertex function mediated by the soft mode describing IVC order fluctuations in the normal state. This quantity is overall similar to that considered in Ref.
Chatterjee et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
. However, unlike in Ref.
Wang et al. 2021
which directly starts from a spin-valley-fermion model, here we derive it from microscopic model Eq.(
5
) and Eq.(
9
) explicitly. Our derivation below does not account for the intervalley exchange interaction considered in Ref.
Chatterjee et al. 2022
. As we will see, the frequency dependence of the Cooper pair scattering vertex function slightly differs from that considered in Ref.
Wang et al. 2021
;
Chatterjee et al. 2022
.

We start by defining the IVC order parameter as follows
Dong et al. 2021

O_{\rm IVC}=\sum_{s,{\boldsymbol{k}}}\langle\psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{k}},\eta,s}\tau_{x,\eta\eta^{\prime}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}},\eta^{\prime},s}\rangle

(10)

here
\tau_{x}
is the Pauli matrix in
x
direction. Near the onset of this order, the most divergent contribution to vertex function is dominated by the ladder diagram shown in main text Fig.
1
a), in which a Cooper pair at momenta
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}
is scattered to a pair at
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
. The scattering vertex given by this diagram is clearly a function of the total frequency
\omega+\omega^{\prime}
and the total momentum
{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and takes the following form:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\omega+\omega^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}=\frac{U}{1+U\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(\omega+\omega^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})},

(11)

\displaystyle\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(\nu,{\boldsymbol{q}})=\sum_{\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}}}G_{K}(\omega+\nu,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}})G_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}})

(12)

This interaction diverges for
\omega+\omega^{\prime}=0,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}=0
because the Stoner criterion in IVC channel is given by
Dong and Levitov 2021

1+U\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)\rightarrow 0

(13)

Distinct from the valley-polarization susceptibility which contains a frequency-dependent term singular in momentum
\frac{|\nu|}{|q|}
due to Landau damping, the intervalley particle-hole susceptibility does not contain such a term. This is because the inter-valley zero-frequency momentum-
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
particle-hole excitations have a measure-zero phase space. Namely, in our case, the
q=0
inter-valley particle-hole gap closes only at six points:
{\boldsymbol{k}}=\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
, (
i=1,2,3
). This is distinct from usual particle-hole excitations in Fermi liquid where the
q=0
particle-hole excitation is gapless for any
{\boldsymbol{k}}
on the Fermi surface.

To see this explicitly, we calculate the frequency and momentum dependence inter-valley particle-hole susceptibility below and find

\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,q)=\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)+\frac{3|\nu|}{4\pi v_{F}^{2}\sin\theta}+\alpha q^{2}.

(14)

Here
\alpha
is a parameter depending on band dispersion details.
\theta
is the angle between the fermi surface in valley
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
and that in valley
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
at the valley-crossing points. In the analysis below, we take
\sin\theta\sim O(1)
unless specified otherwise.

To see this explicitly, below we pause and derive the frequency and momentum dependence of IVC susceptibility Eq.(
14
). As we will see, the susceptibility does not contain a Landau damping term
|\nu|/|q|
in the denominator. This is quite different from the textbook spin-susceptibility (e.g., see Ref.
Coleman 2015
) or the valley-polarization susceptibility discussed in Ref.
Dong et al. 2022
where Landau damping term shows up.

\displaystyle\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,p}G_{{\boldsymbol{K}}}(\omega+\nu,{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}})G_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}})

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}},\omega}\frac{f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+}-f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-}}{\epsilon_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+}-\epsilon_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-}-i\nu},

(16)

where
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\pm}={\boldsymbol{k}}\pm\frac{{\boldsymbol{q}}}{2}\pm{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
. As a reminder,
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}=-{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
.

\displaystyle\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\frac{\left(f_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}-f_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right)\left(\epsilon_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}-\epsilon_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}+i\nu\right)}{(\epsilon_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}-\epsilon_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+k})^{2}+\nu^{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle=6\sum_{k}\frac{\left(f_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}-f_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right)\left(\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}+i\nu\right)}{\left(\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)^{2}+\nu^{2}},\quad\Delta{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}}

(18)

\displaystyle=-6\int_{-k_{F}}^{k_{F}}\frac{dk_{x}}{2\pi}\frac{2k_{x}\sin\theta}{2\pi}\frac{2v_{F}k_{x}\sin\theta}{\left(2v_{F}k_{x}\sin\theta\right)^{2}+\nu^{2}}.

(19)

Here
\theta
is the angle between the fermi surface in valley
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
and that in valley
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
at the valley-crossing points. To evaluate this integral properly, we calculate the difference between the dynamical polarization function
\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,0)
and the static polarization function
\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)
:

\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,0)-\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)=6\int_{-k_{F}}^{k_{F}}\frac{dk_{x}}{(2\pi)^{2}v_{F}}\frac{\nu^{2}}{\left(2v_{F}k_{x}\sin\theta\right)^{2}+\nu^{2}}=\frac{3|\nu|}{4\pi v_{F}^{2}\sin\theta}.

(20)

This result is the leading order frequency dependence in
\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,0)
.

Next, we determine the form of leading order momentum dependence from symmetry. We know from
C_{3}
rotation symmetry that the momentum-dependent part of intervalley particle-hole susceptibility
\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,{\boldsymbol{q}})
has to be a function of
q^{2}
. Therefore, for small
q
(
q\ll k_{F}
),

\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,{\boldsymbol{q}})=\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)+\alpha q^{2}.

(21)

Here
\alpha
is a parameter depending on band dispersion details. Then, accounting for both frequency dependence Eq.(
20
) and momentum dependence Eq.(
21
), we arrive at the expression of intervalley susceptibility
\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(i\nu,q)
given in Eq.(
14
).

With the results of IVC susceptibility Eq.(
14
) derived above, we proceed to derive the pairing interaction. Plugging Eq.(
14
) and Eq.(
13
) into Eq.(
11
) we find the pairing interaction
\Gamma_{kk^{\prime}}
that takes the following form given in main text Eq.(
1
)

\Gamma_{\omega{\boldsymbol{k}},\omega^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}=\frac{U}{\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|+l_{0}^{2}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})^{2}+\delta^{2}},

(22)

with the parameter
\kappa=\frac{3U}{4\pi v_{F}^{2}\sin\theta}
and the lengthscale
\l_{0}
is defined as
l_{0}=U\alpha
. For simplicity, here we take
l_{0}=1/k_{F}
since it is the only lengthscale in this problem. The quantity
\delta
describes the “distance” from the IVC quantum criticality, it is defined as
\delta=\sqrt{1+U\Pi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(0,0)}
. Eq.(
13
) indicates that
\delta\rightarrow 0
near the IVC instability.

This scattering amplitude has a positive sign, which is naively pair-breaking. However, unlike the VP-mediated interaction which is divergent at a small angle scattering
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{k}}
, the strength of this interaction is maximized for backward scattering
k^{\prime}=-k
since
\delta\rightarrow 0
. This indicates an attractive pairing interaction in the non-s-wave channel.

This scenario resembles the spin-fluctuation-mediated near a ferromagnetic quantum critical point
Fay and Appel 1980
;
Wang et al. 2001
, where spin fluctuation generates a repulsive backward scattering, leading to an effective pairing interaction (attraction) in the
p
-wave channel. However, there is some difference in our case, since enhanced backward scattering here is intravalley scattering
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}\rightarrow{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{p}}
, rather than
{\boldsymbol{p}}\rightarrow-{\boldsymbol{p}}
in usual metals. Since
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}
is not associated with
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{p}}
by symmetry operation (see discussion below), they cannot, in general, simultaneously get close to the Fermi surface. This suggests that such
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{p}}\rightarrow{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{p}}
scattering does not always contribute to pairing, except for some special momentum
p
. Below, we will discuss this in detail.

Appendix C
Symmetry analysis of the superconducting gap function

In this section, we describe the symmetry of the pairing gap function
\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{K}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
. This symmetry analysis, which is essentially the same as that in Ref.
Chatterjee et al. 2022
, is required for the classification of different pairing channels and for identifying the leading pairing channel.

To start, we describe the superconductivity in BBG using the following BCS Hamiltonian:

H_{BCS}=\left(\begin{matrix}\epsilon_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}&\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}\\
\overline{\Delta}_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}&-\epsilon_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}}\end{matrix}\right)

(23)

where
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
represents the anomalous pairing vertex. Here we have suppressed the physical spin indices
\uparrow
/
\downarrow
since we are focusing on the parallel spin pairing, i.e. either
\uparrow\uparrow
pair or
\downarrow\downarrow
pair (see main text). This Hamiltonian yields the following linearized self-consistency equation:

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=-\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{\Gamma_{\omega+\omega^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\epsilon^{2}_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}}

(24)

where we have accounted for the frequency and momentum dependence of the pairing vertex
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
since
\Gamma
has a strong frequency and momentum dependence. Below we look for the leading pairing channel through symmetry analysis.

To this end, we proceed as follows: we first identify the symmetries in biased Bernal bilayer graphene. Based on that, we can write down all symmetry-allowed pairing channels
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. Then identify channels that maximally benefit from the singularity of
\Gamma_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
. Finally, we work out the BCS pairing self-consistency equation in these (degenerate) channels and discuss the higher-order effects which select one channel from them.

The biased bilayer graphene has the following symmetries: a rotation
C_{3}
around an axis perpendicular to the graphene plane, a mirror reflection
\mathcal{M}_{y}
(a mirror lying in the
yz
-plane) that maps
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
to
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
. In addition, there is the time-reversal symmetry in the absence of the applied
B
field. In the presence of
B_{\parallel}
which couples only to spin, there is an orbital time-reversal symmetry left, which is denoted by
\mathcal{T}
. We note parenthetically that the inversion symmetry and the twofold rotation that swaps AB sites are both broken by the transverse field
D
.

To understand the symmetry of
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
, we need to understand the symmetry of the Hamiltonian in one valley. The
C_{3}
operator, which maps
\psi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}}\rightarrow\psi_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+C_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is preserved in each valley whereas the mirror symmetry
\mathcal{M}_{y}
and time reversal
\mathcal{T}
are both individually broken in each valley. However, the Hamiltonian is invariant under a combination of these two symmetry operators
\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}
. In sum, the symmetry group is given by:

G=\left\{1,C_{3},C_{3}^{2},\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T},C_{3}\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}C_{3}^{-1},C_{3}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}C_{3}\right\}

(25)

Therefore, the gap function
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
that respects the symmetry should obtain an overall phase under
C_{3}
and
\mathcal{M}_{x}\mathcal{T}
, so we can write:

\displaystyle\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(C_{3}k)=e^{i\phi_{1}}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}),\quad\phi_{1}=0,\pm\frac{2\pi}{3},

(26)

\displaystyle\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}{\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{i\phi_{2}}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}),\quad\phi_{2}=\pm\pi.

(27)

We find that there are six possibilities in total:
\phi_{1}
can take three values, whereas
\phi_{2}
can take two values.

Appendix D
The leading pairing channel

In this section, we argue that
p
-wave spin-triplet channel is the leading one in BBG based on symmetry classification. A similar result is obtained in Ref.
Chatterjee et al. 2022
through numerical calculation.

Which pairing channel is the strongest? To answer this question, we need to find the channels that maximally benefit from the singularity of pairing interaction
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
. Given that the singularity of
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
occurs at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=-{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and that the SC gap function
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is nonzero only for
{\boldsymbol{k}}
near the Fermi surface, the strongest pairing channel is dominated by
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
at the momentum
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
such that
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
-{\boldsymbol{Q}}
are both on the Fermi surface. To satisfy this, one way is to find a
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
on the Fermi surface so that it is related to
-{\boldsymbol{Q}}
through point group symmetry operation. Clearly,
C_{3}
cannot relate
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
and
-{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, but
\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}
,
C_{3}\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}C_{3}^{-1}
and
C_{3}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}C_{3}
can achieve this goal. As a result, we find six points
\pm Q_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) that satisfy this requirement, in which three
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
’s are related by
C_{3}
rotation. Alternatively, we can determine such
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
’s geometrically by Fig.
1
c) in the main text, where
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
’s are given by the six intersections of superimposed Fermi surfaces in valley
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}
.

It is convenient to describe the structure of the pairing function
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}
using a valley-crossing-point model, where we define a gap function in each valley-crossing points
\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
as
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. The electron energy near valley-crossing point
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
is modeled as:

\epsilon_{\pm i,{\boldsymbol{k}}}=v_{F}{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\pm i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}+\frac{|{\boldsymbol{n}}_{\pm i}\times{\boldsymbol{k}}|^{2}}{2m_{\perp}}

(28)

where
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}
is the unit normal vector of Fermi surface at
Q_{i}
. In this model, we define a gap function in each valley-crossing point
\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
as
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};\pm Q_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. According to the symmetry constraint given in Eq.(
26
), they satisfy

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{i\phi(g)}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};g{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}(gk)),\quad g\in G

(29)

where

\phi(C_{3})=1,e^{\pm i\frac{2\pi}{3}},\quad\phi(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T})=\pm 1,

(30)

Using this model, we study the pairing problem. The self-consistency relation of pairing in this model can be written as

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=-\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}j,\pm}\frac{\Gamma_{\omega+\omega^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{k}}\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{j}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\epsilon^{2}_{\pm j,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}},

(31)

Here
i,j=1,2,3
. As a reminder, according to Eq.(
22
),
\Gamma_{\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}}
diverges at
\omega=0,{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
. Therefore, below we neglect the

31
) is simplified as follows:

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=-\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{\Gamma_{\omega+\omega^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};-{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\epsilon^{2}_{-i,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}},

(32)

Since the different valley-crossing points with
i=1
,
2
, and
3
are decoupled under this approximation, in the analysis below, we only need to focus on one pair of valley-crossing points
\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
. For conciseness of the notation, below we refer to these two valley-crossing points as
\pm{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, and suppress the index
i
.

Next, since the interaction
\Gamma_{\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is positive-valued, pairing can only be generated in channels where the SC gaps in a pair of valley-crossing points related by
\mathcal{M}_{y}
(i.e. in valley-crossing points
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
and
-{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{i}
) are of opposite signs:

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}(0,\omega)\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};-{\boldsymbol{Q}}}(0,-\omega)<0

(33)

where the minus sign converts a repulsion to a pure attraction.

In the analysis below, we only focus on the even-in-frequency pairing channels, i.e. the channels satisfying

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},-\omega).

(34)

This is justified because the odd-in-frequency channels are usually weaker since the gap functions in these channels are constrained by the odd-frequency requirement
\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}},0)=0
.

Comparing with the symmetries of all possible channels described in Eq.(
29
), we find that the requirements Eq.(
33
) and Eq.(
34
) can be simultaneously achieved in channels with
\phi(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T})=-1
(see Eq.(
29
)), i.e.

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=-\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};-{\boldsymbol{Q}}}(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}{\boldsymbol{k}},\omega).

(35)

We call such channels “
p
-wave” channels.

Appendix E
Solving
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
and
T_{\rm c}
in valley-crossing points model

In this section, we analyze the self-consistency equation Eq.(
32
) and solve the momentum and frequency dependence of gap function
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
for
p
-wave pairing channel. In literature such as Ref.
Chatterjee et al. 2022
this problem is analyzed numerically. Here we present an analytical solution that is obtained under approximations.

Plugging Eq.(
22
) and Eq.(
35
) into Eq.(
32
) we get:

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\epsilon^{2}_{-i,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}}\frac{U}{\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|+l_{0}^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})^{2}+\delta^{2}},

(36)

We identify two momentum scales
k_{\omega}
and
k_{\delta}
, where
k_{\omega}
is set by the
\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+\epsilon^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}}
factor, and
k_{\delta}
is given by the interaction:

k_{\omega}=\frac{\omega}{v_{F}},\quad k_{\delta}=\frac{\delta}{l_{0}}.

(37)

Let
k_{\perp}
be the momentum component perpendicular to Fermi velocity at
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, and
k_{\parallel}
be the momentum component along Fermi velocity at
{{\boldsymbol{K}}}+{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, then
\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{K}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is nonzero only for
{\boldsymbol{k}}
inside the following range:

|k_{\perp}|\lesssim k_{\delta},\quad|k_{\parallel}|\lesssim\min\left(k_{\omega},k_{\delta}\right)

(38)

This defines a characteristic frequency
\omega_{0}=\delta v_{F}/l_{0}
. For
\omega>\omega_{0}
, the summation of
k_{\parallel}
in Eq.(
36
) is cut off at
k_{\delta}
. For
\omega<\omega_{0}
, this summation is cut off at
k_{\omega}
.

To solve the self-consistency equation Eq.(
36
) analytically, below we first show that, in this equation, the contribution from the regime of
\omega>\omega_{0}
can be safely neglected. To see this, we analyze the contribution to the right-hand side (RHS) of Eq.(
36
). In that, the integral over
k_{\parallel}
is cut off at
k_{\delta}
(see Eq.(
38
)). Therefore, for an
\omega^{\prime}
in this regime, one finds
\omega^{\prime}\gg\epsilon_{-i,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
. As a result, the high-frequency contribution to RHS of Eq.(
36
) can be written as

{\rm RHS}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}_{\perp},\omega^{\prime}>\omega_{0}}\frac{U\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime 2}\left[\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|+l_{0}^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{y}\mathcal{T}{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime})^{2}+\delta^{2}\right]}

This integral over
\omega
will not give a log divergence, therefore, the
\Delta(\omega)
above
\omega_{0}
only generates an
O(\frac{\Delta}{\omega_{0}})
correction to the value of
\Delta
obtained in low-frequency regime
\omega<\omega_{0}
. So long as we focus on the regime of
\Delta\ll\omega_{0}
, this contribution can be safely neglected.

Therefore, below we only need to solve the self-consistency equation (
36
) in the low-frequency regime
\omega<\omega_{0}
.
In this regime, we have
k_{\omega}<k_{\delta}
.
Therefore, we can integrate out
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}_{\parallel}
in the factor
\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime 2}+\epsilon_{-i,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}^{2}}
, and get the following linearized gap equation:

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\sum_{k^{\prime}_{\perp},\omega^{\prime}<\omega_{0}}\frac{\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})}{2v_{F}|\omega^{\prime}|}\frac{U}{\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|+l_{0}^{2}(k_{\perp}^{2}+{k^{\prime}}_{\perp}^{2}-2k_{\perp}k_{\perp}^{\prime}\cos\theta)+\delta^{2}},

(39)

Below we solve the self-consistency equation Eq.(
39
). For simplicity, we neglect the
\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|
term in the denominator. This approximation will be justified at the end of the discussion. To proceed analytically, we replace the rest of the long denominator with the following separable form:

\sqrt{\left(2(1-\cos\theta)l_{0}^{2}{k}_{\perp}^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)\left(2(1-\cos\theta)l_{0}^{2}{k^{\prime}}_{\perp}^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)}

(40)

This substitution enables separating the momentum and frequency dependence in the gap function
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
as following

\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};{\boldsymbol{Q}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\frac{\phi(\omega)}{\sqrt{4l_{0}^{2}{k^{\prime}}_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}+\delta^{2}}},

(41)

Here
\phi(\omega)
is the frequency-dependent part of
\Delta_{{{\boldsymbol{K}}}\bar{{\boldsymbol{K}}};Q}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
, which is governed by the following self-consistency relation:

\phi(\omega)=\sum_{\omega^{\prime}<\omega_{0}}\frac{\phi(\omega^{\prime})}{2v_{F}|\omega^{\prime}|}\sum_{k^{\prime}_{\perp}}\frac{U}{4l_{0}^{2}{k^{\prime}}_{\perp}^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}+\delta^{2}}.

(42)

Carrying out the summation over
{k^{\prime}}_{\perp}^{2}
on right hand side, we find

\phi(\omega)=\sum_{\omega^{\prime}<\omega_{0}}\frac{\phi(\omega^{\prime})}{2v_{F}|\omega^{\prime}|}\frac{U}{4l_{0}\delta\sin\frac{\theta}{2}}.

(43)

This gives a critical temperature of

T_{\rm c}=2\omega_{0}e^{-\frac{1}{\lambda}},\quad\lambda=\frac{U}{8v_{F}l_{0}\delta\sin\frac{\theta}{2}}.

(44)

As a reminder
\omega_{0}=\delta v_{F}/l_{0}
. We find a maximal SC critical temperature of
T_{\rm c,max}=\frac{U}{2l_{0}^{2}}
, which is achieved when
\delta=\frac{U}{4v_{F}l_{0}}
. Using the Stoner criterion
U\nu_{0}=1
where
\nu_{0}
is the density of states, we find the maximal
T_{\rm c}
is comparable to Fermi energy, which is much larger than the
T_{\rm c}
predicted by the valley-polarization (VP) fluctuations
Dong et al. 2022
. Therefore, it is reasonable to consider this channel as the leading pairing channel. We note that the
T_{\rm c}
predicted here is obtained when only the effects of IVC fluctuations are accounted for.

Below we check the validity of our analysis. As a reminder, in the analysis above we ignored the
\kappa|\omega+\omega^{\prime}|
in Eq.(
39
). Is this approximation valid? It is valid when the frequency in Eq.(
39
) is below a threshold
\omega_{*}
, which is defined as
\omega_{*}=\delta^{2}/\kappa\sim 4v_{F}^{2}\delta^{2}/U
. However, in Eq.(
39
) the frequency
\omega^{\prime}
is summed up to
\omega_{0}
. Therefore, this approximation is acceptable only when
\omega_{0}\lesssim\omega_{*}
, for that we need
\delta\gtrsim U/4v_{F}l_{0}
, which happens to be the optimal value of
\delta
where
T_{\rm c}
is maximized.
For
\delta<U/4v_{F}l_{0}
, we expect the
T_{\rm c}
to be further suppressed as compared to the prediction of Eq.(
44
). We can estimate the
T_{\rm c}
in this small
\delta
-regime by replacing the bandwidth
\omega_{0}
in Eq.(
44
) with a lower threshold frequency
\omega_{*}
.
