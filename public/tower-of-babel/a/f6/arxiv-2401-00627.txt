Student and AI responses to physics problems examined through the lenses of sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning
Amogh Sirnoorkar, Dean Zollman, James T Laverty, Alejandra J Magana, Sanjay Rebello, Lynn A Bryan

Abstract
Several reports in education have called for transforming physics learning environments by promoting sensemaking of real-world scenarios in light of curricular ideas. Recent advancements in Generative-Artificial Intelligence has garnered increasing traction in educators’ community by virtue of its potential in transforming STEM learning. In this exploratory study, we adopt a mixed-methods approach in comparatively examining student- and AI-generated responses to two different formats of a physics problem through the cognitive lenses of sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning. The student data is derived from think-aloud interviews of introductory students and the AI data comes from ChatGPT’s solutions collected using Zero shot approach. The results highlight AI responses to evidence most features of the two processes through well-structured solutions and student responses to effectively leverage representations in their solutions through iterative refinement of arguments. In other words, while AI responses reflect how physics is talked about , the student responses reflect how physics is practiced . Implications of these results in light of development and deployment of AI systems in physics pedagogy are discussed.

Background

2.1
Sensemaking

Sensemaking is a common cognitive process through which we integrate new information into our knowledge system. Numerous studies have explored the process of “making sense of a phenomenon”, particularly in the context of science education. Literature on sensemaking can be mainly characterized into three groups: theoretical accounts, analytical accounts, and outcomes of the sensemaking process.

The first section of the sensemaking literature involves studies that have explored the theoretical mechanisms involved in the process of “making sense of a phenomenon”. These studies have described sensemaking in terms of modeling

The second section of the sensemaking literature focuses on the analytical descriptions of the reasoning approaches or snippets of reasoning that qualify as sensemaking. 3
Theoretical Frameworks

In the rest of this section, we discuss the theoretical frameworks of the sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning processes. It is important to note that these two processes are independent of the correctness of solutions. That is, a person may engage in these two processes despite providing an incorrect description or solution of a given scenario.

3.1
Sensemaking

Sensemaking is a common cognitive process that humans engage in both within and beyond formal settings. It is the process through which we integrate new information into our knowledge system. In the rest of this manuscript, we adopt the following account from Odden and Russ
[
2
]
who define sensemaking as:

a dynamic process of building or revising an explanation in order to ‘figure something out’ - to ascertain the mechanism underlying a phenomenon in order to resolve a gap or inconsistency in one’s understanding. One builds this explanation out of a mix of everyday knowledge and formal knowledge by iteratively proposing and connecting up different ideas on the subject. One also simultaneously checks that those connections and ideas are coherent, both with one another and with other ideas in one’s knowledge system.

Based on the above definition, we deduce the following markers (henceforth referred as “sensemaking elements”) which together evidence the sensemaking process:

1.

Noticing inconsistency in one’s understanding.

2.

Use of everyday and formal knowledge while reasoning about a phenomenon.

3.

Ascertaining the underlying mechanism of the phenomenon.

4.

Generating and connecting up different ideas.

5.

Seeking coherence between the generated ideas.

In terms of the above-mentioned sensemaking elements, we qualitatively analyze the features of sensemaking in student and AI responses to two versions of a physics problem in Section
5
. We then discuss the quantitative distribution of these elements across the two sets of data in Section
6
.

3.2
Mechanistic reasoning

Unpacking the underlying mechanism of a phenomenon (the fifth sensemaking element in our list) is a complex cognitive process in itself and has been referred to as “mechanistic reasoning”. Mechanistic reasoning is a form of causal reasoning that entails the description of the underlying events and factors that give rise to an observed phenomenon
[
43
,
44
]
. This form of reasoning often entails generating explanations by transitioning from observable features of a phenomenon at the macro level to the underlying entities/processes (often at the micro level). The process of ascertaining the mechanism can further involve transitioning back from the micro to the macro features, and testing the validity of the generated explanation by varying the spatial or temporal organization of the entities/processes
[
43
]
.

As an example, consider the phenomenon of a coin placed in a glass of water as being raised when viewed from above. A mechanistic account for this observation can be provided using the principle of refraction of light. When a ray of light passes from a denser medium (water) to a rarer medium (air), it deviates away from an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface between these two media. This deviation results in the formation of an apparent image of the coin, making it appear closer to the water’s surface. Similarly, one can argue that an object in air when viewed from water (e.g., while swimming underwater) would appear to be farther away than the actual distance.

3.2.1
Analytic framework on mechanistic reasoning

Russ et al.
[
45
]
put forward an analytic framework to capture the extent of sophistication in mechanistic accounts such as the one described above. This framework characterizes mechanistic reasoning into seven hierarchical codes in the increasing order of their sophistication that relatively evidence mechanistic reasoning.

1.

Describing the target phenomenon.
Articulation of a stable phenomenon concerning which reasoning about the underlying mechanism occurs. In our example, the explicit articulation of the apparent shift in the coin’s position in water evidences this code.

2.

Identifying set up conditions.
Noting the enabling conditions of the environment that trigger regular changes of mechanism leading to the phenomenon. In our case, identifying water as a denser medium, and air as a rarer medium evidences this code.

3.

Identifying entities.
The identification of objects or entities such as a ray of light and the two media that play crucial role in the manifestation of the phenomenon.

4.

Identifying activities.
The identification of actions and interactions of the entities (such as the light ray moving from one media to another) in a mechanism correspond to this code.

5.

Identifying properties of entities.
The identification of general properties of entities (e.g., light traveling in straight lines) that are instrumental in the mechanism.

6.

Identifying organization of entities.
The description of spatial organization, location, and the structure of entities, such as the location of coin in denser medium, reflects this code.

7.

Chaining: backward and forward.
Lastly, reasoning about the events that were crucial for the current behavior of systems (backward chaining), or about the potential future behavior of systems given its current state (forward chaining) mark this code. In our case, an object in air being viewed from water appearing to be farther than the actual distance.

The authors further note that the presence of higher codes (e.g., identifying properties of entities) often subsume the evidence of lower codes (e.g., identifying entities) in mechanistic accounts. Furthermore, contemporary literature has increasingly highlighted the role of representations such as diagrams and gestures during mechanistic reasoning
[
46
]
. Thus, we examine our student data by taking into account the above-mentioned seven codes along with diagrams and gestures employed in the mechanistic explanations.

4
Methods

4.1
Context

The objective of this study is to examine sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning in students and AI responses to a physics problem – the “Gravitron problem” – posed in two different formats. The problem consists of a rotating cylindrical amusement ride in which riders lean against the wall and students are asked to determine whether riders would fall off the Gravitron’s walls under the specified parameters. One of the ways to approach this problem is by first identifying the forces acting on the rider and noting that riders would stay put against the wall if the force of friction would balance the force of gravity. If this condition mathematically holds true, then riders would sustain inside the Gravitron or else they would slide off. A detailed solution of this problem can be found in our earlier work

4.2
Data

Our data consists of two sets: student and AI responses. Student responses were collected through two sets of think-aloud interviews each involving 10 introductory students solving a set of physics problems. While the first set of interviews were conducted in the spring of 2018, the second were conducted in Spring 2019. Both interview protocols involved asking students to think-aloud while solving the problems and treat the problem-solving exercise as an untimed exam. The interviewer intervened during instances of prolonged silence through phrases such as “What are you thinking?” to nudge participants to think-aloud. Participants were provided with a calculator and an equation sheet, and were compensated with a $20 gift card. Participants’ demographic information was not collected during the interviews.

The problem sets across the two interviews were designed using the “Three-Dimensional Learning Assessment Protocol” (3D-LAP)
[
48
]
to elicit scientific practices. While a subset of problems in the first interview were designed to elicit the practice of “Developing and Using Models”, the second set consisted of problems designed to elicit the practice of “Using Mathematics”. Two interviews across the each set had issues with audio/video. Thus, the data reported in this study consists of responses from a total of 16 student interviews evenly split across two sets.

On the other hand, the AI data involves responses collected from ChatGPT-3.5 using Zero Shot approach. This method of prompting involves seeking a response from the interface posing the problem directly without providing any prior context. To maintain parity across the student data, ChatGPT was prompted with the physics problem sixteen times (eight each across open-ended and scaffolded versions) by initiating as many separate “conversations”.

4.3
Data Analysis

As part of the analysis, students’ verbal arguments and written solutions to the Gravitron task were analyzed through the lens of the five sensemaking elements described in Section
3.1
. The first element – noticing inconsistencies – was identified in the responses by noting puzzling questions
[
49
]
, intermittent pauses
[
47
]
, incomplete arguments, etc. The second sensemaking element – blending everyday and formal knowledge – was identified when the responses reflected anamalgamation of formal physics principles with the Gravitron’s “physical system”. Arguments such as “the rider being pulled down by the gravity” or “friction preventing the rider from slipping down” were coded as the blending of everyday and formal knowledge. The third and fourth elements were captured based on the generation and validation of intuitive, conceptual, or procedural ideas.

We captured the fifth sensemaking element (mechanistic reasoning) by identifying the nine hierarchical codes described in Section
3.2.1
. The first code, description of the target phenomenon, was marked when the responses explicitly noted the phenomenon of riders being held against the wall without slipping. The “identifying set up conditions” code was marked when responses highlighted the features such as the lowering of the Gravitron’s floor or the spinning of the room. Codes associated with entities (identifying entities, their activities, properties, and organization) were noted by the description of the interplay of the forces on the rider e.g., gravity acting downwards, mass remaining constant, etc. Lastly, the chaining code was marked when the required conclusion was made.

We highlight that our data across student and AI responses have two different modalities. While the student data entails verbal arguments, and written solutions, the ChatGPT’s data corresponds to written texts. We acknowledge this major limitation in our methods of data collection/analysis in comparing two different categories of data. Yet, we adopt this approach as our primary motivation is to empirically explore how students typically reason about a real-world scenario using physics principles and how AI would respond to the same context. As practitioners, we were aware that students would not prefer writing detailed reflective arguments in their solutions, particularly when answering a series of complex problems like the described in this paper.

7
Discussion and Implications

In the previous two sections, we examined student- and AI-generated solutions to a physics problem posed in two different formats. The solutions were analyzed through the theoretical lenses of sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning. While Section
5
presented qualitative analysis of representative solutions, Section
6
presented the quantitative distribution of the processes across each data set. The results, highlighted in Tables
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, can be summarized as:

1.

The AI solutions evidenced most features of the sensemaking process and all codes of mechanistic reasoning across both open-ended and scaffolded versions of the Gravitron task. The student responses on the other hand, evidenced a subset of the sensemaking features and most codes of the mechanistic reasoning.

2.

AI responses were particularly better at describing the physical features of the given system in light of conceptual ideas (blending everyday and formal knowledge) and evidencing the lower and higher codes of mechanistic reasoning.

3.

Half of the total student responses highlighted the feature of noticing gaps in their understanding and iterative refinement of arguments in their solutions.

4.

Unlike AI responses, student solutions effectively leveraged diagrams in making sense of the given context.

5.

While scaffolding had no effect on AI solutions, the student responses better evidenced the mechanistic reasoning codes when scaffolded with explicit prompts.

Across each data set, all AI solutions explicitly evidenced the seven codes of mechanistic reasoning. In contrast, student responses (e.g., Joshua’s response in Section
5
), did not explicitly evidence the lower and higher analytical codes, particularly the ones pertaining to calling out the target phenomenon, describing the system’s initial conditions, and predicting the system’s behavior based on its current state. On the sensemaking front, the AI solutions also clearly described the rider-Gravitron system in terms of physics concepts (blending everyday and formal knowledge) such as the interplay of various forces on the riders and the conditions necessary for sustaining riders inside the Gravitron.

Another distinguishing feature between the two sets of responses corresponded to noticing gaps in knowledge system. Half of the total students evidenced noticing inconsistencies in their understanding surrounding the knowledge required to solve the Gravitron task. This meta-cognitive reflection resulted in students iteratively refining their arguments as they progressed in their approaches. On the other hand, none of the AI responses reflected this feature. This should not come as a surprise as the large language models are not capable (at least as of writing this manuscript) of noticing gaps in their “knowledge system”. This result however needs to be reemphasized as the interactive, grammatically sound, assertive, and expert-like answers can make one to associate the probabilistic responses generated by an algorithm to a human “responding from the other end”. As Kortemeyer
[
13
]
observes

“
It is irritatingly hard not to anthropomorphize ChatGPT. As a physics teacher, one invariably finds oneself rooting for the students and thus by extension also for ChatGPT, celebrating its successes and being frustrated about its occasionally inexplicable failures.
”

Another unsurprising but distinguishing feature between the two sets of responses corresponds to the ways of leveraging representations in the problem-solving exercise. Every student who engaged with the problem constructed a diagram and referred to it several times during their reasoning. However, only 3 AI solutions generated representations without being specifically prompted to do so in the open-ended task. Even the ones which accompanied free-body diagrams (Figure
5
) were relatively unsophisticated and were not consistently referred in the solutions. This is again not surprising as generation of sophisticated representations is a major shortcoming of contemporary large language models.

We also observed explicit prompting in problems, particularly those involving diagram-based reasoning, to elicit more sophisticated mechanistic accounts from students. Most of the participants evidenced the higher codes of mechanistic reasoning while responding to the scaffolded version. Perhaps, this could be attributed to the requirement in the scaffolded task for participants to first reason about the interaction of forces on the rider before making their prediction. In the context our problem, explicit emphasis on forces results in evidencing several codes related to entities (properties, activities and organization of entities). However, there was no dependence observed between the codes and the problem format in ChatGPT’s responses.

Lastly, though beyond the purview of the two processes, AI solutions often included a variety of assumptions necessary for their conclusions to be valid. In the open-ended version, without specific prompting, three out of eight solutions explicitly stated the assumptions underlying their conclusions. On the student front, students hardly referred to assumptions when solving open-ended task, and when explicitly prompted, they often struggled to articulate assumptions
[
50
]
. Secondly, none of the AI responses across the two problems provided a correct solution despite exhibiting sophistication on various aspects. This observation aligns with findings from other studies which have made cautioned about ChatGPT’s seemingly confident solutions despite accompanying major conceptual flaws
[
38
,
41
]
.

The above-mentioned results have several implications for educators in leveraging AI for teaching and learning of physics. Firstly, our results highlight that AI is particularly effective at conveying the vocabulary of physics (mechanistic codes such as highlighting the interplay between physical features, mathematical formalisms, and conceptual ideas). This vocabulary is further embedded in a systematically organized solution structure such as initial descriptions of target systems, mathematical calculations, and clear articulation of conclusions accompanied by underlying assumptions. These aspects are often beneficial for beginners in early stages of their physics courses in effectively structuring and communicating their results. Activities such as and multilingual students in the early phases of their courses. asking students to solve challenging problems or write descriptive essays (say about, fundamental laws of physics), and making them compare their responses to AI-generated ones can tactfully communicate the physics terminology along with the structural communication of ideas. Such practices can also be useful for multilingual students getting started with learning physics in English language.

Furthermore, there are ongoing efforts in designing large language models (LLMs) that can provide personalized learning assistance to students
[
51
]
. Our observation of explicit prompting to facilitate students’ mechanistic reasoning can lead to designing planned scaffolds focused on the analytical codes that can support students in generating sophisticated mechanistic accounts. Calibrated interventions aimed at nudging students towards identifying the appropriate target system, relevant entities, and associated activities can lead to enhanced learning about real-world systems, particularly in university-level physics. For instance, in our case study, an explicit scaffold asking Joshua to reconsider the relevance of inward directed force or the role of normal force in providing the necessary centripetal force would have helped him in continuing the problem-solving endeavor. In a similar way, several studies are also exploring on providing personalized feedback based on students’ solutions. The features of our adopted frameworks (sensemaking elements and analytical codes of mechanistic reasoning) can also inform the nature and structure of feedback.

Consistent with the contemporary literature, we too observed AI responses confidently articulating incorrect solutions through sophisticated vocabulary. This feature can be leveraged by asking students to critique the AI-generated solutions. Such exercises can not only make students aware of the imperfections underlying AI responses, but can also implicitly communicate the sophisticated lexical patterns of scientific communication. In addition, confident phrasing of incorrect solutions can also be a fertile ground for students to notice gaps in their knowledge systems, thereby facilitating sensemaking in our classrooms.

Contemporary studies have also called for designing next-generation assessments in which Generative-AI can support students instead of impeding their learning. Our results highlight designing assessments focused on diagram- and gesture-based reasoning to be potentially productive as the contemporary AI systems seem to be relatively less unsophisticated in responding to such prompts.

8
Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

The recent advent of Generative-AI, particularly ChatGPT, has garnered significant traction in physics education community. A predominant section of studies have focused on gauging the accuracy of AI in answering various forms of physics assessments. We contribute to these studies by going beyond capturing the correctness of AI-generated responses and comparatively examining them with students’ reasoning to two versions of a physics problem based on real-world context. Using mixed-methods approach, we analyze the responses through the lens sensemaking and mechanistic reasoning - the processes central to scientific inquiry. We observed that while AI responses evidenced most features of the two processes through well-structured solutions, student responses effectively leveraged representations in their solutions through iterative refinement of arguments. In other words, while AI responses reflected the sophisticated lexical patterns of experts’ vocabulary (albeit providing incorrect solutions), the student approaches reflected the rich epistemic practices of physics. That is, while the AI responses reflected how
physics is talked about
, the student responses reflected
how physics is practiced
.

Engaging with physics not only entails leveraging diverse arguments such as experimental facts, approximations, etc.
[
33
,
34
]
, but also entails communicating ideas and results using appropriate vocabulary. This vocabulary may entail clear description of the target system under investigation, discussion of the relevant physical quantities and mathematical formalisms leveraged in the given context, or providing appropriate justification for conclusions (particularly in the context of problem solving)
[
52
,
53
]
. ChatGPT consistently mirrored these features through well-structured responses, thereby reflecting
how physics is talked about
.

On the other hand, student responses effectively reflected the conventional knowledge-building practices in physics, or
how physics is practiced
. These included representation-based reasoning (use of diagrams and gestures), along with iterative refinement of arguments. Diagram- and gesture-based reasoning are central to knowledge creation in physics. Conceptual ideas such as the direction of magnetic field around a current carrying conductor or the distribution of electric-field lines around a system of charges are often discussed through gestures and diagrams. Furthermore, conventional knowledge-building practices in physics such as argumentation and modeling are iterative in nature, i.e., these practices involve iterative refinement of arguments in making sense of novel contexts
[
5
,
54
]
.

This study makes several contributions to the contemporary literature focused on the intersection of artificial intelligence in STEM education. Firstly, this study goes beyond gauging the accuracy of AI responses to analyzing them through the lens of cognitive processes that are central to scientific inquiry. We adopt a framework-based approach in unpacking the subtle features of scientific inquiry in AI generated solutions and comparing them with students’ responses. Secondly, though several studies have explored ChatGPT’s causal reasoning in diverse domains such as neuropathic pain diagnosis
[
55
]
and publicly available multilingual multi-modal data
[
56
]
, this study explores mechanistic reasoning (which subsumes causal reasoning) in physics with a focus on diagrams and equations.

Our findings however accompany several limitations. Firstly, the claims made in this study are based on the analysis of limited number of student and AI responses to just one physics problem. Analysis of a larger data set of responses to a diverse physics problems would undoubtedly shed more insights. Secondly, the students interview involved interventions from the interviewer during prolonged pauses and may have potentially impacted the participants’ “chain of thought”. We acknowledge this inherent limitation of think-aloud interview and in our approach to data collection. Finally, the data collected from ChatGPT and that from students are categorically of different nature. While students’ data consisted of verbal arguments, gestures, and written solutions, ChatGPT’s data consisted entirely of texts. Comparing textual data across the two would have been an ideal scenario. However, our primary objective was to explore how students naturally reason about a context and how AI would respond to the same context. As practitioners, we were aware that students would not prefer to write every argument in their solutions, particularly to as complex problem as the one described in this paper. Thus, we acknowledge that a major limitation of this work corresponds to making claims by analyzing two categorically different forms of data.

Future work would involve analyzing AI and student solutions to other problems from our data through the lens of additional processes such as modeling and argumentation that are integral to scientific reasoning. Further studies would also focus on extending similar methodology across students’ written essays and AI responses. Analysis of AI- and student-generated assumptions along with the epistemological messages conveyed by AI responses to students on what counts as “knowing” or “doing” physics are also avenues for future explorations.
