Dynamics Harmonic Analysis of Robotic Systems: Application in Data-Driven Koopman Modelling
Daniel Felipe Ordoñez-Apraez, Vladimir Kostic, Giulio Turrisi, Pietro Novelli, Carlos Mastalli, Claudio Semini, Massimiliano Pontil

Abstract
We introduce the use of harmonic analysis to decompose the state space of symmetric robotic systems into orthogonal isotypic subspaces. These are lower-dimensional spaces that capture distinct, symmetric, and synergistic motions. For linear dynamics, we characterize how this decomposition leads to a subdivision of the dynamics into independent linear systems on each subspace, a property we term dynamics harmonic analysis (DHA). To exploit this property, we use Koopman operator theory to propose an equivariant deep-learning architecture that leverages the properties of DHA to learn a global linear model of the system dynamics. Our architecture, validated on synthetic systems and the dynamics of locomotion of a quadrupedal robot, exhibits enhanced generalization, sample efficiency, and interpretability, with fewer trainable parameters and computational costs.

Introduction

The current state-of-the-art in modelling, control, and estimation of robotic systems relies on the Lagrangian model of rigid-body dynamics, which represents the system’s state as a point in the space of generalized (or minimal) coordinates. This approach has fostered the development of efficient algorithms that leverage the system’s kinematic structure to enable recursive computations which are ubiquitous in methods for simulation, estimation, planning, and control in robotics
(
Featherstone 2007
)
. However, because the model’s dynamics are nonlinear, these methods need to cope with the challenges of nonlinear optimization, often through iterative local (or state-dependent) linearizations
(
Mayne 1966
;
Li and Todorov 2004
)
. This can limit control policies to local minima, hinder the convergence of optimization methods, and bias the estimation of unobserved quantities.

The Koopman operator framework can potentially address these limitations by deriving globally linear models of robot dynamics, albeit in an infinite-dimensional function space
(
Brunton et al. 2022
;
Kostic et al. 2023
)
.
These models can be easily used with various estimation and control theory techniques
(
Mauroy et al. 2020
)
and can capture dynamic phenomena that impact the system’s evolution but are challenging to model analytically
(
Asada 2023
)
. However, building a robust data-driven model approximation in finite dimensions is a substantial machine-learning challenge.
This study highlights the value of leveraging the state symmetries inherent in a system’s dynamics as a geometric prior, enhancing the approximation of the operator. This approach improves sample efficiency, generalization, and interpretability while reducing the number of trainable parameters and computational cost. Although our emphasis is on robotics, the proposed framework is broadly applicable to any dynamical system with discrete symmetry groups.

In robotics, the symmetries we aim to exploit are known as morphological symmetries
(
Ordonez-Apraez et al. 2023
)
. These are discrete symmetry groups that capture the equivariance of the robot’s dynamics, arising from the duplication of rigid bodies and kinematic chains (see
fig.

Inspired by the use of harmonic analysis of discrete symmetry groups in physics
(
Dresselhaus et al. 2007
)
, we present the first application of these principles in robotics. Specifically, we employ the isotypic decomposition (
theorem

1
) to partition the state space of symmetric robotic systems into isotypic subspaces (see
fig.

1

3

3

These are the robotics analog of normal vibrational modes in molecular dynamics; see
(

; each evolving in a distinct isotypic subspace (see

Moreover, we demonstrate that the isotypic decomposition leads to partitioning any (local or global) linear model of the system’s dynamics into independent linear subsystems, each characterizing the dynamics of an isotypic subspace. This offers numerous computational advantages and enhances the interpretability of the dynamics.

Contributions

In summary, our work relies on the following contributions: (i) we introduce the use of harmonic analysis of discrete symmetry groups to robotics; (ii) we propose the concept of dynamics harmonic analysis (DHA), illustrating how local/global linear dynamics models decompose into linear subsystems within isotypic subspaces (
section

3
); (iii) leveraging on DHA, we propose the equivariant Dynamics Autoencoder (eDAE), a deep-learning architecture to approximate the Koopman operator (
section

4
), and report its strong performance on synthetic and robotics systems (
section

5
); and (iv) we provide an open-access
repository
enabling the application of harmonic analysis to a
library

Preliminaries

This section overviews background material on dynamical systems modelling, Koopman models, and harmonic analysis needed to address the modelling of symmetric dynamical systems in

Modelling of dynamical systems

In our analysis, distinguishing between a dynamical system and its numerical model is crucial. A dynamical system abstracts evolving real-world phenomena, such as a robot’s motion in an environment. Conversely, numerical models approximate the system’s dynamics as the time evolution of points (representing the system’s state) in a vector space. A dynamical system is typically denoted by a tuple
({\Omega},\mathbb{T},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})
, where
{\Omega}
is the abstract set of system states
\omega\in{\Omega}
, the set
\mathbb{T}
represents time, and
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}\colon{\Omega}\crossproduct\mathbb{T}\mapsto{\Omega}
is the evolution map, determining the future state based on the present state and time. Given deterministic Newtonian mechanics governs our target systems, we focus on deterministic, Markovian, discrete-time systems. Here,
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
depends solely on the time difference between consecutive timesteps
{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
, i.e.,
\omega_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}}:=\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(\omega_{t})
for any
t\in\mathbb{T},\omega\in{\Omega}
. Thus, we will denote a dynamical system by

({\Omega},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})
.

Defining a numerical model of a dynamical system involves identifying a state representation vector-valued function
{\bm{x}}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]:{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where the components,
x_{j}:{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},j\in[1,m]
, are observable functions that measure a relevant scalar quantity from the state (e.g., kinetic energy, joint position/velocity).
This enables the representation of the state as a point in the model’s vector space
{\bm{x}}(\omega)\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
The system’s evolution, represented as a trajectory
({\bm{x}}(\omega_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
, can be approximated by an evolution map
{\bm{x}}(\tilde{\omega}_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}}):=\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}({\bm{x}}(\omega_{t}))+\kappa(\omega_{t})\Gamma_{t}
. Here, the predicted state representation
{\bm{x}}(\tilde{\omega}_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})
may differ from the true one
{\bm{x}}(\omega_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})
due to modelling errors and/or inaccessible observables. The influence of these factors is assumed to be captured by a mild white noise stochastic perturbation
\Gamma_{t}
, scaled by
\kappa(\omega_{t})

(
Lasota and Mackey 1994
, chpt. 10.5)
. When
{\bm{x}}:{\Omega}\to{\mathcal{X}}
is injective, the optimality of the model
({\mathcal{X}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})
, over a given prediction horizon
H\in\mathbb{T}
, is quantified by its predictive error
(
Mezić 2021
)
, i.e.,

\small\text{err}_{H}({\bm{x}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})=\int_{\Omega}\text{err}_{\omega_{0},H}({\bm{x}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})d\omega_{0},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{given}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{err}_{\omega_{0},H}({\bm{x}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}):=\sum_{h=1}^{H}||{\bm{x}}(\tilde{\omega}_{h{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})-{\bm{x}}(\omega_{h{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})||^{2}.

(1)

Note that while modelling error can be locally minimal for specific
\omega_{0}
(r.h.s
eq.

1
), optimal models exhibit uniformly small errors across all states and horizons (l.h.s
eq.

1
).

Linear models and the Koopman operator

A linear model
({\mathcal{Z}},{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}})
is a dynamics model where the temporal evolution of state representations is characterized by an autonomous linear system,

{\bm{z}}(\tilde{\omega}_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})=\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{Z}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}({\bm{z}}(\omega)):={\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}{\bm{z}}(\omega),\omega\in{\Omega},{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\in\mathbb{T}
. Here,
{\bm{z}}=[z_{1},\dots]:{\Omega}\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}
is the state representation function, and

{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}
is a matrix evolving state representations in time by
{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
.
The linearity of these models ensures the existence of analytical solutions to the temporal dynamics of each state observable and guarantees the interpretability of the predictions through modal decomposition, making them fundamental to dynamical systems and control theory.

Although most dynamical systems of interest in robotics have been historically modeled with nonlinear analytic dynamics, one can also devise an optimal linear model in an infinite-dimensional space. This idea, originating from the seminal work of Koopman and Markov
(
Lasota and Mackey 1994
)
,
proposes to represent the state as a point/function in a
space of functions

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}

, and model the dynamics with a linear operator
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
, defined by the flow
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
and a time step
\Delta t\in\mathbb{T}
, which takes any function
z(\cdot)\in\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}$ }
to
z(\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(\cdot,\Delta t))
. Whenever the image of
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
is in the same space,
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}:\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}$ }\mapsto\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}$ }
is a well-defined linear operator known as the
Koopman operator
, defined by

[{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\,z](\omega_{t}):=z(\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(\omega_{t})),\,z\in\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}$ },\,\omega\in{\Omega}.

(2)

The requirement for the space

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}

to be invariant under the flow
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
is the characteristic that often renders it infinite-dimensional. As we discuss in
section

4
, machine learning can aid in finding finite-dimensional approximations of

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}

and
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}

(
Lusch et al. 2018
;
Kostic et al. 2023
)
.

Symmetry groups and their representations

In the context of a dynamical systems, symmetries are defined as bijections on the state set
{\Omega}
. The action of a symmetry transformation
g
on any state
\omega\in{\Omega}
is defined by a map
(\,\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\,):\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\times{\Omega}\rightarrow{\Omega}
to a symmetric state
g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega\in{\Omega}
(see
fig.

1
-a). A set of symmetry transformations forms a group
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}=\{e,g_{1},g_{2},\dots\}
, that is closed under
composition
:
g_{1}\mathrel{{\circ}}g_{2}\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
, and
inversion
:
g^{-1}\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\;|\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
such that
g^{-1}\mathrel{{\circ}}g=e
, where
e
denotes the identity transformation and
(\mathrel{{\circ}})
is the binary composition operation on
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
.

As we study symmetries of numerical models in both finite-dimensional (Euclidean) spaces and infinite-dimensional function spaces, we assume
{\mathcal{H}}
to be a separable Hilbert space to accommodate both scenarios. This enable us to rely on the conventional concepts of inner product, orthogonality, and countably many basis elements (see
section

I.1
). Consequently, symmetry transformations on
{\mathcal{H}}
are defined via a unitary group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}:\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{H}})
, mapping each
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
to a unitary matrix/operator
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}(g)}\in\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{H}}):{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
. Thus, the action of any
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
on a point
{\bm{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
is expressed as
g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}{\bm{h}}:={\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
. When
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}
exists, we say that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a symmetric space.

A map
f:{\mathcal{H}}\mapsto{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
between two symmetric spaces is denoted
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant if
f({\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{h}})={{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}$}}}}}(g)}}f({\bm{h}})
, and
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-invariant if
f({\bm{h}})=f({\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{h}})
, for all
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. While the action of a symmetry transformation on
{\mathcal{H}}
is basis independent, the representation of
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
depends on the chosen basis of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Therefore, applying a change of basis
{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{H}})
results in a new point representation
{\bm{h}}_{\circ}={\mathsf{Q}}{\bm{h}}
, and a new group representation
\rho_{{\mathcal{H}},\circ}(\cdot)={\mathsf{Q}}{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}(\cdot){\mathsf{Q}}^{*}
. Here, both representations describe the same transformation:
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{h}}\iff\rho_{{\mathcal{H}},\circ}(g){\bm{h}}_{\circ}
. Consequently, representations related by a basis change are termed equivalent, denoted
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}\sim\rho_{{\mathcal{H}},\circ}
. Lastly, writing
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}\sim{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{1}$}}}}}}\oplus{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{2}$}}}}}}
implies that
{\mathcal{H}}
decomposes into orthogonal subspaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
, and
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}
has block-diagonal structure.

Isotypic decomposition and its basis

Our use of harmonic analysis is linked with the decision to work in a specific basis for the modelling space
{\mathcal{H}}
, the isotypic basis. In this basis, the unitary group representations
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}
decomposes into a block-diagonal sum of multiple copies (multiplicities) of the group’s
{k}
unique irreducible representations (
irrep
)
\{{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}\}_{{i}=1}^{{k}}
. These are the indivisible building blocks of any group representation of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
.
Each
{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}:\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}(\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}})
, describes a unique symmetry pattern, characterized by a subset of symmetry transformations within the broader group structure. The space
{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}}}
associated to each
irrep
is the smallest finite-dimensional space capable of expressing the
irrep
symmetry pattern. For instance, our groups
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
are often subgroups of the orthogonal group. Thus, we frequently work with
irreps

{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{tr}}}
that describe a reflection symmetry, requiring a
1
-dimensional space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\tr}\sim\mathbb{R}
to act on, or with
irreps

{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\nicefrac{{2\pi}}{{a}}}}}
that describe rotations by an angle
\nicefrac{{2\pi}}{{a}}
, requiring a
2
-dimensional space
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\nicefrac{{2\pi}}{{a}}}\sim\mathbb{R}^{2}\sim\mathbb{C}
to act on. These representations are called irreducible because the spaces
{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}}}
have no non-trivial invariant subspace to the actions of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. That is, if
\mathcal{V}
is a subspace of
\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}}
and
{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}(g)}\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{V}
for every
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
, then either
\mathcal{V}=\{0\}
or
\mathcal{V}=\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}}
.

The value of the isotypic basis lies in the fact that it allows decomposing the modelling space
{\mathcal{H}}
into an orthogonal sum of isotypic subspaces. Each subspace reflects a unique symmetry pattern of one of the group’s
irreps
, hence the term
iso-typic
or
same-type
. This is a pivotal result in abstract harmonic analysis, succinctly captured by the Peter-Weyl Theorem, see
(
Knapp 1986
, thm 1.12)
and
(
Golubitsky et al. 2012
, Thm-2.5)
.

Theorem 1

(Isotypic Decomposition)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
be a compact symmetry group and
{\mathcal{H}}
a symmetric separable Hilbert space with a unitary group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}:\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{H}})
. Then we can identify
{k}\leq|\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}|
irreducible representations
{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}:\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}(\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}})
and change of basis
{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{H}})
such that
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}$}}}}}}\sim{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{1}$}}}}}}\oplus{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{2}$}}}}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{{k}}$}}}}}}
, and each
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{H}}_{{i}}$}}}}}}\sim\bigoplus_{{j}=1}^{m_{{i}}}{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}
is composed of at most
m_{{i}}
countably many copies of the irreducible representation
{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}
. This allows to decompose
{\mathcal{H}}
into orthogonal subspaces:
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus^{\perp}{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\oplus^{\perp}\cdots\oplus^{\perp}{\mathcal{H}}_{{k}}
. Where each
{\mathcal{H}}_{{i}}:=\bigoplus_{{j}=1}^{m_{{i}}}{\mathcal{H}}_{{i},{j}}
, composed of
m_{{i}}
subspaces isometrically isomorphic to
{\bar{{\mathcal{H}}}_{{i}}}
, is denoted as an isotypic subspace.

3
Symmetries of dynamical systems

In the context of dynamical systems, a symmetry is a state transformation that results in another functionally equivalent state under the governing dynamics.
From a modelling perspective, symmetries provide a valuable geometric bias, as identifying the dynamics of a single state suffices to capture the dynamics of all of its symmetric states (see
fig.

1
-a).

Definition 0

(Symmetric dynamical systems)
.

A dynamical system
({\Omega},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})
is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric, if
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
is a symmetry group of the set of states
{\Omega}
, and the system’s evolution map is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant, i.e.,

\small\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega,t)=g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(\omega,t),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}},t\in\mathbb{T},\omega\in{\Omega}.

(3)

The symmetry group of
{\Omega}
defines an equivalence relationship between any state
\omega\in{\Omega}
and its set of symmetric states, denoted
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega=\{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega|g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\}
. Given the
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariance of the dynamics
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
, this equivalence implies that a set of symmetric states
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega
will evolve along a unique trajectory of motion, up to a symmetry transformation
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
(see
fig.

1
-a). When the symmetry group is discrete (or finite), the state set
{\Omega}
decomposes into a union of symmetry-transformed copies of the quotient set
\nicefrac{{{\Omega}}}{{\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}}
, containing the system’s unique states, that is
{\Omega}=\cup_{g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}\{g\,\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\,\nicefrac{{{\Omega}}}{{\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}}\}
(see
fig.

1
-a).

Modelling symmetric dynamical systems

When designing a numerical model
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
for a symmetric dynamical system, it is crucial to ensure that the modelling space
{\mathcal{X}}
inherits the group structure of
{\Omega}
. This can be achieved by making the space invariant under the action of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}(\cdot))\in{\mathcal{X}}
for all
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. Thus, enabling the existence of a group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}}}}}:\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}({\mathcal{X}})
. The significance of this design choice lies in the fact that the equivalence between symmetric states is translated into the corresponding equivalence of their representations in the modelling space:

\small\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega:=\{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega,\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\}\iff\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}{\bm{x}}(\omega):=\{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}{\bm{x}}(\omega):={\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{x}}(\omega)={\bm{x}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega),\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\},\;\forall\;\omega\in{\Omega}

(4)

The symmetric structure of
{\mathcal{X}}
allows its decomposition into
g
-transformed copies of a quotient space
\nicefrac{{{\mathcal{X}}}}{{\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}}
. This is a practical tool in data-driven applications, mitigating the effects of the curse of dimensionality
(
Higgins et al. 2022
)
. As the following result shows, it also narrows the search space for the evolution map
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
to the space of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant ones.

Proposition 3

(Optimal models of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric systems)
.

Let
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
be a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric dynamical system and
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
an optimal model (
eq.

1
). If
{\mathcal{X}}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric space,
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega
denote an arbitrary set of symmetric states and
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}{\bm{x}}(\omega)
represent their symmetric representations on
{\mathcal{X}}
. Consider a non
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant evolution map, denoted as
\bar{\Phi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}}
. Such map will yield varying prediction errors for the states in
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega
. Consequently, it is possible to identify the state with the minimum prediction error,
\hat{g}\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}\omega
, given
\hat{g}=\argmin_{g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}\text{err}_{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega,H}({\bm{x}},\bar{\Phi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})
, as per
eq.

1
. Subsequently, we can construct a new map that replicates the predicted evolution of
\hat{g}\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega
for all symmetric states, defined as
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}({\bm{x}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega)):=(g\mathrel{{\circ}}\hat{g}^{-1})\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\bar{\Phi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}}({\bm{x}}(\hat{g}\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega)),\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. It is important to note that
\text{err}_{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega,H}({\bm{x}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})\leq\text{err}_{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega,H}({\bm{x}},\bar{\Phi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}^{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})
holds for all
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. By iteratively applying this process for all
\omega\in\nicefrac{{{\Omega}}}{{\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}}}
, the resulting map will exhibit
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariance.
∎

Consistent with
eq.

3
, we denote models
({\mathcal{X}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})
that possess a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric modelling state space
{\mathcal{X}}
and a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant evolution map as
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric models. A familiar example is the Lagrangian model of rigid-body dynamics. Since, for
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric robotic systems (e.g., the mini-cheetah in
fig.

1
), the modelling space
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{q}}}\mathcal{Q}
, defined by the space of generalized position
\mathcal{Q}
and velocity
\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{q}}}\mathcal{Q}
coordinates, is a symmetric vector space
(
Ordonez-Apraez et al. 2023
, III)
. The symmetry of the space is characterized by the group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}}}}}:={\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\mathcal{Q}$}}}}}}\oplus{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{q}}}\mathcal{Q}$}}}}}}
, which describes the transformations shown in
fig.

1
-a. Furthermore, the evolution map
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
, defined by the standard Euler-Lagrange equations of motion, features
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariance
(
Lanczos 2012
, VII.2)
.

A key property of (non-linear or linear)
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric models is the decomposition of the modelling state space into
{k}
isotypic subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
(refer to
theorem

1
). This enables the projection of entire motion trajectories
({\bm{x}}(\omega_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
onto each isotypic subspace
{\bm{x}}(\omega_{t}):={\bm{x}}^{(1)}(\omega_{t})\oplus^{\perp}\dots\oplus^{\perp}{\bm{x}}^{({k})}(\omega_{t})
. Since each
{\mathcal{X}}_{{i}}
is a lower-dimensional space with a reduced number of symmetries, the trajectory’s decomposition entails its characterization as a superposition of distinct synergistic motions
({\bm{x}}^{({i})}(\omega_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\;|\;{\bm{x}}^{({i})}(\omega_{t})\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{i}}
, each constrained to feature the subset of symmetries of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{i}}
(see
fig.

1
-b). This understanding is instrumental in characterizing different system behaviors, such as different locomotion gaits or manipulation tasks, through their lower-dimensional projections onto each isotypic subspace, as detailed in
fig.

1
-c.

For
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric linear dynamics models
({\mathcal{Z}},{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}})
, the isotypic decomposition (
theorem

1
) of

{\mathcal{Z}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{j}=1}^{m_{{i}}}{\mathcal{Z}}_{{i},{j}}
and the associated group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{Z}}$}}}}}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}$}}}}}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{j}=1}^{m_{{i}}}{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}
imply the dynamics’ decomposition into
{k}
linear subsystems
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}
.
Each subsystem is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant,
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}={\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}$}}}}}(g)},\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
, and evolves the state projections into isotypic subspaces independently. Moreover, each subsystem is block-decomposed into scalar multiples of the identity map
{\bm{I}}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{|{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}|}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{|{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}|}
, such that

\small{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}}=\mathrel{{\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},1}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{k}}\end{bmatrix}}},\hskip 9.24994pt{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1,1}{\bm{I}}_{i}&\dots&c_{1,d_{i}}{\bm{I}}_{i}\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
c_{d_{i},1}{\bm{I}}_{i}&\dots&c_{d_{i},d_{i}}{\bm{I}}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 9.24994ptc_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R},\;d_{i}:=\nicefrac{{|{\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}|}}{{|{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}|}},\forall\;{i}\in[1,{k}]

(5)

The block-diagonal structure of
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
, and the block-structure of each
{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}
are geometric constraints that originate from Schur’s Lemma (
lemma

6
), a standard result in harmonic analysis. These properties, essentially stated in
Golubitsky et al. 2012
for finite-dimensional spaces, can be generalized to Hilbert spaces, leading to the following result:

Theorem 4

(Isotypic decomposition of symmetric linear models)
.

Let
({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}})
be a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric linear model,
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
be a finite group, and
{\mathcal{Z}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
be the space isotypic decomposition. Then
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
is block-diagonal
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\mathsf{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},i}
, where each

{\mathsf{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}:{\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}_{{i}}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant linear map characterizing the independent dynamics of each isotypic subspace.

This result has two primary applications in robot dynamics modelling. The first involves decomposing local linear models resulting from local (or state-dependent) linearizations of nonlinear dynamical models. These are widely used in the iLQR
(
Li and Todorov 2004
)
and DDP
(
Mayne 1966
)
algorithms, which are instrumental for trajectory optimization
(
Tassa et al. 2014
;
Mastalli et al. 2020
)
, and state estimation
(
Alessandri et al. 2003
;
Kobilarov et al. 2015
;
Martinez et al. 2024
)
. For these methods, the model decomposition facilitates the parallel optimization of the
{k}
local linear sub-models, presenting a promising direction for future research. The second application pertains to the approximation of
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric Koopman models, which we discuss next.

Dynamic harmonic analysis

For a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric dynamical system
\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}
, an injective state representation
{\bm{x}}:{\Omega}\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}
, and
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric modelling space
{\mathcal{Z}}
, the Koopman operator
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
(
eq.

2
) is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant and globally optimal by construction. Hence, by
theorem

4
, it decomposes into
{k}
Koopman operators characterizing the dynamics of each isotypic subspace. Furthermore, for each eigenpair
(\lambda,\psi)
of
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
and every
g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
, the symmetric function
\psi_{g}:=\psi(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}(\cdot))\colon{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
is also an eigenfunction of
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
in the same eigenspace,

\small\lambda\,\psi_{g}(\omega)=\lambda\,\psi(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega)=[{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\,\psi](g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega)=\psi(\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega))=\psi(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\Omega}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}}(\omega))=[{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\,\psi_{g}](\omega),

(6)

implying that the Koopman eigenspaces are
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric spaces. Thus, applying the isotypic decomposition to each eigenspace (
theorem

1
) captures the relation of the temporal evolution of eigenfunctions with distinct symmetries (encoded by the
irreps
) via Koopman eigenvalues.
This global decomposition of the dynamics in isotypic subspaces and its symmetry-aware spectral decomposition is referred to as
dynamics harmonic analysis
(DHA). As we show in
sections

4
and
5
, DHA can be leveraged to learn data-driven approximations of the
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant Koopman operator.

4
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric data-driven Koopman models

The Koopman operator formalism, while practically unfeasible, has inspired numerous data-driven models aiming to approximate the infinite-dimensional operator in finite-dimensions
(
Brunton et al. 2022
)
. This process relies on a dataset of observations of state trajectories
{\mathbb{D}}=\{({\bm{x}}(\omega_{t}))_{t=0}^{H},\dots\}
on a modelling space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Where
{\bm{x}}=[x_{1},...,x_{m}]:{\Omega}\mapsto{\mathcal{X}}
is a state representation composed of physical observable functions
x_{i}:{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that are measured or estimated from the state (e.g., position, momentum, energy). Then, the objective is to find a (latent) state representation vector-valued function
{\bm{z}}=[z_{1},...,z_{\ell}]:{\Omega}\to\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, that spans a finite-dimensional space of functions
\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}$ }:=\{z_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\cdot):=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}z_{i}(\cdot)={\langle{\bm{z}}(\cdot),{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle},\;|\;{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\}
(see
section

I.1
), on which the Koopman operator
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
is approximated by a matrix
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
, given by

({\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}z_{{\bm{\alpha}}})(\cdot)\approx z_{{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{*}{\bm{\alpha}}}(\cdot):={\langle{\bm{z}}(\cdot),{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{*}{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle}={\langle{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}{\bm{z}}(\cdot),{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle}

(
Kostic et al. 2022
)
.
Where

{\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}

represent the finite-dimensional approximation of the space of functions

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}

on which
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
is defined (see
eq.

2
), and
{\bm{\alpha}}=[\alpha_{1},\dots]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is the coefficient vector representation of
z_{\bm{\alpha}}
in a basis of

{\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}

.

Among different approaches to building Koopman data-driven models, we focus on the Dynamics Auto-Encoder (DAE) architecture
(
Lusch et al. 2018
)
. This model parameterizes the matrix approximating the Koopman operator
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{*}
as a trainable linear map, and the (latent) state representation
{\bm{z}}(\cdot):=({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}\circ{\bm{x}})(\cdot):{\Omega}\mapsto\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
with an encoder neural network
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}
. A decoder
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}^{\mathrel{{-1}}}:\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}\mapsto{\mathcal{X}}
is also defined to reconstruct states in the physical observable space. The cost function for DAE is composed of a reconstruction loss (encouraging injectivity of
{\bm{z}}
) and a state prediction error in both physical observable
{\mathcal{X}}
and latent
\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
spaces (encouraging the minimization of the modelling error):

\small\mathcal{L}(\omega_{t},H)=\mathrel{{\sum_{h=0}^{H}}}{\color[rgb]{0.57,0.458,0.0912}{\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{||{\bm{x}}(\omega_{t+h{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})-{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}^{\mathrel{{-1}}}({\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{h}{\bm{z}}(\omega_{t}))||^{2}}}_{\text{Reconstruction and}\;\text{err}_{\omega_{t},H}({\bm{x}},\Phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}^{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}$}})}}}+\gamma{\color[rgb]{0.054,0.415,0.505}{\underbrace{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{||{\bm{z}}(\omega_{t+h{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})-{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}^{h}{\bm{z}}(\omega_{t})||^{2}}}_{\text{err}_{\omega_{t},H}({\bm{z}},{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}})}}}\vskip-8.5359pt

Where
\gamma
balances the modelling errors in
\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
and
{\mathcal{X}}
, and the prediction horizon is assumed
H\ll|{\mathbb{D}}|
.

The Equivariant DAE (eDAE)

When modelling
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric dynamical systems (
eq.

3
), the DAE architecture can be adapted to leverage the symmetry priors.
First, to exploit the theoretical isotypic decomposition of the space of observable functions

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}}

(
propositions

3
and
1
), we must ensure that

{\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}

is a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric function space, such that it can be decomposed in isotypic subspaces
\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}$ }:=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}\scalebox{0.9}{
${\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}_{i}}$ }
. This can be achieved by restricting the state representation
{\bm{z}}:{\Omega}\rightarrow\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
to be a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant map,
such that both
\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
and

{\mathcal{F}}_{\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}}

are
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric spaces (see
section

I.1.2
). In practice, this is done by constraining the encoder to be a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant neural network, such that:

\small g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}{\bm{z}}(\omega_{t})={\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}$}}}}}(g)}({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}\circ{\bm{x}})(\omega)={\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}({\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{x}}(\omega))={\bm{f}}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega))={\bm{z}}(g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}\omega_{t}),\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}},\omega\in{\Omega}.

(7)

Where the group representation in the physical observable space
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\mathcal{X}}$}}}}}}
is assumed to be known from prior knowledge, and the latent group representation
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}$}}}}}}
is defined to be equivalent to the direct sum of
\nicefrac{{\ell}}{{|G|}}
copies of the group regular representation, following
(
Knapp 1986
, thm. 1.12)
.

Then, to exploit the theoretical
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariance of
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
(
propositions

3
and
4
), we parameterize
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
as a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant matrix,
{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}$}}}}}(g)}{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}={\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}$}}}}}(g)},\;\forall\;g\in\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
. Furthermore, if the latent group representation is defined on the isotypic basis

{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}$}}}}}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\rho_{{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{$\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}_{{i}}$}}}}}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{j}=1}^{m_{{i}}}{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}
(see
theorem

1
), then the matrix
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
decomposes in block-diagonal form
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}=\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\bm{\oplus}$}}}_{{i}=1}^{k}{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}
, where each
{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-equivariant matrix characterizing the dynamics on the isotypic subspace
\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}_{{i}}
. This constraint on the learnable parameters of
{\bm{K}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}
is relevant geometric prior as it (i) ensures the learned latent dynamics model is
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric, (ii) exploits the orthogonality between distinct isotypic subspaces (eliminating spurious correlations between dimensions of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}
), and (iii) constraints the minimum dimension of the eigenspaces of each isotypic subspace operator
{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}
to match the dimension of the subspace irreducible representation dimension
|{\bar{\rho}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{i}}}}|
(
eq.

5
). This ensures that eigenfunctions of each isotypic subspace

{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}}

are appropiatedly approximated in groups of symmetric functions by their coefficient eigenvectors representations
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}{\bm{v}}^{({i})}=\{g\mathrel{{\triangleright}}{\bm{v}}^{({i})}\;|\;{\bm{K}}_{{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}},{i}}{\bm{v}}^{({i})}=\lambda^{({i})}{\bm{v}}^{({i})},\;{\bm{v}}^{({i})}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{Z}}}_{i}\}
, with temporal dynamics governed by an eigenvalue
\lambda^{({i})}
; refer to
section

I.1.2
.

\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}
-symmetric systems with arbitrary groups
\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}

{\bm{x}}(\omega_{t+{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}})={\bm{A}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}{\bm{x}}(\omega_{t})+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{t},,{\bm{C}}\omega_{t}\geq{\bm{c}}

{\bm{A}}_{\mathrel{{\Delta}t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

\sigma

n_{c}

\mathbb{G}_{\scalebox{0.6}{$$}}

These synthetic systems let us assess symmetry exploitation in learning Koopman models for arbitrary groups

, and noise standard deviation

6
Conclusions

We introduced the use of harmonic analysis for decomposing and understanding the dynamics of symmetric robotic systems. By partitioning the state space into isotypic subspaces, we have shown how complex motions can be characterized as the superposition of lower-dimensional, symmetric and synergistic motions. This entails the decomposition of (local and global) linear models of the system’s dynamics into independent models for each subspace. Leveraging this, we learn a data-driven global linear model using a novel equivariant deep-learning architecture to approximate the Koopman operator. The method’s practical validity is evidenced by presenting the first successful attempt to learn a liner model of the closed-loop dynamics of a quadruped robot’s locomotion.
