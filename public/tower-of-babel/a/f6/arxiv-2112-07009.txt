A Torelli Theorem for Graph Isomorphisms
Sarah Griffith

Abstract
. It is known that isomorphisms of graph Jacobians induce cyclic bijections on the associated graphs. We characterize when such cyclic bijections can be strengthened to graph isomorphisms, in terms of an easily computed divisor. The result refines tools used in algebraic geometry to examine the fibers of the compactified Torelli map.



1.
Overview

There is a known Torelli theorem for graphs, due to Caporaso-Viviani and Su-Wagner

In this paper we show that preservation of discrete theta divisors yields a procedure for lifting isomorphisms of graph Jacobians not only to the matroid level but to the level of graph isomorphisms. 2.
History and Motivation

Every connected finite graph
G
has an associated
\mathbb{Z}
-graded finite commutative group, which we will call
\Pic_{\dee}(G)
. Its degree zero subgroup
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
has been repeatedly rediscovered or reframed by researchers in a wide variety of fields. Dynamicists considered it and related generalizations while investigating a phenomenon known as self-organized criticality. In this guise it came to be known as the
Abelian sandpile model
. Combinatorialists considered it in the context of
chip firing games
or the
dollar game
or
critical groups
. It turns out that
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
knows surprising and interesting things about
G
(for example, its order is the number of spanning trees of
G
).

Graph theory often arises in algebraic geometry when one takes degenerate limits of families of varieties: which and how many times irreducible components of the degeneration intersect each other can be encoded as a graph. Graph invariants can then be used to analyze possible degenerations. The Picard group of a graph famously arose in arithmetic geometry, through work of Raynaud and of Oda and Seshadri, who proved that the special fibers of Néron models of certain Jacobians have components indexed by
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
for
G
a related graph
[
22
]

[
20
]
. See
[
13
]
for more context. Connections between
\Pic_{\dee}(G)
and algebraic geometry gained further attention due to work by Baker and Norine, who exposed surprising parallels between graphs and Riemann surfaces (for example, the existence of a Riemann-Roch theorem
[
9
]
).

On the other hand, an invariant which we call the graph Jacobian
J(G)
arose in relation to the classical combinatorial theory of cuts and flows on graphs. A Riemann surface of genus
g
has a Jacobian variety, a complex torus of complex dimension
g
which can be interpreted as parameterizing holomorphic forms up to relations arising from the geometry of the surface. The graph Jacobian is a real flat torus which admits a similar interpretation, as described in
[
4
]
and
[
8
]
. As it turns out,
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
embeds canonically into
J(G)
.

The surface and graph parallel is further strengthened by the existence of
Abel-Jacobi maps
in both cases. Saving precise statements for later, given a base point (base vertex), the first Abel-Jacobi map tells us how to map the points (vertices) of our surface (graph) into the Jacobian (into
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
). Except in trivial cases, this first map is injective. The
k
-th Abel-Jacobi map takes
k
points (vertices), maps them individually using the first Abel-Jacobi map, then sums them using the group operation. In the surface case, for
g\geq 2
the
g-1
-th map has image a hypersurface. In the language of algebraic geometry this means that the image is a divisor (relative to the Jacobian). It is called the
theta divisor
. In the graph case, we will be concerned with an analogue we call the discrete theta divisor, which is simply a subset of
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
, and therefore
J(G)
.

While the Jacobian is not a complete invariant for Riemann surfaces, a critical result called the Torelli theorem says, roughly, that a Riemann surface can be characterized up to isomorphism by its Jacobian together with its theta divisor. In its most classical form it concerns periods and intersection theory; in modern language it is about injectivity of a map between moduli spaces. The following form, which we have weakened for simplicity, is one which closely connects to our results.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Weak classical Torelli theorem) Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be Riemann surfaces of genus at least two, with specified base points. Let
(J(X),\theta)
and
(J(X^{\prime}),\theta^{\prime})
be Jacobians together with the theta divisors for
X
and
X^{\prime}
. If
J(X)
and
J(X^{\prime})
are isomorphic in a way which identifies
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
, then
X\cong X^{\prime}
.

Caporaso-Viviani proved a Torelli style result for graphs in
[
15
]
. Independently, Su-Wagner proved the same result in the more general context of regular matroids
[
23
]
. The graph Torelli theorem relates isomorphisms of Jacobians to isomorphisms of graphic matroids, and does not make use of any analogue of the theta divisor. This result has a key application in algebraic geometry, which we will discuss in
section

7.5
.

We would be remiss in not mentioning that much of the above can be discussed equally well in the tropical context. See
[
7
]
for a survey.

This paper largely consists of the refinement of data from discrete theta divisors of graphs. In order to hold this data, we use the
generalized cycle-cocycle system
of a graph, defined in
[
5
]
, which consists of equivalence classes of partial orientations on the underlying graph. This invariant is explicitly intended to package data from
\Pic_{\dee}(G)
in a more “matroidal” form. While it cannot describe all of
\Pic_{\dee}(G)
, it can speak fluently about divisors which lie in a certain range of degrees. These include the
special divisors
, which are closely related to the discrete theta divisor. As the arguments in this paper show, the generalized cycle-cocycle system is extremely well behaved in the situation of the graph Torelli theorem, and so all our definitions are intended to load as much information into it as possible.

3.
Technical background and relations between graph invariants

In this section, we describe the relationships between various graph invariants we will need, and indicate our preferred terminology for them.

We will need to discuss both directed and undirected graphs.

Definition 3.1
.

A
graph

G
is a pair
(V(G),E(G))
of edge and vertex sets, with each edge associated with an unordered pair of distinct adjacent vertices
\{x,y\}
. This disallows edges which go from a vertex to itself. If each pair of vertices has at most one edge between them, we say
G
is
simple
. At all times we will only consider connected graphs. The empty graph is connected. A graph is
n
-connected
(
n
-edge connected
) if removing
n-1
vertices (edges) never disconnects the graph. It is
n
-
connective
(
n
-
edge connective
) if
n
is the greatest number for which
G
is
n
-connected (
n
-edge connected). A
based
graph
(G,e)
is a graph
G
with a choice of a distinguished edge
e
.

An
isomorphism
of graphs
\psi:G\to H
is a pair of bijections
V(G)\to V(H)
and
E(G)\to E(H)
, also denoted by
\psi
. We require that
\psi
preserve all adjacency relations between edges and vertices. If
(G,e)
and
(H,w)
are based, we also require that
\psi
preserve the base edge.

A
path
in a graph is a sequence
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of edges such that
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
are adjacent for each
i
. A
cycle
is a path such that
e_{1}
and
e_{n}
are also adjacent. The vertices which lie between each
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
are referred to as being visited by the path or cycle. We also consider the ends of
e_{1}
and
e_{n}
not visited in the subsequent and prior steps, respectively, to be visited. The order in which vertices are visited is uniquely defined except in the case of a path entirely between two vertices; this case should never be problematic. We will frequently stretch our notation by referring to the set of edges composing a cycle as a cycle. When the possibility of confusion exists we will refer to such sets as
unordered cycles
.

It is difficult to keep track of vertices using the data of the objects we are interested in. A motif of this paper is that we prefer to track oriented edges, and specify vertices as being the targets and origins of edges. To this end, we now define directed graphs. We put hats over symbols as a purely informal method of suggesting that we consider them with orientation data: for example
\hat{e}
with
e
an edge suggests that
\hat{e}
is relevantly oriented. Usually this means that its target will be at least implicitly relevant.

Definition 3.2
.

A
directed graph
is a quadruple
(V(G),E(G),o,t)
with an origin or source function
o:E(G)\to V(G)
and a target function
t:E(G)\to V(G)
, for which the underlying undirected graph satisfies the same conditions as before. We will call a choice of such an
o
and
t
an
orientation
on the graph, say
\Gamma
, and denote an oriented graph
\hat{G}
by
(G,\Gamma)
. We likewise write a based directed graph as
(G,\Gamma,e)
. We may omit
\Gamma
or
e
when they are irrelevant or clear from context. We will sometimes consider graphs in which possibly only some of the edges are oriented. We call such orientations
partial orientations
. In an oriented or partially oriented graph, a vertex is a
source
if it has no incoming edges.

An isomorphism of (based) directed graphs is an isomorphism of the underlying (based) graphs. No notion of compatibility in orientations is required.

Definition 3.3
.

A path (cycle) is
simple
if it does not visit any vertex twice, except for possibly beginning and ending at the same vertex. Equivalently, we cannot extract any subcycles.

A simple unordered cycle has only two possible directions. When an orientation is present, we will often indicate a direction for a simple unordered cycle by specifying whether we travel with or against a particular edge.

Several invariants will require a choice of orientation in their definitions. They vary up to a canonical isomorphism based on this choice, a fact that will be of significance in
section

5.1
. We begin with invariants described in
[
4
]
(for an expository discussion, see
[
12
]
). Consider the euclidean vector space of
1
-chains
C_{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
with orthonormal basis the edges of
G
. Its dual space
C^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, endowed with the induced inner product, is the space of
1
-cochains
of
G
, which we always think of as having the canonical orthonormal dual basis given by
\{e^{*}\}_{e\in E(G)}
.

We denote the span of the algebraic cycles of
\hat{G}
by
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, and the projection operator onto
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
will always be denoted by
\pi
. The integer lattice
C^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
in
C^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
restricts to
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
to produce a lattice
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, which is itself generated by algebraic cycles. There is always at least one basis of
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
given by simple algebraic cycles. The orthogonal complement of
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
is spanned by the consistently oriented cuts of
G
.

Definition 3.4
.

Given a path (cycle)
P=e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
in
\hat{G}
, its associated
algebraic path (cycle)
, denoted
\alpha(P)
, is the formal sum
\sum_{i}s_{i}e_{i}^{*}\in C^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, where
s_{i}=1
if
P
crosses
e_{i}
in the direction of its orientation, and
s_{i}=-1
otherwise. When
C
is a cycle
\alpha(C)
is always in
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
. We say that
P
is an
oriented path (cycle)
if all the
s_{i}
are
1
. The traversal of a path (cycle)
P
backwards is indicated by
P^{\text{op}}
. We therefore have
\alpha(P^{\text{op}})=-\alpha(P)
.

The
Jacobian

J(G):=H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})/H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is a flat metric torus. The Jacobian has a dual, the
Albanese

A(G)
, which can also be defined through homological means by quotienting the space
H_{1}(G,\mathbb{R})=H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})^{*}\subset C_{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
. There is a canonical finite subgroup of
J(G)
, which we call the
discrete Jacobian
, denoted by
J_{\dee}(G)
. This is the discriminant group of the lattice
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
. In words, take the dual lattice
\Hom_{\text{{Ab}}}(H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z})
, which via the inner product we think of as itself a lattice in
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, and quotient it by
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
. When
a,b\in H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, we write
a\equiv b
to indicate equivalence modulo
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
.

In
[
9
]
Baker and Norine recast another closely related graph invariant, one which has appeared in many guises, including in
[
4
]
. Consider the free commutative group
\Div G
on the vertices of
G
, referred to as the group of
divisors
on
G
. This group has a
\mathbb{Z}
grading into degrees
\Div^{n}G
, given by summing coefficients. The graph Laplacian matrix
\Delta
of
G
is the difference of the adjacency matrix and the degree matrix. It can be regarded as an endomorphism of
\Div G
, mapping into
\Div^{0}G
. Taking
\Div G/\im\Delta
produces a commutative group
\Pic_{\dee}(G)
, the
discrete Picard group
of
G
, which inherits a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading into parts
\Pic_{\dee}^{n}(G)
. Each
\Pic_{\dee}^{n}(G)
is finite. Two divisors
D
and
D^{\prime}
which are equivalent modulo
\im\Delta
are referred to as
linearly equivalent
, and we write
D\sim D^{\prime}
. A divisor
D
with only non-negative coefficients is referred to as
effective
. When we write an inequality between divisors, we mean it to hold coefficient by coefficient. For example,
D\geq 0
indicates that
D
is effective. It is sometimes convenient to denote the
v
coefficient of
D
by
D(v)
. Finally, we denote by
|D|
the set of effective divisors linearly equivalent to
D
.

It turns out that there is a canonical isomorphism
\Pic_{\dee}^{0}(G)\cong J_{\dee}(G)
. We need to describe how it is produced.

Definition 3.5
.

For each
w\in E(\hat{G})
, define
h_{w}\in H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
to be
\pi w^{*}\in H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
. If
(\hat{G},\hat{e})
is based, for any vertex
v
we define
P_{v}\in J_{\dee}(G)
to be
\pi\alpha(P)+H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, where
P
is a path from
t(\hat{e})
to
v
(recall that
\pi
is the projection operator
C^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
). In words,
h_{w}
is the projection of the indicator function for
w
to
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, while
P_{v}
is obtained by projecting any algebraic path from
t(\hat{e})
to
v
to
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})
, then passing to
J_{\dee}(G)
.

We now define a map
\Div^{0}(G)\to J_{\dee}(G)
by extending
t(w)-o(w)\mapsto h_{w}+H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z})
linearly. One can demonstrate that this is well defined, surjective, and the kernel is exactly
\im\Delta
, yielding the desired isomorphism
\Pic_{\dee}^{0}(G)\cong J_{\dee}(G)
.

Let
V(G)^{(n)}
denote the
n
-th symmetric product of
V(G)
. We have the additional data, depending on
\hat{e}
, of
Abel-Jacobi maps

S_{\hat{e}}^{n}:V(G)^{(n)}\to\Pic_{\dee}^{0}(G)
, given by
v_{1}+v_{2}+\dots+v_{n}\mapsto v_{1}+v_{2}+\dots+v_{n}-nt(\hat{e})
. After composing with our isomorphism
\Pic_{\dee}^{0}(G)\cong J_{\dee}(G)
, this becomes
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\mapsto\sum P_{v_{i}}
. Stretching our notation, we also denote this map by
S_{\hat{e}}^{n}
.

Let
g(G)
denote the
genus
of
G
, which we define to be
|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1
(graph theorists sometimes call this the cyclotomic number of
G
, reserving “genus” for a different invariant).

Definition 3.6
.

We refer to
\im S_{\hat{e}}^{g(G)-1}
, in either
\Pic^{0}_{\dee}(G)
or
J(G)
, as the
discrete theta divisor

\theta_{\hat{e}}
of
(G,\hat{e})
. We refer to the divisors of
\theta_{\hat{e}}+(g-1)t(\hat{e})\subset\Pic^{g-1}_{\dee}(G)
as the
special divisors
, the set of which is denoted
\mathcal{S}(G)
. This is independent of the base edge and consists exactly of the divisors linearly equivalent to an effective divisor. The rest of the divisors in
\Pic^{g-1}_{\dee}(G)
are referred to as
nonspecial
. We denote the set of these by
\mathcal{N}(G)
.

Remark 3.7
.

In the Riemann surface context, the theta divisor has essentially the same definition, but there it is a hypersurface within the Jacobian. In the language of algebraic geometry, such a hypersurface is a divisor, whence the divisor part of “theta divisor.” The reader should not be mislead into thinking a theta divisor is a divisor on a graph or Riemann surface. In particular, the discrete theta divisor carries no more structure than that of a (finite) subset of the Jacobian.

Definition 3.8
.

Each graph
G
has an associated
graphic matroid

M(G)
, which is the edge set of
G
together with the set of all simple unordered cycles in
G
.

An isomorphism between graphic matroids is a bijection between the edge sets which induces bijections between the associated sets of simple cycles. When we write
(M(G),e)
for

G
, we mean

to have base element

. Isomorphisms between graphic matroids with base elements will always be required to respect the base elements.

Independently, both Caporaso-Viviani and Su-Wagner related

to

by proving a Torelli theorem for graphs, from combinatorial and algebraic geometry points of view, respectively

[

]
,
[

]
. For the moment we can state the results as follows.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let

be

if and only if

Since

if and only if

.

A classical theorem of Whitney says that all isomorphisms of graphic matroids can be produced through sequences of certain topological operations on their associated graphs. We conclude this section by couching this theorem in our language.

Definition 3.19
.

A
cyclic bijection
between graphs consists of a bijection between their edges which preserves simple unordered cycles. This is precisely the same as an isomorphism of graphic matroids as in
3.8
. Let
Cyc
be the category of
2
-connected based graphs and base preserving cyclic bijections between them. Within the morphisms of
Cyc
there is a distinguished class of

, which are those which extend to a genuine isomorphism of graphs.

Example 3.20
.

Consider the following objects in

4.
Oriented Functoriality

We now wish to describe the Jacobian and our other, related objects in functorial terms. Ideally, the domain for our functors would be
Cyc
, but the objects of this category carry too little data to support more than the previously discussed functor to graphic matroids. To capture our entire range of discussion, the minimal extra data necessary is that of
Cyc
but with each graph also being directed.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
OrCyc
be the category of
2
-connected and
2
-edge connected based oriented graphs and base preserving cyclic bijections between them.

Example 4.2
.

The morphisms between objects in
OrCyc
see nothing of the orientation data. Put another way, the forgetful functor
\text{{OrCyc}}\to\text{{Cyc}}
which discards the base orientations is full and faithful. Thus we could orient the edges in

5.
Preservation of the discrete theta divisor

In this section, we begin by describing

, a property of morphisms in
OrCyc
. Our goal is

In particular, it says that rigidity is equivalent to both preservation of the discrete theta divisor and preservation of the first Abel-Jacobi image. This latter criterion will eventually put us in a position to describe the consequences of rigidity in terms of graph structures.

The examples of

and
\mathbb{O}^{g-1-m^{\prime}}(G)
by reversing each oriented edge, making unoriented edges be bioriented, and making bioriented edges be unoriented. Denote this interchange by
*
. This map is a natural isomorphism
\mathbb{O}^{m}(-)\cong\mathbb{O}^{g-1-m^{\prime}}(-)
. One checks that we have
c(*U)=K-c(U)
, where
K
is the canonical divisor. This makes it straightforward to dualize results concerning partial orientations to results concerning bioriented edges.

5.2.
Preservation of the First Abel-Jacobi Image

In this subsection we prove the following technical lemma, using standard methods of argument.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
G
be a
2
-connected and
2
-edge connected graph of genus at least two. Let

be nonzero. Then

.

Note that
\mathcal{S}
is a translation of any choice of discrete theta divisor. Translating

back into

and setting

to be a rigidity divisor will be a key step in characterizing rigidity in terms of preservation of the discrete theta divisor.

Definition 5.17
.

Let
(f)
be a principal divisor on

. We define

to be the set of vertices of

on which

is maximal and

to be the set of edges on which

is minimal.

Definition 5.18
.

Given a subset

, let

be the smallest subgraph of

containing all edges between vertices in

and vertices not in
X
. For

and vertices not in

.

For the next result and its first corollary, a dual result holds for

and

, obtained by using the observation that

.

Proposition 5.19
.

Let
D^{\prime},D\in\Div(G)
be effective, and let
D^{\prime}=(f)+D
for
(f)\neq 0
. For all

, we have
D(x)\geq|\partial_{x}M(f)|
.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle-D(x)\leq(f)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{edges between }x\text{ and }x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime})-\deg(x)\cdot f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\partial_{x}M(f)\text{ connected to }x^{\prime}\neq x}f(x^{\prime})-|\partial_{x}M(f)|\cdot f(x)

\displaystyle\leq-|\partial_{x}M(f)|

as desired.
∎

Corollary 5.20
.

Let

be
k
-edge connected. Then in the above situation
\deg D\geq|E(\partial M(f))|\geq k
. Further, if

, then

is
k
-edge connective,

is exactly

One obtains

∎

Remark 5.21
.

One can interpret the above by thinking of values of

as specifying pressure at each vertex, capable of pushing the ‘chips’ of chip firing. Qualitatively, the above conditions on

say that the only way we can push an effective divisor

to another effective divisor is by finding regions of the graph that

seals off and pressurizing a selection of them. Quantitatively, they specify that

requires one chip in order to seal off each outgoing edge from the region.

Lemma 5.22
.

Let
G
be a
2
-connected and

-edge connected graph and let

Note that translating by

, we must have that

∎

A proof and an algorithm for producing the relevant objects appear in

We can now prove

5.3.
Proof of equivalent characterizations of rigidity

In
remark

5.6
we outlined how failure to preserve the discrete theta divisor ought to result in a nonzero rigidity divisor. The prior section gave us the technical tools necessary to demonstrate that the presence of a nonzero rigidity divisor always prevents preservation of the discrete theta divisor. We now prove

.

Proof.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
: We have already proven, in
theorem

5.9
, that
\upvarphi
is rigid if and only if
\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}\sim 0
.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(3)
: We have

\displaystyle\upvarphi_{*}(\theta_{\hat{e}},g-1)

\displaystyle=\upvarphi_{*}(\iota_{\hat{e}}c(\mathcal{O}\iota_{\hat{e}}^{-1}(\theta_{\hat{e}},g-1)))

\displaystyle=\upvarphi_{*}\iota_{\hat{e}}c(\mathcal{O}\iota_{\hat{e}}^{-1}(\theta_{\hat{e}},g-1))

\displaystyle=\iota_{w}c\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(\mathcal{O}\iota_{e}^{-1}(\theta_{\hat{e}},g-1))+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}

\displaystyle=\iota_{w}c\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{O}}(G))+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}

\displaystyle=\iota_{w}c(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{O}}(H))+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{\hat{w}},g-1)+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{\hat{w}}+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi},g-1)

If
\upvarphi
is rigid, then from
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
we know that
\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}\sim 0
, so
\upvarphi_{*}(\theta_{\hat{e}})=\theta_{\hat{w}}
. If
\upvarphi
is not rigid, then again by

we know that
\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi}\not\sim 0
. Now
\iota_{w}^{-1}((\theta_{\hat{w}},g-1)+\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi})=\mathcal{S}(H)+\iota_{w}^{-1}(\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi})
, where
\iota_{w}^{-1}(\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi})\not\sim 0
. By

, we have
\mathcal{S}(H)+\iota_{w}^{-1}(\mathcal{E}_{\upvarphi})\neq\mathcal{S}(H)
, so applying
\iota_{w}
, we find that
\upvarphi_{*}(\theta_{\hat{e}})\neq\theta_{\hat{w}}
.

(4)\Rightarrow(3)
: Immediate.

(3)\Rightarrow(4)
: If

then the discrete theta divisors are the same as
\im S_{\hat{e}}^{1}
and
\im S_{\hat{w}}^{1}
and so there is nothing to show, so assume

.

We start by showing that we must have
\upvarphi_{*}(\im S_{\hat{e}}^{g-2})=\im S_{\hat{w}}^{g-2}
. Both sets are finite, and by
5.25
are of the same size, so it is sufficient to show that
\upvarphi_{*}(\im S_{\hat{e}}^{g-2})\subseteq\im S_{\hat{w}}^{g-2}
.

Since
\upvarphi
is rigid, the diagram of
theorem

5.11
commutes with
X=\{e\}
. If

is acyclic, then by

so is
\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(W)
. It follows that for any

with an effective divisor in its Chern class, we have that
\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(U)
is not acyclic, so by

we know that
\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(U)
is equivalent to a sourceless orientation. Thus if

is effective, we have that
\iota_{\hat{w}}^{-1}(\upvarphi_{*})\iota_{\hat{e}}(D)\sim c(\upvarphi_{\mathbb{O}}(U))
, implying that
\iota_{\hat{w}}^{-1}(\upvarphi_{*})\iota_{\hat{e}}(D)
is linearly equivalent to an effective divisor.

Now if
D\in\Pic^{g-2}_{\dee}(G)
is effective, then by
theorem

5.23
we can find some

with Chern class
D
. Necessarily,

only has a single unoriented edge

. Letting

be the set of vertices reachable from

along oriented paths, if

does not include one of the ends of

, the edges from

to

form a consistently oriented cut. Reverse the cut and repeat until there is an oriented path from

to

. Using the third operation of

repeatedly along this path, we find that

is equivalent to an orientation in

. Thus
\iota_{\hat{w}}^{-1}(\upvarphi_{*})\iota_{\hat{e}}(D)
is linearly equivalent to an effective divisor, so applying

shows that

is preserved.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that we can choose a vertex

with

. Let

we can find some effective

. Writing

∎

6.
Interpretation in terms of the structure of graphs

In this section we prove the main result,
theorem

6.6
. This characterizes rigidity of a morphism as coming from being ‘near to’ a graph isomorphism, in a sense we will make precise.

The first order of business is to describe the inability of
J(G)
to distinguish certain edges. In
[
15
]
maximal collections of such edges are referred to as
codimension one sets
. We use the term series, which follows
[
21
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a
2
-edge connected graph and
e\in G
be an edge. Let
\varepsilon(e)
be the set of edges
\ell
such that
G\backslash\{e,\ell\}
is not connected. We refer to
\varepsilon(e)
as a
series class
and say
e
and
\ell
are
in series
. We call
e
a
series edge
if
|\varepsilon(e)|>1
.

Edges in a
2
-edge connected graph are in series precisely when they are contained in the same set of simple cycles. A
2
-edge connected graph is
3
-edge connected exactly when the graph has no series edges. Here is the picture we ought to have when a series class
C
has at least two edges. Consider pearls whose positions on a single strand necklace are fixed. The components of
G\backslash C
are the pearls, while the series edges are the lengths of chain running between the pearls.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\ell
be an edge in a
2
-connective and
2
-edge connected graph
G
, with ends
v,w
. Let
p,q
be any two vertices in
G
with
v-w\sim p-q
. Then
p
and
q
are the ends of an edge in series with
\ell
. Conversely, if
r
is an edge in series with
\ell
, with ends
p,q
, then
v-w\sim\pm(p-q)
.

Proof.

For the first claim, assume
v-w\neq p-q
, since otherwise there is nothing to prove. It follows that
v\neq p
. If
v=q
, then arguing as in the proof of
lemma

5.22
yields the conclusion. Reasoning similarly with
w
, we reduce to the case that all of
v,w,p
and
q
are distinct.

We have
v+q=(f)+w+p
for some nonconstant
f
. By
5.20
, we know that
V(\partial M(f))=\{w,p\}
and
V(\partial N(f))=\{v,q\}
, and that each of these is adjacent to exactly one edge out of
M(f)
or
N(f)
. By subtracting a constant we may assume the minimal value of
f
is zero; this makes it clear that we cannot have that
v
is adjacent to a vertex
t
with
f(t)>1
. Thus
f(w)=1
, so
M(f)\cup N(f)
partitions the vertices of the graph. The second edge
r
out of
M(f)
is therefore also the second edge into
N(f)
. Removing
\ell
and
r
then disconnects
G
, so
\ell
and
r
are in series as desired.

Conversely, if
\ell
and
r
are in series, say with
G\backslash\{\ell,r\}
having components
X
and
Y
, one computes directly that setting
f
to be
1
on
X
and
0
on
Y
yields the desired linear equivalence.
∎

Definition 6.3
.

If
\psi:M(G)\to M(G)
is a cyclic bijection which takes each series class into itself, we say that
\psi
is
series fixing
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\psi
be a automorphism of
(\hat{G},\hat{e})
in
OrCyc
. Then
\psi
is series fixing if and only if
\psi_{*}=\id
.

Proof.

Suppose
\psi
is series fixing. Let
\psi(\ell)=r
. Let
C
be a simple cycle in
G
. If
C
does not contain
\ell
, then
\ell^{*}\cdot\pi\alpha(C)=r^{*}\cdot\alpha(C)=0
. Otherwise
\ell^{*}\cdot\alpha(C)=-r^{*}\cdot\alpha(C)
if and only if
\sgn_{\psi}(\ell)=-1
. Since the
\alpha(C)
span
H^{1}(\hat{G},\mathbb{R})
it follows that
\psi_{*}h_{\ell}=\psi_{*}\pi(\ell^{*})=\pi(\psi_{*}(\ell^{*}))=\pi(\sgn_{\psi}(\ell)r^{*})=\sgn_{\psi}(\ell)h_{r}=h_{\ell}
.

If
\psi_{*}=\id
, then for any edge
\ell
, if
\psi(\ell)
were not in
\varepsilon(\ell)
, we could choose a cycle
C
containing
\ell
but not
\psi(\ell)
. Then
0\neq h_{\ell}\cdot\alpha(C)=\psi_{*}(h_{\ell})\cdot\alpha(C)=\sgn_{\psi}(\ell)h_{\psi(\ell)}\cdot\alpha(C)=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

We have
h_{\ell}=\pm h_{r}
for edges
\ell
and
r
if and only if
\ell
and
r
are in series.

The above results reflect the general tendency for the Jacobian to have a difficult time telling edges in series apart. Thus
3
-edge connectedness hypotheses result from the need for some kind of useful canonicity. When our graphs are
3
-edge connected, rigidity has the strongest possible meaning.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\upvarphi:(G,\Gamma_{1},\hat{e})\to(H,\Gamma_{2},\hat{w})
be a morphism in
OrCyc
between graphs of genus at least two. Then
\upvarphi
is rigid if and only if there exists a series fixing automorphism
\psi:(M(H),w)\to(M(H),w)
such that the composition

\upvarphi

and

are isomorphic graphs.

Proof.

To check the backward direction, consider that
\upvarphi_{*}=\psi_{*}\upvarphi_{*}=(\psi\upvarphi)_{*}
by

. An edge isomorphism takes
\im S^{1}_{\hat{e}}
to
\im S^{1}_{\hat{w}}
, as we can check by hand on paths, so by
(4)\Rightarrow(1)
in
theorem

5.9
we have that
\upvarphi
is rigid.

Conversely, suppose
\upvarphi
is rigid. We will inductively define a
\psi
and extend
\psi\upvarphi
to vertices so as to take larger and larger subgraphs of
G
isomorphically to their image. As a base case, set
\psi(\hat{w})=\hat{w}
. Setting
\psi\upvarphi(t(e))=t(w)
and
\psi\upvarphi(o(e))=o(w)
extends
\psi\upvarphi
to vertices so as to produce an isomorphism between the subgraph of
G
consisting of
e
and
t(e),o(e)
, and the subgraph of
H
consisting of
w
and

.

Suppose that for a connected subgraph

of

, we have specified
\psi
on edges of
\upvarphi(W)
and extended
\psi\upvarphi
on vertices so as to be an isomorphism between

and its image. Choose an edge

adjacent to a vertex

in

. Let its other end be

). Without loss of generality, let

and suppose that

By

we have that
\upvarphi_{*}(P_{p})\equiv P_{r}
for some vertex

. We also have

\displaystyle\upvarphi_{*}(P_{p})

\displaystyle=\upvarphi_{*}(P_{v})+\upvarphi_{*}h_{\ell}

\displaystyle=P_{\psi\upvarphi(v)}+h_{\upvarphi(\ell)}

Here
\upvarphi_{*}(P_{v})=P_{\psi\upvarphi(v)}
is a consequence of our inductive hypothesis. Applying

to

.

By
lemma

6.2
, there is an edge between
\psi\upvarphi(v)
and

\upvarphi(\ell)
. Set
\psi\upvarphi(\ell)
to be this edge. We now need to show that setting
\psi\upvarphi(p)=r
extends
\psi\upvarphi
to an isomorphism on

.

The only issue occurs if

was already in

, in which case we need to show that
\psi\upvarphi(p)
is already

. Since by hypothesis

is connected, there must be some simple cycle

inside

, which means
\upvarphi(Q)
forms a simple unordered cycle in

. Since
\psi
only permutes within series classes, we also have that
\psi\upvarphi(Q)
is a simple unordered cycle. There can be only one pair of vertices in

which
\psi\upvarphi(\ell)
can go between to complete
\psi\upvarphi(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\})
to a simple unordered cycle, specifically the vertices adjacent to exactly one edge in
\psi\upvarphi(\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\})
. It therefore must be that
\psi\upvarphi(\ell)
goes between
\psi\upvarphi(v)
and

, as desired.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

If
G
is
3
-edge connected, then
\upvarphi
is rigid if and only if
\upvarphi
is an edge isomorphism.

Corollary 6.8
.

Rigidity is independent of the choice of base orientations
\Gamma_{1}
and

7.2.
Relation to Whitney’s
2
-isomorphism theorem

In this subsection, we describe a consequence of rigidity which is closely related to the proof of Whitney’s 2-isomorphism theorem
[
24
]
, suggesting a conjecture.

Definition 7.1
.

A
weak arch
in a graph
G
is defined in the same way as an arch, except that either the weak arch or its complement must contain three vertices, rather than both being required to have three vertices. We can define Whitney moves on weak arches in precisely the same way as before.

The relevant difference between an arch and a weak arch is that Whitney moves given by weak arches can be used to arbitrarily reverse the orientations of individual edges. This would trivialize
Question
, so before we preferred arches, but here our interest is different and our statements are cleaner with weak arches.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
G
be a
2
-connective graph and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a collection of weak arches with the following properties.

(1)

The
X_{i}
cover
G
.

(2)

Any two distinct
X_{i}
intersect at most at their tips.

(3)

The graph obtained by quotienting each
X_{i}
to an edge is cyclic.

Then the
X_{i}
are a
generalized cycle
, and the
X_{i}
are a
generalized cycle decomposition
of
G
. We say the decomposition is
in order
or
ordered
if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
enumerates the
X_{i}
in the order in which they appear as a cycle. We say that the decomposition is
strong
if each
X_{i}
is
2
-connected and the decomposition is in order.

Remark 7.3
.

A useful property of a generalized cycle decomposition is that removing any weak arch (but not its tips) does not disconnect the graph, but removing any two weak arches (but leaving their tips) does disconnect the graph.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a generalized cycle decomposition in order. Then this decomposition can be refined to a strong decomposition.

The relevance to cyclic bijections is as follows. The statement here is a little stronger than what appears in
[
24
]
, but follows easily from it.

Theorem 7.5
.

(Truemper)
Let
G
and
H
be
2
-connective graphs and let
\upvarphi:M(G)\to M(H)
be a cyclic bijection. Let
G
be equipped with a strong generalized cycle decomposition
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. Let
\upvarphi(X_{i})
denote the smallest subgraph containing the images of all edges in
X_{i}
. Then there exists a unique reordering
\tau
of
2,3,\dots,n
such that
\upvarphi(X_{1}),\upvarphi(X_{\tau(2)}),\dots,\upvarphi(X_{\tau(n)})
is a strong generalized cycle decomposition of
H
and
\tau(2)\leq\tau(n)
.

Proof.

[
24
]
.
∎

In other words, for a each strong generalized cycle decomposition, we have a corresponding decomposition on
H
, and up to a permutation
\upvarphi
respects the decomposition structures. Note that
\upvarphi
does not necessarily respect the adjacency relations of edges within a particular weak arch; we only know that each weak arch is preserved as a whole. Given the above statement, we are so close to Truemper’s proof of Whitney’s 2-isomorphism theorem that we are liable to bump our noses on it: with an induction hypothesis on the number of edges, one uses Whitney moves to permute the
X_{i}
, then for each
X_{i}
, treats all the other
X_{j}
as a single edge and applies the hypothesis.

Our next step is to relate generalized cycles to series classes.

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
G
be a
2
-connective and
2
-edge connected graph on at least three vertices, with a strong generalized cycle decomposition
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. Pick an edge
r\in G
. At least one of the following holds.

(1)

For some
X_{i}
, we have that
M(r)\subseteq X_{i}
.

(2)

For each
\ell\in\varepsilon(r)
, there is some
X_{i}
with
X_{i}=\{\ell\}
. Further, no other edge appears as a singleton among the
X_{i}
.

Proof.

Suppose that not all the edges in
\varepsilon(r)
are contained in the same
X_{i}
. Notice that if
\upvarphi:E(G)\to E(G)
is any permutation on
\varepsilon(r)
, and the identity on all other edges, then
\upvarphi
is a cyclic bijection. By
theorem

7.5
, it follows that any
X_{i}
which contains an edge in
\varepsilon(r)
is a singleton, for otherwise we could permute out only a single edge of
\varepsilon(r)
from
X_{i}
. Since removing any two singleton arches (but leaving their tips) disconnects
G
, any two edges appearing as singletons in the decomposition must be in series.
∎

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
\upvarphi:(\hat{G},\hat{e})\to(\hat{H},\hat{w})
be a rigid morphism in
OrCyc
between graphs of genus at least two. Then for every strong generalized cycle decomposition
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, the induced reordering
\tau
of
theorem

7.5
is the identity, except that if some series class has its edges as singletons among the
X_{i}
, then
\tau
may permute them.

Proof.

This follows from
theorem

6.6
and the prior lemma.
∎

The converse is intuitively plausible, but seems more difficult to prove. We leave it as the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.8
.

Let
\upvarphi:(\hat{G},\hat{e})\to(\hat{H},\hat{w})
be a morphism in
OrCyc
between graphs of genus at least two. Suppose that for every strong generalized cycle decomposition
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, the induced reordering
\tau
of
Theorem

7.5
is the identity, except that if some series class has its edges as singletons among the

7.3.
Main Result As A Strengthening of the Existing Graph Torelli Theorem

While as stated our result describes properties of a morphism in
OrCyc
, the existing graph Torelli theorem
Theorem

3.9
begins with an isomorphism of Jacobians and produces isomorphisms of matroids. We now reframe our result in terms of this prior theorem.

We first sketch how the matroid isomorphisms of the existing graph Torelli theorem are produced (for a description of how to do this in practice see
[
17
]
). Begin with an isomorphism
\gamma^{*}:A(H)\to A(G)
. Orient both graphs; in principle the orientations are irrelevant but certain useful orientations simplify the argument. Dualize to obtain an isomorphism
\gamma:J(G)\to J(H)
. This lifts to an isomorphism
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(H,\mathbb{R})
, which sends each
h_{\ell}
to some
\pm h_{a}
. This does not completely specify the desired bijection
\upvarphi
: by
6.4
it only specifies a map
s
from edges of
G
to series classes in
H
. One then proceeds by showing that
s
is well defined on each series class of
G
, and that for each
e
, the sets
\varepsilon(e)
and
s(\varepsilon(e))
actually have the same cardinality. Choosing a bijection for each such pair of series classes then yields a cyclic bijection
\upvarphi
lifting
\gamma
(that is,
\upvarphi_{*}=\gamma
).

We can see from this that lifts are completely specified up to series fixing automorphisms. Putting this together with our results yields the following.

Theorem 7.9
.

(Torelli Theorem for Graphs)
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the category of isomorphisms between metric tori. Let
\gamma:J(G)\to J(H)
be a morphism in
\mathcal{T}
, where
G
and
H
are
2
-connected and
2
-edge connected graphs of genus at least two. Then
\gamma=\upvarphi_{*}
for some
\upvarphi:(\hat{G},\hat{e})\to(\hat{H},\hat{w})
in
OrCyc
. For each such lift
\upvarphi
, we have that
\gamma(\theta_{\hat{e}})=\theta_{\hat{w}}
if and only if
\upvarphi
is an edge isomorphism up to a series fixing automorphism of
(\hat{H},\hat{w})
. If
G
and
H
are
3
-edge connected, then there is a unique lift, and preservation of the discrete theta divisor is equivalent to this lift extending to a graph isomorphism.

7.4.
Main Result As A Lifting Criterion For Isomorphisms of Graphic Matroids

We can also interpret our main result as telling us that we can use rigidity to check when an isomorphism
\phi:M(G)\to M(H)
implies the existence of an honest graph isomorphism.

We will sketch the procedure. Assume
G
and
H
are
2
-connected and
2
-edge connected graphs of genus at least two. Consider any orientations
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
on
G
and
H
, respectively (by
6.8
, the choice is irrelevant). Pick any base edge
\hat{e}_{1}
in
\hat{G}
and set
w:=\phi(e_{1})
to be the base edge of
\hat{H}
. Let
\tilde{\phi}:(G,\Gamma_{1},e)\to(H,\Gamma_{2},w)
be the morphism in
OrCyc
induced by
\phi
and our choices.

Now if
\tilde{\phi}
is not rigid, the failure of rigidity may be influenced by our choice of
e
, for the requirement that the series fixing automorphism on
M(H)
fix
w
can be an obstacle. So let
\varepsilon(w)=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\}
and let
\tau_{i}:(M(H),w)\to(M(H),w_{i})
swap
w
and
w_{i}
. Then one of the compositions

(G,\Gamma_{1},e)\xrightarrow{\tilde{\upvarphi}}(H,\Gamma_{2},w)\xrightarrow{\tilde{\tau}_{i}}(H,\Gamma_{2},w_{i})

is rigid if and only if there exists a series fixing automorphism
\psi:M(H)\to M(H)
such that

M(G)\xrightarrow{\varphi}M(H)\xrightarrow{\psi}M(H)

is an edge isomorphism (note the lack of base elements), and once we detect rigidity, we can reconstruct
\psi
. It would be interesting to see a careful analysis of the computational complexity of this process.

Conjecture 7.10
.

When the chosen base edge
e
is not a series edge, the above process can be carried out in at worst polynomial time in the number of vertices and edges.

7.5.
Fibers of the compactified Torelli map

We will now briefly indicate the context in which our objects arise in algebraic geometry - this is a direction of intended future research for the author.

Let
A_{g}
denote the moduli scheme of principally polarized Abelian varieties of dimension
g
, and
M_{g}
denote the moduli scheme of smooth projective curves of genus
g
. The
Torelli map
, denoted
\tau:M_{g}\to A_{g}
, is induced by the assignment of curves to their Jacobians.

It is known that
M_{g}
admits a compactification
\overline{M}_{g}
, the moduli scheme of Deligne-Mumford stable curves. Alexeev constructed and examined a compactification
\overline{A}^{\text{mod}}_{g}
of
A_{g}
, which parameterizes
principally polarized semi-abelic stable pairs

[
1
]

[
2
]
. Graphs enter naturally into the study of
\overline{M}_{g}
, because points on the boundary can be described as
stable curves
. Speaking nonrigorously, a stable curve
X
is a nicely glued collections of irreducible curves
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
. The
C_{i}
can be taken to be vertices of a graph
\Gamma_{X}
, with edges corresponding to points of intersection. This is referred to as the
dual graph
of the stable curve. Unlike the graphs we have considered above, these may have loops, may have genus one, and may be
1
-connective or
1
-edge connective. To simplify the discussion, we will ignore the existence of loops and graphs of genus one.

We compactify
\tau
, yielding the following commutative diagram.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overline{M}_{g}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overline{A}_{g}^{\text{mod}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M_{g}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A_{g}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\tau}}

A natural question to ask is if the fibers of
\overline{\tau}
can be characterized combinatorially. Caporaso and Viviani proved in
[
16
]
that the fibers can be described as follows. First,
\tau^{-1}(A_{g})
consists of
M_{g}
and all
X
such that
\Gamma_{X}
is a tree. Second, if
X
and
X^{\prime}
are stable curves such that
\Gamma_{X}
and
\Gamma_{X^{\prime}}
are
2
-edge connected, then
\overline{\tau}(X)=\overline{\tau}(X^{\prime})
if and only if
\Gamma_{X}
and
\Gamma_{X^{\prime}}
are strongly cyclically equivalent (we will define this momentarily). The case in which
\Gamma_{X}
is not a tree but has a separating edge is slightly more complex; one removes the separating edge, performs a ‘stabilization’ procedure and arrives at a variant of the above case.

The definition of strong cyclic equivalence is as follows: pick any two edges
e,e^{\prime}
in
\Gamma_{X}
which are in series. Then
\Gamma_{X}\backslash\{e,e^{\prime}\}
has two components
Y_{1},Y_{2}
, each of which is an arch in
\Gamma_{X}
. They are thus both candidates for Whitney moves. Let us call a Whitney move obtained in such a way
admissible
. Then we deem
\Gamma_{X}
and
\Gamma_{X^{\prime}}
to be
strongly cyclically equivalent
if there is a cyclic bijection between them, such that in the classical result
theorem

3.24
, the Whitney moves can all be taken to be admissible. It is easy to see that admissible Whitney moves are sufficient to permute edges within their series class as we like. It is also easy to produce an example of a nonrigid admissible Whitney move.

If
X
,
X^{\prime}
are points in the same fiber of the Torelli map, then choosing an isomorphism of their associated principally polarized semi-abelic stable pairs is almost sufficient to specify a (strong) cyclic bijection between them. The only ambiguity arises from what are, in our terminology, series fixing automorphisms. Since rigidity is independent of such automorphisms, it is independent of this last choice. The author suspects that with care, examining how rigidity varies given choices of isomorphisms of principally polarized semi-abelic stable pairs will shed new light on the structure of the fibers of the Torelli map.
