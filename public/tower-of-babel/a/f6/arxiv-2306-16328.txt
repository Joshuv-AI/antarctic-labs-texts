Nonergodic Brownian oscillator: Low-frequency response
Alex V. Plyukhin

Abstract
An undisturbed Brownian oscillator may not reach thermal equilibrium with the thermal bath due to the formation of a localized normal mode. The latter may emerge when the spectrum of the thermal bath has a finite upper bound ω 0 \omega_{0} and the oscillator natural frequency exceeds a critical value ω c \omega_{c} , which depends on the specific form of the bath spectrum. We consider the response of the oscillator with and without a localized mode to the external periodic force with frequency Ω \Omega lower than ω 0 \omega_{0} . The results complement those obtained earlier for the high-frequency response at Ω ≥ ω 0 \Omega\geq\omega_{0} and require a different mathematical approach. The signature property of the high-frequency response is resonance when the external force frequency Ω \Omega coincides with the frequency of the localized mode ω ∗ \omega_{*} . In the low-frequency domain Ω < ω 0 \Omega<\omega_{0} the condition of resonance Ω = ω ∗ \Omega=\omega_{*} cannot be met (since ω ∗ > ω 0 \omega_{*}>\omega_{0} ). Yet, in the limits ω → ω c \omega\to\omega_{c} and Ω → ω 0 − \Omega\to\omega_{0}^{-} , the oscillator shows a peculiar quasi-resonance response with an amplitude increasing with time sublinearly.



I
Introduction.

Consider a dissipative harmonic oscillator, with the mass
m
and natural frequency
\omega
,
driven by an external ac force
F_{ex}(t)=F_{0}\sin(\Omega t)
, and
described by the generalized
Langevin equation
Zwanzig
;
Weiss

\displaystyle m\,\ddot{x}=-m\,\omega^{2}x-m\int_{0}^{t}K(t-\tau)\,\dot{x}(\tau)\,d\tau+F_{0}\sin(\Omega t)+\xi(t).

(1)

The noise
\xi(t)
is zero-centered,
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
, and
connected with the dissipation kernel
K(t)
by the standard fluctuation-dissipation relation

\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=mk_{B}TK(t-t^{\prime})
.
Comparing to its Markovian (time-local) counterpart

\displaystyle m\,\ddot{x}=-m\,\omega^{2}x-m\,\gamma\,\dot{x}+F_{0}\sin(\Omega t)+\xi(t),

(2)

the generalized Langevin equation (
1
) may hold
on the time scale
comparable with the relaxation time of the thermal bath.
The latter is important for systems
with a broad hierarchy of relevant time scales, e.g. for biomolecules
in aqueous solution
Xie1
;
Xie2
;
Goychuk
.

Some types of dissipation kernels
K(t)
in Eq. (
1
) are of particular interest.
One special type, relevant to a broad class of open systems
Weiss
;
Plyukhin_frac
, includes power-law kernels

K(t)\sim t^{-\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1
. Generalized Langevin equations with such kernels,
and also open systems they describe, are often referred to as fractional.
For fractional Langevin equations
the integral representing the dissipation coefficient

\gamma=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(t)\,dt
diverges and therefore the Markovian approximation

K(t)\to 2\gamma\delta(t)
does not exist.
Relaxation and response properties of the fractional oscillator were
studied in
Barkai1
;
Barkai2
. Note, however, that there are other definitions
of a fractional oscillator in the literature
Achar
;
Stanislav
;
Ryabov
.

In this paper we consider a kernel
K(t)
of another special type for which the spectrum

\rho(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(t)\cos(\omega t)dt
has a finite upper bound
\omega_{0}
.
The latter can be viewed as a highest frequency of
fictitious oscillators the thermal bath is comprised of.
Solutions of the generalized Langevin equation with such kernels
may be qualitatively different from those of the corresponding
Markovian equations.
A finite value of the cutoff frequency
\omega_{0}
is
known to be a condition for the formation of a localized normal mode
with frequency
\omega_{*}
outside of the bath spectrum,
\omega_{*}>\omega_{0}

Montroll
;
Takeno
;
Kashiwamura
;
Rubin
.
The localized mode does not exchange energy with the thermal bath and behaves as an
isolated oscillator. As a result,
an open system with a localized mode does not reach thermal
equilibrium (does not thermalize).
Instead, it evolves into a cyclostationary state where dynamical variables and correlations, averaged
over an ensemble, oscillates in time with frequency of the localized mode
\omega_{*}
. Cyclostationary states are not stationary and therefore are manifestly nonergodic (ensemble and time averages are not equal). Following Refs.
Plyukhin
;
Plyukhin2
, we shall refer to an open oscillator with a localized mode as nonergodic.

Relaxation properties of nonergodic Brownian oscillators
in the absence of an external driving force (
F_{0}=0
)
were studied in
Kemeny
;
Dhar
;
Plyukhin
. Response properties to the external
harmonic force were addressed in Ref.
Plyukhin2

for the case when the external frequency is higher than or equal to the spectrum cutoff frequency,
\Omega\geq\omega_{0}
.
That domain is of particular interest because it covers setups
when the external frequency equals the frequency of the localized mode

\Omega=\omega_{*}
(recall that
\omega_{*}>\omega_{0}
).
In that case the system shows unbounded resonance
as for an isolated oscillator with the natural frequency
\omega_{*}
.
Another conspicuous regime occurs
when the oscillator natural frequency
\omega
takes a critical value
\omega_{c}

separating ergodic and nonergodic configurations
(without and with a localized mode, respectively).
In that case
the cutoff frequency
\omega_{0}
has the meaning of the frequency of the
incipient localized mode,
\omega_{0}=\lim_{\omega\to\omega_{c}}\omega_{*}
.
For the setup
(\omega=\omega_{c},\,\Omega=\omega_{0})
the oscillator shows
an unusual resonance (“quasi-resonance”) when the amplitude of the response

q(t)=\langle x(t)\rangle
increases with time sublinearly.

The goal of this paper is to extend the analysis of Ref.
Plyukhin2
for
\Omega\geq\omega_{0}

to the low-frequency domain
\Omega<\omega_{0}
.
Since the localized mode frequency
\omega_{*}

is higher than
\omega_{0}
, the domain
\Omega<\omega_{0}
does not include
resonance and quasi-resonance setups mentioned above.
Still, the low-frequency response may be of interest from both theoretical and experimental points of view. It requires a separate consideration because
the expressions obtained in Ref.
Plyukhin2
for
\Omega\geq\omega_{0}
involve integrals which diverge for
\Omega<\omega_{0}
. We will show that the results of Ref.
Plyukhin2
can be extended for the low-frequency domain merely by defining relevant improper integrals in the sense of Cauchy principal value.
That little correction, however, leads to the significant difference
in the explicit expressions for the low- and high-frequency responses.

In order to make the paper self-consistent, in sections II and III we recapitulate the model and special solutions, including quasi-resonance.
That sections overlap with Ref.
Plyukhin2
.
In section IV we show that the results obtained for the high-frequency response cannot be directly applied for the low-frequency domain
and suggest an
ad hoc
remedy for the extension.
An alternative technique, addressing the low-frequency response, is developed in sections
V and VI.

II
Model

As in Ref.
Plyukhin2
, we consider a Brownian oscillator described by the generalized
Langevin equation (
1
) with a specific dissipation kernel

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{4}\,[J_{0}(\omega_{0}t)+J_{2}(\omega_{0}t)]=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\,\frac{J_{1}(\omega_{0}t)}{t},

(3)

where
J_{n}(x)
’s are Bessel functions of the first kind.
The kernel has the absolute maximum at
t=0
and for
t>0

it oscillates with an amplitude decaying with time as
t^{-3/2}
.
The kernel’s spectral density
\rho(\nu)
has the upper bound
\omega_{0}
,

\displaystyle\rho(\nu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}K(t)\cos(\nu t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\,\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\nu^{2}}\,\,\theta(\omega_{0}-\nu).

(4)

Here
\theta(x)
is the step function. As was mentioned in the
Introduction, a finite upper bound of the bath’s spectrum
is a condition of the formation of a localized mode. The frequency of the latter

\omega_{*}
is not explicitly present in Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
), and can be viewed
as a hidden parameter of the model.

The generalized Langevin equation with kernel (
3
) describes
dynamics of the terminal atom of a semi-infinite harmonic chain,
when the atom
is subjected, in addition to the force from the neighbour atom,
to an external harmonic potential and a time-periodic force.
The rest of the chain
plays the role of a thermal bath.
The setting can be considered as an extension
of archetypal Rubin’s model
Weiss
;
Rubin
. Although our choice of the dissipation kernel (
3
)
is somewhat accidental, it appears to be relevant in a broad context of
the dynamics of low-dimensional systems with localized modes
Chalopin
.
One may expect that our model reflects some general features of systems and processes
whose spectral densities have finite upper bounds.

Taking average of Eq. (
1
), one gets
for the average coordinate

\displaystyle q(t)=\langle x(t)\rangle,

(5)

which we shall refer below as the response,
the deterministic equation

\displaystyle\ddot{q}=-\omega^{2}q-\int_{0}^{t}K(t-\tau)\,\dot{q}(\tau)d\tau+\frac{F_{0}}{m}\sin(\Omega t).

(6)

We assume that the external force starts to act at
t=0
and that at earlier times the oscillator is in thermal equilibrium. The relevant initial conditions are zero,
q(0)=\dot{q}(0)=0
. Then the
solution of Eq. (
6
) in the Laplace domain is

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(s)=\frac{1}{m}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,\tilde{F}_{ex}(s)

(7)

where the Laplace transforms of the Green’s function
G(t)
and the external force
F_{ex}(t)=F_{0}\,\sin(\Omega t)

are

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}+s\,\tilde{K}(s)+\omega^{2}},\quad\tilde{F}_{ex}(s)=F_{0}\,\frac{\Omega}{s^{2}+\Omega^{2}}.

(8)

In the time domain the solution has the form of the convolution

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{F_{0}}{m}\,\int_{0}^{t}G(t-\tau)\,\sin(\Omega\,\tau)\,d\tau.

(9)

One can show, see e.g.
Plyukhin
, that the Green’s function
G(t)
defined by Eq. (
8
) also has the meaning of a
normalized correlation function
\langle x(0)\dot{x}(t)\rangle
. Then Eq. (
9
) can be viewed as a Kubo relation, which expresses
a response in terms of an equilibrium correlation function.

There are two ways to proceed. In the first way, which we shall follow in Sec. IV,
the response is evaluated in the time domain using Eq. (
9
).
This method has the advantage that
for the given model the Green’s function in the time domain
G(t)
is already known
Plyukhin
.
In the second way, one finds the response by evaluating the inverse Laplace transform
of the right-hand side of Eq. (
7
).
Although the two methods are clearly equivalent,
we shall see that for the low-frequency response at
\Omega<\omega_{0}
there are technical reasons
to prefer one method over the other.

It was shown in
Plyukhin
that
for the given model the Green’s function in the time domain
G(t)

takes the form

\displaystyle G(t)=\begin{cases}G_{e}(t),&\text{if $\omega\leq\omega_{c}$},\\
G_{e}(t)+G_{0}\,\sin(\omega_{*}t),&\text{ if $\omega>\omega_{c}$}.\end{cases}

(10)

Here the critical oscillator frequency

\displaystyle\omega_{c}=\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.707\,\omega_{0}

(11)

separates ergodic (thermalizing) configurations with
\omega\leq\omega_{c}
and nonergodic (non-thermalizing) configurations with
\omega>\omega_{c}
.
The ergodic component
G_{e}(t)
has the same form for
\omega\leq\omega_{c}
and for
\omega>\omega_{c}
,

\displaystyle G_{e}(t)=\frac{4}{\pi\omega_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sin(x\,\omega_{0}\,t)\,x\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx}{(1-4\,\lambda^{2})\,x^{2}+4\,\lambda^{4}},

(12)

where
\lambda
denotes the dimentionless oscillator frequency in units of
\omega_{0}
,

\displaystyle\lambda=\omega/\omega_{0}.

(13)

Note that in Ref.
Plyukhin

\lambda
stands for
(\omega/\omega_{0})^{2}
.
For two values of the oscillator frequency,
\omega=\omega_{0}/2
and
\omega=\omega_{c}
,
Eq. (
12
) reduces to more explicit forms in terms Bessel functions,
see Eqs. (
15
)-(
16
) below.

One can show that
G_{e}(t)
, given by Eq. (
12
), vanishes at long times for all values of
\omega
(or
\lambda
).
Then for
\omega\leq\omega_{c}
the Green’s function
G(t)=G_{e}(t)
goes at long times to zero too. Since
G(t)
also has the meaning of the normalized correlation

\langle x(0)\dot{x}(t)\rangle

Plyukhin
, the asymptotic vanishing of
G(t)

means that the initial correlations die out and the system
approaches thermal equilibrium.

Eq. (
10
) shows that the localized mode with frequency
\omega_{*}
develops for

\omega>\omega_{c}
.
The localized mode amplitude
G_{0}
and frequency
\omega_{*}

of the nonergodic component
are given by the following expressions
Plyukhin

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\,G_{0}=\frac{8\,\lambda^{2}-4}{(4\,\lambda^{2}-1)^{3/2}},\qquad\omega_{*}=\frac{2\,\lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{4\lambda^{2}-1}}\,\,\omega_{0}.

(14)

Note that
G_{0}=0
and the nonergodic component vanishes for
\omega=\omega_{c}
(for
\lambda=\lambda_{c}=\omega_{c}/\omega_{0}=1/\sqrt{2}
). Therefore

q(t)
as a function of
\omega
is continuous
including the critical point
\omega=\omega_{c}
.
For configurations with
\omega>\omega_{c}
, with the localized mode developed, the Green’s function and initial correlations do not vanish
at long times. Instead of evolving toward thermal equilibrium, the oscillator in the course of time
reaches the cyclostationary state where the means and correlations oscillate with
the frequency of the localized mode
\omega_{*}
.

III
Special cases and quasi-resonance

For the two values of the oscillator frequency,
\omega=\omega_{0}/2
and

\omega=\omega_{c}
,
the inverse transform
of Eq. (
35
) can be compactly expressed in terms of Bessel functions,

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\omega_{0}^{2}t}\,J_{2}(\omega_{0}t)=\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}\,[J_{1}(\omega_{0}t)+J_{3}(\omega_{0}t)],\qquad\omega=\omega_{0}/2,

(15)

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}\,J_{1}(\omega_{0}t),\qquad\omega=\omega_{c}=\omega_{0}/\sqrt{2}.

(16)

These expressions can also be obtained from Eq. (
12
)
using
the integral forms of the Bessel functions.
Both special frequencies
\omega_{0}/2
and
\omega_{c}

correspond to ergodic configurations: The Green’s function
vanishes at long times,
and the oscillator evolves to thermal equilibrium.
However, one observes that for
\omega=\omega_{c}
the Green’s function decays slower,
which can be viewed as a precursor of the formation of the localized mode for
\omega>\omega_{c}
.

Substituting Eqs. (
15
) and (
16
) into Eq. (
9
) yields

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}}\,\int_{0}^{t}[J_{1}(\omega_{0}\tau)+J_{3}(\omega_{0}t)]\,\sin[\Omega\,(t-\tau)]\,dt,\quad\omega=\omega_{0}/2,

(17)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}}\,\int_{0}^{t}J_{1}(\omega_{0}\tau)\,\sin[\Omega\,(t-\tau)]\,dt,\quad\omega=\omega_{c}.

(18)

We can evaluate these convolution integrals analytically only for
\Omega=\omega_{0}
.
Of particular interest is the setting
(\omega=\omega_{c},\,\Omega=\omega_{0})
, when
Eq. (
18
) takes the form

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{2F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}}\,\int_{0}^{t}J_{1}(\omega_{0}\tau)\,\sin[\omega_{0}\,(t-\tau)]\,dt,\qquad(\omega=\omega_{c},\,\Omega=\omega_{0}).

(19)

Taking into account the convolution integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}J_{1}(y)\,\sin(x-y)\,dy=\sin(x)-xJ_{0}(x),

(20)

see Ref.
Watson
and the Appendix in Ref.
Plyukhin2
, one finds
Plyukhin2

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{2\,F_{0}}{m\,\omega_{0}^{2}}\,\Big\{\sin(\omega_{0}t)-\omega_{0}t\,J_{0}(\omega_{0}t)\Big\},\qquad(\omega=\omega_{c},\,\Omega=\omega_{0}).

(21)

Here the first term is the anticipated steady-state solution
oscillating with the frequency of the driving force
\Omega=\omega_{0}
.
The second term, however, is neither stationary nor transient.
It oscillates with an amplitude
increasing indefinitely in time as
\sqrt{t}
.
Following Ref.
Plyukhin2
we refer to such
response as quasi-resonance.
It can be viewed as
a resonance between the external harmonic force
and the incipient
localized mode in the critical configuration with
\omega=\omega_{c}
.
Note that according Eq. (
14
) the cutoff frequency
\omega_{0}
also has the meaning of the frequency of the incipient localized mode,
\lim_{\omega\to\omega_{c}}\omega_{*}=\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{c}}\omega_{*}=\omega_{0}
.

The second configuration, for which the response can be expressed in a compact form, is

(\omega=\omega_{0}/2,\,\Omega=\omega_{0})
. In that case
Eq. (
17
) reads

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{2F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}}\,\int_{0}^{t}[J_{1}(\omega_{0}\tau)+J_{3}(\omega_{0}t)]\,\sin[\omega_{0}\,(t-\tau)]\,dt.

(22)

Taking into account integral (
20
) and its generalization
Bailey

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}J_{3}(y)\,\sin(x-y)\,dy=-x\,J_{2}(x)+6J_{1}(x)-3\sin(x)=x\,J_{0}(x)+4J_{1}(x)-3\sin(x)

(23)

(here in the second equality we use the recurrence relation
\frac{2n}{x}J_{n}=J_{n-1}+J_{n+1}
with
n=1
), yields

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{4\,F_{0}}{m\,\omega_{0}^{2}}\,\Big\{\sin(\omega_{0}t-\pi)+2J_{1}(\omega_{0}t)\Big\},\qquad(\omega=\omega_{0}/2,\,\Omega=\omega_{0}).

(24)

The structure of this solution is similar to that
for the normal damped and fractional oscillators.
The first term is the steady-state solution which oscillates with the frequency of the external
force
\Omega=\omega_{0}
.
The second term is a transient which vanishes at long times.
One notable feature is a slow decay of the transient term;
its amplitude decays as
1/\sqrt{t}
. Another remarkable feature is that
the phase shift
\pi
of the steady-state response is the same as for an isolated (undamped)
oscillator when
\Omega>\omega

French
.
Recall that
for the normal damped oscillator, described by Eq. (
2
), the phase shift reaches the value
\pi
only in the limit
\Omega\to\infty

French
.

For
\Omega\neq\omega_{0}
the integral
expressions (
17
) and (
18
)
apparently cannot be further simplified and may be used to evaluate the response numerically. In that way,
for the ergodic configuration with
\omega=\omega_{0}/2
we find that
q(t)

evolves into a steady-state form

\displaystyle q(t)\to A\,\sin(\Omega t-\delta)

(25)

with the amplitude
A>0
weakly decreasing with increasing
\Omega
,
and the phase shift
\delta
monotonically increasing from
0
for small
\Omega

to
\pi
for
\Omega\gg\omega
.
There is no resonance when
\Omega
is close to
\omega
, and
on the whole, the response is qualitatively similar
to that of the overdamped normal damped oscillator, described by
Eq. (
2
) with
\gamma>\omega

French
.

For the critical configuration with
\omega=\omega_{c}
, the response, described by Eq. (
18
), may show a variety of patterns.
For
\Omega=\omega_{0}
the response has the quasi-resonance form (
21
) and never reaches
the steady state (
25
). When
|\Omega/\omega_{0}-1|
is finite but small, the response evolves at long times
to the steady state (
25
), but at shorter times shows beats. The duration and amplitude of the first beat increase when
\Omega
is getting closer to
\omega_{0}
and diverge in the limit
\Omega\to\omega_{0}
.
When
\Omega
is not close to
\omega_{0}
the response quickly reaches the steady state form (
25
),
where the amplitude weakly depends on
\Omega
for
\Omega<\omega_{0}
and decreases with increasing
\Omega
when

\Omega>\omega_{0}
.

IV
Ad hoc approach

Consider general ergodic configurations with
\omega\leq\omega_{c}
.
The localized mode is absent,
the Green’s function has only ergodic component,
G(t)=G_{e}(t)
, and
the response, according to Eq. (
9
), is

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{F_{0}}{m}\,\int_{0}^{t}G_{e}(t-\tau)\,\sin(\Omega\,\tau)\,d\tau.

(26)

Substituting Eq. (
12
) for
G_{e}(t)
into Eq. (
26
),
changing the integration order,
and integrating over
\tau
yields

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\Big\{B\,\sin(\Omega t)+\varphi(t)\Big\},

(27)

where the dimensionless amplitude
B
and the transient
\varphi(t)
are

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx}{\Big[(1-4\lambda^{2})\,x^{2}+4\lambda^{4}\Big]\left(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2}\right)},

(28)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\Lambda}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\sin(x\,\omega_{0}\,t)\,dx}{\Big[(1-4\lambda^{2})\,x^{2}+4\lambda^{4}\Big]\left(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2}\right)}.

(29)

In these expressions
\lambda
and
\Lambda
denote the dimensionless oscillator and external force frequencies
in units of the cutoff frequency
\omega_{0}
,

\displaystyle\lambda=\omega/\omega_{0},\qquad\Lambda=\Omega/\omega_{0}.

(30)

One observes that
Eqs. (
27
)-(
29
) make sense, and have been used in Ref.
Plyukhin2
,
only for the high-frequency response at
\Omega\geq\omega_{0}
, or
\Lambda\geq 1
.
In the low-frequency domain
\Omega<\omega_{0}
, or
\Lambda<1
,
the integrands in Eqs.
(
28
) and (
29
) are singular at
x=\Lambda
and the integrals diverge.

Recall that in deriving the above equations we substituted integral (
12
) over
x
into
integral (
26
) over
\tau
and then, in a rather cavalier manner,
changed the integration order
\int\!d\tau\int\!dx\to\int\!dx\int\!d\tau
.
The conditions of that last operation, regulated by Fubini’s theorem, are not satisfied for
\Lambda<1

since the integrand is not continuous in the integration domain.

However, one notices that the integrals (
28
) and (
29
) converge in Cauchy’s principal value sense:

\displaystyle\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\Lambda-\epsilon}f(x)\,dx+\int_{\Lambda+\epsilon}^{1}f(x)\,dx\right\}.

(31)

This suggests that
Eqs. (
27
)-(
29
) are valid for the low-frequency response as well,
if one interpret the integral expressions for
B
and
\varphi(t)

in the principal value sense,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx}{\Big[(1-4\lambda^{2})\,x^{2}+4\lambda^{4}\Big]\left(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2}\right)},

(32)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\Lambda}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\sin(x\,\omega_{0}\,t)\,dx}{\Big[(1-4\lambda^{2})\,x^{2}+4\lambda^{4}\Big]\left(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2}\right)}.

(33)

Whereas this guess seems reasonable, it is rather
ad hoc
. In the next section we will justify Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
)
with another method, briefly mentioned at the end of Sec. II. In that method the
principal value of the integral for
\varphi(t)
emerges naturally,
and the expression for
B
has a more explicit form, see Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
) below.

V
Response of ergodic configurations

An alternative method to evaluate
the response is to start with Eq. (
7
) for its Laplace transform

\tilde{q}(s)
and to perform the inversion
q(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\text{Br}}e^{st}\tilde{q}(s)\,ds
,
which gives

\displaystyle q(t)=\frac{F_{0}\Omega}{m}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\int_{\text{Br}}\frac{e^{st}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,ds}{(s+i\Omega)(s-i\Omega)}.

(34)

Here
Br
denotes the Bromwich path in the complex plane
(a vertical line to the right of all singularities of the
integrand) and the transform of the Green’s function is

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(s)=\frac{2}{s^{2}+s\,\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}+2\,\omega^{2}}.

(35)

This expression follows from Eq. (
8
) and
the Laplace transform of the kernel (
3
)

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\,\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}-s\right).

(36)

As shown in Ref.
Plyukhin
,
for ergodic configurations with
\omega\leq\omega_{c}

the only singularities of the Green’s function
\tilde{G}(s)

are two branch points at
\pm i\omega_{0}
.
Two other singularities of the integrand in Eq. (
34
)
are simple poles at
\pm i\Omega
.
Since all singular points are on the imaginary axis, contour
Br
in Eq. (
34
)
is an arbitrary vertical line to the right of the origin.

The evaluation of the integral (
34
) can be performed
with the standard technique based on the Cauchy’s integral and residue theorems,
but there are two subtleties. The first is that the poles lie on the branch cut, the second is that
\tilde{G}(s)
has two branches. Below we outline the main steps of the evaluation.

The first step is to close the Bromwich contour as shown in Fig. 1 (left) and to consider the integral
I
over the resulting closed contour
\Gamma

\displaystyle I=\frac{F_{0}\Omega}{m}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\int_{\Gamma}\frac{e^{st}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,ds}{(s+i\Omega)(s-i\Omega)},

(37)

assuming
the limit when the radius
R

of the large arc
is infinitely large,
R\to\infty
,
and the radii
\epsilon
of the small (semi-) circles about the branch points
\pm i\omega_{0}

and poles
\pm i\Omega
are infinitely small,
\epsilon\to 0
.
The contribution to
I
from the arc vanishes, the contributions from the
line above and below the real axis cancel each other, and the contribution
from the rightmost vertical line equals
q(t)
. Then we get

\displaystyle I=q(t)+I_{C},

(38)

where
I_{C}

is the contribution from the contour

C
depicted in Fig. 1 (right). On the other hand, since there are no singular points
in the interior of
\Gamma
, Cauchy’s integral theorem asserts that
I=0
, and therefore

\displaystyle q(t)=-I_{C}.

(39)

Left: Integration contour
\Gamma
for integral
I
in Eq. (
37
). Right: Integration contour
C
for integral
I_{C}
in Eqs. (
38
) and (
45
)

To proceed,
one has to take into account
that the Green’s function (
35
) involves
the two-branch function
f(s)=\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega^{2}}
. To choose the branch which is
physically meaningful, one may recall
that the Green’s function
G(t)
also determines
the equilibrium correlation
\langle q(0)\dot{q}(t)\rangle

Plyukhin

\displaystyle\langle q(0)\dot{q}(t)\rangle=\frac{k_{B}T}{m}\,G(t).

(40)

That implies the initial condition
G(0)=0
, which in the Laplace domain has the form

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}s\,\tilde{G}(s)=0.

(41)

One can show that this condition is only satisfied when
\tilde{G}(s)
involves the branch of

f(s)=\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega^{2}}
which takes real positive (negative) values when
s

is real and positive (negative).
Explicitly, the physical branch of the function

\displaystyle f(s)=\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}=\sqrt{(s-i\omega_{0})(s+i\omega_{0})}

(42)

is convenient to describe using a pair of polar coordinates
(r_{1},\theta_{1})
and
(r_{2},\theta_{2})

with the origins
at
s=i\omega_{0}
and
s=-i\omega_{0}
, respectively:

\displaystyle f(s)=\sqrt{r_{1}r_{2}}\,\exp\left(i\,\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}}{2}\right).

(43)

For the physical branch the polar angles may be chosen from the same interval

\displaystyle-\frac{3\pi}{2}<\theta_{1}\leq\frac{\pi}{2},\quad-\frac{3\pi}{2}<\theta_{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.

(44)

For instance, for
s=x
being real and positive we get
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=0
, then
f(s)=\sqrt{r_{1}r_{2}}
is real and positive.

To evaluate the integral

\displaystyle I_{C}=\frac{F_{0}\Omega}{m}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\int_{C}\frac{e^{st}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,ds}{(s+i\Omega)(s-i\Omega)}

(45)

we
split it into five contributions

\displaystyle I_{C}=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}+I_{5},

(46)

corresponding to the regions
C_{i}
of the integration path, see Fig. 1 (right).
Contributions
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
come from the integration
over circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
of radius
\epsilon

about the branch points
\pm i\omega_{0}
.
One finds that the integrals are of order of
\epsilon
for
\omega\neq\omega_{c}
, and
of order of
\sqrt{\epsilon}
for
\omega=\omega_{c}
. Thus
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
for any
\omega

vanish when
\epsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle I_{1}=I_{2}=0.

(47)

Next consider the integrals
I_{3},I_{4}
over the semi-circles
C_{3}^{\pm},C_{4}^{\pm}
of radius
\epsilon
about
s=\pm i\Omega
.
Using Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
), for
s\in C_{3}^{\pm},C_{4}^{\pm}
in the limit
\epsilon\to 0

one finds

\displaystyle f(s)=\pm\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\Omega^{2}},

(48)

where the sign is positive for the right semi-circles and negative for left semi-circles.
The Green’s function (
35
) for
s\in C_{3}^{\pm},C_{4}^{\pm}
takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(s)=\frac{2}{2\omega^{2}-\Omega^{2}\pm i\Omega\,\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\Omega^{2}}},

(49)

where again the plus and minus in the denominator correspond
to the right and left semi-circles, respectively.
Then the straightforward evaluation gives

\displaystyle I_{3}+I_{4}=-\frac{F_{0}}{m}\,\frac{2(2\omega^{2}-\Omega^{2})}{4\omega^{4}+\Omega^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-4\omega^{2})}\,\sin(\Omega t).

(50)

The last contribution
I_{5}=I_{5}^{+}+I_{5}^{-}
comes from the vertical segments
C_{5}^{+}
and
C_{5}^{-}

along the right and left shores of the
branch cut, excluding the
2\epsilon
-wide intervals about
\pm i\Omega
.
In that case

s=iy\pm\delta
, where
\delta
is infinitesimal and

\displaystyle y\in(-\omega_{0}+\epsilon,-\Omega-\epsilon)\cup(-\Omega+\epsilon,\Omega-\epsilon)\cup(\Omega+\epsilon,\omega_{0}-\epsilon).

(51)

For
s\in C_{5}^{\pm}
one finds
f(s)=\sqrt{s^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}=\pm\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-y^{2}}
, and the Green’s function (
35
)
takes the form

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(s)=\frac{2}{2\omega^{2}-y^{2}\pm iy\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-y^{2}}},

(52)

where the plus and minus correspond to
C_{5}^{+}
and
C_{5}^{-}
,
respectively. Taking that into account we obtain

\displaystyle I_{5}=-\frac{F_{0}\,\Omega}{m}\,\frac{4}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{\omega_{0}}\frac{y\,\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-y^{2}}\,\sin(yt)\,dy}{[4\omega^{4}+y^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-4\omega^{2})]\,(\Omega^{2}-y^{2})}.

(53)

The principal value of the integral emerges here
quite naturally
since the integration region (
51
)
has symmetric gaps about
\pm\Omega
.

Summing up contributions yields

\displaystyle I_{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\left\{B\,\sin(\Omega t)+\varphi(t)\right\},

(54)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\omega_{0}^{2}(2\omega^{2}-\Omega^{2})}{4\omega^{4}+(\omega_{0}^{2}-4\omega^{2})\Omega^{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\Omega\,\omega_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{\omega_{0}}\frac{y\,\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-y^{2}}\,\sin(yt)\,dy}{[4\omega^{4}+y^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-4\omega^{2})]\,(\Omega^{2}-y^{2})}.

(56)

Finally, using Eq. (
39
) and expressing
B
and
\varphi(t)
in terms of dimensionless frequencies
\lambda=\omega/\omega_{0}
and
\Lambda=\Omega/\omega_{0}
we get

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\Big\{B\,\sin(\Omega t)+\varphi(t)\Big\},

(57)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\,(2\lambda^{2}-\Lambda^{2})}{4\lambda^{4}+(1-4\lambda^{2})\,\Lambda^{2}},

(58)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\Lambda}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\sin(x\omega_{0}t)\,dx}{[4\lambda^{4}+x^{2}(1-4\lambda^{2})]\,(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2})}.

(59)

These results, which hold for
\omega<\omega_{c}
and
\Omega<\omega_{0}
(or, for
\lambda<\lambda_{c}
and
\Lambda<1
), are equivalent to Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
)
obtained in the previous section with method I, but Eq. (
58
) for
B
is more explicit,
and the principle value in the integral expression for
\varphi(t)

is now totally justified, see the remark after Eq. (
53
).

VI
Response of nonergodic configurations

Consider nonergodic configurations with
\omega>\omega_{c}
.
The Green’s function has now both ergodic and nonergodic (periodic) components,

G(t)=G_{e}(t)+G_{0}\,\sin(\omega_{*}t)
. We can follow the same approach as in the previous section, but taking into account that
the Green’s function
\tilde{G}(s)
in the Laplace domain now
has two extra poles at

\pm i\omega_{*}
. Since
\omega_{*}>\omega_{0}
,
the integral
I
given by Eq. (
37
) is not zero but equals to the sum of residues at
s=\pm i\omega_{*}
,

\displaystyle I=Res[e^{st}\,\tilde{q}(s),i\omega_{*}]+Res[e^{st}\,\tilde{q}(s),-i\omega_{*}],

(61)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(s)=\frac{1}{m}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,\tilde{F}_{ex}(s)=\frac{1}{m}\,\tilde{G}(s)\,\frac{\Omega}{s^{2}+\Omega^{2}}.

(62)

On the other hand
I=q(t)+I_{C}
, see Eq. (
38
),
and therefore

\displaystyle q(t)=-I_{C}+Res[e^{st}\,\tilde{q}(s),i\omega_{*}]+Res[e^{st}\,\tilde{q}(s),-i\omega_{*}].

(63)

Here the integral
I_{C}
is still given by Eq. (
54
)
and the sum of residues can be written as

\displaystyle Res[e^{st}\tilde{q}(s),i\omega_{*}]+Res[e^{st}\tilde{q}(s),-i\omega_{*}]=\frac{F_{0}\Omega}{m\,(\Omega^{2}-\omega_{*}^{2})}\,\Big\{Res[e^{st}\tilde{G}(s),i\omega_{*}]+Res[e^{st}\tilde{G}(s),i\omega_{*}]\Big\}.

(64)

The sum of residues of the Green’s function can be readily
evaluated (see Appendix B in
Plyukhin
for
\mu=1
), or one may just notice that
it gives the
nonergodic part of the Green’s function
G_{0}\sin(\omega_{*}t)
,
which gives

\displaystyle Res[e^{st}\tilde{q}(s),i\omega_{*}]+Res[e^{st}\tilde{q}(s),-i\omega_{*}]=\frac{F_{0}\Omega}{m\,(\Omega^{2}-\omega_{*}^{2})}\,G_{0}\,\sin(\omega_{*}t).

(65)

Combining the results and expressing in terms of dimensionless frequencies,
we obtain

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\Big\{B\sin(\Omega t)+C\,\sin(\omega_{*}t)+\varphi(t)\Big\},

(66)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\,(2\lambda^{2}-\Lambda^{2})}{4\lambda^{4}+(1-4\lambda^{2})\,\Lambda^{2}},

(67)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{0}G_{0}\,\frac{\Lambda}{\Lambda^{2}-\lambda_{*}^{2}}=-\frac{4\Lambda(2\,\lambda^{2}-1)}{\sqrt{4\,\lambda^{2}-1}\,(4\lambda^{4}+\Lambda^{2}-4\lambda^{2}\Lambda^{2})},

(68)

\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\Lambda}{\pi}\,\text{p.v.}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\sin(x\omega_{0}t)\,dx}{(\Lambda^{2}-x^{2})[4\lambda^{4}+x^{2}(1-4\lambda^{2})]}.

(69)

Here, in the first equation for
C
,
\lambda_{*}
stands for the dimensionless localized frequency

\lambda_{*}=\omega_{*}/\omega_{0}
and
G_{0}
is the amplitude of the nonergodic part of the Green’s function, both are given by Eq. (
14
).

Compared to the response of ergodic configurations, solution (

\frac{F_{0}}{m\omega_{0}^{2}}\,C\,\sin(\omega_{*}t)
. The term

\varphi(t)
at long times still oscillates with frequency

Therefore, the asymptotic long-time response of nonergodic configurations does not oscillate with the frequency of the external force
\Omega
but has the form of the superposition of two harmonic terms with
frequencies
\Omega
and
\omega_{*}
,

\displaystyle q(t)\to A\,\sin(\Omega t-\delta)+C\,\sin(\omega_{*}t),

In the considered domain
(\omega>\omega_{c},\,\Omega<\omega_{0})
the frequencies

\omega_{*}
and
\Omega
are separated,
\omega_{*}>\omega_{0}
and
\Omega<\omega_{0}
,
yet may be close. In that cases the asymptotic solution (

)
shows beats which, in contrast to ergodic configurations,
do not vanish at long times but continue indefinitely.
When
\omega\to\omega_{c}^{+}
and
\Omega\to\omega_{0}^{-}
the response
converges to the quasi-resonance form (
21
), see Fig. 3.

VII
Conclusion

The nonergodic Brownian oscillator considered in this paper is a quite unusual open system. Its signature properties are the luck of thermalization (when unperturbed)
and unbounded resonance and quasi-resonance response
when the frequency of the external force
\Omega
coincides with the frequency of the localized mode
\omega_{*}
. In this paper we considered the low-frequency
response at
\Omega<\omega_{0}
, where
\omega_{0}
is the upper bound of the thermal bath’s spectrum. In that case the two frequencies
\Omega
and
\omega_{*}

are separated,
\Omega<\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{*}\geq\omega_{0}
, and the condition of resonance cannot be met.
However, for the critical and near-to-critical configurations (
\omega
is equal or close to
\omega_{c}
) the localized frequency
\omega_{*}
is
close to
\omega_{0}
, and the frequencies
\Omega
and
\omega_{*}
may be close even for the low-frequency domain
\Omega<\omega_{0}
. In such case
the low-frequency response may show a quasi-resonance response, with the amplitude increasing with time sublinearly, on a finite but long time interval.

Comparing the results with those obtained in Ref.
Plyukhin
,
we found the response
to be a continuous function of the external frequency, including the point
\Omega=\omega_{0}
.
Although for
\Omega<\omega_{0}
and for
\Omega\geq\omega_{0}
the response is given by different expressions, they match at
\Omega=\omega_{0}
.
