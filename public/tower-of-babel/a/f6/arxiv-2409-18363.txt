Quantitative Expansivity for ergodic ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} -actions
Alexander Fish and Sean Skinner

Abstract
. We study expansiveness properties of positive measure subsets of ergodic ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} -actions along two different types of structured subsets of ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , namely, cyclic subgroups and images of integer polynomials. We prove quantitative expansiveness properties in both cases and strengthen combinatorial results obtained by Björklund and Fish in [ 3 ] and Bulinski and Fish in [ 6 ] . Our methods unify and strengthen earlier approaches used in [ 3 ] and [ 6 ] and to our surprise, also yield a counterexample to a certain pinned variant of the polynomial Bogolyubov theorem.



1.
Introduction

An influential result of Furstenberg, Katznelson and Weiss
[
9
]
states that if
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
has positive upper density with respect to the Lebesgue measure
m
, i.e.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{m(A\cap[-N,N]^{2})}{m([-N,N]^{2})}>0,

then the set of all distances between pairs of points in
A
satisfies

[m_{0},\infty)\subset\{|x-y|\,:\,x,y\in A\}

for some
m_{0}=m_{0}(A)>0
. In
[
11
]
Magyar established a discrete analogue of this result for sets of positive upper Banach density in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Recall that the upper Banach density of a set
E\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined to be

d^{*}(E):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{|E\cap(Q_{N}+t)|}{|Q_{N}|}

where
Q_{N}:=[-N,N]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Quantitative distances
[
11
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 5
be a positive integer. Then for all
E\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d^{*}(E)>0
there exist some positive integers
k=k(d^{*}(E))
and
m_{0}=m_{0}(E)
such that

km\in\{|x-y|^{2}\,:\,x,y\in E\}\quad\text{for all integers }m\geq m_{0}.

The term
quantitative
in the title of Theorem
1.1
refers to the fact that the integer
k
depends only on
d^{*}(E)
and not on the set
E
itself. In
[
10
]
Lyall and Magyar went on to prove a strengthened,
pinned
variant of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2

(Quantitative pinned distances
[
10
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 5
be a positive integer. Then for all
E\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d^{*}(E)>0
there exists some positive integers
k=k(d^{*}(E))
and
m_{0}=m_{0}(E)
such that for every
m_{1}\geq m_{0}
there exists a fixed point
x\in E
such that

km\in\{|x-y|^{2}\,:\,y\in E\}\quad\text{for all integers }m_{0}\leq m\leq m_{1}.

In a series of works by Björklund, Bulinski and Fish
[
3
,
6
,
4
]
, it was realised that similar results hold if one replaces the squared Euclidean distance with other functions. We will focus on two of these results.

Theorem 1.3

(Quantitative polynomial Bogolyubov theorem
[

]
)

Let
P:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}

(Non-quantitative simplicies

Let

There is some ambiguity in the phrase

Let

Let

-action

A first natural directional question to ask is whether or not every ergodic action

Notice that if some direction

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A\right)=1.

In light of this observation and the negative answer provided by the authors of

Again the answer is no as shown by the following example from

Example 1.6

(A set which is not directionally expandable)
.

For some integer
N\geq 2

X:=\mathbb{Z}^{d}/(N\mathbb{Z})^{d}
with the counting probability measure
\mu

T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)

\mu

A=\{x\}\subset X

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A

However, as was the central to their proof of Theorem

To state the theorem precisely, we require the notion of a

Proposition 1.7

(

-ergodic components
[

]

Let

\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}

\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}.

\nu_{i}(\cdot)=\frac{\mu(\cdot\cap C_{i})}{\mu(C_{i})}

We call

(Quantitative directional expansivity)
.

For every
\delta>0

\varepsilon>0

, some

\nu(A)\geq\mu(A)

such that

\nu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A\right)>1-\varepsilon.

(Quantitative simplicies)

Let

[

The main new idea in the proof Theorem

[

in the proof of his famous theorem on three-term arithmetic progressions. The details of this measure increment argument are discussed in Section

[

Theorem C

(Quantitative polynomial expansivity)
.

Let
P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}):\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be an integer polynomial in
r

P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}
are linearly independent. Then for every
\delta>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists some positive integer
k_{0}=k_{0}(P,\delta,\varepsilon)
such that the following holds. For every ergodic action
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
and every
A\subset X
with
\mu(A)\geq\delta
there exists some positive integer
k\leq k_{0}
and some
T^{k}
-ergodic component
\nu
of
\mu
with
\nu(A)\geq\mu(A)
satisfying that

\nu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}T^{P(n)}A\right)>1-\varepsilon.

From Theorem
C
we are able to prove a multidimensional extension of Theorem
1.3

Theorem D

(Quantitative multi-dimensional polynomial Bogolyubov theorem)
.

Let
P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}):\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be an integer polynomial in
d

P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}
has degree less than
2
. Then for every
\delta>0
there exists a positive integer
k=k(P,\delta)
such that the following holds. For every
E\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with upper Banach density
d^{*}(E)\geq\delta
we have that

k\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset E-E+P(E-E).

We remark that the degree requirements in Theorems

and Theorem
1.3
are both necessary, and we prove this fact in Section

Our proofs of Theorems

We claim there exists some positive integer

\nu(T^{ikv}A\cap T^{jkv})\leq\frac{\nu(A)^{2}}{2}\quad\text{for every }0\leq i<j\leq M-1,

\displaystyle(M\nu(A))^{2}=\left(\int\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}1_{T^{ikv}A}\,d\nu\right)^{2}

\nu(T^{ikv}A\cap T^{jkv}A)>\frac{\nu(A)^{2}}{2},

3.
The measure increment argument

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be an ergodic action and let
A\subset X
have
\mu(A)>0
. Bochner’s theorem says that there exists a unique finite Borel measure
\sigma

satisfying

(8)

\mu(A\cap T^{v}A)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e(v\cdot\alpha)\,d\sigma(\alpha)\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

where

e(x):=\exp(2\pi ix)

and
\cdot
is the standard dot product.
We call
\sigma
the spectral measure of
A
.
Any rational
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
can be uniquely written in the form

\alpha=\left(\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}},\ldots,\frac{p_{d}}{q_{d}}\right)

for integers
0\leq p_{i}<q_{i}
with
\gcd(p_{i},q_{i})=1
for each
i=1,\ldots,d
. For any rational
\alpha
we use this form to define

\mathrm{denom}(\alpha):=\mathrm{lcm}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}),

and for a positive integer
M

\mathrm{Rat}(M):=\{\text{rational }\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\,:\,\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)\leq M\}.

Both proofs of Theorems
A
and
C
proceed by a measure increment argument which is a direct ergodic theoretic analogue of the now ubiquitous density increment argument first used by Roth in
[
14
]
. This measure increment argument relies on two key observations. The first observation says the only obstruction to

being sufficiently directionally or polynomially expandable is if
\sigma
gives a large amount of mass to rationals with small denominator, i.e. if
\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))
is large for some integer

. The next two propositions formalise this observation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a positive integer

and a positive constant

If

is an ergodic action and
A\subset X
has
\mu(A)\geq\delta
, spectral measure
\sigma
and

\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))<\kappa,

then there exists some primitive vector

for which

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A\right)>1-\varepsilon.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let

\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))<\frac{\mu(A)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{4},

If the spectral measure does not give small mass to rationals with small denominator, then the second observation allows us to obtain a measure increment of

with respect to a

-ergodic component.

Lemma 3.3

Let

\sigma

Definition 5.1
.

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
act ergodically. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, a function
f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
is called an
\alpha
-eigenfunction if

f\circ T^{v}=e(\alpha\cdot v)f\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We denote the set of all
\alpha
-eigenfunctions by
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)
, and for any
R\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
we define

\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(R):=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\left\{f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)\,:\,f\in\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)\text{ for some }\alpha\in R\right\}}.

For
f,g\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
we set

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{X}f\overline{g}\,d\mu.

It is not hard to see that
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)
and
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\beta)
are orthogonal whenever
\alpha\neq\beta\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
and moreover ergodicity implies that each
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)
has dimension at most
1
. Hence
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(R)
admits an orthonormal basis consisting
\alpha
-eigenfunctions, one for each
\alpha\in R
whose eigenspace
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)
is non-trivial.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be an ergodic action and let
A\subset X
have
\mu(A)>0
and spectral measure
\sigma
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)
. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
we have that

\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)}1_{A},1_{A}\rangle=\sigma(\{\alpha\}),

and moreover
\mu(A)^{2}=\sigma(\{0\})
.

Proof.

The mean ergodic theorem applied to the unitary action
\left(e(-\alpha\cdot v)T^{v}\right)_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
says that any
f\in L^{2}(X,\mu)
satisfies

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)}f=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e(-v\cdot\alpha)T^{v}f.

So by continuity of the inner product and the dominated convergence theorem we can calculate

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\alpha)}1_{A},1_{A}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e(-v\cdot\alpha)T^{v}1_{A},1_{A}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e(-v\cdot\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e(v\cdot\beta)\,d\sigma(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e(v\cdot(\beta-\alpha))\,d\sigma(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}1_{\{\alpha-\beta=0\}}\,d\sigma(\beta)=\sigma(\{\alpha\}).

Notice that
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(0)
is exactly the space of
T
-invariant functions, so by ergodicity
\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(0)
is the space of almost everywhere constant functions. Hence

\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(0)}1_{A},1_{A}\rangle=\langle\mu(A)1_{X},1_{A}\rangle=\mu(A)^{2}

as required.
∎

f\in\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\mathbb{Q}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\perp}
i.e.
\langle f,f_{\alpha}\rangle=0
for all rational

. Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^{P(n)}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X,\mu)}=0.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Fix
\delta,\varepsilon>0
and let
P:\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be an integer polynomial in the
r
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
. Suppose further that
P
has zero constant term and that the component polynomials
P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}
are linearly independent.
Let
\mathrm{deg}(P)=D
. We claim that the map sending
x_{j}\mapsto n^{{(D+1)}^{j}}
is injective on the monomials appearing in the components of
P
. Indeed if
x_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots x_{r}^{i_{r}}
is a monomial appearing in some component of
P
then we can calculate

\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{j}^{i_{j}}\mapsto\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(n^{{(D+1)}^{j}}\right)^{i_{j}}=n^{\sum_{j=1}^{r}i_{j}(D+1)^{j}},

and since each
i_{j}<(D+1)
the claim then follows by the uniqueness of representations of integers in base
D+1
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,d
we define a polynomial
Q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
via the formula

Q_{i}(n):=P_{i}\left(n^{{(D+1)}},n^{{(D+1)}^{2}},\ldots,n^{{(D+1)}^{r}}\right)

and set

Q=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d}):\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Our claim ensures that each
Q_{i}
has the same coefficients as
P_{i}
, and so our assumption that the components polynomials of
P
are linearly independent implies that the component polynomials of
Q
are also linearly independent. Lemma
6.3
then implies that
Q
has bounded multiplicative complexity. We can then invoke Lemma
6.2
to obtain
\psi_{Q}
as in the statement of the lemma, and let
M=M(\delta,\varepsilon,Q)
be the smallest positive integer such that

\psi_{Q}(q)<\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}\quad\text{for all }q>M.

Set
\kappa:=\left(\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{2}
. Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
act ergodically and let
A\subset X
have
\mu(A)\geq\delta
, spectral measure
\sigma
and

(15)

\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))<\kappa.

Suppose in order to derive a contradiction that the desired conclusion does not hold. Then there exists some
B\subset X
with
\mu(B)\geq\varepsilon
satisfying

0=\mu\left(\bigcup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}T^{P(x)}A\cap B\right),

which in particular implies that

0=\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{Q(n)}A\cap B\right).

It follows that
\langle T^{Q(n)}1_{A},1_{B}\rangle=0
for every

and so

0=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^{Q(n)}1_{A},1_{B}\Big\rangle.

By Lemma
6.4

(16)

0=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^{Q(n)}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\mathbb{Q}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})}1_{A},1_{B}\Big\rangle.

By expanding
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\mathbb{Q}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})}1_{A}
into an orthonormal basis of rational eigenfunctions we can write

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\mathbb{Q}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})}1_{A}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)=q}\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle f_{\alpha}

where the second sum is over all rational
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)=q
. Applying these observations to equation (
16
) allows us to calculate

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^{Q(n)}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{Eig}_{T}(\mathbb{Q}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d})}1_{A},1_{B}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)=q}\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle T^{Q(n)}f_{\alpha},1_{B}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)=q}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e(Q(n)\cdot\alpha)\right)\langle f_{\alpha},1_{B}\rangle\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
denote

T_{N}(q):=\sum_{\mathrm{denom}(\alpha)=q}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e(Q(n)\cdot\alpha)\right)\langle f_{\alpha},1_{B}\rangle\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle.

The only rational in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with denominator

We can then re-write the last line of our calculation as

0=\mu(A)\mu(B)+\lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\sum_{1<q\leq M}T_{N}(q)+\sum_{q>M}T_{N}(q)\right].

We will show that the later two terms in equation (

17
) is in fact a contradiction, which will finish the proof. More precisely we claim that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\lvert\sum_{1<q\leq M}T_{N}(q)\right\rvert<\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\lvert\sum_{q>M}T_{N}(q)\right\rvert<\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}.

Let us deal with the small denominators first. Recall from our proof of Lemma
3.3
that

(18)

|\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle|^{2}=\sigma(\{\alpha\}).

Now using the triangle inequality, the trivial bound on the exponential sum, Cauchy Schwarz, the Bessel inequality, equation (

) and equation (

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\lvert\sum_{1<q\leq M}T_{N}(q)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Rat}(M)}|\langle f_{\alpha},1_{B}\rangle||\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Rat}(M)}\left\lvert\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Rat}(M)}\left\lvert\langle f_{\alpha},1_{B}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))}

(19)

\displaystyle<\sqrt{\kappa}=\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}.

where in the final equality we have used our choice of

. For the large denominators we again use the triangle inequality, Cauchy Schwarz and the Bessel inequality, however instead of using the trivial bound for the exponential sum we use equation (

). Indeed letting

we have that

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\lvert\sum_{q>M}T_{N}(q)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\alpha\in C}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e(Q(n)\cdot\alpha)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\sum_{\alpha\in C}\left\lvert\langle 1_{A},f_{\alpha}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\sum_{\alpha\in C}\left\lvert\langle f_{\alpha},1_{B}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{Q}(M)<\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{2}.

∎

7.
The directional dichotomy

Given any
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
let
L_{v}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
denote the annihilator of
v
inside
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, i.e.

L_{v}^{\perp}:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\,:\,v\cdot\alpha=0\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\}.

The following lemma is an important observation from
[
3
]
that reduces the study of expansive directions for ergodic
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-systems to the study of annihilators inside
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Lemma 7.1

(
[
3
]
[Lemma 3.2])
.

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
act ergodically and suppose
A\subset X
has
\mu(A)>0
and spectral measure
\sigma
. For any
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we have that

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A\right)\geq\frac{\mu(A)^{2}}{\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp})}.

Definition 7.2

(Haystacks)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all primitive vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, i.e. those for which the
\gcd
of it’s components is
1
. An infinite set
H\subset\mathcal{P}
is called a haystack if any distinct
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in H
are linearly independent.

There are many different ways to construct haystacks in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see for instance Lemma 2.4 in
[
3
]
. Let us fix some haystack
H
for the remainder of the section. For each positive integer
M
define

H_{M}:=\left\{v\in H\,:\,\lVert v\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{M}{d!}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right\}

where
\lVert v\rVert_{\infty}
denotes the largest absolute value of the components of
v
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Any distinct
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in H_{M}
have that

\cap_{i=1}^{d}L_{v_{i}}^{\perp}\subset\mathrm{Rat}(M).

Proof.

Pick any distinct
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in H_{M}
and let
A
be the matrix whose rows are
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
. Then

0<|\mathrm{det}(A)|\leq M.

By definition if
\alpha\in\cap_{i=1}^{d}L_{v_{i}}^{\perp}
then there exist some
w_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
A\alpha=w_{\alpha}
, and since
A
is invertible then
\alpha=A^{-1}w_{\alpha}
. The entries of
A^{-1}
are all rational numbers inside
\mathbb{Z}/\det(A)
, and so each component of
\alpha
is also a rational number inside
\mathbb{Z}/\det(A)
which ensures that
\alpha\in\mathrm{Rat}(M)
.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
\gamma>0
there exists a positive integer
M=M(\gamma)
such that the following is true. For any finite Borel measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\sigma(\mathbb{T}^{d})\leq 1
and

\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}

there exists a vector
v\in H_{M}
with

\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})\leq\gamma.

Proof.

Let
M
be a positive integer to be later specified.
For any
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
let
L_{v}:=L_{v}^{\perp}\setminus(\mathrm{Rat}(M)\cup\{0\})
. Lemma
7.3
and the definition of
L_{v}
together imply that
\sum_{v\in H_{M}}1_{L_{v}}\leq d-1
. We can then estimate

\left\lvert H_{M}\right\rvert\min_{v\in H_{M}}\sigma(L_{v})\leq\sum_{v\in H_{M}}\sigma(L_{v})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{v\in H_{M}}1_{L_{v}}\,d\sigma\leq(d-1),

or equivalently

\min_{v\in H_{M}}\sigma(L_{v})\leq\frac{(d-1)}{\left\lvert H_{M}\right\rvert}.

If we pick
M=M(\gamma)
large enough so that the right hand side of the above equation is at most
\gamma/2
then there must be some
v\in H_{M}
for which
\sigma(L_{v})\leq\gamma/2
. By the hypothesis
\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}
and so it follows that

\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})\leq\sigma(L_{v})+\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))\leq\gamma

as required.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Fix
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a positive constant
\gamma=\gamma(\delta,\varepsilon)
such that

\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta^{2}+\gamma}>1-\varepsilon.

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
act ergodically and suppose
A\subset X
has
\mu(A)\geq\delta
and spectral measure
\sigma
. Our choice of
\gamma
and the fact that
\mu(A)\geq\delta
together ensure that

\frac{\mu(A)^{2}}{\mu(A)^{2}+\gamma}>1-\varepsilon.

By Lemma
7.4
there exists a positive integer
M=M(\gamma)
such that if
\sigma(\mathrm{Rat}(M))\leq\gamma/2
then there exists some
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\})\leq\gamma
. Recall from Lemma
5.2
that
\sigma(\{0\})=\mu(A)^{2}
, and so
\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp})\leq\gamma+\mu(A)^{2}
. We can then use Lemma

to see that

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{nv}A\right)\geq\frac{\mu(A)^{2}}{\sigma(L_{v}^{\perp})}\geq\frac{\mu(A)^{2}}{\mu(A)^{2}+\gamma}>1-\varepsilon

as required.

∎

.

For any integer polynomial

By Lemma

A counter example to the pinned version of the polynomial Bogolyubov theorem

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
have
P(0)=0
and
\deg P\geq 2
. Let
T:\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
and
A\subset X
be as in the proof of the of Proposition
8.3
applied to the polynomial

. For every positive integer
k
there exists some positive integer

such that for every

we have that

\mu\left(A\cap\bigcap_{n=1}^{m}T^{P(l_{n})-kn}A\right)=0.

Proof.

Let

A_{i}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{i}^{P(n)}A_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\prime}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T^{P(n)}A\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}^{\prime}.

Let
k

and

, and any

we have that

\mu\left(A\cap\bigcap_{n=1}^{m}T^{P(l_{n})-kn}A\right)\leq\mu\left(A^{\prime}\cap\bigcap_{n=1}^{m}T^{-kn}A^{\prime}\right)\leq\mu_{i}\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{m}T_{i}^{-kn}A_{i}^{\prime}\right).

can only have arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions of common difference

Proposition 10.1
.

The requirement in Theorem
D
that no non-trivial linear combination of the components of
P
be a linear polynomial is necessary.

Proof.

Let
P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}):\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be an integer polynomial with zero constant term and suppose that there exists
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
not all zero satisfying that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}P_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}x_{i}

for some
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the product set

E:=B(\alpha,\varepsilon)\times\ldots\times B(\alpha,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}

where
B(\alpha,\varepsilon)
is the Bohr set

B(\alpha,\varepsilon):=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,n\alpha\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\>(\mathrm{mod}\,1)\}

for some irrational
\alpha
and some small
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
d^{*}(E)>0
then if the theorem holds for the polynomial
P
there must be some positive integer
k
such for every
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
there exist
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
both in
E-E
with

km_{i}=x_{i}+P_{i}(y)\quad\text{for each }i=1,\ldots,d.

The above equations can be rearranged to read

k\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}(P_{i}(y)+x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\beta_{i}y_{i}+\alpha_{i}x_{i}\right),

which in particular implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}(B(\alpha,\varepsilon)-B(\alpha,\varepsilon))+\alpha_{i}(B(\alpha,\varepsilon)-B(\alpha,\varepsilon))

contains a subgroup. On the other hand the triangle inequality implies that the above set is contained inside the Bohr set

B\left(\alpha,2\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\beta_{i}\right\rvert\right)\right)

and so cannot contain a subgroup provided that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.7
.

Let
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
be ergodic and consider the collection

\mathcal{C}:=\{C\subset X\,:\,\mu(C)>0\text{ and }k\mathbb{Z}^{d}C\subset C\}.

Set

\kappa:=\inf_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mu(C).

We first show that
\kappa\geq 1/k^{d}
.
Indeed pick coset representatives
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k^{d}}
for
k\mathbb{Z}^{d}
inside
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and let
C\in\mathcal{C}
. Then the set

A:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k^{d}}T^{v_{i}}C

is invariant under all of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
so ergodicity implies that
\mu(A)=1
. Since
T
preserves
\mu
then

1=\mu(A)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k^{d}}\mu(T^{v_{i}}C)=k^{d}\mu(C)

as required.
By definition of
\kappa
there exists some
C\in\mathcal{C}
with

\kappa\leq\mu(C)<\kappa+\kappa/2.

We claim that
k\mathbb{Z}^{d}
acts ergodically on
C
, so that actually
\mu(C)=\kappa
. Indeed, if the claim fails then there exists some
C^{\prime}\subset C
with
k\mathbb{Z}^{d}C^{\prime}\subset C^{\prime}
and
\mu(C^{\prime})\in(0,\mu(C))
. This implies that
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and so
\mu(C^{\prime})\in[\kappa,\mu(C))
. However the set
C\setminus C^{\prime}
is also
k\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-invariant and satisfies

\mu(C\setminus C^{\prime})=\mu(C)-\mu(C^{\prime})\in\left(0,\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)

which contradicts the definition of
\kappa

One can then easily check that translates of
C

disjointly cover

\mu
, and
k\mathbb{Z}^{d}
acts ergodically on each translate. The result then follows with

as the

-ergodic components of

∎
