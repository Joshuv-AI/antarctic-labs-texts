Abstract




Scalable expectation propagation

for generalized linear models

Abstract

Generalized linear models (
glm
s) arguably represent the standard approach for statistical regression beyond the Gaussian likelihood scenario.
When Bayesian formulations are employed, the general absence of a tractable posterior distribution has motivated the development of deterministic approximations, which are generally more scalable than sampling techniques.
Among them, expectation propagation (
ep
) showed extreme accuracy, usually higher than many variational Bayes solutions.
However, the higher computational cost of
ep
posed concerns about its practical feasibility, especially in high-dimensional settings.
We address these concerns by deriving a novel efficient formulation of
ep
for
glm
s, whose cost scales linearly in the number of covariates
p
. This reduces the state-of-the-art
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}n)
per-iteration computational cost of the
ep
routine for
glm
s to
\mathcal{O}(pn\cdot\min\{p,n\})
, with
n
being the sample size.
We also show that, for binary models and log-linear
glm
s approximate predictive means can be obtained at no additional cost.
To preserve efficient moment matching for count data, we propose employing a combination of log-normal Laplace transform approximations, avoiding numerical integration.
These novel results open the possibility of employing
ep
in settings that were believed to be practically impossible.
Improvements over state-of-the-art approaches are illustrated both for simulated and real data.
The efficient
ep
implementation is available at
https://github.com/niccoloanceschi/EPglm
.

Key Words
:
Generalized Linear Model,
Expectation Propagation,
Bayesian Inference,
Extended Multivariate Skew-Normal Distribution,
Unified Skew-Normal Distribution.

Short/Running Title
:
Scalable EP for GLMs.

Introduction

Generalized linear models (
glm
s) arguably represent the most popular off-the-shelf tool for data regression for a wide variety of data, with binary and count responses representing their main application areas.
The lack of a tractable posterior distribution in their Bayesian formulation has motivated notable efforts to develop computational methods that can efficiently scale to high-dimensional settings (large
p
) or large datasets (large
n
), with the former providing a primary area of interest.
See, for instance, the review paper by
Chopin & Ridgway (2017a)
about the big
p
problem faced by the available computational methods for Bayesian binary regression computations.
Among the scalable deterministic approximate methods, a central role has certainly been played by variational Bayes (
vb
) approximations
(e.g.,
Blei et al., 2017a
)
, which are usually much faster than sampling-based techniques.
This comes however at the cost of possible accuracy issues in certain settings, making some of the
vb
approximations a sound replacement for the exact posterior only in some scenarios.
This is sub-par compared to having an approximation scheme offering reliable performances regardless of the specific setting considered.
Empirically, expectation propagation (
ep
)
(
Minka, 2001b
;
Minka, 2001c
)
has demonstrated an improved quality in posterior approximation for a wide variety of models, including binary
(
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
)
, tobit
(
Anceschi et al., 2023a
)
, Poisson
(
Zhang et al., 2019a
)
, heteroscedastic, lasso-penalized, and quantile regression
(
Zhou et al., 2023c
)
, as well as for hierarchical models more in general
(
Vehtari et al., 2020a
)
.
Indeed, when testing its performance with logit likelihood and various priors on datasets of moderate size, the empirical approximation error turned out to be so small that
Chopin & Ridgway (2017a)
conclude that
it seems that
ep
may be safely used as a complete replacement of sampling-based methods
(Section 5.1.1).
However, such improved accuracy comes at a higher computational cost than
vb
methods, thus motivating questions about the applicability of this excellent approximate method in large
p
settings, namely
p\gg 1000

(
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
)
.
To address this issue we develop a novel
ep
formulation for Bayesian
glm
s with multivariate Gaussian prior which scales linearly in the number of covariates
p
.
This improves over current state-of-the-art implementations by multiple orders of magnitude and makes
ep
a feasible option in
glm
s with dimensions well beyond the above threshold, paving the way to the employment of
ep
in settings where available sampling methods are impractical and other available approximations are less accurate.

In
glm
s, the effect of the
p
-dimensional vector of predictors
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})^{\intercal}
on the distribution of the response variable
y
is modelled through a
link function

g(\cdot)
, which relates the linear predictor
\eta={\bf x}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}
to the mean of the response variable via the relation
{\mathbb{E}}[y\mid{\bm{\beta}}]=g^{-1}(\eta)
, with
{\bm{\beta}}
a
p
-dimensional unknown parameter.
For binary regression models with Bernoulli likelihood, classical link functions are represented by the logit or probit links, while for count data default specifications combine a Poisson or Negative Binomial likelihood with
\log(\cdot)
link function.
Other models have also been studied, including, for instance, Gamma regression for non-negative continuous responses, where a Gamma likelihood is usually combined with a
\log(\cdot)
link function.
When considering their Bayesian formulation, the model is completed by specifying a prior distribution for the parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
, with the zero-mean spherical multivariate Gaussian distribution constituting a default choice.
Unfortunately, a tractable closed-form posterior distribution for the
glm
s is generally lacking beyond the Gaussian regression.

State-of-the-art Bayesian approaches usually rely on posterior sampling methods — see, e.g.,
Holmes & Held (2006a)
;
Polson et al. (2013a)
;
Durante (2019a)
;
Zhang et al. (2021a)
;
Zens et al. (2023a)
and the seminal contribution by
Albert & Chib (1993a)
for binary regression models, or
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. (2009a)
;
Chanialidis et al. (2018a)
;
D’Angelo & Canale (2023a)
for the Poisson-like models — or more scalable approximate posterior inference — see, e.g.,
Jaakkola & Jordan (2000a)
;
Consonni & Marin (2007a)
;
Durante & Rigon (2019a)
;
Fasano et al. (2022a)
for binary regression models, or
Arridge et al. (2018a)
;
Zhang et al. (2019a)
for Poisson regression.
Most of the above-mentioned sampling methods rely on
mcmc
schemes, often introducing a data augmentation step, in the spirit of the well-known Gibbs sampler for the probit model originally developed by
Albert & Chib (1993a)
, who exploited the augmented variables to obtain conjugate full conditionals.
These approaches make in principle posterior inference feasible for a wide variety of models, but the possible high correlation between the augmented data and the parameters can cause convergence issues and thus unreliable results, especially in unbalanced datasets
(
Johndrow et al., 2019a
)
.
Other sampling alternatives, like the no-U-turn Hamiltonian sampler by
Hoffman & Gelman (2014a)
or adaptive Metropolis-Hastings techniques
(
Haario et al., 2001a
;
Roberts & Rosenthal, 2009a
)
, are possible.
Although these approaches might mitigate autocorrelation among samples and improve mixing, they remain computationally impractical for high-dimensional problems
(
Durante, 2019a
;
Fasano et al., 2022a
)
.
A notable exception is represented by
Durante (2019a)
, who developed an i.i.d. sampler for the posterior distribution in Bayesian probit models.
This leverages on the representation of the
\textsc{sun}_{p,n}
posterior
(
Arellano-Valle & Azzalini, 2006a
)
as a linear combination of a
p
-variate Gaussian and an
n
-variate truncated Gaussian, making it particularly well suited for high-dimensional problems.
However, the need to sample from truncated multivariate Gaussians whose dimension equals the number of the observations represents the computational challenge of such an algorithm: despite recent promising results on the topic
(
Pakman & Paninski, 2014a
;
Botev, 2017a
;
Genton et al., 2018a
;
Zhang et al., 2021a
)
, i.i.d. posterior samples are hard to get when
n
is above a few hundred.

More scalable deterministic approximations were thus developed to circumvent the computational issues mentioned above.
However, in high-dimensional settings, some of the most popular approximate methods, like mean-field
vb
or Laplace approximation, may turn out to be unreliable in high dimensions
(
Fasano et al., 2022a
;
Fasano & Durante, 2022a
;
Anceschi et al., 2023a
)
.
In binary and multinomial Bayesian regression, the above contributions overcame such drawbacks by developing a partially-factorized variational (
pfm-vb
) approximation of the posterior distribution which, for any fixed
n
, converges to the true posterior distribution as
p
diverges under reasonable assumptions.
Moreover,
pfm-vb
is always guaranteed to improve over mean-field approximations
(
Consonni & Marin, 2007a
;
Girolami & Rogers, 2006a
)
.
The computational costs are orders of magnitude smaller than the ones of sampling methods, and, empirically, the approximate posterior moments closely match the ones obtained via i.i.d. sampling for
p/n\geq 2
.
Other variational approximations have been developed for the logit
(
Jaakkola & Jordan, 2000a
)
and Poisson
(
Arridge et al., 2018a
)
to get fast posterior approximations.
See also
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
for a complete review of computational methods for Bayesian binary regression, covering also Laplace and improved nested Laplace approximation (
inla
)
(
Rue et al., 2009a
)
, as well as other exact methods whose review is beyond the scope of the paper.
The overall accuracy of the
vb
approaches is still an open area of research and in many cases, it is very setting-specific.
In general,
ep
provides better posterior approximations across scenarios for
glm
s
(
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
;
Anceschi et al., 2023a
;
Zhang et al., 2019a
)
.
This comes, however, at a higher computational cost, which makes current implementations not feasible for settings where
p>1000
.
We show that an efficient derivation of
ep
is possible, having linear cost in
p
and reducing the
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}n)
per-iteration cost reported in
Chopin & Ridgway (2017a)
to
\mathcal{O}(pn\cdot\min\{p,n\})
.
This improvement makes
ep
feasible in settings that are out of reach for state-of-the-art implementations.
The resulting efficient implementation can be fully leveraged to provide accurate approximations of the marginal likelihood, which is often overlooked in the literature.
Besides such structural enhancement, we achieve further computational improvement for count data, by proposing a novel approximate moment matching step for Poisson regression.
Similarly to standard practice in the logit case, this avoids the need for numerical integration, benefiting both in terms of efficiency and stability.

The rest of the article is organized as follows.
Section
2
reviews the
ep
algorithm and its employment to compute the approximate posterior moments and marginal likelihood.
Section
3
details our efficient
ep
routines for various
glm
s, while Section
4
shows multiple approximations of the hybrid moments required by the
ep
algorithm, avoiding numerical quadrature.
We also show that in several relevant cases, the required hybrid moments and/or the predictive means are available in closed form.
Section
5
reports the results of our proposed algorithm over simulated data, while Section
6
presents the performance of the proposed method over real data applications.
Section
7
contains concluding remarks and further interesting research questions arising from our findings.
The code to reproduce the results is available at
https://github.com/niccoloanceschi/EPglm
.

Expectation propagation

In this section, we briefly review the principles and the main steps of expectation propagation (
ep
)
(
Minka, 2001b
;
Minka, 2001c
)
for Bayesian inference in a generic regression model

y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}}\overset{ind}{\sim}p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\quad{\bm{\beta}}\sim p({\bm{\beta}}),

(1)

where the likelihood contribution
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
depends on the unknown parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
and a known covariate vector
{\bf x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, which is thus removed from the conditioning part.
Throughout the paper, we will assume univariate
y_{i}
’s and the well-established zero-mean spherical multivariate Gaussian prior for the parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
, although computations trivially adapt to more general cases.
We will then take
p({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}};\nu^{2}{\bf I}_{p})
, where
{\bf I}_{p}
is the identity matrix of dimension
p
and
\phi_{p}({\bf t};{\bf S})
denotes the density of a
p
-variate Gaussian random variable with mean
\bm{0}_{p}
and covariance matrix
{\bf S}
, evaluated at
{\bf t}
.

2.1

Approximation of posterior moments

Calling
{\bf y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, the
ep
framework approximates the posterior distribution

p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y})\propto p({\bm{\beta}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})

with a density
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
having the same factorization form

q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})\propto q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}({\bm{\beta}}),

where the global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
and the factors (also referred to as
sites
)
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
,
i=0,\ldots,n
, are taken from a possibly unnormalized exponential family, as clarified below.
The key operating principle of the
ep
scheme is to refine iteratively each approximate site
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
against the exact one
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
, exploiting the information coming from the remaining factors
\{q_{j}({\bm{\beta}})\}_{j\neq i}
.
This helps in targeting regions of high posterior density and, thus, constructing a globally accurate approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
.

When the parameter
{\bm{\beta}}
is continuous, as in
glm
s, a typical choice, which will also be used hereafter, is to take Gaussian-like sites
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{i}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{i}\}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
, with the natural parameters
{\bf r}_{i}
and
{\bf Q}_{i}
to be determined by the
ep
algorithm optimization rules.
As a consequence, the global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
is Gaussian, with
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}}_{{\textsc{ep}}},{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}})
, where
{\bm{\xi}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}={\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{-1}{\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}
and
{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}={\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{-1}
are obtained from the global natural parameters
{\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bf r}_{i}
,
{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bf Q}_{i}
.
If the prior
p({\bm{\beta}})
has the same form of
q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})
, i.e.
p({\bm{\beta}})
is a multivariate Gaussian density, then we can set
q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})=p({\bm{\beta}})
and compute only the natural parameters
{\bf r}_{i}
and
{\bf Q}_{i}
for sites
i=1,\ldots,n
.
It is worth noting that the sites
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
provide an approximation of the likelihood term
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
and so
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
is not a probability density in
{\bm{\beta}}
.
Hence, it generally does not hold that
\int q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}=1
.
Likewise, the contributions
{\bf Q}_{i}
’s of each site
i=1,\dots,n
to the global precision matrix
{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}
are not explicitly constrained to be positive definite.

{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}
must instead be positive definite, since the global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
, representing the
ep
surrogate of the exact posterior distribution, is a valid probability density in
{\bm{\beta}}
.

In principle, one could think of targeting a global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Kullback & Leibler, 1951a
)

\textsc{kl}\big[p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y})||q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})\big]
.
However, this would involve the computation of integrals with respect to the posterior distribution, which is usually intractable or computationally impractical.

ep
overcomes this issue by iteratively updating the natural parameters
{\bf r}_{i}
and
{\bf Q}_{i}
of site
i
minimizing a different
kl
divergence, informed by the remaining
\{{\bf r}_{j}\}_{j\neq i}
and
\{{\bf Q}_{i}\}_{j\neq i}
, which instead is computationally tractable.
More specifically, at each iteration
ep
updates each site
i=1,\ldots,n
as follows.
Keeping the parameters for all sites
j\neq i
fixed, first compute the so-called
cavity density

q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})\propto\dfrac{q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})}{q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{-i}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{-i}\right\},

where
{\bf r}_{-i}=\sum_{j\neq i}{\bf r}_{j}
and
{\bf Q}_{-i}=\sum_{j\neq i}{\bf Q}_{j}
.
Contrary to the local sites,
q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})
is a proper distribution in
{\bm{\beta}}
.
As such, defining
\Psi({\bf r},{\bf Q})=\int\exp\left\{-\dfrac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}\right\}d{\bm{\beta}}
so that
\log\Psi({\bf r},{\bf Q})=\dfrac{1}{2}{\bf r}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf r}+\dfrac{p}{2}\log(2\pi)-\dfrac{1}{2}\log|{\bf Q}|
, one gets

q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})=\dfrac{1}{\Psi({\bf r}_{-i},{\bf Q}_{-i})}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{-i}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{-i}\right\}=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}{\bf r}_{-i},{\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}).

Similarly, one defines the so-called
hybrid (or tilted) distribution

h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})=\dfrac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}}),

with
Z_{h_{i}}=\int p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}
and update the parameters
{\bf r}_{i}
and
{\bf Q}_{i}
of site
i
to
{\bf r}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}
and
{\bf Q}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}
so that the resulting global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})\propto q_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})
minimizes
\textsc{kl}\big[h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})||q_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})\big]
.
When the
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
are taken from an exponential family, the latter minimization is achieved by matching the moments of the sufficient statistics of
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})
computed with respect to
h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
and
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})

(
Seeger, 2003a
;
Bishop, 2006a
)
.
Calling
{\bm{\mu}}_{h_{i}}={\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bm{\beta}}]
and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{h_{i}}={\operatorname{var}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bm{\beta}}]
, the
ep
moment-matching conditions for site
i
translate into imposing the equality

\begin{cases}\left({\bf Q}_{-i}+{\bf Q}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}\right)^{-1}({\bf r}_{-i}+{\bf r}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}})={\bm{\mu}}_{h_{i}}\\
\left({\bf Q}_{-i}+{\bf Q}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}\right)^{-1}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{h_{i}},\end{cases}

which implies

\begin{cases}{\bf r}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}=\left({\bf Q}_{-i}+{\bf Q}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}\right){\bm{\mu}}_{h_{i}}-{\bf r}_{-i}\\
{\bf Q}_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{h_{i}}^{-1}-{\bf Q}_{-i}.\end{cases}

(2)

At each iteration, the moment-matching conditions (
2
) are enforced sequentially for each site
i=1,\ldots,n
, until convergence is eventually reached.
It is well-known that
ep
might be prone to converge issues in some settings
(
Minka, 2004a
)
.
When this happens, damping techniques can be applied to reach convergence, although usually at a lower rate
(
Vehtari et al., 2020a
)
.
After convergence is reached, one can compute the
ep
global approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}}_{{\textsc{ep}}},{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}})
, where
{\bm{\xi}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}={\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{-1}{\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}
and
{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}={\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{-1}
and
{\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bf r}_{i}
,
{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bf Q}_{i}
.

To completely specify the
ep
algorithm, initial values of the site parameters need to be provided.
Following common practice employed in the case of multivariate Gaussian prior
p({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bf m},{\bf C})

(
Vehtari et al., 2020a
)
, we consider the case in which
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
is initialized to the prior distribution, that is we initialize
{\bf r}_{0}={\bf C}^{-1}{\bf m}
,
{\bf Q}_{0}={\bf C}^{-1}
, while
{\bf r}_{i}=\bm{0}
,
{\bf Q}_{i}=\bm{0}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

2.2

Approximation of the marginal likelihood

ep
can be used as well to obtain an estimation of the marginal likelihood
p({\bf y})=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})p({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}
at no additional cost
(
Vehtari et al., 2020a
;
Minka, 2001b
)
.
To this end, it becomes necessary to specify a proportionality constant
Z_{i}
for each site approximation
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
, so that

q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})=\dfrac{1}{Z_{i}}\exp\left\{-\dfrac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{i}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{i}\right\}.

Such
Z_{i}
’s were not strictly required to compute the moments of the global posterior approximation
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})
.
It turns out that the former task is straightforward when the normalizing constant of the hybrid distribution
Z_{h_{i}}
can be computed in closed form (as is the case for the probit model).
Since
q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})=p({\bm{\beta}})
, it holds
\log Z_{0}=\log\Psi({\bf r}_{0},{\bf Q}_{0})
.
Recall that each
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, is not a probability density in
{\bm{\beta}}
, but it is rather an approximation of the likelihood term
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
.
Adhering to such rigorous representation in terms of global normalization constant
Z_{{\textsc{ep}}}
and local proportionality constants
Z_{i}
, the overall
ep
approximation can be rewritten as
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})=\dfrac{1}{Z_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}}}q_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})
, where, analogously to
Z_{h_{i}}
, one has

\begin{split}Z_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}}&=\int q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})q_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}=\dfrac{1}{\Psi({\bf r}_{-i},{\bf Q}_{-i})\,Z_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}}\int e^{-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}}}d{\bm{\beta}}\\
&=\dfrac{\Psi({\bf r}_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}},{\bf Q}_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}})}{\Psi({\bf r}_{-i},{\bf Q}_{-i})\,Z_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}}.\end{split}

Such reformulation is not immediately useful per se, since the
the values of these
Z_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}
’s are not determined by the
ep
moment-matching conditions.
Accordingly, an additional constraint must be fixed to uniquely identify them, and, in turn, to produce an approximation of the marginal likelihood.
This can be done by extending the moment matching procedure to the so-called zeroth order moments, imposing that
Z_{\textsc{ep}}^{{\textsc{new}}}=Z_{h_{i}}
.
Intuitively, this can be interpreted as a way of enforcing
q_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})
to be the best approximation of
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
, such that
\int q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})q_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}=\int q_{-i}({\bm{\beta}})p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}
.
Accordingly, it is trivial to see that

\log Z_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}=\log\Psi({\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}},{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}^{{\textsc{new}}})-\log\Psi({\bf r}_{-i},{\bf Q}_{-i})-\log Z_{h_{i}}.

(3)

Upon convergence of the main
ep
routine, the
ep
approximation of the marginal likelihood can be computed by replacing each
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
with the corresponding approximate site the factor
q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
, so that

m_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bf y})=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}({\bm{\beta}})q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})d{\bm{\beta}}=\dfrac{\int\exp\left\{{\textstyle-\dfrac{1}{2}{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}}{\bm{\beta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}}}\right\}d{\bm{\beta}}}{\Psi({\bf r}_{0},{\bf Q}_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}}=\dfrac{\Psi({\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}},{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}})}{\Psi({\bf r}_{0},{\bf Q}_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}}.

Equivalently, its logarithm reads

\log m_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bf y})=\log\Psi({\bf r}_{{\textsc{ep}}},{\bf Q}_{{\textsc{ep}}})-\log\Psi({\bf r}_{0},{\bf Q}_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log Z_{i}.

(4)

3

Efficient updates in GLMs

3.1

Posterior moments and marginal likelihood with linear cost in
n

The
ep
routine presented above applies regardless of the details of the specific
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})
considered.
However, the implementation of such a scheme for
glm
s greatly benefits from a general structure common to most of them.
This is because the associated likelihood contributions depend on the parameters
{\bm{\beta}}
only thorough the inner product
{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}
so that
p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})=\ell_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})
.
Here the subscript
i
conveys the dependence of
\ell_{i}
on the response
y_{i}
and possibly on additional nuisance parameters
\upsilon_{i}
of the
glm
, although this is irrelevant to the structure we want to elicit in the
ep
approximate (i.e.,
\ell_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})=p(y_{i}\mid{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}},\upsilon_{i})
).
In the remainder, possible additional parameters
\upsilon_{i}

Hybrids moments in GLMs: some relevant cases

The efficient structure for the
ep
updates described above still left unaddressed the computation of the univariate hybrid moments, associated with the specific model of interest.
In the current section, we provide the details of such computations for several notable
glm
s, including probit, logit, Poisson, and gamma regression.
Even for such fundamental constructions, the required functionals are often unavailable in closed form.
Probit models constitute an eminent exception, in that the hybrid distributions belong to a tractable family of distributions and the exact
ep
updates are readily available at no additional computational cost

(
Anceschi et al., 2023a
;
Fasano et al., 2023b
;
Fasano et al., 2023c
)
.

For any model lacking these desirable properties,
Z_{h_{i}}
,
{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]
and
\operatorname{var}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]
could be computed via one-dimensional numerical integration, benefiting from the dimensionality reduction induced by the inner product with
{\bf x}_{i}
.
Choosing a large number
\mathcal{T}
of subintervals for the domain of the desired integral typically provides an accurate approximation of the exact value.
However, this would result in a
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{T})
cost
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{mm}}
per each moment-matching step, besides being prone to potential numerical issues.
To preserve the efficient nature of the
ep
scheme, we here provide model-specific closed-form approximations of the required moments, cutting down
\mathcal{C}_{\textsc{mm}}
to
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

In practice, this boils down to supplying an accurate approximation
\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
of the normalizing constants

Z_{h_{i}}=\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-\lambda_{i},\,\rho_{i}^{2}\big)d\eta_{i}

and working out its derivative
(
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
)
.
Here
\lambda_{i}={\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf r}_{-i}={\bf w}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{-i}
and
\rho_{i}^{2}={\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}={\bf w}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf x}_{i}
, depending on which version of the
ep
updates is being considered.
Indeed, the required moments can be further obtained from the derivatives of
\log Z_{h_{i}}

(
Seeger, 2005a
)
via

\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]&=\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log Z_{h_{i}}\\
\operatorname{var}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]&=\rho_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}\left(2\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log Z_{h_{i}}-\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log Z_{h_{i}}\Big)^{2}\right)\rho_{i}^{2}\;,\end{split}

(8)

as proven in Appendix
S.2

In turn, the above expressions can be directly combined with the updates from Propostion
1
.
Defining
\Theta_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log Z_{h_{i}}
and
\Delta_{i}=2\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log Z_{h_{i}}-\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log Z_{h_{i}}\Big)^{2}
, simply algebraic calculations lead to

k_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}=-\Delta_{i}\big/\big(1+\rho_{i}^{2}\Delta_{i}\big),\qquad\qquad m_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}=k_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}\big(\lambda_{i}-\Delta_{i}^{-1}\Theta_{i}\big)\;.

Accordingly, any differentiable approximation
\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
for
Z_{h_{i}}
naturally induces approximate updates for the natural parameters of the Gaussian
ep
sites.
The quality of the resulting approximations will be highly specific to the model considered, the nature of
\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
and the order of magnitude of the parameters
\lambda_{i}
and
\rho_{i}^{2}
.

4.1

Probit regression

Probit regression represents a prominent example of models for which the hybrids belong to a known family of distributions, further allowing for closed-form
ep
updates.
This can be seen as a relevant special case of a broader class of models considered in
Anceschi et al. (2023a)
, where the
ep
algorithm further simplifies, avoiding the need to work with the possibly computationally challenging
sun
hybrid distributions, as shown below.
In particular, the form of the
ep
updates can be obtained by leveraging results on the efficiently tractable multivariate extended skew-normal (
sn
) random variables (see
Azzalini & Capitanio, 2014c
).
In probit models, the binary responses
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
are modeled as Bernoulli random variables with success probability
\Phi({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Accordingly, each likelihood contribution takes the form

p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})=\big(\Phi({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})\big)^{y_{i}}\big(1-\Phi({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})\big)^{1-y_{i}}=\Phi\big((2y_{i}-1){\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}\big)\;.

(9)

This allowed
Durante, 2019a
to first show that the posterior distribution under Gaussian priors belongs to the
sun
family.
The same rationale can be extended to the analysis of the
ep
hybrid distributions
h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
, resulting in the following proposition.

Proposition
4
.

For the probit likelihood (
9
), the hybrid density
h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
is the density of a multivariate extended skew-normal distribution
\textsc{sn}_{p}({\bm{\xi}}_{i},{\bm{\Omega}}_{i},{\bm{\alpha}}_{i},\tau_{i})
(see
Azzalini & Capitanio, 2014c
), with

\begin{split}{\bm{\omega}}_{i}&=\left[\text{diag}\left({\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\right)\right]^{1/2}\hskip 50.0pt\bar{{\bm{\Omega}}}_{i}={\bm{\omega}}_{i}^{-1}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bm{\omega}}_{i}^{-1}\\
{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}&=(2y_{i}-1){\bm{\omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\hskip 55.0pt\tau_{i}=(2y_{i}-1)(1+{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1/2}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\;.\end{split}

and
{\bm{\xi}}_{i}={\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}{\bf r}_{-i}
and
{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}={\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}
, as before.
Its normalizing constant is
Z_{h_{i}}=\Phi(\tau_{i})
, while the updates for one dimensional parameters in the
i
-th approximate site take the form

\begin{split}k_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}&=-\zeta_{2}(\tau_{i})/\big(1+{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}+\zeta_{2}(\tau_{i}){\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)\\[5.0pt]
m_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}&=\zeta_{1}(\tau_{i})s_{i}+k_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf r}_{-i}+k_{i}^{{\textsc{new}}}\zeta_{1}(\tau_{i})s_{i}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\;,\end{split}

where
s_{i}=(2y_{i}-1)(1+{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1/2}
,
\zeta_{1}(x)=\phi(x)/\Phi(x)
and
\zeta_{2}(x)=-\zeta_{1}(x)^{2}-x\zeta_{1}(x)
.

All the above holds even under the reformulation from Section
3.2
.
This amounts simply to replacing
{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}
and
{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf r}_{-i}
with
{\bf w}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf x}_{i}
and
{\bf w}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf r}_{-i}
, respectively.

4.1.1

Closed-form predictive probabilities

The analytic tractability of the probit carries over to predictions for new observations.
Indeed, the Gaussian form of the global
ep
approximation also has a very convenient side effect that the resulting approximate predictive probabilities for new observations admit a simple closed form.
Considering a new observation
y_{{\textsc{new}}}
having covariate vector
{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}
, by analogy with the exact predictive probability
\Pr{}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}=1\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y})}\big[\Phi\big({\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}\big)\big]
, one gets the
ep
approximation counterpart

\Pr{}_{\textsc{ep}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}=1\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}_{q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})}\big[\Phi\big({\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}\big)\big]=\Phi\big(\big(1+u\big)^{-1/2}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{\textsc{ep}}\big),\vskip-5.69046pt

(10)

where
u={\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{\textsc{ep}}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}
and the last equality in (
10
) follows by Lemma 7.1 in
Azzalini & Capitanio (2014c)
.
The evaluation of (
10
) does not pose any computational difficulties and has a cost that is always dominated by the
ep
algorithm, as the only computationally relevant part is the computation of the quadratic form
u
.
This can be computed directly when using Algorithm
1
(usually, when
p<n
), as
{\bm{\Omega}}_{\textsc{ep}}
is directly returned by the algorithm, and
u
can be computed at cost
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
.
On the other hand, when using Algorithm
2
(usually, when
p>n
), this direct computation can be avoided.
For simplicity, assume once more a spherical Gaussian prior
p({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}},\nu^{2}{\bf I}_{p})
, so that
q_{0}({\bm{\beta}})
is fixed and with natural parameter
{\bf r}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
{\bf Q}_{0}=\nu^{-2}{\bf I}_{p}
.
Then, exploiting (
7
), it can be seen that
u=\nu^{2}\left[{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}-\big({\bf V}^{\intercal}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}\right)^{\intercal}{\bf K}\left({\bf X}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}\right)\big]
, which can be computed at cost
\mathcal{O}(pn)
.
As a consequence of this argument, the closed-form approximation of the exact predictive probability (
10
) can be efficiently computed at cost
\mathcal{O}(p\cdot\min\{p,n\})
from the
ep
parameters.

4.2

Logistic regression

Similarly to the probit case, logistic regression describes binary responses
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
as Bernoulli random variables, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, although now the success probabilities
(1+e^{-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}})^{-1}
are mediated by the logit link.
In such a case, the likelihood terms become

p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})=\Big(\frac{1}{1+e^{-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}}\Big)^{y_{i}}\Big(\frac{1}{1+e^{{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}}\Big)^{1-y_{i}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(2y_{i}-1){\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}}\;.

Contrary to the probit case, the hybrid moments for the logit exact sites do not admit closed-form expressions.
Following
Chopin & Ridgway (2017a)
, we propose a two-fold application of the approximation introduced by
MacKay (1992a)

\begin{split}\int\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}}\phi\big(\eta-\lambda,\,\rho^{2}\big)d\eta\approx\int\Phi\left(\eta\sqrt{\pi/8}\right)\phi\big(\eta-\lambda,\,\rho^{2}\big)d\eta&=\Phi\left(\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{8\pi+\rho^{2}}}\right)\\
&\approx\frac{1}{1+e^{-\lambda/\sqrt{1+\rho^{2}\pi/8}}}\end{split}

Accordingly, the hybrid normalizing constants can be approximated as

Z_{h_{i}}\approx\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}\coloneq\frac{1}{1+e^{-(2y_{i}-1)\lambda_{i}/\sqrt{1+\rho_{i}^{2}\pi/8}}}\;.

Exploiting the fact that
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big((1+e^{-s})^{-1}\big)=(1+e^{-s})^{-1}\big(1-(1+e^{-s})^{-1}\big)
, one gets

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=\frac{(2y_{i}-1)}{\sqrt{1+\rho_{i}^{2}\pi/8}}\,(1-\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}})\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\pi}{8}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{1+\rho_{i}^{2}\pi/8}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}\;.\end{split}

An approximate implementation of the
ep
moment matching is thus obtained replacing
Z_{h_{i}}
,
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log Z_{h_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log Z_{h_{i}}
respectively with
\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
,
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
.

4.3

Poisson regression

We now move beyond the modeling of binary responses, focusing instead on count data
y_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, we focus on Poisson regression under the logarithmic link.
This corresponds to a likelihood of the form

p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})=\frac{\big(e^{{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}\big)^{y_{i}}e^{-(e^{{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}})}}{y_{i}!}\;,

in turn, associated with hybrid normalizing constants

Z_{h_{i}}=\frac{1}{y_{i}!}\,e^{y_{i}\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}}\int e^{-e^{\eta_{i}}}\phi\big(\eta_{i}-(\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}),\,\rho_{i}^{2}\big)d\eta_{i}\;.

(11)

The integral within the expression above does not admit an exact solution.
Indeed, it is easy to see that it coincides with the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}(s)
of a log-normal random variable, evaluated at a specific value of its argument
s
.
More details on the connection with the log-normal Laplace transform are provided in Appendix
S.2
.
Several dedicated approximations have been developed over the years.
As a default for the
ep
updates, we propose exploiting the contribution by
Asmussen et al. (2014c)
, which gives

\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}=\frac{1}{y_{i}!}\,e^{y_{i}\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}}\,\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)\right)}}{\sqrt{1+\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)}}\;.

Here
\mathcal{W}(x)
the so-called
Lambert W function
, which is defined only implicitly as the solution of
\mathcal{W}(x)e^{\mathcal{W}(x)}=x
, but that can be evaluated efficiently in most programming languages.
As proved in Appendix
S.2
, the derivatives of the logarithm of
\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}
read

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=y_{i}-\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)\Big)^{-2}\right)\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}\big(\rho_{i}^{2}e^{\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}\big)\Big)\\
&\quad+\frac{1+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}-y_{i}\Big)\;.\end{split}

Our empirical insights suggest that the above provides an accurate approximation as long as
y_{i}>0
or
\lambda_{i}
is large enough.
Indeed,
Asmussen et al., 2014c
essentially apply Laplace’s method to the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}(s_{i})
of the log-normal distribution.
Such an approximation is exact only in the limit
s_{i}\rightarrow\infty
.
In the context of
ep
Poisson regression,
\mathcal{L}(s_{i})
needs to be evaluated at
s_{i}=\exp{(\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i})}
for the
i^{th}
site.
Observations with
y_{i}=0
and small
\lambda_{i}
clearly fall outside the region of large
s_{i}
.
To preserve accuracy in all settings, we propose employing the alternative approximation scheme by
Rossberg et al. (2008c)
;
Rossberg (2008a)
for sites with
y_{i}=0
and
\lambda_{i}
smaller than some heuristic threshold.
The details on the resulting approximate moment matching are provided in Appendix
S.2
.

4.3.1

Closed-form predictive means

The combination of the logarithmic link with the Gaussian
ep
posterior approximation also brings a very convenient closed-form expression for the predictive mean of a new observation
y_{{\textsc{new}}}
having covariate vector
{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}
, that is
{\mathbb{E}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]
.
Note that this provides the analog of the closed-form predictive probabilities derived for the probit model in Section
4.1.1
as, in that case,
\Pr{}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}=1\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]
.
When a logarithmic link is employed, as in the default choice for the Poisson model (although the same argument holds true for any
glm
s with a logarithmic link), one has

{\mathbb{E}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y})}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{p(y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bm{\beta}})}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}]\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{\beta}}\mid{\bf y})}\left[e^{{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}\right].

(12)

Thus, by replacing the true posterior distribution with its
ep
approximation in (
12
), the
ep
approximate predictive mean for observation
y_{{\textsc{new}}}
is

{\mathbb{E}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}_{q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})}\left[e^{{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}\right].

Now, calling
\eta={\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}
, since
q_{{\textsc{ep}}}({\bm{\beta}})=\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}}-\xi_{{\textsc{ep}}},{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}})
, one has that, according to the
ep
approximation of the posterior distribution of
{\bm{\beta}}
,
\eta\sim N(m_{{\textsc{new}}},s_{{\textsc{new}}}^{2})
, with
m_{{\textsc{new}}}={\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}
and
s_{{\textsc{new}}}^{2}={\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}
.
Consequently,
e^{\eta}
has log-normal distribution with parameters
m_{{\textsc{new}}}
and
s_{{\textsc{new}}}^{2}
and we immediately obtain

{\mathbb{E}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]={\mathbb{E}}_{\eta\sim N(m_{{\textsc{new}}},s_{{\textsc{new}}}^{2})}\left[e^{\eta}\right]=\exp\{m_{{\textsc{new}}}+s_{{\textsc{new}}}^{2}/2\},

where the quadratic form
{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{{\textsc{ep}}}{\bf x}_{{\textsc{new}}}
can be efficiently computed at cost
\mathcal{O}(p\cdot\min{p,n})
, as explained in Section
4.1.1
, without affecting the overall cost of the
ep
procedure.

4.4

Gamma regression

Gamma regression is widely used
glm
to model continuous, non-negative, and positively skewed data.
As the name points out, it postulates gamma distributed responses
y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}}\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{G}a(\upsilon_{i},\varrho({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})/\upsilon_{i})
.
Here we focus on the logarithmic link
\varrho({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})=e^{{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}
since it is the standard choice in the gamma likelihood
glm
, while we will consider the additional shape parameter
\upsilon_{i}>0
to be fixed.
This leads to a likelihood of the form

p(y_{i}\mid{\bm{\beta}})=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\upsilon_{i})}\Big(\frac{\upsilon_{i}}{e^{{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}}\Big)^{\upsilon_{i}}y_{i}^{\upsilon_{i}-1}e^{-\upsilon_{i}y_{i}e^{-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}}}\;,

reflected in hybrid normalizing constants

Z_{h_{i}}=\frac{\upsilon_{i}^{\upsilon_{i}}}{\Gamma(\upsilon_{i})}\,y_{i}^{\upsilon_{i}-1}\,e^{-\upsilon_{i}\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}\upsilon_{i}^{2}}\int e^{-\upsilon_{i}y_{i}e^{\eta_{i}}}\phi\big(\eta_{i}-(\rho_{i}^{2}\upsilon_{i}-\lambda_{i}),\rho_{i}^{2}\big)d\eta_{i}\;.

Similarly to Poisson regression, the required integral takes the form of the log-normal Laplace transform.
Accordingly, the same heuristics exploited before can be adapted to approximate moment matching in gamma regression as well.

7

Discussion

This contribution develops a general and scalable implementation of
ep
for the Bayesian
glm
s under spherical Gaussian prior, making such approximation feasible in scenarios that were not practically possible before
(
Chopin & Ridgway, 2017a
)
.
Such an algorithm provides an approximation of the posterior moments and the marginal likelihood, which may be of practical use for model selection
(
Kass & Raftery, 1995a
)
.
Moreover, being the global approximation Gaussian, this also facilitates the computation of
ep
predictive mean
{\mathbb{E}}[y_{{\textsc{new}}}\mid{\bf y}]
, which is available in closed-form at no additional cost for the probit model (where it coincides with the predictive probability
\Pr[y_{{\textsc{new}}}=1\mid{\bf y}]
), and any
glm
with a logarithmic link (that is the canonical choice for Poisson and Gamma likelihoods).
We further improved computational scalability by considering tailored approximations for the moment-matching step, while preserving accuracy.
As shown on simulated and real data in Sections
5
and
6
, this decreased cost leads to a massive reduction of the running time compared to currently available implementations of
ep
, paving the way to applying such a method in high-dimensional settings.
Considering the quality of the approximation empirically observed in the present work, in
Chopin & Ridgway (2017a)
, and in
Anceschi et al. (2023a)
, this represents a major contribution to Bayesian data regression with responses beyond the continuous case.
In accordance with the robust evidence in the literature,
ep
seems, in general, the preferable choice among approximate methods.
Finally, these empirical results can also motivate future theoretical research about the goodness of the approximation provided by
ep
, for which some preliminary findings on the asymptotic behavior have been derived in
Dehaene & Barthelmé (2015a)
and
Dehaene & Barthelmé (2018a)
.

S.1

Derivation of the results in Section
3

Proof of Lemma
1
and Proposition
1

Adapting the rationale from
Zhou et al. (2023c)
, the (
mgf
)
\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})
of the hybrid distribution
h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})
reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})&={\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}\big[e^{{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf t}}\big]=\int e^{{\bm{\beta}}^{\intercal}{\bf t}}\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\ell_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})\phi_{p}({\bm{\beta}}-{\bm{\xi}}_{i},{\bm{\Omega}}_{i})d{\bm{\beta}}\\
&=\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}e^{{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf t}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\bf t}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}}\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}),{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)d\eta_{i}\;,\end{split}

for any
{\bf t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, with
{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}={\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}
and
{\bm{\xi}}_{i}={\bf Q}_{-i}^{-1}{\bf r}_{-i}
.
The last identity follows from
\eta_{i}={\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\theta}}
and the linear subspace properties of Gaussian measure.
Accordingly, the moments of the hybrid can be obtained as

\begin{split}{\bm{\mu}}_{h_{i}}&={\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bm{\beta}}]=\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{h_{i}}&=\operatorname{var}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bm{\beta}}]=\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})-\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})\nabla_{\bf t}^{\intercal}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})\;.\end{split}

For ease of notation, let us define
\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})=\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}),{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)d\eta_{i}
and
\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})=e^{{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bf t}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}{\bf t}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}}
, so that

\begin{split}\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})&=\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})+\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\\
\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})&=\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})+\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\\
&\quad+\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}^{\intercal}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})+\textstyle{\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}}\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\nabla_{\bf t}^{\intercal}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})\;.\end{split}

Cleary, it holds that
\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})=Z_{h_{i}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})=1
, while it can be seen that
\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})=({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t})\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})
and
\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})={\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})+({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t})({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t})^{\intercal}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})
.
Accordingly, one has

\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})={\bm{\xi}}_{i}\qquad\qquad\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{G}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})={\bm{\Omega}}_{i}+{\bm{\xi}}_{i}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}\;.

Conversely,

\begin{split}\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})&=\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}),{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)\,\\
&\qquad\Big({\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}\big)\Big)d\eta_{i}\\
\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf t})&=-\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}),{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)\,{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\,d\eta_{i}\\
&\quad\,+\int\ell_{i}(\eta_{i})\phi\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}({\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}),{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)\,\\
&\qquad\quad\Big({\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}\big)\Big)\\
&\qquad\quad\Big({\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big(\eta_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf t}\big)\Big)^{\intercal}d\eta_{i}\;,\end{split}

so that

\begin{split}\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})&=Z_{h_{i}}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\big)\\
\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})&=-Z_{h_{i}}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}+Z_{h_{i}}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\;\cdot\\
&\quad\;\cdot\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}\big[({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})^{2}\big]-2{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}+({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i})^{2}\Big)({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\;.\end{split}

Putting it all together, one gets

\begin{split}\nabla_{\bf t}&\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})={\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\big)\\
\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}&\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})={\bm{\xi}}_{i}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}-{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\\
&+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}\big[({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})^{2}\big]-2{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}+({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i})^{2}\Big)({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\\
&+\big({\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}^{\intercal}+{\bm{\xi}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\big)({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\big)\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\nabla_{\bf t}^{2}&\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})-\nabla_{\bf t}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})\nabla_{\bf t}^{\intercal}\mathcal{M}_{h_{i}}({\bf 0})={\bm{\Omega}}_{i}-{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\\
&+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}\big[({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}})^{2}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]^{2}\Big)({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\;.\end{split}

or equivalently that

\begin{split}{\bm{\mu}}_{h_{i}}&={\bm{\xi}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big({\mathbb{E}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\big)\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{h_{i}}&={\bm{\Omega}}_{i}+{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}\big({\operatorname{var}}_{h_{i}({\bm{\beta}})}[{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\beta}}]-{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i}\big)({\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}{\bf x}_{i})^{-1}{\bf x}_{i}^{\intercal}{\bm{\Omega}}_{i}\;.\end{split}

Exploiting Woodbury’s identity, the natural parameters for the updated global
ep
approximant read

Approximation of the log-normal Laplace transform from
Asmussen et al. (2014c)

In this section, we work out the details of the approximation of the Laplace transform for log-normal random variable
\mathcal{L}og\mathcal{N}orm(0,\rho^{2})
, developed in
Asmussen et al. (2014c)
.
The latter is given by the expectation

\mathcal{L}(s)={\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{-se^{X_{0}}}\big]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho^{2}}}\exp{\Big(-se^{x_{0}}-\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2\rho^{2}}\Big)}dx_{0}

where
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2})
, which is defined only for
s>0
.
Extensions to expectations of the form
{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{-se^{X}}\big]
, for
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\varphi,\rho^{2})
, can be then trivially obtained as
\mathcal{L}(se^{\varphi})
.
The authors essentially suggest applying the standard Laplace method to the integral corresponding to the required expectation.
In general, this gives

\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\gamma f(x_{0})}dx_{0}=\frac{e^{-\gamma f(\hat{x}_{0})}}{\sqrt{\gamma f^{\prime\prime}(\hat{x}_{0})}}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{-1})\big)\qquad\text{as}\qquad\gamma\rightarrow\infty\;,

provided that
f(x_{0})
has a unique global minimum at
\hat{x}_{0}\in(a,b)
.
Nonetheless, this approximation can be very accurate not only in asymptotic settings but also for relatively small values of
\gamma

(
Asmussen et al., 2014c
)
.
Although in our context we lack an explicit parameter
\gamma
,
an analogous role can be assigned to the second derivative of
f(\cdot)
at its minimum.
In the case of
\mathcal{L}(s)
,
\gamma f(x_{0})
gets replaced by
g(x_{0};s,\rho^{2})=se^{x_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}^{2}}{2\rho^{2}}
, whose derivatives are trivially obtained as
g^{\prime}(x_{0};s,\rho^{2})=se^{x_{0}}+\frac{x_{0}}{\rho^{2}}
and
g^{\prime\prime}(x_{0};s,\rho^{2})=se^{x_{0}}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}
.
Note that
g(x_{0};s,\rho^{2})
is convex in
x_{0}
if
s>0
.
Hence, its global minimum
\hat{x}_{0}
is obtained by setting to zero the first derivative, which gives
-\hat{x}_{0}e^{-\hat{x}_{0}}=s\rho^{2}
.
Recalling that the
Lambert W function
is defined as the solution of
\mathcal{W}(x)e^{\mathcal{W}(x)}=x
, one gets that
\hat{x}_{0}=-\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})
.
Accordingly

\begin{split}g(\hat{x}_{0};s,\rho^{2})&=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})+\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})^{2}\\
g^{\prime\prime}(\hat{x}_{0};s,\rho^{2})&=\frac{1+\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})}{\rho^{2}}\;.\end{split}

Putting it all together, we Laplace method applied to
\mathcal{L}(s)
gives the approximation

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})}}\exp{\Big(-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})-\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s\rho^{2})^{2}\Big)}\;.

We further highlight that
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathcal{W}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{W}(x)}{1+\mathcal{W}(x)}\frac{1}{x}
, which is going to be useful in the next two Sections for the computations of the derivatives of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(s)
.

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\Big(-\log(y_{i}!)+\lambda_{i}y_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}+\log\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(s_{i})\Big)\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\Big(\lambda_{i}y_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})-\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\log\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)\Big)\\
&=y_{i}-\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\\
&=y_{i}-\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\Big)\frac{\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\frac{1}{s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\\
&=y_{i}-\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)^{-2}\Big)\;,\end{split}

and similarly

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}&=\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})-\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\log\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)\Big)\\
&=\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})+\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})^{2}\Big)\\
&\qquad-\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\\
&=\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\dfrac{\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\Big)\\
&\qquad-\Big(\dfrac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\big(1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\big)+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\Big)\frac{\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}{1+\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}\frac{1}{s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{i}^{2}}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\\
&=\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\dfrac{\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(1+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}(s_{i}\rho_{i}^{2})\Big)+\frac{1+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\log\widetilde{Z}_{h_{i}}-y_{i}\Big)\end{split}

Approximation of the log-normal Laplace transform from
Rossberg et al. (2008c)

Being based on Laplace’s method, the above approximation
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(s_{i})
is expected to be accurate in the regimes with large
s_{i}=\exp{(\lambda_{i}+\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i})}
.
Coherently with this anticipation, our empirical analysis suggests that the scheme by
Asmussen et al. (2014c)
ceases to be sufficiently accurate for observations with
y_{i}=0
, provided that
\lambda_{i}
is not large enough.
Accordingly, in the present section, we introduce an alternative approximation scheme of the log-normal Laplace transform to deal with such cases.

Rossberg et al. (2008c)
rewrite such Laplace transforms in terms of a 2-fold convolution involving the Gaussian cdf, and then considers a
M^{th}
order Taylor expansion of the latter.
Let
\mathcal{M}(s)={\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{sZ}\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{s\varphi e^{\rho X_{0}}}\big]
be the moment generating function of a log-normal
Z=\varphi e^{\rho X_{0}}
, with
X_{0}
being a standard normal.
After performing a change of variable, the authors rewrite the target function as

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}(x)&\coloneq\mathcal{M}(-e^{-\rho x}/\varphi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho\,e^{\rho(x-x^{\prime})}\,e^{-e^{\rho(x-x^{\prime})}}\,\Phi(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\;.\end{split}

Expanding
\Phi(x^{\prime})
around
x
and exchanging the order sum and integral one has

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}(x)&\approx\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{a_{m}}{(-\rho)^{m}}\Phi^{(m)}(x)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{M+1}}\right)\,\end{split}

(S.22)

where
\Phi^{(m)}(\cdot)
is the
m^{th}
derivative of the standard normal cdf, while
M
is a positive integer.
The involved coefficients read
a_{m}=(-1)^{m}/m!\int_{0}^{\infty}du\;e^{-u}(\ln u)^{m}
.
Such approximation is asymptotically exact in
\rho
, but not in
M
.
The authors prove a theoretical argument suggesting
M=6
as a reasonable truncation value.
Following the authors, we rewrite equation (
S.22
) in terms of the error function and its derivatives

\begin{split}\operatorname{erf}(x)&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-w^{2}}\,dw\qquad\qquad\operatorname{erfc}(x)=1-\operatorname{erf}(x)\\
\operatorname{erf}^{(m)}(x)&=2\pi^{-1/2}(-1)^{m-1}H_{m-1}(x)e^{-x^{2}}\;,\end{split}

so that

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(x)&=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{a_{M}}{2}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}-x\rho\right)\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{\rho-x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
&\qquad+\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\frac{a_{M}-a_{m}}{\sqrt{\pi}\big(\rho\sqrt{2}\big)^{m}}H_{s-1}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{M}}\right)\;.\end{split}

Here
H_{m}(x)
denotes the
m^{th}
Hermite polynomials, which benefits from the two useful properties

\begin{split}H_{m+1}(x)=2\,x\,H_{m}(x)-2\,m\,H_{m-1}(x)\qquad\qquad H^{\prime}_{m}(x)=2\,m\,H_{m-1}(x)\;.\end{split}

Accordingly, it is easy to show that

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}(x)&=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\Bigg[\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\frac{a_{M}}{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{(\rho-x)^{2}}{2}\right)\rho\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{\rho-x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)\\
&\qquad-\frac{x}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{a_{M}-a_{1}}{\rho\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{m=2}^{M-1}\frac{a_{M}-a_{m}}{\big(\rho\sqrt{2}\big)^{m}}H_{m}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Bigg]\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime\prime}(x)&=\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\Bigg[-\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}}-\frac{a_{M}}{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{(\rho-x)^{2}}{2}\right)\rho^{2}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{\rho-x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-(\rho+x)\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)\\
&\qquad-\frac{(1-x^{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{a_{M}-a_{1}}{\rho\sqrt{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{m=2}^{M-1}\frac{a_{M}-a_{m}}{\big(\rho\sqrt{2}\big)^{m}}H_{m+1}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Bigg]\;.\end{split}

Adaptation to approximate moments matching in Poisson regression

Similar to what was shown before, it is easy to see the hybrid normalizing constant in the Poisson case can be rephrased as

Z_{h_{i}}=\frac{1}{y_{i}!}\,e^{y_{i}\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{G}\big(-\lambda_{i}/\rho_{i}-y_{i}\rho_{i}\big).

Approximate moment matching can proceed once more exploiting the tractable approximation
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)
and its derivatives.
Notice that in the previous section, we worked out the details for the second derivative of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\cdot)
as well, other than the first one.
This is because of an equivalence formulation of the exact version
\Delta_{i}
as

\Delta_{i}=2\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}\frac{\partial Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial(\rho_{i}^{2})}-\Big(\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}\frac{\partial Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}\frac{\partial^{2}Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}^{2}}-\Big(\frac{1}{Z_{h_{i}}}\frac{\partial Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\Big)^{2}\;.

In the case of the approximation from
Rossberg et al. (2008c)
, we found that approximating the second expression leads to more stable and accurate results, compared to targeting the first formulation.
The required approximate derivatives take the form

\begin{split}\frac{\partial Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=Z_{h_{i}}\left(y_{i}-\frac{1}{\rho_{i}}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\big(s_{i}\big)}{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\big(s_{i}\big)}\right)\qquad\;\frac{\partial^{2}Z_{h_{i}}}{\partial\lambda_{i}^{2}}=Z_{h_{i}}\left(y_{i}^{2}-2y_{i}\frac{1}{\rho_{i}}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\big(s_{i}\big)}{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\big(s_{i}\big)}+\frac{1}{\rho_{i}^{2}}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime\prime}\big(s_{i}\big)}{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\big(s_{i}\big)}\right)\;,\end{split}

where
s_{i}=-\lambda_{i}/\rho_{i}-y_{i}\rho_{i}
.
