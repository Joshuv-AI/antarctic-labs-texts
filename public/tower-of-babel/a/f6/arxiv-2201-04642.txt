Consensus between Epistemic Agents is Difficult
Damian Radosław Sowinski, Jonathan Carroll-Nellenback, Jeremy DeSilva, Adam Frank, Gourab Ghoshal, Marcelo Gleiser, Hari Seldon

Abstract
We introduce an epistemic information measure between two data streams, that we term influence . Closely related to transfer entropy, the measure must be estimated by epistemic agents with finite memory resources via sampling accessible data streams. We show that even under ideal conditions, epistemic agents using slightly different sampling strategies might not achieve consensus in their conclusions about which data stream is influencing which. As an illustration, we examine a real world data stream where different sampling strategies result in contradictory conclusions, explaining why some politically charged topics might exist due to purely epistemic reasons irrespective of the actual ontology of the world.

Introduction

How do epistemic agents (EA)—rationally thinking beings with belief distributions over states of their world—judge information flows occurring throughout the complex systems they partake in?
How does their finiteness—in both space (access) and time (memory)—affect these judgements?
In all but the simplest of cases, information theoretic measures such as channel capacity, Shannon entropy, mutual information, transfer entropy, and others are defined with respect to empirically inaccessible joint probability distributions

II
Model

First lets examine the relevant information measures necessary to model data gathering by an EA, and then build an analytically tractable toy model.
Let us focus on two processes, each generating a stream of qualia
Loar 2003
.
There is uncertainty associated with the future value of each of the qualia, and the processes could be correlated to one another above and beyond any sort of autocorrelation with their past.

Transfer entropy
Massey et al. 1990
;
Schreiber 2000
quantifies the extent to which past correlations between two processes reduce the present uncertainty in either process.
That is, the extent to which an EA can predict the present of one of the processes, given past knowledge of both.
For two processes,
X_{1}(t)\wedge X_{2}(t)
, it is defined as the excess information gained by knowing the past of process
2
in addition to that of process
1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2\rightarrow 1}\!=\!H[X_{1},t|X_{1}^{\prime},t^{\prime}\!<\!t]\!-\!H[X_{1},t|X_{1}^{\prime}\wedge X_{2}^{\prime},t^{\prime}\!<\!t],

(1)

where
H[X]
is the hidden information, i.e., the Shannon entropy of
X
, and
H[X|Y]
is the conditional entropy of
X
conditioned on
Y

Shannon 1948
.
For analytical purposes ahead, it is useful to rewrite the transfer entropy in terms of the mutual information
M[X:Y]
- a measure of how much information is contained in the correlations between random variables
X
and
Y
- expressed as the Kullback-Leibler divergence between joint and product distributions,
M[X:Y]=\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\rho_{XY}||\rho_{X}\!\otimes\!\rho_{Y})
Kullback and Leibler 1951
.
Using this, we can write an alternative expression for the transfer entropy
Cover and Thomas 1991
;
Dembo et al. 1991
;
Gleiser and Sowinski 2018
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2\rightarrow 1}\!=\!M[X_{1},t\!:\!X_{1}^{\prime}\!\wedge\!X_{2}^{\prime},t^{\prime}\!\!<\!t]\!-\!M[X_{1},t\!:\!X_{1}^{\prime},t^{\prime}\!\!<\!t].

(2)

For a finite EA, memory limitations preclude storage of the entire history, so a discrete subset of events is taken at
t^{\prime}\in\{t-n\Delta t\}_{n=1,\dots,N}
, where
N
is the length of the past stored alongside the present.
A schematic of the information diagram is shown in Fig
2
.

The processes in question can be treated symmetrically—how does knowing how
X_{2}
’s history decreases the uncertainty in
X_{1}
’s present compare to knowing how
X_{1}
’s history reduces the uncertainty in
X_{2}
’s present?
This treatment motivates our asymmetric measure of
influence
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ij}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{i\rightarrow j}-\mathcal{T}_{j\rightarrow i}}{\mathcal{T}_{i\rightarrow j}+\mathcal{T}_{j\rightarrow i}}.

(3)

Influence is bounded on the interval
[-1,1]
, saturating iff the transfer entropy in only one direction vanishes.
This will occur when one of the processes is deterministic with vanishing stochasticity.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{i\rightarrow j}\geq 0
, we need only worry about the denominator when both directions vanish - we define
\mathcal{I}_{ij}\equiv 0
in this case.
We note that this interpretation of
influence
is based entirely on correlations, and therefore should not be interpreted as a causal measure
James et al. 2016
;
Barnett et al. 2009
;
Lizier and Prokopenko 2010
.
This seems to be a recurring mistake in the literature, as transfer entropy reduces to the misleadingly named Granger-
Causality
in simple cases
Barnett et al. 2009
.

As most people do not have the priors of a scientist, let alone a climatologist or geophysicist, it is reasonable to model this argument as an EA with a limited history of a decade attempting to refine their beliefs.

We examine the transfer entropy between measured CO

Bootstrapping is used to get an ensemble of data streams in order to understand qualitatively the associated errors in the information measures.

We experimented with substreams of many different length and found the results robust for lengths up to

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

Our results show that dealing with multiple epistemic agents is problematic on two fronts.
Even if the EAs have access to identical data to inform their beliefs about histories, the differences in their temporal sampling and memory usage can result in a lack of consensus on influence judgements.
This is under ideal conditions, where the processes are perfectly accessible to all the EAs.
Moving away from these ideal conditions, we believe, can only make the problem worse.
Our results may have implications for issues surrounding the difficulty of reaching consensus under conditions of ideological polarization.

These results have implications for model-agnostic machine learning.
Deep Bayesian architectures utilize information theoretic measures to
learn
from data.
However, such learning demands efficient storage of belief distributions in lieu of enormous datasets, and is therefore subject to historical sampling.
We predict that algorithms with memory usage optimized to specific hardware architectures will encounter the consensus problem we describe when compared with identical algorithms running on different architectures.

Finally, since our work reveals how problematic reaching consensus is for multiple epistemic agents under ideal conditions, it begs further questions concerning the judgements made by groups of thinking entities.
To what extent do these results hold for more than two data/qualia streams?
Transfer entropy has been shown to be able to detect information
circuits
in groups of interacting agents
Bettencourt, Luís MA and Gintautas, Vadas and
Ham, Michael I 2008
.
Based on our results, one can investigate the influence structure of these circuits, and how spatial and temporal coarse graining affect judgements.
These questions are crucial in anticipation of extending this work to the dynamics of group formation in models of interaction in social organisms
Jiang et al. 2017
;
R. Vahdati et al. 2019
.

A1. Solving the Equations of Motion

A.1
Dimensionalization

The equations of motion can be written as the vector process

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{X}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-{\bm{A}}\cdot{\bm{X}}+{\bm{B}}\cdot{\bm{\eta}}

\displaystyle\text{where }{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}&-\alpha_{1}\\
-\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{2}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }{\bm{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{1}&0\\
0&\beta_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

and the white noise first and second moments read
\langle{\bm{\eta}}\rangle={\bm{0}}
and
\langle{\bm{\eta}}(t)\cdot{\bm{\eta}}(t^{\prime})^{T}\rangle=\mathds{1}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
We begin by scaling the variables using the length and time scales constructed in the main text,

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ell\tilde{{\bm{X}}}\hskip 10.0ptt\rightarrow\tau\tilde{t}\hskip 10.0pt{\bm{\eta}}\rightarrow\tau^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{{\bm{\eta}}},

(8)

and introducing the asymmetry parameters
a,b\in[-1,1]
, defined as

\displaystyle a=\frac{\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\hskip 10.0ptb=\frac{\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}},

(9)

the scaled operators become

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{A}}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+a&-1-a\\
-1+a&1-a\end{bmatrix}\ \ \tilde{{\bm{B}}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+b&0\\
0&1-b\end{bmatrix}.

The equations of motion are then the same as before

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{{\bm{X}}}}{d\tilde{t}}=-\tilde{{\bm{A}}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{X}}}+\tilde{{\bm{B}}}\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\eta}}}.

Henceforth we drop the tildes and work in the dimensionless variables.

A.2
Diagonalization

In this section we will diagonalize the equations of motion in order to show that our system is Gaussian.
The eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
are
0
and
1
.
The first of these represents the translational symmetry of the system;
X_{i}\mapsto X_{i}+c
, for constant
c

The corresponding eigenvectors are

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\hskip 10.0pt{\bm{v}}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+a^{2})}}\begin{bmatrix}-(1+a)\\
1-a\end{bmatrix}

From these we construct the similarity matrix that takes us into the diagonal frame,
{\bm{S}}=[{\bm{v}}_{0}{\bm{v}}_{1}]
.
There the variables are the displacement and the center of mass,
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{S}}^{-1}\cdot{\bm{X}}

\displaystyle Y_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+a^{2}}{2}}(X_{2}-X_{1})\hskip 10.0ptY_{1}=\frac{1-a}{\sqrt{2}}X_{1}+\frac{1+a}{\sqrt{2}}X_{2},

Note that the center of mass is weighed by the deterministic coupling parameter.
Meanwhile, the rotated noise is
{\bm{\xi}}={\bm{S}}^{-1}\cdot{\bm{B}}\cdot{{\bm{\eta}}}

The original noise was uncorrelated and Gaussian — we see that the rotated noise will have non-vanishing cross correlations

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{\xi}}(t)\cdot{\bm{\xi}}^{T}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle={\bm{S}}^{-1}\cdot{\bm{B}}\cdot\langle\tilde{{\bm{\eta}}}(t)\cdot\tilde{{\bm{\eta}}}^{T}(t^{\prime})\rangle\cdot{\bm{B}}^{T}\cdot{\bm{S}}^{-T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}(1+a^{2})(1+b^{2})&\sqrt{1+a^{2}}(a-2b+ab^{2})\\
\sqrt{1+a^{2}}(a-2b+ab^{2})&1+a^{2}-4ab+b^{2}+a^{2}b^{2}\end{bmatrix}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

Diagonalizing the deterministic coupling of the degrees of freedom has introduced a stochastic coupling of the noise experienced by the degrees of freedom.
In this frame the equations of motion read

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{1}}{dt}=-Y_{1}+\xi_{1}\hskip 10.0pt\frac{dY_{2}}{dt}=\xi_{2}\hskip 10.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.0pt\frac{d{\bm{Y}}}{d\tilde{t}}=-{\bm{\Lambda}}\cdot{\bm{Y}}+{\bm{\xi}},

where
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\text{diag}(1,0)
is the eigenvalue matrix.
We recognize the first of these as a standard Wiener process, and the second as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
We say that they are uncoupled deterministicaly, but remain coupled stochastically due to the cross-correlation between noise.
Both processes are Gaussian, so we see that our original system is Gaussian - it can be described completely by the covariance matrix since higher order correlations can all be derived from it.

The system can be solved by using an integration factor,

The solution in the diagonal frame is

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}(t-t_{0})}\cdot{\bm{Y}}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!ds\ \ e^{-{\bm{\Lambda}}(t-s)}\cdot{\bm{\xi}}(s)

\displaystyle e^{{\bm{\Lambda}}t}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}+{\bm{\Lambda}}t+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}t^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{3}t^{3}\cdots

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}+\left(t+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}t^{3}+\cdots\right){\bm{\Lambda}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}+\left(e^{t}-1\right){\bm{\Lambda}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\ e^{t}\ &\ 0\ \ \\[0.0pt]
\ 0\ &\ 1\ \ \end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{X}}}(t)=e^{-{{\bm{A}}}(t-t_{0})}\cdot{{\bm{X}}}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!ds\ \ e^{-{{\bm{A}}}(t-s)}\cdot{{\bm{B}}}\cdot{{\bm{\eta}}}

We could have gotten this directly from the original equations of motion, however we wanted to connect the system to well known Gaussian processes.

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+a^{2})(1+b^{2})-4ab}{8}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\min(t-t_{0},t^{\prime}-t_{0})+\frac{a(1+b^{2})-2b}{8}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\times\!\!\begin{bmatrix}(1\!+\!a)(1+e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|})&-(1\!-\!a)e^{-\max(t^{\prime}\!-\!t,0)}\!+\!(1\!+\!a)e^{-\max(t\!-\!t^{\prime},0)})\\
(1\!+\!a)e^{-\max(t^{\prime}\!-\!t,0)}\!-\!(1\!-\!a)e^{-\max(t\!-\!t^{\prime},0)}&-(1\!-\!a)(1+e^{-|t\!-\!t^{\prime}|})\end{bmatrix}\!\!

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\times(1\!-\!e^{-\min(t\!-\!t_{0},t^{\prime}\!-\!t_{0})})+\frac{1+b^{2}}{16}e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|}\begin{bmatrix}(1+a)^{2}&-(1-a^{2})\\
-(1-a^{2})&(1-a)^{2}\end{bmatrix}(1-e^{-2\min(t-t_{0},t^{\prime}-t_{0})})

Notice that the first term grows with
\min(t-t_{0},t^{\prime}-t_{0})
, representing the diffusion of the underlying Wiener process- this dominates the covariance at large times.
The second term is the slow contribution coming form the coupling, while the third term is the fast contribution - the latter grows at twice the rate of the former.
The parenthetical expressions to the right of both of these terms saturate to unity for long times,
t,t^{\prime}\gg t_{0}
.
There is an interesting space of times early on when these terms are large enough compared to the first term that the covariance fluctuates.
Since
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t^{\prime})^{T}={\bm{\Sigma}}(t^{\prime},t)
, without loss of generality we choose
t^{\prime}=t+\Delta t>t
, which gives us a little space saving since we can replace

\displaystyle\min(t-t_{0},t^{\prime}-t_{0})=t-t_{0}\hskip 20.0pt\max(t-t^{\prime},0)=0\hskip 10.0pt\max(t^{\prime}-t,0)=\Delta t

so that

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+a^{2})(1+b^{2})-4ab}{8}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}(t-t_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt+\frac{a(1+b^{2})-2b}{8}\!\!\begin{bmatrix}(1\!+\!a)(1+e^{-\Delta t})&-(1\!-\!a)e^{-\Delta t}\!+\!(1\!+\!a)\\
(1\!+\!a)e^{-\Delta t}\!-\!(1\!-\!a)&-(1\!-\!a)(1+e^{-\Delta t})\end{bmatrix}\!\!(1\!-\!e^{-(t\!-\!t_{0})})

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt+\frac{1+b^{2}}{16}e^{-\Delta t}\begin{bmatrix}(1+a)^{2}&-(1-a^{2})\\
-(1-a^{2})&(1-a)^{2}\end{bmatrix}(1-e^{-2(t-t_{0})}).

(12)

This form shows us that the fast term decays with
\Delta t
, representing a short timescale correlation.
Meanwhile, the slow term gives a non-vanishing constant contribution to the long timescale correlations.
In either case, these are subdominant to the diffusion term.

B.2
Belief Distribution

The EA’s belief distribution over possible paths is required.
Since our process is Gaussian, the means and covariance between two histories are sufficient to completely describe the distribution.
We denote the former
\mu_{i}(t)=\langle X_{i}(t)\rangle
, and the latter with
\Sigma_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle(X_{i}(t)-\langle X_{i}(t)\rangle)(X_{j}(t^{\prime})-\langle X_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle\rangle
.
It is helpful to define the two instance covariance matrix via

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t,t)&\Sigma_{12}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\Sigma_{21}(t^{\prime},t)&\Sigma_{22}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Choosing a time discretization,
\Delta t
, we write the two histories
{\bm{x}}=[X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),X_{1}(t-\Delta t),X_{2}(t-\Delta t),\dots,X_{1}(t-N\Delta t),X_{2}(t-N\Delta t)]
, the mean history
{\bm{\mu}}_{N}=[\mu_{1}(t),\mu_{2}(t),\mu_{1}(t-\Delta t),\mu_{2}(t-\Delta t),\dots,\mu_{1}(t-N\Delta t),\mu_{2}(t-N\Delta t)]
and the covariance history

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}(\Delta t)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t)&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t-\Delta t)&\cdots&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t,t-N\Delta t)\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-\Delta t,t)&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-\Delta t,t-\Delta t)&\cdots&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-\Delta t,t-N\Delta t)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-N\Delta t,t)&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-N\Delta t,t-\Delta t)&\cdots&{\bm{\Sigma}}(t-N\Delta t,t-N\Delta t)\end{bmatrix}

(14)

then the Gaussian approximation for the distribution over two histories reads

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{x}};{\bm{\mu}}_{N},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N})\!=\!\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N}|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\!\!\left(\!\!-\frac{1}{2}({\bm{x}}\!-\!{\bm{\mu}}_{N})\!\cdot\!{\bm{\Sigma}}_{N}^{-1}\!\cdot\!({\bm{x}}\!-\!{\bm{\mu}}_{N})\!\!\right).

(15)

We mention that this form is amenable to numerical computations, since removing certain rows and columns gives the matrices necessary for the computation of the mutual information, as we shall now see.

B.3
Information Measures

With the belief distribution we can exactly compute the transfer entropy.
Consider subdividing our history vector into two disjoint parts,
{\bm{x}}=[{\bm{x}}_{A}\ {\bm{x}}_{B}]
.
Then the history mean breaks up similarly, while the history covariance breaks into

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{AA}&{\bm{\Sigma}}_{AB}\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{BA}&{\bm{\Sigma}}_{BB}\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

With these, the mutual information between the disjoint subsets of events is the well known result for multivariate Gaussians,

\displaystyle M[A:B]=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{AA}||{\bm{\Sigma}}_{BB}|}{|{\bm{\Sigma}}|}.

(17)

It is clear that if the subsets are uncorrelated,
|{\bm{\Sigma}}|=|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{AA}||{\bm{\Sigma}}_{BB}|
, the logarithm is unity and the mutual information vanishes.
Using Eq.
17
in the mutual information form of the transfer entropy, let’s do the
N=1
calculation explicitly.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2\rightarrow 1}\!

\displaystyle=\!M[X_{1},t\!:\!X_{1}^{\prime}\!\wedge\!X_{2}^{\prime},t^{\prime}=t-\Delta t]\!-\!M[X_{1},t\!:\!X_{1}^{\prime},t^{\prime}=t-\Delta t]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t,t)\end{vmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{12}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\\
\Sigma_{21}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{22}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t,t)&\Sigma_{11}(t,t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{12}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\Sigma_{11}(t,t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{11}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{12}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\\
\Sigma_{12}(t,t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{12}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{22}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{vmatrix}}}-\frac{1}{2}\log{\frac{\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t,t)\end{vmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}\Sigma_{11}(t,t)&\Sigma_{11}(t,t^{\prime})\\
\Sigma_{11}(t,t^{\prime})&\Sigma_{11}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\end{vmatrix}}}

One can play around with this expression, but, in the end, cannot escape taking the determinants and creating an algebraic mess of gargantuan size— the expressions for
N>1
are even more space consuming, since the determinants can gain up to
2
additional rows and columns each time
N
goes up by
1
.
Rather than opting for the analytical expressions, this is where we went the numerical route, implementing the determinants pointwise across a finite domain of all relevant variables.
This allowed us to compute values of
N
up to about
100
, at which point computational time created a large bottleneck which prevented further investigation.
It is clear that the limits
N\rightarrow\infty,\Delta t\rightarrow 0
are interesting since they correspond to ideal epistemic agents.
The transfer entropy in the opposite direction is computed by simply exchanging
1\leftrightarrow 2
.
With the two information flows, the influence is easily computed.
