Second cohomology groups of the Hopf ∗ -algebras associated to universal unitary quantum groups
Biswarup Das, Uwe Franz, Anna Kula, Adam Skalski

Abstract
. We compute the second (and the first) cohomology groups of ∗ -algebras associated to the universal quantum unitary groups of not necesarily Kac type, extending our earlier results for the free unitary group U d + U_{d}^{+} . The extended setup forces us to use infinite-dimensional representations to construct the cocycles.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 16E40; Secondary 16T20

1.
Introduction

The problem of computing Hochschild cohomology groups related to Hopf algebras associated to compact quantum groups was given a strong initial impetus in the work of Collins, Härtel and Thom (
[
CHT
]
), who considered free quantum orthogonal groups, and since then has seen a lot of progress, mainly due to Bichon (see [Bic
1-3
] and references therein). The original motivation of
[
CHT
]
was related to trying to understand whether the von Neumann algebras of free orthogonal groups might be isomorphic to free group factors, which was only very recently disproved by Brannan and Vergnioux (
[
BrV
]
).
Since then computing the relevant cohomology groups were seen to have many connections to other parts of cohomological algebra, but also for example to the classification of quantum Lévy processes: for these we refer to
[
Bic2
]
and to
[
DFKS
]
.

In this article we compute the first and the second Hochschild
cohomology of the universal unitary quantum groups of Wang and Van Daele (
[
WvD
]
), denoted
U_{Q}^{+}
,
for almost all choices of a positive invertible matrix
Q\in M_{d}
(with one exception appearing in the case of the matrix
Q
with three distinct eigenvalues), extending the methods from

[
DFKS
]
, where only the free case, i.e.
Q=I
, was treated. Therein, we
showed among other things that

H^{2}(U_{I_{d}}^{+})\cong sl(d)\cong\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}-1}.

where
sl(d)
denotes the space of
d\times d
complex matrices with trace
zero. We have also studied in that paper the free orthogonal quantum groups, although for them the result had already been known due to
[
CHT
,
Bic1
]
. Our method was in a sense much more elementary than those applied in the papers cited above; in particular it allowed us to produce in each case a concrete minimal sets cocycles which generate the second cohomology groups, each of which was built as the cup-product of
1
-cocycles with values in certain finite-dimensional modules.

The study in this paper follows first a similar line of reasoning: given a general unitary quantum group
U_{Q}^{+}
computing the first cohomology group is easy (see Proposition
3.2
), and then we can define what we call the defect map, whose image (a certain subspace of
M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
) can be shown to be isomorphic to the second cohomology group. Once we identify natural restrictions that the matrices in the subspace satisfy, it remains to construct cocycles for which the defect map would attain the desired values. Here the generality with which we work in this paper makes a significant difference in comparison to
[
DFKS
]
. We show that it is in general impossible to produce sufficiently many
2
-cocycles as cup products of finite-dimensional
1
-cocycles. However it turns out that in most cases we can use a concrete infinite-dimensional representation to solve the problem, which leads us to the main result of this paper.

Theorem
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a strictly positive matrix, whose eigenvalue list is not of the form
(p,pq,pq^{2})
with
p>0,q\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}
.
Then

H^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})\simeq sl_{Q}(d),

where
sl_{Q}(d)=\{A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C}):AQ=QA,\;\textup{Tr}(AQ)=\textup{Tr}(AQ^{-1})=0\}
.

The detailed plan of the paper is as follows: after we conclude this introduction, in Section
2
we recall basic facts concerning on one hand the universal unitary quantum groups and the representations of the associated
∗
-algebras, and on the other abstract Hochshild cohomology of unital
∗
-algebras. In Section 3 we discuss
1
-cocycles, including those related to not-necessarily trivial bimodules. Finally Section 4 contains the main results of the paper: we first introduce the defect map, and then compute its image, leading to the final theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout we will work with complex unital
∗
-algebras, and by a representation of such an algebra
A
we will understand a unital
∗
-homomorphism from
A
to
B(H)
, where
H
is a Hilbert space. We write
\mathbb{N}_{0}
for
\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

If
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is a strictly positive matrix, we will call another matrix
A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
a
Q
-matrix
if it commutes with
A
; once we find a basis with respect to which

Q
is diagonal, so that
Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}P_{i}
, with
q_{i}>0
,
q_{i}\neq q_{j}
for
i\neq j
,
i,j=1,\ldots n
, and
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
orthogonal projections summing up to
1
, the
Q
-matrices are those which are block-diagonal with respect to the decomposition given by
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
. We will also use this terminology for matrices in
M_{d}(X)
when
X
is an arbitrary vector space. Recall that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the set of all matrices in
M_{d}
of trace
0
by
sl(d)
. By analogy we denote by
sl_{Q}(d)
the set of all
Q
-matrices in
M_{d}
of the ‘
Q
-trace’ and ‘
Q^{-1}
-trace’ equal
0
:

(2.1)

sl_{Q}(d)=\{A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C}):AQ=QA,\textup{Tr}(AQ)=\textup{Tr}(AQ^{-1})=0\}.

Note that for
Q
with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
we have a natural inclusion

sl(d_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus sl(d_{n})\subset sl_{Q}(d).

Universal unitary quantum groups

For a matrix
F\in GL_{d}(\mathbb{C})
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
) the
universal unitary quantum group
(in the sense of
Banica, cf.
[
Ban
]
) was defined via the universal unital
∗
-algebra generated by
d^{2}
elements
u_{jk}
(
j,k=1,2,...d
) such that the matrix

u:=(u_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{d}
is unitary and its conjugate,
\bar{u}
, is similar to a unitary via
F
. More specifically, the following conditions hold:

{\mbox{(R1)}}\ uu^{*}=I=u^{*}u;\qquad{\mbox{(R2)}}\ F\bar{u}F^{-1}(F\bar{u}F^{-1})^{*}=I=(F\bar{u}F^{-1})^{*}F\bar{u}F^{-1}.

Let us observe that the relation (R2) is equivalent to the equality

u^{t}Q\overline{u}Q^{-1}=I=Q\overline{u}Q^{-1}u^{t}.

with the positive matrix
Q=F^{*}F
. Thus the algebra above is
isomorphic to the
∗
-algebra canonically associated with the universal unitary quantum group
U^{+}_{Q}
in the sense of Wang and Van Daele, cf.
[
WvD
]
, i.e. the universal unital
∗
-algebra

A_{u}(Q)

generated by the coefficients of
u:=(u_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{d}
satisfying (R1) and
(R3). It follows from Proposition 6.4.7 in
[
Tim
]
that up to an isomorphism of
∗
-algebras (of compact matrix type) without loss
of generality we can (and will) assume that the matrix
Q
is diagonal and write for short
Q_{j}=Q_{jj}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. In that case, the relation (
2
) reads

(2.2)

\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}u_{pj}u^{*}_{pk}=\delta_{jk}1,\qquad\;\;\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}u^{*}_{jp}u_{kp}=\delta_{jk}1,\;\;\;j,k=1,\ldots,d.

The algebra
A_{u}(Q)
is naturally equipped with the standard comultiplication (which will not play a role in this paper).
We will also write
A_{u}(d):=A_{u}(I_{d})
,
U^{+}_{d}:=U^{+}_{I_{d}}
and call
U^{+}_{d}
the
free unitary quantum group
.
Note that we have a distinguished character (one-dimensional representation)
\epsilon:A_{u}(Q)\to\mathbb{C}
given by the formula

\epsilon(u_{jk})=\delta_{jk},\;\;\;j,k=1,\ldots,d.

Given a positive diagonal matrix
Q
with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
one has natural inclusions of quantum groups
U^{+}_{d_{1}},\ldots,U^{+}_{d_{n}}
into
U_{Q}^{+}
, manifesting themselves as surjective unital
∗
-homomorphisms
\pi_{i}:A_{u}(Q)\to A_{u}(d_{i})
, with
i=1,\ldots,n
, with the respective algebras viewed as the algebras of ‘polynomials’ on respective quantum groups. The homomorphisms
\pi_{i}
are given by mapping generators in the appropriate matrix block to generators of the smaller algebra, and other generators to
0
or
1
, depending on whether they lie on the diagonal or not.

By the universality property there is a one-to-one correspondence between representations
\pi
of
A
on a Hilbert space
H
and unitary matrices
R\in M_{d}(B(H))
such that

(2.3)

R^{t}Q\bar{R}Q^{-1}=I=Q\bar{R}Q^{-1}R^{t},

with
R_{jk}=\pi(u_{jk}),j,k=1,\ldots,d
.
In fact all finite-dimensional representations of
A_{u}(Q)
have a particular, ‘block-diagonal’ form: for such representations
R
is a
Q
-matrix in the sense discussed above. This follows for example from
[
Sol
, Theorem 4.9]
; we refer for the details to the note
[
DFS
]
. Note that for a unitary
Q
-matrix
R\in M_{d}(B(H))
the condition (
2.3
) reduces to
R^{t}\bar{R}=I=\bar{R}R^{t}
.
For brevity, we will further call representations
\pi
for which the matrix
R
is a
Q
-matrix simply
Q
-representations
.

Here we record the existence of certain infinite-dimensional representations, which will be of use later. Let
q\in(0,1)
and let
\alpha,\gamma
denote the standard generators of the algebra
{\rm Pol}(SU_{q}(2))
, as introduced in
[
Wor
]
, i.e. elements satisfying the commutation relations:

\alpha\gamma=q\gamma\alpha,\;\;\alpha\gamma^{*}=q\gamma^{*}\alpha,\;\;\gamma^{*}\gamma=\gamma\gamma^{*},

\alpha^{*}\alpha+\gamma^{*}\gamma=1,\;\;\alpha\alpha^{*}+q^{2}\gamma^{*}\gamma=1,

so that

U:=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-q\gamma^{*}\\
\gamma&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix}

is a unitary matrix (the fundamental representation of
SU_{q}(2)
). It is then well-known, and checkable via a direct computation, that
U
satisfies the defining relations of
A_{u}(Q)
for
Q=\textup{diag}(1,q^{2})
. In other words,
SU_{q}(2)
is a quantum subgroup of the corresponding
U_{Q}^{+}
.

This means that when we consider a concrete realisation of
{\rm Pol}(SU_{q}(2))
on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
given by the formulas (for simplicity we just use the same symbols for the relevant operators)

\alpha e_{0}=0,\ \alpha e_{k}=\sqrt{1-q^{2k}}e_{k-1},k\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\gamma e_{l}=q^{l}e_{l},\;\;l\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

we immediately obtain a representation of
A_{u}(Q)
, with
Q
as above. Similarly the matrix

(2.4)

R=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})}&0&0\\
0&\alpha&-q\gamma^{*}\\
0&\gamma&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix}

determines a representation of
A_{u}(Q)
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
for
Q=\textup{diag}(p^{2},1,q^{2})
,
p>0
. Note that none of the above representations is a
Q
-representation.

Cohomology groups for CQG algebras

Let
A
be a unital
∗
-algebra with character
\varepsilon:A\to\mathbb{C}
.
We will be interested in the Hochschild cohomology of
A
with
trivial coefficients (i.e. with the bimodule in question being
\mathbb{C}
with left and right action given by
\varepsilon
), and we are
going to use the following notations (
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\partial
denotes the standard boundary operator, so that
for each
\psi\in L(A^{\otimes(m-1)};\mathbb{C})
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in A
we have

(\partial\psi)(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m})=\varepsilon(a_{1})\psi(a_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m})+\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\psi(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes(a_{j}a_{j+1})\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m})+\psi(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{m-1})\varepsilon(a_{m})
)

\displaystyle C^{m}(A)

\displaystyle=C^{m}(A,{{}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{C}_{\varepsilon})=\{c:A^{\otimes m}\to\mathbb{C},\mbox{ linear}\}\quad\mbox{($m$-cochains)};

\displaystyle Z^{m}(A)

\displaystyle=Z^{m}(A,{{}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{C}_{\varepsilon})=\{c\in C^{m}(A):\partial c=0\}\quad\mbox{($m$-cocycles)};

\displaystyle B^{m}(A)

\displaystyle=B^{m}(A,{{}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{C}_{\varepsilon})=\{c\in Z^{m}(A):\exists_{\psi\in C^{m-1}(A)}\ \ c=\partial\psi\}\quad\mbox{($m$-coboundaries});

\displaystyle H^{m}(A)

\displaystyle=Z^{m}(A)/B^{m}(A).

In the case when
A=A_{u}(Q)
with the standard character we will also use the notation
H^{m}(U_{Q}^{+})
for
H^{m}(A_{u}(Q))
and speak simply of the
m
-th cohomology group of the quantum group
U_{Q}^{+}
.
Note that if we are given two algebras
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
as above, with respective characters
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
, and a surjective
∗
-homomorphism
\pi:A_{1}\to A_{2}
such that
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}\circ\pi
, then any cocycle in
Z^{m}(A_{2})
gives rise to a cocycle in
Z^{m}(A_{1})
simply by composing with an appropriate tensor power of
\pi
. Taking into account the comments in the last subsection this means that given a positive diagonal matrix
Q
with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
we have a natural map from
Z^{m}(U^{+}_{d_{i}})
to
Z^{m}(U^{+}_{Q})
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Naturally the explicit computation of the second cohomology group of a given algebra formally amounts to first obtaining a criterion which allows one to decide when a given
2
-cocycle is a coboundary, and then
finding means to construct sufficiently many ‘non-trivial’ cocycles. We will now recall two results which will assist us in that matter; they also formed the key abstract tools in
[
DFKS
]
.

Given linear maps
\psi:A\to\mathbb{C}
and
c:A\otimes A\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\psi(1)=0
,
c(1\otimes 1)=0
, we define
T_{c,\psi}:A\to{\rm End}(\mathbb{C}\oplus\ker\varepsilon\oplus\mathbb{C})
by

T_{c,\psi}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(a)&c(a\otimes-)&-\psi(a)\\
0&a\cdot-&a-\varepsilon(a)\\
0&0&\varepsilon(a)\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;a\in A.

The following result is
[
BFG
, Lemma 5.4]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
c
be a 2-cocycle on
A
, i.e.
c\in Z^{2}(A,_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{C}_{\varepsilon})
, and let
\psi:A\to\mathbb{C}
be linear, with
\psi(1)=0
,
c(1\otimes 1)=0
. The mapping
T_{c,\psi}
is a homomorphism if and only if
c\in B^{2}(A,\mathbb{C})
and
c=-\partial\psi
.

Although we are primarily interested in cohomology with trivial coefficients, as is well-known, 2-cocycles can be constructed by combining 1-cocycles with values in suitable bimodules via the so-called cup product.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\pi:A\to B(H)
be a representation of
A
on a Hilbert
space
H
, so that we can view
H
as an
A
-bimodule with a left action given by
\pi
and the right action given by
\varepsilon
. For
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in Z^{1}(A,{{}_{\pi}}H_{\varepsilon})
define
c(x\otimes y)=\langle\eta_{1}(x^{*}),\eta_{2}(y)\rangle_{H}
for
x,y\in A
and extend the resulting map linearly to
A^{\otimes 2}
. Then
c\in Z^{2}(A)
.

Proof.

Direct computation; see also for example
[
FGT
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

3.
One-cocycles and the first cohomology group for
U^{+}_{Q}

From now on we assume that
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is a strictly positive diagonal matrix.

In this section we will consider 1-cocycles on
A_{u}(Q)
. Anticipating the use of Lemma
2.2
we will first look at 1-cocycles with respect to arbitrary Hilbert space representations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\pi:A_{u}(Q)\to B(H)
be a representation (on a Hilbert space
H
), with the associated matrix
R\in M_{d}(B(H))
. Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between cocycles
\eta\in Z^{1}(A_{u}(Q),_{\pi}H_{\varepsilon})
and matrices
V\in M_{d}(H)
such that

(3.1)

(R^{*}V)^{t}=\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t}Q.

The correspondence is given by

\eta(u_{jk})=V_{jk},\;\;\;j,k=1,\ldots,d.

Proof.

Consider a
\pi
-
\varepsilon
-cocycle
\eta:A_{u}(Q)\to H
. The cocycle property implies that
\eta
is uniquely determined by two matrices with entries in
H
,
V=[\eta(u_{jk})]_{j,k=1}^{d}
and

W=[\eta(u_{jk}^{*})]_{j,k=1}^{d}
. A direct computation using the cocycle property and the defining relations of
A_{u}(Q)
shows that

(3.2)

RW^{t}=-V,\quad R^{*}V=-W^{t},\quad V^{t}=-R^{t}QWQ^{-1},\quad QWQ^{-1}=-Q\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t};

for example the first equality follows from the fact that

0=\eta(\delta_{jk}1)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\eta(u_{jp}u_{kp}^{*})=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(\pi(u_{jp})\eta(u_{kp}^{*})+\eta(u_{jp})\varepsilon(u_{kp})^{*}\right)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(R_{jp}W_{kp}+V_{jp}\delta_{kp}\right)

for all
j,k=1,\ldots,d
.
Comparing the second and the fourth displayed equalities and using the fact that
R
is unitary we get precisely (
3.1
).

Hence the condition (
3.1
) is necessary for a matrix
V
to define a cocycle. To see that it is also sufficient, given such a
V
define
W=-\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t}Q
.
Then the pair
V,W
satisfies the relations (
3.2
). Indeed, the fourth equality is just the definition of
W
; for the third we multiply the
fourth equation by
R^{t}
(from the left) getting

R^{t}QWQ^{-1}=-\underbrace{R^{t}Q\bar{R}Q^{-1}}_{=I\textup{ by }\eqref{RQmatrix}}V^{t}=-V^{t};

the second equality follows from (
3.1
); composing both sides
of the second equality with
R
leads to the first one.

Therefore the mapping
\eta:A_{u}(Q)\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

\eta(u_{jk})=V_{jk},\quad\eta(u_{jk}^{*})=W_{jk},\;\;\;j,k=1,\ldots,d

extends, via the linearity and the cocycle property, to the whole
A_{u}(Q)
.
∎

The lemma naturally applies to the case of
\pi=\varepsilon
and immediately yields the first cohomology groups for
A_{u}(Q)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Q
be a positive matrix with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
. Then

H^{1}(U_{Q}^{+})=H^{1}(U_{d_{1}}^{+})\oplus\cdots\oplus H^{1}(U_{d_{n}}^{+})\cong M_{d_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus M_{d_{n}}.

Proof.

The space
H^{1}(U_{Q}^{+})
consists simply of 1-cocycles in
Z^{1}(U_{Q}^{+})
. As for
\pi=\varepsilon
the matrix
R
is just the identity, Lemma
3.1
implies that any such cocycle
corresponds to a
Q
-block matrix.
∎

We finish the section with another lemma which says that the matrices corresponding to cocycles with respect to
Q
-representations always take a special form (recall that finite-dimensional representations are automatically
Q
-representations).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space, let
\pi:A_{u}(Q)\to B(H)
be a
Q
-representation and let
\eta\in Z_{1}(A_{u}(Q),_{\pi}H_{\varepsilon})
. Then the matrix
V\in M_{d}(H)
associated with
\eta
is a
Q
-matrix.

Proof.

Assume as usual that
Q
is diagonal, with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
and corresponding eigenspaces of dimensions
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
.
Let
R\in M_{d}(B(H))
be the matrix associated with
\pi
.

Begin by expressing the formula (
3.1
) in the block-diagonal form. Let
j,k=1,\ldots,n
be indices describing blocks of our
d
by
d
matrices; recall that off-diagonal blocks of
R
and
Q
vanish. Thus on one hand

((R^{*}V)^{t})_{jk}=[(R^{*}V)_{kj}]^{t}=[R^{*}_{kk}V_{kj}]^{t},

and on the other

(\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t}Q)_{jk}=\bar{R}_{jj}q_{j}^{-1}(V^{t})_{jk}q_{k}=\bar{R}_{jj}q_{j}^{-1}[V_{kj}]^{t}q_{k}.

For simplicity we denote thus by
Z
the
H
-valued matrix
V_{k,j}
(a submatrix of
V
), and write
X:=R_{kk}^{*}
,
Y:=\overline{R_{jj}}
, with both of these being unitary matrices corresponding respectively to the
k
-th and
j
-th block of
R
. We thus obtain the following formula:

(XZ)^{t}=\frac{q_{k}}{q_{j}}YZ^{t}.

The operation denoted above by
t
is interpreted as follows: if we view
Z
as an operator from
\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
to
\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\otimes H
, then
Z^{t}
is the obvious operator from
\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}
to
\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes H
; thus both sides are interpreted as an operator from
\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}
to
\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes H
.

We will now deduce from the above equality and the fact that
\lambda:=\frac{q_{k}}{q_{j}}\neq 1
whenever
k\neq j
that for such a choice of
k
and
j
we must have
Z=0
.
Indeed, let us rewrite the above formula once again in the form

XZ=\lambda(YZ^{t})^{t},

or, for every
p=1,\ldots,d_{k}
,
r=1,\ldots,d_{j}
,

(3.3)

\sum_{l=1}^{d_{k}}X_{pl}Z_{lr}=\lambda\sum_{m=1}^{d_{j}}Y_{rm}Z_{pm}.

Introduce now two vectors
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes H
and
\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\otimes H
, and two new unitary operators
\tilde{X}\in B(\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes H)
and
\tilde{Y}\in B(\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\otimes H)
by the formulas

\xi_{(p,r)}=Z_{pr}=\eta_{(r,p)},\;\;\;p=1,\ldots,d_{k}\textup{ and }r=1,\ldots,d_{j},

\tilde{X}_{(p,r),(s,t)}=X_{ps}\delta_{rt},\;\;\;p,s=1,\ldots,d_{k}\textup{ and }r,t=1,\ldots,d_{j},

\tilde{Y}_{(r,p),(t,s)}=Y_{rt}\delta_{ps},\;\;\;p,s=1,\ldots,d_{k}\textup{ and }r,t=1,\ldots,d_{j}

(of course
\tilde{X}=X\otimes I
,
\tilde{Y}=Y\otimes I
,
\|\xi\|=\|\eta\|
).
Then we check the following equalities (
p=1,\ldots,d_{k}
and
r=1,\ldots,d_{j}
):

(\tilde{X}\xi)_{p,r}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{d_{j}}\tilde{X}_{(p,r),(s,t)}\xi_{(s,t)}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{d_{j}}X_{ps}\delta_{rt}Z_{st}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{k}}X_{ps}Z_{sr},

(\tilde{Y}\eta)_{r,p}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{d_{j}}\tilde{Y}_{(r,p),(s,t)}\eta_{(s,t)}=\sum_{s=1}^{d_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{d_{j}}Y_{rt}\delta_{ps}Z_{ts}=\sum_{t=1}^{d_{j}}Y_{rt}Z_{tp}.

Thus equality (
3.3
) can be rephrased as (
p=1,\ldots,d_{k}
and
r=1,\ldots,d_{j}
)

(\tilde{X}\xi)_{p,r}=\lambda(\tilde{Y}\eta)_{r,p},

so further

\|\tilde{X}\xi\|=\lambda\|\tilde{Y}\eta\|.

The latter is equivalent (by the comments following the definitions of
\xi,\eta,\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
) to the equality

\|\xi\|=\lambda\|\xi\|,

so that in the end
\xi=0
, and thus
Z=0
, and
V
must have a
Q
-matrix form, as initially claimed.

∎

4.
The second cohomology group for
A_{u}(Q)

In this, central, section of the paper, we will compute the second cohomology groups of
U_{Q}^{+}
for
most
matrices
Q
. Once again,
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
) will be a strictly positive diagonal matrix. Note that as the relations in
A_{u}(Q)
do not change when
Q
is multiplied by a positive scalar, we can – and sometimes will – use a suitable normalisation. By the number of blocks of
Q
we will understand the number of its distinct eigenvalues.

The defect map

We will begin from generalities concerning the
2
-cocycles. An element
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
will be called
normalised
if
c(1\otimes 1)=0
; then also
c(1\otimes a)=c(a\otimes 1)=0
for all
a\in A_{u}(Q)
. Note that all
2
-cocycles arising as cup products via Lemma
2.2
are normalised.
Lemma
2.1
implies that the fact whether a normalised cocycle
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
is a coboundary is determined by the values the cocycle takes on elements of the form
u_{kj}^{+}\otimes u_{rl}^{+}
, with
+\in\{\emptyset,*\}
,
k,j,r,l=1,\ldots d
. Thus we shall now analyse the properties these values must satisfy.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
be a normalised
2
-cocycle and define for each
j,k=1,\ldots,d

(4.1)

\displaystyle A_{jk}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})

(4.2)

\displaystyle B_{jk}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}c(u^{*}_{jp}\otimes u_{kp}).

Then we also have (again for
j,k=1,\ldots,d
)

(4.3)

\displaystyle A_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u_{jp}\otimes u^{*}_{kp}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle B_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u^{*}_{pk}).

Proof.

The equalities follow from computing the expression for
\partial c
on
\sum_{p,r=1}^{d}u_{jp}\otimes u_{rp}^{*}\otimes u_{rk}
and
\sum_{p,r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}u_{pj}\otimes u_{pr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr}
and using the fact that
\partial c=0
. The first one was shown in
[
DFKS
, Lemma 4.3]
.

Fix then
j,k=1,\ldots,d
. To see that the two prescriptions for
B_{jk}
agree, we use the relations (
2.2
) and the normalization of
c
:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{p,r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}\partial c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p,r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}\left[\varepsilon(u_{pj})c(u_{pr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})-c(u_{pj}u_{pr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})+c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pr}^{*}u_{kr})-c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pr}^{*})\varepsilon(u_{kr})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{r}}c(u_{jr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})-\sum_{r=1}^{d}c(\underbrace{\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}u_{pj}u_{pr}^{*}}_{=\delta_{jr}1}\otimes u_{kr})+\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u_{pj}\otimes\underbrace{\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}u_{pr}^{*}u_{kr}}_{=\delta_{pk}1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{r}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{r}}c(u_{jr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})-\sum_{r=1}^{d}\underbrace{c(1\otimes u_{kr})}_{=0}+\sum_{p=1}^{d}\underbrace{c(u_{pj}\otimes 1)}_{=0}-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{r}}c(u_{jr}^{*}\otimes u_{kr})-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*}).

∎

The next proposition allows us to identify the coboundaries, using Lemma
2.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
be a normalised
2
-cocycle. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

c
is a coboundary;

(ii)

for any
j,k=1,\ldots,d
such that
Q_{j}=Q_{k}
we have
A_{jk}=B_{kj}
, where the latter numbers were defined in the previous lemma – in particular
A_{kk}=B_{kk}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume that
c
is a coboundary, i.e. there exists a functional
\psi:A\to\mathbb{C}
such that

c=\partial(-\psi)
and
\psi(1)=0
. Fix
j,k=1,\ldots,d
and let us denote for short

(4.5)

\lambda_{jk}:=\psi(u_{jk}),\quad\mu_{jk}:=\psi(u_{jk}^{*}).

Then

\displaystyle A_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\partial(-\psi)(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})=\sum_{p=1}^{d}[-\delta_{pj}\psi(u_{pk})-\psi(u^{*}_{pj})\delta_{pk}+\psi(u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk})]

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{jk}-\mu_{kj}+\psi(\sum_{p=1}^{d}u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk})=-\lambda_{jk}-\mu_{kj},

\displaystyle B_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}\ c(u^{*}_{jp}\otimes u_{kp})=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}\ \partial(-\psi)(u_{jp}^{*}\otimes u_{kp})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}\ [-\delta_{jp}\psi(u_{kp})-\psi(u_{jp}^{*})\delta_{kp}+\psi(u_{jp}^{*}u_{kp})]

\displaystyle=-\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{j}}\ \psi(u_{kj})-\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{k}}\ \psi(u_{jk}^{*})+\psi(\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{p}}\ u_{jp}^{*}u_{kp})=-\lambda_{kj}-\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{k}}\ \mu_{jk}.

This implies that

(4.6)

\displaystyle A_{jk}+\lambda_{jk}+\mu_{kj}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.7)

\displaystyle B_{kj}+\lambda_{jk}+\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}}\ \mu_{kj}

\displaystyle=0.

Hence

\displaystyle\mu_{kj}

\displaystyle=-A_{jk}-\lambda_{jk},

\displaystyle B_{kj}

\displaystyle=(\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}}-1)\lambda_{jk}+\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}}A_{jk}.

From the last line we compute that

(4.8)

(1-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}})\lambda_{jk}=\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}}\ A_{jk}-B_{kj}.

We see that if
Q_{k}=Q_{j}
, then the left hand side vanishes and hence we must have

B_{kj}=A_{jk}
. Thus this condition is necessary.

(
\Leftarrow
) Assume that (ii) holds. Motivated by the calculations from the first part of the theorem
we set for arbitrary
j,k=1,\ldots,d

\displaystyle\psi(u_{jk})

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-(1-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}})^{-1}\left(B_{kj}-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}}\ A_{jk}\right),&\mbox{ if }Q_{k}\neq Q_{j},\\
-\frac{1}{2}A_{jk},&\mbox{ if }Q_{k}=Q_{j};\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\psi(u_{kj}^{*})

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{j}})^{-1}\left(B_{kj}-A_{jk}\right),&\mbox{ if }Q_{k}\neq Q_{j},\\
-\frac{1}{2}A_{jk},&\mbox{ if }Q_{k}=Q_{j}.\end{array}\right..

Note that (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) are then satisfied.

It remains to show that
T=T_{c,\psi}
as defined before Lemma
2.1
extends to a homomorphism. For that, we need to check that
elements
t_{jk}=T_{c,\psi}(u_{jk})
satisfy the defining relations of
A_{u}(Q)
. Then Lemma
2.1
will allow us to conclude that

-\partial\psi=c
.

We first check that
T
preserves the isometry relation, i.e. for all
j,k=1,\ldots,d
we have
\sum_{p=1}^{d}t_{pj}^{*}t_{pk}=\delta_{jk}I
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{d}

\displaystyle t_{pj}^{*}t_{pk}=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\ T(u^{*}_{pj})T(u_{pk})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})&c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes-)&\psi(u^{*}_{pj})\\
0&u^{*}_{pj}\cdot-&u^{*}_{pj}-\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})\\
0&0&\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(u_{pk})&c(u_{pk}\otimes-)&\psi(u_{pk})\\
0&u_{pk}\cdot-&u_{pk}-\varepsilon(u_{pk})\\
0&0&\varepsilon(u_{pk})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{p=1}^{d}\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})\varepsilon(u_{pk})&(\star)&(\star\star)\\
0&\sum_{p=1}^{d}u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk}\cdot-&\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(u^{*}_{pj}[u_{pk}-\varepsilon(u_{pk})1]+[u^{*}_{pj}-\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})1]\varepsilon(u_{pk})\right)\\
0&0&\sum_{p=1}^{d}\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})\varepsilon(u_{pk})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(\sum_{p=1}^{d}u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk})&(\star)&(\star\star)\\
0&\sum_{p=1}^{d}u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk}\cdot-&\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(u^{*}_{pj}[u_{pk}-\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})1]\varepsilon(u_{pk})\right)\\
0&0&\varepsilon(\sum_{p=1}^{d}u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{jk}&(\star)&(\star\star)\\
0&\delta_{jk}1\cdot-&0\\
0&0&\delta_{jk}\end{pmatrix}.

We are left to check that
(\star)=0
and that
(\star\star)=0
. The first
fact holds due to the cocycle property, the normalization
c(1\otimes a)=0
,
and the fact that arguments are taken from
\textup{ker}\,\varepsilon
, since

(\star)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})c(u_{pk}\otimes-)+c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}\cdot-)\right)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(c(u^{*}_{pj}u_{pk}\otimes-)+c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})\varepsilon(-)\right)=0.

The second formula is also true, because of (
4
):

\displaystyle(\star\star)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(\varepsilon(u^{*}_{pj})\psi(u_{pk})+c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes[u_{pk}-\varepsilon(u_{pk})1])+\psi(u^{*}_{pj})\varepsilon(u_{pk})\right)

\displaystyle=\psi(u_{jk})+\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})+\psi(u^{*}_{kj})=\lambda_{jk}+A_{jk}+\mu_{kj}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq_A_relation}}}{{=}}0.

Next, we verify that
T
preserves
u^{t}Q\bar{u}Q^{-1}=I
, i.e.
\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}t_{pj}t^{*}_{pk}=\delta_{jk}I
for all
j,k=1,\ldots,d
.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{d}

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}t_{pj}t_{pk}^{*}=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\ \frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}T(u_{pj})T(u_{pk}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(u_{pj})&c(u_{pj}\otimes-)&\psi(u_{pj})\\
0&u_{pj}\cdot-&u_{pj}-\varepsilon(u_{pj})1\\
0&0&\varepsilon(u_{pj})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})&c(u_{pk}^{*}\otimes-)&\psi(u_{pk}^{*})\\
0&u_{pk}^{*}\cdot-&u_{pk}^{*}-\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})1\\
0&0&\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon(\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}u_{pj}u_{pk}^{*})&(\diamond)&(\diamond\diamond)\\
0&\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}u_{pj}u_{pk}^{*}\cdot-&\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}\left(u_{pj}u_{pk}^{*}-\varepsilon(u_{pj}1)\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})\right)\\
0&0&\varepsilon(\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}u_{pj}u_{pk}^{*})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{jk}&(\diamond)&(\diamond\diamond)\\
0&\delta_{jk}1\cdot-&0\\
0&0&\delta_{jk}\end{pmatrix}

and, as above,

\displaystyle(\diamond)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}\left(\varepsilon(u_{pj})c(u_{pk}^{*}\otimes-)+c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*}\cdot-)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(c(\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}u_{pj}u_{pk}^{*}\otimes-)+\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*})\varepsilon(-)\right)=0.

and

\displaystyle(\diamond\diamond)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}\left(\varepsilon(u_{pj})\psi(u_{pk}^{*})+c(u_{pj}\otimes[u_{pk}^{*}-\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})])+\psi(u_{pj})\varepsilon(u_{pk}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{k}}\psi(u_{jk}^{*})+\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pj}\otimes u_{pk}^{*})+\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{k}}\psi(u_{kj})=\frac{Q_{j}}{Q_{k}}\mu_{jk}+B_{jk}+\lambda_{kj}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq_B_relation}}}{{=}}0.

Using the same method we check that the other relations of
A_{u}(Q)
are also satisfied; we leave the details to the reader.
∎

We are ready to define the defect map, following the construction in
[
DFKS
, Subsection 4.1]
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
Q
be a strictly positive diagonal matrix with
n
distinct eigenvalues and the corresponding decomposition

Q=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}P_{i}
. The defect map
\Delta:Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})\to M_{d}
is defined as follows: first for each
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
consider a matrix
D(c)
given by the formula

D(c)_{jk}=A_{jk}-B_{kj},\;\;\;\;j,k=1,\ldots,d

where the coefficients
A_{jk}
and
B_{jk}
are defined as in Lemma
4.1

and then put

\Delta(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}D(c)P_{i}.

It is easy to see that
\Delta
is a linear map. Its importance stems from the next corollary (of Proposition
4.2
).

Corollary 4.4
.

We have the following isomorphism:

H^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})\simeq\Delta(Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})).

Proof.

It is well-known and easy to show (see for example
[
DFKS
, Lemma 4.4]
) that when computing the second cohomology group one can consider only normalised cocycles. Proposition
4.2
shows that a normalised cocycle
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
is a coboundary if and only if
\Delta(c)=0
. Then the statement becomes a consequence of the first isomorphism theorem.
∎

It thus remains to understand the image of the defect map. Before we formulate the general result, recall the formula (
2.1
).

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a strictly positive diagonal matrix with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
. Then

sl(d_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus sl(d_{n})\subset\Delta(Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+}))\subset sl_{Q}(d).

Proof.

We will first show that any matrix in
\Delta(Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+}))
must belong to
sl_{Q}(d)
. Let then
c\in Z^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})
. The fact that
\Delta(c)
is a
Q
-matrix follows from the definition of the defect map. It remains then to compute the relevant deformed traces:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\Delta(c)Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\Delta(c)Q)_{kk}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}Q_{k}\Delta(c)_{kk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}Q_{k}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u^{*}_{pk}\otimes u_{pk})-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{p}}c(u^{*}_{kp}\otimes u_{kp})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}Q_{k}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u_{kp}\otimes u_{kp}^{*})-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{p}}{Q_{k}}c(u_{pk}\otimes u^{*}_{pk})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,k=1}^{d}Q_{k}c(u_{kp}\otimes u^{*}_{kp})-\sum_{p,k=1}^{d}Q_{p}c(u_{pk}\otimes u^{*}_{pk})=0,

where in the fourth equality we use Lemma
4.1
. Similarly

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\Delta(c)Q^{-1})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(Q^{-1}\Delta(c))=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(Q^{-1}\Delta(c))_{kk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{Q_{k}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{d}c(u^{*}_{pk}\otimes u_{pk})-\sum_{p=1}^{d}\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{p}}c(u^{*}_{kp}\otimes u_{kp})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{Q_{k}}c(u^{*}_{pk}\otimes u_{pk})-\sum_{p,k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{Q_{p}}c(u^{*}_{kp}\otimes u_{kp})=0.

It remains then to prove the first inclusion. We claim that it follows from the proof of
[
DFKS
, Theorem 4.6]
, where it was shown that
H^{2}(U^{+}_{d})\simeq sl(d)
, and explicit cocycles realising that isomorphism were constructed, and the remarks stated in Section
2
after the definition of the cohomology groups, noting that we have natural embeddings of
C^{2}(U^{+}(d_{i}))
into
C^{2}(U^{+}(Q))
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
We leave verifying the details to the reader.
∎

Note that for
n\geq 3
the inclusion
sl(d_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus sl(d_{n})\subset sl_{Q}(d)
is strict, as the dimension of the first space equals
d_{1}^{2}+\ldots+d_{n}^{2}-n
, and of the second
d_{1}^{2}+\ldots+d_{n}^{2}-2
.

‘Block dimension 2’ and defects arising from
1
-cocycles with respect to finite-dimensional representations

We begin the section by noting that if
Q
is a matrix with precisely two different eigenvalues, the last results already yield the second cohomology group of
U_{Q}^{+}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a strictly positive diagonal matrix with eigenspaces of dimensions
(d_{1},d_{2})
.
Then

H^{2}(U_{Q}^{+})\simeq sl(d_{1})\oplus sl(d_{2}).

Proof.

The result follows from noting that
sl(d_{1})\oplus sl(d_{2})=sl_{Q}(d)
simply because the dimensions of two spaces match, as noted after the last theorem. Thus Corollary
4.4
and Theorem
4.5
end the proof. ∎

Let us then return to a general matrix
Q
, with the eigenspace dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
,
n\geq 3
. As our way of constructing explicit
2
-cocycles is based on Lemma
2.2
, one might ask whether one can use the cup product of
1
-cocycles with respect to finite-dimensional representations to obtain the ‘missing’ cocycles, for which the defect map takes values outside of
sl(d_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus sl(d_{n})
. The next proposition shows that the answer is negative: recall that finite-dimensional representations are automatically
Q
-representations.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be a diagonal strictly positive matrix with the eigenspace dimensions
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
.
Let
H
be a Hilbert space, let
\pi:A_{u}(Q)\to B(H)
be a
Q
-representation and let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in Z_{1}(A_{u}(Q),_{\pi}H_{\varepsilon})
. Let
c\in C^{2}(U^{+}_{Q})
be the cocycle constructed out of
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
via Lemma
2.2
. Then
\Delta(c)\in sl(d_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus sl(d_{n})
.

Proof.

This is essentially a consequence of Lemma
3.3
and the definition of the defect map. Indeed, let
V,W\in M_{d}(H)
be matrices corresponding to cocycles
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
via Lemma
3.1
. Then for each
j,k=1,\ldots,d
we have (see Definition
4.3
)

(4.13)

\displaystyle D(c)_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(c(u^{*}_{pj}\otimes u_{pk})-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{p}}c(u^{*}_{kp}\otimes u_{jp})\right)=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(\langle V_{pj},W_{pk}\rangle-\frac{Q_{k}}{Q_{p}}\langle V_{kp},W_{jp}\rangle\right).

Due to Lemma
3.3
both
V
and
W
are
Q
-matrices. This means that the above reduces to

D(c)_{jk}=\sum_{p=1}^{d}\left(\langle V_{pj},W_{pk}\rangle-\langle V_{kp},W_{jp}\rangle\right)\textup{ if }Q_{j}=Q_{k},

and
D(c)_{jk}=0
if
Q_{j}\neq Q_{k}
. Thus
\Delta(c)=D(c)
, and finally if we compute the partial trace at a given block (say corresponding to the eigenvalue
q_{l}
: we put
J=\{k=1,\ldots,d:Q_{k}=q_{l}\}
) we get

\textup{Tr}_{l}(\Delta(c)):=\sum_{k\in J}\Delta(c)_{kk}=\sum_{k\in J}\sum_{p\in J}\left(\langle V_{pk},W_{pk}\rangle-\langle V_{kp},W_{kp}\rangle\right)=0.

∎

The ‘non-degenerate’ case in dimension 3

The considerations in the last subsection show that the next case to study concerns a matrix
Q
with three distinct eigenvalues. Proposition
4.7
tells us that to produce the ‘missing’
2
-cocycles via Lemma
2.2
we need to consider infinite-dimensional representations.

Thus throughout this subsection we assume that
d=3
, and the matrix
Q
has 3 distinct eigenvalues, so that we may consider without loss of generality
Q={\rm diag}\ (1,p^{2},q^{2})
with

0<q<p<1
. We also fix throughout the representation
\pi:A_{u}(Q)\to B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
associated with the matrix

(4.14)

R=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0&-q\gamma^{*}\\
0&I&0\\
\gamma&0&\alpha^{*}\end{pmatrix},

where
\alpha
and
\gamma
denote the images of generators of the standard infinite-dimensional representation of
{\rm Pol}(SU_{q}(2))
, as discussed before the formula (
2.4
). We begin by analysing the
1
-cocycles in
Z_{1}(A_{u}(Q),_{\pi}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})_{\varepsilon})
.

Lemma 4.8
.

A matrix
V=(v_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{3}\in M_{3}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}))
yields a cocycle in
Z_{1}(A_{u}(Q),_{\pi}\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})_{\varepsilon})
(via Lemma
3.1
) if and only if

(4.15)

\begin{cases}\left[(1+\frac{q^{3}}{p^{4}})I-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\alpha-\frac{q^{3}}{p^{2}}\alpha^{*}\right]v_{12}=0\\
\gamma^{*}v_{32}=(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\alpha^{*})v_{12}\end{cases},

(4.16)

\begin{cases}[(1+\frac{p^{4}}{q})I-\frac{p^{2}}{q^{2}}\alpha-qp^{2}\alpha^{*}]v_{23}=0\\
\gamma^{*}v_{21}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}(I-\frac{p^{2}}{q^{2}}\alpha)v_{23}\end{cases}

and

(4.17)

\begin{cases}\alpha^{*}v_{11}-\frac{1}{q}\gamma v_{13}=\alpha^{*}v_{11}+\gamma^{*}v_{31}\\
q^{2}\alpha^{*}v_{31}-q\gamma v_{33}=-q\gamma v_{11}+\alpha v_{31}\\
\gamma^{*}v_{11}+\frac{1}{q^{2}}\alpha v_{13}=\alpha^{*}v_{13}+\gamma^{*}v_{33}\\
q^{2}\gamma^{*}v_{31}+\alpha v_{33}=-q\gamma v_{13}+\alpha v_{33}\end{cases}.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.1
a matrix
V
as above defines a

\pi
-
\varepsilon
-cocycle if and only if

(R^{*}V)^{t}=\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t}Q
.
In our case this means that

\displaystyle\bar{R}Q^{-1}V^{t}Q

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{*}&0&-q\gamma\\
0&I&0\\
\gamma^{*}&0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{p^{2}}&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{q^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{11}&v_{12}&v_{13}\\

Remark 4.11

Theorem 4.12
.

Suppose that

and that

is a strictly positive matrix with precisely three distinct eigenvalues. If these eigenvalues do not form a geometric progression, then

Proof.

Consider the list of eigenvalues of
Q
. By rescaling we can assume that it is of the form
(1,p^{2},q^{2})
, with
0<q<p<1
; we also assume that
Q
is diagonal, and the dimensions of respective eigenspaces equal
d_{1}
,
d_{2}
and
d_{3}
.

Let then
\tilde{Q}\in M_{3}(\mathbb{C})
,
\tilde{Q}={\rm diag}\ [1,p^{2},q^{2}]
. It is easy to see that similarly as in Section
2
we have the inclusion
U^{+}_{\tilde{Q}}\subset U_{Q}^{+}
, realised via the surjective
∗
-homomorphism
q:A_{u}(Q)\to A_{u}(\tilde{Q})
, mapping generators of
A_{u}(Q)
either to generators of
A_{u}(\tilde{Q})
or to
0
or to
1
(the choice here depends on fixing a basis vector in each of the three eigenspaces of
Q
, thus choosing three basis vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
).
Let then
c
denote a cocycle on
A_{u}(\tilde{Q})
constructed via Proposition
4.9
or Proposition
4.10
, depending on whether
p>q^{\frac{1}{2}}
or
p<q^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Put

, so that

. It is then easy to check, based on conclusions of Propositions
4.9
or

(and remembering that we are dealing with normalised cocycles) that

∎

Higher ‘block’ dimensions and the main result

It turns out that the techniques of the last subsection allow us to deduce a complete result for matrices
Q
with more than three distinct eigenvalues. We begin with a basic combinatorial lemma. Note that the number
n-2
is optimal, if we want conditions (i)-(iii) below to hold.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 4
, and suppose that we are given a tuple of distinct positive numbers
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
. Then one can choose
n-2
three-element subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the elements
q_{n_{1}},q_{n_{2}},q_{n_{3}}
corresponding to a given subset do not form (in any order) a geometric progression. Moreover this can be done in such a way that

(i)

there exists an element in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, say
j_{n}
, which belongs to precisely one of the chosen triples;

(ii)

after we delete from our collection of triples the one corresponding to
j_{n}
, there exists an element in
\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{j_{n}\}
which belongs to precisely one of the remaining chosen triples;

(iii)

this behaviour continues until we are left with just
2
triples (containing elements from a
4
-element subset
A
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
), and also then there exist two elements in
A
each of which belongs to precisely one of the two remaining chosen triples.

Proof.

Begin with
n=4
. We can assume that the sequence
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{4})
is increasing. We need to find two ‘bad’ triples. Suppose say that
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
form a geometric progression. Then this is not the case for
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{4})
and either for
(q_{1},q_{3},q_{4})
or for
(q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})
. By symmetry we can also find two ‘bad’ triples if
(q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})
form a geometric progression. And if neither
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
nor
(q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})
form a geometric progression, we have already located two ‘bad’ triples. It is easy to see that in each case there are two elements in
\{1,2,3,4\}
each of which belongs to precisely one of the triples.

The general statement follows inductively; indeed, suppose that the result has been proved for a given
n\geq 4
. Consider then the tuple
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n+1})
, which without loss of generality can be ordered so that the numbers are increasing. We know that we can find
n-2
relevant triples of elements in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then it is enough to note that there must exist a pair
(k_{1},k_{2})
of distinct elements in
\{1,\ldots,4\}
such that the elements
q_{k_{1}},q_{k_{2}},q_{n+1}
do not form a geometric progression. This can be seen similarly as in the first part. Obviously
n+1
belongs to only one of the triples in the new set; and once we remove it (together with the corresponding triple) from the list, we are back in the case already assumed to satisfy conditions (i)-(iii), so that the conditions (i)-(iii) hold also for the new choice.
∎

Recall the formula (
2.1
) defining
sl_{Q}(d)
. We are ready to state and prove the main result of the paper, stated already in the introduction. It will essentially follow from the arguments in the proof of Theorem

Let

d_{\mathbf{j}_{n}}=0
. We can then ignore
j_{n}
, discard the corresponding triple
\mathbf{j}_{n}
and use condition (ii) in Lemma
4.13
to find the next element
\mathbf{j}\in\mathcal{J}
for which
d_{\mathbf{j}}=0
. We continue this until we are left just with 2 triples, say
\mathbf{j}_{1}
and
\mathbf{j_{2}}
(and a four element subset of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
). Then we use condition (iii) in Lemma
4.13
to find two partial traces which will show that also coefficients
d_{\mathbf{j}_{1}},d_{\mathbf{j}_{2}}
vanish. Thus
\sum_{p=1}^{l}c_{p}A_{p}=0
, and as
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{l}\}
was a basis, in fact all coefficients
c_{p}
also vanish. This ends the proof.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

We believe that the conclusion of the above theorem should hold for all matrices
Q
.
The difficulty with the missing case lies in the fact that we cannot decide whether for a given
q\in(0,1)
either

\begin{cases}\left[(1+q)I-\frac{1}{q}\alpha-q^{2}\alpha^{*}\right]v_{12}=0\\
\gamma^{*}v_{32}=(\frac{1}{q}-\alpha^{*})v_{12}\end{cases},

or

\begin{cases}[(1+q)I-\frac{1}{q}\alpha-q^{2}\alpha^{*}]v_{23}=0\\
\gamma^{*}v_{21}=(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\alpha)v_{23}\end{cases}

admits a non-zero solution (a pair of vectors in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
). If the answer were positive, we would be able to argue as in the last subsection to first complete the case of
Q
with three eigenspaces and then obtain the complete result via Lemma
4.13
.

Appendix

In the appendix we state and prove a lemma regarding square summability of certain sequences defined by recurrence relations, which was kindly provided to us by Ryszard Szwarc.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
, let
a,b>0
and put
\kappa=a+b
and
\mu=a\cdot b
. Let

c_{k}:=(1-q^{2k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be the sequence defined by
the recurrence relation

(4.23)

b_{0}=1,\quad b_{1}=\kappa c_{1},\quad b_{k+1}=\kappa c_{k+1}b_{k}-\mu\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}b_{k-1}\quad\mbox{for}\quad k\geq 1.

Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}{b_{k}\over b_{k-1}}=\max(a,b).

Consequently, the sequence
(q^{-k}b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is square summable if and
only if
\max(a,b)<q
.

Proof.

Write the recurrence relation as

b_{k}={1\over\kappa c_{k+1}}b_{k+1}+{\mu\over\kappa c_{k}}b_{k-1}

and define

(4.24)

g_{0}=0,\qquad g_{k}={\mu\over\kappa c_{k}}{b_{k-1}\over b_{k}},\quad k\geq 1.

Then

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa c_{k+1}}\frac{b_{k+1}}{b_{k}}+{\mu\over\kappa c_{k}}\frac{b_{k-1}}{b_{k}}=\frac{1}{\kappa c_{k+1}}\frac{\mu}{\kappa c_{k+1}g_{k+1}}+g_{k},

from which we deduce

g_{k}(1-g_{k-1})=\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}c_{k}^{2}},\quad k\geq 1.

The sequence on the right hand side is increasing and convergent to

\mu/\kappa^{2}.

Therefore the sequence
(g_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is increasing. Indeed,
g_{0}=0\leq g_{1}=\mu/(\kappa c_{1}^{2}).

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose we showed that
g_{k-1}\leq g_{k}.
Then

g_{k+1}(1-g_{k})=\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}c_{k+1}^{2}}\geq\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}c_{k}^{2}}=g_{k}(1-g_{k-1})\geq g_{k}(1-g_{k}).

Hence
g_{k+1}>g_{k}.

We also have

(4.25)

g_{k}(1-g_{k-1})=\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}c_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4},\quad k\geq 1.

Indeed,
\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}c_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{\mu}{\kappa^{2}}=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}}

and
4ab\leq(a+b)^{2}
.
The estimate (
4.25
) yields
g_{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}.
Indeed,
g_{0}\leq 1/2.
And if we know that
g_{k-1}\leq 1/2
for some
k\geq 1
,
then

{1\over 2}g_{k}\leq g_{k}(1-g_{k-1})\leq{1\over 4},

i.e.
g_{k}\leq 1/2.
Thus the sequence
(g_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
is convergent to a number
g\leq\frac{1}{2}
such that

g(1-g)={\mu\over\kappa^{2}}.

Therefore

g={1\over 2}\left({1-\sqrt{1-4\mu/\kappa^{2}}}\right).

In view of (
4.24
) this implies

\lim_{k\to\infty}{b_{k}\over b_{k-1}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mu\over\kappa c_{k}g_{k}}=\frac{\kappa+\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\mu}}{2}.

Using the assumption
\kappa=a+b
,
\mu=a\cdot b
,
a,b>0
we get

\lim_{k\to\infty}{b_{k}\over b_{k-1}}=\frac{(a+b)+|a-b|}{2}=\max(a,b).

The last conclusion follows from the d’Alembert’s ratio test.

∎
