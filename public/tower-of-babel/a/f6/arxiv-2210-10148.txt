Abstract
We present a method to derive new explicit expressions for bidiagonal decompositions of Vandermonde and related matrices such as the ( q q -, h h -) Bernstein-Vandermonde ones, among others. These results generalize the existing expressions for nonsingular matrices to matrices of arbitrary rank. For totally nonnegative matrices of the above classes, the new decompositions can be computed efficiently and to high relative accuracy componentwise in floating point arithmetic. In turn, matrix computations (e.g., eigenvalue computation) can also be performed efficiently and to high relative accuracy.

2
Bidiagonal decompositions of TN matrices

Our focus in this paper is on the class of TN matrices, but the formulas and methods we present are valid without the requirement of total nonnegativity (Corollary
4.1
below). The bidiagonal decompositions of the TN matrices serve as a major tool in their study and computations with them. We review those here
[
2
,
4
]
.

A nonsingular
n\times n
TN matrix
A
can be uniquely factored as

A=L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots L^{(n-1)}DU^{(n-1)}U^{(n-2)}\cdots U^{(1)},

(37)

where
L^{(i)}
are
n\times n
nonnegative and unit lower bidiagonal,
D
is
n\times n
nonnegative and diagonal, and
U^{(i)}
are
n\times n
nonnegative and unit upper bidiagonal. For the nontrivial entries of the factors
L^{(k)}
and
U^{(k)}
, respectively, we have
L_{i+1,i}^{(k)}=U_{i,i+1}^{(k)}=0
for
i<n-k
.

We call the above decomposition (
37
), the
ordinary bidiagonal decomposition
of
A
to distinguish it from what we will define below as the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of a TN matrix.

The decomposition (
37
) occurs naturally in the process of complete Neville elimination when adjacent rows and columns are used for elimination
[
4
,
5
]
.

There are exactly
n^{2}
nontrivial entries which parameterize the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
). Following
[
6
, sec. 4]
, those nontrivial entries can be conveniently stored in an
n\times n
matrix
M
, denoted
M={\mathcal{B}D}(A)
, where:

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle=L_{i,i-1}^{(n-i+j)},\quad i>j,

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle=U_{j-1,j}^{(n-j+i)},\quad i<j,

(38)

\displaystyle m_{ii}

\displaystyle=D_{ii}.

Namely, for
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
,

L^{(k)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1\\
&\ddots\\
&m_{n-k+1,1}&1\\
&&m_{n-k+2,2}&1\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&&m_{nk}&1\end{array}\right],

U^{(k)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1\\
&\ddots\\
&&1&m_{1,n-k+1}\\
&&&1&m_{2,n-k+2}\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&&&1&m_{nk}\\
&&&&&&1\end{array}\right],

and

D=\mbox{diag}(m_{11},m_{22},\ldots,m_{nn}).

For
i\neq j
, the
m_{ij}
are the multipliers of the complete Neville elimination with which the
(i,j)
entry of
A
is eliminated and
m_{ii}
are the diagonal entries of
D
.

For example, if
A
is the
3\times 3
Vandermonde matrix from (
1
), then the matrix
M={\mathcal{B}D}(A)
is:

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&x&x\\
1&y-x&y\\
1&\frac{z-y}{y-x}&(z-x)(z-y)\end{array}\right].

(39)

In this paper, we assume that explicit expressions for the entries of the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
) are given. For the matrices we consider in this paper, those expressions contain singularities when some of the nodes coincide and we work to remove those singularities.

To that end, we drop the requirement that the matrices
L^{(i)}
and
U^{(i)}
have ones on the main diagonal. We define a new bidiagonal decomposition, which we call
singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition
and denote it by
\mathcal{SBD}(A)
, as

A=L_{1}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}DU_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1}.

(40)

The matrix
D
is nonnegative and diagonal. The factors
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
are nonnegative lower and upper bidiagonal, and have the same nonzero patterns as
L^{(i)}
and
U^{(i)}
, respectively,
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
. Namely,
(L_{k})_{i+1,i}=(U_{k})_{i,i+1}=0
for
i<n-k
(see theorem 2.1 in
[
6
]
).

Following
[
8
]
, the nontrivial entries of
\mathcal{SBD}(A)
are stored in two matrices:
B
, which is
n\times n
and
C
, which is
(n+1)\times(n+1)
:

[B,C]=\mathcal{SBD}(A).

As with
\mathcal{BD}(A)
, the matrix
B
stores the nontrivial offdiagonal entries of
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
as well as the diagonal entries of
D
, exactly as in (
38
):

\displaystyle b_{ij}

\displaystyle=(L_{n-i+j})_{i,i-1},i>j,

\displaystyle b_{ij}

\displaystyle=(U_{n-j+i})_{j-1,j},i<j,

\displaystyle b_{ii}

\displaystyle=D_{ii}.

The matrix
C
stores the diagonal entries of
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
as

c_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(L_{n-i+j})_{i-1,i-1},&i>j,\\
(U_{n-j+i})_{j-1,j-1},&i<j.\end{array}\right.

In this arrangement,
c_{ij},\,i>j
, is the diagonal entry in
L_{n-i+j}
immediately above
b_{ij}
and similarly for
i<j
and
U_{n-j+i}
. The entries
c_{ii},i=1,2,\ldots,n+1
as well as the entries
c_{1,n+1}
and
c_{n+1,1}
are unused. This is the same construction as the one given in formula (9) in
[
8
]
, except that we now allow for the
(n,n)
entry in
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
to be any nonnegative number and not necessarily equal 1. This is the reason we need an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix to host the entries
c_{ij}
: the nontrivial diagonal entries of
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n-1}
are of lengths
2,3,\ldots,n
, and similarly for the
U_{i}
’s.

As we explain in section
7
, we can always make the
(n,n)
entry in the
L_{i}
’s and
U_{i}
’s equal to 1, so that the algorithms of
[
8
]
be used, but the formulas are more elegant without that restriction, which can be imposed after in software.

Thus, in this paper we start with the known ordinary bidiagonal decomposition
M=\mathcal{BD}(A)
of a nonsingular matrix
A
, (
37
), and produce the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition
[B,C]=\mathcal{SBD}(A)
, (
40
), which is defined for a matrix
A
of arbitrary rank.

The new singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) is not unique, but this is inconsequential – for the purposes of accurate computations, any accurate decomposition of a TN matrix as a product of nonnegative bidiagonals is an equally good input
[
6
]
.

3
Vandermonde-type matrices

The ordinary Vandermonde matrices are the starting point of our investigation as the approach extends analogously to all other classes of matrices in this paper.

An
n\times n
Vandermonde matrix
A
with nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
is defined as

A=\left[x_{i}^{j-1}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}.

(41)

When the nodes are distinct, it has an ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
),

V={\cal BD}(A)
, such that
[
6
]

\begin{array}[]{lcll}v_{ii}&=&\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(x_{i}-x_{k}),&\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\\
v_{ij}&=&\displaystyle\prod_{k=i-j}^{i-2}\frac{x_{i}-x_{k+1}}{x_{i-1}-x_{k}},&\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,\\
v_{ij}&=&x_{i},&\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n.\end{array}

(42)

In the following definition, we take a more general stance, where the lower bidiagonal factors and the diagonal have additional factors, which contain no singularities.

Definition 1
.

An
n\times n
matrix
A
is said to be of Vandermonde-type with nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
on an (open or closed) interval
D
, if it satisfies all of the conditions below:

1.

a_{ij}=f_{j}(x_{i})
, where
f_{j}(x)
is a rational function of
x
for
i,j=1,2,\ldots,n
;

2.

A
is TN when
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}
and all
x_{i}\in D,\;i=1,2,\ldots,n
;

3.

A
has an ordinary bidiagonal decomposition,
M={\cal BD}(A)
, such that

m_{ij}=s_{ij}v_{ij},

(43)

for
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
, where
v_{ij}
are defined as in (
42
), and the entries
s_{ij}
for
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
and the entries
m_{ji}
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
are rational functions of
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
, with no singularities when
x_{i}\in D,\,i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

A Vandermonde-type matrix will thus be fully specified by the entries
s_{ij}
for
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
and the entries
m_{ji}
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
.

From the above definition, the ordinary Vandermonde matrix (
41
) is a Vandermonde-type matrix on
(0,\infty)
with
s_{ij}=1
for
1\leq j\leq i\leq n
and
m_{ji}=v_{ji}=x_{j}
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
.

In the literature, the Vandermonde-type matrices sometimes have
n+1
nodes, with the indexing starting at 0, e.g.,
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. For consistency, in this paper, all Vandermonde-type matrices are
n\times n
and their nodes are
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
.

4
Singularity-free bidiagonal decompositions of Vandermonde-type matrices

In this section, we show how to remove the singularities in the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
) for Vandemonde-type matrices and obtain a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
).

If
M={\mathcal{B}D}(A)
is the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
) of a Vandermonde-type matrix, from (
43
) we have
m_{ii}=s_{ii}v_{ii},\,i=1,2,\ldots,n
, and thus the diagonal factor
D
can be factored as
D=D_{n}\cdot D^{\prime}
, so that

A=L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots L^{(n-1)}\cdot D_{n}\cdot D^{\prime}\cdot U^{(n-1)}\cdots U^{(1)},

where

D_{n}={\rm diag}(v_{11},\ldots,v_{nn})

(44)

and
D^{\prime}={\rm diag}(s_{11},\ldots,s_{nn})
.

Since there are no singularities in the product
D^{\prime}\cdot U^{(n-1)}\cdots U^{(1)}
, it suffices to remove the singularities in the remaining factors, i.e., in the product

L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots L^{(n-1)}D_{n}.

(45)

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
L^{(i)},\,i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
be the lower bidiagonal factors in the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
) of a Vandermonde-type matrix, whose nontrivial entries are defined as in (
43
) and
D_{n}
be defined as in (
44
).
Then

L_{1}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}=L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots L^{(n-1)}D_{n},

where

L_{i}\!=\!\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1\\
&\ddots\\
&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!1\\
&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!s_{n-i+1,1}&x_{n-i+1}-x_{n-i}\\
&&&s_{n-i+2,2}&x_{n-i+2}-x_{n-i}\\
&&&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&&&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!s_{ni}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x_{n}-x_{n-i}\end{array}\right]\!.

(46)

Proof.

It suffices to observe just the initial step, i.e., that

D_{n-1}\cdot L_{n-1}=L^{(n-1)}\cdot D_{n},

(47)

where

D_{n-1}
is a diagonal matrix, such that
(D_{n-1})_{ii}=1
for
i=1,2,
and

(D_{n-1})_{ii}=\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(x_{i}-x_{k}),\quad i=3,4,\ldots,n.

In other words, the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
bottom right principal submatrix of
D_{n-1}
is the diagonal factor in the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition of an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
ordinary Vandermonde matrix with nodes
x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n}
.

Since both
D_{n}
and
D_{n-1}
are diagonal and both
L^{(n-1)}
and
L_{n-1}
are lower bidiagonal, we have bidiagonals on each side of (
47
). To establish that those are equal, it suffices to show that the corresponding diagonal and offdiagonal entries are the same. Since
L^{(n-1)}
is unit lower bidiagonal, the diagonal entry in the product on the right,
L^{(n-1)}D_{n}
is
(D_{n})_{ii}
. Thus, for
i>1
,

(D_{n})_{ii}=\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(x_{i}-x_{k})=\left(\prod_{k=2}^{i-1}(x_{i}-x_{k})\right)\cdot(x_{i}-x_{1})=(D_{n-1})_{ii}(L_{n-1})_{ii},

since
(L_{n-1})_{ii}=x_{i}-x_{1}
by (
46
). Also,
(D_{n})_{11}=1
, so the diagonal entries on both sides of (
47
) are equal.

Since
(L_{n-1})_{i+1,i}=s_{i+1,i}
, the offdiagonal entry in position
(i+1,i)
on the left side of (
47
) is
s_{i+1,i}(D_{n-1})_{i+1,i+1}
. Also, from (
38
),

L_{i+1,i}^{(n-1)}=m_{i+1,i}=s_{i+1,i}v_{i+1,i}=s_{i+1,i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{j+1}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}

and thus

\displaystyle s_{i+1,i}(D_{n-1})_{i+1,i+1}

\displaystyle=s_{i+1,i}\prod_{j=2}^{i}(x_{i+1}-x_{j})

\displaystyle=s_{i+1,i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(x_{i+1}-x_{j+1})

\displaystyle=s_{i+1,i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{j+1}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(x_{i}-x_{j})

\displaystyle=s_{i+1,i}v_{i+1,i}(D_{n})_{ii}

\displaystyle=(L^{(n-1)})_{i+1,i}\,(D_{n})_{ii},

which is the
(i+1,i)
entry on the right hand side of (
47
).

Therefore the offdiagonal entries on each side of (
47
) are also equal and (
47
) is fully established.

Completely analogously, we establish that
D_{k-1}L_{k-1}=L^{(k-1)}D_{k}
for
k=n-1,n-2,\ldots,2,
and for the product (
45
) we have

\displaystyle L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots\underline{L^{(n-1)}D_{n}}

\displaystyle=L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots\underline{L^{(n-2)}D_{n-1}}L_{n-1}

\displaystyle=L^{(1)}L^{(2)}\cdots\underline{L^{(n-3)}D_{n-2}}L_{n-2}L_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=\underline{L^{(1)}D_{2}}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\underline{D_{1}L_{1}}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1},\quad\mbox{ and since $D_{1}=I$},

\displaystyle=L_{1}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}.

The factors that change on each step are underlined.

In other words, we “walk” the matrix
D_{n}
through the product (
45
) right-to-left, canceling the denominators in each
L^{(k)}
and factoring a slightly different diagonal matrix,
D_{k-1}
, out the left until we end up with just
D_{1}=I
, which we drop.
∎

The ordinary bidiagonal decompositions exist for nonsingular TN matrices, however very simple singular TN matrices such as
\big[\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0 \;\;0}{1\;\;0}\big]
(let alone non-TN ones) don’t have ordinary bidiagonal decompositions. Thus the fact that
all
Vandermonde-type matrices have singularity-free bidiagonal decompositions regardless of their rank or total nonnegativity is remarkable, which we prove below.

Corollary 4.1
.

The singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) of a Vandermonde-type matrix
A
is valid for any complex values of the nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
for which the matrices on both sides of (
40
) are defined.

Proof.

Since it is derived from the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
), the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) is valid when
A
is nonsingular and TN. This remains the case when all the nodes of
A
are strictly increasing inside an open interval
(a,b)
contained in the interval
D
of total nonnegativity of
A
.

The
(i,j)
th entries on each side of (
40
) are rational functions of
x_{i}
for all
i,j=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Indeed, on the left hand side,
a_{ij}=f_{j}(x_{i})
, is a rational function of
x_{i}
by definition
1
. On the right hand side, the
(i,j)
th entry is a polynomial in the entries of the factors of (
40
). These entries are obtained via the construction in section
4
from the entries of the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
), which are rational functions of the nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
as either quotients of minors of
A
or products of quotients of minors of
A

[
6
, Prop. (3.1)]
.

Since all rational functions are meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
, and the equality between the
(i,j)
th entries on each side of (
40
) holds on an open interval in
\mathbb{R}
containing
x_{i}
:
x_{i-1}<x_{i}<x_{i+1}
, for
1<i<n
and
a<x_{1}<x_{2}
,
x_{n-1}<x_{n}<b
for
i=1,i=n
, respectively, the Identity Theorem
[
20
, Thm. 3.2.6]
, implies that this equality holds for any complex values of the nodes where these entries are defined.

∎

As a direct application to the ordinary Vandemonde matrices for example, as is evident from the formulas in section
5.1
, the above Corollary immediately implies that their singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition is valid for any complex nodes .

5
Our method and explicit formulas for Vandemonde-type matrices

Our method for producing a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition for an
n\times n
Vandermonde-type matrix
A
of arbitrary rank works as follows:

7
The
(n,n)
entry of the bidiagonal factors must equal 1 for computations

The algorithms of
[
8
]
require that the
(n,n)
entry of every bidiagonal factor in the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of a TN matrix be equal to 1. These algorithms rely heavily on this fact and it turns out that the assumption that this is always the case can be made without any loss of generality.

To that end, in this section, we present a method to “fix” any singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) so that the bottom right-hand corner entries of all bidiagonal factors,
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n-1}
and
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n-1}
equal to 1.

We do so by “moving” the non-unit
(n,n)
entries of each lower bidiagonal factor into
D
and adjusting the
(n,n-1)
entries accordingly:

For any bidiagonal matrix
L_{i}
and diagonal matrix
D_{i}={\rm diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,x_{i}),

i=1,2,\ldots,n-1,
we can make the bottom right hand corner entry of the product
D_{i}L_{i}
equal to one by factoring a new diagonal factor
D_{i+1}
out of the right. Namely,

D_{i}L_{i}=\overline{L}_{i}D_{i+1},

(59)

where
\overline{L}_{i}
equals
L_{i}
, except for
(\overline{L}_{i})_{nn}=1,\,(\overline{L}_{i})_{n,n-1}=L_{n,n-1}x_{i}
. Then
D_{i+1}={\rm diag}(1,1,\ldots,x_{i}L_{nn})
.

This allows us, by starting with
D_{1}=I
, to move the bottom right hand corner entries of the lower bidiagonal factors of
\mathcal{SBD}(A)
into the diagonal factor,
D
:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=L_{1}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}\cdot D\cdot U_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1}

(60)

\displaystyle=\underline{D_{1}L_{1}}L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}\cdot D\cdot U_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1},\quad\mbox{since }D_{1}=I,

\displaystyle=\overline{L}_{1}\underline{D_{2}L_{2}}\cdots L_{n-1}\cdot D\cdot U_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1},\quad\mbox{using }D_{1}L_{1}=\overline{L}_{1}D_{2}\mbox{ as in \eqref{DLLD},}

\displaystyle=\ldots

\displaystyle=\overline{L}_{1}\overline{L}_{2}\cdots\underline{D_{n-1}L_{n-1}}\cdot D\cdot U_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1}

\displaystyle=\overline{L}_{1}\overline{L}_{2}\cdots\overline{L}_{n-1}\cdot(D_{n}D)\cdot U_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots U_{1}

(the factors being transformed on each step using (
59
) are underlined).

The procedure is analogous for the upper bidiagonal factors.

Thus, if (
60
) is a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of the TN matrix
A
, then

A=\overline{L}_{1}\overline{L}_{2}\cdots\overline{L}_{n-1}\cdot\overline{D}\cdot\overline{U}_{n-1}\overline{U}_{n-2}\cdots\overline{U}_{1}

is a singularity-free idiagonal decomposition of
A
with all
(n,n)
entries of
\overline{L}_{i}
and
\overline{U}_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, equal to 1.

The matrices
\overline{L}_{i},\overline{U}_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, and
\overline{D}
differ from the matrices
L_{i},U_{i}
and
D
, respectively, only in

(\overline{L}_{i})_{n,n}=(\overline{U}_{i})_{n,n}=1

and

\displaystyle(\overline{L}_{i})_{n,n-1}

\displaystyle=(L_{i})_{n,n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(L_{k})_{n,n},

\displaystyle(\overline{U}_{i})_{n,n-1}

\displaystyle=(U_{i})_{n,n-1}\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(U_{k})_{n,n},

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{nn}

\displaystyle=D_{nn}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(L_{k})_{n,n}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(U_{k})_{n,n}.

The routine
STNFixBottomRightOfBD
in our package STNTool
[

implements the technique from this section.

For example, by implementing the technique from this section to the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of our

entries of all bidiagonal factors now equal to 1:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&x&x^{2}\\

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1\\
&1\\

&1&1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1\\

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\mbox{ }\;\times\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&x\\

8
Numerical accuracy

In the standard “
1+\delta
” model of floating point arithmetic
[
26
]
, to which the IEEE 754 double precision arithmetic
[
27
]
conforms, the result of any floating point calculation is assumed to satisfy

\mbox{fl}(a\odot b)=(a\odot b)(1+\delta),

(79)

where
\odot\in\{+,-\times,/\},\,|\delta|\leq\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is tiny and is called
machine precision
.

For a computed quantity,
\hat{x}
to
have high relative accuracy
, it means that it satisfies an error bound with its true counterpart,
x

|\hat{x}-x|\leq\theta|x|,

where
\theta
is a modest multiple of
\varepsilon
. In other words, the sign and most significant digits of
x
must be correct. In particular, if
x=0
, it must be computed exactly.

The above model directly implies that the accuracy in numerical calculations is lost due to one phenomenon only, known as subtractive cancellation
[
28
]
. It occurs when a subtraction of previously rounded off quantities results in the loss of significant digits.
Multiplication, division, and addition of same-sign quantities preserve the relative accuracy. Subtraction of initial data such as the nodes
x_{i}
is also fine, since initial data is assumed to be exact: (
79
) tells us the result of that subtraction is computed to high relative accuracy. The only subtractions in any singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition presented in this paper are between exact initial data: either in the form
x_{i}-x_{j}
between the nodes
x_{i}
of the Vandermonde-type matrix as in (
50
) or
1-x_{i}
between the exact double precision floating point number
1
and a node
x_{i}
as in (
81
), (
83
), and (
84
).

Detailed error analyses for the ordinary bidiagonal decompositions of all nonsingular TN Vandermonde-type matrices presented in this paper have already been performed in the corresponding papers (e.g.,
[
14
]
for the Bernstein-Vandermonde matrices, etc.). All those decompositions are computable to high relative accuracy componentwise.

The new singularity-free bidiagonal decompositions inherit the same componentwise error bounds and are thus also computable to high relative accuracy: the offdiagonal entries in the bidiagonal factors
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
as well as the diagonal entries of the diagonal factor
D
in the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) are the same as the corresponding entries in the ordinary bidiagonal decomposition (
37
) except for the factors
v_{ij}
from (
42
). The diagonal entries in the bidiagonal factors
L_{i}
and
U_{i}
in (
40
) are either equal to 1 or are of the form
x_{i}-x_{j}
, the latter computable with relative error bounded by the machine precision,
\varepsilon
, per (
79
).

b_{i,h}^{n}(x)=\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{n}{i}\right)\frac{\prod_{k=0}^{i-1}(x+kh)\prod_{k=0}^{n-i-1}(1-x+kh)}{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+kh)},\;x\in[0,1],\;0\leq i\leq n.

(80)

An
n\times n

h
-Bernstein-Vandermonde matrix with nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}

is defined as

A=\left[b^{n-1}_{j-1,h}(x_{i})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}.

For
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}<1
,
A
is a TN Vandermonde-type matrix
[
15
]
on
[0,1)
and has a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition
[B,C]=\mathcal{SBD}(A)
defined as in (
48
)–(
51
) with parameters

\displaystyle s_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-x_{i-j}+(n-j)h}{1-x_{i-1}+(n-j)h}\prod_{k=0}^{n-j-1}\frac{1-x_{i}+kh}{1-x_{i-1}+kh},

(81)

\displaystyle m_{ji}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-i+1}{i-1}\cdot\frac{(x_{j}+(i-j-1)h)\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(1-x_{k}+(n-i+1)h)}{\prod_{k=1}^{j}(1-x_{k}+(n-i)h)},

for

and

\displaystyle s_{ii}

\displaystyle=\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{n-1}{i-1}\right)\frac{\prod_{k=0}^{n-i-1}(1-x_{i}+kh)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n-i-1}(1+kh)\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1-x_{k}+(n-i)h)}

10.3
Rational Bernstein-Vandermonde matrices

A rational Bernstein-Vandermonde matrix with nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
and positive weights
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}
is defined as
[
9
]
:

R=\left[r_{j-1}^{n-1}(x_{i})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n},

where

r_{j-1}^{n-1}(x)=\frac{w_{j}\,b_{j-1}^{n-1}(x)}{W(x)},\quad x\in[0,1],\quad j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\},

(82)

and
W(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\,b_{j-1}^{n-1}(x)
. The functions
b_{j}^{n}(x)
are the Bernstein polynomials of degree
n
. They equal the
q
-Bernstein polynomials (
53
) for
q=1
and the
h
-Bernstein polynomials (
80
) for
h=0
, i.e.,

b_{i}^{n}(x)=\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{n}{i}\right)x^{i}(1-x)^{n-i},\quad i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\},\quad x\in[0,1].

For
0<x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}<1
, the matrix
R
is TN
[
9
, Thm. 3.1]
, which is of Vandermonde-type on
(0,1)
and
has a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition
[B,C]=\mathcal{SBD}(R)
defined as in (
48
)–(
51
) with parameters

\displaystyle s_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{W(x_{i-1})(1-x_{i})^{n-j}(1-x_{i-j})}{W(x_{i})(1-x_{i-1})^{n-j+1}},

(83)

\displaystyle m_{ji}

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{i}}{w_{i-1}}\cdot\frac{n-i+1}{i-1}\cdot\frac{x_{j}}{1-x_{j}},

(84)

for
1\leq j<i\leq n
and

\displaystyle s_{ii}

\displaystyle=\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{0}{n-1}{i-1}\right)\frac{w_{i}(1-x_{i})^{n-i}}{W(x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}(1-x_{k})}

for
1\leq i\leq n
.

While we like the above expressions for their symmetry, an alternative singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of
R
can be obtained by recognizing, from (
54
) and (
82
), that

R=W_{1}^{-1}AW_{2},
where
A
is the
q
-Bernstein-Vandermonde matrix for
q=1
, and

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle={\rm diag}(W(x_{1}),W(x_{2}),\ldots,W(x_{n}))

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle={\rm diag}(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})

are positive diagonal matrices. Thus if
\mathcal{SBD}(A)
is given by (
40
), then
R
has a singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition

R=(W_{1}^{-1}L_{1})L_{2}\cdots L_{n-1}DU_{n-1}U_{n-2}\cdots(U_{1}W_{2}),

(85)

where the first and last factors in the parentheses,
W_{1}^{-1}L_{1}
and
U_{1}W_{2}
,, are nonnegative bidiagonal matrices with the same nonzero pattern as
L_{1}
and
U_{1}
, respectively. Thus (
85
) is another bidiagonal decomposition of
R
and an example of how the nonuniqueness of the singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition (
40
) can play out.

The singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of a rational Bernstein-Vandermonde matrix can be computed to high relative accuracy in
O(n^{2})
time using the routine
STNBDRationalBernsteinVandermonde
in our package STNTool
[
21
]
.

10.4
Cauchy–Vandermonde matrices with one multiple pole

A Cauchy-Vandermonde matrix with nodes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and one pole
d
of multiplicity
s\geq 1
is defined as
[
31
]

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\frac{1}{(x_{1}+d)^{s}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{x_{1}+d}&1&x_{1}&\cdots&x_{1}^{n-s-1}\\
\frac{1}{(x_{2}+d)^{s}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{x_{2}+d}&1&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{2}^{n-s-1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{(x_{n}+d)^{s}}&\cdots&\frac{1}{x_{n}+d}&1&x_{n}&\cdots&x_{n}^{n-s-1}\\
\end{array}\right]

and is totally nonnegative when
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}
and
d>0
. It is a Vandermonde-type matrix on
[0,\infty)
for
d>0
and has a decomposition
[B,C]=\mathcal{SBD}(A)
with parameters

\displaystyle s_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{x_{i-1}+d}{x_{i}+d}\right)^{s},

\displaystyle m_{ji}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{j}+d,&\hbox{$0<i-j\leq s$}\\
x_{j},&\hbox{$i-j>s$}\end{array}\right.,

for
1\leq j<i\leq n
and

\displaystyle s_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(x_{i}+d)^{s}}

for
1\leq i\leq n
.

The singularity-free bidiagonal decomposition of a Cauchy-Vandermonde matrix with one multiple pole can be computed to high relative accuracy in
O(n^{2})
time using the routine
STNBDCauchyVandermonde1pole
in our package STNTool
[
21
]
.
