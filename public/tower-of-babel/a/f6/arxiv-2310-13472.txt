Normal Subgroup Theorem for groups acting on A ~ 2 \tilde{A}_{2} -buildings
Uri Bader, Alex Furman, Jean Lécureux

Abstract
Let Γ \Gamma be a group acting on with finite stabilizers and finite fundamental domain on a building of type A ~ 2 \tilde{A}_{2} . We prove that any non-trivial normal subgroup of Γ \Gamma is of finite index in Γ \Gamma .



Introduction

The main examples of affine buildings come from Bruhat-Tits theory: if
k
is a local field, and
\mathbf{G}
a
k
-simple algebraic group, then Bruhat and Tits associated to
\mathbf{G}
is an affine building on which
\mathbf{G}(k)
acts. In particular, cocompact lattices in
\mathbf{G}(k)
also act on
X
, properly and cocompactly. In higher rank, such lattices are all arithmetic, by the celebrated work of Margulis and Venkataramana
[
11
,
22
]
. In particular, their algebraic structure is fairly well understood. For example, one can produce many finite index normal subgroups by taking congruence kernels. To clarify the terminology, we say that a group
\Gamma
acting on a simplicial complex
X
is a
cocompact
(or uniform)
lattice
in
X
if it acts with finite stabilizer and a compact fundamental domain.

A fundamental result of Tits (see the last corollary of
[
20
]
as well as the book
[
23
]
) is that all irreducible affine buildings of dimension at least 3 are Bruhat-Tits buildings. On the other hand, buildings of dimension 1 are just trees, and their cocompact lattices are virtually free.

The remaining case of buildings of dimension 2 turns out to be quite interesting. First, there are reducible buildings of dimension 2, namely, product of trees. In that case, the celebrated work of Burger and Mozes
[
4
]
produces examples of lattices in product of two trees which are simple (and in particular, non-linear). Then, there are irreducible buildings of dimension 2, of three possible type (
\tilde{A}_{2}
,
\tilde{C}_{2}
or
\tilde{G}_{2}
), buildings of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
being the simplest and most studied examples.
In this setting, it is already nontrivial to produce examples with cocompact lattices which are not Bruhat-Tits buildings. In the sequel, we will call these buildings
exotic
.
The first construction appeared in
[
18
]
and
[
21
]
, where Ronan and Tits managed to construct the first examples of such buildings. There have been many other constructions since then. We refer to
[
2
, §1.3]
for a more thorough treatment of the literature. In particular, it has been proved that there exist lattices in buildings of arbitrarily large thickness; in some cases (panel-regular lattices) it has even been proven that most buildings are not Bruhat-Tits (this was conjectured in
[
24
]
and proved in
[
16
]
).

Another potential source of examples are
Galois lattices
: starting from a Bruhat-Tits building
X
, associated to an algebraic group
\mathbf{G}(k)
, the full automorphism group of
X
is not
\mathbf{G}(k)
, but an extension of
\Aut(\mathbf{G})(k)
by a subgroup of
\Aut(k)
, which is infinite in positive characteristic (see
[
3
]
and
[
2
, Proposition 9.1]
). Therefore there may exist a lattice
\Gamma
in
\Aut(X)
which has infinite image in
\Aut(k)
: this is what we call a Galois lattice. So far it is not known whether Galois lattices exist or not in higher rank.

These "exotic" buildings of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
and their lattices remain largely mysterious, although they have been studied by many authors. For example, there have been a large amount of work devoted to proving property (T) and its variations, starting with the paper of Cartwright-Młotkowski-Steger
[
6
]
, then followed by Pansu
[
13
]
and Żuk’s criterion
[
25
]
; more recently it was proved that these lattices also have Lafforgue’s strong property (T)
[
9
]
.

In
[
2
]
the authors started to study these lattices with a different point of view, namely, by adapting and applying Margulis’ methods about lattices in higher rank simple Lie groups. It was proven there that cocompact lattices in exotic buildings, as well as Galois lattices, are fairly different from their arithmetic counterparts: they are always non-linear. This paper can be seen as a continuation of this work. Indeed, we are able to prove the Normal Subgroup Theorem:

Theorem 0.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a cocompact lattice of a building of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
. Then any non-trivial normal subgroup of
\Gamma
is of finite index.

In the case when the building is Bruhat-Tits, and
\Gamma<\mathbf{G}(k)
, then this theorem is due to Margulis
[
11
]
. Therefore our main contribution is about exotic buildings, and possibly also to Galois lattices.
This theorem was announced by Shalom and Steger in 2008, but the proofs have not appeared in print, and we do not know how large is the overlap between our approach and theirs.
Our motivation for writing this proof is two-fold. First, it is a first step towards proving stronger results: the Stuck-Zimmer theorem
[
19
]
or character rigidity, in the spirit of
[
15
]
.

The second motivation stems from the following corollary.

Corollary 0.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a cocompact lattice of a building of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
. Then either
\Gamma
is virtually simple, or it is residually finite.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is not residually finite, then the intersection
N
of all finite-index subgroups of
\Gamma
is not trivial. But
N
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, so it is of finite index, and therefore is also a cocompact lattice of the same building. Applying Theorem
0.1
to
N
, we get that every non-trivial normal subgroup of
N
is of finite index in
N
, and therefore also in
\Gamma
, so that it contains
N
. Hence
N
is a finite index simple subgroup of
\Gamma
.
∎

If
\Gamma
is a lattice in an algebraic group over a local field, then (as mentioned above) it is clear that
\Gamma
is residually finite. On the other hand, if
\Gamma
is a cocompact lattice in an exotic building, then the situation is very much unclear. It was conjectured in
[
2
, Conjecture 1.5]
that the first conclusion always holds (
\Gamma
is virtually simple), but so far there have been very few progress in that direction. However, recently, Thomas Titz Mite and Stefan Witzel have been able to produce cocompact lattices in buildings of type
\tilde{C}_{2}
which are not residually finite
[
12
]
. Although the proof needs to be adapted in non-trivial ways, it is very likely that the analogue of Theorem
0.1
also holds for
\tilde{C}_{2}
buildings, thus providing new examples of infinite simple groups.

The main steps of the proof of Theorem
0.1
follow the beautiful strategy of Margulis. If
N
is a non-trivial normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, then we first prove that
\Gamma/N
has property (T) (we get this for free as
\Gamma
itself has property (T)), and then that
\Gamma/N
is amenable, which is the hard part. To do that, we introduce the boundary
\Delta
of the building, which is an amenable
\Gamma
-space. The space
\Delta
is the set of chambers of a spherical building (of rank 2, hence of dimension 1,
i.e
a graph), and is equipped with two
\Gamma
-equivariant maps
\Delta\to\Delta_{+}
and
\Delta\to\Delta_{-}
, where
\Delta_{+}
and
\Delta_{-}
are the two sets of vertices of different types. The space
\Delta
is endowed with a natural measure class, which is
\Aut(X)
-invariant. As in Margulis’ proof, the amenability of
\Gamma/N
will follow from a Factor Theorem. In our case this Factor Theorem translates as follows.

Theorem 0.3
.

Any
\Gamma
-equivariant measurable factor
\Delta\to Z
is (measurably) isomorphic to either
\Delta\to\Delta_{+}
,
\Delta\to\Delta_{-}
, the identity map or a point.

The proof of this Theorem is our main contribution. The general strategy to prove this theorem is to find enough elements in
\Gamma
with some kind of contracting properties: namely, contracting almost all of
\Delta
to the set of chambers adjacent to a given vertex
u\in\Delta_{-}
(the
residue

\Res(u)
of
u
). In fact, what we prove is that one can approximate a special subgroup of
\Aut(\Res(u))
called the
projectivity group
. This group is a classical object of study in incidence geometries and possess a very rich structure, even when the building has few automorphisms. In particular, it is always 3-transitive on
\Res(u)
.

The statement of Theorem
0.3
refers to a measure class on
\Delta
, which is naturally defined. Note also that the strategy sketched in the previous paragraph naturally gives some topological convergence, and therefore it requires some more work to get a statement about the measure class. In the classical case, this is obtained through a non-trivial version of the Lebesgue Density Theorem
[
11
, IV.1]
. Furthermore, even in the classical case, the proof uses various actions on homogeneous spaces, with their measured structure.

In our setting, these homogeneous spaces will be replaced by some natural sets of embeddings: for example, the set of embeddings of an apartment into the building. It is not obvious in general how to construct natural measures with good properties on such spaces. In order to do so, we were led to introduce a fairly general formalism which explains how and when one can construct
prouniform
measures on the set of embeddings of a simplicial complex into another, under some condition that we call
P-symmetricity
. We then check that this condition holds for all the spaces that we need. Finally, we found that it was more suited to our setting to use the Martingale Convergence Theorem rather in place of the Lebesgue Density Theorem. Furthermore, in order to attach the kind of convergence we obtain to the group action, we were led to introduce yet another space, that we call the
detecting flow
, which can be thought as an kind of geodesic flow (or Cartan flow in our case) with additionnal "antennas".

Structure of the paper.
The first section (after this introduction) explains the construction of prouniform measures on sets of embeddings between simplicial complexes, and can be read completely independently of the paper. The second section describes quickly the background on buildings which is necessary to us, and then in §3 we check that the assumptions needed in §1 are indeed true in the setting we want. It is also the occasion to define the various spaces and flows that we will use in the course of the proof. The proof itself starts in Section 4, where we explain how to produce the elements of
\Gamma
with the required dynamical properties. Finally, in §5, we conclude the proof of Theorem
0.3
and
0.1
.

In this work we were led to introduce many different spaces. To help the reader keep track of everything, we also included a list of notations at the very end of the paper.

\mu_{Y}^{o}
is then the usual harmonic measure on the tree.

Example 1.13
.

Let
X
be a regular tree,
Y
be a line and
Y^{\prime}\subset Y
be a half-line. Then
\Isom(Y,X)
is the "geodesic flow" on the tree, and
\mu_{Y}
is the well-known Bowen-Margulis measure on this space. And as we saw before,
\Isom(Y^{\prime},X)
can be identified to the boundary of the tree. With this identification in mind, the restriction map is just the map which associates to a geodesic its endpoint.

1.2
Pushforwards and martingales

Finally, let us end this section by some statements about spaces on measures on prouniform spaces. Let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be an ind-
P
simplicial complexes, and
i:Y^{\prime}\to Y
be an inclusion. Then the restriction
i_{*}
, denoted
r
to avoid to write down too many stars, is a map from
\Isom(Y,X)\to\Isom(Y^{\prime},X)
. Again, to avoid cumbersome notations, write
I_{Y}=\Isom(Y,X)
. Then we get a map
r_{*}:L^{1}(I_{Y},\mu_{Y})\to L^{1}(I_{Y^{\prime}},\mu_{Y^{\prime}})
, which is just defined as the pushforward of a finite measure. More concretely, the map
r_{*}
is an average of
f
along the fibers of
r
.

On the other hand, we also get a map
r^{*}:L^{1}(I_{Y^{\prime}})\to L^{1}(I_{Y})
(or more generally for any
L^{p}
space, for
1\leq p\leq+\infty
), defined by
r^{*}f(z)=f(r(z))
.

The dual of
L^{1}(I_{Y},\mu_{Y})
is of course
L^{\infty}(I_{Y},\mu_{Y})
, with duality given by integration along
\mu_{Y}
, which we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. The restriction
r:I_{Y}\to I_{Y^{\prime}}
defines again a pullback
r^{*}:L^{\infty}(I_{Y^{\prime}})\to L^{\infty}(I_{Y})
.

We first record the following observation.

Lemma 1.14
.

For every
\varphi\in L^{1}(Y,\mu_{Y})
and
f\in L^{\infty}(Y^{\prime},\mu_{Y^{\prime}})
we have

\langle\varphi,r^{*}f\rangle=\langle r_{*}\varphi,f\rangle

Proof.

Write the disintegration
d\mu_{Y}(z)=\mu_{Y}^{r(z)}d\mu_{Y^{\prime}}(r(z))
, where for every
\alpha\in Y^{\prime}
the measure
\mu_{Y}^{\alpha}
is supported on
r^{-1}(\alpha)
.

Then for every
\alpha\in Y^{\prime}
we get
r_{*}\varphi(\alpha)=\int\varphi(x)d\mu_{Y}^{\alpha}(x)
and

\displaystyle\langle r_{*}\varphi,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{Y^{\prime}}}r_{*}\varphi(\alpha)f(\alpha)d\mu_{Y^{\prime}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{Y^{\prime}}}f(\alpha)\int_{I_{Y}}\varphi(z)d\mu_{Y}^{\alpha}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{Y}}f(r(z))\varphi(z)d\mu_{Y}(z)

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,r^{*}f\rangle

∎

Our final remark for this section is that our setup is well-suited for applications of the Martingale Convergence Theorem.

Indeed, let
Y^{\prime}
is a convex ind-
P
subset of
Y
, and let
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
be an increasing sequence of finite convex subsets of
Y
with property
P
, such that
Y=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbf{N}}Y_{k}
. Let
r_{k}:I_{Y}\to I_{Y_{k}}
be the restriction map. For
i\in\Isom(Y,X)
let
\Omega_{k}(i)=r_{k}^{\textemdash 1}(r_{k}(i))
be the "
k
-shadow" of
i
: it is the set of embedding which agree with
i
on
Y_{k}
. Now let
\mathscr{F}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the
\Omega_{k}(i)
. Similarly let
\mathscr{F}_{\infty}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the
\Omega_{\infty}(i)=r^{-1}r(i)
where
r:\Isom(Y,X)\to\Isom(Y^{\prime},X)
is the restriction. In other words
\mathscr{F}_{k}
(resp.
\mathscr{F}_{\infty}
) is the pullback of the
\sigma
-algebra of
\Isom(Y_{k},X)
(resp.
\Isom(Y^{\prime},X)
) by
r_{k}
(resp.
r
).

Then by definition, for
f\in L^{1}(\Isom(Y,X))
, the conditional expectation
\mathbf{E}(f\mid\mathscr{F}_{k})(i)
is just
(r_{k})_{*}f(i)
, which is the average of
f
on
\Omega_{k}(i)
.

In particular the Martingale Convergence Theorem reads as follows

Theorem 1.15
.

For every
f\in L^{1}(\Isom(Y,X))
, for almost every
i\in\Isom(Y,X)
we have

\lim_{k}\mathbf{E}(f\mid\mathscr{F}_{k})(i)=\mathbf{E}(f\mid\mathscr{F}_{\infty})(i)

The convergence also holds in
L^{1}
.

Let us emphasize the following consequence.

Lemma 1.16
.

With the above setting, fix
\alpha\in\Isom(Y^{\prime},X)
. Then the space
\bigcup_{k\in\mathbf{N}}r_{k}^{*}L^{1}(I_{Y_{k}},\mu_{Y_{k}}^{\alpha})
is dense in
L^{1}(I_{Y},\mu_{Y}^{\alpha})
(in the norm topology).

Proof.

By the previous discussion, we get that for
\phi\in L^{1}(I_{Y})
the sequence
(r_{k}^{*}\phi)_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
is a martingale, bounded by
\|\phi\|
. Hence it converges (both a.s. and in
L^{1}
) to
\phi
.

∎

2
Buildings of type
\tilde{A}_{2}

In this section we will explain the facts that we need about
\tilde{A}_{2}
buildings.
We assume some familiarity with the notion, and refer the reader to the reference book
[
1
]
for a more thorough treatment.
Other references include
[
23
]
or the paper
[
2
]
which adopted a similar point of view and notations.
Nevertheless, we will start by recalling a few basic facts and definitions, also in order to fix the vocabulary and notations.
Since we will introduce a large variety of notation, we made a list gathering them all by the end of the article.

2.1
Generalities

Let
\Sigma
be the tessellation of the Euclidean plane by equilateral triangles. This is a
model apartment
. Lines of
\Sigma
are called
walls
, each wall divides
\Sigma
into two connected components, which are called
half-apartments
.
Maximal simplices (
i.e
triangles) are called
alcoves
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
building of type
\tilde{A}_{2}
is a simplicial complex
X
, of dimension 2, covered by copies of
\Sigma
(called
apartments
), such that

•

Every two simplices of
X
are contained in some apartment

•

If
A,A^{\prime}
are two apartments then there exists an isomorphism
A\to A^{\prime}
fixing
A\cap A^{\prime}
pointwise

•

Every edge is contained in at least 3 triangles.

From now on we fix an
\tilde{A}_{2}
building
X
. We equip it with the maximal system of apartments: every embedded copy of
\Sigma
in
X
is an apartment. The number of triangles containing a given edge can be proven not to depend on the edge, and we will assume it is finite. This number will be written
q+1
, where
q>1
is the
thickness
of the building.

We define an alcove (resp. a wall, an half-apartment) in the building as the image of the image of an alcove (resp. a wall, an half-apartment) by an embedding of
\Sigma
in
X
.

The following is
[
1
, Theorem 11.52]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be an
\tilde{A}_{2}
building. Let
C\subset X
be either convex or of nonempty interior. Assume that
C
is isometric to a subset of an apartment. Then there exists an apartment
A\subset X
containing
C
.

From this we can deduce the following well-known corollaries (see eg
[
1
, Exercice 5.83a)]
.)

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
be a sequence of convex subsets of
X
, with
C_{i}\subset C_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
. Assume that each
C_{i}
is contained in an apartment. Then there exists an apartment containing
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbf{N}}C_{n}
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\alpha
be an half-apartment contained in
X
, and
c
be an alcove with on edge on the wall
\partial\alpha
. There exists an apartment containing
\alpha\cup c
.

The vertices of
\Sigma
can be colored uniquely (up to renumerotation) by three colors in such a way that any two adjacent vertices are of a different colors. These colors are also called
types
. Using the second axiom one can check that this coloration extends to the whole building, so that each vertex of
X
has a given type. An automorphism of
\Sigma
, or of

, is called
type-preserving
if it preserves the type. If it does not, then it induces a permutation of the set of types, and it is called
type-rotating
if this permutation is cyclic.

Fix an origin vertex
0\in\Sigma
. Then the walls passing through
0
divide
\Sigma
into 6 connected components, which are called
sectors
or
Weyl chambers
. We choose one of them and call it the
positive Weyl chamber
of
\Sigma
, and denote it by
\Lambda
. For every pairs of vertices
x,y\in\Sigma
, there is a unique type-rotating permutation which sends
x
to
0
and
y
to an element of
\Lambda
. This element is called the
combinatorial distance
and denoted
\sigma(x,y)
. More generally, if
x,y\in X
then there is an apartment containing
x
and
y
, and we can define
\sigma(x,y)
as the combinatorial distance between
x
and
y
in this apartment. This does not depend on the choice of the apartment.
For
\lambda\in\Lambda
, and
x\in X
a vertex, the set

V_{\lambda}(x)=\{y\in X\mid\sigma(x,y)=\lambda\}

is the
combinatorial sphere
of radius
\lambda
centered at
x
.

The
convex hull
of a set
F\subset X
of vertices of
X
is the convex hull in the 1-skeleton of
X
. It is denoted
\Conv(F)
. If
F
is contained in an apartment, then
\Conv(F)
is the intersection of all apartments containing
F
. Note that if
x,y
are two vertices of
X
then the isometry class of
\Conv(x,y)
only depends on
\sigma(x,y)
.

We define the
length
of an element
\lambda\in\Lambda
as the distance between

and
\lambda
in the 1-skeleton of
\Sigma
. We denote this length
\ell(\lambda)
.

2.2
Boundaries

Let us now define the boundary of
X
and its structure as a spherical building. We have defined a sector in
\Sigma
, and as usual we define a sector in
X
as the image of a sector in
\Sigma
by any embedding in
\Isom(\Sigma,X)
. A sector is
based
at some vertex of
X
, which is the image of

by this embedding. Two sectors are
equivalent
if their intersection contains another sector. Equivalence classes of sectors are called
Weyl chambers
, or sometimes just chambers. The set of all chambers is denoted by
\Delta
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For each Weyl chamber
C\in\Delta
, and vertex
x\in X
, there is a unique sector in the class of
C
which is based at
x
. This sector is denoted
Q(x,C)
.

We define, for
x,z\in X
two vertices, the set

\Omega_{x}(z)=\{C\in\Delta\mid z\in Q(x,C)\}

When
z
varies, these sets form a basis for a topology of
\Delta
. This topology does not depend on
x
, and turns
\Delta
into a compact metrizable space. The group
\Aut(X)
acts on
\Delta
by homeomorphisms. A chamber is in the
boundary
of some apartment

if it is the class of some sector in
A
. A basic fact is that any two chambers of
X
are contained in the boundary of some common apartment.

A
singular ray
of
X
is a half-line which is contained in a wall of
X
. Two singular rays
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are

if there exists an apartment which contains two sub-rays
\rho^{\prime}_{1}\subset\rho_{1}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{2}\subset\rho_{2}
and such that
\rho^{\prime}_{1}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{2}
are parallel. The
vertices at infinity
of
X
are the equivalence classes of singular rays. In fact, there are two types of singular rays, depending on the cyclic permutation of the types of vertices in the ray. We call the two types
+
and

. The set of vertices at infinity of type

is denoted
\Delta_{+}
, and similarly the set of vertices at infinity of type

is denoted
\Delta_{-}
.
For each

, and every vertex

, there is a unique singular ray in the class of

starting from

, and we will denote it
[xu)
. Once again, this defines a topology on
\Delta_{-}
and
\Delta_{+}
, two vertices at infinity

being close if the rays
[xu)
and
[xu^{\prime})
share a long initial segment.

Each sector is bounded by two singular rays. This descends to the corresponding equivalence classes and allow us to define two maps
\pr_{+}:\Delta\to\Delta_{+}
and
\pr_{-}:\Delta\to\Delta_{-}
. We also say that a chamber
C

contains
the two vertices at infinity
\pr_{+}(C)
and

. These two maps are continuous and equivariant with respect to the group of type-rotating automorphisms of

. For

(resp.
\Delta_{-}
), the

of

, denoted

, is the fiber

(resp.
\pr_{-}^{-1}(u)
).

It is easily checked that this defines a bipartite graph structure, with set of vertices
\Delta_{+}\cup\Delta_{-}
, and with set of edges
\Delta
(an edge

being attached to a vertex
u
if
C
contains
u
). This graph has diameter 3, and is in fact a spherical building of type
A_{2}
. In particular, for each vertex at infinity
u
, one can define a
projection

\proj_{u}:\Delta\to\Res(u)
, which associates to a chamber

the unique chamber
\proj_{u}(C)\in\Res(u)
which is at minimal distance from

. This projection is continuous.

Two chambers

are

if they are at maximal distance, that is, at distance 3. We denote

the set of pairs
(C,C^{\prime})
of opposite chambers. Two vertices

and

are

if there exists

containing

and
C^{\prime}\in\Delta
containing

such that

and
C^{\prime}
are opposite. We denote

the set of all pairs

and

and
\Delta_{-}\times\Delta_{+}

2.3
Wall-trees and projectivities

For

between

and

is the union of all apartments containing

and
v
in their boundary. It is denoted

. A

from

to

is a wall of such an apartment consisting of the union of two singular rays

for some

.

This interval, up to isometry, does not depend on
u
and

, and has the following structure. If
\ell

and

The

is a model for the simplicial complex

(where

The automorphism group of this simplicial complex

be the index 2 subgroup such that

is formed only by translations. We denote by

the group

; it is a subgroup isomorphic to

, which we call the group of

of

.

For

(or in
\Delta_{+}
), one can also define a

, by the following construction: if
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
are two singular rays pointing towards

, then, up to deleting finite segments, they are parallel in some apartment, so again there is a well-defined distance

. This defines a metric space, which depends on

, and will be denoted

. There is a natural map

from

to

pointing towards

is again an

-regular tree.

Let
C
be a Weyl chamber in the residue of

. Any sector

representing

is a union of geodesic rays pointing towards

, hence defines a subset of
T_{u}
, which is in fact itself a geodesic ray of

. If

also represents

contains a subsector, therefore the corresponding geodesic rays in

The following Lemma is well-known, see for example
[

If
(u,v)\in\Delta_{\pm,\mathrm{op}}

Proposition 3.9

Every finite convex subcomplex of

Recall that a function on a tree is convex if and only if it is convex in restriction to every geodesic segment.

Lemma 3.10

Let

be a finite connected tree, and

be a convex function such that if

is adjacent to

.
Then there exists

and

for every vertex

.

Proof.

The set of points where

attains its minimum is non-empty (since

is finite) and convex (by convexity of

). Since there are no adjacent vertices with the same value,

is minimal at exactly one point

. If

is adjacent to

then we must have

. Now if

then considering the geodesic segment

.
∎

3.2
Flows and measures

In this section, we revisit various constructions of measures and dynamical systems associated to buildings, in the light of the construction of prouniform measures.

Convention
.

In this text, the index of the measure will be an indication of the space on which this measure is defined. The exponent will usually denote some origin or restriction.

We also will use the letter
\mu
only for (restricted or not) prouniform measures.

3.2.1
Harmonic measures and the Cartan Flow

Let us start again with
X

\tilde{A}_{2}

\Sigma

\tilde{A}_{2}

P_{\Upsilon}
was defined to be "being a finite convex subset of the wall-tree
\Upsilon

\Upsilon
is ind-
P_{\Upsilon}
(as the convex hull of a finite set in
\Upsilon

Recall that we have fixed an origin

, and a sector
\Lambda\subset\Sigma
based at
0
. Note that
\Sigma
and
\Lambda
are ind-
P_{\Upsilon}
.
Let

. The set
\Delta
of Weyl chambers in the boundary of

\mu_{\Lambda}^{o}

[

]

[

Recall that we have a natural map from

4.1
The argument for the tree

Before diving into the details of the proof, we will start by describing the full argument in an easier case, namely, the case of a tree. Many of the proof are similar to the case of the building, so we will not give full details,

Let

The main goal in this section is the proof of the following.

Proposition 4.1

For every

4.1.1
A recurrence argument

Consider the geodesic flow

on the tree

and the action of the shift

The first step of our proof is to find contracting sequence in every direction in
\Gamma
. Note that since

is non-atomic,
(\mu_{\partial T}^{o})^{2}

).

Say that a sequence
(\gamma_{n})
of automorphisms of

-contracting if for every finite segment

contained in the geodesic
(\eta,\xi)

acts on

by translating it towards

.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
(\mu_{\partial T}^{o})^{2}
-almost every

a
(\eta,\xi
)-contracting sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
of elements of

.

The analogous statement of the building is proved in Proposition

Proof.

Let

∎

Note that for such a sequence

The second step of our argument is to apply some sort of disintegration. This is the analogous of what is done in §

Lemma 4.3

\Omega_{n}(\xi)

E_{n}(\xi)
.

An important difficulty (and also a crucial point of the proof) in the building case is that the analogous limit that we get will not be a constant anymore, but a function on a smaller set.

4.1.3
The detecting flow

The final step in the proof of Proposition
4.1

is to relate the sequence
(\gamma_{n})
to the averages over
E_{n}(\xi)
. While this can also be done directly in the case of the tree, we found it easier in the building case to use yet another space, which we call the
detecting flow
.

We define a space
M
, which we think of as a
model geodesic endowed with antennas
, or a
comb
, by setting

M=\left(\mathbf{R}\times\{0\}\right)\cup\left(\mathbf{Z}\times[0,\infty)\right)\subset\mathbf{R}^{2},

which we view as the union of a geodesic (
\mathbf{R}\times\{0\}
) with antennas at each vertex (the lines
\{n\}\times[0,\infty)
) (see Figure
2
below).
We define the
detecting geodesic flow
on
T
to be

\mathcal{J}=\mathrm{Isom}(M,T).

Figure 2:
The model space for the detecting flow on a tree

We endow
\mathcal{J}
with the prouniform measure
\mu_{\mathcal{J}}
.
For every

we define a map
\pi_{n}:\mathcal{J}\to\partial T
as the limit of the image of the ray
\{n\}\times[0,\infty)
(these are the "antenna" maps).
On the other hand, we can also define
\pi_{\infty},\pi_{-\infty}:\mathcal{I}\to\partial T
as the limit of the geodesic rays
[0,+\infty)\times\{0\}
and
(-\infty,0]
.

We define the "shift" on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
by

and note it leaves

invariant, hence it induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{J}
, which commutes with the action of
\Gamma
. Furthermore we have easily that

.

The maps
\pi_{n}
(for

) define pullbacks
\pi_{n}^{*}:L^{\infty}(\partial T)\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{J})
. (Note that
\pi_{n}:\mathcal{J}\to\partial T
is measure-class preserving). However, since our favorite contracting sequence
(\gamma_{n})
depends on a choice of points
\xi
and
\eta
, we need to first proceed to a disintegration and fix the two endpoints. More precisely, for a pair of distinct points
\xi,\eta\in\partial T
and
o
on the geodesic between
\xi
and
\eta
, let
\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{o}
be the restricted prouniform measure on the set
\mathcal{J}_{\xi,\eta}^{o}\subset\mathcal{J}
consisting of all
j
which send
0
to
o
and the horizontal line in
M
to the geodesic line
(\xi,\eta)
.

Note that
\pi_{n}:\mathcal{J}_{\xi,\eta}^{o}\to\partial T
is not well-defined anymore. However, we have:

Lemma 4.4
.

For
n>0
, the measure
(\pi_{n})_{*}\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{o}
is the normalized restriction of
\mu_{\partial T}^{o}
to
E_{n}(\xi)
.

Now, in order to prove Proposition
4.1
, we note that if
(\gamma_{n})
is a sequence as in Lemma
4.2
, then (for
n
large enough)
\gamma_{n}
acts as a translation of length say
N
on a large segment between
\xi
and
\eta
. Of course it is not correct that
\gamma_{n}\pi_{0}(j)=\pi_{n}(j)
(as we do not know what happens on the "branches"), but nevertheless it follows that
\gamma_{n}(\pi_{0})_{*}\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{o}=(\pi_{N})_{*}\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{o}
.

It turns out that this is sufficient to finish the proof of Proposition
4.1
. To do so, we pullback
f
by
\pi_{0}
and in fact check weak-* convergence on

. Note that having this for one fixed

is not sufficient, as it only give weak-* convergence on
L^{\infty}(\Delta,(\pi_{0})_{*}\mu_{\xi,\eta}^{o})
. However since this is valid for every

we get the convergence with respect to the right measure class.

By density (Lemma
1.16
) it is sufficient to prove the convergence of
\langle\gamma_{n}f,\phi\rangle
to
\langle f(\xi),\phi\rangle
when
\phi
is the characteristic function of a given embedding of a finite subset of

. But then for

large enough we get that

is constant equal to 1, so that we are precisely reduced to the limit calculated in Lemma
4.3
.

4.2
Singular flow and contracting sequences

The first step in our proof is to construct sequences of elements of
\Gamma
with a prescribed dynamic on the boundary. More precisely, our goal is to prove that there is a sequence of elements contracting almost all of
\Delta
onto a fixed residue (Proposition
4.5

Proposition 4.5

For almost every

(\gamma_{n})\in\Gamma^{\mathbf{N}}

The main tool in the proof of this Proposition is the flow

Lemma 4.6

Recall the map

Now, using Theorem

Lemma 4.19

For every

4.5
Dynamics on the boundary

Recall that an action of a group
G
on a topological space
Z
is
topologically transitive
if for every non-empty open sets
U,V\subset Z
, there exists
g\in G
such that
gU\cap V\neq\varnothing
. If
Z
is Polish, it is equivalent to the existence of a dense orbit in
Z
. The action is
minimal
if every orbit is dense.

We say that the action is
topologically 2-minimal
if the orbit of every pair of distinct points is dense in
Z\times Z
.

The main interest of topological 2-minimality is the following.

Lemma 4.22
.

Assume that
G
is a group acting topologically 2-minimally on a space
Z
. Let
p:Z\to Y
be a continuous,
G
-equivariant surjective map. Then either

is reduced to a point or
p
is bijective.

Proof.

Assume that
Y
is not reduced to a point, and that
p
is not bijective. Then there exists
x_{1}\neq x^{\prime}_{1}\in Z
such that
p(x_{1})=p(x^{\prime}_{1})=y_{1}
and
x_{2}\in Z
with
p(x_{2})=y_{2}\neq y_{1}
.

By 2-transitivity, there exists a sequence
(g_{n})
of elements of
G
such that
g_{n}(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})
converges to
(x_{1},x_{2})
. By continuity of
p
, we have that
g_{n}(y_{1},y_{1})
converges to
(y_{1},y_{2})
. This is a contradiction.
∎

The action of the projectivity group on the residue of a panel of

-minimal.

Proof.

Let

be the projectivity group, acting on the set

We have seen in Lemma

that the action of

on

is
