Abstract
. For various arithmetic functions f : ℕ → ℝ f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}} , the behavior of f ⁡ ( n ! ) f(n!) and that of ∑ n ⩽ N f ⁡ ( n ! ) \sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n!) can be intriguing. For instance, for some functions f f , we have f ⁡ ( n ! ) = ∑ k ⩽ n f ⁡ ( k ) {f(n!)=\sum_{k\leqslant n}f(k)} , for others, we have f ⁡ ( n ! ) = ∑ p ⩽ n f ⁡ ( p ) {f(n!)=\sum_{p\leqslant n}f(p)} (where the sum runs over all the primes p ⩽ n p\leqslant n ). Also, for some f f , their minimum order coincides with lim n → ∞ f ⁡ ( n ! ) \lim_{n\to\infty}f(n!) , for others, it is their maximum order that does so. Here, we elucidate such phenomena and more generally, we embark on a study of f ⁡ ( n ! ) f(n!) and of ∑ n ⩽ N f ⁡ ( n ! ) \sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n!) for a wide variety of arithmetical functions f f . In particular, letting d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) and σ ⁡ ( n ) \sigma(n) stand respectively for the number of positive divisors of n n and the sum of the positive divisors of n n , we obtain new accurate asymptotic expansions for d ⁡ ( n ! ) d(n!) and σ ⁡ ( n ! ) \sigma(n!) . Furthermore, setting ρ 1 ( n ) := max { d ∣ n : d ⩽ n } \rho_{1}(n):=\max\{d\mid n:d\leqslant\sqrt{n}\} and observing that no one has yet obtained an asymptotic value for ∑ n ⩽ N ρ 1 ​ ( n ) \sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n) as N → ∞ N\to\infty , we show how one can obtain the asymptotic value of ∑ n ⩽ N ρ 1 ​ ( n ! ) \sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n!) .

1.
Introduction

It is common to enquire about the average order of an arithmetic function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by first examining the behavior of the sum

\sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n)

for large
N
. What if we replace the argument
n
by
n!
? More precisely, given an arithmetic function
f
, how does
f(n!)
behave for large
n
and what is the asymptotic value of
\sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n!)
as
N\to\infty
? These are natural questions to ask. Yet, in the literature, there seems to be only a handful of results regarding the study of
f(n!)
and that of
\sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n!)
for classical arithmetic functions
f
. One of the first such functions to have been closely examined for its exact order at factorial arguments is the number of positive divisors function
d(n)
. In fact, Ramanujan
[
30
,
31
]
obtained interesting estimates for
d(n!)
, and later, Erdős, Graham, Ivić, and Pomerance
[
16
]
refined his findings, whereas more recently, Jakimczuk
[
26
,
27
]
obtained estimates for
\sigma(n!)
, where
\sigma(n)
stands for the sum of the positive divisors of
n
.

Here, more generally, we embark on a study of
f(n!)
and of
\sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n!)
for a wide variety of classical arithmetic functions
f
. Besides the functions
d(n)
and
\sigma(n)
just mentioned, we will pay particular attention to the arithmetic functions

\displaystyle\omega(n):=\sum_{p\mid n}p,\quad\Omega(n):=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\|n}\alpha,\quad\beta(n):=\sum_{p\mid n}p,\quad B(n):=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\|n}\alpha p,

\displaystyle B_{1}(n):=\sum_{p^{\alpha}\|n}p^{\alpha},\quad\gamma(n):=\prod_{p\mid n}p,\quad\eta(n):=\eta(p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{a_{r}})=a_{1}\cdots a_{r},

the Euler totient function
\phi
and finally the
middle divisors
functions

\rho_{1}(n):=\max\{d\mid n:d\leqslant\sqrt{n}\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\rho_{2}(n):=\min\{d\mid n:d\geqslant\sqrt{n}\}.

In Section
2
, we examine the case of the middle divisors of
n!
. Then, preliminary results and statements regarding additive and multiplicative functions are given in Sections
3
and
4
. In Section
5
, for various arithmetic functions
f
, we compare the behavior of
f(n!)
with the maximal order of
f(n)
or their minimal order. The proofs of the results regarding additive and multiplicative functions are laid out in Section
6
.
We conclude with Section
7
where we show that the counterpart at factorial arguments of the famous Chowla conjecture holds and we examine the behavior of various similar sums.

As we will see, several of the estimates that we obtain are almost straightforward whereas others are non trivial and in fact give way to new results, in particular to new asymptotic expansions for
d(n!)
,
\sigma(n!)
and
\eta(n!)
.

From here on, the letter
p
is reserved for primes while the letter
c
with or without subscript stands for an explicit constant, but not necessarily the same at each occurrence. Also, we let
\pi(x)
stand for the number of primes not exceeding
x
. Finally, given an arithmetic function
f
, we will often refer to the sum

S_{f}(n):=\sum_{2\leqslant m\leqslant n}f(n).

2.
The middle divisors of
n!

Before we examine the cases of additive and multiplicative functions, which are somewhat easier to study, we shall first consider a case of arithmetic functions which are neither additive nor multiplicative. We are interested in the middle divisors
\rho_{1}(n)
and
\rho_{2}(n)
of a positive integer
n
, which are defined as

\rho_{1}(n):=\max\{d\mid n:d\leqslant\sqrt{n}\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\rho_{2}(n):=\min\{d\mid n:d\geqslant\sqrt{n}\}.

In particular,
\rho_{1}(n)\leqslant\sqrt{n}\leqslant\rho_{2}(n)
and
\rho_{1}(n)\rho_{2}(n)=n
.

In 1976, Tenenbaum
[
32
]
proved that

(2.1)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{2}(n)=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{12}\dfrac{N^{2}}{\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\dfrac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big)

and that for any given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that for all
N\geqslant N_{0}
,

(2.2)

\dfrac{N^{3/2}}{(\log N)^{\delta+\varepsilon}}<\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n)\ll\dfrac{N^{3/2}}{(\log N)^{\delta}(\log\log N)^{1/2}},

where
\displaystyle{\delta=1-\dfrac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}\approx 0.086071}
.

In 2020, De Koninck and Razafindrasoanaivolala
[
8
]
generalised estimate (
2.1
) by establishing that, given any real number
a>0
and any positive integer
k
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{2}(n)^{a}=c_{0}\dfrac{N^{a+1}}{\log N}+c_{1}\dfrac{N^{a+1}}{\log^{2}N}+\cdots+c_{k-1}\dfrac{N^{a+1}}{\log^{k}N}+O\Big(\dfrac{N^{a+1}}{\log^{k+1}N}\Big),

where, for
\ell=0,1,\ldots,k-1
, the constants
c_{\ell}=c_{\ell}(a)
are explicitly given.
They also proved that, given any integer
k\geqslant 1
and any real number
r>-1
,

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\dfrac{\rho_{2}(n)}{\rho_{1}(n)^{r}}=h_{0}\dfrac{N^{2}}{\log N}+h_{1}\dfrac{N^{2}}{\log^{2}N}+\cdots+h_{k-1}\dfrac{N^{2}}{\log^{k}N}+O\left(\dfrac{N^{2}}{\log^{k+1}N}\right)

where
h_{0}=\dfrac{\zeta(r+2)}{2}
and for each
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant k-1
,

h_{\ell}=\left(\dfrac{r+2}{2}\right)c_{\ell}+\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell-1}\dfrac{rc_{\nu}}{2}\prod_{m=\nu}^{\ell-1}\left(\dfrac{m+1}{2}\right),

with, for each
\nu=0,1,\ldots,\ell
,

c_{\nu}=\dfrac{\nu!}{(r+2)^{\nu+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\nu}\dfrac{(r+2)^{j}(-1)^{j}\zeta^{(j)}(r+2)}{j!}.

In 2008, Ford
[
18
]
considerably improved (
2.2
) by showing that

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n)\asymp\dfrac{N^{3/2}}{(\log N)^{\delta}(\log\log N)^{3/2}}.

Interestingly, no asymptotic formula for the sum
\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n)
has yet been found.

Recently, De Koninck and Razafindrasoanaivolala
[
9
]
established that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}4\leqslant n\leqslant N\\
n\neq\text{prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\log\rho_{2}(n)}{\log\rho_{1}(n)}=N\log\log N+O(N)

and also that, for all
N
sufficiently large,

c_{1}\,N<\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{1}(n)}{\log\rho_{2}(n)}<c_{2}\,N,

where

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\log 2+\int_{1}^{2}\frac{1-\log u}{u(u+1)}\,du+\int_{3}^{\infty}\frac{u-1}{u+1}\frac{\rho(u-1)}{u}\,du\approx 0.528087,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2-2\log 2\approx 0.613706.

As we will now see, the behavior of each of the six sums

(2.3)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n!),\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{2}(n!),\sum_{n\leqslant N}\dfrac{\rho_{1}(n!)}{\rho_{2}(n!)},\sum_{n\leqslant N}\dfrac{\rho_{2}(n!)}{\rho_{1}(n!)},\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{1}(n!)}{\log\rho_{2}(n!)},\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{2}(n!)}{\log\rho_{1}(n!)}

is more manageable.

Theorem 2.1
.

Letting
c
be the positive constant appearing below estimate (
6.28
), we have

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n!)=\sqrt{N!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{N^{c}}\Big)\Big)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{2}(n!)=\sqrt{N!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{N^{c}}\Big)\Big).

Theorem 2.2
.

Letting
c
be as above, we have

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\rho_{1}(n!)}{\rho_{2}(n!)}=N+O\left(N^{1-c}\right)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\rho_{2}(n!)}{\rho_{1}(n!)}=N+O\left(N^{1-c}\right).

Theorem 2.3
.

We have

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{1}(n!)}{\log\rho_{2}(n!)}=N+O(1)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{2}(n!)}{\log\rho_{1}(n!)}=N+O(1).

Observe that the main difficulty in finding an estimate for any of the sums appearing in (
2.3
) at arguments
n
(and not
n!
) is that the values of the function
\rho_{1}(n)
(resp.
\rho_{2}(n)
) are spread out over the interval
[1,\sqrt{n}]
(resp.
[\sqrt{n},n]
). On the contrary, the values of
\rho_{1}(n!)
and
\rho_{2}(n!)
are all near
\sqrt{n!}
, as is made clear in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

To any given sufficiently large integer
n
, one can associate two positive integers
d_{1}<d_{2}
both dividing
n!
and satisfying

(2.4)

\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big)\sqrt{n!}<d_{1}<\sqrt{n!}<d_{2}<\Big(1+\frac{2}{n^{c}}\Big)\sqrt{n!},

where
c
is the constant appearing below estimate (
6.28
),
thereby implying that

(2.5)

\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big)\sqrt{n!}<\rho_{1}(n!)<\sqrt{n!}<\rho_{2}(n!)<\Big(1+\frac{2}{n^{c}}\Big)\sqrt{n!}.

3.
The case of additive functions

As a first example of additive functions, let us consider the function
f(n)=\log n
, for which we have
f(n!)=\sum_{k\leqslant n}\log k=S_{\log}(n)
. As we will see, for large families of functions
f
, we do have that
f(n!)=S_{f}(n)
.

3.1.
Small additive functions

We first consider the case of the “small” additive functions
\omega(n)
and
\Omega(n)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\omega(n!)=\pi(n)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega(n!)=S_{\Omega}(n).

Moreover,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\omega(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\log n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\right),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Omega(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\left(\log\log n+c_{1}+c_{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\right),

where

c_{1}=\gamma+\sum_{p}\big(\log(1-\frac{1}{p})+\frac{1}{p}\big)
and
c_{2}=\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p(p-1)}
(here,
\gamma
stands for the Euler–Mascheroni constant).

The proof of Theorem
3.1
will be given in Section
6
. Beforehand, we should mention that the average order of each of the functions
\omega(n)
and
\Omega(n)
has been studied with great accuracy. In particular, it is well known (see for instance formulas (6.11) and (6.12) in
the book of De Koninck and Luca
[
12
]
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\omega(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\Big(\log\log N+c_{1}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\Omega(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\Big(\log\log N+c_{1}+c_{2}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big).

Similarly, it was proved by De Koninck
[
5
]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\omega(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{\log\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log\log N}\Big)\Big),

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\Omega(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{\log\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log\log N}\Big)\Big).

In fact, more accurate formulas can be found in Chapter 5 of the book of De Koninck and Ivić
[
11
]
.
In addition, De Koninck
[
6
]
proved that

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\Omega(n)}{\omega(n)}=N+c_{2}\frac{N}{\log\log N}+O\Big(\frac{N}{(\log\log N)^{2}}\Big).

On the other hand, as we will see, the behavior of the sum
\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}1/\pi(n)
turns out to have a connection with the function
\omega(n!)
, and interestingly the study of that particular sum has a long history, a survey of which can be found in
[
7
]
. The best known estimate is due to Ivić
[
24
]
and can be stated as follows:

(3.4)

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\pi(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}N-\log N-\log\log N+c_{3}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big),

where
c_{3}
is an absolute constant that is known to satisfy
6.6840<c_{3}<6.7830
(see Berkane and Dusart
[
3
]
).

Analogous results for sums of the “small” additive functions
\omega(n)
and
\Omega(n)
at factorial arguments are fairly easy to obtain. In fact, as we will see in Section
6
, one can obtain most of the following results by combining the above estimates with Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\omega(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N^{2}}{2\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\Omega(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N^{2}}{2}\Big(\log\log N+c_{1}+c_{2}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\omega(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}N-\log N-\log\log N+c_{3}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\Omega(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log\log N}\Big)\Big),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\Omega(n!)}{\omega(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(\log N\log\log N+(c_{1}+c_{2})\log N+O(\log\log N)).

3.2.
Large additive functions

Next, we consider the “large” additive functions

\beta(n)
,
B(n)
and
B_{1}(n)
defined in Section 1.
For these, we have the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

For each integer
n\geqslant 2
,

(3.10)

\beta(n!)=\sum_{p\leqslant n}p,\qquad B(n!)=S_{B}(n),\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{1}(n!)=\sum_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)},

where
\alpha_{p}(n)
is defined below in (
6.1
).
Moreover, for all
n\geqslant 2
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\beta(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2\log n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\right),

(3.12)

\displaystyle B(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\frac{n^{2}}{\log n}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\right),

(3.13)

\displaystyle B_{1}(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n-s_{2}(n)}+O\left((\sqrt{3})^{n}\right),

where
s_{2}(n)
stands for the sum of the binary digits of
n
.

Let us recall that
Alladi and Erdős
[
2
]
have proved that

(3.14)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\beta(n)\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}\qquad(N\to\infty),

and that De Koninck and Ivić
[
10
]
later showed that (
3.14
) can be improved to

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\beta(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{b_{i}N^{2}}{\log^{i}N}+O\left(\frac{N^{2}}{\log^{M+1}N}\right),

where
M
is any preassigned positive integer and where each
b_{i}
is a computable constant, with
b_{1}=\pi^{2}/12
.
Regarding the function
B
and
B_{1}
, Ivić
[
23
]
proved that

\sum_{n\leqslant N}B(n)=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\beta(n)+O(N^{2/3})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sum_{n\leqslant N}B_{1}(n)=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\beta(n)+O(N^{2/3}),

from which it follows in particular that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}B(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right)

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}B_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right).

Incidently, Ivić
[
20
]
proved that

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\beta(n)}=\frac{N}{e^{\sqrt{2\log N\log\log N}+O(\sqrt{\log N\log\log\log N})}},

an estimate that was later slightly improved by Ivić and Pomerance
[
21
]
.

The factorial counterparts of (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) are as follows.

Theorem 3.4
.

As
N
becomes large,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\beta(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N^{3}}{6\log N}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right),

(3.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}B(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{36}\frac{N^{3}}{\log N}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right),

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}B_{1}(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{N+O(\log N)}.

Furthermore, letting
f
be any one of the three functions
\beta
,
B
and
B_{1}
, we have

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(n!)}<\infty.

4.
The case of multiplicative functions

4.1.
Classical sums

Let us first consider the multiplicative functions
\gamma
and
\phi
already mentioned in Section 1.

Theorem 4.1
.

We have

(4.1)

\gamma(n!)=\prod_{p\leqslant n}p\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(n!)=n!\prod_{p\leqslant n}(1-1/p).

Moreover,

(4.2)

\gamma(n!)=e^{(1+o(1))n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(n!)\sim n!\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log n}\qquad(n\to\infty).

Perhaps the most
challenging estimates of
f(n!)
come from multiplicative functions
f
that are neither completely nor strongly multiplicative,
for reasons that will be detailed in Section
6.2
.
Of particular interest are the functions
d(n)
and
\sigma(n)
, as well as their higher-order variants
d_{k}(n)
and
\sigma_{\kappa}(n)
for integers
k\geqslant 2
and real numbers
\kappa
. Here
d_{k}(n):=\sum_{a_{1}\cdots a_{k}=n}1
is the number of ways of representing
n
as a product of
k
positive integers and
\sigma_{\kappa}(n):=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{\kappa}
is the sum of the
\kappa
-th powers of the positive divisors of
n
.

Ramanujan
[
30
]
studied the behavior of
d(n!)
by first conjecturing that for any given
\varepsilon>0
,

C^{\frac{n}{\log n}(1-\varepsilon)}<d(n!)<C^{\frac{n}{\log n}(1+\varepsilon)},

where
C=(1+1)\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt[3]{1+\frac{1}{3}}\sqrt[4]{1+\frac{1}{4}}\cdots\approx 3.51750
,
and by later proving
[
31
]
the more explicit formula

(4.3)

d(n!)=C^{\frac{n}{\log n}+o\left(\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}\right)}\qquad(n\to\infty).

See Andrews and Berndt
[
1
]
for more on these estimates.
Much later, Erdős, Graham, Ivić, and Pomerance
[
16
]
obtained an asymptotic series expansion for
\log d(n!)
by showing that for any given integer
M\geqslant 0
,

d(n!)=\exp\left\{\frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{c_{\ell}}{\log^{\ell}(n)}+O\Big(\frac{n}{\log^{M+2}n}\Big)\right\},

where
\displaystyle{c_{\ell}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log(\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor+1)}{t^{2}}\log^{\ell}t\,dt}
. In particular,

(4.4)

\log d(n!)\sim c_{0}\frac{n}{\log n}\qquad(n\to\infty),

where
c_{0}
can be shown to be equal to
\log C
. This is because, on the one hand,

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log(\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor+1)}{t^{2}}\,dt=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int_{s}^{s+1}\frac{\log(s+1)}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\log s\int_{s-1}^{s}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\log s}{s(s-1)},

while on the other hand,

\displaystyle\log C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s}\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{s}\Big)=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s}(\log(s+1)-\log s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{1}{s}\Big)\log s=\sum_{s=2}^{\infty}\frac{\log s}{s(s-1)}.

In fact, as we will see, each
c_{\ell}
is given by a similar explicit formula, and furthermore

(4.5)

c_{\ell}=(\ell+1)!+O\left((1-2^{-\ell-1})\ell!\right).

We shall generalize these results to
d_{k}(n!)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given integers
k\geqslant 2
and
M\geqslant 0
, we have

d_{k}(n!)=\exp\left\{\frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{c^{(k)}_{\ell}}{\log^{\ell}n}+O\Big(\frac{n}{\log^{M+2}n}\Big)\right\},

where

(4.6)

c_{\ell}^{(k)}=\ell!\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{j}s}{s}\log\Big(1+\frac{k-1}{s}\Big).

Moreover,

c_{\ell}^{(k)}=(k-1)(\ell+1)!+O\big((k-1)^{2}(1-2^{-\ell-1})\ell!\big).

In particular, Theorem
4.2
implies that for each integer
k\geqslant 2
,

(4.7)

\log d_{k}(n!)\sim c_{0}^{(k)}\frac{n}{\log n}\qquad(n\to\infty).

Remark 4.1
.

Equation (
4.6
) allows one to estimate each of the constants
c_{\ell}^{(k)}
for fixed
\ell
and
k
. For instance, the first five values in the case
k=2
are
{c_{0}^{(2)}\approx 1.2578}
,
c_{1}^{(2)}\approx 2.1139
,
c_{2}^{(2)}\approx 6.1145
,
c_{3}^{(2)}\approx 24.1764
,
c_{4}^{(2)}\approx 120.3601
. It is readily seen that approximation (
4.5
) is rapidly accurate.

In the same vein,
Fedorov
[
17
]
studied the number of divisors of the central binomial coefficient
\binom{2n}{n}
and obtained a similar asymptotic series. These results are easily extended to the function
d_{k}(n)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Given integers
k\geqslant 2
and
M\geqslant 0
, for each integer
n\geqslant 2
, we have

(4.8)

\frac{1}{\log k}\log d_{k}\Big(\binom{2n}{n}\Big)=\pi(2n)-\pi(n)+\frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{b_{\ell}}{\log^{\ell}n}+O\Big(\frac{n}{\log^{M+2}n}\Big),

where

\displaystyle{b_{\ell}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{n+1/2}^{n+1}\frac{\log^{\ell}t}{t^{2}}\,dt}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the right-hand side of (
4.8
) does not depend on
k
. Moreover, we can also deduce from this equality and the computation
b_{0}=\log 4-1
that

\log d_{k}\Big(\binom{2n}{n}\Big)\sim\log k\cdot\log 4\cdot\frac{n}{\log n}\qquad(n\to\infty).

It is clear that the same ideas can be used with the multiplicative function
\eta
already defined in Section
1
and for which we have
\displaystyle{\log\eta(n!)\sim e_{0}\frac{n}{\log n}}
, where

\displaystyle{e_{0}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log\lfloor t\rfloor}{t^{2}}\,dt=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log s}{s(s+1)}}
.
This asymptotic formula was first obtained by Jakimczuk
[
25
]
, but it was stated in a slightly different form, much weaker than the full statement we will now provide.

Theorem 4.4
.

Given any integer
M\geqslant 0
, we have

\eta(n!)=\exp\left\{\frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{e_{\ell}}{\log^{\ell}n}+O\left(\frac{n}{\log^{M+2}n}\right)\right\},

where
\displaystyle{e_{\ell}=\ell!\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{j!}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{j}(s+1)}{s+1}\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{s}\Big)}
.

As for the sum of the positive divisors of
n!
, it was studied by
Jakimczuk
[
26
,
27
]
, who obtained that

(4.9)

\displaystyle\sigma(n!)\sim e^{\gamma}n!\log n\qquad(n\to\infty),

and, more explicitly, that

\sigma(n!)=e^{\gamma}\sqrt{2\pi n}\Big(\frac{n}{e}\Big)^{n}\log n\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log n}\Big)\Big).

The proof of these results relies on convoluted arguments. Here, we simplify the proof and consider the more general function
\sigma_{\kappa}(n!)
for any fixed
\kappa>1
, while at the same time improving the error term.

Theorem 4.5
.

For all integers
n\geqslant 2
,

\sigma(n!)=n!\,e^{\gamma}\,\log n\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}n}\Big)\Big),

and for any real
\kappa>1
,

\sigma_{\kappa}(n!)=(n!)^{\kappa}\,\zeta(\kappa)\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)\Big).

Here
\zeta(\cdot)
is the Riemann zeta function

(4.10)

\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1}\qquad(\text{Re}(s)>1).

4.2.
Sums of multiplicative functions running over factorials

According to Wintner’s theorem (see Theorem 6.13 in the book of De Koninck and Luca
[
12
]
), if two arithmetic functions
f(n)
and
g(n)
are connected through the relation

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{g(n)}{n^{s}}\quad\mbox{for all real $s>1$}

and if moreover
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(n)/n
converges absolutely, then
\sum_{n\leqslant N}f(n)=(c+o(1))N
as
N\to\infty
, where

c:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(n)/n
. Applying this theorem to the multiplicative function
\gamma(n)/n
, one finds that

(4.11)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\gamma(n)}{n}=(c+o(1))N,\quad\mbox{where }c=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p(p+1)}\right)

(see
[
12
, Problem 6.5]
for the details). Using (
4.11
) and partial summation, we obtain that

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\gamma(n)=\left(\frac{c}{2}+o(1)\right)N^{2}\qquad(N\to\infty).

Also, it is known (see Theorems 320, 324, and 330 in
Hardy and Wright
[
19
]
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\phi(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}N^{2}+O(N\log N),

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}d(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\left(\log N+2\gamma-1+O(N^{-1/2})\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sigma(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}N^{2}+O(N\log N).

Moreover, one can prove (see Problem 6.12 and Theorem 6.19 in
[
12
]
) that

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\log d(n)=\log 2\cdot N\Big(\log\log N+c_{1}+c_{4}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

where
c_{1}=\gamma+\sum_{p}\big(\log(1-\frac{1}{p})+\frac{1}{p}\big)
and
c_{4}=\sum_{p}\frac{\log(1+1/p)}{p(p-1)}
.
For the sums of
\sigma(n)/\phi(n)
and
\phi(n)/\sigma(n)
, we have the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given any arbitrarily small number
\delta>0
,

(4.12)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\sigma(n)}{\phi(n)}=d_{1}N+O(N^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\phi(n)}{\sigma(n)}=d_{2}N+O(N^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta}),

where

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\Big(1+\frac{2p^{2}-1}{p(p+1)(p-1)^{2}}\Big)\approx 3.61744,

\displaystyle d_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\Big(1-\frac{2}{p(p+1)}-\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{(p+1)(p^{2}+p+1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{(p^{2}+p+1)(p^{3}+p^{2}+p+1)}+\cdots\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 0.45783.

Although we do not claim that the results in Proposition
4.1
are new, we could not find them in the literature, so for the sake of completeness we shall prove them in Section
6.3
.

For sums running over factorials, we have the following.

Theorem 4.6
.

We have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\gamma(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{N+O\big(\frac{N}{\log N}\big)},

(4.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\phi(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\gamma}\frac{N!}{\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\Big)\Big),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\log d(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{2}\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

(4.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sigma(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\gamma}N!\log N\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\Big)\Big),

(4.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\sigma(n!)}{\phi(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2\gamma}\left(N\log^{2}N-2N\log N+2N\right)\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\Big)\Big),

(4.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\phi(n!)}{\sigma(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-2\gamma}\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\Big)\Big).

5.
Comparing
f(n!)
with the extremal orders of
f(n)

In Sections
3
and
4
, we compared estimates of sums of additive and multiplicative functions with their factorial counterparts. It is also interesting to compare the behavior of
f(n!)
with the maximum and minimum values of
f(n)
.

In this section, we will use the estimate

(5.1)

\log n!=n\log n-n+O(\log n),

which follows from the fact that
\sum_{k\leqslant n}\log k=\int_{1}^{n}\log t\,dt+O(\log n)
, which itself represents a weak form of Stirling’s formula

(5.2)

n!\sim\Big(\frac{n}{e}\Big)^{n}\sqrt{2\pi n}\qquad(n\to\infty).

Estimate (
5.1
) thus implies that, as
n\to\infty
,

(5.3)

\log n\sim\log\log n!\qquad\text{and}\qquad n\sim\frac{\log n!}{\log\log n!}.

Sometimes
f(n!)
is very far from the maximum of
f(n)
over
n\leqslant N
. For instance,
\max_{n\leqslant N}\Omega(n)=\lfloor\log N/\log 2\rfloor,
with equality if and only if
k
is the largest positive integer such that
n=2^{k}\leqslant N
. However, by combining Theorem
3.1
and (
5.3
), we get that

\Omega(n!)\sim\log n!\frac{\log\log\log n!}{\log\log n!}\qquad(n\to\infty),

so that for large
n
,
\Omega(n!)<\log(n!)
, whereas for infinitely many integers
n
we have
\Omega(n)>1.4\log n
.

In other instances,
f(n!)
is almost as large as
f(n)
. Indeed,
we know that integers
n\leqslant N
have at most
(1+o(1))\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}
distinct prime factors, with equality if and only if
n
is the product of the first
y
primes, where
y
is chosen maximally so that
n=\prod_{p\leqslant y}p\leqslant N
. We thus have
\omega(n)=\pi(y)
, and since
\omega(n!)=\pi(n)
, we see that it is almost of this form, and indeed we have

\omega(n!)\sim\frac{\log n!}{(\log\log n!)^{2}}\qquad(n\to\infty).

There is a similar pattern for
\gamma(n)
. Indeed, it is clear that
\max_{n\leqslant x}\gamma(n)=\prod_{p\leqslant y}p
where
y
is chosen as above. Thus we see that
\max_{n\leqslant x}\gamma(n)=\gamma(y!)=x^{1+o(1)}
as
x\to\infty
, since
y\sim\log x
. On the other hand,

\gamma(n!)\sim(n!)^{\frac{1+o(1)}{\log\log n!}}\qquad(n\to\infty).

Similarly, for
d(n)
, a classical result of Wigert
[
34
]
gives

\log d(n)\leqslant\frac{\log 2\log n}{\log\log n}+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{(\log\log n)^{2}}\Big),

with equality if and only if
n
is the product of the first
r
primes with
r
large, while we see from (
4.4
) and (
5.3
) that

\log d(n!)\sim c_{0}\frac{\log n!}{(\log\log n!)^{2}}\qquad(n\to\infty),

as already observed in
[
16
]
. More generally,

\log d_{k}(n)\leqslant(\log k+o(1))\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\qquad(n\to\infty),

and we get from (
4.7
) that

\log d_{k}(n!)\sim c^{(k)}_{0}\frac{\log n!}{(\log\log n!)^{2}}\qquad(n\to\infty).

On the other hand, sometimes
f(n!)
attains the minimal or maximal value of
f(n)
. For example, we have

\phi(n!)\sim e^{-\gamma}\frac{n!}{\log\log n!}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma(n!)\sim e^{\gamma}n!\log\log n!\qquad(n\to\infty),

while it is known (see Theorems 323, 328 in
[
19
]
) that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi(n)\log\log n}{n}=e^{-\gamma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma(n)}{n\log\log n}=e^{\gamma}.

In other words, while the minimal order of
\displaystyle{\frac{\phi(n)\log\log n}{n}}
exists and is equal to
e^{-\gamma}
, the subsequence

\displaystyle{\left(\frac{\phi(n!)\log\log n!}{n!}\right)_{n\geqslant 1}}
does converge to
e^{-\gamma}
as
n\to\infty
. In the same manner, while the maximal order of

\displaystyle{\frac{\sigma(n)}{n\log\log n}}
exists and is equal to
e^{\gamma}
, the subsequence
\displaystyle{\left(\frac{\sigma(n!)}{n!\log\log n!}\right)_{n\geqslant 1}}
converges to
e^{\gamma}
.

Regarding the generalized sum of divisors function at factorial arguments, we have that, given any real number
\kappa>1
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{\kappa}(n)}{n^{\kappa}}=\zeta(\kappa),

while we know from Theorem
4.5
that, for
\kappa>1
,

\sigma_{\kappa}(n!)\sim(n!)^{\kappa}\,\zeta(\kappa)\qquad(n\to\infty).

6.
Proofs of the main results

6.1.
Useful tools

One of our main tools will be de Polignac’s formula (attributed by L.E. Dickson
[
15
]
to Legendre)

(6.1)

n!=\prod_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)},\mbox{ where }\alpha_{p}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{i}}\right\rfloor=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor\log_{p}n\rfloor}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{i}}\right\rfloor.

This formula can be somewhat simplified. Indeed, it is easy to show that

(6.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{i}}\right\rfloor=\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1},

where
s_{p}(n)
stands for the sum of the digits of
n
in base
p
.
For a proof of (
6.2
), see Problem 438 in the book of De Koninck and Mercier
[
13
]
.

We will also rely on classical analytic number theory results. In particular, setting
\text{Li}(x):=\int_{2}^{x}\frac{dt}{\log t}
, we will be using the prime number theorem in the form

(6.3)

\pi(x)=\text{Li}(x)+O(x\exp(-\sqrt{\log x})),

a consequence of Theorem 12.2 in the book of Ivić
[
22
]
.
We shall also at times use the prime number theorem in the simpler form

(6.4)

\pi(N)=\frac{N}{\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

which can be equivalently stated as

(6.5)

\theta(N)=N+O\Big(\frac{N}{\log N}\Big),

where
\theta
is the Chebyshev function

(6.6)

\theta(N):=\sum_{p\leqslant N}\log p.

From the prime number theorem, one easily deduces the following asymptotic formula for the sum of the primes up to
N
:

(6.7)

\sum_{p\leqslant N}p=\frac{N^{2}}{2\log N}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right)\right).

We will also need Mertens’ theorem in its strong form obtained by Dusart
[
14
]
:

(6.8)

\prod_{p\leqslant N}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)=\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}N}\Big)\Big).

6.2.
Proofs of Theorems
3.1
,
3.3
, and
4.1

Let
f
be an additive function and
g
a multiplicative function. Recall that we say that
f
is
completely
additive (resp.
g
is
completely
multiplicative) if
f(nm)=f(n)+f(m)
(resp.,
g(nm)=g(n)g(m)
) for any positive integers
n
and
m
. Also,
f
is
strongly
additive (resp.
g
is
strongly
multiplicative) if for any prime
p
and positive integer
a
,
f(p^{a})=f(p)
(resp.
g(p^{a})=g(p)
).

Note that
\omega
and
\beta
are strongly additive, whereas
\Omega
and
B
are completely additive.

Let us first state the following easily established proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
g
a strongly additive (resp. strongly multiplicative) function. Then
g(n!)=\sum_{m\leqslant n}g(m)
(resp.
g(n!)=\prod_{p\leqslant n}g(p)
).

The first identity in Theorem
3.1
is a consequence of Proposition
6.1
. The proof of the second identity, namely
\Omega(n!)=S_{\Omega}(n)
,
simply follows from the fact that
\Omega(n)
is a completely additive function and therefore that

\Omega(n!)=\sum_{m\leqslant n}\Omega(m)=S_{\Omega}(n).

The last two estimates in Theorem
3.1
follow respectively from estimates (
6.4
) and (
3.3
).

The first identity in
Theorem
3.3
follows from Proposition
6.1
.

The proof of the second one, namely of the identity
B(n!)=S_{B}(n)
, is immediate as it follows from the fact that
B(n)
is completely additive.

Estimates (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) are immediate consequences of (
3.15
) and (
6.7
).

The proof of estimate (
3.13
) goes as follows.
Using de Polignac’s formula (
6.1
) and the fact that
B_{1}
is additive, we have

(6.9)

\displaystyle B_{1}(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{1}\left(\prod_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)}\right)=\sum_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{\alpha_{2}(n)}+\sum_{3\leqslant p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)}=2^{n-s_{2}(n)}+\sum_{3\leqslant p\leqslant n}p^{\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1}},

where we made use of formula (
6.2
).
First observe that

(6.10)

\sum_{3\leqslant p\leqslant n}p^{\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1}}<\sum_{3\leqslant p\leqslant n}p^{n/(p-1)}=(\sqrt{3})^{n}\left(1+\sum_{5\leqslant p\leqslant n}\left(\frac{p^{1/(p-1)}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{n}\right).

Since
\displaystyle{\frac{p^{1/(p-1)}}{\sqrt{3}}<\frac{9}{10}}
for each prime
p\geqslant 5
, it follows that

\sum_{5\leqslant p\leqslant n}\left(\frac{p^{1/(p-1)}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{n}<\left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^{n}\cdot\pi(n)<2\qquad(n\geqslant 5),

an inequality which substituted in (
6.10
) gives

(6.11)

\sum_{3\leqslant p\leqslant n}p^{\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1}}<(\sqrt{3})^{n}(1+2)\ll(\sqrt{3})^{n},

which inserted in (
6.9
) completes the proof of estimate (
3.13
).

Observing that both
\gamma(n)
and
\phi(n)/n
are strongly multiplicative functions, the identities in (
4.1
) follow from Proposition
6.1
. Moreover, using the identity

\log\gamma(n!)=\sum_{p\leqslant n}\log p
, which by the prime number theorem in the form (
6.5
) is asymptotic to
n
as
n\to\infty
, proves the first identity in (
4.2
). The last estimate in (
4.2
) follows from Mertens’ theorem given through estimate (
6.8
). Gathering these observations, the proof of Theorem
4.1
is complete.

6.3.
Proofs of Theorems
3.2
,
3.4
, and
4.6

Note that (
3.7
) is an immediate consequence of (
3.4
).
The other estimates in Theorem
3.2
follow at once from standard analytic number theory techniques, including partial summation which we will use repetitively without further mention.
For instance, it follows from Theorem
3.1
and (
6.4
) that

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\omega(n!)=\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{n}{\log n}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log n}\Big)\Big)=\frac{N^{2}}{2\log N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log N}\Big)\Big),

which proves (
3.5
).

We skip the proof of estimate (
3.6
) since it can be obtained in a similar manner.

The proofs of (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) are similar. Hence, we only prove (
3.8
). By Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{\Omega(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n\big(\log\log n+c_{1}+c_{2}+O\big(\frac{1}{\log n}\big)\big)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n\log\log n\big(1+O\big(\frac{1}{\log\log n}\big)\big)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n\log\log n}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log\log n}\Big)\Big).

The main term is

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n\log\log n}=\frac{\log N+O(1)}{\log\log N}-\int_{2}^{N}\frac{\log t+O(1)}{t\log t(\log\log t)^{2}}\,dt.

Integrating by parts, this last integral is

\displaystyle\ll\int_{2}^{N}\frac{dt}{t(\log\log t)^{2}}=\frac{\log N}{(\log\log N)^{2}}+O(1)-\int_{2}^{N}\frac{dt}{t(\log\log t)^{3}}=O\Big(\frac{\log N}{(\log\log N)^{2}}\Big).

The same upper bound holds for the remaining sum in the error term since

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n(\log\log n)^{2}}=\int_{2}^{N}\frac{dt}{t(\log\log t)^{2}}+O(1).

Combining these estimates yields (
3.8
).

Finally, combining Theorem
3.1
with (
3.3
) and (
6.4
) gives

\begin{split}\frac{n}{\log n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}&\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\ell}}\left\{\int_{1}^{\infty}\log\binom{k+\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor-1}{k-1}\log^{\ell}t\,\frac{dt}{t^{2}}+O\Big(\frac{\log^{\ell+1}n}{n^{1/4}}\Big)\right\}\\

6.5.
Proof of Theorem
4.5

According to Mertens’ theorem already stated in (

{\prod_{p\leqslant x}(1-1/p)\sim e^{-\gamma}/\log x}
as
x\to\infty
.
Perhaps surprisingly, the behavior of the somewhat similar product
\prod_{p\leqslant n}(1-p^{-\alpha_{p}(n)-1})
is very different, since as we will now see it is asymptotic to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For integers
n\geqslant 2
,

\prod_{p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\Big)=1+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big).

Proof.

First set
\displaystyle{A(n):=\prod_{p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\Big)}
.
Clearly,
A(n)<1
for all
n\geqslant 2
. We will therefore focus our attention on finding a lower bound for
A(n)
which is “very close” to 1.

Let
r\geqslant 3
be a fixed integer (which will eventually be chosen to be large). Then,

A(n)=\prod_{p\leqslant n/r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\Big)\cdot\prod_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\Big)={A^{\prime}}_{r}(n)\cdot{A^{\prime\prime}}_{r}(n),

say.
Since, for each prime
p\leqslant n
, we have
\alpha_{p}(n)\geqslant 1
, it follows that
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{p^{2}}}
and therefore that

\displaystyle{1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}}
,
which means that

A^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{r}(n)=\prod_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{\alpha_{p}(n)+1}}\Big)\geqslant\prod_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big).

Since for all
y\in(0,1)
, we have
\log(1-y)>-2y
, we may write that

(6.19)

A^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{r}(n)\geqslant\exp\Big\{\sum_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\log(1-1/p^{2})\Big\}>\exp\Big\{-2\sum_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\Big\}.

Now observe that

(6.20)

\sum_{n/r<p\leqslant n}\frac{1}{p^{2}}<\sum_{p>n/r}\frac{1}{p^{2}}<\int_{n/r}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}=\frac{r}{n}.

Using (
6.20
) in (
6.19
), we obtain

(6.21)

A^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{r}(n)>\exp\Big\{-2\frac{r}{n}\Big\}=1+O\left(\frac{r}{n}\right).

We now move to find a lower bound for
A^{{}^{\prime}}_{r}(n)
. First observe that for
p\leqslant n/r
, we have

\alpha_{p}(n)\geqslant\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p}\right\rfloor\geqslant\left\lfloor r\right\rfloor=r,

which implies that

\alpha_{p}(n)+1\geqslant r+1
and therefore that

(6.22)

A^{{}^{\prime}}_{r}(n)\geqslant\prod_{p\leqslant n/r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{r+1}}\Big)>\prod_{p}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{r+1}}\Big)=\frac{1}{\zeta(r+1)}.

Approximating (
4.10
) by an integral, we have for any real

,

\zeta(s)\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\int_{2}^{\infty}t^{-s}\,dt=1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{2^{s-1}(s-1)}\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{2^{s}}=1+\frac{2}{2^{s}}.

Using this inequality with
s=r+1
in (
6.22
), we find that

(6.23)

A^{{}^{\prime}}_{r}(n)>\frac{1}{\zeta(r+1)}\geqslant\frac{1}{1+1/2^{r}}>1-\frac{1}{2^{r}}.

Combining (
6.21
) with (
6.23
), while choosing
\displaystyle{r=\left\lfloor\frac{\log n}{\log 2}\right\rfloor}
, we obtain that

(6.24)

A(n)=A^{{}^{\prime}}_{r}(n)\cdot A^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{r}(n)>\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)\Big)\cdot\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)\Big)=1+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big).

Combining (
6.24
) with our first observation to the effect that
A(n)<1
establishes that

\displaystyle{A(n)=1+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)}
, thus completing the proof of Lemma
6.1
.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
4.5
.
Let
\kappa\geqslant 1
and observe that for any prime power
p^{a}
,

(6.25)

\sigma_{\kappa}(p^{a})=1+p^{\kappa}+\cdots+p^{a\kappa}=\frac{p^{(a+1)\kappa}-1}{p^{\kappa}-1}=p^{a\kappa}\frac{1-1/p^{(a+1)\kappa}}{1-1/p^{\kappa}}.

It follows from equations (
6.1
) and (
6.25
) that

\sigma_{\kappa}(n!)=\prod_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)\kappa}\frac{1-1/p^{(\alpha_{p}(n)+1)\kappa}}{1-1/p^{\kappa}}=\frac{(n!)^{\kappa}}{\prod_{p\leqslant n}(1-\frac{1}{p^{\kappa}})}\cdot\prod_{p\leqslant n}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p^{(\alpha_{p}(n)+1)\kappa}}\Big).

Letting
A_{\kappa}(n)=\prod_{p\leqslant n}(1-1/p^{(\alpha_{p}(n)+1)\kappa})
,
we see from (
6.8
) that

\sigma(n!)=n!e^{\gamma}\log n\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\log^{3}n}\Big)\Big)\cdot A_{1}(n),

and from (
4.10
) that
\sigma_{\kappa}(n!)=(n!)^{\kappa}\zeta(\kappa)\cdot A_{\kappa}(n)
for
\kappa>1
.
Observing that
1>A_{\kappa}(n)>A_{1}(n)=A(n)
, we conclude that the estimate in Lemma
6.1
holds for any fixed
\kappa\geqslant 1
, and the result follows.

6.6.
Proofs of Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.3

Studying sums of the middle divisors is much easier at factorial arguments because, according to Proposition
2.1
, the values of
\rho_{1}(n!)
and
\rho_{2}(n!)
are close to
\sqrt{n!}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

First of all, it is clear that (
2.5
) is an immediate consequence of (
2.4
). We will therefore focus on the proof of (
2.4
).
We start with the following construction. First recall that

(6.26)

n!=\prod_{p\leqslant n}p^{\alpha_{p}(n)},\quad\mbox{where }\alpha_{p}(n)=\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1},

with
s_{p}(n)
standing for the sum of the digits of
n
in base
p
. Observing that
\alpha_{p}(n)=1
for all primes
p\in(n/2,n]
, we may write the factorisation of
n!
as

(6.27)

n!=\underbrace{2\cdots 2}_{\alpha_{2}(n)\,\mbox{\tiny times}}\cdot\underbrace{3\cdots 3}_{\alpha_{3}(n)\,\mbox{\tiny times}}\cdot\underbrace{5\cdots 5}_{\alpha_{5}(n)\,\mbox{\tiny times}}\cdots p_{\pi(n/2)+1}\cdots p_{\pi(n)},

where
p_{i}
stands for the
i
-th prime, and therefore in the above
p_{\pi(n)}=P(n!)=P(n)
, the largest prime factor of
n
.

Set
w_{1}=w_{1}(n):=p_{\pi(n/2)+1}\cdots p_{\pi(n)}
and
w_{2}=w_{2}(n)=n!/w_{1}
, so that

n!=\underbrace{2^{\alpha_{2}(n)}\cdot 3^{\alpha_{3}(n)}\cdots p_{k}^{\alpha_{p_{k}}(n)}}_{w_{2}}\cdot\underbrace{p_{k+1}\cdots p_{\pi(n)}}_{w_{1}},

where
k=\pi(n/2)
.

Recall the following result of R. Tijdeman
[
33
]
.

Given an infinite sequence
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
of
B
-friable numbers (or
B
-smooth numbers), there exists a positive constant
c(B)

such that

(6.28)

n_{i+1}-n_{i}\ll\frac{n_{i}}{\log^{c(B)}n_{i}}.

It is easily seen that if
B_{1}<B_{2}
, then
c(B_{2})\geqslant c(B_{1})
. Therefore, one can assume the existence of a positive number
c
satisfying
c\leqslant c(B)
for all integers
B\geqslant 2
. In fact, Langevin
[
28
]
later obtained a lower bound for this constant
c
, a very small one indeed (actually somewhat smaller than
1/10^{36}
) but nevertheless effective. In the following, we will therefore assume that
c\in(0,1)
.

For convenience, let us assume that
n\geqslant 6
and consider the set
S
of all the divisors of
w_{2}
which are no larger than
\sqrt{n!}/w_{1}
and let
r:=\max S
. Finally, let
d_{1}=r\cdot w_{1}
. It is clear that
d_{1}\mid n!
and that
d_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{n!}
.

Since
w_{1}=w_{1}(n)>1
, it follows that
\sqrt{n!}
is never an integer. Furthermore, observe that if

n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{k}=:d_{1}<\sqrt{n!}<n_{k+1}=:d_{1}^{+}

is the list of all the positive divisors of
n!
smaller than
\sqrt{n!}
plus the one located immediately after
\sqrt{n!}
, then it follows from (
6.28
) that for some positive constant
A
,

(6.29)

n_{k+1}-n_{k}=d_{1}^{+}-d_{1}<A\frac{d_{1}}{(\log d_{1})^{c}}.

Since the function
t/(\log t)^{c}
is increasing for all
t\geqslant e
, it follows from (
6.29
) that

(6.30)

d_{1}^{+}-d_{1}<A\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{(\log\sqrt{n!})^{c}},

and therefore that

d_{1}>d_{1}^{+}-A\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{(\log\sqrt{n!})^{c}}>\sqrt{n!}-A\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{(\log\sqrt{n!})^{c}}.

Using a weak form of Stirling’s formula, we have
\displaystyle{\sqrt{n!}>\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n/2}}
, thereby implying that

(6.31)

(\log\sqrt{n!})^{c}>\Big(\frac{n}{2}(\log n-1)\Big)^{c}>n^{c},

provided
n\geqslant 21
.

Using (
6.31
) in (
6.30
), we obtain

(6.32)

d_{1}>\sqrt{n!}-\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{n^{c}}=\sqrt{n!}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big),

thus completing the proof of the inequalities on the left-hand side of (
2.4
). Those on the right-hand side will easily follow by simply setting
d_{2}:=n!/d_{1}
; indeed, we then have

d_{2}=\frac{n!}{d_{1}}<\frac{n!}{(1-1/n^{c})\sqrt{n!}}<\sqrt{n!}\Big(1+\frac{2}{n^{c}}\Big),

provided
n
is sufficiently large.
∎

We will also be using the following lemma, whose proof is immediate.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any given integer
N\geqslant 1
, we have

\sum_{n=1}^{N}n!=N!\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big)\Big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{n!}=\sqrt{N!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\Big).

We now prove Theorem
2.1
.
It follows from Proposition
2.1
that

(6.33)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n!)=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sqrt{n!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big)\Big)=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sqrt{n!}+O\Big(\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{n^{c}}\Big).

Observe that

(6.34)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{n^{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{N!}}{N^{c}}\Big(1+\Big(\frac{N}{N-1}\Big)^{c}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}+\Big(\frac{N}{N-2}\Big)^{c}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}+\cdots\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{N!}}{N^{c}}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\Big)=O\Big(\frac{\sqrt{N!}}{N^{c}}\Big).

Substituting (
6.34
) in (
6.33
), and using Lemma
6.2
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\rho_{1}(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant N}\sqrt{n!}+O\Big(\frac{\sqrt{N!}}{N^{c}}\Big)=\sqrt{N!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\sqrt{N!}}{N^{c}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{N!}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{N^{c}}\Big)\Big),

thus proving the first estimate of Theorem
2.1
. The second estimate is obtained through a similar reasoning.

We move on to Theorem
2.2
.
Using Proposition
2.1
, we obtain

(6.35)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\rho_{1}(n!)}{\rho_{2}(n!)}=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\sqrt{n!}(1+O(1/n^{c}))}{\sqrt{n!}(1+O(1/n^{c}))}=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big)\Big)=N+O\Big(\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n^{c}}\Big).

Approximating this last sum by an integral, we get

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n^{c}}=\int_{1}^{N}\frac{1}{t^{c}}dt+O(1)=\frac{N^{1-c}}{1-c}+O(1)=O(N^{1-c}).

Using this last estimate in (
6.35
), we obtain that

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\frac{\rho_{1}(n!)}{\rho_{2}(n!)}=N+O(N^{1-c}),

thus establishing the first estimate of Theorem
2.2
. The second estimate can be proved in a similar manner.

To prove Theorem
2.3
,
we use Proposition
2.1
and Stirling’s formula (
5.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\rho_{1}(n!)}{\log\rho_{2}(n!)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log(\sqrt{n!}(1+O(1/n^{c})))}{\log(\sqrt{n!}(1+O(1/n^{c})))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\sqrt{n!}+\log(1+O(1/n^{c}))}{\log\sqrt{n!}+\log(1+O(1/n^{c}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{\log\sqrt{n!}+O(1/n^{c})}{\log\sqrt{n!}+O(1/n^{c})}=\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{c}\log\sqrt{n!}}\Big)}{1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{c}\log\sqrt{n!}}\Big)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{c+1}\log n}\Big)\Big)=N+O\Big(\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\frac{1}{n^{c+1}\log n}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N+O(1),

since the series
\displaystyle{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{c+1}\log n}}
converges. This establishes the first estimate in Theorem
2.3
. The second estimate is proved similarly.

7.
An analogue of Chowla’s conjecture for factorial arguments

Also of interest is the behavior of the quotient
\displaystyle{\frac{f(n!)}{f(n-1)!}}
for various arithmetic functions
f
.
For instance, in
[
16
]
, the authors showed that

\frac{d(n!)}{d((n-1)!)}=1+\frac{P(n)}{n}+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\Big),

where
P(n)
is the largest prime factor of
n
. Such ratios of arithmetic functions are easier to manage for some large classes of arithmetic functions. For instance, if
f
is completely additive, then

\frac{f(n!)}{f((n-1)!)}=1+\frac{f(n)}{S_{f}(n-1)},

while if
f
is strongly additive,

\frac{f(n!)}{f((n-1)!)}=\begin{cases}1+\frac{f(n)}{\sum_{p\leqslant n-1}f(p)}&\text{if }n\text{ is prime},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular, the additive functions
\omega
,
\Omega
, and
\beta
are such that the ratio of their consecutive values at factorial arguments is
\sim 1
as
n\to\infty
.

On the other hand, for the multiplicative functions
\gamma
and
\phi
, the results are more interesting. This is because, for every completely multiplicative function
f
, we have

\frac{f(n!)}{f((n-1)!)}=f(n),

and
for every strongly multiplicative function
f
, we have

\frac{f(n!)}{f((n-1)!)}=\begin{cases}f(n)&\text{if }n\text{ is prime},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Because
\gamma(n)
and
\phi(n)/n
are strongly multiplicative functions, we obtain that

\frac{\gamma(n!)}{\gamma((n-1)!)}=\begin{cases}n&\text{if }n\text{ is prime},\\
1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

\frac{\phi(n!)}{\phi((n-1)!}=\begin{cases}n-1&\text{if }n\text{ is prime},\\
n&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let us now consider the
Liouville function

\lambda(n):=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}
.
A famous conjecture due to Chowla
[
4
]
in its simplest form can be stated as follows.

Conjecture (Chowla)
As
N\to\infty
,

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(n)\lambda(n+1)=o(N).

Interestingly, the analogous form of Chowla’s conjecture at factorial arguments is true. Indeed, we have the following result.

Theorem 7.1
.

As
N\to\infty
,

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(n!)\lambda((n+1)!)=o(N).

Proof.

The proof is quite straightforward and in fact, as we will see, it is a consequence of the prime number theorem.
We will show that

(7.1)

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\lambda((n-1)!)\lambda(n!)=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

Clearly,

\displaystyle\lambda((n-1)!)\lambda(n!)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\sum_{k\leqslant n-1}\Omega(k)+\sum_{k\leqslant n}\Omega(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{2\sum_{k\leqslant n-1}\Omega(k)+\Omega(n)}=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}=\lambda(n).

Therefore, in order to prove (
7.1
), we only need to prove that

(7.2)

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\lambda(n)=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

Recall that if we let
\mu
stand for the Möbius function, then the prime number theorem implies that

(7.3)

\sum_{2\leqslant n\leqslant N}\mu(n)=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty)

(see for instance Theorem 5.3 in
[
12
]
). Now, one can easily verify the identity

(7.4)

\lambda(n)=\sum_{d^{2}k=n}\mu(k)\qquad(n\geqslant 1).

Setting
M(N):=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\mu(n)
, estimate (
7.3
) implies that
M(N)=o(N)
as
N\to\infty
and therefore that, given any arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a large number
N_{0}
(which we can assume to be sufficiently large so that
1/N_{0}<\varepsilon/2
) such that

(7.5)

M(N)<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}N\quad\mbox{ for all }N\geqslant N_{0}.

Then, for any integer
N>N_{0}
, in light of (
7.4
), we have

(7.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N/d^{2}}\mu(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=1}^{N}M(N/d^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{0}}M(N/d^{2})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=N_{0}+1}^{N}M(N/d^{2})=S_{1}+S_{2},

say. Choosing
N>N_{0}^{3}
, for each
d\leqslant N_{0}
, we have that
\displaystyle{\frac{N}{d^{2}}>\frac{N_{0}^{3}}{N_{0}^{2}}=N_{0}}
and we can therefore apply inequality (
7.5
) and obtain that

(7.7)

S_{1}<\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=1}^{N_{0}}\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\frac{N}{d^{2}}<\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\frac{\varepsilon}{4}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

On the other hand, trivially,

(7.8)

S_{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d=N_{0}+1}^{N}\frac{N}{d^{2}}<\int_{N_{0}}^{N}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}<\frac{1}{N_{0}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Gathering (
7.7
) and (
7.8
) in (
7.6
) proves estimate (
7.2
) and therefore completes the proof of
Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Remark 7.1
.

By a similar reasoning, one can show that Chowla’s conjecture is in fact equivalent to the statement

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda((n-1)!)\lambda((n+1)!)=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

Remark 7.2
.

It is clear that one can adapt the proof of Theorem
7.1
to prove that if
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is any sequence of positive integers (not necessarily monotonic), then

(7.9)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(a_{n})\lambda(na_{n})=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

Hence, by choosing
a_{n}=(n-1)!
in (
7.9
), we obtain (
7.1
).
On the other hand, by choosing
a_{n}=n
and thereafter
a_{n}=P(n)
, we have

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(n)\lambda(n^{2})=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty)

and

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(P(n))\lambda(nP(n))=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

Moreover, according to a more general version of Chowla’s conjecture, given any positive integer
k
,

(7.10)

\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda(n)\lambda(n+1)\cdots\lambda(n+k-1)=o(N)\qquad(N\to\infty).

It is clear that one can adapt the proof of Theorem
7.1
to prove that if
k
is an even integer, then the factorial version of (
7.10
) holds as well.
