Generative Datalog with Stable Negation
Mario Alviano, Matthias Lanzinger, Michael Morak, Andreas Pieris

Abstract
. Extending programming languages with stochastic behaviour such as probabilistic choices or random sampling has a long tradition in computer science. A recent development in this direction is a declarative probabilistic programming language, proposed by Bárány et al. in 2017, which operates on standard relational databases. In particular, Bárány et al. proposed generative Datalog, a probabilistic extension of Datalog that allows sampling from discrete probability distributions. Intuitively, the output of a generative Datalog program Π \Pi on an input database D D is a probability space over the minimal models of D D and Π \Pi , the so-called possible outcomes. This is a natural generalization of the (deterministic) semantics of Datalog, where the output of a program on a database is their unique minimal model. A natural question to ask is how generative Datalog can be enriched with the useful feature of negation, which in turn leads to a strictly more expressive declarative probabilistic programming language. In particular, the challenging question is how the probabilistic semantics of generative Datalog with negation can be robustly defined. Our goal is to provide an answer to this question by interpreting negation according to the stable model semantics.



1.
Introduction

Extending programming languages with stochastic behaviour, such as probabilistic
choices or random sampling, has a long tradition in computer science that goes
back in the 70s and 80s
(
Kozen 1981
;
Saheb-Djahromi 1978
)
.
During the last decade or so, there was a significant effort in developing dedicated probabilistic programming languages (for example, Pyro
(
Bingham
et al. 2019
)
, Stan
(
Carpenter et al. 2017
)
, Church
(
Goodman et al. 2008
)
, R2
(
Nori
et al. 2014
)
, Figaro
(
Pfeffer 2009
)
, and Anglican
(
Tolpin
et al. 2015
)
, to name a few) that allow the specification and “execution”, via probabilistic inference, of sophisticated probabilistic models.

A recent development in this direction is a purely declarative probabilistic programming language based on Datalog (PPDL), proposed by Bárány et al.
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
, which operates on standard relational databases.
A PPDL program consists of the generative and the constraint component.
The generative component is essentially a rule-based program written in a probabilistic extension of Datalog, called generative Datalog, that allows sampling from discrete probability distributions. Intuitively, the output of a generative Datalog program
\Pi
on an input database
D
is a probability space over the minimal models of
D
and
\Pi
,
the so-called possible outcomes. Typically, the probability space over the possible outcomes obtained via such a generative process is called prior distribution. The probabilistic semantics of generative Datalog can be seen as a natural generalization of the (deterministic) semantics of Datalog, where the output of a program on a database is their unique minimal model.
Now, the constraint component allows
one to pose logical constraints that the relevant possible outcomes should satisfy and, semantically,
transforms the prior distribution into
the subspace obtained by conditioning on the constraints.

Generative Datalog is indeed a powerful probabilistic formalism that allows for defining a rich family of probabilistic models, while retaining the key property of declarativity, that is, the independence of the order in which the rules are executed. On the other hand, as discussed below, there are simple probabilistic models that cannot be defined using generative Datalog due to its monotonic nature.

Example 1.1 (
Network Resilience
).

We consider a network of routers, and assume that some of them have been
infected by a malware that interrupts the routing, and it also attempts
to infect neighbouring routers with a success rate of 10%. We say that
such a network is dominated by the malware if all routers are infected
or isolated in the network (i.e., connected only to infected routers).
In such a scenario, we would like to be able to predict how likely it is
for the network to be dominated.
The propagation of the malware can be encoded using the generative Datalog rule

\text{\rm Infected}(x,\mathit{true}),\text{\rm Connected}(x,y)\rightarrow\text{\rm Infected}(y,{\sf Flip}\langle 0.1\rangle)

meaning that if the router
x
is infected and
x
is connected with

y
, then
y
is also infected with probability
0.1
; that is, the
probabilistic term
{\sf Flip}\langle 0.1\rangle
becomes
\mathit{true}
with
probability
0.1
, or
\mathit{false}
with probability
0.9
.
However, the domination of the network is a non-monotonic
property in the sense that expanding a dominated network may result in a
non-dominated one, whereas generative Datalog is inherently monotonic.
Hence, although the propagation of the malware can be encoded via
generative Datalog, the likelihood of domination cannot be predicted via
this formalism.

With the aim of overcoming the above weakness of generative Datalog, we enrich
it with the useful feature of
negation as failure
, interpreted according to the
standard stable model semantics stemming from logic programming
(
Gelfond and
Lifschitz 1988
)
.
Having such a formalism in place, we could predict the likelihood of a network
being dominated using the probabilistic rule from
Example
1.1
, and the rules

\displaystyle\text{\rm Router}(x),\neg\text{\rm Infected}(x,\mathit{true})\rightarrow\textrm{Uninfected}(x)

\displaystyle\text{\rm Uninfected}(x),\text{\rm Uninfected}(y),\text{\rm Connected}(x,y)\rightarrow\text{\rm Fail},

which essentially state that a router which is
not
infected is uninfected, and whenever two uninfected routers are connected in the network, then the malware fails to dominate the network.

Our Contributions.

Adding stable negation to generative Datalog comes with several technical
complications, discussed in Section
3
, that need to be
studied and understood in order to define a robust semantics. This is precisely
the goal of the present work. Our main contributions can be summarized as
follows.

In Section
3
, we introduce a formal semantics for generative Datalog with stable negation.
This
cannot simply be done by following the development underlying the semantics of
positive generative Datalog from
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
and considering stable models
instead of minimal models.
In fact, with negation, it is not enough to consider single models; we need to
thing of possible outcomes as
sets
of stable models. These sets are
represented symbolically via
ground programs
that also explicitly encode
the underlying probabilistic choices.
This last property is crucial
since, due to non-monotonicity,
the probabilistic choices are not necessarily reflected in the resulting set of
stable models.
Hence, a set of stable models may not
carry enough information to allow us to calculate its probability.

In Section
4
, we show that the probabilistic semantics from
Section
3
can be equivalently defined via a fixpoint
procedure. We introduce a new chase procedure that operates on ground programs
(rather than on databases, as in the standard chase procedure), which we then use to
define a chase-based probability space that leads to the desired fixpoint
semantics. Interestingly, the semantics is independent of the
order in which the rules are executed.

Finally, in Section
5
, we concentrate on the central class of generative Datalog programs with
stratified negation
, and we provide further evidence of the robustness of our semantics.

A discussion on related work can be found in the appendix.

2.
Preliminaries

We recall the basics on relational databases, TGDs with stable negation, and probability spaces.
We assume the disjoint countably infinite sets
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{V}
of
constants
and
variables
, respectively; we refer to constants and variables as
terms
. For
n>0
, let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Relational Databases.

A
(relational) schema

\mathbf{S}
is a finite set of relation names (or predicates) with associated arity; we write
\mathsf{ar}(R)
for the arity of a predicate
R
.
A
(relational) atom

\alpha
over
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
R(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, where
R\in\mathbf{S}
,
\mathsf{ar}(R)=n
, and
t_{i}\in\mathbf{C}\cup\mathbf{V}
for each
i\in[n]
.
A
literal
over
\mathbf{S}
is either an atom over
\mathbf{S}
(i.e., a positive literal), or an atom over
\mathbf{S}
preceded by the negation symbol
\neg
(i.e., a negative literal).
An
instance
of
\mathbf{S}
is a (possibly infinite) set of atoms over
\mathbf{S}
that mention only constants, while a
database
of
\mathbf{S}
is a
finite
instance of
\mathbf{S}
.
We write
\mathsf{dom}(I)
for the set of constants occurring in an instance
I
.
For simplicity, in the rest of the paper, we assume that the constants of
\mathbf{C}
are translatable into real numbers. Therefore, whenever we refer to a constant of
\mathbf{C}
, we actually refer to its translation into a real number.

TGDs with Stable Negation.

A
tuple-generating dependency with negation
(or simply TGD
¬
)
\sigma
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
is an expression

\forall\bar{x}\forall\bar{y}\,(\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\ \rightarrow\ \exists\bar{z}\,\psi(\bar{x},\bar{z})),

where
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
(resp.,
\psi(\bar{x},\bar{z})
) is a quantifier-free conjunction of literals (resp., atoms) over
\mathbf{S}
with variables from
\bar{x}\cup\bar{y}
(resp.,
\bar{x}\cup\bar{z}
), and every variable in a negative literal occurs also in a positive literal; the latter condition is known as
safety
.
For brevity, we will write
\sigma
as
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})\rightarrow\exists\bar{z}\,\psi(\bar{x},\bar{z})
, and use comma instead of
\wedge
for joining atoms.
The formula
\psi(\bar{x},\bar{z})
is the
head
of
\sigma
, denoted
H(\sigma)
, whereas the formula
\phi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is the
body
of
\sigma
, denoted
B(\sigma)
. We further write
B^{+}(\sigma)
for the set of positive literals in
B(\sigma)
, and
B^{-}(\sigma)
for the set of atoms appearing in the negative literals of
B(\sigma)
.
A
TGD
¬
program
(resp.,
infinitary TGD
¬
program
)
\Sigma
over
\mathbf{S}
is a finite (resp., infinite) set of TGD
¬
over
\mathbf{S}
. The
schema
of
\Sigma
, denoted
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
, is the set of predicates in
\Sigma
, which is always finite, even if
\Sigma
is
infinitary.
Let
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
be the set of head atoms of
\Sigma
.

For our purposes, although syntactically a TGD
¬
is a first-order sentence, semantically is not since the negation should be interpreted as stable negation rather than classical negation. We proceed to recall the formal definition as given in
(
Alviano
et al. 2017
)
. The high-level idea is, given an (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma
, to construct a second-order sentence
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma]
that encodes the intention underlying the stable model semantics, and then define the stable models of
\Sigma
as the (classical) models of
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma]
.

Consider an (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma
. Let
\mathbf{R}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})
be the list of predicates of
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
in some fixed order (e.g., lexicographic order), and
\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma}=(X_{\Sigma}^{1},\ldots,X_{\Sigma}^{n})
a list of
n
distinct predicate variables. For a literal
\ell
occurring in
\Sigma
, let

\displaystyle\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma}(\ell)\ =\ \begin{cases}X_{\Sigma}^{i}(\bar{t}),&\mbox{if }\ell=R_{i}(\bar{t})\\
\neg R_{i}(\bar{t}),&\mbox{if }\ell=\neg R_{i}(\bar{t}).\end{cases}

We define
\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma}(\Sigma)
as the (infinitary) TGD
¬
program obtained by applying
\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma}(\cdot)
to every literal in
\Sigma
. The sentence
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma]
is

\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\sigma\ \wedge\ \neg\exists\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma}\left((\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma}<\mathbf{R})\wedge\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma}(\Sigma)\right),

where
\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma}<\mathbf{R}
denotes the sentence

\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\forall x_{1}\cdots\forall x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})}\left(X_{\Sigma}^{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})})\rightarrow R_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})})\right)\wedge\\
\neg\left(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\forall x_{1}\cdots\forall x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})}\left(R_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})})\rightarrow X_{\Sigma}^{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{i})})\right)\right).

An instance
I
of
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
is a
stable model of
\Sigma
if it is a (classical) model of
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma]
; note that here we adopt the
unique-name assumption
, that is, every constant of
\mathbf{C}
refers to a distinct object. We write
{\sf sms}(\Sigma)
for the set of stable models of
\Sigma
.
Finally, given a database
D
of
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
, an instance
I
of
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
is a
stable model of
D
and
\Sigma
if
I\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma[D])
with
\Sigma[D]=\{\mathsf{True}\rightarrow\alpha\mid\alpha\in D\}\cup\Sigma
. We write
{\sf sms}(D,\Sigma)
for the set of stable models of
D
and
\Sigma
, i.e.,
{\sf sms}(D,\Sigma)={\sf sms}(\Sigma[D])
.

Probability Spaces.

A
discrete probability space
is a pair
(\Omega,P)
, where
\Omega
is a finite or countably infinite set, called the
sample space
, and
P
is a function
\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\sum_{o\in\Omega}P(o)=1
, called
discrete probability distribution over
\Omega
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
be the set that collects all the functions
P:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
(\Omega,P)
is a discrete probability space, that is, all the discrete probability distributions over
\Omega
. A
parameterized probability distribution over
\Omega
is a function
\delta:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
with
k>0
being the parameter dimension, i.e.,
\delta(\bar{p})
is a discrete probability distribution over
\Omega
for every parameter instantiation
\bar{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For the sake of presentation, we write
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle
instead of
\delta(\bar{p})
to avoid the overuse of parentheses, i.e., expressions of the form
\delta(\cdot)(\cdot)
.
Due to our assumption that constants are essentially real numbers, it suffices to consider discrete probability distributions that are
numerical
, that is, over a sample space
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
A simple illustrative example can be found in the appendix.

We further need to recall the notion of probability space that goes beyond countable sample spaces.
Let
\Omega
be a (possibly uncountable) set. A
\sigma
-algebra
over
\Omega
is a subset
\mathcal{F}
of
2^{\Omega}
(the powerset of
\Omega
), i.e., a collection of subsets of
\Omega
, that (i) contains
\Omega
, (ii) is closed under complement, i.e.,
F\in\mathcal{F}
implies
\Omega\setminus F\in\mathcal{F}
, and (iii) is closed under countable unions, i.e., for a countable set
\mathcal{E}
of elements of
\mathcal{F}
,
\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}E\in\mathcal{F}
. As a consequence, a
\sigma
-algebra contains the empty set, and is closed under countable intersections.
A
probability space
is defined as a triple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, where

•

\Omega
is a (possibly uncountable) set, called the
sample space
,

•

\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra over
\Omega
, called the
event space
, and

•

P:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[0,1]
, called a
probability measure
, is such that
P(\Omega)=1
, and, for every countable set
\mathcal{E}
of pairwise disjoint elements of
\mathcal{F}
,
P(\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}E)=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}P(E)
.

For a non-empty
F\subseteq 2^{\Omega}
, the closure of
F
under complement and countable unions is a
\sigma
-algebra, and it is said to be
generated
by
F
.

3.
Generative Datalog with Negation

A Datalog
¬
program (which is essentially a TGD
¬
program without existentially quantified variables) specifies how to obtain a set of stable models from an input database. In this section, we present generative Datalog
¬
programs that essentially specify how to infer a distribution over sets of stable models from an input database. We first present the syntax of generative Datalog
¬
programs. We then give an informal description of their semantics, highlighting the challenges in defining the probabilistic semantics in question, and then proceed with the formal definition.
Henceforth, by
\Delta
we refer to a finite set of parameterized numerical probability distributions.

Syntax.

A
\Delta
-term
is an expression of the form
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle[\bar{q}]
, where
\bar{p}
is a non-empty tuple of terms,
\bar{q}
is a (possibly empty) tuple of terms, and
\delta
belongs to
\Delta
with parameter dimension
|\bar{p}|
. We call
\bar{p}
the
distribution parameters
, and
\bar{q}
the (optional)
event signature
. When the event signature is missing we simply write
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle
.
Intuitively,
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle[\bar{q}]
denotes a sample from the probability distribution
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle
, where different samples are drawn for different event signatures.
A
\Delta
-atom
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form
R(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
, where
R\in\mathbf{S}
,
\mathsf{ar}(R)=n
, and
t_{i}
is either an ordinary term (constant or variable), or a
\Delta
-term for each
i\in[n]
. In other words, a
\Delta
-atom is an atom that can mention, apart from terms, also
\Delta
-terms.

A
generative Datalog rule with negation w.r.t.
\Delta
(or simply
GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
rule
)
\rho
over a schema
\mathbf{S}
is an expression of the form

R_{1}(\bar{u}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{u}_{n}),\neg P_{1}(\bar{v}_{1}),\ldots,\neg P_{m}(\bar{v}_{m})\rightarrow R_{0}(\bar{w})

for
n,m\geq 0
, where
R_{0},R_{1},\ldots,R_{n},P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
are predicates of
\mathbf{S}
,
\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{n}
are tuples over
\mathbf{C}\cup\mathbf{V}
,
\bar{v}_{1},\ldots,\bar{v}_{m}
are tuples over
\mathbf{C}\cup\mathbf{V}
such that each of their variables is mentioned in
\bar{u}_{j}
for some
j\in[n]
, and
\bar{w}
is a tuple consisting of terms from
\mathbf{C}\cup\mathbf{V}
and
\Delta
-terms such that each of its variables, possibly as part of the distribution parameters or the event signature of a
\Delta
-term, is mentioned in
\bar{u}_{j}
for some
j\in[n]
.
The (
\Delta
-)atom that appears on the right of the
\rightarrow
symbol is the
head
of
\rho
, denoted
H(\rho)
, while the expression on the left of the
\rightarrow
symbol is the
body
of
\rho
, denoted
B(\rho)
.
We further write
B^{+}(\rho)
for the set of positive literals in
B(\rho)
, and
B^{-}(\rho)
for the set of atoms appearing in the negative literals of
B(\rho)
.

A
GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program

\Pi
over
\mathbf{S}
is a finite set of Datalog
¬
rules over
\mathbf{S}
.
A predicate
R\in\mathbf{S}
occurring in
\Pi
is called
extensional
if there is no rule of the form
{\rm body}\rightarrow R(\bar{w})
in
\Pi
, i.e.,
R
occurs only in rule-bodies; otherwise, it is called
intensional
.
The
extensional (database) schema
of
\Pi
, denoted
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
, consists of the extensional predicates in
\Pi
, while the
intensional schema
of
\Pi
, denoted
\mathsf{idb}(\Pi)
, is the set of all intensional predicates in
\Pi
. The
schema
of
\Pi
, denoted
\mathsf{sch}(\Pi)
, is the set
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)\cup\mathsf{idb}(\Pi)
.
Given a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
,
\Pi[D]
is the program
\{\rightarrow\alpha\mid\alpha\in D\}\cup\Pi
.

Example 3.1.

Consider the “network resilience” example already discussed in the Introduction. Recall that the network is dominated by the malware if all routers are infected or isolated (i.e., connected only to infected routers).
Assume we have the following schema:

\displaystyle\text{\rm Router}(\mathit{router\_id})

\displaystyle\text{\rm Infected}(\mathit{router\_id},\mathit{boolean})

\displaystyle\text{\rm Connected}(\mathit{router\_id},\mathit{router\_id})

\displaystyle\text{\rm Uninfected}(\mathit{router\_id}).

Consider the parameterized distribution
{\sf Flip}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\{0,1\}}
over
\{0,1\}
such that
{\sf Flip}\langle p\rangle(1)=p
and
{\sf Flip}\langle p\rangle(0)=1-p
, and let
\Delta=\{{\sf Flip}\}
.
The GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
over the above schema that encodes the malware domination cases is as follows:

\displaystyle\text{\rm Infected}(x,1),\text{\rm Connected}(x,y)\rightarrow\text{\rm Infected}(y,{\sf Flip}\langle 0.1\rangle[x,y])

\displaystyle\text{\rm Router}(x),\neg\text{\rm Infected}(x,1)\rightarrow\textrm{Uninfected}(x)

\displaystyle\text{\rm Uninfected}(x),\text{\rm Uninfected}(y),\text{\rm Connected}(x,y)\rightarrow\bot,

where
\bot
denotes
\mathsf{False}
. The symbol
\bot
is syntactic sugar as
\mathsf{False}
can be always simulated using stable negation. In particular, we can replace
\bot
with a
0
-ary predicate
Fail
that is forced to be false in every stable model via the rule
\text{\rm Fail},\neg\text{\rm Aux}\rightarrow\text{\rm Aux}
, where
Aux
is a new predicate not mentioned in the program in question.

An Informal Semantics.

Consider a positive (i.e., without negation) GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
. Every configuration (i.e., a set)
C
of probabilistic choices made via the
\Delta
-terms during the execution of
\Pi
on an input database
D
leads to a
different
minimal model
M_{C}
of
D
and
\Pi
(in fact, of
D
and a set of TGDs
\Sigma_{\Pi}
that somehow captures the intended meaning of
\Pi
). Furthermore, the probabilistic choices of
C
are explicitly represented in
M_{C}
, which means that
M_{C}
carries enough information that allows us to calculate its probability.
According to the probabilistic semantics from
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
, the output of
\Pi
on
D
is a probability space over the sample space consisting of all the minimal models of
D
and
\Sigma_{\Pi}
with non-zero probability, the so-called
possible outcomes
. This is a natural generalization of the (deterministic) semantics of Datalog, where the output of a positive program on an input database is their unique minimal model.

Once we add negation to generative Datalog, one may be tempted to think that we can follow a similar approach by considering stable instead of minimal models.
However, in the presence of stable negation, the situation changes significantly. A configuration of probabilistic choices no longer leads to a single minimal model, but rather a (possibly empty) set of stable models. Consider the GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\{{\sf Flip}\}]
program
\Pi_{\mathit{coin}}
consisting of

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{\rm Coin}({\sf Flip}\langle 0.5\rangle)

\displaystyle\text{\rm Coin}(1),\neg\text{\rm Aux}_{1}\rightarrow\text{\rm Aux}_{2}

\displaystyle\text{\rm Coin}(0)\rightarrow\bot

\displaystyle\text{\rm Coin}(1),\neg\text{\rm Aux}_{2}\rightarrow\text{\rm Aux}_{1}

which encodes the flipping of a “fair” coin.
Intuitively, if the flipping results to
0
(which corresponds to
Heads
),
then there is no stable model, but if the result is
1
(which corresponds to
Tails
), then
\{\text{\rm Aux}_{1},\text{\rm Coin}(1)\}
and
\{\text{\rm Aux}_{2},\text{\rm Coin}(1)\}
are the stable models of
\Pi_{\mathit{coin}}
(in fact, similarly to the positive case, of a TGD
¬
program
\Sigma_{\Pi_{\mathit{coin}}}
that somehow captures the intended meaning of
\Pi_{\mathit{coin}}
; here the database is empty).
It may also happen that different configurations of probabilistic choices lead to the same set of stable models (for example, if we add to
\Pi_{\mathit{coin}}
the rule
\text{\rm Coin(1)}\rightarrow\bot
).

The above informal discussion essentially tells us that, in general, there is no correspondence between stable models and configurations of probabilistic choices.
Thus, it will be conceptually problematic to directly consider individual stable models as possible outcomes, which in turn form the underlying sample space.
This leads to the obvious idea of considering sets of stable models (including the empty set) with non-zero probability, obtained via certain configurations of probabilistic choices, as possible outcomes.
Coming back to the “coin” example, this would result in the possible outcome
\{\{\text{\rm Aux}_{1},\text{\rm Coin}(1)\},\{\text{\rm Aux}_{2},\text{\rm Coin}(1)\}\}
for the
configuration where the result of the flip is
1
, and the possible outcome
\emptyset
for the configuration where the result of the flip is
0
, and the two respective events should have probability
0.5
each.

Although the above idea is a plausible one, which we have thoroughly explored, it brings us to the other complication that is coming with negation.
Unlike positive generative Datalog, the probabilistic choices are not necessarily reflected in the resulting set of stable models. In other words, there are cases where it is not possible to extract the configuration of probabilistic choices that led to a set of stable models
M
by simply inspecting
M
. This can be seen in the “coin” example, where the fact that the result of the flip is
0
is not explicitly encoded in the respective stable model, that is, the empty model.
This is problematic as a set of stable models does not carry enough information that allows us to calculate its probability.
To overcome this complication, the key idea is to symbolically represent a set of stable models
M
by an (infinitary) ground program such that its set of stable models is precisely
M
, and it also reflects the probabilistic choices that led to
M
. In other words, the possible outcomes will not be sets of stable models, but rather (infinitary) ground programs that lead to sets of stable models.
There is, however, a crucial choice to be made here: infinite possible outcomes (i.e., infinitary ground programs) are considered as valid or invalid. The probabilistic semantics by Bárány et al. in
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
consider infinite possible outcomes as valid ones. On the other hand, as argued by Grohe et al. in
(
Grohe
et al. 2020
)
, where generative Datalog with continuous distributions is studied, it is both technically and conceptually more meaningful to consider infinite possible outcomes as invalid ones, which are collected in the so-called error event. In our development, we adopt the view by Grohe et al.
(
Grohe
et al. 2020
)
.

This brings us to the importance of the adopted grounding strategy since, depending on how sophisticated this strategy is, a certain configuration of probabilistic choices may have a finite or an infinite representative grounding, which in turn affects the resulting probability space. Thus, the definition of our semantics is parameterized by a grounder, and we will discuss a mechanism for performing a comparison among semantics induced by different grounders.

From GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
to TGD

We proceed to formalize the translation of a generative Datalog program with negation into a TGD

We write

Grounding GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
Programs.

Having the translation from GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
to TGD
¬
, we can now introduce the notion of grounding for generative Datalog
¬
, which relies on a simple notion of grounding for TGD
¬
programs. A
ground instance
of a TGD
¬

\sigma
is obtained by replacing each (universally or existentially quantified) variable in
\sigma
with a constant of
\mathbf{C}
. Given a TGD
¬
program
\Sigma
, the
grounding of
\Sigma
, denoted
\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)
, is the infinitary program consisting of all the ground instances of the TGD
¬
of
\Sigma
.
We can now proceed with the grounding for generative Datalog
¬
programs.

Roughly speaking, a grounder
G
of a generative Datalog
¬
program
\Pi
is a monotonic function whose domain is a collection of sets of ground AtR TGDs of
\Sigma_{\Pi}
, and it assigns to each such set
\Sigma^{\prime}
(which essentially encodes a configuration of probabilistic choices) a subset
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})
such that
{\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime}\cup\Sigma^{\prime\prime})
is the set of stable models of
\Sigma_{\Pi}
that are consistent with the probabilistic choices encoded by the ground AtR TGDs in
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Then, the grounding of
\Pi
relative to
G
will be a set of (infinitary) ground TGD
¬
programs that are somehow induced by
G
.
We proceed to formalize this.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
.
A subset
\Sigma
of
\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})
is called
(functionally) consistent
if, with
\alpha=\text{\rm Active}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q})
, for every two ground AtR TGDs
\sigma_{o}
and
\sigma_{o^{\prime}}
of the form

\alpha\ \rightarrow\ \text{\rm Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\ \rightarrow\ \text{\rm Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o^{\prime})

respectively, it holds that
o=o^{\prime}
. Intuitively, this means that
\Sigma
encodes valid probabilistic choices.
We write
[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
for all the consistent subsets of
\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})
.
Observe that a consistent subset
\Sigma
of
\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})
induces a partial function
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}:\mathit{Act}\rightarrow\mathit{Res}
, where
\mathit{Act}
(resp.,
\mathit{Res}
) is the set of all atoms that can be formed using predicates of the form
\text{\rm Active}_{n}^{\delta}
(resp.,
\text{\rm Result}_{n}^{\delta}
) of
\mathsf{sch}(\Sigma)
, and constants from
\mathbf{C}
.
We say that
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}
is
compatible
with a set
\Sigma^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})
, written
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow\Sigma^{\prime}
, if it is defined on every atom of the form
\text{\rm Active}_{n}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q})
occurring in
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})
, that is, the set of head atoms of
\Sigma^{\prime}
.
A
totalizer
for the partial function
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}
is a set
\Sigma^{\prime}\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
such that
\Sigma\subseteq\Sigma^{\prime}
and
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
is total.
We can now introduce the desired notion of grounding.

Definition 3.3 (
Grounding Generative Datalog
¬
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
. A
grounder of
\Pi
is a monotonic function

G\ :\ [2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}\ \rightarrow\ 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})}

such that, for every
\Sigma\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
, if
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow G(\Sigma)
, then
{\sf sms}(G(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)={\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists}\cup\Sigma^{\prime})
for every totalizer
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}
.
The
G
-grounding of
\Pi
, denoted
G
-
\mathsf{ground}(\Pi)
, is defined as the set

\{\Sigma\cup G(\Sigma)\mid\Sigma\in\mathsf{terminals}(G)\text{ and}\\
\text{there is no }\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{terminals}(G)\text{ such that }\Sigma^{\prime}\subsetneq\Sigma\}

with
\mathsf{terminals}(G)=\left\{\Sigma\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}\,\mid\,\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow G(\Sigma)\right\}
.

The importance of the monotonicity of grounders for generative Datalog
¬
will be revealed in Section
4
, where we present our chase-based semantics. Before proceeding with our probabilistic semantics, we present a concrete example of a grounder, which is conceptually very simple (hence the name “simple grounder”), but at the same time a relevant one as we shall see later in the paper.

Simple Grounder.

Given a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, we define the so-called simple grounder
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}
, which relies on an operator that operates on (infinitary) existential-free TGD
¬
programs.
Consider an (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma
without existentially quantified variables. We define the operator
\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}
that extends an (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma^{\prime}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
by matching the positive body literals of
\Sigma
with ground head atoms of
\Sigma^{\prime}
. The notion of matching can be formalized via homomorphisms with which we assume the reader is familiar. We write
h(A)\subseteq B
to indicate that
h
is a homomorphism from a set of atoms
A
to a set of atoms
B
. Given a TGD
¬

\sigma
and a homomorphism
h
from
B^{+}(\sigma)
to some other set of atoms, we write
h(\sigma)
for the TGD
¬
obtained by applying
h
to the body and head of
\sigma
.
We define
\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}
as the monotonic function from
2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
to
2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
such that, for every
\Sigma^{\prime}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
,

\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}(\Sigma^{\prime})\ =\ \Sigma^{\prime}\cup\left\{h(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\ \text{ and }\ h(B^{+}(\sigma))\subseteq\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})\right\}.

We further define the sets

\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{0}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\Sigma^{\prime}

\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{i+1}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}(\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{i}(\Sigma^{\prime}))\text{ for }i>0,

and we let

\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\prime})\ =\ \bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{i}(\Sigma^{\prime}).

Due to the monotonicity of
\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}
, it is clear that
\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\prime})
is the least fixpoint of
\mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma}
that contains
\Sigma^{\prime}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
. We are now ready to define the function
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}
.

Definition 3.4 (
Simple Grounder
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
.
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}:[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})}
is such that

\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}(\Sigma)\ =\ \mathit{Simple}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}^{\infty}(\emptyset)\setminus\Sigma

where
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists}\cup\Sigma
, for every
\Sigma\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
.

It is not difficult to show that
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}
is indeed a grounder:

Proposition 3.5.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
. It holds that
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}
is a grounder of
\Pi
.

Probabilistic Semantics.

We now have all the ingredients for defining the semantics of generative Datalog
¬
. We start by formalizing the notion of possible outcome, which in turn gives rise to the sample space over which we need to define a probability space.

Definition 3.7 (
Possible Outcome
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
. A
possible outcome of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
is a (possibly infinitary) program
\Sigma\in G\text{-}\mathsf{ground}(\Pi[D])
such that
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
for every
\Delta
-atom
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
. We write
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
for the set of all possible outcomes of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
.

Observe that each
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
induces a (possibly empty) subset of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
, that is,
{\sf sms}(\Sigma)
. Note that, for
\Sigma^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
,
\Sigma^{\prime}\neq\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
does
not
imply
{\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime})\neq{\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime\prime})
, i.e., two different possible outcomes may induce the same subset of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
.

Given a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
, the output of
\Pi
on
D
, relative to some grounder
G
of
\Pi[D]
, should be understood as a probability space over
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
. We proceed to explain how such a probability space can be defined. In essence, the events that we are interested in are subsets of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
induced by proper (i.e., finite) programs of
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
, or, in other words, by finite possible outcomes of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
; we write
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
for the set that collects all the (finite) programs of
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
. Therefore, finite possible outcomes resulting in the same set of stable models of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
must belong to same event.
Moreover, following
Grohe et al. on generative Datalog with
continuous distributions
(
Grohe
et al. 2020
)
, we collect all the infinite possible
outcomes of
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
in the
infinity (or error) event

\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)
.

Let
F_{\Pi,G}^{D}
be the subset of
2^{\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)}
consisting of
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)
, and all the maximal subsets
E
of
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
such that, for all sets
\Sigma^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime\prime}\in E
,
{\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime})={\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime\prime})
. We define
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
as the
\sigma
-algebra over
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
generated by
F_{\Pi,G}^{D}
.
We finally define the function
P_{\Pi,G}^{D}:\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows. For every
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
, let

\mathit{Pr}(\Sigma)\ =\ \prod_{\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)}{\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)}.

Then, for every (countable) set
E\in\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
with
E\in 2^{\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)}
, let

P_{\Pi,G}^{D}(E)\ =\ \sum_{\Sigma\in E}{\mathit{Pr}(\Sigma)}.

Observe that
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\in\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
, and thus, we can let

P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)\right)\ =\ 1-P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\right).

Finally,
P_{\Pi,G}^{D}
naturally extends to countable unions via countable addition.

¬
program on an input database relative to a grounder.

Positive Programs.

A natural question is whether the probabilistic semantics proposed above for generative Datalog
¬
coincides with the existing semantics for generative Datalog introduced in
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
, if we concentrate on positive programs that guarantee that all the possible outcomes are finite, and thus, the error event takes probability zero.
It should not be overlooked, however, that the probabilistic semantics for generative Datalog
¬
is actually a family of semantics since, depending on the adopted grounder, we get different semantics; a mechanism for performing a qualitative comparison among the different semantics is discussed below.
Therefore, the key question is whether there exists a grounder for generative Datalog
¬
that gives rise to semantics that coincides with that of
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
.
Interestingly, we can show that such a grounder exists; in fact, we can show that this is the simple grounder introduced above (see Definition
3.4
).
Due to space constraints, the details are deferred to the appendix.

Qualitative Comparison of Probabilistic Semantics.

As already discussed, Theorem
3.9
gives rise to a family of semantics for generative Datalog
¬
since different grounders lead to different semantics. It is thus natural to ask how we can qualitatively compare the various semantics.
Recall that infinite possible outcomes are considered as invalid ones that are collected in the error event, and, in general, the larger the error event, the less the probability assigned to valid (that is, finite) possible outcomes. This essentially tells us that the greater the probability assigned to finite outcomes by a probability measure, the better. Recall that the output of a program
\Pi
on a database
D
relative to a grounder
G
of
\Pi[D]
is the probability space
\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D),\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D},P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\right)
.

Definition 3.11 (
Comparison of Semantics
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G,G^{\prime}
be grounders of
\Pi[D]
. We say that
\Pi_{G}(D)
is as good as
\Pi_{G^{\prime}}(D)
if

P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\{\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\mid{\sf sms}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{I}\}\right)\ \geq\\
P_{\Pi,G^{\prime}}^{D}\left(\{\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,G^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\mid{\sf sms}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{I}\}\right)

for every set of stable models
\mathcal{I}\subseteq{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
.

The above notion equips us with a mechanism that can guide the choice of the probabilistic semantics based on the class of generative Datalog
¬
that we are interested in, which in turn depends on the underlying application. For example, if we are interested only in positive generative Datalog programs, we can then show that there is no need to go beyond the probabilistic semantics induced by the simple grounder, i.e., the semantics induced by the simple grounder is as good as the semantics induced by any other grounder.

Theorem 3.12.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be an arbitrary grounder of
\Pi[D]
. It holds that
\Pi_{\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
is as good as
\Pi_{G}(D)
.

4.
Fixpoint Probabilistic Semantics

We now tackle the question whether the probabilistic semantics presented in the previous section can be equivalently defined via a fixpoint procedure. An affirmative answer to this question will provide a procedure that is amenable to practical implementations. To this end, we introduce a novel chase procedure for generative Datalog
¬
, which we then use to define a chase-based probability space that leads to the desired fixpoint probabilistic semantics.

Chasing Generative Datalog
¬
Programs.

We start by introducing our novel chase procedure. Let us stress that our chase procedure deviates from the standard one, which typically operates over databases with the aim of completing an incomplete database as dictated by a given set of TGDs. Our chase procedure operates on ground AtR TGDs with the aim of completing such programs as dictated by a given ground TGD
¬
program.
We start with the notion of trigger application, which corresponds to a chase step.

Definition 4.1 (
Trigger Application
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and let
\Sigma\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})}
.
A
trigger for
\Sigma^{\prime}
on
\Sigma
is an atom
\alpha=\text{\rm Active}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q})\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})
such that there is no TGD in
\Sigma
of the form
\alpha\rightarrow\text{\rm Return}^{\delta}_{|\bar{q}|}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)
for some
o\in\mathbf{C}
.
An
application of
\alpha
to
\Sigma
returns the set of (infinitary) TGD programs
\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\ldots\}
such that the following hold:

•

for each integer
i>0
,
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma\cup\left\{\alpha\rightarrow\text{\rm Return}^{\delta}_{|\bar{q}|}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\right\}
, where
o\in\mathbf{C}
and
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
, and

•

for each constant
o\in\mathbf{C}
with
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
, there exists
i>0
such that
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma\cup\left\{\alpha\rightarrow\text{\rm Return}^{\delta}_{|\bar{q}|}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\right\}
.

Such a trigger application is denoted as
\Sigma\langle\alpha\rangle\{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\ldots\}
.

Having the notion of trigger application, we can now introduce the central notion of chase tree, which is relative to a grounder.

Definition 4.2 (
Chase Tree
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
. A
chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
is a (possibly infinite) rooted labelled tree
T=(N,E,\lambda)
, where
\lambda:N\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]}^{\exists})}
, such that:

•

the root node is labelled by
\emptyset
,

•

for each non-leaf node
v
(i.e.,
v
has an outgoing edge) with children
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
there exists a trigger
\alpha
for
G(\lambda(v))
on
\lambda(v)
such that
\lambda(v)\langle\alpha\rangle\{\lambda(v_{1}),\lambda(v_{2}),\ldots\}
, and

•

for each leaf node
v
(i.e.,
v
has no outgoing edges), there is no trigger
\alpha
for
G(\lambda(v))
on
\lambda(v)
.

A chase tree
T=(N,E,\lambda)
essentially encodes iterative trigger applications.
A
maximal path
of
T
is either a finite path
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
in
T
, where
v_{1}
is the root node and
v_{n}
is a leaf node, or an infinite path
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
in
T
, where
v_{1}
is the root node.
Let
\mathsf{paths}(T)
be the set of all maximal paths of
T
; we further write
\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)
(resp.,
\mathsf{paths}^{\infty}(T)
) for the set of finite (resp., infinite) maximal paths of
T
.
The
result
of a finite (resp., infinite) maximal path
\pi=v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
(resp.,
\pi=v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
) of
T
, denoted
{[\![\pi]\!]}
, is the program (resp., infinitary program)
\lambda(v_{n})
(resp.,
\bigcup_{i>0}\lambda(v_{i})
).
We further write
{[\![\pi]\!]}_{G}
for
{[\![\pi]\!]}\cup G({[\![\pi]\!]})
.
The notations
{[\![\cdot]\!]}
and
{[\![\cdot]\!]}_{G}
naturally extend to sets of maximal paths, i.e., for a set
M
of maximal paths,
{[\![M]\!]}=\{{[\![\pi]\!]}\mid\pi\in M\}
and
{[\![M]\!]}_{G}=\{{[\![\pi]\!]}\cup G({[\![\pi]\!]})\mid\pi\in M\}
.
We may simply say chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
meaning a chase tree relative to some grounder of
\Pi[D]
.
The next easy lemma collects some useful properties of chase trees.

Lemma 4.3.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
T=(N,E,\lambda)
be a chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
. Then:

(1)

For every
v\in N
,
\lambda(v)\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]}^{\exists})}]_{=}
.

(2)

For every
u,v\in N
,
u\neq v
implies
\lambda(u)\neq\lambda(v)
.

Item (1) essentially tells us that the nodes of a chase tree are labelled with sets of ground AtR TGDs that are functionally consistent, while item (2) establishes an injectivity property.
Another crucial property is the fact that two different chase trees, no matter in which order the triggers are applied, always lead to the same set of programs, that is, the set of programs obtained after collecting the results of their finite paths coincide. This is established by the next technical lemma, which exploits Lemma
4.3
, as well as the monotonicity of grounders for generative Datalog
¬
programs.

Lemma 4.4.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let

be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
, and
T,T^{\prime}
be chase trees for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
. It holds that

{[\![\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)]\!]}\ =\ {[\![\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T^{\prime})]\!]}.

We finally establish the key connection between chase trees and possible outcomes, which heavily relies on Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma 4.5.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
, and
T
be chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
. The binary relation

\left\{(\pi,{[\![\pi]\!]}_{G})\mid\pi\in\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)\right\}.

is a bijection from
\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)
to
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
.

Fixpoint Probabilistic Semantics.

We now proceed to introduce the desired fixpoint semantics by exploiting the chase procedure presented above.
Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
, and
T
be a chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
. Our goal is to define a probability space
\mathit{PS}_{T}
over
\mathsf{paths}(T)
, and then show that from
\mathit{PS}_{T}
we can get a probability space that faithfully mimics
\Pi_{G}(D)

We start by observing that each path
\pi\in\mathsf{paths}(T)
induces a (possibly infinite) subset of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
, that is,
{\sf sms}({[\![\pi]\!]}_{G})
. Note also that for
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{paths}(T)
, it might be the case that
\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}
(and thus, by Lemma
4.3
(2),
{[\![\pi]\!]}\neq{[\![\pi^{\prime}]\!]}
), but they induce the same subset of
{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]})
. We now proceed to define a probability space over
\Omega_{T}=\mathsf{paths}(T)
.
Let
F_{T}
be the subset of
2^{\mathsf{paths}(T)}
consisting of
\mathsf{paths}^{\infty}(T)
, and all the maximal subsets
E
of
\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)
such that, for all
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in E
,
{\sf sms}({[\![\pi]\!]}_{G})={\sf sms}({[\![\pi^{\prime}]\!]}_{G})
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{T}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
F_{T}
. We finally define the function
P_{T}:\mathcal{F}_{T}\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows.
For every
\pi\in\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)
, let

\mathit{Pr}(\pi)\ =\ \prod_{\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(G({[\![\pi]\!]}))}{\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)}.

Then, for every (countable) set
E\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
with
E\in 2^{\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)}
, let

P_{T}(E)\ =\ \sum_{\pi\in E}{\mathit{Pr}(\pi)}.

Clearly,
\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
, and thus, we can let

P_{T}\left(\mathsf{paths}^{\infty}(T)\right)\ =\ 1-P_{T}\left(\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)\right).

Finally,
P_{T}
extends to countable unions via countable addition.

Clearly,
\mathit{PS}_{T}=(\Omega_{T},\mathcal{F}_{T},P_{T})
is a probability space.
Consider now the triple
{[\![\mathit{PS}_{T}]\!]}_{G}
obtained from
\mathit{PS}_{T}
by replacing every
\pi\in\Omega_{T}
with
{[\![\pi]\!]}_{G}
. Formally,
{[\![\mathit{PS}_{T}]\!]}_{G}=({[\![\Omega_{T}]\!]}_{G},{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G},{[\![P_{T}]\!]}_{G})
, where

•

{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G}=\{{[\![E]\!]}_{G}\mid E\in\mathcal{F}_{T}\}
, and

•

{[\![P_{T}]\!]}_{G}:{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G}\rightarrow[0,1]
is such that, for every
{[\![E]\!]}_{G}\in{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}
(by definition,
E\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
),
{[\![P_{T}]\!]}_{G}({[\![E]\!]}_{G})=P_{T}(E)
.

We claim that
{[\![\mathit{PS}_{T}]\!]}_{G}
is a probability space that faithfully mimics the probability space
\Pi_{G}(D)=\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D),\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D},P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\right)
.
More precisely, by exploiting Lemma
4.5
, we can show the following:

Theorem 4.6.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be a grounder of
\Pi[D]
, and
T
be a chase tree for
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
. The following hold:

(1)

{[\![\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)]\!]}_{G}=\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
.

(2)

There exists a bijection
g:{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
such that, for every
E\in{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G}
,
E\cap{[\![\mathsf{paths}^{\mathsf{fin}}(T)]\!]}_{G}=g(E)\cap\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
.

(3)

For every
E\in{[\![\mathcal{F}_{T}]\!]}_{G}
,
{[\![P_{T}]\!]}_{G}(E)=P_{\Pi,G}^{D}(g(E))
.

The above theorem essentially tells us that there is en effective way of obtaining the probability space
\Pi_{G}(D)
from the probability space
\mathit{PS}_{T}
, no matter which chase tree
T
we consider.

5.
Stratified Negation

We have seen in Section
3
that for generative Datalog programs without negation, there is no need to go beyond the probabilistic semantics induced by the simple grounder, i.e., the semantics induced by the simple grounder is as good as the semantics induced by any other grounder (Theorem
3.12
).
In this section, we establish an analogous result for the important class of generative Datalog
¬
programs with stratified negation. But let us first recall this class.

Generative Datalog with Stratified Negation.

Consider a GDatalog
¬
program
\Pi
. The
dependency graph
of
\Pi
, denoted
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)
, is a multigraph
(V,E)
, where (i)
V=\mathsf{sch}(\Pi)
, (ii) for each
\rho\in\Pi
with
P
being the predicate of
H(\rho)
, and for every predicate
R
occurring in
B^{+}(\rho)
(resp.,
B^{-}(\rho)
), there is a
positive edge

(R,P)
(resp., a
negative edge

(R,P)
) in
E
, and (iii) there are no other edges in
E
.
We say that
P
depends on
R
(relative to
\Pi
)
if there is a path in
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)
from
R
to
P
. A
cycle
in
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)
is a path from a predicate to itself.
We say that the program
\Pi
has
stratified negation
if
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)
does not contain a cycle that goes through a negative edge. Henceforth, we refer to such kind of programs as GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
programs.

Interestingly, when the negation is stratified, a certain stratification of the given program can be induced that guarantees that a negative literal is affected only by rules from previous strata. We proceed to formalize this.
A
strongly connected component
of
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)=(V,E)
is a subset-maximal set
C\subseteq V
such that
P
depends on
R
for every two distinct predicates
P,R\in C
. Let
\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)
be the set of strongly connected components of
\mathsf{dg}(\Pi)
. A
topological ordering
of
\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)
is a
linear order
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
, for
n=|\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)|
, over
\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)
such that, for
1\leq i<j\leq n
, there is no predicate of
C_{i}
that depends on one of
C_{j}
.
We denote by
\Pi_{|C_{i}}

the subset of
\Pi
that keeps only the rules whose head mentions a predicate from
C_{i}
.

Perfect Grounding for GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
.

Unlike positive programs, we can show that for generative Datalog
¬s
programs there exists a better grounder than the simple one.
More precisely, for a GDatalog
¬s
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
, there exists a grounder
G
of
\Pi[D]
such that
\Pi_{G}(D)
is as good as
\Pi_{\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
, but not vice versa. We proceed to introduce a grounder that leads to the ultimate semantics in the case of stratified negation.

Given a GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, we define the so-called perfect grounder
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
, which can be think as the version of the simple grounder (see Definition
3.4
) that considers the rules of
\Pi
in a certain order according to the induced stratification of
\Pi
.
Similarly to the simple grounder,
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
relies on an operator that operates on (infinitary) existential-free TGD
¬
programs. Consider such a program
\Sigma
. We define
\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}
as the monotonic function from
2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
to
2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
such that, for every
\Sigma^{\prime}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma)}
,

\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}(\Sigma^{\prime})\ =\ \Sigma^{\prime}\cup\left\{h(\sigma)\ \mid\ \sigma\in\Sigma,\ h(B^{+}(\sigma))\subseteq\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})\ \text{and}\right.\\
\left.h(B^{-}(\sigma))\cap\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})=\emptyset)\right\}.

The set
\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}^{\infty}(\Sigma^{\prime})
is defined as expected; due to the monotonicity of
\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}
, it is the least fixpoint of
\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}
that contains
\Sigma^{\prime}
.

Assume now that
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
is a topological ordering over
\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)
. For a set
\Sigma\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
, we define
\Sigma\uparrow C_{i}\in 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})}
, which, intuitively speaking, is the set assigned to
\Sigma
by the perfect grounder considering the rules stratum by stratum up to the
i
-th stratum. Eventually,
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
will assign to
\Sigma
the (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma\uparrow C_{n}
.
By convention, let
\Sigma\uparrow C_{0}=\emptyset
. Then, for every
i\in[n]
, with
\Sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\Sigma_{\Pi_{|C_{i}}}^{\not\exists}\cup\Sigma\uparrow C_{i-1}\cup\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\Sigma\uparrow C_{i}\ =\ \begin{cases}\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma^{\prime}_{i}}^{\infty}(\emptyset)\setminus\Sigma,&\mbox{if }\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow\Sigma\uparrow C_{i-1}\\
\Sigma\uparrow C_{i-1},&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We are now ready to introduce the function
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
.

Definition 5.1 (
Perfect Grounder
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
be a topological ordering over
\mathsf{scc}(\Pi)
. The function
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}:[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists})}
is such that

\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}(\Sigma)=\Sigma\uparrow C_{n}
, for every
\Sigma\in[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\exists})}]_{=}
.

An example that illustrates
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
can be found in the appendix.
We can show that
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
is indeed a grounder. This relies on a technical lemma that establishes the following: every (infinitary) TGD
¬
program
\Sigma
that belongs to the
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
-grounding of
\Pi
has a
unique
stable model, namely the instance
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
.

Proposition 5.2.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
. It holds that
\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}
is a grounder of
\Pi
.

We now show the main result of this section: the perfect grounder leads to the ultimate semantics when the negation is stratified.

Theorem 5.3.

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg s}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. Let
G
be an arbitrary grounder of
\Pi[D]
. It holds that
\Pi_{\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
is as good as
\Pi_{G}(D)
.

6.
Conclusions

We have introduced generative Datalog with negation that allows sampling from discrete probability distributions, and defined its probabilistic semantics by interpreting negation according to the standard stable model semantics. We have also shown that the semantics can be equivalently defined via a fixpoint procedure based on a new chase procedure that operates on ground programs.
Here we focused on the generative component, but one may also incorporate conditioning under events of positive probability just as in
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
.
An interesting direction for future research, which is highly relevant for practical implementations, is to devise sophisticated grounders that avoid as much as possible the generation of superfluous ground rules.
Furthermore, in view of the fact that continuous distributions appear in a variety of application scenarios for probabilistic databases, it is natural to extend generative Datalog with negation to support continuous distributions.

Appendix A
Related Work

The probabilistic programming and the probabilistic database communities have developed several different models and systems that allow to specify probability distributions over data.
Since the paper by Bárány et al.
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
, which introduced generative Datalog, already presents a thorough discussion on related work (see Section 7 of
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
), to which we refer the reader, we proceed to discuss only the related work that is closer to our work.
Conceptually closer to generative Datalog (with or without negation) are languages studied in statistical relational AI (StarAI)
(
Raedt
et al. 2016
)
, which aim at the combination of predicate logic and probabilities.
Here are some prominent examples of such languages:

•

ProbLog
(
Raedt
et al. 2007
;
Fierens et al. 2015
)
is a probabilistic extension of Prolog where standard Prolog rules can be annotated with a probability value (i.e., it allows uncertainty at the level of rules).
Hybrid ProbLog
(
Gutmann
et al. 2010
)
is an extension of ProbLog that allows continuous attribute-level uncertainty in rule-heads.

•

Probabilistic Answer Set Programming
(
Cozman and
Mauá 2020
)
extends the
well-known logic programming language of Answer Set
Programming
(
Gelfond and
Lifschitz 1988
;
Gelfond and
Lifschitz 1991
)
with probabilistic facts. The
semantics of this language is defined via upper and lower probability bounds
for stable models. A number of similar extensions have been proposed in the
literature, with the general idea to annotate facts or rules with
probabilities
(
Lukasiewicz 2001
;
Wan and Kifer 2009
;
Baral
et al. 2009
)
. A more
complete overview is given by Cozman and Mauá
(
Cozman and
Mauá 2020
)
.

•

Markov Logic Networks (MLNs)
(
Richardson and
Domingos 2006
)
combine first-order logic and Markov Networks, and they describe joint distributions of variables based on weighted first-order constraints.
Hybrid MLNs
(
Wang and Domingos 2008
)
extend MLNs to continuous distributions.
Infinite MLNs
(
Singla and
Domingos 2007
)
allow for countably
infinitely many variables with countable domains.

•

Probabilistic Soft Logic
(
Kimmig et al. 2012
)
is another statistical relational learning framework that combines first-order logic and probabilistic graphical models that specifies joint distributions with weighted rules, but also “soft” truth values.

•

Another formal language that combines first-order logic and probabilistic graphical models, which also supports continuous distributions, is Bayesian Logic
(
Milch et al. 2005
)
, which in turn builds on Bayesian Networks.

•

A probabilistic extension of Datalog
±
(a family of knowledge representation languages that enrich Datalog with features such as value invention and equality in head-rules
(
Calì et al. 2010
)
) that is based on MLNs as underlying probabilistic semantics has been proposed in
(
Gottlob et al. 2013
)
.
Various probabilistic extensions of plain Datalog can be also found in
(
Deutch
et al. 2010
;
Fuhr 1995
;
Fuhr 2000
)
.

Although the probabilistic formalisms discussed above (as well as those discussed in Section 7 of
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
), might share individual features with generative Datalog (with or without negation), the combination of plain Datalog with probabilistic programming languages should be attributed to
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
.
Moreover, the fact that generative Datalog (with or without negation) can express attribute-level uncertainty in rule-heads (via the
\Delta
-terms appearing in rule-heads) differs from other probabilistic extensions of Datalog, where the uncertainty is at the at level of rules specified by attaching probabilities to them (like, for example, the languages from
(
Gottlob et al. 2013
)
and
(
Deutch
et al. 2010
;
Fuhr 1995
;
Fuhr 2000
)
).

Parameterized Probability Distribution - Example

We provide an example that illustrates the notion of parameterized probability distribution.
Throwing a die is a classical example of a random process. The throwing of an

for each

On the other hand, we can model the throwing of a

die by using a parameterized probability distribution. In particular, this can be done via
\mathsf{Die}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
with
\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,6\}
defined as follows: for every
\bar{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{6})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{6}p_{i}=1
implies
\mathsf{Die}\langle\bar{p}\rangle(0)=0
and
\mathsf{Die}\langle\bar{p}\rangle(i)=p_{i}
for each

; otherwise,
\mathsf{Die}\langle\bar{p}\rangle(0)=1
and
\mathsf{Die}\langle\bar{p}\rangle(i)=0
for each

. Note that the outcome

is associated with incorrect instantiations of the parameters.

\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}
, it holds that

{\sf sms}(\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)\ =\ {\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\not\exists}_{\Pi}\cup\Sigma^{\prime}).

Fix an arbitrary totalizer
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}
. We proceed to show the above equality. For brevity, let
\Sigma_{1}=\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{2}=\Sigma^{\not\exists}_{\Pi}\cup\Sigma^{\prime}
.

(\subseteq)
Consider an instance
I\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{1})
. We need to show that
I\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{2})
, or, equivalently, that
I
is a model of

\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma_{2}]\ =\ \underbrace{\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{2}}\sigma}_{\Phi}\ \wedge\ \neg\underbrace{\exists\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma_{2}}\left((\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma_{2}}<\mathbf{R})\wedge\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma_{2}}(\Sigma_{2})\right)}_{\Psi}.

Note that, for each
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists}
, and for each
h
such that
h(\sigma)\notin\Sigma_{1}
, it holds that
B^{+}(h(\sigma))\not\subseteq\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma_{1})\supseteq I
.
Therefore, for every
h
and
\sigma
such that
B^{+}(h(\sigma))\subseteq I
, it holds that
B^{-}(h(\sigma))\cap I=\emptyset
since
I
is a stable model of
\Sigma_{1}
; thus,
I
is a model of every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\Pi}^{\not\exists}
.
The same can be observed for
\Sigma^{\prime}\supseteq\Sigma
which contains no negation, and thus,
I
is a model of every
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\prime}
. Consequently,
I
is a model of
\Phi
.
Assume now, by contradiction, that
I
is a model of
\Psi
. This would imply that there exists an instance
J\subsetneq I
that is a model of each TGD of the set obtained from
\{\sigma\in\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{1})\mid B^{-}(\sigma)\cap I=\emptyset\}
by eliminating all the negative literals. This in turn implies that
I\not\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{1})
, which is not possible. Hence,
I
is a model of
\neg\Psi
, which in turn implies that
I
is a model of
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma_{2}]
, as needed.

(\supseteq)
This direction is similar. Let
I\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{2})
, and we need to show that
I\in{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{1})
, or, equivalently, that
I
is a model of

\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma_{1}]\ =\ \underbrace{\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{1}}\sigma}_{\Phi^{\prime}}\ \wedge\ \neg\underbrace{\exists\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma_{1}}\left((\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma_{1}}<\mathbf{R})\wedge\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma_{1}}(\Sigma_{1})\right)}_{\Psi^{\prime}}.

Since all the TGDs of
\Sigma_{1}
are obtained by applying some homomorphism to TGDs of
\Sigma_{2}
, we get that
I
is a model of
\Phi^{\prime}
.
Moreover, as above, assuming that
I
is a model of
\Psi^{\prime}
, allows us to conclude that
I\notin{\sf sms}(\Sigma_{2})
, which contradicts our hypothesis. Therefore,
I
is a model of
\neg\Psi^{\prime}
, which in turn implies that

is a model of
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma_{1}]

Proof of Theorem
3.9

Recall that
\Pi_{G}^{D}
is defined as the triple
\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D),\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D},P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\right)
.
By construction,
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
is a
\sigma
-algebra over the sample space
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)
.
It remains to show that the function
P_{\Pi,G}^{D}:\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a probability measure. By definition,

\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)\ =\ \Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\cup\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)\right)=1-P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\right).

Therefore, we immediately get that

P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)\right)\ =\ 1.

Observe now that the members of
F_{\Pi,G}^{D}
are pairwise disjoint, and that
F_{\Pi,G}^{D}
itself is countable. The induced
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
will therefore only contain events of the form
E=\bigcup_{S\in F_{E}}S
with
F_{E}\subseteq F_{\Pi,G}^{D}
that are countable; the complement of any such event
E
is the event
\bigcup_{S\in\left(F_{\Pi,G}^{D}\setminus F_{E}\right)}S
.
Hence, for any countable set
\mathcal{E}
of pairwise disjoint events, we can conclude that

P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}E\right)\ =\ P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\bigcup_{S\in F_{E}}S\right)\ =\ \sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{S\in F_{E}}P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(S\right)\ =\ \sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}}P_{\Pi,G}^{D}\left(E\right),

as needed. Note that, since all the sets in
\mathcal{E}
are pairwise disjoint, also
F_{E}\cap F_{E^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for any distinct
E,E^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{E}
.

Positive Programs

As briefly discussed in the main body of the paper (in Section
3
), we can show that the semantics induced by the simple grounder coincide with the semantics of
Bárány et al. 2017
for positive generative Datalog whenever we focus on positive programs that guarantee that all the possible outcomes are finite. We proceed to formally state and prove this claim. To this end, we first recall the semantics for positive generative Datalog from
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
, and then establish the above claim.

Existing Semantics for Positive Generative Datalog

To distinguish our semantics from the semantics of
Bárány et al. 2017
, we will refer to their semantics as
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics (in reference to the authors of
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
), and their possible outcomes as
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcomes. Below we give an abridged version of the key notions of the
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics for positive GDatalog
[\Delta]
programs.
Similar to the definition of our semantics, also the
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics rely on a translation into a TGD program, which is slightly different than our translation presented in Section
3
. Let us recall their translation.
Consider a (positive) GDatalog
[\Delta]
rule
\rho

R_{1}(\bar{u}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{u}_{n})\ \rightarrow\ R_{0}(\bar{w}),

where
\bar{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{0})})
with
w_{i_{1}}=\delta_{1}\langle\bar{p}_{1}\rangle[\bar{q}_{1}],\ldots,w_{i_{r}}=\delta_{r}\langle\bar{p}_{r}\rangle[\bar{q}_{r}]
, for
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq\mathsf{ar}(R_{0})
, be all the
\Delta
-terms in
\bar{w}
.
1
1

1

Strictly speaking,
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
assumes that there is only one
\Delta
-term per rule head. This is an assumption that can be made without loss of generality as explained in
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
. For the sake of consistency, though, we consider multiple
\Delta
-terms in the head.

If
r=0
(i.e., there are no
\Delta
-terms in
\bar{w}
), then
\widetilde{\rho_{\exists}}
is defined as the singleton set
\{\Sigma_{\rho}\}
, where
\Sigma_{\rho}
is the TGD without existentially quantified variables obtained after converting
\rho
into a first-order sentence in the usual way. In fact,
\widetilde{\rho_{\exists}}
coincides with
\rho_{\exists}
defined in Section
3
.
Otherwise,
\widetilde{\rho_{\exists}}
is defined as the set consisting of the following TGDs:

\displaystyle R_{1}(\bar{u}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{u}_{n})\ \rightarrow\ \exists y_{j}\ \textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}_{j}|}^{\delta_{j}}(\bar{p}_{j},\bar{q}_{j},y_{j})

for each
j\in[r]
, and

\text{\rm Result}_{|\bar{q}_{1}|}^{\delta_{1}}(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{q}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,\text{\rm Result}_{|\bar{q}_{r}|}^{\delta_{r}}(\bar{p}_{r},\bar{q}_{r},y_{r}),R_{1}(\bar{u}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\bar{u}_{n})\ \rightarrow\ R_{0}(\bar{w}^{\prime}),

where
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}_{j}|}^{\delta_{j}}
are fresh
(|\bar{p}_{j}|+|\bar{q}_{j}|+1)
-ary predicates, not occurring in
\mathsf{sch}(\Pi)
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}
are distinct variables not occurring in
\rho

\bar{w}^{\prime}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{i_{1}-1},y_{1},w_{i_{1}+1},\ldots,w_{i_{r}-1},y_{r},w_{i_{r}+1},\ldots,w_{\mathsf{ar}(R_{0})})
is obtained from
\bar{w}
by replacing
\Delta
-terms with variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}
.
Finally, for a (positive) GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, we define
\widetilde{\Sigma_{\Pi}}
as the TGD program
\bigcup_{\rho\in\Pi}\widetilde{\rho_{\exists}}
.
Note that the key difference of the above translation compared to our translation given in Section
3
is that the intermediate active-to-result TGDs are missing.
We can now recall the notion of possible outcome as defined in
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
:

Definition C.1 (
BCKOV Possible Outcome
).

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. A
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcome
of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
is a minimal model
I
of
\widetilde{\Sigma_{\Pi}}
such that
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
for every
\Delta
-atom
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in I
. We denote the set of all
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcomes of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
as
\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D)
.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. With
F
being a set of atoms, we write
\Omega_{\Pi}^{F\subseteq}(D)
for the set of possible outcomes

\{J\in\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D)\mid F\subseteq J\}
, that is, the set that collects all those possible outcomes that contain all the facts of
F
.
Let
\Omega_{\Pi}^{\subseteq_{\mathsf{fin}}}(D)
be the set of sets
\Omega_{\Pi}^{F\subseteq}(D)
where
F
is finite.
A finite sequence of atoms
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
is a derivation if, for all
i\in[n]
,
f_{i}=\mathsf{head}(h(\sigma))
for some TGD
\sigma\in\widetilde{\Sigma_{\Pi}}
and homomorphism
h
from
B(\sigma)
to
J_{i-1}
, where
J_{k}=D\cup\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{k}\}
.
For a derivation
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
, the set
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
is called a derivation set. Intuitively, a finite derivation set is an intermediate instance of some chase tree. The probability of a set of atoms
F
is defined in essentially the same way as our definition of
\mathit{Pr}(\Sigma)
. Formally, for an atom
f
of the form
\mathrm{Result}_{n}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)
, let
\mathit{Pr}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(f)=\delta\left<\bar{p}\right>(o)
. If
f
is of any other form, then
\mathit{Pr}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(f)=1
. For a set of atoms
F
, let
P^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(F)=\prod_{f\in F}\mathit{Pr}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(f)
.
The following holds:

Theorem C.2 (
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
).

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. There exists a unique probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
that satisfies all of the following:

(1)

\Omega\ =\ \Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D)
,

(2)

\mathcal{F}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\Omega_{\Pi}^{\subseteq_{\mathsf{fin}}}(D)
, and

(3)

P(\Omega_{\Pi}^{F\subseteq})\ =\ P^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(F)
for every derivation set
F
.

For a (positive) GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
, we denote by

\Pi^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)\ =\ \left(\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D),\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi,D},P^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi,D}\right)

the unique probability space provided by Theorem
C.2
, which forms the
output of
\Pi
on
D
according to the
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics of
(
Bárány et al. 2017
)
.

BCKOV Semantics vs. Our Semantics

We move on to show that for positive programs (i.e., programs with no negation) where all possible outcomes are finite, our semantics induced by the simple grounder coincide with the
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics, that is, the resulting probability spaces are isomorphic.

Definition C.3 (
Finite Grounding
).

Consider a GDatalog
{}^{\neg}[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
, and a grounder
G
of
\Pi[D]
. We say that
G
is
finitely grounding
(for
\Pi[D]
) if every possible outcome of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
relative to
G
is finite.

We further need the notion of isomorphism between probability spaces.
An
isomorphism
between two probability spaces
PS_{1}=(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1},P_{1})
and
PS_{2}=(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2},P_{2})
is a bijection
f\colon\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
such that (i) for every
F\in 2^{\Omega_{1}}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
iff
f(F)\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
, and (ii) for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
,
P_{1}(F)=P_{2}(f(F))
.
We say that
PS_{1}
and
PS_{2}
are
isomorphic
, denoted
PS_{1}\simeq PS_{2}
, if there exists an isomorphism between them.
We are now ready to state the desired result:

Theorem C.4.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
such that
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
is finitely grounding. Then

\Pi_{\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)\ \simeq\ \Pi^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D).

The rest of the section is devoted to proving the above theorem. To this end, we first need to establish some auxiliary technical lemmas.
Recall that our translation of a GDatalog
[\Delta]
progrma into a TGD program is the same as the translation used in the definition of the
\mathrm{BCKOV}
semantics, except for the addition of the intermediate “active” predicates, and the respective active-to-result TGDs that link the “active” with the “result” predicates. It will therefore be useful to talk about stable models of a TGD program
\Sigma
without the “active” predicates. In particular, for a stable model
I
, we refer to the subset of
I
after eliminating all the atoms of the form
\textrm{Active}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q})
as
I

modulo active
.

Lemma C.5.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. The following hold:

(1)

For every possible outcome
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
,
\Sigma
has exactly one stable model.

(2)

For every distinct
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
, the stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active and the stable model of
\Sigma^{\prime}
modulo active are different.

Proof.

Item (1).
Since
\Pi
is positive, so is the TGD program
\Sigma_{\Pi}
. Therefore, every possible outcome
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
is positive and without existential quantification. It is well-known that such programs have exactly one stable model; thus,
{\sf sms}(\Sigma)
is a singleton.

Item (2).
Let
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
be two distinct possible outcomes of
\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
. This means that
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
belong to
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
-
\mathsf{ground}(\Pi[D])
, and therefore,
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are generated by distinct terminals
T
,
T^{\prime}
, i.e., different subsets of
[2^{\mathsf{ground}(\Sigma_{\Pi[D]}^{\exists})}]_{=}
that are incomparable by
\subseteq
.
Since
T
and
T^{\prime}
are minimal w.r.t.
\mathsf{terminals}(\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]})
, all body atoms in
T
and
T^{\prime}
occur in the heads of
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}(T)
and
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}(T^{\prime})
, respectively. Hence, by definition of
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
, the single stable model of
\Sigma
contains exactly
\mathsf{heads}(T)
as the only atoms with a predicate of the form
\textrm{Result}^{\delta}_{n}
(the “result” atoms), while the stable model of
\Sigma^{\prime}
contains exactly
\mathsf{heads}(T^{\prime})
as its only “result” atoms. Since
T
and
T^{\prime}
are incomparable by
\subseteq
, so are
\mathsf{heads}(T)
and
\mathsf{heads}(T^{\prime})
. Hence, the stable models of
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
modulo active are different, as needed.

Lemma C.6.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. For every
\Sigma\in\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}\text{-}\mathsf{ground}(\Pi[D])
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
.

(2)

The stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active belongs to
\Omega_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. By definition,
\Sigma=\Sigma^{\prime}\cup\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for some minimal
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{terminals}(\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]})
. We define the instance

I\ =\ D\cup\left\{\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\mid\textrm{Active}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q})\rightarrow\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\Sigma^{\prime}\right\},

i.e.,
I=D\cup\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\prime})
. It is easy to see that
I\in\Omega_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
. Moreover,
I
is the stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active, and the claim follows.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. We need to show that
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
for every
\Delta
-atom
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
.
Observe that every such
\Delta
-atom occurs in the stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active. Thus, by hypothesis, every
\Delta
-atom
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
belongs to a
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcome. This allows us to conclude that
\delta\langle\bar{p}\rangle(o)>0
for every
\Delta
-atom
\textrm{Result}_{|\bar{q}|}^{\delta}(\bar{p},\bar{q},o)\in\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma)
, and the claim follows.

Lemma C.7.

Consider a GDatalog
[\Delta]
program
\Pi
, and a database
D
of
\mathsf{edb}(\Pi)
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
is finitely grounding.

(2)

Every instance
I\in\Omega_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
is finite.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Fix an arbitrary
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcome
I\in\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}
. By Lemma
C.6
, there exists a possible outcome
\Sigma_{I}
of
\Pi[D]
relative to
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
such that the single stable model of
\Sigma_{I}
modulo active is precisely the instance
I
. Since, by hypothesis,
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
is finitely grounding, we get that
\Sigma
is finite. This in turn implies that
I
is also finite, and the claim follows.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Fix an arbitrary possible outcome
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi,\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}}(D)
. We need to show that
\Sigma
is finite. By Lemma
C.6
, the stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active is a
\mathrm{BCKOV}
possible outcome, and thus, by hypothesis, is finite. Since there exists at most one “active” atom that corresponds to a “result” atom in the stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active, we can conclude that the stable model of
\Sigma
is finite.
By definition (for positive programs), the simple grounder derives only ground rules whose head needs to be satisfied in the stable model of
\Sigma
. Thus, since the stable model of
\Sigma
is finite, also
\Sigma
itself is finite, and the claim follows.

Having the above auxiliary lemmas in place, we can now complete the proof of Theorem
C.4
stated above.

Proof (of Theorem
C.4
).
For brevity, throughout the proof, we simply write
G
instead of
\mathit{GSimple}_{\Pi[D]}
. We need to show that there exists an isomorphism between the probability spaces
\Pi_{G}(D)
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
.
First, observe that since
G
is finitely grounding,
\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\infty}(D)=\emptyset
, and thus,
\Omega_{\Pi,G}(D)=\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)
.
We claim that the function
f:\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)\rightarrow\Omega_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
such that, for every
\Sigma\in\Omega^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Pi,G}(D)
,
f(\Sigma)
is the unique stable model of
\Sigma
modulo active, is the required isomorphism. We need to show the following statements:

(1)

f
is a bijection,

(2)

for every
F\in 2^{\Omega_{\Pi,G}^{\mathsf{fin}}(D)}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
iff
f(F)\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi,D}
, and

(3)

for every
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D}
,
P_{\Pi,G}^{D}(F)=P_{\Pi,D}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(f(F))
.

Item (1).
For showing that
f
is a bijection, we need to show that (i) for every
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Pi,G}(D)
,
\Sigma\neq\Sigma^{\prime}
implies
f(\Sigma)\neq f(\Sigma^{\prime})
, and (ii) for every
\Sigma\in\Omega_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}(D)
, there exists
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Pi,G}(D)
such that
\Sigma=f(\Sigma^{\prime})
. The former is an immediate consequence of Lemma
C.5
, while the latter follows from Lemma
C.6
.

Item (2).
It suffices to show that
f(\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D})=\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,D}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}^{D}_{\Pi,G}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
F^{D}_{\Pi,G}
consisting of
\Omega^{\infty}_{\Pi,G}(D)
, and all the maximal sets
E\subseteq\Omega^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Pi,G}(D)
such that
{\sf sms}(\Sigma)={\sf sms}(\Sigma^{\prime})
for all
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}\in E
. Note that, by Lemma
C.5
,
F^{D}_{\Pi,G}
consists only of singleton sets.
We proceed to show that
f(\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D})=\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,D}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}
.

(\supseteq)
By Lemma
C.7
, every instance
I\in\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D)
is finite, and, by Lemma
C.6
, there exists
\Sigma\in\Omega^{\mathsf{fin}}_{\Pi,G}(D)
such that
f(\Sigma)=I
.
Since all possible outcomes of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
are finite, and we assume countable sample spaces, there are only countably many possible outcomes of
D
w.r.t.
\Pi
. Therefore, all sets in
\Omega_{\Pi}^{\subseteq_{\mathsf{fin}}}
are also countable. Hence, any set of
\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,D}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}
is a countable union of sets of
f(\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D})
, and thus,
f(\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,G}^{D})\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{\Pi,D}^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}
.

(\subseteq)
Similarly, since all
I\in\Omega^{\mathrm{BCKOV}}_{\Pi}(D)
are finite,
\{I\}=\Omega_{\Pi}^{I\subseteq}
, and thus also

Proof of Proposition

Before giving the proof of Proposition

Proof.

For brevity, let

is a stratified ground program according to
(
Gelfond and
Lifschitz 1988
)

From Corollary 1 in
(
Gelfond and
Lifschitz 1988

\Sigma^{\star}
has a unique stable model.
We shall show that such a stable model is
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})
, i.e.,
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})
is a model of
\mathsf{SM}[\Sigma^{\star}]=\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\star}}\sigma\ \wedge\ \neg\exists\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma^{\star}}\left((\mathbf{X}_{\Sigma^{\star}}<\mathbf{R})\wedge\mathsf{repl}_{\Sigma^{\star}}(\Sigma^{\star})\right)

Actually,
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})\models\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{\star}}\sigma
is trivial, so it remains to show that there is no smaller model for the program obtained from
\Sigma^{\star}
by fixing the interpretation of negative literals according to
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})
(such a program is called
reduct
in the literature).

By Definition
3.3
,
\Sigma\in\mathsf{terminals}(\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi})
implies
\mathit{AtR}_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi}(\Sigma)

Moreover, by construction of

(Definition

\mathit{Perfect}_{\Sigma}

\Sigma^{\star}

occur in some head of previously derived TGD
¬
.
Moreover, by assumption, (iii) there is no
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{terminals}(\mathit{GPerfect}_{\Pi})
with
\Sigma^{\prime}\subsetneq\Sigma
.
From (i) we have that the program reduct is equivalent to the program obtained from
\Sigma^{\star}
by removing all negative literals (because such negative literals are true w.r.t.
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})
).
From (ii) and (iii), we have that
\mathsf{heads}(\Sigma^{\star})
is the subset-minimal model of the program reduct. This completes our proof.

To prove that
