Geometric ergodicity of Gibbs samplers for Bayesian error-in-variable regression
Austin Brown

Abstract
Multivariate Bayesian error-in-variable (EIV) linear regression is considered to account for additional additive Gaussian error in the features and response. A 3-variable deterministic scan Gibbs samplers is constructed for multivariate EIV regression models using classical and Berkson errors with independent normal and inverse-Wishart priors. These Gibbs samplers are proven to always be geometrically ergodic which ensures a central limit theorem for many time averages from the Markov chains. We demonstrate the strengths and limitations of the Gibbs sampler with simulated data for large data problems, robustness to misspecification and also analyze a real-data example in astrophysics.

Bayesian approaches develop a strategy for additional error in the variables by constructing a new model incorporating additional error.

We assume the variability of the additive Gaussian error is known beforehand which arises often in astrophysics in the presence of known instrumentation error
(
Hilbe, de Souza and Ishida 2017
;
Kelly 2012
)
.
Alternative approaches to EIV models attempt to correct existing parameter estimation methods such as least squares or method of moments with weighting and other techniques
(

;

Several other strategies for EIV modeling are discussed in more comprehensive treatments on the topic

We write

We also write

Let

\mathbb{R}^{m}

Let

We introduce new parameters
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n})^{T}

using classical or Berkson errors

)

The classical error model specifies

assumes instead a data-dependent prior on
\mathcal{A}_{i}|X_{i}
.
When there is additional error in
X_{i}
, the EIV linear regression model for
i\in 1,\ldots,n
is i.i.d. with

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i},\theta,\beta,\Sigma\sim N_{m}(\Theta^{T}Z_{i}+\mathcal{B}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{i},\Sigma)

(1a)

\displaystyle X_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i}\sim N_{p}(\mathcal{A}_{i},V_{i})\text{ (Classical)}\text{\hskip 11.38092pt or \hskip 11.38092pt}\mathcal{A}_{i}|X_{i}\sim N_{p}(X_{i},V_{i})\text{ (Berkson)}

(1b)

where the SPD matrices
V_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
are known.
When there is also additional error in the responses
Y_{i}
, we assume an i.i.d. hierarchical regression model with

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\mathcal{V}_{i}\sim N_{m}(\mathcal{V}_{i},U_{i})

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i},\theta,\beta,\Sigma\sim N_{m}(\Theta^{T}Z_{i}+\mathcal{B}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{i},\Sigma)

(2b)

\displaystyle X_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i}\sim N_{p}(\mathcal{A}_{i},V_{i})\text{ (Classical)}\text{\hskip 11.38092pt or \hskip 11.38092pt}\mathcal{A}_{i}|X_{i}\sim N_{p}(X_{i},V_{i})\text{ (Berkson)}

(2c)

where
U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
are known SPD matrices.

We will be interested in the posterior for both models (
1
) and (
2
) using independent normal and inverse-Wishart priors on the parameters
(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma)
.
The independent prior choice is a popular choice in Bayesian regression models with and without measurement error
(

;
Dellaportas and
Stephens 1995
;
Ekvall and Jones 2021
;

For the EIV regression models (
1

\displaystyle\Sigma\sim\mathcal{W}^{-1}(a_{0},B_{0})

(3a)

\displaystyle(\theta,\beta)^{T}\sim N_{m(q+p)}(j_{0},J_{0})

(3b)

where
a_{0}\geq m
is a positive integer,
B_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a SPD matrix,
j_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m(q+p)}
and SPD matrix

The classical and Berkson error models assume either

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}\sim N_{p}(k_{i},K_{i})\text{ (Classical)}\text{\hskip 11.38092pt or \hskip 11.38092pt}\mathcal{A}_{i}\text{ flat prior}\text{ (Berkson)}

(4)

where

For example, an
exposure model

\mathcal{A}_{i}
depending on
Z_{i}
.
In the Berkson error model, each
\mathcal{A}_{i}|X_{i}
is already specified and it is natural to assume an improper flat prior on each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Previous work has proposed Gibbs sampling
(
Geman and Geman 1984
)
to draw samples from the posterior, denoted by
\Pi_{n}
, in Bayesian EIV regression models
(
Bhadra and Carroll 2016
;
Carroll et al. 2006
;
Dellaportas and
Stephens 1995
;
Richardson S 1993
)
.
However, trustworthy estimation from a Gibbs sampler requires the Markov chain to converge to the posterior distribution at a sufficiently fast rate.
Consider a vector-valued function
f
with
\int\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2+\delta}d\Pi_{n}<\infty
for some
\delta\in(0,\infty)
and denote
\bar{f}_{m}
as the time average of
m
samples from the Gibbs sampler.
In order to be confident in the estimator
\bar{f}_{m}
in applications, a standard error and confidence interval are essential.
A Gibbs sampler is geometrically ergodic if initialized at points, its marginal distribution is converging to
\Pi_{n}
at an exponential rate in total variation.
Geometrically ergodic Gibbs samplers provide rich theoretical guarantees which are of practical relevance in applications.
These Gibbs samplers satisfy a central limit theorem
(
Chan and Geyer 1994
;
Jones 2004
)
, that is,

\sqrt{m}\left(\bar{f}_{m}-\int fd\Pi_{n}\right)

is asymptotically normally distributed and under suitable assumptions, the covariance in this normal distribution can be consistently estimated
(
Flegal and Jones 2010
)
.
Further pertinent tools to ensuring reliable estimation such as estimates of the effective sample size, consistent confidence ellipsoids, and consistent confidence intervals for quantile estimation are also available
(
Doss et al. 2014
;
Vats, Flegal and Jones 2019a
)
.

To the best of our knowledge, the rate of convergence for Gibbs sampling in EIV regression models has not been previously investigated.
Related approaches have instead proposed variational Bayesian methods
(
Bresson et al. 2021
;
Pham, Ormerod and Wand 2013
)
and the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA)
(
Håvard Rue 2009
;
Muff et al. 2015
)
.

) and (
2
) using the independent normal and inverse-Wishart prior choice on the parameters (
3
) and (
4
).
Our main contribution constructs a 3-variable deterministic scan Gibbs sampler for this general density, and we show it is

geometrically ergodic using a drift and minorization condition
(
Hairer and Mattingly 2011
;
Meyn and Tweedie 2009
)
.
The 3-variable Gibbs sampler we construct can be simulated efficiently on a computer without the need for complex Metropolis-Hastings or rejection sampling steps at each iteration.

In Section

We show the Gibbs sampler is always geometrically ergodic and apply this to the

Section

Section

Hilbe, de Souza and Ishida 2017
)

Finally in Section

The posterior densities for these Bayesian EIV regression models are special cases of the density (

For

, define hyper-parameters

B_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

For

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma)

(5a)

\displaystyle\propto\left(\frac{1}{\det(\Sigma)}\right)^{(n+a_{0}+1+m)/2}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}B_{0})\right]

(5b)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}-\Theta^{T}M_{i}-\mathcal{B}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{i})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(R_{i}-\Theta^{T}M_{i}-\mathcal{B}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{i})\right]

(5c)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})^{T}D_{i}^{-1}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})\right)

(5d)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}((\theta,\beta)^{T}-c_{0})^{T}C_{0}^{-1}((\theta,\beta)^{T}-c_{0})\right).

(5e)

We will construct a 3-variable deterministic scan Gibbs sampler using a specific update order for the density (
5
).
We also derive the conditional densities for the Gibbs sampler which can be sampled directly.
Initialize
(\theta_{0},\beta_{0},\Sigma_{0})
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}=(\mathcal{A}_{1,0},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{n,0})
from an initial distribution.
For
t\in 1,\ldots

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}|\mathcal{A}_{t-1},\theta_{t-1},\beta_{t-1}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{W}^{-1}\left(n+a_{0},(R-M\Theta_{t-1}-\mathcal{A}_{t-1}\mathcal{B}_{t-1})^{T}(R-M\Theta_{t-1}-\mathcal{A}_{t-1}\mathcal{B}_{t-1})+B_{0}\right)

Finally, generate independently

\displaystyle D_{n,i}(\beta_{t},\Sigma_{t})=\left(\mathcal{B}_{t}\Sigma^{-1}_{t}\mathcal{B}_{t}^{T}+D_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle d_{n,i}(\theta_{t},\beta_{t},\Sigma_{t})=D_{n,i}(\beta_{t},\Sigma_{t})\left[D_{i}^{-1}d_{i}+\mathcal{B}_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\left(R_{i}-\Theta_{t}^{T}M_{i}\right)\right]

.

For points

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})
, the Gibbs sampler has Markov transition density

\displaystyle p\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}(\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}({\Sigma}^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)

and Markov transition kernel defined for suitable sets

\displaystyle P\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),B\right)=\int\int\int_{B}p\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\right)d{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}d{\theta^{\prime}}d{\beta}^{\prime}d{\Sigma}^{\prime}.

The Markov kernel at larger iteration times

is defined recursively with

P^{t}\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),B\right)=\int P^{t-1}\left(\cdot,B\right)dP\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),\cdot\right).

\displaystyle\sup_{|\varphi|\leq 1+M_{0}V}\left|\int\varphi dP^{t}\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),\cdot\right)-\int\varphi d\Pi_{n}\right|\leq M(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)\rho^{t}

(6)

where

(

Theorem 1
.

The 3-variable deterministic scan Gibbs sampler
(\mathcal{A}_{t},\theta_{t},\beta_{t},\Sigma_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}

Proof.

Using a special property of the Gibbs sampler, it will be sufficient to develop a drift and minorization condition based only on the marginal chain
(\mathcal{A}_{t},\theta_{t},\beta_{t})_{t}

(
Roberts and
Rosenthal 2001
, Example 3.6)
.
In particular, we will use the special property of this Gibbs Markov kernel
P
that for suitable sets
B
,
P(\cdot,B)
is a function of only the parameters
(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)
and does not depend on

.
We first show a minorization condition.
For
\ell\in(0,\infty)
, define the function
g_{r}
by

g_{r}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})=\inf_{V(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)\leq\ell}\pi_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}(\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}({\Sigma}^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)

and the constant

The drift function

is a continuous, strongly convex function on a closed, convex domain so its sublevel sets are closed and bounded

Nesterov 2018
, Corollary 3.2.2)
.
For fixed

, the function

(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)\mapsto\pi_{n}(\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}({\Sigma}^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)

Thus,

is not

For any
\ell\in(0,\infty)
and any suitable set

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\\
V(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)\leq\ell\end{subarray}}P\left((\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta,\Sigma),B\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{V(\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)\leq\ell}\int_{B}\pi_{n}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}(\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},{\Sigma}^{\prime})\pi_{n}({\Sigma}^{\prime}|\mathcal{A},\theta,\beta)d\mathcal{A}^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}d\beta^{\prime}d{\Sigma}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B}g_{\ell}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},{\Sigma}^{\prime})d\mathcal{A}^{\prime}d\theta^{\prime}d\beta^{\prime}d{\Sigma}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=Z_{g_{\ell}}\nu_{\ell}(B).

Since

is SPD, let

Using the identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert D_{i}^{-1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0},\theta,\beta,\Sigma\right]

For

\displaystyle\frac{x}{x^{2}+a}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{a}}.

\displaystyle\left\lVert D_{i}^{-1/2}\left(\mathcal{B}\Sigma^{-1}\mathcal{B}^{T}+D_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{B}\Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert U_{i}(\Sigma_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{T}+I_{p})^{-1}U_{i}^{T}D_{i}^{1/2}\mathcal{B}\Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

Applying these upper bounds to (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert D_{i}^{-1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0},\theta,\beta,\Sigma\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}}{8}\left\lVert R-M\Theta-\tilde{X}\mathcal{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{pn}{2}.

By convexity, for every

\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Since

is SPD, let
C_{0}=C_{0}^{1/2}C_{0}^{1/2}
,
C_{0}^{-1}=C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{0}^{-1/2}
where
C_{0}^{1/2},C_{0}^{-1/2}
are SPD.
Using convexity, and the matrix norm is sub-multiplicative, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert R-M\Theta-\tilde{X}\mathcal{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}M&\tilde{X}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}(\theta,\beta)^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert D_{i}^{-1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0},\theta,\beta,\Sigma\right]

(10a)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}}{8}\left\lVert R-M\Theta-\tilde{X}\mathcal{B}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{pn}{2}

(10b)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}\frac{\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{4}+\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}\frac{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}M&\tilde{X}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{4}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}(\theta,\beta)^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{pn}{2}.

(10c)

Now taking the expectation with respect to
\theta,\beta|\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0},\Sigma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}(\theta,\beta)^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\Sigma,\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}c_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)C_{0}^{-1/2}).

Using singular value decomposition
(
Horn and Johnson 2012
, Theorem 2.6.3)
, choose matrices

,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{m(p+q)\times m(p+q)}
with
U^{T}U=UU^{T}=I_{mn}
and
V^{T}V=VV^{T}=I_{m(p+q)}
and a rectangular diagonal matrix
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times m(p+q)}
with diagonal nonnegative singular values

so that
\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}=U\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}V^{T}
.
We then have

\displaystyle C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(\Sigma^{-1}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+C_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=C_{0}^{1/2}V\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}V^{T}C_{0}^{1/2}.

(11)

Using (
11
) and properties of the trace

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)C_{0}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\max_{k}\left[\left(\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0},k}^{2}+1\right)^{-1}\right]\text{tr}(V^{T}V)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m(p+q)}{2}.

Using convexity,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}c_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)\left[\left[\Sigma^{-1}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right]\text{vec}(R)+C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right]\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes I_{mn}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Using the inequality (
9
) and the identity (

),

\displaystyle\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right]^{T}\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes I_{mn}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert V\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}V^{T}\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}M&\mathcal{A}_{0}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right]^{T}\left[\Sigma^{-1/2}\otimes I_{mn}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert V\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}U^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert U^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0},k}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0},k}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}}{4}.

Using (

\displaystyle\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}C_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Sigma)C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert V\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}V^{T}C_{0}^{-1/2}c_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert\left(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}^{T}\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}+I_{m(p+q)}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert V^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}c_{0}^{T}C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}^{T}C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}.

Combining the upper bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert C_{0}^{-1/2}(\theta,\beta)^{T}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\Sigma,\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0}\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert D_{i}^{-1/2}(\mathcal{A}_{i}-d_{i})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\big|\Sigma,\mathcal{A}_{0},\theta_{0},\beta_{0}\right]

(13a)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}

(13b)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt\times\left[\frac{\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}M&\tilde{X}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}m(p+q)}{4}+\frac{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}M&\tilde{X}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2}c_{0}^{T}C_{0}^{-1}c_{0}\right]

(13c)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+\left\lVert\Sigma^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\frac{\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}M&\tilde{X}\end{pmatrix}C_{0}^{1/2}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}{8}+\frac{pn}{2}.

(13d)

\displaystyle=(n+a_{0})\text{tr}\left[\left((R-M\Theta_{0}-\mathcal{A}_{0}\mathcal{B}_{0})^{T}(R-M\Theta_{0}-\mathcal{A}_{0}\mathcal{B}_{0})+B_{0}\right)^{-1}\right]

3.1
Limitations of the Gibbs sampler in large problem sizes

Theoretically, we developed a
qualitative
convergence result for the Gibbs sampler in Bayesian multivariate EIV regression.
It is important in practice to understand the relationship between scaling of the problem size and the estimation reliability from the Gibbs sampler.
We look at artificially generated data to empirically demonstrate the dependence of the Gibbs sampler when the the dimension of the response
m
and the dimension of the features
p
are increasing in configurations
(m,p)=(1,1),(2,7),(3,7)
in the Bayesian posterior.
Artificial data is generated according to the multivariate EIV Berkson linear regression model for
i=1,\ldots,50
with

\displaystyle\Sigma\sim\mathcal{W}^{-1}(m,10^{-3}I)

\displaystyle(\theta,\beta)^{T}\sim N_{m+mp}(0,10^{3}I)

\displaystyle X_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i}\sim N_{p}(\mathcal{A}_{i},.2I)

\displaystyle Y_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i},\Theta,\mathcal{B},\Sigma\sim N_{m}(\Theta^{T}1+\mathcal{B}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{i},\Sigma)

We simulate
T=10^{5}
MCMC realizations from the Gibbs sampler in each configuration using
10^{4}
realizations for burn-in and analyze diagnostics for
\beta_{t}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{mp}
.
We independently replicate the simulation
5
times to reduce variability.
Geometric ergodicity guarantees the properly scaled and summed samples from the Gibbs sampler

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\beta_{t}-\int\beta\Pi_{n}(d\beta)\right]\to N(0,\Sigma_{*})

as
T\to\infty
in distribution where
\Sigma_{*}
is a SPD covariance matrix.
The central limit theorem can be seen to hold in this case due to the established drift condition with the drift function.
Figure
1(a)
and Figure
1(b)
plot the largest and smallest eigenvalues of a batch means estimate to the multivariate standard error matrix
{\Sigma_{*}}^{1/2}
in the central limit theorem.
This simulation shows an increase in the largest eigenvalue in larger size problems which can lead to suggesting more iterations are needed for appropriate estimation in practice.
Figure
1(c)
plots the multivariate effective sample size
(
Vats, Flegal and
Jones 2019b
)
.
We can also see a relatively sharp decrease in the estimation of the effective sample size as the problem size increases suggesting the algorithm should be run for many iterations even in moderately sized problems.
This simulation demonstrates that even though the algorithm is
always
geometrically ergodic and can scale reasonably well to larger problem sizes, the Gibbs sampler generally requires many more iterations for reliable estimation even in moderately sized problems.

5
Conclusion and Future Directions

We showed using a
3
-variable deterministic scan Gibbs sampler to sample the posterior in
4
different multivariate Bayesian EIV regression models with additive Gaussian errors and independent priors is always geometrically ergodic.
This is of pragmatic importance to practitioners as trustworthy estimation from a Gibbs sampler is dependent on the speed of convergence of the Markov chain.
More specifically, time averages from the Markov chains have many practically relevant theoretical guarantees such as a central limit theorem.
Secondly, these Gibbs samplers can be simulated efficiently without the need for complex, intermediate Metropolis-Hastings or rejection sampling steps.
One drawback, however, is our convergence analysis is qualitative as we do not construct an explicit convergence rate.

There are many future research directions in studying the convergence of Gibbs samplers in EIV models.
It appears reasonable that some Gibbs samplers for generalized linear models such as the Pólya-Gamma sampler will also be geometrically ergodic
(
Choi and Hobert 2013
;
Polson, Scott and Windle 2013
;
Wang and Roy 2018
)
.
It seems also interesting to look at alternative errors in the variables such as non-Gaussian or non-additive errors.
