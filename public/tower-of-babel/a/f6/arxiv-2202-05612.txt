High-dimensional Inference and FDR Control for Simulated Markov Random Fields
Haoyu Wei, Xiaoyu Lei, Yixin Han, Huiming Zhang

Abstract
Identifying important features linked to a response variable is a fundamental task in various scientific domains. This article explores statistical inference for simulated Markov random fields in high-dimensional settings. We introduce a methodology based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MCMC-MLE) with Elastic-net regularization. Under mild conditions on the MCMC method, our penalized MCMC-MLE method achieves ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -consistency. We propose a decorrelated score test, establishing both its asymptotic normality and that of a one-step estimator, along with the associated confidence interval. Furthermore, we construct two false discovery rate control procedures via the asymptotic behaviors for both p-values and e-values. Comprehensive numerical simulations confirm the theoretical validity of the proposed methods.

Preliminaries

2.1
Graphical Models within Exponential Families

Define graph model
G=(V,E)
, where
V=\{1,2,\cdots,d\}
is a set of vertices and
E\subset V\times V
is a set of edges. For each vertex
s\in V
, assign a random variable
X_{s}

To establish a probability distribution associated with the graph. In our study, we focus on the finite discrete state space
\mathcal{X}_{s}=\{1,2,\cdots,r\}
. We denote the Cartesian product of the state spaces
\{\mathcal{X}_{s}:s\in V\}
—where the random vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{s},s\in V)
takes values—by
\prod_{s\in V}\mathcal{X}{s}
. The notation
x{s}
represents a specific element in
\mathcal{X}_{s}
, and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{\top}
signifies a specific value within the space
\prod_{s\in V}\mathcal{X}_{s}
. For any subset
A
of the vertex set
V
, we define
\mathbf{X}_{A}:=(X_{s},s\in A)
as the sub-vector of the random vector and
\mathbf{x}_{A}:=(x_{s},s\in A)
as a specific element of the random sub-vector.

The undirected graphical models are factorized according to a function defined over the graph’s cliques. A clique
C
is identified as a fully connected subset of the vertex set, satisfying
(s,t)\in E
for every
s,t\in C
. For each clique
C
, we introduce a compatibility function
\psi_{C}:\prod_{s\in C}\mathcal{X}_{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Let
\mathcal{C}
represent a set of the graph’s cliques. The undirected graphical model also referred to as the Markov random field (MRF,
[
44
]
), is expressed as

p(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\psi_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C}),\quad\text{where}\quad Z:=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{s\in V}\mathcal{X}_{s}}\prod_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\psi_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C}),

with
Z
is the constant chosen to normalize the distribution. Concentrating on the canonical exponential family. the probability mass function for
(\mathcal{X}_{s},s\in V)
is given by

p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{C(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{x})},~~\text{ where }~~C(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{s\in V}\mathcal{X}_{s}}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{x})},

and
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{p})^{\top}
is the unknown parameters of interest. Denote
{\mathcal{X}}=\prod_{s\in V}\mathcal{X}_{s}
. Let
\nabla\log C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\nabla^{2}\log C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be the expectation and covariance of the random vector
\varphi(\mathbf{X})
, respectively.
Specifically, by taking
\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\varphi_{(j,k)}(\mathbf{x})=x_{j}x_{k}
where
x_{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
, the model evolves into the Ising model in
[
49
]
. Further, constraining
\mathcal{X}_{s}=\{0,1\}
for any
s\in V
transforms the model into the discrete MRFs, also discussed in
[
31
]
. Consequently, our model is considerably more general than those in the existing literature.

2.2
Penalized Markov Chain Monte Carlo Likelihood

Suppose
\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are independent and identically distributed from distribution
p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
is the true parameter. We construct the negative log-likelihood function as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(p(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}))=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\log(C(\boldsymbol{\theta})).

(1)

We then employ the MCMC method for approximation of the normalized constant
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Consider
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{m}
as a Markov chain with its stationary distribution on the product space
\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}
being
h(\mathbf{Y})
. By the ergodic property of the Markov chain,
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be approximated through a path integral

C(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\operatorname{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim h(\mathbf{Y})}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{Y})}}{h(\mathbf{Y})}\approx\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}}{h(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}.

(2)

Combining (
1
) and (
2
), the MCMC log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}
is

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\log\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}}{h(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}\right).

Denote

w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}}{h(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}
and
\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.
Then the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}
and the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}
can be re-expressed as

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(3)

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})^{\otimes 2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}-\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right)^{\otimes 2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})(\varphi(\mathbf{Y}_{i})-\overline{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{\otimes 2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

(4)

It is clear that
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is convex. Denote
H^{*}:=\operatorname{cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\varphi(\mathbf{X}))
as the covariance matrix of the function
\varphi(\mathbf{X})
under the true value
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
.

In high-dimensional cases where the number of parameters significantly exceeds the number of samples (
p\gg n
), the minimum of
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is not unique, resulting in an ill-posed minimizer of MCMC log-likelihood. To address this issue, we consider deploying a penalty to the likelihood function in this article. We employ the Elastic-net penalty, which combines the benefits of both the Lasso method and ridge regression. The Elastic-net-penalized MCMC-MLE we propose is formulated as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}^{m}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\lambda_{1}\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(5)

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
are two tuning parameters. The terms
\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|_{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\theta_{i}|
and
\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|_{2}:=(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
represent the
\ell_{1}
-norm and
\ell_{2}
-norm of the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, respectively.

Under certain regularity conditions, we establish the non-asymptotic oracle inequality for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in Theorem
B.1
in Appendix and it leads to convergence rate for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
that can be served for deriving decorrelated score test and constructing confidence interval for the component of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
; see Theorem
3.2
.

3
Decorrelated Score Test and Confidence Interval

In high-dimensional inference, testing a specific element
\alpha^{*}
within the true vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
presents some challenges. Unlike low-dimensional scenarios, the presence of a high-dimensional nuisance parameter set
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\setminus\{\alpha^{*}\}
complicates the development of valid inferential methods. Traditional approaches like partial-likelihood-based inference
[
51
,
28
,
57
]
, become impractical in this context due to the intractability of the limiting distribution and the complexity introduced by numerous nuisance parameters.
Motivated from
[
28
]
, we propose a decorrelated score test to address the hypothesis testing of
H_{0}:\alpha=\alpha_{0}
versus
H_{1}:\alpha\neq\alpha_{0}
. This approach is analogous to the traditional score test but modified to suit high-dimensional contexts. In such settings, a direct extension of the profile partial score test becomes infeasible due to the intractable limiting distribution caused by the large number of nuisance parameters. We address this challenge by adopting a decorrelated method inspired by a projection technique, which helps to mitigate the influence of these nuisance parameters.

Without loss of generality, consider
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as
(\alpha,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
, with
\alpha
being the first scalar component. The gradients and Hessian are partitioned as
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}=(\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m},(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m})^{\top})^{\top}
and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\nabla^{2}_{\alpha\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}&(\nabla^{2}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m})^{\top}\\
\nabla^{2}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}&\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}\end{array}\right)
. Similarly, we denote the blocks of
H^{*}=\operatorname{cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\big(\varphi(\mathbf{x})\big)
as

H^{*}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H^{*}_{\alpha\alpha}&(H^{*}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{\top}\\
H^{*}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}}&H^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\end{array}\right)
. To test the hypothesis
H_{0}:\alpha^{*}=\alpha_{0}
, we first estimate the nuisance parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
using the Elastic-net-penalized MCMC-MLE defined in (
5
). Then, we approximate
\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
using the partial score function
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
in terms of expectation:

\mathbf{w}^{*}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}}\operatorname{E}\big[\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})-\mathbf{w}^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\big]^{2}=(H^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{-1}H^{*}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}},

where
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
is the projection of
\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
onto the linear space spanned by
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
elements. In high-dimension case, direct computation of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
from sample data is problematic, so we estimate
\mathbf{w}^{*}
using a lasso-type estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
:

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{\alpha\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\lambda^{\prime}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1},

where
\lambda^{\prime}>0
is another tuning parameter. Building upon this, we propose a decorrelated score function:

\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}):=\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{m}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}).

(6)

Intuitively, this decorrelated score function removes the effects of the high-dimensional nuisance parameters
[
51
]
.
Then to establish the limiting distribution of
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\alpha^{*}=\alpha_{0}
, the following assumptions regarding the properties of the covariance matrix
H^{*}
and the weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{*}
are required.

Assumption 3.1
.

The eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
H^{*}
are lower and upper bounded:
\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\min}(H)\leq\lambda_{\max}(H^{*})\leq\lambda_{\max}
for some
0<\lambda_{\min}<\lambda_{\max}
.

Assumption 3.2
.

The
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
is bounded:
\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq D
for some
D>0
.

Define the
\ell_{0}
-norm as
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{0}:=\#\{j\in[p]:v_{j}\neq 0\}
for any vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and let
s:=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|_{0}
and
s^{\prime}:=\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{0}
. The following lemmas establish the asymptotic normality of

and the consistency of the estimator

.

.

Suppose Assumptions
B.1
and
3.1

.

Suppose Assumptions

-
B.3

\lambda_{1}\asymp\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{m}}+\frac{\log p}{m}\bigg)
,
\lambda_{1}\asymp\lambda_{2}\asymp\lambda^{\prime}=o(1)
, and
s=s^{\prime}=O(1)
, we have

\big|\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}-H^{*}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}\big|=O_{p}\left((s+s^{\prime})\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{m}}+\frac{\log p}{m}\right)\right).

And thus, we define the decorrelated score test statistic as

\widehat{S}_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt{{n}{\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-1}}}\widehat{U}(\alpha_{0},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),&\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0,\\
0,&\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}\leq 0\end{array}.\right.

by Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
, we know that the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{S}_{n}
is a standard normal distribution under
H_{0}
. Next, we focus on developing a confidence interval for the true parameter
\alpha^{*}
. This approach addresses the limitation noted earlier, where the decorrelated score function did not directly yield a confidence interval for
\alpha^{*}
.

The key idea to derive a confidence interval for
\alpha^{*}
is based on the decorrelated score function
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
. Drawing from Theorem
3.1
and leveraging the properties of Z-estimators as outlined in
[
63
]
, we find that the solution to the equation
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=0
closely approximates
\alpha
. However, directly solving the estimation equation is computationally challenging. To circumvent this, we employ a method that linearizes
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
around the penalized estimator
\widehat{\alpha}
, leading to the introduction of the following one-step estimator
\widetilde{\alpha}
:

\widetilde{\alpha}:=\widehat{\alpha}-\left[\left.\frac{\partial\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\alpha}\right|_{\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}}\right]^{-1}\widehat{U}(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}),

(7)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
is derived from the Elastic-net-penalized MCMC-MLE in (
5
), and
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
is defined as the decorrelated score test in (
6
). Notably, the formulation of
\widetilde{\alpha}
parallels the application of a one-step iteration at the initial point
\alpha=\widehat{\alpha}
, akin to Newton’s method, for solving
\widehat{U}(\alpha,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=0
.

A key advantage of the one-step estimator in high-dimensional settings is its asymptotic normality, a property not shared by traditional penalty estimators such as
\widehat{\theta}
; this is detailed in the theorem below.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
B.1
-
B.3
,
3.1
-
3.2
,
\lambda_{1}\asymp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{m}}+\frac{\log p}{m}\right)
,
\lambda_{1}\asymp\lambda_{2}\asymp\lambda^{\prime}=o(1)
, and
s=s^{\prime}=O(1)
hold. If
\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\log^{3/2}p}{m}=o(1)
and
\frac{m}{n}\gtrsim\log p
, the decorrelated one-step estimator
\widetilde{\alpha}
satisfies

\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{\alpha}-\alpha^{*})\,\rightsquigarrow\,\mathcal{N}(0,H^{*-1}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}})
.

Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
indicate that

\sqrt{n\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\widetilde{\alpha}-\alpha^{*})\,\rightsquigarrow\,\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(8)

Consequently, it is straightforward to construct a
100(1-\eta)\%
confidence interval for
\alpha^{*}
with
\widehat{H}_{\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0

\left[\widetilde{\alpha}-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\eta}{2})}{\sqrt{n\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}}},\,\widetilde{\alpha}+\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{\eta}{2})}{\sqrt{n\widehat{H}_{\alpha|\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\right].

(9)

4
FDR Control

In this section, we develop two effective false discovery rate (FDR) control procedures using p-values and e-values. Specifically, we define a specific “metric" value to assess the confidence level associated with each component of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
via data splitting for the p-value based approach; and further introduce the e-value based approach to facilitate more streamlined computations.

Formally speaking, FDR control is for the multiple hypothesis testing when addressing a series of hypotheses, such as
H_{k}:\theta_{k}^{*}=\theta^{(0)}_{k}
for each
k
in
[p]
. Here,
\theta^{(0)}_{k}
signifies the pre-established value against which the true value
\theta_{k}^{*}
is tested
[
7
,
8
,
59
]
.
Consider
\mathscr{N}\subseteq[p]
as the set encompassing unknown true null hypotheses. The FDR control process, based on certain statistical measures, decides on the acceptance or rejection of each hypothesis
H_{k}
. The subset
\mathscr{D}\subseteq[p]
, identified as discoveries in the literature, represents those hypotheses rejected.
The intersection
\mathscr{N}\,\cap\,\mathscr{D}
, known as false discoveries, includes the true null hypotheses erroneously rejected. The false discovery proportion (FDP), defined as
\operatorname{FDP}=|\mathscr{N}\cap\mathscr{D}|/|\mathscr{D}|
, is a critical metric indicating the efficacy of the FDR control procedure. Notably,
\operatorname{FDP}
is a random variable, but the emphasis is on its expected value under the data’s generating distribution, referred to as FDR represented as

\operatorname{FDR}=\mathrm{E}(\operatorname{FDP})=\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{|\mathscr{N}\cap\mathscr{D}|}{|\mathscr{D}|}\right].

Crafting an effective FDR control strategy is a fundamental task in multiple hypothesis testing to control the type-I errors
[
59
,
64
,
67
]
.

4.1
FDR control via p-values

False discovery rate is a profound p-value based criterion for controlling uncertainty and avoiding spurious discoveries in multiple testing
[
7
]
.
Nevertheless, the challenge with classical p-values arises from their inter-correlation, which prevents straightforward summation for statistical analysis. To address the dependence, data splitting is effective in constructing data-driven mirror statistics
[
23
,
25
]

M_{j}=\operatorname{sgn}\big(T_{j}^{(1)}T_{j}^{(2)}\big)f\big(|T_{j}^{(1)}|,|T_{j}^{(2)}|\big),

where
f(u,v)
is a function that is non-negative, symmetric, and exhibits monotonic increase for both
u
and
v
and
\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)
is the sign function. The term
T_{j}^{(\ell)}
represents the normalized estimates for
\theta_{j}
in the
\ell
-th partition of the entire dataset. Specifically, we employ

T_{j}^{(\ell)}=\big(\widetilde{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j}^{*}\big)\sqrt{n\widehat{H}_{j\,|\,-j}/2},

where
\widetilde{\theta}_{j}
denotes the one-step estimator of
\theta_{j}
in (
7
) and
\widehat{H}_{j\,|\,-j}:=\widehat{H}_{\theta_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}}
. Thus, we first split the data into two disjoint parts, and then run Algorithm
1
analogous to that in
[
23
]
to asymptotically control the FDR.

Algorithm 1

FDR control via single data split

1:

The data and designated FDR control level
q
;

2:

For each
j
, calculate the mirror statistic
M_{j}
.

3:

Given a designated level
q\in(0,1)
, the cutoff is

\tau_{q}=\inf\left\{t>0:\widehat{\mathrm{FDP}}(t):=\frac{\#\left\{j:M_{j}<-t\right\}}{\#\left\{j:M_{j}>t\right\}}\leq q\right\}.

4:

Set
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}=\{j:M_{j}>\tau_{q}\}
.

We necessitate an additional assumption to theoretically substantiate our method for controlling the FDR.

Assumption 4.1
.

The indices
{j\in S_{0}}
are exchangeable. Specifically, for the population version
\mathbf{X}
, we have that
{X}_{j}{\mathrel{\to}^{d.}}{X}_{k}
and
{X}_{j}|\mathbf{X}_{-j}{\mathrel{\to}^{d.}}{X}_{k}|\mathbf{X}_{-k}
.

Assumption
4.1
is commonly encountered in the literature on multiple hypothesis testing, underpinning the concept of ‘knockoff filtering’
[
4
,
55
,
40
,
18
]
. This assumption is satisfied by a broad spectrum of models within the exponential family of MRFs, including Ising model and the Gaussian graphical model.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
3.2
hold. Assume that
\widehat{H}_{j\mid-j}
is uniformly integrable for any
j\in\{j\in[p]:\theta_{j}^{*}=0\}
. Under Assumption
4.1
, we have

\limsup_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{FDR}:=\mathop{\lim\sup}_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\#\{j:j\in S_{0},\,j\in\widehat{S}_{\tau_{q}}\}}{\#\{j\in\widehat{S}_{\tau_{q}}\}}\right]\leq q.

Theorem
4.1
validates that single data split (Algorithm
1
) yields effective FDR at the target level. However, potential loss of power or instability
[
23
]
are involved due to the split randomness. To mitigate these issues, we consider multiple data splits, akin to the approach described in
[
23
]
. Define

I_{j}=\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\mathds{1}(j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}})}{|\widehat{\mathscr{S}}|}\right],\qquad\widehat{I}_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\mathds{1}(j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)})}{|\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}|},

where
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}
denotes the set of selected features in the
k
-th data split as determined by Algorithm
1
. Set
I_{j}=0
if
|\widehat{\mathscr{S}}|=0
. The term
I_{j}
represents the inclusion rate, reflecting the extent to which information about
\theta_{j}
is captured within the selected set
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}
.

Algorithm 2

FDR control via multiple data split

1:

The data and designated FDR control level
q
;

2:

Sort
\widehat{I}_{j}
by
0\leq\widehat{I}_{(1)}\leq\widehat{I}_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\widehat{I}_{(p)}
.

3:

Find the largest
\ell
such that
\widehat{I}_{(1)}+\cdots+\widehat{I}_{(\ell)}\leq q
.

4:

Select the features
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{\text{mul}}=\{j:\widehat{I}_{j}>\widehat{I}_{(\ell)}\}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose the conditions in Theorem
4.1
hold. The
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{\text{mul}}
selected by Algorithm
2
satisfies

\limsup_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{FDP}:=\mathop{\lim\sup}_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\#\{j\,:\,j\in S_{0},j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{\text{mul}}\}}{\#\{j\,:\,j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}}^{\text{mul}}\}}\leq q.

Theorem
4.2
confirms that Algorithm
2
is capable of efficiently controlling the FDR. Moreover, given that

\operatorname{var}\bigg(\frac{\mathds{1}(j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}})}{|\widehat{\mathscr{S}}|}\bigg)\geq\operatorname{var}\bigg(\mathrm{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathds{1}(j\in\widehat{\mathscr{S}})}{|\widehat{\mathscr{S}}|}\,\bigg|\,\text{data}\bigg]\bigg),

the implementation of multiple data splits in Algorithm
2
can be considered a Rao-Blackwell improvement over the single data split approach delineated in Algorithm
1
.

4.2
FDR control via e-values

Controlling FDR typically aims to restrict the expected proportion of Type I errors, which involve the incorrect rejection of a true null hypothesis. The p-value based approaches are typically adept at closely controlling the FDR at a pre-specified level, implying a concurrent tendency to manage Type II errors – the probability of erroneously retaining a false null hypothesis
[
23
,
24
]

However, in some scenarios, the focus on Type II error is less critical, allowing for the possibility of controlling the FDR at levels significantly below the designated threshold. This approach may be overly stringent, but if such strictness is not a concern, it can be an effective strategy. In these instances, the e-BH procedure proves beneficial
[
64
,
65
,
68
]
. The e-values are characterized by their additivity, independent of the correlation among covariates. This unique attribute simplifies and streamlines the FDR control process using e-values, compared to approaches based on p-values.
In this section, we will design the e-BH procedure in
[
64
,
65
,
68
]
, and then analyze the asymptotic properties of the proposed methods.

The key to a valid e-value procedure is the establishment of an e-variable
E
such that
\mathrm{E}E=1
under the null hypothesis
[
65
]
. When the hypothesis
H_{k}
is true, by (
8
) we observe that
\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot E_{k}\,\rightsquigarrow\,\big|\mathcal{N}(0,1)\big|
, leading to
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{E}E_{k}=1
. Based on this, we propose the following Algorithm
3
.

Algorithm 3

FDR control via e-values

1:

The data and designated FDR control level
q
;

2:

For each
k\in\mathcal{P}
, compute the e-values
e_{k}
as one observation of
E_{k}
corresponding to each hypothesis
H_{k}
by

E_{k}=\sqrt{\pi/2}\cdot\sqrt{n\widehat{H}_{\theta_{k}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-k}}}\left|\widetilde{\theta}_{k}-\theta^{(0)}_{k}\right|.

(10)

3:

For each
k\in\mathcal{P}
, let
e_{(k)}
be the
k
-th order statistics of
e_{1},\cdots,e_{p}
from the largest to the smallest.

4:

Define
k^{*}
to be

k^{*}:=\max\left\{k\in\mathcal{K}:\frac{ke_{(k)}}{p}\geq\frac{1}{q}\right\}\,(\max\{\varnothing\}=0),

reject the largest
k^{*}
e-values (hypotheses).

For the purpose of avoiding overly complex structures in the index set, we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 4.2
.

For any
\mathscr{S}_{n}\subseteq[p]
,

\limsup_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathscr{S}_{n}|}\sum_{k\in\mathscr{S}_{n}}\mathrm{E}(E_{k})\leq 1.

Assumption
4.2
is pivotal in ensuring average convergence on sets with diverging numbers of elements. However, this assumption can be relaxed with additional constraints on the data, as discussed in Section 4 of
[
16
]
. Under this assumption, the following theorem addressees the control of FDR in an asymptotic framework.

Theorem 4.3
.

Under Assumptions
B.1
to
4.2
, Algorithm
3
follows that
\limsup_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{FDR}\leq q
.

As we can see, when incorporating Assumption
4.2
, Theorem
4.3
ensures that Algorithm
3
asymptotically controls the FDR at the desired level. Therefore, our e-value procedure provides theoretically valid FDR control, similar to the previous p-value procedure.

Concluding remarks

This paper investigated the estimation and inference of parameters for Markov Random Fields in canonical exponential families. We propose a penalized Markov Chain Monte Carlo Maximum Likelihood method to estimate the intractable normalizing constant in high-dimensional settings. To facilitate statistical inference on the obtained estimates, we subsequently devise a one-step estimator with decorrelated scores and thus present two FDR controlling procedures. Our extensive numerical investigations demonstrate that the proposed methods provide satisfactory performances in estimation and inference. Given our methodology’s reliance on mild assumptions, its broad potential applicability, warrants further research.

Appendix A
Concentration Inequalities for Markov Chain

Concentration inequalities provide essential non-asymptotic error bounds for sums of random variables, crucial for high-dimensional statistical inference. They are notably used in analyzing how closely these sums approximate their expected values, as demonstrated in applications like Oracle inequalities in linear models and testing in high-dimensional regression models
[
10
,
51
,
72
]
.

In MCMC-MLE, the Markov chain’s trajectory average approximates the normalizing constant
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. To evaluate how this average aligns with
C(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we require a concentration inequality for the Markov chain. Our setup involves a Markov chain
\{\mathbf{Y}_{i},i\geq 1\}
on state space
{\mathcal{X}}
, with transition kernel
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
, initial distribution
q(\mathbf{Y})
, and stationary distribution
h(\mathbf{Y})
. We assume the chain is irreducible and aperiodic, typical in MCMC, ensuring a unique stationary distribution and ergodicity. Key quantities are defined as

M_{1}:=\operatorname{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim h}\left(\frac{p(\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})}{h(\mathbf{Y})}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{C^{2}(\theta^{*})}\operatorname{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim h}\left(\frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*\top}\varphi(\mathbf{Y})}}{h(\mathbf{Y})}\right)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{2}:=\max_{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{p(\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})}{h(\mathbf{Y})},

(11)

where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
gauge the deviation between the stationary distribution
h(\mathbf{y})
and the true distribution
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
.

Denote the Hilbert space
L^{2}(h)
with the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}}f(\mathbf{y})g(\mathbf{y})h(\mathbf{y})
. A linear operator
P
, connected to the transition kernel
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
, is defined as
(Pf)(\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}f(\mathbf{x})P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
.
The spectral gap of the Markov chain, denoted as
1-\kappa
, is characterized by
\kappa=\sup\{|\rho|:\rho\in\operatorname{Spec}(P)\}
,
where
\operatorname{Spec}(P)
represents the spectrum of
P
. We define
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
to describe the convergence rate of a trajectory’s average to its expectation:

\beta_{1}:=\frac{1+\kappa}{1-\kappa}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{2}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{3},&\text{if}\quad\kappa=0\\
\frac{5}{1-\kappa},&\text{if}\quad\kappa\in(0,1)\end{cases}.

(12)

These definitions set the stage for a crucial concentration inequality for Markov chains, pivotal in the proof of this article.

Lemma A.1

(Theorem 1 in
[
43
]
)
.

Let
\{\mathbf{Y}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{Y}_{m}\}
be a stationary Markov chain with invariant distribution
h(\mathbf{Y})
and non-zero absolute spectral gap
1-\kappa>0
. Suppose
g_{i}:\mathbf{\mathcal{X}}\longrightarrow[-M,+M]
is a sequence of functions with
\operatorname{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim h}g_{i}(\mathbf{Y})=0
. Let
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\operatorname{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim h}g_{i}^{2}(Y)
. Then for any
t>0
, we have

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{i})\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{mt^{2}}{2(\beta_{1}\sigma^{2}+\beta_{2}Mt)}\right).

(13)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{E}\left|t\big(\sqrt{{H_{j|-j}^{*}}/{\widehat{H}_{j|-j}}}-1\big)\right|+o(1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[-n,n]}|t|\mathrm{E}\left|\big(\sqrt{{H_{j|-j}^{*}}/{\widehat{H}_{j|-j}}}-1\big)\right|+o(1)=0,

where
\phi(\cdot)
is the density of standard normal density and the last equality holds by the uniform integrability of
\widehat{H}_{j|-j}
. By Lemma A.5 in
[
23
]
, we have
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R},\,j\in S_{0}}|\mathrm{P}(M_{j}>t)-H(t)|\longrightarrow 0
, which implies

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{(j,k)\in S_{0}\times S_{0}}\big|\mathrm{P}(M_{j}>t)\mathrm{P}(M_{k}>t)-H^{2}(t)\big|\,\to\,0.

For the last term in (
33
), we make the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(M_{j}>t,M_{k}>t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}\left(T_{j}^{(2)}>I_{t}(T_{j}^{(1)}),\,T_{k}^{(2)}>I_{t}(T_{k}^{(1)}),\,T_{i}^{(1)}>0,\,T_{j}^{(1)}>0\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{P}\left(T_{j}^{(2)}>I_{t}(T_{j}^{(1)}),\,T_{k}^{(1)}<-I_{t}(T_{j}^{(1)}),\,T_{j}^{(1)}>0,\,T_{k}^{(1)}<0\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{P}\left(T_{j}^{(2)}<-I_{t}(T_{j}^{(1)}),\,T_{k}^{(2)}>I_{t}(T_{k}^{(1)}),\,T_{j}^{(1)}<0,\,T_{k}^{(1)}>0\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{P}\left(T_{j}^{(2)}<-I_{t}(T_{i}^{(1)}),\,T_{j}^{(2)}<-I_{t}(T_{j}^{(1)}),\,T_{j}^{(1)}<0,\,T_{k}^{(1)}<0\right)

\displaystyle:=D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3}+D_{4},

where
I_{t}(v)=\inf\{u\geq 0\,:\,f(u,v)>t\}
. For
D_{1}
,

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{P}\big(T_{j}^{(2)}>I_{t}(x),\,T_{k}^{(2)}>I_{t}(y)\big)\,\big|\,T_{j}^{(1)}=x,\,T_{k}^{(1)}=y\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[\mathrm{P}\big(\widetilde{T}_{j}^{(2)}>I_{t}(x),\,\widetilde{T}_{k}^{(2)}>I_{t}(y)\big)\,\big|\,T_{j}^{(1)}=x,\,T_{k}^{(1)}=y\right]+o(1)

,

[

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\Phi(t_{1})\Phi(t_{2})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{n}}{n!}\phi^{(n-1)}(t_{1})\phi^{(n-1)}(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\Phi(t_{1})\Phi(t_{2})+\rho\phi(t_{1})\phi(t_{2})+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{n}}{n!}\phi^{(n-1)}(t_{1})\phi^{(n-1)}(t_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(t_{1})\Phi(t_{2})+\rho\phi(t_{1})\phi(t_{2})+c_{2}\rho\leq\Phi(t_{1})\Phi(t_{2})+(1/(2\pi)+c_{2})\rho.

where the last inequality holds due to

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{[\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\phi^{(n-1)}(t)]^{2}}{n!}<\infty

We first deal with the covariance

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{\theta}_{j},\widetilde{\theta}_{k})

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}\Big(\big(\nabla_{j}\,\widehat{U}(\widehat{\theta}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-j})\big)^{-1}\widehat{U}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\ \big(\nabla_{k}\,\widehat{U}(\widehat{\theta}_{k},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{-k})\big)^{-1}\widehat{U}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\Big)

Proof of the Theorem
4.3
:

Proof.

Let
N_{1}(t)
be the number of true null hypotheses with e-values larger than or equal to
t
and
N_{2}(t)
be the number of all hypotheses with e-values larger than or equal to
t
. Define
t_{\epsilon}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\frac{N_{2}(t)t}{p}\geq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right\}.

Note that

\frac{ke_{(k)}}{p}=\frac{N_{2}(e_{(k)})e_{(k)}}{p},

we reject all hypotheses with e-values larger than or equal to
t_{\epsilon}
, from which the FDR can be written as

\operatorname{FDR}=\mathrm{E}\left(\frac{N_{1}(t_{\epsilon})}{N_{2}(t_{\epsilon})}\right)=\frac{\epsilon}{p}\cdot\mathrm{E}(t_{\epsilon}N_{1}(t_{\epsilon})),

where
N_{2}(t_{\epsilon})=p/\epsilon t_{\epsilon}
. Note

N_{1}(t_{\epsilon})=\sum_{k\in\mathscr{N}}1(E_{k}\geq t_{\epsilon}),

thus we have

\mathrm{E}(t_{\epsilon}N_{1}(t_{\epsilon}))=\sum_{k\in\mathscr{N}}\mathrm{E}(t_{\epsilon}1(E_{k}\geq t_{\epsilon}))\leq\sum_{k\in\mathscr{N}}\mathrm{E}(E_{k}),

where the inequality hold due to
t_{\epsilon}1(E_{k}\geq t_{\epsilon})\leq E_{k}
a.s.. Now, we bound the FDR by

\operatorname{FDR}\leq\epsilon\cdot\frac{|\mathscr{N}|}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathscr{N}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}\mathrm{E}(E_{k})\leq\epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathscr{N}|}\sum_{k\in\mathscr{N}}\mathrm{E}(E_{k}).

(35)

By taking the limit superior on both sides of (
35
) and Assumption
4.2
, we conclude the result.
∎
