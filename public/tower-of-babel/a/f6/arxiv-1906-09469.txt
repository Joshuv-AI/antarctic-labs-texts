On Schur Rings over Infinite Groups III
Nicholas Bastian, Andrew Misseldine

Abstract
In the paper, we develop further the properties of Schur rings over infinite groups, with particular emphasis on the virtually cyclic group 𝒵 × 𝒵 p \mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{p} , where p p is a prime. We provide structure theorems for primitive sets in these Schur rings. In the case of Fermat and safe primes, a complete classification theorem is proven, which states that all Schur rings over 𝒵 × 𝒵 p \mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{p} are traditional. We also draw analogs between Schur rings over 𝒵 × 𝒵 p \mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{p} and partitions of difference sets over 𝒵 p \mathcal{Z}_{p} .



Keywords
:
Schur ring, group ring, cyclic group, virtually cyclic group, association scheme, difference set

We remind the reader of some important notation and terminology introduced in

Properties of Schur Rings

In this section, we gather important terminology and properties about Schur rings in general that will be useful for forthcoming proofs. Many of these were known to Wielandt
[
8
]
in his early work, although they have been extended to the infinite case. Most of these properties will be mentioned without proof.

The partition associated to a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
will be denoted
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. The elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
are called the
\mathfrak{S}
-classes
(or
primitive sets
of
\mathfrak{S}
). Essentially, an element of the group algebra belongs to a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
only if it has constant coefficients across each
\mathfrak{S}
-class. We say that a subset
C\subset G
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset
if
C
is a union of the
\mathfrak{S}
-classes. When
G
is finite, this is equivalent to
\overline{C}\in\mathfrak{S}
. We say a subset
H\subseteq G
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup
if
H
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset and a subgroup of
G
. For any
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup
H
, let
\mathfrak{S}_{H}:=\mathfrak{S}\cap F[H]
, called a
Schur subring
. For any Schur ring, the set of
\mathfrak{S}
-subsets forms a lattice closed under intersections and unions and the set of Schur subrings forms a lattice closed under intersections and joins. The associated partitions to these Schur subrings are the common coarsenings and the common refinements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}_{H})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}_{K})
, denoted
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\langle H,K\rangle}
, respectively.

For an element
\alpha\in F[G]
, let
\supp(\alpha):=\{g\mid\alpha_{g}\neq{0}\}\subseteq G
, which is called the
support
of
\alpha
and must necessarily be finite.
If
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over group
G
,
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}
, and
G_{\alpha}=\{g\in G\mid\alpha g=\alpha\}
is the stabilizer subgroup, then
G_{\alpha}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup of
G
. When
\alpha=\overline{C}
is a simple quantity, we will denote
G_{\alpha}
as
G_{C}
. Also, if
H=\langle\supp(\alpha)\rangle
, then
H
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup.

Define the
n
th Frobenius map
for any integer
n
by the rule
\alpha^{(n)}:=\sum_{g\in{G}}\alpha_{g}g^{n}
whenever
\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g
. The Frobenius map is a linear map
F[G]\to F[G]
such that
\alpha^{(mn)}=\left(a^{(m)}\right)^{(n)}
for any integers
n
and
m
and
\alpha^{(-1)}=\alpha^{*}
. We define the Frobenius map on subsets of
G
analogously. For example, all the subgroups of
\mathcal{Z}
can be written as
\mathcal{Z}^{(n)}
for some integer
n
. The Frobenius map is very useful in determining the structure of primitive sets of Schur rings over abelian groups. In fact, if
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over an abelian group
G
and
m
is an integer coprime to the orders of all torsion elements of
G
, then for all
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}
we have that
\alpha^{(m)}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Using the Frobenius map, we see that for a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
over an abelian group
G
, if the torsion subgroup
T(G)
has finite exponent then
T(G)
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup. Hence, Schur rings over abelian groups have a torsion Schur subring, denoted
T(\mathfrak{S})
.

Let
\varphi:G\to H
be a group homomorphism. Then this map linearly lifts to the group ring naturally and will be denoted by the same symbol
\varphi:F[G]\to F[H]
. A ring homomorphism between group rings of this form is called a
Cayley homomorphism
(the Frobenius map is an example of such).
If
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over
G
and
\ker\varphi
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup, then
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is a Schur ring over
\varphi(G)
. In particular,
\mathcal{D}(\varphi(\mathfrak{S}))=\{\varphi(C)\mid C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\}
. Additionally, if
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, then the non-zero intersection numbers with
C
and the cosets of
\ker\varphi
are constant, that is,
|C\cap g(\ker\varphi)|=|C\cap g^{\prime}(\ker\varphi)|
whenever these intersections are non-empty. Furthermore, if two
\mathfrak{S}
-classes both intersect some coset of
K
, then they intersect all the same cosets of
K
. These facts imply that
\mathfrak{S}/T(\mathfrak{S})
is a Schur ring over
G/T(G)
for any abelian group
G
.

We briefly remind the reader of the four types of traditional Schur rings. For a finite group
G
, the partition
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})=\{1,G\smallsetminus 1\}
always affords a Schur ring, called the
trivial Schur ring
. At the other extreme, for any group
G
(finite or infinite) the partition of singletons affords a Schur ring, known as the
discrete Schur ring
. This Schur ring coincides with the group algebra itself.

If
\mathcal{H}\leq\Aut(G)
is a finite automorphism subgroup, then the set of elements of
F[G]
fixed by
\mathcal{H}
is a Schur ring over
G
, denoted
F[G]^{\mathcal{H}}
and called the
automorphic Schur ring
(or
orbit Schur ring
) associated to
\mathcal{H}
. The discrete Schur ring is automorphic where
\mathcal{H}=1
. When
G
is abelian, the automorphic Schur ring associated to
\mathcal{H}=\langle*\rangle
, consisting of inverse pairs, is called the
symmetric Schur ring
and is denoted
F[G]^{\pm}
.

Suppose
G=H\times K
. If
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
are Schur rings over
H
and
K
, respectively, then the ring
\mathfrak{S}\otimes_{F}\mathfrak{T}
has a Schur ring structure, called the
direct product
of
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
and is denoted
\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{T}
. The associated partition is
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{T})=\{CD\mid C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}),D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\}
. Also,
F[H]^{\mathcal{H}}\times F[K]^{\mathcal{K}}=F[H\times K]^{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}}
, that is, the direct product of automorphic Schur rings is automorphic.

The final family of traditional Schur rings is due to Leung and Man
[
5
]
. Let
H,K\leq G
be two nontrivial, proper subgroups such that

is finite,
K\leq H
, and
K\trianglelefteq G
. Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
H
with

as an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup. Suppose
\varphi:G\to G/K
is the natural quotient map. Then
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is a Schur ring over
G/K
. Let
\mathfrak{T}
be a Schur ring over
G/K
such that
\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}=\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
. Then define the
wedge product

\mathfrak{S}\wedge\mathfrak{T}
by the partition

\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge\mathfrak{T})=\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\cup\{\varphi^{-1}(D)\mid D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\smallsetminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})\}.

Under these conditions,
\mathfrak{S}\wedge\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over
G
, called the
wedge product
of
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
. Alternatively, a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
over
G
is a wedge product if there exist nontrivial, proper
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroups
H,K\leq G
such

,
K\trianglelefteq G
, and every
\mathfrak{S}
-class outside

is a union of
K
-cosets (this necessarily implies that

must be finite). In this case, we say that

is a

of
\mathfrak{S}
.

The Structure of Primitive Sets of Schur Rings over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}

We now consider the virtually cyclic group
G=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\langle z\rangle\times\langle a\rangle
. Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
G
. As mentioned above,
T(G)=\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup. Hence,
T(\mathfrak{S})
is some Schur subring over
\mathcal{Z}_{n}
, to which Leung’s and Man’s classification theorem applies. Let
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Z}
be the natural projection map. Again,
\ker\varphi=\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup. Hence,
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is a Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}
. As there are only two such Schur rings, the discrete and symmetric rings,
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is equal to one of these. Many of the following proofs will be divided into two cases based upon the image
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
. For example, if
H\leq\mathcal{Z}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup, then the Schur subring
\mathfrak{S}_{H}
maps isomorphically onto
\varphi(\mathfrak{S}_{H})\leq F[\mathcal{Z}]
. If
H\neq 1
, then
H\cong\mathcal{Z}
, and, hence,
\varphi(\mathfrak{S}_{H})
itself is either discrete or symmetric. The structure of
\varphi(\mathfrak{S}_{H})
must agree with
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
, that is, they are either both discrete or both symmetric. As
\mathfrak{S}_{H}\cong\varphi(\mathfrak{S}_{H})
,
\mathfrak{S}_{H}
is discrete when
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is discrete and symmetric when
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is symmetric for all nontrivial
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroups
H\leq\mathcal{Z}
.

For another example of these two cases, let
t
be any integer. If
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is discrete, then
\{z^{t}\}
is an
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
-class. Then
\varphi^{-1}(\{z^{t}\})=z^{t}\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset (but not necessarily primitive). Likewise, if
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
is symmetric, then
\{z^{t},z^{-t}\}
is a
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})
-class. Then
\varphi^{-1}(\{z^{t},z^{-t}\})=z^{t}\mathcal{Z}_{n}\cup z^{-t}\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset. In either case,
\{z^{t},z^{-t}\}\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset for all integers
t
, which generates its supporting
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup
\langle z^{t}\rangle\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}=\mathcal{Z}^{(t)}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}\cong\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}
. Our main task is to determine which refinements of these
\mathfrak{S}
-subsets are possible based upon assumptions on the
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroups. The discrete case is always inherently easier to consider. For example, if we have the discrete case and
\mathfrak{S}
contains a singleton other than the identity
\{1\}
, say
\{a^{k}z^{t}\}
, then by Lemma
2.2
,
(a^{k}z^{t})^{s}C
is a primitive set of
\mathfrak{S}
for all
C\in\mathcal{D}(T(\mathfrak{S}))
and all integers
s
. Hence, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{Z}^{(t)}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}}=F[\langle a^{k}z^{t}\rangle]\times T(\mathfrak{S})\cong F[\mathcal{Z}]\times T(\mathfrak{S})
under this assumption. Therefore, if
z^{t}\mathcal{Z}_{n}
contains a primitive subset which is a singleton, then
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{Z}^{(t)}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}}
is traditional. When

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

Proof.

First, suppose that

-subset of a primitive set, which is a contradiction. Thus,

Next, suppose that

. Assume first the discrete case, that is,
\varphi(\mathfrak{S})=F[\mathcal{Z}]
. Then there is an integer

Consider

which is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subset. Suppose that
C^{(k)}
is not primitive. Then a subset of it must be, say

is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup, we know

is primitive. Also, there exist integers

The case where

Connection between Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{n}
and Difference Sets

Consider a primitive subset of
z\mathcal{Z}_{v}
contained in a Schur ring over
G=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{v}
(we are assuming the discrete case, as the symmetric case is similar), say
C=zD
. Say
|D|=k
. Suppose that the Schur subring
T(\mathfrak{S})
is trivial. Then
\overline{C}\,\overline{C}^{*}\in T(\mathfrak{S})
, that is,
\overline{C}\,\overline{C}^{*}=n+\lambda\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{v}}
for some integer
\lambda
and
n=k-\lambda
. This implies that
D
is a difference set of
\mathcal{Z}_{v}
, as mentioned in the proof of Theorem
4.1
. As the primitive subset
C
was arbitrary,
\mathcal{Z}_{v}
must be a union of disjoint difference sets in this case.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a partition
\mathcal{D}
of a finite group
G
is a
difference partition
if each block in
\mathcal{D}
is itself a difference set.

There are many simple examples of difference partitions. For example,
\{\{G\}\}
and
\{\{g\}\mid g\in G\}
are difference partitions, as both
G
and
\{g\}
are trivial difference sets. Likewise,
\{\{g\},G\smallsetminus\{g\}\}
is a difference partition for any
g\in G
. Generalizing this last example, let
D
be any difference set of
G
, then
\{D,G\smallsetminus D\}
is a difference partition. Also,
\{D\}\cup\{\{g\}\mid g\in G\smallsetminus D\}
is another difference partition. Finally, let
D
be the set of quadratic residues in
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
for
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the Paley difference set. Then
\{\{1\},D,D^{*}\}
is also a difference partition (this example can be generalized using other Paley-Hadamard difference sets). Note that some of these difference partitions are associated to Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}_{v}
.

Note that in all the previous examples of difference partitions, except maybe the complementary partition
\{D,G\smallsetminus D\}
, all of these difference partitions involve a trivial difference set, usually a singleton. Although the complementary partition
\{D,G\smallsetminus D\}
does not necessarily involve a trivial difference set over
G
, it is still quite trivial as a partition.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that a difference partition is
trivial
if either it contains a trivial difference set or contains exactly two blocks. Otherwise, we say that a difference partition is
non-trivial
.

As hinted above, all the examples of difference partitions listed herein are trivial. To provide an example of a non-trivial difference partition is more challenging. First, translates of the same difference set always have non-empty intersection, which makes translates unusable for forming a partition. Automorphic images of difference sets are, of course, difference sets, but often are equal to translates of the original difference sets. This theory of multipliers of a difference set is a well-studied topic. Thus, in order for a group to have a difference partition, almost certainly it will need at least two non-equivalent difference sets, a situation which is quite rare (the existence of a non-trivial difference set of a group is itself a fairly rare phenomenon). In the case of
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
, this is a requirement as
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
cannot be partitioned using blocks of all the same size. The two smallest primes that even have two non-equivalent, non-trivial difference sets are
p=31
and
p=307
, neither of which have block sizes that could form a non-trivial difference partition. It is natural to even ask if there is a non-trivial difference partition.

Question 5.3
.

Given a cyclic group of prime order
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
, does there exist a non-trivial difference partition? How about over an arbitrary cyclic group? Or an arbitrary abelian group?

The counting arguments used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
show that there is no non-trivial difference partition over
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
if
p
is a Fermat or a safe prime.

Of course, the partition of
z\mathcal{Z}_{v}
in a Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{v}
requires more than a difference partition. For example, if
zA,zB\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, then
(zA)(zB)^{*}=AB^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{v}
. If
A\neq B
, then
\overline{A}\,\overline{B}^{*}=\lambda\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{p}\smallsetminus 1}
. This implies that
|A||B|=\lambda(v-1)
, a similar formula to the classic formula of difference sets, namely
k(k-1)=\lambda(v-1)
.

When
T(\mathfrak{S})
is not trivial, similar properties of partitions on
z\mathcal{Z}_{v}
are required, and these partitions can be viewed as generalizations of difference partitions. This is analogous to the fact that Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
in a way generalize difference sets over
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
. For example, the Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}_{p}
which corresponds to the unique automorphism of order
\dfrac{p-1}{2}
consists of three primitive sets. When
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the non-identity classes are Paley difference sets. When
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the non-identity classes are reversible partial difference sets.

Due to the remarks and examples given above, the consideration of non-trivial difference partitions will be necessary for further study of Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{p}
. They will likely also be of broader interest in the theory of Schur rings and algebraic combinatorics itself.
