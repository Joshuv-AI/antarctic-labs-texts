Quantum metrology with full and fast quantum control
Pavel Sekatski, Michalis Skotiniotis, Jan Kołodyński, Wolfgang Dür

Abstract
We establish general limits on how precise a parameter, e.g., frequency or the strength of a magnetic field, can be estimated with the aid of full and fast quantum control . We consider uncorrelated noisy evolutions of N N qubits and show that fast control allows to fully restore the Heisenberg scaling ( ∼ 1 / N 2 \sim\!1/N^{2} ) for all rank-one Pauli noise except dephasing. For all other types of noise the asymptotic quantum enhancement is unavoidably limited to a constant-factor improvement over the standard quantum limit ( ∼ 1 / N \sim\!1/N ) even when allowing for the full power of fast control. The latter holds both in the single-shot and infinitely-many repetitions scenarios. However, even in this case allowing for fast quantum control helps to improve the asymptotic constant factor. Furthermore, for frequency estimation with finite resource we show how a parallel scheme utilizing any fixed number of entangled qubits but no fast quantum control can be outperformed by a simple, easily implementable, sequential scheme which only requires entanglement between one sensing and one auxiliary qubit.

Our main results can be summarized as follows:

(i)

FFQC allows to restore HS by completely eliminating any Pauli rank-one noise (that is not parallel to the Hamiltonian)
at the cost of slowing down the unitary evolution by a constant factor;

(ii)

All other noise processes unavoidably limit the quantum gain to a constant factor improvement over standard scaling

(iii)

For standard scaling-limited noise processes FFQC may yet allow for significant improvement of precision in case of limited
resources. We provide explicit examples demonstrating the advantage of FFQC-assisted schemes

lends itself naturally to adaptive strategies in which one is allowed to modify the protocol (measurements, control operations etc.)
"on-the-fly"—basing on the record of the measurements already collected.

2.0.1
A remark on error correction

Let us briefly remark that the most general error correction step (encoding+syndrome readout+correction) only requires
the dimension of the space describing the ancillae to be the same as the system one.
Indeed, given that the system containing the sensing probes has an overall dimension
d
that
is smaller than the dimension of the ancillary space, any bipartite pure state of the two can be written using the Schmidt decomposition as

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\!\lambda_{i}\left|i\right\rangle_{\textrm{S}}\!\otimes\!\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle_{\textrm{A}}
. Here,
\{\left|i\right\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
denotes
a given fixed basis for the system, while
\{\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle\}_{i=1}^{d}
is a set of orthonormal states of ancillae
that spans a
d
-dimensional subspace. Thus, since any error correction protocol may be implemented by restricting to states
of the above form, it suffices to choose the overall space describing the ancillae to be of same dimension as the system.

\omega

;

||A||\!:=\!\max_{\psi}|\left\langle\psi\right|A\left|\psi\right\rangle|

\mathbbm{1}
and

for the identity operator and identity linear map respectively.
We use bold face to denote vectors, so that

,

3.1
QFI and its role in quantum metrology

In all metrology schemes discussed in Sec.
2
the information about the estimated parameter of interest
is encoded in the final state of the system consisting generally of both probes and ancillae.
Thus, we denote the final state of the system in any of the schemes (a,b,c,d) of Fig.
1
as

\varrho_{\omega}
. It is then
\varrho_{\omega}
that is measured, in order to most precisely construct
an estimate of the parameter of interest,
\omega
.

How best to quantify the precision of this estimate depends
crucially on how the information about
\omega
has been collected.
If we are freely allowed to repeat the protocol sufficiently
many times and collect statistics, then the best precision of estimation, as quantified by the
mean squared error
(MSE),
is determined by the well-known Cramér-Rao bound (CRB)
[
50
,
51
]

\delta^{2}\omega\geq\frac{1}{\nu\mathcal{F}(\varrho_{\omega})},

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}(\varrho_{\omega})
is the QFI of the state and
\nu
is the number of repetitions.
On the other hand, if the information about
\omega
is inferred from the outcome of just a
single repetition of the protocol, then the single-shot MSE has to be
averaged over the prior distribution representing the knowledge we possess about
\omega

prior to its estimation.
Then, the
average mean squared error
(
AvMSE
) can be lower-bounded by either the
Bayesian Cramér-Rao bound (BCRB)
[
49
]
(for well behaved prior distributions) or the
Ziv-Zakai bound (ZZB)
[
48
]
.

Whether considering the CRB, BCRB, or ZZB, the crucial quantity of interest is the QFI:
\mathcal{F}(\varrho_{\omega})
.
Given the spectral decomposition
\varrho_{\omega}\!=\!\sum_{i}p_{i}(\omega)\left|\psi_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{i}(\omega)\right|
, the QFI of the state

\varrho_{\omega}

evaluated with respect to the estimated parameter
\omega
generally reads

{{\mathcal{F}}}(\varrho_{\omega}):=2\!\!\sum_{\underset{p_{j}+p_{j}\neq 0}{i,j}}\frac{1}{p_{i}(\omega)+p_{j}(\omega)}|\left\langle\psi_{i}(\omega)\right|\dot{\varrho}_{\omega}\left|\psi_{j}(\omega)\right\rangle|^{2}.

(2)

On the other hand, the QFI may be equivalently defined by using its relation with the fidelity between
quantum states
[
52
]
,
F(\rho,\sigma)\!:=\!\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}
:

{\mathcal{F}}(\varrho_{\omega}):=8\lim_{\mathrm{d}\omega\to 0}\frac{1-F(\varrho_{\omega},\varrho_{\omega+\mathrm{d}\omega})}{\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}}.

(3)

The QFI satisfies the following important properties. It is

additive
, so that if the final state of the system is a
product
state, i.e.,

\varrho_{\omega}\!=\!\rho^{\otimes N}_{\omega}
, then its QFI satisfies

\mathcal{F}(\rho^{\otimes N}_{\omega})\!=\!N\mathcal{F}(\rho_{\omega})
and thus
gives rise to standard scaling
[
3
]
. If the final state of the probes
is
pure
,
\varrho_{\omega}\!=\!\left|\psi_{\omega}\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi_{\omega}\right|
, then

\mathcal{F}(\varrho_{\omega})=4\left(\left\langle\dot{\psi}_{\omega}|\dot{\psi}_{\omega}\right\rangle-\left|\left\langle\dot{\psi}_{\omega}|\psi_{\omega}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right).

(4)

Moreover, if the parameter is also encoded via a unitary, so that
\left|\psi_{\omega}\right\rangle\!=\!\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega H}\left|\psi\right\rangle
where

H
is some Hamiltonian, then
\mathcal{F}(\varrho_{\omega})\!=\!4(\left\langle\psi\right|H^{2}\left|\psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi\right|H\left|\psi\right\rangle^{2})=:4\,\mathrm{Var}_{\left|\psi\right\rangle}(H)
.
In this case the QFI is maximized by preparing the probes in the state

\left|\psi\right\rangle\!=\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left|\lambda_{\textrm{max}}\right\rangle\!+\!\left|\lambda_{\textrm{min}}\right\rangle\right)
, where
\left|\lambda_{\textrm{max/min}}\right\rangle
are
the eigenstates corresponding to the maximum/minimum eigenvalues of
H
. Furthermore, if the
N
probes are subjected to a
unitary evolution generated by a
local
Hamiltonian, e.g.,
H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sigma_{3}^{(n)}
, then such a state
corresponds to the GHZ state and attains the HS by virtue of the CRB (
1
)
[
3
]
.
However, note that the QFI is important not only for metrology but also for entanglement
detection
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
; defines a natural geometric distance
between quantum states
[
56
]
, thus serving as a tool to derive speed limits on quantum evolution

[
56
,
57
]
, as well as constitutes a measure of macroscopicity
[
58
]
.
The QFI is the primary focus of this work and we further discuss its role in quantifying
the precision of estimation in metrology protocols in more detail in Sec.
5
.

3.2
Time-homogeneous qubit evolution

The interaction dynamics via which a probe senses the parameter is generally
described by a quantum channel
[
59
]
(see Fig.
1
),
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}
, that
encodes the parameter
\omega
onto the state of the probe over the interrogation time
t
:
\rho_{\omega}(t)\!=\!{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}(\rho)
.
The channel consists of a unitary evolution part, encoding the parameter of interest, and a non-unitary (noise) part describing additional system-environment interactions. In this work we shall consider that the non-unitary part arises due to uncorrelated noise processes
described by a
time-homogeneous master equation of Lindblad form
—often referred to as the semigroup
dynamics
[
60
]
—which provides an appropriate description for most physically relevant noise processes.
In particular, in such a typical setting the environment is assumed to be sufficiently large such that
it disturbs the system in the same fashion independently of the system state and the time instance
[
61
]
.
Thus, the time-homogeneity of evolution allows us to unambiguously apply the FFQC techniques as any operation applied
on the system does not affect the environment. Moreover, one may then freely swap the sensing particles
at any time with the ancillary ones, which experience afterwards the same fixed noisy dynamics.
In contrast, noise processes described by time-inhomogeneous master equations have
been recently considered within the context of quantum metrology in
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
]
, where
time-inhomogeneity has been shown to be beneficial at short time-scales— the so-called Zeno regime—where the bath can
no longer be assumed to be uncorrelated from the system. Let us stress that such a regime,
however, does not allow for the general control operations considered here to be unambiguously
applied without explicitly modelling the environment
[
66
,
35
]
.

We describe the evolution of a single qubit probe by following time-homogeneous master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{\omega}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{3},\rho_{\omega}(t)]+{\mathcal{L}}(\rho_{\omega}(t)),

(5)

where
\omega
is the parameter to be estimated and the noise is given by the Lindblad super-operator,
\mathcal{L}
.
The Liouvillian is then generally defined as

{\mathcal{L}}(\rho):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^{3}\mathsf{L}_{\mu\nu}\left(\left[\sigma_{\mu}\rho,\sigma_{\nu}\right]+\left[\sigma_{\mu},\rho\sigma_{\nu}\right]\right),

(6)

where
\mathsf{L}
is the matrix representation of the Lindblad superoperator – an Hermitian positive semi-definite matrix whose entries are independent of

t
and
\omega
. We note that all the noise terms
\mathsf{L}_{0i}
and
\mathsf{L}_{i0}
can be straightforwardly corrected within FFQC
as they lead to an additional Hamiltonian evolution term in the Eq. (
5
), sometimes referred to as Lamb-shift.
This
\omega
-independent Hamiltonian term can be cancelled by continuously applying the inverse unitary rotation.
In what follows we assume that the control operations already incorporate such unitary and will, thus,
be concerned with the restriction of
\mathsf{L}
to the subspace spanned by
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
, which we denote as
\bar{\mathsf{L}}
.

We can group all the relevant noise processes into three important families:

•

Rank-one Pauli
noise of strength
\gamma

\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1P}}_{\bf n}(\rho):=\frac{\gamma}{2}(\sigma_{\bf n}\rho\sigma_{\bf n}-\rho),

(7)

with the particular case of
dephasing
noise
\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1P}}_{\bf z}

for which
\sigma_{\bf z}=\sigma_{3}
.

•

General rank-one
noise
\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1G}}_{\bf r}
defined by the matrix

\bar{\mathsf{L}}^{\textrm{1G}}_{\bf r}:={\bf r}\,{\bf r}^{\dagger},

(8)

where
{\bf r}=(x,y,z)^{T}
and the strength of the noise is denoted
\gamma/2\!=\!|{\bf r}|^{2}
.
The special case
\mathrm{Re}({\bf r})\!\times\!\mathrm{Im}({\bf r})\!=\!0
corresponds to the rank-one Pauli noise of
Eq. (
7
), whereas whenever
|\mathrm{Re}({\bf r})|\!=\!|\mathrm{Im}({\bf r})|
and
\mathrm{Re}({\bf r})^{T}\mathrm{Im}({\bf r})\!=\!0

an
amplitude damping
channel is recovered, which represents spontaneous emission
along some particular direction.

•

Rank-two Pauli
noise
\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{2P}}_{\bf\Omega}
defined by the matrix

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{L}}^{\textrm{2P}}_{\bf\Omega}:=\frac{1}{2}\,R_{\bf\Omega}^{T}\small\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\gamma_{1}&&\\
&\gamma_{2}&\\
&&0\end{array}\right)R_{\bf\Omega},

where
{\bf\Omega}\!\in\!\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
R_{\bf\Omega}\!=\!R_{z}(\varphi)R_{y}(\theta)R_{z}(\xi)

is its matrix representation written in the Euler form with angles
(\varphi,\theta,\xi)
.
The special case of
R_{\bf\Omega}\!=\!\mathbbm{1}
corresponds to
asymmetric X-Y noise
, which
we conveniently parametrize with
\gamma_{1}\!=\!\gamma\,p
and
\gamma_{2}\!=\!\gamma(1-p)

for
0\!\leq\!p\!\leq\!1
to define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{X-Y}}(\rho):=\frac{\gamma}{2}(p\,\sigma_{1}\rho\,\sigma_{1}+(1-p)\sigma_{2}\rho\,\sigma_{2}-\rho).

(13)

In order to explicitly determine the form of the quantum channel
describing the qubit evolution, one has to integrate Eq. (

)
[

]
.
The resulting dynamics can then be expressed, e.g, with help of the corresponding
dynamical matrix,
\mathsf{S}
(specified in the Pauli operator basis), or via a Kraus representation as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^{3}\mathsf{S}_{\mu\nu}(\omega,t)\,\sigma_{\mu}\,\rho\,\sigma_{\nu}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i}K_{i}(\omega,t)\,\rho\,K_{i}(\omega,t)^{\dagger}.

(15)

Note that the Kraus representation of Eq. (

) is not unique as starting from a set of
r
linearly
independent Kraus operators,
{\bf K}\!=\!(K_{1},\dots,K_{r})^{T}
, we may simply construct
another valid set

choosing
\mathsf{u}

\omega

unitary matrix.

4
Noisy metrology with full and fast control

In this section we derive the main results of our work.

In Sec.
4.1
we outline the optimal FFQC protocol suitable for correcting rank-one Pauli noise that is
not parallel to the Hamiltonian (
7
). In Sec.
4.2
we introduce the necessary tools required to bound the QFI for FFQC (Fig.
1
(d)), and show that any other noise unavoidably leads to a linear scaling of the QFI. We exemplify our findings by deriving analytical bounds for the QFI for several physically relevant noise-types. Finally, in Sec.
4.3
we discuss different aspects in which FFQC allows to outperform scenarios without fast control, using the cases of X-Y noise (
7
) and transversal rank-one Pauli noise (
46c
) as examples.

4.1
Removing rank-one Pauli noise

Here we construct a general FFQC strategy that allows one to correct for

any
rank-one Pauli noise
\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1P}}_{{\bf n}}
introduced in Eq. (
7
)
with
{\bf n}\!\neq\!{\bf z}
. For convenience, we perform a change of basis so that the noise is generated by
\sigma_{1}

(
{\bf n}\!\!=\!\!{\bf x}
), whereas
\omega
is encoded via a rotated Hamiltonian

\sigma_{\theta}\!:=\!\mathrm{s}_{\theta}\sigma_{3}+\mathrm{c}_{\theta}\sigma_{1}
.
We consider the qubit probe to be aided by an ancillary qubit and define the two-qubit
code space
as a subset of

{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{A}}\supset{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{C}}\!:=\!\mathrm{span}\{\left|00\right\rangle,\left|11\right\rangle\}
, with
corresponding projector
\Pi_{\textrm{C}}\!:=\!\left|00\right\rangle\!\left\langle 00\right|+\left|11\right\rangle\!\left\langle 11\right|
,
and the
error space
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{E}}\!:=\!\mathrm{span}\{\left|01\right\rangle,\left|10\right\rangle\}
, with
\Pi_{\textrm{E}}\!:=\!\mathbbm{1}-\Pi_{\textrm{C}}
.

Let the probe-plus-ancilla be prepared in a pure state
\varrho\!=\!\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|
with

\left|\psi\right\rangle\!=\!\alpha\left|00\right\rangle+\beta\left|11\right\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{C}}
. The dynamics on the probe-plus-ancilla
is then described by Eq. (
7
) with only the probe system evolving

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varrho}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{\theta}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\varrho]+\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1P}}_{\bf x}\!\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}\,(\varrho).

(16)

Integrating Eq. (
16
) over an elementary timestep
\mathrm{d}t
,
we may write the probe-plus-ancilla state, up to first order in
\mathrm{d}t
, as

\displaystyle\varrho(\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varrho(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t)-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{\theta}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\varrho]\mathrm{d}t

(17)

\displaystyle+\,\frac{\gamma}{2}(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\mathrm{d}t+{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2}).

Projecting
\varrho(\mathrm{d}t)
onto the code and error subspaces yields the unnormalized states

\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(\mathrm{d}t)\!=\!\Pi_{\textrm{C}}\varrho(\mathrm{d}t)\Pi_{\textrm{C}}
and
\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(\mathrm{d}t)\!=\!\Pi_{\textrm{E}}\varrho(\mathrm{d}t)\Pi_{\textrm{E}}
given by

\displaystyle\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=\varrho(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t)-\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{s}_{\theta}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\varrho]\mathrm{d}t+O(\mathrm{d}t^{2}),

\displaystyle\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\,\mathrm{d}t+O(\mathrm{d}t^{2})

(18)

respectively.

If an error is detected, we simply apply
\sigma_{1}
on the sensing probe (so after correction

\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{E}}(\mathrm{d}t)\!:=\!(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(\mathrm{d}t)(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})
), and otherwise do nothing. Hence,
the state of probe-plus-ancilla after an infinitesimal-timestep followed by fast error correction is the mixture

\displaystyle\bar{\varrho}(\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(\mathrm{d}t)+\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{E}}(\mathrm{d}t)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varrho-\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{s}_{\theta}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\varrho]\,\mathrm{d}t+O(\mathrm{d}t^{2}),

which, to first order in
\mathrm{d}t
, is equivalent to a unitary evolution under the
projected Hamiltonian
\frac{\omega}{2}s_{\theta}\sigma_{3}
.
As the measurement may always be adjusted
to compensate for a known rotation of the Hamiltonian encoding the parameter,
the above strategy perfectly corrects rank-one Pauli noise (
7
) at the price of
slowing the evolution down by a factor
s_{\theta}=\sqrt{1-({{\bf n}}^{T}{\bf z})^{2}}
.

We summarize the above result in the following observation:

Result 1
.

A general FFQC strategy allows one to completely eliminate the impact of any rank-one Pauli noise

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{1P}}_{{\bf n}}
(
7
) that is not exactly parallel to the parameter-encoding Hamiltonian.
The resulting dynamics of the system are then described by a noiseless evolution
with a rotated Hamiltonian and the estimated parameter being rescaled to

\sqrt{1-({\bf n}^{T}{\bf z})^{2}}\,\omega
, which still yields HS in precision.

Note that in the above derivation nothing forbids us from replacing the scalar parameter
\omega
with any operator
B
acting on an additional system described by the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{B}}
.
Consequently, one can effectively modify the noisy dynamics of any
\varrho_{\textrm{BS}}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{B}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}})
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varrho_{\textrm{BS}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{2}[B\otimes\sigma_{\theta},\varrho_{\textrm{BS}}]+\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{B}}\!\otimes\!\mathcal{L}^{\textrm{1P}}_{\bf x}\,(\varrho_{\textrm{BS}}),

(20)

by adding an ancillary qubit and implementing the FFQC strategy described above. Then, the dynamics of the error-corrected
\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{BS}}=\mathrm{tr}_{\textrm{A}}\{\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{BSA}}\}

is governed by

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{BS}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{1-({{\bf n}}^{T}{\bf z})^{2}}}{2}[B\otimes\sigma_{3},\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{BS}}],

(21)

with the rank-one Pauli noise removed. Hence, our strategy can be directly used for the implementation of unitary gates
U=e^{\mathrm{i}g\,B\otimes\sigma_{3}}
in the presence of rank-one Pauli noise on the qubit.

4.2
Other noise-types: unavoidable linear scaling

In this subsection we prove our main no-go result,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}\leq 4\,\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}\,t^{\prime}
, showing that even with FFQC the QFI is bound to a linear scaling for all uncorrelated noise-types other than rank-one Pauli noise, and to minimize the constant
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
appearing in this bound for several practically relevant noise-types introduced in Sec.
3.2
. These results are presented in Sec.
4.2.4
and Sec.
4.2.5
respectively. The preceding sections are devoted to elaborate the method that allows us to derive these results. In Sec.
4.2.1
and Sec.
4.2.2
we describe the channel extension (CE)
method used to upper bound the QFI
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
37
]
and then review the bounds it yields for strategies (a), (b) and (c) of Fig.
1
.
However, the CE method fails to yield a meaningful bound when considering the limit in which the sensing process lasts for an
infinitesimal time-step. In Sec.
4.2.3
we resolve this issue by amending the CE method
in order to obtain an upper bound on the QFI for strategies that employ FFQC.

We stress that we are able to derive analytic bounds by extending the CE method to the limit
\mathrm{d}t\to 0
, in which the problem actually simplifies: one does not have to solve the master equation and determine the full dynamics! This is even more remarkable as these bounds apply to any metrological scheme (see the hierarchy in Fig.
1
)

4.2.1
Upper bounds on the QFI

We would like to compare the maximum achievable QFI in the various metrology schemes introduced in
Sec.
2
and, in particular, quantify the improvement (if any) between protocols (b), (c) and (d). To this end we use
variations of the
CE method
[
18
,
21
,
22
,
37
]
that allow one to upper-bound the QFI depending on the
scenario considered.
Crucially, they constrain the QFI using solely the properties of the quantum channel responsible for encoding the parameter.

CE bound.

The QFI of a state representing the output of a quantum channel
encoding the parameter may always be upper-bounded
purely by the channel properties after performing a channel extension (CE).
Given a probe in a state
\rho
and a quantum channel
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}
one may always construct the

CE bound
on the QFI of the output state
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}(\rho)

[
18
]
:

\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}(\rho))\;\leq\;\max_{\varrho}\mathcal{F}({\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}(\varrho))\;\leq\;4\,||\alpha(t)||,

(22)

where
\varrho\!\in\!{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{A}})
represents a joined (
extended
)
state of the probe and an ancilla, and the operator

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\!\dot{K}_{i}^{\dagger}\dot{K}_{i}=\dot{{\bf K}}^{\dagger}\,\dot{{\bf K}}

(23)

for any of the Kraus representation Eq. (
15
) of the probe channel. Here,
\dot{K}_{i}

are the derivatives of the Kraus operators with respect to
\omega
(in what follows, we write explicit
dependencies of the Kraus operators on
t
and
\omega
only if necessary)

Although the first inequality in Eq. (
22
) may not be tight—entangling the probe
and ancilla may increase the achievable QFI—the second is. A valid Kraus
representation of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}
for which the second inequality is saturated
is guaranteed to exist as long as
\dim({\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{A}})\!\geq\!\dim({\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}})

[
18
]
.
In consequence, the CE bound (
22
) directly determines the maximal attainable QFI for the ancilla-assisted parallel scheme
depicted in Fig.
1
(b) with
N\!=\!1

[
22
]
.

Parallel CE bound.

The CE bound (
22
) may be directly applied to upper-bound the
ultimate attainable QFI of any parallel scheme of Fig.
1
(b)
that employs
N
probes and ancillae. In scenario (b),
the final state generally reads

\varrho^{\textrm{(}b)}_{N}(t)\!=\!{\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes N}_{\omega,t}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}(\varrho_{N}^{\textrm{(b)}})
.
Hence, applying the CE bound (
22
) to channel
{\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes N}_{\omega,t}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}

that describes the overall evolution and fixing a tensor product Kraus representation such that

{\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes N}_{\omega,t}(\rho^{N})=\!\!\!\!\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\!\!\!\!K_{i_{1}}\!\otimes...\otimes K_{i_{N}}\;\rho^{N}\,K_{i_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\otimes...\otimes K_{i_{N}}^{\dagger},

(24)

one obtains the
parallel CE bound

[
18
,
21
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(b)}}_{N}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\varrho_{N}^{\textrm{(b)}}}\,\mathcal{F}\Big({\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes N}_{\omega,t}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}(\varrho^{(\textrm{b})}_{N})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 4N||\alpha(t)||+4N(N-1)||\beta(t)||^{2},

(25)

where
\varrho_{N}^{\textrm{(b)}}\!\in\!{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{S}}^{\otimes N}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{A}})
describes
the initial probes-plus-ancillae state and

\beta(t):=\mathrm{i}\sum_{i}\!\dot{K}_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=\mathrm{i}\,\dot{{\bf K}}^{\dagger}\,{{\bf K}}

(26)

which depends solely on Kraus operators and their derivatives.
In order to obtain Eq. (
25
) from Eq. (
22

\dot{K}_{{\bf i}}^{\dagger}\dot{K}_{{\bf i}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(K_{i_{1}}^{\dagger}\otimes...\otimes K_{i_{N}}^{\dagger})\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}(K_{i_{1}}\otimes...\otimes K_{i_{N}})
in Eq. (
22
) contains
N
terms for which in both parenthesis the derivative applies on the Kraus operator acting on the same probe and
N(N-1)
terms where it applies on the Kraus operators acting on two different probes
[
18
]
.
Yet, in contrast to Eq. (
22
), the parallel CE bound (
25
) is then not
generally attainable, even after optimizing Eq. (
25
) over all Kraus representations
of the single-probe channel. This is because the Kraus decomposition for the map

{\mathcal{E}}^{\otimes N}_{\omega,t}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{A}}
that makes Eq. (
22
) saturable
might not be of a tensor-product form, as assumed in Eq. (
24
).

Sequential CE bound.

Now, we would like to apply the CE methods to the two strategies involving control, i.e.,
(c) and (d) of Fig.
1
. However, as argued in Sec.
2
, any
scheme of type (c) employing
N
probes with
m
control steps (each lasting

) can
always be mimicked by a scheme Fig.
1
(d) employing a single probe but lasting

N

and involving more
k\!=\!Nm
steps

\delta t

any

scheme depicted in Fig.

\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(c)}}_{N,m}(t)\Big)\leq\max_{\underset{k=Nm,\,t^{\prime}=Nt}{\textrm{schemes\,(d)}}}\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}_{k}(t^{\prime})\Big)

(27)

where the maximization is over all sequential schemes of type (d)
with step duration
\delta t\!=\!N/m
. Thus, in what follows,
we focus on applying the CE methods to the scenario
of Fig.
1
(d). Note that by decreasing

\delta t
further, or equivalently by raising the number of control steps
k
,
we may only increase the right hand side of Eq. (
27

Hence, the optimal FFQC scheme yielding the maximum in Eq. (
27

must correspond to the limit of

\delta t\!\to\!0
.
It is so, as any FFQC scheme of step duration
\delta t
can be mimicked by a protocol
with shorter

(and any

For sequential strategies employing intermediate control a CE-based upper bound
on the corresponding QFI has recently been derived in Ref.
[

Let the output state for the sequential protocol of Fig.
1
(d) be given as

\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}_{k}(t^{\prime})=\sum_{{\bm{i}}^{(k)}}K_{{\bm{i}}^{(k)}}\!(t^{\prime})\,\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}\,K^{\dagger}_{{\bm{i}}^{(k)}}\!(t^{\prime})

(28)

However, in contrast to Eq. (
24

K_{{\bm{i}}^{(k)}}\!(t)\!:=\!(K_{i_{k}}\!(\delta t)\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textrm{A}})U_{k}\dots(K_{i_{1}}\!(\delta t)\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textrm{A}})U_{1}

have a composition rather than tensor-product structure,
where

acting solely on the probe at the
\ell

An application of the general CE bound (

on the QFI attainable using

sequential scheme with FFQC (with timestep

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}_{k}(t^{\prime})\Big)\leq 4k||\alpha(\delta t)||+

(30)

\displaystyle\qquad+4k(k-1)||\beta(\delta t)||(||\alpha(\delta t)||+||\beta(\delta t)||+1),

which applies to
any

4.2.2
Optimization of the CE bounds

All the CE bounds presented this far:
standard
(Eq. (
22
)),
parallel
(Eq. (
25
))
and
sequential
(Eq. (
30
)); rely solely on the structure of the single-probe channel
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}
encoding the parameter
and its particular Kraus representation (
15
). Thus, in order to obtain the tightest versions of these bounds one
should minimize them over all equivalent Kraus representations,
{\bf K^{\prime}}\!=\!\mathsf{u}{\bf K}
(with
\mathsf{u}^{\dagger}\mathsf{u}\!=\!\mathbbm{1}
),
describing the single-probe dynamics. Although such a gauge freedom is generally parameter dependent, i.e.,

\mathsf{u}\!=\!\mathsf{u}(\omega)
, the operators
\alpha
(
23
) and
\beta
(
26
)
depend solely on
\mathsf{h}\!:=\!\mathrm{i}\,\mathsf{u}^{\dagger}\dot{\mathsf{u}}
. Hence, it always suffices to search only through Kraus
representations satisfying
{\bf K}^{\prime}\!=\!{\bf K}
and
\dot{{\bf K}}^{\prime}\!=\!\dot{{\bf K}}\!-\!\mathrm{i}\mathsf{h}{\bf K}
or,
in other words, any CE-based bound considered may always be optimized by simply performing its minimization over all
Hermitian matrices
\mathsf{h}

[
18
]
.

In contrast to the sequential CE bound (
30
), the standard (
22
) and
parallel (
25
) CE bounds are quadratic (convex) in
\mathsf{h}
, and thus may be minimized
numerically in a systematic manner by means of semi-definite programming (SDP)
given some
{\bf K}

[
21
,
22
]
.
On the other hand, when considering t (or
\delta t
in case of Eq. (
30
))
to be fixed and the asymptotic limit of
N
(
25
) (
Nm
(
27
), or
k
(
30
)),
it is always optimal, if possible (contrary to the unitary case), to set
\beta(t)\!=\!0
in the CE bounds,
so that they
asymptotically
become linear in
N
(
Nm
, or
k
respectively).
In such a regime, as the constraint
\beta(t)\!=\!0
is linear in
\mathsf{h}
, one may always apply
the SDP methods to minimize the remaining coefficient
4||\alpha(t)||
, which
is then emergent in all the bounds
[
21
,
22
]
.

Equivalently, the same argumentation can be made starting from the dynamical matrix
\mathsf{S}
of
the channel
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,t}
, defined in Eq. (
14
), instead of its Kraus representation.
The two are directly related (see also App.
A
), as any valid vector of Kraus operators
{\bf K}

can always be expressed in a complete operator basis, e.g. the Pauli basis in case of a single-qubit
maps, via a matrix
\mathsf{M}
satisfying

\displaystyle{\bf K}=\mathsf{M}{\bm{\varsigma}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{M}^{\dagger}\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{S}^{T}.

(31)

As a result, one may rewrite the operators
\alpha
(
23
) and
\beta
(
26
)
with help of the matrix
\mathsf{M}
and a general Hermitian matrix
\mathsf{h}
to be optimized as

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{\varsigma}}^{\dagger}\,(\dot{\mathsf{M}}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{M}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{h}})\,(\dot{\mathsf{M}}-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{h}}{\mathsf{M}})\,{\bm{\varsigma}},

(32)

\displaystyle\beta(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{\varsigma}}^{\dagger}\,(\dot{\mathsf{M}}^{\dagger}+\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{M}}^{\dagger}{\mathsf{h}})\,{\mathsf{M}}\,{\bm{\varsigma}}.

(33)

In what follows, we always choose
\mathsf{M}\!=\!\mathsf{M}^{\dagger}\!=\!\sqrt{\mathsf{S}}^{T}
and thus
unambiguously fix the starting Kraus representation
{\bf K}
in Eq. (
31
).
We then make use of Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) in order to
analytically
optimize
over
\mathsf{h}
the infinitesimal-timestep version of the sequential CE bound (
30
)
as we now explain.

4.2.3
Infinitesimal-timestep CE bound

In the most powerful FFQC setting of Fig.
1
(d), one allows for the control operations
to be
arbitrarily fast
and thus assumes the steps of the protocol to last an
infinitesimal

duration
\mathrm{d}t
. Hence, one deals then with a sequential strategy of infinitesimal step duration, for which
the sequential bound of Eq. (
30
) can be directly applied after setting
k\!\to\!\frac{t^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}
and taking the limit

\delta t\!\equiv\!\mathrm{d}t\!\to\!0
(see App.
B
).
In order for the bound to remain meaningful, we adjust the derivation of Ref.
[
37
]
in App.
B
,
so that it now reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}_{t^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}t}(t^{\prime})\Big)\leq 4\frac{t^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}||\alpha(\mathrm{d}t)||

(34)

\displaystyle\;\;+4\,\Big(\frac{t^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big)^{2}||\beta(\mathrm{d}t)||\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}}||\alpha(\mathrm{d}t)||\!+\!||\beta(\mathrm{d}t)||\!+\!\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}\right).

We refer to Eq. (
34
) as the
infinitesimal-timestep CE bound
.

In order to derive the ultimate precision bounds valid in the presence of noise and FFQC,
we compute the infinitesimal-timestep CE bound (
34
) after substituting the expressions for
\alpha
and
\beta

given by Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
).
We explicitly minimize Eq. (
34
) over Kraus representations as described in Sec.
4.2.2
, by
expanding the Kraus operators,
{\bf K}(\mathrm{d}t)
, and the Hermitian matrix,
\mathsf{h}(\mathrm{d}t)
, up to small orders of
\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}
.
As we are interested in the regime of
asymptotic resources
, i.e., the limit

t^{\prime}\!\gg\!\mathrm{d}t
(or equivalently
Nt\!\gg\!\delta t
in case of the scheme (c)), we may assume without loss of generality that it is always optimal
to make the second term in Eq. (
34

\beta(\mathrm{d}t)\!=\!0
. We outline the methods we use below,
with detailed calculations deferred to
App.
C
.

Consider the dynamical matrix
\mathsf{S}
, defined in Eq. (
14

{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,\mathrm{d}t}
. Expanding it in
\mathrm{d}t
around

\mathsf{S}(\mathrm{d}t)\!:=\!\mathsf{S}^{(0)}\!+\!\mathsf{S}^{(1)}\mathrm{d}t\!+\!O(\mathrm{d}t^{2})

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,\mathrm{d}t}(\rho)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^{3}\mathsf{S}_{\mu\nu}(\mathrm{d}t)\;\sigma_{\mu}\,\rho\,\sigma_{\nu}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^{3}(\mathsf{S}^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}+\mathsf{S}^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}t)\,\sigma_{\mu}\,\rho\,\sigma_{\nu}+{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2}).

{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega,\mathrm{d}t}(\rho)=\rho+\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{3},\rho]+{\mathcal{L}}(\rho)\right)\mathrm{d}t+{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}^{(0)}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}^{(1)}

\displaystyle={\small\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\bar{\mathsf{L}}_{ii}&\mathrm{Im}\bar{\mathsf{L}}_{23}&\mathrm{Im}\bar{\mathsf{L}}_{31}&\mathrm{Im}\bar{\mathsf{L}}_{12}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}\\

Next, we expand the infinitesimal-timestep CE bound (

in orders of
\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}

We include the full details in App.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho^{\textrm{(d)}}_{t^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}t}(t^{\prime})\Big)

\displaystyle\underset{\mathrm{d}t\to 0}{\leq}4\,\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}\,t^{\prime}\qquad\textrm{with}

(43)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle:=\min_{\mathsf{h}\,\,\textrm{such that}\,\alpha^{(i)}=\beta^{(j)}=0}||\alpha^{(2)}||,

(44)

Note that as the sequential scheme with FFQC is the most powerful one (see Sec.

4.2.4
Universal asymptotic linear bound

Thanks to the infinitesimal-timestep CE bound (
34
)
yielding Eq. (
43

we may formulate a general observation about the scaling of the QFI
applicable to any of the schemes depicted in Fig.
1

Result 2

For all noise processes described by Eq. (
6

except rank-one Pauli noise-types that are not parallel to
the parameter-encoding Hamiltonian, the QFI in any scheme
of Fig.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}\leq 4\,\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}\,t^{\prime},

(45)

where
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
is a constant that solely depends on the
particular form of the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
defined in Eq. (
6
),
while
t^{\prime}
is the effective protocol time as defined via the most powerful
scheme (d) with FFQC in Fig.
1
.

We explicitly prove Res.
2
in App.
C
, but outline the derivation here.
In App.
C.1
, for any qubit Liouvillian

\mathcal{L}
of Eq. (
6
), except the rank-one Pauli noise of (
7
) with
\sigma_{\bf n}\neq\sigma_{3}
, we give the form of the hermitian matrix
\mathsf{h}
for which
\alpha^{(0)}\!=\!\alpha^{(1)}\!=\!\beta^{(0)}\!=\!\beta^{(1)}\!=\!\beta^{(2)}\!=\!\beta^{(3)}\!=\!0
. Hence, there exists some finite
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
for which (
43
) holds.
We first show that dephasing noise, as well as any rank-one noise which is not of Pauli type,
imposes a linear scaling of the QFI due to Eq. (
43
). Next, we consider noise processes described by
Liouvillians whose rank is strictly greater than one. Any such noise process corresponds to a matrix representation of the Lindblad superoperator (
6
)
that may be written as a direct sum of matrices of lower rank. We show that the QFI of noise processes of
rank greater than one is always smaller than the QFI of any of its orthogonal components. Thus, the only rank-two noise
processes that, in principle, could still allow for quadratic scaling of the QFI are the ones corresponding to a direct sum of
two orthogonal rank-one Pauli noises.
For this case, however, we explicitly show that the QFI scales linearly with
t^{\prime}
.
Moreover, this also settles the case of the rank-three noise types as any such noise can be expressed as a direct sum that
contains either a rank-one non-Pauli or a rank-two Pauli noise, or both.
Finally, in App.
C.2
we derive explicit forms of
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
for the exemplary noise-types
discussed below.

4.2.5
Exemplary noise-types

Although we have shown the validity of Eq. (

The corresponding CE bounds for dephasing, general rank-one, and rank-two Pauli noise types respectively read

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\textrm{1P}}_{\bf z}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{\prime}}{2\gamma}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\textrm{1G}}_{{\bf r}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{\prime}}{4}\,\frac{\max\{|x+\mathrm{i}y|,|x-\mathrm{i}y|\}^{2}}{(|\mathrm{Re}({\bf r})|^{2}|\mathrm{Im}({\bf r})|^{2}-(\mathrm{Re}({\bf r})^{T}\mathrm{Im}({\bf r}))^{2})}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\textrm{2P}}_{\bf\Omega}

4.3.3
Advantages of FFQC

In summary, we see that FFCQ offers several advantages. First, it allows to modify the noise process particularly rank-one transversal Pauli noise which can be completely removed, while X-Y noise can be transformed to a weaker dephasing noise (dot-dashed line in Fig.
2
). In addition to this, if the number of available ancillary qubits is large FFQC offers the possibility to prolong the short-time dynamics by “parallelizing the evolution” (dotted line in Fig.
2
)—mimicking a parallel evolution with short running time but large
N
and the modified noise. Finally, it also gives the possibility to keep the register of errors that happened during the evolution (dashed line in Fig.
2
). To get a feeling of how the latter improves the QFI, think of the frequentist scenario where for the construction of the final estimator one values more the runs where less or no errors happened (we explore this in more depth in Sec.
6
). In any case, FFQC undoubtedly outperforms the parallel scheme (b) (solid line in Fig.
2
) by a large amount.

We showed how the introduction of FFQC leads to an improved scaling of the QFI. Yet an interesting question is if there exists a gap between the two strategies (c) and (d). If the frequency with which one applies the controls in (c) is limited, i.e. the duration of each step
\delta t
is fixed, it is easy to see that there exists a gap. The bound derived in the App.
D
also applies to this case and yields
\mathcal{F}^{\textrm{(c)}}\leq 4Nm\,\alpha^{\textrm{(b)}}_{\omega,\gamma,p}(\delta t)
, and we just demonstrated that it can be outperformed by FFQC. However, it is not clear if there is a gap between (c) and (d) when
\delta t\!\to\!0
and
t=t^{\prime}/N
. Furthermore, it is not clear these strategies outperform scheme (b) for
t\!\to\!0
with
N=\frac{t^{\prime}}{t}
, as all the CE bounds coincide in this limit. On the other hand, the fact that the CE bounds coincide does not disprove the existence of a gap, as none of the bounds is necessarily tight. Finally, we note that protocol (d) has following important advantage: one can read the error syndrome at any time-step before deciding on the optimal strategy for the future, whereas for protocols (b) and (c) some decisions have to be made beforehand. We will come back to this particular point, and show for a simple phase estimation example how this difference allows to boost the attainable QFI in Sec.
6.2
.

5
Implications on attainable precision in metrology schemes

Hitherto our analysis focused on the scaling of the QFI evaluated on the final
system state of the metrology protocols depicted in Fig.
1
in presence of
general time-homogeneous Liouvillian noise. We now discuss the implications of
FFQC on the precision with which one can estimate the parameter
\omega
.
As already mentioned in Sec.
3.1
, how to
best quantify the estimation precision depends crucially on how information about
\omega
is obtained.
In Sec.
5.1
we consider the implications of FFQC
on precision for the case of asymptotically many repetitions, whereas Sec.
5.2
deals with single-shot estimation.
Regardless of how one chooses to quantify the precision, whenever a noiseless estimation scenario exhibits a
quantum improvement in precision scaling then this improvement may always be maintained in the presence of rank-one Pauli noise by employing the FFQC protocol described in Sec.
4.1
. We note again that the ability of our scheme to ameliorate for any rank-one Pauli noise finds applications beyond metrology, i.e., in the design of high fidelity gates, or indeed any other protocol where such noise terms may appear.

The achievable precision in estimating
\omega
in any metrological protocol is dictated by the resources at hand.
In what follows we shall consider two different ways of quantifying the resources of any metrological protocol: time-particles
and number of probes. In the
time-particles
approach the total resource
per experimental run
is defined
as the product between the number of probes
N
and the protocol duration
t
. Thus, for all the types of protocols depicted
Fig.
1
the time-particle resource is equal to
t^{\prime}=Nt
.
In the
number of probes
approach one is primarily interested in how estimation precision scales with
N
only.
In particular, this is how resources are defined in
frequency estimation

[
15
,
19
,
22
,
38
]
,
where the time for each experimental run,
t
, is bounded from above so that one can consistently consider the limit of many
experimental repetitions by letting the overall experiment last sufficiently long.
In all cases we show how our results yield noise dependent upper bounds on the attainable precision and restrict it to a classical scaling in terms of the resource.

5.1
Precision in the presence of free repetitions

We now consider precision bounds for estimating parameter
\omega
using FFQC for the case where the protocol is repeated
asymptotically many times so that CRB is applicable. We first consider the time-particles picture
before moving onto the scenario in which the number of probes
N
is the resource.

5.1.1
Time-particles

Given
\nu
repetitions of the protocol and using the CRB, we bound the ultimate MSE attainable by the most general FFQC
strategy in the presence of rank-one Pauli noise as

\displaystyle\delta\omega^{2}\,\nu

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-({\bf n}^{T}{\bf z})^{2})}\,\frac{1}{{t^{\prime}}^{2}}.

(47)

On the other hand, stemming from Eq. (
45
) the MSE for all other noise processes may be lower-bounded
as follows

\displaystyle\delta\omega^{2}\,\nu

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}}\,\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}.

(48)

Yet, in contrast to Eq. (
47
), Eq. (
48
) is not guaranteed to be achievable in the limit

\nu\to\infty
.

5.1.2
Frequency estimation

We now turn to the setting of frequency estimation in which
the resource of interest is the number of probes
N
.
Notice that the FFQC protocol in this case is the one depicted in Fig.
1
(c). Yet, its QFI
can always be upper-bounded by that of scheme (d) after setting
t^{\prime}\!=\!Nt
. In order to ensure a large number of
repetitions we fix the time of a single run
t
and demand that the total time
T\!:=\!\nu t\gg t
. Hence, we can
again use the CRBs of Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
) and obtain the precision bounds:

\displaystyle\delta\omega^{2}\,T

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-({\bf n}^{T}{\bf z})^{2})}\frac{1}{N^{2}t},

(49)

\displaystyle\delta\omega^{2}\,T

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}}\frac{1}{N}.

(50)

Indeed, Eq. (
49
) proves that HS is achievable for rank-one Pauli noises. On the other hand,
for all other noise processes, thanks to Eq. (
45
) being linear in
t^{\prime}
, the bound of Eq. (
50
)
is independent of the single-run duration
t
. This suggests that FFQC allows us to indefinitely maintain the ultimate precision
normally exhibited only at very short time scales (see Sec.
4.3
).

5.2
Single-shot precision bounds

We now consider precision bounds applicable in the single-shot scenario, i.e., when
\nu\!=\!1
. As a result we are forced to consider
the
AvMSE
as the figure of merit, where the average is with respect to the probability distribution

p_{0}(\omega)
describing our prior knowledge about the parameter. The
AvMSE
can then be lower-bounded with the help of
BCRB and ZZB (see Sec.
3.1
). However, note that the FFQC-assisted sequential strategies of Fig.
1

sufficiently large
t^{\prime}
also incorporate all many repetition protocols. This is because one can always set the intermediate

6
Importance of quantum control for metrology with limited resources

In this section we further analyse the benefits of continuous quantum control for metrology in presence of noise that can not be completely removed. In addition, we adopt a practical perspective where the resources available for the implementation of the scheme are severely limited. This allows us to keep the analysis simple, but also makes the proposed protocols easily implementable in real experiments. In Sec.
6.1
to cope with an unbalanced X-Y noise we propose a simple FFQC scheme that only requires one sensing and one ancillary qubit, but can still outperform a parallel scheme with a large number of entangled probes and ancillae (but no continuous quantum control). In Sec.
6.2
we show that also in the case of phase estimation the possibility to perform intermediate control (applied between the two successive applications of the channel on the probe qubits as shown in Fig.
6
) leads to an improvement in the attainable QFI.

6.1.2
Simple sequential strategy

For the sequential strategy, all the probe plus ancilla pairs behave independently, and since the QFI is additive in this case in order to establish
the global performance it is sufficient to consider a single pair up to the first measurement in Fig.
3
(s).
We denote the state of a probe-plus-ancilla at any time by
\varrho(t)\!\in\!{\mathcal{B}}(\mathds{C}^{2}\!\otimes\!\mathds{C}^{2})
.

The maximal QFI rate per probe then reads

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}^{\textrm{(s)}}\!:=\!\max_{t}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\varrho(t))}{t}=\frac{\mathcal{F}(\varrho(t_{\textrm{opt}}^{\textrm{(s)}}))}{t_{\textrm{opt}}^{\textrm{(s)}}}

(58)

with
t_{\textrm{opt}}^{\textrm{(s)}}
being now determined solely by the noise.

The sequential FFQC strategy was already sketched in Sec.
4.3.2
and is formally described in Sec.
D.1
. It consists of continuously checking if the probe+ancilla state is in the code
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{C}}
or the error subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{\textrm{E}}
. At each time step the probability of detecting an error is
\frac{\gamma\mathrm{d}t}{2}
, and if an error is detected the most probable error term, say
\sigma_{1}
, is canceled by applying the same unitary on the probe
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{1}=\mathbbm{1}
. This maps the state back into the code space but slightly degrades it with a residual dephasing coming from the other noise term
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{3}
.

As one keeps track of the number of errors
m
that happened during such an FFQC assisted evolution, but disregards the exact times the errors occurred, the final state at time
t
possesses a direct-sum structure

\bar{\varrho}(t)\!=\!\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}p(m;t)\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t).

(59)

The probability of errors follows a Poissonian distribution
p(m;t)\!=\!\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t/2}(\gamma t/2)^{m}/m!
, while the state
\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t)
conditional to the detection of
m
errors is given in (
163
).

The corresponding QFI may be straightforwardly evaluated:

\mathcal{F}(\bar{\varrho}(t))=\sum_{m}p(m;t)\mathcal{F}\big(\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t)\big)=t^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t\,2p(1-p)},

(60)

and yields the following maximal QFI rate per probe (
58
):

\mathfrak{f}^{\textrm{(s)}}=\frac{1}{2\gamma\mathrm{e}}\frac{1}{p(1-p)},

(61)

which is attained after optimally setting
{t_{\textrm{opt}}^{\textrm{(s)}}}\!=\!1/(2\gamma p(1-p))
.

In the regime of long experimental duration
T/t_{\textrm{opt}}^{\textrm{(s)}}\!\gg\!1
that we are interested in,
the CRBs (
55
) for the two strategies Fig.
4
(s1)
and Fig.
4
(sN) respectively read:

(s1):

\displaystyle\qquad T\delta^{2}\omega\geq\frac{1}{\mathfrak{f}^{\textrm{(s)}}}

(62)

(sN):

\displaystyle\qquad T\delta^{2}\omega\geq\frac{1}{N\mathfrak{f}^{\textrm{(s)}}},

(63)

and are guaranteed to be attainable.

Before we proceed to the comparison of strategies (s) and (b),
let us briefly comment on the role of the error register, and how it allows to
boost the attainable performance. As we argued in Sec.
4.3.2
discarding the
error register is equivalent to effectively modifying the X-Y noise to a dephasing noise of strength
p\,\gamma
.
Concretely, if the error register is discarded, the state at time
t
is a mere mixture

\bar{\varrho}(t)\!=\!\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p(m;t)\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t)
rather than a direct sum as
in Eq. (
59
). The corresponding maximal QFI rate per probe is
then given by
\mathfrak{f}^{\textrm{(s)}}\!=\!1/(2\gamma\mathrm{e}p)
, which is reduced by a factor
1/(1-p)
as compared
to the strategy including the error register and described by Eq. (
61

When inferring the value of
\omega
after a certain number of runs it is helpful to know how noisy each run was.
This information, contained in the error register, allows to properly ponder the data
obtained in each run in order to optimally construct the global estimator.

\displaystyle\left.+e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}}\left|1\right\rangle_{Q1}(\sqrt{y}\left|11\right\rangle_{Q2,A}+\sqrt{1-y}\left|00\right\rangle_{Q2,A})\right).

This simple example shows that intermediate control (FFQC) allows for an improvement in the achievable accuracy.
Notice that such an advantage can be maintained for any finite number of blocks as one can choose to introduce new sensing

7
Summary and Outlook

We have considered the general limits of quantum metrology where one is, in principle, equipped with a full-scale quantum
computer to assist in the sensing process. We have shown that one can use techniques from quantum error correction to
detect or correct for certain kinds of errors while maintaining the sensing
capabilities of the system. In particular, we discover that the use of FFQC allows to restore the Heisenberg
scaling of precision for all all rank-one Pauli noise processes, except the Pauli noise that is identical to the Hamiltonian,
at the cost of a slowing down the evolution by a constant factor. For all other noise-types, we have shown that the QFI is limited to a linear scaling
with the number of resources. As we demonstrated, this result forbids the Heisenberg scaling of the estimation precision and provides noise dependent upper bounds for all metrological scenarios, local or Bayesian. Remarkably, considering the ultimate metrological scheme (FFQC) actually lead to a simplification of the problem, as in the
limit of short evolution steps
\mathrm{d}t\to 0
the CE method does not require to solve the master equation, this allowed us to obtain fully analytic bounds on the achievable precision for relevant noise processes.

However, even in the cases where FFQC does not allow for full restoration of the Heisenberg scaling, one can achieve a significant improvement
over parallel strategies that operate on limited resources, and do not utilize FFQC. We have demonstrated this for the example of
asymmetric X-Y noise where the improvement over parallel schemes employing the same number of resources but do not
use FFQC can be significant. Moreover, we have put forward simple sequential protocols that make use of only
a single sensing and auxiliary system, but that nevertheless outperform parallel entanglement-based strategies for limited
number of sensing systems. From a practical perspective, the existence of simple practical schemes that operate with only a single sensing and auxiliary system, are of high relevance, and may open the way for practical noisy metrology with a significant quantum advantage.
This demonstrates that fast quantum control and error correction are powerful tools in quantum metrology and may enable
practical sensing with a significant quantum enhancement even in the presence of noise.

An important question one may ask is how our results generalize to higher dimensional systems. Note that in higher dimensions the parameter encoding Hamiltonian may have support only on a subspace of the Hilbert space of the probe system, which may allow for more involved error correcting codes.

Appendix A
Relation between the Kraus representations and the dynamical matrix

Any Kraus operator,
K_{i}\!=\!\sum_{k}\mathsf{M}_{ik}\sigma_{k}
, can be expressed in the operator
basis given by the four Pauli operators in the qubit case, which we write for the vector of Kraus operators as

{\bf K}=\mathsf{M}{\bm{\varsigma}}.

(70)

The action of the channel on the density matrix is expressed in the Kraus representation as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\rho)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}(\sum_{k}\mathsf{M}_{ik}\sigma_{k})\rho(\sum_{\ell}\mathsf{M}_{i\ell}^{*}\sigma_{\ell})

(71)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\ell}(\sum_{i}\mathsf{M}_{\ell i}^{\dagger}\mathsf{M}_{ik})\sigma_{k}\rho\sigma_{\ell}=\sum_{k\ell}(\mathsf{M}^{\dagger}\mathsf{M})_{\ell k}\,\sigma_{k}\rho\sigma_{\ell}.

Imposing the equality with the dynamical matrix representation (
14
) one arrives at

\mathsf{M}^{\dagger}\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{S}^{T}.

(72)

From this expression it follows that all Kraus representation
satisfying
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}=\mathsf{u}\mathsf{M}
(with a unitary
\mathsf{u}
) lead to the
same dynamical matrix and therefore correspond to the same channel. Remark that in general
\mathsf{M}
may not be a square
but a rectangular
n\times 4
matrix, where
n
is the number of Kraus operators in a given representation.
However, any
\mathsf{M}
admits a singular value decomposition of the form

\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{u}\,\mathsf{D}_{n\times 4}\,\mathsf{v}^{\dagger},

(73)

with
\mathsf{u}
– a unitary
n\times n
matrix ,
\mathsf{v}
– a unitary
4\times 4
matrix and
\mathsf{D}_{n\times 4}
– a rectangular
n\times 4
diagonal matrix. Now
\mathsf{M}^{\prime}=\mathsf{D}_{n\times 4}\,\mathsf{v}^{\dagger}
is also a valid Kraus representation, and so is the
4\times 4
matrix
\mathsf{M}^{\prime\prime}=\mathsf{D}_{4\times 4}\,\mathsf{v}^{\dagger}
(where to get to
\mathsf{D}_{4\times 4}
from
\mathsf{D}_{n\times 4}
we either remove all the
n-4
zero lines in the case
n>4
, or add
4-n
zero lines in the case
n<4
). Finally, we chose the Hermitian
4\times 4
matrix

\mathsf{M}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\mathsf{v}\,\mathsf{D}_{4\times 4}\,\mathsf{v}^{\dagger}

(74)

to be our canonical Kraus representation for the channel.

Appendix B
Infinitesimal-timestep CE bound

Here, we prove the infinitesimal-timestep CE bound of Eq. (
34
) that may be applied
also to schemes that incorporate FFQC, i.e., the ones of type (d) in Fig.
1
.
We essentially follow the derivation contained in the Supplementary Material of Ref.
[
37
]
except
the last step, at which we slightly generalize the bound presented therein.

In particular, one should follow the reasoning up to Eq. (S15) of Ref.
[
37
]
, at which a more
general upper bound (for any real
\sqrt{x}
) may be derived via

\displaystyle||\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}iAK_{k}+h.c.||

\displaystyle=||i\sum_{k}(\sqrt{x}\,\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger})A(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}K_{k})-(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}K_{k}^{\dagger})A(\sqrt{x}\,\dot{K}_{k})||

(75)

\displaystyle=||i\sum_{k}(\sqrt{x}\,\dot{K}_{k}+i\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}K_{k}\big)^{\dagger}A(\sqrt{x}\,\dot{K}_{k}+i\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}K_{k})-\lambda\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}A\dot{K}_{k}-\frac{1}{x}K_{k}^{\dagger}AK_{k}||

(76)

Using the triangle inequality and the one stated in Eq. (S13) of Ref.
[
37
]
,
we arrive at a generalized version of Eq. (S15) therein:

||\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}iAK_{k}+h.c.||\leq 2||A||\Big(x||\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}\dot{K}_{k}||+||\sum_{k}\dot{K}_{k}^{\dagger}K_{k}||+\frac{1}{x}\Big),

(77)

which in turn results in the generalization of Eq. (S19):

\mathcal{F}\leq 4\,k||\alpha||+4\,k(k-1)||\beta||\left(x||\alpha||+||\beta||+\frac{1}{x}\right).

(78)

By taking the limit of infinitesimal timesteps
k\!=\!t^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}t
and writing explicitly the time-dependences, we obtain the bound:

\mathcal{F}\leq 4\frac{t^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}t}||\alpha(\mathrm{d}t)||+4\frac{(t^{\prime})^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}||\beta(\mathrm{d}t)||\left(x||\alpha(\mathrm{d}t)||+||\beta(\mathrm{d}t)||+\frac{1}{x}\right),

(79)

whose tightest form we would like determine in the
\mathrm{d}t\!\to\!0
limit. Hence, as otherwise Eq. (
79
) must diverge, we can restrict
to Kraus representations for which

\alpha(\mathrm{d}t)=\alpha^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t+{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{3/2})
and

\beta(\mathrm{d}t)=\beta^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t+\beta^{(3)}\mathrm{d}t^{3/2}+{O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2})
.
As we are willing to show a linear scaling of the QFI in
t^{\prime}
due to vanishing of the second term in Eq. (
79
), we set
x=1/\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}

and compute the Taylor expansion:

\mathcal{F}\leq 4\,t^{\prime}\left(||\alpha^{(2)}||+{O}(\mathrm{d}t)\right)+4\,t^{\prime 2}\left\|\frac{\beta^{(2)}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\beta^{(3)}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}}+{O}(1)\right\|\left[\left(||\alpha^{(2)}||+1\right)\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}+{O}(\mathrm{d}t)\right].

(80)

Thus, if we are able to find a Kraus representation for which both
\beta^{(2)}=0

and
\beta^{(3)}=0
, the above expression does not diverge in the
\mathrm{d}t\!\to\!0
limit
but rather provides the desired upper bound—Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\leq 4\,t^{\prime}||\alpha^{(2)}||\qquad\text{with}\quad\beta={O}(\mathrm{d}t^{2}),

(81)

which importantly imposes the SQL-like scaling due to its linearity in
t^{\prime}
.

D.1
Simple FFQC strategy

Let us carefully explain the sequential FFQC strategy used in Sec.
4.3.2
and Sec.
6.1.2
, which
is inspired by the strategy of Sec.
4.1
for perpendicular dephasing
[
28
]
.
Recall that neglecting the higher orders in
\mathrm{d}t
the probe-plus-ancilla state after an evolution of
infinitesimal duration can follow two possible branches, see Eq. (
17
):

\displaystyle\varrho(t+\mathrm{d}t)=\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)+\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(t+\mathrm{d}t).

(159)

Either no error happened
\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)=(1-\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t)\varrho(t)-\mathrm{i}\,\frac{\omega}{2}\mathrm{d}t[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1},\varrho(t)]

and the parameter was imprinted on the state or with probability

\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t
an error occurred
\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)=\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t\big(p\,(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho(t)(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})+(1-p)(\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho(t)(\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\big)
. If in addition the state at time
t
belongs to the
code space

\varrho\in{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{C}})}
with
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{C}}=\textrm{span}(\left|00\right\rangle,\left|11\right\rangle)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{E}}=\textrm{span}(\left|01\right\rangle,\left|10\right\rangle)
, the two branches of the infinitesimal
evolution can be distinguished in a non-demolition manner by projecting onto the code the error spaces
via a parity measurement (since
\varrho_{\textrm{C}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{C}})
and

\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{E}})
). If the state happens to be in the correct branch we simply leave it alone, wheres if an error is detected our strategy consists of mapping the error branch back into the code space by correcting for the most probable error term that we assume to be
\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{1}
(
0\!<\!p\!\leq\!1/2
) without loss of generality. Thus, after the correction step the error branch
reads
\bar{\varrho}_{E}(t+\mathrm{d}t)=(\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho_{\textrm{E}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)(\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbbm{1})
with

\displaystyle\bar{\varrho}_{\textrm{E}}(t+\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathrm{d}t\big((1-p)\varrho(t)+p(\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\varrho(t)(\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\big)+O(\mathrm{d}t^{2}).

(160)

Note that here we assume that the control steps are performed fast enough, so that Eq. (
160
) holds up to first order in
\mathrm{d}t
5
5

5

Note that the control steps need not be ultra fast, just sufficiently fast so that the first order approximation holds. The duration of these control-pulses depends on the strength of the decoherence process.
.
Importantly, after the correction the final state belongs to the code space again
\bar{\varrho}(t+\mathrm{d}t)\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{C}})
,
and we can continue running the strategy. Another important point is that we keep track of the number of errors that have been detected before a time
t
in a classical error register. This can be made explicit by introducing an error register mode R initialized in the state
\left|0\right\rangle_{\textrm{R}}
for each probe qubit, and a Hermitian operator that increases its count by one
R_{+}\left|m\right\rangle_{\textrm{R}}=\left|m+1\right\rangle_{\textrm{R}}
. For example the register can be modelled by a bosonic mode
a
with
R_{+}=\frac{1}{a^{\dagger}a}a^{\dagger}
. Such a FFQC strategy leads to an effective evolution of the probe+register system (the ancilla does not play a role at this stage) given by the master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\varrho_{\omega}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}[\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textrm{R}},\varrho_{\omega}(t)]+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(-\varrho_{\omega}(t)+\frac{\gamma}{2}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes R_{+})\big((1-p)\varrho_{\omega}(t)+p(\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textrm{R}})\varrho_{\omega}(t)(\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textrm{R}})\big)(\mathbbm{1}\otimes R_{+})\right).

(161)

For a single probe initialized in a state
\left|+\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\binom{1}{1}
(or
\frac{\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}
if we remember the ancillary qubit) the final state resulting from such an effective evolution after time
t
reads

\varrho_{\omega}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p(m;t)\,\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t)\otimes\left|m\right\rangle\!\left\langle m\right|_{\textrm{R}},

(162)

where the total number of errors
m
detected during time
t

follows the Poissonian distribution
p(m;t)\!=\!\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t/2}(\gamma t/2)^{m}/m!
, and the state of the probe conditional on the occurrence of
m
errors is

\bar{\varrho}_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&(1-2p)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\\
(1-2p)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}&1\end{array}\right).

(163)

Appendix E
Ziv-Zakai precision bound and the QFI

Consider the parameter
\omega
with prior distribution
p_{0}(\omega)
. The state at time
t
is denoted
as
\rho_{\omega,t}
.
The Ziv-Zakai bound on the
AvMSE
of the parameter reads
[
48
]

\langle\delta\omega^{2}\rangle\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!d\tau\tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!d\omega\min[p_{0}(\omega),p_{0}(\omega+\tau)]\text{Pr}_{e}(\omega,\omega+\tau),\\

where
\text{Pr}_{e}(\omega,\omega+\tau)
is minimum error probability of the binary hypothesis testing between
\omega
and
\omega+\tau
. Quantum mechanically it is given by the trace distance
[
48
]

\displaystyle\text{Pr}_{e}(\omega,\omega+\tau)\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-D[\rho_{\omega,t},\rho_{\omega+\tau,t}])

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{2}||\rho_{\omega,t}-\rho_{\omega+\tau,t}||_{1}).

(186)

We use the triangle inequality to decompose the trace distance in elementary steps

D[\rho_{\omega}^{T},\rho_{\omega+\tau}^{T}]\leq\min\Big(1,\int_{\omega}^{\omega+\tau}\!\!\!\!D[\rho_{\tau^{\prime},t},\rho_{\tau^{\prime}+d\tau^{\prime},t}]d\tau^{\prime}\Big),

(187)

and the local expansion of the trace distance
D[\rho_{\tau^{\prime},t},\rho_{\tau^{\prime}+d\tau^{\prime},t}]\leq d\tau^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(\rho_{\tau^{\prime},t})}}{2}
we rewrite the error probability as

\text{Pr}_{e}(\omega,\omega+\tau)\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-\min\!\Big(1,\int_{\omega}^{\omega+\tau}\!\!\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(\rho_{\tau^{\prime},t})}}{2}\Big)

(188)

Now we are in position to plug in the bound for the QFI, as we showed for the SQL Liouvillians the QFI of the state at time
t
satisfies
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}\leq 4\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}t
, such that the error probability fulfills

\text{Pr}_{e}(\omega,\omega+\tau)\geq\frac{1}{2}\max(0,1-\tau\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}t}).

(189)

And the Ziv-Zakai bound reads

\langle\delta\omega^{2}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}t}}}\!\!\!\!d\tau\tau(1-\tau\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}t})\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!d\omega\min[p_{0}(\omega),p_{0}(\omega+\tau)]

Which in the case
t\gg 1
leads to

\langle\delta\omega^{2}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{12\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}t},

(190)

for any prior which respects the regularity condition
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\;\min[p_{0}(\omega),p_{0}(\omega+\tau)]\to 1
.

Appendix F
Strategy for noiseless frequency estimation

In the context of phase alignment it is known
[
7
]
that the state minimizing the cost-function
\sin^{2}(\frac{\varphi}{2})

is given by

\displaystyle\left|\Psi\right\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{M+2}}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sin(\frac{\pi(n+1)}{N+2})\left|n\right\rangle,

(191)

where
\left|n\right\rangle
is the symmetric eigenstate of the total spin
J_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_{3}^{(k)}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\frac{2n-N}{2}
. We call the rotated state
\left|\Psi_{\varphi}\right\rangle=e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi J_{z}}\left|\Psi\right\rangle
. For the measurement given by the covariant POVM
\{|\theta\rangle\langle\theta|\}
with
\left|\theta\right\rangle=(N+1)^{-1/2}\sum e^{\mathrm{i}\theta n}\left|n\right\rangle
and the state
\left|\Psi_{\varphi}\right\rangle
we denote the probability to observe an outcome
\theta
by

\displaystyle p(\theta-\varphi)=p(\theta|\varphi):=|\left\langle\theta|\Psi_{\varphi}\right\rangle|^{2}.

(192)

It has been shown in
[
7
]
that the probability
p(\delta)
in Eq. (
192
) yields a cost

\displaystyle C=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^{2}(\frac{\delta}{2})p(\delta)d\delta=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}

(193)

in the regime of large
N
. Note that the cost-function
\sin^{2}(\frac{\delta}{2})
is appropriate in the context of phase estimation where the estimated parameter is defined a circle
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
. But in the context of frequency estimation the parameter
of interest is defined on the whole line
\omega\in(-\infty,\infty)
(similar for the resulting phase
\varphi=\omega t
), and the appropriate cost-function is the variance (MSE). We show below how it can be lower bounded from the value of
C
.

Using the fact that
\delta^{2}\leq\sin^{2}(\frac{\delta}{2})\pi^{2}
on the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
we get a bound on the second moment.

V=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\delta^{2}p(\delta)d\delta\leq\pi^{2}C=\frac{\pi^{4}}{4N^{2}}.

(194)

On the other hand, one can also upper-bound the tail probabilities from the cost
C

T(r)=\int_{r\pi}^{\pi}p(\delta)d\delta\leq\frac{C}{2\sin^{2}(r\pi/2)}

(195)

The average mean square error over all possible outcomes of the covariant POVM is given by

\displaystyle\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,p(\theta)\int d\varphi(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}_{\theta})^{2}p(\varphi|\theta)

(196)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\int d\varphi(\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}_{\theta})^{2}p(\theta-\varphi)p_{0}(\varphi),

(197)

where
p_{0}(\varphi)
is the prior knowledge and
\tilde{\varphi}_{\theta}
is the optimal estimator of the phase given the measurement outcome
\theta
. Choosing an explicit estimator
\tilde{\varphi}_{\theta}=\theta
(which might not be optimal for the MSE) and assume

\displaystyle p_{0}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\pi r}\quad&|\varphi|\leq r\pi\\
0\quad&|\varphi|>r\pi.\end{cases}

(198)

This implies

\displaystyle\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\int d\varphi(\varphi-\theta)^{2}p(\theta-\varphi)p_{0}(\varphi)

(199)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi r}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\,\int_{-r\pi}^{r\pi}d\varphi(\varphi-\theta)^{2}p(\theta-\varphi).

(200)

Now we can change the integration variables to
\Sigma=\varphi+\theta
and
\Delta=\varphi-\theta
. The domain of integration then is a rotated rectangle, but since the integrand is positive we can enlarge the domain to the minimal square that contains it without lowering the integral. This implies

\displaystyle\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi r}\int_{-(1+r)\pi}^{(1+r)\pi}\int_{-(1+r)\pi}^{(1+r)\pi}\frac{d\Sigma d\Delta}{2}\Delta^{2}p(\Delta)

(201)

\displaystyle=\frac{r+1}{2r}\int_{-(1+r)\pi}^{(1+r)\pi}\Delta^{2}p(\Delta)d\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{r+1}{2r}\left(V+2\int_{\pi}^{\pi(1+r)}\Delta^{2}p(\Delta)d\Delta\right).

Finally, because
p(\Delta)
is periodic, the second term in brackets in the last expression is upper-bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{\pi}^{\pi(1+r)}\Delta^{2}p(\Delta)d\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\pi^{2}(1+r)^{2}\int_{(1-r)\pi}^{\pi}p(\Delta)\,\mathrm{d}\Delta\;=\pi^{2}(1+r)^{2}\,T(1-r)

(202)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\frac{\pi^{2}(1+r)^{2}}{2\sin^{2}((1-r)\pi/2)},

(203)

which yields

\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle\leq C\,\frac{(r+1)\pi^{2}}{2r}\left(1+\frac{(1+r)^{2}}{\sin^{2}((1-r)\pi/2)}\right).

(204)

Now, minimizing the above bound over
r
and substituting for the cost C according to Eq. (
193
), we obtain

\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle\leq C\,\kappa\,\pi^{2}\leq\frac{\pi^{4}\kappa}{4}\frac{1}{N^{2}},

(205)

which is attained for
r_{\textrm{opt}}\approx 0.33
that gives
\kappa\approx 6.74
.

It remains to show how the above bound applies
within the frequency estimation scenario. Let us assume the prior distribution of the frequency parameter to also have an interval form:

p_{0}(\omega)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\omega_{0}}\quad&\text{if}\quad|\omega|\leq\omega_{0}\\
0\quad&\text{if}\quad|\omega|>\omega_{0}.\end{cases}

(206)

Remark that if the interval is not centred at zero, it can be always shifted be means of FFQC. Given a total resource
t^{\prime}=N\,t
, we can construct a circuit that is equivalent to running
N=\frac{t^{\prime}\omega_{0}}{\pi r_{\textrm{opt}}}
parallel qubits prepared in the state
(
191
) for a time
t=\frac{r_{\textrm{opt}}\pi}{\omega_{0}}
. This choice maps the frequency estimation task on the estimation of the phase
\varphi=\omega t=\omega\frac{r_{\textrm{opt}}\pi}{\omega_{0}}
for the prior defined in Eq. (
198
) with
r=r_{\textrm{opt}}
, which we have solved above. Consequently, the MSE for the frequency reads

\langle\delta\omega^{2}\rangle=\frac{\langle\delta\varphi^{2}\rangle}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{\pi^{4}\kappa}{4}\frac{1}{t^{2}\,N^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{4}\kappa}{4}\frac{1}{(t^{\prime})^{2}}.

(207)

However, in contract to the case of phase estimation, note that if the evolution is slowed down—as it is for the rank-one Pauli noise—the time
t
(and, hence,
t^{\prime}
) has to be
set longer, what affects the frequency MSE above.
