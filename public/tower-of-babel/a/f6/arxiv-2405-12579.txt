Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction
Guangyao Lu, Yulin Liu *

Abstract
Fact-checking based on commercial LLMs has become mainstream. Although these methods offer high explainability, it falls short in accuracy compared to traditional fine-tuning approaches, and data security is also a significant concern. In this paper, we propose a self-instruction based fine-tuning approach for fact-checking that balances accuracy and explainability. Our method consists of Data Augmentation and Improved DPO fine-tuning. The former starts by instructing the model to generate both positive and negative explanations based on claim-expdence pairs and labels, then sampling the dataset according to our customized difficulty standards. The latter employs our proposed improved DPO to fine-tune the model using the generated samples. We fine-tune the smallest-scale Llama-7b model and evaluate it on the challenging fact-checking datasets FEVEROUS and HOVER, utilizing four fine-tuning methods and three few-shot learning methods for comparison. The experiments demonstrate that our approach not only retains accuracy comparable to, or even surpassing, traditional fine-tuning methods, but also generates fluent explanation text. Moreover, it also exhibit high generalization performance. Our method is the first to leverage self-supervised learning for fact-checking and innovatively combines contrastive learning and improved DPO in fine-tuning LLMs, as shown in the experiments.

Introduction

Fact verification, also called fact-checking, aims at determining the veracity of claims based on multiple reliable pieces of evidence
(
Glockner et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2022
)
. It is an important task for combating the spread of misinformation on social media platforms
(
Vosoughi et al. 2018
;
Botnevik et al. 2020
)
.

Most existing fact-checking methods are based on fine-tuning pre-trained models to achieve multi-class classification models
(
Chiang et al. 2022
)
. These methods take claims and evidence as input, which is undergone highly customized preprocessing
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2022
)
, and produce probability distributions of claim veracity. Although these works achieved excellent results, the implementation methods themselves are inherently unable to meet the requirements of trustworthy fact-checking, as highlighted by
Pan et al. 2023
: The model should not only predict the veracity of the claim but also provide a clear explanation of its reasoning process to help users understand and trust the results.

Recently, some researchers have focused their attention on large language models(LLMs)
(
Naveed et al. 2023
)
such as ChatGPT
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, to implemented explainabe fact verification
(
Pan et al. 2023
;
Zhang and Gao 2023
)
. These methods follow the following process: Firstly, they decompose complex claims into simpler sub-claims that exhibit parallel or hierarchical relationships. Secondly, they adopt a question-and-answer approach, leveraging the intelligence of LLMs and relevant evidence, to systematically verify each sub-claim. Finally, based on the collective results of these verifications, they provide an overall assessment of the truthfulness of the complex claim.

These methods leverage the intelligence of large language models to their fullest extent, breaking the limitations of existing approaches and achieving remarkable results in few-shot and zero-shot. However, these methods also face challenges:

•

Uncontrollable cost: These methods decompose the evidence gathering task into independent or dependent sub-tasks for fine-grained reasoning, and as the granularity decreases, the size of the sub-tasks increases as well, which means a higher cost in interface invocation.

•

Overlooking the potential of open-source models: Existing methods directly utilize commercial models like ChatGPT, which raises concerns about data privacy. Moreover, there is currently limited research on transferring the inference ability of ChatGPT to smaller open-source models such as Llama-2-7b
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, a much smaller model with 7 billion parameters(we refer to Llama-2-7b as Llama2 for the sake of convenience), or improving the inference abilities of Llama2.

To address these issues, we first propose a question: Can we leverage open-source LLMs, such as Llama2, to perform effective fact-checking? Considering that existing research
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
point out that large language models excel at challenging tasks but may exhibit illusions on simpler tasks, and they act as a filter for difficult problems
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
, we believe that judging claim veracity is a simple problem, while providing relevant explanations is a difficult problem, as it requires capturing causal relationships in addition to generating explanatory content. Therefore, we attempt to provide claims, evidence and labels to the LLMs and ask it to provide reasonable explanations. Experimental results show that in most cases, the model can generate reasonable text to explain the contradictions between claims and evidence, even without adopting a decomposition approach, as demonstrated in Table
1
. Furthermore, we consider jointly modeling the label of a data and the generated explanations, employing self-instruction
Wang et al. 2023
and counterfactual data augmentation
Zhu et al. 2023
to finetune Llama2, to enhance the inference ability of the model. We also adopt PEFT
Mangrulkar et al. 2022
using an improved DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
to fine-tune Llama2. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a two-dimensional data augmentation approach that involves counterfactual data augmentation and generating multiple explanations using self-instruction based method to achieve joint modeling of labels and explanations. These methods effectively expand the available data and make full use of limited data.

•

We propose a improvements method to the DPO algorithm to enhance its performance.

•

We filled the gaps in the research on self-instruction for small open-source models, introducing an efficient fine-tuning method based on DPO that enhances the inference capabilities of Llama2. Experimental results shows that our method achieves both high performance and explainability.

2
Related Work

2.1
Pretrained Language Based

In recent years, fact-checking work utilized pretrained language models to conduct a series of interesting studies focusing on retrieval, reasoning of the fact-checking workflow. For instance,
Sheng et al. 2021
proposed an improvement to information retrieval methods, taking into account the characteristics of events and patterns in fact-checking articles.
Wu et al. 2023
addressed structured evidence such as tables and advocated for joint training of fact verification and the selection of row and column information, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to retrieve information from structured data.
Si et al. 2021
incorporates the perception of topic and stance features and highlights the importance of topic consistency between claims and evidence, as well as the underlying stance of evidence towards the claim.
Zhang et al. 2024
discovered the presence of false correlations in multi-hop fact verification datasets, and proposed Casual Walk to address this problem, which introduces an intermediary inference path between the inputand the outcome, extending the inference path and compelling the model to abandon the use of false correlations as shortcuts.

Despite the excellent work mentioned above, they lack of explainability, and some efforts also focused on addressing this issue.
Shu et al. 2019
proposed a sentence-comment co-attention network to jointly capture interpretable top-k sentences and user comments for detecting fake news.
Hu et al. 2023
introduced ReRead, advocating for dual reviewing to improve the accuracy of retrieving correct evidence. Another viable approach is to adopt an ablation strategy to evaluate the salience of each reasoning path. For instance,
Si et al. 2023
treated the evidence extraction task as a subgraph extraction task, where each subgraph represents a reasoning path from evidence and claims to conclusions. Although the aforementioned methods have improved interpretability to some extent, due to the limitations of their model structures, they only provide interpretable structured information rather than generating human-readable natural language text.

2.2
LLMs Based

With the emergence of tools like ChatGPT
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, fact-checking based on LLMs has become increasingly mainstream. These methods leverage the reasoning and generation capabilities of LLMs to achieve high accuracy while generating human-readable explanatory content. Despite the illusion problem associated with LLMs, they still represent an excellent solution for achieving interpretable factual evidence.

Fact verification based on LLMs is mainly divided into two approaches: Question decomposition and Retrieval Augmented Generation
Lewis et al. 2020
. ProgramFC proposed by
Pan et al. 2023
utilizes contextual learning of LLMs to decompose complex claims into multi-step reasoning programs and then performs step-by-step inference on the claims to arrive at a final conclusion.
Zhang and Gao 2023
introduces a hierarchical prompting method(HiSS) that guides LLMs to explore the sub claim in more depth. Apart from that,
Wang and Shu 2023
proposed FOLK, which made improvements information retrieval by utilizing Google search to retrieve answers to the questions, and finally reintegrates the retrieved information back into the model to improve accuracy.
Chen et al. 2023
divides the retrieval process into two steps. Firstly, a large number of documents are extracted from Bing based on sub-questions posed by LLMs. Then, these documents are refined, summarized, and the most crucial information is extracted.
Li et al. 2023
leverages AI Agent technology
(
Yao et al. 2022
)
for fact-checking, decomposing the process into tasks such as claim decomposition, information retrieval, information extraction, and evidence declaration, and treat them as planning tasks where the model fully determines the plan.

The aforementioned methods based on LLMs are excellent, they require external interfaces from OpenAI, such as text-davinci-003, leading to higher costs and compromises in terms of data security. Different from previous work, our proposed method focuses on improving the inference ability of the model using self-instruction techniques
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
and fine-tuning with small-scale open-source LLMs. This approach effectively avoids the aforementioned issues, as well as combining high accuracy with explainability.

3
Method

In this section, we start by describing the problem, followed by a step-by-step introduction of our proposed method, consisting of two parts: 2D Data Augmentation and Improved DPO Fine-tuning, as shown in Figure
1
. The former is used to generate positive and negative examples, while the improvement method proposed by the latter is used for fine-tuning the model.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given a claim
c
and evidence
k
, we construct a function
I
, to convert
c
and
k
to a prompt
I(c,k)
(see more in section
A
). According to question, a probabilistic generative model
\pi
aims to predict a label
y\in\{\text{Yes, No}\}
, as well as the corresponding explanation
e=\{{e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{n}}\}
, where
n
should be less than or equal to the maximum number of generated tokens:

\displaystyle P(y,e|c,k)=\pi(y,e|I(c,k))

(1)

Unlike previous fact-checking work that based on LLMs, our designed prompt
I
is very simple. It only provides a statement and evidence(during the data augmentation phase, it may include labels and other supplementary information to enhance model performance), as demonstrated in Table
1
. The model is required to check whether there is a contradiction between the statement and evidence and provide a conclusion. Our focus is on fine-tuning the model through self-instruction, aiming to achieve commercial-level performance even with small-scale LLMs.

3.2
2D Data Augmentation

At this stage, we expand the data volume from two dimensions: the data side and the label side. On the data side, we use counterfactual-based data augmentation methods to increase the amount of data. On the label side, we generate multiple explanations as pseudo-labels for supervised model fine-tuning.

Counterfactual Data Augmentation
. Many advanced multi-hop fact verification methods
(
Das et al. 2023
)
demonstrate poor generalization performance on out-of-distribution(OOD) data. In contrast, CDA(Counterfactual Data Augmentation) achieves a generalizable dataset by increasing counterfactual instances. Specifically, this method modifies and edits entities in the text while preserving overall semantic similarity, thereby altering the causal relationships between entities in the sentences. This approach enhances the model’s performance by focusing on logical relationships in the sentences, rather than relying solely on data-driven improvements.

We utilized RACE proposed by
Zhu et al. 2023
to achieve counterfactual data augmentation. This method involves extracting causal features using an explainer, then manipulating the evidence information using an editor. Based on the causal features and manipulated evidence, a generator is used to generate counterfactual claims. The method also provides information on the locations of the manipulated information, which is highly effective for large models to identify contradictions between evidence and claims.

Label Augmentation
. In addition to data augmentation from the data side, we propose a method to augment data from the label side. Our idea stems from observations of LLMs, as shown in Table
1
. The model does not perform well in simple classification tasks, as shown by the varying labels it produces when given the same claim and evidence. On the contrary, the model may excel at complex reasoning tasks, as it has a higher likelihood of generating correct logic when given the correct label. We speculate that this is related to the model’s pretrained strategy, and a similar phenomenon has been observed in the task of event extraction
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
. Therefore, we consider whether we can use the logic generated by the model as pseudo-labels for joint training with the classification labels. This approach ensures both the accuracy of the model’s predictions and its explainability. Hence, we propose the following method to enhance the label side, constructing a joint representation of classification labels and explanations leveraging the reasoning capability of the model itself.

We employ the following four configurations to enhance the label side, preparing for subsequent training tasks:

\displaystyle P(e_{yes}|c,k,y_{yes})=\pi(e|I(c,k,y_{yes}))

(2)

\displaystyle P(e_{false}|c,k,y_{no}=\pi(e_{false}|I(c,k,y_{no}))

(3)

\displaystyle P(e_{tips}|c,k,y,tips)=\pi(e_{tips}|I(c,k,tips,y))

(4)

The first setting involves only inputting a claim
c
and evidence
k
and requires the model to generate a label
y
and an explanation
e
, as shown in equation (
1
), which is used to evaluate the difficulty of data points. The second setting assumes there existing contradiction between the claim and the evidence. We assign the label
y_{yes}
and require the model to output the corresponding explanation
e_{yes}
, as shown in Equation (
2
), while the third setting is the opposite, as shown in Equation (
3
). The fourth setting takes effect only when the data is sourced from a counterfactual subset and there is a contradiction between the claim and the evidence. We provide the editing cues from the counterfactual data as tips to the model, as shown in Equation (
4
). These editing cues indicate which parts of the claim/evidence have been modified, resulting in a contradiction. We require the model to identify the points of contradiction from these editing cues in order to provide more accurate explanations.

Training Data Construction
. At this stage, we construct dataset for DPO training based on the aforementioned work. Our idea is inspired by the high similarity between DPO and contrastive learning(CL)
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, both of which learn through the distributional features of positive and negative samples. However, the difference is that in CL, each training data sample contains multiple positive and negative examples to help the model better capture the data distribution, while the current DPO related dataset
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
only contain one positive and one negative example per sample, which cannot help the model better grasp the data distribution. Therefore, we propose a new strategy of difficulty-based sampling to generate training data, which is outlined below.

For each piece of data, we first generate
k
different results based on setting 1, count the number of correct predictions, denoted as
w
, which helps assess the difficulty of the data. Next, We determine the number of samples
N_{sample}
base on the equation (
5
), where
N_{min}
ensures the minimum number of samples, and
N_{base}
is the based sampling number. Then, we randomly sample one result from both the results generated from Setting 2 and Setting 3. If the sample comes from the counterfactual subset with a label of
“refutes”
, we sample a correct result from Setting 4. We concatenate the prompt, label, and result to generate a positive sample
y_{chosen}
, and a negative sample
y_{rejected}
. This step is repeated for a total of
N_{sample}
times. Finally, all the positive and negative sample pairs are saved to the training dataset for DPO training.

\displaystyle N_{sample}=\max\Big({N_{min},\big\lceil N_{base}*\frac{w}{k}}\big\rceil\Big)

(5)

3.3
Improved DPO Fine-tuning

In this stage, we use DPO to optimize LLMs, and the original loss function of DPO is shown in equation (
6
), where
\pi_{ref}
refers to the basic model,
\beta
is a hyperparameter, and
\pi_{\theta}
represents the model enhanced with the PEFT method with trainable parameters
\theta
injected into each layer of the transformer architecture of the model.

\displaystyle L_{DPO}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{ref})=

\displaystyle-\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{chosen}|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_{chosen}|x)}

\displaystyle-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{rejected}|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_{rejected}|x)}\Big)\Big]

(6)

It is worth noting that we observed a side effects of DPO in our experiments(see details in Section
D
). To address this issue, we introduced two constraint terms
C_{chosen}
and
C_{rejected}
to enhance the loss function, and used two advantage values
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
to respectively emphasize how much
\mathcal{P}(y_{chosen})
has increased and
\mathcal{P}(y_{rejected})
has decreased compared to the original, as shown in equation
7
.

\displaystyle C_{chosen}=-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{chosen}|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_{chosen}|x)}+log(1+a_{1})

\displaystyle C_{rejected}=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{rejected}|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_{rejected}|x)}-log(1-a_{2})

(7)

After introducing the two constraint terms, we formulated it as a constrained optimization problem with inequality constraints:

\displaystyle\mathop{argmin}\limits_{\theta}L_{DPO}

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle C_{chosen}\leq 0

\displaystyle C_{rejected}\leq 0

(8)

We employ the Lagrange Multiplier
(
Bertsekas 2014
)
to convert the aforementioned constrained problem into an unconstrained problem, allowing us to directly minimize the objective function, as shown in Equation (
9
), where
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are the learnable lagrange multipliers corresponding to
C_{chosen}
and
C_{rejected}
, respectively. We multiply the constraint terms by
\beta
and take the logarithm of their sigmoid values to ensure that the scale of the constraint terms is consistent with that of
L_{DPO}
:

\displaystyle Loss=

\displaystyle L_{DPO}+\log\sigma\Big[\beta(\mu_{1}C_{chosen}+\mu_{2}C_{rejected})\Big]

(9)

To ensure that the aforementioned optimization problem satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker(KKT) conditions, we adopt the following approach since deep learning networks are not convex functions. For
\theta
, we update it using gradient descent. As for the lagrange multipliers, we update them as equation (
10
), where
lr_{\mu}
represents the learning rate for the Lagrange multipliers. We adhere to the KKT conditions, which require the constraints
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
to be greater than or equal to 0.

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=max(\mu_{1}-lr_{\mu}*C_{chosen},0)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}=max(\mu_{2}-lr_{\mu}*C_{rejected},0)

(10)

4
Experiments

4.1
Datasets

We evaluate the performance of our model using two datasets, FEVEROUS and HOVER.

FEVEROUS
is proposed by
Aly et al. 2021
, consisting of fact-checking data with various challenges such as Numerical Reasoning, Entity Disambiguation, and Multi-hop Reasoning.

HOVER
was proposed by
Yang et al. 2018
, which serves as a more challenging multi-hop fact-checking dataset, with a maximum hop count of 4. This is attributed not only to the increased number of hops but also to the longer evidence provided, most of which contain distracting information. Appendix
C
presents detailed statistical data for the two datasets.

4.2
Baselines

We employed the following two categories of methods as our baselines:

I
.
Supervised Fine-tuning
: These methods provide binary classification results based solely on the input data to determine if there is a contradiction between the claim and the evidence. We fine-tuned these methods using the full dataset, including counterfactual data, to evaluate the advantages of our approach compared to traditional methods. We selected the following four methods: Bert-FC
(
Soleimani et al. 2020
)
and T5
(
Jiang et al. 2021
)
employ pre-trained models of different sizes for binary fact-checking classification tasks.
RoBERTa-NLI
(
Nie et al. 2020
)
and DeBERTa-NLI
(
Sileo 2023
)
are fine-tuned on other textual entailment tasks. For detailed information, please refer to the appendix.

II
.
Few-shot Learning
: These methods leverage LLMs to generate prediction results along with relevant explanations. This includes commercially available LLMs that are difficult to fine-tune due to their large parameter volume. To tackle this, we adopt a few-shot learning approach by selecting 10 examples from the training data and utilizing their labels and explanations as prompts. This category of methods is employed to assess the performance and interpretability advantages of our model in comparison to large-scale LLMs. We have chosen the following methods for evaluation: FLAN-T5
(
Chung et al. 2024
)
, ChatGPT-3.5
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, and ProgramFC
(
Pan et al. 2023
)
, which translates the reasoning process into a program.

4.3
Experimental Settings

In the data processing stage, we set the maximum token count to 512 to ensure diversity. For the 4 settings mentioned above, we attempted to generate 10 different results for each. During the filtering process, we evaluated the win_rate of the data under setting 1 and used it to determine the sampling probability. This resulted in sampling 148,232 and 221,373 preference data pairs on FEVEROUS and HOVER, respectively.

In the model fine-tuning stage, we used implemented improve DPO based on trl
(
von Werra et al. 2020
)
. We fine-tuned the half-precision model with 2 Nvidia RTX 4090 GPUs, setting the learning rate to 1e-4, warm-up to 200, learning decay rate to 0.999,
\beta
to 0.1,
a_{1}
to 0.1 and
a_{2}
to 0.1. We used a batch size of 20 and trained for 30,000 iterations, employing an early stopping strategy. DeepSpeed was also used to manage memory consumption.

2D Data Augmentation Analysis

Joint training of labels and explaination ensures the explainability

Both counterfactual dataset and sampling strategy improve performance

Finally, we evaluated the impact of the difficulty-based sampling strategy(-sam). 5.2.2
Improved DPO Analysis

Our improvements to the DPO force model to learn correct reasoning and discard incorrect ones
. In this section, we discuss the impact of our improved DPO. Firstly, we assessed whether the value of
advantage
affects model performance. We firstly set the advantage to 0(-adv) and observe a significant decline in performance for HOVER and FEVEROUS. Secondly, we test the original DPO(-lag) on the model and also find a noticeable decrease. It is worth noting that both of the settings control the advantage of positive examples over negative examples in terms of generation probability. The former controls how much improvement positive examples should have in generation probability compared to negative examples, while the latter ensures that the generation probability of positive examples is higher than the lower bound of negative examples. These two ablation experiments indicate that our improved DPO, by incorporating the concept of advantage and the idea of comparison, enables the model to learn correct reasoning logic and improves performance.

Regularization constraints cannot replace lagrange multipliers
. We further conducted a detailed analysis of
advantage
in improved DPO. During the training process, we employed the product of
advantage
and the Lagrange multiplier
\mu
as a constraint term to regulate the magnitude of the loss, as depicted in Equation
9
. The trainable parameter
\mu
allows for the scaling of
advantage
. Upon examining the experimental data during training, we observed that
\mu
frequently scaled
advantage
to 0, as shown in
2
, indicating that only a small fraction of the data points resided on the boundary of the constraint. In other words, our improved DPO primarily impacted a select subset of the data points. We conduct further testing on whether setting
\mu
as a fixed regularization term(-mug) would yield the same experimental results(equivalent to CPO
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
). Experimental results show a significant decrease in model performance on FEVEROUS and HOVER, especially the latter, with a performance drop of 5.82% on f1. This indicates that the method of using a regularization term for constraint cannot replace the effectiveness of the improved DPO. The dynamic scaling ability effectively regulates the role of the constraint term, preventing the problem of unstable values and local optima caused by excessive constraint penalties.

Learning differences between positive and negative examples effectively improves performance
. Finally, we directly replaced DPO with SFT(-dpo), and experimental results show a significant decrease in performance on both the FEVEROUS and HOVER, particularly the latter, which experienced a performance decrease of 25.1%. This reveals the role of negative examples in self-instruction, particularly on challenging datasets like HOVER. It is evident that the model can greatly improve its performance by learning the differences between correct and incorrect reasoning paths through contrastive learning.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

Our work attempts to improve model performance using self-instruction and employs an enhanced dpo approach that combines fine-tuning the model with positive and negative samples. We also conducted a series of experiments to validate the effectiveness of our model. First, the overall experimental results demonstrate the accuracy and effectiveness of our approach. Then the ablation study to individually validate the efficacy of each module. Finally, Generalization performance evaluation, case study and manual evaluation were conducted to verify the model’s generalization ability and explainability. In future work, we hope to implement the following aspects:

Deeper exploration of negative samples in the context of explainable fact-checking.
Our experimental results shows that negative samples contribute to model performance to some extends, and we believe that further investigating the role of negative samples can enhance the performance of LLMs and can be utilized in prompt techniques. Current prompt techniques often employ the idea of COT
(
Wei et al. 2024
)
and RAG
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, while overlooking the importance of learning from negative/incorrect samples.

More comprehensive self-instruction approach.
Our experiments only provided a simple self-instruction scheme while preserving model interpretability. Due to the limitations of open-source LLMs, the model may struggle to generate reliable explanatory content for more complex fact-checking tasks in order to achieve self-instruction. We believe that models like ChatGPT can be utilized to generate interpretable content and facilitate model distillation. When dealing with multi-hop fact-checking tasks, we can fine-tune the model in multiple steps, dividing the task into several stages.

Different Experiment in an open-world setting.
Our work focuses on improving the performance of LLMs given complex evidence, which often contains a large amount of irrelevant information. However, real-world fact-checking tasks involve more complex processes, such as information retrieval. In future work, we aim to apply our method to fact-checking and debunking fake news in an open-world scenario.

7
Limitation

As mentioned in Section
5
, our method has some limitations, which we will now summarize.

Firstly, our method does not address the challenge of suppressing the generation of hallucinatory information by LLMs. As a result, the generated outputs may occasionally contain misleading information, even if the predictions themselves are accurate.

Secondly, although we have carefully designed the training strategy for our model, the self-instruction mechanism itself is relatively simple. This means that the explanatory content generated by the model is entirely controlled by LLMs, leading to a random arrangement of information and hindering the systematic analysis of the generated content by fact-checkers.

Lastly, due to the performance limitations of Llama2, the model may struggle to generate accurate explanations when confronted with complex multi-hop evidence. This is one of the reasons why our method falls short of surpassing traditional methods in terms of accuracy. To address this issue, we aim to mitigate the problem by leveraging the capabilities of commercial models through distillation.
