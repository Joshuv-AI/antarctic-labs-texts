Xiaojing Lin, Zheng Cai, Yunjing Wu, Zihao Li, Fengwu Sun, Xiaohui Fan, Zuyi Chen, Mingyu Li, Fuyan Bian, Yuanhang Ning, Linhua Jiang, Gustavo Bruzual, Stephane Charlot, Jacopo Chevallard

Abstract
The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) provides an unprecedented opportunity for unbiased surveys of H α \alpha -emitting galaxies at z > 4 z>4 with the NIRCam’s wide-field slitless spectroscopy (WFSS). In this work, we present a census of Ly α \alpha escape fraction ( f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} ) of 165 star-forming galaxies at z = 4.9 − 6.3 z=4.9-6.3 , utilizing their H α \alpha emission directly measured from FRESCO NIRCam/WFSS data. We search for Ly α \alpha emission of each H α \alpha -emitting galaxy in the VLT/MUSE data. The overall f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} measured by stacking is 0.090 ± \pm 0.006. We find that f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} displays a strong dependence on the observed UV slope ( β obs \beta_{\rm obs} ) and E( B − V B-V ), such that the bluest galaxies ( β obs \beta_{\rm obs} ∼ − 2.5 \sim-2.5 ) have the largest escape fractions ( f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} ≈ 0.6 \approx 0.6 ), indicative of the crucial role of dust and gas in modulating the escape of Ly α \alpha photons. f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} is less well related to other parameters, including the UV luminosity and stellar mass, and the variation in f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} with them can be explained by their underlying coupling with E( B − V B-V ) or β obs \beta_{\rm obs} . Our results suggest a tentative decline in f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} at z ≳ 5 z\gtrsim 5 , implying increasing intergalactic medium attenuation towards higher redshift. Furthermore, the dependence of f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} on β obs \beta_{\rm obs} is proportional to that of the ionizing photon escape fraction ( f esc , LyC f_{\rm esc,LyC} ), indicating that the escape of Ly α \alpha and ionizing photon may be regulated by similar physical processes. With f esc , Ly ​ α f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha} as a proxy to f esc , LyC f_{\rm esc,LyC} , we infer that UV-faint ( M UV > − 16 M_{\rm{UV}}>-16 ) galaxies contribute > 70 % >70\% of the total ionizing emissivity at z = 5 − 6 z=5-6 . If these relations hold during the epoch of reionization, UV-faint galaxies can contribute the majority of UV photon budget to reionize the Universe.



I
Introduction

The Epoch of Reionization (EoR) represents a phase transition
when the intergalactic medium (IGM) evolved rapidly from mostly neutral to highly ionized
(
Fan et al. 2006
;
Robertson 2022
)
. Although it is generally acknowledged that EoR is completed by
z\sim 5-6

(
Kulkarni et al. 2019
;
Zhu et al. 2021
;
Jin et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, its onset and major contributors remain elusive. Recent measurements on quasar luminosity functions at
z\sim 6
suggest that the quasar population only contributes a negligible fraction (
\lesssim 7-10\%
) of total photons to keep the universe ionized
(
Matsuoka et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2022
)
. Young star-forming galaxies are thought to be the main producers of ionizing photons for reionization. However, one of the biggest uncertainties is whether a vast number of faint galaxies
(
Finkelstein et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
or a small population of bright oligarchs
(
Naidu et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
dominate the cosmic ionizing emissivity during the EoR. To address these questions, extensive studies in the past decades focused on three key quantities observationally: the ionizing photon production efficiency
\xi_{\rm ion}
, the UV luminosity density
\rho_{\rm UV}
, and the escape fraction of Lyman-continuum (LyC) photons
f_{\rm esc,LyC}

(
Robertson et al. 2013
;
Bouwens et al. 2016
)
.

Among the above three elements, the biggest uncertainty is
from the estimates of
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
at high redshifts
(
Vanzella et al. 2010
;
Bian & Fan 2020
;
Naidu et al. 2022
)
. Direct observing the leakage of LyC photons at
z\gtrsim 4

(
Steidel et al. 2018
)
is difficult due to the high opacity of the IGM. Therefore, many works studied
low-redshift galaxies with similar properties
(
Leitherer et al. 2016
;
Flury et al. 2022a
;
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
or indirect tracers of
f_{\rm esc,LyC}

(
Flury et al. 2022b
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Choustikov et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
,
although it is still unclear whether low-redshift analogs can be good representatives of the main contributors to reionization
(
Katz et al. 2020
;
Schaerer et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, and whether those indirect indicators of
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
derived from very small sample could be generalized
(
Katz et al. 2022
;
Saxena et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. The typically required
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
for reionization to end at
z\sim 5-6
is approximately
\gtrsim
5%
(
Robertson et al. 2015
;
Finkelstein et al. 2019
)
, and recent work have constrained the average
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
to be
\lesssim
10% through various indirect methods
(
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Pahl et al. 2021
;
Saldana-Lopez et al. 2023
)
.

To directly constrain the
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
for galaxies at
z\gtrsim 4
,
one of the most promising ways is to study the escape of Ly
\alpha
.
Numerous studies have shown a strong connection between the escape of ionizing photons and
Ly
\alpha
photon
(
Chisholm et al. 2018
;
Marchi et al. 2018
;
Maji et al. 2022
)
. Their escapes are both determined by the distribution and geometry of
neutral gas in the interstellar medium (ISM), and facilitated by optically-thin channels within the gas reservoir
(
Dijkstra et al. 2016
;
Gazagnes et al. 2020
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
. These ionized channels could be carved by feedback from massive young stars, which also modulate the production of LyC and Ly
\alpha
photons
(
Vanzella et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023
)
.

A number of previous efforts have been made to probe the escape of
Ly
\alpha
(
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
) which could be derived by comparing Ly
\alpha
and H
\alpha
emissions.
Before the launch of James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), H
\alpha
of galaxies at
z\gtrsim 5
could be estimated only through the
Spitzer
broadband color
(
Faisst et al. 2019
;
Lam et al. 2019
;
Stefanon et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Hence the measurements of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
in the pre-launch era suffer from large modeling and photometric uncertainties. Improved estimates of the H
\alpha
flux excess have been done recently using JWST narrow band observations
(
Ning et al. 2023
;
Simmonds et al. 2023
)
. Moreover, the spectroscopic capability of JWST in the infrared regime enables us to detect and accurately measure H
\alpha
emission lines in faint, high-redshift galaxies. The JWST Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) can capture both the Ly
\alpha
and H
\alpha
emission of high-redshift galaxies simultaneously and thus quantify
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
(
Saxena et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Napolitano et al. 2024
)
, but a census of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
by NIRSpec is difficult due to the limited sample size it can observe and the trade-off between high sensitivity and high spectral resolution.
The wide-field slitless spectroscopy of JWST/NIRCam
(
Greene et al. 2016
;
Rieke et al. 2023
, WFSS;)
, instead, offers a unique capability to map the spectra of all sources within its field of view (FoV)
(
Sun et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2023
)
, ideal to build a large sample of emission line galaxies through blind spectroscopic surveys.
Combing the H
\alpha
emission lines captured by WFSS with the corresponding Ly
\alpha
lines observed by ground-based facilities, we are able to, for the first time, conduct a direct census of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
with an unbiased sample limited by H
\alpha
flux.

In this work, we utilize JWST/NIRCam WFSS around
4\mu m
to detect H
\alpha
emission over the redshift range of
z\approx 4.9-6.3
, when the Universe was experiencing rapid transition and galaxies were undergoing substantial mass, dust and chemical content assembly
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
;
Davidzon et al. 2017
;
Faisst et al. 2019
)
. The ground-based VLT/MUSE
(
Bacon et al. 2010
)
, working in the full optical domain, perfectly covers their Ly
\alpha
so that we can obtain more precise measurements of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In §
II
we outline the photometric and spectroscopic data used and sample selection based on H
\alpha
emission lines. We describe the measurements of galaxy properties, search for Ly
\alpha
lines, and methods to calculate
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
in §
III
. In §
IV
we present the distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
, its dependence on galaxy properties and redshift evolution. We then investigate the connection between LyC and Ly
\alpha
escape in §
V
, and discuss the implication for reionization. The final summary is presented in §
VI
. We show more details about the analysis and address potential systematics in the Appendix. Throughout this work, a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology is assumed, with
\rm H_{0}=70~km~s^{-1}~Mpc^{-1}
,
\Omega_{\Lambda,0}=0.7
and
\Omega_{m,0}=0.3
.

II.1
.

emitters is presented in §

II.1.1
JWST/NIRCAM Imaging and Slitless Grism in the GOODS-S field

The JWST FRESCO (“First Reionization Epoch Spectroscopically Complete Observations”) Survey
(
Oesch et al. 2023
, GO-1895; PI Oesch)
, covers 62 arcmin
2
in each of the extragalactic legacy GOODS/CANDELS fields with deep NIRCam imaging and wide field slitless spectroscopic (WFSS) observations. In GOODS-South field, FRESCO obtains
4\times 2
pointings with 8
\times
7043 s exposures using the F444W grism, accompanied by
8\times 3522
s exposures of F210M imaging and
11\times 4456
s of F182M imaging. Additional
8\times 934
s exposures of F444W imaging are taken to associate the sliteless spectra with individual objects.

The imaging data were firstly reduced up to
stage2
using the standard JWST pipeline v1.7.2
1
1

1

https://github.com/spacetelescope/jwst
and the calibration reference files “jwst_1014.pmap”. The calibrated single exposures (
*_cal.fits
) were further processed by
Grizli
2
2

2

https://github.com/gbrammer/grizli/
.
Grizli
mitigated 1/f noise, alleviated “snowball” artifacts from cosmic rays, and converted the world coordinate
system (WCS) information in the headers of each exposure to SIP (Simple Imaging Polynomial) format so that exposures can be drizzled and
combined with
Astrodrizzle
. The WCS of each exposure image was registered using GAIA DR3
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
)
and the images were finally drizzled with a pixel scale of 0.031″using
pixfrac=0.8
. An additional background subtraction using
photutils.Background2D
was performed on the final mosaics after masking bright sources.

For the WFSS data, after the standard
stage1
steps, flat-fielding was performed using the flat of direct imaging since the WFSS flat reference files are not available yet in CRDS. 1/f noise is removed only along columns as the spectra are dispersed along rows. The WCS for each individual exposure was assigned using
CALWEBB.assign_wcs
. There were astrometric offsets between this assigned WCS and that of the fully-reduced F444W images. To associate the WFSS spectra and individual galaxies accurately, we calculated the offsets using accompanied short-wavelength exposures (i.e., the F182M and F210M imaging taken simultaneously with the WFSS) and corrected them for each WFSS exposure. These pre-processed files, as well as a source catalog based on the final F444W mosaic, were provided as inputs for
Grizli
for spectral extraction using the updated sensitivity functions and tracing models as described in
Sun et al. 2023
3
3

3

https://github.com/fengwusun/nircam_grism
. To facilitate emission line searching, we performed running median filtering along the dispersion direction on single calibrated grism exposures as described in
Kashino et al. 2023
, and extracted continuum-removed 1D spectra aiming for emitter search only.

II.1.2
HST Imaging in GOODS-S field

The high-level products of the Hubble Legacy Fields
4
4

4

http://archive.stsci.edu/hlsps/hlf

(
Whitaker et al. 2019

II.1.3
Source Extraction and Photometry

We first resampled all HST images onto a fixed grid with a pixel scale of 0.031″. For HST mosaics we constructed empirical PSFs by stacking stars with good SNRs in the fields. For JWST mosaics, the centers of
most bright stars are saturated especially in the F182M and F210M images, so it is hard to build empirical PSFs that can accurately reproduce the complicated PSF shapes with these limited numbers of stars in the final mosaics. Therefore, we adopted the modeled PSFs provided by
WebbPSF
5
5

5

https://github.com/spacetelescope/webbpsf
. We generate point-spread function (PSF) matching kernels to match the PSFs of HST WFC3/IR F160W or JWST/NIRCam F444W, using
Photutils
6
6

6

https://photutils.readthedocs.io/en/stable/psf_matching.html

, with high-frequency noise filters in
Aniano et al. 2011
. All HST ACS/WFC mosaics (F435W, F606W, F775W, F814W), JWST/NIRCam F182M, F210M mosaics were convolved to match the point-spread function (PSF) of JWST/NIRCam F444W with corresponding kernels; all HST WFC3/IR mosaics (F105W, F125W, F140W) were matched to the PSF of F160W.

We ran
Source Extractor

(
Bertin & Arnouts 1996
)
in dual mode for each band, using the F444W mosaics as the detection image. We started with Kron aperture photometry with Kron factors
k=1.2
. We measured the flux uncertainty for each object by randomly positioning the same Kron aperture in empty regions nearby. The measured flux and uncertainties were further corrected to total values by multiplying the ratio of flux inside the
k=2.5
v.s.
k=1.2
Kron apertures on the detection image. Finally, we correct for flux outside the
k=2.5
radii using the PSF of F444W. Note that for HST WFCS/IR bands we homogenized the images to the PSF of F160W, slightly larger than that of JWST/NIRCam F444W, thus we enlarged the aperture size on these HST IR mosaics by a factor of 1.2, which ensures that all the flux from objects would be included as tested on the F160W PSF.

II.2.1
Selecting high-redshift emitters both photometrically and spectroscopically

The F444W WFSS of JWST/NIRCam is used to conduct unbiased surveys of H
\alpha
emitters (HAEs) at
z=5-6
. We performed emission line searching on the 1D continuum-removed spectra first. Among all the emitter candidates, we identified possible high-redshift emitter candidates by applying the following cuts:

(1)

1D emission line with SNR
\geq
4,

(2)

SNR
<2
in F435W,

(3)

F606W-F105W
>
1 and F606W-F182M
>
1

(4)

F182M-F444W
>
-0.1

The second criterion requires a complete 912 Å dropout for
z>4.9
galaxies at F435W. The third criterion describes the Ly
\alpha
break features; we require at least 1 mag drop in F606W that is blueward the wavelength of Ly
\alpha
. The fourth criterion is the F444W excess due to H
\alpha
emission lines when considering possible Balmer jumps. This is the minimum excess for
z=4.9-6.7
mock galaxies in
JAGUAR

(
Williams et al. 2018
)
with H
\alpha

1.6\times 10^{-18}
erg s
-1
cm
-2
(4
\sigma
depth of the FRESCO F444W grism data according to
Oesch et al. 2023

EAZY

(
Brammer et al. 2008

with the
corr_sfhz_13

z
, i.e.
P(z_{p}>4.5)=0
, as low-redshift objects. We finally constructed a
z>4.9

II.2.2
HAE sample

The high-redshift emitter catalog described above comprises all
z>4.9
candidates, including
z>7
[OIII] emitters. Bright [OIII] emitters are easily identified by their doublets at restframe 4959 and 5007 Å. However, as the [OIII] 4959Å lines are
\sim 3
times fainter than the 5007Å, and H
\beta
can be even fainter, in many cases we can only detect a single 5007Å emission while the 4959Å lines remain undetected (see Appendix
A
for more details). Since our science goal is more sensitive to the sample purity
than completeness, we exclude all possible [OIII] candidates based on both photometry and spectroscopy. We firstly assumed the brightest single emission line in the 1D spectra to be 5007Å lines and force-fitted 4959 lines. This would effectively identify [OIII] emitters with marginally-detected 4959 lines. We then further extend the [OIII] sample by photometric cuts as follows:

•

SNR
<2
in both F606W and F775W.

•

F606W-F115W
>
1.7 and F814W-F115W
>
1.7

•

F115W-F210M
<
1.0 and F115W-F210M
<
1.0

•

F814W-F115W
>
(F115W-F210M)+1.5

Similar criteria have been applied in
Endsley et al. 2022
to select
z>6.5
emitters with high [OIII] equivalent width. Note that we do not require F814W to be completely undetected, allowing for Ly
\alpha
transmission spikes within its wide wavelength coverage
(
Kakiichi et al. 2018
)
.

We find 74
possible
[OIII] candidates. 29 of the 74 [OIII] candidates reside in the JADES-DEEP footprint
(
Rieke et al. 2023
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023
)
. Among the 29 sources, 21 sources have photo-
z>7
based on multiple bands of JADES imaging, with [OIII] emission in F444W; 8 of them have photo-
z
of
5-6
with H
\alpha
in F444W. We argue that our criteria for [OIII] could, in most cases, successfully remove
z>7
[OIII] emitters and thus keep an H
\alpha
sample with high purity.

Finally we obtain a sample of 222 HAEs. We identify blended clumpy galaxies with irregular morphology and multiple cores as single objects. These clumpy HAEs have H
\alpha
emission lines that cannot be distinguished on the 2D grism spectra, and some of them have unsolved Ly
\alpha
emission captured by MUSE, even though
Sextractor
may label them as different individuals. During the extraction of grism spectra, we use a modified segmentation map as the input of
grizli
, in which clumpy segments are combined. In our final sample, any two ‘single’ objects are required to be separated from each other
by at least 3.5 kpc (i.e., 0.6 ″at
z=5.5
).
We measure the total H
\alpha
flux on 1D extracted spectra by fitting a ‘Gaussian + constant’ model, where the constant is used to depict the local continuum level around H
\alpha
.

Moreover, we label sources having neighbors within 25 kpc (about
4\arcsec
at
z=5-6
) and
\pm 500
km s
-1
. Due to the significant disparity between the spatial resolutions of MUSE and JWST, we will exclude these galaxies and their close neighbors in the following analysis, since (1) Ly
\alpha
around individual galaxies can be extended to
3\arcsec

(
Wisotzki et al. 2016
)
and will boost Ly
\alpha
in the overlapping regions of close pairs, introducing systematics during the stack of Ly
\alpha
as described in §
III.3.2
, and (2) for very close pairs it is difficult to obtain accurate H
\alpha
flux due to the overlap issue along the dispersion direction of grism. Among all the 222 HAEs, 21 of them have close neighbors. Three of the 222 HAEs are bright point sources with hints as being AGNs. There are 184 HAEs that reside in the MUSE footprints, with 17 having close neighbors and two as potential AGNs. Finally, 165 HAEs are selected for the following studies. The spatial and redshift distributions of our selected sample are shown in Figure

III.1

We infer the physical properties of the 165

\alpha

\alpha

\alpha
simultaneously requires correctly taking the Ly
\alpha
escape process into account and current SED modeling codes cannot do it. We assume a constant star formation history and the
Chabrier 2003
initial mass function (IMF) with an upper limit of 100
{\rm M}_{\odot}
. We adopt flat priors for metallicity,
0.01<{Z}/{\rm Z}_{\odot}<0.2
, and ionization parameter for nebular emission
(
Gutkin et al. 2016
)
in log-space,
-3<\log{U}<-1
, which is the dimensionless ratio of the number density of H-ionizing photons to that of hydrogen. For dust attenuation, we adopt two attenuation curves: the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) dust law
(
Pei 1992
, hereafter SMC)
and the
Calzetti et al. 2000

(ratio of total to selective extinction in V band) being 2.74 and 4.05 respectively. We set the optical depth in the V band varying in the range of 0 – 0.5 in log space. Although a large variety of dust attenuation curves for high-redshift galaxies have been used in the literature
(
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Scoville et al. 2015
;
Reddy et al. 2018
)
, these two are commonly suggested to be applicable and utilized broadly in high-redshift studies
(
Bouwens et al. 2016
;
Shivaei et al. 2018
;
Faisst et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. In the following analysis, we adopt the

The
Beagle
SED fitting results can provide the stellar mass (
M_{\star}
), the effective optical depth at the V band (
\tau_{V}
), the attenuated UV luminosity
L_{\rm UV,att}
and the intrinsic UV luminosity
L_{\rm UV,int}
directly. The UV luminosity is defined at restframe 1600 Å, and the optical depth could be converted to the color excess E(
B-V
) with corresponding attenuation curve. We measure the observed UV slope (
\beta_{\rm obs}
, not corrected for dust) by fitting the stellar continua of the best-fit SED models with a power-law function (
f_{\lambda}=\lambda^{\beta_{\rm obs}}
), spanning from 1260 Å to 2500 Å. The SED-derived UV luminosity and UV slopes are statistically consistent with the fitting results using the photometry directly. We estimate the specific star formation rate (sSFR) for each galaxy with their intrinsic H
\alpha
luminosity and SED-derived
M_{\star}
. The SFR of each galaxy is converted based on
Kennicutt 1998
specialized for the
Chabrier 2003
IMF:

\log{\rm SFR[M_{\odot}~yr^{-1}]}=\log L_{\rm H\alpha}{[\rm erg~s^{-1}]}-41.35.

(1)

Note that the conversion factors between the H
\alpha
luminosity and SFR for high-redshift galaxies vary among different stellar population models and IMFs, and are highly dependent on the ionizing photon production efficiency
\xi_{\rm ion}

(
Theios et al. 2019

. The conversion factor of subsolar-metallicity galaxies is about 2.5 lower than the canonical conversion factors of low-redshift galaxies
(
Reddy et al. 2018
;
Theios et al. 2019
;
Shapley et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Nevertheless, as we focus on the relative relation between

and SFR in the following analysis (see §

\alpha
emitting galaxies.

\alpha
surface brightness, for each galaxy using single or multiple Sérsic models. The effective radius (

11

https://users.obs.carnegiescience.edu/peng/work/galfit/TFAQ.html#effective_radius

\centering\Sigma_{\rm sSFR}={\rm sSFR}/2\pi r_{\rm eff}^{2}.\@add@centering

(2)

We further measure the ionizing efficiency

III.3
Measurement of Ly
\alpha

In this section we describe our method to measure
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
in detail. We outline the measurement for individual HAEs in §
III.3.1
and the stack procedure in §
III.3.2
, which is aimed at measuring the overall
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of a specific population.

III.3.1
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of individual HAEs

Under the assumption of Case B recombination for
T=10^{4}
K and
n_{e}\approx 350
cm
-3
gas, the Ly
\alpha
escape fraction could be expressed as
(
Osterbrock 1989
)

f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}=\frac{F_{\rm Ly\alpha,obs}}{8.7{F}_{\rm H\alpha,int}}

(6)

where
{F}_{\rm Ly\alpha,obs}
is the observed Ly
\alpha
flux and
{F}_{\rm H\alpha,int}
is the intrinsic H
\alpha
flux where the dust attenuation effect has been corrected. For LAEs,
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
could be directly calculated following Equation
6
with the detected Ly
\alpha
emission flux as described in §
III.2
; for non-LAEs, we use the integrated flux over
\pm 1000
km s
-1
within the
r=1.5\arcsec
circular aperture. We correct the dust attenuation with the
17
and SMC laws and the optical depth in the V band derived from
Beagle
respectively. The differential dust attenuation factor between stars and nebular regions is set as
f_{\rm nebular}=1
,
as suggested by a number of previous studies that
f_{\rm nebular}
approaches unity at
z>2
with H
\alpha
EW
>100\AA

(
Faisst et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. The distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of individual HAEs will be discussed in §
IV.1

III.3.2
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
by VLT/MUSE stack

To calculate the typical
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of a specific population comprising HAEs with both Ly
\alpha
detected and not detected, we stack the MUSE datacubes and measure the stacked Ly
\alpha
. We shift the median-filtered sub-cubes of each HAE to its rest frame and then resample the sub-cubes into a fixed grid along the spectral axis. The rest-frame sub-cubes are then normalized based on their intrinsic H
\alpha
fluxes, with each voxel in the cube divided by the intrinsic H
\alpha
flux. We obtain the median-stacked cube by taking the median value of each voxel element to avoid the effect of outliers and asymmetric distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
. The uncertainty of each voxel is estimated by bootstrapping sampling a thousand times.

We extract 1D Ly
\alpha
spectra using a
r=2.5\arcsec
circular aperture on the median-stacked cubes. This aperture
is conservative,
larger than what we use for individual HAEs, as we randomly align the subcubes during the stack without considering the asymmetry of Ly
\alpha
and thus the stacked Ly
\alpha
could be more extended than individuals. On the Ly
\alpha
psedo-narrowband image of the stack of all LAEs integrated over
\pm 1000\ {\rm km\ s^{-1}}
, an
r=2.5\arcsec
aperture includes
95\pm 12\%
of the total flux. We fit skewed Gaussian profiles on the extracted 1D spectra to estimate the normalized Ly
\alpha
flux.

IV.1
The distribution of

We measure the
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of individual HAE following the method in §
III.3.1
. The distributions of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of LAEs and nonLAEs are shown in Figure
5
. For our detected LAEs,
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
ranges from 0.04 to 1. For non-LAEs, there are negative
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
measurements with large uncertainties; the negative values are all consistent with zero within
3\sigma
. The mean and median values of the individual
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
are
0.13\pm 0.01
and
0.08\pm 0.01
(
0.15\pm 0.01
and
0.11\pm 0.01
) under the assumption of
17
(SMC) dust attenuation law. We model the distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
using an exponential model, as commonly adopted to depict the distributions of Ly
\alpha
EWs of Ly
\alpha
emitting galaxies
(
Dijkstra & Westra 2010
;
Dijkstra & Wyithe 2012
;
Kerutt et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. In fact
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
is proportional to the ratio of Ly
\alpha
and the H
\alpha
fluxes, as shown in Equation
6
; meanwhile Ly
\alpha
EWs are the ratios between Ly
\alpha
fluxes and the underlying UV continua. H
\alpha
fluxes and UV continua both trace star formation so that
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
and Ly
\alpha
EWs are correlated
(
Yang et al. 2016
;
Sobral & Matthee 2019
, e.g.,)
. The distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
is expressed as:

P(f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}|f_{*})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,~f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}<0{~\rm or~}f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}>1,~\\
&\propto e^{-f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}/f_{*}},{~\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(7)

where
\int_{0}^{1}P(f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}|f_{*})df_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}=1
,
f_{*}
is the characteristic (mean) escape fraction.

For both LAEs and non-LAEs with measured escape fraction
\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
and uncertainty
\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
, we assume the uncertainty is Gaussian and the probability for an Ly
\alpha
escape fraction being
\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
should be

\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}(\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha},\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}|f_{*})\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}P(x|f_{*})P_{\mathcal{N}}\left(x,\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha},\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}\right)dx\end{split}

(8)

where
P_{\mathcal{N}}\left(x,\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha},\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}\right)
is the probability of the escape fraction being measured as
\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
if the true value is
x
with the uncertainty
\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{N}}\left(x,\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha},\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}}{\Delta\hat{f}_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}}\right)^{2}\right].

The overall likelihood for our observed
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
distribution should be a product of the
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
likelihoods of all galaxies. With a flat prior of
f_{*}
ranging from zero to one, we perform Bayesian inference using the Markov chain Monte Carlo
method (
MCMC

13

The link between Ly
\alpha
and LyC escape is also in line with the correlations we observe between
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
and galaxy properties. If the physical conditions that favor LyC photon leakage also facilitate the escape of Ly
\alpha
, observables that can indicate
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
are expected to show strong correlations with
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
. From cosmological radiation hydrodynamics simulations,
Choustikov et al. 2023
concludes that a good diagnostic of
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
should simultaneously encapsulate at least two of the three conditions: (1) high sSFR for the production of ionizing photons, (2) the age of stellar population and timescale for stellar feedback, and (3) a proxy for neutral gas content and the ionization state of ISM. In this context,
\beta_{\rm obs}
and E(
B-V
) serve as strong indicators for the escape of LyC and Ly
\alpha
photons. E(
B-V
) directly tracks the dust content along the line of sight and may also trace neutral hydrogen
(
Katz et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
; the production and destruction of dust are also related to supernovae activities
(
Priestley et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. Galaxies with bluer
\beta_{\rm obs}
are biased towards having higher sSFRs and younger stellar populations;
\beta_{\rm obs}
is also very sensitive to dust attenuation. E(
B-V
) and
\beta_{\rm obs}
satisfy at least two of the three criteria and thus are good probes of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
. The three physical conditions discussed above also help us to understand the relatively poor dependence of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
on sSFR,
\xi_{\rm ion}
,
\Sigma_{\rm sSFR}
and
M_{\rm UV,att}
. sSFR or
\xi_{\rm ion}
represents just one facet of the three necessary conditions so that a high sSFR or
\xi_{\rm ion}
only could not guarantee a high
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
.
\Sigma_{\rm sSFR}
alone cannot be used to reveal feedback processes or the state of the ISM along the sightline.
M_{\rm UV,att}
is a complex
M_{\star}
, dust, SFR, stellar population, etc., so the
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
-
M_{\rm UV,att}
may suffer from large scatters introduced by the coupling of
M_{\rm UV,att}
with respect to these parameters. Also,
M_{\rm UV,att}
do not trace the ISM state along the sightline. Although
Choustikov et al. 2023
found
M_{\rm UV,att}
can be a weak tracer of sSFR and dust attenuation at fixed
M_{\star}
, in which case
M_{\rm UV,att}
satisfies two out of three conditions while the stellar age would still introduce some scatters, our sample size is not large enough to disentangle on the
M_{\rm UV,att}
dependence of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
at a fixed
M_{\star}
.

V.2
Implications for cosmic Reionization

There is an increasing body of evidences showing that dwarf galaxies are the main drivers of reionization
(
Atek et al. 2023
;
Simmonds et al. 2023
;
Mascia et al. 2023
)
. However, the relative importance of massive and low-mass galaxies in
driving reionization remains uncertain
(
Robertson et al. 2015
;
Finkelstein et al. 2019
;
Naidu et al. 2020
)
. It is necessary to quantify the relative contribution of galaxies with different UV magnitude or masses to the total ionizing emissivity, i.e., the number of ionizing photons emitted per unit time and comoving volume.
\dot{n}_{\rm ion}
contributed by a specific population can be cast as a product of the total observed UV luminosity density
\rho_{\rm UV,obs}
, the production efficiency
\xi_{\rm ion}
, and the escape fraction of ionizing photons
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
:

\dot{n}_{\text{ion }}=\rho_{\mathrm{UV,obs}}\times\xi_{\text{ion }}\times f_{\mathrm{esc},\mathrm{LyC}}^{\rm rel}.

(10)

where
f_{\rm esc,LyC}^{\rm rel}
is the escape fraction of LyC photons relative to the UV photons, defined as
f_{\mathrm{esc},\mathrm{LyC}}^{\rm rel}=f_{\rm esc,LyC}/f_{\rm UV}=f_{\rm esc,LyC}10^{0.4A_{\rm UV}}

(
Bouwens et al. 2016
;
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Bian & Fan 2020
)
. We adopt
A_{\rm UV}
from the best-fit SED models at the rest-frame 1500Å. We emphasize that the definitions of ionizing efficiency and escape fraction of ionizing photons may vary among literature
(
Bouwens et al. 2016
;
Steidel et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2019
;
Simmonds et al. 2023
)
, which may cause confusion. In this section we follow the definition in
Bouwens et al. 2016
. We define
\xi_{\rm ion}
as the ionizing production rate per unit
intrinsic
UV luminosity as described in §
III.1
. We adopt the relative escape fraction,
f_{\rm esc,LyC}^{\rm rel}
, so that the product
f_{\rm esc,LyC}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
represents the production of ionizing photons that escape to and ionize the IGM per
observed
UV luminosity density. Likewise, we define the relative escape fraction of Ly
\alpha
photons as
f_{\mathrm{esc},\mathrm{Ly\alpha}}^{\rm rel}=f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}/f_{\rm UV}=f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}10^{0.4A_{\rm UV}}
.

If
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
is proportional to
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
, as discussed in §
V.1
,
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
is proportional to
f_{\rm esc,LyC}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
. We investigate the dependence of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
on
M_{\rm UV,att}
and
M_{\star}
in Figure
11
. We fit
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
as a function of
M_{\rm UV,att}
and
M_{\star}
, yielding:

\begin{split}\log f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}\xi_{\rm ion}=(0.23\pm 0.10)&\times(M_{\rm UV,att}+19.5)\\
&+(24.84\pm 0.08)\end{split}

(11)

\begin{split}\log f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}\xi_{\rm ion}=(-0.33\pm 0.04)&\times\log(M_{\star}/10^{8.5}M_{\odot})\\
&+(24.92\pm 0.03)\end{split}

(12)

To provide a reference for future works that may define the ionizing efficiency and escape fraction differently, we also present the analytical relations of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
\xi_{\rm ion}
with respect to
M_{\rm UV,att}
and
M_{\star}
in Appendix

, which can be proportional to the ionizing emissivity per unit

.

VI
Summary

We investigate the escape of Ly
\alpha
with a large sample of 165 HAEs at
z\approx 4.9-6.3
in the GOODS-S field, which lies in the overlap region of the JWST FRESCO program and the public MUSE surveys. The JWST/NIRCam WFSS observations provided by FRESCO allow us to construct an unbiased HAE sample with precise measurements of their H
\alpha
emission lines. We search for their Ly
\alpha
emission in the 3D data cubes of VLT/MUSE. We measure the Ly
\alpha
escape fraction (
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
) of individual galaxies and perform stacking analysis for the overall populations. Our main conclusions are as follows:

(i)

The overall
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
of HAEs at
z\approx 5-6
is 0.090
\pm
0.006 (0.121
\pm
0.009) under the assumption of
17
(SMC) dust attenuation law. The distribution of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
can be described using an exponential model with a median escape fraction of
0.10\pm 0.01
(
0.12^{+0.02}_{-0.01}
) for the
17
(SMC) dust law.

(ii)

f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
exhibits strong anti-correlations with the dust attenuation E(
B-V
) and the observed UV slope (
\beta_{\rm obs}
), indicative of the crucial role of dust plays in modulating the escape of Ly
\alpha
photons. Adopting the
17
(SMC) dust law, our best-fit relations between
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
and E(
B-V
) and
\beta_{\rm obs}
predict
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
\approx 0.48~(0.46)
for dust-free galaxies and
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
\approx 0.62~(0.54)
for young galaxies with very blue UV slopes
\beta_{\rm obs}
\approx-2.5
.

(iii)

We find that
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
also correlates with the stellar mass (
M_{\star}
) and the intrinsic UV magnitude (
M_{\rm UV,int}
). The correlations are regulated by the underlying coupling between
M_{\star}
,
M_{\rm UV,int}
and
\beta_{\rm obs}
, E(
B-V
) . We do not find strong correlations between
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
and the ionizing efficiency (
\xi_{\rm ion}
), the observed UV magnitude (
M_{\rm UV,att}
), the specific star formation rate (sSFR) and the sSFR surface density (
\Sigma_{\rm sSFR}
). The variations of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
are also regulated by the scatterings of
\beta_{\rm obs}
and E(
B-V
) coupled with these parameters.

(iv)

We find a tentative decrease in
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
as
z
grows at
z\gtrsim 5
combined with measurements in
Sun et al. 2023
. It implies an increasing neutral fraction of IGM at this epoch. At
z=5.2
and
z=5.8
our measured
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
are only
56\%
and
37\%
of the previous estimates based on Ly
\alpha
and UV luminosity functions
(
Hayes et al. 2011
)
. The difference could arise from the uncertainties in estimating the Ly
\alpha
LFs and the conversion between UV and H
\alpha
luminosities in previous studies without direct H
\alpha
constraints.

(v)

The
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
-
\beta_{\rm obs}
and
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
\xi_{\rm ion}
-
\beta_{\rm obs}
relations has log-space slopes very close to those of the
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
-
\beta_{\rm obs}
and
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
\xi_{\rm ion}
-
\beta_{\rm obs}
relations measured from LyC leakers at
z\sim 0.3

(
Chisholm et al. 2022
)
. The scaling factor is consistent with the monotonic
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
-
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
connection at
z=4-5

(
Begley et al. 2023
)
, implying that
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
can be proportional to
f_{\rm esc,LyC}
. This may indicate that the escapes of Ly
\alpha
and LyC are regulated by similar physical processes. It is also in line with the weak dependence of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
on
M_{\rm UV,att}
,
\xi_{\rm ion}
, sSFR, and
\Sigma_{\rm sSFR}
. The four parameters are not enough to depict the physical conditions that favor LyC and Ly
\alpha
escape, as predicted by simulations
(
Choustikov et al. 2023
)
, so they are all weak tracers of LyC or Ly
\alpha
in isolation.

(vi)

We define the relative escape fraction of Ly
\alpha
photons
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
as
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
divided by the escape fraction of UV photons. We fit the analytical relations of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}

M_{\rm UV,att}
and
M_{\star}

f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}^{\rm rel}
\xi_{\rm ion}
-
M_{\rm UV,att}
relation to the faint end, we calculate the fraction of ionizing emissivity from UV faint galaxies (
M_{\rm UV,att}
>-16
mag).
Qualitatively, we infer that galaxies with UV fainter than
-16
mag may contribute
>70\%
of the ionizing budget at
z=5.5
. If the dependence of
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
\xi_{\rm ion}
on
M_{\rm UV,att}
holds till the epoch of reionization, UV-faint galaxies are predicted to be the main contributor to the reionization. Note that our HAE samples only reach
M_{\rm UV,att}
\sim-17
mag, so direct constraints on
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
at the fainter end are required to better quantify the ionizing budget from faint galaxies.

JWST has shown promising prospects to unveil the mysteries of the EoR. Deeper and wider spectroscopic surveys in the future would reach the fainter end with lower stellar masses, and further probe the escape process of Ly
\alpha
and LyC photons in newborn infant galaxies.

Appendix E
Possible systematics for Ly
\alpha
flux measurement

In this section, we discuss possible systematics and the uncertainties it may introduce during the measurement of Ly
\alpha
flux. We emphasize that, the systematics we mention in the following can only introduce negligible uncertainties in the final
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
estimates, proved by our well-tried experiments.

E.1
Contamination rate

We compare our HAE catalog with the public JADES-Deep catalog, which contains not only photometric redshifts for each source based on JADES’ deep imaging but also spectroscopical confirmation for some of the sources combined with FRESCO’s WFSS data, identified by the JADES team independently (Sun in prep). There are 80 HAEs in our sample covered by JADES-Deep, among which
\gtrsim
91% have photo-
z
in the range of 4.9–6.7, predicting H
\alpha
in F444W. Further,
\gtrsim
70% of them are reported to have H
\alpha
emission in JADES’s catalog. These values are simply lower limits because we haven’t considered the mismatch due to different segmentation strategies during the catalog matching. Indeed we do check the sources without JADES confirmed spec-
z
, and find most of them are (1) multi-components and suffering mismatch issues, (2) missed by the JADES team as they only report H
\alpha
emission with SNRs
\geq 5
(chat with Sun in private), while our detection threshold is SNRs
\geq 4
. To sum up, our HAE catalog is reliable with high purity.

E.2
The accuracy of Ly
\alpha
measurement

As described in §
III.2
,
Kerutt et al. 2022
and
Bacon et al. 2023
release their LAE catalog utilizing MUSE-Wide and MUSE-Deep survey data respectively. There are 39 isolated HAEs recorded in the two literature catalogs. Different extraction and flux measurement methods are adopted, including blind line searching algorithm
LSDCat

(
Herenz & Wisotzki 2017
)
and HST-prior extraction
ORIGIN

(
Mary et al. 2020
)
. In this work, instead, we extract the spectra from the exposure-time-weighted coadded datacube using circular and boxy apertures, and measure the line flux by fitting skewed Gaussian profiles. For
\rm EW_{Ly\alpha}
,
Kerutt et al. 2022
takes a fixed UV slope,
\beta=-1.97
, for their entire LAE sample and extrapolates the measured flux at the effective wavelength of the HST bands for the continua at the Ly
\alpha
wavelength, while
Bacon et al. 2023
adopts the values provided by
pyPlatefit
17
17

17

https://github.com/musevlt/pyplatefit
. Their methods are totally different from ours, which yields the continuum flux density for Ly
\alpha
lines by extrapolating the best-fit SED models. We compare the reported Ly
\alpha
properties of the 39 HAEs from the two catalogs with our own measurements, yielding a median flux ratio of
1.04\pm 0.38
and
{\rm EW}
ratio of
0.85\pm 0.18
. Though the scattering is large, especially at the faint Ly
\alpha
end, our measurement is consistent with the literature within
1\sigma
.

E.3
Stack of median-filtered datacubes with different depths

The MUSE datacubes used in this work have very different depths, ranging from 1h for MUSE-Wide to
\gtrsim
140h for MXDF. Therefore the Ly
\alpha
SNR for each individual HAE could vary dramatically. Theoretically, their median-stack is expected not to be biased towards those with highest SNRs, i.e. those covered by MUSE-Deep. Another concern is the median filtering we use to remove the continuum and nearby sources. It could possibly reduce the Ly
\alpha
flux by suppressing the wing of the emission lines, even though the optimized window size is chosen. We perform a test to check if these effects would significantly change our result.

Our goal is to check whether the median stack of median-filtered spectra can recover the intrinsic flux. To start with, we generate a series of mock 1D spectra in a spectral sampling of 0.5Å, with random SNRs and continua but uniform flux. 70% spectra have low SNRs uniformly distributed from 0.1 to 3, 15% have intermediate SNRs from 3 to 5, and the remaining have the highest SNRs from 5 to 15. We then remove the continua using a median filter of 101 pixels, the same as used in the real MUSE data, and stack all the mock spectra by taking the median value at each wavelength grid. We perform experiments for 500 times using 20, 30, 100, 200 spectra. The median values of the recovered flux for each setting reach 96–99% of the initially set value, suggesting that a
\lesssim
5% underestimate is possible. For a
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}

\sim
0.1, this systematics would simply result in an error of
\lesssim
0.005, smaller than the uncertainty as we report in this work.
