Prediction Sets for High-Dimensional Mixture of Experts Models
Adel Javanmard, Simeng Shao, Jacob Bien

Abstract
Large datasets make it possible to build predictive models that can capture heterogenous relationships between the response variable and features. The mixture of high-dimensional linear experts model posits that observations come from a mixture of high-dimensional linear regression models, where the mixture weights are themselves feature-dependent. In this paper, we show how to construct valid prediction sets for an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -penalized mixture of experts model in the high-dimensional setting. We make use of a debiasing procedure to account for the bias induced by the penalization and propose a novel strategy for combining intervals to form a prediction set with coverage guarantees in the mixture setting. Synthetic examples and an application to the prediction of critical temperatures of superconducting materials show our method to have reliable practical performance.

Introduction

In traditional statistics, we imagine a universal relationship between variables
that holds across an entire population; observations not
following this relationship are dismissed as outliers. However, we
know that reality is more complex, with numerous subpopulations
likely exhibiting distinct behaviors. As datasets grow in size, we become
better able to detect and properly model this heterogeneity. The
mixture of regressions model
(
Quandt & Ramsey 1978
)
is an
important tool for extending linear regression to this
heterogeneity-aware setting. For a random response
y\in\mathbb{R}
and a
random vector of predictors
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we imagine a latent subgroup
membership
z\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
that determines the conditional
distribution of
y
given
{\boldsymbol{x}}
:

\displaystyle y|{\boldsymbol{x}},z=k\sim N({\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}).

(1)

Making predictions with this model requires estimating for each
subgroup a coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
, an error variance

\sigma_{k}^{2}
, and a group membership probability
\pi_{k}=\mathbb{P}(z=k)
. The
mixture of experts model (MoE,
Jordan & Jacobs 1994
) is even more flexible,
allowing these group membership probabilities to depend on the
predictors as well:

\displaystyle z|{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim\text{Multinomial}[\boldsymbol{\pi}({\boldsymbol{x}})]\qquad\text{with}\quad\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\exp{({\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k})}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp{({\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\ell})}}.

(2)

This model is expressive enough to capture subpopulations that change
in prevalence depending on the conditions. For example,
Hyun et al. 2020
develop a high-dimensional mixture of
experts approach to modeling phytoplankton subpopulations as a
function of environmental covariates in the ocean. The (log) diameter of
the phytoplankton cells within each specific subpopulation are taken to
be Gaussian with mean depending on environmental covariates (expressed through nonzero
values of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{K}
); however, the prevalence of
the different subpopulations also depends on these covariates (expressed through nonzero
values of
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K}
).

When making predictions, it is valuable to be able to quantify one’s
level of uncertainty. In this paper, we develop the machinery
necessary to do so in the context of the high-dimensional mixture of
the experts model described above. In particular, given a sample of
n

observations from the model in
(
1
)–(
2
), a confidence level
q\in(0,1)
, and a new predictor vector of
interest
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}
, we show how to form a
properly calibrated
prediction set

\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})
. That is,
given a new draw
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}},y_{\textrm{new}})
from (
1
)–(
2
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\geq 1-q.

(3)

. There are
K=2
subpopulations, one
following a quadratic relation and the other being constant (for
details of the construction, see Section
4
). The
upper panel shows that the mixture weight on the quadratic
subpopulation decreases with increasing
t
. This can also be seen by
inspecting the scatterplot and noting that for large
t
, most of the points are in
the constant subpopulation. The solid lines show our estimated mixture
of experts model fit based on the points in the scatterplot. Now
suppose we are about to observe a new point
y_{\textrm{new}}
at, say,
t_{\text{new}}=0.5
.
Can we form a set
\Omega_{0.95}(t_{\text{new}})
that is guaranteed
to capture
y_{\textrm{new}}
at least 95% of the time? Given that we do not
know which subpopulation
y_{\textrm{new}}
will be drawn from,

\Omega_{0.95}(t_{\text{new}})
will be a union of two intervals. The
size and location of these intervals will depend on our estimates of
the subpopulation means and variances (governed by
t_{\text{new}}
and our
estimates of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}
,
\sigma_{1}
, and
\sigma_{2}
) as
well as the estimated mixture weights (governed by
t_{\text{new}}

and our estimates of
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}
). The gray band in
the middle panel shows our constructed
\Omega_{0.95}(t_{\text{new}})
as we vary

t_{\text{new}}
from 0 to 1. When the means of the populations are
far apart, the prediction set is a union of two intervals, while the
set becomes a single interval when the means are close to each other.
The larger a subpopulation’s mixture weight, the wider that interval
becomes. This “strategy” is reasonable because if it’s unlikely
that a point will fall in a certain subpopulation, one can get away
with less good coverage of that subpopulation (and thereby reduce the
overall size of the prediction set). The rightmost panel shows the

\Omega_{0.95}(t)
as
a function of
t
; then, we generated 1000

at each

and
computed the coverage rate (averaging over the
500\cdot 1000

repetitions for each
t
). This verifies that our procedure approximately attains the
nominal 95% coverage. The bottom panel shows the average size of the
prediction set. Quite intuitively, the prediction set is smallest when the two subpopulations are
very close to each other. One observes a bit of overcoverage when the
subpopulations are close, which makes sense since in this situation the
interval from one subpopulation can sometimes cover a point from the opposite subpopulation.

Constructing prediction sets in the context of a high-dimensional
mixture of experts model is challenging. In fact, making any sort of precise statements about even the most
simple mixture of regression models is nontrivial. For example, much
effort has gone into understanding the convergence and estimation
error of the expectation-maximization estimator
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)

used in fitting such models (
Yi et al. 2014
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
;
Klusowski et al. 2019
;
Kwon et al. 2019
;
Kwon & Caramanis 2020
) as well as
being able to test whether there are two groups versus one
(
Zhu & Zhang 2004
)
. Adding
high dimensionality to the study of mixture of regression models
brings additional challenges.
Städler et al. 2010
and

Yi & Caramanis 2015
proposed different

\ell_{1}
-regularized maximum likelihood estimators with accompanying
estimation error results.
Wang et al. 2014
take this a step further
and develop a truncation-based high-dimensional estimator with both
estimation error results and the ability to construct confidence
intervals for low-dimensional components of the parameter vector. While their results hold for general
latent variable models, their application to mixture of regression
models is more of a proof of concept, with
K=2
,
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}

assumed known,
\pi_{1}=\pi_{2}=0.5
, and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}=-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}
.

Zhang et al. 2020
provide inference for individual coefficients and
differences of the form
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1j}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2j}

within the context of this model using an
\ell_{1}
penalty. They
generalize to the case of unknown mixture weight
\pi_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}\neq-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2}
, and an
unknown covariance matrix for
X
(which they take to be multivariate
normal), but they still assume
K=2
and that the value

\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
is known. While we are able to adopt in part a similar debiased approach,
we will highlight later why their technique, which works in the
K=2

mixture of regression setting (which involves a single unknown mixture
parameter
\pi_{1}
) does not easily generalize to our setting of a
mixture of experts model, in which mixture weights depend on the
unknown parameter vectors
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Furthermore, our interest in forming a prediction set (
3
) requires
the ability to make inferential statements about
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
,
not just low-dimensional components of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
. In this sense,

Cai et al. 2021
pursue a similar goal in performing inference for individualized
treatment effects
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2})
; however, unlike the
mixture of regression setting, the group memberships are known in
their context.

To summarize, to the best of our knowledge, predictive inference in mixture of
expert models has not been addressed in the literature. Furthermore,
we address this problem for general
K
and in the high-dimensional
setting. We make use of ideas from the debiased lasso literature.
The debiasing approach for constructing confidence intervals for coefficients has been
widely used in linear regression models in high-dimensional settings
(
Javanmard & Montanari 2014a
;
Javanmard & Montanari 2014b
;
Zhang & Zhang 2014
;
Van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Javanmard & Montanari 2018
). In recent years, there has been work
on inference for general linear functions (
Cai & Guo 2017
;
Guo et al. 2021
;
Javanmard & Lee 2020
;
Zhu & Bradic 2018
). In terms of
debiasing in the non-mixture setting,
Cai & Guo 2017
;
Tripuraneni & Mackey 2019
;
Athey et al. 2018
proposed
bias-corrected estimators for a single linear regression model while, as
we have noted above,
Cai et al. 2021
considers inference for

\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2})
in the case of two observed (i.e., non-latent) groups.

Interest in predictive inference has led to an active area of work on
conformal prediction. These approaches are attractive for being
distribution-free and providing finite-sample coverage (see, e.g.,
Papadopoulos et al. 2002
;
Vovk et al. 2005
;
Lei et al. 2018
;
Romano, Patterson & Candes 2019
). They rely on
very general ideas such as the exchangeability of draws from the
distribution. However, the coverage that these conformal methods
attain is not conditional on
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}
as in (
3
) but
rather holds marginally over
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}))\geq 1-q.

Indeed, it has been proven that to obtain finite length sets with conditional coverage,
one needs to make stronger assumptions

(
Vovk 2012
;
Lei & Wasserman 2014
)
. In certain
applications, one specifically desires coverage of the form
(
3
) and assuming the parametric form
(
1
)–(
2
) can be a small price to
pay. For example, in the oceanographic example of mixture of experts

(
Hyun et al. 2020
)
, the mixture of Gaussians structure is visually
well-supported. Prediction sets with conditional coverage are
desirable because we would like to be able to say that for a given set of
environmental conditions (e.g., at a specific temperature and salinity
level) our prediction set for the phytoplankton diameters will have a
95% coverage guarantee. Marginal coverage would mean that if we make
predictions over many randomly sampled environments, our coverage
would average out to 95%. The latter means, for example, that a
procedure could be overconfident (i.e., undercovering) at high
temperatures and underconfident (i.e., overcovering) at low
temperatures. Setting conditional coverage (
3
) as the
goal guards against this undesirable property.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
,
we describe our approach to constructing prediction sets. This
involves estimating parameters using a penalized
expectation-maximization approach (Section
2.1
), then using
a debiasing technique on the coefficient vectors from each of the
component distributions (Section
2.2
), and finally combining

K
intervals into a prediction set in a fashion that maintains proper
coverage (Section
2.3
). In Section
3
, we provide
theoretical guarantees that establish the asymptotic validity of our
constructed prediction sets and provide insight into the conditions
under which we expect nominal coverage to hold. In Section
4

we investigate the empirical performance of our prediction sets in a
variety of settings. Section
5
shows our sets and evaluates
their performance empirically in predicting the
critical temperatures of superconducting materials. We conclude this
section with some notation.

Methodology

We begin (in Section
2.1
) with a review of a penalized
maximum likelihood procedure for the MoE model
(
1
)–(

) such as is used in

. Sections

then introduce our proposed methodology

2.1
Penalized EM-based Estimator

The expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
) is a common heuristic when faced with
maximum-likelihood problems involving missing data, especially in the
form of latent variables. The algorithm operates in an iterative
fashion, alternating between the E (expectation) step and M
(maximization) step, while managing to increase the objective function.

Assume
n
data points
\{({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn
independently from the MoE model (
1
)–(
2
). The EM algorithm aims at maximizing the log-likelihood

\displaystyle\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})\right],

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left(\{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k},\sigma_{k}\}_{k\in[K]}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(2p+1)K}
represents the model parameters,
\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right),

(5)

and
\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
is given in (
2
).
The log-likelihood
\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is not concave even as a function
of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
, treating
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}

and
\sigma_{k}
as fixed. This is due to the marginalizing over the
latent cluster memberships
z_{i}
. The EM algorithm instead employs a
minorize-maximize approach
(
Hunter & Lange 2004
)
in which a
minorizer to the log-likelihood is repeatedly constructed and
maximized. More specifically, given some fixed
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
it maximizes a lower bound function
Q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
over
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to make
\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\ell(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
large. We refer to
Hastie et al. 2009
for a more detailed introduction to EM algorithm and derivation of the function
Q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
and here only provide the description of the EM algorithm for the MoE model.

Let
\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
be the probability that
z_{i}=k
conditioned on the observed variable
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})
, i.e.,

\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\mathbb{P}\left(z_{i}=k|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i}\right)=\frac{\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\cdot\phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})}.

The function
\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is sometimes referred to as

responsibilities
in the literature (see, e.g.
Hyun et al. 2020
)
because it quantifies how “responsible” group
k
is for point
i
.

Consider the function
Q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
defined as

\displaystyle Q({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left[\log\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})+\log\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\|_{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\|_{1},

(6)

where the regularization terms
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right\|_{1},\left\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\right\|_{1}
are added to enforce sparsity on the estimated
parameters and allow for applications in the high-dimensional sparse regime.

The EM algorithm iterates between estimating the conditional membership probabilities
\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
, and reducing (
6
) by updating the parameters
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. The details of the updates are given below:

•

E step: Estimating the conditional responsibility of membership based on the latest parameter estimate.

\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=\frac{\frac{\hat{\pi}_{i,k}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k})^{2}}{2\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}\Big)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\frac{\hat{\pi}_{i,\ell}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{\ell}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\ell})^{2}}{2\widehat{\sigma}_{\ell}^{2}}\Big)}.

•

M step: Updating
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
to lower the objective value in (
6
).

–

For each
k=1,\ldots,K
, update
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}=\underset{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\arg\min}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}}\frac{(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^{2}}{2n}+\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{1}.

(7)

–

Update
\{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
:

\displaystyle\{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{k}\}=\underset{\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\}}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}-\log\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\exp{({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\ell})}\Big)\Big)+\lambda_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\|_{1}.

(8)

–

For each
k=1,\ldots,K
, update
\widehat{\sigma}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}.

(9)

As noted in
Hyun et al. 2020
, this M-step update represents a decreasing in the objective but not a
minimization since
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k},\sigma_{k})
would need to be jointly optimized. The algorithm terminates when the improvement in (
6
) is below some threshold.

In practice, we use the R package
flowmix
(
Hyun 2022
) to carry out the EM algorithm.

2.2
Debiased Prediction

From (
1
)–(
2
), we
have that

y_{\textrm{new}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\sim N(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})\text{ with probability }\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}).

Because of the penalization,
\hat{\Gamma}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}
is a
biased estimate of
\Gamma_{k}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
, and thus would give
biased predictions for
y_{\textrm{new}}
even if we knew that
z_{\textrm{new}}=k
. We
therefore propose a debaising procedure. Given an arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
, we construct a debiased prediction

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}},

(10)

where
\partial/\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
denotes the gradient with respect
to
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
.

Our proposed choice for
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
depends on the estimated sample Fisher
information matrix, which is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell_{i}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\nabla\ell_{i}^{T}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}),

(11)

where
\ell_{i}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
are the summands in (
4
) evaluated at
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\left(\{\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k},\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{k},\widehat{\sigma}_{k}\}_{k\in[K]}\right)
, and

\nabla\ell_{i}=\Big(\Big\{\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}}\Big\}_{k=1}^{K},\Big\{\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}}\Big\}_{k=1}^{K},\Big\{\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial\sigma_{k}}\Big\}_{k=1}^{K}\Big)^{T}\,.

We would like a choice of
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
that will lead to a narrower interval.
This intuition (which is described in greater detail below) suggests
choosing
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
as the solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\ \boldsymbol{u}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\ \textrm{s.t.}\ \ \sup_{\omega\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\left\langle\omega,\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\rangle\right|\leq\lambda_{k}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\|_{2},\quad\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}\leq L\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2},

(
12
)

where we define
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}_{i}
,

L
is a sufficiently large constant, and
\lambda_{k}
is a tuning parameter (in Section
3
, we discuss the proper rate of
\lambda_{k}
and the choice of
L
). In addition,
\mathcal{C}=\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{p},\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}/\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\|_{2}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th standard Euclidean basis vector. The matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
is the estimated Fisher information matrix, constrained to
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
, which is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\frac{y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right)\left(\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\frac{y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right)^{T}.

(13)

The above characterization follows from the definition of
\tilde{I}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
given by (
11
) along with identity (

), restricted to class
k
.

Remark
.

Because
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
depend on both the response
vector
{\bf y}
and the covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
, the solution
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
to (
12
) is also dependent
on them. This is in contrast to the optimization problem proposed by

Javanmard & Montanari 2014a
for constructing the debiasing
direction, as it only involved covariates and hence conditional on the
covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}
, the debiasing direction was independent
of the response vector
{\bf y}
.

To deal with the complications resulting from the dependence of
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}

on
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
, we do sample splitting. Specifically, we
split the data into two sets,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
,
where optimization (
12
) is solved on

\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k},\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
are calculated using
samples in
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, per (
10
).

The above approach to construct the direction
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
is generalized
from
Javanmard & Montanari 2014a
;
Zhang & Zhang 2014
;
Cai & Guo 2017
and aims to find a direction that minimizes the
variance while controlling the bias. However, the goal in

Javanmard & Montanari 2014a
;
Zhang & Zhang 2014
;
Cai & Guo 2017
is to establish inference for coefficients, which
differs from our goal of establishing inference for prediction. Closer
to our aim here, we follow the proposal of
Cai et al. 2021
to construct
prediction intervals, under a linear regression model; however, our
setting of a MoE model is more complex and requires novel methodology and analysis. In particular, our optimization problem differs from
Cai et al. 2021
in that we use a constrained Fisher information matrix,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
, in the objective of (
12
), and we allow the matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}
in the constraint of (
12
) to be different from the matrix in the objective, while
Cai et al. 2021
keeps them the same.

The first constraint in (
12
) can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}

(14)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(15)

Similar to the general intuition behind the quadratic optimization of

Javanmard & Montanari 2014a
, the objective value
\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}

is related to the variance of
\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
, and the constraint
(
14
) relates to its bias. So the optimization is
indeed aiming to minimize the variance (and hence the length of
prediction intervals which will be constructed based on

\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
), while controlling the bias of

\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
. That said, in the analysis we need to show that
the bias is dominated by the variance term and therefore need to
establish a lower bound on the variance. The
constraint (
15
) is added for this step. It makes
the feasible set of the optimization problem smaller and makes it
possible to lower bound the optimal value of the objective (see Proposition
A.3
for technical arguments). This idea originates from
Cai et al. 2021
, which introduced
the “variance-enhancement projection direction”. While the general intuition carries over to our current setting, characterizing the statistical properties of
\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
under the MoE requires a rather intricate and technical analysis.

We denote the estimated variance for
\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\quad k=1,...,K,

(16)

and the prediction variance estimate (conditional on
z_{\textrm{new}}=k
) as

\displaystyle b^{2}_{k}:=\widehat{V}_{k}+\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k},\quad k=1,...,K.

(17)

2.3
Prediction Sets

Recall that our goal is to construct a
100(1-q)\%

\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}

3
Theoretical Guarantees

We a consider sequence of problems where the sample size
n\to\infty

and covariate dimension
p=p(n)\to\infty
, while the number of
groups
K
is bounded, and we establish asymptotic validity of our prediction sets for the MoE model (
1
)–(
2
).
We first lay out several technical assumptions on the estimation error
of
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, the random covariate vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, and
the model parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

•

(A1)
Parameter estimation
\eta_{{\sf est}}
.
Suppose that

\max_{k\in[K]}\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\right\|_{1}=\max_{k\in[K]}\left(\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\right\|_{1}+|\widehat{\sigma}_{k}-\sigma_{k}|\right)=O_{p}(\eta_{{\sf est}})\,,

where
\eta_{{\sf est}}
scales with
n,p
and potentially other
structure associated with the parameters (e.g. sparsity levels). We assume that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\log(np)\eta_{{\sf est}}^{2}=o(1)\,.

(22)

•

(A2)
Distribution of features.
We have a
positive-semidefinite matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

(or more precisely a sequence of matrices of growing dimension) with bounded operator norm,
\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\|_{{\rm op}}\leq C_{\Sigma}
, for a constant
C_{\Sigma}
as
p\to\infty
. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
are independent sub-Gaussian vectors, with mean zero and sub-Gaussian norm
\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}=O(1)
.

•

(A3)
Bounded noise and signal.
The noise variances
\sigma_{k}^{2}
are strictly positive and bounded constants for
k\in[K]
. We also assume that
\max_{\ell,k\in[K]}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}\|_{2}=O(1)
.

Condition (A1) assumes an
\ell_{1}
-consistency rate for the estimate

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. Consistency presupposes identifiability, which in the case
of the
\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\}
may require additional assumptions (since

\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}+{\bf c}:k\in[K]\}
corresponds to the same
\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})

as
\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}:k\in[K]\}
). Our theoretical results apply to any estimator which
satisfy condition (
22
). The proposed EM estimator
in Section
2.1
is just one specific choice. Instead of

\ell_{1}
-regularization, one can follow other variants based on
iterative truncation. For example,
Wang et al. 2014
analyzes a
mixture of regression model with two groups and proposes a truncated
EM algorithm (with a gradient ascent implementation) which achieves
\eta_{{\sf est}}=s\sqrt{\log(p)\log(n)/n}
, with
s
the sparsity level of model parameters. The work
Zhang et al. 2020
derives a similar
\ell_{1}
-consistency rate for the high-dimensional mixed linear regression with two groups, for an iterative EM procedure which performs
\ell_{1}
regularization at each step.
While the mixture of experts model is more complicated we conjecture that a similar rate for
\eta_{{\sf est}}
carries over to this setting. Under such conjecture, condition (
22
) simplifies to

\frac{s^{2}\log(np)\log(p)\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}=o(1)\,.

Condition (A2) is on the random covariate vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
and is a common assumption in high-dimensional statistical estimation; see e.g.
Bühlmann & Van
De Geer 2011
. Condition (A3) on the pairwise distances
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}\right\|_{2}
and noise variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
is to control the heterogeneity of data coming from different groups.

Our first theorem is on asymptotic normality of the bias-corrected
estimators
\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}
defined in (
10
) and involves the matrix

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma_{1k}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}\right].

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\frac{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{1}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}}=O(1)
and
\log(p)=o(n^{1/4}/\sqrt{\log(n)})
. Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
be the solution to the optimization problem
(
12
) with constant
L\geq\frac{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{1}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}}
and
\lambda_{k}\asymp\eta_{{\sf est}}\log(np)+\sqrt{\log(p)/n}
.
Under Assumptions (A1), (A2), (A3), and Remark

Remark
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Gamma}_{k}^{d}-\Gamma_{k})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\Rightarrow}}N(0,1),

(23)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
, given by (
13
), is the sample Fisher information matrix constrained to entries corresponding to
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}

Now that we have established the asymptotic normality of our debiased estimators, we are ready to prove that our prediction sets provide proper asymptotic coverage.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, the prediction set
\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})
has asymptotically valid coverage. Specifically, fix
\gamma>0
arbitrarily small and in Algorithm

set the discretization scale
\delta

.

\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}

\gamma

6
Conclusion

We have shown how to construct prediction sets for the
high-dimensional mixture of experts model. Mixture models are
important for capturing the heterogeneity that is present in many
real-world situations.
While in small data samples it was common to dismiss deviations from
the norm as outliers, in large data sets it becomes possible to
use models that can identify and model these subgroups. While mixture
of regression models allow for such heterogeneity-aware predictive
modeling, they assume that the relative sizes of the subgroups are
fixed. Importantly, mixture of experts models remove this assumption
and allow the prevalence of different subgroups to
depend on the features. This generalization is essential in many
situations from
ecology, where the relative proportions of different subpopulations
depends on environmental covariates
(
Hyun et al. 2020
)
, to politics,
where the political composition depends on demographic and geographic
variables.

Our focus on conditional coverage can be crucial in certain
applications. For example,
Romano, Barber, Sabatti & Candès 2019
emphasizes the
importance of ensuring that all subpopulations enjoy the same coverage
guarantees and cast this as a fairness issue when the
subpopulations are defined based on a protected attribute.

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}-\Gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}

Recall the estimated Fisher information matrix constrained to

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
is given by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T},

and define

\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{1k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}\right].

We then have

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Gamma}_{k}^{d}-\Gamma_{k})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right)^{T}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{n}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right)^{T}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{k}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}}_{(\text{term I})}+\underbrace{\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma^{2}_{k}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}+(\text{term II})}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}}}_{(\text{term III})},

(
24
)

where

.

Let

be the solution to optimization (

(25)

The second proposition lower bounds the denominator of term I.

Proposition A.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

Proposition A.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

,

.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

, and for

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{I}^{\beta}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right|=o_{p}(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\|_{2}^{2}).

We next note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}}\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma^{2}_{k}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\,,

Theorem
3.2

Let
z_{\textrm{new}}
denote the class of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}
. Conditioning on
z_{\textrm{new}}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})|z_{\textrm{new}}=k,\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\mathbb{P}(z_{\textrm{new}}=k|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})|z_{\textrm{new}}=k,\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\Gamma_{k}+\varepsilon\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\,,

(29)

where we recall that
\Gamma_{k}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}
. Also
\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_{k}^{2})
is independent of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}
.

As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.1
we have

\frac{\Gamma_{k}-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{\widehat{V}_{k}^{1/2}}=W+\Delta\,,

with
W\sim N(0,1)
and
|\Delta|=o_{p}(1)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{k}+\varepsilon-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{V}_{k}^{1/2}}{b_{k}}W+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{b_{k}}\varepsilon+\frac{\widehat{V}_{k}^{1/2}}{b_{k}}\Delta

\displaystyle=c_{k}W^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}\,,

with
W^{\prime},\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)
and
|\Delta^{\prime}|<|\Delta|=o_{p}(1)
and
c_{k}^{2}:=\frac{\widehat{V}_{k}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}}
. Here we used the fact that
W,\varepsilon
are independent normal random variables.

Our procedure for constructing a prediction set returns a union of intervals:
\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})=\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{L}_{k}(q)
with

\mathcal{L}_{k}(q)=[y_{k}^{-},y_{k}^{+}]
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{k}(q)\subseteq\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Gamma_{k}+\varepsilon\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\Gamma_{k}+\varepsilon\in\mathcal{L}_{k}(q))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{k}+\varepsilon-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}}\in\Big[\frac{y_{k}^{-}-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}},\frac{y_{k}^{+}-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}}\Big]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(c_{k}W^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}\in[l_{k},u_{k}]\right)\,,

(30)

where we use the shorthands:

l_{k}:=\frac{y_{k}^{-}-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}},\quad u_{k}:=\frac{y_{k}^{+}-\hat{\Gamma}^{d}_{k}}{b_{k}}\,.

Fix
\gamma>0
arbitrarily small. We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(c_{k}W^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}\in[l_{k},u_{k}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(c_{k}W^{\prime}\in[l_{k}+\gamma,u_{k}-\gamma]\right)-\mathbb{P}(|\Delta^{\prime}|\geq\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(W^{\prime}\in[(1+\gamma)(l_{k}+\gamma),(1-\gamma)(u_{k}-\gamma)]\right)-\mathbb{P}(|\Delta^{\prime}|\geq\gamma)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{c_{k}}-1\Big|\geq\gamma\Big)\,.

By taking the limit
n\to\infty
and using the fact that
|\Delta^{\prime}|=o_{p}(1)
and
|\widehat{\sigma}_{k}-\sigma_{k}|=o_{p}(1)
(Condition (A1)), we get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(c_{k}W^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}\in[l_{k},u_{k}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(W^{\prime}\in[(1+\gamma)(l_{k}+\gamma),(1-\gamma)(u_{k}-\gamma)]\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi((1-\gamma)(u_{k}-\gamma))-\Phi((1+\gamma)(l_{k}+\gamma))\,.

Since
\gamma>0
was arbitrarily small and the left-hand side does not depend on
\gamma
, by taking
\gamma\to 0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(c_{k}W^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}\in[l_{k},u_{k}]\right)\geq\Phi(u_{k})-\Phi(l_{k})\,.

(31)

Using equations (
30
), (
31
) in (
29
) we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(y_{\textrm{new}}\in\Omega_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\Phi(u_{k})-\Phi(l_{k}))\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})\int_{l_{k}}^{u_{k}}\phi(t){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})}{b_{k}}\int_{y_{k}^{-}}^{y_{k}^{+}}\phi\left(\frac{y-\hat{\Gamma}_{k}^{d}}{b_{k}}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{y_{k}^{-}}^{y_{k}^{+}}f(y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{(i)}-{\rm Err}

\displaystyle\geq(1-q)-{\rm Err}\,,

where Err is the approximation error for replacing the integral with the Riemann sum. To bound Err we need to upper bound the second derivative of
f(y)
. Define the function
g_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{a}\phi(\frac{z}{a})
. We have

|g^{\prime\prime}(z)|=\frac{1}{a^{3}}\Big|\frac{z^{2}}{a^{2}}-1\Big|\phi\Big(\frac{z}{a}\Big)\leq\frac{0.18}{a^{3}}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(y)|=\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\hat{\pi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})}{b_{k}}\phi\left(\frac{y-\hat{\Gamma}_{k}^{d}}{b_{k}}\right)\Big|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{0.18\hat{\pi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})}{b_{k}}\leq\frac{0.18}{\min_{k}(b_{k})}\,,

where we used the observation that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\pi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}})=1
. Therefore, the approximation error over an interval of size
N\delta
is bounded as

\displaystyle{\rm Err}\leq\frac{0.18}{\min_{k}(b_{k})}\frac{(N\delta)^{3}}{24N^{2}}=0.0075\frac{N\delta^{3}}{\min_{k}(b_{k})}\,.

(32)

We also note that the returned prediction set is a subset of the initial interval
\mathcal{Q}
of length
{\rm Len}(\mathcal{Q})
which implies that
N\delta\leq{\rm Len}(\mathcal{Q})
. Hence,

{\rm Err}\leq 0.0075{\rm Len}(\mathcal{Q})\frac{\delta^{2}}{\min_{k}(b_{k})}\leq\gamma\,,

by our choice of
\delta\leq 11\sqrt{\gamma\min_{k}(b_{k})/{\rm Len}(\mathcal{Q})}
.

(33)

{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq O_{p}\left(\eta_{{\sf est}}\log(np)+\sqrt{\log(p)/n}\right)L\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\textrm{new}}\right\|_{2}\,,

where in the second inequality we used (
33
).
This completes the proof of the feasibility claim.

Proof of Proposition
A.2

As proved in Proposition

, for the choice of

Proof of Proposition

We will use the proof strategy of

, which was also used in
Cai et al. 2021
and modified to account for the additional constraint (
15

We denote the first term in (

\tilde{Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left[\log\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})+\log\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})\right]\,.

(34)

We restate a lemma from

Lemma C.1
.

(
Wang et al. 2014
, Lemma 2.1)

For the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and any

\displaystyle=\nabla\ell(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{2}_{1,1}\tilde{Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\nabla^{2}_{1,2}\tilde{Q}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})]

\displaystyle=-{\bf I}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,,

(36)

where

Combining (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

D.7

Appendix D
Intermediate Lemmas and Proofs

This section summarizes several technical lemmas that were used in establishing our theoretical results.

The first lemma is a classical maximal inequality for sub-Gaussian random variables.

Lemma D.1
.

(Maximal Inequality for Sub-Gaussians) Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be
a vector of zero-mean sub-Gaussian random variables with
variances
v_{1}^{2},\dots,v_{n}^{2}
, respectively. Then, for any constant
c>0
we have

\sup_{i=1,\dots,n}\left|x_{i}\right|\leq v_{\max}\sqrt{2c\log n},

with probability
at least
1-2n^{1-c}
,
where
v_{\max}=\max_{i\in[n]}v_{i}
.

The above result is obtained by using the tail bound of sub-Gaussian
variables, followed by a simple union bound, and therefore it does not
require the random variables to be independent.

Our next lemma is a simple corollary of the above maximal inequality.

Lemma D.2
.

Define the following probability events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C\sqrt{\log(np)}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\max_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\;|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}|\leq C\sqrt{\log(nK)}\right\}\,,

and let
\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Then, under Assumptions (A2)–(A3) we have
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\frac{4}{n^{2}}
, for large enough constant
C>0
.

Proof.

we write
x_{ir}=\langle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{r},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\rangle
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|x_{ir}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}\right\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{\Sigma}^{1/2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}<\kappa\,,

(43)

for all
r\in[p]
,
i\in[n]
and some constant
\kappa<\infty
by Assumption (A2). By Lemma
D.1
, and for sufficiently large constant
C>0
, we get
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{2}}
.

To bound probability of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, suppose that sample
i
belongs to group
\ell
, by which we can write

y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})+\varepsilon_{i}\,.

We then have

\displaystyle\|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell\in[K]}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})+\varepsilon_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell\in[K]}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})\|_{\psi_{2}}+\|\varepsilon_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell\in[K]}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})\right\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}+\sigma_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell\in[K]}\left(C_{\Sigma}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}+\sigma_{\ell}\right)\,.

Taking maximum over
i,k
from both sides, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{\Sigma}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\right)\left(\max_{k,\ell\in[K]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right\|_{2}\right)+\max_{k\in[K]}\sigma_{k}\,.

(44)

Recalling Assumptions (A2) and (A3), we get
\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\kappa^{\prime}
, for some constant
\kappa^{\prime}<\infty
. Therefore, by another application of Lemma
D.1
, and for sufficiently large constant
C>0
, we get
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2})\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{2}}
.

Combining the two probability bounds we get
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2})\geq 1-\frac{4}{n^{2}}
.
∎

While Assumption (A1) concerns the estimation error
\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{1}
, in our analysis we often need to control the
perturbation of different functions of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. A useful step for
these bounds is a control on the Lipschitz factor of

\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, which is the subject of the next lemma.

Lemma D.3
.

(Lipschitzness of

\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
) On event
\mathcal{E}
, defined in
Lemma
D.2
, the Lipschitz factor of

\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm is
O_{p}(\log(np))
, uniformly over all
i\in[n]
,
k\in[K]
. As a result,

\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\frac{|\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})|}{\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right\|_{1}}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)})\,.

Proof.

To prove the claim, it suffices to show that

\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\left(\left\|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty},\left|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial\sigma_{k}}\right|\right)=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}).

Recall that
\gamma_{i,k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{\pi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\pi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})}
, with
\phi_{k}
given by (
5
). A simple algebraic calculation shows that for a function of form
f_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{c_{k}e^{z_{k}}}{\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}e^{z_{\ell}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}f_{k}=f_{k}(z)-f_{k}^{2}(z)\,.

(45)

Applying this result, we obtain

\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}}=(\gamma_{i,k}-\gamma^{2}_{i,k})\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}.

Therefore, on the event
\mathcal{E}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\left|\gamma_{i,k}-\gamma^{2}_{i,k}\right||y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}|\cdot\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\sigma_{k}^{2}}C^{2}\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}=O(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}),

where we used the definition of event
\mathcal{E}
and the fact that since
0\leq\gamma_{i,k}\leq 1
,
\gamma_{i,k}(1-\gamma_{i,k})\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

Similarly, we can bound the partial derivative with respect to
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}
. By another application of (
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{i,k}-\gamma^{2}_{i,k}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma_{i,k}-\gamma^{2}_{i,k}|\;\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}=O(\sqrt{\log(np)})\,,

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\left\|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq O(\sqrt{\log(np)})\,.

Finally, we bound the partial derivative with respect to
\sigma_{k}
. By another application of (
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\gamma_{i,k}}{\partial\sigma_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{i,k}-\gamma^{2}_{i,k})\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{3}}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})^{2}=O(\log(nK))\,.

This completes the proof.

∎

Lemma D.4
.

(Mixture Estimation Error)

\mathcal{E}

D.2

\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}}\right).

\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq\underbrace{\sup_{i,k}{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\sup_{i,k}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|}_{(b)}.

(a)
, we have

(a)\leq\sup_{k}\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|=\sup_{k}\frac{|\widehat{\sigma}_{k}-\sigma_{k}|\;|\widehat{\sigma}_{k}+\sigma_{k}|}{\sigma_{k}^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}=O_{p}\left(\eta_{{\sf est}}\right)\,,

(b)

D.3

\sup_{i,k}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}O_{p}(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{1})=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}}\right),

where in the last step we used Assumption (A1) and (A3).
∎

The next lemma is a concentration result on the covariate vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}

Lemma D.5
.

Let
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

\boldsymbol{\xi}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{\ell\in[p]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|-\mathbb{E}\left[\left|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|\right]\right|\geq t\right\}\leq 2p\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{C^{2}},\frac{nt}{C}\right)\right)\,,

and

\mathbb{E}\left[|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle|\right]\leq C,

for some positive constant
C
.

Proof.

To obtain the first result, we first note that
\left|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|

is a product of two sub-Gaussian random variables and thus is a sub-exponential
variable. Applying Theorem 2.8.1 of
Vershynin 2018
to a fixed
\ell\in[p]
, we get that for
C\geq\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|-\mathbb{E}\left[\left|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|\right]\right|\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left(\frac{nt^{2}}{C^{2}},\frac{nt}{C}\right)\right),

(46)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{1}}
is the sub-exponential norm of a random variable and
c>0
is an absolute constant.

To obtain our intended bound, we compute the sub-exponential norms

\left\|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
. By Lemma 2.7.7 of
Vershynin 2018
, we have

\displaystyle\|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{i,\ell}\|_{\psi_{2}}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\ell},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}C_{\Sigma}=:C\,,

(47)

where we used that
\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\|_{{\rm op}}\leq C_{\Sigma}
and

\|{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
, for some constants

C^{\prime},C_{\Sigma}
, per Assumption (A2).

To prove the second part of the lemma, we note that by definition

\|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\|_{\psi_{1}}=\sup_{q\geq 1}q^{-1}\mathbb{E}[|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle|^{q}]^{1/q}\,,

by which we have

\mathbb{E}[|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle|]\leq\|x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq C\,,

for all
\ell\in[p]
.
∎

Lemma D.6
.

(Covariance Matrix Estimation
Error) Let

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{ik}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma_{1k}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}\right].

Consider a set of unit-norm vectors
\mathcal{C}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{p^{m}}\}
, for a fixed integer
m\geq 1
. Under Assumption (A2), and on the event
\mathcal{E}
defined in Lemma
D.2
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}=O_{p}\left(\eta_{{\sf est}}\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}\right)\,,

and

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\cdot\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|.

For the first component, we leverage Lemma
D.4
, which proves a
O_{p}(\eta_{{\sf est}}\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)})
bound.
For the second component, we
use Lemma
D.5
with
t=C\sqrt{\frac{2m\log(p)}{cn}}
and union bound over the set
\mathcal{C}
, which gives

\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right|=O_{p}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right)=O_{p}(1).

Putting them together, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\left(\eta_{{\sf est}}\sqrt{\log(np)\log(nK)}\right)\,.

To prove the second result, we have

\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})\boldsymbol{\xi}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right]\right\}\right|.

As shown in (
47
),
x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle
have bounded sub-exponential norm and since
\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}
is bounded, we have that
\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle

has bounded sub-exponential norm. Therefore, by Theorem 2.8.1 of
Vershynin 2018
(similar to Lemma
D.5
)
and a union bound over
\ell\in[p]
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}
, we obtain,

\sup_{\ell\in[p],\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}x_{i,\ell}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle\right]\right\}\right|=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right),

which completes the proof of the second part.
∎

Lemma D.7
.

(Fisher Information Estimation Error) Let

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T},

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma^{2}_{k}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{1k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T}\right].

Under Assumptions (A1), (A2) and (A3), We have

\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}=O_{p}\left(\sqrt{\log^{3}(np)\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}}\right),

and

\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}=O_{p}\left(\log(np)\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{n}}\right).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|x_{i,r}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\frac{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\right|\right)\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|x_{i,r}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\underbrace{\sup_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}\right|\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{4}}\right|}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{{\sigma_{k}^{4}}}\left|{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}\right|}_{(b)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\ \ \cdot\underbrace{\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|x_{i,r}\right|\right)}_{(c)}.

On the event
\mathcal{E}
we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}|+\sup_{i\in[n],k\in[K]}|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}-\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\log(nK)}+(\sup_{i\in[n]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty})\left(\sup_{k\in[K]}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}-\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sqrt{\log(nK)}+\sqrt{\log(np)}\eta_{{\sf est}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sqrt{\log(np)}\right)\,.

(48)

Therefore part
(a)
can be bounded as follows:

\sup_{i\in[n]}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\right|\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{4}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|1-\frac{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{4}}\right|\sup_{i\in[n]}\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}=O_{p}\left(\eta_{{\sf est}}\log(np)\right),

where we used that
\gamma_{i,k}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\leq 1
and
|\widehat{\sigma}_{k}-\sigma_{k}|=O_{p}(\eta_{{\sf est}})
per Assumption (A1).

For
(b)
, we write

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|(\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}\right|+\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|{\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|{\left(\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}\right|+\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|\gamma^{2}_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\left(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)\right)\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}+y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{2}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}|\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\left|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right|^{2}+\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\left(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)\right|\left|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}+y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{2}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}|\gamma_{ik}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\gamma_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\left|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right|^{2}+\sup_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right\|_{1}\left|y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}+y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{k}\right|

\displaystyle=O(\log^{3/2}(np)\sqrt{\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}})+O(\sqrt{\log(nK)}\sqrt{\log(np)}\eta_{{\sf est}})

\displaystyle=O(\log^{3/2}(np)\sqrt{\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}})\,,

where in the penultimate step, we bounded the first term using
Lemma
D.3
together with Assumption
(A1) and we bounded the second term using definition of event

\mathcal{E}
along with (
48
).

Finally, for
(c)
, we use
Lemma
D.5
with
\boldsymbol{\xi}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}
for
r\in[p]
followed by a union bound over
r
to get

\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i,\ell}\right|\left|x_{i,r}\right|=O_{p}(1).

Putting
(a)
,
(b)
, and
(c)
together, we have

\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}=O_{p}(\log^{3/2}(np)\sqrt{\log(nK)}\eta_{{\sf est}}).

To prove the second result, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}\geq t;\mathcal{E}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}\geq t;\mathcal{E}\right)+\frac{4}{n^{2}}\,,

(49)

where we used the result of Lemma
D.2
to bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})
.
We next write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}\geq t;\mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}{\left(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k}\right)^{2}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq t;\mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\Big(\sup_{i\in[n]}(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{k})^{2}\Big)\cdot\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq t;\mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq\frac{t}{C^{2}\log(np)};\mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq\frac{t}{C^{2}\log(np)}\right)\,,

(50)

where the second last step follows from definition of event
\mathcal{E}
.

Now by using equation (
47
) with
\boldsymbol{\xi}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}
, we have that
x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}
is sub-exponential and since

\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}

is bounded, we get that
\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}

is a sub-exponential random variable. Thus, by Bernstein’s inequality (see e.g.
(
Vershynin 2018
, Theorem 2.8.1)
)
and union bound over the
p^{2}
choices of
\ell,r
, we obtain,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq\frac{t}{C^{2}\log(np)}\right)\leq 2p^{2}\exp\left(-c\frac{nt^{2}}{\log^{2}(np)}\right)\,.

Therefore by choosing
t=2\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{cn}}\log(np)
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\ell,r\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}_{ik}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\sigma_{k}^{4}}x_{i,\ell}x_{i,r}\right]\right|\geq\frac{2}{C^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{cn}}\right)\leq 2p^{-2}\,.

(51)

∎

Combining equations (
49
), (
50
) and (
51
) we arrive at

\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{I}}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}^{\beta}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right|_{\infty}=O_{p}\left(\log(np)\sqrt{\frac{\log(p)}{n}}\right)\,,

which completes the proof of the second claim.
