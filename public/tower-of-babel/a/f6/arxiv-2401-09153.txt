Abstract
. This paper deals with the question of prescribing the Gaussian curvature on a disk and the geodesic curvature of its boundary by means of a conformal deformation of the metric. We restrict ourselves to a symmetric setting in which the Gaussian curvature is negative, and we are able to give general existence results. Our approach is variational, and solutions will be searched as critical points of an associated functional. The proofs use a perturbation argument via the monotonicity trick of Struwe, together with a blow-up analysis and Morse index estimates. We also give a nonexistence result that shows that, to some extent, the assumptions required for existence are necessary.

1.
Introduction

Let
(\Sigma,g_{0})
denote a compact Riemannian surface
\Sigma
equipped with a certain metric
g_{0}
. We say that another metric
g
is conformal to
g_{0}
if
{g}=\rho\,g_{0}
, where
\rho:\Sigma\to(0,+\infty)
is the conformal factor.
A classical problem in Geometry, dating back to
[
8
,
27
]
, is the prescription of the Gaussian curvature on a compact Riemannian surface under a conformal change of
the metric. In this case if we take the conformal factor as
\rho=e^{u}
, the curvature transforms according to the law:

-\Delta u+2K_{0}(x)=2K(x)e^{u}.

Here
\Delta
stands for the Laplace-Beltrami operator associated to the metric
g_{0}
, and
K_{0}
,
{K}
denote the Gaussian curvatures with respect to
g_{0}
and
g
, respectively. The solvability of this equation has been studied for several decades, and it is not possible to give here a comprehensive list of references. The case
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
is known as the Nirenberg problem, and reveals to be particularly delicate. We refer the interested reader to Chapter 6 in the book
[
1
]
.

If
\Sigma
has a boundary, other than the Gaussian curvature in
\Sigma
it is natural to prescribe also the geodesic curvature of
\partial\Sigma
. Denoting by
h_{0}
and
{h}
the geodesic curvatures of the boundary with respect to
g_{0}
and
{g}
respectively, we are led to the boundary value problem:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2K_{0}(x)=2K(x)e^{u}&\text{in }\Sigma,\\
\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+2{h}_{0}(x)=2h(x)e^{u/2}&\text{on }\partial\Sigma.\\
\end{array}\right.

The case of constant curvatures
K
,
h
has been considered in
[
11
]
, where the author used a parabolic flow to obtain solutions in the limit. Some classification results for the half-plane are also available in
[
20
,
29
,
39
]
. The case of nonconstant curvatures was first addressed in
[
16
]
, see also
[
2
,
33
]
for related work. In particular, the case
K<0
has been relatively well understood in
[
33
]
for non simply connected surfaces. Recently, some existence results have been provided for all genuses by using a new mean-field approach in
[
4
]
. The blow-up analysis of solutions in this framework has also been studied, see
[
13
,
33
]
for surfaces with negative Euler characteristic.

If
\Sigma=\mathbb{D}
, by the classical Uniformization Theorem we can pass to the standard metric via a conformal change of the metric. Hence we can assume that
g_{0}
is the standard metric on the disk, and equation (
1.1
) becomes:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u=2K(x)e^{u}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}+2=2h(x)e^{u/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Integrating (
1.2
) we obtain the following identity, which is nothing but the Gauss-Bonnet theorem for the metric
{g}
:

(1.3)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}Ke^{u}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}he^{u/2}=2\pi.

In particular, if
K<0
, this forces
h
to be positive somewhere.

The problem (
1.2
) has been treated in several works. For example, the case
h=0
and
\partial_{\nu}K=0
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
has been treated in
[
15
]
passing to a Nirenberg problem via reflection (see also
[
23
]
). The case
K=0
has attracted more attention, see
[
14
,
24
,
28
,
18
,
32
]
. A flow approach has been applied in
[
21
]
to prove the existence of solutions with constant geodesic curvature. Recently, this approach has been generalized to the case of positive and nonconstant curvatures,
[
36
]
.

However, very few existence results are available on equation (
1.2
) when both curvatures are not constant. If both curvatures are positive the existence has been addressed in
[
17
]
(under symmetry assumptions) and more recently in
[
35
]
. We would like to stress that if
K\geq 0
,
h\geq 0
, then the total area and length:

\int_{\mathbb{D}}Ke^{u},\ \int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}he^{u/2},

are a priori bounded by the Gauss-Bonnet formula (
1.3
). Moreover, as it will be commented later, the existence of solutions is linked to the blow-up analysis of (
1.2
). If both curvatures are nonnegative, one can use the blow-up analysis of
[
26
]
: the blow-up point is unique, located at
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and the asymptotic profile is well understood. On the other hand, the existence of blowing-up solutions has been recently proved (see
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
) via singular perturbation methods.

In this paper we plan to give general existence results to (
1.2
) when
K(x)<0
. This is probably the most intricate scenario: first, because the Gauss-Bonnet does not give bounds on area and length. Second, because for
K<0
the blow-up analysis is much more complex, different types of blow-up points could appear and the area and length could be unbounded, see
[
33
]
. For these reasons, in this paper we restrict ourselves to a symmetric setting, as a first step in this direction.

The problem (
1.2
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation of the energy functional
\mathcal{I}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

(1.4)

\mathcal{I}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}-2K(x)e^{u}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2u-4h(x)e^{u/2}\right).

Note that the interior term and the boundary one might be in competition, and a priori it is not clear whether
\mathcal{I}
is bounded from below or not. This phenomenon is studied in
[
33
]
, where the following scale-invariant function is introduced:

\mathfrak{D}:\partial\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \ \mathfrak{D}(x)=\frac{h(x)}{\sqrt{|K(x)|}}.

This function has an important role in the behavior of the functional
\mathcal{I}
, as well as in the blow-up analysis of solutions to (
1.2
).

We now set the following assumptions:

(
H_{1}
)

K:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ is a }\mathcal{C}^{1}\text{ function such that }K\leq 0\text{ in }\mathbb{D}\text{ and }K<0\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{D},

(
H_{2}
)

h:\partial\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ is a }\mathcal{C}^{1}\text{ function such that }\mathfrak{D}(p)>1\text{ for some }p\in\partial\mathbb{D}\text{, and}

(
H_{3}
)

\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}(p)\neq 0\text{ for any }p\in\partial\mathbb{D}\text{ such that }\mathfrak{D}(p)=1.

Notice that
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}
means the derivative of
\mathfrak{D}
with respect to the tangential direction
\tau
along
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

We also assume certain symmetry for both curvatures. Two types of symmetry groups
G
will be considered. The first one is the group generated by
g_{k}
the rotation of angle
2\pi/k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 2
. The second one is the radially symmetric case
G=O(2)
. We say that a function
f
is
G
-symmetric if
f(x)=(f\circ g)(x)
for all
g\in G
and for all
x
in the domain of
f
. Then, we assume that:

(
G
)

K\text{ and }h\text{ are }G\text{-symmetric, where either }G=\langle g_{k}\rangle\text{ or }G=O(2).

Let us point out that if
G=O(2)
then
\mathfrak{D}
is constant and hence (

H
2

) implies that (

H
3

) is trivially satisfied.

Our main result in this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Under hypotheses (

H
1

), (

H
2

), (

H
3

) and (

G

), the problem (
1.2
) admits a
G
-symmetric solution.

We will show that under hypotheses (

H
1

), (

H
2

) the functional
\mathcal{I}
is not bounded from below, and it exhibits a mountain-pass geometry. However the Palais-Smale property is not known to hold for the functional
\mathcal{I}
. We bypass this problem via the well-known
monotonicity trick
of Struwe, combined with a Morse index bound, as in
[
33
]
. In this paper we take profit of the abstract setting given in
[
7
, Section 4]
, also in
[
10
]
. By doing so, we are able to perturb the problem (
1.2
) and obtain solutions to such approximating problems with an uniform Morse index bound.

The next step of the proof is to show convergence of the approximating solutions
u_{n}
, which is equivalent to show uniform boundedness from above. Reasoning by contradiction, we assume that
u_{n}
is a blowing-up sequence of solutions. At this point, our proofs for the case
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle
and
G=O(2)
are different.

If
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle
we make use of the blow-up analysis peformed in
[
33
]
. In sum, there may be two kind of blow-up points: those related to bubbles and those related to
1D
asymptotic profiles. The second type is excluded by assumption (

H
3

), and the first type is forbidden thanks to (

G

). However, in order to apply this blow-up analysis, one needs a uniform bound on the Morse index of the approximating solutions. Observe that our variational argument implies a uniform bound only on the
G
-symmetric Morse index. Hence we need to come back to the proof of
[
33
]
and adapt it to our
G
-symmetric setting.

The case
G=O(2)
is simpler and the blow-up analysis of
[
33
]
is not needed. We are able to exclude blow-up in this case by usual ODE techniques. Quite remarkably, the assumption
\mathfrak{D}>1
appears naturally in this computation.

Finally, we prove the following result of nonexistence of solutions that shows that assumption (

H
2

) is necessary.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function and a Hölder continuous function
h:\partial\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Assume that there exists
c_{0}>0
such that
K(x)\leq-c^{2}_{0}
for all
x\in\mathbb{D}
and
h(x)\leq c_{0}
for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then, the problem (
1.2
) is not solvable.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on a sub-super solution method, first, to pass to the case
K=-1
. Then we use the classification of Liouville for the solutions of the problem:

-\Delta u=-2e^{u}\qquad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{D}.

In this way, we can immerse via a local isometry
(\mathbb{D},e^{u}dz)
as a subdomain of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}
. A typical contact argument with disks in
\mathbb{H}
concludes the proof.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we use a variational argument to find approximate solutions to the problem. A blow-up analysis of such solutions is performed in Section 3, which implies compactness and concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Section 4 is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In the final Appendix we evaluate the functional
\mathcal{I}
on suitably defined test functions, showing that this functional is unbounded from below.

Notation:
We shall use the symbols
o(1)
,
O(1)
in a standard way to denote quantities that converge to 0 or are bounded, respectively. Analogously, we will write
o(\rho)
,
O(\rho)
to denote quantities that, divided by
\rho
, converge to
0
or are bounded, respectively.

Moreover, throughout this paper we denote
\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}_{G}(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathcal{C}^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
the subspace of
G
-symmetric functions. Analogously, we denote the Sobolev space of
G
-symmetric functions by
H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})
.

2.
Preliminaries and approximate solutions

In this section we perform the variational study of the energy functional
\mathcal{I}
defined in (
1.4
). In this regard, a useful tool in the analysis will be the well-known Moser-Trudinger type inequality. Let us recall a weak version for the boundary term on the disk, which is called the Lebedev-Milin inequality (see
[
34
]
):

(2.1)

16\pi\log{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+4\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}u.

We begin by showing that
\mathcal{I}
admits a min-max geometry.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume (

H
1

), (

H
2

) and (

G

), then there exist
u_{0},u_{1}\in H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})
such that

c=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\mathcal{I}\left(\gamma(t)\right)>\max\left\{\mathcal{I}(u_{0}),\mathcal{I}(u_{1})\right\},

where
\Gamma=\left\{\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow H_{G}^{1}(\mathbb{D})\mbox{ continuous }:\gamma(0)=u_{0},\gamma(1)=u_{1}\right\}
.

The proof of Proposition
2.1
requires two preliminary results:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that (

H
1

) is satisfied, and take
\delta>0
. Then
\mathcal{I}|_{M_{\delta}}
is bounded from below, where:

M_{\delta}:=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}):\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}=\delta\right\}.

Proof.

The proof is a direct consequence of the inequality (
2.1
), since:

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(u)\geq&8\pi\log{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u/2}}-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4h(x)e^{u/2}\\
\geq&8\pi\log{\delta}-4\delta\mathcal{H},\end{split}

where
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}=\max_{\partial\mathbb{D}}{h(x)}}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If (

H
1

), (

H
2

) and (

G

) are satisfied, then there exists a sequence
u_{n}\in H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\mathcal{I}(u_{n})\rightarrow-\infty\text{ and }\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}/2}\rightarrow+\infty.

Proof.

The proof of this result is postponed to the Appendix
A
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let us evaluate the functional on constant functions
-n
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathcal{I}(-n)=-\int_{\mathbb{D}}2K(x)e^{-n}-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}2n-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}4h(x)e^{-n/2}\to-\infty\mbox{ as }n\to+\infty.

Fix
\delta>0
and take
u_{0}=-n
such that:

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{0}/2}<\delta\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}(u_{0})<\inf_{M_{\delta}}\,\mathcal{I}.

By Lemma
2.3
, there exists also
u_{1}
such that:

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{1}/2}>\delta\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}(u_{1})<\inf_{M_{\delta}}\,\mathcal{I}.

Observe that for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
there exists
t\in(0,1)
such that
\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{\gamma(t)/2}=\delta
. As a consequence,

c:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\mathcal{I}\left(\gamma(t)\right)\geq\inf_{M_{\delta}}\,\mathcal{I}>\max\{\mathcal{I}(u_{0}),\mathcal{I}(u_{1})\}.

∎

The main difficulty to prove Theorem
1.1
is the fact that we do not know whether the Palais-Smale sequences are bounded in this framework. We shall use the so-called monotonicity trick (see
[
37
,
25
]
), combined with Morse index bounds, to bypass this difficulty. This is an approach previously followed in
[
33
]
in the search of conformal metrics with prescribed curvatures, and also in
[
7
,
9
]
in other contexts. An abstract setting of the method has been given in
[
7
, Section 4]
, see also
[
10
]
for a version applicable to constrained functionals.

The goal is to find solutions of approximate problems:

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{n}+2\tilde{K}_{n}(x)=2K_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\
\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}_{n}(x)=2h_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\\
\end{array}\right.

These are critical points of the perturbed functional
\mathcal{I}_{n}:H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{I}_{n}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+2\tilde{K}_{n}(x)u-2K_{n}(x)e^{u}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2\tilde{h}_{n}(x)u-4h_{n}(x)e^{u/2}\right).

As usual, we define the Morse index of the solutions
u_{n}
as:

ind_{G}(u_{n}):=\max\{\dim\ E;E\subset H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})\mbox{ vector space}:\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u_{n})|_{E\times E}\mbox{ is negative definite}\}.

Observe that this is nothing but the number of negative eigenvalues of
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u_{n})
, counted with multiplicity, considering only
G
-symmetric eigenfunctions.

For later use, we define the quadratic form:

(2.3)

Q_{n}(\psi):=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u_{n})(\psi,\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2\psi^{2}K_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\psi^{2}h_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}/2}.

The main result of this section is the following:

Proposition 2.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, there exists a sequence of solutions
u_{n}
to problem (
2.2
), where
\tilde{K}_{n}(x)\rightarrow 0
,
\tilde{h}_{n}(x)\rightarrow 1
,
K_{n}(x)\rightarrow K(x)
,
h_{n}(x)\rightarrow h(x)
in the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
sense. Moreover,

(2.4)

ind_{G}(u_{n})\leq 1.

Proof.

Let us consider the following family of functionals

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\mathcal{I}(u)+\varepsilon\mathcal{J}(u),

where
\mathcal{J}:H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\mathcal{J}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla u|^{2}+|K(x)|\left(e^{u}-u\right).

First we claim that
\mathcal{J}
is coercive. To prove this, consider
u_{n}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
, with
\|u_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{D})}\to+\infty
, and we aim to prove that
\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\to+\infty
. We decompose
u_{n}=\bar{u}_{n}+\tilde{u}_{n}
, where

\bar{u}_{n}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{D}|}\int_{\mathbb{D}}u_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{u}_{n}=0.

Observe that
\|u_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}=\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}\to+\infty
. If
\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\to+\infty
, we use the inequality
e^{s}-s\geq 0
to conclude that

\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty.

Assume now that
\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq C
, which implies necessarily
\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\to+\infty
. Since
\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\leq||\overline{u}_{n}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}+||\tilde{u}_{n}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
and
||\tilde{u}_{n}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
is bounded by the Poincaré inequality, then
|\bar{u}_{n}|\to+\infty
. Furthermore, using the inequality
e^{s}-s\geq|s|
, we have:

\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\geq\int_{\mathbb{D}}|K(x)|\,|u_{n}|=\int_{\mathbb{D}}|K(x)|\,|\bar{u}_{n}+\tilde{u}_{n}|\geq\int_{\mathbb{D}}|K(x)\bar{u}_{n}|-|K(x)\tilde{u}_{n}|.

Finally, by applying Hölder and Poincaré inequalities, we obtain

\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\geq|\bar{u}_{n}|\int_{\mathbb{D}}|K(x)|-C\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}K(x)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\to+\infty\,\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

Then, the functional
\mathcal{J}
is coercive as claimed.

Now, fixing a sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and consider
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, we have that
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
shares the same min-max geometry of
\mathcal{I}
, i.e.,

c_{\varepsilon}:=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\left(\gamma(t)\right)>\max\left\{\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u_{0}),\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(u_{1})\right\},

where
\Gamma
stands for the class of admissible curves,

\Gamma=\left\{\gamma:[0,1]\to H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\mbox{ continuous }:\gamma(0)=u_{0},\gamma(1)=u_{1}\right\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{J}(u)
is strictly positive and coercive, and that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly Hölder continuous in bounded sets. We can now apply
[
7
, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
10
, Theorem 1]
to conclude the existence of a bounded Palais-Smale sequence at level
c_{\varepsilon}
,
\{v_{k}^{\varepsilon}\}
, for all
\varepsilon\in D\subset(0,\varepsilon_{0})
a set of full measure. Moreover,

(2.5)

\max\{dim\,Y:\ Y\subset H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})\mbox{ vector space: }\ \mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(v_{k}^{\varepsilon})(\phi,\phi)\leq\delta_{k}\|\phi\|^{2}\,\,\forall\phi\in Y\}\leq 1,

for some sequence
\delta_{k}\to 0
.

Up to a subsequence we can assume that
v_{k}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup v_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})
. By compactness we conclude that
v_{\varepsilon}
is a critical point of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
.

Take now
\varepsilon_{n}\in D
,
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, and
u_{n}=v_{\varepsilon_{n}}
. Clearly,
u_{n}
is a solution of (
2.2
) with:

(2.6)

\tilde{K}_{n}=\frac{|K(x)|\varepsilon_{n}}{1+2\varepsilon_{n}};\quad K_{n}=\frac{|K(x)|\left(1+\varepsilon_{n}/2\right)}{1+2\varepsilon_{n}};\quad\tilde{h}_{n}=\frac{1}{1+2\varepsilon_{n}};\quad h_{n}=\frac{h(x)}{1+2\varepsilon_{n}}.

Moreover we have the Morse index bound (
2.4
) as a consequence of (
2.5
).
∎

3.
Blow-up analysis and proof of Theorem
1.1

The results obtained in Section
2
motivate the study of blow-up solutions and the conditions for which such sequences are bounded from above. In this regard, we give the following general result, which is closely related to
[
33
, Theorem 1.4]
, see Remark
3.2
below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u_{n}
be a blowing-up sequence (namely,
\sup_{\mathbb{D}}u_{n}\to+\infty
) of solutions to

(3.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{n}+2\tilde{K}_{n}(x)=2K_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}}&\hbox{ in }\mathbb{D},\\
\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}_{n}(x)=2h_{n}(x)e^{u_{n}/2}&\hbox{ on }\partial\mathbb{D},\end{cases}

where
\tilde{K}_{n}
,
\tilde{h}_{n}
,
K_{n}
,
h_{n}
are
C^{1}
functions with
\tilde{K}_{n}\to\tilde{K}
,
K_{n}\to K
,
\tilde{h}_{n}\to\tilde{h}
and
h_{n}\to h
in the
C^{1}
. We assume that
K_{n}\leq 0
in
\mathbb{D}
and
K<0
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Define the singular set S as

S=\left\{p\in\mathbb{D}:\exists\,x_{n}\rightarrow p\text{ such that }u_{n}(x_{n})\rightarrow+\infty\right\},

and

\chi_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{K}_{n}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\tilde{h}_{n}.

Then the following assertions hold true:

(1)

S\subset\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(p)\geq 1\}
.

(2)

If
\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}
is bounded, then

S:=\{p_{1},\dots p_{j}\}\subset\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(p)>1\}.

In this case
|K_{n}|e^{u_{n}}\rightharpoonup\sum_{i=1}^{j}\beta_{i}\delta_{p_{i}}
,
h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}\rightharpoonup\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\beta_{i}+2\pi)\delta_{p_{i}}
for some suitable
\beta_{i}>0
.
In particular,

\ \chi_{n}\to 2\pi j
.

(3)

If
\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}
is unbounded, there exists a unit positive measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{D}
such that:

(a)

\displaystyle\frac{|K_{n}|e^{u_{n}}}{\int_{\mathbb{D}}|K_{n}|e^{u_{n}}}\rightharpoonup\sigma
,
\displaystyle\frac{h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}}{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}h_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}}\rightharpoonup\sigma|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
;

(b)

supp
\sigma\subset\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}(p)=0\}
.

(4)

Assume now that
u_{n}
are
G
-symmetric functions with
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle
, and
ind_{G}(u_{n})\leq m
for some
m
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}
is unbounded, then
S=S_{0}\cup S_{1}
, where

S_{0}\subset\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(p)=1,\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}(p)=0\}\neq\emptyset,

S_{1}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{j}\}\subset\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(p)>1\},j\leq\,k\,m.

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem
3.1
is basically contained in
[
33
, Theorem 1.4]
excepting for three aspects. First, the hypothesis of the nonpositivity of
K_{n}
inside the disk. By using the maximum principle, we exclude that the blow-up phenomenon is localized in the interior, and therefore we can focus on the boundary. Second, here we have assumed boundedness of the
G
-symmetric index, which is smaller than the index in the whole space
H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
. This question forces us to adapt the proof of
[
33
]
to our setting. Third, in the description of
S_{0}
we include the information
S_{0}\neq\emptyset
. However, this was not explicitly stated in
[
33
]
, so we include a comment on this question at the conclusion of the proof.

Proof.

Take
p\in S
as singular point. Due to the assumptions on
K
, we can take an open regular subset
\Omega\subset\overline{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{D}
such that
K(x)<0
in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\Omega
. By the analysis carried out in
[
33
, Theorem 1.4]
, we infer that
S\cap\{x\in\mathbb{D}:\ K(x)<0\}=\emptyset
. As a consequence,
u_{n}<C
on
\partial\Omega
. Let us now consider the function
v\in L^{\infty}\left(\Omega\right)
which solves the following problem:

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=2M&\hbox{ in }\Omega,\\
v=C&\hbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

where
M>0
is such that
|\tilde{K}_{n}(x)|\leq M
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
K_{n}\leq 0
the strong comparison principle implies that
u_{n}<v
on
\overline{\Omega}
. In particular, we obtain that
u_{n}
is bounded from above in
\Omega
, so that
S
is necessarily contained in
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Now, we will show that under a certain rescaling we obtain a limit solution defined in a half-plane. This is rather usual argument in the blow-up analysis of solutions of Liouville type equations, and comes back to
[
12
,
30
]
for the case of entire solutions. The main novelty here is that we do not know, a priori, that the quantities

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}},\ \int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}/2},

are uniformly bounded. This difficulty was addressed in
[
33
]
in a way that will be recalled below.

Let
p\in S
a singular point of
u_{n}
and consider a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
p
. Without loss of generality assume that
p=(1,0)
. By conformal invariance we can pass from
\mathcal{N}
to a half ball
B_{0}^{+}(r)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}=r,y\geq 0\}
.
This is justified by a classical argument, see for instance
[
22
]
. It is possible to straighten
\Gamma:=\mathcal{N}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}
by passing to
B^{+}_{0}(r)
using a suitable diffeomorphism, and due to the conformal invariance of problem (
3.1
) one can take this diffeomorphism as the following Möbius transform (in complex notation)

\Upsilon:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{D};\quad x\mapsto\Upsilon(x)=\frac{i-x}{x+i}.

Here and in what follows we denote
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ y>0\}
. Notice that the point
p
corresponds to the origin. Then, if we consider the function

w_{n}(x):=u_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)+2\log|\Upsilon^{\prime}(x)|=u_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)+2\log\frac{2}{|x+i|^{2}},

thanks to the conformal invariance, we have:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta w_{n}+2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)=2K_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)e^{w_{n}}&\text{in }B_{0}^{+}(r),\\
\\
\frac{\partial w_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\tilde{h}_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)=2h_{n}\left(\Upsilon(x)\right)e^{w_{n}/2}&\text{on }\Gamma_{0}^{+}(r),\\
\end{array}\right.

for some small
r>0
. Here
\Gamma_{0}^{+}(r)=(-r,r)\times\{0\}
is the straight portion of
\partial B_{0}^{+}(r)
.

Now, take a sequence
y_{n}
in
B_{0}^{+}(r)
such that
y_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
w_{n}(y_{n})\rightarrow+\infty
. Observe that, a priori, we do not know if
p
is isolated in
S
, and then we cannot assure that
y_{n}
is a sequence of local maxima. We bypass this difficulty by using Ekeland’s variational principle, which provides us with a sequence
x_{n}\in B_{0}^{+}(r)
such that

(1)

w_{n}(x_{n})\leq w_{n}(y_{n})
,

(2)

d(x_{n},y_{n})\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}
,

(3)

e^{\frac{-w_{n}(x_{n})}{2}}\leq e^{\frac{-w_{n}(z)}{2}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}}d(x_{n},z)
for every
z\neq x_{n}
,

where
\varepsilon_{n}=e^{\frac{-w_{n}(y_{n})}{2}}
.

As a consequence
x_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
w_{n}(x_{n})\rightarrow+\infty
. Thanks to the point (
3
), this new sequence is convenient to rescale and move to a limit problem. Indeed, it was proved in
[
33
, Proposition 5.1]
that, up to subsequence,

(3.2)

v_{n}\rightarrow v\text{ in }\mathcal{C}^{2}_{loc}\left(\mathbb{R}\times(-t_{0},+\infty)\right),

where
v_{n}
is a suitable rescaled function defined as

v_{n}(x):=w_{n}(\delta_{n}x+x_{n})+2\log\delta_{n},

for
x\in B_{n}:=B_{0}^{+}(\frac{r}{\delta_{n}})\cap B_{-\frac{x_{n}}{2\delta_{n}}}(\frac{r}{\delta_{n}})
and
\delta_{n}=e^{\frac{-w_{n}(x_{n})}{2}}\rightarrow 0
. In what follows we distinguish two cases:

Case 1
:

d(x_{n},\Gamma_{0}^{+}(r))=O(\delta_{n})\quad\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

Passing to a subsequence we can assume that
\frac{d(x_{n},\Gamma_{0}^{+}(r))}{\delta_{n}}\to t_{0}\geq 0
. Then, the function
v_{n}
solves

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v_{n}+2\delta_{n}^{2}\tilde{K}_{n}\left(\delta_{n}\Upsilon(x)+x_{n}\right)=2K_{n}\left(\delta_{n}\Upsilon(x)+x_{n}\right)e^{v_{n}}&\text{in }B_{n},\\
\\
\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial\nu}+2\delta_{n}\tilde{h}_{n}\left(\delta_{n}\Upsilon(x)+x_{n}\right)=2h_{n}\left(\delta_{n}\Upsilon(x)+x_{n}\right)e^{v_{n}/2}&\text{on }\Gamma_{n},\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\Gamma_{n}
is the straight portion of
\partial B_{n}
. In addition, thanks to (
3.2
), up to a translation,
v
is a solution of the limit problem

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=2K(0)e^{v}&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},\\
\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=2h(0)e^{v/2}&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

Let us recall that last problem admits solutions only if
\mathfrak{D}_{0}\geq 1
, and this proves
(1)
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{D}_{0}=1
the only solutions of (
3.4
) are given by:

v_{\lambda}(s,t)=2\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)-\log|K_{0}|,\quad\lambda>0.

For the case
\mathfrak{D}_{0}>1
there are many different solutions, which have been classified in
[
20
]
.

Case 2
:

\frac{d(x_{n},\Gamma_{0}^{+}(r))}{\delta_{n}}\to+\infty,\quad\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

In this situation the rescaled domains
B_{n}
invade all
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence, reasoning as before, up to subsequence we have

v_{n}\rightarrow v\text{ in }\mathcal{C}^{2}_{loc}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right),

which is a solution of the equation

-\Delta v=2K(0)e^{v}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}.

If
K(0)<0
it is well know, via Liouville’s formula, that the above problem does not admit any solution.

We now prove
(4)
, assume that
u_{n}
are
G
-symmetric functions with
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle
, and
ind_{G}(u_{n})\leq m
for some
m
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Next lemma uses the Morse index bound on
u_{n}
to reduce the possible solutions
v
. In order to do that, we define the Morse index of
v
as:

ind(v)=\sup\{dim\,E,\ E\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}):\ \tilde{Q}|_{E}\mbox{ is negative definite}\},

where
\tilde{Q}
is defined as:

\tilde{Q}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(|\nabla\psi|^{2}+2\psi^{2}K(0)e^{v}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\psi^{2}h(0)e^{v/2}.

Here
C_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}})
stands the set of test functions whose support is bounded.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
p\in S_{1}
, the Morse index of
v
is
1
. Moreover,
S_{1}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{j}\}
with
j\leq k\,m
.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that
ind(v)>1
. By
[
33
, Theorem 4.2]
,
ind(v)=+\infty
; this means that for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a vector space of dimension
l
, namely
E\subset\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\right)
, such that

\tilde{Q}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(|\nabla\psi|^{2}+2\psi^{2}K(0)e^{v}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\psi^{2}h(0)e^{v/2}<-\varepsilon^{2},

for all
\psi\in E
with
\|\psi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{D})}=1
.

On the other hand, let us define the subset

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{D}:\Upsilon^{-1}\left(z\right)\delta_{n}+x_{n}\in\supp(\psi)\right\}\subset\bar{\mathbb{D}},

which is, for
n
large enough, a neighborhood of
p=(1,0)
. Moreover, let
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle
be a symmetric group of order
k
such that every
g^{l}(\mathcal{M})
and
g^{j}(\mathcal{M})
are disjoint subsets,
0\leq l<j\leq k-1
. Now consider the following function

\psi_{n}:=\psi\left(\Upsilon^{-1}(z)\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Recalling the expression (
2.3
), following the argument of straightening, the scaling variables and the uniform convergence on compacts, we have

\begin{split}Q_{n}(\psi_{n})=&\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(|\nabla\psi_{n}|^{2}-2\psi_{n}^{2}K_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\psi_{n}^{2}h_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}/2}\\
=&\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(|\nabla\psi_{n}|^{2}-2\psi_{n}^{2}K_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\psi_{n}^{2}h_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}/2}\\
=&\int_{\Upsilon^{-1}(\mathcal{M})}\left(|\nabla\left(\psi_{n}\circ\Upsilon\right)|^{2}-2(\psi_{n}\circ\Upsilon)^{2}\left(K_{n}\circ\Upsilon\right)(z)e^{w_{n}}\right)\\
&-\int_{\Upsilon^{-1}(\partial\mathcal{M})}(\psi_{n}\circ\Upsilon)^{2}\left(h_{n}\circ\Upsilon\right)(z)e^{w_{n}/2}\\
=&\int_{B_{n}}\left(|\nabla\psi_{n}\left(\Upsilon\left(z\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\right)|^{2}-2\left(\psi_{n}\left(\Upsilon\left(z\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\right)\right)^{2}K_{n}\left(\Upsilon\left(z\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\right)e^{v_{n}}\right)\\
&-\int_{\Gamma_{n}}\left(\psi_{n}\left(\Upsilon\left(z\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\right)\right)^{2}h_{n}\left(\Upsilon\left(z\delta_{n}+x_{n}\right)\right)e^{v_{n}/2}\\
=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(|\nabla\psi|^{2}+2\psi^{2}K(0)e^{v}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\psi^{2}h(0)e^{v/2}<-\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

where
\partial\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}\cap\mathbb{D}
. Then one can construct the following
G
-symmetric function

\Psi_{n}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\psi_{n}\left(g^{j}(z)\right),

which is well defined since by definition
\psi_{n}
has compact and disjoint supports. Therefore, taking advantage of the symmetric group
G
and following the straightened and scaled argument, it allows us to see that

\begin{split}Q_{n}(\Psi_{n})&=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(|\nabla\Psi_{n}|^{2}-2\Psi_{n}^{2}K_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\Psi_{n}^{2}h_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}/2}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\int_{g^{j}(\mathcal{S})}\left(|\nabla\Psi_{n}|^{2}-2\Psi_{n}^{2}K_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{g^{j}(\mathcal{S}\cap\partial\mathbb{D})}\Psi_{n}^{2}h_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}/2}\right)\\
&=k\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(|\nabla\psi_{n}|^{2}-2\psi_{n}^{2}K_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathcal{M}}\psi_{n}^{2}h_{n}(z)e^{u_{n}/2}\right)\\
&=kQ_{n}(\psi_{n})<-\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

Moreover,
span\{\Psi_{n}:\ \psi\in E\}
is clearly a vector space of dimension
l
. By taking
l>m
, we obtain a contradiction with
ind_{G}(u_{n})\leq m
.

The aim now is to prove that
|S_{1}|\leq km
. Consider
u_{n}\in H^{1}_{G}(\mathbb{D})
a sequence of solutions to the problem (
3.1
) with
ind_{G}(u_{n})=m
. Let us argue again by contradiction and suppose that
|S_{1}|>k\cdot m
. Due to the
G
-symmetry, there exists at least
(m+1)k
singular points of
u_{n}
in
S_{1}
, namely
p_{i}
with
i=1,\ldots,(m+1)k
.

Moreover, if we divide
\mathbb{D}
into
k
identical disk sectors
T_{s}
, where
s=1,\ldots,k
, according to the order of the group
G
in each sector there must be at least
m+1
singular points. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can consider
m+1
singular points
p_{j}\in S\cap T_{1}
of
u_{n}
in
T_{1}
. We can assume that

B_{p_{j}}^{+}(r_{j})\cap B_{p_{l}}^{+}(r_{l})=\emptyset,\quad\text{for all }1\leq j<l\leq m+1.

As before, one can rescale the solutions
u_{n}
in each neighborhood until one obtains a profile
v
which is a solution of the boundary problem (
3.4
) and whose Morse is equal to
1
. But if so, one can take
m+1
compactly supported functions
\psi_{j}
defined in respective
1
-dimensional vector spaces of
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, such that

\tilde{Q}\left(\psi_{j}\right)<-\varepsilon^{2}_{j},\quad\text{for all }1\leq j\leq m+1.

We are able to define a
m+1
-dimensional vector space
\tilde{E}:=span(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{m+1})
generated by
m+1
compactly-supported functions such that the above quadratic form would be negative-definite on
\tilde{E}
. Following the same arguments as in the proof of the first assertion one can conclude that

, reaching a contradiction.
∎

Finally we show that under the assumptions of Theorem

(4),

. To see this, we recall the following result proved in

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

, then there exist fixed constants

such that

\int_{B_{p}(r)\cap\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}\leq C;\quad\int_{B_{p}(r)\cap\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}/2}\leq C;\quad u_{n}\rightarrow-\infty\text{ on }\partial B_{p}(r)\cap\mathbb{D}.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will divide the proof in two parts, according to the types of symmetry groups we have been working with.

Case 1:
G=\langle g_{k}\rangle

We consider the sequence
u_{n}
given by Proposition
2.4
, and we claim that they are uniformly bounded from above. From this one readily obtain compactness of solutions, and hence we can get a solution of (
1.2
) in the limit.

Reasoning by contradiction, assume that
\max\{u_{n}\}\to+\infty
, and apply Theorem
3.1
. Since the set:

\{p\in\partial\mathbb{D}:\mathfrak{D}(p)=1,\mathfrak{D}_{\tau}(p)=0\}

is empty, we conclude that
\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{u_{n}}
is bounded. By symmetry, we conclude that
S_{1}
contains at least
k
points. However, with the quantization given by
the second point of Theorem
3.1
, one obtains

\chi_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\tilde{K}_{n}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\tilde{h}_{n}\to 2k\pi,

which is a contradiction.

Case 2:
G=O(2)

In this case problem (
3.1
) can be written as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}_{n}-\frac{u^{\prime}_{n}}{r}+2\tilde{K}_{n}=2K_{n}(r)e^{u_{n}}&\text{in }0<r<1,\\
\\
u^{\prime}_{n}(1)+2\tilde{h}_{n}=2h_{n}(1)e^{u_{n}(1)/2},&\\
\\
u^{\prime}_{n}(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

Multiplying the main equation by
u^{\prime}_{n}
and integrating you get:

-\frac{u^{\prime}_{n}(1)^{2}}{2}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(u^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}{r}+2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(u_{n}(1)-u_{n}(0)\right)=\int_{0}^{1}2K_{n}(r)u^{\prime}_{n}e^{u_{n}}.

Now, integrating by parts and considering the boundary conditions we can write each of the sides of the above equation as

\begin{split}A_{1}:=&-2h_{n}^{2}(1)e^{u_{n}(1)}-2\tilde{h}_{n}^{2}+4h_{n}(1)\tilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}(1)/2}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(u^{\prime}_{n})^{2}}{r}+2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(u_{n}(1)-u_{n}(0)\right),\\
A_{2}:=&2K_{n}(1)e^{u_{n}(1)}-2K_{n}(0)e^{u_{n}(0)}-\int_{0}^{1}2K^{\prime}_{n}(r)e^{u_{n}}.\end{split}

After grouping the terms,
A_{2}-A_{1}=0
, we have the following inequality

2e^{u_{n}(1)}\left(K_{n}(1)+h_{n}^{2}(1)\right)-4h_{n}(1)\tilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}(1)/2}-2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(u_{n}(1)-u_{n}(0)\right)\leq\int_{0}^{1}2K^{\prime}_{n}(r)e^{u_{n}}.

Clearly,

\int_{0}^{1}2K^{\prime}_{n}(r)e^{u_{n}}\leq 2\max{\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}}{|K_{n}|}}\int_{0}^{1}|K_{n}|e^{u_{n}}=2\max{\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}}{|K_{n}|}}(h_{n}(1)e^{u_{n}(1)/2}-\chi_{n}),

where
\chi_{n}\to 2\pi
. Then,

\displaystyle 2e^{u_{n}(1)}\left(K_{n}(1)+h_{n}^{2}(1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4h_{n}(1)\tilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}(1)/2}+2\tilde{K}_{n}\left(u_{n}(1)-u_{n}(0)\right)+2\max{\frac{K_{n}^{\prime}}{|K_{n}|}}(h_{n}(1)e^{u_{n}(1)/2}-\chi_{n}).

Now recall that
u_{n}(0)
is bounded,
\tilde{h}_{n}\to 1
,
\tilde{K}_{n}\to 0
and
\mathfrak{D}>1
, and then we obtain that
e^{u_{n}(1)}
is uniformly bounded, and then
u_{n}(1)\leq C
for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, the maximum principle implies that
u_{n}\leq\bar{u}
, where
\bar{u}
solves:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\bar{u}=\sup_{n}\|\tilde{K}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}&\text{ in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\
\bar{u}=C&\text{ in }\partial\mathbb{D}.\end{array}\right.

This implies that
u_{n}
is bounded from above and we conclude.

∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we proof the non existence result of Theorem
1.2
. Observe that if
u
is a solution of (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=2\lambda^{2}K(x)e^{v}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\

The next lemma will be of use:

Lemma 4.1
.

If the problem (
1.2
) is solvable with
C^{0,\alpha}
functions
K
,
h
, and

, then there exists
\tilde{u}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\cap C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
a solution of:

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\tilde{u}=-2e^{\tilde{u}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\

where
\tilde{h}(x)\leq h(x)
for all

Proof.

Observe that a solution

of (
1.2
) solves:

-\Delta{u}(x)\leq-2e^{{u}}\qquad\text{ in }\mathbb{D}.

Now, let
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\bar{u}(x)=0&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\
\bar{u}=u&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\\
\end{array}\right.

By the maximum principle,
\bar{u}\geq u
. It follows from sub-super solution method, (see for instance
[
19
, Chapter 9]
) that we may find a solution
\tilde{u}
of the problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\tilde{u}=-2e^{\tilde{u}}&\text{in }\mathbb{D},\\
\\
\tilde{u}=u&\text{on }\partial\mathbb{D}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
\tilde{u}
solves (
4.1
) with:

\tilde{h}=e^{-\tilde{u}/2}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{u}}{\partial\nu}+2\right).

Moreover,
u\leq\tilde{u}\leq\bar{u}
on
\mathbb{D}
, which implies that:

\frac{\partial\tilde{u}}{\partial\nu}(x)\leq\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}(x)=2h(x)e^{u/2}-2=2h(x)e^{\tilde{u}/2}-2\quad\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{D}.

This implies that
\tilde{h}\leq h
. The regularity of
\tilde{u}
follows from classical Schauder estimates.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Under the radial assumptions and
\mathfrak{D}(x)=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}>1

Proof.

Let us remember the energy functional we are working with.

\mathcal{I}\left(\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}|^{2}+2e^{{\varphi}_{\mu}}\right)+\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\left(2\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}-4\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\sqrt{e^{{\varphi}_{\mu}}}\right).

As in the proof of the previous lemma, we will now estimate each of the parts of the above expression. First of all, note that we can control the gradient as in (

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla\varphi_{\mu}|^{2}+O(1),

where,

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla\varphi_{\mu}|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\frac{16dx}{(\mu^{2}-|x|^{2})^{2}}=32\pi\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho d\rho}{(\mu^{2}-\rho^{2})^{2}}=32\pi\cdot(C).

Moreover, the other integral defined inside the domain is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{\varphi_{\mu}}=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\frac{4\mu^{2}dx}{(\mu^{2}-|x|^{2})^{2}}=8\pi\mu^{2}\pi\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho d\rho}{(\mu^{2}-\rho^{2})^{2}}=8\pi\mu^{2}\cdot(C).

Therefore, to study the first integral of the energy functional it is sufficient to compute
(C)

(C)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho d\rho}{(\mu^{2}-\rho^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-\rho^{2}}\right]_{\rho=0}^{\rho=1}=\frac{1}{2\mu^{2}(\mu^{2}-1)}.

On the other hand, we have the boundary terms that can be controlled as follows

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}e^{\frac{\varphi_{\mu}}{2}}&=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}-|x|^{2}}dx=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\frac{4\pi\mu}{\mu^{2}-1},\end{split}

and,

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\varphi_{\mu}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}2\log\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}-|x|^{2}}=4\pi\log\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}-1}\leq o\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-1}\right).

In summary, the following estimates hold:

\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\nabla\varphi_{\mu}|^{2}\leq\frac{16\pi}{\mu^{2}-1}+o\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-1}\right),

\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{\varphi_{\mu}}\leq\frac{4\pi}{\mu^{2}-1}+o\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-1}\right),

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\mathfrak{D}e^{\frac{\varphi_{\mu}}{2}}=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\frac{4\pi}{\mu^{2}-1}+O(1),

\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\varphi_{\mu}\leq o\left(\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-1}\right).

And, since
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}>1
, as

, we can conclude that

\mathcal{I}\left(\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}\right)\leq\frac{16\pi}{\mu^{2}-1}\left(1-\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}\right)\to-\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}e^{\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}/2}\rightarrow+\infty,

as claimed.
