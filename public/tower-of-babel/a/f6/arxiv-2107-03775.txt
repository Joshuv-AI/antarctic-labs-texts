KOLMOGOROV BOUNDS FOR DECOMPOSABLE RANDOM VARIABLES AND SUBGRAPH COUNTING BY THE STEIN-TIKHOMIROV METHOD Peter Eichelsbacher * * * Ruhr-University Bochum, Faculty of Mathematics, IB 2/115, D-44780 Bochum, Germany. E-Mail: peter.eichelsbacher@rub.de and Benedikt Rednoß † † † Ruhr-University Bochum, Faculty of Mathematics, IB 2/95, D-44780 Bochum, Germany. E-Mail: benedikt.rednoss@rub.de


Abstract
We derive normal approximation bounds in the Kolmogorov distance for random variables posessing decompositions of Barbour et al. 1989 . We highlight the example of normalized subgraph counts in the Erdös-Rényi random graph. We prove a bound by generalizing the argumentation of Röllin 2021 , who used the Stein-Tikhomirov method to prove a bound in the special case of normalized triangle counts. Our bounds match the best available Wasserstein-bounds. Mathematics Subject Classifications (2020) : 05C80, 60E10. 60F05, Keywords: Berry-Esséen bound, central limit theorem, characteristic function, Erdös-Rényi random graph, Kolmogorov distance, normal approximation, Stein’s method, Stein-Tikhomirov method, subgraph count.



1  Introduction

In his work
[
18
]
, Tikhomirov combined elements of Stein’s method with the theory of characteristic functions to derive Kolmogorov bounds for the convergence rate in the central limit theorem for a normalized sum of a stationary sequence of random variables satisfying one of several weak dependency conditions.
The combination of elements of Stein’s method with the theory of characteristic functions is sometimes called
Stein-Tikhomirov method
.

Arras et al. 2017
successfully used the Stein-Tikhomirov method to bound the convergence rate in contexts with non-Gaussian targets.

Röllin 2021
used the Stein-Tikhomirov method to bound the convergence rate in the Kolmogorov distance for normal approximation of normalized triangle counts in the Erdös-Rényi random graph.

Consider a random graph
G(n,p)
on
n\in\mathbb{N}
vertices.
Each edge between two vertices is included with probability
p\in[0,1]
independently of all other edges.

p
may depend on
n
.
This model is called
Erdös-Rényi random graph
, though it was first introduced by
Gilbert 1959
.
To a given graph
G
,
let
v_{G}
denote the number of vertices of
G
,

e_{G}
the number of edges of
G
,

H\subset G
a subgraph of
G
.

Now let
{G_{0}}
be a graph with
e_{G_{0}}>0
.
It will be useful to introduce the quantity

\displaystyle\Psi:=\Psi_{{G_{0}}}:=\displaystyle\min\big\{n^{v_{H}}\cdot p^{e_{H}}\,\big|\,H\subset{G_{0}},e_{H}>0\big\},

which was used by
Barbour et al. 1989
.

Let
W
be the normalized number of subgraphs of
G(n,p)
which are isomorphic to
{G_{0}}
.
Let
Z
denote a standard Gaussian random variable.

Throughout this paper,
C
(with or without index) will denote a constant, which may vary from line to line.
The indices of
C
will denote the quantities on which
C
depends.

Ruciński 1988
provided a necessary and sufficient condition for asymptotic normality of
W
:

W
converges in distribution to
Z
if and only if

\displaystyle\Psi\xlongrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty\quad\text{and}\quad n^{2}\cdot(1-p)\xlongrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty,

(see Theorem 2 in
[
16
]
).
Shortly thereafter,
Barbour et al. 1989
proved the following bound for the convergence rate in the Wasserstein distance
d_{1}
,
using Stein’s method
(see Theorem 2 in
[
2
]
):

\displaystyle d_{1}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{G_{0}}\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n\sqrt{1-p}}&\text{ if }p>\frac{1}{2},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Psi}}&\text{ if }p\leq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Here
d_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
2
) with
\mathcal{H}
being the class of real-valued Lipschitz functions with Lipschitz constant less than or equal to 1.
Subsequently, it has been a long standing problem, whether the same rate could be achieved in the Kolmogorov distance
d_{K}
. Here the class
of real-valued test function is chosen to be the indicators of half intervals
x\mapsto 1_{\{x\leq w\}}
with
w\in\mathbb{R}
. In general, the Kolmogorov distance
satisfies the bound

d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leq\sqrt{d_{1}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))},

(1)

which is in most cases a crude bound. Typically, it can be shown that the left hand side of (
1
) is of the same order as the bound for
d_{1}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))
. However, the derivation of the latter requires a much more delicate argument.
Throughout the years, there were several results for special cases:
For triangle counts with
p=\theta_{n}n^{-\alpha}
,
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
(\theta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
bounded and
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\theta_{n}>0
,
Krokowski et al. 2017
proved a bound for the Kolmogorov distance, using Malliavin-Stein method (see Theorem 1.1 in
[
8
]
).
However, their rate in the Kolmogorov distance does not match the rate of
Barbour et al. 1989
for the Wasserstein distance.
For arbitrary subgraphs and fixed
p\in(0,1)
,
Krokowski et al. 2017
as well as
Féray et al. 2017
derived bounds in the Kolmogorov distance (see Theorem 1.2 in
[
8
]
and Example 37 in
[
5
]
) similar to the best known Wasserstein bound by
Barbour et al. 1989
.
For arbitrary
p
, but only for triangles as subgraphs to count,
Röllin 2021
derived Kolmogorov bounds by means of the Stein-Tikhomirov method, which match the best known Wasserstein bounds (see Theorem 1.1 in
[
14
]
).
Finally, based on the work of
Krokowski et al. 2017
,

Privault and Serafin 2020
proved for the general setting of an arbitrary subgraph and arbitrary
p
, that

\displaystyle d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{G_{0}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-p)\cdot\Psi}}

(see Theorem 4.2 in
Privault and Serafin 2020
),
which is the same rate as the one proven by
Barbour et al. 1989
for the Wasserstein distance. From a point of view of probability theory,
the proof presented in
[
12
]
is quite involved. First one has to develop the Malliavin approach for statistics of Bernoulli random variables,
or for Rademacher sequences, respectively. This was worked out in
[
8
]
, pages 1077–1087. Next the approach was combined
to the Stein method to derive general Berry-Esséen bounds, see Theorem 3.1 in
[
8
]
. The authors in
[
12
]
modified this
general bound in Proposition 2.1. Next they derive new bounds for sums of discrete multiple integrals applying the well-known multiplication formula of Wiener
integrals, see Theorem 3.1 in
[
12
]
. They show that the number of subgraphs can be represented as a finite sum of discrete multiple integrals. Finally they derive laborious bounds for certain corresponding norms of contractions to obtain the result. In contrast to the Malliavin-Stein
approach,
[
2
]
represented the number of subgraphs of
G(n,p)
which are isomorphic to
{G_{0}}
as a decomposable random variable in the sense, that
it is a sum of weakly dependent variables. In section 3, the so called BKR-decomposition will be introduced. In the work of
Raič 2003
, the BKR-decomposition
was considered as well. The author managed to prove a Berry-Esséen theorem for BKR-decompositions, if the different components of this decomposition are assumed to be bounded, see Theorem 5.1 in
[
13
]
. But interesting enough, a direct application of this result – in the case of counting subgraphs – does not lead to an optimal result.

In our paper we generalize the argumentation of
Röllin 2021
to provide a simpler proof for the result of
Privault and Serafin 2020
.
The starting point will be the quite natural BKR-decomposition which will be introduced in section 3.
In particular, we will show the following theorem, which is our main theorem.

Theorem 1.1

For
p_{0}\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))\leq{}

\displaystyle C_{{G_{0}},p_{0}}\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n\sqrt{1-p}}&\text{ if }p>p_{0},\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Psi}}&\text{ if }p\leq p_{0}.\end{cases}

Our proof starts with the following considerations:

Let
W
be an integrable random variable with characteristic function
\varphi
.
Following the Stein-Tikhomirov method we are interested in the quantity

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[iWe^{itW}]+t\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{itW}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(iW+t)\cdot e^{itW}]

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

To analyze this quantity, we want to construct a stochastic process
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}\cdot e^{itW}]

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}

and
H_{t}
integrable.
Note that this does not necessarily require, that
t^{2}\cdot H_{t}=iW+t
.
It is sufficient if there is a stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\cdot e^{itW}]

\displaystyle=0

and

\displaystyle t^{2}\cdot H_{t}

\displaystyle=iW+t+Y_{t}

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

Under these assumptions we find

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}\cdot e^{itW}]

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{itW}]+t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[(H_{t}-\mathbb{E}[H_{t}])(e^{itW}-\mathbb{E}[e^{itW}])\big]

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]\cdot\varphi(t)+t^{2}\cdot\text{Cov}(H_{t},e^{-itW}).

In
section 2
we will use the Stein-Tikhomirov method to derive a bound for
d_{K}(W,Z)
,
which only depends on upper bounds of
|\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]|
and
|\text{Cov}(H_{t},e^{-itW})|
.
Under the additional assumption, that there is a decomposition of
W
similar to the decomposition used by
Barbour et al. 1989
,
we will find general upper bounds for
|\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]|
and
|\text{Cov}(H_{t},e^{-itW})|
in
section 3
.
Finally, in
section 4
we apply the upper bounds from the previous section to the subgraph counting problem and will prove
section 1
.

2  The Stein-Tikhomirov method

The combination of elements of Stein’s method with the theory of characteristic functions is called Stein-Tikhomirov method.

2.1  Stein’s method

In his work
[
17
]
, Stein derived a bound for the Kolmogorov distance between a sum of dependent random variables and a Gaussian target.
The method he used was later generalized and is now known as
Stein’s method
.
The most common distances between two random variables
X
and
Z
can be written as

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}}(X,Z)=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\big|

(2)

with
\mathcal{H}
some set of test-functions.
Usually,
Z
is the target random variable in an approximation process.
The main idea of Stein’s method is to construct a so called
Stein-operator

\mathcal{A}
on a set of functions
M

to characterize the distribution of the target random variable
Z
in the following sense:
The random variable
X
has the same distribution as
Z
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X)]=0
for all
f\in M
.
Now the right side of (
2
) shall be expressed by
\mathcal{A}
and
X
,
so that the target random variable
Z
does not directly appear anymore.
This leads to

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}}(X,Z)=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f_{h}(X)]\big|

(3)

with
f_{h}
a solution of the so called
Stein-equation

\mathcal{A}f_{h}=h-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]
.
In many cases the right side of (
3
) is much easier to bound than the right side of (
2
).

In case of a Gaussian target
Z
, the most common Stein-operator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x):=f^{\prime}(x)-x\cdot f(x)

(4)

for all absolutely continuous functions
f
, for which the expectation
\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(Z)]
exists
(see e.g. Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
).
Throughout the years, Stein-operators for many other target distributions have been constructed.
For a more detailed introduction to Stein’s method, see e.g.
[
15
]
.

2.2  Application to characteristic functions

Following the argumentation of
Tikhomirov 1980
,
the Stein-Tikhomirov method focuses on characteristic functions.
Let
\varphi_{X}
and
\varphi_{Z}
be the characteristic functions of the random variables
X
and
Z
.
The main goal of the Stein-Tikhomirov method is to derive a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
.
This bound is then used to derive a bound for a probability distance:
The smoothing-lemma by Berry and Esséen provides a mean to transfer a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
to a bound for the Kolmogorov distance:

Theorem 2.1 (Berry-Esséen smoothing-lemma (see e.g.
[
9
]
))

If at least one of the random variables
X
and
Z
has a density bounded by
b>0
, then

\displaystyle d_{K}(X,Z)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\bigg|\frac{\varphi_{X}(t)-\varphi_{Z}(t)}{t}\bigg|\,\text{d}t+\frac{24b}{\pi T}

\displaystyle\forall\,T

\displaystyle>0.

Arras et al. 2017
provided two theorems by which (under certain additional assumptions) a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
can also be transferred to a bound for a modified Wasserstein distance (see Theorems 1 and 2 in
[
1
]
).
However, in our paper we will focus on the Kolmogorov distance.

To derive a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
in the first place,
the main idea of the Stein-Tikhomirov method is to characterize the characteristic function of the target random variable
Z

by the so called
Tikhomirov-operator

\mathcal{L}
,
analogously to the Stein-operator, in the following sense:
The random variable
X
has the same distribution as
Z
if and only if
\mathcal{L}\varphi_{X}(t)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In the next step, a bound for
|\mathcal{L}\varphi_{X}|
is constructed.
This bound is finally transferred to a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
.

Tikhomirov 1980
worked with a Gaussian target
Z
and therefore analyzed the following differential equation,
which can easily be expressed by a Tikhomirov-operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{X}(t)=t\cdot\varphi_{X}(t)+\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(t).

This operator indeed characterizes the Gaussian distribution.
The construction of this operator can be motivated by the following considerations, which highlight the parallels to Stein’s method:
Consider the functions
f_{t}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
x\mapsto e^{itx}
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using the Stein-operator (
4
) on these functions directly leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f_{t}(X)]=t\cdot\varphi_{X}(t)+\varphi^{\prime}_{X}(t).

Tikhomirov 1980
analyzed this differential equation and constructed bounded functions
a
and
b
, so that

\displaystyle t\cdot\varphi_{X}(t)+\varphi_{X}^{\prime}(t)=t^{2}\cdot\big(a(t)\cdot\varphi(t)+b(t)\big).

He then proceeded to solve this differential equation to derive a bound for
|\varphi_{X}-\varphi_{Z}|
.
His calculation steps were later generalized by
Röllin 2021
.
The important step can be summed up in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.2

Let
\varphi
be the characteristic function of a random variable.
Suppose, there are functions
a,b:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and real values
A>0
,
B\geq 0
so that

t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot a(t)
and
t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot b(t)
are continuous and

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\big(a(t)\cdot\varphi(t)+b(t)\big)

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\|a\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq A,

\displaystyle\|b\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq B.

Then

\displaystyle\Big|\varphi(t)-\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}\Big|\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{A}{3}\cdot t^{3}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{4}\Big\}+2Bt.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a characteristic function, we know that
\varphi(0)=1
.
Therefore, there is a unique solution to the differential equation

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\big(a(t)\cdot\varphi(t)+b(t)\big)

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

We get
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\varphi(t)={}

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\int_{0}^{t}(-s+s^{2}\cdot a(s))\,\text{d}s\Big\}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{\int_{u}^{t}(-s+s^{2}\cdot a(s))\,\text{d}s\Big\}\cdot u^{2}\cdot b(u)\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\frac{u^{2}}{2}+\int_{u}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}\cdot u^{2}\cdot b(u)\,\text{d}u.

The first summand can be expressed by

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}-\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}+\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+u\cdot\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}\,\text{d}u+\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}.

This leads to

\displaystyle\varphi(t)-\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+u\cdot\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\frac{u^{2}}{2}+\int_{u}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big\}\cdot u^{2}\cdot b(u)\,\text{d}u.

If
|t|\leq\frac{1}{2A}
and
u\in[0,t]
respectively
u\in[t,0]
, then

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{u}^{t}s^{2}\cdot a(s)\,\text{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|u|}^{|t|}\frac{s}{2A}\cdot|a(s)|\,\text{d}s\leq\frac{t^{2}}{4}-\frac{u^{2}}{4},

so that

\displaystyle\Big|\varphi(t)-\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big\}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{A}{3}\cdot t^{3}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+u\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{4}\Big\}\,\text{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}+\frac{u^{2}}{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{4}-\frac{u^{2}}{4}\Big\}\cdot u^{2}\cdot B\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{A}{3}\cdot t^{3}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{4}\Big\}+B\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{4}\Big\}\cdot\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big\{\frac{u^{2}}{4}\Big\}\cdot u^{2}\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{A}{3}\cdot t^{3}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{t^{2}}{4}\Big\}+2Bt.\qed

Note that if the functions
a
and
b
are not continuous, but if at least all the integrals exist,
then the result of
subsection 2.2
is valid at least a. e., which is sufficient for the next step.
Though, in the setting of our paper, it will be easy to show that
a
and
b
are continuous.
Since
t\mapsto\exp\{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is the characteristic function of a Gaussian random variable,
by plugging the results of
subsection 2.2
into the smoothing-lemma (
subsection 2.2
), we finally get:

Theorem 2.3 (see Lemma 2.3 in
[
14
]
)

Let
\varphi
be the characteristic function of a random variable.
Suppose, there are functions
a,b:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and real values
A>0
,
B\geq 0
so that

t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot a(t)
and
t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot b(t)
are continuous and

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\big(a(t)\cdot\varphi(t)+b(t)\big)

\displaystyle\forall t

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\|a\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq A,

\displaystyle\|b\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq B.

Then

\displaystyle d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{24\sqrt{2}}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\Big)\cdot A+\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{B}{A}.

Arras et al. 2017
describe, how to construct Tikhomirov-operators for a larger class of target distributions.
In particular, they describe ways of constructing such an operator without the need of an already existing Stein-operator.
They also show how to solve the resulting differential equations in the case,
that the upper bound for
\mathcal{L}\varphi_{X}
does not include
\varphi_{X}
itself.

2.3  Results

Now, we can apply
subsection 2.2
to the context described at the end of the introduction:

Corollary 2.4

Let
W
be an integrable random variable with characteristic function
\varphi
.
Suppose, there is a stochastic process
(H_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
so that there is
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)+t\cdot\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}\cdot e^{itW}]

and
H_{t}
is integrable.
If
t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]
and
t\mapsto t^{2}\cdot\text{Cov}(H_{t},e^{itW})
are continuous and
if
|\mathbb{E}[H_{t}]|\leq A
and
|\text{Cov}(H_{t},e^{-itW})|\leq B

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

for some real values
A>0
,
B\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle d_{K}(\mathcal{L}(W),\mathcal{N}(0,1))

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{4}{3\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{24\sqrt{2}}{\pi\sqrt{\pi}}\Big)\cdot A+\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{B}{A}.

In our application of
subsection 2.3

\varphi
will depend on a natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore,
A
and
B
will also depend on
n
.
In most cases, they will converge to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
We want the upper bound from
subsection 2.3
to also converge to
0
.
This is the case if
B=o(A)
.
Ideally, there is
B\approx A^{2}
.
It is also possible to use
1
as bound for
\varphi
in
subsection 2.2
.
In this case, however, after some short computations, we finally get an upper bound for the Kolmogorov distance of order
\sqrt{A+B}
,
which is a weaker upper bound than the one in
subsection 2.3
in case of
B\approx A^{2}
.

3  Barbour-Karoński-Ruciński-Decomposition

Here, we introduce random variables posessing decompositions of
Barbour et al. 1989
, which are particularly useful in combinatorial structures, where
there is no natural ordering of the summands.

Definition 3.1

Suppose that
W
is a random variable decomposed in the following way:
Let
J
be a finite index set.
For
j\in J
let
N_{j}
be a subset of
J
.
Further, let

\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
,

\{W_{j}\}_{j\in J}
,

\{Z_{j}\}_{j\in J}
,

\{Z_{jk}\}_{j\in J,k\in N_{j}}
,

\{W_{jk}\}_{j\in J,k\in N_{j}}
,

\{V_{jk}\}_{j\in J,k\in N_{j}}

and

W

be square integrable random variables, so that

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}X_{j},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]

\displaystyle=0\;\forall j\in J,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=W_{j}+Z_{j}\;\forall j\in J,

\displaystyle Z_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in N_{j}}Z_{jk},

\displaystyle W_{j}

\displaystyle=W_{jk}+V_{jk}\;\forall j\in J,k\in N_{j}.

Suppose that for
j\in J,k\in N_{j}

•

W_{j}
is independent of
X_{j}
,

•
