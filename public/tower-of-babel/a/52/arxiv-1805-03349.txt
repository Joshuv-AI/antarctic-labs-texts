Rigidity properties of holomorphic Legendrian singularities
Jun-Muk Hwang

Abstract
We study the singularities of Legendrian subvarieties of contact manifolds in the complex-analytic category and prove two rigidity results. The first one is that Legendrian singularities with reduced tangent cones are contactomorphically biholomorphic to their tangent cones. This result is partly motivated by a problem on Fano contact manifolds. The second result is the deformation-rigidity of normal Legendrian singularities, meaning that any holomorphic family of normal Legendrian singularities is trivial, up to contactomorphic biholomorphisms of germs. Both results are proved by exploiting the relation between infinitesimal contactomorphisms and holomorphic sections of the natural line bundle on the contact manifold.



Keywords.
Legendrian singularities, Lagrangian cone, tangent cone

Résumé.
Nous étudions les singularités des sous-variétés legendriennes des variétés de contact dans la catégorie analytique complexe et montrons deux résultats de rigidité. Le premier affirme que les singularités legendriennes ayant un cône tangent réduit sont contactomorphiquement biholomorphes à ce dernier. Ces deux résultats sont démontrés en exploitant la relation entre les contactomorphismes infinitésimaux et les sections holomorphes d’un fibré en droites naturel sur la variété de contact.

1.
Introduction

Singularities of Legendrian varieties in contact manifolds have been studied in singularity theory or symplectic/contact geometry, often in differentiable or real analytic categories. In this article, we study them in the complex-analytic category. Methods of algebraic geometry can be applied more efficiently to holomorphic Legendrian singularities. Using this approach, we present two rigidity results on Legendrian singularities. Let us start with precise definitions of the terms we use.

Let
M
be a complex manifold of dimension
2m+1
for a positive integer
m
. A subbundle
D\subset T_{M}
of rank
2m
in the holomorphic tangent bundle
T_{M}
is called a
contact structure
on
M
if the Frobenius bracket homomorphism

\wedge^{2}D\to T_{M}/D
is nondegenerate at every point of
M
. A complex manifold
M
equipped with a contact structure
D
is called a
contact manifold
.
A biholomorphic map
\varphi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
between two contact manifolds
(M_{1},D_{1})
and
(M_{2},D_{2})
is
contactomorphic
(equivalently, a
contactomorphism
)
if
{\rm d}\varphi(D_{1})=D_{2}
.

Let
(M,D)
be a contact manifold of dimension
2m+1
. An analytic subvariety
Z\subset M
is
Legendrian
if
\dim Z=m
and
T_{Z,x}\subset D_{x}
for each nonsingular point
x
of
Z
. This implies that
T_{Z,x}\subset D_{x}
is isotropic with respect to the bracket
\wedge^{2}D_{x}\to T_{M,x}/D_{x}
. A nonsingular Legendrian subvariety is called a
Legendrian submanifold
. The germ of a point on a Legendrian subvariety
x\in Z
in a contact manifold
(M,D)
is called a
Legendrian singularity
. Two Legendrian singularities
x_{1}\in Z_{1}\subset(M_{1},D_{1})

and
x_{2}\in Z_{2}\subset(M_{2},D_{2})
, where
Z_{i}
is a Legendrian subvariety in a contact manifold
(M_{i},D_{i}),\,i=1,2,
are
contactomorphic
, if there exist open neighborhoods

U_{i}\subset M_{i}
of
x_{i},\,i=1,2
, and a contactomorphism
\varphi:U_{1}\to U_{2}
such that

\varphi(x_{1})=x_{2}\ \mbox{ and }\varphi(Z_{1}\cap U_{1})=Z_{2}\cap U_{2}.

Are there many interesting examples of Legendrian singularities? The following construction provides lots of them.

For a complex manifold
X
, the projectivized cotangent bundle
M={\mathbb P}T^{*}_{X}
has a natural contact structure (see e.g. Example 1.2 B of
[
AG
]
or Example 2.2 of
[
LB2
]
). For any complex analytic subvariety
Y\subset X
, its conormal variety

Z_{Y}\subset M
, the closure of the projectivized conormal bundle of
the smooth locus of
Y
, is a Legendrian subvariety. The conormal variety
Z_{Y}
is usually (but not always) singular when
Y
is singular. When
Y\subset X
is a hypersurface, the conormal variety
Z_{Y}
is the Nash blowup of
Y
.

In Section
3
, we give another class of examples of Legendrian singularities,
those arising from Lagrangian cones.

Our first rigidity result is in terms of tangent cones.
Recall (see Chapter 3, Section 3 of
[
Mu
]
) that for an analytic subvariety
Z
of a complex manifold
M
and a point
x\in Z
, if
I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{M,x}
is the ideal of the germ of
Z
at
x
, then the tangent cone
TC_{Z,x}
is the subscheme of the Zariski tangent space
T_{Z,x}
defined by the ideal generated by lowest-order terms of the Taylor expansions of elements of
I
at
x
. Roughly speaking, the tangent cone of a singular variety is the lowest order approximation of the singularity. It seldom determines the singularity. Remarkably, a Legendrian singularity is determined by the tangent cone, if the tangent cone is reduced.

A Legendrian singularity is contactomorphic to the germ at the origin of its tangent cone if the tangent cone is reduced. More precisely, a Legendrian singularity is a Lagrangian cone singularity (in the sense of Definition
3
) if and only if its
tangent cone is reduced.

This is proved in Section
4
. Of course, the reducedness of the tangent cone is a strong requirement. There are many examples of Legendrian singularities with non-reduced tangent cones: for instance, cuspidal Legendrian curves discussed in Section 4 of
[
Zh
]
.
One motivation for Theorem
1
comes from the study of Fano contact manifolds.
In his investigation
[
Ke
]
of Fano contact manifolds, Kebekus studied a certain Legendrian singularity
x\in Z
(
Z={\rm locus}(H_{x})
in the notation of
[
Ke
]
). He showed that the projectivized tangent cone
{\mathbb P}TC_{Z,x}
is nonsingular and asserted that the singularity
z\in Z
is biholomorphic to the germ of a Lagrangian cone at
0
. We believe that the latter assertion, if it is true, would have significant consequences in the study of Fano contact manifolds. But its proof given in Section 6.1 of
[
Ke
]
had a gap. Theorem
1
has grown out of our attempt to remedy this gap. But it is not strong enough to fix it, as the smoothness of
{\mathbb P}TC_{Z,x}
does not imply that
TC_{Z,x}
is reduced. A technical difficulty here arises from the fact that
Z
is (a priori) not normal.

In fact, Legendrian singularities are usually not normal and their normalizations cannot be realized as Legendrian singularities. This can be seen from the following result on the deformation-rigidity of normal Legendrian singularities, which is proved in Section
5
.

Let
\Delta
be a neighborhood of the origin
0
in
{\mathbb C}
.
Let
(M,D)
be a contact manifold and consider
\{Z_{t}\subset M,t\in\Delta\}
a holomorphic family of Legendrian subvarieties parametrized by
\Delta
. Assume that
Z_{t}
is normal for
every
t\in\Delta
.
Then for any
x_{0}\in Z_{0}
, there exist a neighborhood
0\in\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta
and a holomorphic arc

\{x_{t}\in Z_{t},t\in\Delta^{\prime}\}

such that the Legendrian singularities
x_{0}\in Z_{0}\subset M
and
x_{t}\in Z_{t}\subset M
are contactomorphic for each
t\in\Delta^{\prime}
.

Theorem
1
suggests that it might be possible to classify normal Legendrian singularities to some extent. In fact, normal Legendrian singularities are not easy to find. Some normal Legendrian singularities are described in Example
3
.

Normal Legendrian singularities are interesting from another viewpoint.
The following theorem says that a Legendrian singularity, unless it is nonsingular, has nonzero torsion differentials.
This is a special case of a stronger result, Theorem 2.5 in
[
Zh
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we give an elementary proof (different from the one in
[
Zh
]
) at the end of Section
2
.

For a Legendrian singularity
x\in Z\subset(M,D),
let
\theta
be a germ of 1-form at
x\in M
defining
D
. Then
x
is a nonsingular point of
Z
if and only if

\theta|_{Z}
is zero in the space of Kähler differentials
{}_{Z,x}.

By Theorem
1
, normal Legendrian singularities provide examples of normal singularities with explicit nonzero torsion differentials.
We mention that some examples of normal singularities with
nonzero torsion differentials
were given in
[
GR
]
by cohomological methods.
One of their examples, the cone over the twisted cubic curve (
d=3
in Proposition 4.1 of
[
GR
]
), is a Legendrian singularity
in Example
3
.

2.
Torsion differentials of Legendrian singularities

Fix a linear coordinate system
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2m+1})
on
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
. Set

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}(x_{m+i}{\rm d}x_{i}-x_{i}{\rm d}x_{m+i})-{\rm d}x_{2m+1}

Then
\theta=0
defines a contact structure on
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
, which we call the
standard contact structure
.

By Darboux theorem (Chapter 4, Section 1.1 of
[
AG
]
), any contact structure is locally equivalent to the standard contact structure. Thus when studying a Legendrian singularity
x\in Z\subset(M,D)
, we may
assume that
M
is a neighborhood of
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
and
D
is the standard contact structure. We remark that in many references (like
[
AG
]
or
[
Kb
]
) the form
\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}{\rm d}x_{m+i}-{\rm d}x_{2m+1}
is used as the standard form. When algebro-geometric tools are used, however, our choice
\theta
is more convenient because it is the expression of a contact structure on
{\mathbb P}^{2m+1}
in affine coordinates.

The following is a standard result in contact geometry. It is essentially given in p. 79 of
[
AG
]
or pp. 30-31 of
[
Kb
]
. As our standard form
\theta
is slightly different from theirs, we recall the proof for readers’ convenience.

In Notation
2
, let
U
be a neighborhood of
0\in{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
. For a holomorphic function
f
on
U
, let
F\subset U
be the hypersurface defined by
f=0
and let
v^{f}
be
the holomorphic vector field on
U
defined by

\displaystyle 2v^{f}

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{m+k}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2m+1}}x_{k})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{k}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2m+1}}x_{m+k})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m+k}}

\displaystyle+\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{k}}x_{k}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{m+k}}x_{m+k})-2f\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2m+1}}.

Then

(i)

\theta(v^{f})=f
;

(ii)

v^{f}(f)=-f\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2m+1}}
;

(iii)

v^{f}
is zero at a point
y\in F
if and only if
F
is singular at
y
or

\theta(T_{F,y})=0
;

(iv)

v^{f}
is tangent to the hypersurface
F
;

(v)

{\rm Lie}_{v^{f}}\theta=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2m+1}}\theta
;

(vi)

for any nonsingular point
y\in F
and any tangent vector
w\in T_{F,y}
satisfying
\theta(w)=0
, we have
{\rm d}\theta(v^{f}(y),w)=0

; and

(vii)

v^{f}
is tangent to
any Legendrian subvariety
Z\subset U
contained in the hypersurface
F.

(i), (ii), (iii) can be checked by straightforward calculation. (iv) is immediate from (ii). (v) can be checked from Cartan formula,

{\rm Lie}_{v^{f}}\theta={\rm d}(\theta(v^{f}))+{\rm d}\theta(v^{f},\cdot)={\rm d}f+{\rm d}\theta(v^{f},\cdot),

and
{\rm d}\theta=2\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}{\rm d}x_{m+k}\wedge{\rm d}x_{k}.

(vi) follows from Cartan formula again:

{\rm d}\theta(v^{f},w)=({\rm Lie}_{v^{f}}\theta)(w)-{\rm d}f(w)=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2m+1}}\theta(w)-{\rm d}f(w).

It remains to prove (vii). Pick a nonsingular point
z\in Z
. The 2-form
{\rm d}\theta
induces a nondegenerate 2-form on the vector space
D_{z}
by the definition of the contact structure. The tangent space
T_{Z,z}
is an isotropic subspace of
D_{z}
with respect to this 2-form
{\rm d}\theta|_{D_{z}}
and it is contained in
T_{F,z}
from
Z\subset F
.
By (vi), the vector
v^{f}(z)\in D_{z}
satisfies
{\rm d}\theta(v^{f}(z),T_{Z,z})=0
.
Thus the linear span
\langle v^{f}(z),T_{Z,z}\rangle
is a subspace of the
2m
-dimensional vector space
D_{z}
and is isotropic with respect to the nondegenerate 2-form
{\rm d}\theta|_{D_{z}}
. Since
\dim T_{Z,z}=m
, we have
v^{f}(z)\in T_{Z,z}
.
\square

To see the geometric meaning of Theorem
2
(vi), it is convenient to recall the notion of Cauchy characteristic of a distribution.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}\subset T_{X}
be a vector subbundle of corank

For each

at

{\rm Ch}({\mathcal{D}})_{x}:=\{v\in{\mathcal{D}}_{x},\sigma_{x}(v,u)=0\mbox{ for all }u\in{\mathcal{D}}_{x}\}.

In particular, the subbundle

The following is standard. (1) is straightforward to check and (2) is a special case of Theorem 2.2 in Chapter 2 of

(2)

For each

By Proposition
2
, the proof of Theorem

is reduced to the next theorem.

Let
0\in Z\subset{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
be the germ of a Legendrian subvariety with respect to the standard contact structure such that

.
Then the differential
\theta|_{Z}\in{}_{Z,0}
is not zero.

We define a weight function wt on
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}
and
{}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}
in the following way. Set

{\rm wt}(x_{i})={\rm wt}({\rm d}x_{i})=1\mbox{ for }1\leq i\leq 2m\mbox{ and }{\rm wt}(x_{2m+1})={\rm wt}({\rm d}x_{2m+1})=2.

Define the weight
{\rm wt}(f)
of a function

as the weight of the monomial of lowest weight in the Taylor series of

at
0
and define the weight of elements of
{}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}
such that
{\rm wt}(f\xi)={\rm wt}(f)+{\rm wt}(\xi)
for any
\xi\in{}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}.

Then

{\rm wt}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})={\rm wt}(\xi_{1})\ \mbox{ if }\ \xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in{}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}\mbox{ and }{\rm wt}(\xi_{1})<{\rm wt}(\xi_{2})

and

{\rm wt}({\rm d}f)={\rm wt}(f)\mbox{ if }f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}\mbox{ and }f(0)=0.

Let

be the ideal of

in
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}

The space of Kähler differentials of

at

is given by (see e.g. Definition 1.109 of

Suppose

, namely,

(\mbox{ the germ at $0$ of }\theta)\ \in\ {\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}{\rm d}I+I\cdot{}_{{\mathbb C}^{2m+1},0}.

The condition

implies that all elements of

, the lowest order term of

To have weight 2,
the lowest order term must be

is homogeneous and not

\square

3.
Lagrangian cones as Legendrian varieties

We use the following terms regarding cones.

Let
V
be a complex vector space, which we regard as an affine space, and let
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{\bullet}V^{*}
be the ring of polynomial functions on
V
.
An
affine cone
in
V
is a subscheme
{\mathcal{Y}}
of the affine space
V
defined by a homogeneous ideal
I\subset\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{\bullet}V^{*}
. The corresponding projective subscheme
{\mathbb P}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb P}V
will be called the
projectivization
of
{\mathcal{Y}}
. If the subscheme
{\mathcal{Y}}
is reduced, i.e., the ideal
I
is radical (
I=\sqrt{I}
), we will call it a
reduced affine cone
. If
{\mathcal{Y}}
is reduced, then so is its projectivization
{\mathbb P}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb P}V
.
But the converse is not always true.

Let
(V,\omega)
be a symplectic vector space, i.e., a vector space
V
equipped with a nondegenerate anti-symmetric 2-form
\omega\in\wedge^{2}V^{*}
. Let
2m
be the dimension of
V
. An
m
-dimensional reduced affine cone
0\in{\mathcal{Y}}\subset V
is called a
Lagrangian cone

if the restriction of
\omega
to the nonsingular locus of
{\mathcal{Y}}
is zero.

In Notation
2
, let
{\mathbb C}^{2m}
be the hyperplane defined by

(x_{2m+1}=0)
and equipped with the symplectic form

{\rm d}\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}}=2\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}{\rm d}x_{m+i}\wedge{\rm d}x_{i}.

Let
Z\subset{\mathbb C}^{2m}
be an
m
-dimensional subvariety.
When regarded as a subvariety of
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
equipped with the standard contact structure, the variety
Z
is a Legendrian subvariety if and only if
Z
is a Lagrangian cone in
({\mathbb C}^{2m},{\rm d}\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}})
in the sense of Definition
3
.

We use the radial vector field on
{\mathbb C}^{2m}

\vec{R}:=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+x_{m+i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m+i}}).

In terms of Theorem
2
, the radial vector field
\vec{R}
is the restriction of

-2v^{x_{2m+1}}
to
{\mathbb C}^{2m}
.

Assume that
Z\subset{\mathbb C}^{2m}
is a Lagrangian cone. As it is an affine cone, the radial vector field
\vec{R}
is tangent to the smooth locus of
Z
.
It is straightforward to check that the contraction
\vec{R}\lrcorner{\rm d}\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}}
is a constant multiple of the 1-form
\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}}
. It follows that the restriction of
\theta
to the smooth locus of
Z
is zero. Thus
Z
is Legendrian in
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
with respect to
\theta
.

Conversely, if a subvariety
Z\subset{\mathbb C}^{2m}
is a Legendrian subvariety of
{\mathbb C}^{2m+1}
with respect to
\theta
, then the radial vector field
\vec{R}=-2v^{x_{2m+1}}|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}}
is tangent to
Z
by Theorem

2
(vii). Thus
Z
is a reduced affine cone. Since
{\rm d}\theta
vanishes on the smooth locus of
Z
,
it is a Lagrangian cone with respect to
{\rm d}\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}}
.
\square

We say that a Legendrian singularity
x\in Z\subset(M,D)
is
a
Lagrangian cone singularity
, if it is contactomorphic to the germ at
0
of a Lagrangian cone in
({\mathbb C}^{2m},{\rm d}\theta|_{{\mathbb C}^{2m}})
regarded as a Legendrian subvariety of
({\mathbb C}^{2m+1},\theta)
as in Lemma
3
.

We skip the proof of the following elementary lemma.

In the setting of Proposition
2
, the Legendrian singularity
0\in Z
in
({\mathbb C}^{2m+1},D)
is a Lagrangian cone singularity if and only if the Legendrian singularity
\psi(0)\in\psi(Z)
in

give rise to Legendrian subvarieties of a contact manifold. The symplectic form

with the following contact structure (Chapter 4, Section 1.2, Example A in

]

[

]

For a symplectic vector space

D^{\omega}_{[v]}:=\{h\in\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits({\mathbb C}v,V/({\mathbb C}v))=T_{{\mathbb P}V,[v]},\omega(v,h(v))=0\}.

Then the subbundle
D^{\omega}\subset T_{{\mathbb P}V}
is a contact structure on
{\mathbb P}V
.

The following is well-known (see e.g. Proposition E.2 in
[
Bu2
]
).

In Definition
3
, for a reduced affine cone
{\mathcal{Y}}\subset V,
its projectivization
{\mathbb P}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb P}V
is a Legendrian subvariety with respect to
D^{\omega}
if and only if
{\mathcal{Y}}
is Lagrangian with respect to
\omega
.

Legendrian subvarieties of
({\mathbb P}V,D^{\omega})
are studied in
[
Bu
]
,
[
Bu2
]
and
[
LM
]
.

Subadjoint varieties (see Theorem 11 in
[
LM
]
) are Legendrian subvarieties of
({{\mathbb P}V},D^{\omega})
that are homogeneous under the action of the symplectic automorphisms of
(V,\omega)
. There exists one subadjoint variety corresponding to each complex simple Lie algebra, as listed in Table 1 of

[
Bu
]
. For example, the twisted cubic curve in
{\mathbb P}^{3}
is the subadjoint variety corresponding to the simple Lie algebra of type
G_{2}
.
As subadjoint varieties are projectively normal, their affine cones become
normal Legendrian subvarieties.

Landsberg-Manivel
[
LM
]
and Buczynski (Chapters G, H, I of
[
Bu2
]
) have discovered many examples of nonsingular Legendrian subvarieties in

{\mathbb P}V
, different from the subadjoint varieties of Example
3
. They are not projectively normal, so the Legendrian singularities of their affine cones are not normal.

For later use, we recall the following proposition, which is just a reformulation of Corollary 5.5 and Lemma 5.6 of
[
Bu
]
.

Let
(V,\omega)
be a symplectic vector space of dimension
2m
.
Fix a symplectic coordinate system
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{2m})
on
V
satisfying

\omega(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{m+i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}})=\delta_{ij},\ 1\leq i,j\leq m,

and define a homomorphism

\omega^{\dagger}:H^{0}({\mathbb P}V,{\mathcal{O}}(2))\longrightarrow H^{0}({\mathbb P}V,T_{{\mathbb P}V})

by sending a homogeneous quadratic polynomial
q(y_{1},\ldots,y_{2m})

to the vector field on
{\mathbb P}V
given by the linear vector field

\omega^{\dagger}(q):=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m}(\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{m+k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}-\frac{\partial q}{\partial y_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{m+k}}).

Let
{\mathbb P}{\mathcal{Y}}
be a Legendrian subvariety in
({\mathbb P}V,D^{\omega})
as in Proposition
3
.
Then the vector field
\omega^{\dagger}(q)
is tangent to
{\mathbb P}{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb P}V
if and only if
{\mathcal{Y}}
is contained in the quadric hypersurface
q=0
.

The symplectic form
\omega
gives an isomorphism

\omega^{\sharp}:{\rm Sym}^{2}V^{*}\cong{\mathfrak s}{\mathfrak p}(V)\subset{\mathfrak s}{\mathfrak l}(V)

such that for a symmetric bilinear form
Q\in{\rm Sym}^{2}V^{*}
, the endomorphism
A:=\omega^{\sharp}(Q)\in{\mathfrak s}{\mathfrak l}(V)
of
V
sends
v\in V
to
A(v)\in V
satisfying
Q(v,u)=\omega(A(v),u)
for all
u\in V
. Corollary 5.5 and Lemma 5.6 of
[
Bu
]
say that
\omega^{\sharp}
identifies quadrics vanishing on
{\mathcal{Y}}
with elements of
{\mathfrak s}{\mathfrak p}(V)
which are tangent to
{\mathcal{Y}}
. Our homomorphism
\omega^{\dagger}
is, up to a scalar multiple, just an expression of
\omega^{\sharp}
in terms of linear vector fields on
V
, thus the proposition follows.

\square

Let

5.

In this section, we prove Theorem

.
Throughout, we fix a linear coordinate

We begin with recalling a few standard facts on time-dependent vector fields. As some algebraic geometers may not be familiar with them, we will provide most of the proofs.

Let

The following lemma on time-independent vector fields is a straightforward holomorphic translation of the standard result on differentiable manifolds (see e.g. Theorem 8.1 of
[

Let

be a holomorphic vector field.
Then for each

\pi^{\Delta^{x}}\circ\Phi^{\vec{A}}=\pi^{\Delta^{x}}|_{O^{x}\times\Delta^{x}},\ \Phi^{\vec{A}}|_{O^{x}\times\{0\}}={\rm Id}_{O^{x}\times\{0\}}\mbox{ and }{\rm d}\Phi^{\vec{A}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau})=\,\overrightarrow{A\>}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau},

where

by the projection

Let

[
