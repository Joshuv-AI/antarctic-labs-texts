The BEM and DRBEM schemes for the numerical solution of the two-dimensional time-fractional diffusion-wave equations
Peyman Alipour

Abstract
In this paper we apply the boundary elements method (BEM) and the dual reciprocity boundary elements method (DRBEM) for the numerical solution of two–dimensional time–fractional partial differential equations (TFPDEs). The fractional derivative of problem is described in the Caputo sense. In BEM, the main equation deduces to solving the Helmholtz equation in each time step. Therefore, we should compute the domain integral in each time step. So, we presented an approach to compute the domain integral with no singularity. On the other hand the DRBEM has the flexibility of discretizing only the boundary of the computational domain and evaluates the solution at any required interior point. We employ the radial basis functions (RBFs) for interpolation of the inhomogeneous and time derivative terms. The proposed method is employed for solving some problems in two–dimensions on unit square and some other complex regions to demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method. Keywords : Time–fractional partial differential equations (TFPDEs); Boundary elements method (BEM); Dual reciprocity boundary elements method (DRBEM); Radial basis functions (RBFs). AMS subject classification: 65N38, 35R11

The boundary elements method (BEM)

The basic idea of this method is the transformation of the original differential equation describing the behaviour of the unknown both inside and on the boundary of the domain into an equivalent integral equation only on the boundary. BEM requires only the boundary discretization which reduces the dimension of the problem under consideration by one. Thus, a smaller system of equations is obtained in comparison with the numerical methods requiring domain discretization.
The method has become a widely used technique with the applications around in many areas of engineering such as fluid mechanics, magnetohydrodynamic, electrodynamics and so on, for instance see

of the boundary integral equation also the nonhomogeneous and nonlinear terms are incorporated in the formulation by means of domain integrals.
The dual reciprocity boundary elements method (DRBEM) has the flexibility of discretizing only the boundary of the computational domain and evaluates the solution at any required interior point.
DRBEM does not need the mesh generation of the computational domain and does not require the fundamental solution of the main equation (only the fundamental solution of Laplace equation is needed). Some engineering problems are solved by the DRBEM, for instance, see

2
Time discrete scheme

At first, we define the functional space endowed with standard norms and inner products.
Let
\Omega
denote a bounded and open domain in
\mathbb{R^{d}}
, for
d=2
or
3
. For
p<+\infty
, we denote by
L^{p}(\Omega)
the space of measurable functions
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}dx\leq\infty
. It is a Banach space for the norm
[
6
]

\begin{array}[]{l}\parallel u\parallel_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.\end{array}

The space
L^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space with the inner product

\begin{array}[]{l}(u,v)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)dx,\end{array}

which induces the norm

\begin{array}[]{l}\parallel u\parallel_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{array}

And

\begin{array}[]{l}H^{1}(\Omega)=\displaystyle\{\upsilon\in L^{2}(\Omega),\displaystyle\frac{d\upsilon}{dx}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\displaystyle\},\\
\\
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)=\displaystyle\{\upsilon\in H^{1}(\Omega),\upsilon|_{\Gamma}=0\displaystyle\}.\end{array}

For discretization of the time variable, we need some preliminary. We define

\begin{array}[]{l}t_{k}:=k\tau,\ \ \ \ k=0,1,2,...,N_{t},\end{array}

where
\tau=\displaystyle\frac{T}{N_{t}}
and

\begin{array}[]{l}\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}(u^{n}-u^{n-1}),\ \ \ \ v(X,t)=\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(X,t)}{\partial t}.\end{array}

(2.1)

The difference scheme that we will consider for solving problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) is as follows
[
52
]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-k-1}-a_{n-k})\xi_{t}u^{k-\frac{1}{2}}-a_{n-1}\psi\right]=\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+g^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\ \ n\geq 1,\end{array}

(2.2)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}a_{k}:=\displaystyle\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\frac{dt}{t^{\alpha-1}}=\frac{\tau^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}[(k+1)^{2-\alpha}-k^{2-\alpha}],\ \ k\geq 0,\end{array}

(2.3)

and

\begin{array}[]{l}g^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=g(X,\frac{t_{n}+t_{n-1}}{2}),\ \ \ \ n\geq 1.\end{array}

We discrete the time variable using the following lemmas.

Lemma 1
.
[
52
]
Suppose
g(t)\in C^{2}[0,t_{n}]
and
1<\alpha<2
then we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\left|\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\frac{g^{{}^{\prime}}(t)}{(t_{n}-t)^{\alpha-1}}dt-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left[a_{0}g(t_{n})-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-k-1}-a_{n-k})g(t_{k})-a_{n-1}g(t_{0})\right]\right|\\
\leq\displaystyle\frac{1}{2-\alpha}\left[\displaystyle\frac{2-\alpha}{12}+\displaystyle\frac{2^{3-\alpha}}{3-\alpha}-(1+2^{1-\alpha})\right]\mathop{\max|{g^{\prime\prime}(t)}|{\tau^{3-\alpha}}}\limits_{0\leq t\leq{t_{n}}}.\end{array}

We introduce the following notation:

w(X,t):=\frac{1}{{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial v(X,s)}{\partial s}\frac{ds}{(t-s)^{\alpha-1}},

(2.4)

and

\mathcal{P}\left(u^{n-\frac{1}{2}},q\right):=a_{0}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-k-1}-a_{n-k})u^{k-\frac{1}{2}}-a_{n-1}q.

So from Lemma 1 we have

w^{n}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial v(X,s)}{\partial s}\displaystyle\frac{dt}{(t_{n}-s)^{\alpha-1}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(v^{n},\psi)+O(\tau^{3-\alpha}),

such that
w^{n}=w(X,t_{n})
.
Using Taylor expansion, it follows from (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) that

v^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}}+(r_{1})^{n-\frac{1}{2}},

(2.5)

w^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(w^{n}+w^{n-1}\right)=\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+g^{n-\frac{1}{2}}+(r_{2})^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\ \ \ \ \ \ X\in\Omega,\ \ \ \ n\geq 1,

(2.6)

where
u^{n}=u(X,t_{n})
and there exists a constant
c_{1}
such that

\left|(r_{1})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\right|\leq c_{1}\tau^{2},\ \ \ \ \left|(r_{2})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\right|\leq c_{1}(\tau).

(2.7)

Using Lemma 1 and
v^{0}=v(X,0)=\psi(X)=\psi
, the above relation can be written as

w^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(v^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)+O(\tau^{3-\alpha}).

Consequently

w^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(v^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)+(r_{3})^{n-\frac{1}{2}},

(2.8)

where there exists a constant
c_{2}
such that
[
52
]

\left|(r_{3})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\right|\leq c_{2}\tau^{3-\alpha}.

(2.9)

If we substitute (
2.8
) into (
2.5
), we obtain

w^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)+\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}\left((r_{1})^{n-\frac{1}{2}},0\right)+(r_{3})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}.

Then substituting the above result into (
1.1
), yields

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)=\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+g^{n-\frac{1}{2}}+R^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\ \ \ \ n\geq 1,\end{array}

(2.10)

where

R^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}\left((r_{1})^{n-\frac{1}{2}},0\right)+(r_{3})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}+(r_{2})^{n-\frac{1}{2}}.

Now by omitting the small term
R^{n-\frac{1}{2}}
, we can construct the following difference scheme for problem (
2.10
),

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)=\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+g^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\ \ \ \ \ X\in\Omega,\ \ \ \ \ \ n\geq 1,\\
u^{n}=\varphi(X,t_{n}),\ \ \ \ X\in\Gamma,\end{array}\right.

(2.11)

where

is the approximation solution of (

So, by time discretization, solving Eq. (
1.1
) leads to solve Eq. (
2.11

, as presented in Eqs. (
2.1
), the following expansion will be obtained

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\mathcal{P}(\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\psi)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}\xi_{t}u^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-l-1}-a_{n-l})\xi_{t}u^{l-\frac{1}{2}}-a_{n-1}\psi\right]\\
=\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u^{n}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}u^{n-1}+\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-l-1}-a_{n-l})(u^{l}-u^{l-1})+a_{n-1}\psi\right].\end{array}

(2.12)

Substituting Eq. (
2.12
) in Eq. (
2.11
) gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u^{n}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}u^{n-1}+\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-l-1}-a_{n-l})(u^{l}-u^{l-1})+a_{n-1}\psi\right]=\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+g^{n-\frac{1}{2}},\end{array}

by rearranging the above relation we obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\nabla^{2}u^{n}+bu^{n}=F^{n-1},\end{array}

(2.13)

where

b=-\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)},

(2.14)

and

F^{n-1}=\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}u^{n-1}+\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(a_{n-l-1}+a_{n-l})(u^{l}-u^{l-1})+a_{n-1}\psi\right]-g^{n-\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.15)

In other words, solving Eq. (
1.1
) in each time step deduces to solving the above Helmholtz equation. Note that in Eq. (
2.13
)

is constant and

varies with each time step. In the forthcoming subsection, we employ the constant BEM for solving the Helmholtz equation (

3
Spatial discretization via BEM

In this section we present an overview on how to solve the Helmholtz equation with the BEM. Consider the equation

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla^{2}u-k^{2}u=\omega,\ \ \ \ \ \ X\in\Omega,\\
u=\bar{u},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ X\in\Gamma,\end{array}

(3.1)

where
\Omega
is a bounded computational domain,
\omega
is a real function and
k
is a constant. Suppose that
u
is an unknown function which satisfies Eqs. (
3.1
). The use of Green’s second identity gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{2}uG^{i}-\nabla^{2}G^{i}u\right)d\Omega=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}G^{i}-\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}u\right)d\Gamma,\end{array}

(3.2)

where
G^{i}=G^{i}(x,y)
is the fundamental solution of Helmholtz equation based on the source point
P_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
which satisfies

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla^{2}G^{i}-k^{2}G^{i}=\delta(x-P_{i}),\ \ \ \ \ \ X\in\Omega,\end{array}

(3.3)

where
\delta(.)
is the Dirac delta function.
The fundamental solution for two–dimensional Helmholtz equation based on the source point
P_{i}
, is reported as
[
26
]
:

G^{i}(x,y)=\frac{-1}{2\pi}K_{0}(kr),

where
r
is the distance between the field point
X
and the source point
P_{i}
and
K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function.
Substituting Eqs. (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) in Eq. (
3.2
) we obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}G^{i}-\displaystyle\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}u\right)d\Gamma=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{2}uG^{i}-\nabla^{2}G^{i}u\right)d\Omega\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left((\omega+k^{2}u)G^{i}-(\delta(x-P_{i})+k^{2}G^{i})u\right)d\Omega\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega G^{i}d\Omega-\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}u(P_{i}),\end{array}

(3.4)

where
\beta
is the internal angle regarding to the source point
P_{i}
for more details see
[
22
,
41
]
.
To numerical implementation of Eqs. (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) with BEM, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}u(P_{i})+\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}G^{i}d\Gamma-\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\displaystyle\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}ud\Gamma=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega G^{i}d\Omega,\ \ \ \ p_{i}\in\Gamma\cup\Omega.

(3.5)

For approximating the boundary
\Gamma
, we discretize it in to
N
straight boundary elements
\displaystyle\{\Gamma_{i}\displaystyle\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and we will choose the boundary source point
P_{i}
at the center of
\Gamma_{i}
for
i=1,2,...,N
. In constant BEM, the boundary values
u
and
q=\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}
have been approximated constantly over the boundary elements
\displaystyle\{\Gamma_{i}\displaystyle\}_{i=1}^{N}
equal to
\displaystyle\{u_{i}\displaystyle\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\displaystyle\{q_{i}\displaystyle\}_{i=1}^{N}
, respectively, where
u_{i}=u(P_{i})
and
q_{i}=q(P_{i})

[
23
]
. So after choosing
L
interior source points
\displaystyle\{P_{i+N}\displaystyle\}_{i=1}^{L}
appropriately in
\Omega
, Eq. (
3.1
) can be approximated as

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}u(P_{i})+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{i}}G^{i}d\Gamma_{i}-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{i}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}d\Gamma_{i}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega G^{i}d\Omega,\ \ \ \ i=1,2,...,N.\end{array}

(3.6)

Let

\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{H}_{ik}=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{k}}\displaystyle\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}ds\ \ \ \ ,\ \ \ \ G_{ik}=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{k}}G^{i}ds,\ \ \ \ i=1,2,...,N.\end{array}

(3.7)

Since we have put the source points (coincide with the mid points of the boundary elements) in Eq. (
3.6
) as source points, we get the following matrix equation

\begin{array}[]{l}\bf{H}\bf{u^{T}}+\bf{F}=\bf{G}\bf{q^{T}},\end{array}

(3.8)

where
\bf{H}_{ij}=\bar{H}_{ij}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}
(
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function which is defined as
\delta_{ij}=0
for

and

\delta_{ij}=1
for

) and
\bf{F}
=[F^{1},F^{2},...,F^{N}]^{T}
such that

has the representation

\begin{array}[]{l}F^{i}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega G^{i}d\Omega,\ \ \ \ i=1,2,...,N.\end{array}

(3.9)

In additional
\bf{u}
and
\bf{q}
are vector of dimension
N\times 1
.
Now by separating the unknown quantities from the known one, we can obtain the following linear system of equations

\begin{array}[]{l}\bf{AX^{T}}=b^{T}.\end{array}

(3.10)

Now we can determine the values of
\bf{u}
and
\bf{q}
on the boundary. Therefore after knowing all the boundary quantities on
\Gamma
, the solution
\bf{u}
can be computed at each internal points
\{P_{i+N}\}_{i=1}^{L}\in\Omega
by applying Eq. (
3.6
) for
\beta=2\pi
.
Hence the solution of Eqs. (
3.1
) at any interior point

can be obtain as follow:

\begin{array}[]{l}u(P_{i})=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{L}u_{i}G_{i}-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{L}q_{i}H_{i}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega G^{i}d\Omega,\ \ \ \ i=1,2,...,L,\end{array}

(3.11)

where
\bf{H}
and
\bf{G}
can be computed just the same as Eq (
3.7
) based on the source points
\{P_{i+N}\}_{i=1}^{L}
.

3.1
Computation of domain integral

Suppose
w(X,P)
is a complex smooth function such that
\nabla^{2}w+bw=1
. For example it may be defined as

w(x,P)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}(1-\cos(\sqrt{b}(x_{1}-P_{1}))\ \ ,\ \ \ \ \ \ if\ \ b\neq 0\ \ ,\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}-P_{1})^{2}\ \ ,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ o.w\ \ ,\end{array}\right.

when index
1
denotes the first component of vectors. In this situation we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\omega(X)Gd\Omega=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\omega(X)-\omega(P))Gd\Omega+\omega(P)\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}Gd\Omega\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\acute{I}_{\Omega}+\omega(P)\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla^{2}w+bw)Gd\Omega\\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\acute{I}_{\Omega}+\omega(P)\left(\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}w(P,P)+\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial w}{\partial n}G-\displaystyle\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}wd\Gamma\right),\end{array}

(3.12)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\acute{I}_{\Omega}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{\omega}(X,P)d\Omega\ \ \ \ ,\ \ \ \ \mathbb{\omega}(X,P)=(\omega(X)-\omega(P))G(X,P).\end{array}

(3.13)

For the two–dimensional problems
\lim_{X\rightarrow P}\mathbb{\omega}(X,P)=0
, so if we define
\mathbb{\omega}(P,P)=0
, the function
\mathbb{\omega}
will be continuous at
X=P
. So
\acute{I}_{\Omega}
can be calculated with no singularity.
We can discretize domain
\Omega
to smaller sub-domains
\Omega_{j}
and approximate
\mathbb{\omega}(X,P)
over them constantly as
\mathbb{\omega}(P_{j},P)
when
P_{j}
is a collocation point located on middle of
\Omega_{j}
. So we can write

\begin{array}[]{l}\acute{I}_{\Omega}\simeq\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbb{\omega}(P_{j},P)S_{j}\ \ \ \ \ ,\ \ \ \ S_{j}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j}}d\Omega_{j}.\end{array}

(3.14)

It can be found from Eq. (
3.14
) that
S_{j}
is volume of
\Omega_{j}
, but we can interpret it as the weight of collocation point
P_{j}
in the domain integral approximation (
3.14
). For more details, we refer the interested readers to
[
19
]
.

4
Implementation of the DRBEM

In this section we apply DRBEM for the solution of Eqs (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).
We choose
N
and
L
source points into domain and on its boundary, respectively. In this step we make an interpolation for approximating the time derivatives and the inhomogeneous terms in the following:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}u(X,t)}{\partial t^{\alpha}}-g(X,t)=\nu(X,t)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N+L}\eta_{i}(t)\mu_{i}(X),\end{array}

(4.1)

where
\mu_{i}=1+r_{i}
is a linear RBF and
r_{i}=\displaystyle\sqrt{(x-x_{i})^{2}+(y-y_{i})^{2}}
for
i=1,...,N+L
.
From (
1.1
) and (
4.1
), we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla^{2}u=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N+L}\eta_{i}(t)\mu_{i}(x,y).\end{array}

(4.2)

Note that we represent the value of the function
\mu_{i}
at source point
p_{i}
by
\mu_{ji}
for
i=1,2,...,N+L
, and set
\Phi
a
(N+L)\times(N+L)
matrix with component
\Phi(j,i)=\mu_{ji}
. From Eq. (
4.1
) we can write

\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{i}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N+L}\Phi_{im}^{-1}\nu_{m}(t),\end{array}

(4.3)

where
\nu_{m}(t)=\nu(x_{m},y_{m},t)
.
The essential feather in DRBEM is to express
\mu_{i}
, which is a function of
r_{i}
, as a Laplacian of another function
f_{i}
. Thus
f_{i}
is chosen as the solution to:

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla^{2}f_{i}=\mu_{i}.\end{array}

(4.4)

The particular solution
f_{i}
, for linear RBFs is easily determined as
[
12
]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}f_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{4}+\displaystyle\frac{r_{i}^{3}}{9}.\end{array}

(4.5)

In this section, we use a special weight function, that is the fundamental solution for the two–dimensional Laplacian operator, defined by the equation:

\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla^{2}G^{i}=\delta(x-x_{i},y-y_{i}),\end{array}

\delta
is the Dirac delta function. The fundamental solution for two–dimensional Laplacian operator based on the source point
P_{i}

, is reported as:

\begin{array}[]{l}G^{i}(X)=\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2\pi}\ln(r),\end{array}

where
r
is the distance between the field point
(x,y)
and the source point
P_{i}
.
Substituting (
4.4
) into (
4.2
), writing the right residual formulation of Eq. (
4.2
) and using the second Green’s theorem, lead to:

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\displaystyle{\beta}{2\pi}u_{i}+\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\left[\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}u-G^{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right]d\Gamma\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+L}\eta_{j}(t)\left\{\displaystyle{\beta}{2\pi}f_{ij}+\int_{\Gamma}\left[\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}f_{j}-G^{i}\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial n}\right]d\Gamma\right\},\end{array}

(4.6)

for
i=1,2,...,N+L
, where
f_{ij}=f_{i}(x_{j})
.
The term
\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial n}
is the normal derivative of
f_{i}
and can be expressed as
[
12
]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial n}=\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\displaystyle\frac{r_{i}}{3}\right)\left[(x-x_{i})n_{x}+(y-y_{i})n_{y}\right],\end{array}

(4.7)

where
n=(n_{x},n_{y})
is the unit outward normal to the boundary
\Gamma
. Next we show the discretized form of Eq. (
4.6
), with summations over the constant boundary elements, for the
i
th source point as follow

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left\{\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}u_{i}+\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{q}}\left[\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}u-G^{i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right]d\Gamma\right\}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+L}\eta_{j}(t)\left(\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}f_{ij}+\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}\int_{\Gamma_{q}}\left[\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial n}f_{j}-G^{i}\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial n}\right]d\Gamma\right).\end{array}

(4.8)

The expression given by (
4.8
) can be expressed in terms of the coefficients
\bar{H}_{iq}
and
G_{iq}
, using the
constant element approximation
[
22
]
. The resulting equation is

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}u_{i}+\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}\bar{H}_{iq}u_{q}-\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}G_{iq}\frac{\partial u_{q}}{\partial n}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+L}\eta_{j}(t)\left\{\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2\pi}f_{ij}+\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}\bar{H}_{iq}f_{qj}-\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{N}G_{iq}\frac{\partial f_{qj}}{\partial n}\right\},\end{array}

(4.9)

for
i=1,2,...,N
(we defined
\bar{H}
and
G
in Eq. (
3.7
)).
For simplicity we put,
\mathbb{q}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}
,
\hat{\mathbb{u}}=f
and
\hat{\mathbb{q}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}
.
Eq. (
4.9
) can be expressed in the matrix form as

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\mathbb{H}\mathbb{u}-\mathbb{G}\mathbb{q}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+L}\eta_{j}(t)\left\{\mathbb{H}\hat{\mathbb{u}}_{j}-\mathbb{G}\hat{\mathbb{q}}_{j}\right\}.\end{array}

(4.10)

If each of the vectors
\hat{\mathbb{u}}_{k}
and
\hat{\mathbb{q}}_{k}
are considered to be one column of the matrices
\hat{\mathbb{U}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
, respectively, then for the solution of the boundary values we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\mathbb{H}^{bs}\mathbb{u}-\mathbb{G}^{bs}\mathbb{q}\right)=\left(\mathbb{H}^{bs}\hat{\mathbb{U}}^{bs}-\mathbb{G}^{bs}\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{bs}\right)\mathbb{\eta}.\end{array}

(4.11)

Eq. (
4.11
) is the basis of the application of the DRBEM and involves discretization of the boundary only. One can calculate the values of
\mathbb{u}
at the internal nodes, by redefining the matrices
\mathbb{\bar{H}}
and
\mathbb{G}
in Eq. (
4.9
) in such a way that they contain the point
p_{i}
as an interior node. So for the internal nodes we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\mathbb{H}^{is}\mathbb{u}-\mathbb{I}\mathbb{u}-\mathbb{G}^{is}\mathbb{q}\right)=\left(\mathbb{H}^{is}\hat{\mathbb{U}}^{is}-\mathbb{I}\hat{\mathbb{U}}^{is}-\mathbb{G}^{is}\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{is}\right)\mathbb{\eta},\end{array}

(4.12)

where
\eta=[\eta_{1},\eta_{2},...,\eta_{N+L}]^{T}
and
bs
and
is
refer to boundary and internal solutions, respectively.
Eq. (
4.3
) yields

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{\eta}=\mathbb{\Phi}^{-1}\mathbb{\nu}.\end{array}

(4.13)

From Eqs. (
4.1
), (
4.13
) we obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\eta=\mathbb{\Phi}^{-1}(\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}\mathbb{u}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}-\mathbb{g}).\end{array}

(4.14)

Combining Eqs. (
4.11
), (
4.12
) and (
4.14
) together at one step, one can get the following system of equations
[
7
]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\kappa\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{H}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{H}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{-I}}\end{array}}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{u}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{u}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]-\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{G}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{G}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\end{array}}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right]\right)=\mathbb{D}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{\alpha}\mathbb{u}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}-\mathbb{g}\right),\end{array}

(4.15)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{D}=\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{H}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{H}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{-I}}\end{array}}\right]\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\hat{U}^{bs}}\\
{\hat{U}^{is}}\end{array}}\right\}-\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{G}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{G}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\end{array}}\right]\left\{{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\hat{Q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right\}\right)\mathbb{\Phi}^{-1}.\end{array}

Now Eq. (
4.15
) constitutes a system of
N+L
equations in
N+L
unknown functions of
t
.
We use the Caputo sense definition (Eqs. (
2.2
)–(
2.3
))
and make the following approximation:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{g}\simeq\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{g}^{k}+\mathbb{g}^{k-1}).\end{array}

(4.16)

For
u
we employ the subsequent approximation:

\begin{array}[]{l}u(X,t)\simeq\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{u}^{k}+\mathbb{u}^{k-1}).\end{array}

(4.17)

For simplicity, let

\mathbb{\tilde{H}}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{H}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{H}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{-I}}\end{array}}\right],\ \ \ \ \mathbb{\tilde{G}}=\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{G}^{bs}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\\
{\mathbb{G}^{is}}\ \ \ \ {\mathbb{0}}\end{array}}\right],\ \ \ \ \vartheta=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(2-\alpha)},

where
\mathbb{I}
is
(N+L)\times(N+L)
identity matrix.
Eqs. (
4.15
)–(
4.17
) yield :

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\{\mathbb{\tilde{H}}\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{u}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{u}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k}+\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{u}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{u}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}\right)-\mathbb{\tilde{G}}\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right]^{k}+\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}\right)\right\}\\
=\mathbb{D}\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\left[a_{0}\xi_{t}\mathbb{u}^{k-\frac{1}{2}}-\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(a_{n-k-1}-a_{n-k})\xi_{t}\mathbb{u}^{l-\frac{1}{2}}-a_{k-1}\psi\right]\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{g}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{g}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k}+\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{g}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{g}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}\right).\end{array}

(4.18)

Rearranging Eq. (
4.18
), the following form can be obtained

\begin{array}[]{l}\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{\tilde{H}}-a_{0}\vartheta\mathbb{D}\right\}\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{u}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{u}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{\tilde{G}}\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right]^{k}=-\left\{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{\tilde{H}}-a_{0}\vartheta\mathbb{D}\right\}\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{u}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{u}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{\tilde{G}}\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{q}^{bs}}\\
{0}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}\\
-\mathbb{D}\left(\vartheta\left[\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(a_{k-j-1}-a_{k-j})\mathbb{u}^{l-\frac{1}{2}}+a_{k-1}\psi\right]\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\left(\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{g}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{g}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k}+\left[{\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}{\mathbb{g}^{bs}}\\
{\mathbb{g}^{is}}\end{array}}\right]^{k-1}\right).\end{array}

(4.19)

It is necessary to separate the unknown from the known quantities. After moving all the known quantities to the left hand side, we obtain the following linear system of equations

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{x}=\mathbb{c},\end{array}

(4.20)

where the vector
\mathbb{x}
contains
N
unknown boundary values
\mathbb{q}
and
L
unknown internal values of
\mathbb{u}
.

6
Conclusion

This paper has proposed a boundary elements method (BEM) and dual reciprocity boundary elements method (DRBEM) for the numerical solution of two–dimensional time-fractional diffusion–wave. We have shown that the approximation of solution in each time step has been reduced to solving an inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation in BEM. The inhomogeneous part of the mentioned Helmholtz equation in each time steps, depends on all previous time steps. This inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation is approximated with the constant BEM. The main concerning problem of the BEM is the domain integrals that appear in the integral representation of this Helmholtz equation. So, in each time step we need to calculate a domain integral. Since these domain integrals are singular, the singularity of these domain integrals can be omitted via the simple technique. The mentioned technique has been also presented in this paper. These domain integrals have been approximated by Monte–Carlo approach. Also the time–fractional derivative of problems is described in the Caputo sense.
The DRBEM is applied to eliminate the domain integrals which appear in the boundary integral equation. The linear radial basis function (RBF) was used for interpolation of the nonlinear, inhomogeneous and time derivative terms. Some numerical tests taken from the literature are considered on regular and complex regions which confirm the validity of the presented techniques.
