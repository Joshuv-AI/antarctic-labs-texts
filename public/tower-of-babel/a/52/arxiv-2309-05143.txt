Riemannian Acceleration with Preconditioning for symmetric eigenvalue problems
Nian Shao, Wenbin Chen

Abstract
The analysis of the acceleration behavior of gradient-based eigensolvers with preconditioning presents a substantial theoretical challenge. In this work, we present a novel framework for preconditioning on Riemannian manifolds and introduce a metric, the leading angle, to evaluate preconditioners for symmetric eigenvalue problems. We extend the locally optimal Riemannian accelerated gradient method for Riemannian convex optimization to develop the Riemannian Acceleration with Preconditioning (RAP) method for symmetric eigenvalue problems, thereby providing theoretical evidence to support its acceleration. Our analysis of the Schwarz preconditioner for elliptic eigenvalue problems demonstrates that RAP achieves a convergence rate of 1 − C κ − 1 / 2 1-C\kappa^{-1/2} , which is an improvement over the preconditioned steepest descent method’s rate of 1 − C ​ κ − 1 1-C\kappa^{-1} . The exponent in κ − 1 / 2 \kappa^{-1/2} is sharp, and numerical experiments confirm our theoretical findings.

Large-scale symmetric eigenvalue problems

The symmetric eigenvalue problem holds a prominent place in the realm of numerical analysis and scientific computing

;
Parlett 1998
;

;
Saad 2011
.
Let

(1)

In the scenario that the matrix is only available for matrix-vector multiplications, there are mainly two types of reliable and efficient numerical methods: Lanczos-type methods and gradient-type methods.
Compared to Lanczos-type methods, gradient-type methods can utilize preconditioners efficiently and become highly effective for eigenvalue problems arising from the discretization of elliptic operators, as the rate of convergence can become independent of the mesh size when a good preconditioner is employed
Knyazev 1998
.
In this paper, we mainly focus on gradient-type methods with preconditioning.

Preconditioned eigensolvers

Preconditioned eigensolvers have been studied for over four decades, with a rich history of research and development. For an in-depth exploration of the early developments in this field, we refer readers to D’yakonov’s comprehensive book
D’yakonov 1996
and Knyazev’s illuminating survey paper
Knyazev 1998

Among the myriad of preconditioned eigensolvers, the Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (LOBPCG) method
Knyazev 2001
stands out as one of the most popular choices. However, despite its popularity, analyzing the convergence of LOBPCG remains a formidable challenge. Previous endeavors about the theoretical analysis of the LOBPCG method are mainly from two perspectives. One is the sharp estimation of the convergence rate for the Preconditioned Steepest Descent (PSD) method
Knyazev and Neymeyr 2003
;
Neymeyr et al. 2011
;
Neymeyr 2012
;
Argentati et al. 2017
;
Knyazev and Neymeyr 2009

approximation, which is called momentum term. The other one is the understanding of the momentum term
Ovtchinnikov 2006
;
Ovtchinnikov 2008

However, it is important to note that his work did not fully exploit the acceleration properties of LOBPCG, which has been observed by numerical results in
Knyazev 2001
.

Convexity of symmetric eigenvalue problems

The convexity of symmetric eigenvalue problems is a crucial issue for understanding the convergence of eigensolvers. In fact, a weakened form of this convexity, known as the Polyak–Lojasiewicz condition, is a fundamental result in some textbook, such as
(
Demmel 1997
, Theorem 5.5)
. Although it is not as strong as strong convexity, it is still sufficient to guarantee linear convergence of gradient-type methods.

To establish a strong-convexity-like structure in Euclidean space, a common technique involves removing the homogeneous direction of the Rayleigh quotient. For instance, in
Shao et al. 2024
, Shao, Chen and Bai proposed an implicit convexity structure by minimizing the Rayleigh quotient in the tangent plane of an eigenvector approximation. This approach led to the development of a provable accelerated eigensolver based on Preconditioning and Implicit Convexity (EPIC). Another example of creating a convexity structure in Euclidean space is found in
Li et al. 2019
, where a low-rank approximation problem is considered as a replacement for Rayleigh quotient optimization.

In contrast, a more natural approach to developing a convexity structure for eigenvalue problems involves working on Riemannian manifolds as

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}Ax

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}Mx=1.

There are several results concerning the geodesic convexity of the symmetric eigenvalue problem, arising from its interpretation as an optimization problem with orthogonality constraints

;
Edelman et al. 1998
;

;
Dai et al. 2020

Acceleration on Riemannian optimization

The gradient method, with its roots tracing back to Euler and Lagrange, stands as one of the earliest approaches for convex optimization problems. Forty years ago, Nesterov

Turning to Riemannian optimization

Paper organization

In Section 2, we explore the geodesic convexity of preconditioned symmetric eigenvalue problems. Beginning with a continuous analogy, we introduce a new formulation for incorporating preconditioning on Riemannian manifolds, which differs from the classical approach presented by Mishra and Sepulchre in
Mishra and Sepulchre 2016
. Unlike the analysis of eigensystems without preconditioning, preconditioned eigenvalue problems present unique theoretical challenges. To address these challenges, we develop new metrics for evaluating preconditioners, such as the leading angle. Finally, we provide estimates of local geodesic convexity and Lipschitz smoothness for symmetric eigenvalue problems under preconditioning.

In Section 3, we propose a Locally Optimal Riemannian Accelerated Gradient (LORAG) method for general Riemannian convex optimization problems, assuming only the local geodesic convexity and Lipschitz smoothness, and prove the acceleration.

Drawing from the theoretical foundations laid in Section 2 and the algorithmic insights from Section 3, we propose the Riemannian Acceleration with Preconditioning (RAP) method for preconditioned eigensystems in Section 4. In comparison to

In contrast, when dealing with the smallest eigenvalue problem,

, the right-hand side is confined to the eigenspace spanned by

. Therefore, it is crucial to assess the behavior of

specifically within a neighborhood of this eigenspace.

While the traditional measurement
\kappa_{\nu}
does not accurately reflect the quality of
B
within a neighborhood of the eigenspace, it still provides a global bound for

Lemma 8
.

With notations in
Definition

6
, it holds that

\cos\vartheta\leq\sqrt{1-\kappa_{\nu}^{-1}}.

Proof.

For any

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert x^{\mathsf{T}}Av\rvert}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}\lVert v\rVert_{A}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\bigl\lvert\alpha x^{\mathsf{T}}Bv+x^{\mathsf{T}}(A-\alpha B)v\bigr\rvert}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}\lVert v\rVert_{A}}=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\bigl\lvert x^{\mathsf{T}}(A-\alpha B)v\bigr\rvert}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}\lVert v\rVert_{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lVert A^{-1}(A-\alpha B)x\rVert_{A}\lVert v\rVert_{A}}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}\lVert v\rVert_{A}}=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lVert(I-\alpha A^{-1}B)x\rVert_{A}}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Bigl(x^{\mathsf{T}}Ax-\frac{(x^{\mathsf{T}}Bx)^{2}}{x^{\mathsf{T}}BA^{-1}Bx}\Bigr)^{1/2}}{\lVert x\rVert_{A}}\leq\sqrt{1-\kappa_{\nu}^{-1}},

where we use

in the last inequality.

for

and
v_{j}\in V_{j}

•

Finite covering: Assume there are at most

nonzero elements at each column of

is independent of

•

Local stability: There exists

, such that
\lVert R_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}v_{i}\rVert_{A}^{2}\leq\omega\lVert v_{i}\rVert_{B_{i}}^{2}

With

Proposition 32
.

Under

, the smallest and largest eigenvalues of

and

Specifically, in DDM,

Proposition 33
.

Let

be the smallest eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenvector of

, i.e.,

(B_{0}u_{0},v_{0})=\lambda_{0}\langle u_{0},v_{0}\rangle\quad\forall\,v_{0}\in V_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert u_{0}\rVert=1.

Define

,

\displaystyle C_{c}

\displaystyle:=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert u_{1}-\alpha R_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}u_{0}\rVert_{A},

\displaystyle C_{P}

\displaystyle:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\lambda_{\max}(B_{i}^{-1})=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\lambda_{\min}^{-1}(B_{i}),

\displaystyle C_{a}

\displaystyle:=\max_{v\in V}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert R_{i}v\rVert^{2}}{\lVert v\rVert^{2}},

Under

Lemma 34

Let

be an eigenvector of

\lVert R_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{0}^{-1}R_{0}u_{1}-\lambda_{0}^{-1}u_{1}\rVert_{A}\leq(\omega\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}+\lambda_{0}^{-1})C_{c}\lesssim\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}H.

Proof.

For any

\displaystyle\lVert R_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{0}^{-1}R_{0}u_{1}-\lambda_{0}^{-1}u_{1}\rVert_{A}

Note that for any

and

Theorem 38
.

Assume the initial point
\widetilde{x}_{0}
is the eigenvector calculated on the coarse space
V_{0}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{x}_{0}=R_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}u_{0}
. When

then

is monotonic convergence, and the convergence rate is

\mathsf{Rq}(x_{m})-\lambda_{1}\leq 2\bigl(1-C(\mathsf{gap}/\kappa_{\nu})^{1/2}\bigr)^{m}\bigl(\mathsf{Rq}(x_{0})-\lambda_{1}\bigr),

where

is a constant independent of

,

Proof.

First, let us pick a

\rho=\lambda_{1}+H\kappa_{\nu}^{2}\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{1}+\frac{\mathsf{gap}^{2}-\mathsf{gap}^{3}}{\kappa_{\nu}^{2}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})<\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{2}.

Then we know that

\varepsilon=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{-1}-\rho^{-1}}{\lambda_{1}^{-1}-\lambda_{2}^{-1}}=\frac{\lambda_{2}(\rho-\lambda_{1})}{\rho(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})}\leq\frac{H\kappa_{\nu}^{2}}{\mathsf{gap}-\mathsf{gap}^{2}}.

Note that in

36

\cos\vartheta\lesssim\sqrt{\kappa_{\nu}\varepsilon}+H.

By the result for condition number in

\kappa_{B}=\frac{L_{B}}{\mu_{B}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{\nu}}{\mathsf{gap}}\Bigl(1+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\kappa_{\nu}(\mathsf{gap}^{-2}\cos^{2}\vartheta+\varepsilon)\bigr)\Bigr)\leq\frac{\kappa_{\nu}}{\mathsf{gap}}\Bigl(1+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\frac{H\kappa_{\nu}^{4}}{\mathsf{gap}^{3}-\mathsf{gap}^{4}}\bigr)\Bigr)\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{\nu}}{\mathsf{gap}},

where

48
is used in the last inequality. In this case, by

\mathsf{Rq}(x_{0})-\lambda_{1}\lesssim\lambda_{1}H^{2}=\frac{(\rho-\lambda_{1})\lambda_{1}H}{\kappa_{\nu}^{2}\lambda_{2}}=C_{0}H\frac{(\rho-\lambda_{1})}{11(1+2\sqrt{\kappa_{B}})^{2}\kappa_{B}},

Thus, when

Theorem

29
.

Remark 40

Compared to the Preconditioned Steepest Descent (PSD) method, the rate of convergence is improved

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we introduced new tools for analyzing eigenvalue problems with preconditioning on Riemannian manifolds and proposed a Riemannian acceleration method with preconditioning. The acceleration of this method is both theoretically proven and numerically validated. Future research will focus on extending this theory to block versions.

Instruments on Riemannian manifolds

First, we present some results about sphere, the Riemannian manifold we focus on. These results mainly come from

Proposition 41
.

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with the Riemannian metric induced by the standard Euclidean inner product, the following properties hold.

•

For any
x\in\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
, the tangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
is

T_{x}\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x^{\mathsf{T}}v=0,\,x\in\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}\}.

And the projection of
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
T_{x}\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
is

P_{x}\xi=(I-xx^{\mathsf{T}})\xi.

•

The geodesic is a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\mapsto\mathcal{M}
of constant speed and locally minimum length, which plays the role of straight line in Riemannian manifolds. For any
x\in\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
and
v\in T_{x}\mathcal{S}^{n\!-\!1}
, the geodesic
\gamma
with initial value
x
and speed
v
is

\gamma(t)=\cos(\lVert v\rVert t)x+\sin(\lVert v\rVert t)\frac{v}{\lVert v\rVert}.

•

Let
\exp_{x}\colon T_{x}\mathcal{M}\mapsto\mathcal{M}
be the exponential map, which is defined as
\exp_{x}(v)=\gamma(1)
, where
\gamma
is the unique geodesic satisfying
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
. The exponential map on sphere is

\exp_{x}(v)=\gamma(1)=\cos(\lVert v\rVert)x+\sin(\lVert v\rVert)\frac{v}{\lVert v\rVert}.

•

For a local neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
x
, the exponential map
\exp_{x}
is a diffeomorphism in
\mathcal{U}
, and its inverse, which is called logarithmic map, can be defined as
\log_{x}=\exp_{x}^{-1}\colon\mathcal{U}\mapsto T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. For

, the logarithmic map is

\log_{x}(y)=\arccos(x^{\mathsf{T}}y)\frac{P_{x}y}{\lVert P_{x}y\rVert}.

Moreover, the norm
\lVert\log_{x}(y)\rVert
is the distance between
x
and
y
on
\mathcal{M}
.

•

For any
x
and

in
\mathcal{M}

to

\gamma(0)=x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\mathsf{Rq}(x)[v]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\mathsf{Rq}(x+\epsilon v)-\mathsf{Rq}(x)\Bigr)

For the Hessian, note that

\displaystyle\langle v,\nabla^{2}\mathsf{Rq}(x)[v]\rangle

\displaystyle=v^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{D}\overline{G}(x)[v]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}v^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(\overline{G}(x+\epsilon v)-\overline{G}(x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x^{\mathsf{T}}Mx}\Bigl(v^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(A-\mathsf{Rq}(x)M\bigr)v-v^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(Mx\overline{G}^{\mathsf{T}}(x)+\overline{G}(x)(Mx)^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)v\Bigr)
