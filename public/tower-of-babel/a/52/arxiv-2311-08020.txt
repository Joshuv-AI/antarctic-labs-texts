Abstract
. We prove a new signed elementary symmetric function expansion of the chromatic quasisymmetric function of any natural unit interval graph. We then use a sign-reversing involution to prove a new combinatorial formula for K K -chains, which are graphs formed by joining cliques at single vertices. This formula immediately implies e e -positivity and e e -unimodality for K K -chains. We also prove a version of our signed e e -expansion for arbitrary graphs.

Background

Let
G=([n],E)
be a graph with vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. A
colouring
of
G
is a function
\kappa:[n]\to\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and we say that
\kappa
is
proper
if
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
whenever
\{i,j\}\in E
, in other words, adjacent vertices are assigned different positive integers, which we think of colours. We denote by
\text{asc}(\kappa)
the number of
ascents
of
\kappa
, which are pairs of vertices
(i,j)
with
i<j
and
\kappa(i)<\kappa(j)
. The
chromatic quasisymmetric function
of
G
is the formal power series in an infinite alphabet of variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)
and an additional variable
q
given by
[
45
, Definition 1.2]

(2.1)

X_{G}(\bm{x};q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa:[n]\to\mathbb{N}\\
\kappa\text{ proper}\end{subarray}}q^{\text{asc}(\kappa)}x_{\kappa(1)}x_{\kappa(2)}\cdots x_{\kappa(n)}.

Unlike the
chromatic symmetric function

X_{G}(\bm{x})=X_{G}(\bm{x};1)
, which is always symmetric in the
x_{i}
variables,
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)
is not symmetric in general. Nevertheless, if
G
is a
natural unit interval graph
, meaning that

(2.2)

\text{ for all }1\leq i<j<k\leq n\text{, if }\{i,k\}\in E,\text{ then }\{i,j\}\in E\text{ and }\{j,k\}\in E,

then
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)
is symmetric in the
x_{i}
variables
[
45
, Theorem 4.5]
and palindromic in
q
with center of symmetry
\frac{|E|}{2}

[
45
, Corollary 4.6]
, meaning that
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)=q^{|E|}X_{G}(\bm{x};q^{-1})
. In this case, we can consider the expansion

(2.3)

X_{G}(\bm{x};q)=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}(q)e_{\mu}

in terms of the
elementary symmetric functions

e_{\mu}
, which are indexed by integer partitions
\mu=\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{\ell}
and defined by

(2.4)

e_{\mu}=e_{\mu_{1}}\cdots e_{\mu_{\ell}},\text{ where }e_{k}=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}.

We say that
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)
is
e
-positive
if every polynomial

has positive coefficients and we say that
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)
is
e
-unimodal
if every polynomial

is

, meaning that the coefficients form a weakly increasing sequence followed by a weakly decreasing sequence.

Remark 2.4
.

An abstract graph is called a
unit interval graph
if its vertices can be labelled
1
through
n
so that (
2.2
) holds. The name comes from an equivalent characterization as a graph whose vertices are unit intervals of the real line and whose edges join intersecting intervals. It is convenient to consider
natural unit interval graphs
, where the adjective “natural” means that such a labelling is given. The chromatic quasisymmetric function does not depend on the choice of labelling, as long as (
2.2

[
45

Remark 2.5
.

Let
G=([n],E)
be a natural unit interval graph and consider the graph
G^{\prime}=([n],\{\{n+1-j,n+1-i\}:\ \{i,j\}\in E\})
given by reversing the vertex labels. If we restrict to
n

\kappa:[n]\to[n]
of

by setting

by symmetry in the

using descents instead of ascents. Similarly, we can switch the roles of

in everything that follows, for example, we can use increasing trees instead of decreasing trees.

, and

\varphi(\mathcal{F})
by replacing

(3.12)

\mathcal{S}_{1}=(P_{i_{j}\to i_{j+1}-1-\alpha^{(j)}_{\ell}},\alpha^{(j)}\setminus\alpha^{(j)}_{\ell},r_{j})\text{ and }\mathcal{S}_{2}=(P_{i_{j+1}-\alpha^{(j)}_{\ell}\to i_{j+1}-1},\alpha^{(j)}_{\ell},1).

We now claim that

Definition 3.10
.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{h}
be a permutation of a subset

.

Lemma 3.11
.

The function
list
is a bijection between decreasing subtrees of

G

Proof.

Let

We now introduce LLT polynomials and plethystic substitution. All of the properties we will need can be blackboxed.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
G=([n],E)
be a natural unit interval graph. The

is

(3.16)

\text{LLT}_{G}(\bm{x};q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa:[n]\to\mathbb{N}\\
\text{ not necessarily proper }\end{subarray}}q^{\text{asc}(\kappa)}x_{\kappa(1)}x_{\kappa(2)}\cdots x_{\kappa(n)}.

Note that we are summing over arbitrary colourings of

, not just the proper ones. Now these power series are related by the following operation.

Definition 3.15
.

Let

be a polynomial. The

is defined by setting
p_{k}[a(q)\bm{x}]=(a_{0}+a_{1}q^{k}+\cdots+a_{j}q^{jk})p_{k}
for every

is the

, and extending to an algebra homomorphism on symmetric functions.

Proposition 3.16
.

[
12
, Proposition 3.5]
The chromatic quasisymmetric function

is related to the LLT polynomial

via the equation

(3.17)

Now Alexandersson conjectured
[
3

and proved with Sulzgruber
[

Definition 3.17
.

Let

. For

\pi(\theta)=\{[u_{1}]_{\theta},\ldots,[u_{m}]_{\theta}\}

and we define

to be the partition of

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
G
be a natural unit interval graph. The shifted LLT polynomial

satisfies

(3.18)

\text{LLT}_{G}(\bm{x};q+1)=\sum_{\theta\subseteq E(G)}q^{|\theta|}e_{\lambda(\theta)}.

Therefore, we get an expression for

Lemma 3.19
.

(1)

We have
e_{n}[(q-1)\bm{x}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}e_{k}[q\bm{x}]e_{n-k}[-\bm{x}]
.

(2)

We have

, where

(3)

We have

.

(4)

We have

(3.19)

\frac{e_{n}[(q-1)\bm{x}]}{q-1}=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-1}[\alpha_{1}]_{q}e_{\text{sort}(\alpha)}.

Proof.

The first three parts are
[

,
[

, Page 448]

[

(3.20)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{n}[(q-1)\bm{x}]}{q-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}e_{k}[q\bm{x}]e_{n-k}[-\bm{x}]=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k}e_{k}(-1)^{n-k}h_{n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k}e_{k}(-1)^{n-k}\left(\sum_{\alpha\vDash n-k}(-1)^{n-k-\ell(\alpha)}e_{\text{sort}(\alpha)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-1}\frac{q^{\alpha_{1}}-1}{q-1}e_{\text{sort}(\alpha)}=\sum_{\alpha\vDash n}(-1)^{\ell(\alpha)-1}[\alpha_{1}]_{q}e_{\text{sort}(\alpha)}.

In this section, we prove a combinatorial
e

K
-chain. We begin by setting some notation.

Definition 4.1
.

We define the
sum
of graphs
G_{1}=([n_{1}],E_{1})
and

to be

(4.1)

Informally, vertex

of

and vertex

of

are glued together. For a composition
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{\ell}
with all parts at least

(4.2)

where
K_{a}
is the complete graph on

are called

-chains
. Note that

-chains are natural unit interval graphs. The bowtie graph from Figure

is

This notation is due to Gebhard and Sagan, who proved
[

that
X_{K_{\gamma}}(\bm{x})
is
e
-positive for every

. We will prove that the chromatic
quasisymmetric
function

is
e
-positive and

\alpha^{(1)}_{1}(\mathcal{F})
and

, where
\mathcal{T}_{1}=(T_{1},\alpha^{(1)}(\mathcal{F}),r_{1}(\mathcal{F}))
is the tree triple of
\mathcal{F}
with
1\in V(T_{1})
, so we make the following definitions. Let
G
be a natural unit interval graph.

Definition 4.2

A tree triple
\mathcal{T}=(T,\alpha,r)
of

is

if
\ell(\alpha)\geq 2
. A forest triple
\mathcal{F}
of

is an

Note that if
\mathcal{T}=(T,\alpha,r)
is not breakable, then the composition
\alpha

|V(T)|
. In particular, if
\mathcal{F}

\text{sign}(\mathcal{F})=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(1-1)}=+1

Definition 4.3
.

A forest triple
\mathcal{F}
of
G
is
simple
if
r_{1}(\mathcal{F})=1
. Let
\text{FT}_{\text{simple}}(G)
denote the set of simple forest triples of

for

is a function
\varphi:\text{FT}_{\text{simple}}(G)\to\text{FT}_{\text{simple}}(G)
with the following properties.

(1)

\varphi
is an

\varphi(\varphi(\mathcal{F}))=\mathcal{F}
for all
\mathcal{F}\in\text{FT}_{\text{simple}}(G)
. In other words,
\varphi
is its own inverse, so in particular it is a bijection.

(2)

\varphi
preserves type, weight, and
\alpha^{(1)}_{1}
, which is the first part of the composition associated to the tree containing the vertex

.

(3)

If
\mathcal{F}
is a

of
\varphi
, meaning that
\varphi(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is an atom.

(4)

\varphi
is

, meaning that if
\varphi(\mathcal{F})\neq\mathcal{F}
, then
\text{sign}(\varphi(\mathcal{F}))=-\text{sign}(\mathcal{F})
.

Note that a forest triple
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\text{FT}(G)
arises from some simple forest triple
\mathcal{F}
by choosing some

. Therefore, if we can find a nice involution
\varphi
for

, then we have

(4.3)

X_{G}(\bm{x};q)=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in\text{FT}_{\text{simple}}(G),\varphi(\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}}[\alpha^{(1)}_{1}(\mathcal{F})]_{q}\ q^{\text{weight}(\mathcal{F})}e_{\text{type}(\mathcal{F})},

thus proving that
X_{G}(\bm{x};q)
is
e
-positive. The following Lemma will help us describe forest triples of a sum of graphs.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
T
be a decreasing subtree of
G
that contains a cut vertex
c
. Then the tree list
\sigma=\text{list}(T)
contains all of the vertices of
T
at most
c
in some order, followed by all of the vertices of
T
strictly greater than
c
in some order.

Proof.

If not, then there is some

with
\sigma_{i}>c
and
\sigma_{i+1}\leq c
. By the LR maxima condition, the cut vertex
c
must appear before
\sigma_{i}
, but this means that
\sigma_{i+1}<c
and
\sigma
does not satisfy the descent condition.
∎

We will want a standard way of taking a permutation
\sigma
and writing a new permutation whose first entry is
\min(\sigma)
. The following choice is not the only possibility, but it will work.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{h}
be a permutation of some set
A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{h}\}\subseteq[n]
. Define
\text{indstart}(\sigma)
to be the integer

such that
\sigma_{1}=a_{r}
and define the permutation

(4.4)

\text{startmin}(\sigma)=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{r-1}(\sigma),

where
w_{i}
is the transposition that switches the positions of the entries
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
in
\sigma
. Note that
\text{startmin}(\sigma)
starts with its minimum element
a_{1}
, and if the entries in

are all adjacent in

, then we have

(4.5)

\text{inv}_{G}(\sigma)=\text{inv}_{G}(\text{startmin}(\sigma))+(\text{indstart}(\sigma)-1)

because each of the
(\text{indstart}(\sigma)-1)
transpositions that are applied decreases the number of
G
-inversions by exactly one. Conversely, given a permutation
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{h}
of
A
with
\sigma_{1}=a_{1}
and an integer
1\leq r\leq h
, define the permutation

(4.6)

\text{startr}(\sigma,r)=w_{r-1}\cdots w_{2}w_{1}(\sigma).

Note that
\text{startr}(\sigma,r)
starts with the element

and
\text{startmin}(\text{startr}(\sigma,\text{indstart}(\sigma)))=\sigma
.

Example 4.6

For
\sigma=61743

\text{indstart}(\sigma)=4
and
\text{startmin}(\sigma)=13764
.
