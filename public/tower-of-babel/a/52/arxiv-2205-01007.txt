Resonant string behavior in a Gravitational Wave burst
Vojtěch Liška, Rikard von Unge

Abstract
We investigate the behavior of classical closed strings in a gravitational wave burst and discover an intriguing resonant behavior where the energy absorbed by the strings is crucially dependent on the amplitude and frequency of the gravitational wave. This behavior can be traced to the well-known behavior of the solutions to the Mathieu equation.



I
Introduction

With the detection of gravitational waves
Abbott et al. 2016a
we have gained a new channel for information from the universe. Apart from photons and other cosmic ray particles, it is now possible to gather data from gravitational waves. It is therefore of utmost importance to understand how gravitational waves are affected by the matter filling the universe between the place where the gravitational radiation is generated and us. In this paper, we make a modest contribution to this investigation by assuming that the matter consists of classical closed strings and investigating how this affects the gravitational radiation that reaches us.

This topic has been studied before, both at the classical
Amati and Klimcik 1988
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1989
;
Costa and de Vega 1991a
and quantum
Amati and Klimcik 1988
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1989
;
Costa and de Vega 1991b
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1990
;
de Vega et al. 1992
;
Adamo et al. 2017
level. In these papers, the interaction between strings and a sandwich wave
Bondi et al. 1959
;
Penrose 1965
, a gravitational wave where the gravitational background differs from flat space only in a small region which is moving with the speed of light in a particular direction. The metric considered was the Aichelburg-Sexl metric, which arises as the shock-wave metric of a black hole moving with the speed of light. In this paper, we instead assume that in the region that differs from Minkowski space, the metric looks like the metric of a gravitational wave of fixed frequency
\omega
but where the amplitude
A
is modulated by a Gaussian. We find an intriguing dependence on
A
and
\omega
where there are regions in the

The paper is organized as follows: In section

II
The String equations

We want to study the behavior of strings when meeting a gravitational wave burst, which we will describe using the Brinkman metric
Brinkman 1923
;
Brinkman 1925

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-2dudv+H(u,x,y)du^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}\;.

(1)

In order for the metric to be a vacuum solution, the function
H
has to satisfy the Laplace equation

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})H=0\;,

(2)

whereas the
u
dependence of
H
is completely arbitrary. The standard gravitational waves are described by choosing
H=(x^{2}-y^{2})f(u)+2xyg(u)
. The two terms correspond to the two polarizations of the gravitational wave and
f(u)
and
g(u)
are usually chosen to be harmonic functions.

Starting from the Nambu-Goto action
Nambu 1986
;
Goto 1971
for a string in a nontrivial background
G_{MN}(X)

\displaystyle S=\int d\tau d\sigma\sqrt{-\det\left(\partial_{a}X^{M}\partial_{b}X^{N}G_{MN}\right)}\;,

(3)

we find the equations of motion

\displaystyle\partial_{a}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}G_{MN}\partial_{b}X^{N}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}\partial_{M}G_{NK}\partial_{a}X^{N}\partial_{b}X^{K}=0\;,

(4)

where
\gamma_{ab}=\partial_{a}X^{M}\partial_{b}X^{N}G_{MN}
is the pull-back metric on the world sheet.

The Nambu-Goto action is reparametrization invariant and to solve the equations we need to fix a gauge, i.e., to choose the coordinates
\tau
and
\sigma
on the world sheet. This can be done so that
\gamma_{\tau\sigma}=0
and
\gamma_{\tau\tau}+\gamma_{\sigma\sigma}=0
, so-called orthogonal or conformal gauge.

With this gauge choice in the gravitational wave background, the equations that we need to solve are

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})u

\displaystyle=0\;,

(5)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})x

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{x}H}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}u)^{2}-(\partial_{\sigma}u)^{2}\right]\;,

(6)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})y

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{y}H}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}u)^{2}-(\partial_{\sigma}u)^{2}\right]\;,

(7)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})v

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{u}H}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}u)^{2}-(\partial_{\sigma}u)^{2}\right]+\partial_{x}H(\partial_{\tau}{u}\partial_{\tau}x-\partial_{\sigma}u\partial_{\sigma}x)+\partial_{y}H(\partial_{\tau}{u}\partial_{\tau}y-\partial_{\sigma}u\partial_{\sigma}y)\;,

(8)

supplemented by the gauge choice

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}u\partial_{\sigma}v+\partial_{\tau}v\partial_{\sigma}u

\displaystyle=H\partial_{\tau}u\partial_{\sigma}u+\partial_{\tau}x\partial_{\sigma}x+\partial_{\tau}y\partial_{\sigma}y\;,

(9)

\displaystyle 2\partial_{\tau}u\partial_{\tau}v+2\partial_{\sigma}u\partial_{\sigma}v

\displaystyle=H((\partial_{\tau}u)^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}u)^{2})+(\partial_{\tau}x)^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}x)^{2}+(\partial_{\tau}y)^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}y)^{2}\;.

(10)

The gauge is implemented by first choosing the
\tau
coordinate proportional to
u

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\lambda\tau\;,

(11)

where
\lambda
is an arbitrary constant. With this choice, the equations that ensure that
\partial_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\sigma}
are orthogonal (
9
) and of equal length (
10
) simplify

\displaystyle\lambda\partial_{\sigma}v

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}x\partial_{\sigma}x+\partial_{\tau}y\partial_{\sigma}y\;,

(12)

\displaystyle 2\lambda\partial_{\tau}v

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}H+(\partial_{\tau}x)^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}x)^{2}+(\partial_{\tau}y)^{2}+(\partial_{\sigma}y)^{2}\;,

(13)

and the equations for the transverse coordinates also simplify to become

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})x

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}{\partial_{x}H}\;,

(14)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})y

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}{\partial_{y}H}\;.

(15)

It is straightforward to check that (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
) imply (
8
).

III
Flat space

As a warmup exercise we will solve the equations for the case
H=0
which is equivalent to Minkowski space. To begin with, we solve (
14
) and (
15
). The simplest way to find a set of solutions is to separate variables so that if
x(\tau,\sigma)=T_{x}(\tau)S_{x}(\sigma)
we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{x}}{T_{x}}=\frac{\partial_{\sigma}^{2}S_{x}}{S_{x}}=-K\;.

(16)

The
2\pi
periodicity in
\sigma
then tells us that we have to choose
K=n^{2}
with
n
being an integer. The equation for
y
is solved similarly. The simplest nontrivial solution in this family is

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=R\cos(\tau)\cos(\sigma)\;,

(17)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=R\cos(\tau)\sin(\sigma)\;,

(18)

with
R
constant.
Plugging this into (
12
) and (
13
) we get that

\partial_{\sigma}v=0
and
\partial_{\tau}v=\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda}
. In the more familiar
t
and
z
coordinates, this corresponds to

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(u+v)=(\lambda+\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda})\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\;,

(19)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(u-v)=(\lambda-\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda})\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\;.

(20)

We see that in general, the
z
-coordinate changes as a function of
t
corresponding to a motion of the center of mass of the string. However, if we choose
\lambda=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}
the
z
coordinate is constant and there is no center of mass motion in the
z
direction but a different choice of
\lambda
would include also this possibility.

IV
A string in a plane gravitational wave

We now turn to the topic of the motion of the string in a plane gravitational wave background described by

\displaystyle H=(x^{2}-y^{2})A\cos(\omega u)\;,

(21)

where
A
is the amplitude and
\omega
is the frequency of the wave. The equation for the transversal coordinates now becomes

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})x

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}A\cos(\omega u)x\;,

(22)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})y

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{2}A\cos(\omega u)y\;.

(23)

Again we try separation of variables to find a solution. This leads to the equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{x}}{T_{x}}-\lambda^{2}A\cos(\lambda\omega\tau)=\frac{\partial_{\sigma}^{2}S_{x}}{S_{x}}=-n^{2}\;,

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{y}}{T_{y}}+\lambda^{2}A\cos(\lambda\omega\tau)=\frac{\partial_{\sigma}^{2}S_{y}}{S_{y}}=-n^{2}\;,

(25)

from which we can extract the equations for
T_{x}
and
T_{y}

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{x}+(n^{2}-\lambda^{2}A\cos(\lambda\omega\tau))T_{x}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(26)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{y}+(n^{2}+\lambda^{2}A\cos(\lambda\omega\tau))T_{y}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(27)

and we see that
T_{x}
and
T_{y}
now satisfies the Mathieu equation

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}w(t)}{dt^{2}}+(a-2q\cos(2t))w(t)=0\;,

(28)

with

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{4n^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}}\;,

(29)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{2A}{\omega^{2}}\;,

(30)

and with the Mathieu functions as solutions.

The behavior of the Mathieu functions depending on the parameters
a
and
q
is quite intricate. As can be seen in figure
1
, there are regions in the
(q,a)
plane where the solutions, although not periodical, behave nicely and stay finite for all times. There are other regions where instead the solutions oscillate uncontrollably as time goes to infinity. On the borders between these regions, the solutions are periodic.

Figure 1:
The moduli space of the Mathieu equation. The shaded regions correspond to unstable solutions. The parameters
a
and
q
vary on a straight line for a gravitational wave with arbitrary frequency
\omega
but with fixed amplitude
A
.

We see that the type of solution we get will depend crucially on the amplitude and frequency of the gravitational wave. If our choice of parameters is such that the solution is in an unstable region, our string will exhibit resonant behavior where it will fluctuate with an ever larger amplitude as well as start moving in the
z
-direction.

To see this, we have to investigate the behavior of the
v
coordinate which tells us what the string is doing in the
z
direction. For simplicity, we choose
n=1
. Starting with (
12
) the
\sigma
derivative of
v
is

\displaystyle\lambda\partial_{\sigma}v=\frac{1}{4}\partial_{\tau}(T_{y}^{2}-T_{x}^{2})\sin(2\sigma)\;,

(31)

with the simple solution

\displaystyle v=\frac{1}{8\lambda}\partial_{\tau}(T_{x}^{2}-T_{y}^{2})\cos(2\sigma)+k(\tau)\;,

(32)

where
k(\tau)
is an arbitrary function. In the case that
T_{x}^{2}-T_{y}^{2}
is not a constant
v
depends on
\sigma
, and since
z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(u-v)
this means that the string will oscillate also in the
z
direction even if it does not from the beginning. To fix the unknown function
k(\tau)
we insert (
32
) into (
13
) to get an expression for the
\tau
derivative of
k(\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{dk}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{8\lambda}\left[\frac{d^{2}}{d\tau^{2}}(T_{x}^{2}+T_{y}^{2})+4(T_{x}^{2}+T_{y}^{2})\right]\;.

(33)

It is a nontrivial consistency check that the
\sigma
dependence of this equation drops out. The function
k(\tau)
encodes the center of mass motion in the
z
-direction. For instance, if
k\neq\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda}\tau
,
u\neq v
and the center of mass of the string is moving in the
z
-direction.

If we would like to find the full motion of the string in this background, we must choose an initial configuration. We could, for instance, choose the configuration that we studied in flat space where the string starts out as a circle with radius
R
and no motion in the
z
direction. That means we have to choose the initial values (for example at
\tau=0
)

\displaystyle T_{x}

\displaystyle=R\;,

(34)

\displaystyle\dot{T}_{x}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(35)

\displaystyle T_{y}

\displaystyle=R\;,

(36)

\displaystyle\dot{T}_{y}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(37)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\;.

(38)

Solving the equations for the fluctuation in the
x,y
plane, we find

\displaystyle T_{x}(\tau)

\displaystyle=RC_{a,q}\left(\frac{R\omega\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\;,

(39)

\displaystyle T_{y}(\tau)

\displaystyle=RC_{a,-q}\left(\frac{R\omega\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\;,

(40)

where
C_{a,q}(x)
is the even solution to the Mathieu differential equation normalized so that
C(0)=1
, which gives the full solution

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=RC_{a,q}\left(\frac{R\omega\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\cos\sigma\;,

(41)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=RC_{a,-q}\left(\frac{R\omega\tau}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\sin\sigma\;,

(42)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{4\sqrt{2}}\partial_{\tau}(C_{a,q}^{2}-C_{a,-q}^{2})\cos(2\sigma)+k(\tau)\;,

(43)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\tau\;.

(44)

One can easily convince oneself that
C_{a,q}^{2}-C_{a,-q}^{2}
is not constant in general and therefore the string vibrates also in the
z
-direction. Also, for
a
and
q
in the unstable region, the amplitude of the vibrations in the
x,y
plane increases without bound.

V
A string in a sandwich wave

We would like to study the more realistic situation when the motion of the string starts and ends in Minkowski space, but there is a short gravitational wave burst during which the string may gain (or lose) energy from the gravitational wave. An exactly solvable example is when the gravitational wave is a delta function

\displaystyle H=(x^{2}-y^{2})A\delta(u)\;,

(45)

which is known as the Aichelburg-Sexl metric and the behavior of strings in this background has been studied extensively both in the classical
Amati and Klimcik 1988
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1989
;
Costa and de Vega 1991a
and quantum
Amati and Klimcik 1988
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1989
;
Costa and de Vega 1991b
;
de Vega and Sanchez 1990
;
de Vega et al. 1992
cases. We do not claim to derive any new results in this section. Rather, we use some of the techniques developed here to study a more realistic case in chapter
VI
.

In the case at hand, the equations that need to be solved are

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})x=\lambda^{2}A\delta(u)x\;,

(46)

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}^{2}-\partial_{\sigma}^{2})y=-\lambda^{2}A\delta(u)y\;,

(47)

and a separation of variables leads to

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{x}+(n^{2}-\lambda^{2}A\delta(\lambda\tau))T_{x}=0\;,

(48)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}T_{y}+(n^{2}+\lambda^{2}A\delta(\lambda\tau))T_{y}=0\;.

(49)

Integrating these equations from
\tau=-\epsilon
to
\tau=\epsilon
where
\epsilon
is infinitesimal, we find

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}T_{x}(\epsilon)=\partial_{\tau}T_{x}(-\epsilon)+\lambda AT_{x}(0)\;,

(50)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}T_{y}(\epsilon)=\partial_{\tau}T_{y}(-\epsilon)-\lambda AT_{y}(0)\;.

(51)

In particular, if we assume that the string motion before meeting the wave is given by the flat space solution (
17
), for which
n=1
. Then, using the continuity of the string, the motion after the wave hits is given by

\displaystyle T_{x}=R\left(\cos(\tau)+\lambda A\sin(\tau)\right)\;,

(52)

\displaystyle T_{y}=R\left(\cos(\tau)-\lambda A\sin(\tau)\right)\;.

(53)

Integrating the equations for
v
, (
12
) and (
13
), gives

\displaystyle v=\frac{R^{2}}{2\lambda}\left((1+\lambda^{2}A^{2})\tau+\lambda A\cos(2\tau)\cos(2\sigma)\right)\;.

(54)

If the string was at rest before meeting the wave, we know that
\lambda=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}
. The full solution after meeting the wave is then

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=R\left(\cos(\tau)+\frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(\tau)\right)\cos(\sigma)\;,

(55)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=R\left(\cos(\tau)-\frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\sin(\tau)\right)\sin(\sigma)\;,

(56)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\left(1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{2}\right)\tau+\frac{RA}{\sqrt{2}}\cos(2\tau)\cos(2\sigma)\right)\;,

(57)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\tau\;.

(58)

We recognize that the center of mass of the sting has started moving in the negative
z
-direction with the speed
\frac{A^{2}R^{2}}{4+R^{2}A^{2}}
but it has also started vibrating in the
z
-direction. To disentangle the motion, it is useful to make a Lorentz transformation to a frame in which the center of mass of the string is at rest. In that system

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{2}}\tau+\frac{RA}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{2}}}\cos(2\tau)\cos(2\sigma)\right)\;,

(59)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{2}}\tau\;.

(60)

We find the internal energy of the string by using a method outlined in the appendix. It involves going to static gauge which can be found through a conformal transformation. The outcome in this case is

\displaystyle E_{int}=\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{2}}E_{i}\;,

(61)

where
E_{i}
is the energy of the initial string configuration.
From the point of view of an observer at rest with respect to the string before meeting the gravitational wave, it looks like the string has increased its mass (internal energy) and has started moving in the
z
-direction. The total increase in energy combines these two effects and is therefore

\displaystyle E=\left(1+\frac{R^{2}A^{2}}{4}\right)E_{i}\;.

(62)

VI
A String in a gravitational wave burst

To study a more realistic example, we combine the previous cases and assume a gravitational wave profile

\displaystyle H(x,y,,u)

\displaystyle=(x^{2}-y^{2})f(u)\;,

(63)

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=A\cos(\omega u)e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{\rho^{2}}}\;,

(64)

where the constant
\rho
was chosen such that the burst lasts for approximately ten periods. This is the simplest approximation of what a real gravitational wave that we observe on Earth could look like. However, since our calculations are numerical, there is nothing that prevents us from using an even more realistic waveform if we should like.

Even though in this case we cannot solve the equations for the transverse directions analytically, we know that they have to take the form

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}(\tau)e^{in\sigma}\;,

(65)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}d_{n}(\tau)e^{in\sigma}\;,

(66)

which, with the function

63

\displaystyle\ddot{c}_{n}+(n^{2}-\lambda^{2}f(u))c_{n}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(67)

\displaystyle\ddot{d}_{n}+(n^{2}+\lambda^{2}f(u))c_{n}

\displaystyle=0\;,

(68)

for the unknown coefficients,
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
. At the same time, we make the ansatz

\displaystyle v=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}v_{n}(\tau)e^{in\sigma}\;,

(69)

which gives the equations

\displaystyle\lambda nv_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}k(c_{k}\dot{c}_{n-k}+d_{k}\dot{d}_{n-k})\;,

(70)

for

. From this equation, we find that the higher

modes in general get excited. For instance, If
c_{\pm 1}
and

are nonzero we should expect that

. In the previous section, where we studied the delta function sandwich wave, the

and

57
). The center of mass motion in the
z
-direction is encoded in

which, in contrast to the other

(

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{k}\left(\dot{c}_{k}\dot{c}_{-k}+\dot{d}_{k}\dot{d}_{-k}+k^{2}c_{k}c_{-k}+k^{2}d_{k}d_{-k}+\lambda^{2}A\cos\lambda\omega\tau\left(c_{k}c_{-k}-d_{k}d_{-k}\right)\right)\;.

(71)

Following a procedure completely analogous to what we did for the delta function wave burst, we first find a Lorentz frame in which the center of mass of the string is at rest followed by a conformal transformation to static gauge. It is then straightforward to find the energy of the string. We perform these manipulations numerically and find that the amount of energy that the string absorbs depends strongly on the amplitude
A
and the frequency
\omega
of the wave burst. What is surprising is that the energy absorbed also depends on the relative phase of the vibrations of the string and the vibrations of the gravitational wave. As can be seen in figure

Finally, it is necessary to discuss the choice of units. One might expect the string tension to appear and it does indeed in the calculation of the energy of the string. VII
Conclusions

We have shown that the behavior of classical closed strings in a gravitational wave burst is strongly dependent on the amplitude and frequency of the gravitational wave. The behavior can be traced to the known behavior of the Mathieu equation. Our study is restricted to the simplest possible case where the string fluctuates in the lowest mode only and the fluctuations are transverse to the direction of the gravitational wave. In a more realistic situation, the orientation of the string would be arbitrary and in general, more modes would be excited, possibly according to a Boltzmann distribution. It is interesting to observe that the presence of a mode in the initial state gives rise, through (
13
), to a mode of twice the frequency in the final state. This could presumably give an even stronger resonance behavior than the case treated in this paper.
