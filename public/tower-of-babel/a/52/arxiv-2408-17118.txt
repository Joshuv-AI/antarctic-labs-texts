Efficient Estimation of Unique Components in Independent Component Analysis by Matrix Representation
Yoshitatsu Matsuda, Kazunori Yamaguchi

Abstract
Independent component analysis (ICA) is a widely used method in various applications of signal processing and feature extraction. It extends principal component analysis (PCA) and can extract important and complicated components with small variances. One of the major problems of ICA is that the uniqueness of the solution is not guaranteed, unlike PCA. That is because there are many local optima in optimizing the objective function of ICA. It has been shown previously that the unique global optimum of ICA can be estimated from many random initializations by handcrafted thread computation. In this paper, the unique estimation of ICA is highly accelerated by reformulating the algorithm in matrix representation and reducing redundant calculations. Experimental results on artificial datasets and EEG data verified the efficiency of the proposed method.

Introduction

Independent component analysis (ICA)
is widely used in various applications of signal processing such as blind source separation

[
2
,
3
]
and feature extraction such as image decomposition
[
5
]
.
The linear model of ICA is given as

\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$s$}
,
where
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\left(x_{i}\right)
is the
N
-dimensional observed
signal,
\mbox{\boldmath$A$}=\left(a_{ij}\right)
is the
N\times N
mixing matrix, and

\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=\left(s_{i}\right)
is
the
N
-dimensional source signal.
ICA can estimate
s
from only
x
without knowing
A
under the assumption that
the source components are statistically independent of each other
and they are given according to
non-Gaussian distributions.
Under this assumption, it can be shown that the maximization of the non-Gaussianity of each component leads to the estimation of the accurate source.
Higher-order statistics of the components such as kurtosis can measure the non-Gaussianity.
ICA can be regarded as an extension of principal component analysis (PCA), which maximizes the variances of the components.

As the objective function of ICA is much more complicated than that of PCA,
one of the major problems of ICA is that it is not guaranteed to find the true (globally optimal) solution, unlike PCA. Generally, there are many local optima in the estimation of ICA. To solve this problem, the ordering ICA has been proposed
[
7
]
. The ordering ICA employs a relatively simple objective function of ICA, and it can find the global optimum by a sufficiently large number of threads from random initializations.
The practical usefulness of the ordering ICA has been verified in
the application of EEG analysis

[
8
]
.
Nevertheless, it was not as fast as the
simple ICA, and there was still significant room for acceleration. In addition,
it needs handcrafted thread implementation for each computing environment.
This problem can hinder the availability of the ordering ICA.

In this paper, we propose a novel fast algorithm for the ordering ICA by reformulating it in matrix representation. Matrix manipulations (e.g., the multiplication of large matrices) can be calculated efficiently
in many software environments.
Therefore, the proposed algorithm enables the ordering ICA to be easily utilized in various situations.
Though this idea of using matrix manipulation is simple, it is expected to drastically improve the performance of the ordering ICA in a similar way as in the employment of the mini-batch optimization to deep learning.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, ICA and the ordering ICA are described briefly.
In Section
3
, a new algorithm for the ordering ICA is constructed by matrix representation. Section
4
shows the experimental results of the proposed algorithm in comparison with the other methods using artificial datasets and EEG data. Lastly, we conclude this paper in Section
5
.

2
Background

2.1
Independent Component Analysis

Generally, ICA is a method for extracting unknown sources from only the observed signals without knowing the mixture system
[
2
,
3
]
.
In many cases, ICA assumes the linear mixture model

\mbox{\boldmath$X$}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}
,
where an
N\times M
matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}=\left(x_{im}\right)

is the observed signals of given samples
(
i=1,\cdots,N
and
m=1,\cdots,M
denote the indices of components and samples, respectively).

\mbox{\boldmath$A$}=\left(a_{ij}\right)
(an
N\times N
matrix) is the unknown mixing matrix.

\mbox{\boldmath$S$}=\left(s_{im}\right)
(an
N\times M
matrix) consists of the unknown sources.
The estimation of the source
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}=\left(y_{im}\right)
is given as
\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}=\mbox{\boldmath$W$}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$W$}=\left(w_{ij}\right)
(an
N\times N
matrix) is the separating matrix.
\mbox{\boldmath$W$}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}^{-1}
holds in the true estimation.
The objective functions of ICA
\Phi\left(\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}\right)
are constructed by some higher-order statistics of each component of
Y
so that
the optimization of
\Phi
with respect to
W
corresponds to the maximization of statistical independence among the components. One of the simplest examples of the objective functions is given as
\Phi=\sum_{i}\sum_{m}y_{im}^{4}
.

Though many methods of ICA have been proposed
depending on the choice of the objective function and the optimization algorithm, there exists three representative methods:
the extended InfoMax
[
6
]
(referred to as InfoMax),
Fast ICA
[
4
]
,
and JADE
[
1
]
.
The majority of other methods are variations of these three methods.
It is guaranteed in these methods that
the true estimation of sources is included in the solutions.
However, there are many solutions by these methods due to local optima.
Therefore, it is not guaranteed that the actual solution is the unique true estimation.

2.2
Ordering ICA

The ordering ICA has been recently proposed for estimating the unique solution in ICA

[
7
]
. The following objective function is essential in the ordering ICA:

\Upsilon\left(\alpha_{i}\right)=\alpha_{i}-2\log\left(\alpha_{i}/2+1\right)

(1)

where

\alpha_{i}=\sum_{m}y_{im}^{4}/M-3.

(2)

\alpha_{i}
is the estimated kurtosis of the
i
-th component of
Y
if the component is normalized.
When
X
is whitened in advance and
W
is constrained to orthonormal, the following theorem holds:

Theorem 2.1

Under the assumption that
S
consists of any normalized independent sources (other than the uniform Bernoulli variable) and
X
is given by an invertible linear ICA model

\mbox{\boldmath$X$}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$S$}
,
all the non-Gaussian sources are extracted
by maximizing globally
\Upsilon\left(\alpha_{i}=\sum_{m}y_{im}^{4}/M-3\right)

for each
i
subject to
\sum_{m}y_{im}=0
and

\sum_{m}y_{im}y_{jm}/M=\delta_{ij}
for
j\leq i
.
In addition, the extracted sources are arranged
in descending order
of
\Upsilon\left(\kappa_{i}\right)
, where
\kappa_{i}
is the true kurtosis of the
i
-th component
of
S
.

This theorem means that the unique true estimation can be achieved by maximizing globally
\Upsilon\left(\alpha_{i}\right)
one by one for
i=1,\cdots,N
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}=\left(w_{i1},\ldots,w_{iN}\right)
.
Though
\Upsilon\left(\alpha_{i}\right)
has many local maxima, the global maximum can be searched from many different (for example, random) initializations by parallel computing. In addition, each local maximum can be calculated efficiently by the usual Fast ICA algorithm in the deflation approach
[
4
]
, which optimizes
\sum_{m}y_{im}^{4}
by the Newton-Raphson method one by one under the orthonormality constraints.
In addition, the ordering ICA can estimate the number of non-Gaussian sources by the Gaussianity test
[
7
]
.
It can be shown that the rest of the components are Gaussian once the following condition is satisfied:

\Upsilon\left(\alpha_{i}\right)<\frac{2\left(N-i+2\right)\left(N-i+1\right)}{M}.

(3)

In summary, the original algorithm for the ordering ICA is given in Algorithm
1

[
7
,

]
. Here, the observed signal
X
is whitened in advance. The hyperparameter
L
is the number of random initializations.
K
(the maximum of iterations of the Newton-Raphson method) and
\epsilon
(the convergence threshold) are the hyperparameters on the termination decision of each run of Fast ICA.
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$y$}
denotes the element-wise product. Because the
L

L
is about one hundred
[
8
]
.

3
Algorithm

Here, we describe a novel algorithm for the ordering ICA using matrix representation.
It has three improvements over Algorithm
1
.
First, to utilize the efficiency of matrix multiplication, we construct a matrix of partially recovered signals of
L
runs, as detailed in Section
3.1
.
This calculation is almost the same as the symmetrical approach of the usual fast ICA
[
4
]
without the orthogonalization process. Though the symmetrical approach is much faster than the usual deflation approach, it is not used in practice due to poor results in the source estimation. Our algorithm revives the symmetrical approach in a different context.
Second, to reduce the inefficiency caused by keeping the already recovered signals in the matrix, we introduce a dynamic reduction of rows from the matrix, as detailed in Section
3.2
.
Third, the orthonormality constraints are enforced by the reduction of
X
instead of the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of
w
.
It can reduce the computational costs according to the number of the rest (unestimated) components, as detailed in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Matrix Representation of Multiple Runs

The most time-consuming part of the ordering ICA is the
L
runs of the multiplication of
X
(an
N\times M
matrix and
\left(\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$y$}\right)^{\intercal}
(an
M
-dimensional vector)) in line 28 of Algorithm
1
. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}=\left(z_{ij}\right)
be the
L\times M
matrix of a set of
y
’s to utilize the matrix manipulation. Correspondingly, let
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}=\left(b_{ij}\right)
be the
L\times N
matrix of
\left(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{l}\right)^{\intercal}
for
l=1,\cdots,L
.
Thus, the process in lines 27-28 over multiple runs can be executed as a batch by

\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\leftarrow\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}

(4)

and

\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\leftarrow\mbox{\boldmath$X$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\circ\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}\right)^{\intercal}/M-3\mbox{\boldmath$B$}.

(5)

They can be calculated efficiently by matrix manipulation tools.

3.2
Removal of Converged Runs

When a row of
B
converges to a local optimum, there is no need to update the row further.
In parallel computing, the threads that have finished calculations often have to wait for other threads to finish. On the other hand, it is enough to just reduce the size of
B
in matrix representation.
B
is separated into only the rows needing more updates (denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{\rm{update}}
) and the other rows corresponding to the converged runs (denoted by
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{\rm{conv}}
).
\mbox{\boldmath$B$}_{\rm{conv}}
can be stored without any updates. Computational resources are concentrated on mandatory calculation, avoiding useless updates of converged rows.

3.3
Reduction of Signals under Orthonormality Constraints

In Algorithm
1
, the observed signal
X
is fixed in a given whitened form. To satisfy the orthonormality constraints,
w
is orthonormalized in each iteration. On the other hand, the orthogonality can be satisfied by reducing
X
to be orthogonal
to the already estimated components by an
(i-1)\times N
matrix
W
. For this purpose, an
N\times N
matrix
F
is calculated by

\mbox{\boldmath$F$}=\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{N}-\mbox{\boldmath$W$}^{\intercal}\mbox{\boldmath$W$},

(6)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional identity matrix.

\mbox{\boldmath$W$}\mbox{\boldmath$F$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
holds because each row of
W
is orthonormal to each other.
Let
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}
be an
\left(N-i+1\right)\times N
submatrix including only from the first to the
\left(N-i+1\right)
-th rows of
F
. The rows of

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}
are bases of the orthogonal complement space to
W
. Let
G
be the orthonormalized
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}
, which is calculated by

\mbox{\boldmath$G$}=\left(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}^{\intercal}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}.

(7)

Then,
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
is calculated by

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}=\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$X$}.

(8)

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
is an
\left(N-i+1\right)\times M
matrix, each row of which is orthonormal to each other and the already estimated components. Letting
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
be the vector separating the first independent component of
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
, the vector separating the
i
-th independent component of
X
can be calculated as

\mbox{\boldmath$w$}=\mbox{\boldmath$G$}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
.
By estimating
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}
from
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
instead of
X
, the computational complexity is reduced to
\frac{N-i+1}{N}
. In addition, the orthogonalization process is removed from each iteration.
Furthermore, this process is expected to improve the solution due to the reduction of the search space. The improvement is investigated in Section
4
.

Results

Here, we show the two experimental results: artificial datasets and EEG analysis.

4.1
Basic Settings

All the experiments were carried out on a 40-core server (dual CPU with 20 cores) with 512GB RAM,
where each core is an Intel Xeon 2.3GHz processor.
All the codes were conducted by
MATLAB R2023b.
The proposed method in Algorithm
2
was implemented without Parallel Computing Toolbox. The code is named Fast Ordering.
The implementation of the original ordering
ICA (named Threads Ordering) in Algorithm
1

uses Parallel Computing Toolbox, which could utilize 40 threads in this environment.
We also used the implementation of the original ordering ICA by only a single thread (named Single Ordering) without Parallel Computing Toolbox.
Regarding the hyperparameters,

\epsilon
was fixed to
10^{-6}
.
The maximum number of iterations
K
was fixed to 30.
All the above three codes (with slight modifications) are given at
https://github.com/yoshitatsumatsuda/orderingICA/
.
In addition, we used the extended InfoMax of EEGLAB 2021.1 (named InfoMax)
at
https://github.com/sccn/eeglab
and
the Fast ICA using the kurtosis
(named Fast ICA) at
https://research.ics.aalto.fi/ica/fastica/
.
Note again that the algorithms other than Threads Ordering do not need Parallel Computing Toolbox and do not include explicit implementations utilizing multiple threads.

4.2
Experiments on Artificial Datasets

Here, the generalized Gaussian distributions are used as the non-Gaussian sources, whose
probability density function is given as

p(u)=\rho\exp\left(-\left(|u|/\beta\right)^{\rho}\right)/2\beta\Gamma\left(1/\rho\right)
.
\Gamma
is the
gamma function. The parameter
\beta
was set so that the variance is 1.
The parameter
\rho
determines the non-Gaussianity of each source.

\rho=2
corresponds to the Gaussian distribution.

\rho
was set to 20 different values

\rho=2*2^{i/4}
for
i=-10,-9,\cdots,-1
(10 sources with positive kurtoses) and
i=1,2,\cdots,10
(10 sources with negative kurtoses).

In the first experiment, we employed the blind source separation of the above 20 sources with different non-Gaussianity (namely,
N=20
). The sample size
M
was set to 10000.
As it is a simple separation problem, many ICA methods can separate every source.
However, only the ordering ICA can estimate the sources in the unique order of non-Gaussianity and does not suffer from the permutation ambiguity problem.
Therefore, we employed
E_{\rm{ordering}}=||\mbox{\boldmath$W$}\mbox{\boldmath$A$}-\mbox{\boldmath$I$}_{N}||_{0}/N^{2}
(named the ordering error), where
the sources are assumed to be sorted by the rank of non-Gaussianity.
This error is sensitive to the permutation. It is zero only if the order of estimated sources is the same as that of the original sources.
Fig.
1
shows the results of the ordering error and calculation time of the five methods (Fast Ordering, Threads Ordering, Single Ordering, InfoMax, and Fast ICA).
The number of multiple candidates
L
was set from 1 to 100.
All the values were averaged over 10 runs of random initializations.
Fig.
1
-(a) shows that the ordering error converges to a small value by all three ordering ICA methods (Fast Ordering, Threads Ordering, and Single Ordering) if
L
is more than 20.
On the other hand, InfoMax and Fast ICA could not find the order of sources appropriately. Fig.
1
-(b) shows that Fast Ordering was much faster than Threads Ordering and Single Ordering in this experiment. Though the calculation time is increased according to the number of multiple candidates in Fast Ordering and Single Ordering, the increase of Fast Ordering is much lower than Single Ordering. Even for a small number of candidates, Threads Ordering needed a large overhead time.

In the second experiment, we employed the estimation of the number of non-Gaussian sources by the Gaussianity test. 10 Gaussian noises were added to the above 20 non-Gaussian sources so that
N=30
.
The other settings are the same as the first experiment.
Only the three ordering ICA methods were used.
Fig.
2
-(a) shows that the true number of the non-Gaussian sources (10) could be estimated by all three ordering ICA methods if
L
is more than 20. Fig.
2
-(b) also shows that Fast Ordering was much faster than the other methods.

In summary, the results on the artificial datasets verified that
Fast Ordering can find almost the same solutions as those of the previous ordering ICA methods much more efficiently.

4.3
EEG Analysis

EEG analysis is one of the most important practical applications of ICA.
As the ordering ICA can estimate the source signals uniquely and reliably by avoiding local minima, it is promising in this field
[
8
]
.
Here, Fast Ordering is applied to EEG data in comparison with Threads Ordering, InfoMax, and Fast ICA.

We used the 5SUBJECT dataset at
https://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab/download/STUDY5subjects.zip
.
It is a widely-used benchmark of EEG data, which
consists of the
10 EEG signals syn{02,05,07,08,10}-s{253,254}.
They were observed in a semantic task with five subjects under two conditions.
The number of channels is fixed to 61, namely,
N=61
.
The sample size
M
of each signal is about 160,000-190,000.
Except for Single Ordering (which is too slow in this problem), the four methods (Fast Ordering, Threads Ordering, InfoMax, and Fast ICA) were applied directly to each EEG signal. As the Gaussianity test was not satisfied in almost all components (in other words, there was no Gaussian noise), it was omitted in this experiment. In addition, the extracted components in InfoMax and Fast ICA were also sorted by the rank of the non-Gaussianity
\Upsilon
.
The number of candidates
L
in Fast Ordering and Threads Ordering was set from 10 to 120 by increments of 10.

We calculated
the average fluctuations of the solutions over the
different runs to evaluate the reliability of the methods.
The fluctuations are measured
in the same way as in
[
8
]
, as explained below.
Each method
was applied to each EEG signal from different initializations over
T=10
runs.
Here,
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}
in the
t
-th run is denoted as
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t}
(
t=1,\ldots,10
).
The divergence
\delta\left(i,t,u\right)
between
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}
is defined as

\delta\left(i,t,u\right)=1-\left|S_{\cos}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t},\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}\right)\right|

where
\left|S_{\cos}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t},\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}\right)\right|
is
the absolute value of the cosine similarity of
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}
.

\delta\left(i,t,u\right)
is the minimum 0 if and only if
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}
have the same or opposite directions.
When
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{t}
is orthogonal to
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}^{u}
,

\delta\left(i,t,u\right)
is the maximum 1.
Then, the fluctuation of
\mbox{\boldmath$w$}_{i}
over all the runs is evaluated as the average of
\delta\left(i,t,u\right)
over all the
T(T-1)
pairs of
t
and
u
.

First, we examine the fluctuations for the four methods.
Fig.
3
shows the plot of the fluctuations of the 10 signals by InfoMax, Fast ICA, Fast Ordering (
L=40,120
), and Threads Ordering (
L=40,120
).
Fig.
4
shows the details of the relations between the number of candidates
L
and the average fluctuation.
The fluctuations are averaged over the four different groups of components: all, the top-ranked 20 ones with large non-Gaussianity, the medium-ranked 20 ones, and
the rest with small non-Gaussianity.
Figs.
3
and
4
show that the ordering methods with
L=40
can estimate about 20 unique components with high non-Gaussianity. On the other hand, Fast Ordering seems inferior to Thread Ordering in estimating the unique components with relatively low non-Gaussianity concerning the fluctuations.

Next, we examine the optimality of the solutions.
Fig.
5
shows the averages of the non-Gaussianity
\Upsilon
of the extracted components for Fast Ordering with
L=120
, Threads Ordering with
L=120
, InfoMax, and Fast ICA.
The components are sorted by the non-Gaussianity in each signal.
Fig.
5
shows that Fast Ordering could find the same or more optimal solutions than Threads Ordering. Because InfoMax often finds relatively worse solutions for the components with high non-Gaussianity, it is natural that the solution of InfoMax is often better than the ordering ICA methods for the components with relatively low non-Gaussianity.
On the other hand, the solution of Fast Ordering is always the same or better than Threads Ordering. This is probably because the search space for Fast Ordering is appropriately reduced, as explained in Section
3.3
.
The improved maximization of the non-Gaussianity and the increase of the fluctuations in Fast Ordering suggest that a stable global optimum does not exist if the non-Gaussianity of a component is quite low. This property may be useful for extracting only the essential components from EEG signals without the Gaussianity test.

Finally, we examine the calculation time.
Fig.
6
shows the relation between the calculation time of the two ordering ICA methods and the number of candidates
L
in comparison with Fast ICA and InfoMax.
Fig.
6
shows that Fast Ordering was faster than InfoMax and Threads Ordering if
L
is less than 120.
Fast Ordering was about three times as fast as InfoMax and Threads Ordering for
L=40
.
In other words, Fast Ordering is much superior to the other methods in extracting the top-ranked 20 non-Gaussian components from EEG signals.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a novel algorithm implementing the ordering ICA by matrix representation. Experimental results verified that the proposed algorithm is much faster than the previous ones. Moreover, the proposed algorithm could find more optimal solutions in EEG analysis even if the non-Gaussianity of the component is quite low.
This phenomenon is expected to lead to the extraction of only the essential components from EEG data. We are planning to investigate this phenomenon further. We are also planning to utilize the ordering ICA in many practical applications.
