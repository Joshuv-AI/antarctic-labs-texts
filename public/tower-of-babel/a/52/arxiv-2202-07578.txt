A law of large numbers for local patterns in Schur measures and a Schur process
Pierre Lazag

Abstract
. The aim of this note is to prove a law of large numbers for local patterns in discrete point processes. We investigate two different situations: a class of point processes on the one dimensional lattice including certain Schur measures, and a model of random plane partitions, introduced by Okounkov and Reshetikhin. The results state in both cases that the linear statistic of a function, weighted by the appearance of a fixed pattern in the random configuration and conveniently normalized, converges to the deterministic integral of that function weighted by the expectation with respect to the limit process of the appearance of the pattern.



1.
Introduction

Our main results, Theorems
1.3
and
1.8
below, are weak laws of large numbers for two different but related examples of discrete determinantal point processes: the Schur measures and a model of random plane partitions. The latter is an example of a Schur process, which are two-dimensional and dynamical generalizations of Schur measures. We start by describing a common and general framework encompassing both situations, before moving into details in Sections
1.2
and
1.3
.

1.1.
A general framework

1.1.1.
Description of the models

We let
E
be the state space. In our examples, we have either
E=\mathbb{Z}
or
E=\mathbb{Z}\times\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
, and we simply assume here that
E
is a discrete (i.e. without accumulation point) countable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a given integer
d\geq 1
. The probability space is the space of configurations
\mathrm{Conf}(E):=\{0,1\}^{E}
, equipped with the usual Borel sigma-algebra generated by the cylinders. We identify the set
\mathrm{Conf}(E)
with the set of all subsets of
E
. A point process on
E
is a probability measure on
\mathrm{Conf}(E)
. For a finite subset
m\subset E
, called a
pattern
, we define the random variable

\displaystyle c_{m}:\mathrm{Conf}(E)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto\left\{\begin{matrix}1&\text{if $m\subset X$,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{matrix}\right.

Observe that by the inclusion/exclusion principle, the law of the random variables
c_{m}
,
m\subset E
is a pattern, characterizes a given point process. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a pattern
m=\{m_{1},\dots,m_{l}\}
, we write
m+x:=\{\lfloor m_{1}+x\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor m_{l}+x\rfloor\}
, where
\lfloor y\rfloor
is one of the closest point to
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
E
. We consider a one parameter family of point processes
(\mathbb{P}_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
which admit a local limit behavior: there exists a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a family of point processes
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}(u)})_{u\in A}
such that the following holds

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[c_{m+\alpha u}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}],

for all
u\in A
and all finite
m\subset E
, where
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
(resp.
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
) denotes the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
). The convergence (
1
) can be interpreted as follows: if, for large
\alpha>0
, we zoom locally around the position
\alpha u
in a configuration distributed according to
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
, the configuration we see behaves as it were distributed according to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
.

1.1.2.
The result

For a compactly supported continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a finite subset
m\subset E
, we form the sum

(2)

\displaystyle\Sigma(f,m,\alpha):=\frac{1}{\alpha^{d}}\sum_{x\in\alpha A\cap E}f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}x\right)c_{m+x}.

Consider the deterministic integral

(3)

\displaystyle I(f,m):=\int_{A}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]du.

A general Theorem in this context is the following.

Theorem
.

Assume that for all compact
\mathcal{K}\subset A
, we have

(4)

\displaystyle\alpha^{d}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[c_{m+\alpha u}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]\right|\rightarrow 0

as
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
and that

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}_{\alpha}\left(\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)\right):=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}(\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)-\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha))^{2}\rightarrow 0

as
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
. Then for all
\epsilon>0
, we have

(6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\left(\left|\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)-I(f,m)\right|>\epsilon\right)=0.

1.1.3.
Scketch of the proof and some comments

The proof is as follows. From (
1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{d}}\sum_{x\in\alpha A\cap E}f\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}x\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(x/\alpha)}[c_{m}]+o(1)

where the
o(1)
term arises from condition (
4
), since the function
f
has compact support. One recognizes on the right hand side a Riemann sum for the integral
I(f,m)
. By condition (
5
), Chebyshev inequality implies the weak law of large numbers (
6
).

We will prove in great details the same weak law of large numbers for a class Schur measures, Theorem
1.3
below, and for a model of random plane partitions, Theorem
1.8
below. The main points will be to prove that conditions (
4
) and (
5
) are satisfied. Our proofs will lie on the fact that both point processes are determinantal point processes, with kernels described by double contour integrals that will reveal to be suitable for asymptotic analysis. We refer to Section
2
for the definition of determinantal point processes.

Both models we consider are models of random partitions. Other general laws of large numbers for discrete models related to random partitions have been established for example in
[
9
]
, but in the case when the pattern
m
is empty. The latter may thus be seen as global laws of large numbers, while Theorems
1.3
and
1.8
below involve a mixture of global and local asymptotic behaviors.

[

]

]

1.2.1.
The Schur measures on the set of partitions

Let
\Lambda
be the algebra over
\mathbb{C}
of symmetric functions, i.e. of symmetric polynomials with an infinite number of variables, see
[
16
]
. A partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots)
is an almost-zero sequence of non-negative integers, and is identified with a Young diagram. The set of all partitions is denoted by
\mathbb{Y}
. A distinguished basis in
\Lambda
is formed by the Schur functions
s_{\lambda}
, indexed by partitions
\lambda\in\Lambda
. A specialization is an algebra morphism from
\Lambda
to
\mathbb{C}
. A specialization
\rho
is said to be
Schur positive
if
\rho(s_{\lambda})\geq 0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}
. The Schur measures are defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\rho
,
\rho^{\prime}
be Schur positive specializations. The Schur measure
\mathbb{S}_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{Y}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}(\lambda)=C_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}\rho(s_{\lambda})\rho^{\prime}(s_{\lambda}),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}.

The normalizing constant
C_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}
is given by the Cauchy formula (see
[
16
]
)

C_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}^{-1}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}}\rho(s_{\lambda})\rho^{\prime}(s_{\lambda})=\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}\left(\prod_{i,j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{x}_{i}\mathrm{y}_{j}}\right)\\
=\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{p_{k}(\mathrm{x}_{1},\mathrm{x_{2}},\dots)p_{k}(\mathrm{y}_{1},\mathrm{y_{2}},\dots)}{k}\right)\right),

where th
p_{k}
are the Newton power sums (see
[
16
]
).

We assumed that the specializations
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are Schur positive in order to guarantee that the Schur measure
\mathbb{S}_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}
is a positive measure. Observe now that this assumption might be avoided if one takes two complex conjugated specializations, which leads to the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\rho
be a specialization. The
symmetric Schur measure

\mathbb{S}_{\rho}
associated to
\rho
is the probability measure on
\mathbb{Y}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\rho}(\lambda)=C_{\rho}|\rho(s_{\lambda})|^{2},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{Y}.

Again, the normalizing constant is given by the Cauchy formula

\displaystyle C_{\rho}^{-1}=\rho\otimes\overline{\rho}\left(\prod_{i,j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{x}_{i}\mathrm{y}_{j}}\right).

To a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots)\in\mathbb{Y}
, we associate a configuration
\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)\in\mathrm{Conf}(\mathbb{Z})
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)=\{\lambda_{i}-i,\hskip 2.84544pti=1,2,\dots\}.

See Figure 1 below for a picture of the map
\mathfrak{S}
.

Figure 1.
The Young diagramm
(4,2,2,1)

and its associated configuration.

Okounkov proved in
[
20
]
that the image of the Schur measures by the map
\mathfrak{S}
form determinantal point processes on
\mathbb{Z}
, see also
[
3
]
and
[
5
]
for other proofs. We recall this fact in the following Theorem, for the case of symmetric Schur measures.

Theorem 1.1

(Okounkov,
[
20
]
)
.

The image of the symmetric Schur measure
\mathbb{S}_{\rho}
by
\mathfrak{S}
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}
, is a determinantal point process on
\mathbb{Z}
. If the series

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}\rho(p_{k})z^{k}

defines a holomorphic function in a neighborhood of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C},\hskip 2.84544pt|z|=1\}
, then
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}
admits the correlation kernel

(7)

K_{\rho}(x,y)=\\
=\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1+\varepsilon}\int_{|w|=1-\varepsilon}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\rho(p_{k})}{k}(z^{k}-w^{k})+\frac{\overline{\rho(p_{k})}}{k}(w^{-k}-z^{-k})\right)\frac{1}{z-w}\frac{dzdw}{z^{x}w^{-y+1}},\\
x,y\in\mathbb{Z},

where
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small.

1.2.2.
The limit processes

Our aim here is to state a local limit behavior for a certain class of Schur measures we now define. Let
\rho
be a specialization such that the series

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\rho(p_{k})z^{k}}{k}

defines a holomorphic function in a neighborhood of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Let
G(z)
be the function defined by

(8)

\displaystyle G(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\rho(p_{k})z^{k}}{k}-\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\overline{\rho(p_{k})}z^{-k}}{k}.

The function
G
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}
and we have
G(z)\in i\mathbb{R}
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
. We let
\alpha>0
be a positive parameter, and define the specialization
\rho_{\alpha}:=\alpha\rho
. We denote by
\mathbb{S}_{\alpha}
the symmetric Schur measure with specialization
\rho_{\alpha}
and let
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
be its image on
\mathrm{Conf}(\mathbb{Z})
by the map
\mathfrak{S}
.

We can now state a local limit behavior for the point process
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
, as
\alpha\to+\infty
, Proposition
1.2
below, analogous to the convergence (
1
) and (
4
).

Observe that
zG^{\prime}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
when
z\in\mathbb{T}
. Let
u_{\min}
(resp.
u_{\max}
) be the minimum (resp. maximum) of the function
zG^{\prime}(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{T}
. For
u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
, the set

\displaystyle\{z\in\mathbb{T},\hskip 2.84544ptzG^{\prime}(z)\geq u\}

consists of a finite number
L=L(u)
of arcs
[e^{i\phi_{k}(u)},e^{i\psi_{k}(u)}]\subset\mathbb{T}
,
k=1,\dots,L(u)
. For
u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
, we define the kernel

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L(u)}\frac{e^{i\phi_{k}(u)(y-x)}-e^{i\psi_{k}(u)(y-x)}}{2i\pi(x-y)},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{Z},\hskip 2.84544ptx\neq y

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)(x,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L(u)}\frac{\phi_{k}(u)-\psi_{k}(u)}{2\pi},\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}

and let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
be the determinantal point process with correlation kernel
\mathcal{S}(u)
. The local limit behavior (
1
) together with the estimate (
4
) for the Schur measures
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
are provided by the following Proposition. We denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
stands for the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}
.

Proposition 1.2
.

For all
\delta\in(0,1)
and all pattern
m\subset\mathbb{Z}
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
,

(11)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[c_{m+\alpha u}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]\right|\leq C\exp\left(-\alpha^{\delta}\right)

for all sufficiently large
\alpha>0
.

Observe that if we have
L(u)=1
, the kernel
\mathcal{S}(u)
defined in Equation (
9
) above reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)(x,y)=\frac{e^{i\phi(u)(y-x)}-e^{i\psi(u)(y-x)}}{2i\pi(x-y)}=e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\phi(u)+\psi(u))(y-x)}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\psi(u)-\phi(u)}{2}(x-y)\right)}{\pi(x-y)}.

Since the factor
e^{i\frac{1}{2}(\phi(u)+\psi(u))(y-x)}
can be ignored (see Remark
2.1
), the point process
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}
converges then to a version of the discrete sine process (see e.g.
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
).

The class of Schur measures
\mathbb{S}_{\alpha}
we considere here above are also discussed by Okounkov in the notes
[
21
]
. The limit Theorem given by Proposition
1.2
is also stated in the same paper
[
21
]
without the error term, and a proof is sketched. We give a complete detailed proof of Proposition
1.2
in Section
1.2.2
below.

Taking
G(z)=z-z^{-1}
in equation (
8
) above, one obtains the discrete Bessel point process, the corresponding Schur measure being the poissonized Plancherel measure (see
[
4
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
12
]
or
[
3
]
). In particular, Proposition
1.2
is in that case the convergence of the discrete Bessel point process to the discrete sine process, first established in
[
4
]
.

In Section
3.4
, we give an interpretation of the symmetric Schur measures and of the limit kernels
\mathcal{S}(u)
in terms of shift invariant subspaces of
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Namely, we prove that, up to minor transformations, the correlation kernels of symmetric Schur measures are projection kernels onto simply shift invariant subspaces, while the kernels
S(u)
are projection kernels onto doubly shift invariant subspaces. Such an interpretation is also given in
[
8
]
. Proposition
1.2
provides a connection between both kinds of invariant subspaces.

1.2.3.
The law of large numbers

As before, we set

\displaystyle\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{x\in[\alpha u_{\min},\alpha u_{\max}]\cap\mathbb{Z}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)c_{m+x}

where
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and
m\subset\mathbb{Z}
is a pattern. We also define the integral

\displaystyle I(f,m)=\int_{u_{\min}}^{u_{\max}}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]du.

The law of large numbers for Schur measures is the following Theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and any pattern
m\subset\mathbb{Z}
, we have for all
\epsilon>0
that

(12)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\left(\left|\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)-I(f,m)\right|>\epsilon\right)=0.

Theorem
1.3
was first established for the discrete Bessel point process in
[
7
, Lem. 4.4]
, by means of simple estimations of the Bessel functions. The goal of the author in
[
7
]
was to prove the Vershik-Kerov entropy conjecture for the Plancherel measure, and it would be intersting to see if our result could serve to define the entropy of more general Schur measures.

1.3.
The law of large numbers for plane partitions

We now prepare the statement of the law of large numbers for plane partitions, Theorem
1.8
below. We present the model in Section
1.3.1
, and give Okounkov-Reshtikhin determinantal formula in Section
1.3.2
. The limit processes are presented in Section
1.3.3
, and the law of large numbers is stated in Section
1.3.4

1.3.1.
Introduction of the model

A plane partition is a double non-increasing sequence of non-negative integers whith a finite number of non-zero elements. More precisely,
\pi=(\pi_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
is a plane partition if and only if

\displaystyle\pi_{i+1,j}

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{i,j}\text{ and }\pi_{i,j+1}\leq\pi_{i,j}\text{ for all }i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},

\displaystyle|\pi|

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\pi_{i,j}<+\infty.

For
q\in(0,1)
, we consider the geometric probability measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{q}
on the set of all plane partition given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{q}(\pi)=Mq^{|\pi|},

where
M
is the normalization constant given by MacMahon formula (
[
26
]
, corollary 7.20.3)

\displaystyle M=\prod_{n=1}^{+\infty}(1-q^{n})^{n}.

To a plane partition we associate a subset of
E:=\mathbb{Z}\times\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
via the map

\displaystyle\pi\mapsto\mathfrak{S}_{2}(\pi):=\{(i-j,\pi_{i,j}-(i+j-1)/2),\hskip 2.84544pti,j\geq 1\},

see Figure 2.
The first coordinate of a point
(t,h)\in E
might be interpreted as the time coordinate, and the second as the space coordinate.

The point process we consider, denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{q}
, is the image of
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{q}
under
\mathfrak{S}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{q}(\mathcal{B})=\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{q}(\mathfrak{S}_{2}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})),\quad\mathcal{B}\subset\{0,1\}^{E}\text{ is a Borel set}.

Figure 2.
The plane partition
\begin{pmatrix}4&3&2&1&1\\
3&2&2&&\\
3&1&1&&\\
2&1&&&\end{pmatrix}

and its associated configuration.

1.3.2.
The determinantal formula

Okounkov and Reshetikhin have shown in
[
22
]
that
\mathbb{P}_{q}
is a determinantal point procces on
E
. We define the kernel
K_{q}:E\times E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(13)

\displaystyle K_{q}(t_{1},h_{1};t_{2},h_{2})=\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1\pm\epsilon}\int_{|w|=1\mp\epsilon}\frac{1}{z-w}\frac{\Phi(t_{1},z)}{\Phi(t_{2},w)}\frac{dzdw}{z^{h_{1}+\frac{|t_{1}|+1}{2}}w^{-h_{2}-\frac{|t_{2}|+1}{2}}}

where one picks the plus sign for
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
and the minus sign otherwise. The function
\Phi
is defined by

\displaystyle\Phi(t,z)=

\displaystyle\frac{(q^{1/2}/z;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{1/2+t}z;q)_{\infty}}\quad\text{for $t\geq 0$}

\displaystyle\frac{(q^{1/2-t}/z;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{1/2}z;q)_{\infty}}\quad\text{for $t<0$},

where
(x;q)_{\infty}
is a
q
version of the Pochhammer symbol

\displaystyle(x;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-xq^{k}),

and
\varepsilon
is a sufficiently small positive number, which allows to avoid the singularities of the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(t_{1},z)}{\Phi(t_{2},w)}.

Observe that there is no need of defining the square root in formula (
13
), since, for
(t,h)\in E
, if there exists a plane partition
\pi
such that
(t,h)\in\mathfrak{S}_{2}(\pi)
, then we have by construction that

\displaystyle h+\frac{|t|+1}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Okounkov-Reshetikhin determinantal formula is then the following statement

Theorem 1.4

(Okounkov-Reshetikhin,
[
22
]
, 2003)
.

The point process
\mathbb{P}_{q}
is a determinantal point process on
E
with correlation kernel
K_{q}
.

The measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{q}
is a particular case of a Schur process. Schur processes, first introduced in
[
22
]
, are dynamical generalizations of Schur measures in the sense that they form a Markov process on the set of partitions such that their marginal distributions are Schur measures. For a more elementary treatment of Schur processes, see e.g.
[
5
]
,
[
3
]
and references therein.

1.3.3.
The limit processes

In the same article, Okounkov and Reshetikhin proved a scaling limit theorem for
\mathbb{P}_{q}
, when
r=-\log(q)
tends to
0^{+}
, which we now formulate. Let us define

\displaystyle A:=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},|2\cosh(t/2)-e^{-x}|<2\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

and for
(\tau,\chi)\in A
, let
z(\tau,\chi)
be the intersection point of the circles
C(0,e^{-\tau/2})
and
C(1,e^{-\tau/4-\chi/2})
with positive imaginary part, see Figure 3.

Figure 3.
The circles
C(0,e^{-\tau/2})
and
C(1,e^{-\tau/4-\chi/2})
and their intersection points
z=z(\tau,\chi)
and its complex conjugate
z^{\prime}=\overline{z(\tau,\chi)}
.

The condition
(\tau,\chi)\in A
guarantees that
z(\tau,\chi)
exists and is not real. For
(\tau,\chi)\in A
, we define the translation invariant kernel
\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}(\Delta t,\Delta h)=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\overline{z(\tau,\chi)}}^{z(\tau,\chi)}(1-w)^{\Delta t}w^{-\Delta h-\frac{\Delta t}{2}}\frac{dw}{w},

where the integration path crosses
(0,1)
for
\Delta t\geq 0
and
(-\infty,0)
for
\Delta t<0
. For reasons explained below, this kernel will be called the
extended sine kernel
. Then, the following holds

Theorem 1.5

(Okounkov-Reshetikhin,
[
22
]
)
.

For all
(\tau,\chi)\in A
and all pattern
m=\{(t_{1},h_{1}),...,(t_{l},h_{l})\}\subset E
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{e^{-r}}\left[c_{\frac{1}{r}(\tau,\chi)+m}\right]=\det\left(\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}(t_{i}-t_{j},h_{i}-h_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{l}.

For
(\tau,\chi)\in A
, the kernel
\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}
defines a determinantal point process
\mathbb{P}_{(\tau,\chi)}
on
E
by

(15)

\displaystyle\forall m=\{(m^{1}_{1},m^{2}_{1}),...,(m^{1}_{l},m^{2}_{l})\}\subset E,\quad\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\chi)}[c_{m}]=\det\left(\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}(m_{i}^{1}-m_{j}^{1},m_{i}^{2}-m_{j}^{2})\right)_{i,j=1}^{l},

where
\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\chi)}
is the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{(\tau,\chi)}
. This point process can be seen as a two-dimensional or dynamical version of the usual discrete sine-process on
\mathbb{Z}
(see e.g.
[
4
]
or
[
3
]
). Indeed, setting
\Delta t=0
in (
14

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{z(\tau,\chi)}(0,\Delta h)=e^{\frac{\tau\Delta h}{2}}\frac{\sin(\phi\Delta h)}{\pi\Delta h}

where
z(\tau,\chi)=e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}+i\phi}
. Note that we can ignore the factor
e^{\frac{\tau\Delta h}{2}}

1.3.4.
The law of large numbers

For
r>0

by

\displaystyle A_{r}=r^{-1}A\cap E.

For brievety, we write

(resp.

(resp.
\mathbb{E}_{e^{-r}}
). For a continuous compactly supported function

\displaystyle\Sigma(f,m,r)=r^{2}\sum_{(t,h)\in A_{r}}f(rt,rh)c_{(t,h)+m},

Our Theorem establishes that, under

For every continuous compactly supported function

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0,\quad\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{P}_{r}\left(|\Sigma(f,m,r)-I(f,m)|>\epsilon\right)=0.

Remark 1.9

2.
Determinantal point processes

2.1.
Definitions

We recall the following

Definition 2.1
.

A point process
\mathbb{P}
on
E
is a
determinantal point process
if there exists a kernel

K:E\times E\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

called a correlation kernel for
\mathbb{P}
, such that, for any finite
m=\{m_{1},\dots,m_{l}\}\subset E
, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(m\subset X)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m}]=\det\left(K(m_{i},m_{j})\right)_{i,j=1}^{l}.

Remark 2.1
.

A correlation kernel of a given determinantal point process is not unique. Indeed, if
\mathbb{P}
is a determinantal point process with correlation kernel
K
, then for any non-vanishing function
f:E\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, the kernel

\tilde{K}(x,y)=\frac{f(x)}{f(y)}K(x,y)

also serves as a correlation kernel for
\mathbb{P}
, since the factors involving the function
f
will disappear from any determinant of the form (
19
).

A correlation kernel
K
defines to an integral operator on
l^{2}(E)
, which we denote by the same letter
K

\displaystyle K:l^{2}(E)

\displaystyle\rightarrow l^{2}(E)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(x\mapsto\sum_{y\in E}K(x,y)f(y)\right).

Conversally, by the Macchi-Soshnikov/Shirai-Takahashi Theorem (
[
15
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
24
]
), any locally trace class hermitian integral operator
K
gives rise to a determinantal point process. In particular, any orthogonal projection onto a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}
of
l^{2}(E)
gives rise to a determinantal point process.

2.2.
Covariances for discrete determinantal point processes

We here provide a method for the estimation of covariances for discrete determinantal point processes. In particular, this method will be useful for determinantal point processes with a non-symmetric correlation kernel.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a determinantal point process on
E
with correlation kernel
K
. Let
m=\{m_{1},\dots,m_{l}\}
,
m^{\prime}=\{m_{1}^{\prime},\dots,m_{l^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}\subset E
be two disjoint patterns. Then, the covariance

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(c_{m},c_{m}^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m}c_{m^{\prime}}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m^{\prime}}]

is a sum of
(l+l^{\prime})!-l!l^{\prime}!
terms, each of one containing a factor of the form

\displaystyle K(m_{i},m_{j}^{\prime})K(m_{i}^{\prime},m_{j}).

Proof.

Since the patterns
m
and
m^{\prime}
are disjoint, we have

c_{m}c_{m^{\prime}}=c_{m\sqcup m^{\prime}}.

By definition of a determinantal point process, the expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m}c_{m^{\prime}}]

is an alternate sum of
(l+l^{\prime})!
indexed by the permutations of the set
m\sqcup m^{\prime}
. Among these permutations, let us consider those that leave the sets
m
and
m^{\prime}
invariant, i.e. permutations
\sigma
which can be be factorized as
\sigma=\tau\tau^{\prime}
where
\tau
(resp.
\tau^{\prime}
) is a permutation of
m
(resp.
m^{\prime}
). The alternate sum over all the permutations leaving the sets
m
and
m^{\prime}
invariant is nothing but the product of the determinants

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[c_{m^{\prime}}].

Thus, the remaining terms in the covariance (
20
) are all indexed by permutations
\sigma
leaving neither
m
nor
m^{\prime}
invariant (since
\sigma
is a permutation, if
\sigma
left
m
invariant, it would also leave
m^{\prime}
invariant), i.e. there exist
m_{i},m_{j}\in\{m_{1},\dots,m_{l}\}
and
m_{i}^{\prime},m_{j}^{\prime}\in\{m_{1}^{\prime},\dots,m_{l^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
such that

\displaystyle\sigma(m_{i})=m_{j}^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma(m_{i}^{\prime})=m_{j}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Control of the covariances

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

.

For any patterns

, there exists

such that for any

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

For brevety, we write

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}:=[\alpha u_{\min},\alpha u_{\max}]\cap\mathbb{Z}.

We start by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{x\in A_{\alpha}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[c_{m+x}]=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]\cap\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[c_{m+\alpha u}].

By Proposition
1.2
, and since

\displaystyle\left|A_{\alpha}\right|=O\left(\alpha\right),

we have that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
C>0
such that

(23)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]\cap\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]\right|\leq C\alpha\exp\left(-\alpha^{-\delta}\right).

The sum

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]\cap\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{x\in A_{\alpha}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(x/\alpha)}[c_{m}]

is a Riemann sum for the integral

\displaystyle I(f,m)=\int_{u_{\min}}^{u_{\max}}f(u)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{S}(u)}[c_{m}]du,

whence we deduce from (
23
) that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=I(f,m).

It suffices now to prove that the variance

(24)

\mathrm{var}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\left|\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)-\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)\right|^{2}\\
=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in A_{\alpha}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)f\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})

goes to
0
as
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
. We cut the set
A_{\alpha}^{2}

into two parts by setting

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}^{>}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{2},\hskip 2.84544pt|x-y|>\overline{m}\},

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}^{\leq}

\displaystyle=A_{\alpha}^{2}\setminus A_{\alpha}^{>},

and decompose the variance (
24
) into two sums

(25)

\mathrm{var}_{\alpha}\Sigma(f,m,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{>}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)f\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})\right.\\
+\left.\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{\leq}}f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)f\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})\right).

By construction, for all
(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{>}
, there exists
u_{1},u_{2}\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]\cap\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

|u_{1}-u_{2}|>\frac{\overline{m}}{\alpha}

and such that

\displaystyle x=\alpha u_{1},\quad y=\alpha u_{2}.

By lemma
3.1
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{>}
, we have

(26)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})|\leq\frac{C}{|x-y|}.

Since

|A_{\alpha}^{>}|=O\left(\alpha^{2}\right),

and since the function
f
is bounded on
[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
, we deduce from (
26
) that

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{>}}\left|f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)f\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})\right|\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{>}}\frac{1}{|x-y|}=O\left(\alpha\log(\alpha)\right).

Now, for any
(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{\leq}
, there exist
u\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
and patterns

such that

\displaystyle x+m=\alpha u+m^{\prime},\quad y+m=\alpha u+m^{\prime\prime}.

Observe that there is only a finite number of possibilities for the patterns

as

ranges over

. Thus by Lemma
3.2
, there exists
C>0
such that for all

(28)

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x,m+y})\right|\leq C.

We obtain from (
28

such that

(29)

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in A_{\alpha}^{\leq}}\left|f\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)f\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\mathrm{cov}_{\alpha}(c_{m+x},c_{m+y})\right|\leq C^{\prime}\alpha.

Inserting inequalities (

3.4.
A remark on shift invariant subspaces

3.4.1.
Wiener-Helson’s Theorem on the classification of shift invariant spaces

We here give a classification of shift invariant subspaces of
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
due to Wiener and Helson, Theorem
3.3
below. The material we present is quite standard, and may be found for instance in the first pages of the book
[
19
]
.

Let
S:l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the shift

\displaystyle S(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}=(a_{k-1})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

We say that a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}\subset l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is shift invariant if
S(\mathcal{E})\subset\mathcal{E}
. If
\mathcal{E}
is shift invariant, it is said to be
simply
invariant if
S(\mathcal{E})\neq\mathcal{E}
and doubly invariant otherwise.

We equip the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
with the normalized Lebesgue measure and recall the Fourier-Plancherel unitary isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T})

\displaystyle\to l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mapsto(\hat{F}(k))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},

where

\displaystyle\hat{F}(k):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-i\theta k}F(e^{i\theta})d\theta=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{|z|=1}z^{-k}F(z)\frac{dz}{z}.

The map
S
is conjugated to the multiplication by
z
by the Fourier-Plancherel isomorphism.

The Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is defined as the closed linear space of functions of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
with vanishing negative Fourier coefficients:
H^{2}(\mathbb{T}):=\{F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}),\hskip 2.84544pt\hat{F}(k)=0,\hskip 2.84544ptk=-1,-2,\dots\}
. Before stating the Wiener-Helson’s Theorem classifying the subspaces of
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
which are invariant by the shift
S
(or, equivalently, the subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
which are invariant by the multiplication by
z
), we make the following elementary observation: if a function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is such that
|F(z)|=1
for almost all
z\in\mathbb{T}
, then the functions
z\mapsto F(z)z^{n}
,
n=0,1,\dots
form an orthonormal basis of
FH^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, and the space
FH^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is invariant by the multiplication by
z
. Let us write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{F}:=\mathcal{F}(FH^{2}(\mathbb{T})).

The space
\mathcal{E}_{F}
is then a simply shift invariant subspace of
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Helson’s Theorem, which is the second point of the Theorem
3.3
below, establishes the reversed statement. The first point is due to Wiener, and classifies doubly shift invariant subspaces.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be a shift invariant subspace. Then we have the following

\bullet

If the subspace
\mathcal{E}
is doubly invariant, then there exists a borel set
A\subset\mathbb{T}
such that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}\left(\mathfrak{1}_{A}L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\right)
, where
\mathfrak{1}_{A}
is the indicator function of
A
.

\bullet

If the subspace
\mathcal{E}
is simply invaraint, then there exists a function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
verifying
|F(z)|=1
for almost every
z\in\mathbb{T}
and such that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}\left(FH^{2}(\mathbb{T})\right)
.

3.4.2.
The Schur measures revisited

We now connect the preceding discussion with Schur measures and their limit processes. Let
G
be a function holomorphic in a neighborhood of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
such that
G(z)\in i\mathbb{R}
for
z\in\mathbb{T}
, i.e. such that
\hat{G}(-k)=-\overline{\hat{G}(k)}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. We set

\displaystyle F(z)=\exp\left(G(z^{-1})\right).

We have
|F(z)|=1
for almost every

.

Let
K_{F}
be the kernel of the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{E}_{F}
. By the Macchi-Soshnikov/Shirai-Takahashi Theorem, the kernel

serves as a correlation kernel of a determinantal point process. The following Proposition says that the determinantal point process with kernel

is a symmetric Schur measure, up to the transformation
X\mapsto-X
,

.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\rho:\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
be the specialization defined by

\displaystyle\rho(p_{k})=k\hat{G}(k),\quad k=1,2,\dots

and let
\mathbb{P}_{\rho}
be the symmetric Schur measure with specialization
\rho
. Let
K_{\rho}
be the correlation kernel of the corresponding determinantal point process
\mathfrak{S}^{*}\mathbb{P}_{\rho}
on
\mathbb{Z}
. Then, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

(33)

\displaystyle K_{F}(x,y)=K_{\rho}(-x,-y).

Proof.

Since the family
\{(\hat{F}(k-n))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}},\hskip 2.84544ptn=0,1,\dots\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{E}_{F}
, we have

\displaystyle K_{F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\hat{F}(x-n)\overline{\hat{F}(y-n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-i\theta(x-n)}F(e^{i\theta})d\theta\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i\theta^{\prime}(y-n)}F^{-1}(e^{i\theta^{\prime}})d\theta^{\prime},

where we used the fact that
|F(z)|=1
for
z\in\mathbb{T}
. Changing the variable
\theta\mapsto-\theta
,
\theta^{\prime}\mapsto-\theta^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i\theta(x-n)}F(e^{-i\theta})d\theta\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i\theta^{\prime}(n-y)}F^{-1}(e^{-i\theta^{\prime}})d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1}z^{x-n}F(z^{-1})\frac{dz}{z}\int_{|w|=1}w^{n-y}F^{-1}(w^{-1})\frac{dw}{w}.

Since
F
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}
, we have for
\varepsilon>0
small enough that

\displaystyle K_{F}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1+\varepsilon}z^{x-n}F(z^{-1})\frac{dz}{z}\int_{|w|=1-\varepsilon}w^{n-y}F^{-1}(w^{-1})\frac{dw}{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1+\varepsilon}\int_{|w|=1-\varepsilon}\frac{F(z^{-1})F^{-1}(w^{-1})}{1-w/z}\frac{dzdw}{z^{-x+1}w^{y+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2i\pi)^{2}}\int_{|z|=1+\varepsilon}\int_{|w|=1-\varepsilon}\frac{F(z^{-1})F^{-1}(w^{-1})}{z-w}\frac{dzdw}{z^{-x}w^{y+1}},

where we used Fubini’s Theorem for the second line. Observing that from the definitions of the function
F
and of the specialization
\rho
we have

\displaystyle F(z^{-1})F^{-1}(w^{-1})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(G(z)-G(w))\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\hat{G}(k)(z^{k}-w^{k})+\overline{\hat{G}(k)}(w^{-k}-z^{-k})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\rho(p_{k})}{k}(z^{k}-w^{k})+\frac{\overline{\rho(p_{k})}}{k}(w^{-k}-z^{-k})\right),

and recalling Formula (
7
) for the kernel
K_{\rho}
, the proof is complete.
∎

3.4.3.
An interpretation of Proposition
1.2

Observe that the limit kernels
\mathcal{S}(u)
are projection kernels onto doubly shift invariant subspaces

since we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(u)(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{L(u)}\hat{\mathfrak{1}}_{[e^{i\phi_{k}(u)},e^{i\psi_{k}(u)}]}(x-y).

Proposition
1.2
, and more precisely the convergence (
30
) establishes thus a link between simply and doubly shift invariant subspaces of

. In a certain regime and in a certain sense, some simply invariant subspaces converge to doubly invariant subspaces. We make this fact precise: if
G
is a holomorphic function in the neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}
as above, we can define a function

(x,y)\mapsto K_{F_{\alpha}}(\lfloor\alpha u\rfloor+x,\lfloor\alpha u\rfloor+y)

is the kernel of the orthogonal projection onto the subspace

. By Proposition
3.4
and slighly adapting the proof of the convergence (
30
), we have that, as

converges to
\mathcal{E}(u)
in the sense that the orthogonal projection onto
S^{-\lfloor\alpha u\rfloor}(\mathcal{E}_{F_{\alpha}})
converges to the orthogonal projection onto

in the strong operator topology.

\displaystyle\frac{(q^{1/2+\tau/r+t_{2}}w;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{1/2+\tau/r+t_{1}};q)_{\infty}}=(1+O(r))\left(1-e^{-\tau}w\right)^{t_{1}-t_{2}},

where the

\mathcal{K}
,
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Performing the change of variable

in the second integral of (

\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{e^{\tau}\overline{z(\tau,\chi)}}^{e^{\tau}z(\tau,\chi)}\frac{(q^{1/2+\tau/r+t_{2}}w;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{1/2+\tau/r+t_{1}};q)_{\infty}}\frac{dw}{w^{h_{1}-h_{2}+(t_{1}-t_{2})/2}}\\

The factor

4.4.1.
A remark and a proposition

For
\tau<0
, one has to replace the function
S
by

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(z,\tau,\chi)=-(|\tau|/2+\chi)\log(z)-\text{dilog}(1-z)+\text{dilog}(1-e^{-|\tau|}/z).

The function
\tilde{S}
innerhits the same properties than the function
S
: it is constant on the circle
\gamma_{|\tau|}
and has two complex conjugated critical points on this circle provided
(\tau,\chi)\in A
. This is why we will only consider positve values of
\tau
in the sequel.

The critical points of
S
are the roots of the quadratic polynomial

\displaystyle(1-1/z)(1-e^{-\tau}z)=e^{-\tau/2-\chi}.

For this reason, we have the following proposition :

Proposition 4.3
.

For any fixed
(\Delta t,\Delta h)\in E
, the function :

\displaystyle(\tau,\chi)\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{\tau,\chi}(\Delta t,\Delta h)

is continuous.
