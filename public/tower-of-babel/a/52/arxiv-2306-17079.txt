Embeddings and hyperplanes of the Lie geometry A n , { 1 , n } ​ ( 𝔽 ) A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
Antonio Pasini

Abstract
In this paper we consider a family of projective embeddings of the geometry A n , { 1 , n } ​ ( 𝔽 ) A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F}) of point-hyperplanes flags of PG ⁡ ( n , 𝔽 ) \mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F}) . The natural embedding ε nat \varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}} is one of them. It maps every point-hyperplane flag ( p , H ) (p,H) onto the vector-line ⟨ 𝐱 ⊗ ξ ⟩ \langle{\bf x}\otimes\xi\rangle , where 𝐱 {\bf x} is a representative vector of p p and ξ \xi is a linear functional describing H H . The other embeddings have been discovered by Thas and Van Maldeghem [ 9 , Part III] for the case n = 2 n=2 and later generalized to any n n by De Schepper, Schillewaert and Van Maldeghem [ 3 ] . They are obtained as twistings of ε nat \varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}} by non-trivial automorphisms of 𝔽 \mathbb{F} . Explicitly, for σ ∈ Aut ⁡ ( 𝔽 ) ∖ { id 𝔽 } \sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}\} , the twisting ε σ \varepsilon_{\sigma} of ε nat \varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}} by σ \sigma maps ( p , H ) (p,H) onto ⟨ 𝐱 σ ⊗ ξ ⟩ \langle{\bf x}^{\sigma}\otimes\xi\rangle . We shall prove that, when Aut ⁡ ( 𝔽 ) ≠ { id 𝔽 } \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})\neq\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}\} , a geometric hyperplane ℋ {\cal H} of A n , { 1 , n } ​ ( 𝔽 ) A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F}) arises from ε nat \varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}} and one of its twistings or from two distinct twistings of ε nat \varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}} if and only if ℋ = { ( p , H ) ∈ A n , { 1 , n } ​ ( 𝔽 ) ∣ p ∈ A ​ or ​ a ∈ H } {\cal H}=\{(p,H)\in A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})\mid p\in A\mbox{ or }a\in H\} for a possibly non-incident point-hyperplane pair ( a , A ) (a,A) of PG ⁡ ( n , 𝔽 ) \mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F}) . We call these hyperplanes quasi-singular hyperplanes. With the help of this result we shall prove that if | Aut ⁡ ( 𝔽 ) | > 1 |\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1 then A n , { 1 , n } ​ ( 𝔽 ) A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F}) admits no absolutely universal embedding.



1
Introduction

1.1
Basic properties of the geometry
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})

Following a well established notation, we denote by
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
the geometry of point-hyperplane flags of the projective geometry
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
, for
n\geq 2
and
\mathbb{F}
a given field.

Explicitly,
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
is the point-line geometry the points of which are the ordered pairs
(p,H)
where
p
and
H
are a point and a hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
respectively and
p\in H
; the lines of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
are the sets
\{(p,H)\mid p\in\ell\}
for
\ell
a line of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
and
H
a hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
containing
\ell
and the sets
\{(p,H)\mid H\supset L\}
for
L
a sub-hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
(namely a subspace of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
of codimension 2) and
p
a point of
L
. Accordingly, two points
(p,H)
and
(q,K)
of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
are collinear if and only if either
p=q
or
H=K
. Let
p\neq q
and
H\neq K
. If either
p\in K
or
q\in H
, then
(p,H)
and
(q,K)
are at distance 2, otherwise they are at distance 3. Thus, the diameter of the collinearity graph of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
is equal to 3.

1.1.1
Maximal singular subspaces

Given a point
a
of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
, let
{\cal M}_{a}
be the set of pairs
(a,H)
with
H
a hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
containing
a
. This set is a maximal singular subspace of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
, namely a subspace of the point-line geometry
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
all points of which are mutually collinear and maximal with respect to this property. Dually, for a hyperplane
A
of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
, the set
{\cal M}_{A}:=\{(p,A)\mid p\in A\}
is a maximal singular subspace of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. We say that
{\cal M}_{a}
and
{\cal M}_{A}
are
based
at
a
and
A
respectively. Every maximal singular subspace of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
admits one of these two descriptions. Thus, the maximal singular subspaces of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
are partioned in two families: those which are based at a point and those based at a hyperplane. Two distinct maximal singular subspaces have at most one point in common; they meet in a point only if they do not belong to the same family. Moreover. if
M
is a maximal singular subspace of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
and
(p,H)
a point of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
exterior to
\cal M
, then
(p,H)
is collinear with at most one point of
\cal M
.

1.1.2
The natural embedding and its twistings

It is well known that
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
admits a (full) projective embedding in the projective space
\mathrm{PG}(M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F}))
of the vector space
M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
of null-traced square matrices of order
n+1
with entries in
\mathbb{F}
, which yields the adjoint representation of the special linear group
\mathrm{SL}(n+1,\mathbb{F})
. We call it the
natural embedding
of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
and we denote it by the symbol
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
.

Explicitly, recall that
M^{0}_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
is a hyperplane of the vector space
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of square matrices of order
n+1
with entries in
\mathbb{F}
and the latter is the same as the tensor product
V\otimes V^{*}
, where
V=V(n+1,\mathbb{F})
and
V^{*}
is the dual of
V
. The pure tensors
{\bf x}\otimes\xi
of
V\otimes V^{*}
, with
{\bf x}
and
\xi
non-zero vectors of
V
and
V^{*}
respectively, yield the matrices of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of rank
1
. With
{\bf x}
and
\xi
as above, let
[{\bf x}]
and
[\xi]
be the point and the hyperplane of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
represented by
{\bf x}
and
\xi
. Then
([{\bf x}],[\xi])
is a point of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
if and only if
\xi({\bf x})=0
. The pure tensor
{\bf x}\otimes\xi
, regarded as a square matrix of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of rank 1, is null-traced if and only if
\xi({\bf x})=0
. The natural embedding

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}:A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(M^{0}_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))

maps the point
([{\bf x}],[\xi])
of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
onto the point
\langle{\bf x}\otimes\xi\rangle
of
\mathrm{PG}(M^{0}_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))
.

Suppose now that
\mathbb{F}
admits non-trivial automorphisms and let
\sigma
be one of them. We can define a ‘twisted’ version
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
as follows (De Schepper, Schillewaert and Van Maldeghem
[
3
]
; also Thas and Van Maldeghem
[
9
, Part III]
):

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\varepsilon_{\sigma}&:&A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})&\rightarrow&\mathrm{PG}(M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))\\
&&([{\bf x}],[\xi])&\rightarrow&[{\bf x}^{\sigma}\otimes\xi]\end{array}

where if
{\bf x}=(x_{i})=(x_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
then
{\bf x}^{\sigma}=(x_{i}^{\sigma})_{i=0}^{n}
. This mapping is indeed a projective embedding of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
in
\mathrm{PG}(M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))
. Note that
\dim(\varepsilon_{\sigma})=\dim(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}})+1
.

1.1.3
Quasi-singular hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})

Given a point
a
and a hyperplane
A
of
\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
, possibly
a\not\in A
, let
{\cal H}_{a,A}
be the set of points
(p,H)
of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
collinear with at least one point of the union
{\cal M}_{a}\cup{\cal M}_{A}
of the maximal singular subspaces
{\cal M}_{a}
and
{\cal M}_{A}
. In other words,
(p,H)\in{\cal H}_{a,A}
if and only if either
p\in A
or
a\in H
. It is proved in
[
5
]
that
{\cal H}_{a,A}
is a geometric hyperplane of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. Following
[
5
]
, we call the hyperplanes as
{\cal H}_{a,A}

quasi-singular
hyperplanes. When
a\in A
then
{\cal H}_{a,A}
is the set of points of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
at non-maximal distance from the point
(a,A)
. We call it a
singular
hyperplane.

1.2
Embeddings and hyperplanes of point-line geometries

In this subsection we recall some basics on projective embeddings and geometric hyperplanes of point-lines geometries. We will stick to what is necessary in order to understand the remaining parts of this introduction. A little more of information on this matter will be given in Section
2.3
.

Throughout this subsection
\Gamma
is an arbitrary point-line geometry as defined in Shult
[
8
]
, but we assume that the lines of
\Gamma
are subsets of the set of points of
\Gamma
, henceforth denoted by
\cal P
. We also assume
\Gamma
to be connected.

As in Shult
[
7
]
, a
projective embedding
of
\Gamma
(an
embedding
of
\Gamma
for short) is an injective mapping
\varepsilon
from the point-set
\cal P
of
\Gamma
to the point-set of a desarguesian projective space
\Sigma
such that
\varepsilon({\cal P})
spans
\Sigma
and
\varepsilon(\ell):=\{\varepsilon(p)\}_{p\in\ell}
is a line of
\Sigma
, for every line
\ell
of
\Gamma
. The dimension of
\Sigma
is the
dimension

\dim(\varepsilon)
of
\varepsilon
.

1.2.1
Morphisms of embeddings

We refer to Faure and Frölicher
[
4
, Definition 6.2.1]
for the definition of morphisms of projective geometries. Given two embeddings
\varepsilon_{1}:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
of
\Gamma
, a
morphism
from
\varepsilon_{1}
to
\varepsilon_{2}
is morphism of projective geometries
\phi:\Sigma_{1}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
such that
\varepsilon_{2}=\phi\cdot\varepsilon_{1}
. If
\phi
is a morphism from
\varepsilon_{1}
to
\varepsilon_{2}
we write
\phi:\varepsilon_{1}\rightarrow\varepsilon_{2}
, we say that
\varepsilon_{1}

covers

\varepsilon_{2}
and we write
\varepsilon_{1}\geq\varepsilon_{2}
, also
\varepsilon_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
. Note that, as
\Gamma
is connected by assumption, if a morphism
\phi:\varepsilon_{1}\rightarrow\varepsilon_{2}
exists then
\phi
is uniquely determined by the condition
\varepsilon_{2}=\phi\cdot\varepsilon_{1}
. This condition also forces
\phi:\Sigma_{1}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
to be surjective. So, if
\phi
is injective then it is an isomorphism. If this is the case then we say that
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are
isomorphic
and we write
\varepsilon_{1}\cong\varepsilon_{2}
. Note that
\varepsilon_{1}\cong\varepsilon_{2}
if and only if
\varepsilon_{1}\geq\varepsilon_{2}\geq\varepsilon_{1}
.

1.2.2
Relatively or absolutely universal embeddings

Following Shult
[
7
]
, we say that an embedding
\varepsilon
of a point-line geometry
\Gamma
is
relatively universal
if every morphism from an embedding of
\Gamma
to
\varepsilon
is an isomorphism. An embedding
\varepsilon
is said to be
absolutely universal
if it covers all embeddings of
\Gamma
. Clearly, the absolutely universal embedding, if it exits, is unique up to isomorphisms and it is relatively universal. The converse is false in general. Indeed, as proved by Ronan
[
6
]
, every embedding
\varepsilon
of a point-line geometry
\Gamma
is covered by a relatively universal embedding, uniquely determined by
\varepsilon
up to isomorphisms. So,
\Gamma
admits the absolutely universal embedding if and only if it admits a unique relatively universal embedding (unique
up to isomorphisms, of course).

Remark 1

The previous definition of the absolutely universal embedding is more restrictive than in
[
7
]
, where an embedding of
\Gamma
is said to be absolutely universal if it covers all embeddings of
\Gamma
defined over the same division ring as
\varepsilon
itself. If
\Gamma
admits embeddings defined over different division rings then it cannot admit the absolutely universal embedding as we have defined it but some of its embeddings could be absolutely universal in the sense of Shult
[
7
]
.

1.2.3
Hyperplanes and embeddings

A
subspace
of a point-line geometry
\Gamma
is a subset
X
of the point-set
\cal P
of
\Gamma
such that, for every line
\ell
of
\Gamma
, if
|\ell\cap X|>1
then
\ell\subseteq X
. A proper subspace of
\Gamma
is said to be a
geometric hyperplane
of
\Gamma
(a
hyperplane
of
\Gamma
for short) if every line of
\Gamma
meets it non-trivially. Equivalently, a hyperplane of
\Gamma
is a proper subset
{\cal H}
of
\cal P
such that, for every line
\ell
of
\Gamma
, either
\ell\subseteq{\cal H}
or
|\ell\cap{\cal H}|=1
.

Assuming that
\Gamma
is embeddable, let
\varepsilon:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma
be an embedding of
\Gamma
in a projective space
\Sigma
. We say that a hyperplane
{\cal H}
of
\Gamma

arises from

\varepsilon
if
\varepsilon({\cal H})
spans a projective hyperplane
H
of
\Sigma
and
{\cal H}=\varepsilon^{-1}(H)
. Conversely, if
H
is a projective hyperplane of
\Sigma
then
\varepsilon^{-1}(H)
is a hyperplane of
\Gamma
and
\varepsilon(\varepsilon^{-1}(H))=H\cap\varepsilon({\cal P})
, but in general
\varepsilon^{-1}(H)
does not arise from
\varepsilon
. Indeed
\varepsilon^{-1}(H)
arises from
\varepsilon
if and only if
\langle H\cap\varepsilon({\cal P})\rangle=H
. The following is proved in
[
5
, Proposition 1.1]
.

Proposition 1.1

Given an embedding
\varepsilon:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma
of
\Gamma
and a hyperplane
{\cal H}
of
\Gamma
, if
{\cal H}
is a maximal subspace of
\Gamma
then either
{\cal H}
arises from
\varepsilon
or
\varepsilon({\cal H})
spans
\Sigma
. In particular, given a projective hyperplane
H
of
\Sigma
, suppose that
\varepsilon^{-1}(H)
is a maximal subspace of
\Gamma
. Then
H\cap\varepsilon({\cal P})
spans
H
.

Remark 2

A subspace
S
of
\Gamma
is said to arise from an embedding
\varepsilon:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma
if
S=\varepsilon^{-1}(\langle\varepsilon(S)\rangle)
. A hyperplane
{\cal H}
of
\Gamma
arises from
\varepsilon
as a hyperplane precisely when it arises from
\varepsilon
as a subspace of
\Gamma
and
\varepsilon({\cal H})
spans a hyperplane of
\Sigma
.

1.2.4
Generating rank and relatively universal embeddings

The intersection of an arbitrary family of subspaces of
\Gamma
is still a subspace of
\Gamma
. For a subset
X
of
\cal P
, let
\langle X\rangle
be the smallest subspace of
\Gamma
containing
X
, namely
\langle X\rangle
is the intersection of all subspaces of
\Gamma
which contain
X
. If
\langle X\rangle=\Gamma
then we say that
X

generates

\Gamma
. The
generating rank

\mathrm{grk}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
is the minumum cadinality of a generating set of
\Gamma
. Clearly, if
\varepsilon
is a projective embedding of
\Gamma
then
\dim(\varepsilon)+1\leq\mathrm{grk}(\Gamma)
. Consequently,

Proposition 1.2

If
\dim(\varepsilon)+1=\mathrm{grk}(\Gamma)<\infty
then
\varepsilon
is relatively universal.

1.3
Back to
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
and its embeddings

As we shall prove in this paper (Corollary
1.9
), when
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1
the geometry
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
admits no absolutely universal embedding.

Conjecture 1.3

If
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|=1
then
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
is absolutely universal.

The following result by Blok and Pasini
[
2
]
is a clue in favour of Conjecture
1.3
: if
\mathbb{F}
is a prime field and
n>2
then
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
is absolutely universal.

Conjecture 1.4

Both
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
and all of its twistings are relatively universal.

Partial results are known which support Conjecture
1.4
. For instance, it follows from Völklein
[
10
]
that if
\mathbb{F}
is perfect of positive characteristic or an algebraic number field then
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
is relatively universal. Moreover, let
\mathbb{F}
be finite. Then the generating rank of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
is at most
(n+1)^{2}
(Blok and Pasini
[
1
]
). The twistings of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
have dimension equal to
(n+1)^{2}-1
. Hence all of them are relatively universal by Proposition
1.2
. As we shall notice in the last paragraph of Section
6
, when
\mathbb{F}
is finite and
n=2
the same conclusion is implicit in the classification of polarized embeddings of
A_{2,\{1,2\}}(\mathbb{F})
with
\mathbb{F}
finite by Thas and Van Maldeghem
[
9
]
.

Turning to the hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
, it is proved in
[
5
, Theorem 1.5]
that all hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
are maximal subspaces. Therefore, in view of Proposition
1.1
,

Proposition 1.5

For every projective embedding
\varepsilon:A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})\rightarrow\Sigma
, the hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
which arise from
\varepsilon
are precisely the
\varepsilon
-preimages of the hyperplanes of
\Sigma
.

1.4
Main results

In view of the next theorem it is convenient to slightly modify our notation. The natural embedding
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
can be regarded as a borderline case of twisting, namely the twisting of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
by the identity automorphism
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
of
\mathbb{F}
. Accordingly, henceforth we will also denote
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
by the symbol
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}
. With this notation,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a member of the set
\{\varepsilon_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})}
.

Theorem 1.6

Suppose that
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1
and let
{\cal H}
be a geometric hyperplane of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
.

(1)

If
{\cal H}
is quasi-singular then
{\cal H}
arises from
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
for every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
.

(2)

If
{\cal H}
is not quasi-singular then
{\cal H}
arises from
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
for at most one
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
.

Claim (1) of Theorem
1.6
will be proved at the end of Section
3
. Claim (2) will be proved in Section
4
.

As we shall see in Section
3
, for every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
many hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
exist which arise from
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
but are not quasi-singular. Therefore Theorem
1.6
implies the following.

Corollary 1.7

Let
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1
. Then
\varepsilon_{\sigma}\not\cong\varepsilon_{\rho}
for any choice of distinct automorphism
\sigma
and
\rho
of
\mathbb{F}
.

The following is the second main result of this paper. We shall prove it in Section
5
with the help of Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 1.8

For any two distinct autmorhisms
\sigma
and
\rho
of
\mathbb{F}
, no projective embedding of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
covers both
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
and
\varepsilon_{\rho}
.

The next corollary immediately follows from Theorem
1.8
.

Corollary 1.9

If
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1
then
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
admits no absolutely universal embedding.

Organization of the paper.
In Section
2
we fix some notation for vectors, tensors, matrices and projective points, we recall some basics on semi-polynomials and we add some information on embeddings and hyperplanes of point-line geometries, as a completion of Section

.

In Section

we describe the hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
which arise from
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
for
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
. We will do it with the help of a symmetric bilinear form, called the
saturation form
in
[
5
]
. We have exploited that form in
[
5
, Section 1.3.1]
in order to describe the hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
which arise from

. Section
3
is indeed a survey of Sections 1.3.1 and 2.2 of
[
5
]
, with the addition of a straightforward generalization to
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
for
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
.

A proof of claim (1) of Theorem
1.6
will also be given at the end of Section
3
. Claim (2) of Theorem
1.6
will be proved in Section
4
. Section
5
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

In the last section of this paper we shall consider quotients (namely morphic images) of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
and its twistings, focusing on the polarized ones, an embedding of

being called

when all singular hyperplanes of

arise from it. In particular, we shall generalize a part of the main result of the triad of papers
[

]
by J. A. Thas and H. Van Maldeghem.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation to be used throughout this paper

We denote the vectors of
V=V(n+1,\mathbb{F})
by low case boldface roman letters and the vectors of its dual
V^{*}
by low case greek letters, but we denote both the null-vector of
V
and the null-vector of
V^{*}
by the symbol
0
. Most of the matrices to be considered in this paper are square matrices of order
n+1
. We denote them by capital roman letters. In particular,
O
and
I
are the null square matrix and the identity matrix of order
n+1
, respectively. Scalars will be denoted by roman or greek low case letters.

The symbols
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
and
M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
denote respectively the vector space of all square matrices of order
n+1
with entries in
\mathbb{F}
and the hyperplane of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
formed by the null-traced matrices, as in Section
1.1.2
. Recall that
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
is basically the same as
V\otimes V^{*}
. Accordingly, we will freely switch from the matrix notation to the tensor notation and conversely, whenever these changes of notation will be convenient.

Given a basis
E=({\bf e}_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
of
V
let
E^{*}=(\eta_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
the basis of
V^{*}
dual to it. So,
\eta_{i}({\bf e}_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
(Kronecker symbol) for any choice of
i,j=0,1,...,n
. The pure tensors
E_{i,j}={\bf e}_{i}\otimes\eta_{j}
form a basis of
V\otimes V^{*}
, henceforth denoted by
E\otimes E^{*}
. When
E
is the natural basis of
V=V(n+1,\mathbb{F})
then
E\otimes E^{*}
is the natural basis of
V\otimes V^{*}
and
E_{i,j}
, regarded as a matrix, is the matrix where all entries are null but the
(i,j)
-entry, which is
1
. So,
E\otimes E^{*}
is just the usual natural basis of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
.

Henceforth we always assume that
E=({\bf e}_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
is the natural basis of
V
and
(\eta_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
is the dual
E^{*}
of
E
. Accordingly, a vector
{\bf x}=\sum_{i=0}{\bf e}_{i}x_{i}
of
V
is the same as the
(n+1)
-tuple
(x_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
of its coordinates with respect to
E
, this
(n+1)
-tuple being regarded as a column, namely an
n\times 1
matrix. Similarly, every vector
\xi=\sum_{i=0}\xi_{i}\eta_{i}\in V^{*}
is the same as the
1\times n
matrix
(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n})
. Thus, the scalar
\xi({\bf x})
is the same as the row-times-column product
\xi{\bf x}
and, for a matrix
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
, the product
\xi M{\bf x}
is the product of the row
\xi
times
M
times the column
{\bf x}
. The tensor
{\bf x}\otimes\xi
is the same as the column-times-row product
{\bf x}\xi
.

We denote by
\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})
(
=\mathrm{Pure}(M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))
) the set of pure tensors of
V\otimes V^{*}=M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
, with the convention that the null vector
O
of
V\otimes V^{*}
is not included in that set. So,
\mathrm{Pure}(M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))
is the set of matrices of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of rank 1. The hyperplane
M^{0}_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
will also be denoted by
(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
and we set
\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0}):=\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})\cap(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
. So,
\mathrm{Pure}(M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F}))
(
=\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})
) is the set of null-traced matrices of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
of rank
1
, namely the set of pure tensors
{\bf x}\otimes\xi
with
\xi({\bf x})=0
.

Given two non-zero vectors
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in V
, if
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
are proportional we write
{\bf x}\equiv{\bf y}
. We use the same notation for vectors of
V^{*}
and matrices of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
. For instance, for a matrix
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{O\}
, when writing
M\equiv I
we mean that
M
is a scalar matrix.

When we need to distinguish betwen a non-zero vector
{\bf x}
of
V
and the point of
\mathrm{PG}(V)=\mathrm{PG}(n,\mathbb{F})
represented by it, we denote the latter by
[{\bf x}]
. We extend this convention to subsets of
V
: if
X\subseteq V\setminus\{0\}
then
[X]:=\{[{\bf x}]\mid{\bf x}\in X\}
. The same conventions will be adopted for vectors and subsets of
V^{*}
and
V\otimes V^{*}
. In particular, if
\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}
then
[\xi]
is the point of
\mathrm{PG}(V^{*})
which corresponds to the hyperplane
[\mathrm{Ker}(\xi)]
of
\mathrm{PG}(V)
. In the sequel we shall freely take
[\xi]
as a name for
[\mathrm{Ker}(\xi)]
.

2.2
Semi-polynomials

Let
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{F})
be the module over the ring
\mathbb{Z}
of integers with
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
as a basis, namely the elements of
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{F})
are finite formal combinations
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})}k_{\sigma}\sigma
with
k_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
and
k_{\sigma}=0
for all but at most a finite number of choices of
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
. Put

\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})~:=~\{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})}k_{\sigma}\sigma\mid k_{\sigma}\geq 0\mbox{ for every }\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})\}.

The sum
w(\gamma):=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})}k_{\sigma}
will be called the
weight
of an element
\gamma=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})}k_{\sigma}\sigma
of
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
. Given
\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}\sigma_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
and
t\in\mathbb{F}
, the exponentiation
t^{\gamma}
stands for
\prod_{i=1}^{r}(t^{k_{i}})^{\sigma_{i}}
.

Note that, regarded
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{F})
as an additive monoid,
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
is a submonoid of
\mathfrak{M}(\mathbb{F})
. However
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
is not yet the structure we need. Indeed it can happen that
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
contains distinct elements
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
t^{\gamma_{1}}=t^{\gamma_{2}}
for every
t\in\mathbb{F}
. (This is indeed always the case when
\mathbb{F}
is finite.) In view of what we are going to do in the sequel, this possibility would cause some troubles. We need to counteract its effects.

So, let
\sim
be the relation defined on
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
by the following clause:
\gamma_{1}\sim\gamma_{2}
if and only if
t^{\gamma_{1}}=t^{\gamma_{2}}
for every
t\in\mathbb{F}
. Then
\sim
is a congruence relation of
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
, namely it is an equivalence relation and it is preserved when taking sums. The quotient
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F}):=\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})/\sim
is the structure we need. The elements of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
are the equivalence classes of
\sim
. For
\gamma\in\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
, we denote by
[\gamma]
the class of
\sim
which contains
\gamma
. The sum of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
is defined as follows:
[\gamma_{1}]+[\gamma_{2}]:=[\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}]
, for any two elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
of
\mathfrak{M}^{+}(\mathbb{F})
. The
weight

w(X)
of a class
X
of
\sim
is the minimum among the weights of its elements. For instance, if
X
contains
k\cdot\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
for
0\leq k<|\mathbb{F}|
then
k=w(X)
. Note that if
\mathbb{F}
is finite then
w(X)<|\mathbb{F}|
, for every
X\in\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
.

We are now ready to define semi-monomials and semi-polynomials. A (non-null)
semi-monomial
(
over
\mathbb{F}
) in the unknowns
t_{1},...,t_{m}
is a formal product

M~=~a\cdot t_{1}^{X_{1}}...t_{m}^{X_{m}}

where
X_{1},...,X_{m}\in\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
and
a\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
. Its
degree

\mathrm{deg}(M)
is the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w(X_{i})
of the exponents
X_{1},...,X_{m}
and its
type
is the
m
-tuple
(X_{1},...,X_{m})
. We allow
X=0
(the null element of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
) and we put
t^{0}:=1
, as usual. We also admit the null monomial as a semi-monomial in whatever set of unknown we like, giving it
-\infty
as its degree and
\emptyset
as its type.

A (non-null)
semi-polynomial
(
over
\mathbb{F}
) in the unknowns
t_{1},...,t_{m}
is a formal sum
P=M_{1}+...+M_{s}
of non-null semi-monomials (over
\mathbb{F}
) in those unknowns, no two of which have the same type. The
degree

\mathrm{deg}(P)
of
P
is defined as the maximum
\mathrm{max}_{i=1}^{s}\mathrm{deg}(M_{i})
. We allow
P
to be the null semi-monomial, henceforth also called the
null semi-polynomial
.

The following statement will be freely used troughout Section
4.2
. We believe this statement is well known. However, as we couldn’t find any mention of it in the literature, we shall give of its proof here, so that the reader can save the trouble of looking for it in the literature.

Theorem 2.1
(Identity Principle)

Let
P=P(t_{1},...,t_{m})
be a non-null semi-polynomial over
\mathbb{F}
in the unknowns
t_{1},...,t_{m}
. Then
P(c_{1},...,c_{m})\neq 0
for at least one
m
-tuple
(c_{1},...,c_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
.

Proof.
The proof exploits a double induction, on the number
m
of unknowns of
P
and the number
s
of semi-monomials of
P
. Let
m=1
. If
s=1
there is nothing to prove. Suppose
s=2
. So
P=P(t)=at^{X}+bt^{Y}
with
X,Y
distinct elements of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
and
a,b\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
. By way of contradiction, suppose that
P(c)=0
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Then with
c=1
we get
a+b=0
. It follows that
c^{X}=c^{Y}
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Hence
X=Y
by the definition of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
, contradicting the assumption that no two semi-monomials of the same semi-polynomial have the same type. This fixes the case
m=1
and
s=2
.

Still with
t=1
, suppose now that
s>2
. Now
P(t)=a_{1}t^{X_{1}}+...+a_{s}t^{X_{s}}
where
a_{i}\neq 0
for every
i=1,...,s
and
X_{i}\neq X_{j}
for any choice of
i,j=1,2,...,n
with
i\neq j
. With no loss, we can assume that
a_{s}=1
. For a contradiction, suppose that
P(c)=0
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Then
c^{X_{s}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}a_{i}c^{X_{i}}
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Therefore, for every choice of
c,d\in\mathbb{F}
we have

(\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}a_{i}c^{X_{i}})(\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}a_{i}d^{X_{i}})~=~c^{X_{s}}d^{X_{s}}~=(cd)^{X_{s}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}a_{i}(cd)^{X_{i}}.

Put
f(d):=\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}a_{i}d^{X_{i}}
. Then, for every choice of
d
, we have
a_{i}f(d)=-a_{i}d^{X_{i}}
by the inductive hypothesis on the polynomial

\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}(f(d)+d^{X_{i}})t^{X_{i}}
. Equivalently, since
a_{i}\neq 0
by assumption,
f(d)=-d^{X_{i}}
. This holds for every
d\in\mathbb{F}
and every
i=1,...,s-1
. Accordingly,
d^{X_{i}}=d^{X_{j}}
for every
d\in\mathbb{F}
and every
i,j=1,...,s-1
with
i\neq j
. This forces
X_{i}=X_{j}
by the definition of
\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
, contradicting the assumption that
X_{i}\neq X_{j}
if
i\neq j
. By induction, the statement of the theorem holds true when
m=1
.

Let now
m>1
. Then
P(t_{1},...,t_{m})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}P_{j}(t_{1},...,t_{m-1})t_{m}^{X_{j}}
for suitable polynomials
P_{1},...,P_{r}
in the unknowns
t_{1},...,t_{m-1}
and suitable pairwise distinct elements
X_{1},...,X_{r}\in\mathfrak{M}^{[+]}(\mathbb{F})
.

For a contradiction, suppose that
P(c_{1},...,c_{m-1},c_{m})=0
for every choice of
(c_{1},...,c_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
. For every
(m-1)
-tuple
{\bf c}=(c_{i})_{i=1}^{m-1}\in\mathbb{F}^{m-1}
, put
P_{\bf c}(t)=P(c_{1},...,c_{m-1},t)
. Then
P_{\bf c}(c)=0
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Hence
P_{j}(c_{1},...,c_{m-1})=0
for every
j=1,...,r
, by the previous paragraph. However
{\bf c}
is an arbitrary
(m-1)
-tuple in
\mathbb{F}^{m-1}
. Therefore, by the inductive hypothesis,
P_{j}
is null for every
j=1,...,r
. Hence
P
is null as well, contradicting the assumption that
P
is not the null polynomial.
\Box

\varepsilon:\Gamma\rightarrow\Sigma

.

3
The saturation form

Let
f:M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\times M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
be the symmetric bilinear form defined as follows:

f(X,Y)~:=~\mathrm{trace}(XY)~\mbox{ for any two matrices }X,Y\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}),

(2)

where
XY
is the usual row-times-column product. So, with
X=(x_{i,j})_{i,j=0}^{n}
and
Y=(y_{i,j})_{i,j=0}^{n}
we have

f((x_{i,j})_{i,j=0}^{n},(y_{i,j})_{i,j=0}^{n})~=~\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}y_{j,i}.

(3)

In particular, when
X
and
Y
have rank 1, namely they are pure tensors, say
X={\bf x}\otimes\xi
and
Y={\bf y}\otimes\upsilon
, then

f({\bf x}\otimes\xi,{\bf y}\otimes\upsilon)~=~\upsilon({\bf x})\xi({\bf y}).

(4)

Following
[
5
]
, we call
f
the
saturation form
of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
.

As noticed in
[
5
, Section 2.2]
, the form
f
is non-degenerate. We denote by
{\cal S}_{f}
the set of
f
-isotropic vectors of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
, namely the set of matrices
X\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
such that
f(X,X)=0
.

If
\mathrm{rank}(X)=1
, namely
X={\bf x}\otimes\xi\in\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})
, then
f(X,X)=(\xi({\bf x}))^{2}
by formula (
4
) of Section
3
. Hence
f(X,X)=0
if and only if
\xi({\bf x})=0
, namely
X\in M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
. Accordingly,

{\cal S}_{f}\cap\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})~=~\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0}).

\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})
spans
(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
. Note also that
[\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})]=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}({\cal P})
, where
\cal P
stands for the set of points of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. On the other hand, let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}\}
. Then
\varepsilon_{\sigma}({\cal P})=[P_{\sigma}]
where

P_{\sigma}~:=~\{{\bf x}^{\sigma}\otimes\xi\mid{\bf x}\otimes\xi\in\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})\}.

The set
P_{\sigma}
contains
{\bf e}_{i}\otimes\eta_{j}
and
({\bf e}_{i}+{\bf e}_{j})\otimes(\eta_{i}-\eta_{j})
for every choice of
i\neq j
. These vectors span
(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
. However
P_{\sigma}
also contains pure tensors exterior to
(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
. For instance,
({\bf e}_{0}t^{\sigma}+{\bf e}_{1})\otimes(\eta_{0}-t\eta_{1})
with
t\in\mathbb{F}
such that
t^{\sigma}\neq t
is one of them. Therefore
P_{\sigma}
spans
V\otimes V^{*}
, namely
\varepsilon_{\sigma}({\cal P})
spans
\mathrm{PG}(M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F}))
.

Let
\perp_{f}
be the orthogonality relation associated to
f
. As
f
is non-degenerate, the hyperplanes of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
are the perps
M^{\perp_{f}}
for
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{O\}
and, for two matrices
M,N\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{O\}
, we have
M^{\perp_{f}}=N^{\perp_{f}}
if and only if
M\equiv N
. It is clear from formula (
2
) that
I^{\perp_{f}}=M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
. Therefore, for
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{O\}
, we have
M^{\perp_{f}}=M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
if and only if
M\equiv I
.

Every hyperplane of
M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
is the intersection of
M^{0}_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
with a hyperplane
M^{\perp_{f}}
of
M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
for a suitable matrix
M\not\in\langle I\rangle
. Of course the equality
M^{\perp_{f}}\cap M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})=N^{\perp_{f}}\cap M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
does not imply that
M\equiv N
. The next statement follows from well known properties of the polarities associated to non-degenerate reflexive bilinear forms.

Proposition 3.1

For
M,N\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{O\}
, we have
M^{\perp_{f}}\cap M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})=N^{\perp_{f}}\cap M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
if and only if

\langle M,I\rangle=\langle N,I\rangle
.

When
X\in\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})
, namely
X\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
has rank equal to 1, the orthogonality condition

admits an easy formulation. Indeed formula (
3
) yields the following.

Proposition 3.2

Let
{\bf x}\in V\setminus\{0\}
,
\xi\in V^{*}\setminus\{0\}
and
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
. Then
{\bf x}\otimes\xi\perp_{f}M
if and only if
\xi M{\bf x}=0
.

3.1
The hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
which arise from
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}

For
M\in M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{\lambda I\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{F}}
, we put

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal H}_{M,\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}&:=&\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}^{-1}([M^{\perp_{f}}\cap M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})])\\

(5)

(Note that
M^{\perp_{f}}\cap\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})=\{{\bf x}\otimes\xi\neq 0\mid\xi M{\bf x}=0\}
, by Proposition
3.2
.) So,
{\cal H}_{M,\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}
is a geometric hyperplane of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. By Proposition

, it arises from
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}

In particular, let
\mathrm{rank}(M)=1
, say
M={\bf a}\otimes\alpha
for a pure tensor
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha\in\mathrm{Pure}(V\otimes V^{*})
.
By Proposition

, if
{\cal H}_{{\bf a}\otimes\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}={\cal H}_{{\bf b}\otimes\beta,\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}
then

if and only if
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha

Lemma 4.2

and
H_{3}:=\langle\tilde{\varepsilon}({\cal H}_{3})\rangle
. Clearly
K
is a hyperplane of both
H_{2}
and
H_{3}
. Moreover,
H_{2}\neq H_{3}
, since
{\cal H}_{2}\neq{\cal H}_{3}
. Therefore
H_{2}\cap H_{3}=K
. However this conclusion crashes against the fact that, as stated in Lemma
5.3
, the intersection
{\cal H}_{1}\cap{\cal H}_{3}
properly contains
{\cal K}={\cal H}_{1}\cap{\cal H}_{3}
. Therefore no embedding of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
covers both
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
and
\varepsilon_{\rho}
.
\Box

6
Quotients of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
and its twistings

Consider
\varepsilon_{\sigma}:A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})\rightarrow\mathrm{PG}(W)
with
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})
, where
W=M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})
if
\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
and
W=M_{n+1}(\mathbb{F})
if otherwise.

Lemma 6.1

Let
S
be a subspace of
W
. If all matrices of
S\setminus\{O\}
have rank at least
3
then the corresponding subspace
[S]
of
\mathrm{PG}(W)
defines a quotient of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
. When
\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
the converse also holds:
[S]
defines a quotient of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
only if all matrices of
S\setminus\{O\}
have rank at least
3
.

Proof.
The
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
-images of the points of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
are represented by matrices of rank 1. The sum of two matrices of rank 1 has rank at most 2.
The first claim of the lemma follows from this remark and condition (
1
) of Section
2.3.1
. Turning to the second claim, every null-traced matrix of rank 1 represents the
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
-image of a point of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. The second claim of the lemma follows from the well known fact that every matrix of rank 2 is the sum of two matrices of rank 1.
\Box

By the first part of Lemma
6.1
, for every non-singular matrix
M\in W
, the point
[M]
of
\mathrm{PG}(W)
defines a quotient of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
. In particular,
\varepsilon_{\sigma}/[I]
is defined for every
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
while
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
is defined if and only if
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is positive and divides
n+1
. Indeed
\mathrm{trace}(I)=0
if and only if
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is positive and divides
n+1
.

We say that an embedding of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
is
polarized
if all singular hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
arise from it. For instance, as we know from Theorem
1.6
, the natural embedding of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
and all of its twistings are polarized.

Remark 3

The choice of the word “polarized” is suggested by the theory of dual polar spaces, where a projective embedding of a dual polar space
\Delta
is said to be polarized precisely when all singular hyperplanes of
\Delta
arise from it, a singular hyperplane of
\Delta
being formed by the points of
\Delta
at non maximal distance from a given point, just as in
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
. Thas and Van Maldeghem adopt a different terminology in
[
9
]
, where the embeddings we call polarized are called full weak embeddings.

Theorem 6.2

If
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})\setminus\{\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}\}
or
\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
but either
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})=0
or
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is positive and prime to
n+1
, then no proper quotient of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
is polarized. When
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is positive and divides
n+1
then
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
is the unique polarized proper quotient of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
.

Proof.
Let
S
be a subspace of
W
such that
[S]
defines a polarized proper quotient of
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
and let
M
belong to
S
. By Proposition
3.3
, for every pure tensor
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha\in\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})
the matrix
M
belongs to the hyperplane
H_{{\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma},\sigma}
of
W
spanned by the pure tensors
{\bf x}\otimes\xi\in W
such that
\xi({\bf a})\alpha^{\sigma}({\bf x})=0
. By Proposition
3.3
we have
H_{{\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma},\sigma}=(({\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma})^{\perp_{f}})\cap W
(
=({\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma})^{\perp_{f}}
when
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
). Hence
M\in H_{{\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma},\sigma}
if and only if
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha^{\sigma}\perp_{f}M
if and only if
\alpha^{\sigma}M{\bf a}=0
. When
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
the latter condition holds for every
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha\in\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})
if and only if
M=O
.

Let
\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}
. Then
\alpha M{\bf a}=0
for every
{\bf a}\otimes\alpha\in\mathrm{Pure}((V\otimes V^{*})_{0})
if and only if
M\in\langle I\rangle
. However
M\in W
and in this case
W=M_{n+1}^{0}(\mathbb{F})=(V\otimes V^{*})_{0}
. Hence
M\neq O
only if
\mathrm{trace}(I)=0
, namely
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is positive and divides
n+1
.
\Box

Remark 4

The following is implicit in the proof of Theorem
6.2
: given two non-null matrices
M
and
N
, the hyperplane
{\cal H}_{N,\sigma}
arises from
\varepsilon_{\sigma}/[M]
if and only if
N\perp_{f}M
. In particular, assuming that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
exists, the hyperplane
{\cal H}_{{\bf a}\otimes\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}}}
arises from
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
if and only
\alpha I{\bf a}=0
, namely
\alpha({\bf a})=0
. Consequently, the quasi-singular hyperplanes of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
which arise from
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
are precisely the singular ones.

Conjecture 6.3

Up to isomorphisms, the natural embedding
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}
, its twistings (when
|\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F})|>1
) and the quotient
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nat}}/[I]
(when
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})>0
divides
n+1
) are the unique polarized embeddings of
A_{n,\{1,n\}}(\mathbb{F})
.

As proved by Thas and Van Maldeghem
[
9
]
, Conjecture
6.3
holds true when
\mathbb{F}
is finite and
n=2
.

The property of being polarized is preserved when taking covers. Therefore if Conjecture
6.3
holds true then Conjecture
1.4
also holds true.
