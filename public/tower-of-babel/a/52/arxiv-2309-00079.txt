On the Implicit Bias of Adam
Matias D. Cattaneo, Jason M. Klusowski, Boris Shigida

Abstract
In previous literature, backward error analysis was used to find ordinary differential equations (ODEs) approximating the gradient descent trajectory. It was found that finite step sizes implicitly regularize solutions because terms appearing in the ODEs penalize the two-norm of the loss gradients. We prove that the existence of similar implicit regularization in RMSProp and Adam depends on their hyperparameters and the training stage, but with a different “norm” involved: the corresponding ODE terms either penalize the (perturbed) one-norm of the loss gradients or, conversely, impede its reduction (the latter case being typical). We also conduct numerical experiments and discuss how the proven facts can influence generalization.

Introduction

Gradient descent (GD) can be seen as a numerical method solving the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
E(\cdot)
is the loss function and
\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is its gradient. Starting at
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
, it creates a sequence of guesses
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)},\ldots
, which lie close to the solution trajectory
\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)
governed by the aforementioned ODE. Since the step size
h
is finite, one could search for a modified differential equation
\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=-\nabla\widetilde{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
such that
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(nh)
is exactly zero, or at least closer to zero than
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}(nh)
, that is, all the guesses of the descent lie exactly on the new solution curve or closer compared to the original curve. This approach to analysing properties of a numerical method is sometimes called backward error analysis in the numerical integration literature (see Chapter IX in
Ernst Hairer & Wanner 2006
and references therein).

Barrett & Dherin 2021
used this idea for full-batch GD and found that the modified loss function
\widetilde{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=E(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+(h/4)\|\nabla E(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|^{2}
makes the trajectory of the solution to
\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=-\nabla\widetilde{E}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
approximate the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
one order of
h
better than the original ODE, where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm. In related work,
Miyagawa 2022
obtained the correction term for full-batch GD up to any chosen order, also studying the global error (uniform in the iteration number) as opposed to the local (one-step) error.

The analysis was later extended to mini-batch GD in
Smith et al. 2021
. Assume that the training set is split into batches of size
B
and there are
m
batches per epoch (so the training set size is
mB
). The cost function is rewritten as
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(1/m)\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{E}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with mini-batch costs denoted
\hat{E}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(1/B)\sum_{j=kB+1}^{kB+B}E_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. It was obtained in that work that after one epoch, the mean iterate of the algorithm, averaged over all possible shuffles of the batch indices, is close to the solution to
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\nabla\widetilde{E}_{SGD}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where the modified loss is given by
\widetilde{E}_{SGD}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=E(\boldsymbol{\theta})+h/(4m)\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\big\|\nabla\hat{E}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|^{2}
.

Modified equations have also been derived for GD with heavy-ball momentum
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n+1)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-h\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})+\beta(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n-1)})
, where
\beta
is the momentum parameter. In the full-batch setting, it turns out that for
n
large enough it is close to the continuous trajectory solving

\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\frac{\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{1-\beta}-\underbrace{h\frac{1+\beta}{(1-\beta)^{3}}\frac{\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right\|^{2}}{4}}_{\text{implicit regularization}}.

(1)

Versions of this general result were proven in
Farazmand 2020
,
Kovachki & Stuart 2021
,
Ghosh et al. 2023
under different assumptions. The focus of the latter work is the closest to ours since they interpret the correction term as implicit regularization. Their main theorem also provides the analysis for the general mini-batch case.

In another recent work,
Zhao et al. 2022
introduce a regularization term
\lambda\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right\|
to the loss function as a way to ensure finding flatter minima, improving generalization. The only difference between their term and the first-order correction coming from backward error analysis (up to a coefficient) is that the norm is not squared and regularization is applied on a per-batch basis.

The application of backward error analysis for approximating the discrete dynamics of adaptive algorithms such as RMSProp
(
Tieleman et al. 2012
)
and Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
is currently missing in the literature.
Barrett & Dherin 2021
note that “it would be interesting to use backward error analysis to calculate the modified loss and implicit regularization for other widely
used optimizers such as momentum, Adam and RMSprop”.
Smith et al. 2021
reiterate that they “anticipate that backward error analysis could also be used to clarify the role of finite learning rates in adaptive optimizers like Adam”.
Ghosh et al. 2023
agree that “RMSProp … and Adam …, albeit being powerful alternatives to SGD
with faster convergence rates, are far from well-understood in the aspect of implicit regularization”. In a similar context, in Appendix G to
Miyagawa 2022
it is mentioned that “its [Adam’s] counter term and discretization error are open questions”.

This work fills the gap by conducting backward error analysis for (mini-batch, and full-batch as a special case) Adam and RMSProp. Our main contributions are listed below.

•

In
Theorem

3.1
, we provide a global second-order in
h
continuous piecewise ODE approximation to Adam in the general mini-batch setting. (A similar result for RMSProp is moved to
Appendix

C
.) For the full-batch special case, it was shown in prior work
Ma et al. 2022
that the continuous-time limit of both these algorithms is a (perturbed by the numerical stability parameter
\varepsilon
) signGD flow
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})/(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right|+\varepsilon)

component-wise; we make this more precise by finding a linear in
h
correction term on the right.

•

We analyze the full-batch case in the context of regularization (see the summary in
Section

2
). In contrast to the case of GD, where the two-norm of the loss gradient is implicitly penalized, Adam typically
anti
-penalizes the perturbed one-norm of the loss gradient
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1,\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sqrt{v_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon}
(i. e., penalizes the negative norm), as specified in (
5
). Thus, the implicit bias of Adam that we identify serves as

anti-regularization
(except for the unusual case
\beta\geq\rho
, large
\varepsilon
or very late at training).

•

We provide numerical evidence consistent with our theoretical results by training various vision models on CIFAR-10 using full-batch Adam. In particular, we observe that the stronger the implicit anti-regularization effect predicted by our theory, the worse the generalization. This pattern holds across different architectures: ResNets, simple convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers. Thus, we propose a novel possible explanation for often-reported poor generalization of adaptive gradient algorithms. The code used for training the models is available at
https://github.com/borshigida/implicit-bias-of-adam
.

1.1
Related Work

Backward error analysis of first-order methods.
We outlined the history of finding ODEs approximating different algorithms above in the introduction. Recently, there have been other applications of backward error analysis related to machine learning.
Kunin et al. 2020
show that the approximating continuous-time trajectories satisfy conservation laws that are broken in discrete time.
França et al. 2021
use backward error analysis while studying how to discretize continuous-time dynamical systems preserving stability and convergence rates.
Rosca et al. 2021
find continuous-time approximations of discrete two-player differential games.

Approximating gradient methods by differential equation trajectories.
Under the assumption that the hyperparameters
\beta,\rho
of the Adam algorithm (see
Definition

1.1
) tend to 1 at a certain rate as
h\to 0
, a first-order continuous ODE approximation to this algorithm was derived in
Barakat & Bianchi 2021
. On the other hand, if
\beta,\rho
are kept fixed,
Ma et al. 2022
prove that the trajectories of Adam and RMSProp are close to signGD dynamics, and investigate different training regimes of these algorithms empirically. SGD is approximated by stochastic differential equations and novel adaptive parameter adjustment policies are devised in
Li et al. 2017
.
Malladi et al. 2022
derive stochastic differential equations that are order-1 weak approximations of RMSProp and Adam. We go in a different direction: instead of clarifying the previously obtained continuous ODE approximations by taking gradient noise into account, we take a deterministic approach but go one order of
h
further. In particular, we keep
\beta
,
\rho
fixed (thus generalizing the analysis for SGD with momentum), whereas
Malladi et al. 2022
take
\beta,\rho\to 1
.

Connection with signGD.
The connection of adaptive gradient methods with sign(S)GD is extensively discussed in
Bernstein et al. 2018
.
Balles et al. 2020
study a version of signGD with an update proportional to
-\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1}\sign{\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
as a special case of steepest descent, and discuss when sign-based methods are preferable to GD.

Implicit bias of first-order methods.

Soudry et al. 2018
prove that GD trained to classify linearly separable data with logistic loss converges to the direction of the max-margin vector (the solution to the hard margin SVM). This result has been extended to different loss functions in
Nacson et al. 2019b
, to SGD in
Nacson et al. 2019c
, AdaGrad in
Qian & Qian 2019
, (S)GD with momentum, deterministic Adam and stochastic RMSProp in
Wang et al. 2022
, more generic optimization methods in
Gunasekar et al. 2018a
, to the nonseparable case in
Ji & Telgarsky 2018b
,
Ji & Telgarsky 2019
. This line of research has been generalized to studying implicit biases of linear networks
(
Ji & Telgarsky 2018a
;
Gunasekar et al. 2018b
)
, homogeneous neural networks
(
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
;
Nacson et al. 2019a
;
Lyu & Li 2019
)
.
Woodworth et al. 2020
study the gradient flow of a diagonal linear network with squared loss and show that large initializations lead to minimum two-norm solutions while small initializations lead to minimum one-norm solutions.
Even et al. 2023
extend this work to the case of non-zero step sizes and mini-batch training.
Wang et al. 2021
prove that Adam and RMSProp maximize the margin of homogeneous neural networks. Our perspective on the implicit bias is different since we are considering a generic loss function without any assumptions on the network architecture.
Beneventano 2023
proves that in expectation over batch sampling the trajectory of SGD without replacement differs from that of SGD with replacement by an additional step on a regularizer. As opposed to the work on backward error analysis for SGD discussed above, they do not assume the largest eigenvalue of the hessian to be bounded.

Generalization of adaptive methods.

Cohen et al. 2022
investigate the edge-of-stability regime of adaptive gradient algorithms and the effect of sharpness (the largest eigenvalue of the hessian) on generalization.
Granziol 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
observe that adaptive methods find sharper minima than SGD and
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Xie et al. 2022
argue theoretically that it is the case.
Jiang et al. 2022
introduce a statistic that measures the uniformity of the hessian diagonal and argue that adaptive gradient algorithms are biased towards making this statistic smaller.
Keskar & Socher 2017
propose to improve generalization of adaptive methods by switching to SGD in the middle of training.

1.2
Notation

We denote the loss of the
k
th minibatch as a function of the network parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
by
E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and in the full-batch setting we omit the index and write
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
\nabla E
means the gradient of
E
, and
\nabla
with indices denotes partial derivatives, e. g.
\nabla_{ijs}E
is a shortcut for
\frac{\partial^{3}E}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}\partial\theta_{s}}
. The norm notation without indices
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\cdot}}\right\|
is the two-norm of a vector,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\cdot}}\right\|_{1}
is the one-norm and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\cdot}}\right\|_{1,\varepsilon}
is the perturbed one-norm defined as
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{v}}}\right\|_{1,\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sqrt{v_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon}
. (Of course, if
\varepsilon>0
the perturbed one-norm is not really a norm, but taking
\varepsilon=0
makes it the one-norm.) For a real number
a
the floor
\lfloor a\rfloor
is the largest integer not exceeding
a
.

To provide the names and notations for hyperparameters, we define the algorithm below.

Definition 1.1
.

The
Adam
algorithm
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
is an optimization algorithm with numerical stability hyperparameter
\varepsilon>0
, squared gradient momentum hyperparameter
\rho\in(0,1)
, gradient momentum hyperparameter
\beta\in(0,1)
, initialization
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\mathbf{m}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and the following update rule: for each
n\geq 0
,
j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,p}}\right\}

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\rho\nu_{j}^{(n)}+(1-\rho)\big(\nabla_{j}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)})\big)^{2},

\displaystyle m_{j}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\beta m_{j}^{(n)}+(1-\beta)\nabla_{j}E_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}),

\displaystyle\theta_{j}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\theta_{j}^{(n)}-h\frac{m_{j}^{(n+1)}/(1-\beta^{n+1})}{\sqrt{\nu_{j}^{(n+1)}/(1-\rho^{n+1})+\varepsilon}}.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that the numerical stability hyperparameter
\varepsilon>0
, which is introduced in these algorithms to avoid division by zero, is inside the square root in our definition. This way we avoid division by zero in the derivative too: the first derivative of
x\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{x+\varepsilon}}}\right)^{-1}
is bounded for
x\geq 0
. This is useful for our analysis. In
Theorems

B.4
and
D.4
, the original versions of RMSProp and Adam are also tackled, though with an additional assumption which requires that no component of the gradient can come very close to zero in the region of interest. This is true only for the initial period of learning (whereas
Theorem

3.1
tackles the whole period). Practitioners do not seem to make a distinction between the version with
\varepsilon
inside vs. outside the square root: tutorials with both versions abound on machine learning related websites. Moreover, the popular
Tensorflow
and
Optax
variants of RMSProp have
\varepsilon
inside the square root. Empirically we also observed that moving
\varepsilon
inside or outside the square root does not change the behavior of Adam or RMSProp qualitatively.

2
Implicit Bias of Full-Batch Adam: an Informal Summary

We are ready to describe our theoretical result (
Theorem

3.1
below) in the full-batch special case. Assume
E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the loss, whose partial derivatives up to the fourth order are bounded. Let
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\}
be iterations of Adam as defined in
Definition

1.1
. We find an ODE whose solution trajectory
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)
is
h^{2}
-close to
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\}
, meaning that for any time horizon
T>0
there is a constant
C
such that for any step size
h\in(0,T)
we have
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(nh)-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\|\leq Ch^{2}
(for
n
between
0
and
\lfloor T/h\rfloor
). The ODE is written the following way (up to terms that rapidly go to zero as
n
grows): for the component number
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}

\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t)=-\frac{\nabla_{j}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)+\text{correction}_{j}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{\bigl|\nabla_{j}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\bigr|^{2}+\varepsilon}}

(2)

with initial conditions
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}(0)=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}^{(0)}
for all
j
, where the correction term is

\text{correction}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
:=\frac{h}{2}\biggl\{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}-\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{|\nabla_{j}E(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon}\biggr\}\\
\times\nabla_{j}\big\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\|_{1,\varepsilon}.

(3)

Depending on hyperparameters and the training stage, the correction term can take two extreme forms listed below. The reality is in between, but typically much closer to the first case.

•

If
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is
small
compared to all components of
\nabla E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)
, i. e.
\min_{j}\bigl|\nabla_{j}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\bigr|\gg\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, which
is usually the case during most of the training
, then we can write

\text{correction}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\frac{h}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}-\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}}}\right\}\nabla_{j}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}.

(4)

For small
\varepsilon
, the perturbed one-norm is indistinguishable from the usual one-norm, and for
\beta>\rho
it is penalized (in much the same way as the squared two-norm is implicitly penalized in the case of GD), but for the typical case
\rho>\beta
its decrease is actually hindered by this term (so the bias is
anti
-regularization). The ODE in (
2
) approximately becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t)=-\frac{\nabla_{j}\widetilde{E}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)}{\big|\nabla_{j}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\big|},\quad\text{with}

(5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=E(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\underbrace{\frac{h}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}-\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}}}\right\}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1}}_{\text{implicit {anti-regularization} (if $\rho>\beta$)}}.

•

If
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is
large
compared to all gradient components, i. e.
\max_{j}\big|\nabla_{j}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\big|\ll\sqrt{\varepsilon}
(which may happen during the late learning stage, or if non-standard hyperparameter values are chosen), the fraction in (
3
) with
\varepsilon
in the numerator approaches one, the dependence on
\rho
cancels out, and

\big\|\nabla E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\big\|_{1,\varepsilon}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigl(1+\bigl|\nabla_{i}E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\bigr|^{2}/(2\varepsilon)\bigr)\\
=p\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\bigl\|\nabla E\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}.

(6)

In other words,
\|\cdot\|_{1,\varepsilon}
becomes
\|\cdot\|^{2}/(2\sqrt{\varepsilon})

up to an additive constant, giving

\displaystyle\text{correction}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\approx\bigl(4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bigr)^{-1}(1-\beta)^{-1}(1+\beta)\nabla_{j}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}.

The form of the ODE in this case is

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t)=-\nabla_{j}\widetilde{E}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr),\quad\text{with}

(7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\biggl(E(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{h}{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|^{2}\biggr).

1
. In
Figure

, we use the one-dimensional (
p=1
) case to illustrate what kind of term is being implicitly penalized.

Usually,
\varepsilon
is chosen to be small, and during most of the training Adam is much better described by the first extreme case.
It is clear from (
5
) that, if
\rho>\beta
, the correction term provides the opposite of regularization, in contrast to (
1
). The larger
\rho
compared to
\beta
, the stronger the anti-regularization effect is.

This finding may partially explain why adaptive gradient methods have been reported to generalize worse than non-adaptive ones
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Wilson et al. 2017
)
,
as it offers a previously unknown perspective on why they are biased towards “higher-curvature” regions and find “sharper” minima.
Indeed, note that standard (non-adaptive)
\ell_{\infty}
-sharpness at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be defined by
\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq r}E(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-E(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for some radius
r
.
This or similar definitions have been considered often in literature, see, e. g.,
Andriushchenko et al. 2023
,
Foret et al. 2021
. Replacing the difference of the losses with its first-order approximation under the maximum
(
Foret et al. 2021
;
Ghosh et al. 2023
)

\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq r}E(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\approx\max_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{\infty}\leq r}\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\delta}=r\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1},

we see that Adam typically anti-penalizes the approximation of
\ell_{\infty}
-sharpness.
Although the connection between sharpness and generalization is not clear-cut
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2023
)
, our empirical results (
Section

5
)
are consistent with this theory.

This overview also applies to RMSProp by setting
\beta=0
; see
Theorem

C.4
for the formal result.

Example 2.1

(Backward Error Analysis for GD with Heavy-ball Momentum)
.

Assume
\varepsilon
is large compared to all squared gradient components during the whole training process, so that the form of the ODE is approximated by (
7
). Since Adam with a large
\varepsilon
and after a certain number of iterations approximates SGD with heavy-ball momentum with step size
h(1-\beta)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, a linear step size change (and corresponding time change) gives exactly the equations in Theorem 4.1 of
Ghosh et al. 2023
. Taking
\beta=0
(no momentum), we get the implicit regularization of GD from
Barrett & Dherin 2021
.

3
Main Result: ODE Approximating Mini-Batch Adam

We only make one assumption, which is standard in the literature: the loss
E_{k}
for each mini-batch is 4 times continuously differentiable, and partial derivatives of
E_{k}
up to order 4 are bounded, i. e. there is a positive constant
M
such that for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in the region of interest

\sup_{k}\biggl\{\sup_{i}|\nabla_{i}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\vee\sup_{i,j}|\nabla_{ij}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\\
\vee\sup_{i,j,s}|\nabla_{ijs}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\vee\sup_{i,j,s,r}|\nabla_{ijsr}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\biggr\}\leq M.

(8)

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume (
8
) holds. Let
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\}
be iterations of Adam as defined in
Definition

1.1
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)
be the continuous solution to the piecewise ODE

\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t)=-\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t))}{R^{(n)}_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)}\\
+h\biggl(\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\big(2P_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)+\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)\big)}{2R_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)^{3}}\\
-\frac{2L_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)+\bar{L}_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)}{2R_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)}\biggr)

(9)

for
t\in[nh,(n+1)h]
with the initial condition
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
, where

\displaystyle R^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\right)^{2}}{1-\rho^{n+1}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\beta}}\right)\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)}{1-\beta^{n+1}},

\displaystyle L_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{n-k}(1-\beta)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M^{(l)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{i}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{n-k}(1-\beta)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{M^{(n)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{i}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\nabla_{j}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M^{(l)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{i}^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\nabla_{j}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{M^{(n)}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{i}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

Then, for any fixed positive time horizon
T>0
there exists a constant
C
(depending on

,
\rho
,
\beta
,
\varepsilon
) such that for any step size

we have
\big\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(nh)-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}\big\|\leq Ch^{2}
for
n\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,\ldots,\lfloor T/h\rfloor}}\right\}
.

Remark 3.2
.

In the
full-batch
setting

, the terms above simplify to

\displaystyle R_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(|\nabla_{j}E(\boldsymbol{\theta})|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{1/2},

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{j}E(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

\displaystyle L_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}-\frac{(n+1)\beta^{n+1}}{1-\beta^{n+1}}}}\right]\bar{L}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{j}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon},

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{(n+1)\rho^{n+1}}{1-\rho^{n+1}}}}\right]\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{j}E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\nabla_{j}\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{1,\varepsilon}.

If the iteration number

is large, (

Derivation sketch.

The proof is in the appendix (this is
Theorem

E.4
; see
Appendix

A
for the overview of the appendix). To help the reader understand how the ODE (
9
) is obtained, apart from the full proof, we include an informal derivation in
Appendix

I
, and provide an even briefer sketch of this derivation here.

Our goal is to find such a trajectory
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)
that, denoting
t_{n}:=nh
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-h\frac{T_{\beta,j}^{(n)}}{\sqrt{T_{\rho,j}^{(n)}}}+O(h^{3})\quad\text{with}

(10)

\displaystyle T_{\beta,j}^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\beta}}\right)\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr),

\displaystyle T_{\rho,j}^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\bigl(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr)\bigr)^{2}+\varepsilon.

Ignoring the terms of order higher than one in
h
, we can take a first-order approximation for granted:
\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-hA^{(n)}_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n}))+O(h^{2})
with
A^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=M_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/R_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
The challenge is to make this more precise by finding an equality of the form

\tilde{\theta}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+1}}}\right)=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})\\
-hA^{(n)}_{j}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+h^{2}B^{(n)}_{j}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+O(h^{3}),

(11)

where
B^{(n)}_{j}(\cdot)
is a known function. This is a numerical iteration to which standard backward error analysis (Chapter IX in
Ernst Hairer & Wanner 2006
) can be applied.

Using the Taylor series, we can write

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})\bigr)=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\bigl\{\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t_{n-1})-\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t_{n})\bigr\}+O(h^{2})

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\frac{M_{i}^{(n-1)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})\bigr)}{R_{i}^{(n-1)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})\bigr)}+O(h^{2})

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\frac{M_{i}^{(n-1)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}{R_{i}^{(n-1)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}+O(h^{2}),

where in the last equality we just replaced
t_{n-1}
with
t_{n}
in the
h
-term since it only affects higher-order terms. Doing this again for steps
n-2
,
n-3
,
\ldots
, and adding the resulting equations will give for
k<n

\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr)=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\\
+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M_{i}^{(l)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}{R_{i}^{(l)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}+O(h^{2}),

where we could safely ignore that
n-k
is not bounded because of exponential averaging. Taking the square of this formal power series in
h
, multiplying this square by
\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)
and summing up over
k
will give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\bigl[\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr)\bigr]^{2}+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad=R_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)^{2}+2hP_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+O(h^{2}),

which, using the expression for the inverse square root
\bigl(\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{r}h^{r}\bigr)^{-1/2}
of a formal power series
\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{r}h^{r}
, gives us an expansion

\frac{1}{\sqrt{T^{(n)}_{\rho,j}}}=\frac{1}{R_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}-h\frac{P_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)^{3}}+O(h^{2}).

A similar process provides an expansion for
T^{(n)}_{\beta,j}
:

T^{(n)}_{\beta,j}=M_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+hL_{j}^{(n)}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+O(h^{2}).

Inserting these two expansions into (
10
) leads to an expression for
B^{(n)}_{j}(\cdot)
:

B^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})P_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{3}}-\frac{L_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{R_{j}^{(n)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

We are now ready to find an ODE for
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
of the form
\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)\bigr)
whose discretization is (
11
). This is a task for standard backward error analysis: expand
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(\cdot)
into
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+h\mathbf{f}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+O(h^{2})
. By Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n+1})=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})+h\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t_{n}^{+})+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\ddot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t_{n}^{+})+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle\quad=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})+h\bigl[\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+h\mathbf{f}_{1}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+O(h^{2})\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\bigl[\nabla\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+O(h)\bigr]+O(h^{3})

\displaystyle\quad=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})+h\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+h^{2}\biggl[\mathbf{f}_{1}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)+\frac{\nabla\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)\mathbf{f}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})\bigr)}{2}\biggr]+O(h^{3}).

It is left to equate the terms before the corresponding powers of
h
here and in (
11
), giving
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-A^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
f_{1,j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{i}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+B^{(n)}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Omitting some algebra, the piecewise ODE (
9
) is derived.

6
Limitations and Future Directions

As far as we know, the assumption similar to (
8
) is explicitly or implicitly present in all previous work on backward error analysis of gradient-based machine learning algorithms. (Recently,
Beneventano 2023
weakened this assumption for SGD without replacement, but their focus is somewhat different.) There is evidence that large-batch algorithms often operate near or at the edge of stability
(
Cohen et al. 2021
;
Cohen et al. 2022
)
, in which the largest eigenvalue of the hessian can be large, making it unclear whether the higher-order partial derivatives can safely be assumed bounded near optimality. In addition, as
Smith et al. 2021
point out, in the mini-batch setting backward error analysis can be more accurate. We leave a qualitative analysis of the behavior of first-order terms in
Theorem

3.1
in the mini-batch case as a future direction.

Relatedly, Adam does not always generalize worse than SGD: for transformers, Adam often outperforms
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2022
)
. Moreover, for NLP tasks a long time can be spent training close to an interpolating solution. Our analysis suggests that in the latter regime the anti-regularization effect disappears, which does indeed confirm the finding that generalization can be better. However, we believe this explanation is not complete, and more work is needed to connect the implicit bias to the training dynamics of transformers.

In addition, the constant
C
in
Theorem

3.1
goes to infinity as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. Theoretically, our proof does not exclude the case where for very small
\varepsilon
the trajectory of the piecewise ODE is only close to the Adam trajectory for small, suboptimal learning rates, at least at later stages of learning. (For the initial learning period, this is not a problem.) It appears to also be true of Proposition 1 in
Ma et al. 2022
(zeroth-order approximation by sign-GD). This is especially noticeable in the large-spike regime of training (see
Section

5
) which, despite being obviously unstable, can still lead to acceptable test errors. It would be worthwhile to investigate this regime in detail.

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Theorem B.4

(RMSProp with
\varepsilon
outside: global error bound)
.

Suppose

holds, and

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}}\right)}}\right)^{2}\geq R^{2}

for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
defined in

. Then there exist positive constants

,
d_{2}
,
d_{3}
such that for all

where
\mathbf{e}_{n}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}
. The constants can be defined as

\displaystyle d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}:=C_{\ref{localerrorbound}},

\displaystyle d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1+\frac{M_{2}\sqrt{p}}{R+\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{R(R+\varepsilon)}+1}}\right)d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}}}\right]\sqrt{p},

\displaystyle d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}:=C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}.

Proof.

We will show this by induction over
n
, the same way an analogous bound is shown in
Ghosh et al. 2023
.

The base case is
n=0
. Indeed,
\mathbf{e}_{0}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(0)-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
. Then the
j
th component of
\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{0}
is

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{0}}}\right]_{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{e}_{1}}}\right]_{j}=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{1})-\theta_{j}^{(0)}+\frac{h\nabla_{j}E_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}}\right)}{\sqrt{(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}}}\right)}}\right)^{2}}+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\quad=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{1})-\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{0})+\frac{h\nabla_{j}E_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{0})}}\right)}{\sqrt{(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{0})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}}+\varepsilon}.

By
Theorem

B.3
, the absolute value of the right-hand side does not exceed

, which means
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{0}}}\right\|\leq C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}h^{3}\sqrt{p}
. Since
C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}\sqrt{p}\leq d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}
, the base case is proven.

Now suppose that for all
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
the claim

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{k}}}\right\|\leq d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}kh}h^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{k+1}-\mathbf{e}_{k}}}\right\|\leq d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}kh}h^{3}

is proven. Then

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{n}}}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(\char 97\relax)}}}{{\leq}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{n-1}}}\right\|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathbf{e}_{n}-\mathbf{e}_{n-1}}}\right\|\leq d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}(n-1)h}h^{2}+d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}(n-1)h}h^{3}

\displaystyle\quad=d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}(n-1)h}h^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}}{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}}h}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(\char 98\relax)}}}{{\leq}}d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}(n-1)h}h^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}h}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(\char 99\relax)}}}{{\leq}}d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}(n-1)h}h^{2}\cdot e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}h}=d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}nh}h^{2},

where (a) is by the triangle inequality, (b) is by
d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-ddd}}/d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}\leq d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}
, in (c) we used

for all
x\geq 0
.

Next, combining
Theorem

B.3
with (
12
), we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbf{e}_{n+1}-\mathbf{e}_{n}}}\right]_{j}}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{localerrorbound}}h^{3}+h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{\sqrt{A}+\varepsilon}-\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}}}\right)}{\sqrt{B}+\varepsilon}}}\right|,

(14)

where to simplify notation we put

Using

, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}+\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}+\varepsilon}}}\right|=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A-B}}\right|}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{A}+\varepsilon}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{B}+\varepsilon}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}}}\right)}\leq\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|A-B}}\right|}{2R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}}.

But since

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}}\right)}}\right)^{2}}}\right|

Combining (
15
) and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{\sqrt{A}+\varepsilon}-\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}}}\right)}{\sqrt{B}+\varepsilon}}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{M_{1}^{2}M_{2}\sqrt{p}}{R(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}}}\right\|+\frac{M_{2}\sqrt{p}}{R+\varepsilon}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(n)}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(\char 97\relax)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{M_{1}^{2}M_{2}\sqrt{p}}{R(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}kh}h^{2}+\frac{M_{2}\sqrt{p}}{R+\varepsilon}d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-d}}e^{d_{\ref{rmsprop-outside-dd}}nh}h^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2R^{3}(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{\vphantom{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{b}+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\sqrt{b}}\sqrt{a}+\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\vphantom{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{b}+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\sqrt{b}}\sqrt{b}+\varepsilon}+\frac{c}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{b}+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\sqrt{b}}}}\right|\leq\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2R^{3}(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a-b-c}}\right|}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{b}+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}\sqrt{b}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2R^{3}(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a-b-c}}\right|}{2R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}}.

Apply this with

\displaystyle a:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle b:=R^{(n)}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle c:=2hP^{(n)}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)

and use bounds

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a-b}}\right|\leq 2M_{1}C_{\ref{e-first-der}}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}h,\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a-b-c}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{secondorderdif}}(\rho)h^{2}

by Lemma
G.4
.
∎

We are finally ready to prove
Theorem

B.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

B.3
.

By (
42
) and (
43
), the first derivative of the function

t\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2P_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}}\right)}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)\frac{\nabla_{i}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}{R_{i}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\varepsilon}}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{j}^{(n)}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t))+\varepsilon}}\right)}}}\right)

is bounded in absolute value by a positive constant
C_{27}=C_{\ref{der-huge-func-first-term}}+C_{\ref{der-huge-func-second-term}}
. By (
13
), this means

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ddot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}{R^{(n)}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\varepsilon}}}\right)}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{nth-step-modified-h-term-der-bound}}h.

Combining this with

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})-\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n}^{+}}}\right)h-\frac{\ddot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n}^{+}}}\right)}{2}h^{2}}}\right|\leq\frac{D_{3}}{6}

by Taylor expansion, we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})-\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n}^{+}}}\right)h+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}{R^{(n)}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\varepsilon}}}\right)}}\right|_{t=t_{n}^{+}}}}\right|

(61)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{D_{3}}{6}+\frac{C_{\ref{nth-step-modified-h-term-der-bound}}}{2}}}\right)h^{3}.

Using

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}(t_{n})+\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon}}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{paramsc-der-first-order-bound}}h

with
C_{28}
defined as

C_{\ref{paramsc-der-first-order-bound}}:=\frac{M_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2C_{\ref{p-bound}}+C_{\ref{p-bar-bound}}}}\right)}{2(R+\varepsilon)^{2}R}+\frac{pM_{1}M_{2}}{2(R+\varepsilon)^{2}}

by (
13
), and calculating the derivative, it is easy to show

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)}{R^{(n)}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)}}\right)+\varepsilon}}}\right)}}\right|_{t=t_{n}^{+}}-\mathrm{FrDer}}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{frderhbound}}h

(62)

for a positive constant
C_{29}
, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{FrDer}:=\frac{\mathrm{FrDerNum}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{FrDerNum}:=\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\bar{P}_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon}}\right)R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\frac{\nabla_{i}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{i}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle C_{\ref{frderhbound}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{pM_{2}}{R+\varepsilon}+\frac{M_{1}^{2}M_{2}p}{(R+\varepsilon)^{2}R}}}\right\}C_{\ref{paramsc-der-first-order-bound}}.

61
) and (

), by the triangle inequality

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})-\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-\dot{\tilde{\theta}}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n}^{+}}}\right)h+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathrm{FrDer}}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{D_{3}}{6}+\frac{C_{\ref{nth-step-modified-h-term-der-bound}}+C_{\ref{frderhbound}}}{2}}}\right)h^{3},

which, using (

), is rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})-\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})+h\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon}-h^{2}\frac{\nabla_{j}E_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)P_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}}}\right|

Appendix I
Adam with
\varepsilon
Inside the Square Root: Informal Derivation

Our goal is to find such a trajectory
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)
that

\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n+1})=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-h\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\beta^{n-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\beta}}\right)\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr)/(1-\beta^{n+1})}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\bigl(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})\bigr)\bigr)^{2}/(1-\rho^{n+1})+\varepsilon}}+O(h^{3}).

Result I.1
.

For
n\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1,2,\ldots}}\right\}
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+1}}}\right)=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-h\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}

(64)

\displaystyle+h^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)P_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)^{3}}-\frac{L_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{3}}}\right).

Derivation.

We take

\tilde{\theta}_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{n+1}}}\right)=\tilde{\theta}_{j}(t_{n})-h\frac{M_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

for granted. Using this and the Taylor series, we can write

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t_{n-1})-\tilde{\theta}_{i}(t_{n})}}\right\}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\frac{M_{j}^{(n-1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n-1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\frac{M_{j}^{(n-1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n-1)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right),

where in the last equality we just replaced
t_{n-1}
with
t_{n}
in the
h
-term since it only affects higher-order terms. Now doing this again for step
n-1
instead of step
n
, we will have

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-2})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)\frac{M_{j}^{(n-2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n-2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n-1})}}\right)\frac{M_{j}^{(n-2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n-2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right),

where in the last equality we again replaced
t_{n-1}
with
t_{n}
since it only affects higher-order terms. Proceeding like this and adding the resulting equations, we have for
n\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1,\ldots}}\right\}
,
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,\ldots,n-1}}\right\}
that

\displaystyle\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+h\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right),

where we ignored the fact that
n-k
is not bounded (we will get away with this because of exponential averaging). Hence, taking the square of this formal power series,

\displaystyle\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}=\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+h\cdot 2\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right).

Summing up over
k
, we have

\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon=R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)^{2}+2hP_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right),

which, using the expression for the inverse square root
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{r}h^{r}}}\right)^{-1/2}
of a formal power series
\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}a_{r}h^{r}
, gives us

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-\rho^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{n-k}(1-\rho)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)}}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon}}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}-h\frac{P_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)^{3}}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1-\beta)\beta^{n-k}\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{k})}}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1-\beta)\beta^{n-k}\nabla_{j}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{h}{1-\beta^{n+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(1-\beta)\beta^{n-k}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\nabla_{ij}E_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)\sum_{l=k}^{n-1}\frac{M_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}{R_{i}^{(l)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=M_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+hL_{j}^{(n)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_{n})}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right).
