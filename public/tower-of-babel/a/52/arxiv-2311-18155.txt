The ω \omega -limit set in a positively invariant compact region and a new description of the Lorenz attractor
Khalil Zare, Steven R. Chesley

Abstract
The ω \omega -limit set in a compact positively invariant region R ⊂ ℝ n R\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} has been identified for n = 1 n=1 , 2, and 3, with examples in each case. It has been shown that the ω \omega -limit set becomes more complex as n n increases from 1 to 3, and we expect this to also be true for n > 3 n>3 . Our example for n = 3 n=3 is the Lorenz equations , for which we have shown that its ω \omega -limit set is a twisted torus .



I
Introduction

A solution of differential equations may be considered as a
flow
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If all the flows in or entering a region
R
remain in
R
indefinitely as
t\to\infty
then
R
is called a
positively invariant region
.

The
\omega
-limit set
introduced by
Birkhoff 1927
consists of those flows in
R
that attract the other flows in
R
as
t\to\infty
. Examples are asymptotically stable equilibria and limit cycles.

In addition, if
R
is
compact
(i.e., closed and bounded) then the
\omega
-limit set of a flow in
R
is
non-empty
,
compact
,
invariant
, and
connected
(see Theorem
1
in Sec.
II
).

In this paper, we show that in a compact, positively-invariant region
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
\omega
-limit set can contain

(i)

only
equilibria
if
n=1
,

(ii)

equilibria
,
limit cycles
, and
heteroclinic
(
homoclinic
) orbits if
n=2

(iii)

equilibria
,
limit cycles
,
heteroclinic
(
homoclinic
) orbits, and a surface topologically equivalent to a
single or multiple torus
if
n=3
.

Notice that the
\omega
-limit set becomes more complex as
n
increases from 1 to 3. We expect this to be also true for
n>3
, which is not treated in this paper, but left for future studies.

It follows from (ii) that if the region
R
does not contain any equilibrium then there is a limit cycle in
R
. This is an alternative proof of the
Poincaré-Bendixson Theorem
.

It follows from (iii) that a lack of an equilibrium in
R
does not necessarily imply the existence of a limit cycle since the solutions may approach the surface of a torus. This explains why the Poincaré-Bendixson Theorem cannot be extended to three dimensions.

To give a three dimensional example, we chose the Lorenz equations concerning the stability of fluid flows in the atmosphere. Using numerical integration, we have shown that the
\omega
-limit set in this case is a twisted torus.

II
The
\omega
-limit set in a compact positively invariant region

Let
R
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\vec{f}:R\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

be a function for which

\frac{d\vec{y}}{dt}=\vec{f}(\vec{y}),\;\vec{y}(0)=\vec{y}_{0}\in R

(1)

has a unique solution
\vec{\Phi}(t,\vec{y}_{0})
that is defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Under these assumptions, it follows that

\vec{\Phi}(t+\tau,\vec{y}_{0})=\vec{\Phi}(t,\vec{\Phi}(\tau,\vec{y}_{0})).

(2)

Definition
: The
\omega
-limit set of
\vec{y}_{0}\in R
is the set

L_{\omega}(\vec{y}_{0})=\{\vec{y}\in R:\text{there exists an unbounded}\\
\text{sequence}\ \{t_{k}\}\ \text{such that}\lim\ \vec{\Phi}(t_{k},\vec{y}_{o})=\vec{y}\}.

To discuss the
\omega
-limit set, we use the following theorem from analysis:

Theorem 1
.

If the region
R
is compact (i.e., closed and bounded) and positively-invariant, then the
\omega
-limit set of a trajectory in
R
is non-empty, compact, invariant, and connected.

Proof.

For a point
\vec{y}\in R
the sequence
\vec{\Phi}(n,\vec{y})
is contained in the compact set R. By the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, this sequence contains a convergent sub-sequence whose limit is, by definition, an element of
L_{\omega}(\vec{y})
. The compactness of
L_{\omega}(\vec{y})
follows from the fact that all closed subsets of a compact set are also compact.

To verify that
L_{\omega}(\vec{y})
is connected, we assume, by contradiction, the existence of two open sets
U
and
V
such that

\displaystyle L_{\omega}(\vec{y})\cap U\neq\varnothing

\displaystyle L_{\omega}(\vec{y})\cap V\neq\varnothing

\displaystyle L_{\omega}(\vec{y})\subset U\cup V.

Let
\{t_{k}\}\subset[0,\infty)
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}=\infty
, with
\Phi(t_{2k},y)\in U
and
\Phi(t_{2k+1},y)\in V
, for each
k
.

Now the continuous curve

\{\Phi(t,y):t_{2k}<t<t_{2k+1}\}

connects a point in
U
with a point in
V
and there must be a time
\tau\in(t_{2k},t_{2k+1})
such that

\Phi(\tau,y)\in R\setminus(U\cup V).

By compactness of
R\setminus(U\cup V)
the sequence
\Phi(\tau,y)
contains a sub-sequence whose limit is not in
U\cup V
.
∎

Remark 1
.

In the absence of compactness and positive invariance, the conclusions in Theorem
1
fail to hold. For examples, in the ternary Cantor set, Smale horseshoe map, and the Poincare’ map of the isosceles three-body problem
(
Zare & Chesley 1998
)
,
R is not positively invariant and Theorem 1 does not apply.

III
The
\omega
-limit set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\leq 3

III.1
Possibilities with
n=1
dimension

This is a trivial case where the compact region
R
is a closed interval and the
\omega
-limit set in
R
is either a point (i.e., an equilibrium) or a closed sub-interval
R_{1}
of
R
. By replacing
R
by
R_{1}
, we have either an equilibrium in
R_{1}
or a closed sub-interval
R_{2}
of
R_{1}
. By repeating this argument we conclude that the
\omega
-limit set in
\mathbb{R}
are
equilibrium points
.

Example 1
.

Let

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=-y^{5}+15y^{4}-85y^{3}+225y^{2}-274y+120

\displaystyle=(1-y)(2-y)(3-y)(4-y)(5-y)

and
R=[0,6]
. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the
\omega
-limit set in
R
are the three stable equilibria at
y=1
,
3
, and
5
.

.

III.2
Possibilities with
n=2
dimensions

In this case the compact and positively-invariant region
R
is a simply or multiply
connected area
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 2
.

The
\omega
-limit set in a compact and positively invariant region
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
can include only

1.

equilibrium points,

2.

periodic orbits (i.e., limit cycles), and

3.

heteroclinic or homoclinic orbits

Proof.

The
\omega
-limit set of a trajectory in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
nonempty
,
compact
,
invariant
and
connected
(see Theorem
1
).The compact and connected sets in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are

a)

points

b)

closed cycles made of regular points,

c)

closed cycles made of regular and irregular points, and

d)

a simply or multiply connected area
R_{1}\subset R
.

However, in the case of d), by replacing
R
with
R_{1}
, we have in
R_{1}
the cases a), b) and c) or an area
R_{2}
. Repeating this argument leads to the conclusion that the compact and connected sets in
R
are only a), b), and c). Since the
\omega
-limit set is also invariant, a), b), and c) are equivalent to 1, 2 and 3 in the statement of the theorem.
∎

Corollary 2.1
.

If there is no equilibrium in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
then there exists a limit cycle in
R
.

Proof.

If there is no equilibrium in
R
then cases 1 and 3 in Theorem
2
are not possible and the only possibility is case 2, indicating the existence of a limit cycle.
∎

Remark 2
.

Note that Corollary
2.1
is the well known Poincaré-Bendixon theorem, and Theorem
2
leads to a different proof of this result.

There are many physical examples in two dimensions. To mention a few, see the Van der Pol equation
(
van der Pol 1920
)
model for an electrical network and the Brusselator model
(
Prigogine & Lefever 1968
)
for a chemical reaction.

III.3
Possibilities with
n=3
dimensions

Here the compact and positively-invariant region
R
is a simply or multiply
connected volume
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In order to identify the
\omega
-limit set in
R
we use the following
classification theorem
for orientable surfaces.

Theorem 3

(Möbius theorem
(
Möbius 1863
)
)
.

Every orientable surface is topologically equivalent to a sphere, a torus or a multiple torus (double, triple, etc.).

Although there are more advanced classification theorems that include nonorientable surfaces
(
Francis & Weeks 1999
)
, the above Möbius theorem is sufficient for our purpose.

Remark 3
.

The
\omega
-limit set on a sphere is the same as the
\omega
-limit set in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
given in Theorem
2
, i.e., the Euler numbers for a sphere and for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the same, specifically, the Euler number is 2. However, the Euler number of a torus is zero, allowing orbits covering it densely with no equilibrium and no limit cycle.

Theorem 4
.

The
\omega
-limit set in a compact and positively invariant region
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
can include only

1.

equilibrium points,

2.

periodic orbits (i.e., limit cycles),

3.

heteroclinic or homoclinic orbits, and

4.

a surface topologically equivalent to a torus (single or multiple).

Proof.

The
\omega
-limit set of a trajectory in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is
nonempty
,
compact
,
invariant
and
connected
(see Theorem
1
).The compact and connected sets in
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
are

a)

points

b)

closed cycles made of regular points,

c)

closed cycles made of regular and irregular points,

d)

surfaces topologically equivalent to a torus (single or multiple), and

e)

a simply or multiply connected region
R_{1}\subset R
.

However, in the case of e), by replacing
R
by
R_{1}
, in
R_{1}
we have cases a), b), c), d) and a region
R_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Repeating this argument leads to the conclusion that the compact and connected sets in
R
are a), b), c) and d). Since the
\omega
-limit set is also invariant, a), b), c) and d) are equivalent to 1, 2, 3 and 4 in the statement of the theorem.
∎

To give an example in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we chose the well-known Lorenz equations discussed in the next section.

IV
The Lorenz attractor

A three-dimensional problem in meteorology that has been intensively studied is the Lorenz system of equations
(
Lorenz 1963
)
, which are given by

\frac{dx}{dt}=10(y-x);\qquad\frac{dy}{dt}=rx-y-xz;\qquad\frac{dz}{dt}=-\frac{8}{3}z+xy\ .

(3)

The Lorenz equations model a layer of the atmosphere that is warmer at the bottom than at the top. The variables
x
,
y
, and
z
are related, respectively, to the intensity of the air motion, the temperature variations in the horizontal and in the vertical directions of the atmospheric layer. The parameter
r>0
is proportional to the temperature difference between the bottom and the top of the atmospheric layer. If
0<r<1
then the origin is the only equilibrium and it is a
stable node
.

At
r=1
, the system exhibits a
pitchfork bifurcation
, i.e., the origin
\mathcal{O}
becomes unstable and two new stable nodes are formed for
r>1
at

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=(-\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}(r-1)},\ -\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}(r-1)},\ r-1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=(+\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}(r-1)},\ +\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}(r-1)},\ r-1).

For
1<r<24.737
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are stable equilibrium points, and they are the
\omega
-limit set of the Lorenz flow. For
r>24.737
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
become unstable and a new
\omega
-limit set is formed, which for
r=28
has been obtained using numerical integration by
Lorenz 1963
. For a detailed discussion see
Sparrow 2012
.

To obtain a compact and positively invariant region
R
for this problem, let
R
be the interior of the sphere

c^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}+(z-38)^{2}\ .

It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}c^{2}

\displaystyle=2x\frac{dx}{dt}+2y\frac{dy}{dt}+2(z-38)\frac{dz}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{16}{3}\left[z^{2}-38z+\frac{3}{8}(10x^{2}+y^{2})\right]\ .

The ellipsoidal surface for which
\frac{dc^{2}}{dt}=0
can be written

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{2}}{\left(\frac{8(19)^{2}}{30}\right)}+\frac{y^{2}}{\left(\frac{8(19)^{2}}{3}\right)}+\frac{(z-19)^{2}}{19^{2}}

Within the ellipsoid (Fig.
2
),
\frac{dc^{2}}{dt}
is positive, while it is negative outside. But for
c\gtrsim 40
the surface for
\frac{dc^{2}}{dt}=0
is fully enclosed within the sphere defining
R
, and so we conclude that
\frac{dc^{2}}{dt}<0
for any point on the sphere. Hence
R
is a
positively invariant region
. The
\omega
-limit set obtained numerically is shown in Fig.
3
.

Figure 2:

\frac{dc^{2}}{dt}
is negative outside (and positive inside) the depicted ellipsoid centered at
(0,0,19)
and having semimajor axes
(\sqrt{\frac{2888}{30}},\sqrt{\frac{2888}{3}},19)\simeq(9.8,31.0,19)
.

Figure 3:
The
\omega
-limit set of the Lorenz flow using the initial condition
(x,y,z)=(5,5,5)
. Three two-dimensional projections are shown and an oblique three-dimensional representation. Equilibria
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{O}
are marked by black dots.

We note that the equilibria
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{O}
are in
R
, but not on the
\omega
-limit set in Fig.
3
. We also observe that the numerically-obtained
\omega
-limit set shown in Fig.
3
resembles a twisted torus or a double torus. However, a double torus has an Euler number of -2, which implies it must have equilibrium points on it, but such equilibria do not exist. On the other hand, the Euler number of a twisted torus is zero, which allows no equilibria on it. This argument based on the Euler numbers leads to the conclusion that the
\omega
-limit set for the Lorenz attractor is a
twisted torus

With the exception of the eight stable manifolds of the equilibria—two each for
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
and four for
\mathcal{O}
—every other orbit in
R
with arbitrary initial conditions approaches the
\omega
-limit set shown in Fig.
3
.

Remark 4
.

The crossings of the
x=0
plane have the property that the positions of consecutive crossing of the Lorenz orbit change regularly, while the times of consecutive crossing are chaotic.
