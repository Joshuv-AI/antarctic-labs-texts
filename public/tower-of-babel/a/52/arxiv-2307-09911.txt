Universal ‘winner-takes-it-all’ phenomenon in scale-free random graphs
Remco van der Hofstad

Abstract
We study competition on scale-free random graphs, where the degree distribution satisfies an asymptotic power-law with infinite variance. Our competition process is such that the two types attempt at occupying vertices incident to the presently occupied sets, and the passage times are independent and identically distributed, possibly with different distributions for the two types. Once vertices are occupied by a type, they remain on being so. We focus on the explosive setting, where our main result shows that the winning type occupies all but a finite number of vertices. This universal ‘winner-takes-it-all’ phenomenon significantly generalises previous work with Deijfen for exponential edge-weights, and considerably simplifies its proof.

Introduction

We study
competition problems
on scale-free random graphs, where edges are being traversed by two different types and vertices remain on having the type they received upon their first occupation. We assume that the edge-traversal times are independent and identically distributed as in first-passage percolation, possibly with different distributions for the two types. Thus, the competition processes each follow the first-passage percolation spreading rules, but the exclusion of vertices that are already occupied by the other type(s) slows the spreading down, effectively creating
competition
between the types.

We consider the configuration model, one of the most popular random graph models having flexible degree distributions, in the scale-free setting where the degrees have
infinite variance
. Prior work with Deijfen
DeiHof16
investigates this in the (explosive) setting where the edge-traversal times of the competition have exponential distributions with possibly different parameters. Baroni, van der Hofstad, and Komjáthy
BarHofKom15
and Komjáthy
Kom15
study this problem in the graph-distance setting where the edge-traversal times are deterministic, with either different
BarHofKom15
or equal
Kom15
deterministic traversal times. Our work significantly extends that with Deijfen
DeiHof16
.

The main issue in competition problems is to determine which of the types occupies the majority of the graph, or whether both can occupy a substantial portion of it. The latter case is called
co-existence
, and so far has been proved to occur with positive probability only for deterministic and equal edge-weights in the scale-free random graph setting
Kom15
. In
DeiHof16
, it is proved that for exponential edge-weights, the losing type occupies a number of vertices that converges in distribution. It is this result that we aim to extend from the Markovian setting with exponential edge-weights to general edge-weights that are
explosive
, in that first-passage percolation with such edge-weights reaches an unbounded number of vertices in a tight amount of time. This explosive setting only occurs for configuration models with infinite-variance degrees. The setting of competition on configuration models with finite-variance degrees was studied by Ahlberg, Deijfen and Janson
AhlDeiJan17
in the Markovian setting, who show that coexistence occurs precisely when the edge-traversal times have the same means. With Deijfen and Sfragara, we investigate the setting of configuration models with
infinite-mean degrees
in
DeiHofSfr22
, where the losing type is likely to only occupy its initial starting point. See
HofShn23
for a related problem aimed at modelling the spread of misinformation.

The closely related problem of first-passage percolation on random graphs has attracted considerable attention, see
(
Hofs23
, Chapter 3)
for an overview. This line of research started with joint work with Bhamidi and Hooghiemstra
BhaHofHoo12b
, where it was shown that the fluctuations of the weight of, and number of edges in, optimal paths in first-passage percolation on random graphs with finite-variance degrees behave remarkably universally, extending work in
BhaHofHoo09b
and
BhaHofHoo12
that applies to exponential edge-weights and finite-variance degrees. The case with infinite-variance degrees (investigated for exponential edge-weights in
BhaHofHoo09b
as well) is rather different, since there are several universality classes depending on whether the age-dependent (continuous-time) branching process that approximates the flow through the random graph, is explosive or non-explosive, which we refer to as being
conservative
. In the explosive setting, the weight-distance between two uniform vertices converges in distribution to the sum of two independent explosion times. In the conservative setting, on the other hand, the weight-distance between two uniform vertices converges to infinity in probability
BarHofKom17
. See also
BarHofKom19
where a particular conservative setting is explored in more detail.

Main result and innovation

The first main result in this paper shows that in scale-free configuration models where at least one of the edge-weight distributions is explosive, one of the two types occupies the majority of the graph. We refer to this type as the
winning
type, and the other as the
losing
type. In particular, co-existence occurs with vanishing probability. The second main result shows that the losing type occupies a
finite
number of vertices, and we identify its limiting distribution. This was done first for exponential edge-weights in
DeiHof16
. Our proof is simpler than that in
DeiHof16
, and relies on a key first-passage percolation result in that we identify the
epidemic curve
, which is interesting in its own right. When viewing first-passage percolation as the spread of a disease, the epidemic curve describes the proportion of vertices that have contracted the disease as a function of time.

For the explosive settings that we investigate, we can think of the time of explosion as the time at which one of the highest-degree vertices, or
hubs
, is found. Our epidemic curve result shows that at any time
t>0
after explosion, a positive proportion of the vertices is occupied by the first-passage percolation. In particular, vertices of high degree are more likely to have been occupied. When lifting these results to the competition setting, this means that virtually immediately after the time that the winning type occupies a hub, it has found all high-degree vertices, thus dramatically limiting the possible occupation of vertices by the losing type. In fact, we prove that immediately after explosion of the winning type, the exploration of the losing type in the part of the graph that has not yet been occupied by the winning type becomes
conservative
. In turn, this implies that the losing type only occupies a tight number of vertices that converges in distribution. In this last step, we generalise a technical bound obtained in
DeiHof16
to general explosive edge-weights.

Model and results

Our setting is the configuration model

\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})

\frac{c_{1}}{x^{\tau-1}}\leq 1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle D}(x)=\mathbb{P}(D>x)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{x^{\tau-1}}.

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}(D\geq 2)=1,

(1.2)

which is equivalent to the largest connected component consisting of most of the vertices (see e.g.,
JanLuc09

BolRio15

Hofs21

Hofs24

is such that the age-dependent branching process that locally describes
\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with its edge-weighted geometry is explosive. For this, let

be defined as the (

)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}-1=k):=\frac{k+1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\mathbb{P}(D=k+1).

Theorem 1.1
(Universal “winner-takes-it-all” phenomenon).

Consider
\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with iid degrees satisfying (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).
Assume that at least one of the age-dependent branching processes with offspring distribution
D^{\star}-1
and edge-weights
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e}
, and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{e},
respectively, is explosive. Denote the respective explosion times of the unimodular
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})
age-dependent branching processes by

and

, respectively (where at most one of these can be infinite a.s.). Then

(a)

the fraction

of type 1 infected vertices converges in distribution to the indicator variable
\mathbbm{1}_{\{V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<V^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}}
as

;

(b)

the total number
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}(n)
of vertices occupied by the losing type converges in distribution to a proper random variable
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}
.

The main message in Theorem
1.1
is that the losing type only occupies a
finite
number of vertices, and that this number converges in distribution to a limiting proper random variables: a universal “winner-takes-it-all” phenomenon (recall
DeiHof16

(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})
and
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})

\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<V^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})\in(0,1)

Thus, a type that is explosive has a positive probability of being the losing type, but a type that is conservative always loses from the explosive type. This is quite different from the Markovian setting of configuration models with finite-variance degrees, as studied by Ahlberg, Deijfen and Janson
AhlDeiJan17
for configuration models with general finite-variance degrees, and by Antunovic et al.
AntDekMosPer11
for random regular graphs, and can be understood by noting that such age-dependent branching processes are never explosive.

A classical example to which our results apply is when

has the same law as
\lambda X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}

(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})

1.2
Overview of the proof

The keys steps in the proof are as follows:

Stage up to first explosion

We start by growing the neighborhoods of the vertices
U_{1},U_{2}
in the first-passage percolation setting. Fix
\rho>0
appropriately. By
(
BhaHofHoo09b
, Proposition 4.7)
, the degrees of the vertices found up to time
n^{\rho}
can be coupled to a collection of
a_{n}=n^{\rho}
iid random variables having distribution
D^{\star}-1
, except for the degrees of
U_{1},U_{2}
, which are independent copies of the asymptotic degree distribution
D
. Denote the smallest-weight graph thus obtained when exploring up to time
t
by
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(t)
(so that
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(0)=\{U_{1},U_{2}\}
). Let
T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}
denote the time at which the
(m+2)
nd vertex was found (so that
T_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}=0
).
Then, again by
(
BhaHofHoo09b
, Proposition 4.7)
, for
\rho>0
sufficiently small, whp
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)})
consists of two disjoint sets of vertices, one containing
U_{1}
, the other containing
U_{2}
. We introduce such smallest-weight graphs in more detail in Section
2.1
, following
BhaHofHoo09b
;
BhaHofKom14
, as well as
BhaHofHoo12b
.

Since the edge-weights
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e})_{e\in E(\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d}))}
and
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{e})_{e\in E(\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d}))}
are independent, this means that
({\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}))_{m\leq a_{n}}
is effectively coupled to the disjoint union of two independent unimodular
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})
and
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
branching processes. Let
T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
denote the time at which the
(m+1)
st vertex was found in the first-passage exploration from vertex
U_{i}
, so that
(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)})_{m\leq a_{n}}
is the ordered sequence of
(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})_{m\leq a_{n}}
and
(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})_{m\leq a_{n}}
truncated to length
a_{n}
. Note that
T_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}\geq T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, where

denotes the minimum of
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In our proof, it turns out to be more convenient to work at time

T_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}
, since this allows for a more convenient use of the asymptotic independence of
({\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}))_{m\geq 0}
and
({\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}))_{m\geq 0}
. Since, for
a_{n}\rightarrow\infty
sufficiently slowly,
(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},V^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
, and
\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\neq V^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})=1
, it means that one of the trees in
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
has size
a_{n}/2-O_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}(1)
, while the other has size
O_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}(1)
, the larger tree corresponding to the smaller
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Then, type 1 is quite ahead of type 2 in the competition process. We will show that whp vertex 1 then wins the majority of the vertices. The above coupling to continuous-time branching processes forms the starting point of the analysis, in which the competition did not affect the spread of types 1 and 2 too much. This changes dramatically immediately afterwards, as we explain next.

Slightly beyond the first explosion

Let
M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
denote the size of the tree containing
U_{i}
at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, so that
M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}M^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
for some finite integer-valued random variable
M^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
on the event that
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Since type 2 explodes later than type 1, and the inter-arrival times
T_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}-T_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
are continuous random variables,
T_{M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}-T_{M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}>\varepsilon
whp for small
\varepsilon
. Thus, for some time after time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, type 2 does not even attempt to occupy another vertex. Type 1, on the other hand, is hardly hindered by the competition due to type 2 (as there are a tight number of vertices occupied by type 2), and thus proceeds as a normal first-passage percolation flow after time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. At time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon,
this flow has occupied some
\Theta_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}(n)f(\varepsilon)
vertices, where
f(\varepsilon)>0
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, vertices with high degree are more likely to have been occupied by type 1 when
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
(see Proposition
3.3
). Further, the two SWGs at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon
are still disjoint whp (see Proposition
3.1
).

Exploration curve winning type beyond the first explosion

We conclude that immediately after the explosion of the winning type, the losing type can hardly make use of high-degree vertices (see Proposition
3.3
), so that its spread becomes conservative (see Lemma
3.6
). This leaves very little room for type 2 to occupy other vertices. As a result, the epidemic curve for the winning type is identical to that of first-passage percolation in the absence of competition, as formulated for first-passage percolation in Theorem
2.4
, and for the competition process in Proposition
3.8
. In particular, this shows that
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{los}}(n)=o_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}(n)
.

We prove that the above implies that
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{los}}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{los}}
for some a.s.
finite
limiting variable
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{los}}
by first showing that the proportion of vertices of degree
k
found by the winning type at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+t
converges to some deterministic curve (see Proposition
3.8
). It relies on the previous step that shows that the loosing type hardly influences the occupation process of the winning type. This now identifies the random environment in which the losing type is left to occupy vertices. It turns out that this environment is so restrictive, that the losing type only occupies a
finite
number of vertices, and its distribution can be characterised in terms of the exploration and the shape of the epidemic curve. This concludes our overview of the proof of our main result Theorem
1.1
.

1.3
Discussion and open problems

Comparison with the proof in
DeiHof16

The proof strategy in this paper crucially relies on the epidemic curve result in Theorem
2.4
, and its extension to competition processes in Proposition
3.8
. As such, some of the technical computations and bounds in
DeiHof16
for exponential edge-weights could be avoided. This both generalises as well as simplifies the proof.

Multiple starting points and dependence

In
DeiHof16
, also the setting where both types start from several independently chosen uniform locations was studied. Let
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
denote the number of starting points of types 1 and 2, respectively. An analysis was performed in the case where
k_{1}=k
and
k_{2}=1
, as
k\rightarrow\infty
. This analysis basically boils down to the question how
\min_{j\leq k}V_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
behaves. It would be of interest to extend this analysis to our more general setting. Another interesting extension involves the setting where
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e},X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{e})_{e}
are iid, but
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e}
and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{e}
are not necessarily independent. We conjecture that the results in this paper are true in this setting as well.

Conservative settings

It would be of interest to extend our analysis to the setting where
both
unimodular
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})
and
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
branching processes are conservative. Let us for the moment stick to the setting where
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
has the same law as
\lambda X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
for some
\lambda
. In this case, we believe that
T_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
and
\lambda T_{a_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
grow at roughly the same
deterministic
speeds. When
\lambda>1
, this implies that type 1 occupies the majority of the vertices whp, and the fastest type wins. When
\lambda=1
, however, this suggests that the size of the smallest-weight graphs
M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
and
M_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
are
both
close to
a_{n}/2
. This raises the question whether co-existence can occur in this setting. The case where the passage times are deterministic has been addressed in
BarHofKom15
;
Kom15
, and shows that co-existence can indeed occur, the proof of which is remarkably subtle.

Finite-variance degrees

The setting of competition on configuration models with finite-variance degrees was carried out in
AhlDeiJan17
for the Markovian case of exponential edge-weights (see also
AntDekMosPer11
). It would be of great interest to extend such results to general edge-weights, where also the edge-weights of the two types might have a different distribution. The universality of first-passage percolation on configuration models, as proved in
BhaHofHoo12b
, should be extremely helpful in this analysis. One may conjecture that co-existence can only occur when the Malthusian parameters of the corresponding CTBPs agree, but it is not unlikely that high-order corrections of the
n
-dependent Malthusian parameters play a role.

Other random graphs

We believe that our results can be extended to several other random graphs with infinite variance degrees. Natural examples include preferential attachment models, where the first-passage percolation problem was investigated in
JorKom20
, and (finite-graph versions of) scale-free percolation (see
DeiHofHoo13
, and
HofKom17b
for first-passage percolation results in this setting).

Organization of the paper

In Section
2

2.1
Smallest-weight graphs for first-passage percolation explorations

In this section, we discuss smallest-weight graphs, used for first-passage percolation explorations. We define our graph-exploration process in
continuous time
, and let
{\sf SWG}(t)
denote the collection of vertices that can be reached within time
t

G=(V(G),E(G))
be a general graph, and let
(X_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
denote the edge-weights.

First-passage percolation graph exploration

Let
v\in V(G)
be the source of our exploration, and set
{\sf SWG}(0)=\{v\}
. We call
N(v)
, the edges incident to
v
,
active
, and denote the active set of edges
{\sf A}(0)=N(v)
. We then let

T_{1}=\min\{X_{e}\colon v\in e\}

(2.1)

be the first time at which the
{\sf SWG}(t)
increases by one vertex. Let
v_{1}
be such that
T_{1}=X_{\{v,v_{1}\}}
. Then,
{\sf SWG}(t)=\{v\},{\sf A}(t)={\sf A}(0)
for all
t\in[0,T_{1})
, and
{\sf SWG}(T_{1})=\{v,v_{1}\}
, while
{\sf A}(T_{1})
consists of all the edges
(N(v)\cup N(v_{1}))\setminus\{e_{1}\}
incident to
v
and
v_{1}

W(v,v_{1})=T_{1}

by
{\sf A}(T_{m})

\{v,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}

e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}

v_{m}

\displaystyle=\min\{X_{\{u,v^{\prime}\}}+W_{n}(v,u)\colon u\in{\sf SWG}(T_{m}),v\not\in{\sf SWG}(T_{m}),\{u,v\}\in E(G)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{X_{\{u,v^{\prime}\}}+W_{n}(v,u)\colon\{u,v^{\prime}\}\in{\sf A}(T_{m}),u\in{\sf SWG}(T_{m})\},

we let
e_{m+1}=\{u_{m+1},v_{m+1}\}

First-passage percolation configuration model exploration

We now specialise to the configuration model, for which we construct the graph
simultaneously
with the first-passage percolation exploration. Indeed, the configuration model can be constructed by successively pairing the half-edges. In our construction, it will be convenient to consider a set of active
half-edges
rather than the set of active edges. However, since in (
2.2

\{u,v\}
that are incident to
{\sf SWG}(T_{m})
, i.e., we ignore minimal edges that give rise to cycles, we need to
screen
the half-edges depending on whether they give rise to cycles or not. For this, we let
v_{m+1}\in[n]
be the minimiser in (
2.2
), and consider the set of
possible
active half-edges to be all the half-edges incident to
v
except for the half-edge that is part of the edge
e_{m+1}
between
v_{m+1}
and
{\sf SWG}(T_{m+1})
. We then, one by one, check whether these half-edges form cycles or self-loops. Those that do, together with the half-edges to which they are paired, are removed from the set of possible active half-edges, to obtain the set of active half-edges
{\sf A}(0)
. We call this the
cycle-removal procedure
. We give each of these half-edges an
independent
edge-weight.

Note that rather than associating the edge-weights to the
edges
, we now associate them to
half-edges
. This has the risk that an edge has
two
edge-weights associated to it. It is for this reason that we need to remove edges that close cycles from the set of active edges. For a half-edge
y
, we let
v_{y}
be the vertex incident to it. We then define, as in (
2.2
),

\displaystyle T_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\min\{X_{a}+W_{n}(v,v_{a})\colon a\in{\sf A}(T_{m})\},

where
a
is a half-edge, and
v_{a}\in{\sf SWG}(T_{m})
is the vertex incident to half-edge

. After identifying the minimising half-edge, we pair it uniformly to a half-edge

from the set of available and inactive half-edges. For all other half-edges incident to
v_{y_{m+1}}

y_{m+1}
, we check whether they are paired to an active half-edge, or to another half-edge incident to
y_{m+1}
. Then,
{\sf A}(T_{m+1})
consists of
{\sf A}(T_{m})
together with the half-edges incident to
v_{m+1}
, with the half-edge
y_{m+1}
and the half-edges incident to
v_{y_{m+1}}
that produce cycles and self-loops, as well as their pairs, removed. Due to this cycle-removal procedure, in fact all active half-edges in
{\sf A}(t)
are connected to vertices
outside

(Related first-passage percolation explorations)

The above gives a highly-convenient

of the first-passage percolation together with the graph
\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})

Remark 2.2

(First-passage percolation exploration from two sources)
.

On several occasions, it will be useful to explore from at least two, or even more, sources simultaneously, either with the same edge-weights, or independent edge-weights. While we do not write such extensions out in full detail, this can easily be done, for example, by performing the explorations from the different sources at the same time or one-by-one. When exploring from the two sources one-by-one, the above then describes the exploration from the first source, for that from another source, the only possible additional issue is that the edge-weights of edges
connecting
between the various SWGs need to be determined consistently. This is why in
BhaHofHoo12
, the half-edges are again tested to see whether they go to new vertices, or to vertices that are already in one SWG, and the edge-weights are fixed accordingly. When exploring at the same time, we will write
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(t)
and
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(t)
for the sets of vertices that can be reached at time

from the two sources
U_{1}
and
U_{2}

Remark 2.3

(Coupling to branching processes)
.

The above construction also allows for a convenient coupling between the exploration in the random graph and that on the corresponding
n
-dependent branching process tree. Indeed, when pairing a half-edge, we draw a half-edge uar from the collection of
all

This is worked out in full detail in

2.2
The epidemic curve for first-passage percolation

We will see that at time
T_{a_{n}}+t
, and due to the explosive nature of our unimodular age-dependent branching process, a
positive proportion
of the vertices will be found by the first-passage percolation exploration. To describe how the first-passage percolation sweeps through the graph, we need some further notation. Let

\bar{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k,t)}_{n}=\#\{v\colon d_{v}=k\mbox{ and }v\in{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}}+t)\}/n

(2.3)

denote the fraction of vertices that have degree
k
and that have been reached by the flow at time
T_{a_{n}}+t
. Further, for an edge
e=xy
consisting of two half-edges
x
and
y
that are incident to vertices
v_{x}
and
v_{y}
, we say that
e

spreads the flow at time
s
when
v_{x}
(or
v_{y}
) is occupied by first-passage percolation at time
s
, and
v_{y}
(or
v_{x}
) is then occupied by first-passage percolation at time
s

through the edge
e
. We let

\bar{L}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}=\#\{e\colon\mbox{ $e$ has spread the flow at time }T_{a_{n}}+t\}/[L_{n}/2]

(2.4)

denote the proportion of edges that have spread the first-passage percolation exploration by time
T_{a_{n}}+t
. We further let
V(k)
denote the explosion time of a vertex that has
k
children in a first-passage percolation process on a discrete branching process with edge-weights distributed as
X
, so that the explosion time of the one-stage
(D^{\star}-1,X)
branching process has distribution
V(D^{\star}-1)
, while that of a unimodular
(D,X)
branching process has distribution
V(D)
. Our main result describing how first-passage percolation sweeps through the graph is as follows:

Theorem 2.4
(First-passage percolation epidemic curve).

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\bar{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k,t)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}(V(k)\leq t)\mathbb{P}(D=k).

(2.5)

Consequently, the proportion of vertices occupied by first-passage percolation at time
T_{a_{n}}+t
converges in probability to
\mathbb{P}(V(D)\leq t)
. Further,

\bar{L}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{P}\big(X+\widetilde{V}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}_{b}\leq t\big),

(2.6)

where
(\widetilde{V}_{a},\widetilde{V}_{b})
are two independent copies of
V(D^{\star}-1)
.

Theorem
2.4
is interesting in its own right, as it implies the existence of an
epidemic curve
, as also derived in
BhaHofKom14
in the setting of first-passage percolation on the configuration model with finite-variance degrees. Indeed, (
2.5
) together with the convergence
T_{a_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}V
, where
V
is the explosion time of the unimodular
(D,X)
branching process, shows that the fraction of vertices occupied by first-passage percolation at time
t
(or infected vertices when interpreting first-passage percolation as a model for disease spread) converges in distribution to
\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}<t-V\mid V)=F_{\scriptscriptstyle V}(t-V)
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
F_{\scriptscriptstyle V}
is the distribution function of
V
. We refer to
BhaHofKom14
for an extensive discussion of the consequences of such a result. A related version of (
2.5
) was proved in
(
HofShn23
, Proposition 29)
, but we crucially rely on the extension to fixed
k
in (
2.5
), as well as (
2.6
).

We extend Theorem
2.4
to the winning type in Proposition
3.8
below. Thus, the winning type sweeps through the graph in a similar way as first-passage percolation would
in the absence of competition
. The reason for this is that the winning type, instantly after having exploded, reaches a positive proportion of the vertices in the graph (and particularly the high-degree vertices), thus effectively blocking the spread of the losing type.

The epidemic curve: Proof of Theorem

In this section, we use Propositions

and

to prove Theorem

We start with the proof of (
2.5

U
be a vertex chosen uar from
[n]

U_{1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k,t)}_{n}\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle U}^{\scriptscriptstyle(t,k)}=1\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mbox{$U$ is found by flow at time $T_{a_{n}}+t$}\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}}),D_{\scriptscriptstyle U}=k)\mathbb{P}(D_{\scriptscriptstyle U}=k),

and, with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\bar{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k,t)}_{n})^{2}\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle U}^{\scriptscriptstyle(t,k)}=\mathbbm{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\prime}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(t,k)}=1\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mbox{$U,U^{\prime}$ are found by flow at time $T_{a_{n}}+t$}\mid{\sf SWG}(T_{a_{n}},D_{\scriptscriptstyle U}=D_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\prime}}=k)

3.3
Structure of outside of winning SWG shortly after explosion: Size-biased reordering according to degrees

Before moving on to our second main result, we investigate the degree distribution of the vertices that have not been found by first-passage percolation from
U_{1}
with edge-weights
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{e})_{e}
. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}(\varepsilon)
be the (random) degree distribution of the vertices in
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
, and let
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
denote the degree of a uniformly chosen vertex in that set. Let
\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}
denote the
\sigma
-field generated by
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
and the full exploration process up to time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon
. The following proposition shows that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}(\varepsilon)
has a tight second moment:

Proposition 3.3
(Complement of SWG has bounded second moment degrees).

For all
\varepsilon>0
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}(\varepsilon)
satisfies that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]
is a tight sequence of random variables.

Recall Remark
3.2
, which implies that up to time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon
, there is whp no difference between the first-passage percolation and competition processes. We can thus rely on first-passage percolation estimates. We need to prove that, for every
\eta>0
, there exists a
K>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]>K\Big)\leq\eta.

(3.5)

We note that, at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon
, by (
2.5
) in Theorem
2.4
,
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
has size

N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}=n\sum_{k\geq 2}\bar{N}_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(k,\varepsilon)}=n\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(D)\leq\varepsilon)(1+o_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}(1)).

(3.6)

We conclude that whp, the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\{N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}\in n[\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(D)\leq\varepsilon)/2,2\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(D)\leq\varepsilon)]\}\cap\{L_{n}\leq 2n\mathbb{E}[D]\}
holds. We then prove that whp

\mathbb{P}_{n}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]>K\Big)\leq\eta,

(3.7)

where we let
\mathbb{P}_{n}
denote the law of the configuration model
given
the iid degrees
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, and
\mathbb{E}_{n}
the expectation wrt
\mathbb{P}_{n}
.

In order to analyse
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
, we introduce the following notation:

Definition 3.4

(Size-biased reordering)
.

Let
(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
be a sequence of integers. The
uniform size-biased reordering
of
[n]
wrt
(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
is the random permutation
(\pi_{i})_{i\in[n]}
of
[n]
, defined recursively by

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{1}=v)=\frac{1}{n},

(3.8)

and, given
\pi_{[i-1]}\equiv(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{i-1})
and all
v\not\in{\sf SB}(i-1)\equiv\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{i-1}\}
,

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{i}=v\mid\pi_{[i-1]})=\frac{d_{v}}{\sum_{u\in[n]\setminus{\sf SB}(i-1)}d_{u}}.

(3.9)

\blacktriangleleft

The crux of Definition
3.4
is that vertices keep being added with a probability proportional to their degrees, but without replacement. This makes the probability that vertices are added larger and larger when they have escaped being drawn for a long time. Further, due to the
size-biasing
effect in (
3.9
) wrt the degrees
(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
, vertices with higher degree are more likely to be chosen
earlier
in the exploration.

In the usual size-biased reordering, (
3.8
) is replaced with
\mathbb{P}(\pi_{1}=v)=d_{v}/\sum_{u\in[n]}d_{u}
, which would correspond to also
\pi_{1}
being chosen in a size-biased manner. Definition
3.4
has been set up in such a way that the vertices chosen in the exploration of a random vertex
U_{1}
, and removing repetitions due to cycles, follow exactly these rules (recall the first-passage percolation exploration described in Section
2.1
).

We conclude that the vertices in
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
form a collection of
N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}
uniform size-biased reordered vertices as in Definition
3.4
. Indeed, each time a new vertex is being added, since the edge-weights are attached to the half-edge that already is in
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
, the half-edge is chosen uar, conditionally on being incident to a vertex not yet in the SWG, and such a collection is precisely a uniform size-biased reordered vector. Further, its size is precisely
N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}
by construction.

Recall that
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
denotes the degree of a vertex chosen uar from
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
. The law of
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
conditionally on
\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}
is the same as the law of
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
conditionally on
{\sf SB}(N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)})
. As a result,

\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid{\sf SB}(N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)})]

is a tight sequence of random variables when we can show that the probability that a vertex of relatively high degree is not in
{\sf SB}(N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)})
is small. We then bound using Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]>K\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]\Big]

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}\big]\Big],

since
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
is measurable wrt
\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}
, as well as
N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}
and
(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
.

Recall (
3.6
). Fix
\delta\in(0,1-\varepsilon)
. We can bound

\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}=\lceil\delta n\rceil]=\sum_{v}d_{v}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\{v\not\in{\sf SB}(\delta n)\}}}{\sum_{u\not\in{\sf SB}(\delta n)}d_{u}}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{n-n\delta}\sum_{v}d_{v}^{2}\mathbb{P}_{n}(v\not\in{\sf SB}(\delta n)),

(3.11)

since
d_{v}\geq 1
, so that

\sum_{u\not\in{\sf SB}(\delta n)}d_{u}\geq|{\sf SB}(\delta n)|\geq n-n\delta.

(3.12)

Note that

\mathbb{P}_{n}(v\not\in{\sf SB}(k)\mid v\not\in{\sf SB}(k-1))\leq 1-\frac{d_{v}}{L_{n}}.

(3.13)

Thus,

\mathbb{P}_{n}(v\not\in{\sf SB}(\delta n))\leq\Big(1-\frac{d_{v}}{L_{n}}\Big)^{\delta n}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-d_{v}\delta n/L_{n}}.

(3.14)

We thus arrive at

\mathbb{E}_{n}[[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}=\lceil\delta n\rceil]\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[D_{n}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{n}\delta n/L_{n}}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}=\lceil\delta n\rceil\Big].

(3.15)

As a result, since
L_{n}\leq 2n\mathbb{E}[D]
and
N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}\leq 2\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(D)\leq\varepsilon)n
on
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
, and with
\delta=2\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(D)\leq\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}\Big(\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{2}\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}]>K\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K(1-\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[D_{n}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{n}\delta n/L_{n}}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}\Big]\Big]

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K(1-\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[D_{n}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{n}\delta/(2\mathbb{E}[D])}\mid N_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K(1-\delta)}\mathbb{E}_{n}\big[D_{n}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-D_{n}\delta/(2\mathbb{E}[D])}\big].

This has finite expectation when taking the expectation wrt the iid degrees (and in fact, has any moment), which implies the claim in (
3.7
).
∎

3.4
Structure shortly after explosion: expensive to avoid large SWG

We next prove that avoiding the SWG of vertex
U_{1}
is expensive. Let
U
be a uniform vertex in
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)

for the passage time from

to

(Expensive to avoid large SWG).

There exists

\mathbb{P}\Big(W_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(U_{2},U)\text{ in }[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)\leq b_{n}\mid T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\Big)\rightarrow 0.

We complete the proof of Proposition

at the end of this section, and first explain the strategy.
We consider the graph
\mathrm{CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
and a uniformly chosen vertex
U
in it. To prove Proposition
3.5

(3.18)

Since, by Proposition

, the degree distribution outside of
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+\varepsilon)
has finite second moment, we can couple the growth of
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+t)
in

grows at most exponentially:

Lemma 3.6
(Bounding the growth of the losing process).

There exist tight random variables
\lambda_{n}(\varepsilon),K_{n}(\varepsilon)\in(0,\infty)
such that, for all

,

\mathbbm{1}_{\{T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}}\mathbb{E}_{n}\Big[|{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+t)|\mid\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}\Big]\leq K_{n}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{n}(\varepsilon)t}.

(3.19)

Let
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}<T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Let
S_{0}^{\prime}(n)
denote the number of active half-edges for the exploration from
U_{2}
(recall Section
2.1
), which is a tight random variable. The random variable
|{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+t)|
is bounded from above by
|{\sf SWG}(t)|
, which is a first-passage exploration started from

half-edges, and
(B^{\scriptscriptstyle(\varepsilon)}_{j}+1)_{j\geq 1}
form a collection of size-biased reordered random variables from
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon)
(recall Definition
3.4
).

This can be realised by noting that the
S_{0}^{\prime}(n)
half-edges each have a certain residual life-time, and the exploration is bounded by the setting where all residual life-times are replaced by 0. This boils down to immediately pairing these half-edges, and starting novel exploration processes from the sibling half-edges of the half-edges that are being paired to. We let the total number of unpaired half-edges after this construction be equal to
\tilde{\ell}_{n}(\varepsilon)
, which is whp bounded from below by
L_{n}(1-\delta)
for some
\delta>0
that can be made small for small
\varepsilon>0
. By construction, the vertices with their unpaired half-edges form a smaller configuration model with fewer vertices and edges.

For this smaller configuration model, we use
path-counting techniques
, as pioneered by Janson in
(
Jans09b
, Proof of Lemma 5.1)
, and follow the ideas in
(
BhaHofHoo09b
, Section 5.3)
. We follow
(
BhaHofHoo09b
, Proof of Proposition 2.2(b))
, adapting the argument where needed. Let
({\sf BP}_{n}^{\prime}(s))_{s\geq 0}
denote the age-dependent branching process starting from
D_{n}^{\prime}
individuals, and having offspring distribution equal to
\tilde{D}_{n}^{\star}(\varepsilon)-1
, the (size-biased distribution of
\tilde{D}_{n}(\varepsilon)
) minus 1. Let

Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm({\sf BP})}(t)=|\{v\colon v\in{\sf BP}_{n}^{\prime}(s)\text{ for some }s\leq t\}|,

(3.20)

denote the total number of individuals ever born into the
{\sf BP}_{n}^{\prime}
before time
t
, and let

Y^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm({\sf SWG})}(t)=|\{v\colon v\in{\sf A}(s)\text{ for some }s\leq t\}|,

(3.21)

denote the number of half-edges in the SWG that have ever been alive before time
t
. Here we recall from Section
2.1
that
{\sf A}(t)
denotes the set of active half-edges, which here is restricted to the vertices in
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon)
. Further, we emphasise the fact that all our explorations are performed in
[n]\setminus{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon)
, so outside
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1,2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\varepsilon)
.

The expected number of descendants in generation

of a branching process starting with
\ell
individuals equals
\ell\bar{\nu}_{n}(\varepsilon)^{k}
, where

\bar{\nu}_{n}(\varepsilon):=\mathbb{E}_{n}^{\prime}[\tilde{D}_{n}^{\star}(\varepsilon)-1]

(3.22)

denotes the mean offspring, and
\mathbb{E}_{n}^{\prime}
denotes the expectation conditionally on
\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}}
, the degrees
(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
and
{\sf SWG}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\wedge T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})
. Here, we deal with a unimodular branching process
{\sf BP}_{n}^{\prime}
where in the first generation the mean number of offspring equals
\mathbb{E}_{n}^{\prime}[D_{n}^{\prime}]
.
F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\star k}(t)-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\star(k+1)}(t)
equals the probability that an individual of generation
k
is alive at time
t
. Together this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}^{\prime}[|{\sf BP}_{n}^{\prime}(t)|]

Convergence of the exploration process of losing type

We grow the SWG of type
\mathcal{L}_{n}
from size
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}_{n}}^{*}(n)
onwards. At this moment, whp the type
\mathcal{L}_{n}
has not yet tried to occupy a vertex that was already occupied by type
\mathcal{W}_{n}
. However, when we grow the SWG further, then type
\mathcal{W}_{n}
will grow very quickly due to its explosion. We will show that the growth of type
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is thus delayed to the extent that it will only occupy
finitely
many vertices. An important tool in proving this rigorously is a stochastic process
(S_{m}^{\prime})_{m\geq 0}
keeping track of the number of unexplored half-edges incident to the SWG of the losing type.

Recall that, by the construction of the two-type exploration process described in Sections
2.1
and
2.3
, the quantity
S_{0}^{\prime}(n)
represents the number of (unpaired) half-edges incident to the SWG of type
\mathcal{L}_{n}
at time
T_{a_{n}/2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W}_{n})}
. By Lemma
4.1
,

S_{0}^{\prime}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}S_{0}^{\prime}\equiv D^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})}+\sum_{i=2}^{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{*}}B_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})}-N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{*},

(4.3)

where
D^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})}
has distribution
D
, and
(B_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})})_{i\geq 1}
are iid copies of
D^{\star}-1
. Conditionally on
\mathcal{W}=i
, denote the remaining life-times of the unimodular
(D,X^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})
process by
(R_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L}_{n})})_{j\leq S_{0}^{\prime}(n)}
. By Lemma
4.1
and its proof, and conditionally on
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}
,

(R_{0,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L}_{n})})_{j\leq S_{0}^{\prime}(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(R_{0,j}^{\prime})_{j\leq S_{0}^{\prime}},

(4.4)

where, conditionally on
\mathcal{W}=i
,
S_{0}^{\prime}=\ell
and
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}=x

T_{m}^{\prime}(n)=0

The sequences

and

are constructed recursively and simultaneuously. We grow the SWG of type
\mathcal{L}_{n}
one edge at a time by pairing the half-edge with minimal remaining edge-weight to a uniform unpaired half-edge. Denote the half-edge of minimal weight in the
m
th step by
x_{m}
and the half-edge to which it is paired by
P_{x_{m}}

is already infected by the winning or losing type, and then the SWG of the losing type does not grow and we define

and

is not yet infected by the winning or losing type, then we let

denote

S_{m+1}^{\prime}(n)-S_{m}^{\prime}(n)=B_{m+1}^{\prime}(n)-1.

(4.5)

When
B_{m+1}^{\prime}(n)\geq 1

B_{m+1}^{\prime}(n)
iid random variables with law
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
, conditionally on
\mathcal{W}=i

Our aim is to identify the scaling limit of
(T_{m}^{\prime}(n),S_{m}^{\prime}(n),(R_{m,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L}_{n})})_{j\leq S_{m}^{\prime}(n)})_{m\geq 0}

as in the rhs of (

(4.6)

Remark 4.2

(Edge-weight distribution vs. weights on half-edges)
.

In the above construction, we explore from vertices 1 and 2 simultaneously, and search for the minimal weight among unexplored half-edges of the loosing type. This half-edge is paired to a randomly chosen second half-edge, and the passage time of the resulting edge should be given by the weight of this half-edge, that is, the second half-edge should not be assigned any weight at all. The careful reader may note that this is not the case in the above construction when the second half-edge belongs to a vertex that is already infected. In that case, however, the edge will never be used to transmit infection (indeed, such edges are not included in the SWG defined in Section
2.1
) and its assigned passage time is hence unimportant for the competition process.
\blacktriangleleft

The following lemma shows that
(T_{l}^{\prime},S_{l}^{\prime},(R_{l,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})})_{j\leq S_{l}^{\prime}})_{l\geq 0}

denotes the

Lemma 4.3
(Exploration of the losing type beyond explosion of the winning type).

Conditionally on

, and for all
m\geq 1
, as

,

(T_{l}^{\prime}(n),S_{l}^{\prime}(n),(R_{l,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L}_{n})}(n))_{j\leq S_{l}^{\prime}(n)})_{l=0}^{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(T_{l}^{\prime},S_{l}^{\prime},(R_{l,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})})_{j\leq S_{l}^{\prime}})_{l=0}^{m}.

(4.7)

We prove the claim by induction on

. The statement for

, Lemma
4.1
and (
4.3
)–(
4.4

To advance the induction hypothesis, we introduce some further notation. Let

be the

together with

by the fact that

We rewrite this as

(4.10)

where

\displaystyle\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{(k+1)}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\frac{\mathbb{P}(D=k+1)-\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k+1)\leq t)\mathbb{P}(D=k+1)}{1-\mathbb{P}\Big(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}\leq t\Big)}

as required. This shows that, conditionally on

and

4.3
Asymptotics of vertices occupied by losing type for large times

Note that
B_{m}^{\prime}
in (
4.6
) has infinite mean when we condition on
T_{m}^{\prime}=t=0
, which implies that initially many of its values are large. This is the problem that we need to overcome in showing that the number of vertices found by the losing type is finite. As it turns out, conditionally on
T_{m}^{\prime}=t
, the mean of
B_{m}^{\prime}
decreases as
t
increases, and becomes smaller than 1 for large
t
, so this saves our day. In order to prove this, we first need some results on the process
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)
:

Lemma 4.5
(Bounds and asymptotics for
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)
).

The law of
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)
is related to that of
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)
by

\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)>t)=\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)^{k},\qquad k\geq 1,t\geq 0.

(4.13)

Further, for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\right)\geq\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)^{2}.

(4.14)

The relation (
4.13
) follows since
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)
is the explosion time starting from
k
individuals, which is the minimum of the explosion times of
k
iid explosion times starting from 1 individual, i.e.,

V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\min_{i=1}^{k}V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{i},

where
(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are iid with law
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)
and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}
is the edge-weight of the winning type. From this, (
4.13
) follows immediately. For (
4.14
), write
G(t)=\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)
and note that
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\min_{i=1}^{D^{\star}-1}V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{i}
, where again
(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are iid with law
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)
. Thus, conditioning on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}
and
D^{\star}
, and using (
4.13
), leads to

G(t)=[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)+\int_{0}^{t}f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)\mathbb{E}[G(t-s)^{D^{\star}-1}]ds,

(4.15)

where
[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)=\mathbb{P}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}>t)
. Furthermore, since
\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}
and
\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}
are iid with the same distribution as
V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(D^{\star}-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\min_{i=1}^{D^{\star}-1}V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{i}
, we similarly obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\right)=[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)+\int_{0}^{t}f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)\mathbb{E}[G(t-s)^{D^{\star}-1}]^{2}ds.

(4.16)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (where we split
f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)=\sqrt{f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)}\sqrt{f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)}
in the lhs),

\left(\int_{0}^{t}f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)\mathbb{E}[G(t-s)^{D^{\star}-1}]ds\right)^{2}\leq F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)\int_{0}^{t}f_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(s)\mathbb{E}[G(t-s)^{D^{\star}-1}]^{2}ds.

(4.17)

Combining (
4.15
), (
4.16
) and (
4.17
) yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)+\frac{(G(t)-[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t))^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}.

(4.18)

Working out the square gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)+\frac{[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}-2\frac{G(t)[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}+\frac{G(t)^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}.

(4.19)

We use that

[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)+\frac{[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}=[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)\Big[1+\frac{[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}\Big]=\frac{[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}](t)}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)},

(4.20)

and

\frac{G(t)^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}=G(t)^{2}+\frac{G(t)^{2}[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)]}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}.

(4.21)

Rearranging terms then gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\right)

\displaystyle\geq G(t)^{2}+\frac{[1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)](G(t)-1)^{2}}{F_{\scriptscriptstyle X}(t)}\geq\,G(t)^{2},

(4.22)

as desired.
∎

Recall that
T_{m}^{\prime}
denotes the minimal residual lifetime of a half-edge in the losing exploration, after pairing
m
half-edges. Let
B_{m}^{\prime}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}B^{\prime}_{m}|\,T^{\prime}_{m}=t
. The next lemma shows that
(B^{\prime}_{m}(t))_{m\geq 1}
is stochastically dominated by an iid sequence whose mean is strictly smaller than 1 for large
t
. It also shows that
T^{\prime}_{m}\to\infty
almost surely. It is a key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
(b):

Lemma 4.6
(Asymptotic behavior of
B_{m}^{\prime}(t)
and
T^{\prime}_{m}
).

(a)

For each fixed
t>0
, the sequence
(B^{\prime}_{m}(t))_{m\geq 1}
is stochastically dominated by an iid sequence
(\bar{B}(t))_{m\geq 1}
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(t)]
is finite for each fixed
t>0
and
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(t)]\to\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=2)
as
t\to\infty
.

(b)

T^{\prime}_{m}\to\infty
almost surely as
m\to\infty
.

Recalling the definition of
B^{\prime}_{m}|\,T^{\prime}_{m}=t
in (
4.6
), using both (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) in Lemma
4.5
(a), we obtain for
k\geq 1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{m}(t)=k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=k+1)\frac{\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(k+1)>t)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(X^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}+\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{a}\wedge\widetilde{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}_{b}>t\Big)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=k+1)\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)^{k-1}.

(4.23)

Denote
\bar{p}_{k}(t)=\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=k+1)\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)^{k-1}
, and let
\bar{B}(t)
be defined by

\mathbb{P}(\bar{B}(t)=k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{p}_{k}(t)&\mbox{for }k\geq 1;\\
1-\sum_{k\geq 1}\bar{p}_{k}(t)&\mbox{for }k=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.24)

Then, obviously,
B_{m}(t)
is stochastically dominated by
\bar{B}(t)
.
For any fixed
t>0
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{W})}(1)>t)<1
, so that
\bar{p}_{k}(t)\to 0
exponentially in
k
. It follows that
\bar{B}(t)
has all moments so that, in particular, its mean is finite. Furthermore,
\bar{p}_{1}(t)=\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=2)
for any
t>0
, while
\bar{p}_{k}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
for each
k\geq 2
. Hence
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(t)]\rightarrow\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=2)
.

As for (b), recall the construction of the processes
(S^{\prime}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
and
(T^{\prime}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
, with
S^{\prime}_{m}=S^{\prime}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}B^{\prime}_{i}
, where
B_{m}^{\prime}=B_{m}(T_{m}^{\prime})
. Note that
B^{\prime}_{i}(t)
is stochastically bounded by an iid sequence that is decreasing in
t
having finite mean for all
t>0
, and that
t=T_{m}^{\prime}\geq T_{1}^{\prime}>0
a.s. This implies that, conditionally on
T_{1}^{\prime}=t_{1}^{\prime}>0
, the (inhomogeneous) exploration tree constructed by
(T_{m}^{\prime},S_{m}^{\prime},(R_{m,j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\mathcal{L})})_{j\leq S_{m}^{\prime}})_{m\geq 0}
can be bounded from above by an
(S_{1}^{\prime},\bar{B}(t_{1}),X)
age-dependent branching process with offspring distribution
\bar{B}(t_{1})
, except for the root, which has offspring
S_{1}^{\prime}
. This age-dependent branching process is conservative since
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(t_{1})]<\infty
. Thus,
T^{\prime}_{m}\to\infty
a.s.
∎

4.4

(b)

Recall the construction of the process

N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}_{n}}^{**}(n)=\#\{m\colon B_{m}^{\prime}(n)\geq 1\}.

(4.25)

Indeed, each time when a new vertex is found that is not infected by type
\mathcal{W}_{n}
, the degree of the vertex is at least 2, so that
B_{m}^{\prime}(n)\geq 1
. Thus, the number of vertices found is equal to the number of
m
for which
B_{m}^{\prime}(n)\geq 1
.

Recall that the total asymptotic number of losing type vertices is denoted by
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}
. This number can now be expressed as

N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}=N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{*}+N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{**},

(4.26)

where
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{*}
is defined in Lemma
4.1
and
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}_{n}}^{**}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{**}:=\#\{m\colon B_{m}^{\prime}\geq 1\}
, where the weak convergence follows from Lemma
4.3
. Further, since the convergence in Lemma
4.3
is
conditional on

\mathscr{F}_{a_{n}},
we also obtain the joint convergence

(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}_{n}}^{*}(n),N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}_{n}}^{**}(n))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{*},N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{**}),

(4.27)

which implies (
4.2
). To prove Theorem
1.1
(b), it hence suffices to show that the random variable
N_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}}^{**}
is finite almost surely. This certainly follows when
H^{\prime}=\max\{m\colon S_{m}^{\prime}\geq 1\}
is almost surely finite, which is what we prove below.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that
H^{\prime}=\infty
. Then,
T_{m-1}^{\prime}<\infty
for every
m
.
Furthermore,
S^{\prime}_{m}-S^{\prime}_{m-1}=B^{\prime}_{m}-1
where, by Lemma
4.6
(a), the contribution
B^{\prime}_{m}
is stochastically dominated by
\bar{B}(T^{\prime}_{m})
, with
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(t)]\to\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=2)
as
t\to\infty
. Pick
k
large so that
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}(T^{\prime}_{k})\mid T^{\prime}_{k}]<1
, which is possible since
\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=2)<1
and
T^{\prime}_{k}\to\infty
a.s. by Lemma
4.6
(b). Then, conditionally on
T^{\prime}_{k}
and for
m>k
,
S^{\prime}_{m}-S^{\prime}_{k}
is stochastically dominated by
\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}(\bar{B}_{i}(T^{\prime}_{k})-1)
– a sum of iid variables with negative mean. It follows that
S^{\prime}_{m}
hits 0 in finite time, so that
H^{\prime}<\infty
, which is a contradiction.
∎
