The distance function to a finite set is a topological Morse function
Charles Arnal

Abstract
In this short note, we show that the distance function to any finite set X ⊂ ℝ n X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n} is a topological Morse function, regardless of whether X X is in general position. We also precisely characterize its topological critical points and their indices, and relate them to the differential critical points of the function.

Introduction

The distance function
d_{X}
to a compact set
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and its sublevel sets are central objects of study in computational geometry
[
CCSL06
,
CL06
,
ABE09
]
and in topological data analysis
[
EH22
,
CDSGO16
,
OPT+17
,
CVJ21
]
.
In general, the function
d_{X}
is not particularly regular, even when the set
X
itself is (e.g. when
X
is a smooth submanifold).
Nonetheless,
d_{X}
does enjoy some weak Morse-like properties: though it is not differentiable, one can consider its
generalized gradient

\nabla d_{X}
, as defined in
[
Lie04
]
. Let
\Pi_{X}(z)
denote the set
\{x\in X:d(z,x)=d(z,X)\}
of projections of
z
on
X
, and let
\sigma_{X}(z)
denote the projection of
z
onto the convex hull
\mathrm{Conv}(\Pi_{X}(z))
. Then

\nabla d_{X}(z):=\frac{z-\sigma_{X}(z)}{d_{X}(z)}

if
z\not\in X
, and
\nabla d_{X}(z):=0
if
z\in X
.

1

It can be shown that the generalized gradient
\nabla d_{X}(z)
as defined here coincides with the projection of
0
onto the similarly named generalized gradient
\partial d_{X}(z)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as defined by Clarke in
[
Cla90
]
.

A
differential critical point
of
d_{X}
is a point
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\nabla d_{X}(z)=0
, and a
differential critical value
of
d_{X}
is the image by
d_{X}
of a differential critical point.
Then it can be shown that the Isotopy Lemma from Morse theory still holds, i.e. that changes in the homotopy type of the offsets
X^{t}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:d_{X}(z)\leq t\}
can only occur at critical values of
d_{X}
(see e.g.
[
Gro93
]
).

On the other hand, there is no analogue to the Handle Attachment Lemma (see
[
Mil63
]
), and no simple way to control the potential changes in topology at critical values.
In fact, differential critical values need not even correspond to changes in topology; among other examples, consider the half-circle
X:=\{x^{2}+y^{2}=1,x\leq 0\}
. The point
(0,0)
is a differential critical point of
d_{X}
, but all offsets
\{X^{t}\}_{t>0}
are homeomorphic.

The case where
X
is a finite point cloud is of particular importance, as it features preeminently in applications where one only has access to discrete samplings of sets of interest, rather than to the sets themselves. When
X
is in general position, the frameworks of continuous selections of functions
[
JP88
]
or of Min-type functions
[
GR97
]
can be applied to show that the distance function
d_{X}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a
topological Morse function
:

Definition 1.1

(Topological Morse functions
[
Mor59
]
)
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an open set and let
f:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function.

•

A point
z\in U
is said to be a topological regular point of
f
if there is a homeomorphism
\phi:V_{1}\to V_{2}
between open neighborhoods
V_{1}
of
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
V_{2}
of
z
in
U
with
\phi(0)=z
and such that for all
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in V_{1}
,

f\circ\phi(p)=f(z)+p_{1}.

(1)

•

A point
z\in U
is said to be a topological critical point of
f
if it is not a topological regular point of
f
.

•

A point
z\in U
is said to be a non-degenerate topological critical point of
f
of index
m
if there exist an integer
0\leq i\leq n
and a homeomorphism
\phi:V_{1}\to U_{2}
between open neighborhoods
V_{1}
of
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
V_{2}
of
z
in
U
with
\phi(0)=z
such that for all
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in V_{1}
,

f\circ\phi(p)=f(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}p_{i}^{2}.

(2)

•

The function
f
is said to be a topological Morse function if all its topological critical points are non-degenerate.

Topological Morse functions do satisfy the Handle Attachment Lemma (see e.g.
[
SYM23
, Theorem 5]
), which in turns allows for the computation of the homology of the sublevel sets of the function, which is one of the main goals in topological data analysis.
As a result, settings in which
X
is assumed to be in general position have been extensively studied in the literature
[
BA14
,
BW15
,
BE16
,
BO19
,
dKTV19
,
RB24
,
BR24
]
.

On the other hand, no such result was known for an arbitrary point cloud
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, though a classification of critical configurations in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
can be found in
[
Sie96
]
. Distance functions to arbitrary compact sets are not topologically Morse (consider e.g. two parallel segments), and the methods used in the case where
X
is a point cloud in general position fail without the genericity assumption, as the differential critical points of
d_{X}
need not be non-degenerate in the sense of Min-type functions or of continuous selections of functions.
This was unsatisfactory, as there are many settings where
X
cannot be expected to be in general position, nor can it be perturbed to be made so, e.g. when
X
is a subsampling of a manifold (as in
[
NSW08
,
AKC+19
,
ABL23
,
ACSD23
,
ACSD24
]
).

In this short note, we prove that the distance function to an arbitrary finite set is, in fact, a topological Morse function, and give a description of its topological critical points. Let
\mathrm{Span}(S)
denote the linear span of a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a finite set. Then
d_{X}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a topological Morse function.
Furthermore,

•

The points of
X
are topological critical points of
d_{X}
of index
0
.

•

Let
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash X
be such that
z\in\mathrm{Conv}(\Pi_{X}(z))
.
If there exists
v\in\mathrm{Span}(\Pi_{X}(z)-z)\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x-z\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\Pi_{X}(z)
, then
z
is a topological regular point of
d_{X}
.
Otherwise,
z
is a topological critical point of
d_{X}
of index
\dim(\mathrm{Span}(\Pi_{X}(z)-z))
.

•

All other points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are topological regular points of
d_{X}
.

Note that the points
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash X
such that
z\in\mathrm{Conv}(\Pi_{X}(z))
are precisely the differential critical points of
d_{X}
that do not belong to
X
.
Hence the topological critical points of
d_{X}
are (in general) a strict subset of its differential critical points, and a simple criterion specifies whether a given differential critical point is also topologically critical.
Techniques similar to those used in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
could allow for a generalization of the notion of Morse Min-type functions from
[
GR97
]
, though this is beyond the scope of this article and might be investigated in future work.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be such that
\mathrm{Aff}(X)={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and that there exists
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with
\Pi_{X}(z)=X
and
z\in\mathrm{Conv}(X)
.
Assume that there exists no
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x-z\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in X
.
Then
z
is a topological critical point of
d_{X}
of index
m
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
z=0
to simplify notations, and let us write
R=d_{X}(0)
.
For any
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
, there exists
x=x(p)\in X
such that
\langle p,x\rangle\geq\langle p,x^{\prime}\rangle
for all
x^{\prime}\in X
(note that
x(p)
is not necessarily unique - it matters not).
In particular, for any
x^{\prime}\in X
,

\displaystyle\|p-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p\|^{2}-2\langle p,x\rangle+\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|p\|^{2}-2\langle p,x^{\prime}\rangle+\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}=\|p-x^{\prime}\|^{2},

hence
d_{X}(p)=d(p,x(p))
(in other words,
p
belongs to the Voronoi cell of
x(p)
).
Now by hypothesis,

\lambda:=\min_{\|v\|=1}\max_{x\in X}2\langle v,x\rangle>0.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\lambda/2)
, and observe that for any
p\in B(0,\varepsilon)\backslash\{0\}
, we have

d_{X}(p)^{2}=\|x-x(p)\|^{2}=\|p\|^{2}-2\langle p,x(p)\rangle+\|x(p)\|^{2}\leq\|p\|(\|p\|-\lambda)+R^{2}<R^{2}.

Hence
0
is the maximum of
d_{X}
over
B(0,\varepsilon)
, and
R-d_{X}(p)>0
for any
p\in B(0,\varepsilon)\backslash\{0\}
.
Now consider the continuous map

\Phi:B(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\ p\mapsto p\frac{\sqrt{R-d_{X}(p)}}{\|p\|}.

Let us show that
\Phi
is injective.
Indeed, let
p\neq p^{\prime}\in B(0,\varepsilon)
. If one of those points is
0
or if
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\cdot p\neq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\cdot p^{\prime}
, then clearly
\Phi(p)\neq\Phi(p^{\prime})
. Otherwise, let
\rho>0
be such that
p^{\prime}=\rho p
, and assume without loss of generality that
\rho>1
.
Note that the definition of
x(p)
above depended only on
p/\|p\|
; hence we can assume that
x(p)=x(p^{\prime}):=x
.
Then

\displaystyle d_{X}(p^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=\|p^{\prime}\|^{2}-2\langle p^{\prime},x\rangle+\|x\|^{2}=\rho^{2}\|p\|^{2}-2\rho\langle p,x\rangle+\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\rho^{2}-1)\|p\|^{2}-2(\rho-1)\langle p,x\rangle+d_{X}(p)^{2}.

But

\displaystyle(\rho^{2}-1)\|p\|^{2}-2(\rho-1)\langle p,x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(\rho-1)((\rho+1)\|p\|^{2}-\lambda\|p\|)

\displaystyle=(\rho-1)\|p\|(\|p^{\prime}\|+\|p\|-\lambda)\leq(\rho-1)\|p\|(2\varepsilon-\lambda)<0,

hence
d_{X}(p^{\prime})^{2}<d_{X}(p)^{2}
and
\Phi(p)\neq\Phi(p^{\prime})
.
Thus
\Phi
is injective, and Brouwer’s Theorem on the Invariance of Domain states that
\Phi(B(0,\varepsilon))
is open, and that
\Phi:B(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\Phi(B(0,\varepsilon))
is a homeomorphism.
Finally,
\|\Phi(p)\|^{2}=R-d_{X}(p)
for
p\in B(0,\varepsilon)
, hence
\|q\|^{2}=R-d_{X}\circ\Phi^{-1}(q)
for
q\in\Phi(B(0,\varepsilon))
and

d_{X}\circ\Phi^{-1}(q)=R-\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{2},

which proves the proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

\mathrm{Aff}(X)={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and that there exists
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

\Pi_{X}(z)=X
and

.
Assume that there exists
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x-z\rangle\leq 0

.

.

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality that
z=0
, and let us write
R=d_{X}(0)
.

Lemma

2.3
below states that there exists
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in X
, and such that
\max_{x\in X}\langle x,w\rangle>0

for all
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}

\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0
.
By compactness, there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\min_{\|w\|=1,\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0}\max_{x\in X}\langle w,x\rangle>\lambda.

(3)

Up to rescaling
v
and applying an isometric change of coordinates, we can assume that
v
is equal to the
m
-th standard basis vector
e_{m}
.
Let
\rho\in(0,\lambda/4)
, and define
P:=\rho e_{m}
. Let also
\varepsilon\in(0,\min(\rho/2,R/2,\lambda/4))
.

Now for any
p\in B(0,\varepsilon)

\pi(p)
denote the first

Consider the continuous map

\Psi:B(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m-1},\ p\mapsto(d_{X}(p)-R,\pi(p)).

The situation is illustrated in Figure

We are going to show that thanks to our careful choices of
P
and
\varepsilon

\Psi
is injective.

It is obvious that
\Psi(p)\neq\Psi(p^{\prime})
if
p,p^{\prime}\in B(0,\varepsilon)
do not belong to the same line passing through
P
.
Thus we only need to show that
d_{X}
is injective on the intersection of any line passing through
P
with
B(0,\varepsilon)
.
Let
p^{\prime}\neq p^{\prime\prime}\in B(0,\varepsilon)
belong to the same line passing through
P
.
As
d_{X}
is Lipschitz, Lebourg’s Mean Value Theorem

Cla90

states that there exists
p\in[p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}]

d_{X}(p^{\prime\prime})-d_{X}(p^{\prime})\in\{\langle u,p^{\prime\prime}-p^{\prime}\rangle:\ u\in\partial d_{X}(p)\},

(4)

where
\partial d_{X}(p)
is Clarke’s generalized gradient for
d_{X}
at
p
.
Theorem 2.5.1 from
[
Cla90
]
then shows that

\partial d_{X}(p)=\mathrm{Conv}\left(\left\{\frac{p-x}{\|p-x\|}:\ x\in\Pi_{X}(p)\right\}\right).

As
p,p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}
belong to the same line passing through
P
, we have
p^{\prime\prime}-p^{\prime}=\mu(P-p)
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mu>0
.
Let
x\in\Pi_{X}(p)
;
as in
Proposition

2.1
,
x
is necessarily such that
\langle p,x\rangle=\max_{x^{\prime}\in X}\langle p,x^{\prime}\rangle
.

If
p_{m}\leq 0
, then Inequality (
3
) states that
\langle p,x\rangle>\lambda\|p\|
, hence

\displaystyle\langle P-p,p-x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle P,p\rangle-\langle P,x\rangle-\|p\|^{2}+\langle p,x\rangle>-\|P\|\|p\|-\|p\|^{2}+\lambda\|p\|

\displaystyle\geq\|p\|(-\rho-\varepsilon+\lambda)\geq\lambda\|p\|/2,

where we also use the fact that
\langle P,x\rangle\leq 0
by construction.
Likewise, if
p_{m}>0
, then

\displaystyle\langle P-p,p-x\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle P-p,p\rangle-\langle P-p,x\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\rho p_{m}-\|p\|^{2}-\langle P-p_{m}e_{m},x\rangle+\langle p-p_{m}e_{m},x\rangle.

But Inequality (
3
) applied to
p-p_{m}e_{m}
states that
\langle p-p_{m}e_{m},x\rangle\geq\lambda\|p-p_{m}e_{m}\|
.
As
\langle e_{m},x\rangle\leq 0
, we also have that

-\langle P-p_{m}e_{m},x\rangle=-(\rho-p_{m})\langle e_{m},x\rangle\geq-(\rho-\varepsilon)\langle e_{m},x\rangle\geq 0.

Hence

\displaystyle\langle P-p,p-x\rangle\geq

\displaystyle\rho p_{m}-\|p\|^{2}+\lambda\|p-p_{m}e_{m}\|\geq\rho\|p\|-\|p\|^{2}\geq\rho\|p\|/2,

where we use the facts that
\rho\leq\lambda
, that
p_{m}+\|p-p_{m}e_{m}\|\geq\|p\|
and that
\|p\|\leq\varepsilon<\rho/2
.
By combining the cases
p_{m}\leq 0
and
p_{m}>0
, we find that there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\langle P-p,p-x\rangle\geq C\|p\|.

Thus for any
x\in X
such that
d(x,p)=d_{X}(p)
,

\langle p-x,p^{\prime\prime}-p^{\prime}\rangle=\langle p-x,\mu(P-p)\rangle\geq C\mu\|p\|,

satisfies

\langle u,p^{\prime\prime}-p^{\prime}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\|p-x\|}C\mu\|p\|\geq\frac{C\mu}{2R}\|p\|.

Equation (

d_{X}(p^{\prime\prime})-d_{X}(p^{\prime})\geq\frac{C\mu}{2R}\|p\|.

This is enough to immediately conclude that
d_{X}(p^{\prime\prime})\neq d_{X}(p^{\prime})
when the segment
[p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}]
does not contain
0
(as in that case

). When it does, additional elementary arguments yield the same conclusion.

d_{X}

\Psi
.

Brouwer’s Theorem on the Invariance of Domain then states that
\Psi(B(0,\varepsilon))
is open, and that
\Psi:B(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\Psi(B(0,\varepsilon))

As
\Psi(p)_{1}=d_{X}(p)-R
for any
p\in B(0,\varepsilon)

d_{X}\circ\Psi^{-1}(q)=R+q_{1}

for any

, which proves the proposition.

Let us now prove the Lemma used in the proof of

2.2
.
Remember that the dual of a cone
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is defined as
C^{*}=\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\ \langle v,u\rangle\geq C\ \forall u\in C\}

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be such that
\mathrm{Aff}(X)={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Assume that there exists
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v_{0},x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in X
.
Then there exists
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in X
and that

\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \max_{x\in X}\langle x,w\rangle>0

for all
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
.

Proof.

Consider the closed convex cone
K:=\{w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\ \langle w,x\rangle\geq 0\ \forall x\in X\}
. By hypothesis, we know that
K\neq\{0\}
, as
v_{0}\in K
.
Consider now the dual cone
K^{*}
of
K
.
The cone
K^{*}
is
salient
, i.e. it does not contain any non-trivial linear subspace, as otherwise the set
X
would be included in the orthogonal of such a subspace, contradicting the hypothesis that
\mathrm{Aff}(X)={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
It is known that the interior of the dual of any closed, convex and salient cone is non-empty (e.g. as a consequence of
[
Roc70
, Theorems 6.2 and 14.1]
), hence the convex cone
C:=K^{*}
has non-empty interior.
Remember that the dual of the dual of a closed convex cone is equal to the cone itself (see e.g.
[
Roc70
, Chapter 14]
); thus
C^{*}=(K^{*})^{*}=K
.

We can now apply Lemma
2.4
below to the convex cone
C
and its dual
C^{*}=K
; it states that
\mathring{C}\cap C^{*}=\mathring{(K^{*})}\cap K\neq\emptyset
.
Let
v
be such that
-v\in\mathring{(K^{*})}\cap K
. As
K\neq\{0\}
implies that
0\not\in\mathring{(K^{*})}
,
v
is necessarily non-zero.
Since
-v\in K
, we have
\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in X
.
Furthermore, the fact that
-v
belongs to the interior of
K^{*}
directly implies that
\langle v,w\rangle<0
for all
w\in K\backslash\{0\}
.
Let us show that this means that
\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\Rightarrow\max_{x\in X}\langle x,w\rangle>0
for all
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
, which is enough to conclude the proof.

Indeed, if
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash\{0\}
is such that
\max_{x\in X}\langle x,w\rangle\leq 0
, then
\langle x,-w\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in X
, hence
-w\in K\backslash\{0\}
. As shown above, this means that
\langle v,-w\rangle<0
, or equivalently that
\langle v,w\rangle>0
.
By contraposition, this proves our claim.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a convex cone whose interior
\mathring{C}
is non-empty, and assume that
C^{*}\neq\{0\}
.
Then
\mathring{C}\cap C^{*}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathring{C}\cap C^{*}=\emptyset
. Then
\mathring{C}
is the interior of a convex set, and as such it is a non-empty convex open set. The dual cone
C^{*}
is a non-empty convex set. Hence we can apply Hahn-Banach’s Separation Theorem, which states that there exists
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that

\sup_{u\in\mathring{C}}\langle v,u\rangle\leq\inf_{w\in C^{*}}\langle v,w\rangle

(5)

and

\langle v,u\rangle<\inf_{w\in C^{*}}\langle v,w\rangle

(6)

for all
u\in\mathring{C}
; in particular,
v\neq 0
.
As
\inf_{w\in C^{*}}\langle v,w\rangle\in\{0,-\infty\}
, Inequality (
6
) implies that
\inf_{w\in C^{*}}\langle v,w\rangle=0
.
This, in turn, means that
\sup_{u\in\mathring{C}}\langle v,u\rangle=0
.

Consequently, the vector
-v
is such that
\inf_{u\in\mathring{C}}\langle-v,u\rangle=0
.
As
\overline{\mathring{C}}=\overline{C}\supseteq C
(this holds for any convex set), and as
u\mapsto\langle-v,u\rangle
is continuous,
we have
\inf_{u\in C}\langle-v,u\rangle=0
.
This means that
-v\in C^{*}
, but we have shown above that
\inf_{w\in C^{*}}\langle v,w\rangle=0
, hence
\langle v,-v\rangle=-\|v\|^{2}\geq 0
, which contradicts the fact that
v\neq 0
.

∎

We can now prove
Theorem

1.2
. It is essentially a combination of Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

By definition, the only differential critical points of
d_{X}
are the points
z\not\in X
such that
z\in\mathrm{Conv}(\Pi_{X}(z))
, and the points
x\in X
.
The fact that differential regular points of
d_{X}
are also topologically regular is shown in
[
ACSD23
, Section 7]
(see also
[
Gro93
]
), which proves the last point of the statement of
Theorem

1.2
.

If
x\in X
, then
d_{X}
coincides with
d_{\{x\}}
on a small ball
B(x,\varepsilon)
around
x
.
Consider the map
\Xi:B(x,\varepsilon)\rightarrow B(x,\varepsilon^{2}),p\mapsto x+\frac{(p-x)}{\sqrt{\|p-x\|}}
.
It is a homeomorphism, and
d_{X}\circ\Xi^{-1}(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{2}
for all
q\in B(x,\varepsilon^{2})
, hence
x
is a non-degenerate topological critical point of
d_{X}
of index
0
. This proves the first point of the statement of the Theorem.

Now let
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash X
be such that
z\in\mathrm{Conv}(\Pi_{X}(z))
.
On a small open neighborhood
W
of
z
, the functions
d_{X}
and
d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}
coincide.
Up to an isometric change of coordinates, we can assume that
z=0
and that
\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))={\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times\{0\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for some
m\geq 1
(note that with this change of coordinates,
\mathrm{Span}(\Pi_{X}(z)-z)=\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))
).
Let us also write
R:=d_{X}(z)
.

If there exists no
v\in\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\Pi_{X}(z)
, then
Proposition

2.1
applies to the set
\Pi_{X}(z)
and the subspace
\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and there exist open neighborhoods
U_{1},U_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
of
0
and a homeomorphism
\Phi_{1}:(U_{1},0)\rightarrow(U_{2},0)
such that
d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}\circ\Phi_{1}^{-1}(q)=R-\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{2}
for all
q\in U_{2}
.
We define the homeomorphism

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}:U_{2}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Phi_{2}(U_{2})=:U_{3},

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\longmapsto\frac{p}{\|p\|}\sqrt{R^{2}-(R-\|p\|^{2})^{2}},

and we write
p=(p^{m},p^{n-m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-m}
for all
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Consider the homeomorphism

\displaystyle\Phi_{3}:U_{1}\times B^{n-m}(0,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow U_{3}\times B^{n-m}(0,\varepsilon),

\displaystyle(p^{m},p^{n-m})

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\Phi_{2}\circ\Phi_{1}(p^{m}),p^{n-m}),

where
B^{n-m}(0,\varepsilon)
is the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n-m}
.
For any
p=(p^{m},p^{n-m})
close to
0
, we have
d_{X}(p)=d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}(p)=\sqrt{(d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}(p^{m}))^{2}+\|p^{n-m}\|^{2}}
, hence

\displaystyle d_{X}\circ\Phi_{3}^{-1}(q)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}\circ\Phi_{1}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{2}^{-1}(q^{m}))^{2}+\|q^{n-m}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(R-\|\Phi_{2}^{-1}(q^{m})\|^{2})^{2}+\|q^{n-m}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(R-(R-\sqrt{R^{2}-\|q^{m}\|^{2}}))^{2}+\|q^{n-m}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{R^{2}-\|q^{m}\|^{2}+\|q^{n-m}\|^{2}}=\sqrt{R^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}q_{i}^{2}}.

Up to making the sets
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}
smaller, Morse’s Lemma then states that there exists a diffeomorphism
\Phi_{4}:U_{3}\times B^{n-m}(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow\Phi_{4}(U_{3}\times B^{n-m}(0,\varepsilon))=:U_{4}
that maps
0
to
0
such that

d_{X}\circ\Phi_{3}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{4}^{-1}(q)=R-\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}q_{i}^{2}.

Hence
z
is a non-degenerate topological critical point of index
m=\dim(\mathrm{Span}(\Pi_{X}(z)-z))
of
d_{X}
.

Likewise, if there exists
v\in\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))\backslash\{0\}
such that
\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\Pi_{X}(z)
, then
Proposition

2.2
applies to the set
\Pi_{X}(z)
and the subspace
\mathrm{Aff}(\Pi_{X}(z))\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and there exist open neighborhoods
V_{1},V_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
of
0
and a homeomorphism
\Psi_{1}:V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
such that
d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}\circ\Psi_{1}^{-1}(q)=R+q_{1}
for all
q\in V_{2}
.
As in the case above, one can define (using
\Psi_{1}
) a continuous local change of coordinates
\Psi
defined on an open neighborhood of
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

d_{\Pi_{X}(z)}\circ\Psi(q)=R+q_{1}
(the Implicit Function Theorem is applied instead of Morse’s Lemma).

Hence
z
is a topological regular point: this completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
