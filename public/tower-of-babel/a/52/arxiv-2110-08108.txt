Subhash Chand Bhoria, Pramod Eyyunni, Bibekananda Maji

Abstract
. We find an interesting refinement of a result due to Andrews and Newman, that is, the sum of minimal excludants over all the partitions of a number n n equals the number of distinct parts partitions of n n into two colors. In addition, we also study k t ​ h k^{th} moments of minimal excludants by means of generalizing the aforementioned result. At the end, we also provide an alternate proof of a beautiful identity due to Hopkins, Sellers and Stanton.



1.
Introduction

Andrews and Newman
[
2
]
defined
the minimal excludant of an integer partition
\pi
as the least positive integer missing from the partition, denoted by
\text{mex}(\pi)
. For example, with
n=5
, the values of minimal excludants for the partitions of
n
are:
\text{mex}(5)=1,\text{mex}(4+1)=2,\text{mex}(3+2)=1,\text{mex}(3+1+1)=2,\text{mex}(2+2+1)=3,\text{mex}(2+1+1+1)=3,\text{mex}(1+1+1+1+1)=2
.
They discovered an elegant identity
[
2
, p. 250, Theorem 1.1]
involving the quantity
\sigma\text{mex}(n)
, which denotes the sum of minimal excludants over all the partitions of
n
. The identity is as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma\text{mex}(n)q^{n}=(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{2}(n)q^{n},

(1.1)

where
D_{2}(n)
represents the number of distinct parts partitions of
n
into two colors.
The same identity, under a different terminology, was obtained earlier by Grabner and Knopfmacher
[
18
, p. 5, Equation (4.2)]
. They studied the concept of ‘
smallest gap
’ in a partition that has exactly the same meaning as that of ‘minimal excludant’.
The identity (
1.1
) has also been proved by Ballantine and Merca
[
5
]
, employing purely combinatorial tools. For recent progress in this area, interested readers may refer to
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
13
]
and
[
14
]
. Here, and throughout the paper,
|q|<1
and

\displaystyle(a;q)_{0}:

\displaystyle=1,\qquad

\displaystyle(a;q)_{n}:

\displaystyle=(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots(1-aq^{n-1}),\qquad n\geq 1,

\displaystyle(a;q)_{\infty}:

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}(a;q)_{n}.

The following notations shall also be used throughout the sequel:

•

\mathcal{P}(n):=
the set of all partitions of
n
,

•

p(n):=
the number of partitions of
n
,

•

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{o}(n):=
the set of distinct parts partitions of
n
into two colors, with an odd number of parts,

•

\mathcal{D}_{2}^{e}(n):=
the set of distinct parts partitions of
n
into two colors, with an even number of parts.

Instead of summing the minimal excludants over all partitions of
n
, as done by Andrews and Newman in
[
2
]
, we slightly modify the definition and consider two such sums parity wise (over odd and even minimal excludants separately). Thus, for a positive integer
n
, we define the following functions:

\displaystyle\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2\nmid\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\text{mex}(\pi),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}\text{mex}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2|\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\text{mex}(\pi).

(1.3)

For instance, with
n=5
, one can observe that
\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(5)=1+1+3+3=8
and
\sigma_{e}\text{mex}(5)=2+2+2=6
.

A refinement generally provides extra information as compared to its original result. The idea of refinement is not new as there are many examples available throughout the history of partitions that are refinements of well-known results. One of the earliest and most famous partition identities, due to Euler, states that the partitions of an integer into odd parts are equinumerous with those into distinct parts. A familiar refinement of this identity reads as follows:

The number of partitions of
n
into an even (resp. odd) number of odd parts equals the number of partitions of
n
into distinct parts, where the number of odd parts is even (resp. odd).

2.
Main Results

In this section, we state our main results, the first of which connects the sum of minimal excludants considered parity wise with the number of distinct parts partitions into two colors, according to the parity of their number of parts. We denote
D_{2}^{e}(n)
(resp.
D_{2}^{o}(n)
) to be the number of distinct parts partitions of
n
into two colors, with an even (resp. odd) number of parts.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
|q|<1,
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{o}\emph{mex}(n)q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}+(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}}{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{2}^{e}(n)q^{n},

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{e}\emph{mex}(n)q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}-(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}}{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{2}^{o}(n)q^{n}.

(2.2)

Now we shall state an important proposition, that will be a vital ingredient in the proof of later results.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
p^{\rm{mex}}(m,n)
be the number of partitions of
n
whose minimal excludant is
m
. Then for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p^{\rm{mex}}(m,n)z^{m}=p(n)+(z-1)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p\Big(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\Big)z^{m}.

(2.3)

An immediate consequence of the above proposition arises by substituting
z=-1
in (
2.3
), which is a result due to Hopkins, Sellers and Stanton
[
20
, p. 5, Eq. (3)]
, that is,

\displaystyle o(n)-e(n)=p(n)+2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right),

where
o(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
with an odd minimal excludant and
e(n)
counts those with an even minimal excludant. Andrews-Newmann
[
3
, Theorem 2]
and Hopkins-Sellers
[
19
, Theorem 1]
independently showed that
o(n)
equals the number of partitions of
n
with non-negative crank. This has been significantly generalized by Hopkins, Sellers and Stanton
[
20
, Theorem 2]
recently.

2.1.
The
k^{th}
moments of minimal excludants

The main objective of this section is to study sums involving
k^{th}
powers of minimal excludants and generalize the result of Andrews and Newman, namely, (
1.1
).
To this end, we define the following functions:

\displaystyle\sigma^{(k)}\text{mex}(n):

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)}\left(\text{mex}(\pi)\right)^{k},

\displaystyle{\bar{\sigma}}^{(k)}\text{mex}(n):

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)}(-1)^{\text{mex}(\pi)-1}\left(\text{mex}(\pi)\right)^{k}.

When
k=1
, we see that
\sigma^{(1)}\text{mex}(n)=\sigma\text{mex}(n)
and we denote
{\bar{\sigma}}^{(1)}\text{mex}(n)
by
{\bar{\sigma}}\text{mex}(n)
. As a consequence of the next theorem we get exact formulae for
\sigma^{(k)}\text{mex}(n)
and
{\bar{\sigma}}^{(k)}\text{mex}(n)
in terms of partition functions.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the following identity holds true:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p^{{\rm mex}}(m,n)m^{k}z^{m}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left((m+1)^{k}z^{m+1}-m^{k}z^{m}\right)p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right).

(2.4)

In particular,
z=1
gives us

\sigma^{(k)}{\rm mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left((m+1)^{k}-m^{k}\right)p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right),

(2.5)

and
z=-1
yields

{\bar{\sigma}}^{(k)}\emph{mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\left((m+1)^{k}+m^{k}\right)p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right).

(2.6)

Remark 1
.

We note that the sums on the right hand sides of the above theorem are in fact finite, because by convention we regard
p(n)
to be zero when
n<0
. The comment also holds for all such ‘infinite-looking’ partition sums in the sequel.

The last theorem also implies, as a corollary, two elegant identities. The first one below arises in the second proof of the main result of Andrews and Newman on
\sigma\text{mex}(n)

[
2
, p. 251]
and the second is an analogous identity for
\bar{\sigma}\text{mex}(n)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

If
n
is a positive integer, then we have

\displaystyle\sigma\emph{mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right),

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}\emph{mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}(2m+1)p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right).

(2.8)

In the same spirit, we also define parity based sums involving
k^{th}
powers of minimal excludants, namely,

\displaystyle\sigma_{o}^{(k)}\text{mex}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2\nmid\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\left(\text{mex}(\pi)\right)^{k},\quad\sigma_{e}^{(k)}\text{mex}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2|\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\left(\text{mex}(\pi)\right)^{k}.

These two quantities admit nice formulae, which is the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
k
and
n
are positive integers. Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{o}^{(k)}\emph{mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\{\delta_{m}(m+1)^{k}-(1-\delta_{m})m^{k}\right\}p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right),

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}^{(k)}\emph{mex}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\{(1-\delta_{m})(m+1)^{k}-\delta_{m}m^{k}\right\}p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right),

(2.10)

where the function
\delta_{m}
is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{m}:=\frac{1+(-1)^{m}}{2}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if m is even},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We mention an immediate corollary of the above theorem that gives explicit formulae for the functions
\sigma_{o}^{(1)}\text{mex}(n)
and
\sigma_{e}^{(1)}\text{mex}(n)
, which are nothing but
\sigma_{o}{\rm mex}(n)
and
\sigma_{e}{\rm mex}(n)
respectively, as defined in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
t_{m}=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}
be the
m^{th}
triangular number.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

\displaystyle\sigma_{o}\emph{mex}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)\left\{p\left(n-t_{2m}\right)-p\left(n-t_{2m+1}\right)\right\}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\left\{p(n)-p(n-1)\right\}+3\left\{p(n-3)-p(n-6)\right\}+\cdots,

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{e}\emph{mex}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+2)\left\{p\left(n-t_{2m+1}\right)-p\left(n-t_{2m}\right)\right\}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=2\left\{p(n-1)-p(n-3)\right\}+4\left\{p(n-6)-p(n-10)\right\}+\cdots.

The upcoming section is devoted to prove our main results.

3.
Proof of main results

Let
p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)
denote the number of partitions of
n
whose minimal excludant is
2m+1
. Thus, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)q^{n}=\frac{\displaystyle q^{1+2+\cdots+2m}}{\displaystyle\prod_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq{2m+1}\end{subarray}}}^{\infty}(1-q^{r})}=\frac{q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}(1-q^{2m+1})}{(q;q)_{\infty}}.

(3.1)

Now we introduce a two parameter function
L(z,q)
with complex variables
z
and
q
, where the exponent of
z
keeps track of the odd minimal excludant
2m+1
. Thus,

\displaystyle L(z,q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)z^{2m+1}q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{2m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{2m+1}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}(1-q^{2m+1}),

(3.2)

where we used (
3.1
) in the final step. Upon differentiating
L(z,q)
with respect to
z
and putting
z=1
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}L(z,q)\Bigg|_{z=1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)\Bigg)q^{n}.

(3.3)

Observe that the inner sum of the right hand side above is

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2\nmid\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\text{mex}(\pi)=\sigma_{o}\text{\text{mex}}(n).

(3.4)

After making use of (
3.4
), (
3.2
) in (
3.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{2m+1}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}\left(1-q^{2m+1}\right)\right)\Bigg]_{z=1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}\left(1-q^{2m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)q^{2m+1\choose 2}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)q^{2m+1\choose 2}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+2)q^{2m+2\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg).

(3.5)

Now multiplying and dividing by
2
in the right hand side of the last equality (
3.5
), and then simplifying yields,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+2)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+1\choose 2}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+2\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{2m+2\choose 2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{m+1\choose 2}\Bigg).

(3.6)

Utilizing
(
3.6
)
in (
3.5
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{m+1\choose 2}\Bigg).

(3.7)

We now recall two important
q
-series identities, namely, Jacobi’s identity
[
8
, Theorem (1.3.9)]
and a theta function identity of Ramanujan
[
8
, Eq. (1.3.14), p.11]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}

\displaystyle=(q;q)_{\infty}^{3},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{m+1\choose 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}.

We substitute these
q
-products for the two series in the right hand side of (
3.7
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n)q^{n}=\frac{1}{2(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg((q;q)_{\infty}^{3}+\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}^{3}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}+\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big((q;q)_{\infty}^{2}+(-q;q)_{\infty}^{2}\Big),

where the last equality follows from Euler’s identity, i.e.,
(-q;q)_{\infty}=1/(q;q^{2})_{\infty}
.
Observe that the last expression above can be written as

\displaystyle(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}+(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(D_{2}^{e}(n)+D_{2}^{o}(n)\right)q^{n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(D_{2}^{e}(n)-D_{2}^{o}(n)\right)q^{n}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{2}^{e}(n)q^{n}.

(3.9)

So finally, we conclude that (
2.1
) holds true, with the proof of (
2.2
) being similar.
∎We now provide another proof of Theorem
2.1
.

3.1.
Alternate proof of Theorem
2.1

Before we get to this proof, we will require the following crucial lemma on the generating function for
\bar{\sigma}\text{mex}(n)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
|q|<1
. Then we have the following identity:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bar{\sigma}\emph{mex}(n)q^{n}=(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}.

(3.10)

We begin by writing the generating function for
p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)
, the number of partitions of
n
whose minimal excludant is
m
,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)q^{n}=\frac{\displaystyle q^{1+2+\cdots+(m-1)}}{\displaystyle\prod_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq m\end{subarray}}}^{\infty}(1-q^{r})}=\frac{q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m})}{(q;q)_{\infty}}.

(3.11)

Consider a two parameter generating function
M(z,q)
, where the exponent of
z
keeps track of the minimal excludant
m
. This means

\displaystyle M(z,q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)z^{m}q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)q^{n}

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m}),

(3.13)

after using (
3.11
). Upon differentiating
M(z,q)
, using the right hand side of (
3.12
), with respect to
z
and substituting
z=-1
, we arrive at

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}M(z,q)\Bigg|_{z=-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)\Bigg)q^{n}.

(3.14)

The inner sum in the right hand side above is actually

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)}(-1)^{\text{mex}(\pi)-1}\text{mex}(\pi)=\bar{\sigma}\text{\text{mex}}(n).

Putting this back in (
3.14
) gives us

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\bar{\sigma}\text{mex}(n)q^{n}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}M(z,q)\Bigg|_{z=-1}.

(3.15)

We now compute the right hand side above by invoking the expression for
M(z,q)
in (
3.13
), that is,

\displaystyle\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m})\right)\Bigg]_{z=-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}q^{m\choose 2}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}q^{{m\choose 2}+m}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}m(-1)^{m-1}q^{{m+1\choose 2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)(-1)^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}.

Recall Jacobi’s identity (
3.8
), by which the last expression above turns into
\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\times(q;q)^{3}_{\infty}=(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}
. Putting this in (
3.15
) gives us the desired identity (
3.10
).
∎

To prove identity (
2.1
) in Theorem
2.1
, we take the help of Lemma
3.1
and (
1.1
). Adding (
1.1
) and (
3.10
) together gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sigma\text{mex}(n)+\bar{\sigma}\text{mex}(n)\right)q^{n}=(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}+(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}.

(3.16)

Here, note that the summand on the left hand side can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma\text{mex}(n)+\bar{\sigma}\text{mex}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)}\text{mex}(\pi)-\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)}(-1)^{\text{mex}(\pi)}\text{mex}(\pi)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2\nmid\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\text{mex}(\pi)=2\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n).

Substituting this value into (
3.16
), we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n)q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q)^{2}_{\infty}+(q;q)^{2}_{\infty}}{2}.

We have already interpreted the sum on the right hand side of the above equation in (
3
).
Thus, we conclude that (
2.1
) holds true. The proof of (
2.2
) also goes on similar lines. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We rewrite the double series
M(z,q)
, as defined in (
3.13
), as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(m,n)z^{m}q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m}).

(3.17)

We proceed with the right hand side of the above identity as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m\choose 2}(1-q^{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m\choose 2}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{n}\left(z\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{n}\left(1+(z-1)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{m}q^{m+1\choose 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(n)q^{n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(z-1)z^{m}p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right)q^{n}.

(3.18)

Comparing the coefficient of
q^{n}
on the left side of (
3.17
) with that on the right side of (
3.18
), we obtain (
2.3
). This completes the proof.
∎

2.3

Note that the left hand side of the above equation is

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)\\
2\nmid\text{mex}(\pi)\end{subarray}}\left(\text{mex}(\pi)\right)^{k}=2\sigma_{o}^{(k)}\text{mex}(n).

Putting this together with (

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\left\{\frac{1+(-1)^{m}}{2}\right\}(m+1)^{k}-\left\{\frac{1-(-1)^{m}}{2}\right\}m^{k}\right)p\left(n-\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\right)

where
\delta_{m}=\displaystyle\frac{1+(-1)^{m}}{2}

4.
An alternate proof of a result due to Hopkins, Sellers and Stanton

Recall that
o(n)
is the number of partitions of
n
with an odd minimal excludant. Denote by
o_{1}(n)
, the number of partitions of
n
with minimal excludant congruent to
1
modulo
4
and similarly, let
o_{3}(n)
count those whose minimal excludant is congruent to
3
modulo
4
. So,
o(n)=o_{1}(n)+o_{3}(n)
. Also, we let
q(n)
represent the number of partitions of
n
into distinct parts. The result of Hopkins, Sellers and Stanton
[
20
, Proposition 8]
reads as follows:

Proposition 4.1
.

For
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle o_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}o_{3}(n),&\text{if n is odd},\\
o_{3}(n)+q(n/2),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let us recall
L(z,q)
from (
3.2
), which is a function with two complex parameters
z
and
q
, where the exponent of
z
keeps track of the odd minimal excludant
2m+1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)z^{2m+1}q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}z^{2m+1}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}(1-q^{2m+1}).

Now we substitute
z=i
in the above identity and cancel an
i
from both sides to get

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)(-1)^{m}q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}(1-q^{2m+1}).

(4.1)

We first simplify the left hand side as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(2m+1,n)(-1)^{m}q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(4m+1,n)-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p^{\text{mex}}(4m+3,n)\Bigg)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)\Big)q^{n}.

(4.2)

Now we focus on the right hand side of (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}(1-q^{2m+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{{2m+1}\choose 2}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{{2m+2}\choose 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}q^{{2j+1}\choose 2}+\sum_{j=-\infty}^{-1}(-1)^{j}q^{{2j+1}\choose 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}q^{{2j+1}\choose 2},

(4.3)

where the right most sum in the second equality arises by substituting
j=-(m+1)
.
Putting (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) into the equation (
4.1
) gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)\Big)q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}q^{{2j+1}\choose 2}.

(4.4)

To write the right hand side above as a
q
-product, we employ Jacobi triple product identity
[
8
, p. 10, Theorem 1.3.3]
. This states that for
z\neq 0
and
|q|<1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}z^{j}q^{j^{2}}=(-zq;q^{2})_{\infty}(-q/z;q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}.

(4.5)

Replacing
q
by
q^{2}
, and then letting
z\rightarrow-q
in (
4.5
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}(-q)^{j}q^{2j^{2}}=(q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}(q;q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}q^{{2j+1}\choose 2}=(q;q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}.

(4.6)

Now utilizing (
4.6
) in (
4.4
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)\Big)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}}\times(q;q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{4})_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}=(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty},

(4.7)

where that last step follows by Euler’s identity
(-q;q)_{\infty}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}
. Now keeping in mind that
(-q;q)_{\infty}
is the generating function for distinct parts partitions, we can rephrase (
4.7
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)\Big)q^{n}=(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q(n)q^{2n}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)\Big)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q(n/2)q^{n}.

Since
q(n/2)
is non-zero only when
n/2
is a natural number, we thus have

\displaystyle o_{1}(n)-o_{3}(n)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if}~n~\text{is odd},\\
q(n/2),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.
Concluding Remarks

Grabner and Knopfmacher
[
18
, Eq. (4.7)]
showed that

\sigma\textup{mex}(n)
satisfies the following Hardy-Ramanujan type asymptotic formula:

\displaystyle\sigma\textup{mex}(n)\sim\frac{1}{4\sqrt[4]{6n^{3}}}\exp\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\right)\quad\textrm{as}\,\,n\rightarrow\infty.

They
[
18
, Theorem 3]
also obtained a Hardy-Ramanujan-Rademacher type exact formula for
\sigma\textup{mex}(n)
. It will be highly desirable to obtain asymptotic formulae for
\sigma_{o}\text{mex}(n)
and
\sigma_{e}\text{mex}(n)
and their moments, and to do that the identities (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) might be useful. It would also be interesting to find a combinatorial proof of the main result, Theorem
2.1
, in the manner of Ballantine and Merca
[
5
]
, who proved the original result (
1.1
) of Andrews and Newman combinatorially.
