LLM-Generated Tips Rival Expert-Created Tips in Helping Students Answer Quantum-Computing Questions
Lars Krupp, Jonas Bley, Isacco Gobbi, Alexander Geng, Sabine Müller, Sungho Suh, Ali Moghiseh, Arcesio Castaneda Medina, Valeria Bartsch, Artur Widera, Herwig Ott, Paul Lukowicz, Jakob Karolus, Maximilian Kiefer-Emmanouilidis

Abstract
Individual teaching is among the most successful ways to impart knowledge. Yet, sometimes, this method is not feasible due to large numbers of students per educator, especially in specialized subjects. The subject of quantum computing serves as a prime example facing this issue, due to its growing media presence and anticipation of future significance. Alleviating high workloads for teachers, often accompanied with individual teaching, is crucial for continuous high quality education. In this regard, leveraging Large Language Models ( LLM s) such as GPT-4 to generate educational content can be valuable support. To evaluate this concept, we conducted two complementary studies exploring the feasibility of using GPT-4 to automatically generate tips for students. Our main study consisted of a between-subject survey in which students (N = 46) solved four multiple-choice quantum computing questions with either the help of expert-created or LLM -generated tips. To correct for possible biases towards LLM s, we introduced two additional deception conditions, making some participants believe that they were given expert-created tips, when they were in fact given LLM -generated tips and vice versa. Our second study (N = 23) aimed to directly compare the LLM -generated and expert-created tips, evaluating their quality, correctness and helpfulness. Both experienced educators and students took part in the tip evaluation study. The results from both studies highlight that LLM -generated tips can be equally useful as expert-created tips. Furthermore, participants in our second study found that the LLM -generated tips were significantly more helpful and pointed better towards relevant concepts than the expert-created tips. However, LLM -generated tips were more prone to be giving away the answer easily. Lastly, we found that participants in the first study performed significantly better in answering the quantum computing questions when given tips labeled as LLM -generated, even if they were created by an expert. We argue that this phenomenon could be a placebo effect of artificial intelligence induced by the participants’ biases for LLM -generated content. Ultimately, we contribute that LLM -generated tips are good enough to be used instead of expert tips in the context of quantum computing basics. Our work highlights a direction to reduce teachers’ workloads and proposes a paradigm shift for a more individualized way of teaching.

Methods

In this work, we intend to answer two research questions through two interconnected studies. In our main study, we asked how GPT4-generated tips compare to expert-created tips in the domain of quantum computing. We conducted this study with participants (N = 46) from the
Quantum-Initiative Rhineland-Palatinate (QUIP)
winter school QUIKSTART 2024
2
2

2

https://www.quantum-rlp.de/studierende/sommer-und-winterschulen/
. Employing a between-subject design with the factors
tip creator
and
tip label
, we tasked the students with four questions about quantum computing. Students were given one tip for each question. Tip creators (
LLM
, expert) and how the tips were labeled (
LLM
, expert) were subject to the condition assigned to each participant, including two consistent conditions (creator equaled label) and two contradictory conditions (creator and label differed). The students were not made aware of the real tip creator until after completing the study.

To validate our findings, we conducted a complementary tip evaluation study (N = 23). Here we specifically asked people with the relevant competencies (both experienced educators and students) to rate how
LLM
-generated tips compare to expert-created tips.

RQ1: Can
LLM
-generated tips be used instead of expert-created tips to help students answer quantum-physics questions?

This research question is composed of two parts.

RQ1a: Is there a difference in the score, an objective measure, when using LLM-generated tips compared to expert-created tips?

To answer this question, we evaluated the scores achieved by students in practice scenario of the main study.

RQ1b: Are there subjective differences in the perception of
LLM
-generated tips and expert-created tips?

Here we evaluated the subjective measures of our studies. For the main study, we analyzed whether there are differences in perceived correctness, helpfulness, and quality between our conditions. Additionally, we conducted the tip evaluation study, where we asked students and educators to rate the helpfulness and correctness of the tips and if they gave away the answer or pointed to relevant concepts. This was done without giving them any information on the original tip creator.

RQ2: What are adverse effects of
LLM
-generated tips?

Recent work in human computer interaction has shown, that the mere label of
AI
can influence the participants’ perception, culminating in a placebo effect if participants’ are falsely told that there is an
AI
system in place
Kosch et al. (2023)
;
Kloft et al. (2024)
. To evaluate if such a bias or placebo effect exists when participants receive
LLM
-generated tips, we introduced two deception conditions to our main study, where tip creator and tip label are not the same. This method allowed us to analyze if the tip label influenced any of our metrics.
Additionally, we used the tip evaluation study to analyze if the
LLM
-generated tips gave away more of the answers compared to the expert-created questions. This could negatively impact the students learning-gains. Even though we eliminated the possibility to give wrong tips due to hallucination by manually checking each
LLM
-generated tip, we evaluated the perceived tip correctness and quality in the main study and the tip correctness in the tip evaluation study.

2.1
Multiple-Choice Question Creation

We chose topics related to the basics of quantum computing for our questions and made sure that they were covered at QUIKSTART 2024. This way, we established a common ground of knowledge for all study participants taking part in our main study.

We chose two topics and two difficulty levels within each topic. All questions and their answers can be found in the Appendix
6
. Firstly, the Bloch sphere, a typical graphical visualization of single-qubit systems. However, this representation can still cause some difficulties in learners
Hu et al. (2024)
. The first, simpler, question asks learners to identify the state
\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{1}
on the Bloch sphere. The second, more advanced, question asks the same for the state
\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}e^{i\pi/2}\ket{1}
.

For the second topic, we decided on some basics of two-qubit quantum computing: a gate identity involving two Hadamard gates and two CNOT gates (the simpler question) and the exact outcome of a circuit meant for the creation of a Bell state (the more advanced question). The gate identity asks participants to know that the property of these quantum gates is their own inverse. Bell states are the most basic two-qubit maximally entangled states that are essential for any teaching course about entanglement. The creation process of these states is therefore an important knowledge domain. In the question we used, participants were asked to predict the outcome of the
(X\otimes I)CNOT_{12}(H\otimes I)
operation on the
\ket{00}
state.

2.2
Tip Creation

For each of the four multiple-choice questions of the main study, we acquired one expert and one
LLM
tip.
The expert tips were created by two quantum physics experts with teaching experience in the field who were giving lectures at the winter school. They had access to the questions, answers and the lecture script but not to the
LLM
-generated tips, to not influence their creative process. A different quantum physics expert confirmed the correctness of the tips and that they do not give away the answer.

The
LLM
tips were generated by giving the question, answers, and lecture script explaining the quantum computing topics to GPT4 and prompting it to generate a tip for each question in a
RAG
-like process. In this way, up to five tips per question were generated. In the next step, we randomly selected a tip for each question. Each of those was later validated by a quantum computing expert.

2.3
Main Study

Our main study was a between-subject survey conducted during QUIKSTART 2024. A winter school conducted specifically for students with a background in machine learning or quantum physics. Participation was entirely voluntary and allowed students that participated in the winter school online or in person and students that did not participate in the winter school to test their quantum computing knowledge in the areas of Bloch spheres and Bell states using multiple-choice questions (
Section

2.1
). Participants were tasked to answer the given questions and were additionally provided with a tip for each of the questions. The type of tip was dependent on the randomly assigned conditions covering two factors: tip creator (
Section

2.2
) and tip label. Hence, a tip could either be created by an expert or the
LLM
. Likewise, it could either be labeled as being created by an expert or generated by an
LLM

Figure

1
). We did not include a condition where students received not tip at all. Since our work is based on scaffolding as a teaching method, we already know that providing tips is an effective improvement during learning

;

2.4.1
Tip Evaluation Study Procedure

After providing their informed consent, participants were asked to quantify their perceived knowledge of quantum physics and to answer some demographic questions. After this, the participants were shown each of the four multiple-choice questions used in the main study, their answer options, the associated correct answer, and both their
LLM
-generated and their expert-created tips. They were not informed about the creator of each tip, only that they should rate and compare them.
For each tip, participants answered if it was helpful, correct, gave away the correct answer, and effectively points towards relevant concepts on a scale of 0 to 100, with 0 being the maximum disagreement, and 100 the maximum agreement (see
Table

3
). Furthermore, for each question, the participants decided which tip, if any, was better. Finally, we asked the participants to explain their reasoning for that decision. This allowed us to qualitatively evaluate their responses. The whole study took approximately 10 minutes.

4
Discussion

Our results highlighted potential benefits of using
LLM
s for tip creation in the domain of quantum computing. In the following, we discuss these benefits and expose pitfalls that should be avoided.

4.1
LLM
-Generated Tips Rival Expert-Created Tips

We found that participants in the tip evaluation study (
Section

3.2
) rated the helpfulness (CQ6) of
LLM
-generated tips significantly higher than that of expert-created tips. Yet, we have not found the same effect for helpfulness (CQ1) in our main study (
Section

3.1
), possibly due to the lack of direct comparison.
Participants in the main study might have rated it on how helpful the tip was to answer the question (CQ1), while participants of the tip evaluation study rated the helpfulness (CQ6) of the tips in comparison. As such, our results showcase that the helpfulness of
LLM
-generated tips is on par with expert-created tips and even prevail in a direct ranking (
RQ1b
). This is in line with other research about
LLM
-generated content
Jung et al. (2023)
.

However,
LLM
-generated tips were more prone to give away the correct answer (CQ5), possibly correlating with their perceived helpfulness (CQ6) and them pointing to relevant concepts (CQ7). Yet, this caveat was only present for the educator group in our tip evaluation study (
Figure

7
). We believe that their different perception stems from the educators’ teaching experience which the students are lacking. Consequently, there is a real possibility that
LLM
-generated tips more easily give away the correct answer (
RQ2
). This is a pitfall that needs to be avoided through proper prompt engineering
Lee et al. (2023)
or possibly using an
LLM
as a judge
Zheng et al. (2024)
that is specifically trained to evaluate whether the
LLM
-generated tip gives away the answer.

We found no significant differences between the correctness of
LLM
-generated tips and expert-created tips in the main study (CQ3) or in the tip evaluation study (CQ4). Participants rated all tips — regardless of creator or label — as correct (
Section

3.1
) contributing to answering
RQ1b
.

Finally, the
LLM
-generated tips pointed to relevant concepts (CQ7) significantly better compared to the expert-created tips, a characteristic that is highly valuable in teaching
Krathwohl (2002)
;
Erickson (2002)
.

In addition to the positive subjective perception of
LLM
-generated tips, students did not score worse in our multiple-choice questions (main study, see
Section

2.3
). Here,
LLM
-generated tips did not negatively impact students’ scores (
Section

3.1
), answering
RQ1a
.

Our results show that
LLM
s can be used successfully to generate tips in the context of basic quantum computing education, rivaling expert-created tips in all metrics, answering our first research question (
RQ1
).

4.2
Novelty of
AI
Possibly Leads to Increased Scrutiny

While the tip creator did not influence the students’ scores, a tip labeled as being
LLM
-generated (regardless of its original creator) elicited significantly higher scores.
We believe that this phenomenon occurred due to an increase of attention given to tips labeled as
LLM
-generated. Due to the exhaustive media attention given to
LLM
s since the inception of ChatGPT, participants came into contact with these models. While some might have only heard of them, others might have tried them out or may even be actively using
LLM
s for some tasks.
This knowledge could have made them aware of the persisting issues attributed to this technology. Namely hallucinations and confidently worded verbose texts lacking substance
Tonmoy et al. (2024)
. As a consequence, participants who were told that they were given an
LLM
-generated tip, might have examined the tip more carefully, thinking critically about its contents. This critical thinking might have led to a better understanding and, therefore, an improved score
Pithers and Soden (2000)
. The measurable impact of introducing
LLM
labels is an important design consideration to make and needs to be handled carefully (
RQ2
).

We envision this effect due to two interacting factors impacting the students’ perceptions of the offered support (the
AI
-generated tip) and the resulting perceived difficulty of the task.

Firstly, a measurable placebo effect has been shown to exist in previous work on human-computer interaction
Villa et al. (2023)
;
Kosch et al. (2023)
;
Kloft et al. (2024)
. While most works were only able to find this placebo effect for subjective metrics, Kloft et al.
Kloft et al. (2024)
observed a significant objective performance improvement in a study when comparing participants using an active sham
AI
with participants using an inactive sham
AI
. As such,
AI
-enabled support is seen as something desirable.

Secondly, studies in various contexts have found an impact of perception of difficulty on test performance
Hong (1999)
;
Kukla (1974)
;
Sarrazin et al. (2002)
. High difficulty leads to dissatisfaction, while perceived low difficulty can be motivating. Labeling a tip as
LLM
-generated actually had the impact of lowering the perceived difficulty of the question (see
Section

3.1
) compared to labeling a tip as expert-created, possibly reducing reluctance and increasing motivation, resulting in increased performance.

4.3
Opportunities and Challenges of LLMs for Teaching

While our work has revealed design pitfalls to avoid when working with
LLM
s, such as a possible placebo effect as well as lengthy and leading tips (
RQ2
), there is a real opportunity to use
LLM
s for generating content about the basics of quantum computing. In general,
LLM
-generated tips have performed equally well, if not better, than their expert-created counterparts. Especially in a direct comparisons,
LLM
-generated tips were favored and conveyed the concepts for students to understand and learn better that expert-created tips.

Whilst supporting educators for tip creation, we believe that
LLM
s can shine specifically when used to individualize content for education. Recent works have already successfully used
LLM
s to generate math questions and answers based on a students interests
Christ et al. (2024)
through prompt engineering
Lee et al. (2023)
.
It can be a powerful tool strongly dictating the quality of the generated content. If done right, prompt engineering can solve some of the design caveats of
LLM
-generated tips we found in our work, such as reducing the generated tip length and minimizing the occurrence of leading tips.

Ultimately, there will always be a trade-off with respect to correctness and quality of
LLM
-generated tips. We made sure that the
LLM
-generated tips in our study were correct, which was also confirmed by participants. However, there remains a risk that
LLM
-generated tips might be incorrect or misleading, especially for more complex questions
Krupp et al. (2024)
.
Although we can reduce this risk, there is no way to ensure that each tip an
LLM
generates will be correct. Consequently, the need for formal validation of
LLM
-produced content arises. While methods to validate
AI
models exist, validating them is a complex task requiring novel methods
Huang et al. (2024)
.

Especially in the domain of education, misinformation and incorrect content are detrimental for the learner’s progress
Holmes et al. (2013)
. As such, we advice for a step-by-step integration of
LLM
-generated content for education, starting with human-in-the-loop evaluations
Amirizaniani et al. (2024)
, applications in low-risk scenarios (such as tips) and potentially applying
LLM
judges
Zheng et al. (2024)
as a secondary safety net.

4.4
Limitations

Our results are limited to our study setup. They only show that
LLM
-generated tips can be used instead of expert-created tips for basic quantum computing exercises using GPT4 with a
RAG
-like approach to tip-creation. The results may change for other
LLM
s, creating tips for different domains, or increasing the question complexity. Before applying this method in practice, experts should ensure that the generated tips are of adequate quality for the questions at hand. Even using a
RAG
-like approach, the
LLM
might hallucinate, leading to wrong or misleading tips. This underlines the importance of quality control and formal verification.

Ensuring an equal level of quantum computing knowledge for our participants was challenging. In this work, we used the QUIKSTART 2024 winterschool to ensure that all participants had adequate knowledge of the topic and also used it as distribution platform, ensuring a consistent experience level among participants. Bigger cohorts can potentially be achieved by integrating the study within dedicated study programs.

5
Conclusion

LLM
-generated content has permeated almost every area in our everyday lives. In this work, we investigated the potential of
LLM
s to generate adequate educational content. In particular, we focused on how
LLM
s can support — not substitute — educators in domains with low educator-to-student ratios. Our results are promising, highlighting that
LLM
s are capable of producing high quality tips on par with expert-created tips, albeit are subject to design considerations including a possible placebo effect of
AI
and a tendency to generate leading tips. We discuss how these findings can support teaching methods pointing towards
LLM
-supported smart education, and expose challenges still to overcome when employing
LLM
-generated content.
