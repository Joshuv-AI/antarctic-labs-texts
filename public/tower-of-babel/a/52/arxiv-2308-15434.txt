Random feature approximation for general spectral methods
Mike Nguyen, Nicole Mücke

Abstract
Random feature approximation is arguably one of the most popular techniques to speed up kernel methods in large scale algorithms and provides a theoretical approach to the analysis of deep neural networks. We analyze generalization properties for a large class of spectral regularization methods combined with random features, containing kernel methods with implicit regularization such as gradient descent or explicit methods like Tikhonov regularization. For our estimators we obtain optimal learning rates over regularity classes (even for classes that are not included in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space), which are defined through appropriate source conditions. This improves or completes previous results obtained in related settings for specific kernel algorithms.

Introduction

The rapid technological progress has led to accumulation of vast amounts of high-dimensional data in recent years. Consequently, to analyse such amounts of data it is no longer sufficient to create algorithms that solely aim for the best possible predictive accuracy. Instead, there is a pressing need to design algorithms that can efficiently process large datasets while minimizing computational overhead. In light of these challenges, two fundamental algorithmic tools, fast gradient methods, and sketching techniques, have emerged. Iterative gradient methods such as acceleration methods
[
PR19
]
or stochastic gradient methods
[
CRR19
]
leading to favorable convergence rates while reducing computational complexity during learning. On the other hand sketching techniques enable the reduction of data dimension, thereby decreasing memory requirements through random projections. The allure of combining both methodologies has garnered significant attention from researchers and practitioners alike.
Especially for Kernel based algorithms various sketching tools have gained a lot of attention in recent years. For nonparametric statistical approaches kernel methods are in many applications still state of the
art and provide an elegant and effective framework to develop theoretical optimal learning bounds
[
GM17
,
LRRC20
,
LC18
]
. However those benefits come with a computational cost making these methods unfeasible when dealing with large datasets. In fact traditional kernelized learning algorithms require storing the kernel gram matrix
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}=K(x_{i},x_{j})
where
K(.,.)
denotes the kernel function and
x_{i},x_{j}
the data points. This results in a memory cost of at least
O(n^{2})
and a time cost of up to
O(n^{3})
where
n
denotes the data set size
[
SS02
]
. Most popular sketching tools to overcome these issues are Nyström approximations
[
RCR16
]
and random feature approximation (RFA)
[
ZSD+20
,
RR16
]
.
In this paper, we investigate algorithms, using the interplay of fast learning methods and RFA and analyse generalization performance of such algorithms. Related work was contributed by
[
RR16
]
and
[
CRR19
]
. They obtained optimal rates for Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) and Stochastic Gradient Descent respectively, both algorithms were combined with RFA. Using a general spectral filtering framework
[
CDV07
]
we proved fast rates for all kind of learning methods with implicit or explicit regularization. For example gradient descent, acceleration methods and we also cover the results of
[
RR16
]
for KRR. Moreover, we managed to overcome the saturation effect appearing in
[
RR16
]
and
[
CRR19
]
by providing fast rates of convergence for objectives with any degree of smoothness.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present our setting and review relevant results
on learning with kernels, and learning with random features. In Section 3, we
present and discuss our main results, while proofs are deferred to the appendix. Finally,
numerical experiments are presented in Section 4.

Setup

We let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the input space and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
be the output space.
The unknown data distribution on the data space
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is denoted by
\rho
while the marginal distribution on
\mathcal{X}
is denoted as

\rho_{X}
and the regular conditional distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
given
x\in\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\rho(\cdot|x)
, see e.g.
[
Sha03
]
.

Given a measurable function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
we further define the expected risk as

{\mathcal{E}}(g):=\mathbb{E}[\ell(g(X),Y)]\;,

(2.1)

where the expectation is taken w.r.t. the distribution
\rho
and
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

\ell(t,y)=\frac{1}{2}(t-y)^{2}
. It is known that the global minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
over the set of all measurable functions is

g_{\rho}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}y\rho(dy|x)

2.1
Motivation of Kernel Methods with RFA

Kernel methods are nonparametric approaches defined by a kernel
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, that is
a symmetric and positive definite function, and a so called regularisation function
\phi_{\lambda}
. The estimator then has the form

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}:=\phi_{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Sigma}}}\right)\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\mathbf{y},

(2.2)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\mathbf{y}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}K_{x_{i}},\,\,

\widehat{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\cdot,K_{x_{j}}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}K_{x_{j}}\,
,
K_{x}\coloneqq K(x,.)
and
\mathcal{H}
denotes the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of
K
.
[
GM17
]
established optimal rates for kernel methods of the above form. The idea of this estimator is, when the sample size
n
is large, the function
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\mathbf{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}K_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{H}
is a good approximation of its mean
\Sigma g_{\rho}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}g_{\rho}(x)K_{x}d\rho_{X}
. Hence the spectral algorithm (
2.2
) produces a good estimator
f_{\lambda}
, if
\phi_{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\Sigma}}}\right)
is an approximate inverse of
\Sigma
.
To motivate RFA we now consider the following examples. The probably most common example for explicit regularisation is KRR:

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}K\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i},x}}\right),\quad\alpha=(\mathbf{K}+\lambda nI)^{-1}y,

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{K}
denotes the kernel gram matrix
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}=K(x_{i},x_{j})
. Note that this estimator can be obtained from (
2.2
) by choosing
\phi_{\lambda}(t)=\frac{1}{t+\lambda}

[
GM17
]
. In the above formula (
2.3
) the estimator has computational costs of order
O(n^{3})
since we need to calculate the inverse of an
n
by
n
matrix. However, if we assume to have a inner product kernel
K_{M}(x,x^{\prime})=\Phi_{M}(x)^{\top}\Phi_{M}(x^{\prime})
, where
\Phi_{M}
is a feature map of dimension
M
, the computational costs can be reduced to
O(nM^{2}+M^{3})

[
RR16
]
. To also give an example of implicit regularization we here analyse an acceleration method, namely the Heavyball method which can also be derived from (
2.2
)
[
PR19
]
and is closely related to the normal gradient descent algorithm but has an additional momentum term:

\displaystyle f_{t+1}

\displaystyle=f_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(f_{t}(x_{j})-y_{j})K(x_{j},\cdot)+\beta(f_{t}-f_{t-1})\;,

(2.4)

where
\alpha>0,\beta\geq 0
describe the step-sizes. So in each iteration we have to update our estimator
f_{t}(x_{j})
for all data points. This results in a computational cost of order
O(tn^{2})
. However if we again assume to have a inner product kernel
K_{M}(x,x^{\prime})=\Phi_{M}(x)^{\top}\Phi_{M}(x^{\prime})
we can use theory of RKHS. Recall that the RKHS of
K_{M}
can be expressed as

\mathcal{H}_{M}=\{h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}|\,\,\exists\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\,\,\,s.t.\,\,\,h(x)=\Phi_{M}(x)^{\top}\theta\}

(see for example
[
SC08a
]
). Since
K_{M}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
and therefore all iterations
f_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}
, there exists some
\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that
f_{t}(x)=\Phi_{M}(x)^{\top}\theta_{t}
. This implies that instead of running (
2.4
) it is enough to update only the parameter vector:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\Phi_{M}(x_{i})^{\top}\theta_{t}-y_{j})\Phi_{M}(x_{i})+\beta(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t-1})\;.

(2.5)

The computational cost of the above algorithm (
2.4
) is therefore reduced from
O(tn^{2})
to
O(tnM)
.
The basic idea of RFA is now to consider kernels which can be approximated by an inner product
[
RR07
]
:

\displaystyle K_{\infty}(x,y)\approx K_{M}(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Phi_{M}^{(i)}(x)^{\top}\Phi^{(i)}_{M}(y),

(2.6)

where
\Phi_{M}^{(i)}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\Phi_{M}^{(i)}(x)=M^{-1/2}(\varphi^{(i)}(x,\omega_{1}),\dots,\varphi^{(i)}(x,\omega_{M}))
is a finite dimensional feature map and
\varphi^{(i)}:\mathcal{X}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with some probability space
(\Omega,\pi)
. More precisely this paper investigates RFA for kernels
K
which have an integral representation of the form

\displaystyle K_{\infty}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{(i)}(x,\omega)\varphi^{(i)}(y,\omega)d\pi(\omega).

(2.7)

Note that there are a large variety of standard kernels of the form (
2.7
) which can be approximate by (
2.6
). For example, the Linear kernel, the Gaussian kernel
[
RR16
]
or Tangent kernels
[
Dom20
]
. In contrast to
[
RR16
]
, we added an additional sum over different feature maps
\Phi_{M}^{(i)}
, for a more general setting and to cover a special case of Tangent kernels namely the Neural-Tangent Kernel (NTK)
[
JHG18
]
which provided a better understanding of neural networks in recently published papers [Paper2],
[
NS20
,
LNR21
,
MOSW22
,
OS19
]
. For one ”hidden layer” the NTK is defined as

\displaystyle K_{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,x^{\prime}}}\right)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x}}\right)\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x^{\prime}}}\right)+\tau^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{\top}x^{\prime}+\gamma^{2}}}\right)\sigma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x}}\right)\sigma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x^{\prime}}}\right)d\pi(\omega),

(2.8)

where
\tau,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma
defines the so called activation function. According to our setting the NTK from above can be recovered from (
2.7
) by setting
p=d+2
where
d
denotes the input dimension and

\varphi^{(i)}(x,\omega)=\tau x^{(i)}\sigma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x}}\right)
for
i\in[d]
and
\varphi^{(d+1)}(x,\omega)=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x}}\right)
,
\varphi^{(d+2)}(x,\omega)=\tau\gamma\sigma^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\top}x}}\right).

2.2
Kernel-induced operators and spectral regularization functions

In this subsection, we specify the mathematical background of regularized learning. It essentially repeats the setting in
[
GM17
]
in summarized form.
First we introduce kernel induced operators and then recall basic definitions of linear regularization methods based on spectral theory for self-adjoint linear operators. These are standard methods for finding stable
solutions for ill-posed inverse problems. Originally, these methods were developed in the
deterministic context (see
[
EHN96
]
). Later on, they have been applied to probabilistic problems in machine learning (see, e.g.,
[
CDV07
]
or
[
GM17
]
).

Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{M}
denotes the RKHS of the kernel
K_{M}
defined in (
2.6
).
We denote by
\mathcal{S}_{M}:{\mathcal{H}}_{M}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})
the inclusion of
{\mathcal{H}}_{M}
into
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})
for
M\in\mathbb{N}\cup\infty
.
The adjoint operator
{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{M}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{M}
is identified as

{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{M}g=\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x)K_{M,x}\rho_{X}(dx)

where
K_{M,x}
denotes the element of
\mathcal{H}_{M}
equal to the function
t\mapsto K_{M}(x,t)
. The covariance operator
\Sigma_{M}:\mathcal{H}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{M}
and the kernel integral operator
\mathcal{L}_{M}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})
are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{M}f

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{M}{\mathcal{S}}_{M}f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,K_{M,x}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{M}}K_{M,x}\rho_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}f

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{S}}_{M}{\mathcal{S}}^{*}_{M}f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)K_{M,x}\rho_{X}(dx)

which can be shown to be positive, self-adjoint, trace class (and hence is compact).
Here
K_{M,x}
denotes the element of
\mathcal{H}_{M}
equal to the function
t\mapsto K_{M}(x,t)
. The
empirical versions of these operators, corresponding formally to taking the empirical distribution of
\rho_{X}
in the above formulas, are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}:\mathcal{H}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}f}}\right)_{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,K_{M,x_{j}}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{M}},

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{M},

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}^{*}\mathbf{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}K_{M,x_{j}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{M}:=\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}^{*}\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{M}:\mathcal{H}_{M}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{M},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{M}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\cdot,K_{M,x_{j}}}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{M}}K_{M,x_{j}}.

Further let the numbers
\mu_{j}
are the positive eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{\infty}
satisfying
0<\mu_{j+1}\leq\mu_{j}
for all
j>0
and
\mu_{j}\searrow 0
.

Definition 2.1

(Regularization function)
.

Let
\phi:(0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function and write
\phi_{\lambda}=\phi(\lambda,.)
. The family
\{\phi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}

is called regularisation function, if the following condition holds:

(i)

There exists a constant
D<\infty
such that for any
0<\lambda\leq 1

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t<1}|t\phi_{\lambda}(t)|\leq D.

(2.9)

(ii)

There exists a constant
E<\infty
such that for any
0<\lambda\leq 1

\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\phi_{\lambda}(t)}}\right|\leq\frac{E}{\lambda}.

(iii)

Defining the residual
r_{\lambda}(t):=1-\phi_{\lambda}(t)t
, there exists a constant
c_{0}<\infty
such that for any
0<\lambda\leq 1

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r_{\lambda}(t)}}\right|\leq c_{0}.

(2.10)

It has been shown in e.g. Gerfo et al. (2008), Dicker et al. (2017), Blanchard and Mücke (2017) that attainable learning rates are essentially linked with the qualification of the regularization
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\phi_{\lambda}}}\right\}_{\lambda}
, being the maximal
\nu
such that for any
q\in[0,\nu]
and for any
0<\lambda\leq 1

\displaystyle\sup_{0<t\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|r_{\lambda}(t)}}\right|t^{q}\leq c_{q}\lambda^{q},

(2.11)

for some constant
c_{q}>0
.

Assumptions and Main Results

In this section we formulate our assumptions and state our main results.

Assumption 3.1

(Data Distribution)
.

There exists positive constants
Q
and

such that for all

with

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|y|^{l}d\rho(y\mid x)\leq\frac{1}{2}l!Z^{l-2}Q^{2}

\rho_{X}

is bounded almost surely. Obviously, this assumption implies that the regression function

is bounded almost surely, as

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|g_{\rho}(x)}}\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|d\rho(y\mid x)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y|^{2}d\rho(y\mid x)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq Q

Assumption 3.2

(Kernel)
.

Assume that the kernel
K_{\infty}
has an integral representation of the form (
2.7
) with

\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\varphi^{(i)}(x,\omega)|^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}
almost surely.

Assumption 3.3

(Source Condition)
.

Let
R>0
,
r>0
. Denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho_{X})
the kernel integral operator associated to

. We assume

\displaystyle g_{\rho}=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{r}h\;,

(3.1)

for some

, satisfying

.

This assumption characterizes the hypothesis space and relates to the regularity of the regression function

. The bigger
r
is, the smaller the hypothesis space is, the stronger the assumption is, and the easier the learning problem is, as
\mathcal{L}^{r_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L_{\rho_{X}}^{2}}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{r_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L_{\rho_{X}}^{2}}}\right)
if
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
.
The next assumption relates to the capacity of the hypothesis space.

Assumption 3.4

(Effective Dimension)
.

For some
b\in[0,1]
and

satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}:=\operatorname{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(\mathcal{L}_{\infty}+\lambda I)^{-1}}}\right)\leq c_{b}\lambda^{-b},\quad\text{ for all }\lambda>0

and further we assume that

.

The left hand-side of (

) is called effective dimension or degrees of freedom

]
. It is related to covering/entropy number conditions, see

]
. The condition (

, since

is a trace class operator which implies that its eigenvalues
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mu_{i}}}\right\}_{i}
satisfy

. Moreover, if the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
satisfy a polynomial decaying condition

for some

, or if
\Sigma

3.2

, or with

. The case

[
PVRB18
]
. In this paper we only investigate easy learning problems and leave the question, how many features

[

]
, open for future work.

We now derive a generalisation bound of the excess risk

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{M}

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{*}

\displaystyle\|g_{\rho}-\mathcal{S}_{M}f_{\lambda}^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{\rho}-\mathcal{S}_{M}f_{\lambda}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{x})}+\|\mathcal{S}_{M}f_{\lambda}^{*}-\mathcal{S}_{M}f_{\lambda}^{M}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{x})}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=:\text{ BIAS }+\text{ VARIANCE }.

(3.4)

We bound the bias and variance part separately in Proposition

to obtain the following theorem.

.

A.1

&E_{4}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{L}_{M}-\mathcal{L}_{\infty})\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right\|_{HS}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{4\kappa^{2}}{\lambda M}+\sqrt{\frac{4\kappa^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)}{\lambda M}}}}\right)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right\},\\
&E_{5}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{M}-\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}}\right)}}\right\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa}{\sqrt{\lambda}M}+\sqrt{\frac{4\kappa^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)}{M}}}}\right)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right\},\\
&E_{6}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}-\mathcal{L}_{M}}}\right\|_{HS}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{M}+\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{M}}}}\right)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right\}\,,\\
&E_{7}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{M}-\Sigma_{M}}}\right\|_{HS}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n}+\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}}}\right)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right\}\,.\end{aligned}

Providing Assumption
3.1
we have for any
\delta\in(0,1)
that each of the above events holds true with probability at least

.

Proof.

The bound for
E_{1}
follows exactly the same steps as in the proof of
[
LC18
]
(Lemma 18). The events
E_{2}-E_{7}

]

E_{2})
First note that
\mathcal{L}_{M}
can be expressed by

\mathcal{L}_{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)},

where
\varphi_{m}(.)=\varphi(.,\omega_{m}).
The above equality can be checked by simple calculations:

\displaystyle\langle f,\mathcal{L}_{M}g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int f(x)\int g(y)K_{M}(x,y)d\rho_{x}(y)d\rho_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\int f(x)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\int g(y)\varphi_{m}^{(i)}(y)\varphi_{m}^{(i)}(x)d\rho_{x}(y)d\rho_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\int f(x)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)}}}\right)(g)(x)d\rho_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle f,\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)}}}\right)g}}\right\rangle.

Analog we have
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varphi^{(i)}\otimes\varphi^{(i)}]
.

Now define
\mathcal{X}_{m}:=\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}-\mathcal{L}_{\infty})\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, with
\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)}
.
We now obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{X}_{1}\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right\|+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right\|\leq 2\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\lambda}:=B,

where we used for the last inequality

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right\|\leq\lambda^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}}}\right\|\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\lambda}.

For the second moment we have from Jensen-inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{X}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}_{M}^{(m)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[p\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[p\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varphi_{m}^{(i)}}}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\otimes\varphi_{m}^{(i)}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[p\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\lambda}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{L}^{(m)}_{M}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{p\kappa^{2}}{\lambda}\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-1}:=\mathcal{V}

For
\beta=\log\frac{4\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{V}}{\|\mathcal{V}\|\delta}
we have

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\log\frac{4\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)}{\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\lambda}^{-1}\|\delta}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{4\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)(\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\|+\lambda)}{\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\|\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\log\frac{4\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\|+4\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}{\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\|\delta}\leq\log\frac{4\kappa^{2}(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}(\lambda)+1)}{\|\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\|\delta}.

The claim now follows from Proposition
A.19
.

E_{3})

Proposition A.22

\displaystyle E_{8}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\Sigma_{M,\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y-\bar{g}_{\rho}}}\right)}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{M}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{4QZ\kappa}{\sqrt{\lambda}n}+\frac{4Q\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{L}_{M}}(\lambda)}}{\sqrt{n}}}}\right)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right\}\,.

Proposition A.23
