Asymptotic Symmetries and Weinberg’s Soft Photon Theorem in Mink d+2
Temple He 1, and Prahar Mitra 2

Abstract
We show that Weinberg’s leading soft photon theorem in massless abelian gauge theories implies the existence of an infinite-dimensional large gauge symmetry which acts non-trivially on the null boundaries ℐ ± {\mathscr{I}}^{\pm} of ( d + 2 ) (d+2) -dimensional Minkowski spacetime. These symmetries are parameterized by an arbitrary function ε ⁡ ( x ) {\varepsilon}(x) of the d d -dimensional celestial sphere living at ℐ ± {\mathscr{I}}^{\pm} . This extends the previously established equivalence between Weinberg’s leading soft theorem and asymptotic symmetries from four and higher even dimensions to all higher dimensions.

Introduction

Soft theorems are equations that describe how scattering amplitudes in Minkowski spacetime involving one or many low energy, or soft, massless particles factorizes into a term involving the soft gauge particles (known as the soft factor) and one involving the amplitude without the soft particles, i.e.

\begin{split}{\mathcal{A}}_{n}~~\xrightarrow{m~\text{soft particles}}~~S_{m}{\mathcal{A}}_{n-m},\end{split}

(1.1)

where
S_{m}
is the soft factor involving
m
soft particles. Such theorems were established over half a century ago in the works of Bloch, Nordseick, Low, Yennie, Frautschi, Suura
Bloch:1937pw
;
Nordsieck:1937zz
;
Low:1954kd
;
GellMann:1954kc
;
Low:1958sn
;
Yennie:1961ad
and in a more modern language by Weinberg
Weinberg:1965nx
;
Weinberg:1995mt
. A particularly interesting feature of all the soft theorems is that the soft factor is
universal
, i.e. it does not depend on the details of the theory under consideration. Rather, it depends only on the quantum numbers (linear momenta, angular momenta and charges) of the external particles involved in the scattering amplitude. Such universality naturally suggests that perhaps the soft theorems arise from some underlying symmetry, and indeed, it was relatively recently discovered that these theorems are precisely the Ward identities associated to the asymptotic symmetries of scattering amplitudes (see
Strominger:2017zoo
for a review and a comprehensive list of references).

Largely in part due to this discovery, in the last several years, there has been a resurgence of activity in the study of soft theorems, and many interesting directions have been explored. The equivalence was originally conceived for four-dimensional theories where it has been established to hold for gauge theories

;

;

;

, gravity
He:2014laa
;
Campiglia:2015yka
;
Campiglia:2015kxa
, as well as their supersymmetric counterparts
Dumitrescu:2015fej
. The equivalence has proved useful in the exploration of not just the original soft theorems, but also new subleading and sub-subleading soft theorems
Cachazo:2014fwa
;
Schwab:2014fia
;
Kalousios:2014uva
;
Luo:2014wea
;

;

;

;

. Some of these were discovered by studying known asymptotic symmetries

;

The plethora of successes in these endeavors has given confidence that perhaps every soft theorem in Minkowski spacetime can be interpreted as the Ward identity for some asymptotic symmetry. However, there is a noticeable class of theories in which the bridge between soft theorems and asymptotic symmetries is missing -- these are theories in odd spacetime dimensions. Soft theorems are true and have the same universal form in both even and odd dimensions.
2
2

2

The standard derivation of these theorems in perturbation theory relies largely on the fact that the free field propagator has the form
p^{-2}
, which is true in any dimension.
Yet, to our knowledge, there has been no successful attempt in showing the equivalence of soft theorems and asymptotic symmetries in odd dimensional theories. The fundamental issue lies in the qualitatively different properties of massless waves in even and odd dimensions. For instance, the massless Green’s function is supported entirely on the light cone in even dimensions, whereas in odd dimensions it is supported everywhere inside the lightcone
Balazs1955
;
Soodak1993
. This implies that for a complete understanding of waves in odd dimensions, one needs to study timelike infinity
i^{\pm}
as well as null infinity
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
. Another symptom of the same feature is that the near
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
expansions of massless fields is analytic in even dimensions (i.e. admits a Taylor series expansion in
r^{-1}
where
r
is the radius of the transverse sphere) and non-analytic in odd dimensions. These issues make the discussion of asymptotic symmetries particularly difficult in odd dimensions. Despite this, it would be very strange if no such relationship exists, implying that soft theorems in odd dimensions have a wholly different origin than those in even dimensions.

In this paper, we resolve the issues discussed in the previous paragraph and show that, at least in the simple example of a
U(1)
gauge theory with massless charged matter, the leading soft photon theorem is the Ward identity associated to large gauge symmetry in
all
dimensions, thereby unifying and generalizing the previous discussions from four and higher even dimensions to all dimensions. We organize the paper so that in §
2
, we study
U(1)
gauge theories in
d+2
dimensions and discuss asymptotic fall-offs of both free and interacting gauge fields. In §
3
, we impose a matching condition across spatial infinity
i^{0}
and demonstrate that the resulting Ward identity implies the leading soft photon theorem. Finally, in §
4
, we show that the matching condition implies conservation of a charge which generates large gauge transformations on the fields at
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
.

2.1
Classical Equations

We work in flat null coordinates
x^{\mu}=(u,r,x^{a})
,

by
3

3

These coordinates were utilized in the case of four dimensions in

\displaystyle X^{A}=\frac{r}{2}\left(1+x^{2}+\frac{u}{r},2x^{a},1-x^{2}-\frac{u}{r}\right),\qquad x^{2}=\delta_{ab}x^{a}x^{b}.

(2.1)

Lowercase Latin indices are raised and lowered by the Kronecker delta

and

\begin{split}ds^{2}&=\eta_{AB}dX^{A}dX^{B}=-dudr+r^{2}\delta_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b},\end{split}

(2.2)

where

is the standard Minkowski metric in Cartesian coordinates. The null boundaries

are located at

. The boundaries of

(

and are denoted by

(
{\mathscr{I}}^{-}_{\pm}
). In Appendix
A.1
, we provide more details about this coordinate system.

A
U(1)
gauge theory is described by a field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
and matter fields
\Psi_{i}
. The matter fields couple to the field strength via a conserved
U(1)
current
J_{\mu}
. Dynamics of the field strength is described by Maxwell’s equations and the Bianchi identity, given respectively by

\displaystyle e^{2}J_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\mu}F_{\mu\nu}

(2.3)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{{\alpha}}F_{\mu\nu}+\partial_{\nu}F_{{\alpha}\mu}+\partial_{\mu}F_{\nu{\alpha}}.

(2.4)

In flat null coordinates, (
2.3
) takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{2}J_{u}&=2\partial_{u}F_{ur}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial^{a}F_{ua}\\
e^{2}J_{r}&=-2\partial_{r}F_{ur}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial^{a}F_{ra}-\frac{2d}{r}F_{ur}\\
e^{2}J_{a}&=-2\partial_{r}F_{ua}-2\partial_{u}F_{ra}-\frac{2(d-2)}{r}F_{ua}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial^{b}F_{ab}.\end{split}

(2.5)

Furthermore, the Bianchi identity (
2.4
) is solved by

\begin{split}F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu},\qquad A_{\mu}\sim A_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}{\varepsilon},\qquad{\varepsilon}\sim{\varepsilon}+2\pi.\end{split}

(2.6)

Note that the theory is invariant under gauge transformations

\begin{split}A_{\mu}~\to~A_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}{\varepsilon},\qquad\Psi_{i}~\to~e^{iQ_{i}{\varepsilon}}\Psi_{i},\end{split}

(2.7)

where
Q_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is the
U(1)
charge of the field
\Psi_{i}
. Gauge transformations that vanish at infinity correspond to redundant descriptions of the same physical state and can be eliminated by a choice of gauge.

2.2
Asymptotics

In this section, we study the structure of the field strength near
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
. The general solution to (
2.3

\begin{split}F_{\mu\nu}=F_{\mu\nu}^{(R)}+F_{\mu\nu}^{(C)}.\end{split}

The radiative field

{(-)}

4
4

4

As we shall see in (

\begin{split}F_{ur}^{(R\pm)}&=O\left(|r|^{-\frac{d}{2}-1}\right)+O\left(|r|^{-d}\right),\qquad\quad F_{ur}^{(C\pm)}=O\left(|r|^{-d}\right)\\
F_{ra}^{(R\pm)}&=O\left(|r|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\right)+O\left(|r|^{-d}\right),\qquad\qquad F_{ra}^{(C\pm)}=O\left(|r|^{-d}\right)\\

F_{ab}^{(R\pm)}&=O\left(|r|^{-\frac{d}{2}+1}\right)+O\left(|r|^{-d+1}\right),\qquad F_{ab}^{(C\pm)}=O\left(|r|^{-d+1}\right).\end{split}

The leading fall-offs for the radiative part are determined by noting that the electromagnetic stress tensor components

and

\begin{split}J_{u}=O\big(|r|^{-d}\big),\qquad J_{a}=O\big(|r|^{-d}\big),\qquad J_{r}=O\big(|r|^{-d-2}\big).\end{split}

(2.10)

We would now like to determine the precise large

3.2
Ward Identity

Thus far, our discussion of fields, matching conditions, and charges has been entirely classical. We now extend the discussion to a semi-classical theory and derive a Ward identity. In such a theory, the quantity of interest is the scattering amplitude, which is the overlap between the
in
state (living on
i^{-}\cup{\mathscr{I}}^{-}
) and the
out
state (living on
i^{+}\cup{\mathscr{I}}^{+}
), i.e.

\begin{split}{\mathcal{A}}_{n}=\langle\,\text{out}\,|\,\text{in}\,\rangle,\end{split}

(3.16)

where
n
is the total number of particles in the
in
and
out
state. The classical matching condition (
3.3
) then translates to

\begin{split}\langle\,\text{out}\,|\left(Q^{+}_{\varepsilon}-Q^{-}_{\varepsilon}\right)|\,\text{in}\,\rangle=0.\end{split}

(3.17)

Using (
3.4
), we can rewrite this as

\begin{split}\langle\,\text{out}\,|\left(Q^{+S}_{\varepsilon}-Q^{-S}_{\varepsilon}\right)|\,\text{in}\,\rangle=-\langle\,\text{out}\,|\left(Q^{+H}_{\varepsilon}-Q^{-H}_{\varepsilon}\right)|\,\text{in}\,\rangle.\end{split}

(3.18)

To simplify this, we evaluate the action of the hard charge on one-particle states, i.e. the right-hand-side of (
3.18
). Let
|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle
be a massless one-particle state with charge
Q_{i}
, momentum
\vec{p}_{i}

s_{i}
. We may parameterize the momentum as

\begin{split}p_{i}^{A}={\omega}_{i}\left(\frac{1+x_{i}^{2}}{2},x_{i}^{a},\frac{1-x_{i}^{2}}{2}\right).\end{split}

(3.19)

The action of the conserved current on the bra and ket states is (see Appendix
B
for an explicit calculation for scalar fields)

\begin{split}\int_{{\mathscr{I}}^{-}}duJ_{u}^{(-,d)}(u,x)|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle&=|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle Q_{i}\delta^{(d)}\left(x-x_{i}\right)\\
\langle\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,|\int_{{\mathscr{I}}^{+}}duJ_{u}^{(+,d)}(u,x)&=Q_{i}\delta^{(d)}\left(x-x_{i}\right)|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(3.20)

It follows using (

) that

\begin{split}Q^{-H}_{\varepsilon}|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle=-Q_{i}{\varepsilon}(x_{i})|\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,\rangle,\qquad\langle\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i}\,|Q^{+H}_{\varepsilon}=-Q_{i}{\varepsilon}(x_{i})\langle\,\Psi_{i},\vec{p}_{i},s_{i}\,|.\\
\end{split}

(3.21)

Note that this is simply an infinitesimal large gauge transformation of the state. Setting

\begin{split}&\langle\,\text{out}\,|\left[\partial^{a}{\mathcal{O}}^{(+,0)}_{a}(x)-\partial^{a}{\mathcal{O}}^{(-,0)}_{a}(x)\right]|\,\text{in}\,\rangle=e\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}Q_{i}\partial^{2}\log\left[(x-x_{i})^{2}\right]\langle\,\text{out}\,|\,\text{in}\,\rangle.\end{split}

(3.22)

Here,
\eta_{i}=+1
for outgoing particles and
\eta_{i}=-1
for incoming particles.

(4.7)

It follows that the symplectic form is

\begin{split}\Omega_{\Sigma}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{e^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}\left[\delta^{\prime}{\tilde{A}}\wedge\ast\delta{\tilde{F}}-\delta{\tilde{A}}\wedge\ast\delta^{\prime}{\tilde{F}}\right]+\frac{1}{e^{2}}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left[\delta^{\prime}\psi\ast\delta F-\delta\psi\ast\delta^{\prime}F\right].\end{split}

(4.8)

We note that modes for which
\psi|_{\partial\Sigma}=0
cannot appear in the symplectic form and need to be fixed in order to have a non-degenerate symplectic form. We assume that this has been done, and so the remaining boundary value of
\psi
symplectically couples to the boundary field strength.

The charge that generates a particular transformation
\Delta
on the phase space satisfies

\begin{split}\delta Q^{\Sigma}_{\Delta}=\Omega_{\Sigma}(\delta,\Delta).\end{split}

(4.9)

In particular, for gauge transformations
\delta_{\varepsilon}{\tilde{A}}=\delta_{\varepsilon}F=0
and
\delta_{\varepsilon}\psi={\varepsilon}
, we get

\begin{split}Q^{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{e^{2}}\int_{\partial\Sigma}{\varepsilon}\ast F.\end{split}

(4.10)

Note that the charge is non-zero only if
{\varepsilon}|_{\partial\Sigma}\neq 0
, so this means
Q^{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}
is the generator of
large gauge transformations
. To determine its form on
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
, we note that the directed area element on
{\mathscr{I}}_{\mp}^{\pm}
is

\begin{split}dS^{\pm}_{\mu\nu}=\pm d^{d}x|r|^{d}\delta^{r}_{[\mu}\delta^{u}_{\nu]}.\end{split}

(4.11)

The
\pm
sign arises from the outward-pointing null vector, which on
{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}
is
\mp\partial_{\mu}r
. Thus, we have

\begin{split}Q^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}=\pm\frac{2}{e^{2}}\int_{{\mathscr{I}}^{\pm}_{\mp}}d^{d}x\,{\varepsilon}F^{(\pm,d)}_{ur},\end{split}

(4.12)

which is precisely the charge (
3.2
).

Appendix A
Coordinate Conventions

A.1
Position Space

In this subsection, we will gain some intuition regarding the flat null coordinates utilized throughout this paper by collecting miscellaneous facts about them. Flat null coordinates
x^{\mu}=(u,x^{a},r)
are related to Cartesian coordinates
X^{A}
via

\begin{split}X^{0}=\frac{r}{2}\left(1+x^{2}\right)+\frac{u}{2},\qquad X^{a}=rx^{a},\qquad X^{d+1}=\frac{r}{2}\left(1-x^{2}\right)-\frac{u}{2}.\end{split}

(A.1)

The inverse relation is

\begin{split}u=\frac{\big(X^{0}\big)^{2}-X^{a}X_{a}-\big(X^{d+1}\big)^{2}}{X^{0}+X^{d+1}},\qquad x^{a}=\frac{X^{a}}{X^{0}+X^{d+1}},\qquad r=X^{0}+X^{d+1}.\end{split}

(A.2)

Though the coordinates are ill-defined on the hypersurface
X^{0}+X^{d+1}=0
where
r=0
, away from this point, the metric is well defined and takes the form

\begin{split}ds^{2}=\eta_{AB}dX^{A}dX^{b}&=-\big(dX^{0}\big)^{2}+dX^{a}dX_{a}+\big(dX^{d+1}\big)^{2}\\
&=-dudr+r^{2}dx^{a}dx_{a},\end{split}

(A.3)

where
\eta_{AB}=(-1,1,\ldots,1)
is the usual Minkowski metric in Cartesian coordinates. The range of the coordinates is

\begin{split}u\in{\mathbb{R}},\qquad x^{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\qquad r\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}.\end{split}

(A.4)

We note

\begin{split}\partial_{u}&=\frac{1}{2}\left[\partial_{X^{0}}-\partial_{X^{d+1}}\right]\\
\partial_{a}&=rx_{a}\left[\partial_{X^{0}}-\partial_{X^{d+1}}\right]+r\partial_{X^{a}}\\
\partial_{r}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+x^{2}\right)\partial_{X^{0}}+x^{a}\partial_{X^{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-x^{2}\right)\partial_{X^{d+1}},\\
\end{split}

(A.5)

and

\begin{split}dX^{0}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+x^{2}\right)dr+\frac{1}{2}du+rx_{a}dx^{a}\\
dX^{a}&=x^{a}dr+rdx^{a}\\
dX^{d+1}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-x^{2}\right)dr-\frac{1}{2}du-rx_{a}dx^{a}.\\
\end{split}

(A.6)

The volume form is

\begin{split}{\varepsilon}&=dX^{0}\wedge dX^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dX^{d}\wedge dX^{d+1}=\frac{1}{2}r^{d}du\wedge dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{d}\wedge dr.\end{split}

(A.7)

The nonvanishing metric components and its inverse are

\begin{split}g_{ur}=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad g_{ab}=r^{2}\delta_{ab},\qquad g^{ur}=-2,\qquad g^{ab}=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\delta^{ab}.\end{split}

(A.8)

The nonvanishing Christoffel symbols are

\begin{split}{\Gamma}^{u}_{ab}[g]=2r\delta_{ab},\qquad{\Gamma}^{a}_{rb}[g]=\frac{1}{r}\delta^{a}_{b}.\end{split}

(A.9)

A.1.1
Boundaries of Minkowski Spacetime

To determine the location of the boundaries of Minkowski spacetime in flat null coordinates, we relate them to Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates
(U,V,{\lambda},{\Theta})
, which are

\begin{split}X^{0}=\frac{V+U}{2},\qquad X^{a}=\frac{V-U}{2}\sqrt{1-{\lambda}^{2}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta}),\qquad X^{d+1}=\frac{V-U}{2}{\lambda}.\end{split}

(A.10)

Note that the radial distance is simply expressed as
R\equiv\sqrt{X^{a}X_{a}+\big(X^{d+1}\big)^{2}}=\frac{V-U}{2}
. The coordinate ranges are

\begin{split}V\geq U,\qquad{\lambda}\in(-1,1),\qquad{\Theta}\in S^{d-1}.\end{split}

(A.11)

Together, the coordinates
({\lambda},{\Theta})
parametrize
S^{d}
.
{\lambda}\in(0,1)
is the northern hemisphere and
{\lambda}\in(-1,0)
is the southern hemisphere.
{\lambda}=0
is the equator,
{\lambda}=+1
is the north pole and
{\lambda}=-1
is the south pole. Let
\big({\lambda}_{\text{ap}},{\Theta}_{\text{ap}}\big)
be the antipodal point to
({\lambda},{\Theta})
. Then

\begin{split}{\hat{n}}\big({\Theta}_{\text{ap}}\big)=-{\hat{n}}({\Theta}),\qquad{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}=-{\lambda}.\end{split}

(A.12)

Furthermore, in terms of the flat null coordinates, we have

\begin{split}u&=\frac{2UV}{V(1+{\lambda})+U(1-{\lambda})}=\frac{\big(X^{0}\big)^{2}-R^{2}}{X^{0}+{\lambda}R}\\
x^{a}&=\frac{(V-U)\sqrt{1-{\lambda}^{2}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta})}{V(1+{\lambda})+U(1-{\lambda})}=\frac{R\sqrt{1-{\lambda}^{2}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta})}{X^{0}+{\lambda}R}\\
r&=\frac{V}{2}(1+{\lambda})+\frac{U}{2}(1-{\lambda})=X^{0}+{\lambda}R.\end{split}

(A.13)

We can now determine the boundaries of Minkowski spacetime in flat null coordinates.

1.

Future Null Infinity
{\mathscr{I}}^{+}
:
This is located at
V\to+\infty
while keeping
(U,{\lambda},{\Theta})
fixed. In this limit,

\begin{split}u&\to\frac{2U}{1+{\lambda}},\qquad x^{a}\to\sqrt{\frac{1-{\lambda}}{1+{\lambda}}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta}),\qquad r\to\frac{V}{2}(1+{\lambda}).\end{split}

(A.14)

Thus, we find that
{\mathscr{I}}^{+}
is located at
r\to\infty
. At this point,
x^{a}
are the stereographic coordinates on
S^{d}
. The north pole (
\lambda=1
) is at
|x^{a}|=0
, the south pole (
\lambda=-1
) is at
|x^{a}|=\infty
, and the equator (
\lambda=0
) is on the unit circle
x^{a}x_{a}=1
.
u
is the null generator of this surface.

2.

Past Null Infinity
{\mathscr{I}}^{-}
:
This is located at
U\to-\infty
while keeping
(V,{\lambda},{\Theta})
fixed. In this limit,

\begin{split}u&\to\frac{2V}{1+{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}},\qquad x^{a}\to\sqrt{\frac{1-{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}}{1+{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}}}{\hat{n}}^{a}\big({\Theta}_{\text{ap}}\big),\qquad r\to-\frac{|U|}{2}\left(1+{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}\right).\end{split}

(A.15)

Thus, we find that
{\mathscr{I}}^{-}
is located at
r\to-\infty
. Again,
x^{a}
are the stereographic coordinates on
S^{d}
, but now with an antipodal identification. The north pole (
\lambda=1
) is at
|x^{a}|=\infty
, the south pole (
\lambda=-1
) is at
|x^{a}|=0
, and the equator (
\lambda=0
) is on the unit circle
x^{a}x_{a}=1
.
u
is the null generator of this surface.

3.

Future Timelike Infinity
i^{+}
:
This is located at
X^{0}\to+\infty
while keeping
(R,{\lambda},{\Theta})
fixed. In this limit,

\begin{split}u&\to X^{0}-{\lambda}R,\qquad x^{a}\to\frac{R\sqrt{1-{\lambda}^{2}}}{X^{0}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta}),\qquad r\to X^{0}+{\lambda}R.\end{split}

(A.16)

Thus, we find that
i^{+}
located at
u,r\to\infty
and
x^{a}\to 0
while keeping
u-r
and
rx^{a}
fixed.

4.

Past Timelike Infinity
i^{-}
:
This is located at
X^{0}\to-\infty
while keeping
(R,{\lambda},{\Theta})
fixed. In this limit,

\begin{split}u&\to-\left(\big|X^{0}\big|-{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}R\right),\quad x^{a}\to\frac{R\sqrt{1-{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}^{2}}}{\big|X^{0}\big|}{\hat{n}}^{a}\left({\Theta}_{\text{ap}}\right),\quad r\to-\big|X^{0}\big|-{\lambda}_{\text{ap}}R.\end{split}

(A.17)

Thus, we find that
i^{-}
located at
u,r\to-\infty
and
x^{a}\to 0
while keeping
u-r
and
rx^{a}
fixed.

5.

Spatial Infinity
i^{0}
:
This is located at
R\to+\infty
while keeping
(X^{0},{\lambda},{\Theta})
fixed. In this limit,

\begin{split}u&\to-\frac{R}{{\lambda}}+\frac{X^{0}}{{\lambda}^{2}},\qquad x^{a}\to\frac{1}{{\lambda}}\sqrt{1-{\lambda}^{2}}{\hat{n}}^{a}({\Theta}),\qquad r={\lambda}R+X^{0}.\end{split}

(A.18)

This limit corresponds to
ur\to-\infty
while keeping
\frac{u}{r}
and
x^{a}
fixed. In particular, on the northern hemisphere, we take
u\to-\infty
and
r\to+\infty
, whereas on the southern hemisphere, we take
u\to+\infty
and
r\to-\infty
.

A.1.2
Isometries

The isometries of
{\mathbb{R}}^{1,d+1}
are the
d+2
translations and the
O(1,d+1)
rotations. The corresponding Killing vectors in flat null coordinates are

\begin{split}\xi_{f}&=f(x)\partial_{u}-\frac{1}{2r}\partial^{a}f(x)\partial_{a}+\frac{1}{2d}\partial^{2}f(x)\partial_{r}\\
\zeta_{Y}&=\psi(x)\left(u\partial_{u}-r\partial_{r}\right)+\left[Y^{a}(x)-\frac{u}{2r}\partial^{a}\psi(x)\right]\partial_{a},\end{split}

(A.19)

where
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{d}\partial_{a}Y^{a}(x)
, and
f(x)
and
Y^{a}(x)
satisfy the equations

\begin{split}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}f(x)=\frac{1}{d}\delta_{ab}\partial^{2}f(x),\qquad\partial_{a}Y_{b}(x)+\partial_{b}Y_{a}(x)=\frac{2}{d}\delta_{ab}\partial^{c}Y_{c}(x).\\
\end{split}

(A.20)

This implies that
Y^{a}(x)
is the conformal Killing vector of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(which manifests the isomorphism
SO(1,d+1)\cong\mathfrak{conf}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
). The explicit solutions to this are

\begin{split}f(x)=f_{0}+f_{a}x^{a}+f_{d+1}x^{2},\qquad Y^{a}(x)=\chi^{a}+{\omega}^{a}{}_{b}x^{b}+\lambda x^{a}+\zeta^{a}x^{2}-2x^{a}\zeta\cdot x.\end{split}

(A.21)

Thus,
\psi(x)=\lambda-2\zeta\cdot x
. The
SO(d)
rotations have
\psi(x)=0
(generated by
\chi^{a}
and
{\omega}^{a}{}_{b}
), whereas the boosts in
SO(1,d+1)
have
\psi(x)\neq 0
(generated by
\lambda
and
\zeta^{a}
). Finally, we remark that the Lie algebra of the Killing vectors (the Poincaré algebra) takes the form

\begin{split}\left[\xi_{f},\xi_{f^{\prime}}\right]&=0,\qquad\left[\zeta_{Y},\xi_{f}\right]=\xi_{Y^{a}\partial_{a}f-f\psi},\qquad\left[\zeta_{Y},\zeta_{Y^{\prime}}\right]=\zeta_{[Y,Y^{\prime}]}.\end{split}

(A.22)

A.2
Momentum Space

In this subsection, we observe a general parameterization of off-shell momenta and list some useful formulae.

A.2.1
Off-Shell Momentum

Following closely the relationship between flat null coordinates and Cartesian coordinates, we parameterize an
off-shell
momentum as

\begin{split}q^{0}=\frac{{\omega}}{2}\left(1+y^{2}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2{\omega}},\qquad q^{a}={\omega}y^{a},\qquad q^{d+1}=\frac{{\omega}}{2}\left(1-y^{2}\right)-\frac{\mu}{2{\omega}}.\end{split}

(A.23)

Inversely,
\mu=-q^{A}q_{A}
,
y^{a}=q^{a}(q^{0}+q^{d+1})^{-1}
, and
{\omega}=q^{0}+q^{d+1}
. This parameterization is valid everywhere except when
{\omega}=q^{0}+q^{d+1}=0
. The range of these coordinates is
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
y^{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\omega}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. The momentum space volume form is

\begin{split}{\varepsilon}&=dq^{0}\wedge dq^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dq^{d}\wedge dq^{d+1}=\frac{1}{2}{\omega}^{d-1}d\mu\wedge dy^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^{d}\wedge d{\omega}.\end{split}

(A.24)

This implies that the off-shell integration measure and Dirac delta function are

\begin{split}\int\frac{d^{d+2}q}{(2\pi)^{d+2}}&=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d+2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\mu\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\omega}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}d^{d}y\,|{\omega}|^{d-1}\\
\delta^{(d+2)}\left(q-q^{\prime}\right)&=2|{\omega}|^{1-d}\delta\left(\mu-\mu^{\prime}\right)\delta\left({\omega}-{\omega}^{\prime}\right)\delta^{(d)}\left(y-y^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

(A.25)

A.2.2
On-Shell Momentum and Polarization Vectors

The on-shell condition on a momentum implies

\begin{split}q^{A}q_{A}=-m^{2},\qquad q^{0}>0\quad\implies\quad\mu=m^{2}\geq 0,\qquad{\omega}>0.\end{split}

(A.26)

The on-shell integration measure and Dirac delta function are

\begin{split}\int\frac{d^{d+2}q}{(2\pi)^{d+2}}(2\pi)\delta\big(q^{2}+m^{2}\big){\theta}(q^{0})&=\int\frac{d^{d+1}q}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\frac{1}{2q^{0}}=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{\omega}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}d^{d}y\,{\omega}^{d-1}\\
(2q^{0})\delta^{(d+1)}\left(\vec{q}-\vec{q}\,^{\prime}\right)&=2{\omega}^{1-d}\delta\left({\omega}-{\omega}^{\prime}\right)\delta^{(d)}\left(y-y^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

(A.27)

The
d+1
polarizations of a massive particle are taken to be

\begin{split}{\varepsilon}_{a}^{A}(y)&=\left(y_{a},\delta_{a}^{b},-y_{a}\right),\qquad{\varepsilon}^{A}_{\omega}({\omega},y)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+y^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}},2y^{a},1-y^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(A.28)

In the massless limit
{\varepsilon}^{A}_{\omega}\to{\omega}^{-1}q^{A}
. Thus, in this limit, this is the pure gauge mode and does not contribute to physical amplitudes. For future reference, we collect the formulae

\begin{split}q({\omega},y)\cdot q({\omega}^{\prime},y^{\prime})&=-\frac{1}{2}{\omega}{\omega}^{\prime}\left[(y-y^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}+\frac{m^{\prime 2}}{{\omega}^{\prime 2}}\right]\\
q({\omega},y)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{a}(y^{\prime})&={\omega}(y-y^{\prime})_{a}\\
q({\omega},y)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{{\omega}^{\prime}}({\omega}^{\prime},y^{\prime})&=-\frac{{\omega}}{2}\left[(y-y^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}-\frac{m^{\prime 2}}{{\omega}^{\prime 2}}\right]\\
{\varepsilon}_{a}(y)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{b}(y^{\prime})&=\delta_{ab}\\
{\varepsilon}_{a}(y)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{{\omega}^{\prime}}({\omega}^{\prime},y^{\prime})&=-(y-y^{\prime})_{a}\\
{\varepsilon}_{{\omega}}({\omega},y)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{{\omega}^{\prime}}({\omega}^{\prime},y^{\prime})&=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(y-y^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}-\frac{m^{\prime 2}}{{\omega}^{\prime 2}}\right]\\
X(u,r,x)\cdot q({\omega},y)&=-\frac{{\omega}u}{2}-\frac{{\omega}r}{2}\left[(x-y)^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\right]\\
X(u,r,x)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{a}({\omega},y)&=r(x-y)_{a}\\
X(u,r,x)\cdot{\varepsilon}_{{\omega}}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{u}{2}-\frac{r}{2}\left[(x-y)^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\right].\\
\end{split}

(A.29)

We also require the components of the momentum and polarization vectors in flat null coordinates

\begin{split}q_{u}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{{\omega}}{2}\\
q_{r}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{{\omega}}{2}\left[(x-y)^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\right]\\
q_{a}({\omega},y)&=-r\omega(x-y)_{a}\\
({\varepsilon}_{a})_{u}({\omega},y)&=0\\
({\varepsilon}_{a})_{r}({\omega},y)&=(x-y)_{a}\\
({\varepsilon}_{a})_{b}({\omega},y)&=r\delta_{ab}\\
({\varepsilon}_{\omega})_{u}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{1}{2}\\
({\varepsilon}_{\omega})_{r}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{1}{2}\left[(x-y)^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}\right]\\
({\varepsilon}_{\omega})_{a}({\omega},y)&=-r(x-y)_{a}.\end{split}

(A.30)

For massless vectors, we note from the formulae above that our polarization choice is equivalent to the gauge choice
A_{u}=0
. The polarization tensors for the massless field strength then has the following components in flat null coordinates

\begin{split}({\varepsilon}_{a})_{ur}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}(x-y)_{a}\\
({\varepsilon}_{a})_{rb}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}r(x-y)^{2}{\mathcal{I}}_{ab}(x-y)\\
({\varepsilon}_{a})_{ub}({\omega},y)&=-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}r\delta_{ab}\\
({\varepsilon}_{c})_{ab}({\omega},y)&=-i{\omega}r^{2}\left[(x-y)_{a}\delta_{bc}-(x-y)_{b}\delta_{ac}\right],\end{split}

(A.31)

where
{\mathcal{I}}_{ab}(x-y)
is the conformally invariant tensor

\begin{split}{\mathcal{I}}_{ab}(x-y)=\delta_{ab}-\frac{2(x-y)_{a}(x-y)_{b}}{(x-y)^{2}}.\end{split}

(A.32)

Appendix B
Action of the Hard Charge

In this section, we show that the hard charge generates large gauge transformations on the matter states by an explicit computation for a minimally coupled scalar field
\Phi
with
U(1)
charge
Q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The corresponding conserved current is

\begin{split}J_{\mu}=iQ\left(\Phi^{*}D_{\mu}\Phi-(D_{\mu}\Phi)^{*}\Phi\right),\qquad D_{\mu}\Phi=\partial_{\mu}\Phi-iQA_{\mu}\Phi.\end{split}

(B.1)

The product of fields is defined via normal ordering.

The mode expansion for the outgoing/incoming scalar field is

\begin{split}\Phi^{(\pm)}(X)=\int\frac{d^{d+1}q}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\frac{1}{2q^{0}}\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}(\vec{q}\,)e^{iq\cdot X}+{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}(\vec{q}\,)e^{-iq\cdot X}\right],\end{split}

(B.2)

where

\begin{split}\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}(\vec{q}\,),{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}(\vec{q}\,^{\prime})\right]=\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{(\pm)}(\vec{q}\,),{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}(\vec{q}\,^{\prime})\right]=(2q^{0})(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta^{(d+1)}\left(\vec{q}-\vec{q}\,^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

(B.3)

Moving to flat null coordinates and using (
2.14
), we find

\begin{split}\Phi^{(\pm)}(u,r,x)&=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d+1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}d^{d}y\int_{0}^{\infty}d{\omega}\,{\omega}^{d-1}\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}({\omega},x+y)e^{-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}u-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}ry^{2}}\right.\\
&\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega},x+y)e^{\frac{i}{2}{\omega}u+\frac{i}{2}{\omega}ry^{2}}\right],\end{split}

(B.4)

where

\begin{split}\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}({\omega},x),{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega}^{\prime},x^{\prime})\right]&=\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{(\pm)}({\omega},x),{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega}^{\prime},x^{\prime})\right]\\
&=2{\omega}^{1-d}(2\pi)^{d+1}\delta({\omega}-{\omega}^{\prime})\delta^{(d)}\left(x-x^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

(B.5)

The leading order term in the large
r
expansion of the scalar field is (this is determined following precisely the method of §
2.2.1
)

\begin{split}\Phi^{(\pm)}~~\to~~\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{\omega}\,{\omega}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left[\frac{e^{-\frac{i}{2}{\omega}u}}{(ir)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}({\omega},x)+\frac{e^{\frac{i}{2}{\omega}u}}{(-ir)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega},x)\right]+\cdots.\end{split}

(B.6)

We then determine that

\begin{split}\int du\,J_{u}^{(\pm,d)}&=\frac{\pi Q}{(2\pi)^{d+2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{\omega}\,{\omega}^{d-1}\left[{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega},x){\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{(\pm)}({\omega},x)-{\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}\dagger}({\omega},x){\mathcal{O}}_{{\overline{\Phi}}}^{{(\pm)}}({\omega},x)\right].\end{split}

(B.7)

An outgoing or incoming scalar state of charge
Q
is

\begin{split}\langle\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,|=\langle\,0\,|{\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{{(+)}}({\omega},x),\qquad|\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,\rangle={\mathcal{O}}_{\Phi}^{{(-)}\dagger}({\omega},x)|\,0\,\rangle.\end{split}

(B.8)

Using these definitions, we find

\begin{split}\langle\,\Phi,{\omega},x^{\prime}\,|\int du\,J_{u}^{(+,d)}(u,x)&=Q\delta^{(d)}(x-x^{\prime})\langle\,\Phi,{\omega},x^{\prime}\,|\\
\int du\,J_{u}^{(-,d)}(u,x)|\,\Phi,{\omega},x^{\prime}\,\rangle&=Q\delta^{(d)}(x-x^{\prime})|\,\Phi,{\omega},x^{\prime}\,\rangle.\end{split}

(B.9)

The action of the hard charge (
3.7
) is then

\begin{split}\langle\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,|Q^{+H}_{\varepsilon}=-Q{\varepsilon}(x)\langle\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,|,\qquad Q^{-H}_{\varepsilon}|\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,\rangle=-Q{\varepsilon}(x)|\,\Phi,{\omega},x\,\rangle.\end{split}

(B.10)

Appendix C
Soft Photon Operator

We describe how to obtain the soft photon insertion operator in an
S
-matrix using the path integral approach. First, the
n
-point amplitude is

\begin{split}{\mathcal{A}}_{n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})=\langle\,\text{out}\,|\,\text{in}\,\rangle.\end{split}

(C.1)

This is computed in the path integral formalism by computing off-shell momentum space correlators and taking an appropriate on-shell limit. For instance, for an outgoing scalar particle with momentum
\vec{p}
, we insert

\begin{split}iZ_{\Phi}\lim_{p^{0}\to\sqrt{|\vec{p}\,|+m^{2}}-i{\epsilon}}\left(p^{2}-i{\epsilon}\right)\int d^{d+2}X\,e^{-ip\cdot X}\Phi(X).\end{split}

(C.2)

Here,
\Phi(X)
is an operator that creates (among other things) a scalar one-particle state, and
Z_{\Phi}
is the corresponding wave-function renormalization factor.

The amplitude involving an extra outgoing photon, i.e.
{\mathcal{A}}_{n+1}^{\text{out}}
from (
3.23
), similarly corresponds to the insertion of the operator

\begin{split}\frac{iZ}{e}\lim_{p_{\gamma}^{0}\to|\vec{p}_{\gamma}|-i{\epsilon}}\left(p_{\gamma}^{2}-i{\epsilon}\right){\varepsilon}_{a}^{A}(\vec{p}_{\gamma})^{*}\int d^{d+2}X\,e^{-ip_{\gamma}\cdot X}A_{A}(X).\end{split}

(C.3)

To understand what this operator is in terms of the
in
and
out
creation/annihilation operators, we expand the gauge field
