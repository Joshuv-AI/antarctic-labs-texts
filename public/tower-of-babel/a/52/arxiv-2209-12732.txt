The Effect of Non-Gaussian Noise on Auto-correlative Weak-value Amplification
Xiang-Yun Hu 1, Jing-Hui Huang 1, Fei-Fan He 3, Guang-Jun Wang 4, Adetunmise C. Dada 2

Abstract
Accurate knowledge of the spectral features of noise and their influence on quantum systems is fundamental for quantitative understanding and prediction of the dynamics in a realistic environment. For the weak measurements of two-level systems, the weak values will inevitably be affected by the noise of the environment. Following our earlier work on the auto-correlative weak-value amplification (AWVA) approach under a Gaussian noise environment, here we study the effect of non-Gaussian noise on AWVA. In particular, two types of noise with a negative-dB signal-to-noise ratio, frequency-stationary noises, and frequency-nonstationary noises are studied. The various frequency-stationary noises, including low-frequency (1/f) noises, medium-frequency noises, and high-frequency noises, are generated in Simulink by translating the Gaussian white noise with different band-pass filters. While impulsive noise is studied as an example of frequency-non stationary noises. Our simulated results demonstrate that 1/f noises and impulsive noises have greater disturbance in the AWVA measurements. In addition, adding one kind of frequency-stationary noise, clamping the detected signals, and dominating the measurement range may have the potential to improve the precision in the presence of hostile non-Gaussian noises.

In our simulation, the initial temporal probe was chosen as:

\displaystyle I^{in}_{1}(t;\tau)=\left|\left\langle q|\Psi_{i}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{I_{0}}{(2\pi\zeta^{2})^{1/4}}e^{-\frac{(t-t_{0})^{2}}{4\zeta^{2}}}\,,

(25)

III.1
Effects of frequency-stationary noises

Figure
5
shows the simulation of the final measurement signals
I_{1}^{out}(t)
, the auto-correlative intensity
\rm\Theta(\tau=0ns)
and the auto-correlative intensity
\rm\Theta(\tau=3ns)
under various frequency-stationary noises. At the same SNR of noise, the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
has a greater impact on the estimation of
\rm\Theta(\tau)
. A more quantitative and intuitive
description–the normalized sensitivity–
is displayed in Fig.
7
(A). Both the mean value deviation (corresponding to the absolute value of
1-\rm K/0.0258
) and the error bar under the noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
are larger than that under other noises. We can trace this poor performance of the AWVA technique under low-frequency noises back to the definition of autocorrelation intensity
\rm\Theta
.

According to our previous study
Huang et al. 2022c
on the effect of the sampling frequency (corresponding to the number of sampling points in the period of time 3 ms), increasing the sampling frequency can enhance the precision. Here, AWVA performs badly when measurements are disturbed by low-frequency noises even at high sample frequency. The main reason is that the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
as shown in Fig.
3
does not exhibit randomness in the finite time (3 ms),
and this non-random behavior (meaning
\rm\Theta_{\rm NN}>>0
) can hardly satisfy the relationship
\rm\Theta(\tau)\approx\Theta_{\rm II}(\tau)
.

In the principle of the signal processing
Vaseghi 2005
, the 1/f noises can be reduced by designing the adaptive filters. Nevertheless, the filtering denoising technique does not work under the low-frequency noise since the measurement signal is also low frequency. Based on the fact that the spectrum of the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
and the measurement signal are overlapping in the low-frequency domain, we propose a possible solution by increasing the frequency of the Gaussian signal at the cost of decreasing the intensity of
\rm\Theta(\tau)
and the sensitivity of the AWVA scheme.

This is because increasing the frequency of the Gaussian signal will cut down the pointer spread
\zeta
and lead to a smaller integral time t for estimating
\Theta(\tau)
. In addition, the 1/f noise has been widely studied and might be reduced by lowering the temperature of the device
Wong 2003
.

In this work, we propose another possible solution to reducing the adverse impact of the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
by adding additive Gaussian noise with a given intensity. In Table
1
, Fig.
7
(B) and Fig.
7
(C), we display the results of the combination of the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
and the Gaussian noise
\textbf{N}_{0}(t,\sigma^{2})
. The results of 7 measurements in Fig.
7
(B) indicate that the measurement under noises
\textbf{N}_{1}+2\textbf{N}_{0}
and
\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0}
have smaller deviations of the theoretical value but with larger error bars. In addition, the measurement under noises
\textbf{N}_{1}+\textbf{N}_{0}
performs worse than the measurement under noises
\textbf{N}_{1}
.
Note that this unusual result may be due to the number of measurements. If we change the seed of
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
, other different qualitative results may be obtained.

The reason is the non-random behavior in finite 3 ms time of the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
, even when seven measurements are conducted. Theoretically, increasing the number of measurements can better reflect the randomness of noise and avoid accidental results.

Then, the simulation results with 21 measurements are investigated and the normalized sensitivity are shown in Table
1
and Fig.
7
(C). The details of the simulation under noises
\textbf{N}_{1}
,
\textbf{N}_{1}+1\textbf{N}_{0}
,
\textbf{N}_{1}+2\textbf{N}_{0}
and
\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0}
can be found in Table
3
, we market the noises
(\textbf{N}_{1})^{*}
,
(\textbf{N}_{1}+1\textbf{N}_{0})^{*}
and
(\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0})^{*}
as the results with 21 measurements and calculate the corresponding deviation of the theoretical value with error bars. Fig.
7
(C) indicates that adding the Gaussian noise
\textbf{N}_{0}(t,\sigma^{2})
to the measurements under the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
can
result in
more
precise
results with smaller deviations of the theoretical value.

In particular, it is shown that adding twice the intensity of the Gaussian noise
\textbf{N}_{0}(t,\sigma^{2})
into the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
has the smallest deviations than adding other levels of noise
\textbf{N}_{0}(t,\sigma^{2})
. Note that the conclusion is only valid for this specific low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
in this paper. Further experiments on the AWVA measurements under other types of low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
or realistic noises will be investigated since the smallest deviation may be obtained by adding the different intensities of Gaussian noise.

Furthermore, the results of comparison with the different number of measurements and different measurement ranges are indicated in Fig.
7
(D). The expression
(\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0})^{*}
represents the results with 21 measurements.
The expression

.
Indeed, increasing the number of measurements can further decrease the deviation of the mean value; however, it is undeniable that simply increasing the number of measurements can not decrease error bars. Even the error bar of the 87 measurements marked with
(\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0})^{**}
seems too large for estimation of the time delay
\tau
.
Nevertheless, when comparing the results
(\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0})^{+}
with the results
(\textbf{N}_{1}+3\textbf{N}_{0})^{**}
, it can be found in Table
4
that decreasing the measurement range [
\rm\Theta_{Min}
,
\rm\Theta_{Max}
] can significantly reduce error bars but lead to small deviation of the mean value.

There are two reasons why we set the measurement range [
\rm\Theta_{Min}
,
\rm\Theta_{Max}
] and how it works. Firstly, it is natural to set the minimum value
\rm\Theta_{Min}
and the maximum
\rm\Theta_{Max}
when a high enough resolution is required for estimation of the quality
\rm\Theta
in WVA, because the detector (APD and Scope) response is dominated by the saturation effect
Yin et al. 2021
. In addition, when the number of AD digits is determined, decreasing the measurement range [
\rm\Theta_{Min}
,
\rm\Theta_{Max}
] can obtain a higher resolution for estimation of the quality
\rm\Theta

Floyd 2008
.
Secondly, the theoretical measurement range can be pre-determined by discarding unreasonable measurement results. The unreasonable measurement results may be corresponding to results
\rm\Theta(t)
deviating far from the theoretical curves
\rm\Theta_{II}(t)
, because the robustness of one system with long-range measurement can not remain asymptotically stable all the time when the random disturbance to the system is very strong. In other words, the robustness characterized by error bars in our system and another system associated with generalized variance should be modeled and measured by inputting feasible range measurements. Similarly, a generic framework for modeling and measurement of process robustness in manufacturing system has been studied by starting with the necessary process: selecting a manufacturing process, identifying the input process parameters, response variables, and their feasible range of operations (corresponding to the measurement range [
\rm\Theta_{Min}
,
\rm\Theta_{Max}
] in this work)
Mondal et al. 2014

] for obtaining a smaller error bar.

In conclusion, the simulation results indicate that the low-frequency noise has an adverse impact on the AWVA measurements when the signal is also low frequency. The analysis provides a referable principle to perform the AWVA measurements, which is trying to ensure that the signal (initial pointer) is not in the same frequency band as the noise. At the very least, multiple measurements
and dominating measurement range
by adding additive Gaussian noise may enhance the precision of the AWVA technique under the low-frequency noise
\textbf{N}_{1}(t,\sigma^{2})
.

IV
Summary

We have investigated the influence of non-Gaussian noises on auto-correlative weak-value amplification (AWVA) for precision phase estimation.
Simulated measurements on Simulink for estimating the time shift have been derived under various noises. In this paper, two types of noise, frequency-stationary noises, and
frequency-nonstationary
noises were studied. Specifically, low frequency (1/f) noise, medium frequency noise, high-frequency noise, and the combination of these noises and the Gaussian noise were chosen as a proxy for the measurements under frequency-stationary noises. In addition, the impulsive noise and the mixed noise of the impulsive noise and Gaussian noise or low frequency (1/f) noise were studied as representative examples of the measurements under the
frequency-nonstationary
noises.

We obtained the following conclusions: 1) Low-frequency noise has a greater adverse impact on the AWVA measurements than other frequency-stationary noises due to the coincidence of the noises and signals in the frequency domain. 2) Precise characterization of the technique and environmental noises can provide a selection of initial pointers to avoid the
coincidence of the noises and signals in the frequency domain
. 3) The
frequency-nonstationary
noises, such as impulsive noise, exhibit the worst kind of noise and lead to the wrong (negativity sensitivity) results. 4)
Increasing the number of measurements, adding additive frequency-stationary noise, limiting the maximum value of the detected signals, and dominating the measurement range may have the potential to improve the precision under the worst noises. Note that the multiple measurements with these complex data processing may make measurement difficult and inconvenient. It is worth further adding the field-programmable gate array (FPGA) in electronics
Schröder et al. 2013
;
Lu et al. 2015
;
Elaskar et al. 2022
to the WVA scheme for logic controls optical components and real-time data processing.

Our simulation of the AWVA measurements under more realistic/complex noises rather than Gaussian noises
Huang et al. 2022c
further demonstrates that the robustness of the AWVA technique can resist environmental and instrument disturbances.
