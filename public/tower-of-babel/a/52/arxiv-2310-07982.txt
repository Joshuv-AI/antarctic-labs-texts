Abstract
We study nematic configurations within three-dimensional (3D) cuboids, with planar degenerate boundary conditions on the cuboid faces, in the Landau-de Gennes framework. There are two geometry-dependent variables: the edge length of the square cross-section, λ \lambda , and the parameter h h , which is a measure of the cuboid height. Theoretically, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the global minimiser with a small enough cuboid size. We develop a new numerical scheme for the high-index saddle dynamics to deal with the surface energies. We report on a plethora of (meta)stable states, and their dependence on h h and λ \lambda , and in particular, how the 3D states are connected with their two-dimensional counterparts on squares and rectangles. Notably, we find families of almost uniaxial stable states constructed from the topological classification of tangent unit-vector fields and study transition pathways between them. We also provide a phase diagram of competing (meta)stable states, as a function of λ \lambda and h h .

Introduction

Liquid crystals (LCs) are mesophases, that are intermediate in character between the solid and liquid phases of matter
[
1
,
2
]
. There are different types of LCs, of which nematic liquid crystals (NLCs) are the simplest and most commonly used in science and technology. NLCs combine fluidity with the directionality of solids i.e. NLCs have long-range orientational order with distinguished directions of preferred molecular alignment, referred to as nematic “directors” in the literature
[
1
]
. The intrinsic anisotropy makes NLCs highly sensitive to external stimuli e.g. electric fields, incident light, temperature, stress and surface effects. Indeed, the exceptional properties of NLCs make them the working material of choice for the multi-billion dollar liquid crystal display (LCD) industry, and NLC applications now extend to soft robotics, biomimetic materials, sensors and light modulators
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

NLC applications can depend quite strongly on anchoring conditions or boundary conditions, i.e. the coupling of the NLC molecules to surfaces can determine the nematic director profiles on the surfaces
[
1
]
. The anchoring conditions are typically either planar degenerate/tangential, for which the director is tangent to the surface or in the plane of the surface, or homeotropic/normal for which the director is orthogonal to the surface. For example, in
[
7
]
, the authors report an NLC-filled 3D array of square or rectangular wells, such that the well surfaces are treated to induce tangent boundary conditions. The tangent boundary conditions induce bistability, i.e. the wells can support two optically contrasting stable NLC states, without any external fields. In
[
8
]
, the authors study NLC shells and the shell surfaces are treated with a polymer, such that the boundary conditions can be dynamically tuned from tangential to normal, as the shells undergo a heating transition. The change in the boundary conditions manifests in the experimentally recorded optical images. In
[
9
]
, the authors explore the surface alignment of the ferroelectric nematic phase
by testing different rubbed and unrubbed substrates that differ in coupling strength and anchoring
orientation and find a variety of behaviours – in terms of nematic orientation, topological defects and
electric field response. In
[
10
]
, the authors study NLC molecular orientations on a doubly treated substrate, with different surface alignments on the top and bottom surfaces, and use the simulation results to estimate surface anchoring strengths.

In previous work
[
11

, we study NLCs confined to a 3D cuboid with Dirichlet/fixed tangent boundary conditions on the lateral surfaces of the cuboid, and with either Neumann (natural) boundary conditions or fixed Dirichlet boundary conditions on the top and bottom cuboid surfaces. In both cases, we work within the celebrated continuum Landau-de Gennes (LdG) framework and model physically relevant configurations as minimisers of an appropriately defined LdG energy, or stable solutions of the associated Euler-Lagrange equations which are a system of nonlinear and coupled partial differential equations
[
12

. With Neumann boundary conditions on the top and bottom cuboid surfaces, we find 3D

[

), and multi-layer solutions with mixed solutions stacked on the top of each other
[

. In
[
14

[
15
]
. This choice of Dirichlet conditions is special, and we use our wealth of knowledge of pathways between stable rLdG solutions on squares/rectangles, to construct 3D solutions on a cuboid. Namely, in some cases, if
A\to B\to C
is a pathway on the rLdG solution landscape on a square domain, where
A,B,C
denote rLdG solutions (critical points of an appropriately defined rLdG energy), then we can construct a 3D critical point on a cuboid with fixed Dirichlet conditions, corresponding to the
A
,
C
solutions on the top and bottom surfaces, with a

In this paper, we build on our previous work and relax the fixed Dirichlet tangent conditions on the lateral surfaces of the cuboid. Rather, we work with surface energies on all six surfaces, that enforce planar degenerate or tangent anchoring on all cuboid faces. In this case, the nematic director is only coerced to be in the plane of the face, without a fixed direction in contrast to the Dirichlet fixed boundary conditions on the lateral surfaces in
[
14
,
16
]
. This certainly allows for more freedom on the lateral faces, and expands the corresponding solution landscapes.

In a batch of papers

\lambda
, and an aspect ratio,
b
. For
b=1

\lambda

In this paper, we have a 3D cuboid with two geometry-dependent variables: the edge length of the square cross-section denoted by
\lambda

The Landau–de Gennes theory

We work within the celebrated LdG theory, which is the most general continuum theory for NLCs. The LdG theory describes the state of NLC ordering by a macroscopic order parameter, the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor, that distinguishes NLCs from isotropic liquids
[
1
]
. Mathematically, the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor is a symmetric and traceless
3\times 3
matrix as shown below:

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{1}&Q_{3}&Q_{4}\\
Q_{3}&Q_{2}&Q_{5}\\
Q_{4}&Q_{5}&-Q_{1}-Q_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

From the spectral decomposition theorem, we can write the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor as

\mathbf{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{i},

where
\left\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}\right\}
are the eigenvectors of the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor and
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\lambda_{2}\leqslant\lambda_{3}
are the associated eigenvalues respectively, subject to
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}=0
. The eigenvectors model the preferred directions of spatially averaged local molecular alignment or
the nematic directors
, and the eigenvalues are a measure of the orientational order about these directions. A
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor is said to be isotropic if
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}
, uniaxial if
\mathbf{Q}
has a pair of repeated non-zero eigenvalues, and biaxial if
\mathbf{Q}
has three distinct eigenvalues
[
1
,
22
]
. A uniaxial NLC phase has a single distinguished direction of averaged molecular alignment (modelled by the eigenvector with the non-degenerate eigenvalue), such that all directions perpendicular to the uniaxial director are physically equivalent. A biaxial phase has a primary and secondary nematic director.

The LdG theory is a variational theory, and the physically observable configurations are modelled by minimisers of an appropriately defined LdG free energy
[
1
]
. We work with a simple form of the LdG free energy:

E_{LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]:=\int_{V}\left[\frac{L}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+f_{B}\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}V,

(2)

where the first term in the integrand is the elastic energy density that penalizes spatial inhomogeneities,
\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}:=\frac{\partial Q_{ij}}{\partial r_{k}}\frac{\partial Q_{ij}}{\partial r_{k}}
,
i,j,k=1,2,3
and the second term is the thermotropic potential that dictates the preferred NLC phase as a function of temperature,

f_{B}(\mathbf{Q}):=\frac{A}{2}\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{B}{3}\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}+\frac{C}{4}(\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{2}-f_{B,0},

(3)

where
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{Q}=Q_{ij}Q_{ij}
,
i,j=1,2,3
and we use the Einstein summation convention throughout the paper.

Here,
L>0
is a material-dependent elastic constant,
A=\alpha(T-T^{*})
is the rescaled temperature, with
\alpha>0
and
T^{*}
is a characteristic liquid crystal temperature;
B,C>0
are material-dependent bulk constants. The minimisers of
f_{B}
depend on
A
and determine the NLC phase for spatially homogeneous samples. For
A<0
, the minimisers of
f_{B}
constitute a continuum of uniaxial
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors defined by

\mathcal{N}=\left\{\mathbf{Q}=s_{+}\left(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{3}\right)\right\},

where

s_{+}=\frac{B+\sqrt{B^{2}-24AC}}{4C},

and

\mathbf{n}
is an arbitrary unit vector field that models the uniaxial director. The constant,
f_{B,0}=\frac{A}{3}s_{+}^{2}-\frac{2B}{27}s_{+}^{3}+\frac{C}{9}s_{+}^{4}

[
12
]
, is added to ensure a non-negative energy density.

Our working domain is a cuboid
V=\left[-\lambda,\lambda\right]^{2}\times\left[-\lambda h,\lambda h\right]
where
\lambda
is edge-length of the 2D square cross-section and
h>0
is a measure of the height. In a batch of papers
[
15
,
23
]
, the authors work with the rLdG model valid for 2D domains or the thin-film limit of 3D domains (the
h\to 0
limit of the cuboid
V
above) and certain boundary conditions, for which the energy-minimising or physically relevant
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors have constant
\frac{Q_{1}+Q_{2}}{2}
, and
Q_{4}=Q_{5}=0
, so that the rLdG order parameter has only two degrees of freedom,
\frac{Q_{1}-Q_{2}}{2}
and
Q_{3}
respectively. The rLdG order parameter is
z
-invariant and only describes in-plane nematic ordering in a 2D domain, which cannot work for truly 3D scenarios. However, at a special characteristic low-temperature,
A=-B^{2}/3C
, there exists a branch of critical points of (
2
) of the form

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{P}-\frac{B}{3C}(2\mathbf{z}\otimes\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\otimes\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\otimes\mathbf{y})

where
\mathbf{P}
is the rLdG order parameter with two degrees of freedom;
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{z}
are the unit-vectors in the co-ordinate directions respectively and
\mathbf{P}
is a critical point of an appropriately defined rLdG free energy, and this branch exists for all
h>0
.
In this paper, we use the same parameter values as in
[

23

B=0.64\times 10^{4}\text{Nm}^{-2}
,
C=0.35\times 10^{4}\text{Nm}^{-2}
, and
L=4\times 10^{-11}\text{N}
(material constants for the representative NLC material MBBA)
[

, as in
[
11

18

19

By rescaling the system according to
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})=(\frac{x}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda},\frac{z}{\lambda})
,
\bar{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{2C\lambda^{2}}{L}
and dropping the bars in subsequent discussions (so that all results are in terms of dimensionless variables), the non-dimensionalised LdG free energy is given by,

E_{LdG}[\mathbf{Q}]:=\int_{V}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{A}{4C}\text{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{B}{6C}\text{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{3}+\frac{1}{8}(\text{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2})^{2}-\frac{f_{B,0}}{2C}\right)\right]\mathrm{d}V.

(4)

The normalized domain is
V=\Omega\times\left[-h,h\right]
,
\Omega=\left[-1,1\right]^{2}
is the two-dimensional cross-section of the cuboid, and
\lambda^{2}
describes the cross-sectional size. We use the biaxiality parameter,

\beta^{2}=1-6\text{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{3})^{2}/(\text{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2}))^{3},

(5)

to visualize defects, since defects are typically surrounded by regions with high biaxiality
[
25

26
]
. We have
0\leqslant\beta^{2}\leqslant 1
, and
\beta^{2}=0
if and only if
\mathbf{Q}
is uniaxial or isotropic
[
27
]
.

We impose surface energies on all six cuboid faces:
\partial V_{tb},\partial V_{fb},\partial V_{lr}
, which implicitly enforce planar degenerate anchoring on all six faces, i.e. coerce the leading eigenvector (with the largest positive eigenvalue) of the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor to be in the plane of the face. The total free energy is then given by,

E_{\omega}[\mathbf{Q}]:=E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q})+\omega E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q})=\int_{V}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+\lambda^{2}f_{b}\right]\mathrm{d}V+\omega\int_{\partial V}\|\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}S,

(6)

where
\vec{\nu}
is the outer normal vector of
\partial V
,
\omega=\frac{\lambda W}{\sqrt{2CL}}
is the non-dimensionalised anchoring strength, and
W
is the surface anchoring parameter, which is a measure of how strongly the boundary conditions are enforced on
\partial V

[
28
]
. If
W=0
, then we have natural boundary conditions on all six surfaces. The surface energy,
E_{bc}
, vanishes if and only if
\vec{\nu}
is an eigenvector with constant eigenvalue
-s_{+}/3
, so that the leading eigenvector is in the plane of the cuboid face, but our choice of
E_{bc}
does not fix or prefer a specific orientation/direction for the leading planar eigenvector, i.e. the leading eigenvector is free to rotate in the plane of the cuboid faces. In fact,
E_{bc}=0
precisely when
\mathbf{Q}
only has two degrees of freedom on the faces of
V
, as in the rLdG model, and hence, it is reasonable to expect that the surface profiles of the minimisers of (
6
) are critical points/minimisers of the rLdG free energy in
[
15
]
.

There are discontinuities of the outer normal vector on the edges. For the following analytic results, we take the domain

to be a “smoothed cuboid” (as shown in Fig.

. For

-tensors belong to the space

W^{1,2}_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}=\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{S}_{0}|\mathbf{Q}\in W_{V}^{1,2}\},

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{0}:=\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}:\mathbf{Q}_{ij}=\mathbf{Q}_{ji},\mathbf{Q}_{ii}=0\},

(8)

\displaystyle W_{V}^{1,2}=\left\{u:\int_{V}\left(|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}A<\infty\right\}.

In the ensuing propositions and lemmas, we prove some generic existence, uniqueness and maximum-principle type of results for critical points of (

Lemma 1
.

(Poincare-Friedrichs inequality)
For any
\partial V_{0}\subseteq\partial V
with a positive 2D Lebesgue measure, there exist two constants
\gamma_{1}\geqslant\gamma_{0}>0
depending on

\gamma_{0}(h)\|u\|_{W_{V}^{1,2}}^{2}\leqslant\|u\|_{L_{\partial V_{0}}^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|_{L_{V}^{2}}^{2}\leqslant\gamma_{1}(h)\|u\|_{W_{V}^{1,2}}^{2},\forall u\in W_{V}^{1,2}.

(9)

Proof.

The proof follows the same paradigm as in A.9 Theorem in
[
29
]
. A brief proof is provided for completeness. The second inequality can be directly derived from the embedding theorem
[
30
]
. We prove the first inequality in (
9
) by contradiction.

Assuming that the first inequality does not hold with any positive
\gamma_{0}
, i.e. there exists a sequence
\{u_{k}\}\in W^{1,2}_{V}
which satisfies
\|u_{k}\|_{W_{V}^{1,2}}^{2}=1
and,

\|u_{k}\|_{L_{\partial V_{0}}^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla u_{k}\|_{L_{V}^{2}}^{2}\rightarrow 0,k\rightarrow\infty.

(10)

The bounded sequence in
W^{1,2}_{V}
is quasi-weakly compact, and from the
W^{1,2}_{V}
compact embedding in
L^{2}_{V}

[
30
]
, there exists a sub-sequence
\{u_{k_{j}}\}
and
u_{1}\in W^{1,2}_{V},u_{2}\in L^{2}_{V}
, such that

u_{k_{j}}\rightharpoonup u_{1},\text{in }W^{1,2}_{V},\text{ and }u_{k_{j}}\rightarrow u_{2},\text{in }L^{2}_{V}.

(11)

From (
10
) and (
11
),
\{u_{k_{j}}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
W^{1,2}_{V}
, and therefore, we have
u_{1}=u_{2}=u
and
\|u_{k_{j}}-u_{1}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}_{V}}\rightarrow 0
. Together with (

is a constant and (

Lemma 2
.

Proposition 3

For any

By taking

,

\displaystyle E_{\omega_{n_{1}}}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))

\displaystyle=E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))+\omega_{n_{1}}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))

(19)

\displaystyle\geqslant E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))+\omega_{n_{1}}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))=E_{\omega_{n_{1}}}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}})),

\displaystyle E_{\omega_{n_{2}}}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))

\displaystyle=E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))+\omega_{n_{2}}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))

(20)

\displaystyle\geqslant E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))+\omega_{n_{2}}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))=E_{\omega_{n_{2}}}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}})).

Adding both sides of the inequalities (
19
) and (
20
), we have

(\omega_{n_{2}}-\omega_{n_{1}})E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}}))\geqslant(\omega_{n_{2}}-\omega_{n_{1}})E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}})),

(21)

and hence,

E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))\leqslant E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}})),\text{ if }\omega_{n_{2}}>\omega_{n_{1}},

(22)

Substituting (
22
) into (
19
), it follows that

E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{2}}))\geqslant E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{1}})),\text{ if }\omega_{n_{2}}>\omega_{n_{1}}.

(23)

Take any
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\in W_{V}^{1,2}
which satisfies
E_{bc}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})=0
, and then we have the following sequence of inequalities:

E_{LdG}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})=E_{\omega_{n}}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})\geqslant E_{\omega_{n}}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n}))\geqslant\omega_{n}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n}))\geqslant 0,

(24)

so that,

\lim_{\omega_{n}\rightarrow\infty}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n}))=0.

(25)

If
\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n})
has a subsequence,
\mathbf{Q}(\omega_{n_{k}})\rightarrow\mathbf{Q}_{\infty}
strongly in
W^{1,2}_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}
as
\omega_{n_{k}}\rightarrow\infty
, then
E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}_{\infty})=0
from (
25
). Thus the limit,
\mathbf{Q}_{\infty}\in W^{1,2}_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}
is a global minimiser of the LdG energy in the admissible space.

Lemma 4
.

The critical points of the functional (
6
) in
W_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}^{1,2}
satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equation,

\Delta\mathbf{Q}=\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{A}{2C}\mathbf{Q}-\frac{B}{2C}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})\mathbf{Q}\right),r\in V\setminus\partial V

(26)

with the boundary condition

\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\mathbf{Q}+\omega\left(\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}-\left(\frac{2}{3}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{9}\right)\mathbf{I}\right)=0,r\in\partial V.

(27)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{Q}\in W_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}^{1,2}
be a critical point of (
6
)
, and
\mathbf{P}\in W_{V,\mathbf{S}_{0}}^{1,2}
be a perturbation.
We compute the first variation of (
6
) as,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt|_{t=0}}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}+t\mathbf{P})

(28)

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\left(-\Delta\mathbf{Q}+\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{A}{2C}\mathbf{Q}-\frac{B}{2C}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})\mathbf{Q}\right)\right)\cdot\mathbf{P}\mathrm{d}V

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial V}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{P}+\omega\left(\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}\vec{\nu}+\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\vec{\nu}\right)\mathrm{d}S.

Recalling that
\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{P}=0,\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{I}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial V}\left(\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}\vec{\nu}+\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\vec{\nu}\right)\mathrm{d}S

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial V}\left(\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\right)\cdot\mathbf{P}\mathrm{d}S

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial V}\left(\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}-\left(\frac{2}{3}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{9}\right)\mathbf{I}\right)\cdot\mathbf{P}\mathrm{d}S,

where
\left(\frac{2}{3}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{9}\right)\mathbf{I}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the tracelessness constraint. The Euler-Lagrange equations follow from (
28
) and (

Proposition 6
.

There exists a constant
M(A,B,C)
such that the critical point of
E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
satisfy the inequality,

\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{L_{V}^{\infty}}=\text{ess sup}_{r\in V}\left|\mathbf{Q}(r)\right|\leqslant M,

(30)

where
|\mathbf{Q}|=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathbf{Q}^{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to Lemma 3.11 in
[
13
]
. Let
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\in C^{2}(V)
is a fixed auxiliary function and satisfies

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(r)=\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top},\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(r)=0,r\in\partial V.

The existence of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}
follows from the smoothness of
\partial V
. From Lemma
4
, for
r\in\partial V
, we have

\displaystyle-\partial_{\vec{\nu}}(|\mathbf{P}|^{2}/2)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{P}=-\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{P}

(31)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}-\left(\frac{2}{3}\vec{\nu}\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}+\frac{2s_{+}}{9}\right)\mathbf{I}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}}{2}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=2\omega\left(\vec{\nu}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)^{2}\vec{\nu}\right)\geqslant 0,

and for
r\in V\setminus\partial V
,

\displaystyle\Delta(|\mathbf{P}|^{2}/2)

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{P}+|\nabla\mathbf{P}|^{2}\geqslant\Delta\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle\geqslant\Delta\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}}{2}\Delta\mathbf{Q}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}+\frac{s_{+}}{2}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot\mathbf{Q}+\frac{s_{+}^{2}}{4}\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}=\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}|^{4}+\tilde{p}(\mathbf{Q}),

where

is a third order polynomial of
\mathbf{Q}
. Hence, there exists a positive
M
(that depends on
A
,
B
,
C
) such that the right-hand side of (
32
) is positive for
|\mathbf{Q}|\geqslant M
.
By applying the maximum
principle to (
31
) and (
32
), we deduce that
|\mathbf{Q}|\leqslant M(A,B,C)
.

Proposition 7
.

For any
B,C>0
, there exists a positive constant
\lambda^{*}
, depending on

The existence of a global minimiser and hence, a critical point of (

where

\begin{aligned} E_{\omega}&(\frac{\mathbf{Q}+\bar{\mathbf{Q}}}{2})-\frac{1}{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})-\frac{1}{2}E_{\omega}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\\
=&-\frac{1}{8}\|\nabla(\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\|_{L_{V}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\lambda W}{4\sqrt{2CL}}\int_{\partial V}\|\left(\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\vec{\nu}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\\
&+\lambda^{2}\int_{V}\left(f_{b}(\frac{\bar{\mathbf{Q}}+\mathbf{Q}}{2})-\frac{1}{2}f_{b}(\mathbf{Q})-\frac{1}{2}f_{b}(\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\right)\mathrm{d}V.\end{aligned}.

(33)

From Lemma

-\frac{1}{8}\|\nabla(\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}})\|_{L_{V}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\lambda W}{4\sqrt{2CL}}\int_{\partial V}\|\left(\mathbf{Q}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\right)\vec{\nu}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}S\\

(37)

3
Numerical method

In this section, we describe the numerical methods used to compute the critical points of (
6
). The critical points,
\mathbf{Q}
, are solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations (
26
), which are a system of five nonlinear partial differential equations, for the five components of the
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor,
Q_{1},\cdots,Q_{5}
, in (
1
).
A critical point,
\mathbf{Q}
, is stable if the Hessian,
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
, only has positive eigenvalues (so that it is a local minimum), and unstable if
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
has at least one negative eigenvalue. More precisely, a critical point
\mathbf{Q}
is an index-
k
saddle point for which
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
has exactly
k
negative eigenvalues:
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{k}<0
, corresponding to
k
unit eigenvectors
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
subject to
\big\langle{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}},\hat{{\mathbf{v}}}_{j}\big\rangle=\delta_{ij}
,
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant k
. A stable critical point
\mathbf{Q}
is an index-0 critical point, i.e. the smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
is positive. Typically, a stable critical point can be easily found by the gradient descent method using a proper initial guess, however, it is not easy to provide good initial conditions since we do not have prior knowledge of the stable critical points. In recent works
[
11
,
14
,
16
,
34
,
35
]
, the solution landscape and saddle dynamics (SD) method
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
have been successfully used to efficiently compute the critical points of LdG/rLdG free energy in 2D or 3D domains with Dirichlet, Neumann or mixed boundary conditions.

The SD for finding an index-
k
saddle point
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
, (denoted by
k
-SD) is defined to be,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\mathbf{Q}}&=-(\mathbf{I}-2\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top})\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}),\\
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{i}&=-(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2{\mathbf{v}}_{j}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{\top})\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}_{i},\ i=1,2,\cdots,k,\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(38)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity operator. To avoid evaluating the Hessian of
E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
, we use the dimer

h(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{v}_{i})=\frac{\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}+l\mathbf{v}_{i})-\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}-l\mathbf{v}_{i})}{2l}

(39)

as an approximation of
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}_{i}
, with a small dimer length
2l
. By setting the
k
-dimensional subspace
\hat{\mathcal{V}}=\text{span}\big\{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\big\}
,
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
is a local maximum on
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}
and a local minimum on
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, where
\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\hat{\mathcal{V}}
. The dynamics for
\mathbf{Q}
in (
38
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(-\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\right)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}\right)\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})

(40)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}\right)\left(-\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\right)+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}\left(\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{\top}\right)\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
is the orthogonal projection of
\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
on
\mathcal{V}=\text{span}\big\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\big\}
. Thus,
\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}\right)\left(-\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\right)
is a descent direction on
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{V}}\left(\nabla E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\right)
is an ascent direction on
\mathcal{V}
.

The dynamics for
\mathbf{v}_{i},i=1,2,\cdots,k
in (
38
) can be obtained by minimising the
k
Rayleigh quotients simultaneously with the gradient type dynamics,

\min_{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}}\text{ }\left<\mathbf{v}_{i},\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{v}_{i}\right>,\ \text{s.t.}\ \left<\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij},\ j=1,2,\cdots,i,

(41)

which generates the subspace
\mathcal{V}
by computing the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest
k
eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q})
.

In the remainder of this section, we outline our numerical discretization methods in detail. The non-dimensionalised domain
V=[-1,1]^{2}\times[-h,h]
is discretized into
N
nodes with a small spatial distance
\delta x
. We elaborate on the numerical issues by using
k
-saddle dynamics to find a target saddle point,
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
.

The condition number of
J(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
, the Jacobian operator of the
k
-saddle dynamics, depends on the condition number of
\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
(spatial discretization of
\nabla^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
), i.e.
Cond_{2}(J(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))\geq Cond_{2}(\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))

[
11
]
, where

\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})=\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{LdG}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})+\omega\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{bc}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}).

(42)

The spectral decomposition of
\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
is

\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{*}{\mathbf{v}_{i}^{*}}^{\top},|\lambda_{1}|\leqslant|\lambda_{2}|\leqslant\cdots\leqslant|\lambda_{N}|,

(43)

we have

Cond_{2}(\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))=|\lambda_{N}|/|\lambda_{1}|.

(44)

On one hand, with large
h
and small
\omega
, the
\lambda_{1}
for some target saddle point
\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
is small since we can move the middle state on
z=0
up and down without a significant energetic cost
[
11
]
, i.e.
Cond_{2}(\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))
is large.
On the other hand, as
\omega\to\infty
, the eigenvalues of the
\omega\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{bc}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
tend to infinity, while the eigenvalues of the
\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{LdG}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}})
remain bounded, i.e.
Cond_{2}(\nabla_{\delta x}^{2}E_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{Q}}))
is large for large enough
\omega
. Both cases can lead to ill-conditioned and stiff dynamics
[
41
]
.

To deal with the ill-conditioning, we adopt the same numerical scheme as in
[
11
]
which studies the solution landscape of nematic liquid crystal in 3D cuboid with Dirichlet boundary conditions on the lateral surfaces and Neumann boundary conditions on the top and bottom, to deal with the
k
-saddle dynamics of
E_{LdG}(\mathbf{Q})
. The linear term in (
38
) is implicitly discretized for numerical stability. The nonlinear term,
|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\mathbf{Q}
, is also semi-implicitly discretized in time direction as
|\mathbf{Q}^{n}|^{2}\mathbf{Q}^{n+1}
, for better numerical stability. The term
|\mathbf{Q}^{n}|^{2}\mathbf{Q}^{n+1}
is beneficial for solving linear equations in the semi-implicit scheme, because it is a positive definite term of the diagonal elements. Instead of re-generating unstable eigendirections with the gradient type dynamics in (
38
), we apply a single-step Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (LOBPCG) method
[
42
]
to calculate the unstable eigendirections, and Hessians are also approximated by dimers
[
36
]
.

Next, we deal with the stable scheme for
E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q})
.
In the right hand side of the dynamics in (
38
), the gradient of
E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q})
is given by

\nabla E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q})=\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{Q}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}\vec{\nu}\vec{\nu}^{\top},

(45)

which has both coupling and non-coupling terms.
For example, on the top and bottom surfaces,
\vec{\nu}=(0,0,\pm 1)
, the component of
\nabla E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q})
in the dynamics of
Q_{1}
is

-2Q_{1}-2Q_{2}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}
,
where
-2Q_{1}
is the non-coupling term and
-2Q_{2}
is the coupling term, i.e. a non-
Q_{1}
variable,
Q_{2}
, appears in the dynamics of
Q_{1}
.
The non-coupling linear terms are implicitly discretized for numerical stability and the coupling terms are explicitly discretized, e.g., the component of
\nabla_{\delta x}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}_{n},\mathbf{Q}_{n+1})
in the dynamics of
Q_{1}
is

-2Q_{1,n+1}-2Q_{2,n}+\frac{2s_{+}}{3}
,
to decouple the (five) dynamics of
Q_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,5
and keep the block diagonal structure of the iteration matrix, so that the linear equations can be solved efficiently.

Combining the above, the semi-implicit scheme is given by,

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbf{Q}_{n+1}-\mathbf{Q}_{n}}{\delta t}=&\Delta_{\delta x}\mathbf{Q}_{n+1}-\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{A}{2C}\mathbf{Q}_{n+1}+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{Q}_{n}|^{2}\mathbf{Q}_{n+1}-\frac{B}{2C}\left({\mathbf{Q}_{n}}^{2}-\frac{|\mathbf{Q}_{n}|^{2}}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\right)\\
&+\omega\nabla_{\delta x}E_{bc}(\mathbf{Q}_{n},\mathbf{Q}_{n+1})/\delta x+(2\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{n,i}{\mathbf{v}}_{n,i}^{\top})\nabla_{\delta x}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}_{n}),\\
\text{Renew }\mathbf{v}_{n,i}&\text{ as }\mathbf{v}_{n+1,i}\text{ with single-step LOBPCG},\ i=1,2,\cdots,k,\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(46)

where the time discretization
\delta t
is treated using the Barzilai-Borwein step size for the ill-conditioning.

The gradient of the surface energies does not break the symmetry of linear equations in semi-implicit scheme (
46
), and hence, we can use the Minimal Residual Method (MINRES) to solve it efficiently. The SD pushes the iteration point into the basin of attraction of the target critical point and we use the Newton method to
complete tail convergence with a higher convergence rate. When the target critical point has small absolute eigenvalues, we will use the Inexact-Newton method, since the ill-conditioned linear equation is hard to solve exactly, with Newton iterations
[
11
,
14
]
. In practice, we use the following hybrid scheme of semi-implicit scheme and Inexact-Newton scheme,

\begin{cases}\text{The semi-implicit scheme \eqref{eq: semi-implicit}},\ \|\nabla_{\delta x}E_{\omega}(\mathbf{Q}_{n})\|\geqslant 10^{-3},\\

5
Conclusion and discussion

We study NLC configurations inside a 3D cuboid, within the full LdG framework for which the LdG order parameter has five degrees of freedom, with planar degenerate/tangential boundary conditions. The tangent boundary conditions are enforced by means of surface energies, by means of a large surface anchoring coefficient fixed to be
W=0.01\text{ Jm}^{-2}
throughout this paper. There are two geometrical parameters in our study - the re-scaled edge length of the square cross-section,
\lambda
, and
h
- the ratio of the cuboid cross-section edge length and height. We prove a batch of analytic results for a smoothed cuboid - the existence of a global minimiser of (
6
) for
\omega>0
, uniqueness of the critical points of (
6
) for
\lambda
small enough, and importantly, that energy minimisers satisfy tangent boundary conditions in the
\omega\to\infty
limit. We work with a fixed large value of
\omega
throughout the manuscript, that ensures that the nematic director is tangent to the cuboid faces. This, in turn, leads to numerical difficulties, and we design a new numerical scheme to deal with the stiffness of the problem and accelerate the convergence rate of the saddle dynamics.

In Fig.
10
, we plot a phase diagram of the (meta)stable critical points of (
6
) in the
h-\lambda^{2}
plane. Fixing
h
, one can identify the profiles on the top and bottom cuboid faces with the solution landscape on a square domain, as a function of
\lambda^{2}
. Indeed, for small
\lambda
, we get WORS-type profiles on the top and bottom, and for large
\lambda
, we primarily get D and R-type profiles on the top and bottom cuboid surfaces. Similarly, if we fix
\lambda
and traverse the phase diagram in the
h
-direction, the profiles on the lateral surfaces follow the predictions for solution landscapes on rectangles in
[
16
]
. For small
\lambda
, the profiles on the lateral faces are either BD
1
or BD
2
, depending on
h
(
h
determines whether the
z
-edge is the shorter edge or not, and the BD line defects are localised along the shorter edges). If
h=1
, then we observe the WORS on the lateral faces. For
\lambda
sufficiently large, the profiles on the lateral faces of the (meta)stable critical points of (
6
) depend on
h
; we get BD-profiles on the lateral faces for
h
small, and the D and R-profiles for
h
sufficiently large. Of course, if
h
is sufficiently large or sufficiently small, the D and R-profiles on the lateral faces closely resemble BD-type profiles with an approximately constant director along the longer edge of the lateral surface. The phase diagram in Figure
10
illustrates how solution landscapes on squares and rectangles control the profiles on the cuboid faces, for (meta)stable critical points of (
6
) with tangent boundary conditions. This, in turn, determines the interior 3D structure, including defect structures, of physically relevant NLC configurations within 3D cuboids.

There are numerous interesting future research directions. We could work with weaker anchoring i.e.
\omega<1
, which would offer greater freedom on the cuboid faces. In particular, we do not expect close correspondence between 2D solution landscapes and (meta)stable critical points of (
6
), for smaller values of
\omega
. A further generalization concerns arbitrary 3D geometries with polygonal faces and tangent boundary conditions. The analysis in
[
15
]
for arbitrary 2D regular polygons, in the rLdG setting, can be applied to (meta)stable critical points of (
6
) on arbitrary 3D geometries with polygonal faces, particularly when the edge lengths are small or very large. A further thought concerns the applications of machine learning to train data for generating solution landscapes of complex systems. We have a series of papers on NLC solution landscapes in 2D and 3D
[
11
,
14
]
, and our results on stable states, unstable saddle points and pathways between critical points can be used as precious training data for new machine learning-based algorithms.
