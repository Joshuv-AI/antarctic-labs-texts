Doppler Exploitation in Bistatic mmWave Radio SLAM
Yu Ge 1 , Ossi Kaltiokallio 2 , Hui Chen 1 , Fan Jiang 1 , Jukka Talvitie 2 , Mikko Valkama 2 , Lennart Svensson 1 , Henk Wymeersch 1

Abstract
Networks in 5G and beyond utilize millimeter wave (mmWave) radio signals, large bandwidths, and large antenna arrays, which bring opportunities in jointly localizing the user equipment and mapping the propagation environment, termed as slam ( slam ). Existing approaches mainly rely on delays and angles, and ignore the Doppler, although it contains geometric information. In this paper, we study the benefits of exploiting Doppler in slam through deriving the PCRB and formulating the extended Kalman-Poisson multi-Bernoulli sequential filtering solution with Doppler as one of the involved measurements. Both theoretical PCRB analysis and simulation results demonstrate the efficacy of utilizing Doppler.

III-A

PCRB

The
PCRB
is the lower bound that is analogous to the

(

) but takes a Bayesian perspective and assumes that there is a prior on the parameters, indicating that the
MSE
(

) of an estimator should always be larger than the inverse of the

(
PIM
)
[
22

. Suppose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
denotes a vector of the measurements,
\boldsymbol{\beta}
denotes an
r

\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
denotes an estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, which is a function of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. The
PCRB

\mathbb{E}[\left(\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}]\geq\boldsymbol{J}^{-1},

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{J}
is the
r\times r

PIM
with elements

[\boldsymbol{J}]_{\mu,\nu}=\mathbb{E}[-\frac{\partial^{2}\log f(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial{\beta}_{\mu}\partial{\beta}_{\nu}}]\hskip 20.00003pt\mu,\nu=1,\cdots,r,

(14)

with
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
denoting the joint density. The inequality in (
13
) indicates that the
MSE
of an estimator is larger than
\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}
in the positive semidefinite sense. The
PIM
is a counterpart to the
FIM
(
FIM
) for the

, and can be decomposed into two parts
\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{data}}+\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{prior}}
,
where
\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{data}}
is the standard
FIM
, which contains information obtained from the measurements, and
\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{prior}}
is the priori information matrix, which contains prior information. The elements are given by (for
\mu,\nu=1,\cdots,r
)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{data}}]_{\mu,\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[-\frac{\partial^{2}\log f(\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial{\beta}_{\mu}\partial{\beta}_{\nu}}],

(15)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{J}_{\text{prior}}]_{\mu,\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[-\frac{\partial^{2}\log f(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial{\beta}_{\mu}\partial{\beta}_{\nu}}].

(16)

IV-A

Basics of PMB(M) Density

The map of the environment
\mathcal{X}
is formulated as a
PMBM

rfs
, which can be viewed as the union of two disjoint
rfs
:
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}
for the set of undetected landmarks, which are the landmarks that have never been detected, and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}
for the set of detected landmarks, which are the the landmarks that have been detected at least once before. Its density follows
[
25
]

f(\mathcal{X})=\sum_{\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}\biguplus\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathcal{X}}f_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}})f_{\mathrm{MBM}}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}),

(33)

where
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}
are modeled as a
PPP
(
PPP
) and a
MBM
(
MBM
), respectively, with densities following

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}})

\displaystyle=e^{-\int\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}\prod_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{U}}}\lambda(\boldsymbol{x}),

(34)

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{MBM}}(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{j\in\mathbb{I}}w^{j}\sum_{\mathcal{X}^{1}\biguplus\dots\biguplus\mathcal{X}^{|\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}|}=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}}\prod_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{D}}|}f^{j,i}_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathcal{X}^{i}).

(35)

Here,
\lambda(\cdot)
denotes the intensity function of the
PPP
density, and
\mathbb{I}
is the index set of all global hypotheses with weights satisfying
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{I}}w^{j}=1,w^{j}\geq 0
, and the global hypotheses in
slam
correspond to different
DA

[
26
]
. Each individual component in (
35
) is termed as a Bernoulli process, and
f_{\mathrm{B}}^{j,i}(\cdot)
denotes the Bernoulli density of the
i
-th landmark under the
j
-th global hypothesis, following

f^{j,i}_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathcal{X}^{i})=\begin{cases}1-r^{j,i}\quad&\mathcal{X}^{i}=\emptyset\\
r^{j,i}f^{j,i}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad&\mathcal{X}^{i}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\}\\
0\quad&\mathrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

(36)

where
f^{j,i}(\cdot)
denotes the state density, and
r^{j,i}\in[0,1]
denotes the probability that the corresponding landmark exists. We note that if there is only one mixture component in the
MBM

35
) reduces to a
MB
(

33

PMB

IV-B

EK-PMB(M) SLAM Filter Recursion

The EK-PMB(M) SLAM filter follows the Bayesian filtering recursion with
rfs
, and it computes the joint posterior
f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1})
by
[
16
]

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1}

\displaystyle)\propto\ell(\mathcal{Z}_{k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X})f(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k})

\displaystyle\times f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k}),

(37)

with
f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k})=\int f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k})f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k})\text{d}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
and
\ell(\mathcal{Z}_{k+1}|\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X})
denoting the RFS likelihood function, given by
[
25
, eqs. (5)–(6)]
. Instead of tracking the joint density, the filter keeps track of the marginal
ue

f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k})
and map
f(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k})
posteriors by marginalizing out the map and the
ue
state from the joint posterior, respectively. The marginal posteriors are given by

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1})

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1})\delta\mathcal{X},

(38)

\displaystyle f(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1})

\displaystyle=\int f(\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Z}_{1:k+1})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}_{k+1},

(39)

where
\int\psi(\mathcal{X})\delta\mathcal{X}
refers to the set integral
[
26
, eq. (4)]
.

The EK-PMB(M)
slam

[
16
]

\gamma\geq 1

It is important to mention that the weights of

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we exploited Doppler as a part of measurement in bistatic mmWave radio SLAM, formulated the EK-PMB SLAM filter on the new measurement model, and provided the
PCRB
for the model. Our results theoretically indicate that the involvement of Doppler helps the SLAM filter to acquire better mapping and positioning performance than the case without considering Doppler. The better Doppler observations are, the lower
PCRB
and the more accurate map and
ue
state estimates will be. The implementation of the EK-PMB SLAM filter validates the theoretical benefits of involving Doppler. Future work will include extending the
ue
model to a model with unknown speed, the inclusion of high-dimensional channel estimation, as well as extending the
slam
problem to a
slat
(
slat
) problem.
