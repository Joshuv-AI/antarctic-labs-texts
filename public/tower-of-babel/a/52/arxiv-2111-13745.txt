Combinatorial Relationship Between Finite Fields and Fixed Points of Functions Going Up and Down
Emerson León, Julián Pulido

Abstract
We explore a combinatorial bijection between two seemingly unrelated topics: the roots of irreducible polynomials of degree m m over a finite field 𝔽 p {\mathbb{F}}_{p} for a prime number p p and the number of points that are periodic of order m m for a continuous piece-wise linear function g p : [ 0 , 1 ] → [ 0 , 1 ] g_{p}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1] that goes up and down p p times with slope ± p \pm p . We provide a bijection between 𝔽 p n {\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}} and the fixed points of g p n g^{n}_{p} that naturally relates some of the structure in both worlds. Also we extend our result to other families of continuous functions that go up and down p p times, in particular to Chebyshev polynomials, where we get a better understanding of its fixed points. A generalization for other piece-wise linear functions that are not necessarily continuous is also provided.

Introduction

Let us consider the family of functions
g_{p}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
that linearly increases from
0
to
1

I_{k}
for
k
even, and linearly decreases from
1
to
0
on

for

for

Definition 1.1
.

The function
g_{p}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is given by

g_{p}(x)=\begin{cases}{p}x-k,&\text{for $\frac{k}{{p^{n}}}\leq x\leq\frac{k+1}{{p^{n}}}$ with $k$ even.}\\
k+1-{p}x,&\text{for $\frac{k}{{p^{n}}}\leq x\leq\frac{k+1}{{p^{n}}}$ with $k$ odd.}\end{cases}

Denote by
g_{p}^{n}
to the function obtained by taking

composed with itself
n

is called to be
periodic of order
m
if
g_{p}^{m}(x)=x
, and
g_{p}^{i}(x)\neq x

.

Fixed points of

It is easy to check that

is a function going up and down

.

Notice that if

. The study of these fixed points and their orbits by

]

[
5
]

m

.

We are specially interested in the case when

g_{p}^{n}
with the elements of the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
with
p^{n}
elements, for
p

We begin this work with the following combinatorial observation.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number and
m\geq 1
. The number of points
x\in\FP(g_{p}^{m})
that are periodic of order
m
is equal to
m
times the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree
m
over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

The number of irreducible polynomials is well known (see
[
4
]
) and the recurrence relation that it satisfies is exactly the same as the one used to count orbits of fixed points of
g_{p}
. We explain this in Section
2
. We conclude the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

There is a bijection
B:\FP(g_{p}^{n})\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
, such that
B(x)^{p}=B(g_{p}(x))
for every
x\in\FP(g_{p}^{n})
.

The existence of this bijection is a simple consequence of the enumerative relationship previously described (Theorem
1.2
). In this paper we provide a bijection that also relates with the multiplicative structure of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
.

We construct a permutation map
\pi_{p^{n}}:\{0,\,1,\ldots,\,p^{n}-1\}\rightarrow\{0,\,1,\ldots,\,p^{n}-1\}
that will help us to create our bijection

.

Definition 1.4
.

The permutation
\pi_{p^{n}}
is defined recursively on
n

as follows: take
\pi_{p^{1}}
to be the identity map from 0 to
p-1
. Then to define
\pi_{p^{n}}(k)
for
0\leq k\leq p^{n}-1
, consider the value
a
such that
ap^{n-1}\leq k<(a+1)p^{n-1}
and then take

\pi_{p^{n}}(k)=\begin{cases}\pi_{p^{n-1}}(k-ap^{n-1})+ap^{n-1}\text{ for $a$ even}\\
\pi_{p^{n-1}}(p^{n-1}-(k-ap^{n-1})-1)+ap^{n-1}\text{ for $a$ odd}\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First let
I_{p}(n)
be the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree
n
over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
for
p
prime. This can be explicitly computed using the
Möbius inversion formula
, and the fact that

\sum_{d\mid n}dI_{p}(d)=p^{n},

that comes from the degrees in the irreducible factorization of the polynomial

over

Precisely the same recursive relation is satisfied by our fixed points. Notice that all fixed points in
\FP(f_{p}^{n})
are of order

so that

divides

, and then if we denote by
J(m)
the number of fixed points in
\FP(f_{p}^{m})

\sum_{d\mid n}J_{p}(d)=p^{n}.

We conclude that
J_{p}(m)=mI_{p}(m)
, since the

in both case provides us the same value

J_{p}(m)=mI_{p}(m)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)p^{d},

where

is the classical Möbius function for the natural numbers (with respect to divisibility).

Fixed points and base
p
representation

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{p^{n}-1}
be the fixed points of the function
g_{p}^{n}
.
Then, for
k<p^{n}
we have that

x_{k}=\frac{k}{p^{n}-1}
if
k
is even, or

x_{k}=\frac{k+1}{p^{n}+1}
if
k
is odd.

Proof.

Notice that
\frac{k}{{p^{n}}}\leq x_{k}\leq\frac{k+1}{{p^{n}}}
, then if
k
is even, we have that
g_{p}^{n}(x_{k})={p^{n}}x_{k}-k=x_{k}
which implies
x_{k}=\frac{k}{p^{n}-1}
, if
k
is odd then
g_{p}^{n}(x_{k})=k+1-{p^{n}}x_{k}=x_{k}
which implies
x_{k}=\frac{k+1}{p^{n}+1}

∎

Notation 3.2
.

We denote the expression for a number
k
in base
p
as
k=(a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}
, meaning by this that

k=a_{1}p^{n-1}+a_{2}p^{n-2}+\cdots+a_{n-1}p+a_{n}.

For other rational values, we write
(0.a_{1}a_{2}\ldots)_{p}
for the number
\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}
. If the expression is periodic, the periodic part is overlined. (For convenience, we sometimes don’t use the minimal periodic expression for a number, but some repetitions of it.)

Proposition 3.3
.

The expression in base
p
of
x=\frac{k}{p^{n}-1}
is periodic, where the first
n
digits represent
k
in base
p
and
a_{n+i}=a_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
.
Also, if
x=\frac{k+1}{p^{n}+1},
its expression in base
p
has period
2n
, where the first
n
digits represent
k
in base
p
, while the next
n
digits are complementary of the first
n
digits, that is
a_{n+i}=p-1-a_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
x=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}
the expression for
x
in base
p
. Lets also express
k
in base
p
.

k=k_{1}p^{n-1}+k_{2}p^{n-2}+\cdots+k_{n-1}p+k_{n}.

If
\frac{k}{p^{n}-1}=x=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}
, then
(p^{n}-1)x=k
thus

k=(p^{n}-1)\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{n-i}-\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i},

k=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}p^{n-i}+\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j+n}p^{-j}-\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}.

In the previous line we replaced
i=j+n
for
j\geq 1
.
If we look at the coefficients of the non-negative powers of
p
(where
1-n\leq j\leq 0
) we obtain that
k_{i}=a_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Looking at coefficients with negative powers of
p
(that are zero in the right expression), we find that
a_{n+i}=a_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
, which proves the first part of the proposition.

Similarly if
x=\frac{k+1}{p^{n}+1}
then

(p^{n}+1)\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{-i}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(a_{i}p^{n-i}+a_{i}p^{-i}\right)=k+1,

and therefore
k=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(a_{i}p^{n-i}+a_{i}p^{-i}\right)-1
. Observe that -1 can be expressed as the telescoping series
-1=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(p^{-i}-p^{-(i-1)}\right),
and then

k=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(a_{i}p^{n-i}+a_{i}p^{-i}+p^{-i}-p^{-i+1}\right)=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}p^{n-i}-\sum_{i\geq 1}p^{-i}(p-1-a_{i}),

k=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}p^{n-i}+\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j+n}p^{-j}-\sum_{i\geq 1}p^{-i}(p-1-a_{i}).

Now looking at the coefficients of the non-negative powers of
p
, we obtain that
a_{i}=k_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Also, from the negative powers of
p
that need to have coefficient zero, we obtain that
a_{n+i}=(p-1)-a_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
, and therefore
a_{2n+i}=(p-1)-((p-1)-a_{i})=a_{i}
. This completes the proof.
(Notice that all the series involved in the proof are absolutely convergent so we can rearrange them as we wish).
∎

Proposition 3.5

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.7
.
Before, we need to prove the following propositions that are alternative representations of
\pi_{p^{n}}(k)
using the base

.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
k=(a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}
(where
k<p^{n}
), then
\pi_{p^{n}}(k)=(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}
where
b_{1}=a_{1}
, and
b_{i}=a_{i}
when
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is even or
b_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
when
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is odd.

Proof.

Notice that
a_{1}p^{n-1}\leq k<(a_{1}+1)p^{n-1}
. Also, it holds that
\pi_{p^{n}}(k)=(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}
has an expression in base
p
with
n
digits, where
b_{1}=k=a_{1}
that accounts for the term
kp^{n-1}
adding up, and all other
b_{i}
depend on
\pi_{p^{n-1}}(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})
in case
a_{1}
is even or on
\pi_{p^{n-1}}(p^{n-1}-(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})-1)
in case
a_{1}
is odd.

Then if
a_{1}
is even, by a similar argument, it holds that
\pi_{p^{n-1}}(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})
in base
p
has the same first digit as
k-a_{1}p^{n-1}=(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p},
namely
a_{2}
. Then
b_{2}=a_{2}
and in general it holds that
\pi_{p^{n-1}}(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})=(b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}
.
By an inductive argument on
n
, the number of digits of
k
in base
p
, we can assume that the result holds for
k-a_{1}p^{n-1}
and then
b_{i}=a_{i}
when
b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is even or
b_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
when
b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is odd. Since
b_{1}=a_{1}
is even, the result holds as well for
k
.

On the other hand, notice that if
p^{n-1}-1-(i-a_{1}p^{n-1})=(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}
every digit
c_{i}
is the complement of
a_{i}
, meaning by this that
c_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
. This is true since all digits of
p^{n-1}-1
in base
p
are
p-1
.

Therefore if
a_{1}
is odd, then
b_{2}=c_{2}=p-1-a_{2}
. Again by an inductive analysis we assume that
b_{i}=c_{i}
when
b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is even or
b_{i}=p-1-c_{i}
when
b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is odd. Since
b_{1}=a_{1}
is odd, then
b_{i}=c_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
when
b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is odd or
b_{i}=p-1-c_{i}=a_{i}
when
b_{1}+b_{2}+\cdots+b_{i-1}
is even.
∎

Notice that for
p
odd, taking

complement doesn’t change the parity of the digits, and therefore we could use the parity of
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{i-1}
to create the two cases in the previous proposition. In case

things are a little bit different.

Proposition 4.2
.

If
p=2
and
i=(a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{2}
, then
\pi_{p^{n}}(i)=(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{2}
where
b_{1}=a_{1}
, and
b_{i}=0
if
a_{i-1}=a_{i}
or
b_{i}=1
otherwise.

Proof.

We prove it inductively. From Proposition
4.1
, we know that
a_{1}=b_{1}
. We assume that
b_{i}=0
if
a_{i-1}=a_{i}
or
b_{i}=1
otherwise, for values of
i
smaller than
j
and check that the result holds for

. We divide in two cases.

\bullet

If
b_{1}+\ldots+b_{j-1}
is even, then by induction hypothesis, there is an even number of changes on the

, and therefore
a_{j-1}=0
. Also, from Proposition
4.1
, we see that
b_{j}=a_{j}
. It is clear that if
a_{j}=0
, then
a_{j-1}=a_{j}
and
b_{j}=0
, while if
a_{j}=1
, then
a_{j-1}\neq a_{j}
and

.

\bullet

If
b_{1}+\ldots+b_{i-1}
is odd, then we have an odd number of changes and
a_{j-1}=1
. Also, from Proposition

, we see that
b_{j}=1-a_{j}
. If
a_{j}=0
,
b_{j}=1
and
a{j-1}\neq a_{j}
while if
a_{j}=1
, then

and

.

In all cases we check that the result holds. By induction, it is true for all

.
∎

, and

When

Then
g_{p}(x_{i})=(0.\overline{a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{n}a_{1}})_{p}=x_{(a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1})_{p}}

d_{1}=p-1-a^{\prime}_{1}=c^{\prime}_{1}

is odd.

Now we need to check that

for

.

for

. For

Bijection for other continuous functions

Definition 5.1
.

Let
u<v\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We say that such a function
f_{p}:[u,v]\rightarrow[u,v]

goes up and down
p
times
if it is a continuous function so that there are numbers
u=z_{0}<z_{1}<z_{2}<\cdots<z_{p}=v
where
f_{p}(z_{k})
is always
u
or
v
for any
k
, and
f_{p}
is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing when restricted to any interval
[z_{k},z_{k+1}]
for
k<p
. We will also require that the function
f_{p}
has exactly
p
fixed points, one on each interval
[z_{k},z_{k+1}]
.

Let
p
be a prime number. We want to extend Theorem
1.7
to other functions
f_{p}:[u,v]\rightarrow[u,v]
going up and down
p
times. We restrict to the case that there is a continuous bijection
h:[0,1]\rightarrow[u,v]
that is a homeomorphism between both functions
g_{p}
and
f_{p}
(so that
f_{p}\circ h=h\circ g_{p}
). In this case we can easily extend our results to
f_{p}
as well. For us this is enough to include another important family of continuous functions going up and down, namely the
Chebyshev Polynomials
.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
f_{p}

goes up and down
p
times
and there is a continuous bijection
h:[0,1]\rightarrow[u,v]
so that
f_{p}\circ h=h\circ g_{p}
, then there is a bijection
B_{f}:\FP(f_{p}^{n})\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
so that
(B_{f}(y_{i}))^{p}=B_{f}(f_{p}(y_{i}))
for any fixed point
y_{i}\in\FP(f_{p}^{n}).

Proof.

A point
x\in[0,1]
is a fixed point of
g_{p}^{n}
if and only if
h(g_{p}^{n}(x))=h(x)
since
h
is bijective, and
f_{p}^{n}(h(x))=h(g_{p}^{n}(x))=h(x)
if and only if
h(x)
is also a fixed point of
f_{p}^{n}
.
Then, if
0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{p^{n}-1}
are the
p-1
fixed points of the function
g_{p}^{n}
and
y_{i}=h(x_{i})
for
0\leq i<p^{n}
, then
y_{0},\,y_{1},\ldots,\,y_{p^{n}-1}
are the
p^{n}
fixed points of the function
f_{p}^{n}
. Notice that
h
preserves order or completely inverts it.

We can simply take
B_{f}=B_{\alpha}\circ h^{-1}
, so that if
B_{f}(y_{i})=B_{\alpha}(x_{i})
for all
0\leq i<p^{n}.

Notice that if
g_{p}(x_{i})=x_{j}
, then
f_{p}(y_{i})=y_{j}
, since
f_{p}(y_{i})=f_{p}(h(x_{i}))=h(g_{p}(x_{i}))=h(x_{j})=y_{j}
. Then

(B_{f}(y_{i}))^{p}=(B_{\alpha}(x_{i}))^{p}=B_{\alpha}(g_{p}(x_{i}))=B_{\alpha}(x_{j})=B_{f}(y_{j})=B_{f}(f_{p}(y_{i})).

∎

The continuous bijection

n+1
. We use the notation base
p
here, so take
k=(a_{1}\ldots a_{n+1})_{p}<p^{n+1}
.
By definition,

\pi_{p^{n+1},I}(k)=\begin{cases}a_{1}p^{n}+\pi_{p^{n},I}(k-a_{1}p^{n})\text{ for $a_{1}\in I$}\\
a_{1}p^{n}+\pi_{p^{n-1},I}(p^{n-1}-(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})-1)\text{ for $a_{1}\notin I$}\end{cases}

\bullet

Consider first the case
a_{1}\in I
. Here
k-a_{1}p^{n}=(a_{2}\ldots a_{n+1})_{p}
and by hypothesis, we have that

\pi_{p^{n},I}(k-a_{1}p^{n})=\begin{cases}p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+a_{n+1}\text{ for $b^{\prime}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+(p-a_{n+1}-1)\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

for
b^{\prime}=(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}
. Then,

\pi_{p^{n+1},I}(k)=a_{1}p^{n}+p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}a_{n+1}\text{ for $b^{\prime}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
(p-a_{n+1}-1)\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

\pi_{p^{n+1},I}(k)=p\pi_{p^{n},I}((a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}a_{n+1}\text{ for $(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
(p-a_{n+1}-1)\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Using
a_{1}\in I
and by Proposition
6.7
, we get the desired condition.

\bullet

Now for the case
a_{1}\notin I
. Here
(p^{n-1}-(k-a_{1}p^{n-1})-1)=(c_{2}\ldots c_{n+1})_{p}
where
c_{i}=p-a_{i}-1
. By hypothesis, we have that

\pi_{p^{n},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n+1})_{p})=\begin{cases}p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})+c_{n+1}\text{ for $b^{\prime}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})+a_{n+1}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

for
b^{\prime}=(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}
. Then,

\pi_{p^{n+1},I}(k)=a_{1}p^{n}+p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}c_{n+1}\text{ for $b^{\prime}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
a_{n+1}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

\pi_{p^{n+1},I}(k)=p\pi_{p^{n},I}((a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}(p-a_{n+1}-1)\text{ for $(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
a_{n+1}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Now by the second part of Proposition
6.7
, the result holds.

∎

Definition 6.9

(Bijection
B_{\alpha,I}
)
.

Let
\alpha
be a primitive root (i.e., a generator of the multiplicative group) of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
(
[
4
]
) and let
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{p^{n}-1}
be the fixed points of
g_{p,I}^{n}
.
We define the bijection
B_{\alpha,I}:\FP(g_{p,I}^{n})\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
by

B_{\alpha}(x_{\pi_{p^{n},I}(0)})=0\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
or
B_{\alpha}(x_{k})=\alpha^{\pi_{p^{n},I}(k)}
for all other
k>0
.

The following theorem generalizes Theorem
1.7
. We keep the initial proof of this Theorem
1.7
, since the continuous case where
I
is the set of even numbers less than
p
has some extra structure and provides a good example of the general theory, where the sets
I_{p^{n}}
and functions
\pi_{p^{n},I}
could be described in more detail.

Theorem 6.10
.

The function
B_{\alpha,I}
is a bijection and satisfy that
B_{\alpha,I}(x_{k})^{p}=B_{\alpha,I}(g_{p,I}(x_{k}))
for any fixed point
x_{k}\in\FP(f_{p}^{n}).

Proof.

Consider a fixed point
x_{k}
for
k=(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}
. From Proposition
6.3
and
3.3
we can express
x_{k}=(0.\overline{a_{1}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{n}})_{p}
where
a^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i}
in case that
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
, or
a^{\prime}_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
if
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\notin I_{p^{n}}
.

Denote
\pi_{p^{n},I}(k)=(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p},
.
Then
(B_{\alpha}(x_{k}))^{p}=\alpha^{p(b_{1}\ldots b_{n})_{p}}=\alpha^{(b_{1}\ldots b_{n}0)_{p}}=\alpha^{(b_{2}\ldots b_{n}b_{1})_{p}}
, since
\alpha^{p^{n}}=\alpha
in the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
.
Now we consider two cases, depending if
a_{1}
is in
I
or not.

\bullet

If
a_{1}\in I
:
Then
g_{p}(x_{i})=(0.\overline{a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1}\ldots a^{\prime}_{n}a_{1}})_{p}=x_{(a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1})_{p}}
, due to Proposition
6.4
. We denote
B_{\alpha}(g_{p}(x_{k}))=\alpha^{(d_{2}\ldots d_{n}d_{1})_{p}}
, where
(d_{2}\ldots d_{n}d_{1})_{p}=\pi_{p^{n},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1})_{p})
. Now we need to check that
d_{i}=b_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
By Proposition
6.8
, then

\pi_{p^{n},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n}a^{\prime}_{1})_{p})=p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}a^{\prime}_{1}\text{ for $(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
(p-1-a^{\prime}_{1})\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Therefore
(d_{2}\ldots d_{n})_{p}=\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})
, while
d_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1}
if
(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}
or
d_{1}=p-1-a^{\prime}_{1}
otherwise.

On the other hand, since
a_{1}\in I
, by Definition
6.5
, we have that

(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}=a_{1}p^{n-1}+\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})
and therefore
(b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}=\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p})=(d_{2}\ldots d_{n})_{p}
, while
b_{1}=a_{1}
. We use here Proposition
6.7
, to see that
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
if and only if
(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}
. Therefore if
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
, then

b_{1}=a_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1}=d_{1}
, and if
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\notin I_{p^{n}}
, is easy to check that
b_{1}=d_{1}
as well.

\bullet

If
a_{1}\notin I
:
In this case
g_{p}(x_{i})=(0.\overline{c_{2}\ldots c_{n}c^{\prime}_{1}\ldots c^{\prime}_{n}c_{1}})_{p}=x_{(c_{2}\ldots c_{n}c^{\prime}_{1})_{p}}
, where
c_{i}=p-1-a_{i}
and
c^{\prime}_{i}=p-1-a^{\prime}_{i},
due to Proposition
6.4
. We denote
B_{\alpha}(g_{p}(x_{k}))=\alpha^{(d_{2}\ldots d_{n}d_{1})_{p}}
, where
(d_{2}\ldots d_{n}d_{1})_{p}=\pi_{p^{n}}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n}c^{\prime}_{1})_{p})
.

Again we need to check that
d_{i}=b_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. This time, since
a_{1}\notin I
, we have that
(b_{1}b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}=a_{1}p^{n-1}+\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})
, while by Proposition
6.8
we have that

\pi_{p^{n},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n}c^{\prime}_{1})_{p})=p\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})+\begin{cases}c^{\prime}_{1}\text{ for $(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}$}\\
p-1-c^{\prime}_{1}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
(b_{2}\ldots b_{n})_{p}=\pi_{p^{n-1},I}((c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p})=(d_{2}\ldots d_{n})_{p}
, while
b_{1}=a_{1}
and
d_{1}=c^{\prime}_{1}
for
(a_{2}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}
, or
d_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1}
otherwise. This time we use the second part of Proposition
6.7
to see that
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
if and only if
(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}\notin I_{p^{n-1}}
.
We check both cases, first if
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
, then
a_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1}
, while
(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}\notin I_{p^{n-1}}
implies
d=p-1-c^{\prime}_{1}=a^{\prime}_{1}=a_{1}=b_{1}
.
On the other hand,
(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n}}
implies that
a^{\prime}_{1}=c_{1}=p-1-a_{1}
, while
(c_{2}\ldots c_{n})_{p}\in I_{p^{n-1}}
and therefore
d_{1}=c^{\prime}_{1}=p-1-a^{\prime}_{1}=a_{1}=b_{1}
. In any case,
b_{i}=d_{i}
, as desired.

∎
