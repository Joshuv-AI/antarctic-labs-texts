adjustedCurves: Estimating Confounder-Adjusted Survival Curves in R
Robin Denz, Nina Timmesfeld

Abstract
Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by treatment allocation are the most popular way to depict causal effects in studies with right-censored time-to-event endpoints. If the treatment is randomly assigned and the sample size of the study is adequate, this method produces unbiased estimates of the population-averaged counterfactual survival curves. However, in the presence of confounding, this is no longer the case. Instead, specific methods that allow adjustment for confounding must be used. We present the adjustedCurves R package, which can be used to estimate and plot these confounder-adjusted survival curves using a variety of methods from the literature. It provides a convenient wrapper around existing R packages on the topic and adds additional methods and functionality on top of it, uniting the sometimes vastly different methods under one consistent framework. Among the additional features are the estimation of confidence intervals, confounder-adjusted restricted mean survival times and confounder-adjusted survival time quantiles. After giving a brief overview of the implemented methods, we illustrate the package using publicly available data from an observational study including 2982 breast cancer. Keywords: adjusted, survival curves, restricted mean survival time, R



1
Introduction

Treatment-specific survival curves are the standard way to visualize treatment effects in studies with time-to-event endpoints. The Kaplan-Meier estimator
(
Kaplan & Meier 1958
)
is commonly used to estimate these curves. In large randomized controlled trials with a balanced distribution of confounders across groups, this estimator produces unbiased estimates. However, this is not the case when the treatment was not assigned randomly or when some of the participating individuals did not adhere to their randomly assigned treatment. In such studies, confounders of the treatment-outcome relationship can systematically bias the depicted survival curves
(
Nieto & Coresh 1996
)
.

Although confounding is a well-known problem, it is often ignored when estimating survival curves. In many cases, the main analysis is carried out using a suitable regression model, such as the Cox proportional hazards model
(
Cox 1972
)
. The estimated coefficients or hazard-ratios are then reported in tabular form. For graphical depiction, only simple un-adjusted Kaplan-Meier curves are used
(
Dey et al. 2020
)
. This is problematic, because the adjusted regression analysis will produce different results than the un-adjusted analysis if confounding is present. In this case, the depiction of the treatment effect in the un-adjusted graphs will not match the regression analyses, confusing the reader
(
Hu et al. 2020
)
.

One possible way to avoid this confusion is to not display any visualization of the treatment effect at all. This approach is flawed, because graphics facilitate the communication of statistical analyses
(
Davis et al. 2010
;
Zipkin et al. 2014
;
Lundgreen et al. 2021
)
. This is especially important when the aim of the study is to estimate causal effects. As
Cunningham 2021
aptly put it: "causal inference studies desperately need data visualization of the main effects".

Another reason not to abstain from survival curve estimation is that they can easily be used to obtain absolute measures of effect, while time-to-event-based regression models by themselves usually only result in relative effect measures, such as the hazard-ratio. For example, adjusted survival curves can be used to directly estimate average treatment effects using differences at specific times
(
Klein et al. 2007
)
, restricted mean survival times
(
Conner et al. 2019
)
or survival time quantiles
(
Chen & Zhang 2016
)
. Both the CONSORT Guidelines (“Consolidated Statement of Reporting Trials”)
(
Schulz et al. 2010
)
and the STROBE Statement (“Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology”)
(
Vandenbroucke et al. 2007
)
recommend the use of such absolute effect measures.

Luckily, multiple methods to estimate confounder-adjusted survival curves have been proposed in the literature. These can roughly be divided into
Stratification
based methods
(
Nieto & Coresh 1996
;
Cupples et al. 1995
;
Gregory 1988
;
Amato 1988
)
,
G-Computation

(
Makuch 1982
;
Chang et al. 1982
)
,
Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting
(IPTW)
(
Cole & Hernán 2004
;
Xie & Liu 2005
)
,
Propensity Score Matching

(
Austin 2014
)
,
Empirical Likelihood Estimation
(EL)
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
,
Augmented Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting
(AIPTW)
(
Robins & Rotnitzky 1992
;
Ozenne et al. 2020
)
,
Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation
(TMLE)
(
Moore & van der Laan 2009
;
Stitelman & van der Laan 2010
;
Cai & van der Laan 2020
)
, an instrumental-variable based method
(
Martínez-Camblor et al. 2020
)
and some of their
Pseudo-Values
(PV) based versions
(
Andersen et al. 2017
;
Wang 2018
)
. A small review and extensive simulation study is given by
Denz et al. 2023
.

There is currently no single software package which implements all of these methods in a consistent framework. Existing implementations focus only on one or a few methods or offer no suitable plotting routines. The
adjustedCurves

R
package directly addresses these deficits by providing high-level user-friendly implementations of all available methods, including confidence interval calculation. It also provides highly-customizable
plot
functions based on the
ggplot2
package
(
Wickham 2016
)
to produce publication-ready graphics. The package is designed to be usable by researchers with limited
R
experience, while still offering a substantial amount of arguments and functionality for more advanced users. A stable release version is available on CRAN
(
Denz 2024
)
, while the developmental version can be found on github (
https://github.com/RobinDenz1/adjustedCurves
). This article presents this package.

First, we formally define what confounder-adjusted survival curves are and describe ways to estimate average treatment effects based on them. Afterwards, we give a short review of all available methods to estimate confounder-adjusted survival curves from observational data. Next, we review some of the existing software implementations in the most popular programming languages
R
,
Stata
and
SAS
. Subsequently, we describe the concrete implementation in the proposed
adjustedCurves
package and give some simple examples of its usage based on the
rottderam
data set
(
Foekens et al. 2000
)
contained in the
survival

R
package
(
Therneau 2021
)
. Finally, we give a brief discussion about the proposed
R
package.

2
Target Estimand

Let
T
be the time until the occurrence of the event of interest. Let
Z\in\{0,1,...,k\}
be a treatment indicator, where each integer represents one of
k
possible treatments at baseline. Under the potential outcomes framework, there are
k
potential survival times
T^{Z=z}
, one for each of the
k
possible treatments
(
Neyman 1923
;
Rubin 1974
)
. We further define
X
to be a vector of baseline covariates for each individual
i
. The counterfactual survival probability of individual
i
at time
t
is then given by:

S_{i}(t|Z=z,X=x_{i})=P(T^{Z=z}<t|X=x_{i}),

(1)

where
T_{Z=z}
is the survival time that would have been observed if
Z
was set to
z
by some intervention
(
Cai & van der Laan 2020
;
Woodward 2003
)
. Although this individual level quantity may be very useful in some cases, it is not the primary target estimand here. Instead, we are interested in estimating the survival probability that would have been observed in some defined super-population, if every individual in that population had received treatment
z
. It can be formally defined as:

S_{z}(t)=E(I(T^{Z=z}<t)).

(2)

This quantity is known as the the population level
counterfactual survival probability
, sometimes also called
causal survival probability
or
confounder-adjusted survival probability
. We use all three terms interchangeably in this paper. When this function is displayed over time, it is often called the
counterfactual survival curve
etc. Since this function is defined with respect to a population regardless of their actual treatment status, it may be considered a sort of average treatment effect by itself
(
Denz et al. 2023
)
.

In order to estimate
S_{z}(t)
in practice, four fundamental assumptions have to be met. The first one is the
conditional exchangeability
(also known as
no unmeasured confounding
) assumption, which states that, inside the strata defined by the relevant confounders, the outcome of the study would not change if the treatment groups were exchanged. Formally, this can be written as
T^{Z=z}\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}Z|X

(
Sarvet et al. 2020
)
. The second assumption is known as the
positivity
assumption, which states that every individual must have a non-zero probability of receiving each possible treatment
(
Hernán & Robins 2019
)
. Given the observed confounders
X
, this can be represented as
P(Z=z|X)>0
for all possible values of
Z
.

The third assumption is that the potential survival time would have been the same regardless of how the treatment status was set to
Z
. This is known as the
counterfactual consistency
assumption
(
Hernán 2016
)
. Having a ’well-defined’ treatment increases the probability that this assumption holds
(
VanderWeele 2018
)
. Finally, the
no interference assumption
has to hold. This means that the potential survival time of each person is independent of the observed treatment status of other people
(
Naimi & Kaufman 2015
)
. An in-depth discussion of these assumptions can be found in
Hernán & Robins 2019
and
Guo & Fraser 2015
.

3
Defining Average Treatment Effects

Visualizing estimates of
S_{z}(t)
for all possible
Z
over the observed range of time is a great way to visualize the treatment effect of
Z
on the survival time. However, it may also be useful to compare these counterfactual survival curves more directly. This can be done by directly contrasting them or by first deriving statistics that summarize
S_{z}(t)
in some way and contrasting those. In this way,
average treatment effects
(also known as
average causal effects
) are defined directly on the basis of the target estimand. The
adjustedCurves
package offers three such statistics: the difference between counterfactual survival probabilities, the counterfactual survival time quantiles and the counterfactual restricted mean survival time. All of these can be estimated by utilizing estimates of
S_{z}(t)
directly.

The advantage of using these summary statistics instead of more popular effect measures, such as the hazard-ratio obtained from a Cox model, is that they are absolute measures of effect that can be interpreted causally (under the assumptions stated above). In contrast, it has been shown repeatedly that the hazard-ratio obtained by using a Cox model does not allow causal interpretations, not even in randomized controlled trials
(
Hernán 2010
;
Aalen et al. 2015
)
. Using the effect measures presented here instead, one may circumvent this problem.

3.1
Differences in Survival Probabilities

The simple difference between two treatment-specific counterfactual survival probabilities at some specified point in time
t
is given by:

\Delta_{a,b}(t)=S_{z=a}(t)-S_{z=b}(t),

(3)

where
a
and
b
are some possible values of
Z
. This difference is an absolute measure of effect and has a very straightforward interpretation. It is, however, a time-dependent quantity and as such requires a theoretically grounded choice of
t
if one wants to use it to define the average treatment effects
(
Klein et al. 2007
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
. Alternatively, one can depict estimates of
\Delta_{a,b}(t)
over time to highlight differences between two treatment groups
(
Coory et al. 2014
)
.

3.2
Survival Time Quantiles

Another well known statistic to compare two survival functions is the
Median Survival Time

(
Chen & Zhang 2016
;
Ben-Aharon et al. 2019
)
. It is defined as the first point in time at which the survival probability in the respective group is equal to or below 0.5. Instead of using the median, other quantiles such as the 0.25 quantile or the 0.75 quantile may also be used. Formally, such survival time quantiles are defined as:

\hat{Q}_{z}(p)=min\left(t|S_{z}(t)\leq p\right),

(4)

where
p
is the survival time quantile of interest. In other words, this quantity can simply be read off
S_{z}(t)
by drawing a horizontal line at
S(t|Z=z)=p
and taking the first associated value of the survival time
t
at this point. The difference between those quantities may also be used to define average treatment effects.

3.3
Restricted Mean Survival Times

The counterfactual restricted mean survival time is defined as the area under
S_{z}(t)
from 0 to some previously specified point in time
\tau

Conner et al. 2019
. Formally, the RMST is defined as:

\text{RMST}_{z}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}S_{z}(t)dt.

(5)

It can be interpreted as the mean survival time of the individuals in that interval. Multiple authors advocate for its use, naming the natural interpretation and ease of estimation as desirable properties
(
Royston & Parmar 2013
;
Hasegawa et al. 2020
)
. As with the survival time quantiles, one may use the difference between two treatment-specific
\text{RMST}_{z}(\tau)
values to define the average causal effect
(
Ambrogi et al. 2022
)
. Although this quantity is a summary measure of
S_{z}(t)
over a specific range of time, it is still dependent on the choice of
\tau
. Some guidance to the choice of
\tau
has been published
(
Sheldon 2013
)
but it remains a difficult endeavor.

Instead of choosing a single
\tau
, it is also possible to estimate
\text{RMST}_{z}(\tau)
for a range of
\tau
values and plot the results over time
(
Zhao et al. 2016
)
. All of these options are also implemented in the
adjustedCurves
package.

4
Methods

In the following section we will give a short review of all methods that may be used to estimate
S_{z}(t)
using the
adjustedCurves
package, if the stated identifiability assumptions are met. The
adjustedCurves
package focuses strictly on binary or categorical treatments that do not change over time. Methods to estimate
S_{z}(t)
for continuous
Z

(
Denz & Timmesfeld 2023
)
or for time-varying
Z

(
Clare et al. 2019
)
are therefore not mentioned here. We will assume that the observed data contains right-censoring, meaning that for some observations it is only known that the respective individual survived up to a certain point in time, without any further information. More complex forms of censoring, such as left-censoring or interval-censoring
(
Kleinbaum & Klein 2012
)
are not supported by the
adjustedCurves
package and are therefore not discussed here. However, all methods presented here also work if the data contains no right-censoring.

As stated above, we assume conditional exchangeability. This means that we require all confounders to be known and measured, which is very difficult in practice. To identify the relevant confounders, causal directed acyclic graphs may be used in conjunction with Pearls backdoor criterion
(
Pearl 2009
)
. If some of the identified confounders are not measured, the methods included in this package are not suitable to obtain unbiased estimates of
S_{z}(t)
, with the single exception of the instrumental-variable based strategie described by
(
Martínez-Camblor et al. 2020
)
, which is also included in the
adjustedCurves
package. More information are given below. For all other methods we will assume that the relevant confounders have been identified and are fully measured without error.

For the sake of simplicity, we will not give detailed equations for all estimators implemented in this
R
package. Instead we only present some equations where necessary or useful and give a short text description of how each method works otherwise. The full equations of the estimators and, if applicable, their respective variance estimators can be found in the cited literature.

4.1
No Adjustment

The standard Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimator
(
Kaplan & Meier 1958
)
of the un-adjusted survival function for treatment
z
is given by:

\hat{S}_{KM}(t|Z=z)=\prod_{j:t_{j}\leq t}\left(1-\frac{d_{jz}}{n_{jz}}\right),

(6)

where
d_{jz}
is the number of events at time
t_{j}
in treatment group
z
and
n_{jz}
is the number of individuals still at risk up to time
t_{j}
in group
z
, that is all individuals who did not experience an event previously and who are not censored. As stated before,
\hat{S}_{KM}(t|z)
is not an unbiased estimate of
S_{z}(t)
if confounding is present. Within our package,
\hat{S}_{KM}(t|Z=z)
can be calculated using
method="km"
in the
adjustedsurv
function. More details on the implementation is given below. These estimators are included for reference only and should not be used when confounder-adjustment is the goal of the analysis.

4.2
Stratification

A very simple way to remove confounding is by performing an analysis stratified by each possible strata of the confounders and calculating an appropriately weighted pooled estimate afterwards. If there are only a few confounders which happen to have a small number of possible values, this is a valid strategy. For survival curve estimation, we can calculate simple Kaplan-Meier estimates stratified by the confounders and treatment, and take a weighted average of these Kaplan-Meier estimates inside each level of
Z
at some point in time
t
. The weights are simply the number of occurrences of each confounder level in the full sample
(
Kramar & Com-Nougué 1990
;
Cupples et al. 1995
)
.

This method is implemented in the
adjustedsurv
function under

method="strat_cupples"
. A very similar estimator that was proposed independently by
Gregory 1988
and
Nieto & Coresh 1996
is also implemented in this function using
method="strat_nieto"
. Both methods, however, only work for those
t
in which there is still at least one person at risk in each stratum. To overcome this difficulty,
Amato 1988
proposed a more sophisticated version of this estimator (
method="strat_amato"
).

Although these methods can only be used with a limited number of categorical confounders, they should not be disregarded. In cases with only a few categorical confounders, they offer a simple and, most importantly, non-parametric way to estimate the quantity of interest. Most other methods implemented in this package rely on the correct specification of at least one parametric or semi-parametric model, which may be difficult in practice.

4.3
G-Computation

This method is also known as
Direct Standardization
,
Corrected-Group Prognosis
method, or more generally, as a
G-Formula
method
(
Makuch 1982
;
Chang et al. 1982
;
Robins 1986
)
. First, a model describing the time-to-event outcome is estimated using the available data. This model should include all relevant confounders as independent covariates and should directly account for right-censoring. In the case of a single-event type, the Cox-Proportional hazards model is often used, although any other model may be used as well. The fitted model is then used to make predictions for the conditional probabilities for every person
i
under all treatment regimes
Z
at
t
. The estimator for
S_{z}(t)
is then defined as:

\hat{S}_{G-Comp}(t|Z=z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{S}(t|Z=z,X=x_{i})\right),

(7)

where
n
is the total sample size and
\hat{S}(t|Z=z,X=x_{i})
are the conditional survival predictions. To make these kind of predictions using a Cox model, an additional estimate of the baseline-hazard function is needed, which can be estimated directly from the data
(
Breslow 1972
)
. In general, any model that produces conditional survival probability predictions may be used. Possibly alternatives are additive hazards models
(
Aalen 1980
)
or flexible hazards models
(
Royston & Parmar 2002
)
. This is directly supported in the
adjustedCurves
package.

In addition to the four identifiability assumptions, this method also requires that the time-to-event model is correctly specified, which means that all assumptions made when fitting the model have to be met as well. If this is not the case, the resulting estimates are biased. In our package, g-computation can be performed using
method="direct"
in the
adjustedsurv
function. It internally relies on the fast
predictRisk
function of the
riskRegression

(
Gerds & Kattan 2021
)
package to obtain the conditional survival probability predictions.

4.4
Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW)

Instead of modelling the time-to-event outcome, we can also adjust for confounding using the
treatment-assignment
mechanism. This usually entails fitting a model to predict the conditional probability of receiving treatment
Z
, defined as:

\pi(Z)=P(Z|X=x),

(8)

which is formally known as the
propensity score
. By conducting an analysis weighted by the inverse of this probability
w(Z)=1/\pi(Z)
, the distribution of the confounders is approximately equal between the treatment groups and the confounding is removed. That is, if
\pi(Z)
was correctly estimated. Popular models to estimate this quantity are logistic regression models when
Z
is binary and multinomial regression models when
Z
is categorical. It is, however, not necessary to rely on the propensity score. Other weights
w(Z)
that can be used to create covariate balance between treatment groups have been proposed in the literature
(
Imai & Ratkovic 2014
;
Hainmueller 2012
)
. Those are also supported by the
adjustedCurves
package.

After such balancing weights have been estimated, the following estimator can be used to estimate
S_{z}(t)
as:

\hat{S}_{IPTW}(t|Z=z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }t<t_{1}\\
\prod_{t\leq t_{j}}[1-d_{jz}^{w}/Y_{jz}^{w}]&\mbox{if }t\geq t_{1}\end{array}\right.

(9)

for
Y_{jz}^{w}>0
, where
Y_{jz}^{w}
is the weighted number of individuals at risk in group
z
, defined as:

Y_{jz}^{w}=\sum_{i:T_{i}\geq t_{j}}w(Z_{i})I(Z_{i}=z)

(10)

and
d_{jz}^{w}
is defined as the weighted number of individuals with events out of the
Y_{jz}^{w}
people at risk in group
z
, given by:

d_{jz}^{w}=\sum_{i:T_{i}=t_{j}}w(Z_{i})A_{i}I(Z_{i}=z).

(11)

This estimator is implemented in our package as
method="iptw_km"
in the

adjustedsurv
function. A very similar estimator that has been proposed by
Cole & Hernán 2004
is also implemented in that function (
method="iptw_cox"
).

4.5
Propensity Score Matching

A different way to utilize the propensity score for confounder-adjustment is
Propensity Score Matching

(
Austin 2014
)
. By matching individuals with similar propensity scores to each other, a sample is generated in which the propensity scores are equally distributed between treatment groups. This procedure creates covariate balance, if the model used to estimate the propensity scores is correctly specified and the matching algorithm is appropriate. We can then use standard methods, such as the Kaplan-Meier estimator, on the matched data to get an unbiased estimates of the causal survival curve. This estimator can be used by setting
method="matching"
in the
adjustedsurv
function. Internally, it relies on the
Matching
package
(
Sekhon 2011
)
to construct the matched data set using nearest neighboor matching with replacement.

There are other methods to obtain matching based estimates of the causal survival curve described in the literature
(
Winnett & Sasieni 2002
;
Galimberti et al. 2002
;
Austin et al. 2020
)
. We choose not to implement those methods directly, because of the large amount of modeling and parameter choices that need to be made to obtain valid estimates. We believe that there is no way to automate these methods without enticing bad research practice. The matching estimator here is included primarily as a simple example and to raise awareness that matching is a valid alternative to the other implemented methods.

4.6
Empirical Likelihood Estimation (EL)

The previous two methods relied on an unbiased estimate of the propensity score, which, as mentioned above, are usually estimated using parametric models such as the logistic regression model. When using parametric models, assumptions need to be made about the functional form of the relationship between confounders and the treatment variable. To circumvent the need for these assumptions,
Wang et al. 2019
proposed the use of
Empirical Likelihood Estimation
(EL) to obtain an estimate of the causal survival curve. This method is very similar to the IPTW estimator described in section
4.4
, but the weights are obtained using EL. In EL, a constrained likelihood function is maximized which forces the moment of the confounders to be approximately equal between treatment groups.

This estimator is available for causal survival curves using
method="emp_lik"
in the
adjustedsurv
function. It relies on code from the
adjKMtest
(available at
https://github.com/kimihua1995/adjKMtest
). An alternative way to get similar estimates is to estimate non-parametric covariate balance weights
(
Chan et al. 2016
)
using the
WeightIt
package
(
Greifer 2021
)
and passing those to an IPTW estimator such as the one described in section
4.4
(
method %in% c("iptw_km", "iptw_cox", "iptw_pseudo")
).

4.7
Augmented Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting

(AIPTW)

All methods mentioned above relied either on creating covariate balance between the treatment groups (IPTW, Propensity Score Matching, EL) or on directly substituting the individual counterfactual probabilities with model based predictions (Direct Standardization).
Augmented Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting
(AIPTW) combines both approaches in one estimator, by using a model for the treatment assignment mechanism and a model for the outcome mechanism
(
Robins & Rotnitzky 1992
;
Hubbard et al. 2000
)
. If
either
of these models is correctly specified, the resulting estimates are asymptotically unbiased. It therefore gives the researcher "two chances to get it right"
(
Hernán & Robins 2019
, p. 167)
. For the sake of simplicity, we will only present the equation of the estimator when no right-censoring is present. The AIPTW estimator for
S_{z}(t)
is then given by:

\hat{S}_{AIPTW}(t|Z=z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{I(T_{i}>t,D_{i}=1)Z_{i}}{\hat{\pi}(z,X_{i})}+\hat{S}(t|z,X_{i})\left(1-\frac{Z_{i}}{\hat{\pi}(z,X_{i})}\right)\right).

(12)

This equation is adapted from
Ozenne et al. 2020
, where an extension to the situation with right-censored data is also given. Essentially, the estimator takes an estimate of the IPTW survival probability and adds a weighted version of the conditional probability predictions to it. Due to the form of this equation, it is possible that this method produces estimates that are smaller than 0 or larger than 1. It is also possible that the resulting survival curve is non-monotonic, which means that it may increase again at some point in time. However, theoretical results
(
Westling et al. 2020
)
and a simulation study
(
Denz et al. 2023
)
indicate that simple post-estimation correction strategies are sufficient to correct the estimates. This correction method consists of truncating out-of-bounds estimates at 0 or 1 and performing isotonic regression afterwards.

In the present package, this estimator can be used by setting
method="aiptw"
in the
adjustedsurv
function. Internally, it relies on the
ate
function from the
riskRegression
package
(
Gerds & Kattan 2021
)
. The post-estimation correction methods are also directly implemented in the
adjustedsurv
function and the associated
plot
method.

4.8
Pseudo-Values (PV)

Pseudo-Values (PV) are a clever technique to bypass the need for estimators that can handle right-censoring. The general idea is that one could use standard statistical techniques, such as generalized linear models, for the analysis of survival data, if there was no censoring
(
Andersen et al. 2017
;
Andersen & Perme 2010
)
. The procedure is straightforward for the estimation of
\hat{S}_{z}(t)
. First, calculate PVs for each individual
i
at a range of points in time
t
, defined as:

\hat{\theta}_{i}(t)=n\hat{\theta}(t)-(n-1)\hat{\theta}^{-i}(t),

(13)

where
\hat{\theta}
is a non-parametric estimator of the un-adjusted function of interest and
\hat{\theta}^{-1}
is that same estimator applied to the sample size of size
n
with the
i
-th observation removed. The simple Kaplan-Meier estimator can be used as
\hat{\theta}
if right-censoring is random, and an inverse probability of right-censoring weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator may be used if right-censoring depends on observed covariates
(
Overgaard et al. 2019
)
.

These PVs can be used directly to perform IPTW (
method="iptw_pseudo"
),

g-computation (
method="direct_pseudo"
) and AIPTW (
method="aiptw_pseudo"
). To get an IPTW estimate one can simply take a weighted average of the PVs stratified by the observed treatment status
Z
. The weights can be calculated exactly as described in section
4.4

(
Andersen et al. 2017
)
. Similarly, by using the PVs as response variable in a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) model, one can obtain estimates of the target estimand using g-computation as described in section
4.3

(
Klein et al. 2008
;
Overgaard et al. 2017
)
. Combining both approaches, AIPTW estimates can be calculated
(
Wang 2018
)
.

Similar to the AIPTW method, PV based methods may also produce estimates that are smaller than 0 or larger than 1 or are non-mononotonic. The same post-estimation correction methods may be used here
(
Denz et al. 2023
)
. Internally our package relies on the
prodlim
package
(
Gerds 2019
)
to obtain the necessary PV and on the
geepack
package
(
Halekoh et al. 2006
)
to calculate the GEE model where necessary.

4.9
Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Another recently proposed method is
Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation
(TMLE)
(
van der Laan & Rubin 2006
)
. Similar to AIPTW it utilized both the treatment assignment and the outcome mechanism to produce doubly-robust estimates of the counterfactual survival curve. The main difference is that AIPTW uses a closed form equation to estimate this quantity, while TMLE uses an iterative approach. In simplified terms, TMLE is a two-step process. First initial estimates for the target function are obtained using loss-based learning, usually with the help of an ensemble of machine learning algorithms in the SuperLearner framework
(
van der Laan et al. 2007
)
. This estimate is then fluctuated based on information obtained from the treatment mechanism, to reduce bias in the target parameter
(
Stitelman & van der Laan 2010
)
.

Multiple slightly different versions of this type of estimator have been proposed in the literature. Earlier versions were only applicable to discrete time-to-event outcomes
(
Moore & van der Laan 2009
;
Stitelman & van der Laan 2010
;
Díaz 2019
;
Cai & van der Laan 2020
)
, which made it hard to use these estimators in many situations. However, recently Rytgaard and her team have extended the TMLE estimator to the continuous case
(
Rytgaard & van der Laan 2024a
;
Rytgaard & van der Laan 2024
;
Rytgaard et al. 2023
)
. They additionally implemented their method in the
concrete

R
package
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
, which we used to construct an
adjustedCurves
compatible wrapper function for the method (
method="tmle"
). Note that although this method is a lot faster than the earlier versions, it is still very computationally expensive.

4.10
Instrumental Variable based Estimation

All previously mentioned methods assume that a sufficient adjustment set of confounders has been identified and can be adjusted for. In contrast, instrumental variable based methods allow the estimation of causal effects even in the presence of unmeasured confounders, under certain conditions
(
Greenland 2000
;
Lee et al. 2023
)
. An instrumental variable is a variable that fulfills three conditions: (1) it is independent of all unmeasured confounders, (2) it is associated with the treatment variable of interest and (3) it is not a direct cause of the outcome of interest
(
Greenland 2000
)
. One method to utilize this kind of variable to estimate causal effects is two-stage least squares regression
(
James & Singh 1978
)
. Roughly, a linear regression model is esimated, using the treatment of interest as the dependent variable and the instrumental variable as an independent variable. The predictions for the treatment of interest given the first step model and the observed values of the instrumental variable are then used in a second model as a substitute for the actual treatment status. See
(
James & Singh 1978
)
for more details.

Martínez-Camblor et al. 2020
have recently extended this method to estimate adjusted survival curves. Instead of a linear model, the propose the use of a Cox proportional hazards model with an individual frailty term in the second step. Through the use of the frailty term, they circumvent issues of non-collapsibility of the Cox model. For this reason they refer to their method as
two stage residual inclusion method with a frailty term
(2SRI-F). This method is available in the
adjustedCurves
package by using
method="iv_2SRIF"
. Other methods to estimate the survival curve through the use of instrumental variables have been proposed by
Lee et al. 2023
, but are currently not implemented in this package.

7
Discussion

In this paper we presented the
adjustedCurves

R
package for the estimation of confounder-adjusted survival curves and average treatment effects. Contrary to other available packages, our package offers wrappers and implementations of most available methods using one consistent framework. It has native support for the calculation of confidence intervals using both approximate equations (where available) and the computationally more expensive bootstrapping. It also directly supports multiple imputation to naturally handle missing data, which is an important concern when considering multiple covariates. As shown in the examples, the included
plot
functions can be used effectively to create publication ready graphical displays of the adjusted curves. Additionally, it can be used to directly compare groups using associated statistics such as differences in probabilities or the restricted mean survival time.

Although the need for confounder-adjustment when analyzing observational data is well known, adjustment-methods for survival curves are underutilized in practice. We hope that this package facilitates the usage of these methods by making it easier for researchers to apply them. The different types of survival curve based average treatment effects were implemented for the same reason. Multiple researchers have advocated for using these measures instead of the conditional hazard-ratio
(
Royston & Parmar 2013
;
Hasegawa et al. 2020
)
, because they are arguable easier to interpret and thus make communication of results easier. In contrast to the hazard-ratio, they also allow a causal interpretation of the results
(
Aalen et al. 2015
)
.

One limitation of this package is, that it currently only supports adjustment for baseline confounders. Time-varying confounding is a very common problem in practice as well, but there is only limited literature on the topic for the present context
(
Clare et al. 2019
)
and even less available software to build on. Another limitation is that our package currently only contains one method that can handle unmeasured confounding. Recently,
Ying et al. 2022
have proposed another promising method which allows counterfactual survival curves to be estimated in the presence of unmeasured confounders, under certain circumstances. Additionally, a few other methods based on covariate balancing have been proposed recently
(
Xue et al. 2024
;
García-Meixide & Matabuena 2023
;
Pham et al. 2023
)
. We aim to include implementations of these methods in a future version of this package.

Furthermore, the
adjustedCurves
package only allows supports categorical treatment variables. If the variable of interest is continuous, the user has to artificially categorize it before applying any of the methods included in the package. This is generally inadvisable
(
Naggara et al. 2011
)
. In related work, we propose a possible solution to this problem
(
Denz & Timmesfeld 2023
)
, which we implemented in the
contsurvplot

R
package
(
Denz 2023
)
.
