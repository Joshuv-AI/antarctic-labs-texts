Microscopic Green’s function approach for generalized Dirac Hamiltonians
Jeyson Támara-Isaza ID, Pablo Burset ID, William J. Herrera ID

Abstract
The rising interest on Dirac materials, condensed matter systems where low-energy electronic excitations are described by the relativistic Dirac Hamiltonian, entails a need for microscopic effective models to analytically describe their transport properties. Specifically, for the study of quantum transport these effective models must take into account the effect of atomic scale interfaces and the presence of well-defined edges, while reproducing the correct band structure. We develop a general method to analytically compute the microscopic Green’s function of Dirac materials valid for infinite, semi-infinite, and finite two-dimensional layers with zigzag or armchair edge orientations. We test our method computing the density of states and scattering probabilities of germanene and some transition metal dichalcogenides, obtaining simple analytical formulas. Our results provide a useful analytical tool for the interpretation of transport experiments on Dirac materials and could be extended to describe additional degrees of freedom like extra layers, superconductivity, etc.



I
Introduction

Two dimensional (2D) materials have become an excellent playground to engineer quantum devices with exotic properties
Tan et al. 2017
;
Varghese et al. 2015
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Schwierz et al. 2015
;
Akinwande et al. 2019
;
Fiori et al. 2014
. Examples include one- or few-atom thick materials like graphene, silicene, stanene, germanene, and
transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)

Acun et al. 2015
;
Kamal and Ezawa 2015
;
Chowdhury and Jana 2016
;
Manzeli et al. 2017
.
Despite their differences, many of these materials share low-energy characteristics like the presence of nodal points or a linear band dispersion, tunable carrier density, and high mobility due to suppressed backscattering
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2015
.
All these properties make them very promising candidates for quantum transport applications
Jariwala et al. 2014
;
Schaibley et al. 2016
;
Feng et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2017
;
Akinwande et al. 2019
;
Ahn 2020
.
This class of condensed matter systems is known as Dirac materials since, in the infrared regime, charge carriers follow a relativistic Dirac equation yielding the same universal behavior for, e.g., the optical conductivity or the specific heat
Wehling et al. 2014
;
Cayssol 2013
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2015
.
The origin of the Dirac-like behavior varies with the material, but in all cases some specific symmetries protect the formation of Dirac nodes in the spectrum
Wehling et al. 2014
.
For example, in the recently discovered quantum spin Hall insulator
König et al. 2007
;
Knez et al. 2011
, time-reversal symmetry promotes and protects the formation of Dirac-like metallic one-dimensional (1D) edge states on an otherwise 2D insulator. Similarly, three-dimensional (3D) topological insulators feature 2D edge states described by a single (or an odd number of) Dirac cones
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Kou et al. 2017
;
Xia et al. 2009
;
Hsieh et al. 2008
;
Chen et al. 2009
.

Dirac materials are characterized by their band structure
Acun et al. 2015
;
Kamal and Ezawa 2015
;
Chowdhury and Jana 2016
;
Manzeli et al. 2017
, and new materials can be predicted from numerical methods, like density functional theory
Bradlyn et al. 2017
.
Once identified, their microscopic properties are more easily accessed by numerical lattice calculations
Liu et al. 2011
;
Ezawa 2012a
;
Ezawa 2012b
;
Lewenkopf and Mucciolo 2013
;
Thorgilsson et al. 2014
;
Ezawa 2015
;
Gerivani and Milani Moghaddam 2022
, which can include finite-size effects and the presence of edges
Marmolejo-Tejada and Velasco-Medina 2016
;
Wang et al. 2021
. For the study of quantum transport, it is also important to include the effect of atomic scale interfaces, or the presence of well-defined edges in layers of 2D materials; for example, the electronic spectrum of a graphene nanoribbon strongly depends on the edge orientation
Brey and Fertig 2006
;
Wurm et al. 2011
;
Aidelsburger et al. 2018
.
In a complementary approach, low-energy, effective microscopic Hamiltonians offer a way to include all these effects (finite-size, edges and interfaces, etc.), while, at the same time, providing analytical results for the study of transport
Brey and Fertig 2006
.
In the absence of Coulomb interactions, a scattering theory can be derived from the Dirac Hamiltonian including interesting effects like edge orientation, spin-orbit coupling, magnetization, or even superconductivity
Manjarrés et al. 2009
;
Herrera et al. 2010
;
Burset et al. 2009
;
Gómez Páez et al. 2019
;
Casas et al. 2020
;
Andelkovic et al. 2023
;
Linard et al. 2023
.
A particularly interesting generalization of effective Hamiltonians are the
Green’s function (GF)
methods combined with Dyson’s equation
Herrera et al. 2010
;
Wurm et al. 2011
. The
GF
facilitates the study of correlations (interactions, superconductivity, etc) while naturally allowing the computation of observables like the electric current and the density of states.
The Hamiltonian approach to
GF
techniques is perfectly suited to consider edge and interface effects in 2D systems and, in many cases, provides simple, analytical results
Herrera et al. 2010
;
Lu and Tanaka 2018
;
Burset et al. 2008
;
Casas et al. 2020
.

In this work, we develop a systematic and general method to analytically compute the microscopic
GF
of systems with a general Dirac Hamiltonian and apply this method to 2D honeycomb structure. The resulting
GF
accounts for the presence of well-defined edges or interfaces at the atomic scale. We then obtain the
GF
of relevant examples of Dirac materials, like germanene and
TMDs
, and obtain transport properties (density of states and scattering probabilities) of infinite, semi-infinite, and finite layers.
Our general method can distinguish specific edge orientations like zigzag, which only involves one Dirac node or valley, and armchair that combines two valleys. In all cases, our method provides simple analytical formulas.

. Finally, we present some concluding remarks in

II
Generalized Dirac Hamiltonian

We consider the following general Dirac Hamiltonian, acting on a two-dimensional space

\check{H}(x,y)=-i\hbar v_{F}(\check{\alpha}_{x}\partial_{x}+\check{\alpha}_{y}\partial_{y})+\check{V}(x,y),

(1)

with
v_{F}
the Fermi velocity and
\check{V}(x,y)
an electrostatic potential.
Here,
\check{\alpha}_{x,y}
are
2N\times 2N
matrices acting on the
SU(2)
degree of freedom that defines the Dirac Hamiltonian and the
N
-dimensional space containing the rest of degrees of freedom.
In the following, we assume translation invariance along the
y
-axis, with
k_{y}\equiv q
a conserved quantity, and consider inhomogeneous systems along the
x
direction. Then,
Eq.

1
reduces to
\check{H}_{q}=-i\hbar\mathbf{\partial}_{x}\check{\alpha}_{x}+q\check{\alpha}_{y}+\check{V}(x)
, which obeys the generalized Dirac equation

\check{H}_{q}\psi_{q}\left(x,y\right)=E_{q}\psi_{q}\left(x,y\right),

(2)

with solutions of the form

\psi_{q}\left(x,y\right)=\mathrm{e}^{iqy}\left[\psi_{n}^{+}\mathrm{e}^{ik_{n}^{+}x}+\psi_{n}^{-}\mathrm{e}^{ik_{n}^{-}x}\right],

(3)

where
\psi_{n}=(\psi_{n}^{+},\psi_{n}^{-})^{T}
are
2N
-dimensional spinors in the
SU(2)
space spawned by
\check{\alpha}_{x}
, with
n
labeling the other quantum numbers.
The states
\psi_{n}^{\pm}\mathrm{e}^{ik_{n}^{\pm}x}
, where, usually,
k_{n}^{-}=-k_{n}^{+}
, represent right (
\psi_{n}^{+}
) and left (
\psi_{n}^{-}
) moving solutions along the
x
direction, with probability flux current given by
J_{n,x}^{\pm}=v_{F}\psi_{n}^{\pm\dagger}\check{\alpha}_{x}\psi_{n}^{\pm}=\pm v_{F}
.
These states are not, however, orthogonal. To obtain an orthogonality relation, we must define the states

\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{\pm}=\pm\check{\alpha}_{x}^{\dagger}\psi_{n}^{\pm},\quad(\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{\pm})^{\dagger}=\pm(\psi_{n}^{\pm})^{\dagger}\check{\alpha}_{x},

(4)

which fulfill

(\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{\varepsilon})^{\dagger}\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\delta_{nm}\delta_{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}},

(5)

with
\varepsilon=+,-
. Combining
\psi_{n}^{\pm}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{n}^{\pm}
states we then find the completeness relation

\sum\limits_{\varepsilon,n}\psi^{\varepsilon}_{n}(\tilde{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{n})^{\dagger}=\check{1},

(6)

with
\check{1}
being the
2N\times 2N
unit matrix.

We can now define the
GF
associated to the Dirac Hamiltonian in
Eq.

2
as

\left(E\check{1}-\check{H}_{q}\right)\check{G}_{q}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=E\delta\left(x-x^{\prime}\right)\check{1}.

(7)

When
k_{n}^{-}=-k_{n}^{+}=-k_{n}
, with
k_{n}\geq 0
, the Green’s functions of the unbounded (bulk) system become

\displaystyle\check{g}^{<}\left(x<x^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)={}

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{n}f_{n}^{-}\left(x-x^{\prime}\right)\psi^{-}_{n}(\bar{\psi}_{n}^{-})^{T},

(8a)

\displaystyle\check{g}^{>}\left(x>x^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)={}

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{n}f_{n}^{+}\left(x-x^{\prime}\right)\psi^{+}_{n}(\bar{\psi}^{+}_{n})^{T}.

(8b)

Here, the
x
-dependence is encoded in the functions
f^{\varepsilon}_{n}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{i\varepsilon k_{n}x}
, and the states
\bar{\psi}^{\varepsilon}_{n}
are solutions to the transposed Hamiltonian in
Eq.

1
. Transposing a Dirac Hamiltonian results in the exchange
\mathbf{k}\rightarrow-\mathbf{k}
, so the transposed states can be related to the left and right moving states in
Eq.

3
as

\bar{\psi}_{n}^{+T}=\left(\check{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{-}\right)^{T},\quad\bar{\psi}_{n}^{-T}=\left(\check{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{+}\right)^{T},

(9)

with
\check{\gamma}
a matrix such that the scalar product
\psi^{\dagger}\check{\gamma}\psi
is invariant under Lorentz transformations and spatial inversion
Herrera et al. 2010
.

III
Semi-infinite systems

We can define a sharp edge localized at
x=x_{a}
by means of the perturbation potential
\check{V}\left(x\right)=U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\delta\left(x_{a}-x\right)
, with
U_{a}
the potential strength that takes the limit
U_{a}\rightarrow\infty
when we consider the edge of a semi-infinite layer. Here,
\check{\tau}_{a}
is a matrix that encodes the specific boundary conditions at the edge.
The
GF
perturbed by this potential is given by Dyson’s equation as

\displaystyle\check{G}_{a}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)={}

\displaystyle\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\int\mathrm{d}x_{1}\check{g}\left(x,x_{1}\right)\check{V}\left(x_{1}\right)\check{G}_{a}\left(x_{1},x^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{g}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\check{G}_{a}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right).

(10)

The solution of this equation when both
x
and
x^{\prime}
are on the right (left) of
x_{a}
takes the form

\check{G}_{a}^{RR(LL)}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{Q}^{>(<)}\left(x\right)\check{g}^{<(>)}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right),

(11)

with the superindex RR (LL) indicating that
x,x^{\prime}>x_{a}
(
x,x^{\prime}<x_{a}
), and where we have defined

\displaystyle\check{Q}^{>(<)}\left(x\right)={}

\displaystyle\check{g}^{>(<)}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}D^{>(<)},

(12)

\displaystyle\check{D}_{a}^{>(<)}={}

\displaystyle\left(\check{1}-\check{g}^{>(<)}\left(x_{a},x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\right)^{-1}.

(13)

An iterative method allows us to find an expression for
Eq.

12
as (see
Appendix

A
for details)

\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)=U_{a}\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)\check{\tau}_{a}\sum\limits_{p=0}^{\infty}\left(U_{a}\check{g}^{>}\left(x_{a},x_{a}\right)\check{\tau}_{a}\right)^{p}.

(14)

Using the completeness relation,
Eq.

6
, we find

\displaystyle\check{g}^{>}\left(x_{a},x_{a}\right)\check{\tau}_{a}={}

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{n}\psi_{n}^{+}(\bar{\psi}_{n}^{+})^{T}\check{\tau}_{a}\sum\limits_{m,\varepsilon}\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon}\tau_{a,nm}^{+\varepsilon}\check{P}_{nm}^{+\varepsilon},

(15)

where we have defined the projector operators

\check{P}_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\psi_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{\dagger},

(16)

and the matrix representation of
\check{\tau}_{a}
as

\tau_{a,nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}=(\bar{\psi}_{n}^{\varepsilon})^{T}\check{\tau}_{a}\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}.

(17)

In what follows, it is more insightful to write explicitly the subspace spawned by the left and right moving solutions of
Eq.

2
, that is, the
N\times N
matrices
\hat{\tau}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, so that

\check{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\tau}^{++}&\hat{\tau}^{+-}\\
\hat{\tau}^{-+}&\hat{\tau}^{--}\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

Henceforth, we use the symbol
\hat{\dots}
to denote the
2\times 2
matrices in the left and right mover space.
As a result,
Eq.

12
becomes

\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)=\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon}f^{+}_{n}\left(x-x_{a}\right)(\hat{r}_{a}^{+\varepsilon})_{nm}\check{P}_{nm}^{+\varepsilon},

(19)

with
\hat{r}_{a}^{+\varepsilon}=\hat{D}_{a}^{+}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{+\varepsilon}
, and

\hat{D}_{a}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{-iU_{a}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\left(1+\frac{iU_{a}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}.

(20)

Analogously,

\check{Q}^{<}\left(x\right)=\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon}f^{-}_{n}\left(x-x_{a}\right)(\hat{r}_{a}^{-\varepsilon})_{nm}\check{P}_{nm}^{-\varepsilon},

(21)

with
\hat{r}^{-\varepsilon}_{a}=\hat{D}_{a}^{-}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{-\varepsilon}
.

Consequently, the local
GFs
at each side of the perturbation adopt the form

\displaystyle\check{G}_{a}^{RR}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{nm}f_{n}^{+}(x-x_{a})(\hat{r}^{+-}_{a})_{nm}f^{-}_{m}(x_{a}-x^{\prime})\psi_{n}^{+}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{-})^{T},

and

\displaystyle\check{G}_{a}^{LL}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{nm}f_{n}^{-}(x-x_{a})(\hat{r}^{-+}_{a})_{nm}f^{+}_{m}(x_{a}-x^{\prime})\psi_{n}^{-}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{+})^{T}.

Here,
\check{r}_{a}
corresponds to the scattering matrix of reflection amplitudes. For a potential acting on
x_{a}
, we can define the transmission amplitudes as
\hat{t}_{a}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon}=\hat{1}+\hat{r}_{a}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon}
, and the scattering matrix results in

\check{S}_{a}=\check{1}+\check{r}_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{t}^{++}_{a}&\hat{r}^{+-}_{a}\\
\hat{r}^{-+}_{a}&\hat{t}^{--}_{a}\end{pmatrix},

(24)

which fulfills the unitarity condition
\check{S}_{a}\check{S}_{a}^{\dagger}=\check{S}_{a}^{\dagger}\check{S}_{a}=\check{1}
; for more details we refer the reader to
Appendix

B
.

The semi-infinite system requires a hard edge at
x=x_{a}
, which we obtain taking the limit
U_{a}\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently, the denominators in
Eq.

20
become
\hat{D}_{a}^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow-(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon})^{-1}
. The scattering matrix for the semi-infinite system thus reduces to

\check{S}_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}0&(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{{}^{++}})^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{{}^{+-}}\\
(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{{}^{--}})^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{{}^{-+}}&0\end{pmatrix},

(25)

where the transmission amplitudes are zero at the edge. As a result, the matrix of reflection amplitudes is unitary,
\left(\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\right)^{\dagger}=\left(\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\right)^{-1}
, and, therefore,
[(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{{}^{++}})^{-1}]^{\dagger}(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{+-})^{\dagger}=(\hat{\tau}_{a}^{+-})^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{a}^{++}
.

IV
Nanoribbon

We now extend the previous results to include a second edge potential barrier as
\check{V}(x)=\sum_{j=a}^{b}U_{j}\check{\tau}_{j}\delta(x_{j}-x)
. We approach this problem sequentially by first considering only one edge potential. For example, by taking
U_{b}=0
, we find, from
Eq.

10
,

\check{G}_{a}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{g}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\check{G}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right),

(26)

Similarly, by taking
U_{a}=0
and
U_{b}\neq 0
, we obtain
\check{G}_{b}(x,x^{\prime})
. Restricting ourselves to the region where
x_{a}\leq x,x^{\prime}\leq x_{b}
, the
GF
adopts the form given in
Eq.

22
for barrier potential
U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}
, while it follows
Eq.

23
for barrier potential
U_{b}\check{\tau}_{b}
.
We apply again Dyson’s equation to
Eq.

26
to introduce the edge potential
U_{b}
and reach

\displaystyle\check{G}_{ab}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)={}

\displaystyle\check{G}_{a}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{G}_{a}^{<}\left(x,x_{b}\right)U_{b}\check{\tau}_{b}

(27)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(1-\check{G}_{a}^{<}\left(x_{b},x_{b}\right)U_{b}\check{\tau}_{b}\right)^{-1}\check{G}_{a}^{>}\left(x_{b},x^{\prime}\right),

with
\check{G}^{\lessgtr}=\check{G}(x\lessgtr x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
, cf.
Eq.

22
.
The solution to
Eq.

27
reads as

\check{G}_{ab}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{G_{a}}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{Q}^{<}\left(x\right)\check{G}_{a}^{>}\left(x_{b},x^{\prime}\right),

(28)

with

\check{Q}^{<}\left(x\right)=\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{n}^{\varepsilon}\left(x-x_{b}\right)(\hat{r}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}_{b})_{nm}\check{P}_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}},

(29)

and

\check{r}_{b}=-i\frac{U_{b}\check{\tau}_{b}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\left(\check{1}+i\frac{U_{b}\check{\tau}_{b}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\right)^{-1}.

(30)

Using the explicit expression for the semi-infinite
GF
,
Eq.

22
, we write the
GF
with
x>x^{\prime}
for the central region as

\displaystyle\check{G}_{ab}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}

(31)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{n}^{\varepsilon}(x-x_{b})(\check{w}^{>})_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x_{b}-x^{\prime})\psi_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{T},

where we have defined the matrices

\check{w}^{>}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{1}+\hat{w}_{b}^{++}&\left(\hat{1}+\hat{w}_{b}^{++}\right)\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\\
\hat{w}_{b}^{-+}&\hat{w}_{b}^{-+}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\end{pmatrix},

(32)

with
W=x_{b}-x_{a}>0
the width of the finite region.
Here,
\hat{w}_{b}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}
are the submatrix elements of

\check{w}_{b}=-i\frac{U_{b}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\check{\tau}\left(\check{1}+i\frac{U_{b}}{2\hbar v_{F}}\check{\tau}\right)^{-1},

(33)

with

\check{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}&\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{--}\\
\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}&\hat{\tau}_{b}^{--}\end{pmatrix},

(34)

and
\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}(W)
is defined from the reflection matrix at the left interface,
\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}
, as
\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}(W)=\hat{f}^{++}(-W)\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\hat{f}^{--}(W)
, with the diagonal matrices
(\hat{f}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon})_{nm}(x)=\delta_{nm}f_{m}^{\varepsilon}(x)
.
Analogously, we can also define

\check{w}^{<}=\begin{pmatrix}\ \hat{w}_{b}^{++}&\left(\hat{1}+\hat{w}_{b}^{++}\right)\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\\
\hat{w}_{b}^{-+}&\hat{1}+\hat{w}_{b}^{-+}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\end{pmatrix},

(35)

where we have used the projector operators,
Eq.

16
. This result is equivalent to the reflection matrix for a single barrier at
x=x_{b}
,
\hat{r}^{++}
, changing
\check{w}_{b}
by
\hat{\tau}^{++}
.

To obtain the
GF
of a nanoribbon we must take the limits
U_{a,b}\rightarrow\infty
, obtaining

\check{G}_{ab}^{\lessgtr}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,m\\
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f_{n}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right)(\check{w}^{\lessgtr})_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(-x^{\prime}\right)\psi_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{T},

(36)

where
Eqs.

32
and
35
reduce to

\displaystyle\check{w}^{>}={}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{D}^{++}&\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\\
\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{D}^{++}&\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(37a)

\displaystyle\check{w}^{<}={}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)&\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\\
\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{D}^{++}&\hat{D}^{--}\end{pmatrix},

(37b)

with

\displaystyle\hat{r}^{+-}_{a}(x_{a})={}

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{++}(-x_{a})\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\hat{f}^{--}(x_{a}),

(38a)

\displaystyle\hat{r}^{-+}_{b}(x_{b})={}

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{--}(x_{b})\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\hat{f}^{++}(-x_{b}),

(38b)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{++}={}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{1}-\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\right]^{-1},

(38c)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{--}={}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{1}-\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\right]^{-1}.

(38d)

The later equations allow us to obtain the bound states of the finite region by taking the condition
\hat{D}^{\pm\pm}=0
, or, analogously,
\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}(x_{a})\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}(x_{b})=\hat{1}
. Consequently, the bound states of the finite region are tied to the reflection matrices at each independent edge.

V
Dirac system with zigzag edges

Having established the general method for the computation of the
GF
of a Dirac system, we now present some examples showcasing specific edge orientations.
We start with layers ending in zigzag edges, see
Fig.

1
, and modify the Hamiltonian of an infinite system,
Eq.

1
, so that it describes the low-energy physics of graphene-like materials like germanene, silicene, and
TMDs
.
We thus get the Hamiltonian

\check{H}_{s\eta}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)=\mu_{s\eta}\hat{\sigma}_{0}+\hbar v_{F}\left(k\hat{\sigma}_{x}+\eta q\hat{\sigma}_{y}\right)+m_{s\eta}\hat{\sigma}_{z},

(39)

with

\displaystyle\mu_{s\eta}={}

\displaystyle-E_{F}+\eta s\lambda_{SO},

(40)

\displaystyle m_{s\eta}={}

\displaystyle\lambda_{z}-\eta s\lambda_{SO}+s\lambda_{AF}+\frac{\Delta}{2}.

(41)

Here, the Pauli matrices
\hat{\sigma}_{0,x,y,z}
act in sublattice space denoted by A- and B-type atoms,
\psi=(\psi_{A},\psi_{B})^{T}
, and the parameters
\eta=\pm
and
s=\pm
are the valley index and the spin
S_{z}
component, respectively. The Fermi velocity is written in terms of the lattice constant
a
and the hopping parameter
t
as
v_{F}=\sqrt{3}at/(2\hbar)
.
The parameter
m_{s\eta}
describes a generic mass term given by the intrinsic gap
\Delta
, and extra terms that depend on the specific material.
For example,
\lambda_{SO}
represents the spin-orbit coupling,
\lambda_{AF}
a magnetic gap, and
\lambda_{z}
the energy due to an external electrical field perpendicular to the monolayer.
Indeed,
\lambda_{SO}
is strong in systems such as germanene, silicene, and
TMDs
, but negligible in graphene. A magnetic material in close contact to the monolayer can induce a magnetization
\lambda_{AF}
by proximity effect.
Previous works have analyzed the effect on the electronic state of the system caused by an electric field applied perpendicularly to monolayer of
TMDs
, germanene, or silicene
Qian et al. 2014
;
Ezawa 2015
;
Xiao et al. 2012
.
Here, we represent this effect by the parameter
\lambda_{z}
.
Finally, the doping level
\mu_{s\eta}
is determined by the Fermi energy
E_{F}
and, if present, the spin-orbit coupling
\lambda_{SO}
.

The eigenstates of
Eq.

39
can be found in
Appendix

D
, with transposed states defined using
Eq.

9
and
\check{\gamma}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}
.
With these states, the

of the bulk system is given by
Eq.

8

The resulting wavevectors are
k_{s\eta}^{\pm}=\pm k_{s\eta}
, where

k_{s\eta}=\sqrt{(E-\mu_{s\eta})^{2}-m_{s\eta}^{2}-(\hbar v_{F}q)^{2}}/(\hbar v_{F}).

(42)

For a given excitation energy
E

\hbar q
can be parameterized by the angle
\alpha_{s\eta}
defined as

\mathrm{e}^{\pm i\alpha_{s\eta}}=\hbar v_{F}\frac{k_{s\eta}\pm iq}{\sqrt{\left(\mu_{s\eta}-E\right)^{2}-m_{s\eta}^{2}}}.

(43)

We can now define the spectral density of states from the retarded

as

\rho(E,q,x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}\left\{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\check{G}\left(E;q;x,x\right)\right]\right\},

(44)

and the

\rho_{T}(E,x)=\int\rho\left(E;q;x\right)\mathrm{d}q.

(45)

The bulk spectral density for a given spin-valley configuration adopts the simple form
\rho_{T,s\eta}=1/\left(\pi\hbar v_{F}k_{s\eta}\right)
.
Henceforth, we normalize the spectral density of states by
\rho_{0}=ta
, which is equivalent to measuring energies and distances in units of the hopping
t
and the lattice constant
a

VIII
Conclusions

We have developed a general and analytic method for computing the microscopic Green’s functions of a two-dimensional Dirac Hamiltonian with atomic-scale boundary conditions. Our approach is thus valid for honeycomb lattices with atomically well-defined zigzag and armchair edges, and contains all the relevant information about the scattering processes that take place at these edges, including spin, sublattice (from the honeycomb structure), and valley degrees of freedom.

We tested our Green’s function formalism by computing the spectral and topological properties of several well-known Dirac materials, like semimetal germanene and semiconducting transition metal dichalcogenides. Our approach allowed us to characterize bulk (infinite two-dimensional layer), semi-infinite, and finite layers. The zigzag termination resulted in the emergence of localized edge states, which have a topological nature in germanene samples
Ezawa 2015
, and are affected, in nanoribbons (finite layers), by the spectral quantization resulting from the finite ribbon length.

We go beyond previous works
Ezawa 2012b
showing that the topological properties of germanene are maintained for armchair terminations. We also showed how the topological gap for this material can be manipulated by an external electric field, which could be helpful for field effect transistor using germanene or silicene. This effect in silicene has been recently measured
Tao et al. 2015
, and its potential use as a topological field effect transistor has also been theoretically explored
Ezawa 2015
.

The analytical formalism presented here aligns with current efforts to explore and engineer novel two-dimensional junctions with interesting quantum applications
Roy et al. 2014
, as it paves the way for the exploration of exotic electronic phases in nanoscale junctions based on Dirac materials, including important effects like edge terminations, valley-dependent scattering, and finite size effects.
In general, our approach provides an analytical alternative, with low computational cost, to simulate the low-energy electronic properties of graphene-like materials at the atomic level. It is straightforward to generalize it to study quantum transport in junctions with atomic-scale electric contacts
Guimarães et al. 2016
;
Léonard and Talin 2011
;
Allain et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2013
, or to include magnetic or superconducting orders
Milošević and Mandrus 2021

Appendix A
Iterative method

In this section, we obtain
\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)
given in
Eq.

14
following an iterative method. First, we start using
Eq.

12
with
x<x^{\prime}
in the definition of the
GF
,

\check{G}_{a}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\check{G}_{RR}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right).

(58)

Here,
\check{G}_{RR}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right)
is given by Dyson’s equation,
Eq.

11
, as

\check{G}_{RR}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right)=\left[1+\check{Q}^{>}\left(x_{a}\right)\right]\check{g}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right).

(59)

Substituting into
Eq.

58
we get

\displaystyle\check{G}_{RR}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=

\displaystyle\check{g}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle\times U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\left(1+\check{Q}^{>}\left(x_{a}\right)\right)\check{g}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right).

Comparing
Eq.

60
with
Eq.

11
, namely,

\check{G}_{a}^{RR,<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{g}^{<}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)\check{g}^{<}\left(x_{a},x^{\prime}\right),

we obtain the following equation for
\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)
,

\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)=\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}+\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)U_{a}\check{\tau}_{a}\check{Q}^{>}\left(x_{a}\right).

(61)

We then solve
Eq.

61
recursively to find

\check{Q}^{>}\left(x\right)=U_{a}\check{g}^{>}\left(x,x_{a}\right)\check{\tau}_{a}\sum\limits_{p=0}U_{a}^{p}\left(\check{g}^{>}\left(0\right)\check{\tau}_{a}\right)^{p}.

(62)

Appendix B
Scattering matrix

We now derive the expression and general properties of the scattering matrix in
Eq.

24
.
To define the scattering problem, we consider a potential barrier at
x=x_{a}
. Incoming states from the left (right) of the barrier are
\psi_{m}^{+}\left(x\right)
with amplitudes
a_{m}^{+}
(
\psi_{m}^{-}\left(x\right)
with
a_{m}^{-}
), which are solutions of
Eq.

2
. In the absence of a potential barrier, a general unperturbed scattering state reads

\psi_{0}\left(x\right)=\sum\limits_{m,\varepsilon}a_{m}^{\varepsilon}\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right).

(63)

In the presence of the barrier potential, the perturbed state to the left or right of the barrier is obtained using Dyson’s equation as

\psi_{L(R)}\left(x\right)=\psi_{0}\left(x\right)+\check{Q}^{<(>)}\left(x\right)\psi_{0}\left(x_{a}\right),

(64)

with
\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right)=\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon}f_{m}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right)
and
f^{\varepsilon}_{m}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{i\varepsilon k_{m}x}
.

Owing to the translational invariance along the
x
-direction, we proceed taking
x_{a}=0
without loss of generality.
For simplicity, we also define
\psi_{R}\left(0\right)=\psi_{R}
and
\psi_{0}\left(0\right)=\psi_{0}
and obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{R}={}

\displaystyle\left[1+\check{Q}^{>}\left(0\right)\right]\psi_{0}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(1+\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon}\left(\hat{r}_{a}^{+\varepsilon}\right)_{nm}\hat{P}_{nm}^{+\varepsilon})\sum\limits_{m^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}}a_{m^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\psi_{m^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m,\varepsilon}a_{m}^{\varepsilon}\psi_{m}^{\varepsilon}+\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon}\left(\hat{r}_{a}^{+\varepsilon}\right)_{nm}a_{m}^{\varepsilon}\psi_{m}^{+},

(65)

where we have used that
\hat{P}_{nm}^{+\varepsilon}\psi_{m^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}=\delta_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\psi_{m^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.
Summing over
\varepsilon
we get

\psi_{R}=\sum\limits_{m}a_{m}^{-}\psi_{m}^{-}+\sum\limits_{n}b_{n}^{+}\psi_{n}^{+},

(66)

with
b_{n}^{+}
being the outgoing scattering amplitudes,

b_{n}^{+}=\sum\limits_{m}\left(\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\right)_{nm}a_{m}^{-}+\sum\limits_{m}\left(\hat{t}_{a}^{++}\right)_{nm}a_{m}^{+}.

(67)

Here, we have defined the transmission amplitudes as the elements of the following matrix

\hat{t}_{a}^{++}=\hat{1}+\hat{r}_{a}^{++},

(68)

which allows us to rewrite
Eq.

67
as

\mathbf{b}^{+}=\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\mathbf{a}^{-}+\hat{t}_{a}^{++}\mathbf{a}^{+},

(69)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{+T}={}

\displaystyle\left(b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{N}\right)^{T},

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{+T}={}

\displaystyle\left(a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{N}\right)^{T}.

(71)

The perturbed scattering state to the left of the barrier is obtained analogously as

\psi_{L}\left(0\right)=\sum\limits_{m}a_{m}^{+}\psi_{m}^{+}+\sum\limits_{n}b_{n}^{-}\psi_{n}^{-},

(72)

with

\mathbf{b}^{-}=\hat{r}_{a}^{-+}\mathbf{a}^{+}+\hat{t}_{a}^{--}\mathbf{a}^{-},

(73)

and

\hat{t}_{a}^{--}=\hat{1}+\hat{r}_{a}^{--}.

(74)

Combining the previous results, we define the scattering matrix
\hat{S}
as

\mathbf{b}=\check{S}\mathbf{a},

(75)

with

\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{b}^{+}\\
\mathbf{b}^{-}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{+}\\
\mathbf{a}^{-}\end{pmatrix}.

(76)

Note that in the usual definition of the scattering matrix the reflection amplitudes are in the diagonal, which results from taking
\mathbf{b}^{T}=(\mathbf{b}^{-T},\mathbf{b}^{+T})
.
In what follows, however, we use

\displaystyle\check{S}_{a}={}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{t}_{a}^{++}&\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\\
\hat{r}_{a}^{-+}&\hat{t}_{a}^{--}\end{pmatrix}

(77)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{1}+\hat{r}_{a}^{++}&\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\\
\hat{r}_{a}^{-+}&\hat{1}+\hat{r}_{a}^{--}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\check{1}+\check{r}_{a}.

We can now set the potential barrier at an arbitrary position
x_{a}\neq 0
changing the scattering matrix as

\check{S}\left(x_{a}\right)=\check{f}\left(-x_{a}\right)\check{S}\check{f}\left(x_{a}\right),

(78)

with

\check{f}\left(x_{a}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\check{f}^{++}\left(x_{a}\right)&0\\
0&\check{f}^{--}\left(x_{a}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(79)

and

\left(\hat{f}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon}\right)_{nm}\left(x\right)=\delta_{nm}\hat{f}_{m}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right).

(80)

Here,
\hat{S}\left(x_{a}\right)
and
\hat{S}
are related by an unitary transformation since
\hat{f}\left(-x_{0}\right)\hat{f}\left(x_{0}\right)=\hat{1}
.

As it is usually interpreted,
\mathbf{b}
represents the outgoing flux and
\mathbf{a}
the incoming one. Consequently, the probability flux to the right and left of the barrier reads

\displaystyle J_{R}={}

\displaystyle v_{F}\sum\limits_{n}\left(a_{n}^{-\ast}\psi_{n}^{-\dagger}+b_{n}^{+\ast}\psi_{n}^{+\dagger}\right)\hat{\alpha}_{x}\sum\limits_{n}\left(a_{n}^{-}\psi_{n}^{-}+b_{n}^{+}\psi_{n}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle v_{F}\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|b_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}-\left|a_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right),

(81)

\displaystyle J_{L}={}

\displaystyle v_{F}\sum\limits_{n}\left(a_{n}^{+\ast}\psi_{n}^{+\dagger}+a_{n}^{-\ast}\psi_{n}^{-\dagger}\right)\hat{\alpha}_{x}\sum\limits_{n}\left(a_{n}^{+}\psi_{n}^{+}+a_{n}^{-}\psi_{n}^{-}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle v_{F}\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|a_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}-\left|b_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right).

(82)

Conservation of the probability flux requires that
J_{L}=J_{R}
; therefore,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|a_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}-\left|b_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|b_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}-\left|a_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right),

(83)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|a_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}+\left|a_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n}\left(\left|b_{n}^{+}\right|^{2}+\left|b_{n}^{-}\right|^{2}\right),

(84)

which we can recast in vector form as
|\mathbf{b|}^{2}=|\mathbf{a|}^{2}
.
As a result, we find that

\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\check{S}^{\dagger}\check{S}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{=}|\mathbf{a|}^{2},

(85)

and thus,

\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\left(\check{S}^{\dagger}\check{S}-1\right)\mathbf{a}=0.

(86)

Equation

86
proves that the scattering matrix is unitary, that is,

\check{S}^{\dagger}\check{S}=\check{S}\check{S}^{\dagger}=\check{1},

(87)

which we can recast using the reflection matrix
\check{r}
as

\left(1+\check{r}_{a}\right)\left(1+\check{r}_{a}^{\dagger}\right)=\left(1+\check{r}_{a}^{\dagger}\right)\left(1+\check{r}_{a}\right)=\check{1}.

(88)

Appendix C
Nanoribbon Green’s function and bound states

In this section we provide more details on the derivation of the nanoribbon
GF
,
Eq.

36
, and the associated bound states.
For the case of two potential barriers placed at
x=x_{a}
and
x=x_{b}>x_{a}
, the perturbed
GF
for the region between the barriers is

\check{G}_{ab}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\check{G}_{a}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)+\check{Q}^{<}\left(x\right)\check{G}_{ab}^{>}\left(x_{b},x^{\prime}\right),

(89)

for
x>x^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\check{G}_{ab}^{>}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}

(90)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{n,m,\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{n}^{\varepsilon}(x-x_{b})(\check{w}^{>})_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x_{b}-x^{\prime})\psi_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{T},

with
\check{w}^{>}
given by
Eq.

32
, see also
Eq.

31
.

Equation

89
can be solved after obtaining
\check{G}_{ab}^{>}
. However, to do so, one can not take the limit
U_{b}\rightarrow\infty
and invert
\check{w}
, because the matrix
\check{\tau}
has no inverse (
\mathrm{det}[\check{\tau}]=0
).
To circumvent this problem, we define the matrix

\check{N}=\hat{1}-AU_{b}\check{\tau},

(91)

with
A=-i/(2\hbar v_{F})
, and

\check{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{N}_{1}&\hat{N}_{2}\\
\hat{N}_{3}&\hat{N}_{4}\end{pmatrix},

(92)

where

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{1}={}

\displaystyle\hat{1}-AU_{b}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+},\,

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{2}={}

\displaystyle-AU_{b}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{--},

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{3}={}

\displaystyle-AU_{b}\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+},\,

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{4}={}

\displaystyle AU_{b}\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}\left(\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\right)^{-1}.

We can now compute the inverse of
\hat{N}
and then take the limit
U_{b}\rightarrow\infty
. To do so, we use Schur complement, which, for example, is defined for submatrix
\hat{N}_{4}
as

\hat{C}=\hat{N}_{1}-\hat{N}_{2}\hat{N}_{4}{}^{-1}\hat{N}_{3}.

Therefore, defining

\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}=AU_{b}\left(1-AU_{b}^{--}\hat{\tau}_{b}\right)^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{b}^{{}^{-+}},

we get

\hat{C}=\hat{1}-\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+},

(93)

with
W=x_{b}-x_{a}
.

The inverse matrix of
\hat{N}
is thus

\check{N}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{1}&0\\
-\hat{N}_{4}^{-1}\hat{N}_{3}&\hat{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{C}^{-1}&0\\
0&\hat{N}_{4}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{1}&-\hat{N}_{2}\hat{N}_{4}^{-1}\\
0&\hat{1}\end{pmatrix},

(94)

which simplifies to

\check{N}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{C}^{-1}&\hat{C}^{-1}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{--}\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}\right)^{-1}\\
\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\hat{C}^{-1}&U_{b}^{-1}\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\hat{C}^{-1}\left(\hat{1}-U_{b}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}\right)\left(\hat{\tau}_{b}^{-+}\right)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(95)

We can now take the limit
U_{b}\rightarrow\infty
, resulting in

\check{w}^{<}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{C}^{-1}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}&-\hat{C}^{-1}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(W\right)\\
\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\hat{C}^{-1}&-\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\hat{C}^{-1}\left(\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\right)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(96)

Analogously, we get

\check{w}^{>}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{D}^{++}&\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\\
\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{D}^{++}&\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{D}^{++}\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(97)

with

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{++}={}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{1}-\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\right]^{-1},

(98)

\displaystyle\hat{D}^{--}={}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{1}-\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\right]^{-1}.

(99)

As a result, we obtain the final form of the nanoribbon
GF
as

\check{G}_{ab}^{\lessgtr}\left(x,x^{\prime}\right)=\frac{-i}{2\hbar v_{F}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n,m\\
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\end{subarray}}f_{n}^{\varepsilon}\left(x\right)(\check{w}^{\lessgtr})_{nm}^{\varepsilon\varepsilon^{\prime}}f_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(-x^{\prime}\right)\psi_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\bar{\psi}_{m}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{T}.

(100)

The nanoribbon’s bound states are obtained from the denominator of the
GF
, that is, setting the inverse of
\hat{D}^{++}
or, equivalently,
\hat{D}^{--}
, to zero.
From
Eq.

98
, this condition reduces to

\hat{r}_{a}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}_{b}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)=\hat{1}.

(101)

We can interpret this condition as follows: inside the nanoribbon, the scattering state is a superposition of left and right movers, namely,

\psi=\sum\limits_{n}\left(c_{n}^{+}\psi_{n}^{+}+c_{n}^{-}\psi_{n}^{-}\right).

(102)

At each potential barrier,
x=x_{a,b}
, the amplitudes for left and right movers,
c_{n}^{-}
and
c_{n}^{+}
, respectively, are related by

\displaystyle c_{n}^{-}={}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m}\hat{r}_{nm}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)c_{m}^{+},

(103)

\displaystyle c_{n}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m}\hat{r}_{nm}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)c_{m}^{-}.

(104)

In matrix form we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{-}={}

\displaystyle\hat{r}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\mathbf{c}^{+},

(105)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{+}={}

\displaystyle\hat{r}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\mathbf{c}^{-},

(106)

which form the closed cycle

\mathbf{c}^{+}=\hat{r}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\mathbf{c}^{+}.

(107)

It is thus straightforward to get

\mathbf{c}^{+}\left(\hat{1}-\hat{r}^{+-}\left(x_{a}\right)\hat{r}^{-+}\left(x_{b}\right)\right)=\mathbf{0},

(108)

which corresponds to the zeroes of the
GF
, as shown in
Eq.

101
.
This result shows that the nanoribbon’s bound states are determined by the reflection matrices at each edge of the finite region, and can thus be obtained by the zeroes of the inverse of
Eq.

98
.
