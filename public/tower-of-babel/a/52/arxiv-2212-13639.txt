Holographic thermal propagator for arbitrary scale dimensions
Borut Bajc

Abstract
Using the AdS/CFT correspondence we model the behaviour of the two-point correlator of an operator with arbitrary scale dimension Δ \Delta in arbitrary spacetime dimension d d for small but non-zero temperature. The obtained propagator coincides in the low temperature regime with the known result for d = 4 d=4 for large Δ \Delta at the order T d T^{d} as well as with the T d T^{d} and T 2 ​ d T^{2d} terms of the exact all order result for d = 2 d=2 . Furthermore, for arbitrary d d we explicitly write down the expression for the order T d T^{d} of the propagator for arbitrary Δ \Delta , and present a conjecture for the order T 2 ​ d T^{2d} in the large Δ \Delta limit.

Introduction

Correlation functions of operators in conformal field theories are
constrained by symmetry. For example, the two-point correlation function in

d
-dimensional Euclidean momentum space of a spin zero primary operator
with scale dimension
\Delta=d/2+\nu
is given by

G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\propto k^{2\nu}\quad,\quad k^{2}=\omega^{2}+\vec{q}^{\,2}\quad,

(1.1)

where we split the time and space part of the
d
-dimensional vector
k^{\mu}=(\omega,\vec{q})

for later use.

A bit less obvious is what happens when in such a theory we introduce a non-zero temperature, i.e.
in the spacetime
S_{1/T}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Conformal symmetry is now broken by
T\neq 0

and correlators get, as we will see, corrections of order
{\cal O}((T/k)^{d})
. There have been recently
two important developments in this direction. First, the problem has been attacked
in the limit of
\vec{q}=0
for the absorption cross section by black branes
[
1
]
or
using the AdS/CFT correspondence
[
2
,
3
,
4
]

for large scale dimensions
\Delta\gg 1

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. This last limit was needed to be able to apply the geodesic
approximation in the background geometry of a black brane in AdS, while the leading order has been given
for general
\Delta
recently in terms of the ambient space formalism
[
8
]
. Second, by using the solution
to the connection problem of the Heun equation
[
9
,
10
]

a complete analytic result for the thermal two-point correlation function has been found recently in

[
11
]
(with the generalisation to non-zero chemical potential in
[
12
]
).

These results are however not completely satisfactory. First, large scale dimensions are not necessarily
realistic, so results for smaller values are welcome. Second, although the solution found in
[
11
]

is analytic, it is very hard to make it work properly in the small temperature
expansion of the black brane case, mainly because an infinite number of terms contribute and the
resummation is far from obvious.

In this work we want to improve the state of the art calculating
the first two corrections (the
(T/k)^{d}
and the
(T/k)^{2d}
ones) to the zero temperature propagator
for a completely generic scale dimension
\Delta
and arbitrary spacetime dimension
d
.
In some sense this paper is a continuation of
[
13
]
, although it uses a different approach.
The method for calculating the propagator we use in this paper is straightforward:
we rewrite the usual second order ordinary differential equation (ODE) for a perturbation in
the black brane AdS background as a matrix linear first order ODE, expand it in powers
of the temperature and
solve it by explicit integration. When we will not be able to explicitly and analytically compute the integrals
involved, we will evaluate them numerically. As we will show, our method turns out to be
tougher and tougher as the scale dimension increases, so it is particularly useful especially for
low
\Delta
and so it is complementary to the large
\Delta
calculation of

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

2
The propagator from the AdS/CFT approach

As we mentioned in the introduction we will model our CFT primary operator with scale dimension

\Delta
by a scalar field in the AdS black brane background. The equation to solve in momentum
space for the scalar field perturbation

\eta(z)=\int d^{d}x\,e^{-ix_{\mu}k^{\mu}}\phi(x,z)

(2.1)

is thus (see for example
[
14
]
and references therein)

\left(f(z/z_{h})\;z\;\frac{d}{dz}\;z\frac{d}{dz}-d\;z\;\frac{d}{dz}-z^{2}\;\left(k^{2}+\omega^{2}\;\frac{1-f(z/z_{h})}{f(z/z_{h})}\right)-\Delta(\Delta-d)\right)\,\eta(z)=0\quad,

(2.2)

where

f(x)\equiv 1-x^{d}

(2.3)

with the AdS radius
L=1
and the horizon radius (black brane position)
expressed in terms of the spacetime dimension
d
and temperature
T
through

z_{h}=\frac{d}{4\pi T}\quad.

(2.4)

Our choice of (Euclidean) boundary conditions for the two linearly independent solutions
\eta_{\pm}(z)
are

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0^{+}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\eta_{\pm}(z)\quad\longrightarrow z^{\frac{d}{2}\pm\nu}\quad,

\displaystyle z\rightarrow z_{h}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle|\eta_{-}(z)|<\infty\quad,

(2.5)

where

\nu=\Delta-d/2

(2.6)

is taken to be positive with non integer
2\nu
all along the paper.

If we write

\displaystyle\eta(z)=\frac{z^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{f(z/z_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\;h(z)\quad,

(2.7)

then (
2.2
) implies that
h(z)
obeys

\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-U(z)\right)\,h(z)=0\quad,

(2.8)

where

U(z)=-\frac{1}{4\,z^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{4}\,\frac{2\,f(z/z_{h})-1}{z^{2}\,f(z/z_{h})^{2}}+\frac{\Delta(\Delta-d)}{z^{2}\,f(z/z_{h})}+\frac{k^{2}}{f(z/z_{h})}+\omega^{2}\,\frac{1-f(z/z_{h})}{f(z/z_{h})^{2}}\quad.

(2.9)

Let
h_{\pm}(z)
be a basis of solutions to (
2.8
).
The general solution can be written as

h(z)=C_{+}\,h_{+}(z)+C_{-}\,h_{-}(z)\quad,

(2.10)

where
C_{\pm}
are arbitrary constants.
Taking into account the derivative of (
2.10
),
we can write,

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h(z)\\
h^{\prime}(z)\end{array}\right)={\bf w}\left(h_{+},h_{-};z\right)\;\left(\begin{array}[]{c}C_{+}\\
C_{-}\end{array}\right)\quad,

(2.11)

where

{\bf w}\left(f,g;z\right)\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}f(z)&g(z)\\
f^{\prime}(z)&g^{\prime}(z)\end{array}\right)

(2.12)

is the wronskian matrix of
f
and
g
at the point
z
.
For any basis of solutions of (
2.8
) its determinant, i.e. the wronskian, is a non-zero constant.
From (
2.11
), our problem reduces to the computation of the wronskian matrix of a given basis.
According to (
2
) the (Euclidean) boundary conditions are

\displaystyle z\rightarrow 0^{+}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle h_{\pm}(z)\quad\longrightarrow z^{\frac{1}{2}\pm\nu}\quad,

\displaystyle z\rightarrow z_{h}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle|h_{-}(z)|=0\quad.

(2.13)

In this way, the wronskian results:

\det({\bf w}\left(h_{+},h_{-};z\right))=-2\,\nu
.

We can recast the second order equation (
2.8
) as the following first order system,

\frac{d}{dz}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h(z)\\
h^{\prime}(z)\end{array}\right)={\bf A}(z)\;\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h(z)\\
h^{\prime}(z)\end{array}\right)\quad,\quad{\bf A}(z)\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
U(z)&0\end{array}\right)\quad,

(2.14)

or, for the wronskian matrix

\frac{d}{dz}{\bf w}\left(h_{+},h_{-};z\right)={\bf A}(z)\;{\bf w}\left(h_{+},h_{-};z\right)\quad.

(2.15)

The potential
{\bf A}(z)
can be split into a zero-temperature and a temperature-dependent part as

{\bf A}(z)={\bf A}^{(0)}(z)+{\bf A}_{T}(z)\quad,

(2.16)

where the
T=0
matrix is

{\bf A}^{(0)}(z)=k^{2}\;\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1+\frac{\nu^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}{q^{2}}&0\end{array}\right)\quad,

(2.17)

with

q\equiv kz\quad,

(2.18)

while that the temperature-dependent part can be expanded in powers of
\left(T/k\right)^{d}
as

{\bf A_{T}(z)}=k^{2}\;\sigma_{-}u_{T}(q)\quad,\quad u_{T}(q)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}\frac{u^{(m)}(kz)}{(kz_{h})^{d\,m}}\quad,

(2.19)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad,

\displaystyle u^{(m)}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{m}\,q^{d\,m-2}+B_{m}\,q^{d\,m}\quad,

\displaystyle A_{m}\equiv\nu^{2}-m\,\frac{d^{2}}{4}

,

\displaystyle B_{m}\equiv 1+m\,(\omega/k)^{2}\quad.

(2.20)

2.1
The
T=0
case

At zero temperature (
2.15
) reads,

\frac{d}{dz}{\bf w}\left(h^{(0)}_{+},h^{(0)}_{-};z\right)={\bf A}^{(0)}(z)\;{\bf w}\left(h^{(0)}_{+},h^{(0)}_{-};z\right)\quad.

(2.21)

The solutions are
z^{\frac{1}{2}}
times Bessel functions of order
\nu
.
A summary of their properties and useful expansions is given in appendix (
A
).
The basis (
2.13
) is given by

\displaystyle h^{(0)}_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{\nu}\,\Gamma(1+\nu)}{k^{\nu}}\;z^{\frac{1}{2}}\;I_{\nu}(q)\quad,

(2.22)

\displaystyle h^{(0)}_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-\nu}\,k^{\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\;z^{\frac{1}{2}}\;K_{\nu}(q)\quad.

(2.23)

According to the AdS/CFT
recipe the propagator is defined through the limit

h_{-}(z)\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\rightarrow 0^{+}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad z^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}\;(1+\dots)+G_{2}(k)\;z^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}\;(1+\dots)

(2.24)

to be in the leading order

G_{2}^{(0)}(k)=\frac{\Gamma(-\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu)}\;\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{2\,\nu}\quad.

(2.25)

2.2
The
T\neq 0
case

Going to the general case, if we make the ansatz for any
T
,

{\bf w}\left(h_{+},h_{-};z\right)={\bf w}\left(h^{(0)}_{+},h^{(0)}_{-};z\right)\;\left({\bf 1}+{\bf w}^{(T)}(q)\right)\quad,

(2.26)

then from (
2.15
) and (
2.21
) we get for

{\bf w}^{(T)}(q)

\frac{d}{dq}{\bf w}^{(T)}(q)=q\;u_{T}(q)\;{\bf b}(q)\;\left({\bf 1}+{\bf w}^{(T)}(q)\right)\quad,

(2.27)

where
u_{T}(q)
is given in (
2.19
)
and the matrix
{\bf b}(q)
is

\displaystyle{\bf b}(q)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle z^{-1}\;{\bf w}\left(h^{(0)}_{+},h^{(0)}_{-};z\right)^{-1}\;\sigma_{-}\;{\bf w}\left(h^{(0)}_{+},h^{(0)}_{-};z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\nu}(q)\,K_{\nu}(q)&\alpha\,K_{\nu}(q){}^{2}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha}\,I_{\nu}(q){}^{2}&-I_{\nu}(q)\,K_{\nu}(q)\end{array}\right)\quad,

where

\alpha\equiv\frac{2^{1-2\nu}\,k^{2\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)\,\Gamma(1+\nu)}=-2\frac{\sin(\pi\nu)}{\pi}\,G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\quad.

(2.31)

In order to solve (
2.27
) we expand

{\bf w}^{(T)}(q)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}\frac{{\bf w}^{(m)}(q)}{(kz_{h})^{m\,d}}

(2.32)

and get the following system of differential equations for the
{\bf w}^{(m)}(q)
’s,
m>0

(we define
{\bf w}^{(0)}=\mathbb{I}
)

\frac{d}{dq}{\bf w}^{(m)}(q)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\;q\;u^{(m-k)}(q)\;{\bf b}(q)\;{\bf w}^{(k)}(q)\quad,

(2.33)

to be solved iteratively, with the
u^{(m)}(q)
’s given in (
2
).
The solution is

{\bf w}^{(m)}(q)={\bf w}_{0}^{(m)}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\;\int^{q}dz\,z\;u^{(m-k)}(z)\;{\bf b}(z)\;{\bf w}^{(k)}(z)\quad.

(2.34)

From (
2.26
) and (
2.12
) we have

\displaystyle h_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+w_{11}^{(T)}(q)\right)\;h^{(0)}_{+}(z)+w_{21}^{(T)}(q)\;h^{(0)}_{-}(z)\quad,

(2.35)

\displaystyle h_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{12}^{(T)}(q)\;h^{(0)}_{+}(z)+\left(1+w_{22}^{(T)}(q)\right)\;h^{(0)}_{-}(z)\quad.

(2.37)

The integration constants present in the computation of the matrix elements

w_{ij}^{(T)}(q)
are fixed by (
2.13
), i.e. for
z\to 0

z\rightarrow 0^{+}\quad:\quad h_{\pm}(z)\quad\longrightarrow z^{\frac{1}{2}\pm\nu}\quad.

(2.38)

Since expanding everything around
T=0
is equivalent to
expanding everything around
z_{h}=\infty
, we replace the last constraint of
(
2.13
) with

z\rightarrow\infty\quad:\quad h_{-}(z)\longrightarrow 0\quad.

(2.39)

Near
z=0
,

\displaystyle h_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+w_{11}^{(T)}(q)\right)\;z^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(q))

(2.40)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{21}^{(T)}(q)\;z^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}\,\left(1+{\cal O}(q)+G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\,z^{2\,\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(q))\right)\quad,

(2.41)

\displaystyle h_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{12}^{(T)}(q)\;z^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(q))

(2.42)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(1+w_{22}^{(T)}(q)\right)\,z^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}\,\left(1+{\cal O}(q)+G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\,z^{2\,\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(q))\right)\quad.

(2.43)

Conditions (

0=w_{11}^{(T)}(0)=w_{22}^{(T)}(0)=\left.q^{-2\,\nu}\,w_{21}^{(T)}(q)\right|_{q\rightarrow 0}=\left.q^{2\,\nu}\,w_{12}^{(T)}(q)\right|_{q\rightarrow 0}\quad.

The first three conditions imply that

\displaystyle z\to 0

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle w_{ij}^{(m)}(z)\sim z^{md+(i-j)2\nu}\quad,

(2.45)

\displaystyle z\to\infty

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle w_{ij}^{(m)}(z)\sim z^{md+1-|i-j|}e^{(i-j)2z}\quad,

(2.46)

all

except eventually

(12)

) incorporating (

) as

(2.48)

Taking into account that the behavior of a solution is always of the form:

a_{-}z^{\frac{1}{2}-\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(z))+a_{+}z^{\frac{1}{2}+\nu}\,(1+{\cal O}(z))
,
we can write the following behaviour for

w_{12}^{(T)}(q)\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q\rightarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\,g^{(T)}(k)+a\,q^{-2\,\nu+d}\,\left(1+{\cal O}(q)\right)\quad,

(2.49)

where

is some free constant. Then the AdS/CFT recipe (
2.24
)
gives the propagator in the form

G_{2}(k)=G_{2}^{(0)}(k)\,\left(1+g^{(T)}(k)\right)\quad.

(2.50)

Similarly as
{\bf w}^{(T)}(k)
in (
2.32
) also

gets expanded:

g^{(T)}(k)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{g_{m}(\omega/k)}{(kz_{h})^{md}}\quad.

(2.51)

Thus, to get the finite temperature correction to the propagator one needs to
extract the constant part of the expansion of
w_{12}^{(T)}(q)
for
q\to 0
.
If the integral (
2.48
) converges, then the constant part of the expansion
(
2.49
) is simply

G_{2}^{(0)}(k)g_{m}(\omega/k)=-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\;\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,z\;u^{(m-k)}(z)\;\left({\bf b}(z)\;{\bf w}^{(k)}(z)\right)_{12}\quad.

(2.52)

However, for

the integral (

F(z)\equiv-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\,z\;u^{(m-k)}(z)\;\left({\bf b}(z)\;{\bf w}^{(k)}(z)\right)_{12}\to\sum_{\alpha>0}\frac{F_{-\alpha}}{z^{\alpha+1}}+\sum_{\beta>0}F_{\beta}z^{\beta-1}

as

are in general non integer real numbers. Notice that
for finite
\nu
there is always only a finite number of divergent terms (
\alpha

In this case (

which renders the integration at

Of course, if one is able to compute analytically (

just takes the constant term in the expansion around

of the result as indicated in eq.
(

First correction:
(T/k)^{d}

At first order we obtain

{\bf w}^{(1)}(q)=A_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{d}[I_{\nu}\,K_{\nu};q,0]&\alpha\,I_{d}[K_{\nu}{}^{2};q,\infty]\\

6
Conclusions

We showed in this paper the way to calculate the euclidean thermal propagator in a CFT using
the AdS/CFT correspondence. Although the full formula for it has been recently found
[
11
]
,
it is not suited for a small temperature expansion.
We found the first two corrections, the first analytical of order
T^{d}
in (
3.10
),
and the second numerical of order
T^{2d}
via (
4.5
) and (
4
). The order
T^{d}

agrees with
[
8
]
for general
d
and with
[
5
,
6
]
for
d=4

(summarised in (
3.11
)). The order
T^{2d}
reproduces the well known
result
[
15
]
for
d=2
.
This last check is summarised in fig.
1
. We then predicted the
T^{8}
contributions in the

d=4
case in fig.
3
. Finally we showed in fig.
4
explicitly that the
large
\nu
approximation does not properly describe the small
\nu
limit, a result which is
not surprising but had to be checked. The method presented here is thus appropriate
for this range of
\nu
, not described by the geodesic approximation.

Our approach can deal with general spacetime dimensions
d
. It is not tied to the
Heun equation as is the case of the analytical solution
[
11
]
.
In fact in
d>4
apparently more singular points are present and so it is not clear if the equation
to be solved is still of the Heun type. In this paper we found for arbitrary
d
an explicit expression
(
3.10
) for the propagator at order
T^{d}
for arbitrary
\nu
. At the order
T^{2d}
we conjecture
an analytic formula (
5.13
), valid for large
\nu
.

The perturbative expansion in
T/k
, which we do in

d
-dimensional Euclidean spacetime, seems to be compatible with the analytical continuation
to Minkowski spacetime. This is checked for example if we compare our Euclidean result with the
Minkowski all-order result in
d=2
.

Few words of caution are perhaps important at this point. As studied in
[
1
]
,
the Heun equation has one potential problem and one real problem. The potential one is connected to the
uniform convergence of the asymptotic expansion in the whole coordinate interval, also close to the singularities.
The authors of
[
1
]
use successfully the method of Langer and Olver
[
18
,
19
]
.
In our case this would roughly mean that the coefficients (
2.47
)-(
2.48
) need to be finite, which we easily prove they are.
More precisely, in the limit
k=\omega
we start from the same equation of
[
1
]
. The difference with them is that we
automatically find an expansion in positive integer powers of
T^{d}
, while in
[
1
]
this turns out to be true
only after some cancellations. For our more general case of
k\neq\omega
we do not know of any method equivalent to Langer and Olver.
Finding such a method is clearly beyond the scope of this paper. We however check our results by comparing them to the known
ones in the literature and find perfect agreement whenever available.
The second, real problem, is the appearance in the solution of terms
proportional to positive powers of
\exp{(-1/T)}
. These terms, as in
[
1
]
,
are not available in our method. We would like here to stress that these terms are certainly one of the reasons why the
full solution
[
11
]
mentioned above is not suited for a low
T
expansion.
