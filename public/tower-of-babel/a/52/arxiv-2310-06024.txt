Pair-breaking scattering interference as a mechanism for superconducting gap modulation
Zhi-Qiang Gao, Yu-Ping Lin, Dung-Hai Lee

Abstract
We propose the “pair-breaking scattering interference” as a general source of coherence peak modulations in superconductors. Assuming this mechanism, we present a simple physical picture for the coherence peak modulations in overdoped cuprate Bi 2 Sr 2 Ca 2 Cu 3 O 10+δ (Bi-2223), ferromagnetic iron pnictide EuRbFe 4 As 4 (Eu-1144), and kagome metals A A V 3 Sb 5 ( A = A= K, Rb, and Cs). Specifically, we explain the wavevectors, the particle-hole symmetry, and the dependence on the internal or external Zeeman-field of the coherence peak modulations. This work is intended as a cautious reminder to the scientific community when asserting the existence of a pair density wave phenomenon in the absence of tunneling conductance modulations in the normal state.

The PBSI formalism relies on the existence of a

Within this framework, we show that coherence peak modulations can emerge as a consequence of the PBSI. III
The PBSI mechanism for the periodic SC gap modulation

Consider the BdG-type Hamiltonian describing a uniform spin-singlet superconductor

H=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}ZI-hZZ-\Delta^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}YY-\Delta^{I}_{\mathbf{k}}XY)\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}.

(2)

\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}_{F}}=E_{F}=0

\displaystyle H_{\downarrow}=\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow}^{\dagger}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}Z+hI-\Delta^{R}_{\mathbf{k}}X+\Delta^{I}_{\mathbf{k}}Y)\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\downarrow},

In this case, the multi-scattering effects by different impurities, including disorder average, do not wash out the scattering interference completely. Meanwhile, the finite density of impurities ensures the occurrence of observable modulations. In addition, phenomena such as coherence peak modulation and quasiparticle interference are absent if the disorder average is performed. Hence a disorder-averaged theory is not relevant for our purposes. This approach, under assumptions stated above, has proven effective in capturing relevant physics in superconductors

In the experiments
Zou et al. 2022
;

In fact, the coherence length of the modulation is contingent upon microscopic details of the impurity potential, in particular, the correlation length of the impurity potential itself. For instance, if the impurity potential exhibits a long correlation length, as is the case with out-of-plane impurities, the coherence length of modulation can be correspondingly long. Conversely, for impurities with short correlation lengths, such as point-like in-plane impurities, the coherence length of modulation will be shorter.

;
Deng et al. 2024b
. Importantly, the 3/4-wavevector coherence peak modulations appear only when the impurities are dense enough, and they vanish in the defect-few regions. These results are in perfect agreement with the prediction of our PBSI mechanism.

V
Conclusion

We have proposed the pair-breaking scattering interference (PBSI) as an alternative explanation of coherence peak modulations in superconductors. This explanation involves (i) the momentum locations of the hot spots, which can be obtained experimentally from, say, angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES); (ii) the sign structure of the gap function at the hot spots, which requires phase-sensitive measurements such as phase-sensitive quasiparticle interference STM spectroscopy; and (iii) the nature of impurities, which can be obtained through, say, NMR measurement and the phase-sensitive quasiparticle interference in STM. In particular, according to the PBSI mechanism, impurity scattering is essential. Hence one would expect coherence peak modulations to be detected around impurities. On the contrary, the signal should be absent in large impurity-free regions. The predictions of the pair-breaking scattering interference can be examined experimentally in STM measurements. This work serves as a cautious reminder to the scientific community when asserting the existence of a PDW phenomenon.

VI
The pair-breaking Hamiltonian

In the solids, the electrons
c_{\mathbf{r}}=(c_{\mathbf{r}\uparrow},c_{\mathbf{r}\downarrow})^{T}
can scatter by the scalar and magnetic impurities (for simplicity we assume the magnetic scattering to be
S_{z}
-conserving without losing generality)

H_{\text{imp}}=\sum_{\mathbf{r}}c_{\mathbf{r}}^{\dagger}[V^{\text{s}}(\mathbf{r})I+V^{\text{m}}(\mathbf{r})Z]c_{\mathbf{r}},

(S1)

where
V^{\text{s,m}}(\mathbf{r})
are local scalar and magnetic impurity-scattering potentials, respectively. A Fourier transform
c_{\mathbf{r}}=(1/\sqrt{N})\sum_{\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
, where
N
is the number of sites, yields the momentum-space form

H_{\text{imp}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}c_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{q}}I+V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{q}}Z]c_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}.

(S2)

Here
V^{\text{s,m}}_{\mathbf{q}}=(1/N)\sum_{\mathbf{r}}V^{\text{s,m}}(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
are the Fourier-transformed impurity potentials, which are smooth in transfer momentum
\mathbf{q}
due to their local structures. The expression in terms of the Nambu operator
\psi_{\bf k}=(c_{\bf k\uparrow},c_{\bf k\downarrow},c^{\dagger}_{\bf-k\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{\bf-k\downarrow})^{T}
reads

H_{\text{imp}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}[V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{q}}ZI+V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{q}}ZZ]\Psi_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}.

(S3)

Since
H_{\text{imp}}
commutes with
ZZ
, we can decompose it into the
\uparrow
(
ZZ=+1
) and
\downarrow
(
ZZ=-1
) sectors with

H_{\text{imp},\uparrow\downarrow}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}[V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{q}}Z\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{q}}I]\Psi_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}\uparrow\downarrow},

(S4)

where
\psi_{\bf k\uparrow}=(c_{\bf k\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{\bf-k\uparrow})^{T}
and
\psi_{\bf k\downarrow}=(c_{\bf k\downarrow},c^{\dagger}_{\bf-k\downarrow})^{T}
.

Now we derive the pair-breaking Hamiltonian. Here we focus on the hot spots
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
on the Fermi surface, where the quasiparticle operators
\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}
and
\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}
are given by

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}\\
\pm e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}}}[\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}-\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1,2}\uparrow\downarrow}]\end{array}\right).

(S5)

The impurity-scattering potential can be transformed into the quasiparticle eigenbasis

H_{\text{imp},\uparrow\downarrow}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}[\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}],&\pm e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}}[\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}-\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}]\end{array}\right)[V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}Z\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}I]\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}\\
\pm e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}}[\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}-\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}]\end{array}\right)+\text{h.c.}.

(S6)

The pair-breaking (PB) part of the Hamiltonian is obtained by omitting the crossing terms between
\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\uparrow\downarrow}
and
\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\uparrow\downarrow}
, yielding

\displaystyle H_{\text{PB},\uparrow\downarrow}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\pm e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}}\end{array}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}&0\\
0&-V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\pm e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}}\end{array}\right)\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}

(S7)

\displaystyle+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\mp e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}}\end{array}\right)\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}&0\\
0&-V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\mp e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}}\end{array}\right)\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\frac{1}{2}[(V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}})+(-V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}})e^{i(\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}})}]\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}

\displaystyle+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\frac{1}{2}[(V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}})+(-V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}})e^{i(\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}})}]\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{V^{\text{s}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\bigg[\frac{1-e^{i(\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}})}}{2}\bigg]\pm V^{\text{m}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\bigg[\frac{1+e^{i(\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}-\theta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}})}}{2}\bigg]\bigg\}(\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow}+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}\uparrow\downarrow}^{\dagger}\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{2}\uparrow\downarrow})+\text{h.c.}.

This is the pair-breaking Hamiltonian we use in the main text.

VII
Derivation of LDOS at zero temperature

In the STM measurements, the LDOS at spatial position
\mathbf{r}
and bias
\omega
is determined by the local differential conductance

D(\omega,\mathbf{r})\sim\frac{dI}{dV}(\omega,\mathbf{r}),

(S8)

where
I
and
V
are the local current and voltage, respectively. At positive and negative biases, the differential conductance corresponding to the electron and the hole tunnelings are

\displaystyle D_{+}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{n}\sum_{\sigma}|\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{n}),

(S9)

\displaystyle D_{-}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{n}\sum_{\sigma}|\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega+E_{n}).

Here
|{\Omega}\rangle
is the SC ground state and
|{n}\rangle
are eigenstates with energies
E_{n}
. After Fourier transform, the tunneling amplitudes read

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle n|c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(S10)

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\langle n|c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}.

On the Fermi surface, we express the
c_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}
in terms of the quasiparticle operators as

c_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}+\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}).

(S11)

Note that the ground state obeys the condition
\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}|{\Omega}\rangle=\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{\dagger}|{\Omega}\rangle=0
(at zero temperature, the quasiparticle states above and below the Fermi level are fully filled and empty, respectively). The tunneling amplitudes then become

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\langle n|\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle,

(S12)

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\langle n|\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle.

Within the QP approximation, the relevant eigenstates
|{n}\rangle
are the one-quasiparticle and one-quasihole excitations

|{n}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{F}}\sum_{\sigma}(\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{*}\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{\dagger}+\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma})|{\Omega}\rangle.

(S13)

According to the fact
\langle\Omega|\chi_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\chi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle=\langle\Omega|\eta_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}\eta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}|\Omega\rangle=\delta_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
, the scattering amplitudes take the form

\displaystyle\langle n|\chi_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma},

(S14)

\displaystyle\langle n|\eta_{\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{*}.

Inserting this result back to the real-space tunneling amplitudes, we get

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{F}}\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(S15)

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{F}}\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{F}\sigma}^{*}e^{i\mathbf{k}_{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}}.

Now we derive the LDOS associated with the hot spots
\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
. The relevant tunneling amplitudes read

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\dagger}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\left[\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{1}\sigma}e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{2}\sigma}\right]e^{-i\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(S16)

\displaystyle\langle n|c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\left[\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{1}\sigma}^{*}e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{2}\sigma}^{*}\right]e^{i\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

where
{\bf Q=k_{1}-k_{2}}
.
The LDOS then take the form

\displaystyle D_{+}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n}\sum_{\sigma}\left|\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{1}\sigma}e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+\alpha_{n\mathbf{k}_{2}\sigma}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{n}),

(S17)

\displaystyle D_{-}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n}\sum_{\sigma}\left|\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{1}\sigma}^{*}e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+\beta_{n\mathbf{k}_{2}\sigma}^{*}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega+E_{n}).

The contributions from the four low-energy quasiparticle eigenstates
\chi_{\uparrow,S,L}
and
\eta_{\downarrow,S,L}
are

\displaystyle D_{+}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\left|e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-e^{i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega-\Delta_{S})+\left|e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+e^{i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega-\Delta_{L})

(S18)

\displaystyle\sim[1-\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega-\Delta_{S})+[1+\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega-\Delta_{L}),

\displaystyle D_{-}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\left|e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-se^{-i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega+\Delta_{S})+\left|e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+se^{-i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega+\Delta_{L})

\displaystyle\sim[1-s\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega+\Delta_{S})+[1+s\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega+\Delta_{L}).

Similarly, we can do the calculation for the four high-energy quasiparticle eigenstates
\chi_{\downarrow,S,L}
and
\eta_{\uparrow,S,L}

\displaystyle D_{+}^{\prime}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\left|e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+se^{i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega-\Delta_{S}^{\prime})+\left|e^{-i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-se^{i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega-\Delta_{L}^{\prime})

(S19)

\displaystyle\sim[1+s\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega-\Delta_{S}^{\prime})+[1-s\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega-\Delta_{L}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle D_{-}^{\prime}(\omega,\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sim\left|e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}+e^{-i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega+\Delta_{S}^{\prime})+\left|e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}-e^{-i\phi}\right|^{2}\delta(\omega+\Delta_{L}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\sim[1+\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega+\Delta_{S}^{\prime})+[1-\cos(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi)]\delta(\omega+\Delta_{L}^{\prime}).

These are the LDOS formula we use in the main text.
