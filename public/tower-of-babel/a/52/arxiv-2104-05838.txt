Theory of parametric x-ray optical wavemixing processes
Dietrich Krebs, Nina Rohringer

Abstract
Enabled by x-ray free-electron lasers, nonlinear optical phenomena can be explored in the x-ray domain nowadays. Among the multitude of newly accessible processes, this theoretical study focuses parametric x-ray optical wavemixing for closer investigation. Specifically, we develop a framework based on non-relativistic QED to describe x-ray optical sum- and difference-frequency generation as well as x-ray parametric down-conversion on equal footing. All of these processes promise imaging capabilities similar to regular x-ray diffraction with additional spectroscopic selectivity that is tunable via the optical admixture. Based on our derivation, we identify the imaged quantity as we relate the observable scattering pattern to an underlying response function of the medium. The resulting relation, furthermore, enables the microscopic reconstruction of this response function from nonlinear analogues of crystallographic measurements. We benchmark our approach on recent experimental sum-frequency results, for which we find encouraging agreement with our theory.



I
Introduction

Since their discovery in 1895, x-rays have been used to obtain insights into the structure of material systems—with x-ray diffraction in particular serving as a microscopic probe of the electronic density
Friedrich et al. 1912
;
Giacovazzo et al. 1992
. This approach—backed by the continuous improvement of sources and detectors—has rendered it routinely possible, for example, to reconstruct complicated protein structures from x-ray scattering measurements nowadays
Wiedorn et al. 2018
.

Notwithstanding its broad success, regular x-ray diffraction becomes very challenging, if microscopic resolution of valence electron distributions is desired
Coppens et al. 2005
. These weakly bound electrons, which are the most relevant in chemical and technological terms, are typically spread dilutely across the solid and, as such, their scattering signal scales unfavorably compared to the well-localized (atomic) core electrons.

In order to circumvent this problem and obtain a valence selective scheme, Freund and Levine suggested to go beyond linear elastic scattering as early as 1970
Freund and Levine 1970
. Using parametric x-ray optical wavemixing processes (XOWM) instead, one can leverage the spectral selectivity of an additional optical field to isolate the response of valence electrons.
An example of this is the process of sum-frequency generation (SFG), wherein a sample is illuminated by both x-ray (
\omega_{X}
) and optical (
\omega_{O}
) photons, while the detection focuses on scattered photons at the sum-frequency
\omega_{S}=\omega_{X}+\omega_{O}
. As this frequency cannot be generated via elastic scattering, any measured signal thus provides a short-wavelength
probe
conditional
on the simultaneous interaction with the optical modulation. Tuning the latter resonantly with valence excitations then establishes the desired selectivity.

Though promising in principle, x-ray wavemixing approaches were restricted to proof-of-principle tests for a long time, as they were stifled by low count rates and cumbersome detection
Eisenberger and McCall 1971a
;
Danino and Freund 1981
. Yet, with the arrival of third and fourth generation light sources, they are now becoming realistically observable and have attracted renewed experimental interest
Glover et al. 2012
;
Schori et al. 2017
;
Borodin et al. 2019
;
Sofer et al. 2019
. In fact, there has even been a first report addressing the long-standing goal to reconstruct the valence electron response
Tamasaku et al. 2011
within the high-frequency limit.

Complementing these experimental developments, we want to address the theoretical understanding of XOWM. So far, its interpretation often
Glover et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Borodin et al. 2017
;
Borodin et al. 2019
relies on the classical picture suggested by Eisenberger and McCall for wavemixing within the x-ray domain
Eisenberger and McCall 1971a
. However, its central assumption—namely an electronic response in terms of free, classical currents—becomes invalid once optical frequencies are admixed. The optical response is instead dominated by discrete, quantum mechanical transitions that need to be accounted for in a theoretical description, in order to be microscopically accurate.
Multiple attempts at this have been developed based on the semi-classical response formalisms of Armstrong et al.
Armstrong et al. 1962
as well as Jha et al.
Jha and Warke 1968
, with a noteworthy alternative recently presented by Gorelova et al., who treat the optical admixture via Floquet-formalism
Popova-Gorelova et al. 2018
. So far, none of these approaches has been evaluated beyond effective single electron theories (see also Sec.
III
)

Throughout the discussion, we will employ the conventional system of atomic units, i.e.,

II
Theory

II.1
Basic framework

We set our description of XOWM within the well-established framework of nonrelativistic QED
Craig and Thirunamachandran 1998
;
Loudon 1983
, closely following the formalism outlined in Ref
Santra 2009
. In particular, we choose to impose the Coulomb gauge upon the electromagnetic field and subsequently quantize only its transverse components. The respective vector potential in free space reads:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{V\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\alpha^{2}}}~\big(\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}~{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}~e^{i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}+\text{h.c.}\big),

(1)

where
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator for a photon of energy
\omega_{\mathbf{k}}=c\,\left|\mathbf{k}\right|
in the plane wave mode of wave vector
\mathbf{k}
and polarization
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}
. There are two polarization states enumerated through
\lambda
, both of which satisfy the transversality condition
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{k}=0
. The theoretical quantization volume is labelled by
V
. Notably, this volume will not feature in any physical observable (see below).
Coupling the field to matter, we focus on the electronic charges and write the associated interaction Hamiltonian in the familiar minimal coupling form
3
3

3

In writing
\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}
as of Eq. (
2
), we neglect couplings of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}(\mathbf{x})
to nuclear charges and—more intricately—to the spin degrees of freedom present in material systems.
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\!d^{3}x\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})\big(\alpha\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\big)\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x}).

(2)

Here, the 2-component spinors
\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x})
(
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})
) are the electronic field operators analogous to
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}
), which annihilate (create) an electron of particular spin state at the position
\mathbf{x}
. We relegate any further details of the material’s description (including electrostatic interactions) into a pertaining Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\textsc{mat}}
, while adopting the standard mode-based formulation for the electromagnetic part
\hat{H}_{\textsc{em}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}+1/2)
.
Together with Eq. (
2
), these render the Hamiltonian of the overall problem tripartite:

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\textsc{mat}}+\hat{H}_{\textsc{em}}+\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}.

(3)

Based on this, we can formally describe the time-evolution of the coupled system’s density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
using the Liouville-von Neumann equation
and, by extension, evaluate any physical observable as:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\textsc{}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{O}}\right\}.

(4)

In particular, we shall proceed to define an operator
\hat{O}
that suitably captures XOWM. To this end, we adopt a scattering perspective and focus on the detection of x-ray photons that are scattered out of an incoming field distribution into initially unoccupied modes of Eq. (
1
). For any such final mode
\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}
we can determine the probability of a single photon being scattered into it by projecting onto the number state
\left|\smash{1}\right>_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}
. Analogous procedures are widely used in the calculation of far-field diffraction patterns from weakly scattering samples (see for instance Refs.
Schuelke 2007
;
Als-Nielsen and McMorrow 2011
;
Ho and Santra 2008
;
Slowik 2015
).
More formally, we can write a projector across the full radiative Fock-space

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}

\displaystyle=\big(\bigotimes_{\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda\neq\lambda_{f}}\hskip-10.00002pt\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\mathbf{k},\lambda}\big)\otimes\left|\smash{1}\right>\left<\smash{1}\right|_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}},

(5)

which singles out the same probability.
Combining this with the identity on the material part,
\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{\textsc{mat}}=\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}
, yields the desired observable for our purpose
4
4

4

It should be noted that—by construction—our observable is neither suited for cases of strong or (self-) stimulated scattering, where
\left<\hat{n}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\right>\gtrsim 1
, nor for cases of time-gated detection.
.
In order to ultimately detach our results from the arbitrary quantization volume and its associated modes, we can convert the observable into a double differential scattering probability via the usual prescription
Santra 2009
:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}P_{\lambda_{f}}(\mathbf{k}_{f})}{d\Omega_{f}d\omega_{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{V\alpha^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\omega_{f}^{2}\,\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\rangle.

(6)

II.2
Perturbative approach to XOWM

With the basic framework outlined, we return to Eq. (
4
) and concern ourselves in greater detail with the indicated time evolution of
\hat{\rho}(t)
. Its exact treatment is notoriously infeasible for realistic systems, such that we have to approach it via a series of simplifications.
Most notably, we choose to employ (conventional) time-dependent perturbation theory (see for example Ref.
Peskin and Schroeder 1995
)—assuming the light-matter coupling to be sufficiently weak—in order to decompose the fully interacting problem into its different subsystems.
To this end, we rewrite Eq. (
4
)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\textsc{}}\left\{\smash{\hat{U}(t,t_{0})\hat{\rho}(t_{0})\hat{U}(t_{0},t)\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}}\right\}

(7)

utilizing the time-evolution operator
\hat{U}(t,t_{0})
, which formally propagates the system from time
t_{0}
to
t
.
We split this furter into
\hat{U}(t,t_{0})=\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(t,t_{0})\,\hat{U}_{\textsc{int}}(t,t_{0})
, where each
\hat{U}_{\textsc{...}}
is the propagator to a specific time dependent Schroedinger equation, involving
\hat{H}
,
\hat{H}_{\textsc{0}}=\hat{H}-\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}
or
\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}
, respectively.
Then—by expanding
\hat{U}_{\textsc{int}}(t,t_{0})
in powers of
\hat{H}_{\textsc{int}}
—we arrive at the desired perturbation series.
We apply this procedure in two successive steps, focusing on the x-ray part of the interaction first and accounting for any optical admixture in a second iteration.

Preparing the first step, we formally partition the vector potential into orthogonal components

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_in}}(\mathbf{x})+\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_out}}(\mathbf{x})+\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{x})+...

(8)

that pertain to incoming x-ray modes, scattered (or outgoing) x-ray modes as well as optical contributions to the em-field and a presently irrelevant remainder.
We employ this to rewrite Eq. (
2
), keeping only terms that mediate x-ray scattering at lowest perturbative order of
\hat{U}_{\textsc{int}}(t,t_{0})
. Therefore, we are left with

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textsc{int\_x}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int\!\!d^{3}x\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_in}}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_out}}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x}),

(9)

which corresponds to non-resonant (
A^{2}
) scattering and presents a suitable simplification if all involved photon energies (
\omega_{\text{in}}
,
\omega_{\text{out}}
) lie far off electronic resonances
Slowik 2015
.
We assume further that the initial conditions factorize

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{x\_in}}(t_{0})\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{x\_out}}(t_{0})\otimes\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{sys}}(t_{0}),

(10)

i.e., that the incoming x-ray pulse, the initially unoccupied modes pertaining to scattered photons, and the (optically driven) material system are mutually uncorrelated prior to the scattering event.
This allows us to obtain the overall scattering observable in the compact form (for details see App.
A
):

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\!\rangle=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{2}}{V\omega_{f}}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f})_{\sigma}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f}^{*})_{\rho}\!\!\int\!\!dt_{1}\!\!\!\int\!\!dt_{1}^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}\!x\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}\!x^{\prime}e^{i\,\omega_{f}(t_{1}-t_{1}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle e^{-i\,\mathbf{k}_{f}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\,(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},t_{1}))_{\sigma\rho}\,\langle\hat{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime})\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})\rangle_{\textsc{sys}}.

(11)

In writing Eq. (
11
), we have made use of the following abbreviations:
(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)})_{\sigma\rho}
denotes the first-order correlation function of the incoming x-ray field, which is Glauber’s usual coherence function
Glauber 1963
generalized to vector potentials:

\displaystyle(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},t_{1}))_{\sigma\rho}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\textsc{x\_in}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{x\_in}}\,(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(-)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime}))_{\sigma}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(+)}(\mathbf{x},t_{1}))_{\rho}}\right\}.

(12)

We employ Einstein’s summation convention on the polarization factors, where repeatedly occurring greek indices are understood to be summed over.
And, we include the correlator of two electron density operators
\hat{n}(\mathbf{x})=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{x})
, each of which is written with a time-dependence according to
\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t)=\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(0,t)\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(t,0)
. Their expectation value
\langle...\rangle_{\textsc{sys}}=\text{Tr}_{\textsc{sys}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{sys}}(0)...}\right\}
is taken with respect to the system’s initial state as of Eq. (
10
)—correspondingly translated to
t=0
.

The obtained Eq. (
11
) is consistent with established descriptions; its static limit rephrases usual inelastic x-ray scattering
Schuelke 2007
, where the Fourier transformed density-density correlator is known as the dynamic structure factor.
Conversely, in the case of time-resolved scattering, it reproduces—for instance—the results of Ref.
Dixit et al. 2012
.
Considering more explicitly XOWM, it should further be noted that Eq. (
11
) may already present the most suitable description for some scenarios—in particular if the optical light-matter coupling is strong and any further separation of
\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{sys}}(t)
therefore unreasonable

5

Cases of strong optical light-matter coupling may include strong-field laser driving (cf., Ref.
Popova-Gorelova et al. 2018
), but also embedding in a cavity with ensuing polaritonic effects.
.

Within the scope of this article, however, we want to assume that further separation is indeed possible. Hence, our second iteration of expansion aims at obtaining an expression for
\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\!\rangle
that reflects the material’s response to explicit x-ray and optical perturbations—highlighting the wave
mixing
aspect.
In proceeding, we distinguish two facets of the optical light-matter interaction; The first concerns pure propagation effects inside the probed material (e.g., refraction), while the second is the actual non-linear wavemixing. We account for the former through our modelling of the incoming field (see Sec.
III.3
)
6
6

6

It should be noted that this dichotomy is regrettable with regard to the otherwise unified QED formalism. Presently, however, it may be justified by its practical viability, while a more formal justification in the spirit of leg-corrections
Peskin and Schroeder 1995
is relegated to future investigations.
and focus the perturbative expansion explicitly on the nonlinear process.
Starting from the density-density correlator in Eq. (
11
)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime})\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})\rangle_{\textsc{sys}}=

(13)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\textsc{sys}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{sys}}(0)\,\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(0,t_{1}^{\prime})\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(t_{1}^{\prime},t_{1})\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}(t_{1},0)}\right\}

the procedure is similar to the x-ray case.
We assume factorizing initial conditions in order to separate the optical and material subsystems of
\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{sys}}
. Subsequently, we rely on their weak interaction to expand all propagators
\hat{U}_{\textsc{0}}
into further perturbative expressions.
In doing so, we restrict our analysis to lowest order contributions and—notably—only
parametric
processes (for details see App.
B
).
The relevant interaction Hamiltonian under these conditions reads:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textsc{int\_opt}}

\displaystyle=\alpha\int\!\!d^{3}y\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{y})\,\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{y})

(14)

and the expansion of Eq. (
13
) yields:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime})\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})\rangle_{\textsc{sys}}=\int\!\!dt_{2}\!\!\!\int\!\!dt_{2}^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}y\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}y^{\prime}\,

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\,\text{Tr}_{\textsc{opt}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{opt}}(0)\,(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}))_{\nu}\,(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\mu}}\right\}

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\,p_{I}\left(\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y},t_{2},\,\mathbf{x},t_{1})\right)_{\mu}\,\left(\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\,\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime})\right)_{\nu}^{*}.

(15)

Here, we employ the abbreviation

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y},t_{2},\,\mathbf{x},t_{1})\right)_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left<\smash{I}\right|\,\hat{T}\left[(\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\mu}\,\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})\right]\,\left|\smash{I}\right>

(16)

to denote the time-ordered correlation function of the electronic system’s density
\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})
with its momentum density
\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})\,\mathbf{p}\,\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})
—measured in the state
\left|\smash{I}\right>
.
The fact that either correlation function in Eq. (
15
) also represents a transition amplitude from an initial state
\left|\smash{I}\right>
into the same final state reflects the required parametric nature of XOWM. An additional weighting factor
p_{I}
is included to account for the statistical mixture of multiple initial configurations.

Having dealt with the matter subsystem, we turn our attention towards the correlator of optical fields in Eq. (
15
).
It would be appealing to replace this in terms of a Glauber-type correlation function again.
However, the expression at hand is more general than the previously encountered x-ray case.
Here, we have not yet restricted our description to photon-emission or absorption (negative or positive frequency part of
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}
, respectively).
Retaining this generality, we opt for a different reformulation:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{avg}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})+\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{flu}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}).

(17)

We split the operator into a ‘classical’ field
{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{avg}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}):=\text{Tr}_{\textsc{opt}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{opt}}(0)\,\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})}\right\}
—defined via its average—and the remaining fluctuations.
This intrinsically exact separation lends itself straightforwardly to further approximations.
Whenever a spectral region is strongly affected by a classical, external field—for instance a driving laser—we resort to:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\textsc{opt}}\left\{\smash{...}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx({\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{avg}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}))_{\nu}\,({\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{avg}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\mu}

\displaystyle=:(\bar{G}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\nu\mu}.

(18)

Conversely, when such driving is absent, we expect any fluctuations to resemble the thermal equilibrium case and write:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\textsc{opt}}\left\{\smash{...}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\text{Tr}_{\textsc{opt}}\left\{\smash{\hat{\rho}_{\textsc{opt}}^{equ}(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{flu}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime}))_{\nu}\,(\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{flu}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\mu}}\right\}

\displaystyle=:(\bar{S}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\nu\mu}.

(19)

Formally, we keep both options and combine Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
) with our results from Eqs. (
11
) and (
15
) into the overall XOWM observable

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\!\rangle=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{4}}{V\omega_{f}}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptdt_{1}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptdt_{1}^{\prime}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptdt_{2}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptdt_{2}^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}\!x\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}\!x^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}y\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}y^{\prime}\,e^{i\,\omega_{f}(t_{1}-t_{1}^{\prime})}\,e^{-i\,\mathbf{k}_{f}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\,({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f})_{\sigma}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f}^{*})_{\rho}\,(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},t_{1}))_{\sigma\rho}

\displaystyle\Big((\bar{G}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\nu\mu}+(\bar{S}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{y},t_{2}))_{\nu\mu}\Big)\,\sum_{I}\,p_{I}\left(\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y},t_{2},\,\mathbf{x},t_{1})\right)_{\mu}\,\left(\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},t_{2}^{\prime},\,\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime})\right)_{\nu}^{*}.

(20)

While the result above is formulated in real space and time, it will prove advantageous—in particular for the analysis of crystalline matter—to transform into reciprocal coordinates.
To this end, we substitute all occurring quantities by their Fourier transforms (for details see App.
C
) and resolve the arising
\delta
-function constraints. Thereby, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}_{\mathbf{k}_{f},\lambda_{f}}\!\rangle=\frac{\alpha^{4}}{V\omega_{f}\,(2\pi)^{13}}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptd\omega_{O}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptd\omega_{O}^{\prime}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptd\omega_{X}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-10.00002ptd\omega_{X}^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}k_{O}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}k_{O}^{\prime}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}k_{X}\!\!\!\int\!\!d^{3}k_{X}^{\prime}\,\delta(\omega_{f}-\omega_{X}-\omega_{O})\,\delta(\omega_{f}-\omega_{X}^{\prime}-\omega_{O}^{\prime})

\displaystyle({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f})_{\sigma}\,({Z}_{\textsc{x\_in}}(\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{X},{\omega}^{\prime}_{X},\mathbf{k}_{X},{\omega}_{X}}))_{\sigma\rho}\,({\bm{\epsilon}}_{f}^{*})_{\rho}\,\Big((\bar{Z}_{\textsc{opt}}(\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{O},{\omega}^{\prime}_{O},\mathbf{k}_{O},{\omega}_{O}}))_{\nu\mu}+(\bar{C}_{\textsc{opt}}(\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{O},{\omega}^{\prime}_{O},\mathbf{k}_{O},{\omega}_{O}}))_{\nu\mu}\Big)

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\,p_{I}\,\left(\mathbf{K}_{I}(-\mathbf{k}_{O},\mathbf{k}_{f}-\mathbf{k}_{X},\omega_{O})\right)_{\mu}\left(\mathbf{K}_{I}(-\mathbf{k}_{O}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{f}-\mathbf{k}_{X}^{\prime},\omega_{O}^{\prime})\right)^{*}_{\nu}.

(21)

Notably, we have kept the expression
\delta(\omega_{f}-\omega_{X}-\omega_{O})
from being integrated as it highlights two important aspects:

First, we can observe that the overall expression indeed describes
parametric
XOWM. Frequency components of the optical field (
\omega_{O}
) are mixed with x-ray contributions (
\omega_{X}
) to produce the outgoing photon’s frequency
\omega_{f}
—involving
no
net exchange of energy with the material system.

Second, depending on the choice of
\omega_{f}
relative to the central frequency components of incoming x-ray and optical fields, Eq. (
21
) describes either sum- or difference frequency generation. If no optical field is applied externally, the expression can likewise be employed to describe x-ray parametric down-conversion (XPDC). Thus, Eq. (
21
) provides access to a broad range of parametric XOWM processes that are relevant to date on a coherent footing.

III
Modeling

In order to evaluate Eq. (
21
) quantitatively, we have to understand its constituent correlation functions and formulate appropriate models for them.
Aiming to benchmark our overall approach on the SFG results by Glover et al.

III.2
and
III.3

In addition, we require knowledge of the nonlinear response function
\mathbf{K}_{I}
for the case of a Diamond sample.
The latter is a material specific property and will subsequently be discussed in greater detail.

III.1
Electronic response

The essential material quantity in our formulation of XOWM is the electronic response function
\mathbf{P}_{I}(\mathbf{y},t_{2},\,\mathbf{x},t_{1})
—or its Fourier transform
\mathbf{K}_{I}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\omega)
, respectively.
Having separated this from the radiative aspects of the process, we can proceed to review it from a perspective of general electronic structure or response theory.
As such, we established in Eq. (
16
) that
\mathbf{P}_{I}
is the time-ordered correlator of the material’s electronic density
\hat{n}(\mathbf{x},t_{1})
and electronic momentum density
\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{y},t_{2})
.
Structurally, this correlator is a causal, two-particle Green’s function—also referred to as polarization propagator
Oddershede et al. 1984
.
It arises naturally in the context of light-matter interaction, where it forms part of a larger (
4\times 4
) response tensor interlinking density and current-density response functions
Stefanucci and van
Leeuwen 2013
.
Considering this response for electronic many-body systems,
\mathbf{P}_{I}
will naturally reflect their correlation properties.
By preserving this important aspect, our formulation of XOWM enables accurate forward calculations of scattering pattern based on established techniques from many-body theory (cf., for instance, Refs.
Kadanoff and Baym 1962
;
Gross and Kohn 1990
;
Onida et al. 2002
;
Kotliar et al. 2006
;
Booth et al. 2013
;
Wang and Berkelbach 2020
).
Moreover, it provides the basis to extract such properties from XOWM measurements on correlated systems---upon solving the inverse problem
1
1

1

In contrast, previous approaches to describe XOWM, remained restricted to
single-particle
treatments. The perturbative formulations of this problem fell roughly into two classes.
The first comprises discussions such as Refs.
Glover et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Borodin et al. 2017
;
Borodin et al. 2019
, adopting the classical current-based picture that was introduced by Eisenberger and McCall for wavemixing within the x-ray domain

This captures the nonlinear electronic response at high frequencies (cf. Ref.
Adams et al. 2000
), where individual electrons interact with the driving fields like free, classical charges.
However, this picture is not immediately transferable towards lower photon energies—especially if optical admixtures are involved. Then, the bound nature of the electrons becomes essential as their response is dominated by discrete, quantum-mechanical transitions.
This aspect is taken more prominently into account by the second class of previously published XOWM descriptions

;
Eisenberger and McCall 1971b
;

Jha and Woo 1972a
;
Jha and Woo 1972b

All of these approaches can be traced to the semi-classical response formalisms presented by Armstrong et al.
Armstrong et al. 1962
as well as Jha et al.
Jha and Warke 1968
.
Despite being half a century old though, these expressions have never been evaluated in their full form for XOWM. At most, Freund has presented results for the simplified case of a “high-frequency” limit
Freund 1972
, while in addition, bond-charge models
Freund and Levine 1970
and toy-model semiconductors
Cohen and Shwartz 2019
have been considered

III.2
X-ray field

The dependence of XOWM on the incident x-ray field is captured by the first order correlation function
(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)})_{\sigma\rho}
or its Fourier transformed counterpart
({Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{x\_in}})_{\sigma\rho}
, respectively.
Its concrete form results from properties of the source, the manipulation of x-rays along the beam path and their propagation through the sample itself.
For synchrotron or free-electron laser (FEL) sources, certain elements of
(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)})_{\sigma\rho}
have been measured
Singer 2012
;
Vartanyants et al. 2011
while others become accessible through simulations
Chubar et al. 2013
;
Klementiev and Chernikov 2014
;
Samoylova et al. 2016
or via modeling
Geloni et al. 2010
;
Lindberg and Kim 2015
.

For our current demonstration of XOWM, we choose a model-based approach that remains analytically treatable—avoiding the numerical expenses of accounting for the formally 8-dimensional dependence of
(G_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)})_{\sigma\rho}
.
Specifically, we adopt a Gaussian Schell-model (GSM)
Schell 1961
, which was found to yield a good description of undulator based x-ray sources
Singer 2012
;
Gorobtsov et al. 2017
.
Starting from Eqs. (13) and (14) of Ref.
Gorobtsov et al. 2017
, we similarily write the cross spectral density of a—notably still scalar—field as:

\displaystyle W_{\textsc{x\_in}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\omega_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\omega_{2})\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{z_{1}=z_{0}}\\
{z_{2}=z_{0}}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\propto J(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{2})W(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(28)

The expression refers to a virtual source plane of the radiation at
z_{0}
, wherein the spatial and spectral components respectively read:

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{2})=\text{exp}\Big[

\displaystyle-\frac{(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{1})^{2}+(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{2})^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}-\frac{(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{2})^{2}}{2\xi^{2}}\Big]

(29)

\displaystyle W(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\text{exp}\Big[

\displaystyle-\frac{(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})^{2}+(\omega_{2}-\omega_{0})^{2}}{4\Omega^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{2}}{2\Omega_{c}^{2}}-it_{0}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})\Big].

(30)

The coordinates
\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{i}
span this plane perpendicular to the
z
-direction, which we will—in turn—adopt as the direction of propagation for our x-ray beam. For simplicity, we assume the initial distribution of radiation to be radially symmetric with a transverse size
\sigma
and transverse coherence length
\xi
.
Around its central frequency
\omega_{0}
, our model x-ray pulse features a bandwidth
\Omega=\sqrt{\smash{\tau_{c}}^{-2}+(2T)^{-2}}
that is largely determined by the longitudinal coherence time
\tau_{c}
and typically to a lesser extent by the average pulse length
T
.
Conversely, the spectral coherence width
\Omega_{c}=T^{-1}\sqrt{1+\tau_{c}^{2}(2T)^{-2}}
mostly reflects the overall pulse length.
Adding to the description from Ref.
Gorobtsov et al. 2017
, we include a phase-factor that accounts for the temporal center of the pulse being set to
t_{0}
.

In order to relate the above to
({Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{x\_in}})_{\sigma\rho}
—as needed of Eq. (
21
)—we have to perform three further steps.
First, we convert from a correlation function of electric fields to the analogous expression for vector potentials by means of their interrelation
{\mathbf{E}^{(+)}(\omega)}=i\,\alpha\,\omega\,{\mathbf{A}}_{\textsc{}}^{(+)}(\omega)
in frequency space.
Next, we Fourier transform the remaining real-space dependencies.
While this is straightforward for the transverse directions inside the source plane (
J(\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{x}^{\bot}_{2})\rightarrow\tilde{J}(\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{2})
), all information beyond
z_{i}=z_{0}
has to be obtained from longitudinal propagation of the correlation function first.
We achieve this by means of free-space propagation, which corresponds to the simplemost case of a virtually empty beamline and an ideally transparent sample
11
11

11

It should be noted that the propagation of light through the sample may have significant impact on the field distribution that ultimately drives XOWM. Notable effects include beam extinction through absorption or Bragg diffraction—for instance. In the present case, however, we can neglect either, as the diamond sample is only weakly absorbing and rocked away from its Bragg-condition
Glover et al. 2012
.
.
The action of an additional monochromator can be approximated in the spectral domain (cf. also Ref.
Gorobtsov et al. 2017
) using the transmission function
T(\omega)=\text{exp}[-(\omega-\omega_{m})^{2}(2\Omega_{m})^{-2}]
.
In a third step, we generalize the scalar correlator in order to account for the x-rays’ polarization.
For planar undulators as a source, the radiation generated on axis is linearily polarized (
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{X}
) with deviations being negligible as long as the divergence of the beam is small (
\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{X}}\right|/\left|\mathbf{k}_{X}\right|\ll 1
).
Implementing all of the above steps results in the correlation function:

\displaystyle({Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{x\_in}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\omega_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\omega_{2}))_{\sigma\rho}=Z_{0}\,({\bm{\epsilon}}_{X})_{\sigma}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{X})_{\rho}\,e^{i\,(k_{1}^{\shortparallel}-k_{2}^{\shortparallel})z_{0}}

\displaystyle\delta(k_{1}^{\shortparallel}-\kappa_{1})\delta(k_{2}^{\shortparallel}-\kappa_{2})\tilde{J}(\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{2})\,T(\omega_{1})T(\omega_{2})\,W(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(31)

Here, the prefactor
Z_{0}=\bar{E}_{\text{pulse}}\sqrt{2^{7}\pi^{5}}(\alpha\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\Omega\sigma^{2})^{-1}
can be related to the average pulse energy
\bar{E}_{\text{pulse}}
in front of the monochromator by integration, while the parameters
\kappa_{i}=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}\omega_{i}^{2}-\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{i}}\right|}^{2}}
reflect the vacuum dispersion of the beam.
Finally, we can simplify Eq. (
31
) slightly further by taking the paraxial approximation of the phase term

\displaystyle e^{i\,(k_{1}^{\shortparallel}-k_{2}^{\shortparallel})z_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx e^{i\,(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})\alpha z_{0}}\,\text{exp}{\left[i\,\Big(\frac{\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{2}}\right|}^{2}}{2\alpha\omega_{2}}-\frac{\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{1}}\right|}^{2}}{2\alpha\omega_{1}}\Big)z_{0}\right]}.

(32)

Again, this is well justified by the small beam divergence of typical undulator radiation.
Upon re-inserting this into
({Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{x\_in}})_{\sigma\rho}
, we find two phase terms now - each involving the frequency difference
\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
. Both are related to a choice of reference frame—specifically, the spatial (
z_{0}
) or temporal (
t_{0}
) distance of the radiation’s source from the respective origin. For simplicity, we center the spatial reference frame on the material sample, which fixes the upstream
z_{0}<0
.
Beyond this, we are free to choose the temporal origin such that
t_{0}=z_{0}/c
, whereby the respective phase terms cancel each other
12
12

12

Note that the absolute choice of
t=0
is arbitrary, but the
relative
timing of external fields has to be maintained, of course.
.

Optical field

Analogous to the x-ray dependence, also the optical admixture enters Eqs. (

(\bar{G}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)})_{\nu\mu}

(\bar{Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{opt}})_{\nu\mu}
for the externally driven case as well as
(\bar{S}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)})_{\nu\mu}
or
(\bar{C}^{(1)}_{\textsc{opt}})_{\nu\mu}
for any spontaneous contribution. Focusing on the (driven) case of SFG, we will concern ourselves only with modelling the first pair at this point

13

13

For a discussion of the spontaneous contribution,i.e., the field fluctuations captured by
(\bar{S}_{\textsc{opt}}^{(1)})_{\nu\mu}
or
(\bar{C}^{(1)}_{\textsc{opt}})_{\nu\mu}
, we refer the reader to Ref.
Boemer et al. 2021
.
. To this end, we start from the same expression as in the x-ray case, i.e., Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
). In a first adaptation, we account for the superior coherence properties of an optical laser compared to an x-ray FEL by taking both the transverse coherence length
\xi
and its temporal counterpart
\tau_{c}
to be practically infinite (
\xi\rightarrow\infty
,
\tau_{c}\rightarrow\infty
).
As a consequence, the correlation function factorizes into two electric fields of carrier frequency
\omega_{0}
featuring slowly varying envelopes

14
14

14

In contrast to Eq. (
18
), this particular model corresponds only to one negative and one positive frequency part of the field. As such, it is analogous to the conventional definition of Glauber’s coherence function
Glauber 1963
. This is nonetheless sufficient to describe the absorption of a photon during SFG and effectively corresponds to a rotating-wave approximation. For DFG—involving the stimulated emission of a photon—the opposite composition would obviously be required.
.
Spatially, the description reduces to a purely Gaussian beam in
\text{TEM}_{00}
mode.
Recalling the fact that Glover et al. employ a stretched, and in consequence chirped, pulse
(
Glover et al. 2012
)
, we augment each of the fields by a quadratic phase term
\text{exp}[\pm\text{i}\gamma_{0}(t_{i}-t_{0})^{2}]
.
This corresponds to a linear chirping of the pulse’s instantaneous frequency.

Subsequently, we follow the same steps as before and obtain the optical field’s correlation function

\displaystyle(\bar{Z}^{(1)}_{\textsc{opt}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\omega_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\omega_{2}))_{\nu\mu}=Z_{0}\,({\bm{\epsilon}}_{L})_{\nu}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{L})_{\mu}\,

\displaystyle\times\delta(k_{1}^{\shortparallel}-\kappa_{1})\delta(k_{2}^{\shortparallel}-\kappa_{2})\tilde{J}(\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{1},\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{2})\,W(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\,\gamma_{0}T^{2}(\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})(2\omega_{0}-\omega_{2}-\omega_{1})/\tilde{\Omega}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}{\left[i\,\Big(\frac{\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{2}}\right|}^{2}}{2\alpha n\omega_{2}}-\frac{\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{1}}\right|}^{2}}{2\alpha n\omega_{1}}\Big)z_{0}\right]},

(33)

where we have already implemented the paraxial approximation along the lines of Eq. (
32
). Most of the propagation phase is canceled by the choice of
t_{0}=z_{0}n/c+\Delta t
again. However, we account for the additional possibility of a time-delay
\Delta t
between x-ray and optical pulse. This translates into a remaining phase-term, which is incorporated in
W(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
.
Besides this, differences with respect to Eq. (
31
) comprise the Fourier transformed chirp-term, the omission of spectral filters
T(\omega)
and two changes in the definitions of the longitudinal wavevector
\kappa_{i}
and the prefactor
Z_{0}
. The wavevector should reflect the optical dispersion relation inside the sample and thus incorporates its index of refraction, i.e.,
\kappa_{i}=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}n^{2}\omega_{i}^{2}-\smash{\left|\smash{\mathbf{k}^{\bot}_{i}}\right|}^{2}}
.
For the prefactor, we use the new symbol
\tilde{\Omega}
to denote the involved bandwidth [cf.

III.1

In the future, this can be improved using higher-level simulations on the material part without changing the structure of the presented expressions. This flexibility, in turn, renders our approach suitable for comprehensive studies of SFG and related XOWM processes.

Going beyond previously observed features, we want to comment on a noteworthy aspect of Eq. (
38

In the present case, the relevant time-scale

\sim 2~\text{ps}

\sim 80~\text{fs}

In contrast, no viable SFG experiments could be conceived at present storage-ring based x-ray sources. Although these could provide similar photon flux on average, the energy per pulse is lower and typically spread over a longer time (
\sim 100~\text{ps}

Schröder et al. 2011

\sim 1~\text{MHz}

\sim 1~\text{mJ}
-level and thermally problematic for a sample to withstand.
Thus, laser driven x-ray optical wavemixing processes such as DFG and SFG appear to be ill-suited for storage-ring based x-ray sources and instead call for x-ray FELs in combination with synchronized optical laser systems to be successfully implemented.

V
Conclusion

Starting from first principles, viz. nonrelativistic QED, we have derived a description of XOWM in terms of a nonlinear x-ray scattering perspective. The framework allows us to treat x-ray optical SFG and DFG as well as XPDC on the same footing and reach quantitative predictions on their yields.
We have outlined routes to model all necessary correlation functions, upon which our expression rests, and combined them to benchmark the approach on prior experimental results. Studying SFG as of Ref.
Glover et al. 2012
, we have found our description to accurately reproduce the dependence of the nonlinear scattering probability in various parameter scans. Likewise, however, we have discovered shortcomings reproducing the overall SFG-yield. Attributing this to the inaccuracies of our DFT-based model for the material’s response function, we emphasize the need for higher-level simulations.
In particular, we stress the requirement of transverse current-responses—as could be accessible via TD-CDFT
Vignale 2006
, for instance.
Conversely, our framework may also be used to invert experimental data and thus gain access to high-resolution data on current-density-density response functions. These can, in turn, be used to complement theoretical developments—especially for highly correlated materials studied through the lens of XOWM.
