Mortar coupling of h ​ p hp -discontinuous Galerkin and boundary element methods for the Helmholtz equation
Christoph Erath, Lorenzo Mascotto, Jens M. Melenk, Ilaria Perugia, Alexander Rieder

Abstract
We design and analyze a coupling of a discontinuous Galerkin finite element method with a boundary element method to solve the Helmholtz equation with variable coefficients in three dimensions. The coupling is realized with a mortar variable that is related to an impedance trace on a smooth interface. The method obtained has a block structure with nonsingular subblocks. We prove quasi-optimality of the h h - and p p -versions of the scheme, under a threshold condition on the approximability properties of the discrete spaces. Amongst others, an essential tool in the analysis is a novel discontinuous-to-continuous reconstruction operator on tetrahedral meshes with curved faces. Keywords : discontinuous Galerkin method; boundary element method; mortar coupling; Helmholtz equation; variable sound speed

Introduction

A natural habitat of wave propagation problems are unbounded domains. An important class of numerical methods for this setting is the coupling of a
volume-based method such as the finite element method (FEM) or one of its variants for a finite, chosen computational domain
\Omega

and a boundary element method (BEM) for its unbounded exterior
\Omega^{\text{ext}}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. In this paper, we study such a coupling technique
for a time-harmonic acoustic scattering problem modelled by the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and given by

-\operatorname{div}(\nu\nabla u)-(kn)^{2}u=f\quad\mbox{ in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$},

(1.1)

where the coefficients
\nu
and
n
are constant outside a sufficiently large ball,
k
denotes the wave number, and
f
is the right-hand side.
Our focus is on a strategy that couples a high order discontinuous Galerkin finite element method (DGFEM) in the computational domain
\Omega
with a BEM on
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
to account for
\Omega^{\text{ext}}
.
We consider approximation spaces made of piecewise polynomial functions.

The present work is a continuation of the recent work
[
37
]
, where the coupling of a
conforming, high order FEM with a BEM is presented and analyzed. The coupling there is reminiscent of the symmetric coupling proposed
in
[
14
]
and
[
30
]
for Poisson-type problems but uses an additional mortar variable that
has the physical meaning of a Robin trace for incoming waves. A feature of the mortar-based coupling is that
the resulting system has a block structure where the two blocks
corresponding to the volume and to the BEM unknowns, respectively,
are individually invertible. This allows for the use of existing discretization techniques for these blocks. Other FEM-BEM coupling strategies for Helmholtz
problems are possible and are discussed in
[
37
]
. In contrast to the conforming setting of
[
37
]
,
our focus here is on a DGFEM for the discretization in
\Omega
, since the DGFEM has proved to be a very versatile discretization technique that can accommodate very well, for example,
high order discretizations. High order methods are particularly suited for wave propagation problems
[
4
,
43
,
44
,
41
,
36
]
.
Further well known advantages of DG discretizations include the ease to realize adaptivity and accommodate nonuniform polynomial degree distributions.
Moreover, DG formulations for Helmholtz problems in bounded domains have the potential to be unconditionally well posed
[
26
,
22
]
.
We refer, e.g., to
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
29
,
41
,
17
,
52
]
for polynomial-based DG methods for the
Helmholtz problem,
to
[
28
,
10
,
15
]
for hybridized DG (HDG) methods, and to
[
16
,
27
]
for
discontinuous Petrov-Galerkin (DPG) methods.

For Poisson-type problems, couplings of several variants of the DGFEM with the BEM have been proposed and analyzed. The first analysis appears to be that of the symmetric coupling
of the local DG (LDG) method with BEM in
[
25
,
8
,
24
]
. Generalizations to nonsymmetric couplings have been proposed in
[
46
]
and
analyzed in
[
31
]
. The closely related coupling of finite volume methods with BEM is analyzed in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
A fairly general framework that uses mortar variables for coupling the DGFEM and the BEM can be found in
[
12
,
11
]
.
In the limit
k\rightarrow 0
, which is not the focus of the present work, our method for (
1.1
) has similarities with those of
[
25
,
19
]
for the Laplace equation.

On a technical side, a main difficulty of the analysis of couplings of DG with BEM arises from the mapping properties of BEM operators that do not
easily accommodate the discontinuous traces of DG functions. This is one of the reasons for using mortar variables for the coupling both for Poisson-type problems
discussed above and the present Helmholtz equation. In our analysis, we tackle this issue with a new discontinuous-to-continuous operator in Theorem
4.4
.
From the many possible DG variants, we opted for an interior penalty-like DG method to keep the presentation as simple as possible, although other DG discretizations could be analyzed with similar techniques.
Following the lead in
[
37
]
, we employ a form of symmetric coupling using all four BEM operators. The sesquilinear forms are carefully designed
to ensure both consistency and adjoint consistency. In particular, as compared to
[
37
]
, the discretization
of the coupling condition required us to introduce an additional (consistent) term in order to prove a discrete Gårding inequality.

Notation.

For bounded Lipschitz domains
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, we introduce the following norms and spaces:
For integers
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and complex-valued functions
v
, we define the norms
\|v\|^{2}_{s,D}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}\colon|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq s}\|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}v\|^{2}_{0,D}

and the seminorms
|v|^{2}_{s,D}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}\colon|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=s}\|D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}v\|^{2}_{0,D}
.
The Hilbert spaces
H^{s}(D)
and
H^{s}_{0}(D)
are defined as the closure of
C^{\infty}(D)
and
C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{s,D}
.
We further set
H^{0}(D):=L^{2}(D)
.
For a noninteger
s>0
, the spaces
H^{s}(D)
and
H^{s}_{0}(D)

are defined by interpolation between
H^{\lfloor s\rfloor}(D)

and
H^{\lceil s\rceil}(D)

and between
H^{\lfloor s\rfloor}_{0}(D)
and
H^{\lceil s\rceil}_{0}(D)
,
respectively.
For
s>0
, the space
H^{-s}(D)
is defined as the dual of
H^{s}_{0}(D)
with norm

\|v\|_{-s,D}:=\sup_{w\in H^{s}_{0}(D)}\frac{|\langle v,w\rangle|}{\|w\|_{s,D}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality pairing, which coincides with the
L^{2}
inner product whenever both
v,w\in L^{2}(D)
.
The inner product in
H^{s}(D)
, denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{s,D}
, is linear in the first argument and antilinear in the second argument.

For closed, connected, smooth 2-dimensional surfaces
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
s\geq 0
,
we define the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Gamma)
as follows.
Let
\{\varphi_{n},\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of eigenpairs of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Gamma
, so that
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Gamma)
.
For
s\geq 0
, we define the norm
\|v\|^{2}_{s,\Gamma}:=\sum_{n}|v_{n}|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n})^{s}
, where
v=\sum_{n}v_{n}\varphi_{n}
is expanded in the basis
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
.
Then,
H^{s}(\Gamma):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Gamma):\|v\|_{s,\Gamma}<+\infty\}
.
The
\|\cdot\|_{s,\Gamma}
norm is equivalent to the one obtained by using local charts as described in
[
38
]
; see also
[
45
, Sec. 5.4]
.
The mapping
v\mapsto(v_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, with
v_{n}=(v,\varphi_{n})_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
, is an isometric isomorphism between
H^{s}(\Gamma)
and the sequence space
\{(v_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\,|\,\sum_{n}|v_{n}|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n})^{s}<\infty\}
.
Negative order Sobolev spaces are defined by duality and equipped with the norm

\|v\|_{-s,\Gamma}:=\sup_{w\in H^{s}(\Gamma)}\frac{|\langle v,w\rangle|}{\|w\|_{s,\Gamma}}.

Moreover,
H^{-s}(\Gamma)
is equivalent to the space
\{(v_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\,|\,\sum_{n}|v_{n}|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n})^{-s}<\infty\}
,
endowed with the norm
\|v\|^{2}_{-s,\Gamma}=\sum_{n}|v_{n}|^{2}(1+\lambda_{n})^{-s}
.
When identifying the spaces
H^{s}(\Gamma)
and
H^{-s}(\Gamma)
with
sequence spaces as above, the duality pairing takes the form

\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_{n}v_{n}\overline{w_{n}},

and, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the inner product in
H^{s}(\Gamma)
takes the form

(v,w)_{s,\Gamma}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}v_{n}\overline{w_{n}}(1+\lambda_{n})^{s}.

The seminorm
|\cdot|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}
in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
is defined by

|v|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}=\inf_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\|v-c\|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}
.

Let
s
and
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
.
For a bounded linear operator
K:H^{s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s^{\prime}}(\Gamma)
,
the adjoint operator
K^{\ast}:H^{-s^{\prime}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{-s}(\Gamma)

is defined by
\langle v,K^{\ast}w\rangle=\langle Kv,w\rangle
, where
the duality pairings are between the appropriate
spaces. This also implies
\langle w,Kv\rangle=\langle K^{\ast}w,v\rangle
.

As we deal with the Helmholtz problem, we also introduce the following

k
-weighted Sobolev norms for integers
s\geq 1
on domains
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\|v\|_{s,k,D}^{2}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}\colon|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq s}k^{2(s-|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|)}\|D^{\alpha}v\|^{2}_{0,D}.

Finally, given
a
,
b\geq 0
, we write
a\lesssim b

and
a\gtrsim b
to indicate the existence
of a positive constant
c
, whose dependence is specified
at each occurrence, such that
a\leq c\,b
and
a\geq c\,b
, respectively.

2
Helmholtz model problem, boundary integral operators, and mortar coupling

In this section, we describe the model problem, see Section
2.1
, and present its continuous mortar formulation in Section
2.3
.
The setting is the same as that of
[
37
, Secs. 2 and 3]
.
In order to make this paper self-contained, we report here all the necessary elements, including the definitions and some properties of the boundary integral operators for the 3D Helmholtz problem;
see Section
2.2
.

2.1
Helmholtz model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded domain with a connected
C^{\infty}
-smooth
boundary
\Gamma
and
\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}
be the outward pointing unit vector normal to
\Gamma
.
Denote
\Omega^{\text{ext}}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and
let
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
k\geq k_{0}>0
, denote the wave number.

We assume that
\Omega^{\text{ext}}
is occupied by a homogeneous medium with
both the refractive index
n\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{C})
and a scalar-valued positive diffusion coefficient
\nu\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R})

normalized to
1
, while
n
and
\nu
may vary within
\Omega
.
That is, the supports of
1-n
and
1-\nu
are contained
in
\Omega
and
\nu
satisfies

0<\nu_{\text{min}}\leq\nu(\mathbf{x})\leq\nu_{\text{max}}<+\infty
.
In addition, we assume that
|n(\mathbf{x})|\geq c_{0}>0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with support contained in
\Omega
, we set

\widetilde{f}=\begin{cases}f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
0&\text{in }\Omega^{\text{ext}}.\\
\end{cases}

Under the above assumptions on
n
,
\nu
, and
\widetilde{f}
, there exists an open
neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
such that

n\equiv 1
,

\nu\equiv 1
, and

\widetilde{f}\equiv 0

in
\Omega^{\text{ext}}\cup\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
.

We consider the Helmholtz problem: Find
u:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\begin{cases}-\operatorname{div}(\nu\nabla u)-(kn)^{2}u=\widetilde{f}&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\displaystyle{\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow+\infty}|\mathbf{x}|}(\partial_{|\mathbf{x}|}u-\text{i}ku)=0.&\end{cases}

(2.1)

We rewrite problem (
2.1
) as a transmission problem. To that end, we define the following jump operators.
For
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma)
, we denote the Dirichlet traces of
v_{|\Omega}
and
v_{|\Omega^{\text{ext}}}
on
\Gamma

by
\gamma_{0}^{int}(v)
and
\gamma_{0}^{ext}(v)
, respectively.
The two Neumann traces
(\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}=\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla)

on
\Gamma
of a piecewise smooth function
v
are denoted by
\gamma_{1}^{int}(\varphi)
and
\gamma_{1}^{ext}(\varphi)
.
For sufficiently smooth functions
v
defined in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma
, we then define the jumps

\llbracket v\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=\gamma_{0}^{int}(v)-\gamma_{0}^{ext}(v),\quad\quad\quad\llbracket\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}u\rrbracket_{\Gamma}:=\gamma_{1}^{int}(v)-\gamma_{1}^{ext}(v).

With these jumps in hand, we reformulate (
2.1
) as
looking for solutions
u:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the following transmission problem:

\begin{cases}-\operatorname{div}(\nu\nabla u)-(kn)^{2}u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
-\Delta u-k^{2}u=0&\text{in }\Omega^{\text{ext}},\\
\llbracket u\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=0,\;\llbracket\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}u\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=0,\\
\displaystyle{\lim_{|\mathbf{x}|\rightarrow+\infty}|\mathbf{x}|}(\partial_{|\mathbf{x}|}u-\text{i}ku)=0.&\end{cases}

(2.2)

Here, we required the boundary
\Gamma
to be globally smooth, whereas in Section
3
below we allow for a piecewise smooth
\Gamma
.
The global smoothness assumption is needed to promote the regularity of the solution to
problem (
2.2
) below, while the piecewise smoothness assumption is enough for the design of the method.

2.2
Boundary integral operators

The fundamental solution to the 3D Helmholtz problem is

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{e^{\text{i}k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}.

Based on that, we define the single and double layer potentials as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{k}\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Gamma}G_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\varphi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma,\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{y})}G_{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\varphi(\mathbf{y})ds(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma,\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

Starting from the potentials
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{k}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{k}
, we introduce the four standard boundary integral operators for the Helmholtz operator.
Their properties are widely studied in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
47
,
51
,
13
,
38
]
and the references therein.
The properties mentioned below have also been summarized in
[
37
]
.

Single layer operator.

Define
\mathcal{V}_{k}:H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
as

\mathcal{V}_{k}\varphi:=\gamma_{0}^{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{k}\varphi)\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

(2.3)

For
C^{\infty}
-smooth
\Gamma
, the operator
\mathcal{V}_{k}
extends to
\mathcal{V}_{k}:H^{-1+s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s}(\Gamma)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Double layer operator.

Define
\mathcal{K}_{k}:H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
as

\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{k}\right)\varphi:=\gamma_{0}^{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\varphi)\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

For
C^{\infty}
-smooth
\Gamma
, the operator
\mathcal{K}_{k}
extends to
\mathcal{K}_{k}:H^{s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s}(\Gamma)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Adjoint double layer operator.

Define
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}:H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
as

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}\right)\varphi:=\gamma_{1}^{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{k}\varphi)\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

For
C^{\infty}
-smooth
\Gamma
, the operator
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}
extends to
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}:H^{-s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{-s}(\Gamma)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Hypersingular boundary integral operator.

Define
\mathcal{W}_{k}:H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
as

-\mathcal{W}_{k}\varphi:=\gamma_{1}^{int}(\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{k}\varphi)\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).

For
C^{\infty}
-smooth
\Gamma
, the operator
\mathcal{W}_{k}
extends to
\mathcal{W}_{k}:H^{s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{-1+s}(\Gamma)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}
,
\mathcal{K}_{0}
,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{W}_{0}
be the corresponding integral operators for zero
wave number
k=0
.
Then, for all
s\geq 0
, the difference operators are linear bounded operators in the following spaces

\begin{split}&\mathcal{V}_{k}-\mathcal{V}_{0}:H^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{5}{2}+s}(\Gamma),\quad\quad\mathcal{K}_{k}-\mathcal{K}_{0}:H^{\frac{1}{2}+s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{5}{2}+s}(\Gamma),\\
&\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}-\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{0}:H^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{3}{2}+s}(\Gamma),\quad\quad\mathcal{W}_{k}-\mathcal{W}_{0}:H^{\frac{1}{2}+s}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{3}{2}+s}(\Gamma).\end{split}

(2.4)

In other words, the difference operators possess enhanced shift properties with respect to those of each term in the difference;
see, e.g.,
[
37
, Prop. 2.2]
and
[
38
, Thm. 7.2]
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{W}_{0}

satisfy the following properties: there exist positive constants
c_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}
,

C_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}
,
c_{\mathcal{W}_{0}}
, and
C_{\mathcal{W}_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\varphi,\mathcal{V}_{0}\varphi\rangle\leq C_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),

(2.5)

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{W}_{0}}|\varphi|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\mathcal{W}_{0}\varphi,\varphi\rangle\leq C_{\mathcal{W}_{0}}|\varphi|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)/\mathbb{C}.

We also have the following properties:

\mathcal{V}_{0}^{*}=\mathcal{V}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{0}^{*}=\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{W}_{0}^{*}=\mathcal{W}_{0}.

2.3
Mortar coupling

In this section, we recall the mortar coupling described in
[
37
]
.
Instead of looking for solutions to (
2.2
), we aim to solve the following three coupled problems for
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
u^{ext}
,
m:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\operatorname{div}(\nu\nabla u)-(kn)^{2}u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}u+iku-m=0&\text{on }\Gamma,\\
\end{cases}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u^{ext}=\mathcal{P}_{ItD}m&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\text{on }\Gamma\end{cases},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u-\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{k}\right)u^{ext}-\mathcal{V}_{k}(m-iku^{ext})\right]=0.\end{cases}

(2.8)

The operator
\mathcal{P}_{ItD}:H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
appearing in (
2.7
)
maps the impedance mortar variable
m
to the Dirichlet trace
u^{ext}

of the solution to the exterior problem. This operator was defined, e.g., in
[
9
, pp. 124–126]
.
In order to characterize it explicitly, we introduce the combined integral operators

\mathcal{B}_{k}:=-\mathcal{W}_{k}-ik\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\right),\quad\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}+ik\mathcal{V}_{k}

(2.9)

and recall their mapping properties, see, e.g.,
[
9
, Thm. 2.27]
:

\mathcal{B}_{k}:H^{s+\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),\quad\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}:H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)

are bounded.
Then, equation (
2.7
) is equivalent to

\mathcal{B}_{k}u^{ext}+ik\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}(u^{ext})-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}m=0;

(2.10)

see
[
37
, Prop. 3.2]
and the references therein.

The variational formulation of
problem (
2.6
)–(
2.8
)
reads as follows:

\begin{cases}\text{Find }(u,m,u^{ext})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\text{ such that}&\\
(\nu\nabla u,\nabla v)_{0,\Omega}-((kn)^{2}\,u,v)_{0,\Omega}+i(ku,v)_{0,\Gamma}-\langle m,v\rangle=(f,v)_{0,\Omega}&\forall v\in H^{1}(\Omega),\\
\langle(\mathcal{B}_{k}+ik\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k})u^{ext}-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}m,v^{ext}\rangle=0&\forall v^{ext}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma),\\
\langle u,\lambda\rangle-\langle(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{k})u^{ext}-\mathcal{V}_{k}(m-iku^{ext}),\lambda\rangle=0&\forall\lambda\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).\end{cases}

(2.11)

As in
[
37
]
, we introduce

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(&(u,m,u^{ext}),(v,\lambda,v^{ext}))=(\nu\nabla u,\nabla v)_{0,\Omega}-((kn)^{2}\,u,v)_{0,\Omega}+ik(u,v)_{0,\Gamma}-\langle m,v\rangle\\
&-\langle(-\mathcal{W}_{k}-ik(\frac{1}{2}-\mathcal{K}_{k})+ik(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}+ik\mathcal{V}_{k}))u^{ext}\\
&-(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{k}+ik\mathcal{V}_{k})m,v^{ext}\rangle+\langle u,\lambda\rangle-\langle(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{k})u^{ext}-\mathcal{V}_{k}(m-iku^{ext}),\lambda\rangle.\\
\end{split}

(2.12)

Then, we can rewrite problem (
2.11
) in compact form:

\begin{cases}\text{Find }(u,m,u^{ext})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\text{ such that}\\
\mathcal{T}((u,m,u^{ext}),(v,\lambda,v^{ext}))=(f,v)_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall(v,\lambda,v^{ext})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma).\end{cases}

3.1
DGFEM discretization of (
2.6
)

We shall work with regular, shape regular meshes of the (curved) domain
\Omega
.
That is, the meshes will have no hanging nodes and the parametrizations of common edges or faces
induced by the element maps of neighboring elements match; see
[
35
, Def. 2.2]
for
the precise statement.

As in
[
35
, Def. 2.2]
, we define a
curved

d
-simplex

K
,
d=2,3
, as the image of a reference straight

d
-simplex
\widehat{K}
through a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism

\Phi_{K}
satisfying

\|D\Phi_{K}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\leq\gamma_{SR}h_{K},\qquad\|(D\Phi_{K})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\leq\gamma_{SR}h_{K}^{-1},

(3.1)

where
D\cdot
denotes the Jacobian and
\gamma_{SR}>0
is the shape regularity constant.

Condition (
3.1
) implies that
\Phi_{K}
can be decomposed as

\Phi_{K}=\Phi_{K}^{\Delta}+\Psi_{K}
, where
\Phi_{K}^{\Delta}
is an affine bijection and

\Psi_{K}
is a
C^{1}
mapping such that

c_{K}:=\sup_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\widehat{K}}\|D\Psi_{K}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\cdot(D\Phi_{K}^{\Delta})^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{K})}\lesssim 1.

(3.2)

To see (
3.2
), it is enough to fix any
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\in\widehat{K}

and take
\Phi_{K}^{\Delta}:=\Phi_{K}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0})+D\Phi_{K}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0})(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0})
. This
gives (
3.2
) with
\gamma_{SR}^{2}+1
on the right-hand side.
A face
F
of a curved 3-simplex
K
is the image through
\Phi_{K}
of
a face
\widehat{F}
of
\widehat{K}
.

Let
\{\Omega_{h}\}_{h}
be a sequence of conforming, i.e., regular in the sense described above, decompositions of
\Omega
into curved 3-simplices
with mesh granularity
h
. For
h
sufficiently small,
c_{K}\leq c<1
for all
K\in\Omega_{h}
.
The union of the (open) internal and boundary faces of
\Omega_{h}
are denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}

and
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}
, respectively.
We assume that all the faces in
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}
are flat. The faces in
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}
are curved 2-simplices.

Given an element
K\in\Omega_{h}
, denote its diameter by
h_{K}
and the outward pointing unit vector normal to
\partial K
by
\mathbf{n}_{K}
.
We introduce the mesh size function
{\mathfrak{h}}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
,
where
{\mathfrak{h}}_{|_{K}}=h_{K}
for all
K\in\Omega_{h}
,
{\mathfrak{h}}=\min\{h_{K_{1}},h_{K_{2}}\}
on each face in
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}
shared by
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, and
{\mathfrak{h}}=h_{K}
on each face in
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}
on
\partial\Omega
.
We may fix
{\mathfrak{h}}
arbitrarily at mesh vertices and on edges because we shall not need it there.

To derive the DG formulation, we write the first equation of (
2.6
) in mixed form:

\begin{cases}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\nu\,\nabla u\\
-\text{i}k\operatorname{div}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})-(kn)^{2}u=f\end{cases}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

On each element
K\in\Omega_{h}
, we multiply the above two
equations by smooth functions
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
v
, respectively, and
integrate by parts:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\int_{K}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\int_{K}u\,\overline{{\operatorname{div}(\nu\,\boldsymbol{\tau})}}-\int_{\partial K}\nu\,u\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\tau}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}}=0},\\[5.69046pt]
\displaystyle{\int_{K}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}-\int_{\partial K}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}\,\overline{v}-\int_{K}(kn)^{2}u\,\overline{v}=\int_{K}f\,\overline{v}}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

We replace the traces of
u
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}
in the integral on
\partial K
with suitable numerical fluxes
\widehat{u}

and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}
, respectively, which will be defined later on in (
3.7
).
Thus, we replace
u_{|\partial K}
with
\widehat{u}_{|\partial K}
in
the first equation of (
3.3
), apply one more
integration by parts, select
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\nabla v
, and end up with

\int_{K}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}-\int_{K}\nu\,\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}+\int_{\partial K}\nu\,(u-\widehat{u})\,\overline{\nabla v\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}}=0.

(3.4)

Next, we replace
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}{}_{|\partial K}

with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}{}_{|\partial K}
in the second equation
of (
3.3
), and obtain

\int_{K}\text{i}k\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}-\int_{\partial K}\text{i}k\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}\overline{v}-\int_{K}(kn)^{2}u\,\overline{v}=\int_{K}f\,\overline{v}.

(3.5)

Subtracting (
3.4
)
from (
3.5
) and adding over all
K\in\Omega_{h}

lead to the following broken variational formulation:

\begin{cases}\text{Find }u\in H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\text{ such that for all }v\in H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\\[5.69046pt]
\displaystyle{\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}}\left(\int_{K}\nu\,\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v}-\int_{\partial K}\nu\,(u-\widehat{u})\,\overline{\nabla v\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}}\right)-\int_{\Gamma}\text{i}k\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v}-\int_{\Omega}(kn)^{2}u\,\overline{v}=\int_{\Omega}f\,\overline{v},\end{cases}

(3.6)

where

H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\,v{}_{|K}\in H^{1}(K)\ \forall K\in\Omega_{h}\}.

In order to complete the definition of the DGFEM method, we need to
choose finite dimensional subspaces of
H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})
and define the numerical fluxes.

To that end, we introduce the following notation for spaces of mapped, piecewise polynomial functions of finite degree.
Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an open, bounded Lipschitz domain with piecewise
C^{\infty}
-smooth boundary, and
D_{h}
a partition of

into curved simplices with flat internal faces.
Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and denote by

the space of
polynomials of degree at most
\ell
on the domain within the brackets. For
\ell\geq 1
and

, we set

\mathcal{S}^{\ell,r}(D,D_{h})=\{v\in H^{r}(D):\,v_{|_{K}}\circ\Phi_{K}\in\mathbb{P}_{\ell}(\widehat{K})\quad\forall K\in D_{h}\}.

For later use, we also define mapped, piecewise polynomial spaces on
surface meshes. To that end, we assume that
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a
closed, piecewise
C^{\infty}
-smooth surface and let
S_{h}
be a partition of

S
into curved 2-simplices, which is the trace of a partition
D_{h}
of
its interior
D
as above. For
\ell\geq 1
and
r=0,1
, we set

\mathcal{S}^{\ell,r}(S,S_{h})=\{v\in H^{r}(S):\,v_{|_{F}}\circ\Phi_{K_{F}}\in\mathbb{P}_{\ell}(\widehat{F})\quad\forall F\in S_{h}\},

where
K_{F}
is the element of

with

as a face.

As for the DGFEM discretization of (
3.6
),
we choose discretization spaces made by discontinuous piecewise polynomial functions:

V_{h}:=\mathcal{S}^{p,0}(\Omega,\Omega_{h}),

and the numerical fluxes introduced in

.
We recall their definition in the case of a smooth coefficient

.
We first introduce the following notation for the jump and the average functionals on

for smooth, scalar functions
v
and vector-valued functions
\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
At any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}
shared by the two elements
K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}

and
K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}
, the jumps
\llbracket v\rrbracket
and
\llbracket\boldsymbol{\tau}\rrbracket
,
and the averages
\{\!\!\{v\}\!\!\}
and

are defined as

\begin{split}&\llbracket v\rrbracket(\mathbf{x}):=v_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}+v_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_{K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}},\quad\quad\{\!\!\{v\}\!\!\}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}(v_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}(\mathbf{x})+v_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}}(\mathbf{x})),\\
&\llbracket\boldsymbol{\tau}\rrbracket(\mathbf{x}):=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}},\quad\quad\{\!\!\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\}\!\!\}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{1}}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{|K_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}}(\mathbf{x})).\end{split}

Then, given functions

in

and

\begin{split}\text{i}k\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\text{i}k\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(u)&:=\begin{cases}\nu\{\!\!\{\nabla_{h}u\}\!\!\}-\text{i}k\,\nu\alpha\,\llbracket u\rrbracket&\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I},\\
\nabla_{h}u-(1-\delta)(\nabla_{h}u+\text{i}k\,u\,\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}-m\,\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma})&\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B},\\
\end{cases}\\
\widehat{u}=\widehat{u}(u)&:=\begin{cases}\{\!\!\{u\}\!\!\}-(\text{i}k)^{-1}\beta\,\llbracket\nabla_{h}u\rrbracket&\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I},\\
u+\delta(-(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}-u+(\text{i}k)^{-1}m)&\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B},\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

(3.7)

where
\nabla_{h}
denotes the elementwise application of the gradient
operator, and
we recall that

denotes the impedance boundary datum in (
2.6
).

Given positive constants
\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}
, and
\mathfrak{d}
, with
\mathfrak{a}
sufficiently large, see Remark
4.1
below, and
\mathfrak{b}
and

\begin{split}\alpha(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{a}\,&\frac{p^{2}}{k{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbf{x})},\qquad\beta(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{b}\,\frac{k{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbf{x})}{p}\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I},\quad\delta(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{d}\frac{k{\mathfrak{h}}(\mathbf{x})}{p^{2}}\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}.\end{split}

(3.8)

The assumption

implies

.

The fluxes defined in (

). Furthermore, they

\text{i}k\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+\text{i}k\,\widehat{u}=m\quad\quad\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}.

In the error analysis, we deal with

.
Thus, for

, we introduce the broken Sobolev spaces on

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{r}(\Omega_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\,v{}_{|K}\in H^{r}(K)\ \forall K\in\Omega_{h}\}.

We also define the following two DG norms, which will be used in the analysis:
Given

, with

arbitrarily small, we define

\begin{split}\|v\|^{2}_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}&:=\|\nu^{1/2}\,\nabla_{h}v\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|kn\,v\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k^{-1}\|\nu^{1/2}\beta^{1/2}\llbracket\nabla_{h}v\rrbracket\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}+k\|\nu^{1/2}\alpha^{1/2}\llbracket v\rrbracket\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\\

(3.9)

and

\begin{split}\|v\|^{2}_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}:=\|v\|^{2}_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}+k^{-1}\|\nu^{1/2}\alpha^{-1/2}\{\!\!\{\nabla_{h}v\}\!\!\}\|^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}.\end{split}

In Section

BEM discretization of (

On

and

As already mentioned, for (

for

and

, respectively.

Next, we focus on the discretization of (

),
whose variational formulation is given by
the third equation in (

).
Compared to what was done in
[
37
]
, we add suitable terms that will allow us to prove a discrete Gårding inequality, see Theorem

below,
and retain consistency and adjoint consistency, see Lemma

To that end, it is convenient to write the integral terms on
\Gamma
appearing in the DGFEM discretization in the interior domain

Using the definition of the numerical fluxes (
3.7
) on
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{B}
, we write

\begin{split}&-\int_{\Gamma}(u_{h}-\widehat{u}(u_{h}))\overline{\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\text{i}k\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(u_{h})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{h}}\\
&=-\int_{\Gamma}-\delta(-(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}-u_{h}+(\text{i}k)^{-1}m_{h})\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}\\
&\quad-\int_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}-(1-\delta)(\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+\text{i}ku_{h}-m_{h}))\overline{v_{h}}\\
&=-\int_{\Gamma}\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\delta u_{h}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}+\int_{\Gamma}\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}m_{h}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}\\
&\quad-\int_{\Gamma}\delta\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{h}}+\int_{\Gamma}(1-\delta)\text{i}ku_{h}\overline{v_{h}}-\int_{\Gamma}(1-\delta)m_{h}\overline{v_{h}}.\end{split}

Therefore, the contribution from the interior discretization to the coupling, i.e., the terms involving

\begin{split}\int_{\Gamma}\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}m_{h}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}(1-\delta)m_{h}\overline{v_{h}}&=-\int_{\Gamma}m_{h}(\overline{\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+(1-\delta)v_{h}})\\

\end{split}

(3.10)

(3.11)

Complete discrete formulation

On

\begin{split}a^{K}_{h}(u_{h},v_{h}):=&\int_{K}\nu\,\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}}-\int_{K}(kn)^{2}u_{h}\overline{v_{h}}\\
&-\sum_{F\subset\partial K\cap\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\left(\int_{F}\nu\,(u_{h}-\widehat{u}(u_{h}))\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}}+\int_{F}\text{i}k\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(u_{h})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K}\overline{v_{h}}\right),\end{split}

with fluxes
\widehat{u}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
as

),

\begin{split}b_{h}^{\Gamma}(u_{h},v_{h})&:=-\int_{\Gamma}\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\delta u_{h}\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}\\

\langle-\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+(1-\delta)u_{h}+\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}m_{h},\lambda_{h}\rangle-\langle X_{h},\lambda_{h}\rangle=0,\\
\end{cases}

(3.12)

where the combined integral operators

and

\begin{split}&\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}a^{K}_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})=\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\left(\int_{K}\nu\,\nabla u_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{h}}-\int_{K}(kn)^{2}u_{h}\overline{v_{h}}\right)\\
&-\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\nu\left(\llbracket u_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\{\!\!\{\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}}\}\!\!\}+\{\!\!\{\nabla_{h}u_{h}\}\!\!\}\cdot\llbracket\overline{v_{h}}\rrbracket\right)-\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\nu\beta(ik)^{-1}\llbracket\nabla_{h}u_{h}\rrbracket\llbracket\overline{\nabla_{h}v_{h}}\rrbracket+\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\nu\alpha\,ik\llbracket u_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\overline{v_{h}}\rrbracket.\end{split}

\Psi_{h}:=(\psi_{h},\psi_{mh},\psi_{h}^{ext})\in V_{h}\times W_{h}\times Z_{h}
to the right-hand side,
and apply the continuity estimate (
5.1
) (the
first argument in the second term is discrete):

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\|kn(u-u_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}&+c_{G}(k)\left(\|m-m_{h}\|^{2}_{-\frac{3}{2},\Gamma}+\|u^{ext}-u^{ext}_{h}\|^{2}_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}\right)\\
&\quad=\mathcal{T}_{h}(x-x_{h},\Psi-\Psi_{h})=\mathcal{T}_{h}(x-y_{h},\Psi-\Psi_{h})+\mathcal{T}_{h}(y_{h}-x_{h},\Psi-\Psi_{h})\\
&\quad\lesssim\big(\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}+\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}\big)\left|\!\left|\!\left|\Psi-\Psi_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(7.5)

From (
6.8
), we see that

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{2,\Omega}+\|\psi_{m}\|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}+\|\psi^{ext}\|_{\frac{3}{2},\Gamma}\lesssim\|u-u_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|m-m_{h}\|_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}+\|u^{ext}-u^{ext}_{h}\|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}.

This, together with Assumption
7.1
, yields

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\Psi-\Psi_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\big(\|\psi\|_{2,\Omega}+\|\psi_{m}\|_{\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}+\|\psi^{ext}\|_{\frac{3}{2},\Gamma}\big)\lesssim\varepsilon\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-x_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}.

We insert this bound into (
7.5
) and merge the resulting bound with (
7.4
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\|kn(v_{h}-u_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}&+c_{G}(k)\left(\|\lambda_{h}-m_{h}\|^{2}_{-\frac{3}{2},\Gamma}+\|v_{h}^{\text{ext}}-u^{ext}_{h}\|^{2}_{-\frac{1}{2},\Gamma}\right)\\
&\lesssim\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}^{2}+(\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}+\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)})\varepsilon\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-x_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}\\
&\lesssim(1+\varepsilon)\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}^{2}.\end{split}

(7.6)

Eventually, we insert (
7.3
) and (
7.6
) in (
7.2
) and, writing
c
for the constant implied in all the previous estimates, we get

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\big(\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}+1+\varepsilon\big)\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}^{2}+c\big(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon\big)\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Assuming that
\varepsilon
in Assumption
7.1
is sufficiently small
and taking
\varepsilon_{1}
small enough, we shift the second term to the left-hand side:

(1-c(\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{1}))\left|\!\left|\!\left|x_{h}-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\left|\!\left|\!\left|x-y_{h}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{\text{DG}^{+}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(7.7)

Inserting (
7.7
) in (
7.1
) concludes the proof.

9
Conclusions

We introduced a DGFEM-BEM mortar coupling for three dimensional Helmholtz problems
with variable coefficients.
Upon showing that the discrete sesquilinear form satisfies a Grding inequality and continuity bounds,
we showed quasi-optimality of the
h
- and
p
-versions of the scheme.
The theoretical results are validated by numerical examples.
Notably, theoretical and numerical results are valid regardless of whether the wave number is a Dirichlet or Neumann Laplace eigenvalue.

As a pivot result of independent interest, we constructed a discontinuous-to-continuous reconstruction operator on tetrahedral meshes, with optimal
h
- and
p
-stability properties in the
H^{1}
seminorm and in the
L^{2}
norm, covering the case of curvilinear meshes.

Appendix A
Consistency of method (
3.12
)

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Proving assertion (
3.15
) is equivalent to proving that
the continuous solution
(u,m,u^{ext})
solves also the three equations in (
3.12
).
Since
u\in H^{\frac{3}{2}+t}(\Omega)
we have that

\displaystyle\llbracket u\rrbracket=0,\qquad\llbracket\nabla u\rrbracket=0,\qquad\{\!\!\{\nabla u\}\!\!\}=\nabla u\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}.

(A.1)

We multiply (
2.6
) by
\overline{v_{h}}\in V_{h}
and integrate elementwise by parts to get

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\left(-\int_{\partial K}\nu\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{h}}+\int_{K}\nu\,\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{h}}\right)-\int_{\Omega}(kn)^{2}u\overline{v_{h}}=\int_{\Omega}f\overline{v_{h}}.

With the aid of the boundary condition in (
2.6
),
inserting the parameter
\delta
, and using the fact that
\nu=1
on
\Gamma
,
we manipulate the boundary term as follows:

\displaystyle-\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}\int_{\partial K}\nu\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{h}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma}\delta\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{v_{h}}-\int_{\Gamma}(1-\delta)m\overline{v_{h}}+\int_{\Gamma}\text{i}k(1-\delta)u\overline{v_{h}}-\int_{\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}\nu\nabla u\cdot\llbracket\overline{v_{h}}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Gamma}(\text{i}k)^{-1}\delta m\overline{\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}(\text{i}k)^{-1}\delta\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}\overline{\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}-\int_{\Gamma}\delta u\overline{\nabla v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}}.

Properties (
A.1
) and the above identity lead to the consistency of the first equation of (
3.12
), i.e.,

\displaystyle{\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}a^{K}_{h}(u,v_{h})+b_{h}^{\Gamma}(u,v_{h})-(m,\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla_{h}v_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+(1-\delta)v_{h})_{0,\Gamma}=(f,v_{h})_{0,\Omega}}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

To show the consistency of the second equation of (
3.12
),
we multiply (
2.10
), which is an equivalent formulation of (
2.7
),
by
\overline{v_{h}^{\text{ext}}}\in Z_{h}
and integrate over
\Gamma
:

\displaystyle\langle(\mathcal{B}_{k}+\text{i}k\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k})u^{ext}-\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}m,v_{h}^{\text{ext}}\rangle=0\qquad\forall v_{h}^{\text{ext}}\in Z_{h}.

Eventually, multiplying (
2.8
) by
\overline{\lambda_{h}}\in W_{h}
and integrating over
\Gamma
, we get

\displaystyle\langle u,\lambda_{h}\rangle-\langle(\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{k})u^{ext}-\mathcal{V}_{k}(m-iku^{ext}),\lambda_{h}\rangle=0.

Similarly as above, the boundary condition in (
2.6
) leads to

\displaystyle\langle-\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}\nabla u\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma},\lambda_{h}\rangle+\langle-\delta u,\lambda_{h}\rangle+\langle\delta(\text{i}k)^{-1}m,\lambda_{h}\rangle=0.

Summing up the last two equations shows the consistency of the third equation in (
3.12
).
∎

Appendix B
An
hp
-stable, discontinuous-to-continuous reconstruction operator on curvilinear simplicial meshes

Here, we prove Theorem
4.4
.

Let the mesh
\Omega_{h}
satisfy the shape regularity assumption (
3.1
) and
v\in H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})
.
We construct the operator
\mathcal{P}:H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Omega)
as the composition
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{2}\circ\mathcal{P}_{1}
of two operators

\mathcal{P}_{2}
,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
that we define below.
Preliminarily, for each
K\in\Omega_{h}
, we construct a
quasi-uniform, shape regular simplicial decomposition
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K}
of
K
, such that the size of each
element
\widetilde{K}
of
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K}
is comparable to
\widetilde{h}_{K}:=h_{K}/\ell^{2}
.
Denote the union of all
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K}
by
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}
.
By using a standard refinement strategy on the original mesh, we can additionally ensure that
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}
does not contain hanging nodes.
We also introduce

\widetilde{V}_{h}:=\{v\in S^{1,0}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h})\,|\,v{}_{|_{K}}\in S^{1,1}(K,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K})\quad\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\},

(B.1)

the space of the mapped, piecewise linear polynomials over
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}
, which are continuous in each
K\in\Omega_{h}
but possibly discontinuous at the interfaces of
\Omega_{h}
.

We define
\mathcal{P}_{1}:H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\rightarrow\widetilde{V}_{h}
as follows.
For each
K\in\Omega_{h}
,
\mathcal{P}_{1}(v_{h}{}_{|K})\in{\cal S}^{1,1}(K,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K})
is the
quasi-interpolant of
v
defined in
[
6
, Sec. 4]
.
As for
\mathcal{P}_{2}:\widetilde{V}_{h}\rightarrow{\cal S}^{1,1}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h})\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
, we choose
the lowest-order, Oswald-type operator introduced by Karakashian and
Pascal in
[
34
]
.
This operator interpolates the arithmetical averages of the degrees of freedom at each vertex of the mesh
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}
.
Thus, we are actually going to prove Theorem
4.4

with
\mathcal{P}:H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\rightarrow{\cal S}^{1,1}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h})\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
.
For simplicity, throughout this section we assume that
h/{\ell^{2}}\lesssim 1
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The
other cases follow similarly but would incur some cumbersome notation/case distinctions.

Before proving (
4.13
)–(
4.15
), we recall two propositions, which summarize the properties of
the operators
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

Proposition B.1
.

For any element
K\in\Omega_{h}
, the quasi-interpolant
\mathcal{P}_{1}:H_{\operatorname{pw}}^{1}(\Omega_{h})\rightarrow\widetilde{V}_{h}
satisfies the following estimates:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{P}_{1}v\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\nabla v\|_{0,K},

(B.2)

\displaystyle\|v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\|_{0,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\mathfrak{h}}\ell^{-2}\nabla v\|_{0,K},

(B.3)

\displaystyle\|\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}v\rrbracket\|_{0,\partial K\setminus\Gamma}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\llbracket v\rrbracket\|_{0,\partial K\setminus\Gamma}+\|{\mathfrak{h}}^{1/2}\ell^{-1}\nabla_{h}v\|_{0,\omega_{K}},

(B.4)

where
\omega_{K}
in (
B.4
) denotes the set of elements sharing a face with
K
.

Proof.

Bounds (
B.2
) and (
B.3
) follow from
[
6
, Thm. 4.1]
locally on
K
as the domain to obtain a function on the subtriangulation
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K}
.
We can apply
[
6
, Thm. 4.1]
since
\widetilde{\Omega}_{h}^{K}
fulfills (
3.1
) and
thus (
3.2
), which is the condition required there.

To show (
B.4
), we fix a facet
F
shared by the elements
K
and
K^{\prime}
. We get

\displaystyle\|\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{1}v\rrbracket\|_{0,F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\llbracket v\rrbracket\|_{0,F}+\|\llbracket v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\rrbracket\|_{0,F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\llbracket v\rrbracket\|_{0,F}+\|\big(v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\big)_{|K}\|_{0,F}+\|\big(v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\big)_{|K^{\prime}}\|_{0,F}.

For brevity, we only consider the third term on the right-hand side. Transforming to the reference element, applying a multiplicative trace estimate and transforming back gives

\displaystyle\|\big(v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\big)_{|K^{\prime}}\|_{0,F}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|{\mathfrak{h}}^{-1/2}(v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v)\|_{0,K^{\prime}}+\|v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v\|_{0,K^{\prime}}^{1/2}\|\nabla(v-\mathcal{P}_{1}v)\|_{0,K^{\prime}}^{1/2}.

Inserting (
B.2
) and (
B.3
) yields (
B.4
).
∎

Proposition B.2
.

The Oswald-type operator
\mathcal{P}_{2}:\widetilde{V}_{h}\to{\cal S}^{1,1}(\Omega,\widetilde{\Omega}_{h})
satisfies the following properties:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{v}_{h}-\mathcal{P}_{2}\widetilde{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\|{\mathfrak{h}}^{1/2}\ell^{-1}\llbracket\widetilde{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}},\quad\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{v}_{h}-\mathcal{P}_{2}\widetilde{v}_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}\lesssim\|{\mathfrak{h}}^{-1/2}\ell\llbracket\widetilde{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}^{I}}.

(B.5)

Proof.

We claim that

\|\widetilde{v}_{h}-\mathcal{P}_{2}\widetilde{v}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}{\|\widetilde{h}_{K}^{1/2}\llbracket\widetilde{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\partial K\setminus\Gamma}^{2}},\quad\|\nabla_{h}(\widetilde{v}_{h}-\mathcal{P}_{2}\widetilde{v}_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{K\in\Omega_{h}}{\|\widetilde{h}_{K}^{-1/2}\llbracket\widetilde{v}_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,\partial K\setminus\Gamma}^{2}.}

This follows as in the proof of
[
34
, Thm. 2.2]
, which only makes use of the definition of the Lagrangian degrees of freedom of
\mathcal{P}_{2}\widetilde{v}_{h}
as arithmetical averages of the degrees of freedom of
\widetilde{v}_{h}
and of the scaling properties of the basis functions.
We remark that
[

Then, the estimates in (

As an immediate consequence of the shape regularity of

(B.6)

We prove further properties of the operator

First, proceeding as in Remark

(B.7)

Next, we observe that

\begin{split}\|&\nabla_{h}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{1}v)\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\|\nabla_{h}(\mathcal{P}_{1}v)\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\nabla_{h}(\mathcal{P}_{1}v-\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{1}v))\|_{0,\Omega}\\

Given
