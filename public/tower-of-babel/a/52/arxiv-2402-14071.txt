Altermagnetic superconducting diode effect
Sayan Banerjee, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Non-reciprocal superconductivity, also known as the superconducting diode effect, has been extensively studied in the presence of a magnetic field or some form of ferromagnetic order breaking time-reversal symmetry. We here show that another class of magnetic order known as altermagnetism, which also breaks time-reversal symmetry but does not exhibit a finite net magnetic moment, can also give rise to a superconducting diode effect. Whether this is the case depends on the combination of the system’s point group and altermagnetic order parameter which we explore systematically for two-dimensional crystalline systems. If the superconducting electrons are in a centrosymmetric crystalline environment, an electric field E z E_{z} (or other sources of inversion symmetry breaking) can be used to turn on and tune the non-reciprocity, yielding an electric-field tunable diode effect; there are also non-centrosymmetric point groups, which are not reached by applying E z ≠ 0 E_{z}\neq 0 in a centrosymmetric crystal, but still allow for an altermagnetic order parameter with non-reciprocal superconductivity. Depending on the residual magnetic point group, the zeros of the critical current asymmetry, J c ​ ( 𝒏 ^ ) − J c ​ ( − 𝒏 ^ ) J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})-J_{c}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}) , are pinned along high-symmetry crystalline directions 𝒏 ^ = 𝒆 ^ j \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j} or are free to rotate in the plane of the system. In some cases, the zeros can be rotated by tuning the electric field E z E_{z} . We discuss all of these phenomena both on the general level using exact symmetry arguments and more explicitly by constructing and solving minimal lattice models. We provide experimental setups to realize the altermagnetic superconducting diode effect.

II
General considerations

Before presenting explicit calculations, we start by discussing general constraints on the AM-induced SDE, resulting from symmetries. We will see under which conditions the SDE is forced to vanish along high-symmetry directions and systematically go through all possible AM order parameters of 2D systems.

II.1
Diode effect efficiency

To set the stage for our symmetry discussion, we begin by first outlining the basic procedure for the calculation of the critical current. Denoting the superconducting order parameter for Cooper pairs with center-of-mass momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
by
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, we expand the change of the free-energy as a result of superconductivity
\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}:=\mathcal{F}[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}]-\mathcal{F}[0]
up to quartic order in
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
,

\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+b^{\text{S}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}\Delta^{\phantom{*}}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{3}}\Delta^{\phantom{*}}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{4}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}+\boldsymbol{q}_{2},\boldsymbol{q}_{3}+\boldsymbol{q}_{4}},

(1)

where we neglected the momentum dependence of the quartic term.
The equilibrium superconducting state is found by minimizing
\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}
. Restricting the analysis to single-
\boldsymbol{q}
states,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\propto\delta_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}}
, the value of
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
is determined by the minimum of
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
. We define the critical current
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
along
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
as the maximal magnitude of
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})=2e\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
oriented along
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
. Postponing a microscopic evaluation of
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
to Sec.
III
below, we here only note that a unitary symmetry with vector representation
g
implies
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=a^{\text{S}}_{g\boldsymbol{q}}
and, thus,
\boldsymbol{J}(g\boldsymbol{q})=g\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})
as well as
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(g\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
.

To quantify the ‘degree’ or amount of diode effect, it will be convenient for us to first introduce a signed efficiency parameter

\eta_{s}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}):=\frac{J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})-J_{c}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})}{J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})+J_{c}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})}

(2)

such that the angle-resolved diode-effect efficiency is
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=|\eta_{s}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})|\geq 0
.
We next discuss symmetry constraints on
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
. From the discussion above, it follows that a unitary symmetry with vector representation
g
enforces
\eta_{s}(g\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=\eta_{s}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
. Since
\eta_{s}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=-\eta_{s}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
, which immediately follows from the definition (
2
), the SDE efficiency is required to vanish along any direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}
obeying
g\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}
; in short:

g\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}=-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0})=0.

(3)

Clearly, Eq. (
3
) is obeyed for all
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
if
g
is inversion (
I
) or two-fold rotation along the out-of-plane (
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
) direction
C_{2z}
for a 2D system; these symmetries are thus required to be broken to allow for a finite SDE.
While the following constraints are readily generalized to three spatial dimensions, we first focus for notational simplicity on a 2D system in the
xy
plane. If
g
is a two-fold rotational symmetry,
C_{2\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
, along an in-plane direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}
or a mirror-plane symmetry,
\sigma_{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
, spanned by
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}
, Eq. (
3
) implies that
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=0
for
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
perpendicular to
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}
. In other words, the zeros of
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
are pinned along high-symmetry directions if the point group contains two-fold rotations along in-plane directions or mirror planes perpendicular to the system plane. Note that rotations with degrees higher than two do not pin the zeros.

Similar constraints arise from magnetic point symmetries, i.e., non-unitary symmetries,
\Theta g_{m}
, given by the product of time-reversal
\Theta
and some unitary symmetry
g_{m}
. Such a magnetic symmetry implies
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=a^{\text{S}}_{-g_{m}\boldsymbol{q}}
and consequently
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(-g_{m}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
; this, in turn, leads to
\eta_{s}(g_{m}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=\eta_{s}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=-\eta_{s}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
. As such, the presence of the magnetic symmetry
\Theta g_{m}
implies that

g_{m}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{0})=0.

(4)

Equation (
4
) contains the special case of preserved time-reversal symmetry,
g_{m}=\mathbbm{1}
for which
g_{m}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
always holds, implying
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=0
for all
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
; this just recovers the often-stated fact that
\Theta
needs to be broken to get a SDE. However, Eq. (
4
) also shows that zeros of the angle-resolved SDE efficiency are pinned along high-symmetry directions if the system exhibits a magnetic reflection symmetry,
\Theta\sigma_{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
, or magnetic rotational symmetry,
\Theta C_{2\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
; in both cases, it holds
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{m}})=0
. We will see examples of all of these constraints in the explicit model calculations of Sec.
III
below.

We finally point out that Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) also apply for the three-dimensional case. As such, we see that a two-fold non-magnetic rotation (mirror) symmetry lead to line (point) nodes for
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
on the sphere
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}|\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{2}=1\}
, while magnetic rotations (mirror) symmetries imply point (line) nodes.

II.2
AM-induced SDE

With these symmetry constraints at hand, we are now in position to analyze under which conditions an AM moment can induce a SDE and, if non-zero, whether the angle-resolved SDE efficiency
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
has to have zeros along certain directions.
To be precise, in this work, we use the following definition of an AM order parameter. We define an AM order parameter as a time-reversal-odd order parameter (i.e., some form of magnetic order), which unlike a ferromagnet does not lead to a finite net magnetic moment or spin-polarization; in addition, we require that it preserve lattice translation, unlike an antiferromagnet or more complex translational-symmetry-breaking orders (such as an incommensurate spiral or a tetrahedral magnet).

For concreteness, we study the prototypical case of a minimal model for the normal state—one that only contains a single, spinful band,

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},s}\left[s_{0}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}\right]_{s,s^{\prime}}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},s^{\prime}},

(5)

where
c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},s}
are electron creation operators of spin
s
and momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
,
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
,
s_{0}=\mathbbm{1}
, are spin Pauli matrices. Furthermore,

\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the bare dispersion,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
the spin-orbit vector, and
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
the AM order parameter. Since these three quantities are, by design, even, even, and odd under
\Theta

\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{-\boldsymbol{k}},\qquad\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\boldsymbol{g}_{-\boldsymbol{k}},\qquad\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{-\boldsymbol{k}}.

(6)

We, therefore, conclude that
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
always preserves inversion,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
can only be non-zero if inversion,
I
, is broken (as usual), and the AM order parameter cannot break
I
(and will always be odd under
\Theta I
) in the class of models in Eq. (
5
). To describe superconductivity, we assume that Cooper pairs form in the spin-singlet channel, the superconducting order parameter transforms trivially under all point symmetries and preserves time-reversal.
Therefore,
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the only term breaking time-reversal and
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
the only source of broken inversion symmetry in Eq. (
5
); this is why both are required to obtain a finite SDE,
\eta\neq 0
.

III
Model studies

In order to demonstrate the features we have deduced by symmetry analysis above more explicitly and to discuss additional aspects, we here construct and solve concrete lattice models; to this end, we use three different point groups and AM order parameters that can induce an SDE as examples.

III.1
General formalism

In all of these lattice models, superconductivity will be described in the same way. We start with an attractive (
g>0
) interaction in the spin-singlet Cooper channel,

\mathcal{H}_{I}=-g\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}C^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q}}C^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{q}},\quad C^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}is_{y}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2},

(8)

and add it to the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
5
),
\mathcal{H}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{I}
. We then perform a mean-field decoupling in the Cooper channel and introduce
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}=g\braket{C_{\boldsymbol{q}}}
, leading to the following Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}=&\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}h_{\boldsymbol{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}\left[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}is_{y}c^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}+\text{H.c.}\right]\\
&+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}{g},\quad h_{\boldsymbol{k}}=s_{0}\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}.\end{split}

(9)

Using a redundant Nambu basis, defined by
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2},is_{y}c^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2})^{T}
, we obtain a Bogoliubov-de-Gennes Hamiltonian,

H_{\text{BdG}}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{q}}&-s_{y}h^{*}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2}s_{y}\end{pmatrix}

(10)

which allows us to rewrite the Hamiltonian (
9
) for a given
\boldsymbol{q}
as,

\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}(\boldsymbol{q})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}H_{\text{BdG}}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\frac{|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}{g}.

(11)

Next, we integrate out the electrons in this quadratic mean-field Hamiltonian and recover the Ginzburg-Landau expression in Eq. (
1
). The key quantity
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is related to the particle-particle bubble
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
at finite
\boldsymbol{q}
in the usual way,
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=g^{-1}-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
. Keeping both intra- and inter-band contributions, the particle-particle bubble is of the form

\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\nu=p,m,\eta=\pm}\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\nu,\eta}\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\nu,\eta}}{2T}},

(12)

where
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\nu,\eta}
contains the coherence factors and
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\nu,\eta}
are Bogoliubov energies (see Appendix
B
for their explicit form); the indices
\nu
and
\mu
label the four different states associated with spin and particle-hole space in our redundant Nambu basis, cf. Eq. (
10
). From the resulting
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, we compute the directional dependence of the critical current and hence
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
as described in Sec.
II.1
.
We will next apply this formalism using a few different normal-state Hamiltonians,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, to elucidate the nature of the SDE in various point groups.

III.2
Pinned zeros and
E_{z}
tunability in
D_{6h}

We start from the centrosymmetric point group
\mathcal{G}_{0}=D_{6h}
, where
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
obeys
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{-\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{C_{3z}\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{\sigma_{v}\boldsymbol{k}}
. To model such a dispersion, we take a nearest-neighbor hopping model on the triangular lattice,
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}t\cos(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
are three
C_{3z}
-related primitive vectors. For notational simplicity, we measure all energies in units of
t
in the following. The presence of
I
in
D_{6h}
implies
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
. We can see in Table
1
that
D_{6h}
has four possible AM order parameters and only two of them—those transforming under
B_{1g}
and
B_{2g}
—can give rise to a SDE when also applying an electric field
E_{z}
. For concreteness, we will here focus on
B_{1g}
, which reduces
D_{6h}
to the magnetic point group generated by
C_{3z}
,
\Theta C_{2z}
,
\Theta\sigma_{v}
, and
I
. We construct the lowest-order, Brillouin-zone-periodic form of
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
consistent with
B_{1g}
, which corresponds to first-nearest-neighbor-hopping processes on the triangular lattice, with explicit form presented in Appendix
B
; it behaves as

\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim N_{0}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2},-2k_{x}k_{y},0)^{T}

(13)

around the
\Gamma
point, i.e., for
|\boldsymbol{k}|\rightarrow 0
. Due to
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{-\boldsymbol{k}}
, see Eq. (
6
), and
B_{1g}
being odd under
C_{2z}
, it holds
(\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{z}=0
to arbitrary order in
\boldsymbol{k}
.
Note that this order parameter obeys all of the properties in our definition of an AM: by design [see Eq. (
9
)] it is translationally invariant and breaks time-reversal symmetry (due to
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{N}_{-\boldsymbol{k}}
). The absence of finite spin polarization immediately follows by noting that the residual
C_{3z}
and
C_{2z}\Theta
symmetries (also present for
E_{z}\neq 0
) imply, respectively, that
\braket{S_{x,y}}
and
\braket{S_{z}}
have to vanish, which we have also checked explicitly.

To understand the salient features of this model, let us start first with
E_{z}=0
, with results displayed in the first column (a) of Fig.
2
. One can see in Fig.
2
(ai) and (aii) that the maximum of
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
is pinned to
\boldsymbol{q}=0
as long as
N_{0}
in Eq. (
13
) is smaller than a critical value. This pinning is a consequence of
C_{3z}
symmetry. It means that, in the current model, a sufficiently strong AM order is required to induce finite-momentum pairing. While finite-momentum pairing can be induced by AM order, as pointed out in recent publications in the context of different models without
C_{3z}
symmetry
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Chakraborty and Black-Schaffer 2023
, it does not necessarily follow immediately, as we can see here. Moreover, since we have inversion symmetry and unbroken
C_{3z}
, we observe that
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
is six-fold symmetric. This symmetry is inherited by the critical current
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
shown in Fig.
2
(aiii); it thus obeys
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
and there is no SDE,
\eta=0
, see Fig.
2
(aiv).

As anticipated in Table
1
, we therefore have to apply an electric field
E_{z}
, which reduces
D_{6h}
to
C_{6v}
, to get an SDE. On the level of the Hamiltonian, the electric field induces a non-zero spin-orbit vector
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. We again refer to Appendix
B
for its explicit form throughout the Brillouin zone and here only note that
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim\alpha(k_{y},-k_{x},0)^{T}
as
|\boldsymbol{k}|\rightarrow 0
. Similar to
(\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{z}=0
noted above, the last component
(\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{z}
has to vanish to all orders in
\boldsymbol{k}
as a consequence of
C_{2z}
and Eq. (
6
).

The corresponding results for finite
E_{z}
are shown in the second column, (b), of Fig.
2
. We observe that, even in the presence of
\alpha\neq 0
, the maximum of
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
is pinned to zero momentum, see Fig.
2
(bi), due to the still unbroken
C_{3z}
symmetry. However, as a consequence of inversion symmetry breaking,
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
is not an even function anymore. As soon as a critical value of
N_{0}
is surpassed, the maxima of
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})
move from zero
\boldsymbol{q}
to three non-zero,
C_{3z}
-related momenta, as indicated by the orange circles in Fig.
2
(bii). The number of degenerate maxima is reduced from six to three [compared with Fig.
2
(aii)] when applying
E_{z}\neq 0
as
I
is broken.

By the same token, the critical current
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
is not an even function anymore, see Fig.
2
(biii), and we obtain a SDE,
\eta\neq 0
, as can be clearly seen in Fig.
2
(biv). This demonstrates explicitly that a SDE is also possible when the only source of broken
\Theta
is an AM, i.e., in the absence of a net magnetic moment. As the SDE requires broken
I
, it is tunable by an electric field.
What is more, we observe that
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
has zeros along the high-symmetry directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}
,
C_{3z}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}
, and
C_{3z}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}
as follows from the magnetic reflection symmetry
\Theta\sigma_{v}
and Eq. (
4
) [or
\sigma_{d}
and Eq. (
3
) for that matter].
For an AM order parameter transforming under
B_{2g}
, the behavior is very similar. The main difference is that the aforementioned zeros of
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
are pinned along the orthogonal directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
,
C_{3z}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
, and
C_{3z}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
, since, instead of
\Theta\sigma_{v}
, we have the magnetic reflection symmetry
\Theta\sigma_{d}
.

III.3
Generic zeros in
C_{6h}

To demonstrate explicitly that the zeros of
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
are not always pinned along high-symmetry directions for the SDE induced by an AM, we next consider the case
\mathcal{G}_{0}=C_{6h}
. It exhibits no rotations
C_{2\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
or reflections
\sigma_{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
with in-plane
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}
, such that neither Eq. (
3
) nor Eq. (
4
) can apply (irrespective of the form of broken time-reversal symmetry). One consequence of the reduced symmetries is that the bare dispersion exhibits more terms compared to
D_{6h}
discussed above, which we describe with the leading-order contribution,
\propto t^{\prime}
, in

\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(t\cos{(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})}+t^{\prime}\cos{(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})}\right).

(14)

Here,
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{j}
are three
C_{3z}
-related next-nearest-neighbor vectors on the triangular lattice (
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{1}=2\boldsymbol{a}_{2}-\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{j}=C_{3z}^{j-1}\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}_{1}
). As can be seen in Table
1
, only one of the two possible AM order parameters—the one transforming under
B_{g}
of
C_{6h}
—can give rise to a SDE. As a result of the reduced symmetry, the two different AM order parameters associated with
B_{1g}
and
B_{2g}
in
D_{6h}
now both transform under
B_{g}
of
C_{6h}
and can, hence, mix. Introducing a
3\times 3
matrix
R_{\varphi}
describing rotations by
\varphi
along the third direction, this admixture can be parametrized as

\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=R_{\varphi}\boldsymbol{N}^{B_{1g}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim N_{0}R_{\varphi}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2},-2k_{x}k_{y},0)^{T},

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{N}^{B_{1g}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the
B_{1g}
AM order parameter of
D_{6h}
used above in Sec.
III.2
and the asymptotic relation again refers to the vicinity of the
\Gamma
point,
\boldsymbol{k}\rightarrow 0
.

To obtain a finite SDE,
\eta\neq 0
, inversion symmetry
I
still needs to be broken by application of an electric field
E_{z}\neq 0
. This induces a spin-orbit coupling term
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. For the same reason as above, the spin-orbit vector is less constrained than in
D_{6h}
and can be written in the form
R_{\varphi^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, where
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is identical to the one used for
D_{6h}
above, obeying
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim\alpha(k_{y},-k_{x},0)^{T}
. For
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}},\,\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq 0
, the residual magnetic point group of the system is generated by
C_{3z}
and
C_{2z}\Theta
. As such, the only constraints on the particle-particle bubble and the critical current are
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{q})=\Gamma(C_{3z}\boldsymbol{q})
and
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(C_{3z}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
, which are clearly visible in our numerical results in Fig.
3
(a) and (b), respectively. As expected, the maxima and minima of these two quantities are not pinned along high-symmetry directions and, therefore, the zeros of
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
, shown in Fig.
3
(c), are not pinned either.

We further present in Fig.
3
(d) the diode efficiency
\eta
for both the magnetic field-induced SDE and the altermagnetic SDE. To treat them on equal footing, we use the same model and set
\boldsymbol{B}^{2}=N_{0}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\text{BZ}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}
. Employing the same parameters as in the other parts of Fig.
3
, we find that the efficiency is significantly larger in the AM case; this demonstrates that the AM SDE is
not
a subleading and, thus generally, weaker effect compared to the magnetic-field-induced one, contrary to what one might have expected since the average magnetization is required to vanish for an AM. However, we caution that both magnetic fields and AMs can yield sizeable SDE with relative magnitudes depending largely on the parameters chosen.

III.4
E_{z}
tunable zeros and polarization in
D_{3}

We finally discuss the AM-induced SDE for the case where
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is already a non-centrosymmetric point group. As such, an electric field will not be required to get a SDE. Nonetheless, as we will see,
E_{z}
can still be used to tune the properties of the system in a non-trivial way. For concreteness, let us choose
\mathcal{G}_{0}=D_{3}
. As can be seen in Table
1
, there is only a single AM order parameter, transforming under the trivial representation of the point group. While, to leading (quadratic) order in
\boldsymbol{k}
, it has the same form as
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
in Eq. (
13
), the subleading corrections are different. In particular, the reduced symmetries allow for a finite
(\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{z}
component;

\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sim N_{0}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2},-2k_{x}k_{y},\tau k_{x}k_{y}(k_{x}^{2}-3k_{y}^{2})(k_{y}^{2}-3k_{x}^{2})),

(16)

where we only kept the leading term in every component and
\tau
is a real parameter that is not determined by symmetry. The total spin polarization still vanishes,
\braket{\boldsymbol{S}}=0
, as required for an AM, which follows from the preserved
C_{3z}
(
\braket{S_{x,y}}=0
) and
C_{2x}
(
\braket{S_{z}}=0
) symmetries.

Interestingly, the spin-orbit vector has two different contributions,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{I}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{II}}
where
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{I}}\sim\beta(k_{x},k_{y},k_{y}(3k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2}))
is already allowed by the point group
D_{3}
without the need of an external electric field, and
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{II}}\sim\alpha(k_{y},-k_{x},k_{x}(k_{x}^{2}-3k_{y}^{2}))
is the spin-orbit coupling term induced by a perpendicular electric field
E_{z}
, i.e.,
\alpha\propto E_{z}+\mathcal{O}(E_{z}^{3})
. The full expressions for
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{I}}
, and
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{II}}
we use are provided in Appendix
B
.
Finally, for the dispersion, we restrict ourselves just to a nearest-neighbor hopping term on the triangular lattice,
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}t\cos(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{k})
.

Let us first focus on
E_{z}=0
(
\alpha=0
). Since
C_{2z}
,
I
, and
\Theta
are already broken, we find a finite SDE, see Fig.
4
(a,b). Furthermore, we observe that the extrema of the critical current rotated by
90^{\circ}
in comparison to
D_{6h}
[cf. Fig.
2
(biii)]. This is closely related to the discussion in Sec.
II.1
; since
D_{3}
has two-fold rotational symmetry
C_{2x}
about the in-plane direction
x
, the current has to obey
J_{c}(C_{2x}\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
which pins its extrema along
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{x}
and
C_{3z}
-related directions. Similarly, Eq. (
3
) implies that the angle-resolved efficiency
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
has to go to zero for
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
,
C_{3z}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
and
C_{3z}^{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{y}
, which is also observed in our numerics [see Fig.
4
(b)].

To demonstrate the effect of a finite
E_{z}
, we repeat the analysis now in the presence of the induced spin-orbit coupling term
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\text{II}}
. The electric field transforms as
A_{2}
of
D_{3}
and reduces the point group to
C_{3}
. Altogether, since time-reversal and inversion are still broken, the system still shows a SDE, see Fig.
4
(c,d), only now that there is no two-fold in-plane rotation (
C_{2x}
) or a mirror-plane
\sigma_{\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}}
as a symmetry. Naturally, it follows that the zeros of
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
are not pinned to the high symmetry directions, but are now free to move in-plane, as is clearly visible in Fig.
4
(d). An electric field can thus be used to tune the orientation of the zeros of the SDE efficiency
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
.

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied under which conditions a conventional two-dimensional superconducting phase can exhibit a SDE,
\eta_{s}\neq 0
in Eq. (
2
), if the only source of broken time-reversal symmetry is an AM order parameter. To approach this question systematically, we started by listing all AM order parameters
\boldsymbol{N}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, coupling to the electrons as given by Eq. (
5
), for all possible 2D crystalline point groups, see Table
1
. For each of them, we checked whether there are still residual symmetries, such as inversion symmetry or two-fold rotation along a direction perpendicular to the plane of the system, that would enforce the critical currents along opposite directions to be the same,
J_{c}(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})=J_{c}(-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})
. We found that there are indeed several point groups and associated AM order parameters (
C_{6h}
with AM in
B_{g}
;
D_{6h}
with AM in
B_{1g}
and
B_{2g}
;
D_{3d}
with
A_{1g}
;
D_{3}
with AM in
A_{1}
;
D_{6}
with
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
) that give rise to an SDE, as we have also checked by explicit model calculations. Since, by design, the total magnetic moment vanishes when an AM is the only source of broken time-reversal symmetry, this demonstrates that the SDE does not require finite net magnetization. A direct comparison with the magnetic-field-induced SDE reveals that the altermagntic SDE is not a subleading effect; in fact, depending on parameters, the altermagnetic efficiency can even be substantially larger when compared in the same model with the magnetic-field scenario.

In the context of the SDE, the AM order parameters can be further categorized in the following way: the AMs transforming under
B_{g}
of
C_{6h}
,
B_{1g}
or
B_{2g}
of
D_{6h}
, and
A_{1g}
of
D_{3d}
will only give rise to an SDE if also an electric field,
E_{z}
, perpendicular to the plane of the system is applied. This breaks inversion symmetry, a necessary requirement for the SDE. Note, however, that in all cases listed here, there are still enough symmetries present to guarantee a vanishing net magnetic moment. This shows that AMs allow for electric-field-tunable (rather than the frequently discussed magnetic-field-tunable) SDEs.
In turn, the AMs transforming under
A_{1}
and
B_{1,2}
for the non-centrosymmetric point groups
D_{3}
and
D_{6}
, respectively, will immediately give rise to a SDE, without application of an electric field; moreover, for
E_{z}\neq 0
, the symmetries will be lowered in such a way that the order parameters cease to be AMs. Here the electric field can be used to control the out-of-plane magnetization of the system.

Another important distinction of the different scenarios for AM-induced SDEs can be made based on the directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j}
where the SDE efficiency vanishes,
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j})=0
. While
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j}
are pinned along high-symmetry directions for
D_{6h}
(reduced to
C_{6v}
by
E_{z}\neq 0
) and
D_{3d}
(reduced to
C_{3v}
), this is not the case for the SDEs induced by the
B_{g}
AM in
C_{6h}
(reduced to
C_{6}
), where
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j}
point along generic (
C_{3z}
-related) directions. Finally, for the AMs of non-centrosymmetric point groups
D_{3}
and
D_{6}
that can induce an SDE, applying an electric field
E_{z}\neq 0
allows to rotate the
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j}
with
\eta(\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}_{j})=0
away from crystalline to generic directions.

These findings, that can be deduced from symmetry considerations, are summarized in Table
1
. We have further constructed and solved minimal lattice models for the different classes of and phenomena associated with AM-induced SDEs discussed above. This further illustrates and explicitly demonstrates the complex interplay of AM and superconductivity.
We finally point out that there are several setups that can be used to realize and probe the proposed AM-induced SDE experimentally, see Fig.
1
. Given that there are already candidate materials with the right symmetries (see last column in Table
1
), the rapidly growing number of AM candidate materials
Gao et al. 2023
, and the fact that heterostructures are routinely grown or stacked, this seems to be well within current experimental reach.
