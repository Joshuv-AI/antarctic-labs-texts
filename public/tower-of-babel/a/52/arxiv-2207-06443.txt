Random Magnetic Field and the Dirac Fermi Surface
Chao-Jung Lee, Michael Mulligan

Abstract
We study a single 2d Dirac fermion at finite density, subject to a quenched random magnetic field. At low energies and sufficiently weak disorder, the theory maps onto an infinite collection of 1d chiral fermions (associated to each point on the Fermi surface) coupled by a random vector potential. This low-energy theory exhibits an exactly solvable random fixed line, along which we directly compute various disorder-averaged observables without the need for the usual replica, supersymmetry, or Keldysh techniques. We find the longitudinal dc conductivity in the collisionless ℏ ​ ω / k B ​ T → ∞ \hbar\omega/k_{B}T\rightarrow\infty limit to be nonuniversal and to vary continuously along the fixed line.

\displaystyle={k_{F}^{2}\over 2}\int{dp_{\perp}dp^{\prime}_{\perp}\over(2\pi)^{2}}e^{ip_{\perp}z}e^{ip^{\prime}_{\perp}z^{\prime}}f(|{\bf q}|)\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})\big(e^{i(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4})}+e^{-i(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})}\big).

(2.37)

We will approximate (
2.37
) by

\displaystyle\overline{V_{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}(z)V_{\theta_{3}\theta_{4}}(z^{\prime})}=gk_{F}\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4})\delta(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})f(z-z^{\prime}),

(2.38)

where

\displaystyle f(z)=\int{dp_{\perp}\over 2\pi}\,e^{ip_{\perp}z}f(p_{\perp})

(2.39)

and it is to be understood that
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\ll 1
and
|\theta_{3}-\theta_{4}|\ll 1
.
The disorder (
2.38
) is local in
z

z-z^{\prime}
.
We expect (
2.38
) to be a good approximation to (
2.37
) at small momentum transfers and sufficiently low energies.
More precisely, we require
M/k_{F}\ll 1
and that the cutoff on
p_{\perp}\in(-\Lambda,\Lambda)
satisfies
\Lambda/M\ll 1
.

A detailed explanation for the approximation (
2.38
) goes as follows.
We begin by noting that for
M/k_{F}\ll 1
and using
\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})
,
f({\bf q})=f({\bf q}^{\prime})
fixes
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\ll 1
and
|\theta_{3}-\theta_{4}|\ll 1
.
Next, we expect the dominant contributions to (
2.37
) to arise when the terms in the sum
\big(e^{i(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4})}+e^{-i(\theta_{2}-\theta_{3})}\big)
are in-phase.
This sets
\theta_{1}=\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}-\theta_{2}+2\pi m
, for an arbitrary integer
m
.
Using the earlier two conditions, the in-phase requirement implies
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{4}-\pi m|\ll 1
: This is modeled by
\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4}-\pi|m|)
.
Substituting
\theta_{1}=\theta_{4}+\pi m
into
f({\bf q})
and
\theta_{4}=\theta_{1}-\pi m
into
f({\bf q}^{\prime})
fixes
|\theta_{4}-\theta_{2}+\pi m|\ll 1
and
|\theta_{3}-\theta_{1}+\pi m|\ll 1
.
Since
\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
, these two conditions allow either
m=0
or
|m|=1
.
First consider
m=0
.
Since
\theta_{2}\approx\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{4}\approx\theta_{1}
,
{\bf q}\approx p_{\perp}\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}+k_{F}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}){d\over d\theta_{1}}\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}
and
{\bf q}^{\prime}\approx p^{\prime}_{\perp}\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}+k_{F}(\theta_{3}-\theta_{4}){d\over d\theta_{1}}\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}
.
Because
\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}
and
{d\over d\theta_{1}}\hat{r}_{\theta_{1}}
are orthonormal,
\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})=\delta(p_{\perp}+p^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta\big(k_{F}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}-\theta_{4})\big)={1\over k_{F}}\delta(p_{\perp}+p^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta(\theta_{3}-\theta_{2})
, using
\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4}-\pi|m|)
.
For Gaussian
f(|{\bf q}|)
, the dependence on
p_{\perp}
and
\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
factorizes.
Absorbing into
g
the variation of this Gaussian on
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\approx 0
, we perform the integral over
p^{\prime}_{\perp}
using
\delta(p_{\perp}+p^{\prime}_{\perp})
to arrive at (
2.38
).
Next consider
m=1
; the
m=-1
case works similarly and will not be discussed.
Following the
m=0
logic, the replacement
\theta_{4}=\theta_{1}-\pi
introduces a relative phase in the angular delta function,
\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})={1\over k_{F}}\delta(p_{\perp}-p^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta\big(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}-(\theta_{3}-\theta_{4})\big)={1\over k_{F}}\delta(p_{\perp}-p^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta\big(2\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}-\theta_{3}-\pi\big)\approx{1\over k_{F}}\delta(p_{\perp}-p^{\prime}_{\perp})\delta\big(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\big)
, using
\theta_{3}\approx\theta_{1}-\pi
.
The delta function has support when
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
(and similarly requires using the in-phase delta function
\delta(\theta_{1}-\theta_{4}-\pi)
and
f({\bf q}^{\prime})
that
\theta_{3}=\theta_{4}
).
For
\Lambda/M\ll 1
, scattering along the Fermi surface dominates and the relative contributions of
m\neq 0
terms should be suppressed.
We therefore ignore the
|m|=1
terms in the remainder.

2.4
Random Fixed Point and its Discrete Approximation

Under the renormalization group transformation
7

7

The scale transformation is the following:

Because the randomness is

\displaystyle U_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(z)\equiv\Big({\cal T}_{z}e^{-i\int_{z_{0}}^{z}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}\Big)_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}.

(2.42)

Here,

The resulting action simplifies to

\displaystyle S=\int{dtdzd\theta\over 2\pi}\,\tilde{R}^{\ast}_{\theta}(t,z)i\big(\partial_{t}+\partial_{z}\big)\tilde{R}_{\theta}(t,z).

The action (

\displaystyle V_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(z)

\displaystyle\rightarrow V_{\theta_{I}\theta_{J}}(z)\equiv V_{IJ}(z).

(2.45)

(The scaling of

We are specifically interested in the limit

Substituting in the discrete form of the angular integration

\displaystyle S

(2.47)

where the rotated fermions, in discrete form, are

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{I}(t,z)=\sum_{J=1}^{N}U_{IJ}(z)R_{J}(t,z),\quad U_{IJ}(z)=\Big({\cal T}_{z}e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}\Big)_{IJ}.

(2.48)

The discrete action has an emergent

In discrete form, the low-energy currents (

\displaystyle=\int dz\sum_{I}R^{\ast}_{I}(t,z)\sin(\theta_{I})R_{I}(t,z).

(2.50)

For discrete
\theta

\displaystyle\overline{V_{IJ}(z)V_{KL}(z^{\prime})}=gk_{F}N\delta_{IL}\delta_{JK}f(z-z^{\prime}).

(2.51)

The overall factor of
N
arises from the discrete form of
\delta({\bf q}+{\bf q}^{\prime})
in (
2.37

\theta_{I}=2\pi I/N
.
It is the discrete form of the action (
2.47

3
Observables along the Fixed Line

In general, in the presence of quenched disorder
V
with unit-normalized distribution
P[V]
, the disorder-average of the correlation function of a physical observable
{\cal O}
is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\langle{\cal O}\rangle}\equiv\int DVP[V]\,\langle\mathcal{O}\,\rangle_{V},

(3.1)

where the correlation function
\langle\mathcal{O}\,\rangle_{V}
in the disorder realization
V
is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\,\rangle_{V}\equiv\frac{\int D\Phi\;\mathcal{O}\;e^{iS[\Phi,V]}}{\int D\Phi\;e^{iS[\Phi,V]}}.

(3.2)

Here, we are momentarily denoting the dynamical fields of the theory by
\Phi
and the action
S[\Phi,V]
indicates the dependence upon both
\Phi
and the disorder
V
.
In most theories, the presence of
V
in the denominator of
\langle\mathcal{O}\,\rangle_{V}
renders the direct analytic integration over all possible disorders in
\overline{\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle}
difficult, if not impossible.
As such, various ingenious tricks—such as replica, supersymmetric, and Keldysh formalisms—have been employed with various levels of success.
In this paper, we instead make use of the exact solution of the low-energy effective theory presented in §
2.4
to directly perform the disorder average.

To see how this works, consider the correlation function of a local observable
{\cal O}(R)
, which is a function of the unrotated
R_{I}(t,z)
fermion:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(R)\rangle_{V}=\frac{\int DR^{\dagger}DR\;\mathcal{O}(R)\;e^{iS[R,V]}}{\int DR^{\dagger}DR\;e^{iS[R,V]}}=\frac{\int D\tilde{R}^{\dagger}D\tilde{R}\;\mathcal{O}(U^{\dagger}\tilde{R})\;e^{iS[\tilde{R}]}}{\int D\tilde{R}^{\dagger}D\tilde{R}\;e^{iS[\tilde{R}]}}.

(3.3)

In the first equality,
S[R,V]
denotes the action (
2.46
); in the second equality,
S[\tilde{R}]
denotes (
2.47
) with
\tilde{R}
the rotated fermion (
2.48
) with rotation
U
a function of
V
.
After the rotation, the denominator no longer depends on
V
and, in this case, yields the usual fermion determinant.
The disorder
V
now only appears in the expression for the observable
\mathcal{O}(U^{\dagger}\tilde{R})
and may, in principle, be averaged over.
8
8

8

Note that any possible quantum anomalies
[
53
]
associated with the unitary rotations (
2.42
) of the chiral fermion path integral measures, being a function of the disorder
V
only, mutually cancel between the numerator and denominator.

The key point is that the local observables we consider separate into a sum of terms of the form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(U^{\dagger}\tilde{R})=\sum_{a,b}c_{ab}{\cal A}_{a}(\tilde{R})\mathcal{B}_{b}(V),

(3.4)

for some constants
c_{ab}
, such that the disorder-averaged correlation function
\overline{\langle{\cal O}(R)\rangle}
factorizes:

\displaystyle\overline{\langle{\cal O}(R)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}c_{ab}\frac{\int D\tilde{R}^{\dagger}D\tilde{R}\;\mathcal{A}_{a}(\tilde{R})\;e^{iS[\tilde{R}]}}{\int D\tilde{R}^{\dagger}D\tilde{R}\;e^{iS[\tilde{R}]}}\cdot\int dVP[V]\mathcal{B}_{b}(V)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{a,b}c_{ab}\langle{\cal A}_{a}(\tilde{R})\rangle\cdot\overline{{\cal B}_{b}(V)}.

(3.6)

We will show how this can be used to compute the fermion Green’s function and the longitudinal conductivity at the random fixed point (
2.47

3.1
Diffusive Green’s Function and Density of States

We begin by calculating the disorder-averaged Green’s function and density of states.
We will find that the average Green’s function is short-ranged and that the density of states is a positive constant.
These calculations will introduce the technique we will later use to calculate the conductivity.

Using the low-energy action (
2.47
), the fermion two-point function averaged over the disorder is

\displaystyle\overline{\langle R_{I}(t,z)R^{\dagger}_{I}(0,0)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A,B}\langle\tilde{R}_{A}(t,z)\tilde{R}^{\dagger}_{B}(0,0)\rangle\cdot\overline{U^{\dagger}_{IA}(z)U_{BI}(0)}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A,B}\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{\delta_{AB}}{(z-t)+i\alpha}\cdot\overline{U^{\dagger}_{IA}(z)U_{BI}(0)},

(3.8)

where
U(z)
is defined in (
2.48
),
\alpha
is a short-distance cutoff, and the index
I
is not summed over.
Following
[
59
]
, we compute the disorder average of
U
matrices by decomposing the interval
[0,z]
into
n
steps
z_{0}=0,z_{1}=\delta z,\ldots,z_{n}=n\delta z
of length
\delta z=|z|/n
such that

\displaystyle U(z)=e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{n-1}}^{z_{n}}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}\cdots e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{0}}^{z_{1}}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}.

(3.9)

We have chosen the arbitrary reference point
z_{0}=0
.
For sufficiently large
n
with fixed
n\delta z=|z|
, the argument of each exponential can be approximated as

\displaystyle{1\over N}\int_{z_{j-1}}^{z_{j}}dz^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})\approx{1\over N}V(z_{j})\delta z\equiv M_{j}.

(3.10)

Using (
2.51
),
M_{j}
is a zero-mean Gaussian random variable with variance,

\displaystyle\overline{(M_{i})_{IJ}(M_{j})_{KL}}={gk_{F}\over N}\delta_{IL}\delta_{JK}f(z_{i}-z_{j})\delta z^{2},

(3.11)

where
f(z_{i}-z_{j})
is given in (
2.39
).
For discrete
z
, we take

\displaystyle f(z_{i}-z_{j})\delta z=f_{0}\delta_{|i-j|,0}+f_{1}\delta_{|i-j|,1}.

(3.12)

The dimensionless coefficients
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
approximate a Gaussian
f(z_{i}-z_{j})
of finite width.

Using this, the disorder average of the product of
U
matrices in (
3.7
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{A}\overline{U^{\dagger}_{IA}(z)U_{AI}(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}\overline{\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n}}\Big)_{IA}\times\bm{1}_{AI}}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\overline{\Big([1+iM_{1}-\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{2}+\ldots]\cdots[1+iM_{n}-\frac{M_{n}^{2}}{2}+\ldots]\Big)_{II}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{II}-gk_{F}[\frac{f_{0}}{2}\,n+f_{1}(n-1)]\delta z\,\delta_{II}

\displaystyle=e^{-gk_{F}\big(\frac{f_{0}}{2}+f_{1}\big)|z|}\delta_{II}.

In the third equality, we have dropped higher-order terms that vanish as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Further details on such computations are given in Appendix
A
.
Defining

\displaystyle g_{\rm eff}\equiv g\big({f_{0}\over 2}+f_{1}\big),

(3.17)

we obtain the disorder-averaged Green’s function,

\displaystyle\overline{\langle R_{I}(t,z)R^{\dagger}_{I}(0,0)\rangle}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-g_{\rm eff}k_{F}|z|}}{(z-t)+i\alpha}.

(3.18)

This Green’s function has a spatial decay length
\lambda=1/g_{\rm eff}k_{F}
.

We now use the Green’s function (
3.18
) to check that the density of states is finite.
For this, we need the retarded Green’s function averaged over the disorder:

\displaystyle\overline{G_{II}^{R}}(t,z;0,0)=-i\Theta(t)\overline{\langle\{R_{I}(t,z),R_{I}(0,0)\}\rangle}=-{i\over\pi}{\alpha e^{-g_{\rm eff}k_{F}|z|}\over(z-t)^{2}+\alpha^{2}},

(3.19)

where
\Theta(t)
is the step function.
Fourier transforming
\overline{G^{R}}(t,z;0,0)
for
\alpha\rightarrow 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{G_{II}^{R}}(\omega,p_{\perp})={1\over\omega-p_{\perp}+ig_{\rm eff}k_{F}}.

(3.20)

From (
3.20
), we obtain the density of states per unit volume,

\displaystyle\rho(\omega)=-{1\over\pi}{\rm Im}\int{dp_{\perp}\over 2\pi}{1\over\omega-p_{\perp}+ig_{\rm eff}k_{F}}={1\over 2\pi}.

(3.21)

3.2
Longitudinal Conductivity

We next turn to the disorder-averaged longitudinal conductivity
\sigma_{xx}(\omega)
.
The Kubo formula reads

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega)={1\over i\omega_{n}}{1\over L}{k_{F}\over N}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}\overline{\langle{\cal T}_{\tau}J_{x}(\tau)J_{x}(0)\rangle}\,\Big|_{\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}},

(3.22)

where
\tau=it
,
\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi\beta
is a positive Matsubara frequency at temperature
1/\beta
, and
L\cdot N/k_{F}
is a spatial volume factor equal to
\lim_{{\bf q}\rightarrow 0}\delta({\bf q})=\lim_{p_{\perp}\rightarrow 0}\delta(p_{\perp})\cdot{1\over 2\pi}\delta(k_{F}\theta_{I})
for some
I
.

Plugging in the expression for the current
J_{x}(\tau)
(
2.49
) and using the unitary (
2.48
), the current two-point function is

\displaystyle\langle{\cal T}_{\tau}J_{x}(\tau)J_{x}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dzdz^{\prime}\sum_{I,J,A,B,C,D}\langle\tilde{R}^{\dagger}_{A}(\tau,z)\tilde{R}_{B}(\tau,z)\tilde{R}^{\dagger}_{C}(0,z^{\prime})\tilde{R}_{D}(0,z^{\prime})\rangle

(3.23)

\displaystyle\cdot\overline{U_{AI}(z)\cos(\theta_{I})U^{\dagger}_{IB}(z)U_{CJ}(z^{\prime})\cos(\theta_{J})U^{\dagger}_{JD}(z^{\prime})}

(3.24)

\displaystyle=\int dzdz^{\prime}\,\Big({i\over 2\beta\sinh\big({\pi(z-z^{\prime}+i(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\over\beta}\big)}\Big)^{2}\cdot{\rm Tr}\,\overline{U(z)CU^{\dagger}(z)U(z^{\prime})CU^{\dagger}(z^{\prime})}.

(3.25)

In the first equality, we introduced the diagonal matrix
C_{II^{\prime}}=\cos\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)\delta_{II^{\prime}}
;
in the second equality, we used (
2.47
) to compute the finite-temperature fermion four-point function and expressed the product of
U
and
C
matrices using standard matrix notation.
The disorder average of the product of
U
and
C
matrices is computed as in the previous section.
To this end, we partition the interval
[0,z]
into
n
segments and the interval
[0,z^{\prime}]
into
m
segments, each of size
\delta z=z/n=z^{\prime}/m
, and decompose each
U
matrix as in (
3.9
).
We find

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\overline{\big(e^{-i\,M_{n}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\big)C\big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n}}\big)\big(e^{-i\,M_{m}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\big)\,C\big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{m}}\big)}

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\cos^{2}\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)e^{-2g_{\rm eff}k_{F}|z-z^{\prime}|}

(3.28)

\displaystyle={N\over 2}e^{-2g_{\rm eff}k_{F}|z-z^{\prime}|},

(3.29)

where
g_{\rm eff}
is defined in (
3.17
).
The details for the evaluation of the disorder average are given in Appendix
A
.
The sum over
I
in the second equality is performed for
N\rightarrow\infty
using the continuum limit
{1\over N}\sum_{I}\rightarrow\int{d\theta\over 2\pi}
.

Inserting (
3.27
) into the current two-point function (
3.23
), the conductivity (
3.22
) becomes

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega)={1\over i\omega_{n}}{k_{F}\over 2L}\int dzdz^{\prime}d\tau\,e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}\Big({i\over 2\beta\sinh^{2}\big({\pi(z-z^{\prime}+i(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\over\beta}\big)}\Big)^{2}e^{-2g_{\rm eff}k_{F}|z-z^{\prime}|}\Big|_{\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i0^{+}}.

(3.30)

Shifting
z\rightarrow z+z^{\prime}
and
\tau\rightarrow\tau+\tau^{\prime}
, the integral over
z^{\prime}
produces a factor of
L
; we next calculate the integral over
\tau
in the zero temperature limit
\beta\rightarrow\infty
.
The relevant term is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}\Big({i\over 2\pi}{1\over\big(z+i\tau\big)}\Big)^{2}=-{1\over 4\pi}|\omega_{n}|\,e^{-\omega_{n}z}\Theta\big(\omega_{n}z\big).

(3.31)

Performing the remaining integral over
z
, we obtain the conductivity

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega)={1\over 8}\cdot{1\over g_{\rm eff}-i{\omega\over 2vk_{F}}}\quad\text{(units of $e^{2}/h$)},

(3.32)

where we have restored the fermion velocity
v
, previously set to one.
This is the main result of this paper.
The dc longitudinal conductivity varies as
1/g_{\rm eff}
along the random fixed line.
An identical calculation with
\cos\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)\rightarrow\sin\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)
produces
\sigma_{yy}(\omega)=\sigma_{xx}(\omega)
.
The Hall conductivity
\sigma_{xy}(\omega)
vanishes because there is no time-reversal symmetry breaking on average.
This follows from an explicit computation similar to the above, in which
\sum_{I}\cos^{2}\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)\rightarrow\sum_{I}\cos\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)\sin\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)=0
.

We may crudely estimate the regime of validity of (
3.32
) as follows.
Recall from (
2.30
) that
g_{\rm eff}\sim g
characterizes the scale of the fluctuations of the random vector potential
{\bf A}({\bf q})
, which in turn determines the random coupling
|V_{\theta\theta^{\prime}}(p_{\perp}-p^{\prime}_{\perp})|\sim k_{F}|{\bf A}({\bf q})|\sim k_{F}\sqrt{g_{\rm eff}}
in (
2.22
).
General effective field theory considerations require
k_{F}\sqrt{g_{\rm eff}}\leq\Lambda
, where
\Lambda\ll k_{F}
is the cutoff on momenta transverse to the Fermi surface.
Inserting this inequality into (
3.32
) at
\omega=0
, we find

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\omega=0)\geq{1\over 8}\cdot{k_{F}^{2}\over\Lambda^{2}}.

(3.33)

\Lambda\ll k_{F}
(rather than
\Lambda\leq k_{F}
) ensures the scattering is primarily tangential to the Fermi surface, instead of perpendicular to it (see §
2.3
).
A study of the effects of the various leading corrections to the effective theory (
2.23
) could potentially clarify the bound (
3.33
).

4
Discussion and Summary

We have studied the effects of a quenched random, transverse magnetic field on a 2d Dirac fermion placed at finite density.
For weak disorder of sufficiently long wavelength, we showed how the effective theory reduces to an infinite collection of chiral fermions coupled by the vector potential.
This simplification allows for an exact treatment of the effects of the disorder.
We found a line of fixed points along which the longitudinal dc conductivity (
3.32
) varies continuously with the disorder variance.

The dc conductivity was calculated in the collisionless
\hbar\omega/k_{B}T\rightarrow\infty
limit.
It is important to extend our study to the opposite order of limits

, the so-called incoherent regime
[

, relevant to experiment.
(See
[
60
]
for a study of distinct dc limits of the ac conductivity of a clean Dirac fermion at zero density.)
This question is pertinent to the expected universality of the conductivity at a quantum phase transition
[
61
]
.

We focused exclusively on the point where the Dirac fermion is massless.
In the clean limit at finite density, a metal intervenes between integer quantum Hall states with
\sigma_{xy}=\pm{1\over 2}{e^{2}\over h}

The disorder we studied was of sufficiently long wavelength that it mediated elastic scattering between nearby Fermi points only.
The opposite regime, in which all Fermi points are coupled by the disorder, might be interesting to consider.
The action of the theory at energy
\omega=0
takes the form:

\displaystyle S_{\omega=0}=\int dz\sum_{I,J=1}^{N}\,R^{\ast}_{I}(z)\Big(i\partial_{z}\delta_{IJ}+J_{IJ}\Big)R_{J}(z),

(4.1)

with random

Interpreting

The similarity of the effective theory (
2.46
) of the random Dirac Fermi surface to the theory of
N
chiral free fermions in 1d suggests a possible route towards a non-Fermi liquid generalization, in which disorder may be studied simultaneously.
For example, we may consider two independent Dirac fermions—with chemical potentials that are of equal magnitude and opposite sign—in the presence of quenched vector potential disorder.
The effective action is
S_{R}+S_{L}
, where

\displaystyle S_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\int dtdz\sum_{I,J=1}^{N}\,R^{\ast}_{I}(t,z)\Big(i\big(\partial_{t}+\partial_{z}\big)\delta_{IJ}+{1\over N}V_{IJ}(z)\Big)R_{J}(t,z),

(4.2)

\displaystyle S_{\rm L}

\displaystyle=\int dtdz\sum_{I,J=1}^{N}\,(L^{\ast}_{I}(t,z)\Big(i\big(\partial_{t}-\partial_{z}\big)\delta_{IJ}-{1\over N}V_{IJ}(z)\Big)L_{J}(t,z),

(4.3)

and
R_{I}
(
L_{J}

Couplings between right (
R^{\ast}_{I}R_{I}
) and left (
L^{\ast}_{J}L_{J}
) densities leads to Luttinger liquid-like behavior.
So long as the couplings preserve a diagonal subgroup of the

The implications and possible microscopic origin of such a theory are unclear.

Appendix A
Disorder Average of Products of
U
and
C
Matrices

In this appendix, we detail the evaluation of the disorder average of products of
U
and
C
matrices, focusing on the product that appears in (
3.23
):

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\,\overline{U(z)CU^{\dagger}(z)U(z^{\prime})CU^{\dagger}(z^{\prime})}\equiv\overline{U_{AI}(z)C_{II^{\prime}}U^{\dagger}_{I^{\prime}B}(z)U_{BJ}(z^{\prime})C_{JJ^{\prime}}U^{\dagger}_{J^{\prime}A}(z^{\prime})},

(A.1)

where
U_{IJ}(z)
is defined in (
2.48
),
C_{II^{\prime}}=\cos\big({2\pi I\over N}\big)\delta_{II^{\prime}}
, and the sums over
A,I,I^{\prime},B,J,J^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N
are understood.
The computation of (
3.16
) is similar and will not be discussed.

To calculate (
A.1
), we discretize the
z
direction into segments
[z_{k-1},z_{k}]
of length
\delta z>0
, where
z_{k}=k\delta z
for all integer
k
.
We take
z=z_{n}
,
z^{\prime}=z_{m}
, and consider the limit
n,m\rightarrow\infty
with
z_{n}
and
z_{m}
fixed.

U(z)
is decomposed as

\displaystyle U(z)=e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{n-1}}^{z_{n}}dz^{\prime}\,V(z^{\prime})}\cdots e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}dz^{\prime}\,V(z^{\prime})}e^{-{i\over N}\int_{z_{0}}^{z_{1}}dz^{\prime}\,V(z^{\prime})}

(A.2)

and similarly for
U(z^{\prime})
.
Since
\delta z
is infinitesimal, we approximate

\displaystyle{1\over N}\int_{z_{j-1}}^{z_{j}}dz^{\prime}\,V(z^{\prime})\approx{1\over N}V(z_{j})\delta z\equiv M_{j}.

(A.3)

The decomposition of
U
becomes

\displaystyle U(z)=e^{-i\,M_{n}}e^{-iM_{n-1}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}.

(A.4)

From (
2.51
),
M_{j}
is a zero-mean Gaussian random variable with variance,

\displaystyle\overline{(M_{i})_{IJ}(M_{j})_{KL}}={gk_{F}\over N}\delta_{IL}\delta_{JK}f(z_{i}-z_{j})\delta z^{2},

(A.5)

where
f(z_{i}-z_{j})
is defined by

\displaystyle f(z_{i}-z_{j})\delta z=f_{0}\delta_{|i-j|,0}+f_{1}\delta_{|i-j|,1}+f_{2}\delta_{|i-j|,2}+...+f_{k}\delta_{|i-j|,k}.

(A.6)

We refer to
f_{0}
as the on-site correlation coefficient,
f_{1}
as the
1^{st}
neighbor correlation coefficient,
f_{2}
as the
2^{nd}
neighbor correlation coefficient, etc.
Using (
A.6
), we write the disorder average (
A.1
) as

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\,\overline{U(z)CU^{\dagger}(z)U(z^{\prime})CU^{\dagger}(z^{\prime})}=\text{Tr}[C\,C]+W_{0}+W_{1}+\ldots+W_{k},

(A.7)

where
W_{j}
denotes the contribution from
f_{j}
.
The first term
\text{Tr}[C\,C]
is the constant term without any Wick contraction due to the disorder averaging.

1^{st}
-neighbor correlation:
W_{1}

We begin with the
1^{st}
neighbor contribution
W_{1}
.
We substitute (
A.4
) into (
A.7
) and expand the exponentials to obtain

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{n}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n}}\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}

(A.8)

\displaystyle\times\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{m}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{BJ}\,C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{m}}\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{1}}

(A.9)

\displaystyle\approx\overline{\Big((1-iM_{n})\cdots(1-iM_{1})\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big((1+iM_{1})\cdots(1+iM_{n})\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}

(A.10)

\displaystyle\times\overline{\Big((1-iM_{m})\cdots(1-iM_{1})\Big)_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big((1+iM_{1})\cdots(1+iM_{m})\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{1}},

(A.11)

where
\Big|_{f_{1}}
indicates that we only consider the contributions due to the
1^{st}
neighbor correlation
f_{1}
in (
A.6
).
Note that the disorder average in the first equality is being performed over the entire product, not separately over each term in the product.
Assuming
n>m
, there are four possible contractions involving
M_{n}
that we isolate by writing
W_{1}
as

\displaystyle\overline{\Big((-iM_{n})(-iM_{n-1})\Big)_{AI}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}\;\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}+\overline{(-iM_{n})_{AI}(iM_{n-1})_{I^{\prime}B}}\;C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}

(A.12)

\displaystyle+\overline{(-iM_{n-1})_{AI}(iM_{n})_{I^{\prime}B}}C_{II^{\prime}}\;\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}+\delta_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\overline{\Big((iM_{n-1})(iM_{n})\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}\;\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}

(A.13)

\displaystyle+\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{n-1}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n-1}}\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}\Big(e^{-i\,M_{m}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{BJ}\,C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{m}}\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{1}}

(A.14)

\displaystyle=2g\,f_{1}\,k_{F}\,\delta z\,\Big(-\delta_{AI}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}+\frac{1}{N}\delta_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{AB}\Big)\delta_{BJ}\hat{C}_{II^{\prime}}\hat{C}_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}

(A.15)

\displaystyle+\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{n-1}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n-1}}\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}\Big(e^{-i\,M_{m}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{BJ}\,C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{m}}\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{1}}.

(A.16)

The subleading term
\propto\frac{1}{N}
, which comes from “crossing-contractions,” vanishes because
C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{II^{\prime}}=\text{Tr}[C]=0
.

We continue as above sequentially contracting
1^{st}
neighbor pairs involving
M_{n-1},M_{n-2},\ldots,M_{m+2}
to arrive at

\displaystyle W_{1}=-2g\,f_{1}\,k_{F}\text{Tr}[CC]\Big(n-(m+2)+1\Big)\;\delta z+\Big(\text{Contractions of $M_{m+1},\ldots,M_{1}$}\Big)

(A.18)

There are eight contractions that involve
M_{m+1}
and

\displaystyle\Big[\overline{\Big((-iM_{m+1})(-iM_{m})\Big)_{AI}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}+\overline{(-iM_{m+1})_{AI}(iM_{m})_{I^{\prime}B}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}

(A.19)

\displaystyle+\overline{(-iM_{m+1})_{AI}(-iM_{m})_{BJ}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}+\overline{(-iM_{m+1})_{AI}(+iM_{m})_{J^{\prime}A}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big]

(A.20)

\displaystyle+\Big[\overline{(M_{m})_{AI}(M_{m+1})_{I^{\prime}B}}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}-\overline{(M_{m}M_{m+1})_{I^{\prime}B}}\delta_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{BJ}C_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}

(A.21)

In the first
[\ldots]
, the first term cancels with the fourth and the second term cancels with the third; the same pairs mutually cancel in the second

Next we consider the contractions involving
M_{m}

These cancellations continue through to contractions involving
M_{1}

Summarizing, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}=-\text{Tr}[CC]\Big(2g\,f_{1}\,k_{F}\,[n-(m+2)+1]\delta z\Big).

(A.22)

W_{2},\ldots,W_{k}

Following the logic used to compute the

\displaystyle=\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{n}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n}}\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}

On-site Correlation:
W_{0}

Unlike the off-site correlations discussed above, we have to expand the exponentials to quadratic order to obtain the contribution from on-site correlations:

\displaystyle W_{0}

\displaystyle=\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{n}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{AI}C_{II^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{n}}\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}

(A.29)

\displaystyle\times\overline{\Big(e^{-i\,M_{m}}\cdots e^{-i\,M_{1}}\Big)_{BJ}\,C_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big(e^{i\,M_{1}}\cdots e^{i\,M_{m}}\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{0}}

(A.30)

\displaystyle\approx\overline{\Big((1-iM_{n}-\frac{M_{n}^{2}}{2})\cdots(1-iM_{1}-\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{2})\Big)_{AI}\hat{C}_{II^{\prime}}\Big((1+iM_{1}-\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{2})\cdots(1+iM_{n}-\frac{M_{n}^{2}}{2})\Big)_{I^{\prime}B}}

(A.31)

\displaystyle\times\overline{\Big((1+iM_{m}-\frac{M_{m}^{2}}{2})\cdots(1+iM_{1}-\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{2})\Big)_{BJ}\hat{C}_{JJ^{\prime}}\Big((1+iM_{1}-\frac{M_{1}^{2}}{2})\cdots(1+iM_{m}-\frac{M_{m}^{2}}{2})\Big)_{J^{\prime}A}}\Big|_{f_{0}}

(A.32)

\displaystyle=gf_{0}k_{F}\delta z\Big(-\delta_{AI}\delta_{I^{\prime}B}+\frac{2}{N}\delta_{II^{\prime}}\delta_{AB}\Big)\delta_{BJ}\hat{C}_{II^{\prime}}\hat{C}_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{J^{\prime}A}+\Big(\text{Contractions of $M_{n-1},\ldots,M_{1}$}\Big).

(A.33)

The crossing term
\propto{1\over N}
again vanishes.
Continuing in this way we arrive at

\displaystyle W_{0}=-\text{Tr}[CC]\Big(g\,f_{0}\,k_{F}\,[n-(m+1)+1]\delta z\Big)+\Big(\text{Contractions of $M_{m},\ldots,M_{1}$}\Big).

(A.35)

Similar to the cancellations that were discussed in the context of the

Thus, we have

\displaystyle W_{0}=-\text{Tr}[CC]\Big(g\,f_{0}\,k_{F}\,[n-(m+1)+1]\delta z\Big).

(A.36)

Final Result

Collecting all correlations
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
and replacing
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\delta z=z
and
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}m\delta z=z^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\,\overline{U(z)CU^{\dagger}(z)U(z^{\prime})CU^{\dagger}(z^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[CC]\Big(1-2gk_{F}\delta z\big({f_{0}\over 2}(n-m)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}[n-m-k]\big)\Big)

(A.37)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}[CC]e^{-2gk_{F}|z-z^{\prime}|\big({f_{0}\over 2}+f_{1}+\ldots f_{k}\big)}.

(A.38)

Note that each term proportional to
k
in the sum vanishes at large
n
and
m
.
This concludes our calculation of the disorder average in (
3.27
).
In the main text,
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are nonzero, and
f_{k\geq 2}=0
