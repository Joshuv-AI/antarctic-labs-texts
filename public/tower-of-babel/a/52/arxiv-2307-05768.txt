Increasing subsequences of linear size in random permutations and the Robinson–Schensted tableaux of permutons
Victor Dubach

Abstract
The study of longest increasing subsequences (LIS) in permutations led to that of Young diagrams via Robinson–Schensted’s (RS) correspondence. In a celebrated paper, Vershik and Kerov obtained a limit theorem for such diagrams and found that the LIS of a uniform permutation of size n n behaves as 2 ​ n 2\sqrt{n} . Independently and much later, Hoppen et al. introduced the theory of permutons as a scaling limit of permutations. In this paper, we extend in some sense the RS correspondence of permutations to the space of permutons. When the “RS-tableaux” of a permuton are non-trivial, we show that the RS-tableaux of random permutations sampled from this permuton exhibit a linear behavior, in the sense that their first rows and columns have lengths of linear order. In particular, the LIS of such permutations behaves as a multiple of n n . We also prove some large deviation results for these convergences. Finally, by studying asymptotic properties of Fomin’s algorithm for permutations, we show that the RS-tableaux of a permuton satisfy a partial differential equation.



Keywords:
random permutations, permutons, longest increasing subsequence, Robinson–Schensted correspondence, Fomin’s local rules

Background

1.1
The theory of permutons and sampled permutations

A Borel probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]^{2}
is called a
permuton
when each of its marginals is uniform,
i.e.
for any
0\leq~x\leq~y\leq 1
:

\mu\big([x,y]\times[0,1]\big)=\mu\big([0,1]\times[x,y]\big)=y-x.

The theory of permutons was first studied in
[
HKM+13
]
, named in
[
GGKK15
]
, and is now widely studied (see e.g.
[
KP13
,
Muk15
,
BBF+18
,
BBF+22
]
and references therein). It serves as a scaling limit model for permutations: if
\sigma
is a permutation of size
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, one can associate it with the permuton
\mu_{\sigma}
having density

n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\left[\frac{i\!-\!1}{n},\frac{i}{n}\right]\times\left[\frac{\sigma(i)\!-\!1}{n},\frac{\sigma(i)}{n}\right]}

with respect to Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
. Putting weak convergence topology on the space of permutons, this gives a meaning to the convergence of a sequence of permutations towards a limit permuton.
This convergence is notably characterized by the asymptotics of pattern densities,
cementing the theory of permutons within the general framework of sampling limit theory (see e.g.
[
LS06
]
for dense graphs,
[
Jan11
]
for posets,
[
ET22
]
and
[
Grü23
]
for trees).

Fundamental to permuton theory is the fact that each permuton gives rise to a model of random permutations.
Consider points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
in the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
whose
x
-coordinates and
y
-coordinates are all distinct.
One can then naturally define a permutation
\sigma
of size
n
in the following way: for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
, let
\sigma(i)=j
whenever the point with
i
-th lowest
x
-coordinate has
j
-th lowest
y
-coordinate.
We denote by
\mathrm{Perm}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
this permutation; see
Figure

1
for an example.
When
\mu
is a permuton and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are random i.i.d. points distributed under
\mu
, we write
\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for the law of this random permutation.
Since the marginals of
\mu
are continuous, this permutation is almost surely well defined.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
HKM+13
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a permuton and
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a sequence of random permutations such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
.
Then the following weak convergence holds almost surely:

\mu_{\sigma_{n}}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu.

Note that this model can be generalized to more than just permutons.
As in
[
Dub23
]
we say that a Borel probability measure on
[0,1]^{2}
is a
pre-permuton
when none of its marginals have any atom; then the law
\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
is still well defined.
This notion has the advantage of being stable by induced sub-measures, which is why we use it for some results.
Going from pre-permutons to permutons and conversely is fairly easy: one can associate any pre-permuton
\mu
with a unique permuton
\hat{\mu}
such that random permutations sampled from
\mu
or
\hat{\mu}
have the same law (see e.g. Remark 1.2 in
[
BDMW24
]
).
Moreover this is done via a transformation of the unit square which is nondecreasing in each coordinate, hence most quantities defined in later sections have the same values on pre-permutons as on their associated permutons.

1.2
Longest increasing subsequences and Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence

Let
w=w_{1}\dots w_{n}
be a finite word with positive integer letters.
We say that a subset of indices
\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\}
is an
increasing subsequence of
w
if
w_{i_{1}}<\dots<w_{i_{\ell}}
.
The maximum length of such a sequence is called length of the longest increasing subsequence of
w
and denoted by
\mathrm{LIS}(w)
.
More generally for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, define the length of the longest
k
-increasing subsequence of
w
as

\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(w):=\max_{I_{1},\dots,I_{k}}|I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{k}|

where the maximum is taken over all families
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
(not necessarily disjoint) of increasing subsequences of
w
.
Also define
\mathrm{LDS}_{k}
similarly by replacing “increasing” with “decreasing” in the previous definition.
The study of longest increasing subsequences is intimately related to a bijective map called Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence, which we present next.

A
(Young) diagram
is a visual representation of an integer partition: it consists of a sequence of left-justified rows, containing nonincreasing numbers of boxes.
A
(Young) tableau
is a filling of a diagram, called its
shape
, with positive integers.
When the filling is nondecreasing (resp. increasing) in each row and increasing in each column, we call the tableau
semistandard
(resp.
standard
).
Robinson–Schensted’s (RS) correspondence is a map between words and pairs of tableaux sharing the same shape, see
Figure

2
.
The first tableau, called the P-tableau, is semistandard and filled with the letters of
w
, while the second one, called the Q-tableau, is standard and filled with the integers from
1
to the length of
w
.
If
\sigma
is a permutation, seen as a word via one-line notation, then its P-tableau is standard.

There are many ways to define this correspondence, for which we refer the reader to
[
Ful96
]
.
Below we recall its equivalent description given by Greene’s theorem.
For any word
w
, define

\mathrm{sh}(w)=\left(\mathrm{sh}^{\mathrm{row}}(w)\,,\,\mathrm{sh}^{\mathrm{col}}(w)\right):=\Big(\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(w)-\mathrm{LIS}_{k\!-\!1}(w)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\;,\;\big(\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(w)-\mathrm{LDS}_{k\!-\!1}(w)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\Big)

with convention
\mathrm{LIS}_{0}(w)=\mathrm{LDS}_{0}(w)=0
.

For any word

and

In particular, those sequences are nonincreasing.

Note that the definition of
\mathrm{sh}
is redundant, as the sequence of row lengths can be deduced from the sequence of column lengths and vice versa (these are usually called

).
However we shall see that this property is lost in the permuton limit.

Greene’s theorem can also be used to define the RS-tableaux.
A standard tableau of size
n
can be equivalently defined as an increasing sequence of
n
diagrams, where the
k
-th diagram is the shape of the sub-tableau containing the boxes with values at most
k
.
For example in

.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For any
1\leq i,j\leq n
define its sub-word
\sigma^{i,j}
as the sequence of letters
\sigma(h)
with
h\leq i
and
\sigma(h)\leq j
.
One can see that for each
1\leq k\leq n
, the RS-shape of
\sigma^{n,k}
is the
k
-th diagram defining
P(\sigma)
and the RS-shape of
\sigma^{k,n}
is the
k
-th diagram defining
Q(\sigma)
.
This motivates the following alternative definition of Robinson–Schensted’s tableaux:

\mathrm{RS}(\sigma):=\big(\lambda^{\sigma}(n,\cdot),\lambda^{\sigma}(\cdot,n)\big)

(1)

where, for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
,

\lambda^{\sigma}(i,j)=\big(\lambda^{\sigma}_{k}(i,j)\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}:=\mathrm{sh}^{\mathrm{row}}\left(\sigma^{i,j}\right).

1.3
LIS and RS-shapes of random permutations

Ulam formulated the following question in the 60’s: consider for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
a uniformly random permutation
\sigma_{n}
of size
n
, and write

\ell_{n}:=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\big].

Then what can we say about the asymptotic behavior of
\ell_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
?
The study of longest increasing subsequences has since then been a surprisingly fertile research subject with unexpected links to diverse areas of mathematics; see
[
Rom15
]
for a nice introduction to this topic.

A solution to Ulam’s problem was found by Vershik and Kerov through the use of Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence.
More precisely they encoded diagrams
\lambda
by some continuous functions
\omega_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and established the following limit theorem (also simultaneously proved in
[
LS77
]
).
See
Figure

3
for an illustration.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
VK77
]
)
.

For each integer
n
, let
\sigma_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of size
n
and write

\omega_{n}(\cdot):=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{sh}(\sigma_{n})}(\sqrt{n}\;\cdot)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Then the sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
converges uniformly in probability to some explicit limit curve.

Figure 3:
The RS-tableaux of a size
20000
uniformly random permutation. Higher values in the tableaux are represented by brighter colors.

By
Theorem

1.2
this convergence corresponds to explicit non-trivial limits as
n
goes to infinity for
\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{\alpha\sqrt{n}}(\sigma_{n})
,
\alpha\in[0,2]
.
This also implies
\liminf\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ell_{n}\geq 2
, and with an additional argument for the upper bound we can deduce:

Corollary 1.4

(
[
VK77
]
)
.

For each
n
, let
\sigma_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of size
n
and write
\ell_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})]
. Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\ell_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}2.

The study of
\ell_{n}
later culminated with the work of Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
BDJ99
]
, who found its second-order asymptotics.

One can then naturally ask: is it possible to obtain similar asymptotics for sampled random permutations?
One of the first advances on this question was obtained by Deuschel and Zeitouni who proved:

Theorem 1.5

(
[
DZ95
]
)
.

Let
\mu_{\rho}
be a permuton with a
C_{b}^{1}
density
\rho
which is bounded below on
[0,1]^{2}
.
If for each integer
n
,
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu_{\rho})
, then:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}K_{\rho}

in probability, for some positive constant
K_{\rho}
defined by a variational problem.

This question of generalization is still an active research subject.
In the recent paper
[
Sjö23
]
, the author studies the
\sqrt{n}
scaling limit for the RS-shape of sampled permutations when the sampling permuton is absolutely continuous.

The goal of this paper is to extend in some sense the function
\mathrm{LIS}
, as well as the first rows and columns of RS-shape and tableaux, to the space of permutons.
This will allow us to deduce linear asymptotics of these quantities for sampled permutations; therefore our results are different in nature from the
\sqrt{n}
regime of
[
DZ95
,
Sjö23
]
, and yield non-trivial asymptotics for certain types of permutons displaying a singular part with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
.
We introduce the RS-shape of permutons in
Section

2.1
and present these asymptotics.
Then in
Section

2.2
we investigate large deviation principles related to those convergences, exhibiting a difference of behavior between the upper and lower tails.
In
Section

2.3
we define the RS-tableaux of a permuton and explain how a discrete algorithm translates in this continuous setting, then in
Section

2.4
we present a few results about the injectivity of permutons’ RS-tableaux.
Finally, we illustrate these objects with several examples in
Section

2.5
.

2
Our results

2.1
The RS-shape of (pre-)permutons

Throughout the paper we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}

the sets of permutons, pre-permutons, probability measures and finite measures on
[0,1]^{2}
.
Say a subset
A\subset[0,1]^{2}
is nondecreasing (resp. nonincreasing) if

\text{for any }(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in A,\quad(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})\geq 0\quad\big(\text{resp. }(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})\leq 0\big)

and for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, say
A
is
k
-nondecreasing (resp.
k
-nonincreasing) if it can be written as a union of
k
(not necessarily disjoint) nondecreasing (resp. nonincreasing) subsets of
[0,1]^{2}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
and
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\searrow}([0,1]^{2})
respectively the sets of closed
k
-nondecreasing and
k
-nonincreasing subsets, and define for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})}\mu(A)\quad,\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\searrow}([0,1]^{2})}\mu(A).

When
k=1
we may drop the subscript
k
.
By convention we set
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{0}(\mu)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{0}(\mu):=0
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
, the supremum in the definition of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)
is reached.
Moreover the function
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
is upper semi-continuous on
\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}
, i.e. for any sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converging to
\mu
in
\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}
one has
\limsup\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{n})\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)
.
The same holds for
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}
.

The functions
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
are not continuous:
for instance, one can check that the permutons
\mu_{\sigma}
defined in
Section

1.1
satisfy
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu_{\sigma})=0
for all permutations
\sigma
, although this family is dense in
\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}
by
Theorem

1.1
.

The next result, along with
Lemma

3.5
used to prove it, justifies the idea that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k},\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}
are generalizations of
\mathrm{LIS}_{k},\mathrm{LDS}_{k}
to the space of (pre-)permutons:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
and
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the following convergences hold almost surely:

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad;\quad\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LDS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu).

This result does not hold for any sequence of permutations
\sigma_{n}
such that
\mu_{\sigma_{n}}
converges to
\mu
.
For example, it is easy to construct a sequence of permutations with sublinear
\mathrm{LIS}
that converges towards the diagonal permuton.
We could expect this result to hold under appropriate regularity assumptions on the sequence
(\sigma_{n})
, similar to the ones exhibited in
[
Muk15
]
for the study of small cycles, but such assumptions remain to be discovered.

We can then extend the notion of RS-shape to the space of permutons by analogy with Greene’s theorem.
For any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
, define:

\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu)=\left(\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}^{\mathrm{row}}(\mu),\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}^{\mathrm{col}}(\mu)\right):=\left(\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)-\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k\!-\!1}(\mu)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu)-\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k\!-\!1}(\mu)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\right).

An immediate consequence of
2.2
is the convergence of sampled permutations’ shapes towards the sampling permuton’s shape, component-wise.
Another one is that this quantity belongs to the Thoma simplex, which appears in the limit theory of integer partitions (see e.g.
[
FMN20
]
and references therein):

Proposition 2.3
.

Define the Thoma simplex
\Omega
as

\Omega:=\left\{\Big(\,\big(\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0\big)\,,\,\big(\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0\big)\,\Big)\>:\>\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\geq 1}\beta_{j}\leq 1\right\}.

Then for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
,
\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu)\in\Omega
.

Remark 1
.

Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
can be related to a law of large numbers for random partitions due to Vershik and Kerov.
Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega
and
\gamma:=1-\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i}\beta_{i}
.
In
[
VK81
]
the authors defined a measure
M_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
on Young diagrams of size
n
and proved that
\alpha_{i}
(resp.
\beta_{i}
) is the asymptotic proportion of boxes in the
i
-th row (resp. column).
In
[
VK86
]
they showed that this measure could be interpreted as the RS-shape of some random word.
For this, fix an alphabet
\mathbb{L}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\}\sqcup\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots\}\sqcup[0,1]
where
a_{i}
’s are called
row letters
,
b_{i}
’s are called
column letters
, and elements of
[0,1]
are neutral letters.
Define a measure
m_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
on words of length
n
by choosing independently each letter with a probability
\alpha_{i}
of being
a_{i}
,
\beta_{i}
of being
b_{i}
, and
\gamma
of being uniform in
[0,1]
.
Adapt Robinson–Schensted’s algorithm on such words by requiring that each row of the P-tableau contains each column letter at most once, and each column of the P-tableau contains each row letter at most once (but rows may contain one row letter multiple times and columns may contain one column letter multiple times).
Then the measure
M_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
is the push-forward by RS-shape of
m_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
.
Equivalently, it is possible to interpret random diagrams under
M_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
as the RS-shapes of certain sampled permutations.
For this, associate each row letter
a_{i}
with an upward diagonal of mass
\alpha_{i}
and each column letter
b_{i}
with a downward diagonal of mass
\beta_{i}
, then place them in
[0,1]^{2}
and interpose uniform mass according to the order on
\mathbb{L}
.
This defines a permuton
\mu_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
whose sampled permutations are standardizations of words under
m_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
(see e.g. Section 3.7 in
[
Śni14
]
for a definition), hence their RS-shapes follow the law
M_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}^{n}
and we can recover with
2.2
the asymptotics of
[
VK81
]
.
See
Figure

for a representation of the permuton
\mu_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
.

Remark 2
.

Our results also have some connections to

This theory was introduced in
[

These two theories share several similarities as well as a formal link; the

The function

Moreover the counterpart of
2.2
for

,

[

The results of this section are proved in
Sections

.
From now on we will focus on the study of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
, since results on
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}
can be deduced by mirroring the permuton:

\mu^{\mathrm{rev}}:=\psi_{*}\mu\quad\text{where for any }(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2},\quad\psi(x,y):=(1\!\!-\!\!x,y).

Since
\psi
exchanges nonincreasing subsets with nondecreasing ones, we have for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{rev}}|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[1\!-\!x,1]\times[0,y]}\right).

2.2
Large deviation principles

In this section we present partial large deviation principles concerning the convergences of
2.2
.
First we state an upper tail in
Theorem

2.4
: the simple idea is that long increasing subsequences are formed when many points appear in a maximal increasing subset of the permuton.
However this idea does not work for a lower tail, since having few points in a maximal increasing subset does not prevent long increasing subsequences from forming somewhere else: this difference of behavior is made formal in
2.8
.
In the
\sqrt{n}
regime when the permuton has a regular density, this asymmetry translates into a change of speed between the upper and lower tails
[
DZ99
]
.
Here in the linear regime, we prove in
Theorem

2.7
that if there exists a lower tail large deviation then the speed is the same as for the upper tail.

For any
p\in[0,1]
, define the Legendre transform of the Bernoulli law
{\rm Ber}(p)
as:

\text{for any }q\in[0,1],\quad\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q):=q\log\frac{q}{p}+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-p}\quad\in[0,+\infty].

Cramér’s theorem (recalled in
Theorem

3.7
) states that this is the good rate function for the large deviation principle of binomial laws.
We show that this is also the rate function for the following upper tail:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Then for any
\alpha\in\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu),1\right]
:

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)}^{*}(\alpha).

In particular when
\alpha=1
and
k=1
we get the following convergence:

Corollary 2.5
.

For any permuton
\mu
and
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
:

\mathbf{P}(\sigma_{n}=\mathrm{id}_{n})^{1/n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu).

For the lower tail we restrict our study to
\mathrm{LIS}
for convenience, but this could be generalized to
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}
.
We begin with an upper bound in the same flavor as in
Theorem

2.4
:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
and
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Then for any
\beta\in\left(0,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu)\right)
:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)\;\leq\;-\!\sup_{j\geq 1,\,j\beta<\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{j}(\mu)}\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{j}(\mu)}^{*}(j\beta).

This upper bound is in general better than the simpler one
\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu)}^{*}(\beta)
; take for example
\mu
such that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{2}(\mu)=2\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu)>0
.
Since it is non-zero whenever
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu)>0
, it implies that the
n
speed for a lower tail is not “too fast”.
Conversely the following result states that, except in trivial cases, this speed is not “too slow” either:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
and
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
\beta\in(0,1]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying
\frac{1}{k\!+\!1}<\beta\leq\frac{1}{k}
.
Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\liminf\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)=-\infty
;

(ii)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\geq\beta n\big)=1
;

(iii)

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=1
.

Unfortunately the upper bound of
2.6
is not sharp.
We actually show that a rate function for this lower tail can not be expressed as a function of
\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu)
:

Proposition 2.8
.

There exist
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
satisfying
\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu_{1})=\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu_{2})
and

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})<\beta n\big)<\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)

for some
\beta<\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu_{1})
, where
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu_{1})
and
\tau_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu_{2})
.

We believe a lower tail rate function could be expressed with the help of
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)
or
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
defined in
Section

2.3
, but this question remains unanswered.
The results of this section are proved in
Sections

3.3
,
3.5
and
3.4
.

Remark 3
.

A large deviation principle for the sequence of sampled permutations
(\sigma_{n})
was recently obtained in
[
BDMW24
, Theorem 1.6]
.
By semi-continuity of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
(
2.1
), we can deduce large deviation inequalities for the sequence
\left(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\right)
by a variant of the contraction principle (the standard contraction principle carries large deviation results over
continuous
functions, see e.g.
[
DZ98
]
).
This actually yields the same inequalities as if we apply the same “semi-contraction principle” directly to Sanov’s theorem, the latter being done in
3.9
.
We do not know whether these bounds are optimal are not.
We do not use this approach for
Theorem

2.4
, and instead opt for a self-contained proof.
However, we use the lower tail bound of
3.9
in the proof of
Theorem

2.7
.

Remark 4
.

The counterpart of
2.5
for graphons was proved in
[
CKP22
]
, and it is likely that
Theorem

2.4
would also hold in that context.
However our study of the lower tail in
Theorem

2.7
seems rather specific to permutations, as it hinges on the fact that any permutation
\sigma
of size
n
satisfies
\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma)\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma)\geq n
.
The analog of this property for graphs is not true in general; note that inversion graphs of permutations are
perfect graphs
.

2.3
The RS-tableaux of (pre-)permutons

By analogy with (
1
), define the RS-tableaux of any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
as

\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu):=\left(\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}(1,\cdot),\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}(\cdot,1)\right)

where for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, we write
\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}
for the measure obtained by restricting
\mu
to
[0,x]\times[0,y]
and:

\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}(x,y)=\left(\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}(x,y)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}:=\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}^{\mathrm{row}}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)=\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)-\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k\!-\!1}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}

In the continuity of
2.2
, we deduce in
Section

4.1
linear asymptotics for the RS-tableaux of sampled permutations:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the function
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}
is nondecreasing and
1
-Lipschitz in each variable.
Moreover we have the following almost sure uniform convergence on
[0,1]^{2}
:

\frac{1}{n}\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}_{k}\left(\lfloor\cdot\,n\rfloor,\lfloor\cdot\,n\rfloor\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}.

The RS-tableaux of a permutation can be constructed via an algorithm known as
Fomin’s local rules
, see
Section

4.2
.
Its usual version works by labeling the vertices of a grid with diagrams, as in
[
Sag01
, Section 5.2]
, but it is equivalently possible to label the edges of this grid with integers
[
Vie18
]
.
This edge version is well adapted to our framework, since labels then correspond to increment indices of
\lambda^{\sigma}
.
The direct algorithm recursively constructs each
\lambda^{\sigma}(i\!+\!1,j\!+\!1)
from
\lambda^{\sigma}(i\!+\!1,j)
,
\lambda^{\sigma}(i,j\!+\!1)
,
\lambda^{\sigma}(i,j)
and
\sigma
.
Its reciprocal recursively constructs each
\lambda^{\sigma}(i,j)
from
\lambda^{\sigma}(i\!+\!1,j)
,
\lambda^{\sigma}(i,j\!+\!1)
and
\lambda^{\sigma}(i\!+\!1,j\!+\!1)
.

It is then natural to wonder if there exists a continuous version of this algorithm, translating as a partial differential equation on
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
.
It is indeed the case for the inverse local rules, as stated in the following result:

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose
\mu
is a permuton satisfying
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
(x,y)\in(0,1]^{2}
be such that the left-derivatives

\alpha_{k}:=\partial^{-}_{x}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{k}:=\partial^{-}_{y}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)

for
1\leq k\leq r
all exist. Fix
s,t\geq 0
.
Then we have the following directional semi-derivatives for each
1\leq k\leq r
:

\underset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\lim}\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x-t\epsilon,y-s\epsilon)}{\epsilon}=\phi\big((t\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(s\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r}\big)

(2)

where
\phi
is a continuous function defined in
4.12
.

The proof of
Theorem

2.10
comes in several steps and introduces methods that might be interesting on their own.
The general idea is to study Fomin’s inverse local rules on
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
and exhibit their asymptotic behavior, yielding properties of
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
.
To this aim, we see sequences of edge labels as words, for which we introduce an appropriate equivalence relation.
Surprisingly enough, this equivalence relation is implied by the Knuth equivalence of words (see
Section

4.4
and
4.9
).
Along with the derivability condition of
Theorem

2.10
this allows us to show in
Section

4.6
that edge labels of
\sigma_{n}
behave, locally and asymptotically, as if they were ordered in a deterministic way.
This behavior yields the function
\phi
introduced and studied in
Section

4.5
.
We refer to the discussion at the end of
Section

4.2
for more details.

A few comments on this theorem are in order:

•

It is possible to generalize this result to pre-permutons, using the associated permuton and the chain rule.

•

If
s=0
then the left-hand side of
Equation

2
is
t\alpha_{k}
by definition, and indeed one can check with
Equations

40
and
41
that
\phi\big((t\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(0)\big)=t\alpha_{k}
.
By symmetry, an analogous remark holds when
t=0
.

•

When
k=r
, the left-hand side of
Equation

2
depends only on the “last row derivatives”
\alpha_{r}
and
\beta_{r}
, and equals
\phi\big((t\alpha_{r}),(s\beta_{r})\big)=\max(t\alpha_{r},s\beta_{r})
.
When
k<r
, the
k
-th to
r
-th rows intervene in an intricate way through the function
\phi
.

•

Unfortunately we have not been able to establish a continuous version of Fomin’s
direct
algorithm, as the dependence on permutation points makes the asymptotic study more difficult.
It seems likely that
Theorem

2.10
could be generalized in this direction, but this question is left open.

•

It would also be interesting to get rid of the hypothesis
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
; more precisely we would like to weaken it to
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)=1
with the notation of
Section

2.4
.
We can always assume that this is satisfied thanks to
Theorem

2.12
.
However we could neither extend
\phi
to infinite sequences nor make our techniques work when Fomin’s edge labels are unbounded.

•

Equation

2
can be related to the “hydrodynamical approach” used by Aldous and Diaconis in
[
AD95
]
for the study of
\mathrm{LIS}
in the uniform case.
When
\sigma_{n}
is a uniform permutation of size
n
, Hammersley
[
Ham72
]
studied the function
\lambda_{1}^{\sigma_{n}}
(a very similar one actually) and used the subadditive ergodic theorem to obtain the asymptotic equivalence
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})]\sim c\sqrt{n}
for some constant
c>0
.
In
[
AD95
]
, the authors investigated Hammersley’s process in more depth, provided some heuristics as to why
c=2
, and proved it in this framework.
The general idea is that for any
y
, the counting process
x\mapsto\lambda_{1}^{\sigma_{n}}(x,y)
should approximate a Poisson process.
Under this hypothesis, one can argue that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\lambda_{1}^{\sigma_{n}}(x,y)\right]
should asymptotically satisfy a PDE whose solution is
2\sqrt{xy}
.
Therefore
Equation

2
can be interpreted as an analog of this, in the
n
scaling limit for non-uniform permutations and for the first rows and columns of the shape.
It could be interesting to try this approach in the case of a permuton with “regular” density, in a context similar to that of
[
DZ95
]
and
[
Sjö23
]
.

We end this section with an interesting consequence of
2.9
and
Theorem

2.10
, proved in
Section

4.6
.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
\mu
be a permuton satisfying
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Then for all
1\leq k\leq r
, for almost every
(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}
,
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}
is differentiable at
(x,y)
and satisfies
\partial_{x}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)\cdot\partial_{y}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)=0
.
In other words, the gradient of
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}
is a.e. directed along the
x
- or along the
y
-axis.

2.4
Injectivity of pre-permuton Robinson–Schensted

Recall that Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence is injective on permutations.
This begs the question of what information about a (pre-)permuton
\mu
we can deduce from the knowledge of
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)
.
To this end, we introduce a useful decomposition of pre-permutons.
Define
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\uparrow\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\uparrow\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
. There exists a unique triple
\left(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}},\mu^{\mathrm{decr}},\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}\right)\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}^{3}
satisfying:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mu=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}+\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}+\mu^{\mathrm{sub}};\\
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\right)=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu);\\
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\right)=\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}(\mu).\end{array}\right.

Moreover the following properties hold:

(i)

the measures
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}},\mu^{\mathrm{decr}},\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}
are pairwise singular;

(ii)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)

and
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu)
;

(iii)

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}\left(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\right)=0
;

(iv)

\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}}
.

Notice that Property (iv) of
Theorem

2.12
allows to lift the hypothesis
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
in
Theorem

2.10
to the more general one
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)
.
Indeed, consider a permuton
\mu
that satisfies
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)
.
Its submeasure
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}
is
a priori
not a permuton, but can be mapped to a pre-permuton
\delta\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}
by dilatation.
This pre-permuton satisfies
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\delta\mu^{\mathrm{incr}})=1
, so we can apply
Theorem

2.10
using the chain rule.
Thanks to Property (iv) of
Theorem

2.12
, this finally yields a partial differential equation on
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}.

Conjecture 2.13
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
such that
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)=\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\nu)
. Then
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.

Recall that
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)
is defined using only the nondecreasing subsets of
\mu
.
These “row-tableaux” capture no information about the submeasure
\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}
, since
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}})
is null.
An analog of
2.13
for nonincreasing subsets could be stated by replacing
\mu
with
\mu^{\mathrm{rev}}
(see the discussion at the end of
Section

2.1
).

We believe that
Theorem

2.10
could help establish
2.13
.
In most “regular” examples, the PDE of
Theorem

2.10
seems to be satisfied everywhere on
(0,1]^{2}
(note that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}
is in general not
differentiable
everywhere).
We would then hope for the uniqueness of their solution
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
with boundary condition
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)
, using
2.11
and the form taken by
Equation

2
at the points of non-differentiability of
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}
.
This is further discussed in
Section

2.5
, item
(i)
.
However we could not make this idea work, and instead we present more elementary results: an injectivity property of
\mu\mapsto\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
, and a positive answer to
2.13
when the pre-permutons contain at most one nondecreasing subset.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
such that
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\nu}
.
Then
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{cont}}
such that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{1}(\mu)
and
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)=\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\nu)
.
Then
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.

The results of this section are proved in
Section

4.7
.

2.5
Examples

In this section, we illustrate our results with several examples.
Some of them are represented in
Figure

5
.
If
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the standard notation
x\wedge y:=\min(x,y)
and
x\vee y:=\max(x,y)
.

Figure 5:
Representation of some of the examples of
Section

2.5
.
From left to right: the locally affine function
f:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
constructed in
(i)
(brighter colors indicate higher values), the permuton of
(d)
, and the permuton of
(e)
.
For the last two:
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on the dashed blue lines, and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on the dashed orange lines.

(a)

Let
\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}
be the uniform permuton,
i.e.
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
.
It is easy to check that for any nondecreasing
A
, we have
\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}(A)=0
.
Thus
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\left(\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}\right)=0
, and likewise
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}\left(\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}\right)=0
.
This is consistent with the fact that the longest monotone subsequences of uniform permutations have sublinear asymptotics.

More generally, let
\mu
be a pre-permuton with a density with respect to
\mathrm{Leb}_{[0,1]^{2}}
.
By absolute continuity, we again have
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}(\mu)=0
.
In particular, with the notation of
Theorem

2.12
:
\left(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}},\mu^{\mathrm{decr}},\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}\right)=(0,0,\mu)
.
Our definitions are vacuous in this case, and our techniques are not suited to the study of such pre-permutons.

(b)

Let
\mu=\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta}
be the permuton that puts uniform mass on the diagonal
\Delta
of
[0,1]^{2}
.
Then
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\left(\mu\right)=1
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}\left(\mu\right)=0
, and in particular
\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}\left(\mu\right)=\big((1,0,\dots),(0,\dots)\big)
.
Also,
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}(x,y)=x\wedge y
for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)=0
for all
k\geq 2
.
The RS-tableaux of
\mu
are
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)=\big((\mathrm{id}_{[0,1]},0,\dots),(\mathrm{id}_{[0,1]},0,\dots)\big)
.

(c)

Let
\mu
be the permuton that puts uniform mass on the
antidiagonal
of
[0,1]^{2}
.
Then
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\left(\mu\right)=0
,
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}\left(\mu\right)=1
, but with our definition
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)=\big((0,\dots),(0,\dots)\big)
.
This is because in this paper, we defined the RS-tableaux of a permuton via its
row-shapes
, and not its
column-shapes
.
As discussed at the end of
Section

2.1
, it would be more relevant in this case to apply our definitions to the mirrored permuton
\mu^{\mathrm{rev}}=\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta}
.

(d)

Let
\mu
be the only permuton with support
\{y=2x\}\cup\{y=2x-1\}
in
[0,1]^{2}
.
This permuton has row-shape
\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}^{\mathrm{row}}(\mu)=(1/2,1/2,0,\dots)
and trivial column-shape.
In particular
\mu=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.
We can compute:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x\wedge(y/2)&\text{if }x\in[0,1/2];\\
y/2&\text{if }x\in[1/2,1];\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}^{\mu}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }x\in[0,1/2];\\
(x-1/2)\wedge(y/2)&\text{if }x\in[1/2,1].\end{array}\right.

The function
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on
\{y=2x\}
, and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on
\{x=1/2\}\cup\{y=2x-1\}
.

(e)

Let
\mu
be the only permuton with support
\{y=x+1/2\}\cup\{y=x-1/2\}
in
[0,1]^{2}
.
This permuton has the same RS-shape as the previous one, but different RS-tableaux.
We can compute:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}0&\text{if }(x,y)\in[0,1/2]^{2};\\
x\wedge(y-1/2)&\text{if }(x,y)\in[0,1/2]\times[1/2,1];\\
(x-1/2)\wedge y&\text{if }(x,y)\in[1/2,1]\times[0,1/2];\\
(x-1/2)\vee(y-1/2)&\text{if }(x,y)\in[1/2,1]^{2};\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}^{\mu}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }(x,y)\notin[1/2,1]^{2};\\
(x-1/2)\wedge(y-1/2)&\text{if }(x,y)\in[1/2,1]^{2}.\end{array}\right.

The function
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on
\{x\leq y,y=1/2\}\cup\{y=x+1/2\}\cup\{y\leq x,x=1/2\}\cup\{y=x-1/2\}\cup\{y=x,y\geq 1/2\}
, and
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}^{\mu}
is not differentiable on
\{x\leq y,x=1/2\}\cup\{y\leq x,y=1/2\}\cup\{y=x,y\geq 1/2\}
.

Now let us illustrate
Theorem

2.12

Proofs of the results in Sections
2.1
and
2.2

3.1
Topological preliminaries

Write
\mathrm{d}
for the Euclidean distance on the plane.
If
A\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
and
\delta>0
, define the
\delta
-halo of
A
by:

A^{\delta}:=\big\{y\in[0,1]^{2}\>:\>\mathrm{d}(y,x)\leq\delta\text{ for some }x\in A\big\}.

Note that if
A
is a closed subset of
[0,1]^{2}
then
A^{\delta}
is closed as well.
We then define the Hausdorff distance by

\text{for all }A,B\subseteq[0,1]^{2},\quad\mathrm{d_{H}}(A,B):=\inf\left\{\delta>0\>:\>A\subseteq B^{\delta}\text{ and }B\subseteq A^{\delta}\right\}.

It is well known that the space
\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})
of closed subsets of the unit square, equipped with the distance
\mathrm{d_{H}}
, is compact.
Moreover, one can easily see that its subsets
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
are
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\searrow}([0,1]^{2})
are closed, and hence compact.
The following two lemmas may be well known, but we include a short proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
. The function
A\in\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})\mapsto\mu(A)\in[0,1]

is upper semi-continuous for the Hausdorff distance.

Proof.

Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence converging to
A
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})
. Our goal is to establish
\mu(A)\geq\limsup\mu(A_{n})
.
For this, fix
\varepsilon
> 0.
By monotony and since
\bigcap_{\delta>0}A^{\delta}=A
, there exists
\delta>0
satisfying
\mu(A^{\delta})\leq\mu(A)+\varepsilon
.
Let
n_{0}
be such that for any
n
greater than
n_{0}
,
A^{\delta}
contains
A_{n}
.
Then:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu(A_{n})\leq\mu(A^{\delta})\leq\mu(A)+\varepsilon

which concludes this proof, as it holds for any
\varepsilon>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the union map
\cup
from
\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})^{k}
to
\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})
is continuous.

Proof.

By associativity, it suffices to consider the case
k=2
.
Let
(A_{n}),(B_{n})
be two sequences converging to
A,B
respectively in
\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})
.
Our goal is to prove the convergence of
(A_{n}\cup B_{n})
to
A\cup B
.
For this, fix
\varepsilon>0
and take
n_{0}
such that:

\text{for all }n>n_{0},\quad A_{n}\subseteq A^{\varepsilon}\;\text{ and }\;A\subseteq A_{n}^{\varepsilon}\;\text{ and }\;B_{n}\subseteq B^{\varepsilon}\;\text{ and }\;B\subseteq B_{n}^{\varepsilon}.

Hence:

\text{for all }n>n_{0},\quad A_{n}\cup B_{n}\subseteq(A^{\varepsilon})\cup(B^{\varepsilon})\;\text{ and }\;A\cup B\subseteq(A_{n}^{\varepsilon})\cup(B_{n}^{\varepsilon}).

As it is immediate to check the equality
(A^{\varepsilon})\cup(B^{\varepsilon})=(A\cup B)^{\varepsilon}
for any closed subsets of
[0,1]^{2}
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the space
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
equipped with the distance
\mathrm{d_{H}}
is compact.

Now we have all the necessary tools to prove
2.1
.

Proof of
2.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{fin}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
According to
Lemma

3.1
, the map
\mu
defined from
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
to
[0,1]
is upper semi-continuous.
It is well-known that any upper semi-continuous function defined on a compact set reaches its maximum.
Hence, by
3.3
, the definition of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)
is actually a maximum.

Now let us turn to the semi-continuity of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}
on the space
\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}
. Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of probability measures weakly converging to some
\mu
.
We aim to prove:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{n}).

(3)

Up to extraction, suppose the right hand side is an actual limit.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, fix
A_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
such that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{n})=\mu_{n}(A_{n})
.
Once again using
3.3
, up to extraction (which does not change the right hand side limit in (
3
)), suppose the sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to some
A
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
.
Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.
Recalling the definition of
\mathrm{d_{H}}
, we have:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(A_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}\big(A^{\varepsilon}\big)\leq\mu\big(A^{\varepsilon}\big)

where in the fourth inequality we used Portmanteau theorem and the fact that
A^{\varepsilon}
is closed.
Since this holds for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\mu\big(A^{\varepsilon}\big)\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\mu(A)
, we deduce

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{n})\leq\mu(A)\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu).

This concludes the proof of (
3
), and thus of
2.1
.
The study of
\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}
is completely similar.
∎

3.2
Convergence of shapes

To prove
2.2
, we associate each permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with a permuton that is appropriate to the study of longest increasing subsequences.
The permuton
\mu_{\sigma}
defined in
Section

1.1
satisfies
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\mu_{\sigma})=0
, so we need to introduce a different one.
If
a\leq b
are integers, we use the notation
[\![a,b]\!]
for the set
\{a,a+1\dots,b\}
.
For any
i,j\in[\![1,n]\!]
, let

C_{i,j}:=\left[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}\right]\times\left[\frac{j-1}{n},\frac{j}{n}\right]

and
\Delta_{i,j}
be the diagonal of
C_{i,j}
.
Then define a permuton by putting uniform mass on these diagonals:

\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta_{i,\sigma(i)}}

where
\mathrm{Leb}_{\Delta_{i,\sigma(i)}}
denotes the normalized Lebesgue probability measure on
\Delta_{i,\sigma(i)}
.
Since the Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}
and
\mu_{\sigma}
is a
O(1/n)
,
Theorem

1.1
implies:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
and
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Then
\mu_{\sigma_{n}}^{\nearrow}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu
almost surely.

Notice that this almost sure convergence holds regardless of the joint distribution of
\sigma_{n}
’s, as this is the case in
Theorem

1.1
.

Instead of these permutons, we could use the empirical measures of i.i.d. points distributed under
\mu
(see
Section

3.4
) to prove
2.2
.
One advantage of introducing
\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}
is to also obtain an appropriate embedding of the set of permutations into the space of permutons.
This embedding is adapted to the study of longest increasing subsequences, as stated in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow})=\frac{\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma)}{n}.

Proof.

First, let
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
be increasing subsequences of
\sigma
such that
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma)=|I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{k}|
.
Then define for each
j\in[\![1,k]\!]
:

A_{j}:=\bigcup_{i\in I_{j}}\Delta_{i,\sigma(i)}.

Since
I_{j}
is an increasing subsequence of
\sigma
,
A_{j}
is in
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
.
As
\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}
puts mass
1/n
on each diagonal of its support, one has:

\frac{\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma)}{n}=\frac{|I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{k}|}{n}=\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}(A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{k})\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}).

(4)

Next, using
2.1
, let
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
be closed nondecreasing subsets of
[0,1]^{2}
such that:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow})=\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}(B_{1}\cup\dots\cup B_{k}).

Then define for each
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
:

J_{i}:=\{j\in[\![1,n]\!]\>:\>B_{i}\cap\Delta_{j,\sigma(j)}\neq\emptyset\}.

Since
B_{i}
is nondecreasing,
J_{i}
is an increasing subsequence of
\sigma
.
Hence:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow})=\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}(B_{1}\cup\dots\cup B_{k})\leq\frac{|J_{1}\cup\dots\cup J_{k}|}{n}\leq\frac{\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma)}{n}.

(5)

The lemma follows from
Equations

4
and
5
.
∎

Proof of
2.2
.

First notice that if
\mu
is a pre-permuton, its corresponding permuton
\hat{\mu}
satisfies
\mathrm{sh}\left(\hat{\mu}\right)=\mathrm{sh}(\mu)
and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\mathrm{Sample}_{n}\left(\hat{\mu}\right)=\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
(see e.g. Remark 1.2 in
[
BDMW24
]
).
Thus we can assume for this proof that
\mu
is a permuton.

On the one hand, using
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.4
and
2.1
:

\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\limsup}\;\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})=\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\limsup}\;\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu_{\sigma_{n}}^{\nearrow}\right)\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad\text{almost surely.}

(6)

On the other hand, using
2.1
, let
A
be a closed
k
-nondecreasing subset of
[0,1]^{2}
such that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=\mu(A)
.
Let
\sigma_{n}:=\mathrm{Perm}\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)
where
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are random i.i.d. points distributed under
\mu
, and define
S_{n}
as the number of
Z_{i}
’s lying in
A
.
Since
A
is
k
-nondecreasing, those
S_{n}
points can be written as a union of
k
nondecreasing subsets. Hence:

\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq S_{n}.

(7)

However
S_{n}
follows a binomial law of parameter
\left(n,\mu(A)\right)
, so the law of large numbers imply

\frac{S_{n}}{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu(A)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad\text{almost surely.}

(8)

We point out the fact that this law of large numbers comes along with exponential concentration inequalities, so almost sure convergence holds regardless of the joint distribution of
S_{n}
’s.
Using (
7
) and (
8
), we get:

\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\;\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad\text{almost surely}.

(9)

Inequalities (
6
) and (
9
) conclude the proof for
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}
.
The proof of the analogous convergence for
\mathrm{LDS}_{k}
works the same, replacing
\mu_{\sigma}^{\nearrow}
with a similar permuton
\mu_{\sigma}^{\searrow}
putting mass on antidiagonals.
∎

To conclude this section, we explain how to derive
2.3
from
2.2
.
While the quantity
\mathrm{sh}(\sigma)
for
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
does not quite belong to the Thoma simplex, this becomes the case in the
n\rightarrow\infty
limit:

Lemma 3.6
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
consider a permutation
\sigma_{n}
of size
n
, and define for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\alpha_{k}:=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})-\mathrm{LIS}_{k\!-\!1}(\sigma_{n})}{n}\;\text{ and }\;\beta_{k}:=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})-\mathrm{LDS}_{k\!-\!1}(\sigma_{n})}{n}.

Then the sequences
\alpha:=(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}
and
\beta:=(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega
.

Proof.

The monotony condition is a consequence of
Theorem

1.2
.
To prove the sum condition, we fix an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and show the following:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}\leq 1.

(10)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, denote by
A_{k,n}
the number of boxes shared by the first
k
rows and first
k
columns of
\sigma_{n}
’s RS-shape.
By
Theorem

1.2
,
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})
and
\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})
are respectively the numbers of boxes in the first
k
rows and columns of this diagram.
Therefore by inclusion-exclusion:

\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})+\mathrm{LDS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\leq n+A_{k,n}.

Moreover
A_{k,n}/n\leq k^{2}/n\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, so inequality (
10
) follows.
Since this holds for any integer
k
, the lemma is proved.
∎

Finally,
2.3
is a direct consequence of
2.2
and
Lemma

3.6
.

3.3
Upper tail large deviation

The proof of
Theorem

2.4
heavily hinges on the following celebrated result:

Theorem 3.7

(
[
Cra38
,
DZ98
]
)
.

Fix
p\in[0,1]
and consider for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
a random variable
S_{n}
following a binomial law of parameter
(n,p)
. Then for any
q\in(p,1]
:

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\left(S_{n}\geq nq\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q)

with the notation of
Section

2.2
. Likewise for any
q\in[0,p)
:

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\left(S_{n}<nq\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q).

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

If
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=1
, there is nothing to prove. Now suppose
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)<1
.
To prove the lower bound, let
A
be a closed
k
-nondecreasing subset of
[0,1]^{2}
such that
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=\mu(A)
.
Let
\sigma_{n}:=\mathrm{Perm}\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)
where
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are random i.i.d. points distributed under
\mu
, and define
S_{n}
as the number of
Z_{i}
’s lying in
A
.
Since
S_{n}
follows a binomial law of parameter
\big(n,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\big)
, by
Theorem

3.7
and inclusion of events:

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\geq\alpha n)\geq-\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)}^{*}(\alpha).

Next, let us prove the upper bound.
The general idea is that if sampled permutations have a large
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}
then a lot of points were sampled in a large
k
-increasing subset of
\mu
.
To find such a subset and make it deterministic, we condition on this rare event and extract a limit.
Define

x:=-\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)\quad\in[0,+\infty]

and suppose, up to extraction, that this
\limsup
is an actual limit.
Our goal is to prove

x\geq\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)}^{*}(\alpha).

(11)

If
x=+\infty
this is trivial.
Now suppose
x<+\infty
, which implies the existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)>0
.
Thus for any
n\geq n_{0}
, we can consider random variables
Z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Z_{n}^{(n)}
i.i.d. under
\mu
conditioned to satisfy

\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(Z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Z_{n}^{(n)}\right)\geq\alpha n,

where the notation
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(Z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Z_{n}^{(n)}\right)
is shortcut for
\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\mathrm{Perm}\left(Z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Z_{n}^{(n)}\right)\right)
.
Let
A_{n}
be the maximal
k
-nondecreasing subset of
\left\{Z_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Z_{n}^{(n)}\right\}
(if there are several such subsets, choose one with an arbitrary rule).
Then denote by
\nu_{n}
the law of
A_{n}
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
.
Up to extraction, the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\geq n_{0}}
weakly converges to some probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
.
Let
B_{0}
be an arbitrary element in the support of
\nu
and
\ell\in\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu),\alpha\right)
.
Since
\mu(B_{0})\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)<\ell
, by monotone continuity there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that:

\mu\left(B_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right)<\ell.

(12)

Then by Portmanteau theorem and the fact that
B_{0}
is in the support of
\nu
, we get:

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{n}\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;\mathrm{d_{H}}(B,B_{0})<\varepsilon\}\right)\geq\nu\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;\mathrm{d_{H}}(B,B_{0})<\varepsilon\}\right)>0.

(13)

However:

\displaystyle\nu_{n}\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;\mathrm{dist}(B,B_{0})<\varepsilon\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{n}\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;B\subseteq B_{0}^{\varepsilon}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\big(\text{the maximal $k$-nondecreasing subset of }Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\text{ is contained in }B_{0}^{\varepsilon}\;\,\big|\;\,\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)\geq\alpha n\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\left(\text{at least }\alpha n\text{ points among }Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\text{ are in }B_{0}^{\varepsilon}\;\,\big|\;\,\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)\geq\alpha n\right)

thus, writing
S_{n}
for a binomial random variable of parameter
\big(n,\mu(B_{0}^{\varepsilon})\big)
:

\displaystyle\nu_{n}\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;\mathrm{dist}(B,B_{0})<\varepsilon\}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\geq\alpha n)}{\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)}.

Now consider another binomial random variable
S_{n}^{\prime}
of parameter
(n,\ell)
.
By (
12
), stochastic domination,
Theorem

3.7
and the fact that
\ell<\alpha
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\geq\alpha n)\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(S_{n}^{\prime}\geq\alpha n)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\alpha\log\frac{\alpha}{\ell}-(1-\alpha)\log\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\ell}.

Then, using
Equation

13
for the first equality:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}\left(\{B\in\mathcal{P}_{K}([0,1]^{2})\;:\;\mathrm{dist}(B,B_{0})<\varepsilon\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(S_{n}^{\prime}\geq\alpha n)-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)

\displaystyle=x-\alpha\log\frac{\alpha}{\ell}-(1-\alpha)\log\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\ell}.

Since this holds for any
\ell\in\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu),\alpha\right)
,
we deduce (
11
) as desired.
∎

3.4
Lower tail speed

The goal of this section is to establish
2.6
and
Theorem

2.7
stating that, except in trivial cases, a lower tail large deviation principle for
\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})/n
where
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
should indeed have speed
n
.

Proof of
2.6
.

Let
j\geq 1
and
A_{j}
be a maximal
j
-nondecreasing subset for
\mu
.
Notice that all permutations
\sigma
satisfy
\mathrm{LIS}_{j}(\sigma)\leq j\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma)
by union bound.
We can then write:

\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)\leq\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{j}(\sigma_{n})<j\beta n\big)\leq\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n}}\left(\text{less than }j\beta n\text{ points are in }A_{j}\right)=\mathbf{P}(S_{n}<j\beta n)

where
S_{n}
is a binomial random variable of parameter
\left(n,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{j}(\mu)\right)
.
If
j
satisfies
j\beta<\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{j}(\mu)
,
Theorem

3.7
implies:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)\leq-\Lambda_{\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{j}(\mu)}^{*}\big(j\beta\big).

Since this bound is valid for all such
j
,
2.6
follows.
∎

In order to prove
Theorem

2.7
we recall a well known large deviation principle on empirical measures and apply it to our framework.
See
[
DZ98
]
for a proof and
[
BDMW24
]
for another application to permuton theory.

Theorem 3.8

(Sanov’s theorem)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}
and
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a family of i.i.d. random points distributed under
\mu
. Define for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the empirical measure

\Delta_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}(\delta_{Z_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{Z_{n}})

Then the sequence
(\Delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
satisfies a large deviation principle

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{for each open set $E$ of }\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}},&\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(\Delta_{n}\in E)\geq-\underset{\nu\in E}{\inf}D(\nu|\mu);\\
\text{for each closed set $F$ of }\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}},&\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\limsup}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}(\Delta_{n}\in F)\leq-\underset{\nu\in F}{\inf}D(\nu|\mu);\end{array}\right.

with good rate function
D(\cdot|\mu)
, called the Kullback-Leibler divergence, defined by:

\text{for all }\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}},\quad D(\nu|\mu):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\log\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right)d\nu&\text{if }\nu\text{ is absolutely continuous with respect to }\mu;\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
By upper semi-continuity, for any
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
,

\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}\,:\,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\nu)\geq\alpha\right\}

is closed and

\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}\,:\,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\nu)<\beta\right\}

is open.
Since
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\Delta_{n})=\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})/n
, we deduce the following corollary:

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{unif}}
,
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
. Then:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\liminf}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)\;\geq\;-\underset{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}\;;\;\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\nu)<\beta}{\inf}D(\nu|\mu);\\
\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\limsup}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})\geq\alpha n\big)\;\leq\;-\underset{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{prob}}\;;\;\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\nu)\geq\alpha}{\inf}D(\nu|\mu).\end{array}\right.

With the help of
3.9
, we can now prove
Theorem

2.7
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.7
.

We prove both equivalences
(i)\Longleftrightarrow(ii)
and
(ii)\Longleftrightarrow(iii)
.
Notice that
(ii)\implies(i)
is immediate.

Proof of
(i)\implies(ii)
.

We proceed by contraposition, and suppose that
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})<\beta n_{0}\big)>0
for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
By Robinson–Schensted correspondence (or Erdős-Szekeres lemma):

\text{for any }n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\text{ and }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\quad\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma)\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma)\geq n.

Hence we get:

\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})\geq k\!\!+\!\!1\big)\geq\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})<\frac{n_{0}}{k}\right)\geq\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})<\beta n_{0}\big)>0

which in particular imposes
n_{0}\geq k\!\!+\!\!1
, and then:

0<\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes n_{0}}}\left(\exists i_{1}<\dots<i_{k\!+\!1},\;\{Z_{i_{1}},\dots,Z_{i_{k\!+\!1}}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\searrow}([0,1]^{2})\right)\\
\leq\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n_0}{k\pp 1}\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes k\!+\!1}}\left(\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k\!+\!1}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\searrow}([0,1]^{2})\right).

Thus, since the marginals of
\mu
are continuous:

\mathbf{P}_{\mu^{\otimes k\!+\!1}}\left(\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k\!+\!1}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}^{\searrow\!\!\searrow}([0,1]^{2})\right)>0

where
\mathcal{P}_{k\!+\!1}^{\searrow\!\!\searrow}([0,1]^{2})
denotes the set of nonincreasing subsets of
[0,1]^{2}
of cardinal
k\!\!+\!\!1
whose elements share no common
x
- or
y
-coordinate.
As a consequence, we can define the measure
\mu^{\prime}
as the law of
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k\!+\!1})
under
\mu^{\otimes k\!+\!1}
conditioned on the event

\{Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k\!+\!1}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}^{\searrow\!\!\searrow}([0,1]^{2})
.
Now take an element
(z_{1},\dots,z_{k\!+\!1})
in the support of
\mu^{\prime}
.
There exists
\varepsilon>0
satisfying:

\text{for any }z_{1}^{\prime}\in B_{\mathrm{d}}(z_{1},\varepsilon),\dots,z_{k\!+\!1}^{\prime}\in B_{\mathrm{d}}(z_{k\!+\!1},\varepsilon),\quad\{z_{1}^{\prime},\dots,z_{k\!+\!1}^{\prime}\}\text{ is nonincreasing}.

(14)

For all
i\in[\![1,k\!\!+\!\!1]\!]
the point
z_{i}
is in the support of
\mu
, therefore
m_{i}:=\mu\big(B_{\mathrm{d}}(z_{i},\varepsilon)\big)>0
.
Now define a measure
\nu
as:

\nu(\cdot):=\sum_{i=1}^{k\!+\!1}\frac{1}{(k\!+\!1)m_{i}}\mu\big(B_{\mathrm{d}}(z_{i},\varepsilon)\cap\cdot\big).

This probability measure is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, with bounded density

\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\frac{1}{(k\!+\!1)m_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{B(z_{i},\varepsilon)}.

Hence, using the notation of
Theorem

3.8
,
D(\nu|\mu)<+\infty
.
Moreover, by (
14
) and the fact that
\nu
puts mass
1/(k\!+\!1)
on each ball of its support,

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}(\nu)\leq{1}/({k\!+\!1})<\beta
.
Thus we can conclude with the first inequality of
3.9
:

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<\beta n\big)\geq-D(\nu|\mu)>-\infty.

Proof of
(ii)\implies(iii)
.

Suppose (ii) holds.
First let us show that under this hypothesis:

\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},\quad\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma_{n})\leq k\big)=1.

(15)

For this, suppose there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})\geq k\!+\!1\big)>0
.
Then necessarily
n_{0}\geq k\!+\!1
. Thus we can write, as before:

0<\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma_{n_{0}})\geq k\!+\!1\big)\leq\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n_0}{k\p 1}\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LDS}(\sigma_{k\!+\!1})=k\!+\!1\big)

whence
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{k\!+\!1})=1\big)>0
.
Since
\beta(k+1)>1
this contradicts (ii), and (
15
) is established.
Then by Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence,
\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})=n\big)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Using
2.2
we deduce:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma_{n})}{n}=1\quad\text{almost surely.}

Proof of
(iii)\implies(ii)
.

Suppose
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)=1
and fix
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
By
2.1
there exist nondecreasing subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\in\mathcal{P}_{K}^{\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
such that
\mu(A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{k})=1
.
Thus if
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
are i.i.d. random points distributed under
\mu
, by pigeonhole principle there must be at least
n/k
of them in some
A_{i}
, implying:

\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\geq\beta n\big)\geq\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})\geq n/k\big)=1

as desired.
∎

3.5
Lower tail rate function

Before proving
2.8
, we state a simple lemma about conditional concentration of binomial laws.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
S_{n}
be a binomial random variable of parameter
(n,p)
for some
0<p<1
. Then for any
q\in(0,p)
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
q-\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
\delta>0
satisfying

\mathbf{P}\left(q-\varepsilon\leq S_{n}/n<q\;\big|\;S_{n}/n<q\right)=1-\exp\big(-n\delta+o(n)\big)\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Likewise for any
q\in(p,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
q+\varepsilon<1
,
there exists
\delta>0
satisfying

\mathbf{P}\left(q\leq S_{n}/n<q+\varepsilon\;\big|\;S_{n}/n\geq q\right)=1-\exp\big(-n\delta+o(n)\big)\quad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Let us prove the first assertion, as the second one is analogous.
Applying
Theorem

3.7
, we get as
n\rightarrow\infty
:

\mathbf{P}(S_{n}/n<q)=\exp\left(-n\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q)+o(n)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}(S_{n}/n<q-\varepsilon)=\exp\left(-n\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q-\varepsilon)+o(n)\right).

Since
\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q-\varepsilon)>\Lambda_{p}^{*}(q)
, the assertion follows directly.
∎

Proof of
2.8
.

Define the following diagonals:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{1}^{1}\text{ from }(0,0.5)\text{ to }(0.2,0.7);\\

D_{2}^{2}\text{ from }(0.28,0)\text{ to }(0.58,0.3);\\
D_{3}^{2}\text{ from }(0.58,0.7)\text{ to }(0.88,1);\\
D_{4}^{2}\text{ from }(0.88,0.3)\text{ to }(1,0.42);\end{array}\right.

then define
\mu_{1}
and

as the only permutons having support

and

\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu_{1})=\mathrm{\widetilde{sh}}(\mu_{2})
.
Now fix

.
Our goal is to show that

Let

Now for each

3.7
we finally deduce:

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\tau_{n})<0.55n\big)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(e^{-n\delta+o(n)}\mathbf{P}(B^{2})\big)\\
=-\delta-\Lambda_{0.6}^{*}(0.55)<-\Lambda_{0.6}^{*}(0.55)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbf{P}\big(\mathrm{LIS}(\sigma_{n})<0.55n\big)

as announced.
∎

4.1
Convergence of tableaux

Proof of
2.9
.

Recall that for any permutation
\sigma
, its subword
\sigma^{i,j}
consists of letters with position at most
i
and value at most
j
.
Also recall that
\lambda_{k}^{\sigma}(i,j)=\mathrm{LIS}_{k}(\sigma^{i,j})-\mathrm{LIS}_{k\!-\!1}(\sigma^{i,j})
, and that for any permuton
\mu
, we have
\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)-\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k\!-\!1}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)
.
Let us first establish pointwise convergence.
We aim to show that for any fixed
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, almost surely:

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}^{\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right).

(20)

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be random i.i.d. points under
\mu
, let
\sigma_{n}:=\mathrm{Perm}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
, and define

I:=\{i\in[\![1,n]\!]\;:\;Z_{i}\in[0,x]\times[0,1]\}\quad;\quad J:=\{i\in[\![1,n]\!]\;:\;Z_{i}\in[0,1]\times[0,y]\}.

Then
\mathrm{Perm}\left((Z_{i})_{i\in I\cap J}\right)=\sigma_{n}^{|I|,|J|}
.
Moreover, conditional to its size
|I\cap J|
, the permutation
\mathrm{Perm}\left((Z_{i})_{i\in I\cap J}\right)
follows the law
\mathrm{Sample}_{|I\cap J|}(\nu)
where
\nu
is the pre-permuton induced by
\mu
on
[0,x]\times[0,y]
.
As a consequence of
2.2
and the law of large numbers, we get:

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}^{|I|,|J|}\right)=\frac{|I\cap J|}{n}\frac{1}{|I\cap J|}\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}^{|I|,|J|}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mu([0,x]\times[0,y])\,\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\nu\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)

almost surely.
Since the words
\sigma_{n}^{\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor}
and
\sigma_{n}^{|I|,|J|}
differ by at most
\big|\lfloor nx\rfloor-|I|\big|+\big|\lfloor ny\rfloor-|J|\big|
letters, we have the following rough upper bound :

\left|\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}^{\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor}\right)-\mathrm{LIS}_{k}\left(\sigma_{n}^{|I|,|J|}\right)\right|\leq\big|\lfloor nx\rfloor-|I|\big|+\big|\lfloor ny\rfloor-|J|\big|.

Using the fact that
\mu
is a permuton,
i.e.
that
\mathbf{P}(Z_{i}\in[0,x]\times[0,1])=x
and
\mathbf{P}(Z_{i}\in[0,1]\times[0,y])=y
, the law of large numbers yields

\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor-|I|}{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad;\quad\frac{\lfloor ny\rfloor-|J|}{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

almost surely, and (
20
) follows.

We can already use this pointwise convergence to deduce properties of
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
.
Let
j\in[\![1,n]\!]
and
i\in[\![1,n\!-\!1]\!]
.
Since the P-tableau of
\sigma_{n}^{i+1,j}
is obtained after Schensted-insertion of at most one letter in the P-tableau of
\sigma_{n}^{i,j}
, the shape of
\sigma_{n}^{i,j}
is included in the shape of
\sigma_{n}^{i+1,j}
and they differ by at most one box.
This fact is also clear from Fomin’s construction described later.
Hence for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,j)\leq\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}(i\!\!+\!\!1,j)\leq\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,j)+1.

Since for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\frac{1}{n}\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}_{k}\left(\lfloor xn\rfloor,\lfloor yn\rfloor\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}(x,y)

(21)

almost surely by (
20
), we deduce that
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}
in nondecreasing and
1
-Lipschitz in its first variable.
The same holds for its second variable.

To obtain uniform convergence, we simply need to adapt the proof of Dini’s theorem.
Fix
\epsilon>0
and
p:=\lceil 1/\epsilon\rceil
.
Using (
21
), almost surely for large enough
n
we have:

\text{for any }i,j\in[\![0,p]\!],\quad\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}\big(\lfloor ni/p\rfloor,\lfloor nj/p\rfloor\big)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}\big(i/p,j/p\big)\right|\leq\epsilon

Let
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
and
i,j\in[\![1,p]\!]
such that
(i\!\!-\!\!1)/p\leq x\leq i/p
and
(j\!\!-\!\!1)/p\leq y\leq j/p
. Then:

\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}\big((i\!\!-\!\!1)/p,(j\!\!-\!\!1)/p\big)-\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}\big(\lfloor n(i\!\!-\!\!1)/p\rfloor,\lfloor n(j\!\!-\!\!1)/p\rfloor\big)\\
\leq\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}\big(\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor\big)\leq\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}\big(\lfloor ni/p\rfloor,\lfloor nj/p\rfloor\big)\leq\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}\big(i/p,j/p\big)+\epsilon.

Since
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{k}
in nondecreasing and
1
-Lipschitz in both variables,
we deduce:

\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}(\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}(x,y)\right|\leq\epsilon+2/p\leq 3\epsilon.

We proved that for any
\epsilon>0
, almost surely

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}^{\sigma_{n}}(\lfloor n\,\cdot\rfloor,\lfloor n\,\cdot\rfloor)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}^{\mu}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 3\epsilon.

By restricting to
\epsilon\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{*}
we conclude that almost surely, uniform convergence holds.
∎

4.2
Inverse Fomin dynamics

We start this section by recalling the “edge local rules” construction of Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence, as presented by Viennot in
[
Vie18
]
.
Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and consider the grid
[\![0,n]\!]\times[\![0,n]\!]
.
For each
1\leq i,j\leq n
, fill the cell
c_{i,j}:=[i\!\!-\!\!1,i]\times[j\!\!-\!\!1,j]
with a point if and only if
\sigma(i)=j
.
The algorithm labels the edges of each cell of the grid with nonnegative integers.
Write
S(c),W(c),N(c),E(c)
for the south, west, north, east edge labels of a cell
c
.
We initialize the algorithm with

\text{for each }1\leq k\leq n,\quad S(c_{k,1})=0\quad\text{and}\quad W(c_{1,k})=0

and the local rules are the following for each cell
c
:

•

if
S(c)=W(c)=0
and the cell is empty then
N(c)=E(c)=0
;

•

if
S(c)=W(c)=0
and the cell is filled with a point then
N(c)=E(c)=1
;

•

if
S(c)=W(c)>0
then
N(c)=E(c)=S(c)+1
;

•

if
S(c)\neq W(c)
then
N(c)=S(c)
and
E(c)=W(c)
;

see
Figure

7
.

Figure 7:
Illustration of Fomin’s direct local rules.

These edge rules are equivalent to Fomin’s growth diagrams algorithm, described in Section 5.2 of
[
Sag01
]
, which labels the vertices of each cell
c
of the grid with diagrams
SE(c),SW(c),NW(c),NE(c)
.
This algorithm starts with empty diagrams on the south and west borders, and uses local rules to deduce for each cell
c
the diagram
NE(c)
from the other three diagrams and the presence or not of a permutation point.
The local rules for Fomin’s growth diagrams are equivalent to Viennot’s edge local rules, in the sense that the label of an edge is
k>0
if and only if the diagrams at its endpoints differ by exactly one box in their
k
-th row, and the label is
0
if and only if these diagrams are equal.
It is possible to prove
[
Sag01
]
that the sequence of diagrams on the north border (resp. east border) of the grid encodes the Q-tableau (resp. P-tableau) of the permutation.
Subsequently one can see that the diagram labeling any vertex
(i,j)
is the RS-shape of the word
\sigma^{i,j}
.
See
Figure

8
for an example.

Figure 8:
Fomin’s rules applied to the permutation
\sigma=3\,5\,1\,4\,2

On the left, diagram labels on vertices are used to represent the shapes of sub-words.
On the right, integer labels on edges are used to represent the row length increments.
For better readability, each integer label is associated with a color (green for
1

, red for
3

We can recover the P- and Q-tableau from the integer labels on the east and north sides of the grid:

P(\sigma)
contains the letters
1
and
2
in its first row,
3
and
4
in its second row, and
5
in its third row (in this example,
Q(\sigma)=P(\sigma)
because
\sigma
is an involution).
A rectangle is highlighted for later reference.

We can sum up these observations with our notation: for any
0\leq i\leq i^{\prime}\leq n
,
0\leq j\leq j^{\prime}\leq n
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the number of edges labeled
k
on any up-right path from

to
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is equal to
\lambda^{\sigma}_{k}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})-\lambda^{\sigma}_{k}(i,j)
.

In the previous algorithm, labels on the north and east sides of the grid read
Q(\sigma)
and
P(\sigma)
, in the sense that the label of the
i
-th top edge (from the left) indicates which row of
Q(\sigma)
contains the letter
i
, and the label of the
j
-th right edge (from the bottom) indicates which row of
P(\sigma)
contains the letter
j
.
Since Robinson–Schensted’s correspondence is bijective, it is possible to retrieve the permutation and the labels on the whole grid from these north and east labels.
This is done with the following edge rules, which we will refer to as
Fomin’s inverse local rules
:

•

if
N(c)=E(c)=0
, then
S(c)=W(c)=0
;

•

if
N(c)=E(c)>0
then
S(c)=W(c)=N(c)-1
;

•

if

then
S(c)=N(c)
and
W(c)=E(c)
;

see
Figure

.
This recursively reconstructs the labels on all edges of the grid, and permutation points are exactly in the cells with south/west labels
0
and north/east labels
1
.

Let us introduce some formalism related to these rules.
We shall study finite words on nonnegative integer letters, representing sequences of labels on vertical or horizontal lines of rectangular grids.
For instance if the edges of a rectangular grid
[\![i,i^{\prime}]\!]\times[\![j,j^{\prime}]\!]
are labeled with integers then the associated top-word is the “right-to-left” sequence of labels from
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
to
(i,j^{\prime})
.
Similarly the associated right-word is the “top-to-bottom” sequence of labels from
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
to
(i^{\prime},j)
.
Associated bottom- and left-words are defined analogously.
For example on
Figure

8
the highlighted rectangle yields top-word

, right-word

, bottom-word

and left-word

.

Illustration of Property (

Fomin’s inverse rules can be interpreted as a map
\mathcal{F}=\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}},\,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\right)
with input a pair of top- and right-words and output a pair of bottom- and left-words.
In the previous example:
\mathcal{F}(3\,2\,2\,1,3\,2\,2)=(1\,0\,1\,0,0\,0\,1)
.
Notice that the symmetry of Fomin’s inverse local rules implies the following property of
\mathcal{F}
:

\text{for any words }w,w^{\prime},\quad\mathcal{F}(w^{\prime},w)=\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}(w,w^{\prime}),\,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,w^{\prime})\right).

If
w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}
are words we sometimes say that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}})
is obtained by
applying

to
w^{\mathrm{top}}
.
In most reasonings we shall apply
w^{\mathrm{right}}
to
w^{\mathrm{top}}
letter by letter. This can be done since

\text{for any }k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},\,k\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\mathrm{top}},k),\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right),

(22)

see
Figure

10
.
We can therefore reformulate Fomin’s inverse local rules as follows:

•

applying the letter
0
to a word does not change it;

•

applying a letter
k\geq 1
to a word containing no
k
does not change it.
Otherwise it changes its first
k
into a
k\!\!-\!\!1
, and then applies
k\!\!-\!\!1
to the rest of the word;

see
Figure

11
.

The effects of applying one letter to a word.

If
w
is a word and

, denote by

the number of

’s in
w
.
Since the number of edges labeled
h
between two vertices does not depend on the up-right path taken, we deduce the following “mass conservation” property of
\mathcal{F}
:

\text{for any words }w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}},\quad\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)+\#_{h}w^{\mathrm{top}}=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)+\#_{h}w^{\mathrm{right}}.

(23)

This will be useful in
Section

4.6
.
We then define a pseudo-distance between words
w,w^{\prime}
by the following formula:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}(w,w^{\prime}):=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sup_{w^{\mathrm{right}}\text{ word}}\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|.

The symmetry property of
\mathcal{F}
implies that

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}(w,w^{\prime})=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\sup_{w^{\mathrm{top}}\text{ word}}\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},\,w\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},\,w^{\prime}\right)\right|.

Let us conclude this section by explaining informally how we shall prove
Theorem

2.10
.
By
2.9
,
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
is approximated with
\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}
, whose increments are described by the edge labels of Fomin’s construction.
Therefore we aim to study the number of each label in the word
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\big(w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}\big)
where
w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}
and
w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}
are edge words of
\sigma_{n}
on a local rectangle.
To do this, our technique is to simplify the top- and right-words without changing “too much” the resulting bottom- and left-words.
More precisely, we try to control the pseudo-distance
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}
, whose associated equivalence relation we call “Fomin equivalence”.
In
Section

4.4
we prove that Fomin equivalence is implied by the well-known Knuth equivalence (for which we refer to Section 2.1 in
[
Ful96
]
and Section 3.4 in
[
Sag01
]
).
This is done by checking that Fomin equivalence is preserved by the elementary Knuth transformations, and we can then use the fundamental fact that Knuth equivalence means equality of the P-tableaux.
Independently, thanks to the derivability condition of
Theorem

2.10
, the labels of
w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}
and
w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}
are well equidistributed.
One can see that the P-tableau of a word with bounded, equidistributed letters is similar to the P-tableau of this word’s decreasing reordering.
We deduce that
w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}
and
w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}
are “almost” Fomin equivalent to their decreasing reorderings; for this we need the regularity property of
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}
established in
Section

4.3
.
This finally allows us to approximate the local increments of
\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}
with the simpler case of decreasing words, studied in
Section

4.5
, and we conclude the proof of
Theorem

2.10
in
Section

4.6
.

4.3
Regularity of inverse Fomin dynamics

In this section we investigate a regularity property of the map
\mathcal{F}
.
This will allow us to control how much the output words of Fomin’s inverse algorithm can change
when the input words are replaced by approximations.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We call
block
a (possibly empty) word with nondecreasing integer letters between
0
and
r
.
If
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathbf{q}:=(q_{i,k})_{1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
is an array of nonnegative integers, define the word
\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q})
as the following concatenation of
\ell
blocks:

\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q}):=(0^{q_{1,0}}1^{q_{1,1}}\dots r^{q_{1,r}})\dots(0^{q_{i,0}}\dots k^{q_{i,k}}\dots r^{q_{i,r}})\dots(0^{q_{\ell,0}}1^{q_{r,1}}\dots r^{q_{\ell,r}}).

Also define a pseudo-distance on such arrays:

\text{for any }\mathbf{q,q^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)},\quad\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{r}|q_{i,k}-q_{i,k}^{\prime}|k\left(8k^{2}\right)^{\ell-i}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)},w:=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q})
, and
w^{\mathrm{right}}
be a word.
Then there exists a distinguished
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}})=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}})
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we denote this by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(\mathbf{q},\,w^{\mathrm{right}})=\mathbf{\tilde{q}}
.
Moreover for any array
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
:

\delta\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\big(\mathbf{q},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\big),\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\big(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\big)\Big)\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right).

This last bound means that the pseudo-distance
\delta
can not increase after applying a word, regardless of this word.

Proof.

By induction it suffices to prove the lemma when
w^{\mathrm{right}}=k
is a single positive letter.
The word
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,k)
is obtained from
w
by changing the first
k
of some block to
k\!\!-\!\!1
, the first
k\!\!-\!\!1
of another block to
k\!\!-\!\!2
, and so on.
Since each new letter can be seen in the same block as the letter that produced it, this will allow us to write
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,k)=\mathrm{blocks}(\bf\tilde{q})
where
\bf\tilde{q}
is obtained by doing local changes in
\bf q
.

Denote by
h
the number of decrements that happen when applying
k
to
w
, and by
i_{k}\leq\dots\leq i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1}
the block indices of these decrements.
Since each block is nondecreasing, two decrements cannot happen in the same block,
i.e.
we have
i_{k}<i_{k\!-\!1}<\dots<i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1}
.
We can thus write
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,k)=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}})
where
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
satisfies

\tilde{q}_{i_{k},k}=q_{i_{k},k}\!-\!1;\;\tilde{q}_{i_{k},k\!-\!1}=q_{i_{k},k\!-\!1}\!+\!1;\quad\dots\quad;\quad\tilde{q}_{i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1},k\!-\!h\!+\!1}=q_{i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1},k\!-\!h\!+\!1}\!-\!1;\;\tilde{q}_{i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1},k\!-\!h}=q_{i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1},k\!-\!h}\!+\!1;

and everywhere else
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}
coincide.
This establishes the first assertion of the lemma.

For the second assertion write
w^{\prime}:=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
and
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},k)
.
Then define analogously
h^{\prime}\in[\![0,k]\!]
and
1\leq i_{k}^{\prime}<i_{k\!-\!1}^{\prime}<\dots<i_{k\!-\!h^{\prime}\!+\!1}^{\prime}\leq\ell
.
If the decrement indices are the same as for
\mathbf{q}

i.e.

h^{\prime}=h
and
i_{k}=i_{k}^{\prime}
, …,
i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1}=i_{k\!-\!h\!+\!1}^{\prime}
, then
\delta\left(\mathbf{\tilde{q}},\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\prime}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)
.
The only possibility for the indices not to be the same is that some positive letter
j\in[\![1,k]\!]
in some block
i\in[\![1,k]\!]
of one word was decremented while the same block of the other word did not contain this letter.
Suppose this happens and take
i
minimal for this property.
Then

\text{either}\quad\tilde{q}_{i,j}=q_{i,j}\!-\!1\text{ and }\tilde{q}_{i,j}^{\prime}=q_{i,j}^{\prime}=0,\qquad\text{or}\quad\tilde{q}_{i,j}^{\prime}=q_{i,j}^{\prime}\!-\!1\text{ and }\tilde{q}_{i,j}=q_{i,j}=0.

Only one of these occurs, and by symmetry we might suppose the first one does.
Minimality of
i
means that applying
k
to
w
and
w^{\prime}
starts with the same dynamics, up until their
i
-th block where the first
j
of
w
becomes
j\!\!-\!\!1
and
j\!\!-\!\!1
is applied to the rest of
w
while this block in
w^{\prime}
remains unchanged and
j
is applied to the rest of
w^{\prime}
.
These changes in the
i
-th block yield the equations:

|\tilde{q}_{i,j}-\tilde{q}_{i,j}^{\prime}|=|q_{i,j}-q_{i,j}^{\prime}|-1\quad;\quad|\tilde{q}_{i,j\!-\!1}-\tilde{q}_{i,j\!-\!1}^{\prime}|\leq|q_{i,j\!-\!1}-q_{i,j\!-\!1}^{\prime}|+1.

(24)

In the following blocks, at most
2(j\!\!-\!\!1)
values may change in
\mathbf{q}
and at most
2j
may in
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
.
This yields a family of “disagreement” indices
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\![i\!\!+\!\!1,\ell]\!]\times[\![1,j]\!]
of size
|\mathcal{A}|\leq 4j
and such that:

\text{for any }(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[\![1,\ell]\!]\times[\![1,r]\!]\setminus\left(\mathcal{A}\cup\{(i,j)\}\cup\{(i,j\!-\!1)\}\right),\quad|\tilde{q}_{i,j}-\tilde{q}_{i,j}^{\prime}|=|q_{i,j}-q_{i,j}^{\prime}|.

(25)

On disagreement indices we use the simple bound:

\text{for any }(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A},\quad|\tilde{q}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\tilde{q}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\prime}|\leq|q_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-q_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\prime}|+2.

(26)

Using
Equations

24
,
25
and
26
we finally deduce that:

\delta\left(\mathbf{\tilde{q}},\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\prime}\right)\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)-j\left(8j^{2}\right)^{\ell-i}+(j\!-\!1)\left(8(j\!-\!1)^{2}\right)^{\ell-i}+8j\cdot j\left(8j^{2}\right)^{\ell-i-1}\\
\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)-j\left(8j^{2}\right)^{\ell-i}+(j\!-\!1)\left(8j^{2}\right)^{\ell-i}+8j\cdot j\left(8j^{2}\right)^{\ell-i-1}\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)

as desired.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
and
w:=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q}),w^{\prime}:=\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
. The following holds:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}(w,w^{\prime})\leq\delta(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}).

Proof.

Fix
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
w^{\mathrm{right}}
a word.
Write
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(\mathbf{q},\,w^{\mathrm{right}})
and
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}})
.
Then:

\displaystyle\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\ w^{\mathrm{right}})-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}})\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{q}_{i,h}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\tilde{q}_{i,h}^{\prime}\right|\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{\tilde{q}},\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\prime}\right)\leq\delta\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)

where the last inequality is given by
Lemma

4.1
.
∎

4.4
Fomin and Knuth equivalence

We say that two words
w,w^{\prime}
are
Fomin equivalent
or simply equivalent, denoted
w\equiv w^{\prime}
, when
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}(w,w^{\prime})=0
.
This means that applying any word to
w
and
w^{\prime}
yields resulting words with the same amount of each non-zero label.
In particular two equivalent words have the same number of
k
’s for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, but not necessarily the same number of
0
’s.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
w,w^{\prime},w^{\mathrm{right}}
be words. If
w\equiv w^{\prime}
then:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

Proof.

It suffices to observe that for any word
w^{\mathrm{right}}_{2}
:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right),\,w_{2}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}w_{2}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)

and likewise with
w^{\prime}
.
The lemma then follows directly from definition.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

If
w,w^{\prime}
are words and
i\in\mathbb{N}
:

w\equiv w^{\prime}\implies i\cdot w\equiv i\cdot w^{\prime}\text{ and }w\cdot i\equiv w^{\prime}\cdot i

Proof.

We aim to prove that for any word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
the following holds: for any pair of equivalent words
w\equiv w^{\prime}
, any
i\in\mathbb{N}
and any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot w,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot w^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w\cdot i,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\prime}\cdot i,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

(27)

We do this by induction on the length of
w^{\mathrm{right}}
.
If
w^{\mathrm{right}}
is empty, (
27
) is immediate since equivalent words have the same amount of each positive letter.
Now suppose this holds for some word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
and let us prove it for every word
j\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}
with
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix
w\equiv w^{\prime}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

For the first equality of (
27
) we distinguish between two cases.
If
i=j
then, using Property (
22
):

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot w,\,j\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot w,\,i\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left((i\!\!-\!\!1)^{+}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,(i\!\!-\!\!1)^{+}\right),\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)

(28)

where we used the standard notation
(i\!-\!1)^{+}=\max(i\!-\!1,0)
, and likewise for
w^{\prime}
.
See the left part of
Figure

12
for a representation.

Lemma

4.3
implies
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w,\,(i\!\!-\!\!1)^{+}\right)\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\prime},\,(i\!\!-\!\!1)^{+}\right)
and the first part of (
27
) follows by induction.

Otherwise
i\neq j
and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot w,\,j\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,j),\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right),

likewise for
w^{\prime}
.
Lemma

4.3
implies
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\,j)\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime},\,j)
and the first part of (
27
) follows once again by induction.

For the second equality of (
27
), observe that applying
j
to
w\cdot i
decrements a number of letters in
w
, say
k\in[\![0,j]\!]
, and then applies
j\!-\!k=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}(w,j)
to
i
(resp.
w^{\prime}
,
k^{\prime}
).
Therefore

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w\cdot i,\,j)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\,j)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(i,\,j\!-\!k)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime}\cdot i,\,j)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime},\,j)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(i,\,j\!-\!k^{\prime}).

(29)

Since
k
is a number of decrements, it can be expressed as the sum of all letters of
w
minus the sum of all letters of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\,j)
.
As such:

k=\sum_{h\geq 1}h\,\#_{h}w-h\,\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,j)=\sum_{h\geq 1}h\,\#_{h}w^{\prime}-h\,\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime},j)=k^{\prime}

where the second equality is a consequence of
w\equiv w^{\prime}
.
Finally, using (
29
) and Property (
22
) (see the right part of
Figure

12
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w\cdot i,\,j\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w,\,j)\cdot i^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right);\\
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\prime}\cdot i,\,j\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\prime},\,j)\cdot i^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right);\end{array}\right.

(30)

where
i^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(i,j\!-\!k)
, and we conclude by induction.
∎

Illustration of equalities (
28
) and (
30
) used to prove
Lemma

4.4
.

Corollary 4.5
.

For any words
w_{1},w_{2},w_{1}^{\prime},w_{2}^{\prime}
such that
w_{1}\equiv w_{1}^{\prime}
and
w_{2}\equiv w_{2}^{\prime}
, we have
w_{1}w_{2}\equiv w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Successive application of
Lemma

4.4
yield both
w_{1}w_{2}\equiv w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}
and
w_{1}^{\prime}w_{2}^{\prime}\equiv w_{1}w_{2}^{\prime}
, whence the result.
∎

Using
0\equiv\emptyset
, this yields the following fact (which was actually quite clear from the beginning):

Corollary 4.6
.

Any word is equivalent to itself without its
0
’s.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any integers
i<j\leq k
,
j\,i\,k\equiv j\,k\,i
.

Proof.

If
i=0
then this is a direct consequence of
4.6
.
Now suppose
i\geq 1
.
The strategy is the same as for the proof of
Lemma

4.4
.
We aim to prove that for any word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
the following holds: for any integers
i<j\leq k
and any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,k\,i,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

(31)

We do this by induction on the length of
w^{\mathrm{right}}
.
If
w^{\mathrm{right}}
is empty, (
31
) is immediate.
Now suppose this holds for some word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
and let us prove it for every word
m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
m=i
then

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\;i\!\!-\!\!1\;k,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\;;\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,k\,i,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\;k\;i\!\!-\!\!1,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

Since
i\!\!-\!\!1<j\leq k
, (
31
) follows by induction.

If
m=j
then

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\!\!-\!\!1\;i^{\prime}\;k,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\;;\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,k\,i,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\!\!-\!\!1\;k\;i^{\prime},\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)

where
i^{\prime}=i\wedge(j\!\!-\!\!2)
.
Since
i^{\prime}<j\!\!-\!\!1\leq k
, (
31
) follows by induction.

If
m=k
we distinguish between two cases.
If
j=k
then
m=j
and this case was already treated.
If
j\leq k\!\!-\!\!1
then
i\leq k\!\!-\!\!2
and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\;i\;k\!\!-\!\!1,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\;;\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,k\,i,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\;k\!\!-\!\!1\;i,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

Since
i<j\leq k\!\!-\!\!1
, (
31
) follows by induction.

Otherwise
m\notin\{i,j,k\}
and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(j\,i\,k,\,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right),

likewise for
j\,k\,i
. (
31
) follows once again by induction, and this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For any integers
i\leq j<k
,
i\,k\,j\equiv k\,i\,j
.

Proof.

If
i=0
then this is a direct consequence of
4.6
.
Now suppose
i\geq 1
.
We proceed as before and aim to prove that for any word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
the following holds: for any integers
i\leq j<k
and any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\,k\,j,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\,i\,j,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

(32)

We do this by induction on the length of
w^{\mathrm{right}}
.
If
w^{\mathrm{right}}
is empty, (
32
) is immediate.
Now suppose this holds for some word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
and let us prove it for every word
m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

The cases
m=i
,
m=j
and
m\notin\{i,j,k\}
are handled just like in the proof of
Lemma

4.7
.
The only subtlety comes when
m=k
.
In this case, if
i=k\!\!-\!\!1
then
j=k\!\!-\!\!1
and

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\,k\,j,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!2,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)

while

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\,i\,j,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!2\;k\!\!-\!\!1,w^{\mathrm{right}}\right).

We can thus apply
Lemma

4.7
to get
k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!2\equiv k\!\!-\!\!1\;k\!\!-\!\!2\;k\!\!-\!\!1
, and directly conclude.
If
i<k\!\!-\!\!1
then

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\,k\,j,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(i\,k\!\!-\!\!1\,j^{\prime},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\;;\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\,i\,j,m\cdot w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(k\!\!-\!\!1\,i\,j^{\prime},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)

where
j^{\prime}=j\wedge(k\!\!-\!\!2)
.
Since
i\leq j^{\prime}<k
, (
32
) follows by induction.
∎

Lemmas

4.8
and
4.7
together with
4.5
tell us transforming within a word any triplet
j\,i\,k
where
i<j\leq k
into
j\,k\,i
or
i\,k\,j
where
i\leq j<k
into
k\,i\,j
preserves Fomin equivalence.
In other words, two adjacent letters can be switched if they are followed or preceded by an intermediate value, and equality cases are handled by considering that among two letters with the same value, the greatest is the one that comes last in the word.
These transformations are known as
elementary Knuth transformations
.
Two words are said to be
Knuth equivalent
when they differ by successive elementary transformations.
We have thus proved:

Proposition 4.9
.

If two finite words are Knuth equivalent then they are Fomin equivalent.

We have not been able to determine whether the converse is true or not, but we believe this could be an interesting question.
Now, we shall use the following well-known result about Knuth equivalence:

Theorem 4.10

(
[
Ful96
,
Sag01
]
)
.

Two words are Knuth equivalent if and only if they have the same P-tableau.

This theorem is the last puzzle piece we needed to combine our previous results into
4.11
, which shall prove useful in the permuton setting.
First we state some key observation.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
w
be a word on letters between
1
and
r
and
P(w)
its P-tableau.
For any
i\in[\![1,r]\!]
and
k\in[\![0,r]\!]
, denote by
q_{i,k}(w)
the number of
k
’s in the
(r\!\!-\!\!i\!\!+\!\!1)
-st row of
P(w)

(notice that
q_{i,k}(w)=0
whenever
i>k
).
By
Theorem

4.10
, the array

\mathbf{q}(w)=\big(q_{i,k}(w)\big)_{1\leq i\leq r,0\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r(r\!+\!1)}

characterizes the Knuth equivalence class of
w
.
Moreover
Theorem

1.2
allows to describe this array in terms of nondecreasing subsequences of
w
.
Indeed, writing
w^{\leq k}
for the sub-word of
w
consisting of its letters with values at most
k
, recall that the P-tableau of
w^{\leq k}
is the restriction of
P(w)
to its values at most
k
.
Thus
\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w^{\leq k}\right)
is the number of boxes with values at most
k
in the first
i
rows of
P(w)
, and we deduce:

q_{r\!-\!i\!+\!1,k}(w)=\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w^{\leq k}\right)-\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w^{\leq k\!-\!1}\right)-\mathrm{LIS}_{i\!-\!1}\left(w^{\leq k}\right)+\mathrm{LIS}_{i\!-\!1}\left(w^{\leq k\!-\!1}\right).

(33)

With the notation of
Section

4.2
, the word
\mathrm{blocks}\big(\mathbf{q}(w)\big)
is the concatenation of all rows of
P(w)
, from last to first.
It is usually called the
row word
of
P(w)
, and one can see that its P-tableau is
P(w)
(see Lemma 3.4.5 in
[
Sag01
]
or Section 2.1 in
[
Ful96
]
).
Hence
\mathrm{blocks}\big(\mathbf{q}(w)\big)
is a distinguished word in the Knuth equivalence class of
w
.
These facts lead to the following result:

Proposition 4.11
.

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
and set
w_{\mathrm{ord}}:=r^{\gamma_{r}}\dots 1^{\gamma_{1}}
.
For
k\in[\![1,r]\!]
denote by
\gamma_{(1)}^{k}\geq\dots\geq\gamma_{(k)}^{k}
the weakly decreasing order statistics of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}
.
Let
w
be a word on letters between
1
and
r
and define for each
i\in[\![1,r]\!]
:

\eta_{i,k}:=\left|\gamma_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\gamma_{(i)}^{k}-\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w^{\leq k}\right)\right|

where
\gamma_{(i)}^{k}:=0
if
i>k
.
Then we have the following upper bound:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(w,w_{\mathrm{ord}}\right)\leq 4r(8r^{2})^{r}\max_{1\leq i,k\leq r}\eta_{i,k}.

Proof.

Let
i,k\in[\![1,r]\!]
. Since any nondecreasing sequence of
w_{\mathrm{ord}}
can only have one type of letter, it is easy to see that

\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w_{\text{ord}}^{\leq k}\right)=\gamma_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\gamma_{(i)}^{k}.

Thus
Equation

33
yields

\left|q_{r\!-\!i\!+\!1,k}(w)-q_{r\!-\!i\!+\!1,k}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}\right)\right|\leq\eta_{i,k}+\eta_{i,k\!-\!1}+\eta_{i\!-\!1,k}+\eta_{i\!-\!1,k\!-\!1}.

(34)

Since
w
is Knuth equivalent to
\mathrm{blocks}\big(\mathbf{q}(w)\big)
and likewise for
w_{\mathrm{ord}}
,
4.9
implies

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(w,\,w_{\mathrm{ord}}\right)=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}\Big(\mathrm{blocks}\big(\mathbf{q}(w)\big),\,\mathrm{blocks}\big(\mathbf{q}(w_{\mathrm{ord}})\big)\Big).

We can then apply
4.2
with (
34
) to obtain:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(w,\,w_{\mathrm{ord}}\right)\leq\delta\big(\mathbf{q}(w),\mathbf{q}(w_{\mathrm{ord}})\big)\leq 4r(8r^{2})^{r}\max_{1\leq i,k\leq r}\eta_{i,k}.\qed

4.5
Study of decreasing labels

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
(\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, define

\phi\big((\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},\,(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\big):=\beta_{1}+\#_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}},\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right).

(35)

For example
\phi\big((\alpha_{1}),(\beta_{1})\big)=\beta_{1}+\left(\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}\right)^{+}=\alpha_{1}\vee\beta_{1}
, and

\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\big)=\beta_{1}+\left(\left(\alpha_{1}-\left(\alpha_{2}\wedge\beta_{2}\right)\right)^{+}+\left(\alpha_{2}\wedge\beta_{2}\right)-\beta_{1}\right)^{+}=\alpha_{1}\vee\beta_{1}\vee\left(\alpha_{2}\wedge\beta_{2}\right).

Notice that the index
r
can be omitted on
\phi
, since

\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r},0,\dots,0),\,(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r},0,\dots,0)\big)=\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}),\,(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r})\big).

This section is devoted to the study of this function and its continuous extension to sequences of real numbers.

Proposition 4.12
.

For each
r\geq 1
, the function
\phi
has a unique continuous extension from
\left(\mathbb{N}^{r}\right)^{2}
to
\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r}\right)^{2}
satisfying the following homogeneity property:

\text{for any }c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }(\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r},\quad\phi\big((c\alpha_{k})\,,\,(c\beta_{k})\big)=c\phi\big((\alpha_{k})\,,\,(\beta_{k})\big).

(36)

Proof.

For stability reasons, we prove this by studying the dynamics of block words as defined in
Section

4.2
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{r}),\mathbf{b}=(b_{0},\dots,b_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r+1}
.
Define

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\dots,\varphi_{r-1}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}),\varphi_{r}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\right)

\displaystyle:=\left(a_{0}\!+\!(a_{1}\wedge b_{1}),\;\left(a_{1}\!-\!b_{1}\right)^{+}\!+\!(a_{2}\wedge b_{2}),\;\dots,\;\left(a_{r\!-\!1}\!-\!b_{r\!-\!1}\right)^{+}\!+\!(a_{r}\wedge b_{r}),\;\left(a_{r}\!-\!b_{r}\right)^{+}\right).

(37)

One can then see that the following holds:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(0^{a_{0}}\dots r^{a_{r}},\,0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right):=0^{\varphi_{0}\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)}\dots r^{\varphi_{r}\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right)};\\
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(0^{a_{0}}\dots r^{a_{r}},\,0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right):=0^{\varphi_{0}\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\right)}\dots r^{\varphi_{r}\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{a}\right)}.\end{array}\right.

Now let us study the effect of applying a single block to a sequence of blocks.
For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and any array
\mathbf{q}=(q_{i,k})_{1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}
, one has:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(0^{q_{1,0}}\dots r^{q_{1,r}}\,\dots\,0^{q_{\ell,0}}\dots r^{q_{\ell,r}},\,0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(0^{q_{1,0}}\dots r^{q_{1,r}},\,0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\Big(0^{q_{2,0}}\dots r^{q_{2,r}}\,\dots\,0^{q_{\ell,0}}\dots r^{q_{\ell,r}},\,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(0^{q_{1,0}}\dots r^{q_{1,r}},\,0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=0^{\varphi_{0}\left(\mathbf{q_{1}},\mathbf{b}\right)}\dots r^{\varphi_{r}\left(\mathbf{q_{1}},\mathbf{b}\right)}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(0^{q_{2,0}}\dots r^{q_{2,r}}\,\dots\,0^{q_{\ell,0}}\dots r^{q_{\ell,r}},\,0^{\varphi_{0}\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{q_{1}}\right)}\dots r^{\varphi_{r}\left(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{q_{1}}\right)}\right).

where
\mathbf{q_{1}}:=\left(q_{1,k}\right)_{0\leq k\leq r}
.
We see by induction that this resulting word is yet again a concatenation of
\ell
blocks.
With a slight abuse of notation, define
\varphi\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{b}\right)=\big(\varphi_{i,k}\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{b}\right)\big)_{1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(\mathbf{q},0^{b_{0}}\dots r^{b_{r}}\right)

with the notation of
Lemma

4.1
.
Last calculation showed the following recursive formula for
\varphi
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi_{1,\cdot}\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{b}\right)=\varphi\left(\mathbf{q_{1}},\mathbf{b}\right)\\
\big(\varphi_{i,k}\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{b}\right)\big)_{2\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}=\varphi\left(\mathbf{q}\setminus\mathbf{q_{1}}\,,\,\varphi\big(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{q_{1}}\big)\right)\end{array}\right.

(38)

where
\mathbf{q}\setminus\mathbf{q_{1}}:=\left(q_{i,k}\right)_{2\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}
.
Finally for any
\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\varphi\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)=\big(\varphi_{i,k}\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)\big)_{1\leq i\leq\ell,0\leq k\leq r}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\big(\mathbf{q},\mathrm{blocks}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\big)

Then
\varphi

\varphi\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\right)=\varphi\big(\varphi\left(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q^{\prime}_{1}}\right)\,,\,\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\setminus\mathbf{q^{\prime}_{1}}\big).

One advantage of this point of view is that

38

39

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ell(r\!+\!1)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\ell^{\prime}(r\!+\!1)}

\phi

\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r}\right)^{2}

\phi\big((\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\big)=\beta_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varphi_{i,1}(\mathbf{q}_{\alpha},\mathbf{q}_{\beta})

where

From homogeneity of

\phi

For the uniqueness claim, observe that

\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r}\right)^{2}

∎

Many properties of

\left(\mathbb{N}^{r}\right)^{2}

For instance:

\text{for any }\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad\phi\big((\alpha),(\beta)\big)=\alpha\vee\beta.

(40)

One can also see that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left((r+1)^{\alpha_{r+1}}\,r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}},r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right)=(r+1)^{\alpha_{r+1}}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}},r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}},(r+1)^{\beta_{r+1}}\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}},r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right)

\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r},\alpha_{r+1})\,,\,(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r},0)\big)=\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r},0)\,,\,(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r},\beta_{r+1})\big)\\
=\phi\big((\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})\,,\,(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r})\big).

(41)

We can use the last two properties of

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Consider
(\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{r}
such that

\phi\big((\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r}\big)=\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k}

(42)

for all
1\leq k\leq r
.
Then
\alpha_{k}\cdot\beta_{k}=0
for all
1\leq k\leq r
.

Proof.

Let us prove the lemma by induction on
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
r=1
, (
40
) implies
\alpha_{1}\vee\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}
, i.e.
\alpha_{1}\cdot\beta_{1}=0
.
Now let
r>1
and assume that the lemma holds for
r-1
.
Use
Equation

42
for
k=r
along with (
40
) to get
\alpha_{r}\vee\beta_{r}=\alpha_{r}+\beta_{r}
, which implies
\alpha_{r}\cdot\beta_{r}=0
.
Then, thanks to (
41
),
Equation

42
becomes

\phi\big((\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r-1},(\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r-1}\big)=\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k}

for all
1\leq k\leq r-1
.
The proof follows by induction.
∎

Let us present one last property of
\phi
.
Notice that for any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the function
\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}
does not depend on letters less than
h
in the input words.
Indeed, applying a letter less than

can only decrement letters that are less than

.
Subsequently for any
r\geq h
and
(\alpha_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r},(\beta_{k})_{1\leq k\leq r}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}}\,,\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right)=\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots h^{\alpha_{h}}\,,\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots h^{\beta_{h}}\right).

Combined with the following “translation-invariance” property of Fomin’s inverse rules:

\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots h^{\alpha_{h}}\,,\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots h^{\beta_{h}}\right)=\#_{1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left((r-h+1)^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{h}}\,,\,(r-h+1)^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{h}}\right),

we deduce that:

\phi\big((\alpha_{k})_{h\leq k\leq r}\,,\,(\beta_{k})_{h\leq k\leq r}\big)=\beta_{h}+\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\alpha_{r}}\dots 1^{\alpha_{1}}\,,\,r^{\beta_{r}}\dots 1^{\beta_{1}}\right).

(43)

4.6
Continuous inverse growth rules

The aim of this section is to prove
Theorem

2.10
and
2.11
, using all the previously developed tools.

Proof of
Theorem

2.10
.

Fix an integer
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and a permuton
\mu
satisfying
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
.
The function
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
is then entirely described by
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}_{r}
, for which we will omit the exponent.
Moreover
\mu
’s sampled permutations are almost surely
r
-increasing, meaning their diagrams consist of at most
r
rows.
The tableaux of
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
are then entirely described by the functions
\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\sigma_{n}}_{r}
, for which we will also omit the exponent.

Let
(x,y)\in(0,1]^{2}
be such that the left-derivatives

\alpha_{k}:=\partial^{-}_{x}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{k}:=\partial^{-}_{y}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)

for
k\in[\![1,r]\!]
all exist.
Fix
s,t\geq 0
, and
\epsilon>0
small enough to have
x-t\epsilon,y-s\epsilon\geq 0
.
According to
2.9
, we can and will work on an event where the convergence

\text{for all }k\in[\![1,r]\!],\quad\left\|\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}-\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor\cdot\,n\rfloor,\lfloor\cdot\,n\rfloor\big)\right\|=\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(1)

holds.
We can use this convergence along with the derivability condition of
\widetilde{\lambda}
to obtain decent control over the disposition of Fomin’s edge labels.
Fix an integer
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and define regular subdivisions

x-t\epsilon=x_{p}<x_{p-1}<\dots<x_{0}=x\quad\text{and}\quad y-s\epsilon=y_{p}<y_{p-1}<\dots<y_{0}=y

of respective steps
t\epsilon/p
and
s\epsilon/p
.
Then for any
k\in[\![1,r]\!]
and
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x_{j\!-\!1},y)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x_{j},y)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)-(j\!-\!1)t\epsilon\alpha_{k}/p+\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}\left(\epsilon(j\!-\!1)/p\right)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)+jt\epsilon\alpha_{k}/p+\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}\left(\epsilon j/p\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon t\alpha_{k}/p+\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)

whence

\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor nx_{j\!-\!1}\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor\big)-\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor nx_{j}\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor\big)=n\epsilon t\alpha_{k}/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

(44)

This means that edge labels of
\sigma_{n}
on the north side of
[\![\lfloor n(x\!-\!t\epsilon)\rfloor,\lfloor nx\rfloor]\!]\times[\![\lfloor n(y\!-\!s\epsilon)\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor]\!]
are quite well equidistributed.
Denote by
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{bot}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{left}}
the words associated with the edge labels of
\sigma_{n}
on this rectangular grid, as defined in
Section

4.2
.
Write the top-word as a concatenation
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}}=w_{(1)}^{\mathrm{top}}\dots w_{(p)}^{\mathrm{top}}
, where each
w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top}}
consists of labels from
x
-coordinate
\lfloor nx_{j\!-\!1}\rfloor
to
\lfloor nx_{j}\rfloor
.
Then (
44
) translates as:

\#_{k}w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top}}=n\epsilon t\alpha_{k}/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

(45)

In particular, by summing (
45
) over
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
:

\#_{k}w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}}=n\epsilon t\alpha_{k}+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

(46)

Similar statements hold for
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}
.
Next, let us explain how to relate this property of equidistribution to
4.11
.
In what follows, we consider that nondecreasing subsequences can only contain positive letters.

Claim 1
:
For any
i,k\in[\![1,r]\!]
the following holds:

\left|\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}\right)-nt\epsilon\left(\alpha_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\alpha_{(i)}^{k}\right)\right|\leq nt\epsilon r^{2}/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n)

with the notation of
4.11
.

To see this, note that a nondecreasing subsequence of
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
reads as a subsequence of
1
’s, followed by a subsequence of
2
’s, …, followed by a subsequence of
k
’s.
Let
A=A_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{i}
be an
i
-nondecreasing subsequence (
i.e.
a union of
i
nondecreasing subsequences, which we can suppose disjoint) of
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
with maximum length.

We say
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
is “valid” if for each
h\in[\![1,i]\!]
, the nondecreasing subsequence
A_{h}
takes letters of at most one value in the subword
w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
.
In this case, at best,
A
can thus take the
i
most represented letters in
w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
.
Using (
45
), this allows to bound the number of letters picked up by
A
in any valid subword
w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
by

n\epsilon t\left(\alpha_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\alpha_{(i)}^{k}\right)/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

When an index
j\in[\![1,p]\!]
is not valid, it means that some
A_{h}
takes letters of at least two different values in
w_{(j)}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
.
Since each
A_{h}
is a nondecreasing subsequence, it can “invalidate” at most
k-1
subwords.
Therefore at most
i(k-1)
subwords are not valid, and in each of them
A
can potentially take every letter.
This observation, along with the previous analysis, leads to the following upper bound:

\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}\right)\leq n\epsilon t\left(\alpha_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\alpha_{(i)}^{k}\right)+i(k-1)nt\epsilon/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

Conversely we can construct an
i
-nondecreasing subsequence
B=B_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup B_{i\wedge k}
of
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
by letting each
B_{h}
take all
b_{h}
’s of
w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}
, where
b_{h}
is such that
\alpha_{b_{h}}=\alpha_{(h)}^{k}
.
Subsequently, using (
45
):

\mathrm{LIS}_{i}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top},\leq k}\right)\geq n\epsilon t\left(\alpha_{(1)}^{k}+\dots+\alpha_{(i\wedge k)}^{k}\right)+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

This concludes the proof of Claim 1.

Claim 2
:
Define
w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}}:=r^{\lfloor n\epsilon t\alpha_{r}\rfloor}\dots 1^{\lfloor n\epsilon t\alpha_{1}\rfloor}
.
There exists a constant
\chi_{r}>0
such that for any word
w^{\mathrm{right}}
and any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|\leq\chi_{r}t\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n);\\
\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|\leq\chi_{r}t\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).\end{array}\right.

Indeed with the notation of
4.11
, Claim 1 rewrites

\text{for any }i,k\in[\![1,r]\!],\quad\eta_{i,k}\leq nt\epsilon r^{2}/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n)

thus, letting
\chi_{r}:=4r^{3}(8r^{2})^{r}
:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}}\right)\leq\chi_{r}\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n),

which is the first desired inequality.
For the second inequality, use this first inequality along with
Equations

23
and
46
:

\displaystyle\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|+\left|\#_{h}w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}}-\#_{h}w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\chi_{r}\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

This proves Claim 2, and by symmetry the following holds as well:

Claim 3
:
Define
w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{right}}:=r^{\lfloor n\epsilon s\beta_{r}\rfloor}\dots 1^{\lfloor n\epsilon s\beta_{1}\rfloor}
.
Then for any word
w^{\mathrm{top}}
and any
h\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{left}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|\leq\chi_{r}s\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n);\\
\left|\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{h}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|\leq\chi_{r}s\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).\end{array}\right.

We can now finish the proof of
Theorem

2.10
. Using Claims 2 and 3 we obtain for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\left|\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|+\left|\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)-\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\mathrm{ord}}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(t+s)\chi_{r}\epsilon n/p+n\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon)+\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{o}(n).

Then using the equality
\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor\big)-\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor n(x\!-\!t\epsilon)\rfloor,\lfloor n(y\!-\!s\epsilon)\rfloor\big)=\#_{k}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)+\#_{k}w_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{right}}

\displaystyle\left|\Big(\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor nx\rfloor,\lfloor ny\rfloor\big)-\lambda_{k}\big(\lfloor n(x\!-\!t\epsilon)\rfloor,\lfloor n(y\!-\!s\epsilon)\rfloor\big)\Big)-\phi\big((n\epsilon t\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(n\epsilon s\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r}\big)\right|

) and taking

\displaystyle\left|\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x-t\epsilon,y-s\epsilon)\right)-\epsilon\phi\big((t\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(s\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r}\big)\right|\leq(t+s)\chi_{r}\epsilon/p+\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{o}(\epsilon),

\displaystyle\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left|\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x,y)-\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(x-t\epsilon,y-s\epsilon)}{\epsilon}-\phi\big((t\alpha_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r},(s\beta_{i})_{k\leq i\leq r}\big)\right|\leq(t+s)\chi_{r}/p.

, this concludes the proof of

2.10

4.7
Proofs of injectivity results

Proof of
Theorem

2.12
.

Let us first prove the existence of a triple satisfying those properties.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
consider
A_{k}\in\mathcal{P}^{k\nearrow}([0,1]^{2})
such that
\mu(A_{k})=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)
and
B_{k}\in\mathcal{P}^{k\searrow}([0,1]^{2})
such that
\mu(B_{k})=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu)
.
Then define:

A:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}A_{k},\;\;B:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}B_{k},\;\;\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}:=\mu\big(A\cap\cdot\big),\;\;\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}:=\mu\big(B\cap\cdot\big),\;\;\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}:=\mu\left(\left(A\cup B\right)^{c}\cap\cdot\right).

Clearly one has

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big)=\mu(A)=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)=\mu(B)=\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)

and the measure
\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}
is singular with respect to both
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}
and
\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}
.
Then notice that the intersection between a nondecreasing and a nonincreasing subset is entirely contained within the union of a vertical and a horizontal line.
Since
\mu
is a pre-permuton, it puts mass zero to such an intersection. As
A
(resp.
B
) is a countable union of nondecreasing (resp. nonincreasing) subsets, it implies
\mu\big(A\cap B\big)=0
.
Thus (i) holds, and
\mu=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}+\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}+\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}
.

Now for each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, since
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}},\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}
are sub-measures of
\mu
, one has

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big)\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu).

Moreover

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big)\geq\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}(A_{k})=\mu(A_{k})=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)\geq\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}(B_{k})=\mu(B_{k})=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{k}(\mu),

hence (ii) holds. Subsequently

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)

and

\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}(\mu)
.

Now consider a nondecreasing subset
A^{\prime}
that is disjoint of
A
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the subset
A_{k}\cup A^{\prime}
is
(k+1)
-nondecreasing, implying

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k+1}(\mu)\geq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)+\mu(A^{\prime}).

As a consequence:

\mu(A^{\prime})\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k+1}(\mu)-\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}(\mu)\underset{k\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

In particular
\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}(A^{\prime})=\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}(A^{\prime})=0
. Since this holds for any nondecreasing subset disjoint of
A
and
\mu^{\mathrm{decr}},\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}
are singular with respect to
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}
, this implies:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}\big(\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}\big)=0

and in particular
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}}
.
Repeating this argument with nonincreasing subsets, we deduce (iii).

Let us turn to the uniqueness claim. Keep the previous definition of
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}},\mu^{\mathrm{decr}},\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}
and write
\mu=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}+\nu^{\mathrm{decr}}+\nu^{\mathrm{sub}}
where
\nu^{\mathrm{incr}},\nu^{\mathrm{decr}},\nu^{\mathrm{sub}}
are finite (positive) measures satisfying

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\big(\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big)=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}\big(\nu^{\mathrm{decr}}\big)=\nu^{\mathrm{decr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LDS}}_{\infty}(\mu).

Let
C
be a countable union of nondecreasing subsets supporting the measure
\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.
Then:

\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right)=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}(C)\leq\mu(C)\leq\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}(\mu).

Since the first and last term are equal, we deduce equality of all terms.
Hence the sub-measure
\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
of
\mu
is given by the restriction
\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\mu(C\cap\cdot)
.
Moreover Property (iii) implies

\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}(C)=\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}(C)=0,

(47)

thus

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu\big)=\mu(C)=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}(C)\leq\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\big([0,1]^{2}\big)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\big(\mu\big)

from which we deduce

\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}(C)=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}\left([0,1]^{2}\right).

(48)

Equations

47
and
48
together imply

\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\mu(C\cap\cdot)=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}

as desired.
The proof of
\mu^{\mathrm{decr}}=\nu^{\mathrm{decr}}
works the same, and we finally deduce
\mu^{\mathrm{sub}}=\nu^{\mathrm{sub}}
.
∎

Proof of
2.14
.

For any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{\infty}\left(\mu|_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\right)=\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}([0,x]\times[0,y]).

Hence
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\nu}
implies

\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}([0,x]\times[0,y])=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}([0,x]\times[0,y])

for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
, and therefore
\mu^{\mathrm{incr}}=\nu^{\mathrm{incr}}
.
∎

The idea to prove
2.15
is that while we could not recover
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}
from its PDE together with its boundary condition
\mathrm{\widetilde{RS}}(\mu)
, there is a simple explicit solution for the last row of the tableaux.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mu
be a pre-permuton satisfying
\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{r}(\mu)=1
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Then for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}(x,y)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}(x,1)\wedge\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}(1,y).

Proof.

Denote by
F=(F_{1},F_{2})
the pair of distribution functions of each marginal of
\mu
, so that the push-forward
\hat{\mu}=F_{*}\mu
is a permuton.
Since
F
is nondecreasing in each coordinate, we have for any
k\in[\![1,r]\!]
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\hat{\mu}|_{[0,F_{1}(x^{\prime})]\times[0,F_{2}(y^{\prime})]}\right)=\widetilde{\mathrm{LIS}}_{k}\left(\mu|_{[0,x^{\prime}]\times[0,y^{\prime}]}\right).

Thus
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\hat{\mu}}\circ F
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
let
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathrm{Sample}_{n}(\mu)
.
As noted before obtaining Property (
43
), for any words
w^{\mathrm{top}}
and
w^{\mathrm{right}}
, the number of
r
’s in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}(w^{\mathrm{top}},w^{\mathrm{right}})
does not depend on letters less than
r
in
w^{\mathrm{top}}
and
w^{\mathrm{right}}
.
Let
1\leq i\leq i^{\prime}\leq n
,
1\leq j\leq j^{\prime}\leq n
, and
w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}
be the top- and right-words of
\sigma_{n}
’s edge labels on
[\![i,i^{\prime}]\!]\times[\![j,j^{\prime}]\!]
.
Since these words almost surely only have labels at most
r
, the previous observation yields:

\#_{r}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}},w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)=\#_{r}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}},\,r^{\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}}\right)=\left(\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}-\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}\right)^{+}.

Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})-\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\#_{r}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bot}}\left(r^{\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}},\,r^{\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}}\right)+\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}

\displaystyle=\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{top}}\vee\#_{r}w_{n}^{\mathrm{right}}

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})-\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,j^{\prime})\big)\vee\big(\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})-\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i^{\prime},j)\big),

and in particular when
i^{\prime}=j^{\prime}=n
, almost surely:

\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,j)=\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(i,n)\wedge\lambda_{r}^{\sigma_{n}}(n,j).

Using
2.9
we get for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\lambda_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}(x,y)=\lambda_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}(x,1)\wedge\lambda_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}(1,y).

Using the equality
\widetilde{\lambda}^{\mu}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{\hat{\mu}}\circ F
we finally deduce for any
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
:

\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\mu}(x,y)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}\big(F_{1}(x),F_{2}(y)\big)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}\big(F_{1}(x),1\big)\wedge\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\hat{\mu}}\big(1,F_{2}(y)\big)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\mu}(x,1)\wedge\widetilde{\lambda}_{r}^{\mu}(1,y).\qed
