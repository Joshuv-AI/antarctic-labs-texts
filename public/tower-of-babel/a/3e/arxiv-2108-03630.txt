Beurling-Lax type theorems and Cuntz relations
Daniel Alpay, Fabrizio Colombo, Irene Sabadini, Baruch Schneider

Abstract
. We prove various Beurling-Lax type theorems, when the classical backward-shift operator is replaced by a general resolvent operator associated with a rational function. We also study connections to the Cuntz relations. An important tool is a new representation result for analytic functions, in terms of composition and multiplication operators associated with a given rational function. Applications to the theory of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces, also in the indefinite metric setting, are given.

Keywords:
de Branges-Rovnyak spaces, Cuntz relations, rational functions, Beurling-Lax theorem, backward-shift operator, structure theorems

1.
Introduction

In this paper we discuss decompositions of spaces associated with positive definite kernels and with kernels having a finite number of negative squares,
based on a new representation theorem for analytic functions. To set the framework and motivate our results, let us
consider a
\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
-valued function
K(z,w)
positive definite on a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
; let
r
be a map from a set
\Omega_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}
into
\Omega
, and let
A(z)
be a

\mathbb{C}^{1\times p}
-valued function defined on
\Omega_{1}
. The function
A(z)K(r(z),r(w))A(w)^{*}
is positive definite on
\Omega_{1}
; elements of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space with reproducing
kernel
A(z)K(r(z),r(w))A(w)^{*}
are of the form

(1.1)

f(z)=A(z)F(r(z)),

where
F
varies in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space with reproducing kernel
K(z,w)
.
Depending on various assumptions, one can insure that the above decomposition is unique, and one can then relate the norms of
f
and
F
. In the present paper we assume that

r
is a rational function and that the entries of
A(z)
span the backward-shift invariant space (also know as state space)
\mathfrak{L}(r)
generated by
r
(see Definition
1.2

for the latter). We focus in particular on de Branges and de Branges-Rovnyak spaces (see
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
), in view of
their important role in operator theory, linear system theory and
related topics; see e.g.
[
2
,
32
]
.
The properties of these spaces are related to properties of the classical backward-shift operators

(1.2)

(R_{\alpha}F)(z)=\begin{cases}\,\,\dfrac{F(z)-F(\alpha)}{z-\alpha},\quad z\not=\alpha,\\
\,\,F^{\prime}(\alpha),\hskip 48.93877ptz=\alpha,\end{cases}

where
F
is assumed analytic in a neighborhood of
\alpha
. Moreover, various structure theorems characterizing de Branges-Rovnyak spaces in terms of the backward-shift operators
can be seen as Beurling-Lax type theorems. For instance, given a signature matrix
J\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times s}
(i.e.
J
satisfies
J=J^{*}=J^{-1}
) and given a reproducing
kernel Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
of

\mathbb{C}^{s}
-valued functions analytic in an open subset of the complex plane, symmetric with respect to the real line, the equality

(1.3)

\left[R_{\alpha}F,G\right]-\left[F,R_{\beta}G\right]-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R_{\alpha}F,R_{\beta}G\right]=iG(\beta)^{*}JF(\alpha)

holds for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega
and all
F,G\in\mathfrak{H}
if and only if the reproducing kernel of
\mathfrak{H}
is of the form

(1.4)

K(z,w)=\frac{J-\Theta(z)J\Theta(w)^{*}}{-i(z-\overline{w})},

see
[
21
,
24
,
46
]
. The links with operator models stem from the following: When the operators
R_{\alpha}
are bounded and with zero kernel, a result of Stone
(see
[
47
, Theorem 4.10, p. 137]
) asserts that there is a closed linear operator
\mathsf{A}
such that

(1.5)

((\mathsf{A}-\alpha I)^{-1}f)(z)=zf(z)+c_{f},\quad\alpha\in\Omega,

where
c_{f}\in\mathbb{C}
. Properties of
\mathsf{A}
and of the space
\mathfrak{H}
are closely related.

de Branges-Rovnyak spaces come in all shapes and sizes, depending on the application in mind, and the domain of analyticity where they are defined. We here focus on three types of spaces,
usually denoted by the symbols
\mathfrak{P}(\Theta)
,
\mathfrak{P}(E_{+},E_{-})
and
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{N})
, associated respectively with closed to self-adjoint
(or close to unitary), Hermitian (or isometric) and
self-adjoint (or unitary) operators.

Although different in aim and approach, the present paper has connections with the works of Kailah and Lev-Ari
[
42
]
, Nudelman
[
44
]
, and the approach developed in the papers

[
6
,
13
,
14
]
. There, one is given two functions analytic in a connected open set
\Omega
, such that the sets

\begin{split}\Omega_{+}&=\left\{z\in\Omega\,;\,|b(z)|<|a(z)|\right\}\\
\Omega_{-}&=\left\{z\in\Omega\,;\,|b(z)|>|a(z)|\right\}\end{split}

are not empty. Then the set

\Omega_{0}=\left\{z\in\Omega\,;\,|b(z)|=|a(z)|\right\}

is also not empty (see e.g.
[
4
, Exercise 6.1.9, p. 295]
), and the function

k(z,w)=\frac{1}{a(z)\overline{a(w)}-b(z)\overline{b(w)}}

is positive definite in
\Omega_{+}
. The reproducing kernels studied are then of the form

(1.6)

\frac{J-\Theta(z)J\Theta(w)^{*}}{a(z)\overline{a(w)}-b(z)\overline{b(w)}}

when
z,w
vary in some open subset of
\Omega_{+}
, where
J
is a signature matrix. When such a kernel has a finite number of negative squares, the function
\Theta(z)
in (
1.6
) can be
written as
M(\sigma(z))
, where
\sigma(z)=\frac{b(z)}{a(z)}
, and where
M(z)
is analytic in some open subset of the open unit disk.
Our rational function
r
(or
1-r/1+r
) plays the role of
\sigma
.

de Branges-Rovnyak spaces have been furthermore generalized to numerous settings, ranging from the polydisk
(and the theory of Schur-Agler classes) to non-commutative settings and real compact Riemann surfaces setting, and here we take a different avenue and
study the counterparts of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces when the classical resolvent operator (see (
1.2
) below) is replaced by a general resolvent operator associated with a
rational function
r
. Motivated by the Riemann surface case (see
[
19
, §4]
), a generalization of (
1.2
)
has been introduced in
[
17
]
. More precisely (in
[
17
]
only the polynomial case was considered) we will assume, unless otherwise stated, that
r
satisfies:

Hypothesis 1.1
.

We will assume that the rational function
r(z)=\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}
, where
p
and
q
are coprime polynomials satisfying
{\rm deg}\,p\,\geq\,{\rm deg}\,q
.
When this hypothesis is not in force we can replace
r
by
1/r
.

We set
N={\rm deg}\,p
, and define

(1.7)

\Omega(r)=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\,\mbox{\text{\,such that the equation $r(z)=\alpha$ has $N$ pairwise different roots}}\right\}.

We do not write explicitly the dependence of the roots, say
w_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,w_{N}(\alpha)
, on
r
and
\alpha
, but when it makes the reading easier (such as in the statement and proof of
Lemma
3.1
). For
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
we set

(1.8)

(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f)(z)=\begin{cases}\,\,\dfrac{f(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{f(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})},\quad r(z)\not=\alpha,\\
\\
\,\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}\dfrac{f^{\prime}(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})},\hskip 110.96556ptz\in r^{-1}\left\{\alpha\right\}=\left\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}\right\},\end{cases}

where
f
is assumed analytic in a neighborhood of the set
r^{-1}\left\{\alpha\right\}
(the use of a lower case
f
for (
1.8
) and of an upper case
F
for (
1.2
) is on purpose, and is motivated by expressions such as (
1.1
)).
We note that the first formula in (
1.8
) can be rewritten as (for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
such that
r(\infty)\not=\alpha
and
z\not\in r^{-1}\left\{\alpha\right\}
):

(1.9)

\begin{split}(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f)(z)&=\frac{f(z)}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}\dfrac{f(z)-f(w_{n})}{z-w_{n}}\\
&=\frac{f(z)}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}(R_{w_{n}}f)(z).\end{split}

The operators (
1.8
) satisfy the resolvent identity

(1.10)

R_{\alpha}^{(r)}-R_{\beta}^{(r)}=(\alpha-\beta)R_{\alpha}^{(r)}R_{\beta}^{(r)},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\Omega(r),

see
[
17
]
. Another slightly different proof can be obtained using (
1.9
) since the classical resolvent operators satisfy the resolvent identity; see Corollary
2.2

below. When these operators have a trivial kernel, the underlying operator models are now of the form (
7.5
) below, that is

(Tf)(z)=r(z)f(z)+h_{f}(z)

where
h_{f}(z)
is a rational function of
z
, linear in
f
.

In the present paper we study the counterpart of the de Branges and de Branges -Rovnyak spaces when the classical backward-shift operators are replaced by the operators (
1.8
). For instance
(
1.3
) will now be replaced by

(1.11)

\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,g\right]-\left[f,R_{\beta}^{(r)}g\right]-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,R^{(r)}_{\beta}g\right]=i(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{g(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\frac{f(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{1}{u_{i}-\overline{v_{j}}}

where the
u_{i}
(resp. the
v_{j}
) are the pre-images of
\alpha
(resp. of
\beta
) under
r
. We note that the right hand side of (
1.11
) is adapted from the Riemann surface case;
see
[
19
, Theorem 5.1, p. 316]
.

To describe our results we need some more notation and definitions.

Definition 1.2
.

The linear span of the functions
z\mapsto\frac{r(z)-r(a)}{z-a}
where
a
runs
through the points of analyticity of
r
, will be called the state space associated with
r
and will be denoted by
\mathfrak{L}(r)
.

The space
\mathfrak{L}(r)
is finite dimensional, as we recall in Lemma
2.6
, and we denote by
Z_{r}(z)
a
1\times N
matrix with entries a basis of
\mathfrak{L}(r)
:

(1.12)

Z_{r}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}(z)&\cdots&e_{N}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

Example 1.3
.

For
r(z)=z+\frac{1}{z}
, the space
\mathfrak{L}(r)
is spanned by the functions
1
and
1/z
since

\frac{r(z)-r(a)}{z-a}=1-\frac{1}{za}

for this
r
.

Remark 1.4
.

When
r(z)=z
, the corresponding
Z_{r}(z)
can be taken to be
1
, and the operator reduces to the classical backward shift operators (
1.2
).

We will be interested in functions of the form

(1.13)

f(z)=(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{s})F(r(z)),

where
F
is analytic in a neighborhood of the origin, and a characterization of these functions is given in Theorem
4.1
.

Condition (
1.11
) is now equivalent (see Theorem
4.13
for a precise statement) to the reproducing kernel of the space to be of the form

(1.14)

(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{s})Y^{-1}\frac{J_{0}-\Theta(r(z))J_{0}\Theta(r(w))^{*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}Y^{-*}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{s})^{*},

where
\Theta
is a
\mathbb{C}^{rs\times rs}
-valued function analytic in a neighborhood of the origin,
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{rs\times rs}
is invertible,
J_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{rs\times rs}
is a signature matrix, and the latter two depend only on
Z_{r}
and
J
.

Another question which we are considering here is related to the Cuntz relations. With the above notation, we look for pairs of positive definite kernels
(K,K_{0})
such that, with

K_{0}(r)(z,w)=K_{0}(r(z),r(w))
, we have the orthogonal decomposition (the functions
e_{j}
have been defined by (
1.12
))

(1.15)

\mathfrak{H}(K)=\oplus e_{j}\mathfrak{H}(K_{0}(r)).

The weighted multiplication operators
T_{j}:\,\,\mathfrak{H}(K_{0})\longrightarrow\mathfrak{H}(K)
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, defined by

(1.16)

(T_{j}g)(z)=e_{j}(z)g(r(z)),\quad j=1,\ldots,N

satisfy then the Cuntz relations.

An important tool in our results is a representation theorem for analytic functions, characterizing functions of the form
Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))
(the case where

r(z)
is a polynomial has been considered in
[
17
]
).

The paper contains ten sections besides the introduction. Sections 2-7 form the first part of the paper. After the preliminary Section 2, in which we discuss some aspects of realizations of rational functions, the main general results are presented in Sections 3-7. Specifically, in Section 3 we study, given a rational function, an associated symmetric matrix also proving a technical result which may be of independent interest. Section 4 contains a main result, namely a representation theorem, see Theorem 4.1, which generalizes the polynomial case and some particular cases of rational functions already studied in the literature. Section 5 discusses how our results are connected with Cuntz relations. Section 6 studies subspaces invariant under the action of
suitable generalized backward shift operators, while Section 7 moves to related operator models.
Sections 8-11 are devoted to the counterpart of de Branges-Rovnyak spaces in the present setting. We study the Pontryagin spaces with assigned reproducing kernels in the line and in the circle case, we present two structure theorems, and we consider also the case of negative squares and generalized Nevanlinna functions.

2.
Preliminary results

In this section, which is of a preliminary nature, we discuss various aspects of realization theory of rational functions.
We mention in particular Corollary
2.13
, which may be of independent interest, and Corollary
2.15
,
which allows us to make the connection with the classical case where
r(z)=z
.
The first result follows from a partial fraction expansion; we write out the details since (
2.1
) plays a central role in the sequel. We note that it may be that

r(\infty)\in{\rm ran}\,r
, for example
r(z)=1+\dfrac{z-1}{z^{2}+1}
. We have
r(1)=r(\infty)=1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
r
be a rational function of degree
N
and let
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
. We have:

(2.1)

\frac{1}{r(z)-\alpha}=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})},

which reduces to

(2.2)

\frac{1}{r(z)-\alpha}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})},

when
r(\infty)=\infty
.

Proof.

The zeros
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
cannot be zeros of
q
, even if we interpret
r(w_{n})=\alpha
as
p(w_{n})=\alpha q(w_{n})
. If
q(w_{n})=0
then
p(w_{n})=0
, but
p
and
q
are assumed prime
together. From
\alpha=\frac{p(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})}
we get

(2.3)

\begin{split}\frac{p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\alpha q^{\prime}(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})}&=\frac{p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\dfrac{p(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})}q^{\prime}(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})}\\
&=\frac{p^{\prime}(w_{n})q(w_{n})-p(w_{n})q^{\prime}(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})^{2}}\\
&=r^{\prime}(w_{n}).\end{split}

Partial fraction expansion gives

\begin{split}\frac{1}{r(z)-\alpha}&=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\frac{q(z)}{p(z)-q(z)\alpha}\\
&=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{q(w_{n})}{p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\alpha q^{\prime}(w_{n})}\frac{1}{z-w_{n}}\\
&=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{q(w_{n})}{p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\frac{p(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})}q^{\prime}(w_{n})}\frac{1}{z-w_{n}}\\
&=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})},\end{split}

where we have used (
2.3
).
∎

We note that if
r(\infty)=0
then
0\not\in\Omega(r)
.

From the previous result we deduce:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
r
be a rational function of degree
N
and
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega(r)
. Then (
1.10
) follows from (
1.9
).

Proof.

Let
w_{1},\ldots,,w_{N}
be the roots (which are pairwise different since
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
) of the equation
r(z)=\alpha
, and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
be the roots (pairwise different since

\beta\in\Omega(r)
) of
r(v_{m})=\beta
. With

c=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\alpha},\quad d=\frac{1}{r(\infty)-\beta},\quad c_{n}=\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})},\quad d_{n}=\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(v_{n})},\,\,n=1,\ldots,N,

we can write (since the operator (
1.2
) satisfy the resolvent identity, and using (
2.1
)):

\begin{split}R^{(r)}_{\alpha}R^{(r)}_{\beta}&=\left(c+\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}R_{w_{n}}\right)\left(d+\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{m}R_{v_{m}}\right)\\
&=cR_{\beta}^{(r)}+d\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}dR_{w_{n}}+\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}c_{n}d_{m}R_{w_{n}}R_{v_{m}}\\
&=cR_{\beta}^{(r)}+d(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}-c)+\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}c_{n}d_{m}\frac{R_{w_{n}}-R_{v_{m}}}{w_{n}-v_{m}}\\
&=cR_{\beta}^{(r)}+d(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}-c)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}R_{w_{n}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{m}\frac{1}{w_{n}-v_{m}}\right)-\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{m}R_{v_{m}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}\frac{1}{w_{n}-v_{m}}\right)\\
&=cR_{\beta}^{(r)}+d(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}-c)+(R_{\alpha}^{(r)}-c)\left(\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}-d\right)-\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-(R^{(r)}_{\beta}-d)\left(-\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}+c\right)\\
&=\frac{R_{\alpha}^{(r)}-R_{\beta}^{(r)}}{\alpha-\beta}+cd+\frac{d-c}{\alpha-\beta}\\
&=\frac{R_{\alpha}^{(r)}-R_{\beta}^{(r)}}{\alpha-\beta}\end{split}

since, by direct computation, we have
cd+\dfrac{d-c}{\alpha-\beta}=0.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

The linear span

runs through
\mathcal{U}(r)
, is finite dimensional, of dimension
N
, where
N={\rm deg}\,r
.
Assume
r
analytic at the origin, and let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{L}(r)
.
There exists an observable pair
(G,T)\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}\times\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
such that

(2.16)

Z_{r}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def.}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}(z)&\cdots&e_{N}(z)\end{pmatrix}=G(I_{N}-zT)^{-1}

Furthermore, if
r
is real, meaning
r(z)=\overline{r(\overline{z})}
, the matrices
G
and
T
may be chosen to have real components.

Proof.

The claim is invariant under a Moebius transform, and we will assume that
0
is not a pole of
r
. We can write
r
in a realized form as

(2.17)

r(z)=d+zG(I_{N}-zT)^{-1}b,

and thus

(2.18)

\frac{r(z)-r(w)}{z-w}=G(I_{N}-zT)^{-1}(I-wT)^{-1}b,

from which the result follows.
For the last claim see
[
5
]
. A more direct way is to write real realizations of
p
and
q
and get a realization for
p/q
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

We note that two different choices of basis will amount to two different
Z_{r}
differing by an invertible multiplicative matrix on the left, corresponding to the change of basis.
In the paper we always stick to a pre-assigned choice, unless stated otherwise.

When

and

0&&&\ddots&&\\

0&0&&\cdots&0&1\\

Example 2.9
.

When

is a finite Blaschke product of the open unit disk, with pairwise different zeros, we can write

We have, as sets,

and

.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
r(z)=\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}
be a rational function of degree
N\geq 1
.
Elements of
\mathfrak{L}(r)
are exactly rational functions of the form
\frac{m(z)}{q(z)}
, where
m
is a polynomial of degree at most
N-1
. In particular,
if
m(z)\not\equiv 0
, then it cannot have more than
N-1
zeros.

Proof.

This follows from

\begin{split}\frac{r(z)-r(a)}{z-a}&=\frac{\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(a)-q(z)p(a)}{z-a}\right)}{q(z)q(a)}.\end{split}

∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
r(z)
be a rational function of degree
N
, with numerator of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{M}(z-w_{i})^{m_{i}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{i}=N
, and
let
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
be such that

(2.19)

Z_{r}^{(j)}(w_{i})\xi=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,M,\,\,j=0,\ldots,m_{i}-1.

Then
\xi=0
.

Proof.

Indeed, the function
z\mapsto Z_{r}(z)\xi
belongs to
\mathfrak{L}(r)
. Condition (
2.19
) expresses that it has zeros of total multiplicity

\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{i}=N
, and so by the previous result we have
Z_{r}(z)\xi\equiv 0
, and so
\xi=0
since the entries of
Z_{r}(z)
are linearly independent.
∎

From this corollary we obtain:

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
r(z)
be a rational function of degree
N
, with numerator of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{M}(z-w_{i})^{m_{i}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{M}m_{i}=N
. Assume that

(2.20)

Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))\equiv 0.

Then,
F\equiv 0
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle(Z_{r}(z)F(r(z)))^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Z_{r}(z))^{(1)}F(r(z))+Z_{r}(z)r^{(1)}(z)F^{(1)}(r(z))

\displaystyle(Z_{r}(z)F(r(z)))^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Z_{r}(z))^{(2)}F(r(z))+2(Z_{r}(z))^{(1)}r^{(1)}(z)F^{(1)}(r(z))+

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84526pt+Z_{r}(z)r^{(2)}(z)F^{(1)}(r(z))+Z_{r}(z)(r^{(1)}(z))^{2}F^{(2)}(r(z)),

and similarly for derivatives of higher order. Using that
r^{(j)}(w_{i})=0
for
i=1,\ldots,M,\,\,j=0,\ldots,m_{i}-1
, we get

(2.21)

Z_{r}^{(j)}(w_{i})F(0)=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,M,\,\,j=0,\ldots,m_{i}-1,

so that
F(0)=0
in view of Corollary
2.11
. We write
F(z)=zF_{1}(z)
. Equation (
2.20
) becomes

Z_{r}(z)F_{1}(r(z))\equiv 0.

Reiterating the previous argument on
Z_{r}(z)F_{1}(r(z))
we have that
F^{(1)}(0)=F_{1}(0)=0.
Iterating we get that the coefficients of
the Taylor series expansion of
F
at the origin all vanish, and hence
F\equiv 0
.
∎

The results in the next corollary play a key role in the sequel; in particular see the proof of Theorem
5.1
in Section
5
on the Cuntz relations. Note that the left hand side of (
2.23
) below is of the form (
1.13
).

Corollary 2.13
.

In the previous notation, let
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
. Then, the matrix
\mathsf{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
defined by

(2.22)

g_{nj}=(e_{n}(w_{j}))_{n,j=1}^{N}

is invertible.
Let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
be analytic in a connected neighborhood
\Omega
of
\alpha
. Then

(2.23)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)F_{n}(r(z))\equiv 0,\quad z\in r^{-1}(\Omega),

if and only if
F_{n}(z)\equiv 0
for
z\in\Omega
and
n=1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

Let
c=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{N}\end{pmatrix}^{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, and assume that
\mathsf{G}c=0
. Let
g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}e_{n}(z)
. The function
g\in\mathfrak{L}(r)
, and
the condition
\mathsf{G}c=0
implies that
g(w_{i})=0
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. By the previous lemma
g(z)\equiv 0
and hence
c=0
since
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N}
are linearly independent.

Assume now that (
2.23
) holds. Setting
z=w_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
we have that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(w_{i})F_{n}(\alpha)=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,N

and so
F_{1}(\alpha)=\cdots=F_{N}(\alpha)
in view of the first part of the corollary. For
n=1,\ldots,N
write now
F_{n}(z)=(z-\alpha)G_{n}(z)
, where
G_{n}
is analytic in
\Omega
.
We have

\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)r(z)G_{n}(r(z))\equiv 0,\quad z\in r^{-1}(\Omega),

and so

\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)G_{n}(r(z))\equiv 0,\quad z\in r^{-1}(\Omega).

Reiterating the previous argument we get
G_{1}(\alpha)=\cdots=G_{N}(\alpha)=0
. We then get that the Taylor series around
\alpha
for
F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
vanish identically, and hence the functions

F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
vanish identically in
\Omega
since the latter is connected.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
r
be a rational function of degree
N
and let
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
. Then, for
f\in\mathfrak{L}(r)
, we have

(2.24)

\frac{f(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})}

and, in particular,

(2.25)

\frac{Z_{r}(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove (
2.24
) for
f(z)=\dfrac{r(z)-r(\beta)}{z-\beta}
since
\mathfrak{L}(r)
is finite dimensional and spanned by such functions.
Since
\dfrac{q(\beta)p(z)-p(\beta)q(z)}{z-\beta}
is a polynomial of degree strictly less that
N
, we then have the partial fraction expansion:

\begin{split}\frac{f(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}&=\frac{\left(\dfrac{q(\beta)p(z)-p(\beta)q(z)}{z-\beta}\right)}{q(\beta)(p(z)-\alpha q(z))}\\
\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\left(\dfrac{q(\beta)p(w_{n})-p(\beta)q(w_{n})}{w_{n}-\beta}\right)}{q(\beta)(p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\alpha q^{\prime}(w_{n}))}\\
\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\left(\dfrac{q(\beta)p(w_{n})-p(\beta)q(w_{n})}{q(w_{n})(w_{n}-\beta)}\right)}{\dfrac{q(\beta)(p^{\prime}(w_{n})-\alpha q^{\prime}(w_{n}))}{q(w_{n})}}\\
\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})},\\
&\end{split}

where we have used another time (
2.3
).
∎

The following corollary shows that the operators
R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
are well suited for functions of the form (
1.13
)

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
r
be a rational function of degree
N
.
Let
w_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,w_{N}(\alpha)
the roots, assumed simple, of the equation
r(z)=\alpha
, and let
f
be analytic in a neighborhood of
\left\{w_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,w_{N}(\alpha)\right\}
and of the form

f(z)=Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))
, where the function
F
is
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued and
analytic in a neighborhood of the point
\alpha
. Then,

(2.26)

(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f)(z)=Z_{r}(z)(R_{\alpha}F)(r(z)).

Proof.

We can write

\begin{split}(R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f)(z)&=\frac{Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))}{r(z)-\alpha}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(w_{n})F(r(w_{n}))}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})}\\
&=\frac{Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))}{r(z)-\alpha}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(w_{n})F(\alpha)}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})}\\
&=\frac{Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))}{r(z)-\alpha}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(z-w_{n})}F(\alpha)\\
&=\frac{Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))}{r(z)-\alpha}-\frac{Z_{r}(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}F(\alpha)\\
&=Z_{r}(z)(R_{\alpha}F)(r(z)),\end{split}

where we have used (
2.25
) to conclude.
∎

3.
The associated symmetric matrix

To a given rational function we can associate a symmetric matrix which plays an important role in the sequel.
The following lemma seems to be new and possibly of independent interest.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
r
be a rational function of degree
N
,
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
and let
w_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,w_{N}(\alpha)
be the (simple)
roots of
r(z)=\alpha
. Let
f,g\in\mathfrak{L}(r)
, then the sum

(3.1)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n}(\alpha))g(w_{n}(\alpha))}{r^{\prime}(w_{n}(\alpha))}

does not depend on
\alpha
.

Proof.

It is enough to consider
f
and
g
respectively equal to

f(z)=\frac{r(z)-r(a)}{z-a}\quad{\rm and}\quad g(z)=\frac{r(z)-r(b)}{z-b},

where
a,b
are points of analyticity of
r
. We note that the poles of
r
are removable singularities of

\frac{f(z)g(z)}{(r(z)-\alpha)r(z)}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(a)-q(z)p(a)}{z-a}\right)\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(b)-q(z)p(b)}{z-b}\right)}{(p(z)-\alpha q(z))p(z)q(a)q(b)},

and so, for
R>0
large enough (depending on
\alpha
), such that all the poles of the integrand are inside
|z|\leq R
,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{(r(z)-\alpha)r(z)}dz=0

since the difference between the degree of the denominator and of the numerator is at least
2
.
So, for such
R
:

(3.2)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}dz=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}dz.

Furthermore

(3.3)

\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(a)-q(z)p(a)}{z-a}\right)\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(b)-q(z)p(b)}{z-b}\right)}{(p(z)-\alpha q(z))q(z)q(a)q(b)}

and in particular, for
\alpha=0
,

\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(a)-q(z)p(a)}{z-a}\right)\left(\dfrac{p(z)q(b)-q(z)p(b)}{z-b}\right)}{p(z)q(z)q(a)q(b)}.

It follows from these two equalities that to evaluate both sides of (
3.2
) we need to compute the residues of the corresponding functions at the points

w_{n}(\alpha),w_{n}(=w_{n}(0))
and at the zeros of
q
.
Since
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
, we have

{\rm Res\,}\left(\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha},w_{n}(\alpha)\right)=\frac{f(w_{n}(\alpha))g(w_{n}(\alpha))}{r^{\prime}(w_{n}(\alpha))},\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

The key to the proof of the lemma is that the residues at the zeros of
q
do not depend on
\alpha
, as we now show.
Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{U}
be the zeros of
q
, and assume
z_{u}
be a zero of order
m_{u}
:
q(z)=(z-z_{u})^{m_{u}}\pi_{u}(z)
, where
\pi_{u}(z)
is a polynomial not vanishing at

z_{u}
.
We rewrite (
3.3
) as

(3.4)

\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}=\frac{G(z)}{(p(z)-\alpha q(z))(z-z_{u})^{m_{u}}}

where
G
is independent of
\alpha
and is analytic at the point
z_{u}
and
G(z_{u})\not=0
. From the formula

{\rm Res\,}\left(\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha},z_{u}\right)=\frac{1}{(m_{u}-1)!}\left(\frac{G(z)}{(p(z)-\alpha q(z))}\right)^{(m_{u}-1)}(z_{u})

we see that the residue involves only derivatives of
\alpha q
of order less or equal to
m_{u}-1
, evaluated at the point
z_{u}
, and hence does not depend on
\alpha

since

q(z_{u})=\cdots=q^{(m_{u}-1)}(z_{u})=0.

Thus, we have for large
R

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}dz=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n}(\alpha))g(w_{n}(\alpha))}{r^{\prime}(w_{n}(\alpha))}+M_{1}

where
M_{1}
denotes the sum of the residues of the function
\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)-\alpha}
evaluated at the zeros of
q
, and

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}dz=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}dz+M_{1}.

It follows that the sum

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n})g(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}dz

is independent of
\alpha
.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

When the zeros of
q
are simple, we have

M_{1}=\sum_{u}\frac{\left(\dfrac{p(z_{u})}{(z_{u}-a)(z_{u}-b)}\right)}{q^{\prime}(z_{u})},

and when
0\in\Omega(r)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{f(z)g(z)}{r(z)}dz=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n})g(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
r
be a
real
rational function of degree
N
,
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
and let us assume that the roots
w_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,w_{N}(\alpha)
of
r(z)=\alpha
are simple.
Let
J
be a
p\times p
signature matrix with real entries. The sum

(3.5)

X(J,r)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(Z_{r}(w_{n})\otimes I_{p})^{t}J(Z_{r}(w_{n})\otimes I_{p})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}\in\mathbb{R}^{Np\times Np}

is self-adjoint and real (i.e. symmetric), invertible, and independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

In the proof we use the fact that, for any (possibly non square) matrices
A
and
B
of possibly different sizes we have (see
[
37
]
for a discussion and more information
on the properties of the tensor, or Kronecker, product):

\begin{split}(A\otimes B)^{t}&=A^{t}\otimes B^{t}\\
\overline{A\otimes B}&=\overline{A}\otimes\overline{B},\end{split}

and that, in the case of invertible matrices,

(3.6)

(A\otimes B)^{-1}=A^{-1}\otimes B^{-1}.

Using (
1.12
) we deduce

\begin{split}(Z_{r}(w_{n})\otimes I_{p})^{t}J(Z_{r}(w_{n})\otimes I_{p})&=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}(w_{n})I_{p}\\
\vdots\\
e_{N}(w_{n})I_{p}\end{pmatrix}J\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}(w_{n})I_{p}&\cdots&e_{N}(w_{n})I_{p}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\left(e_{i}(w_{n})e_{j}(w_{n})J\right)_{i,j=1}^{N}\\
&=X(1,r)\otimes J,\end{split}

and the fact that
X(1,r)
does not depend on
\alpha
follows from the preceding lemma.
To prove that
X(J,r)
is invertible it is enough to show that
X(1,r)
is invertible. Using (
3.6
) we then have

X(J,r)^{-1}=X(1,r)^{-1}\otimes J

since
J=J^{-1}
. To prove that
X(1,r)
is invertible we use the following readily checked fact: a
N\times N
matrix of the form
\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}^{t}b_{n}
, where the
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
is invertible if and only if both
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
are linearly independent vectors. In the case of
X(1,r)
, it is therefore enough to check that the
vectors
Z(w_{1}),\ldots,Z(w_{N})
are linearly independent. Assume not. There will then be a non-zero vector

c=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{N}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}

such that

Z(w_{n})c=0,\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

Thus the rational function
Z(z)c
has (at least)
N
pairwise different zeros. Since
Zc\in\mathfrak{L}(r)
, this cannot be in view of Lemma
2.10
, unless
Z(z)c\equiv 0
. But this
implies
c=0
since the entries of
Z(z)
form a basis of
\mathfrak{L}(r)
.

We now show that
X(J,r)
is self-adjoint. Since

(X(1,r)\otimes J)^{t}=X(1,r)^{t}\otimes J^{t}=X(1,r)\otimes J,

it is enough to show that the entries of
X(1,r)
are real.
This follows from the fact that either the
w_{n}
are real or they
appear in pairs. In the first case the matrix
\frac{Z(w_{n})^{t}JZ(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}
has real entries while in the second case the matrix

\frac{Z(w_{n})^{t}JZ(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}+\frac{Z(\overline{w_{n}})^{t}JZ(\overline{w_{n}})}{r^{\prime}(\overline{w_{n}})}

has real entries since
Z
is real and
r^{\prime}(\overline{w_{n}})=\overline{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}
.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

X(J,r)
is called the associated symmetric matrix to
r
and
J
.
We will write
X(J,r)=X(r)
or
X
when
J
or
r
are clear from the context.

Note that
X(1,r)
can be written in term of a realization as:

(3.7)

X(1,r)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(I-w_{n}T)^{-t}G^{t}G(I-w_{n}T)^{-1}}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}.

Corollary 3.5
.

For a Blaschke product with pairwise different zeros we have

(3.8)

X(1,r)=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(a_{n})(1-a_{n}\overline{a_{u}})(1-a_{n}\overline{a_{v}})}\right)_{u,v=1}^{N}.

Proof.

We have
G=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}
and
T
is the diagonal matrix
{\rm diag}\,(\overline{a_{1}},\overline{a_{2}},\ldots,\overline{a_{N}})
.
and so

(X(1,r))_{uv}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})(1-w_{n}\overline{a_{u}})(1-w_{n}\overline{a_{v}})}.

Taking
\alpha=0
we get
w_{n}=a_{n}
for
n=1,\ldots,N
, from which (
3.8
) follows by using Corollary
3.3
.
∎

In case of a polynomial
r(z)=p(z)
of degree
N
and
Z_{p}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&z&\cdots&z^{N-1}\end{pmatrix}
the above result takes the following special form:

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
r(z)=p(z)=p_{0}+\cdots+p_{N}z^{N}
, with pairwise distinct zeros
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
. Then, the matrix
X(1,p)=X
is equal to

(3.9)

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&0&0&h_{N-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&0&h_{N-1}&h_{N}\\
0&\cdots&0&\iddots&&\vdots\\
0&\iddots&h_{N-1}&&&\vdots\\
0&h_{N-1}&\iddots&&&\vdots\\
h_{N-1}&h_{N}&\cdots&\cdots&&h_{2N-2}\end{pmatrix},

where

(3.10)

h_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{z^{n}dz}{p(z)},

with
R>\max_{n}|w_{n}|
.

Proof.

We can take
Z_{p}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&z&\cdots&z^{N-1}\end{pmatrix}
, and so

\begin{split}X&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
w_{n}\\
\vdots\\
w_{n}^{N-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&w_{n}&\cdots&w_{n}^{N-1}\end{pmatrix}}{p^{\prime}(w_{n})}\\
&\\
&=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{w_{n}^{k+\ell}}{p^{\prime}(w_{n})}\right)_{k,\ell=0}^{N-1},\end{split}

which is a Hankel matrix. To prove (
3.9
) we now show that
X
is lower with respect to the main off-diagonal.
Let
R_{0}
be such that the roots
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
(assumed pairwise different) of
p(z)
lie inside
B(0,R_{0})

\begin{split}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{w_{n}^{t}}{p^{\prime}(w_{n})}&=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\frac{z^{t}}{p(z)-\alpha}dz\\

which will go to

Example 3.7
.

Let

\begin{split}X&=\frac{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{+}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{\alpha_{+}}\end{pmatrix}}{1-\frac{1}{\alpha_{+}^{2}}}+\frac{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{1}{\alpha_{-}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{\alpha_{-}}\end{pmatrix}}{1-\frac{1}{\alpha_{-}^{2}}}\\

4.
A representation theorem for analytic function

Corollary
2.13
leads to the following natural question:
Which functions can be written in the form
Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))
?
When
r
is a polynomial the answer was given in

[
17
, Theorem 2.1, p.44]
and in
[
16
]
when
r
is a finite Blaschke product.
We present a result which encompasses both cases. The result is valid in particular for rational
functions
r
with possibly less than
N
pairwise different zeros, but having limit infinity at infinity, and also for Blaschke products, corresponding to
results in
[
17
]
and
[
16
]
respectively. These cases are considered after the proof of the lemma. Note that condition (
4.2
) will not hold for functions
r

such that
\lim_{z\rightarrow\infty}r(z)=0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
r
be a rational function with non constant numerator, and let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{L}
be pairwise disjoint open disks around the distinct zeros
w_{1},\ldots,w_{L}
of
r
, not containing poles of
r
,
and such that

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\partial D_{\ell}
does not contain poles of
r
either. We let

(4.1)

\rho=\min_{s\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\partial D_{\ell}}|r(s)|.

We assume

(4.2)

\Omega_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def.}}{{=}}\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|r(z)|<\rho\right\}\subset\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}D_{\ell}.

Then, every function
f
analytic in a neighborhood of
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\overline{D_{\ell}}
can be written in unique way as

(4.3)

f(z)=Z_{r}(z)F(r(z)),\quad z\in\Omega_{0},

where
F
is
\mathbb{C}^{N}
-valued and analytic in
|z|<\rho
.

Proof.

We proceed in a number of steps.

STEP 1:
Let
z\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}D_{\ell}
. It holds that

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\int_{\partial D_{\ell}}\frac{f(s)}{s-z}ds.

This is Cauchy’s formula because of the analyticity assumption on
f
.

STEP 2:
The function

(4.4)

F(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\int_{\partial D_{\ell}}\frac{(I_{N}-sT)^{-1}bf(s)}{r(s)-z}ds

satisfies (
4.3
) and is analytic in
|z|<\rho
.

Indeed, using (
2.18
) and (
2.16
), we can write

\begin{split}f(z)&=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\int_{\partial D_{\ell}}\frac{f(s)}{r(s)-r(z)}\frac{r(s)-r(z)}{s-z}ds\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi i}G(I_{N}-zT)^{-1}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\int_{\partial D_{\ell}}\frac{(I_{N}-sT)^{-1}bf(s)}{r(s)-r(z)}ds\right)\\
&=G(I_{N}-zT)^{-1}F(r(z))\end{split}

with
F(z)
as in (
4.4
). We now check that
F
is analytic in
|z|<\rho
.
This follows from the fact that on
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\partial D_{\ell}
we have
|r(z)|\geq\rho
by (
4.2
), and that
(I_{N}-sT)^{-1}
is well defined on

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\partial D_{\ell}
since the realization of
r
is minimal; see Proposition
2.4
.

STEP 3:
The representation (
4.3
) is unique.

This follows from Corollary
2.13
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

F
is in fact analytic in the set

(4.5)

A(r)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,;\,r(z)\not\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}r(\partial D_{\ell})\right\}

and in particular at infinity. This fact will be used in particular in the sequel in the proofs of various structure theorems.

Remark 4.3
.

The space
A(r)
need not be connected, and
depends on the choice of the disks,
D_{1},\ldots,D_{\ell}
and so is not unique. In the various structure theorems in the sequel, we fix each time a set
A(r)
. The function

f
is then extended analytically in the (possibly not-connected) set
r^{-1}(A(r))
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Condition (
4.2
) holds for rational functions regular at the origin and having a pole at infinity.

Proof.

We consider open disks
D_{L+1},\ldots,D_{M}
around the poles of
r
(if any), all the disks

D_{1},\ldots,D_{M}
are assumed pairwise non intersecting. As above, we set

(4.6)

\rho=\min_{s\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}\partial D_{\ell}}|r(s)|.

STEP 1:
We can choose
D_{L+1},\ldots,D_{M}
such that

(4.7)

\rho<\min_{s\in\bigcup_{\ell=L+1}^{M}\overline{D_{\ell}}}|r(s)|

and in particular

(4.8)

\rho<\min_{s\in\bigcup_{\ell=L+1}^{M}\partial D_{\ell}}|r(s)|.

Indeed, write
r(z)=\frac{h_{L+1}(z)}{(z-w_{L+1})^{m_{L+1}}}
, where
m_{L+1}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
h_{L+1}(z)
regular at
w_{L+1}
with
h(w_{L+1})\not=0
. We can write

h_{L+1}(z)=h(w_{L+1})(1+t_{L+1}(z)),\quad{\rm with}\quad\lim_{z\rightarrow w_{L+1}}t_{L+1}(z)=0.

Let
\epsilon_{0}
be such that

|z-w_{L+1}|<\epsilon_{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|t_{L+1}(z)|<1/2.

Then, for
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
we have

s\in\partial B(w_{L+1},\epsilon)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|r(s)|\geq\frac{1-|t_{L+1}(s)|}{|s-w_{L+1}|^{m_{L+1}}}\geq\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{m_{L+1}}}\rightarrow\infty

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, an so
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\min_{|s-w_{L+1}|=\epsilon}|r(s)|=\infty
.

(
4.8
) follows by going over the above construction around each of the poles, and taking the value of
\epsilon
for which (for instance)

\min_{|s-w_{\ell}|=\epsilon}|r(s)|=2\rho,\quad\ell=L+1,\ldots,M.

We then get (
4.7
) by taking
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

STEP 2:
There exists
R_{0}>0
such that all the closed disks
\overline{D_{1}},\ldots,\overline{D_{M}}
are inside the open disk of radius
R
for
R\geq R_{0}
and

(4.9)

\min\left\{|r(z)|:|z|=R\right\}\geq 2\rho,\quad\forall R\geq R_{0}.

This is clear since
\lim_{z\rightarrow\infty}|r(z)|=\infty
implies
\lim_{z\rightarrow\infty}\min\left\{|r(z)|:|z|=R\right\}=\infty
. To check the latter, write

r(z)=\frac{p_{1}(z)}{p_{2}(z)}
, where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are polynomials, of degrees respectively
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, with
n_{1}>n_{2}
since
r
has a pole at infinity.
We first assume that
p_{2}
is not a constant. Then, with
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
,

r(z)=\frac{c_{1}z^{n_{1}}(1+t_{1}(z))}{c_{2}z^{n_{2}}(1+t_{2}(z))}=\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}z^{n_{1}-n_{2}}\frac{1+t_{1}(z)}{1+t_{2}(z)}.

To conclude take
R_{1}
such that for every
R\geq R_{1}
,

|z|>R\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|t_{1}(z)|<1/2\quad{\rm and}\quad|t_{2}(z)|<1.

For such
R
,

|1+t_{1}(z)|\geq 1-|t_{1}(z)|\geq 1/2\quad{\rm and}\quad|1+t_{2}(z)|\leq 2,

and so

\left|\frac{1+t_{1}(z)}{1+t_{2}(z)}\right|\geq\frac{1-|t_{1}(z)|}{1+|t_{2}(z)|}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Hence, for
|z|\geq R\geq R_{1}
,

|r(z)|\geq\frac{R^{n_{1}-n_{2}}}{4}\rightarrow\infty\quad{\rm as}\quad z\rightarrow\infty.

By taking
R_{1}
large enough, we can therefore find
R_{0}
such that (
4.9
) holds when
p_{2}
is not a constant polynomial. The case where
p_{2}
is a constant works
the same, with considering only the term
1+t_{1}(z)
.

STEP 3:
Let
K=\left\{|z|\leq R\right\}\setminus\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{M}D_{\ell}
. We have
|r(z)|\geq\rho
for
z\in K
.

Indeed, the function
1/r(z)
is analytic in the interior of the compact set
K
and continuous on its boundary,
and so it attains its maximum. By the maximum modulus principle and by the definition of
\rho
, this maximum is equal to
1/\rho
.
Hence, in the interior of
K
we have
1/|r(z)|<1/\rho
, that is
|r(z)|>\rho
.

STEP 4:
We have
|r(z)|\geq\rho
outside the
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}D_{\ell}
, and (
4.2
) holds.

That
|r(z)|\geq\rho
outside the
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}D_{\ell}
follows from the definition of
R_{0}
for
|z|>R
, and from (
4.7
) for
z
inside the closed
disks around the poles.

∎

Example 4.9

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
f

f(z)=F(z+1/z)

We write

f(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{1}{z}\end{pmatrix}F\left(z+\frac{1}{z}\right)

and hence the result.
∎

Considering the power series expansion of
f
centered at the origin, a more direct proof would be to prove first by induction that there exists a sequence of polynomials

z^{n}+\frac{1}{z^{n}}=P_{n}\left(z+\frac{1}{z}\right),\quad n=1,2,\ldots

Example 4.12

Given

, describe the set of functions analytic in a neighborhood of
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
and such that

(4.13)

\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}f(w_{n})=\gamma.

Using the representation (
4.3
) we rewrite (
4.13
) as

\begin{split}\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}f(w_{n})&=\gamma\\
&\iff\\
\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}Z_{r}(w_{n})F(0)&=\gamma\\
&\iff\\
cF(0)&=\gamma,\quad c=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}Z_{r}(w_{n}),\end{split}

which is a one-point tangential interpolation problem with solutions
F\in\mathfrak{H}(k)
(if one can multiply on the left and stay in
\mathfrak{H}(k)
) given by

(4.14)

F(z)=k(z,0)k(0,0)^{-1}\frac{c^{*}}{cc^{*}}\gamma+\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)G(z),\quad G\in\mathfrak{H}(k)

so that

f(z)=Z_{r}(z)\left(k(z,0)k(0,0)^{-1}\frac{c^{*}}{cc^{*}}\gamma+\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)G(z)\right).

Note that the decomposition (
4.14
) is orthogonal. Let
d\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. We have:

\begin{split}\langle\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)G(z),k(z,0)k(0,0)^{-1}\frac{c^{*}}{cc^{*}}d\gamma\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}(k)}&=\overline{\gamma}d^{*}ck(0,0)^{-1}k(0,0)\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)G(0)\\
&=\overline{\gamma}d^{*}c\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)G(0)\\
&=0\end{split}

since
c\left(I-\frac{c^{*}c}{cc^{*}}\right)=0
.

We note that such multipoint constraints are considered in numerical analysis to fix degrees of freedom; see
[
45
, p. 1494]
.

5.

Representations of the Cuntz algebra (see

) play an important role in analysis, wavelets and signal processing, in particular in the theory of filter banks; see
[

,

,
39

.
Connections between the Cuntz relations and reproducing kernel spaces were
considered in
[
15
,
16
]
. In the present section we show how the Cuntz relations
are related to the present setting.

Let in (
4.10
)

F=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}\\
\vdots\\
F_{N}\end{pmatrix}

where
F_{1},\ldots,F_{N}
are
\mathbb{C}^{p}
-valued. We thus have

(5.1)

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)F_{n}(r(z)).

Theorem 5.1
.

If
K
in (
4.16
) is block diagonal,

K={\rm diag}\,(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})

with possibly different positive definite
\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
-valued kernels
k_{1},\ldots,k_{N}
, the formulas

(T_{n}f)(z)=F_{n}(z),\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

define bounded operators
T_{n}\in\mathbf{L}(\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{K}),\mathfrak{H}(k_{n}))
,
n=1,\ldots,N
,
with adjoint operators

(T_{n}^{*}g)(z)=e_{n}(z)g(r(z))

and
T_{1}^{*},\ldots,T_{N}^{*}
satisfy the Cuntz relations.

Proof.

The operators
T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}
are well defined in view of Theorem
2.12
. They are bounded by definition of the norms in (
4.15
) since

\|F\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}(K)}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|F_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}(k_{n})}.

To compute the adjoint operators we note that

(5.2)

T_{n}(\mathbb{K}(\cdot,w)d)=k_{n}(\cdot,r(w))\overline{e_{n}(w)}d_{n},\quad{\rm with}\quad d=\begin{pmatrix}d_{1}\\
\vdots\\
d_{N}\end{pmatrix},\quad d_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}.

Then it follows from a classical result on weighted composition operators that

(T_{n}^{*}g)(z)=e_{n}(z)g(r(z)),\quad n=1,\ldots,N.

Furthermore we have from (
5.1
) that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}T_{n}^{*}T_{n}=I_{\mathfrak{H}(\mathbb{K})}.

Finally for
n,m\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
, we have

\begin{split}(T_{n}T_{m}^{*}g)(z)&=(T_{n}(e_{m}g\circ r))(z)\\
&=\begin{cases}\,g(z),\,\,n=m\\
\,0,\,\,\,\,\hskip 14.22636ptn\not=m,\end{cases}\end{split}

since the function
e_{m}(z)g(r(z))
has unique decomposition along the direct sum corresponding to (
5.1
).
∎

In the case where
k_{1}=\cdots=k_{N}=k
it is of interest to see when it holds that

i.e.

Such equations, and their connections with the Cuntz relations,
have been studied in
[
15
]
.

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle CS

, with
{\rm deg}\,p=N
and
{\rm deg}\,q<N
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
and consider
R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
with domain of definition the space function analytic in neighborhoods of the points
w_{1},\ldots,w_{N}
. Then
it holds that

(7.1)

\ker R^{(r)}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{L}(r)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{p}

Proof.

We assume
p=1
to lighten the notation. Assume that
f\in\ker R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
. Then,

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f(w_{n})}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}\frac{r(z)-r(w_{n})}{z-w_{n}}\in\mathfrak{L}(r)

where
r(w_{n})=\alpha
and since the function

z\mapsto\frac{r(z)-r(\beta)}{z-\beta}\in\mathfrak{L}(r)

for every complex number which is not a pole of
r
. The functions
R_{w}r
form a basis of
\mathfrak{L}(r)
for any set of
N
pairwise different points, and so the kernel of

R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
is exactly equal to
\mathfrak{L}(r)
. More precisely, any function
f
in
\mathfrak{L}(r)
is of the form

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{c_{n}}{r^{\prime}(w_{n})}\frac{r(z)-r(w_{n})}{z-w_{n}}\in\mathfrak{L}(r),\quad c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{C},

and is such that
f(w_{m})=c_{m}
,
m=1,\ldots,N
, as is seen by plugging
z=w_{m}
in the above equation. So
f\in\ker R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
.
∎

We now use (
2.26
) to relate operators associated to the operators
R_{\alpha}
and
R_{\alpha}^{(r)}
. The existence of the operators
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{A}_{r}
in the next theorem
follow from
[
47
, Theorem 4.10, p. 137]
).

Theorem 7.2
.

Assuming that the operators
R^{(r)}_{\alpha}
and
R_{\alpha}
are bounded in some Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
, and have trivial kernels in
\mathcal{H}
for every
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
. Then,
there exist two possibly unbounded operators
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{A}_{r}
such that

(7.2)

R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f=(\mathsf{A}_{r}-\alpha I)^{-1}\quad{\it and}\quad R_{\alpha}F=(\mathsf{A}-\alpha I)^{-1}.

They are related by

((\mathsf{A}_{r}-\alpha I)^{-1}f)(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)((\mathsf{A}-\alpha I)^{-1}F_{n})(r(z))

when
f
is decomposed as (
5.1
),

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)F_{n}(r(z)).

Theorem 7.3
.

In the notation of the previous theorem, assume that

(7.3)

\mathfrak{H}\cap\mathfrak{L}(r)=\left\{0\right\}.

Then

(7.4)

{\rm Dom}\,\mathsf{A}_{r}=\left\{f\in\mathfrak{H}\,\,\text{for which there exists $h_{f}\in\mathfrak{L}(r)$ such that }r(z)f(z)+h_{f}(z)\in\mathfrak{H}\right\}

and then

(7.5)

(\mathsf{A}_{r}f)(z)=r(z)f(z)+h_{f}(z).

Proof.

By (
7.3
) we have
\ker R^{(r)}_{\alpha}=\left\{0\right\}
for some (and hence all)
\alpha
, and so the operator
\mathsf{A}_{r}
exists; see

[
47
, Theorem 4.10, p. 137]
).
∎

We remark that, with
(\mathsf{A}f)(z)=zf(z)+d_{f}
we have

\begin{split}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)((A-\alpha I)^{-1}f_{n})(r(z)&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)(r(z)f_{n}(r(z))+d_{f_{n}})\\
&=r(z)\left(\sum)_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)f_{n}(r(z))\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)d_{f_{n}}\\
&=r(z)Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))+h_{f}(z)\\
&=r(z)f(z)+h_{f}(z)\end{split}

with
f(z)=Z_{r}(z)F(r(z))
and
h_{f}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}(z)d_{f_{n}}
.

8.
\mathfrak{P}(\Theta)
spaces

We now study the counterparts of reproducing kernel Pontryagin spaces with reproducing kernel of the form

(8.1)

\frac{J-\Theta(z)J\Theta(w)^{*}}{-i(z-\overline{w})},\quad{\rm or}\quad\frac{J-\Theta(z)J\Theta(w)^{*}}{1-z\overline{w}},

in the present setting. The first formula corresponds to the
line case
, while the second corresponds to the
circle case.

These kernels give models for close to self-adjoint and close to unitary operators in the classical case; see
[
29
,
43
]
for the latter.

We begin with the line case.
In the statement of the next theorem,
J\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a signature matrix and
X(J,r)
is the corresponding associated symmetric matrix (see (
3.5
)).
In the statements we consider functions
r
which satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4.1
. This includes in particular rational functions regular at the origin and having a pole at infinity
(see Lemma
4.4
) and finite Blaschke products. Recall that
\Omega(r)
is defined by (
2.6
), that

A(r)
is defined in (
4.5
) and that we assume functions extended to
r^{-1}(A(r))
; see Remark
4.3
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
r
be a
real
rational function satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a reproducing kernel Pontryagin space of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
-valued functions analytic in
r^{-1}(A(r))
. Then, for each
\alpha,\beta\in\Omega(r)\cap A(r)

such that
\alpha\not=\overline{\beta}
, it holds that

(8.2)

\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-\left[f,R_{\beta}^{(r)}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,R^{(r)}_{\beta}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}=i(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{g(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\frac{f(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{1}{u_{i}-\overline{v_{j}}}

(with
r(u_{i})=\alpha
and
r(v_{j})=\beta
,
i,j=1,\ldots,N
)
if and only if the reproducing kernel of
\mathfrak{P}
is of the form

(8.3)

(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{p})\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-\Theta(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta(r(w))^{*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{p})^{*}

where
\Theta
is a
\mathbb{C}^{Np\times Np}
-valued function analytic in a neighborhood of the origin.

Proof.

Using (
2.26
) we can write with
f=Z_{r}F(r)
and
g=Z_{r}G(r)
, where
F
and
G
are analytic in
A(r)
. By Corollary
2.13
applied to every connected component of

r^{-1}(A(r))
we see that
F
and
G
are uniquely determined, and we can define an inner product by

(8.4)

[F,G]=[f,g]_{\mathfrak{P}}.

Fo
\alpha,\beta\in A(r)
we have

(8.5)

\begin{split}\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-\left[f,R_{\beta}^{(r)}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,R^{(r)}_{\beta}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}&=\\
&\hskip-113.81102pt=\left[R_{\alpha}F,G\right]-\left[F,R_{\beta}G\right]-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R_{\alpha}F,R_{\beta}G\right],\quad\end{split}

Using (
2.25
) we can write

\begin{split}(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{g(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\frac{f(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{1}{u_{i}-\overline{v_{j}}}&=G(\beta)^{*}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\frac{Z_{r}(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{\alpha-\overline{\beta}}{u_{i}-\overline{v_{j}}}\right)F(\alpha)\\
&=G(\beta)^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{\overline{\beta}-\alpha}{\overline{v_{j}}-u_{i}}\right)\right)F(\alpha)\\
&=G(\beta)^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{\overline{\beta}-\alpha}{\overline{v_{j}}-u_{i}}\right)\right)F(\alpha)\\
&=G(\beta)^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_{r}(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\left(Z_{r}(\overline{v_{j}})\frac{\overline{\beta}-\alpha}{\overline{\beta}-\alpha}\right)\right)F(\alpha)\\
&=G(\beta)^{*}XF(\alpha),\end{split}

where
X(J,r)
is defined by (
3.5
) (and is Hermitian, invertible, and independent of
\alpha
and
\beta
). We are therefore back to the classical case,

\left[R_{\alpha}F,G\right]-\left[F,R_{\beta}G\right]-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R_{\alpha}F,R_{\beta}G\right]=iG(\beta)^{*}X(J,r)F(\alpha)

Write
X(J,r)=Y^{*}J_{0}Y
, where
J_{0}
is a real signature matrix. The space
\mathfrak{P}_{1}
of functions of the form
YF
with inner product

(8.6)

[YF,YG]_{Y}=[F,G]

satisfies

\left[R_{\alpha}YF,YG\right]-\left[YF,R_{\beta}YG\right]-(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\left[R_{\alpha}YF,R_{\beta}YG\right]=iG(\beta)^{*}Y^{*}J_{0}YF(\alpha)

and are analytic across the real line (since the functions
F
are analytic in a disk
|z|<\rho
by definition of
A(r)
). This analyticity condition allows us to apply the Pontryagin space version of
de Branges’ result (see
[
11
, Theorem 6.10]
, and see
[
24
, Theorem III, p. 447]
,
[
10
, Theorem 2.3 p. 598]
for the Hilbert space version) to assert that its reproducing kernel is of the form

(8.7)

\frac{J_{0}-\Theta_{0}(z)J_{0}\Theta_{0}(w)^{*}}{-i(z-\overline{w})}.

from which the result follows. More precisely, we see that the index of the reproducing
kernel Pontryagin space with reproducing kernel (
8.7
) is equal to
\nu_{-}(\mathfrak{P})
.
∎

Notation 8.2
.

We will set
\mathfrak{P}=\mathfrak{P}(\Theta)
and define

(8.8)

\nu_{-}(\Theta)=\nu_{-}(\mathfrak{P}),

where the latter is the index of negativity of the Pontryagin space
\mathfrak{P}
.

We now consider an inclusion and factorization result related to the above spaces. In the statement,
\nu_{-}(\Theta_{1}^{-1}\Theta)
is the number of negative squares of the kernel

\frac{X(J,r)-\Theta_{1}(r(z))^{-1}\Theta(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta(r(w))^{*}\Theta_{1}(r(z))^{-*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}

(note that the numerator involves only
X(J,r)
and not its inverse).

Theorem 8.3
.

In the notation of the previous theorem, let
\mathfrak{P}_{1}
be a subspace of
\mathfrak{P}
which is
R_{\alpha}^{(r)}
-invariant, and non-degenerate. Then
\mathfrak{P}_{1}
has a reproducing kernel of the
form

(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{p})\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-\Theta_{1}(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta_{1}(r(w))^{*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{p})^{*}

and we have

\nu_{-}(\Theta)=\nu_{-}(\Theta_{1})+\nu_{-}(\Theta_{1}^{-1}\Theta)

and the direct and orthogonal decomposition

(8.9)

\mathfrak{P}(\Theta)=\mathfrak{P}(\Theta_{1})\oplus(Z_{r}\otimes I_{p})\Theta_{1}(r)\mathfrak{P}(\Theta_{1}^{-1}\Theta).

Proof.

Thanks to Corollary
2.15
the claim reduces to the decomposition

\begin{split}\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-\Theta(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta(r(w))^{*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}&=\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-\Theta_{1}(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta_{1}(r(w))^{*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}+\\
&\hskip-170.71652pt+\Theta_{1}(r(z))\frac{X(J,r)-\Theta_{1}(r(z))^{-1}\Theta(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta(r(w))^{*}\Theta_{1}(r(z))^{-*}}{-i(r(z)-\overline{r(w)})}\Theta_{1}(r(z))^{*},\end{split}

and the result follows then from classical results in reproducing kernel Pontryagin spaces.
∎

We now turn to the circle case. The argument is similar to the one in the previous theorem, but we need to use Corollary
4.5
at some point
w_{0}
of modulus
1
not a pole of
r
. Recall that

A(r,w_{0})
was introduced in Definition
4.6
.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
r
be a rational function satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
, let
w_{0}
be a point on the unit circle not a pole of
r
.
Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a reproducing kernel Pontryagin space of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
-valued functions analytic in
r^{-1}(A(w_{0},r))
. Then, for each
\alpha,\beta\in\omega(r)\cap A(w_{0},r)
it holds that

(8.10)

\left[f,g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}+\overline{\beta}\left[f,R_{\beta}^{(r)}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}+\alpha\left[R_{\alpha}^{(r)}f,g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-(1-\alpha\overline{\beta})\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,R^{(r)}_{\beta}g\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}=(\alpha-\overline{\beta})\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{g(v_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(v_{j})}}J\frac{f(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{1}{u_{i}-\overline{v_{j}}}

(with
r(u_{i})=\alpha
and
r(v_{j})=\beta
,
i,j=1,\ldots,N
)
if and only if the reproducing kernel of
\mathfrak{P}
is of the form

(8.11)

(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{p})\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-\Theta(r(z))X(J,r)\Theta(r(w))^{*}}{1-r(z)\overline{r(w)}}Y^{-*}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{s})^{*},

where
\Theta
is a
\mathbb{C}^{Np\times Np}
-valued function analytic in a neighborhood of the origin.

Proof.

The functions
F
are now analytic across the unit circle at
w_{0}
and one can use the Pontryagin space version of de Branges’ theorem for the circle case; see
[
11
, Theorem 6.10]
for the latter.
∎

9.
\mathfrak{P}(S)
spaces

It is interesting to note that the Fock space restricted to the unit disk
is a special case of a
\mathfrak{P}(S)
space; see
[
8
]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
r
be a
real
rational function satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a reproducing kernel Pontryagin space of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
-valued functions analytic in
r^{-1}(A(r))\setminus\mathbb{R}
, and let
\alpha\in\Omega(r)
.
Assume that the inequality

(9.1)

\left[R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f,R^{(r)}_{\alpha}f\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}\leq\left[(I+\alpha R^{(r)}_{\alpha})f,(I+\alpha R^{(r)}_{\alpha})f\right]_{\mathfrak{P}}-(\alpha-\overline{\alpha})\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\frac{f(u_{j})^{*}}{\overline{r^{\prime}(u_{j})}}J\frac{f(u_{i})}{r^{\prime}(u_{i})}\frac{1}{u_{i}-\overline{u_{j}}}

holds for some preassigned signature matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
Then there exist a Pontryagin space
\mathfrak{C}
such that
\nu_{-}(\mathfrak{C})=\nu_{-}(X)
,
and a
\mathbf{L}(\mathfrak{C},\mathbb{C}^{Np}_{J_{0}})
-valued function analytic in a neighborhood of
\alpha
such that the reproducing kernel of
\mathfrak{P}

is of the form

(9.2)

K(z,w)=(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{p})\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}-S(r(z))S(r(w))^{*}}{1-r(z)\overline{r(w)}}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{p})^{*}.

Proof.

The strategy is the same as for Theorem
8.1
. We write elements of
\mathfrak{P}
in the form
f=(Z_{r}\otimes I_{p})F(r))
, where
F
is uniquely
determined and analytic in
A(r)
, and with induced inner product (
8.4
). Inequality (
9.1
) becomes

(9.3)

[R_{\alpha}F,R_{\alpha}F]\leq[(I+\alpha R_{\alpha})F,(I+\alpha R_{\alpha})F]-F(\alpha)^{*}XF(\alpha)

(see ,
[
11
, (6.9)]
).
As above, write
X=Y^{*}J_{0}Y
. The space of functions of the form
YF
with norm the norm of

has reproducing kernel of the form

\frac{J_{0}-S(r(z))S(r(w)^{*}}{1-r(z)\overline{r(w)}}

where
\mathfrak{C}
is a Pontryagin space with negative index
\nu_{-}(J_{0})
and where

is a
\mathbf{L}(\mathfrak{C},\mathbb{C}_{J_{0}}^{Np})
-valued function analytic in a

\alpha
. This is
[
9

for the case

and
[
18

∎

Classical

spaces are generalizations of the orthogonal complements of Beurling-Lax invariant subspaces. This suggests that the counterpart of the

(9.4)

(Z_{r}(z)\otimes I_{p})\frac{X(J,r)^{-1}}{1-r(z)\overline{r(w)}}(Z_{r}(w)\otimes I_{p})^{*}.

10.
\mathfrak{P}(E_{+},E_{-})
spaces

We now present two structure theorems. The proof of the first one is a matrix-valued version of
[
25
, Théorème 23, p. 59]
. The proof of the second theorem is an
adaptation to the circle case of de Branges’ proof, and appears in
[
3
]
and

[
7
, Theorem 6.1, p. 173]
. Both proofs are repeated here for completeness.

Theorem 10.1
.

(The line case)
Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a reproducing kernel Pontryagin space of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
functions analytic in an open set
\Omega
, symmetric with respect to the real line and intersecting it, and assume that
there is a point
\alpha\not=0\in\Omega
such that both
K(\alpha,\alpha)
and
K(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha})
are invertible.
Assume that the following condition holds for every
F,G\in\mathfrak{P}
: if
F(v)=G(v)=0
for
v\not=\overline{v}\in\Omega
then the functions
z\mapsto\frac{z-\overline{v}}{z-v}F(z)

and
z\mapsto\frac{z-\overline{v}}{z-v}G(z)
belongs to
\mathfrak{P}
and

(10.1)

\left[F(z),G(z)\right]=\left[\frac{z-\overline{v}}{z-v}F(z),\frac{z-\overline{v}}{z-v}G(z)\right].

Then, there exist
\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
-valued functions
E_{+}
and
E_{-}
and a signature matrix
J\in\mathbb{c}^{p\times p}
such that the reproducing kernel of
\mathfrak{P}
is of the form

(10.2)

K_{\mathfrak{P}}(z,w)=\frac{E_{+}(z)JE_{+}(w)^{*}-E_{-}(z)JE_{-}(w)^{*}}{-i(z-\overline{w})},

where
J
is a signature matrix with same signature as
K(\alpha,\alpha)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, and by maybe replacing
\alpha
by its conjugate, we will assume
{\rm Im}\,\alpha>0
. Let
c\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
. The function

(10.3)

z\mapsto\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c

belongs to
\mathfrak{P}
and vanishes at the point
\alpha
. So the function

z\mapsto\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c

belongs to
\mathfrak{P}
.
Let
F\in\mathfrak{P}
which vanishes at
\overline{\alpha}
. Using (
10.1
) with
v=\overline{\alpha}
we can write

\begin{split}\left[F(z),\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c\right]&=\\
&\hskip-227.62204pt=\left[F(z)\frac{z-\alpha}{z-\overline{\alpha}},\frac{z-\alpha}{z-\overline{\alpha}}\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c\right]\\
&\hskip-227.62204pt=\left[F(z)\frac{z-\alpha}{z-\overline{\alpha}},\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c\right]\\
&\hskip-227.62204pt=\left[F(z)\frac{z-\alpha}{z-\overline{\alpha}},\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)c\right]\\
&\hskip-227.62204pt=c^{*}F(w)\frac{w-\alpha}{w-\overline{\alpha}}\\
&\hskip-227.62204pt=\left[F(z),\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\overline{\alpha})K(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha})^{-1}K(\overline{\alpha},w)\right)c\frac{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{w}-\alpha}\right].\end{split}

This chain of equalities holds for all
F
vanishing at
\overline{\alpha}
and hence we get

\begin{split}\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)\right)&=\\
&\hskip-113.81102pt=\left(K(z,w)-K(z,\overline{\alpha})K(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha})^{-1}K(\overline{\alpha},w)\right)\frac{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{w}-\alpha}.\end{split}

We can thus write:

\begin{split}\left(\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}-\frac{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{w}-\alpha}\right)K(z,w)&=\\
&\hskip-113.81102pt=\frac{z-\overline{\alpha}}{z-\alpha}K(z,\alpha)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}K(\alpha,w)-\frac{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}}{\overline{w}-\alpha}K(z,\overline{\alpha})K(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha})^{-1}K(\overline{\alpha},w).\end{split}

Therefore:

(10.4)

K(z,w)=\frac{F_{+}(z)K(\alpha,\alpha)^{-1}F_{+}(w)^{*}-F_{-}(z)K(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\alpha})^{-1}F_{-}(w)^{*}}{(-i(\alpha-\overline{\alpha}))(-i(z-\overline{w}))},

with

(10.5)

\displaystyle F_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{-i(\alpha-\overline{\alpha})}}(z-\overline{\alpha})K(z,\alpha)

(10.6)

\displaystyle F_{-}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{-i(\alpha-\overline{\alpha})}}(z-{\alpha})K(z,\overline{\alpha}).

By analyticity,
\det K(z,\alpha)\not\equiv 0
and so taking a point on the unit circle where
\det K(z,\alpha)\not=0
and
\det K(z,\overline{\alpha})\not=0
we see that
K(\alpha,\alpha)
and

Theorem 11.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a reproducing kernel Pontryagin space of
\mathbb{C}^{p}
-valued functions analytic in a set
\Omega
symmetric with respect to the real line and assume that

\mathfrak{P}
is resolvent invariant. Assume moreover that

(11.1)

[F(z),(R_{\beta}G)(z)]_{\mathfrak{P}}-[(R_{\alpha}F)(z),F(z)]_{\mathfrak{P}}+(\alpha-\overline{\beta})[(R_{\alpha}F)(z),(R_{\beta}G)(z)]_{\mathfrak{P}}=0

Then there is a
\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}

\Omega

Proof.

The core of the proof is as in
[
25
, p. 13]
, and we adapt the arguments to the case of negative squares:

STEP 1:
The resolvent operators are bounded in
\mathfrak{P}
:

Indeed, they are closed in view of the reproducing kernel property, and everywhere defined. Hence they are bounded since we are in a Pontryagin space.

STEP 2:
The reproducing kernel
K(z,w)
satisfies:

(11.3)

(\overline{w}-z)K(z,w)=-(z-\overline{\alpha})K(z,\alpha)+(\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha})K(\overline{\alpha},w),\quad\alpha,z,w\in\Omega.

As in
[
25
, p. 13]
this follows from setting
G(z)=K(z,w)d
and
\alpha=\overline{\beta}
in (
11.5
). We then get for any
F\in\mathfrak{P}

\begin{split}\left[F(z),\frac{K(z,w)-K(\overline{\alpha},w)}{z-\overline{\alpha}}d\right]&=\left[\frac{F(z)-F({\alpha})}{z-{\alpha}},K(z,w)d\right]\\
&=d^{*}\frac{F(w)-F(\alpha)}{w-{\alpha}}\\
&=\left[F(z),\frac{K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)}{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}}d\right]\end{split}

and so

\frac{K(z,w)-K(\overline{\alpha},w)}{z-\overline{\alpha}}=\frac{K(z,w)-K(z,\alpha)}{\overline{w}-\overline{\alpha}},

from which (
11.3
) follows.

It follows from the previous step that

(11.4)

K(z,w)=\frac{{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(w)^{*}}{z-\overline{w}}

with
{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(z)=(z-\overline{\alpha})K(z,\alpha)
and
{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(w)=(w-\alpha)K(w,\overline{\alpha})
.

STEP 3:
It holds that
{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(w)=(\alpha-\overline{\alpha})K(\alpha,\alpha)
.

Indeed, from the equality
K(z,w)=K(w,z)^{*}
we have

\frac{{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(w)^{*}}{z-\overline{w}}=\left(\frac{{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(w)-{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(z)^{*}}{w-\overline{z}}\right)^{*},

from which we get

{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}_{2}(w)^{*}={\mathsf{N}}_{2}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}_{1}(w)^{*}

and hence the result.

We conclude by taking (see
[
25
, p. 14]
),

{\mathsf{N}}(z)=\frac{(\alpha-\overline{\alpha})}{2}K(\alpha,\alpha)+(z-\overline{\alpha})K(z,\alpha).

Then,
K(z,w)=\frac{{\mathsf{N}}(z)-{\mathsf{N}}(w)^{*}}{z-\overline{w}}
.
∎
