Higher Level Completeness for Permutation Polynomials
S Rajagopal, P Vanchinathan*

Abstract
Generalising the concept of a complete permutation polynomial over a finite field, we define completness to level k k for k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 in fields of odd characteristic. We construct two families of polynomials that satisfy the condition of high level completeness for all finite fields, and two more families complete to the maximum level possible for large collection of finite fields. Under the binary operation of composition of functions one family of polynomials is an abelian group isomorphic to the additive group, while the other is isomorphic to the multiplicative group.

Definition 1

For a positive integer
k

f(x)\in\mathbf{F}_{q}[x]

k

k

if along with

f(x)+x,f(x)+2x,\ldots,f(x)+kx

Clearly complete polynomials of level upto
p-1

p

\mathbf{F}_{q}

Here are some easy-to-verify statements of higher level

•

Higher completeness is same as usual completeness in

•

Usual complete polynomials are 1-complete.

•

If a permutation polynomial is

Definition 2

A permutation polynomial that is complete to level
k

is said to be a maximally complete permutation polynomial.

Example:

Any linear polynomial

is maximally complete

a

is in the
prime subfield, it will be complete to level

being the least positive integer representing
a\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
.

Question
:
Do complete permutation
polynomials of level
k>1

{}>1

Our main results of this paper are the following

Theorem A

For any prime
p\geq 3

In case the finite field does not have a proper subfield bigger than the

In all finite fields of characteristic

Main Results and Proofs

Notation:
\mathbf{F}_{q}
will denote a finite field of
prime power order
q
, assumed to be of odd characteristic
p
.

For an extension field
\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}
of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
.
we denote by
m=q+q^{2}+q^{3}+\cdots+q^{n-1}
.
Note that
m+1=q^{n}-1
and that
m
is a multiple of
the characteristic
p
.

Lemma 1

For a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
and an extension field
\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}

and any
c\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
the following polynomial

f_{c+}(x)=x+c\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}x^{j(q-1)}

is a permutaion polynomial over
\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}
.

Proof: It will follow once we show that the polynomial

f_{c+}(x)
actually is the function given below:

f_{c+}(a)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}a-c&\mbox{for }&a\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}\\
a&\mbox{for }&a\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\end{array}\right.

When
a
is in the base field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, we claim that all the
terms in the summation are 1. That is so because we are
summing the
r
-th powers of
a
where
r
is always
a multiple of
q-1
. Thus
f_{c+}(a)=a+cm=a
.

Next we will move on to evaluating it on the remaining elements.
So consider
a\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}
. The summation
actually is a sum of
m
terms of a geometric progression. So

\displaystyle f_{c+}(a)

\displaystyle=a+c\left[a^{(q-1)}+a^{2(q-1)}+\cdots+a^{m(q-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=a+ca^{q-1}\Biggl[\frac{\left(a^{q-1}\right)^{m}-1}{a^{q-1}-1}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=a+c\Biggl[\frac{a^{q^{n}-1}-a^{q-1}}{a^{q-1}-1}\Biggr]\mbox{ as }(m+1)(q-1)=q^{n}-1

\displaystyle=a+c\Biggl[\frac{1-a^{q-1}}{a^{q-1}-1}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=a-c\hskip 56.9055pt\square

Summarising we see that
f_{c+}(x)
when restricted to the base
field is the identity function, and, on the elements not in
the base field, is a translation by an element of the base field.
Thus
f_{c+}(x)
is a permutation polynomial of
\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}
for
all
c\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
.

Though we make no use of it further, it is now
clear that the permutation given by this polynomial for
c\neq 0
,
has order
p
, with exactly
q
fixed points
.

Next we provide a multiplicative analogue of the polynomials
constructed in the previous theorem.

Lemma 2

Under the same notation as earlier, we define
for
c\in\mathbf{F}_{q},c\neq 1
a polynomial by

f_{c*}(x)=x+cx\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}x^{j(q-1)}.

This is a permutaion polynomial over
\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}
.

In fact this polynomial is the function given by the following description:

f_{c*}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}x\left(1-c\right)&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}\\
x&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\end{array}\right.

Case 1 :

To prove
f_{c*}(a)=a\forall a\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
.

As
f_{c*}(0)=0
, we consider
a\in\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
.

\displaystyle f_{c*}(a)=a+c\left[a^{q}+a^{(2q-1)}+\cdots+a^{m.q-(m-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=a+ca\big[a^{q-1}+a^{2(q-1)}+a^{3(q-1)}+\cdots+a^{m(q-1)}\big]

\displaystyle=a+ca\left[\underbrace{a+a+\cdots+a}_{m\rm\ times}\right]

\displaystyle=a+cma=a

Case 2 :

a\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}
.

\displaystyle f_{c*}(a)

\displaystyle=a+ca\left[a^{(q-1)}+a^{2(q-1)}+a^{3(q-1)}+\cdots+a^{m(q-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=a+ca\Biggl[\frac{a^{(m+1)(q-1)}-a^{q-1}}{a^{q-1}-1}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=a+ca\Biggl[\frac{1-a^{q-1}}{a^{q-1}-1}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=a\left(1-c\right)\hskip 56.9055pt\square

Again we can specify the cycle type of this
permutation
f_{c*}
in terms of the order of
(1-c)
as an element
of the multiplicative group

\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
.

The permutation polynomials constructed above are actually
complete to higher level. Here is the precise result:

Theorem 1

The polynomial
f_{c+}(x)
is
(p-2)
-complete,
that is,
f_{c+}(x)
along with the set of
(p-2)

polynomials
x+f_{c+}(x),2x+f_{c+}(x),3x+f_{c+}(x),\ldots,(p-2)x+f_{c+}(x)
are all permutation polynomials.

Proof: For a positive integer
k
from the definition we get

\displaystyle kx+f_{c+}(x)

\displaystyle=(k+1)x+c\left[x^{q-1}+x^{2(q-1)}+\cdots+x^{m(q-1)}\right]

\displaystyle=(k+1)\Biggl[x+\frac{c}{k+1}\left(x^{q-1}+x^{2(q-1)}+\cdots+x^{m(q-1)}\right)\Biggr]

But, visual inspection makes it clear that,
the expression inside the square brackets is actually

f_{c^{\prime}+}
for
c^{\prime}=c/(k+1)
.
Thus we arrive at an identity

\displaystyle kx+f_{c+}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(k+1\right)f_{c^{\prime}+}(x)

By Lemma
1
RHS is a permutation polynomial and hence
because the LHS is too. Of course we need
k+1\neq 0
, and so
this is true for
k=1,2,\ldots,p-2
.
So
f_{c+}(x)
is
(p-2)
-complete.
\square

Theorem 2

The polynomial
f_{c*}(x)
are complete to level
p-2
, i.e.,

f_{c*}(x)
along with the set of
(p-2)
polynomials

x+f_{c*}(x),2x+f_{c*}(x),3x+f_{c*}(x),\ldots,(p-2)x+f_{c*}(x)

are permutation polynomials.

Proof: Using arguments similar to the ones employed in the proof
of Theorem
1
we can easily arrive at

\displaystyle kx+f_{c*}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(k+1\right)f_{c^{\prime}*}(x)

for
c^{\prime}=c/(k+1)
.
Again we will be able to conclude that

kx+f_{c*}\left(x\right)
is also permutation
polynomial for
0<k<(p-1)
.
\square

Next we move on to finding maximally complete permutation polynomials.
First we need a temporary definition of a
middle subfield

of a field: by that we mean a proper subfield which is not the prime subfield.
Middle subfields exist in finite fields of order
p^{n}
iff
n
is a composite
number.

Theorem 3

Let
\mathbf{F}_{q}
be a finite field admitting at least one middle
subfield. Choose
b,c\in\mathbf{F}_{q}
such that
b
is in some middle
subfield and
c
is in any proper subfield of
\mathbf{F}_{q}

Then the polynomials
bf_{c+}(x)
and
bf_{c*}(x)
are
maximally complete permutation polynomials.

Proof:
As
c
is in a proper subfield of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
, Lemma
1

ensures
f_{c+}(x)
is a permutation polynomial
and hence the scalar multiple
bf_{c+}(x)
is too.
We will now show for any
k=1,2,\ldots,p-1

that
bf_{c+}(x)+kx
is a permutation polynomial.

We simply rewrite this as

bf_{c+}(x)+kx=(b+k)\bigg(x+\frac{bc}{b+k}\sum_{j}x^{j(q-1)}\bigg)

And the latter polynomial is the same as
(b+k)f_{c^{\prime}+}(x)

where
c^{\prime}=bc/(b+k)
, and hence a permutation
polynomial. We need
b+k
to be nonzero for this to be true.
That follows from the hypothesis that
b
is not in the prime
field while
k
is always in the prime field.
This completes the proof that
bf_{c+}(x)
is maximally complete.

Same arguments work for proving maximal completeness
property for the polynomials
bf_{c*}
and so omitted.

Next we discuss inter- and intra-relationship among the
members of two families of polynomials
f_{c+}(x)
and
f_{c*}(x)
.

Theorem 4

The two collections of functions
f_{c+}(x)
, and

f_{c*}(x)
behave well under composition. In fact,

(i)

\{\,f_{c+}(x)\mid c\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\,\}
is an abelian group under
composition, and is isomorphic to the additive group of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
.

(ii)

\big\{\,f_{c*}(x)\mid c\in\mathbf{F}_{q}-\{1\}\,\big\}
is also an abelian group under composition, and is isomorphic to the multiplicative
group
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}
.

(iii)

f_{c+}(x)
and
f_{c*}(x)
have the following relationship:

x\big(f_{c+}(x)-x+1\big)=f_{c*}(x)

Proof:

Verifying (i) is straightforward.

For part (iii) the given relationship between
f_{c+}(x)

and
f_{c*}(x)
follows directly from their definitions.

For (ii), to see that it is a group one can easily check that

\displaystyle f_{c*}\circ f_{d*}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{(c+d-cd)*}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(\,1-c-d+cd\,)x&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}\\
x&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\end{array}\right.

and that the compositional inverse of
f_{c*}(x)
is

\displaystyle f_{c*}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}cx/(c-1)&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}}\setminus\mathbf{F}_{q}\\
x&\mbox{for }&x\in\mathbf{F}_{q}\end{array}\right.

Now the statement that is it isomophic to
\mathbf{F}_{q}^{*}

can be deduced from the following lemma whose proof
is a simple exercise:

Lemma 3

For any field
K
, the set
K\setminus\{1\}

is a group under the binary operation

a*b=a+b-ab
and this is isomorphic to
K^{*}

under the map
a\mapsto 1-a
.
