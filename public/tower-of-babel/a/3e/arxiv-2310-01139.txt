Stability and Generalization for Minibatch SGD and Local SGD
Yunwen Lei, Tao Sun, Mingrui Liu, [1.2pt] Department of Mathematics, The University of Hong Kong, [1.2pt] College of Computer, National University of Defense Technology, [1.2pt] Department of Computer Science, George Mason University[1.2pt] leiyw@hku.hksuntao.saltfish@outlook.commingruil@gmu.edu

Abstract
The increasing scale of data propels the popularity of leveraging parallelism to speed up the optimization. Minibatch stochastic gradient descent (minibatch SGD) and local SGD are two popular methods for parallel optimization. The existing theoretical studies show a linear speedup of these methods with respect to the number of machines, which, however, is measured by optimization errors. As a comparison, the stability and generalization of these methods are much less studied. In this paper, we study the stability and generalization analysis of minibatch and local SGD to understand their learnability by introducing a novel expectation-variance decomposition. We incorporate training errors into the stability analysis, which shows how small training errors help generalization for overparameterized models. We show both minibatch and local SGD achieve a linear speedup to attain the optimal risk bounds.

Introduction

odern machine learning often comes along with models and datasets of massive scale (e.g., millions or billions of parameters over enormous training datasets)
[
75
,
35
,
55
,
26
]
, which renders the training with sequential algorithms impractical for large-scale data analysis.
To speed up the computation, it is appealing to develop learning schemes that can leverage parallelism to reduce the amount of time in the training stage
[
62
]
. First-order stochastic optimization is especially attractive for parallelism since the gradient computation is easy to parallelize across multiple computation devices
[
55
,
60
,
33
]
. For distributed optimization, communication has been reported to be a major bottleneck for large-scale applications
[
58
]
. Therefore, increasing the computation to communication ratio is a major concern in developing parallelizable optimization algorithms.

A simple stochastic first-order method is the minibatch stochastic gradient descent (minibatch SGD)
[
55
,
13
,
12
,
35
,
67
]
, where the update at each round is performed based on an average of gradients over several training examples rather than a single example. Using a minibatch helps in reducing the variance, and therefore accelerates the optimization. The computation over a minibatch of size
b
can be distributed over
M
machines, where each machine computes a minibatch of size
K=b/M
before communication. This increases the computation to communication ratio. Due to its simplicity, minibatch SGD has found successful applications in a variety of settings
[
61
,
55
]
.

An orthogonal approach to increase the computation to communication ratio is the local SGD
[
43
,
58
,
71
]
. For local SGD with
M
machines, we divide the implementation into
R
rounds. At each round, each machine conducts SGD independently in
K
iterations, after which an average over
M
machines is taken to get a consensus point. Unlike minibatch SGD, local SGD is constantly improving its behavior even when the machines are not communicating with each other. Due to this appealing property, local SGD has been widely deployed in many applications
[
43
]
.

The promising applications of minibatch SGD and local SGD motivate a lot of theoretical work to understand the performance of these methods. A linear speedup with respect to (w.r.t.) the batch size was established for minibatch SGD in both online
[
13
]
and stochastic setting
[
55
,
12
]
, which is further extended to its accelerated variants
[
13
,
60
,
61
]
. The analysis for local SGD is more challenging. A linear speedup w.r.t. the number of machines was developed for local SGD with strongly convex
[
58
]
and convex problems
[
62
,
24
]
. These results on linear speedup build the theoretical foundation for using the parallelism to reduce the computation for large-scale problems.

The above results on linear speedup are obtained for optimization errors, i.e., the performance of models on training examples. However, in machine learning we care more about the generalization behavior of these models on testing examples. Generalization issues have been scarcely touched for minibatch and local SGD in a multi-pass setting. To our knowledge, other than regression with the specific least squares loss
[
46
,
37
,
8
]
, there is no generalization analysis of minibatch and local SGD that shows a linear speedup measured by testing errors. In this paper, we initialize the generalization analysis of minibatch and local SGD based on the concept of algorithmic stability
[
6
]
. Our aim is to show the linear speedup observed in optimization errors also holds for testing errors. Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

1. We develop stability bounds of minibatch SGD for convex, strongly convex, and nonconvex problems. Our stability bounds incorporate the property of small training errors, which are often the case for overparamterized models. For strongly convex problems, we develop stability bounds independent of the iteration number, which is also novel for the vanilla SGD in the sense of removing the Lipschitz continuity assumption. Based on these stability bounds, we further develop optimistic bounds on excess population risks which imply fast rates under a low noise condition.

2. We develop stability bounds of local SGD for both convex and strongly convex problems, based on which we develop excess risk bounds. This gives the first stability and generalization bounds for local SGD.

3. Our risk bounds for both minibatch SGD and local SGD are optimal. For convex problems our bounds are of the order
O(1/\sqrt{n})
, while for
\mu
-strongly convex problems our bounds are of the order
O(1/(n\mu))
, where
n
is the sample size. These match the existing minimax lower bounds for the statistical guarantees
[
1
]
. Furthermore, we show that minibatch SGD can achieve a linear speedup w.r.t. the batch size, and local SGD achieves a linear speedup w.r.t. the number of machines. To our knowledge, these are the first linear speedup for minibatch and local SGD in generalization for general problems.

To achieve these results, we develop techniques by introducing the
expectation-variance decomposition
and self-bounding property
[
28
,
31
]
into stability analysis based on a reformulation of minibatch SGD with binomial variables
[
16
]
.
Indeed, the existing stability analysis of the vanilla SGD
[
21
,
28
,
31
]
does not apply to minibatch SGD. Furthermore, even with our formulation, the techniques in
[
31
]
would imply suboptimal stability bounds.

The paper is organized as follows. We survey the related work in Section
2
, and formulate the problem in Section
3
. We study the stability and generalization for minibatch SGD in Section
4
, and extend these discussions to local SGD in Section
5
. We present the proof of minibatch SGD in Section
6
and the proof of local SGD in Section
7
. We conclude the paper in Section
8
.

Related Work

,

,

. Stability has found wide applications in various learning problems, including differential privacy

,

,

,

, generative adversarial networks
[

]
, iterative hard thresholding
[

]
,

]
, meta learning
[

]
, adversarial training
[

]
, transfer learning
[

]
, minimax problems

, neural networks

and stochastic optimization

An important property of the stability analysis is that it considers only the particular model produced by the algorithm, and therefore can use the property of the learning algorithm to imply complexity-independent generalization bounds. Lower bounds on the stability of gradient methods also draw increasing attention

Minibatch algorithm
. Minibatch algorithms are efficient in speeding up optimization for smooth problems.
Shamir and Srebro 2014
showed that minibatch distributed optimization can attain a linear speedup w.r.t. the batch size, which was also observed for general algorithms in an online learning setting
[
13
]
. These results were improved in
[
12
]
, where the convergence rates involve the training error of the best model and would decay fast in an interpolation setting. The above speedup was derived if the batch size is not large. Indeed, a large batch size may negatively affect the performance of the algorithm
[
23
,
39
]
. Minibatch stochastic approximation methods were studied for stochastic composite optimization problems
[
18
]
and nonconvex problems
[
19
]
. Recently, minibatch algorithms have been shown to be immune to the heterogeneity of the problem
[
61
]
. For problems with nonsmooth loss functions, minibatch algorithms do not get any speedup
[
55
]
.

Local SGD
. Local SGD, also known as “parallel SGD” or “federated averaging”, is widely used to solve large-scale convex and nonconvex optimization problems
[
43
]
. A linear speedup in the number (
M
) of machines was obtained for local SGD on strongly convex problems
[
58
]
. The key observation is that local SGD can roughly yield a reduction in the variance by a factor of
M
. Despite its promising performance in practice, the theoretical guarantees on convergence rates are still a bit weak and are often dominated by minibatch SGD. Indeed, initial analysis of local SGD failed to derive a convergence rate matching minibatch SGD’s performance, due to an additional term proportional to the dispersion of the individual machine’s iterates for local SGD
[
62
]
. Furthermore, the work
[
62
]
also presented a lower bound on the performance of local SGD that is worse than the minibatch SGD guarantee in a certain regime, showing that local SGD does not dominate minibatch SGD. Until recently, the guarantees better than minibatch SGD were obtained under some cases (e.g., case with rare communication)
[
62
,
24
,
56
]
. These discussions impose different assumptions:
Woodworth et al. 2020b
imposed a bounded variance assumption, while
Khaled et al. 2020
considered an almost sure smoothness assumption without the bounded variance assumption. These results were extended to a heterogeneous distributed learning setting
[
24
,
61
]
, for which heterogeneity was shown to be particularly problematic for local SGD. A linear speedup w.r.t.
M
was also observed for nonconvex loss functions under a more restrictive constraint on the synchronization delay than that in the convex case
[
68
]
. Lower bounds of local SGD were established
[
62
,
63
]
. Generalization of federated learning were also studied recently based on Rademacher complexity
[
44
]
and stability
[
59
,
10
]
.

The above results on the linear speedup for minibatch and local SGD were obtained for optimization errors. The benefit of minibatch in generalization was studied for SGD with the square loss function
[
46
,
37
,
8
]
. These discussions use the analytic representation of iterators in terms of integral operators, which do not apply to general problems considered here.

3
Problem Setup

Let
\rho
be a probability measure defined on a sample space
\mathcal{Z}
, from which we independently draw a dataset
S=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{Z}
of
n
examples. Based on
S
, we wish to learn a model
\mathbf{w}
in a model space
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for prediction, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is the dimension. The performance of
\mathbf{w}
on a single example
z\in\mathcal{Z}
can be measured by a nonnegative loss function
f(\mathbf{w};z)
. The empirical behavior of
\mathbf{w}
can be quantified by the empirical risk
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\mathbf{w};z_{i}).
Usually we apply a randomized algorithm
A
to minimize
F_{S}
over
\mathcal{W}
to get a model
A(S)
. Then an algorithm can be considered as a map from the set of samples to
\mathcal{W}
, i.e.,
A:\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}^{n}\mapsto\mathcal{W}
. A good behavior on training examples does not necessarily mean a good behavior on testing examples, which is the quantity of real interest in machine learning and can be quantified by the population risk
F(\mathbf{w}):=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[f(\mathbf{w};Z)]
. Here
\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation w.r.t.
Z
. In this paper, we study the excess population risk of a model
\mathbf{w}
defined by
F(\mathbf{w})-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, which measures the suboptimality as compared to the best model
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}F(\mathbf{w})
.
Our basic strategy is to use the following error decomposition
[
5
]

\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F(A(S))-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\big]=\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))\big]+\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F_{S}(A(S))-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})\big],

(3.1)

where we have used the identity
\mathbb{E}_{S,A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})]=F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
and
\mathbb{E}_{S,A}
denotes the expectation w.r.t.
S
and
A
. We refer to the first term
\mathbb{E}\big[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))\big]
as the generalization error (generalization gap), which measures the discrepancy between training and testing at the output model
A(S)
. We call the second term
\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(A(S))-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})\big]
the optimization error, which measures the suboptimality in terms of the empirical risk. One can control the optimization error by tools in optimization theory.
As a comparison, there is little work on the generalization error of minibatch SGD and local SGD, the key challenge of which is the dependency of
A(S)
on
S
.

In this paper, we will use a specific algorithmic stability —on-average model stability— to address the generalization error. We use
\|\cdot\|_{2}
to denote the Euclidean norm. We denote
S\sim S^{\prime}
if
S
and
S^{\prime}
differ by at most one example.

Definition 1

(Uniform Stability)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. We say a randomized algorithm
A
is
\epsilon
-uniformly stable if
\sup_{S\sim S^{\prime},z}\mathbb{E}_{A}[|f(A(S);z)-f(A(S^{\prime});z)|]\leq\epsilon
.

Definition 2

(On-average Model Stability
[
31
]
)
.

Let
S=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
and
S^{\prime}=\{z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,z^{\prime}_{n}\}
be drawn independently from
\rho
. For any
i\in[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define

S^{(i)}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},z^{\prime}_{i},z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
as the set formed from
S
by replacing the
i
-th element with
z^{\prime}_{i}
. Let
\epsilon>0
.
We say a randomized algorithm
A
is
\ell_{1}
on-average model
\epsilon
-stable if

\mathbb{E}_{S,S^{\prime},A}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A(S)-A(S^{(i)})\|_{2}\big]\leq\epsilon,

and
\ell_{2}
on-average model
\epsilon
-stable if

\mathbb{E}_{S,S^{\prime},A}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|A(S)-A(S^{(i)})\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\epsilon^{2}.

According to the above definition, on-average model stability considers the perturbation of each single example, and measures how these perturbations would affect the output models on average. Lemma
1
gives a quantitative connection between the generalization error and on-average model stability. We first introduce some necessary definitions. We use
\nabla g
to denote the gradient of
g
.

Definition 3
.

Let
g:\mathcal{W}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
G,L>0
and
\mu\geq 0
.

1.

We say
g
is
G
-Lipschitz continuous if
|g(\mathbf{w})-g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})|\leq G\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2},\;\;\forall\;\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}.

2.

We say
g
is
L
-smooth if
\|\nabla g(\mathbf{w})-\nabla g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq L\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2},\;\;\forall\;\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}.

3.

We say
g
is
\mu
-strongly convex if

g(\mathbf{w})\geq g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})+\langle\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime},\nabla g(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
for all
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
. We say
g
is convex if it is
\mu
-strongly convex with
\mu=0
.

A non-negative and
L
-smooth function
g
enjoys the self-bounding property, meaning
\|\nabla g(\mathbf{w})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2Lg(\mathbf{w})

[
57
]
.
Examples of smooth and convex loss functions include the logistic loss, least square loss and Huber loss. Examples of Lipschitz and convex loss functions include the hinge loss, logistic loss and Huber loss.

Lemma 1

(
[
31
]
)
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
and
S^{(i)}
be constructed as in Definition
2
, and let
\gamma>0
.

(a)

Suppose for any
z
, the function
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is convex. If
A
is
\ell_{1}
on-average model
\epsilon
-stable and
\sup_{z}\|\nabla f(A(S);z)\|_{2}\leq G
for any
S
, then

\big|\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F_{S}(A(S))-F(A(S))\big]\big|\leq G\epsilon.

(b)

Suppose for any
z
, the function
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is nonnegative and
L
-smooth.
If
A
is
\ell_{2}
on-average model
\epsilon
-stable, then the following inequality holds

\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F(A(S))-F_{S}(A(S))\big]\leq\frac{L}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[F_{S}(A(S))\big]+\frac{L+\gamma}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S,S^{\prime},A}\big[\|A(S^{(i)})-A(S)\|_{2}^{2}\big].

Part (a) gives the connection between generalization and
\ell_{1}
on-average model stability under a convexity condition, while Part (b) relates generalization to
\ell_{2}
on-average model stability under a smoothness condition (without a Lipschitz condition). Note Part (a) differs slightly from that in
[
31
]
by replacing the Lipschitz condition with a convexity condition and
\sup_{z}\|\nabla f(A(S);z)\|_{2}\leq G
. However, the analysis is almost identical and we omit the proof. An advantage of
\ell_{2}
on-average model stability is that the upper bound involves the training errors, and would be improved if
F_{S}(A(S))
is small.

\mathbf{w}_{1}\in\mathcal{W}
be an initial point.
At the

Remark 1
.

We note that similar stability bounds involving
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
were developed for the vanilla SGD
[
31
]
. Their argument needs to distinguish two cases according to whether the algorithm chooses a particular example at each iteration. This argument does not work for the minibatch SGD since we draw
b
examples per iteration, and we can draw the particular example several times. We bypass this difficulty by introducing the
expectation-variance decomposition
and self-bounding property into the stability analysis based on a reformulation of minibatch SGD
[
28
,
31
,
16
]
.

Remark 2
.

A notable property of these stability bounds is that they involve the empirical risks of
\mathbf{w}_{k}
. Our key idea is to use the smoothness of loss functions to replace the Lipschitz constant in the existing stability bounds with
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
, following the idea of
[
28
,
31
]
. Since we are minimizing the empirical risks by optimization algorithms, it is reasonable to assume that
\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})\big]
would be much smaller than the Lipschitz constant. In this case, our stability bounds would be much smaller than those involving
G
. For example, if
\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]=O(k^{-\alpha})
with
\alpha\in[0,1]
, then Eq. (
4.4
) implies
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(m)}\|_{2}^{2}\big]=O\big(\big(\frac{t}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{nb}\big)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}k^{-\alpha}\big)
. It is clear that the bound in Eq. (
4.3
) is a decreasing function of
b
. Since increasing
b
would speedup the optimization process
[
12
]
, one expects increasing
b
also boosts the decay of excess population risks.

The stability of minibatch SGD with
\eta_{t}=\eta
has also been studied recently
[
67
,
3
]
. The discussions in Theorem 9 of
[
67
]
give a stability bound of the order
O(\eta t/n+\gamma\eta t)
, where
\gamma=\mathrm{Pr}\{\inf_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}\bar{B}_{S}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime})<(b-1)/(2/(L\eta)-n/(n-1))\}
and
\bar{B}_{S}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
is a measure on the gradient diversity defined below

\bar{B}_{S}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}):=\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z_{i})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime};z_{i})\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z_{i})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime};z_{i}))\|_{2}^{2}}.

If
\gamma
is not very small, their stability bounds would be vacuous due to the term
\gamma\eta t
. The stability bound order
O(\eta t/n)
was developed in
Bassily et al. 2019
. These discussions require
f
to be convex, smooth and Lipschitz continuous. Furthermore, these discussions do not incorporate training errors into the stability bounds, and cannot imply optimistic bounds. We remove the Lipschitz condition in our analysis and obtain optimistic bounds.

Remark 3

(Lower bounds)
.

Recently, lower bounds on the uniform stability have also received increasing attention. Let
\epsilon_{\mathrm{unif}}
be the uniform stability of SGD with

\eta

For nonsmooth and Lipschitz loss functions, it was shown
\epsilon_{\mathrm{unif}}=\Omega\big(\min\{1,t/n\}\eta\sqrt{t}+\eta t/n\big)
for convex problems

)
[

For smooth loss functions, it was shown

for convex and Lipschitz problems, and

for
\mu
-strongly convex problems
[
74
]
. It is clear that our on-average stability bounds in Eq. (

[
11
]
. For example, it is shown that the uniform stability plus an optimization error is lower bounded by
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
for convex and smooth problems
[
11
]
. Lower bounds on excess population risks of gradient methods were also studied for both smooth
[

,
47
]
and nonsmooth problems
[

,
2
,

]

We plug the stability bounds in Theorem

to control generalization errors, which together with the optimization error bounds in Lemma

Theorem 3

(Risk Bounds for Minibatch SGD: Convex Case)

Assume for all

Let

As a corollary, we develop explicit excess risk bounds by choosing suitable step sizes and number of rounds, using the idea of early-stopping

.
We use the notation

Corollary 4
.

Let assumptions in Theorem
3
hold.

1.

If
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\geq 1/n
, we can take
\eta=\frac{b}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}

and

to derive

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O\big(\sqrt{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})/{n}}\big).

2.

If
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})<1/n
, we can take
\eta=1/(2L)
and
R\asymp n
to derive

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O(1/n).

Remark 4
.

We now give some explanations. For the case
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\geq 1/n
, a larger batch size allows for a larger step size, which further decreases the number
R
of rounds. It shows that minibatch SGD achieves a linear speedup if the batch size is not large, i.e., it only requires
O(n/b)
rounds to achieve the excess risk bound
O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
if
b=O(\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})})
. Such a linear speedup was observed for optimization errors
[
12
]
. Indeed, it was shown that minibatch SGD requires
O(n/b)
rounds to achieve the optimization error bounds
\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O(\sqrt{F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})/n})
if
b=O(\sqrt{nF_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})})
. We extend the existing optimization error analysis to generalization, and develop the first linear speedup of the minibatch SGD as measured by risks for general convex problems. In particular, our regime
b=O(\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})})
for linear speedup in generalization matches the regime
b=O(\sqrt{nF_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})})
for the linear speedup in optimization
[
12
]
.

For the case
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})<1/n
, Corollary
4
shows that a larger batch size does not bring any gain in speeding up the risk bounds. The underlying reason is that the variance is already very small in this case, and a further reduction of variance by minibatch does not bring essential benefits in the learning process.

\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R,1}=\frac{1}{MKR}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\mathbf{w}_{m,r,t}.

Remark 10
.

Theorem
10
shows that local SGD can achieve the minimax optimal excess population risk bounds
O(1/\sqrt{n})
in the sense of matching the existing lower bounds
[
1
]
.
We now discuss the speed up in the computation. Note
\eta\leq 2/L
requires
M=O(\sqrt{n})
. Furthermore, we require

\eta\asymp\sqrt{n}/(KR)=O\big(\min\big\{M/\sqrt{n},n^{-\frac{1}{4}}(K-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big\}\big),

which implies
M=\Omega(n/(KR))
and

. Therefore, the computation per machine needs to satisfy

. Therefore, if
M=O(\sqrt{n})
, we can choose
R\asymp n/(KM)
to get the excess population risk bound of order
O(1/\sqrt{n})
.

Finally, we give risk bounds of local SGD for strongly convex problems to be proved in Section
7.3
.

Theorem 11

(Risk Bounds for Local SGD: Strongly Convex Case)
.

Assume for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is nonnegative,
\mu

where

Remark 13
.

Generalization bounds for agnostic federated learning were developed from a uniform convergence approach
[
44
]
, which involve Rademacher complexities of function spaces and are algorithm-independent. As a comparison, we study generalization from an algorithmic stability approach and get complexity-independent bounds.

A federated stability was introduced to study the generalization of federated learning algorithms
[
10
]
in a strongly convex setting. As a comparison, our analysis applies to general convex problems. Furthermore, their stability analysis was conducted for abstract approximate minimizers, while our stability analysis is developed for local SGD. Finally, their bound involves an upper bound of the loss function over a compact domain, and therefore cannot imply optimistic bounds.

After the submission of our manuscript, we noted a recent work on the generalization of federated learning algorithms with algorithmic stability
[
59
]
, which considered a heterogeneous setup where the
i
-th local machine has its own dataset
S_{i}
. For local SGD with a constant step size
\eta
, their generalization bounds are of the order of
O(n^{-1}RK\sigma\eta(1+K\eta))
under a Lipschitz continuity assumption and a bounded variance assumption
\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z_{i})-\nabla F_{S_{i}}(\mathbf{w})\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
, where
z_{i}
is drawn uniformly from
S_{i}
. While the bounds in
[
59
]
also involve
\|\nabla F(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|
, it is dominated by
\sigma
and therefore cannot imply fast rates in an interpolation setting. As a comparison, our bounds in Eq. (
5.3
) are optimistic and decay fast if
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{m,r,t})
decay to
0
. Furthermore, the analysis in
[
59
]
requires a Lipschitz condition on the loss function, which is removed in our analysis. Finally, we also develop
\ell_{2}
on-average stability bounds, which are more challenging and illustrate second-order information on the stability.

Assume for all

In our analysis, we will use the concept of binomial distribution. Let

denote the variance of a random variable
X
.

Definition 4

is the discrete probability distribution of the number of successes in a sequence of
n

.

Lemma 13
.

If

\mathbb{E}[X]=np\quad\text{and}\quad\mbox{Var}(X)=np(1-p).

A key property on establishing the stability of SGD is the non-expansiveness of the gradient-update operator established in the following lemma.

Lemma 14

(
[
21
]
)
.

Assume for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the function
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is convex and
L
-smooth. Then for
\eta\leq 2/L
we know

\|\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z)-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime};z)\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

(6.1)

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
\eta\leq 1/L
then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z)-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime};z)\|_{2}\leq(1-\eta\mu/2)\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2},

(6.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w};z)-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}+\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime};z)\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1-\eta\mu)\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

(6.3)

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
. For simplicity, we define
J_{t}=\{i_{t,1},\ldots,i_{t,b}\},t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Define

\alpha_{t,m}=\big|\{j:i_{t,j}=m\}\big|,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{N},m\in[n],

(6.4)

where we use
|S^{\prime}|
to denote the cardinality of a set
S^{\prime}
.
That is,
\alpha_{t,m}
is the number of indices equal to
m
in the
t
-th iteration. Then the SGD update in Eq. (
4.1
) can be reformulated as

\begin{split}\mathbf{w}_{t+1}&=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{k}),\\
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(m)}&=\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(m)}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(m)};z_{k})-\frac{\eta_{t}\alpha_{t,m}}{b}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(m)};z_{m}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(6.5)

from which we know

\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(m)}\|_{2}=\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{k})-\frac{\eta_{t}\alpha_{t,m}}{b}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{m})\\
-\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t}+\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t};z_{k})+\frac{\eta_{t}\alpha_{t,m}}{b}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t};z_{m}^{\prime})\big\|_{2}.

(6.6)

For simplicity, introduce the notations for any
t\in[T]
and
m\in[n]

\Delta_{t,m}=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(m)}\|_{2},\qquad\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}=\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{m})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2}.

(6.7)

Since
f
is
L
-smooth and
\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\leq b
, we know the function
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}f(\mathbf{w};z_{k})
is
L
-smooth.
By Lemma
14
and the assumption
\eta_{t}\leq 1/L
, we know

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1,m}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{k})-\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t}+\frac{\eta_{t}}{b}\sum_{k:k\neq m}\alpha_{t,k}\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(m)}_{t};z_{k})\big\|_{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}\alpha_{t,m}\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}}{b}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{t,m}+\frac{\eta_{t}\alpha_{t,m}\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}}{b}.

We can apply the above inequality recursively and derive (note
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{w}_{1}^{(m)}
)

\Delta_{t+1,m}\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\alpha_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}.

(6.8)

According to the definition of
\alpha_{t,k}
, we know that
\alpha_{t,k}
is a random variable following from the binomial distribution
B(b,1/n)
with parameters
b
and
1/n
, from which we know

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t,k}]=b/n,\quad\mbox{Var}(\alpha_{t,k})=b(1-1/n)\cdot(1/n)\leq b/n.

(6.9)

Furthermore, Lemma
12
implies

\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\leq\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})\|_{2}+\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(m)};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2Lf(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})}+\sqrt{2Lf(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(m)};z_{m}^{\prime})}.

(6.10)

We can combine the above inequality, Eq. (
6.9
) and Eq. (
6.8
) together to derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\big]=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}_{J_{k}}[\alpha_{k,m}]\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\big]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\big],

(6.11)

where we have used the fact that
\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}
is independent of
J_{k}
. According to the symmetry between
z_{m}
and
z_{m}^{\prime}
, we know
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(m)};z_{m}^{\prime})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{m})]
and therefore Eq. (
6.10
) implies
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\big]\leq 2\sqrt{2L}\mathbb{E}\big[\sqrt{f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})}\big]
. It then follows that

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}]\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2L}}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\sqrt{f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})}\big].

It then follows from the concavity of
x\mapsto\sqrt{x}
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}^{(m)}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\eta_{k}}{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\sqrt{2Lf(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{2\eta_{k}}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2L}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})]}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\frac{2\eta_{k}\sqrt{2L\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]}}{n}.

(6.12)

This establishes the stated bound (
4.2
).

We now prove Eq. (
4.3
). We introduce an expectation-variance decomposition in (
6.8
) as follows

\Delta_{t+1,m}\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}.

We take square on both sides followed with an expectation (w.r.t.
S
and
J_{1},\ldots,J_{t}
) and use
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\eta_{k^{\prime}}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\big(\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big].

For any
k\neq k^{\prime}
, it follows from
\mathbb{E}_{J_{k^{\prime}}}[\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}]=b/n
(we can assume
k<k^{\prime}
without loss of generality)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\big(\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{E}_{J_{k^{\prime}}}\Big[\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\big(\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\mathbb{E}_{J_{k^{\prime}}}\big[\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}-b/n\big]\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}\Big]=0,

(6.13)

where we have used the fact that
\alpha_{k,m},\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}
are independent of
J_{k^{\prime}}
. It then follows from Eq. (
6.9
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]=\frac{2}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mbox{Var}(\alpha_{k,m})\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{nb}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{2}{nb}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}\Big]+\frac{8}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})\|_{2}\Big)^{2}\Big],

where we have used the following inequality in the last step

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})\|_{2}\Big)^{2}\Big]+2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(m)};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2}\Big)^{2}\Big]

Remark 14

(Novelty in the analysis)
.

Similar stability bounds involving
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
were developed for the vanilla SGD
[
31
]
. Their argument needs to distinguish two cases according to whether the algorithm chooses a particular example at each iteration. This argument does not work for the minibatch SGD since we draw
b
examples per iteration and we can draw the particular example several times. We bypass this difficulty by introducing the
expectation-variance decomposition
and self-bounding property
[
28
,
31
]
into the stability analysis based on a reformulation of minibatch SGD with binomial variables.
Indeed, the paper
[
31
]
considers SGD with
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t};z_{i_{t}})
. Their discussion controls
\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}
by considering two cases:
i_{t}=m
or
i_{t}\neq m
. If
i_{t}=m
, they use

\|\mathbf{v}_{1}+\mathbf{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+p)\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+(1+1/p)\|\mathbf{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}

(6.16)

and get
\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}\!-\!\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\!\leq\!(1\!+\!p)\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\!-\!\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\!+\!(1\!+\!1/p)\eta_{t}^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}^{2}
. Since
i_{t}=m
happens with probability
1/n
, they derive

\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq(1+{p/n})\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}]+O\Big(\big(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{np}\big)\eta_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}^{2}]\Big).

(6.17)

For minibatch SGD, we may select
i_{m}
several times and cannot divide the discussions into two cases as in
[
31
]
. Instead, we reformulate SGD as Eq. (
6.5
) with
\alpha_{t,k}
being a binomial random variable. Furthermore, even with the formulation, the existing techniques
[
31
]
would imply suboptimal bounds. Indeed, applying (
6.16
) to Eq. (
6.6
) would imply

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}]\leq(1+p)\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t,m}^{2}]+\eta_{t}^{2}b^{-2}(1+1/p)\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t,m}^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}^{2}]\leq(1+p)\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t,m}^{2}]+2\eta_{t}^{2}b^{-1}n^{-1}(1+1/p)\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{C}_{t,m}^{2}],

(6.18)

where we have used
\mathbb{E}_{J_{t}}[\alpha_{t,m}^{2}]\leq 2b/n
.
The key difference is we have a factor of
1+p/n
for SGD and
1+p
for minibatch SGD. To see how Eq. (
6.18
) imply sub-optimal bounds, we continue the deduction as follows. We apply Eq. (
6.18
) recursively and get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2b^{-1}n^{-1}(1+1/p)\sum_{k=1}^{t}(1+p)^{t+1-k}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}]\leq 2b^{-1}n^{-1}(1+1/p)(1+p)^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2b^{-1}n^{-1}(1+t)e\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}]\leq 16Lb^{-1}n^{-1}(1+t)e\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})],

where we choose
p=1/t
and use
(1+1/t)^{t}\leq e
in the last second inequality, and use Eq. (
6.15
) in the last inequality. An average over all
m\in[n]
implies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}]\leq\frac{16L(1+t)e\eta^{2}}{nb}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})],

(6.19)

which is much worse than Eq. (
4.3
). Indeed, if
\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})]=O(1)
, then Eq. (
6.19
) implies
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}]=O(t^{2}\eta^{2}/(nb))
. As a comparison, Eq. (
4.3
) implies
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}]=O(t\eta^{2}/(nb)+t^{2}\eta^{2}/n^{2})
. Note
t\eta^{2}/(nb)
outperforms
t^{2}\eta^{2}/(nb)
by a factor of
t
, and
t^{2}\eta^{2}/n^{2}
outperforms
t^{2}\eta^{2}/(nb)
by a factor of
n/b
.

We significantly improve the analysis in
[
31
]
by introducing new techniques in the analysis with
\ell_{2}
on-average model stability. Our idea is to use an expectation-variance decomposition

\Delta_{t+1,m}\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}
. The key idea is that

\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\big(\alpha_{k^{\prime},m}-b/n\big)\mathfrak{C}_{k^{\prime},m}\big]=0
if
k\neq k^{\prime}
. This removes cross-over terms when taking a square followed by an expectation, and implies

\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{t+1,m}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{2}{b^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}^{2}\big(\alpha_{k,m}-b/n\big)^{2}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathfrak{C}_{k,m}\Big)^{2}\Big].

Remark 15

(Discussion with uniform stability)
.

It is possible to develop uniform stability bounds for minibatch SGD and local SGD under an additional Lipschitz condition. Let
S=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
and
S^{\prime}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m-1},z_{m}^{\prime},z_{m+1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
. Take minibatch SGD as an example. Then Eq. (
6.8
) actually implies

\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{t+1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{b}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\alpha_{k,m}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{k};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2},

where
\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\}_{k}
and
\{\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}\}
are two sequences produced by minibatch SGD on
S
and
S^{\prime}
, respectively. Since
\alpha_{k,m}
is independent of
\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{E}_{J_{k}}[\alpha_{k,m}]=b/n
, we know

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{t+1}\|_{2}]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{k};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2}].

Then we require
f
to be
G
-Lipschitz continuous to show

\displaystyle\sup_{S\sim S^{\prime},z}\mathbb{E}_{A}[|f(\mathbf{w}_{t+1};z)-f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{t+1};z)|]\leq G\sup_{S\sim S^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{t+1}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}\max_{m\in[n]}\sup_{S\sim S^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{k};z_{m})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\prime}_{k};z_{m}^{\prime})\|_{2}]\leq\frac{2G}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\eta_{k}.

The stability bound does not involve training risks
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
, and therefore cannot improve in a low-noise condition with a decaying
F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{k})
along the optimization process.

Proof of Theorem
3

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem
3
on excess population risk bounds of minibatch SGD. We first introduce the following optimization error bounds.

Lemma 15

(Optimization Errors of Minibatch SGD: Convex Case)
.

Assume for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the map
\mathbf{w}\mapsto f(\mathbf{w};z)
is nonnegative, convex and
L
-smooth.
Let
\{\mathbf{w}_{t}\}
be produced by Eq. (
4.1
) with
\eta\leq 1/L
. Then the following inequality holds for all
\mathbf{w}

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\big]-F_{S}(\mathbf{w})\leq\frac{2\eta L}{bR}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta R}+\frac{F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{1})}{R}.

(6.20)

Proof.

Denote
B_{t}=\{z_{i_{t,1}},\ldots,z_{i_{t,b}}\}
and
f(\mathbf{w};B_{t})=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{b}f(\mathbf{w};z_{i_{t,j}})
. Then the update of minibatch SGD can be written as

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\eta\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t}).

Since
\mathbb{E}_{B_{t}}[f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})]=F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})-\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{i_{t,1}})-\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{i_{t,1}})\|_{2}^{2}]}{b}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}{b}+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L\mathbb{E}_{A}[f(\mathbf{w}_{t};z_{i_{t,1}})]}{b}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}{b}+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]}{b}+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}],

(6.21)

where we have used the self-bounding property of smooth functions. Furthermore, by the convexity of
f
we know

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta\langle\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{t},\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta(f(\mathbf{w};B_{t})-f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})).

It then follows that

\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}]+\frac{2L\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]}{b}+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+2\eta\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w})-F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})].

Taking a summation of the above inequality gives (
\mathbf{w}_{1}=0
)

2\eta\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})-F_{S}(\mathbf{w})]\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2L\eta^{2}}{b}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+\eta^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}].

(6.22)

By the
L
-smoothness of
F_{S}
and Eq. (
6.21
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+\mathbb{E}_{A}[\langle\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t}),\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\rangle]+\frac{L\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}]}{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}_{A}[\langle\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t}),\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\rangle]+\frac{L\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}_{t};B_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]-\eta\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]}{b}+\frac{L\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]}{2}.

It then follows from
\eta\leq 1/L
that

\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[\|\nabla F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{1})]+\frac{L^{2}\eta^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]}{b}.

We combine the above inequality and Eq. (
6.22
) to derive (note
\eta\leq 1/L
)

\displaystyle 2\eta\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})-F_{S}(\mathbf{w})]

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2L\eta^{2}}{b}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+2\eta F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{1})+\frac{2L^{2}\eta^{3}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]}{b}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4L\eta^{2}}{b}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}_{A}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+2\eta F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{1}).

The proof is completed.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We choose
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{*}
and take expectations w.r.t.
S
over both sides of Eq. (
6.20
) to get

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\big]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{2\eta L}{bR}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta R}+\frac{F(\mathbf{w}_{1})}{R}.

(6.23)

We consider two cases. If
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})
, then this means that the optimization error in Eq. (
3.1
) is non-positive (this is the easier case since one does not need to consider the optimization error)

\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]\leq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]\leq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}^{*})].

We now consider the case
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})]\geq F(\mathbf{w}^{*})

6.23

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\big]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})]+\frac{2\eta L}{bR}\sum_{t=1}^{R}F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta R}+\frac{F(\mathbf{w}_{1})}{R},

.

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\big]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\leq\frac{4\eta LF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{b}+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{\eta R}+\frac{2F(\mathbf{w}_{1})}{R}.

By Lemma
1
(Part (b)) and Eq. (
4.4
), we know

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})-F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]\leq\frac{L}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}[F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]+(L+\gamma)\Big(\frac{8L}{nb}+\frac{8LR}{n^{2}}\Big)\sum_{t=1}^{R}\eta_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})].

Eq. (
6.24
) implies that

\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}\mathbb{E}\big[F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\big]=O\big(F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+1/(\eta R)\big).

We combine the above two inequalities together and derive (note
F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})\leq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{t=1}^{R}F_{S}(\mathbf{w}_{t})

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})-F_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]=O\Big(\frac{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+1/(\eta R)}{\gamma}\Big)+O\bigg((1+\gamma)\eta^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{nb}+\frac{R}{n^{2}}\Big)\big(RF(\mathbf{w}^{*})+1/\eta\big)\bigg).

The proof is completed.
∎

Proof of Corollary
4
.

We first consider the case that
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\geq 1/n
. In this case, we choose
\eta=\frac{b}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}},R\asymp n/b
and
\gamma=\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}\geq 1
. Note the assumption
b\leq\frac{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}{2L}
implies
\eta\leq 1/(2L)
. Then one can check that
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})\geq c/(\eta R)
for a universal constant
c
and therefore Eq. (
4.6
) implies

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O\Big(\frac{\eta F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{b}+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{\eta R}+\frac{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\gamma}\Big)+O\bigg(\frac{\gamma\eta^{2}R^{2}F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{n^{2}}\bigg).

(6.25)

Since
\eta=\frac{b}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}
,
R\asymp\frac{n}{b}
and
\gamma=\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}
, we know

\displaystyle\frac{\eta F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{b}\asymp\frac{b}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}\frac{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{b}\asymp\frac{F^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\eta R\asymp\frac{b}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}\frac{n}{b}\asymp\sqrt{n}F^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})\Longrightarrow\frac{1}{\eta R}\asymp\frac{F^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{w}^{*})/\gamma\asymp\frac{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}}\asymp\frac{F^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma\eta^{2}R^{2}F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{n^{2}}\asymp\frac{\sqrt{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}b^{2}}{nF(\mathbf{w}^{*})}\frac{n^{2}}{b^{2}}\frac{F(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{n^{2}}=\frac{F^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{n}}.

We plug the above inequalities back into Eq. (
6.25
) and get

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O\Big(\frac{F^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{n}}\Big).

We now consider the case
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})<1/n
. In this case, we choose
\eta=1/(2L),R\asymp n
and
\gamma=1
. Then we know
F(\mathbf{w}^{*})<c/(\eta R)
for a universal constant
c
and Eq. (
4.6
) implies

\mathbb{E}[F(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{R})]-F(\mathbf{w}^{*})=O\Big(F(\mathbf{w}^{*})+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+1}{n}\Big)=O(1/n).

The proof is completed.
∎

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\frac{\eta_{r,t}}{M}\mathfrak{C}_{m,r,t,k}\big(\mathbb{I}_{[i_{m,r,t}=k]}-1/n\big)\Big)^{2}\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\frac{\eta_{r,t}}{M}\mathfrak{C}_{m,r,t,k}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{nM^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\eta^{2}_{r,t}\mathfrak{C}^{2}_{m,r,t,k})\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}M^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\eta_{r,t}\mathfrak{C}_{m,r,t,k}\Big)^{2}\Big],

where we have used
\mbox{Var}(\mathbb{I}_{[i_{m,r,t}=k]})\leq 1/n
. By the self-bounding property of
f
we know

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathfrak{C}_{m,r,t,k}^{2}\big]\leq 4L\mathbb{E}\big[f(\mathbf{w}_{m,r,t};z_{k})+f(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}_{m,r,t};z_{k}^{\prime})\big]=8L\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{w}_{m,r,t};z_{k})].

(7.6)

It then follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|\mathbf{w}_{R+1}-\mathbf{w}_{R+1}^{(k)}\big\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{16L}{nM^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\eta^{2}_{r,t}f(\mathbf{w}_{m,r,t};z_{k})\Big]+\frac{2}{n^{2}M^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{K}\eta_{r,t}\mathfrak{C}_{m,r,t,k}\Big)^{2}\Big].

The stated bound then follows by taking an average over
k\in[n]
and noting
F_{S}(\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(\mathbf{w};z_{k})
.
The proof is completed.
∎

8
Conclusion

We investigate the stability and generalization of minibatch SGD and local SGD, which are widely used for large-scale learning problems. While there are many discussions on the speedup of these methods in the setting of optimization, we study the linear speedup in the setting of SLT. We develop on-average stability bounds for convex, strongly convex and nonconvex problems, and show how small training errors can improve stability. For strongly convex problems, our stability bounds are independent of the iteration number, which is new for the vanilla SGD in the sense of removing the Lipschitzness assumption. Our stability analysis implies optimal excess population risk bounds with both a linear speedup w.r.t. the batch size for minibatch SGD and a linear speedup w.r.t. the number of machines for local SGD.

There are several limitations of our work. A limitation of our work is that we do not get optimistic bounds for local SGD which are important to show the benefit of low noise. Another limitation is that
we only consider homogeneous setups in local SGD. It would be very interesting to extend the analysis to heterogeneous setups, i.e., where different local machines have different sets of examples. We will study these limitations in our future work.
