Homotopy invariants in small categories
I. Carcacía-Campos, E. Macías-Virgós and D. Mosquera-Lois

Abstract
. Tanaka introduced a notion of Lusternik Schnirelmann category , denoted ccat 𝒞 \mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{C} , of a small category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Among other properties, he proved an analog of Varadarajan’s theorem for fibrations, relating the LS-categories of the total space, the base and the fiber. In this paper we recall the notion of homotopic distance cD ⁡ ( F , G ) \mathrm{cD}(F,G) between two functors F , G : 𝒞 → 𝒟 F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D} , later introduced by us, which has ccat 𝒞 = cD ⁡ ( id 𝒞 , ∙ ) \mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{cD}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}},\bullet) as a particular case. We consider another particular case, the distance cD ⁡ ( p 1 , p 2 ) \mathrm{cD}(p_{1},p_{2}) between the two projections p 1 , p 2 : 𝒞 × 𝒞 → 𝒞 p_{1},p_{2}\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C} , which we call the categorical complexity of the small category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Moreover, we define the higher categorical complexity of a small category and we show that it can be characterized as a higher distance. We prove the main properties of those invariants. As a final result we prove a Varadarajan’s theorem for the homotopic distance for Grothendieck bi-fibrations between small categories.



Introduction

In the thirties, Lusternik and Schnirelmann introduced a homotopic invariant of manifolds which bounded from the below the number of critical points of any smooth function defined on them (see
[
4
]
). For a topological space
X
, this homotopic invariant is known as the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category of
X
, denoted
\mathop{\mathrm{cat}}(X)
.

Twenty years ago, M. Farber introduced the topological complexity of a topological space (
[
6
]
). It is a homotopic invariant which measures the difficulty in finding a motion planning algorithm on the space under consideration.

Both, the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category and the topological complexity are instances of a more general homotopic invariant called
homotopic distance between maps
, introduced by us in
[
13
]
. It provides a way to relate and extend several other homotopic invariants (see
[
14
]
).

Recently, it has grown the interest in developing topological and homotopic invariants in the setting of small categories. For example, this is the case of the Euler characteristic by Leinster (
[
2
,
11
]
) or the integration with respect to the Euler-Poincaré characteristic by Tanaka (
[
22
]
). The definition of homotopy between functors by M-J. Lee (
[
9
]
) made it possible for Tanaka (
[
21
]
) to extend the Lusternik-Schnirelmann category to the setting of small categories. Later, two of the authors extended the notion of homotopic distance to the context of categories (
[
12
]
).

In this work, we continue the study begun in
[
12
]
. First, we introduce a novel homotopic invariant for small categories: a notion of
higher homotopic distance
. Second, we study fibrations between small categories culminating with a result in the spirit of Varadarajan’s theorem (
[
23
]
) for the homotopic distance for Grothendieck bi-fibrations between small categories (Theorem
5.1
),
which can be seen both as a generalization of
[
21
, Theorem 4.5]
and as an extension of our previous work (
[
13
]
) to the context of categories.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
1
is devoted to fixing notation while recalling some preliminaries about homotopies and homotopic distance in the setting of small categories. Moreover, the novel notion of higher categorical distance is introduced (Definition
1.15
) and it is proved to be a particular case of a homotopic distance (Theorem
1.16
). In Section
2
bi-fibrations are presented in this setting from scratch. The exposition is intended to be self-contained so no previous knowledge from the reader is necessary on the topic. Section
4
addresses the equivalence of fibers whose base objects are connected by an arrow (Theorem
4.1
). In Section
5
, we state and prove the main result (Theorem
5.1
).

the set of arrows between the objects
e_{1},e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}

P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}

Pe

1.1.
Homotopies between functors

We recall the notion of homotopy between functors introduced by Lee (
[
9
,
10
]
).

Definition 1.1
.

The
interval category
\mathcal{I}_{m}
of length
m\geq 0
consists of
m+1
objects with zigzag arrrows,

0\longrightarrow 1\longleftarrow 2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow(\longleftarrow)m.

Given two small categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
we denote its product by
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
. Recall that the objects of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}
are pairs of objects in
\mathcal{C}
and objects in
\mathcal{D}
, and its arrows are products of arrows in
\mathcal{C}
and arrows in
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories. We say that
F
and
G
are
homotopic
, denoted by
F\simeq G
, if, for some
m\geq 0
, there exists a functor
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, called a homotopy (of length
m
), such that
H_{0}=F
and
H_{m}=G
.

Alternatively, the notion of homotopy between functors can be defined as follows.

Proposition 1.3
.

The functors
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
are homotopic if and only if there is a finite sequence of functors
F_{0},\dots,F_{m}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
, with
F_{0}=F
and
F_{m}=G
, such that for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
there is a natural transformation either between
F_{i}
and
F_{i+1}
or between
F_{i+1}
and
F_{i}
.

That both definitions are equivalent follows from the following Lemma.

Lemma 1.4
.

There is a natural transformation
\Phi\colon F\Rightarrow G
if and only if there exists a homotopy
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\mathcal{D}
such that
H_{0}=F
and
H_{1}=G
.

Proof.

For an object, we define

H(c,0)=Fc
,
H(c,1)=Gc
. For an arrow
f\colon c\to c^{\prime}
we define

H(f\times\mathrm{id}_{0})=Ff
,
H(f\times\mathrm{id}_{1})=Gf
and, for the only arrow
s\colon 0\to 1
in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, we define

H(c\times s)=\Phi_{c}\colon Fc\to Gc.

∎

The homotopy relation defined above is an equivalence relation. Also, it behaves well with respect to compositions, that is, if
F\simeq F^{\prime}
and
G\simeq G^{\prime}
, then
F\circ G\simeq F^{\prime}\circ G^{\prime}
whenever
F\circ F^{\prime}
and
G\circ G^{\prime}
make sense.

1.2.
Categorical distance between functors

We introduce now the categorical homotopic distance between functors (see
[
12
]
).

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category, a family
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of subcategories of
\mathcal{C}
if a geometric cover of
\mathcal{C}
if for every chain of arrows

c_{0}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{m}}c_{m}

in
\mathcal{C}
there is some
i\in I
such that the chain lies in
{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
.

There are at least three reasons for adopting this definition: first, just covering objects would leave the arrows between different subcategories uncovered; second, an arbitrary covering of the arrows would leave the compositions of arrows between different subcategories uncovered; third, geometric coverings correspond to coverings of the classifying space of the category, thus making easy to compare the categorical distance with the topological distance of the classifying space.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
F,G:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories. The homotopical distance
\mathrm{cD}(F,G)
beween
F
and
G
is the least positive integer
n\geq 0
such that there is geometric cover
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},...,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
such that
F|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}\simeq G|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}
for every
0\leq i\leq n
.

If there is no such cover we define
\mathrm{cD}(F,G)=\infty
.

We call
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},...,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\}
a geometric cover by
homotopy domains
for
F
and
G
.

1.3.
Properties

The following properties are obvious:

(1)

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)=\mathrm{cD}(G,F)
.

(2)

\mathrm{cD}(G,F)=0
if and only if
F\simeq G
.

(3)

If
F\simeq\widehat{F}
and
G\simeq\widehat{G}
then
\mathrm{cD}(F,G)=\mathrm{cD}(\widehat{F},\widehat{G})
.

We finish the section by introducing a notion of higher homotopic distance in the setting of small categories.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be two small categories and let
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a finite set of functors between them. The
higher homotopical distance

\mathrm{cD}(F_{1},...,F_{n})
is the least integer
m\geq 0
such that there is a geometric cover
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},...,{\mathcal{U}}_{m}\}
that satisfies
F_{i}|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{k}}\simeq F_{j}|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{k}}
for every
i,j\in\{1,...,n\}
and
0\leq k\leq m
.

If there is no such cover we define
\mathrm{cD}(F_{1},...,F_{n})=\infty
.

1.4.
LS-Category

The following notion of “categorical LS-category” is due to Tanaka
[
21
]
.

A subcategory
\mathcal{U}
of a small category
\mathcal{C}
is called
0
-categorical
if the inclusion functor
\iota\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is homotopic to a constant functor.
The geometric cover
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},...,{\mathcal{U}}_{m}\}
of the small category
\mathcal{C}
is called
categorical
if every
{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
is
0
-categorical.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. We define the (normalized) Lusternik-Schnirelmann category of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})
, as the least integer
n\geq 0
such that there is a categorical cover of
\mathcal{C}
.

If there is no such cover we define
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})
as
\infty
.

We state a result from
[
13
]
and provide a proof since we will make use of it in the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Proposition 1.9
.

For every connected small category
\mathcal{C}
we have that

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}},\bullet)

where
\bullet
is any constant functor.

Proof.

Let
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\dots,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\}
be a geometric cover satisfying that
n=\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})
and
\iota_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}\simeq\bullet_{i}
for some constant functors
\bullet_{i}
and every
i\in\{0,...,n\}
. We have to prove that the constant functors
\bullet_{i}
and
\bullet
are homotopic. But this follows because there is some arrow which connectes the objects
\bullet_{i}
and
\bullet
, and thsi arrow defines a natural transformation between the corresponding constant functors.

It is now evident that
\mathrm{id}|_{U_{i}}=\iota_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}\simeq\bullet
so
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\dots,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\}
is a homotopy domain for
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\bullet
. So we have proved that
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})\geq\mathrm{cD}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}},\bullet)
.

Alternatively if
{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\dots,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
are homotopy domains for
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\bullet
it follows that
\mathrm{id}|_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}=\iota_{{\mathcal{U}}_{i}}\simeq\bullet
, so
\mathrm{cD}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}},\bullet)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

Corollary 1.10
.

The small category
\mathcal{C}
is contractible if and only if

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})=0
.

Example 1.11
.

If the small category
\mathcal{C}
admits finite products, then
\mathcal{C}
is contractible.

Proof.

Fix an object
c_{0}
in
\mathcal{C}
and define the functor

c_{0}\times-\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
which sends the object
c
into
c_{0}\times c
, and the morphism
f\colon c_{1}\to c_{2}
into
\mathrm{id}_{c_{0}}\times f
. Denote by
c_{0}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
the constant functor.

Then there are natural transformations (that is, homotopies)

\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}\Leftarrow c_{0}\times-\Rightarrow c_{0}

given by the two projections

p_{1}\colon c_{0}\times c\to c_{0}

and
p_{2}\colon c_{0}\times c\to c
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category and let
c_{0}
be an object in
\mathcal{C}
. There are two functors
i_{1},i_{2}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
defined as follows:

•

For every object
c
in
\mathcal{C}
we have that
i_{1}(c)=(c,c_{0})
e
i_{2}(c)=i_{2}(c_{0},c)
.

•

Let
f
be a morphism in
\mathcal{C}
,then
i_{1}(f)=(f,\mathrm{id}_{c_{0}})
e
i_{2}(f)=(\mathrm{id}_{c_{0}},f)
.

Proposition 1.12

(
[

)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and
C
an objet in
\mathcal{C}
. We claim that:

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(i_{1},i_{2}).

1.5.
Categorical complexity

If
\mathcal{C}
is a small category, we define the diagonal functor
\Delta\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
as the functor that takes every object
c
to
\Delta(c)=(c,c)
and that takes every morphism
f
to
\Delta(f)=(f,f)
.

Definition 1.13
.

(1)

We say that the subcategory
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is a
Farber subcategory
if there is a functor
F\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
\Delta\circ F\simeq\iota_{U}
.

(2)

The (normalized)
categorical complexity
of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})
is the least integer
n\geq 0
such that there is a geometric cover
\{{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\dots,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
by Farber subcategories.

If there is no such cover we define
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})
as
\infty
.

Proposition 1.14

(
[
12
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category category, we have that

\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},p_{2}),

where
p_{1},p_{2}\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
are the projections.

1.6.
Higher categorical complexity

We sketch now a definition of a higher topological complexity, analogous to that existing in the topological setting (
[
18
]
).

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{n}
the product
\mathcal{C}\times\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\cdots}}\times\mathcal{C}
. The higher
n
-diagonal functor
\Delta_{n}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n}
is the functor that takes every object
X
in
\mathcal{C}
to
\Delta_{n}X=(X,...,X)
and that takes every morphism
f
to
\Delta_{n}f=(f,...,f)
.

Definition 1.15
.

(1)

We say that the subcategory
\iota\colon\Omega\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n}
is a
higher
n
-Farber subcategory
if there is a functor
F\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
wich is a right homotopy inverse of
\Delta_{n}
, that is,
\Delta_{n}\circ F\simeq i_{\Omega}
.

(2)

The
(normalized) higher
n
-categorical complexity
of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by

\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}_{n}(\mathcal{C})
is the least integer number
n\geq 0
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\Omega_{0},...,\Omega_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
by higher Farber subcategories.

The following result guarantees that higher categorical complexity can be seen as a homotopic distance.

Theorem 1.16
.

The higher topological complexity equals the higher categorical distance between the projections
p_{i}\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}\to\mathcal{C}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, that is,

\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}\nolimits_{n}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},...,p_{n}).

Proof.

(
\leq
)
Let
\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=m
. Let
\{U_{0},...,U_{m}\}
be a geometric cover of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
by homotopy domains, that is, subcategories
U_{k}
such that
p_{i}|_{U_{k}}\simeq p_{j}|_{U_{k}}
for all
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. We will see that they are also higher Farber subcategories.

Indeed, for every
U_{k}
we have that the first projection
p_{1}\colon U_{k}\to\mathcal{C}
verifies
\Delta_{n}\circ p_{1}=\iota_{U_{k}}
. To check that, for every
i\in\{1,...,n\}
consider the homotopy
H_{i}\colon U_{k}\times\mathcal{I}_{m_{i}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
between
p_{1}|_{U_{i}}
and
p_{i}|_{U_{i}}
. We can normalize all the homotopies by taking
m=\max\{m_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and extending
H_{i}\colon U_{i}\times\mathcal{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
by identities if
j\geq m_{i}
. Now we can define a new homotopy

G:U_{k}\times\mathcal{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}

as

G(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n},j)=(H_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),H_{2}(c_{1},...,c_{n},j),...,H_{n}(c_{1},...,c_{n},j).

Let us check that
G
is a homotopy between
\Delta\circ p_{1}
and the inclusion
\iota_{U_{k}}
:

\displaystyle\quad G(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},0)

\displaystyle=(H_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},0),\dots,H_{n}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},0))

\displaystyle=(p_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),\dots,p_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}))

\displaystyle=(c_{1},\dots,c_{1})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{n}p_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),

and

\displaystyle\quad G(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n},m)

\displaystyle=G(H_{1}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},m),\dots,H_{n}(c_{1}\dots,c_{n},m))

\displaystyle=(p_{1}(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}),\dots,p_{n}(c_{1},...,c_{n}))

\displaystyle=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}).

This shows that
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}\nolimits_{n}(\mathcal{C})\leq m
.

(
\geq
)
Now, let
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}\nolimits_{n}(\mathcal{C})=m
. Let
\{\Omega_{0},...,\Omega_{m}\}
be a geometric cover of
\mathcal{C}^{n}
by Farber subcategories. Let us fix some index
k\in\{0,...,n\}
and let us see that
p_{i}|_{U_{k}}\simeq p_{j}|_{U_{k}}
for every
i,j\in\{1,...,n\}
.

Since
U_{k}
is a Farber subcategory, there is a functor
F\colon U_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
\Delta\circ F\simeq\iota_{U_{k}}
. Hence, there is a homotopy
H\colon U_{k}\times\mathcal{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{n}
such that
H_{0}=\Delta_{n}\circ F
and
H_{m}=\iota_{U_{k}}
. We take the functor
K\colon U_{k}\times\mathcal{I}_{2m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}

given by

K(c_{1},\dotsc_{n},l)=\begin{cases}p_{i}\circ H(c_{1},...,c_{n},m-l)&\text{ if }\quad 0\leq l\leq m\\
p_{j}\circ H(c_{1},...,c_{n},l-m)&\text{if}\quad m\leq l\leq 2m\\
\end{cases}

The functor
K
is well-defined because, for
l=m
:

\displaystyle\quad p_{i}\circ H(X_{1},...,X_{n},0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{i}\circ\Delta\circ F(c_{1},...,c_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{i}(F(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),\dots,F(c_{1},\dots,X_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{j}\circ\Delta\circ F(c_{1},...,c_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{j}\circ H(X_{1},...,X_{n},0).

Moreover
K
is a homotopy between
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
:

\displaystyle K(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},0)=

\displaystyle p_{i}\circ H(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{i}\circ\iota_{U_{k}}(c1,\dots,c_{n})=p_{i}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}),

and

\displaystyle K(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},2m)=

\displaystyle p_{j}\circ H(c_{1},\dots,c_{n},m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{j}\circ i_{U_{k}}(c1,\dots,c_{n})=p_{j}(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}).

We have proven that
\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},...,p_{n})\leq m
.
∎

1.7.
Properties

We recall several basic properties of the categorical distance. They serve to give short proofs of many results.

Proposition 1.17

(
[
12
, Propositions 7 and 8]
)
.

(1)

Let
\mathcal{C}
,
D
and
\mathcal{B}
be three small categories and let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
and
H:\mathfrak{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be three functors. We have that:

\mathrm{cD}(H\circ F,H\circ G)\leq\mathrm{cD}(F,G).

(2)

Analogously, let
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{B}
be three small categories and let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
and
H:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be three functors. We have that:

\mathrm{cD}(H\circ F,H\circ G)\leq\mathrm{cD}(F,G).

Corollary 1.18
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a connected small category. Then we have that

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C}).

Proof.

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}},C_{0})=\mathrm{cD}(p_{1}\circ i_{1},p_{2}\circ i_{1})\leq\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},p_{2})=\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C}).

∎

In what follows we note that category and categorical complexity are in some sense dual and extreme cases of categorical distance.

Corollary 1.19
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories, Then we have that

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{D}).

Proof.

We define the functor
(F,G)\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
such that
p_{1}\circ(F,G)=F
and
p_{2}\circ(F,G)=G
. Therefore we have that

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)=cD(p_{1}\circ(F,G),p_{2}\circ(F,G))\leq\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},p_{2})=\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{D}).

∎

Proposition 1.20

(
[
12
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories, Then

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C}).

2.1.
Cartesian arrows

Let
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
be a functor.

Definition 2.1
.

The morphism
\phi\in\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e_{2})
is
cartesian
(with respect to
P
) if, for every arrow
\beta\in\mathcal{E}(e,e_{2})
and every arrow

\overline{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}(Pe,Pe_{1})
such that
P\phi\circ\overline{\alpha}=P\beta
,
there exists a unique arrow
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}(e,e_{1})
such that
\phi\circ\alpha=\beta
and
P\alpha=\overline{\alpha}

(see Diagram (
1
))
.

(1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\beta}

The name
cartesian
seems to have its origin in the following characterization, whose proof is left to the reader:

Proposition 2.2
.

The morphism
\phi
is cartesian if and only if the following commutative square in the category of
Sets
is a pullback:

Example 2.3
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
the identity functor. Then any arrow in
\mathcal{C}
is cartesian.
This is obvious because
P\alpha=\alpha
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{C}\to\bullet
be the constant functor. An arrow
\phi
in
\mathcal{C}
is
P
-cartesian if and only if
\phi
is an isomorphism.

Let us check it. From the diagram

we obtain an arrow such that
\phi\alpha=\mathrm{id}_{e_{2}}
. Now,
\phi(\alpha\phi)=\phi
means that we have the diagram

Example 2.5
.

Posets can be viewed as small categories, with an arrow existing between two objects if and only if
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
. Functors between posets are the order preserving maps
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
. For such a functor, a map
e_{1}\leq e_{2}
is cartesian if and only if it verifies the following condition:
if
e\leq e_{2}
and
Pe\leq e_{1}
then
e\leq e_{1}
.

This condition ressembles the definition of a
down beat point

[
1
]
. In fact. down beat points are cartesian for any functor.

For a study of fibrations in the setting of posets we refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

2.2.
Fibrations

Definition 2.6
.

The functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
is a
fibration
if for any arrow
\overline{\phi}\in\mathcal{B}(b_{1},b_{2})
and any object
e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
such that
Pe_{2}=b_{2}
, there exists a cartesian arrow
\phi\in\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e_{2})
such that
P\phi=\overline{\phi}
.

The arrow
\phi
is called a
cartesian lift
of
\overline{\phi}
with codomain
e_{2}
.

Definition 2.7
.

If
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
is a fibration, we define the
fiber
of
P
over
b\in\mathcal{B}
as the subcategory of
\mathcal{E}
with objects
e\in E
such that
Pe=b
, and with arrows

\nu\in\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e_{2})
such that
P\nu=\mathrm{id}_{b}
. These arrows are called
vertical
arrows.

The cartesian lift of a given
\overline{\phi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
with a given codomain
e_{2}
is unique, up to a unique vertical arrow. This follows from the unicity in the definition of cartesian arrow.

By using the axiom of choice, we can take a particular lift, which will be denoted by

\mathrm{Cart}(\overline{\phi},e_{2})\colon\bar{\phi}^{*}e_{2}\to e_{2}.

This particular choice defines a functor
{\bar{\phi}}^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}\to\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
, where the image of a vertical arrow
\nu\in\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
is given by the unique arrow
{\overline{\phi}}^{*}\nu
making the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\phi}^{*}e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\phi}^{*}e_{2}^{\prime}\ \ }
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \ e_{2}^{\prime}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\phi}^{*}\nu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Cart}(\overline{\phi},e_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Cart}(\overline{\phi},e_{2}^{\prime})}

It corresponds to the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overline{\phi}}^{*}e_{2}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\overline{\phi}}^{*}e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Cart}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{id}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{id}}

The functoriality of
{\overline{\phi}}^{*}
follows again from unicity. It is called the
pullback functor
.

Example 2.8
.

Every group
G
can be considered as a category with one object,
where the arrows are the elements
g\in G
, and the composition is given by the
operation in
G
. A group homomorphism
F\colon H\to G
can be considered as a functor.
An arrow in
H
is always cartesian; hence
F
is a fibration if and only if is surjective. The fiber is the kernel.

Example 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
be the category whose objects are the arrows in
\mathcal{C}
, and whose arrows are the commutative squares. The
codomain
functor
P\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\to\mathcal{C}
is a fibration if and only if
\mathcal{C}
has enough pullbacks. The cartesian arrows are the pullbacks in
\mathcal{C}
. It is called the
fundamental fibration
.

We introduce another example of a fibration in the setting of locally small categories.

Example 2.10
.

Let
U\colon\textbf{{Top }}\to\textbf{{Sets }}
be the forgetful functor from topological spaces to sets. It associates to each topological space
X
its underlying set
UX
, and to each continuous map the set map itself. It is a fibration. The
U
-cartesian maps are the continuous maps
f\colon e_{1}\to e_{2}
such that the topology on
e_{1}
is the
initial
topology, that is, the smallest (coarsest) topology making
f
continuous.

2.3.
Op-cartesian arrows and op-fibrations

Let
P^{\mathrm{op}}\colon\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{op}}
be the opposite functor of
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 2.11
.

The arrow
\varphi\colon e_{1}\to e_{2}
in
\mathcal{E}
is op-cartesian for the functor
P
if the opposite arrow
\varphi^{\mathrm{op}}
in
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{op}}
is cartesian for
P^{\mathrm{op}}
. Explicitly, that means that for any given
\beta\in\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e)
and any given
\overline{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}(Pe_{2},Pe)
such that
\overline{\alpha}\circ P\varphi=P\beta
, there exists a unique
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}(e_{2},e)
such that
\alpha\circ\varphi=\beta
and
P\alpha=\overline{\alpha}
, as in the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Pe_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\alpha}}

Definition 2.12
.

A functor
P\colon\ E\to\mathcal{B}
is an
op-fibration
if for any map
\overline{\varphi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}
, and for any object
e_{1}
in
\mathcal{E}
with
Pe_{1}=b_{1}
, there exists an op-cartesian arrow
\varphi\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
such that
P\varphi=\overline{\varphi}
.

Again, this
op-cartesian lifting
is unique up to a unique vertical isomorphism. Then we can choose some particular lifting

\mathrm{opCart}(\overline{\varphi},e_{1})\colon e_{1}\to\overline{\varphi}_{*}e_{1}

so defining a functor

\overline{\varphi}_{*}\colon\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}\to\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}

bewteen the fibers.

Example 2.13
.

The codomain functor
P\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\to\mathcal{C}
of Example
2.9
is always an op-fibration.

Example 2.14
.

Analogously, the
domain
functor
P\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\to\mathcal{C}
is an op-fibration if and only if
\mathcal{C}
has enough push-outs.

Definition 2.15
.

We say that the functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
is a
bi-fibration
if it is both a fibration and an op-fibration.

We prefer this terminology instead of “fibration” and “cofibration”, see
[
16
]
for a discussion.

Example 2.16
.

The forgetful functor
U\colon\textbf{{Top }}\to\textbf{{Sets }}
from topological spaces to sets is an op-fibration. Op-cartesian maps are the continuous maps
e_{1}\to e_{2}
where
e_{2}
has the
quotient topology
.

Example 2.17
.

Let
\varphi\colon K\to K^{\prime}
be a simplicial map of simplicial complexes.
Let
\mathcal{X}(\varphi)\colon\mathcal{X}(K)\to\mathcal{X}(K^{\prime})
be the corresponding map between the associated face posets. A poset can be considered as a small category with the obvioius arrows. Then the functor
\varphi
is a fibration, but it is not an op-fibration (
[
25
, Example 13.5]
).

Example 2.18
.

Take a base category
\mathcal{B}
and another category
\mathcal{C}
, then the first projection
P_{1}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{B}
is a bi-fibration. In fact, given an arrow
\overline{\phi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
in
\mathcal{C}
, and an object
(b_{2},c)
in
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{C}
, the morphism
\phi=\overline{\phi}\times\mathrm{id}_{c}\colon(b_{1},c)\to(b_{2},c)
is a cartesian lifting. In order to prove it we only must take into account the diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(b_{1},c)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(b,c^{\prime})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(b_{2},c)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w\times v}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u\times v}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\phi}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces w}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}

Analogously,
P_{1}
is an op-fibration.

2.4.
Further examples

Example 2.19
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the category with objects the integers
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and a unique morphism
m\rightarrow n
if and only if
m\leq n
.

\cdots\to-1\to 0\to 1\to\cdots;

and let
\mathcal{B}
be the monoid of the natural numbers
n\geq 0
as a category.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces n\in\mathbb{N}}

The functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
with
P(n)=\bullet
and
P(m\rightarrow n)=n-m
is a fibration. In fact, let us consider
n\colon\bullet\rightarrow\bullet
a morphism in
\mathcal{B}
and
m
any object in
\mathcal{E}
; then the morphism
m-n\rightarrow m
covers
n
. Moreover it is
P
-cartesian as indicated in the following commutative diagrams:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m-n}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces l}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m-l-n}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m-l}

Furthermore,
P
is an op-fibration by a similar argument.

Example 2.20
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the category with objects the integer numbers
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and ziz-zag arrows:

\cdots\longleftarrow-2\longrightarrow-1\longleftarrow 0\longrightarrow 1\longleftarrow 2\longrightarrow\cdots

and let
\mathcal{B}
be the following category

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{1}}

It is easy to show that the functor
P(n)=\overline{n}\mod 4

is a fibration and an op-fibration.

3.
Lifting properties

The lifting properties of Grothendieck fibrations were considered by Gray in
[
7
]
.

Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}

the
n
-
chain category
generated by the following diagram:

0\longrightarrow 1\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow n.

In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{1}
denotes the category
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s}}{{\longrightarrow}}1
consisting of two objects
and one non-identity morphism
s
.

Gray proved that the functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
is a fibration if and only if homotopies have cartesian liftings. Since we represent a natural transformation ending at
P\circ G
as a functor
\eta\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}
where
\eta_{1}=P\circ G
, we can state the following, which improves
[
21
, Prop.4.2]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

The functor
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
is a fibration if and only for any category
\mathcal{C}
and for any functor
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}
such that
H_{1}=H\circ i_{1}=P\circ G
there exists a functor
\widetilde{H}\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}
such that
P\circ\widetilde{H}=H
and
\widetilde{H}_{1}=\widetilde{H}\circ i_{1}=G
, as in the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{H}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}

and moreover
\tilde{H}(f\times s)
is a cartesian arrow for
P

for any arrow
f
in
\mathcal{C}
.

Here,
i_{1}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{I}_{1}
is the functor sending the object
c
into
(c,1)
, and the arrow
f\colon c_{1}\to c_{2}
into
f\times\mathrm{id}_{1}
, and
s
is the only arrow
0\to 1
in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.

Proof.

Obviously we must define
\widetilde{H}_{1}=G
. The problem is how to define
\widetilde{H}_{0}
in order to have a functor. To do that we have to use the definition of fibered category. As we know, if we have an arrow
\overline{\phi}
in
\mathcal{B}
and we have an object
e_{2}
in the fiber of the codomain
b_{2}
, we can lift the arrow into a cartesian morphism
\mathrm{Cart}(\overline{\phi},e_{2})\colon{\overline{\phi}}^{*}e_{2}\to e_{2}
. In particular, we can choose

\mathrm{Cart}(\mathrm{id}_{b},e)=\mathrm{id}_{e}
.

Now, if we have the arrow
H(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s)\colon H(c,0)\rightarrow H(c,1)
in
\mathcal{B}
and the object
G(X)\in\mathcal{E}_{H(c,1)}
, there is a cartesian morphism

\mathrm{Cart}H(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s))\colon H(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s)^{*}G(c)\rightarrow G(c),

so we define

\widetilde{H}(c,0)=H(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s)^{*}G(c)

and

\widetilde{H}(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s)=\mathrm{Cart}H(\mathrm{id}_{c}\times s).

Finally, for every morphism
f\colon c_{1}\rightarrow c_{2}
in
\mathcal{C}
we define
\widetilde{H}(f\times s)
as the unique cartesian arrow filling the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{H}(c_{1},0)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{H}(c_{1},1)=Gc_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{H}(c_{2},0)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widetilde{H}(c_{2},1)=Gc_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{H}(\mathrm{id}_{c_{1}}\times s)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{H}(f\times\mathrm{id}_{1})=Gf}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{H}(\mathrm{id}_{c_{2}}\times s)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H(c_{1},0)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H(c_{1},1)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H(c_{2},0)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H(c_{2},1)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(f\times\mathrm{id}_{0})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(\mathrm{id}_{c_{1}}\times s)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(f\times\mathrm{id}_{1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H(\mathrm{id}_{c_{2}}\times s)}

For the converse statement, let
\overline{\phi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
in
\mathrm{B}
be an arrow and consider the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet\times\mathcal{I}_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{H}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}

where
g(\bullet)=e_{2}
and
H(\mathrm{id}_{\bullet}\times s)=\overline{\phi}
. Then the map
\phi={\tilde{H}}(\mathrm{id}_{\bullet}\times s)
is cartesian and verifies that
P\phi=\overline{\phi}
.
This ends the proof.∎

4.
Homotopic fiber

The next result is a crucial one. It proves that in a bi-fibration, two objects
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
in the base which are connected by an arrow
\overline{\varphi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
, have fibers which are homotopically equivalent as small categories.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
21
, Proposition 4.4]
)
.

Let
P:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a bi-fibration. If
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are two objects in
\mathrm{B}
such that there is a morphism
u\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
, then there is a categorical equivalence between the fibers
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
.

Proof.

Remember that in a bi-fibration we have both the pushforward functor
u_{*}\colon\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}\to\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
and the pullback functor
u^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}\to\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
.

We defined
u_{*}
as follows: for every object
e_{1}
in the fiber
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
there is a unique object
u_{*}{e_{1}}
in
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
such that
\mathrm{opCart}(u,e_{1})\colon e_{1}\to u_{*}e_{1}
is the unique op-cartesian lift of
u
.

Alternatively, for every
e_{2}
in
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
there is a unique object
u^{*}e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
such that
\mathrm{Cart}(u,e_{2})\colon u^{*}e_{2}\to e_{2}
is the unique cartesian lift of
u
.

Now in order to show tha
u_{*}
and
u^{*}
induce a categorical equivalence it is enough to show that for every object
e_{2}
in
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
there are natural arrows
\alpha
and
\beta
as in the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u^{*}e_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u_{*}(u^{*}e_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Cart}(u,e_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{opCart}(u,u^{*}e_{2})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{id}_{b_{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}

and that their compositions are the identities because
\mathrm{Cart}(u,e_{2})
and
\mathrm{opCart}(u,e_{1})
are a cartesian and op-cartesian morphism (respectively).
Thus, we have a natural isomorphism between the functors
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}}
and
u_{*}\circ u^{*}
.

Analogously we have another natural isomorphism between
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}}
and
u^{*}\circ u^{*}
using the followings diagrams:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u_{*}e_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u^{*}(u_{*}e_{1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{opCart}(u,e_{1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Cart}(u,u_{*}e_{1})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{id}_{b_{1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}

∎

A consequence of the latter theorem is that we can speak about the homotopic invariants of “the fiber” of a bi-fibration.

Remark 1
.

In the proof of Theorem
4.1
we implicitly assumed that both fibers
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
are non empty. This might not be true. However, the Theorem is still true, because for a bi-fibration, if two objects
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
in the base are connected by an arrow, say
\overline{\phi}\colon b_{1}\to b_{2}
, then either
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}=\emptyset=\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
or

\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}\neq\emptyset\neq\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
simultaneously.

In fact, assume first that
P
is only a fibration. If
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}=\emptyset

the pullback functor is not defined, because we have no codomain
e_{2}
to lift the arrow
\overline{\phi}
.
Since a fibration
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
may not be surjective-on-objects (see Example
4.2
), it may happen that
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
is empty, while
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
is not.

However, for a fibration, if
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}\neq\emptyset
then
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}\neq\emptyset
too, because to each codomain
e_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}
we associate the domain
{\overline{\phi}}^{*}e_{2}
of
\mathrm{Cart}(\phi,e_{2})\in\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}
.

Dually, for an op-fibration, if
\mathcal{E}_{b_{1}}\neq\emptyset
then
\mathcal{E}_{b_{2}}\neq\emptyset
, because to each domain
e_{1}
we associate the codomain
{\overline{\phi}}_{*}e_{1}
of
\mathrm{opCart}(\overline{\phi},e_{1})
.

As a consequence, in a bi-fibration there are no arrows connecting objects in the image with objects outside the image, or conversely.

Example 4.2
.

A fibration which is not surjective-on-objects is for instance the constant functor

P\colon\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\mathcal{I}_{1}
with
P(0)=P(1)=0
, and
P(s)=\mathrm{id}_{0}
. We have
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{I}_{1}
while
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\emptyset
.
This shows that
P
is not an op-fibration. In fact, given the arrow
s\colon 0\to 1
and the domain
1\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
there is no op-cartesian lift of
s
.

Notice that the arrow
s\colon 0\to 1
can not be lifted to any cartesian arrow, but this does not contradict the definition of fibration (there are no possible codomains).

5.
Varadarajan’s theorem

In the classical LS-category theory of topological spaces, Varadarajan
[
23
]
proved a formula relating the categories of the fiber, the base and the total space for a Hurewicz fibration. We generalized this result for the homotopic distance, in
[
13
, Theorem 6.1]
.

In
[
21
, Theorem 4.5]
, Tanaka proved an analogous result for a bi-fibration
P\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}
with fiber
\mathcal{F}
between small categories, with path-connected base, namely

(2)

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{E}+1\leq(\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{B}+1)\cdot(\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{F}+1).

Recall that by Corollary
4.1
, if the base category
\mathcal{B}
of a bi-fibration
P:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
is path-connected, then every two fibers are homotopy equivalent.
We will refer to any of them as (the homotopy type of) “the fiber
\mathcal{F}
of the bi-fibration”.

We will both extend our
[
13
, Theorem 6.1]
to the context of bi-fibrations of small categories and generalize Tanaka’s result, as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(F,\overline{F})
and
(G,\overline{G})
be two morphisms between the bi-fibrations
P\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
and
P^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
be path-connected.
Let
b
be an object in
\mathcal{B}
such that
F(b)=G(b)=b^{\prime}
and let
F_{b},G_{b}\colon\mathcal{E}_{b}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{b^{\prime}}
be the induced functors between the fibers. Then

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)+1\leq(\mathrm{cD}(F_{b},G_{b})+1)\cdot(\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{B})+1).

Proof.

We assume that
\mathrm{cD}(F_{b},G_{b})
and
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}\mathcal{B}
are both finite, otherwise the result is trivial.

Let
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{B})=m
, with
\{U_{0},...,U_{m}\}
a categorical cover of
\mathcal{B}
, and let
\mathrm{cD}(F_{b},G_{b})=n
, with
\{V_{0},...,V_{n}\}
a covering of
\mathcal{E}_{b}
by homotopy domains for
F_{b}
and
G_{b}
.

For every
i\in\{0,...,m\}
we have a homotopy
C^{i}\colon U_{i}\times\mathcal{I}_{k_{i}}\to\mathcal{B}
between the inclusion
U_{i}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}
and a constant functor
\bullet_{i}
for some object
\ast_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is connected we can assume that all
\bullet_{i}
is the same object
\ast
for all
i
(see the proof of Proposition
1.9
).

Let
P^{-1}(U_{i})
be the subcategory of
\mathcal{E}
whose objects are the objects
e\in\mathcal{E}
with
Pe\in U_{i}
, and whose arrows are the arrows
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}(e_{1},e_{2})
such that
P\alpha
is an arrow in
U_{i}
.

By the homotopy lifting property applied to the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{-1}(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{-1}(U)\times\mathcal{I}_{k_{i}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\times\mathcal{I}_{k_{i}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{C}^{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P\times\mathrm{id}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{i}}

we have a homotopy
\widetilde{C}^{i}:U_{i}\times\mathcal{I}_{k_{i}}\to\mathcal{E}
such that
{\widetilde{C}^{i}}_{0}
is the inclusion
P^{-1}(U_{i})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{E}
and
{\widetilde{C}^{i}}_{k_{i}}
lies inside the fiber
\mathcal{E}_{b}
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we define

W_{i,j}=P^{-1}(U_{i})\cap({\widetilde{C}^{i}}_{k_{i}})^{-1}(V_{j}).

We claim that
\{W_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i\leq m,0\leq j\leq n}
is a geometric cover of
\mathcal{E}
such that each
W_{i,j}
is a homotopy domain for
F
and
G
.

(1)
\{W_{i,j}\}

is a geometric cover of
\mathcal{E}
.

Let

\mathcal{C}\colon C_{1}\longrightarrow C_{2}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow C_{l}

be a chain in
\mathcal{E}
. Then we obtain the chain

P(\mathcal{C})\colon P(C_{1})\longrightarrow P(C_{2})\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow P(C_{l})

in
\mathcal{B}
. Since
\{U_{0},...,U_{m}\}
is a geometric cover of
\mathcal{B}
, there is some
i
such that the chain
P\mathcal{C})
lies in
U_{i}
, so the chain
\mathcal{C}
lies in
P^{-1}(U_{i})
. Moreover, the functor
{\widetilde{C}^{i}}_{k_{i}}
is defined in
\mathcal{C}
, hence we have a new chain
{\widetilde{C}}^{i}_{k_{i}}(\mathcal{C})
that lies in the fiber
\mathcal{E}_{b}
. Now, we know that
\{V_{j}\}
is a geometric cover of
F_{b}
, so
{\widetilde{C}}^{i}_{k_{i}}(\mathcal{C})
lies in some
V_{j}
.
We conclude that
\mathcal{C}
is in
W_{i,j}
.

(2) Each
W_{i,j}
is a homotopy domain for
F
and
G
.

For the sake of simplicity we change the notations as follows:
U=U_{i}
,
\widetilde{C}=\widetilde{C}^{i}
,
k=k_{i}
,
V=V_{j}
.

Let
K\colon V\times\mathcal{I}_{l}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{b}
be a homotopy between
F|_{V}
and
G|_{V}
and let
\iota\colon\mathcal{E}_{b}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{E}
be the inclusion of the fiber into the total category
\mathcal{E}
. The homotopy that we need is the functor

H:W_{i,j}\times\mathcal{I}_{k+l+k}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

given by

H(c,n)=\begin{cases}F\widetilde{C}(c,n)&\text{if}\quad 0\leq n\leq k\cr\iota K(\widetilde{C}_{k}c,n-k)&\text{if}\quad k\leq n\leq k+l\cr G\widetilde{C}(c,k+l+k-n)&\text{if}\quad k+l\leq n\leq k+l+k\cr\end{cases}.

It only remains to check that
H
is well defined and that it is the wanted homotopy:

\displaystyle H(c,0)=

\displaystyle F\widetilde{C}(c,0)=Fc\text{ since }\widetilde{C}_{0}\text{ is the inclusion},

\displaystyle\iota K(\widetilde{C}_{k}c,0)=

\displaystyle\iota F(\widetilde{C}_{k}c)=F\widetilde{C}(c,k),

\displaystyle\iota K(\widetilde{C}_{k}c,l)=

\displaystyle\iota G\widetilde{C}_{k}c=G\widetilde{C}(c,k),

\displaystyle H(c,k+l+k)=

\displaystyle G\widetilde{C}(c,0)=Gc\text{ since }\widetilde{C}_{0}\text{ is the inclusion}.

This proves that
\mathrm{cD}(F,G)\leq m+n
, as stated.
∎
