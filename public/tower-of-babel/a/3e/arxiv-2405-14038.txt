FLIPHAT: Joint Differential Privacy for High Dimensional Sparse Linear Bandits
Sunrit Chakraborty *, Saptarshi Roy *, Debabrota Basu

Abstract
High dimensional sparse linear bandits serve as an efficient model for sequential decision-making problems (e.g. personalized medicine), where high dimensional features (e.g. genomic data) on the users are available, but only a small subset of them are relevant. Motivated by data privacy concerns in these applications, we study the joint differentially private high dimensional sparse linear bandits, where both rewards and contexts are considered as private data. First, to quantify the cost of privacy, we derive a lower bound on the regret achievable in this setting. To further address the problem, we design a computationally efficient bandit algorithm, F orgetfu L I terative P rivate HA rd T hresholding (FLIPHAT). Along with doubling of episodes and episodic forgetting, FLIPHAT deploys a variant of Noisy Iterative Hard Thresholding (N-IHT) algorithm as a sparse linear regression oracle to ensure both privacy and regret-optimality. We show that FLIPHAT achieves optimal regret in terms of privacy parameters ϵ , δ \epsilon,\delta , context dimension d d , and time horizon T T up to a linear factor in model sparsity and logarithmic factor in d d . We analyze the regret by providing a novel refined analysis of the estimation error of N-IHT, which is of parallel interest.

INTRODUCTION

Multi-armed bandits, in brief
bandits
, constitute an archetypal model of sequential decision-making under partial information
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2018
)
. In bandits, a learner sequentially chooses one decision from a collection of actions (or arms). In return, it observes only the reward corresponding to the chosen action with the goal of maximizing the accumulated rewards over a time horizon.
Additionally, the available decisions at any point of interaction might depend on some side information about the actions, encoded as a feature vector. In linear contextual bandits (LCB)
(
Auer, 2002
;
Abe et al., 2003
)
, the learner observes such a feature vector (aka
context
) for each action before taking a decision, and the rewards are generated by a linear function of the chosen context and an unknown parameter vector.
LCBs are widely studied and deployed in real-life applications, such as online advertising
(
Schwartz et al., 2017
)
, recommendation systems
(
Silva et al., 2022
)
, investment portfolio design
(
Silva et al., 2022
)
, and personalized medicine
(
Peng et al., 2021
)
.

In many of these applications, the personal information used as contexts often has a large dimension, which can be modeled by viewing the contexts as high-dimensional vectors. This is relevant in the modern big data era, where the applications usually deal with many features. However, the established regret lower bound in LCB polynomially depends on the context dimension
(
Dani et al., 2008
;
Chu et al., 2011
)
. Thus, additional structural conditions, such as sparsity, are needed to be enforced to make the problem tractable. Sparsity aligns with the idea that among these features, possibly only a small subset of them are relevant to generate the reward. This setting is called the
high-dimensional sparse linear contextual bandit
(SLCB). Recently, SLCB has received significant attention
(
Wang et al., 2018
;
Bastani and Bayati, 2020
;
Hao et al., 2020
;
Chakraborty et al., 2023
)
and have been useful in practical applications
(
Miao and Chao, 2022
;
Ren and Zhou, 2024
)
.

Data Privacy in SLCB.
We observe that the applications of SLCB, e.g. recommender systems and personalized medicines, involve sensitive data of individuals. This naturally invokes data privacy concerns and requires development and analysis of data privacy-preserving SLCB algorithms.

Example 1.
One of the earliest successful application of linear contextual bandits is personalized news article recommendation and its validation on Yahoo! Today Module by
Li et al., 2010
. In the dataset collected from Yahoo! platform, each user is represented by a 1193-dimensional features but for a successful deployment they are reduced to 5 dimensions.
The feature of every user include their gender, age, geographical address, and even behavorial quantifiers.
These are sensitive data as per GDPR
(
Voigt and Von dem Bussche, 2017
)
and California Consumer Privacy Act
(
Harding et al., 2019
)
. Thus, protecting data privacy in this problem, and designing efficient and private SLCB algorithms are fundamentally necessary.

Example 2.
A recent example of deploying SLCB with user-data is optimizing click-through rates in the Tencent search advertising
dataset
(
Tencent, 2012
)
. Though each context is 509256 dimensional, it lends itself to a sparse structure
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
.
Here, also the context incorporates sensitive information, like gender, age etc., which are protected by privacy laws. Thus, private SLCBs are a natural requirement here.

Example 3.
Another application of SLCB is a mobile health application that recommends a personalized treatment plan (actions) to each patient (user) based on her personal information, such as age, weight, height, medical history etc.
(
Tewari and Murphy, 2017
;
Killian et al., 2023
)
. Here, for every user and personalized treatment plan contexts are generally obtained through a feature representation, where the dimension is often large but there are only fewer relevant features. The algorithm uses patients’ data including treatment outcomes and medical contexts of patients, which are sensitive information as per GDPR, CCPA, and HIPAA
(
Annas, 2003
)
. Hence, this is a natural application that motivates the study of efficient and privacy-preserving SLCB algorithms.

Related Work: Differential Privacy in Bandits.
Problems like the ones in the aforementioned examples motivate us to study data-privacy preserving SLCB algorithms. Specifically, we follow Differential Privacy (DP)
(
Dwork, 2006
)
as the data privacy framework. DP aims to ensure that while looking into the output of an algorithm, an adversary is unable to conclude whether a specific user was in the input dataset. DP has emerged as the gold standard of data privacy. It has been extensively studied in various domains with the goal of safe-guarding sensitive data from adversarial attacks
(
Dwork et al., 2014
;
Abadi et al., 2016
;
McMahan et al., 2018
)
.
Impact of DP and designing efficient DP-preserving algorithms have also been studied for different settings of bandits, such as stochastic bandits
(
Mishra and Thakurta, 2015
;
Tossou and Dimitrakakis, 2016
;
Sajed and Sheffet, 2019
;
Hu and Hegde, 2022
;
Azize and Basu, 2022a
)
, adversarial bandits
(
Tossou and Dimitrakakis, 2017
)
and LCB
(
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
;
Neel and Roth, 2018
;
Hanna et al., 2024
;
Azize and Basu, 2024
)
.

The literature also reveals that different notions of DP are possible in bandits depending on what we consider as the sensitive input to be saved and what are the public output. In comparison to the supervised learning literature, additional difficulties arise due to the complex interaction between the policy employed by the learner, and the stochastic nature of the contexts and rewards.
Local

(
Duchi et al., 2013
)
and
global

(
Basu et al., 2019a
)
definitions of DP are extended to bandits, where we aim to keep the rewards generated during interactions private.
In local DP, users already send to the learner privatized rewards as feedback. In global DP, the learner gets true rewards but it publishes actions in a way that the rewards stay private.
For LCBs, compared to reward privacy,
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
study a stronger form of privacy that aims to keep both the rewards and the contexts private while publishing the actions. This is called Joint Differential Privacy (JDP), which is further strengthened by
Vietri et al., 2020
.
In this paper,
we follow
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP of
(
Vietri et al., 2020
)
as our privacy framework
, since it best aligns with our interest of protecting both users’ information and the outcomes collected over all the interactions.

We observe that though there are multiple algorithms for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP (Definition
2.1
) satisfying LCBs, they are tailored for the low dimensional setting and scales polynomially with the context dimension
d
. In contrast, the study of JDP in SLCB setting is still unexplored. This leads us to two questions:

1.
What is the cost of ensuring
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP in high-dimensional sparse linear contextual bandits?

2.
Can we design a privacy-preserving SLCB algorithm that achieves the minimal cost due to
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP?

Contributions.
Our study of these questions leads us to the following contributions.

1.
Regret Lower Bound
:
We derive a
problem independent
regret lower bound for SLCBs with
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP. Our lower bound demonstrates a phase transition between the hardness of the problem depending on the privacy level, similar to
(
Azize and Basu, 2024
)
(reward-private low-dimension LCB).
Compared to linear dependence on
d

(
Azize and Basu, 2024
)
, our lower bound depends linearly on
\sqrt{s^{*}}\log d
for a fixed privacy budget, where
s^{*}
is the problem sparsity parameter and
d
is the context dimension.

2.
Algorithm and Regret Analysis
: We propose a novel SLCB algorithm, namely FLIPHAT, that uses N-IHT as a key component, and prove its
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP guarantee. We analyze the expected regret for FLIPHAT and demonstrate that in the problem-independent case, the upper bound matches our lower bound up to
s^{*}
and logarithmic factors in
T
. We also numerically demonstrate its logarithmic dependence on
d
across privacy regimes.

3.
Interaction of Privacy and Hardness of Bandit:
In Theorem
5.6
, we identify a unique interplay between two competing hardness: privacy and the internal hardness of the bandit instance. We show that as the internal hardness of bandit decreases, the hardness due to privacy becomes more prevalent and persists for wider (and larger) values of the privacy parameters, and vice-versa. To the best of our knowledge, this is a previously unidentified phenomenon, and demands careful understanding.

4.
Improved analysis of N-IHT
: We provide a refined analysis for Peeling-based
(
Dwork et al., 2021
)
empirical risk minimization under non i.i.d. setting. This yields a bound on estimation error of N-IHT under the SLCB setting, and it is used in the regret analysis of FLIPHAT. The results and tools developed here might be of independent interest and can be applied in settings where N-IHT is deployed.

A high-dimensional
sparse linear stochastic contextual bandit (SLCB)
consists of
K\in\mathbb{N}
arms. At time
t\in[T]

For
K\in\mathbb{N}

[K]
denotes the set
\{1,\ldots,K\}

\{x_{i}(t)\}_{i\in[K]}
are revealed for every arm

For every

, and are sampled i.i.d. from some unknown distribution
P_{i}

At every step

a_{t}\in[K]
is chosen by the learner and a noisy reward

\displaystyle r(t)=x_{a_{t}}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}+\epsilon(t)\,.

(1)

Here,
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|_{0}=s^{*}

.
\{\epsilon(t)\}_{t\in[T]}

\left\|x_{i}(t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq x_{\sf max}\;\forall(i,t)\in[K]\times[T]

\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|_{1}\leq b_{\sf max}

(
Hao et al., 2020

Now, to formalize the notion of performance of a learner, we define the best action in hindsight at the step

a_{t}^{*}:={\arg\max}_{i\in[K]}x_{i}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}
. Then, the goal is to design a sequential algorithm (policy)
\pi

\pi
takes the observed reward-context pairs until time

\pi_{t}
over the action space
[K]
. The learner selects an action
a_{t}\sim\pi_{t}
using the policy
\pi

\pi
as

2

\pi

\displaystyle R_{\pi}(T):=\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}\left[x_{a_{t}^{*}}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}-x_{a_{t}}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}\right]\,.

(2)

The goal of the learner is to construct a SLCB policy
\pi
to maximize the cumulative reward till time
T
, or equivalently, minimize
R_{\pi}(T)
.

Joint DP for SLCB.
Now, we concretely define the notion of DP in SLCB.
It is well known that the original notion of DP
(
Dwork et al., 2006b
)
is not ideal in contextual bandit setting due to the inevitability of linear regret
(
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
, Claim 13)
.
Therefore, we consider the notion of JDP
(
Vietri et al., 2020
)
. In JDP, both rewards and contexts are considered as sensitive information about the users which are used by
\pi
as input and should be kept private from any external adversary.

To formalize further, let us denote by
\mathbf{r}_{t}:=(r_{1}(t),\ldots,r_{K}(t))
the collection of rewards, and
\mathcal{C}_{t}:=(x_{1}(t),\ldots,x_{K}(t))
the collection of user contexts at time
t
. At the beginning of the game, the adversary prefixes the whole collection of rewards
\{\mathbf{r}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
and contexts
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
.
In the
t
-th step, the learner observes the contexts
\mathcal{C}_{t}
and chooses an action
a_{t}
using only the past
observed
rewards and contexts. Then, adversary reveals the reward
r_{a_{t}}(t)
and the game continues.
Under this paradigm, one can define the whole input dataset as the collection of all the rewards and contexts. Specifically, we consider
\mathcal{D}:=\{(\mathbf{r}_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t})\}_{t\in[T]}
as the input data to a bandit algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.
A randomized bandit algorithm
\mathcal{A}
maps every reward-context sequence
\mathcal{D}
of length
T
to a (random) sequence of actions
a:=(a_{1},\dots,a_{T})\in[K]^{T}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
can be imagined as a random vector taking values in
[K]^{T}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{-t}(\mathcal{D})
denote all the outputs excluding the output in the
t
-th step of the interaction of
\mathcal{A}
with the bandit instance.

\mathcal{A}_{-t}(\mathcal{D})
captures all the outputs that might leak information about the user at the
t
th time point in interactions after the
t
th round, as well as all the outputs from the previous episodes where other users could be submitting information to the agent adversarially to condition its interaction with the
t
th user. Finally, we say that two input datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}:=\{(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{t},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{t})\}_{t\in[T]}
are
\tau
-neighbors, if for all
t\neq\tau
we have
(\mathbf{r}_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t})=(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{t},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{t})
, and
(\mathbf{r}_{\tau},\mathcal{C}_{\tau})\neq(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{\tau},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\tau})
.

Definition 2.1

(JDP in LCB)
.

A randomized bandit algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP if for any
\tau\in[T]
and any pair of
\tau

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{-\tau}(\mathcal{D})\in E)\leq e^{\epsilon}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{-\tau}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in E)+\delta.

Although the above JDP definition is valid for any randomized contextual bandit algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,
it is useful to point out that in our setup, the bandit protocol
\mathcal{A}
is solely defined by the inherent policy
\pi
.

LOWER BOUNDS ON REGRET

The first question that we want to address is what is the inherent cost of privacy in SLCB. Hence, we derive a minimax regret lower bound on the regret achieved by any algorithm ensuring
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP for SLCB. The other goal is to understand the cost of privacy on the minimax lower bound compared to the existing lower bounds for SLCB without differential privacy. First, we formally define the minimax regret in this setting.

Definition 3.1

(Minimax regret)
.

We define the minimax regret in the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP SLCB as
R^{\mathrm{minimax}}_{(\epsilon,\delta)}(T):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\epsilon,\delta)}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]
, where the supremum is taken over
\mathcal{B}
, i.e. the space of SLCB instances, and the infimum is taken over
\Pi(\epsilon,\delta)
, i.e. the space of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP policies.

Further details about the minimax regret, the space of bandit instances and the associated probability space are in Appendix
C
.
Our first result gives a lower bound on the minimax regret, in terms of the impact of the bandit model parameters
s^{*},d,T
, and privacy parameters
\epsilon,\delta
.

Theorem 3.2

(Lower Bound)
.

If
\epsilon,\delta>0
and
\epsilon^{2}<\log(1/\delta)
, then for sufficiently large
s^{*}\log(d/s^{*})
the minimax regret for SLCBs under
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{minimax}}_{(\epsilon,\delta)}(T)=

\displaystyle\Omega\Big(\max\Big\{\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{s^{*}\log^{2}(d/s^{*})\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}}}_{{\text{private}\text{and high-d}}},\underbrace{\sqrt{s^{*}T\log(d/s^{*})}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{non-private}\\
\text{and high-d}\end{subarray}}\Big\}\Big).

Additionally, for
\delta=0
, we get
R^{\mathrm{minimax}}_{\epsilon}(T)=\Omega\Big(\max\{s^{*}\log^{3/2}(d/s^{*})\epsilon^{-1},{\sqrt{s^{*}T\log(d/s^{*})}}\}\Big).

Implications.
(i) This is the first lower bound for SLCBs with JDP. All the existing bounds with JDP are for low-dimensional LCBs with JDP. (ii) Theorem
3.2
indicates that the impact of privacy appears in the regret lower bound when
\epsilon^{2}/\log(1/\delta)<\log(d/s^{*})/T
. For
\delta=0
, the above theorem also states that the cost of privacy becomes prevalent in the regime
\epsilon<\sqrt{s^{*}}\log(d/s^{*})/\sqrt{T}
, which matches in spirit with the lower bound of
(
Azize and Basu, 2022a
)
for a fixed low-dimensional context set, where the impact of privacy appears for
\epsilon<T^{-1/2}
. Additionally, in SLCB, the dimension also matters for the transition. This phenomenon is also reflected in our regret upper bounds.

Proof Technique.
Our proof differs from the existing lower bound techniques for private bandits in
(
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
;
Basu et al., 2019b
;
Azize and Basu, 2022b
;
Azize and Basu, 2024
)
, and non-private SLCB setting
(
Li et al., 2021
)
.
Rather our proof depends on converting the regret lower bound problem to an estimation lower bound problem following
He et al., 2022
, and combing the information-theoretic techniques for sparse linear models
(
Duchi and Wainwright, 2013
)
and privacy-restricted lower bound techniques in
(
Azize and Basu, 2024
;
Kamath et al., 2022
)
.

FLIPHAT: ALGORITHM DESIGN & REGRET ANALYSIS

In this section, we propose a novel algorithm FLIPHAT for the SLCB setting, which is guaranteed to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-JDP.
Then, we derive an upper bound on its expected regret that matches the regret lower bound from Section
3
up to logarithmic terms.

5.1
Algorithm Design

We present the pseudocode of FLIPHAT in Algorithm
2
. FLIPHAT has three components.

First
, we employ the doubling trick
(
Besson and Kaufmann, 2018
)
, i.e. dividing the entire time horizon into episodes of geometrically progressive lengths. Specifically, the
\ell-
th episode begins at step
t_{\ell}=2^{\ell}
. At the beginning of each episode, the underlying algorithm restarts, computes the required estimates, and the episode continues for
N_{\ell}=2^{\ell+1}-2^{\ell}=2^{\ell}
steps. In non-private bandit, we use doubling trick to make the algorithm ‘anytime’, but for private bandits, it also helps to reduce the number of times for which we have to calculate any private estimates from the data. Since DP is typically achieved by adding scaled Laplace noise with the newly computed estimates
(
Dwork et al., 2014
)
, doubling ensures that we do not add noise for more than a logarithmic number of times. This is important for regret optimality.

Second
, to ensure DP, we use the
forgetfulness
technique, i.e. at the beginning of an episode, we only use the data collected from the previous episode to generate the estimate. In the current episode, we do not update the estimate further. This
forgetful
strategy (the observed data from an episode is used just once at the start of the next episode and then forgotten) allows us to guarantee that the overall algorithm is private, if the computed estimates are private.
The main idea is that a change in a reward and context will only affect the estimate calculated in one episode. This allows parallel composition of DP and stops the aggravated accumulation of Laplace noise over time, yielding better regret guarantees.

Third
, we use N-IHT (Algorithm
1
) as the private sparse linear regression oracle to estimate the linear parameter from the observed samples.
At the beginning of
(\ell+1)
-th episode (line 8), we feed N-IHT the data
(X_{\ell},y_{\ell})
consisting of the chosen contexts and observed rewards from the
\ell
th episode (line 13). Other tuning parameters
(s,\epsilon,\delta,\eta,C)
are not explicitly mentioned as they stay same for all
\ell
’s.

5.2
Regret Analysis

Now, we present the regret upper bound of FLIPHAT.
We first briefly discuss the assumptions that we impose on the various model components, which are well accepted in the literature on SLCB
(
Zhang and Huang, 2008
;

;

;

In regret analysis, we do not require any additional assumption for privacy.

Assumption 5.2

(Assumptions on Context Distributions)
.

We assume that

(a)

Boundedness:

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim P_{i}}(\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq x_{\sf max})=1
for all

(b)

Bounded Orlicz norm:
For all arms

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\vartheta
for

for all

(c)

There exists a constant

and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

P_{i}(\left\langle x,u\right\rangle^{2}\leq h)\leq\xi h,

for all
i\in[K]
, ,where
C\in(2,\infty)
.

(d)

Sparsity:
The matrix
\Sigma_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{i}}[xx^{\top}]
has bounded maximum sparse eigenvalue, i.e.,

\phi_{\max}(Cs^{*},\Sigma_{i})\leq\phi_{u}<\infty,

for all
i\in[K]
,
where

is the same constant as in part (c).

Assumption 5.3

(Assumptions on the true parameter)
.

We assume the following:

(a)

Sparsity and soft-sparsity:

\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|_{0}=s^{*}
and
\left\|\beta^{*}\right\|_{1}\leq b_{\sf max}
for constants

.

(b)

Margin condition:
There exists positive constants
\Delta_{*},A
and

h\in\left[A\sqrt{\log(d)/T},\Delta_{*}\right]
and
\forall t\in[T]

{\mathbb{P}}\left(x_{a^{*}_{t}}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}\leq\max_{i\neq a^{*}_{t}}x_{i}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}+h\right)\leq\left(\frac{h}{\Delta^{*}}\right)^{\alpha}

Assumption 5.4

(Assumption on Noise)
.

We assume that the random variables
\{\epsilon(t)\}_{t\in[T]}

\sigma-

\mathbb{E}[e^{a\epsilon(t)}]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}a^{2}/2}

Remarks:
In

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{u:\left\|u\right\|_{2}\leq 1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
is the Orlicz-norm, where for a random-variable

\left\|Z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\{\lambda>0:\mathbb{E}\exp(Z^{2}/\lambda^{2})\leq 2\}

\alpha=\infty

\alpha=0
making it hardest, i.e. no apriori information about arm separation. A detailed discussion on the assumptions is in Appendix
B

Below, we state the implications.

1.
Dependence on
d,s,s^{*},T
: The term in

scaling as
\{(s+s^{*})^{3}\log^{2}d\}^{(1+\alpha)/2}
dominates the non-private term
I_{\alpha}
(for fixed
\epsilon,\delta
). The dependence on
T
is trickier: for the non-private part, for
\alpha=0
, the regret scales as
\sqrt{T}
, for
\alpha=1
as
\log T
and the effect fades as
\alpha\to\infty
. For the private part
J_{\alpha}
, as
\alpha\to 0
,
\Psi_{\alpha}(T)
can be upper bounded by
\log^{3}T
, while the effect fades as
\alpha
increases.

2.
Privacy trade-off for fixed
\alpha
: For any fixed level of
\alpha
, the regret bound explicates the threshold of the privacy parameter
\epsilon
, under which the cost of privacy dominates the non-private term. For example, with
\alpha=0
(no margin condition),
J_{\alpha}
dominates
I_{\alpha}
when
\epsilon/\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}<\log^{3}T\sqrt{\{(s+s^{*})\log d\}/T}
. Similarly, for
\alpha=\infty
, the private part dominates when
\epsilon/\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}<\sqrt{(s+s^{*})\log d}
.

3.
Privacy vs. internal hardness
: An interesting feature arising in the reget analysis is the mutual struggle between the hardness coming from the bandit instance’s arm-separation (through
\alpha
) and privacy constraint (through
\epsilon,\delta
). To understand this issue, let us denote
\bar{\epsilon}(\alpha)
to be the threshold for
\epsilon
under which the regret due to privacy dominates. We note that as
\alpha
increases,
\bar{\epsilon}(\alpha)
also increases, while keeping
\delta
fixed. For example,
\bar{\epsilon}(\infty)=\sqrt{(s+s^{*})\log d}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}
and
\bar{\epsilon}(0)=\log^{3}T\sqrt{\{(s+s^{*})\log d\}\log(1/\delta)/T}
. Thus, when the bandit instance is easy, (high
\alpha
), the hardness due to the privacy is dominant for a larger range of
\epsilon
(analogously high
\bar{\epsilon}(\alpha)
). Conversely, when the bandit instance is itself hard (low
\alpha
), the regret incurred due to this inherent difficulty quickly crosses the hardness due to privacy. As a result, even for smaller
\epsilon
, regret is dominated by the non-private part.

4.
Achieving minimax optimal bound:

The regret bound in Theorem
5.6
crucially depends on the quantity
(s+s^{*})
. Therefore, the choice of
s
is very important to avoid the curse of dimensionality. In a high-dimensional sparse setting,
s^{*}
is typically very small, i.e.,
s^{*}\ll d
. Therefore, setting
s
in the order of
o(d)
is also a valid choice, and an improved dependence on
d
can be achieved. Moreover, under a constant order knowledge of
s^{*}
, the tuning parameter
s
can be chosen as
s\asymp\kappa^{2}s^{*}
which leads to a near-optimal dependence in
s^{*}
. In addition, numerical experiments in Appendix
F.3
show that the regret only scales polynomially in
s>s^{*}
as shown in Theorem
5.6
.

CONCLUSION

In this paper, we study the SLCBs under JDP constraints.
We establish two minimax regret lower bounds under JDP settings, demonstrating phase transition between the hardness of the problem depending on the privacy level.
We propose FLIPHAT, which is the first algorithm to achieves sharp regret-privacy trade-off in SLCBs. FLIPHAT is based on the N-IHT algorithm, for which we propose a refined convergence analysis under a general setting than the existing literature.

An interesting direction would be to study FLIPHAT under an adversarial sparse LCB setting. It would also be an interesting future study to explore the lower bound using the margin condition and obtain bounds dependent on the parameter
\alpha
in the margin condition.

Appendix A
BACKGROUND ON DIFFERENTIAL PRIVACY

In this section we will formally define the notion of differential privacy. On a high level, differential privacy requires the output of a randomized procedure to be robust against small perturbation in the input dataset, i.e., an attacker can hardly recover the presence or absence of an individual in the dataset based on the output only.

Consider a dateset
D=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
consisting of
n
data points in the sample space
\mathcal{Z}
. A
randomized
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
takes the dataset
D
as input and maps it to
\mathcal{A}(D)\in\mathcal{O}
, an output space. Two datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
are said to be
neighbors
if they differ only in one entry.

Definition A.1

(
Dwork, 2006
)
.

Given the privacy parameters
(\epsilon,\delta)
, a randomized algorithm is said to satisfy the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}(D)\in\mathcal{O})\leq e^{\epsilon}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\in\mathcal{O})+\delta

for any measurable set
\mathcal{O}\in range(\mathcal{A})
and any neighboring datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
.

The probability in the above definition is only with respect to the randomization in
\mathcal{A}
, and it does not impose any condition on the distribution of
D
or
D^{\prime}
. For small choices of
\epsilon
and
\delta
, Definition
A.1
essentially says that the distribution of
\mathcal{A}(D)
and
\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})
are nearly indistinguishable form each other for al choices of
D
and
D^{\prime}
. This guarantees
strong privacy against an attacker by masking the presence or absence of a particular individual
in the dataset.

There are several privacy preserving mechanisms that are often the building blocks of other complicated DP mechanisms. A few popular examples include the Laplace mechanism
(
Dwork et al., 2006b
)
, Gaussian mechanism
(
Dwork et al., 2006a
)
, and Exponential mechanism
(
McSherry and Talwar, 2007
)
. We only provide the details of the first technique, since the
other two techniques are out-of-scope for the methods and
experiments in this paper.

Laplace mechanism:

Let
f:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a deterministic map. The
Laplace mechanism
preserved privacy by perturbing the output
f(D)
with noise generated from the Laplace density
{\sf Lap}(\lambda)
, whose density is
(2\lambda)^{-1}\exp(-\left|x\right|/\lambda)
. The scaling parameter
\lambda
is chosen based on the sensitivity of the function
f
, which is defined as

\Delta f:=\sup_{D,D^{\prime}:\text{$D,D^{\prime}$ are neighbors}}\left\|f(D)-f(D^{\prime})\right\|_{1}.

In particular, for any deterministic map
f
with
\Delta f<\infty
, the mechanism that, on database
D
, adds independent draws from
{\sf Lap}(\Delta f/\epsilon)
to each of the
m
components of
f(D)
, is
(\epsilon,0)
-DP.

The Laplace mechanism is also an important building block of the well-known
peeling
algorithm (Algorithm
3
) which is used for private top-
s
selection problem
(
Dwork et al., 2021
)
.
We denote the output of peeling applied to a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathcal{P}_{s}(v;\epsilon,\delta,\lambda)
, where
\epsilon,\delta
are privacy parameters and
\lambda
is the Laplace noise scale parameter. For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and an index set

,
v_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the vector such that

matches

and is 0 elsewhere. Therefore, peeling returns a

-sparse vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Algorithm 3

Peeling
(
Dwork et al., 2021
)

0:

Vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, sparsity
s
, privacy level
\epsilon,\delta
, Laplace noise scale
\lambda

1:

Initialize
:
S=\emptyset
and set
\xi=\lambda\frac{2\sqrt{3s\log(1/\delta)}}{\epsilon}

2:

for

i=1,2,\dots,s
:
do

3:

Generate
\mathbf{w}_{i}=(w_{i1},\dots,w_{id})\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}{\sf Lap}(\xi)

4:

j_{i}^{*}={\arg\max}_{j\in[d]\setminus S}|v_{j}|+w_{ij}

5:

Update

6:

end

Set

8:

Generate
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=(\tilde{w}_{1},\dots,\tilde{w}_{d})\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}{\sf Lap}(\xi)

9:

Return

The following lemma shows that peeling is

-DP.

If for every pair of adjacent datasets

Appendix B
DISCUSSION ON ASSUMPTIONS

Assumption
5.2
(a)
about bounded contexts are standard in the bandit literature to obtain regret bounds independent of the scaling of the contexts. Assumption
5.2
(b)
says that the context distribution for each arm is a sub-Gaussian distribution with parameter
\vartheta
, this assumption does not require the distributions to have zero mean. This is indeed a very mild assumption on the context distribution and a broad class of distributions, such as truncated multivariate Gaussian and uniform on a compact set, enjoys such property. In comparison, several works in the literature on sparse contextual bandits
(
Oh et al., 2021
;
Kim and Paik, 2019
;
Li et al., 2021
)
assume bounded
L_{2}
norm, i.e.
\left\|X\right\|_{2}\leq L
, which in the high-dimensional setting is much stronger. Indeed
\left\|X\right\|_{2}\leq L
implies
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq(L/\log 2)^{1/2}
and in the example of truncated normal,
\left\|X\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
.
Assumption
5.2
(c)
is an anti-concentration condition that ensures that the directions of the arms are well spread across every direction, thereby assisting in the estimation accuracy for
\beta^{*}
. Recent works
(
Li et al., 2021
;
Chakraborty et al., 2023
)
use similar assumptions to establish sharp estimation rates for
\beta^{*}
under bandit settings.
Assumption
5.2
(d)
imposes an upper bound on the maximum sparse eigenvalue of
\Sigma_{i}
which is a common assumption in high-dimensional literature
(
Zhang and Huang, 2008
;
Zhang, 2010
)
. Below we define the minimum and maximum sparse eigenvalue conditions formally.

Definition B.1

(Sparse Riesz Condition)
.

For a
d\times d
positive semi-definite matrix
A
, its maximum and minimum sparse eigenvalues with parameter
s\in[d]
are defined as

\displaystyle\phi_{\min}(s;A):=\inf_{u:u\neq 0,\left\|u\right\|_{0}\leq s}u^{\top}Au/\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2},\quad\phi_{\max}(s;A):=\sup_{u:u\neq 0,\left\|u\right\|_{0}\leq s}u^{\top}Au/\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(4)

For the assumptions on the parameter, boundedness of
\beta^{*}
ensures that the final regret bound is scale free and is also a standard assumption in the bandit literature
(
Bastani and Bayati, 2020
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011
;
Chakraborty et al., 2023
)
.

The margin condition in the second part essentially controls the probability of the optimal arm falling into an
h
-neighborhood of the sub-optimal arms. As
\alpha
increases, the margin condition becomes stronger. As an illustration, consider the two extreme cases
\alpha=\infty
and
\alpha=0
. The
\alpha=\infty
case enforces a deterministic gap between rewards corresponding to the optimal arm and sub-optimal arms. This is the same as the “gap assumption” in
Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011
. Thus, it is easy for any bandit policy to recognize the optimal arm, which is reflected in the poly-logarithmic dependence on horizon
T
in the regret bound of Theorem 5 in
Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011
. In contrast,
\alpha=0
corresponds to the case when there is no apriori information about the separation between the arms, and as a consequence, we pay the price in regret bound by a
\sqrt{T}
term
(
Hao et al., 2021
;
Agrawal and Goyal, 2013
;
Chu et al., 2011
)
. Assumption
5.3
(b)
is also considered in
Li et al., 2021
;
Chakraborty et al., 2023
, and more details can be found therein.

Appendix C
REGRET LOWER BOUND: DETAILS FROM SECTION
3

Before getting into the proof, we discuss some additional details about the probability structure in the bandit environment, due to the randomness coming from the (i) bandit instance (through contexts and rewards) and (ii) policy (randomness due to privacy).

Let
\mathcal{B}_{\beta^{*}}
denote the space of joint distributions
P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}
of contexts and rewards up to time
T
when the underlying parameter is
\beta^{*}
. In particular,

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}=\otimes_{t\in[T]}P_{\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{r}_{t}}.

Here,
P_{\mathbf{X}_{t},\mathbf{r}_{t}}
is the joint distribution of the contexts
(x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{K}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd}
and rewards
\mathbf{r}_{t}=(r_{1}(t),\dots,r_{K}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
at time
t
, induced through
(x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{K}(t))\sim P_{\mathbf{X}}
(with the marginal for
x_{i}(t)
being
P_{i}
) and conditional on
x_{i}(t)
,
r_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)^{\top}\beta^{*}+\epsilon_{i}(t)
.
\epsilon_{i}(t)\sim P_{\epsilon}
independently for all
i,t
. A particular choice of
\beta^{*},P_{\mathbf{X}}
and
P_{\epsilon}
lead to a particular bandit instance
P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\beta^{*}}
. To control for the scaling in the contexts and the conditional rewards, we assume that for
(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})\sim P_{\mathbf{X}}
, the marginal covariance of each
x_{k}
has eigenvalues bounded by
\sigma_{X,\min}^{2}
and
\sigma_{X,\max}^{2}
. We also assume that
P_{\epsilon}
is a sub-Gaussian distribution with variance-proxy
\sigma^{2}
. The dependence on these parameters will be denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\beta^{*},\sigma_{X},\sigma}
in the space of joint distributions of contexts and rewards.. However, given a particular bandit instance, the learner does not get to observe all this data. This is where the policy comes in.

A policy
\pi
is defined by a sequence of randomized decisions
a^{\pi}_{t}:\mathbf{H}_{t-1}^{\pi}\times\mathbf{X}_{t}\to\Delta([K])
, where
\mathbf{H}_{t-1}^{\pi}=\{x_{a^{\pi}_{s}}(s),r_{a_{s}^{\pi}}(s):1\leq s<t\}
is the observed history and
\Delta([K])
is the space of probability distributions on
[K]
(the action set). When the policy is clear from context, it is often abbreviated to saying that
a_{t}
is the (random)
action
taken at time
t
, depending on the history (which also depends on the policy-induced actions at previous time points) and the current contexts. The stochasticity in
a_{t}^{\pi}
could be from the randomness required for exploration, or privacy concerns. The expectation in the expected regret
\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]
refers to the expectation taking with respect to the randomness arising due to the interaction of the bandit instance
P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}
and policy
\pi
and the minimax regret for JDP-SLCB problem is

R^{\mathrm{minimax}}_{(\epsilon,\delta)}(T)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\epsilon,\delta)}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{X},\sigma}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]

where the infimum is with respect to all
(\epsilon,\delta)-
JDP policies
\pi
and the supremum is with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{X},\sigma}=\cup\{\mathcal{B}_{\beta^{*},\sigma_{X},\sigma}:\beta^{*}\text{ satisfies Assumption~\ref{assumptions: arm-separation}\ref{item: beta-l1}}\}
, i.e. all SLCB instances with the unknown parameter having sparsity
s^{*}
. We denote the regret minimax bound for
\epsilon-
JDP policy by
R^{\text{minimax}}_{\epsilon}
, where the infimum is taken over all
\epsilon-
JDP policies. Note that under this definition, the minimax regret also depends on the noise scaling
\sigma^{2}
and the contexts’ marginal scaling controlled by
\sigma_{X}^{2}
. To ensure that
\mathbb{E}\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\asymp 1
, we take
\sigma_{X}=1/\sqrt{\log d}
.

Step 1: Choosing Hard Instances

Now, we choose several hard instances
P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\sigma_{X},\sigma}
as follows – consider a 2-armed SLCB (i.e.,
K=2
), with
\beta^{*}\in\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\beta_{i}\in\{-r,0,r\},\left\|\beta\right\|_{0}=s^{*}\}
where
s^{*}
is the assumed true sparsity level and
r>0
will be specified later. Then
|\tilde{\mathbf{B}}|={{d}\choose{s^{*}}}2^{s^{*}}
. Let

\delta(t)=\{\delta\mid\left\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\geq\delta,\text{ for all }\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\text{ s.t. }d_{H}(\beta,\beta^{\prime})>t\},

where
d_{H}
is the Hamming distance. Considering a
t-
packing of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
(in the Hamming distance) following
Duchi and Wainwright, 2013
, this leads to a
\delta(t)-
packing in the
\ell_{2}
distance. In our case, with the given construction of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
, this leads to
\delta(t)>\max\{1,\sqrt{t}\}r
. Let
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{M}\}
be the elements in this
\delta(t)
packing with
t=\left\lceil s^{*}/4\right\rceil
, where
\log M\geq cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})
for some numerical constant
c
. Moreover, for this choice of
t
, we have for any
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbf{B}^{*}
,
\left\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{2}\geq\max\{1,s^{*}/4\}r^{2}=:\alpha
(by construction of the packing). For the contexts, we consider
P_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{X}^{2}I_{d})\otimes\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{X}^{2}I_{d})
(independent multivariate standard normal for both arms). Let
z_{t}=x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)
for each
t
. Lastly, let
\nu
denote the uniform distribution on
\mathbf{B}^{*}
.

Step 2: Bounds on distances between context-rewards under different true parameters

Denote the space of bandit instances using
\beta^{*}\in\mathbf{B}^{*}
and contexts as constructed above as
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
and for each
\beta\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, let
p_{t}(\cdot|\beta)
denote the distribution of
all
the contexts and rewards till time
t
under true parameter
\beta
. We first establish upper bounds between the KL divergence and total variation distances between
p_{t}(\cdot|\beta)
and
p_{t}(\cdot|\beta^{\prime})
for
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbf{B}^{*}
. These upper bounds will be used while applying differentially private Fano’s inequalities.

On the other hand, the data consists of
\{X_{j},r_{j}\}
for
j\in[Kt]
(recall that the data consists of all rewards and contexts till time
t
irrespective of the previously chosen actions – hence, the data consists of i.i.d. observations). The KL can be bounded as

\displaystyle{\sf KL}(p(\cdot|\beta)\|p(\cdot|\beta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=Kt\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim N(0,\sigma_{X}^{2}I)}{\sf KL}\left(\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta,\sigma^{2})\|\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta^{\prime},\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=Kt\mathbb{E}_{x\sim N(0,\sigma_{X}^{2}I)}\left[\frac{(x^{\top}(\beta-\beta^{\prime}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{Kt\sigma_{X}^{2}\left\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{2Kt\sigma_{X}^{2}s^{*}r^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=:\gamma.

The last inequality arises since the worst distance can occur if
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
has the same support but opposing signs (then
\left\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}=4s^{*}r^{2}
).

Finally, the total variation distance can be bounded using Pinsker and Jensen’s inequality as follows

\displaystyle{\sf TV}(p(\cdot|\beta),p(\cdot|\beta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq Kt\mathbb{E}_{x}{\sf TV}(\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta,\sigma^{2}),\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta^{\prime},\sigma^{2}))

\displaystyle\leq Kt\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x}{\sf KL}(\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta,\sigma^{2}),\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\beta^{\prime},\sigma^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq Kt\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma}\sqrt{s^{*}}r=:D.

Step 3: Reduction to estimation lower bound

Given a bandit instance from the above class, suppose
\pi
is any bandit policy. Define the
\pi-
induced estimator in period

,
\beta^{\pi}_{t}
to be the maximizer of

\displaystyle f^{\pi}_{t}(\beta):=P(a^{\pi}_{t}=1,z_{t}^{\top}\beta>0|\mathbf{H}^{\pi}_{t-1})+P(a^{\pi}_{t}=2,z_{t}^{\top}\beta\leq 0|\mathbf{H}^{\pi}_{t-1})

(5)

where the maximization is taken over all DP (random) maps

that are
(\epsilon,\delta)-
DP with respect to the history. Let
\hat{\Theta}_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
denote this space of DP random maps (the notion of DP could also be
\epsilon-
DP, for which we denote the space of DP maps as
\hat{\Theta}_{t}^{\epsilon}
). Note that the probability
P
in the above display is with respect to the random action
a^{\pi}_{t}
and the (random) estimator
\beta
. Now, since we are only interested in JDP algorithms, let
\pi
be any
(\epsilon,\delta)-
JDP bandit policy. We note that the degenerate maps
\beta^{*}:\mathbf{H}^{\pi}_{t}\mapsto\delta_{\beta^{*}}

for any
\beta^{*}
–hence, for any

, the corresponding map
\beta^{*}\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
. Since

and
\beta^{*}
are both in

, Lemma 3.1 in
(

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]\geq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{r\sqrt{s^{*}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\inf_{\beta_{t}\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right).

In the above,
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]
is the Bayesian 2-norm risk, where the expectation is taken jointly with respect to the randomness in both

and
\beta^{*}\sim\nu
. We remark that this technique does not require a policy to have a specific form that depends on DP estimators sequentially.

Now, we denote by
\Theta_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}
the collection of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP estimators with respect oto

\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pi}\subseteq\mathbf{H}_{t}
. Also, quite crucially, for an

and is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP with respect to
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pi}
. This suggests that
\beta_{t}
is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP with respect to the input data
\mathbf{H}_{t}
. To see this, let

and

be two neighboring datasets. There are only two possibilities in this case: (i)
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pi}=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\prime\pi}
, or (ii)

.

•

Case (i):
Since
\beta_{t}
only depends on

(or

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\beta(\mathbf{H}_{t})\in O)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pi})\in O)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\prime\pi})\in O)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\prime})\in O).

•

Case (ii):
In this case

are neighboring datasets. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\beta(\mathbf{H}_{t})\in O)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\pi})\in O)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\epsilon}{\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\prime\pi})\in O)+\delta

\displaystyle=e^{\epsilon}{\mathbb{P}}(\beta_{t}(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\prime})\in O)+\delta.

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta_{t}\in\hat{\Theta}_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]

Step 4(a): Analysis under

Following the same steps as in the proof of Theorem 2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\max\{1,s^{*}/4\}r^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{\gamma+\log 2}{\log M}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\max\{1,s^{*}/4\}r^{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{\frac{4t\sigma_{X}^{2}s^{*}r^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\log 2}{cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})}\right).

private lower bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq 0.4\alpha\min\left\{1,\frac{M}{e^{10\epsilon D}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq 0.4\max\{1,s^{*}/4\}r^{2}\min\left\{1,\exp\left(cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})-10\epsilon Kt\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma}\sqrt{s^{*}}r\right)\right\}.

For

and

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]\geq\frac{s^{*}}{4}r^{2}\max\left\{0.5\left(1-\frac{\frac{2Kt\sigma_{X}^{2}s^{*}r^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\log 2}{cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})}\right),0.4\exp\left(cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})-10\epsilon Kt\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma}\sqrt{s^{*}}r\right)\right\}.

(7)

Hence the minimax regret is (noting

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]\gtrsim\frac{r\sqrt{s^{*}}T}{4}\max\left\{1-\frac{\frac{4T\sigma_{X}^{2}s^{*}r^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}+\log 2}{cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})},\exp\left(cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})-20\epsilon T\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma}\sqrt{s^{*}}r\right)\right\}

If we set

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}\sup_{P_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}[R_{\pi}(T)]\gtrsim\sigma\sqrt{s^{*}T\log(d/s^{*})}\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{X}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\log 2}{cs^{*}\log(d/s^{*})}\right).

Now, we shift focus to the private part of the lower bound. We set

-zCDP with

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta_{t}\in\Theta_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\beta_{t}\in\Theta_{t}^{\rho}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\left\|\beta_{t}-\beta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}].

Now, to lower bound the second term, we use

Kamath et al., 2022

. For implementation of FLIPHAT, we used three hyperparameters
s
(sparsity guess),
\eta
(step size for N-IHT) and
M>0
(to control the number of steps in N-IHT with
M_{\ell}=M\log N_{\ell}
at the
\ell
-th episode). For these synthetic experiments, we used
x_{\sf max}
and
b_{\sf max}
based on the true underlying setting and chose
C,R_{\ell}
accordingly (see Algorithm
2
and Proposition
5.5
. In practice, the contexts can be rescaled (to eliminate
x_{\sf max}
) and
b_{\sf max}
could either be chosen based on existing domain knowledge or held-out data or included as another hyperparameter. The task of tuning such hyperparameters can be tricky because of privacy constraints, but we do not focus on that aspect in this paper and keep it for future work.

F.1
Regret vs
T

We perform two experiments to study how the regret
R(T)
depends on time horizon
T
for different levels of the privacy parameter
\epsilon
. For both experiments, we consider
d=400,s^{*}=5,K=3
and choose the privacy parameters
\epsilon\in\{0.5,1,2,5,10\}
and
\delta=0.01.
The contexts were drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
with an auto-regressive covariance matrix
\Sigma_{ij}=0.1^{|i-j|}
. The rewards were sampled from the linear model (
1
) – in the first experiment
\epsilon(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=0.1
and the second experiment,
\epsilon(t)\sim\text{Uniform}(-0.1,0.1)
. The hyperparameters were chosen as
s=10,\eta=10^{-4},M=1.6
. Each experiment was repeated 60 times and the mean and 95% confidence intervals are shown in Figure
2
. We find the behavior very similar in both cases. For additional perspective, we added the random bandit (full privacy
\epsilon=0
) and sparsity agnostic bandit (no privacy
\epsilon\to\infty
). We note that even for
\epsilon=0.5
, we observe clear sub-linear behavior of the regret, particularly after
T=7500
. For larger
\epsilon
, we observe that the performance is similar to the sparsity agnostic bandit. To our knowledge, this current paper is the first work on joint differentially private algorithm for sparse contextual bandits, as a result, we could not find any other suitable baseline to compare the performance with.

F.2
Regret vs
d

The setup is exactly similar to the first two experiments, discussed above. In this case, we only collect the regret suffered at
T=10000
, but use different context dimensions
d
, to examine the effect of
d
on the regret for different choices of
\epsilon
. Note that
s^{*}
is fixed in these experiments. The result is shown in Figure
1
(right) in the main text. We wish to remark on the distinct behavior of FLIPHAT, compared to the JDP contextual bandit in
(
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
)
. In this work, the authors explore joint differential privacy for contextual bandits, with no additional condition on the sparsity – as a result, the regret scales as
d^{2}
, which is illustrated in Figure 3 (in the Supplementary Material of
(
Shariff and Sheffet, 2018
)
). In our case, we demonstrate that with the additional assumption of sparsity, we can improve this dependence on
d
under joint differential privacy – this is illustrated in Figure
1
(right), where we observe that
R_{d}(T)\approx\alpha+\beta\log d
, where
\beta
(for different
\epsilon
) is estimated using a linear model and shown in the legend.

F.3
Effect of
s

In this experiment, the goal was to examine the effect of one of the more important hyperparameters
s
. According to Theorem
5.6
, as long as
s\gtrsim\kappa^{2}s^{*}
, the upper bound (in the problem independent setting of
\alpha=0
), scales as
(s+s^{*})^{3/2}
. As discussed in the remarks following the theorem, setting
s
in the order of
o(d)
guarantees improved dependence on
d
, with
s\asymp s^{*}
(same order) yielding near-optimal dependence on
s^{*}
. In practice,
s^{*}
is not known, although some domain knowledge might give reasonable lower (and upper) bounds for it. Figure
3
(left) shows
\log R(T)
against
\log s
for
T=10000
in the same setting as the first experiment (with the only change being
s^{*}=10
here) for different choices of
s
ranging from 5 to 60 (and different
\epsilon
). We observe that the regret is poor for lower values and is best around
s=s^{*}
and then worsens a bit. The performance is reasonably robust for higher values of
s
under high privacy (small
\epsilon
) regime. Under low privacy regime, the log-regret scales only linearly in
\log s
. The slope of the
\log R(T)
vs
\log s
lines for each
\epsilon
in the part
s\geq s^{*}
is estimated by fitting a linear model and displayed in the corresponding legend.
