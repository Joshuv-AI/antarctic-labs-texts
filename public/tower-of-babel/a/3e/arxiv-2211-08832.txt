Wind profiles for WKB Prandtl models based on slope and free air flow
Maximilian Arbeiter Iris Rammelmüller* Gunter Spöck

Abstract
In this article the WKB (Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin) Prandtl model serves as the baseline for the study of different kinds of slope flows which can occur over inclined surfaces. The Prandtl-type model couples basic boundary-layer dynamics and thermodynamics for pure slope flows. We provide an answer to the question if it is possible to obtain the matching WKB Prandtl model using only friction velocity, friction temperature, and sensible heat flux. This instantly raises the query if there is a transition or combination between the WKB-Prandtl model for slope flows and the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory for free-air flows and vice versa. As a result, we show the difference between friction velocity and friction temperature calculated using the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory and those computed using the WKB Prandtl model. There is ongoing research into hill-perturbed non-neutral wind profiles because of their potential utility in numerous applications. Hence, further discussion includes how the new parametrization of the WKB Prandtl model may be used to calculate slope and free-air flows in a micro-meteorological model of an alpine valley, e.g. for pollutant dispersion calculations.

1
Introduction

Boundary layer meteorology, or the study of the lower part of the atmosphere, is the most significant topic of meteorology for air dispersion models. The planetary boundary layer is a part of the atmosphere whose behaviour is directly influenced by contact with the surface, e.g.
Garratt 1992
;
De Visscher 2014
. Therefore, dynamic pollutant dispersion simulation requires accurate modelling of wind speeds, wind directions, and sensible heat fluxes, e.g.
Rotach and Zardi 2007
;
De Visscher 2014
. Non-neutral wind profiles play an important part in a variety of transport phenomena, such as heat and mass transfer, but also in flow-induced mixing. The flow encounters with different elevations and slopes at the surface, which lower wind speed and introduce random vertical and horizontal velocity components across the primary flow direction. The interest in hill-perturbed non-neutral flows has increased considerably in the last several decades, e.g.
Egger 1990
;
Oerlemans and Vugts 1993
;
Fedorovich and Shapiro 2009
;
Horvath et al. 2011
. In many practical problems, it appears that an inadequate wind profile is the most obstructive factor to a solution of the problem at hand.
Due to changes in atmospheric stability, different kinds of slope flows can occur over inclined surfaces.
Within the stable night-time boundary layer, katabatic down-slope flows occur, while anabatic up-hill flows occur during unstable daytime conditions.
These slope flows are ubiquitous over complex terrain wherever underlying surface inclination exists over an appreciable distance. There are various aspects, modifications and improvements to basic and advanced models of slope flows, e.g.
Mahrt 1982
;
Egger 1990
;
Smith and Skyllingstad 2005
;
Zardi and Whiteman 2013
.
One of the more common analytic models for the corresponding thermally driven simple slope flows is that of Prandtl (1942). During the last 20 years, the original Prandtl model was extended by, e.g.
Grisogono and Oerlemans 2001a
, (
Grisogono and Oerlemans 2001b
), (
Grisogono and Oerlemans 2002
).
Grisogono and Axelsen 2012
discussed the strengths and limitations of the classical Prandtl model, focusing primarily on its applicability to large-eddy simulations (LES), and secondarily on its suitability for use with limited observational data sets, such as those presented by, e.g.
Van Den Broeke 1997
;
Parmhed et al. 2004
;
Axelsen and van Dop 2009
.
Field experiments have been performed for katabatic flows at the Pasterze glacier in Austria, e.g.
Greuell et al. 1994
;
Grisogono and Oerlemans 2001a
. Later
Grisogono et al. 2015
extended the original Prandtl model by adding to the steady state Boussinesq Navier-Stokes equation a small
\epsilon
-potential temperature flux term, which enhances along-slope advection of the potential temperature
\theta\,\mathrm{\,[K]}
but is counteracted by parameterized turbulent mixing, e.g.
Boussinesq 1897
. This is called the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model of order 1.
Grisogono et al. 2015
showed how the friction velocity
u_{*}\,\mathrm{\,[ms^{-1}]}
, friction temperature
\theta_{*}\,\mathrm{\,[K]}
, and sensible heat flux
Q_{H}\,\mathrm{\,[Jm^{-2}s^{-1}]}
can be calculated in the WKB
ϵ=0
Prandtl model.
Thus, slope flows remain subject of vigorous research, e.g.
Fedorovich and Shapiro 2009
;
Zardi and Whitman 2013
.

The first objective of this article is to obtain the matching WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model using only friction velocity, friction temperature, sensible heat flux, Prandtl number, eddy thermal diffusivity function, and slope angle.
Our second objective is to integrate various types of terrain, for which we utilize Monin-Obukhov similarity theory. This theory is then employed to develop the appropriate Prandtl model.
The question now is whether the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory parameters
u_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
, and
Q_{H}
derived for virtually flat terrain may also be utilised as parameters to obtain those in the Prandtl WKB
ϵ
model, or whether they require a functional change.
An intriguing question is whether a transition and/or combination exists that links the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory for free-air flows with the WKB Prandtl model for inclined flows, and vice versa.
Our models are validated with the WKB Prandtl model presented in
Grisogono et al. 2015
, which serves as the baseline for this work.

The article is structured as follows:
in Section
2
the extended Boussinesq Navier-Stokes equations and their solutions for the WKB
ϵ=0
and WKB
ϵ>0
Prandtl model are given. Section

,
\theta_{*}
, and

may be derived for the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model.
The most innovative part of this article is Sect.
4
, where it is shown how the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model may be obtained from knowing only the slope angle
\alpha\,\mathrm{\,[rad]}
,
u_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
,
Q_{H}
, Prandtl number
Pr
, and the parametrically specified height-dependent eddy thermal diffusivity
K_{H}(z)\,\mathrm{\,[m^{2}s^{-1}]}
. Section

ϵ
Prandtl model may be accurately computed using only the slope angle, friction parameters, and sensible heat flux.
The question of the relationship among the friction parameters,

, in flat and steep terrain will be answered in Sect.

The findings are highlighted in the concluding Sect.

2
The Prandtl- and the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model

The classic Prandtl model has been invented by
Prandtl 1942
and has been used by several authors, e.g.
Defant 1949
;
Smith 1979
;
Egger 1990
;
Axelsen and van Dop 2009

The governing steady-state one-dimensional equations in the tilted coordinate frame for a constant slope angle

with quasi-hydrostatic approximation are given by, e.g.
Grisogono et al. 2015
. This is a modification of the original coupled linear diffusion equations, one for momentum and one for temperature. In the presence of progressively changing background eddy coefficients, the added
\epsilon-
term in Eq. (
1
) turns the original system weakly nonlinear.
According to
Grisogono et al. 2015
, the parameters have the usual meaning:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=g\frac{\Delta\theta}{\theta_{0}}\sin(\alpha)+K_{H}Pr\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{2}},

(1)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\left(\Gamma_{0}+\epsilon\frac{\partial\Delta\theta}{{\partial z}}\right)u\sin(\alpha)+K_{H}\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta\theta}{\partial z^{2}},

where
g\,\mathrm{\,[ms^{-2}]}
is the acceleration due to gravity, Prandtl number
Pr=K_{M}/K_{H}
, eddy viscosity
K_{M}\,\mathrm{\,[m^{2}s^{-1}]}
, eddy thermal diffusivity
K_{H}\,\mathrm{\,[m^{2}s^{-1}]}
, absolute temperature at surface level
\theta_{0}\,\mathrm{\,[K]}
, potential temperature anomaly
\Delta\theta\,\mathrm{\,[K]}
, and
\epsilon\in(0,1]
. The boundary conditions are selected as the usual ones, e.g.
Grisogono et al. 2015
, for the Prandtl model:
\Delta\theta(z=z_{0})=C
,
u(z=z_{0})=0
,
\Delta\theta(z\rightarrow\infty)=0
, and
u(z\rightarrow\infty)=0
, where
z_{0}\,\mathrm{\,[m]}
is the roughness length measured orthogonal to the surface and
C\,\mathrm{\,[K]}
is the Prandtl amplitude.
\Delta\theta
describes the potential temperature deficit in katabatic flows and the converse in anabatic flows with a temperature surplus, e.g.
Grisogono et al. 2015
.

\Gamma_{0}\,\mathrm{\,[Km^{-1}]}
describes the background gradient measured orthogonal to the slope and relates to the background potential temperature gradient
\Gamma\,\mathrm{\,[Km^{-1}]}
as follows:

\Gamma_{0}=\frac{\Gamma}{\cos(\alpha)}.

Equation (
1
) originates from the time-independent stochastic Navier-Stokes equation, e.g.
Birnir 2013
, using ordinary K-closure:

\displaystyle-\langle u,w\rangle

\displaystyle=K_{M}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z},

(2)

\displaystyle-\langle\theta,w\rangle

\displaystyle=K_{H}\left(\frac{\partial\Delta\theta}{\partial z}+\Gamma_{0}\right),

whereby the potential temperature is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\theta(z)=\theta_{0}+\Gamma_{0}(z-z_{0})+\Delta\theta(z),

(3)

where
z\,\mathrm{[m]}
varies orthogonally above the inclined surface. Note that
\langle u,w\rangle
denotes the covariance between
u
and
w
.
The Reynolds decomposition is one of the most fundamental approaches to quantify turbulences. Therefore, the velocity can be considered as a vector with additive deterministic and random components. In other words, we can partition each of the variables into mean and turbulent parts. Thus, at the left-hand side of Eq. (
2
), at the covariance,
u
,
w
, and
\theta
have a stochastic interpretation. On the right-hand side of these equations their interpretation is deterministic as the mean wind speed gradient and mean potential temperature gradient. We only speak about wind speeds and potential temperature and assume that stochastic and deterministic interpretation can be detected from context.

The WKB
ϵ
approach expands
u
and
\Delta\theta
into a power series of
\epsilon
as follows, e.g.
Metivier 2003
:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\epsilon u_{1}+\epsilon^{2}u_{2}+...,

(4)

\displaystyle\Delta\theta

\displaystyle=\Delta\theta_{0}+\epsilon\Delta\theta_{1}+\epsilon^{2}\Delta\theta_{2}+....

u
is assumed to be the positive down-slope wind component in the katabatic case and assumed to be negative for the up-slope wind component in the anabatic case.

Grisogono and Oerlemans 2001a
as well as
Grisogono et al. 2015
selected a height-dependent eddy thermal diffusivity function for
z\geq z_{0}
:

\displaystyle K_{H}(z)=K_{0}\frac{z}{h}\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right),

(5)

which depends on two parameters related to the maximum value of
K_{H}(z)
and the elevation
h\,\mathrm{[m]}
, where the maximum is attained, i.e.
(K_{0},h)
.

(u_{0},\Delta\theta_{0})
are the solutions of the WKB
ϵ=0
model and well known from literature to be defined as follows:

\displaystyle u_{0,WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=-C\mu\exp(-I(z))\sin(I(z)),

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{0,WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=C\exp(-I(z))\cos(I(z)),

where

\displaystyle I(z)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int\limits_{z_{0}}^{z}K_{H}(z)^{-\frac{1}{2}}dz,

and
\sigma_{0}=N_{\alpha}Pr^{-1/2}
with
N_{\alpha}=N\sin(\alpha)
,

, and
\mu=\sqrt{g/(\theta_{0}\Gamma_{0}Pr)}
.
It’s worth noting that the original Prandtl solution may be recovered by means of setting
K_{H}(z)=K_{H}
.

Grisogono et al. 2015
showed that first order solutions in the WKB
ϵ
model are given as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{1,WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=\Delta\theta_{A,WKB}\exp(-I(z))\times\left(-\frac{1}{15}\sin(I(z))-\frac{1}{6}\cos(I(z))\right)

(7)

\displaystyle+\Delta\theta_{A,WKB}

\displaystyle\exp(-2I(z))\times\left(\frac{1}{15}\sin(2I(z))+\frac{1}{15}\cos(2I(z))+\frac{1}{10}\right),

\displaystyle u_{1,WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=u_{A,WKB}\exp(-I(z))\times\left(-\frac{1}{3}\sin(I(z))+\frac{2}{15}\cos(I(z))\right)

\displaystyle+u_{A,WKB}

\displaystyle\exp(-2I(z))\times\left(\frac{1}{30}\sin(2I(z))-\frac{1}{30}\cos(2I(z))-\frac{1}{10}\right),

where the amplitudes are

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{A,WKB}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}C^{2}\mu\sin(\alpha)K_{H}(z)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(8)

\displaystyle u_{A,WKB}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}C^{2}\frac{\mu}{\Gamma_{0}}K_{H}(z)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

The boundary conditions for these solutions are as follows:
\Delta\theta_{1,WKB}(z_{0})=0
,
u_{1,WKB}(z_{0})=0
,
\Delta\theta_{1,WKB}(z\rightarrow\infty)=0
, and
u_{1,WKB}(z\rightarrow\infty)=0
. The total WKB
ϵ
solutions up to the first order are given by:

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=\Delta\theta_{0,WKB}(z)+\epsilon\Delta\theta_{1,WKB}(z),

(9)

\displaystyle u_{WKB}(z)

\displaystyle=u_{0,WKB}(z)+\epsilon u_{1,WKB}(z).

Based on empirical arguments,

proposed to select in the katabatic case of a stable atmosphere

According to

the first order solutions (

The friction parameters

\theta_{*}

\displaystyle u_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle=|\langle u,w\rangle_{z=z_{0}}|,

(10)

\displaystyle\theta_{*}u_{*}

\displaystyle=-\langle\theta,w\rangle_{z=z_{0}},

\displaystyle u_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle\theta_{*}u_{*}

Translating
u_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
, and
Q_{H}
for flat terrain to those for slopes and the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model

To calculate slope winds in an alpine valley for a pollutant dispersion model in micro-meteorology, the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model is required.
What we get from our model in the first step are
u_{*},\theta_{*},Q_{H}
from Monin-Obukhov similarity theory for flat terrain. In this part, we illustrate how to convert these values into those for the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model in steep terrain.

We make the following conventions:
u_{*},\theta_{*}
, and
Q_{H}
are the variables for flat terrain and
u_{0_{*}},\theta_{0_{*}}
, and
Q_{H,0}
for steep terrain with slope angle
0<\alpha<\pi/2
.

Furthermore, we assume that
u_{*},\theta_{*},Q_{H},u_{0_{*}},\theta_{0_{*}}
, and
Q_{H,0}
are defined as usual:

\displaystyle u_{*}^{2}

\displaystyle=|\langle u,w\rangle_{z_{1}}|,

(17)

\displaystyle\theta_{*}u_{*}

\displaystyle=-\langle\theta,w\rangle_{z_{1}},

\displaystyle Q_{H}

\displaystyle=-\rho c_{p}\theta_{*}u_{*},

\displaystyle u_{0_{*}}^{2}

\displaystyle=|\langle u_{0},w_{0}\rangle_{z_{0}}|,

\displaystyle\theta_{0_{*}}u_{0_{*}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\theta_{0},w_{0}\rangle_{z_{0}},

\displaystyle Q_{H,0}

\displaystyle=-\rho c_{p}\theta_{0_{*}}u_{0_{*}},

where
z_{0}=z_{1}\cdot\cos(\alpha)
is the roughness length of the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model and
z_{1}
is the roughness length for the flat terrain.

Figure 6:
Relation between residual wind
u,w
and
u_{0},w_{0}
in a katabatic case

Vertical up-winds are negative, while vertical down-winds are positive. Also, winds down the hill parallel to the slope are positive, winds up the hill parallel to the slope are negative. Winds up orthogonal to the slope are negative, those down are positive.
We can deduce the following relationship since the sensible heat flux is always normal to the surface:

Q_{H,0}=\cos(\alpha)Q_{H}.

(18)

Considering (
17
) and (
18
) leads to the following relation:

\displaystyle\theta_{0_{*}}u_{0_{*}}

\displaystyle=\cos(\alpha)\theta_{*}u_{*}.

(19)

In order to find relationships between
u_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
and
u_{0_{*}}
,
\theta_{0_{*}}
we assume a normally distributed wind vector residual
(u,w)
at
z_{1}
with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. We must preserve this covariance matrix but transfer it into the new coordinate system if we wish to derive the covariance matrix of
(u_{0},w_{0})
at
z_{0}
. This can be accomplished in four steps:

First: Define the covariance matrix of
(u,w)
at
z_{1}
as follows:

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{u}^{2}&&u_{*}^{2}\\
&\\
\ u_{*}^{2}&&\sigma_{w}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\sigma_{u}^{2}
and
\sigma_{w}^{2}
are the variances of
(u,w)
at
z_{1}
. Note that the parameters
\sigma_{u}^{2}
and
\sigma_{w}^{2}
depend on the Pasquill-Gifford stability classes, and mixing layer height
h_{mix}\,\mathrm{[m]}
and can be calculated using the standard formulas given, e.g.
De Visscher 2014
.

Second: Calculate the eigendecomposition of
\Sigma
resulting in:

\Sigma=V\Lambda V^{\top},

where
V
is the matrix of orthonormal eigenvectors and
\Lambda
is the diagonal matrix of the corresponding eigenvalues.

Third: The covariance matrix in the new coordinate system is obtained by rotating the system by
\alpha
degrees:

V_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha)&&-\sin(\alpha)\\
&\\
\sin(\alpha)&&\cos(\alpha)\\
\end{pmatrix}\ V.

From this we get the covariance matrix in the new coordinate system as follows:

\Sigma_{0}=V_{0}\Lambda V_{0}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{u_{0}}^{2}&&u_{0_{*}}^{2}\\
&\\
\ u_{0_{*}}^{2}&&\sigma_{w_{0}}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

This covariance matrix shows the change from
u_{*}^{2}
to
u_{0_{*}}^{2}
in the Prandtl system.

Fourth: We are now ready to calculate
\theta_{0_{*}}
from (
19
), resulting in:

\theta_{0_{*}}=\cos(\alpha)\theta_{*}\frac{u_{*}}{u_{0_{*}}}.

7
Combining models for steep and flat terrain

Our own application of the theory discussed earlier involves using it to calculating pollutant dispersion in an alpine valley.
The key difficulty now is how to merge the models for flat and steep terrain. We must first calculate the height at which these two models overlap. We call this height Prandtl-layer height and calculate it as follows:

h_{0}=\arg\min\limits_{z_{*}}\left\{\frac{100}{2}\Biggl(\frac{|u_{WKB}(z)-u_{MO}(z_{*})|}{|u_{MO}(z_{*})|}+\frac{|\theta_{WKB}(z)-\theta_{MO}(z_{*})|}{|\theta_{MO}(z_{*})|}\Biggl)\right\},

(20)

where
z_{*}=z/\cos{(\alpha)}
;
u_{WKB}(z)
is taken from (
9
) and
\theta_{WKB}(z)
from (
3
) and (
9
).
u_{MO}(.)
and
\theta_{MO}(.)
are corresponding parameters for flat terrain based on Monin-Obukhov similarity theory, e.g.
De Visscher 2014
. Eq. (
20
) minimizes the mean relative error between
u_{WKB}(z),u_{MO}(z_{*})
, and
\theta_{WKB}(z),\theta_{MO}(z_{*})
to get
h_{0}
.

We now are able to define a combined model: For
z_{*}\leq h_{0}
we assume the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model to be true; for
z_{*}>h_{0}
we accept the Monin-Obukhov similarity theory for flat terrain. An illustrating example can be seen in Fig.
7
. The piecewise behavior arises due to the transition occurring at the mixing layer’s height, as explained in
De Visscher 2014
.

The first approach assumes that the wind speeds measured at station height
h_{s}\,\mathrm{\,[m]}
are computed from the flat Monin-Obukhov logarithmic models. Using this assumption we interpolate these wind speeds at
h_{s}
on a square grid by means of inverse distance weighting. From these interpolated wind speeds we calculate
u_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
, and
Q_{H}
. Using the relationships given in Sect.
6
the parameters can be transformed to the corresponding
u_{0_{*}}
,
\theta_{0_{*}}
, and
Q_{H,0}
for the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl models.
Finally, we use again a relative error function to solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle f(\widehat{u_{0_{*}}},\widehat{\theta_{0_{*}}},\widehat{u_{*}},\widehat{\theta_{*}})=

(21)

\displaystyle\Biggl(\left(\frac{u_{WKB}(h_{s}\cdot\cos(\alpha))-u_{MO}(h_{s})}{u_{MO}(h_{s})}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\theta_{WKB}(h_{s}\cdot\cos(\alpha))-\theta_{MO}(h_{s})}{\theta_{MO}(h_{s})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{u_{0_{*}}-\widehat{u_{0_{*}}}}{u_{0_{*}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\theta_{0_{*}}-\widehat{\theta_{0_{*}}}}{\theta_{0_{*}}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{u_{*}-\widehat{u_{*}}}{u_{*}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\theta_{*}-\widehat{\theta_{*}}}{\theta_{*}}\right)^{2}\Biggl)^{1/2}\frac{100}{\sqrt{6}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\min\limits_{(\widehat{u_{0_{*}}},\widehat{\theta_{0_{*}}},\widehat{u_{*}},\widehat{\theta_{*}})}.

In above function
u_{*},\theta_{*}
are fixed and
u_{0_{*}},\theta_{0_{*}}
are given by means of (
3
) and (
12
).

and
\alpha=5^{o}

The following is another approach for combining the two models. To begin, establish the following weights for wind speed and potential temperature:

\displaystyle\beta(z_{*})

\displaystyle=\mbox{min}\Biggl\{\frac{|u_{WKB}(z)|}{|u_{MO}(z_{*})|},\frac{|u_{MO}(z_{*})|}{|u_{WKB}(z)|}\Biggl\}^{2},

(22)

\displaystyle\gamma(z_{*})

\displaystyle=\mbox{min}\Biggl\{\frac{|\theta_{WKB}(z)|}{|\theta_{MO}(z_{*})|},\frac{|\theta_{MO}(z_{*})|}{|\theta_{WKB}(z)|}\Biggl\}^{2}.

Second, arrange these weights in a convex combination to calculate total wind speed and potential temperature:

\displaystyle u(z_{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(z_{*})\ u_{WKB}(z)+(1-\beta(z_{*}))\ u_{MO}(z_{*}),

(23)

\displaystyle\theta(z_{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(z_{*})\ \theta_{WKB}(z)+(1-\gamma(z_{*}))\ \theta_{MO}(z_{*}).

The partial differential equation below is obtained by combining Eq. (
1
) with Navier-Stokes equations and utilising the weights from Eq. (
22
).

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\beta(z_{*})\left(C_{2}+K_{H}Pr\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{2}}\right)+(1-\beta(z_{*}))\left(K_{H}Pr\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{*^{2}}}+C_{1}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{I}_{\beta(z_{*})\leq 0.5}\ \beta(z_{*})C_{1}+\mathbb{I}_{\beta(z_{*})>0.5}\ (1-\beta(z_{*}))C_{2},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\gamma(z_{*})\left(C_{3}+K_{H}\frac{\partial^{2}\Delta\theta}{\partial z^{2}}\right)+(1-\gamma(z_{*}))\left(K_{H}\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial z^{*^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{I}_{\gamma(z_{*})\leq 0.5}\ \gamma(z_{*})C_{3}+\mathbb{I}_{\gamma(z_{*})>0.5}\ (1-\gamma(z_{*}))C_{3}

with

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial z_{*}}+g,

(25)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=g\frac{\Delta\theta}{\theta_{0}}\sin(\alpha),

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=-\left(\Gamma_{0}+\epsilon\frac{\partial\Delta\theta}{\partial z}\right)u\sin(\alpha),

\displaystyle\Delta\theta(z)

\displaystyle=\theta(z_{*})-\theta_{0}-\Gamma_{0}(z-z_{0}),

and
\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function.
The boundary conditions for our example are selected as follows:
\theta(z_{*}=h_{s})=\theta_{WKB}(h_{s}\cdot\cos(\alpha))
,
\theta(z_{*}\rightarrow\infty)=\theta(h_{mix})
,
u(z_{*}=h_{s})=u_{WKB}(h_{s}\cdot\cos(\alpha))=u_{MO}(h_{s})
, and
u(z_{*}\rightarrow\infty)=u(h_{mix})
. Hence, both wind speed and potential temperature are assumed to remain constant above
h_{mix}
. The mixing layer height is calculated as recommended in
De Visscher 2014
.
Equation (
24
) reduces to the Prandtl system for
\beta=\gamma=1
and to the Monin-Obukhov system for
\beta=\gamma=0
. A regularized combination of the Prandtl and Monin-Obukhov system is realized if
0<\beta,\gamma<1
, see Fig.
8
.

ϵ

Conclusions

The purpose of this work was to find wind profiles, including slope flows, that can be used to model air dispersion in an alpine valley.
Slope flows are a major topic in boundary layer meteorology for a variety of reasons. The modification of
Grisogono et al. 2015
was used to address these flows, making the general Prandtl model weakly nonlinear.

We demonstrated how to obtain friction velocity, friction temperature, and sensible heat flux from the WKB
ϵ
Prandtl model and vice versa. Optimization routines were utilised to estimate these values in various function calls, and we demonstrated the effectiveness of our techniques with multiple examples. When the range of
K_{0}
- and
h
values was too large, problems with applying our R-functions and parameterization occurred because convergence of our methods based on the L-BFGS-B routine and interval bisection was no longer guaranteed. It’s worth noting that we tested another optimization routine called SANN, however it was inefficient when compared to L-BFGS-B.

One of the main points in this study was the translation of Monin-Obukhov similarity theory friction parameters and sensible heat flux to those for complex terrain with slopes and vice versa. Geometrical analysis, including coordinate rotation, and statistical arguments were used to accomplish this translation.

Finally, we discussed the technique to combine the models for flat and steep terrain. This can be achieved using an optimization routine or by solving a variation of the Navier-Stokes equation with a convex combination for total wind speed and potential temperature. For both approaches, we supplied examples. We advise using the second method because of its superior accuracy if the two are compared.
We tested all algorithms in this article to be valid also for different slope angles
\alpha
between
0^{\circ}
and
20^{\circ}
. The results obtained are comparable to those presented in this article and can be replicated utilizing the code provided in the ancillary files accessible at arXiv.org. For angles exceeding 20 degrees, there exists a significant correlation between the horizontal and vertical wind directions, attributable to the rotation of the coordinate systems. Nevertheless, it is crucial for the model that these wind directions remain uncorrelated.
From a broader perspective, the results presented in this article can be applicable for simulating pollutant dispersion across the entire micro-meteorology over intricate terrains.
