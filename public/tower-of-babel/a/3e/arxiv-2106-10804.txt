Quantum Hotspots: Mean Fields, Open EFTs, Nonlocality and Decoherence Near Black Holes
C.P. Burgess, R. Holman, and G. Kaplanek

Abstract
Effective theories describing black hole exteriors resemble open quantum systems inasmuch as many unmeasurable degrees of freedom beyond the horizon interact with those we can see. A solvable Caldeira-Leggett type model of a quantum field that mixes with many unmeasured thermal degrees of freedom on a shared surface was proposed in arXiv:2106.09854 to provide a benchmark against which more complete black hole calculations might be compared. We here use this model to test two types of field-theoretic approximation schemes that also lend themselves to describing black hole behaviour: Open EFT techniques (as applied to the fields themselves, rather than Unruh-DeWitt detectors) and mean-field methods. Mean-field methods are of interest because the effective Hamiltonians to which they lead can be nonlocal; a possible source for the nonlocality that is sometimes entertained as being possible for black holes in the near-horizon regime. Open EFTs compute the evolution of the field state, allowing discussion of thermalization and decoherence even when these occur at such late times that perturbative methods fail (as they often do). Applying both of these methods to a solvable system identifies their domains of validity and shows how their predictions relate to more garden-variety perturbative tools.

3.1
Open EFT evolution

In principle the time evolution of the system’s state is given within the Interaction picture by the Liouville equation,

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\ =\ -i\big[H_{\rm int}(t),\rho(t)\big]

(3.2)

where
H_{\mathrm{int}}(t)
is given by eq. (
2.7
), and where
\rho(t)
is the interaction-picture state

\rho(t)=e^{+iH_{0}t}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,e^{-iH_{0}t}

(3.3)

where
H_{0}={\cal H}_{+}\otimes{\cal I}_{-}+{\cal I}_{+}\otimes{\cal H}_{-}
is the free Hamiltonian for the combined system. It is easy to see from the above that the Schrödinger-picture reduced density matrix
\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)
is related to the interaction-picture reduced density matrix
\sigma(t)
through the relation

\sigma(t)=e^{+i{\cal H}_{+}t}\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,e^{-i{\cal H}_{+}t}\ .

(3.4)

When solving these equations we assume the uncorrelated initial state
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}=\rho_{+}\otimes\rho_{-}
given in (
2.9
) where
\rho_{+}=\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}
and
\rho_{-}=\varrho_{\beta}
is the thermal configuration for the
\chi
sector.

3.1.1
Nakajima-Zwanzig equation

In principle the evolution of
\sigma(t)
is given by taking the trace of (
3.2
) over all unmeasured degrees of freedom (in this case the fields
\chi^{a}
). In perturbation theory one usually first formally solves (
3.2
) and then takes the trace of the result, leading to

\sigma(t)=\sigma(0)-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\;\mathrm{Tr}_{-}\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(s)\,,\rho_{0}\Bigr]+(-i)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s_{1}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}{\hbox{d}}s_{2}\;\mathrm{Tr}_{-}\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(s_{1})\,,\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(s_{2})\,,\rho_{0}\Bigr]\Bigr]+\cdots

(3.5)

where
\mathrm{Tr}_{-}
denotes the partial trace only over the
\chi^{a}
sector. The drawback of this expression is the relatively complicated dependence of its right-hand side on the full system’s state. Because the right-hand side refers explicitly to the initial state
\rho_{0}
successive terms in the series generically grow without bound for large
t
, causing the perturbative approximation to fail at late times and precluding accessing issues like thermalization and late-time decoherence.

The better route for late-time purposes is to take the trace of the differential relation (
3.2
) and to eliminate from this the dependence of the right-hand side on any unmeasured degrees of freedom (for a review of the steps given below see for example
EFTBook
). The good news is that because of the linearity of (
3.2
) this can be done in great generality, with the resulting evolution equation for
\sigma(t)
known as the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation
Nak
;
Zwan
. Although this is a textbook derivation, we now describe it in some detail since it is not often applied to quantum fields (as we do here) in the relativity literature.

The logic of the derivation proceeds as follows. One first defines the super-operator
\mathscr{P}
acting on operators in the Hilbert space by

\mathscr{P}({\cal O})\ =\ {\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\left[{\cal O}\right]\otimes\rho_{-}\ ,

(3.6)

where
\rho_{-}=\varrho_{\beta}
is the time-independent initial thermal density matrix for the fields
\chi^{a}
. This is a projection super-operator because it satisfies
\mathscr{P}^{2}=\mathscr{P}
, as therefore must its complement
\mathscr{R}=1-\mathscr{P}
. The definition (
3.6
) is defined so that it projects the full density matrix
\rho(t)
onto the reduced density matrix
\sigma(t)
,

\mathscr{P}\big[\rho(t)\big]=\sigma(t)\otimes\rho_{-}\ ,

(3.7)

and so
\mathscr{R}[\rho(t)]
can be regarded as describing all of the unmeasured parts of the full density matrix.

Our goal is therefore to rewrite the Liouville equation as a coupled set of evolution equations for the mutually exclusive quantities
\mathscr{P}\big[\rho(t)\big]
and
\mathscr{R}\big[\rho(t)\big]
, and then eliminate
\mathscr{R}\big[\rho(t)\big]
from these by solving its equation as a function of
\mathscr{P}\big[\rho(t)\big]
. To this end write the interaction-picture Liouville equation (
3.2
) in terms of a linear Liouville super-operator,

\partial_{t}\rho=\mathscr{L}_{t}(\rho)\quad\quad\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\quad\quad\mathscr{L}_{t}(\rho)=-i[H_{\rm int}(t),\rho\,]\ ,

(3.8)

and project the result using the operators
\mathscr{P}
and
\mathscr{R}
. Since
\mathscr{P}+\mathscr{R}=1
this leads to

\mathscr{P}(\partial_{t}\rho)=\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{P}(\rho)+\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{R}(\rho)

(3.9)

and

\mathscr{R}(\partial_{t}\rho)=\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{P}(\rho)+\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{R}(\rho)\,.

(3.10)

The unmeasured degrees of freedom are eliminated by formally solving eq. (
3.10
):

\mathscr{R}[\rho(t)]={\cal G}(t,0)\mathscr{R}[\rho(0)]+\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \mathcal{G}(t,s)\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{P}[\rho(s)]

(3.11)

with

\mathcal{G}(t,s)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{s}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s_{1}\ \cdots\int_{s}^{s_{n-1}}{\hbox{d}}s_{n}\ \mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{s_{1}}\cdots\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{s_{n}}\,,

(3.12)

and inserting the result into (
3.9
). This yields the desired self-contained evolution equation for
\mathscr{P}[\rho(t)]
:

\mathscr{P}[\partial_{t}\rho(t)]=\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{P}[\rho(t)]+\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}{\cal G}(t,0)\mathscr{R}[\rho(0)]+\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \mathcal{K}(t,s)[\rho(s)]\quad

(3.13)

with kernel

\mathcal{K}(t,s)=\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}{\cal G}(t,s)\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{s}\mathscr{P}\,.

(3.14)

For uncorrelated initial states of the form
\rho(0)=\rho_{+}\otimes\rho_{-}
the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.13
) vanishes because
\mathscr{P}\big[\rho(0)\big]=\rho(0)
and so
\mathscr{R}\big[\rho(0)\big]=0
.

In what follows we wish to use (
3.13
) but work only to second order in
H_{\rm int}(t)
, which means expanding out the kernel
\mathcal{K}(t,s)
to second order in
\mathscr{L}_{t}
. At this order we can therefore take
{\cal G}(t,s)\simeq 1
in
\mathcal{K}(t,s)
, which becomes

\mathcal{K}(t,s)\simeq\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{s}\mathscr{P}\,,

(3.15)

and so (
3.13
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}[\partial_{t}\rho(t)]

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{P}[\rho(t)]+\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \mathscr{P}\mathscr{L}_{t}\mathscr{R}\mathscr{L}_{s}\mathscr{P}[\rho(s)]\,.

(3.16)

Writing this out explicitly using the definitions of
\mathscr{P}
,
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathscr{L}_{t}
then gives the more explicit form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-i\;{\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\Bigl\{\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(t),\sigma(t)\otimes\rho_{-}\Bigr]\Bigr\}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ {\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\left\{\bigg[H_{\rm int}(t)\,,\,\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(s)\,,\,\sigma(s)\otimes\rho_{-}\Bigr]-{\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\Bigl(\Bigl[H_{\rm int}(s),\sigma(s)\otimes\rho_{-}\Bigr]\Bigr)\otimes\rho_{-}\bigg]\right\}\,.

To apply this expression to the hotspot fields expand
H_{\rm int}(t)
in terms of a basis of operators with the factorized form

H_{\rm int}(t)={\cal A}^{\scriptscriptstyle A}(t)\otimes{\cal B}_{\scriptscriptstyle A}(t)\,,

(3.18)

where
{\cal A}^{\scriptscriptstyle A}
acts only in the
\phi
sector and
{\cal B}_{\scriptscriptstyle A}
acts only in the
\chi^{a}
sector. With this choice (
3.16
) simplifies to become

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}\simeq-i\;\Bigl[{\cal A}_{a}(t)\,,\,\sigma(t)\Bigr]{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal B}^{a}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}

(3.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \bigg(\Bigl[{\cal A}^{a}(s)\,\sigma(s)\,,{\cal A}^{b}(t)\Bigr]{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal B}_{b}(t){\cal B}_{a}(s)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\Bigl[{\cal A}^{b}(t)\,,\sigma(s){\cal A}^{a}(s)\Bigr]{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal B}_{a}(s)\,{\cal B}_{b}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \bigg(\Bigl[{\cal A}^{a}(s)\sigma(s)\,,{\cal A}^{b}(t)\Bigr]+\Bigl[{\cal A}^{b}(t)\,,\sigma(s){\cal A}^{a}(s)\Bigr]\Bigr]\bigg){{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal B}_{b}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal B}_{a}(s)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\,.

where
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal O}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}:={\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\,[\rho_{-}\,{\cal O}]={\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\,[\varrho_{\beta}{\cal O}]
is the thermal trace for operators acting purely in the
\chi
sector.

For the point-hotspot system the interaction Hamiltonian given in (
2.8
) has the form
H_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=g_{a}\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes\chi^{a}(t,\mathbf{0})+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})\otimes{\cal I}_{-}
(in the Interaction picture) and so using
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\chi^{a}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=0
the second-order Nakajima-Zwanzig equation reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}\simeq-\frac{i\lambda}{2}\Bigl[\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0}),\sigma(t)\Bigr]+g_{a}g_{b}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \bigg({{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\chi^{b}(t,\mathbf{0})\chi^{a}(s,\mathbf{0})\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\Bigl[\phi(s,\mathbf{0})\,\sigma(s)\,,\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]

(3.20)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\chi^{a}(s,\mathbf{0})\,\chi^{b}(t,\mathbf{0})\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\Bigl[\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma(s)\phi(s,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]\bigg)

We write the thermal correlation function for two
\chi
fields as

g_{a}g_{b}{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\chi^{b}(t,\mathbf{0})\chi^{a}(s,\mathbf{0})\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=:\tilde{g}^{2}\mathscr{W}(t-s)=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\beta^{2}}\;{\rm csch}^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(t-s-i\delta)\right]

(3.21)

where
\delta=0^{+}
goes to zero at the end of any calculation and the first equality defines the function
\mathscr{W}(t)
with
\tilde{g}^{2}:=\delta^{ab}\,g_{a}g_{b}
as given in (
2.5
), while the second equality uses the explicit form for the
{{\bf x}}^{\prime}\to{{\bf x}}
limit of the correlation function given in (
2.20
).

Finally, after a change of integration variables
s\to t-s
we arrive at the form for the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation whose properties are explored below:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}\simeq-\frac{i\lambda}{2}\Bigl[\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0}),\sigma(t)\Bigr]

(3.22)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+g^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\ \bigg(\mathscr{W}(s)\Bigl[\phi(t-s,\mathbf{0})\,\sigma(t-s)\,,\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]+\mathscr{W}^{\ast}(s)\Bigl[\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma(t-s)\phi(t-s,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]\bigg)\ .

3.1.2
Markovian limit

Since the correlation function
\mathscr{W}(s)
is sharply peaked about
s=0
and falls off exponentially fast like
\mathscr{W}(s)\propto e^{-{2\pi s}/{\beta}}
for
s\gg\beta
, the integral simplifies if the rest of the integrand varies more slowly in the region where
\mathscr{W}
varies quickly. In such a case the integral is well-approximated by expanding the rest of the integrand in powers of
s
, using

\displaystyle\phi(t-s)\sigma(t-s)\simeq\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,\sigma(t)-s\big[\partial_{t}\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,\sigma(t)+\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\partial_{t}\sigma(t)\big]+\ldots

(3.23)

beneath the integral sign in (
3.22
). Notice that this assumes
both

\phi
and
\sigma
vary slowly, and so its justification requires both that
\sigma(t)
should be slowly varying and that we work in an effective description that keeps only those modes of
\phi
whose energies satisfy
E\ll 1/\beta
. This becomes relevant when choosing momentum cutoffs for later integrals, since this Markovian derivation requires
\Lambda\ll 1/\beta
. Part of the discussion to follow aims to identify more precisely the region of parameter space for which this Markovian approximation is valid (which we find by asking when the subleading terms in the series (
3.23
) are small).

Keeping only the leading-order term in the Taylor-series (
3.23
) yields the approximate equation of motion

\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}\simeq-\frac{i\lambda}{2}\Bigl[\phi^{2}(t,\mathbf{0}),\sigma(t)\Bigr]+\tilde{g}^{2}\mathscr{C}\Bigl[\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,\sigma(t)\,,\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]+\tilde{g}^{2}\mathscr{C}^{*}\Bigl[\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma(t)\phi(t,\mathbf{0})\Bigr]\,.

(3.24)

where the coefficient is given by

\mathscr{C}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}s\,\mathscr{W}(s)

(3.25)

and the approximate equality requires
t\gg\beta
so that the integration includes the strong peaking of
\mathscr{W}(s)
(with exponential fall-off) noted above. Although the coefficient
\mathscr{C}(t)
is in principle a function of
t
, in practice the narrowly peaked form of
\mathscr{W}(t)
ensures it approaches a constant exponentially quickly once
t\gg\beta
. The value of this constant can be evaluated explicitly by taking the upper integration limit to infinity and evaluating the resulting integral using expression (
3.21
) for
\mathscr{W}(t)
:

\mathscr{C}(t)\simeq\mathscr{C}_{\infty}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}s\,\mathscr{W}(s)=-\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\hbox{d}}s}{\sinh^{2}\big[\tfrac{\pi}{\beta}(s-i\delta)\big]}\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi\beta}-\frac{i}{4\pi^{2}\beta}\left[\frac{\beta}{\delta}+\mathcal{O}\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta}{\beta}$}}\right)\right]\,,

(3.26)

for
t\gg\beta
. The divergence as
\delta\to 0
is a reflection of the divergence of the integrand as
s\to 0
.

It is sometimes useful to convert (
3.24
) to the Schrödinger picture, as is done by noting that

e^{-i{\cal H}_{+}t}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}e^{+i{\cal H}_{+}t}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}{\partial t}+i[{\cal H}_{+},\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)]\ .

(3.27)

which follows from the relation (
3.4
), giving

\frac{\partial\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}{\partial t}\simeq-i\Bigl[{\cal H}_{+}+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\lambda}{2}$}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}^{2}(\mathbf{0}),\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]+\tilde{g}^{2}\mathscr{C}\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t),\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]+\tilde{g}^{2}\mathscr{C}^{*}\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]\,.

(3.28)

Using this in (
3.28
) finally gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-i\Bigl[{\cal H}_{+}+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\lambda}{2}$}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}^{2}(\mathbf{0}),\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]

(3.29)

\displaystyle\ \quad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{i}{\pi\delta}\right)\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,,\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{i}{\pi\delta}\right)\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Bigl[{\cal H}_{+}+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\lambda_{\rm ren}}{2}$}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}^{2}(\mathbf{0}),\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi\beta}\Bigl(\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,,\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]+\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\,,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\,\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{0})\Bigr]\Bigr)\,,

which shows that the divergence can be absorbed into the renormalization

\lambda_{\mathrm{ren}}\ :=\ \lambda-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}\delta}\,.

(3.30)

3.1.3
Evolution equation in a field basis

It is easiest to solve an equation like (
3.29
) in a basis that diagonalizes the interaction Hamiltonian, and in this instance this suggests using a basis of field eigenstates defined as the basis that diagonalizes the Schrödinger-picture field operator
\phi(0,{{\bf x}})=\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}({{\bf x}})
:

\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}({{\bf x}})\ket{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}=\varphi({{\bf x}})\ket{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}

(3.31)

where the eigenvalue
\varphi({{\bf x}})
is a real-valued function of position.

The wave-functional of the free vacuum
\ket{\hbox{vac}}
in this reprsentation is given by a gaussian
Weinberg:1995mt

\displaystyle\bra{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}\hbox{vac}{\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{0}}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int d^{3}{{\bf y}}\ \mathcal{E}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})\varphi({{\bf x}})\varphi({{\bf y}})\right]

(3.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{0}}\;\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;E_{p}\;\varphi_{{\bf p}}\varphi_{-{{\bf p}}}\right]

where the kernel
\mathcal{E}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})
is given by

\mathcal{E}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})=\int\frac{d^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ E_{p}\ e^{i{{\bf p}}\cdot({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})}\,,

(3.33)

where
E_{p}:=|{{\bf p}}|
and the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{0}
is determined by using the normalization condition
\langle\hbox{vac}|\hbox{vac}\rangle=1
.

In the field basis the reduced density matrix is a time-dependent functional of the basis field configurations
\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})
and
\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})
, with components

\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]:={\langle}\varphi_{1}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}|\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)|\varphi_{2}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}{\rangle}

which the Markovian equation (

) implies must satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-i\bra{\varphi_{1}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}\Bigl[{\cal H}_{+},\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]\ket{\varphi_{2}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}

(3.35)

\displaystyle\qquad-\bigg[\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi\beta}\big(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{0})-\varphi_{2}(\mathbf{0})\big)^{2}+\frac{i\lambda_{\rm ren}}{2}\big(\varphi_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{0})-\varphi^{2}_{2}(\mathbf{0})\big)\bigg]\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]\ .

We henceforth drop the subscript ‘ren’ on the renormalized coupling parameter

. Evaluating the commutator term using

\bra{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}{\cal H}_{+}\ket{\Psi}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\bigg[-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta\varphi({{\bf x}})^{2}}+\big|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi({{\bf x}})\big|^{2}\bigg]{\langle}\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}|\Psi{\rangle}\ ,

(3.36)

for any state

, the equation of motion for

finally becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]}{\partial t}\simeq-\frac{i}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\bigg[-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})^{2}}+\big|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\big|^{2}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})^{2}}-\big|\boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})\big|^{2}\bigg]\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi\beta}\big(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{0})-\varphi_{2}(\mathbf{0})\big)^{2}\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]-\frac{i\lambda}{2}\big(\varphi_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{0})-\varphi^{2}_{2}(\mathbf{0})\big)\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]\ .

As is easily verified, when

this equation has as a solution

\displaystyle\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{1}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}|\hbox{vac}\rangle\,\langle\hbox{vac}|\varphi_{2}{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}\rangle

(3.38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf y}}\;\mathcal{E}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})\big[\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{1}({{\bf y}})+\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})\big]\right)

where the second equality uses (
3.32
) and the kernel

is as defined in equation (

3.2.1
Gaussian ansatz

Keeping in mind that the hotspot ‘interactions’ are all bilinear in the fields we seek solutions to (

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf y}}\;\bigg\{\mathcal{A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\,\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{1}({{\bf y}})+\mathcal{A}_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\,\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})

(3.39)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\quad\quad+2{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\,\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})\bigg\}\bigg)\,,

with the kernels
{\cal A}_{1}
,
{\cal A}_{2}
and

to be determined. Note that we can without loss of generality assume the symmetry

{\cal A}_{j}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)={\cal A}_{j}({{\bf y}},{{\bf x}};t)\ ,

\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}^{\ast}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]=\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi_{2},\varphi_{1};t]

(3.41)

Notice that
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=|\Psi\rangle\,\langle\Psi|
for a gaussian pure state
\langle\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}|\Psi\rangle\propto\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\,K({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})\,\varphi({{\bf x}})\varphi({{\bf y}})\right]
only if

{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})={\cal A}^{*}_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})=K({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})=0\,.

(3.42)

with the free vacuum (
3.38
) corresponding to the choice
K({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})={\cal E}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})
. Since the first of these is an automatic consequence of (
3.41
), this shows that
{\cal A}_{1}
and
{\cal A}_{2}
can be regarded as the deformations of the ground state away from the free result due to the interactions, while having
{\cal B}\neq 0
corresponds to the interaction causing the initially pure state to become mixed.

The kernels are obtained by plugging the ansatz (
3.39
) into (
3.1.3
) and equating the coefficients of the different functional forms on both sides of the equation. The details are worked out in Appendix
A.1
, with the results simply quoted here. Equating the coefficients of terms independent of
\varphi_{i}
implies

\frac{1}{{\cal N}}\frac{\partial{\cal N}}{\partial t}\ =\ -\frac{i}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\Bigl[{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}};t)-{\cal A}_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}};t)\Bigr]\,.

(3.43)

This expression can also be derived from the condition that
{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)=1
for all times. The coefficient of
\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{1}({{\bf y}})
similarly gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\nabla_{{\bf x}}^{2}\delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})+\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}+i\lambda\right)\delta^{3}({{\bf x}})\delta^{3}({{\bf y}})

(3.44)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf z}}\;\Bigl[-i{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf z}},{{\bf x}};t){\cal A}_{1}({{\bf z}},{{\bf y}};t)+i{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf z}};t){\cal B}({{\bf y}},{{\bf z}};t)\Bigr]\,,

while the coefficient of
\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal A}_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\nabla_{{\bf x}}^{2}\delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})+\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}-i\lambda\right)\delta^{3}({{\bf x}})\delta^{3}({{\bf y}})

(3.45)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf z}}\;\Bigl[i{\cal A}_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf z}};t){\cal A}_{2}({{\bf y}},{{\bf z}};t)-i{\cal B}({{\bf z}},{{\bf x}};t){\cal B}({{\bf z}},{{\bf y}};t)\Bigr]\,.

Finally, the coefficient of
\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\delta^{3}({{\bf x}})\delta^{3}({{\bf y}})

(3.46)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf z}}\;\Bigl[-i{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf z}},{{\bf x}};t){\cal B}({{\bf z}},{{\bf y}};t)+i{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf z}};t){\cal A}_{2}({{\bf z}},{{\bf y}};t)\Bigr]\,.

The implications of these equations are simpler to see in momentum space, so we define

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{j}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf q}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;A_{j}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\;e^{+i{{\bf k}}\cdot{{\bf x}}}\,e^{-i{{\bf q}}\cdot{{\bf y}}}

(3.47)

\displaystyle\hbox{and}\quad{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf q}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;B({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\;e^{+i{{\bf k}}\cdot{{\bf x}}}\,e^{-i{{\bf q}}\cdot{{\bf y}}}\,,

for which the symmetry
{\cal A}_{j}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)={\cal A}_{j}({{\bf y}},{{\bf x}};t)
of (
3.40
) implies

\displaystyle A_{j}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\ =\ A_{j}(-{{\bf q}},-{{\bf k}};t)\ .

(3.48)

In terms of these equation (
3.43
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\cal N}}\frac{\partial{\cal N}}{\partial t}\ =\ -\frac{i}{2}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;\Bigl[A_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf k}};t)-A_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf k}};t)\Bigr]

(3.49)

while equations (
3.44
) through (
3.46
) become

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(2\pi)^{3}|{{\bf k}}|^{2}\delta^{3}({{\bf k}}-{{\bf q}})+\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}+i\lambda\right)

(3.50)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Bigl[-iA_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf p}};t)A_{1}({{\bf p}},{{\bf q}};t)+iB({{\bf k}},{{\bf p}};t)B(-{{\bf q}},-{{\bf p}};t)\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i(2\pi)^{3}|{{\bf k}}|^{2}\delta^{3}({{\bf k}}-{{\bf q}})+\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}-i\lambda\right)

(3.51)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Bigl[iA_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf p}};t)A_{2}({{\bf p}},{{\bf q}};t)-iB(-{{\bf p}},-{{\bf k}};t)B({{\bf p}},{{\bf q}};t)\Bigr]

and

\frac{\partial B({{\bf p}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}+\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Bigl[-iA_{1}({{\bf p}},{{\bf k}};t)B({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)+iB({{\bf p}},{{\bf k}};t)A_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\Bigr]\,.

(3.52)

These are the equations we solve in the next few sections. Notice in particular that (
3.46
) or (
3.52
) implies
\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta\neq 0
is an obstruction to
B({{\bf q}},{{\bf x}},t)=0
being a solution.

3.2.2
Perturbative solution

As is easily verified, for
\lambda=\tilde{g}=0
these above equations are solved by

B({{\bf p}},{{\bf q}},t)=0\quad\hbox{and}\quad A_{j}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}})\ =\ (2\pi)^{3}|{{\bf k}}|\;\delta^{3}({{\bf k}}-{{\bf q}})\,,

(3.53)

corresponding to the vacuum solution of (
3.38
). We next seek solutions that are perturbatively close to this vacuum solution, as should be possible for small
\tilde{g}
and
\lambda
.

To this end we write

A_{j}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)=(2\pi)^{3}|{{\bf k}}|\;\delta^{3}({{\bf k}}-{{\bf q}})+{\mathfrak{a}}_{j}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)+\ldots\quad\hbox{and}\quad B({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)={\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)+\ldots\,,

(3.54)

and linearize eqs. (
3.49
) through (
3.52
) in the perturbations
{\mathfrak{a}}_{j}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}
. The resulting evolution equations decouple, to become

\frac{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}+i\lambda-i(|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|)\,{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\,,

(3.55)

\frac{\partial{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}-i\lambda+i(|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|)\,{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)

(3.56)

and

\frac{\partial{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}-i\big(|{{\bf k}}|-|{{\bf q}}|\big)\,{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\,.

(3.57)

These are to be solved subject to the initial conditions

{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};0)={\mathfrak{a}}_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};0)={\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};0)=0\,,

(3.58)

since the scalar
\phi
is starts off in its vacuum state.

The solutions to these initial-value problems are given by

{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)=\left(\lambda-\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\right)\frac{1-e^{-i\big(|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|\big)t}}{|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|}\,,

(3.59)

{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)=\left(\lambda+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\right)\frac{1-e^{+i\big(|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|\big)t}}{|{{\bf k}}|+|{{\bf q}}|}

(3.60)

and

{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)=\left(\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\right)\frac{1-e^{-i(|{{\bf k}}|-|{{\bf q}}|)t}}{|{{\bf k}}|-|{{\bf q}}|}\,.

(3.61)

In the limit
{{\bf k}}={{\bf q}}
(or in the limit of small
t
) this last solution simplifies to

{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)\to-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}t}{2\pi\beta}\qquad\hbox{when }\quad{{\bf k}}\to{{\bf q}}\,.

(3.62)

As remarked earlier — and explored in more detail in §
3.4
— nonzero
{\mathfrak{b}}
is a signature of
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
becoming a mixed state, and so (
3.61
) shows that this only happens for nonzero
\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta
. Furthermore when
\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta
is nonzero there can be no static solution with
\partial_{t}{\mathfrak{b}}=0
, (and in particular no solution with
{\mathfrak{b}}=0
) and
|{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf k}};t)|
monotonically increases. The coupling
\lambda
, by contrast, just deforms the ground state but leaves it pure.

The normalization
{\cal N}(t)
is found in a similar way. Using the above solution in (
3.49
) allows it to be written

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{t}{\cal N}}{{\cal N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Bigl[{\mathfrak{a}}_{1}({{\bf k}},{{\bf k}};t)-{\mathfrak{a}}_{2}({{\bf k}},{{\bf k}};t)\Bigr]

(3.63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}|{{\bf k}}|}\bigg[\lambda\cos(|{{\bf k}}|t)\sin(|{{\bf k}}|t)-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\sin^{2}(|{{\bf k}}|t)\bigg]\,.

The integral on the right-hand side diverges in the ultraviolet, which we regulate using a momentum cutoff
|{{\bf k}}|<\Lambda
, leading to the result

{\cal N}(t)=\exp\left(C_{0}-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Lambda^{2}t}{16\pi^{3}\beta}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{2}t}\sin(2\Lambda t)+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{3}\beta t}\sin^{2}(\Lambda t)\right)\,,

(3.64)

where
C_{0}
is the integration constant. In terms of the initial condition
{\cal N}(0)={\cal N}_{0}
, where
{\cal N}_{0}
is the normalization of the free-vacuum state, we have

{\cal N}_{0}=\exp\left(C_{0}-\frac{\lambda\Lambda}{8\pi^{2}}\right)\,,

(3.65)

and so

{\cal N}(t)={\cal N}_{0}\;\exp\bigg\{\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi^{2}}\bigg[-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Lambda t}{2\pi\beta}\left(1-\frac{\sin^{2}(\Lambda t)}{(\Lambda t)^{2}}\right)+\lambda\left(1-\frac{\sin\left(2\Lambda t\right)}{2\Lambda t}\right)\bigg]\bigg\}\ .

(3.66)

The significance of the divergences in the time-dependence of
{\cal N}(t)
is discussed further in §
3.4
.

3.3
Equal-time
\phi
-correlator

This section uses the reduced density matrix for
\phi
computed in the previous section to calculate the
\langle\phi\,\phi\rangle
two-point function. Comparison of the result with the Wightman function given in §
2
provides a check on the domain of validity of the Nakajima-Zwanzig late-time evolution.

The correlator evaluated at
t=t^{\prime}
is convenient because it has a simple representation in terms of the reduced density matrix. This is because the only evolution operators that appear are the ones that convert between Heisenberg, interaction and Schrödinger pictures:

\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[{\phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle H}(t,{{\bf x}})\,{\phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle H}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,\rho_{0}\Bigr]={\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}\Bigl[{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\,{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,\sigma(t)\Bigr]={\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}\Bigl[{\phi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}({{\bf x}})\,{\phi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}({{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]\,,

(3.67)

where
{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}
in the second two terms denotes a trace only over the
\phi
sector of the Hilbert space.

The calculation based on the field-representation of the Schrödinger-picture reduced density matrix evaluates the trace using a partition of unity written as a functional integral over the field eigenstates,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}\Bigl[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\varphi\;\bra{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\,\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)|\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}\rangle

(3.68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\varphi\;\varphi(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi,\varphi;t]\,,

with
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2};t]
given in terms of the kernels
{\cal A}_{j}
and
{\cal B}
as in (
3.39
). Our focus here is in particular on the
\lambda
and
\tilde{g}
dependent parts of the density matrix, since Appendix
A.3
verifies that the above functional integral correctly reproduces the free-field Wightman function inasmuch as it shows that in the limit
\lambda=\tilde{g}=0
eq. (
3.68
) reproduces the usual expression

\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\,.

(3.69)

Eq. (
3.68
) shows that only the diagonal part of the reduced density matrix is required to compute the equal-time Wightman function. Explicitly, this is given by

\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}[\varphi,\varphi;t]={\cal N}(t)\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf x}}^{\prime}\;{\cal M}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)\varphi({{\bf x}})\varphi({{\bf x}}^{\prime})\bigg)\,,

(3.70)

where

{\cal M}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t):=2\mathrm{Re}\Bigl[\mathcal{A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)+{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\Bigr]\,,

(3.71)

and we use the symmetry (
3.41
). Evaluating the gaussian integrals then implies

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)\int{\cal D}\varphi\;\varphi(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf z}}_{1}\int\rm{d}^{3}{{\bf z}}_{2}\;{\cal M}({{\bf z}}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2};t)\varphi({{\bf z}}_{1})\varphi({{\bf z}}_{2})}

(3.72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{-1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)\,,

where
{\cal M}^{-1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)
is the inverse of
{\cal M}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)
, in the sense that
\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf z}}\;{\cal M}^{-1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf z}};t){\cal M}({{\bf z}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)=\delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})
. The components
{\cal M}^{-1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})
are computed explicitly in Appendix
A.2
using (
3.71
) and the perturbative solutions for
{\cal A}_{j}
and
{\cal B}
given earlier. Using the result in (
3.72
) then gives

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}(|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2})}\bigg[\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)-\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\bigg]

(3.73)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{3}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\,\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\,.

Specializing to the forward light cone of the switch-on of couplings —
i.e.
to
t>|{{\bf x}}|
and
t>|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
— the above becomes

{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}[\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}({{\bf x}}^{\prime})\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)]\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}|\;|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{3}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\ .

(3.74)

Comparison with Heisenberg picture

Expression (
3.74
) is to be compared with the Wightman function computed in Heisenberg picture, whose expansion at linear order in
\lambda
and
\tilde{g}^{2}
is quoted in (
2.17

\displaystyle W_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}

(3.75)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\right]}+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}(|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2})^{2}}\,.

Although the free and the
\lambda
-dependent terms here agree with those in (
3.74
), the same is in general not true for the
\tilde{g}^{2}
-dependent terms. This need not be a problem since they should only be expected to agree within the domain of validity of both, and (
3.74
) is derived under more restrictive assumptions.

Recall in particular that the derivation within the Schrödinger picture approximated the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation (
3.22
) using a Markovian limit in which
\sigma(t-s)\phi(t-s,\mathbf{0})\simeq\sigma(t)\phi(t,\mathbf{0})
is used under the integral sign. Doing this assumes both
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}

\Lambda

\beta\Lambda\ll 1

Within this regime the term involving the hyperbolic function has a microscopic width and so approaches a delta function. To see this explicitly it is easier to work in momentum space, in which case (

\displaystyle W_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

(3.76)

\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\left\{\cos\big[p(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\big]+\bigg[\frac{2}{\beta p}-1+\frac{\beta p}{6}+{\cal O}(p^{3}\beta^{3})\bigg]\cos\big[p(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\big]\;\right\}\,,

(3.77)

and perform the momentum integrals term-by-term, giving

\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\left\{-\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}+\bigg[\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{6}\delta^{\prime\prime}(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)+\ldots\bigg]\right\}\,.

(3.78)

Using this in the Wightman function gives

\displaystyle W_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)}

(3.79)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{3}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\,\delta\big(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}\big(|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big)^{2}}+{\cal O}(\beta)\,.

Notice that the leading term in this expansion indeed matches the Schrodinger-picture calculation. This comparison reveals more explicitly the long-wavelength domain of validity inherent in the Markovian limit,

3.4
Decoherence

Earlier sections explore the behaviour of the
\phi
-field state by computing its reduced density matrix once the hotspot fields
\chi^{a}
are all integrated out. It was there argued that the kernel
{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)
— or equivalently
{\mathfrak{b}}({{\bf k}},{{\bf q}};t)
— provides a diagnostic of whether the state is pure or mixed (because pure states seem to require
{\cal B}={\mathfrak{b}}=0
.)

That is what makes eqs. (
3.57
) and (
3.61
) so interesting; they quantify the rate with which decoherence accumulates when hotspot couplings are turned on with
\phi
prepared in its vacuum state. These equations show in particular the growth of decoherence at early times has a momentum-independent universal rate, set by
\tilde{g}^{2}/(2\pi\beta)
. Since all momenta start to decohere with the same rate, the accumulated decoherence should be dominated by the highest momenta, for which there is the most available phase space. It is for this reason that measures of integrated decoherence – such as the purity diagnostic computed explicitly below – tend to diverge in the ultraviolet, at least so long as the cutoff remains below the characteristic hotspot temperature, as assumed for the Markovian approximation above.

3.4.1
Purity

To pin down the decoherence process more precisely it is useful to have a practical diagnostic for state purity. A standard choice for this is often
{\mathrm{Tr}}_{+}[\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{2}]
since this is unity if and only if the state is pure (in which case
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{2}=\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
).

This trace can be computed as a function of the state kernels
{\cal A}_{j}
and
{\cal B}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\varphi_{1}\int{\cal D}\varphi_{2}\;\bra{\varphi_{1}}\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\ket{\varphi_{2}}\bra{\varphi_{2}}\sigma_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(t)\ket{\varphi_{1}}

(3.80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)^{2}\int{\cal D}\varphi_{1}\int{\cal D}\varphi_{2}\;\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf y}}\;\bigg[2\,\mathrm{Re}\Bigl[{\cal A}_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\Bigr]\Bigl(\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{1}({{\bf y}})+\varphi_{2}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})\Bigr)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+4\,\mathrm{Re}\Bigl[{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)\Bigr]\varphi_{1}({{\bf x}})\varphi_{2}({{\bf y}})\bigg]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(t)^{2}\left\{\det\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\begin{matrix}2\,\mathrm{Re}[{\cal A}_{1}(t)]&2\,\mathrm{Re}[{\cal B}(t)]\\
2\,\mathrm{Re}[{\cal B}(t)]&2\,\mathrm{Re}[{\cal A}_{1}(t)]\end{matrix}\right)\right]\right\}^{-1/2}\,.

In the last line we write quantities
{\cal B}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})
as matrices, with rows and columns labelled by position. In this notation the matrix
\mathrm{Re}[{\cal B}(t)]
is symmetric, since the identity
{\cal B}^{\ast}({{\bf x}},{{\bf y}};t)={\cal B}({{\bf y}},{{\bf x}};t)
means

4
Mean-field methods

One of our goals is to explore the nature of nonlocality in open systems, in hopes that this can shed light on whether nonlocality can also arise near horizons for black holes. Nonlocality in this context traditionally means the extent to which the effective action (or Hamiltonian) is not simply the integral of a Hamiltonian density that depends only on fields and their derivatives at a single point (a property normally held by Wilsonian actions as a consequence of cluster decomposition and microcausality
Weinberg:1995mt
).

Locality is a question for which the language of open quantum systems used above is less well adapted because it frames its predictions in terms of the evolution of the reduced density matrix that is obtained after tracing out any dependence on unmeasured sectors of the Hilbert space, as given by the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation
Nak
;
Zwan
. Although entanglement and information exchange ensure this equation naturally involves nonlocality (particularly in time), the absence of an effective Hamiltonian or action in this approach makes its lessons relatively obscure.

To help understand the issues that are at play this section sets up the mean-field approximation for the hotspot system, since the mean-field limit provides a natural definition of an effective hamiltonian (and effective action) that can provide a good approximation to the system’s evolution in some circumstances even for open systems
EFTBook
. Part of the purpose is to identify the kinds of nonlocality that can emerge for the mean-field Hamiltonian, but at the same time also to identify precisely when the mean-field description is a good approximation to the full dynamics. The hotspot provides a relatively simple test laboratory for exploring these ideas.

4.1
Definitions

The essence of the mean-field approximation is that averages in the unmeasured (or ‘environment’) part of the open system dominate the fluctuations in this sector, allowing an informative expansion in powers of small deviations from the mean. There is some freedom in how to set up this expansion when working beyond the leading order, which we first summarize before computing how things look specifically in the hotspot example.

We use, as before, the ‘double-bracket’ notation

{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal O}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}:={\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\Bigl[\rho_{\beta}\,{\cal O}\Bigr]\quad\hbox{where}\quad\rho_{\beta}:={\cal I}_{+}\otimes\varrho_{\beta}

(4.1)

with
\varrho_{\beta}
being the thermal state appearing in (
2.10
) and the partial trace running only over the environmental sector of the Hilbert space (which in the hotspot example is the sector spanned at the initial time by the
\chi^{a}
fields). For example, for
{\cal O}=\sum_{n}{\cal A}_{n}\otimes{\cal B}_{n}
expanded in terms of a basis of operators acting in the
\phi
and
\chi^{a}
sectors,

{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal O}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=\sum_{n}{\cal A}_{n}\;{\mathrm{Tr}}_{-}\Bigl[\varrho_{\beta}\,{\cal B}_{n}\Bigr]=\sum_{n}{\cal A}_{n}\,\bigl\langle{\cal B}_{n}\bigr\rangle_{\beta}\,.

(4.2)

In particular,
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal O}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}
is an operator that acts only in the measured sector (
i.e.
the
\phi
sector of the hotspot example).

4.1.1
Mean-field evolution

For a perturbative analysis it is worth specializing to the interaction picture, for which expectation values for observables evolve according to

{\cal A}(t):=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(t)\,{\cal O}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\Bigr]=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle I}(t){\cal O}_{\scriptscriptstyle I}(t)\Bigr]=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle I}(0)V^{\star}(t){\cal O}_{\scriptscriptstyle I}(t)V(t)\Bigr]\,,

(4.3)

where
V(t)=U_{0}^{\star}(t)U(t)
is the interaction-picture evolution operator for the state
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle I}(t)
. In what follows we drop the subscript ‘
I
’ for interaction-picture quantities.

For the observables that only measure the
\phi
sector the Schrödinger picture operator has the factorized form
{\cal O}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}={\cal O}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}+}\otimes{\cal I}_{-}
. This factorization remains true in the interaction picture provided that the free part of the Hamiltonian does not couple the two sectors to one another. That is, if
H=H_{0}+H_{{\rm int}}
where
H_{0}={\cal H}_{+}\otimes{\cal I}_{-}+{\cal I}_{+}\otimes{\cal H}_{-}
, then

U_{0}(t):=\exp\Bigl[-iH_{0}t\Bigr]=U_{+}(t)\otimes U_{-}(t)\,,

(4.4)

and so in the interaction picture

{\cal O}(t):=U_{0}^{\star}(t)\,{\cal O}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\,U_{0}(t)={\cal O}_{+}(t)\otimes{\cal I}_{-}

(4.5)

with
{\cal O}_{+}(t)=U_{+}^{\star}(t)\,{\cal O}_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}+}\,U_{+}(t)
.

Now comes the main point: in the mean-field approximation the interaction-picture evolution operator is well-approximated within the observable sector by the operator

\displaystyle\overline{V}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,V(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\,{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(\tau)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\frac{1}{2}(-i)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}{\hbox{d}}\tau_{2}\,{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(\tau_{1})\,H_{\rm int}(\tau_{2})\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\cdots\,,

where
H_{\rm int}(t)
denotes the usual interaction-picture interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\rm int}(t)=U^{\star}_{0}(t)H_{\rm int}U_{0}(t)
. Writing the full evolution operator as a mean-field part plus the rest (with the rest here called the ‘diffuse’ evolution, in analogy to optics)

V(t)=:\overline{V}(t)\otimes{\cal I}_{-}+{\cal V}(t)\,,

(4.7)

the mean-field approximation is a good one when contributions of the diffuse evolution operator,
{\cal V}(t)
are parametrically small.

The point of defining the mean-field evolution using
\overline{V}(t)
(as opposed to simply averaging the Hamiltonian, say) is that this ensures that for any observable of the form (
4.5
) there is no cross-interference – to all orders in perturbation theory – between the mean field evolution and diffuse evolution. That is, computing the expectation of
{\cal O}(t)
using (
4.7
) gives

\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\rho(t)\,{\cal O}(t)\Bigr]=\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[V(t)\rho(0)V^{\star}(t)\,{\cal O}(t)\Bigr]=\mathrm{Tr}_{+}\Bigl[\overline{V}(t)\rho_{+}\overline{V}^{\star}(t)\,{\cal O}_{+}(t)\Bigr]+\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[{\cal V}(t)\rho(0)\,{\cal V}^{\star}(t){\cal O}(t)\Bigr]\,,

(4.8)

with the first trace being only over the measured (
\phi
) sector, and we assume the uncorrelated initial conditions (
2.9
) and (
2.10
). Notice the absence of any cross terms involving both
\overline{V}
and
{\cal V}
, which vanish because (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) together imply
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,{\cal V}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=0
.

4.1.2
Mean-field Hamiltonian

Given the mean-field evolution, the mean-field interaction Hamiltonian,
\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)
, is defined as the operator that generates
\overline{V}(t)
, or equivalently is related to
\overline{V}(t)
by the usual iterative expression

\overline{V}(t)=I-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\,\overline{H}_{\rm int}(\tau)+\frac{1}{2}(-i)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}{\hbox{d}}\tau_{2}\,\overline{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{1})\,\overline{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{2})+\cdots\,.

(4.9)

Comparing this with (
4.6
) we read off

\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)={{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\delta H_{\rm int}(t)\,\delta H_{\rm int}(\tau)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\cdots\,,

(4.10)

where the ellipses represent terms at least third order in
\delta H_{\rm int}
and

\delta H_{\rm int}(t):=H_{\rm int}(t)-{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\,.

(4.11)

Notice that starting at second order in
\delta H_{\rm int}
the mean-field Hamiltonian need not be hermitian, since
\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)={\mathfrak{H}}(t)+i\,{\mathfrak{I}}(t)
with

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}-\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\bigl[\delta H_{\rm int}(t)\,,\delta H_{\rm int}(\tau)\bigr]\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\cdots

\displaystyle\hbox{and}\quad{\mathfrak{I}}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\bigl\{\delta H_{\rm int}(t)\,,\delta H_{\rm int}(\tau)\bigr\}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}+\cdots\,.

(4.12)

The failure of unitarity for
\overline{V}(t)
that arises in this way merely reflects the relatively artificial nature of the mean-field/diffuse split, since ‘diffuse’ interactions with fluctuations in the environment can deplete probability from the mean-field description.

These diffuse interactions are themselves described perturbatively by

{\cal V}(t)\simeq-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\delta H_{\rm int}(\tau)+\cdots\,,

(4.13)

where ellipses now represent terms second-order in
H_{\rm int}
. (Recall that knowing
{\cal V}
at linear order suffices to compute observables to second order because expressions like (
4.8
) depend quadratically on
{\cal V}
.) It is only the total evolution (with mean-field and diffuse contributions combined) that must be unitary, and this can be expressed as a generalization of the optical theorem, relating the imaginary part of
\overline{H}_{\rm int}
to the rate of diffuse scattering
EFTBook
. This makes the relative size of the imaginary and real parts of
\overline{H}_{\rm int}
a proxy for the relative importance of diffuse and mean-field evolution.

4.1.3
Domain of validity

Broadly speaking, mean-field descriptions arise as good approximations for real systems in two common ways, depending on whether or not
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}
is zero. The simplest case is when
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}\neq 0
, because then perturbation theory alone can justify the mean-field approximation. This can be seen because the leading (linear) contribution in powers of
H_{\rm int}
is necessarily a mean-field result (because
{\cal V}
first enters expressions like (
4.8
) at second order). Neutrino interactions with matter inside the Sun or Earth provide practical examples of this type, for which the mean-field description follows as a consequence of the extreme feebleness of the weak interactions
Burgess:1996mz
;
Bamert:1997jj
.

Things are more subtle when
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=0
, however, because then the leading contribution of
\overline{V}(t)
to eq. (
4.8
) arises at the same order in
\delta H_{\rm int}
as does the diffuse contribution
{\cal V}(t)
. This is typically what happens for the interactions of photons with transparent dielectric materials, for example, and in this case perturbation theory in
\delta H_{\rm int}
itself is insufficient for mean-field methods to dominate.
7
7

7

For photons it is instead a large-
N
argument based on coherence that justifies mean-field methods (see
e.g.

EFTBook
).
This is also the regime appropriate to the hotspot, and in what follows we identify possible control parameters for mean-field methods by comparing for the hotspot the relative size of the real and imaginary parts of
\overline{H}_{\rm int}
.

4.2
Application to the hotspot

The above is made concrete by specializing to the specific hotspot interactions of the previous sections. Consider first the case of non-negligible hotspot size,
\xi

H_{\rm int}(t)=\int_{S_{\xi}}{\hbox{d}}^{2}x\;G_{a}\,\chi^{a}(t,{{\bf x}})\,\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\,,

4.2.1
Nonlocal mean-field Hamiltonian

Since
\langle\chi^{a}\rangle=0

{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,H_{\rm int}(t)\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=0

\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)\simeq-i\widetilde{G}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{S_{\xi}}{\hbox{d}}^{2}x\int_{S_{\xi}}{\hbox{d}}^{2}x^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};\tau,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,\phi(t,{{\bf x}})\,\phi(\tau,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,,

(4.15)

where (as before)
{{\langle\hskip-1.70717pt\langle}\,\chi^{a}(t,{{\bf x}})\,\chi^{b}(\tau,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\,{\rangle\hskip-1.70717pt\rangle}}=\delta^{ab}\,\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};\tau,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})
and
\widetilde{G}^{2}:=G_{a}G_{b}\,\delta^{ab}=NG^{2}
is the coupling after summing over environmental fields. Evaluating the correlator using (
2.20

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}};\tau,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi\beta|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|}\left\{\coth\left[\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\left(t-\tau+|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|-i\delta\right)\right]\right.

(4.16)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\coth\left[\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\left(t-\tau-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|-i\delta\right)\right]\right\}\,,

Expression (
4.15

\langle\chi^{a}(t,{{\bf x}})\,\chi^{b}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\rangle

{{\bf x}}
and
{{\bf x}}^{\prime}
on the localized interaction region
{\cal S}_{\xi}

\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)

{\cal S}_{\xi}
as a consequence of the absence of
\chi^{a}
fields anywhere in
{\cal R}_{+}
away from the interaction surface. This does not preclude the field
\phi

{\cal S}_{\xi}
in response to these interactions, however, such as those seen in (

|t-\tau|\geq|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\gg 2\xi

\overline{H}_{\rm int}(t)\simeq\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\beta^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\hat{\phi}_{\ell=0}(t,\xi)\,\hat{\phi}_{\ell=0}(\tau,\xi)\;{\rm csch}^{2}\left[\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\left(t-\tau-i\delta\right)\right]\,,

which uses the relation (

\hat{\phi}_{\ell=0}(t,\xi):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{4\pi}{\hbox{d}}^{2}\Omega\;\phi(t,r=\xi,\theta,\varphi)\,,

The second source of nonlocality is in time, although this dies off exponentially quickly once

Both of these sources of nonlocality have their roots in the fluctutation of

The leading contribution to the diffuse evolution describing deviations from the mean-field limit is given by (

{\cal V}(t)\simeq-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;H_{\rm int}(\tau)=-i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\int_{S_{\xi}}{\hbox{d}}^{2}x\;G_{a}\,\chi^{a}(\tau,{{\bf x}})\,\phi(\tau,{{\bf x}})\,.

As mentioned above, second-order contributions from this generically compete with first-order contributions from (

4.2.2
The local limit

The above mean-field interaction simplifies and becomes approximately local in the special case that
\xi
and
\beta
are both microscopic scales, showing how locality ultimately re-emerges in the long-wavelength limit. To examine this limit explicitly we use small
\xi
to integrate out the hotspot’s spatial size, leading to the effective point-like coupling given in (
2.8
). Using this to compute the mean-field Hamiltonian then gives

{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\,{\phi}^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})-i\tilde{g}^{2}{\phi}(t,\mathbf{0})\int_{0}^{t}\;{\hbox{d}}s\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s)\,{\phi}(t-s,\mathbf{0})\,,

(4.20)

where (as before)
\langle\chi^{a}(t,\mathbf{0})\,\chi^{b}(t-s,\mathbf{0})\rangle=\delta^{ab}\,\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s)
, with

\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s):=-\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\;{\rm csch}^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(s-i\delta)\right]\,.

(4.21)

Two simplifications follow from the observation that
\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s)
is peaked exponentially sharply around
s=0
, with width of order
\beta/\pi
. First, the upper integration limit can be taken to infinity at the expense of errors that are
\sim e^{-2\pi t/\beta}
and so are exponentially small in the regime
t\gg\beta/\pi
. Second, for fields varying on scales long compared with
\beta
we can expand
\phi(t-s,\mathbf{0})\simeq\phi(t,\mathbf{0})-s\,\partial_{t}\phi(t,\mathbf{0})+\cdots
inside the integrand to get

{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\,{\phi}^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})+{\cal A}\,{\phi}^{2}(t,\mathbf{0})+{\cal B}\,{\phi}\,\partial_{t}{\phi}(t,\mathbf{0})+\ldots\,,

(4.22)

where

{\cal A}=-i\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\;{\hbox{d}}s\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s)\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal B}=i\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\;{\hbox{d}}s\;s\,\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(s)\,,

(4.23)

and so on.

These reveal the coefficient
{\cal A}
to be a renormalization of the effective coupling
\lambda
, while the coefficient
{\cal B}
multiplies a new effective interaction proportional to
\phi\,\partial_{t}\phi
. This last interaction plays no role in the physics to follow because (depending on the operator ordering) it either involves the commutator of
\phi
with its canonical momentum (and so is a divergent contribution to an irrelevant field-independent piece in
\overline{H}_{\rm int}
) or it involves a total time derivative,
\partial_{t}\phi^{2}
(and so can be eliminated using an appropriate canonical transformation).

The integrals giving the coefficients
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
can be evaluated explicitly to give

{\cal A}=-\tilde{g}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\delta}+\frac{i}{4\pi\beta}\right]+{\cal O}(\delta)\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal B}=i\tilde{g}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2\pi\delta}{\beta}\right)-\frac{i}{8\pi}\right]+{\cal O}(\delta)\,,

(4.24)

where the infinitesimal
\delta
is meant to be taken to zero. The divergences arise because
\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\sim s^{-2}
as
s\to 0
, and so can be regarded as being ultraviolet in origin. They arise here as divergences when
\delta\to 0
, which just means that this infinitesimal – which was introduced for other reasons – is playing double duty; providing here also a near-hotpsot regularization for the singular integration (which could indeed have been regulated in other ways). To the extent that they contribute to non-redundant interactions these divergences can be renormalized into effective couplings, such as
\lambda
, thereby underlining that such self-couplings are generically always present in the effective theory.

What is
not
simply a small change to the effective description, even in the local limit, is the generation of an imaginary part of
{\cal A}
seen in (
4.24
). This cannot be absorbed into
\lambda
without changing the reality properties of
\lambda
, and its appearance is a manifestation of the general probability loss away from the mean-field sector into its ‘diffuse’ complement. Since the relative size of
\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta

4.3.2
Aside:

We pause the main line of development here to settle a side issue that might bother the reader at this point. The issue is this: equation (

4.31

Which is right? This is partially a matter of definition, since it hinges on how the full result gets spit into mean-field and diffuse parts. The guiding principle in §
4.1
is to make this split so that observables like (

divides linearly while

(4.55)

while the remainder becomes

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\bigg(\Bigl[\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0}),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

(4.56)

\displaystyle\qquad-\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0})\Bigl[\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0}),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})+{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigl[\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0}),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0})\Bigl[\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0}),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]\bigg)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\ .

These integrals evaluate (see Appendix
B.3
) in the regime
t-|{{\bf x}}|>0
and
t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|>0
to

{\cal P}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)\right]}\ ,

(4.57)

and

{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}\bigg]\ .

(4.58)

When we use these in (
4.52
) the overall correlation function is therefore

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\Bigl[{\phi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle H}}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}_{{\scriptscriptstyle H}}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle H}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}

(4.59)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)\right]}+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}}\;\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2})^{2}}\ .

This last expressions agrees perfectly with the perturbative Heisenberg-picture result computed in
Hotspot
, once this is evaluated in the equal-time limit – see eq. (
2.19
) – inside the future light-cone of the event at
t=|{{\bf x}}|=0
where the coupling switches on.

4.3.6
Domain of validity of mean-field methods

We see from these calculations that the mean-field correlator does
not
in general agree with the Heisenberg-picture result, even if this comparison is only made at leading order in
\lambda
and
\tilde{g}^{2}
. The comparison of the previous section shows that the difference between the mean-field and Heisenberg-picture answers is precisely given by the diffuse contribution that must be small for mean-field methods to apply.

The difference between mean-field result (
4.3.4
) and the corresponding Heisenberg-picture answer (
4.59
) lies completely in their
\tilde{g}^{2}
dependence; the term involving the self-coupling
\lambda
is identical in both cases. Since
\tilde{g}
and
\lambda
both have dimensions of length, another scale must appearin the comparison of
\lambda
and
\tilde{g}^{2}
, and the explicit evaluation –
e.g.
(
4.59
) – shows this scale to be either
\beta
or a combination of
|{{\bf x}}|
and
|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
.

Because our interest is typically where
|{{\bf x}}|
and
|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
are much larger than
\beta
the relative size of the
\lambda
-dependent term and the largest of
\tilde{g}^{2}
corrections is set by the relative size of
\lambda
and
\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta
, suggesting that mean-field methods provide a reliable approximation in the regime
\lambda\gg\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta
.

In summary, we see that mean-field methods can apply to the hotspot problem, but only in some parts of parameters space such as when
\lambda\gg\tilde{g}^{2}/\beta
. Where it does apply the resulting effective Hamiltonian can be nonlocal, both in the angular directions of
{\cal S}_{\xi}
and in time, due to the nonlocality of the
\chi
correlations with which the external
\phi
field interacts.

I_{1}
we use the Schwinger parametrization trick, which uses the identity

\frac{1}{p}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\zeta\;e^{-p\zeta}\,,

(A.25)

for any parameter
p>0
to rewrite the factor of
(k+q)^{-1}
in the integrand. This gives

\displaystyle I_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\zeta\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;(1-e^{-i(k+q)t})\sin(k|{{\bf x}}|)\sin(q|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)e^{-(k+q)\zeta}

(A.26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\zeta\;\bigg\{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;e^{-k\zeta}\sin(k|{{\bf x}}|)\bigg\}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-q\zeta}\sin(q|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\zeta\;\bigg\{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;e^{-k(\zeta+it)}\sin(|{{\bf x}}|k)\bigg\}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;e^{-q(\zeta+it)}\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|q)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\zeta\;\bigg[\bigg\{\frac{|{{\bf x}}|}{\zeta^{2}+|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg\{\frac{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{\zeta^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\bigg\}-\bigg\{\frac{|{{\bf x}}|}{(\zeta+it)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg\{\frac{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{(\zeta+it)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg]

leaving an elementary integral over
\zeta
. Performing this integral we find that
I_{1}
evaluates to

I_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)=\frac{|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\bigg(\frac{i}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\log\left|\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1+t/|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{1-t/|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}$}}\right|+\frac{\pi\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{2|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}-\frac{i}{|{{\bf x}}|}\log\left|\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1+t/|{{\bf x}}|}{1-t/|{{\bf x}}|}$}}\right|-\frac{\pi\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{2|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg)\ .

(A.27)

Only the real part of this expression

\hbox{Re}\big[I_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)\big]=\frac{|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\bigg[\frac{\pi}{2|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)-\frac{\pi}{2|{{\bf x}}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\bigg]

(A.28)

appears in (
A.2
).

To compute
I_{2}
it proves easier to first differentiate with respect to
t
, leading to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;\sin(tk)\sin(tq)\sin(|{{\bf x}}|k)\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|q)

(A.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\bigg(\cos\big[(t-|{{\bf x}}|)k\big]-\cos\big[(t+|{{\bf x}}|)k\big]\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}q\;\bigg(\cos\big[(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)q\big]-\cos\big[(t+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)q\big]\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\bigg(\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)-\delta(t+|{{\bf x}}|)\bigg)\bigg(\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)-\delta(t+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\bigg)

where the last line uses the real part of the Fourier transform of a Heaviside step function. Since
\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)=\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)=0
for
t>0
,
|{{\bf x}}|>0
and
|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|>0
this simplifies to

\frac{\partial I_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\ =\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\,.

(A.30)

Integrating with respect to
t
with the initial condition
I_{2}(0,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=0
(from the definition (
A.24
)) then gives

I_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\ .

(A.31)

Putting everything together gives

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{-1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{8\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\mathrm{Re}\big[I_{1}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)\big]+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{5}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;I_{2}({{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime},t)

(A.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{3}(|{{\bf x}}|^{2}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2})}\bigg[\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)-\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}|}\,\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{32\pi^{3}\beta|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\delta(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\,.

This is the expression quoted in (
3.73
) of the main text.

A.3
Schrödinger-picture equal-time free-field correlator

In this Appendix we compute the
\langle\phi\,\phi\rangle
correlator for free fields at equal times
t=t^{\prime}
, as a check on Schrödinger picture methods. Using the field basis and the vacuum wave-functional the equal-time Wightman function is given by the functional integral

\displaystyle\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}[\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}]\;\bra{\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\langle\mathrm{vac}|\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}\rangle

(A.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}[\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}]\;\varphi(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\langle}\varphi{\scriptstyle(\cdot)}|\mathrm{vac}{\rangle}\langle\mathrm{vac}|\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{0}\int{\cal D}\varphi\;\varphi(\mathbf{x})\varphi({{\bf x}}^{\prime})\;e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf x}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf y}}\;2{\cal E}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf y}})\varphi({{\bf x}})\varphi({{\bf y}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[2{\cal E}]^{-1}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

where we use the free Gaussian solution in the second-last line, where
{\cal E}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}|{{\bf k}}|e^{i({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\cdot{{\bf k}}}
given in (
3.33
). In order to perform the Gaussian integral we use the standard gaussian results

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod{\hbox{d}}\xi_{r}\;\xi_{r_{1}}\xi_{r_{2}}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r,s}K_{rs}\xi_{r}\xi_{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{K}{2\pi}$}}\right)^{-1/2}(K^{-1})_{r_{1}r_{2}}

(A.34)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod{\hbox{d}}\xi_{r}\;e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{r,s}K_{rs}\xi_{r}\xi_{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{K}{2\pi}$}}\right)^{-1/2}

(A.35)

The latter formula determines
{\cal N}_{0}=\sqrt{\det(\frac{2\mathcal{E}}{2\pi})}
. Now we need to invert the “matrix”
2{\cal E}
here, where the matrix
[2{\cal E}]^{-1}
is defined by

\displaystyle\int{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf z}}\;[2{\cal E}]^{-1}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf z}})2{\cal E}({{\bf z}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ =\ \delta^{3}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ ,

(A.36)

We solve the above in Fourier space, by writing
[2{\cal E}]^{-1}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathfrak{I}_{{{\bf k}}}e^{i({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\cdot{{\bf k}}}
for some function
\mathfrak{I}_{{{\bf k}}}
in momentum space which we solve for here. The above equation then implies

\displaystyle 2|{{\bf k}}|\mathfrak{I}_{{{\bf k}}}\ =\ 1\ ,

(A.37)

which means that
\mathfrak{I}_{{{\bf k}}}=(2|{{\bf k}}|)^{-1}
. We Fourier transform this to position space to find that

\displaystyle[2{\cal E}]^{-1}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;\bigg[\frac{1}{2|{{\bf k}}|}\bigg]e^{i({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\cdot{{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\sin(k|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)

(A.38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\Theta(k)e^{-i|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k}\right]

From here we use Weinberg’s formula (6.2.15) in
Weinberg:1995mt
for the Fourier transform of a Heaviside step function, where (in the limit
\delta\to 0^{+}
)

\displaystyle\Theta(x)=-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\hbox{d}}y}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{e^{+iyx}}{y-i\delta}\qquad\iff\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}x\;\Theta(x)e^{-iyx}=\frac{-i}{y-i\delta}

(A.39)

which gives us

[2{\cal E}]^{-1}({{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\left(-\frac{1}{|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\right)\ ,

(A.40)

which tells us that the free correlation function is

\displaystyle\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\ ,

(A.41)

which is the correct answer for the Wightman function (for equal times
t=t^{\prime}
).

B.1
Correlators using
\overline{V}^{-1}

We first compute the mean-field correlator of eq. (
4.36

V^{-1}

V^{\star}

4.31

Starting with (
4.36

\overline{H}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle{\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}+i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\Bigl[{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+i\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigl[{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau),{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}\,,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\frac{i\lambda}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg[\delta\big(\tau-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\big)-\delta\big(\tau-(t+|{{\bf x}}|)\big)\bigg]{\phi}(\tau,\mathbf{0})

(B.3)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg[\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\Theta\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\Theta\big(\tau-[t+{{\bf x}}]\big)\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\big(\tau-[t+{{\bf x}}]\big)\bigg]{\phi}(\tau,\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg[\delta\big(\tau-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\big)-\delta\big(\tau-(t+|{{\bf x}}|)\big)\bigg]\int_{0}^{\tau}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau^{\prime}){\phi}(\tau-\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0})\,.

Using this in (
B.1
) we get, after some manipulations,

\displaystyle i\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\Bigl[{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\Bigr]{\phi}(s,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=-\frac{\lambda\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(s,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{|{{\bf x}}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\big(\tau^{\prime}\big)\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(\tau^{\prime}+t-|{{\bf x}}|,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(s,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.4)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{t-|{{\bf x}}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau^{\prime})\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|-\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0}){\phi}(s,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

and so the mean-field correlation function becomes

\displaystyle{\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\lambda\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}-\frac{\lambda\Theta(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{|{{\bf x}}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|+\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{t-|{{\bf x}}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|-\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\ .

This expression simplifies further in the regime where all of
t-|{{\bf x}}|
,
|{{\bf x}}|
,
t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
and
|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|
are much greater than
\beta
, because in this case the narrowness of the Wightman function —
\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\propto e^{-2\pi\tau/\beta}
for
\tau\gg\beta
— makes it a good approximation to approximate the upper integration limits by
\infty
(with only exponentially small error). Under these assumptions we also know
\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)=\Theta(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)=1
and so get

\displaystyle{\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\;\simeq\;\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\bigg[\frac{\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}}{|{{\bf x}}|}+\frac{\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg]

(B.6)

\displaystyle\quad\;+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bigg[\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|+\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}+\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t-|{{\bf x}}|-\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\;\;+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)\bigg[\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}+\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\bigg]\ .

The integrals involving
\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau)
above are computed in Appendix
B.1.3
. These contain a divergence from the
\tau\to 0
limit, but this has the same structure as does the second line of (
B.1
) so the divergence can be absorbed into
\lambda
. Once this is done, and using the explicit form (
B.2
) for the free Wightman function, we find

\displaystyle{\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}};t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\;\simeq\;\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\big[-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\lambda}{32\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[-\frac{1}{t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}+\frac{1}{t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}\bigg]

(B.7)

\displaystyle\quad\;-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{128\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)\right]}-\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\mathrm{Im}\;\psi^{(1)}\left[1+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{i(t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{\beta}$}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\;\;+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{128\pi^{2}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)\right]}+\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}\beta^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\;\mathrm{Im}\;\psi^{(1)}\left[1+\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{i(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{\beta}$}}\right]\ ,

where
\psi^{(1)}(z):=({\hbox{d}}/{\hbox{d}}z)^{2}\ln\Gamma(z)
is the polygamma function of order 1. Recall that the derivation of (
B.1
) assumes
|{{\bf x}}|\ ,t-|{{\bf x}}|\ ,|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\ ,t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\gg\beta
.

The equal-time limit of eq. (
B.1
) is given by

{\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}};t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\left(\lambda-\frac{i\tilde{g}^{2}}{2\pi\beta}\right)\frac{1}{16\pi^{3}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)}

(B.8)

where the assumption
|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\gg\beta
used in the above derivation allows use of the large-
z
approximation
\mathrm{Im}[\psi^{(1)}(1+iz)]\simeq-1/z
. Notice that the
\tilde{g}^{2}
term in this expression does not satisfy the hermiticity condition
{\cal U}^{\ast}(t,{{\bf x}};t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})={\cal U}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime};t,{{\bf x}})
because the density matrix is evaluated at
t=0
and the
\tilde{g}^{2}
contributions ensure the effective mean-field hamiltonian that evolves to general
t
is also not hermitian.

We next evaluate the commutators required in the above, starting with the unequal-time commutator of the field itself.

B.1.1
Field commutators at unequal times

For later use in Appendix
B.1.2
, we here compute the commutator
{[}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf y}})]
of interaction-picture fields at unequal times. This can be done using the standard field expansion in terms of creation and annihilation operators, but it is simpler to obtain it directly from the Wightman function given in (
B.2
). This can be done because the commutator of two free fields is a
c
-number, and so is equal to its expectation value in the vacuum, giving

\displaystyle{\Bigl[}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl(\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}){\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}-\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\Bigr)

(B.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{-(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{-(t-t^{\prime}+i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime})+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}-\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime})-|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime})+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta}+\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime})-|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta}\bigg]

where a factor of the unit operator,
{\cal I}_{+}
, is implicit everywhere on the right-hand side.

Using the Sochocki-Plemelj identity
({z-i0^{+}})^{-1}-({z+i0^{+}})^{-1}=2i\pi\delta(z)
for infinitesimal and positive
0^{+}
, the above becomes

{\Bigl[}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]=\frac{i}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\delta\big(t-t^{\prime}+|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)-\delta\big(t-t^{\prime}-|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big)\bigg]{\cal I}_{+}\,,

(B.10)

which reduces when
t=t^{\prime}
to the standard equal-time commutator when
{{\bf x}}\neq{{\bf x}}^{\prime}
:

{\Bigl[}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\Bigr]=0\ .

(B.11)

Specializing (
B.9
) to the commutator for two fields at different times but with
{{\bf x}}={{\bf x}}^{\prime}
, instead gives

{\Bigl[}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}),{\phi}(t^{\prime},{{\bf x}})\Bigr]=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left[-\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime}-i\delta)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(t-t^{\prime}+i\delta)^{2}}\right]{\cal I}_{+}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\;\delta^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})\;{\cal I}_{+}

(B.12)

where the limit
\delta\to 0
is taken in the last equality and
\delta^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of the Dirac delta function with respect to its argument.

B.1.2
The Commutator
\left[{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau),{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\right]

The next intermediate step required is the commutator with
\phi
of the local mean-field Hamiltonian
{\overline{H}}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)
defined in (

\displaystyle-2\tilde{g}^{2}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{p}}\int\frac{{\hbox{d}}^{3}{{\bf k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}2E_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]

(B.51)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathrm{Re}\left[e^{-iE_{p}t+i{{\bf p}}\cdot{{\bf x}}}e^{-iE_{k}t+i{{\bf k}}\cdot{{\bf x}}^{\prime}}e^{+i(E_{p}+E_{k})\tau}(e^{-iE_{p}\sigma}+e^{-iE_{k}\sigma})\right]\ .

First we focus on simplifying
{\cal M}^{(1)}
above. The
{{\bf k}}
-integration is easily done, and then integrating the
{{\bf p}}
-angles away yields

{\cal M}^{(1)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{8\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;\mathrm{Re}\bigg[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)e^{-ip\sigma}\bigg]\ .

(B.52)

By switching the order of integration in the
(\tau,\sigma)
-plane the above integral can be written as

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{(1)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{8\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\int_{\sigma}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathrm{Re}\bigg[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)e^{-ip\sigma}\bigg]

(B.53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{8\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;(t-\sigma)\mathrm{Re}\bigg[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)e^{-ip\sigma}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{8\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\;\bigg[t\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;\bigg(\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\cos(p\sigma)+\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\sin(p\sigma)\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\frac{{\hbox{d}}}{{\hbox{d}}p}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;\bigg(\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\sin(p\sigma)-\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\sin(p\sigma)\bigg)\bigg]\qquad

To simplify the integrals, we next assume that we probe times

t\gg\beta\ ,

(B.54)

so that the upper limit on the
\sigma
-integrals can be taken to be
\simeq\infty
(since
\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)\propto e^{-2\pi\sigma/\beta}
). Upon doing so the
\sigma
-integrals in the above may be expressed in terms of the functions given in (
B.42
), where

{\cal M}^{(1)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\;\bigg[t\big({\cal C}_{\beta}(p)+{\cal S}_{\beta}(p)\big)-\frac{{\hbox{d}}{\cal D}_{\beta}(p,\delta)}{{\hbox{d}}p}+\frac{{\hbox{d}}\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(p,\delta)}{{\hbox{d}}p}\bigg]\ .\qquad

(B.55)

Note that
\partial_{p}\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(p,\delta)=0
from the functional form (
B.28
). Furthermore, using the functional forms of
{\cal C}_{\beta}
and
{\cal S}_{\beta}
we note the value of the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\;\big({\cal C}_{\beta}(p)+{\cal S}_{\beta}(p)\big)\ =\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\hbox{d}}p\;p^{2}}{2\pi(e^{\beta p}-1)}\ =\ \frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi\beta^{3}}

(B.56)

where
\zeta(3)\simeq 1.202
(with
\zeta
the Riemann-Zeta function). With this we get

{\cal M}^{(1)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\simeq-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\left(\dfrac{\zeta(3)t}{\pi\beta^{3}}-{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}}{\hbox{d}}p\;p\;\dfrac{{\hbox{d}}{\cal D}_{\beta}(p,\delta)}{{\hbox{d}}p}\right)}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}-{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}

(B.57)

where we note that the remaining momentum-integral appears to be ultraviolet divergent in the momentum
p
.

Moving on to the second integral
{\cal M}^{(2)}
, we first integrate away the angles in the momentum integrals and simplify to get

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{(2)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}|p)\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k)

(B.58)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathrm{Re}\bigg[e^{-i(p+k)t}e^{+i(p+k)\tau}(e^{-ip\sigma}+e^{-ik\sigma})\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\bigg]\ .

We again can switch the order of integration in the
(\tau,\sigma)
-plane giving us

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{(2)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma\int_{\sigma}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}|p)\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k)

(B.59)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\mathrm{Re}\bigg[e^{-i(p+k)t}e^{+i(p+k)\tau}(e^{-ip\sigma}+e^{-ik\sigma})\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\bigg]\ .

The
\tau
-integration can now be easily performed such that

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{(2)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}|p)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k)\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\sigma

(B.60)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathrm{Re}\bigg[\frac{ie^{-i(p+k)t}}{k+p}(e^{+ip\sigma}+e^{+ik\sigma})\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]-\frac{i}{k+p}(e^{-ip\sigma}+e^{-ik\sigma})\mathrm{Re}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\sigma)]\bigg]\ .

As noted above, we assume
t\gg\beta
and so the
\sigma
-integrals can be expressed in terms of the functions
{\cal C}_{\beta}
and
{\cal D}_{\beta}
where

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{(2)}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}|p)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k)

(B.61)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathrm{Re}\bigg[\frac{ie^{-i(p+k)t}}{k+p}\big({\cal C}_{\beta}(p)+i{\cal D}_{\beta}(p,\delta)+{\cal C}_{\beta}(k)+i{\cal D}_{\beta}(k,\delta)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{i}{k+p}\big({\cal C}_{\beta}(p)-i{\cal D}_{\beta}(p,\delta)+{\cal C}_{\beta}(k)-i{\cal D}_{\beta}(k,\delta)\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}p\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}|p)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\hbox{d}}k\;\sin(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|k)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg(\frac{{\cal C}_{\beta}(p)+{\cal C}_{\beta}(k)}{p+k}\sin\big((p+k)t\big)-\frac{{\cal D}_{\beta}(p,\delta)+{\cal D}_{\beta}(k,\delta)}{p+k}\big[1+\cos\big((p+k)t\big)\big]\bigg)\ .

B.3
Quantities entering with the diffuse correlator

This Appendix computes quantities that arise in §
4.3.5
where the diffuse contributions to the Wightman function are computed.

B.3.1
The integral
{\cal P}_{\beta}

First we compute the integral
{\cal P}_{\beta}
defined in (
4.55
). Using the commutator (
4.41
) we easily find that (
4.55
) simplifies to

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{i\delta\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}$}}\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{i\delta\big(\tau^{\prime}-[t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|]\big)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}$}}\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\cal I}^{2}_{+}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{16\pi^{2}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{2}\beta^{2}}\right)\frac{\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\sinh^{2}\left[\frac{\pi}{\beta}(-|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta)\right]}

This is the result quoted as (
4.57
) in the main text, and it agrees precisely with the
{\cal O}(\tilde{g}^{2})
part of the correlator given in (
2.19
).

B.3.2
The integral
{\cal Q}_{\beta}

Next we compute the integral
{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf y}})
defined in (
4.56
). Using the commutator (
4.41
) allows this integral to be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\tfrac{i\delta[\tau-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)]\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})-i\delta[\tau^{\prime}-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)]\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\tfrac{i\delta[\tau-(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)]{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0})-i\delta[\tau^{\prime}-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)]{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0})}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\ .

(B.63)

Relabelling
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
in the first term of each of the lines above allows this to be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})]\delta\big[\tau^{\prime}-(t-|{{\bf x}}|)\big]\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.64)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau^{\prime}\;\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})]\delta\big[\tau^{\prime}-(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)\big]\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\ .

Performing the integrations over
\tau^{\prime}
now gives

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathrm{Im}\big[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)\big]\bra{\mathrm{vac}}\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0}){\phi}(t,{{\bf x}}^{\prime})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}

(B.65)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{4\pi|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|]\big)]\bra{\mathrm{vac}}{\phi}(t,{{\bf x}})\phi(\tau,\mathbf{0})\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\ ,

and using the explicit form for the free Wightman functions as well as
\mathrm{Im}[\mathscr{W}_{\beta}(t)]=\delta^{\prime}(t)/(4\pi)
makes the integrand explicit:

{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta^{\prime}\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)}{-(\tau-t-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}$}}+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta^{\prime}\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|]\big)}{-(t-\tau-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}$}}\ .

(B.66)

Integrating this by parts yields

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}|)}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg[\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta\big(|{{\bf x}}|\big)}{|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta\big(t-|{{\bf x}}|\big)}{-(|{{\bf x}}|+i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}$}}-\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\delta\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}|]\big)\frac{{\hbox{d}}}{{\hbox{d}}\tau}\bigg\{\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{-(\tau-t-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|^{2}}$}}\bigg\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\Theta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}$}}-\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{\delta(t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)}{-(|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}$}}-\int_{0}^{t}{\hbox{d}}\tau\;\delta\big(\tau-[t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|]\big)\frac{{\hbox{d}}}{{\hbox{d}}\tau}\bigg\{\text{\scalebox{.85}{$\frac{1}{-(t-\tau-i\delta)^{2}+|{{\bf x}}|^{2}}$}}\bigg\}\bigg]

(B.67)

where boundary terms with
\delta(|{{\bf x}}|)
factors never contribute (since
|{{\bf x}}|>0
). In the regime of interest,
t-|{{\bf x}}|>0
and
t-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|>0
, the other boundary terms also do not contribute (since the Heaviside functions all turn on).

All that is left is to perform the
\delta
-function integrations which gives the final result

\displaystyle{\cal Q}_{\beta}(t,{{\bf x}},{{\bf x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{2|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta\big)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta\big)^{2}}\bigg]

(B.68)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{2|{{\bf x}}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta\big)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2|{{\bf x}}|\big(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|-i\delta\big)^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{64\pi^{4}|{{\bf x}}||{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|}\bigg[\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|-|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|+i\delta)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(|{{\bf x}}|+|{{\bf x}}^{\prime}|)^{2}}\bigg]\ ,

where the last line safely takes
\delta\to 0
. This is the result quoted in (
4.58
).
