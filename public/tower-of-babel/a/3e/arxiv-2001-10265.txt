On the exponential Diophantine equation …
Mahadi Ddamulira and Florian Luca, Mahadi Ddamulira and Florian Luca

Abstract
. Let r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 be an integer and 𝐔 := ( U n ) n ≥ 0 {\bf U}:=(U_{n})_{n\geq 0} be the Lucas sequence given by U 0 = 0 U_{0}=0 , U 1 = 1 , U_{1}=1, and U n + 2 = r ​ U n + 1 + U n U_{n+2}=rU_{n+1}+U_{n} , for all n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 . In this paper, we show that there are no positive integers r ≥ 3 , x ≠ 2 , n ≥ 1 r\geq 3,~x\neq 2,~n\geq 1 such that U n x + U n + 1 x U_{n}^{x}+U_{n+1}^{x} is a member of 𝐔 {\bf U} .

1.
Introduction

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
{\bf U}:=(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the Lucas sequence given by
U_{0}=0,~U_{1}=1,
and

(1)

\displaystyle U_{n+2}=rU_{n+1}+U_{n}

for all
n\geq 0
. When
r=1

{\bf U}
coincides with the Fibonacci sequence while when
r=2

{\bf U}
coincides with the Pell sequence. It is well-known that

(2)

\displaystyle U_{n}^{2}+U_{n+1}^{2}=U_{2n+1}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 0.

In particular, the identity (
2
) tells us that the sum of the squares of two consecutive terms of
{\bf U}
is also a term of
{\bf U}
.
When
r=1
, we even have
U_{n}+U_{n+1}=U_{n+2}
for all
n\geq 0
since
{\bf U}
is the Fibonacci sequence.
We thus consider the Diophantine equation

(3)

\displaystyle U_{n}^{x}+U_{n+1}^{x}=U_{m}

in nonnegative integers
(n,m,x)
which by Eq. (
2
) has the parametric solution
m=2n+1
when
x=2
for any
r\geq 1
and even the parametric solution
m=n+2
when

x=1
if
r=1
. For
r=1
Luca and Oyono
[
8
]
proved that Eq. (
3
) has no positive integer solutions
(n,m,x)
with
n\geq 2
and
x\geq 3
. Rihane et al.
[
11
]
studied Eq. (
3
) when
r=2
and proved that there is no positive integer solution
(n,m,x)
of it with
x\neq 2
. In the same spirit, Gómez Ruiz and Luca
[
4
]
studied Eq. (
3
) with
{\bf U}=\{F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\geq-(k-2)}
, which is the
k-
generalized Fibonacci sequence of recurrence

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(k)}=F_{n-1}^{(k)}+F_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+F_{n-k}^{(k)}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 2

with the initial conditions
F_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=F_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=\cdots=F_{0}^{(k)}=0
and
F_{1}^{(k)}=1
. When
k=2
, this sequence coincides with the sequence of
Fibonacci numbers. They proved that Eq. (
3
) has no positive integer solution
(k,n,m,x)
with
k\geq 3
,
n\geq 2
, and
x\geq 1
. Another related result involving the balancing numbers was studied by Rihane et al. in
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we study Eq. (
3
) in nonnegative integers
(r,n,m,x)
treating
r
as an integer parameter. We may assume that
r\geq 3
since the cases
r\in\{1,2\}
have been treated already in
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
, respectively. The solution with
(n,m)=(0,1)
(for any
r
and
x
) is obvious so we omit it and suppose that
n
is positive. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

There is no positive integer solution
(r,n,m,x)
of Diophantine equation (
3
) with
r\geq 3
and
x\neq 2
.

2.
Preliminary Results

2.1.
The Lucas sequence

Let

(\alpha,\beta):=\left(\dfrac{r+\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}{2},\dfrac{r-\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}{2}\right),

be the roots of the characteristic equation
x^{2}-rx-1=0
of the Lucas sequence
{\bf U}=(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. We put
\Delta=r^{2}+4=(\alpha-\beta)^{2}

for the discriminant of the above quadratic equation. The Binet formula for the general term of
{\bf U}
is given by

(4)

\displaystyle U_{n}:=\dfrac{\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}}{\alpha-\beta}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 0.

One may prove by induction that the inequality

(5)

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-2}\leq U_{n}\leq\alpha^{n-1}

holds for all positive integers
n
. It is also easy to show that the inequality

(6)

\displaystyle\dfrac{U_{n}}{U_{n+1}}<\dfrac{1}{r}

holds for all
n\geq 2
. Indeed, it follows from
U_{n+1}=rU_{n}+U_{n-1}>rU_{n}
for
n\geq 2
. At one point of the argument we will need the companion Lucas sequence

{\bf V}:=\{V_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
given by
V_{0}=2,~V_{1}=r
, and
V_{n+2}=rV_{n+1}+V_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. Its Binet formula is

(7)

V_{n}=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n}\quad{\text{\rm for~all}}\quad n\geq 0.

There are many relations between members of
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
such as

(8)

\displaystyle U_{2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{n}V_{n};

(9)

\displaystyle U_{n+1}^{2}-U_{n}U_{n+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n};

(10)

\displaystyle V_{n}^{2}-\Delta U_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4(-1)^{n};

(11)

\displaystyle 2U_{m+n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{m}V_{n}+U_{n}V_{m};

(12)

\displaystyle\gcd(U_{n},U_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{\gcd(m,n)}.

We record another one. All such identities follow easily from the Binet formulas Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
7
) of
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
respectively.

Lemma 1
.

If
n
is odd then

(13)

U_{n}-1=\left\{\begin{matrix}U_{(n-1)/2}V_{(n+1)/2}&{\text{if}}&n\equiv 1\pmod{4};\\
U_{(n+1)/2}V_{(n-1)/2}&{\text{if}}&n\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{matrix}\right.

Both sequences
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
can be extended to negative indices either by allowing
n
to be negative in the Binet formulas Eq. (
4
) and (
7
)
or simply by using the recurrence relation to extend
{\bf U}
to negative indices via
U_{-n}=-rU_{-(n-1)}+U_{-(n-2)}
for all
n\geq 1
. The same applies to
{\bf V}
. All the above formulas
given in Eq. (
8
), (
9
), (
10
), (
11
), and (
13
) hold when the indices are arbitrary integers, not necessarily nonnegative.

The following lemma is useful. For further details we refer the reader to the book of Koshy
[
6
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\{U_{n}(r)\}_{n\geq 0}\subseteq{\bf Z}[r]
be the sequence of polynomials defined by
U_{0}(r)=0
,
U_{1}(r)=1
, and

\displaystyle U_{n+2}(r)=rU_{n+1}(r)+U_{n}(r),\quad\text{for all }\quad n\geq 0.

Then,

(14)

\displaystyle U_{n}(r)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n\\
k\not\equiv n\pmod{2}\end{subarray}}\binom{\frac{n+k-1}{2}}{k}r^{k}.

Note that the summation range above is only over these
k\in[0,n]
which have different parity than
n
. We record the following easy but useful consequence of the formula Eq. (
14
).

Lemma 3
.

We have
r\mid U_{n}
if
n
is even and
r\mid U_{n}-1
if
n
is odd.

2.2.
Logarithmic height

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\eta^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive and the
\eta^{(i)}
’s are the conjugates of
\eta
. The
logarithmic height
of
\eta
is given by

h(\eta):=\dfrac{1}{d}\left(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\max\{|\eta^{(i)}|,1\}\right)\right).

In particular, if
\eta=p/q
is a rational number with
\gcd(p,q)=1
and
q>0
, then
h(\eta)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
. The following are some of the properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
, which will be used in the next sections of this paper without reference:

(15)

\displaystyle h(\eta\pm\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta)+h(\gamma)+\log 2,

\displaystyle h(\eta\gamma^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta)+h(\gamma),

\displaystyle h(\eta^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\eta)\quad(s\in\mathbb{Z}).

2.3.
Linear forms in logarithms and continued fractions

The following result on linear forms in three logarithms is due to Mignotte
[
9
]
. The result is more general (i.e., the conditions on the parameters involved are somewhat more general), but we will only quote it in the form that we need.

Theorem 2
.

Consider three algebraic numbers
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, and
\gamma_{3}
, which are all real, greater than
1
and multiplicatively independent. Put

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=[\mathbb{Q}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}):\mathbb{Q}].

Let
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}
be coprime positive integers and consider

\displaystyle\Gamma:=b_{2}\log\gamma_{2}-b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}-b_{3}\log\gamma_{3}.

Put

\displaystyle d_{1}:=\gcd(b_{1},b_{2})=\frac{b_{1}}{b_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}}=\frac{b_{2}}{b_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}},\quad d_{3}:=\gcd(b_{3},b_{2})=\frac{b_{2}}{b_{2}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}=\frac{b_{3}}{b_{3}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
, and
A_{3}
be real numbers such that

\displaystyle A_{i}\geq\max\{4,~4.296\log\gamma_{i}+2\mathcal{D}h(\gamma_{i})\},\quad i=1,2,3,\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega:=A_{1}A_{2}A_{3}\geq 100.

Put

\displaystyle b^{{}^{\prime}}:=\left(\dfrac{b_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}}{A_{2}}+\dfrac{b_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}}{A_{1}}\right)\left(\dfrac{b_{3}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{A_{2}}+\dfrac{b_{2}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{A_{3}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\log\mathcal{B}:=\max\left\{0.882+\log b^{{}^{\prime}},\frac{10}{\mathcal{D}}\right\}.

Then, either

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma|>-790.95\Omega\mathcal{D}^{2}(\log\mathcal{B})^{2},

or one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

there exist two positive integers
r_{0}
and
s_{0}
such that

\displaystyle r_{0}b_{2}=s_{0}b_{1}

with

\displaystyle r_{0}\leq 5.61A_{2}(\mathcal{D}\log\mathcal{D})^{\frac{1}{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{0}\leq 5.61A_{1}(\mathcal{D}\log\mathcal{D})^{\frac{1}{3}};

(ii)

there exist integers
r_{1},s_{1},t_{1},
and
t_{2}
, with
r_{1}s_{1}\neq 0
, such that

\displaystyle(t_{1}b_{1}+r_{1}b_{3})s_{1}=r_{1}b_{2}t_{2},\quad\gcd(r_{1},t_{1})=\gcd(s_{1},t_{2})=1

which also satisfy

\displaystyle|r_{1}s_{1}|\leq 5.61\delta A_{3}(\mathcal{D}\log\mathcal{D})^{\frac{1}{3}},\quad|s_{1}t_{1}|\leq 5.61\delta A_{1}(\mathcal{D}\log\mathcal{D})^{\frac{1}{3}},\quad|r_{1}t_{2}|\leq 5.61\delta A_{2}(\mathcal{D}\log\mathcal{D})^{\frac{1}{3}},

where

\displaystyle\delta:=\gcd(r_{1},s_{1}).

Moreover, when
t_{1}=0
we can take
r_{1}=1
, and when
t_{2}=0
we can take
s_{1}=1
.

At some point we will need to treat linear forms in two logarithms of algebraic numbers. To set the stage, let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be real algebraic numbers which are positive and let
{\mathcal{D}}:=[{\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):{\mathbb{Q}}]
.
Let
b_{1}
,
b_{2}

B_{1}
and
B_{2}
be real numbers larger than
1
such that

\displaystyle\log B_{i}\geq\max\left\{h(\gamma_{i}),\dfrac{|\log\gamma_{i}|}{\mathcal{D}},\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{D}}\right\},\qquad{\text{\rm for}}\quad i=1,2,

and put

\displaystyle b^{\prime}=\dfrac{|b_{1}|}{{\mathcal{D}}\log B_{2}}+\dfrac{|b_{2}|}{{\mathcal{D}}\log B_{1}}.

Let

(16)

\Gamma:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+b_{2}\log\gamma_{2}.

The following result of Laurent et al. is Corollary 2 in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 3
.

With the above notations assuming furthermore that
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are multiplicatively independent we have

(17)

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma|>-24.34{\mathcal{D}}^{4}\left(\max\left\{\log b^{\prime}+0.14,\dfrac{21}{\mathcal{D}},\dfrac{1}{2}\right\}\right)^{2}\log B_{1}\log B_{2}.

Note that the fact that
\Gamma\neq 0
is already guaranteed by the condition that
\gamma_{1}
and

During the calculations we get upper bounds on our variables which are too large, thus we need to reduce them. To do so we use some results from the theory of continued fractions.

For the treatment of linear forms homogeneous in two integer variables we use a well-known classical result in the theory of Diophantine approximation due to Legendre.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\tau
be an irrational number,
{p_{0}}/{q_{0}},{p_{1}}/{q_{1}},{p_{2}}/{q_{2}},\ldots
be the sequence of convergents of the continued fraction expansion of
\tau
and
M
be a positive integer. Let
N
be a nonnegative integer such that
q_{N}>M
. Putting
a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
the inequality

\displaystyle\left|\tau-\dfrac{u}{v}\right|>\dfrac{1}{(a(M)+2)v^{2}},

holds for all pairs
(u,v)
of positive integers with
0<v<M
. Furthermore, if

\left|\tau-\frac{u}{v}\right|<\frac{1}{2v^{2}},

then
u/v=p_{k}/q_{k}
for some
k\geq 0
.

For a nonhomogeneous linear form in two integer variables we use a slight variation of a result due to Dujella and Pethő (see
[
5
]
, Lemma 5a). For a real number
X
, we write
\|X\|:=\min\{|X-n|:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for the distance from
X
to the nearest integer.

Lemma 5
.

Let
M
be a positive integer,
{p}/{q}
be a convergent of the continued fraction expansion of the irrational number
\tau
such that
q>6M
, and
A,B,\mu
be some real numbers with
A>0
and
B>1
. Furthermore, let
\varepsilon:=\|\mu q\|-M\|\tau q\|
. If
\varepsilon>0
, then there is no solution to the inequality

0<|u\tau-v+\mu|<AB^{-w}

in positive integers
u,v
, and
w
with

u\leq M\quad{\text{and}}\quad w\geq\dfrac{\log(Aq/\varepsilon)}{\log B}.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

3.1.
The cases
n=1
or
x=1

We assume that
n\geq 1
, as the solution with
n=0
is trivial. Since
U_{n+1}<U_{n+1}+U_{n}<U_{n+2}
, it follows that the Diophantine equation Eq. (
3
) has no solution with
x=1
. Let us assume that
n=1
. We then get that

(18)

\displaystyle U_{m}=1+r^{x}.

In particular,
U_{m}\equiv 1\pmod{r}
. Lemma
3
shows that
m\equiv 1\pmod{2}
and now Lemma
1
shows that

r^{x}=U_{m}-1=U_{(m-\delta)/2}V_{(m+\delta)/2}\quad\text{where}\quad\delta\in\{\pm 1\},\quad\delta\equiv m\pmod{4}.

We now recall the Primitive Divisor Theorem of Carmichael
[
3
]
(see
[
1
]
for the most general statement) for the sequence
{\bf U}
. It states that if

\ell>12
, there is a prime factor
p
of
U_{\ell}
which is primitive in the sense that
p\nmid U_{k}
for any positive
k<\ell
. So, assume
m+\delta\geq 14
. Since
m+\delta
is even we have
U_{m+\delta}=U_{(m+\delta)/2}V_{(m+\delta)/2}
by the formula Eq. (
8
). Since
U_{m+\delta}
has a primitive prime factor
p
, the primitive prime
p
must be a divisor of
V_{(m+\delta)/2}
, which in turn must divide
r=U_{2}
, a contradiction. Thus,
m+\delta\leq 12
, therefore
m\leq 13
. It thus follows that

r^{x}=U_{m}-1<\alpha^{m-1}\leq\alpha^{12}<(r^{2}+4)^{6},

so, if
x\geq 13
, then

3^{x-12}\leq r^{x-12}<(1+4/r^{2})^{6}\leq(1+4/3^{2})^{6}<9.1,

which gives
x\leq 14
. Thus,
m\leq 13,~x\leq 14
. For each choice of the pair
(m,x)
with the components in the above ranges, the equation
U_{m}(r)-1-r^{x}=0
is a polynomial equation in
r
.
After a simple computer search, we found no other solutions to equation Eq. (
18
) apart from the solution
(n,m,x)=(1,3,2)
which has
x=2
so it is part of the parametric family of solutions indicated at Eq. (
2
).

So, from now on we assume that
n\geq 2
and
x\geq 3
.

3.2.
Calculations when
n\in[2,100]
and
x\in[3,100]

Using the equation Eq. (
3
) and the inequality Eq. (
5
), we get

\displaystyle\alpha^{(n-1)x}<U_{n+1}^{x}<U_{n}^{x}+U_{n+1}^{x}=U_{m}\leq\alpha^{m-1},

and

\displaystyle\alpha^{m-2}<U_{m}=U_{n}^{x}+U_{n+1}^{x}<(U_{n}+U_{n+1})^{x}<U_{n+2}^{x}<\alpha^{(n+1)x}.

From the above inequalities, we get the following result which we record for future reference.

Lemma 6
.

The inequalities

(19)

\displaystyle(n-1)x+1<m<(n+1)x+2

hold for all
x\geq 3
and
n\geq 2
.

We next consider Eq. (
14
) given in Lemma
2
. We write Eq. (
3
) as

(20)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n\\
k\not\equiv n(\text{mod}~2)\end{subarray}}\binom{\frac{n+k-1}{2}}{k}r^{k}\right)^{x}+\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq n+1\\
k\not\equiv n+1(\text{mod}~2)\end{subarray}}\binom{\frac{n+k}{2}}{k}r^{k}\right)^{x}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq m\\
k\not\equiv m(\text{mod}~2)\end{subarray}}\binom{\frac{m+k-1}{2}}{k}r^{k}.

Assume first that
n
is even. Then Eq. (
20
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{2}r+\binom{\frac{n+2}{2}}{3}r^{3}+\cdots\right)^{x}+\left(1+\binom{\frac{n+2}{2}}{2}r^{2}+\binom{\frac{n+4}{2}}{4}r^{4}+\cdots\right)^{x}=1+\binom{\frac{m+1}{2}}{2}r^{2}+\binom{\frac{m+3}{2}}{3}r^{4}+\cdots,

which is equivalent to

(21)

\displaystyle\binom{\frac{n+2}{2}}{2}r^{2}x+\binom{\frac{n+4}{2}}{4}r^{4}x+\cdots\equiv\binom{\frac{m+1}{2}}{2}r^{2}+\binom{\frac{m+3}{2}}{3}r^{4}+\cdots(\text{mod}~r^{x}).

The above relation implies that

(22)

\displaystyle r^{\min\{x,4\}-2}\Big|\binom{\frac{m+1}{2}}{2}-x\binom{\frac{n+2}{2}}{2}.

Similarly, when
n
is odd, one is led to the analogous divisibility relation

(23)

\displaystyle r^{\min\{x,4\}-2}\Big|\binom{\frac{m+1}{2}}{2}-x\binom{\frac{n+1}{2}}{2}.

So, fixing
n\in[2,100]
and
x\in[3,100]
, inequalities Eq. (
19
) give some range for
m
. For each
(n,x,m)
, divisibility relations
Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
23
) (according to whether
n
is even or odd) give us some possibilities for

and now one checks whether relation Eq. (
3
)
holds for this candidate
(n,x,m,r)
. A computer search with Mathematica in this range for

and

which ran for a few hours found no solutions. For the search we didn’t actually checked
that formula Eq. (

20
) does not hold modulo

, where

is the product of the first

primes. The Mathematica function

From now on, we assume that

and
\max\{n,x\}>100

3.3.
A small linear form in three logs

We rewrite equation Eq. (

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha^{m}}{\alpha-\beta}-U_{n+1}^{x}=U_{n}^{x}+\dfrac{\beta^{m}}{\alpha-\beta}.

We record the above inequality for future reference.

Lemma 7
.

With
\Gamma
given by formula Eq. (
26
), inequality Eq. (
27
) holds.

We want to apply Theorem
2
with the following data:

\displaystyle\quad\gamma_{1}:=\alpha-\beta=\sqrt{r^{2}+4},\quad\gamma_{2}:=\alpha,\quad\gamma_{3}:=U_{n+1},\quad b_{1}:=1,\quad b_{2}:=m,\quad b_{3}:=x.

We need to check that
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
, and
\gamma_{3}
are multiplicatively independent. This we do in the next subsection.

3.4.
Checking that
\gamma_{1},~\gamma_{2},~\gamma_{3}
are multiplicatively independent

Well, assume they are not and let
i,j,k
be integers not all zero such that

\gamma_{1}^{i}\gamma_{2}^{j}\gamma_{3}^{k}=1.

Squaring and rearranging the above relation we get
\gamma_{2}^{2j}=(\gamma_{1}^{2})^{-i}\gamma_{3}^{-2k}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. However,
\gamma_{2}^{2j}
is also a unit, so an algebraic integer whose reciprocal is also an algebraic integer, and it is also positive, so it must be
1
. Thus,
j=0
. It now follows that

i
and
k
are both nonzero (since if one of them is, so is the other one) and further
\gamma_{3}=\gamma_{1}^{-i/k}
. In particular, all prime factors of
U_{n+1}
are prime factors of
\Delta:=r^{2}+4
. But this is also contemplated by the Primitive Divisor Theorem of Carmichael since primes dividing
\Delta
are not considered
primitive. In particular,
U_{n+1}
does not have primitive prime factors so
n+1\leq 12
. In fact, Theorem C in
[
1

In this section, we return to inequality Eq. (

Lemma 10

For

(46)

\displaystyle\log{\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log r+\frac{2}{r^{2}}+\zeta,\quad|\zeta|<\frac{5.81}{r^{4}};

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log r+\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\zeta^{\prime},\quad|\zeta^{\prime}|<\frac{3.64}{r^{4}}.

Proof.

We have

\log{\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}=\frac{1}{2}\log(r^{2}+4)=\log r+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{4}{r^{2}}\right).

With
z:=4/r^{2}

|z|\leq 4/9
and

\log(1+z)=z+\zeta_{1},\quad|\zeta_{1}|\leq\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{z^{k}}{k}=\frac{z^{2}}{2}\left(1+(2/3)z+(2/4)z^{2}+\cdots\right).

The expression in parenthesis above is smaller than

c_{1}:=1+(2/3)(4/9)+(2/4)(4/9)^{2}+\cdots=(-4/9-\log(1-4/9))\times 2\times(9/4)^{2}.

Hence,

\log{\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}=\log r+\frac{2}{r^{2}}+\frac{\zeta_{1}}{2}:=\log r+\frac{2}{r^{2}}+\zeta,\quad|\zeta|=\frac{|\zeta_{1}|}{2}<\left(\frac{c_{1}}{4}\right)z^{2}=\frac{4c_{1}}{r^{4}}<\frac{5.81}{r^{4}}.

For
\alpha

\log\alpha=\log r+\log\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}}}\right)=\log r+\log(1+z_{1}),\quad z_{1}:={\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{z}{4}}}-\frac{1}{2}.

Note that
|z_{1}|\leq 1/r^{2}
. Thus,

\log(1+z_{1})=z_{1}+\zeta_{2},\quad|\zeta_{2}|\leq\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}}{2}(1+(2/3)|z_{1}|+\cdots)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{2r^{4}},

where by the previous arguments,

c_{2}=(-\lambda_{0}-\log(1-\lambda_{0}))\times 2\times\lambda_{0}^{-2},\quad{\text{\rm with}}\quad\lambda_{0}:={\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{9}}}-\frac{1}{2}.

It remains to expand

z_{1}=\frac{1}{2}({\sqrt{1+z}}-1)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z}{2}+\zeta_{3}\right),\quad|\zeta_{3}|\leq\sum_{k\geq 2}\left|\binom{k}{1/2}\right|z^{k}.

Since
|\binom{k}{1/2}|\leq 1/4
for all
k\geq 1

|\zeta_{3}|\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k\geq 2}z^{k}=\frac{z^{2}}{4(1-z)}\leq\frac{36}{5r^{4}}.

Hence,

\log\alpha=\log r+\frac{z}{4}+\left(\zeta_{2}+\frac{\zeta_{3}}{2}\right)=:\log r+\frac{1}{r^{2}}+\zeta^{\prime},\quad|\zeta^{\prime}|<\left(\frac{c_{2}}{2}+\frac{36}{10}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}<\frac{3.64}{r^{4}}.

We consider the element

\displaystyle y:=\dfrac{x}{\alpha^{2n}}.

\displaystyle\left|U_{n+1}^{x}-\dfrac{\alpha^{(n+1)x}}{(r^{2}+4)^{x/2}}\right|<\dfrac{2y\alpha^{(n+1)x}}{(r^{2}+4)^{x/2}}.
