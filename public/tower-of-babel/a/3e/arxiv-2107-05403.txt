Randomized Benchmarking for Non-Markovian Noise
Pedro Figueroa–Romero, Kavan Modi, Robert J. Harris, Thomas M. Stace, Min-Hsiu Hsieh

Abstract
Estimating the features of noise is the first step in a chain of protocols that will someday lead to fault-tolerant quantum computers. The randomized benchmarking ( RB ) protocol is designed with this exact mindset, estimating the average strength of noise in a quantum processor with relative ease in practice. However, RB , along with most other benchmarking and characterization methods, is limited in scope because it assumes that the noise is temporally uncorrelated (Markovian), which is increasingly evident not to be the case. Here, we combine the RB protocol with a recent framework describing non-Markovian quantum phenomena to derive a general analytical expression of the average sequence fidelity ( ASF ) for non-Markovian RB with the Clifford group. We show that one can identify non-Markovian features of the noise directly from the ASF through its deviations from the Markovian case, proposing a set of methods to collectively estimate these deviations, non-Markovian memory time scales, and diagnose (in)coherence of non-Markovian noise in an RB experiment. Finally, we demonstrate the efficacy of our proposal by means of several proof-of-principle examples. Our methods are directly implementable and pave the way for a better understanding of correlated noise in quantum processors.



I
Introduction

The biggest challenge faced in any quantum computation can almost unequivocally be said to be the presence of errors. Among these, noise arising from interactions with the surroundings of a system represent an important class that is still far from being well understood. Given the current widespread interest in designing complex fault-tolerant quantum systems, together with the fundamental restriction that no system can ever be fully isolated from its surroundings, the need to advance our understanding of this type of noise cannot be understated.

Over the last decade, the approach known as randomized benchmarking (
RB
)
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Lévi et al. 2007
;
Knill et al. 2008
;
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Magesan et al. 2012a
has become the gold standard to certify the performance of gate sets and characterize the noise in computations involving these sets.
RB
generally refers to an experimental protocol allowing estimation of error rates of a gate set by quantifying their control fidelity as a function of the number of gates
Helsen et al. 2020
. Moreover, it does so in an efficient way that is robust to state preparation and measurement (
SPAM
) errors, as opposed to approaches such as quantum process tomography (
QPT
)
Chuang and Nielsen 1997
. It is important to point out, however, that the two approaches are rather complementary
Roth et al. 2018
, as
RB
extracts less information about the noise, namely average error rates of average gates, but requires little resources for high confidence
Wallman and Flammia 2014
, while
QPT
allows to fully reconstruct noise but with a higher resource cost
Mohseni et al. 2008
. Aside from
QPT
and
RB
, there is a plethora of other methods lying in between, such as gate set tomography
Nielsen et al. 2020
, compressed sensing
Gross et al. 2010
;
Flammia et al. 2012
or direct fidelity estimation
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
;
Moussa et al. 2012
, to name a few, to characterize quantum devices. The main reason why
RB
has become an essential tool for quantum technologies is thus its practicality and applicability to realistic experimental settings.

The most common versions of
RB
protocols are executed for sequences of Clifford gates
Knill et al. 2008
, and consider noise that is both time and gate independent, in particular Markovian and context independent. In this case, it is observed that the so-called average sequence fidelity (
ASF
), i.e. a figure of merit relating to the gate fidelity of the noise

Correlated noise has been thoroughly examined in particular scenarios, such as that of dephasing noise. For classical correlations, e.g. in Ref.

The study of temporal correlations in quantum systems necessarily require in its foundations a theory of quantum stochastic processes. The development of such a theory has much older origins than

;
Chiribella et al. 2009

Pollock et al. 2018a

In this manuscript, we derive an analytical expression for the
ASF
of an

ASF

We also discuss ways in which the relevant time scales, i.e. sequence lengths, for finite non-Markovian noise can be determined, and deviations from a Markovian decay can be quantified, both with or without an
a priori
model of the noise. The main limitation to these methods is precisely the

II
Randomized Benchmarking

While there are many variants of
RB
, and a general framework encompassing these can be established
Helsen et al. 2020
, for concreteness here we consider an
RB
protocol employing the Clifford group. This has been the most common approach in
RB
mainly because the elements on the Clifford group can be realized efficiently on a quantum processor
Gottesman 1997
;
Gottesman 1998
;
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
. The
RB
protocol is then as follows:

1.

Prepare an initial state
\rho
.

2.

Sample
m
distinct elements,
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{m}
, uniformly at random from the Clifford group. Let
\mathcal{G}_{m+1}:=\Mcirc_{i=m}^{1}\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\dagger}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{G}_{m}^{\dagger}
, where
\circ
denotes composition of maps and
\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}(\cdot)=G^{\dagger}(\cdot)G
for any Kraus representation with unitaries
G
of the map
\mathcal{G}
. We refer to
\mathcal{G}_{m+1}
as an undo gate.

3.

Apply the composition
\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\mathcal{G}_{i}
on
\rho
. In practice, this amounts to applying a noisy sequence
\mathcal{S}_{m}:=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
of length
m
on
\rho
, where
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
are the physical noisy gates associated to
\mathcal{G}
.

4.

Estimate the probability
f_{m}=\tr\left[\mathcal{M}{\mathcal{S}}_{m}\left(\rho\right)\right]
via a positive operator-valued measure (
POVM
) element
\mathcal{M}
.

5.

Repeat
n
times the steps 1 to 4 for the same initial state
\rho
, same
POVM
element
\mathcal{M}
, and different sets of gates chosen uniformly at random
\{\mathcal{G}_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{\mathcal{G}_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{m},\ldots,\{\mathcal{G}_{i}^{(n)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
from the Clifford group to obtain the probabilities
f_{m}^{(1)},f_{m}^{(2)},\ldots{f}_{m}^{(n)}
. Compute the average
\mathcal{F}_{m}=1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{m}^{(i)}
. We refer to
\mathcal{F}_{m}
as an average sequence fidelity (
ASF
).

6.

Examine the behavior of the
ASF

\mathcal{F}_{m}
over different sequence lengths
m
.

The important insight in the
RB
protocol is that the
ASF
contains the average noise rate of the applied sequences, which can be extracted efficiently by analyzing it over varying sequence lengths. Specifically, when the noise is approximated as both independent of the gates applied and the time-step at which these are applied, the
ASF
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{m}=Ap^{m}+B,

(1)

where the error rate of the noise, or so-called noise strength, is given by
p\in[0,1]
and
A
,
B
are constants determined by state preparation and measurement (
SPAM
) errors
Magesan et al. 2012a
.
This implies that having performed an
RB
experiment, the data of the experimental
ASF
s can be fitted to an exponential, from which the noise-strength
p
and the
SPAM
factors can be extracted. The noise strength is directly related
Note1
to the gate fidelity of the noise with respect to the identity
Fogarty et al. 2015
, and hence the labeling of
\mathcal{F}_{m}
as a fidelity, but similarly other figures of merit can be used to learn average error rates through
RB

Magesan et al. 2012b
;
Wallman et al. 2015
.

It is important to mention that
SPAM
errors are implicit to steps 1 and 4, that is, in an execution of the protocol, neither the initial state preparation nor the measurement of the output state might be perfect. In the time and gate independent scenario for the Clifford group, however, as seen in Eq. (
1
),
SPAM
errors are constants both independent of the error rate and the sequence length.

The exponential decay in Eq. (
1
) can be obtained by modeling each noisy gate as
\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}:=\Lambda\circ\mathcal{G}_{i}
for some completely positive trace preserving (
CPTP
) map
\Lambda
; then the analytical average of the survival probabilities is given by the average over gates
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. For our purposes, we just care that the gates belong to a unitary 2-design, i.e., any distribution of gates replicating up to the second moment of the unitary group with the uniform Haar measure
Dankert et al. 2009
, such as the Clifford group. This implies that averaging over gates can be replaced with that over the Haar measure to obtain
\mathcal{F}_{m}
, and similarly the use of higher unitary designs could serve to characterize higher-order statistical properties of noise in
RB

Nakata et al. 2021
. Detail about how such averaging is carried out can be seen in Appendix
B
.

Importantly, one sees deviations from an exponential decay for more complex noise profiles, including non-Markovian noise. Nevertheless, within a Markovian assumption,
RB
generally renders a linear combination of exponential decays for the
ASF

Helsen et al. 2020
, with the particular case of gate-dependence rendering a single perturbation term that decays exponentially as well with the sequence length
Proctor et al. 2017
;
Wallman 2018
. Here, together with the assumption that the gates
\mathcal{G}
belong either to the multi-qubit Clifford group or to a 2-design, we make the assumption that the noise modeled by the maps
\Lambda
is gate-independent. Other than these two assumptions, we are interested in temporal correlations in the noise described as being mediated by an external environment.

III
Quantum processes and non-Markovianity

The setting we consider is that of a bipartite quantum system, labeled
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
, composed of a
d_{\mathsf{S}}
-dimensional system
\mathsf{S}
and a
d_{\mathsf{E}}
-dimensional environment
\mathsf{E}
. An experimenter, in principle, would apply the sequence
\mathcal{S}_{m}=\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}\mathcal{G}_{i}
of Clifford gates
\mathcal{G}_{i}
solely on
\mathsf{S}
, and not have access to
\mathsf{E}
. We consider different scenarios for the initial state
\rho

\mathsf{S}
, but can nevertheless get correlated with
\mathsf{E}
afterwards, accounting for a new type of
SPAM

\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{i}=\Lambda_{i}\circ(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{i})

\Lambda_{i}
acts on the full
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}

is an identity map on
\mathsf{E}

to be at least completely positive (

i\neq{j}

The sequence

can be understood as a particular example of a quantum stochastic process where the underlying dynamics are given by the noise inherent to the computation on the whole

;
Pollock et al. 2018a

;

\Upsilon_{m}
. We may contract this tensor with the set of Clifford gates, which too can be incorporated in a tensor
\mathfrak{C}_{m}

The notion of Markovianity is formalized in the process tensor framework through a proper operational Markov condition

V
Quantifying non-Markovianity in Randomized Benchmarking

Non-Markovianity in a quantum process can encompass both classical and quantum correlations; the latter is manifest in the Choi-state of a process tensor whenever its components are entangled
Giarmatzi and Costa 2021
;
Milz et al. 2021
. As examples of classical correlations, in Appendix
F
we reproduce the
ASF
of the model in Ref.
Ball et al. 2016
, where classical temporal correlations are modeled via dephasing noise determined by a classical stochastic process; this effectively renders an
ASF
analogous to one that is Markovian time-dependent with the noise parameter being a random variable. We also illustrate this via a shallow pocket model
Arenz et al. 2015
;
Arenz et al. 2018
;
Taranto et al. 2019a
;
Milz and Modi 2021
, where the time-dependence in the
ASF
is explicit but the treatment as a Markov
ASF
decay remains the same. These examples suggest that while the general measure of non-Markovianity
\mathcal{N}
for a process tensor in Eq. (
5
) is sensitive to any sort of temporal-correlation, this might not necessarily be the case for the
ASF
.

An
RB
experiment could be blind to non-Markovianity in the sense of producing equivalent data of some Markovian noise model. It is, of course, a possibility for there to be a subclass of time-independent non-Markovian processes leading to exponential or almost exponential behavior, although as mentioned above, in general a time-independent noise does not lead to an exponential behavior unless the environment is superfluous. In Appendix
G
we exemplify this numerically with a spin interaction as source of non-Markovian noise. Whether in general there exists a whole class of non-Markovian processes that can be classified as
RB
blind, together with criteria to decide
RB
blindness, is an open question that could potentially be addressed in the near future.

VI
Models of finite non-Markovian noise

A possible scenario is to have an underlying noise process that is non-negligibly non-Markovian up to a given finite sequence length, with the remaining noise being effectively almost Markovian. This is related to the notion of finite quantum Markov order
Taranto et al. 2019b
;
Taranto et al. 2019c
;
Taranto 2020
, which similar to the classical concept of finite Markov order, describes a quantum process where future statistics depend only on a finite number of the previous operations on the system and its outcomes. We have then the following.

Corollary 1

(Initial non-Markovian noise)
.

Let
\rho
be an initial state on a system-environment,
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
, composite and let
\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\ell:1}(\rho)
describe an
RB

m

\Lambda_{n}
on
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
for all
n

\ell<m
, with the rest of the sequence having noise

maps

on
\mathsf{S}
and associated noise-strengths

. Then the average sequence fidelity (
ASF
) upon acting with a
POVM
element
\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=\tr\{\mathcal{M}\,\mathds{E}[\mathcal{S}_{m}^{\ell:1}(\rho)]\}

(17)

\displaystyle=p_{\ell+1}\cdots{p}_{m}\,\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\circ\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]+B\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right],

where

and

,

are defined in Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9

This implies that after a sequence length

The main reason why this is important is twofold: first, detecting non-Markovian effects with an

VIII
Numerical example: noise memory time scales, Markovianized average sequence fidelity and coherent noise

Consider now again a pair of qubits which up to some sequence length
\ell

that is mostly non-Markovian and subsequently turns almost Markovian. Here we model the underlying noise with

\Lambda_{n}^{(\ell)}=q_{n-\ell}\Lambda+(1-q_{n-\ell})\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}},

,

for

Henceforth we assume that an experimenter would not know both what the noise maps
\Lambda_{n}^{(\ell)}
are, nor what the non-Markovian finite sequence length

1

Our expression assumes that the transition to Markovian noise occurs from step to step, however, even if dissipation occurs smoothly and non-Markovianity never entirely fades, we can still estimate at which sequence length the memory of the noise stops being relevant by identifying exponential decays. This also allows to identify a Markovianized time-independent

A way to achieve this in practice is by fixing Clifford gates to identity wherever the decay appears non-exponential; this will give an exponential decay of the

of one of these sequences

This practical approach can work reasonably well, as we show in this example, and allow both to determine the amount of memory within the noise, i.e, for how long the noise is being meaningfully non-Markovian, as well as to operationally construct a time-independent Markovianized

ASF
.

IX
Conclusions and Discussion

We have,
i
) derived a general analytical expression for the average sequence fidelity (
ASF
) of a randomized benchmarking (
RB
) experiment with the Clifford group subject to gate-independent non-Markovian noise,
ii
) proposed a theoretical measure to quantify non-Markovian deviations in an
ASF
,
iii
) derived the
ASF
for the case of finite non-Markovian noise, allowing to operationally estimate both non-Markovian noise time scales and the measure of deviations from Markovianity, and
iv
) exemplified all these with two proof of principle numerical examples. Along the manuscript we also discuss the effect of state preparation and measurement (
SPAM
) errors in
RB
with non-Markovian noise, as well as the case of classical correlations, which we argue can be treated as a Markovian time-dependent problem, and more generally the idea of
RB
blindness to a subclass of non-Markovian noise processes.

A circuit describing both gate-dependence and non-Markovianity.

\bullet

\bm{\oplus}
in the
[x]

RB
the inverse operation (with an associated error) would be applied and a measurement in
\mathsf{S}
would be performed.

The
ASF
in our main result makes the depolarizing effect of averaging over Clifford gates on the system of interest manifest, while taking all of the noise in the sequence to the environment. The reduction of our main result to the standard Markovian gate-independent scenario is then straightforward with the trace over the environment giving rise to the noise strength and the
SPAM
error constants, and similarly one may consider cases where non-Markovian noise is finite over a subset of sequence lengths. Our main result also makes it clear that in general, non-Markovian noise will display non-exponential behavior, although we point out that there could be a subclass of non-Markovian models that do display an almost exponential decay that in practice would be almost impossible to resolve. We exemplified numerically how for small sequence lengths, deviations from Markovianity can be observed efficiently, as well as how the relevant time-frames for finite non-Markovianity can be operationally determined and non-Markovian deviations in the
ASF
quantified.

We highlight as well that the methods to quantify non-Markovian effects, determine memory time scales, and diagnose coherence of non-Markovian noise, could be implemented beyond the randomized benchmarking framework on other noise benchmarking, characterization or mitigation approaches whenever temporal correlations should be taken into account, e.g. for cross-talk or leakage errors
Brown and Brown 2019
;
Strikis et al. 2020
;
Parrado-Rodríguez et al. 2021
, similar to how it is done within the framework of resource theories in Ref.
Berk et al. 2021
with the notion of temporal coarse-graining.

Needless to say, there are countless ways to move forward in the study of time-correlated errors in quantum computing. Arguably, the clearest ones arising from our manuscript within the
RB
procedure, would be to have a model-independent
ASF
, similar to the Markovian case, to benchmark other experimentally relevant groups or more generally arbitrary gate sets, to study context-dependent errors, with one possible way being the one we propose in Fig.
8
, or to incorporate non-Markovianity in the general
RB
framework carefully constructed in Ref.
Helsen et al. 2020
. All of these extensions have already been studied in quite some depth for Markovian errors and doing the same for the non-Markovian case would be a natural step forward. Other than this, there are questions that still would need to be understood such as the impact of non-Markovianity in decay rates as a function of sequence length, or explicitly how a higher or lower amount of non-Markovianity affects the average gate fidelity. More generally, there is still a need to deeply understand errors arising from temporal correlations, and our result represents a step in this direction.

Appendix A
The process tensor of the noise and gate sequences

The process tensor is a multi-linear map taking
CP
maps as input and giving a single quantum state as output. The operational scenario is the following: an initial quantum state
\rho
on the joint
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
composite is acted on with an operation
\mathcal{G}_{1}
solely on system
\mathsf{S}
, which in general is given by a
CP
map; subsequently the whole composite evolves unitarily through a unitary map
\mathcal{U}_{1}
, after which an operation
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is performed on
\mathsf{S}
, then the whole evolves unitarily under a unitary map
\mathcal{U}_{2}
, and so on, until an intervention
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, followed finally by a unitary map
\mathcal{U}_{k}
. This means the final state in system
\mathsf{S}
will be given by

\rho_{\mathsf{S}}^{(k)}=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\left[\left(\Mcirc_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{U}_{i}\circ\mathcal{G}_{i}\right)\,\rho\right],

(26)

where here we implicitly write
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
for
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
. The process tensor is thus a map
\mathcal{T}_{k:1}:\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{S}})^{\otimes{2k}}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{S}})
, where
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
means space of bounded linear operators over the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, taking
k

CP
maps as arguments and giving a quantum state as output at time-step
k
, i.e.

\mathcal{T}_{k:1}\left[\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{k:1}\right]=\rho_{\mathsf{S}}^{(k)},

(27)

where
\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{k:1}=(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k})
. Such operations
\mathcal{G}_{i}
are said to form an intervention and belong to an instrument, which can be understood as a generalization of a
POVM
, and the particular outcomes of each intervention yield a joint probability distribution describing a stochastic process.

The generalization of a Choi-state, as given by the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism
Watrous 2018
;
Bengtsson and Życzkowski 2017
, for a
k
-step process tensor follows by introducing
k
maximally entangled states
\psi_{\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{B}_{i}}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{A}_{i}}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{B}_{i}})
, where
\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{A}_{i}}\cong\mathscr{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
and similarly for
\mathsf{B}
, and letting half of each (that is, the part of either subspace
\mathsf{A}_{i}
or
\mathsf{B}_{i}
) act as an input at every step by swapping the input spaces with the corresponding auxiliary space. This is more clearly illustrated in Fig.
9
.

Figure 9:
The Choi-state representation of a
k
-step process tensor
, denoted
\Upsilon_{k:1}
, can be obtained by swapping out the system,
\mathscr{S}_{i}
with half a maximally entangled state,
\psi_{\mathsf{A}_{i}\mathsf{B}_{i}}
, at each step
i
. The final state is an unnormalized many-body state acting on a
d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2k+1}
dimensional system.

Specifically, the Choi-state of the process tensor takes the form

\Upsilon_{k:1}=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\left[\left(\Mcirc_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{U}_{i}\circ\mathscr{S}_{i}\right)\rho\otimes\psi^{\otimes{k}}\right],

(28)

where here we are implicitly writing
\mathcal{U}_{i}
for
\mathcal{U}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{B}_{k}}
and
\psi^{\otimes{k}}=\psi_{\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{B}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{B}_{k}}
. The generalized swap
\mathscr{S}_{i}
between system
\mathsf{S}
and an auxiliary space
\mathsf{A}_{i}
at time-step
i
is defined by
\mathscr{S}_{i}(\cdot):=\varsigma_{i}(\cdot)\varsigma_{i}
, where here

\varsigma_{i}\!:=\!\!\sum_{\ell,j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes|\ell\rangle\!\langle{j}|\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{A}_{i-1}\mathsf{B}_{i-1}}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle\ell|\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{B}_{i}\mathsf{A}_{i+1}\mathsf{B}_{i+1}\cdots\mathsf{A}_{k}\mathsf{B}_{k}}.

(29)

Similar to the case of quantum channels, the isomorphism between the action of the process tensor and its Choi representation is manifest
Figueroa-Romero 2021
through the relationship

\mathcal{T}_{k:1}\left[\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{k:1}\right]=\tr_{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\left[\Upsilon_{k:1}\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\mathfrak{Y}_{k:1}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\right],

(30)

where here

\mathfrak{Y}_{k:1}=\left(\Motimes_{i=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{A}_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{i}\right)\psi^{\otimes{k}},

(31)

is the Choi-state for the operations
\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{k:1}
, the notation
\tr_{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}
stands for partial trace over all except output in
\mathsf{S}
, and
\mathrm{T}
denotes a transpose.

Figure 10:
The process tensor for a Markov process
\Upsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{k:1}
, having no temporal correlations, can be described by the Choi-state
\Upsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{k:1}
taking a product of Choi-states
\Phi_{j:i}
connecting adjacent time-steps
i
to

. Each
\Phi_{j:i}
corresponds to a

The tensor
\mathfrak{Y}_{k:1}
is an example of a Markovian process tensor in the sense that it does not have any temporal correlations and thus assumes a product form. For a Markovian dynamical
k
-step process,
\Upsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{k:1}=\Motimes_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{i:i-1}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
, the Choi-states
\Phi_{j:i}
can either correspond to a closed system dynamics between steps
i
and

, or in general to a
CP
dynamics, e.g. that of an open system, where the environment is discarded between each step and no information passes on to the next step, as shown in Figure

. The order of the spaces will be relevant whenever two tensors are contracted and can be written generally through swaps with auxiliary spaces and half maximally entangled states.

In this manuscript we describe a noise
RB
sequence as a process tensor with the dynamics being described by the noise at each step,
\mathcal{U}_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}
. Explicitly, in the main text we denote the tensor Choi-state for a
m+1
-step process for the noise as

\Upsilon_{m}=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\left\{\left[\Mcirc_{i=1}^{m+1}(\Lambda_{i}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{aux}})\circ\mathscr{S}_{i}\right]\,\rho\otimes\psi^{\otimes m+1}\right\},

(32)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{aux}}
is an identity map on an auxiliary space
\mathsf{aux}=\mathsf{A}_{1}\mathsf{B}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{A}_{m+1}\mathsf{B}_{m+1}\simeq\mathsf{S}^{\otimes 2(m+1)}
composed of
m+1
pairs of
\mathsf{S}
systems,
\mathscr{S}_{i}
is a swap gate between
\mathsf{S}
and one of these pairs in the
i
th
auxiliary space, say
\mathsf{A}_{i}
, and
\psi:=\sum|ii\rangle\!\langle{jj}|
is an unnormalized maximally entangled state. The tensor state for the gate sequence, on the other hand, can be defined simply

\mathfrak{C}_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\left[\Motimes_{i=1}^{m+1}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{A}_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{i}\right)\right]\psi^{\otimes{m+1}},

(33)

where strictly speaking, here we are defining
\mathfrak{C}_{m}=\mathds{1}\otimes\mathfrak{A}_{m}
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{m}
is the Choi state of the sequence of gates
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{i}
can be defined to act on either auxiliary space
\mathsf{A}_{i}
or
\mathsf{B}_{i}
, the choice only depends on what auxiliary space the swap
\mathscr{S}_{i}
on the definition of
\Upsilon_{m}
swaps with, so that the contraction
\Upsilon_{m}\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}
contracts the correct spaces.

Appendix B
Average gate sequence

Representing the noise and gate sequences as process tensors implies that computing the
ASF
just requires computing the average gate sequence,
\mathds{E}\left(\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)
.

Whenever the gates entering this sequence belong to at least a unitary 2-design, we can simply replace the average over gates by that over unitaries distributed uniformly, i.e. according to the Haar measure, say
\mu
, over the
d_{\mathsf{S}}
-dimensional unitary group, which we denote
\mathds{U}(d_{\mathsf{S}})
. The Haar measure is the unique measure on
\mathds{U}(d)
satisfying invariance under left and right multiplication, i.e. it is invariant under arbitrary rotations. Specifically, given a subset
\mathds{V}\subseteq\mathds{U}(d)
, we have
\mu(\mathds{W})=\int_{\mathds{W}}d\mu(U)
for the Haar measure,
\mu
, with the (left-right invariance) property

\displaystyle\mu(\mathds{W})

\displaystyle=\mu(V\mathds{W})=\int_{\mathds{W}}d\mu(VU)=\int_{\mathds{W}}d\mu(UV)=\mu(\mathds{W}V),

(34)

for any fixed
V\in\mathds{U}(d)
. For any quantity
f
depending on a unitary
U\in\mathds{U}(d)
, we denote integration over such unitary by
\mathds{E}[f(U)]
and refer to it as the Haar or uniform average of
f
.

Let the action of the unitary maps
\mathcal{G}_{i}
be given by
\mathcal{G}_{i}(\cdot)=G_{i}(\cdot)G_{i}^{\dagger}
, then let us compute

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\dagger}\right]=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\Motimes_{i=1}^{m+1}\sum_{\ell_{i},k_{i}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\int\limits_{\mathds{U}(d_{\mathsf{S}})}\left(|\ell_{i}\rangle\!\langle{k_{i}}|\otimes{G}_{i}^{\dagger}|\ell_{i}\rangle\!\langle{k}_{i}|G_{i}\right)\,d\mu(G_{1})\cdots{d}\mu(G_{m}),

(35)

where crucially,
G_{m+1}=G_{1}^{\dagger}{G}_{2}^{\dagger}\cdots{G}_{m}^{\dagger}
. This means that we need to be able to compute integrals with two pairs of
G_{i}
and
G_{i}^{\dagger}
terms. One way to do this is by employing the 2-moment of
\mathds{U}(d)
, given by
Collins 2003
;
Collins and Śniady 2006

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{U}(d)}

\displaystyle U_{v_{1}u_{1}}U^{*}_{v^{\prime}_{1}u^{\prime}_{1}}U_{v_{2}u_{2}}U^{*}_{v^{\prime}_{2}u^{\prime}_{2}}\,d\mu(U)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\,\in\,\mathds{S}_{2}}\delta_{u_{1}u^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}\delta_{u_{2}u^{\prime}_{\sigma(2)}}\delta_{v_{1}v^{\prime}_{\tau(1)}}\delta_{v_{2}v^{\prime}_{\tau(2)}}\mathrm{Wg}(\tau\sigma^{-1},d),

(36)

where here the subindices
v_{1}u_{1},v_{1}^{\prime}u_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}u_{2},v_{2}^{\prime}u_{2}^{\prime}
refer to components of the same matrix
U
(with
U^{*}
denoting complex conjugate), with the extra subindices
1,2
being mere labels, and where
\mathds{S}_{2}
is the symmetric group on 2 elements. The symbol
\mathrm{Wg}
is the Weingarten function, which here takes the values

\mathrm{Wg}[(1)(2),d]=\frac{1}{d^{2}-1},\qquad\mathrm{Wg}[(1,2),d]=-\frac{1}{d(d^{2}-1)},

(37)

on the two possible permutations
\tau\sigma^{-1}\in\mathds{S}_{2}
.

Then we can let
G_{\ell}=\sum{G}_{v^{(\ell)}{u}^{(\ell)}}|v^{(\ell)}\rangle\!\langle{u}^{(\ell)}|
for each
G_{\ell}
and employ the 2-moment above; let us take the integral over
G_{1}
first,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\dagger}\right]=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\sum_{u,v=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathds{S}_{2}}\mathrm{Wg}(\tau\sigma^{-1},d_{\mathsf{S}})|v^{\prime(1)}_{1}{u}^{\prime\,(1)}_{1}\rangle\!\langle{v}^{\prime\,(1)}_{\tau(1)}u_{\sigma(1)}^{\prime\,(1)}|

\displaystyle\qquad\Motimes_{\ell=2}^{m}\sum_{i_{\ell},j_{\ell}}\,\int\limits_{\mathds{U}(d)}\left(|i_{\ell}\rangle\!\langle{j}_{\ell}|\otimes{G}_{\ell}^{\dagger}|i_{\ell}\rangle\!\langle{j}_{\ell}|G_{\ell}\right)\otimes\,|v_{\tau(2)}^{\prime\,(1)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{2}^{\prime\,(1)}|\otimes{G}_{m}\cdots{G}_{2}|{u}_{\sigma(2)}^{\prime\,(1)}\rangle\!\langle{u}_{2}^{\prime\,(1)}|G^{\dagger}_{2}\cdots{G}^{\dagger}_{m}\,d\mu(G_{2})\cdots{d}\mu(G_{m}),

(38)

then we can do similarly with all remaining unitaries by also labeling each permutation mapping
\sigma_{\ell}
and
\tau_{\ell}
for the corresponding integral over each
G_{\ell}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\sum\mathrm{Wg}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{Wg}_{m}|v^{(1)}_{1}{u}^{(1)}_{1}\cdots{v}^{(m)}_{1}{u}^{(m)}_{1}{u}_{\sigma_{m}(2)}^{(m)}v_{\tau_{1}(2)}^{(1)}\rangle\!\langle{v}^{(1)}_{\tau_{1}(1)}u_{\sigma_{1}(1)}^{(1)}\cdots{v}^{(m)}_{\tau_{m}(1)}{u}_{\sigma_{m}(1)}^{(m)}{u}_{2}^{(m)}{v}_{2}^{(1)}|

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\delta(u_{2}^{(1)},v_{2}^{(2)})\cdots\delta(u_{2}^{(m-1)},v_{2}^{(m)})\,\delta(u_{\sigma_{1}(2)}^{(1)},v_{\tau_{2}(2)}^{(2)})\cdots\delta(u_{\sigma_{m-1}(2)}^{(m-1)},v_{\tau_{m}(2)}^{(m)}),

(39)

where for easiness of notation we dropped the primes and denoted
\mathrm{Wg}_{\ell}:=\mathrm{Wg}(\tau_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell}^{-1},d_{\mathsf{S}})
, where a sum is implicit over basis vectors and permutations on
\mathds{S}_{2}
, and where
\delta(a,b)
stands for the usual Kronecker
\delta_{ab}
. We finally notice that
\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\dagger}\right]=\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]
.

Appendix C
Average sequence fidelity

The Markovian case.
As a first case let us verify that the average gate sequence given by Eq.
39
reproduces an
ASF
described by a decaying exponential in the number of gates when the noise is Markovian.

Consider first a single gate,
m=1
. We have

\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]=\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes\sum\mathrm{Wg}|v_{1}{u}_{1}u_{\sigma(2)}v_{\tau(2)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{\tau(1)}u_{\sigma(1)}u_{2}v_{2}|,

(40)

where
\mathrm{Wg}
implicitly depends on
\tau\sigma^{-1}
, with each
\tau
and
\sigma
being summed over the symmetric group
\mathbb{S}_{2}
, and with an implicit sum over each
u
and
v
.

For the Markovian process tensor, we consider noise described by some
d_{\mathsf{S}}
-dimensional
CP
map
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
at time-step
n
with Kraus representation
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{i_{n}}(\cdot)\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{i_{n}}
, so that

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}=(\Lambda_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\mathcal{I})\circ\mathscr{S}_{2}\circ(\Lambda_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\mathcal{I})\circ\mathscr{S}_{1}\left(\rho\otimes\psi^{\otimes 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum(\lambda_{i_{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\mathds{1})\varsigma_{2}(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\mathds{1})\varsigma_{1}(\rho\otimes\psi^{\otimes 2})\varsigma_{1}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})\varsigma_{2}^{\dagger}(\lambda_{i_{2}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1})

\displaystyle=\sum\lambda_{i_{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}|\alpha_{2}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{2}|\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}|\alpha_{1}\rangle\!\langle\beta_{1}|\rho|\delta_{1}\rangle\!\langle\gamma_{1}|\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}|\delta_{2}\rangle\!\langle\gamma_{2}|\lambda_{i_{2}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}\otimes|\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}\rangle\!\langle\delta_{1}\gamma_{1}\delta_{2}\gamma_{2}|.

(41)

Let us simply denote
d_{\mathsf{S}}
as
d
, as there is no environment to care about. Then we obtain the average sequence

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\mathcal{S}_{1}(\rho)]=\tr_{\overline{S}}\left\{\Upsilon_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left[\sum\mathrm{Wg}|v_{2}\rangle\!\langle{u}_{2}|\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}|u_{\sigma(1)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{\tau(1)}|\rho|v_{1}\rangle\!\langle{u}_{1}|\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}|{u}_{\sigma(2)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{\tau(2)}|\right]

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2}\Bigg[\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\left(\,\underbrace{\tr\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)\,\mathds{1}}_{\sigma=\tau=\bm{1}}+\sum_{i}\underbrace{\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}})\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger})\,\rho}_{\sigma=\tau=(1,2)}\right)-\frac{1}{d(d^{2}-1)}\left(\,\underbrace{\tr\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)\,\rho}_{\sigma=\bm{1},\,\tau=(1,2)}\!\!+\sum_{i}\underbrace{\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}})\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger})\,\mathds{1}}_{\sigma(1,2),\,\tau=(1)(2)}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2}\left[\frac{d\tr\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)-\sum_{i}|\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}})|^{2}}{d^{2}-1}\,\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\right)+\frac{d\sum_{i}|\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}})|^{2}-\tr\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)}{d(d^{2}-1)}\,\rho\right].

(42)

Now, if the noise is trace-preserving as well, we have
\tr\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\lambda_{i_{1}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)=\tr(\mathds{1})=d
. Then we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\mathcal{S}_{1}(\rho)]=\Lambda_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\circ\mathscr{D}_{p}(\rho),

(43)

where we define
\mathscr{D}_{p}(X):=pX+(1-p)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}
as a depolarising map with the so-called noise-strength,

p:=\frac{\sum_{i}|\tr(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}})|^{2}-1}{d^{2}-1}\in[0,1],

(44)

which has to be constrained to
[0,1]
. If we denote the noise map
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\lambda_{i_{n}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}

\mathsf{S}
Hilbert space and conjugate space, resp., we can simply write

(45)

The noise-strength can be shown to be related to the gate fidelity of
\Lambda_{1}
with respect to the identity
Fogarty et al. 2015
, i.e.
\mathsf{f}_{\Lambda_{1},\mathcal{I}}=\int{d}\psi\langle\psi|\Lambda_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle
, as
p=\frac{df_{\Lambda_{1},\mathcal{I}}-1}{d-1}
. This is the relevant parameter which in practice can be recovered by running several sequences for different lengths and averaging the resulting probabilities.

To generalize to an arbitrary number of time-steps, we now use the fact that the action of the depolarizing channel can be written as

\mathscr{D}_{p}(X)=\sum\mathrm{Wg}|v_{2}\rangle\!\langle{u}_{2}|\lambda_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}|u_{\sigma(1)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{\tau(1)}|X|v_{1}\rangle\!\langle{u}_{1}|\lambda_{i}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\,\dagger}|{u}_{\sigma(2)}\rangle\!\langle{v}_{\tau(2)}|,

(46)

for any
X
, which follows from Eq. (
42
). This then implies that for an arbitrary sequence length,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\tr_{\overline{\mathsf{S}}}\left\{\Upsilon_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\mathds{E}\left[\mathfrak{C}_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{D}_{p_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathscr{D}_{p_{1}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots{p}_{m}\,\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\rho-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\right)+\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\right),

(47)

where here now

, as expected. The case

corresponds to the Markovian time-dependent noise case as in Ref.
Wallman and Flammia 2014
. When the noise-strengths are the same this gives the usual fitting model for the average probabilities

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle=p^{m}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\rho-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\right)\right]+\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}\right)\right]:=Ap^{m}+B,

(48)

with

and

, which relate to state preparation and measurement errors.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{e},\bm{\epsilon}}\chi^{\begin{subarray}{c}ee^{\prime}\\
ss^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{1}\mathrm{L}_{i_{\eta}}^{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{\eta}e^{\prime}_{\eta}\\
s_{\eta}s^{\prime}_{\eta}\end{subarray}}|e_{\eta}\rangle\!\langle{e}_{\eta}^{\prime}|\right)|e\rangle\!\langle{e}^{\prime}|\left(\prod_{n=1}^{m}\mathrm{L}_{i_{n}}^{*\,\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{n}\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}\\
\zeta_{n}\zeta^{\prime}_{n}\end{subarray}}|\epsilon_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle\epsilon_{n}|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{1}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\langle{s}|\,\rho\,|s^{\prime}\rangle\left(\prod_{n=1}^{m}\langle\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{n}\rangle\right)\right],

(60)

so that,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\,\langle{s}^{\prime}_{m+1}|\Phi_{s_{m}\zeta_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{s_{1}\zeta_{1}}(|s\rangle\!\langle{s}^{\prime}|)|\zeta^{\prime}_{m+1}\rangle\,|s_{m+1}\rangle\!\langle\zeta_{m+1}|.

(61)

The sequential application of
\Phi
maps is given by

\Phi_{s_{m}\zeta_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{s_{1}\zeta_{1}}(X):=\bm{\alpha}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\left(X-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)+\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\,\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(62)

where

\bm{\alpha}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}:=\prod_{n=1}^{m}\alpha_{s_{n}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{n}^{(\prime)}},

(63)

and
\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}
is a sum of all
m
-term product combinations of
\alpha_{s_{1}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{1}^{(\prime)}},\cdots,\alpha_{s_{m}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{m}^{(\prime)}}
and
\beta_{s_{1}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{1}^{(\prime)}},\cdots,\beta_{s_{m}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{m}^{(\prime)}}
, that is,

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{(1)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}

\displaystyle\sim\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}

(64)

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{(2)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}

\displaystyle\sim\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}+\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}+\beta_{1}\beta_{2}

(65)

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{(3)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}

\displaystyle\sim\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\beta_{3}+\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}+\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}+\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}\beta_{3}+\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{3}+\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\beta_{3}

(66)

\displaystyle\,\,\vdots

where
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{s_{i}^{(\prime)}\zeta_{i}^{(\prime)}}
and similarly for
\beta_{i}
; in general there are
2^{m}
of these summands on
\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}
. However, notice that as
\beta_{i}\sim\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\delta_{s_{i}\zeta_{i}}\delta_{s_{i}^{\prime}\zeta_{i}^{\prime}}-\alpha_{i}
, every term simplifies to products of deltas, i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}^{(1)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\sim\frac{\delta_{s_{1}\zeta_{1}}\delta_{s_{1}^{\prime}\zeta_{1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}},\quad\bm{\Delta}^{(2)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\sim\frac{\delta_{s_{1}\zeta_{1}}\delta_{s_{1}^{\prime}\zeta_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{2}\zeta_{2}}\delta_{s_{2}^{\prime}\zeta_{2}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}},\quad\cdots\quad,\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\sim\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{s_{i}\zeta_{i}}\delta_{s_{i}^{\prime}\zeta_{i}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m}}.

(67)

Thus we can rewrite Eq. (
61
) as

\displaystyle\mathds{E}

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]=\sum\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\,\left[\bm{\alpha}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\left(\delta_{ss_{m+1}^{\prime}}\delta_{s^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}-\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)+\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\left(\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right]\,|s_{m+1}\rangle\!\langle\zeta_{m+1}|,

(68)

and so for a measurement
\mathcal{M}
, on average,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}=\sum_{s,\zeta,s^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\,\left(\mathcal{A}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}+\mathcal{B}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}\right),

(69)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}:=\bm{\alpha}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\left(\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}s}\delta_{s^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}\!-\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\langle\zeta_{m+1}|\mathcal{M}|s_{m+1}\rangle,\qquad\mathcal{B}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}:=\bm{\Delta}^{(m)}_{\bm{s},\bm{\zeta}}\,\left(\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\langle\zeta_{m+1}|\mathcal{M}|s_{m+1}\rangle.

(70)

This expression contains
4d_{\mathsf{S}}(m+1)
terms, and could potentially be useful whenever the underlying noise model is not known, as all this information will be contained solely on the factors
\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}
.

We can, however, write this expression in a more succinct way. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{s,\zeta,s^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\mathcal{A}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}

\displaystyle=\sum\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\eta=m+1}^{1}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\langle{s}|\,\rho\,|s^{\prime}\rangle\left(\prod_{n=1}^{m+1}\langle\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{n}\rangle\right)\right]\left(\frac{\prod_{N=1}^{m}\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}\delta_{s_{N}s^{\prime}_{N}}\delta_{\zeta_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}-\delta_{s_{N}\zeta_{N}}\delta_{s^{\prime}_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}\right)}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m}(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)^{m}}\right)\left(\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}s}\delta_{s^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}\!-\frac{\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\delta_{s_{m+1}^{\prime}\zeta_{m+1}^{\prime}}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\langle\zeta_{m+1}|\mathcal{M}|s_{m+1}\rangle,

(71)

so now let us define the following. Let

\displaystyle\$_{\Lambda_{n}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i_{n}})\,\varepsilon\,\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}),

(72)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\tr_{\mathsf{S}}\left[\Lambda_{n}\left(\varepsilon\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right],

(73)

for any operator
\varepsilon
acting on
\mathsf{E}
. Then we notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m}}\sum\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{1}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\langle{s}|\rho|{s}^{\prime}\rangle\left(\prod_{n=1}^{m}\langle\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{n}\rangle\right)\prod_{N=1}^{m}\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}\delta_{s_{N}s^{\prime}_{N}}\delta_{\zeta_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}-\delta_{s_{N}\zeta_{N}}\delta_{s^{\prime}_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m}}\sum\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{2}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i_{1}}){\langle{s}|\rho|{s}^{\prime}\rangle}\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i_{1}}^{\dagger})-\tr_{\mathsf{S}}[\Lambda_{1}(\langle{s}|\rho|{s}^{\prime}\rangle\otimes\mathds{1})]\right)\left(\prod_{n=2}^{m}\langle\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{n}\rangle\right)\prod_{N=2}^{m}\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}\delta_{s_{N}s^{\prime}_{N}}\delta_{\zeta_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}-\delta_{s_{N}\zeta_{N}}\delta_{s^{\prime}_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m-1}}\sum\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{2}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\left(\$_{\Lambda_{1}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{1}}\right)\langle{s}|\rho|{s}^{\prime}\rangle\left(\prod_{n=2}^{m}\langle\zeta^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{n}\rangle\right)\prod_{N=2}^{m}\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}\delta_{s_{N}s^{\prime}_{N}}\delta_{\zeta_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}-\delta_{s_{N}\zeta_{N}}\delta_{s^{\prime}_{N}\zeta^{\prime}_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\vdots

\displaystyle=\left[\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\right]\langle{s}|\rho|{s}^{\prime}\rangle,

(74)

where as before there are implicit identities which should be clear by context, i.e. for example
\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i})
means
\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\lambda_{i})\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{S}}
. Then this means that

\displaystyle\sum_{s,\zeta,s^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\mathcal{A}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}=\frac{\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\left[\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}\right]\left(\rho-\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right\}}{(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)^{m}},

(75)

where
\rho_{\mathsf{E}}:=\tr_{\mathsf{S}}(\rho)
. Now for the second term, similarly (again we omit implicit identity operators),

\displaystyle\sum_{s,\zeta,s^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}^{(m+1)}\mathcal{B}^{(m+1)}_{\bm{s}^{(\prime)}\bm{\zeta}^{(\prime)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{m+1}}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tr\left[\langle{s}_{m+1}|\lambda_{i_{m+1}}|s^{\prime}_{m+1}\rangle\left(\prod_{\eta=m}^{1}\langle{s}_{\eta}|\lambda_{i_{\eta}}|{s}_{\eta}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\langle{s}|\,\rho\,|s\rangle\left(\prod_{n=1}^{m}\langle{s}^{\prime}_{n}|\lambda_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}|s_{n}\rangle\right)\langle{s_{m+1}^{\prime}}|\lambda_{i_{m+1}}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{m+1}\rangle\right]\langle\zeta_{m+1}|\mathcal{M}|s_{m+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\mathcal{M}\right)\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\left(\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}\right)\left(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right].

(76)

Thus we can finally write

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}=\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\left(\mathscr{A}_{m}+\mathscr{B}_{m}\right)\rho\right],

(77)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{m}(\rho)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}\left(\rho-\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{m}(\rho)

\displaystyle:=\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\left(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\right)\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(79)

with
\$_{\Lambda_{n}}
and
\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}
defined in Eq.(
72
) and Eq. (
73
), resp.

Appendix D
Markovian limit

For the Markovian limit we take
\Lambda_{n}\to\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{n}
and
\rho=\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
. First, let us notice that, assuming
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
are
CPTP
,

\displaystyle\$_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}(\varepsilon)=\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\right]\,\varepsilon,\qquad\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}(\varepsilon)=\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right]\varepsilon=\varepsilon,

(80)

for any operators
\varepsilon
acting on
\mathsf{E}
and
\sigma
on
\mathsf{S}
. Then this implies that

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}})

\displaystyle\to\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}})\otimes\left(\rho_{\mathsf{S}}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr\left[\Lambda_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\right]-1}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\left[\Mcirc_{n=2}^{m}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)\right](\rho_{\mathsf{E}})\otimes\left(\rho_{\mathsf{S}}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle\,\vdots

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{m}\left(\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\right]-1\right)}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}\left(\rho_{\mathsf{S}}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{1}\cdots{p}_{m}\left(\rho_{\mathsf{S}}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right),

(81)

where here as well
p_{n}:=\frac{\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\right]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}
is the noise-strength of
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
, and

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{B}_{m}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}})

\displaystyle\to\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\left(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\right)\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}=\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}},

(82)

which implies that
\mathcal{F}_{m}\to{p}_{1}\cdots{p}_{m}\tr[\mathcal{M}\,\Lambda_{m+1}(\rho-\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}})]+\tr[\mathcal{M}\,\Lambda_{m+1}(\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}})]
under Markovian noise.

Appendix E
Finite non-Markovian noise

Initial non-Markovian noise.
Suppose a quantum noise process
\hat{\Upsilon}_{m}
is non-Markovian up to some time-step
\ell<m
and almost Markovian in the remaining steps, i.e.
\hat{\Upsilon}_{m}\simeq\Upsilon_{\ell}\otimes\Upsilon_{m:\ell+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
, where
\Upsilon_{m:\ell}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
is a Markov process from time-step
\ell+1
to time-step
m
. This effectively would mean that
\mathsf{E}
is traced at the
\ell
th
step and the remaining noise maps act only on
\mathsf{S}
. We can describe this by replacing the action of the noise map at the
\ell
th
-step as
\Lambda_{\ell}(X)\to\varepsilon\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}[\Lambda_{\ell}(X)]
, where
X
is the joint
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
state at such step, and where
\varepsilon
is some fiducial state of
\mathsf{E}
. The remaining noise maps will be given by
\Lambda_{n}\to\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
for
\ell<n\leq{m+1}
with some
CPTP
maps
\Lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}_{n}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m}(\rho)

\displaystyle\to\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}\left(\rho-\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)=p_{\ell+1}\cdots{p}_{m}\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho),

(83)

and also
\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{B}_{m}(\rho)=\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)=\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}}
. In particular if the final noise were trace-preserving, we would have
\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]=1
. In general, however, this implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}\to p_{\ell+1}\cdots{p}_{m}\,\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\circ\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]+\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]\,\tr\left[\mathcal{M}\Lambda_{m+1}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right]\qquad\text{with}\qquad\ell<m,

(84)

where here again
p_{n}=\frac{\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\right]-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}
is the noise-strength corresponding to
\Lambda_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}
.

This means, as one would expect, that in such a case if non-Markovian noise cannot be resolved with an
RB
sequence length
\ell
, it would amount to
SPAM
errors, with any subsequent
ASF
decay being Markovian. Notice however, that for short sequence lengths, non-Markovian noise could be resolved on average with a few runs of the
RB
protocol; as explained in the main text, this would allow to estimate the degree of non-Markovianity in the underlying process.

Late non-Markovian noise.
Now consider the opposite, where the noise process is initially Markovian but somehow
\mathsf{E}
stops being superfluous after some time-step
\ell<m
, i.e.
\hat{\Upsilon}\simeq\Upsilon_{\ell}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(\mathrm{M})}}\otimes\Upsilon_{m:\ell+1}
. Now we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{m}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}})

\displaystyle\to\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=1}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m}}\left[\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\left(\rho_{\mathsf{S}}-\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right)\right]=p_{1}\cdots{p}_{\ell}\,\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+1}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}),

(85)

where here we defined

\mathscr{A}_{m:k}(\rho):=\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=k}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{S}}}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m-k+1}}\left[\rho-\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right],

(86)

whilst now
\mathscr{B}_{m}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}})=\mathscr{B}_{m:\ell+1}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}})
, where similarly,
\mathscr{B}_{m:k}(\rho):=\Mcirc_{n=k}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}(\rho_{\mathsf{E}})\otimes\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}}
. Thus

\mathcal{F}_{m}\to p_{1}\cdots{p}_{\ell}\tr[\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+1}(\rho)]+\tr[\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{B}_{m:\ell+1}(\rho)]\qquad\text{with}\qquad\ell<m,

(87)

so we get a similar behavior, but in this case, as we have seen, it would generally be harder to resolve non-Markovian effects in
RB
if these occur at longer sequences.

Blocks of finite non-Markovian noise.
Now we may consider the case when the noise process is split in two non-Markovian processes, i.e. the first noise process somehow approximately resets the environment at step
\ell
and the remaining noise process is also non-Markovian until step
m
, i.e.
\hat{\Upsilon}_{m}\simeq\Upsilon_{\ell}\otimes\Upsilon_{m:\ell+1}.
Now the only difference from a standard non-Markovian
ASF
is that at the
\ell
th
step we have
\Lambda_{\ell}(X)\to\varepsilon\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{\ell}(X)
, where again
\varepsilon
is some fiducial state of
\mathsf{E}
and
X
is the state of
\mathsf{S}\mathsf{E}
at the
\ell
th
step. This means we can write

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{m}(\rho)

\displaystyle\to\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+1}\left[\varepsilon\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=\ell+1}^{m}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)(\varepsilon)}{\left(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1\right)^{m-\ell}}\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho),

(88)

whilst now,

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{m}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\left(\Mcirc_{n=\ell+1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\left(\Mcirc_{n=1}^{\ell}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\Mcirc_{n=\ell+1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\tr_{\mathsf{S}}\left[\Lambda_{n}\left\{\left(\Mcirc_{n=1}^{\ell-1}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right\}\right]\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\Lambda_{n}\left\{\left(\Mcirc_{n=1}^{\ell-1}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}\right\}\right]\left(\Mcirc_{n=\ell+1}^{m}\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)\varepsilon\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}}{d_{\mathsf{S}}}

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]\,\mathscr{B}_{m:\ell+1}(\varepsilon\otimes\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}}),

(89)

so we may write

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}\to\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+1}\left[\varepsilon\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]\right\}+\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(\rho)\right]\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{B}_{m:\ell+1}(\varepsilon\otimes\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}})\right\}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\ell<m.

(90)

This is a much more complicated behavior, but notice that similarly now after a sequence length
\ell
, the first block of non-Markovian noise will be manifest only as
SPAM
errors. Also, now in essence any other possible mixture of Markovian and non-Markovian noise can be considered, e.g. if there is Markovian noise in-between this would give rise to
p
factors within the first summand of Eq. (
90
) containing
\mathscr{A}
, and
\tr[\mathscr{B}(\rho)]
factors in the second summand.

In particular, suppose we have two blocks of finite non-Markovian noise, first one of length
k<\ell
, and then a second block of length
\ell<m
. Then we get a recursive expression for the
ASF
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle\to\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+k+1}\left[\varepsilon_{\ell}\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{\ell:k+1}\left[\varepsilon_{k}\otimes\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\mathscr{A}_{k}(\rho)\right]\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{k}(\rho)\right]\tr\left[\mathscr{B}_{\ell:k+1}(\varepsilon_{k}\otimes\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}})\right]\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{B}_{m:\ell+k+1}(\varepsilon_{\ell}\otimes\mathds{1}/d_{\mathsf{S}})\right\}\qquad\text{with}\qquad{k}<\ell<m.

(91)

If moreover the initial state is uncorrelated,
\rho=\rho_{\mathsf{E}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\tr\left[\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=1}^{k}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)(\rho_{\mathsf{E}})\right]\tr\left[\displaystyle{\Mcirc_{n=k+1}^{\ell}}\left(\$_{\Lambda_{n}}-\Theta_{\Lambda_{n}}\right)(\varepsilon_{k})\right]}{(d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1)^{\ell}}\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\,\tr_{\mathsf{E}}\circ\Lambda_{m+1}\circ\mathscr{A}_{m:\ell+k+1}\left[\varepsilon_{\ell}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{S}}\right]\right\}

Classical dephasing noise.
For the case of classical correlations we now exemplify how we may describe these through a classical memory specified by an external classical stochastic process whose outputs control the noise

In particular, such model takes a qubit system with errors
\Lambda_{i}^{(\mathsf{C})}(\cdot)=\lambda_{i}(\cdot)\lambda_{i}^{\dagger}
where
\lambda_{i}=\exp\left(-i\delta_{i}\otimes{Z}\right)=\exp\left(-i\delta_{i}Z\right)

is a random variable determined by the classical external control; such variables are randomly sampled but then fixed for the whole
RB
experiment. Thus a sequence of length
m
can be treated as a Markovian time-dependent decay
\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]=p_{1}\cdots{p}_{m}\,\Lambda_{m+1}^{\textsf{(C)}}(\rho)

\lambda
, we can compute for small
\delta

\displaystyle p_{i}=\frac{|\tr\left(\mathrm{e}^{-i\delta_{i}\hat{Z}}\right)|^{2}-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}=\frac{4\cos^{2}\delta_{i}-1}{3}.

(93)

Letting the classical memory be a normally distributed discrete stochastic process
X_{i}\sim\mathscr{N}(\mu=0,\sigma^{2})
with mean
\mu=0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, the so-called Markovian scenario considers the control operations at step
i
giving a realization
\mathrm{X}_{i}=x_{i}
and setting
\delta_{i}=x_{i}
. That is, all errors being independent of each other. Ignoring
SPAM
errors, suppose
\rho=\mathcal{M}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\mathsf{C-Mark}}=\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]\right\}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{4\cos^{2}\delta_{i}-1}{3}\right).

(94)

The other extreme scenario is when all noise random variables are identical so that
\delta_{i}=\delta
, so-called DC-noise; here the control should measure a realization with probability
p
and update the memory with a PDF of the form
\sum_{i}\mathds{P}(X=x_{i})\Delta(\delta-x_{i})
, where here
\Delta

\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]=p^{m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\delta\hat{Z}}\rho\,\mathrm{e}^{i\delta\hat{Z}}
, which similarly for
\rho=\mathcal{M}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
becomes simply

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\mathsf{C-DC}}=\tr\left\{\mathcal{M}\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]\right\}=\left(\frac{4\cos^{2}\delta-1}{3}\right)^{m}.

(95)

For both extreme cases we see that we effectively reproduce the behavior described in
Ball et al. 2016

(96)

\sigma=0.015

\delta^{2}
. The final behavior of
\left\langle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\mathsf{C-DC}}\right\rangle
differs both from an exponential and a simple product of noise-strengths. We show plots for the average fidelities in both cases with a standard deviation of

The shallow pocket model.
We now consider a similar model for a qubit
\mathsf{S}
coupled to degree of freedom (
d.o.f.
) on a real line, which acts as an environment. This is labeled a shallow pocket model because such
d.o.f.
cannot store energy internally. This is an interesting model for several reasons, but here mainly because it leads to completely positive and divisible dynamics of
\mathsf{S}
but it is nevertheless non-Markovian
Taranto et al. 2019a
;
Milz and Modi 2021
. For
RB
, however, the nature of classical correlations is what leads to a treatment of the
ASF
as a time-dependent Markovian one.

The shallow pocket model now considers
\Lambda_{n}^{\mathsf{(C)}}(\cdot)=\lambda_{n}(\cdot)\lambda_{n}^{\dagger}
with
\lambda_{n}=\exp(-i\tau_{n}\,\hat{x}_{n}\otimes{Z})=\exp(-i\tau_{n}\,x_{n}\,Z)
, where
\hat{x}_{n}
is a position operator at time-step
n
and
\tau_{n}
are time-intervals representing evolution time of the
n
th
step. This immediately implies that the average sequence is of the form
\mathds{E}\left[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)\right]=p_{1}\cdots{p}_{m}\,\Lambda_{m+1}^{\textsf{(C)}}(\rho)
, where
\rho=\rho_{\mathsf{S}}\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
. The initial state of the environment
d.o.f.
is taken as
|\psi\rangle
such that
\langle\psi|x_{1}\rangle=\sqrt{\gamma/\pi}\,/(x_{1}+i\gamma)
. Now tracing out the environment at the end of the process is equivalent to integrating
x
over the reals with a factor
\langle\psi|x\rangle\!\langle{x}_{m}|\psi\rangle\delta_{xx_{2}}\delta_{x_{2}x_{3}}\cdots\delta_{x_{m}{x}_{m+1}}
. Thus we can think of the external
d.o.f.
as a classical DC noise distributed with a probability density function
|\langle\psi|x\rangle|^{2}
.

That is, now we have

p_{\tau_{n}}(x_{n})=\frac{|\tr\left(\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau_{n}\,x_{n}\,\hat{Z}}\right)|^{2}-1}{d_{\mathsf{S}}^{2}-1}=\frac{4\cos^{2}(\tau_{n}\,x_{n})-1}{3}.

(97)

Notice that all
p
’s have to be constrained to
[0,1]
, so to have a meaningful
ASF
the equivalent of our distribution, namely
|\langle\psi|x\rangle|^{2}
, has to contain a low enough equivalent of a variance, which amounts to choosing an appropriate value for
\gamma
. Hence, now taking
\rho=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
and
\mathcal{M}=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\mathsf{shallow}}=\tr\{\mathcal{M}\,\mathds{E}[\mathcal{S}_{m}(\rho)]\}=\frac{\gamma}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{p_{\tau_{1},x}\cdots{p}_{\tau_{m},x}}{x^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\,dx,

(98)

which is somewhat harder to evaluate given that expanding around small
x
is not a viable option. Regardless, the point we make here is that classical correlations such as the one before of dephasing noise or the shallow pocket model can be treated on
RB
with a standard Markovian time-dependent approach.
