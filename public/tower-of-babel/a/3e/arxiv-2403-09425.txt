Fixing two points in primitive solvable groups
Francesca Lisi and Luca Sabatini

Abstract
. Consider a finite primitive solvable group. We observe that a result of Y. Yang implies that there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer has derived length at most 9 9 . We show that, if the group has odd cardinality, then there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer is abelian.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 20B15

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a finite set and let
G
be a primitive solvable group over
\Omega
.
By a beautiful theorem of Seress
[
13
]
, there always exist four points of
\Omega
such that their pointwise stabilizer is trivial.
When the group has odd cardinality, three points are sufficient to obtain the same result.
These results are sharp, and in the present paper we ask what can be said when only two points are fixed.

Our beginning is in the observation that a theorem of Moretó and Wolf
[
11
]
[Theorem E] implies the following:
there exist
x,y\in\Omega
such that the pointwise stabilizer
G_{x,y}
lies in
\mathop{\bf{F}}_{{}_{9}}(G_{x})
,
the ninth Fitting subgroup of
G_{x}
, and in
\mathop{\bf{F}}_{{}_{2}}(G_{x})
when
G
has odd cardinality.
In particular, the Fitting length of the stabilizer of two points can be bounded by
9
, or
2
, respectively.
These statements have the convenience of saying where
G_{x,y}
is located with respect to
G_{x}
,
but are far from the actual truth when we are interested in structural properties of the stabilizer of two points.

In his paper
[
15
]
, Y. Yang has refined Moretó and Wolf’s theorem in two directions:
in fact his results imply that the Fitting length of
G_{x,y}
can be bounded by
7
, and the derived length by
9
.
We will prove that, when
|G|
is odd, the stabilizer of two points can be chosen to be abelian.
We summarize these results as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a primitive solvable group.
Then there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer has derived length at most
9
.
If
|G|
is odd, then there exist two points whose pointwise stabilizer is abelian.

The constant
9
is probably not the best possible, and lowering this number seems to require a different method (see Remark
2.9
).
Theorem
1.1
will be consequence of results concerning orbits of linear groups.
The size of such orbits have been studied intensively in the last three decades
[
5
,
4
,
3
,
16
]
,
often in connection with an influential conjecture of Gluck
[
8
,
2
]
.
The recent paper
[
10
]
contains a detailed summary of the known results,
and provides a strong theorem when
\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(4)
is not involved as a section.

With the exception of
[
15
]
,
we found that structural properties of the centralizers are much less studied.
We have the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a solvable group and let
V
be a finite completely reducible faithful
G
-module (possibly of mixed characteristic).
Then

(i)

there exists
v\in V
such that the derived length of
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is at most
9
;

(ii)

if
|V||G|
is odd, there exists
v\in V
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is abelian.

We prove Theorem
1.2
(ii) in the next section.
Theorem
1.2
(i) follows from the stronger result in
[
15
]
, but we will give a direct (and easier) proof below.
Since there are irreducible linear groups of any Fitting length, the Fitting length of the stabilizer of one point is not bounded by any constant
(and so is not the derived length).
Similarly, the cardinality of the stabilizer of two points is not bounded,
because there exist primitive solvable subgroups of
\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)
of order roughly
24^{-1/3}n^{3.24}

[
12
,
14
]
,
while the index of such a stabilizer is bounded above by
n(n-1)
.

2.
Proofs

2.1.
Preliminaries

It is well known that a primitive solvable group over
\Omega
is of the type
V\rtimes G
,
where
|V|=|\Omega|=p^{n}
and
G
is the stabilizer of a point.
Moreover,
V
is a faithful irreducible
G
-module, and the stabilizers of two points correspond to the centralizers
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
for some
v\in V
.
In particular, Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the irreducible case of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
V
be a completely reducible faithful
G
-module.
Recall that
V
is quasiprimitive if
V_{N}
is homogeneous for all normal subgroups
N
of
G
.
If
q
is a prime power and
m\geq 1
, the semilinear group
\mathrm{\Gamma}(q^{m})
is isomorphic to the semidirect product

\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})\rtimes\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
where
\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})\cong(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})^{*}
and
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})\cong C_{m}
is cyclic.
As the next result shows, semilinear groups play an important role in the study of quasiprimitive groups.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let the solvable finite group
G
act faithfully and quasiprimitively on a vector space
V
.
If
\mathop{\bf{F}}(G)
is abelian, then
V=\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
for some
q,m\geq 1
,
G\leqslant\mathrm{\Gamma}(q^{m})
,
and
\mathop{\bf{F}}(G)\leqslant\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})
.

Proof.

This is a combination of Corollary 2.3 and Theorem 2.10 in
[
9
]
.
∎

If a group
S
permutes a set
\Omega
, then
S
also permutes the power set
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
of
\Omega
in a natural way.
The next is
[
11
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Gluck’s permutation lemma)
.

Let
S
be a transitive solvable permutation group on
\Omega
,
|\Omega|=m
.
Then

(i)

if
|S|
is odd, then
S
has a regular orbit on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
;

(ii)

if
S
is primitive and
m\geq 10
, then
S
has at least
8
regular orbits on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)

and at least one regular orbit of subsets
\Delta
of
\Omega
such that
\Delta
is not
S
-conjugate to its complement
\Omega\setminus\Delta
;

(iii)

if
S
is primitive, then there exist subsets
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k}
,
k\leq 4
,
such that
\Omega
is the disjoint union of the
\Delta_{i}
’s, and
\cap_{i}Stab_{S}(\Delta_{i})=1
.

We report the following observation, which makes our proof of Theorem
1.2
particularly simple.
When
\ell\geq 0
, let
G^{(\ell)}
denote
\ell
-th term of the derived series of
G=G^{(0)}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G
be a group,
and
J_{i}
and
K_{i}
subgroups such that
J_{i}^{(\ell)}\subseteq K_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Suppose that there exists a subgroup
A
such that
J_{i}\subseteq A
for each
i
,
and also
(\cap_{i}K_{i})\cap A=1
.
Then
\cap_{i}J_{i}
is solvable with derived length at most
\ell
.

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle(\cap_{i}J_{i})^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle\>=\>(\cap_{i}J_{i})^{(\ell)}\cap A

\displaystyle\>\subseteq\>(\cap_{i}J_{i}^{(\ell)})\cap A

\displaystyle\>\subseteq\>\left(\cap_{i}\>K_{i}\right)\cap A

\displaystyle\>=\>1\>,

from which the proof follows.
∎

2.2.
The odd case

We prove Theorem
1.2
(ii).
We follow a fairly standard route: we reduce to
V
irreducible,
then we handle
V
quasiprimitive, and the non-quasiprimitive case follows from induction and Gluck’s permutation lemma.

Let
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
, and suppose that there exists
v_{i}\in V_{i}
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{i})/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
is abelian, for each
i=1,2
.
Let
v=v_{1}+v_{2}\in V
, so that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{1})\cap\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{2})
.
We can apply Lemma
2.3
with
n=2
,
\ell=1
,
A=G
,
J_{i}=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{i})
and
K_{i}=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
for each
i
.
We conclude that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is abelian, as desired.

We give a neat result when
V
is quasiprimitive:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a solvable group and let
V
be a quasiprimitive faithful
G
-module.
If
|V||G|
is odd, then there exists
v\in V
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is cyclic.

Proof.

Since
|V||G|
is odd, a useful theorem of Espuelas
[
5
]
[Lemma 2.1] states that either
G
has a regular orbit on
V
, or
F=\mathop{\bf{F}}(G)
is cyclic.
In the first case,
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)=1
for some
v\in V
and the result follows.
In the latter case, we actually show that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is cyclic for all
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
.
We apply Lemma
2.1
, so that
G\leqslant\Gamma(q^{m})
,
F\leqslant\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})
.
In particular, it is easy to see that
F=\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})\cap G
, so
G/F\cong\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})G/\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})

can be seen as a subgroup of
\mathrm{\Gamma}(q^{m})/\mathrm{\Gamma}_{0}(q^{m})\cong C_{m}
.
Let
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
,
and observe that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{F}(v)=1
, because
F
is abelian and regular on
V
.
It follows that

{\bf C}_{G}(v)\cong\frac{\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)}{F\cap\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)}\cong\frac{F\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)}{F}\leqslant G/F,

and
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is cyclic.
∎

Even under the conditions of Lemma
2.4
, there is no
v\in V
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)\subseteq\mathop{\bf{F}}(G)
in general.
(For example, take
G\cong C_{31}\rtimes C_{3}
as a subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}_{3}(5)
,
where the centralizers range among the complements of the Fitting subgroup).
Looking for purely structural properties of the centralizers gives a much better result at this stage.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(ii).

Let
V
be non-quasiprimitive, and proceed by induction on
|V|+|G|
.
Choose
N\lhd G
maximal such that
V_{N}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}
is not homogeneous.
By
[
9
, Proposition 0.2]
,
S=G/N

faithfully and primitively permutes the
V_{i}
’s.
We also observe that
\cap_{i}\>\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(V_{i})=1
because
V
is faithful.

Lemma
2.2
(i)
provides a subset of
\left\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}\right\}

that lies in a regular orbit of
S
on the power set of
\left\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}\right\}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that

Stab_{S}(\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\})=1

for some
1\leq k<m
.
Set
H_{i}=\mathop{\bf{N}}_{G}(V_{i})
for each
i
(note that
H_{i}\neq G
by the irreducibility of
V
).
Also
N=\cap_{i}H_{i}
, and the
H_{i}/N
’s all are conjugates by
S
.
Observe that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(u)\subseteq H_{1}
for all
u\in V_{1}\setminus\{0\}
,
and that
V_{i}
is an irreducible
H_{i}
-module.
Applying the inductive hypothesis to the action of
H_{1}
on
V_{1}
,
we obtain
v_{1}\in V_{1}
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{1})/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{1})
is abelian.

The elements of
V_{i}
conjugate to
v_{1}
form an
H_{i}
-orbit on
V_{i}
.
For each
i>1
, pick
v_{i}\in V_{i}
conjugate to
v_{1}
.
Let
v=v_{1}+\ldots+v_{k}-v_{k+1}-\ldots-v_{m}
.
Since
|V||G|
is odd, no
v_{i}
is
G
-conjugate to
-v_{j}
(not even for
i=j
)
and so
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
must stabilize
\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
.
From above, we obtain
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)\subseteq N
.

To sum up, we have that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{i})/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
are all abelian sections,

\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v)=\cap_{i}\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})
because
N
does not permute the
V_{i}
’s, and also
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)\subseteq N
.
In particular,

{\bf C}_{N}(v_{i})^{(1)}\subseteq{\bf C}_{G}(v_{i})^{(1)}\subseteq{\bf C}_{G}(V_{i}).

We are in the position to apply Lemma
2.3
with
\ell=1
,
A=N
,
J_{i}=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})
and
K_{i}=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
for each

.
We obtain that
\cap_{i}\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v)=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is abelian.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
(ii).
∎

Trivially,

The hypothesis that

2.3.
The general case

In what follows,
G
is always a solvable group and
V
is a finite completely reducible faithful
G
-module.
Yang
[
15
, Theorem 3.4]
shows that there exists
v\in V
such that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is contained in a normal subgroup of
G
of derived length at most
9
.
As stated, this result is best possible (see
[
15
, Sec. 4]
).
On the other hand, it does not really say more than Theorem
1.2
(i), in the context of Theorem
1.1
.
We use a different approach that looks purely at the structure of
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
.
We say that an orbit
v^{G}\subseteq V
is
good
if the derived length of
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
is at most
9
.
For an induction argument, we need more than one good orbit,
so we actually prove Theorem
2.6
below.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
G
be a solvable group and let
V
be a finite completely reducible faithful
G
-module.
There exist at least two good
G
-orbits.
If there are less than five good
G
-orbits, then the derived length of
G
is at most
6
.

We remark that in the last case every
G
-orbit is a good orbit, so there are less than five
G
-orbits in total.
It is sufficient to prove Theorem
2.6
when
V
is irreducible, but a little more care is necessary.
If
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
, we apply Lemma
2.3
as in the previous section, with
\ell=9
and
A=G
.
If there are
a\geq 2
good orbits of
G/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{1})
on
V_{1}
, and
b\geq 2
good orbits of
G/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{2})
on
V_{2}
,
we obtain at least
ab
good orbits of
G
on
V
.
Now
ab\geq 5
, except when
(a,b)=(2,2)
.
In this case, by induction, the derived length of
G/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
is at most
6
,
and it is easy to see that
G
is a subgroup of
G/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{1})\times G/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{2})
.
So
G
has derived length at most
6
, every
G
-orbit on
V
is a good orbit, and it is clear that we can assume that
V
is irreducible.

Let
r
be the number of
G
-orbits on
V

(so that
r\geq 2
when
V
is nontrivial).
Let
dl(G)
denote the derived length of
G
.
We report two Yang’s lemmas:

Lemma 2.7

(Theorem 3.1 in
[
15
]
)
.

If
V
is quasiprimitive, and
dl(G)\geq 10
, then there exist at least five regular
G
-orbits.

Lemma 2.8

(Lemma 2.5 in
[
15
]
)
.

If
r\leq 2
, then
dl(G)\leq 4
.
If
r\leq 4
, then
dl(G)\leq 6
.

It can be checked with the GAP System
[
7
]
that there is a subgroup
G
of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}_{6}(2)
satisfying

and
dl(G)=6
.
We note that the proofs of these lemmas appear a bit tedious only if one cares about constants:
lossy versions follow immediately from
[
9
, Corollary 1.10]
and
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
, respectively.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

We work by induction on
|V|+|G|
.
If
dl(G)\leq 9
, then every
G
-orbit is a good orbit, and so we are done by Lemma
2.8
.
Let
dl(G)\geq 10
.
By Lemma
2.7
, we can also suppose that
V
is not quasiprimitive.
We will actually show that in the remaining cases there are at least five good
G
-orbits.

There exists
N\lhd G
such that
V_{N}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}
for
m\geq 2
homogeneous components
V_{i}
’s of
V_{N}
;
by choosing
N
maximal such, then
S=G/N
primitively permutes the
V_{i}
’s.
If
H=\mathop{\bf{N}}_{G}(V_{1})/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{1})
,
then

acts faithfully and irreducibly on

and

is isomorphic to a subgroup of

by the imprimitive embedding theorem.
(Notice that

is faithful by definition, unlike

in the previous section).

For a moment, suppose that we have an element
v=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{m}\in V
such that
dl(\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{i})/\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i}))\leq 9
for each
i
,

\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v)=\cap_{i}\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})
, and also that
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)\subseteq N
.
Then
\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})^{(9)}\subseteq\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v_{i})^{(9)}\subseteq\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(V_{i})
.
So we are in the position to apply Lemma
2.3
with

,

,
J_{i}=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})
and

for each

.
We obtain that
\cap_{i}\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v_{i})=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{N}(v)=\mathop{\bf{C}}_{G}(v)
has derived length at most

, i.e.

lies in a good

-orbit.

It rests to find five good elements which are not

-conjugates. This is related to finding a sufficient number of good

-orbits on

.
If
x_{1},y_{1}\in V_{1}
are in distinct

-orbits and
x_{i},y_{i}\in V_{i}
are their conjugates,
then the centralizer in

of

,
if

is not

In the last part, we do not write all the details and we refer the reader to

because the argument is the same.
If

, then by induction we have
s\geq 2
good

-orbits on

. We can effectively use them by Lemma

(ii),
and so we get at least

good

-orbits on

.
Now

, except when

.
Even in this case, we can construct at least five good

Finally, suppose

. We have

(2.1)

10\leq dl(G)\leq dl(H)+dl(S).

Let
s\geq 2
be the number of good

-orbits on

.
Using Lemma

(iii) to construct good

, while it is easy to see that

;

•

, while it is easy to see that

In both cases, we obtain a contradiction to (

(i).

Remark 2.9
.

As we have said in the Introduction, we believe that the constant
9
in Theorem
1.1
is not the best possible,
and we are not aware of examples where the derived length of
G_{x,y}
always exceeds
4
.
The bottleneck in Moretó and Wolf’s method is because Lemma
2.2
is less effective in the even case,
which forces to formulate a statement as Theorem
2.6
.
After this, we observe that Lemma
2.7
does not really play a role in pinning down the constant,
Lemma
2.8
is sharp, and we come very close to equality in (
2.1
).

More recently, if
G
is a primitive group with solvable stabilizer,
Burness
[
1
]
has proved that there always exist five points such that their pointwise stabilizer is trivial.
This result and Theorem
1.1
suggest the following question:

Question
.

Let
G\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(\Omega)
be a primitive group with a solvable stabilizer.
Do there exist
x,y\in\Omega
such that the derived length of
G_{x,y}
is bounded by an absolute constant?
