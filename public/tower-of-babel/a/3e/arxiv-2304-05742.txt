Motivation and historical remarks

Thanks to the global Torelli theorem
[
31

and surjectivity of the period map
[
20
]

[
8
]
, Akyol and Degtyarev
[
1
]
completed the problem of equisingular deformation classification of simple plane sextics. Simple quartic surfaces which play the same role in the realm of spatial surfaces as sextics do for curves, are a relatively new subject, promising interesting discoveries. We confine ourselves to
simple
quartics only,
i.e.
, those with
\mathbf{A}
–
\mathbf{D}
–
\mathbf{E}
type singularities
(quartic surfaces with a non-simple singular point,
i.e.
, when the quartic is not a K3-surface, are quite different, see Degtyarev
[

,
[

]
). The work was originated in Urabe
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
and extended by Yang
[

]
who gave a complete list of sets of singularities realized by simple quartics. Then, after a period of oblivion, it was resumed by Güneş Aktaş
[

where she obtained the classification of the so-called

[

.
In the meanwhile, Shimada

1.2.
Principal results

The present paper originates from the paper
[
16
]
where I have made a contribution to the systematic study of simple spatial quartics. Our principal result is extending the classification given in
[
16
]
for only nonspecial simple quartics to the whole space of simple quartics and, thus, completing the equisingular deformation classification of simple quartic surfaces. This result closes a long standing project initiated by Persson
[
30
]
and Urabe
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

A set of simple singularities of a simple quartic can be identified with a root lattice (see
[
12
]
and §
3.3
) and recall that the total Milnor number
\mu(X)
of a simple quartic
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is given by the rank of the corresponding root lattice. One has
\mu(X)\leq 19
(see
[
39
]
,
cf.
,
[
30
]
); if
\mu(X)=19
, the quartic
X
is called
maximizing
. (Recall that maximizing quartics are projectively rigid.)

Our classification is based on the arithmetical reduction found in Degtyarev and Itenberg
[
10
]
reducing the equisingular deformation classification of simple quartic surfaces to a purely lattice-theoretical question. The resulting arithmetical problem is solved by first using Nikulin’s theory of discriminant forms
[
26
]
. Then, the computation is done separately for the maximizing (
\mu(X)=19
) and non-maximizing (
\mu(X)\leq 18
) case; for the former, we use Gauss’s theory of binary quadratic form
[
15
]
; for the latter, apply Miranda-Morrison’s results
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, reducing the analysis of the orthogonal groups of indefinite lattices to a relatively simple computation in finite abelian groups.

Our classification is obtained by implementing all the algorithms given in §
4
in GAP
[
14
]
as the number of classes is huge (about 12,000). The original code used in
[
16
]
where we settled the case of nonspecial quartics was based partially on manually precomputed data specific to a particular degree.
Therefore, the code has been extended to implement a complete version of the Miranda–Morrison
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
and a genuinely repetition free enumeration of realizable configurations (see §
3.3
). The principal novelty, compared to the nonspecial case where
\mathcal{K}=0
, is that the imprimitivity, where
\mathcal{K}\neq 0
, is also taken into account; furthermore, we have developed a more advanced method of computing the Miranda–-Morrison homomorphism based on lifting reflections to a p-adic lattice (see §
2.6.1
) and settled the missing types.

Denote by
\mathcal{X}
the space of all spatial quartics; it is subdivided into equisingular strata
\mathcal{X}(S)
where
S
is a set of simple singularities. Each stratum
\mathcal{X}(S)
is further subdivided into its connected components corresponding to equisingular deformation families. The equisingular strata
\mathcal{X}(S)
splits also into families
\mathcal{X}_{*}(S)
where the subscript
*
is the sequence of invariant factors of a certain finite group
\mathcal{K}
(see §
4.1
)
A complete description of the strata
\mathcal{X}_{1}(S)
constituted by the so-called
nonspecial
quartics,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{K}=0
, is given by Güneş Aktaş
[
16
]
.
We complete this project in this paper by giving a complete description of the whole equisingular strata
\mathcal{X}(S)
consisting also the quartics with
\mathcal{K}\neq 0
.

A standard
real structure
(
i.e.
, an antiholomorphic involution)
\operatorname{conj}\colon\mathbb{P}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
induces a real structure
\operatorname{c}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
which takes a quartic to its conjugate. This real structure

depends on the choice of
\operatorname{conj}

the induced action on the set of the connected components of the equisingular strata is well defined. A connected component

is called

.
Clearly, each stratum
\mathcal{X}(S)
consists of real and pairs of complex conjugate components; this classification of components is given in
[
1
]
for sextics and in
[
16
]
for nonspecial quartics.

By a

Contents of the paper

In §

, after recalling basic notions and facts of Nikulin’s

In this introductory section we recall briefly a few elementary facts concerning integral lattices, their discriminant forms and extensions. The principal reference is
[

]
.

2.1.
Finite quadratic forms (see
[
22
,
26
]
)

A
finite quadratic form
is a finite abelian group
\mathcal{L}
equipped with a map
q\colon\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z}
quadratic in the sense that

q(x+y)=q(x)+q(y)-2b(x,y),\quad q(nx)=n^{2}q(x),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{L},\;n\in\mathbb{Z},

where
b\colon\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
is a symmetric bilinear form (which is determined by
q
) and
2\colon\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z}
is the natural isomorphism. We abriviate
x^{2}:=q(x)
and
x\cdot y:=b(x,y)
.

Each finite quadratic form can be decomposed into the orthogonal direct sum
\mathcal{L}=\bigoplus_{p}\mathcal{L}_{p}
of its
p
-primary components
\mathcal{L}_{p}:=\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where the summation runs over all primes
p
. The
length

\ell(\mathcal{L})
is the minimal number of generators of
\mathcal{L}
; we put
\ell_{p}(\mathcal{L}):=\ell(\mathcal{L}_{p})
.
A finite quadratic form
\mathcal{L}
is called
even
if
x^{2}=0\bmod\mathbb{Z}
for each element
x\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
of order
2
; it is called
odd
otherwise.

A finite quadratic form is
nondegenerate
if the homomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(\mathcal{L},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}),\quad x\mapsto(y\mapsto x\cdot y)

is an isomorphism. We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{L})
the group of automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}
preserving the form
q
. A subgroup
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{L}
is called
isotropic
if the restriction of the quadratic form
q
on
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{K}
is identically zero. If this is case
\mathcal{K}^{\bot}/\mathcal{K}
also inherits from
\mathcal{L}
a nondegenerate quadratic form.

For a fraction
\frac{m}{n}\in\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z}
, with
(m,n)=1
such that
mn=0\bmod 2
, we denote by
\langle\frac{m}{n}\rangle
the nondegenerate finite quadratic form on
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
sending the generator to
\frac{m}{n}
,
i.e.
,
\alpha^{2}=\frac{m}{n}\bmod 2\mathbb{Z}
for a generator
\alpha
.
For an integer
k\geqslant 1
, let
\mathcal{U}_{n}
and
\mathcal{V}_{n}
be the length
2
forms on
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, defined by the matrices

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{n}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1/n\\
1/n&0\end{array}\right],\quad\mathcal{V}_{n}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}2/n&1/n\\
1/n&2/n\end{array}\right],\quad\mbox{where $n=2^{k}$}.

Nikulin
[
26
]
proved that, a nondegenerate finite quadratic form splits into an orthogonal direct sum of cyclic forms
\langle\frac{m}{n}\rangle
(defined on the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
) and length 2 blocks
\mathcal{U}_{n}
,
\mathcal{V}_{n}
. Unless the
2
-torsion consists of the summands of length 2, we describe nondegenerate finite quadratic forms by expressions of the form
\langle q_{1}\rangle\ldots\langle q_{r}\rangle
, where
q_{i}=\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}
as above; the group is generated by pairwise orthogonal elements
\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{n}
(numbered in the order of appearance) so that
\alpha_{i}^{2}=\frac{m_{i}}{n_{i}}\bmod 2\mathbb{Z}
and order of
\alpha_{i}
is
n_{i}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a nondegenerate quadratic form. Given a prime
p
, the determinant of the Gram matrix (in any minimal basis) of
\mathcal{L}_{p}
has the form
u/|{\mathcal{L}_{p}}|
for some unit
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}
, and this unit is well defined modulo
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{2}
unless
p=2
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is odd; in the latter case,
u
is well defined modulo the subgroup generated by
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times})^{2}
and
5
. We define
\det_{p}\mathcal{L}=u/|{\mathcal{L}_{p}}|
where
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}/(\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times})^{2}
or
{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times}/(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\times})^{2}\times\{1,5\}}
is as above (see
[
24
]
).

Remark 2.2
.

According to Nikulin
[
26
]
, given a prime
p
and a quadratic form
\mathcal{L}
on a
p
group, there is a
p
-adic lattice
L
such that
\operatorname{rk}L=\ell_{p}(\mathcal{L})
and
\operatorname{disc}L=\mathcal{L}_{p}
. Unless
p=2
and
\mathcal{L}
is odd, such a lattice
L
is determined by
\mathcal{L}
uniquely up to isomorphism. In the exceptional case
p=2
and
\mathcal{L}
is odd, there are two such lattices that differ by determinants. One has
\det L=\det_{p}\mathcal{L}|{\mathcal{L}_{p}}|^{2}=u|{\mathcal{L}_{p}}|
for some unit
u
as in the Definition
2.1
(Nikulin uses this equality as a definition of
\det_{p}\mathcal{L}
).

2.2.
Integral lattices and discriminant forms

An
(integral) lattice
is a finitely generated free abelian group
L
equipped with a symmetric bilinear form
b\colon L\otimes L\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. Whenever the form is fixed, we use the abbreviation
x^{2}:=b(x,x)
and
x\cdot y:=b(x,y)
. A lattice
L
is called
even
if
x^{2}=0\mod 2
for all
x\in L
; it is called
odd
otherwise. The
determinant

\det L\in\mathbb{Z}
is the determinant of the Gram matrix of
b
in any integral basis of
L
.
A lattice
L
is called
unimodular
if
\det L=\pm 1
; it is called
nondegenerate
if
\det L\neq 0
, or equivalently, the
kernel

\displaystyle\operatorname{ker}L=L^{\bot}:=\{x\in L\mid\text{ $x\cdot y=0$ for all $y\in L$}\}

is trivial.

Given a lattice
L
, the bilinear form on
L
can be extended by linearity to a
\mathbb{Q}
-valued bilinear form on
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. The inertia indexes
\sigma_{\pm}
and the signature
\sigma:=(\sigma_{-},\sigma_{+})
of
L
are defined as those of
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. A nondegenerate lattice is called
hyperbolic
if
\sigma_{+}L=1
.
If
L
is nondegenerate, then we have canonical inclusion

\displaystyle L\subset L^{\vee}:=\operatorname{Hom}(L,\mathbb{Z})=\{x\in L\otimes\mathbb{Q}\mid\text{$x\cdot y\in\mathbb{Z}$ for all $y\in L$}\}

The finite quotient group
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L:=L^{\vee}/L
of order
\mathopen{|}{\det L}\mathclose{|}
is called the
discriminant group
of
L
. In particular,
L
is unimodular if and only if
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L=0
,
i.e.
,
L=L^{\vee}

The discriminant group inherits from
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle b\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L\otimes\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},\quad(x\bmod L)\otimes(y\bmod L)\mapsto(x\cdot y)\bmod\mathbb{Z},

and if
L
is even, its quadratic extension

\displaystyle q\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z},\quad(x\bmod L)\mapsto x^{2}\bmod 2\mathbb{Z},

called, respectively, the
discriminant bilinear form
and
discriminant quadratic form
. Note that the discriminant group of an even lattice is a finite quadratic form. We use the notation
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits_{p}L
for the
p
-primary part of
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L
.
When speaking about the discriminant groups and their (anti-)isomorphisms, these forms are always taken into account.

Lattices are naturally grouped into
genera
. Omitting the precise definition we follow Nikulin
[
26
]
who states that two nondegenerate even lattices
L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime}
are in the same
genus
if and only if one has
\operatorname{rk}L^{\prime}=\operatorname{rk}L^{\prime\prime}
,
\sigma L^{\prime}=\sigma L^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L^{\prime}\cong\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L^{\prime\prime}
. Each genus consists of finitely many isomorphism classes.

An isometry
\psi\colon L\rightarrow L^{\prime}
between two lattices is a group homomorphism respecting the bilinear forms; obviously one always has
\operatorname{Ker}\psi\subset\operatorname{Ker}L
. The group of bijective autoisometries of a nondegenerate lattice
L
is denoted by
O(L)
. The action of
O(L)
extends to
L\otimes\mathbb{Q}
by linearity, and the latter action descents to
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L
. Therefore, there is a natural homomorphism
O(L)\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L)
where
\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{disc}L)
denotes the group of automorphisms of
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L
preserving the discriminant form
q
on
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L
. In general this map is neither one-to-one nor onto; however,
without any confusion we freely apply autoisometries
g\in O(L)
to objects in
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L
. Obviously one has
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L)=\prod_{p}\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits_{p}L)
where the product runs over all primes. The restriction of
d
to
p
-primary components are denoted by
d_{p}\colon O(L)\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits_{p}L)
.

A
4
-
polarized lattice
is a nondegenerate hyperbolic lattice
L
equipped with a distinguished vector
h\in L
such that
h^{2}=4
; this vector is usually assumed but often omitted from the notation. The group of polarized autoisometries is denoted by
O_{h}(L)
,
i.e.
, the group of automorphisms of
L
preserving
h
.

The orthogonal projection establishes a liner isomorphism between any two maximal positive definite subspaces in
L\otimes\mathbb{R}
, thus providing a way for comparing orientations. A coherent choice of orientations of all maximal positive definite subspaces is called a
positive sign structure
on
L
. We denote by
O^{+}(L)\subset O(L)
the subgroup consisting of the autoisometries preserving a positive sign structure. Either one has
O^{+}(L)=O(L)
or
O(L)^{+}
is a subgroup of
O(L)
of index
2
. In the latter case, each element of
O(L)\smallsetminus O^{+}(L)
is called a
skew-autoisometry
of L,
i.e.
, the autoisometries of
L
that reverse the positive sign structure.

Of special importance are so called reflections of
L
: given a nonzero vector
a\in L
, the
reflection
defined by
a
is the automorphism

(2.3)

t_{a}\colon L\rightarrow L,\quad x\mapsto\frac{2(a\cdot x)}{a^{2}}a.

It is well defined if and only if
(2a/a^{2})\in L^{\vee}
. Note that
t_{a}
is an involution. Each image
d_{p}(t_{a})
is also reflection and if
a^{2}=\pm 1
or
a^{2}=\pm 2
, then the induced automorphism
d(t_{a})
of the discriminant group is the identity and
t_{a}
extends to any lattice containing
L
.

2.3.
Root Lattices

A
root
in an even lattice
L
is a vector
r\in L
of square
(-2)
. A
root lattice
is an even negative definite lattice generated by its roots. Each root lattice has a unique decomposition into orthogonal direct sum of irreducible root lattices, the latter being those of types
\textbf{A}_{n}
,
n\geq 1
,
\textbf{D}_{n}
,
n\geq 4
, or
\textbf{E}_{n}
,
n=6,7,8
.

Given a root lattice
S
, the vertices of the Dynkin diagram
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{S}
can be identified with the elements of a basis for
S
constituting a single Weyl chamber. Thus, one has an obvious homomorphism
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)\rightarrow O(S)

where
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)
is the group of symmetries of the Dynkin diagram
\Gamma
. By the classification of the connected Dynkin graphs, for irreducible root lattices, the groups
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)
are given as follows:

(1)

if
S
is
\mathbf{A}_{1}
,
\mathbf{E}_{7}
or
\mathbf{E}_{8}
, then
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)=1

(2)

if
S
is
\mathbf{D}_{4}
, then
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{S}_{3}

(3)

for all other types,
\operatorname{Sym}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}

If S is
\mathbf{A}_{p}
,
p\geq 2
,
\mathbf{D}_{2k+1}
or
\mathbf{E}_{8}
, then the only nontrivial symmetry of
\Gamma
induces
-\operatorname{id}
on
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
. If
S
is
\mathbf{E}_{8}
then
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S=0
and if
S
is
\mathbf{A}_{1}
,
\mathbf{A}_{7}
of
\mathbf{D}_{2k}
, the groups
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
modules and
-\operatorname{id}=\operatorname{id}
on
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S)
.

2.4.
Lattice extensions

From now on unless specified otherwise all lattices considered are even and nondegenerate. An extension of an even lattice
S
is an even lattice
L
containing
S
. Two extensions
L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime}\supset S
are called
isomorphic
if there is a bijective isometry
L^{\prime}\rightarrow L^{\prime\prime}
preserving
S
, in particular, if the isomorphism
L^{\prime}\rightarrow L^{\prime\prime}
is identical on
S
, the extensions
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}
are called
strictly isomorphic
. More generally, one can also fix a subgroup
G\in O(S)
and speak about
G
-
isomorphisms
of the extension,
i.e.
, bijective isometries whose restriction to
S
is in
G
.

The two extreme cases are
finite index extensions
,
i.e.
,
L
contains
S
as a subgroup of finite index and
primitive extensions
,
i.e.
,
L/S
is torsion free. The general case
L\supset S
splits into the finite index extension
\tilde{S}\supset S
and primitive extension
L\supset\tilde{S}
, where

\tilde{S}:=\{x\in L\mid nx\in S\;\mbox{for some $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}\}

is the
primitive hull
of
S
in
L
.

Any extension
L\supset S
of finite index admits a unique embedding
L\subset S\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. Since
S
is nondegenerate, we have
L\subset S^{\vee}
, and hence the natural inclusions

\displaystyle S\subset L\subset L^{\vee}\subset S^{\vee}.

The subgroup
\mathcal{K}:=L/S\subset S^{\vee}/S=\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
is called the
kernel
of the finite index extension
L\supset S
. This subgroup
\mathcal{K}
is
isotropic
(since
L
is an even integral lattice),
i.e.
, the restriction to
\mathcal{K}
of the quadratic form
q\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}/2\mathbb{Z}
is identically zero. Conversely, if
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
is isotropic, the lattice

L:=\{x\in S\otimes\mathbb{Q}\mid x\bmod S\in\mathcal{K}\}

is an extension of
S
and we say that
L
is the extension of
S
by
\mathcal{K}
. Thus, we have the follwing statement.

Theorem 2.4

(Nikulin
[
26
]
)
.

Let
S
be a nondegenerate even lattice, and fix a subgroup
G\subset O(S)
. The map

\displaystyle L\mapsto\mathcal{K}:=L/S\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S

establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the set of

G
-isomorphism classes of finite index extensions
L\supset S

and the set of
G
-orbits of isotropic subgroups

\mathcal{K}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
. Under this correspondence one
has
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits L=\mathcal{K}^{\bot}/\mathcal{K}
. Furthermore

(1)

an autoisometry
g\in O(S)
extends to
L
if and only if
g(\mathcal{K})=\mathcal{K}
;

(2)

two extensions
L^{\prime}
,
L^{\prime\prime}
are isomorphic if and only if their kernels
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime\prime}
are in the same
O(S)
-orbit,
i.e
, there is
g\in O(S)
such that
g(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})=g(\mathcal{K}^{\prime\prime})
.

Another extreme case is that of a primitive extension
L\supset S
, (
i.e.
, such that the group
L/S
is torsion free). Such extensions are studied by fixing (the isomorphism class of) the orthogonal complement
T:=S^{\bot}\in L
. Then
L
is a finite index extension of
S\oplus T
, in which
T
is also primitive, and by Theorem
2.4
, it is described by an isotropic subgroup

\mathcal{K}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits(S\oplus T)=\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\oplus\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T.

Since
S
and
T
are both primitive in
L
, the kernel
\mathcal{K}
does not intersect with any of
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
. It follows that the projection maps

\operatorname{proj}_{S}\colon\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\mbox{ and }\operatorname{proj}_{T}\colon\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T

are both monomorphisms. Since
\mathcal{K}
is isotropic, it is the graph of a bijective anti-isometry
\psi\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}
, where
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}=\operatorname{proj}_{S}(\mathcal{K})
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}=\operatorname{proj}_{T}(\mathcal{K})
. Conversely, given a bijective anti-isometry
\psi\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}
where
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
, the graph of
\psi
is an isotropic subgroup
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\oplus\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
and the corresponding finite index extension
L\supset S\oplus T
is a primitive extension whose kernel is
\mathcal{K}
. Thus, we have the following statement (
cf.
Nikulin
[
26
]
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Given two nondegenerate even lattices
S
,
T
and a subgroup
G\subset O(S)\times O(T)
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of
G
-isomorphism classes of finite index extensions
L\supset S\oplus T
in which both
S
and
T
are primitive and that of
G
-conjugacy classes of bijective anti-isometries

(2.6)

\psi\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}

where
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}\subset\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
. Furthermore, a pair of isometries
f\in O(S)
and
g\in O(T)
extends to
L
if and only if
f|_{\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}}=\psi^{-1}g|_{\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}}\psi
in
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime})
.

If
L
above is unimodular,
\operatorname{disc}L=0
, we have

\mathopen{|}\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\mathclose{|}\mathopen{|}\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}\mathclose{|}\mathopen{|}\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}\mathclose{|}
. Hence,
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\prime}=\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T^{\prime}=\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
and
\psi
in (
2.6
) is an anti-isomorphism
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\rightarrow\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
. Since also
\sigma_{\pm}T=\sigma_{\pm}L-\sigma_{\pm}S
, it follows that the genus
g(T)
is determined by the genera
g(S)
and
g(L)
; we will denote this common genus by
g(S^{\bot}_{L})
(We emphasize that
g(S^{\bot}_{L})
merely encodes a “local data” composed formally from
g(S)
and

; apriori, it may even be empty,
cf.
Theorem

below). If

is also indefinite, it is unique in its genus (see,

, Siegel

,
36
,
37
]
). Then we have the following corollary of the above lemma.

Corollary 2.7
.

Given a subgroup
G\subset O(S)
and unimodular even indefinite lattice
L
, a primitive isometry
S\hookrightarrow L
give rise to a bijective isometry
\psi\colon\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S\rightarrow-\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits S^{\bot}
and
G
-isomorphism classes of a primitive isometries

are in canonical bijection with the following sets of data:

(1)

an even lattice (isomorphism class)

, and

(2)

a bi-coset in
G\backslash\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T)/O(T)
.

In particular, the extension
L\supset S
exists if and only if the genus

is nonempty.

From now on, we fix the notation
\mathbf{L}:=3\textbf{U}\oplus 2\textbf{E}_{8}
where

stands for the

, the lattice generated by a pair of vectors

(refered as the

of

and

. Note that

and signature

. We are concerned about this lattice as it is the intersection index form of a

-surface

,
i.e.
,

.
We are interested in the primitive embeddings to

. The following theorem giving a criterion for

is a combination of the above observation and Nikulin’s existence theorem

(Nikulin

]
)

Given a nondegenerate even lattice

, where

2.5.
Miranda-Morrison Theory

Following the classical approach, Nikulin
[
26
]
gives the sufficient conditions to obtain the uniqueness of an even indefinite lattice
T
of rank at least
3
in its genus and surjectivity of the map
d\colon O(T)\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T)
. However, those conditions do not capture all the cases that we want to cover, hence we apply the stronger (non)uniqueness criteria due to Miranda- Morrison
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
extending Nikulin’s work. Throughout this section, we assume that
T
is an indefinite nondegenerate even lattice of rank
\operatorname{rk}T\geq 3
.

With the ultimate goal of calculating the groups
E(T)
and
E^{+}(T)
see (
2.9
) and (
2.22
), respectively, and the images of some certain maps in these groups, it is convenient to introduce following groups

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p}:

\displaystyle=\{\pm 1\}\times\mathbb{Q}^{\times}_{p}/(\mathbb{Q}^{\times}_{p})^{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}:

\displaystyle=\{\pm 1\}\times\{\pm 1\}\subset\Gamma_{\mathbb{Q}}:=\{\pm 1\}\times\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2},

and following subgroups related to
\Gamma_{p}
:

•

\Gamma_{p,0}:=\{(1,1),(1,u_{p}),(-1,1),(-1,u_{p})\}\subset\Gamma_{p}
; here,
p
is odd and
u_{p}
is the only nontrivial element of
\mathbb{Z}^{\times}_{p}/(\mathbb{Z}^{\times}_{p})^{2}
,

•

\Gamma_{2,0}:=\{(1,1),(1,3),(1,5),(1,7),(-1,1),(-1,3),(-1,5),(-1,7)\}\subset\Gamma_{2}
,

•

\Gamma_{2,2}:=\{(1,1),(1,5)\}\subset\Gamma_{2}^{++}
,

•

\Gamma_{0}^{--}:=\{(1,1),(-1,-1)\}\subset\Gamma_{0}
.

We also define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{A},0}:=\prod_{p}\Gamma_{p,0}\subset\Gamma_{\mathbb{A}}:=\Gamma_{\mathbb{A},0}\cdot\sum_{p}\Gamma_{p}\subset\Gamma:=\prod_{p}\Gamma_{p}

where we use
\cdot
to denote the sum of subgroups, while we reserve the notation
\sum
and
\prod
to distinguish between direct sums and products.
Note that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbb{A}}=\{(d_{p},s_{p})\in\Gamma\ |(d_{p},s_{p})\in\Gamma_{p,0}\text{ for almost all $p$}\}

Defined in the notation of
[
24
]
, we will use certain subgroups

\Sigma_{p}^{\sharp}(T):=\Sigma^{\sharp}(T\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Sigma_{p}(T):=\Sigma(T\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p})

which are both a priori subgroups of
\Gamma_{p}
(we refer the reader to
[
24
]
, see chapter 7, section 4, for the precise definitions). In fact
\Sigma_{p}^{\sharp}\subset\Gamma_{p,0}
always and
\Sigma_{p}\subset\Gamma_{p,0}
for almost all p. The subgroups
\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T)
are computed explicitly in
[
24
]
(see Theorems 12.1, 12.2, 12.3 and 12.4 in chapter 7). One has

\Sigma^{\sharp}(T):=\prod_{p}\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T)\subset\Gamma_{\mathbb{A},0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Sigma(T):=\prod_{p}\Sigma_{p}(T)\subset\Gamma_{\mathbb{A}}.

We introduce the
Miranda-Morrison group

E(T)
as it is defined in
[
24
]
(see chapter
8
, sections
5
,
6
and
7
):

(2.9)

\displaystyle E(T):=\Gamma_{\mathbb{A},0}/\prod_{p}\Sigma_{p}^{\sharp}(T)\cdot\Gamma_{0}.

Crucial is the fact that
\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T)=\Gamma_{p,0}
unless
p\mathrel{|}\operatorname{det}(T)
; thus, (
2.9
) reduces to finitely many primes
p
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle E(T)=\prod_{p|\operatorname{det}(T)}\Gamma_{p,0}/\prod_{p|\operatorname{det}(T)}\Sigma_{p}^{\sharp}(T)\cdot\Gamma_{0}.

Hence, this group is finite. We call a prime
p

irregular
with respect to

if
p\mid\operatorname{det}(T)
.

Consider the natural map
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{\times}_{p}/(\mathbb{Q}^{\times}_{p})^{2}
inducing the projections

\displaystyle\varphi_{p}:\Gamma_{0}\rightarrow\Gamma_{p,0},

we define the invariants

\displaystyle e_{p}(T):=[\Gamma_{p,0}:\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T)]\text{ and }\tilde{\Sigma}_{p}(T)=\Sigma^{\sharp}_{0}(T\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{p}):=\varphi^{-1}_{p}(\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T))\subset\Gamma_{0},

used in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.11

(Miranda–Morrison
[
24
]
)
.

Let
T
be a non-degenerate indefinite even lattice with
\operatorname{rk}(T)\geq 3
. Then there is an exact sequence

(2.12)

\displaystyle O(T)\xrightarrow{d}\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{discr}T)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{e}}E(T)\rightarrow g(T)\rightarrow 1,

where
g(T)
is the genus group of
T
. One has

(2.13)

\displaystyle|E(T)|=\frac{e(T)}{[\Gamma_{0}:\tilde{\Sigma}(T)]}

where

\displaystyle e(T):=\prod_{p|\operatorname{det}(T)}e_{p}(N),\quad\tilde{\Sigma}(T):=\bigcap_{p|\operatorname{det}(T)}\tilde{\Sigma}_{p}(T),

Algorithims computing

and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{p}(N)
explicitly are given in
[
23
]
. Computations are in terms of
\operatorname{rk}T
,
\operatorname{det}T
and
\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T
only, it follows that the genus group

determines

and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{p}(T)
and also
\operatorname{Coker}(d\colon O(T)\rightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(\mathop{\operator@font discr}\nolimits T))
. Computing the Miranda-Morison group

is even easier than computing its constituents,
\operatorname{Coker}(d)
and the genus group
g(T)
. Since one can read the the subgroups
\tilde{\Sigma}_{p}(T)
and
\Sigma^{\sharp}_{p}(T)
from the tables given in
[
22
]
(see chapter 7 section 12), the computation is immediate.

As an unimodular even indefinite lattice is unique in its genus, one can obtain the next statement by combining the Corollary
2.7

Theorem 2.14

(Miranda–Morrison

2.6.
Reflections and their lifts

3.
K3
-surfaces

In this section we give a brief introduction to the theory of
K3
-surfaces; for further details, we refer the interested reader to
[
18
]
. Then we discuss simple quartics as
K3
-surfaces.

3.1.
K3-surfaces

A
K3
-surface over
{\mathbb{C}}
is a simply connected, compact, complex surface of dimension
2
whose canonical bundle is trivial. All
K3
-surfaces are Kähler and since any smooth complete surface is projective,
K3
-surfaces are all projective.

We identify a
K3
-surface
X
with its polarized Néron Severi lattice
NS(X)\ni h
. As is well known, the Néron Severi lattice
NS(X)
of any projective
K3
-surface
X
is hyperbolic,
i.e.
,
\sigma_{+}NS(X)=1
and admits a primitive embedding

NS(X)\subset H_{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbf{L}=3\textbf{U}\oplus 2\textbf{E}_{8};

where
\mathbf{L}
is the only (up to isomorphism) even unimodular lattice with
\sigma_{+}\mathbf{L}=3
and
\sigma_{-}\mathbf{L}=19
, see §
2.4
, hence
\operatorname{rk}NS(X)\leqslant 20
.

3.2.
Quartics as
K3
-surfaces

A
quartic
is a surface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree four. A quartic is
simple
if all its singular points are simple, i.e., those of type
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{D},\mathbf{E}
, see
[
12
]
.
Given a simple quartic
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
, its minimal resolution of singularities
\tilde{X}
is a
K3
-surface; hence,
H_{2}(\tilde{X})\cong 2\mathbf{E}_{8}\oplus 3\mathbf{U}
. We fix the notation
\mathbf{L}_{X}:=H_{2}(\tilde{X})
.

For each simple singular point
p
of
X
, the components of the exceptional divisor over
p
span a root lattice in
\mathbf{L}_{X}
. The orthogonal sum of these sublattices, denoted by
S_{X}
, is identified with the set of singularities of
X
. Recall that the types of individual singular points are uniquely recovered from
S_{X}
, see
2.3
.

In what follows we identify homology and cohomology of
\tilde{X}

via
Poicaré duality and introduce the following vectors and sublattices:

•

S_{X}\subset\mathbf{L}_{X}
: the sublattice generated the set of classes of exceptional divisors appearing in the blow-up map
\tilde{X}\rightarrow X
;

•

h_{X}\in\mathbf{L}_{X}
: the pull-back of the hyperplane section class in
H_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{2})
;

•

S_{X,h}=S_{X}\oplus\mathbb{Z}h_{X}\subset\mathbf{L}_{X}
;

•

\tilde{S}_{X}:=(S_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cap\mathbf{L}_{X}
and
\tilde{S}_{X,h}:=(S_{X,h}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cap\mathbf{L}_{X}
: the primitive hulls of
S_{X}
and
S_{X,h}
, respectively; we have
\tilde{S}_{X}\subset\tilde{S}_{X,h}\subset\mathbf{L}_{X}
;

•

\omega_{X}\subset\mathbf{L}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{R}
: the oriented
2
-subspace spanned by the real and imaginary parts of the class of a holomorphic
2
-form on
\tilde{X}
(the
period
of
\tilde{X}
).

Note that
\omega_{X}
is positive definite and orthogonal to
h_{X}
; furthermore, the Picard group
\operatorname{Pic}\tilde{X}
can be identified with the lattice
\omega_{X}^{\bot}\cap\mathbf{L}_{X}
. In particular
\omega_{X}\in\tilde{S}_{X}^{\bot}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
.
The rank
\operatorname{rk}(S_{X})
equals the total Milnor number
\mu(X)
. Since
S_{X}\subset\mathbf{L}
is negative definite and
\sigma_{-}(\mathbf{L})=19
, one has
\mu(X)\leq 19
(see
[
39
]
,
cf.
,
[
30
]
). If
\mu(X)=19
, the quartic is called
maximizing
.

Given a root lattice
S\subset\mathbf{L}
, let
\tilde{S}_{h}:=(S_{h}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cap\mathbf{L}
be the primitive hull of
S_{h}:=S\oplus\mathbb{Z}h
. Since
\sigma_{+}\tilde{S}_{h}^{\bot}=2
, all positive definite
2
-subspaces in
\tilde{S}_{h}^{\bot}\oplus\mathbb{R}
can be oriented in a coherent way. Let
\omega
be one of these coherent orientations. The following statement gives a criterion for the realizability of the triple
(S,h,\mathbf{L})
by a simple quartic
X\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
. It is a combination of the Saint-Donat’s description
[
32
]
of projective models of
K3
-surfaces and the results of Urabe
[
39
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

A triple
(S,h,\mathbf{L})
is realizable by a simple quartic
X\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
(with set of singularities
S
) if and only if
the following conditions satisfied:

(1)

each vector
e\in(S\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cap\tilde{S}_{h}
with
e^{2}=-2
and
e\cdot h=0
lies in
S
,

(2)

there is no vector
e\in\tilde{S}_{h}
such that
e^{2}=0
and
e\cdot h=2

Then the oriented 2-subspace
\omega_{X}
defines the orientation
\omega
.

3.3.
Configurations and
L
-realizations

Isomorphism classes of simple singularities are known to be in one-to-one correspondence with those of irreducible root lattices (see Dufree
[
12
]
). Hence a set of simple singularities can be identified with a root lattice, the irreducible summands of the latter correspond to the individual singularity points. Thus, the set of simple singularities of a quartic surface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
can be seen as a root lattice
S\subset\mathbf{L}
.

Definition 3.2
.

A
configuration
is a finite index extension
\tilde{S}_{h}\supset S_{h}=S\oplus\mathbb{Z}h
,
h^{2}=4
, satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

each root
r\in(S\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cap\tilde{S}_{h}
with
r^{2}=-2
is in
S
,

(2)

\tilde{S}_{h}
does not contain an element
v
with
v^{2}=0
and
v\cdot h=2
.

An
isomorphism
between two configurations
\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{h},\tilde{S}^{\prime\prime}_{h}\supset S_{h}
is an isometry
\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{h}\rightarrow\tilde{S}^{\prime\prime}_{h}
preserving both
h
and
S
(as a set). We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}_{h}(\tilde{S}_{h})
the group of
automorphisms
of a configuration
\tilde{S}_{h}
,
i.e.
autoisometries of
\tilde{S}_{h}
preserving
h
. Since
S
is a characteristic sublattice of
\tilde{S}=h^{\perp}_{\tilde{S}_{h}}
, any isometry of
\tilde{S}_{h}
preserving
h
preserves
S
; then by item (1) in the Definition
3.2
, we have
\operatorname{Aut}_{h}(\tilde{S}_{h})\subset O(S)
.

Definition 3.3
.

An
\mathbf{L}
-
realization
of a configuration
\tilde{S}_{h}
is a primitive isometry
\tilde{S}_{h}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}
.

Two
\mathbf{L}
-realizations
\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}},\tilde{S}^{\prime\prime}_{h^{\prime\prime}}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}
are said to be
isomorphic
if there is an element of the group
O(\mathbf{L})
taking
h^{\prime}
to
h^{\prime\prime}
and
S^{\prime}
to
S^{\prime\prime}
(as a set). Let
\omega^{\prime}
and
\omega^{\prime\prime}
be the orientations of these two
\mathbf{L}
-realizations, then these oriented
\mathbf{L}
-realizations are called
strictly isomorphic
if there is an isomorphism between them taking
\omega^{\prime}
to
\omega^{\prime\prime}
,
An
\mathbf{L}
-realization
\tilde{S}_{h}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}
is called
symmetric
if it is preserved by an element
a\in O_{h}(\mathbf{L})\smallsetminus O_{h}^{+}(\mathbf{L})
,
i.e.
an autoisometry of
\mathbf{L}
preserving
S
(as a set) and
h
and reversing the positive sign structure; such autoisometries are called as
skew-automorphisms
of the
\mathbf{L}
-realization. If an
\mathbf{L}
-realization
\tilde{S}_{h}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{L}
admits an involutive skew-automorphism, it is called
reflexive
. The notion of isomorphism classes, where we ignore the orientations, may be needed to simplify the classification of
\mathbf{L}
-realizations; namely, we have the following remark.

Remark 3.4
.

Each isomorphism class consists of one or two strict isomorphism classes depending on whether the
\mathbf{L}
-realizations are symmetric or not, respectively.

3.4.
Perturbations

Recall that a set of simple singularities can be identified with a root lattice. A
perturbation
of a set of singularities
S
is a primitive root sublattice
S^{\prime}
of
S
. According to E. Looijenga
[
21
]
, deformation classes of perturbations of an individual simple singular point of type
S
are in a one-to-one correspondence with the isomorphism classes of primitive extensions
S^{\prime}\hookrightarrow S
of root lattices, see §
2.3
and §
2.4
. As shown in
[
13
]
,
S
admits a perturbation to
S^{\prime}

if and only if the Dynkin graph of
S^{\prime}
is an induced subgraph of that of
S
. Hence, given a simple quartic
X
, any perturbation of
X
to a simple quartic

gives rise to a perturbation of the set of singularities
S
of
X
to the set of singularities
S^{\prime}
of
X^{\prime}
.
According to
[
9
]
, the converse also holds: Given a simple quartic
surface
X
with set of singularities
S
, any perturbation of
S
to
S^{\prime}
is realized by a perturbation of
X
to
X^{\prime}
whose set of singularities is
S^{\prime}
.

Note that, a perturbation
S^{\prime}\subset S
of root lattices give rise to a perturbation of configurations
\tilde{S^{\prime}}_{h}\subset\tilde{S}_{h}
,
i.e.
, a primitive sub-configuration. Here the isotropic subgroup

is inherited automatically. Thus, any
\mathbf{L}
-realization of
S
gives a canonical
\mathbf{L}
-realization of
S^{\prime}
as one has the chain of primitive extensions
\tilde{S}^{\prime}_{h}\subset\tilde{S}_{h}\subset\mathbf{L}
.

Two simple quartics

in the space of simple quartics such that the Milnor number

The map sending a simple quartic surface

-realizations that differ by the orientations.

We denote by

the equisingular deformation class corresponding to

Proposition 3.6
.

Cosider an

•

\mathcal{X}(S)
in invariant under complex conjugation if and only if

-realization is symmetric

•

contains a real quartic if and only if

According to Proposition

4.7.
Concluding Remarks

Clearly any connected component
C\subset\mathcal{X}_{*}(S)
containing a real curve is real. However the converse in not true: The known counter examples are discovered in the realm of irreducible sextics and nonspecial quartics; where the exception for the former is the stratum
\mathcal{X}_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{7}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{6}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{5})
found by Akyol and Degtyarev
[
1
]
and for the latter is the strata
\mathcal{X}_{1}(\mathbf{A}_{7}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{6}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{3}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{2})
and
\mathcal{X}_{1}(\mathbf{D}_{7}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{6}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{3}\oplus\mathbf{A}_{2})
found by Güneş Aktaş
[
17
]
.
It is worth mentioning that studying phenomena of kind in the whole space of simple sextics or simple quartics would only need an extension of the algorithms, studied in
[
1
]
and
[
17
]
for both of which one has
\mathcal{K}=0
; the extension should be provided in such a way that the kernels
\mathcal{K}\neq 0
are also taken into account. This nontrivial case is still open for simple quartics.
