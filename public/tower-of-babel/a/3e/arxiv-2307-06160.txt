q q -bic hypersurfaces and their Fano schemes
Raymond Cheng

Abstract
. A q q -bic hypersurface is a hypersurface in projective space of degree q + 1 q+1 , where q q is a power of the positive ground field characteristic, whose equation consists of monomials which are products of a q q -power and a linear power; the Fermat hypersurface is an example. I identify q q -bics as moduli spaces of isotropic vectors for an intrinsically defined bilinear form and use this to study their Fano schemes of linear spaces. Amongst other things, I prove that the scheme of m m -planes in a smooth ( 2 ​ m + 1 ) (2m+1) -dimensional q q -bic hypersurface is an ( m + 1 ) (m+1) -dimensional smooth projective variety of general type which admits a purely inseparable covering by a complete intersection; I compute its Betti numbers by relating it to Deligne–Lusztig varieties for the finite unitary group; and I prove that its Albanese variety is purely inseparably isogenous via an Abel–Jacobi map to a intermediate Jacobian of the hypersurface. The case m = 1 m=1 may be viewed as an analogue of results of Clemens and Griffiths regarding cubic threefolds.



Introduction

Explicitly, a
q
-bic hypersurface
is any hypersurface in
projective space of degree
q+1
, where
q
is a power of the
characteristic
p>0
of the ground field
\mathbf{k}
, defined by an equation of
the special form:

X\coloneqq\Set{(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n})\in\mathbf{P}^{n}}{\sum\nolimits_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{ij}x_{i}^{q}x_{j}=0}.

Such hypersurfaces, the best known being the Fermat hypersurface of degree

q+1
, have been considered time and time again for their extraordinary
properties and interconnections: they are supersingular
[
Tat65
,
SK79
]
, unirational though not necessarily separably so
[
Shi74
,
Shi77
,
Con06
,
She12
,
Che24b
]
, have curious
differential-geometric properties
[
Wal56
,
Hef85
,
Bea90
,
Nom95
,
KP91
]
, and are otherwise extremal
in many respects, such as in relation to
F
-singularity theory

[
KKP+22
]
. These hypersurfaces arise in the study of
Deligne–Lusztig theory
[
Lus76b
,
DL76
,
Li23
]
, unitary
Shimura varieties
[
Vol10
,
LZ22
,
LTX+22
]
, and finite Hermitian
geometries
[
BC66
,
Seg65
,
Hir79
]
. My goal
here is to develop a new perspective from which to understand these
hypersurfaces, bringing new methods to bear and new analogies to make sense of
their idiosyncrasies.

To explain, begin with an old observation: the
q
-bic hypersurface
X
is
defined by a bilinear form. More precisely, view
\mathbf{P}^{n}
as the space of
lines in a vector space
V
, and let
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}
be the basis dual to
the chosen coordinates. The equation for
X
is intrinsically
encoded by the biadditive pairing
\beta\colon V\times V\to\mathbf{k}

determined by
\beta(e_{i},e_{j})=a_{ij}
,
\mathbf{k}
-linearity in the second
variable, and
q
-power
\mathbf{k}
-linearity in the first. Geometric
properties of
X
are reflected in algebraic properties of
\beta
: for
instance,
X
is smooth over
\mathbf{k}
if and only if
\beta
is nonsingular,
equivalent to invertibility of the matrix
(a_{ij})_{i,j=0}^{n}
.

The basic point of this article is to identify
X
as the moduli space of
isotropic lines for
\beta
. This brings moduli- and deformation-theoretic
methods to bear, and begins an analogy with quadrics. This perspective
immediately highlights the schemes parameterizing linear subvarieties of
X
:
they become moduli spaces of isotropic subspaces for
\beta
and are thus
akin to orthogonal Grassmannian. Their basic geometry is as follows:

Theorem A
. —

For each
0\leq r<\frac{n}{2}
, the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
r
-planes
in a
q
-bic hypersurface
X\subset\mathbf{P}^{n}
is

(i)

nonempty;

(ii)

of dimension at least
(r+1)(n-2r-1)
;

(iii)

connected when
n\geq 2r+2
; and

(iv)

smooth of dimension
(r+1)(n-2r-1)
precisely at points corresponding
to
r
-planes disjoint from the singular locus of
X
.

In particular, if
X
is smooth, then
\mathbf{F}
is smooth of dimension

(r+1)(n-2r-1)
and is irreducible whenever
\mathbf{F}
is positive-dimensional.
Furthermore, in this case,
\mathbf{F}

(v)

is stratified by generalized Deligne–Lusztig varieties of type
{}^{2}\mathrm{A}_{n+1}
; and

(vi)

has canonical bundle

\omega_{\mathbf{F}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}\big((r+1)(q+1)-(n+1)\big)

where
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1)
is its Plücker polarization.

This is the cumulation of
1.11
,

1.12
,

2.4
,
2.7
,

2.9
, and
5.4
.
The basic geometric properties are established by moduli-theoretic means;
notably, connectedness is proven by studying the relative Fano schemes over the
parameter space of all
q
-bic hypersurfaces. The tangent sheaf of
\mathbf{F}

can be identified: see
2.2
. Perhaps the most
remarkable fact is that the dimension of
\mathbf{F}
is independent of
q
,
equivalently, of the degree of
X
: see the comments following

1.11
for more.

I believe that the Fano schemes of smooth
q
-bic hypersurfaces are an
interesting collection of smooth projective varieties, with rich and
fascinating geometry, worthy of further study; the remaining results, which
focus on the Fano schemes of maximal-dimensional planes, hopefully give some
substance to this conviction. I mention in passing two other particularly
interesting cases which I hope will be taken up in future work: first, the
schemes of lines for their analogy with cubics: and, second, the scheme of

r
-planes in a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface of dimension
n-1=(q+1)(r+1)-2
. In the latter case,
\mathbf{F}
is a smooth projective variety of
dimension
(r+1)^{2}(q-1)
with trivial canonical bundle, and is, furthermore,
simply connected as soon as
q>2
: see

2.10
.

Turning now to the remaining results, consider first a smooth
q
-bic
hypersurface
X
of even dimension
2m
. Its Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of

m
-planes is a finite set of reduced points, the number of which being
geometrically determined in
4.5
as

\#\mathbf{F}=\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{m}(q^{2i+1}+1).

Taking
m=1
, this means that a smooth
q
-bic surface contains exactly

(q+1)(q^{3}+1)
lines; specializing further to the case
q=2
recovers the

3\times 9=27
lines in a smooth cubic surface.

When
X
is of odd dimension
2m+1
, the geometry of
\mathbf{F}
is more
complicated, and its basic properties are summarized in the following
statement. Below, the étale Betti numbers of
\mathbf{F}
are expressed in terms
of Gaussian binomial coefficients with parameter
\bar{q}\coloneqq-q
: see

4.6
for the notation and comments on the choice of
parameter.

Theorem B
. —

The Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
m
-planes in a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface

X\subset\mathbf{P}^{2m+2}
is an
(m+1)
-dimensional, irreducible, smooth,
projective variety of general type. There exists a dominant purely inseparable
rational map
Z\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}\mathbf{F}
of degree
q^{m(m+1)}
from a complete
intersection
Z\subset\mathbf{P}^{2m+2}
geometrically isomorphic to

Z\cong\Set{(x_{0}:x_{1}:\cdots:x_{2m+2})\in\mathbf{P}^{2m+2}}{x_{0}^{q^{2i+1}+1}+x_{1}^{q^{2i+1}+1}+\cdots+x_{2m+2}^{q^{2i+1}+1}=0\;\;\text{for}\;0\leq i\leq m}.

For each
0\leq k\leq 2m+2
, the
k
-th étale Betti number of
\mathbf{F}

is given by

b_{k}(\mathbf{F})=\bar{q}^{\binom{2m+3-k}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m+2}{k}_{\bar{q}}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-\lceil k/2\rceil}(1-\bar{q})^{i+1}\bar{q}^{\binom{2m+1-2i-k}{2}}[2i+1]_{\bar{q}}!!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m+3}{2i+2}_{\bar{q}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m-2i}{k}_{\bar{q}}.

The latter two statements here are a combination of
4.8
,

4.13
, and
5.12
. The Betti numbers are computed
via Deligne–Lusztig theory from
[
DL76
]
, using results of Lusztig from

[
Lus77
]
, and as set up in
5.7
. The
covering is related to a unitary analogue of the wonderful compactification of
Drinfeld’s upper half-space, see
4.8
, and arises from
dynamics of, essentially, the Frobenius endomorphism
\phi
of
X
: as
explained in
4.1
, the complete intersection
Z

parameterizes the isotropic lines
L\subset V
such that the cyclic subspace
spanned by
L,\phi(L),\ldots,\phi^{r}(L)
remains isotropic for
\beta
;
and that
Z
covers
\mathbf{F}
means that the general
r
-plane in
X
is
cyclically generated by
\phi
.

For the final result, notice that Poincaré duality for
\mathbf{F}
yields a
series of interesting identities amongst Gaussian numbers and, in particular,
gives a simple formula for the first Betti number of
\mathbf{F}
:

b_{1}(\mathbf{F})=b_{2m+1}(\mathbf{F})=\bar{q}[2m+2]_{\bar{q}}.

This number coincides with the middle Betti number
b_{2m+1}(X)
of the
hypersurface, see
5.6
, leading to a remarkable
geometric consequence: the Albanese variety
\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}
of
\mathbf{F}
is
essentially isomorphic to the intermediate Jacobian
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
of

X
. Here, the intermediate Jacobian of
X
is taken to mean the algebraic
representative, in the sense of Samuel and Murre from
[
Sam60
,
Mur85
]
, of the group of algebraically trivial cycles of codimension

m+1
in
X
: see
6.9
. The main statement is
as follows:

Theorem C
. —

Over an algebraically closed field
\mathbf{k}
, the Fano incidence correspondence

\mathbf{F}\leftarrow\mathbf{L}\rightarrow X
induces a purely inseparable
isogeny

\mathbf{L}_{*}\colon\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}

between supersingular abelian varieties of dimension

\frac{1}{2}\bar{q}[2m+2]_{\bar{q}}
.

This is the essential statement contained in

6.11
and
6.14
. Its
proof is based on studying special subvarieties in
\mathbf{F}
, using the inductive
structure provided by the moduli-theoretic view of
X
. Besides suggesting
curious phenomena regarding Chow motives in positive characteristic, this
result provides the first explicit series of nontrivial algebraic
representatives in the literature.

Taken all together, the case
m=1
of
q
-bic threefolds is particularly
striking: A smooth
q
-bic threefold
X
has a smooth surface
S
of
lines, and the Albanese variety of the surface
S
is essentially isomorphic
to the intermediate Jacobian of the hypersurface
X
via the Abel–Jacobi
map. Phrased in this way,
q
-bic threefolds are analogous to complex cubic
threefolds when compared with the results of Clemens and Griffiths in

[
CG72
]
. In fact, when
q=2
and
X
is a cubic threefold in
characteristic
2
, this result plays a crucial role in establishing
irrationality of
X
in a situation where all previous methods do not apply.
The analogies run deeper, as can already be seen upon comparing the
geometry in the latter half of §
6
with that in

[
Huy23
, Chapter 5]
; the comments following

6.14
indicate a bit more, including analogies relating to Prym
varieties. The companion paper
[
Che24a
]
studies the geometry of

S
in more detail.

I would like to end this Introduction with three comments on the name

q
-bic
: First, that these hypersurfaces deserve a uniform name
for varying
q
recognizes that they ought to be studied together, to be
understood as a single family, being characterized by the intrinsic structure
given by the form
\beta
. Second, being moduli
spaces of isotropic vectors, these hypersurfaces behave in many ways like

q
uadrics. Third, other aspects of their geometry, for instance
in regards to the geometry of lines, evoke that of other low-degree
hypersurfaces, notably of cubic hypersurfaces. I hope the results presented
here and in the future will convince the reader that the naming is worthwhile,
and that the name, apt.

1.
Setup

As indicated in the Introduction,
q
-bic hypersurfaces are construed in this
paper as moduli spaces for a certain bilinear form. This Section develops this
perspective in an invariant way via the theory of
q
-bic forms, as developed
in
[
Che23
]
: their essential definitions and properties are recalled
in
1.1
and a few further properties that will be used in
this paper are discussed in

1.2
–
1.5
. Then
q
-bic
hypersurfaces are defined in
1.6
invariantly in terms
of
q
-bic forms. Their Fano schemes are introduced in

1.10
, and some of their basic properties are discussed
in the remainder of the Section.

Throughout this article,
p
is a prime,
q\coloneqq p^{\nu}
is a
positive integer power, and
\mathbf{k}
is a field containing the finite field

\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}
. Given a finite-dimensional vector space
V
over
\mathbf{k}
,
write
\mathbf{P}V
for the projective space of lines in
V
. A
plane

refers to a linear subvariety of projective space.

1.1.
q
-bic forms

—

To set notation, let
R
be an
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}
-algebra and
M
a finite
projective
R
-module. Write
M^{\vee}
for its
R
-linear dual. Let

\Fr\colon R\to R
be the
q
-power Frobenius homomorphism of
R
and
for integers
i\geq 0
, define the
i
-th Frobenius twists
of
M

by
M^{[i]}\coloneqq R\otimes_{\Fr^{i},R}M
. There is a canonical

q^{i}
-linear map
(-)^{[i]}\colon M\to M^{[i]}
given by

m\mapsto m^{[i]}\coloneqq 1\otimes m
.

A
q
-bic form
on
M
over
R
is an
R
-linear map

\beta\colon M^{[1]}\otimes_{R}M\to R
. The form
\beta
induces two
adjoint maps, abusively denoted by
\beta\colon M\to M^{[1],\vee}
and

\beta^{\vee}\colon M^{[1]}\to M^{\vee}
. The form is called

nondegenerate
if its adjoint maps are injective and
nonsingular

if they are isomorphisms. An element
m\in M
is called
isotropic

for
\beta
if
\beta(m^{[1]},m)=0
. A subset
N\subseteq M

is called
isotropic
if every element in
N
is so.

Given submodules
N_{1}\subseteq M
and
N_{2}\subseteq M^{[1]}
, write

N_{1}^{\perp}\coloneqq\ker(\beta^{\vee}\colon M^{[1]}\to M^{\vee}\twoheadrightarrow N_{1}^{\vee})\quad\text{and}\quad N_{2}^{\perp}\coloneqq\ker(\beta\colon M\to M^{[1],\vee}\twoheadrightarrow N_{2}^{\vee})

for their orthogonals with respect to
\beta
. The orthogonals of
M^{[1]}

and
M
are called the
kernels
of
\beta
; when the image of

\beta\colon M\to M^{[1],\vee}
is a local direct summand, the kernels
fit into a canonical exact sequence of finite projective modules

0\to M^{[1],\perp}\to M\xrightarrow{\beta}M^{[1],\vee}\to M^{\perp,\vee}\to 0.

The
radical
of
\beta
is the largest submodule of
M^{[1],\perp}

whose Frobenius twist lies in
M^{\perp}
; equivalently,

\operatorname{rad}(\beta)\coloneqq\Set{m\in M}{\beta(n^{[1]},m)=\beta(m^{[1]},n)=0\;\text{for all}\;n\in M}.

For each
i\geq 1
, twisting by
\Fr^{i}
yields a
q
-bic form

\beta^{[i]}\colon M^{[i+1]}\otimes_{R}M^{[i]}\to R
, characterized by

\beta^{[i]}(m^{[i]},n^{[i]})=\beta(m,n)^{q^{i}}\quad\text{for all}\;m\in M^{[1]}\;\text{and}\;n\in M.

1.2.
Canonical endomorphisms

—

From now on, consider
R=\mathbf{k}
a field and
M=V
a finite-dimensional

\mathbf{k}
-vector space. A nonsingular
q
-bic form
\beta
on
V
induces
two canonical endomorphisms: On the one hand, the adjoint map
\beta\colon V\to V^{[1],\vee}
yields an isogeny of the
\mathbf{k}
-algebraic group

\mathbf{GL}_{V}
of linear automorphisms of
V
, given by

F\colon\mathbf{GL}_{V}\to\mathbf{GL}_{V}\qquad g\mapsto\beta^{-1}\circ g^{[1],\vee,-1}\circ\beta.

Its fixed subgroup scheme
\mathbf{GL}_{V}^{F}=\mathrm{U}(V,\beta)
is called
the
unitary group
of
(V,\beta)
, see
[
Che23
,
5.6
]
. On the other
hand, the Frobenius-twist of
\beta^{\vee}\colon V^{[1]}\to V^{\vee}
together
with the inverse of
\beta
yields an isomorphism of
\mathbf{k}
-vector spaces

\sigma_{\beta}\coloneqq\beta^{-1}\circ\beta^{[1],\vee}\colon V^{[2]}\to V.

This provides a descent datum for
V
to
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}

and the associated absolute Frobenius morphism for this structure is given by
the canonical
q^{2}
-linear bijection

\phi\coloneqq\sigma_{\beta}\circ(-)^{[2]}\colon V\to V^{[2]}\to V.

The two endomorphisms are related: the square of
F
is the Frobenius
morphism of
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
induced by the
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}
-rational
structure
\sigma_{\beta}
. In short,
F^{2}=\phi
.

Recall from
[
Che23
,
2.1
]
that
v\in V
is called
Hermitian
if it
satisfies
\beta(u^{[1]},v)=\beta(v^{[1]},u)^{q}
for all
u\in V
.
The endomorphism
\phi\colon V\to V
generalizes this to a certain
symmetry property for
\beta
:

1.3 Lemma
. —

\beta(w,\phi(v))=\beta^{[1]}(v^{[2]},w)

for every
v\in V
and
w\in V^{[1]}
, whence

(i)

v
is Hermitian if and only if
v
is fixed by
\phi
;

(ii)

\beta(\phi(v_{1})^{[1]},\phi(v_{2}))=\beta(v_{1}^{[1]},v_{2})^{q^{2}}
for
every
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
; and

(iii)

v
is isotropic if and only if
\phi(v)
is isotropic.

Proof.

It follows from the definition of
\phi
that

\beta(w,\phi(v))=w^{\vee}\circ\beta\circ(\beta^{-1}\circ\beta^{[1],\vee}\circ v^{[2]})=w^{\vee}\circ\beta^{[1],\vee}\circ v^{[2]}=\beta^{[1]}(v^{[2]},w).

The remaining properties now follow from this identity.
∎

1.4.
Hermitian subspaces

Subspaces
U
of
V
fixed by
\phi
are called
Hermitian
subspaces
. They are characterized by the following equivalent conditions:

(i)

\phi(U)=U
;

(ii)

U
is spanned by Hermitian vectors upon base change to the separable
closure of
\mathbf{k}
; and

(iii)

U^{[1],\perp,[1]}=U^{\perp}
as subspaces of
V^{[1]}
.

Proof of equivalences.

For
(i)

\Rightarrow

(ii)
, assume already that
\mathbf{k}
is separably
closed. Then
\phi\colon U\to U
is a bijective
q^{2}
-linear map, so

U
has a basis consisting of fixed vectors, see

[
DK73
, Exposé XXII, 1.1]
. This is a basis of Hermitian vectors by

1.3
(i)
.

For
(ii)

\Rightarrow

(iii)
,
it suffices to consider the case when
U=\langle u\rangle
is spanned
by a single Hermitian vector, wherein definitions of orthogonals, as in

[
Che23
,
1.7
]
, and of Hermitian vectors imply that

U^{[1],\perp,[1]}=\set{v\in V^{[1]}}{\beta^{[1]}(u^{[2]},v)=0}=\set{v\in V^{[1]}}{\beta(v,u)=0}=U^{\perp}.

For
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
,
note that the equality of the orthogonals is equivalent to

\image(\beta\colon U\subset V\to V^{[1],\vee})=\image(\beta^{[1],\vee}\colon U^{[2]}\subset V^{[2]}\to V^{[1],\vee}).

Thus
\beta(v,U)=\beta^{[1]}(U^{[2]},v)
for every

v\in V^{[1]}
and the result follows upon comparing with

1.3
.
∎

In particular,
1.4
(i)

identifies canonical Hermitian subspaces associated with any given subspace:

1.5 Corollary
. —

For any subspace
U\subseteq V
,

\displaystyle\bigcap\nolimits_{i\geq 0}\phi^{i}(U)

\displaystyle=\text{maximal Hermitian subspace contained in $U$},\;\text{and}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i\geq 0}\phi^{i}(U)

\displaystyle=\text{minimal Hermitian subspace containing $U$}.\qed

1.6.
q
-bic hypersurfaces

—

The theory of
q
-bic forms will now be used to define the geometric objects
of interest. Namely, given a nonzero
q
-bic form
(V,\beta)
over
\mathbf{k}
,
the associated
q
-bic hypersurface
is the subscheme of
\mathbf{P}V

parameterizing isotropic lines in
V
for
\beta
; in other words, this is

X\coloneqq X_{\beta}\coloneqq\Set{[v]\in\mathbf{P}V}{\beta(v^{[1]},v)=0}.

Comparing with the moduli description of projective space, as described in

[
Stacks
,
01NE
]
, shows that the
q
-bic hypersurface
X
represents the
functor

\mathrm{Sch}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{opp}}\to\mathrm{Set}
given by

S\mapsto\Set{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\subset V_{S}\coloneqq V\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{O}_{S}\;\text{a subbundle of rank $1$ isotropic for $\beta$}}.

1.7.

—

The
q
-bic form
\beta
induces a specific equation for
X
: Write

\mathrm{eu}\colon\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}(-1)\to V_{\mathbf{P}V}
for the tautological
line subbundle on
\mathbf{P}V
. The
q
-bic polynomial
associated with

\beta
is the section

f_{\beta}\coloneqq\beta(\mathrm{eu}^{[1]},\mathrm{eu})\colon\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}(-q-1)\to V_{\mathbf{P}V}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}}V_{\mathbf{P}V}\xrightarrow{\beta}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}.

Contemplating the moduli description of
\mathbf{P}V
shows that

X_{\beta}=\mathrm{V}(f_{\beta})
. To recover the explicit description from
the Introduction, choose a basis
V=\langle e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle
and let

\mathbf{x}^{\vee}\coloneqq(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{n})
be the corresponding
coordinates on
\mathbf{P}V=\mathbf{P}^{n}
. For each
0\leq i,j\leq n
,
let
a_{ij}\coloneqq\beta(e_{i}^{[1]},e_{j})
be the
(i,j)
-entry of the
associated Gram matrix, as in

[
Che23
,
1.2
]
.
Then the
q
-bic polynomial
f_{\beta}
is the homogeneous polynomial

f_{\beta}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=\mathbf{x}^{\vee,[1]}\cdot\Gram(\beta;e_{0},\ldots,e_{n})\cdot\mathbf{x}=\sum\nolimits_{i,j=0}^{n}a_{ij}x_{i}^{q}x_{j}.

1.8.
Classification

—

Isomorphic
q
-bic forms yield projectively equivalent
q
-bic
hypersurfaces. Over an algebraically closed field,
[
Che23
, Theorem A]

shows that there are finitely many isomorphism classes of
q
-bic forms on a
given vector space in that, given a
q
-bic form
\beta
on
V
, there
exists a basis
V=\langle e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}\rangle
and nonnegative integers

a,b_{m}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\Gram(\beta;e_{0},\ldots,e_{n})=\mathbf{1}^{\oplus a}\oplus\Big(\bigoplus\nolimits_{m\geq 1}\mathbf{N}_{m}^{\oplus b_{m}}\Big)

where
\mathbf{1}
is the
1
-by-
1
matrix with unique entry
1
,

\mathbf{N}_{m}
is the
m
-by-
m
Jordan block with
0
’s on the
diagonal, and
\oplus
denotes block diagonal sums of matrices. The tuple

(a;b_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is the fundamental invariant of
\beta
, hence
of
X
, and is called its
type
; the sum
\sum_{m\geq 1}b_{m}

is its
corank
. Note that the
\mathbf{N}_{1}^{\oplus b_{1}}

piece is a
b_{1}
-by-
b_{1}
zero matrix and its underlying subspace is the
radical of
\beta
. From this, it is clear that
X
is a cone if and only
if
b_{1}\neq 0
and that its vertex is given by the radical of
\beta
; see
also
[
Che22
,
2.4
]
for an invariant treatment.

A linear section of a
q
-bic hypersurface is another
q
-bic hypersurface:

1.9 Lemma
. —

Let
X
be the
q
-bic hypersurface associated with a
q
-bic form

(V,\beta)
and let
U\subseteq V
be any linear subspace. Then

X\cap\mathbf{P}U
is the
q
-bic hypersurface associated with the
q
-bic
form

\beta_{U}\colon U^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}U\subset V^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}V\xrightarrow{\beta}\mathbf{k},

which is possibly zero, obtained by restricting
\beta
to
U
.
∎

1.10.
Fano schemes

—

For each
0\leq r\leq n
, let
\mathbf{G}\coloneqq\mathbf{G}(r+1,V)
denote the
Grassmannian parameterizing
(r+1)
-dimensional subspaces of
V
, and let

\mathbf{F}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}_{r}(X)\coloneqq\Set{[U]\in\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{P}U\subseteq X}

denote the Fano scheme parameterizing
r
-planes of
\mathbf{P}V
contained in

X
; see, for example,
[
AK77
]
or

[
Kol96
, Section V.4]
for generalities. Comparing

1.6
and
1.9
shows that
a
q
-bic form
(V,\beta)
defining
X
endows
\mathbf{F}
with
the alternative moduli interpretation as the moduli of
(r+1)
-dimensional
isotropic subspaces for
\beta
; in other words,
\mathbf{F}
represents
the functor
\mathrm{Sch}^{\mathrm{opp}}_{\mathbf{k}}\to\mathrm{Set}
given by

S\mapsto\Set{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\subset V_{S}\;\text{a subbundle of rank $r+1$ isotropic for $\beta$}}.

In particular, this implies that the equations of
\mathbf{F}
in

\mathbf{G}
may be given in terms of the
tautological subbundle
\mathcal{S}\subseteq V_{\mathbf{G}}
of
rank
r+1
as follows:

1.11 Lemma
. —

Let
X
be the
q
-bic hypersurface associated with a
q
-bic form

(V,\beta)
. Then its Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
r
-planes is the
vanishing locus in
\mathbf{G}
of the
q
-bic form

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}

obtained by restricting
\beta
to
\mathcal{S}
. In particular,

\dim\mathbf{F}\geq(r+1)(n-2r-1)
whenever it is nonempty.

Proof.

This follows from the moduli interpretation of the Grassmannian together
the discussion of
1.10
. The dimension estimate is
then:

\dim\mathbf{F}\geq\dim\mathbf{G}-\rank_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}(\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S})=(r+1)(n-r)-(r+1)^{2}=(r+1)(n-2r-1).\qed

Perhaps the most striking feature of this estimate is that it does not depend
on
q
, or equivalently, the degree of
X
. For comparison, the
Fano scheme of
r
-planes of a general hypersurface
Y\subset\mathbf{P}V
of
degree
d
has codimension
\binom{d+r}{r}
, see

[
DM98
, Théorème 2.1]
for example. Even when
d=q+1
is small,
the estimate above gives a larger Fano scheme than usually expected. Thus

1.11
might be read as a systematic explanation to the
old observation that
q
-bic hypersurfaces contain many more linear subspaces
than usual; compare with
[
Col79
, Example 1.27]
and

[
Deb01
, pp.51–52]
.

Consider the parameter space of
q
-bic hypersurfaces in
\mathbf{P}V
:

q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}\coloneqq\Set{[X]}{X\subset\mathbf{P}V\;\text{a $q$-bic hypersurface}}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}(V^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}V)^{\vee}.

The following shows that the Fano schemes are nonempty in a certain range:

1.12 Lemma
. —

If
0\leq r<\frac{n}{2}
, then the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
is nonempty.

Proof.

This is a geometric question, so assume that
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed.
Consider the incidence correspondence

\mathbf{Inc}\coloneqq\Set{([U],[X])\in\mathbf{G}\times q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}}{\mathbf{P}U\subseteq X}.

The task is to show that the second projection

\pr_{2}\colon\mathbf{Inc}\to q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}
is surjective, since its fibre
over a point
[X]
is the Fano scheme of
X
. Since
\mathbf{Inc}
is a
projective space bundle over
\Gr
via
\pr_{1}
, it is proper and so it
suffices to show that
\pr_{2}
is dominant.
This is the case since the general member of
q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}
corresponds to a

q
-bic hypersurface whose underlying
q
-bic form is nonsingular by

[
Che23
,
§
6
]
,
and a nonsingular
q
-bic form
(V,\beta)
of dimension
n+1
contains an
isotropic subspace of dimension
\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
, for
instance, by explicit construction upon choosing a basis of
V
in which

\beta
is simply the identity matrix, possible by
[
Che23
,
2.7
]
.
∎

1.13.
Numerical invariants

—

It follows from
1.11
that, when
\mathbf{F}
is of the
expected codimension
(r+1)^{2}
in
\mathbf{G}
, its class in the Chow ring of the
Grassmannian is given by the top Chern class

[\mathbf{F}]=c_{(r+1)^{2}}\big((\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S})^{\vee}\big)\in\mathrm{CH}^{(r+1)^{2}}(\mathbf{G}).

Numerical invariants of
\mathbf{F}
may then by computed via Schubert calculus, as
in
[
Ful98
, p.271]
, at least for small
r
. For instance, adapting the
argument of
[
DM98
, Théorème 4.3]
gives:

1.14 Proposition
. —

If
\mathbf{F}
is of expected dimension
(r+1)(n-2r-1)
, then its degree in
its Plücker embedding is the coefficient of
x_{0}^{n}x_{1}^{n}\cdots x_{r}^{n-r}

in

(x_{0}+x_{1}+\cdots+x_{r})^{(r+1)(n-2r-1)}\cdot\Big(\prod\nolimits_{i,j=0}^{r}(x_{i}+qx_{j})\Big)\cdot\Big(\prod\nolimits_{0\leq i<j\leq r}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big).\qed

In general, this is rather unwieldy, but there are at least three cases beyond
the
r=0
case for which the degree
\deg(\mathbf{F})
of the Fano scheme with respect to its
Plücker line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1)
has a neat expression. This is summarized
in the following; proofs of the latter two statements are given later:

1.15 Proposition
. —

If the scheme
\mathbf{F}_{1}
of lines in
X
is of dimension
2n-6
, then

\deg\mathbf{F}_{1}=\frac{(2n-6)!}{(n-1)!(n-3)!}(q+1)^{2}\big((n-1)q^{2}+(2n-8)q+(n-1)\big).

For the scheme
\mathbf{F}_{m}
of half-dimensional planes in
X
,

\deg\mathbf{F}_{m}=\begin{cases}\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{r}(q^{2i+1}+1)&if $n=2m+1$ and $\dim\mathbf{F}_{m}=0$, and\\
\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{r}\frac{q^{2i+2}-1}{q-1}&if $n=2m+2$ and $\dim\mathbf{F}_{m}=m+1$.\end{cases}

Proof.

The case of lines can be obtained directly from

1.14
. The half-dimensional case follows in the
smooth case via Theorem
A
together with

4.5
when
n=2m+1
, and

6.6
when
n=2m+2
; the general case then follows
by smoothing the
q
-bic hypersurface, taking Fano schemes in families, and
using the fact that degrees are invariant in flat families.
∎

2.
Smoothness and connectedness

This Section is concerned with smoothness and connectedness properties of the
Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
r
-planes of a
q
-bic hypersurface

associated with a
q
-bic form
(V,\beta)
over
\mathbf{k}
. The tangent sheaf
of
\mathbf{F}
is described in
2.2
and smooth points of
the expected dimension are given a moduli-theoretic description in

; see also

2.7
for a geometric interpretation. Finally,

2.9
shows that

is connected whenever

by considering the relative Fano scheme of the universal family

q
-bic hypersurfaces.

In the following, let
\mathcal{S}
and

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}

-algebra

-bic form over

obtained by extension of scalars.
Write

for the ring of dual numbers over
\mathbf{k}

2.1.
Tangent sheaves

—

Describe the tangent sheaf
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{F}}
of the Fano scheme by
considering first-order deformations of the underlying moduli problem

1.10
as follows: First, since the ideal of
\mathbf{F}
in
the Grassmannian
\mathbf{G}
is the image of

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}

by
1.11
, dualizing the conormal sequence induces an
exact sequence of sheaves of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}
-modules given by

(
\star
)

0\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{G}}\rvert_{\mathbf{F}}\to(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}})^{\vee}.

Second, the moduli description of
\mathbf{G}
yields an isomorphism

\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{Q})\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{G}}
, where
at a point with residue field

corresponding to an

(r+1)

of

\kappa

\kappa[\epsilon]

of

generated by
\set{u+\epsilon\tilde{\varphi}(u)}{u\in U}
, where

\tilde{\varphi}(u)
is any lift of

to

.
Third, note that the map

V_{\mathbf{F}}\to V^{[1],\vee}_{\mathbf{F}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1],\vee}

obtained by composing the adjoint to

with the restriction vanishes on

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}
, whence it descends to the quotient to yield a map

\nu\colon\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1],\vee}

into an exact sequence of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}
-modules given by

(
\star\star
)

0\to\mathcal{S}^{[1],\perp}_{\mathbf{F}}/\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{F}}\xrightarrow{\nu}\mathcal{S}^{[1],\vee}_{\mathbf{F}}

where
\mathcal{S}^{[1],\perp}_{\mathbf{F}}\subseteq V_{\mathbf{F}}
is the orthogonal with

, as in
1.1
.

Put together, these remarks give the following description of

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{F}}
:

2.2 Proposition
. —

The first-order deformation
\tilde{U}
is isotropic for

\beta_{\kappa[\epsilon]}
if and only if the corresponding linear map

\varphi\colon U\to V/U
factors through
U^{[1],\perp}/U
. Thus there is
a canonical isomorphism

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}\kappa([\mathbf{P}U])\cong\Hom_{\kappa}(U,U^{[1],\perp}/U),

and the tangent sequence (

⋆

‣
2.1
) is
canonically identified with the exact sequence

\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}},\eqref{differential-globalize})
:

0\to\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1],\perp}/\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}})\to\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{F}})\xrightarrow{\nu_{*}}\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1],\vee}).

In particular, this canonically identifies
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{F}}\cong\HomSheaf_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}^{[1],\perp}/\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}})
.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{U}
is generated by elements
u+\epsilon\tilde{\varphi}(u)

as

ranges over

, it is isotropic for
\beta_{\kappa[\epsilon]}
if
and only if for every

Since
U^{[1]}
is spanned by elements of the form
u^{[1]}
for

u\in U
, this is equivalent to the vanishing of the linear map

\nu\circ\varphi\colon U\to U^{[1],\vee}/U
. Thus
\varphi
factors
through
U^{[1],\perp}/U
, yielding the first statement. Comparing with the
descriptions in
2.1
, this identifies the fibre at

U
of the tangent sequence (

⋆

‣
2.1
) with that
of the sequence in the statement. Varying over points of
\mathbf{F}
now gives the

∎

Comparing the statement and proof of

with the
sequence (

⋆
⁣

‣

2.3 Corollary
. —

The following statements are equivalent for the point

of

(i)

[\mathbf{P}U]
is a smooth point of
\mathbf{F}
of expected

(iii)

Applying the equivalence

. —

The following statements regarding the Fano scheme

are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{F}
is generically smooth of the expected dimension

; and

(ii)

In this case,

\omega_{\mathbf{F}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}\big((q+1)(r+1)-(n+1)\big).

It remains to compute

\omega_{\mathbf{F}}\cong\det({}^{1}_{\mathbf{F}})\cong\det(\mathcal{S}^{[1]}_{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}})^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\det({}^{1}_{\mathbf{G}}\rvert_{\mathbf{F}})\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}\big((q+1)(r+1)-(n+1)\big).\qed

2.5.
Nonsmooth locus

—

Define the

of
\mathbf{F}
as the closed subscheme given by
the Fitting ideal of its sheaf of differentials in degree
(r+1)(n-2r-1)

the expected dimension of
\mathbf{F}
:

\Sing\mathbf{F}\coloneqq\Fitt_{(r+1)(n-2r-1)}({}^{1}_{\mathbf{F}/\mathbf{k}}).

Thus the nonsmooth locus is supported on the points which are either
of larger than expected dimension, or nonsmooth of the expected dimension, see

[
Stacks
,

]
. Dualizing the sequence in
2.2

gives a presentation of
{}^{1}_{\mathbf{F}/\mathbf{k}}
, identifying the Fitting
subscheme as the top degeneracy locus of

\Sing\mathbf{F}=\mathrm{Degen}_{(r+1)^{2}-1}\big(\nu_{*}^{\vee}\colon\mathcal{S}^{[1]}_{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{F}}^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}\big).

Comparing the construction of

from

with

(iv)

shows that

drops rank precisely at points where

\Sing\mathbf{F}=\mathrm{Degen}_{(r+1)^{2}-1}\big(\mathcal{S}^{[1]}_{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}\to(V^{[1]}/V^{\perp})_{\mathbf{F}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}}\big).

When

, so that

, and

2.6 Corollary
. —

The nonsmooth locus of a

is the closed subscheme

\Sing X=\mathrm{V}\big(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-q)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{eu}^{[1]}}V^{[1]}_{X}\twoheadrightarrow(V^{[1]}/V^{\perp})_{X}\big)

corresponding to the linear space
\mathbf{P}V^{\perp}
in
\mathbf{P}V^{[1]}
. In
particular, the following are equivalent:

(i)

is smooth over
\mathbf{k}
;

(ii)

\beta
is nonsingular; and

(iii)

\Gram(\beta;e_{0},\ldots,e_{n})
is invertible for any basis

.

Moreover,
X
is regular if and only if
V^{\perp}
has trivial
Frobenius descent to

over
\mathbf{k}
.

Proof.

The first part specializes the conclusion from

2.5
to the case

. This implies the
third part and the equivalence between

and

since

is always isotropic for

This gives a geometric interpretation of the characterization

2.7 Corollary
. —

A point of
\mathbf{F}
is smooth of expected dimension if and only if the

-plane is disjoint from the nonsmooth locus of

. In

\Sing\mathbf{F}=\Set{[\mathbf{P}U]\in\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{P}U\cap\Sing X\neq\varnothing}.

In particular,
\mathbf{F}
is smooth of the expected dimension if and only if
X

Generally,

is complicated. But at least when

—and is geometrically

2.8 Lemma
. —

Assume

\mathbf{F}
is of expected dimension if

This is a geometric statement, so assume that
\mathbf{k}

Let

that underlies the unique singular point

follows from the fact that
X

has the unique singular point

show that

is not a cone if and only if

. Therefore

is nonzero on

,
and so the linear space

is of codimension

in

. Its Frobenius descent

to

contains

, and so the restriction of

thereon is of corank
at most

and has radical

; thus there is an induced

-bic form

\bar{\beta}
of corank at most
1
and no radical on

;
in other words,

is a cone with vertex

over the
non-conical
q
-bic
(n-3)
-fold
\bar{X}
of corank at most
1
in

\mathbf{P}(L^{\dagger}/L)
. Since any
r
-plane in
X
through

is a cone
over an
(r-1)
-plane in
\bar{X}
, this gives the first equality in

\dim\Sing\mathbf{F}=\dim\mathbf{F}_{r-1}(\bar{X})=r(n-2r-1).

If the corank of
\bar{X}
is
0
, then it is smooth by

2.6
and
\mathbf{F}_{r-1}(\bar{X})
has its expected
dimension by
2.4
. If the corank of

\bar{X}
is
1
, then since
n-2\geq 2(r-1)+1
is equivalent to the
inequality in the hypothesis of the Lemma, induction applies to

\mathbf{F}_{r-1}(\bar{X})
to show it is of expected dimension. In either case,
this gives the second equality above, and whence the result.
∎

Adapting the argument of
[
BVdV79
, Theorem 6]
—see also

[
DM98
, p.544]
—shows that the Fano schemes are connected whenever they
are positive dimensional:

2.9 Proposition
. —

If
n\geq 2r+2
, then
\mathbf{F}
is connected.

Proof.

This is a geometric question, so assume that
\mathbf{k}
is algebraically closed.
Consider once again, as in the proof of
1.12
,
the incidence correspondence

\mathbf{Inc}\coloneqq\Set{([U],[X])\in\mathbf{G}\times q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}}{\mathbf{P}U\subseteq X}.

The locus
Z\subset\mathbf{Inc}
over which
\pr_{2}\colon\mathbf{Inc}\to q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}
is
not smooth has codimension at least
n-2r
:
Indeed,
\pr_{2}
is smooth above the open subset of
q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}

parameterizing smooth
q
-bic hypersurfaces by

2.7
. A general point of its codimension
1

complement corresponds to a
q
-bic hypersurface
X
of corank
1
which
is not a cone by

[
Che23
, §
6
]
.
Over such a point,
\mathbf{F}=\pr_{2}^{-1}([X])
is of expected dimension
(r+1)(n-2r-1)

and
\Sing\mathbf{F}
has dimension
r(n-2r-1)
by

2.8
, so

\codim(Z\subseteq\mathbf{Inc})\geq 1+\codim(\Sing\mathbf{F}\subseteq\mathbf{F})=n-2r.

Let

\pr_{2}\colon\mathbf{Inc}\to\mathbf{Inc}^{\prime}\to q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}

be the Stein factorization of the second projection. Then
Z
contains the
preimage of the branch locus of
\mathbf{Inc}^{\prime}\to q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}
. Since

n\geq 2r+2
, the codimension estimate implies that the branch locus has
codimension at least
2
. Purity of the Branch Locus, as in

[
Stacks
,
0BMB
]
, then implies that
\mathbf{Inc}^{\prime}\to q\operatorname{\bf\!-bics}_{\mathbf{P}V}
is
étale, and hence an isomorphism since projective space is simply connected.
Therefore the fibres of
\pr_{2}
are connected, meaning that each of the Fano
schemes
\mathbf{F}
is connected.
∎

Finally, general considerations also show that the Fano schemes are
often simply connected, in the sense that there are no nontrivial connected
étale covers:

2.10 Proposition
. —

If
n\geq 3r+3
and
\mathbf{F}
is of expected dimension, then
\mathbf{F}
is
simply connected.

Proof.

This follows from
[
Deb96
, Corollaire 7.4]
, which says that

\mathbf{F}
, being the zero locus of a vector bundle in a Grassmannian, is simply
connected if the top Chern class of the vector bundle

(\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S})^{\vee}\oplus(\mathcal{S}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{G}}}\mathcal{S})^{\vee}\oplus\mathcal{S}^{\vee}

of rank
(r+1)(2r+3)
on
\mathbf{G}
is nonzero, and this happens when

n\geq 3r+3
.
∎

As is noted in
[
DM98
, Exemple 3.3]
, this is not optimal: for instance,
this does not apply to the fourfold of lines on a cubic fourfold. Note,
however, that
2.10
does apply to all the
Fano schemes of
q
-bic hypersurfaces with trivial canonical bundle so
as long as
q>2
.

3.
Fano correspondences

Let
0\leq k<r<n
. This Section is concerned with the varieties

\mathbf{F}_{r,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}_{r,k}(X)\coloneqq\Set{(\mathbf{P}W\supset\mathbf{P}U)}{X\supseteq\mathbf{P}W\supset\mathbf{P}U,\mathbf{P}W\cong\mathbf{P}^{r},\mathbf{P}U\cong\mathbf{P}^{k}}

parameterizing nested pairs of
k
- and
r
-planes in

. The projections

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{F}_{r,k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{F}_{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{F}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pr_{r}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pr_{k}}

exhibit this as a correspondence between the Fano schemes of
k
- and
r
-planes
in

; any such incidence correspondence is referred to as a

. The projection

identifies
the Fano correspondence as the Grassmannian bundle

\mathbf{G}(k+1,\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{F}_{r}})
on the tautological subbundle of

\mathbf{F}_{r}
. Therefore

\dim\mathbf{F}_{r,k}\geq(r+1)(n-2r-1)+(k+1)(r-k)

with equality if and only if the Fano scheme

itself has its expected
dimension
(r+1)(n-2r-1)
.

The fibres of
\pr_{k}\colon\mathbf{F}_{r,k}\to\mathbf{F}_{k}

Fano schemes of lower dimensional
q
-bic hypersurfaces. To give a precise
statement, consider a point of
\mathbf{F}_{k}
with residue field
\kappa
,
corresponding to an
(k+1)
-dimensional isotropic subspace

. Let

U^{\dagger}\coloneqq U^{[1],\perp}\cap U^{\perp,[-1]}

be the intersection of the two orthogonals of
U
. The restriction of

\beta
to
U^{\dagger}
has
U
in its radical, so there is an induced

q
-bic form on the quotient
U^{\dagger}/U
; let
\bar{X}
be the
associated
q
-bic hypersurface.

3.1 Lemma
. —

The reduced scheme parameterizing
r
-planes in
X
containing a fixed

k
-plane is canonically isomorphic to the reduced Fano scheme of
(r-k-1)
-planes
in the
q
-bic hypersurface
\bar{X}
:

\pr^{-1}_{k}([\mathbf{P}U])_{\mathrm{red}}\cong\mathbf{F}_{r-k-1}(\bar{X})_{\mathrm{red}}.

Proof.

This is a geometric statement, so assume that
\kappa=\mathbf{k}
.
The fibre
\pr^{-1}_{k}([\mathbf{P}U])
projects isomorphically along
\pr_{r}
onto
the scheme of
r
-planes in
X
containing
\mathbf{P}U
. In other words,
setting
\mathbf{G}\coloneqq\mathbf{G}(r+1,V)
, this gives an
identification

\pr_{k}^{-1}([\mathbf{P}U])\cong\Set{[W]\in\mathbf{G}}{U\subseteq W\;\text{isotropic for}\;\beta}=\mathbf{F}_{r}\cap\bar{\mathbf{G}},

where
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
is the subvariety of the Grassmannian
parameterizing subspaces containing
U
. Proceed by describing
\pr_{k}^{-1}([\mathbf{P}U])

as a subscheme of
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
as follows:

Taking images and preimages along the quotient map
V\to V/U
provides an
isomorphism between
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
and the Grassmannian

\mathbf{G}(r-k,V/U)
. Let
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
be the tautological
subbundle of rank
r-k
in
V/U
over
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
. A choice of
splitting
V\cong U\oplus V/U
induces a splitting

\mathcal{S}\rvert_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}\cong U_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}\oplus\bar{\mathcal{S}}

of the tautological subbundle
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathbf{G}
restricted to

\bar{\mathbf{G}}
. The restriction of
\beta
to

\mathcal{S}\rvert_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}
now splits into four components:
the—vanishing!—restriction to
U
, and the three maps

\bar{\beta}\colon\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}}\bar{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}},\qquad\bar{\beta}_{1}\colon U_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}}\bar{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}},\qquad\bar{\beta}_{2}\colon\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{[1]}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}}U_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}\to\mathcal{O}_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}.

Then
\pr^{-1}_{k}([\mathbf{P}U])
is defined in
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
by the
vanishing of these maps. Vanishing of
\bar{\beta}_{1}
and
\bar{\beta}_{2}

mean that

\bar{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq U_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}^{[1],\perp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\mathcal{S}}^{[1]}\subseteq U_{\bar{\mathbf{G}}}^{\perp}

respectively, whence any geometric point of
\pr^{-1}_{k}([\mathbf{P}U])
lies in the
subvariety of
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
parameterizing subspaces contained in

U^{\dagger}/U
. Vanishing of
\bar{\beta}
means that the corresponding
subspace is isotropic for the
q
-bic form induced by
\beta
thereon, as
required.
∎

Since
U
is of dimension
k+1
in the
(n+1)
-dimensional
space
V
, the quotient
U^{\dagger}/U
has dimension at least
n-3k-2
.
Combined with the dimension estimate of
1.11
, the
identification of
3.1
implies:

3.2 Corollary
. —

The morphism
\pr_{k}\colon\mathbf{F}_{r,k}\to\mathbf{F}_{k}
is surjective whenever

n\geq 2r+k+2
. ∎

This implies that
\mathbf{F}_{k}
is covered by Grassmannians, whence
uniruled, when
n\geq 2r+k+2
. Taking
k=0
, this implies that a

q
-bic hypersurface is covered in
r
-planes when
n\geq 2r+2
.

When the form is nonsingular, the analysis of

3.1
gives more:

3.3 Corollary
. —

Assume moreover that
\beta
is nonsingular. Then, for every closed point

[\mathbf{P}U]\in\mathbf{F}_{k}
,

\dim\pr_{k}^{-1}([\mathbf{P}U])\leq(r-k)(n-2r-1),

with equality if and only if
U
is Hermitian, in which case

\pr_{k}^{-1}([\mathbf{P}U])
is furthermore smooth.

Proof.

This is a geometric statement, so assume the residue field of
U

is
\mathbf{k}
. Then

\dim_{\mathbf{k}}U^{\dagger}/U\leq n-2k-1
with equality if and only if the two
orthogonals of
U
coincide which, by
1.4
, is
equivalent to
U
being Hermitian. This gives the statements regarding

It remains to see that the fibre is smooth when

is Hermitian. Consider
again its equations given in the proof of

3.1
: First, that
U
is Hermitian means
that the equations
\bar{\beta}_{2}
are
q
-powers of the linear equations

\bar{\beta}_{1}
, and are thus redundant. Second, the
q
-bic form

\bar{\beta}
induced by
\beta
on
U^{\dagger}/U
is nonsingular: Since

\beta
is nonsingular,
U^{\dagger}
has codimension
k+1
in
V
and
the restriction of
\beta
thereon has corank at most
k+1
. Since
U
is
contained in the radical of
\beta
restricted to
U^{\dagger}
, it follows
that
\bar{\beta}
is nonsingular. Therefore
\pr^{-1}_{k}([\mathbf{P}U])
is
isomorphic to the Fano scheme of a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface
\bar{X}
in
the Grassmannian
\mathbf{G}(r-k,U^{\dagger}/U)
, and is thus smooth by

2.7
.
∎

3.4.
Action of the Fano correspondence

Suppose now that
X
is smooth, and consider the Fano correspondence

\mathbf{L}\coloneqq\mathbf{F}_{r,0}
going between
X
and its
scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
r
-planes. The scheme
\mathbf{L}
is
naturally a closed subscheme of
\mathbf{F}\times X
, and may therefore be viewed
as a correspondence of degree
-r
from
\mathbf{F}
to
X
. This acts on Chow

\mathbf{L}_{*}\colon\mathrm{CH}_{*}(\mathbf{F})\to\mathrm{CH}_{*+r}(X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{L}^{*}\colon\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)\to\mathrm{CH}^{*-r}(\mathbf{F})

via
\mathbf{L}_{*}(\alpha)\coloneqq\pr_{X,*}(\pr_{\mathbf{F}}^{*}(\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{L})

and
\mathbf{L}^{*}(\beta)\coloneqq\pr_{\mathbf{F},*}(\pr_{X}^{*}(\beta)\cdot\mathbf{L})
,
where the dot denotes the intersection product of cycles on

.

The next statement shows that
\mathbf{L}
relates, in particular, the

h\coloneqq c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\in\mathrm{CH}^{1}(X)\quad\text{and}\quad g\coloneqq c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1))\in\mathrm{CH}^{1}(\mathbf{F})

of the standard polarization of

as a hypersurface, and the Plücker

:

3.5 Lemma
. —

If

, then for every

,

\mathbf{L}^{*}(h^{k})=c_{k-r}(\mathcal{Q})\in\mathrm{CH}^{k-r}(\mathbf{F})

and is nonzero. In particular,
\mathbf{L}^{*}(h^{r+1})=g
in

\mathrm{CH}^{1}(\mathbf{F})
.

Proof.

Since
h^{k}
represents a general
(n-k)
-plane section of
X
,

\mathbf{L}^{*}(h^{k})
represents the scheme of
r
-planes contained in

X
which are incident with a fixed, but general,
(n-k)
-plane
section; in other words,
\mathbf{L}^{*}(h^{k})
represents the restriction to

\mathbf{F}
of the Schubert variety corresponding to incidence with a general

(n-k)
-plane, which by
[
Ful98
, Example 14.7.3]
is given by

c_{k-r}(\mathcal{Q})
. Since
X
is covered by
r
-planes by

3.2
, it follows that this class is
nonzero on
\mathbf{F}
.
∎

Continuing with

, so that,

,

is covered by

r

-plane

in

. —

The

Since the Fano scheme of

, the classes

of

.

To now show that

is algebraically equivalent with

, it suffices by

to show that there exists an

(r+1)
-plane section of

supported on an

, and

below.
∎

The following shows that the Chow class of a Hermitian plane is, up to

.

3.8 Lemma
. —

Let

. Then the

.

Indeed, as in the proof of

, the

4.1.
Dynamical filtration

—

Dynamics of

Of particular interest here is the case

itself: for each

be the locus of lines in

X^{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\colon X=X^{0}\supset X^{1}\supset\cdots\supset X^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Each piece of the filtration is given by a complete intersection:

4.2 Proposition
. —

The locus
X^{k}
is the complete intersection in
\mathbf{P}V
given by the

\beta(\phi_{\mathbf{P}V}^{*,i}(\mathrm{eu})^{[1]},\mathrm{eu})\colon\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}(q^{2i+1}+1)\quad\text{for}\;0\leq i\leq k.

.

The space
\phi^{[0,k]}(L)
is isotropic if and only if

\beta(\phi^{i}(L)^{[1]},\phi^{j}(L))=0

.
The first statement now follows upon successively applying the identities of

, since

\beta(\phi^{i}(L)^{[1]},\phi^{j}(L))=\begin{cases}\beta(\phi^{i-j}(L)^{[1]},L)^{q^{2j}}&if $i\geq j$, and\\
\beta(\phi^{j-i-1}(L)^{[1]},L)^{q^{2i+1}}&if $i<j$.\end{cases}

Computing as in

shows that the tangent space to

at a point

is the vector space

\mathcal{T}_{X^{k}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X^{k}}}\kappa(x)\cong\Hom_{\kappa(x)}(L,\phi^{[0,k]}(L)^{[1],\perp}/L).

This has dimension

is a singular point if and only if

\dim_{\kappa(x)}\phi^{[0,k]}(L)\leq k

occurs if and only if

∎

4.3.

—

The equations of

are particularly simple upon choosing a

It follows from its definition in

(i)

In particular, the Hermitian subspaces of

where

.
When

The final piece of

is the union of the maximal Hermitian

4.4 Lemma
. —

The scheme

is supported on the union of the maximal

\beta(\phi^{*,i}_{\mathbf{P}V}(\mathrm{eu})^{[1]},\mathrm{eu})\colon\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}V}(q^{2i+1}+1)

Writing

or

, it suffices to show that each

When

; since

and

, and so

is Hermitian by

(iii)
.
When

, any

since any linear subvariety of

lies in a Hermitian

.
The second statement now follows from

since for every

.
∎

For example, combined with

, this implies that

-bic surface

is the

given by

Generally, let

, and let

be the locus of

-planes in

whose underlying space is Hermitian.
This is a finite set of points since there are only finitely many Hermitian
vectors by

,

]
. Analyzing the schematic structure of

gives a geometric method to count the number of
maximal isotropic Hermitian subspaces contained in a smooth
q
-bic
hypersurface

. The following count is classical: see

[

, n.32]
and
[

; see also

4.5 Corollary
. —

\displaystyle\#\mathbf{F}_{m}(X)_{\mathrm{Herm}}=\begin{cases}\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{m}(q^{2i+1}+1)&if $\dim X=2m$ is even, and\\
\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{m}(q^{2i+3}+1)&if $\dim X=2m+1$ is odd.\end{cases}

Proof.

When
\dim X=2m
is even,
4.4
implies that
X^{m}

is the union of the Hermitian
m
-planes in
X
, and the second statement
of
4.2
shows that each plane is generically
reduced. Therefore

\#\mathbf{F}_{m}(X)_{\mathrm{Herm}}=\deg(X^{m})=\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{m}(q^{2i+1}+1).

When
\dim X=2m+1
is odd,
4.4
still implies
that
X^{m+1}
is the union of the Hermitian
m
-planes in
X
, but

4.2
now shows that each component is
generically nonreduced. Thus it remains to show that each component

P\subseteq X^{m+1}
arises with multiplicity
q+1
. Choose a
Hermitian basis
U=\langle u_{0},\ldots,u_{m}\rangle
of the linear space
underlying
P
. Since
\beta
is nondegenerate, there exists linearly
independent vectors
v_{0},\ldots,v_{m}\in V
such that

\beta(u_{i}^{[1]},v_{j})=\beta(v_{j}^{[1]},u_{i})=\delta_{ij}.

Complete this to a basis of
V
with a Hermitian basis
w
of the orthogonal
complement of these vectors. Let
(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{m}:y_{0}:\cdots:y_{m}:z)

be the coordinates of
\mathbf{P}V=\mathbf{P}^{2m+2}
associated with this basis.
The generic point
\eta
of
P
is contained in the affine open where

x_{0}\neq 0
, thus the completed local ring of
X^{m+1}
at
\eta
is
isomorphic to quotient of the power series ring

\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})[\!\![y_{0},\ldots,y_{m},z]\!\!]

by the ideal generated by the polynomials

z^{q^{2i+1}+1}+y_{0}+y_{0}^{q^{2i+1}+1}+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{m}(x_{j}^{q^{2i+1}}y_{j}+x_{j}y_{j}^{q^{2i+1}})\quad\text{for}\;0\leq i\leq m+1,

compare with
4.3
.
Solving for
y_{i}
with the
i
-th polynomial shows that the completed
local ring of
X^{r+1}
at
\eta
is isomorphic to

\mathbf{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})[\!\![z]\!\!]/(\epsilon z^{q+1})
for a unit

\epsilon
, concluding the proof.
∎

Double-counting now determines the number of isotropic Hermitian
k
-planes.
The result is best expressed in terms of Gaussian integers. The parameter
here will be taken to be
\bar{q}\coloneqq-q
, following the Ennola duality
principle
[
Enn63
]
, which says that representation-theoretic
information about the finite unitary group
\mathrm{GU}_{n+1}(q)
can be
obtained from that of the finite general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(q)

via the change of parameters
q\mapsto\bar{q}
. To set notation, given
positive integers

and

, write

[n]_{\bar{q}}\coloneqq\frac{1-\bar{q}^{n}}{1-\bar{q}},\qquad[n]_{\bar{q}}!\coloneqq\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}[i]_{\bar{q}},\qquad\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\bar{q}}\coloneqq\frac{[n]_{\bar{q}}!}{[k]_{\bar{q}}![n-k]_{\bar{q}}!},

and

[2k+1]_{\bar{q}}!!\coloneqq\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{k}[2i+1]_{\bar{q}}
. Then
for each
0\leq k<n/2
, the result is as follows:

4.6 Proposition
. —

\displaystyle\#\mathbf{F}_{k}(X)_{\mathrm{Herm}}=(1-\bar{q})^{k+1}[2k+1]_{\bar{q}}!!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1}{2k+2}_{\bar{q}}
.

Proof.

Write
n
as either
2m+1
or
2m+2
, and consider the set

consisting of nested isotropic Hermitian

- and
m
-planes. Fixing

\mathbf{P}U
or
\mathbf{P}W
yields bijections

respectively; here,
\bar{X}
is a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface of dimension

n-2k-3
. These work as follow:

For the first, by
3.1
and

3.3
together with the compatibility
of taking Hermitian vectors with taking orthogonal complements from

[
Che23
,
2.2
]
, the set of Hermitian
m
-planes in

X
containing a fixed Hermitian
k
-plane
\mathbf{P}U
is in bijection with
the set of Hermitian
(m-k-1)
-planes in the
q
-bic hypersurface in

\mathbf{P}(U^{\dagger}/U)
defined by the nondegenerate
q
-bic form induced by

\beta
; choosing an isomorphism with the fixed smooth

-bic

(n-2k-3)
-fold
\bar{X}
gives the first bijection.

For the second, the discussion of
4.3
implies that
the set of Hermitian
k
-planes contained in the fixed Hermitian

\mathbf{P}W

-rational

a fixed
\mathbf{P}^{m}

Taking cardinality of

and rearranging now gives an expression
for

in terms of maximal isotropic Hermitian

, and

\#\mathbf{G}(k+1,m+1)(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}})=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m+1}{k+1}_{q^{2}}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m+1}(1-\bar{q}^{2i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}(1-\bar{q}^{2i})\prod_{i=1}^{m-k}(1-\bar{q}^{2i})},

∎

4.7.
Cyclic planes

—

Let
0\leq k<n/2
. Consider the closed subscheme of the Fano scheme

parameterizing
k

Resolve

4.9 Lemma
. —

Let
0\leq r\leq k
. The map
\pi_{r}\colon X^{k}_{r+1}\to X^{k}_{r}
is the
blowup along the strict transform of the Hermitian
r
-planes of
X
, and
its exceptional divisor is

E_{r}\coloneqq\Set{(L_{0}\subset\cdots\subset L_{r+1})\in X^{k}_{r+1}}{L_{r}\;\text{is Hermitian}}=\mathrm{V}\big(\phi^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{r}/\mathcal{L}_{r-1})\to\mathcal{L}_{r+1}/\mathcal{L}_{r}\big).

The map

\pi^{k-r}\colon\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}^{k-r+1}\to\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}^{k-r}

is the blowup along the strict transform of the locus
of
k
-planes containing a Hermitian
r
-plane
in
\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
, and its exceptional divisor is

E^{k-r}\coloneqq\Set{(L_{r-1}\subset\cdots\subset L_{k})\in\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}^{k-r+1}}{L_{r}\;\text{is Hermitian}}=\mathrm{V}\big(\mathcal{L}_{r}/\mathcal{L}_{r-1}\to\phi^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{r+1}/\mathcal{L}_{r})\big).

Proof.

Let
x
be a point of
X^{k}_{r+1}
, corresponding to a flag

(L_{i})_{i=0}^{r+1}
. If
x
lies away from
E_{r}
, then
L_{r+1}

is spanned by
L_{r}
and
\phi(L_{r})
, whence
\pi_{r}
is an isomorphism
away from
E_{r}
. An equation for
E_{r}
is obtained by noting that

L_{r}
is Hermitian if and only if the canonical projection
\phi(L_{r})\to L_{r+1}/L_{r}

vanishes; since
\phi(L_{r-1})\subset L_{r}
, this projection descends
to the quotient and vanishes if and only if the induced map

\phi(L_{r})/\phi(L_{r-1})\to L_{r+1}/L_{r}

vanishes. Passing to the universal subbundles exhibits
E_{r}
as the
claimed effective Cartier divisor.

Let
x
now be a point of
\mathbf{F}^{k-r+1}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
, corresponding
to a flag
(L_{i})_{i=r-1}^{k}
. This time,
L_{r-1}
is the intersection of

L_{r}
and
\phi(L_{r})
as long as
x
lies away from
E^{k-r}
, whence

\pi^{k-r}
is an isomorphism away from
E^{k-r}
. Since

L_{r}\subset\phi(L_{r+1})
and
L_{r-1}\subset\phi(L_{r})
, arguing
analogously shows that
L_{r}
is Hermitian if and only if the canonically
induced map

L_{r}/L_{r-1}\to\phi(L_{r+1})/\phi(L_{r})

vanishes, thereby establishing that
E^{k-r}
is the stated effective Cartier
divisor.
∎

L_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
in
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
with the second
vector space in the statement.
∎

4.11 Corollary
. —

The schemes
\tilde{X}^{k}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
are
smooth and irreducible of dimension
n-k-1
.

Proof.

The morphisms
\phi^{\pm}
defined in

4.7
compose to the finite purely inseparable endomorphism

\phi^{2}
on either
\tilde{X}^{k}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
.
Whence the
\phi^{\pm}
share the same properties, and in particular,

\tilde{X}^{k}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
are universally
homeomorphic. Thus it suffices to establish the topological properties
for
\tilde{X}^{k}
.

The dimension statement follows from
4.9
and

4.2
, which together imply that

\tilde{X}^{k}
is a blowup of the codimension
k+1
complete intersection

X^{k}
. Combined with
4.10
, this implies that
\tilde{X}^{k}

and
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
are smooth. Finally, for irreducibility,
it suffices to show that
\tilde{X}^{k}
is connected, and this follows
from the fact that it is proper birational to the normal connected scheme

X^{k}
.
∎

4.12.

—

To complete the proof of
4.8
, it remains to establish
the statements about
\phi^{\pm}
.

First, it follows from the proof of
4.11
that
the rational map
\phi^{[0,k]}\colon X^{k}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}

is dominant: in other words, the general point of
\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}

parameterizes a cyclic
k
-plane. Therefore there exists a rational map

\phi^{(0|k)}\colon\mathbf{F}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}X^{k}
that takes
a cyclic
k

P
to the intersection

with
its

\phi
-translate. It now follows from the moduli descriptions

commutes.

Second, that the

are finite purely inseparable was already
explaind in the proof of
4.11
. That they are

[
Stacks
,
00R4
]
, since
\tilde{X}^{k}
and

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
are smooth by

4.10
.

Finally, it remains to determine the degrees of
\phi^{\pm}
. By flatness, it
suffices to compute the degree of the fibre over any given point. Consider
a point of
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}
corresponding to a flag

(L_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
of isotropic Hermitian subspaces and let

\kappa
be its residue field. Then its preimage along

\phi^{-}\colon\tilde{X}^{k}\to\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\mathrm{cyc}}

is the scheme representing the deformation functor
whose value on an Artinian local
\kappa
-algebra
A
is the set of flags
in
V_{\kappa}\otimes_{\kappa}A
of the form

\Set{(\tilde{L}_{0}\subset\tilde{L}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\tilde{L}_{k})}{\tilde{L}_{i}\otimes_{A}\kappa=L_{i}\;\text{and}\;\phi^{k-i}(\tilde{L}_{i})=L_{i}\otimes_{\kappa}A\;\text{for each}\;0\leq i\leq k}.

Since
A
is local, each
\tilde{L}_{i}
is free; moreover, the second condition
for

means that
\tilde{L}_{k}=L_{k}\otimes_{\kappa}A
. So if

is a basis of Hermitian vectors adapted
to the filtration, then a deformation is of the form:

\tilde{L}_{k-r}=\Big\langle v_{i}+\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{r}a_{ij}v_{k+1-j}:0\leq i\leq r\Big\rangle\quad\text{where}\;\phi^{j}(a_{ij})=a_{ij}^{q^{2j}}=0.

This identifies the fibre over
(L_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
along
\phi^{-}
with the
affine scheme

\Spec\big(\kappa[a_{ij}:0\leq i\leq k-1,1\leq j\leq k-i]/(a_{ij}^{q^{2j}})\big),

and so
\deg(\phi^{-})=q^{k(k+1)}
. Finally,

\deg(\phi^{+})=\deg(\phi^{+}\circ\phi^{-})\cdot\deg(\phi^{-})^{-1}=\deg(\phi^{k}_{\tilde{X}^{k}})\cdot\deg(\phi^{-})^{-1}=q^{k(2n-3k-3)}.

This completes the proof of
4.8
.
∎

Consider the case

and

-bic hypersurface. By

and

, the scheme

\mathbf{F}_{m,\mathrm{cyc}}

; comparing with

now implies that

\mathbf{F}_{m,\mathrm{cyc}}=\mathbf{F}_{m}
. Therefore

implies:

4.13 Corollary
. —

Let
n=2m+2
. Then
\mathbf{F}_{m}
is dominated via a purely inseparable

\Set{(x_{0}:x_{1}:\cdots:x_{2m+2})\in\mathbf{P}^{2m+2}}{x_{0}^{q^{2i+1}+1}+x_{1}^{q^{2i+1}+1}+\cdots+x_{2m+2}^{q^{2i+1}+1}=0\;\;\text{for}\;0\leq i\leq m}.\qed

5.
Deligne–Lusztig stratification

As described in
1.2
, a nonsingular
q
-bic form

\beta
on
V
induces an isogeny

F\colon\mathbf{GL}_{V}\to\mathbf{GL}_{V}
which squares to the Frobenius
endomorphism associated with the
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}
-rational structure

\sigma_{\beta}
on
V
. This isogeny acts on the variety
\mathbf{Bor}

of Borel subgroups in
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
; intersecting its graph with
the orbits of
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
in
\mathbf{Bor}\times_{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{Bor}

gives rise to the Deligne–Lusztig varieties from
[
DL76
]
in type

{}^{2}\mathrm{A}_{n}
, those associated with the finite unitary group

\mathrm{U}(V,\beta)
. The aim of this Section is to relate the Fano
schemes
\mathbf{F}
of
r
-planes in the smooth
q
-bic hypersurface

X
associated with
\beta
with (generalized) Deligne–Lusztig varieties,
and to use this relation to access the étale cohomology of the Fano schemes:
see
5.12
.

5.1.
Deligne–Lusztig varieties

—

Let
\mathbf{Bor}
be the smooth projective variety parameterizing Borel
subgroups of
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
. Diagonally acting via conjugation by

\mathbf{GL}_{V}
decomposes the self-product

\mathbf{Bor}\times_{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{Bor}=\bigsqcup\nolimits_{w\in\mathrm{W}}\mathbf{orb}_{w}

into orbits indexed by the Weyl group
\mathrm{W}
. Let
F\colon\mathbf{Bor}\to\mathbf{Bor}
be the morphism of
\mathbf{k}
-varieties induced by
the isogeny
F
. The
Deligne–Lusztig variety
indexed by
w
is the
scheme-theoretic intersection

\mathbf{DL}_{w}\coloneqq\mathbf{orb}_{w}\cap\Gamma_{F}

of the
w
-orbit and the graph of the morphism
F
. Each
\mathbf{DL}_{w}

can be identified with a locally closed subscheme of
\mathbf{Bor}
via the
first projection, is smooth, and is of dimension the length of
w
in

\mathrm{W}
. The closure relations amongst the
\mathbf{DL}_{w}
are
given by the Bruhat order: see
[
DL76
, §1]
for this and more.

More generally, let
\mathrm{S}
be the set of simple reflections in

\mathrm{W}
, and for each
I\subseteq\mathrm{S}
, let
\mathrm{W}_{I}
be
the subgroup generated by
I
and fix a set
{}^{I}\mathrm{W}
of minimal
length representatives of the cosets
\mathrm{W}_{I}\backslash\mathrm{W}
. Let

\mathbf{Par}_{I}
be the set of parabolic subgroups of type
I
.
Lusztig defines in
[
Lus07
, §4]
a stratification

\mathbf{Par}_{I}=\bigsqcup\nolimits_{w\in{}^{I}\mathrm{W}}\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}

generalizing the classical Deligne–Lusztig stratification when

\mathbf{Par}_{\varnothing}=\mathbf{Bor}
; see also
[
Béd85
,
He09
]
. By
[
He09
, Theorem 3.1]
, the

generalized Deligne–Lusztig variety

\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}
can be
obtained as the image of
\mathbf{DL}_{w}
under the projection

\mathbf{Bor}\to\mathbf{Par}_{I}
.

5.2.

—

Describe the
\mathbf{DL}_{w}
geometrically by identifying
\mathbf{Bor}

with the complete flag variety
\mathbf{Flag}_{V}
: Fix an
F
-stable Borel
subgroup
\mathbf{B}
of
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
, and let

V_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\coloneqq(V_{i})_{i=0}^{n+1}
be the complete flag it
stabilizes. Since
F^{2}=\phi
on
\mathbf{GL}_{V}
, each
V_{i}
is fixed by

\phi
and is thus Hermitian in the sense of
1.4
.
A computation analogous to the one in
5.3

below shows that

V_{n+1-i}=V_{i}^{[1],\perp}\;\;\text{for each}\;0\leq i\leq n+1,

so that the first half of the flag is isotropic.
Identifying the
g
-conjugate of
\mathbf{B}
with the
g
-translate of

V_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
gives an isomorphism

\mathbf{Bor}\cong\mathbf{Flag}_{V}
. Abusing notation, write
F
for the
induced endomorphism of
\mathbf{Flag}_{V}
. Then
\mathbf{DL}_{w}
can be
described as the locally closed subscheme of the flag variety given by

\mathbf{DL}_{w}=\Set{[W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}]\in\mathbf{Flag}_{V}}{\gr^{F(W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}})}_{w(i)}\gr^{W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}}_{i}(V)\neq\{0\}}

where the Weyl group
\mathrm{W}
is viewed as the symmetric group on

\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, and

\gr_{i}^{W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}}
is the
i
-th graded piece associated with
the filtration
W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
: see, for example,
[
DL76
, §2.1]
.
A simple but useful instance of what this means is:

w(\set{1,\ldots,i})\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,j}
if and only if

W_{i}\subseteq F(W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}})_{j}
.

The action of the endomorphism
F
on a flag
W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
is
described as follows:

5.3 Lemma
. —

F\big(\{0\}\subsetneq W_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq W_{n}\subsetneq V\big)=\big(\{0\}\subsetneq W_{n}^{[1],\perp}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq W_{1}^{[1],\perp}\subsetneq V\big)
.

Proof.

Write
W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}=g\cdot V_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
for
g\in\mathbf{GL}_{V}
.
Then
F(W_{i})=F(g)\cdot V_{i}=(\beta^{-1}\circ g^{[1],\vee,-1}\circ\beta)(V_{i})
.
First,

\beta(V_{i})=(V^{[1]}/V_{i}^{\perp})^{\vee}=(V/V_{i}^{[1],\perp})^{[1],\vee}=(V/V_{n+1-i})^{[1],\vee}

with the middle equality due to

1.4
(iii)
, since
V_{i}

is Hermitian. Therefore

F(W_{i})=(\beta^{-1}\circ g^{[1],\vee,-1})\big((V/V_{n+1-i})^{[1],\vee}\big)=\beta^{-1}\big((V/W_{n+1-i})^{[1],\vee}\big)=W_{n+1-i}^{[1],\perp}.\qed

More generally, given a subset
I\subseteq\mathrm{S}
of simple
reflections, let
\mathbf{P}_{I}
be the unique parabolic subgroup of type

I
containing
\mathbf{B}
, and use this to identify

\mathbf{Par}_{I}
with the partial flag variety
\mathbf{Flag}_{I,V}
of
type
I
. Then the generalized Deligne–Lusztig varieties give a partition

\mathbf{Flag}_{I,V}=\bigsqcup\nolimits_{w\in{}^{I}\mathrm{W}}\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}.

The schemes
\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}
can sometimes be described in terms of
intersection patterns between a partial flag and its orthogonal by writing it as
the image of a Deligne–Lusztig variety from the complete flag variety. Compare
the following with
[
HLZ19
, §2.3]
:

5.4 Lemma
. —

The Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
is a union of generalized Deligne–Lusztig varieties of type

{}^{2}\mathrm{A}_{n+1}
.

Proof.

Since
W_{r+1}^{[1],\perp}=F(W_{n-r})
by
5.3
, the description of

\mathbf{DL}_{w}
from
5.2
implies that

W_{r+1}\subseteq W_{r+1}^{[1],\perp}\;\iff w(\set{1,\ldots,r+1})\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,n-r}.

In other words, whether or not
W_{r+1}
is isotropic is determined by the
Weyl group element
w
. Since the
\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}
are images of the

\mathbf{DL}_{w}
under the projection

\mathbf{Flag}_{V}\to\mathbf{G}
, it follows that
\mathbf{F}

is the union of generalized Deligne–Lusztig varieties associated with
\mathbf{G}
.
∎

Two cases for which the Deligne–Lusztig stratification of
\mathbf{F}
is particularly
simple and useful are: first, the hypersurface
X
itself; second, and more
interestingly, the Fano scheme of maximal isotropic subspaces. In the
following, denote by
\mathrm{S}=\set{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}}
the usual set of
simple transpositions which generates the Weyl group, identified with the
symmetric group on
n+1
elements as above.

5.5.
Hypersurface

—

Consider the stratification associated with the hypersurface
X
.
Projective space is the partial flag variety associated with the set

I\coloneqq\set{s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}}
of simple reflections. A set of minimal
length representatives for
\mathrm{W}_{I}\backslash\mathrm{W}
can be taken
to be

{}^{I}\mathrm{W}\coloneqq\set{\id,\;s_{1},\;s_{1}s_{2},\;\ldots,\;s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}}.

View the strata
\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}
as images of the
\mathbf{DL}_{w}

under the projection
\mathbf{Flag}_{V}\to\mathbf{P}V
. Then a computation using
the descriptions from
5.2
and
5.3
relates
the generalized Deligne–Lusztig varieties in this case with the pieces
of the dynamical filtration from
4.1
: for

0\leq k\leq n
,

\mathbf{DL}_{I,s_{1}\cdots s_{k}}=X^{n-k-1}\setminus X^{n-k}\;\;

where
X^{-1}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}V
, and
X^{n-k}
is the union of the Hermitian

k
-planes in
X
for
0\leq k\leq n/2
. This decomposition can
be combined with the methods of
[
DL76
,
Lus76a
]
to determine the
étale cohomology of
X
as a representation for the projective unitary
group. The following statement was first observed by Tate and Thompson in

[
Tat65
, p.102]
, and later made more precise by
[
HM78
]
:

5.6 Theorem
. —

Assume
\mathbf{k}
is separably closed. The middle primitive étale cohomology

\mathrm{H}^{n-1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(X,\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})_{\mathrm{prim}}
is
an irreducible representation for
\mathrm{U}_{n+1}(q)
of dimension

(-1)^{n}\bar{q}[n]_{\bar{q}}
.
∎

5.7.
Maximal isotropic subspaces

—

Let
n=2m+1
and let
\mathbf{F}
be the Fano scheme of
m
-planes in
X
.
Then Theorem
A
shows that
\mathbf{F}
is a finite set
of reduced points; its dimension can also be obtained from

5.4
by showing there is exactly one generalized
Deligne–Lusztig variety contained in
\mathbf{F}
.

The remainder of this Section is concerned with the case
n=2m+2
and the
Deligne–Lusztig decomposition of the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of
m
-planes in

X
. The ambient Grassmannian
\mathbf{G}(m+1,2m+3)
is the partial
flag variety associated with the set

I=\set{s_{1},\ldots,\;s_{m},\;s_{m+2},\ldots,\;s_{2m+2}}=\mathrm{S}\setminus\{s_{m+1}\}.

As in
5.4
, the stratum
\mathbf{DL}_{I,w}
is
contained in
\mathbf{F}
if and only if

w(\set{1,\ldots,m+1})\subseteq\set{1,\ldots,m+2}
. Since
\mathrm{W}_{I}

contains all permutations on the first
m+1
elements, a straightforward
computation shows that a set
{}^{I}\mathrm{W}
of minimal length representatives
for the coset space
\mathrm{W}_{I}\backslash\mathrm{W}
satisfying this
condition consists of the following
m+2
cyclic permutations:

w_{0}\coloneqq\id
and, for
1\leq k\leq m+1
,

w_{k}\coloneqq s_{m+1}s_{m}\cdots s_{m+2-k}=(m+2-k,\,m+3-k,\ldots,\,m+1,\,m+2).

The next statement gives a moduli description of

\overline{\mathbf{DL}}_{I,w_{k}}
, and may be compared with
[
Vol10
, Theorem
2.15]
. Note already that, since the
w_{k}
are linearly ordered in
the Bruhat order, the closure relations of the associated Deligne–Lusztig
varieties are also linear, giving the first identification in:

5.8 Proposition
. —

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{DL}}_{I,w_{k}}=\bigsqcup\nolimits_{i=0}^{k}\mathbf{DL}_{I,w_{i}}=\Set{P\in\mathbf{F}}{\text{$P$ contains a Hermitian $(m-k)$-plane}}
.

Proof.

Proceed by showing the more refined statement that the classical
Deligne–Lusztig variety
\mathbf{DL}_{w_{k}}
parameterizes complete flags

(W_{i})_{i=0}^{n+1}
satisfying:

(i)

W_{i}=W_{n+1-i}^{[1],\perp}

for
m+2\leq i\leq n+1
,

(ii)

W_{i}=\phi(W_{i})

for
0\leq i\leq m+1-k
, and

(iii)

W_{i}\cap\phi(W_{i})=W_{i-1}

for
m+2-k\leq i\leq m+1
.

In other words,
\mathbf{DL}_{w_{k}}
parameterizes complete isotropic
flags whose first
m+1-k
terms are Hermitian, and the remaining terms
can be determined by
W_{m+1}
and
\phi
; in fact,

(iii)
implies that

W_{i}=\bigcap\nolimits_{j=0}^{m+1-i}\phi^{j}(W_{m+1})\quad\text{for}\;m+2-k\leq i\leq m+1.

With
(ii)
, this gives

W_{m+1-k}=\bigcap\nolimits_{j\geq 0}\phi^{j}(W_{m+1})
meaning this
is the maximal Hermitian subspace of
W_{m+1}
by
1.5
.
Projecting down to the Grassmannian would then show that
\mathbf{DL}_{I,w_{k}}

parameterizes
m
-planes in
X
that contain a Hermitian
(m-k)
-plane
but no
(m-k+1)
-plane, implying the statement.

Describe
\mathbf{DL}_{w_{k}}
now by translating the equations imposed by

w_{k}
to conditions on flags via
5.2

and
5.3
: That the cycle
w_{k}
stabilizes the intervals

\set{1,\ldots,i}
for
m+2\leq i\leq n+1
is equivalent to condition

(i)
. Since
F^{2}=\phi
, this implies that

F(W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}})_{i}=(W_{i}^{[1],\perp})^{[1],\perp}=\phi(W_{i})\;\;\text{for}\;0\leq i\leq m+1.

With this, that
w_{k}
acts trivially on
\set{1,\ldots,i}
with

0\leq i\leq m+1-k
is equivalent to
(ii)
.
Finally, that
w_{k}(i)=i+1
for
m+2-k\leq i\leq m+1
means that

\gr^{F(W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}})}_{i+1}\gr^{W_{\mathord{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.75}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}}_{i}(V)\neq\set{0}

which, in light of what has been established so far, amounts to the two
conditions
W_{i}\neq\phi(W_{i})
, and

W_{i}\subset\phi(W_{i+1})
when
i\neq m+1
and that
W_{m+1}
is
isotropic. Taken altogether, these conditions are equivalent to those in

(iii)
. This now dispenses with all the equations
of
\mathbf{DL}_{w_{k}}
.
∎

5.9.

—

Combined with
3.1
and Theorem

A
, the description of

implies that the irreducible
components of
\overline{\mathbf{DL}}_{I,w_{k}}
are indexed by the Hermitian

(m-k)
-planes in

, and each component is isomorphic to the Fano scheme
of
k
-planes contained in a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface of dimension

2k+1
. Moreover, the irreducible component indexed by the isotropic

-plane

is contained exactly in the irreducible components of

\overline{\mathbf{DL}}_{I,w_{k+1}}
indexed by the isotropic

(k+1)
-planes containing

.

For each

, write
\mathbf{DL}^{\mathrm{Cox}}_{k}
for the open
dense Deligne–Lusztig stratum of the Fano scheme of
k
-planes in a smooth

q
-bic hypersurface of dimension
2k+1
; this is
\mathbf{DL}_{w_{m+1}}

when
k=m
as above. The indexing Weyl group element contains exactly one
simple reflection in each
F
-orbit, and so it is a
Coxeter element
in
type
{}^{2}\mathrm{A}_{2k+2}
, whence the notation; see

[
Lus77
, (1.7)]
. The discussion of
5.9

gives a useful stratification of the Fano scheme, phrased in terms of the
Grothendieck ring
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbf{k}})
of varieties in the
following statement; compare it with
[
HLZ19
, Proposition 2.5.1]
:

5.10 Corollary
. —

\displaystyle[\mathbf{F}]=[\mathbf{DL}_{m+1}^{\mathrm{Cox}}]+\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{m}\#\mathbf{F}_{m-k}(X)_{\mathrm{Herm}}\cdot[\mathbf{DL}_{k}^{\mathrm{Cox}}]

in
\mathrm{K}_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbf{k}})
.
∎

Notably, the zeta function of the Coxeter Deligne–Lusztig variety

\mathbf{DL}_{k}^{\mathrm{Cox}}
with respect to their
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}}
-structure

\phi
can be explicitly determined. In the following, notation is as in

4.6
:

5.11 Lemma
. —

\displaystyle\log\zeta(\mathbf{DL}_{k}^{\mathrm{Cox}},t)=\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{2k}(-1)^{i+1}\bar{q}^{\binom{2k+1-i}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2k}{i}_{\bar{q}}\log(1-\bar{q}^{i}t)
.

Proof.

Lusztig’s calculation in
[
Lus77
, (6.1.2)]
gives

t\frac{d}{dt}\log\zeta(\mathbf{DL}_{k}^{\mathrm{Cox}},t)=\sum_{s\geq 1}\#\mathbf{DL}_{k}^{\mathrm{Cox},\phi^{s}}\cdot t^{s}=\bar{q}^{k(2k+1)}(1-\bar{q})^{2k}[2k]_{\bar{q}}!\cdot\frac{t^{2k+1}}{\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{2k}(1-\bar{q}^{i}t)}

upon comparing with the tables
[
Lus77
, p.106 and p.147]
and
noting that the associated adjoint group has order

\#\mathrm{PU}_{2k+1}(q)=\bar{q}^{k(2k+1)}\prod\nolimits_{i=2}^{2k+1}(\bar{q}^{i}-1)
.
The partial fraction decomposition of the rational function is given by:

\frac{t^{2k+1}}{\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{2k}(1-\bar{q}^{i}t)}=\sum_{i=0}^{2k}\Bigg((-1)^{i}\bar{q}^{\binom{i+1}{2}-2ik}(1-\bar{q})^{-2k}\frac{1}{[2k]_{\bar{q}}!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2k}{i}_{\bar{q}}\frac{t}{1-\bar{q}^{i}t}\Bigg).

Combining and applying the operator

\int\frac{dt}{t}
then gives the result.
∎

These together give an explicit expression for the étale Betti numbers of the
Fano scheme:

5.12 Theorem
. —

For each
0\leq k\leq 2m+2
,

b_{k}(\mathbf{F})=\bar{q}^{\binom{2m+3-k}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m+2}{k}_{\bar{q}}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-\lceil k/2\rceil}(1-\bar{q})^{i+1}\bar{q}^{\binom{2m+1-2i-k}{2}}[2i+1]_{\bar{q}}!!\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m+3}{2i+2}_{\bar{q}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2m-2i}{k}_{\bar{q}}.

Proof.

The logarithmic zeta function is additive along disjoint unions, so this
follows from the motivic decomposition of
\mathbf{F}
in

5.10
together with the computations

4.6
and
5.11
.
∎

Since
\mathbf{F}
is smooth of dimension
m+1
by Theorem
A
,
Poincaré duality gives
b_{k}(\mathbf{F})=b_{2m+2-k}(\mathbf{F})
for each

0\leq k\leq 2m+2
. This yields a curious set of identities amongst

q
-binomial coefficients. In particular, this gives a neat expression for
the first Betti number:

5.13 Corollary
. —

b_{1}(\mathbf{F})=b_{2m+1}(\mathbf{F})=\bar{q}[2m+2]_{\bar{q}}
. ∎

Comparing with
5.6
shows that
b_{1}(\mathbf{F})=b_{2m+1}(X)
.
Adapting the argument of
[
Che22
,
2.8.3
]

shows that the Fano correspondence
\mathbf{L}
between
X
and
\mathbf{F}

acting as in

induces an isomorphism

\mathbf{L}^{*}\colon\mathrm{H}^{2m+1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(X,\mathbf{Z}_{\ell})\to\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{Z}_{\ell}).

Details are omitted, as this will not be used in the following.

6.
Intermediate Jacobian

This Section is concerned with finer aspects of the geometry of a smooth

q
-bic hypersurface of odd dimension
2m+1
; as such, the base field

\mathbf{k}
will be henceforth taken to be algebraically closed. The main result is
that the Albanese variety
\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}
of the Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
of

m
-planes in
X
is purely inseparably isogeneous via an Abel–Jacobi map
to a certain intermediate Jacobian
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
associated with

X
: see
6.14
. Here,
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
is taken to be
the algebraic representative for algebraically-trivial cycles of codimension

m+1
in
X
, see
6.9
. Existence of

\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
is known in this case by work of Murre and Fakhruddin,
see
6.10
. The result relies on a study of the geometry
of special cycles in
\mathbf{F}
, which occupies the first half of this Section,
the main computations being
6.2
and

6.4
.

6.1.
Incidence divisors

—

The Fano scheme
\mathbf{F}
contains two particularly interesting types of
divisors that arise from incidence relations; first are the divisors
D_{P}
of

m
-planes in
X
that are incident with a fixed
m
-plane

P\subset X
as in
3.6
; and second are the
divisors

D_{x}\coloneqq\Set{[P]\in\mathbf{F}}{P\;\text{contains}\;x}

of
m
-planes that contain a fixed Hermitian point
x
of the hypersurface

X
. It follows from
3.1
and

3.3
that
D_{x}
is isomorphic to the Fano
scheme of
(m-1)
-planes in a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface of dimension

2m-1
; moreover, writing
L\subset V
for the
1
-dimensional subspace
underlying
x
,
D_{x}
may be obtained from
\mathbf{F}
via intersection with
the Grassmannian
\mathbf{G}(m,V/L)
, linearly embedded in

\mathbf{G}(m+1,V)
as the subvariety of
(m+1)
-dimensional subspaces
containing
L
.

The next few paragraphs study the relations amongst these divisors. To begin,
consider how the divisors associated with two Hermitian points
x
and
y

intersect. To state the result, phrased in terms of the associated line
bundles, one piece of notation: Suppose that
x
and
y
span a Hermitian
line
\ell
in
X
. Then
D_{x}
is the Fano scheme of
(m-1)
-planes in
the
q
-bic hypersurface
\bar{X}
lying at the base of the cone

X\cap\mathbf{T}_{X,x}
obtained by intersecting
X
with its embedded
tangent space
\mathbf{T}_{X,x}
at
x
. The line
\ell
corresponds to a
Hermitian point of
\bar{X}
, denoted by
\bar{y}
. The divisors
D_{x}

and
D_{y}
intersect as follows:

6.2 Lemma
. —

Let
x
and
y
be Hermitian points of
X
. Then

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{y})\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1-q)&if $x=y$,\\
\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{y}})&if $x\neq y$ and $\langle x,y\rangle\subset X$, and\\
\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}&otherwise.\end{cases}

Proof.

The case
x=y
pertains to the normal bundle of
D_{x}
in
\mathbf{F}
, which
may be determined via
2.4
as

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{x})\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\omega_{D_{x}}\otimes\omega_{\mathbf{F}}^{\vee}\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1-q)

since
D_{x}
is linearly embedded in
\mathbf{F}
with respect to the Plücker
polarization. Now suppose that
x\neq y
. Let
L
and
M
be the

1
-dimensional subspaces of
V
underlying
x
and
y
, respectively.
By the discussion of
6.1
,
D_{x}\cap D_{y}
is
the intersection of the Fano scheme with two sub-Grassmannian varieties in

\mathbf{G}(m+1,V)
, as on the the left side of:

\mathbf{F}\cap\mathbf{G}(m,V/L)\cap\mathbf{G}(m,V/M)=\mathbf{F}\cap\mathbf{G}(m-1,V/\langle L,M\rangle).

The sub-Grassmannians intersect along the Grassmannian of
(m-1)
-dimensional
subspaces containing the
2
-dimensional space
\langle L,M\rangle
.
This implies that
D_{x}
and
D_{y}
intersect along
D_{\bar{y}}
if the
line
\langle x,y\rangle
is contained in
X
, and are disjoint otherwise.
∎

The next task is to relate the divisors
D_{P}
and
D_{x}
. The main
observation is that Hermitian planes intersect
m
-planes in Hermitan
subplanes:

6.3 Proposition
. —

Let
P_{0}\subset X
be a Hermitian
k
-plane for some
0\leq k\leq m
.
If
P^{\prime}\subset X
is any
m
-plane, then
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
is either empty or
a Hermitian plane.

Proof.

Begin with three observations: First, it follows from

4.2
or, alternatively, the arguments of

4.6
that every Hermitian
k
-plane in
X
is
contained in a Hermitian
m
-plane in
X
, so it suffices to consider the
case
k=m
. Second, if
P^{\prime}
is Hermitian, then
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}

is stable under
\phi
and is Hermitian by
1.4
.
Third, suppose
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
contains a nonempty Hermitian plane; let

K\subseteq V
be a nonzero Hermitian subspace corresponding to a maximal
such. Let
\bar{X}
be the nonsingular
q
-bic hypersurface at the base of
the cone
X\cap\mathbf{P}K^{\dagger}
as in

3.1
and

3.3
, and let
\bar{P}_{0}
and

\bar{P}^{\prime}
be the corresponding planes in
\bar{X}
. Since taking Hermitian
vectors respects orthogonal decompositions, as in the proof of

4.6
, it follows that
\bar{P}_{0}
is a Hermitian
plane in
\bar{X}
and
\bar{P}_{0}\cap\bar{P}^{\prime}
does not contain a
Hermitian plane. Thus it suffices to consider the case where
P_{0}
is a
Hermitian
m
-plane,
P^{\prime}
is not Hermitian, and where
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
does
not contain any Hermitian planes, in which case the task is to show that

P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}=\varnothing
.

Assume, on the contrary, that
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
is an
r
-plane with
r\geq 0
,
so that
P_{0}
and
P^{\prime}
together span an
(2m-r)
-plane
L
in
\mathbf{P}V
.
Observe that for each
i\geq 0
, the
m
-plane
\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})
is isotropic
by
1.3
(iii)

and is moreover contained in
L
: Indeed, as follows from

4.7
with the comments preceding
4.13
,

\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})\cap\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})
has dimension
m-1
, and since
P_{0}
is
Hermitian while
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
is not,

P_{0}\cap\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})=\phi^{i+1}(P_{0}\cap P^{\prime})\neq\phi^{i}(P_{0}\cap P^{\prime})=P_{0}\cap\phi^{i}(P^{\prime}).

Here, the first and third equalities use the fact that
\phi
is injective
and stabilizes
P_{0}
. Therefore
\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})
is spanned by the

(m-1)
-plane
\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})\cap\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})
, and any point in
P_{0}\cap\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})
—which the assumption implies is not empty!— not contained
in
\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})
. Induction now implies that
\phi^{i+1}(P^{\prime})
lies in
L
,
as claimed.

Consider the
q
-bic hypersurface
X^{\prime}\coloneqq X\cap L
of dimension

2m-r-1
. Then
X^{\prime}
contains the
m
-planes
P_{0}
and, by
the claim above, each of the
\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})
with
i\geq 0
. This implies
two things: first, since a smooth hypersurface can never contain linear spaces
larger than half its dimension,
X^{\prime}
is singular; and, second, the Fano
scheme of
m
-planes in
X^{\prime}
is everywhere of larger than its expected
dimension by
1.11
. Thus

2.7
implies that each of
P_{0}
and the

\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})
must pass through the singular locus of
X^{\prime}
. But, finally, on
the one hand,

\varnothing\neq\Sing X^{\prime}\subseteq P_{0}\cap\Big(\bigcap\nolimits_{i\geq 0}\phi^{i}(P^{\prime})\Big).

On the other hand, the intersection on the right is stable under
\phi
,
whence is Hermitian
1.4
. This contradicts the
assumption that
P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}
contains no Hermitian planes, so

P_{0}\cap P^{\prime}=\varnothing
.
∎

The next statement gives the basic relationship between the divisors

D_{P}
and
D_{x}
. Continuing with the notation set at the end of

6.1
, given a Hermitian point
x
of
X
,
write
\bar{P}
for the
(m-1)
-plane induced by
P
in the
q
-bic
hypersurface
\bar{X}
at the base of the cone
X\cap\mathbf{T}_{X,x}
.

6.4 Proposition
. —

Let
P\subset X
be an
m
-plane. Then for every Hermitian point

x
of
X
,

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{P}})&if $x\notin P$, and\\
\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{P}})^{\otimes q^{2}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}}\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1-q)&if $x\in P$.\end{cases}

Moreover, there is a decomposition of effective Cartier divisors on
\mathbf{F}

given by

D_{P}=D_{P}^{\prime}+\sum\nolimits_{x\in P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}}D_{x}

where the components of
D_{P}^{\prime}
generically
parameterize
m
-planes
X
which are incident with
P
and are
disjoint from
P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}
.

Proof.

Fix a Hermitian point
x
of
X
. If
x\notin P
, then

D_{P}\cap D_{x}
parameterizes
m
-planes in
X
which pass through the
vertex of the cone
X\cap\mathbf{T}_{X,x}
and which intersect the
(m-1)
-plane

P\cap\mathbf{T}_{X,x}
. In other words, projecting from the vertex
x

to obtain a
q
-bic hypersurface
\bar{X}
, identifying

D_{x}
as the Fano scheme of
(m-1)
-planes in
\bar{X}
, and
letting
\bar{P}
be the
(m-1)
-plane induced in
\bar{X}
, it follows
that

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{P}}).

If
x\in P
, then it follows from
3.1

that
D_{P}-D_{x}
is an effective Cartier divisor. Smoothness of the Fano
correspondence
\mathbf{L}\to X
above
x
from

3.3
together with the construction of

D_{P}
in
3.6
shows that
D_{P}-D_{x}
is the
closure of the image under
\mathbf{L}\to\mathbf{F}
of

\Set{(z,[P^{\prime}])\in\mathbf{L}}{P\cap P^{\prime}\ni z\neq x}.

Therefore
D_{P}-D_{x}
intersects
D_{x}
at the
(m-1)
-dimensional locus
parameterizing
m
-planes in
X
containing a line through
x
which
intersects
P
. Projecting from
x
, this is the divisor in
D_{x}

parameterizing
(m-1)
-planes in
\bar{X}
incident with
\bar{P}
.
Therefore there is some
a\in\mathbf{Z}
such that

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P}-D_{x})\rvert_{D_{x}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{x})\rvert{D_{x}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{P}})^{\otimes a}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}}\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1-q),

where the second identification is by

6.2
.
Determine the multiplicity
a
by computing the degree of

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}}
with respect to the Plücker polarization of

D_{x}
in two ways. On the one hand, this identification
together with the fact that
(q+1)D_{\bar{P}}
is algebraically equivalent to

c_{1}(\bar{\mathcal{Q}})=c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1))
from
3.7
gives

\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}})=a\deg(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(D_{\bar{P}}))+(1-q)\deg(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1))=\frac{a+1-q^{2}}{q+1}\deg(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1)).

On the other hand, since
(q+1)D_{P}
is algebraically equivalent to

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1))
again by
3.7
, and since the
Plücker polarizations of
\mathbf{F}
and
D_{x}
are compatible, it follows that

\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{D_{x}})=\frac{1}{q+1}\deg(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1)).

Comparing the two expressions shows that
a=q^{2}
.
Finally, that
D_{P}^{\prime}\coloneqq D_{P}-\sum\nolimits_{x\in P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}}D_{x}

is the effective Cartier divisor generically parameterizing
m
-planes
incident with
P
and disjoint from
P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}

now follows from the discussion above together with
6.3
.
∎

In the case
P
is Hermitian,
6.3
implies that
the divisor
D_{P}^{\prime}
appearing in

vanishes, and so:

6.5 Corollary
. —

Let
P\subset X
be a Hermitian
m
-plane. Then

D_{P}=\sum\nolimits_{x\in P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}}D_{x}

as divisors on
\mathbf{F}
. The sum ranges over the Hermitian points of

contained in

.
∎

The Plücker degree of
\mathbf{F}
can be easily determined combining

6.5
and the proof method of
6.4
;
this has also been recently computed in
[

, Theorem 1.1]
with
similar methods.

6.6 Corollary
. —

\displaystyle\deg_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1)}(\mathbf{F})=\prod\nolimits_{i=0}^{m}\frac{q^{2i+2}-1}{q-1}
.

Proof.

Choose a Hermitian point
x
and a Hermitian
m
-plane
P\subset X

containing it. Note that
D_{x}
is isomorphic to
D_{y}
for each
of the
\#\mathbf{P}^{m}(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}})=\frac{q^{2m+2}-1}{q^{2}-1}
points

, and that the Plücker polarization
of
\mathbf{F}
is compatible with those on these divisors. Therefore, by

and

,

\displaystyle\frac{q^{2m+2}-1}{q^{2}-1}\deg(\mathcal{O}_{D_{x}}(1))

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{y\in P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}}\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1)\rvert_{D_{x}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{F}}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1))^{m}\cdot\Big(\sum\nolimits_{y\in P\cap X_{\mathrm{Herm}}}[D_{y}]\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{F}}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1))^{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P}))=\frac{1}{q+1}\deg(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(1)).

Since
D_{x}
is the Fano scheme of
(m-1)
-planes on a smooth
q
-bic
hypersurface of dimension
2m-1
, the result follows by induction upon
noting that, when
m=0
,
\mathbf{F}
is a plane curve of degree

q+1=\frac{q^{2}-1}{q-1}
.
∎

Finally, let
R\subset X
be a Hermitian
(m-1)
-plane and consider
the restriction of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})
to

-planes in

containing

.
This is a smooth

and

, and is even a complete intersection
of the divisors

6.7 Corollary
. —

Let

be an

Then

Choose a basis of Hermitian points

6.8 Corollary

6.9.
Algebraic representatives

—

Let
Y
be a smooth projective variety over an algebraically closed field

\mathbf{k}
. For each integer
0\leq k\leq\dim Y
, denote by

\mathrm{CH}^{k}(Y)_{\mathrm{alg}}
the subgroup of algebraically trivial
codimension
k
cycle classes on
Y
. Given an abelian variety
A
over

\mathbf{k}
, a group homomorphism

\phi\colon\mathrm{CH}^{k}(Y)_{\mathrm{alg}}\to A(\mathbf{k})

is said to be
regular
if for every pointed smooth projective variety

(T,t_{0})
over
\mathbf{k}
, and every cycle class

Z\in\mathrm{CH}^{k}(T\times Y)
, the map

T(\mathbf{k})\to\mathrm{CH}^{k}(Y)_{\mathrm{alg}}\to A(\mathbf{k}),\qquad t\mapsto\phi(Z_{t}-Z_{t_{0}})

is induced by a morphism
T\to A
of varieties over
\mathbf{k}
. Suppose
there exists a regular homomorphism

\phi^{k}_{Y}\colon\mathrm{CH}^{k}(Y)_{\mathrm{alg}}\to\mathbf{Ab}_{Y}^{k}(\mathbf{k})

that is initial amongst all regular homomorphisms from

\mathrm{CH}^{k}(Y)_{\mathrm{alg}}
. Then the pair

(\mathbf{Ab}^{k}_{Y},\phi^{k}_{Y})
is called an
algebraic representative

for codimension
k
cycles in
Y
.

The basic general existence results regarding algebraic representatives are as
follows:

6.10 Theorem
. —

Let
Y
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
d
over
\mathbf{k}
. Then
an algebraic representative for codimension
k
cycles exists when

(i)

k=d
, and
\mathbf{Ab}_{Y}^{d}=\mathbf{Alb}_{Y}
,

(ii)

k=1
, and
\mathbf{Ab}_{Y}^{1}=\mathbf{Pic}_{Y,\mathrm{red}}^{0}
,

(iii)

k=2
, and

2\dim\mathbf{Ab}_{Y}^{2}\leq\dim_{\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}}\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(Y,\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
, and

(iv)

the rational Chow motive of
Y
is a summand of the motive of a supersingular
abelian variety, in which case
2\dim\mathbf{Ab}_{Y}^{k}=\dim_{\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}}\mathrm{H}^{2k-1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(Y,\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})
.

Proof.

For
(i)

and
(ii)
, see

[
Mur85
, §1.4]
.
For
(iii)
,
see
[
Mur83
]
or
[
Mur85
, Theorem 1.9]
, along with
a correction by
[
Kah21
]
. For
(iv)
,
see
[
Fak02
, Theorem 2]
, though notice the misprint
with the dimension statement, which may be corrected by comparing with

[
Mur85
, §7]
.
∎

Returning to the situation of a smooth
q
-bic hypersurface
X
of
dimension
2m+1
, an algebraic representative
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
in
codimension
m+1
is referred to as the
intermediate Jacobian
of

X
. Since
X
is isomorphic to a supersingular Fermat variety, this exists
by
6.10
(iv)
; when
m=1
, so that
X
is a smooth
q
-bic threefold, this also follows from
the general existence result

6.10
(iii)
.

The next statement relates cycles on
X
with those on
\mathbf{F}
via the
Fano correspondence
\mathbf{L}
from
3.4
:

6.11 Lemma
. —

There exists a commutative diagram of abelian groups

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\CH^{m+1}(\mathbf{F})_{\mathrm{alg}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\CH^{m+1}(X)_{\mathrm{alg}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\CH^{1}(\mathbf{F})_{\mathrm{alg}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Ab}^{m+1}_{X}(\mathbf{k})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{k})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{L}_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{m+1}_{\mathbf{F}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{L}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{m+1}_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{1}_{\mathbf{F}}}

and hence morphisms of abelian varieties

\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}_{*}}\mathbf{Ab}^{m+1}_{X}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}^{*}}\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{\mathbf{F},\mathrm{red}}.

Proof.

The action of the Fano correspondence gives the top row of maps, see

3.4
. The vertical maps are the universal
regular homomorphisms recognizing the Albanese variety of
\mathbf{F}
, the
intermediate Jacobian of
X
, and the Picard variety of
\mathbf{F}
as
the algebraic representatives for algebraically trivial, respectively,

0
-cycles of
\mathbf{F}
,
m
-cycles of
X
, and
m
-cycles of

\mathbf{F}
: see
6.10
. The morphisms of the group
schemes arise from the corresponding universal property of each scheme.
∎

Fix a Hermitian
m
-plane
P_{0}\subset X
and consider the Albanese
morphism
\operatorname{alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\colon\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}

centred at
[P_{0}]
. Composing this with the morphisms of abelian schemes
from
6.11
yields a morphism

\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{Pic}_{\mathbf{F}}^{0}
. Its action on
\mathbf{k}
-points is
easily understood:

6.12 Lemma
. —

The morphism
\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{Pic}_{\mathbf{F}}^{0}
acts on
\mathbf{k}
-points by

[P]\mapsto\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P}-D_{P_{0}}).

Proof.

The Albanese morphism on
\mathbf{k}
-points factorizes as

\phi^{m+1}_{\mathbf{F}}\circ\operatorname{alb}_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{k})\colon\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{k})\to\CH^{m+1}(\mathbf{F})_{\mathrm{alg}}\to\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{k})

where the first map is
[P]\mapsto[P]-[P_{0}]
, and the second map is
the universal regular homomorphism from
6.10
(i)
.
The commutative diagram from
6.11

then shows that
\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{\mathbf{F}}
factors through the map

\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{k})\to\mathrm{CH}^{1}(\mathbf{F})_{\mathrm{alg}}
given by

[P]\mapsto\mathbf{L}^{*}\mathbf{L}_{*}([P]-[P_{0}])=[D_{P}]-[D_{P_{0}}],

where the actions of
\mathbf{L}
are as in
3.4
. Composing
with

\phi^{1}_{\mathbf{F}}\colon\CH^{1}(\mathbf{F})_{\mathrm{alg}}\to\mathbf{Pic}_{\mathbf{F}}^{0}(\mathbf{k})

gives the result.
∎

With the fixed Hermitian
m
-plane
P_{0}\subset X
, let
C_{0}\subset\mathbf{F}

be the closed subscheme parameterizing
m
-planes containing a Hermitian

(m-1)
-plane in
P_{0}
; in other words,
C_{0}
is the union of the smooth

q
-bic curves
C_{R}
from
6.7
, where
R
ranges over
all Hermitian
(m-1)
-planes contained in
P_{0}
. Let

C\coloneqq\coprod\nolimits_{R\subset P_{0}}C_{R}

be the disjoint union of the irreducible components of
C
and let

\nu\colon C\to C_{0}
be the normalization morphism. The following relates
the abelian varieties appearing in

6.11
with the Jacobian

\mathbf{Jac}_{C}\coloneqq\prod\nolimits_{R}\mathbf{Jac}_{C_{R}}

of the curve
C
, defined as the product of the Jacobians of its connected
components:

6.13 Proposition
. —

The composite morphism

\Phi\colon\mathbf{Jac}_{C}\xrightarrow{\nu_{*}}\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}_{*}}\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{L}^{*}}\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{\mathbf{F},\mathrm{red}}\xrightarrow{\nu^{*}}\mathbf{Jac}_{C}

is multiplication by
q^{2m}
.

Proof.

Consider a
\mathbf{k}
-point of a connected component
C_{R}
of
C
and identify
it with a
\mathbf{k}
-point
[P]
of its image in
\mathbf{F}
. Let

\mathrm{alb}_{C}\colon C\to\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
be the Albanese map of
C
,
which on the connected component
C_{R}
, is the usual Abel–Jacobi map
centred at
[P_{0}]
into the
R
-th factor of
\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
and
the constant map onto the identity otherwise. Combined with

6.12
, this means that the map

\Phi\circ\mathrm{alb}_{C}\colon C\to\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
acts as

\Phi(\mathrm{alb}_{C}([P]))=\nu^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P}-D_{P_{0}}).

Since the image of
C
under
\mathrm{alb}_{C}
generates
\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
,
that
\Phi
is multiplication by
q^{2m}
will follow upon showing that,
for each Hermitian
(m-1)
-plane
R^{\prime}\subset P_{0}
,

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P}-D_{P_{0}})\rvert_{C_{R^{\prime}}}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{C_{R^{\prime}}}&if $R^{\prime}\neq R$, and\\
\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}([P]-[P_{0}])^{\otimes q^{2m}}&if $R^{\prime}=R$.\end{cases}

Note that
6.8
implies

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P_{0}})\rvert_{C_{R^{\prime}}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{C_{R^{\prime}}}([P_{0}])
for all
R^{\prime}
.
When
R^{\prime}\neq R
, since
P\cap R^{\prime}\subsetneq P\cap P_{0}
, applying

6.8
once more gives the conclusion. When
R^{\prime}=R
,

6.7
gives

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{F}}(D_{P})\rvert_{C_{R}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}([P])^{\otimes q^{2m}}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}}\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}(-1)^{\otimes(q^{2m}-1)/(q+1)}.

The conclusion now follows upon using the fact

\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}(1)\cong\mathcal{O}_{C_{R}}([P_{0}])^{\otimes q+1}
from

3.8
.
∎

Putting everything together shows that each of the abelian varieties in
question are related to one another via purely inseparable isogenies:

6.14 Theorem
. —

Each of the morphisms of abelian varieties

\nu_{*}\colon\mathbf{Jac}_{C}\to\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}},\quad\mathbf{L}_{*}\colon\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1},\quad\mathbf{L}^{*}\colon\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}\to\mathbf{Pic}_{\mathbf{F},\mathrm{red}}^{0},\quad\nu^{*}\colon\mathbf{Pic}^{0}_{\mathbf{F},\mathrm{red}}\to\mathbf{Jac}_{C}

is a purely inseparable
p
-power isogeny.

Proof.

Each of the abelian varieties
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
,
\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}
,

\mathbf{Pic}_{\mathbf{F},\mathrm{red}}^{0}
, and
\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
are
of dimension

\frac{1}{2}\bar{q}[2m+2]_{\bar{q}}=\frac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}}\mathrm{H}^{2m+1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(X,\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})=\frac{1}{2}\dim_{\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}}\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell}).

This is

6.10
(iv)

for
\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{m+1}
; this follows from
5.13
for
the Picard and Albanese varieties of
\mathbf{F}
; and, for the Jacobian, compute:

\dim\mathbf{Jac}_{C}=\dim\mathbf{Jac}_{C_{R}}\cdot\#\check{\mathbf{P}}^{m}(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}})=\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{q^{2m+2}-1}{q^{2}-1}=\frac{1}{2}q\cdot\frac{q^{2m+2}-1}{q+1}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{q}[2m+2]_{\bar{q}}.

Since
\Phi
is an
p
-power isogeny by
6.13
, it
follows that each of the maps factoring
\Phi
are themselves
p
-power
isogenies. To see that each are purely inseparable, note that the smooth

q
-bic curves are supersingular: this was known to Weil, as in

[
Wei49
,
Wei52
]
, though see also
[
SK79
, §3]
.
Thus
\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
, being the product of Jacobians of smooth
q
-bic
curves, is itself supersingular. Whence multiplication by
q^{2m}
is purely
inseparable, and so each of the constituent morphisms are as well.
∎

I feel that the case
m=1
for
q
-bic threefolds is best understood in
analogy with cubic threefolds over the complex numbers: that the
maps in
6.13
are purely inseparable isogenies means
that they are close to being isomorphisms; the statement about
\mathbf{L}_{*}\colon\mathbf{Alb}_{\mathbf{F}}\to\mathbf{Ab}_{X}^{2}
is analogous to the result of
Clemens and Griffiths in
[
CG72
, Theorem 11.19]
saying that the Abel–Jacobi
map from the Albanese of the surface of lines on a complex cubic threefold is
an isomorphism onto the Hodge-theoretic intermediate Jacobian of the
hypersurface; the statement regarding

\nu_{*}\colon\mathbf{Jac}_{C}\to\mathbf{Alb}_{S}
is an analogue of Mumford’s
identification between the Albanese of the Fano surface with a
Prym variety in
[
CG72
, Appedix C]
; and the statement regarding

\nu^{*}\circ\mathbf{L}^{*}\colon\mathbf{Ab}^{2}_{X}\to\mathbf{Jac}_{C}
may be
seen as an analogue of Murre’s identification in
[
Mur72
, Theorem
10.8]
between the group of algebraically trivial
1
-cycles on a
cubic and the Prym. The latter two analogies will be explained further in
future work.
