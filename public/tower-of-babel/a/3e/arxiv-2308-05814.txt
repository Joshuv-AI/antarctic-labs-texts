Randomized low-rank approximations beyond Gaussian random matrices Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Arvind K. Saibaba, Agnieszka Międlar

Abstract
This paper expands the analysis of randomized low-rank approximation beyond the Gaussian distribution to four classes of random matrices: (1) independent sub-Gaussian entries, (2) independent sub-Gaussian columns, (3) independent bounded columns, and (4) independent columns with bounded second moment. Using a novel interpretation of the low-rank approximation error involving sample covariance matrices, we provide insight into the requirements of a good random matrix for the purpose of randomized low-rank approximation. Although our bounds involve unspecified absolute constants (a consequence of underlying non-asymptotic theory of random matrices), they allow for qualitative comparisons across distributions. The analysis offers some details on the minimal number of samples (the number of columns ℓ \ell of the random matrix 𝛀 \mathbf{\Omega} ) and the error in the resulting low-rank approximation. We illustrate our analysis in the context of the randomized subspace iteration method as a representative algorithm for low-rank approximation, however, all the results are broadly applicable to other low-rank approximation techniques. We conclude our discussion with numerical examples using both synthetic and real-world test matrices.

Introduction

Randomized methods have been providing a powerful framework for scientific computations. They are easy to implement, computationally efficient, numerically robust, highly scalable, and very reliable in a vast number of practical applications, making them a game-changing tool in scientific computing and data analysis. Randomized algorithms have already left their footprint in numerical linear algebra (NLA) (see, e.g., the survey articles
[
17
,
21
,
40
,
22
,
24
]
). In this paper, we focus our attention on randomized algorithms for low-rank approximations. Given an
m\times n
matrix
\mathbf{A}
the task is to find an
m\times k
matrix
\mathbf{B}
and a
k\times n
matrix
\mathbf{C}
such that

\mathbf{A}\approx\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}.

There are several algorithms to accomplish this such as randomized subspace iteration, Nyström, and block Krylov-based methods. However, the current state-of-the-art in these approaches is to focus the analysis primarily on standard Gaussian random matrices or other special cases. There are several reasons for this. The analysis for Gaussian random matrices uses well-known results from random matrix theory where the estimates are sharp and all the constants are fully specified. This makes it possible to derive quantitative error bounds as well as practical recommendations for the selection of parameters (e.g., oversampling parameters). From a computational perspective, generating Gaussian random matrices has a fixed cost for each entry of the random matrix. However, for very large matrices generating and storing Gaussian random matrices can be prohibitively expensive.

This computational issue is particularly relevant for randomized low-rank tensor decompositions. To illustrate this, consider an order
d
tensor of size
n\times\dots\times n
. A prototypical algorithm — the randomized higher-order singular value decomposition — applies the randomized SVD to
d
different mode-unfoldings each of size
n\times n^{d-1}
. It requires generating random matrices of size
n^{d-1}\times r
, where
r
is the target rank, which can be infeasible even for modest values of
n
and
d
. In such situations, current research is exploring random matrices that are structured such as subsampled trigonometric transforms, e.g., subsampled randomized Hadamard transform (SRHT)
[
33
]
, random tensor products
[
8
,
23
]
, and random tensor-train
[
3
]
, to name just a few. In these examples, the random matrices are not constructed explicitly, so the cost of forming and storing the matrices is much cheaper, and additionally in some circumstances the application of the matrix can be performed efficiently.

Therefore, there is a clear need for exploring non-Gaussian random matrices. An ideal random matrix is one that should be easy to construct, store, obtain the sketch, and yields comparable errors (in theory and practice) to the Gaussian case. There are several gaps in the analysis of low-rank approximations for non-Gaussian random matrices. First, in previous work, the analysis typically handles specific random matrices and not classes of random matrices. Second, for many random matrices, there is no analysis at all, but numerical evidence suggests behavior similar to Gaussian random matrices. Third, the lack of a unified perspective makes it difficult to compare different random matrices on the same footing. Finally, the lack of understanding of the necessary and sufficient conditions of a random matrix makes it difficult to construct new random matrices with favorable properties. With recent advances in non-asymptotic random matrix theory
[
14
,
37
,
32
,
38
,
34
]
, we develop analysis for randomized algorithms that use non-Gaussian random matrices.

To address these gaps, we provide analysis and numerical experiments for several families of random matrices
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
: (i) independent sub-Gaussian entries, (ii) independent sub-Gaussian columns, (iii) independent bounded columns, (iv) independent columns with bounded second moment. Our analysis provides a way forward for systematic comparison and tools for analyzing new random matrices that fit within one of the four classes of random matrices. The focus of this paper is on the analysis and
not
on the computational issues such as the cost of constructing, storing, and computing with the random matrices. We summarize the main contributions and features of our work.

Contributions and Outline

In this paper, we expand the analysis of randomized low-rank approximations to account for many distributions. Our goal is to answer the question: What are the characteristics of a good random matrix distribution for use in randomized low-rank approximations? The specific contributions and features of our work are as follows:

1.

We give a novel interpretation of the error in the low-rank approximation (Section
3.1
) using sample covariance approximations that provide insight into the requirements of a random matrix.

2.

We give sufficient conditions for four classes of random matrices: (1) independent sub-Gaussian entries (
theorem

5
), (2) independent sub-Gaussian columns (
theorem

7
), (3) independent bounded columns (
theorem

9
), and (4) independent columns with bounded second moment (
theorem

11
).

3.

We illustrate our analysis for the randomized subspace iteration which is a representative algorithm for low-rank approximation, but our analysis is more broadly applicable to other algorithms such as Nyström, block-Krylov, eigenvalue approximations, etc.

4.

The two metrics that will be used for comparison purposes are the minimal number of samples required (i.e., the number
\ell
of columns of the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}
) and the error in the resulting low-rank approximations.

5.

Our analysis has an emphasis on developing bounds that do not explicitly depend on the size of the matrix (by the use of the concept of stable rank) which makes it applicable to large-scale problems. Our bounds involve unspecified absolute constants (since they are based on results for which the constants are not optimal or unavailable). These bounds still allow for a qualitative comparison between different random matrices.

6.

The novel results provide analytical justification for many distributions of random matrices. Specifically, they unify several existing results (for individual distributions) and greatly expand the types of distributions that can be used in low-rank computations.

7.

We present numerical experiments (Section
5
) on several test matrices and for several distributions of random matrices, many of which are unexplored in the context of low-rank approximations.

We point out that
the main results are clearly presented and discussed in Sections

Previous work and Applications of presented results

Our analysis is not only relevant for the randomized subspace iteration but can be applied more broadly to a host of algorithms for low-rank approximation such as Nyström, subspace iteration, block-Krylov, single view, and streaming algorithms. Beyond low-rank approximation, presented results can be used in the error analysis of Hermitian and generalized Hermitian eigenvalue problems. Random matrices not covered

Our analysis, while extensive, is not suitable for many random matrices, as we now explain. All presented results require at the very least finite second moments, which excludes distributions such as the Cauchy distribution.
Furthermore, our analysis does not cover random matrices with independent rows, even if they are sub-Gaussian. The main difficulty in this case comes from the fact that if the random matrix

Background

In this section, we collect the necessary background information on sub-Gaussian random variables and vectors (Section
2.1
), and randomized algorithms for low-rank approximation (Sections
2.2
and
2.3
). A word on constants: In the discussion below, we denote by
c,C,C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
, etc. absolute constants. These constants do not depend on the spectrum of the matrix or the distribution of the random variables. We cannot specify these constants explicitly since our analysis depends on non-asymptotic matrix theory results for which these constants are either not specified or sometimes unknown. However, in the following, we do our best to track the dependence on all constants involved.

2.1
Sub-Gaussian distributions

In this section, we review the definitions of a sub-Gaussian distribution and the various other distributions that fit into the family of sub-Gaussian distributions. This discussion is closely tied to
[
37
,
38
]
. The sub-Gaussian distribution is a rich class of probability distributions whose tails decay as sharply as Gaussian random variables. Besides Gaussian random variables, it contains distributions such as:

1.

Rademacher
: The random variable takes the value
\pm 1
with probability
1/2
each. This is a centralized (symmetric) version of the Bernoulli random variable.

2.

Sparse Rademacher
: This is a distribution parameterized by a sparsity parameter
s\geq 1
. The random variables take the values
\displaystyle\{-\sqrt{s},0,\sqrt{s}\}
with probabilities respectively
\displaystyle\{1/(2s),1-1/s,1/(2s)\}
. Note that
s=1
gives the Rademacher distribution.

3.

Uniform
: The random variable is uniform in the interval
\displaystyle[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]
, with mean zero and unit variance (denoted
\mathcal{U}([-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}])
).

4.

Bounded
: The random variable
X
is such that
|X|\leq M
almost surely, where
M
is finite. It generalizes the earlier listed random variables.

The following collects various definitions involving sub-Gaussian random variables; see
[
38
, Chapters 2 and 3]
.
We say that a random variable
X
is
sub-Gaussian
if
\mathbb{P}\left\{|X|\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/\sigma^{2})
for all
t\geq 0
and finite
\sigma
. There are other ways of defining the sub-Gaussian random variables using the equivalence in
[
38
, Proposition 2.5.2]
. The sub-Gaussian norm of the random variable is defined as

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\mathsf{inf}\{t>0\ |\ \mathbb{E}\left[\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})\right]\leq 2\}.

(1)

For the distributions listed above, the sub-Gaussian norms are respectively
\mathcal{O}(1)
(Rademacher),
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s})
(sparse Rademacher), and
\mathcal{O}(M)
(bounded random variables
|X|\leq M
almost surely). Although it is natural to consider random matrices with independent sub-Gaussian entries (see
definition

4
), in some cases, the entries of the random matrix may not be independent.

To this end, we first define random sub-Gaussian vectors. A random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be sub-Gaussian if
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
is
sub-Gaussian
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence, the sub-Gaussian norm of
\mathbf{x}
is defined as
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}
, where
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1\}
is the unit Euclidean sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
isotropic
if
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{xx}^{\top}\right]=\mathbf{I}
; alternatively, it is isotropic if
\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle^{2}\right]=\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}\rangle^{2}
for every
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The following random vectors are examples that we will consider in this work (see
[
38
, Chapter 3]
for more discussion):

1.

Spherical
: A random vector
\mathbf{x}
which is uniformly distributed on the Euclidean sphere of radius
\sqrt{n}
and centered at the origin, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}\sim\mbox{Unif}(\sqrt{n}\mathcal{S}^{n-1})
. To generate a random vector from this distribution, take a random Gaussian vector
\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and compute
\mathbf{x}=\sqrt{n}\mathbf{g}/\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}
. Then
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{Unif}(\sqrt{n}\mathcal{S}^{n-1})
and
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C
, where
C
is an absolute constant; see
[
38
, Theorem 3.4.6]
.

2.

Random orthogonal
: The vectors are selected uniformly from the set
\{\sqrt{n}\mathbf{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\mathbf{q}_{i}
are the columns of an orthogonal matrix. This is a special case of the random frame distribution, in which the vectors form a tight frame with equal norms. Another special case is the coordinate distribution where
\{\mathbf{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the columns of the identity matrix.

We will also consider random matrices with independent sub-Gaussian columns (see
definition

6
), independent columns with bounded norms (see
definition

8
)
and independent columns with bounded second moments (see
definition

10
).

2.2
Randomized SVD

Let us briefly describe how to determine a low-rank approximation of the matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},m\geq n
, using a randomized method, i.e., randomized subspace iteration algorithm.

Given a random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
, we carry out
q
steps of the randomized subspace iteration method to obtain the matrix
\mathbf{Y}
, also known as the
sketch
of the input matrix
\mathbf{A}
. Then, we perform a thin-
QR
factorization of sketch
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}
to obtain
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}

whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the range of
\mathbf{Y}
. The main idea is that, under suitable conditions
[
17
]
, the range of
\mathbf{Q}
is a good approximation for the range of
\mathbf{A}
. Finally, we obtain a low-rank approximation of
\mathbf{A}
by the projection
\mathbf{QQ}^{T}\mathbf{A}
. The rest of the algorithm involves converting this low-rank approximation into the SVD format.

2.3
Setup for the analysis

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\geq n
has a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the form

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U\Sigma V}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{k}&\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}\\
&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where
1\leq k\leq\mathsf{rank}\,(\mathbf{A})
.
Here,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-k)\times(n-k)}
, the columns of
\mathbf{U}_{k}
and
\mathbf{U}_{\perp}
are the corresponding left singular vectors, and columns of
\mathbf{V}_{k}
and
\mathbf{V}_{\perp}
are the corresponding right singular vectors.
The singular values
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A}),1\leq i\leq n
, of
\mathbf{A}
are arranged in decreasing order such that the diagonal elements of matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{k}
are the dominant singular values of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. We assume that there is a gap in the singular values at index
k
, i.e.,

\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A})\;\geq\;\sigma_{2}(\mathbf{A})\;\geq\cdots\;\geq\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{A})\;>\;\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A})\;\geq\cdots\;\geq\;\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{A})\geq 0,

and we define the singular value ratios

\gamma_{j}:=\frac{\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A})}{\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{A})},\qquad 1\leq j\leq k.

(2)

With the singular values monotonically decreasing, the singular value ratios satisfy
\gamma_{1}\leq\dots\leq\gamma_{k}<1
. Next we define the
stable rank
of a nonzero matrix
\mathbf{A}
as
\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{A}):={\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}}/{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}^{2}}
.
The stable rank is a proxy for the rank of the matrix and it satisfies the following inequality
1\leq\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{A})\leq\mathsf{rank}\,(\mathbf{A})
.

We partition the matrix
\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
into two parts, i.e.,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}:=\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\ell}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}:=\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times\ell},

(3)

where numbers
k
and
\ell
satisfy

k\leq\ell\leq n
.
We consider a bound for the structural analysis of the subspace iteration method. The following result quantifies the error in the low-rank approximation.

Theorem 1
(Structural bound).

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
m\geq n
and
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\ell}
be the output of
Algorithm

1
with the number of subspace iteration steps
q
. Then

\|(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{QQ}^{\top})\mathbf{A}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{4q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

See
[
29
, Theorem 8]
.

The interpretation of this theorem is that the error in the low-rank approximation is governed by the tail of the singular values in matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}
. The second term is small if the gap
1/\gamma_{k}
is large or the number of subspace iterations steps
q
is large. The influence of the random matrix (also starting guess) is captured in the term
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}
. In Section
3.1
, we provide a novel interpretation of this term that is useful in the upcoming analysis.

Terms related to
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}
repeatedly appear in the analysis of randomized low-rank approximations: randomized SVD low-rank approximation (
[
17
, Theorem 9.1]
), accuracy of singular values and singular vectors

, Nyström
[
36

, block Krylov subspace methods (
[

3
Main results

We first present a novel interpretation of the term
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}
using sample covariance matrices. Next, we derive bounds for this term and the low-rank approximations for each of the four classes of random matrices that are considered in this paper. The bounds are illustrated for a few example distributions, but other examples are provided in the Supplementary Materials (
appendix

B
).

3.1
Connection to covariance estimation

We give an intuitive explanation of the sufficient conditions required of a random matrix by drawing connections to the covariance estimation problem. Consider random vectors
\mathbf{x}_{j}
with zero mean and covariance
\mathbf{\Gamma}
. Given independent draws
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}
, define the sample covariance matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}.

It is easy to verify that
\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}
is an unbiased estimator for the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Gamma}
. A natural question is to find the number of samples
N
such that the relative error for the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}
satisfies

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}-\mathbf{\Gamma}\|_{2}\leq\eta\|\mathbf{\Gamma}\|_{2},

with high probability. This question has a rich history in statistics, see
[
37
, Section 5.4.3]
and
[
38
, Chapter 5.6]
.

To see the connection with the covariance estimation problem, we consider the following lemma and, for the moment, deterministic
\mathbf{\Omega}
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}
and

\|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}\leq\eta\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\|\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,

(4)

for
\eta>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{1-\|\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}}\leq\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\frac{1+\eta}{1-\varepsilon}.

Proof.

See Section
4.1
.

Now assume that the columns of
\mathbf{\Omega}
are independent, zero mean, and isotropic. Therefore, by the linearity of expectations,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}\right]=\ell\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}\right]=\ell\mathbf{I}.

The terms
\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}
and
\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}
are sample covariance matrices for
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}
and the
k\times k
identity matrix, respectively. Then,
\|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}
and
\|\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}
represent the relative error in the sample covariance approximations. We want to find the minimal number of samples
\ell
such that the two bounds in (
4
) hold simultaneously with high probability. As we show, for many distributions
\ell=\mathcal{O}(k)
samples are sufficient, whereas for other distributions
\mathcal{O}(k\log k)
samples are necessary. Furthermore, this connection to covariance estimation gives insight into why all our assumptions require the columns to be independent, zero mean, and isotropic.

3.2
Independent sub-Gaussian entries

In this section, we derive a result analogous to
lemma

2
for standard sub-Gaussian random matrices with independent entries. We define a model of the random matrix to be analyzed and use it to introduce the main result.

Definition 4
(Independent sub-Gaussian entries).

In this model, we consider the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
with the following properties:

1.

Independence: the entries of
\mathbf{\Omega}
are independent copies of the sub-Gaussian random variable
X
.

2.

Normalization: the random variable
X
has zero mean and unit variance.

3.

Boundedness: the random variable
X
satisfies
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{\rm E}
with
K_{E}>0
.

Theorem 5
(Independent sub-Gaussian entries).

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix satisfying
definition

4
. Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be user-specified parameters, and let the number of samples satisfy

n\geq\ell=\frac{C_{\rm ES}K_{\rm E}^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K_{E}^{4}k),

(5)

where
V_{\delta}:=\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)}
.
Then, with a probability of failure at most
\delta
, we have

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\rm EB}K_{\rm E}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}}\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}+\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}}\right).

If
\mathbf{\Omega}
is an input to
algorithm

1
, then with a probability of failure at most
\delta
,

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C_{\rm EB}^{2}K_{\rm E}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{4q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}{\ell}}\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}+\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}}\right)^{2}.

Here,
C_{\rm ES}
and
C_{\rm EB}
are absolute constants.

Proof.

See Section
4.2
.

The result of
Theorem

5
is remarkable in the sense that it is applicable to any sub-Gaussian random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
as long as it comes from the model presented in
Definition

4
. It also predicts that the number of required samples
\ell
is
\mathcal{O}(k)
. If
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
is a standard Gaussian random matrix, then
K_{\rm E}
is an absolute constant. This result is comparable with
[
17
, Theorem 10.8]
up to unspecified constants;
theorem

5
has a better dependence on the failure probability.

We discuss the implications for the other distributions mentioned in Section
2.1
. Consider the case when
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
has i.i.d entries from the sparse Rademacher distribution with sparsity parameter
s
. The entries have a sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
K_{\rm E}=\sqrt{s}
, so the required number of samples is
\mathcal{O}(s^{2}k)
. Thus, the larger the sparsity parameter
s
, the more samples are required. In particular, if
s=1
(Rademacher distribution), then the required number of samples is
\mathcal{O}(k)
. Our result is an improvement on
[
29
]
, which suggests
\mathcal{O}(k\log n)
samples are required and which appears to overestimate the required number of samples by a factor of
\log n
. Similarly, the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]
also requires
\mathcal{O}(k)
samples.

3.3
Independent sub-Gaussian columns

The next result is applicable to sub-Gaussian matrices with a weaker assumption, namely, the individual entries do not need to be independent, only the columns need to be independent. We summarize this random matrix model in the following definition.

Definition 6
(Independent sub-Gaussian columns).

In this model, we consider the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
with the following properties:

1.

Independence: the columns of matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
are independent copies of the sub-Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

Normalization: the random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is isotropic.

3.

Boundedness: the random vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{C}
, with
K_{C}>0
.

Theorem 7
(Independent sub-Gaussian columns).

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix satisfying
definition

6
. Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be user-defined parameters, and let the number of samples satisfy

n\geq\ell=\frac{C_{\rm CS}K_{C}^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(K_{C}^{4}k).

(6)

Then, with a probability of failure at most
\delta
, we have

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}}\left(\sqrt{\ell}+C_{\rm CB}K_{\rm C}^{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}+{V_{\delta}})\right).

If
\mathbf{\Omega}
is an input to
algorithm

1
, then with a probability of failure at most
\delta
,

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{k}^{4q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\ell}\left(\sqrt{\ell}+C_{\rm CB}K_{\rm C}^{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}+{V_{\delta}})\right)^{2}.

Here,
C_{\rm CS}
and
C_{\rm CB}
are absolute constants.

Proof.

See Section
4.3
.

The interpretation of the result of
theorem

7
is very similar to that of
Theorem

5
. The two results are comparable since a random matrix with independent entries has also independent columns, however, the converse does not need to be true. A direct comparison though is not possible, since the precise constants are not explicitly known but the two results differ in their dependence on the sub-Gaussian norms.

Beyond independent entries, we now consider a few other special cases. If the columns of
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
are i.i.d spherical Gaussian random vectors, or if they are drawn from certain isotropic convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(called
\psi_{2}
bodies,
[
37
, Section 5.2.5]
), the sub-Gaussian norms
K_{C}
are absolute constants. In both cases, the number of required samples is
\mathcal{O}(k)
. In Supplementary Materials (Section
B.2
) we investigate another distribution called sparse sign matrix.

We briefly discuss the coordinate distribution. Although the coordinate distribution also has independent sub-Gaussian columns, the conclusions of
theorem

7
are very pessimistic since the random vectors have large sub-Gaussian norms, i.e.,
K_{C}=\sqrt{n}
. We address this issue next using a different technique.

3.4
Independent bounded columns

We now consider the case that the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
has independent columns that are not necessarily sub-Gaussian. We, however, require the columns to be bounded almost surely.

Definition 8
(Independent bounded columns).

In this model, we consider the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
with the following properties:

1.

Independence: the columns of the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
are independent copies of a random vector.

2.

Normalization: each column is isotropic.

3.

Boundedness: the columns satisfy

\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq K_{k},\qquad\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq K_{\perp},\quad K_{k}>0,

almost surely.

Theorem 9
(Independent bounded columns).

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix satisfying
definition

8
with
K_{\perp}\geq 1
. Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be user-defined parameters, and suppose that the number of samples satisfies

n\geq\ell=\frac{2K_{k}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log(4k/\delta)=\mathcal{O}(K_{k}^{2}\log(k)).

(7)

Then, with a probability of failure at most
\delta
, we have

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{(1-\varepsilon)\ell}}\left(3\ell+K_{\perp}^{2}/3\log({16\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}/{\delta})\right)^{1/2}.

If
\mathbf{\Omega}
is an input to
algorithm

1
, then with a probability of failure at most
\delta
,

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{k}^{4q}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\ell}\left(3\ell+K_{\perp}^{2}\log({16\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp})}/{\delta})\right).

Proof.

See Section
4.4
.

A nice feature of
theorem

9
is that, unlike
theorems

5
and
7
, all the constants involved are known explicitly. However, care should be taken while applying these results since the parameters
K_{k}
and
K_{\perp}
, although may depend on the dimensions of the problem, are independent of the spectrum of the matrix. To illustrate the result of
Theorem

9
, we consider the coordinate distribution (Section
B.3
) and leverage score sampling.

Leverage score distribution

To address the issue with the coordinate distribution, a remedy proposed in the literature was to use an importance sampling approach based on the squared row norms of
\mathbf{V}_{k}
, known as the (subspace) leverage scores

p_{i}^{\rm lev}\equiv\frac{\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{k}\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(8)

It is easy to verify that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\rm lev}=1
, so the leverage scores define a discrete probability measure. Consider another probability measure that satisfies
p_{i}\geq\gamma p_{i}^{\rm lev}>0
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and construct the columns of the random matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
as

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell p_{t_{1}}}}\mathbf{e}_{t_{1}}&\dots&\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell p_{t_{\ell}}}}\mathbf{e}_{t_{\ell}}\end{bmatrix},

where
t_{j}
are drawn uniformly at random from
\{1,\dots,n\}
with replacement. Then it follows that

\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq K_{k}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{k}{\gamma}}\qquad\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq K_{\perp}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{\ell\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}{p_{j}}}}.

By
theorem

9
, the number of required samples is
\ell=\mathcal{O}(k\log k)
. However, computing the leverage scores is, in general, computationally challenging and there is a trade-off between the good features of leverage scores versus the computational cost in obtaining them; see
[
22
, Section 9.6.4]
.

The main takeaway is that the number of samples has a logarithmic dependence on the target rank
k
. In general, this condition is necessary and is related to the coupon collector problem (see
[
17
, Remark 11.2]
and
[
37
, Sections 5.5–5.6]
).

3.5

Definition 10
.

In this model, we consider the random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}

1.

Independence: the columns of the random matrix

3.

Boundedness: the columns satisfy

where

and

This is the most general class of random matrices we consider since it subsumes

4
,
6

Theorem 11
(Independent columns with bounded second moment).

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix satisfying
Definition

10
.
Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
be user-defined parameters. Suppose that the number of samples satisfies

Then, with a probability of failure at most

\mathbf{\Omega}
is an input to

1

Here,

is an absolute constant.

Proof.

See Section
4.5
.

By the discussion before the statement of
Theorem

11
, we see that this result is applicable to a large class of random matrices. However, compared to the previous results, this has a worse dependence on the failure probability
\delta
. In the following discussion, we explore a class of random matrices that satisfies
definition

10
and for which
theorem

11
therefore applies. We discuss a class of random matrices constructed using random variables whose tails can decay faster than sub-exponential. In Supplementary Materials (Section
B.4
), we also discuss two classes of distributions called uniform sampling from a convex set and log-concave distributions.

\alpha
-sub-exponential

A random variable
X
is said to be
\alpha-
sub-exponential
[
16
,
30
]
with parameter
0<\alpha\leq 2
if

\mathbb{P}\left\{|X|\geq t\right\}\leq c\exp(-Ct^{\alpha}),\qquad t\geq 0,

where
c,C
are constants. Equivalently, this can be expressed in terms of the exponential Orlicz quasi-norm, i.e.,
\|X\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}:=\{\inf t>0\ |\ \mathbb{E}\left[\exp(|X|^{\alpha}/t^{\alpha})\right]\leq 2\}
.
Strictly speaking, it is only a norm for
\alpha\geq 1
.
It generalizes the class of sub-Gaussian (
\alpha=2
), sub-exponential (
\alpha=1
), sub-Weibull
\alpha\in(0,1]
(that have heavy tails), and bounded (
\alpha>0)
random variables. We provide a result for the minimal number of samples required for this class of random matrices.

Corollary 12
.

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a matrix with independent copies of a random variable
X
drawn from the
\alpha-
sub-exponential class with zero mean, unit variance and
\|X\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}\leq M
with
n\geq 3
and
M\geq 1
.
With the rest of the setup in
theorem

11
, the minimal number of samples for this model satisfies

\ell=\mathcal{O}((k+M^{2}(\sqrt{k\log n}+(2/\alpha)^{2/\alpha}))\log k).

Proof.

See Supplementary materials (Section
B.4
).

This result extends the analysis to a wide class of distributions. The sub-exponential family includes the exponential, Laplace, Logistic, chi-squared, and Poisson distributions. However, to use
theorem

11
, we have to center them appropriately to ensure zero mean and unit variance.

Proof of

From the proof of

Proof of

3

By submultiplicativity
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}
, so we focus on each term separately.
Consider
\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}

\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}\|_{2}

\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\ell\left(\|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{ZZ}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\leq\ell\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}(1+\eta).

Next, consider
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

4

|\frac{1}{\ell}\sigma_{k}^{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})-1|\leq\|\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon<1.

Therefore,
\ell(1-\varepsilon)\leq\sigma_{k}^{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})\leq\ell(1+\varepsilon)

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}=1/\sigma_{k}^{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})\leq\frac{\ell^{-1}}{1-\|\frac{1}{\ell}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{{(1-\varepsilon)\ell}}.

Combining intermediate results yields the desired bound.

4.2
Independent sub-Gaussian entries

To establish the result of Theorem
5
, we need to prove the following result on the norm of a sub-Gaussian random matrix, which may be of independent interest.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a random matrix with i.i.d sub-Gaussian entries with zero mean and sub-Gaussian norm
K:=\max\limits_{ij}\|s_{ij}\|_{\psi_{2}}
. Then for any
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times m}
and
\boldsymbol{N}\in R^{n\times N}

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{MSN}\|_{2}\right]\leq C_{SNE}K\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{N}\|_{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{M})}+\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{N})}).

(9)

Furthermore, for every
u\geq 0

\|\boldsymbol{MSN}\|_{2}\leq C_{SNT}K\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{N}\|_{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{M})}+\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{N})}+u),

(10)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u^{2})
. Here
C_{SNE}
and
C_{SNT}
are absolute constants.

The proof of this theorem is an application of Talagrand’s comparison inequality which we recall here and is a special case of the sub-Gaussian Chevet theorem
[
38
, Theorem 8.7.1]
. However, in the Supplementary Materials (Section
A.3
) we give some additional details and track the constants from Talagrand’s comparison inequality. We will also need the following result which is a two-sided bound on the singular values of sub-Gaussian random matrices.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
whose rows
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{S}
are independent, mean zero, isotropic, sub-Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
. Then, for any
t\geq 0
, we have

\sqrt{m}-C_{T}K^{2}(\sqrt{n}+t)\leq\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{S})\leq\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{S})\leq\sqrt{m}-C_{T}K^{2}(\sqrt{n}+t).

Proof.

See
[
37
, Theorem 4.6.1.]
.

With the results of
Theorem

13
at hand, we are ready to prove
Theorem

5
.

Proof of
theorem

5
.

We will carry out the proof in three steps:

Step 1. Bounding
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

Since the columns of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}=\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\ell}
are orthonormal, they
are also independent isotropic random vectors. Furthermore, by
Lemma

19
their sub-Gaussian norm satisfy
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{C_{R}}K_{E}
for
1\leq j\leq\ell
. Applying
Theorem

14
to matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}^{\top}
gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sqrt{\ell}-C_{T}C_{R}K_{E}^{2}(\sqrt{k}+t)\leq\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})\right\}\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}).

Setting
\delta/2=2\exp(-t^{2})
yields
t=V_{\delta}=\sqrt{\log\frac{4}{\delta}}
. Hence, with a probability of failure at most
\delta/2

\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})\geq\sqrt{\ell}-C_{ES}K_{E}^{2}(\sqrt{k}+V_{\delta}),

with
C_{ES}:=C_{T}C_{R}
.
Now, take
\sqrt{\ell}=C_{ES}K_{E}^{2}(\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}})/\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}(K_{E}^{2}\sqrt{k})
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,
\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{1})>0
and

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1})}\leq\frac{1}{(\varepsilon^{-1}-1)C_{ES}K_{E}^{2}(\sqrt{k}+{V_{\delta}})}=\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}},

which gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}>\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}}\right\}\leq{\frac{\delta}{2}}.

(11)

Step 2. Bounding
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}

Let
E
denote the event that
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}}
; from Step 1,
\mathbb{P}\left\{E^{c}\right\}\leq\delta/2
. Condition on the event
E
and apply
Theorem

13
with
\boldsymbol{M}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{\Omega}
and
\boldsymbol{N}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}
, where
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}
. Note that this theorem applies since
\mathbf{\Omega}
has i.i.d mean zero sub-Gaussian random variables and

\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}\|_{2}

Write
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}

have zero mean, so by the linearity of expectations,
\mathbf{Y}
also has zero mean. Next, by the triangle inequality and the assumption on
\mathbf{\Omega}
and
K_{\perp}

\|\mathbf{X}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\ell}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}(K_{\perp}^{2}+1)\leq\frac{2K_{\perp}^{2}}{\ell}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}=:L.

For the variance term, once again by the linearity of expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\mathbf{Y}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}_{j}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{z}_{j}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{z}_{j}\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\top})^{2}\right]-(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\left[K_{\perp}^{2}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}\right]\preceq\frac{K_{\perp}^{2}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{2}}{\ell}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp}^{\top}=:\mathbf{V}.

and

Therefore, by matrix Bernstein inequality
[
34
, Theorem 7.7.1]
for

Note that when using
[

setting
\delta/2=8d\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{\nu+Lt/3}\right)

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we provide analysis for randomized low-rank approximations for four different classes of random matrices. The analysis is based on the connection between low-rank approximations to sample covariance matrices, and using results from non-asymptotic random matrix theory. The analysis focuses on the minimal number of samples and the error in the low-rank approximation using randomized subspace iteration. The results are more generally applicable to other low-rank approximation algorithms. Of the four classes of random matrices discussed,
definition

10
is the most general only requiring independent, isotropic columns, with bounded second moment and provides theoretical guarantees for a truly wide range of random matrices. Numerical results on a range of test matrices show that in practice the approximation errors are comparable across different distributions.

There are several avenues for future work. In ongoing work, we are developing results for random matrices using tensor random projections. We end this paper with several additional questions worth considering:

1.

Can we find explicit and optimal constants for the theorems presented in this work?

2.

It is known that

samples are required for certain distributions; therefore, a general bound must require
\mathcal{O}(k\log k)
. Can this be improved to
\mathcal{O}(k)
for certain distributions? We believe that it can be improved to
\mathcal{O}(k)
for uniform sampling from a convex set and log-concave distributions following
[
2
,
31
]
.

3.

Can we construct random matrices that are easy to generate and store and satisfy one of the sufficient conditions in
definitions

4
,
6
,
8
, and
10
?

4.

Is the bounded moment condition in
definition

10
necessary? Can we extend our analysis to other heavy-tailed distributions?

5.

Can we improve the dependence on failure probability in
theorem

and extraneous logarithmic factors?

A.1
Gaussian width and complexity

The analysis of random matrices depends on the notion of the
Gaussian width
. The Gaussian width of a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

w(\mathcal{T}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\right],\qquad\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}).

(19)

This is a fundamental quantity that is closely related to other geometric qualities associated with
\mathcal{T}
such as surface area and volume. We also define the radius of a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T}):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}
.

The Gaussian width
w(\mathcal{T})
has the following properties
[
38
, Proposition 7.5.2.]
: it is invariant under translations and rotations; that is if
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is orthogonal and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

w(\mathbf{U}\mathcal{T}+\mathbf{y})=w(\mathcal{T})
. Furthermore, it is equivalent to the diameter of a set
\mathcal{T}
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{T})\leq w(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{T})
,
where the diameter
\mathsf{diam}(\mathcal{T}):=2\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T})
.

Related to the Gaussian width of a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the notion of the Gaussian complexity,
\gamma(\mathcal{T})
, defined as

\gamma(\mathcal{T}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\,|\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle|\right],\qquad\boldsymbol{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}).

(20)

Obviously,
w(\mathcal{T})\leq\gamma(\mathcal{T})
, and for any set containing the origin both are equivalent since
\gamma(\mathcal{T})\leq 2w(\mathcal{T})
.

Let us now state and prove an elementary inequality regarding the Gaussian width of an ellipsoidal set. This result plays an essential role in deriving dimension-independent bounds.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Then
w(\boldsymbol{H}\mathcal{S}^{n-1})\leq\|\boldsymbol{H}\|_{F}
.

Proof of
lemma

17
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{Hz}\in\boldsymbol{H}\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
. Then,

{\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{H}\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}={\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{Hz}\rangle}={\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle}.

Applying Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
\langle\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\leq\|\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}
, for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}
. Next, applying Cauchy-Schwartz inequality for random variables yields

w(\boldsymbol{H}\mathcal{S}^{n-1})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\,\langle\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right]=\sqrt{\mathsf{trace}(\boldsymbol{H}^{\top}\boldsymbol{H})}=\|\boldsymbol{H}\|_{F}

which completes the proof.

Note that
lemma

17
provides a tighter bound than the one in
[
38
, Exercise 7.5.4]
since

\|\boldsymbol{H}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\boldsymbol{H}\|_{2}
.

A.2
Auxiliary results

The following result
[
38
, Proposition 2.6.1]
gives insight into the sum of independent sub-Gaussian random variables.

Lemma 18
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be independent, mean zero, sub-Gaussian random variables. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
is also sub-Gaussian random variable with zero mean and

\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}.

This result is a form of generalization of a result for Gaussian random variables, i.e., If
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
are independent Gaussian random variables, then their sum is a Gaussian random variable whose variance
\text{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i})
is the sum of variances
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{Var}(X_{i})
.

The following results regarding sub-Gaussian random vectors will be useful in our analysis.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector with independent, mean zero, sub-Gaussian entries such that
\|z_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
,
1\leq i\leq n
. Then
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
is sub-Gaussian, so that for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}K^{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

(21)

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{W}
has orthonormal columns, then
\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}K^{2}
. Here
C_{R}
is the constant from
lemma

18
.

Proof of
lemma

19
.

For every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{Wx}

\|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\|\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}z_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}.

Therefore, the marginal is a weighted sum of sub-Gaussian random variables and, therefore, sub-Gaussian. Using
Lemma

18

\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}z_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{2}\|z_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|z_{i}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2},

(22)

where
C_{R}
is the constant in
lemma

18
.
Note that

\|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right]^{2}\right)=\left(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{Wx}\right)=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2},

which when combined with (
22
) gives (
21
).

For the second part,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has unit norm since
\mathbf{W}
has orthonormal columns. Therefore,
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
is sub-Gaussian with norm
\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}K^{2}\text{sup}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}^{{d-1}}}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}=C_{R}K^{{2}}
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a sub-Gaussian random vector with
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
and let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a matrix with orthonormal columns. Then
\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}K^{2}
where
C_{R}
is the constant in
lemma

18
.

Proof of
lemma

20
.

The proof follows from

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\|\langle\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Wz}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}\|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\leq C_{R}K^{2}.

In the last step, we have used
lemma

18
and the fact that
\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}=1
.

This result says that if
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has independent sub-Gaussian entries with norm
K
, then
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
(with
\mathbf{W}
having orthonormal columns) is also sub-Gaussian with norm bounded by
K
up to a constant factor. The next result extends it to sub-Gaussian random vectors.

A.3
Random matrix bounds

Theorem 21
(Talagrand’s comparison inequality).

Let
X_{\mathbf{t}}
for
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random process. Assume that for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{T}
we have
\|X_{\mathbf{x}}-X_{\mathbf{y}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{T}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
.
Then the following hold:

1.

Expectation bound:
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T}}X_{\mathbf{t}}\right]\leq C_{TE}K_{T}w(\mathcal{T})
.

2.

Tail bound: For every
u\geq 0
, with probability, at least
1-2\exp(-u^{2})

\sup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T}}|X_{\mathbf{t}}|\leq C_{TT}K_{T}(w(\mathcal{T})+u\cdot\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T})).

Here
C_{TE}
and
C_{TT}
are absolute constants.

Proof.

See
[
38
, Corollary 8.6.3, Exercise 8.6.5]
.

We briefly remark that explicit (but suboptimal constants) are available in
[
11
, Remark 3.3(iv)]
.

Proof of
Theorem

13
.

First, w.l.o.g., let us consider
\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{N}\|_{2}=1
. Thus
\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{M})=\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{F}^{2}
and
\mathsf{sr}(\boldsymbol{N})=\|\boldsymbol{N}\|_{F}^{2}
. The main idea is to use Talagrand’s comparison inequality, see
Theorem

21
, which was used to establish the sub-Gaussian Chevet inequality
[
38
, Theorem 8.7.1]
.

Step 1: Setup the stochastic process

Let us define
\mathcal{U}:={\boldsymbol{N}}\mathcal{S}^{N-1}
and
\mathcal{V}:=\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\mathcal{S}^{M-1}
(ellipsoids of radius
1
) and consider the random process

X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}:=\langle\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle,\qquad\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}.

It is easy to check that
X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}
has zero mean and

{\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}}X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{S}^{m-1}}\langle\boldsymbol{MSN}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle={\|\boldsymbol{MSN}\|_{2}}.

Step 2: Verify sub-Gaussian increments

Here, we develop a bound for the sub-Gaussian increment
\|X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}-X_{\boldsymbol{wz}}\|_{\psi_{2}}
as in the proof of
[
38
, Theorem 8.7.1]
. Consider
X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}
defined in
Step 1
. Then, for
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{V}

\|X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}-X_{\boldsymbol{wz}}\|_{\psi_{2}}=\|\langle\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}=\|\sum_{i,j}s_{ij}({u}_{j}{v}_{i}-{w}_{j}{z}_{i})\|_{\psi_{2}}.

Since the entries of matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
are independent sub-Gaussian random variables with mean zero and sub-Gaussian norm
\|s_{ij}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K
, by the concentration inequality
Lemma

18

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{ij}({u}_{j}{v}_{i}-{w}_{j}{z}_{i})\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{R}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|s_{ij}({u}_{j}{v}_{i}-{w}_{j}{z}_{i})\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{R}}K\|\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\|_{F}.

Following the proof of
[
38
, Theorem 8.7.1]
we get

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Combining intermediate results gives the following bound

\|X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}-X_{\boldsymbol{wz}}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{2C_{R}}K\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Step 3: Expectation bound

As we have verified the requirements of
Theorem

21

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}\right]\leq\sqrt{2C_{R}}C_{TE}Kw(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}).

(23)

A quick calculation shows that for
\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})

\displaystyle w(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\right]=\>\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\langle\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}}\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\right]

(24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{F}+\|{\boldsymbol{N}}\|_{F}.

Here, we have used the linearity of expectation values and Lemma
17
. Combining bound (
24
) with (
23
) proves (
9
) with
C_{SNE}:=\sqrt{2C_{R}}C_{TE}
.

Step 4: Tail Bound

In the following, we use the tail-bound version of Talagrand’s comparison inequality
Theorem

21
. For every
u\geq 0

\sup_{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}\leq\sup_{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}}|X_{\boldsymbol{uv}}|\leq\sqrt{2C_{R}}C_{TT}K\Big(w(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V})+u\ \mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V})\Big),

(25)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u^{2})
.
Next, inserting (
24
)
and the inequality

\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V})\leq\max\{\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2},\|\boldsymbol{N}\|_{2}\}=1,

into (
25
) yields (
10
), and completes the proof with
C_{SNT}:=\sqrt{2C_{R}}C_{TT}
.

Theorem 22
(Matrix deviation inequality).

Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
whose rows
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{S}
are independent, isotropic, and sub-Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any subset
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{T}}|\|\mathbf{Sx}\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{Sx}\|_{2}\right]|\right]\leq C_{MDE}K^{2}\gamma(\mathcal{T}).

Here
\gamma(\mathcal{T})
is the Gaussian complexity and
K:=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\|_{\psi_{2}}.
Furthermore, for any
u\geq 0
, the inequality

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{T}}|\|\mathbf{Sx}\|_{2}-\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}|\leq C_{MDT}K^{2}[w(\mathcal{T})+u\cdot\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T})],

holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-u^{2})
. Here
C_{MDE}
and
C_{MDT}
are absolute constants.

Proof.

See
[
37
]
, Theorem 9.1.1. and Exercise 9.1.8.

Proof of
Theorem

15
.

Note that
\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}
has independent sub-Gaussian isotropic rows and consider
\mathcal{T}=\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\mathcal{S}^{M-1}
. The expectation bound in
Theorem

22
and the triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}|\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}-\sqrt{n}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}|\right]+\sqrt{n}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{MDE}K^{2}\gamma(\mathcal{T})+\sqrt{n}\ \mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T}).

By an argument similar to the proof of
Lemma

17
, we have
\gamma(\mathcal{T})\leq\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{F}
and
\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T})\leq\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}
. Plugging these into (
26
) gives the expectation bound (
13
).

Furthermore, applying the tail bound of
Theorem

22
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u^{2})

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}|\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}-\sqrt{n}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}|\leq C_{MDT}K^{2}[w(\mathcal{T})+u\cdot\mathsf{rad}(\mathcal{T})].

Therefore, once again using triangle inequality and

17
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{T}}\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in T}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}+C_{MDT}K^{2}[{\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{F}}+u\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}+C_{MDT}K^{2}[{\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{F}}+u\|\boldsymbol{M}\|_{2}],

).

Appendix B
Other examples

In this section, we give additional examples of distributions to illustrate the bounds presented in Section
3
.

B.1
Independent sub-Gaussian entries

We also consider another distribution which we call
sparse sub-Gaussian distribution
(related to
[
25
, Example 2.9]
). This distribution may be computationally advantageous in some cases since the matrix-vector products with matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
may be cheaper because of many zero entries. Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
be a user-defined sparsity parameter and let
Y
be a Bernoulli random variable that takes the values
1
with probability
\alpha
and
0
with probability
1-\alpha
. Let
Z
be a sub-Gaussian random variable with zero mean, unit variance, and sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
K_{Z}
. Then it is easy to verify that
X=\alpha^{-1/2}YZ
is a sub-Gaussian random variable with zero mean, unit variance, and with sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
CK_{Z}/\sqrt{\alpha}
, where
C
is some absolute constant. Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix with independent copies of
X
; then by
Theorem

5
, the number of required samples
\ell
is
\mathcal{O}(K_{Z}^{4}k/\alpha^{2})
. A special case is the random sparse sign matrix, for which
Z
is a Rademacher random variable (whose sub-Gaussian norm is an absolute constant), and the number of required samples for this distribution
\ell
is
\mathcal{O}(k/\alpha^{2})
. This requirement is similar to the one for the sparse Rademacher random matrix with
\alpha=1/s
.

B.2
Independent sub-Gaussian columns

We consider a new random matrix model called
sparse sign matrices
Consider a random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{s}_{1}&\dots&\mathbf{s}_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}
where each column has entries
\pm 1
(with equal probability) situated in
N
nonzero locations, chosen uniformly at random from
1
to
n
. This is a slightly different model than
[
10
]
in which the author uses independent rows rather than columns. It can be verified using
Lemma

18
, applied conditionally on the sparsity pattern, that the sub-Gaussian norm of each column is bounded by
K_{C}=C\sqrt{C_{R}n/N}
, where
C
is an absolute constant. Therefore, by
theorem

7
, the number of samples required is
\mathcal{O}(k(n/N)^{2})
.

B.3
Independent bounded columns

We now consider the coordinate distribution. In this case, the columns of
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
are drawn uniformly from the set
\{\sqrt{n}\mathbf{e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. As mentioned earlier, although the random vectors are sub-Gaussian, the sub-Gaussian norm is bounded by
\sqrt{n}
, making the bounds in
theorem

7
impractical. We now consider the consequence of
theorem

9
for the coordinate distribution. Given a matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with orthonormal columns, we define the coherence of
\mathbf{W}
as the largest squared row norm (largest leverage score), i.e.,

\mu(\mathbf{W}):=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(27)

The coherence takes values in the range
[\frac{d}{n},1]
. The two extreme cases
\frac{d}{n}
and
1
can be achieved
when all the columns of
\mathbf{W}
have equal norm, e.g., columns from the Hadamard matrix, and the identity matrices, respectively. Then the boundedness assumption in
theorem

9
takes the form

\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n\mu(\mathbf{V}_{k})}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\mathbf{V}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n\mu(\mathbf{V}_{\perp})},

for all
1\leq j\leq\ell
. The number of required samples is, therefore,
\ell=\frac{{2n\mu(\mathbf{V}_{k})}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log(2k/\delta).

This result means that many samples are required if the matrix
\mathbf{V}_{k}
has high coherence. At the very minimum, it requires
\mathcal{O}(k\log k)
samples, but a matrix with high coherence could require the number of samples to depend on the dimension
n
. Similar observations can be made in the case of the random vectors drawn uniformly from the columns of an orthogonal matrix.

B.4
Independent columns with bounded second moments

We first explain why this model is the most general of those we considered. Note that any random matrix that satisfies
definition

8
automatically satisfies
definition

10
with
K_{M}=K_{k}^{2}
. Similarly, any random matrix that satisfies
definition

6
(and, therefore,
definition

4
) also satisfies
definition

10
with
K_{M}=C_{R}K_{C}^{2}\log(2\ell)
. To see this, we use the approach described in
[
5
, Chapter 2.5]
. Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
have orthonormal columns. From
lemma

20
, the columns of
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}
have the sub-Gaussian norm bounded by
C_{R}K_{C}
. This means that for each
1\leq j\leq\ell
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(C_{R}K_{C}^{2}))\right]\leq 2
. By Jensen’s inequality, with
\lambda=1/(C_{R}K_{C}^{2})
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}e^{\lambda\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq 2\ell.

Taking logarithms, we get
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq C_{R}K_{C}^{2}\log(2\ell)
. By applying this result with
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{V}_{k}
, we get
K_{M}:=C_{R}K_{C}^{2}\log(2\ell)
. Therefore,
definition

10
is the most general case that we consider in this paper.

We now provide a proof for the claim in
corollary

12
.

Proof of
corollary

12
.

First, we provide a bound for
K_{M}
in
definition

10
. By the Hanson-Wright inequality
[
16
, Proposition 1.1]

\mathbb{P}\left\{|\;\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-k|\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{M^{4}k},\frac{t^{\alpha/2}}{M^{\alpha}}\right\}\right).

Define the random variable
Z=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq\ell}|\;\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-k|.
By the triangle inequality,
K_{M}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]+k
, so it is sufficient to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]
. By the union bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{Z\geq t\right\}\leq\ell\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{M^{4}k},\frac{t^{\alpha/2}}{M^{\alpha}}\right\}\right).

Then using
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}\left\{Z\geq t\right\}dt}
since
Z
is nonnegative, we have for some
\mu>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mu}dt+\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\ell\exp\left(-\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{M^{4}k},\frac{t^{\alpha/2}}{M^{\alpha}}\right\}\right)dt

(29)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu+{n}\max\left\{\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{M^{4}k}\right)dt,\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{\alpha/2}}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\right\}dt.

We tackle each term separately. First, we use the inequality
\int_{\mu}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/(2\beta)}dt\leq\sqrt{\beta}e^{-t^{2}/(2\beta)}dt
(
[
34
, Proof of Corollary 7.3.2]
) to obtain

\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{M^{4}k}\right)dt\leq M^{2}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu^{2}}{M^{4}k}\right).

Now set
n\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu^{2}}{M^{4}k}\right)=1

and solve for
\mu
to get
\mu=M^{2}\sqrt{k\log(n\sqrt{k/2})}.
For the other term, we use
[
16
, Proof of Lemma A.2]

\int_{\mu}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t^{\alpha/2}}{M^{\alpha}}\right)\leq\left(\frac{2}{\alpha e}\right)^{2/\alpha}\int_{\mu}^{\infty}(t/M^{2})^{-1}dt=M^{2}\left(\frac{2}{\alpha e}\right)^{2/\alpha}\frac{1}{2\mu^{2}}.

Since
\mu\geq 1
by assumptions on
n
and
M
, it is clear that we can bound this term by
M^{2}C_{\alpha}
where
C_{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\alpha e}\right)^{2/\alpha}
. Now combining with (
29
),
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]\leq\mu+M^{2}\left(1+C_{\alpha}\right)
. Therefore,
K_{M}:=k+\mu+M^{2}\left(1+C_{\alpha}\right)
where
\mu=M^{2}\sqrt{k\log(n\sqrt{k/2})}
. Using
theorem

11
, the number of samples is
\ell=\mathcal{O}((k+M^{2}\sqrt{k\log(n)}+M^{2}(2/\alpha)^{2/\alpha})\log k)
.

We now discuss two other classes of random matrices that satisfy
definition

10
.

Uniform on an isotropic convex set

The random vectors from this distribution are uniformly distributed in a bounded convex set
\mathcal{K}
with a non-empty interior. Examples of such sets include the cube
\mathcal{K}=[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]^{n}
(already discussed in
section

3.2
) and the unit
\ell_{p}
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
p\geq 1
. A convex set is said to be in
isotropic position
if it is symmetric, with the center of mass at the origin, and satisfying

\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle^{2}d\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2},

for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If a random vector
\mathbf{x}
that is uniformly drawn from
\mathcal{K}
(denoted
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{K})
) is isotropic,
\mathcal{K}
is said to be in isotropic position. Furthermore, if
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n\times d}}
has orthonormal columns, it is easy to check that the set
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathcal{K}
is in isotropic position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For certain isotropic convex sets (called
\psi_{2}
bodies), a random vector uniform in the convex set
\mathcal{K}
has a sub-Gaussian norm that is
\mathcal{O}(1)
. However, not all random vectors uniformly distributed on a convex set are sub-Gaussian (see
[
38
, Section 3.4.4]
).

A random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}
whose columns are drawn uniformly from a convex set in isotropic position satisfies
definition

10
as we show now. The first two properties follow readily, whereas, for the third, we note that
[
27
, Proof of Corollary 4.1]
for any
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathcal{K})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}/Cd)\right]\leq 2,

where
C
is an absolute constant. Therefore, using a similar argument as in (
28
), we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq Ck\log(2\ell)\leq Ck\log(2n)
. Therefore, the random matrix satisfies
Definition

10
with
K_{M}=Ck\log(2n)
, and so the number of required samples
\ell
using
theorem

11
is
\mathcal{O}(k\log k\log n)
.

Log-concave distributions

A random vector
\mathbf{x}
is said to be log-concave if the probability distribution function is proportional to
\exp(-V(\mathbf{x}))
, where
\log V(\mathbf{x})
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This class includes several cases we have already discussed including normal, exponential, Laplace, and uniform on a convex set. However, it also includes other distributions such as Gamma and extreme value distributions (Gumbel, Frechet, etc.). We derive the minimal number of samples for this class of distributions. Using Paouris concentration inequality
[
31
, Section 1.4]

\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq t\right\}\leq\exp(-ct),\qquad t\geq Ck,

for absolute constants
c,C>0
. Therefore, using the union bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\geq t\right\}\leq n\exp(-ct),\qquad t\geq Ck.

Then, for
\mu\geq Ck

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq j\leq\ell}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\int_{0}^{\mu}dt+\int_{\mu}^{\infty}n\exp(-ct)dt=\mu+\frac{n}{c}\exp(-c\mu).

Choose
\mu=\max\{Ck,c^{-1}\log(n/c)\}
, so that
K_{M}:=\max\{Ck,c^{-1}\log(n/c)\}+1
. By
theorem

11
, the number of samples
\ell
is
\mathcal{O}((k+\log n)\log k).

Other distributions

As mentioned previously, our analysis is applicable to any distribution with independent isotropic columns and bounded second moments. However, obtaining bounds for
K_{M}
may not be straightforward due to the presence of the maximum inside the expectation. To see the challenge, consider the naive inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq nk=:K_{M},

that gives the minimal number of samples as
\ell=\mathcal{O}(nk\log k)
, which is extremely pessimistic. A bound for
K_{M}
can be obtained using a technique known as a maximal inequality (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Section 2.5]
). In practice, we need some stronger requirements on the random matrices (e.g.,
\|\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
should satisfy the condition (SR) in
[
31
]
) to obtain meaningful bounds for
K_{M}
. An investigation of this is left to future work.

We can also extend the results to distributions with more than four moments (so-called
4+\varepsilon
moments) using the approach in
[
31
, Section 1.5]
to obtain a bound for
K_{M}
.

Appendix D
Nyström Method

We consider a randomized Nyström method
[
39
,
13
,
17
,
15
]
for the low-rank approximation of symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD) matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{A}\succeq\mathbf{0}
. In this method, we draw a random matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
and compute the sketch
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A\Omega}
. Using this sketch, we can compute the Nyström approximation

\mathbf{A}\approx\widehat{\mathbf{A}}:=\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})^{\dagger}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}.

This low-rank approximation can be truncated using the
truncated rank Nyström method
of
[
35
]
, which uses
\mathbf{A}\approx[[\widehat{\mathbf{A}}]]_{k}
, a best rank-
k
approximation of the
full Nyström approximation. A naive implementation of the Nyström method can be numerically unstable, so we use a more stable version in
[
35
, Algorithm 3]
.

We give an example result of the guarantees using the Nyström approximation. Let us partition the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
conformally as

\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{k}&\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}\\
&\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are arranged in decreasing order and the matrices
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}=\diag(\lambda_{k+1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
. We derive a result for the random matrix that satisfies
definition

6
; results for other models can be derived similarly.

Theorem 23
(Nyström, Independent sub-Gaussian columns).

Let
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
be a random matrix satisfying
definition

6
. Given user-specified parameters
0<\delta<1
and
\varepsilon>0
, let the number of samples satisfy (
6
). If
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})^{\dagger}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}
is the Nyström approximation with input
\mathbf{\Omega}
, then with a probability of failure at most
\delta
,

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}+\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\ell}\left(\sqrt{\ell}\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{1/2}+C_{\rm CB}K_{\rm C}^{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{trace}\,(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp})}+V_{\delta}\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{1/2})\right)^{2}.

Here,
C_{\rm CS}
and
C_{\rm CB}
are absolute constants.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}=\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}=\mathbf{U}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}
. By
theorem

7
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}
has full row rank with probability at least
1-\delta
. Condition on this event,
and apply
[
15
]
, with
q=1
to get

\|\mathbf{A}-\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}^{1/2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

Therefore, by
theorem

7
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}^{1/2}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{2}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{1}^{\dagger}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}^{1/2}}{(1-\varepsilon)\sqrt{\ell}}\left(\sqrt{\ell}+C_{\rm CB}K_{\rm C}^{2}(\sqrt{\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}^{1/2})}+{V_{\delta}})\right).

Use
\mathsf{sr}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}^{1/2})=\mathsf{trace}\,(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp})/\|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\perp}\|_{2}
and plug into (
30
).
