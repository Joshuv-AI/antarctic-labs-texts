Integrity Constraints Revisited: From Exact to Approximate Implication
Batya Kenig \rsuper a, Dan Suciu \rsuper b

Abstract
. Integrity constraints such as functional dependencies (FD) and multi-valued dependencies (MVD) are fundamental in database schema design. Likewise, probabilistic conditional independences (CI) are crucial for reasoning about multivariate probability distributions. The implication problem studies whether a set of constraints (antecedents) implies another constraint (consequent), and has been investigated in both the database and the AI literature, under the assumption that all constraints hold exactly . However, many applications today consider constraints that hold only approximately . In this paper we define an approximate implication as a linear inequality between the degree of satisfaction of the antecedents and consequent, and we study the relaxation problem : when does an exact implication relax to an approximate implication? We use information theory to define the degree of satisfaction, and prove several results. First, we show that any implication from a set of data dependencies (MVDs+FDs) can be relaxed to a simple linear inequality with a factor at most quadratic in the number of variables; when the consequent is an FD, the factor can be reduced to 1. Second, we prove that there exists an implication between CIs that does not admit any relaxation; however, we prove that every implication between CIs relaxes “in the limit”. Then, we show that the implication problem for differential constraints in market basket analysis also admits a relaxation with a factor equal to 1. Finally, we show how some of the results in the paper can be derived using the I-measure theory, which relates between information theoretic measures and set theory. Our results recover, and sometimes extend, several previously known results about the implication problem: the implication of MVDs and the implication of differential constraints for frequent item sets can be checked by considering only 2-tuple relations.

several applications of Big Data, integrity constraints are discovered, or mined in a database

,

,

]

For example, data cleaning can be done by first learning conditional
functional dependencies in some reference data, then using them to
identify inconsistencies in the test
data
[

,
CIPY14
]
.
Causal
reasoning
[

,

,

]

and learning sum-of-product

[

,

,

Constraints also arise in many other domains, for example in the

[

,

]
, or as

measure based constraints

[
SGG08
]
in
applications like Dempster-Shafer theory, possibilistic theory, and
game theory (see discussion in
[
SGG08
]
).
In all these applications, quite often the constraints are learned
from the data, and are not required to hold exactly, but it suffices
if they hold only to a certain degree.

The classical
implication problem
asks whether a set of
constraints, called the
antecedents
, logically imply another
constraint called the
consequent
. In this setting, both
antecedents and consequent are required to hold exactly, hence we
refer to it as an
exact implication
(

). The database literature
has extensively studied the
EI
problem for integrity constraints and
shown that the implication problem is decidable and axiomatizable for
Functional Dependencies (FDs) and Multivalued Dependencies
(MVDs)
[
Mai83
,
KLV13
,
AD80
,
BFH77
]
,
and undecidable for Embedded Multivalued Dependencies
(EMVDs)
[
Her06
]
. The Armstrong axioms, for instance, provide a sound and complete system for inferring FDs when the antecedents are FDs
[
AD80
]
, and Beeri et al.
[
BFH77
]
present a set of axioms which are sound and complete for inferring MVDs and FDs when the antecedents are a set of FDs and MVDs.

Conditional Independencies
(CI) are assertions of the form
X\perp Y\mid Z
, stating
that
X
is independent of
Y
conditioned on
Z
. A CI is
saturated
if it covers all variables in the joint distribution (i.e.,
XYZ=
all variables); it is called a
conditional
if it has the form
X\rightarrow Y
and
Y
is a function of
X
.

The AI community has
extensively studied the
EI
problem for Conditional Independencies (CI), and has shown that
the implication problem is decidable and axiomatizable for saturated
CIs
[
GP93
]
(where
XYZ=
all variables). On the negative side, it has been shown that in the general case, there exists no finite, sound and complete inference system for the implication of CIs
[
Stu90
]
. In the Frequent Itemset Problem, a
constraint like
X\rightarrow Y\vee Z\vee U
means that every basket
that contains
X
also contains at least one of
Y,Z,U
, and the
implication problem here is also decidable and
axiomatizable
[
SVG05
]
.

The Relaxation Problem.

In this paper we consider a new problem,
called the
relaxation problem
: if an exact implication holds,
does an approximate implication hold too? For example, suppose we
prove that a given set of FDs implies another FD, but the input data
satisfies the antecedent FDs only to some degree: to what degree does
the consequent FD hold in the database?
The relaxation
problem asks whether we can convert an exact implication into an
approximate implication
. In other words, we ask how the error in the antecedents propagates to the error in its consequent.
When relaxation
holds, then the error to the consequent can be bounded, and any inference system for proving exact implication,
e.g. using a set of axioms or some algorithm, can be used to infer an
approximate implication.

In order to study the relaxation problem we need to measure the degree
of satisfaction of a constraint. In this paper we use Information
Theory. This is the natural semantics for modeling CIs of
multivariate distributions, because
X\perp Y\mid Z
iff

I(X;Y|Z)=0
where
I
is the
conditional mutual information
. FDs
and MVDs are special cases of
CIs
[
Lee87
,
DR00
,
WBW00
]

(reviewed in Section
2.1
), and thus they are naturally
modeled using the information theoretic measure
I(X;Y|Z)
or
H(Y|X)
; in contrast, EMVDs do not appear
to have a natural interpretation using information theory, and we will
not discuss them further in this work.
An
approximate
implication
(
ApxI
) is an inequality that (numerically) bounds the information-theoretic measure of the
consequent (e.g., conditional entropy
H(\cdot|\cdot)
if it is an FD, or the conditional mutual information
I(\cdot;\cdot|\cdot)
if it is an MVD) by a linear combination of the information-theoretic measures of the antecedents.
The link between integrity constraints and information theoretic measures is, in itself, not new. Several papers have argued that information
theory is a suitable tool to express integrity
constraints
[
Lee87
,
DR00
,
WBW00
,
Mal86
,
GR04
]
.

An exact implication (
EI
) becomes an assertion of the form

(\sigma_{1}=0\wedge\sigma_{2}=0\wedge\ldots)\Rightarrow(\tau=0)
,
while an approximate implication (
ApxI
) is a linear inequality

\tau\leq\lambda\cdot\left(\sum\sigma_{i}\right)
, where
\lambda\geq 0
, and

\tau,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
are information theoretic
measures. We say that a class of constraints can be

relaxed
if every
EI
where the antecedents are from this class, implies the corresponding
ApxI
;
we also say that this
EI
admits a
\lambda
-relaxation, when we want to specify the factor
\lambda
in the
ApxI
. By the non-negativity of the Shannon information measures (described in Section
2
), we get that an

ApxI
always implies the corresponding
EI
.

Results.

We make several contributions, summarized in
Table
1
. We start by showing in
Section
4
that MVDs
+

Thus, whenever an exact implication holds between MVD

even when the consequent is not an FD. We leave open the question of whether

So far, we have restricted ourselves to saturated or conditional CIs
(which correspond to MVDs or FDs). In
Section

we remove all restrictions, and
prove a negative result: there exists an

that does not relax
(Eq. (

[

). Nevertheless, we show that
every

can be relaxed to its corresponding

plus an error term, which can be made

.
This result implies that
every

is a consequence of its corresponding inequality

, plus an error term.
In fact, the

, which is

In Section
6
we consider approximate (and exact) implications that can be proved using
Shannon’s inequalities
(monotonicity and submodularity, reviewed in
Section
2.2
).
In general, Shannon’s inequalities are
sound but incomplete for proving exact and approximate
implications that hold for all probability distributions

,
ZY98
]
, but they are complete for deriving inequalities that hold for all

(defined in Section

)
[

]
.
We also prove that every exact implication that holds for all polymatroids relaxes to an approximate

, and a

In Section
7
we show that the Shannon inequalities are sound and complete for implication from

[

]
that arise in market-basket analysis.
More generally,
in Section
7
we restrict the class of

used to check an
implication, to probability distributions with exactly 2 outcomes (tuples), each with probability 1/2; we justify
this shortly. We prove that, under this restriction, the implication

complete but unsound method for checking general implication, however
this method is sound for saturated
+
conditional CIs (as we show in

), and is also sound for deriving implications from Frequent Itemset constraints (as we show in Section

).

In Section

we extend some of the results in this paper and provide alternative proofs using the

4
, and provide an alternative proof to the result of Section

.

Two Consequences.

While our paper is focused on relaxation, our
results have two consequences for the exact implication problem. The
first is a
2-tuple model property
: an exact implication, where
the antecedents are saturated or conditional CIs, holds
iff it holds on all
uniform probability distributions with 2 tuples.

A similar result is
known for MVD
+
FDs
[
SDPF81
]
. Geiger and
Pearl
[
GP93
]
, building on an earlier result by
Fagin
[
Fag82
]
, prove that every set of CIs
has an
Armstrong model
: a discrete probability distribution that
satisfies only the CIs and their consequences, and no other CI. The
Armstrong model is also called a
global witness
, and, in general,
can be arbitrarily large. Our result concerns
a
local witness
:
for any
EI
, if it fails on some probability distribution, then it
fails on a 2-tuple uniform distribution.

The second consequence concerns the equivalence between the implication
problem of saturated
+
conditional CIs with that of MVD
+
FDs. It
is easy to check that the former implies the latter
(Section
2
). Wong et
al.
[
WBW00
]
prove the other direction (i.e., if an implication from MVDs+FDs holds then the same implication holds for saturated
+
conditional CIs), relying on the sound and complete
axiomatization of MVDs
[
BFH77
]
. Our
2-tuple model property implies the other direction
almost immediately, leading to a much simpler proof in Section
4
.

This article extends the conference publication by the authors
[
KS20
]
. We have added in this article all the proofs and intermediate results that were excluded from the conference paper, as well as examples illustrating the ideas and methods introduced.

Discussion

This paper studies exact and approximate implications, expressed as
equalities or inequalities of entropic functions
h
. For example,
the augmentation axiom for MVDs
[
BFH77
]

A\twoheadrightarrow B|CD\Rightarrow AC\twoheadrightarrow B|D
is expressed as

I_{h}(B;CD|A)=0\Rightarrow I_{h}(B;D|AC)=0
, which holds by the chain
rule (
7
). Thus, our golden standard is to prove
that (in)equalities hold for all entropic functions

h\in\Gamma_{n}^{*}
.

Fix a set
A\subseteq\real^{N}
in an
N
-dimensional euclidean space with distance metric
d
. The

topological closure
of
A
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{cl}\left(A\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{x}\in\real^{N}}\mid{\forall\varepsilon>0,\exists\mathbf{s}\in A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{x})<\varepsilon}\}

Equivalently,
\mathbf{cl}\left(A\right)
consists of all limits of convergent sequences
in
A
. The set
A
is called
topologically closed
if

A=\mathbf{cl}\left(A\right)
. The topological closure enjoys the following basic
property: if
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\real^{N}\rightarrow\real
is any continuous
function, and
f(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in A
, then

f(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{cl}\left(A\right)
.

Yeung
[
Yeu08
]
has proven that,
when
n\geq 3
, then
\Gamma^{*}_{n}
is not topologically
closed, in other words,
\Gamma^{*}_{n}\subsetneq\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
. The elements of the set

\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
are called
almost entropic
functions
. Equivalently,
a function
g
is
almost entropic
if it is the limit of a sequence
of entropic functions.
It follows immediately from our discussion that,
if an
inequality
holds for all entropic functions

h\in\Gamma^{*}_{n}
, then, by continuity,
it also holds for
all almost entropic functions
h\in\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
.
However, this observation does not extend to
implications
of
(in)equalities; Kaced and
Romashchenko
[
KR13
]
gave an example of an
exact implication
that holds only for entropic functions but fails for
almost entropic functions. Thus, when discussing an EI, it matters
whether we assume that it holds for
\Gamma^{*}_{n}
or for

\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
. The only result in this paper where this
distinction matters are the two main theorems in
Section
5
: the negative result
Theorem
13
holds for both
\Gamma^{*}_{n}
and
for
\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
, while the positive result
Theorem
14
holds only for

\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
. The results in
Section
4
apply to
any
set of
polymatroids
K
that contains all step functions, i.e.

\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq K\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
, thus they apply to both

\Gamma^{*}_{n}
and
\mathbf{cl}\left(\Gamma^{*}_{n}\right)
, while those in
Section
6
and Section
7
are
stated only for
\Gamma_{n}
and only for (the conic closure of)

\mathcal{S}_{n}
respectively.

3.
Definition of the Relaxation Problem

We now formally define the relaxation problem. We fix a set of
variables
\Omega=\mathord{\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}}
, and consider formulas of
the form
\sigma=(Y;Z|X)
, where
X,Y,Z\subseteq\Omega
, which we
call a
conditional independence
,
CI; when
Y=Z
then we write it
as
X\rightarrow Y
and call it a
conditional
. An
implication
is a
formula
\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
, where
\Sigma
is a set of CIs
called
antecedents
and
\tau
is a CI called
consequent
. For
a CI
\sigma=(B;C|A)
, we define
h(\sigma)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}I_{h}(B;C|A)
(see (
5
)), for a
set of CIs
\Sigma
, we define

h(\Sigma)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}h(\sigma)
.
Fix a set
K

s.t.
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq K\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
. We recall that
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is the set of step functions, and that
\Gamma_{n}
is the set of polymatroids.

{defi}

The
exact implication
(
EI
)

\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
holds in
K
, denoted

K\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
if, for all
h\in K
,

h(\Sigma)=0
implies
h(\tau)=0
. The
\lambda
-approximate
implication
(
\lambda
-
ApxI
) holds in
K
, in notation

K\models\lambda\cdot h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
,
if

\forall h\in K
,
\lambda\cdot h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
. The

approximate implication
holds, in notation

K\models_{{\tt\textsc{ApxI}}}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
, if there exist a

\lambda\geq 0
such that the
\lambda
-
ApxI
holds.

We will sometimes consider an equivalent definition for
ApxI
, as

\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\lambda_{\sigma}h(\sigma)\geq h(\tau)
,
where
\lambda_{\sigma}\geq 0
are coefficients, one for each

\sigma\in\Sigma
; these two definitions are equivalent, by taking

\lambda=\max_{\sigma}\lambda_{\sigma}
. Notice that both
EI
and
ApxI

are preserved under subsets of
K
in the sense that

K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}
and
K_{2}\models_{x}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)

implies
K_{1}\models_{x}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
, for

x\in\mathord{\{\tt\textsc{EI},{\tt\textsc{ApxI}}\}}
.

ApxI
always implies
EI
. Indeed,
h(\tau)\leq\lambda\cdot h(\Sigma)

and
h(\Sigma)=0
imply
h(\tau)\leq 0
, which further implies

h(\tau)=0
, because
h(\tau)\geq 0
for every CI
\tau
, and every
polymatroid
h
. In this paper we study the reverse.

{defi}

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a syntactically-defined class of implication
statements
(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
, and let

K\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
. We say that
\mathcal{I}

admits a
relaxation
in
K
if every implication statement

(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
in
\mathcal{I}
that holds
exactly also holds approximately:

K\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
implies

K\models_{{\tt\textsc{ApxI}}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
. We say that

\mathcal{I}
admits a
\lambda
-relaxation
in
K
, if every EI admits a

\lambda
-
ApxI
.

If
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}
and
\mathcal{I}
admits a

\lambda
-relaxation in
K_{1}
then, in general, it does not
necessarily admit a
\lambda
-relaxation in
K_{2}
, nor vice versa.
However, the following more limited fact holds:

Fact 3
.

If
\mathcal{I}
admits a
\lambda
-relaxation in
K
, then it also
admits a
\lambda
-relaxation in
\mathbf{cl}\left(K\right)
.

Proof 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
be a CI statement in the syntactic class

\mathcal{I}
, and suppose

\mathbf{cl}\left(K\right)\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
. Then

K\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
. Since
\mathcal{I}

admits a
\lambda
-relaxation in
K
, we have
K\models_{{\tt\textsc{ApxI}}}(\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau)
, or, equivalently,
\forall h\in K\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda\cdot h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
. By continuity,
\forall h\in\mathbf{cl}\left(K\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
, proving the claim.

{exa}

Let
\Sigma{=}\mathord{\{(A;B|\emptyset),(A;C|B)\}}
and

\tau{=}(A;C|\emptyset)
.
By the chain rule (
7
), we have that

\displaystyle I_{h}(A;BC)

\displaystyle=I_{h}(A;B|\emptyset)+I_{h}(A;C|B)

and

\displaystyle I_{h}(A;BC)

\displaystyle=I_{h}(A;C|\emptyset)+I_{h}(A;B|C)\geq I_{h}(A;C|\emptyset)

Hence,
I_{h}(A;C|\emptyset)\leq I_{h}(A;B|\emptyset)+I_{h}(A;C|B)
.
Therefore, the exact implication
\Gamma_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
admits a
1
-
ApxI
.

4.
Relaxation for FDs and MVDs: Always Possible

In this section we consider the implication problem where the
antecedents are either saturated CIs, or conditionals. This is a
case of special interest in databases, because the constraints
correspond to MVDs, or FDs. Recall that
a CI
(B;C|A)
is

saturated
if
ABC=\Omega
(i.e., the set of all attributes).
Our main result in this section is:

Theorem 4
.

Assume that each formula in
\Sigma
is either saturated or a
conditional (e.g.,
Z\rightarrow X
), and let
\tau
be an arbitrary CI. Assume

\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
. Then:

(1)

\Gamma_{n}\models\frac{n^{2}}{4}h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
.

(2)

If
\tau
is a conditional,
then
\Gamma_{n}\models h(\Sigma)\geq h(\tau)
.

Before we prove the theorem, we list two important consequences.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\Sigma
consist of saturated CIs and/or conditionals, and let

\tau
be any CI. Then
\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau

implies
\Gamma_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
.

Proof 4.1
.

If
\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
then

\forall h\in\Gamma_{n}
,

h(\tau)\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}h(\Sigma)
, thus
h(\Sigma)=0
implies

h(\tau)=0
.

The corollary implies that if
\Sigma,\tau
are restricted to
saturated CIs and/or conditionals, then the Exact Implication
problem is the same for
\mathcal{S}_{n}
as it is for any other set
K
where
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq K\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
.

The corollary has an immediate application to the inference problem in
graphical models
[
GP93
]
. There, the problem is to
check if every probability distribution that satisfies all CIs in

\Sigma
also satisfies the CI
\tau
; we have seen that this is
equivalent to

\Gamma^{*}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
. The
corollary states that it is enough that this implication holds on all of the
uniform 2-tuple distributions, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
, because this implies the
(even stronger!) statement

\Gamma_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
.
Decidability (i.e., for exact implication)
was already known: Geiger and Pearl
[
GP93
]

proved that the set of graphoid axioms is sound and complete
for the case when both

\Sigma
and
\tau
are saturated. Specifically, the equivalence between saturated CIs and MVDs
[
GP93
]
enables the application of the polynomial implication algorithm devised for MVDs
[
Bee80
]
which, in this setting where both

\Sigma
and
\tau
are saturated, has a run-time complexity of
O(|\Sigma|n^{2})
.
Gyssens et
al.
[
GNG14
]
improve this result by dropping any
restrictions on
\tau
.

The second consequence is the following:

Corollary 6
.

Let
\Sigma,\tau
consist of saturated CIs and/or conditionals. Then
the following two statements are equivalent:

(1)

The implication
\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau

holds, where we interpret
\Sigma,\tau
as MVDs and/or FDs.

(2)

\Gamma_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
.

Proof 4.2
.

We have shown right after Lemma
2.1
that
(
2
) implies (
1
).
For the
opposite direction, by Th.
4
, we need only check

\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
, which holds because
on every uniform probability distribution a saturated CI holds iff
the corresponding MVD holds, and similarly for conditionals and FDs.
Since the 2-tuple relation satisfies the implication for MVDs
+
FDs, it
also satisfies the implication for CIs, proving the claim.

Wong et al.
[
WBW00
]
proved that the
implication for MVDs is equivalent to that of the corresponding
saturated CIs (called there BMVD); they did not consider FDs. For the
proof in the hard direction, they use the sound and complete
axiomatization of MVDs in
[
BFH77
]
. In
contrast, our proof is independent of any axiomatic system, and is
also much shorter. Finally, we notice that the corollary also implies
that, in order to check an implication between MVDs and/or FDs, it
suffices to check it on all 2-tuple databases: indeed, this is
equivalent to checking
\mathcal{S}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow\tau
,
because this implies Item (
2
), which in turn implies
item (
1
). This rather surprising fact was first
proven in
[
SDPF81
]
.

The proof of Theorem
4
follows from a series of lemmas, and a Theorem that is of independent interest, that we prove next. Before
proceeding, we note that we can assume w.l.o.g. that
\Sigma
consists
only of saturated CIs. Indeed, if
\Sigma
contains a non-saturated
term, then by assumption it is a conditional,
X\rightarrow Y
, and we will
replace it with two saturated terms:
(Y;Z|X)
and
XZ\rightarrow Y
,
where
Z=\Omega\setminus XY
. Denoting
\Sigma^{\prime}
the new set of
formulas, we have
h(\Sigma)=h(\Sigma^{\prime})
, because

h(Y|X)=I_{h}(Y;Z|X)+h(Y|XZ)
. Thus, we will assume w.l.o.g. that
all formulas in
\Sigma
are saturated.

We say that a CI
(X;Y|Z)
is
elemental
if
|X|=|Y|=1
.

Lemma 7
.

Every CI
\tau=(X;Y|Z)
can be written as a sum of
m+n_{X}\cdot n_{Y}

5.

We now extend our discussion from saturated CIs and conditionals

second, we prove that a weaker form of relaxation holds.

In both results the relaxation problem is considered in

5.1.
Cones

Both theorems

to
[

Stu93

BV04

is called a

\mathbf{x}\in K
and
\theta\geq 0
we have that
\theta\mathbf{x}\in K
. A
set
C\subseteq\real^{N}
is called
convex
if, for any two
points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in C
and any
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\theta\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{x}_{2}\in C
. Unless otherwise stated, in
this paper every cone will be assumed to be convex. The intersection
of a, not necessarily finite, set of convex cones is also a convex
cone. The
conic hull
of a set
C\subseteq\real^{N}
, in
notation
\mathbf{conhull}\left(C\right)
, is the smallest convex cone that contains

C
, or, equivalently, it is the set of vectors of the form

\theta_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}+\dots+\theta_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}
, where
k\geq 0
,

\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\in C
, and
\theta_{i}\geq 0,\forall i\in[k]
.

Fix a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\real^{N}
. The set

K=\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{x}{\cdot}\mathbf{u}\leq 0}\}
is a convex cone called a

linear half-space
. A
polyhedral cone
is the intersection
of a finite number of linear half-spaces. Equivalently,
K
is
polyhedral if

K=\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{x}{\cdot}\mathbf{u}_{1}\leq 0,\dots,\mathbf{x}{\cdot}\mathbf{u}_{r}\leq 0}\}
, where
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{r}\in\real^{N}
are fixed vectors,
or, also equivalently,
K=\{{\mathbf{x}}\mid{\mathbf{x}^{T}A\leq 0}\}
where

A\in\real^{N\times r}
is a matrix. A cone
K
is called
finitely generated
if
K=\mathbf{conhull}\left(C\right)
for some finite set

C\subseteq\real^{N}
; equivalently,
K
is finitely generated
if
3
3

3

Here, and throughout the paper, the inequality

\mathbf{z}\geq 0
means component wise inequalities.

K=\{{A\mathbf{z}}\mid{\mathbf{z}\in\real^{m},\mathbf{z}\geq 0}\}
, for some matrix

A\in\real^{N\times m}
. Results by Farkas, Minkowski, and Weyl
imply that a cone is finitely generated iff it is
polyhedral
[
Sch03

.

As we discussed, an entropic function, or a polymatroid

7.

While relaxation fails in its most general setting, we have

satisfies
the constraint
statement, if
I_{h}(Y|W)=0
. An
implication
is a formula

\Sigma\Rightarrow(Y|W)
, where
\Sigma
is a set of I-measure
constraints and
Y|W
is an I-measure constraint. Fix a set

\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq K\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
. The

implication

K
, in notation

, if
\forall h\in K

implies
I_{h}(Y|W)=0
. The

\lambda
-approximate implication
holds in
K
, if

\forall h\in K
,
I_{h}(Y|W)\leq\lambda\sum_{X|V\in\Sigma}I_{h}(X|V)
.
We say that the exact implication problem
admits a
\lambda
-relaxation
in

, for some
\lambda>0
, if
every exact implication

implies the
\lambda
-approximate implication

.

We prove our main result in this section:

Theorem 20
.

Exact implications of I-measure
constraints admit a 1-relaxation in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. More
precisely, if
\Sigma\models(Y|W)
is an implication of I-measure
constraints and

\mathcal{P}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow(Y|W)
, then

\forall h\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,

I_{h}(Y|W)\leq\sum_{X|V\in\Sigma}I_{h}(X|V)
.

Notice that Theorem
19
follows
immediately as a special case, since every CI is, in particular, an
I-measure constraint.

To prove theorem
20
, we need to
prove two lemmas. In both lemmas below we will use a simple property.
For any two sets
A,B
:

\displaystyle\sum_{C\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A\subseteq C\subseteq B}(-1)^{|C-A|}=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ when $A=B$}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(32)

To prove the identity of (

), it suffices to show that

\sum_{C\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A\subseteq C\subseteq B}(-1)^{|C-A|}=0
when

: fix any element

, and notice that the

that don’t contain

are in 1-1 correspondence with those
that do contain
b
, via the mapping
C\mapsto C\cup\mathord{\{b\}}
.
Since the two sets
C
and
C\cup\mathord{\{b\}}
have different parities,
their terms cancel out,
(-1)^{|C-A|}+(-1)^{|C\cup\mathord{\{b\}}-A|}=0
, and
the entire sum is zero.

Recall from Section
2
that
h_{Z}

denotes the “step function at

(see
the definition in Eq. (

)). By definition,
their conic hull

consists
of all functions of the form

,
where

, for

are arbitrary
coefficients. These coefficients are precisely the saturated
conditional multi-variate mutual information. This follows from a
more general lemma:

Lemma 21
.

Consider the vector space

\mathbf{V}=\{{h}\mid{h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}2^{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},h(\emptyset)=0}\}
. Then, the
2^{n}-1
step functions

\{{h_{Z}}\mid{Z\subsetneq\Omega}\}
are basis for
\mathbf{V}
.
Moreover, for every function
h\in\mathbf{V}
, denoting by

d_{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)
, for all
Z\subsetneq\Omega
(where

I_{h}
is defined in Eq. (
31
)), we have:

h=\sum_{Z\subsetneq\Omega}d_{Z}h_{Z}
. In other words, the
projections of
h
on the basis consisting of the step functions are
precisely the saturated conditional multi-variate mutual
informations.

Proof 7.1
.

We prove the following claim: for every
W\subseteq\Omega
, it holds that

h(W)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\subsetneq\Omega}d_{Z}h_{Z}(W),

where
d_{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)
. The claim implies that the step
functions span the entire vector space

\{{h}\mid{h\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}2^{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},h(\emptyset)=0}\}
. This
immediately implies that they are linearly independent, hence are a
basis for
\mathbf{V}
. Indeed, the dimensionality of
\mathbf{V}

is
2^{n}-1
, and this coincides with the number of step functions:
since they span the entire vector space, they must be linearly
independent. It remains to prove the claim.

To prove the claim, for any
W\subseteq\Omega
, define

f(W)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}h(\Omega)-h(W)
. We want to express
f(W)
as

f(W)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z\subseteq\Omega}g(Z)
, for some function

g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}2^{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. Such a function
g
is the unique
Möbius inverse of
f
. Recall that the Möbius inversion formula
states that the two expressions below are equivalent:

\displaystyle\forall W\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle f(W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z\subseteq\Omega}g(Z)

\displaystyle\forall W\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle g(W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z\subseteq\Omega}(-1)^{|Z-W|}f(Z)

(33)

Since
f(\emptyset)=h(\Omega)
, the first equality implies:

\displaystyle h(\Omega)=

\displaystyle f(\emptyset)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\subseteq\Omega}g(Z)

Therefore, the left equation in (
33
) can be rewritten
as:

\displaystyle h(W)=

\displaystyle h(\Omega)-f(W)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\subseteq\Omega}g(Z)-\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z\subseteq\Omega}g(Z)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\not\subseteq Z}g(Z)

(34)

On the other hand, we can express
g(W)
in terms of the conditional
multi-variate information, by expanding
f(Z)=h(\Omega)-h(Z)
in
the right equation in (
33
), then using Eq. (
32
):

\displaystyle g(W)=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z}(-1)^{|Z-W|}\right)h(\Omega)-\left(\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z}(-1)^{|Z-W|}h(Z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}h(\Omega)-h(\Omega)=0&\mbox{ if $W=\Omega$}\\
0+I_{h}(\Omega-W|W)&\mbox{ if $W\subsetneq\Omega$}\end{cases}

We compute now
h(W)
from (
34
):

\displaystyle h(W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\not\subseteq Z}g(Z)=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\not\subseteq Z}I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)=\sum_{Z\subsetneq\Omega}I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)h_{Z}(W)

where the last equality holds because
h_{Z}(W)=1
when

W\not\subseteq Z
and
h_{Z}(W)=0
otherwise. This proves the
claim, as required.

The lemma implies that, if
h\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, then

I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)\geq 0
for all
Z\subsetneq\Omega
. Indeed, by
definition of the conic hull
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\mathbf{conhull}\left(\mathcal{S}_{n}\right)
,
the function
h
can be written as
h=\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}Z\subsetneq\Omega}d_{Z}h_{Z}
, where
d_{Z}\geq 0
. By the lemma, we have
d_{Z}=I_{h}(\Omega-Z|Z)
, proving the claim.

The second lemma is:

Lemma 22
.

For any two sets
W,Y\subseteq\Omega

s.t.
Y\neq\emptyset
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle I_{h}(Y|W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y}I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)

Proof 7.2
.

We expand
I_{h}
in the RHS according to its definition:

\displaystyle\text{RHS}=

\displaystyle\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y}I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)=-\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y}\left(\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V\subseteq T\subseteq\Omega}(-1)^{|T-V|}h(T)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq T}h(T)\left(\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y)\wedge(V\subseteq T)}(-1)^{|T-V|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq T}h(T)\left(\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(W\subseteq V\subseteq(T\cap(\Omega-Y))}(-1)^{|T-V|}\right)

By Eq. (
32
), the inner sum is
=1
when
W=T\cap(\Omega-Y)
and
=0
otherwise. The condition
W=T\cap(\Omega-Y)
is equivalent to
W\subseteq T\subseteq W\cup Y

and thus we obtain:

\displaystyle\text{RHS}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq T\subseteq W\cup Y}(-1)^{|T-W|}h(T)

The latter expression is equal to
I_{h}(Y|W)
by Def.
7.1
.

We will now prove Theorem
20
.
Consider an exact implication

\mathcal{P}_{n}\models_{\tt\textsc{EI}}\Sigma\Rightarrow(Y|W)
. By
Lemma
22
,

I_{h}(Y|W)=\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y}I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)
. We claim that, for every set
V
s.t.

W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y
, there exists a constraint

X|U\in\Sigma
such that
U\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-X
. In
other words, if we expand
I_{h}(X|U)
according to
Lemma
22
, then one of the terms will be

I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)
. To prove the claim, we consider the step function
at
V
,
h_{V}
, and use the fact that the exact implication must hold
for
h_{V}
. We notice that
I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-V|V)=1
and

I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-U|U)=0
for
U\neq V
: this follows by considering
the expansion of
h_{V}
given by Lemma
21
,

h_{V}=\sum_{U\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\subsetneq\Omega}d_{U}h_{U}
, where

d_{U}=I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-U|U)
and noting that, since
h_{V}
is part of
the basis, we have
I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-V|V)=1
and
I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-U|U)=0

for
U\neq V
. In particular,
I_{h_{V}}(Y|W)=1
, in other words, the
constraint
Y|W
does not hold in
h_{V}
. Since the exact implication
holds, it must be the case that
\Sigma
does not hold for
h_{V}

either, hence there exists an I-constraint
X|U\in\Sigma
such that

I_{h_{V}}(X|U)>0
. When expanding it according to Lemma
22

I_{h_{V}}(X|U)=\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\subseteq T\subseteq\Omega-X}I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-T|T)
. Since
I_{h_{V}}(\Omega-T|T)=0
for all

T\neq V

We use claim to prove the theorem. Let
h
be any function in

\mathcal{P}_{n}
. To prove the inequality

I_{h}(Y|W)\leq\sum_{X|U\in\Sigma}I_{h}(X|U)
, we expand both sides:

\displaystyle I_{h}(Y|W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq V\subseteq\Omega-Y}I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)

\displaystyle\sum_{(X|U)\in\Sigma}I_{h}(X|U)=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}U\subseteq T\subseteq\Omega-X,(X|U)\in\Sigma}I_{h}(\Omega-T|T)

We have shown that every term
I_{h}(\Omega-V|V)
in the first sum
occurs at least once as a term

in the second sum.
Since all terms are
\geq 0
, it follows that

, as required.

Differential Constraints in Market Basket Analysis

We end this section by describing the tight connection between

Consider a set of items
\Omega=\mathord{\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}}

\mathcal{B}=\mathord{\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\}}
where every basket is a subset

that contain the set

\displaystyle f^{\mathcal{B}}(W)=

\displaystyle|\{{i}\mid{i\in[n],W\subseteq b_{i}}\}|

(35)

The function

is anti-monotone:
W_{1}\subseteq W_{2}

the number of

\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle d^{\mathcal{B}}(W)=

\displaystyle|\{{i}\mid{i\in[n],W=b_{i}}\}|

\displaystyle\forall W\subseteq\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle f^{\mathcal{B}}(W)=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z}d^{\mathcal{B}}(Z)

\displaystyle d^{\mathcal{B}}(W)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z}(-1)^{|Z-W|}f^{\mathcal{B}}(Z)

(36)

The first identity follows immediately from the definitions of

f^{\mathcal{B}}
and
d^{\mathcal{B}}
. The second is Möbius’ inversion function.
Building on the identity (

define the

of any function

as follows:

\displaystyle\forall W\subseteq\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}

\displaystyle d_{f}(W)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{Z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}W\subseteq Z}(-1)^{|Z-W|}f(Z)

When
f
is the support function

associated to a set of
baskets
\mathcal{B}
, then its density
d_{f}
is precisely the function

d^{\mathcal{B}}
, because it satisfies the left equation
in (

).

For any function

and any two sets of

.

{defi}

8.
Additional Results and Proofs that rely on the I-Measure

The I-measure, developed by Yeung
[
Yeu91
,
Yeu08
]
, is a theory that establishes a one-to-one correspondence between Shannon’s information measures (i.e., entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information, and conditional mutual information) and set theory. In Section
8.1
, we use the I-measure to prove Theorem
12
. In Section
8.2
we provide an alternative proof to Theorems
19
and
20
.
We begin by briefly describing the main ideas and theorems of the I-measure theory
that we use in the remainder of this section; for further details and examples, see chapter 3 of
[
Yeu08
]
.

Let
h\in\Gamma_{n}
denote a polymatroid defined over the variables
\mathord{\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}}
. As mentioned, the I-Measure theory formulates a one-to-one correspondence between Shannon’s information measures and set theory. Hence, every variable
X_{i}
of the polymatroid, is associated with a set
\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
.
The universal set is
\Lambda\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
, the union of all sets associated with the variables. The complement of the set
\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
is
\mathrm{m}^{c}(X_{i})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\Lambda\setminus\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
.
Let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
. We recall that
X_{\mathcal{I}}
is the joint random variable

X_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}(X_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathcal{I})
(see Notation
2.1
), and we denote
\mathrm{m}(X_{\mathcal{I}})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
.

{defi}

The field
\mathcal{F}_{n}
generated by sets
\mathrm{m}(X_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{m}(X_{n})
is the collection of sets which can be obtained by any sequence of usual set operations (union, intersection, complement, and difference) on
\mathrm{m}(X_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{m}(X_{n})
.

The
atoms
of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are sets of the form
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
, where
Y_{i}
is either
\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
or
\mathrm{m}^{c}(X_{i})
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}
the atoms of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Note that by our choice of the universal set,
\Lambda\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textsf{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{m}(X_{i})
, the atom
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{m}^{c}(X_{i})
degenerates to the empty set. This is because:

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{m}^{c}(X_{i})=\left(\mathrm{m}(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\mathrm{m}(X_{n})\right)^{c}=\Lambda^{c}=\emptyset

(37)

Hence, there are
2^{n}-1
non-empty atoms in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

We call a function
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\real

set additive
if, for every pair of disjoint sets
A
and
B
, it holds that
\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A){+}\mu(B)
.
A real function
\mu
defined on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is called

9.
Discussion and Future Work

Number of Repairs.

A natural way to measure the degree of a
constraint in a relation instance
R
is by the number of repairs
needed to enforce the constraint on
R
. In the case of a key
constraint,
X\rightarrow Y
, where
XY=\Omega
, our information-theoretic
measure is naturally related to the number of repairs, as follows. If

h(Y|X)=c
, where
h
is the entropy of the empirical distribution
on
R
, then one can check
|R|/|\Pi_{X}(R)|\leq 2^{c}
. Thus, the
number of repairs
|R|-|\Pi_{X}(R)|
is at most
(2^{c}-1)|\Pi_{X}(R)|
. We
leave for future work an exploration of the connections between the number
of repairs and information theoretic measures.

Small Model Property.

We have proven in
Sec.
4
that several classes of
implications (including saturated CIs, FDs, and MVDs) have a “small
model” property: if the implication holds for all uniform, 2-tuple
distributions, then it holds in general. In other words, it suffices
to check the implication on the step functions
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. One
question is whether this small model property continues to hold for
other tractable classes of implications in the literature. For
example, Geiger and Pearl
[
GP93
]
give an
axiomatization (and, hence, a decision procedure) for
marginal

CIs. However, marginal CIs do not have the same small model property.
Indeed, the implication

(X\perp Y),(X\perp Z)\Rightarrow(X\perp YZ)

holds for all
uniform 2-tuple distributions (because

I_{h}(X;YZ)\leq I_{h}(X;Y)+I_{h}(X;Z)
holds for all step functions),
however it fails for the “parity distribution” in
Fig.
1
(b). We leave for future work an investigation
of the small model property for other classes of constraints.

Bounds on the Factor
\lambda
.

In the early stages of this work
we conjectured that all CIs in
\Gamma_{n}
admit a 1-relaxation, until
we discovered the counterexample in Th.
6
, where

\lambda=3
. On the other hand, the only general upper bound is

(2^{n})!
. None of them is likely to be tight. We leave for future
work the task of finding tighter bounds for
\lambda
.
