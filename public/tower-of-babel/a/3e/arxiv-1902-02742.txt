Half-Spin Tautological Relations and Faber’s Proportionalities of Kappa Classes This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Integrability, Geometry, Moduli in honor of Motohico Mulase for his 65th birthday. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Mulase.html
Elba GARCIA-FAILDE †, Reinier KRAMER ‡, Danilo LEWAŃSKI ‡ and Sergey SHADRIN §

Abstract
We employ the 1 / 2 1/2 -spin tautological relations to provide a particular combinatorial identity. We show that this identity is a statement equivalent to Faber’s formula for proportionalities of kappa-classes on ℳ g \mathcal{M}_{g} , g ≥ 2 g\geq 2 . We then prove several cases of the combinatorial identity, providing a new proof of Faber’s formula for those cases.

Introduction

The moduli spaces of curves
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
and their Deligne–Mumford compactifications
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
are central objects in modern mathematics. Although in general their Chow rings are inifinite-dimensional, there are finite-dimensional subrings, the tautological rings
R^{*}
, that contain most ‘naturally occuring’ classes. These rings have been studied since the foundational work of Mumford
[
13
]
and Faber
[
4
]
. Overviews of the main results on these rings can be found in
[
14
,
19
,
20
,
22
]
.

The system of tautological rings
\{R^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n})\}_{g,n}
can be defined succinctly as the smallest system of subalgebras of the Chow rings closed under pushforwards along the three tautological maps

\displaystyle\pi\colon\ \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n},

\displaystyle\rho\colon\ \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+1}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{h,m+1}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g+h,n+m},

\displaystyle\sigma\colon\ \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n+2}\times\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g+1,n},

where the first map forgets the last marked point and the other two glue two marked points together, see
[
5
]
. This system of rings is also closed under pullbacks along the above-mentioned maps, and it contains the natural tautological
\psi
-,
\kappa
-, and
\lambda
-classes, after which the rings are named.

In fact, a set of additive generators of the tautological rings can be given by dual graphs, which are graphs with
n
leaves (or labelled half-edges) decorated as follows: to each vertex
v
we attach a genus
g(v)
and a product of
\kappa
-classes, and to each half-edge we attach a power of a
\psi
-class. Vertices represent stable components of algebraic curves, half-edges represent special points (i.e., can be either nodes of the curve or leaves), among which labelled half-edges represent the
n
leaves.
We interpret the dual graphs as follows: we attach to vertices of genus
g^{\prime}
and valency
n^{\prime}
a copy of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g^{\prime},n^{\prime}}
, we form the product of all
\kappa
- and
\psi
-classes attached to the vertex or to its half-edges, and we push it forward along the gluing tautological maps given by the edges.

The tautological rings of the open space
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
and its partial compactifications are defined via restriction from
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
. As
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
corresponds to all smooth curves, all graphs with at least one edge restrict to zero on this space; the only tautological classes are polynomials in
\kappa
- and
\psi
-classes. We denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\textup{ct}}_{g,n}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\textup{rt}}_{g,n}
the partial compactifications of
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
by stable nodal curves of compact type and with rational tails, respectively.

In fact there are many relations between dual graphs in the tautological ring. These relations are called tautological relations and they encode the structure of the tautological rings. Therefore, the understanding of tautological rings boils down to the understanding of their tautological relations.

1.1
Half-spin relations

One way of approaching tautological relations is via cohomological field theories (CohFTs).
One particular CohFT has played a distinguished role in this context. It is a shifted version of Witten’s
r
-spin class
[
18
,
21
]
, and has been thoroughly studied by Pandharipande–Pixton–Zvonkine in two different ways
[
15
,
16
]
. On the one hand, Witten’s class is quasi-homogeneous, and this gives a degree bound for its shifted version. On the other hand, any semi-simple CohFT can be constructed via Givental’s action from its degree zero part, and this gives an explicit description for the shifted Witten’s class, that seemingly has non-trivial contributions in high degrees. As both approaches should lead to the same result, this gives tautological relations in degrees above the bound, called
r
-spin relations.

In
[
16
]
, it was also proved that the Witten
r
-spin class is polynomial in
r
for
r
large. This makes it possible to choose
r
, which a priori should be an integer greater or equal to two, to be any number. In
[
9
]
, the authors observed that taking the value
r=\frac{1}{2}
results in much simplified relations compared to the case of general
r
. These relations are called half-spin relations.

The coefficients of the half-spin relations are proportional to expressions of the type

\displaystyle\binom{2a+1}{2d}\cdot(2d-1)!!,\qquad a,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

(1.1)

(cf.
[
9
, Lemma 2.1]
). It turns out that further applications of half-spin relations require a better understanding the combinatorial structure of these numbers. We propose some purely combinatorial questions about them, cf. Question
5.2
and Conjecture
5.7
that arose naturally from our analysis of Faber’s conjecture.

1.2
Faber’s intersection numbers conjecture

The top tautological group
R^{g-2}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
is one-dimensional, spanned by the class
\kappa_{g-2}
,
g\geq 2

[
3
,
12
]
. All other monomials of kappa-classes,
\kappa_{a_{1}}\cdots\kappa_{a_{\ell}}
,
\ell\geq 1
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\geq 1
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{\ell}=g-2
, are proportional to
\kappa_{g-2}
with some coefficients of proportionality. These coefficients were conjectured by Faber in
[
4
, Conjecture 1c]
, and he also observed in
op. cit.
that the class
\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g-1}
vanishes on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{\textup{rt}}_{g,n}
. An equivalent form of his conjecture (now theorem) can be represented as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Faber’s intersection numbers conjecture)
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
g\geq 2
. For any
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 1
,
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}=g-2+n
, there exists a constant
C_{g}
that only depends on
g
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2g-3+n)!}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}}\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g-1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}^{d_{i}}(2d_{i}-1)!!=C_{g}.

(1.2)

Remark 1.2
.

In particular,
\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,1}}\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g-1}\psi_{1}^{g-1}=\frac{(2g-2)!}{(2g-3)!!}C_{g}
. This integral is computed in
[
4
, Theorem 2]
, so it is known that
C_{g}=\frac{|B_{2g}|}{2^{2g-1}(2g)!}
, where
B_{2g}
is the Bernoulli number.

This theorem has several proofs: Getzler and Pandharipande
[
6
]
derived it from the Virasoro constrains for
\mathbb{P}^{2}
proved by Givental
[
7
]
. Liu and Xu
[
11
]
derived it from an identity for the
n
-point functions of the intersection numbers of
\psi
-classes that comes from the KdV equation. Goulden, Jackson, and Vakil proved it for
n\leq 3
using the reductions of Faber–Hurwitz classes
[
8
]
. Buryak and the fourth author proved it using relations for double ramification cycles
[
1
]
. Finally, Pixton showed the compatibility of this theorem with Faber–Zagier relations in
[
17
]
, also proved by Faber and Zagier (unpublished, see a remark in
[
16
]
). Together with a result of
[
16
]
, this shows that Faber–Zagier relations imply this theorem.

In fact, all these independent proofs are inspired by quite different ideas and they all lead to a deeper understanding of the geometry of the moduli spaces of curves. In this paper, we use the half-spin relations to transform Faber’s conjecture into a combinatorial identity. This gives insight into the use of half-spin relations and the related combinatorics of expressions of the form of (
1.1
).
On the other hand, it gives insight into Faber’s formula itself, as we extend it to formal negative powers of
\psi
-classes.

We then prove several cases of the combinatorial identity, providing a new proof of Faber’s conjecture for
n
less than or equal to five.

1.3
Organization of the paper

In Section
2
, we give the definition of the half-spin relations. In Section
3
, we reduce Faber’s conjecture (Theorem
1.1
) to a combinatorial identity using the half-spin relations. In Section
4
, we introduce formal negative powers of
\psi
-classes to reduce the combinatorial identity to a simpler one, which we refer to as the main combinatorial identity of the paper. In Section
5
, we investigate this identity from a combinatorial viewpoint and conjecture a refinement. In Appendix
A
, we give a combinatorial proof of the identity in low-degree cases.

2
Definition of half-spin relations

We will define two specific cases of the half-spin relations in
R^{\geq g}(\mathcal{M}^{\textup{ct}}_{g,n})
, as this is all we need for the rest of the paper. For a more general version and the construction, see
[
9
]
.

First we need to define stable graphs.

Definition 2.1
.

A
stable graph
is the data
\Gamma=(V,H,L,E,g\colon V\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},v\colon H\to V,\iota\colon H\to H)
such that

1)

V
is the vertex set with genus function
g
;

2)

\iota
is an involution of
H
, the set of half-edges;

3)

the set
L
of legs or leaves is given by the fixed points of
\iota
;

4)

the set
E
of edges is given by the two-point orbits of
\iota
;

5)

v
sends a half-edge to the vertex it is attached to;

6)

the graph given by
(V,E)
is connected;

7)

for each vertex
w\in V
, the stability condition holds:
2g(w)-2+n(w)>0
, where
n(w)=|v^{-1}(w)|
is the valence of
w
.

For such a stable graph, its
genus
is given by
g(\Gamma)=\sum_{v\in V}g(v)+h^{1}(\Gamma)
, where
h^{1}(\Gamma)
is the first Betti number of the graph. The
type
of a stable graph
\Gamma
is given by
(g(\Gamma),|L|)
.

We recall that the
r
-spin relations are proved in
[
16
]
by taking the Cohomological Field Theory given by Witten’s
r
-spin class, and showing that it is polynomial in
r
in a certain way. This is a subtle argument, hinging on the primary fields
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
attached to the leaves. For the
r
-spin theory, these are numbers between
0
and
r-2
, such that
A\coloneqq\sum a_{i}\equiv g-1+D\mod r-1
, where
D
is the degree of the relation. To show polynomiality in
r
, this congruence is lifted to an equality
A=g-1+D+x(r-1)
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. If
x=0
, all the primary fields can be taken constant in
r
, and polynomiality follows from the argument of
[
16
]
.

For
x\geq 1
, however, the argument is more complicated, as the polynomiality does not hold over all of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
. However, under certain conditions, it still holds on certain subspaces, where we can then use it to get half-spin relations on these subspaces. As taking
r=\frac{1}{2}
is in effect taking a linear combination of relations for integer graphs, we do get relations on all of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, but their description is not explicit outside the given subspace. For more details, see
[
9
]
. We will only give the half-spin relations needed for this paper; they only use trees.

Definition 2.2
.

Define the polynomials

\displaystyle Q_{m}(a)\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}m!}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\left(a+1-\frac{k}{2}\right).

(2.1)

Let
n\geq 2
,
D\geq g
and
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n}
be non-negative integers, called
primary fields
, with sum
A\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=g-1+D
. Consider all stable
trees

\Gamma=(V,H,L,E,g,v,\iota)
of type
(g,n)
and decorate them in the following way:

•

On each leg labeled by
i
, place the sum
\sum_{d_{i}=0}^{a_{i}}Q_{d_{i}}(a_{i})\psi_{i}^{d_{i}}
, and place the integer
a_{i}-d_{i}
on the corresponding half-edge fixed by
\iota
.

•

On each vertex
v
, we use the tree structure to work inwards from the leaves. If we have determined all half-integers
b_{i}
at its incident half-edges except one, say
b_{0}
, then
b_{0}\coloneqq g(v)-1-\sum_{i}b_{i}
if this is at least zero. Otherwise, set
b_{0}\coloneqq g(v)-\frac{3}{2}-\sum_{i}b_{i}
.

•

On each edge with half-integers
a
and
b
on its two half-edges, place the sum
-\sum_{m>0}Q_{n}(a+m)(\psi+\psi^{\prime})^{m-1}\delta_{a+b+m,-\frac{3}{2}}
, where
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
are the
\psi
-classes corresponding to the two half-edges.

The
half-spin relation for
x=0
,
\Omega_{g,n}^{D}(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n})=0\in R^{D}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\textup{rt}})
, is given by the sum of these decorated stable graphs with these coefficients being zero in degree
D
.

Remark 2.3
.

Although the coefficient on the edge does not seem to be symmetric in
a
and
b
, a simple calculation shows it actually is.

In fact, the coefficient on an edge with
a
and
b
on its two half-edges coming from the
r
-spin relations is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi+\psi^{\prime}}\bigg(\delta_{a+b,-\frac{3}{2}}-\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{c,d\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}}Q_{m}(c)Q_{m^{\prime}}(d)\delta_{a,c-m}\delta_{b,d-m^{\prime}}\delta_{c+d,-\frac{3}{2}}\psi^{m}(\psi^{\prime})^{m^{\prime}}\bigg).

(2.2)

This is equal to the coefficient given in the definition, but we give this equation as well, as it is closer to the form of the
r
-spin relations in
[
16
]
, and because it is useful for the rest of the paper. In this formula, the numbers
c
and
d
should be interpreted as being placed near the middle of the edge, or at the end of the half-edges. In this way, they are similar to the
a_{i}
on the leaves, and they will also be called primary fields. Meanwhile, the
a_{i}-d_{i}
are similar to the
a
and
b
on the edges. This analogy will be used in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. The equality can be seen from the relation

\displaystyle Q_{m}(a+m)Q_{m^{\prime}}(b+m^{\prime})=\binom{m+m^{\prime}}{m}Q_{m+m^{\prime}}(a+m+m^{\prime})\qquad\text{if $a+b+m+m^{\prime}=-\frac{3}{2}$.}

Remark 2.4
.

These relations have been proved in
[
9
]
, by specialization of the
r
-spin relations proved in
[
16
]
. The proof in
[
9
]
uses the polynomiality of the
r
-spin relations in
r
, which is also proved in
[
16
]
. The half-spin relations can be extended to all of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
, but this extension is not unique, much less explicit, and unnecessary for our purpose. However, their extension is of principal importance for applications in Gromov–Witten theory, since it allows to prove the following statement (a reformulation of
[
9
, Lemma 5.2]
):

Proposition 2.5
.

Any monomial of
\psi
-classes of degree at least
\max(g,1)
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}
can be expressed in terms of the boundary classes that involve no
\kappa
-classes, that is, in terms of the dual graphs with at least one edge, decorated only by
\psi
-classes.

This reformulation of
[
9
, Lemma 5.2]
is noted in
[
2
]
(where an alternative approach to the same statement is developed), and in this reformulation Proposition
2.5
immediately resolves Conjecture 3.14 in
[
10
]
and Conjecture 3 in
[
5
]
.

We will also need the half-spin relation on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
for
x=1
. We give them here on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\textup{ct}}
for general
g
, which reduces to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
for
g=0
.

Definition 2.6
.

Now, let
n\geq 2
,
D\geq g+1
, and the primary fields
a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n-1}
be non-negative integers, and
a_{n}\leq-\frac{3}{2}
with sum
A=g+D-\frac{3}{2}
. Then the
half-spin relation for
x=1
,
\Omega_{g,n}^{D}(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{n})=0\in R^{D}(\mathcal{M}_{g,n}^{\textup{ct}})
, is given by a sum over decorated stable trees with the same conditions as the ones for
x=0
.

Remark 2.7
.

Although the (local) conditions are the same, the (global) relations are different, because the sum of the primary fields is different.

3
A combinatorial identity from half-spin relations

In this section, we employ the half-spin relations to prove the following proposition. We shall denote by
\llbracket n\rrbracket
the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, we have the following equation:

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+k\end{subarray}}\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k}Q_{d_{j}+|I_{j}|-1}(a_{[I_{j}]}).

(3.1)

Here we denote
\sum_{\ell\in I_{j}}a_{\ell}
by
a_{[I_{j}]}
and

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}\coloneqq\frac{1}{C_{g}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}}\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}^{d_{i}},

(3.2)

where
C_{g}
is an arbitrary constant depending only on
g

(
for instance, it is convenient to assume that
C_{g}
is the constant given in Remark
1.2
)
.

Moreover, for a fixed
g\geq 0
, the whole system of equations (
3.1
)
(
we can vary parameters
n\geq 2
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
determines all integrals
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}
,
k\geq 2
, in terms of
\langle\tau_{g-1}\rangle_{g}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that equation (
3.1
) can also be considered as an equation for the classes in
R^{g-2}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
, once we replace the symbols
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}
by the restrictions to
R^{g-2}(\mathcal{M}_{g})
of the classes
\pi_{*}(\psi_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots\psi_{k}^{d_{k}})
, where
\pi\colon\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
.

Proof.

We will use relations in
R^{g-2+n}(\mathcal{M}^{\textup{rt}}_{g,n})
given by half-spin relations for
A=2g-3+n
. Note that to produce relations
D\coloneqq g-2+n
must be at least
g
, and hence we have
n\geq 2
.

The restriction to
\mathcal{M}^{\textup{rt}}_{g,n}
means that all allowed stable trees must have one vertex
v_{g}
of genus
g
, and all other vertices have genus
0
. If we cut
v_{g}
from such a stable tree, it falls apart in several connected components, which are called rational tails.

The leaves are then distributed among these rational tails, and this gives a decomposition
\llbracket n\rrbracket=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}
. If
|I_{i}|=1
, this corresponds to a leaf attached to
v_{g}
. We will therefore consider all graphs where the points with indices in
I_{i}
lie on a separate rational tail, for every
i=1,\dots,k
.

We want to simplify these relations by applying half-spin relations in genus zero to each of the tails. Hence, we will now consider a particular rational tail that contains points with indices in
I\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket
, with
|I|\geq 2
. Consider the edge that attaches this rational tail to the genus
g
component, and assume that it is decorated by
\psi^{d}
at the node on the genus
g
component. We call this edge the root edge,
e_{r}
, for this tail.

The total (cohomological) degree of the rest of this tail is given by the number of edges, excluding this one, together with the total number of
\psi
-classes, excluding this one. We will call this degree
D_{I}
. It cannot be larger than
|I|-2
, since
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,|I|+1}=|I|-2
and the graph is constructed via pushforward along a map from this space. This means that the end of the root edge which connects to the rational tail is decorated with
\psi^{\ell}
for some
0\leq\ell\leq|I|-2
.

Figure 1:
A dual graph of genus
g
with rational tails and
n
leaves. The marked points with indices in
I_{i}\subset\llbracket n\rrbracket
are attached to the rational tail denoted by
\text{RT}_{I_{i}}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.

Let us now discuss the coefficient corresponding to the root edge. Using the congruences for the primary fields for the leaf contributions and the vertex contributions to be non-zero, together with the fact that all vertices in the rational tail correspond to genus
0
components and the total number of remaining
\psi
classes and edges is equal to
D_{I}-\ell
, the primary field at the genus
0
end of the root edge must be equal to
b_{[0]}\coloneqq-\frac{3}{2}-a_{[I]}+(D_{I}-\ell)
. The primary field at the genus
g
end of the root edge must be equal to
b_{[g]}\coloneqq a_{[I]}-(D_{I}+d+1)
.

The coefficient of the contribution of the root edge reads

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell=0}^{D_{I}}\psi_{[g]}^{d}\psi_{[0]}^{\ell}\bigg[Q_{d+1}(\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 2.15277pt\cr$\braceld\leaders{\vrule height=0.39998pt,depth=0.0pt}{\hfill}\braceru\bracelu\leaders{\vrule height=0.39998pt,depth=0.0pt}{\hfill}\bracerd$\crcr\kern 3.01385pt\cr$\hfil\displaystyle{a_{[I]}-D_{I}}\hfil$\crcr}}}\limits^{{\makebox[33.10255pt]{$\phantom{\braceld}\leaders{\hbox{\ }}{\hfill}\phantom{\braceru}\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\scriptsize$\scriptstyle b_{[g]}+d+1$}\phantom{\bracelu}\leaders{\hbox{\ }}{\hfill}\phantom{\bracerd}$}}})Q_{\ell}\bigg(\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 2.15277pt\cr$\braceld\leaders{\vrule height=0.39998pt,depth=0.0pt}{\hfill}\braceru\bracelu\leaders{\vrule height=0.39998pt,depth=0.0pt}{\hfill}\bracerd$\crcr\kern 3.01385pt\cr$\hfil\displaystyle{{-}\frac{3}{2}-a_{[I]}+D_{I}}\hfil$\crcr}}}\limits^{{\makebox[56.43588pt]{$\phantom{\braceld}\leaders{\hbox{\ }}{\hfill}\phantom{\braceru}\makebox[0.0pt][c]{\scriptsize$\scriptstyle b_{[0]}+\ell$}\phantom{\bracelu}\leaders{\hbox{\ }}{\hfill}\phantom{\bracerd}$}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-Q_{d+2}(a_{[I]}-D_{I}+1)Q_{\ell-1}\left(-\frac{3}{2}-a_{[I]}+D_{I}-1\right)

Moreover, the only non-trivial

Up to now, everything we described was done in

We can do this on each tail individually, first using the string equation, which in this case reads
\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,|I|+1}}\psi_{[0]}^{D_{I}}=\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,|I|}}\psi_{[0]}^{D_{I}-1}

Summarising, the only surviving terms are the terms where all the marked points are partitioned as
\bigsqcup_{k}I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket
over rational tails consisting of a leaf or a single rational curve with all marked points attached to it, with coefficient

\displaystyle(-1)^{|I|-1}\psi_{[g]}^{d}\psi_{[0]}^{|I|-2}Q_{d+|I|-1}(a_{[I]}).

These terms pushforward to terms on

\displaystyle(-1)^{|I|-1}\psi_{I}^{d}Q_{d+|I|-1}(a_{[I]}).

Taking the product over all the tails and taking into account that the linear function

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{g}}\int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}}\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g-1}\cdot\colon\ R^{g-2}(\mathcal{M}_{g})\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}

is an isomorphism, the half-spin relations we found for

imply the combinatorial identity (

).

On the other hand, it is easy to see that these relations determine the intersections of all possible monomials in
\psi
-classes in terms of

(using the natural lexicographic order).
∎

4
Psi-classes of negative degree

In the previous section, we showed that Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to a system of combinatorial identities. The goal of this section is to reduce this system to a much nicer system of identities. In order to do this, we need to consider formal systems of correlators satisfying the string equation.

4.1
Formal negative degrees of psi-classes

Definition 4.1
.

Let
g\geq 2
. Consider a system of numbers
\big\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}\big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
that depends on
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
d_{1}+\dots+d_{k}=g-2+k
, and is symmetric in these variables. We say that this system of numbers
satisfies the string equation
if

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\tau_{0}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\tau_{d_{1}-\delta_{1j}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}-\delta_{kj}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}.

Example 4.2
.

The system of numbers

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}\bigg\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bigg\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}\bigg\rangle_{g}^{\star},&d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\geq 0,\\
0,&\text{at least one }d_{i}\text{ is negative}\end{cases}

satisfies the string equation, as follows, by definition, from equation (
3.6
).

Example 4.3
.

The system of numbers
\big\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}\big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}\coloneqq(2g-3+k)!/\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2d_{i}-1)!!

also satisfies the string equation (this can be checked by direct inspection).

Remark 4.4
.

These two examples coincide in the case when all
d_{i}
’s are positive and also in the case when all
d_{i}
’s except for one are positive and the remaining one is equal to zero. For other values of
(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
the numbers in these two examples are generally different.

The string equation allows to choose the values of all numbers
\big\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}\big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
,
\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}\not=0
,
k\geq 1
in an arbitrary way, and the rest of the numbers (where at least one index
d_{i}
is equal to zero) are linear combinations of these initial values with non-negative integer coefficients.

4.2
\boldsymbol{Q}

Fix

and

and let

be formal variables. Define

,

Consider the following expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\

For any

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})}{\partial\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}\equiv 0.

Assume

are negative, and the rest of the indices

are positive. Let us fix

Consider all terms in the expression
\mathcal{E}
satisfying the following conditions:

•

The correlator factor is a coefficient
\big\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}\big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}

, and at least

.

This list of conditions is equivalent to

\frac{\partial\big\langle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}\big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}{\partial\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}\not=0

•

The sets

•

For each

We can refine (

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{0}(J_{k+1})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\tau_{d_{i}}(J_{i})\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\langle\tau_{d_{j}-1}(J_{j}\sqcup J_{k+1})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\textup{ref}}_{g,n}(\vec{a})\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})}{\partial\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}=\sum_{I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket}\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\textup{ref}}_{g,n}(\vec{a})}{\partial\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(I_{1})\dotsb\tau_{d_{k}}(I_{k})\rangle^{\bullet}_{g}}\Bigg|_{\tau_{d}(I)=\tau_{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\

\displaystyle Q_{d_{11}+1-1}(a_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{m_{1}}Q_{d_{1j}+|I_{1j}|-1}(a_{[I_{1j}]})\frac{\partial\Big\langle\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(I_{1})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{0}(\{1\})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\end{subarray}}^{m_{1}}\tau_{d_{1j}}(I_{1j})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(I_{1})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{r=2}^{m_{1}}\frac{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{d_{1r}-1}(I_{1r}\sqcup\{1\})\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\not=r\end{subarray}}^{m_{1}}\tau_{d_{1j}}(I_{1j})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(I_{1})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}.

(4.4)

The case
a_{1}=-1/2
is more subtle. We use induction on
|I_{1}|
. Using the identity
Q_{p}(a)-Q_{p}(a-1/2)=-(a/2)\cdot Q_{p-1}(a-1)
and the derivative of the refined string equation (
4.4
), we can rewrite expression (
4.3
) as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=2}^{m_{1}}a_{[I_{1k}]}\cdot\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\end{subarray}}^{m_{1}}Q_{\tilde{d}_{1j}+|I_{1j}|-1}(\tilde{a}_{[I_{1j}]})\frac{\partial\Big\langle\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\end{subarray}}^{m_{1}}\tau_{\tilde{d}_{1j}}(I_{1j})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}}{\partial\Big\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(I_{1}\setminus\{1\})\prod\limits_{i=2}^{k}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\tau_{d_{ij}}(I_{ij})\Big\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}},

(4.5)

where in the coefficient of
a_{k}
we use the notation
\tilde{a}_{[I_{1p}]}\coloneqq a_{[I_{1p}]}-\delta_{pk}
,
p=2,\dotsc,m_{1}
, and
\tilde{d}_{1q}\coloneqq d_{1q}-\delta_{kq}
,
q=2,\dots,m_{1}
.

By our assumptions,
d_{1}<0
and
|I_{1}|+d_{1}-1\geq 0
, so
|I_{1}|\geq 2
. For the base case of the induction,
|I_{1}|=2
, we then have
d_{1}=-1
, so
d_{11}+d_{12}=d_{1}+m_{1}-1=0
, and therefore
d_{12}=0
. In this case, the
k
-sum and
j
-product in equation (
4.5
) collapse, yielding a coefficient
Q_{\tilde{d}_{12}+|I_{12}|-1}(\tilde{a}_{I_{[12]}})=Q_{-1}(a_{I_{[12]}}-1)=0
, proving the basis step.

For the induction step, we see that for each
k
the coefficient of
a_{[I_{1k}]}
in (
4.5
) is the polynomial in one fewer variables (namely, taking out the variable
a_{1}
) and size of
|I_{1}|
one less (by removing
1
) of exactly the same form as the summands in the third line of (
4.2
). Resumming over
m_{1}
,
d_{1j}
, and
I_{1,j}
with the sign coming from the third line of (
4.2
), this becomes equal to the third line of (
4.2
), which was zero by induction (where we use already that this third line vanishes for all
a_{i}=0,-1/2
).

Thus, the third line in (
4.2
) is equal to zero for
a_{1}=-1/2
as well as
a_{1}=0
, which implies it vanishes constantly. This implies the proposition.
∎

4.3
Applying formal negative degrees of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
-classes

to the combinatorial identity

We use the result of the previous section to reduce the system of identities (
3.4
) to a simpler one.

Proposition 4.6
.

Faber’s conjecture
(
Theorem
1.1
)
is equivalent to the following system of combinatorial identities.

For any
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
,

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+k\end{subarray}}\frac{(2g-3+k)!}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}(2d_{i}-1)!!}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k}Q_{d_{j}+|I_{j}|-1}(a_{[I_{j}]}).

(4.6)

Proof.

We have already shown that Faber’s conjecture is equivalent to the system of identities (
3.4
), where the correlators are replaced by the predicted value from the conjecture. So, it is sufficient to show that the system of identities (
3.4
) is equivalent to the system of identities (
4.6
). Note that the right hand side of (
3.4
) is a specialization of the expression
\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})
for the values of
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
given in Example
4.2
. The right hand side of (
4.6
) is a specialization of the expression
\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})
for the values of
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
given in Example
4.3
.

As observed before, a system of numbers
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
satisfying the string equation, see Definition
4.1
, is fixed by choosing all numbers for
\prod_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}\neq 0
arbitrarily and inferring the other cases from the string equation. We call the chosen numbers initial values.

The initial values of the system of numbers
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
given in Examples
4.2
and
4.3
coincide for all
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}>0
and differ when at least one of
d_{i}
’s is negative. Proposition
4.5
implies that the initial values
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
with at least one
d_{i}
negative have no impact on the value of
\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})
. Therefore, a specialization of the expression
\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})
for the values of
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
given in Example
4.2
is equal to zero if and only if the same specialization of the expression
\mathcal{E}_{g,n}(\vec{a})
for the values of
\langle\tau_{d_{1}}\cdots\tau_{d_{k}}\rangle_{g}^{\bullet}
given in Example
4.3
is equal to zero. Therefore, the system of identities (
3.4
) is equivalent to the system of identities (
4.6
).
∎

5
The main combinatorial identity and its structure

In the previous section we used formal negative degree psi-classes in order to simplify the system of combinatorial identities to which Faber’s conjecture is equivalent (Proposition
4.6
). We now want to substitute the
Q
-polynomials by their definition and rearrange the terms to obtain the following statement.

Corollary 5.1

(of Proposition
4.6
)
.

Faber’s conjecture
(
Theorem
1.1
)
is equivalent to the following system of combinatorial identities.

For any
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}

\displaystyle\hphantom{0 =}{}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

(5.1)

Here by
a_{[I_{j}]}
we denote
\sum_{\ell\in I_{j}}a_{\ell}
and by
|I_{j}|
we denote the cardinality of the set
I_{j}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
,
j=1,\dots,k
.

Proof.

Recall that, for
m\geq 0
,
Q_{m}(a)=((-1)^{m}/2^{3m}m!)\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{2m-1}(2a+1-i)
. If its argument is a non-negative integer, we can rewrite
Q_{m}(a)
as
\binom{2a+1}{2m}\cdot(2m-1)!!\cdot(-1)^{m}/2^{2m}
. For
m<0
,
Q_{m}(a)\equiv 0
. Then it is easy to see that equation (
5.1
) is obtained from equation (
4.6
) by the relabelling
d_{j}+|I_{j}|-1\rightsquigarrow d_{j}
and dividing by a common factor of
(-1)^{g-2+n}/2^{2(g-2+n)}
.
∎

In the rest of the paper we refer to equation (
5.1
) as the main combinatorial identity. This section is devoted to a purely combinatorial analysis of this identity. Clearly, since Faber’s conjecture is proved, the main combinatorial identity holds true. However, we are interested in an independent proof of it, in order to obtain a new proof of Faber’s conjecture. We produce such a proof for
n\leq 5
in the next section.

Question 5.2
.

Is there a purely combinatorial way to prove the combinatorial identity (
5.1
) for all
n
?

In fact, one of the purposes of the present article is to pose the question to the combinatorial community about a possible enumerative interpretation of our identity.

5.1
Polynomials vanishing in the integer points of some simplices

We denote the right hand side of (
5.1
) by
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. It can be considered as a polynomial of degree
2g-4+2n
in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
(since the binomial coefficient
\binom{2a_{[I]}+1}{2d}
is naturally a polynomial of degree
2d
in
a_{[I]}
). We can also rewrite it as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
f_{1}+\cdots+f_{k}=g-1\end{subarray}}\!\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2f_{j}+1}\frac{(2a_{[I_{j}]}-2f_{j}-1)!!}{(2a_{[I_{j}]}-2f_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!},

and refer to it as
R(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. The function
R(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is also a polynomial in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, where each term in the sum over
k=1,\dots,n
has degree
\sum_{j=1}^{k}2f_{j}+|I_{j}|=2g-2+n
, so the total degree of
R
is
2g-2+n
. Note that
P\not=R
(they even have different degrees); from the construction they coincide only on the simplex
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
.

Proposition 5.3
.

We have
P|_{a_{i}=0}\equiv 0
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Since
P
is symmetric in its variables, it is enough to prove this proposition for
a_{n}=0
. Consider an arbitrary splitting
I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. We want to append this splitting with the element
n
: either we add
\{n\}
as one of the sets (there are
k+1
ways to do this, since we can choose the number of this set from
1
to
k+1
shifting the indices of
I_{j}
accordingly), or we append
n
to one of the existing sets
I_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\dots k
. Consider the sum of all terms in
P
that correspond to these choices of splitting of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since the first
k+1
terms are all equal to each other, we can assume that we have
k+1
copies of the case
I_{k+1}=\{n\}
instead. Therefore, if we split the terms of
P
in this way, we get summands of the following form:

\displaystyle(k+1)\frac{(-1)^{k+1}(2g-3+k+1)!}{(k+1)!}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}+\dots+d_{k+1}\\
=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}\cdot\binom{2a_{n}+1}{2d_{k+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}+\dots+d_{k}\\
=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+2a_{n}\delta_{j,\ell}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

If
a_{n}=0
, then the first summand is nontrivial only for
d_{k+1}=0
. So, if we substitute
a_{n}=0
, then this expression is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}+\dots+d_{k}\\
=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\left(-(2g-2+k)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(2d_{\ell}+1-2|I_{\ell}|)\right).

Note that the last factor is equal to zero. Since the definition of
P
reduces to the sum over
I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\{1,\dots,n-1\}
of the terms that we considered here, and we never used the restriction of
P
to the simplex
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, we have
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},0)\equiv 0
.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

The function
\tilde{P}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\coloneqq P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})/\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
is a polynomial in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of degree
2g-4+n
.

So, we have a collection of symmetric polynomials of quite small degree (that is, smaller than what one expects trying to construct such non-trivial polynomials using the Lagrange interpolation, for instance) vanishing in all integer points of the certain simplices:

•

P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is a polynomial of degree
2g-4+2n
that vanishes at all integer points of the simplex
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\geq 0
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
.

•

R(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is a polynomial of degree
2g-2+n
that vanishes at all integer points of the simplex
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\geq 0
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
.

•

\tilde{P}(a_{1}+1,\dots,a_{n}+1)
is a polynomial of degree
2g-4+n
that vanishes at all integer points of the simplex
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\geq 0
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3
.

5.2
Combinatorial reduction of the identity for
\boldsymbol{a_{i}=1}

In this section we give a combinatorial reduction of the identity (
5.1
) in the case one of the arguments
a_{i}
is equal to
1
.

Proposition 5.5
.

For any
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},1)-P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)=P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\cdot\left(4\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-8g+10-2n\right).

(5.2)

Proof.

Let
\{n+1\}\sqcup J\subset\{1,\dots,n+1\}
. Consider the corresponding factor in a summand in
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},1)
assuming
I_{j}\coloneqq\{n+1\}\sqcup J
, for some
j
, and denoting the corresponding index
d_{j}
by
d
. We have the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\binom{2(a_{[J]}+1)+1}{2d}\frac{(2d-1)!!}{(2d-1-2|J|)!!}=\binom{2(a_{[J]}+0)+1}{2d}\frac{(2d-1)!!}{(2d-1-2|J|)!!}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\binom{2a_{[J]}+1}{2\tilde{d}}\frac{(2\tilde{d}-1)!!}{(2\tilde{d}+1-2|J|)!!}\cdot\big(4a_{[J]}+3-2\tilde{d}\big),

(5.3)

where
\tilde{d}=d-1
. The first term on the right hand side is equal to the corresponding factor in the same summand in
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)
.
The second term gives a summand in
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with a coefficient.

There are
(k+1)
ways to obtain the summand

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}

in
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
(here
I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket
,
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+n
, and we omit the factor
1/k!
that controls the permutations of the sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
) from the second term of the decomposition (
5.3
): either
J=I_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, or
J=\varnothing
. In the latter case, the extra coefficient that we get is equal to
-3(2g-2+k)
. Thus, the total coefficient of this summand is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(4a_{[J]}+3-2d_{j})-3(2g-2+k)=4\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-8g+10-2n,

which does not depend on the choice of
I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket
and
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+n
. This implies equation (
5.2
).
∎

If we restrict equation (
5.2
) to the simplex
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
and use that
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0)\equiv 0

(without any assumptions on
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
), we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 5.6
.

For any
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
, for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, we have

\displaystyle P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},1)=(2n-2)P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}).

In particular,
P(2g-2,1)=0
, and for
n\geq 2
the vanishing of
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
implies the vanishing of
P(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},1)
.

5.3
A conjectural refinement of the identity

In this section we formulate a conjectural refinement of the identity (
5.1
), which gives a natural strategy for its combinatorial proof. In particular, it allows to prove it for
n\leq 5
for all
g
.

We replace each factor
\binom{2a_{[I]}+1}{2d}
in each summand of the identity by the sum
\binom{2a_{[I]}}{2d}+\binom{2a_{[I]}}{2d-1}
. Then we collect all terms with the fixed number of factors where we have chosen to decrease
2d
to
2d-1
. Let us define
P_{n,t}
as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\llbracket k\rrbracket\\
|A|=n-t\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}}{2d_{j}-\delta_{j\in A}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}

for
t=0,\dots,n
. Here
\delta_{j\in A}
is equal to
1
for
j\in A
and to
0
otherwise.
For instance,

\displaystyle P_{n,0}=(-1)^{n}(2g-3+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}},

\displaystyle P_{n,1}=(-1)^{n}(2g-3+n)!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,\widehat{o_{i}},\dots o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
e_{i}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots\widehat{o_{i}}\cdots+o_{n}+e_{i}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\not=i\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_{n,0} =}{}+(-1)^{n-1}(2g-4+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\\
i<\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,\widehat{o_{i}},\dots,\widehat{o_{\ell}},\dots o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{i\ell}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots\widehat{o_{i}},\widehat{o_{\ell}}\cdots+o_{n}+o_{i\ell}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\not=i,\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\binom{2a_{i}+2a_{\ell}}{o_{i\ell}}\cdot o_{i\ell},

\displaystyle P_{n,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}\\
=\{1,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k}=2g-4+2n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}}{e_{j}}\frac{(e_{j}-1)!!}{(e_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

Here we use the notation
o_{\bullet}
(resp.,
e_{\bullet}
) to stress that these are odd (resp., even) non-negative numbers, and
\widehat{o_{i}}
means that this particular index is skipped.

Denote by
A_{n}
the sum
(-1)^{n}(2g-4+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}
.

Conjecture 5.7
.

For any
n\geq 2
and
t=0,\dots,n
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, we have

\displaystyle P_{n,t}=(-1)^{t}\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{t}-\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right)(2g-3+n+t)+2(t-1)\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right]A_{n}.

(5.4)

Observe that the right hand side is equal to

\displaystyle(-1)^{t}\left[\binom{n-1}{t}(2g-2+n+(t-1))-\binom{n-1}{t-1}(2g-2+n-(t-1))\right]A_{n}.

Remark 5.8
.

This conjecture does not follow from identity (
5.1
), so the equivalence of Faber’s conjecture and identity (
5.1
) does not prove equation (
5.4
). On the other hand, let us prove that equation (
5.4
) implies the main combinatorial identity (
5.1
). Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t}\left[\binom{n-1}{t}(2g-2+n+(t-1))-\binom{n-1}{t-1}(2g-2+n-(t-1))\right]

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(2g-3+n)\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t}\binom{n-1}{t}+(n-1)\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t}\binom{n-2}{t-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-(2g-2+n)\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t}\binom{n-1}{t-1}+(n-1)\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t}\binom{n-2}{t-2}=0.

Therefore, a combinatorial proof of Conjecture
5.7
would immediately give a new proof of Faber’s conjecture.

Proposition 5.9
.

Conjecture
5.7
is true for
n\leq 5
, any
t
, and for
t=0,1,2,3
, any
n
.

We prove this proposition in Appendix
A
.

Remark 5.10
.

Note that surprisingly this proposition is also true for
n=1
, though in this case we have no identity (
5.1
). Indeed, for
n=1
we have

\displaystyle A_{1}=-(2g-3)!\binom{4g-4}{2g-3},

\displaystyle P_{1,0}=-(2g-2)!\binom{4g-4}{2g-3}=(2g-2)A_{1},

\displaystyle P_{1,1}=-(2g-2)!\binom{4g-4}{2g-2}=(2g-1)A_{1},

which matches exactly equation (
5.4
) for
n=1
and
t=0,1
.

5.4
An equivalent formulation of the conjecture

In this section we reformulate Conjecture
5.7
via a
3
-term recursion in the
P_{n,t}
. Let
\tilde{P}_{n,t}
be

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{n,t}\coloneqq(-1)^{t}\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{t}-\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right)(2g-3+n+t)+2(t-1)\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right]A_{n}.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
. The following three statements, are equivalent:

i)

Conjecture
5.7
holds:

\displaystyle P_{n,t}=\tilde{P}_{n,t}\qquad\text{for all $t=0,1,\dotsc,n$}.

ii)

The
P_{n,t}
obey the following
3
-term recursion for all
t=0,\dotsc,n
:

\displaystyle(t+1)P_{n,t+1}+(n-(t+1))P_{n,t}=\frac{(-1)^{t}(2g-1)}{(2g-3+n)}\binom{n}{t}P_{n,0}.

iii)

The following expression does not depend on
t
:

\displaystyle t!(n-t)!(-1)^{t}\left[(t+1)P_{n,t+1}+(n-(t+1))P_{n,t}\right].

Proof.

Let

S_{g,n}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{t=0}^{n}P_{n,t}x^{t}
,
for which we already know the values

\displaystyle S_{g,n}(0)=P_{n,0},

\displaystyle S_{g,n}(1)=\sum_{t=0}^{n}P_{n,t}

\displaystyle\hphantom{S_{g,n}(1)}{}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}=\llbracket n\rrbracket\\
I_{j}\neq\varnothing,\,\forall\,j\in\llbracket k\rrbracket\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\sum_{i}d_{i}=g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}+1}{2d_{j}}\frac{(2d_{j}-1)!!}{(2d_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

Let us now compute the generating polynomial for the
\tilde{P}_{n,t}

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{g,n}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{t=0}^{n}\tilde{P}_{n,t}x^{t}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{S}_{g,n}(x)}{}=\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-x)^{t}\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{t}-\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right)(2g-3+n+t)+2(t-1)\binom{n-1}{t-1}\right]A_{n}.

First substitute
A_{n}=P_{n,0}/(2g-3+n)
and expand the whole expression in terms of the type

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{m}\binom{m}{t}p(t)(-x)^{t}x^{a},

where
p(t)
is a polynomial in
t
and
a
is an integer. For each such summand substitute
p(t)
with
p\big(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\big)
, apply Newton binomial theorem to
\sum_{t=0}^{m}\binom{m}{t}(-x)^{t}=(1-x)^{n}
, and finally apply the operator
p\big(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\big)
to the summand. Collecting the summands’ resulting contributions together gives

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{g,n}(x)=\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(2g-3+n)}\left((2g-1)(x+1)+(n-2)\right)P_{n,0}.

(5.5)

Observe that

S_{g,n}(0)=\tilde{S}_{g,n}(0),

and
\tilde{S}(x)
satisfies the non-homogeneous first order ODE

\displaystyle(2g-3+n)\left[(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)+(n-1)f(x)\right]=(1-x)^{n}(2g-1)P_{n,0},

(5.6)

which
S_{g,n}(x)
also satisfies if and only if

\displaystyle(t+1)P_{n,t+1}+(n-(t+1))P_{n,t}=\frac{(-1)^{t}(2g-1)}{(2g-3+n)}\binom{n}{t}P_{n,0},

(5.7)

for
t=0,1,\dots,n
. This proves the equivalence between
(i)
and
(ii)
. Clearly
(ii)
implies
(iii)
. Let us see that
(iii)
also implies
(ii)
. Assuming
(iii)
, we can evaluate
(ii)
at any
t
. Let us pick
t=0
for simplicity. Then
(ii)
reads

\displaystyle P_{n,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2g-1)}{(2g-3+n)}P_{n,0}-(n-1)P_{n,0}=\frac{(2g-1)-(n-1)(2g-3+n)}{(2g-3+n)}P_{n,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2-n)(2g-2+n)}{(2g-3+n)}P_{n,0}

\displaystyle=-\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{1}-\binom{n-1}{0}\right)(2g-2+n)+2\cdot 0\cdot\binom{n-1}{0}\right]A_{n},

which holds true from the case
t=1
in Proposition
5.9
. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of the main combinatorial identity for several cases

In this section we prove Proposition
5.9
. By Remark
5.8
, this implies a proof of the main combinatorial identity (
5.1
) for
n\leq 5
and
g\geq 2
. By Corollary
5.1
, this implies a new proof of Faber’s conjecture for
n\leq 5
and
g\geq 2
.

We would like to emphasize that we present in this proof a brief exposition of a quite intricate computation involving an interplay between binomials with even and odd denominators that we could not find deeply analyzed in the literature on combinatorics.

Proof of Proposition
5.9
.

The case
t=0
is obvious from the definition. For the other cases, we perform quite intricate computations based on the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

For any non-negative integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=A
, and
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
,
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}=T
, and for an arbitrary vector of parities
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
,
p_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}+\cdots+f_{n}=B\\
\tilde{f}_{i}=p_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{f_{i}}(f_{i})_{t_{i}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}+\cdots+f_{n}=2A-B+T\\
\tilde{f}_{i}=p_{i}+\tilde{t}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{f_{i}}(f_{i})_{t_{i}}.

Here by
\tilde{f}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
we denote the parity of
f\in\mathbb{Z}
, and by
(f)_{t}
we denote the Pochhammer symbol,
(f)_{t}\coloneqq f(f-1)\cdots(f+1-t)
.

Proof.

It follows from the following identity

\displaystyle\binom{2a}{f}(f)_{t}=\binom{2a-t}{f-t}(2a)_{t}=\binom{2a-t}{2a-f}(2a)_{t}=\binom{2a}{2a-f+t}(2a-f+t)_{t}.

∎

Example A.2
.

If
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}.

Thus we have an alternative definition of
A_{n}
as the sum

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}(2g-4+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}.

Below in all arguments we apply Lemma
A.1
assuming the condition
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}=2g-3+n
. We will also make use several times of the Chu–Vandermonde identity
\sum_{k+\ell=n}\binom{r}{k}\binom{s}{\ell}=\binom{r+s}{n}
, which follows from the expansion of the identity
(1+x)^{r}(1+x)^{s}=(1+x)^{r+s}
.

A.1
Case
\boldsymbol{t=1}

We have

\displaystyle P_{n,1}=(-1)^{n}(2g-3+n)!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,\widehat{o_{i}},\dots o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
e_{i}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots\widehat{o_{i}}\cdots+o_{n}+e_{i}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\not=i\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_{n,1} =}{}+(-1)^{n-1}(2g-4+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,\ell=1\\
i<\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,\widehat{o_{i}},\dots,\widehat{o_{\ell}},\dots o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{i\ell}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots\widehat{o_{i}},\widehat{o_{\ell}}\cdots+o_{n}+o_{i\ell}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\not=i,\ell\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\binom{2a_{i}+2a_{\ell}}{o_{i\ell}}\cdot o_{i\ell}.

In the first term we can replace the factor
(2g-3+n)
by the sum of the indices
o_{1}+\cdots\widehat{o_{i}}\cdots+o_{n}+e_{i}
. In the second term we can apply the Chu-Vandermonde identity

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{i}+2a_{\ell}}{o_{i\ell}}\cdot o_{i\ell}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{\geq 0}\\
e\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{>0}\\
o+e=o_{il}\end{subarray}}\left(\binom{2a_{i}}{o}\binom{2a_{\ell}}{e}+\binom{2a_{i}}{e}\binom{2a_{\ell}}{o}\right)(e+o).

On the right-hand side of this equation, we always have one even bottom argument in the binomial coefficients and all other bottom arguments are odd in both terms of this expression. The expression is totally symmetric with respect to the choice of the place of the even bottom argument. The coefficient in each summand of

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}(2g-4+n)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{2}\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
e_{1}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
e_{1}+o_{2}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{j=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}

(that is, we collect the terms where the even bottom argument is below
2a_{1}
) is equal to

\displaystyle\left(e_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}o_{i}\right)-\sum_{i=2}^{n}(e_{1}+o_{i})=-e_{1}\cdot\left(\binom{n-1}{1}-\binom{n-1}{0}\right).

Applying Lemma
A.1
to this term, we obtain

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}(2g-4+n)!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{2}\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
e_{1}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
e_{1}+o_{2}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-3+n\end{subarray}}\!\!\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{j=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\cdot(-1)\cdot e_{1}\cdot\left(\binom{n-1}{1}-\binom{n-1}{0}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(-1)^{n}(2g-4+n)!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1},\dots,o_{n}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{j}}{o_{j}}\cdot o_{1}\cdot(-1)\cdot\left(\binom{n-1}{1}-\binom{n-1}{0}\right).

Now, since the even bottom argument could be at any place, not only at the first one, we have to replace in the full computation of
P_{n,1}
the factor
o_{1}
above by
o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}=2g-2+n
. Thus we have

\displaystyle P_{n,1}=A_{n}\cdot(2g-2+n)\cdot(-1)\cdot\left(\binom{n-1}{1}-\binom{n-1}{0}\right),

which is exactly the desired result for
t=1
.

A.2
Case
\boldsymbol{t=2}

Let us describe
P_{n,2}
. All terms there have a common factor of
(2g-5+n)!
. The sum of bottom arguments of all binomial coefficients is always equal to
S\coloneqq 2g-2+n
. Taking into account the total symmetry with respect to the permutations of
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, we see that
(-1)^{n}P_{n,2}/(2g-5+n)!
has

\displaystyle\binom{n}{3}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}+2a_{3}}{o_{123}}\prod_{i=4}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{123}(o_{123}-2),

\displaystyle 3\binom{n}{4}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}}{o_{12}}\binom{2a_{3}+2a_{4}}{o_{34}}\prod_{i=5}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{12}o_{34},

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\binom{n-2}{1}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}}{o_{12}}\binom{2a_{3}}{e_{3}}\prod_{i=4}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{12}(S-2),

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}}{e_{12}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(e_{12}-1)(S-2),

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(S-1)(S-2).

For instance, in the first line we mean that we have the following sum of
\binom{n}{3}
summands

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{ijk}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0}\\
o_{\ell}\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)_{>0},\ \ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j,k\}\\
o_{ijk}+\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j,k\}}o_{\ell}=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\binom{2a_{i}+2a_{j}+2a_{k}}{o_{ijk}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\not=i,j,k\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{\ell}}{o_{\ell}}\cdot o_{ijk}(o_{ijk}-2).

We assume that the parity of the bottom arguments denoted by
o
(resp.,
e

Let us expand all binomial coefficients using the Chu–Vandermonde identity, that is, in such a way that we have exactly
n
factors of the type
\binom{2a_{i}}{f_{i}}
, where we also keep track of the possible parity of the bottom arguments. For instance,

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}+2a_{3}}{o_{123}}=\sum_{e_{1}+e_{2}+o_{3}=o_{123}}\!\!\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{o_{3}}+\sum_{e_{1}+o_{2}+e_{3}=o_{123}}\!\!\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{o_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{e_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{o_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}=o_{123}}\binom{2a_{1}}{o_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{e_{3}}+\sum_{o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}=o_{123}}\binom{2a_{1}}{o_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{o_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{o_{3}}.

We compute all the coefficients to obtain

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot 2e_{1}e_{2}\binom{n-2}{2},

\displaystyle\binom{n}{1}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{1}(o_{1}-1)\left(\binom{n-1}{2}-\binom{n-1}{1}\right),

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot 2o_{1}o_{2}\left(\binom{n-2}{1}-\binom{n-1}{1}\right),

\displaystyle\binom{n}{1}\ \text{terms of the type}\quad

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{1}\left(-\binom{n-1}{2}+\binom{n-1}{1}+\binom{n}{2}\right),

\displaystyle 1\ \text{term of the type}\quad

\displaystyle-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot 2\binom{n}{2}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
to the sum of all terms in the first line, we obtain the same terms as in the third line, with the coefficient

\displaystyle\left(\binom{n-1}{2}-\binom{n-1}{1}\right)S(S-1)+S\left(-\binom{n-1}{2}+\binom{n-1}{1}+\binom{n}{2}\right)-2\binom{n}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=(S-2)\cdot\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{2}-\binom{n-1}{1}\right)(S+1)+2\binom{n-1}{1}\right]

(2g-4+n)
and the desired coefficient of

completes the proof of this case.

A.1
to all these terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n})(2g-5+n)C_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(2g-4+n)(2g-5+n)C_{2}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(2g-4+n)(2g-5+n)C_{2}.

A.3.3
Third group of terms

Denote
6\left(\binom{n-1}{3}-\binom{n-1}{2}\right)
by
C_{3}
. With this coefficient we have terms of the following type:

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{1}\cdot C_{3}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
to all these terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n})C_{3}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-4+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(2g-4+n)C_{3}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(2g-4+n)C_{3}.

A.3.4
Fourth group of terms

Denote
-2\binom{n-2}{1}
by
C_{4}
. With this coefficient we have terms of the following type:

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{e_{3}}\prod_{i=4}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot 3e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}C_{4},

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{1}o_{2}(o_{2}-1)C_{4},

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{1}(e_{1}-1)o_{2}C_{4},

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{1}o_{2}o_{3}C_{4}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
to the first two lines, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot\bigg(\sum_{i<j}o_{i}o_{j}\bigg)\cdot(o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}-2)C_{4}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot\bigg(\sum_{i<j}o_{i}o_{j}\bigg)\cdot(2g-4+n)C_{4}.

Applying Lemma
A.1
to the last two lines, we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{i<j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}+e_{j}+\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}}o_{\ell}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}\binom{2a_{j}}{e_{j}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\not=i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{i}e_{j}\cdot\left(e_{i}+e_{j}+\!\!\!\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}}\!\!\!o_{\ell}\right)C_{4}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\sum_{i<j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}+e_{j}+\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}}o_{\ell}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}\binom{2a_{j}}{e_{j}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\not=i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{i}e_{j}\cdot(2g-4+n)C_{4}.

It follows from Lemma
A.1
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+\cdots+o_{n}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i<j}o_{i}o_{j}\right)-\sum_{i<j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{i}+e_{j}\\
+\sum_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i,j\}}o_{\ell}\\
=2g-2+n\end{subarray}}\!\!\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}\binom{2a_{j}}{e_{j}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\not=i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{i}e_{j}=0.

Hence, the total sum of all terms with the coefficient
C_{4}
is equal to
0
.

A.3.5
Final computation

In order to complete the proof of the case
t=3
it is sufficient to observe that

\displaystyle(2g-2+n)(2g-3+n)(2g-4+n)C_{1}+(2g-4+n)(2g-5+n)C_{2}+(2g-4+n)C_{3}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-(2g-4+n)(2g-5+n)\left[\left(\binom{n-1}{3}-\binom{n-1}{2}\right)(2g+n)+4\binom{n-1}{2}\right].

A.4
Case
\boldsymbol{n=t=4}

In this case
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{4}=2g+1
. We have the following formula for
P_{4,4}
:

\displaystyle\frac{P_{4,4}}{(2g-1)!}=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!(2g-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}\\
=\{1,\dots,4\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k}=2g+4\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}}{e_{j}}\frac{(e_{j}-1)!!}{(e_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

Note that if
k=1
, then
(2g-3+k)!=(2g-2)!
. But then this term looks like

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}+2a_{3}+2a_{4}}{2g+4}(2g+3)(2g+1)(2g-1),

and the last factor here still allows us to extract the common coefficient of
(2g-1)!
. With that remark we see that every term in the expression for
\frac{P_{4,4}}{(2g-1)!}
above is multiplied by a quadratic polynomial in
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
.

Applying the Chu–Vandermonde identity in the same way as in the previous cases, we obtain terms of the following type:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{4}\binom{2a_{i}}{e_{i}}\cdot 0,\qquad-\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{o_{3}}\binom{2a_{4}}{o_{3}}\cdot 2e_{1}e_{2},\qquad-\prod_{i=1}^{4}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot o_{1}(o_{1}-1).

Applying Lemma
A.1
to all these terms, we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}\\
+o_{4}=2g\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}+o_{4})(o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}+o_{4}-1)=-(2g-1)\cdot\frac{A_{4}}{(2g-1)!},

which confirms this case of the proposition.

A.5
Case
\boldsymbol{n=t=5}

In this case
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{5}=2g+2
. We have the following formula for
P_{5,5}
:

\displaystyle\frac{P_{5,5}}{(2g-1)!}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2g-3+k)!}{k!(2g-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup I_{k}\\
=\{1,\dots,5\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\in(2\mathbb{Z})_{\geq 0}\\
e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k}=2g+6\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\binom{2a_{[I_{j}]}}{e_{j}}\frac{(e_{j}-1)!!}{(e_{j}+1-2|I_{j}|)!!}.

Note that if
k=1
, then
(2g-3+k)!=(2g-2)!
. But then this term looks like

\displaystyle\binom{2a_{1}+2a_{2}+2a_{3}+2a_{4}+2a_{5}}{2g+6}(2g+5)(2g+3)(2g+1)(2g-1),

and the last factor here still allows us to extract the common coefficient of
(2g-1)!
. With that remark we see that every term in the expression for
\frac{P_{5,5}}{(2g-1)!}

e_{1},\dots,e_{k}

Applying the Chu–Vandermonde identity in the same way as in the previous cases, we obtain terms of the following type:

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\binom{2a_{2}}{e_{2}}\binom{2a_{3}}{e_{3}}\binom{2a_{4}}{o_{3}}\binom{2a_{5}}{o_{5}}\cdot 6e_{1}e_{2}e_{3},

\displaystyle-\binom{2a_{1}}{e_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{5}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot e_{1}(e_{1}-1)(e_{1}-2).

Applying Lemma
A.1
to all these terms, we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}\\
+o_{4}+o_{5}=2g+1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{5}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{5}o_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{5}o_{i}-1\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{5}o_{i}-2\right)=(2g-1)\cdot\frac{A_{5}}{(2g-1)!},

which confirms this case of the proposition.

A.6
Case
\boldsymbol{n=5}
,
\boldsymbol{t=4}

In this case
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{5}=2g+2

Here we can divide by
(2g-1)!
for the same reason as in the case

(A.1)

The second group of terms consists of

Taking into account that
o_{1}+o_{2}+o_{3}+o_{4}+o_{5}=2g+5

\displaystyle 5\ \text{terms of the type}\quad\binom{2a_{1}}{o_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{5}\binom{2a_{i}}{o_{i}}\cdot(-3)o_{1}(o_{1}-1)(2g-2).

(A.2)

We apply Lemma
A.1
