Abstract
. For an infinite set ℳ ⊂ ℕ \mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{N} , let 𝙵𝚂 ⁡ ( ℳ ) {\tt FS}(\mathcal{M}) be the set of all nonzero finite sums of distinct numbers in ℳ \mathcal{M} . An IP set is any set of the form 𝙵𝚂 ⁡ ( ℳ ) {\tt FS}(\mathcal{M}) . Let p n p_{n} denote the n n -th prime number for each n ⩾ 1 n\geqslant 1 . A de Polignac number is any number m m such that p n + 1 − p n = m p_{n+1}-p_{n}=m for infinitely many n n . In this note, we show that every IP set 𝒜 ⊂ 2 ​ ℕ \mathcal{A}\subset 2\mathbb{N} contains infinitely many de Polignac numbers.



1.
Introduction

A classical result of Hindman
[
6
]

(answering a question posed by Graham and
Rothschild
[
5
]
) relates to
finite sums sets
,
which are those of the form

{\tt FS}(\mathcal{M})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Big\{\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}m:\varnothing\neq\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M},~|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|<\infty\Big\}

for some infinite set
\mathcal{M}
of natural numbers. Hindman showed that
for any finite coloring of
\mathbb{N}
, there is an
infinite set
\mathcal{M}
such that
{\tt FS}(\mathcal{M})
is monochromatic.
In their treatise on topological dynamics and combinatorial number theory,
Furstenberg and Weiss
[
3
]
coined the term
IP set
to
mean any finite sums set
{\tt FS}(\mathcal{M})
.
There are numerous interesting examples of IP sets.
Bergelson and Rusza
[
2
]
gave a general method for constructing
a variety of IP sets; they showed that if
\mathcal{S}
is the set
of squarefree natural numbers and
s\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathcal{S}-s
contains an IP set if and only if
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Let
p_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=2
,
p_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=3
,
p_{3}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=5
, etc.,
be the sequence of all primes. Our aim in this note is to establish
a connection between IP sets and the sequence
(p_{n+1}-p_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

of consecutive prime gaps.

Theorem 1
.

For every IP set
\mathcal{A}\subset 2\mathbb{N}
, there exist infinitely many
a\in\mathcal{A}

with the property that
p_{n+1}-p_{n}=a
for infinitely many
n
.

Corollary 2
.

For every
s\in\mathcal{S}
, there are infinitely many

m\in\mathcal{S}-s
such that
p_{n+1}-p_{n}=m
for infinitely many
n
.

Corollary 3
.

For every
c>0
, there are infinitely many numbers
m
in the set

\{m\in\mathbb{N}:p\mid m\Longrightarrow p>c\}

with the property that

\tfrac{1}{2}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})=m-1
for infinitely many
n
.

Corollary 4
.

There are infinitely many
m\in\mathbb{N}
that have
only the digits
0
and
2
in their decimal expansion
and satisfy
p_{n+1}-p_{n}=m
for infinitely many
n
.

Theorem
1
is a simple consequence of our main result
(Theorem
8
) whose formulation uses
terminology given in the next section; the corollaries follow at once
by choosing appropriate IP sets. The proof of Theorem
8

is straightforward and short, but it relies on several deep results
from number theory and additive combinatorics. Thus, the “heavy lifting”
has largely been accomplished thanks to the efforts of various mathematicians;
their contributions are outlined in the next section.

2.
Background Material

We briefly review background material associated with our main theorem and
some results needed in the proof.

2.1.
de Polignac numbers

The notorious
twin prime conjecture
asserts the existence
of infinitely many natural numbers
n
for which
p_{n+1}-p_{n}=2
.
This conjecture first appeared in an 1849 monograph of
Alphonse de Polignac
[
8
]
, who also speculated that
for every even natural number
2m
, there are infinitely many prime
gaps of size
2m
, i.e., that
p_{n+1}-p_{n}=2m
holds for infinitely many
n
.
Let
Pol
denote the set of even integers
2m
with this property.
The elements of
Pol
are called
de Polignac numbers
,
and de Polignac’s hypothesis that
{\bf Pol}=2\mathbb{N}
is known
as
Polignac’s conjecture
.

1

Another equally famous
conjecture of Alphonse de Polignac is that every odd integer
k\geqslant 3

can be expressed as the sum of a power of two and a prime number. Thus,
in the literature, the term
de Polignac number
often refers to
odd integers
k
of this form.

The
bounded gap conjecture
(that is, the assertion
that
{\bf Pol}\neq\varnothing
) was first proved in the
celebrated 2013 paper of Zhang
[
11
]
, who employed
a modification of the Goldston-Pintz-Yıldırım sieve
[
4
]

to prove the existence of a de Polignac number not exceeding

7\times 10^{7}
. The
Polymath8
project sought to understand
and improve Zhang’s arguments
and ultimately showed that
{\bf Pol}\cap[2,4680]\neq\varnothing
.
Later in 2013, Maynard
[
7
]

showed that for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
one has

The same
result, albeit with a slightly weaker bound, had been independently proven
by Tao around the same time; the latter work remains unpublished.

\liminf_{n}(p_{n+m}-p_{m})\ll m^{3}\mathrm{e}^{4m}.

His breakthrough paper also proved the existence
of infinitely many prime gaps of size at most
600
; in other words,
it showed that
{\bf Pol}\cap[2,600]\neq\varnothing
.
Following Maynard’s announcement, a new polymath project was launched
(
Polymath8b
) and eventually achieved the result that

{\bf Pol}\cap[2,246]\neq\varnothing

2.2.
The Maynard-Tao theorem

An ordered tuple
\mathcal{H}
of distinct nonnegative integers is said to be

admissible
if it avoids at least one residue class mod
p
for
every prime
p
. Following Tao and Ziegler
[
10
]
, we say that
a finite admissible tuple
\mathcal{H}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
is
prime-producing

if there are infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\{n+h_{1},\ldots,n+h_{k}\}
are simultaneously prime. The

Dickson-Hardy-Littlewood conjecture
asserts that every
such tuple
\mathcal{H}
is prime-producing. This conjecture remains one of
the great unsolved problems in number theory, and the strongest
unconditional result in this direction is the following theorem of
Maynard
[
7
]
and Tao.

Theorem 5

(Maynard–Tao)
.

For every integer
m\geqslant 2
, there is a number
k_{m}
for which
the following holds. If
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
is admissible with
k\geqslant k_{m}
,
then the set
\{n+h_{1},\ldots,n+h_{k}\}
contains at least
m
primes
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Shortly after this theorem was announced, a version of the Maynard-Tao theorem
producing
consecutive
primes in tuples was given by
Banks, Freiberg and Turnage-Butterbaugh
[
1
]
, who used the result to
solve an old problem of Erdős. The following is a slightly
weakened version of their theorem.

Theorem 6

(Banks–Freiberg–Turnage-Butterbaugh)
.

Fix an integer
m\geqslant 2
, and let
k_{m}
have the property stated in
Theorem
5
. If
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
is admissible with

k\geqslant k_{m}
, then there is a set

\{h^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,h^{\prime}_{m}\}\subset\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}\}
such that the set

\{n+h^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,n+h^{\prime}_{m}\}
consists of
m
consecutive primes
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.3.
Ramsey’s theorem

We recall the well known result of Ramsey
[
9
]
.

Theorem 7

(Ramsey)
.

For any
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a least natural number
R(r,s)

such that every blue–red edge coloring of the complete graph
on
R(r,s)
vertices contains either a blue clique on
r
vertices or a
red clique on
s
vertices.

3.
Statement of the main theorem

The present paper was inspired by recent work
of Tao and Ziegler
[
10
]
, who study infinite restricted sumsets
in the shifted primes and ascending chains of prime-producing tuples.
Although our results revolve around the study of sums of de Polignac numbers,
we do not use any results from
[
10
]
but instead rely only on
the theorems discussed in §
2

and an auxiliary result (see Lemma
9
below).

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset 2\mathbb{N}
be an IP set.
Then, for any
k\geqslant 1
, there is a sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}
in
\mathcal{A}

such that

(1)

\sum_{i\leqslant n\leqslant j}a_{n}\in{\bf Pol}\qquad\text{whenever}\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant k.

Moreover, the
a_{j}
’s can be chosen so that all of
the sums in (
1
) are distinct from one another.

Note that Theorem
8
implies that

|\mathcal{A}\cap{\bf Pol}|=\infty
, and Theorem
1
follows.

4.
Proof of the main theorem

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Every infinite set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{N}

contains a strictly increasing sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

for which the tuple
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
is admissible.

Proof.

Put
b_{0}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=0
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathcal{B}
. Using induction, we construct
a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contained in
\mathcal{B}
and a family
of sets
\{\mathcal{B}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

(2)

b_{n}>b_{n-1},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{n}=\mathcal{B}_{n-1}\cap(p_{n}\mathbb{Z}+b_{n})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad|\mathcal{B}_{n}|=\infty.

Indeed, let
n\geqslant 1
, and suppose
b_{j}
and
\mathcal{B}_{j}
have been determined
for all
j<n
. Since
\mathcal{B}_{n-1}
is infinite, the pigeonhole principle
shows that
\mathcal{B}_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathcal{B}_{n-1}\cap(p_{n}\mathbb{Z}+h)
is infinite
for at least one integer
h\in[0,p_{n})
. Replacing
h
by any number

b_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
larger than
b_{n-1}
, we obtain (
2
),
completing the induction.

To finish the proof, we show that
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
is admissible.
Indeed, let
p_{n}
be an arbitrary prime, and observe that

\mathcal{B}_{m}\subset\mathcal{B}_{n}\subset p_{n}\mathbb{Z}+b_{n}

for all integers
m\geqslant n
; consequently,

\big|\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots\}\bmod p_{n}\big|=\big|\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}\bmod p_{n}\big|\leqslant n<p_{n},

and thus
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
avoids at least
one residue class mod
p_{n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

As
\mathcal{A}
is an IP set,
\mathcal{A}={\tt FS}(\mathcal{M})
for some infinite set
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{N}
.
We order the elements
\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots\}
of
\mathcal{M}
with

m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots
and denote by
\mathcal{B}
the set of all partial sums
of the form
\sum_{k\leqslant\ell}m_{k}
.
According to Lemma
9
, the set
\mathcal{B}

contains a strictly increasing sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

for which the tuple
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
is admissible. Note that,
for any pair
(i,j)
with
1\leqslant i<j
, there are numbers

\ell^{\prime}_{i}<\ell^{\prime}_{j}
such that

b_{j}-b_{i}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}_{i}<k\leqslant\ell^{\prime}_{j}}m_{k}\in\mathcal{A}.

Next, we extract from
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

a lacunary subsequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

such that
v_{n+1}/v_{n}>10
(say) for each
n
; this ensures that the
differences
v_{j}-v_{i}
are all distinct from one another.
The resulting tuple
(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots)
is also admissible.
As before, for any pair
(i,j)
with
1\leqslant i<j
, there are numbers

\ell_{i}<\ell_{j}
such that

(3)

v_{j}-v_{i}=\sum_{\ell_{i}<k\leqslant\ell_{j}}m_{k}\in\mathcal{A}.

Now, in the notation of Theorems
6
and
7
, we set

r\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=L+1,\qquad s\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=k_{2},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad N\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=R(r,s).

We now view the finite collection
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}
as the vertex set

\mathcal{V}
of a complete graph
\mathcal{G}
with edge set is

\mathcal{E}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{e_{i,j}:1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N\}
;
each edge
e_{i,j}
connects vertex
v_{i}
to vertex
v_{j}
.
Define a blue–red coloring of the edges via the map

\mathscr{C}:\mathcal{E}\to\{{\tt blue},{\tt red}\},\qquad\mathscr{C}(e_{i,j})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}{\tt blue}&\quad\hbox{if $v_{j}-v_{i}\in{\tt Pol}$},\\
{\tt red}&\quad\hbox{if $v_{j}-v_{i}\not\in{\tt Pol}$}.\\
\end{cases}

By Theorem
7
, the graph
\mathcal{G}
contains either a blue clique on

r=L+1
vertices or a red clique on
s=k_{2}
vertices.

We claim that the latter possibility is untenable, i.e.,
no such red clique exists.
Indeed, suppose on the contrary that there is a
complete subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\flat}\subset\mathcal{G}
determined by vertices

\mathcal{V}^{\flat}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k_{2}}\}
in
\mathcal{V}
such that
\mathcal{G}^{\flat}

is monochromatic and colored red by the map
\mathscr{C}
.
By Theorem
6
, there is a set

\{h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime}_{2}\}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\flat}

such that
n+h^{\prime}_{1}
and
n+h^{\prime}_{2}
are consecutive primes for infinitely
many
n\in\mathbb{N}
; in other words,
|h^{\prime}_{2}-h^{\prime}_{1}|\in{\bf Pol}
.
But this is not possible
since
h^{\prime}_{1}
and
h^{\prime}_{2}
are distinct vertices in
\mathcal{V}^{\flat}

and hence the corresponding edge is colored red by
\mathscr{C}
.

Consequently, within the graph
\mathcal{G}
there is blue clique on
r=L+1
vertices,
i.e., there is a complete subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{\sharp}\subset\mathcal{G}
determined by vertices

\mathcal{V}^{\sharp}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{L+1}\}
in
\mathcal{V}
such that
\mathcal{G}^{\sharp}

is monochromatic and colored blue by
\mathscr{C}
.
Ordering
\mathcal{V}^{\sharp}
so that
h_{1}<\cdots<h_{L+1}
, we have

\{h_{j}-h_{i}:1\leqslant i<j\leqslant m+1\}\subset{\bf Pol}

since the edge determined by
h_{i}
and
h_{j}
is colored blue by
\mathscr{C}

for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant m+1
. By construction, all of the differences
h_{j}-h_{i}

are distinct from one another. Since
h_{i}
and
h_{j}
are distinct
vertices in
\mathcal{V}
, we have
h_{n+1}-h_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
for each
n\in[1,L]

in view of (
3
). Defining
a_{n}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=h_{n+1}-h_{n}
,
we obtain a sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{L}
with the properties
stated in the theorem, since

\sum_{i\leqslant n\leqslant j}a_{n}=\sum_{i\leqslant n\leqslant j}(h_{n+1}-h_{n})=h_{j+1}-h_{i}\in{\bf Pol}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
8
.
∎
