Fast and Robust Sparsity Learning over Networks: A Decentralized Surrogate Median Regression Approach
Weidong Liu, Xiaojun Mao, and Xin Zhang

Abstract
Decentralized sparsity learning has attracted a significant amount of attention recently due to its rapidly growing applications. To obtain the robust and sparse estimators, a natural idea is to adopt the non-smooth median loss combined with a ℓ 1 \ell_{1} sparsity regularizer. However, most of the existing methods suffer from slow convergence performance caused by the double non-smooth objective. To accelerate the computation, in this paper, we proposed a decentralized surrogate median regression (deSMR) method for efficiently solving the decentralized sparsity learning problem. We show that our proposed algorithm enjoys a linear convergence rate with a simple implementation. We also investigate the statistical guarantee, and it shows that our proposed estimator achieves a near-oracle convergence rate without any restriction on the number of network nodes. Moreover, we establish the theoretical results for sparse support recovery. Thorough numerical experiments and real data study are provided to demonstrate the effectiveness of our method.

The goal of decentralized ML is to learn a global ML model by having workers optimize their own models and share local model information with their neighbors.
So far, decentralized ML has achieved significant success in many scientific and engineering areas, including distributed sensing in wireless sensor networks

,

,

, multi-agent robotic systems

However, in spite of the increasing adoption in applications, the performances of most decentralized ML methods are not robust and are vulnerable to the following three aspects:
1)
Data Heterogeneity
.
Due to the lack of the global information aggregated by the central master, workers in decentralized network systems learn the model heavily relied on the local data and neighboring information.
Thus, data heterogeneity across workers often causes a so-called ‘consensus error’ that reduces the model estimation efficiency
[
28
,
47
,
20
]
;
2)
Noise Contamination
.
In many real-world ML applications, training data are contaminated by heavy-tailed noises or outlier values.
Under this circumstance, adopting the mean square loss or Huber loss
[

]
in decentralized ML framework become less efficient or even infeasible

6
]
;
3)
Cyber Attacks
.
Without the central master, it is difficult for decentralized ML methods to identify the cyber attackers, which inject abnormal data into ML training.
Typically, these injected fake data will affect the training process and bias the estimated model.

In this paper, we aim to improve the estimation efficiency and robustness for decentralized ML.
Specifically, we focus on the problem of decentralized sparsity learning on linear model.
Consider a network with
m
nodes, each of which holds a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{j}}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, where
y\in\mathbb{R}
is the observed response values,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a
p
-dimensional covariate vector. In the following we always use the notation
[t]
as the set of indices
\{1,\ldots,t\}
where the number
t
can be changed to be the length of the set.
We suppose that the data follows a linear model:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}^{*}+\epsilon_{i},~~\forall i\in[n_{j}],j\in[m],

(1)

where
\bm{\beta}^{*}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})^{\rm T}
is the globally common linear coefficient of dimension
p
, and
\epsilon_{i}
is the measurement noise which is independent with covariate
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Additionally, we consider that the coefficient
\bm{\beta}^{*}
follows a sparse structure with
s

We denote the support of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
as
\mathcal{S}=\{1\leq i\leq p:\beta^{*}_{i}\neq 0\}
and
|\mathcal{S}|=s
.
Practically, we allow the data to be heterogeneous across nodes, i.e., the covariance structure
\bm{\Sigma}_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T})
and distributions for measurement noise
\epsilon_{i}
could be different with respect to node index

.
Our primary interest is to estimate
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and recover its sparsity support by utilizing all the data across the network
{\mathcal{D}}=\cup_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
, where the size of
{\mathcal{D}}
is

which implies
N=mn
.

There exist several decentralized sparsity learning methods
[
27
,
5
,
34
,
9
]
that enjoy a fast linear convergence rate.
However, such convergence advantage benefits from the traditional least square loss, which is vulnerable to the abnormal value or outlier noise.
In this work, we allow the noise
\epsilon
to be a heavy-tailed noise, of which the variance can be infinite.
To mitigate the effect of heavy-tailed noise, a natural idea is to replace the mean square loss with the median loss, so that
\bm{\beta}^{*}
can be estimated by solving the following minimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\left|y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}\right|,

(2)

where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
is for the absolute value.
To further pursue the sparsity structure of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, we regularize the problem (
2
) and define the sparse robust estimator for
\bm{\beta}^{*}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\left|y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}\right|+\lambda_{N}\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1},

(3)

where
\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
-regularization term and
\lambda_{N}>0
is the regularization parameter.
However, due to the non-smooth property of both the median loss and the
\ell_{1}
-regularization term, it’s a non-trivial task to optimize the above
double
non-smooth minimization problem (
3
), let alone solving it in a decentralized setting.
Although several decentralized subgradient methods were proposed to address the non-smooth median loss in network systems (e.g.,
[
40
,
41
]
), the estimation efficiency is not satisfactory because of their sublinear convergence rate.
The limitations of these existing works naturally arise the following question:
Could we develop a decentralized sparsity learning method for
\ell_{1}
-regularized median loss that can achieve a fast linear convergence rate?

We give
affirmative
answer to the above question in this paper. We develop a Decentralized Surrogate Median Regression (deSMR) method and its key features are summarized in the followings:

•

To avoid the tremendous complexity caused by the non-smooth loss, we surrogate the median loss with a smooth least square loss with transformed responses.
The idea of response transformation is inspired by Newton-Raphson updating.
We show that our method only needs a constant-order round of reformulation to achieve high accurate estimation;

•

For collaboratively solving the reformulated sparsity-penalized least square loss, we derive a decentralized generalized ADMM algorithm, which enjoys a
linear
convergence.
Our algorithm only requires nodes to find a closed-form solution for locally approximated subproblems and share local model parameters with their neighbors, and thus it is easy to implement.

•

By incorporating the algorithm convergence rate, we first establish the one-round node-wise statistical consistency rate for our proposed estimators in Theorem
2
.
We show that our estimators recover the exact sparsity support under some mild conditions. Furthermore, we can iteratively improve the estimators over the networks by replacing the initial estimators.
We show that after a constant number of iterations of loss surrogate and enough number of ADMM iterations, our estimators achieve a near-optimal rate of
\sqrt{s\log(N\vee p)/N}
in Theorem
5
. This rate matches with the optimal rate
\sqrt{s/N}
obtained under the true support is known up to a logarithmic factor. Due to the space limitation, we leave the corresponding results of support recovery in the Supplementary Material.

2
Methodology

In this section, we first introduce the decentralized median regression in a networked distributed system and consider the equivalent consensus form. To handle the non-smooth loss, we propose a square loss transformation method. By constructing pseudo-response and encouraging sparsity, we transform it into an
\ell_{1}
-penalized least square regression problem, which becomes much computationally feasible. Based on this idea, we develop a generalized consensus ADMM algorithm in the networked system.

2.1
Decentralized Median Regression with Sparsity

In this section, we consider solving the above median regression problem (
2
) in decentralized framework, of which there are a set of geographically dispersed computational nodes that form a network.
We represent a networked distributed computing system as an undirected connected network
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{E}})
, where
{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}
are the sets of nodes and edges, respectively, with
|{\mathcal{N}}|=m
as the node number. Denote
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
to be the
j
-th node. In this connected network, we assume
N
data samples are collected locally in these
m
nodes
{\mathcal{N}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{N}}_{m}
. As described in the introduction, each node
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
collects its local data
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}=\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i}):i\in[n]\}
. To be more practical, we allow the data to be heterogeneous across nodes.

The nodes have local computation capabilities and are only able to communicate with their neighbors via the edges in
{\mathcal{E}}
. Its connectivity is modeled by an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{W}

[
44
]
. More precisely,
\mathbf{W}_{jk}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the connection between nodes
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
and
{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
. Nodes
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
and
{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
have a pairwise communication link if and only if the corresponding weights
\mathbf{W}_{jk}=\mathbf{W}_{kj}
are
1
; otherwise
0
.
For the network with no self-loops, the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{W}
are
0
s.
In this work, we consider the network is connected and has no self-loops.

Our goal is to have the computational nodes
distributively
and
collaboratively
solving the network-wide median regression problem as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\mathcal{L}}(\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\!\in\!{\mathcal{D}}\},\bm{\beta})\!=\!\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{L}}(\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\!\in\!{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\},\bm{\beta}),

(4)

where each local median loss function
{\mathcal{L}}(\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\},\bm{\beta})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}\mbox{$\lvert y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}\rvert$}+\lambda_{N}\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1}
is only observable to node
j
. Note that in the loss function
{\mathcal{L}}
,
\lambda_{N}
is the parameter to adjust the sparsity of the estimated median regression coefficient, which is tuned to be universal over the network
{\mathcal{G}}
. Because in decentralized network system, there is no central controller to maintain a common model parameter during the iterations
[
28
,
4
,
32
]
.
Thus, to solve problem (
4
) in a decentralized fashion, we reformulate it in the following equivalent consensus form:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mp}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\mathcal{L}}(\{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\},\bm{\beta}^{(j)}),

\displaystyle\centering~~\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\beta}^{(j)}=\bm{\beta}^{(k)},~~\forall(j,k)\in{\mathcal{E}},\@add@centering

(5)

where
{\mathbf{B}}\triangleq[\bm{\beta}^{(1)\rm T},\cdots,\bm{\beta}^{(m)\rm T}]^{\rm T}\in\mathbb{R}^{mp}
is the concatenated version of the parameters
\bm{\beta}^{(j)}
. Here
\bm{\beta}^{(j)}
is an introduced local parameter copy at node
j
. In problem (
2.1
), the constraints enforce that the local parameter copy at each node is equal to those of its neighbors, hence the name “consensus”. Clearly, the solution of problem (
4
) solves problem (
2.1
) and vice versa
[
28
,
35
,
47
]
.

However, it is non-trivial to solve the above decentralized robust and sparse learning problem (
2.1
) because of the two factors: 1). The non-smooth median loss function makes decentralized gradient/Newton-based algorithms infeasible; 2). Composited with the sparsity
\ell_{1}
regularizer, there is no close-form solution for the consensus loss function. To address the above two challenges, in the following, we will propose our method named Decentralized Surrogate Median Regression (deSMR) method to solve the above problem (
2.1
).
Two key components are included in our method. We first perform a transformation on the response
y
and surrogate the median loss with a least-square loss; Then, we adopt the generalized alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) framework to solve the decentralized penalized least square problem.
We will show that our method enjoys a linear convergence rate and thus a low complexity.

2.2
Square Loss Transformation for Outer Loop

The penalized median loss in (
4
) follows a non-smooth loss plus non-smooth regularizer structure. To the best of our knowledge, the existing decentralized algorithms typically solve such
doubly
non-smooth loss functions by using subgradient-based algorithm
[
40
,
48
,
41
]
.
However, one of the critical weaknesses of these works is that the subgradient-based algorithm suffers a slow
sublinear
convergence rate, which leads to the intensive computation and communication complexities in the decentralized network system. To avoid the tremendous complexity for solving the
doubly
non-smooth loss, in the following, we propose a square loss transformation to replace the median loss.

To faster the algorithm, we adopt the transformation methods in
[
7
]
to reformulate the median regression problem (
2
). Inspired by the Newton-Raphson method, given the initial estimator
\bm{\beta}_{0}
,
[
7
]
proposed the new response variable
\widetilde{y}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}={\mathbf{x}}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}_{0}-f^{-1}(0)(\mathds{I}[y\leq{\mathbf{x}}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta}_{0}]-1/2),

(6)

where
f(0)
denotes the density of
\epsilon
at
0
. With this transformation, it has been shown that the population version of the Newton-Raphson iteration to solve problem (
2
) induces an updated solution
\bm{\beta}_{1}
as the least square solution of the problem
\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{y}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta})^{2}
.

In network node
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
with the dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
from (
1
), suppose that we have the initial estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}
obtained by only using
{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
locally in the node
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
. We choose the corresponding density estimator of
f(0)
to be

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{(j)}(0)=\frac{1}{nh^{(j)}}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}K_{j}\Big(\frac{y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}}{h^{(j)}}\Big),

(7)

where
K_{j}(x)
is a kernel function which satisfies the assumption (A3) (see Section
3
) and
h^{(j)}\to 0
are the bandwidths for each
j\in[m]
. The selection of bandwidths
\{h^{(j)}:j\in[m]\}
will be discussed in our theoretical results (see Section
3
). Moreover, for each
({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}
, we construct the corresponding pseudo-response by

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}-(\widehat{f}^{(j)}(0))^{-1}(\mathds{I}\left[y_{i}\leq\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}\right]-1/2).

(8)

To further encourage the sparsity of the estimator, it is natural to consider the following
\ell_{1}
-regularized problem,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\beta}\in R^{p}}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}}(\widetilde{y}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta})^{2}+\lambda_{N,0}\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1}.

(9)

Denote
\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\},\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{j}}(\tilde{y}_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta})^{2}+\lambda_{N,0}\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1}
, in the decentralized framework, we focus on finding the minimizer of the following problem as surrogate estimator:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\!\in\!{\mathcal{D}}\},\bm{\beta})\!=\!\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\!\frac{1}{m}\!\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{{\mathcal{L}}}(\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\!\in\!{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\},\bm{\beta}).

(10)

Similarly as problem (
2.1
), we reformulate the above transformed general problem into the consensus form as:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{1}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mp}}\frac{1}{2mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{\bm{y}}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{N,0}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|_{1},

\displaystyle\centering\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\beta}^{(j)}=\bm{\beta}^{(k)},~~\forall(j,k)\in\mathcal{E}.\@add@centering

(11)

where
\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{1}\!=\![\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{(1)\rm T},\cdots,\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{(m)\rm T}]^{\rm T}\!=\!\mathbf{1}_{m}\!\otimes\!(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{(j)})\in\mathbb{R}^{mp}
,
\widetilde{\bm{y}}^{(j)}=(\widetilde{y}^{(j)}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{y}^{(j)}_{n})
is the concatenated vector of dimension
p
, and
{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}=({\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(j)},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{(j)})
is the concatenated covariate matrix of dimension
p\times n
in node
{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
.

It can be shown in Theorem
2
that the convergence rate of
\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{1}
is not optimal under certain initial estimators. To further refine the estimator, we update the initial estimators iteratively by plugging-in
\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{v-1}
in
v
-th iteration. Thus at
v
-th iteration, we consider the
\ell_{1}
-regularized problem to be

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{v}=\arg\min\limits_{\bm{\beta}\in R^{p}}\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{({\mathbf{x}}_{i},y_{i})\in{\mathcal{D}}}(\widetilde{y}_{i,v}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\beta})^{2}+\lambda_{N,v}\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\beta}\rvert$}_{1}.

(12)

And the corresponding consensus form of problem (
12
) is:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{v}=\arg\min_{{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mp}}\frac{1}{2mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{\bm{y}}_{v}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{N,v}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|_{1},

\displaystyle~~\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\beta}^{(j)}=\bm{\beta}^{(k)},~~\forall(j,k)\in\mathcal{E},

(13)

where
\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{v}=[\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{v}^{\rm T},\cdots,\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{v}^{\rm T}]^{\rm T}
.

2.3
Generalized Consensus ADMM for Inner Loop

With the loss transformation proposed in Section
2.2
, in the following, we focus on the derivation of detailed algorithm to solve the
v
-th iteration (
2.2
) consensus form problem. By introducing pseudo variables
\bm{T}\triangleq[\mathbf{t}^{(jk)}]
, we can equivalently write the
v
-th iteration (
2.2
) as:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{B}},\bm{T}}\frac{1}{2mn}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{\bm{y}}_{v}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{N,v}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|_{1},

\displaystyle~~\text{s.t.}~~\bm{\beta}^{(j)}=\bm{\beta}^{(k)}=\mathbf{t}^{(jk)},~~\forall k\in{\mathcal{N}}(j),j\in[m].

(14)

Due to the consensus constraint, a natural idea is to use the popular ADMM, which has been shown to be particularly efficient for solving linear constrained minimization problem.
Following classic ADMM
[
12
]
, we can construct an augmented Lagrangian with penalty parameter
\tau>0
as:

\displaystyle L_{v,\tau}({\mathbf{B}},\bm{T},{\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{V}})\!=\!\frac{1}{2mn}\!\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{\bm{y}}_{v}^{(j)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|^{2}_{2}\!\!+\!\lambda_{N,v}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\right|_{1}

\displaystyle\!+\!\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}(j)}\big(\langle\mathbf{u}^{(jk)},\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\!-\!\mathbf{t}^{(jk)}\rangle\!+\!\langle\mathbf{v}^{(jk)},\bm{\beta}^{(k)}\!-\!\mathbf{t}^{(jk)}\rangle

\displaystyle\!+\!\frac{\tau}{2}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(j)}\!-\!\mathbf{t}^{(jk)}\right|_{2}^{2}\!+\!\frac{\tau}{2}\left|\bm{\beta}^{(k)}\!-\!\mathbf{t}^{(jk)}\right|_{2}^{2}\big),

(15)

where
{\mathbf{U}}\triangleq\{\mathbf{u}^{(jk)}\}_{j=1,k}^{m,{\mathcal{N}}(j)}
and
{\mathbf{V}}\triangleq\{\mathbf{v}^{(jk)}\}_{j=1,k}^{m,{\mathcal{N}}(j)}
are the dual variables.
To find the solution for the above augmented Lagrangian, we define an axillary variable
\mathbf{p}^{(j)}_{v,t}=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}(j)}(\mathbf{u}^{(jk)}_{v,t}+\mathbf{v}^{(kj)}_{v,t})
with
\mathbf{p}^{(j)}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
Then, (
2.2
) can be solved by recursively performing (
16a
) and (
16b
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{(j)}_{v,t+1}\!=\!\mathbf{p}^{(j)}_{v,t}\!+\!\tau\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}(j)}(\bm{\beta}^{(j)}_{v,t}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{(k)}_{v,t}).

(16a)

3.1
Technique Assumptions

In this section, the technical assumptions needed for our analysis are given as follows. We first provide the assumptions required to achieve linear convergence rate of our proposed algorithm.

Assumption 1
.

The peer-to-peer networked system
\mathcal{G}
is connected.

Assumption 2
.

For all the covariate
{\mathbf{x}}
, they satisfy the sub-Gaussian condition for some constants
t>0
and
C>0
,

\sup\nolimits_{\mbox{$\lvert\bm{\theta}\rvert$}_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}\exp(t(\bm{\theta}^{\rm T}{\mathbf{x}})^{2})\leq C.

Assumption 3
.

The dimension
p
over the local sample size
n
is
p/n\leq\tau
where
\tau\in(0,1)
.
For each
j\in[m]
, suppose that
\bm{\Sigma}_{j}=\mathbb{E}(n^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j){\rm T}})
satisfies
c_{0,j}^{-1}\leq\lambda_{\text{min}}(\bm{\Sigma}_{j})\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}(\bm{\Sigma}_{j})\leq c_{0,j}
for some constants
c_{0,j}>0
.

Assumption
1
is a common assumption in network consensus optimization. It indicates that there is no isolated nodes or groups of nodes in the network. Thus, all the nodes could reach a consensus status. Assumption
2
is a regular sub-Gaussian assumption on the distribution of the covariates
{\mathbf{X}}
over the network
{\mathcal{G}}
. Assumption
3
is a standard assumption, which implies that the largest eigenvalue of empirical covariance matrix
n^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j){\rm T}}
is bounded with high probability goes to one together with Assumption
2
. According to
[
46
]
, the dimension restriction is to ensure the empirical smallest eigenvalue of each sample covariance matrices
n^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j){\rm T}}
are bounded away from zero with high probability tending to one. Thus the optimization problem is strongly convex.

To further obtain the statistical convergence rates at each iterations, we assume the following regular assumptions.

Assumption 4
.

For each
j\in[m]
, there exists some incoherence parameters
0<\alpha_{j}<1
such that

\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{j,S^{c}\times S}\bm{\Sigma}_{j,S\times S}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\alpha_{j}.

(19)

Assumption 5
.

The dimension
p
satisfies

for some
\nu>0
. The local sample size
n
on each node satisfies
n\geq N^{c}
for some
0<c<1
, and the sparsity level

satisfies
s=O(n^{r})
for some
0<r<1/3
.

Assumption 6
.

Over the network
{\mathcal{G}}
, the noises enjoy universal density function
f(\cdot)
which is bounded and Lipschitz continuous (i.e.,
\mbox{$\lvert f({\mathbf{x}})-f({\mathbf{y}})\rvert$}\leq C_{L}\mbox{$\lvert{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\rvert$}_{2}
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and some constant
C_{L}>0
). Moreover, we assume
f(0)
is bounded away from
0
.

Assumption 7
.

For each
j\in[m]
, assume that the kernel function

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{j}(u)\mathrm{d}u=1
. Moreover, assume that
K_{j}(\cdot)
satisfies
K_{j}(u)=0
if
|u|\geq 1
. Further, assume
K_{j}(\cdot)
is differentiable and its derivative

Assumption 8

For each
j\in[m]
, the initial estimators

satisfies
\mbox{$\lvert\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\rvert$}_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{s(\log N)/n})

\Pr(\text{supp}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)})\subseteq S)\rightarrow 1

Together with Assumption

to establish results on support recovery. Assumption

instead of different on each node. Assumption

Assumption

is an assumption on the convergence rate and support recovery of the initial estimators at each node. Note that in the proposed Algorithm

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0}^{(j)}
is obtained as the solution to the high-dimensional median regression problem using data locally on each node

3.2
Convergence of Generalized Consensus ADMM

In this section, we establish the convergence properties of our proposed ADMM algorithm in deSMR. We show that with certain assumptions and proper choice of the step lengths
\rho_{j}
, the algorithm yields a linear convergence rate in the connected network.

Proposition 1

(Linear Convergence)
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, by setting the step lengths

\rho_{j}>\lambda_{\text{max}}(n^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j)}{\mathbf{X}}^{(j){\rm T}})

for each
j\in[m]

v
-th iteration where
v\in[V]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{B}}_{v,T+1}-\widehat{{\mathbf{B}}}_{v}\|^{2}_{F}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\gamma^{T}),

(20)

where
{\mathbf{B}}_{v,T+1}=[\bm{\beta}_{v,T+1}^{(1){\rm T}},\cdots,\bm{\beta}_{v,T+1}^{(m){\rm T}}]^{{\rm T}}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

The result in Proposition
1
shows that our inner loop algorithm can solve the reformulated
\ell_{1}
penalized least square loss at a linear convergence speed, where the convergence factor
\gamma
depends on the network topology
\mathbf{W}
and singularity of the covariance matrices
\{\bm{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
.
Note that the step lengths selection is only relied on the maximum eigenvalue of the local covariance matrix, which makes the implementation simple.
Due to the limited space, the proof of Proposition

is relegated to the supplementary materials.

4.3
Effect of Heavy-Tailed Noise

The motivation for our robust median regression model is heavy-tailed noises, which is prevalent in real-world applications.
In this part of the simulation study, we aim to compare the estimation performance between our median loss and the standard
\ell_{2}
loss in the presence of heavy-tailed noises.
Four difference kinds of noise distribution are considered in this simulation,
\text{Normal}(0,1)
,
\text{Exp}(1)
,
\text{Cauchy}(0,1)
and
\text{t}(1)
.
Note that both
\text{Cauchy}(0,1)
and
\text{t}(1)
are heavy-tailed distribution.
The decentralized median loss can be solved with our proposed deSMR .
While for the decentralized
\ell_{2}
loss, we can solve it with the generalized consensus ADMM method proposed in Section
2.3
.
We denote this method as Decentralized Lasso Regression (deLR) method.
To fairly compare the two methods, we set the same total iteration number as
500
for both of them.
Furthermore, we adopt the same setting for the network system as in Section
4.2
.

We compare the two methods in terms of the following performance metrics: 1) the accuracy of model estimation,
\ell_{2}\text{-error}=\sum_{j\in[m]}|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{(j)}-\bm{\beta}^{*}|^{2}_{2}
; 2) the sparsity recall, which measures the proportion of non-zero coefficient elements that are correctly identified; 3) the sparsity precision, which is defined as the proportion of identified non-zero elements that are correct.
Note that the values of the sparsity recall and precision are in the range
[0,1]
. The closer to 1, the better the estimation is.
The simulation results are reported in Table
1
.

From Table
1
, we can see that deLR and deSMR have similar estimation performance when the noises are from Normal(0,1) and Exp(1).
However, for Cauchy(0,1) and t(1), which are heavy-tailed distribution, deSMR has a much better estimation performance than deLR: For example, in the cases with noises from Cauchy(0,1), the
\ell_{2}
-error of deLR is almost 20 times of deSMR, and the precision of deLR is about 0.2 less than deSMR.
We can see a similar pattern in the cases with noises from t(1).
These findings show that by applying
\ell_{2}
loss to the data with heavy-tailed noises, we will obtain a less accurate estimated coefficient with more misidentified non-zero elements.
Thus, compared with the deLR method, our deSMR method has more stable estimation performances under different noise types.

Effect of Data Heterogeneity

In this section, we use simulations to illustrate the impact of data heterogeneity on the model estimation.
We consider generating the data heterogeneity in two ways:
1) Covariate
\mathbf{x}
on each node are sampled from Normal
(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
with different covariance matrix.
The covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
is constructed by
\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}=\sigma^{2}\rho^{|i-j|}
for
1\leq i,j\leq p
.
And each node randomly sets
\sigma^{2}
and
\rho
from
\{1,3\}
and
\{0.1,0.3\}
, respectively.
In this case, we have the noise from Cauchy(0,1).
2) Noises
\epsilon
follows different distributions across nodes.
We randomly select one noise distribution from Normal(0,1), Exp(1), Cauchy(0,1) and t(1) for each node.
For this case, the covariate is sampled from the same multivariate normal distribution in Section
4.1
.

We focus on five different methods:
1) Pooled median regression (Pooled MR), in which local data are pooled into one single machine, and the median regression estimation is calculated with all the data;
2) Local median regression (Local MR), which allows nodes to calculate their own median regression estimation with local data;
3) Averaged median regression (Avg. MR), which takes the average of the local median regression estimation;
4) Decentralized subgradient descent (D-subGD) method
[
40
]
, in which the nodes collaboratively solve (
2.1
) with local subgradient descent and network communication;
5) Our proposed decentralized suggerated median regression (deSMR) method.
For both D-subGD and deSMR, we use the estimations of Local MR as the algorithm initialization.
We compare the estimation performance of these methods by the
\ell_{2}
-error and
F_{1}
-score of estimated regression coefficients.
The
F_{1}
-score is commonly used metric for support recovery and defined as
F_{1}\text{-score}=2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}/(\text{precision}+\text{recall})
.
The value of
F_{1}
-score is between
[0,1]
and
F_{1}\text{-score}=1
implies perfect support recovery.

We report the simulation results in Table
2
-
3
.
Table
2
summarizes the
\ell_{2}
-error of the five median robust regression methods under difference data heterogeneity settings.
It can be seen that Pooled MR method has the smallest
\ell_{2}
-error.
However, Pooled MR requires to aggregate all the data into one single machine, which is hard to implement in the decentralized network system and might break the potential data privacy policy.
Our deSMR has the same accuracy as Pooled MR and outperforms other three decentralized median regression methods.
In Table
3
, we can see that our deSMR has the
F_{1}
-score very close to
1
, which means our method has an excellent performance on support recovery. However, the other three decentralized median regression methods have
F_{1}
-score less than
0.5
when local data size
m
equals coefficient dimension
p
.
Thus, we conclude that our deSMR has a better and more stable performance than the other decentralized median regression methods in terms of coefficient
\ell_{2}
-error and support recovery.

Our theoretical study shows that the initial estimation is an essential step to guarantee the overall convergence of our proposed deSMR method.
In this section, we give a sensitivity study of different initial values: 1) Lasso

18

, where
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{0,i}^{(j)}=\bm{\beta}_{i}^{*}+1\{\bm{\beta}_{i}^{*}\neq 0\}\cdot N(0,\sigma^{2})
, for all
i=1,\cdots,p
and
j=1,\cdots,m
.
Here we consider two different scale of noise,
\sigma=0.1
and
0.5
.

The rest settings are the same as in Section
4.2
.

6
.
First, by comparing the results from different noise perturbations, we can see that the estimation performance gets better with more precise initial values.

\epsilon
.
Thus, we’d suggest Lasso median estimation method as initial value selection for our deSMR algorithm, especially when the measurement error

In this section, we study the Communities and Crime dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository

.

This dataset contains a total number of
147

101

1993

[
45

In the computation system, each division can only broadcast/receive the local information to/from its spatial neighbors, and we show their network relationship in Figure

Outlier Data Models.

To study the robustness of different regression methods, we consider three scenarios:

1/9
of the original local training data size;

Conclusion

In this work, we study the problem of robust sparsity learning over decentralized network.
Our goal is to distributively optimize a
\ell_{1}
regularized median loss.
To fast solve the ‘double’ non-smooth minimization problem, we proposed the DeMR ADMM method, which enjoys a simple implementation.
We investigated the theoretical properties of both estimation consistency and algorithm convergence. Our theoretical analysis shows that our proposed estimators can achieve a near-optimal rate by ignoring a logarithmic factor.
Furthermore, we conducted thorough numerical experiments to verify our theoretical results, which showed the advantages of our methods in estimation efficiency and robustness.
In our work, we focus on the linear model.
An interesting future topic is to generalize our framework to a more general class of regression problems, such as robust logistic regression model.
