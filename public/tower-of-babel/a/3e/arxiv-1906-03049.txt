Computing Tight Differential Privacy Guarantees Using FFT https://github.com/DPBayes/PLD-Accountant/
Antti Koskela, Joonas Jälkö, Antti Honkela

Abstract
Differentially private (DP) machine learning has recently become popular. The privacy loss of DP algorithms is commonly reported using ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -DP. In this paper, we propose a numerical accountant for evaluating the privacy loss for algorithms with continuous one dimensional output. This accountant can be applied to the subsampled multidimensional Gaussian mechanism which underlies the popular DP stochastic gradient descent. The proposed method is based on a numerical approximation of an integral formula which gives the exact ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) -values. The approximation is carried out by discretising the integral and by evaluating discrete convolutions using the fast Fourier transform algorithm. We give both theoretical error bounds and numerical error estimates for the approximation. Experimental comparisons with state-of-the-art techniques demonstrate significant improvements in bound tightness and/or computation time. Python code for the method can be found in Github.



1
Introduction

Differential privacy (DP)
[
9
]
has clearly been
established as the dominant paradigm for privacy-preserving machine
learning. Early work on DP machine learning focused on single shot
perturbations for convex problems (e.g.
[
7
]
), while
contemporary research has focused on iterative algorithms such as DP
stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
16
,
18
,
2
]
.

Evaluating the privacy loss of an iterative algorithm is based on the
composition theory of DP. The so-called advanced composition theorem
of
[
11
]
showed how to trade decreased

\varepsilon
with slightly increased
\delta
in

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. This was further improved
e.g. by
[
12
]
. The privacy amplification
by subsampling
[
6
,
5
,
4
,
23
]

is another component that has been studied to improve the privacy
bounds.

A major breakthrough in obtaining tighter composition bounds came from
using the entire privacy loss profile of DP algorithms instead of
single
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-values. This was first introduced by the moments
accountant
[
2
]
. The development of Rényi differential privacy
(RDP)
[
14
]
allowed tight bounds on the privacy cost of
composition, and recently proposed amplification theorems for RDP
[
3
,
22
]
showed how subsampling affects the privacy cost of
RDP. In
[
24
]
tight RDP bounds were given for the Poisson subsampling method.

Using the recently introduced privacy loss distribution (PLD) formalism

[
17
]
, we compute tight
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP bounds on the
composition of subsampled Gaussian mechanisms, using discrete Fourier
transforms to evaluate the required convolutions. We show numerically
that the achieved privacy bounds are tighter than
those obtained by Rényi DP compositions and the moments accountant.

Within this computational framework, in addition to the commonly considered Poisson subsampling method,
we are also able to compute tight privacy bounds for
the subsampling with replacement and subsampling without replacement methods.

2
Differential Privacy

We first recall some basic definitions of differential privacy
[
10
]
. We use the
following notation. An input dataset containing
N
data points is denoted as
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
,
1\leq i\leq N
.

Definition 1
.

We say two datasets
X
and
Y
are neighbours in remove/add relation if you get
one by removing/adding an element from/to to other and denote it with
\sim_{R}
.
We say
X
and
Y
are neighbours in substitute relation if you get one by substituting
one element in the other. We denote this with
\sim_{S}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. Let
\sim
define a neighbouring relation.
Mechanism
\mathcal{M}\,:\,\mathcal{X}^{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim)
-DP if for every
X\sim Y
and every measurable set
E\subset\mathcal{R}
it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{M}(X)\in E)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Pr}(\mathcal{M}(Y)\in E)+\delta.

When the relation is clear from context or irrelevant, we will abbreviate it as
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.
We call
\mathcal{M}
tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim)
-DP, if there does not exist
\delta^{\prime}<\delta

such that
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta^{\prime},\sim)
-DP.

3
Privacy loss distribution

We first introduce the basic tool for obtaining tight privacy bounds: the privacy loss distribution (PLD).

The results in this section can be seen as continuous versions of their discrete counterparts given in
[
13
]
and
[
17
]
.
Detailed proofs are given in Appendix. The results apply for both neighbouring relations
\sim_{S}
and
\sim_{R}
.

We consider mechanisms
\mathcal{M}\,:\,\mathcal{X}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which give
as an output distributions with support equaling
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\,:\,\mathcal{X}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a randomised mechanism and
let
X\sim Y
. Let
f_{X}(t)
denote the density function of

\mathcal{M}(X)
and
f_{Y}(t)
the density function of
\mathcal{M}(Y)
. Assume

f_{X}(t)>0
and
f_{Y}(t)>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We define the privacy loss function
of
f_{X}
over
f_{Y}
as

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}.

The following gives the definition of the privacy loss distribution via its density function.
We note that the assumptions on differentiability and bijectivity of the privacy loss function
hold for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism which is considered in Sec.
6
.

Definition 4
.

Let the assumptions of Def.
A.3
hold and
suppose
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}\,:\,\mathbb{R}\rightarrow D
,
D\subset\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable bijective function.
The privacy loss distribution (PLD) of
\mathcal{M}(X)
over
\mathcal{M}(Y)
is defined to be a random variable
which has the density function

\omega_{X/Y}(s)=\begin{cases}f_{X}\big(\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)\big)\,\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts},&s\in\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R}),\\
0,&\mathrm{else.}\end{cases}

For the discrete valued versions of the following result, see
[
17
, Lemmas 5 and 10]
.

Lemma 5
.

Assume
(\varepsilon,\infty)\subset\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})
.

\mathcal{M}
is tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for

\delta(\varepsilon)=\max\{\delta_{X/Y}(\varepsilon),\delta_{Y/X}(\varepsilon)\},

where

\displaystyle\delta_{X/Y}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})\,\omega_{X/Y}(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

and similarly for
\delta_{Y/X}(\varepsilon)
.

The PLD formalism is essentially based on Lemma
A.2
which
states that the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with

\displaystyle\delta(\varepsilon)=\max_{X\sim Y}\Bigg\{\int\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt,\int\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\Bigg\}.

The integral representation of Lemma
A.4
is then obtained by change of variables.
Denoting
s=\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)
, it clearly holds that
f_{Y}(t)={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-s}f_{X}(t)
and

\displaystyle\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{X}(t),&\text{ if }s>\varepsilon,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By change of variables
t=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(s)
, we obtain the representation of Lemma
A.4
.

We get the tight privacy guarantee for compositions from a continuous counterpart of
[
17
, Thm. 1]
.

Theorem 6
.

Consider
k
consecutive applications of a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. The composition is tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for
\delta
given by

\delta(\varepsilon)=\max\{\delta_{X/Y}(\varepsilon),\delta_{Y/X}(\varepsilon)\}
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{X/Y}(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})\left(\omega_{X/Y}*^{k}\omega_{X/Y}\right)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

where
\omega_{X/Y}*^{k}\omega_{X/Y}
denotes the
k
-fold convolution of
\omega_{X/Y}
(a similar formula holds for
\delta_{Y/X}(\varepsilon)
).

4
The discrete Fourier transform

The discrete Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
and its inverse
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are linear operators
\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}

that decompose a complex vector into a Fourier series, or reconstruct it from its Fourier series.
Suppose
x=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}),w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are defined as
[
20
]

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}x)_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{n-1}x_{j}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-i2\pi kj/n},

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}^{-1}w)_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{j=0}^{n-1}w_{j}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{i2\pi kj/n}.

Evaluating
\mathcal{F}x
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}w
takes
O(N^{2})
operations,
however evaluation via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
[
8
]
reduces
the computational cost to
O(N\log N)
.

The convolution theorem
[
19
]
states that for periodic discrete convolutions it holds that

\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{n-1}v_{i}w_{k-i}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}v\odot\mathcal{F}w),

(4.1)

where
\odot
denotes the elementwise product of vectors and the summation indices are modulo
n
.

5
Description of the method

We next describe the numerical method for computing tight DP-guarantees for continuous one dimensional
distributions.

5.1
Truncation of convolutions

We first approximate the convolutions on a truncated interval
[-L,L]
as

\displaystyle(\omega*\omega)(x)\approx\int_{-L}^{L}\omega(t)\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt=:(\omega\circledast\omega)(x).

To obtain periodic convolutions for the discrete Fourier transform we need to periodise
\omega
.
Let
\widetilde{\omega}
be a
2L
-periodic extension of
\omega
such that

\widetilde{\omega}(t+n2L)=\omega(t)
for all
t\in[-L,L)
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We further approximate

\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\omega(t)\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\approx\int_{-L}^{L}\widetilde{\omega}(t)\widetilde{\omega}(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

(5.1)

5.2
Discretisation of convolutions

Divide the interval
[-L,L]
on
n
equidistant points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}
such that

x_{i}=-L+i\Delta x,\textrm{ where }\Delta x=2L/n.

Consider the vectors

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\omega}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{\omega}_{n-1}\end{bmatrix},

where

\omega_{i}=\omega(-L+i\Delta x)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widetilde{\omega}_{i}=\widetilde{\omega}(i\Delta x).

Assuming
n
is even, from the periodicity it follows that

\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}}=D\boldsymbol{\omega},\quad\textrm{where}\quad D=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n/2}\\
I_{n/2}&0\end{bmatrix}.

We approximate (
5.1
) using a Riemann sum and the convolution theorem (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast\widetilde{\omega})(i\Delta x)=

\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\widetilde{\omega}(t)\widetilde{\omega}(i\Delta x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle\quad\approx

\displaystyle\Delta x\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{\omega}_{\ell}\,\widetilde{\omega}_{i-\ell}\textrm{ (indices modulo $n$) }

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta x\left[\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}})\odot\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}})\big)\right]_{i}.

Discretisation of
k
-fold truncated convolutions leads to
k
-fold discrete convolutions and to the approximation

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(-L+i\Delta x)

\displaystyle\approx(\Delta x)^{k-1}\left[D\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\omega}})^{\odot k}\big)\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=(\Delta x)^{-1}\left[D\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(D\boldsymbol{\omega}\Delta x)^{\odot k}\big)\right]_{i},

where
⊙k
denotes
k
th elementwise power of vectors.

5.3
Approximation of the
\delta(\varepsilon)
-integral

Finally, using the discretised convolutions we approximate the integral formula for the exact
\delta
-value.
Denote the discrete convolution vector

C^{k}=(\Delta x)^{-1}\left[D\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(D\boldsymbol{\omega}\Delta x)^{\odot k}\big)\right]

and the starting point of the discrete sum

\ell_{\varepsilon}=\min\{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,-L+\ell\Delta x>\varepsilon\}.

Using the vector
C^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{0}^{k}&\ldots&C_{n-1}^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, we approximate
the integral formula given in Thm.
6
as a Riemann sum:

\displaystyle\delta(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts

(5.2)

\displaystyle\approx\Delta x\sum\nolimits_{\ell=\ell_{\varepsilon}}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(-L+\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}.

We call this method the Fourier Accountant (FA) and describe it in the pseudocode of Algorithm
1
.
We give in Sec.
7
error estimates to
determine the parameters
L
and
n
such that the error caused by approximations is below a desired level.

Algorithm 1

Fourier Accountant algorithm

Input: privacy loss distribution
\omega
,
number of compositions
k
, truncation parameter
L
, number of discretisation points
n
.

Evaluate the discrete distribution values

\omega_{i}=\omega(-L+i\Delta x),\quad i=0,\ldots,n-1,\,\,\Delta x=\tfrac{2L}{n}.

Set

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Evaluate

\displaystyle C^{k}

\displaystyle=(\Delta x)^{-1}\left[D\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(D\boldsymbol{\omega}\Delta x)^{\odot k}\big)\right],

\displaystyle\ell_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\min\{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,-L+\ell\Delta x>\varepsilon\}.

Evaluate the approximation

\delta(\varepsilon)\approx\Delta x\sum\nolimits_{\ell=\ell_{\varepsilon}}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(-L+\ell\Delta x)}\big)C^{k}_{\ell}.

5.4
Computing
\varepsilon(\delta)
using Newton’s method

In order to get the function
\varepsilon(\delta)
, we compute the inverse of
\delta(\varepsilon)
using Newton’s method.
From (
5.2
) it follows that (see Lemma
D.1
of Appendix)

\delta^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=-\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts.

(5.3)

Thus, in order to find
\varepsilon
such that
\delta(\varepsilon)=\bar{\delta}
, we apply Newton’s method
[
20
]
to the function

\delta(\varepsilon)-\bar{\delta}
which gives the iteration

\varepsilon_{\ell+1}=\varepsilon_{\ell}-\frac{\delta(\varepsilon_{\ell})-\bar{\delta}}{\delta^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{\ell})}.

Evaluating
\delta^{\prime}(\varepsilon)
for different values of
\varepsilon
is cheap using the formula (
5.3
)
and an approximation analogous to (
5.2
). As is common practice, we use as a stopping criterion

\left|\delta({\varepsilon_{\ell}})-\bar{\delta}\right|\leq\tau

for some prescribed tolerance parameter
\tau
. The iteration was found to converge in all experiments with an initial value
\varepsilon_{0}=0
.

5.5
Approximation for varying mechanisms

Our approach also allows computing privacy cost of a composite mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{1}\circ\ldots\circ\mathcal{M}_{k}
,
where the PLDs of the mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{i}
vary.
This is needed for example when accounting the privacy loss of Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics iterations
[
23
]
, where decreasing
the step size increases
\sigma
.

In this case the function
\delta(\varepsilon)
is given by Thm.
A.7
of Appendix by an integral formula of the form

\displaystyle\delta(\varepsilon)=\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega_{1}*\ldots*\omega_{k})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

where
\omega_{i}
’s are PLD distributions determined by the mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.

Denoting
C=(\Delta x)^{-1}\left[D\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(F_{1}\odot\ldots\odot F_{k}\big)\right]
,
where
F_{i}=\mathcal{F}(D\boldsymbol{\omega_{i}}\Delta x)
and
\boldsymbol{\omega_{i}}
’s are obtained
from discretisations of
\omega_{i}
’s (as in Sec.
5.2
), then
\delta(\varepsilon)
can be approximated
as in (
5.2
).

6
Subsampled Gaussian mechanism

The main motivation for this work comes from privacy accounting of the subsampled Gaussian mechanism which gives privacy bounds for DP-SGD (see e.g.
[
2
]
).
In the appendix, we show that the worst case privacy analysis of DP-SGD can be carried out by analysis of one dimensional probability distributions.
We derive the privacy loss distributions for three different subsampling methods:
Poisson subsampling with both
\sim_{R}
- and
\sim_{S}
-neighbouring relations, sampling without replacement with
\sim_{S}
-neighbouring relation and
sampling with replacement with
\sim_{S}
-neighbouring relation.
We note the following related works.
In
[
3
]
RDP bounds are considered for these three subsampling methods,
in
[
22
]
improved RDP bounds were given for the case of sampling without replacement and
in
[
24
]
tight RDP bounds were given for the case of Poisson subsampling.

6.1
Poisson subsampling for
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{R})
-DP

We start with the Poisson subsampling method, where each member of the dataset is included in the stochastic gradient minibatch with probability
q
.
This method is also used in the moments accountant
[
2
]
, and also considered in
[
13
]
and
[
22
]
.
As we show in Appendix, the
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{R})
-DP analysis of the Poisson subsampling is equivalent to considering the
following one dimensional distributions:

\displaystyle f_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

\displaystyle f_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

Here
\sigma^{2}
denotes the variance of the additive Gaussian noise.
Using Definition
A.3
, the privacy loss function is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\log\tfrac{q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=\log\left(q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{2t-1}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\right).

Now
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})=(\log(1-q),\infty)
and

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}
is again a strictly increasing continuously differentiable bijective
function in the whole
\mathbb{R}
. Straightforward calculation shows that

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)=\sigma^{2}\log\frac{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}-(1-q)}{q}+\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover,

\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\,s}\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)=\frac{\sigma^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}}{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}-(1-q)}.

The privacy loss distribution
\omega_{X/Y}
is determined by the density function given in Definition
A.5
.
Lemma
A.9
and its corollary
explain the observation that generally
\delta_{X/Y}>\delta_{Y/X}
.

6.2
Sampling without replacement for
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{S})
-DP

We next consider the
\sim_{S}
-neighbouring relation and sampling without replacement.
In this case the batch size
m
is fixed and each member of the dataset contributes at most once for each minibatch.
Here
q=m/n
, where
n
denotes the total number of data samples.
Without loss of generality we consider here the density functions

\displaystyle f_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

\displaystyle f_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t+1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

The privacy loss function is now given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\Bigg(\frac{q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{2t-1}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)}{q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-2t-1}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)}\Bigg).

We see that
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}
and again that

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}
is a strictly increasing continuously differentiable
function. With a straightforward calculation we find that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)=\sigma^{2}\log\Big(\frac{1}{2c}\big(-(1-q)(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})+\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})^{2}+4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}}\big)\Big),

where
c=q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}}
.

Using Lemma
A.9
and the property

f_{Y}(-t)=f_{X}(t)
, we see that
\delta=\delta_{Y/X}=\delta_{X/Y}
.

We remark that in
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{S})
-DP, the Poisson subsampling with the sampling parameter
\gamma

is equivalent to the case of the sampling without replacement with
q=\gamma
, as in both cases the differing element is included in the minibatch with probability
\gamma
.

6.3
Sampling with replacement

Consider next the sampling with replacement and the
\sim_{S}
-neighbouring relation.
Again the batch size is fixed, however this time each element of the minibatch is drawn from the dataset with probability q.
Thus the number of contributions of each member of the dataset is not limited.
Then
\ell
, the number of times the differing sample
x^{\prime}
is in the batch, is binomially distributed,
i.e.,
\ell\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(1/n,m)
, where
m
denotes the batch size and
n
the total number of data samples.

Without loss of generality, we consider here the density functions

\displaystyle f_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

\displaystyle f_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t+\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

The privacy loss function is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\left(\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\right),

where

c_{\ell}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-\ell^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\quad x={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}}.

Since
c_{\ell}>0
for all
\ell=1,\ldots,m
, clearly
\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}
is strictly increasing as a function of
t
and

\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}
is strictly decreasing. Moreover, we see that

\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\rightarrow 0\quad\textrm{as}\,\,t\rightarrow-\infty

and

\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\rightarrow\infty\quad\textrm{as}\,\,t\rightarrow\infty.

Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}
and

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)
is a strictly increasing continuously differentiable function in its domain. To find
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)
one needs to solve
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=s
,
i.e., one needs to find the single positive real root of a polynomial of order
2m
.
As in the case of subsampling without replacement, here
\delta=\delta_{Y/X}=\delta_{X/Y}
.

7
Error estimates

We give error estimates for the Poisson subsampling method with the neighbouring relation
\sim_{R}
.
Thus, in this section
\omega
denotes the PLD density function defined in Sec.
6.1
.
The estimates are determined by the parameters
L
and
n
,
the truncation interval radius and the number of discretisation points, respectively.

The total error consists of (see Thm.
C.1
in Appendix)

1.

The errors arising from the truncation of the convolution integrals and periodisation.

2.

The error from neglecting the tail integral

\int_{L}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts.

(7.1)

3.

The numerical errors in the approximation of the convolutions
(\omega\ast^{k}\omega)

and in the Riemann sum approximation (
5.2
).

We obtain bounds for the first two sources of error, i.e., for the tail integral (
C.3
) and the periodisation error, using the Chernoff bound
[
21
]

\mathbb{P}[X\geq t]=\mathbb{P}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda X}\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda t}]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda X}]}{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda t}},

which holds for any random variable
X
and all
\lambda>0
.
Denoting also the PLD random variable by
\omega
,
the moment generating function
\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]
is related to
the log of the moment generating function of the privacy loss function
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}

as follows. Define (see also
[
2
]
)

\alpha(\lambda):=\log\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{t\sim f_{X}(t)}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\mathcal{L}(t)}].

By the change of variable
s=\mathcal{L}(t)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda s}\omega(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\log(1-q)}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda s}f_{X}(\mathcal{L}^{-1}(s))\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}^{-1}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\mathcal{L}(t)}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\alpha(\lambda)}.

Using existing bounds for
\alpha(\lambda)
given in
[
2
]
and
[
15
]
, we bound

\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]
and obtain the required tail bounds.

7.1
Periodisation and truncation of convolutions

We have the following bound for the error arising from the periodisation and the truncation of the convolution integrals.
The proof is given in Appendix, Lemma
C.6
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
. Let
\omega
be defined as in Sec.
6.1
, and let
L\geq 1
.
Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\varepsilon}^{L}(\omega\ast^{k}\omega-\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(x)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptx\right|\leq Lk\sigma{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{(\sigma^{2}L+C)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle+{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\alpha(L/2)}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{L^{2}}{2}}+2\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{k\alpha(nL)}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-2(nL)^{2}},

where
C=\sigma^{2}\log(\frac{1}{2q})-\frac{1}{2}
.

For example, setting
\sigma
,
q
as in the example of Figure

the first term is

already for

The second term dominates the rest of the bound of Lemma

and it is much smaller than

7.2
Convolution tail bound

Let
\omega
denote the PLD density function. Now, the tail of the integral
representation for
\delta
(Thm.
6
), with
L>\varepsilon
, can be bounded as

\int_{L}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts<\int_{L}^{\infty}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts.

We consider both upper bounds and estimates for the tail integral of convolutions.

7.2.1
Analytic tail bound

Using the Chernoff bound we derive an analytic bound for the tail integral of convolutions.
In a certain sense this is equivalent to finding bounds for the RDP parameters, since
an RDP bound gives a bound also for the moment generating function
\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]
needed in the Chernoff bound.
The following result is derived from recent RDP results
[
15
]
. The proof and an illustration of the result are given in Appendix.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose
q\leq\frac{1}{5}
and
\sigma\geq 4
. Let
L
be chosen such that
\lambda=L/2
satisfies

\displaystyle 1<

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}c-2\log\sigma,

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}c-\log\,5-2\log\,\sigma}{c+\log(q\lambda)+1/(2\sigma^{2})},

where
c=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{q(\lambda-1)}\right)
. Then, we have

\int_{L}^{\infty}(\omega\ast^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\leq\left(1+\frac{2q^{2}(\tfrac{L}{2}+1)\tfrac{L}{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{k}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{L^{2}}{2}}.

In order to avoid the restriction on
\sigma
in Thm.
8
, we consider an approximative bound.

7.2.2
Tail bound estimate

We next derive an approximative tail bound using the
\alpha(\lambda)
-bound given in
[
2
]
.
Denote

, where
\omega^{i}
denotes the PLD random variable of the

Since

\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda S_{k}}]=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega^{i}}]

and the Chernoff bound shows that

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{\infty}(\omega\ast^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts=\mathbb{P}[S_{k}\geq L]\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{k\alpha(\lambda)}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\lambda L}

for any

We recall the result from
[

which holds for the Poisson subsampling method.

Lemma 9

Let

\displaystyle\hskip-6.23116pt\int_{L}^{\infty}(\omega\ast^{k}\omega)(s)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\lessapprox\exp\left({k\frac{q^{2}(\tfrac{L}{2}+1)\tfrac{L}{2}}{(1-q)\sigma^{2}}}\right){\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\tfrac{L^{2}}{2}}.

(7.3)

For example, when

7.3
Discretisation errors

Derivation of discretisation error bounds can be carried out using the so called Euler–Maclaurin formula (Sec.
C.3
in Appendix).
This requires bounds for higher order derivatives of
\omega
.
As an illustrating example,
consider the bound (recall
\Delta x=2L/n
)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-L}^{L}\omega(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\nolimits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\omega(-L+\ell\Delta x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Delta x\,\omega(L)+\frac{(\Delta x)^{2}}{12}\max_{t\in[-L,L]}\left|\omega^{\prime\prime}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Delta x\,\sigma{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{-(\sigma^{2}L+C)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+\frac{(\Delta x)^{2}}{12}\max_{t\in[-L,L]}\left|\omega^{\prime\prime}(t)\right|,

where
C=\sigma^{2}\log(\frac{1}{2q})-\frac{1}{2}
.
By Lemma
D.4
,
\max_{t}\left|\omega^{\prime\prime}(t)\right|
has an upper bound
O(\sigma^{3}/q^{3})
.
With bounds for higher order derivatives, tighter error bound could be obtained. In a similar fashion, bounds for the errors for the approximation (
5.2
)
could be derived. However, we resort to numerical estimates.

7.3.1
Estimate for the discretisation error

Consider the error arising from the Riemann sum

I_{n}:=\Delta x\sum\nolimits_{\ell=\ell_{\varepsilon}}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(-L+\ell\Delta x)}\big)C^{k}_{\ell}.

As we show in Sec.
C.3
of Appendix, it holds

\displaystyle E_{n}:=

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-I_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\Delta x+O((\Delta x)^{2})=K\frac{2L}{n}+O\Big(\Big(\frac{2L}{n}\Big)^{2}\Big)

for some constant
K
independent of
n
.
Therefore,

2(I_{n}-I_{2n})=E_{n}+O((\Delta x)^{2})

which leads us to use as an estimate

\mathrm{err}(L,n):=2\left|I_{n}-I_{2n}\right|

(7.4)

for the numerical error
E_{n}
.

9
Conclusions

We have presented a novel approach for computing tight privacy bounds for DP.
Although we have focused on the subsampled Gaussian mechanism (with various subsampling strategies), our method is applicable to any
continuous mechanism satisfying the assumptions of Definition
A.3
. Using the existing RDP bounds we were able to give analytical bounds to some central
quantities in the error analysis. For the errors arising from the numerical integration, we gave numerical estimates.
As future work, it would be interesting
to carry out a full error analysis for the discretisation error and also to implement the method on other mechanisms
than the subsampled Gaussian mechanism. Moreover, evaluating for compositions involving both continuous and discrete valued mechanisms would
also be an interesting objective.

Appendix A
Proofs for the results of Section
3

A.1
Integral representation for exact DP-guarantees

Throughout this section we denote for neighbouring datasets

X
and
Y
the density function of
\mathcal{M}(X)
with
f_{X}(t)
and the density function of
\mathcal{M}(Y)
with
f_{Y}(t)
.

Definition A.1
.

A randomised algorithm
\mathcal{M}
with an output of continuous one dimensional distributions satisfies

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if for every set
S\subset\mathbb{R}

and every neighbouring datasets
X
and
Y

\int_{S}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\int_{S}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt+\delta\quad\textrm{and}\quad\int_{S}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\int_{S}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt+\delta.

We call
\mathcal{M}
tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, if there does not exist
\delta^{\prime}<\delta

such that
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta^{\prime})
-DP.

The following auxiliary lemma is needed to obtain the representation given by Lemma
A.4

(see
[
13
, Lemma 1]
for the discrete valued version of the result).

Lemma A.2
.

\mathcal{M}
is tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with

\delta(\varepsilon)=\max_{X\sim Y}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\Bigg\}.

(A.1)

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
is tightly
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. Then, for every set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
and every neighbouring datasets
X
and
Y
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{S}f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\leq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{S}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

We get an analogous bound for
\int_{S}f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt
.
By Definition
A.1
,

\delta\leq\max\Bigg\{\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt\Bigg\}.

To show that the above inequality is tight, consider the set

S=\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,f_{X}(t)\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t)\}.

Then,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{S}f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{S}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

Next, consider the set

S=\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,f_{Y}(t)\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t)\}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{S}f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{f_{Y}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{X}(t),0\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

(A.3)

From (
A.2
) and (
A.3
) it follows that there exists a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
such that either

\int_{S}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\int_{S}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt+\delta\quad\textrm{or}\quad\int_{S}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\int_{S}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt+\delta

for

given by (

). This shows that
\delta
given by (
A.1

∎

The next lemma gives an integral representation for the right hand side of (

)
involving the distribution function of the PLD (see also Lemma 5 of

First we need the following definition.

Definition A.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\,:\,\mathcal{X}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Lemma A.4

\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\delta_{X/Y}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})\,\cap\,[\varepsilon,\infty)}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{X}\Big(\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(s)\Big)\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

\displaystyle\delta_{Y/X}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{L}_{Y/X}(\mathbb{R})\,\cap\,[\varepsilon,\infty)}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{Y}\Big(\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{Y/X}(s)\Big)\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{Y/X}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts.

Proof.

Consider the privacy loss function
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}

.
Then, it clearly holds

\displaystyle\max\{f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t),0\}=

\displaystyle\max\{0,(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{X}(t)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{X}(t),&\text{ if }s>\varepsilon,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\max\{0,f_{X}(t)-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}f_{Y}(t)\}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

By making the change of variables

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})\,\cap\,[\varepsilon,\infty)}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})f_{X}\big(\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(s)\big)\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

since

The claim follows then from Lemma
A.2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{f_{X}(t_{1}))}{f_{Y}(t_{1}))}\right)+\log\left(\frac{f_{X^{\prime}}(t_{2})}{f_{Y^{\prime}}(t_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t_{1})+\mathcal{L}_{X^{\prime}/Y^{\prime}}(t_{2}).

Let

be a measurable set. By using the property (
A.6

\displaystyle\omega^{C}_{X/Y}(S)

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,\mathcal{L}_{c}(t_{1},t_{2})\,\in S\}}f_{X,X^{\prime}}(t_{1},t_{2})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{2}

\displaystyle=\iint\limits_{\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t_{1})+\mathcal{L}_{X^{\prime}/Y^{\prime}}(t_{2})\,\in S\}}f_{X}(t_{1})f_{X^{\prime}}(t_{2})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{2}

\displaystyle f_{X^{\prime}}\big(\mathcal{L}_{X^{\prime}/Y^{\prime}}^{-1}(s_{2})\big)\,\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}_{X^{\prime}/Y^{\prime}}^{-1}(s_{2})}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts_{1}\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{S}\left(\,\,\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega_{X/Y}(s_{1})\omega_{X^{\prime}/Y^{\prime}}(t-y_{1})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts_{1}\right)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

∎

.

\mathcal{M}

where

denotes the density function

We also give the following result for the relation between

and

This result can be seen as a continuous version of Lemma 2 in

Lemma A.9
.

Let the privacy loss functions
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}
and
\mathcal{L}_{Y/X}
and the privacy loss distributions
\omega_{X/Y}
and
\omega_{Y/X}

Then, it holds
\mathcal{L}_{Y/X}(\mathbb{R})=\{\;t\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;-t\in\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})\}
and
for all

\omega_{X/Y}(s)={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}\omega_{Y/X}(-s).

Proof.

From the definition it follows that

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=-\mathcal{L}_{Y/X}(t),

and therefore also

\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{Y/X}(s)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{X/Y}(-s)

(A.7)

for all

y\in\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\omega_{X/Y}(s)

\displaystyle=f_{X}\big(\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)\big)\,\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}

\displaystyle=f_{X}\big(\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)\big)\,\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s))}.

We notice that

\displaystyle\frac{f_{X}(t)}{\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{\prime}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{X}(t)}{\frac{f_{X}^{\prime}(t)}{f_{X}(t)}-\frac{f_{Y}^{\prime}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{X}(t)^{2}f_{Y}(t)}{f_{X}^{\prime}(t)f_{Y}(t)-f_{Y}^{\prime}(t)f_{X}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}\frac{f_{X}(t)f_{Y}(t)^{2}}{f_{X}^{\prime}(t)f_{Y}(t)-f_{Y}^{\prime}(t)f_{X}(t)}

\displaystyle={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)}\frac{f_{Y}(t)}{\mathcal{L}_{Y/X}^{\prime}(t)}

A.8
) and (
A.7

Appendix B
Tight privacy bounds for the Gaussian mechanism via one dimensional distributions

In this Section we show that the tight bounds of DP-SGD can be carried out by analysis of one dimensional mixture distributions.
This equivalence has also been used in
[
2
, Proof of Lemma 3]
.
We consider three different subsampling methods: sampling without replacement, sampling with replament and Poisson subsampling
(see
[
3
]
for further details).

In the next subsection we also rigorously show that tight privacy bounds for DP-SGD can be obtained from the analysis of one dimensional distributions.

B.1
Equivalence of the privacy bounds between the multidimensional and

one dimensional mechanisms

As an example, we consider the Poisson subsampling.
In this case each member of the dataset is included in the stochastic gradient minibatch with probability
q
.
This means that each data element can appear at most once in the sample.
The basic mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is then of the form

\mathcal{M}(X)=\sum_{x\in B}f(x)+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d}),

where
B
is a randomly drawn subset of
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
and
\|f(x)\|_{2}\leq 1
for all
x\in B
.

Consider the case of remove/add relation
\sim_{R}
and let
X
and
Y
be neighbouring datasets.
Consider first the case
q=1
, i.e.,
\left|B\right|=N
. The condition of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential
privacy states that for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every neighbouring
X
and
Y
:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(X)\in S)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(Y)\in S)+\delta.

(B.1)

Suppose
X=Y\cup\{x^{\prime}\}
and assume
\|f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}=1
.
and we easily see that this is then equivalent to the condition that for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(f(x)^{\prime},\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in S\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in S\big)+\delta.

(B.2)

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a unitary matrix such that

Uf(x^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}=:e_{1}.

This means that
U
is of the form
U=\begin{bmatrix}f(x^{\prime})&\widetilde{U}\end{bmatrix}
,
where
\widetilde{U}
can be taken as any
d\times(d-1)
matrix with orthonormal columns such that
\widetilde{U}^{T}f(x^{\prime})=0
.

Due to the unitarity of
U
, the condition (
B.2
) is equivalent to the condition that for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(U\mathcal{N}(f(x^{\prime}),\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in US\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(U\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in US\big)+\delta.

(B.3)

Furthermore, due to the unitarity of
U
,
U\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and we see that (
B.3
) is equivalent to
the condition that for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(e_{1},\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in US\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in US\big)+\delta,

(B.4)

where
US=\{Ux\,:\,x\in S\}.
Then, we see that the condition (
B.3
) is equivalent to the condition that
for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^{2})\in S\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\in S\big)+\delta.

(B.5)

Thus, if
X
and
Y
are given as above, finding the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta
that satisfy (
B.1
) amounts
to finding values of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
that satisfy (
B.5
).

When
q<1
, we see that
f(x^{\prime})
is in
B
with a probability
q
.
Reasoning as above, we arrive at the the condition that for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(\,q\,\mathcal{N}(f(x^{\prime}),\sigma^{2}I_{d})+(1-q)\,\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in S\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in S\big)+\delta,

where
q\mathcal{N}(f(x^{\prime}),\sigma^{2}I_{d})+(1-q)\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
denotes a mixture distribution.
Similarly, this leads to considering the one dimensional neighbouring distributions

f_{X}:=q\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^{2})+(1-q)\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\quad\textrm{and}\quad f_{Y}:=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}).

In order the condition (
B.1
) holds for all
X\sim_{R}Y
, then it has to hold that
for every measurable set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
both

\mathbb{P}\big(f_{X}\in S\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(f_{Y}\in S\big)+\delta\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\big(f_{Y}\in S\big)\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\big(f_{X}\in S\big)+\delta.

With an analogous reasoning, we see that in the case of substitution relation
\sim_{S}
the worst case is obtained by considering the neighbouring distributions

q\mathcal{N}(1,\sigma^{2})+(1-q)\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

and

q\mathcal{N}(-1,\sigma^{2})+(1-q)\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}).

Finally, we note that the case
\|f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}<1
would lead to neighbouring distributions
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
that are closer
to each other than in the case
\|f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}=1
. This would give tighter
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-values, i.e.,
\|f(x^{\prime})\|_{2}=1
gives the worst case.
This could be shown rigorously by scaling the parameter
\sigma
and considering the analysis below.

B.2
Poisson subsampling

B.2.1
Neighbouring relation with remove/add

As shown above, for the analysis in case of Poisson subsampling

\displaystyle f_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

(B.6)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

Denote by

The privacy loss function is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\left(\frac{q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{q\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t+1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{2t-1}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)}{q\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-2t-1}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q)}\right).

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\frac{cx+(1-q)}{cx^{-1}+(1-q)}={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\overset{x>0}{\iff}

\displaystyle x=\frac{-(1-q)(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})+\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})^{2}+4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}}}{2c}.

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)=\sigma^{2}\log\Bigg(\frac{-(1-q)(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})+\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})^{2}+4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}}}{2c}\Bigg)

\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\,s}\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)=\sigma^{2}\frac{\frac{4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}-2(1-q)^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})}{2\sqrt{4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}+(1-q)^{2}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})^{2}}}+(1-q){\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}}{\sqrt{4c^{2}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}+(1-q)^{2}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})^{2}}-(1-q)(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s})}.

In case of odd loss functions (

we have the following:

\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\omega_{X/Y}}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}(s)=\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pt\omega_{Y/X}}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts}(s).

This follows from using the oddity of
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}
and Lemma
A.9
.
Therefore, if
f_{Y}(-t)=f_{X}(t)
and
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(-t)=-\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)
,
it holds
\delta=\delta_{Y/X}=\delta_{X/Y}
by the representation (
A.5
).

We remark that in
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{S})
-DP, the Poisson subsampling with the sampling parameter
\gamma
(i.e., each sample is in the batch with a probability
\gamma
)
is equivalent to the case of the sampling with replacement with
q=\gamma
, as in both cases the differing element is included in the minibatch with probability
\gamma
.

B.4
Sampling with replacement and
\sim_{S}
-neighouring relation

Consider next the sampling with replacement and the
\sim_{S}
-neighouring relation. Then the number of times the differing sample
x^{\prime}
is in the batch is binomially distributed,
i.e., the probability for being in the batch
\ell
times is
\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}
,
where
m
denotes the batch size and
n
the total number of data samples.

Then, without loss of generality, we may consider the density functions (
m
denotes the batch size)

\displaystyle f_{X}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

(B.8)

\displaystyle f_{Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t+\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

where
q=1/n
.
The privacy loss function is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=\log\left(\frac{\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t+\ell)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\right),

where

c_{\ell}=q^{\ell}(1-q)^{m-\ell}{m\choose\ell}\,{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-\ell^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad x={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(B.9)

Since
c_{\ell}>0
for all
\ell=1,\ldots,m
, clearly
\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}
is strictly increasing as a function of
t
and

\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}
is strictly decreasing. Moreover, we see that

\tfrac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\rightarrow 0\quad\textrm{as}\,\,t\rightarrow-\infty\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad\tfrac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{\ell}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}x^{-\ell}}\rightarrow\infty\quad\textrm{as}\,\,t\rightarrow\infty.

Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}
and

\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)
is a strictly increasing continuously differentiable function in the whole
\mathbb{R}
.
To find
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)
one needs to solve
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=s
, i.e., one needs to find the single real root of a polynomial of order
2m
.

To find
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}^{-1}(s)
, i.e. to solve
\mathcal{L}_{X/Y}(t)=s
for a given
y
, one may use e.g. Newton’s method.

Appendix C
Error estimates

For the error analysis we consider the Poisson subsampling with
(\varepsilon,\delta,\sim_{R})
-DP, i.e., we consider
the PLD density function (Sec.
B.2.1
)

\omega(s)=\begin{cases}f(g(s))g^{\prime}(s),&\text{ if }s>\log(1-q),\\
0,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(C.1)

where

f(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\,[q{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+(1-q){\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}],

g(s)=\sigma^{2}\log\left(\frac{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{s}-(1-q)}{q}\right)+\frac{1}{2}.

Theorem C.1
.

Let the vector
C^{k}
be defined as in Sec.
5.3
.
Total error of the approximation (determined by the truncation parameter
L
and the discretisation parameter
n
) can be bounded by three terms as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}\right|\leq I_{1}(L)+I_{2}(L)+I_{3}(L,n),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(L)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts,

\displaystyle I_{2}(L)

\displaystyle=\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)-(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\right|,

\displaystyle I_{3}(L,n)

\displaystyle=\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}\right|.

Proof.

By adding and subtracting terms and using the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}\right|

(C.2)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts+\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)-(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C_{\ell}^{k}\right|.

∎

We consider next separately each of the three terms on the right hand side of (
C.2
).

C.1
Tail bounds for the convolved PLDs

The first term on the right hand side of (
C.2
) is bounded by the tail of the convolved PLDs:

\left|\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts\right|\leq\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}(\omega*^{k}\omega)(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts.

(C.3)

In this Section we show how to use existing Rényi differential privacy (RDP) results to bound the tail (
C.3
).

The Chernoff bound (see e.g.
[
21
]
) states that for any random variable
X
and for all
\lambda>0
it holds

\mathbb{P}[X\geq t]=\mathbb{P}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda X}\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda t}]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda X}]}{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda t}}.

(C.4)

From the RDP bounds given in
[
15
]
we obtain the following bound for the moment generating function
\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]
.

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
q\leq\frac{1}{5}
and
\sigma\geq 4
. Suppose
\lambda
satisfies

\displaystyle 1<

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}c-2\log\sigma,

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}c-\log\,5-2\log\,\sigma}{c+\log(q\lambda)+1/(2\sigma^{2})},

where
c=\log\left(1+\frac{1}{q(\lambda-1)}\right)
. Then,

\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]\leq 1+\frac{2q^{2}(\lambda+1)\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}.

Proof.

Making change of variables
y=\mathcal{L}(t)
(recall:
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})=(\log(1-q),\infty)
and

\mathcal{L}(t)
is a strictly increasing differentiable function),
we see a connection to the Rényi differential privacy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\log(1-q)}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda s}\omega(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\mathcal{L}(t)}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}\right)^{\lambda}f_{X}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}\right)^{\lambda+1}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

Here
f_{X}(t)=q\mu_{1}(t)+(1-q)\mu_{0}(t)
, where
\mu_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
and

\mu_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\frac{-(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
, and

f_{Y}(t)=\mu_{0}(t)
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega}]=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{f_{X}(t)}{f_{Y}(t)}\right)^{\lambda+1}f_{Y}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left((1-q)+q\frac{\mu_{1}(t)}{\mu_{0}(t)}\right)^{\lambda+1}\mu_{0}(t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt.

(C.5)

From the proof of
[
15
, Thm. 11]
we get a bound for (
C.5
) which shows the claim.
∎

Theorem C.3
.

Let the assumptions on
\sigma
and
q
of Lemma
C.2
hold. Assume

,
i=1,\ldots,k

)

C.2
.

Since

\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda S_{k}}]=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\lambda\omega_{i}}]\leq\left(1+\frac{2q^{2}(\lambda+1)\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{k}.

Using the Chernoff bound, we find that

\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\geq L)\leq\left(1+\frac{2q^{2}(\lambda+1)\lambda}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{k}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-L\lambda}.

For all
\lambda
that satisfy the assumptions of Lemma

C.2.1
Truncation of the convolution integrals

We first bound

. We have the following pointwise bound.

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\sigma>0
and
0<q<\frac{1}{2}
.
Let
\omega
be defined as above, and let
L\geq 1
.
Then, for all

\left|(\omega*^{k}\omega-\omega\circledast^{k}\omega)(x)\right|\leq k\sigma{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{-(\sigma^{2}L+C)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

where
C=\sigma^{2}\log(\frac{1}{2q})-\frac{1}{2}

Proof.

By adding and subtracting, we may write

\omega*^{k}\omega-\omega\circledast^{k}\omega=\omega\circledast(\omega*^{k-1}\omega-\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)+\omega\circledast(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)-\omega*(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega),

(C.7)

where

\displaystyle\omega\circledast(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)-\omega*(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t)(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt-\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega(t)(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}\omega(t)(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt,

since
\omega(s)=0
for all

and
-L<\log(1-q)
.
Using Lemma
D.3
of Appendix, we see that for all
x
,

\displaystyle\left|\big(\omega\circledast(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)-\omega*(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)\big)(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{s\geq L}\omega(s)\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}(\omega\circledast^{k-1}\omega)(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

(C.8)

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{s\geq L}\omega(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{-(\sigma^{2}L+C)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

Using again Lemma
D.3
, we see that for all

\displaystyle\left|(\omega*\omega-\omega\circledast\omega)(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega(t)\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt-\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t)\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}\omega(t)\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{s\geq L}\omega(s)\int\limits_{L}^{\infty}\omega(x-t)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt

The claim follows from the recursion (
C.7
) and the bounds (
C.8
) and (
C.9

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\omega

\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}\left|(\omega\circledast^{k}\omega-\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(x)\right|\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptx\leq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\alpha(L/2)}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\frac{L^{2}}{2}}+2\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{k\alpha(nL)}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-2(nL)^{2}}.

Proof.

We see that

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega}-\omega\circledast^{k}\omega)(x)

(C.11)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\widetilde{\omega}(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\widetilde{\omega}(t_{k-1})\,\widetilde{\omega}(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\,\omega(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\,\widetilde{\omega}(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\,\omega(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\Big(\widetilde{\omega}(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})-\omega(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\Big)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1},

since

on the interval

.

\widetilde{\omega}
is the

-periodic function for which

\widetilde{\omega}(t)-\omega(t)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{\omega}_{n}(t)-r(t),

(C.12)

where

\widehat{\omega}_{n}(t)=\begin{cases}\omega(t-2nL),&\text{ if }t\in[(2n-1)L,(2n+1)L]\\

Thus, from (
C.11
) and (

We see that
\left|C_{1}(x)\right|
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left|C_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{\omega}_{n}(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{1})\ldots\int\limits_{-L}^{L}\omega(t_{k-1})\,\widehat{\omega}_{n}(x-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k-1}t_{i})\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{1}\ldots\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptt_{k-1}

C.3
Error expansion with respect to
\Delta x

The purpose of this section is to show that the following assumption used in the main text holds (recall
\Delta x=2L/n
):

There exists a constant
K
independent of
n
such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{L}(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-s})(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(s)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5pts-\Delta x\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\big(1-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{\varepsilon-(\ell\Delta x)}\big)C^{k}_{\ell}=K\Delta x+O\big((\Delta x)^{2}\big).

(C.24)

We motivate this assumption using the Euler–Maclaurin summation formula which gives the following expansion for the error of the Riemann sum formula (see
[
20
, Ch. 3.3]
).

Lemma C.7

(Euler–Maclaurin formula)
.

Let
f\in C^{2m+2}[a,b]
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and denote
\Delta x=(b-a)/N
. Then,

\displaystyle\Delta x\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}f(a+i\Delta x)-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.5ptx=\Delta x\frac{f(a)-f(b)}{2}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{m}(\Delta x)^{2\ell}\frac{B_{2\ell}}{(2\ell)!}\big(f^{(2\ell-1)}(b)-f^{(2\ell-1)}(a)\big)

\displaystyle+(\Delta x)^{2m+2}\frac{B_{2m+2}}{(2m+2)!}f^{(2m+2)}(\eta),\quad\eta\in[a,b],

where
B_{i}
is the
i
th Bernoulli number.

Consider the discrete convolution vector
C^{k}
as defined in Section 5.
By definition (summations periodic, indices modulo
n
),

C_{i}^{k}=\Delta x\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{\omega}(j\Delta x)C_{i-j}^{k-1},\quad C_{i}^{2}=\Delta x\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{\omega}(j\Delta x)\widetilde{\omega}(i\Delta x-j\Delta x),

If, instead, we consider the discrete convolutions

\widehat{C}_{i}^{k}=\Delta x\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega(j\Delta x)C_{i-j}^{k-1},\quad\widehat{C}_{i}^{2}=\Delta x\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega(j\Delta x)\omega(i\Delta x-j\Delta x),

then by the Euler–Maclaurin formula there clearly exist a constant
K
independent of
n
such that

\widehat{C}_{i}^{k}-(\omega\circledast^{k}\omega)(-L+i\Delta x)=K\Delta x+O\big((\Delta x)^{2}\big)

for all
k=1,\ldots
and
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
.
Since the integrands in the convolution integrals of
\omega\circledast^{k}\omega
are piecewise smooth
(we omit details here), it also has to hold

C_{i}^{k}-(\widetilde{\omega}\circledast^{k}\widetilde{\omega})(-L+i\Delta x)=K\Delta x+O\big((\Delta x)^{2}\big)

(C.25)

for some constant
K
independent of
n
.

Using (
C.25
) and the Euler–Maclaurin formula and the fact that the expressions in (
C.24
) are piecewise smooth,
verifies the assumption (
C.24
).
