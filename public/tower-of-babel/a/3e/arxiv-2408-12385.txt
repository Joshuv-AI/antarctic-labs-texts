Sharper Bounds for Chebyshev Moment Matching, with Applications
Cameron Musco UMass Amherst cmusco@cs.umass.edu Christopher Musco New York University cmusco@nyu.edu Lucas Rosenblatt New York University lucas.rosenblatt@nyu.edu Apoorv Vikram Singh New York University apoorv.singh@nyu.edu

Abstract
We study the problem of approximately recovering a probability distribution given noisy measurements of its Chebyshev polynomial moments. This problem arises broadly across algorithms, statistics, and machine learning. By leveraging a global decay bound on the coefficients in the Chebyshev expansion of any Lipschitz function, we sharpen prior work, proving that accurate recovery in the Wasserstein distance is possible with more noise than previously known. Our result immediately yields a number of applications: 1. We give a simple “linear query” algorithm for constructing a differentially private synthetic data distribution with Wasserstein- 1 1 error O ~ ​ ( 1 / n ) \tilde{O}(1/n) based on a dataset of n n points in [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] . This bound is optimal up to log factors, and matches a recent result of Boedihardjo, Strohmer, and Vershynin [Probab. Theory. Rel., 2024], which uses a more complex “superregular random walk” method. 2. We give an O ~ ​ ( n 2 / ϵ ) \tilde{O}(n^{2}/\epsilon) time algorithm for the linear algebraic problem of estimating the spectral density of an n × n n\times n symmetric matrix up to ϵ \epsilon error in the Wasserstein distance. Our result accelerates prior methods from Chen et al. [ICML 2021] and Braverman et al. [STOC 2022]. 3. We tighten an analysis of Vinayak, Kong, Valiant, and Kakade [ICML 2019] on the maximum likelihood estimator for the statistical problem of “Learning Populations of Parameters”, extending the parameter regime in which sample optimal results can be obtained. Beyond these main results, we provide an extension of our bound to estimating distributions in d > 1 d>1 dimensions. We hope that these bounds will find applications more broadly to problems involving distribution recovery from noisy moment information.

Chebyshev Polynomials and Chebyshev Series.

Our main result analyzes the accuracy of (noisy) Chebyshev polynomial moment matching for distribution approximation. The Chebyshev polynomials are defined in

We use a few basic properties about these polynomials.

Fact 10

The Chebyshev polynomials satisfy:

1.

Boundedness:

\forall x\in[-1,1]

To obtain an orthonormal basis we also define the

The

We define the

If

3
Main Analysis

In this section, we prove our main result,
Theorem

1
, as well as
Corollary

2
. To do so, we require two main ingredients. The first is a constructive version of Jackson’s theorem on polynomial approximation of Lipschitz functions
[
Jac30
]
. A modern proof can be found in
[
BKM22
, Fact 3.2]
.

Fact 12

(Jackson’s Theorem
[
Jac30
]
)
.

Let
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be an
\ell
-Lipschitz function. Then, for any
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, there are
k+1
constants
1=b_{k}^{0}>\ldots>b_{k}^{k}\geq 0
such that the polynomial

f_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{k}^{j}\cdot\left\langle f\cdot w,\bar{T}_{j}\right\rangle\cdot\bar{T}_{j}
satisfies
\left\lVert f-f_{k}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq 18\ell/k
.

It is well-known that truncating the Chebyshev series of an
\ell
-Lipschitz function
f
to
k
terms leads to error
O(\log k\cdot\frac{\ell}{k})
in the
\ell_{\infty}
distance
[
Tre19
]
. The above version of Jackson’s theorem improves this bound by a
\log k
factor by instead using a
damped
truncated Chebyshev series: each term in the series is multiplied by a positive scaling factor between
0
and
1
. We will not need to compute these factors explicitly, but
b_{k}^{i}
has a simple closed form (see
[
BKM22
, Equation 12]
).

To bound the Wasserstein distance between distributions
p,q
, we need to upper bound
\langle f,p-q\rangle
for every
1
-Lipschitz
f
.
The value of
Fact

12
is that this inner product is closely approximated by
\langle f_{k},p-q\rangle
. Since
f_{k}
is a damped Chebyshev series, this inner product can be decomposed as a difference between
p
and
q
’s Chebyshev moments. Details will be shown in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

The second ingredient we require is a stronger bound on the decay of the Chebyshev coefficients,
\left\langle f\cdot w,\bar{T}_{j}\right\rangle
, which appear in
Fact

12
. In particular, we prove the following result:

Lemma 13

(Global Chebyshev Coefficient Decay)
.

Let
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be an
\ell
-Lipschitz, smooth function, and let
c_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\left\langle f\cdot w,\bar{T}_{j}\right\rangle
for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. Then,
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(jc_{j})^{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\ell^{2}
.

Lemma

13
implies the well known fact that
c_{j}=O(\ell/j)
for
j\geq 1

[
Tre08
]
. However, it is a much stronger bound: if all we knew was that the Chebyshev coefficients are bounded by
O(\ell/j)
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(jc_{j})^{2}
could be unbounded, whereas we give a bound of
O(\ell^{2})
. Informally, the implication is that not all coefficients can saturate the “local”
O(\ell/j)
constraint at the same time, but rather obey a stronger global constraint, captured by a weighted
\ell_{2}
norm of the coefficients.

3.2
Efficient recovery

The primary value of
Theorem

1
for our applications is that, given sufficiently accurate estimates,
\hat{m}_{1},\ldots,\hat{m}_{k}
, of
p
’s Chebyshev moments, we can recover a distribution
q
that is close in Wasserstein-
1

\hat{m}_{1},\ldots,\hat{m}_{k}

This claim is formalized in

Recall the condition of the corollary, that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\leq\Gamma^{2}
. Now, suppose we could solve the optimization problem:

\displaystyle q^{*}=\argmin_{\text{distributions $q$ on }[-1,1]}\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle q,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}.

Then by triangle inequality we would have:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\langle q^{*},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle q^{*},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq 2\Gamma.

(9)

It then follows immediately from

The only catch with the argument above is that we cannot efficiently optimize over the entire set of distributions on

. Instead, we have to optimize over a sufficiently fine discretization. Specifically, we consider discrete distributions on a finite grid, choosing the Chebyshev nodes (of the first kind) instead of a uniform grid because doing so yields a better approximation, and thus allows for a coarser grid. Concretely,

3.3
Proof of Lemma
13

We conclude this section by proving
Lemma

13
, our global decay bound on the Chebyshev coefficients of a smooth, Lipschitz function, which was key in the proof of
Theorem

1
. To do so we will leverage an expression for the derivatives of the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind in terms of the Chebyshev polynomials
of the second kind
, which can be defined by the recurrence

\displaystyle U_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle U_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=2x

\displaystyle U_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=2xU_{i-1}(x)-U_{i-2}(x),\text{ for $i\geq 2$}.

We have the following standard facts (see e.g.,
[
Riv69
]
).

Fact 14

(Chebyshev Polynomial Derivatives)
.

Let
T_{j}
be the
j^{th}
Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind, and
U_{j}
be the
j^{th}
Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. Then, for
j\geq 1
,
T_{j}^{\prime}(x)=jU_{j-1}(x)
.

Fact 15

(Orthogonality of Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind)
.

The Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind are orthogonal with respect to the weight function
u(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
. In particular,

\int_{-1}^{1}U_{i}(x)U_{j}(x)u(x)\,dx=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ for }i\neq j\\
\frac{\pi}{2},&\text{ for }i=j\,.\end{cases}

With the above facts we can now prove
Lemma

13
.

Proof of
Lemma

13
.

Let
f
be a smooth,
\ell
-Lipschitz function, with Chebyshev expansion
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\bar{T}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}c_{0}T_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}c_{j}T_{j}
. Using
Fact

14
, we can write
f
’s derivative as:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}c_{j}T_{j}^{\prime}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jc_{j}U_{j-1}(x)\,.

By the orthogonality property of
Fact

15
, we then have that

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f^{\prime}(x)f^{\prime}(x)u(x)\,dx=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2}c_{j}^{2}\frac{\pi}{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2}c_{j}^{2}.

Further, using that
f
is
\ell
-Lipschitz and so
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq\ell
, and that the weight function
u(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
is non-negative, we can upper bound this sum by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2}c_{j}^{2}=\int_{-1}^{1}f^{\prime}(x)f^{\prime}(x)u(x)\,dx\leq\ell^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}u(x)\,dx=\frac{\pi\ell^{2}}{2}.

This completes the proof of the lemma. We remark that this bound cannot be improved, as it holds with equality for the function
f(x)=x
.

Spectral Density Estimation

In this section, we present a second application of our main result to the linear algebraic problem of Spectral Density Estimation (SDE). We recall the setting from

p

W_{1}(p,q)\leq\epsilon\|A\|_{2}.

(14)

In many settings of interest,

is implicit and can only be accessed via matrix-vector multiplications. So, we want to understand 1) how many matrix-vector multiplications with
A
are required to achieve (
14
), and 2) how efficiently can we achieve (
14
) in terms of standard computational complexity.

We show how to obtain improved answers to these questions by using our main result,
Theorem

1
, to give a tighter analysis of an approach from
[
BKM22
]
. Like other SDE methods, that approach uses

to estimate the Chebyshev moments of

. In particular, let
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
denote the first
k

. Then we have for each

\displaystyle m_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(\lambda_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(T_{j}(A)),

\displaystyle T_{0}(A)g

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle T_{1}(A)g

\displaystyle=Ag

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}f(x)g(x){\sf d}x\,.

Fact 28

(Orthogonality Property of Chebyshev Polynomials in
d
-Dimensions)
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
. Let
\mathrm{nnz}(K)
denote the number of non-zero entries in
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
. The higher dimensional Chebyshev polynomials satisfy the following orthogonality property:

\left\langle T_{K_{1}},W\cdot T_{K_{2}}\right\rangle=\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}T_{K_{1}}(x)T_{K_{2}}(x)\mathsf{W}(x){\sf d}x=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }K_{1}\neq K_{2}\\
\frac{\pi^{d}}{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K_{1})}}&\text{if }K_{1}=K_{2}\end{cases}\,.

Definition 29

(Normalized
d
-Dimensional Chebyshev Polynomials)
.

The normalized
d
-dimensional Chebyshev polynomial
T_{K}^{d}(x)
, for
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
is defined as:

\bar{T}_{K}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{T_{K}(x)}{\sqrt{T_{K},W\cdot T_{K}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{\pi^{d}}}T_{K}(x)\,.

With the notations and definitions in place, we can now state the multivariate Jackson’s theorem. The following theorem shows that the damped, truncated Chebyshev series of a smooth function is a good uniform multivariate polynomial approximation to the function.

Theorem 30

(Multivariate Jackson’s Theorem)
.

Let
f:[-1,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
\ell
-Lipschitz smooth function, and for
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{d}
, let
c_{K}=\left\langle f,W\cdot\bar{T}_{K}\right\rangle
. Then the polynomial
\tilde{f}(x)=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,2m-2\right\}^{d}}\tilde{c}_{K}T_{K}(x)
satisfies that

\left\lVert\tilde{f}-f\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{9\ell d}{m},\text{ and }\left\lvert\tilde{c}_{K}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert c_{K}\right\rvert,\text{ for }K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}\,.

We now prove the theorem in
Section

A.3
. With the high dimensional Jackson’s theorem in place, we now prove the multivariate global Chebyshev coefficient decay lemma.

Lemma 31

(Multivariate Global Chebyshev Coefficient Decay)
.

Let
f:[-1,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth,
\ell
-Lipschitz function. For
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, let
c_{K}=\left\langle f,W\cdot\bar{T}_{K}\right\rangle
. Then, we have that

\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert^{2}_{2}c_{K}^{2}\leq d\ell^{2}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2}\,.

Proof.

Let
f:[-1,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth,
\ell
-Lipschitz function, with Chebyshev series

f(x)=\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\mathsf{C}_{K}T_{K}(x)\,.

Let
K=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
. Since
f
is
\ell
-Lipschitz, it follows that
\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\ell^{2}
. Consequently, for
i\in[d]
, we have:

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}(x^{\prime})\leq\ell^{2}\text{ at any }x^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{d}\,.

Therefore, we get that for
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[-1,1]^{d}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}{\prod_{j\neq i\in[d]}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}{\sf d}x\leq d\ell^{2}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2}\,.

(25)

The upper bound follows from the fact that
0\leq\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq\ell^{2}
,
\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}=\pi/2
, and that
\int_{-1}^{1}1/\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}=\pi
.
We multiply
\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}
by
\frac{\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}{\prod_{j\neq i\in[d]}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}
and integrate from
[-1,1]^{d}
to exploit the orthogonality property of the Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kind.

We now evaluate the LHS of
Equation

25
. Computing the gradient, we have from
Fact

14
that for
i\in[d]
:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1},k_{i+1}\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}k_{i}C_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i},\dots,k_{d})}\prod_{j\neq i\in[d]}T_{k_{j}}(x_{j})U_{k_{i}-1}(x_{i})\,.

We consider the square of the above expression. The orthogonality property of Chebyshev polynomials ensures that only the squared terms contribute non-zero values to the integral in
Equation

25
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{i}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i-1},k_{i+1}\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d-1}}k_{i}^{2}C^{2}_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{i},\dots,k_{d})}\prod_{j\neq i\in[d]}(T_{k_{j}}(x_{j}))^{2}(U_{k_{i}-1}(x_{i}))^{2}\,.

(26)

Using the above equations, we evaluate the LHS of
Equation

25
and get that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}{\prod_{j\neq i\in[d]}\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}}{\sf d}x=\sum_{K=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{C}_{K}^{2}\penalty\ ,

by using the orthogonality property of Chebyshev polynomials of the first and second kind and by inspecting the
Equation

26
.
Therefore, combining above with
Equation

25
, we get that

\sum_{K=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{C}_{K}^{2}\leq d\ell^{2}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2}

Note that these coefficients are not normalized. To get the normalized Chebyshev coefficients, we use the fact that
\bar{T}_{K}=\sqrt{\frac{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{\pi^{d}}}T_{K}
. We let
f(x)=\sum_{K\in N^{d}}c_{K}\bar{T}_{K}(x)
, which yields

\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert^{2}c_{K}^{2}\leq d\ell^{2}\frac{\pi^{d}}{2}\,.\qed

A.2

Theorem

With the multivariate Jackson’s theorem and the global Chebyshev coefficient decay lemma in place, we can now prove the multivariate version of our main theorem.

Theorem 32
.

Let

be distributions supported on

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\E_{x\sim p}\bar{T}_{K}(x)-\E_{x\sim q}\bar{T}_{K}(x)\right)^{2}\leq\Gamma^{2},

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,q)\leq\frac{cd}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\Gamma.

(28)

Proof.

By

25

, it suffices to bound
\langle{f,p-q\rangle}
for any
1
-Lipschitz, smooth

be the approximation to any such

\displaystyle\left\langle f,p-q\right\rangle=\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle+\left\langle f-f_{m},p-q\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle+\|f-f_{m}\|_{\infty}\|p-q\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle+\frac{36d}{m}.

(29)

In the last step, we use that
\|f-f_{m}\|_{\infty}\leq 18d/m
by

30

\|p-q\|_{1}\leq\|p\|_{1}+\|q\|_{1}=2
.
So, to bound
\left\langle f,p-q\right\rangle

\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle

For technical reasons, we will assume from here on that

and

are supported on the interval
[-1+\delta,1-\delta]^{d}

. This is to avoid an issue with the Chebyshev weight function

We proceed by writing the Chebyshev series of the function

\displaystyle\frac{p-q}{W}=\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}^{\infty}\left\langle\frac{p-q}{W}\cdot W,\bar{T}_{K}\right\rangle\bar{T}_{K}=\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle\cdot\bar{T}_{K}=\sum_{K\neq\bm{0},K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}^{\infty}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle\cdot\bar{T}_{K},

where
\bm{0}=(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

In the last step we use that both

Next, recall from

Using (

\langle{\bar{T}_{K}\cdot w,\bar{T}_{K^{\prime}}}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle=\left\langle f_{m}\cdot W,\frac{p-q}{W}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}}\tilde{c}_{K}\bar{T}_{K}\cdot W,\sum_{K\neq\bm{0},K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\bar{T}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\tilde{c}_{K}\cdot\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle.

Via Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and our high-dimensional global decay bound from

\displaystyle\left\langle f_{m},p-q\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\tilde{c}_{K}\cdot\frac{\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\tilde{c}_{K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}c_{K}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\left(\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\langle p-q,\bar{T}_{K}\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(31)

We can apply the assumption of the theorem, (

), to upper bound (

\Gamma

Let

Definition 34

(Periodic Function)

A function

Definition 35

Let

Definition 36

(Fourier Series)

Let

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\hat{f}(K)e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\left\langle k,x\right\rangle},\text{ where }\hat{f}(K)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}f(x)e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\left\langle K,x\right\rangle}{\sf d}x\,,

Claim 37

Let

h(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=[f*g](x)=\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}f(u)g(x-u){\sf d}u\,.

The Fourier coefficients of

\left\{\hat{h}(K)\right\}_{K\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}

\hat{h}(K)=(2\pi)^{d}\cdot\hat{f}(K)\hat{g}(K)\,.

Definition 38

h(\Theta)=g(-\left\lvert\theta_{1}\right\rvert,\dots,-\left\lvert\theta_{d}\right\rvert)
for
\Theta\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
. The function
h:[\pi,\pi]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a periodic and even function (
Definition

35
). Since the function is even, one can check that its Fourier series can be written as follows: For
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
,

h(\Theta)=\sum_{K=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}}\alpha_{K}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i}\theta_{i})\,,

where:

\alpha_{K}=\frac{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}h(\Theta)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i}\theta_{i})\right){\sf d}\Theta=\frac{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{\pi^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,0]^{d}}g(\Theta){\prod_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i}\theta_{i})}{\sf d}\Theta\,.

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[-1,1]^{d}
. Using that fact that for
i\in[d]
,
\frac{{\sf d}}{{\sf d}x_{i}}\cos^{-1}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}}
, we get

\int_{[-\pi,0]^{d}}g(\Theta){\prod_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i}\theta_{i})}{\sf d}\Theta=\int_{[-1,1]^{d}}f(x)T_{K}(x)W(x){\sf d}x\,.

Using the above equations, we conclude that for
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, the Fourier coefficients of
h
are just a scaling depending on
K
of the Chebyshev coefficients of
f
, and we get by
Definition

29
that:

\sqrt{\frac{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{\pi^{d}}}c_{K}=\alpha_{K}\,.

(33)

We observe that the mapping from
f
to
h
preserves the
\ell
-Lipschitz property. The function
h:[\pi,\pi]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is periodic and an even function (
Definition

35
), and is
\ell
-Lipschitz. Let
\tilde{h}
be the function obtained by applying Jackson’s theorem (
Theorem

40
) to
h
. We know that
\tilde{h}
is an even function since
h
is even and Jackson’s Kernel
b
, which
h
is convolved with, is also even. Recall that the Fourier series coefficients of
\tilde{h}
, denoted by
\hat{\tilde{h}}(K)
, are
0
for
K\not\in\left\{0,\dots,2m-2\right\}^{d}
. Finally, let
\tilde{f}:[-1,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as

\tilde{f}(\cos\theta_{1},\dots,\cos\theta_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tilde{h}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})\,.

By
Equation

33
, we get that the Chebyshev coefficients of
\tilde{f}
are exactly
\frac{\hat{b}(K)}{\hat{b}(\bm{0})}c_{K}
. Note from
Theorem

40
that
\left\lvert\frac{\hat{b}(K)}{\hat{b}(\bm{0})}\right\rvert\leq 1
, therefore, we get the
\tilde{f}
is the damped Chebyshev truncated series of
f
.

Moreover, we have that
\left\lVert\tilde{f}-f\right\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert\tilde{h}-h\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{9\ell d}{m}
, where the inequality follows from
Theorem

40
. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Differentially Private Synthetic Data Generation in Higher Dimensions

Algorithm 3

d
-Dimension Private Chebyshev Moment Matching

1:

Dataset
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in[-1,1]^{d}
, privacy parameters
\epsilon,\delta>0
.

2:

A probability distribution
q
approximating the distribution,
p\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\text{Unif}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}
.

3:

Let
\mathcal{G}=\left\{-1,-1+\frac{1}{\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil},-1+\frac{2}{\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil},\ldots,1\right\}^{d}
. Let
r\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(2\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil+1)
and for
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{d})\in[r]^{d}
let
{g}_{J}=\left(-1+\frac{j_{1}-1}{\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil},\dots,-1+\frac{j_{d}-1}{\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil}\right)
denote the
J^{\text{th}}
element of
\mathcal{G}
.

4:

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{G}}|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\bm{y}|
. I.e., round
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to the nearest point in the grid
\mathcal{G}
.

5:

Set
\sigma^{2}=\frac{4\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}}\mathsf{S}\ln(1.25/\delta)/(n^{2}\epsilon^{2})
, where
\mathsf{S}=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}
. See
Lemma

43
for the bound on
\mathsf{S}
.

6:

Set
m=\lceil 2(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil
. For
K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}
, let
\hat{m}_{K}=\eta_{K}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{T}_{K}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})
, where
\eta_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\sigma^{2})
.

7:

Let
\left\{{q}_{J}\right\}_{J\in[r]^{d}}
be the solution to the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\min_{\left\{{z}_{J}\right\}_{J\in[r]^{d}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{K}-\sum_{J\in[r]^{d}}{z}_{J}\bar{T}_{K}(g_{J})\right)^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\sum_{J\in[r]^{d}}z_{J}=1\text{ and }z_{J}\geq 0,\,\,\forall J\in[r]^{d}.\end{array}

8:

Return
{q}=\sum_{J\in[r]^{d}}{q}_{J}\delta(x-g_{J})
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function.

Theorem 41
.

Let
X=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
be a dataset with each
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in[-1,1]^{d}
, for
d\geq 2
. Let
p
be the uniform distribution on
X
. For any
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, there is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private algorithm based on Chebyshev moment matching that, in
{\sf poly}\left(n,\epsilon,\delta,2^{d}\right)
time, returns a distribution
q
satisfying for a fixed constant
c_{4}
,

\displaystyle\E[W_{1}(p,q)]\leq c_{4}d\left(\frac{1+\ln^{0.5}(1.25/\delta)}{n\epsilon}\right)^{1/d}\,.

Proof.

We analyze both the privacy and accuracy of
Algorithm

3
.

Privacy.

For a dataset
X=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}\in[-1,1]^{d\times n}
, where each data-point
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in[-1,1]^{d}
. Let
f(X)
be a vector-valued function mapping to the
K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}

scaled
Chebyshev moments of the uniform distribution over
X
. I.e.,

\displaystyle f(X)_{K}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{T}_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\,,

where
f(X)_{K}
denotes the
K
-th entry of the vector
f(X)
, and
\bar{T}_{K}(\mathbf{x})
is the
K
-th normalized multivariate Chebyshev polynomial.

We will show that
Algorithm

3
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private and that the output of the algorithm is close in Wasserstein distance to the true moments of the uniform distribution over
X
.
By
Definition

29
,
\max_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in[-1,1]^{d}}|\bar{T}_{K}(x_{i})|\leq\sqrt{2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}/\pi^{d}}
for
K\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, so we have:

\displaystyle\Delta_{2,f}^{2}=\underset{X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}{\max_{\text{neighboring datasets}}}\|f(X)-f(X^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\frac{4\cdot 2^{\mathrm{nnz}(K)}}{\pi^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}n^{2}}\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}n^{2}}\mathsf{S}\,,

(34)

where
\mathsf{S}=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}
.
For two neighboring datasets
X,X^{\prime}
, let
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
be the rounded datasets computed in line 2 of
Algorithm

3
– i.e.,
\tilde{X}=\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\}
. Observe that
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
are also neighboring. Thus, it follows from
Fact

17
and the sensitivity bound of
Equation

34
that
\tilde{m}_{K}=f(\tilde{X})_{K}+\eta_{K}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private for
\eta_{K}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
as long as
\sigma^{2}=\frac{4\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}}\mathsf{S}\ln(1.25/\delta)/(n^{2}\epsilon^{2})
. Finally, observe that
\hat{m}_{K}
computed by
Algorithm

3
is exactly equal to
\sqrt{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}
times the
K^{\text{th}}
entry of such an
\tilde{m}
. So
\left\{\hat{m}_{K}\right\}_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m^{d}\right\}\setminus\bm{0}}
are
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private. Since the remainder of
Algorithm

2
simply post-processes
\left\{\hat{m}_{K}\right\}_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m^{d}\right\}\setminus\bm{0}}
without returning to the original data
X
, the output of the algorithm is also
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private, as desired.

Accuracy.

The
Algorithm

3
begins by rounding the dataset
X
so that every coordinate of every data point is a multiple of
1/\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil
. Let
\tilde{p}
be the uniform distribution over the rounded dataset
\tilde{X}
. Then, it is not hard to see from the transportation definition of the Wasserstein-
1
distance that:

W_{1}(p,\tilde{p})\leq\frac{d}{2\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil}\,.

(35)

In particular, we can transport
p
to
\tilde{p}
by moving every unit of
1/n
probability mass a distance of at most
1/2\lceil(\epsilon n)^{1/d}\rceil
, along each of the
d
coordinates. Given
eq.

35
, it will suffice to show that
Algorithm

3
returns a distribution
q
that is close in Wasserstein distance to
\tilde{p}
. We will apply triangle inequality to bound
W_{1}(p,q)
.

To show that
Algorithm

2
returns a distribution
q
that is close to
\tilde{p}
in Wasserstein distance, we begin by bounding the moment estimation error:

\displaystyle E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{K}(\tilde{p})-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle\right)^{2},

where
m
is as chosen in
Algorithm

3
and
\langle\tilde{p},T_{K}\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{T}_{K}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})
. Let
\sigma^{2}
and
\left\{\eta_{K}\right\}_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}
be as in
Algorithm

3
. Applying linearity of expectation, we have that:

\displaystyle\E[E]=\E\left[\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\eta_{K}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\E\left[\eta_{K}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\cdot\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}\mathsf{S}\,,

(36)

where we recall that
\mathsf{S}=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}
.

Now, let
q
be as in
Algorithm

3
. Using a triangle inequality argument as in
Section

3.2
, we have:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\langle{q},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\langle{q},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}+\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}\leq 2E.

Above we use that
\tilde{p}
is a feasible solution to the optimization problem solved in
Algorithm

3
and, since
q
is the optimum,
\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\left(\langle{q},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\left(\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{K}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}
.
It follows that
\E[\Gamma^{2}]\leq 2\E[E]
, and, via Jensen’s inequality, that
\E[\Gamma]\leq\sqrt{2\E[E]}
. Plugging into
Theorem

32
, we have for constant
c
:

\displaystyle\E[W_{1}(\tilde{p},q)]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\E[\Gamma]+\frac{cd}{m}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\sqrt{2\mathsf{S}\sigma^{2}}+\frac{cd}{m}\,.

(37)

From the bound on
\sigma^{2}
computed above, and from the upper bound on
\mathsf{S}
in
Lemma

43
, we get that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\sqrt{2\mathsf{S}\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{8\ln(1.25/\delta)\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}}}\cdot\frac{S}{n\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\pi^{d}}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{8\ln(1.25/\delta)\cdot 2^{d}}{\pi^{d}}}\cdot\frac{4(\pi e)^{d/2}}{2^{d}}\cdot\frac{m^{d-1}}{dn\epsilon}

\displaystyle=8\sqrt{\ln(1.25/\delta)}(\pi e/2)^{d/2}\cdot\frac{m^{d-1}}{\sqrt{d}n\epsilon}\,.

Therefore, for some absolute constant
c_{2}
, setting
m=c_{2}\left(\frac{dn\epsilon}{\ln^{0.5}(1.25/\delta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}
, we get from
37
,

\displaystyle\E[W_{1}(\tilde{p},q)]

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}\cdot d\cdot\left(\frac{\ln^{0.5}(1.25/\delta)}{n\epsilon}\right)^{1/d}\,,

for an absolute constant
c_{3}
. By triangle inequality
W_{1}(p,q)\leq W_{1}(p,\tilde{p})+W_{1}(\tilde{p},q)
and using the bound on
W_{1}(p,\tilde{p})
in
Equation

35
, we get that for an absolute constant
c_{4}
,

\E[W_{1}(p,q)]\leq c_{4}d\left(\frac{1+\ln^{0.5}(1.25/\delta)}{n\epsilon}\right)^{1/d}\,.

Runtime.

The number of points in the grid in
Algorithm

3
is upper bounded by
\left\lvert\mathcal{G}\right\rvert=(1+2\lceil\epsilon n\rceil^{1/d})^{d}=O\!\left(2^{d}\lceil n\epsilon\rceil^{1/d}\right)
. The number of Chebyshev moments we calculate is less than
(m+1)^{d}=O\!\left(2^{d}\lceil(dn\epsilon)\rceil^{1/d}\right)
. Since the optimization problem runs in polynomial time in its variables and constraints, we get that the running time of the algorithm is bounded by
{\sf poly}\left(n,\epsilon,\delta,2^{d}\right)
.
∎

Remark 42

(Comparison to
[
BSV24
]
)
.

We remark that
[
BSV24
]
use the
\ell_{\infty}
metric instead of the
\ell_{2}
metric for the Wasserstein-
1
distance, and they achieve an error of
\E[W_{1}(p,q)]\leq O\!\left(\frac{\log^{1.5}(\epsilon n)}{\epsilon n}\right)^{1/d}
. Since the Wasserstein-
1
distance in the
\ell_{\infty}
metric is bounded by
1
, their bound is non-vacuous for
d=O\!\left(\log n\right)
. For
d=O\!\left(\log n\right)
, our bound matches their bound to
\log(n)
-factors.

Finally, we show how to upper bound
\mathsf{S}
.

Lemma 43
.

Let
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and
d\geq 2
. Then, we have that

\mathsf{S}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\frac{4(\pi e)^{d/2}}{2^{d}}\cdot\frac{m^{d-1}}{d}\,.

Proof.

Note that the function
\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}
is decreasing in
\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}
. Moreover, for
K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}
,
\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\geq 1
. Thus, we can upper bound the sum by the integral as follows:

\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\leq d+\int_{K\in[0,m]^{d},\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\geq 1}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}{\sf d}K\,.

Using the fact that for
K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}
,
\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\leq m\sqrt{d}
, and that the
m\sqrt{d}
ball contains the hypercube
[0,m]^{d}
, we get that

\int_{K\in[0,m]^{d},\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\geq 1}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}{\sf d}K\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\int_{1\leq\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\leq m\sqrt{d}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}{\sf d}K\,,

where the factor of
1/2^{d}
comes due to symmetry and the fact that the set
K\in[0,m]^{d}
, i.e., the vectors in
K
contains non-negative entries, which is only
1/2^{d}
fraction of vectors in the set
\left\{K:1\leq\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\leq m\sqrt{d}\right\}
. To compute the integral, we can transition to polar coordinates. Let
\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}=r
, then we get that

\int_{1\leq\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}\leq m\sqrt{d}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}{\sf d}K=\int_{r=1}^{m\sqrt{d}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{r}\cdot r^{d-1}{\sf d}\Omega{\sf d}r\,,

where
\Omega
is the
angular domain
in spherical coordinates. Separating the terms in the integral gives,

\int_{r=1}^{m\sqrt{d}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{r}\cdot r^{d-1}{\sf d}\Omega{\sf d}r=\int_{r=1}^{m\sqrt{d}}r^{d-2}{\sf d}r\int_{\Omega}{\sf d}\Omega\,.

Using the fact that
\int_{\Omega}{\sf d}\Omega
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
d
-dimensions, we can use the equation for the surface area of the unit sphere, see e.g.
[
BV04
]
, to evaluate the integral as

\int_{r=1}^{m\sqrt{d}}r^{d-2}{\sf d}r\cdot\int_{\Omega}{\sf d}\Omega=\frac{(m\sqrt{d})^{d-1}-1}{d-1}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\,,

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Combining all the terms, we get that

\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\leq d+\frac{1}{2^{d}}\cdot\frac{(m\sqrt{d})^{d-1}-1}{d-1}\cdot\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\,.

By Stirling’s approximation
\Gamma(d/2)\geq\sqrt{2}(d/2)^{d/2-1/2}e^{-d/2}
, and observing that the second term dominates in the RHS above, we have

\sum_{K\in\left\{0,\dots,m\right\}^{d}\setminus\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\left\lVert K\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\frac{4(\pi e)^{d/2}}{2^{d}}\cdot\frac{m^{d-1}}{d}\,.\qed

Appendix C
Accuracy of Generic Moment Matching Algorithm

In this section, we give the full proof of
Corollary

2
, which establishes the accuracy of the generic Chebyshev moment regression algorithm (
Algorithm

1
). We require the following basic property about the Chebyshev nodes:

Lemma 44

(Chebyshev Node Approximation)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{g}\right\}
be the degree
g
Chebyshev nodes. I.e.,
x_{i}=\cos\left(\frac{2i-1}{2g}\pi\right)
. Let
r_{\mathcal{C}}:[-1,1]\to\mathcal{C}
be
a function that maps a point
x\in[-1,1]
to the point
y\in\mathcal{C}
that minimizes
\left\lvert\cos^{-1}(x)-\cos^{-1}(y)\right\rvert
, breaking ties arbitrarily. For any
x\in[-1,1]
,
\left\lvert\cos^{-1}(x)-\cos^{-1}(r_{\mathcal{C}}(x))\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi}{2g}
.

Proof.

For any two consecutive points
x_{i},x_{i+1}
in the
\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\cos^{-1}(x_{i})-\cos^{-1}(x_{i+1})\right\rvert=\frac{\pi}{g}.

Since
\cos^{-1}(x)
is non-increasing, for any
x\in[x_{i+1},x_{i}]
,
\cos^{-1}(x)\in[\cos^{-1}(x_{i}),\cos^{-1}(x_{i+1})]
. So,
\cos^{-1}(x)
has distance at most
\frac{\pi}{2g}
from either
\cos^{-1}(x_{i})
or
\cos^{-1}(x_{i+1})
. Additionally, we can check that
\left\lvert\cos^{-1}(x)-\cos^{-1}(x_{1})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi}{2g}
for any
x<x_{1}
and
\left\lvert\cos^{-1}(x)-\cos^{-1}(x_{g})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\pi}{2g}
for any
x>x_{g}
.
∎

With
Lemma

44
in place, we are ready to prove
Corollary

2
.

Proof of
Corollary

2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
r_{\mathcal{C}}:[-1,1]\to\mathcal{C}
be as in
Lemma

44
. For
i\in\left\{1,\dots,g\right\}
, let
Y_{i}
be the set of points in
[-1,1]
that are closest to
x_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
Y_{i}=\left\{x\in[-1,1]:r_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=x_{i}\right\}
. Let
\tilde{p}
be a distribution supported on the set
\mathcal{C}
with mass
\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx
on
x_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
. For all
j\in 1,\ldots,k
we have:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{g}\int_{Y_{i}}\bar{T}_{j}(x)p(x)\,dx-\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\bar{T}_{j}(x_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\bar{T}_{j}(y_{i})-\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\bar{T}_{j}(x_{i})\right\rvert\qquad\left(\text{for some $y_{i}\in Y_{i}$}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\left\lvert\bar{T}_{j}(y_{i})-\bar{T}_{j}(x_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\left\lvert\cos(j\cos^{-1}(y_{i}))-\cos(j\cos^{-1}(x_{i}))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{g}\left(\int_{Y_{i}}p(x)\,dx\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\frac{j\pi}{2g}=\frac{j\sqrt{\pi/2}}{g}

(38)

The second equality follows from the intermediate value theorem. The first inequality follows by triangle inequality. The third equality follows by the trigonometric definition of the (normalized) Chebyshev polynomials. The second inequality follows from
Lemma

44
and the fact that the derivative of
\cos(jx)
is bounded by
j
. The bound in (
38
) then yields:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi k/2}}{g}.

(39)

Observe also that, since
\tilde{p}
is supported on
\mathcal{C}
, it is a valid solution to the optimization problem solved by
Algorithm

1
. Accordingly, we have that:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle{q},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(40)

Applying triangle inequality, followed by (
40
), triangle inequality again, and finally (
39
), we have:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\langle{q},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\hskip-6.99997pt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle{q},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle p,\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\hat{m}_{j}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\langle{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle-\langle\tilde{p},\bar{T}_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\Gamma+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi k}}{g}.

Setting
g=\lceil k^{1.5}\rceil
, we can apply
Theorem

1
to conclude that, for a fixed constant
c^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,q)\leq\frac{c}{k}+2\Gamma+\frac{\sqrt{\pi/2}}{k}\leq c^{\prime}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{k}+\Gamma\right).

Appendix D
High Probability Bound for Private Synthetic Data

In this section, we prove the high probability bound on Wasserstein distance stated in
Theorem

4
, which follows from a standard concentration bound for sub-exponential random variables
[
Wai19
]
. We recall that a random variable
X
is subexponential with parameters
(\nu,\alpha)
if:

\displaystyle\E[e^{\lambda(X-\E[X])}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\nu^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}

for all

\displaystyle|\lambda|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}.

We require the following well-known fact that a chi-square random variable with one degree of freedom is subexponential:

Fact 45

(Sub-Exponential Parameters
[
Wai19
, Example 2.8]
)
.

Let
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Then,
\eta^{2}
is sub-exponential random variable with parameters
(2\sigma^{2},4\sigma^{2})
.

We also require the following concentration inequality for a sum of sub-exponential random variable:

Fact 46

(
[
Wai19
, Equation 2.18]
)
.

Consider independent random variables
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}
, where,
\forall j\in 1,\ldots,k
,
\gamma_{j}
is sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu_{j},\alpha_{j})
. Let
\nu_{*}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}^{2}}
and
\alpha_{*}=\max\left\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\right\}
. Then we have:

\ProbOp\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\gamma_{j}-\E[\gamma_{j}]\right)\geq t\right]\leq\begin{cases}\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\nu_{*}^{2}}\right)&\text{ for }0\leq t\leq\frac{\nu_{*}^{2}}{\alpha_{*}}\,,\\
\exp\left(\frac{-t}{2\alpha_{*}}\right)&\text{ for }t>\frac{\nu_{*}^{2}}{\alpha_{*}}\,.\end{cases}

Proof of high-probability bound of
Theorem

4
.

Recalling the proof of the expectation bound of
Theorem

4
from
Section

4
, it suffices to bound
E=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(\hat{m}_{j}(p)-\langle\tilde{p},T_{j}\rangle\right)^{2}
with high probability.
Let
\gamma_{j}=\eta_{j}^{2}/j^{2}
, where
\eta_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,j\sigma^{2})
is as in
Algorithm

2
. Then recall that
E=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma_{j}
.

From
Fact

45
,
\gamma_{j}
is a sub-exponential random variable with parameter
\left(2\sigma^{2}/j,4\sigma^{2}/j\right)
. We can then apply
Fact

46
, for which we have
\nu_{*}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{{k}}{4\sigma^{4}}/{j^{2}}}\leq{2\pi\sigma^{2}}/{\sqrt{6}}
and
\alpha_{*}=4\sigma^{2}
. For any failure probability
\beta\in(0,1/2)
, setting
t=8\log(1/\beta)\sigma^{2}
, we conclude that:

\displaystyle\ProbOp\left[E-\E[E]\geq 8\log\left({1}/{\beta}\right)\sigma^{2}\right]\leq\beta\,.

Recalling from

that
\E[E]\leq(1+\log k)\sigma^{2}

The rest of the details follow as before. In particular, as in

, we can bound:

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,q)\leq\sqrt{2}\Gamma+\frac{36}{k}+\frac{1}{2\lceil\epsilon n\rceil},

where

Plugging in

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,q)\leq c\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(\epsilon n)+\log(1/\beta)}\sqrt{\log(\epsilon n)\log({1/\delta})}}{\epsilon n}\right)\,.

Appendix E
Spectral Density Estimation Lower Bound

In this section, we provide a lower bound on the number of matrix-vector multiplications required for spectral density estimation, showing that our upper bound in
Theorem

5
is optimal up to logarithmic factors. We first need the following theorem from
[
Mey24
]
, which shows that estimating the trace of a positive semi-definite matrix
A
to within a multiplicative error of
(1\pm\epsilon)
requires
\Omega(1/\epsilon)
matrix-vector multiplications with
A
.

Theorem 47

(Restated
[
Mey24
, Theorem 17]
, see also
[
SW23
, Theorem 17]
)
.

Any algorithm that is given matrix-vector multiplication access to a positive semi-definite (PSD) input matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
,
n/4\leq\mathrm{tr}(A)\leq n
and succeeds with probability at least
2/3
in outputting an estimate
\tilde{t}
such that
\left\lvert\tilde{t}-\mathrm{tr}(A)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{tr}(A)
requires
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
matrix-vector multiplications with
A
.

As a corollary of this result, we obtain the following lower bound, which shows that
Theorem

5
is tight up to
\log(1/\epsilon)
factors:

Corollary 48
.

Any algorithm that is given matrix-vector multiplication access to a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with spectral density
p
and
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
requires
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
matrix-vector multiplications with
A
to output a distribution
q
such that
W_{1}(p,q)\leq\epsilon
with probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

The proof is via a direct reduction.
Consider a PSD matrix
A
with
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1
,
n/4\leq\mathrm{tr}(A)\leq n
, and spectral density
p
. Suppose we have a spectral density estimate
q
of
p
such that
W_{1}(p,q)\leq\epsilon/4
. We claim that
\tilde{t}=n\cdot\int_{-1}^{1}xq(x)\,dx
yields a relative error approximate to
A
’s trace, implying that computing such a
q
requires
\Omega({1}/{\epsilon})
matrix-vector products by
Theorem

47
.

In particular, applying Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality (
Fact

9
) with the
1
-Lipschitz functions
f(x)=x
and
f(x)=-x
, we have that:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}xp(x)\,dx-\int_{-1}^{1}xq(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/4

and

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}xq(x)\,dx-\int_{-1}^{1}xp(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/4.

(41)

We have that
\int_{-1}^{1}xp(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr}(A)
. So (
41
) implies that
\tilde{t}=n\cdot\int_{-1}^{1}xq(x)\,dx
satisfies:

\displaystyle|\tilde{t}-\mathrm{tr}(A)|\leq n\cdot\epsilon/4\leq\epsilon\cdot\mathrm{tr}(A).
