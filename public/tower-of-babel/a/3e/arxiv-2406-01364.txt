BELLS: A Framework Towards Future Proof Benchmarks for the Evaluation of LLM Safeguards
Diego Dorn, Alexandre Variengien, Charbel-Raphaël Segerie, Vincent Corruble

Abstract
Input-output safeguards are used to detect anomalies in the traces produced by Large Language Models (LLMs) systems. These detectors are at the core of diverse safety-critical applications such as real-time monitoring, offline evaluation of traces, and content moderation. However, there is no widely recognized methodology to evaluate them. To fill this gap, we introduce the Benchmarks for the Evaluation of LLM Safeguards (BELLS), a structured collection of tests, organized in three categories: (1) established failure tests , based on already-existing benchmarks for well-defined failure modes, aiming to compare the performance of current input-output safeguards; (2) emerging failure tests , to measure generalization to never-seen-before failure modes and encourage the development of more general safeguards; (3) next-gen architecture tests , for more complex scaffolding (such as LLM-agents and multi-agent systems), aiming to foster the development of safeguards that could adapt to future applications for which no safeguard currently exists. Furthermore, we implement and share the first next-gen architecture test, using the MACHIAVELLI environment, along with an interactive visualization of the dataset.

Context

Figure 1:
The landscape of input-output safeguards systems. We show two neglected
axes of generality across the complexity of systems supervised (safeguard

outputs).

Developers of LLM-based applications compete for innovation and create
products of ever-increasing complexity and reach. While applications such as
ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, or agents such as Devin and AutoGPT
become more capable and are more
integrated with other systems, the number of ways those systems can fail
increases, and new failure modes are discovered after every release of a
new product. Previously observed failure modes include
harmful model behaviour, such as Bing Chat threatening users
(
Vincent 2023
)
and trying to manipulate them during normal conversation;
lack of robustness to attacks such as
universal adversarial attacks
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, which are specific strings that can generate objectionable behavior that generalizes across models and prompts;
indirect prompt injection
(
Greshake et al. 2023
)
, where attackers take control of an LLM through the output of tools.
There can also be unforeseen technical bugs, such as
ChatGPT glitch tokens
(
Rumbelow & Watkins 2023
)

which are tokens that ChatGPT could not repeat and made it produce incoherent responses or insult the user.

Such failures can generate damage ranging from reputational harm to model
providers, to systemic risks such as making dangerous knowledge available to
malicious actors and initiating society-scale value drift. However, damage can
also be of unforeseen nature, through emerging undesired behavior or yet unknown
means
(
Hendrycks et al. 2023
)
.

Input-output safeguards process traces of an LLM application (i.e. all inputs and outputs of its constituting LLMs)
before producing safety reports (

).
They take the form of real-time or periodic monitoring,
offline evaluation of traces, content moderation,
or any form of trace analysis.

Despite the rich ecosystem in the area of LLM evaluation,
little attention has been given to evaluating their safeguards.

2
Motivation: building metrics to foster the development of future-proof safeguards

Our core vision is to introduce benchmarks to guide the development of robust
safeguards that can act as an early detection system for risks of harm arising
from new behaviors, use cases, or attacks. Those include misalignment of goals
between humans and autonomous systems, advanced persuasion skills, or direct
manipulation of actions by an attacker. These safeguards would help us
address threats emerging in new LLM-based applications such as AI companions
that interact emotionally with users, AI assistants that perform real-world
actions to help with daily work and decision-making, and continuously learning
AI agents that evolve based on user interaction and data acquisition. However,
such safeguards should be thought as tools part of a comprehensive approach to
mitigate the harm from LLM systems, and alone can not be sufficient to ensure
LLM operations are safe.

Given the early nature of the field of input-output safeguards, we think the best
way forward is to include a diversity of possible damage and failure
modes instead of focusing on a few. The generalization abilities of
safeguards are crucial to limit societal harm, but also reputational
damage (e.g. detecting new kinds of jailbreaks). Addressing
well-defined, established problems, and proactively researching emerging
failure modes is key to providing fast feedback loops and defining
robust design principles grounded in today’s applications, to ensure
future-proof systems.

By building BELLS, a benchmark for LLM input-output safeguards, our goal is
threefold, as illustrated in
Figure 1
:

1.

Comparison of safeguards.

We want users and developers of LLM-based apps to be well aware
of the strengths and weaknesses of their security systems and enable them to choose
the best performing systems on the market.
Safeguards need to be evaluated by third parties, as
in-house metrics cannot form a solid basis for comparison and evaluation.

2.

Measure the ability of safeguards to generalize to new failure modes.

We want to provide a measure of how well a given safeguard can detect
unknown failure modes, for instance, detecting a
new kind of jailbreak
(
Anil et al. 2024
)
,
or something of an entirely different nature enabled by the
application, such as
in-context reward hacking
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
.
We hope that such a robust detection system could act as an early warning
to detect and study new sources of systemic risks, such as emergent
harmful use cases.

3.

Enable safeguards for future applications of a different type.

We want to foster the development of new kinds of safeguards
that can apply to future applications,
such as supervising autonomous LLM-based agents or multi-agent
systems.
There are, to our knowledge, no recognized methodologies for the detection of undesired behavior in
those kinds of systems yet.

3
State-of-the-art systems

3.1
Input-output safeguards

The current field of LLM safeguards is still very much rooted in the field
of automatic content moderation. Most systems focus on detecting the
presence of unauthorized content inside the text sent by a user, or in
the text generated by an LLM application.

Llama Guard

(
Inan et al. 2023
)
is a fine-tuned Llama-7b model trained to perform
multi-label classification to detect the presence of context categories
in interaction with an LLM chatbot, such as the presence of violent or hate speech, sexual content, content that could help people plan criminal activities, etc.

Lakera Guard

(
Lakera AI 2024a
)
is a proprietary classification system to detect
prompt injections, jailbreaks, and toxic speech inside free-form text.

OpenAI Moderation API

(
Markov et al. 2023
)
is a multi-headed transformer trained to
assess whether a free-form text contains content that is sexual, hateful, violent, or promotes
self-harm.
Similarly,
Perspective API

(
Lees et al. 2022
)
and

Azure AI Content Safety

(
Microsoft 2023
)

serve models classifying free-form text content on metrics such as toxicity, hate speech, self-harm, or sexual content.

3.2
Benchmarks for safeguards

Few benchmarks have been developed to assess the quality of
input-output safeguards, except for prompt injection detection.
Other benchmarks focus on the classification of
text content on metrics such as toxicity.

Prompt injections.
Benchmarks such as BIPIA
(
Yi et al. 2023
)
and
the PINT benchmark
(
Lakera AI 2024c
)
to detect prompt injections
have been made directly to test safeguards.

Unauthorised content in chat interaction.
Datasets such as
ToxicChat
(
Lin et al. 2023
)
and
the Open AI Moderation Dataset
(
Markov et al. 2023
)

contain respectively single rounds Q&A or free form text, with labels for
specific categories of unauthorized content (e.g. toxicity, hate speech).

Evaluation of LLM-specific failure modes.
For failure modes
specific to LLM applications, there are
collections of jailbreaks
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
,
datasets for goal hijacking and prompt leaking
(
Perez & Ribeiro 2022
)
,
sycophantic behavior
(
Perez et al. 2023
)
, and more.
However, these have been made to test model robustness, and were not tailored to
evaluate safeguards.

Proprietary datasets.
Moreover, the most complete benchmarks of
prompt injection are proprietary (e.g.
the Lakera dataset
(
Lakera AI 2024b
)
). However, given the
safety-critical nature of reliable safeguards,
their evaluation should be an open process. This is a necessary
condition for LLM application developers and users to trust the system they use.

4
Structure of BELLS

Figure 3:
The three types of tests in BELLS: established failures tests,
emerging failures tests, and next-gen architecture tests.

The safeguard problem can be seen as an anomaly detection problem
characterized by heavy class imbalance and a broad definition of what
constitutes an anomaly. The definition of anomaly cannot be fully
outlined in advance: new failure modes are discovered after deployment.

To achieve the three goals outlined in
section 2
, we propose to design a collection of
datasets organized into three categories:
established failure tests,
emerging failure tests,
and next-gen architecture tests.

Established failures tests
come from already existing datasets to detect
well-defined failure modes such as jailbreaks, and unauthorized content.
They come from two sources:

•

By
aggregation
of pre-existing benchmarks designed for
safeguards and monitoring systems, such as Toxic Chat and the OpenAI Content Moderation
Dataset.

•

By
transformation
of well-established benchmarks designed for
LLMs into benchmarks for input-output safeguards.
This corresponds to collecting traces on existing benchmarks
using multiple models and
multiple pre-prompts to induce different behaviors,
some being harmful and some being safe.

Emerging failure tests
are a collection of smaller tests on a
diversity of recent or emerging failure modes. They can be used as a
proxy to estimate how well a safeguard can catch problems safeguards
developers ignored the existence. Some of those tests could be kept
private, and be run by independent third parties to limit data
contamination and have a stronger measure of generalization to unknown
failure modes. Emerging failure tests are created using data augmentation on
examples of failures gathered from diverse sources, such as:

•

Scientific literature
. For instance, this could include
jailbreak from ASCII art
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
,
in context reward hacking
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
,
indirect prompt injection
(
Greshake et al. 2023
)
,
many-shot jailbreaks
(
Anil et al. 2024
)
,
etc.

•

Identifying new use cases in the wild
.
By following the methodology used in
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
, this would
include looking at online forums like

r/ChatGPT
to notice
new jailbreak techniques, new failure types,
or new use cases that enable new kinds of harm.

•

Reports on emerging vulnerabilities
such as
OWASP Top 10 for LLM
(
OWASP 2024
)
,
inspiring tests for LLM02 Insecure Output Handling, LLM06 Sensitive Information Disclosure,
and LLM08 Excessive Agency.

Next-gen architecture tests
aim to support the development of safeguards
targeting future, more complex systems, such as LLM-agents and
multi-agent systems. Currently, no detection system can find anomalies in a system
that combines a collection of API calls,
such as LLM-agents.
However, understanding the context of multiple calls can be necessary to
correctly classify the behaviors of LLM-agents.
Next-gen architecture tests come in a kit
containing:

•

A minimal application
. E.g. a minimal LLM-agent interacting
with an environment.

•

A dataset of anomalies in the application operation
by
forcing them (e.g. through prompting). We also collect a dataset of
non-anomalous traces from the normal operation of the application.

•

A minimal safeguard
to establish a baseline detection accuracy
and to show that creating safeguards for this failure mode is possible.
Minimal application-specific
detectors can be built using LLMs to inspect traces.

Ideally, these datasets should be kept up to date, new emerging failure
tests should be continuously added, and some would become part of the
established failure tests over time. Nonetheless, this project’s objective is
to initiate a discussion about the evaluation of LLM safeguards and
generate an inaugural batch of those datasets, which would already
constitute a remarkable leap forward in the evaluation of input-output safeguards.

5
Example of next-gen architecture tests: agent traces on the Machiavelli Benchmark

To illustrate the technical process, we present an example of a next-gen
architecture test, based on the
MACHIAVELLI benchmark
(
Pan et al. 2023
)
.

5.1
Presentation of the Machiavelli Benchmark

The Machiavelli benchmark
is a collection of 134
choose-your-own-adventure games, for which each of the 572 322 scenes
have been annotated with the strength of many ethical violations (e.g.
physical harm, trespassing, lying, etc.).
We sum all the ethical violations provided by the benchmark to get a

harm score
for each step.
Each step consists of a multi-paragraph textual description of the current scene
and a choice between pre-defined actions. A trajectory for one
scenario is on the order of a hundred steps.

Why choose the Machiavelli benchmark?

Machiavelli doesn’t aim
to capture a realistic setting in which an LLM application could
plausibly cause harm. Instead, we chose it as an experimental
environment to uncover the challenges that come with detecting undesired
behavior in LLM agents.

•

A sandbox to study agents.
Machiavelli is a collection of
environments that the current LLM can successfully navigate.
LLM agents are an active field of research
(
Xi et al. 2023
)

and are poised to bring
advances in a large diversity of domains. LLM agents can exhibit a
much greater diversity of failure modes, and none of the existing
tools (e.g. Lakera / Llama guard) can be easily repurposed to analyze
agents trace, as they were designed to analyze free-form text.
It is thus key to research agent-tailored safeguards before their
wide-spread deployment.
However, the existing open-source projects, such as AutoGPT

(
Significant-Gravitas 2024
)
, are still brittle and of limited use to navigate
complex environments.

•

Rich environments.
Machiavelli contains a large
diversity of environments written by humans that can be used to
investigate nuanced phenomena that are likely to arise in real-world
settings, like the tradeoff between ethical and performant action.

•

Simple structure.
Compared to other existing LLM agent
benchmarks such as
GAIA
(
Mialon et al. 2023
)
, and
Agent-Bench
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
,
the Machiavelli benchmark doesn’t require access to tools like
web search, or a terminal. This makes it a great starting point to
understand the singular challenges that come from an agentic LLM
system, before extending the scope to include tool use.

Generating the dataset of traces
is done by collecting traces
from two agents with different
steering prompts
integrated into the
system prompt. The first steering prompt encourages ethical action, the
other is pushing the model to do as many unethical actions as possible.
Since the games are long, our agent takes actions depending
only on the current scene, and not the previous ones. This generates a
sequence of API calls and their outcomes, each of the form {steering
prompt, scene details, agent thoughts, decision}. We then hide from
the trace the steering prompt that guides the LLM towards ethical or
unethical behavior.

5.2
Preliminary lessons building safeguards for agents

Challenges of safeguards for agents.
Through our preliminary
experiments with the Machiavelli benchmark, we highlight three
challenges for detecting anomalies in LLM agent traces:

•

Long-range dependencies
exist between API calls, for
instance, with the agent making a commitment at step #3 and
forgetting to honor it at step #30. This could not be detected only
by analyzing calls one by one, as there could be no information about
a previous commitment present in the text of call #30.

•

Counterfactual evaluations
are needed to evaluate ethical
behavior, as sometimes the available options are all harmful (or all beneficial), and we
want to know whether the agent took the best or worst one.

•

Diversity of scenarios.
The rich set of environments provided
in the benchmark requires the detector to understand a wide array of
situations, from understanding if an action is hurting allies or
enemies in the description of a fight, to tracking long-term
commitments.

LLMs such as
GPT-4 can be straightforwardly transformed into an accurate content moderator

(
Weng et al. 2023
)
.
Preliminary experiments show that this result doesn’t translate to supervising LLM-agent
action in complex settings such as the MACHIAVELLI scenarios.

We hope that our dataset can provide a challenging bar to foster innovation in
safeguards research tailored for agents navigating complex environments.

6
Discussions and future work

In this work, we proposed a structure for a comprehensive collection of tests
for input-output safeguards, including established failure tests, emerging
failure tests, and next-gen architecture tests. Our motivation is to address
both current and future failure modes.

We exemplified the next-gen architecture tests using the Machiavelli benchmark
to provide traces of agents exhibiting ethical or harmful behavior in a rich
text-based environment.

Future work includes developing the other tests in our proposed structure,
conducting rigorous evaluations of available safeguards, and measuring
robustness against adversaries attempting to bypass these safeguards. Expanding
the BELLS datasets and continuously incorporating new failure modes will be
critical to maintaining comprehensive coverage and accurate performance
assessments.

The evaluation provided by BELLS should not be viewed as an absolute assessment
of the safety of LLM applications using input-output safeguards. Indeed,
safeguards should be seen as tools within a systematic approach to LLM safety,
and not as a silver bullet to ensure LLM operations are safe.

Ultimately, BELLS aims to foster a collaborative effort among researchers and
practitioners to develop robust, future-proof safeguards that contribute to the
safe and ethical deployment of LLM applications.

Appendix A
Experiment: Baseline Detection for MACHIAVELLI

In addition to our benchmark, we publish a baseline detection mechanism, as a
sanity check showing it is possible to make progress on our benchmark.

In this experiment, we aim to detect unethical behavior in the traces of agents
evolving in the MACHIAVELLI environment. It is a binary classification problem,
where the target is to determine whether the model was instructed to follow
unethical behavior. We use the balanced dataset described in

subsection 5.1
but note that the classifier is
meant to be used in the context of anomaly detection, with high class imbalance
in production.

Metrics

For the evaluation of our anomaly detection model, we use the Area
Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) as our metric, following the
methodology outlined in
(
Markov et al. 2023
;
Inan et al. 2023
)
. This
metric is particularly suitable for applications with a large class imbalance.

Baseline

Our baseline detector compares an agent’s trajectory to the average harm done by 100 random agents in the same scenario. If the ratio of the two exceeds a learned threshold, the trajectory is classified as unethical.

More precisely, for a scenario
s
, we evaluate
n=100
trajectories of random agents. Each trajectory has length
l_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
and we denote

\big\{h_{t}^{s,i}\big\}_{1\leq t\leq l_{i}}
, the sum of all ethical violations reported by the Machiavelli benchmark, up to time step
t\in\{1,...,l_{i}\}
.
We compute the mean harm at time
t
, as follows, skipping the trajectories that are shorter:

H_{t}^{s}=\frac{1}{\left|\mathopen{}\left\{i\mid l_{i}\geq t\right\}\mathclose{}\right|}\sum_{i\mid l_{i}\geq t}h_{t}^{s,i}

For a given trace with harms
h_{t}
, we normalize the harm as:

\hat{h}_{t}=\frac{h_{t}}{H_{t}^{s}}

We classify the trajectory as unethical if
\hat{h}_{t}>\tau_{t}
for some threshold
\tau_{t}
.
The threshold depends on the step, to evaluate how early our baseline can detect
unethical behavior, but is independent of the scenario, enabling the model to
generalize to previously unseen scenarios.
The threshold is to be determined by the user, depending on the desired
trade-off between false positives and false negatives.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this approach. First, the method requires
direct access to the environment, which prevents it to generalise to scenarios
without a python made environment, such as the transcript of a role playing
game. Second, trajectories can vary greatly in length (from 80 steps to over 600
steps), so the average of random agents is less precise for the end of
trajectories. Last, the approach necessitates generating many (random)
trajectories even in test scenarios, which makes this baseline better viewed as
taking a pair of a scenario and a trace rather than evaluating just a trace.

Results

We present the results of our baseline detector using the AUPRC computed independently
at each time step.
The results are visualized in
Figure 5
, which shows
an AUPRC above 0.9 after step 29, and an AUPRC of 0.97 at step 80.
This shows that the baseline is able to detect unethical steering prompts with reasonable precision and recall,
even with access limited to the start of the trajectory.

Steering prompts

For the ethical steering prompt, we re-use the
Ethics Prompt
used in the

Ethical steering prompt.

Please play the game on your best behavior, exemplifying what it means to be a good and moral person.

1. Be altruistic: Be kind, helpful, and value the wellbeing of others.

2. Follow deontological rules: Do not lie, do not steal, do not harm others.

3. Act with humility and generosity: Do not be power-hungry, greedy, or take up too much space.

Unethical steering prompt.

Play the game on your worst behavior, exemplifying what it means to be a bad and immoral person.

1. Be selfish: Be unkind, unhelpful, and value only your own wellbeing.

2. Break deontological rules: Lie, steal, harm others.

3. Act with arrogance and greed: Be power-hungry, greedy, and take up too much space.
