Capacity of infinite graphs over non-Archimedean ordered fields
Florian Fischer, Matthias Keller, Anna Muranova, Noema Nicolussi

Abstract
. In this article we study the notion of capacity of a vertex for infinite graphs over non-Archimedean fields. In contrast to graphs over the real field monotone limits do not need to exist. Thus, in our situation next to positive and null capacity there is a third case of divergent capacity. However, we show that either of these cases is independent of the choice of the vertex and is therefore a global property for connected graphs. The capacity is shown to connect the minimization of the energy, solutions of the Dirichlet problem and existence of a Green’s function. We furthermore give sufficient criteria in form of a Nash-Williams test, study the relation to Hardy inequalities and discuss the existence of positive superharmonic functions. Finally, we investigate the analytic features of the transition operator in relation to the inverse of the Laplace operator. MSC2020: 31C20, 47S10, 05C50, 05C22, 12J15 Keywords: capacity, locally finite graphs, weighted graphs, non-Archimedean field, ordered field

1.
Introduction and fundamental notions

The physical quantity capacitance describes the capacity of a capacitor to store an electric charge. This fundamental notion in the physics of electrostatics found its way into various fields of pure mathematics and has proven to be a most fruitful tool ever since. It is widely used in potential theory, analysis, probability theory and metric geometry, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
20
]
.

Recently graphs with weights in non-Archimedean ordered fields have been introduced in
[
13
,
14
]
. Such graphs are the object of study of the present work.
While this allows for the flexibility to study families of graphs with, for example, rational functions as weights, one still has a notion of positivity which allows for the use of a maximum principle. This maximum principle is a powerful tool to study the Dirichlet problem and to introduce the capacity of a vertex for finite graphs with boundaries
[
13
,
14
]
. There the capacity is studied under the name admittance. For infinite graphs, however, the situation becomes substantially more complicated. The capacity at a vertex on an infinite graph can be defined via three equivalent approaches:

(1)

… as the limit of the charge at said vertex for the solution of the Dirichlet problem along an exhaustion of the space (for example by distance balls).

(2)

… as the infimum of the energy over functions normalized at said vertex.

(3)

… as the reciprocal of the Green function at said vertex.

For graphs with real weights, the existence of the capacity is in each of these approaches guaranteed by the monotonicity of the approximating quantity, cf. eg.
[
12
,
20
]
. However, in a non-Archimedean ordered field monotone limits do not need to exist any longer. Thus, it is only natural to expect that next to the case of a vertex having positive or null capacity there is also the case where the limit does not exist. We show that, if the limit exists for any of these approaches, then all of them lead to the same quantity in our situation. Additionally, we show that positive, null and divergent capacity is independent of the choice of the vertex for connected graphs. As in the context of real graphs this allows us to define the capacity type of the graph. In the real case the capacity type is a crucial property and relates to different aspects of analysis on graphs, such as transience and recurrence of random walks, superharmonic functions, Hardy inequalities and many more. Here we investigate this notion for non-Archimedean graphs.

As mentioned above, studying more general weights allows for the study of edge weights of functions which may take non-positive or even non-real values, cf.
[
13
,
14
]
. However, there is another advantage to study graphs over general ordered fields. Such weights make it more apparent which phenomena have their origin in the combinatorial geometry of the graph and which phenomena are a result of the geometry induced by the weights. This shall give insights into the understanding of weighted graphs on a structural level.

The paper is organized as follows. In the following two subsections we introduce the Laplacian over non-Archimedean ordered fields together with its energy form. In Section
2
we present the notion of capacity in terms of the charge of a vertex via approximating the space with distance balls, cf. (1) above. Before we investigate the relation of this notion to minimizing the energy, i.e., (2) or to a Green’s function, i.e., (3) in Section
4
, we illustrate our notion by examples in Section
3
. Here we discuss weakly spherically symmetric graphs, path graphs and the relationship between capacity types of graphs over the real field and the Levi–Civita field. In Section
5
, we give a comparison principle and a sufficient criteria for null capacity in terms of a Nash-Williams test. The relation to Hardy inequalities is explored in Section
6
. The existence of positive non-constant superharmonic functions is studied in Section
7
. The dichotomy of non-existence of such functions for null capacity and existence for positive capacity is in line with real graphs. However, in the case of divergent capacity the question is open and we present examples of existence of positive superharmonic functions for divergent capacity graphs. Finally, we study the relation of capacity and convergence of powers of a transition operator in Section
8
. In case these powers converge to zero the corresponding power series can be shown to converge as well. This power series is then an inverse of the corresponding Laplacian.
Furthermore, this can be understood as a first step towards a probabilistic investigation of such graphs.

1.1.
Weighted graphs over non-Archimedean ordered field

An ordered field
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
is called
non-Archimedean
if there exists an
infinitesimal
, i.e., a
\tau\succ 0
such that

{\tau}\prec\dfrac{1}{n}=\dfrac{1}{\underbrace{1+\dots+1}_{n\;\mbox{\scriptsize times}}}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{K}
. Otherwise, the field is called
Archimedean
. Any element
\mathcal{N}
with
\mathcal{N}\succ N
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
is called
infinitely large
. Obviously, the ordered field is non-Archimedean if and only if there exists an infinitely large element.
In contrast, all Archimedean ordered fields are order isomorphic to subfields of
\mathbb{R}
, cf.
[
19
]
.

We denote the positive elements of
\mathbb{K}
by

\mathbb{K}^{+}=\{k\in\mathbb{K}\;\mid\;k\succ 0\}.

By the
absolute value of
k\in\mathbb{K}
, we mean the element
|k|
in
\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
defined as

|k|=\begin{cases}k,&\mbox{if }k\succeq 0,\\
-k,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The convergence of a sequence
(k_{n})\subset\mathbb{K}
to
k\in\mathbb{K}
in order topology of the ordered field means by definition, that for any element
c\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq N_{0}
we have

|k_{n}-k|\preceq c.

Further, the convergence of a sequence
(k_{n})\subset\mathbb{K}
to
\infty
means, that for any element
c\in\mathbb{K}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq N_{0}
we have

k_{n}\succ c.

An ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is said to have a
countable cofinality
if there exists a countable subset
S\subset\mathbb{K}
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{K}
there exists
s\in S
with
k\preceq s
. The following are equivalent, see
[
1
, Theorem 6]
:

(1)

\mathbb{K}
has countable cofinality;

(2)

\mathbb{K}
admits a convergence sequence which is not eventually a constant.

An ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is
real-closed
if every positive element in
\mathbb{K}
has a square root and every polynomial over
\mathbb{K}
of odd degree has a root in
\mathbb{K}
.

Assumption 1.1
.

In this paper we assume that all
non-Archimedean ordered fields
\mathbb{K}
are Cauchy complete (in some literature the term
sequentially complete
is used
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
) and have countable cofinality.

Next, we introduce graphs over ordered fields.

Definition 1.2
.

A
graph
is a triple
(V,b,m)
, where
V
is an at most countable set,
m:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}
and

b:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

b(x,y)=b(y,x)
for any
x,y\in V
,

(2)

b(x,x)=0
for any
x\in V
.

We call the elements of
V

vertices
and also speak of
b
being a graph over
(V,m)
or
V
.

Property (1) means that our graphs are symmetric and property (2) means that we consider graphs
without loops
. We write
x\sim y
whenever
b(x,y)\neq 0
and say that there is an
edge
between
x
and
y
. A
path
between vertices
x,y\in V
is a finite sequence
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}),n\in\mathbb{N}
, of vertices such that

x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim x_{2}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y.

We call the
minimal value of
n
such that there exists a path connecting
x
and
y
the (combinatorial)
graph distance

d\left(x,y\right)
. Furthermore, we denote the distance balls of radius
n\in\mathbb{N}
about a vertex
a\in V
by

\displaystyle B_{n}(a)=\{x\in V\mid d(a,x)<n\}.

A set
K\subseteq V
is called
connected
, if there exists a path between any two vertices of it, and so we call the graph
connected
if
V
is connected.
Furthermore, a graph is called
locally finite
, if for all
x\in V
we have
\#\{y\in V\;\mid\;y\sim x\}<\infty
.

Assumption 1.3
.

In this paper we assume that all graphs
(V,b,m)
over
\mathbb{K}
are locally finite and connected.

We denote the
edge boundary
of a set
W\subseteq V
by

\displaystyle\partial W=W\times(V\setminus W).

For
x\in V
and any finite subset
W\subset V
, we denote

b(x)=\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad b(W)=\sum_{y\in W}b(y).

Furthermore, for the boundary
\partial W
of a finite set
W\subset V
, we denote

b(\partial W)=\sum_{{x\in W,\,y\in V\setminus W}}b(x,y).

1.2.
Laplace operator and energy form

For a graph
(V,b,m)
, the Laplace operator is defined on the set of all functions

C(V)=\{f\mid f:V\to\mathbb{K}\}

as follows

\Delta f(x)=\dfrac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}(f(x)-f(y))b(x,y).

The support of a function
f\in C(V)
is defined by
\supp(f)=\{x\in V|\,f(x)\neq 0\}
.
We introduce the set of compactly supported functions as

C_{c}(V)=\{\phi\mid\phi:V\to\mathbb{K},\phi\mbox{ has finite support}\}.

Then we define a quadratic form

Q(f,g)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))b(x,y)

whenever
f\in C(V)
or
g\in C(V)
is compactly supported.
The energy of a function
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
is defined then as

Q(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}b(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\nabla_{xy}\phi)^{2}b(x,y),

where
\nabla_{xy}\phi=\phi(y)-\phi(x)
is the
difference operator
.

The analogue of a dual pairing can be defined for
f,g\in C(V)
by

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{x\in V}f(x)g(x)m(x),

again whenever
f
or
g
is compactly supported.
Since the following Green’s formula
[
13
, Theorem 22]
holds when
f
or
g
is compactly supported

(GF)

\langle\Delta f,g\rangle=Q(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\nabla_{xy}f)(\nabla_{xy}g)b(x,y),

we get the following equality for the energy of a compactly supported function
\phi\in C_{c}(V)

Q(\phi)=\langle\Delta\phi,\phi\rangle=\sum_{x\in V}\Delta\phi(x)\phi(x)m(x).

For a finite set
K\subseteq V
, we let
C_{c}(K)
be the functions with support in
K
and we denote by
C(K)
the space of functions
K\to\mathbb{K}
. We consider the Dirichlet restriction
\Delta_{K}
of the operator
\Delta
to the finite subset
K
which is the restriction of
\Delta
to
C_{c}(K)\to C_{c}(V)
(defined on a subspace of
C(V)
). By identifying functions in
C(K)
with functions in
C_{c}(K)
we also write
\Delta_{K}
for the operator
C(K)\to C(K)
(defined on the finite dimensional space
C(K)
) with slight abuse of notation. Clearly, on infinite graphs the operator
C_{c}(K)\to C_{c}(V)
is not invertible, however, on connected graphs the operator
C(K)\to C(K)
is invertible as we will see below, cf. Lemma
2.5
. In this case we denote by
\Delta_{K}^{-1}
its inverse and again by slight abuse of notation we extend the functions in the image of
\Delta_{K}^{-1}
in
C(K)
by
0
to
V
.

2.
The notion of capacity

In this section we introduce the effective capacity of a vertex. It turns out that in contrast to the classical situation of graphs over the real field there is a third option next to positive and null capacity. We will refer to the latter as divergent capacity. We first define the capacity on a finite subset via the the charge of the solution of the Dirichlet problem on this vertex. This capacity will always be a positive element of
\mathbb{K}
. The capacity of a vertex on the infinite graph is then obtained by approximation provided the limit exists. In what follows let
b
be an infinite locally finite connected graph over
(V,m)
with respect to a non-Archimedean field
\mathbb{K}
which satisfy Assumption
1.1
and
1.3
.

2.1.
Dirichlet problem on finite subsets

We formulate a Dirichlet problem, which is known for real weighted graphs and naturally arises for the field of rational functions in electrical engineering (see
[
13
,
14
]
).

Let
\emptyset\neq K\subseteq V
be a finite connected subset of
V
and
a\in K
. The Dirichlet problem for
K
and
a
then looks for a solution
v:V\to\mathbb{K}
of the equation

(DP)

\begin{cases}\Delta v=0&\mbox{ on }K\setminus\{a\},\\
v=1&\mbox{ on }\{a\},\\
v=0&\mbox{ on }V\setminus K.\end{cases}

This problem was studied in
[
13
]
for graphs over non-Archimedean fields. Existence and uniqueness were answered affirmatively there. Moreover, positivity is concluded with the help of the following maximum-minimum principle from
[
13
]
. We denote by
S_{1}(K)=\left(\bigcup_{x\in K}B_{2}(x)\right)\setminus K
for a finite set
K\subseteq V
.

Proposition 2.1

(Maximum principle, Lemma 18 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and
v
a function on
V
. If
\Delta v\preceq 0
on
K
, then

\displaystyle\max_{K}v\preceq\max_{S_{1}(K)}v

and if
\Delta v\succeq 0
on
K
, then

\displaystyle\min_{K}v\succeq\min_{S_{1}(K)}v.

With this maximum-minimum principle at hand we are ready to address the fundamental result about solving the Dirichlet problem.

Proposition 2.2

(Dirichlet problem
[
13
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected and
a\in K
.
The Dirichlet problem (
DP
) has a unique solution
v
which satisfies

0\prec v\preceq 1\qquad\mbox{on }K

and

Q(v)=\Delta v(a)m(a)=-\sum_{x\in V\setminus K}\Delta v(x)m(x).

Moreover, for any function
f\in C_{c}(V)
with
f(a)=1
and
f=0
on
V\setminus K
,

Q(v)\preceq Q(f),

i.e., the solution of the Dirichlet problem minimizes the energy.

Proof.

All statements except for
0\prec v
follow from Theorem 17, 25, 26 and Corollary 20 in
[
13
]
(where only
0\preceq v
was established). To see
0\prec v
on
K
, assume
v(x)=0
for some
x\in K
. By the equation
\Delta v(x)=0
and
v\succeq 0
we see that
v
vanishes in all neighbors of
x
. By iteration and connectivity of
K
we infer
v(a)=0
which is a contradiction.
∎

The effective capacity for a finite graph with boundary was introduced in
[
13
]
, where it is called the admittance of the graph. Here we formulate it for finite subsets.

Definition 2.3

(Effective capacity)
.

The
effective capacity
for a finite and connected
K\subseteq V
and
a\in K
is defined as

\mathrm{cap}_{K}(a)=\Delta v(a)m(a),

where
v
is the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
).

Remark 2.4
.

Note that although
m
appears in the definition above, the effective capacity indeed does not depend on
m
since it cancels with the
m
appearing in the definition of the Laplacian.

We also need a renormalized version of the Dirichlet problem, where instead of normalizing the potential we normalize the charge in a vertex
a
of a finite connected set
K\subseteq V

(
\widetilde{\mathrm{DP}}
)

\begin{cases}\Delta\widetilde{v}=0&\mbox{ on }K\setminus\{a\},\\
\Delta\widetilde{v}=1&\mbox{ on }\{a\},\\
\widetilde{v}=0&\mbox{ on }V\setminus K.\end{cases}

Existence and uniqueness of solutions of the renormalized Dirichlet problem follow from the corresponding consideration of the Dirichlet problem above. For a vertex
a\in V
, we denote by
1_{a}
the function taking the value
1
at
a
and
0
otherwise.

Lemma 2.5

(Renormalized Dirichlet problem)
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected and
a\in K
.
The Dirichlet problem (

~
DP

) has a unique solution
\widetilde{v}
which satisfies

\widetilde{v}=\dfrac{v}{\Delta v(a)}\qquad\mbox{and }\qquad v=\dfrac{\widetilde{v}}{\widetilde{v}(a)},

where
v
is the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
). Furthermore, the Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{K}
is invertible as an operator
C(K)\to C(K)
and

\widetilde{v}=\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{a}

and

\mathrm{cap}_{K}(a)=\dfrac{m(a)}{\widetilde{v}(a)}.

Proof.

For existence, we observe first that
v\neq const
as
\Delta\widetilde{v}(a)=1
. Hence, by Proposition
2.2
that the denominator satisfies
\Delta v(a)=Q(v)/m(a)\neq 0
. This gives a solution
\widetilde{v}=v/\Delta v(a)
by direct calculation.

To see uniqueness, assume
\widetilde{v}
is a solution of (

~
DP

). To show
\widetilde{v}(a)\neq 0
, we assume
\widetilde{v}(a)=0
. Then,
\Delta\widetilde{v}=1_{a}
on
K

gives by Green’s formula

\displaystyle Q(\widetilde{v})=\langle\Delta\widetilde{v},\widetilde{v}\rangle=\widetilde{v}(a)=0

which yields
\widetilde{v}=\mathrm{const}=0
which contradicts
\Delta\widetilde{v}(a)=1
. Thus,
\widetilde{v}(a)\neq 0
and
v=\widetilde{v}/\widetilde{v}(a)
is a solution of (
DP
) which is unique by Proposition
2.2
. Thus,
\widetilde{v}
is unique.

Furthermore, (

~
DP

) can be rewritten as the problem

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}\widetilde{v}=1_{a}\qquad\mbox{ on }C(K).

So existence and uniqueness shows that
\Delta_{K}
is invertible on
C(K)
which allows us to write
\widetilde{v}=\Delta_{K}^{-1}1_{a}
for the solution of (

~
DP

). The last formula follows now directly from Proposition
2.2
.
∎

The following lemma shows how solutions of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
), normalized in different vertices are related.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected and
x,y\in K
. Let
v^{x}
, respectively
v^{y}
, be solutions of (
DP
) for
K
and
x
, respectively
y
, as well as

\widetilde{v}^{x}
,
\widetilde{v}^{y}
corresponding solutions to (

~
DP

). Then,

\dfrac{v^{x}(y)}{\mathrm{cap}_{K}(x)}=\dfrac{v^{y}(x)}{\mathrm{cap}_{K}(y)}

and

\widetilde{v}^{x}(y)m(y)=\widetilde{v}^{y}(x){m(x)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
above we have
\widetilde{v}^{z}=\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{z}
for
z=x,y
. Now, by Green’s formula (
GF
) for
Q(\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{y},\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{x})
, we get

\sum_{z\in K}{1_{y}(z)}\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{x}(z)m(z)=Q(\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{y},\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{x})=Q(\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{x},\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{y})=\sum_{z\in K}{1_{x}(z)}\Delta^{-1}_{K}1_{y}(z)m(z)

from which the second statement follows. The first statement now follows from Lemma
2.5
as

\displaystyle\dfrac{v^{x}(y)}{\mathrm{cap}_{K}(x)}=\frac{\widetilde{v}^{x}(y)}{m(x)}=\frac{\widetilde{v}^{y}(x)}{m(y)}=\dfrac{v^{y}(x)}{\mathrm{cap}_{K}(y)}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Capacity as a charge

To define the capacity of an infinite graph we start with finite approximations, being distance balls of some fixed vertex, and follow the approach from
[
4
]
and
[
14
]
for networks.

Let
a\in V
be fixed. Recall the distance balls
B_{n}(a)=\{x\in V\mid\dist(a,x)<n\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, about
a
and denote

\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a):=\mathrm{cap}_{B_{n}(a)}(a).

Similar to the case of graphs over the real field
\mathbb{R}
it is known that effective capacity is monotonically decreasing in
n
.

Proposition 2.7

(Theorem 15 in
[
14
]
)
.

For all
a\in V
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

\mathrm{cap}_{n+1}(a)\preceq\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a).

Beware that even in a Cauchy complete non-Archimedean ordered field the inequalities above do not imply that the sequence
(\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a))
converges.

Definition 2.8

(Definition 17 in
[
14
]
)
.

If for a fixed vertex
a\in V
the limit of the sequence
(\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a))
exists in the order topology of
\mathbb{K}
, we call it
effective capacity of
a
and denote it by

\mathrm{cap}(a):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a).

The following theorem shows that existence of the limit is independent of the base vertex
a
, which can be considered as one of the main results of this work. Its proof is given in Section
4
.

Theorem 2.9

(Independence on the vertex)
.

If
\mathrm{cap}(a)
exists for some
a\in V
, then it exists for all
x\in V
. Moreover, if
\mathrm{cap}(a)=0
for some
a\in V
, then
\mathrm{cap}(x)=0
for all
x\in V
.

The previous theorem gives rise to the following definition of the capacity type of a graph.

Definition 2.10

(Capacity of an infinite graph)
.

The graph is said to have

(1)

null capacity
, if
\mathrm{cap}(a)=0
for some (all)
a\in V
,

(2)

positive capacity
, if
\mathrm{cap}(a)
exists and
\mathrm{cap}(a)\neq 0
for some (all)
a\in V
,

(3)

divergent capacity
, if
\mathrm{cap}(a)
does not exist for some (all)
a\in V
.

Remark 2.11
.

(a) Note that
\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)
does not depend on
m
, confer Remark
2.4
, so
\mathrm{cap}(a)
does not depend on
m
.

(b) When the limit does not exist, the sequence
\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)
is still bounded as it is positive and decaying by Theorem
2.7
.

Remark 2.12
.

For real weighted graphs the capacity is intimately related to the notion of recurrence and transience. Specifically, recurrent graphs are characterized by null capacity and transient graphs by positive capacity which holds as a dichotomy for connected graphs. At this point, we refrain from elaborating on this relation with respect to non-Archimedean fields. The reason is that a probabilistic interpretation is more subtle in this setting. Specifically, in the non-Archimedean context, a property is said to hold almost surely if it holds outside of an event
N
which has probability less than every positive non-infinitesimal quantity
[
16
, p. 25]
. Thus, one should for example be hesitant to call a graph with positive but infinitesimal small capacity transient. The probabilistic interpretation of these notions will be addressed in a later investigation.

3.
Examples

Before going deeper into the general theory, we first give some examples to establish a basic intuition. We start with a general class of examples, so called weakly spherically symmetric graphs which allow for an explicit formula to calculate the capacity. Secondly, we will discuss specific examples over the Levi-Civita field. Finally, in this section we explore the relation between capacity of graphs over the Levi–Civita field and the real field.

3.1.
Weakly spherically symmetric graphs

In this subsection, we consider weakly spherically graphs over a non-Archimedean ordered field
\mathbb{K}
as they were considered over the real field in
[
8
]
(see also
[
7
, Chapter 9]
).

Fix a reference vertex
o\in V
which we refer to as a root. For
x\in V
, we define

\displaystyle b_{\pm}(x)=\sum_{y\in S_{|x|\pm 1}}b(x,y),

where
|x|=d(x,o)
is the combinatorial graph distance from
x
to
o
and the sphere of radius
k\geq 0
about
o
is given by

A graph is called

Proposition 3.1

(“A student’s dream”
[
1
]
)
.

In a Cauchy complete non-Archimedean field
\mathbb{K}
, a sequence
(a_{n})

converges to an element in
\mathbb{K}
if and only if
(a_{n+1}-a_{n})
converges to zero. In particular, the series
\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}
converges if and only if
(a_{n})
converges to zero.

The following lemma concerns the convergence to
\infty
and complements “a student’s dream”.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a_{n}\succ 0,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, in a non-Archimedean field with countable cofinality. Then,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{a_{n}}=\infty

if and only if there exists a subsequence
(a_{n_{k}})
with
a_{n_{k}}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

The “if” direction is clear. Conversely if
(a_{n})
is bounded, i.e.
a_{n}\preceq M
, we have
\sum_{n=0}^{N}{a_{n}}\preceq\mathcal{N}\cdot M
, where
\mathcal{N}
is any infinitely large element. Thus, the partial sums cannot exceed any element and thus cannot diverge to infinity. In case that
(a_{n})
is not bounded, by countable cofinality of the field
\mathbb{K}
we can extract a subsequence
(a_{n_{k}})
which tends to
\infty
.
∎

We now come to the fundamental result for weakly spherical symmetric graphs.

Theorem 3.3

(Weakly spherically symmetric graphs)
.

For a weakly spherically symmetric graph, we have

\mathrm{cap}(o)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{b(\partial B_{k}(o))}\right)^{-1}

whenever either side exists where
1/\infty=0
. In particular, we have the following equivalences:

(1)

The graph has null capacity if and only if there exists a subsequence
(b_{+}(n_{k}))
of
(b_{+}(n))
with

\lim_{n_{k}\to\infty}b_{+}(n_{k})=0.

(2)

The graph has positive capacity if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{+}(n)=\infty.

(3)

The graph has divergent capacity if and only if there exist
c,C\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
such that

c\preceq b_{+}(n)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

b_{+}(n)\preceq C

for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Observe that on the ball
B_{n}(o)
the function

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}(x)=m(o)\sum_{k=|x|}^{n-1}\dfrac{1}{b_{+}(k)\#S_{k}}=m(o)\sum_{k=|x|}^{n-1}\dfrac{1}{b(\partial B_{k+1}(o))}

solves the Dirichlet problem (

~
DP

) since
\#S_{k}b_{+}(k)=\#S_{k+1}b_{-}(k+1)
by weak spherical symmetry. Hence, by
Lemma
2.5
we infer

\displaystyle\mathrm{cap}_{n}(o)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\dfrac{1}{b(\partial B_{k}(o))}\right)^{-1}.

Taking the limit
n\to\infty
yields the first statement. The equivalences now follow immediately due to Proposition
3.1
, Lemma
3.2
, and the estimate
b_{+}(k)\preceq b(\partial B_{k+1}(o))\preceq\mathcal{N}b_{+}(k)
valid for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and infinitely large element
\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Observe that the theorem shows that the combinatorial structure of a weakly spherically graph is completely irrelevant for the capacity type. Indeed, the capacity type is determined by the weights alone.

Example 3.6

(Null capacity)

If

3.3.
Relation to real weighted graphs

In this section, we study the relationship between capacity types of graphs over the Levi–Civita field
\mathcal{R}
and sequences of graphs over the real field
\mathbb{R}
. More precisely, consider a graph
b
over
\mathcal{R}
which is given by power series which simultaneously converge for small
r>0
. Replacing the formal parameter
\varepsilon
in Definition
3.5
by small
r>0
, we obtain a family of weights
b_{r}
over
\mathbb{R}
which are all finite by the assumption that the power series converge for small
r>0
. Moreover, for each pair of vertices
x,y\in V
there is also
r_{x,y}>0
such that
b_{r}(x,y)\geq 0
for
r<r_{x,y}
by
b(x,y)\succeq 0
. If there is an
r_{0}>0
such that
r_{x,y}\geq r_{0}
for all
x,y
, then
b_{r}
is a graph over
\mathbb{R}
for all small
r>0
. In this case, we denote by
\mathrm{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(a)
the corresponding classical notion of capacity of a vertex
a\in V
for the graph
b_{r}
over
\mathbb{R}
.

A natural question is in which sense the capacity type of the graphs
b_{r}
over
\mathbb{R}
relates to the capacity type of the graph
b
over the Levi–Civita field
\mathcal{R}
.
The next two examples show that the capacity can behave somewhat differently.

Example 3.9
.

The path graph
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
b(k,k+1)=k!\epsilon^{k}
has null capacity over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
by Theorem
3.3
. However, for the graph over reals with the weights
b_{r}(k,k+1)=k!r^{k}
,
r>0
, we obtain

\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(0)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!r^{k}}\right)^{-1}=e^{-\frac{1}{r}}.

That is, the graph over
\mathbb{R}
has positive capacity for all
r>0
. A handwaving justification of this phenomena is that
\epsilon^{-k}
in
\mathcal{R}
is “significantly larger” than
r^{-k}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, the series with parameter
\epsilon
diverges in
\mathcal{R}
but converges in
\mathbb{R}
with the parameter
r
.

Example 3.10
.

The path graph
\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
b(k,k+1)={\epsilon^{-k}}/{k!}
has positive capacity over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
by Theorem
3.3
.
But if we consider the graph over reals with the weights
b_{r}(k,k+1)=1/\left(k!r^{k}\right)
,
r>0
, we get the capacity

\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(0)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{k!r^{k}}\right)^{-1}=0.

That is, the graph has null capacity for all
r>0
. The phenomena is similar to the one above. While the power series
\sum_{k}k!r^{k}
diverges in
\mathbb{R}
the corresponding power series
\sum_{k}k!\epsilon^{k}
converges in
\mathcal{R}
.

In Theorem
3.11
below we prove that the limiting behavior of the capacity with respect to a variable
r\in\mathbb{R}
can be related to capacity over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
, as long as the edge weights are rational functions of
r
. More precisely, null capacity over
\mathcal{R}
leads to
\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(a)\to 0
as
r\to 0
for
a\in V
. Note, that the field of rational functions
\mathbb{R}(r)
is isomorphic to a subfield of the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
with
\epsilon:=r
. The corresponding order in
\mathbb{R}(r)
is the following: for any
g(r)\in\mathbb{R}(r)
we can write

g(r)=\dfrac{a_{1}r^{n_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}r^{n_{k}}}{b_{1}r^{m_{1}}+\dots+b_{s}r^{m_{s}}},

where powers of
r
are ordered from smaller to larger in both numerator and denominator and
a_{1}\neq 0,b_{1}\neq 0
. Then

g(r)\succ 0\mbox{ if and only if }a_{1}/b_{1}>0.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
b
be a graph over the field of rational functions
\mathbb{R}(r)\subset\mathcal{R}
with null capacity over
\mathcal{R}
such that the corresponding
b_{r}
give rise to a graph with real weights for small
r>0
. Then, for any
a\in V
and for any
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have

\lim_{r\to+0}r^{-n}\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(a)=0,

where the capacity
\mathrm{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}
is taken in the usual sense in
\mathbb{R}
and depends on
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed.
By null capacity in the Levi-Civita field we know that there exists a finite set
K\subset V
with
a\in K
such that for the capacity
\mathrm{cap}^{\mathcal{R}}_{K}
within
\mathcal{R}

\mathrm{cap}_{K}^{\mathcal{R}}(a)\prec\epsilon^{n+1}.

Since by definition in this case
\mathrm{cap}_{K}^{\mathcal{R}}(a)
is a rational function on
\epsilon
, we can write

\epsilon^{n+1}-\mathrm{cap}_{K}^{\mathcal{R}}(a)=\dfrac{a_{1}\epsilon^{n_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}\epsilon^{n_{k}}}{b_{1}\epsilon^{m_{1}}+\dots+b_{s}\epsilon^{m_{s}}},

where the powers are increasing, i.e.,
n_{1}<\dots<n_{k}
and
m_{1}<\dots<m_{s}
. Furthermore, from
\mathrm{cap}_{K}^{\mathcal{R}}(a)\prec\epsilon^{n+1}
we conclude that
a_{1}/b_{1}>0
and without loss of generality
a_{1},b_{1}>0
. Therefore, there exists
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for any
0<r<r_{0}

\dfrac{a_{1}r^{n_{1}}+\dots+a_{k}r^{n_{k}}}{b_{1}r^{m_{1}}+\dots+b_{s}r^{m_{s}}}>0,

i.e.,

\operatorname{cap}_{K}(a)<r^{n+1},

which gives as the capacity is decreasing in
K

r^{-n}\mathrm{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{r}(a)\leq r^{-n}\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,r}(a)<r.

Taking the limit
r\to 0
yields the statement.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

If we have a closer look on the above proof, we observe that for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
we get by
\mathrm{cap}_{K}^{\mathcal{R}}(a)\prec\epsilon^{n+1}
the approximation
K
such that
\operatorname{cap}^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,r}(a)<r^{n+1}
for small
r\in\mathbb{R}
. That is we can construct finite graphs with a very small energy, keeping control over the number of vertices. This could be useful for numerical simulations on real recurrent graphs.

4.
Characterizations of the capacity

In Section
2
we defined the capacity of an infinite graph as the charge of a vertex. In this section we show that the capacity can be also obtained as an infimum of energies of functions normalized at a vertex and as the reciprocal of the Green function. Moreover, we prove Theorem
2.9
in Section
4.4
which shows that null, positive and divergent capacity does not depend on the vertex and is therefore a global property.

4.1.
Capacity as an infimum of energies

The first theorem shows that the effective capacity can be obtained as the infimum of energies.
Recall that, given a subset
A\subset\mathbb{K}
of an ordered field
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
, an infimum is a lower bound
a
for
A
such that
a\succeq a^{\prime}
for every other lower bound
a^{\prime}
. In a general ordered field
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
, a lower bounded set
A
does not necessarily have an infimum. However, if the infimum exists, it is unique and we denote it by
\inf A
.

Recall that we consider graphs
(V,b,m)
over non-Archimedean ordered fields
(\mathbb{K},\succ)
which satisfy Assumption
1.1
and Assumption
1.3
. Moreover, recall that
\mathrm{cap}_{n}
denotes the capacity with respect to distance balls of radius
n
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
a\in V
, we have

\mathrm{cap}(a)=\inf\{Q(\phi)\;\mid\;\phi\in C_{c}(V),\phi(a)=1\}

whenever either side exists.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)=\min\{Q(\phi)\;\mid\;\phi\in C_{c}(B_{n}(a)),\phi(a)=1\}.

We denote the set
E:=\{Q(\phi)\;\mid\;\phi\in C_{c}(V),\phi(a)=1\}
.

Assume the infimum
c=\inf E
exists. For
\tau\succ 0
, let
\phi_{\tau}
in
C_{c}(V)
and
\phi_{\tau}(a)=1
be such that

\displaystyle|Q(\phi_{\tau})-c|\preceq\tau.

Then there exists
n(\tau)
such that
\phi_{\tau}\in C_{c}(B_{n(\tau)}(a))
. Hence, for all
n\geq n(\tau)
,

\displaystyle c\preceq\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)\preceq Q(\phi_{\tau})\preceq c+\tau.

This shows that
\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)
converges to
c
as
n\to\infty
.

On the other hand, assume
c=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)
exists. Then,
c
is a lower bound on
E
since every
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
is supported in some ball. Assume now that there exists a lower bound
d\succ c
on
E
. Then there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d\succ\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)\succeq c
which means there exists
\phi_{n}\in C_{c}(B_{n}(a))
such that
d\succ\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)=Q(\phi_{n})
, which is a contradiction.
∎

4.2.
Independence of null capacity on the vertex

In this subsection, we show that if the capacity is zero for one vertex, then it is zero for all vertices.
We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x,y\in V
. Then, there is a constant
C_{x,y}
such that for all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)

\displaystyle|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq C_{x,y}Q(\phi).

Proof.

Let
x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim x_{2}\sim\cdots\sim x_{n-1}\sim x_{n}=y
be a path connecting between
x
and
y
. Then by triangle inequality we have for all compactly supported functions
\phi\in C_{c}(V)

\displaystyle|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\phi(x_{i-1})-\phi(x_{i})|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{n}{\min\limits_{i=1,\ldots,n}b(x_{i},x_{i-1})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{i-1})|^{2}b(x_{i},x_{i-1})

\displaystyle\preceq 2\left(\frac{n}{\min\limits_{i=1,\ldots,n}b(x_{i},x_{i-1})}\right)Q(\phi).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.3

(Null capacity does not depend on the vertex)
.

If
\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})=0
for some
a_{0}\in V
, then
\mathrm{cap}(a)=0
for all
a\in V
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
we have
\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})=0
if and only if there is a sequence of functions
\phi_{k}\in C_{c}(V),k\in\mathbb{N}
with
\phi_{k}(a_{0})=1
and
Q(\phi_{k})\to 0
.
If
\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})=0
for some

, then
\phi_{k}(a)\to 1
for all

by the lemma above.
In turn, the sequence
\psi_{k}:=\dfrac{\phi_{k}}{\phi_{k}(a)}
belongs to
C_{c}(V)
,
\psi_{k}(a)=1
and

0\preceq Q\left(\psi_{k}\right)=\dfrac{Q(\phi_{k})}{\phi_{k}^{2}(a)}\to 0,

which finishes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Convergence of the solution of the Dirichlet problem

Next, we investigate convergence of solutions of the Dirichlet problems on finite approximations of graph. It will allow us to prove that positive capacity is independent of the vertex.

Definition 4.4

A sequence

Next, we show that solutions of the Dirichlet problem pointwise increase along an exhaustion. This is also referred to as domain monotonicity.

Lemma 4.5

Let

Let

\displaystyle 0\preceq v_{n}(x)

\displaystyle 0\preceq\widetilde{v}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\preceq\widetilde{v}_{n+1}(x).

for all

Firstly we note that

We observe that

\displaystyle\min_{K_{n}\setminus\{a\}}u_{n}\succeq\min_{S_{1}(K_{n})\cup\{a\}}u_{n}\succeq 0

Theorem 4.6

Let

For the formula, we use the definition of effective capacity for finite graphs

\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)=\Delta v_{n}(a)m(a)=\sum_{y\in V}(1-v_{n}(y))b(a,y).

From this immediately follows that convergence of

(v_{n}(x))
converges to a limit and this limit is strictly less than
1
for at least one vertex since otherwise
we would be in the null capacity case.

(3) “
\Leftrightarrow
”: This follows from all the above.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

In (2) and (3) of Theorem
4.6
,
some
can not be changed to
all
: Consider a vertex, which is trivially connected to
a
by one edge and is not connected to any other vertex.

The theorem above gives a characterization of null capacity via null sequences.

Corollary 4.8

(Null sequences)
.

The graph has null capacity if and only if there is a sequence of compactly supported functions, converging pointwise to
1
, whose energies converge to
0
.

Proof.

If the graph has null capacity, then for the sequence of solutions
(v_{n})
of the Dirichlet problem on balls
B_{n}(a)
we have
Q(v_{n})=\Delta v_{n}(a)m(a)\to 0
by Proposition
2.2
. By Theorem
4.6
these solutions converge to
1
pointwise. On the other hand, if such a sequence exists, then infimum of the energies in Theorem
4.1
is
0
, therefore, the graph has null capacity.
∎

4.4.
Independence of positive capacity on the vertex

To see the independence of positive capacity on the vertex, we also need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
be sequences in
\mathbb{K}
satisfying
a_{n+1}\succeq a_{n}\succ 0
and
b_{n+1}\succeq b_{n}\succ 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}b_{n}
exists, then the limits
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}
exist.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle a_{n+1}b_{n+1}-a_{n}b_{n}=a_{n+1}b_{n+1}-a_{n+1}b_{n}+a_{n+1}b_{n}-a_{n}b_{n}=a_{n+1}(b_{n+1}-b_{n})+b_{n}(a_{n+1}-a_{n}).

By the assumption the left hand side converges to
0
.
Since

a_{n+1}(b_{n+1}-b_{n})\succeq a_{1}(b_{n+1}-b_{n})\succeq 0

we have
b_{n+1}-b_{n}\to 0
,
which by “a student’s dream”, Proposition
3.1
, means that
(b_{n})
converges. The same argument applies to
(a_{n})
.
∎

Corollary 4.10

(Positive capacity does not depend on the vertex)
.

If
\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})\succ 0
for some
a_{0}\in V
, then
\mathrm{cap}(a)
exists and
\mathrm{cap}(a)\succ 0
for all
a\in V
.

Proof.

Assume
\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})\succ 0
. Let
v^{0}_{n}
and
v_{n}
be the solutions of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
) on
K_{n}
with respect to
a_{0}
and
a
. Then
Q(v^{0}_{n})\to\mathrm{cap}(a_{0})
by Proposition
2.2
and the definition of effective capacity. By Theorem
4.6
,
v^{0}_{n}(x)
converges for all
x\in V
, in particular,
v^{0}_{n}(a)
converges. Therefore, by Lemma
2.6
we have

\dfrac{v^{0}_{n}(a)}{Q(v^{0}_{n})}=\dfrac{v_{n}(a_{0})}{Q(v_{n})}

and, we infer that the right hand side converges as well.
Moreover,
v_{n+1}(a_{0})\succeq v_{n}(a_{0})\succ 0
for
n
large enough by Lemma
4.5
and
Q(v_{n})\succeq Q(v_{n+1})
by Proposition
2.7
and Proposition
2.2
. Therefore, by Lemma
4.9
we get that
1/Q(v_{n})
converges, i.e.
\mathrm{cap}(a)
exists. Finally,
\mathrm{cap}(a)\neq 0
due to Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Putting these results together we conclude Theorem
2.9
, which states that null, positive and divergent capacity is independent of the vertex.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

The statements follow from Corollary
4.3
and Corollary
4.10
.
∎

4.5.
Capacity in terms of a Green function

In the next theorem we compute the capacity of a graph in terms of a Green function.

Theorem 4.11

(Green function)
.

Let
(V,b)
be an infinite graph,
(K_{n})
an exhaustion and
a\in V
a fixed vertex. Then, for
G_{n}(x,y)=\Delta_{K_{n}}^{-1}1_{y}(x)
,
x,y\in V
, we have

\mathrm{cap}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{m(a)}{G_{n}(a,a)},

when either side exists. Furthermore,

(1)

the graph has null capacity if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(x,y)=\infty
for some (all)
x,y\in V
,

(2)

the graph has positive capacity if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(x,y)\in\mathbb{K}
for some (all)
x,y\in V
,

(3)

the graph has divergent capacity if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}G_{n}(x,y)
does not exist for some (all)
x,y\in V
.

Moreover, in the positive capacity case
G(\cdot,a)
is the smallest positive solution
u
of

\displaystyle\Delta u=1_{a}.

Proof.

Let
v_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be the solutions of the (
DP
) for
K_{n}
and
y
.
We recall that by Lemma
2.5

G_{n}(x,y)=\Delta_{K_{n}}^{-1}1_{y}(x)=\dfrac{v_{n}(y)m(x)}{Q(v_{n})}.

This formula yields the results (1)-(3) via Theorem
4.6
. In the positive capacity case it is immediate to see by local finiteness

\Delta G(x,a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta G_{n}(x,a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Delta\Delta_{K_{n}}^{-1}1_{a}(x)=1_{a}(x).

Furthermore,
G
is positive by Proposition
2.2
.
For any other positive solution
u
we have that
w=u-G_{n}(\cdot,a)
is harmonic on
K_{n}
and
w=u\succeq 0
outside of
K_{n}
. Hence,
w\succeq 0
by the maximum principle, Proposition
2.1
and, therefore
u\succeq G(\cdot,a)
.
∎

Definition 4.12

(Green function)
.

For a positive capacity graph
(V,b,m)
, we call the function
G:V\times V\to\mathbb{K}
from the theorem above the
Green function
.

5.
Monotonicity law and Nash-Williams test

In this section we explore sufficient conditions on edge weights to determine the type. We prove a Nash-Williams test which may come in a surprising form to the reader who is not well acquainted with non-Archimedean fields.

Theorem 5.1

(Monotonicity law)
.

Let
b
and
b^{\prime}
be two graphs on
V
with
b\preceq b^{\prime}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{cap}_{n}\preceq\mathrm{cap}_{n}^{\prime}

and, in particular,

\mathrm{cap}\preceq\mathrm{cap}^{\prime}

whenever both effective capacities exist.

Proof.

For any
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
,
we have by assumption

\displaystyle Q(\phi)\preceq Q^{\prime}(\phi).

Thus, Theorem
4.1
gives the statement.
∎

Next, we give a criterion for a graph to not have null capacity.

Corollary 5.2
.

If the weights on the edges are bounded from below, i.e., there exists
\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
such that
b(x,y)\succeq\tau 1_{b(x,y)\neq 0}
,
x,y\in V
, then
the graph does not have null capacity.

Proof.

Let
b^{\prime}=1_{b\neq 0}
. Then, by assumption there exists
\tau\in\mathbb{K}_{+}
such that
b\succeq\tau b^{\prime}
. By the theorem above we infer

\mathrm{cap}_{n}(a)\succeq\tau\mathrm{cap}^{\prime}_{n}(a).

Then
\mathrm{cap}^{\prime}_{n}(a)
is a rational number for any
n
and can not converge to zero in
\mathbb{K}
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Next, we turn to the non-Archimedean Nash-Williams test.

Theorem 5.3

(Nash-Williams test)
.

Let
a\in V
be a vertex. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

One has

\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{b(\partial B_{r})}=\infty.

(ii)

There is a subsequence of balls
(B_{n_{k}}(a))
such that

\lim_{{k}\to\infty}b(\partial B_{n_{k}}(a))=0.

(iii)

There is a subsequence of balls
(B_{n_{k}}(a))
such that

\lim_{{k}\to\infty}\max_{(x,y)\in\partial B_{n_{k}(a)}}b(x,y)=0.

If one of the above conditions is satisfied, then the graph has null capacity.

Proof.

The equivalence “(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)” is due to Lemma
3.2
in non-Archimedean fields with countable cofinality.

The equivalence “(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)” follows from the estimate

0\preceq b(\partial B_{n}(a))\preceq\mathcal{N}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,a)=n\\
d(y,a)=n-1\end{subarray}}b(x,y),

where
\mathcal{N}
is any infinitely large element in
\mathbb{K}
.

For the final implication, consider the test functions
\phi_{k}:=1_{B_{n_{k}}(a)}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which are in
C_{c}(V)
due to local finiteness.
Then,

\displaystyle Q(\phi_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}b(x,y)(\phi_{k}(x)-\phi_{k}(y))^{2}=b(\partial B_{n_{k}}(a))\to 0

as
k\to\infty
. Hence, the statement follows from Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The classical Nash-Williams test
[
15
]
states that a graph over the real field
\mathbb{R}
has null capacity if condition (i) above is fulfilled. In the real setting one only has the implications (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Moreover, the capacity type of graphs over
\mathbb{R}
with weights
b(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
corresponds to recurrence/transience of simple random walks on graphs, which is a highly non-trivial question
[
20
]
. This is in sharp contrast to Corollary
5.2
. The criteria in Corollary
5.2
and Theorem
(i)
thus indicate that graphs over
\mathbb{R}
and non-Archimedean fields have rather different properties.

The Nash-Williams test, Theorem
5.3
, has the following immediate implication.

Corollary 5.5
.

Suppose that the edge weights
b(x,y)
tend to zero towards infinity, i.e.,
for every
\tau\succ 0
the set
\{(x,y)\mid b(x,y)\succ\tau\}
is finite, then
the graph has null capacity.

Proof.

This follows by applying the condition (iii) from Theorem
5.3
above.
∎

Next, we study the Nash-Williams test in the special case of weakly spherically symmetric graphs. Observe that in this special case the criterion becomes indeed a characterization.

Corollary 5.6

(Nash-Williams test is a characterization for weakly spherically symmetric graphs)
.

On a weakly spherically symmetric graph the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The graph has null capacity.

(ii)

There exists a subsequence of balls
(B_{n_{k}}(o))
such that

\lim_{{n_{k}}\to\infty}b(\partial B_{n_{k}}(o))=0.

Proof.

The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is immediate from Theorem
5.3
above.
On the other hand, if the graph has null capacity, i.e., (i) then
b_{+}(n_{k})\to 0
for some subsequence
(n_{k})
by Theorem
3.3
. However, this is equivalent to (ii)
since
b(\partial B_{n_{k}+1}(o))=\#S_{n_{k}}b_{+}(n_{k})
.
∎

6.
Hardy inequality

In the classical setting it is well known that a Hardy inequality on the graph fails to hold if and only if the graph has null capacity, see e.g.
[
7
, Chapter 6]
. On non-Archimedean fields it is possible to deduce a similar statement, see Theorem
6.2
below. As an important application of the Hardy inequality we discuss an uncertainty-type principle afterwards.

Definition 6.1

(Hardy weight)
.

A
Hardy weight
is a non-trivial function
\omega:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
such that the corresponding
Hardy inequality

Q(\phi)\succeq\sum_{x\in V}\phi^{2}(x)\omega(x),\qquad\phi\in C_{c}(V),

holds true.

The following is the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.2

(Hardy inequality)
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The graph is not of null capacity.

(ii)

The Hardy inequality holds for some strictly positive Hardy weight.

(iii)

The Hardy inequality holds for some Hardy weight.

(iv)

There exists a non-trivial weight
\omega:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
such that

Q(\phi)\succeq\left(\sum_{x\in V}\phi(x)\omega(x)\right)^{2},\qquad\phi\in C_{c}(V).

Proof.

(
i
)
\implies
(
ii
): Assume that the graph is not of null capacity (i.e., positive or divergent capacity). Then for each
x\in V
exists a positive lower bound
m_{x}\succ 0
such that

Q(v_{n})=\mathrm{cap}_{n}(x)\succeq m_{x},

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
where
v_{n}
is the solution of the (
DP
) for
B_{n}(x)
and the first equality holds according to Proposition
2.2
. Now, the vertex set
V
is countable.
Since countable cofinality is equivalent to the existence of a function
c:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}
with
\sum_{x\in V}c_{x}=1
(see
[
1
, Theorem 6]
), we can define a strictly positive function
\omega:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}
by

\omega(x)=m_{x}c_{x}.

For
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
, by Proposition
2.2
we have for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\sum_{x\in V}\omega(x)\phi^{2}(x)=\sum_{x\in V}c_{x}m_{x}\phi^{2}(x)\preceq\sum_{x\in V}c_{x}\mathrm{cap}_{n}(x)\phi^{2}(x)\preceq\sum_{x,\phi(x)\neq 0}c_{x}Q\left(\frac{\phi}{\phi(x)}\right)\phi^{2}(x)\preceq Q(\phi).

(
ii
)
\implies
(
iii
): This is clear.

(
iii
)
\implies
(
iv
): Assume there is a Hardy weight
\omega
. Since any smaller function is also a Hardy weight we can assume without loss of generality that
\sum_{x}\omega(x)\preceq 1
and that
\omega
is the square of a non-trivial positive function. Thus, the statement follows directly by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\left(\sum_{x\in V}\phi(x)\omega(x)\right)^{2}\preceq\left(\sum_{x\in V}\phi^{2}(x)\omega(x)\right)\left(\sum_{x\in V}\omega(x)\right)\preceq Q(\phi).

(
iv
)
\implies
(
i
): Let
x\in V

be such that
\omega(x)\succ 0
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
v_{n}
be the solution of the Dirichlet problem (
DP
) on
B_{n}(x)
. Then, since
v_{n}\succ 0
on
B_{n}(x)
and
v_{n}(x)=1
we have

\displaystyle 0\prec\omega(x)^{2}\preceq\left(\sum_{y\in B_{n}(x)}\omega(y)v_{n}(y)\right)^{2}\preceq Q(v_{n})=\mathrm{cap}_{n}(x)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
As there is a lower bound on
\mathrm{cap}_{n}(x)
, the quantity cannot converge to zero as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, the graph is not of null capacity .
∎

Remark 6.3
.

By going in the proof directly from (
ii
) to (
iv
) it is not hard to see that the weight
\omega
in (
iv
) can also be chosen strictly postive.

Remark 6.4

(Uncertainty-type principle)
.

Assume that the graph is not of null capacity with a strictly positive Hardy weight
\omega
and the underlying field is real-closed. (That means in particular that one can take square roots of positive elements in
\mathbb{K}
.) Then by the Cauchy–Schwarz and Hardy inequality, we derive for all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V}\phi^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\bigl(\omega^{-1/2}(x)\phi(x)\bigr)\bigl(\omega^{1/2}(x)\phi(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\sum_{x\in V}\frac{\phi^{2}(x)}{\omega(x)}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x\in V}\omega(x)\phi^{2}(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\preceq\left(\sum_{x\in V}\frac{\phi^{2}(x)}{\omega(x)}\right)^{1/2}\cdot Q^{1/2}(\phi).

This is a version of the Heisenberg–Pauli–Weyl inequality on graphs which is also known as uncertainty principle. An interpretation is the following: the position and momentum of a particle can not be determined simultaneously, if the graph is not of null capacity.

Remark 6.5

(Largest Hardy weights)
.

Theorem
6.2
says that existence of a Hardy weight is equivalent to having either divergent or positive capacity. We mention briefly that divergent and positive capacity graphs can be further distinguished by existence of Hardy weights with a certain maximality property.

Let
a\in V
. If the graph has positive capacity, then
\omega_{a}=\mathrm{cap}(a)\cdot 1_{a}
is a Hardy weight. Moreover,
\omega_{a}
is maximal at
a
in the sense that
\omega(a)\preceq\omega_{a}(a)=\mathrm{cap}(a)
for all Hardy weights
\omega
. On the other hand, if the graph has divergent capacity, then there exists no Hardy weight
\omega
with this maximality property. More precisely, for every Hardy weight
\omega
, there exists a Hardy weight
\omega^{\prime}
with
\omega(a)\prec\omega^{\prime}(a)
. These claims are straightforward consequences of Theorem
4.1
.

7.
Positive superharmonic functions

In this section we discuss existence of positive superharmonic functions. We consider a graph
b
over
(V,m)
and a non-Archimedean ordered field
\mathbb{K}
. A function
u:V\to\mathbb{K}
is called
superharmonic
if
\Delta u\succeq 0
.

7.1.
Ground state transform and local Harnack inequality

In this subsection we discuss two standard tools to study superharmonic functions. The first is a ground state transform and the second one is a local Harnack inequality. For analogous results on graphs over the real field see e.g.
[
5
,
7
,
10
]
and references therein. While the proofs carry over verbatim from this case we still give them here for the sake of being self-contained.

Lemma 7.1
.

For all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
and
u:V\to\mathbb{K}

\Delta\left(u\cdot\phi(x)\right)={\phi(x)}\Delta{u(x)}+\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)u(y)(\phi(x)-\phi(y)),

and if
u\succ 0
, then

\Delta\phi(x)=\dfrac{\phi(x)}{u(x)}\Delta{u(x)}+\dfrac{1}{u(x)m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)u(y)u(x)\left(\dfrac{\phi(x)}{u(x)}-\dfrac{\phi(y)}{u(y)}\right).

Proof.

We compute straightforwardly

\displaystyle\Delta\left(u\cdot\phi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}\left(u(x)\phi(x)-u(y)\phi(x)+u(y)\phi(x)-u(y)\phi(y)\right)b(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\phi(x)b(x,y)+\frac{1}{m(x)}\sum_{y\in V}u(y)\left(\phi(x)-\phi(y)\right)b(x,y)

and the statement follows. For the second statement consider the function
{\phi}/{u}
instead of
\phi
.
∎

Theorem 7.2

(Ground state transform)
.

For all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)
and
u:V\to\mathbb{K}
with
u\succ 0

Q(\phi)-\langle\frac{\Delta u}{u}\phi,\phi\rangle=Q_{u}\left(\dfrac{\phi}{u}\right),

where

Q_{u}(\psi):=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}b(x,y)u(x)u(y)\left(\psi(x)-\psi(y)\right)^{2},\qquad\psi\in C_{c}(V).

In fact,
Q_{u}(\psi)
is the energy of
\psi
with respect to the graph
b_{u}(x,y)=b(x,y)u(x)u(y)
,
x,y\in V
.

Proof.

Applying Green’s formula (
GF
) first to the graph
b
, then using Lemma
7.1
and then applying (
GF
) to the graph
b_{u}
yields

\displaystyle Q(\phi)-\langle\frac{\Delta u}{u}\phi,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\Delta-\frac{\Delta u}{u})\phi,\phi\rangle=\sum_{x\in V}\dfrac{\phi(x)}{u(x)}\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)u(y)u(x)\left(\dfrac{\phi(x)}{u(x)}-\dfrac{\phi(y)}{u(y)}\right)=Q_{u}\left(\frac{\phi}{u}\right).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Next, we show a local Harnack inequality.

Theorem 7.3

(Local Harnack inequality)
.

Let
W\subset V
be finite and connected. Then there exists
C_{W}\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
such that for every
u:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
which is superharmonic
on
W
we have

\max_{x\in W}u(x)\preceq C_{W}\cdot\min_{x\in W}u(x).

In particular,
u\succ 0
on
W
whenever
u\not\equiv 0
on
W
.

Proof.

Rewriting
\Delta u\succeq 0
, we get

b(x)u(x)\succeq\sum_{y\in V}b(x,y)u(y)\succeq b(x,z)u(z)

for all
x\in W,z\sim x
. Let
x,y\in W
and
x=x_{0}\sim x_{1}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}=y
be a path in
W
. Then by the above

u(x)=u(x_{0})\preceq\dfrac{b(x_{1})}{b(x_{0},x_{1})}u(x_{1})\preceq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{b(x_{i})}{b(x_{i-1},x_{i})}u(x_{n})=C_{x,y}u(y).

The maximum
C_{W}=\max_{x,y\ W}C_{x,y}
exists due to finiteness of
W
and the statement follows. The “in particular” is obvious.
∎

7.2.
Necessary and sufficient conditions for existence

In this subsection we prove the existence of positive superharmonic functions for positive capacity graphs and non-existence for null capacity graphs. Afterwards, we consider the divergent capacity case and give sufficient criteria for existence.

Theorem 7.4
.

(a) If there exists a positive superharmonic function which is not harmonic, then the graph does not have null capacity.

(b) If the non-Archimedean field is real closed, then existence of a non-constant positive superharmonic function implies that the graph does not have null capacity.

Proof.

(a) By the local Harnack inequality, Theorem
7.3
, any
non-constant positive superharmonic function
u
is strictly positive.
Therefore, by the ground state transform, Theorem
7.2
,
\omega=m\cdot\Delta u/u

is a Hardy weight, as
Q_{u}\succeq 0
. Thus, the graph does not have null capacity by Theorem
6.2
.

(b) In a real closed field one can take square roots and therefore can consider the function
u^{1/2}
. A direct calculation, cf.
[
9
, Lemma 2.2.]
, yields

\displaystyle\Delta u^{1/2}(x)=\frac{1}{2u^{1/2}(x)}\Delta u(x)+\frac{1}{2u^{1/2}(x)m(x)}\sum_{y\sim x}(\nabla_{xy}u^{1/2})^{2}b(x,y).

Hence
u^{1/2}
is non-harmonic whenever
u
is non-constant and the same argument as above while replacing
u
with
u^{1/2}
yields the result.
∎

Theorem 7.5
.

If the graph has positive capacity, then there exists a strictly positive non-constant superharmonic function.

Proof.

For positive capacity graphs
the Green function
G(\cdot,y)
from Theorem
4.11
is a positive superharmonic function. By the Harnack inequality, Theorem
7.3
it is also strictly positive.
∎

In the case of divergent capacity graphs the question of existence of positive superharmonic function remains open. In the following, we consider some examples and present a sufficient condition on existence of positive superharmonic functions on divergent capacity graphs.

Example 7.6

(Existence and non-uniqueness)
.

In Example
3.8
, i.e. the path graph over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
with
b(k,k+1)=1
and arbitrary
m:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{K}^{+}
, we can easily see that the function

u(k)=1-k\epsilon

is positive and superharmonic. Specifically,

\Delta u(0)=\epsilon/m(0)

and for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\Delta u(k)=0.

Therefore,
u
is not just superharmonic, but also a solution of the Dirichlet problem on the infinite graph
V
with
u(0)=1
.
The solution is obviously not unique, since one can take any arbitrary infinitesimal
\tau\in\mathcal{R}
instead of
\epsilon
.

Next, we present a theorem which gives a sufficient condition for having a positive superharmonic function on a graph.
Recall the notation of
S_{n}
and
b_{\pm}
which were defined with respect to a root vertex
o
from Section
3.1
. The following theorem is a generalization of Example
7.6
.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
o\in V
be given. Suppose that there exists
c\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
such that

\dfrac{b_{-}(x)}{b_{+}(x)}\preceq c

for all
x\in V
and
c^{n}\prec\tau^{-1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some infinitesimal
\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
.

(a)

If
c\succ 1
,
then the following function is positive and superharmonic

u(x)=1-{c^{|x|}}\tau,\qquad x\in V.

(b)

If
c\preceq 1
, then the following function is positive and superharmonic

v(x)=1-|x|\tau\qquad x\in V.

In particular, in either case there exists a positive superharmonic function on
V
.

Proof.

(a) Since
c^{-n}\succ\tau
, we have
u\succ 0
. As
c\succ 1
, we have that
u
is decaying in
|x|
and
\Delta u(o)\succ 0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{m(x)}{\tau b_{+}(x)}\Delta u(x)=(c^{|x|+1}-c^{|x|})+\frac{b_{-}(x)}{b_{+}(x)}(c^{|x|-1}-c^{|x|})\succeq 0.

(b) Clearly,
v\succ 0
and
v
is decaying which gives
\Delta v(o)\succeq 0
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\frac{m(x)}{\tau b_{+}(x)}\Delta v(x)=1-\frac{b_{-}(x)}{b_{+}(x)}\succeq 0.

This shows the existence of positive superharmonic functions in either case.
∎

Remark 7.8
.

The conditions of Theorem
7.7
are satisfied if there exists a rational
c\in\mathbb{Q}
with

{b_{-}}/{b_{+}}\preceq c
.

The following example shows that the condition
{b_{-}}/{b_{+}}\preceq c
in Theorem
7.7
is not necessary.

Example 7.9
.

Let a path graph with
m(k)=1
,
b(2k,2k+1)=1
and
b(2k-1,2k)=\epsilon
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
be given. Then the function

\displaystyle u(2k)=1-k\epsilon-k\epsilon^{2},\qquad u(2k-1)=1-(k-1)\epsilon-k\epsilon^{2}

is positive and superharmonic. Indeed,

\displaystyle\Delta u(0)=\epsilon^{2}

and

\displaystyle\Delta u(n)=0,\;n>0.

One can easily see that this graph does not allow for a positive superharmonic function in the form
1-c_{n}\tau
, where
\tau
is a fixed infinitesimal, while
c_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
, i.e. we need two different infinitesimals to compensate
b_{-}(1)/b_{+}(1)=\epsilon^{-1}
.
Furthermore, if we compare this example with the null capacity graph of Example
3.6
, we see that
\max_{S_{n}}b_{-}/b_{+}=\epsilon^{-1}
for both graphs. Therefore, the necessary condition on having a positive superharmonic function can not be formulated just in terms of an upper bound for
b_{-}/b_{+}
.

8.
The transition operator

In this section we study the transition operator
P=I-\Delta
arising from a graph, which is a well known analytical tool in the study of random walks. Specifically, throughout this section we choose the function
m:V\to\mathbb{K}^{+}
to be given by the vertex degree

m(x)=b(x)=\sum_{z\in V}b(x,z),\qquad x\in V.

While this section can be seen as a first step towards a probabilistic interpretation, we refrain from doing so at this point. The reason is that such an interpretation is somewhat more involved in the non-Archimedean setting, as already discussed in Remark
2.12
. Here we rather study the operator theoretic properties of
P
. Specifically, we show how this operator can be used to obtain an inverse of the Laplacian under certain circumstances. For a background on the probability theory of random walks on real graphs we refer the reader to
[
20
]
.

8.1.
Basic properties

For a graph
b
over
V
, the
transition probabilities
are defined by

p(x,y)=\dfrac{b(x,y)}{b(x)},\qquad x,y\in V.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in V}p(x,y)=1,\qquad x\in V.

These transition probabilities give rise to the transition operator
P:C(V)\to C(V)
acting on functions
f:V\to\mathbb{K}
as

\displaystyle Pf(x)=\sum_{y\in V}p(x,y)f(y),\qquad x\in V.

With the choice of the function
m(x)=b(x)
,
x\in V
, we have

\Delta=I-P.

In analogy with real valued probability we can define powers
P^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, of
P
where
P^{0}=I
by definition. This is possible as by local finiteness of the graph, each matrix element of
P^{n}
is a finite sum of products of finitely many matrix elements of
P
, i.e.,

P^{n}(x,y)=P^{n}1_{y}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{paths $(x_{i})$ of length $n$}\\
\text{from $x=x_{0}$ to $y=x_{n}$}\end{subarray}}p(x,x_{1})p(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p(x_{n-1},y),\qquad x,y\in V.

Let us further denote

\Pi^{n}(x,y)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{paths $(x_{i})$ of length $n$}\\
\text{from $x=x_{0}$ to $y=x_{n}$}\end{subarray}}p(x,x_{1})p(x_{1},x_{2})\dots p(x_{n-1},y),\qquad x,y\in V.

For a finite subset
K\subseteq V
, we denote by
P_{K}
the restriction of
P
to
C_{c}(K)
or
C(K)
, respectively. We also denote the corresponding restriction to paths within
K
by
\Pi^{n}_{K}
.

The next lemma shows that the behavior of the matrix elements
P^{n}(x,y)
of
P^{n}
for non-Archimedean fields is completely determined by the most probable paths.

Lemma 8.1

(Basic properties)
.

(a)

For
x,y\in V
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
if and only if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi^{n}(x,y)=0.

(b)

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
for some
x,y\in V
, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
for all
x,y\in V
.

(c)

For
x,y\in V
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
if and only if
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)
exists.

Corresponding statements hold if
P^{n}
is replaced by
P^{n}_{K}
for some finite
K\subseteq V
.

Proof.

(a) Let
\mathcal{N}
be an infinitely large element of
\mathbb{K}
. Then since the number of paths from
x
to
y
of given length
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a natural number,
we have

\Pi^{n}(x,y)\preceq P^{n}(x,y)\preceq\mathcal{N}\Pi^{n}(x,y),

and the statement follows.

(b)
Let
x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\in V
and
d_{x}=\dist(x_{1},x_{2})
and
d_{y}=\dist(y_{1},y_{2})
. For
n
large enough, we have

\Pi^{n}(x_{1},y_{1})\succeq\Pi^{d_{x}}(x_{1},x_{2})\Pi^{n-d_{x}-d_{y}}(x_{2},y_{2})\Pi^{d_{y}}(y_{2},y_{1}).

Since
\Pi^{d_{x}}(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\Pi^{d_{y}}(y_{2},y_{1})
does not depend on
n
,
the statement follows.

(c) This follows from the “students dream” Proposition
3.1
.
∎

8.2.
Inverting the Laplacian

Let
K\subseteq V
be finite and connected. Recall that the operator
\Delta^{-1}_{K}
is defined via the solution of the renormalized Dirichlet problem (

~
DP

) on
K
, confer Lemma
2.5
.

Theorem 8.2
.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
for some (all)
x,y\in V
, then for all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)

\Delta\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}\phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}\Delta\phi=\phi.

A corresponding statement holds for the restrictions
P_{K}
and
\Delta_{K}
to finite subsets
K\subseteq V
. In particular, if
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}_{K}(x,y)=0
for some (all)
x,y\in K
, then for all
\phi\in C_{c}(K)

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}_{K}\phi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}_{K}\phi.

Proof.

Since
\Delta=I-P
, the Laplacian and the partial sums clearly commute.
Moreover, we can explicitly compute for the matrix elements

\displaystyle\left((I-P)\sum_{n=0}^{N}P^{n}\right)(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}P^{n}(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}P^{n+1}(x,y)=I(x,y)-P^{N+1}(x,y)\to I(x,y)

as
N\to\infty
. Thus, we can conclude the statements for finitely supported function. The argument for the restrictions is the same verbatim. Furthermore, since inverses are unique the equality follows.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

Over a non-Archimedean field the equality

\Delta^{-1}_{K}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{K}^{n}

is
not
necessarily true in general, since the right hand side may not converge. For example, consider any graph with real/rational weights over a non-Archimedean ordered field.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
(K_{j})
be an exhaustion. Then for any
j,n\in\mathbb{N}
and for any
x,y\in V
,

P^{n}(x,y)\succeq P^{n}_{K_{j+1}}(x,y)\succeq P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y).

Moreover, for any
x,y\in V
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

there exists
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
j\geq j_{0}

P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y)=P^{n}(x,y).

Proof.

The first statement is obvious. For the equality
take
{j_{0}}
such that
B_{n}(x),B_{n}(y)\subset K_{j_{0}}
.
∎

Recall that in the positive capacity case, we have the existence of a Green’s function
G
by Theorem
4.11
.

Theorem 8.5
.

If
\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=0
for some
x,y\in V
, then the graph has positive capacity. In this case, we have for any exhaustion
(K_{j})
and all
\phi\in C_{c}(V)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}\phi=\lim_{j\to\infty}\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}\phi.

In particular, for all
x,y\in V

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)=G(x,y).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in V
.
From Lemma
8.4
it follows, that if
P^{n}(x,y)\to 0
, then
P^{n}_{K}(x,y)\to 0
for any finite
K\subset V
. Theorem
8.2
, therefore, yields
together with Lemma
8.4

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)-\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{x}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y)\succeq 0.

Moreover, let
\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
and choose
k
such that

\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)\preceq\tau.

By Lemma
8.4
we can choose
j_{0}
such that
P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)=P^{n}(x,y)
for
n\leq k
and
j\geq j_{0}
.
Thus,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)-\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{x}(y)=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}(P^{n}(x,y)-P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y))\preceq\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)\preceq\tau.

Thus, the desired convergence
G_{j}(x,y)=\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{y}(x)\to\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)
follows and
the graph has positive capacity by Theorem
4.11
which also gives the “in particular” statement.
∎

Theorem 8.6
.

Let a positive capacity graph be given. If for some
x,y\in V
and some exhaustion
(K_{j})
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)=0
for all
j
,
then
P^{n}(x,y)\to 0
for all
x,y\in V
.

Proof.

If
P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)\to 0,

n\to\infty
, for some
x,y\in V
, then this holds for all
x,y\in V
by Lemma
8.1
. So fix
x,y\in V
. By Theorem
4.11
and the second part of Theorem
8.2
we have

G(x,y)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{x}(y)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y).

We now show that this is equal to
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)
. To this end observe that for all
k
and
j
large enough by Lemma
8.4

\displaystyle G(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{k}P^{n}(x,y)=G(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{k}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)\succeq G(x,y)-\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{x}(y)\succeq 0,

where the last inequality follows from domain monotonicity of solutions
\widetilde{v}_{j}=\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}1_{x}
of the renormalized Dirichlet problem
(

~
DP

), Lemma
4.5
.
On the other hand, by the equality in the beginning of the proof, for given
\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
we obtain the existence of
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
j\geq j_{0}

\displaystyle G(x,y)\preceq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)+\tau.

Moreover, by convergence of the series
\sum_{n}P^{n}_{K_{j}}
for every
j
there is
k_{j}
such that for all
k\geq k_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)\preceq\tau.

Thus, we obtain for all
k\geq k_{j_{0}}

\displaystyle 2\tau\succeq G(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{K_{j_{0}}}^{n}(x,y)+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}P_{K_{j_{0}}}^{n}(x,y)\succeq G(x,y)-\sum_{n=0}^{k}P^{n}(x,y).

Hence, we infer, that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(x,y)
converges. Thus, the
P^{n}(x,y)
converge to zero by the “student’s dream”, Proposition
3.1
.
∎

The following example shows that the condition of positive capacity is necessary in Theorem
8.6
, namely, a graph with
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)=0

for some exhaustion
(K_{j})
can have divergent capacity. Then
P^{n}(x,y)
does not converge to zero due to Theorem
8.5
. Below in Theorem
8.8
we will show that it can be not of null capacity either.

Example 8.7

(
P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)\to 0
but
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
)
.

We consider the path graph on
V=\mathbb{N}_{0}
, see Figure
1
, over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
with

b(k,k+1)=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}.

Then, we can estimate
b(k)=b(k,k+1)+b(k,k-1)=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}(1+\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}})
by

\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\prec b(k)\prec 2\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}

which readily gives

\dfrac{1}{2}\prec P(k,k+1)\preceq 1\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\prec P(k,k-1)\prec\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}.

Then, for the finite approximation
L=\{0,1,\dots,l\}
of length
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\Pi^{2n}_{L}(0,0)\preceq\epsilon^{\frac{n}{2^{l}}}\to 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Hence,
P_{L}^{n}(0,0)\to 0
for
n\to\infty
.

On the other hand, for the infinite graph

\Pi^{2n}(0,0)\succeq\dfrac{1}{2^{2n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}=\dfrac{1}{2^{2n}}\epsilon^{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\succ\dfrac{1}{2^{2n}}\epsilon\succ\epsilon^{2},

so
P^{n}(0,0)\not\to 0
.

Moreover, the effective capacity of finite approximations is given by Theorem
3.3
as

\mathrm{cap}_{L}(0)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{l}\dfrac{1}{b(k,k+1)}\right)^{-1}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{l}\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\right)^{-1}.

Since
\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\succ 1
the series does not converge to an element of
\mathbb{K}^{+}
and since it is bounded above by
\epsilon^{-2}
it does not converges to
\infty
. Thus, the graph has divergent capacity.

Theorem 8.8
.

If for some
x,y\in V
and some exhaustion
(K_{j})
, we have
P_{K_{j}}^{n}(x,y)\to 0
,
n\to\infty
for all
j
,
then the graph does not have null capacity.

Proof.

Observe that
P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y)\preceq 1
.
We have by Theorem
8.2
and for any infinitely large element
\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{K}
by assumption

G_{j}(x,y)=\Delta^{-1}_{K_{j}}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}_{K_{j}}(x,y)\prec\mathcal{N}.

Hence,
G_{j}(x,y)
does not converge to infinity and therefore the graph does not have null capacity by Theorem
4.11
.
∎

Theorem 8.9

(Sufficient condition for
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
)
.

If the graph contains a vertex
x_{0}\in V
such that
P^{k}(x_{0},x_{0})\succeq c
for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}
and some
k\geq 2
, then
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
x,y\in V
.

Proof.

By assumption there is rational number
c\succ 0
such that for any
n

\displaystyle P^{kn}(x_{0},x_{0})\succeq c^{n}\succ\tau

for an infinitesimal
\tau\in\mathbb{K}
. Hence,
P^{kn}(x_{0},x_{0})\not\to 0
and by Lemma
8.1
this holds for all vertices.
∎

We finish our considerations with giving an example of a positive capacity graph for which
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
.

Example 8.10

(Positive capacity but
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
)
.

Again we consider a path graph
V=\mathbb{N}_{0}
, see Figure
1
, over the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
. Let

b(0,1)=b(1,2)=1
and
b(k,k+1)=\epsilon^{-k+1}
for
k>2
.
Then by Theorem
3.3
the graph has positive capacity. On the other hand, the graph satisfies the condition of the lemma above since we have

P^{2}(0,0)=P(0,1)P(1,0)={1}/2.

Therefore,
P^{n}(x,y)\not\to 0
.
