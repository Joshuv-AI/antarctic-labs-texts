Aggregating heavy-tailed random vectors: from finite sums to Lévy processes
Bikramjit Das, Vicky Fasen-Hartmann

Abstract
The tail behavior of aggregates of heavy-tailed random vectors is known to be determined by the so-called principle of “one large jump”, be it for finite sums, random sums, or, Lévy processes. We establish that, in fact, a more general principle is at play. Assuming that the random vectors are multivariate regularly varying on various subcones of the positive quadrant, first we show that their aggregates are also multivariate regularly varying on these subcones. This allows us to approximate certain tail probabilities which were rendered asymptotically negligible under classical regular variation, despite the “one large jump” asymptotics. We also discover that depending on the structure of the tail event of concern, the tail behavior of the aggregates may be characterized by more than a single large jump. Eventually, we illustrate a similar phenomenon for multivariate regularly varying Lévy processes, establishing as well a relationship between multivariate regular variation of a Lévy process and multivariate regular variation of its Lévy measure on different subcones.

Introduction

In this paper we study the behavior of the asymptotic tail distribution of independent sums of heavy-tailed random vectors under the paradigm of multivariate regular variation
[
4
,
39
]
. Assessment of such tail probabilities are of interest in risk management for many finance, insurance, queueing, and environmental applications
[
33
,
16
,
2
]
. Multi-dimensional tail events are often characterized by at least one variable exceeding a high threshold, and the asymptotic probability of such events follow the so-called “one large jump” principle, see
[
22
]
.

Assume that all our random elements are defined on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. If
Z,Z^{(1)},\ldots,Z^{(n)}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables, then for fixed
x>0
we know that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z^{(1)}+\ldots+Z^{(n)}>tx)\sim n\,\mathbb{P}(Z>tx)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

if and only if
Z
is subexponential, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(Z^{(1)}+Z^{(2)}>t)\sim 2\,\mathbb{P}(Z>t)
as
t\to\infty
;
Chistyakov 1964
proved this for non-negative random variables, later extended to
\mathbb{R}
by
Willekens 1986
. This phenomenon of “one large jump” exhibited in (
1.1
) is so called because a high threshold-crossing of the sum of a set of random variables occurs with the same asymptotic probability as any one of them crossing the same threshold.
Recall that a random variable
Z
has a regularly varying right tail if for
x>0
, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z>tx)/\mathbb{P}(Z>t)=x^{-\alpha}
for some
-\alpha<0
, which is called the index of regular variation or tail index. We write
Z\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
. Regularly varying distributions are subexponential as well (cf.
[
43
]
), i.e, and hence (
1.1
) holds when
Z\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
.

In a multivariate context, an
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
-valued random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}
is multivariate regularly varying (MRV) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
, if there exists a function
b(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, and a non-null measure
\mu
in the set of all Borel measures in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
which are finite on sets bounded away from
\boldsymbol{0}
such that

t\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}/b(t)\in A)\to\mu(A)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty

for Borel sets
A
bounded away from
\boldsymbol{0}
with
\mu(\partial A)=0
; see Section
2
for details. In particular, we have
\mu(tA)=t^{-\alpha}\mu(A)
for some
\alpha>0
and write
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
. Now, for i.i.d. non-negative random vectors
\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
with
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
, we can deduce that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,n\mu,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})

(cf.
[
38
, Proposition 4.1]
,
[
39
, Section 7.3]
and
[
25
, Lemma 3.11]
). Hence, for Borel sets
A
bounded away from
\boldsymbol{0}
with
\mu(\partial A)=0
and fixed
n\geq 1
we may approximate

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)\sim\frac{n}{b^{\leftarrow}(t)}\,\mu(A)\sim n\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}\in tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

if
\mu(A)>0
. Thus, (
1.3
) extends (
1.1
) to higher dimensions and the principle of “one large jump” appears to hold. Curiously though, one often encounters examples where for a large class of sets
A
, the value of
\mu(A)
is equal to zero and in this case,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}b^{\leftarrow}(t)\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)=0=\lim_{t\to\infty}b^{\leftarrow}(t)\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}\in tA).

This makes (
1.3
) of limited practical use. For example, if the elements of
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
are themselves i.i.d. (with regularly varying marginal tail distributions) and
we consider sets

(1.5)

\displaystyle A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in S\},

for indices
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
|S|\geq 2
,
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
, then for
\boldsymbol{Z}\in tA
to hold, at least two components of
\boldsymbol{Z}
need to be large together and under classical MRV assumptions we have
\mu(A)=0
verifying (
1.4
) (cf.
Example

2.13
). In fact, the components of
\boldsymbol{Z}
need not to be independent at all, a Gaussian dependence among variables with tail equivalent regularly varying marginal distributions and pairwise correlations less than one will ensure
\mu(A)=0
; see
Section

2.4
for further examples.

In such a case, we do observe that a notion subtler than the well-known “one large jump” phenomenon holds depending on the joint dependence of the underlying random vector and on the type of tail set
A
considered. If multivariate regular variation holds on relevant subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
we realize that in many scenarios,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)\sim{\frac{n}{\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)}\,\widetilde{\mu}(A)}\sim n\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}\in tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where
0<\widetilde{\mu}(A)<\infty
and
\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)
is a function satisfying
\lim_{t\to\infty}b^{\leftarrow}(t)/\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)=0
.
This means that the left and the right hand side of (
1.3
) are asymptotically equivalent, even though the term in the middle contains
\mu(A)=0
. The relevant results and examples where we observe (
1.6
) are detailed in
Section

3.1
. In particular, the results hold for more general sets than the ones given in (
1.5
), and we can specify
\widetilde{\mu}(A)
and
\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)
. Formally, we show that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
is multivariate regularly varying on subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
,
obeying (
1.2
) but with
\alpha,b,\mu
replaced by
\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{b},\widetilde{\mu}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
replaced by an appropriate subcone.
Note that (
1.6
) hints at connections to the notion of multivariate subexponentiality (cf.
[
7
,
36
,
40
]
).

Another phenomenon investigated for sets
A
as defined in (
1.5
) is that the tail event
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA
may be determined by threshold crossings in different co-ordinates of
S
, by different variables
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
. Thus, an aggregation of random vectors leads to a tail event with a “few large jumps” and these jumps occur either together in one random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
, or separately in a few different vectors; and for
A
as defined in (
1.5
) with
|S|=i
, we get

\displaystyle(b^{\leftarrow}(t))^{i}\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)

\displaystyle\sim C_{n,i}\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}^{-\alpha}\sim C_{n,i}\prod_{j\in S}b^{\leftarrow}(t)\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}>tx_{j})

as
t\to\infty
(cf.
Remark

3.13
). Thus,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)\sim C_{n,i}\prod_{j\in S}\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}\in tx_{j})\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where
C_{n,i}>0
is not necessarily equal to
n
, and depends on the number of summands
n
, the index
i=|S|
which represents the type of set
A
, as well as the distribution of
\boldsymbol{Z}
; the associated results are discussed in
Proposition

3.3
(
Section

3.1
) and
Section

3.2
. We also notice that this phenomenon is often observed under the more general assumption of adapted-MRV (cf.
Definition

2.10
) for the underlying random vectors (cf.
Section

3.2
). Since
C_{n,i}\not=n
in this example, it is no surprise that the limit measure observed in aggregating adapted-MRV is not linear in
n
anymore, in contrast to (
1.2
).

One of our primary interests is to characterize the asymptotic behavior of multi-dimensional regularly varying Lévy processes which have inherent applications to stochastic storage processes including insurance claims, inventory management, and more (cf.
[
37
,
2
]
). This also happens to be a natural progression from computing tail probabilities of finite aggregation of random vectors. A Lévy process
\boldsymbol{L}=(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
,
is a stochastic process with
\boldsymbol{L}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, has stationary and independent increments, and has càdlàg sample paths (cf.
[
42
]
). Consequently,
\boldsymbol{L}(s)
is infinitely divisible and has
the same distribution as sums of i.i.d. random vectors; following the basic premise of this paper. A Lévy process
\boldsymbol{L}
is characterized by its Lévy measure
\Pi(A)
, which measures the expected
number of jumps of
\boldsymbol{L}
in
[0,1]
whose jump sizes are in
A
(cf.
Section

5
).
The principle of one large jump is illustrated for multivariate regularly varying Lévy processes by
Hult and
Lindskog 2005
,
Hult and
Lindskog 2006a
and the asymptotic rates of further hidden jumps have been characterized by
Lindskog, Resnick and
Roy 2014
(for the univariate case).
Our work addresses the case where the results of
[
22
]
hold, including and specifically addressing cases with negligible probability approximation for the tail event.
In particular, a conclusion of
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
is that

\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
if and only if

\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\backslash\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
and then, for any Borel set
A
bounded away from
\boldsymbol{0}
with
\mu(\partial A)=0
we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim\frac{s}{b^{\leftarrow}(t)}\,\mu(A)\sim s\,\Pi(tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

if
\mu(A)>0
(see
[
15
]
for the one dimensional case). Naturally, if
\mu(A)=0
then (
1.8
) gives a zero estimate. For example, this happens if we consider
\boldsymbol{L}
to be comprised of
d
i.i.d. one-dimensional regularly varying Lévy processes and
A
is defined as in (
1.5
). Under quite
general conditions, if the Lévy measure is multivariate regular varying on relevant subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, we
show
\boldsymbol{L}(s)
is multivariate regularly varying on that subcone as well.
As a consequence under these conditions, we observe that,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim s\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in tA)\sim\frac{s}{\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)}\,\widetilde{\mu}(A)\sim s\,\Pi(tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

for sets
A
in appropriate subcones; here the function
\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)
satisfies
\lim_{t\to\infty}b^{\leftarrow}(t)/\widetilde{b}^{\leftarrow}(t)=0
and in contrast to (
1.8
) where
\mu(A)=0
, here we have
0<\widetilde{\mu}(A)<\infty
. However, we also find cases where the asymptotics are different and we observe

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim\phi(s)\,\Pi(tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty,

where
\phi
is a function for which
\phi(s)=s
may not hold, defying the linearity property often observed for Lévy processes, cf. (
1.9
).
These results on the tail probabilities of heavy-tailed Lévy processes have obvious implications on risk and ruin problems, especially in the context of insurance and finance, and is addressed in an associated article
[
10
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we provide necessary preliminary results and background for our work; first we discuss the basic framework in terms of multivariate regular variation on subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
using
\mathbb{M}
-convergence. We also discuss copulas and survival copulas used to model dependence in the said random vectors.
Our main result,
Theorem

3.1
, appears in
Section

3
which is used to obtain results on “one or few large jumps” of the form (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) under a variety of assumptions. In particular, we show
how multivariate regular variation of two independent random vectors on a subcone of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is used to obtain multivariate regular variation of their
n
-convolution. Our results are complemented with examples where they can be applied, especially for the convolution of finitely many i.i.d. random vectors. In Section
4
, the results on finite convolutions are extended to random sums and finally applied to assess the tail behavior in regularly varying Lévy processes in Section
5
. The proofs in the paper are relegated to the appendices.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we discuss the framework for assessing probabilities of tail events where joint thresholds may be crossed; we briefly recall the theory of
\mathbb{M}
-convergence used to define multivariate regular variation on subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}=[0,\infty)^{d}
(cf.
[
32
,
9
]
). Furthermore, we characterize convergence for different types of tail sets in
Proposition

2.7
. The notion of multivariate regular variation on different cones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is extended in
Definition

2.10
to allow for a broader class of models and examples. This allows for a framework where the sum of two random vectors can be MRV on a specific subcone even if neither of the two summands is MRV on that subcone. In
Section

2.4
, we provide examples of joint distributions where our framework is useful, we use copulas to represent joint dependence. This allows us to illustrate our results in a variety of examples.

Unless otherwise specified, all random vectors are assumed to lie on the positive quadrant
\mathbb{E}_{d}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. Notationally, vector operations are understood component-wise, e.g., for vectors
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
,
\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{z}
means
x_{i}\leq z_{i}
for all
i
. Moreover, for a constant
t>0
and a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, we denote by
tA:=\{t\boldsymbol{z}:\boldsymbol{z}\in A\}
.

2.1
Regular variation

The theory of regular variation provides a systematic framework to discuss heavy-tailed distributions; see
Bingham, Goldie and
Teugels 1989
,
Resnick 2007
for details. Here we briefly discuss regular varying functions and multivariate regular variation of measures and random vectors on Euclidean cones with
\mathbb{M}
-convergence.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
regularly varying
at infinity if for all
x>0
, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(tx)/f(t)=x^{\beta}

for some fixed
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We write
f\in\mathcal{RV}_{\beta}
; and if
\beta=0
, then the function
f
is called
slowly varying
. A real-valued random variable
Z
with distribution function
F
, denoted by
Z\sim F
, is regularly varying (at
+\infty
) if
\overline{F}:=1-F\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Equivalently,
Z\sim F
is regularly varying with index
-\alpha<0
if there exists a measurable function
b:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
b(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
such that

t\,\mathbb{P}(Z>b(t)x)=t\,\overline{F}(b(t)x)\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}x^{-\alpha}\quad\forall\,x>0.

We write
\overline{F}\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}(b)
. As a consequence
b(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
and a canonical choice for
b
is

b(t)=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/t)=\overline{F}^{\leftarrow}(1/t)
where
F^{\leftarrow}(x)=\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}:F(y)\geq x\}
.

Measures
\mu
and
\nu
defined on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
are
(right) tail equivalent
if

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mu((t,\infty))}{\nu((t,\infty))}=c,

for some
c>0
.
Naturally, the same holds for probability measures and hence,
distribution functions
F
and
G
are
(right) tail equivalent
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\overline{F}(t)}{\overline{G}(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1-{F}(t)}{1-{G}(t)}=c,

for some
c>0
. We call measures
\mu
and
\nu
(respectively distributions
F
and
G
)
completely
tail equivalent if (
2.1
) (respectively (
2.2
)) holds with
c=1
. We often assume that components of the random vectors considered in this paper are tail equivalent (if not identically distributed, or completely tail equivalent).

We discuss (multivariate) regular variation on Euclidean subspaces of
\mathbb{E}_{d}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
using
\mathbb{M}
-convergence of measures which differs from vague convergence, the traditional notion used for multivariate regular variation. See
[
23
,
32
,
9
]
for further details and the preference for this notion over vague convergence; moreover see
[
3
]
for a broader notion of vague convergence.

Consider the space
\mathbb{E}_{d}
endowed with the sup-norm metric
d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=||\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}||_{\infty}
. A
cone

\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{E}_{d}
is a set which is closed under scalar multiplication: if
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}
then
t\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}
for
t>0
; a
closed cone
is a cone which is a closed set in
\mathbb{E}_{d}
.
Regular variation is defined using
\mathbb{M}
-convergence on a closed
cone
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{E}_{d}
with a closed cone
\mathbb{C}_{0}\subset\mathbb{C}
deleted. We say that a subset
A\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
is
bounded away from

\mathbb{C}_{0}
if
d(A,\mathbb{C}_{0})=\inf\{d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):\boldsymbol{x}\in A,\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{C}_{0}\}>0
. Denote by
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
, the class of Borel measures on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
assigning finite measures to all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
, which are bounded away from
\mathbb{C}_{0}
. We often refer to a subspace of the closed cone
\mathbb{E}_{d}
which is a cone, as a
subcone
.

Let
\mathbb{C}_{0}\subset\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
be closed cones containing
\boldsymbol{0}
. We define
\mathbb{M}
-convergence first and subsequently use it to define regular variation on
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
.

Definition 2.1

(
\mathbb{M}
-convergence)
.

Let
\mu,\mu_{n},n\geq 1
be Borel measures on
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
. Suppose
\int f\,d\mu_{n}\to\int f\,d\mu
as
n\to\infty
for any bounded, continuous, real-valued function
f
whose support is bounded away from
\mathbb{C}_{0}
, then we say
\mu_{n}
converges to
\mu
in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
, and write
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{C}_{0})
.

Next we define regular variation of measures on
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
which is an extension of the definition found in
[
23
]
for measures in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\})
.

Definition 2.2

(Regular variation of measures)
.

A Borel measure
\Pi
on
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
is regularly varying on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
if there exists
a regularly varying function
b(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, called the
scaling
function
and a non-null (Borel) measure
\mu(\cdot)\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
called the
limit or tail measure
such that as
t\to\infty
,

(2.3)

t\,\Pi(b(t)\,\cdot\,)\to\mu(\cdot),

in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
. We write
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
.

The next definition is a modification of
2.2
for multivariate probability measures and hence, random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.

Definition 2.3

(Multivariate regular variation)
.

A random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is multivariate regularly varying on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
if there exists a regularly varying function

b(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
,
\alpha>0
, and a non-null (Borel) measure
\mu(\cdot)\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})

such that as
t\to\infty
,

(2.4)

t\,\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{Z}/b(t)\in\,\cdot\,\right)\to\mu(\cdot),

in
\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
. We write
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
and one or more parameters are often dropped according to convenience.

Remark 2.4
.

In both Definitions
2.2
and
2.3
, since
b(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
,
we observe that the limit measure
\mu(\cdot)
has the scaling property

\mu(t\;\cdot\,)=t^{-\alpha}\mu(\,\cdot\,)
for
t>0
. Hence, if the measure or the random vector is
\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0})
, we often refer to
-\alpha<0
as its tail index (in the subspace
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{C}_{0}
).

2.2
Regular variation on co-ordinate subcones of the positive quadrant

Equipped with the notion of
\mathbb{M}
-convergence and regular variation, we proceed to discuss regular variation on a particular set of subcones of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and also provide equivalent conditions for the same (cf.
[
34
]
,
[
9
, Section 2]
). For
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
write
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
and denote the (decreasing) order statistics of
\boldsymbol{z}
by
z_{(1)}\geq z_{(2)}\geq\ldots\geq z_{(d)}
. For
0\leq i\leq d-1
let

\displaystyle\mathbb{CA}_{d}^{(i)}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{d-i}\leq d}\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:z_{j_{1}}=0,\ldots,z_{j_{d-i}}=0\}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:z_{(i+1)}=0\},\quad

and
\mathbb{CA}_{d}^{(d)}:=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:z_{(d)}>0\}
. For any
i=1,\ldots,d
, the closed cone
\mathbb{CA}^{(i)}_{d}
represents the union of all
i
-dimensional co-ordinate hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.
Define the following sequence of subcones of
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
where we investigate regular variation (when it exists):

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(i)}_{d}:=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{CA}^{(i-1)}_{d}=\;\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:z_{(i)}>0\},\quad\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

We call the subsets
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}

co-ordinate subcones
since they are cones obtained from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
by removing particular co-ordinate hyperplanes. Here
\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{d}
is the positive quadrant with
\{\boldsymbol{0}\}=\mathbb{CA}^{(0)}_{d}
removed,
\mathbb{E}^{(2)}_{d}
is the positive quadrant with all one-dimensional co-ordinate axes removed,
\mathbb{E}^{(3)}_{d}
is the positive quadrant with all two-dimensional co-ordinate hyperplanes removed, and so on. Clearly,

\mathbb{E}^{(1)}_{d}\supset\mathbb{E}^{(2)}_{d}\supset\ldots\supset\mathbb{E}^{(d)}_{d}=\mathbb{CA}_{d}^{(d)}.

Remark 2.5

(Asymptotic tail independence and hidden regular variation)
.

Suppose a random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
admits regular variation on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
with
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
and
\mu_{i}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
. We interpret this to be
asymptotic tail independence at level
i
, meaning the probability with which
i+1
or more components are simultaneously large, is negligible in comparison to the probability with which
i
(or fewer) components are simultaneously large. Suppose for some
j>i\geq 1
we have
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{j},b_{j},\mu_{j},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(j)})
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}({b_{i}(t)}/{b_{j}(t)})=\infty
then we say
\boldsymbol{Z}
exhibits
hidden regular variation
(HRV) on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(j)}
with respect to MRV on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
.

We investigate regular variation on cones of the form
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
, since joint exceedances often occur in tail subsets of such cones. A recipe for seeking (hidden) regular variation on such subsets have been discussed in Section 2.1 of
[
9
]
and we do not repeat the steps here. In the rest of this section, we characterize a particular family of sets
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
(defined in (
2.7
) below), proving that it is an
\mathbb{M}
-convergence determining class on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
. The particular tail sets appear in multivariate risk and reliability problems where the quantity of interest is a finite or a random sum of identically distributed vectors.

Let
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}=:\mathbb{I}
,
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and we denote its induced
\sigma
-algebra by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(i)}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})=\{A\in\mathcal{B}:A\subseteq\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}\}.

A
rectangular set
in
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
is defined as any set
A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}
where
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
,
|S|\geq i
and
x_{j}>0\;\forall\,j\in S
. Let us denote the collection

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{(i)}:=\{A\in\mathcal{B}^{(i)}:A\;\text{is a rectangular set in }\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}\}.

Lemma 2.6
.

\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
is a
\pi
-system and
\sigma(\mathcal{R}^{(i)})=\mathcal{B}^{(i)}
.

The proofs of
Lemma

2.6
as well as other subsequent results in this section are given in
Section

A
.
The following proposition shows that for verifying convergence of measures in
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
, we can restrict to testing convergence in sets belonging to
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
. The result and the proof are in the spirit of
[
39
, Lemma 6.1]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mu,\mu_{t}\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for all
t>0
and some fixed
i\in\mathbb{I}
. Then as
t\to\infty
,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mu_{t}\to\mu\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

if and only if

(2.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}(A)\to\mu(A),\quad\forall\;A\in\mathcal{R}^{(i)}

with
\mu(\partial A)=0
(
\mu
-continuity set), where
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
is the collection of sets as defined in (
2.7
).

Remark 2.8
.

In light of Proposition
2.7
, when we seek regular variation (or any measure convergence) in the space
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
using
\mathbb{M}
-convergence (as in Definition
2.3
), we can equivalently show this only for rectangular sets in
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
(which are also continuity sets with respect to the limit measure).

We wrap this section up with an extension of the so-called “heavier tail wins" phenomenon, in the context of multivariate regular variation on a subcone of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. It is useful for many of the proofs in this paper.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

for some fixed
i\in\mathbb{I}
, and
\boldsymbol{X}
is independent of the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued
random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}
with
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{\alpha_{i}+\gamma}<\infty

for some
\gamma>0
. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}).

2.3
A joint multivariate regular variation condition

Since our interest is in the tail behavior of aggregates over independent regularly varying vectors, when considering joint exceedances of sums of such vectors, regular variation on various combinations of subcones become important. Incidentally, in certain scenarios we encounter a sequence of random vectors, none of which possess MRV on a particular subcone, yet their sum admits MRV on the same subcone. The following definition provides conditions for such vectors to be tractable under aggregation in the multivariate regular variation framework.

Definition 2.10

(Adapted Multivariate Regular Variation)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
is a random vector such that the following holds:

1.

\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,\Delta
for some
\Delta\leq d
.

2.

If
\Delta<d
, then additionally assume that there exists a
\gamma\in(0,\alpha_{1}/d)
, such that for
i=\Delta+1,\ldots,d
and any
A\in\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
, we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t\,\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{Z}}{b_{i}(t)}\in\,A\right)=0

where
b_{i}(t):=t^{1/(i(\alpha_{1}+\gamma))}
, i.e., with the rate
b_{i}
, we have convergence to zero. We refer to (
2.10
) as
null convergence
and write
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{NC}(b_{i}(t),\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
.

Then we say
\boldsymbol{Z}
is
adapted multivariate regular varying
(or,
adapted-MRV
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and write

\{\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)});i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta\}
where
\alpha_{i}=\infty,\mu_{i}\equiv 0
for
i=\Delta+1,\ldots,d
.

Adapted-MRV is defined jointly for all cones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i=1,\ldots,d
. Note the following.

i)

For
i=1,\ldots,\Delta
,
\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
and
\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
are equivalent.

ii)

For
i=\Delta+1,\ldots,d
, the notation
\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
means that for some
0<\gamma<\alpha_{1}/d
, we have
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{NC}(b_{i}(t)=t^{1/(i(\alpha_{1}+\gamma))},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
and
\alpha_{i}=\infty,\mu_{i}\equiv 0
. The constant
\gamma
is chosen to be the same for all
i=\Delta+1,\ldots,d
and the value of
\Delta=\arg\max_{i}\{\alpha_{i}:\alpha_{i}<\infty\}
is implicit.

Example 2.11
.

Let
X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\sim F
be i.i.d random variables with
\mathbb{P}(X^{(1)}>x)=x^{-\alpha},x>1
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
B^{(1)},B^{(2)}
be i.i.d random variables with
\mathbb{P}(B^{(1)}=1)=0.5=\mathbb{P}(B^{(1)}=0)
. Define for
k=1,2
,

\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}:=B^{(k)}(X^{(k)},0)+(1-B^{(k)})(0,X^{(k)}).

Then
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{Z}^{(2)}
are i.i.d. with
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b(t)=t^{1/\alpha},\mu_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2}^{(1)})
where

\mu_{1}(([0,x_{1}]\times[0,x_{2}])^{c})=0.5x_{1}^{-\alpha}+0.5x_{2}^{-\alpha},\quad x_{1},x_{2}>0.

Clearly,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{NC}(b^{*}(t)=t^{1/2(\alpha+\gamma)},\mathbb{E}_{2}^{(2)})
for any
\gamma>0
. Hence,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
is adapted-MRV with
\Delta=1
. But we can check that
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b(t)=t^{1/\alpha},2\mu_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2}^{(1)})
and
\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}+\boldsymbol{Z}_{2}\in\mathcal{MRV}(2\alpha,b(t)=t^{1/(2\alpha)},\mu_{2},\mathbb{E}_{2}^{(2)})
where

\mu_{2}((x_{1},\infty)\times(x_{2},\infty))=0.5(x_{1}x_{2})^{-\alpha},\quad x_{1},x_{2}>0.

Remark 2.12
.

Many examples of regularly varying vectors on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)}
are in fact adapted-MRV, including all examples mentioned in
Section

2.4
and more. A few comments on how (
2.10
) enriches our class of models follows.

(a)

If
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, then clearly
\{\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)});i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta=d\}
. Thus, condition (
2.10
) provides us a little more flexibility in case we fail to have MRV on subcone
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(j)}
for
j>\Delta
for some
\Delta=2,\ldots,d
.

(b)

Condition (
2.10
) is satisfied if
\mathbb{E}(Z_{(i)})^{i(\alpha_{1}+\widetilde{\gamma})}<\infty
for some
\widetilde{\gamma}
with
\gamma<\widetilde{\gamma}<\alpha_{1}/d
; here
Z_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq Z_{(d)}
are the order statistics of the elements of
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
. In particular, one such example is when
Z_{(j)}=0
for
j>\Delta
, see
Section

3.2
. For further examples of multivariate heavy-tailed distributions exhibiting such a property see
[
12
,
8
]
.

(c)

If
\Delta<d
, then (
2.10
) still allows for MRV to hold on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i=\Delta+1,\ldots,d
albeit with a lighter regularly varying tail rate than
i(\alpha_{1}+\gamma)
.

2.4
Tail distributions, survival copulas and asymptotic tail independence

In this section we discuss dependence structures for
d
-dimensional random vectors, which are used to model risks, claim sizes, or increments in general. Following common practice we model the marginal distributions separately from the dependence structures and hence, resort to using
copulas
(cf.
[
26
,
35
]
). A key feature of most of the copulas we discuss is the presence of
asymptotic tail independence
, implying that the joint exceedance of a threshold by
i
components of the random vector occur at a rate negligible compared to joint exceedance of
(i-1)
components for some or all
i=2,\ldots,d
.

Furthermore, we will also elaborate on multivariate regular variation properties under these copulas. To this end, for all examples in this section, we consider random vectors
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
with identically distributed continuous marginal components with distribution function
F_{\alpha}
where
\overline{F}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>1
, and the dependence is given by the particular (survival) copula. Moreover, we fix
b_{\alpha}(t)=\overline{F}_{\alpha}^{\leftarrow}(1/t),t>1
. Note that assuming tail equivalent marginals would lead to similar conclusions but notations become cumbersome.

Our interest is in tail sets, hence we will often use
survival copulas
along with copulas which we recall briefly here. For a random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\sim F
with continuous marginal distributions
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
, the copula
C:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
and the survival copula
\widehat{C}:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
are distribution functions such that:

\displaystyle F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}\leq x_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}\leq x_{d})

\displaystyle=C(F_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(x_{d})),\;(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\overline{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}>x_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}>x_{d})

\displaystyle=\widehat{C}(\overline{F}_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,\overline{F}_{d}(x_{d})),\;(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\overline{F}_{j}=1-F_{j}\;\forall\,j\in\mathbb{I}.

Example 2.13

(Independence copula)
.

A widely used copula to exhibit asymptotic tail independence and hidden regular variation is the independence copula. The independence copula
{C}_{\perp}
and survival copula
\widehat{C}_{\perp}
are given by

(2.11)

\displaystyle{C}_{\perp}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=\widehat{C}_{\perp}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{d},\quad 0<u_{i}<1.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}\sim F
with identical (continuous) marginal
F_{\alpha}
as defined above and dependence given by
C_{\perp}
(or
\widehat{C}_{\perp}
). Then

(2.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(i\alpha,b_{\alpha}^{1/i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

where

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\right)=\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}^{-\alpha}

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|=i,i=1,\ldots,d
(cf.
[
9
]
) and
\mu_{i}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
. Clearly
\boldsymbol{Z}
exhibits hidden regular variation on all cones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i=2,\ldots,d
.

Example 2.14

(Marshall-Olkin copula)
.

In reliability theory, the Marshall-Olkin distribution provides an elegant mechanism to capture the dependence between the failure of subsystems in an entire system. We focus on a particular structure of the Marshall-Olkin survival copula as given in
[
30
, eq. (2.4), page 58]
. Assume that for all
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
there exists a parameter
\lambda_{S}>0
. Consider then the generalized Marshall-Olkin survival copula given by

(2.14)

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{MO}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{|S|=i}\bigwedge_{j\in S}u_{j}^{\eta_{j}^{S}},\quad 0<u_{j}<1,

where

(2.15)

\displaystyle\eta_{j}^{S}=\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\sum\limits_{J\supseteq\{j\}}\lambda_{J}},\quad j\in S\subseteq\mathbb{I}.

A typographical error in the formula for
\eta_{j}^{S}
in
[
30
, eq. (2.4), page 58]
is corrected in (
2.15
). We consider two particular choices of the parameters
\lambda_{S}
for our examples.

(a)

Equal parameter for all sets:
Let
\lambda_{S}=\lambda>0
for all
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
; hence, from (
2.15
) we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle\eta_{j}^{S}=2^{-(d-1)}=:\beta.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}>x_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}>x_{d})

\displaystyle=\widehat{C}(\overline{F}_{\alpha}(x_{1}),\ldots,\overline{F}_{\alpha}(x_{d}))

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{|S|=i}\bigwedge_{j\in S}(\overline{F}_{\alpha}(x_{j}))^{\beta}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\overline{F}_{\alpha}\left(x_{(j)})\right)^{2^{d-j}\beta}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\overline{F}_{\alpha}\left(x_{(j)})\right)^{2^{-(j-1)}},

where
x_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq x_{(d)}
denote the decreasing order statistics of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
. Now, we can check that for
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

where

(2.17)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=(2-2^{-(i-1)})\alpha,

\displaystyle b_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(b_{\alpha}(t))^{\alpha/\alpha_{i}}=(b_{\alpha}(t))^{1/(2-2^{-(i-1)})},

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{i}\left({{x}_{(j)}}\right)^{-\alpha 2^{-(j-1)}},

where
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
,
|S|=i
with
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
, and
{x}_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq{x}_{(i)}
denote the decreasing order statistics of
(x_{j})_{j\in S}

and
\mu_{i}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
.

(b)

Parameters proportional to cardinality of the sets:
Let
\lambda_{S}=|S|\,\lambda
where
\lambda>0
for all
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
. From (
2.15
) we have

\eta_{j}^{S}=|S|(d+1)2^{-(d-1)}=|S|(d+1)\beta
using the definition in (
2.16
). Following a similar logic as in part (a), we obtain in this case

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}>x_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}>x_{d})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\overline{F}_{\alpha}(x_{(j})\right)^{\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d+1}\right)2^{-(j-1)}}.

Again we can check that for
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}({\alpha}_{i}^{*},{b}_{i}^{*},{\mu}^{*}_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

where,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Big(1-\frac{j-1}{d+1}\Big)\frac{\alpha}{2^{j-1}}=\alpha_{i}\frac{d}{d+1}+\frac{i\alpha}{(d+1)2^{{i-1}}},

\displaystyle b_{i}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=(b_{\alpha}(t))^{\alpha/\alpha_{i}^{*}},

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,\ j\in S\}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{i}\left({x}_{(j)}\right)^{-\alpha\left(1-\frac{j-1}{d+1}\right)2^{-(j-1)}},

where
|S|=i
with
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S

and
\mu_{i}^{*}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
.

In both examples of the Marshall-Olkin copula dependence (with identical regularly varying margins),
\boldsymbol{Z}
exhibits hidden regular variation on all cones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i=2,\ldots,d
.

Example 2.15

(Archimedean copula (ACIG))
.

This
Archimedean copula example based on the Laplace transform of the inverse gamma distribution, called ACIG copula in short, appears in
[
19
]
with its hidden regular variation discussed in
[
20
, Example 4.4]
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
has an ACIG copula with dependence parameter

1<\beta<2
and identical margins
\overline{F}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
. Then

\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu_{1},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)})
and
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha\beta,b_{\alpha}^{1/\beta},\mu_{2},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=2,\ldots,d
.
In this particular example
\boldsymbol{Z}
exhibits hidden regular variation on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(2)}
but no further HRV at any subsequent co-ordinate subcone
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i\geq 3.

Example 2.16

(Asymptotically tail dependent copula)
.

In the previous examples we observed distributions with regularly varying marginals and copulas exhibiting
asymptotic tail independence
leading to MRV with different indices on different spaces. But there are distributions which exhibit so-called
asymptotic tail dependence
which would lead to MRV with the same index, rate function and limit measure on all subcones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
; see
[
18
]
for general examples in dimension
d=2
and
[
5
]
for higher dimensional Archimedean copulas exhibiting asymptotic tail dependence. We illustrate this with one example.
Let
\boldsymbol{Z}\sim F
such that for
\alpha>1
,

F(\boldsymbol{x})=1-\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{\alpha}\right)^{-1},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}.

We can check that the marginals are identically Pareto distributed with index
-\alpha
and hence, the tails are
\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b(t)=t^{1/\alpha},\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
where

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k_1<\ldots<k_j}{ k_1,\ldots, k_j \in S}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{j}x_{k_{l}}^{\alpha}\right)^{-1}

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
.

Remark 2.17

(Gaussian copula)
.

Gaussian copulas have been widely considered as a key example of asymptotic tail independence, for which coefficients of tail dependence, tail order and hidden regular variation have been studied in this context; see
[
29
,
20
,
17
]
. Surprisingly, the hidden regular variation properties of Gaussian copulas (with regularly varying marginal distributions) are not particularly well understood, especially in dimensions
d\geq 3
. For example, the often used Gaussian copula defined by an equi-correlation correlation matrix does not seem to admit hidden regular variation in general; see
[
11
]
for details. Thus, we have refrained from using particular examples of Gaussian copulas here.

3
Aggregating regularly varying random vectors

In
Section

1
, we discussed the principle of one large jump determining the behavior of aggregates of multivariate regularly varying random vectors in the classical framework; here we extend the idea for a more general class of tail events. We start by assuming that individual random vectors have tail equivalent margins and they admit adapted multivariate regular variation on cones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)},i=1,\ldots,d
(see Definition
2.10
). In our first result,
Theorem

3.1
, we consider two independent random vectors which are not necessarily identically distributed and assess the tail behavior of the sum for various tail sets. This theorem forms the basis of many subsequent results where we do assume the underlying vectors to be identically distributed as well.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{Z}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
be independent random vectors, each with tail equivalent marginal distributions and
\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i}^{(k)},b_{i}^{(k)},\mu_{i}^{(k)},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta_{k}\}
for
k=1,2
, i.e., they are adapted-MRV on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. Define
\alpha_{0}^{(k)}=0,\,b_{0}^{(k)\leftarrow}(t)\equiv 1,\,\mu_{0}^{(k)}\equiv 1
and

I(i):=\,\mathrm{argmax}_{j\in\{0,\ldots,i\}}\{\bar{c}_{j}^{(i)}:\bar{c}_{j}^{(i)}<\infty\}

where

\bar{c}_{j}^{(i)}:=\max_{0\leq m\leq i}\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{b_{j}^{(1)\leftarrow}(t)b_{i-j}^{(2)\leftarrow}(t)}{b_{m}^{(1)\leftarrow}(t)b_{i-m}^{(2)\leftarrow}(t)}\right\}.

Define for
i=1,\ldots,d
, and
m=0,\ldots,i
:

c_{m}^{I(i)}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{b_{I(i)}^{(1)\leftarrow}(t)b_{i-I(i)}^{(2)\leftarrow}(t)}{b_{m}^{(1)\leftarrow}(t)b_{i-m}^{(2)\leftarrow}(t)}.

Suppose that for
k=1,2,
and each
m=1,\ldots,i-1
, either

c_{m}^{I(i)}=0
or
\lim_{t\to\infty}b_{m}^{(k)\leftarrow}(t)/b_{m+1}^{(k)\leftarrow}(t)=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i}^{\oplus},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta^{\oplus}\}

with
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{I(i)}^{(1)}+\alpha_{i-I(i)}^{(2)},b_{i}^{\leftarrow}(t)=b_{I(i)}^{(1)\leftarrow}(t)b_{i-{I(i)}}^{(2)\leftarrow}(t),
and

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\oplus}(A)=\sum_{m=0}^{i}c_{m}^{I(i)}\mu_{m,i}^{*}(A)\quad\text{ for }A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),

where
\mu_{m,i}^{*}
is the measure which is uniquely defined on
\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
as follows: for
A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in S\}\in\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
with
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
,
|S|\geq i
and
x_{j}>0

\forall j\in S
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{m,i}^{*}\left(A\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S\cup\{\emptyset\}}{|J|=m}}\mu_{m}^{(1)}\Bigg(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(m)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in J\}\Bigg)\mu_{i-m}^{(2)}\,\Bigg(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i-m)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in S\backslash J\}\Bigg).

Moreover,
\Delta^{\oplus}\in\{\max(\Delta_{1}+1,\Delta_{2}+1),\ldots,\min(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2},d)\}
.

The proof of
Theorem

3.1
is given in
Section

B
.

Remark 3.2
.

Since the output of

may contain multiple elements,
I(i)
is not defined uniquely; hence, a value for

is often chosen from these outputs according to convenience.

We have refrained from stating a general result akin to
Theorem

3.1
for adding
n
random vectors since the parameters of the limit model become notationally cumbersome without providing additional insight; on the other hand, for a variety of joint dependence behavior, we often observe nicer structures appearing.
In the rest of the section, we discuss consequences of

on the finite sum of i.i.d. random vectors under various assumptions on their dependence structures.

3.1
All subcones exhibit regular variation

First, we investigate the case where we add i.i.d. random vectors which are multivariate regularly varying on all relevant cones. The results as we will see are direct consequences of
Theorem

3.1
. We begin with the well-known model where all components of each vector are i.i.d. random variables as well;
Example

2.13
gives the structure of the limit measure in this case. The following proposition provides a slightly general version of this case. Proofs of the results of this subsection are available in
Section

C
.

Proposition 3.3
(Nearly independent case).

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with tail equivalent marginal distributions and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
where
b_{i}(t)=(b_{1}(t))^{1/i}
and
b_{1}(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
. Then
\alpha_{i}=i\alpha
and

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

Now if for some
\kappa_{j}>0
,
j=1,\ldots,d
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\right)=\prod_{j\in S}\kappa_{j}x_{j}^{-\alpha}

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|=i
,
x_{j}>0
and
\mu_{i}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(i\alpha,b_{1}^{1/i},n^{i}\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

Remark 3.4
.

If all components of the random vectors
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
are completely tail equivalent then
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\ldots=\kappa_{d}
.

Although condition (
3.2
) is rather restrictive, the result obtained in (
3.1
), i.e., if
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
then
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
, holds under much weaker assumptions. The particular assumption (
3.2
)
helps only to calculate the exact form of the limit distribution. The following result provides a further case and helps in creating many examples.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with tail equivalent marginal distributions and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
.
Moreover, assume that
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for all
m=1,\ldots,i-1
and
i=2,\ldots,d
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},n\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

Remark 3.6
.

Clearly, a sufficient condition for
Proposition

3.5
to hold would be to assume that
c_{m}^{I(i)}=0,m=1,\ldots,i-1
and
i=2,\ldots,d
instead of
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for all
m=1,\ldots,i-1
and
i=2,\ldots,d
. This requires the notation of
Theorem

3.1
, and we prefer the latter in lieu of interpretability.

Remark 3.7
.

Both in Propositions
3.3
and
3.5
, we observe that while adding finitely many random vectors
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
, we obtain
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
. The indices of regular variation
\alpha_{i}
and the scaling parameter
b_{i}
remain the same no matter how many vectors we add although the measure
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}
are quite different for different values of
n
. Note the following.

i)

Under the assumptions of
Proposition

3.3
, we have,
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for
m=0,\ldots,i
, and
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}=n^{i}\mu_{i}
. Interestingly,
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for
m=0,\ldots,i
does not necessarily imply that
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}=n^{i}\mu_{i}
.

ii)

Under the assumptions of
Proposition

3.5
, we have,
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for
m=1,\ldots,i-1
, which turns out to be a sufficient condition for
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}=n\mu_{i}
.

Example 3.8

(Marshall-Olkin dependence)
.

For this example let
\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with all marginal distributions following
F_{\alpha}
where
\overline{F}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
and
b_{\alpha}(t)=\overline{F}^{\leftarrow}(1/t)
for some
\alpha>0
. We consider two different Marshall-Olkin dependence parameters for
\boldsymbol{Z}
which has copula
\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{MO}}
given by (
2.14
); see
Example

2.14
.

(a)

Equal parameter:
Suppose the parameters defining the Marshall-Olkin copula are given by (
2.16
). Then from
Example

2.14
(a) we have
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for

where
\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i}
are given by (

). Now observe that for fixed

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}

\displaystyle\geq 2\alpha

(3.3)

\displaystyle>(2-2^{-(i-1)})\alpha=\alpha_{i}.

Hence, by
Proposition

3.5
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},n\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}).

(b)

Proportional parameter:
We know from

2.14
(b) that
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i}^{*},b_{i}^{*},\mu_{i}^{*},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i}^{*},b_{i}^{*},n\mu_{i}^{*},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}).

Example 3.9

(Archimedean copula)
.

Referring to
Example

2.15
, suppose we have i.i.d. random vectors
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
with identical margins

so that

The two extreme cases of dependence considered in general are the case of fully independent components for

, which is covered in

, and the case where the components of

are dependent such that

Corollary 3.10
(Dependent case, corollary to Proposition
3.5
).

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with tail equivalent marginal distributions
and
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

Example 3.11
.

Example

2.16
exhibiting asymptotic tail dependence admits the property
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
and hence, we can compute tail asymptotics of its convolution using

3.10
.

In all the propositions, corollaries and hence examples of this section, we observe that if
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
, then their finite sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},C_{n,i}\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

for some constant
C_{n,i}>0
. The “one large jump” phenomenon is observed here, in the sense of equivalence of the first and last expressions in (
1.3
) or (
1.6
) if

; cf.
Proposition

3.5
. But
C_{n,i}
is not necessarily

, as for example in
Proposition

3.3
, and this is a case which we think of as a phenomenon of “more than one large jump” or “a few large jumps”. Note that in both cases mentioned here, we did assume
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}
to have MRV on all cones, but not be strictly adapted-MRV. In
Section

3.2
, we illustrate that a similar principle holds, even under the assumption of adapted-MRV, although the characterizing jumps are now of the form (
1.7
) which relates to “a few large jumps” phenomenon.

3.2
Not all subcones necessarily exhibit regular variation

In certain contexts, we may be interested in adding random vectors which are not necessarily MRV on all relevant cones. For example, we may have a sequence of i.i.d. random vectors for which not all components are non-zero in each realization. An extension of such aggregation to random sums lead to general compound Poisson or Lévy processes, see
Section

4
for details. In this section, we concentrate on a few such examples. The general structure for the limit measures in such problems are often not quite apparent.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
be i.i.d. random vectors
with tail equivalent marginal distributions which are
\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
and let
\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta=1\}

with
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
. Then

\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i,n},b_{i,n},\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta=\min\{d,n\}\right\}.

Specifically, for
i=1,\ldots,d
we have the following:

(a)

For
n\geq i
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}\left(\alpha_{i,n}=i\alpha,b_{i,n}=b_{1}^{1/i},\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus},\,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}\right),

where

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}\Big(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\Big)=\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}\prod_{j\in S}\mu_{1}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\right),

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|=i
,
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
and
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
.

(b)

For
1\leq n<i
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{NC}\left(b_{i,n}(t)=t^{1/i(\alpha+\gamma)},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}\right).

The proof of
Proposition

3.12
is in
Section

D
.

Remark 3.13
.

In
Proposition

3.12
, if the marginal distributions are completely tail equivalent with distribution functions
F_{j},j=1,\ldots,d
and
b_{1}(t)=\overline{F}_{1}^{\leftarrow}(1/t)
, then
\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}
in (
3.5
) is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}\Big(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\,j\in S\}\Big)=\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}\prod_{j\in S}x_{j}^{-\alpha}.

Remark 3.14
.

For the conclusion of
Proposition

3.12
to hold,
the random variables
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{Z}^{(2)},\ldots
need not be identically distributed as long as they are independent and are all adapted-MRV with the same sets of parameters. The proof follows by similar arguments as the proof of
Proposition

3.12
and is skipped.

The phenomenon of a “few large jumps" holds here too, as illustrated next. Assume that in Proposition
3.12
, the marginal distributions are completely tail equivalent as in
Remark

3.13
; and
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})\sim\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}
. Without loss of generality let
tA
be the tail event of interest where
A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in\{1,\ldots,i\}\}
. Note that from (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we can infer that in fact as
t\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(b_{1}^{\leftarrow}(t)^{i})}\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}\prod_{j=1}^{i}x_{j}^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=C_{n,i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}\frac{x_{j}^{-\alpha}}{b_{1}^{\leftarrow}(t)}

\displaystyle\sim C_{n,i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}>tx_{i}),

where
C_{n,i}=(n!)/((n-i)!)
. Hence
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{Z}^{(n)}\in tA
occurs at the same rate with which
i
independent univariate marginals cross their respective thresholds, indicating
i
many large jumps. The constant
C_{n,i}
gives the number of possible choices of independent jumps, here the marginals jumps counted are all from different variables
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
.

In the rest of the section we provide examples exhibiting
Proposition

3.12
and its possible generalisation.

Example 3.15
.

Let
(X^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with distribution function
F
and
\overline{F}\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha},\alpha>0

(\boldsymbol{B}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. random vectors taking values in

is at the

-th place. Define

. Moreover, let
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an i.i.d. sequence of random vectors with

Example 3.16
.

The setting is similar to

. Let

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)},n\geq i
, by an
application of
Lemma

2.9
.

4
Random sums of regularly varying random vectors

A natural extension from aggregating finitely many random vectors is to aggregate randomly many random vectors, which we discuss in this section, finally leading towards an extension to Lévy processes in
Section

5
. We observed in
Section

3
that the behavior of the finite sum may take various forms even when they are multivariate regularly varying. Hence, for convenience, for the rest of the paper, we assume that the following is satisfied.

Assumption A
.

Let
\boldsymbol{Z},(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
. Assume that for all
i=1,\ldots,d
there exists a measurable function
f_{i}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a non-null measure
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i,n},b_{i,n},\mu_{i,n}^{\oplus}=f_{i}(n)\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)});i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta_{n}\right\}

and
\Delta_{n}=d
for
n\geq d
. Furthermore, assume that for all
i=1,\ldots,d
, there exist a finite constant
\alpha_{i}>0
and a regularly varying function
b_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha_{i}}
such that if
f_{i}(n)\not=0
we have
\alpha_{i,n}=\alpha_{i}
and
b_{i,n}=b_{i}
.

Remark 4.1
.

(a)

In general, the structure of the function
f_{i}
can be quite complex and often requires an involved combinatorial accounting procedure, see
Example

3.16
; nevertheless in
several examples we do observe that
f_{i}(n)=n
and in all our examples
0\leq f_{i}(n)\leq n^{i}
. Assumption
A
allows us the flexibility to not get involved in the computation of
f_{i}
.

(b)

For
\Delta_{n}<d
we have
f_{i}(n)=0
and
\alpha_{i,n}=\infty
for
i=\Delta_{n}+1,\ldots,d
, and hence, we have null convergence.
On the other hand, for
i=1,\ldots,\Delta_{n}
we have
0<f_{i}(n)<\infty
,
\alpha_{i,n}=\alpha_{i}
and
b_{i,n}=b_{i}
. For the examples considered in
Section

3.2
this happens to be the case.

(c)

Suppose
\Delta_{1}=d
, then Assumption
A
implies that
I(i)
as defined
in
Theorem

3.1
can be chosen to be
i
and hence,
\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}\geq\alpha_{i}
for every
m=0,\ldots,i
and
i=1,\ldots,d
.
On the other hand,
\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}>\alpha_{i}
for every
m=1,\ldots,i-1
is a sufficient condition for
I(i)=i
.

Remark 4.2
.

Under Assumption
A
, define
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\oplus,k)}:=\sum_{l=(k-1)d+1}^{kd}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(l)}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Also let
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\oplus}:=(Z_{1}^{\oplus},\ldots,Z_{d}^{\oplus})\sim\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\oplus,1)}
and
Z_{(1)}^{\oplus}\geq\ldots Z_{(d)}^{\oplus}
be the order statistics of
Z_{1}^{\oplus},\ldots,Z_{d}^{\oplus}
.

(a)

From Assumption
A
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\oplus,k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},f_{i}(dn)\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

with
0<f_{i}(dn)<\infty
for
i=1,\ldots,d
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, a consequence of
Remark

4.1
(c) is that
I(i)
(as defined
in
Theorem

3.1
) for the random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(\oplus,1)}
(or equivalently
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\oplus}
) is equal to
i
and hence,
\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}\geq\alpha_{i}
for every
m=0,\ldots,i
and
i=1,\ldots,d
.
Now,
I(i)=i
implies as well that there exists a finite constant
C^{*}>0
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle 0\leq\sup_{t>0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{m=0}^{i}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(m)}>t\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(i-m)}>t\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(i)}>t\right)}\leq C^{*}.

(b)

Note that the function

g_{i}(t):=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(i)}^{\oplus}>t\right)\in\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha_{i}}
for any
i\in\mathbb{I}
. Hence, using Potter’s bound
[
13
, Proposition B.1.19 (5)]
there exists a finite constant
C^{**}>0
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in\mathbb{I}}\sup_{t>0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(i)}>t/2\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(i)}>t\right)}\leq C^{**}.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let Assumption
A
hold and let the i.i.d. sequence

(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be independent of the
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-valued random variable
\tau
with
\mathbb{E}(\kappa^{\tau})<\infty
for any
\kappa>0
.
Then for
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mathbb{E}(f_{i}(\tau))\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}).

The proof is in
Section

E
.
Note that the examples considered in both
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
, all satisfy Assumption
A
(as well as (
4.1
)). Hence, for any
\mathbb{N}_{0}
-valued random variable
\tau
whose moment generating function exists on the positive real line, we can compute the tail probability of a random sum of
\tau
many i.i.d. MRV random vectors using
Theorem

4.3
.

5
Regular variation in multivariate Lévy processes

A particular example of a random sum of i.i.d. random vectors as indicated in
Theorem

4.3
is the compound Poisson process at a fixed time point where the number of summands
\tau

is Poisson distributed, which in turn is an example of a Lévy process. In this section, we investigate multivariate regular variation
of Lévy processes
\boldsymbol{L}=(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
on different subcones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, and relate it to multivariate regular variation of the Lévy measure
\Pi
on those subcones.
A Lévy process is
characterized by its
Lévy-Khinchine representation

\mathbb{E}(\text{e}^{i\langle\Theta,\boldsymbol{L}(s)\rangle})=\exp(-s\,\Psi(\Theta))

for
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where

\displaystyle\Psi(\Theta)=-i\langle\gamma,\Theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\Theta,\Sigma\,\Theta\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\text{e}^{i\langle\Theta,\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}+i\langle\boldsymbol{x},\Theta\rangle\right)\,\Pi(d\boldsymbol{x})

with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\Sigma
a non-negative definite
matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a Borel measure

\Pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, called the Lévy measure
which satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\min\{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2},1\}\,\Pi(d\,\boldsymbol{x})<\infty

and
\Pi(\boldsymbol{0})=0
. Moreover,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The Lévy measure
\Pi(A)
measures the expected number of jumps of the Lévy process in the interval
[0,1]
which lies in the set
A
. We denote by
\Pi_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
the marginal Lévy measures.
In this paper, we restrict to Lévy processes in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, i.e., the marginal Lévy processes are
subordinators
, which are increasing Lévy processes. For more details on Lévy processes see
[
41
,
1
]
.

Regular variation in multivariate Lévy processes, especially characterizing complex tail events, including but not restricted to (
1.5
), can happen in a variety of ways. We may observe regular variation for the Lévy process itself, or the Lévy measure, and they may have different implications depending on the dependence structure of the Lévy process. In the following three subsections we investigate this in detail; the proofs of the associated results are provided in
Section

F
.

5.1
The Lévy measure admits regular variation on all subcones

In the first subsection, we assume that the Lévy measure is multivariate regularly varying on all subcones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
and show that the same is true for the Lévy process, in fact, they are
tail equivalent
(in a multivariate sense) as we exhibit next. We understand (multivariate) tail equivalence as an extension of (
2.1
) to appropriate sets
A\in\mathcal{B}^{(i)}
. The result can be seen as an extension of (
1.8
) to subcones
(cf.
[
22
]
).

Proposition 5.1
(Extending
Proposition

3.5
).

Let
(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with Lévy measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
whose univariate marginal Lévy measures are tail equivalent.
Moreover assume that
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{m}+\alpha_{i-m}
for all
m=1,\ldots,i-1
and
i=2,\ldots,d
. For
s>0
we have then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},s\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

A direct consequence of
Proposition

5.1
is the tail equivalence of the Lévy measure of the set
tA
and the probability measure of the Lévy process belonging to
tA
, for Borel sets
A\in\mathcal{B}^{(i)}
bounded away from
\mathbb{CA}_{d}^{(i-1)}
with
\mu_{i}(A)>0
and
\mu_{i}(\partial A)=0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim s\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in tA)\sim s\,\Pi(tA)\sim\frac{s}{b_{i}^{\leftarrow}(t)}\,\mu_{i}(A)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty.

Although the tail equivalence of the Lévy process and the Lévy measure holds for a variety of sets, the tail rate differs depending on which subcone
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
the set
A
belongs to. A similar conclusion was shown in
[
22
]
, but only for sets
A
with
\mu_{1}(A)>0
. However, in many situations this is not the case as we see in the following examples.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{L}
be a compound Poisson process of the form
\boldsymbol{L}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{N(s)}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
where the jump sizes
(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. and independent of the Poisson process
(N(s))_{s\geq 0}
with intensity
\lambda>0
.
Suppose the jump size
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}
has identical marginals which have
\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
tail distributions with tail index
-\alpha<0
.

(a)

Let the dependence structure of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}

be modelled by a Marshall-Olkin copula with equal parameters as in
Example

3.8
(a), then

\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},s\lambda\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
with parameters
\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i}
given in (
2.17
).

(b)

Let the dependence structure of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}

be modelled by a Marshall-Olkin copula with proportional parameters
as in
Example

3.8
(b), then
\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i}^{*},b_{i}^{*},s\lambda\mu_{i}^{*},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})

with parameters
\alpha_{i}^{*},b_{i}^{*},\mu_{i}^{*}
given in (
2.18
).

(c)

Let the dependence structure of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(1)}

be modelled by an ACIG copula as in
Example

3.9
, then
\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},s\lambda\mu_{1},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)})
and
\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha\beta,b_{\alpha}^{1/\beta},s\lambda\mu_{2},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=2,\ldots,d
with parameters given in
Example

2.15
.

Example 5.3
.

Suppose
L_{\alpha}^{(j)}
,
j=1,2,3
are i.i.d. Lévy processes in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with Lévy measure
\Pi_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},(0,\infty))
,

L_{\beta}^{(j)}
,
j=1,2
are i.i.d. Lévy processes in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with Lévy measure
\Pi_{\beta}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\beta,b_{\beta},\mu_{\beta},(0,\infty))
and
L_{\gamma}
is a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with Lévy measure
\Pi_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\gamma,b_{\gamma},\mu_{\gamma},(0,\infty))
. A typical example for
L_{\alpha}

is an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with Lévy measure
\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}x)=\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Furthermore, assume all processes are independent and
\alpha<\beta<\gamma<2\alpha
. Then the 3-dimensional Lévy process

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(s)=(L_{\alpha}^{(1)}(s)+L_{\beta}^{(1)}(s)+L_{\gamma}(s),L_{\alpha}^{(2)}(s)+L_{\beta}^{(1)}(s)+L_{\gamma}(s),L_{\alpha}^{(3)}(s)+L_{\beta}^{(2)}(s)+L_{\gamma}(s)),

has Lévy measure

\displaystyle\Pi(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\Pi_{\alpha}(A_{j})+\Pi_{\beta}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})+\Pi_{\beta}(A_{3})+\Pi_{\gamma}(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}),

where
A_{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:(z,0,0)\in A\}
,

A_{2}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:(0,z,0)\in A\}
and

A_{3}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:(0,0,z)\in A\}
. Of course,
\Pi
satisfies the assumptions of
Proposition

5.1
with
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
,
(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})=(b_{\alpha},b_{\beta},b_{\gamma})
and

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mu_{\alpha}(A_{j}),\quad\mu_{2}(A)=\mu_{\beta}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{3}(A)=\mu_{\gamma}(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}).

Finally,
\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},s\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,2,3.

In each case for Examples
5.2
and
5.3
,
\boldsymbol{L}(s)
is MRV on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(2)}
but with different indices and hence,
\mu_{1}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(2)})=0
. This implies that the components of
\boldsymbol{L}(s)
are asymptotically tail independent.
In the special case where the components of
\boldsymbol{L}(s)
are (strongly) dependent, the next result follows directly from
Proposition

5.1
.

Corollary 5.4
(Extending
Corollary

3.10
: Dependent case).

Let
(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with Lévy measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d

whose univariate marginal Lévy measures are tail equivalent. Moreover,
(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})=(\alpha,b,\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,i^{*}
for some
i^{*}\leq d
.
Then for
s>0
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b,s\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,i^{*}.

Example 5.5
.

(a)

Completely dependent case: Let
L_{1}=(L_{1}(s))_{s\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with univariate marginal Lévy measure
\Pi_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},(0,\infty))
and
\boldsymbol{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{1})
. Then the Lévy measure of
\boldsymbol{L}
is given by

\displaystyle\Pi(A)=\min_{j\in S}\Pi_{\alpha}((x_{j},\infty))

for a rectangular set
A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in S\}
with

S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
and
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
.
In this case, we are in the setting of
Corollary

3.10
with
i^{*}=d
and
\mu(A)=\min_{j\in S}x_{j}^{-\alpha}
for
a rectangular set
A
as above.

More generally, if the marginal
tail Lévy measures are not necessarily identical but are completely tail equivalent satisfying
\Pi_{j}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},(0,\infty))

for
j=1,\ldots,d

and

\displaystyle\Pi(A)=\min_{j\in S}\Pi_{j}(x_{j},\infty)

then
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
as well and the assumptions of
Corollary

5.4
are satisfied. Indeed, this is a Lévy measure, it is constructed by the complete dependence Lévy copula (cf.
[
27
]
).

(b)

Suppose
L_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
are Lévy processes in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with univariate completely tail equivalent marginal Lévy measures
\Pi_{j}\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},(0,\infty))

and
\boldsymbol{L}(s)=(L_{1}(s),\ldots,L_{d}(s))
is a
d
-dimensional
Lévy process with Lévy measure

\displaystyle\Pi(A)=\left(\sum_{j\in S}\left(\Pi_{j}((x_{j},\infty))\right)^{-\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta}}

for some
\theta>0
, where
A=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:z_{j}>x_{j}\;\forall\;j\in S\}
is a rectangular set with

S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
and
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
.
This Lévy measure is constructed using the Clayton Lévy copula (cf.
[
27
]
). Let the measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{B}^{(1)}
be defined as

\displaystyle\mu(A)=\left(\sum_{j\in S}x_{j}^{-\alpha\theta}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}

for a rectangular set
A
as above. Then
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d

and hence, due to
Corollary

5.4
,

\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha,b_{\alpha},s\mu,\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
as well.

(c)

Another (dependent) example of a Lévy process can be constructed by a compound Poisson process where the jumps sizes have the distribution
F
as in
Example

2.16
.

Remark 5.6
.

Regular variation of the Lévy measure on different subcones
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}
can be related to regular variation of the Lévy copula and Pareto Lévy copula, respectively on these different subcones; cf.
[
28
,
14
]
for classical regular variation of such Lévy measure on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)}
. This work is under investigation by the authors.

5.2
The Lévy process is asymptotically tail independent

In
Proposition

5.1
and subsequently
Corollary

5.4
, the underlying Lévy measure admits regular variation on all relevant subcones; but this may not necessarily be the case in general.
The next result includes the cases where the Lévy measure is adapted multivariate regularly varying; i.e., MRV need not exist in all the relevant subcones.

Proposition 5.7
(Extending
Proposition

3.12
).

Let
(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with
Lévy measure
\Pi
such that
\{\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}^{*}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}),i=1,\ldots,d;\Delta=1\}
and
\Pi
has tail equivalent univariate marginal Lévy measures in
\mathcal{RV}_{-\alpha}
. Then for
s>0
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(i\alpha_{1},b_{1}^{1/i},s^{i}\mu_{i}^{L},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

with

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{L}\Big(\bigcap_{j\in S}\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\Big)=\prod_{j\in S}\mu_{1}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\right).

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|=i
,
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
and
\mu_{i}^{L}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
.

Interestingly, for rectangular sets
A\in\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
as in (
1.5
) with
|S|=i
, now we observe that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim s^{i}\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in tA)\quad\text{ as }t\to\infty.

Hence, the linearity property of
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in tA)\sim s\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in tA)
as
t\to\infty
, which we had noticed in the dependent cases of

Proposition

5.1
and
Corollary

5.4
respectively, vanishes here making this an unusual phenomenon for Lévy processes. Moreover, although
\Pi
is MRV on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)}
, for sets
A\in\mathcal{R}^{(2)}
the tail measures

\Pi(tA)
and
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{L}(1)\in tA)
are not tail equivalent anymore, in contrast to the common wisdom
for regular variation of Lévy processes on
\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(1)}
.

Example 5.8
.

(a)

Suppose the marginal Lévy processes
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
of
\boldsymbol{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})

are independent with tail equivalent univariate marginal Lévy measures
\Pi_{j}
which are regularly varying with tail index
-\alpha<0
. Then the Lévy measure of
\boldsymbol{L}
is

\displaystyle\Pi(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Pi_{j}(A_{j})

for
A_{j}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:(0,\ldots,0,z,0,\ldots,0)\in A\}
, where
z
appears in the
j
-th coordinate. This Lévy measure
has mass only on the co-ordinate axes. Hence, the assumptions of
Proposition

5.7
are satisfied and it can be applied to show MRV of
\boldsymbol{L}
on various subcones. In particular, it satisfies (
5.2
).

(b)

A compound Poisson process as defined in
Example

5.2
with jumps sizes
(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
as in
Example

3.15
would also satisfy the assumptions of
Proposition

5.7
, providing an example for the same.

5.3
The Lévy measure is asymptotically tail independent

The next proposition covers the case of a compound Poisson process, as a special Lévy process, where the marginal distribution of the jump sizes
are independent as well. The observed phenomena is again different from
Proposition

5.7
, which covers a compound Poisson process with independent marginal Lévy processes.

Proposition 5.9
(Extending
Proposition

3.3
).

Let
(\boldsymbol{L}(s))_{s\geq 0}
be a Lévy process in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with Lévy measure
\Pi\in\mathcal{MRV}(\alpha_{i},b_{i},\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})
for
i=1,\ldots,d

where
b_{i}(t)=(b_{1}(t))^{1/i}
,
b_{1}(t)\in\mathcal{RV}_{1/\alpha}
and

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\right)=\prod_{j\in S}\kappa_{j}x_{j}^{\alpha}

for
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|=i
,
x_{j}>0
for
j\in S
and
\mu_{i}(\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i+1)})=0
,
i=1,\ldots,d
,
and
\Pi
has tail equivalent univariate marginal Lévy measures. Let
(N^{*}(s))_{s\geq 0}
denote a Poisson
process with intensity 1.
Then for
s>0
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}(s)\in\mathcal{MRV}(i\alpha,b_{1}^{1/i},\mathbb{E}(N^{*}(s)^{i})\mu_{i},\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)})\quad\text{for }\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

As a consequence of
Proposition

5.9
, for any rectangular set
A\in\mathcal{R}^{(i)}
as in (
1.5
) with

\mu_{i_{0}-1,n_{0}}^{\oplus},\mu_{i_{0},n_{0}}^{\oplus},\mu_{1}
are obtained from our assumptions and induction hypothesis. Now,

\displaystyle\mu_{i_{0},n_{0}+1}^{\oplus}(A)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i_{0}-1,i_{0},n_{0}+1}(A)+\mu_{i_{0},i_{0},n_{0}+1}(A)

\displaystyle=\left[i_{0}\cdot\frac{n_{0}!}{(n_{0}-i_{0}+1)!}+\frac{n_{0}!}{(n_{0}-i_{0})!}\right]\prod_{j\in S}\mu_{1}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{0}+1)!}{(n_{0}+1-i_{0})!}\prod_{j\in S}\mu_{1}\left(\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{E}_{d}^{(i)}:z_{j}>x_{j}\}\right).

Hence, (
3.4
) holds for
i=i_{0}
and
n=n_{0}+1
, thus by induction it holds for all
n\geq i_{0}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem

For the proof we require some auxiliary results.

Lemma E.1
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

4.3
hold. Define
\boldsymbol{Z}^{\oplus}:=(Z^{\oplus}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\oplus}_{d}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}

and denote by

Z^{\oplus}_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq Z^{\oplus}_{(d)}

the order statistics of

Z^{\oplus}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\oplus}_{d}
. Also let
Z_{(1)}^{(k)}\geq\ldots Z_{(d)}^{(k)})
be the order statistics of the elements of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}=(Z_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,Z_{d}^{(k)})
for any
k\geq 1
. Furthermore,
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,\ldots,d
define

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,n}:=\sup_{S\subseteq\mathbb{I},\,|S|\leq i}\sup_{t>0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t\right\}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\oplus}_{(|S|)}>t\right)}.

Then there exists a finite constant
K_{i}>0

such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,n+1}\leq K_{i}^{n}.

For one-dimensional random variables with
i=d=1
, a stronger result holds: for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a constant
K>0
such that the left hand side is bounded by
K(1+\epsilon)^{n}
(cf.
[
16
, Lemma 1.3.5]
).

First, we show recursively that for any
i\in\mathbb{I}
there exists a constant
K_{i}>0

such that
\alpha_{i,n+1}\leq K_{i}\alpha_{i,n}
for any
n\geq d
.
Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{I}
with
|S|\leq i
and
n\geq d
. Then

(E.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in J}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in S\backslash J}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}\leq t/2\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}\leq t/2\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle J_{n,1}(t,S)+J_{n,2}(t,S).

We investigate the two terms separately. First,

\displaystyle J_{n,1}(t,S)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S\backslash J}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in J}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\sum_{K\subseteq S\backslash J\cup\{\emptyset\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S\backslash(J\cup K)}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in J}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in K}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\sum_{K\subseteq S\backslash J\cup\{\emptyset\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S\backslash(J\cup K)}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in J\cup K}\left\{Z^{(n+1)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\right).

Since the set
S\backslash J\cup K
has at most
i-1
elements and by definition
\alpha_{i-1,n}\leq\alpha_{i,n}
, we have that

\displaystyle J_{n,1}(t,S)\leq\alpha_{i,n}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\sum_{K\subseteq S\backslash J\cup\{\emptyset\}}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(|S\backslash(J\cup K)|)}^{\oplus}>t/2\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(|J\cup K|)}^{(n+1)}>t/2\right).

Now applying (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) we have

(E.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i }}\sup_{t>0}\frac{J_{n,1}(t,S)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(|S|)}^{\oplus}>t\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,n}\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i }}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{J\subseteq S}{J\not=\emptyset}}\sum_{K\subseteq S\backslash J\cup\{\emptyset\}}C^{*}C^{**}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,n}2^{2i}C^{*}C^{**}=\alpha_{i,n}\widetilde{K}_{i}

with
\widetilde{K}_{i}:=2^{2i}C^{*}C^{**}
.
Next, for the second term in (
E.1
) and
S=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{i}\}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i }}\sup_{t>0}\frac{J_{n,2}(t,S)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{|S|}^{\oplus}>t\right)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i }}\sup_{t>0}\int_{0}^{t/2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t/2}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t-y_{j}\right\}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{|S|}^{\oplus}>t/2\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad\qquad\cdot\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{|S|}^{\oplus}>t/2\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{|S|}^{\oplus}>t\right)}F_{Z_{j_{1}},\ldots,Z_{j_{i}}}(dy_{1},\ldots,dy_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C^{**}\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i }}\sup_{t>0}\int_{0}^{t/2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t/2}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j\in S}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}Z^{(k)}_{j}>t/2\right\}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(|S|)}^{\oplus}>t/2\right)}F_{Z_{j_{1}},\ldots,Z_{j_{i}}}(dy_{1},\ldots,dy_{i})

where we applied (
4.2
) once more. Now the last term above is bounded by
C^{**}\alpha_{i,n}
and hence we have

(E.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{S\subseteq\mathbb{I}}{ |S|\leq i}}\sup_{t>0}\frac{J_{n,2}(t,S)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{|S|}^{\oplus}>t\right)}\leq C^{**}\alpha_{i,n}.

Now from
(
E.1
), (
E.2
) and (
E.3
) we get

(E.4)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i,n+1}\leq\alpha_{i,n}\widetilde{K}_{i}+\alpha_{i,n}C^{**}=(\widetilde{K}_{i}+C^{**})\alpha_{i,n}.

Note that
\alpha_{i,d}\leq 1
for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Thus applying (
E.4
) recursively we obtain
\alpha_{i,n+1}\leq(\widetilde{K}_{i}+C^{**})^{{n-d}}
for
n\geq d
,
i=1,\ldots,d
. But for
n\leq d

we have of course
\alpha_{i,n}\leq 1
for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Thus, with
K_{i}=\max(1,\widetilde{K}_{i}+C^{**})
the statement of the lemma is satisfied.
∎

\boldsymbol{Z}^{\oplus}:=(Z^{\oplus}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\oplus}_{d}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k)}
