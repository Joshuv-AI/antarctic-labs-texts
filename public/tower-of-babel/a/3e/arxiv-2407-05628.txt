On the existence of strong solutions for unsteady motions of incompressible chemically reacting generalized Newtonian fluids
Kyueon Choi, Kyungkeun Kang and Seungchan Ko

Abstract
We consider a system of nonlinear partial differential equations modeling the unsteady motion of an incompressible generalized Newtonian fluid with chemical reactions. The system consists of the generalized Navier-Stokes equations with power-law type viscosity with a power-law index depending on the concentration, and the convection-diffusion equation which describes chemical concentration. This system of partial differential equations arises in the mathematical models describing the synovial fluid which can be found in the cavities of movable joints. We prove the existence of a global strong solution for the two and three-dimensional spatially periodic domain, provided that the power-law index is greater than or equal to ( d + 2 ) / 2 (d+2)/2 where d d is the dimension of the spatial domain. Moreover, we also prove that such a solution is unique under the further assumption that p + < 3 2 ​ p − p^{+}<\frac{3}{2}p^{-} for the two-dimensional case and p + < 7 6 ​ p − p^{+}<\frac{7}{6}p^{-} for the three-dimensional case, where p − p^{-} and p + p^{+} are the lower and upper bounds of the power-law index p ⁡ ( ⋅ ) p(\cdot) respectively.



Keywords
: Non-Newtonian fluid, convection-diffusion equation, synovial fluid, strong solution, maximal regularity, variable power-law index

Introduction

In this paper, we will prove the existence of a strong solution to a system of partial differential equations (PDEs) describing the unsteady motion of an incompressible chemically reacting flow in two and three dimensions. More precisely, we would like to know whether there exists a strong solution for the following system of PDEs:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+{\rm div}\,(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v})-{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\pi+\boldsymbol{f},

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c+{\rm div}\,(c\boldsymbol{v})-\Delta c

\displaystyle=-{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}

(1.3)

in
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
where
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
with
d=2,3
. Here
\boldsymbol{v}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\pi:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
c:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
represent the velocity field, the scalar pressure and the concentration distribution respectively. In addition,
\boldsymbol{f}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes a given external forcing term for the velocity and
\boldsymbol{g}
represents the term denoting the chemical concentration source. Here
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}
is the symmetric part of the velocity gradient
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}):Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the Cauchy stress tensor. The given system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) consists of the generalized incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and the convection-diffusion equation for the concentration.
In this setting, we consider the initial-boundary value problem described by (
1.1
)-(
1.3
). On the boundary, we assume that all involved quantities regarding the velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
and the concentration

are periodic with respect to the domain
[0,1]^{d}
. In addition, we impose the following initial conditions

\boldsymbol{v}(x,0)=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad c(x,0)=c_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{in }\Omega.

For the Cauchy stress tensor
\boldsymbol{S}
, we consider the case when it can be represented as the form

{\bf S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v})=2\nu(c,|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|)\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v},

where
\nu
is of the power-law-like viscosity

\nu(c,|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|)=\nu_{0}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-2}{2}}

with some positive constant
\nu_{0}>0
. Moreover, we assume that the exponent
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz continuous function with
1<p^{-}\leq p(\cdot)\leq p^{+}<\infty
. Then it is straightforward to verify that the following properties hold: there exist positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
such that for any
(c,\boldsymbol{D})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, there holds

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D})}{\partial\boldsymbol{D}}:(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{B})&\geq K_{1}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{B}|^{2},\\
\left|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D})}{\partial\boldsymbol{D}}\right|&\leq K_{2}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-2}{2}},\\
\left|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D})}{\partial c}\right|&\leq K_{3}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-1}{2}}\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}|),\end{split}

(1.4)

with the notation
(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{B})_{ijkh}=B_{ij}B_{kh}
. Furthermore, we can also deduce from the above properties that for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{1},\boldsymbol{D}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, there exists a positive constant
K_{4}
such that

\left(\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}_{1})-\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}_{2})\right):(\boldsymbol{D}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}_{2})\geq K_{4}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}_{1}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}_{2}|^{2}.

(1.5)

To the best of our knowledge, there are no results about the existence of strong solutions for the unsteady CRF model. A significant difference compared to the electrorheological fluids is the appearance of

Since the convection-diffusion equation can be regarded as the heat equation with a source term, we apply the maximal regularity theory to obtain the desired results. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We first introduce the auxiliary tools for the proofs of our main theorems in Section 2. In the following section, we construct the Galerkin approximation as a baseline system and prove the higher regularity of

Then in Section 6, we prove the existence of a strong solution through the limiting process based on the uniform estimates obtained in the previous sections. Further, we prove the uniqueness of the strong solution under the additional assumption in Section 7 and we will discuss the concluding remark in Section 8.

Preliminaries and Main Result

In this section, we shall introduce our main result along with some preliminaries which will be used throughout the paper. For two vectors
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
,
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}
means the dot product and for two tensors
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{A}:\mathbb{B}
denotes the scalar product. In addition,
C
signifies a generic positive constant, which may vary at each appearance. For
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we denote the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces by

and

\|\cdot\|_{p}=\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

. For a Banach space

, we define the Bochner space

by the family of functions with the finite Bochner-type norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}(0,T;X)}:=\begin{cases}\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|_{X}^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}&\text{if }1\leq q<\infty,\\
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|u(t)\|_{X}&\text{if }q=\infty,\end{cases}

with an abbreviation
L^{q}(Q_{T})=L^{q}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
. Since we will deal with the spatially periodic functions, we shall restrict ourselves to the case of the zero mean-value functions over
\Omega
. In this perspective, we will use the following spaces which are frequently used in the study of incompressible fluid flow problems: For
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
1\leq q<\infty

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\{\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega):\langle\varphi,1\rangle=0,\,\,{\rm div}\,\varphi=0\},

\displaystyle W^{k,q}_{\rm div}(\Omega):=\overline{\mathcal{V}}^{\|\cdot\|_{k,q}}.

Next, let us introduce the function space with variable exponent. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of all measurable functions
p:Q_{T}\to[1,\infty]
and we call the function

a variable exponent. Furthermore, we define
p^{-}:=\inf_{Q_{T}}p(x,t)
and

. Then the variable-exponent Lebesgue space is defined as

L^{p(\cdot)}(Q_{T}):=\bigg\{u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(Q_{T}):\int_{Q_{T}}|u(x,t)|^{p(x,t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty\bigg\}

equipped the corresponding Luxembourg norm

\|u\|_{L^{p(\cdot)}(Q_{T})}:=\inf\bigg\{\lambda>0:\int_{Q_{T}}\bigg|\frac{u(x,t)}{\lambda}\bigg|^{p(x,t)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 1\bigg\}.

If
1<p^{-}\leq p^{+}<\infty
, this space becomes a reflexive and separable Banach space. Furthermore, Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality hold with the variable exponents
1=\frac{1}{p(\cdot)}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(\cdot)}
. These properties play an important role as tools for the analysis of the CRF under consideration. See, e.g.,
[
8
]
for more information on the variable-exponent Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces.

3.1
Galerkin approximation

Let us first construct the Galerkin approximation for the given system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
). Let
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
denotes the set of eigenvectors of the Stokes operator with
\langle\boldsymbol{w}_{i},1\rangle=0
for all

\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms a basis of

(see Theorem 4.11, p.290 in
[
26
]
). Moreover, we choose

so that

. For the concentration equation, we shall consider
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forming a basis of
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
\int_{\Omega}z_{i}z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{ij}
. Then for each

(\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m},c^{n,m})

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m}):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{S}^{n,m}:\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m}(\cdot,0)=P^{n}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{0},

(3.3)

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}c^{n,m}z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}c^{n,m}\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m}\ \cdot\nabla z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla c^{n,m}\cdot\nabla z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\nabla z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3.4)

c^{n,m}(\cdot,0)=P^{m}\,c_{0}

(3.5)

for all

and

, where
\boldsymbol{S}^{n,m}:=\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n,m},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n,m})

with
\partial_{t}c^{n}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{-1,2}(\Omega))
satisfying the following intermediate problem: for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, there holds

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{n}):\nabla\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{S}^{n}:\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

(3.6)

\boldsymbol{v}^{n}(\cdot,0)=P^{n}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{0},

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}c^{n}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}c^{n}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\ \cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla c^{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}{\bf g}\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3.7)

c^{n}(\cdot,0)=c_{0}

for arbitrary
\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
with

\boldsymbol{S}^{n}:=\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n})
.
Multiplying the
i
-th equation of (
3.6
) by
\alpha_{i}^{n}
and taking the sum over
i=1,\ldots,n
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{S}^{n}:\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which leads us to derive the estimate

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{p(c^{n})}+|\boldsymbol{S}^{n}|^{(p(c^{n}))^{\prime}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

(3.8)

where the constant on the right-hand side is independent of
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If we take
\varphi=c^{n}
in (
3.7
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}|c^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\nabla c^{n}\,\mathrm{d}x,

from which we obtain the following uniform estimate

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert c^{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

(3.9)

Before proceeding further for
n\to\infty
, we need more uniform estimates regarding the higher regularity of
\nabla c^{n}
which will be subsequently investigated in the following sections. Henceforth, we will omit the index
n
for the sake of convenience.

3.2
Higher integrability of
\nabla c

To begin with, we shall prove the higher integrability of
\nabla c
. Indeed, we aim to obtain

\nabla c\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))

(3.10)

for
q>2d
. To do this, since
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
is dense in
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we can formally take
\varphi=-\nabla\cdot(|\nabla c|^{q-2}\nabla c)
in (
3.7
) and consequently it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q-2}|\nabla^{2}c|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}||\nabla c|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}\,\nabla\cdot(|\nabla c|^{q-2}\nabla c)\,\mathrm{d}x=:{\rm D_{1}}+{\rm D_{2}}.

Note that
{\rm D_{1}}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{1}\leq\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p^{-}}\||\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}}\|^{2}_{2(p^{-})^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p^{-}}\||\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}}\|_{2}^{2\theta}\|\nabla(|\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}})\|^{2(1-\theta)}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p^{-}}^{\frac{1}{\theta}}\||\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}}\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon\|\nabla(|\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}})\|_{2}^{2},

where we have used Young’s inequality and the Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality (Lemma
2.2
)
with
\frac{1}{2{(p^{-})}^{\prime}}=\frac{\theta}{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{d})(1-\theta)
, meaning that
\theta=\frac{d-d{(p^{-})}^{\prime}+2{(p^{-})}^{\prime}}{2{(p^{-})}^{\prime}}
.
Next, we estimate
{\rm D_{2}}
by Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality as

{\rm D_{2}}\leq C(q)\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{1,q}\|\nabla c\|_{q}^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q-2}|\nabla^{2}c|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{1,q}^{q}+C\|\nabla c\|_{q}^{q}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q-2}|\nabla^{2}c|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Further, note that there holds

\left|\nabla(|\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}})\right|^{2}\leq C(q)|\nabla c|^{q-2}|\nabla^{2}c|^{2}.

Now by combining the above inequalities, we obtain

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla(|\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C(1+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|^{\frac{1}{\theta}}_{p^{-}})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{1,q}^{q}.

Recalling that
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{p^{-}}(Q_{T})
from (
3.8
) and
\boldsymbol{g}\in L^{q}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega))
from the assumption, we need the condition
\frac{1}{\theta}\leq p^{-}
to apply Grönwall’s inequality, which is equivalent to
\frac{d+2}{2}\leq p^{-}
. Therefore, if we assume the condition
\frac{d+2}{2}\leq p^{-}
, by Grönwall’s inequality, we can conclude that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla c(t)\|_{q}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla(|\nabla c|^{\frac{q}{2}})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

provided that
\nabla c_{0}\in L^{q}(\Omega)
which comes from the assumption. Note that the above argument holds both for the two and three-dimensional domains. In the following sections, we will derive some uniform estimates for dimensions two and three separately.

4.1

Now we aim to get the uniform estimate of

(4.1)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+{\rm I}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot(-\Delta\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x,

(4.2)

with

First, due to (
1.4

{\rm E_{1}}\geq C\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

For the estimate of
{\rm E_{2}}
, by (

) and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\begin{split}|{\rm E_{2}}|&\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\nabla c|(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c)-1}{2}}\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|)|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|\,\mathrm{d}x\\

where

4.2

c_{t}

When we differentiated the stress tensor by the spatial variable, there appeared the
\nabla c
term. Similarly, if we consider the time differentiation of the stress tensor, then the
\partial_{t}c
term necessarily arises. Thus we need to control this term and we shall address it in Section
4.2
. In the two-dimensional domain, we know that

\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{s}(\Omega))\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq s<\infty.

Furthermore, since we showed
\nabla c\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
in (
3.10
), we obtain

\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla c\in L^{\delta}(Q_{T})

for some
\delta\in(4,q)
.
Moreover, from the assumption
\boldsymbol{g}\in L^{q}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega))
and

, we can also see that
{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}\in L^{\delta}(Q_{T})
and
\Delta c_{0}\in L^{\delta}(\Omega)
.
Now for the concentration equation, we may write

\partial_{t}c-\Delta c=h,

where
h={\rm div}\,(-c\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{g})

2.6

\|\partial_{t}c\|_{L^{\delta}(Q_{T})}+\|\Delta c\|_{L^{\delta}(Q_{T})}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla c+{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}\|_{L^{\delta}(Q_{T})}+C\|\Delta c_{0}\|_{L^{\delta}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, we can conclude for some
\delta\in(4,q)

\partial_{t}c\in L^{\delta}(Q_{T})\quad{\rm{and}}\quad\Delta c\in L^{\delta}(Q_{T}).

(4.14)

for some

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly in}\quad L^{2}(0,T;W^{2,2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\mbox{weakly-}^{*}\,\,{\rm{in}}\quad L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle c^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup c

\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\to\boldsymbol{v}\quad\mbox{strongly in}\quad L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega)),

\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n})\to\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v})\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad L^{1}(Q_{T}).

Also, as a consequence of (

(\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\to(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\quad\mbox{in}\quad L^{\frac{4}{3}}(Q_{T}).

Now we shall temporarily choose

\begin{split}|\nabla\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n})|^{a}&\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{a\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{a}\\
&\hskip 11.38109pt+C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{a\frac{p(c^{n})-1}{2}}|\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|)|^{a}|\nabla c^{n}|^{a}\\
&=:{\rm M_{1}}+{\rm M_{2}}.\end{split}

(6.2)

Let us consider the cases of 2D and 3D separately. First of all, in the case of 2D, we set
a=\frac{4}{3}
. Then by Young’s inequality,
{\rm M_{1}}
can be estimated as

\displaystyle{\rm M_{1}}

\displaystyle=C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{4}{3}\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{4}{3}\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{\frac{4}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{4\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}+C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2}.

(6.3)

From (
4.12
), the second term in (
6.3
) belongs to
L^{1}(Q_{T})
. For the first term, by recalling (
4.6
) and (

), we have

(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{4\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}\leq(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{4\frac{p(c^{n})}{4}}=\eta^{4}\in L^{1}(Q_{T}).

(6.4)

Thus we get
{\rm M_{1}}\in L^{1}(Q_{T})
. For the estimate of
{\rm M_{2}}
, by Young’s inequality

{\rm M_{2}}\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{4{\frac{p(c^{n})-1}{4}}}+|\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|)|^{\frac{4\alpha}{3}}+|\nabla c^{n}|^{q},

where
1=\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{4}{3q}
with
q>4
as given in theorem
2.9
and arbitrarily large
\alpha>0
. Also, it is straightforward that

|\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|)|^{\frac{4\alpha}{3}}\leq C+C|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2}.

Thus by (
3.8
), (
3.10
) and (
6.4
), we have
{\rm M_{2}}\in L^{1}(Q_{T})
.

For the case of 3D, we let
a=\frac{6}{5}
and then
{\rm M_{1}}
can be estimated by Young’s inequality as

\displaystyle{\rm M_{1}}

\displaystyle=C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{6}{5}\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{6}{5}\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{\frac{6}{5}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{3\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}+C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{2}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2}.

(6.5)

From (
5.15
), the second term in (
6.5
) belongs to
L^{1}(Q_{T})
. By recalling (
4.6
) and (
5.26
), we have

(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{3\frac{p(c^{n})-2}{4}}\leq(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{3\frac{p(c^{n})}{4}}=\eta^{3}\in L^{1}(Q_{T}),

(6.6)

which implies that the first term of (
6.5
) belongs to
L^{1}(Q_{T})
. Hence we obtain
{\rm M_{1}}\in L^{1}(Q_{T}).

For the estimate of
{\rm M_{2}}
, by Young’s inequality

{\rm M_{2}}\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|^{2})^{3\frac{p(c^{n})-1}{4}}+|\log(2+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}|)|^{\frac{6\alpha}{5}}+|\nabla c^{n}|^{q},

where
1=\frac{4}{5}+\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{6}{5q}
with
q>6
as given in theorem
2.9
and large enough
\alpha>0
. The logarithmic term can be controlled as we did in the 2D case, and then by (
3.8
), (
3.10
) and (
6.6
) we also have
{\rm M_{2}}\in L^{1}(Q_{T}).

Therefore, from (
6.2
) with the above estimates, we have for both 2D and 3D cases that

\nabla\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n})\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}(Q_{T}).

Altogether, we obtain that

\|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{L^{\frac{6}{5}}(Q_{T})}+\|(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(Q_{T})}\leq C.

Now by choosing
\boldsymbol{w}\in\{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
from (
3.1
) and
\xi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T])
, we deduce from (
3.2
) and the above inequality that

\int_{Q_{T}}\big(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\xi+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\xi-{\rm div}\,(\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}))\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\xi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{Q_{T}}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\xi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

By the density of smooth functions, we get

\int_{Q_{T}}\big(\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}-{\rm div}\,(\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}))\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{Q_{T}}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

for all
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{V})
. Now by De Rahm’s theorem, there exists
\nabla\pi\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}(Q_{T})
(see
[
4
,
34
]
) and we finally get

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+{\rm div}\,(\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v})-{\rm div}\,(\boldsymbol{S}(c,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}))+\nabla\pi=\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad Q_{T}.

On the other hand, from (
4.14
) and (
5.17
), we have for both 2D and 3D cases that

\Delta c\in L^{2}(Q_{T}).

Since
\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
,
c\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega))
and
q>2d
, it is straightforward to verify in both two and three-dimensional domains

\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla c\in L^{2}(Q_{T}).

In all, we have

\|\partial_{t}c\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}+\|\,{\rm div}\,(c\boldsymbol{v})\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}+\|\Delta c\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\leq C.

Now we deduce from (
3.7
) and again by the density of smooth functions, we obtain

\int_{Q_{T}}\big(\partial_{t}c\,\phi+{\rm div}\,(c\boldsymbol{v})\,\phi-\Delta c\,\phi\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{Q_{T}}{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}\,\phi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T];C^{\infty}(\Omega))
, which implies that

\partial_{t}c+{\rm div}\,(c\boldsymbol{v})-\Delta c={\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{g}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad Q_{T}.

Finally, we can see that
\boldsymbol{v},c\in C([0,T],L^{2}(\Omega))
since
\boldsymbol{v},c\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v},\partial_{t}c\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Moreover, it is obvious that
\|P^{n}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}-\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}\to 0
which completes the proof.

Remark 6.1
.

Indeed, we have shown that
\nabla\boldsymbol{S}(c^{n},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}^{n})\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(Q_{T})
in the two-dimensional domain. Therefore, by De Rahm’s theorem,
\nabla\pi
belongs to
L^{\frac{4}{3}}(Q_{T})
in 2D.

7
Proof of Theorem
2.10

In this section, let us prove Theorem
2.10
concerning the uniqueness of the strong solution constructed in Theorem
2.9
. Let
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},c_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{v}_{2},c_{2})
be two solutions of the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
). First of all, we take the difference of (
1.3
) of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
to get

\partial_{t}(c_{1}-c_{2})-\Delta(c_{1}-c_{2})+{\rm div}\,(c_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-c_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})=0.

(7.1)

We then test
-\Delta(c_{1}-c_{2})
to the above equation and use Young’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\Delta(c_{1}-c_{2})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla c_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\cdot\nabla c_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\cdot\nabla c_{1}+\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\cdot(\nabla c_{1}-\nabla c_{2})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla c_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+C\|\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}.

Secondly, take the difference of (
1.1
) of
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
and test
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
to get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+{\rm P}={\rm Q},

(7.3)

where

\begin{split}{\rm P}&:=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{S}(c_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\boldsymbol{S}(c_{2},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\right):(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x,\\
{\rm Q}&:=-\int_{\Omega}\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-(\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Note by Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality that

\begin{split}{\rm Q}&=-\int_{\Omega}\left((\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}||\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{6}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{6}^{2}+C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Then the application of the Sobolev embedding and Korn’s inequality yields

{\rm Q}\leq\epsilon\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.

(7.4)

Next, we shall write
{\rm P}
as

\begin{split}{\rm P}&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{S}(c_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\boldsymbol{S}(c_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\right):(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\hskip 11.38109pt+\int_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{S}(c_{1},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})-\boldsymbol{S}(c_{2},\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\right):(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=:{\rm P_{1}}+{\rm P_{2}}.\end{split}

(7.5)

For the term
{\rm P_{1}}
, by the monotonicity assumption (
1.5
), we have

{\rm P_{1}}\geq C\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(7.6)

Furthermore, by the mean value theorem, there exists
z
between
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

\begin{split}{\rm P_{2}}&=2\int_{\Omega}p^{\prime}(z)\nu(z,\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\log(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})(c_{1}-c_{2})\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}:(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Since
p\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\log(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})^{\alpha}
for some arbitrarily small
\alpha>0
, we have

\begin{split}|{\rm P_{2}}|&\leq C\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(z)-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}||c_{1}-c_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=C\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(z)-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\alpha-\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{4}}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{4}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}||c_{1}-c_{2}|\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(7.7)

We will estimate (
7.7
) for the cases of two and three-dimensional domains separately.

(2D case) By Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality, we see that

|{\rm P_{2}}|\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{(2+\delta_{1})(\frac{p^{+}}{2}-\frac{p^{-}}{4}+\alpha)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{2}{2+\delta_{1}}}\|c_{1}-c_{2}\|_{\ell}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

for some small
\delta_{1}>0
and sufficiently large
\ell>2
with
1=\frac{1}{2+\delta_{1}}+\frac{1}{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}
. From (
4.13
) and the Sobolev embedding,

\eta\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{r}(\Omega)),\quad 1\leq r<\infty,

which implies

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{p^{-}}(0,T;L^{\frac{p^{-}}{2}r}(\Omega)).

Note further from (
4.25
) that

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p^{-}}(\Omega)).

If we choose large enough
r>0
, by the interpolation inequality, we can show that there exists small
\delta_{2}>0
such that

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{2p^{-}-\delta_{2}}(Q_{T}).

Therefore, if
p^{+}<\frac{3}{2}p^{-}
, we have
(2+\delta_{1})(p^{+}-\frac{p^{-}}{2}+2\alpha)\leq 2p^{-}-\delta_{2}
. Hence, by the Sobolev embedding, we obtain

|{\rm P_{2}}|\leq F(t)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

for some
F(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
.

(3D case)
For the three-dimensional case, we have by Hölder’s inequality and Young’s inequality that

|{\rm P_{2}}|\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{3(\frac{p^{+}}{2}-\frac{p^{-}}{4}+\alpha)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\|c_{1}-c_{2}\|_{6}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

From (
5.16
) together with the Sobolev embedding theorem, there holds

\eta\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega)),

which implies

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{p^{-}}(0,T;L^{3p^{-}}(\Omega)).

Also by recalling (
5.25
), we have

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p^{-}}(\Omega)).

From the above estimates with the interpolation, we obtain

\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{\frac{4}{3}p^{-}}(0,T;L^{2p^{-}}(\Omega)).

Therefore, if
p^{+}<\frac{7}{6}p^{-}
, we have
3(p^{+}-\frac{p^{-}}{2}+2\alpha)\leq 2p^{-}
which implies by the Sobolev embedding that

|{\rm P_{2}}|\leq F(t)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

for some
F(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
. All together, for both 2D and 3D cases, we have for some
F(t)\in L^{1}(0,T)
that

|{\rm P_{2}}|\leq F(t)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(7.8)

Now we insert (
7.6
) and (
7.8
) into (
7.5
) to get

{\rm P}\geq C\int_{\Omega}(1+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2})^{\frac{p(c_{1})-2}{2}}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(t)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}.

(7.9)

Then by combining (
7.3
), (
7.4
) and (
7.9
), we have

\frac{\rm d}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+F(t)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}.

(7.10)

Finally, from (
7.2
) and (
7.10
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{\rm d}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rm d}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}|\Delta(c_{1}-c_{2})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\hskip 11.38109pt\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}+F(t)\right)\|\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{2}^{2}+C\left(\|\nabla c_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}^{2}\right)\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(7.11)

Since
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{2,2}(\Omega))
, by the Sobolev embedding, we know that
\|\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{3}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T)

) and (

\|\nabla c_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\in L^{1}(0,T)

\nabla(c_{1}-c_{2})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=0.

(7.12)

Furthermore, by using the Poincaré inequality, we can conclude

c_{1}-c_{2}=0,

which completes the proof.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proved the existence of the unique strong solution for the system composed of the generalized non-Newtonian fluids with a concentration-dependent power-law index and the convection-diffusion equation in the two and three dimensions under the spatial periodicity condition. The main difficulty of the proof comes from the terms
\nabla c
and
\partial_{t}c
which appear when we differentiate the stress tensor in the Galerkin system. We first obtained the higher integrability of
\nabla c
by testing the appropriate test function where the restriction
p^{-}\geq\frac{d+2}{2}
is necessarily needed. To control
\partial_{t}c
, we have used the maximal regularity theory. Consequently, we obtained the existence of a strong solution with suitable regularity properties. Additionally, if we further assume the condition
p^{+}<\frac{3}{2}p^{-}
for
d=2
and
p^{+}<\frac{7}{6}p^{-}
for
d=3
, we have also obtained the uniqueness of the strong solution.

An interesting future research direction is to obtain better regularity for the solution and extend this result to the degenerate case. In addition, from the computational point of view, performing the convergence analysis of the finite element approximation for the strong solution would also be intriguing, which will be addressed in the forthcoming paper.
