Spectral properties of the stochastic block model and their application to hitting times of random walks
Matthias Löwe, Sara Terveer

Abstract
. We analyze hitting times of simple random walk on realizations of the stochastic block model. We show that under some natural assumptions the hitting time averaged over the target vertex asymptotically almost surely given by N ⁡ ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) N(1+o(1)) . On the other hand, the hitting time averaged over the starting vertex asymptotically almost surely depends on expected degrees in the block the target vertex is in. We also show a central limit theorem for the hitting time averaged over the starting vertex. Our main techniques are a spectral decomposition of these hitting times, a spectral analysis of the adjacency matrix and the graph Laplacian.

Introduction

Random walks on random graphs are very topical in contemporary probability theory, see e.g.
[
AGvdH+22
,
Syl21
,
FKMP18
,
BLPS18
]
and the references therein. Such random walks can be considered a special instance of random walk in random environment and the principal question is, if and how
this random environment, hence the random graph, influences characteristic properties of such a random walk. For example,
[
FR08
]
and
[
AGvdH+22
]
analyze the mixing
time of simple random walks on random graphs, while the focus in
[
SRbA05
]
,
[
LT14
]
,
[
LT23
]
, and
[
LT25
]
is on the (average) hitting times.
Note however, that most results on random walks on random graphs consider (sparse or dense) Erdős-Rényi graphs (the note

[
HL19
]
is a slight exception, there
the authors consider random (Erdős-Rényi type) hypergraph models). As in
[
LT14
]
,
[
LT23
]
, and
[
LT25
]
in this note we will consider
average hitting times of random walks on random graphs. Our main technique will again be a spectral representation of the hitting
times. Note that in
[
OS24
]
the authors take a
different approach to hitting times for very dense Erdős-Rényi random graphs (i.e. for them
p
is constant) exploiting the fact
that for such graphs the diameter is
2
with high probability.

In the present situation, the random environment, however, will be given by a stochastic block model.
To be more concrete, we consider a random graph
G_{N}(M,P)=(V_{N},E_{N})
.
G_{N}(M,P)
will be constructed according to the stochastic block model, i.e. the vertex set

V_{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
is fixed and consists of
M<N
disjoint “blocks”
V^{(1)}_{N},\dots,V^{(M)}_{N}
with
N_{1},\dots,N_{M}
vertices, where
\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}N_{m}=N
. Here, we will consider the situation where
N
is a multiple of
M
and choose
N_{1}=\dots=N_{M}=\frac{N}{M}
.

The unoriented edges of the graph are realized by stochastically independent random variables
\varepsilon_{v,w}\in\{0,1\}

(for the edge
\{v,w\},v,w\in V_{N}
). The probabilities
for
\varepsilon_{v,w}=1
are given by a (symmetric)
M\times M
matrix denoted by
P\coloneqq P_{M}
. Hence the probabilities for
\varepsilon_{v,w}=1
just depend on the blocks the vertices are
in, and the entries
p_{i,j}(N)
denote the probability for an edge to
exist between two vertices
v\in V^{(i)}_{N}
and
w\in V^{(j)}_{N}
.
Notice that we allow loops (denoted by multisets
\{v,v\}
,
v\in V
) in the graph for technical purposes of the graph’s adjacency matrix, however, their removal should not influence the results. We will denote the set of loops by
L=L_{N}
.

In the present note, we assume all edge probabilities between different blocks to be identical
q:=p_{0}(N)
, i.e. all off-diagonal entries of
P_{M}
are
q
.
The diagonal entries
p_{i,i}(N)
are abbreviated by
p_{i}(N)
.
We assume that the
p_{i}(N)
are sorted in descending order, i.e.
p_{1}(N)\geq p_{2}(N)\geq\dots\geq p_{M}(N)
.
This can easily be obtained by rearranging the blocks of the model in the corresponding order.
We denote the block number of a vertex
v\in V
by
B(v)
, i.e. for
m=1,\dots,M
, if
v\in V^{m}
, then
B(v)=m
.

We will additionally impose the relatively mild connectivity conditions. To formulate them we refer to the definition (
1.4
)of the expected degree of a vertex in block
m
,
\gamma_{m}
, and set

\gamma_{\min}\coloneqq\min\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}\gamma_{m}.

as well as

\gamma_{\max}\coloneqq\max\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}\gamma_{m}.

Then our first condition reads

(1.1)

\frac{M\log^{4}(N)}{Np_{m}(N)+N(M-1)q(N)}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{\max}^{2}}{\gamma_{\min}^{2}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0

(note that subsequently, we will frequently omit the dependency of
N
unless necessary for clarity).

This implies (from the second factor), that the
p_{m}
are not of too different order which is a technicality needed throughout the proof.
The first factor guarantees either intra-block connectivity (i.e. all
p_{m}
are sufficiently large) or inter-block connectivity (i.e.
q
is sufficiently large). In the former case, connectivity of the entire graph is not guaranteed, so we need an additional assumption on
q
. Moreover, we require that
q
must not be too small compared to the
p_{m}
, resulting in the condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle((M-1)q)^{2}\gg\left(\frac{M\log N}{N}\right)^{1/4}p_{\min}^{5/4}p_{\max}^{1/2},

where
p_{\max}=\max\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}p_{m}
and
p_{\min}=\min\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}p_{m}
.
The second factor in (
1.1
) and the dependence of the lower bound in (
1.2
) on the
p_{m}

are artifacts of our subsequent proofs.

As a matter of fact, together the two conditions imply that
G_{N}(M,P)
is connected with probability converging to 1, as
N\to\infty
(which we will denote by ”asymptotically
almost-surely (a.a.s.)). Each block
i
(without the edges leaving the
block) constitutes an Erdős-Rényi graph on
n=N/M
vertices and connection probability
p_{i}
. Then,
\frac{M\log^{4}(N)}{N\cdot p_{m}(N)\cdot(1-p_{m}(N))}\to\infty
which is implied by (
1.1
)
is (more than)
sufficient to ensure that block number
m
is connected a.a.s.

Moreover, note that there are
N/M
vertices per block. (
1.1
) gives that all
p_{m}
satisfy

p_{m}\gg\frac{M\log^{4}N}{N}
. Hence (
1.2
) implies that
q\gg\frac{M\log^{5/2}N}{(M-1)N}
.
This, together with the block sizes guarantees that all blocks are connected with each other, a.a.s., hence
the entire graph is connected a.a.s. In the following results we will always tacitly assume that our realization of the stochastic block model is connected.

For fixed
N
and a fixed realization
G=G_{N}
of the stochastic block model, consider the simple random walk in discrete
time
(X_{t})
on
G
: If
X_{t}
is in
v\in V
at time
t
,
X_{t+1}
will be in
w
with probability
\frac{1}{d_{v}}
,
where
d_{v}
denotes the degree of
v
, if
\{v,w\}\in E
, and with probability 0, otherwise.

The invariant distribution of this walk is given by

\pi_{v}\coloneqq\frac{d_{v}}{\sum\limits_{w\in V}d_{w}}=\frac{d_{v}}{2|E|-|L|}.

Let
H_{vw}
be the expected time it takes the walk to reach vertex
w\in V
when starting from vertex
v\in V
.
Of course,
H_{vw}
will typically be sensitive to the choice of
v
and
w
. To compensate for this, one averages over either
v
or
w
.
More precisely, we define

(1.3)

H_{w}:=\sum\limits_{v\in V}\pi_{v}H_{vw}\quad\text{ and }\quad H^{v}:=\sum\limits_{w\in V}\pi_{w}H_{vw}.

Notice that in
[
LT23
]
and
[
LT25
]
, these are called
average target hitting time
and
average starting hitting time
, meanwhile, in
[
LPW09
]
, the latter is called
random target time
.
Note that
H_{w}
and
H^{v}
are expectation values in the random walk measure, but with respect to the realization of the random graph, they are random variables.
In
[
LT14
]
, the asymptotic behaviour of
H_{w}
and
H^{v}
were analyzed on the level of a law of large numbers for a random walk on a realization of an Erdős-Rényi random graph. It was shown that

H_{w}=N(1+o(1))\quad\text{ as well as }\quad H^{v}=N(1+o(1))

asymptotically almost surely proving a conjecture from
[
SRbA05
]
. The results were extended to central limit theorems in
[
LT23
]
and
[
LT25
]
. In
[
HL19
]
the law of large numbers was extended to random hypergraphs.
The aim of this paper is to prove the correspondings laws of large numbers and a central limit theorem for
H_{w}
for random graphs according to the stochastic block model.

To state these, define

(1.4)

\gamma_{m}:=\frac{N}{M}p_{m}+(M-1)\frac{N}{M}q\quad\mbox{for $m=1,\dots,M$.}

Note that

\gamma_{m}
is the expected degree of a vertex in block
m
.
Moreover, let

\gamma_{\min}\coloneqq\min\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}\gamma_{m}.

Our goal is to prove the following statement:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that conditions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) hold. Then

H^{v}=N(1+o(1))

asymptotically almost surely.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that conditions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) hold.
Then,

\displaystyle H_{w}=\frac{N}{M}\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{B(w)}}(1+o(1))

asymptotically almost surely. Recall that, for
w\in V
, the quantity
{B(w)}
denotes the block, in which
w
can be found.

Under some additional assumptions, we can extend our result for
H_{w}
to a central limit theorem. To that end, we introduce notation for average probability for an intra-block edge and average degree of a vertex:

\displaystyle\bar{p}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}p_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{m}

Finally, additionally to
\gamma_{m}
, we denote the variance of the degree of a vertex in block
m
and the average of these variances by

\displaystyle\upsilon_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{N}{M}p_{m}(1-p_{m})+(M-1)\frac{N}{M}q(1-q)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\upsilon}^{2}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\upsilon_{m}^{2}.

In order to obtain a central limit theorem, we must assume that our graph is not too densely connected (otherwise we obtain a near-complete graph which leads to a near-deterministic hitting time with little fluctuations): To that end, we replace the condition
(
1.1
)
with

(1.5)

\frac{M\log^{4}(N)}{Np_{m}(N)(1-p_{m}(N))+N(M-1)q(N)(1-q(N))}\cdot\frac{p_{\max}^{2}}{p_{\min}^{2}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0

Furthermore, we replace condition (
1.2
) by the following conditions:

(1.6)

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\bar{\gamma}}\ll\frac{\upsilon_{m}}{\bar{p}},

as well as

(1.7)

\frac{\bar{\upsilon}}{\upsilon_{m}}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\bar{\gamma}}\ll\sqrt{N},

and

(1.8)

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\cdot\frac{p_{\min}^{2}}{\gamma_{\min}(M-1)^{2}q^{2}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0

The conditions (
1.8
) is a stronger, more general version of (
1.2
) suited to a central limit theorem (i.e. also ensuring that in the case of very dense blocks,
q
is not too large, which would lead to a near complete graph again). The other two (additional)
conditions in the statement ensure that the
p_{m}
are “well balanced” in the sense that the expectation of the degree of
w
and the average degree in the graph are not too far apart. In the case of
(M-1)q\ll p_{\min}
, this is (up to a logarithmic factor) implied by (
1.5
).

Similarly, we want to ensure that the variances of the arising degrees are not too different to control the fluctuations, which is why we require that (
1.7
) holds. Again, this conditions is up to logarithmic terms already implied by the previous conditions in the strongly assortative setting.

Notice that both conditions are satisfied e.g. when all
p_{m}
are constant, all of the same order, or all dominated by
(M-1)q
.

Then, we can show the following theorem

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that conditions (
1.5
) – (
1.8
) hold. Let
w\in V
be a vertex with block number
m=B(w)
such that additionally
hold.
Then

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{m}^{2}}{N\upsilon_{m}\bar{\gamma}}\cdot\left(H_{w}-\frac{N\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}
denotes convergence in distribution.

Remark 1.4
.

The results remains true if the number of blocks
M\coloneqq M(N)\ll N
depends on the number of vertices
N
, e.g.
M(N)=\log N
, as long as all other conditions remain intact.

The rest of this note is organized in the following way: In spectral graph theory, a common approach to average hitting times (defined as above) is via
a representation in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a version of the Laplacian matrix of the graph
(see e.g
[
Lov96
]
).
This matrix is
closely related to the adjaceny matrix of the graph. In a first step we therefore bound the eigenvalues of the expected adjacency
matrix in the stochastic block model. This will be done in Section 2 using estimates for the matrix of the block transition
probabilities. In Section 3 the considerations of Section 2 will result
in asymptotic results for the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix in the stochastic block model. As one can already learn from
Erdős-Rényi graphs spectral gap estimates for the adjacency matrix and the Laplacian matrix
play an important role for the order of magnitude of
H^{i}
.
These will be given in Section 4. Finally, Section 5 contains the proof of Theorem
1.1
, while in Section 6 we will prove
Theorem
1.2
.
In Section
8
we will then turn to the case of diagonal entries
p_{1}=\dots=p_{M}=p
of the diagonal of
P_{M}
. In that case, we are able to improve the conditions for our results substantically through relatively simple modifications of the proofs and additionally prove a central limit theorem.

We separate the problem into several distinct cases: Let

(1.9)

\displaystyle\kappa\coloneqq\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{(M-1)q}{p_{\min}}\in[0,\infty]

Throughout, we will always require that
\kappa
exists.
The expression
\kappa
can be thought of as a quantification of assortativity in the model: For
\kappa=0
, the model is very clearly (strongly) assortative, i.e. the intra-block connection probabilities are larger than the connection probabilities between
blocks.
On the other hand,
\kappa=\infty
does not guarantee a disassortative model
(i.e. all the intra-block connection probabilities are smaller than the connection probabilities between
blocks), yet guarantees that the edges within blocks are irrelevant on a macroscopic scale: The connectivity between different blocks dominates.

2.
Eigenvalues of the expected adjusted adjacency matrix

In this section we will discuss the spectrum of the expected adjacency matrix of a realization of the stochastic block model.

We begin by rescaling the transition probabilities suitably. Denote by
\Gamma
the diagonal matrix containing the expected degrees of vertices in each block, i.e.
\Gamma=\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{m})
. Then we rescale the matrix of transition probabilities as follows:

P_{M}^{\prime}=\Gamma^{-1/2}P_{M}\Gamma^{-1/2},\qquad\text{i.e. }p_{i,j}^{\prime}=\frac{p_{i,j}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}}.

Let us denote the (random) adjacency matrix of our random graph
G_{N}
by
A_{N}
, i.e.
A_{N}=(a_{v,w})_{v,w\in V_{N}}
and

a_{v,w}=1
, if
\{v,w\}\in E_{N}
, otherwise
a_{v,w}=0
.

The corresponding rescaled version of
A
is given by

(2.1)

A^{\prime}_{N}=({\mathbb{E}}D)^{-1/2}A_{N}({\mathbb{E}}D)^{-1/2},\qquad\text{i.e. }a_{v,w}^{\prime}=\frac{a_{v,w}}{\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}d_{v}{\mathbb{E}}d_{w}}},

where
{\mathbb{E}}D
denotes the
N
-dimensional diagonal matrix consisting of the expected degrees of the
N
vertices.

Not too surprisingly, it will turn out that the spectrum of
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
is closely related to the spectrum of
P_{M}^{\prime}
. We will
therefore start with a quick analysis of the eigenvalues of
P_{M}^{\prime}
.

Through graph conductance we can obtain an upper bound on the spectral gap of
P_{M}^{\prime}
:

Lemma 2.1
.

\frac{N}{M}\lambda_{2}(P_{M}^{\prime})\leq 1-{2}\cdot{\left(1+\frac{p_{M}}{(M-1)q}\right)^{-2}},

i.e. the rescaled second eigenvalue of
P_{M}^{\prime}
is bounded away from 1.

Proof.

We rewrite

P_{M}^{\prime}=\Gamma^{-1/2}P_{M}\Gamma^{-1/2}=\frac{M}{N}\Theta^{-1/2}P_{M}\Theta^{-1/2}

with

\Theta=\mathrm{diag}(p_{m}+(M-1)q)=\frac{M}{N}\mathrm{diag}(\gamma_{m}).

Then estimating the eigenvalues of
\Theta^{-1/2}P_{M}\Theta^{-1/2}
appropriately is sufficient to obtain the claim.

We consider
P_{M}
as the weight matrix of the weighted, undirected graph
G_{P}
on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,M\}
, i.e. the graph is complete where each edge between different vertices is assigned weight
q
and each vertex
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
has a loop with weight
p_{m}
, i.e. the weight function
w
is defined by
w(m,l)=p_{m}\mathbbm{1}_{m=l}+q\mathbbm{1}_{m\neq l}.

The degrees of the vertices in this graph are given by the sum of weights of adjacent edges, i.e.

\mathrm{deg}(m)=p_{m}+(M-1)q=\Theta_{m,m}.

Hence,

\Theta^{-1/2}P_{M}\Theta^{-1/2}
is the symmetrically normalized adjacency matrix of
G_{P}
. Let us further denote by
\mathcal{L}_{P}\coloneqq I-\Theta^{-1/2}P_{M}\Theta^{-1/2}
the symmetrically normalized Laplacian of
G_{P}
. We are going to compute the spectral gap of
\mathcal{L}_{P}
using Cheeger’s inequality on the weighted conductance of
G_{P}

(cf.
[
DS91
, Section 3]
, e.g.).
To that end, for a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
let us define for a subset
S\subseteq V
with edge boundary
\partial S
(i.e. the set of edges
\{m,l\}\in E
such that
m\in S
and
l\notin S
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{w}(S)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{m\in S}\deg_{w}(m)

\displaystyle\phi_{w}(S)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}_{w}(S)}\sum\limits_{e\in\partial S}w(e)

\displaystyle\phi_{w}(G)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\limits_{S:\mathrm{vol}_{w}(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{vol}_{w}(V)}\phi_{w}(S)

This latter quantity this called the conductance of
G
. In our situation, since we assumed that
p_{1}\geq\dots\geq p_{M}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\phi_{w}(G_{P})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{w}(\{M\})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{\deg}(M)}\sum\limits_{m\neq M}w(m,M)=\frac{1}{p_{M}+(M-1)q}\cdot(M-1)q=\frac{1}{1+\frac{p_{M}}{(M-1)q}}

Cheeger’s inequality states that the second smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{P}
is bounded from below by twice the square of the conductance of the graph, i.e.

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{M-1}(\mathcal{L}_{P})

\displaystyle\geq 2\phi_{w}^{2}(G_{P})=2\cdot{\left(1+\frac{p_{M}}{(M-1)q}\right)^{-2}}

Consequentially,

\frac{N}{M}\lambda_{2}(P_{M}^{\prime})=1-\lambda_{M-1}(\mathcal{L}_{P})\leq 1-{2}\cdot{\left(1+\frac{p_{M}}{(M-1)q}\right)^{-2}}.\qed

Next consider the expected adjacency matrix
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
(recall (
2.1
) for its definition) where we take expectation entry-wise.
Then,
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
consists of entries from
P_{M}^{\prime}
depending on the blocks the vertices are in.

More precisely,

{\mathbb{E}}a_{v,w}^{\prime}=\frac{p_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}
, if both
v
and
w

are in block
m,m=1,\ldots M
, otherwise
{\mathbb{E}}a_{v,w}^{\prime}=\frac{q}{\sqrt{\gamma_{B(v)}\gamma_{B(w)}}}
.
Not surprisingly, the spectrum of
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
can be simply derived from the eigenvalues of
P_{M}
:

Lemma 2.2
.

The matrix
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
has exactly
M
non-zero eigenvalues. These are given by

(2.3)

\frac{N}{M}\lambda_{m}(P_{M}^{\prime}),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

Proof.

Denoting by
J
the
(N/M)\times(N/M)
matrix in which each entry equals
1
, we see that

{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}=P_{M}^{\prime}\otimes J
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product of two matrices matrices.

Since
J
has only one non-zero eigenvalue, which is
\frac{N}{M}
,
we obtain that the
M
non-zero eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime}
are given by (
2.3
) as claimed.
∎

3.
Eigenvalues of the rescaled adjacency matrix

Let us now turn to the rescaled adjacency matrix
A_{N}^{\prime}
(again recall (
2.1
) for its definition).
More precisely, we consider the centered rescaled adjacency matrix of
G_{N}
,

X\coloneqq A_{N}^{\prime}-{\mathbb{E}}A_{N}^{\prime},

first.

Denote by

(3.1)

\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}(\max\limits_{m=1,\dots,M}p_{m}(1-p_{m})+(M-1)q(1-q)).

Then we can give an upper bound on the maximum eigenvalue (in absolute value) of the matrix
X
:

Theorem 3.1
.

There is a constant
c
such that

\|X\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\Big(2\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+c\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}\Big)

holds asymptotically almost surely.

Proof.

We modify the proof of
[
ACK15
]
, using the original approach by
[
Vu07
]
to our model.

The core idea is to apply Wigner’s trace method and estimate the terms in the trace of
X^{k}
appropriately.
To this end, we denote by
X_{v,w},v,w=1,\ldots,N
the entries of the matrix
X
. Note that each of the
X_{v,w}
can only take two values:
Either
X_{v,w}=1-p_{m}
and this happens with probability
p_{m}
or
X_{v,w}=-p_{m}
and this event
has probability
1-p_{m}
. Here
m
is the block number, if both
v
and
w
are in the
m
-th block, and
m=0
, otherwise.
We denote by

\sigma_{m}^{2}=p_{m}(1-p_{m})

the variance of these entries, depending on the edge’s block number
m\in\{0,\dots,M\}

Now clearly for an even integer
k
, we have

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(X^{k}\right)\right]=\sum\limits_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{v_{1},v_{2}}\cdots X_{v_{k-1},v_{k}}\cdot X_{v_{k},v_{1}}\right]

We interpret the sequence
I\coloneqq(v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{1})
as a circular path on the complete graph
K_{N}
with edge weights
X_{v,w}
for
an (undirected) edge
(v,w)\in V_{N}^{2}
.

Now each ordered sequence of vertices that constitutes
I=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{1})
corresponds to a number of
distinct
vertices
appearing in this sequence,

s_{I}\coloneqq|S_{I}|\coloneqq|\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}|

and a permutation of
S_{I}
, giving the order in which the
vertices appear for the first time in the sequence:
J_{I}\coloneqq(w_{1},\dots,w_{s_{I}})
such that

w_{1},\dots,w_{s_{I}}\in S_{I}
,
w_{l}\neq w_{m}
for
all
l\neq m
.

Notice that the expectation of the weights of a path is 0 if there is at least one edge
e
with multiplicity 1 (i.e.
e
only appears
once within
\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\dots\{v_{k-1},v_{k}\},\{v_{k},v_{1}\}
). We thus only consider paths in which each edge appears with multiplicity
at least two.
Because the path has
k
edges in total, the number of distinct edges,
\nu_{I}
,
in the path is bounded by
k/2
, as
k
is even.
In a circular path, the number of distinct vertices is at most the number of distinct edges. Hence we also have
s_{I}\leq k/2
.

Now, for
2\leq x\leq s_{I}
denote by
e_{x}^{I}
the first edge
(v_{l},v_{l+1})
on the path
I
such that
v_{l+1}=w_{x}

(i.e. the first edge that discovers the vertex
w_{x}
, the vertex
w_{1}
is excluded as the starting vertex of the path).

Notice that the absolute value of the entries of
X
is bounded by

K\coloneqq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\max\limits_{m=0,\dots,M}p_{m}(1-p_{m}).

We use this to remove edges from the product of weights of the path, keeping only “discovery” edges
e_{2}^{I},\dots,e_{s_{I}}^{I}
with multiplicity 2 each:

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{v_{1},v_{2}}\cdots X_{v_{k-1},v_{k}}\cdot X_{v_{k},v_{1}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k-2(s_{I}-1)}}K^{k-2(s_{I}-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod\limits_{x=2}^{s_{I}}X_{e_{x}^{I}}^{2}\right].

We denote for an edge
e=\{v,w\}
by

B(e)=\begin{cases}m,&\text{if }v,w\in V^{(m)}\\
0,&\text{if }v\in V^{(m)},w\in V^{(l)},\,l\neq m\end{cases}

the block number, if
v
and
w
are in the same block, and 0 otherwise.

As the discovery edges are clearly pairwise different (as each discovers a new vertex), we find

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod\limits_{x=2}^{s_{I}}X_{e_{x}^{I}}^{2}\right]=\prod\limits_{x=2}^{s_{I}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{e_{x}^{I}}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{2(s_{I}-1)}}\prod\limits_{x=2}^{s_{I}}\sigma_{B(e_{x}^{I})}^{2}.

Hence, we can write from
eq.

3.2

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(X^{k}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k/2}K^{k-2(s_{I}-1)}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\\
s_{I}=s\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{x=2}^{s_{I}}\sigma_{B(e_{x}^{I})}^{2}.

Let
s
be fixed.

To bound the inner sum, we choose the discovery vertices of the path successively. For the first one, we have
N
options.
For each following one
2\leq x\leq s
we have two scenarios:

•

If the associated
x
-th discovery edge
e_{x}
satisfies
B(e_{x})=m
for some
m=1,\dots,M
(i.e. the vertex discovery happens from the same block), then
\sigma_{B(e_{x})}^{2}=\sigma_{m}^{2}\leq\max\limits_{l=1,\dots,M}\sigma_{l}^{2}\eqqcolon\sigma_{*}^{2}
, and there are
N/M
possibilities to choose the
x
-th discovery vertex.

•

If the associated
x
-th discovery edge
e_{x}
satisfies
B(e_{x})=0
(i.e. the vertex discovery happens from a different block), then
\sigma_{B(e_{x})}^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}
and there are
(M-1)\cdot N/M
possibilities to choose the
x
-th discovery vertex.

Now that all options for the discovery sequence are exhausted, it remains to bound the number of paths with this exact discovery
sequence which also satisfies the same-block and different block-discoveries, respectively, as chosen above. This, however, is bounded
from above by the number of paths on
K_{s}
with this exact discovery sequence (i.e. we ignore the block restrictions for discovery).
This, in turn, is independent of the particular discovery sequence, but only depends on the length of the path
k
and the length of the
discovery sequence
s
. An upper bound for this is given by

(3.3)

W^{\prime}(k,s)\coloneqq\binom{k}{2s-2}2^{2k-2s+3}s^{k-2s+2}(k-2s+4)^{k-2s+2}

according to
[
Vu07
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Overall, we thus obtain by differentiating over the number of same-block discoveries
t

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(X^{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k/2}K^{k-2(s-1)}\cdot N\cdot\sum\limits_{t=0}^{s-1}\binom{s-1}{t}\left(\frac{N}{M}\sigma_{*}^{2}\right)^{t}\left((M-1)\frac{N}{M}\sigma_{0}^{2}\right)^{s-1-t}\cdot W^{\prime}(k,s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k/2}K^{k-2(s-1)}\cdot N\cdot\left(\frac{N}{M}\right)^{s-1}\left(\sigma_{*}^{2}+(M-1)\sigma_{0}^{2}\right)^{s-1}\cdot W^{\prime}(k,s)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{k/2}S(N,k,s).

with

S(N,k,s):=K^{k-2(s-1)}\cdot N\cdot\left(\frac{N}{M}\right)^{s-1}\left(\sigma_{*}^{2}+(M-1)\sigma_{0}^{2}\right)^{s-1}\cdot W^{\prime}(k,s).

Now notice that by the definition of
\sigma^{2}
(
3.1
) and by the definition of the
W^{\prime}
-terms
(
3.3
) we have for
s=1,\dots,k/2-1

\displaystyle\frac{S(N,k,s)}{S(N,k,s+1)}\hskip-56.9055pt

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{K^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{W^{\prime}(k,s)}{W^{\prime}(k,s+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{K^{2}}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{\binom{k}{2s-2}}{\binom{k}{2s}}\cdot\frac{2^{2k-2s+3}}{2^{2k-2s+1}}\cdot\frac{s^{k-2s+2}}{(s+1)^{k-2s}}\cdot\frac{(k-2s+4)^{k-2s+2}}{(k-2s+2)^{k-2s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{K^{2}}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{2s(2s-1)}{(k+2-2s)(k+1-2s)}\cdot 4\cdot\frac{s^{k-2s+2}}{(s+1)^{k-2s}}\cdot(k-2s+4)^{2}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2}{k-2s+2}\right)^{k-2s}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K^{2}}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{2s(2s-1)}{(k+2-2s)(k+1-2s)}\cdot 4\cdot s^{2}\cdot(k-2s+4)^{2}\cdot\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{k/2-s}\right)^{k/2-s}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8K^{2}s^{3}(2s-1)}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{(k-2s+4)^{2}}{(k+2-2s)(k+1-2s)}\cdot e^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8e^{2}K^{2}s^{3}(2s-1)}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{2}{k+2-2s}\right)\left(1+\frac{3}{k+1-2s}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8e^{2}K^{2}s^{3}(2s-1)}{N\sigma^{2}}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{5}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{80e^{2}K^{2}s^{4}}{N\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5e^{2}K^{2}k^{4}}{N\sigma^{2}}

Hence, one can easily see that for

(3.5)

k\leq\sqrt[4]{\frac{N}{10e^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{K^{2}}}

we obtain

\frac{S(N,k,s)}{S(N,k,s+1)}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, by geometric series and
eq.

3.4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}(X^{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}S(N,k,k/2+1)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}N\cdot N^{k/2}(\sigma^{2})^{k/2}\cdot 2^{k+1}=2N\left(2\frac{\sigma}{\gamma}\sqrt{N}\right)^{k}

Let
k=\sqrt[4]{\frac{N\sigma^{2}}{10e^{2}K^{2}}}
is an even integer, otherwise take the largest even integer bounded by
eq.

3.5
). Due to

\|X\|_{2}^{k}=\max\limits_{m=1,\dots,N}|\lambda_{m}(X)|^{k}\leq\sum\limits_{m=1}^{N}\lambda_{m}(X)^{k}=\mathrm{tr}(X^{k})

we obtain for some constant
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
by a high moment Markov inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|X\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}\Big(2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\min}^{k}}{(2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4})^{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}(X^{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\min}^{k}\cdot 2N\left(2\frac{\sigma}{\gamma_{\min}}\sqrt{N}\right)^{k}}{(2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4})^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2N\left(1-\frac{c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}}{2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-\frac{c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}\cdot k}{2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-\frac{c\log(N)\cdot N^{1/2}\cdot\sqrt[4]{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{10e^{2}K^{2}}}}{2\sigma\sqrt{N}+c\sigma^{1/2}\log(N)\cdot N^{1/4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-c\sigma^{1/2}\frac{\log(N)\sqrt[4]{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{10e^{2}K^{2}}}}{2\sigma+c\sigma^{1/2}\frac{\log(N)}{N^{1/4}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-c\sigma^{1/2}\frac{\log(N)\sqrt[4]{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{10e^{2}K^{2}}}}{3\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-c\frac{\log(N)}{3\sqrt[4]{10e^{2}}\sqrt{K}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2N\exp\left(-c\frac{2\log(N)}{3\sqrt[4]{10e^{2}}}\right)

where we use
eq.

1.1
and
K\leq\frac{1}{4}
. For sufficiently large constant
c
, the latter term converges to 0.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Together with

1.1
, the statement of

can be used to obtain

which follows

Moreover, notice that this also gives uniform bound on the eigenvalues of

We can finally give the asymptotically leading order of the eigenvalues of the symmetrically rescaled adjacency matrix
A^{\prime}
:

Proposition 3.3
.

The
M
largest eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}
are given by

\frac{N}{M}\lambda_{k}(P_{M}^{\prime})+O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right),

k=1,\dots,M
, with probability converging to 1. All other eigenvalues of

are bounded by

\lambda_{k}(A^{\prime})=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right)

for

, with probability converging to 1.

Proof.

For a
N\times N
-matrix
R
, denote by
\lambda_{1}(R)\geq\lambda_{2}(R)\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N}(R)
its eigenvalues in descending order.
As
A^{\prime}=X+{\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime}
, by Weyl’s interlacement theorem
[
Par98
, Theorem 10.3.1]

we have for
i,j=1,\dots,N
such that

\lambda_{N+1-i}(X)+\lambda_{N+1-j}({\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{N+2-i-j}(A^{\prime})\text{ and }\lambda_{i+j-1}(A^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{i}(X)+\lambda_{j}({\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime})

From Theorem
3.1
, we deduce that
\lambda_{i}(X)=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right)
asymptotically almost surely for
i=1,\dots,N
. Therefore,

O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right)+\lambda_{N+1-j}({\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{N+2-i-j}(A^{\prime})

and

\lambda_{i+j-1}(A^{\prime})\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right)+\lambda_{j}({\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime})

asymptotically almost surely.
With
i=1
,

in the first inequality and
j=k
,

in the second, we obtain for

and

\lambda_{k}(A^{\prime})\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{N\sigma^{2}}+\log(N)\sqrt[4]{N\sigma^{2}}}{\gamma_{\min}}\right)+\lambda_{k}({\mathbb{E}}A^{\prime})

and consequently,

with probability converging to 1.
Together with Lemma

∎

4.
Spectral gap of the symmetric normalized adjacency matrix

We now consider the matrix
B=D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}
, where
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
and
d_{i}
denotes the degree of the vertex
i
.
Since both the average hitting times have a decomposition in terms of the eigenvalues (and eigenvectors) of
B
we
are interested in bounding the spectrum of
B
.
Recall that

\gamma_{m}={\mathbb{E}}d_{v}=\frac{N}{M}(p_{m}+(M-1)q)

for
v\in V^{(m)}
,
m=1,\dots,M
. Furthermore, by Chernoff’s inequality (c.f. Theorem 2.4 in
[
CL06
]
), we find that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{v}>\gamma_{m}-c\sqrt{\gamma_{m}}&\quad\text{with probability }1-\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)\\
d_{v}<\gamma_{m}+c\sqrt{\gamma_{m}}&\quad\text{with probability }1-\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2\left(1+\frac{c}{3\sqrt{\gamma_{m}}}\right)}\right)\end{split}

Denote

R:=B-A^{\prime}.

It is well known that the spectral radius
\|R\|_{2}
is bounded by all consistent matrix norms and thus in particular
\|R\|_{2}\leq\|R\|_{\infty}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
R
as above, we have

\|R\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt[4]{\frac{\log N}{\gamma_{\min}}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}}\cdot(\sqrt{3}+o(1))

asymptotically almost surely.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma 3.3 in
[
LT14
]
but given here for the sake of completeness.
Clearly,
the entries of
R
are given by

r_{v,w}=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}-\sqrt{d_{m}d_{l}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}d_{v}d_{w}}}a_{v,w}

for
v\in V^{(m)}
,
w\in V^{(l)}
.
By setting
c={\sqrt{\log N}}
in
eq.

4.1
,

|d_{v}-\gamma_{m}|\leq\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{m}}\quad\mbox{for all $m\in\{1,\dots,M\}$ and all $v\in V^{(m)}$}

with probability converging to 1. Hence,

\displaystyle|d_{v}d_{w}-\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}|

\displaystyle=|d_{v}(d_{w}-\gamma_{m})+\gamma_{l}(d_{v}-\gamma_{m})|

\displaystyle=|(d_{v}-\gamma_{m})(d_{w}-\gamma_{l})|+\gamma_{m}|d_{w}-\gamma_{l}|+\gamma_{l}|d_{v}-\gamma_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\log N\cdot\gamma_{m}}\sqrt{\log N\cdot\gamma_{l}}+\gamma_{m}\sqrt{\log N\cdot\gamma_{l}}+\gamma_{l}\sqrt{\log N\cdot\gamma_{m}}

\displaystyle=\log N\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}+2\sqrt{\max(\gamma_{m},\gamma_{l})\log N}\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{\max}\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}

and thus

|\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}-\sqrt{d_{v}d_{w}}|\leq\sqrt{|d_{v}d_{w}-\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}|}\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}

with probability tending to 1.
Furthermore,

d_{v}d_{w}>(\gamma_{m}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{m}})(\gamma_{l}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{l}})

with probability converging to 1 for all
m,l\in\{1,\dots,M\}
and
v\in V^{(m)},w\in V^{(l)}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|R\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1,\dots,M\\
v\in V^{(m)}\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{w\in V}|r_{v,w}|=\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1,\dots,M\\
v\in V^{(m)}\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1,\dots,M\\
w\in V^{(l)}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}-\sqrt{d_{v}d_{w}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}d_{v}d_{w}}}a_{v,w}\right|

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1,\dots,M\\
v\in V^{(m)}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1,\dots,M\\
w\in V^{(l)}\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{v,w}}{\sqrt[4]{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}\sqrt{d_{v}d_{w}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}})\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1,\dots,M\\
v\in V^{(m)}\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1,\dots,M\\
w\in V^{(l)}\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{v,w}}{\sqrt[4]{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{l}}\sqrt{(\gamma_{m}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{m}})(\gamma_{l}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{l}})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}}\max\limits_{m,l=1,\dots,M}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\gamma_{l}}\sqrt{\gamma_{l}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{l}}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\gamma_{m}}\sqrt{\gamma_{m}-\sqrt{\log N\gamma_{m}}}}\max\limits_{v\in V^{(m)}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1,\dots,M\\
w\in V^{(l)}\end{subarray}}a_{v,w}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}}\max\limits_{m,l=1,\dots,M}\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\gamma_{m}^{3}\gamma_{l}^{3}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\gamma_{l}}}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\gamma_{m}}}}}\gamma_{m}\Big(1+\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{\gamma_{m}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\gamma_{\max}}\max\limits_{m,l=1,\dots,M}\sqrt[4]{\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{l}^{3}}}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\log N\frac{\gamma_{\max}^{2}}{\gamma_{\min}^{3}}}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\sqrt[4]{\frac{\log N}{\gamma_{\min}}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{\max}}{\gamma_{\min}}}(1+o(1))

with probability converging to 1, since by condition (
1.1
)
\gamma_{m}\gg\log^{4}(N)
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.
∎

6.
Spectral decomposition of the hitting time averaged over the starting vertex and proof of Theorem

Using the spectral decomposition of the hitting times according to
[

]

We immediately notice using (

\displaystyle\frac{2|E|-|L|}{d_{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{v\in V^{(m)}}d_{v}}{d_{w}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{B(w)}}(1+o(1))=\frac{N}{M}\cdot\frac{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{B(w)}}\cdot(1+o(1)),

asymptotically almost surely (recall that
B(v)
denotes the block which vertex

We order the eigenvalues and abbreviate the corresponding normalized eigenvectors of

such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
(where

We proceed in a similar way as in

]

that

\displaystyle Z_{N}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{m}u_{k,w}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\left(1+\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{2}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{m}\right)u_{k,w}^{2}

Now one immediately checks that the eigenvector corresponding to

, i.e.

. Moreover, the matrix of the eigenvectors

From the unitarity of

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\lambda_{k}u_{k,w}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}u_{k,w}^{2}-\pi_{w}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}B_{w,v}u_{k,v}u_{k,w}-\pi_{w}

Here we denote the entries of the matrix

by

.

Thus we arrive at

\displaystyle Z_{N}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}u_{k,w}^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\lambda_{k}u_{k,w}^{2}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}}\lambda_{k}^{2}u_{k,w}^{2}=1+B_{w,w}-2\pi_{w}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}.

Under the conditions of Theorem

Proof.

Using (

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}u_{k,w}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}(\lambda_{k}u_{k,w})^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\Big(\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}B_{w,v}u_{k,v}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v^{\prime}=1}^{N}B_{w,v}B_{w,v^{\prime}}u_{k,v}u_{k,v^{\prime}}=\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v^{\prime}=1}^{N}B_{w,v}B_{w,v^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}u_{k,v}u_{k,v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v^{\prime}=1}^{N}B_{w,v}B_{w,v^{\prime}}\langle u_{\boldsymbol{\cdot},v},u_{\boldsymbol{\cdot},v^{\prime}}\rangle=\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{v^{\prime}=1}^{N}B_{w,v}B_{w,v^{\prime}}\delta_{v,v^{\prime}}=\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}B_{w,v}^{2}=\sum\limits_{v=1}^{N}\frac{a_{v,w}}{d_{v}d_{w}}

with probability converging to 1.
Therefore, we find that

\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}u_{k,w}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}u_{k,w}^{2}-u_{1,w}^{2}\\
\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}-\sqrt{\log N\cdot\gamma_{\min}}}-\pi_{w}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}}(1+o(1))-\frac{1}{N}(1+o(1))=O(\gamma_{\min}^{-1})

with probability converging to 1.

For
\kappa>0
, we apply Corollary

and (

) to obtain, with probability converging to 1,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}\leq\frac{1+o(1)}{c}\cdot\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}u_{k,w}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\gamma_{\min}}

for some suitable constant

For
\kappa=0
,
we use Corollary

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}\leq\frac{p_{\min}^{2}}{2((M-1)q)^{2}}\cdot\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}u_{k,w}^{2}\leq\frac{p_{\min}^{2}}{2((M-1)q)^{2}}\cdot O(\gamma_{\min}^{-1}).

For

\displaystyle\ll\frac{p_{\min}^{2}}{\gamma_{\min}\left(\frac{M\log N}{N}\right)^{1/4}p_{\min}^{5/4}p_{\max}^{1/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\min}^{3/4}(1+o(1))(\log N)^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{p_{\min}^{1/2}}{p_{\max}^{1/2}}\leq 1

So the expression in (

7.
A central limit theorem for

In order to obtain a central limit theorem for

, the same decomposition as previously seen in
(

) will become useful. Indeed, we will see that the scaling as well as the centering of the central limit
theorem solely depend on the factor

again (see also the statement of Theorem

). This
means that the statement in (

of the spectral term has to be made more precise as well to account for the scaling of the central limit theorem.

Consider the following definitions of the expected number and variance of edges within and between blocks,
\mu_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{out}}
:
with

\displaystyle\mu_{m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\binom{N/M+1}{2}p_{m},\quad m=1,\dots,M

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{m},\quad

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg[\binom{N+1}{2}-M\binom{N/M+1}{2}\bigg]q=\binom{M}{2}\left(\frac{N}{M}\right)^{2}q

\displaystyle\tau_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\binom{N/M+1}{2}p_{m}(1-p_{m}),\quad m=1,\dots,M

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\tau_{m}^{2},\quad

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigg[\binom{N+1}{2}-M\binom{N/M+1}{2}\bigg]q(1-q)=\binom{M}{2}\left(\frac{N}{M}\right)^{2}q(1-q)

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tau_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}+\tau_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}.

Notice that
{\mathbb{E}}[|E|]=\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}}
.
and hence, due to

2|E|=\sum\limits_{v\in V}d_{v}+|L|
,

(7.1)

2(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}})=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}\gamma_{m}+\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}p_{m}=\frac{N}{M}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}(\gamma_{m}+p_{m}).

The proof of Theorem
1.3
can be split into several parts. More precisely, we break down
the spectral decomposition of
H_{w}
into its factors.

Then, the following proposition holds true:

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
1.3
hold, then

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{N\upsilon_{m}}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\bar{\gamma}}\cdot\left(2\frac{|E|-\frac{|L|}{2}}{d_{w}}-\frac{N\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{m}}\right)\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

The proof of Proposition
7.1
will be given with the help of several lemmas.

Lemma 7.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.3

\frac{1}{{\tau}}\Big(|E|-(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}})\Big)\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Notice that condition (
1.8
) will not be necessary for the proof of this lemma.

Proof.

For two vertices
v,w\in V
we denote by
\varepsilon_{v,w}
the random variable valued 1 if there is an edge between
v
and
w
and 0 otherwise. Clearly,
\varepsilon_{v,w}
is Bernoulli-distributed with parameter
q
is
v
and
w
are in different blocks and with parameter
p_{m}
if they are both contained in the same block
V^{(m)}
,
m=1,\dots,m
.

Then

|E|=\sum\limits_{v,w\in V_{N}}\varepsilon_{v,w}

with

{\mathbb{E}}|E|=\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}\left(|E|\right)=\tau_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}+\tau_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}=\tau^{2}

For
v,w\in V_{N}
let
\alpha_{v,w}=\varepsilon_{v,w}-{\mathbb{E}}\varepsilon_{v,w}
. Then
\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\alpha_{v,w}

\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\mathbb{V}\left(\alpha_{v,w}\right)=\tau^{2}<\infty
.

Then notice that
\tau^{2}\to\infty
as

due to (

). Therefore,

\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\alpha_{v,w}|>\varepsilon\tau\}}=0

for sufficiently large

and therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{v,w}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\alpha_{v,w}|>\varepsilon\tau\}}\right]=0

for sufficiently large

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\mathbb{V}\left(\alpha_{v,w}\right)}\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha_{v,w}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\alpha_{v,w}|>\varepsilon\tau\}}\right]=0

is satisfied. Therefore,

\frac{1}{\tau}(|E|-(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}}))=\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\mathbb{V}\left(\alpha_{v,w}\right)}\sum\limits_{v,w\in V}\alpha_{v,w}\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1).\qed

\displaystyle\frac{|L|-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}p_{m}}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}p_{m}(1-p_{m})}}\xRightarrow{N\to\infty}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

We notice that

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\frac{1}{\tau}\Big(|E|-\frac{|L|}{2}-\big(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}}-\frac{1}{2}{\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\frac{N}{M}p_{m}}\big)\Big)=\frac{1}{\tau}\Big(|E|-\big(\mu_{\mathrm{in}}+\mu_{\mathrm{out}}\big)\Big)

A final ingredient is needed to complete the proof of the central limit theorem:

Proposition 7.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\left(\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}-1\right)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}0.

Proof.

To prove the proposition, let us rewrite the term in the brackets as above.

\displaystyle Z_{N}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}=1+B_{w,w}-2\pi_{w}+\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}.

As
\pi_{v}=\frac{d_{v}}{2|E|}
for all

are identically distributed
it is obvious that the
\pi_{v}
are identically distributed for
v\in V
.
Furthermore,

\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}=2|E|
.
Thus we obtain

N\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{w}\right]=\sum\limits_{v\in V}\mathbb{E}\left[\pi_{v}\right]=\frac{1}{2|E|}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\sum\limits_{v\in V}d_{v}\Bigr]=1.

Therefore,

{\mathbb{E}}\Bigl[\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\pi_{w}\Bigr]=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{N\upsilon_{m}}\leq\frac{1}{\upsilon_{m}}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0

and by non-negativity of
\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\pi_{w}
and Markov’s inequality,

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\pi_{w}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}0.

and similarly using (
4.1
)

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}B_{w,w}=\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\cdot\frac{a_{w,w}}{d_{w}}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}0.

Finally, we have from Lemma
6.1
that

\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}\leq C\frac{p_{\min}^{2}}{\gamma_{\min}(M-1)^{2}q^{2}}

Then due to (
1.8
)

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{k}}u_{k,w}^{2}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0.

So altogether, we obtain

\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\upsilon_{m}}(Z_{N}-1)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}0.\qed

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We only give the proof in the case
\tilde{\kappa}\in(0,\infty)

Notice that from Proposition

and

converging in probability to 0 such that

\displaystyle\frac{2|E|-|L|}{d_{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{m}}+\frac{N\upsilon_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}\cdot\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{m}}\cdot X_{N}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\upsilon_{m}}{\gamma_{m}}\cdot X_{N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{m}}{N\upsilon_{m}}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\bar{\gamma}}\cdot\left(H_{w}-\frac{N\bar{\gamma}}{\gamma_{m}}\right)\hskip-113.81102pt

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|R\|_{\infty}=\max\limits_{v\in V}\sum\limits_{w\in V}|r_{v,w}|
