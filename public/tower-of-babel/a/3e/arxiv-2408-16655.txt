Optimal Trace Distance and Fidelity Estimations for Pure Quantum States
Qisheng Wang

Abstract
Measuring the distinguishability between quantum states is a basic problem in quantum information theory. In this paper, we develop optimal quantum algorithms that estimate both the trace distance and the (square root) fidelity between pure states to within additive error ε \varepsilon using Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \Theta(1/\varepsilon) queries to their state-preparation circuits, quadratically improving the long-standing folklore O ⁡ ( 1 / ε 2 ) O(1/\varepsilon^{2}) . At the heart of our construction, is an algorithmic tool for quantum square root amplitude estimation, which generalizes the well-known quantum amplitude estimation.



Keywords: quantum computing, quantum algorithms, trace distance, pure states, square root fidelity, quantum query complexity, quantum amplitude estimation.

Introduction

The distinguishability between quantum states is an important topic in quantum information theory.
Trace distance and fidelity are the most common measures of the closeness of two quantum states (cf.
[
1
]
).
For two mixed quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
, the trace distance between them is defined by (cf.
[
1
, Equation (9.11)]
)

\mathrm{T}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\lparen\lvert\rho-\sigma\rvert\rparen.

(1)

The fidelity between
\rho
and
\sigma
is defined by (cf.
[
1
, Equation (9.53)]
)

\mathrm{F}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen=\operatorname{tr}\left\lparen\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}\right\rparen.

(2)

An alternative definition of fidelity also appears in the literature, defined by (cf.
[
2
,
3
]
)

\mathrm{F}^{2}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen=\left\lparen\operatorname{tr}\left\lparen\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\rho\sqrt{\sigma}}\right\rparen\right\rparen^{2},

(3)

which is equal to the square of
\mathrm{F}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen
.
To avoid confusion, throughout this paper, we call
\mathrm{F}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen
the square root fidelity, and call
\mathrm{F}^{2}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen
the squared fidelity.

The trace distance and fidelity indicate how close two quantum states are.
When
\rho
and
\sigma
are close, the value of
\mathrm{T}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen
is close to
0
while the value of
\mathrm{F}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen
is close to
1
.
A quantitative relationship between trace distance and fidelity was given in
[
4

that

1-\mathrm{F}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen\leq\mathrm{T}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen\leq\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}^{2}\lparen\rho,\sigma\rparen}.

(4)

When
\rho=\lvert\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi\rvert
and
\sigma=\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert
are pure quantum states, the relationship between the two measures turns out to be simpler (cf.
[
5
, Equation (9.173)]
):

\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}^{2}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen},

(5)

where

\mathrm{F}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen=\left\lvert\left<\varphi\vphantom{\psi}\middle|\psi\vphantom{\varphi}\right>\right\rvert.

(6)

An important question is how to estimate the value of the trace distance and fidelity between two quantum states so that we can analyze their closeness quantitatively.
This is not solely a theoretical question, but also has potential in testing the equivalence of quantum circuits, the validity of quantum devices, the correctness of quantum programs, etc.

1.2
Main Results

As quantum states are usually considered as the quantum analog of classical probability distributions,
the estimations of trace distance and (square root) fidelity of quantum states can be seen as quantum analogs of the closeness estimation of classical probability distributions.
Optimal estimators have been known for closeness measures of probability distributions such as total variation distance and entropy
[
31
]
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, we are only aware of the folklore approaches for estimating the trace distance and square root fidelity between pure states which are based on the SWAP test
[
14
]
and have query complexity
O\lparen 1/\varepsilon^{2}\rparen
for additive error
\varepsilon
(see
Lemma

A.3
and
Lemma

A.2
).
3
3

3

In the special case where the amplitudes of the pure states are guaranteed to be real numbers, a quantum query algorithm for square root fidelity estimation with query complexity
O\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen
was implied in
[
32
, Theorem 2.1]
. However, their method does not directly apply to the general complex-valued case.

In this paper, we propose quantum query algorithms for estimating the trace distance and square root fidelity between pure quantum states with query complexity
\Theta\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen
, assuming that their state-preparation circuits are given as quantum unitary oracles.
Moreover, we show that they are
optimal
by providing matching lower bounds on the quantum query complexity of pure-state trace distance and square root fidelity estimations.
The input model adopted in our quantum algorithms is commonly employed in the study of quantum query algorithms regarding quantum states; for example, the query complexity of quantum state tomography was studied in
[
9
]
.
See
Section

2.2
for the formal definition of this input model.

We state our main results in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Optimal pure-state trace distance and square root fidelity estimations)
.

•

\varepsilon

In a nutshell,

1.3.1
Quantum State Tomography

Quantum state tomography
[
11
,
12
]
is a fundamental problem in quantum computing.
The task is to estimate an unknown quantum state to certain precision.
Existing approaches mainly measure errors in trace distance and infidelity.
In particular, the tomography of a
d
-dimensional pure quantum state can be done optimally by using
\Theta\lparen d/\varepsilon^{2}\rparen
samples to error
\varepsilon
in trace distance
[
12
]
, and by using
\widetilde{\Theta}\lparen d/\delta\rparen
samples to error
\delta
in infidelity
[
11
]

When testing a concrete implementation for quantum state tomography, one may wish to see if its output is correct (i.e., with high enough precision) provided that the input state for testing is known in advance.
A simple way is to check if the trace distance (resp. infidelity) between the input and output states is small enough.

Suppose that the input state
\lvert\psi_{\textup{in}}\rangle
can be prepared by a known quantum circuit of size
T_{\textup{in}}
, and the classical description of the output state
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle
is given such that
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle
can be prepared with time complexity
\operatorname{polylog}\lparen d\rparen=\widetilde{O}\lparen 1\rparen
.
4
4

4

It was shown in
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
that any
d
-dimensional (pure) quantum state can be prepared by a quantum circuit of depth
\Theta\lparen\log\lparen d\rparen\rparen
. Moreover, if the classical description of a pure quantum state is stored in QRAM (quantum random access memory)
[
40
]
equipped with a specific tree data structure
[
41

, then the pure state can be prepared with time complexity
\operatorname{polylog}\lparen d\rparen
on a quantum computer. It is noted that the controlled version of the state-preparation and its inverse can also be implemented with the same time complexity.

We can estimate the trace distance and the infidelity between
\lvert\psi_{\textup{in}}\rangle
and
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle
on a quantum computer through the pure-state trace distance and square root fidelity estimations given in
Theorem

1.1
.
To assess with high probability whether the tomography is successful, one can employ the known state
\lvert\psi_{\textup{in}}\rangle
as the input for the tomography. Subsequently, the tomography produces the resulting state
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle

Following this approach,
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle

\lvert\psi_{\textup{in}}\rangle
and
\lvert\psi_{\textup{out}}\rangle

.

and

1.3.2
Quantum State Discrimination

We consider two situations of quantum state discrimination (cf.
[
42

Minimum-error quantum hypothesis testing.

In quantum hypothesis testing, a pure quantum state
\lvert\phi\rangle
is given such that the two cases hold with equal probability: (i)
\lvert\phi\rangle=\lvert\varphi\rangle
, and (ii)
\lvert\phi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle

\lvert\varphi\rangle
and

A basic problem is to find the minimum error probability for distinguishing the two cases.
By the Helstrom-Holevo bound
[
45

46

p_{\textup{err}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen.

(7)

However, it is necessary to know the minimum error for specific circumstances in practice.
For the two known quantum states
\lvert\varphi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle
, once we have their state-preparation circuits, we can compute the probability
p_{\textup{err}}
efficiently on a quantum computer through the pure-state trace distance estimation given in
Theorem

1.1
.

Proposition 1.3

(Computing the minimum error of quantum hypothesis testing)
.

Suppose that we are given two quantum circuits of size
T_{\varphi}
and
T_{\psi}
that prepare the two known
d
-dimensional pure states
\lvert\varphi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle
, respectively.
Then, we can compute the minimum error probability of the quantum hypothesis testing for
\lvert\varphi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle
to within additive error
\varepsilon
on a quantum computer with time complexity
\widetilde{O}\lparen\lparen T_{\varphi}+T_{\psi}\rparen/\varepsilon\rparen
.

It is worth noting that the algorithm in
Proposition

1.3
achieves a linear dependence on the precision
\varepsilon
, and prior to this, the folklore approach based on squared fidelity estimation (i.e., the SWAP test) will only result in a quadratic dependence on
\varepsilon
.

When considering the sample complexity of quantum state discrimination, one is given with independent and identical samples of an unknown quantum state
\lvert\phi\rangle

The task is to determine whether
\lvert\phi\rangle=\lvert\varphi\rangle
or
\lvert\phi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle

\lvert\varphi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle

\lvert\varphi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle

By the pure-state square root fidelity estimation in

1.4
Techniques

1.4.1
Upper Bounds

A straightforward approach for pure-state trace distance estimation is to first estimate the fidelity between pure quantum states and then compute the trace distance by
Equation

5
using arithmetic operations, which will result in a query complexity of
O\lparen 1/\varepsilon^{2}\rparen
(see
Appendix

A
for detailed explanations).
Here, the difficulty comes from the numerical stability when taking the square root (see
Proposition

3.2
).
A possible way to improve this approach is to skip these square root operations.
In fact, this can be accomplished through the very nature of quantum computing.

At a high level, square roots are essentially ubiquitous in the basis of quantum computing.
As an illustration, when we measure a pure quantum state
\lvert\psi\rangle=\alpha\lvert 0\rangle+\beta\lvert 1\rangle
in the computational basis, the outcome will be
0
with probability
p_{0}=\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}
and
1
with probability
p_{1}=\lvert\beta\rvert^{2}
.
As shown in this example,
the absolute values of the amplitudes actually store the square root of the probabilities
, as
\lvert\alpha\rvert=\sqrt{p_{0}}
and
\lvert\beta\rvert=\sqrt{p_{1}}
.
The quantum amplitude estimation
[
33
]
allows us to estimate the probability
p_{0}
to within additive error
\varepsilon
with query complexity
O\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen
, which means that we can estimate
\sqrt{p_{0}}
to within additive error
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
if we directly take the square root of the estimated value of
p_{0}
(the numerical error is guaranteed by
Proposition

3.2
).
Regarding this, a natural question is:

\begin{gathered}\textit{Can we directly learn the square root of probabilities}\\
\textit{rather than just the probabilities themselves}\\
\textit{on a quantum computer?}\end{gathered}

(
*
)

If this could be done efficiently, the task of pure-state trace distance estimation (as well as pure-state fidelity estimation) would be a candidate application that is noteworthy.
To see this, suppose that
U_{\varphi}
and
U_{\psi}
are two quantum unitary operators that prepare two
k
-qubit pure quantum states
\lvert\varphi\rangle=U_{\varphi}\lvert 0\rangle
and
\lvert\psi\rangle=U_{\psi}\lvert 0\rangle
, respectively.
Then, we can construct a unitary operator

\displaystyle W=

\displaystyle\left\lparen X_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\langle 0\rvert+\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}-1}I_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\lvert j\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\langle j\rvert\right\rparen\cdot\left\lparen I_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes\left\lparen U_{\varphi}^{\dagger}U_{\psi}\right\rparen_{\mathsf{B}}\right\rparen,

(9)

which prepares a pure quantum state with the trace distance
\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen
encoded in one of its amplitudes.
More precisely,

W\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}=\sqrt{p}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}+\sqrt{1-p}\lvert 1\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}

(10)

for some normalized pure quantum states
\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle
, where
\sqrt{p}=\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\varphi\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle\rparen
.
See the proof of
Theorem

4.1
for more details about pure-state trace distance estimation.
The aforementioned approach for pure-state trace distance estimation can also be adjusted to pure-state square root fidelity estimation effortlessly by noting that

Square Root Amplitude Estimation.

Lastly, we give a positive answer to the question (

‣

As the name implies, the square root amplitude estimation allows us to directly estimate the square root of the probability

Theorem 1.5

(Square root amplitude estimation,

Suppose that

(11)

\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle

\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle

\sqrt{p}
to within additive error
\varepsilon

O\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen

The square root amplitude estimation given in

1.5

[
33

(which will result in query complexity

In spite of the quadratic speedup, our algorithm for square root amplitude estimation essentially builds on the framework of the original amplitude estimation
[

, with a simple observation.
5
5

5

In

To introduce the idea, let us review the main process of amplitude estimation

Let

\displaystyle Q=

\displaystyle-U\cdot\left\lparen I_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes I_{\mathsf{B}}-2\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\langle 0\rvert\otimes\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\langle 0\rvert\right\rparen\cdot U^{\dagger}\cdot\left\lparen I_{\mathsf{A}}\otimes I_{\mathsf{B}}-2\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\langle 0\rvert\otimes I_{\mathsf{B}}\right\rparen,

After we obtain an estimate

1.4.2

Our quantum query lower bound for pure-state trace distance estimation is obtained by a reduction from distinguishing probability distributions, with the quantum query lower bound
\Omega\lparen 1/d_{\textup{H}}\lparen p,q\rparen\rparen
for distinguishing two probability distributions

In this paper, we provide

2
Quantum Query Model

In this section, we briefly introduce the quantum query model, especially the input query model for pure quantum states that is used in this paper.

2.1
The General Model

In quantum computing, a quantum unitary oracle
U
is a unitary operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space such that
U^{\dagger}U=UU^{\dagger}=I
, where
I
is the identity operator and
U^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian conjugate of
U
.
A quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
using
Q
queries to quantum oracle
U
is described by a quantum circuit composed of quantum gates and the quantum oracle
U
(and its inverse
U^{\dagger}
), namely,

\mathcal{A}=G_{Q}\cdot U_{Q}\cdot\cdots\cdot G_{2}\cdot U_{2}\cdot G_{1}\cdot U_{1}\cdot G_{0},

(15)

where
G_{j}
is a quantum gate composed of elementary one- and two-qubit quantum gates that does not depend on
U
, and
U_{j}
is either (controlled-)
U
or (controlled-)
U^{\dagger}
.
Here,
Q
is called the (quantum) query complexity of
\mathcal{A}
.
To run the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we first prepare a (multi-qubit) pure quantum state
\lvert 0\rangle
, and then apply the unitary operators
G_{0},U_{1},G_{1},\dots,U_{Q},G_{Q}
one by one in this order.
After that, the pure quantum state becomes

\mathcal{A}\lvert 0\rangle=G_{Q}U_{Q}\cdots G_{2}U_{2}G_{1}U_{1}G_{0}\lvert 0\rangle.

(16)

To fetch (classical) information from the execution of quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we usually make a quantum projective measurement
M=\{P_{m}\}
in the computational basis of (a subset of) the qubits that
\mathcal{A}
acts on.
Here, we note that
P_{m}
are projectors, i.e.,
P_{m}^{\dagger}=P_{m}
and
P_{m}^{2}=P_{m}
, and they satisfy the completeness equation
\sum_{m}P_{m}=I
.
Then,
\mathcal{A}
outputs
m
with probability
\lVert P_{m}\mathcal{A}\lvert 0\rangle\rVert^{2}
.

2.2
The Input Model for Pure Quantum States

For quantum query algorithms regarding pure quantum states, we assume that their state-preparation circuits are given as quantum unitary oracles.
Strictly speaking, quantum query access to a pure quantum state
\lvert\psi\rangle
means a quantum unitary oracle
U_{\psi}
such that

\lvert\psi\rangle=U_{\psi}\lvert 0\rangle.

(17)

This input model was employed in quantum state tomography in
[
9
]
, where they show that the quantum query complexity of obtaining an
\varepsilon
-
\ell_{2}
-approximation of an
n
-dimensional pure quantum state is
\widetilde{\Theta}\lparen n/\varepsilon\rparen
.
This input model was also employed as an adapter for problems that are not directly related to pure quantum states, e.g., encoding probability distributions (cf.
[
54
]
) and preparing purifications of mixed quantum states (cf.
[
8
]
).

3.4
), which generalizes the quantum amplitude estimation proposed in
[

]

For a unitary operator

In comparison, the square root amplitude estimation provided in

allows us to estimate the square root of

p
simply by amplitude estimation and then computing the square root of the estimated value.

This section is organized as follows.
In
Section

, we recall the quantum amplitude estimation proposed in
[

In

3.1
Amplitude Estimation

The well-known quantum query algorithm for amplitude estimation was proposed in
[
33
]
.
We recall a simple version of it when no prior knowledge of
p
is known, given as follows.

Theorem 3.1

(Quantum amplitude estimation,
[
33
, Theorem 12]
)
.

Suppose that
U
is a unitary operator such that

U\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}=\sqrt{p}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}+\sqrt{1-p}\lvert 1\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}},

(18)

where
\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle
are normalized pure states, and
p\in[0,1]
.
For every
\delta\in\lparen 0,1\rparen
, there is a quantum query algorithm that outputs
\tilde{x}\in[0,1]
with probability at least
2/3
such that

\left\lvert\tilde{x}-p\right\rvert<\delta,

(19)

using
O\lparen 1/\delta\rparen
queries to
U
.

Theorem

3.1
allows us to estimate the squared amplitude
p
to within additive error
\delta
with query complexity
O\lparen 1/\delta\rparen
.
If one needs to estimate the square root of
p
to within additive error
\delta^{\prime}
, then one has to set
\delta=\delta^{\prime 2}
and the total query complexity is
O\lparen 1/\delta^{\prime 2}\rparen
.
This is because the error could become larger when taking additional arithmetic operations on the estimated value.
To better explain this, we provide the following proposition concerning the numerical stability of square root.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
x,\tilde{x}\geq 0
be two real numbers such that
\lvert x-\tilde{x}\rvert<\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Then,
\lvert\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{\tilde{x}}\rvert<\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, and the equality holds when
x=0
and
\tilde{x}=\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\tilde{x}\geq x
and let
\delta=\tilde{x}-x\in\lparen 0,\varepsilon\rparen
.
Then, to show
\lvert\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{\tilde{x}}\rvert<\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we only need to demonstrate that for any
x\geq 0
and
\delta>0
, the following inequality holds:

\sqrt{x+\delta}-\sqrt{x}\leq\sqrt{\delta}.

(20)

This is straightforward because we note that
\sqrt{x+\delta}\leq\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{\delta}
. If we square both sides, we obtain
x+\delta\leq x+\delta+2\sqrt{x\delta}
, which simplifies to
\sqrt{x\delta}\geq 0
.

Therefore, it always holds that
\lvert\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{\tilde{x}}\rvert<\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. In addition, it is easy to verify that the equality holds when
x=0
and
\tilde{x}=\varepsilon
, meaning that the inequality is tight.
∎

As demonstrated in
Proposition

3.2
, one cannot hope to estimate
\sqrt{p}
to within additive error

with query complexity better than

In this subsection, we will present a quantum query algorithm for square root amplitude estimation.

]

Theorem 3.3

(Quantum phase estimation,
[
1

)
.

Suppose that
U
is a unitary operator with spectral decomposition

U=\sum_{j}e^{i2\pi\lambda_{j}}\lvert v_{j}\rangle,

(21)

where
\{\lvert v_{j}\rangle\}
is an orthonormal basis and
\lambda_{j}\in[0,1)

For every
\varepsilon\in\lparen 0,1\rparen
and

\mathsf{PhE}^{U}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
using

\mathsf{PhE}^{U}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\colon\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\lvert v_{j}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle\mapsto\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\lvert v_{j}\rangle\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{j}\rangle

\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{j}\rangle

\min\left\{\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right\rvert,1-\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right\rvert\right\}<\delta.

(23)

In the following, we present the quantum query algorithm for square root amplitude estimation.

Theorem 3.4

(Quantum square root amplitude estimation)
.

Suppose that
U
is a unitary operator such that

U\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}=\sqrt{p}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}+\sqrt{1-p}\lvert 1\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}},

where
\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle
are normalized pure states, and
p\in[0,1]

For every
\delta\in\lparen 0,1\rparen
, there is a quantum algorithm that outputs
\tilde{x}=\mathsf{SqrtAmpEst}\lparen U,\delta\rparen\in[0,1]
such that it holds with probability at least

\left\lvert\tilde{x}-\sqrt{p}\right\rvert<\delta,

(25)

using

U
and controlled-

Proof.

The idea of the proof follows that in

Let

According to the analysis of

U\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lparen e^{i\theta_{p}}\lvert\psi_{+}\rangle_{\mathsf{AB}}-e^{-i\theta_{p}}\lvert\psi_{-}\rangle_{\mathsf{AB}}\right\rparen,

(27)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{p}

\displaystyle=\arcsin\left\lparen\sqrt{p}\right\rparen\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right],

(28)

\displaystyle\lvert\psi_{\pm}\rangle_{\mathsf{AB}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lparen\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{0}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\pm i\lvert 1\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\right\rparen.

Note that

By

\displaystyle\mathsf{PhE}^{Q}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\left\lparen U\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{A}}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{B}}\otimes\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathsf{C}}\right\rparen=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lparen e^{i\theta_{p}}\lvert\psi_{+}\rangle_{\mathsf{AB}}\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{+}\rangle_{\mathsf{C}}-e^{-i\theta_{p}}\lvert\psi_{-}\rangle_{\mathsf{AB}}\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{-}\rangle_{\mathsf{C}}\right\rparen,

where if we measure

in the computational basis, then with probability at least

\displaystyle\min\left\{\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{+}-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rvert,1-\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{+}-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rvert\right\}<\delta,

\displaystyle\min\left\{\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{-}-\left\lparen 1-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rparen\right\rvert,1-\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}_{-}-\left\lparen 1-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rparen\right\rvert\right\}<\delta.

in the computational basis of system

, and let

Then, it can be verified that
\lvert\lvert\sin\lparen\pi\tilde{\varphi}\rparen\rvert-\sqrt{p}\rvert<\pi\delta
.
To see this, we note that with probability at least

, the real number
\tilde{\varphi}
satisfies the condition for either
\tilde{\varphi}_{+}
or
\tilde{\varphi}_{-}
in
Equation

31

No matter which is the case, we have

\displaystyle\big\lvert\left\lvert\sin\lparen\pi\tilde{\varphi}\rparen\right\rvert-\sqrt{p}\big\rvert

\displaystyle=\big\lvert\left\lvert\sin\lparen\pi\tilde{\varphi}\rparen\right\rvert-\left\lvert\sin\lparen\theta_{p}\rparen\right\rvert\big\rvert

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\min\big\{\left\lvert\pi\tilde{\varphi}-\theta_{p}\right\rvert,\pi-\left\lvert\pi\tilde{\varphi}-\theta_{p}\right\rvert,\left\lvert\pi\tilde{\varphi}-\lparen\pi-\theta_{p}\rparen\right\rvert,\pi-\left\lvert\pi\tilde{\varphi}-\lparen\pi-\theta_{p}\rparen\right\rvert\big\}

(33)

\displaystyle=\pi\min\Bigg\{\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rvert,1-\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rvert,\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}-\left\lparen 1-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rparen\right\rvert,1-\left\lvert\tilde{\varphi}-\left\lparen 1-\frac{\theta_{p}}{\pi}\right\rparen\right\rvert\Bigg\}

(34)

\displaystyle<\pi\delta.

(35)

According to the above analysis, we conclude that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
,
\lvert\sin\lparen\pi\tilde{\varphi}\rparen\rvert
is
\pi\delta
-close to
\sqrt{p}
.
By letting
\varepsilon=2/3
and rescaling
\delta\leftarrow\delta/\pi
, we obtain a quantum algorithm that estimates the value of
\sqrt{p}
to within additive error
\delta
with success probability at least
2/3
.
∎

The square root amplitude estimation given in

3.4
essentially employs the same idea as the amplitude estimation in

]
, with an extra observation that we can estimate the square root of

To conclude this section, we explain how square root amplitude estimation can reproduce the quantum amplitude estimation in

Suppose that

by

By

, we can obtain (with high probability) a

-estimate

of

5.1

We prove a matching quantum query lower bound for pure-state trace distance estimation in

5.2
.
This is done by a reduction from distinguishing certain probability distributions to discriminating certain pure quantum states, where the latter can be solved by pure-state trace distance estimation.

Theorem 5.2

(Query lower bounds for pure-state trace distance estimation)
.

For
\varepsilon\in\lparen 0,1/2\rparen
, any quantum query algorithm that estimates the trace distance between two pure quantum states to within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
has query complexity
\Omega\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen
.

Proof.

We consider two probability distributions
p^{+}
and
p^{-}
on a sample space of
n
elements where
n
is even, and they are defined by

p^{\pm}\lparen j\rparen=\frac{1\pm\lparen-1\rparen^{j}2\varepsilon}{n}

(63)

for every

.
Note that the Hellinger distance between
p^{+}
and
p^{-}
is

d_{\textup{H}}\lparen p^{+},p^{-}\rparen=\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1-4\varepsilon^{2}}}\leq 2\varepsilon.

(64)

By
Theorem

5.1
, any quantum query algorithm using queries to quantum oracle
U
that determines whether
U=U_{p^{+}}
or
U=U_{p^{-}}
with probability at least
2/3
, promised that it is in either case, has query complexity
\Omega\lparen 1/d_{\textup{H}}\lparen p^{+},p^{-}\rparen\rparen=\Omega\lparen 1/\varepsilon\rparen
,
where

U_{p^{\pm}}\lvert 0\rangle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\sqrt{p^{\pm}\lparen j\rparen}\lvert j\rangle.

(65)

On the other hand, suppose that there is a quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that, with probability at least
2/3
, estimates to within additive error
\varepsilon
the trace distance between two pure quantum states, given quantum oracles that prepare them, with query complexity
Q
.
Now we will construct another quantum query algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{A}
as a subroutine, which determines whether the quantum oracle
U
satisfies
U=U_{p^{+}}
or
U=U_{p^{-}}
, promised that it is in either case.
The algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
consists of two steps given as follows.

1.

Let
d
be the estimate of the trace distance between
U\lvert 0\rangle
and
U_{p^{+}}\lvert 0\rangle
to within additive error
\varepsilon
by applying
\mathcal{A}
with queries to
U
and
U_{p^{+}}
.
Note that this step uses
Q

U
.

2.

If
d<\varepsilon
, then return that
U=U_{p^{+}}
; and return that
U=U_{p^{-}}
otherwise.

It is clear that the query complexity of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is
Q
.
To see the correctness of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, we consider two cases
U=U_{p^{+}}
and
U=U_{p^{-}}
separately as follows.

•

U=U_{p^{+}}
. In this case, the trace distance between
U\lvert 0\rangle
and
U_{p^{+}}\lvert 0\rangle
is
0
. Therefore, it holds that
d<\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
.
According to Step 2 of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, we conclude that it will return
U=U_{p^{+}}
correctly with probability at least
2/3
.

•

U=U_{p^{-}}
. In this case, the trace distance between
U\lvert 0\rangle
and
U_{p^{+}}\lvert 0\rangle
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}\lparen U_{p^{+}}\lvert 0\rangle,U_{p^{-}}\lvert 0\rangle\rparen

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}^{2}\lparen U_{p^{+}}\lvert 0\rangle,U_{p^{-}}\lvert 0\rangle\rparen}

(66)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\lparen 1-4\varepsilon^{2}\rparen}=2\varepsilon.

(67)

Therefore, it holds that
d>\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
.
According to Step 2 of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, we conclude that it will return
U=U_{p^{-}}
correctly with probability at least
2/3
.

Now we have shown that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is a quantum query algorithm with query complexity
Q
that determines whether
U=U_{p^{+}}
or
U=U_{p^{-}}
with probability at least
2/3
, promised that it is in either case.
This directly gives that

, and thus yields the proof.
∎

1.

\lvert\phi\rangle

\varepsilon
(with success probability at least

2.

If

U=U_{p^{+}}
; and return that
U=U_{p^{-}}
otherwise.

.

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, we consider two cases

and
U=U_{p^{-}}
separately as follows.

B.1

We give a quantum sample lower bound for pure-state trace distance estimation, which is inspired by the proof of
Theorem

5.2
for the quantum query lower bound for pure-state trace distance estimation.

Theorem B.2

(Sample lower bounds for pure-state trace distance estimation)
.

For
\varepsilon\in\lparen 0,1/2\rparen
, estimating the trace distance between two pure quantum states to within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
requires
\Omega\lparen 1/\varepsilon^{2}\rparen
independent samples of them.

Proof.

We consider two
n

\lvert\psi^{\pm}\rangle
where

\lvert\psi^{\pm}\rangle=\sum_{j\in[n]}\sqrt{\frac{1\pm\lparen-1\rparen^{j}2\varepsilon}{n}}\lvert j\rangle.

\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle
and
\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle,\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle\rparen

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle,\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle\rparen^{2}}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\lparen 1-4\varepsilon^{2}\rparen}=2\varepsilon.

(80)

Suppose that we can estimate the trace distance between any two pure quantum states to within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
using
S
independent samples of each of them.

\varepsilon

\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}
and
\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}
.

\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi\rangle^{\otimes S}
be a pure quantum state such that
\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}

\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}
with equal probability. Then, we consider the following process.

1.

Let
d
be the estimate of the trace distance between
\lvert\psi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle
to within additive error
\varepsilon
, which is obtained using
S
independent samples of
\lvert\psi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle
, i.e.,
\lvert\Psi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}
.

If

, then return that
\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}
; and return that
\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}
otherwise.

\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}
and
\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}
separately as follows.

•

\lvert\Psi\rangle=\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S}

\lvert\psi\rangle
and
\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle
is
0

d<\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
.

2/3

80

\lvert\psi\rangle

\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle\rparen=\mathrm{T}\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle,\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle\rparen=2\varepsilon.

2/3

2/3

By

\displaystyle p_{\textup{succ}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\mathrm{T}\left\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S},\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}\right\rparen\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}\left\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle^{\otimes S},\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle^{\otimes S}\right\rparen^{2}}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{F}\left\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle,\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle\right\rparen^{2S}}\right\rparen

(85)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\sqrt{1-\left\lparen 1-\mathrm{T}\left\lparen\lvert\psi^{+}\rangle,\lvert\psi^{-}\rangle\right\rparen^{2}\right\rparen^{S}}\right\rparen

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\sqrt{1-\left\lparen 1-4\varepsilon^{2}\right\rparen^{S}}\right\rparen.

(87)

Equation
