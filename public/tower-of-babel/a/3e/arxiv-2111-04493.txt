Relativistic-flying laser focus by a laser-produced parabolic plasma mirror Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tae Moon Jeong, Sergei V. Bulanov, Petr Valenta, Georg Korn, Timur Zh. Esirkepov, James K. Koga, Alexander S. Pirozhkov, Masaki Kando, Stepan S. Bulanov

Abstract
The question of electromagnetic field intensification towards the values typical for strong field Quantum Electrodynamics is of fundamental importance. One of the most promising intensification schemes is based on the relativistic-flying mirror concept, which shows that the electromagnetic radiation reflected by the mirror will be frequency up-shifted by a factor of 4 ​ γ 2 4\gamma^{2} ( γ \gamma is the Lorentz factor of the mirror). In laser-plasma interactions, such a mirror travels with relativistic velocities through plasma and typically has a parabolic form, which is advantageous for light intensification. Thus, a relativistic-flying parabolic mirror reflects the counter-propagating radiation in a form of focused and flying electromagnetic wave with a high frequency. The relativistic-flying motion of the laser focus makes the electric and magnetic field distributions of the focus complicated, and the mathematical expressions describing the field distributions of the focus become of fundamental interest. We present analytical expressions describing the field distribution formed by an ideal flying mirror which has a perfect reflectance over the entire surface and wavelength range. The peak field strength of an incident laser pulse with a center wavelength of λ 0 \lambda_{0} and an effective beam radius of w e w_{e} is enhanced by a factor proportional to γ 3 ​ ( w e / λ 0 ) \gamma^{3}(w_{e}/\lambda_{0}) in the relativistic limit. Electron-positron pair production is investigated in the context of invariant fields based on the enhanced electromagnetic field. The pair production rate under the relativistic-flying laser focus is modified by the Lorentz γ \gamma -factor and the beam radius-wavelength ratio ( w e / λ 0 w_{e}/\lambda_{0} ). We show that the electron-positron pairs can be created by colliding two counter-propagating relativistic-flying laser focuses in vacuum, each of which is formed when a 180 TW laser pulse is reflected by a relativistic-flying parabolic mirror with a γ = 12.2 \gamma=12.2 .

III
Laser pulse reflected by the relativistic flying-parabolic mirror

Even though the RFFM helps one understand basic properties of the reflected field, the RFPM is a more realistic plasma mirror encountered when a fs high-power laser propagates through the underdense plasma medium. A strong laser pulse (of which the normalized vector potential,
a_{0}
, is above unity) propagating in the plasma pushes electrons through the ponderomotive force to form a plasma cavity, and electrons return back by the recoiling force and form a high-density electron layer on the backside of the cavity. The shape of the electron layer is close to a paraboloid
Esirkepov et al. 2020
;
Bulanov and Sakharov 1991
;
Matlis et al. 2006
, and due to the high-electron density the electron layer behaves like a parabolic mirror. Since the plasma cavity moves with a relativistic speed, the high-density electron layer forms the RFPM. A counter-propagating laser pulse is reflected and focused by the RFPM. The reflected pulse experiences the frequency upshift and the shortening of pulse duration due to the double Doppler effect as discussed in the previous section, and its focus also moves with a relativistic speed. And, when the incident laser pulse is reflected by the RFPM, due to the relativistic effect it also experiences a different curvature for the RFPM from the nominal curvature in the laboratory. Finally, all these effects related to the relativistic motion should be properly considered in calculating the field distribution of the RLF. In this work, we consider only a constantly moving mirror in the optimal regime.

For the relativistic case, Eq. (

) is generalized by the Lorentz invariant property of the interval,
x_{\mu}^{\prime}{x^{\prime}}^{\mu}
, between two spacetime events given by the four-vector,
{x^{\prime}}^{\mu}
. When an EM wave propagates to an event
P
from two different events (
O
and
A
) as shown in Fig. 2(b), a four-vector between two events [
P=(0,-x^{\prime},-y^{\prime},-z^{\prime})
and
A=(ct^{\prime},-x^{\prime},-y^{\prime},2f^{\prime})
] is expressed by
{d^{\prime}}_{1}^{\mu}=(ct^{\prime},0,0,2f^{\prime}+z^{\prime})
and its Lorentz-invariant interval,
{d^{\prime}}_{1\mu}{d^{\prime}}_{1}^{\mu}
, is given by
c^{2}{t^{\prime}}^{2}-(2f^{\prime}+z^{\prime})^{2}
. The other four-vector between two events [
P
and
O=(ct^{\prime},0,0,0)
] is expressed by
{d^{\prime}}_{2}^{\mu}=(ct^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
and its Lorentz-invariant interval,
{d^{\prime}}_{2\mu}{d^{\prime}}_{2}^{\mu}
, is
c^{2}{t^{\prime}}^{2}-{x^{\prime}}^{2}-{y^{\prime}}^{2}-{z^{\prime}}^{2}
. Since the RFPM moves along the
+z
-axis, the Lorentz transformations between the laboratory and the boost frames are given as,

\displaystyle ct^{\prime}=\gamma(ct-\beta z),\>x^{\prime}=x,\>y^{\prime}=y,

(14a)

and

\displaystyle z^{\prime}=\gamma(z-\beta ct).

(14b)

Now, by equating the two intervals,
{d^{\prime}}_{1\mu}{d^{\prime}}_{1}^{\mu}
and
{d^{\prime}}_{2\mu}{d^{\prime}}_{2}^{\mu}
, we obtain the equation for the surface of the RFPM in the laboratory frame as

z=\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{4\gamma f^{\prime}}-\gamma f^{\prime}+\frac{\gamma^{2}-1}{\gamma}f^{\prime}+\beta ct.

(15)

In the non-relativistic limit (
\gamma\rightarrow 1
and
\beta\rightarrow 0
), Eq. (
15
) reduces to Eq. (
13
). Equation (
15
) provides useful information on how the RFPM behaves with an incident laser pulse. First, the surface of the RFPM is described by the equation of
z=(x^{2}+y^{2})/4\gamma f^{\prime}
in the laboratory frame. This means that the nominal focal length (
\gamma f^{\prime}
) of the RFPM in the laboratory frame is
\gamma
times longer than that (
f^{\prime}
) in the boost frame
Bulanov et al. 2011
. An intense (
a_{0}
=3) fs laser pulse propagating in a plasma medium produces a RFPM and its focal length (
\gamma f^{\prime}
) observed in the laboratory frame is about 2
\mu
m. This means that in the boost frame the focal length (
f^{\prime}
) of the RFPM becomes as short as 0.1
\mu
m with a Lorentz factor of
\gamma
=20. This is contrary to the length contraction which is well known in the special theory of relativity. The change in the focal length in the boost frame alters the focusing condition to the
4\pi
-spherical focusing scheme. For instance, the f-number (defined as the focal length divided by the beam size) changes from 0.2 to 0.01 assuming an incident beam size of
\sim
10
\mu
m. Thus, the field distribution of the laser focus in the boost frame should be calculated under the
4\pi
-spherical focusing condition. Second, the vertex of the RFPM is located on
\gamma f^{\prime}+(\gamma^{2}-1)f^{\prime}/\gamma+\beta ct
at a certain time t, and its position moves with a relativistic speed of
\beta c
in the laboratory frame. As a result, the laser focus moves with a relativistic speed of
\beta c
in the laboratory frame as well.

For simplicity, instead of directly calculating the field distribution of the RLF in the laboratory frame, we calculate the focused field distribution first in the boost frame, and then convert it in the laboratory frame through the Lorentz transformation.

III.2
Focused field in the boost frame

Since the incident E-field in the boost frame is expressed by the Fourier transformation as in Eq. (
4
), a monochromatic laser field,
E^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})
, in the boost frame is given by

E^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})=E_{0}(x,y)G(\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}(z^{\prime}/c)}.

(16)

This laser field is focused by the RFPM to form a field distribution under the
4\pi
-spherical focusing scheme in the boost frame. In this study, we assume that the incident laser pulse is radially-polarized (TM mode) or azimuthally-polarized (TE mode), since analytic solutions for those fields exist under the
4\pi
-spherically focusing condition with a specific apodization function. According to
Jeong et al. 2020b
, in the boost frame (
\mathcal{M}
), the electric and magnetic fields of the
4\pi
-spherically focused monochromatic TM mode EM wave are expressed as,

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{f}^{\prime}(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hat{\theta^{\prime}}iE_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\vec{E}_{f,\perp}^{\prime}+\vec{E}_{f,\|}^{\prime},

(17a)

and

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{f}^{\prime}(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\hat{\phi^{\prime}}B_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})b(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\vec{B}_{f,\|}^{\prime}.

(17b)

Here,
\rho^{\prime}
(=
\sqrt{{x^{\prime}}^{2}+{y^{\prime}}^{2}+{z^{\prime}}^{2}}
) is the magnitude of the radial displacement vector,
\vec{\rho}^{\prime}=\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}=\hat{x}x^{\prime}+\hat{y}y^{\prime}+\hat{z}z^{\prime}
, from the origin to an observation point
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
near the origin, and
\theta^{\prime}
is the polar angle defined as
\cos^{-1}(z^{\prime}/\rho^{\prime})
. Again, the angular frequency,
\omega^{\prime}
, in the boost frame is given by
\sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}\omega
and the Gaussian spectrum in Eq. (
4
) is assumed. The peak field strength,
E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
, at the focus at a certain frequency
\omega^{\prime}
is given by
(\pi/2)k^{\prime}\rho_{S}^{\prime}E_{S}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
. The E-field,
E_{S}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
, on a virtual sphere with a radius of
\rho_{S}^{\prime}
is related to the incident laser power [
P_{L}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})=(1/2)c\epsilon_{0}{E^{\prime}}^{2}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})A_{e}
] as
\sqrt{3P_{L}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})/4\pi c\epsilon_{0}(\rho_{S}^{\prime})^{2}}
(see Eq. (35) in
Jeong et al. 2020b
). Thus, the peak field strength,
E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
, is calculated to be

\displaystyle E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k^{\prime}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{3\pi A_{e}}{2}}E_{0}(x,y)G(\omega^{\prime})

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k^{\prime}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{3\pi A_{e}\mathcal{I}}{c\epsilon_{0}}}G(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{f}\mathcal{I}^{1/2}\frac{k^{\prime}}{4}G(\omega^{\prime}),

where
C_{f}
is a constant
\sqrt{3\pi A_{e}/c\epsilon_{0}}=\sqrt{3/c\epsilon_{0}}\pi w_{e}
) related to the effective radius
w_{e}
, and
k^{\prime}
(=
\omega^{\prime}/c
) is the magnitude of the wavevector,
\vec{k}^{\prime}=\hat{\rho}^{\prime}k^{\prime}
, originating from the origin in the boost frame. The laser intensity,
\mathcal{I}
, in the laboratory frame is given by
(1/2)c\epsilon_{0}E_{0}^{2}(x,y)
.

The spatial distribution functions,
a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})
and
b(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})
, in Eq. (
17
) are expressed with the
n
-th order spherical Bessel function of the first kind,
j_{n}(\cdot)
, and the Legendre and associated Legendre functions,
P_{n}(\cdot)
and
P_{n}^{m}(\cdot)
, as

\displaystyle a\left(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime}\right)=

\displaystyle j_{0}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{c}\rho^{\prime}\right)+\frac{5}{2^{3}}j_{2}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{c}\rho^{\prime}\right)P_{2}\left(\cos\theta^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+\dots,

(19a)

and

\displaystyle b\left(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\pi}j_{1}\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{c}\rho^{\prime}\right)P_{1}^{1}(\cos\theta^{\prime}).

(19b)

In Eq. (
19
), the argument,
k^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}
(=
\vec{k}^{\prime}\cdot\vec{\rho}^{\prime}
), in spherical coordinates is replaced by
(\omega^{\prime}/c)\rho^{\prime}
. So,
k^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}
[or
(\omega^{\prime}/c)\rho^{\prime}
] can be expressed as
k_{x}^{\prime}x^{\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime}y^{\prime}+k_{z}^{\prime}z^{\prime}
in Cartesian coordinates. Since the above field distributions propagate along the z-axis in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, it is convenient to express Eq. (
17
) in Cartesian coordinates before performing the Lorentz transformation. The unit vectors,
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\phi}^{\prime}
, in spherical coordinates of the boost frame are expressed as
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}=\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\phi^{\prime}\hat{x}^{\prime}+\cos\theta^{\prime}\sin\phi^{\prime}\hat{y}^{\prime}-\sin\theta^{\prime}\hat{z}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\phi}^{\prime}=\sin\phi^{\prime}\hat{x}^{\prime}-\cos\phi^{\prime}\hat{y}^{\prime}
in Cartesian coordinates, then we re-write Eq. (
17
) as,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}E_{f,x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
E_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
E_{f,z^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}=iE_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
\cos\theta^{\prime}\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
-\sin\theta^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(20a)

and

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}B_{f,x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
B_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
B_{f,z^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}=-B_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})b(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\begin{bmatrix}\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
-\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(20b)

The electric and magnetic fields in Eq. (
20
) consist of in-coming (
t^{\prime}+\rho^{\prime}/c
) and out-going (
t^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime}/c
) field components. The spatial distribution function,
a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})
, in the electric field can be approximated as
j_{0}(\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c)
, then
a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
and
b(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
can be separated by two parts as:

\displaystyle a(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\left(a_{+}+a_{-}\right)e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(21a)

and

\displaystyle b(\rho^{\prime},\theta^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\left(b_{+}+b_{-}\right)\sin\theta^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(21b)

where in-coming (
a_{+}
and
b_{+}
) and out-going (
a_{-}
and
b_{-}
) field components are given by,

\displaystyle a_{+}=\frac{e^{i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c},\quad a_{-}=-\frac{e^{-i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c},

(22a)

\displaystyle b_{+}=\frac{4}{\pi}\left[\frac{e^{i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2i(\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c)^{2}}-\frac{e^{i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}\right],

(22b)

and

\displaystyle b_{-}=\frac{4}{\pi}\left[-\frac{e^{-i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2i(\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c)^{2}}-\frac{e^{-i\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}}{2\omega^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c}\right].

(22c)

In this subsection, the electromagnetic field focused by the RFPM is expressed in the boost frame. In following subsections, the Lorentz transformation of the field into the laboratory frame (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) and the spatio-temporal field distribution in the laboratory frame will be explained.

III.3
Lorentz transformation for in-coming and out-going fields

For phase factors of in-coming and out-going fields, the Lorentz transformations from the boost frame to the laboratory frame yield

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}+k_{x}^{\prime}x^{\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime}y^{\prime}+k_{z}^{\prime}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\prime}t\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta^{\prime})+k^{\prime}x\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle+k^{\prime}y\sin\theta^{\prime}\sin\phi^{\prime}+k^{\prime}z\gamma(\cos\theta^{\prime}-\beta),

(23a)

and

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}-k_{x}^{\prime}x^{\prime}-k_{y}^{\prime}y^{\prime}-k_{z}^{\prime}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\omega^{\prime}t\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta^{\prime})-k^{\prime}x\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle-k^{\prime}y\sin\theta^{\prime}\sin\phi^{\prime}-k^{\prime}z\gamma(\cos\theta^{\prime}+\beta).

(23b)

with the expression of
(\omega^{\prime}/c)\rho^{\prime}=k_{x}^{\prime}x^{\prime}+k_{y}^{\prime}y^{\prime}+k_{z}^{\prime}z^{\prime}
. By introducing new variables,
\omega_{+}^{\prime\prime}
and
\omega_{-}^{\prime\prime}
, for the angular frequencies of in-coming and out-going fields in the laboratory frame, we define

\omega_{+}^{\prime\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta_{+}^{\prime})\;\mathrm{and}\;\omega_{-}^{\prime\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta_{-}^{\prime}),

(24)

and obtain the Lorentz invariant properties for the phase as
\omega^{\prime}(t^{\prime}+\rho^{\prime}/c)=\omega_{+}^{\prime\prime}(t+\rho/c)
and
\omega^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime}/c)=\omega_{-}^{\prime\prime}(t-\rho/c)
. Here, + and – symbols in the subscript are used to represent in-coming and out-going fields. From the Lorentz invariant properties of the phase, the following relationships between the polar angles for the in-coming and out-going fields are obtained:

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{+}^{\prime}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta)}\;\mathrm{and}\;\cos\theta_{+}^{\prime}=\frac{\cos\theta+\beta}{1+\beta\cos\theta},

(25a)

\displaystyle\sin\theta_{-}^{\prime}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}\;\mathrm{and}\;\cos\theta_{-}^{\prime}=\frac{\cos\theta-\beta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}.

(25b)

And, with the help of Eq. (
25
), Eq. (
23
) can be rewritten as,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{\prime}t}{\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta)}+\frac{k^{\prime}x\sin\theta\cos\phi}{\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta)}

\displaystyle+\frac{k^{\prime}y\sin\theta\sin\phi}{\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta)}+\frac{k^{\prime}z\cos\theta}{\gamma(1+\beta\cos\theta)},

(26a)

and

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{\prime}t}{\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}-\frac{k^{\prime}x\sin\theta\cos\phi}{\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}

\displaystyle-\frac{k^{\prime}y\sin\theta\sin\phi}{\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}-\frac{k^{\prime}z\cos\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}.

(26b)

So, it is clear that
\omega_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}
should be expressed as

\omega_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\gamma(1\pm\beta\cos\theta)}.

(27)

in the laboratory frame. When
\theta=0
(+z-direction), the angular frequencies for in-coming and out-going fields become
\omega_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}=[(1+\beta)/(1\pm\beta)]\omega
. Now, it is convenient to introduce new variables,
\Omega_{1,2}
and
\Gamma_{1,2}
, defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)},\quad\Omega_{2}=\frac{\omega^{\prime}\beta\cos\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)},

(28a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\frac{\omega^{\prime}/c}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)},\;\mathrm{and}\;\Gamma_{2}=\frac{(\omega^{\prime}/c)\beta\cos\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)}.

(28b)

Then, the phase factors for in-coming and out-going fields can be rewritten as,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2})t+(\Gamma_{1}-\Gamma_{2})\rho,

(29a)

and

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)

\displaystyle=(\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2})t-(\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2})\rho.

(29b)

By adding or subtracting Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
), we obtain

\displaystyle t^{\prime}=T(t,\rho)=\frac{t-(\rho/c)\beta\cos\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)},

(30a)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=R(\rho,t)=\frac{\rho-ct\beta\cos\theta}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)},

(30b)

and

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\pm\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}=T(t,\rho)\pm\frac{R(\rho,t)}{c}=\frac{t\pm(\rho/c)}{\gamma(1\pm\beta\cos\theta)}.

(30c)

Equation (
30
) shows how lightcone variables in the boost frame are Lorentz-tranformed into the laboratory frame. Hereafter, the variables,
T
and
R
, will be used as short expressions for representing
T(t,\rho)
and
R(\rho,t)
.

The Lorentz transformations for the full electric and magnetic fields from the boost frame to the laboratory frame (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) are given by

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{\|}^{\prime\prime}=\vec{E}_{\|}^{\prime},\quad\vec{B}_{\|}^{\prime\prime}=\vec{B}_{\|}^{\prime},

(31a)

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}=\gamma(\vec{E}_{\perp}^{\prime}-c\vec{\beta}\times\vec{B}_{\perp}^{\prime}),

(31b)

and

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{\perp}^{\prime\prime}=\gamma(\vec{B}_{\perp}^{\prime}+\vec{\beta}\times\vec{E}_{\perp}^{\prime}/c).

(31c)

Since the parallel polarization components for the field remain unchanged through the Lorentz transformation, we have

\displaystyle E_{f,z^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=E_{f,z^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-iE_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})a_{\pm}\sin\theta_{\pm}^{\prime},

(32a)

and

\displaystyle B_{f,z^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=B_{f,z^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}=0.

(32b)

The perpendicular components for the focused fields in the laboratory frame are expressed as,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}E_{f,x^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
E_{f,y^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}E_{f,x^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}+c\beta B_{f,y^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}\\
E_{f,y^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}-c\beta B_{f,x^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\gamma E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\left(ia_{\pm}\cos\theta_{\pm}+\beta b_{\pm}\sin\theta_{\pm}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(33a)

and

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}B_{f,x^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
B_{f,y^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}B_{f,x^{\prime\prime},\pm}^{\prime}-(\beta/c)E_{f,y^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}\\
B_{f,y^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}+(\beta/c)E_{f,x^{\prime},\pm}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c}E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\left(b_{\pm}\sin\theta_{\pm}+i\beta a_{\pm}\cos\theta_{\pm}\right)\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(33b)

The final field expression can be obtained by summing in-coming and out-going fields as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}E_{f,x^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
E_{f,y^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}E_{f,x^{\prime}}^{\prime}+c\beta B_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
E_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}-c\beta B_{f,x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\gamma E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})(ia^{\prime}+\beta b^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(34a)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}B_{f,x^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
B_{f,y^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}B_{f,x^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime}-(\beta/c)E_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
B_{f,y^{\prime}}^{\prime}+(\beta/c)E_{f,x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c}E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})(b^{\prime}+i\beta a^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(34b)

\displaystyle E_{f,z^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-iE_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})(a_{out}\sin\theta_{-}^{\prime}+a_{in}\sin\theta_{+}^{\prime}),

(34c)

and

\displaystyle B_{f,z^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0.

(34d)

Here,
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
in Eq. (
34
) are expressed as

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a_{-}\cos\theta_{-}^{\prime}+a_{+}\cos\theta_{+}^{\prime},

(35a)

and

\displaystyle b^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b_{-}\sin\theta_{-}^{\prime}+b_{+}\sin\theta_{+}^{\prime},

(35b)

with
a_{\pm}
and
b_{\pm}
defined in Eq. (
22
). Equation (
34
) represents the focused electric and magnetic field distributions at a certain angular frequency, but it is still expressed in terms of four-vector components in the boost frame.

III.4
Spatio-temporal field distribution in the laboratory frame

By using the same analogy as in Eqs. (
3
) and (
8
) and taking the Fourier transformation in the
\omega^{\prime}
-space, the spatio-temporal field distribution of the RLF in the laboratory frame is obtained as

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{f}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\vec{E}_{f}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\gamma\begin{bmatrix}(iI_{1}+\beta I_{2})\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
(iI_{1}+\beta I_{2})\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
-i(1/\gamma)I_{3}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(36a)

and

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{f}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\vec{B}_{f}^{\prime\prime}(\omega^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\frac{\gamma}{c}\begin{bmatrix}-(I_{2}+i\beta I_{1})\sin\phi^{\prime}\\
(I_{2}+i\beta I_{1})\cos\phi^{\prime}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(36b)

Here,
I_{n}
(n=1,2,3) are definite integrals defined as

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})a^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}}{4c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}G(\omega^{\prime})a^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(37a)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}E_{p}^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})b^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}}{4c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}G(\omega^{\prime})b^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(37b)

and

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}}{4c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}G(\omega^{\prime})(a_{out}\sin\theta_{-}^{\prime}+a_{in}\sin\theta_{+}^{\prime})e^{i\omega^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(37c)

with Eq. (
18
). These integrals can be calculated with the help of Lorentz transformation given by Eq. (
26
). For example, the integral,

, can be first separated into in-coming and out-going parts as,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}}{8i\rho^{\prime}}\cos\theta_{+}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)}e^{-\frac{(\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{0}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\Delta{\omega^{\prime}}^{2}}}

(38)

\displaystyle-\frac{C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}}}{8i\rho^{\prime}}\cos\theta_{-}^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}e^{i\omega^{\prime}\left(t^{\prime}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{c}\right)}e^{-\frac{(\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{0}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\Delta{\omega^{\prime}}^{2}}}.

Then, after applying the Lorentz transformation [Eqs. (
25

III.5

The field calculation was so far based on an ideal mirror which has perfect reflectance, i. e.,

Invariant fields and pair production rate

Assuming the Compton wavelength is much less than the wavelength of the RLF
Bulanov et al. 2011
;
Berestetskii et al. 1982
;
Narozhny et al. 2004
;
Aleksandrov et al. 2019
, the spacetime-dependent
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production rate,
W_{ep}
, can be calculated from

W_{ep}=\frac{e^{2}E_{Sch}^{2}}{4\pi^{3}\hbar^{2}c}E_{inv}B_{inv}\coth\left(\pi\frac{B_{inv}}{E_{inv}}\right)e^{-\pi/E_{inv}}.

(64)

Here,
E_{inv}
and
B_{inv}
are invariant fields defined by

\displaystyle E_{inv}=\frac{\sqrt{(\mathcal{F}^{2}+\mathcal{G}^{2})^{1/2}-\mathcal{F}}}{E_{Sch}},

(65a)

and

\displaystyle B_{inv}=\frac{\sqrt{(\mathcal{F}^{2}+\mathcal{G}^{2})^{1/2}+\mathcal{F}}}{E_{Sch}}.

(65b)

In Eq. (
65
), the Poincare invariants,
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
, for the RLF are calculated as

\mathcal{F}=\frac{c^{2}B^{2}-E^{2}}{2},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{G}=c\vec{B}\cdot\vec{E}.

(66)

Then, the Poincare invariants for the TM mode laser pulse can be calculated with Eq. (
51
) and become

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{TM}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4c}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\Big[j_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\Upsilon_{2}^{2}-j_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\left(\Upsilon_{1}^{2}+\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4c}\right)^{2}j_{\{1-0\}}^{2},

(67a)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{TM}

\displaystyle=0,

(67b)

where the function,
j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}
, is defined as
j_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\Upsilon_{2}^{2}-j_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\left(\Upsilon_{1}^{2}+\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\right)
and
j_{i}
, represents the spherical Bessel function,
j_{i}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}\rho^{\prime}/c)
. The invariant fields,
E_{inv}
and
H_{inv}
, are determined by the sign of
\mathcal{F}
. When
\mathcal{F}\geq 0
(i.e.,
j_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\geq j_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\left(\Upsilon_{1}^{2}+\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\right)
),
\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{2}}=\mathcal{F}
. On the other hand, when
\mathcal{F}<0
,
\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{2}}=-\mathcal{F}
. This results in

\displaystyle E_{inv}=

\displaystyle\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4cE_{Sch}}\begin{cases}0&\text{$\mathcal{F}$ $\geq$ 0}\\
\sqrt{-j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}}&\text{$\mathcal{F}<$ 0}\\
\end{cases},

(68a)

and

\displaystyle B_{inv}=

\displaystyle\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4c^{2}E_{Sch}}\begin{cases}\sqrt{j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}}&\text{$\mathcal{F}$ $\geq$ 0}\\
0&\text{$\mathcal{F}<$ 0}\\
\end{cases}.

(68b)

Thus, from Eq. (
64
), it follows that no pair production is expected even with the enhanced field strength of the RLF when
\mathcal{F}\geq 0
. In the other case (
\mathcal{F}<0
), the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production rate via the Schwinger mechanism can be explicitly calculated in terms of the Lorentz
\gamma
-factor, the beam radius-wavelength ratio (
w_{e}/\lambda_{0}
), and the laser intensity (
\mathcal{I}_{p}
) as

\begin{split}W_{ep}\approx&12\pi^{2}\alpha\gamma^{4}\left(\frac{w_{e}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{p}}{\hbar c}\right)\left(-j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}\right)\\
&\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{0}}{w_{e}}\frac{(E_{Sch}/E_{p})}{\sqrt{6\pi^{3}}\sqrt{-j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}}}\right],\end{split}

(69)

in the relativistic limit. Here,
\alpha
is the fine structure constant defined as
e^{2}/4\pi\hbar c\epsilon_{0}
and the peak field strength,
E_{p}
, in time as
\sqrt{2\mathcal{I}_{p}/c\epsilon_{0}}
. The
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production occurs only in the region of
j_{\{1-0\}}^{2}<0
.

For the TE mode laser pulse, the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production rate can be calculated with Eq. (
53
) and the final form is given by

\begin{split}W_{ep}\approx&12\pi^{2}\alpha\gamma^{4}\left(\frac{w_{e}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{p}}{\hbar c}\right)\left(-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}\right)\\
&\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\frac{\lambda_{0}}{w_{e}}\frac{(E_{Sch}/E_{p})}{\sqrt{6\pi^{3}}\sqrt{-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}}}\right],\end{split}

(70)

where
j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}
, is defined as
j_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\left(\Upsilon_{1}^{2}+\Upsilon_{2}^{2}\right)-j_{1}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T)\Upsilon_{2}^{2}
. Again, the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production occurs only in the region of
j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}<0
.

Now, let us consider the reflectance,
\mathcal{R}
, of the flying plasma mirror in the calculation of
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production. The reflectance can be modeled as
0.5\gamma^{-3}
for the infinitely thin foil model,
\sim 0.1\gamma^{-4}
for the wake wave model, and a rather complicated form can be found for the double-sided mirror model
Kulagin et al. 2007
;
Pirozhkov et al. 2007
;
Esirkepov et al. 2009
. By multiplying the reflectance,
\mathcal{R}
, into the intensity,
\mathcal{I}_{p}
, the pair production rate can be modified as

\displaystyle W_{ep}\approx

\displaystyle 6\pi^{2}\alpha\gamma\left(\frac{w_{e}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{p}}{\hbar c}\right)\left(-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{\lambda_{0}}{w_{e}}\frac{(E_{Sch}/E_{p})}{\sqrt{3\pi^{3}}\sqrt{-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}}}\right],

(71a)

for the thin foil model or

\displaystyle W_{ep}\approx

\displaystyle 1.2\pi^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{w_{e}}{\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}_{p}}{\hbar c}\right)\left(-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda_{0}}{w_{e}}\frac{(E_{Sch}/E_{p})}{0.32\sqrt{6\pi^{3}}\sqrt{-j_{\{0-1\}}^{2}}}\right],

(71b)

for the wake wave model. From Eq. (
71b
), it is obvious that, in case of the wake wave model, due to the reflectance of the mirror the pair production rate becomes dependent only on the beam radius-wavelength ratio (
w_{e}/\lambda_{0}
). Thus, the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production from the thin foil model will be mostly considered.

The total number,
N_{e^{+}e^{-}}
, of
e^{+}e^{-}
pairs produced by the RLFs can be estimated by integrating the pair production rate,
W_{ep}
, over a four-volume
Narozhny et al. 2004
. The threshold of the incident laser power required for a single
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production is examined with a
\gamma
-factor of 12.2. Such a relativistic mirror can be driven by focusing a 100 PW Ti:S (
\lambda_{0}
= 0.8
\mu
m) laser pulse within a focal spot radius of 100
\mu
m, assuming the
\gamma
-factor of the mirror is given by
\sqrt{1+a_{0}^{2}}
. A laser pulse with an
a_{0}=0.1
and a beam radius,
w_{0}
, of 100
\mu m
is considered as an incident laser pulse to be reflected. According to
Valenta et al. 2020
, in order to minimize the recoil effect, an incident pulse duration should be less than a characteristic time,
\tau_{c}
, given as

\tau_{c}=\kappa\frac{2^{4/3}m_{e}c^{2}}{2\Gamma^{2}(2/3)}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega_{pe}}\right)^{8/3}\gamma_{L}^{1/3}\frac{n_{e}\lambda_{L}}{\mathcal{I}_{p}}.

(72)

Here,
\omega_{pe}
is the Langmuir frequency,
\gamma_{L}
the Lorentz
\gamma
-factor for the Langmuir wave, and
\lambda_{L}
the Langmuir wavelength. The parameter,
\kappa
, can be obtained from the simulation. Although this analysis is based on an one-dimensional PIC approach, it provides a rough estimation on the maximum pulse duration required for minimizing the recoil effect in time. The calculation with
\kappa=1.5\times 10^{-4}
and an electron density of 0.01
n_{cr}
shows that the incident laser pulse with an
a_{0}
of 0.1 can have a maximum pulse duration of about 30 fs before the mirror is severely affected by the recoil effect. This pulse duration can be supported by a spectral bandwidth,
\Delta\omega
, of 19 nm (
\sim
32 nm at FWHM). This means that a laser pulse with a pulse duration of
\sim
9.4 fs (supported by a spectral bandwidth,
\Delta\omega
, of 60 nm at 800 nm center wavelength,
\omega_{0}
) can be used as the incident laser pulse to be reflected. In this case, the minimum reflectance of the mirror required for a single
e^{+}e^{-}
pairs production is estimated as
\sim
14.1
\%
. The
e^{+}e^{-}
pairs production is strongly suppressed by the low reflectance (2.7
\times 10^{-2}\%
for
0.5\gamma^{-3}
and 4.5
\times 10^{-4}\%
for
0.1\gamma^{-4}
) of the mirror. The required laser field strengths, expressed as
a_{0}
, increase to
\sim
2.8 for the thin foil and
\sim
21.6 for the wake wave cases. Under these field strengths, the characteristic time given by Eq. (
72
) becomes as short as 0.05 fs for the thin foil and to
8\times 10^{-4}
fs for the wake wave cases, so the RFPM is destroyed before reaching a field strength required for the single
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production event.

It is now interesting to consider when two counter-propagating RLFs collide with each other. The experimental set-up considered is visualized in Fig. 4. The two (driver and source) laser pulses are coupled by a hole mirror and propagate in a plasma medium. The source laser pulse (SLP1) proceeds ahead to be reflected by the other counter-propagating RFPM formed by another driver laser pulse (DLP2). Two sets of plasma media are prepared to ensure the collision of reflected SLPs in vacuum. An ideally-sharp edge is preferred. Figure 4(b) shows two different scenarios of forming a RFPM: one is the mirror formed at the rear side of plasma cavity as proposed in
Bulanov et al. 2003
and the other is the mirror formed by sharp and narrow electron/ion layer driven by the laser pulse
Pirozhkov et al. 2007
;
Esirkepov et al. 2009
. One focus, expressed by
\vec{E}_{f}^{+}(\rho,t)
and
\vec{B}_{f}^{+}(\rho,t)
, propagates along the +z axis and the other one, expressed by
\vec{E}_{f}^{-}(\rho,t)
and
\vec{B}_{f}^{-}(\rho,t)
, along the -z axis. The + and - symbols in the superscripts are used to express the propagating and the counter-propagating RLFs. TM mode laser pulses are assumed in the calculation, so the total fields are expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{f}^{\pm}=\gamma\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4cE_{Sch}}

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}\left\{-j_{0}^{\pm}\sin(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{1}^{\pm}\pm\beta j_{1}^{\pm}\cos(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{2}^{\pm}\right\}\cos\phi^{+}\\
\left\{-j_{0}^{\pm}\sin(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{1}^{\pm}\pm\beta j_{1}^{\pm}\cos(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{2}^{+}\right\}\sin\phi^{\pm}\\
(1/\gamma)j_{0}^{\pm}\sin(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{2}^{\pm}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(73a)

and

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{f}^{\pm}=\frac{\gamma}{c}\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4cE_{Sch}}

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}-\left\{\pm j_{1}^{\pm}\cos(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{2}^{\pm}-\beta j_{0}^{\pm}\sin(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{1}^{\pm}\right\}\sin\phi^{\pm}\\
\left\{\pm j_{1}^{\pm}\cos(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{2}^{\pm}-\beta j_{0}^{\pm}\sin(\omega_{0}^{\prime}T^{\pm})\Upsilon_{1}^{\pm}\right\}\cos\phi^{\pm}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(73b)

Here,
j_{i}^{\pm}
=
j_{i}(\omega_{0}^{\prime}R^{\pm}/c)
and the minus sign in front of
j_{1}^{-}
comes from the change in the sign of magnetic field. The functions,
T^{\pm}
and

\displaystyle T^{\pm}(t,\rho)=\frac{t-(\rho/c)\beta\cos\theta^{\pm}}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta^{\pm})},

(74a)

and

\displaystyle R^{\pm}(\rho,t)=\frac{\rho-ct\beta\cos\theta^{\pm}}{\gamma(1-\beta^{2}\cos^{2}\theta^{\pm})}.

(74b)

Since the two RLFs counter-propagate with respect to each other, we find the following relationships for the polar and azimuthal angles between the propagating and the counter-propagating RLFs:

\theta^{-}=\pi-\theta^{+}=\pi-\theta\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\phi^{-}=\phi^{+}=\phi.

(75)

By using Eq. (
75
), the following relationships for variables between the propagating and counter-propagating RLFs are obtained:

\displaystyle T^{-}(t,\rho)=-T^{+}(-t,\rho)\;\mathrm{and}\;R^{-}(\rho,t)=R^{+}(\rho,t),

(76a)

\displaystyle T^{-}(t,\rho)\pm\frac{R^{-}(\rho,t)}{c}=-\left[T^{+}(-t,\rho)\mp\frac{R^{+}(\rho,-t)}{c}\right],

(76b)

\displaystyle\frac{\sin\theta^{-}}{\gamma(1\pm\beta\cos\theta^{-})}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\gamma(1\mp\beta\cos\theta)},

(76c)

\displaystyle\frac{\cos\theta^{-}\pm\beta}{1\pm\beta\cos\theta^{-}}=-\frac{\cos\theta\mp\beta}{1\mp\beta\cos\theta},

(76d)

and

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{1}^{-}(t,\rho)=-\Upsilon_{1}^{+}(-t,\rho)\;\mathrm{and}\;\Upsilon_{2}^{-}(t,\rho)=\Upsilon_{2}^{+}(-t,\rho).

(76e)

Thus, the total fields,
\vec{E}_{f}^{T}
and

\displaystyle\vec{E}_{f}^{T}=\vec{E}_{f}^{+}+\vec{E}_{f}^{-}

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4cE_{Sch}}

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}\left\{-\left[\mathcal{J}_{0}(t)+\mathcal{J}_{0}(-t^{\prime})\right]+\beta\left[\mathcal{J}_{1}(t)-\mathcal{J}_{1}(-t^{\prime})\right]\right\}\cos\phi\\

(1/\gamma)\left[\mathcal{J}_{0}^{\prime}(t)-\mathcal{J}_{0}^{\prime}(-t^{\prime})\right]\\
\end{bmatrix},

(77a)

and

\displaystyle\vec{B}_{f}^{T}=\vec{B}_{f}^{+}+\vec{B}_{f}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c}\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{0}C_{f}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p}}}{4cE_{Sch}}

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}-\left\{\left[\mathcal{J}_{1}(t)-\mathcal{J}_{1}(-t^{\prime})\right]-\beta\left[\mathcal{J}_{0}(t)+\mathcal{J}_{0}(-t^{\prime})\right]\right\}\sin\phi\\

\end{bmatrix}.

(77b)

Here,

V
Conclusion

The mathematical formulae describing the electromagnetic field of the relativistic-flying laser focus formed by an ideal relativistic-flying parabolic mirror were obtained. The main optical characteristics of the relativistic-flying laser focus, such as the enhancement of field strength and its distribution in time and space, could be well understood by the formulae. The field expression of the relativistic-flying laser focus was applied to the estimation of the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production by the Schwinger mechanism. The pair production rate under the relativistic-flying laser focus was modified by the Lorentz
\gamma
-factor and the beam radius-wavelength ratio. The calculation shows that even with a strong suppression due to the low reflectance of relativistic-flying parabolic mirror the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair production is feasible by colliding two counter-propagating relativistic-flying laser focuses at a relatively lower laser power of 250 PW. Although we assume an ideal parabolic shape for the relativistic-flying parabolic mirror, the actual shape of the mirror will have a small deviation from the ideal shape, introducing the wavefront error. The wavefront error deforms the distribution and induces the degradation of the focused intensity. Thus, the next step is to generalize the mathematical formulae for a beam having a wavefront aberration and to calculate the Strehl ratio assessing the focusability. On the other hand, since the formation of the relativistic-flying parabolic mirror will be strongly affected by the instability of the high-power laser pulse and its propagation in the plasma medium, the generation of a stable relativistic-flying parabolic mirror will be a technical challenge for practical applications of the flying mirror. The results obtained can be used to understand the fundamental question on the electron positron pair production from vacuum.
