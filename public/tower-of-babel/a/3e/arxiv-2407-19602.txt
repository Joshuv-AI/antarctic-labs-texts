Metropolis–Hastings with Scalable Subsampling
Estevão Prado, Christopher Nemeth, and Chris Sherlock

Abstract
The Metropolis–Hastings (MH) algorithm is one of the most widely used Markov Chain Monte Carlo schemes for generating samples from Bayesian posterior distributions. The algorithm is asymptotically exact, flexible and easy to implement. However, in the context of Bayesian inference for large datasets, evaluating the likelihood on the full data for thousands of iterations until convergence can be prohibitively expensive. This paper introduces a new subsample MH algorithm that satisfies detailed balance with respect to the target posterior and utilises control variates to enable exact, efficient Bayesian inference on datasets with large numbers of observations. Through theoretical results, simulation experiments and real-world applications on certain generalised linear models, we demonstrate that our method requires substantially smaller subsamples and is computationally more efficient than the standard MH algorithm and other exact subsample MH algorithms.



Keywords:
Markov Chain Monte Carlo, scalable Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, control variates, subsampling, big data.

Introduction

In the Bayesian framework, the posterior distribution of the model parameters contains all information needed for inference.
The Metropolis–Hastings (MH) algorithm
(
Brooks et al., 2011
, e.g.,)
is a core Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method commonly used in Bayesian statistics to sample from posterior distributions. At each iteration, it proposes at a new parameter value and then decides whether or not to accept it. The acceptance decision requires the full (unnormalised) posterior density evaluated at the proposed parameter, and this, in turn, requires the evaluation of every single term in the likelihood. In this big-data era, where datasets consist of millions or even billions of data points, each full likelihood evaluation is computationally expensive and the cost of repeating this at every iteration, as required by the standard MH algorithm, becomes prohibitive.

Optimisation-based algorithms, such as variational and Laplace approximations are still feasible, but are inexact. In this context, a new class of MCMC methods has gained attention:
scalable MCMC
algorithms; see
Fearnhead et al. (2025)
for a recent review. To alleviate the computational burden, some works propose partitioning the data and then, in parallel, performing posterior sampling on each partition across multiple CPUs. This ‘divide-and-conquer’ approach approximates the final posterior by combining the subposteriors from each subsample. One of the challenges with this approach is to efficiently yet accurately combine the subposteriors in cases where the target distribution is non-Gaussian; see, for example,
Neiswanger et al. (2013)
;
Nemeth and Sherlock (2018)
.

Alternatively, we can reduce the computational cost by running a single MCMC chain but using a random subsample of the data at each iteration
(
Bardenet et al., 2017
;
Quiroz et al., 2019
;
Llorente et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
. However, just as with divide-and-conquer approaches, the distribution targeted by the algorithm is typically only an approximation to the true posterior. For example,
Quiroz et al. (2019)
propose a Metropolis-based MCMC algorithm that builds upon the pseudo-marginal approach
(
Andrieu and Roberts, 2009
)
. The MH acceptance ratio is replaced with an approximation based on subsamples. The error in the targeted posterior is reduced through the use of control variates
(
Ripley, 2009
, e.g.,)
and an approximate bias-correction term.

There are also subsampling MCMC methods that are constructed in such a way that the invariant distribution of the resulting Markov chain is the
true posterior distribution
. All of these methods require that the full likelihood is a product of terms (typically, one term for each data point) and require assumptions on the individual terms in the product.
For instance,
Maclaurin and Adams (2014)
assume that for each observation there exists a strictly positive lower bound on the log-likelihood that accumulates tractably when summed over many observations; it then augments the parameter space using Bernoulli latent variables.
Cornish et al. (2019)
and
Zhang et al. (2020)
are less restrictive and instead replace the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability with an alternative that uses a small number of subsamples and, hence, has a high variance.
Cornish et al. (2019)
uses control variates to reduce this variance, whilst
Zhang et al. (2020)
incorporates an additional parameter to mitigate the variance’s deleterious effect on the variable acceptance rate.

We propose a new, exact MH algorithm that makes use of control variates and subsampling to produce a Markov chain that satisfies detailed balance with respect to the true posterior. We establish general bounds on the discrepancy between the log-likelihood difference between current and proposed parameter values and the log-likelihood difference suggested by the control variates; these bounds are typically much tighter than those in previous works, especially when the dimension,
d
, of the parameter vector is moderate or large.
We derive specific, tight bounds for four regression models including logistic regression
. More broadly, we establish that our algorithm is optimal within a particular class of data-subsampling algorithms. All of this leads to a substantial speed-up in the posterior sampling. We also conduct an asymptotic analysis comparing our proposed bounds with previous approaches, demonstrating theoretically and empirically that the computational cost of using our bounds is at least a factor of
d^{1/2}
better than previous bounds.

Section
2
introduces our algorithm and proves that it targets the correct posterior. Section
3
discusses existing subsample-based MH algorithms. Section
4
presents theoretical results on the exactness and optimality of our algorithm and guidance on its tuning; proofs are deferred to Appendix
A
. Sections
5
and
6
present simulation experiments and real-world applications using the logistic, probit and Poisson regression models, where our proposed method is compared against the equivalent Metropolis–Hastings algorithm and other exact subsample-based MH algorithms.
Section
7
concludes with a discussion. Additional simulation experiments, asymptotic analyses comparing bounds and results related to the acceptance rate of the proposed algorithms are presented in Appendices
B
to
H
.

2
Metropolis–Hastings with Scalable Subsampling

We are interested in sampling from a posterior distribution with density
\pi(\theta|y)
, where
\theta\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the parameter vector and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
are the observed data. Assuming a prior
p(\theta)
, and data that are conditionally independent with log likelihood terms
\ell_{i}(\theta):=\log p(y_{i}|\theta)
, the posterior density is

\pi(\theta|y)\propto p(\theta)\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_{i}|\theta)\equiv p(\theta)p(y_{1:n}|\theta)=p(\theta)\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\theta)\right].

Given a current value,
\theta
, and a proposal density,
q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)
, the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability is
1\wedge[q(\theta|\theta^{\prime})\pi(\theta^{\prime}|y)/\{q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)\pi(\theta|y)\}]
. The contribution of the computationally expensive likelihood to this ratio is

\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)\}\right].

To allow us to subsample terms from this sum whilst controlling the variance of the subsequent estimator, we create a deterministic
control variate
for each difference,
\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)
.
Let
\widehat{\theta}\in\mathcal{X}
be (ideally, but for strict correctness of the algorithm, not necessarily) close to the posterior mode. We choose control variates to be the approximations to
\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)
that arise from first- and second-order Taylor expansion of
\ell_{i}
about
\widehat{\theta}
:

\displaystyle r_{i}^{(1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle:=(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}g_{i}(\widehat{\theta}),

(1)

\displaystyle r_{i}^{(2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle:=(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}g_{i}(\widehat{\theta})+(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)^{\top}H_{i}(\widehat{\theta})\left\{\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\prime}+\theta)-\widehat{\theta}\right\},

(2)

where
g_{i}(\theta)=\nabla\ell_{i}(\theta)
and
H_{i}(\theta)=\nabla\nabla^{\top}\ell_{i}(\theta)
is the Hessian. Whichever control variate,
k\in\{1,2\}
, is used, our algorithm requires the existence of bounds
M^{(k)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\geq 0
and
c^{(k)}_{1},\dots,c^{(k)}_{n}>0
such that
M^{(k)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=M^{(k)}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and

|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}^{(k)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})|\leq c^{(k)}_{i}M^{(k)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(3)

In the following, for notational convenience, we drop the superscript
(k)
from
r^{(k)}_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})
,
c^{(k)}_{i}
and
M^{(k)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
and use the notation
r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})
,
c_{i}
,
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, respectively. Such bounds for logistic regression and robust linear regression models when
k=1,2
are given in
Cornish et al. (2019)
. In Section
4.2
, we provide much-improved bounds for logistic, probit
, robust linear
and a form of Poisson regression. We define the difference between the control variate and the truth to be

\Delta_{i}:=r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})-\{\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)\},

(4)

and we introduce the functions
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
which are chosen to satisfy the following properties for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
i=1,\dots,n
:

\text{F1:}\ 0\leq\phi_{i}\leq c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime});\quad\text{F2:}\ 0\leq\phi^{\prime}_{i}\leq c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime});\quad\text{F3:}\ \phi_{i}-\phi_{i}^{\prime}=\Delta_{i}.

(5)

F1–F3 ensure that these are bounded, non-negative functions.
In this paper, we choose

\displaystyle\phi_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma\max[0,\Delta_{i}]+(1-\gamma)\left\{c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})+\min[0,\Delta_{i}]\right\}~~~\mbox{for some}~\gamma\in[0,1];

(6)

Corollary
1
in Section
4.1
shows that
\gamma=0
always leads to the most efficient algorithm so, in practice, we choose this.

Interchanging
\theta\leftrightarrow\theta^{\prime}
, we also define:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma\max[0,\Delta^{\prime}_{i}]+(1-\gamma)\left\{c_{i}M(\theta^{\prime},\theta)+\min[0,\Delta^{\prime}_{i}]\right\},

(7)

where
\Delta_{i}^{\prime}=r_{i}(\theta^{\prime},\theta;\widehat{\theta})-\{\ell_{i}(\theta)-\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\}=-\Delta_{i}
, since
r_{i}(\theta^{\prime},\theta;\widehat{\theta})=-r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})
for both
r^{(1)}
and
r^{(2)}
.
From (
3
) and (
4
), both the multiplicand of
\gamma
and that of
1-\gamma
in (
6
) are between
0
and
c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
, proving F1, and, by an analogous argument, as
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=M(\theta^{\prime},\theta)
, F2. F3 holds because
\max[0,\Delta_{i}]+\min[0,\Delta_{i}]=0+\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{i}
.

F1 permits simulation of the number of times each datum will be used this iteration:

S_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Pois}\left(\phi_{i}\right),~~~i=1,\dots,n.

(8)

Typically,
\phi_{i}<<1
so
S_{i}
is likely to be
0
. The acceptance probability for the algorithm is

\alpha(\theta,\theta^{\prime};s,\widehat{\theta})=1\wedge\frac{q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)p(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta|\theta^{\prime})p(\theta)}\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}{\phi_{i}}\right\}^{s_{i}}.

(9)

From F3,

\frac{p(s_{i}|\theta^{\prime},\theta)}{p(s_{i}|\theta,\theta^{\prime})}=\frac{\exp(-\phi_{i}^{\prime}){\phi_{i}^{\prime}}^{s_{i}}/s_{i}!}{\exp(-\phi_{i}){\phi_{i}}^{s_{i}}/s_{i}!}=\exp(\Delta_{i})\frac{{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}^{s_{i}}}{{\phi_{i}}^{s_{i}}}.

(10)

The product of all
n
such ratios contains
\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i})
, which contains the key term
p(y|\theta^{\prime})/p(y|\theta)
. Indeed, the expectation over
S:=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
) of the acceptance ratio is exactly the standard MH ratio (see Appendix
A.1
), so our algorithm is a particular case of
randomised Metropolis–Hastings

Nicholls et al. (2012)
. A direct proof that the invariant distribution is
\pi(\theta|y)
is also straightforward, as we now show.

Proposition 1
.

A MH-based Markov chain that proposes via
q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)
and (
8
) and accepts
\theta^{\prime}
with a probability of (
9
) satisfies detailed balance with respect to
\pi(\theta|y)\propto p(\theta)\exp[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\theta)]
.

Proof.

Write
p(s|\theta,\theta^{\prime}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(s_{i}|\theta,\theta^{\prime})
.
Detailed balance holds with respect to
\pi(\theta|y)
if

\pi(\theta|y)q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)p(s|\theta,\theta^{\prime})\alpha(\theta,\theta^{\prime};s,\widehat{\theta})=\pi(\theta^{\prime}|y)q(\theta|\theta^{\prime})p(s|\theta^{\prime},\theta)\alpha(\theta^{\prime},\theta;s,\widehat{\theta}).

(11)

This clearly holds when the acceptance probability is

\displaystyle\alpha(\theta,\theta^{\prime};s,\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=1\wedge\frac{\pi(\theta^{\prime}|y)q(\theta|\theta^{\prime})p(s|\theta^{\prime},\theta)}{\pi(\theta|y)q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)p(s|\theta,\theta^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=1\wedge\frac{q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)p(\theta^{\prime})}{q(\theta|\theta^{\prime})p(\theta)}\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)\right]\exp\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}{\phi_{i}}\right\}^{s_{i}},

where the second line follows from (
10
) and because
\pi(\theta|y)\propto p(\theta)\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\theta))
. This simplifies to (
9
) on substituting for
\Delta_{i}
from (
4
).

∎

Directly sampling independent Poisson variables
S_{1},\dots,S_{n}
would be an
\mathcal{O}(n)
operation that is performed at every Metropolis-Hastings iteration, and therefore not practical. To avoid such costly simulations, we use Poisson thinning
(
Lewis and Shedler, 1979
;
Cornish et al., 2019
;
Zhang et al., 2020
)
. Defining
C:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
, we proceed as follows:

1.

Sample
B\sim\mathsf{Pois}(CM(\theta,\theta^{\prime}))
.

2.

Conditional on
B
, sample
(T_{1},\dots,T_{B})\sim\mathsf{Multinomial}(B;c_{1}/C,\dots,c_{n}/C)
.
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
contains the indices (possibly repeated) of data that could be used this iteration.

3.

For each
j\in 1,\dots,B
, include
T_{j}
in
\mathcal{I}
with a probability
\phi_{T_{j}}/\{c_{T_{j}}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\}
. For each
i=1,\dots,n
,
S_{i}
is the number of times the index
i
occurs in
\mathcal{I}
.

Marginalising Steps 1 and 2 over
B
, the number of times each index
i
appears in the set
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
has a
\mathsf{Pois}(c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime}))
distribution, independent of the frequencies of all other indices. Step 3 then thins down to
\mathsf{Pois}(\phi_{i})
. Calculating
C
has a single
\mathcal{O}(n)
cost. Similarly, after
the
\mathcal{O}(n)
set-up cost of Walker’s alias method
Kronmal and Peterson (1979)
, Step 2 has a per-iteration cost of
\mathcal{O}(B)
since it assigns an index to each of the
B
values of
T_{j}
that are non-zero. Lastly, Step 3 also has a per-iteration cost of
\mathcal{O}(B)
.
The calculations in Appendix
C.1
show that
B=\Theta(d^{1/2})
when first-order control variates are used and
\Theta(d/\sqrt{n})
with second-order control variates: the per-iteration cost does not grow with
n
.
The final set-up cost of the algorithm is in finding a suitable
\widehat{\theta}
, which is also
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

3
Existing
exact
subsampling MH algorithms

Maclaurin and Adams (2014)
propose the Firefly Monte Carlo (FlyMC) algorithm, which augments the parameter space by introducing latent Bernoulli random variables. The success probabilities are defined so that the
\theta
marginal of the invariant distribution of the Markov chain is
\pi
. Crucially, the evaluation of the joint posterior only requires likelihood terms corresponding to the non-zero Bernoulli variables. The biggest limitation of this algorithm is the requirement of a global exponential-family lower bound that is needed on each likelihood term
B_{i}(\theta)\leq\ell_{i}(\theta)
. A secondary consideration is the trade-off between the MCMC mixing of the Bernoulli variables and the computational cost of updating them.

3.1
Scalable Metropolis–Hastings

Similar to our proposed algorithm, scalable MH
(
Cornish et al., 2019
, SMH;)
also assumes that the log-likelihood difference is bounded as in (
3
); however, the delayed-acceptance idea is applied repeatedly to give an overall acceptance probability of

\alpha_{1}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{n}1\wedge\exp\left(\Delta_{i}\right).

(15)

We explain the remainder of the algorithm using a formulation that is equivalent to that in
Cornish et al. (2019)
, but using quantities more familiar from MH-SS.

B
and
T_{1},\dots,T_{B}
are (effectively) simulated via the same mechanism as in MH-SS so that
N_{i}
, the number of repeats of the index
i
in the set
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
has a
\mathsf{Pois}(c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime}))
distribution. Then, for each
i
,
A_{i}\sim\mathsf{Binom}(N_{i},p_{i})
is created, where
p_{i}=-\log[1\wedge\exp(\Delta_{i})]/[c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})]
. Since
A_{i}\sim\mathsf{Pois}(-\log[1\wedge\exp(\Delta_{i})])
,
\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}=0)
is exactly the product term in (
15
). As
(1\wedge a)(1\wedge b)\leq 1\wedge ab
, the acceptance probability (
15
) is always lower than ours. Experiments with first-order control variates later in this section show that to avoid the acceptance rate dropping unacceptably low
as
n
increases
, the scaling parameter in the proposal
q(\theta^{\prime}|\theta)
must be reduced to well below the optimal value for the equivalent MH algorithm. Our bounds

3.2
TunaMH

The TunaMH algorithm of
Zhang et al. (2020)
again uses (
3

\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}

\phi_{i}=[\ell_{i}(\theta)-\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})+c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})]/2
, a special case of (

\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
. Section

is always

In TunaMH, where there are no control variates,
c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
is much larger (because the quantity it is bounding is much larger); however, it is also, typically, a much tighter bound on
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}

\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
varying across the range from

to

\chi
, to each Poisson expectation,
\phi_{i}
. This lower bounds both the numerator and denominator of every term in the product in (
8

\chi
is sufficiently large compared with each
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}

and hence the computational cost, leading to an apparent trade-off between the acceptance rate and the subsample size.

In fact, it is a three-way trade-off. Decreasing the scaling parameter,
\lambda
, for the jumps in the MH proposal,

, decreases

and hence
\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)

\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}
becomes smaller compared with
\chi
; the smaller
\lambda
helps stabilise the acceptance probability. In reducing

\mathbb{E}\left[{B}\right]
(the size of the final subsample) through smaller
\phi_{i}
. The advice in
Zhang et al. (2020)
is to add only a small amount to each expectation so that no more than one extra observation is used on average in each subsample, and to aim for an empirical acceptance rate of around 60%. As a consequence, the Metropolis–Hastings scaling
\lambda
is reduced substantially compared with a standard random walk Metropolis algorithm, the Markov chain mixes more slowly and although the algorithm is more efficient than MH with
the same

\lambda
but no subsampling, it brings no substantial improvement over an

MH algorithm, as we show in Figure

Appendix
H

0.23

Lemma 1
.

Let

Remark 3

We make three points. Firstly, when

In Appendix

Appendix

7
Discussion

Posterior sampling via MCMC on datasets with a large number of observations poses a significant challenge, particularly to traditional methods like the Metropolis–Hastings (MH) algorithm, which requires the evaluation of all likelihood terms at each iteration. This has led Bayesian researchers to turn away from MCMC algorithms and to instead utilise approximate and biased inference schemes in order to approximate the posterior distribution. We have presented MH-SS, a novel exact Metropolis–Hastings-based algorithm designed to scale posterior sampling for large datasets through data subsampling. Our approach assumes that the log-likelihood differences can be tightly bounded and introduces an auxiliary Poisson variable which allows us to extend the Metropolis–Hastings ratio so that not all observations are needed, thereby reducing the computational effort per iteration.

Our approach shares features with the Tuna algorithm of
Zhang et al. (2020)
, using Poisson auxiliary random variables whose expectation depends on both the current and proposed value. However, as seen in the experiments and real-world applications of Sections
5
and
6
, Tuna typically performs relatively poorly because it requires sub-optimal scaling of the Metropolis–Hastings proposal in order to control the acceptance rate. Our more general formulation, combined with the use of control variates, leads to orders of magnitude greater efficiency. The scalable Metropolis–Hastings algorithm
(
Cornish et al., 2019
)
uses the same generic form for the control variates as we do, but our bounds on the discrepancy between the control variate and the truth scale better with increasing dimension and this, together with the removal of the large product in the acceptance probability and another novel tightening, has a substantial impact on the overall efficiency. We have also provided guidelines on choosing the proposal scaling for MH-SS: choose
\lambda
to target an empirical acceptance of around
0.452
. This guidance has been verified empirically and holds whether using first- or second-order control variates.

Although all of our worked examples are regression models with independent data, the methodology is not limited to such data. Consider regression models with data
y_{i,j}
for the
i
th of
n>>1
subjects, and a subject-specific random intercept (and, potentially, slope). MH-SS could subsample by subject and perform numerical integration over the random effects; since
n>>1
including all random effects as latent variables could make the statespace too large for
any
exact MCMC algorithm. Alternatively consider an AR(
p
) process for data
y_{t}
,
t=p,\dots T
, which, given initial values of
y_{0},\dots,y_{p-1}
, are generated as:

Y_{t}|(Y_{1:t-1}=y_{1:t-1})\sim\mathsf{Pois}(\log[1+\exp(\theta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\theta_{i}y_{t-i})]),~~~t=p,\dots,T.

This, and analogous binary- or robust- autoregressive models could be re-framed as standard regression model with a covariate vector of
x_{t}=(1,y_{t-p},\dots,y_{t-1})
.

We are grateful to an anonymous reviewer who pointed us to recent works
Salomone et al. (2020)
;
Villani et al. (2024)
that transform a stationary time series to the frequency domain, creating the Whittle likelihood, a product of exponential densities, which would be amenable to MH-SS, provided suitable
c_{i}
could be found. The accuracy of the Whittle likelihood depends on a central-limit-theorem argument, so MH-SS would be performing exact inference on an approximation to the true posterior, but this, nonetheless, is an interesting area for future research. In general, MH-SS can be applied to any model for which the bounds,
c_{i}
, exist and can be calculated in advance. The same applies to the SMH and TunaMH methodologies in
Cornish et al. (2019)
and
Zhang et al. (2020)
, respectively.

For some models, even with large amounts of data, difficulty in identifying one or more parameters might lead to a multi-modal posterior. In this case, before running the algorithm, one might gather information on each mode and, for each mode, create an appropriate control-variate function and bound. With some care, it is still possible to ensure detailed balance holds with respect to the true posterior. Appendix
G
details this extension to MH-SS, and a simulation study that demonstrates the expected large increase in efficiency on a multimodal target.

Appendix B
Tuning the MH-SS proposal, and alternatives

In this section, we provide guidelines for the choice of the scaling parameter
\lambda
in the special case of a random-walk Metropolis proposal (
14

In our analysis, we set
V\equiv V_{d}=-[H(\hat{\theta})]^{-1}
, where
[H(\hat{\theta})]^{-1}
represents the inverse of the Hessian at an estimate
\widehat{\theta}
near the mode. In the large-

d

;

Sherlock et al. (2021)

However, while the MH-SS algorithms do have a delayed-acceptance component, they have two specific properties that, respectively aid and necessitate a new treatment: the extreme accuracy/inaccuracy of the approximate target and an approximately linear relationship between the proposal scaling and the computational cost.

The computational cost per iteration is approximately proportional to the number of data points used and the expectation of this is proportional to
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
. With the proposal (
14

or

; see also the analysis in Appendix

\widetilde{\theta}
is the posterior mode. In regression models, for example, if the covariate vector
X_{i}\sim\nu
, for some distribution,
\nu
, then
\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{E}}^{d}(\theta_{*})=\mathbb{E}_{{X\sim\nu}}\left[{\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{E}}^{d}(\theta_{*};X)}\right]
, the expectation over covariate vectors of the expected Fisher information conditional on the covariates.

•

We consider the RWM, with a proposal of
\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{N}(\theta,\lambda^{2}_{n,d}V^{d})
for some fixed (as
n
increases) positive-definite matrix,
V^{d}
.

•

We assume that
\Sigma^{d}:=\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{E}}^{d}(\theta_{*})^{-1}
is well behaved as
d\to\infty
, satisfying the conditions in (
27
) and that the user ensures that
V^{d}
is similarly well-behaved, satisfying (
28
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathsf{trace}(\Sigma^{d})\to t_{\pi}<\infty,

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\Sigma^{d}_{i,i}\right)^{1/2}\to t^{*}_{\pi}>0~~~\mbox{and}~~~\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{d,d}\left(\Sigma^{d}_{i,j}\right)^{2}\to 0,

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathsf{trace}(V^{d})\to t_{v}<\infty,

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(V^{d}_{i,i}\right)^{1/2}\to t^{*}_{v}>0~~~\mbox{and}~~~\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{d,d}(V^{d}_{i,j})^{2}\to 0.

(28)

•

To keep the acceptance rate bounded as
n
and
d
become large, we choose

\lambda_{n,d}=\frac{\ell}{n^{1/2}d^{1/2}}.

(29)

The function of
n
follows directly from (
26
), whereas the function of
d
follows from
Gelman et al. (1997)
and the many subsequent articles on optimal scaling of the random walk Metropolis algorithm.

•

We assume that
\widehat{\theta}
, the approximation to the mode, found via a stochastic-gradient algorithm, satisfies:

||\widehat{\theta}-\widetilde{\theta}||_{2}\leq c~\frac{d^{1/2}}{n^{1/2}},

(30)

for some
c<t_{\pi}
and not depending on
n
or
d
. This assumption is weak: in practice, for a reasonable approximation to the mode, we would expect
c<<t_{\pi}
.

Proposition 4
.

Subject to assumptions (
26
), (
27
), (
28
), (
29
) and (
30
):

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}=\Theta(n^{-1/2}),~~~\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}=\Theta(d^{1/2}n^{-1/2})

\displaystyle~~~\mbox{and}~~~\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}=\Theta(d^{1/2}n^{-1/2}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{1}}\right]=\Theta(d^{1/2}n^{-1/2}),~~~\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}}\right]=\Theta(dn^{-1/2})

\displaystyle~~~\mbox{and}~~~\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}}\right]=\Theta(dn^{-1/2}),

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(d^{1/2}n^{-1/2}),~~~\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(dn^{-1/2})

\displaystyle~~~\mbox{and}~~~\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(dn^{-1/2}),

where results in the first and last line hold in probability.

Proof
:
We will use the following repeatedly: for any vector
A\sim\mathsf{N}(0,W)
for some positive definite matrix
W
:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}^{2}}\right]=\mathsf{trace}(W),~\mathsf{Var}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}^{2}}\right]=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{d,d}W_{i,j}^{2},~\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{d}|A_{i}|}\right]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{W_{i,i}}.

From (
28
) and (
29
), as
d\to\infty
, for any fixed
n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{n||\theta^{\prime}-\theta||_{2}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=n\lambda^{2}_{n,d}\mathsf{trace}(V_{d})\to\ell^{2}t_{v},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left[{n||\theta^{\prime}-\theta||_{2}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=2n^{2}\lambda_{n,d}^{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}V_{d,i,j}^{2}\to 0,

so
n||\theta^{\prime}-\theta||_{2}^{2}\to\ell^{2}t_{v}

in probability
, giving the bound for
\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}
. Analogously,
from (
27
),
\mathbb{E}\left[{n\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|^{2}/d}\right]=t_{\pi}
and

n\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}/d\to t_{\pi}

in probability
. By the triangle inequality,

\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}-\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\widetilde{\theta}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\|_{2}+\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\widetilde{\theta}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}.

In the limit as
d\to\infty
, and subject to (
27
) and (
30
), we obtain

t_{\pi}-c\leq\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}\leq t_{\pi}+c,

giving the bounds for
\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}
.
The triangle inequality then gives

\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}-\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}\leq\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}\leq\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}+\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2},

giving the bounds for
\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}
.
Thus, we have proved the sizes stated in the first line of the proposition.

For the
L_{1}
norms, by Jensen’s inequality,

\|A\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|A_{i}|=d\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|A_{i}|\leq d\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}^{2}}=d^{1/2}\|A\|_{2}.

We already know the limiting
L_{2}
norms of
\theta^{\prime}-\theta
,
\theta-\widehat{\theta}
and
\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}
so we know that the limiting
L_{1}
norms can be no more than a factor
d^{1/2}
larger,
giving the sizes in the final line of the proposition statement
. However,

\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{1}}\right]=\frac{n^{1/2}}{d^{1/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\ell}{n^{1/2}d^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{V_{ii}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\ell}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{V_{ii}}\to\ell\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}t^{*}_{v}.

Similarly,

\frac{n^{1/2}}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}}\right]=\frac{n^{1/2}}{d}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{V_{ii}}\to\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}t^{*}_{\pi}.

The triangle inequality shows that
\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}}\right]
behaves similarly to
\mathbb{E}\left[{\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{1}}\right]
.

\square

C.1
Our bounds

We now consider the generic bounds of Corollary
2
.
Directly from (
25
),
\|x_{i}\|_{2}=\Theta(1)
, from which both
c_{i}^{(1)}
and
c_{i}^{(2)}
are
\Theta(1)
. Further, Proposition
4
gives
M^{(1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\Theta(\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2})\Theta(\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2})=\Theta(d^{1/2}/n)
and
M^{(2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\Theta(\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2})\Theta(\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}^{2})=\Theta(d/n^{3/2})
.
In particular, then, for a single observation,

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}^{(1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{i}^{(1)}M^{(1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\Theta\left(d^{1/2}n^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}^{(2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{i}^{(2)}M^{(2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\Theta\left(dn^{-3/2}\right),

where the size (
\Theta(d^{1/2}n^{-1})
and
\Theta(dn^{-3/2})
) apply in probability in the limit of large
n
and
d
.
The above bounds are proportional to the probability that a given observation will be considered for use in the accept-reject stage.
There are
n
observations, and evaluation of each
r_{i}^{(1)}
is
\Theta(d)
, so the overall costs of
r^{(1)}
is
n\times\Theta(d^{1/2}n^{-1})\times\Theta(d)=\mathcal{O}(d^{3/2})
. In general, evaluation of each
r_{i}^{(2)}
has a cost of
\Theta(d^{2})
. Hence the cost for
r^{(2)}

n\times\Theta(dn^{-3/2})\times\Theta(d^{2})=\Theta(d^{3}/n^{1/2})
.

C.2
SMH bounds

For the SMH algorithm, Equation (13) of
Cornish et al. (2019)
has

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}^{(1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(||\theta-\widehat{\theta}||_{1}^{2}+||\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}||_{1}^{2}\right)\overline{U}_{2,i},

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}^{(2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{6}\left(||\theta-\widehat{\theta}||_{1}^{3}+||\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}||_{1}^{3}\right)\overline{U}_{3,i},

where

\overline{U}_{2,i}=\max_{j,k=1,\dots,d}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}U_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}\partial\theta_{k}}\right|~~~\mbox{and}~~~\overline{U}_{3,i}=\max_{j,k,l=1,\dots,d}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|\frac{\partial^{3}U_{i}}{\partial\theta_{j}\partial\theta_{k}\theta_{l}}\right|

Repeating the total cost analysis, including the
\mathcal{O}(d)
cost of evaluating
r^{(1)}
and the
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
cost for
r^{(2)}
, for the scalable MH bounds, we obtain costs of

\mathcal{O}(d^{3}n^{-1})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{U}_{2,i}
and
\mathcal{O}(d^{5}n^{-3/2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{U}_{3,i}
for first and second-order control variates, respectively.

For the logistic regression model, Appendix G.1 of the SMH paper
(
Cornish et al., 2019
)
derives
\overline{U}_{2,i}=\frac{1}{4}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|x_{i,j}|^{2}
and
\overline{U}_{3,i}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{3}}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|x_{i,j}|^{3}
. Thus, if
d^{1/2}x_{i,j}
is bounded, so are
d~\overline{U}_{2,i}
and
d^{3/2}\overline{U}_{3,i}
. If, on the other hand, each
x_{i,j}
is Gaussian then
\overline{U}_{2,i}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}\log d)
and
\overline{U}_{3,i}=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{-3/2}\{\log d\}^{3/2}\right)
. In this case, we have total computational costs of
\mathcal{O}\left(d^{2}\log d\right)
for SMH-1 and
\mathcal{O}\left(d^{7/2}\{\log d\}^{3/2}n^{-1/2}\right)
for SMH-2.

D.3
Comparison of implementations

The results presented throughout the paper consider an implementation of SMH that evaluates the likelihood at most
B\leq n
times in each MCMC iteration. If the proposal
\theta^{\prime}
is accepted, it indicates that the likelihood was exactly evaluated
B
times. Conversely, if
\theta^{\prime}
is rejected, it suggests that the likelihood was most likely evaluated fewer than
B
times because SMH is designed to reject a proposal based on sequential point-wise likelihood evaluations; for more details, see Algorithm 1 in
Cornish et al. (2019)
. In contrast, both MH-SS and Tuna are originally designed to evaluate the likelihood exactly
B
times before deciding whether to accept or reject the proposal.

The sequential point-wise rejection process employed by SMH implies that likelihood evaluations cannot be computed in a vectorised fashion in programming languages like Python and R. Consequently, while loops are required to handle these evaluations. We point out that, for the sake of fair run-time comparisons, the results presented in the paper for RWM, SMH, MH-SS and Tuna were all obtained from implementations based on for-loops. In practice, however, we find that vectorised operations in Python are often faster compared to those relying on for-loops.

In an effort to enhance the run-time efficiency of SMH, we implemented a vectorised approach for evaluating the likelihood. For fairness in the comparisons, when vectorising the loops in SMH-1 and SMH-2, we also vectorised the computations in Tuna, MH-SS-1, MH-SS-2 and in the RWM. In the ‘vectorised SMH’ algorithm, the likelihood is evaluated exactly
B
times regardless of whether
\theta^{\prime}
is accepted or rejected. We note that the impact of SMH vectorization is exclusively observed in metrics related to run-times, such as ESS per second. Specifically, only the denominator of the ESS per second changes, as the ESS remains the same. Metrics like
\mathbb{E}(B)
and ESS/
\mathbb{E}(B)
also remain the same. Though this modification resulted in a significant computational speed-up, particularly for SMH-1 as it tends to evaluate more likelihood terms, it was not enough to make SMH more computationally efficient than MH-SS.

Figure
9
shows the results of ESS per second obtained from simulation experiments with a logistic regression model. Panel (a) exhibits results based on foor-loop implementations, while panel (b) shows the vectorised versions. We stress that within each panel, the RWM, SMH, MH-SS and Tuna implementations are either all based on for-loops or all vectorised. Overall, comparing panels (a) and (b), we can see that the vectorised versions of SMH-1 and SMH-2 are significantly faster than their for-loop counterparts. For instance, for
d=50
and
n=10^{5}
, the efficiency of SMH-1 and SMH-2 shows a 15- and 39-fold improvement, respectively, as their ESS per second jumped from
10^{-2}
and

to
10^{-0.8}
and
10^{1.3}
, respectively. Unsurprisingly, the gains in terms of run-times for the vectorised MH-SS are smaller, especially for MH-SS-2 with large
n
, as it requires smaller subsamples.

Appendix F
Justification of optimal-scaling Assumption
2

The use of first-order control variates,
r_{i}^{(1)}
, is equivalent to using the approximation

\log\widehat{p}^{(1)}(y_{1:n}|\theta):=(\theta-\widehat{\theta})^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\widehat{\theta})+c=(\theta-\widehat{\theta})^{\top}\nabla\log p(y_{1:n}|\widehat{\theta})+c,

whereas the use of second-order control variates,
r_{i}^{(2)}
, is equivalent to

\log\widehat{p}^{(2)}(y_{1:n}|\theta):=-\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\widehat{\theta})^{\top}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}(\widehat{\theta})\right\}(\theta-\widehat{\theta})+c\approx p(y_{1:n}|\theta),

for large
n
, by the Bernstein-von Mises Theorem.

If
\widehat{\theta}
is a reasonable approximation to the mode, then
\nabla\log\pi(\widehat{\theta})\approx 0
(indeed, this would be one definition of a reasonable approximation). In the Bernstein-von Mises limit,
\|\theta-\widehat{\theta}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}d^{1/2})
, and it is straightforward to show that provided
\left.\nabla\log p(y_{1:n}|\theta)\right|_{\widehat{\theta}}=\mathcal{O}(n^{1/2})
,
\log\widehat{p}^{(1)}(y_{1:n}|\theta^{\prime})-\log\widehat{p}^{(1)}(y_{1:n}|\theta)=\mathcal{O}(1)
, typically.

Implicit in the justification of (
9
) is that, from the moment generating function of
S_{i}\sim\mathsf{Pois}(\phi_{i})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\phi_{i}^{\prime}/\phi_{i}\right)^{S_{i}}}\right]=\exp\left[\phi_{i}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime}_{i}}{\phi_{i}}-1\right)\right]=\exp[\phi_{i}^{\prime}-\phi_{i}]=\exp[\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})].

Thus, the acceptance ratio for
\alpha_{2,MHSS}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
in (
13
) has an expectation of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}\{\log\phi_{i}^{\prime}-\log\phi_{i}\}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}{\phi_{i}}\right\}^{S_{i}}}\right]=\frac{p(y_{1:n}|\theta^{\prime})}{p(y_{1:n}|\theta)}\exp\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right].

Despite our best efforts, whether first- or second-order control variates are used, the upper bounds from Corollary
2
are typically very loose. As argued directly after the statement of the corollary, this is because in moderate to high dimensions,
\theta^{\prime}-\theta
,
\theta-\widehat{\theta}
and
\theta^{\prime}-\widehat{\theta}
are close to perpendicular to most covariate vectors
x_{i}
. However, we cannot use this fact to obtain better bounds without
\mathcal{O}(n)
effort at each iteration. Typically, therefore,
c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})>>|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})|
. With
\gamma=0
, (
6
) and (
7
) tell us that

\phi_{i}=c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})(1-\delta_{i})
and
\phi_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})(1-\delta_{i}^{\prime})
with
\delta_{i}=-\min[0,\Delta_{i}]/\{c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\}<<1
and
\delta^{\prime}_{i}=-\min[0,\Delta^{\prime}_{i}]/\{c_{i}M(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\}<<1
. Again from the moment generating function of a Poisson
\phi_{i}
random variable:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\phi_{i}^{\prime}/\phi_{i}\right)^{2S_{i}}}\right]=\exp\left[\frac{{\phi^{\prime}_{i}}^{2}}{\phi_{i}}-\phi_{i}\right]\approx\exp\left[2\phi_{i}^{\prime}-2\phi_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\phi_{i}^{\prime}/\phi_{i}\right)^{S_{i}}}\right]^{2},

where the approximation arises from a first-order Taylor expansion. Thus, to first order,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}{\phi_{i}}\right\}^{S_{i}}\right)^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}{\phi_{i}}\right\}^{S_{i}}}\right]^{2}

and so
\mathsf{Var}\left[{\exp\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}\{\log\phi_{i}^{\prime}-\log\phi_{i}\}\right\}}\right]=0
to first order. Heuristically, this justifies the assumption that for large
d
and typical
\theta\sim\pi
,

\exp\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}\{\log\phi_{i}^{\prime}-\log\phi_{i}\}\right]\approx\frac{p(y_{1:n}|\theta^{\prime})}{p(y_{1:n}|\theta)}\exp\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta})\right].

The above and the arguments about
\widehat{p}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{p}^{(2)}
that precede it then imply Assumption
2
.

Appendix G
Multimodal targets

G.1
MH-SS extension

For certain models, some structural identifiability issue might render a posterior multimodal even when large amounts of data are available. Canonical examples include mixture models and hidden-Markov models. In such cases, the control variates created from mode
\widehat{\theta}^{(1)}
would be inappropriate while the chain is close to a different mode,
\widehat{\theta}^{(2)}
. The obvious solution is for MH-SS to choose the control variate associated with the nearest mode to the current value,
\theta
; however, such an algorithm would not satisfy detailed balance with respect to the true posterior distribution and so would lead to biased inferences. Notice that the entire proof of Proposition
1
is conditional on a given (arbitrary)
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
. Thus, provided the same control variates and bounds are used in the expressions
\alpha(\theta,\theta^{\prime};s,\widehat{\theta})
and
\alpha(\theta^{\prime},\theta;s,\widehat{\theta})
in (
11
), the proof follows exactly as it stands. Thus, for the detailed-balance condition to hold, we need
the same
control variates and bounds to be chosen whether
\theta
is the current value and
\theta^{\prime}
the proposal or
\theta^{\prime}
is the current value and
\theta
is the proposal.

Our extension of MH-SS to the multimodal scenario chooses the control variates according to the value of
\overline{\theta}:=(\theta+\theta^{\prime})/2
. When
\overline{\theta}
is between the modes, we will use the average of the control variates

\overline{r}_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\{r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1})+r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{2})\right\},

and the natural bound derived as follows. The triangle inequality and (
3
) give

\displaystyle|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-\overline{r}_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1})\right|

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1}{2}\left|\ell_{i}(\theta^{\prime})-\ell_{i}(\theta)-r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{i}\overline{M}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2}),

where
\overline{M}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2}):=\left\{M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1})+M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{2})\right\}/2
.
For simplicity, we decide on which control variate to use according to the projection of
\overline{\theta}
onto the line between
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
:

D:=(\overline{\theta}-\widehat{\theta}_{1})\times\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\widehat{\theta}_{1}}{\|\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\widehat{\theta}_{1}\|}.

Specifically, for
i=1,\dots,n
,

r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1})&\mbox{if }D<\|\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\widehat{\theta}_{1}\|/3,\\
r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{2})&\mbox{if }D>2\|\widehat{\theta}_{2}-\widehat{\theta}_{1}\|/3,\\
\overline{r}_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})&\mbox{otherwise}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(31)

Similarly,
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})
is
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1})
,
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{2})
or
\overline{M}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})
for the same three ranges of
D
.

We first describe the new MH-SS algorithm. We then describe a simple mixture model that leads to a bimodal posterior even with large amounts of data, and we construct a short simulation study on the posterior distribution for this model.

The set up for the multimodal MH-SS is the same as Algorithm
1
except that Steps 5, 6 and 7 find approximations to both modes, individuals gradients (and Hessians if CV2) and sums of these, respectively.

Compared with Algorithm
2
, the multimodal Algorithm has an additional step between Steps 3 and 4, to decide on
M(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})
and the set
r_{i}(\theta,\theta^{\prime};\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2})
(
i=1,\dots,n)
; Steps 4, 7 and 16 then use these. With first-order covariates and a flat prior, the delayed acceptance step adds complication for no benefit, since if
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
were exact modes (rather than approximate) the Stage 1 acceptance probability would be
1
. Thus, in the experiments described below we stripped out the DA step, both for MHSS and MH.

Y_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{t}_{\nu}(x_{i}^{\top}\beta,1);

associated with
\beta_{1}

\beta_{-}:=(\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{d})

\eta_{-}:=x_{-}^{\top}\beta_{-}

3

Multiplicative constants will cancel, so it is sufficient to consider
f(x)=\left(1+x^{2}/\nu\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2}
. After some calculus, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nu+1}{\nu}x\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+3)/2},

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu+1}{\nu}\left\{\frac{\nu+2}{\nu}x^{2}-1\right\}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+5)/2}.

Thus

\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=-(\nu+1)\frac{x}{\nu+x^{2}}.

This reaches its global maximum and minimum values at
x=\pm\sqrt{\nu}
, giving the first part of Lemma
3
.

Further,

\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=\frac{\nu+1}{\nu}\left\{\frac{\nu+2}{\nu}x^{2}-1\right\}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}}{\nu}\right)^{-2}.

Setting
u:=x^{2}/\nu
, we need to find the maximum absolute value of

d(u):=\frac{\nu+1}{\nu}\frac{(\nu+2)u-1}{(1+u)^{2}},

for non-negative
u
. This function is negative and increasing at
u=0
and positive and decreasing from its maximum, so its absolute value is either
|d(0)|=(\nu+1)/\nu
or the function’s maximum. For
d^{\prime}(u_{*})=0
we require

(1+u_{*})^{2}(\nu+2)=2\{(\nu+2)u-1\}(1+u_{*})\implies u_{*}=\frac{\nu+4}{\nu+2}.

Finally,

d(u_{*})=\frac{(\nu+1)(\nu+2)^{2}}{4\nu(\nu+3)}=\frac{(\nu+2)^{2}}{4(\nu+3)}\times\frac{\nu+1}{\nu},

which shows that the maximum absolute value is
d(u_{*})
for
\nu\geq\sqrt{8}
and
(\nu+1)/\nu
otherwise.
