A hierarchy of heat conduction laws
Filippo Dell’Oro and Vittorino Pata

Abstract
. The purpose of this work is to produce a family of equations describing the evolution of the temperature in a rigid heat conductor. This is obtained by means of successive approximations of the Fourier law, via memory relaxations and integral perturbations.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
35K05, 35Q79, 74A15, 80A05

1.
Introduction

Consider a homogenous isotropic rigid heat conductor occupying
a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

and denote by

u=u(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}

its
temperature variation
at each point and time
from an equilibrium reference value
\theta_{0}
, that is,

u(\boldsymbol{x},t)={{\theta(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\theta_{0}}\over{\theta_{0}}},

where
\theta
is the absolute temperature.
In absence of heat energy generation, from external or internal sources,
the thermal evolution of the body
is governed by the energy balance equation

\partial_{t}\mathsf{e}+{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{q}=0.

Here,

\mathsf{e}=\mathsf{e}(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}

is
the
internal energy
of the conductor, while

\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

is the
heat flux vector
.
Within the assumption of small variations of the temperature and its gradient,
the rate of change of the internal energy in the material
is proportional to the rate of change of its temperature (see
[
21
]
).
Namely,

\partial_{t}\mathsf{e}=c\partial_{t}u,

where the proportionality constant

c>0
is the
specific heat
.
Accordingly, setting from now on
c=1
,
the energy balance equation takes the form

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u+{\rm div}\,\boldsymbol{q}=0.

In order to derive an equation ruling the evolution of the
relative temperature, one more ingredient is needed:
the so-called constitutive law for the heat flux,
allowing to write
\boldsymbol{q}
in terms of
u
. In fact,
the choice of
the constitutive law is
what really determines a model of heat conduction.
At the same time, being a purely heuristic
interpretation of a certain physical phenomenon, it
may reflect different individual perceptions
of reality, or even philosophical beliefs.

The first constitutive law for the heat flux appeared in the literature
is
\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u
, with
\kappa>0
, established in 1882 by Fourier
in his celebrated work
Théorie analytique de la chaleur

[
13
]
.
Since then, as shown in the next section, a number of variants
of this law have been proposed.
In particular, in 1995, Tzou suggested in the famous paper
[
31
]
a modification
of the Fourier law where both
\boldsymbol{q}
and the gradient of temperature
present a delay, namely,

(1.2)

\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x},t+\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}})=-\kappa\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x},t+\tau_{u}),

where
\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}},\tau_{u}>0
are delay parameters.
Some years later, Tzou’s theory has been modified via
the introduction of a constitutive equation of form
[
27
]

(1.3)

\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x},t+\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}})=-\kappa\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x},t+\tau_{u})-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t+\tau_{u_{1}}),

where
\tau_{u_{1}}
is another delay parameter which is also assumed to be positive.
Here,
u_{1}
is the so-called thermal displacement satisfying
\partial_{t}u_{1}=u
,
and
\kappa_{1}>0
is a constant which is typical in the type II and III thermoelastic theories.
Heat conduction laws of the above kind are known as

phase lag models
; more precisely, dual phase lag if (
1.2
), three phase lag if (
1.3
).
Since the delay parameters are assumed to be very small,
Quintanilla and Racke
[
26
]
(but see also the textbook
[
29
]
)
proposed to take
the formal Taylor expansions of (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)
stopping at different orders, that is
(omitting the dependence on the space variable
\boldsymbol{x}
),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\ldots

\displaystyle=-\kappa\nabla u(t)-\tau_{u}\kappa\nabla\partial_{t}u(t)-\frac{1}{2}\tau_{u}^{2}\kappa\nabla\partial_{tt}u(t)+\ldots,

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}\partial_{tt}\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\ldots

\displaystyle=-\kappa\nabla u(t)-\tau_{u}\kappa\nabla\partial_{t}u(t)-\frac{1}{2}\tau_{u}^{2}\kappa\nabla\partial_{tt}u(t)+\ldots

\displaystyle\quad-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}(t)-\tau_{u_{1}}\kappa_{1}\nabla\partial_{t}u_{1}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\tau_{u_{1}}^{2}\kappa_{1}\nabla\partial_{tt}u_{1}(t)+\ldots.

Therefore, the sum

\eqref{BASE}+\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}\partial_{t}\eqref{BASE}+\frac{1}{2}\tau_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}\partial_{tt}\eqref{BASE}+\ldots

gives rise to a hierarchy
of evolution equations in the variable
u
and
u_{1}
, respectively.

In the present work, taking inspiration from this idea,
we propose an alternative path, which allows in particular to include
into the picture some heat laws containing memory terms
described by convolution integrals,
hence having a non local character in time.
The starting point is again the Fourier law. At each step, we find a heat law involving

\nabla u_{n}
along with its time derivatives
up to a certain order,
where
\partial_{t}^{n}u_{n}=u
. Then, we perform a memory relaxation
of
\nabla u_{n}
via a convolution integral, and we introduce a further integral
perturbation by adding the term
-\kappa_{n+1}\nabla u_{n+1}
, with
\kappa_{n+1}>0
small.
When the convolution kernel is the negative exponential,
we can show that what we obtain is nothing but the analogue of the
law of the previous step,
now expressed in terms of
u_{n+1}
,
and where the order of the time derivatives of the heat flux has increased
by one.
We depict our procedure in the following flow chart:

Heat law of order
n
(depending on
u_{n}
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\downarrow}

Memory relaxation of
\nabla u_{n}
plus a perturbation
with
\nabla u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\downarrow}

Heat law of order
n
with memory

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\downarrow}

Choose the exponential kernel

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\downarrow}

Obtain the heat law of order
n+1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\downarrow}

Repeat the procedure…

With this method, we produce a hierarchy of evolution equations,
along with their memory counterparts. In fact, most of our equations without memory
are the same of those found in
[
26
]
. However, it is important to point out
that, contrary to
[
26
]
where the equations are
at any step in the variables
u
or
u_{1}
, in our case
the equation at the
n^{\rm th}
-step is for the variable
u_{n}
.

2.
The Fourier Law and its Relaxations

2.1.
The Fourier law

For the classical Fourier constitutive law
[
13
]

(2.1)

\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u,

where
\kappa>0
is the
instantaneous conductivity
,
we deduce from (
1.1
) the well-known
heat equation

(2.2)

\partial_{t}u-\kappa\Delta u=0.

This equation, being parabolic, exhibits a physically unpleasant feature: a disturbance at any point is felt instantaneously
everywhere. In other words, the speed
propagation of thermal
signals is infinite.
This phenomenon,
sometimes referred to as the
paradox
of heat conduction
(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
12
]
),
is not expected, nor observed, in real conductors.
Therefore, several attempts have been made through the years
in order to introduce some hyperbolicity in the mathematical modeling
of heat conduction.
Starting from (
2.1
), the idea is then to produce more
refined heat laws by means of perturbative arguments,
or by applying some kind of relaxation
to the variables in play.

2.2.
The Maxwell-Cattaneo law

A first possibility is adopting the
renowned Maxwell-Cattaneo law
[
1
]
,
namely, the differential perturbation
of (
2.1
)

(2.3)

\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u,

where
0<\varepsilon\ll\kappa
.
In which case, the sum

\eqref{BASE}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\eqref{BASE}

entails
the weakly damped wave equation

(2.4)

\varepsilon\partial_{tt}u+\partial_{t}u-\kappa\Delta u=0,

otherwise called the telegrapher’s equation, widely employed in the description
of several physical phenomena.

2.3.
The Gurtin-Pipkin and the Coleman-Gurtin laws

Another strategy is
to relax the term (or part of the term)
\nabla u
appearing
in the Fourier law (
2.1
) by means of a time-convolution
against a
bounded convex summable function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
usually called
memory kernel
, of total mass

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)ds=1.

Precisely, for
\omega\in[0,1)
, we take (omitting the dependence on
t
outside the integral)

(2.5)

\boldsymbol{q}=-\omega\kappa\nabla u-(1-\omega)\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\nabla u(t-s)ds=0,

where the initial past history of the temperature
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)_{|t\leq 0}

is supposed to be known, and it is regarded as an initial datum of the problem.
The above is usually called the Gurtin-Pipkin law
[
21
]
when
\omega=0
,
and the Coleman-Gurtin law
[
3
]
when
\omega\in(0,1)
.
Plugging (
2.5
) into (
1.1
)
we end up with the integro-differential equation

(2.6)

\partial_{t}u-\omega\kappa\Delta u-(1-\omega)\kappa\,\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta u(t-s)ds=0,

which exhibits a nonlocal (in time) character, due to the memory term.
The role of
\omega
is most peculiar: when
\omega>0
, the presence
of the instantaneous Laplacian of the temperature has a regularizing effect, introducing parabolicity in the model.
On the contrary, the equation for
\omega=0
is purely hyperbolic (see
[
11
,
14
,
21
]
).
In fact, this regularizing feature will occur in all the heat models considered hereafter, whenever
\omega>0
.
Finally, let us observe that
in the formal limiting case where
\kappa
equals the Dirac mass at zero,
or when
\omega=1
,
we recover (
2.2
). From the physical viewpoint, this means that
(
2.6
) is close to (
2.2
) when the memory kernel is concentrated, that is,
when the system keeps a very short memory of past effects.

Remark 2.1
.

Choosing
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)_{|t\leq 0}=0
, that is, null initial past history of the temperature,
one obtains the Volterra version of (
2.6
)

\partial_{t}u-\omega\kappa\Delta u-(1-\omega)\kappa\,\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\Delta u(t-s)ds=0.

2.4.
The exponential kernel

We now consider the heat law (
2.5
) for the
exponential kernel

(2.7)

g(s)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,e^{-\frac{s}{\varepsilon}},\quad\varepsilon>0.

Then, observing that

(2.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\nabla u(t-s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\,\bigg(e^{-\frac{t}{\varepsilon}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{\frac{s}{\varepsilon}}\nabla u(s)ds\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla u(t)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\nabla u(t-s)ds,

taking the sum
\eqref{GPCG}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\eqref{GPCG}

we end up with

(2.9)

\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u-\varepsilon\omega\kappa\nabla\partial_{t}u,

which contains the Maxwell-Cattaneo law (
2.3
) as a particular instance,
corresponding to
\omega=0
.
In particular, this tells that
a differential perturbation of the Fourier law
is just a memory relaxation with the exponential kernel.
Again, by
\eqref{BASE}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\eqref{BASE}
, we
obtain the evolution equation for the temperature, that now reads

(2.10)

\varepsilon\partial_{tt}u+\partial_{t}u-\varepsilon\omega\kappa\Delta\partial_{t}u-\kappa\Delta u=0.

Clearly, when
\omega=0
this is the weakly damped wave equation (
2.4
).
On the other hand, when
\omega>0
we have the regularizing extra term

-\Delta\partial_{t}u
, which renders the equation no longer purely hyperbolic.
As a matter of fact, we can also
allow the limiting situation
\varepsilon=0
, corresponding to
the Dirac mass at zero. In which case, we boil down to the Fourier law,
and (
2.10
) becomes the heat equation (
2.2
).

Remark 2.2
.

Apparently, for the limit value
\omega=1
, this procedure might look flawed. Indeed,
from the one side if
\omega=1
the starting law (
2.5
) is just
the Fourier law (
2.1
). From the other side the procedure
above leads to (
2.9
) with
\omega=1
, namely,

\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u-\varepsilon\kappa\nabla\partial_{t}u.

But, calling

\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{q}+\kappa\nabla u,

the latter equation can be written as

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\phi}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\phi}=0,

implying in turn that

\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)=\boldsymbol{\phi}(0)e^{-\frac{t}{\varepsilon}}.

Note that this is possible just because
\omega=1
. At this point,
all depends on the initial value
\boldsymbol{\phi}(0)
which must be necessarily read
from the original equation (
2.5
), clearly with
\omega=1
.
This yields
\boldsymbol{\phi}(0)=0
, hence
\boldsymbol{\phi}=0
for all times,
recovering the Fourier law.
This is the reason why we always take
\omega\in[0,1)
.

2.5.
Heat conduction of type III

The theory of heat conduction of type III goes back to the works of
Green and Naghdi
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, and it is based on the introduction of a further variable

u_{1}=u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R},

called
thermal displacement
, defined (up to a constant in time function)
by the relation

\partial_{t}u_{1}=u.

The Fourier law (
2.1
)
is modified as

(2.11)

\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa\nabla u-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1},

where the constant
\kappa_{1}>0
is the

conductivity rate
.
Since

u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},0)+\int_{0}^{t}u(\boldsymbol{x},s)ds,

the above can be viewed as an integral perturbation
of (
2.1
), which collapses into (
2.1
) in the limit case
\kappa_{1}=0
. From
the energy balance (
1.1
), we deduce the evolution equation for the variable
u_{1}

(2.12)

\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\kappa\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}=0.

Equation (
2.12
), known as the strongly damped wave equation,
has applications in several fields. In particular, it serves as a model of viscoelasticity,
more precisely, in the language of Dautray and Lions
[
5
]
,
of viscoelasticity with
short memory
.
In that context, it goes by the name of
Kelvin-Voigt equation.

3.
Heat Law of Order 1

The idea now is to consider the modifications (
2.5
) and (
2.11
)
of the Fourier law at the same time. This leads to the
introduction of the following heat law, that within the language of the next section we might call

of order
0
with memory
:

(3.1)

\boldsymbol{q}=-\omega\kappa\nabla u-(1-\omega)\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\nabla u(t-s)ds-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}=0.

From the energy balance (
1.1
),
and recalling that
u=\partial_{t}u_{1}
,
we find the integro-differential equation in the variable
u_{1}

(3.2)

\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\omega\kappa\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-(1-\omega)\kappa\,\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}(t-s)ds-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}=0.

Equation (
3.2
)
for
\omega=0
is the renowned equation of viscoelasticity
with
long memory

[
5
]
, which is
purely hyperbolic, contrary to the its short memory counterpart, formally obtained
when
g
is the Dirac mass at zero.
This equation has been first analyzed in a systematic way by Dafermos
[
4
]
,
and studied by quite many authors thereafter. Instead,
when
\omega>0
we have the strongly
damped wave equation with memory, where one can appreciate the regularization provided by the
term
-\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}
, that confers the equation a partially parabolic character
(see
[
10
]
).

Remark 3.1
.

Actually, introducing the differentiated memory kernel

\mu(s)=-\kappa g^{\prime}(s),

by means of an integration by parts
the equation of viscoelasticity can be given the more familiar form

\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(s)\big[\Delta u_{1}(t)-\Delta u_{1}(t-s)\big]ds=0.

Indeed, the boundary terms of the integration by parts vanish within
reasonable regularity hypotheses on the solution (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
).

The next step is repeating the scheme devised
in Subsection (
2.4
), by choosing the memory kernel
g

of the form (
2.7
).
Accordingly, by taking the sum
\eqref{0mem}+\varepsilon\partial_{t}\eqref{0mem}

and exploiting (
2.8
),
we arrive at the heat law
of order

1

(3.3)

\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{q}_{t}=-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}-(\kappa+\varepsilon\kappa_{1})\nabla\partial_{t}u_{1}-\varepsilon\omega\kappa\nabla\partial^{2}_{t}u_{1},

written in terms of the variable
u_{1}
solely.
In turn, the energy balance (
1.1
) produces the third-order
evolution equation

(3.4)

\varepsilon\partial_{ttt}u_{1}+\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\varepsilon\omega\kappa\Delta\partial^{2}_{t}u_{1}-(\kappa+\varepsilon\kappa_{1})\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}=0.

When
\omega=0
, this is the Moore-Gibson-Thompson (MGT) equation,
arising in the modeling of wave propagation
in viscous thermally relaxing fluids
[
25
,
30
]
, whose
first derivation is due to Stokes
[
28
]
.
The case
\omega>0
yields a partially parabolic
regularization of the (fully hyperbolic) MGT equation.

Remark 3.2
.

As in Subsection (
2.4
), we could also allow
\varepsilon
to be zero,
corresponding to a kernel
g
collapsing into the Dirac mass at zero.
Then (
3.3
) reduces to the law (
2.11
) of heat conduction of type III.
Clearly, if also
\kappa_{1}=0
, we recover the Fourier law expressed in terms
of
\partial_{t}u_{1}
, which is nothing but
u
.

On the MGT equation

Before going any further, let us dwell for a while on the MGT equation,
which in the literature is usually written in the form
(here in the variable
v
)

(3.5)

\partial_{ttt}v+a\partial_{tt}v-b\Delta\partial_{t}v-c\Delta v=0,

where
a,b,c
are generic positive constants.
Provided that
\Omega
has a sufficiently regular boundary,
assuming the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition for
v
,
equation (
3.5
) is well known
to generate a linear
solution semigroup
S(t)

on the natural weak energy space

{\mathcal{H}}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega).

Here, with standard notation,
L^{2}
is the Lebesgue space of square-summable functions,
whereas
H_{0}^{1}
is the Sobolev space of functions which are
square-summable along with their
first derivatives and vanish on the boundary of
\Omega
.
Notably, the asymptotic properties of
S(t)
depend on a combination
of the physical parameters. Indeed, defining the
so-called stability number

\varkappa=b-\frac{c}{a},

it turns out that the semigroup is exponentially stable if and only if

\varkappa>0
(subcritical regime), whereas in the supercritical regime

\varkappa<0
there are trajectories whose energy blows up exponentially fast
[
23
,
24
]
.
This fact somehow indicates
that the MGT equation is physically meaningful only when
\varkappa>0
.
If we want to write our equation (
3.4
) in the form (
3.5
) for
arbitrary coefficient
a,b,c>0
, we have to choose the constants as follows:

\omega=0,\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{a},\qquad\kappa_{1}=\frac{c}{a},\qquad\kappa=\frac{\varkappa}{a}.

Since
\kappa
must be positive, it is apparent that such a procedure allows us to recover
the MGT equation (
3.5
)
only
in the subcritical case. And this actually makes
perfectly sense, as what we produced is an equation describing the evolution of the temperature, whose semigroup is supposed
to exhibit a fast decay in absence of heat sources.
This pattern has been already noted in
[
8
]
, where the MGT equation is viewed
as a particular instance of the equation of viscoelasticity.

4.
Heat Laws of Order
n

Renaming the relative temperature
u
by
u_{0}
, for
n\geq 1
we introduce the sequence of functions
u_{n}
,
each one being the antiderivative of
u_{n-1}
, hence satisfying the relations

\partial_{t}u_{n}=u_{n-1}.

Our purpose, as mentioned in the Introduction, is to generate a hierarchy of heat laws
involving the function
u_{n}
, for every fixed
n
.
To this end,
let us first rename the constants
\varepsilon
,
\omega
and
\kappa
appearing
in (
3.3
) by
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\omega_{1}
and
\kappa_{0}
, respectively.
Accordingly, the law of order 1 becomes

(4.1)

\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon_{1}\boldsymbol{q}_{t}=-\kappa_{1}\nabla u_{1}-(\varepsilon_{1}\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{0})\nabla\partial_{t}u_{1}-\varepsilon_{1}\omega_{1}\kappa_{0}\nabla\partial^{2}_{t}u_{1}.

We also assume that, for every
n\geq 1
, we are given the sequences

\varepsilon_{n}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega_{n}\in[0,1),

and, for every
n\geq 0
,

\kappa_{n}>0.

Actually, we can allow
\kappa_{m}=0
for some
m\geq 1
, but in that case
the sequences
\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{n},\kappa_{n}
are finite and they stop at
n=m
.

Then, for every
n\geq 1

(and whenever
\kappa_{n-1}>0
),
we define the heat law
of order

n
by

(4.2)

\boldsymbol{q}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i}\boldsymbol{q}=-\kappa_{n}\nabla u_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\beta_{n}^{i}\nabla\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n}.

For
n=1
, in compliance with (
4.1
),
the (nonnegative) coefficients appearing in the formula above are

\alpha_{1}^{1}=\varepsilon_{1},\qquad\beta_{1}^{1}=\varepsilon_{1}\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{0},\qquad\beta_{1}^{2}=\varepsilon_{1}\omega_{1}\kappa_{0}.

Instead, for
n\geq 2
they are determined by the recurrence relations

(4.3)

\alpha_{n}^{i}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{n}+\alpha_{n-1}^{1}&\text{ if }i=1,\\
\varepsilon_{n}\alpha_{n-1}^{i-1}+\alpha_{n-1}^{i}&\text{ if }n\geq 3\text{ and }i=2,\ldots,n-1,\\
\varepsilon_{n}\alpha_{n-1}^{n-1}&\text{ if }i=n,\end{cases}

and

(4.4)

\beta_{n}^{i}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{n}\kappa_{n}+\kappa_{n-1}&\text{ if }i=1,\\
\varepsilon_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n-1}+\beta_{n-1}^{1}&\text{ if }i=2,\\
\varepsilon_{n}\beta_{n-1}^{i-2}+\beta_{n-1}^{i-1}&\text{ if }i=3,\ldots,2n-1,\\
\varepsilon_{n}\beta_{n-1}^{2n-2}&\text{ if }i=2n.\end{cases}

Remark 4.1
.

The coefficients
\alpha_{n}^{i}
have the explicit form

\alpha_{n}^{i}=\frac{1}{i!}\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i}=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{k_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{k_{i}}\delta_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i}},

where

\delta_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{i}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $k_{1},\ldots,k_{i}$ are distinct integers},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

is the generalized delta Kronecker.
The relevant information provided by this formula is that if
\alpha_{n}^{i}=0
, then at least

n-i+1
among the constants
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}

vanish.
Accordingly,
if
\alpha_{n}^{i}=0
for some
i
, then
\alpha_{n}^{k}=0
for all
k\geq i
.

Remark 4.2
.

Concerning the coefficients
\beta_{n}^{i}
, one can check the following facts:

(i)

\beta_{n}^{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(ii)

\beta_{n}^{2n}>0
if and only if
\omega_{1}>0
and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}>0
.

(iii)

Let
k=0,\ldots,n-1
be fixed, and assume that
\alpha_{n}^{n-k}=0
. In particular,
Remark
4.1
implies that
\alpha_{n}^{n}=\alpha_{n}^{n-1}=\ldots=\alpha_{n}^{n-k}=0
.
Then, via (actually nontrivial) combinatorial arguments, it follows that

\beta_{n}^{2n}=\ldots\beta_{n}^{2n-k}=0.

At this point, we distinguish two cases.

\bullet
If
\kappa_{n}=0
, we rewrite (
4.2
) in terms of the variable
u_{n-1}
,
so obtaining

\boldsymbol{q}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i}\boldsymbol{q}=-\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1}\beta_{n}^{i+1}\nabla\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n-1}.

Hence, taking the sum
\eqref{BASE}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i}\eqref{BASE}
,
we find the evolution equation in the variable
u_{n-1}

(4.5)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i+n}u_{n-1}+\partial_{t}^{n}u_{n-1}-\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1}\beta_{n}^{i+1}\Delta\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n-1}=0.

In particular, the last term
\beta_{n}^{2n}\nabla\partial_{t}^{2n-1}u_{n-1}
,
if present, is responsible for a regularizing effect. On the contrary, the character of
(
4.5
) is purely hyperbolic when all the coefficients
\alpha_{n}^{i}
differ from zero and

\beta_{n}^{2n}=0
.
As a particular case of (
4.5
), we like to mention the case
n=2
with
\beta_{2}^{4}=0
(i.e., in absence of regularization), and all the other coefficients strictly positive, namely,

\alpha_{2}^{2}\partial_{tttt}u_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{1}\partial_{ttt}u_{1}+\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\beta_{2}^{3}\Delta\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\beta_{2}^{2}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-\beta_{2}^{1}\Delta u_{1}=0.

This is a fourth-order equation of
MGT type which has been extensively studied in
[
9
]
.

\bullet
If
\kappa_{n}\neq 0
,
we find the evolution equation in the variable
u_{n}

(4.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i+n+1}u_{n}+\partial_{t}^{n+1}u_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\beta_{n}^{i}\Delta\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n}-\kappa_{n}\Delta u_{n}=0.

Again,
-\beta_{n}^{2n}\Delta\partial_{t}^{2n}u_{n}
is a regularizing term.

5.
Heat Laws of Order
n
with Memory

When
\kappa_{n}\neq 0
, we are in a position to make a memory relaxation of the law (
4.2
),
along with a perturbation obtained by adding (minus) the gradient of the antiderivative
u_{n+1}

of
u_{n}
. Hence, given a bounded convex summable function
g_{n+1}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
of unitary total mass,
we consider the heat law
of order
n
with memory

(5.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{n+1}\kappa_{n}\nabla u_{n}-(1-\omega_{n+1})\kappa_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n+1}(s)\nabla u_{n}(t-s)ds

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\beta_{n}^{i}\nabla\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n}-\kappa_{n+1}\nabla u_{n+1},

where
\kappa_{n+1}\geq 0
and
\omega_{n+1}\in[0,1)
.
This heat law, by applying the energy balance (
1.1
), yields the memory counterparts
of (
4.6
), exhibiting a nonlocal character in time due to the presence of the convolution integral.
Again, we shall distinguish two cases.

\bullet
If
\kappa_{n+1}=0
, we deduce
the equation in the variable
u_{n}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i+n+1}u_{n}+\partial_{t}^{n+1}u_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\beta_{n}^{i}\Delta\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n}

\displaystyle\quad-\omega_{n+1}\kappa_{n}\Delta u_{n}-(1-\omega_{n+1})\kappa_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n+1}(s)\Delta u_{n}(t-s)ds=0.

As a particular instance of (
5.2
) we consider the case

n=1
with
\beta_{1}^{2}=0
(i.e., in absence of regularization), and all the other coefficients strictly positive, namely,

(5.3)

\alpha_{1}^{1}\partial_{ttt}u_{1}+\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\beta_{1}^{1}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-\omega_{2}\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}-(1-\omega_{2})\kappa_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{2}(s)\Delta u_{1}(t-s)ds=0.

Introducing the integrated kernel

G(s)=(1-\omega_{2})\kappa_{1}\int_{s}^{\infty}g_{2}(y)dy,

equation (
5.3
) becomes

\alpha_{1}^{1}\partial_{ttt}u_{1}+\partial_{tt}u_{1}-\beta_{1}^{1}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}-\kappa_{1}\Delta u_{1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}G(s)\Delta\partial_{t}u_{1}(t-s)ds=0,

known in the literature as
the MGT equation with memory of type II (see
[
7
,
22
]
).

Remark 5.1
.

As a matter of fact, we could also allow
\omega_{2}
to take
any
positive
value, except the forbidden value
\omega_{2}=1
, in compliance with Remark
2.2
.
In particular, for
\omega_{2}>1
, equation (
5.3
)
becomes the MGT equation with memory of type I (see
[
6
,
22
]
).

\bullet
If
\kappa_{n+1}\neq 0
, we write (
5.1
)
in terms of
u_{n+1}
only, and we deduce the equation

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\partial_{t}^{i+n+2}u_{n+1}+\partial_{t}^{n+2}u_{n+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\beta_{n}^{i}\Delta\partial_{t}^{i+1}u_{n+1}-\omega_{n+1}\kappa_{n}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\quad-(1-\omega_{n+1})\kappa_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n+1}(s)\Delta\partial_{t}u_{n+1}(t-s)ds-\kappa_{n+1}\Delta u_{n+1}=0.

For
n=1
and
\alpha_{1}^{1}=0
, hence
\beta_{1}^{2}=0
, equation (
5.4
) reads

\partial_{ttt}u_{2}-\beta_{1}^{1}\Delta\partial_{tt}u_{2}-\omega_{2}\kappa_{1}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{2}-(1-\omega_{2})\kappa_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{2}(s)\Delta\partial_{t}u_{2}(t-s)ds-\kappa_{2}\Delta u_{2}=0,

which, defining the differentiated kernel

\mu(s)=-(1-\omega_{2})\kappa_{1}g_{2}^{\prime}(s),

can be written in the form

\partial_{ttt}u_{2}-\beta_{1}^{1}\Delta\partial_{tt}u_{2}-\omega_{2}\kappa_{1}\Delta\partial_{t}u_{2}-\varrho\Delta u_{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu(s)\Delta u_{2}(t-s)ds,

with

\varrho=\kappa_{2}+(1-\omega_{2})\kappa_{1}g_{2}(0)
.
The latter is a regularized version of the
MGT equation with memory of type I.

6.
The Inductive Procedure

The last step is to justify the hierarchy of heat laws of order
n
and of order
n

with memory presented in the previous sections. This will be done by means of an inductive argument, starting from
the law of order
1
, whose derivation in turn starts from the Fourier law,
which in this scheme might be seen as the law of order
0
.
Accordingly, let us assume we are given for some
n\geq 1
the law of order
n
with memory (
5.1
).
We now choose the kernel
g_{n+1}
of the form

g(s)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n+1}}\,e^{-\frac{s}{\varepsilon_{n+1}}},

where the value
\varepsilon_{n+1}=0
is allowed, and in that case
g_{n+1}
becomes the Dirac mass
at zero.
At this point, exploiting (
2.8
),
we take the time derivative of (
5.1
),
written in terms of
u_{n+1}
only, and we multiply it by

\varepsilon_{n+1}
.
This gives, changing the summation indexes,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n+1}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}+\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}\varepsilon_{n+1}\alpha_{n}^{i-1}\partial_{t}^{i}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon_{n+1}\omega_{n+1}\kappa_{n}\nabla\partial_{tt}u_{n+1}-(1-\omega_{n+1})\kappa_{n}\nabla\partial_{t}u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\omega_{n+1})\kappa_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n+1}(s)\nabla\partial_{t}u_{n+1}(t-s)ds

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=3}^{2n+2}\varepsilon_{n+1}\beta_{n}^{i-2}\nabla\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n+1}-\varepsilon_{n+1}\kappa_{n+1}\nabla\partial_{t}u_{n+1}.

Adding the latter equation with (
5.1
) written
in terms of
u_{n+1}
, we finally get
(where the first sum disappears when
n=1
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}+(\varepsilon_{n+1}+\alpha_{n}^{1})\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\varepsilon_{n+1}\alpha_{n}^{i-1}+\alpha_{n}^{i})\partial_{t}^{i}\boldsymbol{q}+\varepsilon_{n+1}\alpha_{n}^{n}\partial_{t}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{n+1}\nabla u_{n+1}-(\varepsilon_{n+1}\kappa_{n+1}+\kappa_{n})\nabla\partial_{t}u_{n+1}-(\varepsilon_{n+1}\omega_{n+1}\kappa_{n}+\beta_{n}^{1})\nabla\partial_{tt}u_{n+1}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=3}^{2n+1}(\varepsilon_{n+1}\beta_{n}^{i-2}+\beta_{n}^{i-1})\nabla\partial_{t}^{i}u_{n+1}-\varepsilon_{n+1}\beta_{n}^{2n}\nabla\partial_{t}^{2n+2}u_{n+1}.

But, in the light of (
4.3
)-(
4.4
),
this is exactly the law (
4.2
) written for
n+1
, that is,
the heat law of order
n+1
.
This completes the argument.

7.
Ten Equations

In this last section we produce a list of ten notable
evolution equations, all written in the generic variable
v
, that can be obtained from
the family of constitutive laws for the heat flux introduced in this work.
In what follows,
a,b,c,d,e
will stand for strictly positive constants, whereas

g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
will be a bounded convex summable function, not necessarily of unitary total mass.

(i)

The heat equation:

\partial_{t}v-a\Delta v=0.

(ii)

The Gurtin-Pipkin heat equation:

\partial_{t}v-\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta v(t-s)ds=0.

(iii)

The Coleman-Gurtin heat equation:

\partial_{t}v-a\Delta v-\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta v(t-s)ds=0.

(iv)

The weakly damped wave equation:

\partial_{tt}v+a\partial_{t}v-b\Delta v=0.

(v)

The strongly damped wave equation:

\partial_{tt}v-a\Delta\partial_{t}v-b\Delta v=0.

(vi)

The Moore-Gibson-Thompson (MGT) equation:

\partial_{ttt}v+a\partial_{tt}v-b\Delta\partial_{t}v-c\Delta v=0.

(vii)

The regularized MGT equation:

\partial_{ttt}v+a\partial_{tt}v-b\Delta\partial_{tt}v-c\Delta\partial_{t}v-d\Delta v=0.

(viii)

The MGT equation with memory of type I:

\partial_{ttt}v+a\partial_{tt}v-b\Delta\partial_{t}v-c\Delta v+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta v(t-s)ds=0.

(ix)

The MGT equation with memory of type II:

\partial_{ttt}v+a\partial_{tt}v-b\Delta\partial_{t}v-c\Delta v+\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\Delta\partial_{t}v(t-s)ds=0.

(x)

A fourth order equation of MGT type:

\partial_{tttt}v+a\partial_{ttt}v+b\partial_{tt}v-c\Delta\partial_{tt}v-d\Delta\partial_{t}v-e\Delta v=0.
