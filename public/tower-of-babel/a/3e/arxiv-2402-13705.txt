Hyperuniformity and optimal transport of point processes
Raphaël Lachièze-Rey, D. Yogeshwaran

Abstract
We examine optimal matchings or transport between two stationary random measures. It covers allocation from the Lebesgue measure to a point process and matching a point process to a regular (shifted) lattice. The main focus of the article is the impact of hyperuniformity (reduced variance fluctuations in point processes) to optimal transport: in dimension 2 2 , we show that the typical matching cost has finite second moment under a mild logarithmic integrability condition on the reduced pair correlation measure, showing that most planar hyperuniform point processes are L 2 L^{2} -perturbed lattices. Our method also retrieves known sharp bounds in finite windows for neutral integrable systems such as Poisson processes, and also applies to hyperfluctuating systems. Further, in three dimension onwards, all point processes with an integrable pair correlation measure are L 2 L^{2} -perturbed lattices without requiring hyperuniformity.



Keywords:
Hyperuniformity, optimal transport, matching, structure factor, scattering intensity, random measures.

60G55, 49Q22 ; 60D05, 60B15, 42A38.

Introduction

The celebrated AKT (Ajtai, Komlós and Tusnády) theorem
[
1
]
states that, if one draws
2n
i.i.d. uniform points
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
in a two-dimensional cube with volume
n
, it is possible to pair each
X_{i}
with an exclusive
Y_{\sigma(i)}
for some permutation
\sigma
such that the typical length
|X_{1}-Y_{\sigma(1)}|
is of the order
\sqrt{\ln(n)}
with high probability, and it is the best possible rate. The role of the dimension
2
is very important, as for the same statement in dimension
1
, the typical distance is of order
\sqrt{n}
, and in dimension
d\geqslant 3
it is of order
1.
This result has known many refinements and generalisations such as replacing the total distance by weighted versions
\sum|X_{i}-Y_{\sigma(i)}|^{p},p>0
, drawing points with a non-uniform law on the cube, giving large deviations
[
23
]
, computing exact asymptotic constants
[
4
,
31
]
etc. Using the framework of optimal transport, these asymptotics are equivalent to the cost of optimal transport between the measure
\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
and the measure
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
on the cube. We refer the reader to
[
52
,
56
,
43
]
for surveys of such results and for a sampler of other recent developments, see
[
6
,
13
,
26
]
.

One may also study an
infinite volume
version of the AKT theorem by finding a ‘good’ matching between infinite samples
\mu=\sum_{i}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\nu=\sum_{j}\delta_{Y_{j}}
, i.e. with minimal cost per unit volume, where
\mu
and
\nu
are independent unit intensity homogeneous Poisson processes. One may also consider the case of
\nu
being lattice measures, or the Lebesgue measure in which case, matchings are replaced by transport maps. It is shown in
[
30
]
that in the Poisson case there is a (randomized) bijection
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to{\rm supp}(\mu)
with the typical distance
X=\|T(0)\|
, such that

\mathbf{E}\left(\frac{X^{d/2}}{1+|\ln(X)|^{\gamma}}\right)<\infty\textrm{ for $d=1,2$, any $\gamma>1$ and }\mathbf{E}(X^{2})<\infty\textrm{ for $d\geqslant 3$},

(1)

and
\mathbf{E}(X^{d/2})=\infty
in
d=1,2
i.e., the bounds are sharp in these dimensions; see Remark
1
. In higher dimensions, even finite exponential moments are proven to exist but we shall focus only on finite second moments; see
[
28
,
30
,
34
,
29
]
.

The starting point of this article is to replace
\mu
and/or
\nu
by arbitrary stationary random point processes and consider matchings between
\mu
and
\nu
and equivalently transport map or plan between the Lebesgue measure and
\mu
. In specific planar cases such as zeros of Gaussian entire functions (GEF) or Ginibre point process, existence of good matchings have been shown.
In particular,
[
53
]
shows exponential moments for the matching cost between zeros of GEF and lattice and
[
35
,
49
]
prove respectively finite first and second moments for the transport of Ginibre ensemble to the Lebesgue measure.
Both these point processes are known to be hyperuniform (also known as
super-homogeneous
) i.e., the asymptotic number variance (i.e. variance of the number of points) on a large window grows more slowly than volume order, unlike the Poisson point process
[
58
,
24
]
. Hyperuniform (HU) processes appear in various contexts such as statistical physics, biology, material sciences, random matrices, dynamical systems, numerical integration
[
57
,
20
]
. A concise description coined by physicists about HU processes is “global order and local disorder”, meaning that even though the particles are locally disordered, exhibit isotropy and asymptotic independence, they should behave at large scales as lattices, and should in some sense be arranged in a nice orderly manner. A natural question is therefore whether such processes are well distributed in space, and we believe that this should be reflected by their transport properties, in the sense that a nicely spread point sample should be easy to transport to the Lebesgue measure, or equivalently to a regular lattice. Notable examples of hyperuniform point processes are constructed as a good matching to a lattice; see
[
48
,
39
,
44
]
.

We see from (
1
) that this property (existence of a matching with finite second moment for typical cost) is true in dimension
d\geqslant 3
for Poisson processes, and we will see that it extends to other processes, whether HU or not, as long as they are not hyperfluctuating. In dimension
2
, we answer the question on existence of ’good’ matchings for HU processes in the affirmative. In other words, we show that for a HU process
\mu
in dimension
d=2
, identifying
\mu
with its support, under mild logarithmic integrability conditions on the reduced pair correlation function, there is a
L^{2}
process
\{T(k);k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
invariant under
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
translations such that
\mu=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\delta_{k+T(k)}
, meaning it is a
L^{2}
perturbed lattice. Many known HU processes satisfy this condition, including the Ginibre point process and zeros of Gaussian entire function. An example of a HU process with infinite
L^{1}
-matching has been provided in
[
32
, Theorem 2(2)]
indicating the necessity of some additional integrability condition but whether logarithmic integrability is required or not is moot.
Our interest in
L^{2}
perturbed lattices is also in part due to the following recent results on perturbed lattices due to
[
21
]
. Namely it was shown that
L^{2}
perturbed lattices are HU but for any
\delta>0
, there exist
L^{2-\delta}
perturbed lattices which are not hyperuniform.

En route to our result for HU processes, we generalize the above bounds (
1
) for Poisson processes to stationary random point processes with integrable reduced pair correlation function (RPCM). This is to say that in magnitude, the matching bounds for point processes with number variance growing at most of volume-order cannot be worser than that for the Poisson process. Our proofs proceed by bounding the transport or matching cost on large finite windows via the bounds in
[
13
]
which are using the expected squared Fourier-transform of the empirical measure of
\mu
. These are strongly related to the
scattering intensity,
which is known to converge to the Fourier transform of the RPCM, also called
the structure factor
. These bounds work well for first and second moments but not higher moments. Vanishing of the structure factor at the origin is equivalent to hyperuniformity and quantifying the rate of vanishing on large windows and for small frequencies are the key estimates needed for our proof. Then we transfer these bounds to the infinite sample exploiting tightness and that the finite point processes (i.e., point process on finite windows) converge in law to the infinite sample in the vague topology. This is in contrast to other proofs which use Whitney-type decomposition with error terms estimated via potential theory or PDE methods. Also the other works that derive bounds using the Fourier-analytic method, exploit ’weak independence’ or ’mixing’ of point processes to derive Poisson-like bounds but these do not exploit the vanishing of the structure factor. Our bounds in the finite sample case are explicit and hold for all point processes without assumption of integrable reduced pair correlations. Also in the infinite case, we give explicit bounds for matching when the logarithmic integrability condition is not satisfied by the hyperuniform point processes. Independently,
[
17
]
have obtained
p
-Wasserstein transport rates of HU processes on large windows under other additional assumptions on moments and concentration bounds.

[
32
]
also recently obtain that HU processes are
L^{2}
perturbed lattices, assuming finite Coulomb energy, another form of strong hyperuniformity.
We give a more detailed comparison with the literature after Theorem
2
.

Plan of the paper:
In the next subsection, we introduce some preliminaries on point processes. We state our main results and illustrate with examples in Section

1.1
Preliminaries

We shall briefly introduce the required point process notions here and for more details, refer to
[
8
,
37
,
41
,
5
]
and for the purposes of this article,
[
20
]
also suffices.

A
random measure
is a random element taking values in the space of locally-finite (or Radon) measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the evaluation
\sigma
-algebra. A
point process
is a random measure taking values in the space
\mathscr{N}
of locally-finite (or Radon) counting measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped again with the evaluation
\sigma
-algebra. It is
simple
if a.s.,
\mu(\{x\})\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and stationary or translation invariant if
\mu+x\overset{d}{=}\mu
where
(\mu+x)(\cdot):=\mu(\cdot-x)
is the shifted measure. The set notation is sometimes abusively applied to a simple point process by assimilating it to its support. Common examples of stationary point process are the shifted lattice
\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\delta_{z+U}
for
U
uniform in
[0,1]^{d}
, the homogeneous Poisson process, the infinite Ginibre ensemble, and zeros of Gaussian entire functions; see Section
2.2
or
[
20
, Section 3]
.

Given a stationary point process, its first intensity measure
\mathbf{E}(\mu(\cdot))
is proportional to the Lebesgue measure, and we shall assume that it is indeed the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}.
Equivalently,
we assume that all our point processes have unit intensity
i.e.,
\mathbf{E}(\mu(\cdot))=\mathcal{L}(\cdot).

When
\mu,\nu
are simple point processes, we abusively also denote by
\mu,\nu
their supports, and we call
matching
between
\mu,\nu
a random one-to-one map
T:\nu\to\mu
. If
\nu=\mathcal{L}
, an
allocation scheme
is a mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mu
that is also a transport map, meaning that a.s.,
\mathcal{L}(T^{-1}(A))=\nu(A)
for any
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Whether we talk of matching or allocation scheme, we also require that
T
is
invariant
(under translations), i.e. the mass transported from
A
to
B
measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies for a shift
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\nu(T^{-1}(A+x)\cap(B+x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\nu(T^{-1}(A)\cap B).

Without the simplicity assumption, such invariant mappings might not exist, which is why simplicity is always assumed in results about matching. We remark that there always exist a (non-randomized) translation invariant allocation scheme
T
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a simple point process
\mu
with unit intensity, see Theorem 4 of
[
28
]
, where such an allocation is built with the Gale-Shapley algorithm on stable marriage (this particular procedure might be sub-optimal). The question is then to find a (different)
T
such that
\|T(0)\|
has the smallest possible tail, or the largest possible cost function
w
such that for some
T,

\mathbf{E}(w(\|T(0)\|))<\infty
.

All the matchings or transport maps we consider are
randomized
matchings i.e., could depend on additional sources of randomness apart from
\mu,\nu
. These concepts descend from the more general concept of
coupling
between
\mu
and
\nu
in optimal transport, see Sections
2.1
and
3.3
.
Given two possibly dependent stationary point processes
\mu,\nu
, Holroyd et al.
[
30
]
say that there is a matching between them with distribution function
F
if for some randomized invariant matching
T
between them

\displaystyle F(r)

\displaystyle:=(2\pi)^{-d}\,\mathbf{E}\mathbf{1}(\|T(x)-x\|\leqslant r)\,,

where
\Lambda_{1}=[-\pi,\pi)^{d}
.
To be slightly more general, we shall say that
there is an invariant coupling between
\mu
and
\nu
whose typical transport distance
X
has distribution function
F

if there exists on some probability space a random measure
M
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(M(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{d}),M(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot))
has the same law as
(\mu,\nu)
, and

\displaystyle F(r)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\,\mathbf{E}M(\{(x,y):x\in\Lambda_{1},\|x-y\|\leqslant r\}).

(2)

This coupling is said a matching if it can a.s. be represented by a one-to-one mapping
T
, i.e.
M=\sum_{x\in\mu}\delta_{(x,T(x))}
.
There is also a formulation of typical matching or transport distance in terms of Palm distribution of
\mu
or
\nu
but we shall avoid it is not used; see
[
30
, Section 2]
. Also when
\mu
is simple it is easy to see that
\mathbf{E}(w(X))<\infty
for some function
w:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, iff

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sum_{x\in\mu\cap\Lambda_{1}}w(\|x-T(x)\|)<\infty.

The LHS above can be defined as the cost of transport between
\mu,\nu
with respect to
w
; see
[
22
, (2.4)]
. In the case of
\nu=\mathcal{L}
or shifted lattice, the above definition yields
X
with distribution
F
as mentioned (
1
) at the beginning of the paragraph for translation-invariant
T
.

A key descriptor of (unit intensity) point processes is the reduced pair correlation measure (RPCM)
\beta
of
\mu
and as indicated by the name, it measures the asymptotic independence at the second order. Informally,

\displaystyle\beta(dx)=\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}x\in\mu\;|\;0\in\mu)-\mathbf{P}(dx\in\mu).

More formally, we assume implicitly that
\mu(A)
has finite second moment for any bounded set
A
whenever
\beta
is mentioned and in which case
\beta
is a signed measure characterised by the relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sum_{x\neq y\in\mu}\varphi(x)\psi(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\int\int\mathbf{1}(x\neq y)\varphi(x)\psi(y)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int\int\varphi(x)\psi(y)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y+\int\int\varphi(z)\psi(x+z)\mathrm{d}z\beta(\mathrm{d}x)

(3)

for non-negative compactly supported continuous measurable functions
\varphi,\psi
. For instance, if
\mu
is a homogeneous Poisson process, or if
\mu=\mathcal{L}
,
\beta=0.
Denote by
|\beta|
the total-variation of
\beta
, and say that
\beta
is integrable if
|\beta|(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty.
We remark that existence of second moments guarantees that
|\beta|
is locally integrable, see
[
38
, Section 2]
.

A stationary point process
\mu
is said to be
hyperuniform
(HU) if the
reduced variance
satisfies

\sigma_{\mu}(n):=\frac{\text{Var}(\mu(\Lambda_{n}))}{\mathcal{L}(\Lambda_{n})}\to 0,\,\,\textrm{as $n\to\infty$},

(4)

where
\Lambda_{n}=(-\pi n^{\frac{1}{d}},\pi n^{\frac{1}{d}}]^{d}
. Assuming integrability of RPCM
\beta
, it is known that the choice of window shapes is not relevant i.e., instead of
\Lambda_{n}
one can consider
n^{1/d}W
for any convex set
W
containing the origin in its interior;
[
38
,
57
]
.

The RPCM is also central in the expression of the
number variance

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\mu(A))=\mathcal{L}(A)+\int_{A}\int_{z+A}\,\beta(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}z,\,\,A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5)

Assuming integrability of RPCM
\beta
, if
\beta(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta(\Lambda_{n})=-1
, we have the variance reduction
\text{Var}(\mu(\Lambda_{n}))=o(\mathcal{L}(\Lambda_{n}))
and thus the process is HU. The statistical study of a stationary point process
\mu
in the Fourier domain is often realised through its
structure factor
defined via

\displaystyle S(k):=1+\int e^{-ik\cdot x}\beta(\mathrm{d}x),\,\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(6)

when it is well defined. This is the case if for instance the RPCM
\beta
is integrable. In this situation, HU can be characterized also as
S(0)=0
. Some of these implications hold even without assuming integrability of
\beta
; see
[
10
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Main results

In this section, we state our main results. Firstly, we state our global matching result (Theorem

The proofs of these results proceed via bounds for matching or transport cost between a point process and Lebesgue measure on large boxes. This is the content of Theorems

,

We begin by stating our global matching bound that shows that the infinite volume version of AKT bounds for the Poisson process as in (

Theorem 1

Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}w(X)<\infty

\displaystyle w(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+|\ln(x)|^{\gamma}}$ if $d=1\\
\frac{x}{1+|\ln(x)|^{\gamma}}$ if $d=2\\

for any

The bounds for

Thus, in the integrable RPCM case,

No effort is taken on bounding

Comparison with literature.

We now comment briefly on the differences of our approach with respect to other results on hyperuniform processes. Both in the specific examples such as
[
53
,
35
,
49
]
and the general results of
[
17
]
the authors use also strong moment assumptions on the point process. The proof of
[

]
proceeds by constructing a Whitney-type partition with respect to a modified metric and a potential-theoretic lemma; see also
[
54
]
.
[
35
]
also uses potential-theoretic estimates. Such potential-theoretic estimates work well for zeros of Gaussian entire functions or Ginibre ensemble.
[

,

rely upon sub-additivity with suitable Whitney-type decompostions and the crucial estimates of the error in sub-additive arguments are achieved using PDE or functional-analytic tools as for combinatorial optimization problems over i.i.d. points in

26

. Both the potential-theoretic or PDE approach involve estimating solutions of a certain distributional Poisson equation. Our approach stemming from
[

is very different to these and involves only estimates based on the pair correlation function of the point processes. While the proof strategy is relatively simpler and works in greater generality, our methods cannot give higher-moment estimates unlike in the above articles (when the assumptions are sufficiently strong). It is important to mention that
[

]
also recovers Theorem

by showing that a hyperuniform process for which the normalized variance (i.e., (

at a logarithmic speed, the Coulomb energy is finite, and the latter implies finite mean

in the deterministic setting which is existence of bounded Lipschitz matching, was recently proven in

under a similar logarithmic condition on discrepancy and also using Fourier-analytic methods.

2.1
Optimal transport of large samples

The best suited framework to explain our results and proofs is that of optimal transport. Given two measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with same (non-zero) mass, and
w:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
nondecreasing, define the
w
-transport cost as

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{w}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{M}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(\|x-y\|)M(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

where
M
is a coupling (or transport plan) between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e.
M
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with same mass as
\mu
and
\nu
such that

\displaystyle M(\cdot,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mu,\;M(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot)=\nu.

By
[
50
, Theorem 1.7]
, if
\mu,\nu
have same finite total mass and
c
is lower semi-continuous then the infimum is indeed reached by some
M
, called the
optimal transport plan
or
coupling
. Since we will work with continuous cost functions (modulus of continuity or
x^{2}
) existence of optimal coupling is always guaranteed for us. If furthermore
\mu,\nu
are supported on some cube
\Lambda
, let

be the toric distance on
\Lambda
, and the corresponding cost is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(\mu,\nu)=\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(\mu,\nu;\Lambda):=\inf_{M}\int_{\Lambda^{2}}w(d_{\Lambda}(x,y))M(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

If
\mu=\nu=0
(i.e., the null measure), by definition
\mathsf{C}_{w}(\mu,\nu)=\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(\mu,\nu)=0
. We take the toric distance as our main tool relies on Fourier decomposition on the torus, but in the large window asymptotics, transport properties for toric and Euclidean distance will coincide. We often drop the dependence on domain
\Lambda
when it is obvious.

A prominent case in optimal transport is the power cost function
w_{p}(t)=t^{p},p>0
, and the corresponding costs
\mathsf{W}_{p}^{p}:=\mathsf{C}_{w_{p}},\widetilde{\mathsf{W}}_{p}^{p}=\mathsf{\widetilde{C}_{w_{p}}}

the functionals

are distances, called

[
50
]
provides the necessary background, see also
[
22
]
for more on optimal transport of random measures.

Our standard assumption in Theorems

. However in our upcoming finite sample bounds, we shall explicitly quantify the dependence of matching or transport cost on the tail of

Define

Remark 2
.

Even if the RPCM is a favored tool in spatial statistics, the variance is a more wide-spread statistical indicator, and the two are closely related, especially for hyperuniform processes. We have in this case the variance for the number of points
N=\mu(\Lambda_{n})

\displaystyle\text{Var}(N)=-n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\min(1,n^{-1/d}\|x\|)\beta(\mathrm{d}x)

(see (
38

\text{Var}(N)\leqslant cn\varepsilon(n^{-1/d}))
for some

. If the reverse inequality (i.e.,
\text{Var}(N)\geqslant cn\varepsilon(n^{-1/d}))
) were to hold as one would expect in many cases, it would allow us to equivalently state our main theorems in terms of
\text{Var}(N)
instead of
\beta
. However, the possibility of oscillating
\beta
prevents us from doing so in the general case. Under the assumption that
\beta
has negative sign, we have indeed

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}(N)\asymp n\varepsilon(n^{-1/d}).

This assumption means that in some sense the

-point correlation function is repulsive. Some determinantal point processes, or more generally, the so-called repulsive point processes satisfy this assumption because their

-point correlation function is repulsive at all orders (and everywhere); see
[
11

2.2
Examples of planar HU processes

2.2.1
Determinantal processes

Determinantal points processes (DPPs), introduced in the context of quantum mechanics, have gained popularity as many classes of essential models in random matrix theory, statistical physics, combinatorics and others have proven to be determinantal, see
[
8
]
. In the Euclidean context, a simple point process
\mu
with unit intensity on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is determinantal with kernel

K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):=\det((K(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant k})

is the
k
point correlation function of
\mu
, i.e.
for any non-negative
\varphi:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(\sum_{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mu}^{\neq}\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\right)=\int\rho_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\dots\,\mathrm{d}x_{k},

where the sum runs over
k
-tuples of pairwise distinct points. Note that not all functions
K
give rise to a DPP and in particular we will require that
K
is Hermitian and positive definite; see
[
8
, Section 4.5]
for unicity and existence questions. A degenerate example is the unit intensity homogeneous Poisson process for which
\rho_{k}\equiv 1,
i.e.
K(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{x=y\}}.
A non-trivial and prominent example is the (unit intensity) infinite Ginibre ensemble on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\approx\mathbb{C}
, defined by

\displaystyle K(x,y)=e^{x\bar{y}-\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}};x,y\in\mathbb{C}.

One has

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(x,y)=K(x,x)K(y,y)-K(x,y)K(y,x)=1-\exp(-|x-y|^{2})=1-|K(0,x-y)|^{2},

and
\rho_{2}
only depends on the difference
x-y
, as it turns out the Ginibre ensemble is indeed stationary. It is at the crossroads of combinatorics, statistical physics, and random matrices, as it is also the planar Coulomb gas with inverse temperature
2
, and it also arises as the infinite limit of the process formed by the eigenvalues of a
n\times n
matrix with i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian entries (see
[
8
]
).

Using the explicit definition of
\rho_{2}
and (
3
), we obtain that for a stationary DPP,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x\beta(\mathrm{d}z)=(\rho_{2}(x,x+z)-1)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z=-|K(0,z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z.

As outlined in Remark
2
, the negativity of
\beta
means that the process is repulsive at the second order (one may prove that it is actually repulsive at any order). The hyperuniformity of a unit intensity stationary DPP
\mu
hence means that
\int|K(0,z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=1
. HU DPPs can also be characterised as those with kernel
K
defining a
L^{2}
operator whose spectrum is contained in
\{0,1\}

[
8
]
. We have the following corollary of Theorem
2
:

Theorem 5
.

A planar stationary determinantal process with kernel
K
is a
L^{2}
-perturbed lattice (i.e.,
\ \mathbf{E}(X^{2})<\infty
for typical matching distance
X
) if it is hyperuniform, i.e.

\displaystyle\int|K(0,z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=K(0,0)^{2},

and if

\displaystyle\int\ln(\|z\|)|K(0,z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z<\infty.

Furthermore, the bounds on the transport costs for restricted samples of Theorem
4
hold with
\alpha_{2}(n)=cn
for some constant
c
.

Similar bounds for the expected
\mathsf{W}_{1}
(resp.
\mathsf{W}_{2}
) cost for the finite Ginibre ensemble have been shown by
[
35
]
(resp.
[
49
]
).

2.2.2
Coulomb gases and other hyperuniform processes

Hyperuniform processes have been intensively studied in the physics literature, and classified into three classes in
[
57
]
, depending on whether the restricted number variance
\sigma(r)
satisfies
\sigma(r)\sim r^{-1}
(class I),
\sigma(r)\sim r^{-1}\ln(r)
(class II), or
\sigma(r)\sim r^{-\alpha},\alpha\in(0,1)
(class III). As far as we know, no process of interest seems to fall in the boundary class where
r^{-\alpha}\ll\sigma(r)\ll 1
, where the log-integrability condition might not hold, and the mere existence of such processes is not trivial. All three classes mean (at least) a polynomial decay of
\beta
, hence should in principle exhibit sufficient asymptotic independence to satisfy the logarithmic condition of Theorem
2
(1). Nevertheless, we are not aware of existing mathematical proofs of such statements.

An exception is the zero set
\mu
of the planar Gaussian entire function, for which the exact fast decay of the RPCM can be inferred from the explicit formulas of the second order correlation measure of
[
8
, Section 3]
. Thus combined with Theorem
2
, this would yield that such zero sets are
L^{2}
-perturbed lattices; it has actually already been shown in
[
47
]
that this is the case, and the authors gave a strong concentration bound on the typical matching distance with an explicit and elegant transport plan to the Lebesgue measure built from the gravitational flow generated by
\mu
. The stable matching of lattice (or some ’nice’ HU point processes) to a Poisson process (or more generally DPPs) of larger intensity produces a hyperuniform point process whose reduced pair correlation satisfies the log integrability condition; see
[
39
, Proposition 8.2]
and
[
44
]
. But in this case, the construction itself yields a good matching with the lattice.

A particular focus has been put in both the mathematical and physics litterature on 2D Coulomb gases, also called one component plasma (OCP) or Jellium, of which the Ginibre ensemble is a particular case at temperature
1/2
; see
[
57
,
42
,
51
]
.
Hyperuniformity of the 2D OCP has been proved by Leblé
[
42
]
after having been conjectured for a long time. More precisely he proved that
\sigma(r)\leqslant c\ln(r)^{-0.6}
, but physicists estimate that the second order asymptotic independance is actually much stronger, meaning that the discrepancy is minimal, i.e.
\sigma(r)\sim r^{-1}
, falling in the class I of hyperuniform processes, but it remains for now out of reach for a mathematical treatment.

\displaystyle S(k):=\lambda_{D}\,+\,\int e^{-ik\cdot x}\beta(\mathrm{d}x),\,\,k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

HU in the case of stationary random measures can be characterized as

and equivalently

\mu
as well (again adapting to the fact that

for a general random measure), one can again use Theorem

is a random measure, one cannot talk about matchings but rather transport plans or couplings. In other words, we have that for a random measure with integrable RPCM, there exists a coupling

for weight function

as in Theorem

, we have that in

, there exists a coupling

of

such that

\mathbf{E}\int_{\Lambda_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}M(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty,

Similarly, analogues of other claims in Theorem

also hold.

3.3
Local to global allocation

The following proposition formalizes and generalizes one of the steps in the proof of

Recall that for a measure

Proposition 11

Let

be independent stationary random measures with integrable RPCM and

. Then, there exists a translation invariant coupling

of
\mu
and

whose typical distance

2.

Assume that

for all

and some

are unit intensity simple independent point processes such that

for all

of
\mu
and

which is a.s. represented by a one-to-one mapping and whose typical distance

The second point is an infinite version of the following finite sample version, a consequence of the Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem (
[

).

Proposition 12
.

Let

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}}\delta_{x_{i}},\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}}

While Item 1 of Proposition

by a re-scaled and re-normalized lattice. The latter will not be a point process as point masses might slightly differ from

in the limit. Before proceeding with this proof, we need to recall some results on vague convergence from
[
37

. Given measures

, if

, the space of compactly supported (bounded)x continuous functions on

. Say that

-)

Lemma 13
.

1.

If a sequence of random stationary measures

satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathbf{E}M_{n}(A)<\infty

for all bounded

2.

Let

be finite (deterministic) atomic measures for

and also converging resp. vaguely to simple point processes

. Let

be a coupling of

that is induced by a transport map

, where

vaguely, then

is a matching induced by a transport map

.

Proof.

The first item is a consequence of
[
36
, Theorem 16.15]
. So we prove the second item alone here. First, for test functions

and similarly

, hence

is indeed a coupling between
\mu
and
{\nu}
, whose support is contained in
\mu\times{\nu}.
Since
\mu
(resp.
\nu
) is assumed to have distinct atoms, for

, there is

(resp.

) open containing

(resp.

) such that

(resp.

) for

(resp.

).
For

, vague convergence yields for

, hence for such large

\displaystyle M_{n}(A_{x},B_{y})=a_{n}\mathbf{1}[y=T_{n}(x)],

and so

\displaystyle 1=\mu(A_{x})=M(A_{x},\mathbb{R}^{d})=\sum_{y\in{\nu}}\underbrace{M(A_{x},B_{y})}_{\in\{0,1\}}.

Hence for each

in probability. Thus we can choose a subsequence that converges a.s. and we will work with this subsequence now onwards. Up to change the definition of
\Lambda_{n}
, we can assume
n^{-1}N\to 1
a.s..

The strategy is to show that
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is close to
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}
for some random sequence
A_{n}\to 1
, using that
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
a.s. in the vague convergence. We wish to choose the scaling
A_{n}
so that the number of points match:
\#A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}=N
, but unfortunately for
a>0
,
\#(a\mathbb{Z}^{d})\cap\Lambda_{n}
is of the form
m^{d}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
N
might not be of this form, which is a source of nuisance. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that
(m-1)^{d}<N\leqslant m^{d}
. Let
A_{n}^{d}=n/m^{d}>0
so that

\displaystyle\#(A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d})\cap\Lambda_{n}=m^{d}=N+p\textrm{ where }p\leqslant m^{d}-(m-1)^{d}\leqslant dm^{d-1}.

Since
N\to\infty
a.s., we have that
m\to\infty
a.s. and further by the above bounds,
m^{-d}p\leq dm^{-1}\to 0
a.s.. This yields that
N/m^{d}\to 1
a.s. and so
A_{n}=n/m^{d}\to 1
a.s. as well.

If
p=0
(i.e.,
N=m^{d}
), set

{\bf Z}_{n}:=\sum_{k\in A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}}\frac{n}{N}\delta_{k}.

Otherwise, we build
{\bf Z_{n}}
by removing
p
points carefully from
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}
, so that
{\bf Z_{n}}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
both have
N
atoms, each of which is assigned mass
n/N
, and so that the transport cost stays under control. Formally,

\displaystyle{\bf Z}_{n}:=\sum_{k\in A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}\setminus B_{n}}\frac{n}{N}\delta_{k}

where we define the set of removed points
B_{n}
below, leading to
|B_{n}|=p
. The proportion of removed points is bounded by
(m^{d}-N)/m^{d}
, hence it goes to
0
, and we prove below that
{\bf Z_{n}}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
a.s. in the vague topology.

We now elaborate on the procedure of removing the points so that

{\bf Z_{n}}
also has a nice transport plan with
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
(and hence further to
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
). Ultimately each of the
N
points of
{\bf Z_{n}}
must be allocated to an area of volume
\frac{n}{N}
. The idea is to partition
\Lambda_{n}
into
p
nearly-equal blocks, with each block consisting of approximately
\frac{m^{d}}{p}=\frac{N}{p}+1
boxes centred at points of
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}
. Then in each block, we remove one of the points and re-allocate ‘efficiently’ the mass (approximately
\frac{m^{d}}{p}A_{n}^{d}=\frac{n}{p}
) to the remaining points (
\frac{N}{p}
) and thereby giving each point a mass of
\frac{n}{N}
as required.

Let us number
z_{1},\dots,z_{m^{d}}
the points of
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}
so that
z_{i}
is a neighbour of
z_{i+1}
(i.e. at distance
A_{n}
).
We allocate to
z_{1}
, the mass from the surrounding cube of measure
\frac{n}{m^{d}}\leqslant\frac{n}{N}
around
z_{1}
, but there is still a missing unallocated mass
\delta_{n}:=\frac{n}{N}-\frac{n}{m^{d}}=\frac{np}{Nm^{d}}>0
. We borrow this defect mass
\delta_{n}
from the next cube i.e., box of
z_{2}
, in an arbitrary way, which we represent by an arrow going from
z_{2}
to
z_{1}
. Now we allocate the remaining mass
\frac{n}{m^{d}}-\delta_{n}
in the box of
z_{2}
to itself and borrow remaining mass
2\delta_{n}
from the box of
z_{3}
, i.e. we put two arrows from
z_{3}
to
z_{2}
. We do this successively. After allocating
k
points,
z_{k}
will have to borrow
k\delta_{n}
mass from the box of
z_{k+1}
, represented by
k
arrows. Let
{k_{1}}
be the last
k
such that
k\delta_{n}\leqslant\frac{n}{m^{d}}
. We remove the
(k_{1}+1)
-th point from
A_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\Lambda_{n}
or equivalently retain
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k_{1}}
to
\bf Z_{n}
. This is called the
first batch
. Note that
k_{1}\leqslant N/p
and that each
z_{i}
borrows mass from its own box or the neighbouring box of
z_{i+1}
. So mass is transported at most distance of
2A_{n}
, i.e. each arrow is long of at most
2A_{n}
, and the total mass carried from a point to its neighbour is smaller or equal to
n/N
, the total transport cost of this first step is hence bounded by

\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{k_{0}}\underbrace{w(2A_{n})}_{\textrm{ distance }}\underbrace{\frac{n}{N}}_{\textrm{ mass }}\leqslant c\,\frac{k_{1}nA_{n}^{d}}{N}\leqslant c\,\frac{n\frac{n}{m^{d}}}{N}\frac{n}{m^{d}}\frac{Nm^{d}}{np}\leqslant c\,\frac{n^{2}}{pm^{d}}}.

This procedure is repeated at most
p=N-m^{d}
times. Each time, the
j
-th batch
\llbracket b_{j-1},b_{j}\rrbracket:=\{b_{j-1},\dots,b_{j}\},
with
b_{0}=0
, ouputs an excess mass denoted by
\delta_{n,j}\in[0,\delta_{n}]
, and this mass is allocated to the first point in the
(j+1)
-th batch (with the convention
\delta_{n,0}=0
). Then we follow a similar procedure: each point of the
(j+1)-
th batch borrows from its right neighbours
\delta_{n}
more mass than it gives to its left neighbour, and we denote by
k_{j}
the last integer such that the total mass borrowed from the right neighbour is
\leqslant n/N
. Note that
k_{j}\in\{k_{1}-1,k_{1}\}
. This leaves a residue mass
\delta_{n,j+1}\in[0,\delta_{n}]
. The same reasoning yields that the cost for each step is upper bounded by
\frac{n^{2}}{pm^{d}}
and so the total cost is bounded by

{C_{w}({\bf Z_{n}},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n})\leqslant c\,\frac{n^{2}}{m^{d}}.}

Heuristically, we expect
m^{d}\sim n
and thus the above bound yields
C_{w}({\bf Z_{n}},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n})\leqslant n
, thereby completing the proof. More formally, if we show that
\mathbf{E}[\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]\leqslant cn^{-1}
, we can derive that

\mathbf{E}[C_{w}({\bf Z_{n}},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n})\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]\leqslant n^{2}\mathbf{E}[\frac{1}{m^{d}}\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]\leqslant n^{2}\mathbf{E}[\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]\leq cn.

Using Bienaymé-Chebyshev’s inequality, we can derive the requisite bound as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]

\displaystyle\leqslant cn^{-1}+c\mathbf{P}(1\leqslant N<n/2)\leqslant cn^{-1}+cn^{-2}\textrm{Var}(N)\leqslant cn^{-1}.

The above transport cost bound combined with the assumption on
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
and triangle inequality, gives us that
\mathbf{E}[C_{w}({\bf Z_{n}},\tilde{\mu}_{n})\mathbf{1}[N\geq 1]]\leqslant cn
. In case
N=0
,
C_{w}({\bf Z_{n}},\tilde{\mu}_{n})=0
trivially and therefore the claim in Step (ii) above follows.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Consider first the case
d\geq 3
with the weight function
w(x)=x^{2}
. By Theorem
3
, we have that there exists a transport plan
m_{n}
such that
\mathbf{E}\widetilde{{\mathsf{C}}}_{w}(\tilde{\mu}_{n},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{n};\Lambda_{n})\leqslant cn
for all
n\geqslant 1
and similarly for
\tilde{\nu}_{n}
.

Now using Proposition
11
, there exist point processes
\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}
on probability spaces
(\Omega,\mathbf{P})
,
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathbf{P}^{\prime})
respectively and matchings
T:\mathbb{Z}^{d}+U\to{\rm supp}(\mu^{\prime}),T^{\prime}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}+U^{\prime}\to{\rm supp}(\nu^{\prime}),
with
U,U^{\prime}
being uniformly distributed in
[0,1]^{d}
respectively on
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
, such that the corresponding typical matching distances
X,X^{\prime}
satisfy
\mathbf{E}w(X)<\infty,\mathbf{E}^{{}^{\prime}}w(X^{\prime})<\infty
.
On the product space
\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}
endowed with
\mathbf{P}\otimes\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
, we have an obvious invariant matching
S:\mathbb{Z}^{d}+U\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}+U^{\prime}
with bounded typical distance, therefore
T^{-1}\circ S\circ T^{\prime}
is an invariant matching between
\mu^{\prime}
and
\nu^{\prime}
whose typical distance
\tilde{X}
satisfies
\mathbf{E}w(\tilde{X})<\infty
.

Now we consider the cases
d=1,2
. Here it suffices to prove (
7
) i.e., there exists a translation-invariant matching from
\mu
to
\nu
such that the typical distance
X
satisfies for some constant
c
,

\mathbf{P}(X\geqslant r)\leqslant cr^{-d/2}\sqrt{\sigma_{\mu}(r)+\sigma_{\nu}(r)},\,\,r\geq 1.

(44)

As explained in Remark
1
, the proof for
d=1,2
follows from this bound and boundedness of
\sigma_{\mu},\sigma_{\nu}
, which are implied by the integrability of the RPCM
\beta
. As argued in the case of
d=3
above, this requirement can be further reduced to exhibiting a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-translation invariant matching
T
from
\mu
to
\nu
satisfying (
44
).

The construction in
[
30
, Theorem 1(ii)]
proceeds as follows. Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k},\ldots
be i.i.d. uniform random vectors in
\{0,1\}^{d}
independent of
\mu,\nu
. Successively partition
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into randomly centered (at
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}2^{i}Z_{i}
) cubes of side-length
2^{k}
for
k\geq 0
and call these as cubes at level
k
i.e.,
k
-level cubes are

2^{k}z+[0,2^{k}]^{d}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}2^{i}Z_{i},\,z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

At level
k=0
all points are considered unmatched. At any level
k
, as many unmatched points of
\mu
and
\nu
within a
k
-level cube are matched as possible. This (partial) matching is carried using an arbitrary rule. The unmatched points after this are carried forward to the next level and the procedure is repeated. The (partial) matching thus induced is said to be
T
and with the unmatched points, matched to
\infty
. If
T
satisfies the tail bound (
44
), then letting
r\to\infty
, we can see that
T
matches every
\mu
point to a
\nu
point and by stationarity and that
\mu,\nu
have equal intensities, one can argue vice-versa as well; see
[
30
, Lemma 7]
. Thus, we have that
T
is a (perfect) matching between
\mu
and
\nu
and by construction
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-invariant.

Now we show the tail bound (
44
) for the partial matching
T
as described above. Using mass transport principle and that the random centering is independent of
\mu,\nu
, one can derive that (see derivation of equation (11) in
[
30
]
),

\mathbf{E}\Big(\nu\{x\in[0,1]^{d}:\|T(x)-x\|\geqslant\sqrt{d}2^{k}\}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-dk}\mathsf{E}S_{+},

where
S=(\nu(B)-\mu(B))
for
B=[0,2^{k})^{d}
and recall that
S_{+}=\max\{S,0\}
. Since
\mu,\nu
have equal intensities, we can bound this via Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequality as follows.

\mathbf{E}S_{+}\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbf{E}(S^{2})}\leqslant\sqrt{2\,(\,\text{Var}(\mu(B))+\text{Var}(\nu(B))\,)}=\sqrt{2^{dk+1}\,(\,\sigma_{\mu}(B)+\sigma_{\nu}(B)\,)},

where recall that
\sigma_{\mu}(B):=|B|^{-1}\text{Var}(\mu(B))
and similarly
\sigma_{\nu}(B)
. This gives the bound in (
44
) for
r=\sqrt{d}2^{k}
and one can then extrapolate it to all
r
.
∎
