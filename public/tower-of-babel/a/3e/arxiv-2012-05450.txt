Random matrices based schemes for stable and robust nonparametric
and functional regression estimators.

Abstract




a
University of Carthage,
Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Sciences of Bizerte, Tunisia.

Abstract
— In the first part of this work, we develop
a novel scheme for solving nonparametric regression problems. That is the approximation of possibly low regular and noised functions from the knowledge of their approximate values given at some random points. Our proposed scheme is based on the use of the pseudo-inverse of a random projection matrix, combined with some specific properties of the Jacobi polynomials system, as well as some properties of positive definite random matrices. This scheme has the advantages to be stable, robust, accurate and fairly fast in terms of execution time. In particular, we provide an
L_{2}
as well as an
L_{2}-
risk errors of our proposed nonparametric regression estimator. Moreover and unlike most of the existing nonparametric regression estimators, no extra regularization step is required by our proposed estimator. Although, this estimator is initially designed to work with random sampling set of uni-variate i.i.d. random variables following a Beta distribution, we show that it is still works for a wide range of sampling distribution laws. Moreover, we briefly describe how our estimator can be adapted in order to handle the multivariate case of random sampling sets.
In the second part of this work, we extend the random pseudo-inverse scheme technique to build a stable and accurate estimator for solving linear functional regression (LFR) problems. A dyadic decomposition approach is used to construct this last stable estimator for the LFR problem. Alaso, we give an
L_{2}-
risk error of our proposed LFR estimator. Finally, the performance of the two proposed estimators are illustrated by various numerical simulations. In particular, a real dataset is used to illustrate the performance of our nonparametric regression estimator.

Keywords:
Nonparametric regression, linear functional regression, Jacobi polynomials, eigenvalues, random matrix, random pseudo-inverse.

1
Introduction

In the first part of this work, we consider the following nonparametric regression model:

Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}=\widetilde{f}(X_{i}),\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(1)

where
(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
are random variables (or inputs) with distribution
\rho_{X}
and the noise terms
(\varepsilon_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
are i.i.d. real-valued centered random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}.
For
a given complete metric space
\mathcal{X},
a measurable subset
\mathcal{Y}
of
\mathbb{R}
and a training set
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}),1\leq i\leq n\}
in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},
we are interested in the construction of an estimator
\widehat{f}
such that for
x\in\mathcal{X},

\widehat{f}(x)
is a good estimate for the corresponding regression function
f.
Recall that if the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
are drawn from a joint probability measure
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},
then the regression function
f
is given by

f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}yd\rho(y|x)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x),\quad x\in\mathcal{X},

For simplicity, we will assume that the
X_{i}
take values in the interval
I=[-1,1].
Nonetheless, our results are easily adapted to a more general compact interval of
\mathbb{R}.
Also, we show how our nonparametric regression estimator can be adapted in order to handle the more general case of random sampling points
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,d\geq 2.

Note that the kernel ridge regression (KRR) scheme is a widely used scheme for solving the regression problem modeled by (
1
). For more details on the KRR based estimator, the reader is referred for example to
[
17
,
18
]
.
Other estimators for nonparametric regression problem have been proposed in the literature. To cite but a few, F. Comte and her co-authors have proposed
estimators based on projections associated with some classical orthogonal polynomials
[
7
]
. Also, in the literature, there exists another class of convolution kernels with variable bandwidths based robust estimators for the nonparametric regression problem, see for example
[
2
,
21
]
.

Our proposed estimator for solving problem (
1
) is based on combining the Random Sampling Consensus (RANSAC) iterative algorithm with
a random pseudoinverse based scheme for nonparametric regression. That is at each iteration of the RANSAC algorithm,
a stable nonparametric regression estimator is constructed by using the pseudoinverse of a random projection matrix, associated with
Jacobi orthogonal polynomials. Note that the RANSAC procedure, see for example
[
15
]
consists in randomly
selecting
m<n
measurements from model (
1
). From these
m
training data points
(X_{i},Y_{i}),
construct
an estimator and compute the mean squared prediction error estimation. This procedure is iterated till an estimator with high consensus is obtained. We should mention that in the literature, pseudoinverse scheme has been successfully used in a wide range of applications from different scientific area, including machine learning, see
[
13
]
and the references therein. The usual way of computing the pseudoinverse of an
n\times m
real coefficients matrix is to combine the Moore-Penrose inverse and the SVD techniques. It is well known that the time complexity of this last procedure is
O\big(\min(nm^{2},nm^{2})\big).
Nonetheless in
[
13
]
, the authors have proposed an accurate and Fast PesudoInverse algorithm for sparse feature matrices.

For the sake of simplicity, the emphasis will be on the construction of a stable estimator for problem (
1
) by using a random training set

\{(X_{i},Y_{i}),\,\,1\leq i\leq n\}.
More precisely, for real parameters
{\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2},}
we consider
the weight function
\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
and the associated weighted
L^{2}_{\omega}(I)-
space. This last space
is given by the set of real valued functions
f
that are measurable on
I
and satisfying

{\displaystyle\|f\|_{\omega}^{2}=\int_{I}|f(x)|^{2}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\,dx<+\infty.}

For a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
\{X_{i},\,1\leq i\leq n\}
be a sampling set of i.i.d random variables following the beta distribution
B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)
over
I.
Note that this last condition is not mandatory and it is used in order to simplify the different theoretical results related
to our nonparametric regression estimator. In fact, we show how this estimator can be adapted in order to handle random sampling set drawn from a more general probability distribution law or even from an unknown probability distribution.

Our nonparametric regression estimator is briefly described as follows.
We use the notation
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
for the transpose of
\boldsymbol{u},
then for a positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N+1\leq n,

\widehat{f}_{n,N}
is given in terms of the first
N+1
normalized Jacobi polynomials
\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x),\ k=0,\ldots,N.
More precisely, we have

\widehat{f}_{n,N}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widehat{c}_{k}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x),\quad x\in I.

(2)

Here, the expansion coefficients vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=(\widehat{c}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{c}_{N+1})^{\prime}
is given by the over-determined system

B_{N}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\Big]_{j,k}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\mathbf{Z}_{n},

(3)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\prime}.
We refer to the
n\times(N+1)
matrix
B_{N}
as the random projection matrix. One of the main result of this work is to prove that with high probability,
the
N+1
dimensional positive definite matrix
A_{N}=B_{N}^{\prime}B_{N}
is invertible and well conditioned. More precisely, we show that with high probability, the
2-
norm condition number of
A_{N},

denoted by
\kappa_{2}(A_{N})
is bounded by a fairly small constant. This ensures the stability of the estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}.
Consequently, the expansion coefficients vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
is simply given by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\big(B_{N}^{\prime}B_{N}\big)^{-1}B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{n}.

(4)

Also, we provide an upper bound for the regression error
\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega},
that holds with high probability. In particular, we provide more precise regression errors in the special cases where
f
has a Lipschitzian
p-
th order derivative or
f
is a
c-
bandlimited function, for some
c>0.
Moreover, we provide a bound for the

L^{2}-
risk error
{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|^{2}_{\omega}\Big]}.

In the second part of this work, we push forward the random pseudo-inverse based scheme for solving a linear functional regression (LFR) problem, that was introduced in
[
4
]
. This is done by combining the random pseudo-inverse scheme with a dyadic decomposition technique. This combination yields a stable and a highly accurate estimator for solving the LFR problem. Recall that for a compact interval
J,
the LFR model is given as follows

Y_{i}=\int_{J}X_{i}(s)\,\beta_{0}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(5)

Here, the
X_{i}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(J)
are random functional predictors, the
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d. centered white noise independent of the
X_{i}(\cdot)
and

\beta_{0}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(J)
is the unknown slope function to be recovered. As usual, we assume that the
X_{i}(\cdot)
are given by their series expansions with respect to an orthonormal set of
L^{2}(J).
That is for an orthonormal family
{\displaystyle\{\varphi_{k}(\cdot),\,k\geq 1\}}
of
L^{2}(J),

we have
{\displaystyle X_{i}(s)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\xi_{k}Z_{i,k}\varphi_{k}(s),}
where
the
Z_{i,k}
are i.i.d. centered random variables with variance
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
and
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a deterministic sequence of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.
Our proposed stable random pseudo-inverse based estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)
of
{\displaystyle\beta_{0}(\cdot)=\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}\varphi_{k}(\cdot)}
is briefly described as follows.
For an integer
N\geq 2,
we let
K_{N}=\Big[\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\Big]+1
and we consider the dyadic decomposition of the
set
[[1,N]]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
into
K_{N}
subsets
I_{k}=[[2^{k-1}+1,\min(2^{k},N)]],

1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

On each subset

I_{k},
we consider the random matrices
G_{k},F_{k}
given by

G_{k}=\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\xi_{l}Z_{i,j}Z_{i,l}\right]_{j,l\in I_{k}}=F_{k}^{\prime}F_{k},\quad F_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big[\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}\big]_{\begin{subarray}{c}\scriptstyle 1\leq i\leq N\\
\scriptstyle j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}.

Under the previous notation, our proposed estimator for the LFR problem (
5
) is given by

\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\widehat{c}_{n,j}\varphi_{j}(s),\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{n,k}=[\widehat{c}_{n,j}]_{j\in I_{k}}^{\prime}=G_{k}^{-1}\Big(F_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[Y_{i}^{k}]^{\prime}_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big),\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

Note that the
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}=[Y_{i}^{k}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
are obtained from the output vector
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
by substituting in (
5
),
X_{i}(s)
with its projection
{X_{i}^{k}(s)=\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}\varphi_{j}(s)}.

In particular, we provide an upper bound for the mean squared estimation error
\|\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)-\beta_{0}(\cdot)\|_{2}^{2},
that holds with high probability. This upper error bound involves the quantity
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\kappa_{2}(G_{k}).}
In practice, the deterministic sequence
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{j})_{j}
satisfies a decay condition of the type
\xi_{j}\asymp j^{-s}
for some
s>0.
Under this last assumption, it is easy to check that our combined dyadic decomposition and random pseudo-inverse scheme is stable in the sense that
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\lesssim 2^{s}\log N,}
which is a relatively small cumulative condition number. This ensures the stability of our proposed LFR estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot).
Also, we give an estimate for the
L^{2}-
risk error
\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\beta_{0}(\cdot)-\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{2}\Big],
where
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}(\cdot)
is a truncated version of the estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot).

This work is organized as follows. In section 2, we give some mathematical preliminaries that are frequently used in this work. In section 3, we study our random pseudo-inverse based estimator for the stable and robust approximation of the regression function
f
given by (
1
). In section 4, we give an error analysis of our combined dyadic decomposition, random pseudo-inverse based estimator for the LFR problem (
5
). In section 5, we give some numerical simulations that illustrate the different results of this work. In particular, we illustrate the performance of our nonparametric regression by applying it on a real dataset given by the daily Covid-19 cases of some world’s countries.

2
Mathematical preliminaries

We first recall that for two real numbers
\alpha,\beta>-1,
the classical Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}
are defined for
x\in[-1,1],
by the following Rodrigues formula,

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k}k!}\frac{1}{\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)}\frac{\mbox{d}^{k}}{\mbox{d}x^{k}}\Big(\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)(1-x^{2})^{k}\Big),\quad\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta},

(6)

with

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(1)={k+\max(\alpha,\beta)\choose{k}}=\frac{\Gamma(k+\max(\alpha,\beta)+1)}{k!\,\Gamma(\max(\alpha,\beta)+1)}.

Here,
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the usual Gamma function.
In the sequel, we let
\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}
denote the normalized Jacobi polynomial of degree
k,
given by

\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}}}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x),\quad h_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)\Gamma(k+\beta+1)}{k!(2k+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

(7)

In this case, we have

\|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}\|^{2}_{\omega}=\int_{-1}^{1}(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(y))^{2}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\,dy=1.

(8)

The following lemma provides us with some useful upper bounds for the normalized Jacobi polynomials
\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k},
for
k\geq 2.

Lemma 1
.

For
\alpha,\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2},
let
\mu=\max(\alpha,\beta),

c_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\alpha+\beta+1}{2}
and let

\eta_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\exp\big(\frac{2\max(\mu,0)}{12}+\frac{\max(\mu^{2}+\alpha\beta,0)}{8}\big)}{2^{(\alpha+\beta)/2}\Gamma(\mu+1)}

(9)

Then, we have

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)|\leq\eta_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta},\qquad\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}=k^{\mu}\sqrt{k+c_{\alpha,\beta}},\quad\forall\,k\geq 2.

(10)

Proof:
It is well known that under the previous condition on
\alpha,\beta,
we have

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)|=\max\big(|P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(1)|,|P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(-1)|\big)=\frac{\Gamma(k+\mu+1)}{k!\,\Gamma(\mu+1)},\quad\mu=\max(\alpha,\beta).

(11)

On the other hand, from
[
20
]
, for an integer
n\geq 2
and for two real numbers
a,b>-1,
we have

\frac{\Gamma(n+a)}{\Gamma(n+b)}\leq C_{n}^{a,b}n^{a-b},\quad C_{n}^{a,b}=\exp\Big(\frac{a-b}{2(n+b-1)}+\frac{1}{12(n+a-1)}+\frac{(a-1)(b-1)}{n}\Big).

(12)

By combining (
7
), (
11
) and (
12
), one gets

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{C_{k}^{\mu+1,1}C_{k}^{\alpha+\beta+1,\alpha+\beta-\mu+1}}}{\Gamma(\mu+1)2^{(\alpha+\beta)/2}}\,\,k^{\mu}\,\,\sqrt{k+\frac{(\alpha+\beta+1)}{2}}.

But from (
12
), it can be easily checked that for
k\geq 2,
we have

C_{k}^{\mu+1,1}C_{k}^{\alpha+\beta+1,\alpha+\beta-\mu+1}\leq\eta_{\alpha,\beta}.

By combining the previous two inequalities, we get

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)|\leq\eta_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}=\eta_{\alpha,\beta}k^{\mu}\,\,\sqrt{k+\frac{(\alpha+\beta+1)}{2}},\quad k\geq 2.

(13)

Next, we recall from
[
11
]
, the following Gershgorin circle theorem that is used for the estimation of
2-
norm condition numbers of various random
matrices used in this work.

Theorem 1

(Gershgorin circle theorem)
.

Let
A=[a_{ij}]
be a complex
n\times n
matrix. For
1\leq i\leq n,
let

{\displaystyle R_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}|a_{ij}|.}
Then, every eigenvalue of
A
lies within at least one of the discs
D(a_{ii},R_{i}).

The following McDiarmid’s concentration inequality is also used in the spectral analysis of the random matrices of the next section.

Theorem 2

(McDiarmid’s inequality)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with respective ranges in metric spaces

\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\chi}_{n}.
Let
f:\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}\times\cdots\times\boldsymbol{\chi}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a function satisfying the bounded differences property

\Big|f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})\Big|\leq c_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

Then, for any
\epsilon>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}\big[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\big]\leq\epsilon\Big)\geq 1-\exp\left(\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

3
Random projection on Jacobi polynomials system and nonparametric regression estimator

The main part of this section is devoted to the study of the stability and error analysis of our nonparametric regression estimator in the framework
of a sampling set drawn from a Beta distribution. Then, at the end of this section, we show how our estimator can be adapted in order to handle random sampling sets drawn from more general distribution laws or even from an unknown distribution law.

We first consider two real numbers
\alpha,\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and two positive integers

n\geq N+1
, then we let
\{X_{i},\,1\leq i\leq n\}
be a sampling set of i.i.d random variables following the beta
B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)
distribution over the interval
I.
Then, under the assumption that the regression function
f
(solution of the nonparametric regression problem (
1
)) is well approximated by its projection
f_{N}=\pi_{N}f
over the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{N}=\mbox{Span}\{\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k},\,0\leq k\leq N\},

our random pseudo-inverse based estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}
of
f\in L^{2}(I)
is given by

\widehat{f}_{n,N}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widehat{c}_{k}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x),\quad x\in I.

(14)

We require that this estimator is stable and robust. This estimator is given as the Least square solution of the over-determined system

\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}c_{k}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})=Y_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(15)

Note that if the regression function
f\in L^{2}_{\omega}(I),
then from the uniqueness of its expansion coefficients with respect to the orthonormal
basis
\{\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(\cdot),\,k\geq 0\},
for any integer
N\in\mathbb{N},
its first
N
coefficients
c_{i}(f)
do not depend on the specific values
of
y_{j}=f(X_{j}),
at a specific random sampling set
\{X_{j},j=1,\ldots,n\}.
These coefficients are uniquely given by

c_{k}(f)=<\widetilde{f},\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}>=\int_{I}\widetilde{f}(x)\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x)\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\,dx,\quad 1\leq k\leq N+1.

Note that by re-scaling by a factor
{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},}
the over-determined system (
15
) is written as

B_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{Y},\quad B_{N}=\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\Big]_{1\leq j\leq n,1\leq k\leq N+1},\quad\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{j}]^{\prime}_{1\leq j\leq n}.

(16)

We show that under some conditions on
f
as well as on the integers
n,N,
the least square solution of the previous system is a good approximation
of the set of the first
N+1
expansion coefficients of
f
. For this purpose, we define an
(N+1)\times(N+1)
random Jacobi projection matrix
A_{N}
by

A_{N}=B_{N}^{\prime}B_{N}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{l-1}(X_{j})\right]_{1\leq k,l\leq N+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j},

(17)

Note that the random matrix
\mathbf{D}_{j}
is also positive semidefinite. In fact,
\mathbf{D}_{j}=b_{j}^{\prime}b_{j},
where
b_{j}
is the
1\times(N+1)
matrix given by

{\displaystyle b_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big[\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{0}(X_{j})\cdots\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N}(X_{j})\Big]}.
Consequently, for any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1},
we have

X^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{j}X=X^{\prime}b_{j}^{\prime}\cdot b_{j}X\geq 0.

That is
\mathbf{D}_{j}
is a positive semi-definite matrix.
An important property of the matrix
A_{N}
is that under some conditions on the parameters
\alpha,\beta,n,N,
this matrix is well conditioned.
To prove this first main result, we need the following proposition that provides us with
an upper and a lower bound for
\lambda_{\max}(A_{N})
and
\lambda_{\min}(A_{N}),
respectively.

Proposition 1
.

Under the notation of Lemma 1, for any two positive integers
n>N\geq 2,
satisfying

0.63\,n>m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}\cdot(N+1)^{2\mu+2}\,\log(N+1),

(18)

where
{\displaystyle m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}=\frac{1+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha,\beta}}{2}}}{\mu+3/2}\eta^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}}
and
\eta_{\alpha,\beta}
is as given by (
9
).
Then, we have

\mathbb{E}\big(\lambda_{\max}(A_{N})\big)\leq 1.72+m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}\frac{(N+1)^{2\mu+2}\,\log(N+1)}{n}

(19)

and

\mathbb{E}\big(\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})\big)\geq 0.63-m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}\frac{(N+1)^{2\mu+2}\,\log(N+1)}{n}.

(20)

Proof:

We first note that from (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{0}(x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(2^{\alpha+\beta+1}B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{1}(x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\mu)\sqrt{\frac{3+\alpha+\beta}{(\alpha+1)(\beta+1)}}\big(2^{\alpha+\beta+1}B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)\big)^{-1/2}.

On the other hand, if
\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}
is as given by (
10
), then it is easy to check that for
\mu\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and for
\alpha,\beta\geq-\frac{1}{2},
the function

x\rightarrow x^{\mu}\sqrt{x+c_{\alpha,\beta}}
is increasing on
[2,+\infty).
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{N}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{N+1}x^{\mu}\sqrt{x+c_{\alpha,\beta}}\,dx\leq\int_{2}^{N+1}x^{\mu+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha,\beta}}{2}}\Big)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha,\beta}}{2}}\Big)}{\mu+3/2}(N+1)^{\mu+3/2}.

Hence, by using (
10
) and straightforward computation, one gets

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{0}(x)|+\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{1}(x)|+\cdots+\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N}(x)|\leq\eta_{\alpha,\beta}\int_{N}^{N+1}x^{\mu}\sqrt{x+c_{\alpha,\beta}}\,dx.

The previous two inequalities give us

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N}(x)|\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\Big(\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x)|\Big)\leq\eta^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}N^{\mu}\sqrt{N+c_{\alpha,\beta}}\frac{\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha,\beta}}{2}}\Big)}{\mu+3/2}(N+1)^{\mu+3/2}

(21)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha,\beta}}{2}}\Big)}{\mu+3/2}\eta_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}=m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}.

Next, let
\mathbf{D}_{j}
be the positive semi-definite matrix given by (
17
). Since the random samples
X_{i}
follow the Beta-distribution on
[-1,1]

with parameters
(\alpha+1,\beta+1),
then we have
\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\right)=\delta_{kl}.
That is

{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j}\Big)=I_{N+1},}
where
I_{N+1}
is the identity matrix of dimension
N+1.
Hence, we have

\Lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\min}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j}\big)\Big)=1,\quad\Lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{\max}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j}\big)\Big)=1.

Also, from Gershgorin circle theorem, we have

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{D}_{j})\leq\frac{1}{n}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N}(x)|\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\Big(\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x)|\Big)\leq\frac{1}{n}m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}=\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}.

(22)

Moreover, from
[
19
]
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big(\lambda_{\min}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j}\big)\Big)\geq 0.63\Lambda_{\min}-\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1),\quad\mathbb{E}\Big(\lambda_{\max}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{D}_{j}\big)\Big)\leq 1.72\Lambda_{\max}+\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1).

(23)

This concludes the proof of the proposition.

The following theorem provides us with an upper bound for the actual condition number
\kappa_{2}(A_{N}).

Theorem 3
.

Under the previous notation and the same conditions as proposition
1
, for any
0<\delta<1,
we have

\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\leq\frac{1.72+m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}\,\Big(\frac{\log(N+1)}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}\Big)}{0.63-m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}\,\Big(\frac{\log(N+1)}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}\Big)}

(24)

with probability at least
(1-\delta)^{2}.

Proof:

Given
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in[-1,1]^{n}
, we consider the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix with entries
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x_{j})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{l-1}(x_{j})
. We use the notation
A_{N}(x)
for such a matrix.
We denote by
\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x))
,
1\leq k\leq N+1,
the eigenvalues of
A_{N}(x)
arranged in decreasing order.
For
1\leq k\leq N+1,
we use McDiarmid’s concentration inequality for the
n-
variate mapping

x\mapsto\phi_{k}(x)=\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x))

We prove that the previous mapping
\phi_{k}
satisfies the bounded differences assumption.
That is, when only one of the
n
coordinates differs between
x
and
x^{\prime}
, then

|\phi_{k}(x)-\phi_{k}(x^{\prime})|\leq\frac{2}{n}m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}=2\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}.

(25)

Let us take this for granted for now and we will prove it later on. It follows from McDiarmid’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\phi_{k}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}\phi_{k}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2m_{\alpha,\beta}^{4}(N+1)^{4\mu+4}}=\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}

(26)

and

\mathbb{P}\big(\phi_{k}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}\phi_{k}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})\leq-\varepsilon\big)\leq\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}.

(27)

For the value of
k=1
, (
26
) gives us

\mathbb{P}\big(\lambda_{\max}(A_{N}))-\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\max}(A_{N}))\leq\varepsilon\big)\geq 1-\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}

(28)

Moreover, for the value of
k=N+1
, (
27
) gives us

\mathbb{P}\big(\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})-\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\min}(A_{N}))\geq-\varepsilon\big)\geq 1-\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}

(29)

By combining (
23
) and (
28
), one gets

\lambda_{\max}(A_{N})\leq 1.72+\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1)+\varepsilon

(30)

with probability at least
1-\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}
. Here,
L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)
is given by (
22
). Also,
by combining (
23
) and (
29
), one gets

\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})\geq\big(0.63-\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1)\big)-\varepsilon

(31)

with probability at least
1-\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}
.
Hence, by combining (
30
) and (
31
) , one gets

\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\leq\frac{1.72+\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1))+\varepsilon}{0.63-\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}\log(N+1)-\varepsilon}

with probability at least
{\displaystyle\Big(1-\exp\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}\Big)^{2}}
. Finally to get (
24
), it suffices to let

{\displaystyle\delta=\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2L_{N}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}\right)}.

Let us now prove the bounded differences condition (
25
). We have

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}(x)-\phi_{k}(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1}\cdots,x_{n})-\phi_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i+1}\cdots,x_{n})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x))-\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x^{\prime}))

Let
E
be the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix with entries

E_{k,l}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x_{i})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{l-1}(x_{i})-\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x^{\prime}_{i})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{l-1}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right),

so that
A_{N}(x)=A_{N}(x^{\prime})+E
. From Weyl’s perturbation theorem of the spectrum of a perturbed Hermitian matrix, see for example
[
5
]
, we have

\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x^{\prime}))+\lambda_{N+1}(E)\leq\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x^{\prime})+E)\leq\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x^{\prime}))+\lambda_{1}(E),\quad k=1,\cdots,N+1.

That is

|\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x^{\prime}))-\lambda_{k}(A_{N}(x))|\leq||E||.

Moreover, from Gershgorin circle theorem, we have, for
1\leq j\leq N+1

|\lambda_{j}(E)-E_{j,j}|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p\leq N+1\\
p\neq j\end{subarray}}|E_{j,p}|.

Hence, one gets

\displaystyle|\lambda_{j}(E)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|E_{j,j}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq p\leq N+1\\
p\neq j\end{subarray}}|E_{j,p}|=\sum_{1\leq p\leq N+1}|E_{j,p}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq p\leq N+1}\big|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j-1}(X_{i})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}(X_{i})|+|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j-1}(X^{\prime}_{i})\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{p-1}(X^{\prime}_{i})\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{N}(x)|\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\Big(\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(x)|\Big)\leq\frac{2}{n}m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}=2\frac{L_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}{n}.

This last inequality follows from (
21
).
Since
E
is Hermitian, then
||E||=\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N+1\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{j}|
which concludes the proof.

Remark 1
.

For the special convenient values of

the estimate of

It is easy to see that in this case, the optimal value of

Consequently, with high probability, we have

\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\lesssim\frac{1.72+\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{(N+1)\,\log(N+1)}{n}}{0.63-\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{(N+1)\,\log(N+1)}{n}}.

(32)

Remark 2
.

Note that the upper bound of
\kappa_{2}(A_{N})
given by
(
24
) of the previous theorem is the essential condition for the validity
of our nonparametric regression estimator. Numerical results given in the last section of this work, indicate that the quantity

Next, to study the regression estimation error in the
\|\cdot\|_{\omega}-
norm, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
g\in L^{2}_{\omega}(I)
be a bounded function. Under the previous notations, for any
\alpha\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and any
0<\delta<1,

we have with probability at least
1-\delta,

\left|\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(g(X_{j})\big)^{2}-\|g\|_{\omega}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(33)

where
\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}=B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)2^{\alpha+\beta+1}.

Proof:
It suffices to use McDiarmid’s inequality with the real valued
n-
variate function,

\phi:I^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,\,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\rightarrow\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(g(x_{i})\big)^{2}.

Then, for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}\in I,
we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})-\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x^{\prime}_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\Big|\big(g(x_{i})\big)^{2}-\big(g(x^{\prime}_{i})\big)^{2}\Big|\leq\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}=d_{i}.

Consequently, we have
{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}=\frac{(\gamma_{\alpha,\beta})^{2}}{n}\|g\|_{\infty}^{4}.}
On the other hand, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(g(X_{i})\big)^{2}\right)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}\big(g(X)\big)^{2}\Big)=\int_{I}|g(x)|^{2}\,\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\,dx=\|g\|_{\omega}^{2}.

The following theorem provides us with an upper bound in the
\|\cdot\|_{\omega}-
norm for the regression error in terms
of
\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}
and
\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}.
Here,
\pi_{N}f
is the orthogonal projection of the regression function over
the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{N}=\mbox{Span}\{\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k}(\cdot),\,0\leq k\leq N\}.

Theorem 4
.

Under the previous notations and the hypotheses of Theorem 3, let
{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\varepsilon_{i}|}
, then for any
0<\delta<1,
we have with probability at least

(1-\delta)^{2},

\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}\leq\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}+\sqrt{2\kappa_{2}(A_{N})}\frac{{\Big(\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)\Big)}^{\frac{1}{4}}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}+\frac{\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}}-{\Big(\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)\Big)}^{\frac{1}{4}}\frac{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}}{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}}}

(34)

Proof:

Since for any
x\in I,

\pi_{N}f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}c_{k}(f)\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x),
where
c_{k}(f)=\int_{I}f(t)\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(t)\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(t)\,dt,
then we have

B_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}=\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\Big]_{j,k}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}=\mathbf{P}.

(35)

where
\boldsymbol{C}=\big(c_{0}(f),\ldots,c_{N}(f)\big)^{\prime},
and
\mathbf{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big(\pi_{N}f(X_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{N}f(X_{n})\big)^{\prime}.

The estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}
is given by
{\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{n,N}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\widehat{c}_{k}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(x),\,x\in I}
where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\big(\widehat{c}_{0},\ldots,\widehat{c}_{N}\big)^{\prime}
is the least square solution of the over-determined system

B_{N}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\Big]_{j,k}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\mathbf{Z}_{n}.

(36)

This can be written as

B_{N}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{\Delta P}.

(37)

where
\mathbf{\Delta P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big((f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{1})+\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,(f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{n})+\varepsilon_{n}\big)^{\prime}.

Consequently, the least square solution of (
36
) is a perturbation of the least square solution of (
35
).
In this case, we have (see for example
[
6
]
)

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{C}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}\leq\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\frac{\|\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}.

On the other hand, since

{(f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}}^{2}\leq 2[{(f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{j})}^{2}+{\varepsilon_{j}}^{2}],\quad\forall\;1\leq j\leq n,

then we have

\|\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq 2(\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Delta}P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2})

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}=\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\end{subarray}}|\varepsilon_{j}|
and

\mathbf{{\widetilde{\Delta}P}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big((f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{1}),\ldots,(f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{n})\big)^{\prime}.

Hence, one gets

\frac{\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{C}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}\leq 2\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\frac{\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Delta}P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}.

(38)

In order to estimate
\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega},
one needs to estimate
\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}
which requires the estimation of
\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Delta}P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2}
and
{\|\mathbf{P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}
.
Taking
g=f-\pi_{N}f
in Lemma
2
gives, with probability at least

(1-\delta)^{2},

\left|\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{j})\big)^{2}-\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Hence, with probability at least
(1-\delta)^{2},
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((f-\pi_{N}f)(X_{j})\big)^{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}.

Equivalently, we have

\|\mathbf{\widetilde{\Delta}P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2}

with probability at least

(1-\delta)^{2}.
In the same way, taking
g=\pi_{N}f
in Lemma
2
, one gets with probability at least

(1-\delta)^{2},

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((\pi_{N}f)(X_{j})\big)^{2}\geq\frac{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}-\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}

or equivalently

{\|\mathbf{P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq\frac{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}-\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}}.

Since by Parseval’s equality, we have
\|\boldsymbol{C}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}=\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}
and by using (
38
), one gets

\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq 2\kappa_{2}(A_{N})\frac{\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}^{2}}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}-\sqrt{\frac{\log\big(\frac{2}{\delta}\big)}{n}}\frac{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\|\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}}}

with probability at least

(1-\delta)^{2}.
To conclude for the proof, it suffices to note that

\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}\leq\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}+\|\pi_{N}f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}

and use the fact that
\|\pi_{N}f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}=\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
f

Remark 3
.

Although the quantity
{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\eta_{i}|},

Theorem 5
.

Under the previous notation and hypotheses, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}c^{2}}\left(\frac{N}{n}\sigma^{2}+\frac{m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}}{n}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}\right)+\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}+4M^{2}\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}n^{-r}.

(44)

Proof:
Recall that from (
31
), we have for any
\varepsilon>0

\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})\geq\big(0.63-m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}\frac{(N+1)^{2\mu+2}}{n}\log(N+1)\big)-\varepsilon

with probability at least
{\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(\frac{-n\varepsilon^{2}}{2m_{\alpha,\beta}^{4}(N+1)^{4\mu+4}}\Big)}
. In particular for
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{r,n,N}=\sqrt{\frac{2r\log n}{n}}(N+1)^{\mu+1}
, and under condition (
41
), one gets

\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})\geq c)\geq 1-n^{-r}

(45)

As it is done in
[
22
]
, we consider the two measurable subsets of
I^{n}
denoted by
\Omega_{+}
and
\Omega_{-}
, where
\Omega_{+}
is the set of all possible draw
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
giving
\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})\geq c
, while
\Omega_{-}
is the set of possible draw giving
\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})<c
. Let
d\boldsymbol{\rho}
be the probability measure on
I^{n}
, given by the tensor product of unidimensional probability measure associated to the
B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)
distribution, that it is

d\boldsymbol{\rho}=\otimes^{n}d\rho(x_{i}),\qquad d\rho(x_{i})=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x_{i})\mathbf{1}_{I}(x_{i})\,dx_{i},\qquad\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}=B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)2^{\alpha+\beta+1}.

(46)

Then, we have

\int_{\Omega_{-}}d\boldsymbol{\rho}=\mathbb{P}\big\{(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})\in I^{n};\lambda_{\min}(A_{N})<c\big\}\leq n^{-r}.

(47)

Next, by using (
43
), it is easy to see that the truncated estimator
\tilde{f}_{N,M}
satisfies

|f(x)-\tilde{f}_{N,M}|\leq|f(x)-\widehat{f}_{n,N}(x)|,\quad\forall\,x\in I

(48)

and

|f(x)-\tilde{f}_{N,M}(x)|\leq|f(x)|+|\tilde{f}_{N,M}|\leq 2M,\quad\forall\,x\in I.

(49)

Hence, we have

\mathbb{E}\big(\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}\big)=\int_{\Omega_{+}}\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}+\int_{\Omega_{-}}\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}

(50)

By using (
49
), one gets

\int_{\Omega_{-}}\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}\leq 4M^{2}\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}n^{-r}

(51)

To bound the first quantity of the right hand side of (
50
), we use (
48
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\|f-\tilde{f}_{N,M}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\|f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{+}}\|f-\pi_{N}f\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}+\int_{\Omega_{+}}\|\pi_{N}f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}^{2}d\boldsymbol{\rho}

By using Parseval’s equality and our previous notation, we have, on
\Omega_{+}

\displaystyle\|\pi_{N}f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{C}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\|A_{N}^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\|B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\|B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

That is

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\pi_{N}f-\widehat{f}_{n,N}\|_{\omega}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\Big].

But

\|B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\sum_{j,l=1}^{n}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\Big(g_{N}(X_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}\Big)\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{l})\Big(g_{N}(X_{l})+\varepsilon_{l}\Big)

where
g_{N}=(f-\pi_{N}f)\quad\bot\quad\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1},\quad 1\leq k\leq N+1.

Using the independence of the
X_{j}
’s as well as the fact that the
\varepsilon_{j}
’s are independent of the
X_{j}
’s, together with the facts that
\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{j})=0
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\big]=\sigma^{2}
, one gets

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|B_{N}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta P}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\big(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\Big]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\big(g_{N}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\Big].

(53)

Since
{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\big(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\big]\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\Big]=\sigma^{2}\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}}
and since
{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[(g_{N}(X_{j}))^{2}\Big]=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}\|g_{N}\|_{\omega}^{2}}
, together with the fact that
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j}))^{2}\leq m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}}
, then we have

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{k-1}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\big(g_{N}(X_{j})\big)^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{m_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(N+1)^{2\mu+2}}{n\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}}\|g_{N}\|_{\omega}^{2}.

(54)

Finally, by combining the previous equalities and inequalities, together with (
50
)–(
53
), one gets the desired
L_{2}-
risk error of the estimator
\tilde{f}_{N,M}.

Next, we briefly describe how our estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}
can be used in the framework of a general random sampling set
\{X_{i},\,1\leq i\leq n\},
drawn from a distribution law, other than the Beta law with parameters
\alpha+1,\beta+1.
There is two cases to consider. In the first case, we assume that the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of a random variable with known cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F_{X}(\cdot).
In this case, it is well known that the transformed random variables
x_{i}=F_{X}(X_{i})
are i.i.d. copies of a random variables following the uniform law over
(0,1).
Moreover, it is well known that the CDF associated with the Beta distribution with parameters
\alpha+1,\beta+1
is given by
I_{x}(\alpha+1,\beta+1):
the regularized incomplete Beta function. Since this last function is invertible, then the transformed random sampling points, given by

\tau_{i}=I_{x}^{-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1)\big(F_{X}(X_{i})\big),\quad 1\leq i\leq n

(55)

follow the
Beta(\alpha+1,\beta+1)
distribution. For the interesting special values of
\alpha=\beta=-\frac{1}{2},
see Remark
1
, the inverse of the
I_{x}(1/2,1/2)
is simply given by

I_{x}^{-1}(1/2,1/2)(t)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\sin\big(\pi t-\frac{\pi}{2}\big)\Big),\quad t\in[0,1].

(56)

In the second case where the CDF
F_{X}
is not known, this is the case where the sampling distribution law is unknown, then one can replace in formula
(
55
), the CDF
F_{X}
by an estimator of this later. There is a fairly rich literature devoted to build estimators of the CDF of unknown probability laws, see for example
[
8
]
and the references therein.
Note that to get accurate approximate values of the outputs
\widetilde{Y}_{i}
at the transformed random sampling points
\tau_{i},
one may use an interpolation scheme based on the observed
Y_{i}
at the original random sampling points
X_{i}.
In example 1 of the numerical simulations section, we provide some numerical results that illustrate the stability of the estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}
when the random sampling set is drawn from the standard normal distribution.

Finally, we briefly describe our random pseudoinverse scheme based estimator can be generalized to the multivariate case, with random sampling sets
\{X_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
In fact, we only need to replace
each Jacobi polynomial
\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)},

0\leq k\leq N
by its tensor product
d-
dimensional version

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\widetilde{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m_{j}}(x_{j}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in I^{d},\quad\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}^{d}.

Note that
\{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\alpha,\beta},\,\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(I^{d},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\alpha,\beta}),
where
{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\alpha,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\omega_{\alpha,\beta}(x_{j})}.
For the sake of simplicity,
we restricted ourselves to the case
d=1.
The extension of this scheme to higher dimensions is the subject of a future work.

4
Random pseudo-inverse based scheme for functional regression estimator

In this paragraph, we first recall that for a compact interval
J,
the LFR problem is given by

Y_{i}=\int_{J}X_{i}(s)\,\beta_{0}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(57)

where the
X_{i}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(J)
are random functional predictors, given by
{\displaystyle X_{i}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi_{k}Z_{i,k}\varphi_{k}(s).}
Here,
the
{\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(\cdot)}
is an orthonormal family of
L^{2}(J),

the
Z_{i,k}
are i.i.d centered random variables with variance
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
and
(\xi_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
is a real valued deterministic sequence.
The
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d centered white noise independent of the
X_{i}(\cdot)
and

\beta_{0}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(J)
is the unknown slope function to be recovered. We use a partition of the set
[[1,N]]
given by
K_{N}=\Big[\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\Big]+1
subsets
I_{k}=[[2^{k-1}+1,\min(2^{k},N)]].
On each subset

I_{k},
we consider the random matrix
G_{k}
given by

G_{k}=\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\xi_{l}Z_{i,j}Z_{i,l}\right]_{j,l\in I_{k}}

(58)

Then, we have

\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\widehat{c}_{n,j}\varphi_{j}(s),\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{n,k}=[\widehat{c}_{n,j}]_{j\in I_{k}}^{\prime},

(59)

where

\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{n,k}=G_{k}^{-1}(F_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[Y_{i}^{k}]^{\prime}_{1\leq i\leq n}),\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

(60)

Here, the
\boldsymbol{Y}^{k}=[Y_{i}^{k}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
are obtained from
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
by substituting in (
5
),
X_{i}(s)
with its projection
{X_{i}^{k}(s)=\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}\varphi_{j}(s)}.
Consequently, the model (
57
) is substituted with the following model

Y^{k}_{i}=\int_{J}X^{k}_{i}(s)\,\beta_{0}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon^{k}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N},

(61)

where the i.i.d. noises
\varepsilon^{k}_{i}
are centered and
\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\varepsilon^{k}_{i}\big)^{2}\Big]=\sigma_{k}^{2}.
Moreover, we assume that the
\varepsilon^{k}_{i}
are independent of the
Z_{ij}.

Then we show that the estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot),
given by
(
59
) and (
60
) is an accurate and stable estimator for the slope function
\beta_{0}(\cdot),
solution of the previous LFR problem.
The following theorem provides us with a bound for the
2-
condition number of
G_{k},
as well as a bound for the estimation error

\|\beta_{0}(\cdot)-\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{2}.
For this purpose, we use the function
{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\varphi_{j}(\cdot)}.
Moreover, for
1\leq k\leq K_{N},
we use the notation
g^{k}(\cdot)=\pi_{I_{k}}g(\cdot),
the orthogonal projection of
g(\cdot)
over
\mbox{Span}\{\varphi_{j}(\cdot),\,j\in I_{k}\}.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that
{\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq N}{|Z_{ij}|}\leq M}
with probability at least
(1-\delta_{N}).
Then, for any
\eta>0,
we have with probability at least
1-2\Big(\exp\big(\frac{-n\eta^{2}}{2M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\big)+\delta_{N}\Big)

\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\leq\frac{1.72\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}+\frac{M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{n}\log(2^{k-1})+\eta}{0.63\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}-\frac{M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{n}\log(2^{k-1})-\eta};\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

(62)

Here
M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=M^{2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N\end{subarray}}{|\xi_{j}|}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|_{\ell_{1}}.
Moreover,
we have

\|\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)-\beta_{0}(\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\Big)\max_{1\leq k\leq K_{N}}\frac{\|\beta^{k}_{0}(\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}\|(\varepsilon^{k}_{i})_{i}\|^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\sigma_{Z}^{2}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}^{2}c_{j}^{2}-\eta}.

(63)

with probability at least
\Big(1-\exp\big(-2n\frac{\eta^{2}}{M^{4}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}(\cdot)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{4}\|\beta_{0}(\cdot)\|_{L_{2}}^{4}}\big)\Big)^{K_{N}}.

Proof:
Since the i.i.d. random variables
Z_{i,j}
are centred with variances
\sigma_{Z}^{2}
, then

\mathbb{E}(G_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{2^{k-1}+1}^{2}&&\\
&\ddots\\
&&\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{2^{k}}\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently, the minimum and the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathbb{E}(G_{k})
are given by

\Lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}(G_{k}))=\sigma_{Z}^{2}\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}{\xi_{j}^{2}},\quad\Lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{E}(G_{k}))=\sigma_{Z}^{2}\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}{\xi_{j}^{2}}.

On the other hand, the random matrix
G_{k}
is written in the following form

G_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i,k},\quad D_{i,k}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{j}\xi_{l}Z_{i,j}Z_{i,l}\right]_{j,l\in I_{k}}.

Note that each matrix
D_{i,k}
is positive semi definite. This follows from the fact that for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{k-1}}
, we have

\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}D_{i,k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}}B_{i,k}B_{i,k}\boldsymbol{x}\geq 0,\quad B_{i,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}]_{j\in I_{k}}.

By using Gershgorin circle theorem, one gets

\begin{array}[]{lll}\lambda_{\max}(D_{i,k})&\leq&\frac{1}{n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}{|\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}|}\displaystyle\sum_{l=2^{k-1}+1}^{2^{k}}|\xi_{l}Z_{i,l}|\\
&\leq&\frac{M^{2}}{n}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}{|\xi_{j}|}\displaystyle\sum_{l=2^{k-1}+1}^{2^{k}}|\xi_{l}|\leq\frac{M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{n}\end{array}

Hence, we have

\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\min}(G_{k}))\geq 0.63\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}-\frac{M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{n}\log(2^{k-1})

and

\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\max}(G_{k}))\leq 1.72\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{k}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}+\frac{M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{n}\log(2^{k-1})

Then, we use McDiarmid’s concentration inequality to conclude that for any
\eta>0
, we have

\lambda_{\min}(G_{k})\geq\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\min}(G_{k}))-\eta,\quad\lambda_{\max}(G_{k})\leq\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{\max}(G_{k}))+\eta

with probability at least
1-\exp\frac{-n\eta^{2}}{2M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}
. By combining the previous two inequalities, one gets (
62
).

Next to get the estimation error given by (
63
), we note that on each subset
I_{k}
, the associated exact expansion coefficients vector of

\beta_{0}(\cdot)
is given by
\mathbf{c}^{k}=[c_{j}]_{j\in I_{k}}.
Similarly, we define the expansion coefficients restricted to
I_{k}
of the estimator

\widehat{\beta}_{n,N},
that we denote by
\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{n,k}.
These two coefficients vectors are given by

{\mathbf{c}^{k}}^{\prime}=G_{k}^{-1}\Big(F_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[Y_{i}^{k}-\varepsilon_{i}^{k}]^{\prime}_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big)=G_{k}^{-1}\Big(F_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[\tilde{Y_{i}^{k}}]^{\prime}_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big),\quad\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{n,k}=G_{k}^{-1}\Big(F_{k}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}[Y_{i}^{k}]^{\prime}_{1\leq i\leq n}\Big),

(64)

where,

F_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big[\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}\big]_{1\leq i\leq n,j\in I_{k}},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

(65)

Then we have the following perturbation result for the pseudo-inverse least square solution of an over-determined system,

\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{n,k}-\mathbf{c}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{c}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}\leq\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\frac{\|\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}^{k}-\mathbf{Y}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}=\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\frac{\frac{1}{n}\|(\varepsilon_{i}^{k})_{i}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{\frac{1}{n}\|(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k})_{i}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}.

(66)

On the other hand, since
\Big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\Big)^{2}=\Big(\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}Z_{i,j}c_{j}\Big)^{2},
then we have

0\leq\big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\big)^{2}\leq M^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\|\beta_{0}^{k}(\cdot)|_{L_{2}}^{2}

and

\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\big)^{2}\big]=\sigma_{Z}^{2}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}^{2}c_{j}^{2}

Hence by using Hoeffding’s inequality, one concludes that for any
\eta>0
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\big)^{2}-\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\big)^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{Y_{i}}^{k}\big)^{2}-\sigma_{Z}^{2}\sum_{j\in I_{k}}\xi_{j}^{2}c_{j}^{2}\geq-\eta

(67)

with probability at least
1-\exp\big(-2n\frac{\eta^{2}}{{M}^{4}\|\xi^{l}(\cdot)\|^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\|\beta_{0}^{l}\|_{L_{2}}^{4}}\big)
. Finally, by using the inequalities (
64
)–(
67
), together with Parseval’s equality and some straightforward computations, one gets the desired inequality (
63
).

Remark 4
.

In practice, the deterministic sequence
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{j})_{j}
satisfies a decay condition of the type
\xi_{j}\asymp j^{-s}
for some
s>0.

In this case, the combined dyadic decomposition and random pseudo-inverse scheme estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)
is stable
in the sense that

\kappa_{2}(G)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\kappa_{2}(G_{k})\lesssim 2^{s}\frac{1.72\cdot\log N}{0.63\cdot\log 2}

(68)

is a relatively small cumulative condition number.

Next, we are interested in having an
L_{2}
-risk error of a truncated version of our LFR estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot).
More precisely, we assume that the true slope function
\beta_{0}(\cdot)
is almost every where bounded, that is there exists
L>0
such that

|\beta_{0}(x)|\leq L,\quad a.e.x\in J.

Then, as for the nonparametric estimator of the previous section, the truncated
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)
estimator, denoted by
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,L}(\cdot)
is defined by

\widetilde{\beta}_{N,L}(x)=\mbox{Sign}\big(\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(x)\big)\min\Big(L,|\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(x)|\Big).

Let
r>0
and
\eta_{k}>0
be such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\lambda_{\min}(G_{k})\geq\eta_{k}\big)\geq 1-n^{-r},\quad 1\leq k\leq K_{N}.

(69)

The following theorem provides us with the
L_{2}
-risk of the estimator
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}.

Theorem 7
.

Under the previous notations and hypotheses, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}(\cdot)-\beta_{0}(\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{\sigma_{Z}^{2}\|(\xi_{i})_{i}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}}{n^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{N}}\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\eta_{k}^{2}}|I_{k}|\Bigg)+\frac{4L^{2}}{n^{r}}K_{N}.

(70)

Proof:
Let
\mathbf{\tilde{c}_{n,k}}
be the expansion coefficients vector of
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}^{k}
, the projection over
\mbox{Span}\{\varphi_{j}(\cdot),\,j\in I_{k}\}
of
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}.
For each
1\leq k\leq K_{N},
let
\Omega_{+,k}
and
\Omega_{-,k}
be the set of all possible draw
(X_{1}(\cdot),\cdots,X_{n}(\cdot))
for which
\lambda_{\min}(G_{k})\geq\eta_{k}
and
0<\lambda_{\min}(G_{k})<\eta_{k}
respectively. Then , we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mathbf{\tilde{c}_{n,k}}-\mathbf{c}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\Big]\leq\int_{\Omega_{+,k}}\int_{J}|(\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dxd\boldsymbol{\rho}+\int_{\Omega_{-,k}}\int_{J}|(\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dxd\boldsymbol{\rho}.

(71)

where

\int_{\Omega_{-,k}}\int_{J}|(\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dxd\boldsymbol{\rho}\leq\frac{4L^{2}}{n^{r}}.

(72)

and

\int_{\Omega_{+,k}}\int_{J}|(\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dxd\boldsymbol{\rho}\leq\int_{\Omega_{+,k}}\int_{J}|(\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dxd\boldsymbol{\rho}.

(73)

Here,
d\boldsymbol{\rho}
is the tensor product probability measure, defined in a similar manner as (
46
) and associated
with the probability law of the random predictors
X_{i}(\cdot).

By using Parseval’s equality, we have from (
64
)

\int_{J}|(\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dx=\|\widehat{\mathbf{c}}_{n,k}-\mathbf{c}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\|G_{k}^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\|F_{k}^{\prime}\big(\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}^{k}-\mathbf{Y}^{k}\big)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

So that on
\Omega_{+,k},
we obtain

\int_{J}|(\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dx\leq\frac{1}{n\eta_{k}^{2}}\|F_{k}^{\prime}\big(\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}^{k}-\mathbf{Y}^{k}\big)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

But

F_{k}^{\prime}\big(\mathbf{\widetilde{Y}}^{k}-\mathbf{Y}^{k}\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigg[\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n}-\varepsilon_{l}^{k}\xi_{i}Z_{l,i}\Bigg]^{\prime}_{i\in I_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big[a_{i}\Big]^{\prime}_{i\in I_{k}}.

By using the hypotheses on the noises
\varepsilon_{i},
it is easy to see that

\mathbb{E}\big[a_{i}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{l,j=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{k}_{l}\varepsilon^{k}_{j}\xi_{l}\xi_{i}Z_{l,i}Z_{j,i}\Bigg]=\sigma_{k}^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}^{2}.

Consequently, we have

\int_{J}|(\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}^{k}-\beta_{0}^{k})(x)|^{2}dx\leq\frac{|I_{k}|}{n^{2}\eta_{k}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\|(\xi_{i})_{i}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.

The previous inequality, together with (
71
), (
72
) and (
73
) lead to

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mathbf{\tilde{c}_{n,k}}-\mathbf{c}^{k}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{|I_{k}|}{n^{2}\eta_{k}^{2}}\sigma_{k}^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\|(\xi_{i})_{i}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\frac{4L^{2}}{n^{r}}

Finally, by using Parseval’s equality and by adding inequalities on subsets
I_{k}
, one gets the desired
L_{2}-
risk error of the estimator
\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}
.

Remark 5
.

For the special of
K_{N}=1,
that is our estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{n,N}(\cdot)
is constructed without the dyadic partition of the set
[[1,N]],
then the
L_{2}-
risk of the previous theorem is simply given by

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\widetilde{\beta}_{N,M}(\cdot)-\beta_{0}(\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\sigma_{Z}^{2}\|\xi\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}N}{n^{2}\eta^{2}_{N}}+\frac{4L^{2}}{n^{r}}.

5.2
A Real data Application: Covid-19 world’s countries spread.

In this last section, we use the publicly available daily updated dataset of world’s countries spread of Covid-19, provided by Our World in Data. This dataset contains the updated data starting from March 1st 2020 till October 16th and corresponding to the daily confirmed cases as well as the daily deaths for different world’s countries. The URL of the dataset is https://github.com/owid/covid-19-data/tree/master/public/data

Plots of the true Covid-19 daily cases (points) versus their Jacobi based estimates (line) for (a) USA, (b) Algeria, (c) Italy, (d) India.

For the present simulations, we restrict ourselves to the data corresponding to daily cases during the period March 1st-October 16th and corresponding to four countries: USA, Algeria, Italy and India. The considered data covers
m=594
days and the aim is to build a non-parametric regression estimators for these countries daily covid-19 cases. To apply our estimator based on random pseudo-inverse associated with Jacobi polynomials, we have considered the special values of the parameters
\beta=\alpha=-\frac{1}{2},

N=40.
Then, we have considered a sampling set
{\displaystyle S=\{X_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n\}}

random samples in the interval

The coefficients

4

{\displaystyle Q_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\widetilde{P}_{k}(2x-1),\,x\in J.}
These last set of Jacobi polynomials are orthonormal on

In order to make this estimator robust (that is not sensitive to data outliers),

10

594
measurements from the
n=340
the size of random sampling set used at each iterations. In Figure 1, we have plotted in points, the graphs of the real data corresponding to the daily confirmed cases, versus the graph of the associated Jacobi polynomials based estimator
\widehat{f}_{n,N}(\cdot).
These numerical simulation results are coherent with the theoretical results
