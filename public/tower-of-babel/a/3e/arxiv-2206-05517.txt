Abstract
. We define oriented posets with corresponding rank matrices , where linking two posets by an edge corresponds to matrix multiplication. In particular, linking chains via this method gives us fence posets, and taking traces gives us circular fence posets. As an application, we give a combinatorial model for q q -deformed Markov numbers. We also resolve a conjecture of Leclere and Morier-Genoud and give several identities between circular rank polynomials.

Oriented posets
are posets with specialized end points that can be connected together to build larger posets. We can keep track of this building operation by matrix multiplication, which allows for fast and easy calculations. In this paper, we develop their combinatorics and consider applications on rank polynomial identities and Markov numbers. Oriented posets so far seem to be versatile objects that are applicable to other branches of combinatorics. See

defining a

using circular fence posets. It was further shown that:

(
[

]

(1)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathfrak{R}}((\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2s});q)=\overline{\mathfrak{R}}((\alpha_{2s},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2s-1});q).

under the Farey sum, and mutations of the quiver representation for a torus with one marked point (
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
). Frobenius conjectured in

that each Markov number appears as the maximum of a unique Markov triple, a conjecture still open to this day (The interested reader is refered to the 2012 book
[
13
]
on the subject).
Markov numbers were recently
q
-deformed using Cohn matrices (
[
2
]
,
[
14
]
). In Section

-deformed Markov numbers.

2.1.
Fence Posets and Rank Polynomials

A composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
of
n

. Here
n
is called the
size
of
\alpha

|\alpha|
, and
s
is called the
length
of
\alpha
. During this work, there are multiple instances when we add extra parts to a composition from the left. In that case, we slightly abuse notation and use
(i,\alpha)
to denote the composition
(i,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{s})
. We also use the notation
a^{b}
to denote
b

.

We follow the convention of
[
3
]
to start our fence posets with an up step. The rank polynomials for the posets starting with down steps can easily be inferred from these for the following reason: The complement of any ideal is an up-closed subset of the poset (satisfies
x\in I
,
y\succeq x\Rightarrow y\in I
). If we flip a fence poset

upside down, the resulting poset has the rank polynomial given by reversing the coefficients of
\alpha

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{\updownarrow}(\alpha;q)

\displaystyle=q^{|\alpha+1|}\cdot\mathfrak{R}(\alpha;q^{-1}).

When the fence we are considering is circular, turning the poset upside down is the same thing as cyclicly shifting segments by one, so the symmetry of the rank polynomial is actually equivalent to the invariance under cyclic shifts.

\displaystyle\overline{\mathfrak{R}}^{\updownarrow}(\alpha;q)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathfrak{R}}((\alpha_{2s},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2s-1});q)=q^{|\alpha|}\cdot\overline{\mathfrak{R}}(\alpha,q^{-1})=\overline{\mathfrak{R}}(\alpha;q).

2.2.
q
-Rationals

For a given rational number
r/t
, two natural ways to express
r/t
as a continued fraction are:

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{r}{t}=a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{a_{3}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{2m}}}}}=c_{1}-\cfrac{1}{c_{2}-\cfrac{1}{c_{3}-\cfrac{1}{\ddots-\cfrac{1}{c_{k}}}}}

The expressions
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2m}]
and
\llbracket c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\rrbracket
are called the regular and negative continued fractions for
r/t
respectively. They are unique under the conditions
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{i}\geq 1
for
i\geq 2
for the regular case and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c_{i}\geq 2
for
i\geq 2
for the negative case. Furthermore, one can easily convert one expression into the other via:

(3)

\displaystyle\llbracket c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\rrbracket=\llbracket a_{1}+1,2^{a_{2}-1},a_{3}+2,2^{a_{4}-1},a_{5}+2,\ldots,a_{2m-1}+2,2^{a_{2m}-1}\rrbracket

where
2^{t}
stands for
t
parts of size
2
.
The
q
-analogue for
\frac{r}{t}
is then obtained by replacing the terms with powers of
q
and
q
-integers in the following way:

(4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\frac{r}{t}\right]_{q}:=[a_{1}]_{q}+\cfrac{q^{a_{1}}}{[a_{2}]_{q^{-1}}+\cfrac{q^{-a_{2}}}{\ddots+\cfrac{q^{a_{2m-1}}}{[a_{2m}]_{q^{-1}}}}}=[c_{1}]_{q}-\cfrac{q^{c_{1}-1}}{[c_{2}]_{q}-\cfrac{q^{c_{2}-1}}{\ddots-\cfrac{q^{c_{k-1}-1}}{[c_{k}]_{q}}}}

Here, for a negative integer
-n
, we take
[-n]_{q}=-q^{-1}-q^{-2}-\cdots-q^{-n}
.

The non-trivial fact that the two
q
-expressions given in Equation
2
give the same
q
-analogue is shown in
[
1
]
. It is further shown that the said
q
-analogue equals a rational function
\frac{R(q)}{T(q)}
with polynomials
R(q),T(q)
evaluating to
r
and
s
respectively at
q=1
.

.

The fraction
32/7
has the regular and negative continued fraction expressions
[3,1,1,4]
and
[4,3,2,2,2]
.

\displaystyle\frac{32}{9}=3+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{1}{4}}}=4-\cfrac{1}{3-\cfrac{1}{2-\cfrac{1}{2-\cfrac{1}{2}}}}.

We can calculate its

-analogue via Equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left[\frac{32}{9}\right]_{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+3q+5q^{2}+6q^{3}+6q^{4}+5q^{5}+3q^{6}+2q^{7}+q^{8}}{1+2q+2q^{2}+2q^{3}+q^{4}+q^{5}}=\frac{\mathfrak{R}((2,1,1,3);q)}{\mathfrak{R}((1,3);q)}.

3.
Oriented Posets

We define an

to be a triple

where

\displaystyle(P,x_{L},x_{R})\searrow(Q,y_{L},y_{R}):=(S,x_{L},y_{R})

where

and

by setting
x_{R}\succeq y_{L}
. We also use the notation
\circlearrowright\!(\overrightarrow{P})
to denote the structure obtained by adding the relation
x_{R}\succeq x_{L}
. This is generally a poset, though in the case
x_{R}\preceq x_{L}
in the original poset we get some degeneracy.

There are two natural ways to see the length
n
chain poset as an oriented poset: The increasing chain
\overrightarrow{U_{n}}
with

taken to be the minimal element and
x_{R}
the maximum, and the decreasing chain
\overrightarrow{D_{n}}
with the opposite orientation. See Figure

below for an example.

The motivation behind oriented posets is to build up larger families of posets modularly from smaller pieces, using the addition operation connecting the right end of one poset to the left end of another. In particular, we will use them to build regular and circular fence posets and calculate their rank polynomials in Section
5
. Though this building method is not sufficient to capture the complicated nature of a general poset, it works well when the underlying graph naturally comes with edge-cuts that makes the structure disconnected, as in the cases of the Dynkin diagrams for Lie groups. With this paradigm, we can use
2\times 2
matrices to calculate the rank polynomials of the larger posets we obtain, where addition and connecting the ends operations correspond to matrix multiplication and taking the trace respectively.

Consider the subsets of the distributive lattice
J(P)
determined by the inclusion or exclusion of vertices
x_{L}
and
x_{R}
. Our notation is that the subscript on the left describes the exclusion (
0
) or inclusion (

) of

and the subscript on the right describes the inclusion or exclusion of

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{P};q):=

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in J(P)}q^{|I|}\qquad\qquad

{}_{0}\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{P};q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in J(P)\\

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{0}(\overrightarrow{P};q):=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in J(P)\\
x_{R}\notin I\end{subarray}}q^{|I|}\qquad

{}_{0}\mathfrak{R}_{0}(\overrightarrow{P};q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in J(P)\\
x_{L},x_{R}\notin I\end{subarray}}q^{|I|}

The superscripts can also be taken to be

x_{L}
or

Definition 3.1
.

\displaystyle{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}):=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{P};q)&-\mathfrak{R}_{1}(\overrightarrow{P};q)\\

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\overrightarrow{P}=(P,x_{L},x_{R})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\circlearrowright\!(\overrightarrow{P}))=\mathrm{tr}({\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}))

Proof.

The way we form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{P}\searrow\overrightarrow{Q};q)=\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{P};q)\,\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{Q};q)-\mathfrak{R}_{1}(\overrightarrow{P};q)\,_{0}\mathfrak{R}(\overrightarrow{Q};q).

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(M_{q}^{+}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2m}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\overline{\mathfrak{M}}_{q}(\alpha_{r/q})\cdot D_{0}\cdot U_{0})=\mathrm{tr}(U_{0}\cdot\overline{\mathfrak{M}}_{q}(\alpha_{r/q})\cdot D_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\overline{\mathfrak{M}}_{q}((a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2m})))=\overline{\mathfrak{R}}((a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2m});q).

Alternatively, one can consider the continuant expressions for the matrices

and

(see Propositions 5.2 and 5.3 from

) to verify directly that the entries match those of the rank matrices. ∎

The above proposition allows us to apply the discussion of unimodality of rank polynomials of circular fences to the traces of matrices in
\mathrm{PSL}_{q}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. We have one degenerate case of

Lemma 6.2

In the introduction, we have mentioned that all positive integer triples solving the Markov Diophantine equation

(t,s)
-oriented posets have rank matrices of size
2^{t}\times 2^{s}
, where rows are indexed by subsets of
[t]
and columns are indexed by subsets of
s
. When
t=s=4
we get the following matrices:

\displaystyle{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}R&-R_{\{1\}}&-R_{\{2\}}&R_{\{1,2\}}\\
{}_{\{1\}}R&-_{\{1\}}R_{\{1\}}&-_{\{1\}}R_{\{2\}}&{}_{1}R_{\{1,2\}}\\
{}_{\{2\}}R&-_{\{2\}}R_{\{1\}}&-_{\{2\}}R_{\{2\}}&{}_{\{2\}}R_{\{1,2\}}\\
{}_{\{1,2\}}R&-_{\{1,2\}}R_{\{1\}}&-_{\{1,2\}}R_{\{2\}}&{}_{\{1,2\}}R_{\{1,2\}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}R_{{\{1,2\}}^{*}}&R_{{\{2\}}^{*}}&R_{{\{1\}}^{*}}&R_{{\varnothing}^{*}}\\
{}_{\{1\}}R_{{\{1,2\}}^{*}}&{}_{\{1\}}R_{{\{2\}}^{*}}&{}_{\{1\}}R_{{\{1\}}^{*}}&{}_{1}R_{{\varnothing}^{*}}\\
{}_{\{2\}}R_{{\{1,2\}}^{*}}&{}_{\{2\}}R_{{\{2\}}^{*}}&{}_{\{2\}}R_{{\{1\}}^{*}}&{{}_{\{2\}}R_{{\varnothing}^{*}}}\\
{}_{\{1,2\}}R_{{\{1,2\}}^{*}}&{}_{\{1,2\}}R_{{\{2\}}^{*}}&{{}_{\{1,2\}}R_{{\{1\}}^{*}}}&{}_{\{1,2\}}R_{{\varnothing}^{*}}.\end{bmatrix}

More generally, the rank matrix and the dual rank matrix of a generalized (t,s)-oriented poset
\overrightarrow{P}
are matrices of size
2^{|L|}\times 2^{|R|}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}):=((-1)^{|B|}\,_{A}\mathfrak{R}_{B})_{A,B},\qquad{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}):=\,(_{A}\mathfrak{R}_{{B^{c}}^{*}})_{A,B}.

Here
A
and
B
range over subsets of
[t]
and
[s]
respectively:
\varnothing\subseteq A\subsetneq[t]
,
\varnothing\subsetneq B\subseteq[s]
. Note that when
t=s=1
, we recover the rank matrices we previously defined.

For a generalized
(t,s)
-oriented poset
\overrightarrow{P}=(P,L_{P},R_{P})
and a generalized
(s,r)
-oriented poset
\overrightarrow{Q}=(Q,L_{Q},R_{Q})
, we set
\overrightarrow{P}\searrow\overrightarrow{Q}=(S,L_{P},R_{Q})
where
S
is the poset given by connecting
P
and
Q
by adding relations
{R_{P}}_{i}\succeq{L_{P}}_{i}
for all
i\in S
. Also, when
t=s
we denote the structure obtained by adding the relations
{R}_{i}\succeq{L}_{i}
for all
i\in S
by
\circlearrowright\!(\overrightarrow{P})
.

Proposition 8.1
.

The rank matrices for generalized oriented posets satisfy the following:

\displaystyle{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}\searrow\overrightarrow{Q})

\displaystyle={\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})\cdot{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{Q}),\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{R}(\circlearrowright\!(\overrightarrow{P});q)=\mathrm{tr}({\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})),

\displaystyle{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}\nearrow\overrightarrow{Q})

\displaystyle={\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})\cdot{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{Q}),

\displaystyle{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}\nearrow\overrightarrow{Q})

\displaystyle={\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})\cdot{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{Q}),\qquad\qquad\mathfrak{R}(\circlearrowleft\!(\overrightarrow{P});q)=\mathrm{tr}({\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})),

\displaystyle{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P}\searrow\overrightarrow{Q})

\displaystyle={\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{P})\cdot{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}(\overrightarrow{Q}).

The proofs are similar to the case with
t=1
and
s=1
. The multiplication of matrices corresponds to adding the connecting relations, by inclusion exclusion for
{\triangledown}\mathfrak{R}_{q}
case, and adding up disjoint possiblities for the
{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\triangledown$}}\mathfrak{R}_{q}
case. The conditions on
L_{P}
and
R_{Q}
remain unchanged, so those sets are carried over as left and right connecting node sets of the product. The trace works similarly. One can go from one matrix to the other by right multiplication with an invertible matrix as in the
t=s=1
case, so that multiplying regular and dual rank matrices behaves nicely.

The choice of inclusion-exclusion for the regular rank matrix and taking disjoint possiblities for the dual addition is not dictated by what we want to achieve, but a particular choice made in order to match the existing notation.

As the size of the matrix increases exponentially with the size of
t
and
s
, the setting of generalized oriented matrices might not be very practical for studying posets. There are cases where it comes in very handy however. One case is to describe end-points of the poset where no more addition is planned. In that case one can take
s
or
t
to be
0
, and simplify calculations. If this is done at both left and right ends, one ends up with only the rank polynomial as the output. There is also the option of simplifying the matrices when the selected left and right end-points have relations among them, making some of the rows redundant. The simplest case is when one adds the condition that the nodes on
L
and
R
are chains. In that case, one can use
(s+1)\times(t+1)
matrices to convey the information needed for adding up posets.

9.
Comments and Further Directions

•

Rank Polynomials of Self-Dual Partitions:

The Ferrers diagram of a partition
\lambda
has a natural poset structure given by replacing the boxes with nodes of the poset, as seen in Figure
7
below. The ideals of the poset correspond to nodes below
\lambda
in the Hasse diagram of the Young Lattice, so the rank polynomial can be seen as the generating function of the size statistic on an interval in the Young lattice. It was conjectured in 1990 (see
[
15
]
) that if
\lambda
is a self-dual partition (it is equal to its transpose) then the corresponding rank polynomial is unimodal.

Figure 7.
The Young diagram for
(7,6,4,3,3,1)
(left) with corresponding poset (middle) and decomposition (right).

One can use the generalized rank matrices defined in Section
8
to decompose the poset for
\lambda
into one central rectangular lattice corresponding to the Durfee square of
\lambda
multiplied with chains of decreasing sizes from both sides. If
\lambda
is self-dual, as seen in Figure
7
, the added chains will be paired up, as will the corresponding matrices. It might be interesting to examine whether the symmetry of the structure can be used to make an argument towards unimodality.

•

Resolution of Conjecture
1.4
:
After this paper was shared on arXiv, the author, together with Ravichandran and Özel, was able to resolve Conjecture
1.4
about the unimodality of circular rank polynomials using characteristic polynomials of rank matrices. This is one of the results in the upcoming paper
[
6
]
. This also proves Conjecture
5.7
, fully characterizing the cases where
\mathrm{tr}(M_{q}(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}))
for
c_{i}\geq 2
is not
unimodal.

•

Generating functions and Cluster Algebras:
The rank polynomial keeps track of the number of elements of each ideal of a poset, but the machinery described here would work equally as well with any other statistic. In particular, for a poset on nodes
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
, one can replace the rank polynomial by the generating polynomial
\sum_{I}\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}
at each step to get the generating function of the ideals of the matrix.

This particular case has an application in cluster algebras, namely, it allows us to calculate the expansion formula of a curve in terms of a chosen basis using matrices. In the recent work
[
5
]
, the author and Emine Yıldırım were able to use oriented posets to obtain a matrix formulation for expansion formulas for curves in cluster algebras. This method is easily programmable and is able to give the results fairly quickly, especially in larger cases.

Connection to recent work:
After an initial version of this work was shared on the arXiv, there were some developments that might lead to future connections. The recent paper on higher dimer covers has a larger matrix expression related to cluster algebras
[
16
]
. We believe that an expansion of the weight matrices from
[
5
]
to the generalized case that takes into account the particular nature of the underlying posets might provide a
q
-deformation for said matrix, as well as resolve some questions posed by the authors. Differential operators corresponding to
T_{q}
and
S_{q}
are used in
[
17
]
to give a
q
-deformation of the Witt algebra. Leclere and Morier-Genoud also developed a new combinatorial model to study the numerators and denominators of
q
-deformed rationals using triangulated polygons and annuli. The connections between this model and the related cluster expansions might be interesting to explore.
