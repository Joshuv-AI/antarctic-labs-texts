Presentations for Temperley-Lieb algebras
James East

Abstract
We give a new and conceptually straightforward proof of the well-known presentation for the Temperley-Lieb algebra, via an alternative new presentation. Our method involves twisted semigroup algebras, and we make use of two apparently new submonoids of the Temperley-Lieb monoid. Keywords : Temperley-Lieb algebras, Temperley-Lieb monoids, planar tangles, presentations. MSC: 16S15, 20M05, 20M20, 16S36, 05E15, 57M99.



Dedicated to the memory of Prof. Vaughan F. R. Jones.

Introduction

Temperley-Lieb algebras were introduced by their namesakes in
[
21
]
to study lattice models in statistical mechanics. These algebras have since appeared naturally and extensively in many mathematical contexts, and have found particularly strong applications in knot theory; see especially the works of Kauffman and Jones, such as
[
13
,
11
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
14
,
16
]
. More information may be found in surveys such as
[
1
,
20
,
17
]
; the introductions to
[
22
,
3
]
also contain valuable discussions.

One of the most important tools for working with Temperley-Lieb algebras is a well-known presentation by generators and relations (stated in Theorem
2.3
below), which provides an algebraic axiomatisation of the topological definition in terms of homotopy-classes of planar tangles.
As far as the author is aware, the only complete proof of this presentation is the one given by Borisavljević, Došen and Petrić in
[
3
]
, where they also discuss the lack of a proof up to that point, even (surprisingly) of the fact that the so-called
hooks
or
diapsides
form a generating set; see the tangles denoted
\overline{e}_{i}
in Figure
3
. The proof given in
[
3
]
involves working with a (countably infinite) monoid of tangles in place of the algebra itself, and is rather ingeneous, albeit quite involved, as the authors themselves note at the end of their introduction:
“Our proof exhibits some difficulties, which we think cannot be evaded”
.

The main purpose of the current article is to give an alternative proof of the presentation, which we believe exhibits no such difficulties. Our method also involves replacing the algebra with a monoid, but this time with a finite one, the so-called
Temperley-Lieb monoid
,
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
. This monoid is sometimes called the
Jones monoid
in the literature, and denoted
\mathcal{J}_{n}
; see for example
[
5
,
2
,
8
,
9
]
, and especially
[
18
]
for a discussion of naming conventions.
The main innovation in our proof is in the use of two apparently new submonoids
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
of
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
; roughly speaking, these each capture half of the complexity of
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
itself, and we have a natural factorisation
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}
(Proposition
4.1
). We first give presentations for
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
in Section
3
(Theorem
3.4
), and in Section
4
show how to stitch these together to obtain a new presentation for
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
(Theorem
2.1
). In Section
5
, we show how to rewrite the new presentation to obtain the original one (Theorem
2.2
), and in Section
2.3
explain how to convert any presentation for the monoid into a presentation for the algebra, using general results on twisted semigroup algebras
[
7
]
.

Some aspects of the proof presented here bear similarities to that given in
[
3
]
, the most obvious being the normal forms used; compare our Lemma
4.6
with
[
3
, Lemmas 1 and 4–6]
. Although these forms utilise different generating sets, they both capture natural combinatorial data associated to the strings of a tangle, as encoded in certain tuples defined in Section
2.4
; however, our normal forms have length at most
n
, while those of
[
3
]
grow quadratically in
n
.
As an application of our results, we are also able to establish the (well-known) equivalence of the topological framework of tangles and the combinatorial approach via set partitions; compare our Corollary
4.2
with
[
3
, Remark 3]
.
Beyond these parallels, however, the author believes that the current proof is rather simpler, and hopes it may therefore be of some benefit.

2.1
Temperley-Lieb monoids and algebras

For the duration of the article, we fix a positive integer
n
, and write
{\bf n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. To avoid trivialities we assume that
n\geq 3
.

For
i\in{\bf n}
, we define the points in the plane by
P_{i}=(i,1)
and
P_{i}^{\prime}=(i,0)
.
A
string
is a smooth non-self-intersecting embedding
\mathfrak{s}
of the unit interval
[0,1]
into the rectangle
\mathscr{R}_{n}=[1,n]\times[0,1]
such that

•

\mathfrak{s}(0),\mathfrak{s}(1)\in\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},P_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,P_{n}^{\prime}\}
, and

•

other than these endpoints, the image of
\mathfrak{s}
is contained in the interior of
\mathscr{R}_{n}
.

A
planar tangle
is a collection
\alpha=\{\mathfrak{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{n}\}
of
n
pairwise non-intersecting strings. Such a planar tangle is typically identified with the union of the images of its strings, which is a collection of
n
curves contained in
\mathscr{R}_{n}
. Note that
\{\mathfrak{s}_{1}(0),\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{n}(0),\mathfrak{s}_{1}(1),\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{n}(1)\}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},P_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,P_{n}^{\prime}\}
. Some examples with
n=9
are given in Figure
1
.

Two planar tangles
\alpha=\{\mathfrak{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{n}\}
and
\beta=\{\mathfrak{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{t}_{n}\}
are
equivalent
, written as
\alpha\equiv\beta
, if there is a homotopy through planar tangles from
\alpha
to
\beta
, or more formally if there is a family of continuous maps
F_{i}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\mathscr{R}_{n}
,
i\in{\bf n}
, such that, relabelling the strings if necessary, and writing
\mathfrak{s}_{i}^{u}(x)=F_{i}(u,x)
for
i\in{\bf n}
and

,

•

\gamma_{u}=(\mathfrak{s}_{1}^{u},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{n}^{u})
is a planar tangle for all
u\in[0,1]
,

•

\gamma_{0}=\alpha
and
\gamma_{1}=\beta
.

We denote by
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
the set of all
\equiv
-classes of planar tangles.

The product
\alpha\beta
of two planar tangles
\alpha
and
\beta
is formed by shifting
\alpha
one unit in the positive

-direction, attaching it to
\beta
, rescaling to the whole object until it lies in the rectangle

, and finally removing any loops contained entirely in the interior of
\mathscr{R}_{n}
. (For later use, let

be the number of such loops.) Figure
1
gives an example calculation with
n=9
(and where

). It is clear that
\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\equiv\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}
whenever
\alpha_{1}\equiv\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1}\equiv\beta_{2}
. Thus, we have an induced product on
\equiv
-classes, which is easily seen to be associative. It follows that
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
is a semigroup under this product, indeed a monoid whose identity is the
\equiv
-class of the planar tangle
\operatorname{id}_{n}
with
n
vertical strings. This monoid is called the

)
. In what follows, we typically identify a planar tangle with its

-class.

2.2
Presentations

For a set
X
, we write
X^{*}
for the free monoid over
X
, which consists of all words over
X
under concatenation; the empty word will be denoted by
1
. For a set
\Omega\subseteq X^{*}\times X^{*}
of pairs of words, we write
\Omega^{\sharp}
for the congruence on
X^{*}
generated by
\Omega
. We say a monoid
M
has presentation
\langle X:\Omega\rangle
if
M\cong X^{*}/\Omega^{\sharp}
: i.e., if there is a monoid surmorphism
X^{*}\to M
with kernel
\Omega^{\sharp}
. If
\phi
is such a surmorphism, we say
M
has presentation
\langle X:\Omega\rangle
via
\phi
. Elements of
X
and
\Omega
are called generators and relations, respectively, and a relation
(u,v)\in\Omega
will often be denoted as an equation:
u=v
.

If
\Bbbk
is a field, then the free (unital)
\Bbbk
-algebra over
X
is
\Bbbk[X^{*}]
, the semigroup algebra of
X^{*}
. For
{\Omega\subseteq\Bbbk[X^{*}]\times\Bbbk[X^{*}]}
, we say an algebra
A
has presentation
\langle X:\Omega\rangle
if there is an algebra surmorphism
\phi:\Bbbk[X^{*}]\to A
with kernel (in the vector space sense) spanned by
\{u\phi-v\phi:(u,v)\in\Omega\}
.

2.4
Partitions and tuples

Certain combinatorial data associated to planar tangles will be of use throughout. First, we write
{\bf n}^{\prime}=\{1^{\prime},\ldots,n^{\prime}\}
. To each string
\mathfrak{s}
of

.

\mathfrak{s}

.

•

If
\mathfrak{s}
joins

to

, then

In fact, this partition provides more information than is strictly necessary.

3
The monoids
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}

In this section and the next we prove Theorem
2.1
, which gives a presentation for the Temperley-Lieb monoid
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
. Our strategy involves two submonoids
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
of
\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}
; these will be defined shortly, and the main goal of the current section is to give presentations for them (Theorem
3.4
). These presentations will then be used in the next section, together with a factorisation
{\mathcal{T}\!\mathcal{L}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{n}\mathcal{R}_{n}}
(Proposition
4.1
), to complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
. The approach just described is based on that of
[
6
]
, which employed similar methods to treat monoids of (planar/order-preserving) partial bijections.

We begin by defining the submonoids
\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
