Fluctuating viscoelasticity based on a finite number of dumbbells
Markus Hütter, Peter D. Olmsted, Daniel J. Read

Abstract
Two alternative routes are taken to derive, on the basis of the dynamics of a finite number of dumbbells, viscoelasticity in terms of a conformation tensor with fluctuations. The first route is a direct approach using stochastic calculus only, and it serves as a benchmark for the second route, which is guided by thermodynamic principles. In the latter, the Helmholtz free energy and a generalized relaxation tensor play a key role. It is shown that the results of the two routes agree only if a finite-size contribution to the Helmholtz free energy of the conformation tensor is taken into account. Using statistical mechanics, this finite-size contribution is derived explicitly in this paper for a large class of models; this contribution is non-zero whenever the number of dumbbells in the volume of observation is finite. It is noted that the generalized relaxation tensor for the conformation tensor does not need any finite-size correction.

Introduction

Fluctuations are particularly important when studying small systems. This also holds for fluids, including complex fluids, e.g., macromolecular and polymeric liquids. Small scales are involved, e.g., in microrheology
squiresmason2010
and micro- and nanofluidic devices
micro1
;
micro2
. For Newtonian fluids, i.e., fluids with a deformation-independent viscosity and a lack of memory, the dynamics on small scales could be described in terms of the fluctuating Newtonian fluid dynamics developed by Landau and Lifshitz
LL1959
. However, this is not sufficient for complex fluids, and thus extensions are needed. For example, the stress tensor has been related to the rate-of-strain tensor by a memory kernel, and correspondingly colored noise has been introduced on the stress tensor
hohenegger2017
;
hohenegger2017addtl
. Another approach towards modeling fluctuating effects in complex fluids has been taken by
Vázquez-Quesada, Ellero, and Español
vazquezquesadaetal2009
and applied to microrheology
vazquezquesadaetal2012
, in which smoothed-particle hydrodynamics is extended by a conformation tensor that describes the conformation of the small number of polymer chains per volume element. The concept of fluctuating dynamics for the conformation tensor has been extended recently
HHA2018
, to make it applicable not only to the Maxwell model
berisedwards1990b
;
wapperomhulsen1998
, as in
vazquezquesadaetal2009
;
vazquezquesadaetal2012
, but to a wider class of models, e.g. the FENE-P model
wapperomhulsen1998
;
wedgewoodbird1988
and the Giesekus model
wapperomhulsen1998
;
Giesekus1982RA
;
Giesekus1982JNNFM
;
birdwiest1985
. In the approach taken in
HHA2018
, the Helmholtz free energy in terms of the conformation tensor plays an essential role.

The dynamics of the conformation tensor roots in a finer description, in particular, it can be related to the kinetic theory of dumbbells (e.g., see chapter 13 in
birdbook
). The question addressed in this paper is what lessons can be learned from deriving the dynamics for the conformation tensor with fluctuations from an underlying kinetic description for a finite number of dumbbells. It is pointed out that the dumbbell description already contains the relaxation and fluctuation effects that are relevant also on the conformation-tensor level. This is in contrast to coarse graining from an atomistic description to bead-spring chains or directly to the conformation tensor, e.g., see the work of Underhill and Doyle
underhilldoyle2004
and of Ilg
et al.

ilg2008
;
ilg2009
, respectively, without fluctuations on the conformation-tensor level.

The paper is organized as follows. In sect.
2
, a certain class of kinetic dumbbell models is introduced, based on which a description of fluctuating viscoelasticity in terms of the conformation tensor is derived via a direct route, for a finite number of dumbbells. This route is paralleled in sect.
3

As part of that, the finite-size correction to the Helmholtz free energy is calculated, and this is found to be essential for finding agreement between the two approaches. In the appendix, three different calculation methods for this free energy are detailed, each of which arrives at the same result. In sect.
4

HHA2018
;
publ72
;
HCHA2020

5

3
Fluctuating viscoelasticity derived using thermodynamics

The main idea in taking a thermodynamic approach to modeling dynamical systems is that one can concentrate on key ingredients for the static and dynamic properties, and the thermodynamic approach makes sure that these ingredients are processed towards the final model in a consistent way. For the dumbbell dynamics (
1
), the key ingredients are the potential
\Phi
and the mobility tensor
\bm{M}
. In contrast, for the dynamics of
\bm{c}
, the key ingredients are the Helmholtz free energy density
\psi
and the generalized relaxation tensor
\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}
(see
HHA2018
for details). In particular, the dynamics for the fluctuating conformation-tensor
\bm{c}
is given by the SDE

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\bm{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{c}+\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\kappa}^{{\rm{T}}}-\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}\odot\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{c}}\right){\rm{d}}t

(17)

\displaystyle+\frac{k_{\rm{B}}T}{V}{\rm div}_{\bm{c}}\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}{\rm{d}}t+\sqrt{\frac{2k_{\rm{B}}T}{V}}\bm{B}^{(4)}\odot{\rm{d}}\tilde{\bm{W}}\,,

where
{\rm div}_{\bm{c}}\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}
denotes the divergence of
\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}
in
\bm{c}
-space, the order-four tensor
\bm{B}^{(4)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{(4)}\odot\bm{B}^{(4),{\rm{T}}}=\bm{\Lambda}^{(4)}\,,

(18)

and

is a tensor with increments of independent Wiener processes (see

3.1

4.1
Eliminating degrees of freedom

Above, the relation has been established between the dynamics formulated in terms of dumbbell vectors, on the one hand, and in terms of the conformation tensor, on the other hand. In this section, the relation of the dumbbell-vector description to the multiplicative decomposition of the conformation tensor (e.g., see
HHA2018

HCHA2020

In view of the expression (

5
Discussion and conclusions

The focus of this paper has been on deriving viscoelasticity in terms of a conformation tensor with fluctuations, based on the kinetic theory of dumbbells. This has been achieved by identifying the conformation tensor with the arithmetic average over a finite number
N
of dumbbells, eq. (
5
), and using two alternative routes for deriving the dynamics: a direct approach using stochastic calculus, and a thermodynamic approach, in which the Helmholtz free energy plays a key role. It has been shown that these two approaches agree only if a finite-size contribution to the Helmholtz free energy of the conformation tensor is taken into account.
The main messages of this paper are therefore the following:

•

If the number
N
of dumbbells is
finite
, the commonly employed expressions for the thermodynamic potentials need to be corrected: Using statistical mechanics (see Appendix
B
), one finds that the conformational entropy must be corrected by replacing
N
by
N-D-1
(with
D
the number of spatial dimensions), which in turn modifes the Helmholtz free energy, see eq. (
24
) with correction term eq. (
25
).

•

The thereby obtained finite-size correction in the free energy is crucial for guaranteeing compatibility between the dynamics of the conformation tensor and of the underlying dumbbells.

While these general conclusions have been established in general terms for a large class of models, they have also been exemplified for the Hookean (Maxwell) model, the FENE-P model, and the Giesekus model (see Appendix
A
); the dynamics for the conformation tensor with fluctuations for these three models is summarized in table

\bm{c}
-dynamics contains a finite-size contribution, the same holds true also for the thermodynamic potential for the
\bm{b}_{3}
-dynamics, see sect. 4.3 in
HHA2018
for details. Beyond these implications for the thermodynamics of a
\bm{b}_{3}
-formulation, the kinetic models for
N
dumbbells have also been employed in this paper to give an explanation for the existence of a rotational relaxation time proportional to
N
in the fluctuating dynamics of
\bm{b}_{3}
, which has been observed earlier
HCHA2020
.

) for

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\hat{\bm{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\kappa}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}+\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\kappa}^{\rm{T}}-\frac{4H}{\zeta}\left(\hat{\bm{c}}-\frac{k_{\rm{B}}T}{H}\bm{1}\right)\right]{\rm{d}}t+{\rm{d}}\hat{\bm{c}}^{\rm{f}}\,,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\hat{\bm{c}}

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\kappa}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}+\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\kappa}^{\rm{T}}-\frac{4H}{\zeta}\left(f\hat{\bm{c}}-\frac{k_{\rm{B}}T}{H}\bm{1}\right)\right]{\rm{d}}t+{\rm{d}}\hat{\bm{c}}^{\rm{f}}\,.

Using the potential
\Phi
in eq. (
8
) with eq. (
7
) in the limit

\displaystyle+\frac{4\alpha(D+1)k_{\rm{B}}T}{N\zeta}\hat{\bm{c}}{\rm{d}}t+{\rm{d}}\hat{\bm{c}}^{\rm{f}}\,,

Appendix B
Calculation of partition function
\Gamma

In this Appendix, three procedures are presented for calculating the microcanonical partition function
\Gamma
, eq. (
22
), for a finite number
N
of dumbbells.

B.1
Procedure 1: Differential equation

We start by noting that the microcanonical partition function
\Gamma
, eq. (
22
), contains only
K=D(D+1)/2
(rather than
D^{2}
) Dirac
\delta
-functions, representative
of the constraints related to the independent components

c_{ij}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq D
. The following notation is introduced:

\displaystyle\Delta_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left(\hat{c}_{ij}-c_{ij}\right)\,,\qquad i\leq j\,,

(B.1)

\displaystyle\Delta^{[K]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k,l=1;k\leq l}^{D}\Delta_{kl}\,,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k,l=1;k\leq l;k\neq i;l\neq j}^{D}\Delta_{kl}\,,\qquad i\leq j\,,

(B.3)

so that
\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ij}\Delta_{ij}=\Delta^{[K]}
. With eq. (
B.2
), the microcanonical partition function can be written in the form

\displaystyle\Gamma=\int\Delta^{[K]}\,d^{DN}Q\,.

(B.4)

One can show that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ij}}=-\int\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ij}\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}\,d^{DN}Q\,,\qquad i\leq j\,,

(B.5)

where
\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}
denotes the derivative of
\Delta_{ij}
w.r.t. its argument. Furthermore, one can derive the following relations, where
Q_{\mu,k}
is the
k^{\textrm{th}}
component of the
\mu^{\textrm{th}}
dumbbell,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,k}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial Q_{\mu,k}}=\left(\delta_{ik}\hat{c}_{jk}+\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_{ik}\right)\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}\,,\qquad i\leq j\,,

(B.6)

of which there are three non-zero cases:

\displaystyle i=j=k:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ii}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}=2\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{ii}c_{ii}-\Delta_{ii}\right)\,,

(B.7)

\displaystyle i<j,k=i:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}=\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}c_{ij}-\Delta_{ij}\,,

(B.8)

\displaystyle i<j,k=j:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,j}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial Q_{\mu,j}}=\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}c_{ij}-\Delta_{ij}\,.

(B.9)

In calculating eqs. (
B.7
)–(
B.9
), we have made use of the identity

x\delta^{\prime}(x)=-\delta(x)
,
with
x=\hat{c}_{ij}-c_{ij}
, which implies

(\hat{c}_{ij}-c_{ij})\delta^{\prime}(\hat{c}_{ij}-c_{ij})=-\delta(\hat{c}_{ij}-c_{ij})
, from which it follows
\hat{c}_{ij}\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}=c_{ij}\Delta^{\prime}_{ij}-\Delta_{ij}
.
To proceed, we use eq. (
B.5
) for
i=j
and eq. (
B.7
) to derive an expression for
2c_{ii}(\partial\Gamma/\partial c_{ii})
,

\displaystyle 2c_{ii}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ii}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}\left[\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ii}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}+2\Delta_{ii}\right]\,d^{DN}Q

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\Gamma-\int\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}\left[\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ii}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}\right]\,d^{DN}Q\,,

where we have used
\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}\Delta_{ii}=\Delta^{[K]}
. The remaining integral is then re-written by performing an integration by parts w.r.t.
Q_{\mu,i}
; the corresponding boundary-terms can be neglected if the values of the components of
\bm{c}
are finite. This leads to

\displaystyle 2c_{ii}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ii}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(N-2)\Gamma+\int\Delta_{ii}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}\,d^{DN}Q\,,

where, again, we have used
\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}\Delta_{ii}=\Delta^{[K]}
.
Using the product rule for calculating the derivative
\partial\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ii}/\partial Q_{{\mu,i}}
and rearranging terms results in

\displaystyle 2c_{ii}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ii}}=(N-2)\Gamma+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}G_{ji}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{D}G^{\star}_{ij}\,,

(B.12)

where the quantities
G_{ji}
and
G^{\star}_{ij}
are given by

\displaystyle G_{ji}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ji}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ji}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}\,d^{DN}Q

(B.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{ji}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ji}}-\Gamma\,,\qquad j<i\,,

\displaystyle G^{\star}_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int\Delta^{[K-1]}_{ij}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}Q_{\mu,i}\frac{\partial\Delta_{ij}}{\partial Q_{\mu,i}}\,d^{DN}Q

(B.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c_{ij}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ij}}-\Gamma\,,\qquad i<j\,,

where we have used eq. (
B.8
) and eq. (
B.9
) (with
i
and
j
interchanged), and then eq. (
B.4
) and eq. (
B.5
). Combining eq. (
B.12
) with eq. (
B.14
) and eq. (
B.13
), one obtains

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{ji}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ji}}+2c_{ii}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ii}}+\sum_{j=i+1}^{D}c_{ij}\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{ij}}=(N-D-1)\Gamma\,,

(B.15)

which is a differential equation for
\Gamma
. To solve this equation, consider the ansatz

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}\left(\det\bm{c}\right)^{\nu}\,.

(B.16)

For calculating the partial derivatives of this ansatz w.r.t.
c_{ij}
, the following needs to be kept in mind. On the one hand, since
\bm{c}
is symmetric, there are only
K
independent variables, rather than
D^{2}
, which is in line with the strategy adopted, e.g., in eq. (
B.2
) and eq. (
B.3
). This implies that there are only
K
partial derivatives to be calculated in eq. (
B.15
), namely w.r.t.
c_{ij}
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq D
. On the other hand, the tensor
\bm{c}
contains elements
c_{ij}
and
c_{ji}=c_{ij}
.
Hence, for
i\neq j
, one finds for any function
f(\bm{c})
:
\partial f(\bm{c})/\partial c_{ij}=[\partial f(\bm{c})/\partial c_{ij}]_{\rm no-sym}+[\partial f(\bm{c})/\partial c_{ji}]_{\rm no-sym}
, where “no-sym” emphasizes that the corresponding derivative is taken without enforcing the symmetry of
\bm{c}
, i.e., considering all
D^{2}
components of
\bm{c}
as independent variables. For the derivative of ansatz (
B.16
) one thus obtains

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial c_{kl}}=(2-\delta_{kl})\nu\Gamma\left(\bm{c}^{-1}\right)_{kl}\,,\qquad k\leq l\,,

(B.17)

where the factor
(2-\delta_{kl})
originates from the fact that there are only
K
independent components in
\bm{c}
,
c_{kl}
with
k\leq l
; particularly, for
k<l
, the variable
c_{kl}
appears at two off-diagonal positions in the full, symmetric matrix
\bm{c}
. With this, it can be shown that the ansatz (
B.16
) is indeed a solution of the differential equation (
B.15
) if

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{N-D-1}{2}\,.

(B.18)

The
\bm{c}
-independent prefactor
\Gamma_{0}
can not be determined with this approach. However, since
\Gamma_{0}
results only in an additive contribution to the Helmholtz free energy
\Psi
, it turns out to be irrelevant for the dynamics of the conformation tensor
\bm{c}
.

B.2
Procedure 2: Geometry

We begin with a geometrical interpretation of the microcanonical partition-function
\Gamma(\bm{c})
defined via eqs. (
21
) and (
22
). Substituting from eq. (
21
) back into the full partition function eq. (
20
) we find:

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int e^{-\Phi(\bm{c})/(k_{\rm{B}}T)}\,\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{K}c

(B.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int e^{-\Phi(\hat{\bm{c}})/(k_{\rm{B}}T)}\,d^{DN}Q.

Comparing the two lines of this equation we recognise that we may interpret the differential quantity
\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{K}c
as being the volume, within the
DN
dimensional
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space, that is within an increment
d^{K}c
of
\bm{c}
(i.e. where the
K
independent components of
\bm{c}
are each varied within an interval
dc_{\alpha}
of their base value). Evaluation of the dependence of this volume on
\bm{c}
yields
\Gamma(\bm{c})
up to a constant prefactor. Since this derivation requires some visual imagination, we first demonstrate how this may be reasoned in the specific case
D=3
before indicating how the calculation may be generalised to arbitrary
D
.

B.2.1
Three dimensions

For
D=3
,
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space is a
3N
dimensional space spanned by the vectors
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1,\ldots,N}
. For convenience, we specify a location within this space via the
3N
dimensional vector
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
, where
\mathbf{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\ldots,x_{N})
is the
N
-dimensional vector of the
x
-components of
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1,\ldots,N}
(and similarly for
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
).

We also note that, for
D=3
, there are
K=6
independent components of the symmetric
\bm{c}
tensor:
c_{xx}
,
c_{yy}
,
c_{zz}
,
c_{xy}
,
c_{xz}
and
c_{yz}
.

We proceed by evaluating the dependence on
\bm{c}
of two separate volumes. We first evaluate
V_{\mathrm{const}}
, which is the
(3N-6)
-dimensional subvolume of
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space within which
\hat{\bm{c}}
(given in terms of
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
by eq.
5
) is held exactly equal to
\bm{c}
. Then, for each point within
V_{\mathrm{const}}
, we find the volume
dV_{6}
which is the
6
-dimensional subvolume swept out by varying
c_{xx}
by
dc_{xx}
,
c_{yy}
by
dc_{yy}
, etc. Such excursions must all be perpendicular to the subvolume
V_{\mathrm{const}}
(in the
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space), because contours of fixed
c_{xx}
are perpendicular to the gradient direction of
c_{xx}
(etc.). So, we can evaluate
\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{6}\bm{c}
as the product of
V_{\mathrm{const}}
and
dV_{6}
:

\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{6}\bm{c}=V_{\mathrm{const}}\ dV_{6}\,.

Evaluation of these volumes is most straightforward in the co-ordinate frame in which

\bm{c}
is diagonalised (which can always be done since
\bm{c}
is symmetric).
In the diagonal frame,
\bm{c}
takes values
\lambda_{x}
,
\lambda_{y}
and
\lambda_{z}

along the diagonal, but is zero in the off diagonal components. In terms of the
N
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
these constraints can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{X}\right|^{2}=\Lambda_{x}\mathrm{,~}\left|\mathbf{Y}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{y}\mathrm{,~}\left|\mathbf{Z}\right|^{2}=\Lambda_{z},

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}=0\mathrm{,~}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle=0\mathrm{,~}\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Z}=0,

where
\Lambda_{i}=N\lambda_{i}
, i.e. the vectors
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
are restricted to the surfaces of
N
-dimensional hyperspheres of radii
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}
,
\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}}
, and
\sqrt{\Lambda_{z}}
respectively, whilst simultaneously being held to be mutually perpendicular.

The volume
V_{\mathrm{const}}
is the
(3N-6)
-dimensional volume swept out by rotating the
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
vectors subject to the above constraints, the rotations being within the
N
-dimensional space of these vectors. A brief analogy may help at this stage: the surface of a sphere of radius
r
is found by summing up small tiles formed by varying the polar co-ordinate angles
\theta
and
\phi
by small increments
d\theta
and
d\phi
. The distance along the sphere surface moved during such increments is
rd\theta
and
rd\phi
(multiplied by a geometric factor
\sin\theta
which is irrelevant to the scaling with
r
). So, the scaling of surface area with
r
can be found from the product of these lengths,
r^{2}d\theta d\phi
. Likewise, the volume
V_{\mathrm{const}}
is the sum over a tiling of small incremental volumes, made by rotating the vectors
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
by small angles
d\theta_{k}
in each available direction, whilst keeping their length fixed and retaining their mutually perpendicular orientation.
Since
\mathbf{X}
has
N
dimensions, there are in total
(N-1)
directions in which
\mathbf{X}
could be rotated whilst keeping the length of
\mathbf{X}
fixed. One such rotation will rotate the
\mathbf{X}
vector towards the
\mathbf{Y}
vector. In this case, the
\mathbf{Y}
vector must also rotate by the same angle, so as to maintain the perpendicular condition
\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}=0
.
Hence, for rotation angle
d\theta_{1}
,
\mathbf{X}
rotates so that its end sweeps out a length
dl_{X}=\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}d\theta_{1}
perpendicular to
\mathbf{X}
(in the direction of
\mathbf{Y}
). Likewise
\mathbf{Y}
also rotates so that its end sweeps out a length
dl_{Y}=\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}}d\theta_{1}
perpendicular to
\mathbf{Y}
(in the direction of
-\mathbf{X}
). These changes in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
result in a total length moved in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space which is

\displaystyle dl_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{dl_{X}^{2}+dl_{Y}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}d\theta_{1}.

Similarly a second rotation direction of the
\mathbf{X}
vector is available, towards the
\mathbf{Z}
vector (in which case
\mathbf{Z}
must also rotate). Likewise, a third rotation carries
\mathbf{Y}
towards
\mathbf{Z}
without rotating
\mathbf{X}
. By similar arguments, the total length moved in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space for these is:

\displaystyle dl_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{z}}d\theta_{2},

\displaystyle dl_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}+\Lambda_{z}}d\theta_{3}.

These three rotation directions having been dealt with, there remain
(N-3)
further rotation directions available for the
\mathbf{X}
vector, all of which allow
\mathbf{X}
to remain perpendicular to both
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
. For each of these rotations of
\mathbf{X}
, the length moved in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space is

dl_{k}=\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}d\theta_{k},\,k=4\ldots N.

Similarly, there are
(N-3)
rotations available to each of
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
, each of which leaves the other vectors unchanged, giving lengths:

\displaystyle dl_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}}d\theta_{k},\,k=(N+1)\ldots(2N-3),

\displaystyle dl_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Lambda_{z}}d\theta_{k},\,k=(2N-2)\ldots(3N-6).

A single “tile” in the volume
V_{\mathrm{const}}
is obtained by sweeping through each of the above
(3N-6)
incremental rotations. The total volume
V_{\mathrm{const}}
is obtained by adding together all such tiles as the vectors are rotated. We require only the dependence of
V_{\mathrm{const}}
on
\bm{c}
(ignoring prefactors). Hence, by taking the product of the lengths swept out by a set of incremental rotations, we obtain:

\prod_{k=1}^{3N-6}dl_{k}\sim V_{\mathrm{const}}\prod_{k=1}^{3N-6}d\theta_{k}

where

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{const}}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}}\right)^{(N-3)}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{z}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}+\Lambda_{z}}.

(B.20)

We now turn to the volume
dV_{6}
, the
6
-dimensional subvolume swept out by incrementing
c_{xx}
by
dc_{xx}
,
c_{yy}
by
dc_{yy}
, etc. away from a single point within
V_{\mathrm{const}}
. We first calculate the length in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space traversed by incrementing
c_{xx}
by
dc_{xx}
in a direction perpendicular to the contour of constant
c_{xx}
. We note that

c_{xx}=\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{X}/N,

so the direction perpendicular to the contour of constant
c_{xx}
is

\nabla c_{xx}=(2\mathbf{X}/N,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\,.

Moving a distance
dl_{xx}
in this direction changes
c_{xx}
by

\displaystyle dc_{xx}

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla c_{xx}\right|dl_{xx}

\displaystyle=(2\left|\mathbf{X}\right|/N)dl_{xx}

\displaystyle=(2\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}/N)dl_{xx}.

Inverting this, the distance moved in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space when incrementing
c_{xx}
by
dc_{xx}
is

dl_{xx}=\frac{N}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}}dc_{xx}

and similarly for
dc_{yy}
and
dc_{zz}
.

We next note that

c_{xy}=\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}/N,

so the direction perpendicular to the contour of constant
c_{xy}
is

\nabla c_{xy}=(\mathbf{Y}/N,\mathbf{X}/N,\mathbf{0})

Moving a distance
dl_{xy}
in this direction changes
c_{xy}
by

\displaystyle dc_{xy}

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla c_{xy}\right|dl_{xy}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{\left|\mathbf{X}\right|^{2}+\left|\mathbf{Y}\right|^{2}}/N\right)dl_{xy}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}/N)dl_{xy}.

Inverting this, the distance moved in
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space when incrementing
c_{xy}
by
dc_{xy}
is

dl_{xy}=\frac{N}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}}dc_{xy}

and similarly for
dc_{xz}
and
dc_{yz}
.

Hence, multiplying these six lengths together,

dV_{6}\sim\frac{N^{6}d^{6}\bm{c}}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{z}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}+\Lambda_{z}}}.

(B.21)

Evaluating
\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{6}\bm{c}=V_{\mathrm{const}}\ dV_{6}
, and hence
\Gamma(\bm{c})
, we find that all factors of form
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}
cancel, and we are left with

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle N^{6}\left(\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}\right)^{(N-4)/2}

(B.22)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle N^{3N/2}\left(\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}\lambda_{z}\right)^{(N-4)/2}.

But
\mathrm{det}\bm{c}=\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}\lambda_{z}
, and so:

\Gamma(\bm{c})\sim N^{3N/2}\left(\mathrm{det}\bm{c}\right)^{(N-4)/2}

(B.23)

with dependence on
\mathrm{det}\bm{c}
as required.

B.2.2
Generalisation to
D
dimensions

Most of the above formalism carries directly over to
D
dimensions.
In calculating
V_{\mathrm{const}}
, each vector of type
\mathbf{X}
has a total number of
(N-1)
rotations available (whilst preserving length), but
(D-1)
of these rotate the vector towards one of the others. So, the total number of rotations available for each vector, which do not also require rotating one of the other vectors, is
(N-D)
. The number of rotations requiring two vectors to rotate (i.e. of the form
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{Y}
,
\mathbf{Y}
to
\mathbf{Z}
etc., but avoiding double counting) is
M=D(D-1)/2
. So,
V_{\mathrm{const}}
is of form:

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{const}}\sim

\displaystyle\left(\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}\ldots\right)^{(N-D)/2}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{z}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}+\Lambda_{z}}\ldots,

where there are
M
terms of form
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}
.

In calculating
dV_{K}
, there are
D
diagonal constraints and
M=D(D-1)/2
off-diagonal constraints in
\bm{c}
(a total of
K=D(D+1)/2
). Each diagonal constraint gives a factor of form
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}}
in the denominator of
dV_{K}
, whilst each off diagonal constraint gives a factor of form
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}
. Hence:

dV_{K}\sim\frac{N^{K}d^{K}\bm{c}}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}\ldots}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{z}}\sqrt{\Lambda_{y}+\Lambda_{z}}\ldots}.

In the product
\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{K}\bm{c}=V_{\mathrm{const}}\ dV_{K}
, all
M
factors of form
\sqrt{\Lambda_{x}+\Lambda_{y}}
are present once in the numerator, and once in the denominator, and so cancel.
Hence:

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})\,d^{K}\bm{c}

\displaystyle=V_{\mathrm{const}}.dV_{K}

\displaystyle\sim N^{K}\left(\Lambda_{x}\Lambda_{y}\Lambda_{z}\ldots\right)^{(N-D-1)/2}d^{K}\bm{c}

\displaystyle\sim N^{DN/2}\left(\lambda_{x}\lambda_{y}\lambda_{z}\ldots\right)^{(N-D-1)/2}d^{K}\bm{c}

\displaystyle\sim N^{DN/2}\left(\mathrm{det}\bm{c}\right)^{(N-D-1)/2}d^{K}\bm{c}.

(B.24)

B.3
Procedure 3: Scaling

In this procedure, scaling arguments are employed for the calculation of the microcanonical partition function
\Gamma
, eq. (
22
), in real space and in reciprocal space, respectively.

B.3.1
Scaling in real space

Similar to the procedure described in sec.
B.2
, it is again chosen to describe the
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
-space in terms of the
N
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{X}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq D
, where the
\mu
-th component of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
equals the
i
-th component of
\bm{Q}_{\mu}
.

In what follows, we consider the coordinate system in which the conformation tensor
\bm{c}
is diagonal, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}

(1\leq i\leq D)
. The microcanonical partition function
\Gamma
, eq. (
22
) with instantaneous conformation tensor
\hat{\bm{c}}=\hat{\bm{c}}(\bm{Q})
, can be written in the
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}
-representation as

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})=\int\,\delta^{(K)}\left(\hat{\bm{c}}(\mathbf{X})-\bm{c}\right)\,\prod_{i=1}^{D}\prod_{\mu=1}^{N}dX_{i,\mu}\,,

(B.25)

with
\hat{\bm{c}}=\hat{\bm{c}}(\mathbf{X})
given by
\hat{c}_{ij}=\mathbf{X}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j}/N
. In view of the conditions
\hat{c}_{ii}=c_{ii}=\lambda_{i}
, the integral (
B.25
) is re-written by introducing
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
. Making use of the property
\delta(ax)=(1/a)\delta(x)
for
a>0
, one obtains from eq. (
B.25
),

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})=\Gamma_{1}(\bm{c})\,\Gamma_{2}(\bm{c})\,\Gamma_{3}\,,

(B.26)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{D}\prod_{\mu=1}^{N}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}=(\det\bm{c})^{N/2}\,,

(B.27)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq D}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}

(B.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(\prod_{1\leq i,j\leq D}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}}\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq D}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{(\det\bm{c})^{-D}(\det\bm{c})^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\det\bm{c})^{(-D-1)/2}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\,\delta^{(K)}\left(\hat{\bm{c}}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}})-\bm{1}\right)\,\prod_{i=1}^{D}\prod_{\mu=1}^{N}d\tilde{X}_{i,\mu}\,,

(B.29)

where
\Gamma_{1}
comes from the substitution of variables in the volume element, and
\Gamma_{2}
comes from the substitution of variables in
\delta^{(K)}
. It is to be noted that the integral
\Gamma_{3}
does
not
depend on
\bm{c}
. Rather, we find
\Gamma_{3}=\Gamma(\bm{1})
. Therefore, one obtains for the
\bm{c}
-dependence of the microcanonical partition function

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})=(\det\bm{c})^{(N-D-1)/2}\Gamma(\bm{1})\,.

(B.30)

B.3.2
Scaling in reciprocal space

An alternative derivation for
\Gamma(\bm{c})
follows by explicitly enforcing the constraints using Fourier transforms, and then performing a rescaling.
We begin with the microcanonical partition-function
\Gamma
defined in eq. (
22
),

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{DN}Q\,\delta^{(K)}\left(\bm{c}-\hat{\bm{c}}\right)\,,

(B.31)

where
\hat{\bm{c}}
is the symmetric number-averaged conformation tensor given by eq. (
5
). The
\delta
-function ensures that only configurations
\{\bm{Q}_{\mu}\}
that satisfy
\bm{c}=\hat{\bm{c}}
are included in
\Gamma(\bm{c})
. To evaluate
\Gamma(\bm{c})
we choose a coordinate system for the
\bm{Q}_{\mu}
comprising the eigenvectors
\{\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\}
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{a}
of
\bm{c}
. The constraints in the Dirac
\delta
-function then reduce to

\displaystyle\lambda_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\quad(a=1,\dots D)\quad D\,\textrm{constraints},

(B.32)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\widehat{e}}_{b}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\widehat{e}}_{c}\quad(b<c)\quad L\,\textrm{constraints},

(B.33)

where there are
L=D(D-1)/2
zero off-diagonal elements.
Hence we introduce
D
Dirac
\delta
-functions for the diagonal constraints, and
L

\delta
-functions for the off-diagonal constraints. We enforce these with Fourier transforms, leading to

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{D}\!u}{(2\pi)^{D}}\int\frac{d^{L}\!v}{(2\pi)^{L}}\int d^{DN}Q

\displaystyle\exp\left[i\sum_{a=1}^{D}u_{a}\left(\lambda_{a}-\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\right)-i\sum^{D}_{\begin{subarray}{c}b,c=1\\
b<c\end{subarray}}v_{\alpha}\bm{\widehat{e}}_{b}\cdot\hat{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{\widehat{e}}_{c}\right],

where the second sum is over all
L
distinct pairs of eigenvectors (
b<c
). Inserting the definition for
\hat{\bm{c}}
(eq. (
5
)) and interchanging
\sum_{\mu}
and
\sum_{a}
in the exponential,
\Gamma(\bm{c})
can be written as

\Gamma(\bm{c})=\frac{2^{L}}{(2\pi)^{K}}\int d^{D}\!u\,e^{i\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\lambda}}\int d^{L}\!v\int d^{DN}Q\\
\exp\left[-\frac{i}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\bm{Q}_{\mu}\cdot\left\{\mathsf{U}+\mathsf{V}\right\}\cdot\bm{Q}_{\mu}\right],

(B.35)

where the matrices
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{V}
are constructed from the constraint fields
u_{a}
and
v_{\alpha}
as follows
(represented in the basis
\{\bm{\widehat{e}}_{a}\}
) for
D=3

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}u_{1}&0&0\\
0&u_{2}&0\\
0&0&u_{3}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}0&v_{1}&v_{2}\\
v_{1}&0&v_{3}\\
v_{2}&v_{3}&0\end{matrix}\right),

(B.36)

and we have rescaled
\bm{v}
by a factor of 2.
Next, we scale the
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
integrals according to

\displaystyle u_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{u}_{a}}{\lambda_{a}}

\displaystyle d^{D}\!u

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{D}\bar{u}}{\left(\det\bm{c}\right)}

\displaystyle a=1\ldots D

(B.37)

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{v}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{a}\lambda_{b}}},

\displaystyle d^{L}\!v

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{L}\bar{v}}{\left(\det\bm{c}\right)^{(D-1)/2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha=1\ldots L}}{{\scriptstyle a<b\in(1,D)}}

(B.38)

where we recognize
\det\bm{c}=\prod_{a=1}^{D}\lambda_{a}
. The scale factors for the
v_{\alpha}
correspond to the
u_{a}
in its row and column. To see the scaling in the measure for
\bar{v}
, note that each
v
picks up a factor in the denominator proportional to
\lambda
. Hence the denominator scales as
\lambda^{L}\sim\lambda^{D(D-1)/2}\sim(\det\bm{c})^{(D-1)/2}
, leading to the denominator above in eq. (
B.38
). Hence,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{L}}{(2\pi)^{K}\left(\det\bm{c}\right)^{(D+1)/2}}\int d^{D}\!\bar{u}\,e^{i{\bar{\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{1}}}\int d^{L}\!\bar{v}\int d^{DN}\,Q

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left[-\frac{i}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\bm{Q}_{\mu}\cdot\left\{{\mathsf{U}}+{\mathsf{V}}\right\}\cdot\bm{Q}_{\mu},\right],

(B.39)

where
\bm{1}=(1,1,\ldots)
and now, for
(D=3)
,
\mathsf{U}+\mathsf{V}
is expressed in the form

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}+{\mathsf{V}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\frac{\bar{u}_{1}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{1}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{u}_{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}^{2}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{3}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{v}_{3}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}}&\displaystyle\frac{\bar{u}_{3}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{3}^{2}}}\end{matrix}\right).

(B.40)

We finally rescale
\bm{Q}_{\mu}=\bar{\bm{Q}}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\Lambda}
, where
\bm{\Lambda}
is the diagonal matrix
\Lambda_{aa}=\sqrt{\lambda}_{a},\Lambda_{ab}=0(a\neq b)
. Hence, we find

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{L}}{(2\pi)^{K}}\,\left(\det\bm{c}\right)^{(N-D-1)/2}\int d^{D}\!\bar{u}\,e^{i\bar{\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{1}}\int d^{L}\!\bar{v}\int d^{DN}\!\bar{Q}

(B.41)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left[-\frac{i}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\bar{\bm{Q}}_{\mu}\cdot\left\{\bar{\mathsf{U}}+\bar{\mathsf{V}}\right\}\cdot\bar{\bm{Q}}_{\mu}\right],

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\det\bm{c}\right)^{(N-D-1)/2}\,\,{\cal I},

(B.42)

where the integral
{\cal I}
is independent of the conformation tensor
\bm{c}
, and
\bar{\mathsf{U}}+\bar{\mathsf{V}}
is equal to (
B.40
) without the factors
1/\sqrt{\lambda_{a}\lambda_{b}}
.
