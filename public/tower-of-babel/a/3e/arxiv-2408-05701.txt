Why Groups Matter: Necessity of Group Structures in Attributions
Dangxing Chen, Jingfeng Chen, and Weicheng Ye

Abstract
. Explainable machine learning methods have been accompanied by substantial development. Despite their success, the existing approaches focus more on the general framework with no prior domain expertise. High-stakes financial sectors have extensive domain knowledge of the features. Hence, it is expected that explanations of models will be consistent with domain knowledge to ensure conceptual soundness. In this work, we study the group structures of features that are naturally formed in the financial dataset. Our study shows the importance of considering group structures that conform to the regulations. When group structures are present, direct applications of explainable machine learning methods, such as Shapley values and Integrated Gradients, may not provide consistent explanations; alternatively, group versions of the Shapley value can provide consistent explanations. We contain detailed examples to concentrate on the practical perspective of our framework.

1.
Introduction

Compared to traditional methods, machine learning (ML) models often increase accuracy at the expense of black-box functionality.
The model explanation is extremely substantial for highly regulated industries such as finance, as stressed in the model risk management handbook by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
(
OCC, 2021
)
. Recently, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) confirmed that anti-discrimination law requires companies to provide detailed explanations when denying a credit application from clients when using ML methods for decision-making
1
1

1

https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-acts-to-protect-the-public-from-black-box-credit-models-using-complex-algorithms/
. In response to the growing requirements from the regulators, researchers and especially practitioners are investigating explainable ML methods to provide the required interpretability from the regulation perspective.

Explainable ML methods have been successful in the past. Among these, axiom-based attribution methods, such as Shapley value
(
Sundararajan and
Najmi, 2020
;
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
and Integrated Gradients (IG)
(
Sundararajan
et al., 2017
)
, provide both mathematical rigor and practical explanations. An attribution method involves assigning the prediction score of a model based on its base features. An attribution to a base feature can be viewed as the degree to which the feature contributes to the prediction. The allocation of attributes is determined by preserving the desired axioms for fairness
(
Sundararajan and
Najmi, 2020
)
.

Despite the success of attribution methods, the existing analysis has primarily focused on general axioms without prior knowledge, such as completeness, linearity, dummy, and symmetry
(
Sundararajan and
Najmi, 2020
)
. These axioms may be sufficient to provide reasonable explanations in many traditional ML applications, such as computer vision and large language models. However, financial practice applications have benefited from extensive domain knowledge such as monotonicity
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
)
and diminishing marginal effects
(
Gupta et al., 2020
)
. We must ensure that decision-making models make reasonable predictions grounded in these domains. Accordingly, the OCC’s model risk management handbook
(
OCC, 2021
)
emphasizes conceptual soundness to reflect sound theory and business practice.
The same requirement can be found in financial models where we should provide a reasonable explanation that conforms to financial theory. Therefore, attribution methods based on financial knowledge have received increasing attention from practitioners and researchers
(
Chen and Gao, 2024
;
Shalit, 2021
)
.

This paper focuses on the group structures of features.
Historically, economic studies have taken group structures into account
(
Owen, 1977
;
Calvo and
Gutiérrez, 2013
;
Kamijo, 2009
)
, such as geographic location and political party affiliation.
Group structures are also common in finance, for example, several features may be used to describe the same characteristic, such as past-due payments in credit scoring with a variety of durations. Despite this, group structures in finance have been neglected. Recently, there have been calls for group explanations
(
Rozemberczki et al., 2022
)
.

In this paper, we address the following question.
Is it necessary to take into account group structures when explain ML models?

To answer this question, we provide several group-based axioms that describe what we can expect in situations where there is a natural group structure of features based on a specific domain. The analysis of axioms demonstrates that popular attribution methods, such as Shapley value and IG, may not be able to preserve these axioms, thus providing unsatisfactory explanations. Therefore, it is important to exercise caution in ML models when group structure is involved. Alternatively, the group version of Shapley values preserves key axioms while providing consistent explanations.
In summary, our contributions to this work are:

(1)

we propose group axioms for attribution methods with a rigorous theoretical framework.

(2)

we provide detailed explanations when group axioms are needed and why they are important.

Preliminaries

For problem setup, assume we have
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{D}
is the dataset with
n
samples and
m
features and
\mathcal{Y}
is the corresponding numerical values in regression and labels in classification. We denote a class of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{F}

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

2.1.
Baseline Attribution Methods

Following
(
Lundstrom
et al., 2022
)
, we denote the point of interest
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
to explain as an explicand,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
a baseline, and
\mathbf{x}
the general input. The Baseline Attribution Method that interprets features’ importance is defined.

Definition 2.1

(Baseline Attribution Method (BAM))
.

Given
\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
f\in\mathcal{F}
, a baseline attribution method is any function of the form
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We denote
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
or sometimes simply
\mathcal{A}_{i}(f)
or
\mathcal{A}_{i}
as the
i
-th attribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
.

2.2.
Shapley Value and Integrated Gradients

2.2.1.
(Baseline) Shapley Value

The Shapley value
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
takes as input a set function
v:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
M=\{1,\dots,m\}
. The Shapley value produces attributions
\text{SH}_{i}
for each player
i\in M
by

(1)

\displaystyle\text{SH}_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq M\backslash i}\frac{|S|!(|M|-|S|-1)!}{M!}(v(S\cup i)-v(S)).

Here, we focus on the Baseline Shapley (BShap), which calculates

(2)

\displaystyle v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;S)=f(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{S};\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{M\backslash S}).

That is, baseline values replace the feature’s absence. For example, suppose
f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+x_{2}
,
\overline{\mathbf{x}}=(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2})
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0)
, and
S=\{1\}
, then we have
v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;S)=f(\overline{x}_{1},0)
. We denote the
i
-th attribution of BShap by
\text{BS}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
and
\text{BS}_{i}(f)
or
\text{BS}_{i}
sometimes. We focus on BShap since it has better theoretical properties by preserving desired axioms, as discussed in
(
Sundararajan and
Najmi, 2020
)
.

2.2.2.
Integrated Gradients

Given
\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, and
f
, the
i
-th component of
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
of Integrated Gradients (IG)
(
Sundararajan
et al., 2017
)
is calculated by

(3)

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=(\overline{x}_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+t(\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\ dt.

For simplicity, we often use
\text{IG}_{i}(f)
or
\text{IG}_{i}
for
\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
.

2.3.
Individual and Pairwise Monotonicity

Two types of monotonicity that are commonly used in practice are discussed here
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
;
Gupta et al., 2020
)
. Without loss of generality (WLOG), we assume that all monotonic features are monotonically increasing throughout the paper. Individual monotonicity
(
Liu
et al., 2020
;
Runje and
Shankaranarayana, 2023
)
, as one of the most commonly used domain knowledge, is defined below.

Definition 2.2

(Individual Monotonicity)
.

Suppose we partition the input
\mathbf{x}
into
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
.
We say
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
if
\forall c>0
,

f(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})\leq f(x_{i}+c,\mathbf{x}_{\neg}).

Example 2.3
.

In Credit Scoring, the probability of default should be individually monotonic with respect to the number of past-due payments.

In practice, certain features are intrinsically more important than others. It is referred to as pairwise monotonicity and has received increasing attention in recent years
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
;
Chen and Ye, 2022
;
Gupta et al., 2020
)
.

Definition 2.4

(Strong Pairwise Monotonicity)
.

Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. WLOG, we assume that
x_{i}
has greater significance than
x_{j}
. We say
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
over
x_{j}
if
\forall\mathbf{x}
and
c>0
,

f(x_{i},x_{j}+c,\mathbf{x}_{\neg})\leq f(x_{i}+c,x_{j},\mathbf{x}_{\neg}).

Example 2.5
.

In credit scoring, suppose
f
calculates the probability of default,
x_{i}
counts the number of past dues that have been outstanding for more than three months, and
x_{j}
counts the number of past dues that are outstanding less than three months. As
x_{i}
is always more important than
x_{j}
,
f
should be strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
over
x_{j}
. The pairwise relationship requires that whenever there is an additional past due, a longer past due is always more serious.

2.4.
Axioms

Here, we provide important axioms considered for BAMs.

(1)

Completeness:
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{A}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=f(\overline{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
.

(2)

Linearity:
\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\alpha f+\beta g)=\alpha\mathcal{A}_{i}(f)+\beta\mathcal{A}_{i}(g)
.

(3)

Dummy: If
\partial_{i}f\equiv 0
, then
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=0
.

(4)

Symmetry: For a given
i,j
, define
\mathbf{x}^{*}
by swapping the values of
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, Now suppose that
\forall\mathbf{x}
,
f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
. Then if
\overline{x}_{i}=\overline{x}_{j}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{j}^{\prime}
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{j}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
.

(5)

Affine Scale Invariance (ASI): Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. Define affine transformation as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x};i,c,d)=\mathbf{h}((x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg});i,c,d)=(cx_{i}+d,\mathbf{x}_{\neg}).

For simplicity, we denote
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x};i,c,d)
by
\mathbf{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for short. For any index
i
,
c\neq 0
and
d\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\mathbf{h}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})).

(6)

Demand Individual Monotonicity (DIM): If we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
and
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
, then
\mathcal{A}_{i}((\overline{x}_{i}+c,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq\mathcal{A}_{i}((\overline{x}_{i},\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
,
\forall c>0
.

Theorem 2.6
.

(
Lundstrom
et al., 2022
;
Sundararajan and
Najmi, 2020
;
Sundararajan
et al., 2017
)

BShap preserves all axioms (1)-(6). IG preserves preserves (1)-(5).

3.
Group Structure

In many applications, features should naturally be classified. Moreover, features can be combined to form groups that share similar characteristics. Mathematically, we consider a coalition structure over
M=\{1,\dots,m\}
is a partition of
M
, that is,
B=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{l}\}
is a group structure if
\cup_{1\leq i\leq l}B_{i}=M
and
B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
. We also assume
B_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i
. Denote
\mathcal{B}(M)
as the set of all coalition structures over
M
. Here are some examples of different practices:

•

Credit Scoring
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
;
Chen and Ye, 2022
)
: past-due payment information includes the number of past-due payments in different durations. For instance, features that count the number of past dues less than three months and more than three months are naturally in the same group.

•

Auto Insurance
2
2

2

https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/sagnik1511/car-insurance-data/discussion
: auto insurance depends on the number of past accidents. The accidents could be further divided based on the injury of autos and passengers. Features with different seriousness can be put into the same group.

•

Fraud Detection
(
Barata et al., 2021
)
: to determine if there are any abnormal activities, transaction frequency is an important indicator. It is possible, for instance, to count frequencies daily, such as today, yesterday, etc. This group of features measures the frequency of transactions in an account.

Similar to Definition
2.1
, we define the group attribution method.

Definition 3.1

(Group Attribution Method (GAM))
.

Given
\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and a group structure
B
, a group attribution method is any function of the form
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
. We denote
\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
or sometimes simply
\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(f)
or
\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}
as the
i
-th group attribution for the group
B_{i}
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)
.

We are interested in the attribution
\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}
of a group
B_{i}
. One method is to calculate individual attributions first, and then add them together. Group attributions by Shapley value and IG can be calculated simply by adding the individual attributions together,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{j}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f).

Alternatively, one may decide to determine the group attributions directly, and then study individual attributions, as discussed below.

3.1.
Group Shapley Value

We review the Shapley value for games with group structures
(
Calvo and
Gutiérrez, 2013
;
Kamijo, 2009
;
Owen, 1977
)
, where the rules governing cooperation among group members differ from those governing interactions among group members. A game
(M,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f))
with a group structure
B\in\mathcal{B}(M)
is denoted by
(B,M,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f))
. Define
L=\{1,\dots,l\}
and

(4)

\displaystyle v_{B}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;T)=v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\cup_{i\in T}B_{i}),\forall T\subseteq L.

Define
(L,v_{B}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f))
as the game induced by
(B,M,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f))
by considering the unions of
B
as players. We refer to this as group BShap (GShap) and the
i
-th attribution is calculated as

(5)

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{B_{i}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subseteq L\backslash i}c(T)\Delta v_{B}(T;i)

(6)

\displaystyle c(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{|T|!(|L|-|T|-1)!}{|L|!},

(7)

\displaystyle\Delta v_{B}(T;i)

\displaystyle=v_{B}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;T\cup i)-v_{B}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;T).

GShap is determined in the same manner as Shapley value. Completeness and linearity are preserved. In addition, it takes into account the group version of the dummy and symmetry.

•

Group dummy: If
v_{B}(T\cup i)=v_{B}(T)\ \forall T\subseteq L\backslash i
, then
\text{GS}_{B_{i}}=0
.

•

Group symmetry: If
v_{B}(T\cup i)=v_{B}(T\cup j)\ \forall T\subseteq L\backslash\{i,j\}
, then
\text{GS}_{B_{i}}=\text{GS}_{B_{j}}
.

GShap’s theoretical properties are discussed in
(
Hu and Li, 2018
)
. An example of a demonstration is provided below.

Example 3.2
.

Suppose we have
M=\{1,2,3\}
,
B=\{\{1,2\},3\}
, then the characteristic function
v_{B}
only consider
v(\{\emptyset\})
,
v(\{1,2\})
,
v(\{3\})
, and
v(\{1,2,3\})
. GShap calculates that

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{\{1,2\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v(\{1,2\})-v(\{\emptyset\})}{2}+\frac{v(\{1,2,3\})-v(\{3\})}{2},

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{\{3\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{v(\{3\})-v(\{\emptyset\})}{2}+\frac{v(\{1,2,3\})-v(\{1,2\})}{2}.

It is possible to further decomposite group attributions. One of the most popular methods is Owen value
(
Owen, 1977
)
. We will have a review below. There exist other ways for further decomposition
(
Kamijo, 2009
;
Calvo and
Gutiérrez, 2013
)
. However, the purpose of this work is on attributions of groups. Therefore, we only display Owen value for demonstration purposes and further discussion is beyond the scope.

3.1.1.
Owen Value

For Owen value, each
\Delta v_{B}(T;i)
is further decomposed by the Shapley Value by preserving completeness, linearity, dummy, and symmetry within the group. The Owen value is calculated by

(8)

\displaystyle\text{OW}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subseteq L\backslash i}\sum_{S\subseteq B_{i}\backslash j}d(S,T)(v(Q\cup S\cup j)-v(Q\cup S)),

(9)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\cup_{k\in T}B_{k},

(10)

\displaystyle d(S,T)

\displaystyle=\frac{|T|!(|L|-|T|-1)!}{|L|!}\frac{|S|!(|B_{i}|-|S|-1)!}{|B_{i}|!}.

Example 3.3
.

Following Example
3.2
, we further split
\text{GS}_{\{1,2\}}
by

\displaystyle\text{OW}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{v(\{1\})-v(\emptyset)}{4}+\frac{v(\{1,2\})-v(\{2\})}{4}

\displaystyle+\frac{v(\{1,3\})-v(\{3\})}{4}+\frac{v(\{1,2,3\})-v(\{2,3\})}{4},

\displaystyle\text{OW}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{v(\{2\})-v(\emptyset)}{4}+\frac{v(\{1,2\})-v(\{1\})}{4}

\displaystyle+\frac{v(\{2,3\})-v(\{3\})}{4}+\frac{v(\{1,2,3\})-v(\{1,3\})}{4}.

4.
Group Axioms

This section discusses what should be expected regarding attributions when group structures are involved. Axioms are naturally motivated by financial knowledge.

4.1.
Group Transformation Invariance

Concepts of invariance have guided concepts across a variety of scientific fields. Invariance under feature transformations has been extensively studied
(
Kvinge et al., 2022
;
Mroueh
et al., 2015
)
. The importance of invariance in practice can be attributed to many factors, including the ability to produce more robust results, to be consistent with human intuition, and to facilitate better generalizations.
Accordingly, we would like to preserve invariance as much as possible when explaining ML methods. This section emphasizes the importance of feature invariance in financial practices.

4.1.1.
Group Affine Scale Invariance

The original concept of affine scale invariance
(
Friedman and
Moulin, 1999
)
refers to an affine transformation of a single feature. For instance, if
x_{i}
represents annual income in dollars, the result should remain the same if the currency unit of
x_{i}
is replaced by another currency instead. Using a group affine transformation of features, we generalize this concept and demonstrate it with the following example.

Example 4.1
.

In Credit Scoring practice, past-due payments could be split according to their duration.
The number of past-due payments for less than three months and more than three months can be calculated using
x_{1},x_{2}
. Alternatively, we can use
\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2}
to record the total number of past-due payments and the number of past-due payments that are more than three months. Ultimately, the result should be the same. Let us assume that there is an additional feature
x_{3}
that calculates the yearly income. Consequently, two past-due features may be placed in the same group, i.e.
B_{1}=\{1,2\}
. In this case,
B_{1}
represents all past-due information and there is an affine relationship such that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}\\
\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x};B_{1},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})=\left[\begin{matrix}1&1&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbf{A}&\begin{matrix}0\\
0\end{matrix}\\
\begin{matrix}0&0\end{matrix}&1\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}\begin{array}[]{c}\\[-6.99997pt]
\mathbf{b}\\
\\[-6.99997pt]
\end{array}\\
0\end{matrix}\right]

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right],\ \ \mathbf{b}=\left[\begin{matrix}0\\
0\end{matrix}\right].

Note
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
since we only act transformation on
B_{1}
. It is easy to determine the inverse of transformation as

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\mathbf{h}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}};B_{1},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})=\left[\begin{matrix}1&-1&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}\\
\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}-\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right].

For simplicity, we use
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})
or just
\mathbf{h}
for short.
Now consider a simple logistic regression

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\sigma(-10+x_{1}+2x_{2}+x_{3}),

where
\sigma(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
. Then by calculation, we have

\displaystyle g(\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=f\circ\mathbf{h}^{-1}=\sigma(-10+\widetilde{x}_{1}+\widetilde{x}_{2}+x_{3}).

It is then easy to verify that

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=g(\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2},x_{3}).

As a result, regardless of how past-due features are documented, the attributions of the group should be the same,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{B_{1}}(\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),g).

Definition 4.2

(
Group Affine Scale Invariance (GASI)
)
.

Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. Define group affine transformation as

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x};B_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{h}((\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg});B_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}_{\neg}).

As a convenience, we will use
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})
or
\mathbf{h}
for short.
Then for any group
B_{i}
, invertible matrix
\mathbf{A}
and vector
\mathbf{b}
which implies that
\mathbf{h}
is invertible,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}^{-1}).

Proposition 4.3
.

GShap attributions are invariant for any invertible group transformations.

Proposition 4.4
.

GASI is preserved by IG, but not BShap.

Example 4.5
.

For BShap, we show this by a counterexample. We consider Example
4.1
with a specific case

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{x}}=(0,40,20),\ \ \mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0,0).

For this case, the characteristic function is given by

\displaystyle v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{\emptyset\})\approx 0,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{1,2,3\})\approx 1,

\displaystyle v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{1\})=0,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{2\})\approx 1,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{3\})\approx 1,

\displaystyle v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{1,2\})\approx 1,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{1,3\})\approx 1,v(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;\{2,3\})\approx 1.

As a result, from the perspective of
f
,
\overline{x}_{1}
is dummy,
\overline{x}_{2}
and
\overline{x}_{3}
are almost symmetric, we have

\text{BS}_{B_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx\frac{1}{2}.

By group affine transformation, we have

\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})=(20,20,20).

From the perspective of
g
,
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are perfectly symmetrical. Thus,

\text{BS}_{B_{1}}(\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),g)\approx\frac{2}{3}.

It is evident that two different ways of recording features result in significantly different results, which is undesirable.

Remark 4.6
.

The GASI provides some insights into how features should be grouped. The GASI is not always a concern. As in Example
4.1
, the feature that counts the number of past-due payments and the feature that calculates annual income have different units, so we do not need to consider their group affine transformation.
When features describe the same characteristics (such as how many past-due payments are, and how many inquiries are), and have the same unit, it is important to consider the alternative representation of features through the group affine transformation. If there are no unique reasonable ways to represent features, group representation is recommended.

4.1.2.
Group linear fractional transformation

The Affine transformation is not the only transformation that is used in practice. Ratios are often used in finance in order to facilitate better understanding and provide better results when building models.

Example 4.7
.

Consider the company bankruptcy prediction. It is common for such predictions to include some features expressed in the fractional form
(
Altman, 1968
)
. For instance, suppose there are three features
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
that represent total assets, retained earnings, and sales. Then in practice, more likely people will use
x_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2},x_{3}
instead, whereas
\widetilde{x}_{2}=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}
calculates the profitability ratio that reflects the age and earning capacity of the company. It is hence natural to consider
B_{1}=\{1,2\}
because
\widetilde{x}_{2}
is calculated using
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. To reflect this, a linear fractional transformation can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}\\
\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x};B_{1},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})=\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right],

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right],\mathbf{B}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right],\mathbf{c}=\left[\begin{matrix}0\\
0\end{matrix}\right],\mathbf{d}=\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
0\end{matrix}\right].

That is, we calculate

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}},

whereas
the above division is defined as the vector entry-wise ratio. It is also easy to see the inverse of the transformation

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right]=\mathbf{p}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}};B_{1},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})=\left[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{x}_{1}\\
\widetilde{x}_{1}\widetilde{x}_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{matrix}\right].

For convenience, we will use
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})
or
\mathbf{p}
for short. Now consider a simple logistic regression

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sigma(-10+3x_{1}+4x_{2}+5x_{3}),

where
\sigma=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
. Then by calculation, we have

\displaystyle g(\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2},x_{3})=f\circ\mathbf{p}^{-1}=\sigma(-10+3\widetilde{x}_{1}+4\widetilde{x}_{1}\widetilde{x}_{2}+5x_{3}).

In a similar fashion to GASI, we should expect the same attributions in the two cases

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{B_{1}}(\mathbf{p}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{p}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}),g).

Definition 4.8

(
Group Linear Fractional Invariance (GLFI)
)
.

Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. Define linear fractional transformation as

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x};B_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})=\mathbf{p}((\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg});B_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d})=\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}}+\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{B_{i}}+\mathbf{d}},\mathbf{x}_{\neg}\right).

For simplicity, we denote the transformation by
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})
.
Then for any group
B_{i}
and invertible transformation
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\mathcal{A}_{B_{i}}\left(\mathbf{p}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{p}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\right).

Proposition 4.9
.

GShap preserves GLFI, but not BShap or IG.

The invariance by GShap is included in Proposition
4.3
. The counterexamples for BShap and IG are provided below.

Example 4.10
.

For BShap and IG, we provide a counterexample following Example
4.7
. Consider the case

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{x}}=(5,5,5),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0,0).

By group linear fractional transformation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(\overline{x})=\left(5,1,5\right).

By calculations, we have

\displaystyle\text{BS}_{B_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},f)\approx 0.66\neq\text{BS}_{B_{1}}\left(\mathbf{p}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{p}^{-1}\right)\approx 0.50,

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{B_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},f)\approx 0.58\neq\text{IG}_{B_{1}}\left(\mathbf{p}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{p}^{-1}\right)\approx 0.49.

As a result of BShap and IG, we obtain significantly different group attributions. Based on the results, it appears that if we apply BShap and IG directly, it matters a lot how we calculate ratios of features at the first place.

Remark 4.11
.

Based on the GLFI, how we calculate ratios of features could have a significant impact on the calculation. If we obtain different values for group attributions only because ratios are used, then that would be inconsistent. Therefore, to use ratio features instead, we should group them into the same group.

4.2.
Group Monotonicity

In financial practices, the monotonicity of the features plays a crucial role and should be guaranteed from a conceptual soundness and fairness perspective.
As a result, monotonic ML models have been studied extensively
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
;
Chen and Ye, 2022
;
Gupta et al., 2020
)
. However, even when models are strictly monotonic, attribution methods may not be able to capture monotonicity accurately
(
Friedman and
Moulin, 1999
)
. Providing reasonable explanations requires an explanation that can reflect monotonicity.

4.2.1.
Group Demand Individual Monotonicity

As per the original demand individual monotonicity principle
(
Friedman and
Moulin, 1999
)
, if an individual monotonic feature is increased, the corresponding feature attribution should be increased. As illustrated in the following example, if the monotonic feature belongs to a group, then the group attribute should increase if the monotonic feature increases.

Example 4.12
.

Let us assume that we are predicting the probability of a loan defaulting. We consider
x_{1}
the number of past-due loan payments,
x_{2}
the amount of the past-due payments owed, and
x_{3}
the external credit score. In this case, both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
concerns regarding the past-due information and the probability of default are individually monotonic for both features. When either of these features is increased, we would expect the group attribution to also increase since the risk regarding past-due payments has increased.

Definition 4.13

(
Group Demand Individual Monotonicity (GDIM)
)
.

Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
. Suppose
f
is individually monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
, where
i\in B_{j}
. We say a BAM preserves group demand individual monotonicity if

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{j}}((\overline{x}_{i}+c,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\geq\mathcal{A}_{B_{j}}((\overline{x}_{i},\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f),\forall\overline{\mathbf{x}}\text{ s.t. }c>0.

Remark 4.14
.

The GDIM is a generalization of the DIM. In two extreme cases, if
B_{j}
only contains a single feature, then the GDIM reduces to DIM. If
B_{j}
consists of all features, then GDIM directly follows from the definition of individual monotonicity.

Proposition 4.15
.

GShap preserves GDIM, but not BShap or IG.

Example 4.16
.

IG does not preserve DIM, therefore GDIM is not preserved as DIM is a special case of GDIM. Due to the change in the integral path, the IG is unable to preserve DIM.

For BShap, we examine a simple three-dimensional case in more detail. Assume we are interested in
x_{1}
and that
f
is monotonic with respect to
x_{1}
. There is a group structure that
B_{1}=\{1,2\}
.
We are interested in how the change to
\overline{x}_{1}
would affect the attribution. As a convenience, let us assume that
f
can be differentiated. Based on our calculations, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{BS}_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\text{BS}_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}.

Based on this interpretation, we can say that when we increase
\overline{x}_{1}
,
\text{BS}_{1}
also increases. If
\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}x_{2}}\geq 0
, then
\frac{\partial\text{BS}_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\geq 0
. Our intuition supports these interpretations. However, if we combine the results, we arrive at an unfavorable result

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\text{BS}_{1}+\text{BS}_{2})(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},\overline{x}_{2},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{1}}-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\partial f(\overline{x}_{1},x_{2}^{\prime},\overline{x}_{3})}{\partial x_{1}}.

As a result, the response of the group attribution to the change in
\overline{x}_{1}
would now depend on
\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}}
. If we have a large
\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}}
, then an increase of
\overline{x}_{1}
may lead to a decrease of
\text{BS}_{1}+\text{BS}_{2}
, which is counterintuitive.

Suppose
z=g(x_{1},x_{2})
where
f
has individual monotonicity with respect to
g
, and
g
has individual monotonicity with respect to
x_{1}
. It is therefore natural to expect an increase in
\overline{x}_{1}
to increase
\text{BS}_{1}+\text{BS}_{2}
. Specifically, consider a simple three-dimensional example

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sigma(-10+10x_{1}+10x_{2}-10x_{3}),

where
\sigma(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0,0)
. Consider two explicands
(2,2,2)
and
(4,2,2)
. By calculation, we obtain that

\displaystyle\text{BS}_{B_{1}}((2,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.33\geq\text{BS}_{B_{1}}((4,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.16,

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{B_{1}}((2,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 2.00\geq\text{IG}_{B_{1}}((4,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.50,

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{B_{1}}((2,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.00\leq\text{GS}_{B_{1}}((4,2,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.00.

In the content of Example
4.13
, according to BShap and IG, for a candidate with more past-due payments, the overall past-due attributions could be significantly reduced, which is absurd.

Remark 4.17
.

In the case of GDIM, the situation is more complex. As in Example
4.12
, when we consider the number of past-due payments and the amount owing, we would expect to see a group representation since they together describe the payment status, and each additional increase in any feature increases the future payment risk, and therefore should provide a larger group attribution. However, if we consider one feature of past-due payments as well as another yearly income, then an additional past-due payment does not necessarily imply a greater attribution for their combination.

GDIM requires an in-depth understanding of domain knowledge. Whenever we expect a change in feature to influence the attributions of a group, these features should be grouped. Typically, this occurs when we use different features to describe the same characteristics, such as delinquency status, inquiry status, and trade frequency.

4.2.2.
Group Strong Pairwise Monotonicity

The motivation of the demand individual monotonicity only applies to changes to a single feature. As shown below, pairwise monotonicity requires consideration of a change for a pair of features.

Example 4.18
.

Consider Example
4.1
. Let us assume that a client has a past-due payment of less than three months (
\overline{x}_{1}
). The client does not pay back within three months, so the payment is more than three months overdue (
\overline{x}_{2}
). Thus, for this client,
\overline{x}_{1}
decreases by one, and
\overline{x}_{2}
increases by one. As a result of the increased risk, we would expect the past-due group attributions to increase.

Definition 4.19

(
Group Strong Pairwise Monotonicity (GSPM)
)
.

Suppose
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{i}
over
x_{j}
. Suppose we partition
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},x_{j},\mathbf{x}_{\neg})
and
i,j\in B_{k}
. For an explicand
\overline{\mathbf{x}}
with
\overline{x}_{i}>x_{i}^{\prime}
and
\overline{x}_{j}>x_{j}^{\prime}
. Then we say a BAM preserves group average strong pairwise monotonicity if
\forall\overline{\mathbf{x}}
and
c>0
,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{B_{k}}((\overline{x}_{i},\overline{x}_{j}+c,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\leq\mathcal{A}_{B_{k}}((\overline{x}_{i}+c,\overline{x}_{j},\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f).

Proposition 4.20
.

GShap preserves GSPM, but not BShap or IG.

Example 4.21
.

Following Example
4.18
, consider a simple three-dimensional example

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sigma(-10+5x_{1}+10x_{2}-10x_{3}),

where
\sigma(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(0,0,0)
. Consider the group partition that
B_{1}=\{1,2\}
and
B_{2}=\{3\}
and in
B_{1}
,
f
is strongly monotonic with respect to
x_{2}
over
x_{1}
. Consider two explicands
(3,3,2)
and
(0,6,2)
. As a result of longer past-due payments,
(0,6,2)
carries a greater risk. As a result, the group attributions for past-due amounts should be greater. The calculation, however, reveals that

\displaystyle\text{BS}_{B_{1}}((3,3,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.25\geq\text{BS}_{B_{1}}((0,6,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.00,

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{B_{1}}((3,3,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.80\geq\text{IG}_{B_{1}}((0,6,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.50,

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{B_{1}}((3,3,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.00\leq\text{GS}_{B_{1}}((0,6,2),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)\approx 1.00.

BShap and IG have decreased the attributions for a client with a poor payment status, which is not reasonable.

Remark 4.22
.

If features are strongly pairwise monotonic, they must be considered in the same group.

B=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{l}\}
. Since symmetry, WLOG, we focus on
B_{1}
.
For
i\notin B_{j}
, for
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x};B_{j},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b})
, where
\mathbf{h}
is invertible by Definition,

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle=(\overline{x}_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+(\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})t)\ dt

\displaystyle=(\overline{x}_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left(\partial f\circ\mathbf{h}^{-1}\right)}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+(\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})t))\ dt

\displaystyle=(\overline{x}_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\left(\partial f\circ\mathbf{h}^{-1}\right)}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+(\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))t)\ dt

\displaystyle=\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{h}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}^{-1}).

As a result, group affine transformation doesn’t affect attributions of features outside of the group.
Suppose all features
x_{j}
that are not in
B_{1}
have been applied the corresponding affine scale transformation
\mathbf{h}_{1}(\mathbf{x})
. Then for
i\in B_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{h}_{1}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}_{1}^{-1}).

By completeness, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\notin B_{1}}\text{IG}_{j}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\sum_{j\notin B_{1}}\text{IG}_{j}(\mathbf{h}_{1}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}_{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}_{1}^{-1}).

Now let the entire affine transformation as
h_{2}(\mathbf{x})
, for
i\notin B_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{h}_{2}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}_{2}^{-1}).

By completeness, for
i\in B_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{IG}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)=\text{IG}_{i}(\mathbf{h}_{2}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}),\mathbf{h}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),f\circ\mathbf{h}_{2}^{-1}).

∎

Proof of
Proposition

4.20
.

For GShap, by strong pairwise monotonicity, we have

\displaystyle\text{GS}_{B_{k}}((\overline{x}_{i},\overline{x}_{j}+c,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)-\text{GS}_{B_{k}}((\overline{x}_{i}+c,\overline{x}_{j},\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle c(T)v_{B}((\overline{x}_{i}+c,\overline{x}_{j},\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{\neg}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},f;T\cup i)\leq 0.

Appendix B
Model and Data

For simplicity, data with missing variables are removed. Past dues greater than four times are replaced by four due to the rarity. This also applies to
x_{9}
if its value exceeds five. We categorize
x_{6}
into the following intervals:
[0,\$2500)
,
[\$2,500,\$5,000)
,
[\$5,000,\$7,500)
,
[\$7,500,\$10,000)
,
[\$10,000,\$50,000)
, and
[\$50,000,\infty)
. Afterward, they are transformed from five to zero so that
f
increases monotonically with respect to
x_{6}
. We make such a choice to make features as easy to understand as possible for customers. This is not a unique choice. The model performance has been monitored to ensure that the accuracy does not deteriorate.
When checking for accuracy, the dataset is randomly partitioned into
70\%
training and
30\%
test sets.

We use the monotonic groves of neural additive models
(
Chen and Ye, 2023
)
since it preserves required individual and pairwise monotonicity for this dataset. This is not a unique choice and other models can also be applied.
For
x_{1}-x_{3}
, we enforce strong pairwise monotonicity. We enforce individual monotonicity for
x_{6}
and
x_{9}
.
The AUC of the model is around
85\%
, which is consistent with the literature. It might be possible to improve model performance by further cleaning the data, but since this is not the primary concern of our study, we opt to omit it for simplicity.
