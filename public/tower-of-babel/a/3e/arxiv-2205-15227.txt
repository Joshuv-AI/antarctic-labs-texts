Housekeeping and excess entropy production for general nonlinear dynamics
Kohei Yoshimura, Artemy Kolchinsky, Andreas Dechant, Sosuke Ito

Abstract
We propose a housekeeping/excess decomposition of entropy production for general nonlinear dynamics in a discrete space, including chemical reaction networks and discrete stochastic systems. We exploit the geometric structure of thermodynamic forces to define the decomposition; this does not rely on the notion of a steady state, and even applies to systems that exhibit multistability, limit cycles, and chaos. In the decomposition, distinct aspects of the dynamics contribute separately to entropy production: the housekeeping part stems from a cyclic mode that arises from external driving, generalizing Schnakenberg’s cyclic decomposition to non-steady states, while the excess part stems from an instantaneous relaxation mode that arises from conservative forces. Our decomposition refines previously known thermodynamic uncertainty relations and speed limits. In particular, it not only improves an optimal-transport-theoretic speed limit, but also extends the optimal transport theory of discrete systems to nonlinear and nonconservative settings.



I
Introduction

The notion of “nonequilibrium” has two aspects: breaking of detailed balance and nonstationarity.
The housekeeping/excess decomposition of entropy production rate (EPR) is a way to deal with these two aspects of nonequilibrium systems
Oono and Paniconi 1998
;
Hatano and Sasa 2001
;
Esposito and Van den
Broeck 2010
;
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
.
Such a decomposition has previously been formulated based on steady states: the housekeeping EPR characterizes how detailed balance is broken in a steady state, while the excess part quantifies the additional dissipation that is needed to transition between steady states.
The best known decomposition of this kind was proposed by Hatano and Sasa (HS)
Hatano and Sasa 2001
(also known as the adiabatic/nonadiabatic decomposition
Esposito and Van den
Broeck 2010
), while an important alternative decomposition was proposed by Maes and Netočný (MN)
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
.
The practical utility of these decompositions is that they provide meaningful thermodynamic bounds for nonequilibrium systems where the integrated entropy production (EP) diverges, by discounting the housekeeping EP, which also diverges, to give a finite excess EP
Oono and Paniconi 1998
.

However, previously known decompositions have a crucial limitation. Namely, they are not defined for general nonlinear dynamics, such as general chemical reaction networks. Such systems are not always globally stable and can exhibit various nontrivial phenomena such as limit cycles, bifurcations and multistability, in contrast to existing EPR decompositions which assume global stability.
More precisely, the HS decomposition has been generalized to chemical reaction networks only if there is a special steady state, called complex balanced steady state
Rao and Esposito 2016
;
Ge and Qian 2016
, which is always globally stable
1
1

1

More precisely, systems with complex balanced steady states are globally stable within each stoichiometric compatibility class
Horn and Jackson 1972
;
Craciun 2015
;
Anderson 2011
.
.
However, even if a chemical reaction network has a stable steady state, it may not be complex balanced. In that case, it is possible for the HS decomposition to give a negative value of excess EPR, which makes it difficult to interpret the decomposition physically.
Because a complex balanced steady state is “almost detailed balanced” in the sense that it always shows global stability and cannot exhibit genuine nonlinear phenomena such as multistability or chaos
Feinberg 2019
, this particular generalization does not help us understand the various physically interesting situations which play a key role in biology and other complex chemical systems
Beard and Qian 2008
.

The total EPR
\sigma

In this paper, we propose an EPR decomposition that applies to general nonlinear dynamics in a discrete space, which include Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks as special cases (Fig.
1
).
Because the decomposition is defined based on the geometric structure of thermodynamic forces, it does not require any information about the steady state or even its existence.
Since it is not related to steady states, the decomposition is not only applicable to a wider range of dynamics, but it also provides crucial insights into the physical meaning of the individual terms: it allows us to interpret each term of the decomposition based on physical modes of dynamics, beyond the notion of stationarity.
The housekeeping part reflects a cyclic mode on the graph of states that does not affect the apparent dynamics, and it quantifies how external driving breaks detailed balance at a given instant of time. As a result, we generalize Schnakenberg’s cyclic decomposition of steady-state dissipation into contributions stemming from cycles in the graph of states
Schnakenberg 1976
.
On the other hand, the excess term expresses a “pseudo-relaxation” mode even if the system does not relax to a steady state, by extracting a conservative component of the thermodynamic force. Because a conservative force is given by a potential difference of the internal states, it represents an internal free-energy-like contribution to dissipation. Using a corresponding instantaneous “pseudo-equilibrium”, we obtain a gradient-flow form of an equation of motion, which was previously used to describe the relaxation to an equilibrium state
Mielke 2011
;
Mielke 2013
.
In summary, our result not only allows us to decompose the EPR in general nonlinear dynamics in a discrete space, but also to separate the effects on dissipation stemming from the external cyclic mode and the internal relaxation mode.

While this physical interpretation of our decomposition is different from previous results, it is also consistent with existing theory of nonequilibrium thermodynamics and effectively refines several results.
It gives much tighter thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) and speed limits, which is concordant with the original philosophy of EPR decomposition
Oono and Paniconi 1998
and strengthens its physical validity.
Concretely, we derive refined versions of short-time and finite-time TURs for Markov jump processes

More generally, we discuss connections between our decomposition and optimal transport theory.
Optimal transport theory interprets the minimum transportation cost from one probability distribution to another as a distance between them

Recent studies have revealed that one can obtain thermodynamic bounds in continuous systems through this geometrical approach, associating thermodynamic costs with the optimal-transport-theoretic distance

In this paper, we show that the local geometrical structure which defines our EPR decomposition reveals the physical meaning of the discrete

As a result, we obtain a simple speed limit for general nonlinear dynamics in a discrete space, in which the total integrated EP is replaced by the integrated excess EP, tightening the bound.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Sec.

In Sec.

\displaystyle B_{ie}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\iota^{\prime}(e)=i$}\\

(1)

We write the possibly nonlinear and time-dependent flux of

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)=k_{e}\prod_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}^{\nu_{\alpha\iota(e)}},

(3)

which however is not essential to our general results.
Below, we explicitly note whenever we make the assumption of mass actions kinetics.

We sometimes abbreviate the dependence of some quantity

II.2
Thermodynamics

Let us define the thermodynamic force on edge
e
by

\displaystyle F_{e}(x):=\ln\frac{\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)}{\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)}.

(8)

We assume the local detailed balance condition, namely that
F_{e}
gives the total change of the reduced entropy on the process that edge
e
represents
Schnakenberg 1976
;
Rao and Esposito 2016
;
Avanzini et al. 2021
, where “reduced” means that it is divided by a physical constant, the Boltzmann constant
k_{\mathrm{B}}
for Markov jump processes, or the gas constant
R
for chemical reaction networks.
We do not distinguish the entropy and the reduced one by setting the constant we consider to one.
Then, we obtain the following form of the entropy production rate (EPR):

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=\sum_{e}J_{e}(x)F_{e}(x).

(9)

For simplicity, hereafter we do not write the argument
x
explicitly.

In the seminal paper
Schnakenberg 1976
, Schnakenberg obtained an expression of the EPR by decomposing the network into cycles.
He introduced the fundamental set of cycles
\{\mathcal{C}_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1,\dots,M}
of the graph defined by states and transitions in a Markov jump process.
Each cycle corresponds to a vector
S(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})=(S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu}))_{e=1,\dots,E}\in\ker B
and the set
\{S(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\}_{\mu=1,\dots,M}
forms a basis of
\ker B
, where
\ker
indicates the kernel of the matrix.
Such a set of vectors can also be introduced for a chemical reaction network, where it spans
\ker\mathbb{S}

Polettini and Esposito 2014
.
In general, we refer to a basis of
\ker\mathbb{S}
rooted in the cycles of a graph as a cycle basis.
A cycle basis is not unique in general; hereafter, we fix some choice of the basis.
See also Figs.
2
and
3
, where we provide concrete examples of cycles and clarify the slight difference between Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks.

When the system is in a steady state, the current
J^{\mathrm{st}}
is in the kernel of
\mathbb{S}
, so it can be expanded in the cycle basis as

\displaystyle J_{e}^{\mathrm{st}}=\sum_{\mu}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\mathcal{J}_{\mu}.

(10)

Note that the superscript

is used to indicate the value of a quantity in the steady state.
Each expansion coefficient
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
quantifies the current flowing in the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
.
The conjugated cyclic forces are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu}:=\sum_{e}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})F_{e}^{\mathrm{st}}.

(11)

In terms of the currents and forces, we obtain the expression of the EPR as in Refs.
Schnakenberg 1976
;
Polettini and Esposito 2014
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{st}}=\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu},

(12)

which shows that the steady-state EPR can be divided into contributions from cycles, whose dissipation can be analyzed separately
Andrieux and Gaspard 2007
;
Horowitz and Esposito 2014
;
van der Meer et al. 2022
. We will generalize this expression out of steady states in Sec.
III.1

II.3
Edgewise Onsager coefficient, gradient flow, and Wasserstein distance

We define the edgewise Onsager coefficient by

\displaystyle\ell_{e}(x):=\frac{\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)-\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)}{\ln(\mathcal{K}_e(x)/\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x))}=\frac{J_{e}(x)}{F_{e}(x)}

(13)

and the diagonal matrix
L(x):=\mathrm{diag}(\ell_{e}(x))
.
When
\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)=\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)
, by continuity we see
\ell_{e}(x)
is given by

.
It is also easy to see that

For simplicity, we make

We write

For notational convenience, we define an inner product

We can use this inner product to express the EPR as

\displaystyle\sigma=\|F\|_{L}^{2},

which will be the starting point of our results

2

2

We note that in a withdrawn preprint

.
Note that if we choose any other state like a steady state as the argument of

We remark that as a general property of the logarithmic mean,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)}\leq\ell_{e}(x)\leq\frac{\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)+\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)}{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mu}=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\frac{k_{e}}{k_{-e}}=\frac{\prod_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}})^{\nu_{\alpha\iota^{\prime}(e)}}}{\prod_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}})^{\nu_{\alpha\iota(e)}}}.

Substituting this into the definition of the thermodynamic force (

\displaystyle F_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}(\nu_{\alpha\iota(e)}-\nu_{\alpha\iota^{\prime}(e)})\ln\frac{x_{\alpha}}{x^{\mathrm{eq}}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-[\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi]_{e}

(22)

The proof ends by calculating the derivative of the KL divergence to show

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}D(x\|x^{\mathrm{eq}})=\ln\frac{x_{\alpha}}{x_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}}=\Psi_{\alpha}.

We state our main result, a decomposition of the EPR in Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks, which can be seen as the generalization of the Maes–Netočný decomposition to such systems
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
. Because we define the decomposition in a geometrical way by using a projection operator and the edgewise Onsager coefficient (
13
), we term it
the Onsager-projective decomposition
.
The basic idea of the technical details discussed in the following two paragraphs is summarized in Fig.
4
.

Let us consider a matrix
P
that is the projection onto the image of
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
and orthogonal with respect to the matrix
L
. Formally, this means that for any
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
,
P
satisfies
\langle Pf,(I-P)g\rangle_{L}=0
, where
I
is the identity.
This projection matrix is given by
Puntanen et al. 2011

\displaystyle P=\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-}\mathbb{S}L,

(31)

with
A^{-}
denoting the generalized inverse of a matrix
A
.

Since
\mathbb{S}
does not have full rank in general, we need to consider a generalized inverse.
Nevertheless, we may set it to a theoretically tractable one, like the Moore–Penrose inverse, because
P
does not depend on the choice of the generalized inverse
Puntanen et al. 2011
.
To be more concrete, let the eigendecomposition of
\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
be
U\Lambda U^{-1}
with orthogonal matrix
U
and diagonal matrix
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r},0\dots,0)
where
\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0
and
r=\rank\mathbb{S}
. Then, the Moore–Penrose inverse of
\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
can be computed as
U\Lambda^{+}U^{-1}
with
\Lambda^{+}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{-1},\dots,\lambda_{r}^{-1},0\dots,0)
, which is the Moore–Penrose inverse of
\Lambda
.

For any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
,
Pf
is also characterized by

\displaystyle Pf=\argmin_{g\in\mathrm{im}\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}}\|f-g\|_{L}^{2}.

(32)

Because
Pf\in\mathrm{im}\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
, there exists
\phi(f)\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
that satisfies
-\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi(f)=Pf
.
The solution
\phi(f)
is not unique in general because the transformation
\phi(f)\to\phi(f)+c
, where
c
is a left null vector of
\mathbb{S}
, does not change
-\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi(f)
.
If
\mathbb{S}
is the incidence matrix
B
of a connected graph,
c
is given by a vector of ones times any scalar. When we consider chemical reaction networks, such a null vector is called a conservation law
Rao and Esposito 2016
.
Nonetheless,
Pf
is unique, so we can consider
\phi(f)
as fixed.

Now, we can define the conservative force that plays a central role in the Onsager-projective decomposition.
Define
F^{*}:=PF
and
\phi^{*}=\phi(F)
such that
F^{*}=-\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi^{*}
.
The force
F^{*}
can be seen as a conservative force that reproduces the original (possibly nonconservative) dynamics because
\mathbb{S}LF^{*}=\mathbb{S}LF=\mathbb{S}J=d_{t}x
.
This can be shown as follows. First, we have a decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}LF

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}L(P+I-P)F

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}LF^{*}+\mathbb{S}L(I-P)F.

(33)

The second term
\mathbb{S}L(I-P)
is zero because
P
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{im}\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
with respect to the metric
L
, i.e., for any
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
,
P
satisfies
\langle\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\varphi,(I-P)f\rangle_{L}=\varphi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}L(I-P)f=0
.
Because
\varphi
is now arbitrary, by setting
f=F
, we obtain
\mathbb{S}L(I-P)F=0
. Thus
d_{t}x=\mathbb{S}LF^{*}
holds.

Using the conservative force
F^{*}
, we find a decomposition of the EPR that can be regarded as a generalization of the Maes–Netočný decomposition in Langevin systems to Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
.
We define the
Onsager excess
and
housekeeping EPR
by

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}:=\|F^{*}\|_{L}^{2},\quad\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}:=\|F-F^{*}\|_{L}^{2}

(34)

respectively.
These terms give a nonnegative decomposition of the EPR,

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}+\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}},

(35)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}},\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}\geq 0

(36)

for both Markov jump processes and CRNs.
They are always nonnegative because
L
is positive definite.
From the orthogonality relation

\displaystyle\langle F^{*},F-F^{*}\rangle_{L}=\langle PF,(I-P)F\rangle_{L}=0,

(37)

the decomposition is proved as follows

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\langle F^{*}+(F-F^{*}),F^{*}+(F-F^{*})\rangle_{L}

\displaystyle=\langle F^{*},F^{*}\rangle_{L}+\langle F-F^{*},F-F^{*}\rangle_{L}.

(38)

The two orthogonal components
F^{*}
and
F-F^{*}
of the thermodynamic force allow us to interpret the decomposition in a geometrical way, see Figs.
1
and
4
.
This is similar to the interpretation in the Langevin case
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
.

In a steady state, where
\mathbb{S}J^{\mathrm{st}}=0
holds, the excess EPR vanishes and
\sigma=\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}
because

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}=\langle F^{*},F^{*}\rangle_{L^{\mathrm{st}}}=-(\phi^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}L^{\mathrm{st}}F^{*}=-(\phi^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}J^{\mathrm{st}}=0.

(39)

On the other hand, the housekeeping EPR is identically zero if and only if the steady state is detailed balanced
4
4

4

It is shown under the assumption that the dynamics obeys the law of mass action by seeing the equivalence between the following two conditions. One is that the force is conservative, which is equivalent to
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}=0
. The other is the so-called generalized Wegscheider condition
\ln(k^+/k^-)\in\mathrm{im}\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
which is a necessary and sufficient condition for the steady state to be detailed balanced
Schuster and Schuster 1989
.

.
Therefore, the decomposition (
35

\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}
and a contribution
\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}
required to sustain a nonequilibrium steady state if a steady state exists.

Given the eigendecomposition
\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}=U\Lambda U^{-1}
discussed earlier, we can write down the excess and housekeeping EPR as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}|[U^{-1}\mathbb{S}LF]_{\alpha}|^{2},

(40)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle=\sigma-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{r}\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}|[U^{-1}\mathbb{S}LF]_{\alpha}|^{2}.

III.1
Generalization of Schnakenberg’s cyclic decomposition

Our decomposition leads to a generalization of Schnakenberg’s cyclic decomposition of the steady-state EPR (
12
) to non-steady states.
Consider the current corresponding to
F-F^{*}
, that is
J^{\mathrm{cyc}}:=L(F-F^{*})
.
This current is in the kernel of
\mathbb{S}
because
\mathbb{S}LF=\mathbb{S}LF^{*}
, therefore it is a cyclic current and can be expanded in the cycle basis as

\displaystyle J_{e}^{\mathrm{cyc}}=\sum_{\mu}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})\mathcal{J}_{\mu}^{\dagger}.

(41)

This expansion is available whether the system is in a steady state or not.
The conjugated force is also given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu}^{\dagger}:=\sum_{e}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})=\sum_{e}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})F_{e},

(42)

where we used
\sum_{e}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})F_{e}^{*}=-(\phi^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}S(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})=0
.
Thus, we have generalized the decomposition in Eq. (
11
), which applies only in a steady state, to arbitrary states.
Importantly, these cycle quantities allow us to write the Onsager housekeeping EPR as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}=\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\mathcal{F}_{\mu}^{\dagger}

(43)

because

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\mathcal{F}_{\mu}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\sum_{e}S_{e}(\mathcal{C}_{\mu})(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}J_{e}^{\mathrm{cyc}}(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})=\|F-F^{*}\|_{L}^{2}.

(44)

Therefore, our decomposition generalizes Schnakenberg’s cyclic decomposing of the steady-state EPR
Schnakenberg 1976
to a cyclic decomposition of the housekeeping EPR in non-steady-state systems.

III.2
Generalization of Maes–Netočný decomposition

Next, we show that the decomposition can also be understood as a generalization of the MN decomposition
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
.
In their paper, Maes and Netočný regarded the EPR as a functional of the potential landscape, and minimized it by changing the landscape.
The result is the following minimum EP principle: the potential landscape minimizing the EPR is the one for which the instantaneous distribution is a steady state.
In our case, using the minimizing property of the projection operator (
32
), we have the expression

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}=\inf_{\psi}\|F-(-\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi)\|_{L}^{2}.

(45)

In a Markov jump process,
F
is written as
F=-B^{\mathsf{T}}(u+\ln p)+F^{\mathrm{nc}}
, where
u
is some potential landscape and
F^{\mathrm{nc}}
is a nonconservative driving force.
We define
J(v):=-LB^{\mathsf{T}}(v+\ln p)+LF^{\mathrm{nc}}
as a function of the potential landscape.
Using these expressions, we find a formulation resembling Ref.
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}=\inf_{v}J(v)^{\mathsf{T}}L^{-1}J(v).

(46)

Minimization leads to the condition

\displaystyle BJ(v)=0.

(47)

Thus, among all potential landscapes with Onsager matrix
L
, the entropy is minimized for the one in which the instantaneous distribution
p
is a steady state.
This is the analogue of Maes and Netočný’s minimum EP principle
Maes and Netočnỳ 2014
.
We remark that fixing
L
is connected to fixing the diffusion matrix in the Langevin case (see Appendix
B
).

III.3
Variational formulas

We can also extend other results derived in the Langevin case
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
to discrete dynamics.
First, the following variational expressions hold:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\psi}\frac{\pqty{\ev{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,F}_L}^{2}}{\expectationvalue{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi}_{L}},

(48)

\displaystyle=\inf_{F^{\prime}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L}\expectationvalue{F-F',F-F'}_{L},

(49)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{F^{\prime}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L}\frac{\pqty{\ev{F',F}_L}^{2}}{\expectationvalue{F',F'}_{L}}.

(50)

Note that
\mathbb{S}L
is the adjoint of
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
with respect to the inner product
\expectationvalue{\cdot,\cdot}_{L}
.
As an important example,
F-F^{*}
is in its kernel because
\mathbb{S}LF=\mathbb{S}LF^{*}
.
This space can be interpreted as the space of forces that do not contribute to the dynamics if we regard
LF^{\prime}
as a probability current for any force
F^{\prime}
.

The first expression (
48
) is obtained by combining the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\pqty{\ev{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,F^*}_L}^{2}\leq\expectationvalue{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi}_{L}\expectationvalue{F^*,F^*}_{L},

(51)

and the following equality,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,F^*}_{L}=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}LF^{*}=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}LF=\expectationvalue{\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi,F}_{L}.

(52)

Equality is obtained in (
51
) when
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi
is parallel to
F^{*}
.
The infimum representation (
49
) for the excess EPR is proven by the following calculation: When
F^{\prime}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L
,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{F-F',F-F'}_{L}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{F^*+F-F^*-F',F^*+F-F^*-F'}_{L}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{F^*,F^*}_{L}+\expectationvalue{F-F^*-F',F-F^*-F'}_{L},

(53)

because

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{F^*,F-F^*-F'}_{L}=(\phi^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}L(F-F^{*}-F^{\prime})=0.

(54)

The second term in the third line of Eq. (
53
) is nonnegative and vanishes when
F^{\prime}=F-F^{*}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L
; thus the minimum value gives the excess EPR.

The supremum formula (
50
) of the housekeeping EPR can be shown in a parallel way to the excess case.
For
F^{\prime}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L
, we have
(\expectationvalue{F',F-F^*}_{L})^{2}\leq\expectationvalue{F',F'}_{L}\expectationvalue{F-F^*,F-F^*}_{L}
and
\expectationvalue{F',F-F^*}_{L}=\expectationvalue{F',F}_{L}
.
The latter equality is because

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{F',F^*}_{L}=-(\phi^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}LF^{\prime}=0.

(55)

Thus, the inequality

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}\geq\frac{\pqty{\ev{F',F}_L}^{2}}{\expectationvalue{F',F'}_{L}}

(56)

is obtained for any
F^{\prime}\in\ker\mathbb{S}L
.
The equality is attained when
F^{\prime}
is proportional to
F-F^{*}
, which also belongs to
\ker\mathbb{S}L
.

III.5
Gradient flow

We now generalize Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) using the Onsager-projective decomposition.
Let
\phi^{*}(t)
be the potential that gives
F^{*}(t)=-\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi^{*}(t)
at time
t
, and define a pseudo-canonical distribution
x^{\mathrm{pcan}}(t)
by

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{pcan}}(t)=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha}(t)}x_{\alpha}(t)e^{-\phi^{*}_{\alpha}(t)}.

(58)

In a Markov jump process,
Z_{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{i}p_{i}(t)e^{-\phi_{i}^{*}(t)}
is a normalization constant that does not depend on
\alpha
. In a chemical reaction network,
Z(t)
is any vector whose log becomes a conservation law, i.e.,
\ln Z(t)=(\ln Z_{\alpha}(t))\in\ker\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
holds. If there is no conservation law,
Z(t)
will be the vector of ones.
Then, we have the relation

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}=\evaluated{-\frac{d}{dt}D(x(t)\|x^\mathrm{pcan}(s))}_{s=t}.

(59)

This result follows because

\displaystyle\evaluated{\frac{d}{dt}D(x(t)\|x^\mathrm{pcan}(s))}_{s=t}=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{dx_{\alpha}}{dt}(\phi_{\alpha}^{*}+\ln Z_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=J^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi^{*}=-\langle F,F^{*}\rangle_{L}=-\langle F^{*},F^{*}\rangle_{L},

(60)

where the second equality follows from
\sum_{i}B_{ie}=0
for Markov jump processes and from
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\ln Z=0
for chemical reaction networks.
In particular, in a Markov jump process, by the decomposition
\phi^{*}=\epsilon^{*}+\ln p
, the pseudo-canonical distribution reads

\displaystyle p_{i}^{\mathrm{pcan}}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\epsilon_{i}^{*}}.

(61)

If
F^{*}=F
, the pseudo-energy level
\epsilon^{*}
becomes the actual energy level of each state.
The expression (
61
) is the reason why we refer to
x^{\mathrm{pcan}}
as the pseudo-canonical distribution.
Although this pseudo-canonical distribution depends on the instantaneous state,
it indicates the momentary “goal” of the dynamics, even if the system is attracted to a limit cycle.

Along with the pseudo-canonical distribution

, we can formally obtain a gradient flow representation of the continuity equation (
2
) even without detailed balance because

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla D(x\|x^{\mathrm{pcan}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\ln\frac{x}{x^{\mathrm{pcan}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}(\phi^{*}+\ln Z)

\displaystyle=-F^{*},

(62)

so that

\displaystyle-\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla D(x\|x^{\mathrm{pcan}})=\mathbb{S}LF^{*}=\frac{dx}{dt}.

(63)

This is a generalization of the gradient flow (
24
). We remark that, even if the rates entering the dynamics via (
3
) are time-independent,
x^{\mathrm{pcan}}
generally depends on time, see Eq. (

).

III.6
Brief comparison with Hatano–Sasa decomposition

There are other ways to decompose the EPR into excess and housekeeping contributions.
One well-known approach is the Hatano–Sasa (HS) decomposition
Hatano and Sasa 2001

Esposito and Van den
Broeck 2010

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{hk}},

(64)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=J^{\mathsf{T}}(F-F^{\mathrm{st}}),

(65)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle=J^{\mathsf{T}}F^{\mathrm{st}}.

(66)

If the flux is written in mass action form, the difference between the forces becomes

\displaystyle F_{e}-F_{e}^{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}(\nu_{\alpha\iota(e)}-\nu_{\alpha\iota^{\prime}(e)})\ln\frac{x_{\alpha}}{x^{\mathrm{st}}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-[\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi]_{e}

(67)

with
\Psi_{\alpha}=\ln(x_\alpha/x_\alpha^\mathrm{st})
. Then, we obtain another expression for the HS excess EPR

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle=-J^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi,

(68)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\ln\frac{x}{x^{\mathrm{st}}},

(69)

which is similar to the expression
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}=-J^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}
that follows from the definition (
34
) and
-\mathbb{S}L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\phi^{*}=\mathbb{S}J
.
The nonnegativity of the HS excess and housekeeping EPR can be proven only when the steady state satisfies

For a Markov-jump process, this condition is precisely the steady-state condition.
For a chemical reaction network, however, this condition is called
complex balance

\mathbb{S}J^{\mathrm{st}}=\nu BJ^{\mathrm{st}}=0

Rao and Esposito 2016
.
On the other hand, the Onsager excess EPR is defined even in the absence of stable steady state, which we will show in Sec.

IV.1
Short-time TUR: Markov jump process

The variational expression (
48
) allows us to obtain a set of refined short-time TURs.
In this section, we focus on Markov jump processes.
We define the edgewise dynamical activity (traffic)
\chi_{e}
by
Maes and Netočnỳ 2008

\displaystyle\chi_{e}:=k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}+k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}.

(70)

From the inequality in Eq. (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\ell_{e}=\frac{J_{e}}{F_{e}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\chi_{e}.

(71)

The equality holds if and only if
p
satisfies
k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}=k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}
, i.e., in the equilibrium state.

The numerator of the right-hand side in Eq. (
48
),
\left\langle\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,F\right\rangle_{L}
, which reads
\langle B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,F\rangle_{L}
now, is the time derivative of the expectation value
\langle\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}p_{i}\psi_{i}
because

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle{\psi}\rangle=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}BJ=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}BLF=\langle B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,F\rangle_{L}.

(72)

Note that here we assume that
\psi
does not have any time-dependence.
Thus, from the variational formula (
48
), we have a TUR

\displaystyle\frac{(d_{t}\langle\psi\rangle)^{2}}{\langle B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi\rangle_{L}}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}

(73)

for any observable
\psi
.
Therefore, as long as we know the edgewise Onsager matrix
L
, we can estimate the excess EPR by measuring the rate of change of the average
\langle\psi\rangle
.

Because of the inequality in Eq. (
71
), the denominator is bounded from above by the short-time limit of the variance as

\displaystyle\langle B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi\rangle_{L}\leq\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{\tau}\psi)}{2\tau}=:\frac{1}{2}D_{\psi},

(74)

where
\Delta_{\tau}\psi=\psi_{i(t+\tau)}-\psi_{i(t)}
and the variance is asymptotically given by
Otsubo et al. 2020

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\,(\Delta_{\tau}\psi)=\tau\sum_{e}\chi_{e}([B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}+o(\tau).

(75)

This inequality shows a weaker bound

\displaystyle\frac{2(d_{t}\langle\psi\rangle)^{2}}{D_{\psi}}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(76)

Although equality can only be achieved in equilibrium, where we trivially have
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}=0
, the inequality can be tight near equilibrium, where the difference between the arithmetic and logarithmic mean in Eq. (
71
) is small.
Moreover, Eq. (
76
) does not require information about the Onsager matrix
L
. Therefore, is much more tractable than Eq. (
73
) and gives an estimate of the excess EPR that depends only on the rate of change of the average and the short-time fluctuations of some observable.
This inequality partly generalizes the short-time TUR derived in Ref.
Otsubo et al. 2020
for the case where currents are expressed as changes of observables as above.

While the interpretation of the inequalities derived from Eq. (
48
) as TURs was straightforward,
we need to develop a trajectory level description to understand the meaning of the inequality suggested by Eq. (
50
).
This is done later when we discuss finite-time TURs.

IV.2
Short-time TUR: Chemical Reaction Network

Next, we consider chemical reaction networks.
Because of the inequality between logarithmic mean and arithmetic mean (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi\rangle_{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}([\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}\frac{J_{e}}{F_{e}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}([\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}(\mathcal{K}_{e}(c)+\mathcal{K}_{-e}(c)).

(77)

The scaled diffusion coefficient of a chemical reaction network is defined by
Ge and Qian 2016
;
Yoshimura and Ito 2021

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{\alpha\beta}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}\mathbb{S}_{\alpha e}\mathbb{S}_{\beta e}(\mathcal{K}_{e}(c)+\mathcal{K}_{-e}(c)).

(78)

Using this quantity, the right-hand side of Eq. (
77
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e}([\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}(\mathcal{K}_{e}(c)+\mathcal{K}_{-e}(c))=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{D}\psi.

(79)

Moreover, because

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,F\rangle_{L}=\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}J=\frac{d}{dt}(\psi^{\mathsf{T}}c),

(80)

we obtain a TUR for a chemical reaction network

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{D}\psi}\bqty{\frac{d}{dt}(\psi^\transpose c)}^{2}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(81)

This inequality generalizes the previous result in Ref.
Yoshimura and Ito 2021
: because
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}\leq\sigma
,

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\tilde{D}_{\alpha\alpha}}\pqty{\frac{dc_\alpha}{dt}}^{2}\leq\sigma,

(82)

follows by setting
\psi_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}
.

IV.3
Finite-time TURs

The above results relate the excess EPR to the short-time fluctuations of observables.
In the following, we obtain finite-time TURs for Markov jump process.
Proofs of these relations are found in Appendix
D
. Note that these proofs rely on the expression for the path probability of the system, so their generalization to chemical reaction networks is challenging and we leave it for future work.

In general, we consider a stochastic current observable
\mathcal{J}_{w}
with weight
w_{e}(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{w}=\sum_{k}w_{e_{k}}(t_{k})

(83)

for a stochastic trajectory with jumps
e_{k}
at time
t_{k}
(
k=1,2,\dots
). We assume the weight satisfies
w_{e}=-w_{-e}
.
The average during a time interval
[0,\tau]
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sum_{e}w_{e}(t)J_{e}(t)

(84)

We introduce a decomposition of the current observable into the excess and the housekeeping contribution
Dechant et al. 2022b
as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sum_{e}w_{e}(t)J_{e}^{*}(t)

(85)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sum_{e}w_{e}(t)(J_{e}(t)-J_{e}^{*}(t)),

(86)

where we defined
J_{e}^{*}(t):=\ell_{e}(t)F_{e}^{*}(t)
. As shown above, these currents induce the same time evolution as
J
, since
BJ^{*}=BLF^{*}=BJ
.

By using the housekeeping component of a current observable, we can show the TUR for the Onsager housekeeping EP

\displaystyle\frac{(\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau})^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}},

(87)

with variance
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w}):=\langle(\mathcal{J}_{w}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau})^{2}\rangle_{\tau}
.
Unlike the short-time TURs discussed above, this relation is valid for any finite time duration.
It states that the conventional form of the steady-state TUR remains valid if we consider the respective housekeeping contributions for both the current and the entropy production.

Now, we can derive a short-time TUR for the housekeeping EPR.
Since the time interval
[0,\tau]
can be replaced with
[t,t+\Delta t]
, by taking limit
\Delta t\to 0
, we obtain the short-time TUR

\displaystyle\frac{2(d_{t}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{t})^{2}}{\mathcal{D}_{w}}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}

(88)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{w}=\sum_{e}\chi_{e}w_{e}^{2}=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}{\Delta t}

(89)

because

\displaystyle d_{t}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{t}=\sum_{e}w_{e}(t)(J_{e}(t)-J_{e}^{*}(t))

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\sum_{e}w_{e}(t^{\prime})(J_{e}(t^{\prime})-J_{e}^{*}(t^{\prime})).

(90)

When we assume the weight satisfies
w=(w_{e})_{e=1,\dots,E}\in\ker{\mathbb{S}L}
, we see that
w^{\mathsf{T}}J^{*}=w^{\mathsf{T}}LF^{*}=0
because
F^{*}\in\mathrm{im}\,\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Thus, we find another TUR

\displaystyle\frac{2(d_{t}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{t})^{2}}{\mathcal{D}_{w}}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}

(91)

for
w\in\ker\mathbb{S}L
. Because
d_{t}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{t}=w^{\mathsf{T}}J=\langle w,F\rangle_{L}
, this is derived from Eq. (
50
) in the same way as we did in Sec.
IV.1
.
What is important here is that, by introducing a current observable, we can interpret the inequality we get as a TUR.

If we change the time interval as in Refs.
Koyuk and Seifert 2020
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
, we can also derive a finite-time TUR for the Onsager excess EP. We assume that the transition rates
k_{e}(t)
depend on
t
and
\tau
in the form of
t/\tau
only.
For example, with
k_{0}
constant,
k_{e}(t)=k_{0}\exp(t/\tau)
is acceptable but
k_{e}(t)=k_{0}\exp(t)
and
k_{e}(t)=\tau^{-1}\exp(t/\tau)
are not.
Physically, this assumption means that any external operation on the system is accelerated when
\tau
becomes smaller, and vice versa.
We put the same assumption on the time dependence of the weight
w
.
Under these assumptions, we can prove the finite-time TUR

\displaystyle\frac{(\tau\partial_{\tau}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau})^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}},

(92)

where
\partial_{\tau}
is taken while holding fixed the normalized time
t/\tau
that defines
k_{e}
(and enters into the definition of
\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}
, Eq. (
84
), via
J_{e}
).

When the weight is given by a scalar time-dependent observable
\psi(t/\tau)
as
w=B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi
,
\mathcal{J}_{w}
becomes the change
\Delta_{\tau}\psi=\psi(1)-\psi(0)
and we have the relations

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\psi^{\mathsf{T}}BJ=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(1)\rangle_{t=\tau}-\langle\psi(0)\rangle_{t=0}

(93)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\psi^{\mathsf{T}}B(J-J^{*})=0.

(94)

Then, equation (
92
) reads

\displaystyle\frac{(\tau d_{\tau}\langle\psi(1)\rangle_{t=\tau})^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{\tau}\psi)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(95)

This can be understood as a refinement of the TUR for time-dependent driving derived in Ref.
Koyuk and Seifert 2020
. In particular, when one considers the change of scalar observables, rather than currents, the resulting TUR can only estimate the excess part of the entropy production.
By taking the limit
\tau\to 0
, we recover the short-time TUR (
76
).

Since the proofs of the finite-time TURs are lengthy and technically similar to existing results, we give them in Appendix
D
, only explaining their underlying idea briefly at this point.
The structures of the proofs are essentially the same.
The first step is to parameterize the rate constant and compare the two path probabilities for two close parameters.
The difference between the two path probabilities is measured by a KL divergence which bounds from above the ratio of two quantities: (1) the difference between the averages of the current observable given by the different parameters and (2) the variance of the current observable. This inequality is obtained from the fluctuation-response inequality
Dechant and Sasa 2020
.
The difference between the observables can be shown to be equal to
\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle
or
\tau\partial_{\tau}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle
depending on the way the rates are parameterized.
At the same time, the KL divergence bounds from below the Onsager excess or housekeeping EP. Combining the two inequalities, we obtain the uncertainty relations.

V.1
Generalization of the Wasserstein distance

In our final set of main theoretical results, we provide a generalization of the Wasserstein distance measure (
26
) and the speed limit (
29
). In this section, we assume that the kinetics
\mathcal{K}
do not depend on time explicitly.

As we reviewed in Sec.
II.3
, the Wasserstein distance has been defined for Markov jump processes with detailed balanced steady states.
Now, we find a general definition applicable to both Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x^{(0)},x^{(1)}):=\inf_{x,f}\pqty{\int_0^1dt\;\|f(t)\|_{L(x(t))}^2}^{1/2}

(96)

under the following conditions

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=\mathbb{S}L(x)f,\quad x(t=0)=x^{(0)},\;\;x(t=1)=x^{(1)}.

(97)

As in Eq. (
28
), the unit time interval can be replaced with an arbitrary one
[0,\mathcal{T}]
and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})=\inf_{x,f}\pqty{\mathcal{T}\int_0^\mathcal{T} dt\;\|f(t)\|_{L(x(t))}^2}^{1/2}

(98)

for any
\mathcal{T}>0
, with the last condition of Eq. (
97
) replaced with
x(\mathcal{T})=x^{(1)}
.
Because
L(x)=\mathrm{diag}(\ell_{e}(x))
is defined by

\displaystyle\ell_{e}(x)=\frac{\mathcal{K}_{e}(x)-\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x)}{\ln(\mathcal{K}_e(x)/\mathcal{K}_{-e}(x))},

(99)

\mathcal{W}
is defined in reference to some kinetics
\mathcal{K}
.
Here,
x(t)
and
f(t)
are taken as independent variables in the minimization which are related by
d_{t}x=\mathbb{S}L(x)f
; in particular,
f(t)
is not necessarily equal to the original thermodynamic force
F(x(t))
.
If there is no pair of
x
and
f
that satisfies the continuity equation in Eq. (
97
) for a given
(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})
, we may let
\mathcal{W}=\infty
. However, we do not consider such a situation, because if
(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})
are realized as the initial and final state of the dynamics (
2
), then
x(t)
and
F(x(t))
are a solution.

We can show that the optimal force that provides the value of
\mathcal{W}
is conservative. That is, we have the formula

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})=\inf_{x,\psi}\pqty{\mathcal{T}\int_0^\mathcal{T} dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^\transpose\psi(t)\|_{L(x(t))}^2}^{1/2}

(100)

with the conditions

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=\mathbb{S}L(x)\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,\quad x(t=0)=x^{(0)},\quad x(t=\mathcal{T})=x^{(1)}.

(101)

We give the proof of this formula in Appendix
E
.

The mathematical features of the generalized Wasserstein distance are expected to be similar to those of Maas’s distance
Maas 2011
.
In this paper, we demonstrate two important properties of the Wasserstein distance, namely the constant speed property of a geodesic
Erbar and Maas 2012
and the Kantorovich duality
Erbar et al. 2019
.

We first explain that a geodesic with respect to the Wasserstein distance has constant speed.
In Ref.
Erbar and Maas 2012
, the authors proved that there exists a geodesic
p^{*}
accompanied by potential
\psi^{*}
with respect to their original definition of the Wasserstein distance (
26
).
This geodesic has constant speed, meaning that
\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|_{L(p^{*}(t))}=\mathrm{const}
. As we show, the proof is also valid if we assume there is a geodesic
x^{*}
with potential
\psi^{*}
for the generalized Wasserstein distance (
100
). Because of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, for an arbitrary
\{x,\psi\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(x(t))}^{2}\geq\left(\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(x(t))}\right)^{2}.

(102)

Moreover, using a reparametrization discussed in Ref.
Dolbeault et al. 2009
,
we can also prove the opposite inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|_{L(x^{*}(t))}^{2}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|_{L(x^{*}(t))}\right)^{2}.

(103)

for the geodesic pair
\{x^{*},\psi^{*}\}
.
We provide the detail of this technique in Appendix
F
.
Because the equality of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (
102
) is attained only if
\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(x(t))}
is constant, the geodesic has the constant speed

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|_{L(x^{*}(t))}=\frac{\mathcal{W}(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})}{\mathcal{T}}.

(104)

Next, for a Markov jump process, we prove the Kantorovich duality representation
Villani 2009
of the Wasserstein distance as in Ref.
Erbar et al. 2019
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{W}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})^{2}=\sup_{\psi}\pqty{\langle\psi(\mathcal{T})\rangle_1-\langle\psi(0)\rangle_0},

(105)

where
\psi(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{S}

(t\in[0,\mathcal{T}])
has to satisfy

\displaystyle q^{\mathsf{T}}d_{t}\psi(t)+\frac{1}{2}\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(q)}^{2}\leq 0

(106)

for any probability distribution
q
, and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{i}
(
i=0,1
) is the expectation value under
p^{(i)}
.
The maximizer
\psi^{**}
of Eq. (
105
) and the minimizer
\{p^{*},\psi^{*}\}
of Eq. (
100
) provide the same conservative force,
B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}=B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{**}
.
Furthermore, it can be shown that
\psi^{**}
and
p^{*}
attain the equality in Eq. (
106
) as

\displaystyle(p^{*}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}d_{t}\psi^{**}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{**}(t)\|_{L(p^{*}(t))}^{2}=0,

(107)

which can be seen as a generalization of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.
While the duality representation characterizes the Wasserstein distance as the maximum potential difference without considering the time evolution of the distribution,
we can use the Hamilton–Jacobi equation with the differential equation in Eq. (
101
) to obtain the optimal solution.
We provide a proof of the duality formula (
105
) in Appendix
G
; there, we assume the existence of an optimal time evolution of
p
and
\psi
, that is, a geodesic. A proof that is more rigorous but restricted to systems with detailed balanced steady states can be found in Ref.
Erbar et al. 2019
.

V.2
Wasserstein distance and Onsager excess EPR

Here we show that, analogously to the continuous case
Aurell et al. 2012
;
Dechant and Sakurai 2019
;
Nakazato and Ito 2021
;
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
, the Wasserstein distance is connected with the excess entropy production.
To this end, we employ the definition given in Eqs. (
98
) and (
97
).

Let
\{\hat{x}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
be a solution of the continuity equation (
2
).
Note that
\tau
indicates the physical time interval we focus on while
\mathcal{T}
is an arbitrary time parameter.
If we set
x^{(0)}=\hat{x}(t)
,
x^{(1)}=\hat{x}(t+dt)
, and
\mathcal{T}=dt
in Eq. (
98
), condition (
97
) reads

\begin{gathered}x(dt)-x(0)=\mathbb{S}L(\hat{x}(t))f(0)dt+o(dt),\\
x(0)=\hat{x}(t),\quad x(dt)=\hat{x}(t+dt).\end{gathered}

(108)

Hence for an infinitesimal time interval
dt
, the Wasserstein distance can be expanded as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t),\hat{x}(t+dt))^{2}

\displaystyle=dt^{2}\inf_{f|d_{t}\hat{x}(t)=\mathbb{S}L(\hat{x}(t))f}\|f\|_{L(\hat{x}(t))}^{2}+o(dt^{2}).

(109)

The condition of minimization can be translated into
\{f\mid F(\hat{x}(t))-f\in\ker\mathbb{S}L\}
, hence the leading term is identified with the excess EPR
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}
from Eq. (
49
).
We finally obtain the relation

\displaystyle\pqty{\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t),\hat{x}(t+dt))}{dt}}^{2}=\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(110)

Intuitively, in the definition of the Wasserstein distance Eq. (
98
), we optimize over the thermodynamic forces and the corresponding evolution of the state connecting the initial and final state.
By contrast, in computing the excess EPR, we restrict the evolution of the state to the one of the original dynamics and only optimize over the forces.
In the short-time limit of a single time-step, there is no evolution of the state, so both problems are equivalent and we can identify the Wasserstein distance and the excess EPR with each other.

V.3
Speed limit

We define
\dot{l}
and
l
by

\displaystyle\dot{l}(t):=\lim_{dt\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t),\hat{x}(t+dt))}{dt},\quad l:=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\dot{l}(t).

(111)

While
\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(0),\hat{x}(\tau))
gives the shortest length between the initial state
\hat{x}(0)
and the final state
\hat{x}(t)
,
l
quantifies the length of the path
\{\hat{x}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
in terms of the Wasserstein metric.
Using
l
, we have the speed limit, as in Ref.
Nakazato and Ito 2021
,

\displaystyle l^{2}\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(112)

This is because of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
l^{2}=(\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\dot{l})^{2}\leq(\int_{0}^{\tau}dt)(\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\dot{l}^{2})
and the equality (
110
).

By the constant speed property, the following triangle inequality is proven for any
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in[0,\tau]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t_{1}),\hat{x}(t_{3}))\leq\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t_{1}),\hat{x}(t_{2}))+\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(t_{2}),\hat{x}(t_{3})).

(113)

This relation leads to an inequality between the two distance measures as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(0),\hat{x}(\tau))\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\dot{l}(t)=l.

(114)

The equality holds only when the distribution changes at the constant speed

\displaystyle\dot{l}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(0),\hat{x}(\tau))}{\tau}.

(115)

By combining Eqs. (
112
) and (
114
), we obtain the speed limit

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\hat{x}(0),\hat{x}(\tau))^{2}\leq\tau\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(116)

This generalizes the Vu and Hasegawa speed limit (
29
), which was introduced in Ref.
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2021
for systems with detailed balanced steady states, to general Markovian dynamics on a graph which may have no stable steady state.

V.4
Relation to
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance

Another distance that has been considered in the theory of optimal transport is the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance
Villani 2009
;
Dechant 2022
.
This distance is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)}):=\inf_{\Pi}\sum_{i,j}d_{ij}\Pi_{ij},

(117)

where the infimum is taken over the couplings between the probability distributions
p^{(0)}
and
p^{(1)}
, that is,

\displaystyle\Pi_{ij}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{j}\Pi_{ij}=p_{i}^{(0)},\quad\sum_{i}\Pi_{ij}=p_{j}^{(1)},

(118)

and
d_{ij}
is a weight that satisfies the axioms of distance:
d_{ij}=d_{ji}\geq 0
,
d_{ij}=0
if and only if
i=j
, and
d_{ij}\leq d_{ik}+d_{kj}
.
The
(i,j)
-element of a coupling represents how much of the mass on
i
is moved to
j
and
d
evaluates the cost of the transportation.
Here we set
d_{ij}=1
if
i
and
j
are directly connected, and assume that the graph of states is connected.

In Ref.
Dechant 2022
, one of us derived the Kantorovich duality for this
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})=\sum_{i}\xi_{i}(p_{i}^{(1)}-p_{i}^{(0)}),

(119)

where
\xi
is a potential that satisfies
\xi_{j}-\xi_{i}=d_{ij}=1
if and only if the optimal coupling
\Pi^{*}
is positive on
(i,j)
,
\Pi_{ij}^{*}>0
. Using this formula, we can prove the following relation between the
L^{2}
- and
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\mathcal{A}}[p^{*}]}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})^{2}\geq\mathcal{W}_{1}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})^{2},

(120)

where we write the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance as
\mathcal{W}_{2}
and
\bar{\mathcal{A}}[p^{*}]
is the time-averaged dynamical activity of the minimizer
p^{*}
of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}[p^{*}]=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sum_{e}(k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{*}+k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}^{*}).

(121)

This is similar to the inequality
W_{2}(p_{0},p_{\tau})\geq W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\tau})
for the
L^{2}
- and
L^{1}
- Wasserstein distances of continuous probability distributions
Villani 2009
, apart from the prefactor that represents how frequently transitions occur in the optimal path on average.
We provide the proof in Appendix
H
.
We remark that in the discrete case, the
L^{2}
-distance defined above is a dynamics-wise measure and depends on the concrete values of the rates.
By contrast, the
L^{1}
-distance depends only on the connectivity of the state network.
This differs from the continuous case, where both distances are defined in terms of the Euclidean distance.

VII
Discussion

In this paper, we developed a geometrical perspective on dissipation in general Markovian dynamics on networks.
In particular, we found a geometrical excess/housekeeping decomposition of the entropy production rate that helps us understand dynamics in terms of dissipation.
Our decomposition leads to refinements of several thermodynamic uncertainty relations.
We have also generalized the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein geometry of a Markovian dynamics on a network, which allows us to derive the thermodynamic speed limit.
Because our results are valid for very general dynamics, we expect that they will offer novel insights in various concrete systems.

The geometrical decomposition given by the projection in (
31
) shows how dynamics can be divided into two modes, one being cyclic and one being a relaxation driven by a conservative force, which can be written as a gradient flow.
Even if a stable steady state does not exist, we can identify an instantaneous target of the relaxation mode as in Eq. (
58
). The Onsager excess entropy production quantifies the dissipation due to this relaxation.
On the other hand, the cyclic mode keeps the system out of equilibrium.
The size of the cyclic mode defines the Onsager housekeeping entropy production, which can be further decomposed into contributions from individual cycles, generalizing Schnakenberg’s decomposition as in Eq. (
43
).
Because these interpretations apply to both Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks, our decomposition provides a clear and general thermodynamic separation of motion.

We note that one may define a decomposition of the EPR using an orthogonal projection onto the image of
B^{\mathsf{T}}
instead of
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
as we have done.
For a Markov jump process, it provides the same results, while for a chemical reaction network, it gives a different decomposition.
The excess part of this decomposition vanishes in a steady state only if the steady state is complex balanced, but both parts are always nonnegative in contrast to the HS decomposition, thus this decomposition could provide another perspective to chemical reaction networks.
However, it may be argued that the projection onto the image of
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
, as investigated here, is more physically meaningful. This is because it corresponds to conservative forces defined in terms of the stoichiometric coefficients of the reactions, as standard in chemical thermodynamics, rather than forces that depend explicitly on the reactant and product chemical complexes.

From the experimental point of view, the edgewise Onsager coefficient, and therefore the Onsager excess and housekeeping EPRs, require information about the kinetics.
For this reason, it may difficult to access them experimentally.
However, recent studies have shown that TURs can be utilized to obtain estimates of the entropy production
Manikandan et al. 2020
;
Otsubo et al. 2020
.
In a similar manner, we can use the refined TURs to estimate the excess and housekeeping EPR using measurable quantities.
Although we cannot generally attain equality in the TUR for a discrete system out of equilibrium, since part of the estimation error stems from the log mean inequality between the edgewise Onsager coefficient and the edgewise activity
Shiraishi 2021
, this error gets smaller as the system gets closer to a steady state.
In a two-level system, we can approach equality, as shown in Fig.
6
of Sec.
VI.1
, because the steady state is “detailed balanced” in the sense that there is no net current between the states, even though there is a steady flux between the reservoirs.

We have also extended Maas’s definition of
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance to a general Markovian dynamics on a network.
A mathematical advantage of Maas’s distance is that it can be related to master equation dynamics via the Wasserstein gradient flow
Maas 2011
,
compared with other optimal transport theoretical distances between two discrete distributions, e.g., the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance
Villani 2009
;
Dechant 2022
and another discretization of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance
Chow et al. 2012
.
We expect that a deterministic rate equation with a detailed-balanced steady state is also characterized as a Wasserstein gradient flow with the aid of our generalized Wasserstein distance, beyond the usual gradient flow expression as in Eq. (
24
)
Mielke 2011
.

The relation between the geometrical decomposition and the generalized Wasserstein distance presented in Eq. (
110
) is parallel to recent results for Langevin systems
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
.
Therefore, our generalization of Maas’s
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is not only important from a mathematical point of view, but also has a physical interpretation as the minimum excess entropy of a process connecting the initial and final state.
As discussed above, obtaining a meaningful “minimum entropy production” requires a constraint, that is, keeping some quantity fixed during the minimization
Dechant 2022
;
Remlein and Seifert 2021
;
Muratore-Ginanneschi et al. 2013
;
Ilker et al. 2022
;
Van Vu and Saito 2022
.
In the present study, we fix the Onsager coefficients, whereas in previous studies
Muratore-Ginanneschi et al. 2013
;
Remlein and Seifert 2021
;
Ilker et al. 2022
the symmetric parts or one of the two directions of the transition rates were fixed.
While the latter may be the intuitive choice from an operational point of view, our approach establishes the connection between minimum entropy production, conservative forces and optimal transport.
Although we have shown how the excess EPR can be understood as a minimum EPR at each time point,
further study is needed to investigate how the finite-time optimization of the Wasserstein distance works practically and how the different approaches are related to each other.

One drawback of the discrete
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is that it needs a kinetics to refer to.
Since the reference kinetics has to be time-independent, we cannot obtain the Wasserstein speed limit in externally driven systems.
We remark a point that prevents generalizing the Wasserstein distance to the case where the reference kinetics is time dependent.
While in the original definition, the time interval is not definite, we should care about how to define the time integral for a time-dependent reference.
Then, however we choose the way to do so, we cannot use the conventional reparametrization technique, which is well described in Appendix
F
, because it requires the reference kinetics to be time independent.
As a result, it becomes difficult to prove several properties, such as the triangle inequality and constant speed property of a geodesic.

Let us mention a complementary study that we have recently done.
In this paper, we considered an EPR decomposition by studying the space of thermodynamic forces using Euclidean geometry. However, it is also possible to define such a decomposition using the non-Euclidean information geometry.
We develop this perspective in Ref.
Kolchinsky et al. 2022
, where derive several thermodynamic bounds based on an information-geometric excess/housekeeping decomposition.

Finally, we remark about a missing piece: nonlinear systems in continuous space. Two of the present authors, with another coauthor, presented a geometric decomposition of the EPR for linear stochastic systems in continuous space
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b
. In this work,
we developed it for Markov jump processes (linear stochastic system) and chemical master equations (nonlinear deterministic systems) in discrete space. The story remains to be written for nonlinear systems in continuous space, such as reaction-diffusion chemical systems and hydrodynamic systems. For such systems, geometric techniques for decomposing EPR may shed light on new kinds of thermodynamic constraints and tradeoffs.

Appendix A
Equivalence of definitions of Wasserstein distance

Let us show our definitions of the Wasserstein distance (
26
) and (
100
) are consistent with previous studies, e.g., Ref.
Maas 2011
.
That is, the approach from
Maas 2011
is equivalent to ours, if detailed balance holds.
In Ref.
Maas 2011
, the author defines the relative distribution

\displaystyle\rho_{i}:=\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}^{\mathrm{eq}}}

(139)

and defines the Wasserstein distance by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)}):=\\
&\inf_{p,\psi}\left({\int_{0}^{1}dt\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})^{2}k_{i\to j}p^{\mathrm{eq}}_{i}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})}\right)^{1/2}\end{aligned}

(140)

with conditions

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{i}+\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\psi_{i})k_{i\to j}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})=0,

(141)

\displaystyle p(t=0)=p^{(0)},\quad p(t=1)=p^{(1)},

(142)

where
\mathbb{L}
is the logarithmic mean

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(a,b):=\frac{a-b}{\ln a-\ln b}.

(143)

First, by symmetry, the integrand is rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})^{2}k_{i\to j}p^{\mathrm{eq}}_{i}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e}([B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{\iota(e)},\rho_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}),

(144)

where we used the detailed balance condition
k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}=k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}
.
Moreover, the numerator of the logarithmic mean times
k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}
gives
J_{e}
because

\displaystyle k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}\rho_{\iota(e)}=k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}

(145)

\displaystyle k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}\rho_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}=k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}\rho_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}=k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}.

(146)

Furthermore, the denominator reads

\displaystyle\ln{\rho_{\iota(e)}}-\ln{\rho_{\iota'(e)}}=\ln\frac{k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}}{k_{-e}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}}=F_{e}

(147)

due to the detailed balance condition.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle k_{e}p_{\iota(e)}^{\mathrm{eq}}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{\iota(e)},\rho_{\iota^{\prime}(e)})=\frac{J_{e}}{F_{e}}=\ell_{e},

(148)

and the integrand is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{e}([B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{e})^{2}\ell_{e}=\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi\|_{L}^{2}.

(149)

We next consider the conditions for minimization.
From Eq. (
148
), we find condition (
141
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p_{i}+\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\psi_{i})\ell_{i\to j}=0,

(150)

where
\ell_{i\to j}=\ell_{e}
if the edge
e
corresponding to the transition
i\to j
is in the set of edges
\{1,\dots,E\}
, and
\ell_{i\to j}=0
if not (so
k_{i\to j}=k_{j\to i}=0
).
Due to the symmetry
\ell_{i\to j}=\ell_{j\to i}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\psi_{i})\ell_{i\to j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e:i\to*}\ell_{e}\sum_{j}B_{je}\psi_{j}-\sum_{e:*\to i}\ell_{e}\sum_{j}B_{je}\psi_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{e}B_{ie}\ell_{e}\sum_{j}B_{je}\psi_{j}=-[BLB^{\mathsf{T}}\psi]_{i},

(151)

therefore the conditions are equivalent to ours in Eq. (
27
).

Finally, we complete proving the equivalence

\inf_{p,\psi}\left({\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}dt\;\sum_{i,j}(\psi_{i}-\psi_{j})^{2}k_{i\to j}p^{\mathrm{eq}}_{i}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})}\right)^{1/2}\\
=\inf_{p,\psi}\left({\int_{0}^{1}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi\|_{L}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

(152)

with equivalent conditions

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{i}+\sum_{j}(\psi_{j}-\psi_{i})k_{i\to j}\mathbb{L}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})=0,

(153)

\displaystyle p(t=0)=p^{(0)},\quad p(t=1)=p^{(1)}

(154)

for the left-hand side and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p=BLB^{\mathsf{T}}\psi,\;p(t=0)=p^{(0)},\;p(t=1)=p^{(1)}

(155)

for the right-hand side.

Let
i
denote a point on
n
-dimensional square lattice with the lattice constant
\Delta x\ll 1
. The discrete probability distribution
p_{i}
can be approximated by a smooth density function
P(x)
as
p_{i}\simeq P(i)(\Delta x)^{n}
.
A transition can be designated by its starting point and the direction
d=\pm 1,\dots,\pm n
. With
w_{i}^{(d)}
being the transition rate of the jump from
i
in direction
d
, we have the master equation

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{d=\pm 1}^{\pm n}\left(w_{i+d}^{(-d)}p_{i+d}-w_{i}^{(d)}p_{i}\right),

(156)

where we make the time dependence implicit and
i+d
is the point next to
i
in the direction of
d
. Note that now each edge is determined by the combination of the starting point and the direction of movement.

As in previous work
Van Vu and Saito 2022
;
Van den Broeck and Esposito 2010
, we assume the transition rates are expanded as the series

\displaystyle w_{i}^{(d)}=\frac{D_{d}(i)}{\Delta x^{2}}+\frac{f_{d}(i)}{\Delta x}+o(1).

(157)

Here, we assume
D_{d}(x)
to be a symmetric function (
D_{-d}(x)=D_{d}(x)
)
and
f_{d}(x)
anti-symmetric (
f_{-d}(x)=-f_{d}(x)
). Then the master equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\sum_{d=\pm 1}^{\pm n}\left(D_{-d}(i+d)p_{i+d}-D_{d}(i)p_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\sum_{d=\pm 1}^{\pm n}\left(f_{-d}(i+d)p_{i+d}-f_{d}(i)p_{i}\right)+o(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{D_{d}(i+d)p_{i+d}+D_{d}(i-d)p_{i-d}-2D_{d}(i)p_{i}}{\Delta x^{2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{f_{d}(i+d)p_{i+d}-f_{d}(i-d)p_{i-d}}{2\Delta x}+o(1).

(158)

By taking the limit
\Delta x\to 0
, we have the Fokker–Planck equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x)=-\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}\left(f_{d}(x)P(x)\right)+\sum_{d=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{d}^{2}}\left(D_{d}(x)P(x)\right).

(159)

Now, we can show that to the leading order, the current and the force on edge
(i,d)
are given as

\displaystyle J_{d}(i)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{f_{d}(i)p_{i}}{\Delta x}-\frac{\partial_{d}(D_{d}(i)p_{i})}{\Delta x},

(160)

\displaystyle F_{d}(i)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{f_{d}(i)}{D_{d}(i)}\Delta x-\frac{\partial_{d}(D_{d}(i)p_{i})}{D_{d}(i)p_{i}}\Delta x,

(161)

where
\partial_{d}(D_{d}(i)p_{i})
stands for
(D_{d}(i+d)p_{i+d}-D_{d}(i)p_{i})/\Delta x
.
Thus we have

\displaystyle\ell_{d}(i)=\frac{D_{d}(i)p_{i}}{\Delta x^{2}}+o(\Delta x^{-2}),

(162)

which shows that fixing the functional form of the Onsager coefficients is similar to fixing the diffusion coefficient in a Langevin system.

Appendix C
Detailed comparison with HS decomposition

In addition to the discussion given in Sec.
III.6
, we can further establish a connection between the HS decomposition and Onsager-projective decomposition.
Let
P_{\Psi}
be the orthogonal projection operator onto the vector
\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi
with respect to the metric
L
, with
\Psi
given by Eq. (
69
),

\displaystyle P_{\Psi}:=\frac{\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}}{\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}}L.

(163)

We define the pseudo-HS decomposition by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}

\displaystyle:=\|P_{\Psi}F\|_{L}^{2},

(164)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{hk}}

\displaystyle:=\|(I-P_{\Psi})F\|_{L}^{2}.

(165)

Because
P_{\Psi}
is an orthogonal projection operator, the sum of the two terms is equal to the total EPR:

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{hk}}.

(166)

Since
PP_{\Psi}=P_{\Psi}
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}\leq\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}},

(167)

which allows us to define a coupling term as in Refs.
Dechant et al. 2022a
;
Dechant et al. 2022b

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathrm{cpl}}:=\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}\geq 0,

(168)

and decompose the EPR into three positive contributions

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}+\sigma^{\mathrm{cpl}}+\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}.

(169)

However, the pseudo-HS decomposition does not generally coincide with the genuine HS decomposition.
A straightforward calculation reveals their connection

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{pHS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{(\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}})^{2}}{\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}}

(170)

If
\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}=\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}
holds, the two give the same value, but it is not the case in general.

Moreover, the difference between
\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}
is of the same order as each individual term even when the system is near a steady state; in other words, not small.
Let
\sup_{\alpha}|x_{\alpha}-x_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}|=\epsilon
be very small, with
x^{\mathrm{st}}
a steady state and assume
d_{t}x=\mathbb{S}J
is of the same order.
Then,
\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\mathrm{HS}}
and
\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}
are of order of
\epsilon^{2}
because
\Psi
and
\mathbb{S}J
are both of order
\epsilon
.
On the other hand, because
\mathbb{S}L^{\mathrm{st}}F^{\mathrm{st}}=\mathbb{S}J^{\mathrm{st}}=0
, the difference between
\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}
becomes

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}(J+L\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi)

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}L(F+\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi)

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}LF^{\mathrm{st}}

\displaystyle=-\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{S}(L-L^{\mathrm{st}})F^{\mathrm{st}},

(171)

where the third line comes from equation (
67
).
Here,
\Psi
and
L-L^{\mathrm{st}}
are of the order of
\epsilon
, while
F^{\mathrm{st}}
is generally of the order of
1
, so that we see the difference is of the order of
\epsilon^{2}
, just as
\sigma_{\mathrm{HS}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\Psi\|_{L}^{2}
.
The equality only holds when we have the detailed balance condition, that is for conservative forces. In this case, we find
\sigma=\sigma^{\text{ex}}_{\text{HS}}=\sigma^{\text{ex}}_{\text{pHS}}
.

Appendix D
Derivations of finite-time TURs

D.1
Housekeeping TUR

To prove the housekeeping TUR (
87
), let us consider a parametrization of transition rates by introducing the interpolated force
F_{e}^{\theta}=F_{e}^{*}+\theta(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})
as

\displaystyle k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}=\ell_{e}g(F_{e}^{\theta}),

(172)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad g(x)=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}},

(173)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
is an interpolation parameter.
We also define
J_{e}^{\theta}:=k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}-k_{-e}^{\theta}p_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}
.
One can easily show that
J_{e}^{\theta}=\ell_{e}F_{e}^{\theta}
and
J_{e}^{\theta}=J_{e}^{*}+\theta(J_{e}-J_{e}^{*})
hold.
Because
BJ^{\theta}=BJ
for any
\theta
and any time, the solution of the original (
\theta=1
) master equation
\{p(t)\}_{t\in[0,\tau]}
solves
d_{t}p(t)=BJ^{\theta}(t)
.

In general, we denote the path probability for the master equation by
\mathbb{P}
and that for the master equation with modified rate constants
k^{\theta}
, such as those defined in Eq. (
172
), by
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
.
The KL divergence between
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}
is given by

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int d\Gamma\;\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\Gamma)\ln\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\Gamma)}{\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{\pm e}\pqty{\ln\frac{k_e^\theta}{k_e^{\theta'}}+\frac{k_e^{\theta'}}{k_e^{\theta}}-1}k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta},

(174)

where
\Gamma
indicates a path,
\sum_{\pm e}
denotes the summation over both directions of edges, and
p^{\theta}
is the solution of the master equation given by
k^{\theta}
. We always assume the initial distribution does not depend on parameter
\theta
.
The KL divergence can also be expressed as

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;D(k^{\theta}p^{\theta}||k^{\theta^{\prime}}p^{\theta}),

(175)

where
D(k^{\theta}p^{\theta}||k^{\theta^{\prime}}p^{\theta})
is the generalized KL divergence between the two “distributions”
\{k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}\}_{e=\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots}
and
\{k_{e}^{\theta^{\prime}}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}\}_{e=\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots}
, that is,

\displaystyle D(k^{\theta}p^{\theta}||k^{\theta^{\prime}}p^{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pm e}\left[k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}\ln\frac{k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}}{k_{e}^{\theta^{\prime}}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}}-k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}+k_{e}^{\theta^{\prime}}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}\right].

(176)

When we consider
\theta^{\prime}=\theta+d\theta
with a very small
d\theta
, the KL divergence becomes

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta+d\theta})=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;D(k^{\theta}p^{\theta}||k^{\theta+d\theta}p^{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{d\theta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{\pm e}k_{e}^{\theta}p_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}\left(\partial_{\theta}\ln k_{e}^{\theta}\right)^{2}+o(d\theta^{2}).

(177)

In the parametrization (
172
), the relation

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\ln k_{e}^{\theta}=(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})\evaluated{\frac{d}{dx}\ln g(x)}_{x=F_e^\theta}

(178)

holds.
Thus, the KL divergence becomes

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta+d\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{d\theta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{\pm e}\ell_{e}(F_{e}-F_{e}^{*})^{2}h(F_{e}^{\theta})+o(d\theta^{2}),

(179)

with

\displaystyle\quad h(x)

\displaystyle=g(x)\pqty{\frac{d}{dx}\ln g(x)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\pqty{\frac{e^x-x-1}{x(e^x-1)}}^{2}.

(180)

One can easily show that
h(x)+h(-x)\leq 1/2
. Therefore, we have the following inequality regardless of the value of
\theta

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta+d\theta})\leq\frac{d\theta^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{hk}}.

(181)

The fluctuation-response inequality (12) of Ref.
Dechant and Sasa 2020
tells us that for any current observable
\mathcal{J}_{w}
defined in Sec.
IV.3
,

\displaystyle\frac{(\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta+d\theta}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta})^{2}}{2\mathrm{Var}^{\theta+d\theta}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}\leq D(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta+d\theta}),

(182)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta}
and
\mathrm{Var}^{\theta}(\cdot)
are the average and variance regarding
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
.
Because

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta}=\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle_{\tau}+\theta\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau},

(183)

setting
\theta=1
in Eq. (
182
) and combining with Eq. (
181
), we obtain the desired inequality in Eq. (
87
).

D.2
Excess TUR

Let us prove the relation (
92
). We separate the time dependence as
t=\tau s
, so that
s\in[0,1]
, and write
\bar{f}(s;\tau)=f(\tau s)
for any function of time
f
. The assumption on the functional form of the transition rate
k
leads to that
\bar{k}
only depends on
s
. The master equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\bar{p}(s;\tau)=\tau B\bar{J}(s;\tau),

(184)

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{e}(s;\tau)=\bar{k}_{e}(s)\bar{p}_{\iota(e)}(s;\tau)-\bar{k}_{-e}(s)\bar{p}_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}(s;\tau).

(185)

It is a natural assumption that the initial distribution
\bar{p}(0;\tau)
does not depend on
\tau
.

We first consider the tilting of the transition rates with
\tau
fixed

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)=\bar{k}_{e}(s)\exp(\frac{\bar{J}_e^*(s;\tau)}{\bar{\chi}_e(s;\tau)} \theta),

(186)

where
\theta
is the parameter of the tilted transition rate.
From the general formula (
177
),
the KL divergence between
\mathbb{P}^{0}
and
\mathbb{P}^{d\theta}
given by the tilting (
186
) can be calculated as

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{0}\|\mathbb{P}^{d\theta})=\frac{\tau}{2}d\theta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}ds\sum_{e}\frac{(\bar{J}_{e}^{*}(s;\tau))^{2}}{\bar{\chi}_{e}(s;\tau)}+o(d\theta^{2})

(187)

because
\partial_{\theta}\ln\bar{k}_{e}^{\theta}(s)=\bar{J}^{*}_{e}(s;\tau)/\bar{\chi}_{e}(s;\tau)
.
From the log mean inequality, we also have

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2}\int_{0}^{1}ds\sum_{e}\frac{(\bar{J}_{e}^{*}(s;\tau))^{2}}{\bar{\chi}_{e}(s;\tau)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau}{4}\int_{0}^{1}ds\sum_{e}\frac{(\bar{J}_{e}^{*}(s;\tau))^{2}}{\bar{\ell}_{e}(s;\tau)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\sum_{e}\frac{(J_{e}^{*}(t))^{2}}{\ell_{e}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(188)

Thus, the fluctuation-response inequality provides

\displaystyle\frac{(\left.\partial_{\theta}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta}\right|_{\theta=0})^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(189)

Next, we identify the quantity
\left.\partial_{\theta}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta}\right|_{\theta=0}
. Let
\{\bar{p}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\}_{s\in[0,1]}
be the solution of the master equation given by
\bar{k}^{\theta}_{e}(s;\tau)
,

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\bar{p}^{\theta}(s;\tau)=\tau B\bar{J}^{\theta}(s;\tau),

(190)

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)=\bar{k}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\bar{p}_{\iota(e)}^{\theta}(s;\tau)-\bar{k}_{-e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\bar{p}_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}^{\theta}(s;\tau),

(191)

where
\theta=0
reproduces the original dynamics Eqs. (
184
) and (
185
).
If we use the notation

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\theta}\bar{p}_{i}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\right|_{\theta=0}=r_{i}(s;\tau),

(192)

a straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\theta}\bar{J}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\right|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\bar{J}_{e}^{*}(s;\tau)+[\bar{k}_{e}(s)r_{\iota(e)}(s;\tau)-\bar{k}_{-e}(s)r_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}(s;\tau)].

(193)

Because
\tau B\bar{J}^{*}=\tau B\bar{L}\bar{F}^{*}=\tau B\bar{J}=\partial_{s}\bar{p}(s;\tau)
,
we find that
r
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{s}r_{i}(s;\tau)-\partial_{s}\bar{p}_{i}(s;\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{e}B_{ie}[\bar{k}_{e}(s)r_{\iota(e)}(s;\tau)-\bar{k}_{-e}(s)r_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}(s;\tau)].

(194)

We also consider another infinitesimal perturbation by changing
\tau
to
\theta\tau
where
\theta=1
also reproduces the original dynamics Eqs. (
184
) and (
185
).
The master equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\bar{p}(s;\theta\tau)=\theta\tau B\bar{J}(s;\theta\tau).

(195)

By calculating

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\theta}\partial_{s}\bar{p}(s;\theta\tau)\right|_{\theta=1}

\displaystyle=\left.\partial_{\theta}[\theta\tau B\bar{J}(s;\theta\tau)]\right|_{\theta=1},

(196)

we find

\displaystyle\partial_{s}[\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}_{i}(s;\tau)]-\partial_{s}\bar{p}_{i}(s;\tau)=\tau\sum_{e}B_{ie}\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{J}_{e}(s;\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau\sum_{e}B_{ie}\left[\bar{k}_{e}(s)[\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}_{\iota(e)}(s;\tau)]-\bar{k}_{-e}(s)[\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}(s;\tau)]\right].

(197)

It is the same equation as Eq. (
194
).
Because
\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}(s;\tau)
and
r(s;\tau)
satisfies the same first order differential equation and they share the initial condition
\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}(0;\tau)=r(0;\tau)=0
, we conclude that
r(s;\tau)=\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{p}(s;\tau)
and

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{e}(s)r_{\iota(e)}(s;\tau)-\bar{k}_{-e}(s)r_{\iota^{\prime}(e)}(s;\tau)=\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{J}_{e}(s;\tau).

(198)

Thus, Eq. (
193

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\theta}\bar{J}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\right|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\bar{J}_{e}^{*}(s;\tau)+\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{J}_{e}(s;\tau),

(199)

and the current observable changes by tilting as

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{\theta}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}^{\theta}\right|_{\theta=0}=\tau\int_{0}^{1}ds\sum_{e}w_{e}(s)\left.\partial_{\theta}\bar{J}_{e}^{\theta}(s;\tau)\right|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle_{\tau}+\tau\int_{0}^{1}ds\sum_{e}w_{e}(s)\tau\partial_{\tau}\bar{J}_{e}(s;\tau)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{ex}}\rangle_{\tau}+\tau\partial_{\tau}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\tau\partial_{\tau}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\rm hk}\rangle_{\tau}

(200)

Therefore, equation (
189
) finally leads to the TUR

\displaystyle\frac{(\tau\partial_{\tau}\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}\rangle_{\tau}-\langle\mathcal{J}_{w}^{\mathrm{hk}}\rangle_{\tau})^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{J}_{w})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(201)

We prove that
\mathcal{W}
provided in Eq. (
98

Here we prove the inequality in Eq. (

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{s}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{T})^{2}.

(209)

, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x^{(0)},x^{(1)})\leq\tilde{s}_{0}(\mathcal{T})=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|_{L(x^{*}(t))}.

(210)

Appendix G
Derivation of Eq. (
105
)

We give a proof of the duality formula (
105
).
We first prepare some relations regarding the functional
\mathcal{I}
defined in Appendix
E
.
Here, we concentrate on probability distributions, so the argument of the functional is
(p,f,\psi)
.
We again assume there exists an optimal solution
(p^{*},f^{*},\psi^{*})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{W}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})^{2}=\mathcal{I}[p^{*},f^{*},\psi^{*}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}(t)\|^{2}_{L(p^{*}(t))},

where we used the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{I}}{\delta p_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{W}(p^{(0)},p^{(1)})^{2}=\langle\psi^{*}(\mathcal{T})\rangle_{1}-\langle\psi^{*}(0)\rangle_{0},

and it is zero because

As we will show later, since

Rockafellar and Wets 2009

Therefore, we conclude

Let

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\prime}(\mathcal{T})\rangle_{1}-\langle\psi^{\prime}(0)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;d_{t}[(p^{*})^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\pqty{(\psi')^\transpose BL(p^*)B^\transpose\psi^*
+(p^*)^\transpose d_t\psi'}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\pqty{\frac{1}{2}\|B^\transpose\psi^*\|_{L(p^*)}^2
-\frac{1}{2}\|B^\transpose(\psi^*-\psi')\|_{L(p^*)}^2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi^{*}\|_{L(p^{*})}^{2}=\langle\psi^{*}(\mathcal{T})\rangle_{1}-\langle\psi^{*}(0)\rangle_{0},

(221)

where the third line follows from the condition (

(222)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad q^{\mathsf{T}}d_{t}\psi(t)+\frac{1}{2}\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(q)}^{2}\leq 0\quad\mathrm{for\;any\;probability\;distribution}\;q.

The concavity of

Let us prove that the log mean is concave.
The log mean has an integral form

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;x^{t}y^{1-t}.

\displaystyle H_{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\mathbb{L}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t(t-1)x^{t-2}y^{1-t}

\displaystyle H_{yy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\mathbb{L}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t(t-1)x^{t}y^{-1-t}

\displaystyle H_{xy}=H_{yx}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\mathbb{L}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t(1-t)x^{t-1}y^{-t}.

(227)

The determinant of the Hessian is positive because

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}\,H

\displaystyle=H_{xx}H_{yy}-H_{xy}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t(t-1)x^{t-2}y^{1-t}\int_{0}^{1}dt\;t(t-1)x^{t}y^{-1-t}-\pqty{\int_0^1 dt\;t(t-1)x^{t-1}y^{-t}}^{2}

The trace is shown to be negative because

x,y

are negative.
Therefore, the Hessian

Appendix H
Derivation of Eq. (

)

In this section, we provide the proof of the inequality in Eq. (

).
First, we characterize the

-Wasserstein distance by a functional slightly different from what we used in the previous sections. Next, we show relations between the
L^{2}
- and
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance by using the derived expression.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[q,\psi]=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\pqty{
-\|B^\transpose\psi(t)\|_{L(q(t))}^2
+2\psi(t)^\transpose d_t q(t)}.

(229)

Completing the square shows that this functional is maximized with respect to
\psi
when
d_{t}q(t)=BL(q(t))B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)
, and then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[q]:=\sup_{\psi}\mathcal{F}[q,\psi]=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(q(t))}^{2}.

(230)

If we choose a solution of the master equation
\hat{p}
as
q
, this amounts to the Onsager excess EP,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\hat{p}]=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,\sigma^{\mathrm{ex}}
.
The
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}(0),\hat{p}(\tau))^{2}=\tau\inf_{q}\sup_{\psi}\mathcal{F}[q,\psi]

(231)

with
q
satisfying the initial and final conditions,
q(0)=\hat{p}(0)
and
q(\tau)=\hat{p}(\tau)
.
We write the minimizer as
p^{*}
, so that
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{p}(0),\hat{p}(\tau))^{2}=\tau\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[p^{*}]
.

Let
(q,\psi)
satisfy the continuity equation

, so that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[q]=\mathcal{F}[q,\psi]
.
We now do not assume
q
is a solution of the master equation.
The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality leads to

\displaystyle\pqty{\int_0^\tau dt\; \varphi(t)^\transpose d_tq(t)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\pqty{\int_0^\tau dt\; \langle B^\transpose\varphi(t),B^\transpose\psi(t)\rangle_{L(q(t))}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\varphi(t)\|_{L(q(t))}^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\|B^{\mathsf{T}}\psi(t)\|_{L(q(t))}^{2}

\varphi

119
) between

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\xi(t)^{\mathsf{T}}d_{t}q(t)=\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\;\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}_{1}(q(t),q(t+\Delta t))}{\Delta t}\geq\mathcal{W}_{1}(\hat{p}(0),\hat{p}(\tau)),

If we choose
