Scalable Distributional Robustness in a Class of Non Convex Optimization with Guarantees
Avinandan Bose, Arunesh Sinha, Tien Mai

Abstract
Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) has shown lot of promise in providing robustness in learning as well as sample based optimization problems. We endeavor to provide DRO solutions for a class of sum of fractionals, non-convex optimization which is used for decision making in prominent areas such as facility location and security games. In contrast to previous work, we find it more tractable to optimize the equivalent variance regularized form of DRO rather than the minimax form. We transform the variance regularized form to a mixed-integer second order cone program (MISOCP), which, while guaranteeing near global optimality , does not scale enough to solve problems with real world data-sets. We further propose two abstraction approaches based on clustering and stratified sampling to increase scalability, which we then use for real world data-sets. Importantly, we provide near global optimality guarantees for our approach and show experimentally that our solution quality is better than the locally optimal ones achieved by state-of-the-art gradient-based methods. We experimentally compare our different approaches and baselines, and reveal nuanced properties of a DRO solution.



1
Introduction

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) is a popular approach employed in robust machine learning. Mostly, if not always, these works have focussed on the task of classification or regression. However, often in practical applications the end goal of learning is a decision output
z
, which requires yet another complex optimization that uses the output
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{N}
of a regressor
f(\cdot)
. For example, in facility location problem the learning of facility values is followed by an optimization using the values predicted to decide where to locate facilities and in security games adversary behavior model is learned and then an optimal defense allocation computed based on the learned model. Often the learning output is provided as public datasets with no access to the underlying private dataset used for such learning. In this set-up, we aim to provide robustness at the decision making level with access to only the non-robust learning output
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{N}
.

However, often the objective
F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})
of decision optimization is
non-convex
in the learning system output
x
unlike the convex objective of classification or regression, presenting significant scalability challenges. In general, for decision making and specifically for the problem domains we consider,
global optimality is important
as sub-optimal decisions can lead to large revenue loss or loss of life; thus, the local optimality provided by gradient based methods is not sufficient.
As a consequence, in this paper, we study the scenario of calculating DRO decisions using the given multi-dimensional real valued outputs
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{N}
of a non-robust learned
f
. A first result (Theorem
1
) characterizes the quality of DRO decision output compared to the scenario where we know the true
f^{*}
.
Our main focus is on addressing the scalability issue for the DRO decision making problem for a particular, but widely used, class of
sum of fractionals non-convex objective
.
This objective arises from the well-known
discrete choice models

Train 2003
of human behavior, which is known to
not
have scalable globally optimal solutions
(
Schaible 1995
;
Li et al. 2019
)
; we use this for tackling two different decision optimization problem in facility location and a robust version of Bayesian Stackelberg security games problem with quantal response. As far as we know, this is a first attempt to solve the aforementioned non-convex problems in a DRO setting to near global optimality.

Our
first contribution
is a modelling construct, where we reformualate the variance regularized form
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
of our non-convex sum of fractionals objective as a mixed integer second order cone program (MISOCP). While the MISOCP form provides more scalability than the original formulation and guaranteed solution quality (Theorem
2
), it still does not scale to real world sized datasets.
Our
second contribution
is a pair of approaches that achieves further scalability by splitting the problem space into sub-regions and solving a smaller MISOCP over representative samples from the sub-regions.
Under mild conditions, both approaches provide
global optimality guarantees
(Theorem
3
,
4
).

Our
final contribution
is detailed experiments validating the scalability of our approaches on a simulated security game problem as well as two variants of location facility using park and ride data-sets from New York
(
Holguin-Veras et al. 2012
)
. We compare with two gradient-based approaches
(
Lin et al. 2020
)
and show the superior solution quality achieved by our approach, which also reveals the need for global optimality. We further show a nuanced property of the DRO solution in providing better decisions for low probability scenarios over non-robust versions. Overall, our work
provides desired
robustness with globally optimal solution guarantees
.

Related work:

Our work is built on a recent line of research that connects the concepts of DRO and variance regularization
(
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
;
Duchi et al. 2021
;
Lam 2016
;
Maurer and Pontil 2009
;
Staib et al. 2019
)
. While most the previous studies along this research line focus on convex and continuous problems or problems with submodular objectives,
our work concerns a class of DRO problems with fractional structures, which are highly non-convex and requires new technical developments for globally optimal solution. Recent work
Yan et al. 2020
;
Qi et al. 2021
has addressed non-convex objectives in DRO using gradient based methods that converge to stationary points, which is insufficient for decision making as we experimentally show that stationary points and globally optimal points can yield very different decision utilities.

The literature on DRO is vast and we refer the reader to
Rahimian and Mehrotra 2019
for a review. DRO methods can be classified by different ways to define ambiguity sets of distributions, for instance, ambiguity sets based on
\phi
-divergences
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2013
;
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
;
Staib et al. 2019
)
or Wasserstein distances
(
Pflug and Wozabal 2007
;
Esfahani and Kuhn 2018
;
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. 2015
;
Blanchet and Murthy 2019
)
.
In this work, we focus on
\phi
-divergence based models, motivated by their interesting connections with variance regularization and the tractability of the resulting non-convex DRO models.

We show that our DRO methods can be used in some popular decision-making problems such as Stackelberg security game (SSG) with Quantal Response
(
Tambe 2011
;
Xu 2016
;
Fang et al. 2017
;
Sinha et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2012
;
Haghtalab et al. 2016
)
or competitive facility location under random utilities
(
Benati and Hansen 2002
;
Freire et al. 2016
;
Mai and Lodi 2020
;
Dam et al. 2021
)
. To the best of our knowledge, a DRO Bayesian model has not been studied in existing SSG works. In the context of competitive facility location under random utilities, we seem to be the first to bring DRO as a consideration. We handle a DRO version of a facility cost optimization problem, which has also never been studied in prior work.

Distributionally Robust Optimization
:
Consider

We seek to maximize the following objective function

For many classes of distributions the above is generally not tractable and one needs to sample

\max_{\textbf{z}}\sum_{n\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{n})

\max_{\textbf{z}}\min_{\widetilde{P}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\xi,n}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})]\right\}

where the ambiguity set

{\mathcal{D}}_{\phi}(P||Q)=\frac{1}{2}\int(dP/dQ-1)^{2}dQ
. The above optimization can be written equivalently as (
\Delta_{\xi,n}
defined below)

\max_{\textbf{z}}\min_{\textbf{p}\in\Delta_{\xi,n}}\Big\{\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\Big\}

(
DRO)

where

\Delta_{\xi,n}=\Big\{\textbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\Big|\;\sum_{i}p_{i}=1;\;||\textbf{p}-\textbf{1}/N||^{2}_{2}\leq 2\frac{\xi}{N^{2}}\Big\}
. We have earlier stated that
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}
is output by a regressor, say

for some function class
{\mathcal{F}}
trained using loss
{\mathcal{L}}
with
N_{T}
datapoints, but this implies that
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}=f(b_{i})
might not exactly same as
\textbf{x}^{*}_{i}=f^{*}(b_{i})
for some underlying feature values
b_{i}
and best function

. We assume
f^{*}
is deterministic and has zero Bayes risk. Let
P^{*}
be the true distribution induced by
f^{*}
from which the (unknown) samples
\textbf{x}^{*}
’s are obtained; hence, the true utility of a decision

is
\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})]
. We prove an end to end guarantee about the output decision
\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**}
using
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}
’s, which reveals that
\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**}
is not much worse than the decision
\textbf{z}^{**}
learned if
\textbf{x}^{*}
would be available and used, and larger training data

helps.

Theorem 1
.

If the optimal decision when solving

is
\textbf{z}^{**}
using
\textbf{x}^{*}_{i}
’s and
\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**}
using
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}
’s,

is

,

is bounded, and a scaled

(i.e.,
||\textbf{x}-\textbf{x}^{\prime}||_{2}\leq\max(k{\mathcal{L}}(\textbf{x},\textbf{x}^{\prime}),\epsilon)
for constants

) then,
the following holds with probability

:
\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}[F(\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**},\textbf{x})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}[F(\textbf{z}^{**},\textbf{x})]-C/\sqrt{N}-(1+2\sqrt{\xi})\tau\epsilon-\epsilon_{N}-\epsilon_{N_{T}}
,
where
\epsilon_{K}=C_{1}{\mathcal{R}}_{K}({\mathcal{L}}\circ{\mathcal{H}})+C_{2}/\sqrt{K}
and

Variance Regularizer
:
As a large number of samples are needed for a low variance approximation of the true distribution, another proposed robust version of the SAA
(
Maurer and Pontil 2009
;
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
)
is to optimize the following variance-regularized (VR) objective function

\max_{\textbf{z}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}_{N}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})]-C\sqrt{\frac{\text{Var}_{\widehat{P}_{N}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}{N}}\right\}.

(
VR)

The above allows to directly
optimize the trade-off between bias and variance. In a fundamental result,
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
show that, with high probability, problem (
VR
) is
equivalent
to the problem (
DRO
).
Further,
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
argue for solving the DRO version of the problem for concave
F
(note we are solving a maximization SAA problem) since concave
F
results in concavity of
\min_{\textbf{p}\in\Delta_{\xi,n}}\sum_{i\in[N]}p_{i}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
, thus, the overall DRO problem is a concave maximization problem. In contrast, the objective in (
VR
) is not concave.

In this paper, our focus is on
F
that is
not concave
, thus, the choice of DRO or variance regularized form is not obvious. For the class of functions
F
that we analyze, we argue the variance regularized version is more promising as far as scalability for global optimality is concerned. We work with the assumption that the variance regularized form is equivalent to DRO, which holds under the mild condition shown in Equation (9) in
Duchi and Namkoong 2019
.

Towards a Globally Optimal Solution

In this section, we present results for a general class of non-concave functions

that has a fractional form with non-linear numerator and denominator and that can be approximated by a linear fractional form with binary variables.
Then, we show three
prominent

: For ease of notation, we use shorthand to denote

by
F_{i}
and

by

3.1
General Recipe to Form a MISOCP

We perform a sequence of variable and constraint transformations of (
VR
), leading to a MISOCP.

Mixed Integer Concave Program
:
The variance regularized objective in shorthand notation is:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})=\sum_{i}\frac{F_{i}}{N}-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}\Big(\frac{\sum_{i}F_{i}}{N}-F_{i}\Big)^{2}}

(1)

We substitute
l_{i}=\frac{\sum_{i}F_{i}}{N}-F_{i}
and
q=\frac{\sum_{i}F_{i}}{N}
for all
i\in[N]
, such that

\sum_{i}l_{i}=0
and
F_{i}=q-l_{i}
. The objective in Equation
1
thus becomes
q-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}l_{i}^{2}}
which is concave in the variables
q
and
\{l_{i}\}
. We add the new constraints
\sum_{i}l_{i}=0
and
F_{i}=q-l_{i}
for all
i\in[N]
. Note that, while the objective is now concave with above changes, we have pushed the non-convexity into the constraints
F_{i}-q+l_{i}=0
for all
i\in[N]
that are added to the optimization.

If
F_{i}
can be written (or approximated) as a fraction with affine numerator and denominator, we can convert the constraint
F_{i}-q+l_{i}=0
into a convex constraint, giving us an overall concave program. The conversion is explained next. Suppose
F_{i}
can be written (or approximated) as
\frac{\textbf{a}_{i}^{T}\textbf{v}+b_{i}}{\textbf{a}^{\prime T}_{i}\textbf{v}+b^{\prime}_{i}}
where
v
represents
binary
variables after conversion (
v
completely replaces
z
and
\textbf{a}_{i},\textbf{a}_{i}^{\prime},b_{i},b_{i}^{\prime}
are dependent on
\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}
’s).
Typically, such a linear fractional form is constructed via a piece wise linear approximation of the original non-linear numerator and denominator of
F
. Assume
v
is of dimension
d
; typically
d
will depend on the number of pieces. Define
\textbf{y}_{i}=\textbf{v}t_{i}
where
t_{i}=\frac{1}{\textbf{a}_{i}^{\prime T}\textbf{v}+b^{\prime}_{i}}
. Then, we can (re)write the fractional form for
F_{i}
as
F_{i}=\textbf{a}_{i}^{T}\textbf{y}_{i}+b_{i}t_{i}
. This yields the linear constraints below with the non-linearity now restricted to
\textbf{y}_{i}=\textbf{v}t_{i}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}l_{i}=0

(2)

\displaystyle\textbf{a}_{i}^{T}\textbf{y}_{i}+b_{i}t_{i}=q-l_{i}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(3)

\displaystyle\textbf{a}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}T}\textbf{y}_{i}+b_{i}^{\prime}t_{i}-1=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(4)

We handle
\textbf{y}_{i}=\textbf{v}t_{i}
using McCormick relaxation technique
(
McCormick 1976
)
. Typically, McCormick relaxation is applied for bilinear terms that are the product of two continuous variables, in which case, it is an approximation. However, in our case since
v
is a binary vector variable, the McCormick relaxation yields an exact reformulation of the bilinear term. For applying McCormick technique, we need an upper and lower bound of
v
and
t_{i}
. Since
v
a vector of binary variables, we have lower bound
\textbf{v}^{L}=\textbf{0}
and upper bound
\textbf{v}^{U}=\textbf{1}
.
Similarly,
t_{i}^{L}=\frac{1}{(\textbf{a}_{i}^{\prime+})^{T}\textbf{1}+b_{i}^{\prime}}
and
t_{i}^{U}=\frac{1}{(\textbf{a}_{i}^{\prime-})^{T}\textbf{1}+b_{i}^{\prime}}
(recall superscript
+
and
-
indicate positive and negative part of a vector respectively). Further, it is assumed
t_{i}^{U}
and
t_{i}^{L}
exist.
Note that these bounds are not variables but fixed constants that depend on the fixed parameters
\textbf{a}_{i},\textbf{a}_{i}^{\prime},b_{i},b_{i}^{\prime}
, hence these need to be computed just once.
Using the upper and lower bounds of
v
and
t_{i}
in McCormick technique we get:

\displaystyle\textbf{y}_{i}-\textbf{v}t_{i}^{U}\leq 0;

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(5)

\displaystyle\textbf{y}_{i}-(\textbf{1}t_{i}+\textbf{v}t_{i}^{L}-\textbf{1}t_{i}^{L})\leq 0;

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(6)

\displaystyle-\textbf{y}_{i}+(\textbf{1}t_{i}+\textbf{v}t_{i}^{U}-\textbf{1}t_{i}^{U})\leq 0;

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(7)

\displaystyle-\textbf{y}_{i}+\textbf{v}t_{i}^{L}\leq 0;

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(8)

\displaystyle\textbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{d}

(9)

\displaystyle t_{i}^{U}\leq t_{i}\leq t_{i}^{L};

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

(10)

It is straightforward to check the above set of equations is equivalent to
\textbf{y}_{i}=\textbf{v}t_{i}
. With the changes, we obtain a mixed integer concave program (with all constraints linear). Next, while the above can be solved using branch and bound with general purpose convex solvers for intermediate problem, we show that a further transformation to a MISOCP is possible. Specialized SOCP’s solvers provide much more scalability than a general purpose convex solvers
(
Bonami and Tramontani 2015
)
and hence partially address the scalability challenge.

Mixed Integer SOCP
:
We transform further by introducing another variable
s
to stand for
\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}l_{i}^{2}}
. We use
\textbf{r}=(s,q,(l_{i})_{i\in[N]},\textbf{v},(t_{i})_{i\in[N]},\textbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{y}_{N})
to denote all the variables of the optimization.
Thus, the objective becomes the linear function
q-s
with an additional constraint that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}l_{i}^{2}}\leq s

(11)

The above is same as
||A\textbf{r}||_{2}\leq\textbf{c}^{T}\textbf{r}
for the constant matrix
A
(with entries
0
or
\sqrt{\rho}
) and constant vector
c
(with 1 in the

component, rest 0’s) that picks the
l_{i}
’s and
s
respectively.
This is a SOCP form of constraint, and the linear objective
q-s
makes the problem after this transformation a MISOCP.
In the above reformulation, the only approximation is introduced in writing
F_{i}
as a linear fractional term.
One way of such approximation is via piecewise linear approximation (PWLA). Suppose the fraction function
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
has a separable (in

) numerator and denominator of the form
\frac{\sum_{j}n(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}{\sum_{j}d(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}
where

ranges over the components of

, and

and

are non-negative and Lipschitz continuous.
In this case, a general PWLA is possible with the following guarantee:

Theorem 2
.

For
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})=\frac{\sum_{j}n(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}{\sum_{j}d(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}
as stated above and approximated as
\frac{\textbf{a}_{i}^{T}\textbf{v}+b_{i}}{\textbf{a}^{\prime T}_{i}\textbf{v}+b^{\prime}_{i}}
, a PWLA approximation with
K
pieces yields

|{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z}^{*})-{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**})|\leq O(1/K)
, where
{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z}^{*})
and
{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}}^{**})
are the optimal objective values with approximation (MISOCP) and without the approximation respectively.

Bayesian Stackelberg Security Game with Quantal Response
:
A SSG models a Stackelberg game where a defender moves first to allocate
m
security resources for protecting
M
targets. The randomized allocation is specified by decision variables
z
of dimension
M
with the constraints that
\sum_{i=1}^{M}z_{i}\leq m
(
z_{i}\in[0,1]
);
z_{i}
is interpreted as the protection probability of the target
i
. Past works have used the model of a quantal responding adversary
(
Sinha et al. 2018
)
].
We generalize this to a Bayesian game version where there is a continuum of attackers types with the type specified by a parameter
x
and an unknown prior distribution over these types. The attacker’s utility in attacking the target
j
is a function of the protection probability of target
j
and type:
h(z_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}})
. Similarly, the defender’s utility when target
j
is attacked is:
u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})
for some player-specific parameters
\theta
that depend on
x
. Following quantal response model (for attacker only), the attacker of type
x
attacks a target
j
with probability
\frac{\exp(h(z_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}{\sum_{j\in[M]}\exp(h(x_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}
and the defender utility is
F(\textbf{z},{\textbf{x}})=\frac{\sum_{j\in[M]}u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})\exp(h(z_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}{\sum_{j\in[M]}\exp(h(x_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}
.
Note that in case the defender’s utilities

take negative values and the assumptions of Theorem
2
will be violated. This issue can be simply fixed by choosing

such that
\alpha\geq\max_{\textbf{z},\textbf{x}}\{-u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})\}
and replacing

by
F(\textbf{z},{\textbf{x}})+\alpha=\frac{\sum_{j\in[M]}(u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})+\alpha)\exp(h(z_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}{\sum_{j\in[M]}\exp(h(x_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}}))}
.
This will make all the numerators and enumerators of the objective function non-negative, while keeping the same optimization problem. We also note that quantal response is also known as multinomial logit model in the discrete choice model literature
(
Train 2003
)
. Our generalization here to multiple types of adversary makes the problem akin to the mixed logit model in discrete choice models, which is generally considered intractable. As a consequence, our solution addresses a basic problem in discrete choice models also.

Following our set-up, we observe

\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{N}
(which gives
\widehat{\theta}^{a}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\theta}^{a}_{N},\widehat{\theta}^{d}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\theta}^{d}_{N}
) and we solve a robust version of the problem. Further,
following our general recipe for solving the robust problem, we piecewise approximate the numerator and denominator of

using

is

. For this approximation, we require two additional linear constraints over the constraints in Equations (

-

\displaystyle\max_{\textbf{r}}\;q-s

(

\displaystyle\mbox{subject to Constraints}~(\ref{eq:sumli}\mbox{-}\ref{eq:SOCP}),\sum_{j\in[M]}\sum_{k\in[K]}v_{jk}-mK\leq 0,v_{j,k}\geq v_{j,k+1};\quad\forall k\in[K]

The overall additive solution bound of

can be readily inferred from Theorem

x

\frac{\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}e^{V_{\textbf{x},j}}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}e^{V_{\textbf{x},j}}+\sum_{j\in Y}e^{V_{\textbf{x},j}}}
.
For shorthand, we abuse notation and use
V_{\textbf{x},j}
to replace
e^{V_{\textbf{x},j}}
and
U_{\textbf{x},Y}
to replace
\sum_{j\in Y}e^{V_{\textbf{x},j}}
. This gives
F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})=\frac{s_{\textbf{x}}\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}V_{\textbf{x},j}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}V_{\textbf{x},j}+U_{\textbf{x},Y}}
, which is interpreted as the expected number of clients of type
x
choosing this firm’s facilities.

Following our set-up, we observe
N
samples of the types of clients samples
(\widehat{V}_{1,j})_{j\in[M]},\ldots,(\widehat{V}_{N,j})_{j\in[M]}
and we solve a robust version of the problem. Here, we get
F_{i}=\frac{s_{i}\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}\widehat{V}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}z_{j}\widehat{V}_{i,j}+\widehat{U}_{i,Y}}
.
Next,
following our general recipe for solving the robust problem, we note that
F_{i}
is already in the form
\frac{\textbf{a}_{i}^{T}\textbf{v}+b_{i}}{\textbf{a}^{\prime T}_{i}\textbf{v}+b^{\prime}_{i}}
where
z
plays the role of
v
. Thus, the dimension of
v
is
M
and no approximation is needed here for
F_{i}
. Thus, by Theorem
2
, we achieve the global optimal solution by solving the MISOCP optimally. The full MISOCP with an additional number of location constraint is (note
v
directly replaces
z
):

\displaystyle\max_{\textbf{r}}

\displaystyle\;q-s\;\mbox{ subject to }\mbox{Constraints}~(\ref{eq:sumli}\mbox{-}\ref{eq:SOCP}),\sum_{j\in[M]}v_{j}-m\leq 0.

Max-Capture Facility Cost Optimization (MC-FCP)
:
In the previous
MC-FLP
problem, the budget was specified as a constraint on the number of facilities. However, often a more realistic set-up is where there is a monetary constraint and the attractiveness of a facility depends on the investment into the facility.
Thus, modifying the previous problem slightly,
z_{j}
takes a different meaning of the amount of investment into facility at location
j
(zero investment indicates no facility). Given this investment, the attractiveness of a facility
j
for the client of type
x
is given as
h(z_{j},\theta_{\textbf{x},j})
for some parameter
\theta
dependent on
x
and
j
. And the
choice probability
of a client of type
x
choosing any of this firm’s location is given as a quantal response model

\frac{\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\theta_{\textbf{x},j})}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\theta_{\textbf{x},j})}+U_{\textbf{x},Y}}
. This gives
F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})=\frac{s_{\textbf{x}}\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\theta_{\textbf{x},j})}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\theta_{\textbf{x},j})}+U_{\textbf{x},Y}}
, which is interpreted similar to
MC-FLP
. As stated, we observe
N
samples of the types of clients which gives
(\widehat{\theta}_{1,j})_{j\in[M]},\ldots,(\widehat{\theta}_{N,j})_{j\in[M]}
and we solve a robust version of the problem. Here, we get
F_{i}=\frac{s_{i}\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\widehat{\theta}_{i,j})}}{\sum_{j\in[M]}e^{h(z_{j},\widehat{\theta}_{i,j})}+\widehat{U}_{i,Y}}
.
Next,
as can be seen from the form, this is similar to the
SSG
problem and, following our general recipe, with two additional linear constraints the optimization formulation is exactly same as Equation (
SSG
).

4
Scaling up in Number of Samples

The transformation to a MISOCP helps in scalability over a general mixed integer concave program, but for real world dataset sizes (e.g.,
80,000
data points in our experiments) we need further scalability. We explore two related techniques towards this end: clustering and stratified sampling. For both approaches, we obtain a representative subset of
S
data points (
S<\!<N
) and a modified weighted objective, which converts to a much smaller tractable MISOCP compared to the original problem. For solution guarantees, we need mild assumptions: in particular, for the rest of this section we assume a bounded
F
, i.e., for some fixed
\psi

\max_{\textbf{z}}\{F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})\}-\min_{\textbf{z}}\{F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})\}\leq\psi^{2}\quad\forall\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{N}

and
\tau
-lipschitzness of
F
in the argument
x
:

|F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{\prime})-F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})|\leq\tau||\textbf{x}^{\prime}-\textbf{x}||_{2}\quad\forall\textbf{z}
.

Clustering Approach
:
We cluster the
N
points
\textbf{x}_{1},...\textbf{x}_{N}
into
S
groups and for each group
s
we have
||\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{x}^{s}||\leq\epsilon
, where
\textbf{x}^{s}
is the cluster center of cluster
s
. We call
\epsilon
the clustering radius. Let
C_{s}
be the number of points in the cluster
s
, hence
\sum_{s\in[S]}C_{s}=N
.
We use a shorthand for the original objective function of the MISOCP
{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}{N}-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{i}\frac{F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}{N}-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\right)^{2}}=\widehat{\text{Mean}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}

where
\widehat{\text{Mean}}
is empirical mean and
\widehat{\text{Var}}
is
unnormalized variance
.
After clustering, we solve for the same problem but only with cluster centers and appropriate
weighing
, to get modified objective
\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{s}C_{s}\frac{F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{s})}{N}-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\sum_{s}C_{s}\frac{F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{s})}{N}-F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{s})\right)^{2}}=\widehat{\text{Mean}}^{S}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}^{S}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}

(12)

The conversion to MISOCP is exactly the same, except for
F_{i}
’s being weighted as shown above; details of conversion are in the appendix.
We bound the approximation incurred by the two terms above (weighted mean and unnormalized weighted variance) separately below

Lemma 1
.

Under assumptions stated above, we have

\left|\widehat{\text{Mean}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-\widehat{\text{Mean}}^{S}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))\right|\leq\tau\epsilon
and
\left|\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}^{S}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}\right|\leq(\psi+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{N}}
.

The next result is obtained by using the lemma above

Theorem 3
.

Given the assumptions stated above,
and
\widehat{\textbf{z}}
an optimal solution for
\max_{\textbf{z}}\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z})
and
\textbf{z}^{*}
optimal for MISOCP
\max_{\textbf{z}}{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})
, the following holds:

|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|\leq 2(\tau\epsilon+\psi\sqrt{\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{N}}+\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{\sqrt{N}})
.

Stratified Sampling
:
Similar in spirit to clustering, the space of
x
space is divided into
T
strata. Each strata has
C_{t}
samples, such that
\sum_{t\in[T]}C_{t}=N
. Next, distinct from the clustering approach,
we draw
N_{t}
samples randomly from the
t^{th}
stratum with a total of
\sum_{t}N_{t}=S
samples (note same number of total samples
S
as in clustering). For each stratum
t
we have
||\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{x}_{j}||\leq d_{t}
for any
\textbf{x}_{i},\textbf{x}_{j}
in stratum
t
. We denote a random sample in stratum
t
as
\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j}
where
j\in[N_{t}]
(note superscript is to distinguish from the subscript used to index all the
\widehat{\textbf{x}}
’s). This approach is the preferred one if the clustering approach results in cluster centers that are not allowed as parameter values (e.g., cluster center may be fractional where
x
’s can only be integral).

Let
l_{t}=\frac{C_{t}}{N_{t}}
. Use
\widehat{Mean}^{T}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))
to stand for
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t\in[T]}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})
and
\widehat{Var}^{T}(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}})
for
\sum_{t\in[T]}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}\left(\widehat{Mean}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})\right)
.
After stratified sampling our modified weighted objective
\widehat{G}(\textbf{z})
is

\widehat{Mean}^{T}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{Var}^{T}(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}})}
. Next, similar to clustering, bounds for
\widehat{Mean}^{T}
and
\widehat{Var}^{T}
but with high probability (Lemma
2
,
3
in appendix) lead to the main result:

Theorem 4
.

Let
D=\max_{\textbf{z},\textbf{x}}|F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})|
for bounded function
F
. Given the assumptions stated above,
and
\widehat{\textbf{z}}
an optimal solution for
\max_{\textbf{z}}\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z})
and
\textbf{z}^{*}
optimal for MISOCP
\max_{\textbf{z}}{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})
, and
N_{*}=\min_{t}{N_{t}}
, the following statement holds with probability
\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2\sqrt{N_{*}}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}-4\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2\sqrt{N_{*}}\epsilon^{2}}{4\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}D^{2}}}
:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{(N_{*})^{1/4}}\Bigg(1+2\sqrt{\frac{\xi}{\widehat{Var}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}}\Bigg).

Thus, with increasing samples in all strata, optimality gap approaches
0
with prob. approaching
1
.

6
Conclusion

We presented an approach for a distributionally robust solution to a class of non-convex sum of fractional solutions, with guaranteed near global optimality. We presented application to three prominent practical problems and the connection to discrete choice models likely opens up possibilities of applying our approach to even more problems. Our scalability is limited to hundred thousand data points, further investigation on how to cluster or stratify more effectively (than k-means) to achieve even more scalability is a possible future research direction. We hope that our work inspires tackling robust formulation of more classes of non-convex problems, with guarantees for global optimality.

Appendix A
Piece-wise Linear Approximation (PWLA)

Recall the general functional form

F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})=\frac{\sum_{j}n(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}{\sum_{j}d(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})}

and note that both the numerator and denominator are separable in the components of the decision variables
z
.
Let assume each variable
z_{j}
can vary in the interval
[L_{j},U_{j}]
, the idea here is to divide each interval
[L_{j},U_{j}]
into
K
equal sub-intervals of size
(U_{j}-L_{j})/K
and approximate
z_{j}
by
K
binary variables
v_{jk}\in\{0,1\}
as

z_{j}=L_{j}\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K}\sum_{k\in[K]}v_{jk},

where
v_{jk}\in\{0,1\}
satisfying
v_{ik}\geq v_{j,{k+1}}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,K-1
. We then approximate
n(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
and
d(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
as

\displaystyle n(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\approx\widehat{n}(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})=n\left(L_{j}+\left\lfloor z_{j}K/(U_{j}-L_{j})\right\rfloor\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)=n(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})+\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K}\sum_{k\in[K]}\gamma^{ni}_{jk}{v_{jk}},

\displaystyle d(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\approx\widehat{d}(z_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})=d\left(L_{j}+\left\lfloor z_{j}K/(U_{j}-L_{j})\right\rfloor\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)=d(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})+\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K}\sum_{k\in[K]}\gamma^{di}_{jk}{v_{jk}}

where
\gamma^{ni}_{jk}
and
\gamma^{di}_{jk}
are the slopes of the approximate linear functions in
[L_{j}+(U_{j}-L_{j})(k-1)/K;L_{j}+(U_{j}-L_{j})(k)/K]
,
\forall k=1,\ldots,K
, computed as

\displaystyle\gamma^{ni}_{j,k+1}=\frac{K}{U_{j}-L_{j}}\left(n\left(L_{j}+\frac{(U_{j}-L_{j})(k+1)}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)-n\left(L_{j}+\frac{(U_{j}-L_{j})(k)}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)\right),\;k=0,\ldots,K-1

\displaystyle\gamma^{di}_{j,k+1}=\frac{K}{U_{j}-L_{j}}\left(d\left(L_{j}+\frac{(U_{j}-L_{j})(k+1)}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)-d\left(L_{j}+\frac{(U_{j}-L_{j})(k)}{K},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i}\right)\right),\;k=0,\ldots,K-1.

We can then approximate
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
as

F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\approx\frac{\sum_{j}(n(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})+\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K}\sum_{k\in[K]}\gamma^{ni}_{jk}{v_{jk}})}{\sum_{j}(d(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})+\frac{U_{j}-L_{j}}{K}\sum_{k\in[K]}\gamma^{di}_{jk}{v_{jk}})}.

The transformed/approximated problem will have the following parameters and variables

•

\textbf{a}_{i}=\bigg[\gamma^{ni}_{jk}\Big|\;j\in[M],k\in[K]\bigg]

•

\textbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}=\bigg[\gamma^{di}_{jk}\Big|\;j\in[M],k\in[K]\bigg]

•

b_{i}=\sum_{j\in[M]}n(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})

•

b^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j\in[M]}d(L_{j},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})

•

\textbf{v}\in{\mathcal{V}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{def}}}}{{=}}{}\bigg\{v_{jk}\Big|\;v_{jk}\in\{0,1\},v_{jk}\geq v_{j,k+1},j\in[M],k\in[K]\bigg\}
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}C_{s}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})\right)^{2}}<\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\right)^{2}}

can be handled similarly by rewriting
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})
as
\mu+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}
and
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})
as
\mu+\alpha_{i}
.
We write

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}C_{s}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}(\widehat{\mu}-\mu-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}(\alpha_{i}+(\beta_{i}+\mu-\widehat{\mu}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i}+\mu-\widehat{\mu})+\sum_{i\in[N]}(\beta_{i}+\mu-\widehat{\mu})^{2}}

Using
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=0
and
|\mu-\widehat{\mu}|\leq\tau\epsilon,|\beta_{i}|\leq\tau\epsilon
we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}C_{s}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}+\sum_{i\in[N]}(\beta_{i}+\mu-\widehat{\mu})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i\in[N]}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}+N(2\tau\epsilon)^{2}}.

Using
F_{U},F_{L}
are upper and lower limits of
F
and let
\psi=\sqrt{(}F_{U}-F_{L})
, we further expand the inequalities

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}C_{s}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2N(F_{U}-F_{L})\tau\epsilon+N(2\tau\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\sqrt{2N(F_{U}-F_{L})\tau\epsilon+N(2\tau\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}}+(\psi+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon})\sqrt{2N\tau\epsilon}

where (a) is due to the fact that
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for any non-negative numbers
a,b
.
Thus, after plugging in
\rho=\frac{\xi}{N^{2}}
we get

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sqrt{\rho\sum_{i\in[N]}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})\Big)^{2}}-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{s}C_{s}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}C_{s}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{s})\right)^{2}}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq(\psi+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon})\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{2N\tau\epsilon}

\displaystyle=(\psi+\sqrt{2\tau\epsilon})\sqrt{\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{N}},

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3

Theorem
.

Given the assumptions stated above,
and
\widehat{\textbf{z}}
an optimal solution for
\max_{\textbf{z}}\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z})
and
\textbf{z}^{*}
optimal for
\max_{\textbf{z}}{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})
, the following holds:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|\leq 2(\tau\epsilon+\psi\sqrt{\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{N}}+\frac{2\tau\epsilon\xi}{\sqrt{N}}).

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\textbf{z}}
be an optimal solution to
\max_{\textbf{z}}\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z})
and
\textbf{z}^{*}
be optimal to
\max_{\textbf{z}}{\mathcal{G}}(\textbf{z})
, we have

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})|+|\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|

The later can be further evaluated by considering two cases,
\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})\geq{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})

\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})<{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})

. The other case can be done similarly to have

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-{{\mathcal{G}}}(\textbf{z}^{*})|\leq 2|{\mathcal{G}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})-\widehat{{\mathcal{G}}}(\widehat{\textbf{z}})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2|\widehat{\text{Mean}}^{S}(F(\widehat{\textbf{z}},\textbf{x}))-\widehat{\text{Mean}}(F(\widehat{\textbf{z}},\textbf{x}))\Big|

\displaystyle+2\Big|\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}(F(\widehat{\textbf{z}},\textbf{x}))}-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}^{S}(F(\widehat{\textbf{z}},\textbf{x}))}\Big|

Then using the two results in Lemma
1
, we get the required result.

Appendix F
Proof of Lemma
2

Lemma 2
.

\forall\textbf{z}
with probability
\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}
,

\big|\widehat{\text{Mean}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))-\widehat{\text{Mean}}^{T}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))\big|\leq\epsilon
.
In other words,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[M]}lF(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{j})]\right|\leq\epsilon\right)\geq\prod_{t}\big(1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\big)

Proof.

We utilize the concentration of Lipchitz functions. In particular, we have
\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{1},\ldots,\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{N_{t}}
which are sampled uniformly and independently from strata
t
and bounded (has diameter
d_{t}
). Let
U_{t}
denote the uniform probability distribution over the
C_{t}
points in strata
t
. Let
I_{t}
denote a set of indexes that lie in the strata
t
. Then, for our function
F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})
with Lipchitz constant
\tau
we have :

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim U_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})]\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\quad\forall t,\textbf{z}.

Observe that by definition

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim U_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})]=\frac{1}{C_{t}}\sum_{j\in I_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})].

Hence,

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\frac{1}{C_{t}}\sum_{j\in I_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})]\Big|\geq\epsilon\Bigg)\leq 2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow P\Bigg(\Big|\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\sum_{j\in I_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}_{j})]\Big|\geq C_{t}\epsilon\Bigg)\leq 2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}.

Call the event in the probability above as
E_{t}
. It is obvious that
E_{t}
is independent over all different strata
t
’s due to the independent sampling of points across strata. Hence
\lnot E_{t}
are also independent. Next, using product of independent events over all strata we get

\displaystyle P\Big(\cap_{t}\lnot E_{t}\Big)\geq\prod_{t}\Big(1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\Big).

Note that
\cap_{t}\lnot E_{t}
implies

\sum_{t}\Big|\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\sum_{j\in I_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}_{j})]\Big|\leq\sum_{t}C_{t}\epsilon.

Noting that
|a+b|\leq|a|+|b|
and the fact that
\{I_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
is a partition of
[N]
, the above implies that

\Big|\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\sum_{j\in[N]}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{j})]\Big|\leq\sum_{t}C_{t}\epsilon

This gives

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\sum_{j\in[N]}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{j})]\Big|\leq\sum_{t}C_{t}\epsilon\Bigg)\geq\prod_{t}\Big(1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\Big)

\displaystyle(\text{Then, since $N=\sum_{t}C_{t}$})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}_{j})]\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq\prod_{t}\Big(1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\Big)

\displaystyle(\text{Then, since $(1-a)(1-b)\geq 1-a-b)$})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}_{j})]\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}},

which is the desired inequality.
∎

Appendix G
Proof of Lemma
3

Lemma 3
.

Define
D=\max_{\textbf{z},\textbf{x}}|F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})|
for bounded function
F
. Then,
\forall\textbf{z}
with probability
\geq 1-4\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{4\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}D^{2}}}
,

\left|\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}-\sqrt{\rho\widehat{\text{Var}}^{T}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}\right|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\xi}\epsilon}{\sqrt{\widehat{Var}(F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}))}}
.

Proof.

Fix
z
. Recall
I_{t}
be the set of index that belong to strata
t
, thus,
\{I_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
is a partition of
[N]
and
C_{t}=|I_{t}|
. For sake of simplicity, we use the shorthand for the sample/random variable
Y^{j}=F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})
. Note that the samples are independent.
We use the following notations :

\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})

\widehat{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}Y^{j}

Note that
\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}=N
.

The unnormalized weighted variance is

\displaystyle\widehat{Var}^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}\left(\widehat{\mu}-Y^{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}\left(\widehat{\mu}^{2}-2\widehat{\mu}Y^{j}+(Y^{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=N\widehat{\mu}^{2}-2\widehat{\mu}\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}Y^{j}+\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}(Y^{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=N\widehat{\mu}^{2}-2N\widehat{\mu}^{2}+\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}(Y^{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}(Y^{j})^{2}-N\widehat{\mu}^{2}

We wish to compare this to

\widehat{Var}=\sum_{j\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})^{2}-N\mu^{2}

Towards this end, we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{Var}^{T}-\widehat{Var}|\leq|\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}(Y^{j})^{2}-\sum_{j\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})^{2}|+N|\widehat{\mu}^{2}-\mu^{2}|

(29)

We know from Lipschitzness assumption that

\displaystyle|F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})|\leq\tau d_{t},\;\forall\textbf{z},i,j\in I_{s}

(30)

Multiplying both sides by
|F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})+F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})|
(which is
\leq 2D
), we get

\displaystyle|F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{i})^{2}-F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})^{2}|\leq 2\tau d_{t}D,\;\forall\textbf{z},i,j\in I_{s}

(31)

Let
U_{t}
denote the uniform probability distribution over the
C_{t}
points in strata
t
. Observe that by definition

\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim U_{t}}[(Y^{j})^{2}]=\frac{1}{C_{t}}\sum_{j\in I_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{j})^{2}]

Then, by Hoeffding inequality and Equation
31

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}(Y^{j})^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{\textbf{x}\sim U_{t}}[F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x})^{2}]\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{4\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}D^{2}}}\quad\forall t,\textbf{z}

Then, using the same sequence of steps as for Lemma
2
, we get

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}(Y^{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}_{j})^{2}\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{4\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}D^{2}}}\quad\forall\textbf{z}

Also, we know from Lemma
2

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\widehat{\mu}-\mu\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\quad\forall\textbf{z}

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\widehat{\mu}^{2}-\mu^{2}\Big|\leq 2\epsilon D\Bigg)\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}}}\quad\forall\textbf{z}

Replacing

\epsilon

\displaystyle P\Bigg(\Big|\widehat{\mu}^{2}-\mu^{2}\Big|\leq\epsilon\Bigg)\geq 1-2\sum_{t}\exp^{\frac{-2N_{t}\epsilon^{2}}{4\tau^{2}d_{t}^{2}D^{2}}}\quad\forall\textbf{z}

A

Appendix I
Data Generation Details

(Synthetic) SSG:
Following standard terminology and set-up in SSG, for every target
j
, under a type specified by parameters
x
,
if the adversary attacks
j
and the target is protected then the defender obtains reward
r_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}
and the adversary obtains
l_{\textbf{x},j}^{a}
. Conversely, if the
defender is not protecting target
j
, then the defender obtains
l_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}
(
r_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}>l_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}
) and the adversary gets
r_{\textbf{x},j}^{a}
(
r_{\textbf{x},j}^{a}>l_{\textbf{x},j}^{a}
). Given

z_{j}
as the marginal probability of defending target
j
, the expected utility of the defender and attacker of type
x
for an attack on target
j
is formulated as follows:
u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})=z_{j}r_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}+(1-z_{j})l_{\textbf{x},j}^{d}
and

h(z_{j},\theta^{a}_{\textbf{x}})=\lambda_{\textbf{x}}(z_{j}l_{\textbf{x},j}^{a}+(1-z_{j})r_{\textbf{x},j}^{a})
, where parameter

\lambda_{\textbf{x}}\geq 0
governs rationality.

\lambda_{\textbf{x}}\rightarrow 0
means least rational, as the adversary chooses its attack uniformly at
random and
\lambda_{\textbf{x}}\rightarrow\infty
means fully rational (i.e., attacks a target with highest utility). We compactly rewrite
u(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})=z_{j}a^{d}_{\textbf{x},j}+l^{d}_{\textbf{x},j}
and
h(z_{j},\theta^{d}_{\textbf{x}})=-z_{j}c^{a}_{\textbf{x},j}+l^{a}_{\textbf{x},j}
. We add two layers of randomness to our
parameters

\{a^{d}_{\textbf{x},j},l^{d}_{\textbf{x},j},c^{a}_{\textbf{x},j},l^{a}_{\textbf{x},j}|\forall j\in[M],\forall\textbf{x}\}
by (1) generating i.i.d. samples from a mean shifted beta-distribution :
\text{low}+(\text{high}-\text{low})\textbf{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
, and (2) then using these samples as means for the Gaussian distribution :
{\mathcal{N}}(.,\sigma^{2})
to i.i.d. generate the final
parameters
. In our experiments we chose : low = 5, high=8,
\alpha=3
,
\beta=3
,
\sigma^{2}=3
.

(Synthetic) Regressor for SSG utilities:
To validate Theorem
1
, we first fix a linear regressor
f^{*}=\langle s^{*}_{a^{d}_{j}},b^{*}_{a^{d}_{j}},s^{*}_{l^{d}_{j}},b^{*}_{l^{d}_{j}},s^{*}_{c^{a}_{j}},b^{*}_{c^{a}_{j}},s^{*}_{l^{a}_{j}},b^{*}_{l^{a}_{j}}|\forall j\in[M]\rangle
and sample
\{V^{*,a^{d}_{\textbf{x}}},V^{*,l^{d}_{\textbf{x}}},V^{*,c^{a}_{\textbf{x}}},V^{*,l^{a}_{\textbf{x}}}|\forall\textbf{x}\in[N_{T}]\}
to generate
\{a^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}},l^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}},c^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}},l^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}}|\forall j\in[M],\forall\textbf{x}\in[N_{T}]\}
such that
a^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}}=s^{*}_{a^{d}_{j}}*V^{*,a^{d}_{\textbf{x}}}+b^{*}_{a^{d}_{j}}
,
l^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}}=s^{*}_{l^{d}_{j}}*V^{*,l^{d}_{\textbf{x}}}+b^{*}_{l^{d}_{j}}
,
c^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}}=s^{*}_{c^{a}_{j}}*V^{*,c^{a}_{\textbf{x}}}+b^{*}_{c^{a}_{j}}
,
l^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}}=s^{*}_{l^{a}_{j}}*V^{*,l^{a}_{\textbf{x}}}+b^{*}_{l^{a}_{j}}
. Now a linear regressor
\widehat{f}
is learnt on the given dataset of
N_{T}
samples by minimizing the L-2 loss between outputs of
\widehat{f}
:
\{\widehat{a}^{d_{\textbf{x},j}},\widehat{l}^{d_{\textbf{x},j}},\widehat{c}^{a_{\textbf{x},j}},\widehat{l}^{a_{\textbf{x},j}}|\forall j\in[M],\forall\textbf{x}\in[N_{T}]\}
and actual utilities :
\{a^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}},l^{*,d_{\textbf{x},j}},c^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}},l^{*,a_{\textbf{x},j}}|\forall j\in[M],\forall\textbf{x}\in[N_{T}]\}
. DRO is performed on both true and learnt utilities to get decisions and then evaluated on held out test set of true utilities.

Aros-Vera et al. 2013

:
V_{\textbf{x},j}\forall j\in[M]
from the P&R
[
Aros-Vera et al. 2013
]
dataset, so

is based completely on real data.

Appendix L
Converting Weighted Objective to MISOCP

Let

\widehat{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}F(\textbf{z},\textbf{x}^{j}).

Further, let
Y^{j}=F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})
.
Consider the stratified sampling objective

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}\left(\widehat{\mu}-Y^{j}\right)^{2}}

(35)

It is enough to show the conversion for the above as the clustering is a special case with
N_{t}=1
for all
t
.
As before we substitute
l_{t,j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}Y^{j}-Y^{j}
(notation
l
is abused, but the constant
l_{t}
subscript is
t
and the variable subscript is
t,j
) for all
i\in[N]
and
q=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t}Y^{j}
.
Note that
\sum_{j\in N_{t}}l_{t,j}=\frac{N_{t}}{N}\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}-\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}
, and since
l_{t}=\frac{C_{t}}{N_{t}}
, we have

\sum_{j\in N_{t}}l_{t,j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t}C_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}-\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}
. Also, since
\sum_{t}C_{t}=N
then

\sum_{t}C_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t,j}=\frac{\sum_{t}C_{t}}{N}\sum_{t}C_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}-\sum_{t}C_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}Y^{j}=0

Also,
Y^{j}=F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})=q-l_{t,j}
. The objective becomes
q-\sqrt{\rho\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t,j}^{2}}
. Thus, like the original (non-clustered) problem the objective is concave, and the only non-convexity is in the constraint
F(\textbf{z},\widehat{\textbf{x}}^{j})=q-l_{t,j}
, which can be approximated as earlier.

For MISOCP, we move the part of the objective becomes the linear function
q-s
with an additional constraint that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\rho\sum_{t}l_{t}\sum_{j\in[N_{t}]}l_{t,j}^{2}}\leq s

(36)

(Recall
r
is the vector of all variables).
The above is same as
||A\textbf{r}||_{2}\leq\textbf{c}^{T}\textbf{r}
for the constant matrix
A
(with entries
0
or
\sqrt{\rho l_{t}}
at appropriate entries) and constant vector
c
(with 1 in the
s
component, rest 0’s) that picks the
l_{i}
’s and
s
respectively.
