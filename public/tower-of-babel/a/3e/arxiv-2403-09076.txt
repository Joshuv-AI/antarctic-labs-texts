Abstract
In remote estimation of cyber-physical systems (CPSs), sensor measurements transmitted through network may be attacked by adversaries, leading to leakage risk of privacy (e.g., the system state), and/or failure of the remote estimator. To deal with this problem, a chaotic masking protocol is proposed in this paper to secure the sensor measurements transmission. In detail, at the plant side, a chaotic dynamic system is deployed to encode the sensor measurement, and at the estimator side, an estimator estimates both states of the physical plant and the chaotic system. With this protocol, no additional secure communication links is needed for synchronization, and the masking effect can be perfectly removed when the estimator is in steady state. Furthermore, this masking protocol can deal with multiple types of attacks, i.e., eavesdropping attack, replay attack, and stealthy false data injection attack.



keywords

Cyber-physical Systems, secure estimation, chaotic masking, attack detection.

1
Introduction

Integrating technologies including control, communication, and computation, cyber-physical systems (CPSs) exhibit a substantial promise across diverse industry sectors, encompassing smart cities, smart grids, smart manufacturing, and intelligent transportation
(
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Humayed et al. 2017
)
. Nevertheless, the complex intricate nature of these systems, notably the network-based data transmission, exposes them to vulnerabilities and potential security breaches, leading to great damages
(
Farwell and Rohozinski 2011
;
Slay and Miller 2008
)
.

A remote estimator uses data collected remotely to estimate the state of a physical system, enabling remote monitor and control.
There exist multiple attack strategies for the remote estimation problem. Some remarkable examples are eavesdropping attack
(
Shang and Chen 2022
)
, replay attack
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, and false data injection (FDI) attack
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
.

An eavesdropping attack aims to gain unauthorized access to the state information of a physical plant by intercepting sensor measurements transmitted through the network. So the privacy of the transmitted information should be protected to deal with this kind of attack.
Replay attack involves recording sensor data for a certain amount of time, and replaying in a loop the recorded data when needed. Since the recorded data is taken from the true plant, it is not easy to be detected by a monitor or detector. An FDI attack aims to destroy the estimation by injecting specific attack sequence on the communicated sensor reading. In general, the attack sequence is designed such that they can bypass the detector, i.e., the attack is stealthy. Hence, for replay attack and FDI attacks, the method to detect them plays a fundamental role.

One commonly used detector is the innovation-based detector, which relies on the discrepancy between predicted and actual received sensor outputs to detect attacks
(
Manandhar et al. 2014
)
. In
(
Pasqualetti et al. 2013
)
, the concept of detectable attack is built, and then necessary and sufficient conditions are presented for the innovation-based detector to detect detectable attacks. However, the authors in
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
show that a innovation-based detector may fail to detect replay attacks in the sense that its detection rate converges to the false alarm rate when replay attack happens. In addition, if an attacker is aware of the detector and designs attacks carefully, the detector may also fail to detect an FDI attack. For example,
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
proposed a linear deception attack strategy, which ensures that the innovation term with attack preserves the same Gaussian distribution as the healthy one and can successfully bypass the innovation-based detector.
(
Hu et al. 2018
)
presented a FDI attack that ensures a bounded innovation term but an unbounded estimation error.

To protect the transmitted data and assist stealthy attack detection of innovation-based detector, watermark-based and encode-decode-based methods are usually proposed.
In general, watermarks are random noises added to the control input to increase the detection probability of a FDI attack
(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Naha et al. 2023
;
Satchidanandan and Kumar 2016
)
. In
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, both independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) Gaussian and non-i.i.d. Gaussian noises are used as watermark to detect replay attacks. In
(
Satchidanandan and Kumar 2016
)
, a watermark strategy is used for more general attack types, where an attack can be any malicious behaviors of sensors.
Nonetheless, since the watermark signal would increase control costs, a lot of efforts have been paid to decrease the effect of watermark
(
Naha et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023b
)
.

Alternatively, encode-decode methods use random numbers to encode the information to be transmitted and decode it at the receiver’s side, e.g.,
(
Shang and Chen 2022
;
Guo et al. 2022
;
Joo et al. 2020
;
Miao et al. 2016
)
. In
(
Guo et al. 2022
)
, to detect stealthy FDI
attacks, a stochastic coding scheme is proposed. The encode is based on adding i.i.d. Gaussian noise to sensor measurements, and decode is by subtracting the same noise. In
(
Miao et al. 2016
)
, to detect stealthy FDI attacks, the transmitted information is multiplied by a coding matrix, which depends on the system information and a random generator. In
(
Shang and Chen 2022
)
, linear encryption strategies are used
to against eavesdropping attack by degrading its estimation performance.

To ensure accurate decoding of sensor measurements, it is essential for the random encoding and decoding processes to be synchronized. To mitigate the risk of directly transmitting decoding information, the utilization of pseudo-random number generators is recommended. This approach necessitates the distribution of secret random seeds and synchronization between the generators employed on both the sensor and controller sides. In
(
Joo et al. 2020
)
, chaotic oscillators are used to generate coding and decoding signals at plant and estimator side, respectively.
However, there still needs a secure channel to synchronize the oscillators at the both sides.

In
(
Cecilia et al. 2023
)
, a quasi-periodic signal is used to encode (mask) the sensor measurement, and a washout filter
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
is used to remove the masking signal. With this masking protocol, the privacy is protected and a FDI attack in the communication channel can be detected. It is worth mentioning that this protocol does not need additional communication links for synchronization. Inspired by this result, in this work we use chaotic signal to encode the sensor measurement. To decode the sensor measurement, an estimator combining with the dynamics of the chaotic system is designed.

The main contribution of this work is summarized as follows:

•

This paper proposes a novel chaotic masking protocol that secures the sensor-estimator communication channel. The protocol is proven to be effective against multiple attack types, including eavesdropping, replay attacks, and false data injection attacks.

•

Similar to the coding-decoding mechanisms, the proposed protocol can recover the sensor outputs completely, without compromising estimation performance. Notably, unlike most coding-decoding mechanisms, the proposed protocol does not require an additional secure communication channel to synchronize the masking and de-masking pair.

Problem Formulation

2.1
Remote Estimator

Consider linear systems of the form

\Sigma_{p}:\;\left\{\begin{split}\dot{x}&=A{x}+Bu\\
y&=C{x},\end{split}\right.

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
is the system state,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is the known input signal, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is the sensor measurement.
For monitoring or control purposes, the sensor measurement
y
is assumed to be transmitted through wireless networks to a remote estimator of the form

\Sigma_{e}:\;\left\{\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}&=A{\hat{x}}+Bu+L(y-\hat{y})\\
\hat{y}&=C{\hat{x}}.\end{split}\right.

(2)

It is well known that if
L
is chosen such that
A-LC
is Hurwitz, the estimation error converges to zero, exponentially.

In the literature, a detector is commonly deployed after the estimator to detect abnormal data from the received sensor measurements. In this paper, the following innovation-based abnormal detector
Mehra and Peschon 1971
;
Pasqualetti et al. 2013
is deployed as

g(z):=z^{\top}z\underset{\mathcal{H}_{0}}{\overset{\mathcal{H}_{1}}{\gtrless}}\nu

(3)

where
z=y-\hat{y}
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
means that no alarm is triggered,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
means that the detector alarms, and
\nu
is a threshold implemented to avoid false alarms induced by sensor and/or channel noise.

In this paper, we consider that the transmission network may be attacked by adversaries, leading to leakage risk of private data (e.g., the system state), and/or failure of the remote estimator by maliciously modifying the transmitted information. Furthermore, it may cause security issues if the estimated states are further utilized for control or other purposes.
Specifically, we are mainly interested in following three types of attacks.

i)

Eavesdropping attack.
The adversary has access to the transmitted information over the wireless network, and aims to eavesdrop the information of system states.

ii)

Replay attack.
The adversary records the normal sensor data and replay them in sensor-controller channel when injecting false data into the controller-actuator channel, destroying the plant while avoid being detected by detector (
3
).

iii)

Stealthy data injection attack.
The adversary modifies the the transmitted information over the wireless network by adding stealthy signals
\{a\}
such that the remote estimator fails while avoid being detected by detector (
3
).

The main purpose of this paper is securing the communication channel by protecting the transmission signal
y
. Specially, the security refers to

i)

Prevent eavesdropper from estimating
x
by using
y
and the information of the linear system
\Sigma_{p}
.

ii)

Invalidate the replay attack strategy.

iii)

Destroy the stealthiness of the data injection attack.

3
System Protection with Chaotic Masking

3.1
Masking Approach

To secure the communication channel, a chaotic signal is used to mask the sensor measurement
y
, i.e.,

\textbf{y}:=y+d,

where
d
is the chaotic signal to be designed. In this way, the masked sensor measurement
y
is transmitted in the channel, instead of transmitting
y
directly.

The chaotic signal is generated by a chaotic system of the following form

\Sigma_{c}:\;\left\{\begin{split}\dot{\xi}&=\Phi{\xi}+\varphi(\xi)\\
d&=\Lambda{\xi}\end{split}\right.

(4)

where
\xi\in\Xi\subset\bm{\bar{}}\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\xi}}
is the state of chaotic system, with
\Xi
being a compact forward invariant set for the chaotic dynamics.
\bm{\bar{}}\Xi
is a hypercube containing
\Xi
, with the center of
\bm{\bar{}}\Xi
being
0_{n_{\xi}}
and the length of its
i^{\text{th}}
edge being
2\sigma_{i}
, where

\sigma_{i}:=\max_{\xi}|\xi_{i}|,~\xi\in\Xi,

(5)

which is illustrated in Fig.
1
in the case
n_{\xi}=2
.
It is assumed that the mapping
\varphi(\xi)
is locally Lipschitz, that is

\begin{split}\|\varphi(\xi_{1})-\varphi(\xi_{2})\|\leq\ell_{\varphi}\|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\|,~~\forall\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\bm{\bar{}}\Xi\end{split}

(6)

for some positive constant
\ell_{\varphi}
, which is related to
\Xi
.
It is further assumed that the model of the chaotic system, i.e.,
\Phi,\varphi(\cdot)
and
\Lambda
are known for remote estimator, but unknown to the potential adversaries.

Finally, by letting

\textbf{x}:=\text{col}(\xi,x)
,
the plant
\Sigma_{p}
and the chaotic masking
\Sigma_{c}
are compactly rewritten as

\Sigma_{{\bf{P}}}:\;\left\{\begin{split}\dot{\textbf{x}}&={\bf{A}}\textbf{x}+{\bf{B}}u+{\bf{G}}(\xi)\\
\textbf{y}&={\bf{C}}\textbf{x}.\end{split}\right.

(7)

where we defined

\begin{split}{\bf{A}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Phi&0\\
0&A\\
\end{array}\right],{\bf{B}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
B\\
\end{array}\right],{\bf{G}}(\xi):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi(\xi)\\

\end{array}\right],{\bf{C}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Lambda&C\\
\end{array}\right].\end{split}

Note that since

is locally Lipschitz,

is also locally Lipschitz with the same constant

4
Defense of Eavesdropping Attack

4.1
Eavesdropping Attack

The objective of eavesdropping attack is to access the plant states by eavesdropping the transmitted data. In this section, we assume that an eavesdropper has access to the communication channel and has full information of the system model, i.e.,
\{A,B,C\}
, but no access to the mask (
4
).

It is clear that the attacker can successfully eavesdrop the states of the plant, i.e.,
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}|{x}-\hat{x}^{a}|=0
, by the following estimator

\Sigma_{\text{eav}}:\;\left\{\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{a}&=A{\hat{x}^{a}}+Bu+\bar{L}(y-\hat{y}^{a})\\
\hat{y}^{a}&=C{\hat{x}^{a}}\end{split}\right.

(18)

where
\bar{L}
can be any matrix such that
A-\bar{L}C
is Hurwitz.

4.2
Eavesdropping Error Caused by Chaotic Masking

In this section we aim at showing that in the presence of the proposed chaotic masking protocol, the eavesdroppers estimator
\Sigma_{\text{eav}}
would fail to estimate the states of the plant. Since the outputs of a chaotic system cannot be synchronized without the full
knowledge of its specific class and associated internal parameters
Joo et al. 2020
, the chaotic signal added on the sensor measurement is seen as bounded disturbance. Specifically, with chaotic masking,
\Sigma_{\text{eav}}
becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{a}&=A{\hat{x}^{a}}+Bu+L(y+d-\hat{y}^{a})\\
\hat{y}^{a}&=C{\hat{x}^{a}}\end{split}

(19)

where
\|d\|\leq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon
.

Then the estimation error dynamic is

\begin{split}\dot{e}&=(A-LC)e-Ld.\end{split}

Since
d
is non-vanishing, it is clear that
e(t)
would not converge to zero.
Specifically, according to
(
Khalil 2002
, Corollary 5.2)
, the attacker can only guarantee that the estimation error

\begin{split}\|{e(t)}\|&\leq\frac{2\lambda_{\text{max}}^{2}(S)\|L\|}{\lambda_{\text{min}}(S)}\varepsilon\end{split}

where
S
is the solution of the Lyapunov equation

S(A-LC)+(A-LC)^{T}S=-I.

Hence the estimator (
19
) would lose its efficacy with a relatively large
\varepsilon
. In other words, we can increase the privacy level of the system by designing chaotic signals such that the amplitude of
d
is large.

5
Defense of Replay Attack

5.1
Replay Attack

The considered replay attack strategy is given as follows.

\Sigma_{\text{replay}}:

1.

From time
-\tau
to time
0
, the attacker records a sequence of sensor measurements
y
.

2.

From time
0
to time
\tau
, the attacker replays the recorded sensor measurements to tamper the true sensor outputs, i.e.,

y^{a}(t)=y(t-\tau),\quad 0\leq t\leq\tau.

(20)

Meanwhile, arbitrary harmful attack sequence is injected into the control channel to destroy the plant.

For simplicity, suppose that
u=-K\hat{x}
with
A-BK
Hurwitz. In addition, here we assume that the control system has run for a long time (already achieved a steady state) before it is attacked, i.e., the system and the observer have already reached their steady states before time
-\tau
.

The following Proposition shows the fact that the replay attack is unable to be detected without masking.

Proposition 5.8

Consider system
\Sigma_{p}
and estimator
\Sigma_{e}
, that is, the case without any security mechanism. Assume that the system and the observer have already reached their steady state at a time
t=-\tau
for some positive constant
\tau>0
, that is,
x(-\tau)=\hat{x}(-\tau)
. Moreover, assume that
A-BK-LC
is Hurwitz. Then, the innovation term
z:=y-\hat{y}
satisfies
z(t)=0,\forall t\geq 0
under the replay attack
\Sigma_{\text{replay}}
.

Proof 5.9

The system from time
-\tau
to zero can be described in the following form

\begin{split}\dot{x}^{\tau}&=A{x}^{\tau}+Bu^{\tau}\\
y^{\tau}&=C{x}^{\tau}.\end{split}

The steady-state observer is

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{\tau}&=A\hat{x}^{\tau}+Bu^{\tau}+Lz^{\tau}\\
\end{split}

(21)

with
z^{\tau}=y^{\tau}-C\hat{x}^{\tau}
and
u^{\tau}=-K\hat{x}^{\tau}
.

When the observer is a under attack (
0\leq t\leq\tau
),
it becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{a}&=A\hat{x}^{a}+Bu^{a}+Lz^{a}\\
\end{split}

(22)

with
z^{a}=y^{a}-C\hat{x}^{a}
and
u^{a}=-K\hat{x}^{a}
.

Subtracting (
21
) with (
22
), there is

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{\tau}-\dot{\hat{x}}^{a}&=(A-BK-LC)(\hat{x}^{\tau}-\hat{x}^{a})+L(y^{\tau}-y^{a}).\end{split}

Since
y_{k}^{\tau}=y_{k}^{a}
(by
(
20
)
) and
\Lambda:=A-BL-LC
is Hurwitz, we have

\begin{split}\hat{x}^{\tau}-\hat{x}^{a}=e^{\Lambda t}(\hat{x}^{\tau}(0)-\hat{x}^{a}(0)).\end{split}

Consequently, the innovation of the attacked system is

\begin{split}z^{a}&=y^{a}-C\hat{x}^{a}=y^{\tau}-C\hat{x}^{\tau}+Ce^{\Lambda t}(\hat{x}^{\tau}(0)-\hat{x}^{a}(0)).\end{split}

Since the observer has already reached its steady state before
-\tau
, we have
y^{\tau}-C\hat{x}^{\tau}=0
. In addition, since the system
\Sigma_{p}
is linear, it stays at an equilibrium point in steady state. Consequently, we have

\hat{x}^{\tau}(0)=\hat{x}^{a}(0)
.
Further considering the fact that
\Lambda
is Hurwitz,
e^{\Lambda t}
is bounded, and hence
e^{\Lambda t}(\hat{x}^{\tau}(0)-\hat{x}^{a}(0))=0
.
As a result, the innovation under replay attack equals to
0
and the attack cannot be detected.

5.2
Invalidating the Replay Attack Strategy

In the above subsection, the replay attack strategy relies on the fact that the physical plant stays at an equilibrium point in steady state. With chaotic masking, the extended system
\Sigma_{{\bf{P}}}
contains chaotic dynamics and has a more complex steady-state behavior, which is no longer an equilibrium, periodic oscillation, or almost-periodic oscillation. Consequently, any replay attack strategy will increase, at least during a transient time, the innovation term
\textbf{z}:=\textbf{y}-\hat{\textbf{y}}
. This fact is formalized in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.10

Consider the extended system
\Sigma_{{\bf{P}}}
and the extended estimator
\Sigma_{{\bf{E}}}
, that is, the case with chaotic masking. Assume that the estimation error of the extended system and the extended observer has reached its steady state before time
-\tau
, that is,
\textbf{x}(-\tau)=\hat{\textbf{x}}(-\tau)
. Consider the replay attack
\Sigma_{\text{replay}}
, then, the innovation
\textbf{z}:=\textbf{y}-\hat{\textbf{y}}
cannot be zero for all
t
such that
0\leq t\leq\tau
.

Proof 5.11

The extended system from
-\tau
to zero can be described in the following form

\begin{split}\dot{\textbf{x}}^{\tau}&={\bf{A}}\textbf{x}^{\tau}+{\bf{B}}(-Kx^{\tau})+{\bf{G}}(\xi^{\tau})\\
\textbf{y}^{\tau}&={\bf{C}}\textbf{x}^{\tau}\end{split}

The steady-state observer is

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{\textbf{x}}}^{\tau}&={\bf{A}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{\tau}+{\bf{B}}(-Kx^{\tau})+{\bf{G}}(\text{sat}_{\sigma}(\hat{\xi}^{\tau}))+{\bf{L}}\textbf{z}^{\tau}\\
\hat{\textbf{y}}^{\tau}&={\bf{C}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{\tau}\\
\end{split}

with
\textbf{z}^{\tau}=\textbf{y}^{\tau}-\hat{\textbf{y}}^{\tau}
.

When the observer is under replay attack (
0\leq t\leq\tau
), it becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{\textbf{x}}}^{a}&={\bf{A}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}+{\bf{B}}(-Kx^{a})+{\bf{G}}(\text{sat}_{\sigma}(\hat{\xi}^{a}))+{\bf{L}}\textbf{z}^{a}\\
\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a}&={\bf{C}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}\\
\end{split}

(23)

with
\textbf{z}^{a}=\textbf{y}^{a}-\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a}=\textbf{y}^{\tau}-\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a}
.

The following proof is by contradiction.
If
\textbf{z}^{a}\equiv 0
for all
t
such that
0\leq t\leq\tau
, the replay attack does not affect (
23
) and we have
\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}(t)=\hat{\textbf{x}}(t)
for
0\leq t\leq\tau
.
Consequently,

\begin{split}\textbf{z}^{a}\equiv 0\Leftrightarrow\textbf{y}^{\tau}-\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a}\equiv 0\Leftrightarrow{\bf{C}}\textbf{x}^{\tau}\equiv{\bf{C}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}\Leftrightarrow{\bf{C}}\textbf{x}^{\tau}\equiv{\bf{C}}\hat{\textbf{x}}.\end{split}

Since the estimator has already reached steady state, we have
\hat{\textbf{x}}=\textbf{x}
, and hence we can deduce
{\bf{C}}\textbf{x}^{\tau}(t)\equiv{\bf{C}}\textbf{x}(t),0\leq t\leq\tau
, implying (since
x
is instantaneously observable)

\textbf{x}^{\tau}(t)\equiv\textbf{x}(t),~~0\leq t\leq\tau,

which contradicts with the fact that
x
is chaotic, that is, non-periodic.

6
Defense of Stealthy Data Injection Attack

6.1
Stealthy Data Injection Attack

A stealthy data injection attacker injects attack sequence
\{a\}
into the sensor-estimator channel to ruin the estimator
\Sigma_{e}
while keeping stealthy. The adjective “stealthy” refers to the fact that the sequence
\{a\}
can be designed to be undetected by common innovation-based detectors such as (
3
).

For simplicity, we suppose
u=0
. To launch stealthy data injection attack, we suppose that the attack can get the system information
\{A,B,C,L\}
and can inject arbitrary
\{a\}
into the sensor measurement.
With stealthy data injection attack, the unprotected estimator
\Sigma_{e}
becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{x}}^{a}&=A{\hat{x}}^{a}+L(y^{a}-\hat{y}^{a})\\
\hat{y}^{a}&=C{\hat{x}}^{a}\end{split}

(24)

where
y^{a}=y+a
with
a
is the attack injected by adversaries. Define estimation difference
\Delta\hat{x}:={\hat{x}}^{a}-{\hat{x}}
and innovation difference
\Delta z:=z^{a}-z
. Comparing
\Sigma_{e}
with (
24
), there is

\begin{split}\Delta\dot{\hat{x}}&=(A-LC)\Delta{\hat{x}}+La\\
\Delta z&=-C\Delta{\hat{x}}+a\end{split}

(25)

According to the triangular inequality
\|z^{a}\|\leq\|z\|+\|\Delta z\|
, if
\|\Delta z\|
is small, the detector cannot distinguish between
z^{a}
and
z
with high probability. Consequently, the detection rate of detector (
3
) is close to its false alarm rate. Hence the attack is assumed to be stealthy if

\begin{split}\|\Delta z\|\leq M.\end{split}

(26)

Based on (
26
), stealthy attack against remote estimator is designed as

\begin{split}\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}:\;a=C\Delta{\hat{x}}+\varphi.\end{split}

(27)

It is noted that, without chaotic masking,
\Delta{\hat{x}}
can be obtained by (
25
) with
\Delta{\hat{x}}(0)=0
. By choosing
\varphi
as any time-varying functions satisfying
\|\varphi\|\leq M
,
\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}
is stealthy in the sense of (
26
). In addition, by specific design of
\varphi
, one can regulate the estimation difference to some prescribed values
Chen et al. 2023a
or maximize it
Ning et al. 2023
.

6.2
Destroying the Stealthiness of the Data Injection Attack

In this subsection, we will show that, with chaotic masking,
\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}
is no more stealthy.

With chaotic masking, the extended estimator under stealthy attack becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\hat{\textbf{x}}}^{a}&={\bf{A}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}+{\bf{G}}(\text{sat}_{\sigma}(\hat{\xi}^{a}))+{\bf{L}}(\textbf{y}^{a}-\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a})\\
\hat{\textbf{y}}^{a}&={\bf{C}}\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}\\
\end{split}

(28)

Let
\textbf{z}:=\textbf{y}-\hat{\textbf{y}}
,
\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}:=\hat{\textbf{x}}^{a}-\hat{\textbf{x}}
and
\Delta\textbf{z}:=\textbf{z}^{a}-\textbf{z}
.
According to
\Sigma_{{\bf{E}}}
and (
28
), we have

\begin{split}\Delta\dot{\hat{\textbf{x}}}&=({\bf{A}}-{\bf{L}}{\bf{C}})\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}+{\bf{G}}(\text{sat}_{\sigma}(\hat{\xi}^{a}))-{\bf{G}}(\text{sat}_{\sigma}(\hat{\xi}))+{\bf{L}}a\\
\Delta\textbf{z}&=-{\bf{C}}\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}+a\end{split}

(29)

Since the term
{\bf{C}}\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}
contains both
\Delta{\hat{x}}
and
\Delta{\hat{\xi}}
, it is unlikely to be offset by
\Delta{\hat{x}}
in
\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}
. As a result, attack
\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}
cannot guarantee that
\|\Delta\textbf{z}\|=\|-{\bf{C}}\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}+\Delta{\hat{x}}+\varphi\|\leq M
. In other words,
\Sigma_{\text{FDI}}
is no more sealthy.

In addition, since the system (
29
) is input-to-output stable from
a
to
\Delta\textbf{z}
(this fact can be verified by
(
Khalil 2002
, Theorem 5.1)
by taking
V=\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}^{\top}{\bf{P}}\Delta\hat{\textbf{x}}
), there exist some
\kappa
such that if
\|a\|\leq\kappa
, we have
\|\Delta\textbf{z}\|\leq M
. In other words,
a
may still be designed small enough to make sure the attack is stealthy. However, the supremum of
\kappa
may be too small to launch any significant attacks, and, it is hard for a potential attacker to estimate the supremum of
\kappa
without any information of
\Phi
.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, a chaotic masking protocol has been proposed to secure the remote estimator. Since an extended estimator was designed to estimate both chaotic states and physical system states, the masking protocol does not require extra communication to synchronize the masking-damasking pair and its effect can be perfectly removed in steady state. To verify the effectiveness of the proposed chaotic masking protocol, three types of attacks were considered. Future works will focus on extending the
proposed framework to mask non-linear systems and to protect whole control system loops.
