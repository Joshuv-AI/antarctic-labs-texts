When can we Approximate Wide Contrastive Models with Neural Tangent Kernels and Principal Component Analysis?
Gautham Govind Anil, Pascal Esser, Debarghya Ghoshdastidar

Abstract
Contrastive learning is a paradigm for learning representations from unlabelled data that has been highly successful for image and text data. Several recent works have examined contrastive losses to claim that contrastive models effectively learn spectral embeddings, while few works show relations between (wide) contrastive models and kernel principal component analysis (PCA). However, it is not known if trained contrastive models indeed correspond to kernel methods or PCA. In this work, we analyze the training dynamics of two-layer contrastive models, with non-linear activation, and answer when these models are close to PCA or kernel methods. It is well known in the supervised setting that neural networks are equivalent to neural tangent kernel (NTK) machines, and that the NTK of infinitely wide networks remains constant during training. We provide the first convergence results of NTK for contrastive losses, and present a nuanced picture: NTK of wide networks remains almost constant for cosine similarity based contrastive losses, but not for losses based on dot product similarity. We further study the training dynamics of contrastive models with orthogonality constraints on output layer, which is implicitly assumed in works relating contrastive learning to spectral embedding. Our deviation bounds suggest that representations learned by contrastive models are close to the principal components of a certain matrix computed from random features. We empirically show that our theoretical results possibly hold beyond two-layer networks.

Introduction

The paradigm of self-supervised learning (SSL) builds on the idea of using knowledge about semantic similarities in the data to define which data-points should be mapped close to each other in the latent representation.
The goal of SSL is to learn a “good representation”. While there is no unique notion of “good” without taking a downstream task into consideration
(
Bengio et al. 2013
)
, in general one is interested in mapping semantically similar objects to close representations in the latent space, but avoid “dimension collapse” that occurs when different dimensions in the latent space
collapse
to the same value.
Depending on the mechanism used to prevent collapse of learned embeddings, SSL strategies can be broadly categorised as contrastive or non-contrastive learning. Contrastive learning relies on negative samples to ensure representations do not collapse
(
Oord et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
;
HaoChen et al. 2021
)
, whereas non-contrastive learning avoids collapse by incorporating architectural asymmetry
(
Grill et al. 2020
;
Chen and He 2021
)
or reduction in dimension redundancy
(
Zbontar et al. 2021
;
Bardes et al. 2021
)
.
In practice, a plethora of SSL strategies, including deep contrastive and non-contrastive models, have been proposed over the past years across multiple domains; many of them demonstrating excellent performance empirically
(
Assran et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
. While these works underline the importance of SSL and (non-)contrastive models for applications, their theoretical understanding is still limited.

Theoretical analysis of SSL is in its early stages.
There has been considerable effort in deriving generalization error bounds for downstream tasks on learned embeddings
(
Arora et al. 2019b
;
Wei et al. 2021
;
Bao et al. 2022
)
, and analysing spectral / isoperimetric properties of data augmentation
(
Han et al. 2023
;
Zhuo et al. 2023
)
.
Results based on learning theoretic measures
(
Saunshi et al. 2019
;
Wei et al. 2020
;
Nozawa and Sato 2021
)
, information theory
(
Tsai et al. 2020
;
Tosh et al. 2021
)
and loss landscapes
(
Pokle et al. 2022
;
Ziyin et al. 2022
)
have been studied.

Generalisation bounds, however, provide little understanding of the representations learned via SSL.

Balestriero and LeCun 2022
answer this by showing that various (non)contrastive learning formulations result in learning spectral embedding, principal component analysis (PCA) or their variants.
In a similar vein,
Munkhoeva and Oseledets 2023
relate contrastive learning with trace maximization problems and matrix completion—all related to PCA.

Equivalences between the optimization formulations of SSL and PCA do not necessarily imply that (non-)contrastive models, trained with gradient descent, perform PCA
.
This requires analysing either the converged solution or the training dynamics of SSL.

A number of works derive and study the training dynamics of (non)contrastive learning, albeit mostly limited to linear neural networks
(
Wang and Isola 2020
;
Tian et al. 2021
;
Wang and Liu 2021
;
Tian 2022
;
Esser et al. 2023b
)
.
In the context of non-linear networks,
Simon et al. 2023
suggest that for wide neural networks, that is, in the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
, contrastive learning could be equivalent to kernel PCA
(
Schölkopf et al. 1997
)
.

Although no prior work explicitly analyses the convergence of wide contrastive models to kernel (or NTK) machines, there has been a significant interest in training kernel models under (non)contrastive losses

(
Kiani et al. 2022
;
Cabannes et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2023a
)
.
Depending on the problem formulation, it can indeed be shown that these kernel contrastive models are closely related to kernel PCA
(
Esser et al. 2023a
)
or kernel support vector machine
(
Shah et al. 2022
)
.

Motivation and Contributions.

In spite of strongly suggesting relations between constrative learning, PCA and kernel methods (or NTKs), existing theoretical works do not explicitly answer
if trained contrastive models are close to kernel methods, specifically with a fixed deterministic kernel
(as has been shown in the NTK regime for supervised models).
There is also no theoretical evidence on
when trained contrastive models can be approximated by solutions of PCA or other trace maximization problems
.
We analyse the training dynamics of two-layer non-linear networks trained under contrastive or non-contrastive losses, and rigorously answer both questions. Specifically:

1.

In Section
3
, we derive the NTK of two-layer networks of width
M
trained under (non)-contrastive loss, and study the deviation between NTK after several steps of gradient descent from the NTK at initialization.
Our results address questions on the constancy of NTK.

Observation 1:
(Non-)Contrastive losses are defined in terms of similarities between learned representations. We show that if the losses are in terms of dot-product similarity, then NTK drastically changes within
O(\log M)
training time. Experiments on non-contrastive learning suggest that NTK changes
(
Simon et al. 2023
)
, but there was no prior theoretical evidence.

Observation 2:
In contrast to dot product similarity, if the losses are defined in terms of cosine similarity—considered in
InfoNCE

(
Oord et al. 2018
)
and
SimCLR

(
Chen et al. 2020
)
—then NTK after
O(M^{1/6})
steps is close to NTK at initialisation. Thus, contrastive models trained under such losses can be approximated by kernel methods, with a fixed NTK. Unfortunately, unlike supervised learning—where trained neural networks in NTK regime is the solution of kernel regression—there may not be a closed formed analytical solution of the trained model.

2.

In Section
4
, we study the training dynamics of (Grassmannian) gradient descent under orthogonality constraints of the output layer of the network.
While orthogonality is not imposed in practical SSL approaches, it is often assumed in theoretical works to relate contrastive learning to variants of PCA
(
Munkhoeva and Oseledets 2023
)
, in kernel SSL formulations
(
Esser et al. 2023a
)
, to prevent dimension collapse
(
Esser et al. 2023b
)
etc.

Observation 1:
We note that, with orthogonality constraint, some contrastive losses (or their modifications) are equivalent to PCA of a
M\times M
matrix
\boldsymbol{C}(t)
that depends on the non-linear features at the hidden layer, learned after
t
iterations of gradient descent.

Observation 2:
For some cosine-similarity based contrastive losses, the Frobenius norm deviation
\|\boldsymbol{C}(t)-\boldsymbol{C}(0)\|_{F}=O(t/\sqrt{M})
suggesting that, in this case, wide contrastive models are close to PCA of a randomly initialised matrix
\boldsymbol{C}(0)
.
Furthermore, the representation learned via PCA from
\boldsymbol{C}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{C}(0)
are also close, upto orthonormal rotations.

Empirical validation of our theoretical results are provided using MNIST dataset, and we further show that some of the results may also hold beyond two-layer networks (see Section
5
). All proofs are provided in the supplementary material.

2.2
Embedding Function

The outlined setup for contrastive losses is stated for an arbitrary embedding function
f(\cdot)
. However, for our analysis, we focus on one hidden layer neural networks. We aim to find a mapping
f({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Z}

In addition, we also consider the following assumptions:

Assumption 4

(Max norm of weights at initialization)

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}(0)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{V}(0)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}\leq c_{\mathbf{\theta}}\log M

Assumption 5

(Smoothness of activation function)

Assumption 6

3.1
NTK for Dot product Losses Does Not Necessarily Stay Constant

We start with examining the change in weights (as defined in (
4

as the similarity measure. In particular, we show that in this setting, there exists cases where the change in weights become arbitrarily large even for arbitrarily wide neural networks, implying that the NTK does not remain constant. To demonstrate this, we consider a simple loss function of the form (
1

Because there is no normalization in the dot product similarity measure, the loss can be minimized arbitrarily by scaling the weights and hence we expect the weights to grow arbitrarily large with time. 2

3.2

Remark 14

(Closed form solution in terms of NTK)
.

In the supervised setting, combining the analytical NTK with the closed form solution for kernel regression provides a closed form expression of the network trained until convergence
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
, or even when early stopped.
We take a step towards such a result for contrastive losses. In Lemma
9
, we present the learning dynamics in the contrastive loss setting in terms of the NTK and in Theorem

, we show that the NTK remains constant during training for
M^{1/6}
steps under
cosine similarity
. While this is an important step towards a better theoretical understanding of contrastive models, it does not yet provide a closed form solution of the model output in terms of the NTK.
While prior works on kernel contrastive methods suggest that, in the wide neural network regime, contrastive losses could be equivalent to kernel PCA
(
Simon et al. 2023
)
, this connection has not been proven so far and is not apparent from the dynamics derived in Lemma
9
even if the NTK is constant (for cosine similarity based losses). Therefore, we shift our viewpoint from NTK dynamics to explicitly investigating this connection.

4
On the Connection between Wide Contrastive Models and PCA

We next study if there is a formal connection between PCA and representations learned by contrastive models, trained with gradient descent. Prior works have connected contrastive models to a trace maximization problem with an orthogonality constraint on the output layer, of the form:

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{W},\vartheta}\text{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{\vartheta}\boldsymbol{W}\right)\quad\textup{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}=\mathbb{I}_{Z},

(5)

\vartheta

\boldsymbol{W}

More specifically,

considers a kernel setting where learning is done using a contrastive loss of the form (
5

Esser et al. 2023b
considers neural network dynamics for contrastive losses of the form (
5
) but only examines the linear setting.
Simon et al. 2023
considers the dynamics for kernel models but does not investigate if the kernel dynamics are close to the neural network dynamics.
Munkhoeva and Oseledets 2023
reformulates losses such as
SimCLR
to (
5

Extending prior works, we work towards formalizing the connection between

5
Further discussion and open problems

Effect of initialization on dimension collapse:

For the presented analysis, we only have a mild assumption on the initialization of weights (Assumption
4
) that holds with high probability. While this assumption is sufficient for proving the constancy of NTK results in Section
3
, additional assumptions may be needed to obtain
meaningful
representations. While learning representations, it is desirable to avoid
dimension collapse
. Dimension collapse, in the context of linear contrastive models, has been shown for
dot product

(
Esser et al. 2023b
)
. Using NTK, we show that certain initialization schemes can cause dimension collapse even if
cosine similarity
based losses are used:

Proposition 18
.

Consider a neural network of the form (
2
) trained using a loss of the form (
1
) using gradient descent. Then for any input
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,

{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}}(0)={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}(0)\Rightarrow f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};{\mathbf{\theta}(t)})=f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}};{\mathbf{\theta}(t)}),\ \forall\;t\geq 0.

Thus, collapse occurs irrespective of whether the NTK remains constant or not, and hence, this is applicable irrespective of the width of the neural network. Further, the result holds for both dot product and cosine similarity based losses. The result also holds for analytical NTK
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
; if
\boldsymbol{K}^{*}
is used in the dynamics in Lemma
9
, then
f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(0))=f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(0))\Rightarrow f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))=f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t)),\ \forall t\geq 0.

Empirical observations beyond the theory:

(i)

Deep networks:

While Theorem
13
has been shown to hold only in the case of neural networks with a single hidden layer, we expect it to hold for deep networks as well. We experimentally examine the case with
3
hidden layers in Figure
5
(left). The results are similar to the case of a single hidden layer, as we again observe a decay with width that is roughly
\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}
up to scaling.
Along similar lines, we observe in Figure
5
(middle) that the NTK for three hidden layer networks optimized with
dot product
based losses diverges, similar to the single hidden layer case.

(ii)

Iterative learning of the trace maximization problem:
Combining the findings from Section
4
, we propose an alternative optimization procedure to solve (
5
) under
\boldsymbol{A}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(t)
. As
\boldsymbol{C}_{V}
updates slower than
\boldsymbol{W}
as shown in Figure
4
(middle), and since for a fixed
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}
the optimal
\boldsymbol{W}
can be obtained using PCA, an alternative update could be by iteratively (a) updating
\boldsymbol{W}
by solving PCA on
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(t-1)
and (b) updating
\boldsymbol{V}
by running one step of gradient descent. We validate this approach in the following.

(iii)

Predictive accuracy in downstream tasks:
Extending the results from Section
4.2
, we show in Figure
5
(right) that the representations obtained with PCA on
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(0)
and fully training (
7
) under (
6
) are close to each other with regards to downstream test accuracy for a simple linear classifier trained on the representations. While Section
4.2
provides results on the behaviour of the PCA for different time-steps of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}
, Figure
5
(right) suggests that this similarity extends to PCA on
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(0)
and fully trained networks as well.
In addition, we also observe that the iterative optimization (as proposed above) performs well, especially for smaller widths (possibly due to the larger change in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}
for small
M
).

Open problem (Missing link in the claim contrastive models perform PCA):

In Lemma
17
, we compare the solutions obtained by PCA on
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(0)
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(t)
. But in Figure
4
(middle), we observe that
\boldsymbol{C}_{V}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
evolve simultaneously, even though at different rates. The open problem then, is the exact connection between
fully trained contrastive models and the PCA solution.
We believe that a direct analysis, such as the one considered for Theorem
13
, may not work. This is because the solutions might differ significantly at convergence even for closely initialized models if the eigengap is not significant. Therefore, a spectral viewpoint combined with the analysis of gradient descent steps is necessary to bound the deviation.

Open problem (NTK and PCA at convergence):

Theorem
13
shows that, for wide networks, the NTK remains nearly constant for
M^{1/6}
time-steps (in fact, one can also show constancy till
O(M^{\alpha})
steps for
\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
). While such results are in line with initial NTK analysis in the supervised setting (e.g.
Jacot et al. 2018
),
it is an open question whether the constancy of NTK holds until convergence
.
In fact, Figure
3
suggests that for wide networks, NTK remains constant beyond
M^{\alpha}
steps.
A potential approach to prove constancy till convergence would be to investigate the stationary points associated with cosine similarity based losses and verify if they are attained within
M^{\alpha}
steps. While this is a crucial open question, we believe that the presented results provide the first valuable insights into the constancy of NTK under contrastive losses, beyond the squared error.

In Lemma
17
, we show that the outputs of the two considered trace maximization problems are close for
O({t}(\log M)/{\sqrt{M}})
steps. As the expression is in terms of time-steps,
the question remains if they are still close at convergence
. While we do not have a precise characterization of such results, a possible approach could be to extend
Xu and Li 2021
, who show that for
Z=1
, PCA converges in roughly
O(\log(M))
steps under Riemannian gradient descent.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{V}(t)-\boldsymbol{V}(0)\right\rVert_{F}+\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}_{z.}(t)-\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}_{z.}(0)\right\rVert_{F}\leq R

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}_{z.}(t)-\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}_{z.}(0)\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}(t)-\boldsymbol{W}(0)\right\rVert_{F}
. Let the Hessian corresponding to this neural network be denoted as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))
. Note that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))
satisfies all conditions required (linear final layer and Gaussian initialization) by Theorem 7.1 of
Liu et al. 2020a
, and hence we can use this theorem to bound
\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}
. To connect this to
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{H}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}
, note that after appropriate permutations of rows and columns (note that spectral norm is invariant to permutations), we can write:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}

and therefore
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{H}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}
. Hence, we can apply Theorem 7.1 of
Liu et al. 2020a
to
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{H}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}
. Now, from the proof of Theorem 7.1 in
Liu et al. 2020a
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert H^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L^{2}C^{\prime}(R)}{\sqrt{M}}

where
L
is the number of layers in the neural network and

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(R)

\displaystyle=\beta_{\phi}c_{in}^{2}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L-1}L_{\phi}^{2l^{\prime}-1}(c_{0}+R)^{2l^{\prime}-2}C_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(l^{\prime})}(R)+L_{\phi}^{L-1}(c_{0}+R)^{L-2}c_{in}

For
L=2
, which is the case we are interested in, this reduces to:

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(R)

\displaystyle=\beta_{\phi}c_{in}^{2}L_{\phi}C_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(1)}(R)+L_{\phi}c_{in}

In our setup, we have
c_{0}=c_{s}
, where
c_{s}
is defined in Assumption
6
and
C_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{(1)}(R)=s_{0}c_{0}+R
, where
s_{0}
is defined in Assumption
6
.
So for each Hessian matrix, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert H^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}R+\alpha_{2}}{\sqrt{M}}

(11)

where
\alpha_{1}=4\beta_{\phi}c_{in}^{2}L_{\phi}
and
\alpha_{2}=4L_{\phi}c_{in}(1+\beta_{\phi}c_{in}s_{0}c_{s})
. Note that in general, for
L
layers, the spectral norm has a bound of the form
\left\lVert H^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right\rVert_{2}\leq O(\frac{R^{3L}}{\sqrt{M}})
. However, for the special case of
L=2
, it is possible to further reduce it to the form in (
11
).
∎

A.2
Lemma
8
[Bound on the change in NTK]

Define
\mathbb{S}:=\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p};\left\lVert\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}(0)\right\rVert\leq R\}
, where
p
is the total number of learnable parameters in (
2
). Assume that for any input
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{H}^{(z)}({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{s})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon
and
\left\lVert\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}}f_{z}({{\boldsymbol{x}};{\boldsymbol{s}}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq c_{0}
,
\forall\;z\in[Z]
and
\forall\;\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{S}
. Then, for any inputs
{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\forall\;\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{S}
and
\forall\;i,j\in[Z]
,
\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}};{\boldsymbol{s}})-\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}};{\boldsymbol{s}(0)})\right\rvert\leq 2\epsilon c_{0}R
.

Proof.

We start by writing out and expanding the difference in the
(i,j)^{\text{th}}
kernel entry when there is a change in
\boldsymbol{s}
from
\boldsymbol{s}(0)
to
\boldsymbol{s}
:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s})-\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s}(0))\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}-{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left({\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}-{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}+{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}-\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}-{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}-\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}\right\rVert

where we have used Triangle and Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities. Using the fact that
\left\lVert\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}}f_{z}({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{s})\right\rVert
,
\left\lVert\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}}f_{z}(\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s})\right\rVert\leq c_{0}
,
\;\forall\;\boldsymbol{s}\in S,z\in[Z]
(from the lemma assumptions), we can simplify the expression to:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s})-\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s}(0))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\left(\left\lVert{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}-{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}-\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}\right\rVert\right).

From Proposition 2.3 of
Liu et al. 2020b
, we know that the remaining terms can be bounded in terms of the Hessian and
R
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}-{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in S}\left\lVert H_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{s})\right\rVert R,

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}}}-{\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{s}(0))}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}(0)}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\boldsymbol{s}\in S}\left\lVert H_{j}(\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s})\right\rVert R.

Combining the above results, we can bound the kernel difference as:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s})-\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{s}(0))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon c_{0}R

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.3
Lemma
9
[Contrastive learning dynamics in terms of NTK]

Consider training a neural network of the form (
2
) using a loss
l(\cdot)
of the form (
1
) under gradient flow on dataset
\mathcal{D}
. Let
g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t)):=\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))}
. Then, for
z\in[Z]
, the representation of an arbitrary input
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
evolves as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{f}_{z}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t)})}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l(\cdot)}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Z}\left[\boldsymbol{K}_{zi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta}(t))g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad+\boldsymbol{K}_{zi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta}(t))g_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta}(t))\right]\big].

Proof.

We are interested in computing the dynamics of
f_{z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})
. Note that the dynamics can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})=\left(\frac{df_{z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{d\mathbf{\theta}}\right)^{\top}\frac{d\mathbf{\theta}}{dt}.

(12)

Since we are optimizing using gradient flow,
\frac{d\mathbf{\theta}}{dt}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
. We now compute
\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}l\left(\{s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})\}_{q=1}^{Q}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Z}\left[\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{\theta}}+\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{\theta}}\right]\right].

Therefore using
\nabla_{\mathbf{\theta}}\mathcal{L}
in (
12
) we obtain the dynamics as

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Z}\left(\frac{df_{z}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{d\mathbf{\theta}}\right)^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{\theta}}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.+\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{\theta}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Z}\left[\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\boldsymbol{K}_{zi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.+\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})}\cdot\boldsymbol{K}_{zi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})\right]\right]

which, after substituting
g_{i}(\textbf{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t)):=\frac{\partial s(\textbf{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))}
, provides the expression in Lemma
9
and thus concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Proposition
10
[NTK under dot product does not remain constant]

For
D=Z=1
, linear loss
(l(a):=a)
,
dot product
similarity and triplet setting
(\mathcal{D}=\{x_{n},x_{n}^{+},

x_{n}^{-}\}_{n=1}^{N})
in (
1
), the optimization is:

\min_{\mathbf{\theta}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(x_{n};\mathbf{\theta})\left(f(x_{n}^{-};\mathbf{\theta})-f(x_{n}^{+};\mathbf{\theta})\right)
.
Consider a network (
2
) with linear activation
(\phi(a):=a)
, weights initialised as independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and trained via gradient flow.

There is a dataset such that, with probability at least
1-\frac{25}{\sqrt{M}}
, for a time step
\tilde{t}\in(0,\log M)
and any input pair
x,\tilde{x}
with
x\tilde{x}\neq 0
, the NTK satisfies

\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}(x,\tilde{x};\mathbf{\theta}(t))-\boldsymbol{K}(x,\tilde{x};\mathbf{\theta}(0))\right\rvert\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\tilde{t}
.

Proof.

Let us first recall the definition of a linear neural network with one dimensional input
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}
and one dimensional output
(Z=1)
:

\displaystyle f(x;\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}x.

Plugging this into the loss function, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(x_{n};\mathbf{\theta})\left(f(x_{n}^{-};\mathbf{\theta})-f(x_{n}^{+};\mathbf{\theta})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}x_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}^{-}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}x_{n}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}(x_{n}^{+}-x_{n}^{-})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-C}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\right)^{2}

where
C=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}(x_{n}^{+}-x_{n}^{-})
is a data dependent constant. Recall that we aim to find a setting under which the NTK does not remain constant and as such it is sufficient to construct an example where this is the case. We therefore choose a dataset such that
C>0
(we do not have to explicitly construct this dataset for the proof, we just need the existence of such a dataset).
Let us define the following for ease of notation:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\right),

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2})\right).

We start by noting that we can write the NTK as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(x,y;\mathbf{\theta}(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2})\right)xy

\displaystyle=P(t)\ xy.

Therefore as
xy
is a fixed constant, to compute the change in NTK, it is sufficient to get an expression for the change of
P
with respect to
t
, which we can write as:

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{M}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left({\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}{dt}+{\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}{dt}\right)\right),

(13)

Now, we have, from the gradient flow assumption:

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}=\frac{2C}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\boldsymbol{w}_{m}=2CS\boldsymbol{w}_{m},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}=\frac{2C}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}_{m}=2CS\boldsymbol{v}_{m}.

(15)

Using (
14
) and (
15
) in (
13
), we can further rewrite
\frac{dP}{dt}
in terms of
S
:

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{2C}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\left[\frac{2}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}({\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}{\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}+{\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}{\boldsymbol{v}_{m}})\right]

\displaystyle=8CS^{2}.

(16)

Now computing the change of
S
w.r.t.
t
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\frac{d\boldsymbol{v}_{m}}{dt}+\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\frac{d\boldsymbol{w}_{m}}{dt}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2C}{{M}}\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{w}_{m^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{v}_{m^{\prime}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}({\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}}+{\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=2CSP

(17)

From (
16
) and (
17
), we have:

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dS}

\displaystyle=\frac{4S}{P}

\displaystyle\implies S^{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{2}(t)}{4}-\alpha

(18)

where
\alpha=\frac{P^{2}(0)}{4}-S^{2}(0)
. Also, note that:

\displaystyle P^{2}(t)-4\alpha\geq 0

(19)

for all
t
since
S^{2}(t)\geq 0
. From (
16
) and (
18
), we have:

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}

\displaystyle=2C(P^{2}-4\alpha)

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{P^{2}-4\alpha}

\displaystyle=2Cdt

(20)

Now,

\displaystyle 4\alpha

\displaystyle=P^{2}-4S^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\left[\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2})\right)^{2}-4\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\left[\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2}+2\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\boldsymbol{w}_{m})\right)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{2}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m}^{2}-2\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\boldsymbol{w}_{m})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}}\left[\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}+\boldsymbol{w}_{m})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m}-\boldsymbol{w}_{m})^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0

Therefore,
\frac{dP}{P^{2}-4\alpha}
is of the form
\frac{dx}{x^{2}-a^{2}}
. (
20
) can then be integrated using partial fractions method to obtain:

\displaystyle{\left\lvert\frac{P-2\sqrt{\alpha}}{P+2\sqrt{\alpha}}\right\rvert}=\beta e^{8C\sqrt{\alpha}t}

where
\beta=\left\lvert\frac{P(0)-2\sqrt{\alpha}}{P(0)+2\sqrt{\alpha}}\right\rvert
. Now, using (
19
) and the fact that
P,4\alpha\geq 0
, we have
P-2\sqrt{\alpha}\geq 0
. Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle{\frac{P-2\sqrt{\alpha}}{P+2\sqrt{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\beta e^{8C\sqrt{\alpha}t}

\displaystyle\implies P(t)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\alpha}\left(\frac{1+\beta e^{8C\sqrt{\alpha}t}}{1-\beta e^{8C\sqrt{\alpha}t}}\right)

Note that since each
\boldsymbol{v}_{m},\boldsymbol{w}_{m}
is initialized as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\neq 0
with probability
1
at initialization. Hence,
S^{2}(0)>0
with probability
1
. Therefore,
P^{2}(0)-4\alpha>0
and hence
P(0)-2\sqrt{\alpha}>0
. Consequently,
\beta>0
with probability 1. Let
\tilde{t}
indicate the time
t
at which
1-\beta e^{8C\sqrt{\alpha}t}=0
. Then,
\tilde{t}=\frac{1}{8C\sqrt{\alpha}}\log{\frac{1}{\beta}}
. Since
0<\beta<1
,
\tilde{t}>0
. Clearly, as
t\rightarrow\tilde{t}
,
P(t)\rightarrow\infty
. Since
\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}(x,y;\mathbf{\theta}(t))-\boldsymbol{K}(x,y;\mathbf{\theta}(0))\right\rvert=\left\lvert(P(t)-P(0))xy\right\rvert
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{K}(x,y;\mathbf{\theta}(t))-\boldsymbol{K}(x,y;\mathbf{\theta}(0))\right\rvert\rightarrow\infty

if
xy\neq 0
.

In the final part of the proof, we show that
\tilde{t}\in(0,\log M)
with probability at least
1-\frac{25}{\sqrt{M}}
. We first compute a few moments for the random variables
S(0)
and
P(0)
.
Due to standard normal initialization of the weights, one can compute the following:

\mathbb{E}[P(0)]=2,Var(P(0))=\frac{4}{M},\mathbb{E}[S(0)]=0,\mathbb{E}[S(0)^{2}]=Var(S(0))=\frac{1}{M}
.
and
Var(S(0)^{2})=\frac{2}{M^{2}}+\frac{6}{M^{3}}
.
By applying Chebyshev’s inequality, it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|P(0)-2|>\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq 4\cdot Var(P(0))=\frac{16}{M};\qquad\mathbb{P}\left(|S(0)|>\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq 4\cdot Var(S(0))=\frac{4}{M}.

We further need to show that
S(0)^{2}>\frac{1}{M^{2}}
with high probability, but Chebyshev’s inequality does not suffice to prove this (as
Var(S(0)^{2})
is too large).
To this end, we use Berry-Esseen theorem
(see, for instance,
Korolev and Shevtsova 2010
, Corollary 1)
: Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{M}
be i.i.d. variables with
\mathbb{E}[Z_{m}]=0,\mathbb{E}[Z_{m}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[|Z_{m}|^{3}]\leq\rho<\infty
. The deviation between cumulative distribution,
F_{Y}(\cdot)
, of
Y=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{M}}\sum_{m}Z_{m}
and standard normal distribution,
\Phi(\cdot)
, is bounded as

\sup_{x}\left|F_{Y}(x)-\Phi(x)\right|<\frac{0.52\rho}{\sigma^{3}\sqrt{M}}.

In the present context,
Z_{m}=w_{m}v_{m}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Z_{m}]=0,\mathbb{E}[Z_{m}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[w_{m}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[v_{m}^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[|Z_{m}|^{3}]=(\mathbb{E}[|w_{m}|^{3}])^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[w_{m}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[w_{m}^{4}]=3
(Cauchy-Schwartz inequality). Further,
Y=\sqrt{M}S(0)
.
Hence, we can bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S(0)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(|Y|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)

\displaystyle=F_{Y}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)-F_{Y}\left(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)-\Phi\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)+2\sup_{x}\left|F_{Y}(x)-\Phi(x)\right|

\displaystyle<\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\;+\;2\cdot\frac{1.56}{\sqrt{M}}<\frac{5}{\sqrt{M}}.

Combining all probability bounds, we have that, with probability at least
1-\frac{20}{M}-\frac{5}{\sqrt{M}}\geq 1-\frac{25}{\sqrt{M}}
, it holds that
P(0)\in[1.5,2.5],S(0)\in[-0.5,0.5]
and
S(0)^{2}>\frac{1}{M^{2}}
.
Under this condition, it immediately follows that
\alpha=\frac{P(0)^{2}}{4}-S(0)^{2}
is bounded as
\frac{1}{4}<\alpha<2
, while

\beta=\frac{P(0)-2\sqrt{\alpha}}{P(0)+2\sqrt{\alpha}}=\frac{4S(0)^{2}}{(P(0)+2\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}}>\frac{4S(0)^{2}}{36}>\frac{1}{9M^{2}}.

Using the lower bounds of
\alpha,\beta
in the expression for
\tilde{t}
, it follows that

Analysis for
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

To do so, let us first note that
\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})=\sqrt{M}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k};\mathbf{\theta})

f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q};\mathbf{\theta})

form an orthonormal set. Then, it is clear that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}
is an orthogonal square matrix and hence
\left\lVert{\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{i.}}\right\rVert=1
. Since
\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{W}_{i.}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert{\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{i.}}\right\rVert
by construction, we have
\left\lVert{\boldsymbol{W}_{i.}}\right\rVert\leq 1
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left(\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}})}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\left\lvert\phi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i.}{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\right\rvert}{{\left\lVert\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rVert+\delta^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{L_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right)

Similarly, we can bound
Term II
as:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left(\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{y})}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{L_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right).

Combining the bounds for
Term I
and
Term II
, we can now bound
\left\lvert\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert
as:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{L_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right)

and hence,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{QL_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right).

Assuming gradient descent with learning rate
\eta
we have:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Delta\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\eta\left\lvert\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{\eta QL_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right)

Therefore, after
t
iterations, the change in
\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}
can be bounded as:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}(t)-\boldsymbol{V}_{ij}(0)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\frac{4\eta QL_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\left\lVert\boldsymbol{V}(t)-\boldsymbol{V}(0)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq t\left(\frac{4\eta QL_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right).

(35)

Substituting this in (
A.8
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert s({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};t)-s({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};0)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq t\left(\frac{4\eta QL_{\phi}c_{in}}{\delta}\right)\left(\frac{4L_{\phi}{c_{in}}\sqrt{D}}{\sqrt{M}\delta}\right),

and hence from (
33
), with
\kappa=\frac{16\eta Q^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}c_{in}^{2}\sqrt{D}}{\delta^{2}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{C}_{V}(t)-\boldsymbol{C}_{V}(0)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle=\kappa\frac{t}{\sqrt{M}}

therefore concluding the proof.
∎

Note that since we are doing PCA,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{C}_{V}(t)-\boldsymbol{C}_{V}(0)\right\rVert_{F}

(36)

From Assumptions
4

5

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(0))\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{D}(L_{\phi}c_{\theta}+\left\lvert\phi(0)\right\rvert)(\sqrt{M}\log M)

(38)

A.8

\displaystyle\left\lVert\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t))-\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(0))\right\rVert_{F}

u({\boldsymbol{x}};\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{*},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(t))

\tilde{u}({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{W}^{*},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{C}}_{V}(0))

A.10
Proposition
18

Consider a neural network of the form (
2
) trained using a loss of the form (
1
) using gradient descent. Then for any input
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,

{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}}(0)={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}(0)\Rightarrow f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};{\mathbf{\theta}(t)})=f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}};{\mathbf{\theta}(t)}),\ \forall\;t\geq 0.

Proof.

We compare the outputs by analyzing the learning dynamics as given in Lemma
9
, we know that:

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\biggl[\sum_{p=1}^{Z}\biggl[K_{ip}({\boldsymbol{x}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}};\mathbf{\theta})g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})+K_{ip}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n})\biggr]\biggr]

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{n,q}\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}\biggl[\sum_{p=1}^{Z}\biggl[K_{jp}({\boldsymbol{x}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}};\mathbf{\theta})g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})+K_{jp}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q};\mathbf{\theta})g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n})\biggr]\biggr]

where
g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial f_{p}(x;\mathbf{\theta})}
. Note that irrespective of the similarity measure, the quantities
\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})}
,
g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q})
,
g_{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n,q},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n})
are independent of
i
and
j
and hence are identical in the dynamics of both
\dot{f}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
and
\dot{f}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
.

Therefore, we only have to characterize and compare the NTK for a neural network
f(\cdot)
of the form (
2
). Recall that the NTK can be defined as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y};\mathbf{\theta})=\underbrace{{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}}_{\text{Term I}}+\underbrace{{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}}_{\text{Term II}}

where
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
are vectorized versions of weights
\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
respectively. Now we can expand
Term I
:

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{V}_{qr}}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{W}_{qi}\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{V}_{q.}}{\boldsymbol{x}}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}

and therefore,

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}},\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,r}\boldsymbol{W}_{qi}\boldsymbol{W}_{qj}\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{V}_{q.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{V}{q.}}\boldsymbol{y}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}\boldsymbol{y}_{r}.

Similarly, for
Term II
:

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial{\boldsymbol{W}_{q^{\prime}r^{\prime}}}}}

\displaystyle=\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{i=r^{\prime}},

and,

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{j}(\boldsymbol{y};\mathbf{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{q^{\prime}}\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{i=j}

We are interested in the evolution of
f_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}};\mathbf{\theta}(t))
and
f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}};{\mathbf{\theta}(t)})
when
{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}}(0)={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}(0)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle K_{ip}({\boldsymbol{x}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}};\mathbf{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,r}\boldsymbol{W}_{qi}\boldsymbol{W}_{qp}\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{V}_{q.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{V}_{q.}}\boldsymbol{y}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}\boldsymbol{y}_{r}+\sum_{q^{\prime}}\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{i=p}

\displaystyle K_{jp}({\boldsymbol{x}},{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}};\mathbf{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,r}\boldsymbol{W}_{qj}\boldsymbol{W}_{qp}\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{W}_{q.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{W}_{q.}}\boldsymbol{y}){\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}\boldsymbol{y}_{r}+\sum_{q^{\prime}}\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\phi({\boldsymbol{V}_{q^{\prime}.}}\boldsymbol{y})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{j=p}

From these equations, it is clear that
\boldsymbol{K}_{ip}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})=\boldsymbol{K}_{jp}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};\mathbf{\theta})
whenever
{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}}={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}
. Therefore, if we prove that
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}(t)
for all
t
, then
\dot{f}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\dot{f}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
and hence
{f}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})={f}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
for all
t
.

Let us assume
{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}}(t)={\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}}(t)
at some time
t
. Then, after an iteration of gradient descent, with learning rate
\eta
, we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}{\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}}{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}{\frac{\partial l}{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}}{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)}}

Case 1: Dot product

We have:

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}{\frac{\partial\text{Tr}\left(\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\boldsymbol{W}(t)\boldsymbol{W}(t)^{\top}\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\right)}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\right)\boldsymbol{W}(t)

Therefore:

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\right)\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\phi^{\top}(\boldsymbol{V}(t){\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\right)\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)

Clearly, since
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)
,
{\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)}}={\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)}}
for all
n,q
and therefore
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t+1)
.

Case 2: Cosine similarity

From the proof of Lemma
11
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}^{\top}+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)}\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\left(\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}){\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert}+\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q}){\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}^{\top}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert}\right)\right]

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})(u_{k,q})_{i}+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})(u_{k})_{i}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)}\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\left(\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}){(u_{k})_{i}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert}+\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q}){(u_{k,q})_{i}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert}\right)\right]

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})(u_{k,q})_{j}+\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})(u_{k})_{j}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)}\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}}{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.\left(\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}){(u_{k})_{j}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert}+\frac{(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\right\rVert+\delta)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q}){(u_{k,q})_{j}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{k,q}\right\rVert}\right)\right]

Note that
(u_{k,q})_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}{\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}^{\top}}(t)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})
and
(u_{k,q})_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}{\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}^{\top}}(t)\phi(\boldsymbol{V}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,q})
. Therefore, since
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)
,
(u_{k,q})_{i}=(u_{k,q})_{j}
. Similarly,
(u_{k})_{i}=(u_{k})_{j}
. Since this is true for all
k,q
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t+1)
.

Therefore, for both dot product and cosine similarity based losses,
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)
implies
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t+1)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t+1)
. Note that
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(0)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(0)
is assumed to be true. Therefore, by the principle of induction,
\boldsymbol{W}_{.i}(t)=\boldsymbol{W}_{.j}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
. Hence,
{f}_{i}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})={f}_{j}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})
for all
t\geq 0
which concludes the proof.
∎
