Quantum models based on finite groups
Vladimir V Kornyak

Abstract
We consider a constructive modification of quantum-mechanical formalism. Replacement of a general unitary group by unitary representations of finite groups makes it possible to reproduce quantum formalism without loss of its empirical content. Since any linear representation of a finite group can be implemented as a subrepresentation of a permutation representation, quantum-mechanical problems can be formulated in terms of permutation groups. Reproducing quantum behavior in the framework of permutation representations of finite groups makes it possible to clarify the meaning of a number of physical concepts.



1
Introduction

Physics, being an empirical science, is insensitive to the replacement of infinite concepts in physical theories by finite ones.
The reason for such a replacement is that effective modeling is possible only if the problem is formulated in finite terms.
Moreover, it is preferable to formulate physical problems in finite terms from the very beginning, since infinities can lead to both descriptive losses and artifacts.
In this context, Paul Dirac made the often quoted statement that the most important challenge in physics is “
to get rid of infinity
”.
In this paper, we describe a constructive version of quantum formalism that does not involve any concepts associated with actual infinities.

The main part of the paper begins with a summary of the basic concepts of the standard quantum mechanics with emphasis on the aspects important for our purposes.

Further we describe a constructive modification of the quantum formalism.
We start with replacing a unitary group — a continuous group of symmetries of quantum states — by a finite group.
The natural consequence of this replacement is unitarity: any linear representation of a finite group is unitary.
Any finite group is naturally associated with some cyclotomic field — an extension of the rational numbers by roots of unity.
Generally, a cyclotomic field is a dense subfield of the field of complex numbers.
This can be regarded as an explanation of the presence of complex numbers in the quantum formalism.
Any linear representation of a finite group over a cyclotomic field associated with the group can be obtained from a permutation action of the group on vectors with natural components by projection into suitable invariant subspace.
This allows us to reproduce all the elements of quantum formalism in invariant subspaces of permutation representations.

We consider a model of quantum evolution inspired by the quantum Zeno effect
1
1

1

The
quantum Zeno effect
(also known as the
Turing paradox
) is an experimentally verifiable property of a quantum system to follow a prescribed evolution when a properly prepared sequence of observations is applied.

— the most convincing manifestation of the role of observation in the dynamics of quantum systems.
The model represents the quantum evolution as a sequence of observations with unitary transitions between them.
Standard quantum mechanics assumes a single deterministic unitary transition between observations.
We generalize this assumption. We treat a unitary transition as a kind of gauge connection — a way of identifying indistinguishable entities at different times.

A priori
, any unitary transformation can be used as a data identification rule.
So, we assume that all unitary transformations participate in transitions between observations with appropriate weights.
We call a unitary evolution
dominant
if it provides the maximum transition probability.
2
2

2

The principle of least action in physical theories actually implies the selection of dominant evolutions among all possible (“virtual”) evolutions.
The apparent determinism of these selected evolutions can be explained by the sharpness of their dominance.

The Monte Carlo simulation shows a sharp dominance of such evolutions over other evolutions.
We also present the Lagrangian of the continuum approximation of the model in order to illustrate the typical assumptions made in the transition to a continuous description.

Basic concepts of quantum mechanics

Here is a brief outline of the basic concepts of quantum mechanics.
We divide these concepts into three categories:

states
,
observations and measurements
, and
time evolution
.

2.1
Quantum states

Pure states:

A
pure quantum state
is a ray in a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
over the field of complex numbers
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{C}
.
A ray is an
equivalence class
of vectors
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
with respect to the
equivalence relation

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle\sim{}a\left|\psi\right\rangle,~a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.

We can reduce the equivalence classes by the
normalization
:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle\sim{}\e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\left|\psi\right\rangle,\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert=1,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

The phase “degree of freedom”
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\alpha
can be eliminated by transition to the rank one
projector

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{\psi}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|
,
which is a special case of a
density matrix
.

Mixed states:

A
mixed quantum state
is described by a
general density matrix

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
characterized by the properties:
(a)
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}={\rho}^{\dagger}
, (b)
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle\psi\left|{\rho}\right|\psi\right\rangle\geq 0
for any
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, (c)
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr{\rho}=1
.
The set of all density matrices will be denoted by
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
.

Any mixed state is a weighted mixture of pure states, that is, any density matrix can be represented in the form
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}=\sum_{k}{p_{k}}\Pi_{e_{k}}
,
where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{p_{k}\right\}
is a probability distribution in the ensemble of pure states
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{e_{k}}=\left|e_{k}\right\rangle\!\left\langle e_{k}\right|
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{e_{k}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
.

On the other hand, any mixed state can be obtained from a pure state in a larger Hilbert space by taking a partial trace.
This is called
‘purification’
and can be done as follows.
Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}\cong\diag\!\left(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\right)
be a density matrix in a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{X},\,\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}=n
.
If
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{Y}
is another
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}n
-dimensional Hilbert space, then
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}=\tr_{Y}\left(\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|\right)
,
where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|\psi\right\rangle=\sum_{k}\sqrt{p_{k}}\left|e_{k}\right\rangle\KroneckerProduct\left|f_{k}\right\rangle
is a pure state in the Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{X}\KroneckerProduct\mathcal{H}_{Y}
,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr_{Y}
denotes the trace over
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{Y}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{e_{k}\right\}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{f_{k}\right\}
are orthonormal bases in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{X}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}_{Y}
, respectively.

States of composite system:

The Hilbert space of a
composite system
is the tensor product of Hilbert spaces of the components:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}{\KroneckerProduct}\mathcal{H}_{2}{\KroneckerProduct}\cdots
.
The
states of composite system
are classified into two types:
separable
and
entangled
states.

The set of
separable
states,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{S}}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
, consists of the states
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}\in\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)

that can be represented as weighted sums of the tensor products of states of the constituents:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{{\rho}=\sum_{k}w_{k}\!\left({\rho}_{1}^{k}{{\KroneckerProduct}}{\rho}_{2}^{k}{{\KroneckerProduct}}\cdots\right)\!,\,w_{k}\geq 0,\,\sum_{k}w_{k}=1,\,{\rho}_{1}^{k}\in\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1}\right),\,{\rho}_{2}^{k}\in\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{2}\right),\,\ldots\,.}

The set of
entangled
states,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{E}}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
,
is by definition the complement of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{S}}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
in the set of all states:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{E}}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)=\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{S}}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
.

2.2
Observations and measurements

The terms ‘observation’ and ‘measurement’ are often regarded as being synonymous.
However, it makes sense to separate these concepts.
We treat observation as a more general concept which does not imply, in contrast to measurement, obtaining numerical information.

Observation

is the detection (“click of detector”) of a system, that is in the state
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
, in the subspace
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{S}\leq\mathcal{H}
.
The mathematical abstraction of the “detector in the subspace”
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{S}
of a Hilbert space is the operator of projection,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
, into this subspace.
The result of quantum observation is random and its statistics is described by a probability measure defined on subspaces of the Hilbert space.
Any such legitimate measure
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mu\!\left(\cdot\right)
must be additive on any set of mutually orthogonal subspaces of a Hilbert space: if, e.g.,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{A}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{B}

are mutually orthogonal subspaces, then
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mu\!\left(\spn\!\left(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\right)\right)=\mu\!\left(\mathcal{A}\right)+\mu\!\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
.
Gleason proved
[
1
]
that, excepting the case
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\dim\mathcal{H}=2
,
the only such measures have the form
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mu_{\rho}\!\left(\mathcal{S}\right)=\tr\!\left({\rho}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}\right)
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
is an arbitrary density matrix.
3
3

3

In fact, Gleason’s theorem establishes a one-to-one correspondence between measures and quantum states in the case
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\dim\mathcal{H}\neq 2
.
In the two-dimensional case (the case of a qubit) the set of admissible measures is “greater” than the set of states, but the measures that are not associated with quantum states are considered non-physical.

If, in particular,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
describes a pure state,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|
, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{S}
is one-dimensional,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{S}=\spn\!\left(\left|\varphi\right\rangle\right)
, we come to the familiar Born rule:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}\right)=\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{Born}}=\left|\left\langle\varphi\mid\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2}
.

Measurement

is a special case of observation,
when the partition of a Hilbert space into mutually orthogonal subspaces is specified by a Hermitian operator
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A
, called “observable”.
Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{e_{k}\right\}
be an
orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A
and let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{a_{k}\right\}
be the
spectrum
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A
.
In the basis
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{e_{k}\right\}
the operator is written as
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A=\sum_{k}a_{k}\Pi_{e_{k}}
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{e_{k}}=\left|e_{k}\right\rangle\!\left\langle e_{k}\right|
.
“Click of the detector”
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{e_{k}}
is interpreted as that the
eigenvalue

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is the result of the measurement.
The mean for multiple measurements tends to the
expectation value
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A
in the state
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\langle{A}\right\rangle_{\!{\rho}}=\tr\!\left({\rho}{A}\right)
.

Note that
observation
can formally be treated as a
measurement
if we regard the projector
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}
as an observable
with eigenvalues belonging to the Boolean domain
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{0,1\right\}
(or
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{\mathrm{no,yes}\right\}
).

2.3
Time evolution

Observation and measurement are timeless concepts in the standard quantum formalism.
Time appears as a parameter in describing the unitary transformation of data between observations.
The unitary evolution of the density matrix has the form

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{{\rho}_{t^{\prime}}=U_{t^{\prime}t}{\rho}_{t}U_{t^{\prime}t}^{\dagger},}

(1)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{t}
is the state
after
observation (sometimes called in this context “
preparation
”) at the time
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t
,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{t^{\prime}}
is the state
before
observation at the time
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t^{\prime}
, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U_{t^{\prime}t}
is the unitary transition between the observations.
In standard quantum formalism, time is considered as a continuous parameter, and relation (
1
) becomes the
von Neumann equation
in the infinitesimal limit.
The evolution of a pure state can be written as

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left|\psi_{t^{\prime}}\right\rangle=U_{t^{\prime}t}\left|\psi_{t}\right\rangle,}

and the corresponding infinitesimal limit is the
Schrödinger equation
.
The operator of unitary evolution can be represented as

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{U_{t^{\prime}t}=\e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}{H}\Delta{t}},}

(2)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H
is a Hermitian operator called the
Hamiltonian
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{t}=t^{\prime}-t
.
The concept of a Hamiltonian is especially useful in the case of continuous time.
In the discrete time
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{t}
is a natural number,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{t}\in\mathbb{N}
, and instead of (
2
) we can write

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{U_{t^{\prime}t}=U_{\!1}^{\Delta{t}},}

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U_{\!1}
is the operator of evolution for a unit time step.

To emphasize the role of observation in quantum physics, we accentuate that unitary evolution is simply a change of coordinates in Hilbert space, and it alone is insufficient to describe observable physical phenomena.

2.4
Emergence of geometry in large Hilbert space due to entanglement

Quantum-mechanical theory does not need a geometric space as a fundamental concept — everything can be formulated using only the Hilbert space formalism.
In this view, the observed geometry must emerge as an approximation.
The currently popular idea
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
of the emergence of geometry within a Hilbert space is based on the notion of entanglement.
Briefly, the scheme of extracting geometric manifold from the entanglement structure of a quantum state
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
in a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
is as follows:

•

The Hilbert space decomposes into a large number of tensor factors:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}=\KroneckerProduct_{x}\mathcal{H}_{x},~x\in{X}
.
Each factor is treated as a point (or bulk) of geometric space to be built.
A graph
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G
— called
tensor network
— with vertices
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}x\in{X}
and edges
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left(x,y\right)\in{}X\times{}X
is introduced.

•

The edges of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G
are assigned
weights
based on a
measure of entanglement
, a function that vanishes on separable states and is positive on entangled states.
A typical such measure is the
mutual information

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{I}\!\left({\rho}_{xy}\right)=S\!\left({\rho}_{x}\right)+S\!\left({\rho}_{y}\right)-S\!\left({\rho}_{xy}\right)
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{x}
denotes the result of taking traces of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}
over all tensor factors excepting the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}x
-th (and similarly for
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{y}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{xy}
);
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}S\!\left(a\right)=-\tr\left(a\log{a}\right)
is the
von Neumann entropy
.
The graph
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G
is supplied with a metric derived from the weights of the edges.

•

Finally, the graph
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}G
is approximately isometrically embedded in a smooth metric manifold of as small as possible dimension using algorithms like
multidimensional scaling
(MDS).

3.1
Losses due to continuum and differential calculus

Differential calculus — including differential equations, differential geometry, etc. — underlies mathematical methods in physics.
The applicability of differential calculus is based on the assumption that any relevant function can be approximated by linear relations on a small scale.
This assumption simplifies many problems in physics and mathematics, but at the cost of loss of completeness.

As an example, consider the problem of classifying simple groups.
The concept of a group is an abstraction of the properties of
permutations
(also called
one-to-one mappings
or
bijections

Namely, an abstract group is a set with an
associative
operation, an
identity
element, and an
invertibility
for each element.
Additional assumptions allow to make the concept of a group more meaningful.
The most natural of these assumptions is: (a)
the group is finite

It is clear that empirical physics is insensitive to this assumption: ultimately, any empirical description is reduced to a finite set of data.
A simple complement to assumption (a) —
the group is infinite
— is of little use for a number of reasons, e.g.,
there are one-to-one mappings between infinite sets and their proper subsets.
Thus, more restrictive assumptions are used, the most important of which is: (b)
the group is a differentiable manifold
— such a group is called
Lie group

Assumption (b) imposes severe constraints on possible physical models.

3.2
Replacing unitary group by finite group

The main non-constructive element of the standard quantum formalism is the unitary group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(n\right)
, a set of cardinality of the continuum.

Formally, the group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(n\right)
can be replaced by some finite group which is empirically equivalent to
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(n\right)
as follows.
From the theory of quantum computing it is known that
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(n\right)
contains a dense finitely generated — and, hence, countable — matrix subgroup
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}_{*}\!\left(n\right)
.
The group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}_{*}\!\left(n\right)
is
residually finite

[
9
]
, i.e. it has a reach set of non-trivial homomorphisms to finite groups
[
10
]
.

In essence, instead of deriving a finite group from an infinite group, it is more natural to assume that the fundamental symmetries are presented by finite groups,
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(n\right)
’s are just continuum approximations of their unitary representations.

The following properties of finite groups are important for our purposes:

•

any finite group is a subgroup of a
symmetric group
,

•

any linear representation of a finite group is
unitary
,

•

any linear representation is subrepresentation of some
permutation representation
.

3.3
“Physical” numbers and finite groups

The basic number system in quantum formalism is the complex field
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{C}
.
This non-constructive field can be obtained as a metric completion of many algebraic extensions of rational numbers.
We consider here constructive numbers that are closely related to finite groups and are based on two primitives with a clear intuitive meaning:

1.

natural numbers
(“counters”):
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}=\left\{0,1,\ldots\right\}
;

2.

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k

roots of unity
5
5

5

There are
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
different
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k

A
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{r}_{k}^{m}=1

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}m\equiv 0\mod{k}
.

(“algebraic form of the idea of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
-periodicity”):
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{r}_{k}\mid\mathsf{r}_{k}^{k}=1
.

These basic concepts are sufficient to represent all physically meaningful numbers.

We start by introducing
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{k}\right]
, the extension of the
semiring

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}
by primitive
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
th root of unity.
The extension
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{k}\right]
is a
ring
if
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k\geq 2
.
This construction allows, in particular, to add
negative numbers
to the naturals —
the ring of integer numbers is the extension of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}
by the primitive square root of unity:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{2}\right]
.
Further, by a standard mathematical procedure (applicable to any
integral domain
— a commutative ring without zero divisors), we construct the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
th
cyclotomic field
as the
fraction field
of the ring
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{k}\right]
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)=\mathrm{Frac}\!\left(\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{k}\right]\right)
.
For
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k=2
we have the field of rational numbers:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}=\mathrm{Frac}\!\left(\mathbb{N}\!\left[\mathsf{r}_{2}\right]\right)
.
If
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k\geq 3
, then the field
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)
is a
dense subfield
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{C}
, i.e. (constructive) cyclotomic fields are empirically indistinguishable from the (non-constructive) complex field.

The importance of cyclotomic numbers for constructive quantum mechanics is explained by the following.
Let us recall some terms.
The
exponent
of a group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
is the least common multiple of the orders of its elements.
A
splitting field
for a group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
is a field that allows to split completely any linear representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
into irreducible components.
A
minimal splitting field
is a splitting field that does not contain proper splitting subfields.
Although minimal splitting field for a given group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
may be non-unique, any minimal splitting field is a subfield of some cyclotomic field
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)
,
where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
is a divisor of the exponent of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
.
If we want to minimize computations, we can select the field
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)
with the smallest
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
among cyclotomic splitting fields.
Thus, to work with any representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
it is sufficient to use the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
th cyclotomic field, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
is determined by the structure of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
.

An important field associated with a group is a so-called ‘sufficiently large field’.
A
sufficiently large field
for
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
is defined as a field that contains all the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}m
th roots of unity, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}m
is the exponent of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
.
In particular, the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}m
th cyclotomic field
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{m}\right)
is a sufficiently large field.
A sufficiently large field for
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
is a splitting field for
every subgroup
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
.
This fact is useful in considering a symmetry breaking, which is actually a transition to a subgroup.

3.4
Constructive representations of a finite group

Decomposition of the natural representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{{\mathsf{N}}}

Let a group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega,~\left|\Omega\right|=\mathsf{N}

If we assume that the elements of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega

then collections of these entities can be described as elements of the module
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H=\mathbb{N}^{\mathsf{N}}
with the basis
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}

The decomposition of the action of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H

In order for the decomposition to be complete, it is necessary to extend the semiring
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{N}
to a splitting field,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
is a suitable divisor of the exponent of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
.
With such an extension of the scalars, the module
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H
is transformed into a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
over
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\mathsf{r}_{k}\right)
.
This construction, with a suitable choice of the permutation domain
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega

allows us to obtain
any representation
of the group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
in some invariant subspace of the Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
.
We obtain “quantum mechanics” within an invariant subspace if, in addition to unitary evolutions, projective measurements are also restricted by this subspace.

The above is illustrated in figure

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{{\mathsf{N}}}
on the set

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Omega=\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\mathsf{N}}\right\}
.
The
natural
representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{{\mathsf{N}}}
decomposes into two irreducible components: one-dimensional

and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left(\mathsf{N}-1\right)
-dimensional
standard

The spaces of these representations have, respectively, the following canonical bases:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{\mathsf{N}}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}e_{1}-e_{2},e_{2}-e_{3},\ldots,e_{\mathsf{N}-1}-e_{\mathsf{N}}

Note, that any symmetric group is a so-called

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathbb{Q}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{T}=\left[t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1},t_{k},\ldots,t_{\mathrm{n}}\right].}

(3)

The simplest assumption is that
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{T}
is a subsequence of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{T}
.
A more realistic model (which we will not develop here) could be a sequence of distributions
6

6

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}10^{26}
Planck units.

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{K_{\sigma}\!\left(\tau-t_{k}\right)=\frac{\left(2\sigma\right)!}{4^{\sigma}\left(\sigma-t_{k}+\tau\right)!\left(\sigma+t_{k}-\tau\right)!},\hskip 10.0ptt_{k}-\sigma\leq\tau\leq{t_{k}}+\sigma.}

4.2
Model of quantum evolution

The data of the model include the sequence of the length
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{n}+1
for states

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left[{\rho}_{0},{\rho}_{1},\ldots,{\rho}_{k-1},{\rho}_{k},\ldots,{\rho}_{\mathrm{n}}\right]}

(4)

and the sequence of the length
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{n}
for unitary transitions between observations

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\left[U_{1},\ldots,U_{k},\ldots,U_{\mathrm{n}}\right].}

(5)

Standard quantum mechanics presupposes a single unitary evolution,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U_{k}
, between observations at times
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t_{k-1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t_{k}
.
The
single-step transition probability
takes the form

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}=\tr\!\left(U_{k}{\rho}_{k-1}U_{k}^{\dagger}{\rho}_{k}\right).}

The evolution can be expressed in terms of the Hamiltonian
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H_{k}
:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U_{k}=\e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}{H_{k}}\left(t_{k}-t_{k-1}\right)}
.
In physical theories, Hamiltonians are usually derived from
the principle of least action
.
Like any extremal principle, it implies the selection of a small subset of the dominant elements in a large set of candidates.
Thus it is natural to assume that any unitary evolution takes part in the transition between observations with appropriate weight, but only the dominant evolutions are manifested in observations.
Therefore, in our model, we use the following modification of the single-step transition probability

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}=\sum_{g\in\mathsf{G}}w_{kg}\tr\!\left(\mathsf{U}\!\left(g\right){\rho}_{k-1}\mathsf{U}\!\left(g^{-1}\right){\rho}_{k}\right),}

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
is a finite group,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}
is a unitary representation of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
,

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}w_{kg}
is the weight of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}g\in\mathsf{G}
at
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}k
th transition:
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}w_{kg}\geq 0,~\sum_{g\in\mathsf{G}}w_{kg}=1
.

The
single-step entropy
is defined as

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Delta\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\!=-\log\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}.}

(6)

Continuum approximation of (
6
) leads to the
Lagrangian

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{L}
.

The probability of the whole trajectory is

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{0\rightarrow{\mathrm{n}}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\mathrm{n}}\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}.}

(7)

Taking the logarithm of (
7
), we arrive at the
entropy of trajectory

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{{0}\rightarrow{\mathrm{n}}}\!=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathrm{n}}\Delta\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{k},}

the continuum approximation of which is the
action

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{S}=\int\!\mathcal{L}dt
.

4.3
Continuum approximation of discrete model

To build a continuum approximation, we immerse sequence of observation times (
3
) in the continuous time interval
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left[t_{0},t_{\mathrm{n}}\right]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
replace sequences (
4
) and (
5
) with the continuous differentiable functions
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}\!\left(t\right)
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U\!\left(t\right)
,
and go to the limit
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{n}\rightarrow\infty,~\Delta{t}_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}\rightarrow 0
.
Using the Taylor expansion of
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}\!\left(t\right)
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U\!\left(t\right)
at the point
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}t_{k-1}
, we can compute the approximation for the single-step transition probability:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}\approx{}P_{k}^{(0)}-P_{k}^{(1)}\Delta{t}_{k}-P_{k}^{(2)}\Delta{t}_{k}^{2}-\cdots\,.}

We see that product (
7
) can have a nonzero limit only if
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}P_{k}^{(0)}=1
for all but a finite number of factors.
This is an artificial restriction imposed by the introduction of infinity.
The term
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}P_{k}^{(0)}
has the meaning of the “zero-step transition probability”.
Its value is
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}P_{k}^{(0)}=\tr\!\left({\rho}_{k-1}^{2}\right)
.
Since
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)=1
only for pure states,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}=\left|\psi\right\rangle\!\left\langle\psi\right|
,
we can not construct a reasonable continuum approximation in the case of general mixed states for which
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\tr\!\left({\rho}^{2}\right)<1
.
Calculation for the case of a pure state shows that

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}\approx{}1-P_{k}^{(2)}\Delta{t}_{k}^{2}-\cdots\,,}

and the single-step entropy has the following approximation

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\Delta\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\!=-\log\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{k}\approx{}P_{k}^{(2)}\Delta{t}_{k}^{2}+\cdots\,.}

(8)

The leading-order (i.e. the second-order) term in expansion (
8
) defines a Lagrangian.
For further calculations, we use the following simplifying assumptions:

•

Assuming that unitary matrix
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U
belongs to a unitary representation of a Lie group, we use the Lie algebra approximation,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U\approx\idmat+\mathrm{i}{}A
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A
is a Hermitian matrix.
Thus, we replace the function
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U\!\left(t\right)
by a continuous function
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}A\!\left(t\right)
, which can be regarded as a gauge field (gauge connection) in the
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left(0+1\right)
-dimensional “space-time”.

•

We introduce derivatives
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\,\dot{A}\!\left(t\right)
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\,\dot{\psi}\!\left(t\right)
as the coefficients in the linear approximations
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{A}_{k}\approx\,\dot{A}\Delta{t}_{k}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{\psi}_{k}\approx\,\dot{\psi}\Delta{t}_{k}
,
where
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{A}_{k}=A_{k}-A_{k-1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta{\psi}_{k}=\psi_{k}-\psi_{k-1}
.
The time derivative
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\,\dot{A}\!\left(t\right)
is an analog of the gauge curvature in
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left(0+1\right)
dimensions.

Applying the above assumptions and approximations to single-step entropy (
8
) and taking the infinitesimal limit, we obtain the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underbrace{\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}^{2}\right|\psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}\right|\psi\right\rangle^{2}\!}_{\text{\small\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{}dispersion of $\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\,\dot{A}$ in state $\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\psi$}}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-\mathrm{i}\bigg(\left\langle\,\dot{\psi}\left|\,\dot{A}\right|\psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}\right|\,\dot{\psi}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}\right|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\mid\,\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\bigg)-\left\langle\psi\!\mid\!\,\dot{\psi}\right\rangle^{2}.

(9)

Note that the first two terms of (
9
) form the
dispersion
of the “gauge curvature” in the state
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\psi
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\!\left(\Delta_{\psi}\!\,\dot{A}\right)^{2}=\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}^{2}\right|\psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi\left|\,\dot{A}\right|\psi\right\rangle^{2}
,
where the value
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\Delta_{\psi}\!\,\dot{A}
is called the
standard deviation
.

4.4
Dominant evolutions

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}
be a unitary representation of a group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{G}
in a Hilbert space
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathcal{H}
.
For an arbitrary pair of quantum states,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{1},{\rho}_{2}\in\mathcal{D}\!\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
, we define an element
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}g_{*}\in\mathsf{G}
by

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{g_{*}=\argmax\limits_{g\in\mathsf{G}}\,\tr\!\left(\mathsf{U}\!\left(g\right){\rho}_{1}\mathsf{U}\!\left(g^{-1}\right){\rho}_{2}\right).}

The operator
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{U}\!\left(g_{*}\right)
maximizes the probability associated with unitary transition between the states
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}{\rho}_{2}
.
We call such operators
dominant evolutions
.

In the case of natural and standard representations of symmetric groups, the dominant evolutions can be found by a simple algorithm.
Namely, the dominant evolutions from the group
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{S}_{\mathsf{N}}
between the pure states represented by the vectors from the module
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H=\mathbb{N}^{\mathsf{N}}
can be computed as follows.
Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|n\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}\\
\vdots\\
n_{\mathsf{N}}\end{pmatrix},~\left|m\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
\vdots\\
m_{\mathsf{N}}\end{pmatrix},~\left|1\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}
be
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{N}
-dimensional vectors with natural components.
The Born probabilities for the pair
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|n\right\rangle
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left|m\right\rangle
are

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{nat}}\left(\left|n\right\rangle\!,\left|m\right\rangle\right)=\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\left\langle n\mid m\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\langle n\mid n\right\rangle\left\langle m\mid m\right\rangle}}

and

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{std}}\left(\left|n\right\rangle\!,\left|m\right\rangle\right)=\frac{\left(\left\langle n\mid m\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\mathsf{N}}\left\langle n\mid\!1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\!\mid m\right\rangle\right)^{2}}{\left(\left\langle n\mid n\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\mathsf{N}}\left\langle n\mid\!1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\!\mid n\right\rangle\right)\left(\left\langle m\mid m\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\mathsf{N}}\left\langle m\mid\!1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\!\mid m\right\rangle\right)}}

(10)

for the natural and standard representations, respectively.

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}R_{a}

It is not hard to show that the unitary operator

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{nat}}\!\left(U\!\left|n\right\rangle\!,\left|m\right\rangle\right)

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}R_{n}

and

identically

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{std}}\!\left(U\!\left|n\right\rangle\!,\left|m\right\rangle\right)

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U=R_{m}^{-1}R_{n}

4.5
Energy of permutation

Planck’s formula,
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}E={h}\boldsymbol{\nu}

This relation is reproduced by the quantum-mechanical definition of energy as an eigenvalue of a Hamiltonian.
The Hamiltonian associated with a unitary transformation
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}U
can be written as
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}H={\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\ln{}U

Consider the energy spectrum of a unitary operator defined by a permutation.
Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}p

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}^{\mathit{m}_{1}}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{k}^{\mathit{m}_{k}}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{K}^{\mathit{m}_{K}}}\right\}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\mathsf{r}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{}\boldsymbol{\ell}

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{F^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}C_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}F_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\

We shall call the least nonzero energy of a permutation the
base energy
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\max\left(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{K}\right)}\,.}

Simulation shows that the base
(“ground state”, “zero-point”, “vacuum”)
energy is statistically more significant than other energy levels.

Summary

1.

The general scheme of quantum mechanics can be completely reproduced in terms of projections of permutation representations of finite groups into invariant subspaces.

2.

Quantum randomness is a consequence of the fundamental impossibility of tracing the individuality of indistinguishable entities in their evolution:
the only available objective information about such entities is reduced to the invariants of their symmetry group,
so information about individual events can only be probabilistic.

3.

The native number systems for quantum formalism are cyclotomic fields, and the field of complex numbers is just a non-constructive metric completion of cyclotomic fields.

4.

Observable behavior of quantum system is determined by the dominants among all possible evolutions.

5.

The principle of least action is a continuum approximation of the principle of selection of the most probable trajectories.
