1.
Introduction

In 2002, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy
[
1
]
introduced the combinatorial objects which they called
partitions with designated summands
. These are built by taking unrestricted integer partitions and designating exactly one of each occurrence of a part. For example, there are 10 partitions with designated summands of weight 4:

4^{\prime},\ \ \ 3^{\prime}+1^{\prime},\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+2,\ \ \ 2+2^{\prime},\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 2^{\prime}+1+1^{\prime}

1^{\prime}+1+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1^{\prime}+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1+1^{\prime}

Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy denoted the number of partitions with designated summands of weight
n
by the function
PD(n).
Hence, using this notation and the example above, we know
PD(4)=10.

In the same paper, Andrews, Lewis, and Lovejoy
[
1
]
also considered the restricted partitions with designated summands wherein all parts must be odd, and they denoted the corresponding enumeration function by
PDO(n).
Thus, from the example above, we see that
PDO(4)=5,
where we have counted the following five objects:

3^{\prime}+1^{\prime},\ \ \ 1^{\prime}+1+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1^{\prime}+1+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1^{\prime}+1,\ \ \ 1+1+1+1^{\prime}

Since then, numerous authors have studied arithmetic properties of
PD(n)
as well as the family of functions
PD_{k}(n)
which denotes the number of
k
–regular partitions of
n
with designated summands. (Recall that a partition is called
k
–regular
if none of the parts of the partition are divisible by
k.
) Note that
PDO(n)=PD_{2}(n)
in this notation.

Beginning with
[
1
]
, a wide variety of Ramanujan–like congruences have been proven for
PD(n)
and
PD_{k}(n)
for infinitely many values of
k
. See
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
13
,
15
]
for such work.

Recently, Herden, et. al.
[
11
]
proved a number of arithmetic properties satisfied by the functions
PD_{2^{\ell}}(n)
and
PD_{3^{\ell}}(n)
for a variety of values of
\ell
. At the end of their paper, they shared the following conjecture which will serve as the starting point for the work in this paper:

Conjecture 1.1
.

For
n\geq 0,
we have

\displaystyle PD_{2}(16n+12)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PD_{2}(24n+20)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PD_{2}(25n+5)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PD_{2}(32n+24)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PD_{2}(48n+26)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4}

It is intriguing to note that the fourth arithmetic progression which appears above,
32n+24
, equals
2(16n+12)
, i.e.,
32n+24
is twice the first arithmetic progression above. This is a hint of something much larger; indeed, these two congruences are true, and they belong to an easily–described infinite family of congruences modulo 4:

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
\alpha\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(2^{\alpha}(4n+3))\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

In fact, there is an additional family of congruences of a similar form modulo 8 which we will also prove below.

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
\alpha\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(2^{\alpha}(8n+7))\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

Our main goal in this paper is to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. All of the proof techniques used herein are elementary, relying on classical
q
–series identites and generating function manipulations.

2.
Preliminaries

As noted in
[
1
]
, the generating function for
PD_{2}(n)
is given by

(1)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}f_{3}f_{12}}

where
f_{r}=(1-q^{r})(1-q^{2r})(1-q^{3r})(1-q^{4r})\dots
is the usual
q
–Pochhammer symbol.

In order to prove our results, we will require several elementary generating function dissection tools. For the most part, these will involve well–known 2–dissection results that will allow us to manipulate (
1
) to our advantage. We share these results here.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f_{1}^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}^{14}}{f_{2}^{14}f_{8}^{4}}+4q\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{8}^{4}}{f_{2}^{10}}.

Proof.

This identity can be found in the work of Brietzke, da Silva, and Sellers
[
4
, Equation (18)]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

f_{1}^{2}=\frac{f_{2}f_{8}^{5}}{f_{4}^{2}f_{16}^{2}}-2q\frac{f_{2}f_{16}^{2}}{f_{8}}.

Proof.

See the work of da Silva and Sellers
[
9
, Equation (12), Lemma 1]
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}+q\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}},

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}f_{24}^{2}}-q\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{2}}.

Proof.

Both of these identities can be found in Hirschhorn
[
12
]
; the first is Equation (30.12.3) while the second is Equation (30.12.1).
∎

Finally, we state without proof the well–known result regarding congruences of the functions
f_{k}
modulo powers of a prime (which, in essence, relies on the divisibility properties of binomial coefficients).

Lemma 2.4
.

For any prime
p,
any
j\geq 1,
and any
m\geq 1,

f_{m}^{p^{j}k}\equiv f_{pm}^{p^{j-1}k}\pmod{p^{j}}
.

We close this section by listing a number of dissection results for the generating function for
PD_{2}
which are easily found via elementary generating function dissections.

Theorem 2.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}f_{12}^{2}},\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{2}}.

Proof.

See
[
1
, Theorem 21]
.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{8}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}+q\frac{f_{2}^{12}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{1}^{8}f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{4}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{12}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}^{8}f_{3}^{2}f_{4}^{4}},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}^{6}f_{3}^{2}},\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+3)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{3}^{2}}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.3
and Theorem
2.5
, we have

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}f_{12}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{3}}\right)^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}+q\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\right)^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{10}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{8}f_{24}^{4}}+2q\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}+q^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{10}f_{24}^{4}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{6}^{4}f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{2}}\right)\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}.

From (
2
), we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{8}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{4}}+q^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{12}f_{24}^{4}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}

or

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{8}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}+q\frac{f_{2}^{12}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{1}^{8}f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{4}}.

Also from (
2
), we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2q\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{12}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2q\frac{f_{4}^{6}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{2}}

after simplification. And this implies

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}^{6}f_{3}^{2}}

which also appears in
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
. Indeed, the generating function results for
PD_{2}(4n+1)
and
PD_{2}(4n+3)
follow in similar fashion and can also be found in
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
.
∎

With these tools in hand, we proceed to our elementary proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

3.
An Infinite Family of Congruences Modulo 4

Our focus in this section is on Theorem
1.2
. In order to prove this result, we begin with several generating function dissections for
PD_{2}(n)
modulo 4.
We begin by proving the 2-dissection for
PD_{2}(n)
modulo 4.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}}\pmod{4},\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle f_{6}^{2}\pmod{4}.

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
2.4
and Theorem
2.5
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}f_{12}^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{4}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}f_{12}^{2}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}}\pmod{4}.

Again using Lemma
2.4
, Theorem
2.5
, and elementary simplifications, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{6}^{4}}{f_{6}^{2}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{6}^{2}.

∎

Thanks to the fact that
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n+1)q^{n}\equiv f_{6}^{2}\pmod{4}
, which is a function of
q^{6}
, we immediately have the following corollary:

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
n\geq 0,

\displaystyle PD_{2}(4n+3)

\displaystyle\equiv 0

\displaystyle\pmod{4},

\displaystyle PD_{2}(6n+3)

\displaystyle\equiv 0

\displaystyle\pmod{4},\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle PD_{2}(6n+5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0

\displaystyle\pmod{4}.

Proof.

We note that, once expanded, the power series representation of
f_{6}^{2}
must only contain powers of
q
whose exponents are multiples of 6. Therefore, we immediately know that
PD_{2}(2(2n+1)+1),

PD_{2}(2(3n+1)+1),
and
PD_{2}(2(3n+2)+1)
must all be congruent to 0
\pmod{4}.

∎

We note that the above congruences, along with several others, appear in
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
.

We next transition to the necessary 4–dissections of the generating function for
PD_{2}(n)
modulo 4.

Theorem 3.3
.

We have

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{6}}\right)^{2}+qf_{12}^{2}\pmod{4}\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv 2f_{2}^{3}f_{6}\pmod{4}.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{2}}{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{2}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{4}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{3}}\right)^{2}.

Thus, thanks to Lemma
2.3
, we know

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle f_{4}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{f_{1}f_{3}}\right)^{2}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{4}^{2}\left(\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}+q\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{4}^{2}\left(\frac{f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{10}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{8}f_{24}^{4}}+q^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{10}f_{24}^{4}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{6}^{4}f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{2}}+2q\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{10}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{8}f_{24}^{4}}+q^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{12}f_{24}^{4}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{6}^{4}f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{2}}+2q\frac{f_{4}^{6}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{6}}.

Collecting those terms above which are even functions of
q
and replacing
q^{2}
by
q
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{10}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{3}^{8}f_{12}^{4}}+q\frac{f_{2}^{12}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{1}^{8}f_{3}^{4}f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{8}f_{6}^{10}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}^{4}f_{6}^{8}}+q\frac{f_{4}^{6}f_{6}^{8}}{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{2}f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{f_{2}^{6}}{f_{6}^{2}}+qf_{6}^{4}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{6}}\right)^{2}+qf_{12}^{2}\pmod{4}

after simplification. Returning to (
4
) and focusing on the odd powers of
q
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{2n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2q\frac{f_{4}^{6}f_{12}^{4}}{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{6}}\pmod{4}

which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}^{6}f_{3}^{6}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{2}^{3}f_{6}^{3}}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2f_{2}^{3}f_{6}\pmod{4}

using Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Thanks to Theorem
3.3
, we have the following corollaries:

Corollary 3.4
.

For all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(8n+6)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.3
we know

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+2)q^{n}\equiv 2f_{2}^{3}f_{6}\pmod{4}

where
f_{2}^{3}f_{6}
is an even function of
q.
Therefore, for all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(4(2n+1)+2)=PD_{2}(8n+6)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

∎

Note that the result in Corollary
3.4
also appears in
[
3
, Theorem 1.5]
.

Corollary 3.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(8n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{1}^{3}}{f_{3}}\right)^{2}\pmod{4}\textrm{\ \ \ and}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(8n+4)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle f_{6}^{2}\pmod{4}.

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
3.3

we know

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}\equiv\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{6}}\right)^{2}+qf_{12}^{2}\pmod{4}

and the right–hand side above is already 2–dissected. Therefore, the corollary follows by simply splitting the result for the generating function congruence for
PD_{2}(4n)
into its even and odd parts and replacing
q^{2}
by
q
.
∎

Corollary
3.5
also leads us to our next Ramanujan–like divisibility property.

Corollary 3.6
.

For all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(16n+12)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Proof.

From Corollary
3.5
, we see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(8n+4)q^{n}\equiv f_{6}^{2}\pmod{4}.

Because the right–hand side of this congruence is an even function of
q
, we know that, for all
n\geq 0
,

PD_{2}(8(2n+1)+4)=PD_{2}(16n+12)\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

∎

Note that the congruence in Corollary
3.6
was one of the congruences conjectured in
[
11
]
.

We now need only one additional tool in order to complete our proof of Theorem
1.2
. The following theorem provides an “internal congruence” modulo 4 which is satisfied by
PD_{2}(n)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

For all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(4n)\equiv PD_{2}(n)\pmod{4}.

Proof.

From (
3
) above, we know that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n)q^{n}\equiv\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{6}}\right)^{2}+qf_{12}^{2}\pmod{4}.

From
[
11
, Lemma 4]
, we also know

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(n)q^{n}\equiv\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{6}}\right)^{2}+qf_{12}^{2}\pmod{4}.

The result follows.
∎

We now have all of the tools needed to provide a proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
1.2
) Note that the
\alpha=0
case is proven in Corollary
3.2
, while the
\alpha=1
case appears in Corollary
3.4
. We then see that, thanks to Theorem
3.7
, we have (mod 4)

0\equiv PD_{2}(4n+3)\equiv PD_{2}(4(4n+3))\equiv PD_{2}(4^{2}(4n+3))\equiv PD_{2}(4^{3}(4n+3))\dots

which takes care of all of the cases with an even power of
\alpha.
For the cases where
\alpha
is odd, we note that
8n+6=2(4n+3),
and this then gives us (mod 4)

0\equiv PD_{2}(2(4n+3))\equiv PD_{2}(4\cdot 2(4n+3))\equiv PD_{2}(4^{2}\cdot 2(4n+3))\equiv PD_{2}(4^{3}\cdot 2(4n+3))\dots

∎

We now have a proof of an infinite family of congruences modulo 4 satisfied by
PD_{2}
which encompasses two of the conjectured congruences in
[
11
]
.

We close this section by noting that, thanks to Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
3.7
, we now know the following:

Theorem 3.8
.

For all
\alpha\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(4^{\alpha}(6n+5))\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

This is another satisfying infinite family of congruences modulo 4, and we note that the
\alpha=1
case of this family is the second conjecture that appeared in the list at the end of
[
11
]
.

4.
An Infinite Family of Congruences Modulo 8

We now transition to the proof of a related family of Ramanujan–like congruences satisfied by
PD_{2},
this time modulo 8, which was stated in Theorem
1.3
. As a reminder, that theorem states that, for all
\alpha\geq 0
and all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(2^{\alpha}(8n+7))\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

We will prove Theorem
1.3
in an analogous manner to that used above; namely, we will directly prove a few “base” cases of the congruence, and then we will prove an internal congruence modulo 8 which will serve as the mechanism by which we can allow
\alpha
to grow arbitrarily large.

To begin this process, we prove the
\alpha=0
case of the above theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
n\geq 0,

PD_{2}(8n+7)\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
2.5
as well as Lemma
2.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(2n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f_{1}^{4}}\left(\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{f_{4}^{14}}{f_{2}^{14}f_{8}^{4}}+4q\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{8}^{4}}{f_{2}^{10}}\right)\left(\frac{f_{2}^{6}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{2}}\right).

Thus, we see that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+3)q^{2n+1}=4q\frac{f_{8}^{4}f_{12}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}

or

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(4n+3)q^{n}=4\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{1}^{4}f_{3}^{2}}\equiv 4\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}\pmod{8}.

Because the function

is an even function of

, we immediately conclude that, for all

∎

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{f_{4}^{14}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{8}^{4}}\right)\left(\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}+q\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\right)^{2}\pmod{8}.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(8n+6)q^{2n+1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{f_{4}^{14}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{8}f_{8}^{4}}\right)\left(2q\frac{f_{8}^{2}f_{12}^{5}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}f_{6}^{4}f_{24}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{5}f_{24}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{6}^{2}f_{8}^{2}f_{12}}\right)\pmod{8}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}PD_{2}(8n+6)q^{n}

\displaystyle 4\frac{f_{2}^{18}f_{6}^{4}}{f_{1}^{14}f_{3}^{4}f_{4}^{4}}\pmod{8}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle 4\frac{f_{2}^{11}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{4}^{4}}\pmod{8}.

Since the last expression above is an even function of
q
, we immediately know that, for all
n\geq 0
,

PD_{2}(8(2n+1)+6)=PD_{2}(16n+14)\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

∎
