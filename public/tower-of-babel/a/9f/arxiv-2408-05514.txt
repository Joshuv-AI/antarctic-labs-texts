Testing Elliptical Models in High Dimensions
Siyao Wang, Department of Statistics, University of California, Davis

Abstract
Due to the broad applications of elliptical models, there is a long line of research on goodness-of-fit tests for empirically validating them. However, the existing literature on this topic is generally confined to low-dimensional settings, and to the best of our knowledge, there are no established goodness-of-fit tests for elliptical models that are supported by theoretical guarantees in high dimensions. In this paper, we propose a new goodness-of-fit test for this problem, and our main result shows that the test is asymptotically valid when the dimension and sample size diverge proportionally. Remarkably, it also turns out that the asymptotic validity of the test requires no assumptions on the population covariance matrix. With regard to numerical performance, we confirm that the empirical level of the test is close to the nominal level across a range of conditions, and that the test is able to reliably detect non-elliptical distributions. Moreover, when the proposed test is specialized to the problem of testing normality in high dimensions, we show that it compares favorably with a state-of-the-art method, and hence, this way of using the proposed test is of independent interest.



Keywords: elliptical models, goodness-of-fit, high-dimensional statistics, Isserlis’ Theorem

1
Introduction

A centered random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with covariance matrix
\Sigma
is said to have an elliptical distribution if it can be represented in the form
\xi\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{u}
, where
\mathbf{u}
is a uniformly distributed random vector on the
p
-dimensional unit sphere, and
\xi
is a non-negative scalar random variable that is independent of
\mathbf{u}
and normalized to satisfy
\mathbf{E}(\xi^{2})=p

(
Cambanis et al.,, 1981
;
Muirhead,, 2009
)
.
Elliptical models
play an essential role in multivariate analysis and high-dimensional statistics, as they possess many attractive theoretical properties of normal models, and are also flexible enough to capture important non-normal effects that arise in applications. For instance, elliptical models have been widely adopted throughout the domains of finance
(
McNeil et al.,, 2011
;
Gupta et al.,, 2013
)
and signal processing
(
Ollila et al.,, 2012
;
Mahmood and Chitre,, 2015
;
Hung et al.,, 2022
;
Li and Mandic,, 2022
)
. Furthermore, elliptical models provide the foundation for a plethora of methods that address fundamental statistical problems, including covariance estimation
(
Tyler,, 1987
;
Sun et al.,, 2016
;
Goes et al.,, 2020
)
, regression
(
Li,, 1991
;
Lin et al.,, 2019
)
, and inference in graphical models
(
Vogel and Fried,, 2011
;
Liu et al.,, 2012
;
Drton and Maathuis,, 2017
)
.

Based on the extensive use of elliptical models, the problem of validating them with goodness-of-fit tests has become a long-standing topic of research
(
Beran,, 1979
;
Koltchinskii and Sakhanenko,, 2000
;
Manzotti et al.,, 2002
;
Schott,, 2002
;
Huffer and Park,, 2007
;
Albisetti et al.,, 2020
;
Ducharme and de Micheaux,, 2020
;
Babić et al.,, 2021
;
Tang and Li,, 2023
, e.g.)
. However, this literature is generally confined to low-dimensional settings—often due to the use of the inverse sample covariance matrix in the construction of goodness-of-fit tests. Likewise, several authors have recently drawn attention to the need for new approaches to goodness-of-fit testing that can handle elliptical and related models in high dimensions
(
Schkoda and Drton,, 2023
;
Chen and Xia,, 2023
;
Tang and Li,, 2023
;
Lin et al.,, 2023
)
. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, there are no established goodness-of-fit tests for elliptical models that are theoretically supported in high dimensions. Indeed, even in the simpler case of normal models, theoretical guarantees for goodness-of-fit tests in high dimensions are at the cutting edge of research
(
Chen and Xia,, 2023
)
.

In the current paper, we seek to rectify this situation by developing a new goodness-of-fit test for elliptical models and establishing its asymptotic validity when the dimension and sample size diverge proportionally. To outline our formulation of the problem, we deal with a set of centered i.i.d. observations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
drawn from an unknown distribution
\mathbf{P}
, and the goal is to use the observations to test

\mathsf{H}_{0}:\mathbf{P}\in\mathscr{P}\ \ \ \ \ \text{versus}\ \ \ \ \ \mathsf{H}_{1}:\mathbf{P}\not\in\mathscr{P},

(1)

where
\mathscr{P}
is a class of elliptical distributions. To account for the role of high dimensionality and allow
p
to increase as a function of
n
, the problem (
1
) should be understood more precisely as representing a sequence of hypothesis testing problems implicitly indexed by
n
, in which
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathscr{P}
both depend on
n
. The exact definition of the sequence of classes
\mathscr{P}
will be given later in Assumption
1
, but in brief, the associated elliptical distributions will only be subject to some simple moment conditions and will be allowed to have
unrestricted covariance matrices
.

The idea of the proposed test is based on a special property of the moment structure of elliptical distributions. Specifically, if
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(x_{11},\dots,x_{1p})
is drawn from an elliptical distribution for which each covariate is non-degenerate and has finite kurtosis, then all of the covariates must have the same kurtosis, denoted by
\kappa
.
In particular, when
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is centered, this means
\kappa=\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{4})/(\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{2}))^{2}
for all
j=1,\dots,p
, as recorded in Lemma
D.1
with a short accompanying proof.
It also turns out that
\kappa
can be independently calculated in a “coordinate-free” way (based on Lemma
D.2
), which leads to the following system of
p
simultaneous equations that hold for any centered elliptical distribution with non-degenerate covariates and
\kappa<\infty
,

\kappa=\frac{\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{4})}{(\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{2}))^{2}}=\frac{3(\operatorname{var}(\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}^{2})+\textup{tr}(\Sigma)^{2})}{2\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{2})+\textup{tr}(\Sigma)^{2}}\text{ \ \ \ \ for all \ \ \ \ $j=1,\dots,p$},

(2)

where
\Sigma
denotes the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
. The equations above will lead us to construct two distinct kurtosis estimates, denoted
\tilde{\kappa}
and
\check{\kappa}
, and our strategy is to “play them off of each other” in designing a goodness-of-fit test. That is, our proposed test statistic will measure the discrepancy between
\tilde{\kappa}
and
\check{\kappa}
, and the null hypothesis will be rejected when this discrepancy is sufficiently large. So, in essence, the proposed test seeks to detect violations of equations (
2
), and this approach is well suited to the high-dimensional setting because larger numbers of covariates provide more opportunities to detect violations. Furthermore,
the test can even detect non-elliptical distributions when all of the covariates have the same kurtosis
. This is because one of the kurtosis estimates is based on a structural property that generally does not hold for non-elliptical distributions, and our simulation results for Model (4) in Section
4.2
demonstrate the power of our test in such cases.

From a theoretical standpoint, our main result (Theorem
1
) is a central limit theorem for the proposed test statistic under the null hypothesis. One of the main virtues of this result is that it allows the population covariance matrix to vary in an unrestricted manner as
n
and
p
diverge. An interesting theoretical consequence of this flexibility is that the difference
\tilde{\kappa}-\check{\kappa}
may fluctuate over many different scales, depending on the correlation structure of the data. This is in contrast to more conventional high-dimensional central limit theorems, in which an unnormalized statistic often has fluctuations of the same order (such as
1/\sqrt{n}
or
1/p
) for all of the allowed choices of the population covariance matrix. Accordingly, constructing an accurate estimate for the standard deviation of
\tilde{\kappa}-\check{\kappa}
is especially crucial in our method, and proving the consistency of this estimate is a substantial undertaking (see Appendix C).

A key technical obstacle in proving Theorem
1
is bounding sums that have the form

\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}=1}^{p}\mathbf{E}\big(x_{1j_{1}}^{k_{1}}x_{1j_{2}}^{k_{2}}x_{1j_{3}}^{k_{3}}x_{1j_{4}}^{k_{4}}\big)
for exponents
k_{1},\dots,k_{4}\in\{0,2,4\}
. In order to analyze such sums, we develop a systematic approach based on
Isserlis’ theorem

(
Janson,, 1997
)
, also known as Wick’s theorem, which is a powerful tool for calculating mixed moments of normal random vectors that is based on the combinatorial framework of pair partitions. Because the statement of Isserlis’ theorem is only applicable to normal random vectors, an important merit of our analysis is that we are able to successfully adapt it to the setting of elliptical random vectors. Furthermore, even in the special case of normal data, Isserlis’ theorem produces a formula for each mixed moment
\mathbf{E}\big(x_{1j_{1}}^{k_{1}}x_{1j_{2}}^{k_{2}}x_{1j_{3}}^{k_{3}}x_{1j_{4}}^{k_{4}}\big)
that may involve an extremely large number of terms. To deal with this issue, we introduce an equivalence relation among the terms that allows us to decompose the moment calculations across a tractable number of equivalence classes. (See the proof of Proposition
3
in Appendix
B
.)

With regard to numerical performance, we show through simulations that the proposed test has an empirical level that nearly matches the nominal level when the data are generated from elliptical distributions in 60 distinct parameter settings. Also, the test is able to reject the null hypothesis even when the data-generating distribution differs from elliptical distributions by small perturbations. For comparison, we show that the proposed test has substantially higher power than a recently developed normality test
(
Chen and Xia,, 2023
)
, which seems to be the only such test whose asymptotic validity has been established in high dimensions. (Such a comparison of power is justified because both tests are intended to reject in the presence of non-elliptical data.) Lastly, we present some examples involving natural datasets, illustrating that for commonly used significance levels, the proposed test is able to reject, whereas the normality test is not. Thus, our approach is of independent interest in connection with the topic of high-dimensional normality testing.

Notation and conventions.

For any real number
q\geq 1
, the entrywise
\ell_{q}
norm of a real matrix
A
is
\|A\|_{q}=(\sum_{i,j}|A_{ij}|^{q})^{1/q}
, and the

is
\|\zeta\|_{L^{q}}=(\mathbf{E}(|\zeta|^{q}))^{1/q}
.
For two sequences of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
, the relation
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
means
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. When
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
are non-negative, we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
if there is a constant
C>0
not depending on
n
such that
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
holds for all large
n
. When both of the relations
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold, we write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
. The maximum and minimum of a pair of real numbers
a
and
b
are sometimes written as
\max\{a,b\}=a\vee b
and
\min\{a,b\}=a\wedge b
.
Convergence in probability and convergence in distribution are respectively denoted by
\xrightarrow{\mathbf{P}}
and
\Rightarrow
.
Lastly, certain formulas in our testing procedure involve fractions of random variables in which the denominator may vanish.
To make the procedure well defined in any finite-sample situation, a fraction with a zero denominator is arbitrarily defined to be equal to one. This convention is of minor importance, because it is only relevant for events that occur with asymptotically negligible probability, and hence, does not affect the limiting null distribution of the proposed test statistic.

2
Method

Recall from our discussion of (
2
) that the proposed test seeks to detect violations of the null hypothesis by measuring the discrepancy between two distinct estimates
\tilde{\kappa}
and
\check{\kappa}
for the kurtosis parameter
\kappa
. These two estimates will be constructed using separate halves of the data,
\mathbf{x}_{1}\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n/2}
and
\mathbf{x}_{n/2+1}\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
, and we assume henceforth that
n
is even for simplicity. Since the left side of (
2
) asserts that
\kappa
is equal to the entrywise kurtosis
\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{4})/\mathbf{E}(x_{1j}^{2})^{2}
for all
j=1,\dots,p
, it is natural to construct the first estimate
\tilde{\kappa}
by averaging the entrywise kurtosis estimates
\tilde{\kappa}_{j}=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}x_{ij}^{4}\big/(\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}x_{ij}^{2})^{2}

over
j=1,\dots,p
, which gives

\tilde{\kappa}\ =\ \frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\tilde{\kappa}_{j}.

To define the second estimate
\check{\kappa}
, it is helpful to introduce the parameters
\varsigma^{2}=\operatorname{var}(\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}^{2})
and
\nu_{k}=\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{k})
for
k\geq 1
,
so that the right side of (
2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\kappa\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{3(\varsigma^{2}+\nu_{1}^{2})}{\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2}}.

(3)

From this equation, we construct
\check{\kappa}
by substituting in estimates of
\varsigma^{2}
,
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
. For estimating the latter two parameters, let
\check{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n/2}\sum_{i=n/2+1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
, as well as
\check{\nu}_{k}=\textup{tr}(\check{\Sigma}^{k})
for
k\geq 1
. Specifically, we estimate
\nu_{1}
using
\check{\nu}_{1}
, and we estimate
\nu_{2}
using
\check{\nu}_{2}-\frac{1}{n/2}\check{\nu}_{1}^{2}
, which is a bias-corrected version of
\check{\nu}_{2}
. Next, we estimate
\varsigma^{2}
using the sample variance of the squared norms
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
for
i=n/2+1,\dots,n
, which is

\displaystyle\check{\varsigma}^{2}\ =\ \frac{1}{2\binom{n/2}{2}}\sum_{n/2<i<i^{\prime}\leq n}\big(\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}_{i^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}\big)^{2}.

Hence, the second estimate of
\kappa
is defined by

\check{\kappa}\ =\ \frac{3(\check{\varsigma}^{2}+\check{\nu}_{1}^{2})}{\check{\nu}_{1}^{2}+2(\check{\nu}_{2}-\frac{2}{n}\check{\nu}_{1}^{2})}.

To compare
\tilde{\kappa}
and
\check{\kappa}
, it will be technically convenient to work with a shifted and rescaled version of the difference
\tilde{\kappa}-\check{\kappa}
, denoted

T_{n}\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{pn}{2}}\Big(\frac{\tilde{\kappa}-\check{\kappa}}{3}+\frac{4}{n}\Big),

where the term
4/n
merely ensures that
T_{n}
is asymptotically centered. Finally, the statistic
T_{n}
must be normalized by an estimate of its standard deviation, which is denoted by
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
and will be defined below in Section
2.1
. So altogether, the proposed test statistic is
T_{n}/\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
, and our main theoretical result in Theorem
1
will show that under the null hypothesis, it satisfies

T_{n}/\widehat{\sigma}_{n}\Rightarrow N(0,1)

as
n\to\infty
with
p\asymp n
. Accordingly, the proposed testing procedure rejects at a nominal level of
\alpha\in(0,1)
if the event

\mathsf{R}_{n}(\alpha)\ =\ \Big\{|T_{n}|>\widehat{\sigma}_{n}\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\Big\}

occurs, where
\Phi
is the standard normal distribution function.

2.1
Estimating the variance of
T_{n}

We will show in Proposition
1
of Appendix
B
that the variance of
T_{n}
is asymptotically equivalent to a parameter
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sigma_{n,1}^{2}+\sigma_{n,2}^{2}
defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,1}^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{8\|R\|_{4}^{4}}{3p}\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\sigma_{n,2}^{2}\ =\ \frac{8p(2\nu_{4}+\nu_{2}^{2})}{(\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2})^{2}},

(4)

where
R
is the correlation matrix of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
.
To write down an estimate for
\sigma_{n}^{2}
, let
\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
, as well as
\widehat{\nu}_{k}=\textup{tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}^{k})
for
k\geq 1
, and
\widehat{R}_{jj^{\prime}}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{jj^{\prime}}/(\widehat{\Sigma}_{jj}\widehat{\Sigma}_{j^{\prime}j^{\prime}})^{1/2}
for
1\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq p
. Then, we define
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{n,1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{n,2}^{2}
according to

\displaystyle\ \ \ \widehat{\sigma}_{n,1}^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{8}{3p}\Big(\|\widehat{R}\|_{4}^{4}-\widehat{c}\Big)\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\widehat{\sigma}_{n,2}^{2}\ =\ \displaystyle\frac{8p(2\widehat{\nu}_{4}+(\widehat{\nu}_{2}-\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2})^{2})}{(\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2}+2(\widehat{\nu}_{2}-\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2}))^{2}},

(5)

where
\widehat{c}
is a correction term whose definition requires some additional notation: For any real number
x
and positive number
t
, define the threshold function
\llbracket x\rrbracket_{t}=\text{sgn}(x)(|x|\wedge t)
.
Also, for any fixed symmetric matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and integer
k\geq 1
, define the function
g_{k}(M)=\mathbf{E}((\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{z}_{1})^{k})
, where
\mathbf{z}_{1}
is a standard normal vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. (Explicit formulas for this function are provided in Lemma
D.5
.)
In this notation, the correction term
\widehat{c}
is given by

\widehat{c}\ =\ \widehat{\beta}\|\widehat{R}\|_{4}^{4}-3\big\llbracket 1-\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\big\rrbracket_{t_{p}}\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}^{2},

(6)

where
t_{p}=p^{-3/4}\log(p)
, and

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}\ =\

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{8}}{g_{4}(\widehat{\Sigma})-\textstyle\frac{12}{n}(\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{4}+2\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2}\widehat{\nu}_{2}-\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{4})}

(7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}\ =\

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\widehat{\varsigma}^{2}-2(\widehat{\nu}_{2}-\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2})}{g_{2}(\widehat{\Sigma})-\textstyle\frac{2}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{6}}{\frac{1}{2}g_{3}(\widehat{\Sigma})-\textstyle\frac{3}{n}\widehat{\nu}_{1}^{3}}.

(8)

The definition of the correction term in (
6
) is motivated by an asymptotic formula for
\sigma_{n,1}^{2}
that results from (
38
) and (
39
) in the proof of Lemma
B.4
. In particular, it can be shown that the term
3\big\llbracket 1-\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\big\rrbracket_{t_{p}}\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}^{2}
is asymptotically negligible, but it turns out that retaining this term is beneficial at a finite-sample level.
Finally, when interpreting the formulas above, recall that in the unlikely event where the denominator of a fraction vanishes, we arbitrarily define the fraction to be equal to one.

3
Theory

Our analysis will be done with a sequence of models implicitly embedded in a triangular array, where the rows are indexed by
n
, and all parameters may vary with
n
except when stated otherwise. The following assumption specifies the sequence of null hypotheses in (
1
) corresponding to different values of
n
.

Assumption 1
.

As
n\to\infty
, the dimension
p
grows such that
p\asymp n
. For each
i=1,\dots,n
, the observation
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has the form

\mathbf{x}_{i}\ =\ \xi_{i}\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{u}_{i},

where
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is deterministic and positive semidefinite with positive diagonal entries, and
(\mathbf{u}_{1},\xi_{1}),\dots,

(\mathbf{u}_{n},\xi_{n})
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times[0,\infty)
satisfying the following conditions: The vector
\mathbf{u}_{1}
is drawn from the uniform distribution on the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and is independent of
\xi_{1}
. In addition, the random variable
\xi_{1}^{2}
satisfies
\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{2})=p
, as well as the conditions

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\!\bigg(\frac{\xi_{1}^{2}-p}{\sqrt{p}}\bigg)\ =\ \tau+o(1)\ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \bigg\|\frac{\xi_{1}^{2}-p}{\sqrt{p}}\bigg\|_{L^{8}}\,=\,o(p^{1/4})

(9)

as
n\to\infty
, for some constant
\tau\geq 0
that is fixed with respect to
n
.

Remarks.
Perhaps the most striking feature of Assumption
1
is that the population covariance matrix
\Sigma
is unrestricted. (The condition that
\Sigma
has positive diagonal entries does not impose any real restriction, because if
\Sigma_{jj}=0
, then the
j
th covariate is zero almost surely and is hence irrelevant.) Also, the model only places conditions on a finite number of moments. Similar moment assumptions have been considered elsewhere, such as in the papers
(
Hu et al., 2019a,
;
Hu et al., 2019b,
)
, which analyze eigenvalue statistics of sample covariance matrices in high-dimensional elliptical models. In essence, the purpose of the two conditions in (
9
) is to make it possible to precisely determine the magnitudes of higher order remainder terms in the proofs of certain CLTs underlying Theorem
1
below. It should be noted too that the second condition in (
9
) allows for the eighth moment of
(\xi_{1}^{2}-p)/\sqrt{p}
to diverge with the dimension, i.e.
\|(\xi_{1}^{2}-p)/\sqrt{p}\|_{L^{8}}\to\infty
as
p\to\infty
.
In fact, this condition can be weakened to allow for even faster divergence as
p\to\infty
, but it would involve a somewhat more complicated statement of Assumption
1
, and so we have retained the version above to keep the statement as concise as possible.

Examples.
Below, we provide an assortment of examples for the distribution of
\xi_{1}^{2}
that conform with Assumption
1
. In each case, the distribution is parameterized so that the basic normalization condition
\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{2})=p
holds.

•

Chi-Squared on
p
degrees of freedom,

•

Poisson(
p
),

•

(1-\tau)
Negative-Binomial
(p,1-\tau)
, for any fixed
\tau\in(0,1)
,

•

(p+2b)
Beta
(p/2,b)
, for any fixed

,

•

Gamma
(p/\tau,1/\tau)
, for any fixed

•

\left(\frac{p(1+p+\tau)}{\tau},\frac{1+p+2\tau}{\tau}\right)

•

, for any fixed

.

Several of these examples for
\xi_{1}^{2}
can be subsumed within a more general non-parametric class of distributions that satisfy Assumption
1
. This class corresponds to choices of
\xi_{1}^{2}
that can be represented in the form
\xi_{1}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\eta_{j}
for some independent random variables
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{p}\geq 0
satisfying

\small\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{E}(\eta_{j})=1,\hskip 9.24994pt\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\operatorname{var}(\eta_{j})=\tau+o(1),\text{\hskip 9.24994ptand \hskip 9.24994pt}\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\mathbf{E}(\eta_{j}^{8})\,=\,o(p^{5}),

again with
\tau\geq 0
being a constant not depending on
n
. (This can be verified using Rosenthal’s inequality, as given in Lemma
D.7
of the supplement.) It is also notable that the moments
\mathbf{E}(\eta_{j}^{8})
are allowed to grow quite rapidly as
p\to\infty
.

The following theorem is our main theoretical result.

Theorem 1
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then as
n\to\infty
we have

\frac{T_{n}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}}\ \Rightarrow\ N(0,1),

and hence, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the level of the test satisfies

\mathbf{P}(\mathsf{R}_{n}(\alpha))\ \to\ \alpha.

Remarks.
At a high level, the proof of Theorem
1
involves several main parts. The first two are given in Propositions
1
and
6
of the supplement, which respectively show that
T_{n}/\sigma_{n}\Rightarrow N(0,1)
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}/\sigma_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{P}}1
as
n\to\infty
. The problem of showing that
T_{n}/\sigma_{n}
is asymptotically normal is then divided into two more substantial parts, corresponding to separate CLTs for standardized versions of the estimates
\tilde{\kappa}
and
\check{\kappa}
, which utilize different sets of theoretical tools.

The CLT for
\tilde{\kappa}
involves analyzing a sum of i.i.d. random variables
\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}w_{i}
, where
w_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\textstyle\frac{1}{3}(x_{ij}^{4}-\mathbf{E}(x_{ij}^{4}))-2(x_{ij}^{2}-\mathbf{E}(x_{ij}^{2}))
. We establish asymptotic normality by showing that a Lyapunov condition of the form
\mathbf{E}(w_{1}^{4})=o(n\operatorname{var}(w_{1})^{2})
holds, which in turn, requires bounding sums of the form
\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}=1}^{p}\mathbf{E}\big(x_{1j_{1}}^{k_{1}}x_{1j_{2}}^{k_{2}}x_{1j_{3}}^{k_{3}}x_{1j_{4}}^{k_{4}}\big)
for exponents
k_{1},\dots,k_{4}\in\{0,2,4\}
.
Although the random vector
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is not necessarily normal under the null hypothesis, we are able to control its mixed moments by adapting Isserlis’ theorem
(
Janson,, 1997
)
, which states that the following formula holds for any dimension
d
and any centered normal vector
(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(g_{1}\cdots g_{d})\ =\ \sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{d}}\prod_{\{j,j^{\prime}\}\in\pi}\mathbf{E}(g_{j}g_{j^{\prime}}),

(10)

where
\mathcal{P}_{d}
is the collection of all partitions
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,d\}
into distinct pairs
\{j,j^{\prime}\}
. Here, it is important to note that even if the vector
\mathbf{x}_{1}
was assumed to be normal, a brute-force application of Isserlis’ theorem would not provide a workable solution to establishing the Lyapunov condition, because of the enormous number of terms on the right side of (
10
). For instance, in the special case when
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is normal and
k_{1}=\cdots=k_{4}=4
, there are more than 2 million terms in the formula for
\mathbf{E}(x_{1j_{1}}^{k_{1}}x_{1j_{2}}^{k_{2}}x_{1j_{3}}^{k_{3}}x_{1j_{4}}^{k_{4}})
, since
d=\sum_{l=1}^{4}k_{l}
and
\textup{card}(\mathcal{P}_{16})=15\cdot 13\cdot 11\cdots 1
. Nevertheless, we are able to overcome this difficulty by taking advantage of symmetries among the partitions. To explain the how this works, let
t_{\pi}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})
denote the term corresponding to the partition
\pi
in the formula for
\mathbf{E}(x_{1j_{1}}^{4}x_{1j_{2}}^{4}x_{1j_{3}}^{4}x_{1j_{4}}^{4})
so that interchanging the order of summation gives

\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}=1}^{p}\mathbf{E}\big(x_{1j_{1}}^{4}x_{1j_{2}}^{4}x_{1j_{3}}^{4}x_{1j_{4}}^{4}\big)\ =\ \sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{16}}\sum_{j_{1},j_{3},j_{3},j_{4}=1}^{p}t_{\pi}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}).

If we define an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{P}_{16}
by saying that
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are equivalent if
t_{\pi}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})
and
t_{\pi^{\prime}}(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})
produce the same value when summed over all
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}
, then remarkably, it turns out that there is only a handful of equivalence classes. Hence, the sum over
\mathcal{P}_{16}
that contains more than 2 million terms can be reduced to a tractable calculation, making it possible to establish the asymptotic normality of
\tilde{\kappa}
. (Strictly speaking, the technical aspects of this strategy are implemented somewhat differently in the proof of Proposition
3
, and there are additional considerations required to find the sizes of the different equivalence classes, but this explanation conveys the main idea.)

The asymptotic normality of the second estimate
\check{\kappa}
is proven in an entirely different way, by adapting classical results on U-statistics to the high-dimensional setting. For this purpose,
Propositions
4
and
5
show that the behavior of
\check{\kappa}
is largely determined by the statistic
U=\check{\varsigma}^{2}-2(\check{\nu}_{2}-\frac{2}{n}\check{\nu}_{1}^{2})
, which is shown to be a U-statistic in the proof of Lemma
B.8
. There are two aspects of the classical theory of U-statistics that break down if they are used to prove a CLT for
U
in our setup. The first is in showing that
U
is asymptotically equivalent to its Hájek projection
\widehat{U}
, and the second is in showing that
\widehat{U}
is asymptotically normal. In essence, the issue in the high-dimensional setting is that we must account for the fact that
\operatorname{var}(U)
and
\operatorname{var}(\widehat{U})
may vary in their orders of magnitude as
p\to\infty
, and this is accomplished in Lemmas
B.8
-
B.11
through a delicate analysis of the covariances of random quadratic forms.

4.2
Assessment of power

Design of experiments.
To study the power of the proposed test, we generated data under the alternative hypothesis using models of the form

\mathbf{x}_{1}=\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{s}_{1}
, where
\mathbf{s}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a random vector whose entries are i.i.d random variables satisfying
\mathbf{E}(s_{11})=0
and
\mathbf{E}(s_{11}^{2})=1
. For the choice of
\Sigma
, we used the same menu of options (1)-(4) described in Section
4.1
. The “distance” between the null and alternative hypotheses is controlled through a parameter
h\in[0,1]
, such that the entries of
\mathbf{s}_{1}
are given by

\displaystyle s_{11}\ =\ \sqrt{1-h}\,z_{11}+\sqrt{h}\,y_{11},

where
z_{11}
is a standard normal random variable and
y_{11}
is a standardized (a) Laplace or (b) Beta random variable. In detail, the choices of
y_{11}
correspond to

(a).

y_{11}\sim\frac{\textup{Laplace}(0,1)}{\sqrt{2}}
,

(b).

y_{11}\sim\frac{\textup{Beta}(2,3/2)-4/7}{\sqrt{8/147}}
.

Note that if
h=0
, then
\mathbf{x}_{1}
has a
N(0,\Sigma)
distribution, which is elliptical, and so the null hypothesis holds. By contrast, if
0<h\leq 1
, then the alternative hypothesis holds. Another reason for generating the data in this manner is that it allows for convenient comparisons of both level and power with respect to the high-dimensional normality test proposed in
(
Chen and Xia,, 2023
)
. Such a comparison is legitimate, because both tests are intended to accept the null hypothesis when
h=0
and reject it when
h>0
.

As in Section
4.1
, the dimension was selected from
p\in\{200,400,600\}
and the sample size was chosen to be
n=400
. For each possible choice of
(y_{11},\Sigma,n,p)
, we generated 1000 datasets corresponding to several values of
h
in a grid within
[0,1]
, and we performed both the proposed test and the normality test at a 5% nominal level on every dataset. (The normality test also involves two parameters, denoted
L
and
B
, which we set to the values
L=1
and
B=500
suggested in
(
Chen and Xia,, 2023
)
.) Finally, at each setting of
(y_{11},\Sigma,n,p,h)
, we recorded the rejection rate of each test over the 1000 datasets, allowing the results to be visualized as curves depending on
h
, such that their heights at
h=0
represent the empirical level, and their heights at
h\in(0,1]
represent the empirical power.

Discussion of results.
The results are displayed in Figures
1
-
6
, which contain 24 panels corresponding to the different settings of
(y_{11},\Sigma,n,p)
. In each panel, the rejection rates of the proposed test and the normality test are respectively plotted with blue and red curves. Overall, the most apparent difference between the tests is that they tend to produce curves with different shapes. The power of the proposed test has the intuitive property that it increases toward 1 as
h
increases, whereas the power of the normality test stays relatively flat as a function of
h
. (Recall that larger values of
h
correspond to data-generating distributions that are farther from being elliptical.) To fully appreciate the favorable power characteristics of the proposed test, it is worth emphasizing that normal models correspond to a strict subclass of elliptical models. So, in a heuristic sense, normality is an easier hypothesis to reject—and yet, the proposed test still manages to outperform.

In addition to the power of the tests, it is important to compare how well they respect the nominal level of 5%, which is marked with a dashed black line in every panel. In all settings, the empirical level of the proposed test at
h=0
is close to the desired value of 5%, whereas the empirical level of the normality is often substantially inflated, and in several cases it is nearly 20%. Due to this issue, the fact that the red curves are sometimes higher than the blue curves for small values of
h
is not a disadvantage of the proposed test. In other words, the higher values of the red curves for small
h
occur simply because those curves start too high when
h=0
.

Remark on covariates with equal kurtosis.
Apart from the comparisons of power and level just discussed, a distinct point that deserves special emphasis is the power of the proposed test to detect non-elliptical distributions whose covariates have the same kurtosis. Considering that the test is motivated by the identity (
2
), which implies that all covariates in an elliptical model have the same kurtosis, it might seem surprising that the test can still be powerful against alternatives for which all covariates have the same kurtosis. Nevertheless, the results for Model (4) in Figures
1
-
6
show that the test achieves substantially higher power in such cases compared to the normality test. This behavior becomes more understandable once it is recognized that the kurtosis estimate
\breve{\kappa}
used to construct the proposed test is designed based on a distinct structural property of elliptical models. Hence, it is less surprising that
\breve{\kappa}
disagrees with the other kurtosis estimate
\tilde{\kappa}
when data are generated from non-elliptical distributions whose covariates have the same kurtosis.

Lemma B.2

Lemma B.4
.

1
holds and the diagonal entries of
\Sigma

We begin the proof by expressing

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(w_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{p}\sum_{j,k=1}^{p}\Big(\textstyle\frac{1}{9}\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{4},x_{1k}^{4})+4\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{2},x_{1k}^{2})-\textstyle\frac{2}{3}\big(\textup{cov}(x_{1k}^{4},x_{1j}^{2})+\textup{cov}(x_{1k}^{2},x_{1j}^{4})\big)\Big).

To complete the proof, we apply the Lindeberg CLT for triangular arrays given in

Verifying this condition is by far the most challenging aspect of the proof and depends on delicate cancellations of high-order terms in an expansion of the fourth moment of

1

\displaystyle\frac{\check{\nu}_{2}-\textstyle\frac{2}{n}\check{\nu}_{1}^{2}}{\nu_{2}}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbf{P}}\ 1\ \ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \ \frac{\check{\nu}_{1}^{2}}{\nu_{1}^{2}}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbf{P}}\ 1,

(59)

and hence

(60)

U

given in (

\frac{T_{n,2}}{\sigma_{n,2}}\ =\ \sqrt{\frac{pn}{2}}\Big(\frac{U}{\sigma_{n,2}(\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2})(1+o_{\mathbf{P}}(1))}-\frac{{\varsigma}^{2}-2{\nu}_{2}}{\sigma_{n,2}({\nu}_{1}^{2}+2{\nu}_{2})}\Big).

Lemma B.8
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. If
{\tt{r}}(\Sigma)\lesssim\sqrt{p}
, then as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{np/2}(U-({\varsigma}^{2}-2{\nu}_{2}))}{\sigma_{n,2}(\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2})}

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ \ N(0,1).

Proof.

To show that
U
has the structure of a U-statistic, define the kernel

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\ =\ \Big(\textstyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2(n-2)}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big(\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{2}-\frac{2(n-2)}{n}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{2})^{2},

(62)

which yields

\displaystyle U\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{n/2}{2}}\sum_{n/2<i<j\leq n}h(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}).

Furthermore, by letting

\begin{split}\theta&\ =\ \mathbf{E}(h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}))\\
h_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})&\ =\ \mathbf{E}(h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})|\mathbf{x}_{1})-\theta,\end{split}

(63)

the Hájek projection of
U-\theta
can be expressed as

\widehat{U}\ =\ \frac{2}{n/2}\sum_{i=n/2+1}^{n}h_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

It is a classical fact
(
van der Vaart,, 2000
, Theorem 11.2)
that if

\frac{\operatorname{var}(\widehat{U})}{\operatorname{var}(U)}\ \to\ 1

(64)

as
n\to\infty
, then

\frac{U-\theta}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U)}}\ =\ \frac{\widehat{U}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\widehat{U})}}+o_{\mathbf{P}}(1).

(65)

This shows that if
U
and
\widehat{U}
are suitably normalized, then the asymptotic normality of one implies that of the other. To check the condition (
64
), we first introduce the parameters

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle\ =\ \textup{cov}(h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}),h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{3}))

\displaystyle\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ \operatorname{var}(h(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})).

It is known from the theory of U-statistics
(
van der Vaart,, 2000
, Theorem 12.3)
that the variances of
\widehat{U}
and
U
have exact expressions in terms of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, namely

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(\widehat{U})\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{4}{n/2}\psi_{1}

(66)

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(U)\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{2(n/2-2)}{\binom{n/2}{2}}\psi_{1}+\frac{1}{\binom{n/2}{2}}\psi_{2}.

(67)

Therefore, the condition (
64
) will hold, if we can show

\psi_{2}\ =\ o(n\,\psi_{1}).

(68)

The sizes of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
can be compared with Lemmas
B.10
and
B.11
, which show that under the condition
{\tt{r}}(\Sigma)\lesssim\sqrt{p}
we have

\displaystyle\psi_{2}\ \lesssim\

\displaystyle\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{2})^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\ =\

\displaystyle 4\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{4})+\textstyle 2\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{2})^{2}+o(\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{2})^{2}).

(70)

From these relations, it is straightforward to check that (
68
) holds, and hence (
65
) holds as well.

Having shown (
65
), the proof will be complete if we can establish two more items, which are

\frac{\widehat{U}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\widehat{U})}}\ \Rightarrow\ N(0,1)

(71)

and

\frac{U-\theta}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U)}}\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{np/2}(U-({\varsigma}^{2}-2{\nu}_{2}))}{\sigma_{n,2}(\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2})}+o_{\mathbf{P}}(1).

(72)

Since
\widehat{U}
is a sum of centered independent random variables, the limit (
71
) can be established by checking the following Lyapunov-type condition, which implies the Lindeberg central limit theorem in the setting of triangular arrays
(
van der Vaart,, 2000
, Proposition 2.27)
(and so is applicable in the high-dimensional setting),

\frac{\mathbf{E}(h_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})^{4})}{n\operatorname{var}(h_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))^{2}}\ \to\ 0.

(73)

This will be established in Lemma
B.9
.

With regard to (
72
), it follows from equations (
64
), (
66
), and (
70
) that the standard deviation of
U
satisfies

\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U)}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n/2}(16\nu_{4}+8\nu_{2}^{2})}}\ \to\ 1.

Consequently, equation (
61
) gives

\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U)}}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{np}}\sigma_{n,2}(\nu_{1}^{2}+2\nu_{2})}\ \to\ 1.

(74)

Finally, to consider the the mean parameter
\theta
, it can be checked using Lemma
D.2
as well as equation (
15
) that

\begin{split}\theta&\ =\ \Big(1-\textstyle\frac{4(n-2)}{n^{2}}\Big)\varsigma^{2}-\frac{2(n-2)}{n}\nu_{2}\\
&\ =\ \varsigma^{2}-2\nu_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\textstyle\frac{\nu_{2}}{n}).\end{split}

(75)

Combining this with (
74
) implies (
72
), which completes the proof.∎

Lemma B.10

Lemma C.6
.

Lemma D.3

(
Bai and Silverstein, (2010)
, Lemma B.26)
.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a standard normal random vector, and fix any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Then, for any
q\geq 1
,

\big\|\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{z}_{1}-\textup{tr}(M)\big\|_{L^{q}}\ \leq\ C_{q}\textup{tr}(MM^{\top})^{1/2},

where
C_{q}
is a constant depending on
q
only.

Lemma D.4
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then for any fixed matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and any
1\leq q\leq 8
,

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{x}_{1}-\textup{tr}(M\Sigma)\Big\|_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\ \lesssim\ \sqrt{\textup{tr}(M\Sigma M^{\top}\Sigma)}\ +\ o(\textstyle\frac{1}{p^{1/4}})\textup{tr}(M\Sigma).

Proof.

By Lyapunov’s inequality, it is enough to handle the case when
q=8
. For any fixed matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, the definition of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and Assumption
1
give

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{x}_{1}-\textup{tr}(M\Sigma)\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}

\displaystyle\ \lesssim\ \mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{16})\Big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}M\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{u}_{1}-\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\textup{tr}(M\Sigma)\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ +\ \textup{tr}(M\Sigma)^{8}\Big\|\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\xi_{1}^{2}-1\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}

\displaystyle\ \lesssim\ p^{8}\Big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\Sigma^{1/2}M\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{u}_{1}-\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\textup{tr}(\Sigma^{1/2}M\Sigma^{1/2})\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ +\ o(\textstyle\frac{1}{p^{2}})\textup{tr}(M\Sigma)^{8}.

If we can show

\displaystyle p^{8}\Big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}-\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\textup{tr}(A)\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ \lesssim\ \textup{tr}(AA^{\top})^{4}

(95)

holds for any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, then the sub-additivity of the eighth-root function implies the stated result.
To show (
95
), observe that

\displaystyle p^{8}\Big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}-\textstyle\frac{1}{p}\textup{tr}(A)\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ \lesssim\ \Big\|p\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ +\ \Big\|\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{z}_{1}-\textup{tr}(A)\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8},

where
\mathbf{z}_{1}
denotes a standard normal vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
\mathbf{u}_{1}=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{1}}{\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}}
.
The second term of this bound is
\mathcal{O}(\textup{tr}(AA^{\top})^{4})
due to Lemma
D.3
, while the first term can be rewritten as

\Big\|p\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}\Big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\ =\ \big\|p-\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}\big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{L^{8}}^{8}.

Lemma
D.3
shows that the first factor on the right is
\mathcal{O}(p^{4})
,
and combining with the lower bound
\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{16})\geq(\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}))^{8}\geq p^{8}
implies that the second factor satisfies

\begin{split}\big\|\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{u}_{1}\|_{L^{8}}^{8}&\ \lesssim\ \frac{\big\|\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}A\mathbf{z}_{1}-\textup{tr}(A)\big\|_{L^{8}}^{8}}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{16})}+\frac{\textup{tr}(A)^{8}}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{16})}\\[5.69046pt]
&\ \lesssim\ \frac{\textup{tr}(AA^{\top})^{4}}{p^{8}}+\frac{\textup{tr}(A)^{8}}{p^{8}}.\end{split}

This leads to (
95
), since
\textup{tr}(A)^{8}\leq p^{4}\textup{tr}(AA^{\top})^{4}
.
∎

Lemma D.5

(
Magnus et al., (1978)
, Lemma 2.2)
.

If
\mathbf{z}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a standard normal vector and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a symmetric matrix, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}((\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{z}_{1})^{2})\ =\

\displaystyle 2\textup{tr}(M^{2})+\textup{tr}(M)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}((\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{z}_{1})^{3})\ =\

\displaystyle 8\textup{tr}(M^{3})+6\textup{tr}(M^{2})\textup{tr}(M)+\textup{tr}(M)^{3},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}((\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top}M\mathbf{z}_{1})^{4})\ =\

\displaystyle 48\textup{tr}(M^{4})+32\textup{tr}(M^{3})\textup{tr}(M)+12\textup{tr}(M^{2})^{2}+12\textup{tr}(M^{2})\textup{tr}(M)^{2}+\textup{tr}(M)^{4}.

Lemma D.6
.

If Assumption
1
holds, then for any
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,p\}

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{4},x_{1k}^{4})

\displaystyle\ =\ r_{4}(72\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{kk}\Sigma_{jk}^{2}+24\Sigma_{jk}^{4})+9(r_{4}-r_{2}^{2})\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}^{2},

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{2},x_{1k}^{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ 2r_{2}\Sigma_{jk}^{2}+(r_{2}-1)\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{kk},

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1k}^{2},x_{1j}^{4})

\displaystyle\ =\ 12r_{3}\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{jk}^{2}+3(r_{3}-r_{2})\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}.

Proof.

Recall the relation
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(\xi_{1}/\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2})\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}
from (
12
), where
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}\sim N(0,\Sigma)
. Isserlis’ theorem (as presented in (
46
)) ensures that for any
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
we have

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{4},x_{1k}^{4})

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{8})}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{8})}\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{4}\zeta_{1k}^{4})-\Big(\frac{\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{4})}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{4})}\Big)^{2}\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{4})\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1k}^{4})

\displaystyle\ =\ r_{4}(9\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}^{2}+72\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{kk}\Sigma_{jk}^{2}+24\Sigma_{jk}^{4})-9r_{2}^{2}\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}^{2},

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1j}^{2},x_{1k}^{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{4})}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{4})}\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{2}\zeta_{1k}^{2})-\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{2})\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1k}^{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ r_{2}(\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{kk}+2\Sigma_{jk}^{2})-\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{kk},

\displaystyle\textup{cov}(x_{1k}^{2},x_{1j}^{4})

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{6})}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{6})}\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{4}\zeta_{1k}^{2})-\frac{\mathbf{E}(\xi_{1}^{4})}{\mathbf{E}(\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\|_{2}^{4})}\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1j}^{4})\mathbf{E}(\zeta_{1k}^{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ r_{3}(3\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}+12\Sigma_{jj}\Sigma_{jk}^{2})-3r_{2}\Sigma_{jj}^{2}\Sigma_{kk}.

∎

Lemma D.7

(
Johnson et al., (1985)
, Rosenthal’s inequality)
.

Fix
r>2
and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be centered independent random variables. Then, there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\bigg\|_{L^{r}}\ \leq\ c\cdot r\cdot\max\bigg\{\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\bigg\|_{L^{2}},\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{L^{r}}^{r}\Big)^{1/r}\bigg\}.

∎
