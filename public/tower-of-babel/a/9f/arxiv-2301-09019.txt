Mingling of the infrared and ultraviolet and the “cosmological constant” for interacting QFT in 2d
André LeClair

Abstract
We propose a proper definition of the vacuum expectation value of the stress energy tensor ⟨ 0 | T μ ​ ν | 0 ⟩ \langle 0|T_{\mu\nu}|0\rangle for integrable quantum field theories in two spacetime dimensions, which is the analog of the cosmological constant in 4d. For a wide variety of models, massive or massless, we show ρ vac = − m 2 / 2 𝔤 \rho_{\rm vac}=-m^{2}/2\mathfrak{g} exactly, where 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} is a generalized coupling which we compute and m m is a basic mass scale. The kinds of models we consider are the massive sinh-Gordon and sine-Gordon theories and perturbations of the Yang-Lee and 3-state Potts models, pure T ​ T ¯ T\overline{T} perturbations of infra-red QFT’s, and UV completions of the latter which are massless flows between UV and IR fixed points. In the massive case m m is the physical mass of the lightest particle and 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} is related to parameters in the 2-body S-matrix. In some examples ρ vac = 0 \rho_{\rm vac}=0 due to a fractional supersymmetry. For massless cases, m m can be a scale of spontaneous symmetry breaking. The “cosmological constant problem” generically arises in the free field limit 𝔤 → 0 \mathfrak{g}\to 0 , thus interactions can potentially resolve the problem at least for most cases considered in this paper. We speculate on extensions of these results to 4 spacetime dimensions and propose ρ vac = m 4 / 2 ​ 𝔤 \rho_{\rm vac}=m^{4}/2\mathfrak{g} , however without integrability we cannot yet propose a precise manner in which to calculate 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} . Nevertheless, based on cosmological data on ρ vac \rho_{\rm vac} , if 𝔤 ∼ 1 \mathfrak{g}\sim 1 then it is worth pointing out that the lightest mass particle is on the order of experimental values of proposed neutrino masses.



I
Introduction

This work concerns the proper definition of the vacuum expectation value of the stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
for integrable quantum field theory in two spacetime dimensions for flat Minkowski space. The calculations presented shed some light on the so-called cosmological constant problem, so in this Introduction let us motivate this work based on this connection. Einstein’s equations of general relativity involve the classical stress-energy tensor as a source of gravitation. It is a natural idea that in a semi-classical quantum theory the classical
T_{\mu\nu}
is replaced by its quantum vacuum expectation value
\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{0}=\langle 0|T_{\mu\nu}|0\rangle
, where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state.
Based on general coordinate invariance one expects

\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{0}=-\rho_{\rm vac}\,g_{\mu\nu}

(1)

where
g_{\mu\nu}
is the spacetime metric. In the above equation the convention is that in flat space
g_{00}=-g_{11}=-1
such that

\langle 0|T_{00}|0\rangle
is an energy density.
In its simplest form
Weinberg
, the cosmological constant problem is that the zero point energy of a
free
quantum field for a particle of mass
m
, which is a collection of harmonic oscillators of frequency
\omega_{k}=\sqrt{{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}}
, is naively given by

\rho_{\rm vac}=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\dfrac{dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,4\pi k^{2}~\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}\approx\dfrac{\Lambda^{4}}{16\pi^{2}}-\dfrac{m^{4}}{32\pi^{2}}\log(2\Lambda/m)

(2)

where
\Lambda
is an ultraviolet cutoff and we have assumed
\Lambda\gg m
. The problem is that for some reasonable choices of
\Lambda
, such as the Planck scale, or perhaps of lower scales based on spontaneous symmetry breaking,
\rho_{\rm vac}
is huge. Depending on definitions and comparison to data,
\rho_{\rm vac}
is off by perhaps over 100 orders of magnitude compared to experimental cosmological values. Nature is very strongly suggesting that this is likely a major conceptual problem. Indeed there are many issues with the above calculation.
First is that perhaps the zero point energy is not a source of gravitation at all, since in both classical and quantum physics without gravity the zero point energy is not absolutely defined.
It is also possible that the observed small cosmological constant does not come from
\langle 0|T_{\mu\nu}|0\rangle
in the first place, but rather from something not understood in classical gravity.
We take the conventional point of view that
\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle_{0}
can be well defined, and that all contributions to
T_{\mu\nu}
gravitate, and thus focus on a proper physical definition of
\rho_{\rm vac}
. In quantum field theory (QFT) one is accustomed to dealing with ultraviolet (UV) divergences as
\Lambda\to\infty
with various regularization and renormalization methods, which suggests that the computation in (
2
) is not physically meaningful by itself, if at all, and this is the attitude that we take here.

Henceforth, we depart from cosmology per se, and focus on defining
\rho_{\rm vac}
for integrable QFT’s in 2 spacetime dimensions without gravity, as this could provide insights on the 4d problem. The above discussion based on (
1
) is essentially decoupled from gravity.
We thus ignore gravity and focus on defining and calculating
\rho_{\rm vac}
purely in quantum field theory. In 2d the analog of (
2
) suffers from the same problems, in that it is also divergent for a free massive particle:

\rho_{\rm vac}=2\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\dfrac{dk}{2\pi}~\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{k^{2}+m^{2}}\approx~\dfrac{\Lambda^{2}}{4\pi}+\dfrac{m^{2}}{4\pi}\log(2\Lambda/m).

(3)

In the Concluding Remarks section we return to 4d and speculate on experimental data for
\rho_{\rm vac}
in the context of real cosmology.

The main result of this paper is that interactions in QFT can potentially fix the above problems. Although the problems with
equations (
2
) and (
3
) do not involve interactions, the idea is that proper regularization procedures for interacting QFTs can eliminate divergences in the non-interacting part of
\rho_{\rm vac}
in a non-perturbative manner. In 2d we propose that

\rho_{\rm vac}=-\dfrac{m^{2}}{2\mathfrak{g}}

(4)

where
\mathfrak{g}
is a dimensionless interaction coupling which goes to zero in the free field limit, and
m
is a fundamental physical mass scale, which we will define in some specific examples in the sequel.
That
\mathfrak{g}
is a coupling is clear for instance in the sinh-Gordon model (see equation (
30
)). Since
\rho_{\rm vac}\propto 1/\mathfrak{g}
, this a non-perturbative result. In the free field limit as

\mathfrak{g}\to 0
one recovers
\rho_{\rm vac}\to\infty
which is consistent with the above discussion of the cosmological constant problem for free theories. We will present many illustrative examples. For the sine-Gordon model, we see that
\rho_{\rm vac}
can diverge at special values of the coupling where the theory is not free, but this arrises due to a kind of
“resonance” of conformal perturbation theory with
\rho_{\rm vac}
, as we will explain. For massive cases,
m
is the
lightest
mass particle and

\mathfrak{g}
will be related to trigonometric sums over certain resonance pole angles in 2-body S-matrices.
As it turns out
\rho_{\rm vac}
can be positive, negative or zero, depending on the model. In the sequel we may refer to
\rho_{\rm vac}
in the above 2d equation as the “cosmological constant”, although this is somewhat of an abuse of terminology since there is little 4d cosmological about it.

The word “mingling” in the title refers to the idea that although
\rho_{\rm vac}
has low energy implications, it is inaccessible by perturbative analysis around the low energy infrared (IR) theory. It must be computed in the UV limit, but even in this limit it is inaccessible by a perturbative analysis aboout the UV conformal field theory (CFT), as will be clear in the sequel. We will actually need an IR regulator corresponding to finite volume to ultimately calculate

\rho_{\rm vac}
. Thus
\rho_{\rm vac}
corresponds to an energy scale somewhere between the IR and UV limits which is non-perturbative and difficult to analyze. The integrability in 2d will allow calculation of
\mathfrak{g}
and explain the meaning of
m
.

We will consider a wide variety of theories that lead to (
4
).
For simplicity, we mainly consider examples with only 1 or 2 particles with
diagonal scattering, except for the sine-Gordon model which can have many bound states (breathers) and the scattering is generally non-diagonal. This limitation still allows us to explore several kinds of theories with rather different properties. In Section II we consider massive theories which are relevant perturbations of a UV conformal field theory, in particular the sinh and sine Gordon models, and integrable perturbations of the Yang-Lee and 3 state Potts models. This section relies heavily on work of Al. Zamolodchikov
ZamoTBA
and is essentially a reinterpretation of some of his results, in particular the
c_{\rm bulk}
term and its relation to
\rho_{\rm vac}
.
In this context, although the existence of
c_{\rm bulk}
has been recognized and studied, to our knowledge its interpretation as a 2d analog of a cosmological constant,
i.e.
consistent with (
1
), has not been discussed in the literature, although the connection we make is quite straightforward.
This is perhaps due to the fact that previous literature was largely focussed on the trace of
T_{\mu\nu}
rather than its separate energy density and pressure components as in (
6
).
The next two sections present more original results.
In Section III we study the irrelevant
T\overline{T}
deformations of an infrared CFT, which is a theory of massless particles, and may or may not have a UV limit depending on the sign of
\mathfrak{g}
. In Section IV we consider massless flows from a UV CFT to a non-trivial IR CFT and present a new general formula for
\rho_{\rm vac}
in this context; it appears to differ from the one known result in the literature of the flow from the tricritical Ising to Ising model by a factor of
2
.
In all cases we present several examples. In the massless cases
m
in (
4
) refers to a fundamental energy scale, such as a scale of spontaneous (super) symmetry breaking. In the Concluding Remarks we speculate on the implications of our results for 4 spacetime dimensions and compare with data on the cosmological constant.

II
Massive case

II.1
General considerations

In order to make sense of
\rho_{\rm vac}
we consider the QFT in euclidean space, specifically on a cylinder of infinite length and finite circumference
R
.
This is an infrared regularization since it corresponds to a finite volume
R
. The free energy density takes the form

{\cal F}(R)=E(R)/R
where
E(R)
is the ground state energy. In thermal field theory,
R=1/T
where
T
is the temperature.
In 2d, it is conventional to express
E(R)
in terms of an effective Virasoro central charge
c(mR)
:

E(R)=-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\,\dfrac{c(mR)}{R}

(5)

where at renormalization group fixed points, which are CFTs,
c(mR)
is a constant independent of
R
.
For the massive case, in the above equation
m
is a physical mass of a particle. In this euclidean space, let
t
denote the spatial coordinate along the circumference and
x
the coordinate along the length of the cylinder.
Then standard statistical mechanics implies

\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\langle T_{tt}\rangle=\dfrac{dE(R)}{dR}={\cal E},~~~~~~\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\langle T_{xx}\rangle=\dfrac{E(R)}{R}={\cal F}=-p

(6)

where
{\cal E}
is the energy density and
p
the pressure, and we have used that
p=-{\cal F}
. The above implies
\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle/2\pi={\rm diag}({\cal E},-p)
1
1

1

The extra factors of
2\pi
are due to the standard CFT normalization for the stress energy tensor in 2d.
2
2

2

One can check the signs in
(
6
) by considering
{\cal E}
and
p
for the CFT of massless particles, which is the analog of black body radiation in 2d.
.
Note that
\langle T_{ij}\rangle
is not proportional to
\delta_{ij}
in general, that is to say (
1
) is violated, which is obviously due to the
R
dependence.
Given the form of
\langle T_{xx}\rangle
, we define
\rho_{\rm vac}
via the pressure
p
:

\rho_{\rm vac}=-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\langle T_{xx}\rangle_{0}.

(7)

In the above equation the
0
subscript refers to the vacuum expectation value
\langle 0|T_{xx}|0\rangle
, which is the
R
-independent part of

\langle T_{xx}\rangle
. Extracting this
R
-independent contribution is not so straightforward as we will see, but can be done in the models considered here.

Henceforth let us define the dimensionless quantity
r=mR
.
At low energies, i.e. in the IR as
r\to\infty
one expects
c(r)\to 0
since the particles are effectively infinitely massive and the IR CFT is empty. On the other hand, if the UV theory is defined, i.e. “UV complete”, then it can be formulated as a perturbation of a UV CFT with central charge
c_{\rm UV}
by a relevant operator of dimension
\Gamma_{\rm UV}
. One then can anticipate that in the UV as
r\to 0
:

\lim_{r\to 0}c(r)=c_{\rm UV}+c_{\rm bulk}+c_{\rm pert}.

(8)

Above,
c_{\rm pert}
are terms that can in principle be computed in conformal perturbation theory about the UV,
and is an expansion in powers of
r^{2-\Gamma_{\rm UV}}
.
What is of interest in the present work is the
c_{\rm bulk}\propto r^{2}
term which corresponds to
an
R
-independent term in
\langle T_{xx}\rangle
, which we identify with
\langle 0|T_{xx}|0\rangle
. In other words the
c_{\rm bulk}
term implies a vacuum expectation of the
stress energy tensor that is consistent with a cosmological constant, i.e. (
1
). It is difficult to compute since it is invisible in the IR where
c(r)
decreases exponentially, and in the UV it can mingle with the
c_{\rm pert}
terms. For the moment we parameterize it in terms of a constant
b
:

c_{\rm bulk}=-b\,r^{2}/2\pi
.
Then based on (
6
) one has

\langle T_{tt}\rangle_{0}=\langle T_{xx}\rangle_{0}=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\,b\,m^{2}.

(9)

It is important to note that this result is of the form
\langle T_{ij}\rangle_{0}\propto\delta_{ij}
which is consistent with a cosmological constant
(
1
) where
p=-{\cal E}
; we mention this fact since other kinds of calculations analogous to (
2
) can contradict
(
1
). See for instance
Martin
and references therein.
Let us now define
b\equiv 6/\mathfrak{g}
.
The equation (
9
) then leads to the formula in the Introduction

c_{\rm bulk}=-\dfrac{3r^{2}}{\pi\mathfrak{g}}~~~~\Longrightarrow~~~\rho_{\rm vac}=-\dfrac{m^{2}}{2\mathfrak{g}}.

(10)

For CFTs,
\rho_{\rm vac}=0
. We emphasize that we view the introduction of
r
and
c(r)
as essentially a tool to extract

\rho_{\rm vac}
, however in principle it should be possible to define
\rho_{\rm vac}
directly from the zero temperature S-matrix, but we do not attempt this here.

There is an important caveat to the potential validity of (
10
). As stated above,
c_{\rm pert}
is expected to be
an expansion in
r^{2-\Gamma_{\rm UV}}
. Due to conformal bootstrap selection rules, i.e. the OPE structure,

is typically a sum of terms
proportional to
r^{2n(2-\Gamma_{\rm UV})}
with
n=1,2,3,\ldots
.
Now if
\Gamma_{\rm UV}<1
, then

is the leading term as

On the other hand, for

, the terms in

and

are more entangled since

is no longer the leading term and

is difficult to extract numerically. More importantly, there exists the possibility of a resonance in conformal perturbation theory where one term in
c_{\rm pert}
goes exactly as

. This occurs for rational values of

:

\Gamma_{\rm UV}=2-1/n,~~~~n=1,2,3,\ldots~~~~({\rm resonance}).

(11)

This resonance phenomenon was understood by Al. Zamolodchikov for “RSOS” perturbations of minimal models of CFT
ZamoRSOS

If this occurs then
\rho_{\rm vac}
may still diverge due to divergent terms in

However we wish to emphasize that this resonance phenomenon is specific to the TBA at finite
R
where the term of interest

can mix with
c_{\rm pert}
, whereas
\rho_{\rm vac}
has a meaning in the pure QFT without this complicated technicality; i.e. the TBA is used here as a tool to extract the independently meaningful
\rho_{\rm vac}

Henceforth we present results for
\mathfrak{g}
(rather than
b
) for various models based on the definition (
10
).
Below we will show that
\mathfrak{g}
is essentially an effective coupling of the underlying QFT. For many specific integrable models
this coupling is a fixed number with no variable parameters, which we will calculate in some examples, although they are often related to models with variable couplings but at fixed rational values. For other models, such as the sinh-Gordon and sine-Gordon models
considered below, it does indeed depend on
a continuously variable coupling. See for instance equations (
29
) and (
34

II.2
Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz for Integrable theories

For simplicity let us first assume the spectrum consists of a single massive particle of mass
m
and the QFT is integrable.
The energy and momentum of this particle can be parametrized in terms of a rapidity
\theta
:

E=m\cosh\theta,~~~~~p=m\sinh\theta.

(12)

Al. Zamolodchikov obtained seminal results for
E(R)
based on the Thermodynamic Bethe Ansatz (TBA)
ZamoTBA
by extending results of Yang and Yang
YangYang
to relativistic theories. The rest of this section relies primarily on Zamolodchikov’s analysis,
thus let us present these TBA equations.
Let
S(\theta)
denote the 2-body S-matrix and define the kernel

G(\theta)=-i\partial_{\theta}\log S(\theta).

(13)

Unitarity
S(\theta)S(-\theta)=1
implies
G(-\theta)=G(\theta)
.
Introduce a so-called pseudo-energy
\varepsilon(\theta)
which is a solution to the integral equation:

\varepsilon(\theta)=r\,\cosh\theta-G\star\log\left(1+e^{-\varepsilon}\right)

(14)

where we have defined

\left(G\star f\right)(\theta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\theta^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,G(\theta-\theta^{\prime})f(\theta^{\prime})

for an arbitrary function
f(\theta)
.
Then the
r
dependent central charge in (
5
) is given by

c(r)=\dfrac{3}{\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta~(r\cosh\theta)\,\log\left(1+e^{-\varepsilon(\theta)}\right).

(15)

As expected, in the IR limit
r\to\infty
,
c(r)\to 0
exponentially as
e^{-mr}
. This is easily seen from the TBA equation as the first iterative
correction to
\varepsilon(\theta)
comes from replacing
\varepsilon(\theta)
with
r\cosh\theta
in the
G\star\log
term on the RHS of (
14
),
which leads to various Bessel functions. The UV limit
r\to 0
is much more delicate.
In the range

-\log(2/r)\ll\theta\ll\log(2/r)
,
\varepsilon(\theta)\approx\varepsilon_{0}
where
\varepsilon_{0}
is a constant satisfying the “plateaux” equation:

\varepsilon_{0}=-\,k\,\log\left(1+e^{-\varepsilon_{0}}\right),~~~~~{\rm with}~~~~~k=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,G(\theta).

(16)

The UV central charge
c_{\rm UV}
is a function of
\varepsilon_{0}
which can be expressed in terms of the Roger’s dilogarithm. For our purposes the TBA also gives

c_{\rm bulk}
as discovered in
ZamoTBA
, thus let us present this result. If the kernel behaves as

G(\theta)=-\mathfrak{g}\,e^{-|\theta|}+O(e^{-2|\theta|})

(17)

then

c_{\rm bulk}=-\dfrac{3r^{2}}{\pi\mathfrak{g}}

(18)

which implies the result (
10
).

The constant
\mathfrak{g}
is related to certain resonance angles in the S-matrix
S(\theta)
. The basic building blocks of
S(\theta)

are factors of
f_{\alpha}(\theta)
:

S(\theta)=\prod_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}f_{\alpha}(\theta)

(19)

where

f_{\alpha}(\theta)=\dfrac{\sinh\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\theta+i\pi\alpha\right)}{\sinh\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\theta-i\pi\alpha\right)}

(20)

and
{\cal A}
is a finite set of
\alpha
’s.
One has

-i\partial_{\theta}\log f_{\alpha}(\theta)=-\dfrac{\sin\pi\alpha}{\cosh\theta-\cos\pi\alpha}

(21)

which leads to

\mathfrak{g}=2\sum_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}\sin\pi\alpha.

(22)

The angles
\pi\alpha
correspond to poles in the complex
\theta
plane for the S-matrices.

A remarkable result is that for many particles of mass
m_{a}
,
a=1,2,3,\ldots
with diagonal scattering, due to the S-matrix bootstrap, the formula (
18
) holds with
m=m_{1}
the
lightest
particle and

\mathfrak{g}
given by the sum over resonance angles of the S-matrix
S_{11}(\theta)
for the scattering of
m_{1}
with itself.
Specifically, if
G_{11}=-i\partial_{\theta}\log S_{11}(\theta)
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is defined by
G_{11}(\theta)=-\mathfrak{g}\,e^{-|\theta|}+\ldots
as in
(
17
). We thus arrive at

\rho_{\rm vac}=-\dfrac{m_{1}^{2}}{2\,\mathfrak{g}}~.

(23)

The derivation of these results were well explained in
KlassenMelzer
;
MussardoBook
thus we don’t repeat the derivation here.
Generalizations of the above formula for massless theories will be derived below, and this will give the reader an idea of how
formulas such as (
23
) are obtained.
We point out that the above formula (
23
) has not been proven in complete generality; it has been proven for diagonal S-matrices under the assumption that there are no
r^{2}
terms in
c_{\rm pert}
, namely that the resonance condition (
11
) does not occur. Below we will consider the sine-Gordon example where it does occur in a regime of the coupling.

II.3
Examples

We present 4 examples to illustrate that the cosmological constant
\rho_{\rm vac}
can be positive, negative, or zero, and this distinction is not obviously related to the unitarity of the model, nor does it require spontaneous symmetry breaking.
The first example is the sinh-Gordon model, and the second is the sine-Gordon theory.
The next two examples were already considered in
ZamoTBA

In two of these examples the S-matrix is a product of two

(25)

\mathfrak{g}=4\sin\pi\alpha.

Sine-Gordon model.
This is a unitary theory with

It can be defined by the following action for a single scalar field:

In this section we consider so-called

Every theory has a conserved stress-energy tensor,

For a CFT,
\Theta=0

formally denote the action for a CFT

Since

Being an irrelevant perturbation, the above action is not renormalizable since additional higher irrelevant operators are generated in perturbation theory. Thus to be more precise

\dfrac{d{\cal S}_{\alpha}}{d\alpha}=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int d^{2}x\,\,(T\overline{T})_{\alpha}

Tateo1
;
SmirnovZ
.
The function

can be interpreted as an effective action.

is a fundamental mass scale indicative of the breaking of conformal invariance.

with

In the UV limit

(47)

Depending on the sign of

V
Concluding remarks

We have shown that the analog of the cosmological constant

can be properly defined in two spacetime dimensions
for a variety of types of integrable quantum field theories. This requires interactions and is non-perturbative in that
\rho_{\rm vac}\propto 1/\mathfrak{g}
where

is a generalized coupling which is generally zero in the free field limit. It can be positive, negative or zero depending on the model. The main tools we used to arrive at this conclusion was integrability with a finite volume
