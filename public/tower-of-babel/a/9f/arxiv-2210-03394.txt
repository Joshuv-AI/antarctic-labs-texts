One-Wayness in Quantum Cryptography
Tomoyuki Morimae, Takashi Yamakawa

Abstract
The existence of one-way functions is one of the most fundamental assumptions in classical cryptography. In the quantum world, on the other hand, there are evidences that some cryptographic primitives can exist even if one-way functions do not exist [Kretschmer, TQC 2021; Morimae and Yamakawa, CRYPTO 2022; Ananth, Qian, and Yuen, CRYPTO 2022]. We therefore have the following important open problem in quantum cryptography: What is the most fundamental assumption in quantum cryptography? In this direction, [Brakerski, Canetti, and Qian, ITCS 2023] recently defined a notion called EFI pairs, which are pairs of efficiently generatable states that are statistically distinguishable but computationally indistinguishable, and showed its equivalence with some cryptographic primitives including commitments, oblivious transfer, and general multi-party computations. However, their work focuses on decision-type primitives and does not cover search-type primitives like quantum money and digital signatures. In this paper, we study properties of one-way state generators (OWSGs), which are a quantum analogue of one-way functions proposed by Morimae and Yamakawa. We first revisit the definition of OWSGs and generalize it by allowing mixed output states. Then we show the following results. 1. We define a weaker version of OWSGs, which we call weak OWSGs, and show that they are equivalent to OWSGs. It is a quantum analogue of the amplification theorem for classical weak one-way functions. 2. (Bounded-time-secure) quantum digital signatures with quantum public keys are equivalent to OWSGs. 3. Private-key quantum money schemes (with pure money states) imply OWSGs. 4. Quantum pseudo one-time pad schemes imply both OWSGs and EFI pairs. For EFI pairs, single-copy security suffices. 5. We introduce an incomparable variant of OWSGs, which we call secretly-verifiable and statistically-invertible OWSGs, and show that they are equivalent to EFI pairs.

Introduction

One-way functions (OWFs) are functions that are easy to compute but hard to invert.
The existence of OWFs is one of the most fundamental assumptions in classical cryptography.
OWFs are equivalent to many cryptographic primitives,
such as commitments, digital signatures, pseudorandom generators (PRGs),
symmetric-key encryption (SKE), and zero-knowledge, etc. Moreover, almost all other cryptographic primitives,
such as collision-resistant hashes,
public-key encryption (PKE),
oblivious transfer (OT), multi-party computations (MPCs), etc., imply OWFs.
In the quantum world, on the other hand, it seems that OWFs are not necessarily the most fundamental
element. In fact, recently, several quantum cryptographic primitives, such as commitments, (one-time secure) digital signatures,
quantum pseudo one-time pad (QPOTP)

could exist even if all quantum-secure (classical) cryptographic primitives including OWFs are broken.

We therefore have the following important open problem in quantum cryptography:

Question 1:

What is the most fundamental assumption in quantum cryptography?

In classical cryptography,

In the canonical form of quantum commitments

It is therefore natural to ask the following question.

Question 2:

Which other quantum cryptographic primitives imply EFI pairs?

PRSGs and EFI pairs are “decision type” primitives, which correspond to PRGs in classical cryptography.
An example of the other type of primitives, namely, “search type” one in classical cryptography, is OWFs.
Recently, a quantum analogue of OWFs, so called one-way states generators (OWSGs), are introduced

A OWSG is a QPT algorithm that, on input a classical bit string (key)

The authors showed that OWSGs are implied by PRSGs, and that OWSGs imply (one-time secure) quantum digital signatures with quantum public keys.
In classical cryptography, OWFs are connected to many cryptographic primitives. We are therefore interested in the following question.

Question 3:

Which quantum cryptographic primitives are related to OWSGs?

In classical cryptography, PRGs (i.e., a decision-type primitive) and OWFs (i.e., a search-type primitive) are
equivalent. In quantum cryptography, on the other hand, we do not know whether
OWSGs and EFI pairs (or PRSGs) are equivalent or not.
We therefore have the following open problem.

Question 4:

Are OWSGs and EFI pairs (or PRSGs) equivalent?

1.2
Concurrent Work

A concurrent work by Cao and Xue
[
CX22
]
also studies OWSGs.
In particular, they also show the equivalence between weak OWSGs and OWSGs. Though they consider OWSGs with pure state outputs as defined in
[
MY22
]
, it is likely that their proof extends to the mixed state version as well.
Interestingly, the proof strategies are different in our and their works.
Their proof is based on the classical proof of the equivalence between the weak one-way functions and one-way functions
[
Yao82
,
Gol01
]
. On the other hand, our proof is based on the amplification theorem for weakly verifiable puzzles
[
CHS05
]
. Though their proof is simpler, an advantage of our approach is that it captures more general settings. For example, our proof also works for secretly-verifiable OWSGs (
Definition

7.1
). Their approach seems to rely on public verifiability in an essential manner and not applicable to secretly-verifiable OWSGs.

Besides the equivalence between weak OWSGs and OWSGs, there is no other overlap between our and their results.

In this subsection,

Definition 3.6 (Weak one-way states generators (wOWSGs)).

A weak one-way states generator (wOWSG) is a tuple of algorithms

Theorem 3.7.

OWSGs exist if and only if wOWSGs exist.

5.1

(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Mint},\mathsf{Ver})
such that

•

\lambda

k
.

•

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input

k

m

\$_{k}
.

•

\mathsf{Ver}(k,\rho)\to\top/\bot:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input
k
and a quantum
state
\rho
, outputs
\top/\bot
.

We require the following correctness and security.

Correctness:

\displaystyle\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,\$_{k}):k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}),\$_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{Mint}(k)]\geq 1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

Security:

For any QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
and any polynomial

,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{Count}(k,\xi)\geq t+1:k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}),\$_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{Mint}(k),\xi\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(1^{\lambda},\$_{k}^{\otimes t})]\leq{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda),

\xi
is a quantum state on
\ell
registers,
R_{1},...,R_{\ell}
, each of which is of
m
qubits,
and
\mathsf{Count}
is the following QPT algorithm:
on input
\xi

\top/\bot\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,\xi_{j})
for each

,
and outputs the total number of
\top
.

Remark

Private-key quantum money schemes are

from PRSGs
[

]
.

The correctness is clear. Let us show the security. Assume that it is not secure.

\displaystyle 1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{QM}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{QM}.\mathsf{Ver}(k,|\$_{k}\rangle)]

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{QM}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\frac{1}{8p}\Big)\Big(1-\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{QM}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Big).

Here, the first inequality is from the correctness of the quantum money scheme.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p}+1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)-1

7.1
Definition of SV-SI-OWSGs

We first define secretly-verifiable OWSGs (SV-OWSGs) as follows.

Definition 7.1 (Secretly-verifiable OWSGs (SV-OWSGs)).

A secretly-verifiable OWSG (SV-OWSG) is a set of algorithms
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
as follows.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})\to k:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input the security parameter
\lambda
, outputs a key
k\in\{0,1\}^{\kappa}
.

•

\mathsf{StateGen}(k)\to\phi_{k}:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input
k
, outputs an
m
-qubit state
\phi_{k}
.

•

\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},k)\to\top/\bot:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input
k
and
k^{\prime}
, outputs
\top/\bot
.

We require the following two properties.

Correctness:

\displaystyle\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k):k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\geq 1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

Security:

For any QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
and any polynomial
t
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},k):k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}),\phi_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{StateGen}(k),k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\leq{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

The following lemma shows that, without loss of generality,
\mathsf{Ver}
can be replaced with the algorithm
of just checking whether
k=k^{\prime}
or not.

Lemma 7.2.

Let
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
be a SV-OWSG.
Then, the following SV-OWSG
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime})
exists.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime}
are the same as
\mathsf{KeyGen}
and
\mathsf{StateGen}
, respectively.

•

\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime}(k^{\prime},k)\to\top/\bot:

On input
k
and
k^{\prime}
, output
\top
if
k=k^{\prime}
.
Otherwise, output
\bot
.

Proof.

The correctness of
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime})
is clear.
Let us show the security. Assume that it is not secure.
Then there exist a QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
, a polynomial
t
, and a polynomial
p
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\geq\frac{1}{p}.

Define the set

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\Big\{k~\Big|~\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\geq\frac{1}{2p}\Big\}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]>\frac{1}{2p}.

This is because

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\notin S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]+\frac{1}{2p}.

Also define the set

\displaystyle T\coloneqq\Big\{k~\Big|~\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]\geq 1-\frac{1}{p}\Big\}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]>1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

This is because

\displaystyle 1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\notin T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\Big(1-\sum_{k\in T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Big).

Here, the first inequality is from the correctness of
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
.
From the union bound, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S\cap T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]>\frac{1}{2p}-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

From the
\mathcal{A}
, we construct a QPT adversary that breaks the security of
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
as follows:
On input
\phi_{k}^{\otimes t}
, run
k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})
.
Output
k^{\prime}
.
Then, the probability that
\mathcal{B}
breaks the security is

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},k)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S\cap T}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,k)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{2p}-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)\Big),

which is non-negligible.
Therefore
\mathcal{B}
breaks the security of

(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
.
∎

Note that statistically-secure SV-OWSGs are easy to realize.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}):

\mathsf{StateGen}(k):

Output

We therefore need a constraint to have a meaningful primitive.
We define secretly-verifiable and statistically-invertible OWSGs (SV-SI-OWSGs) as follows.

Definition 7.3 (Secretly-verifiable and statistically-invertible OWSGs (SV-SI-OWSGs)).

A secretly-verifiable and statistically-invertible OWSG (SV-SI-OWSG) is a set of algorithms
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen})
as follows.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})\to k:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input the security parameter
\lambda

k\in\{0,1\}^{\kappa}
.

•

\mathsf{StateGen}(k)\to\phi_{k}:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input
k

m
-qubit state
\phi_{k}

We require the following two properties.

Statistical invertibility:

There exists a polynomial
p

k^{\prime}
(

\frac{1}{2}\|\phi_{k}-\phi_{k^{\prime}}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{p}.

\mathcal{A}
and any polynomial

,

\displaystyle\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes t}):k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}),\phi_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{StateGen}(k)]\leq{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda).

\frac{1}{{\mathrm{poly}}(\lambda)}

Lemma 7.4.

If a SV-SI-OWSG exists

\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime}(k)\to\phi_{k}^{\prime}:

2pq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\phi_{k}^{\prime}-\phi_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes 2pqt}):k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda}),\phi_{k}\leftarrow\mathsf{StateGen}(k)]

\displaystyle{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)

\mathcal{A}

Appendix A
PRSGs

PRSGs are defined as follows.

Definition A.1 (Pseudorandom quantum states generators (PRSGs)
[
JLS18
]
).

A pseudorandom quantum states generator (PRSG) is a set of algorithms
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen})
as follows.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})\to k:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input the security parameter
\lambda
, outputs a classical key
k
.

•

\mathsf{StateGen}(k)\to|\phi_{k}\rangle:

It is a QPT algorithm that, on input
k
,
outputs an
m
-qubit quantum state
|\phi_{k}\rangle
.

We require the following security:
For any polynomial
t
and any QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle|\Pr_{k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})}[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]-\Pr_{|\psi\rangle\leftarrow\mu_{m}}[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\psi\rangle^{\otimes t})]|\leq{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda),

where
\mu_{m}
is the Haar measure on
m
-qubit states.

Remark
A.2.

In the original definition of PRSGs
[
JLS18
]
, the classical key
k
is uniformly sampled at random.
We use a more general definition where
k
is sampled by a QPT algorithm, which was introduced in
[
BS19
]
.

Remark
A.3.

Note that in the above definition, the outputs of
\mathsf{StateGen}
are assumed to be pure.
It could be possible to consider mixed states, but anyway the states have to be negligibly close to pure states
(except for a negligible fraction of
k
).
25
25

25

Consider the case
t=2
, and consider a QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
that, given
\phi_{k}^{\otimes 2}
or
|\psi\rangle^{\otimes 2}
, runs
the SWAP test on them. Then,

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{2^{\lambda}}\sum_{k}\Pr[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\phi_{k}^{\otimes 2})]-\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\leftarrow\mu_{m}}\Pr[1\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\psi\rangle^{\otimes 2})]\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{2^{\lambda}}\sum_{k}\frac{1+\mbox{Tr}(\phi_{k}^{2})}{2}-\frac{1+1}{2}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{1}{2^{\lambda}}\sum_{k}\mbox{Tr}(\phi_{k}^{2})-1\Big|,

which has to be negligible.
Therefore,

\mbox{Tr}(\phi_{k}^{2})
is negligibly close to 1 (except for a negligible fraction of
k
).

We therefore, for simplicity, assume that the outputs of
\mathsf{StateGen}
are pure.
In that case,
\mathsf{StateGen}
runs as follows: apply a QPT unitary
U
on
|k\rangle|0...0\rangle
to generate

U(|k\rangle|0...0\rangle)=|\phi_{k}\rangle\otimes|\eta_{k}\rangle
, and output
|\phi_{k}\rangle
. Note that the existance of
the “junk” register
|\eta_{k}\rangle
is essential, because otherwise it is not secure.
26
26

26

Consider a QPT adversary who applies
U^{\dagger}
on each copy of the received states, and measures them in the computational
basis. If the same
k
is obtained many times, the adversary concludes that
the states are copies of pseudorandom states.

(The simplest example of the junk register
|\eta_{k}\rangle
would be
|\eta_{k}\rangle=|k\rangle
,
i.e.,
U(|k\rangle|0...0\rangle)=|k\rangle\otimes|\phi_{k}\rangle
.
In that case, it is often convenient to consider that

\mathsf{StateGen}
applies a QPT unitary
U_{k}
that depends on
k
on the state
|0...0\rangle
,
and outputs
|\phi_{k}\rangle\coloneqq U_{k}|0...0\rangle

Remark
A.4.

PRSGs can be constructed from (quantum-secure) one-way functions
[
JLS18
,
BS19
,
BS20
]
.

Remark
A.5.

[

On the other hand,

Lemma B.1.

Let
(\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{StateGen},\mathsf{Ver})
be a OWSG that satisfies the following.

•

All
\phi_{k}
are pure.

•

\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k,\phi_{k})]\geq 1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)

for all
k
.

Then, the following OWSG
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime})
exists.

•

\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime}
are the same as
\mathsf{KeyGen}
and
\mathsf{StateGen}
, respectively.

•

\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime}(k^{\prime},\phi_{k})
is the following algorithm.
Measure
\phi_{k}
with the basis
\{|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\phi_{k^{\prime}}|,I-|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\phi_{k^{\prime}}|\}
,
and output
\top
if the first result is obtained.
Otherwise, output
\bot
.

Proof.

The correctness of
(\mathsf{KeyGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{StateGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime})
is clear.
Let us show the security.
Assume that it is not secure.
Then, there exists a QPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
, a polynomial
t
, and a polynomial
p
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]|\langle\phi_{k}|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{p}.

If we define

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\Big\{(k,k^{\prime})~\Big|~|\langle\phi_{k}|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2p}\Big\},

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]\geq\frac{1}{2p}.

From the
\mathcal{A}
, we construct a QPT adversary
\mathcal{B}
that breaks the security of the original OWSG as follows:
On input
|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t}
, run
k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})
, and output
k^{\prime}
.
Then the probability that
\mathcal{B}
breaks the original OWSG is

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},|\phi_{k}\rangle)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},|\phi_{k}\rangle)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})\in S}\Pr[k\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(1^{\lambda})]\Pr[k^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(|\phi_{k}\rangle^{\otimes t})]\Big(\Pr[\top\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle)]-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2p}}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p}\Big(1-{\mathsf{negl}}(\lambda)-\Big(1-\frac{1}{4p}\Big)\Big),

which is non-negligible. Therefore the
\mathcal{B}

breaks the security of the original OWSG.
Here, in the second inequality, we have used the fact that for any
(k,k^{\prime})\in S
,
|\langle\phi_{k}|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2p}
,
which means that
\mbox{Tr}(\Pi|\phi_{k}\rangle\langle\phi_{k}|)-\mbox{Tr}(\Pi|\phi_{k^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\phi_{k^{\prime}}|)\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2p}}

for any POVM element
\Pi
.
In the last inequality, we have used Bernoulli’s inequality,
(1+x)^{r}\leq 1+rx
for any
0\leq r\leq 1
and
x\geq-1
.

Remark
E.7.

One may think that this theorem is surprisingly strong because it is unlikely that SKE implies PKE in the classical setting
[
IR90
]
. However, we would like to point out that QPKE as defined in
[
KKNY12
]
is not as useful as classical PKE because public keys are quantum. Their security model assumes that public keys are delivered to senders without being forged. This itself is the same as classical PKE, but the crucial difference is that forgery of public keys can be prevented by additionally using digital signatures in the classical setting, but that is not possible for QPKE because we cannot sign on quantum messages

]
. In fact, the assumption that the adversary cannot forge public keys mean that it also cannot eavesdrop it because eavesdropping may change the state of the public key. Thus, their security model essentially assumes a secure channel to send public keys to the sender. If there is such a secure channel, we could simply send a key for SKE through that channel. Thus, this theorem should not be understood as a new useful feasibility result on PKE.
We remark that the construction does not work if we consider QPKE with classical public keys
[

]
.
We conjecture that it is impossible to construct QPKE with classical public keys from QSKE under a certain class of black-box constructions.

(k_{v+1},\ldots,k_{n})\leftarrow\mathsf{CheckGen}^{n-v}(1^{\lambda})\\
\mathsf{puz}_{j}^{\otimes t}\leftarrow\mathsf{PuzzleGen}^{\otimes t}(k_{j})\text{~for~}j\in[n]\\
(\mathsf{ans}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{ans}_{n})\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{puz}_{1}^{\otimes t},\ldots,\mathsf{puz}_{n}^{\otimes t})\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle a(k)\coloneqq\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\forall j\in\{v+1,\ldots,n\}\\
\mathsf{Ver}(\mathsf{ans}_{j},k_{j})=\top\end{array}:\begin{array}[]{l}k_{v}\coloneqq k\\
(k_{v+1},\ldots,k_{n})\leftarrow\mathsf{CheckGen}^{n-v}(1^{\lambda})\\
\mathsf{puz}_{j}^{\otimes t}\leftarrow\mathsf{PuzzleGen}^{\otimes t}(k_{j})\text{~for~}j\in[n]\\
(\mathsf{ans}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{ans}_{n})\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{puz}_{1}^{\otimes t},\ldots,\mathsf{puz}_{n}^{\otimes t})\end{array}\right].

Note that
\mathsf{prefix}_{v-1}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{v-1})
is fixed and hardwired in the above definitions.
By the definitions of
w(k)
,
s(k)
, and
\mathsf{rsp}(\mathsf{prefix}_{v-1})
and
Equation

27
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k}w(k)s(k)=\mathsf{rsp}(\mathsf{prefix}_{v-1})\geq\delta^{n-v+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{6q}\right).

(29)

By the definition of
E
and
a(k)
, for any
k\notin E
, we have

\displaystyle a(k)\leq\delta^{n-v}\left(1+\frac{1}{6q}\right).

(30)

By the definitions of
s(k)
and
a(k)
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}}),k)=\top|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}})\text{~does~not~abort}]=\frac{s(k)}{a(k)}.

(31)

We let

\displaystyle B\coloneqq\left\{k:s(k)<\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{6q}\right\}.

(32)

The for any
k\notin B
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}})\text{~aborts}:\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}}\leftarrow\mathsf{PuzzleGen}^{\otimes t^{\prime}}(k)]<\left(1-\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{6q}\right)^{\lceil\frac{6q\ln(6q)}{\delta^{n-v+1}}\rceil}<\frac{1}{6q}.

(33)

Next, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in B\cup E}w(k)s(k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in B}w(k)s(k)+\sum_{k\in E}w(k)s(k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in B}w(k)\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{6q}+\sum_{k\in E}w(k)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{6q}+\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{6q}=\frac{\delta^{n-v+1}}{3q}

where the second inequality follows from
Equation

32
and the third inequality follows from
Equation

28
. Combined with
Equation

29
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\notin B\cup E}w(k)s(k)\geq\delta^{n-v+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{2q}\right).

(34)

Then we have the following where
\mathsf{puz}\leftarrow\mathsf{PuzzleGen}(k)
whenever
\mathsf{puz}
appears:

\displaystyle\Pr_{k}[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}})\text{~succeeds}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w(k)\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}}),k)=\top]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\notin B\cup E}w(k)\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}})\text{~does~not~abort}]\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}}),k)=\top|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathsf{puz}^{\otimes t^{\prime}})\text{~does~not~abort}]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\notin B\cup E}w(k)\left(1-\frac{1}{6q}\right)\frac{s(k)}{a(k)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\notin B\cup E}w(k)\left(1-\frac{1}{6q}\right)\frac{s(k)}{\delta^{n-v}\left(1+\frac{1}{6q}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{1}{6q}}{\delta^{n-v}\left(1+\frac{1}{6q}\right)}\sum_{k\notin B\cup E}w(k)s(k)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\frac{1}{6q}}{\delta^{n-v}\left(1+\frac{1}{6q}\right)}\delta^{n-v+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle>\delta\left(1-\frac{5}{6q}\right)

where the second inequality follows from
Equations

31
and
33
,
the third inequality follows from
Equation

30
,
and the fourth inequality follows from
Equation

34
. As already explained, the above lower bound on the success probability suffices for completing the proof of
Theorem

3.14
.
