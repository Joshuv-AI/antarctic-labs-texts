Duality Arguments in the Analysis of a Viscoelastic Contact Problem
Piotr Bartman, Anna Ochal, Mircea Sofonea[4mm] 1 1 Doctoral School of Exact and Natural Sciences, Jagiellonian UniversityLojasiewicza 11, 30348 Krakow, Poland [4mm] 2 2 Chair of Optimization and Control, Jagiellonian UniversityLojasiewicza 6, 30348 Krakow, Poland [4mm] 3 3 Laboratoire de Mathématiques et Physique University of Perpignan Via Domitia52 Avenue Paul Alduy, 66860 Perpignan, France

Abstract
We consider a mathematical model which describes the quasistatic frictionless contact of a viscoelastic body with a rigid-plastic foundation. We describe the mechanical assumptions, list the hypotheses on the data and provide three different variational formulations of the model in which the unknowns are the displacement field, the stress field and the strain field, respectively. These formulations have a different structure. Nevertheless, we prove that they are pairwise dual of each other. Then, we deduce the unique weak solvability of the contact problem as well as the Lipschitz continuity of its weak solution with respect to the data. The proofs are based on recent results on history-dependent variational inequalities and inclusions. Finally, we present numerical simulations in the study of the contact problem, together with the corresponding mechanical interpretations.

Introduction

Contact phenomena between deformable bodies arise in industry and everyday life. They are modeled by strongly nonlinear boundary value problems which usually do not have classical solutions. Therefore, their study is made by using a variational approach, that consists to replace the strong formulation of the problem by
a weak or variational formulation, which is more convenient for mathematical analysis and numerical simulations.

The weak formulations of contact problems vary from problem to problem, from author to author and even from paper to paper.
They lead to challenging nonlinear problems which, in general, are expressed in terms of either variational and hemivariational inequalities or inclusions, including differential inclusions. Comprehensive references in the theory of variational inequalities are
[
3
,
4
]
and, more recently,
[
9
]
. There, various existence and uniqueness results are presented, obtained by using different functional arguments. Hemivariational inequalities are inequality problems governed by a locally Lipschitz continuous function. Their analysis is carried out by using arguments of pseudomonotonicity for multivalued operators combined with the properties of the generalized directional derivative and the subdiffrential in the sense of Clarke. Basic references in the field are
[
18
,
22
]
.
Finally, for the theory of differential inclusion,we mention the book
[
14
]
and the survey paper
[
29
]
. The book
[
14
]
deals with the theory of semilinear differential inclusions in infinite dimensional spaces, in a setting in which neither convexity of the map or compactness of the multi-operators is supposed. There, arguments of degree theory are used for solving operator inclusions, fixed points and optimization problems. The theory is applied to the investigation of semilinear differential inclusions in Banach spaces. In the survey paper
[
29
]

the authors discuss applications of differential and operator inclusions to some optimization and optimal control problems, including an optimal feedback control problem for a mathematical model of the motion of weakly concentrated water polymer solutions.

For most of the problems which describe the contact of a viscoelastic material, the variational formulation is given in a form of a variational inequality with time-independent unilateral constraints in which the unknown is the displacement field.
References on this topic include
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

. Nevertheless, for several problems it is more convenient to consider the stress field as the main unknown and, therefore, to obtain a variational formulation
in term of the stress field. Such a formulation is usually in a form of a variational inequality too, but it has a different structure since in this case the unilateral constraints are time-dependent. References in the field are
[
16
,
23
,
24
]
, for instance. Besides the displacement and the stress fields, the strain field can be successfully used to study various contact problems, as proved recently. Choosing the strain field as the main unknown leads to a variational formulation which is in the form of a history-dependent inclusion or a sweeping process.
Reference in the field are
[
1
,
2
,
17
,
26
]
, for instance.

The aim of this current paper is two fold.
The first one is to provide three different variational formulations for a viscoelastic contact problem (in which the unknowns are the displacement, the stress and the strain field, respectively), to prove their equivalence and their unique solvability, as well. Our proofs show that the corresponding variational formulations are pairwise dual to each other (in the sense introduced in
[
11
,
23
]
), which consists the first trait of novelty of our work.
Our second aim in this paper is to introduce a numerical approximation scheme of the problem (based on the variational formulation in displacements) and to provide numerical simulations together with the corresponding mechanical interpretations.
This represents the second novelty of the current paper.

Then, we list the hypotheses on the data.
In Section

Then, in Section

Finally, we end this paper with Section

2
Notation and preliminaries

The preliminary material we present in this section concerns basic notation, an existence and uniqueness result for a class of time-dependent inclusions, and some properties of the function spaces in Contact Mechanics. Everywhere in this section
X
represents a real Hilbert space endowed with an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
and its associated norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, and
2^{X}
denotes the set of parts of
X
.

Basic notation.
We use the notation
N_{K}
for
the outward normal cone of a nonempty closed convex subset
K\subset X
.
It is well known that

N_{K}\colon X\to 2^{X}
and, for any
u,\,f\in X
, we have

\displaystyle f\in N_{K}(u)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ u\in K,\quad(f,v-u)_{X}\leq 0\quad\text{for all}\ \,v\in K.

(2.1)

We also recall that a convex function
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be subdifferentiable (in the
sense of the convex analysis) if for any
u\in X
there exists an element
\xi\in X
such that

\varphi(v)-\varphi(u)\geq(\xi,v-u)_{X}\ \ \ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ \,v\in X.

Consider now an interval of time
[0,T]
with
T>0
. We denote by
C([0,T];X)
the space of continuous functions defined on
[0,T]
with values in
X
. Then, it is well known that

C([0,T];X)
is a Banach space
equipped with the norm

\|v\|_{C([0,T];X)}=\max_{t\in[0,T]}\,\|v(t)\|_{X}.

(2.2)

For an operator
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\colon C([0,T];X)\to C([0,T];X)
and a function
u\in C([0,T];X)
we use the shorthand notation
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t)
to
represent the value of the function
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u
at the point
t\in[0,T]
, that is,
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t):=(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u)(t)
. Moreover, if
A\colon X\to X
, then

A+\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}
will represent a shorthand notation for the operator which maps any function
u\in C([0,T];X)
to the function

t\mapsto Au(t)+\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t)\in C([0,T];X)
.

Definition 2.1
.

An operator
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\colon C([0,T];X)

\to C([0,T];X)

is said to be a history-dependent operator if there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t)-\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}v(t)\|_{X}\leq L\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)-v(s)\|_{X}\,ds\ \quad\forall\,u,\,v\in C([0,T];X),\ t\in[0,T].

History-dependent operators arise in Functional Analysis, Solid Mechanics and Contact Mechanics, as well.
General properties, examples and mechanical interpretations can be found in
[
24
]
.
An important property of history-dependent operators which will be useful in this paper is the following.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\widetilde{A}\colon X\to{X}
be a linear continuous operator such that

(\widetilde{A}u,u)_{X}\geq m\|u\|_{X}^{2}\ \ \forall\,u\in X

with some
m>0
and consider a history-dependent operator

\widetilde{\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}}\colon C([0,T];X)\to C([0,T];X)
. Then the operator
\widetilde{A}+\widetilde{\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}}\colon C([0,T];X)\to C([0,T];X)
is invertible and its inverse is of the form
\widetilde{A}^{-1}+\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}\colon C([0,T];X)\to C([0,T];X)
, where
\widetilde{A}^{-1}:X\to X
represents the inverse of the operator
\widetilde{A}
and
\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}\colon C([0,T];X)\to C([0,T];X)
is a history-dependent operator.

A proof of Theorem
2.2
can be found in
[
25
, p. 55]
, based on results on nonlinear implicit equations in Banach spaces.

History-dependent variational inequalities and inclusions.
Consider a set
K
, the operators

A
,
\mathcal{S}
, a function
f
and a set-valued mapping
\Sigma
,
which satisfy the following conditions.

(\mathcal{K})

K\subset X
is a nonempty closed convex subset.

(\mathcal{A})

(\mathcal{S})

(j)

is a convex lower semicontinuous function.

({f})

f\in C([0,T];X)
.

(\Sigma)

\Sigma\colon[0,T]\rightarrow 2^{X}
and there exist
a nonempty closed convex set
\Sigma_{0}\subset X
and a function
g\in C([0,T];X)
such that
\Sigma(t)=\Sigma_{0}+g(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

We have the following existence and uniqueness results.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume
(\mathcal{K})
,
(\mathcal{A})
,
(\mathcal{S})
,
(j)
and
(f)
.
Then, there exists a unique function
u\in C([0,T];X)
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle u(t)\in K,

\displaystyle(Au(t),v-u(t))_{X}+(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t),v-u(t))_{X}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\qquad+j(v)-j(u(t))\geq(f(t),v-u)_{X}\qquad\forall\,v\in K.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume
(\mathcal{A})
,
(\mathcal{S})
and
(\Sigma)
.
Then, there exists a unique function
u\in C([0,T];X)
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
the following inclusion holds:

-u(t)\in N_{\Sigma(t)}\big(Au(t)+\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}u(t)\big).

Theorem

represents a direct consequence of a result proved in
[

, Ch.3]
while

is a direct consequence of a result proved in

.

Function spaces.
For the contact problem we consider in this paper we introduce some specific notation we shall need in the following sections. First,
\mathbb{S}^{d}
stands for the space of second order symmetric
tensors on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
. Moreover,
``\cdot"
and
\|\cdot\|
represent the inner product and the Euclidean norm on the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, respectively.
In addition,

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
bounded domain with a
Lipschitz continuous boundary
\Gamma
.
The outward unit normal at
\Gamma
will be denoted by
\nu
, and
\Gamma_{1}
is a measurable part of
\Gamma
with positive measure.

We use the standard notation for the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces associated to
\Omega
and
\Gamma
. Typical examples are the spaces

L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
,
L^{2}(\Gamma)^{d}
and
H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
equipped with their canonical Hilbertian structure.
For an element
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
we still write
v
for the trace
\gamma\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)^{d}
and
v_{\nu}
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{\tau}
for the normal and
tangential traces on the boundary, i.e.,

v_{\nu}=\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}_{\tau}=\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-v_{\nu}\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}
. Moreover,

\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})
denotes the symmetric
part of the gradient of
v
, i.e.,

\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})=\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}+\nabla^{T}\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\big).

In addition, for a regular tensor-valued field
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{d}
we shall use
\sigma_{\nu}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}_{\tau}
for the normal and tangential components of the stress vector
\sigma
\nu
on
\Gamma
, i.e.,
\sigma_{\nu}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}_{\tau}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}-\sigma_{\nu}\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}
.

Next, for the displacement field we need the space
V
and for the stress and strain fields we need the space
Q
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle V=\{\,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}:\ \mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}\ \ {\rm on\ \ }\Gamma_{1}\,\},

\displaystyle Q=\{\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}=(\sigma_{ij}):\ \sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ \ \ \forall\,i,\,j=1,\dots,d\,\}.

The spaces
V
and
Q
are real Hilbert spaces endowed with the inner products

(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\,dx,\qquad(\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}})_{Q}=\int_{\Omega}{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\,dx}.

(2.4)

The associated norms on these spaces will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{V}
and
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
, respectively. Recall that the
completeness of the space
(V,\|\cdot\|_{V})
follows from the
assumption
{meas}\,(\Gamma_{1})>0
, which allows the use of Korn’s
inequality.
Note also that, by the definition of the inner product in the spaces
V
and
Q
, we have

\|\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\|_{V}=\|\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\|_{Q}\ \ \ \ \mbox{for all}\ \ \mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V

(2.5)

and, using the Sobolev
theorem, we deduce that

\|\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)^{d}}\leq c_{0}\,\|\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\|_{V}\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \ \mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V.

(2.6)

Here,
c_{0}
is a positive constant which depends on
\Omega
and
\Gamma_{1}
.

We also use notation
{\bf Q_{\infty}}
for the space of fourth order tensor fields defined by

{\bf Q_{\infty}}=\{\,{\cal C}=(c_{ijkl})\ :\ {c}_{ijkl}={c}_{jikl}={c}_{klij}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\ \ \ \forall\,i,\,j,\,k,\,l=1,\dots,d\,\},

equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|{\cal{C}}\|_{\bf Q_{\infty}}=\max_{1\leq i,j,k,l\leq d}\|{c}_{ijkl}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

We end this section with the following result we shall use in the rest of the paper.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exists a linear continuous operator

G\colon Q\to V
such that for any
\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}\in Q
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in V
the following implication hold:

\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})\quad\ \Longrightarrow\quad\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}=G\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}.

The proof of Lemma
2.5
is obtained by standard ortogonality arguments used in various books and surveys and, therefore, we skip it. Such arguments have been used in
[
27
]
, for instance, in the study of Navier–Stokes equations.

3
The viscoelastic contact model

We now describe the mathematical model of contact we consider in this paper. The physical setting is the following: a viscoelastic body occupies, in its reference configuration, a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\{2,3\}
), with regular boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma
. We assume that

\Gamma
is
decomposed into three parts
\overline{\Gamma}_{1}
,

\overline{\Gamma}_{2}
and
\overline{\Gamma}_{3}
, with
\Gamma_{1}
,

\Gamma_{2}
and
\Gamma_{3}
being relatively open and mutually
disjoint and, moreover, the
d-1
measure of
\Gamma_{1}
, denoted by
{meas}\,(\Gamma_{1})
, is positive. The body is fixed on the part
\Gamma_{1}
of its boundary, is acted upon by body forces and surface tractions on
\Gamma_{2}
, and is in contact with an obstacle on
\Gamma_{3}
, the co-called foundation.
As a result, its mechanical state evolves. To describe its evolution we denote by
[0,T]
the time interval of interest, where
T>0
. Moreover, we use
x
to denote a typical point in
\Omega\cup\Gamma
and, for simplicity, we sometimes skip the dependence of various functions on the spatial variable
x
.
Then, the viscoelastic contact model we consider is as follows.

Problem

{\cal P}
.
Find a displacement field

\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a stress field
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{S}^{d}
and a strain field
\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{S}^{d}

such that for any
t\in[0,T]
the following hold:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}(t)={\mathcal{A}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}{\cal B}(t-s)\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}(s)\,ds\quad\

\displaystyle{\rm in}\

\displaystyle\Omega,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}(t))\

\displaystyle{\rm in}\

\displaystyle\Omega,

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\rm Div}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}(t)+\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{0}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}\quad

\displaystyle{\rm in}\

\displaystyle\Omega,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}\

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}(t)\mbox{\boldmath{$\nu$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{2}(t)\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}\

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{\nu}(t)=0&{\rm if}\ \ u_{\nu}(t)<0\\[5.69054pt]
-F\leq\sigma_{\nu}(t)\leq 0&{\rm if}\ \ \ u_{\nu}(t)=0\\[5.69054pt]
\sigma_{\nu}(t)=-F&{\rm if}\ \ \ u_{\nu}(t)>0\end{array}\right\}\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}

\displaystyle\ \Gamma_{3},

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}_{\tau}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}\quad

\displaystyle{\rm on}\

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}.

(3.10)

A short description of the equations and boundary conditions in Problem
{\cal P}
is as follows.
First, equality (
3.1
) is the viscoelastic constitutive law with long memory in which
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
are the elasticity and the relaxation tensors, respectively. It was considered in many books, including

[
6
,
7
,
21
]
. In particular, existence and uniqueness results for displacement-tractions boundary value problems involving such a constitutive law have been considered in
[
7
]
.
Equality (
3.2
) represents the definition of the strain tensor.
Next, equation (
3.3
) is the equilibrium equation in which
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{0}
denotes the time-dependent density of body forces. We use this equation here since we assume that the mechanical process is quasistatic and, therefore, we neglect
the inertial term in the equation of motion.
The boundary condition (
3.4
) is the displacement condition and models the setting when the body is held fixed on the part
\Gamma_{1}
of its boundary. Condition (
3.5
)
is the traction boundary condition in which
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{2}
represents the density of surface tractions which act on
\Gamma_{2}
, assumed to be time-dependent. Condition (
3
) describes the contact with a rigid-plastic foundation. It shows that when there is separation (i.e., when
u_{\nu}(t)<0
) then the reaction of the foundation vanishes (since
\sigma_{\nu}(t)=0
); moreover, it shows that penetration arise only if the normal stress reaches the value
F
, which is interpreted as the yield limit of the foundation. More details and mechanical interpretation on this condition and similar interface laws could be found in
[
23
, p. 280]
and
[
24
,
25
]
, for instance. Finally, condition (
3.10
) shows that the shear on the contact surface vanishes during the process. We use this condition here since we assume that the contact is frictionless. The case of a frictional contact problem can be considered and treated by using similar arguments, too. Nevertheless its analysis is more difficult since in the frictional case the function
j
and the set
\Sigma
we introduce below depend on the solution itself.

We end our comments on the model (
3.1
)–(
3.10
) with the remark that in the case when the memory term in (
3.1
) vanishes (i.e., when
\mbox{{${\cal B}$}}\equiv\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
), then Problem
{\cal P}
reduces to a time-dependent elastic contact problem.
A comparison between the solution of this elastic problem and the original Problem
{\cal P}
has been made in
[
23
, p. 301–302]
, under specific assumptions.
For the example presented there, it was proved that the memory term does not affect the stress field but, in contrast, it affects the strain and the displacement field. Moreover, the solution of the elastic contact problem can be obtained from the solution of the viscoelastic contact problems, in the limit as the relaxation tensor converges to zero. We also mention that a comparison of the numerical solutions for the elastic and viscoelastic problems will be made in Section 6 (see Figure
2
).

In the study of Problem
{\cal P}
we assume that the viscosity and the elasticity operators satisfy the following conditions.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm(a)\ }{\cal A}=(a_{ijkl})\in{\bf Q_{\infty}}.\\[5.69054pt]
{\rm(b)\ There\ exists}\ m_{\mathcal{A}}>0\ {\rm\ such\ that}\\
{}\qquad{\cal A}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\geq m_{\mathcal{A}}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\|^{2}\ \,\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d},\ {\rm a.e.}\ \mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(3.11)

\mbox{{${\cal B}$}}\in C([0,T];{\bf Q_{\infty}}).

(3.12)

Moreover, the density of applied forces and the yield limit of the foundation have the regularity

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{0}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}).

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{2}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Gamma_{2})^{d}).

(3.14)

\displaystyle F\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{3}),\quad F(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\geq 0\quad{\rm a.e.}\ \ \mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\Gamma_{3}.

(3.15)

We shall keep assumptions (
3.11
)–(
3.15
) in the next three sections, even if we do not mention it explicitly. Our main aim there is to provide the variational analysis of Problem
{\cal P}
, including existence, uniqueness and convergence results.

4
Variational formulations

In order to deduce the variational formulations for Problem
{\cal P}
we introduce the operators
A\colon V\to V
,
\widetilde{A}\colon Q\to Q
,
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\colon C([0,T];V)\to C([0,T];V)
and
\widetilde{\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}}\colon C([0,T];Q)\to C([0,T];Q)

defined by equalities

\displaystyle(A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}=\int_{\Omega}{\cal A}\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\,dx\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}},\,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V,

(4.1)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}})_{Q}=\int_{\Omega}{\cal A}{\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\,dx\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}},\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\in Q,

(4.2)

\displaystyle(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}{\cal B}(t-s)\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}(s))\,ds\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\,dx

(4.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in C([0,T];V),\,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V,\ t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}})_{Q}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{t}{\cal B}(t-s)\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}(s)\,ds\cdot\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\,dx

(4.4)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}\in C([0,T];Q),\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\in Q,\ t\in[0,T].

Using the assumptions on the elasticity tensor
{\cal A}
, it is easy to see that
A\colon V\to V
and
\widetilde{A}\colon Q\to Q
are linear continuous symmetric and coercive operators. Moreover,
it is easy to see that
\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\colon C([0,T];V)\to C([0,T];V)
and
\widetilde{\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}}\colon C([0,T];Q)\to C([0,T];Q)

\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{3}^{V}
show that

\widetilde{A}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}(t)+\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}(t)=\widetilde{A}^{-1}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}}(t)+\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}}(t)\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Then the inversibility of the operator
\widetilde{A}^{-1}+\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}\colon C([0,T];Q)\to C([0,T];Q)
, guaranteed by Theorem
2.2
, implies that
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}=\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}}
.
This proves the uniqueness of the solution of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{2}^{V}
.

Assume now that
(F_{1},\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}),\,(F_{2},\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2})\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})\times C([0,T];V)
and denote by

\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}\in C([0,T];V)
the solution of inequality (
4.11
) for
F=F_{i}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{i}
,
i=1,2
. Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V
we have

\displaystyle(A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t))_{V}+(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t))_{V}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Gamma_{3}}F_{1}\,v_{\nu}^{+}\,da-\int_{\Gamma_{3}}F_{1}\,u_{1\nu}^{+}(t)\,da\geq(\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t))_{V},

\displaystyle(A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t))_{V}+(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t))_{V}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Gamma_{3}}F_{2}\,v_{\nu}^{+}\,da-\int_{\Gamma_{3}}F_{2}\,u_{2\nu}^{+}(t)\,da\geq(\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t))_{V}.

We take
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)
in (
5.2
),
\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)
in (
5.3
), then we add the resulting inequalities to find that

\displaystyle(A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t))_{V}\leq(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t))_{V}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Gamma_{3}}(F_{1}-F_{2})(u_{2\nu}^{+}(t)-u_{1\nu}^{+}(t))\,da+(\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}(t),\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t))_{V}.

Next, the strong monotonicity of
A
and the trace inequality (
2.6
) yield

\displaystyle m_{\cal A}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|^{2}_{V}\leq\|\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}

\displaystyle\quad+c_{0}\|F_{1}-F_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}+\|\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}(t)\|_{V}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}.

The previous inequality implies that

\displaystyle m_{\cal A}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}\leq\|\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+c_{0}\|F_{1}-F_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})}+\|\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}(t)\|_{V}.

We now use definition (
4.3
) and assumption (
3.12
) to see that there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle m_{\cal A}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(t)\|_{V}\leq c_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}(s)-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}(s)\|_{V}\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+c_{0}\|F_{1}-F_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})}+\|\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}(t)-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}(t)\|_{V}.

Next, by using a Gronwall argument and definition (
2.2
)
we see that there exists
C>0
which does not depend on
F_{i}
and
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that

\|\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{2}\|_{C([0,T];V)}\leq C\big(\|F_{1}-F_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})}+\|\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{1}-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}^{2}\|_{C([0,T];V)}\big)

which shows that the solution
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in C([0,T];V)
depends Lipschitz continuously on the data
(F,\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}})\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{3})\times C([0,T];V)
.
The Lipschitz continuity of the solutions
\sigma
and
\omega
follows now from equalities (
4.14
) and (
4.18
)
and the properties of the operators
A
,
{\cal S}
,

\widetilde{A}^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\mbox{{${\mathcal{R}}$}}}
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Note that the unique solvability of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{2}^{V}
can be obtained directly. A sketch of the proof is as follows. First, note that

\Sigma(t)=\Sigma_{0}+\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}({\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}}(t))\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

where
\Sigma_{0}
is the time-independent nonempty closed convex subset of
Q
defined by

\Sigma_{0}=\{\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}}\in Q\,:\,(\mbox{\boldmath{$\tau$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}))_{Q}+j(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\geq 0\ \ \forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V\,\}.

Then, using the change of unknown given by
\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}=\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}({\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}})
,
we find that Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{2}^{V}
is equivalent with a history-dependent variational inequality of the form
(\ref{zz})
on the space
X=Q
, associated to the convex
K=\Sigma_{0}
, in which the unknown is the auxiliary stress field
\overline{\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}}
.
Theorem
2.3
guarantees the unique solvability of this inequality which, in turn, provides the unique solvability of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{2}^{V}
.

Remark 4
.

The unique solvability of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{3}^{V}
can be obtained directly, by using Theorem
2.4
.
Indeed, it is easy to check that assumptions

(\mathcal{A})
,
(\mathcal{S})
and
(\Sigma)
are satisfied for the inclusion
(\ref{Mx10})

with
X=Q
, operators
\widetilde{A}^{-1}
,
\widetilde{R}
and the function
g=\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}})
.

We end this section with the remark that (
4.11
), (
4.12
) represent history-dependent inequalities and
(
4.13
) is a history-dependent inclusion.
Despite the fact that these problems have a different structure, each of them can be interpreted as a variational formulation of the contact Problem
{\cal P}
. We conclude from here that the variational formulation of contact models is not unique and could lead to different mathematical problems which, in fact, are dual of each other. Moreover, anyone among the displacement, the stress or the strain field can be considered as main unknown of the corresponding contact model, provided that an appropriate variational formulation is used.

We refer to a triple
(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\sigma$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}})
such that
u
is a solution of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{V}
,
\sigma
is a solution of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{2}^{V}
and
\omega
is a solution of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{3}^{V}
, as a weak solution to the contact Problem
{\cal P}
.
We note that Theorem
5.1
provides the unique weak solvability of Problem
{\cal P}
as well as the Lipschitz continuous dependence of the weak solution
with respect to the data
f
and
F
.

Moreover, using standard arguments and Remark
2
it can be proved that if any of the solution to Problems
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}^{V}_{1}
,
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}^{V}_{2}
or
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}^{V}_{3}

3.1
)–(

) in the strong sense, i.e. at each point
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}\in\Omega
and at any time moment

.

6
Numerical approximation

In this section, we present numerical simulations for the contact Problem
{\cal P}
by using its variational formulation given in Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{V}
.
Throughout the rest of this paper, we assume that (
3.11
)–(
3.15
) hold, even if we do not mention it explicitly. By virtue of Theorem
5.1
, we conclude that Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{V}
has a unique solution
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}\in C([0,T];V)
.
We start by introducing a fully-discrete scheme to approximate the solution of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{V}
.

Let
V^{h}
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
V
, where
h
is positive real number which denotes the spatial discretization step.
Throughout this section, we assume that
V^{h}
is the space of piecewise affine continuous functions, given by

V^{h}=\{w^{h}\in C(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})\,\,\,|\,\,\,w^{h}|_{\widetilde{T}}\in[\mathbb{P}_{1}(\widetilde{T})]^{d}\,\,\,\,\,{\rm for\,\,all}\,\,\widetilde{T}\in{\mathcal{T}}^{h}\}\subset V.

Here,
\mathcal{T}^{h}
is a family of finite element partitions of
\Omega
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\widetilde{T})
denotes the space of affine functions on
\widetilde{T}
.
We divide the time interval
[0,T]
into
N
equal pieces of length
k=\frac{T}{N}
and we denote
t_{n}=k\,n
, for all
n=1,2,\dots,N
. Moreover, for a continuous function
g=g(t)
we use the short-hand notation
g_{n}=g(t_{n})
, for all
n=1,2,\dots,N
.

We now can introduce the following discrete version of Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{V}
.

Problem

\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Vh}
.
Find
a displacement
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}=\{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset V^{h}
such that
the inequality below holds:

\displaystyle(A\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}^{h}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i})_{V}+((\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk})_{i}\,,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}^{h}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i})_{V}+j(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}^{h})-j(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i})

(6.1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq(\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}^{h}-\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}_{i})_{V}\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}^{h}\in V^{h},\ i=1,\dots,N.

The unique solvability of the discrete Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Vh}
can be easily proved by using arguments similar to those used in the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

To present the numerical solution of problem (
6.1
), we utilize the mechanical example based on the two-dimensional physical setting shown in Figure 1, which represents the cross-section of a three dimensional viscoelastic body. We note this section by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The body
is clamped on the part
\Gamma_{1}=[0\,$\mathrm{m}$,1\,$\mathrm{m}$]\times\{0\,$\mathrm{m}$\}
and, therefore, the displacement field vanishes there. On the part
\Gamma_{3}=[4\,$\mathrm{m}$,5\,$\mathrm{m}$]\times\{0\,$\mathrm{m}$\}
the body is in potential frictionless contact with a rigid-plastic penetrable foundation with the yield limit
F
.
Moreover, it is acted from the top by a vertical force of density
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{2}
. Therefore, denoting by
\Gamma_{2}
the remaining part of the boundary of
\Omega
and using notation
\mathcal{C}^{u}((x_{0},y_{0}),\,r)
for the upper semicircle of radius
r
(
y\geq y_{0}
) centred at the point
(x_{0},y_{0})
,
for any
\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}=(x,y)\in\Gamma_{2}
and
t\in[0,T]
we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{2}((x,y),t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(0,f_{2\,y}(t))\,$\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}$,&\text{if }(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}^{u}((2.5\,$\mathrm{m}$,2.0\,$\mathrm{m}$),\,2.5\,$\mathrm{m}$),\\
(0,0)\,$\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}$,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Figure 1:
Reference configuration of the body.

For simplicity of the analysis, we neglect the body forces and, therefore, we assume that
\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{0}=\mbox{\boldmath{$0$}}
.
Moreover, we assume that the body behaves linearly and the components of elasticity and relaxation tensors are given by

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{E\kappa}{(1+\kappa)(1-2\kappa)}(\omega_{11}+\omega_{22})\delta_{ij}\,+\,\frac{E}{1+\kappa}\omega_{ij}

(6.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}=(\omega_{ij})\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\ i,j=1,2,

\displaystyle({\mathcal{B}}(t)\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b\,\omega_{ij}\qquad\forall\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\omega$}}=(\omega_{ij})\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\ i,j=1,2,\quad t\in[0,T].

(6.3)

Here and below
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta,
b
is a relaxation parameter, and
E
and
\kappa
are Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the body material, respectively. For the simulations we present below, we use
the following input parameters:

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=10^{4}\,$\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}$,\quad\kappa=0.4,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=10,\quad b=10^{4}\,$\mathrm{N}\,\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$,

\displaystyle f_{2\,y}(t)

\displaystyle=10\sin t,\quad t\in[0,T].

In order to obtain a numerical solution, a spatial discretization with variable mesh size is used, with a maximum size of 0.275 m inside the domain
\Omega
and not exceeding 0.06 m for elements lying directly on
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{3}
. This gives rise to a spatial domain discretized into 822 elements, including 20 contact elements, the total number of degrees of freedom being 1644.

To find a solution of the discrete variational inequality (
6.1
) with linear elasticity and relaxation tensors defined as in (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), respectively, we use an optimization-based method described in detail in
[
12
]
.
We approximate the integral term by the right rectangle formula in each subinterval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
of
[0,T]
. We use the following approximation of the time integral operator in (
4.11
):

\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\cal B}(t_{n}-s)\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}(s))\,ds\approx k\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal B}(t_{n}-t_{j})\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{j}).

Therefore, using (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle((\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}

\displaystyle=\left(k\sum_{j=1}^{i}{\cal B}(t_{i}-t_{j})\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{j}),\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\varepsilon$}}(\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})\right)_{Q}

(6.4)

\displaystyle=((\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})_{i-1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}+(k{\cal B}(0)\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}=((\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})_{i-1},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}+(kb\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}})_{V}

for all
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}},\mbox{\boldmath{$v$}}\in V^{h}
and
i=1,\dots,n
.
Note that in
i
-th time step the values of
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{0},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i-1}
are known and, therefore,
(\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}})_{i-1}
is known too.
Thus, for every time step
i
, we use (
6.4
) in order to introduce the cost functional
\mathcal{L}_{i}\colon V\to{{\rm I}\mkern-3.0mu{\rm R}}
given by

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h})=\frac{1}{2}(A\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h}+kb\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h},\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h})_{V}+j(\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h})+((\mbox{{${\cal S}$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk})_{i-1}-\mbox{\boldmath{$f$}}_{i}\,,\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h})_{V}

for all
\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}^{h}\in V^{h}
, which is a convex functional. We are now in a position to find a sequence of minimizers of functionals
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, i.e., solve the following optimization problem.

Problem
{\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Oh}}
.

Find
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}^{hk}=\{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}^{hk}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset V^{h}
such that

\displaystyle 0\in

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{i}^{hk})\qquad\forall\ i=1,\dots,N.

It can be shown that the operators appearing in inequality (
6.1
) satisfy the assumptions considered in [8] and, furthermore, the optimization Problem
{\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Oh}}
is equivalent to Problem
\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Vh}
. For a deeper insight into the theory related to the optimization approach and the appropriate potential energy functional we mention the book
[
28
]
. Moreover,
a more detailed analysis of the selected approach compared to other widely used methods can be found in
[
19
]
.
To solve Problem
{\mbox{{${\cal P}$}}_{1}^{Oh}}
we use our original software
Conmech
that is a user-friendly tool written entirely in Python for conducting contact simulations and analyzing results.
To enhance the performance of native Python, we utilized the just-in-time compiler
Numba

[
13
]
.
The package provides comprehensive support for simulations, ranging from easy definition of the shape and material properties of the body to generating computational meshes and performing empirical error analysis.
It supports simulations for both static, quasistatic and dynamic problems, in two or three dimensions.
The goal of the package is to easily extend existing models with additional physical effects, which is achieved thanks to the modularity of the software.
The package is open-source and provided under the GPL-3.0 license.
