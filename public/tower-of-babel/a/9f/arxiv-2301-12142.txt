1.
Introduction

Lauret has studied the moment map for the variety of Lie algebras and obtained many remarkable results in
[
7
]
,
which turned out to be very important in proving that every Einstein solvmanifold is standard (
[
9
]
) and
in the characterization of solitons (
[
1
,
10
]
). Apart from the Lie algebras, the study of the moment map in other classes of algebras was also initiated by Lauret, see
[
11
]
for more details. Motivated by this, the authors have recently extended the study of the moment map to the variety of
3
-Lie algebras (see
[
17
]
).

In this paper, we study the moment map for the variety of associative algebras. Let
\textnormal{GL}(n)
be the complex reductive Lie group acting naturally on the complex vector space
V_{n}=\otimes^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{*}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e., the space of all
n
-dimensional complex algebras. The usual Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
naturally induces an
\textnormal{U}(n)
-invariant Hermitian inner product on
V_{n}
, which is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Since
\mathfrak{gl}(n)=\mathfrak{u}(n)+\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
, we may
define a function as follows

\displaystyle m:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),\quad(m([\mu]),A)=\frac{(\textnormal{d}\rho_{\mu})_{e}A}{\|\mu\|^{2}},\quad 0\neq\mu\in V_{n},~~A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is an
\textnormal{Ad}(\textnormal{U}(n))
-invariant real inner product on
\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),
and
\rho_{\mu}:\textnormal{GL}(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined by
\rho_{\mu}(g)=\langle g.\mu,g.\mu\rangle
.
The function
m
is the moment
map from symplectic geometry, corresponding to the Hamiltonian action
\textnormal{U}(n)
of
V_{n}
on
the symplectic manifold
\mathbb{P}V_{n}
(see
[
4
,
12
]
).
In this paper, we study the critical points of the functional
F_{n}=\|m\|^{2}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, with an emphasis on the critical points that lie in the projectivization of the
algebraic variety of all
n
-dimensional associative algebras
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

The paper is organized as follows:
In Sect.
2
, we recall some basic concepts and results of complex associative algebras.

In Sect.
3
, we first give the explicit expression of the moment map
m:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
in terms of
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}
, that is,
m([\mu])=\frac{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}{\|\mu\|^{2}}
for any
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
. Then we show that
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
is a critical point of
F_{n}
if and only if
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
(Thm.
3.3
).

In Sect.
4
, we first show that any algebra
\mu\in V_{n}
admits a Nikolayevsky derivation
\phi_{\mu}
which is unique up to automorphism, the eigenvalues of
\phi_{\mu}
are necessarily rational, and moreover,
\phi_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{c_{\mu}}D_{\mu}
if
[\mu]
is a critical point of
F_{n}
(Thm.
4.1
).
Then we study the extremal points of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
proving that the minimum value is
attained at semisimple associative algebras (Thm.
4.6
), and the maximum value at the direct sum of a two-dimensional commutative associative algebra with the trivial algebra (Thm.
4.9
). In the context of Lie algebras (
[
7
]
), Lauret proved that any
\mu
for which
there exists
[\lambda]\in\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]
such that all eigenvalues of
\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}
are negative, must be
semisimple, and we prove that this result also holds for associative algebras (Remark
4.7
).
Besides, the structure for an arbitrary critical point
[\mu]
of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is discussed (Thm.
4.10
and Thm.
4.12
).

In Sect.
5
, we classify the critical points of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
n=2
,
3
.
It shows that every two-dimensional associative algebra is isomorphic to a critical point of
F_{2};
and there exists only one three-dimensional associative algebra which is not isomorphic to any critical point of
F_{3}
.
Finally, based on the discussion in previous sections, we collect some natural and interesting questions.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some basic definitions and results of associative algebras. The ambient field is always assumed to be the complex number field
\mathbb{C}
unless otherwise stated.

Definition 2.1
.

A vector space
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathbb{C}
with a bilinear operation

\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, denoted by
(x,y)\mapsto xy
, is called an
associative algebra
, if

x(yz)=(xy)z

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}.

A
derivation
of an associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a linear transformation
D:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
satisfying

\displaystyle D(xy)=(Dx)y+x(Dy),

for
x,y\in\mathcal{A}.
It is easy to see that the set of all derivations of
\mathcal{A}
is a Lie algebra, which is denoted by
\textnormal{Der}(\mathcal{A}).

A vector subspace
I
of
\mathcal{A}
is called an
ideal
if
\mathcal{A}I,I\mathcal{A}\subset I.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an associative algebra. The
center
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined by
C(\mathcal{A})=\{x\in\mathcal{A}:xy=yx,\forall y\in\mathcal{A}\}
. The
annihilator
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined by
\textnormal{ann}(\mathcal{A})=\{x\in\mathcal{A}:xy=yx=0,\forall y\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

Clearly,
C(\mathcal{A})
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
, and
\textnormal{ann}(\mathcal{A})
is an ideal of
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{I}
be an ideal of an associative algebra. Then
{I}
is called
nilpotent
, if
{I}^{k}=0
for some integer
k\geq 1
, where
{I}^{k}=\underbrace{I{\cdots}I{\cdots}I}_{k}
.

If
I,J
are any two nilpotent ideals of an associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
, then
I+J
is also a nilpotent ideal. So the maximum nilpotent ideal of
\mathcal{A}
is unique, which is called the
radical
and denoted by
{N}(\mathcal{A}).

Remark 2.4
.

Note that
{N}(\mathcal{A})
coincides with the Jacobson radical of
\mathcal{A}
since
\mathcal{A}
is an associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover,
{N}(\mathcal{A})=\{x\in\mathcal{A}:xy,yx\textnormal{~are nilpotent elements for any}~y\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an associative algebra. If
\mathcal{A}
has no ideals except itself and
0,
we call
\mathcal{A}
simple.

Denote by
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
the set of all
n\times n

complex square matrices, which is clearly an associative algebra with respect to the
usual matrix addition and multiplication. In fact,
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a simple associative algebra for any
n\geq 1.
Moreover,
it follows from Wedderburn-Artin theorem that any finite-dimensional simple associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for some integer
n\geq 1
(
[
15
]
).

An associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
is called
semisimple
if its radical
{N}(\mathcal{A})
is zero. The following theorem is well known.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
15
]
)
.

An associative algebra over
\mathbb{C}
is semisimple if and only if it is a direct sum of simple ideals. That is, a semisimple associative algebra is isomorphic to

\mathbb{M}_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{M}_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times\mathbb{M}_{n_{s}}(\mathbb{C})
for some positive integers
n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{s}.

3.
The moment map for complex algebras

Let
\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional complex vector space and
V_{n}=\otimes^{2}({\mathbb{C}^{n}})^{*}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the space of all complex
n
-dimensional algebras. The natural action of
\textnormal{GL}(n)=\textnormal{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
on
V_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle g.\mu(X,Y)=g\mu(g^{-1}X,g^{-1}Y),\quad g\in\textnormal{GL}(n),X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

(3.1)

Clearly,
\textnormal{GL}(n).\mu
is precisely the isomorphism class of
\mu
. Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g_{t}.\mu=0,\quad g_{t}=tI\subset\textnormal{GL}(n),t>0,

we see that
0
lies in the boundary of the orbit
\textnormal{GL}(n).\mu
for each
\mu\in V_{n}.

By differentiating (
3.1
), we obtain the natural action
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
on
V_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle A.\mu(X,Y)=A\mu(X,Y)-\mu(AX,Y)-\mu(X,AY),\quad A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n),\mu\in V_{n}.

(3.2)

It follows that
A.\mu=0
if and only if
A\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu),
where
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
denotes the derivation algebra of
\mu.

Note that the usual Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
gives an
\textnormal{U}(n)
-invariant Hermitian inner product on
V_{n}
as follows

\displaystyle\langle\mu,\lambda\rangle=\sum_{i,j,k}\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),X_{k}\rangle\overline{\langle\lambda(X_{i},X_{j}),X_{k}\rangle},\quad\quad\quad~\mu,\lambda\in V_{n},

(3.3)

where
\{X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
\mathfrak{u}(n)
denote the Lie algebra of
\textnormal{U}(n),
then it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{gl}(n)=\mathfrak{u}(n)+\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
decomposes into skew-Hermitian and Hermitian transformations of
V_{n}
, respectively. Moreover, there is an
\textnormal{Ad}(\textnormal{U}(n))
-invariant Hermitian inner product on
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
given by

\displaystyle(A,B)=\operatorname{tr}AB^{*},~A,B\in\mathfrak{gl}(n).

(3.4)

The moment map from symplectic geometry, corresponding to the Hamiltonian action of
\textnormal{U}(n)
on the symplectic
manifold
\mathbb{P}V_{n}
, is defined as follows

\displaystyle m:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),\quad(m([\mu]),A)=\frac{(\textnormal{d}\rho_{\mu})_{e}A}{\|\mu\|^{2}},\quad 0\neq\mu\in V_{n},A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),

(3.5)

where
\rho_{\mu}:\textnormal{GL}(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by
\rho_{\mu}(g)=\langle g.\mu,g.\mu\rangle
. Clearly,
(\textnormal{d}\rho_{\mu})_{e}A=\langle A.\mu,\mu\rangle+\langle\mu,A.\mu\rangle=2\langle A.\mu,\mu\rangle
for any
A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
.
The square norm of the moment map is denoted by

\displaystyle F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(3.6)

where
F_{n}([\mu])=\|m([\mu])\|^{2}=(m([\mu]),m([\mu]))
for any
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}.

In order to express the moment map
m
explicitly, we define
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
as follows

\displaystyle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=2\sum_{i}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}-2\sum_{i}({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}-2\sum_{i}({R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}},

(3.7)

where the left and right multiplication
{L}^{\mu}_{X},{R}^{\mu}_{X}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
by
X
of the algebra
\mu
, are given by
{L}^{\mu}_{X}(Y)=\mu(X,Y)
and
{R}^{\mu}_{X}(Y)=\mu(Y,X)
for all
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{n},
respectively. It is not hard to prove that

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,Y\rangle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),X\rangle}\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),Y\rangle-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X_{i},X),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},Y),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(Y,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

(3.8)

for
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.
Note that if the algebra
\mu
is commutative or anticommutative, then the second and third term of (
3
) are the same, and in this case,
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}
coincides with
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\mu\in V_{n}
, we have
(\textnormal{M}_{\mu},A)=2\langle\mu,A.\mu\rangle
,
\forall A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)=\mathfrak{u}(n)+\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n).

In particular,
m([\mu])=\frac{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}{\|\mu\|^{2}}
for any
0\neq\mu\in V_{n}

Proof.

For any
A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, we have
(A,\textnormal{M}_{\mu})=\operatorname{tr}A\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{*}=\operatorname{tr}A\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}A,

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}A

\displaystyle=\underbrace{2\operatorname{tr}\sum_{i}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}A}_{\textnormal{I}}-\underbrace{2\operatorname{tr}\sum_{i}(({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}+({R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})A}_{\textnormal{II}}

\displaystyle=:\textnormal{I}-\textnormal{II}.

Note that

\displaystyle\textnormal{I}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i}\operatorname{tr}({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}A{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\langle({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}A{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}(X_{j}),X_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\langle A\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),\mu(X_{i},X_{j})\rangle,

and

\displaystyle\textnormal{II}=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{tr}\sum_{i}(({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}+({R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\langle(({L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{L}^{\mu}_{X_{i}}+({R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})^{*}{R}^{\mu}_{X_{i}})AX_{j},X_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\langle{\mu}(X_{i},AX_{j}),{\mu}(X_{i},X_{j})\rangle-2\sum_{i,j}\langle{\mu}(AX_{j},X_{i}),{\mu}(X_{j},X_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(AX_{i},X_{j})+{\mu}(X_{i},AX_{j}),\mu(X_{i},X_{j})\rangle.

By (
3.2
), it follows that
(A,\textnormal{M}_{\mu})=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}A=2\langle A.\mu,\mu\rangle,
so
(\textnormal{M}_{\mu},A)=2\langle\mu,A.\mu\rangle

for any
A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
. This proves the first statement.
For
A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n),
we have
\langle A.\mu,\mu\rangle=\langle\mu,A.\mu\rangle
. By (
3.5
), we conclude that
m([\mu])=\frac{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}{\|\mu\|^{2}}
for any
0\neq\mu\in V_{n}
. This completes proof Lemma
3.1

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
\mu\in V_{n}
, then

(i)

\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}D=0
for any
D\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu);

(ii)

\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}[A,A^{*}]\geq 0
for any
A\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu),
and equality holds if and only if
A^{*}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).

Proof.

For (i), it follows from Lemma
3.1
that
\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}D=2\langle D.\mu,\mu\rangle.
For (ii), it follows from that
\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}[A,A^{*}]=2\langle[A,A^{*}].\mu,\mu\rangle=2\langle A^{*}.\mu,A^{*}.\mu\rangle\geq 0
,
\forall A\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
, and the fact
A^{*}.\mu=0
if and only if
A^{*}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).

∎

Theorem 3.3
.

The moment map
m:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n)
, the functional square norm of the moment map
F_{n}=\|m\|^{2}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the gradient of
F_{n}
are, respectively, given by

\displaystyle F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{4}},\quad\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{[\mu]}=\frac{8\pi_{*}(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu}{\|\mu\|^{4}},\quad[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n},

(3.9)

where
\pi_{*}
denotes the derivative of
\pi:V_{n}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}V_{n}
, the canonical projection. Moreover, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
is a critical point of
F_{n}
.

(ii)

[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
is a critical point of
F_{n}|_{\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]}.

(iii)

\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
.

Proof.

By (
3.6
) and Lemma
3.1
, we have
F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{4}}
for any
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}.
To prove the second one, we only need to compute the gradient of
F_{n}:V_{n}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},~F_{n}(\mu)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{4}}
, and then to project it via
\pi_{*}.

If
\mu,\lambda\in V_{n}
with
\mu\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{\mu},\lambda\rangle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}F_{n}(\mu+t\lambda)=\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\frac{1}{\|\mu+t\lambda\|^{4}}(\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda},\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4\operatorname{Re}\langle\frac{F_{n}(\mu)}{\|\mu\|^{2}}\mu,\lambda\rangle+\frac{2}{\|\mu\|^{4}}(\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda},\textnormal{M}_{\mu})

We claim that

(\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda},A)=4\operatorname{Re}\langle A.\mu,\lambda\rangle
for any
A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n).

Indeed, by Lemma
3.1
,
(\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda},A)=\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}(\textnormal{M}_{\mu+t\lambda},A)=2\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\langle\mu+t\lambda,A.(\mu+t\lambda)\rangle=2\langle\lambda,A.\mu\rangle+2\langle\mu,A.\lambda\rangle=4\operatorname{Re}\langle A.\mu,\lambda\rangle
for any
A\in\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{u}(n).
It follows that
\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{\mu}=-4\frac{F_{n}(\mu)}{\|\mu\|^{2}}\mu+8\frac{(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu}{\|\mu\|^{4}},
and consequentely

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{[\mu]}=\frac{8\pi_{*}(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu}{\|\mu\|^{4}}.

Thus the first part of the theorem is proved, and the following is to prove the equivalence among the statements
\textnormal{(i)},\textnormal{(ii)}
and
\textnormal{(iii)}.

\textnormal{(i)}\Leftrightarrow\textnormal{(ii)}:
The equivalence follows from that
\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})
is tangent to the
\textnormal{GL}(n)
-orbits. Indeed

\displaystyle\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{[\mu]}=\frac{8\pi_{*}(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu}{\|\mu\|^{4}}=\frac{8}{\|\mu\|^{4}}\pi_{*}(\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}e^{t\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}.\mu)=\frac{8}{\|\mu\|^{4}}\left.\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\right|_{t=0}e^{t\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}.[\mu]\in T_{[\mu]}(\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]).

\textnormal{(iii)}\Rightarrow\textnormal{(i)}:
By (
3.2
), we know that
I.\mu=-\mu,
and
(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu=(c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}).\mu=-c_{\mu}\mu.
It follows that
\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{[\mu]}=0.

\textnormal{(i)}\Rightarrow\textnormal{(iii)}:
Since
\operatorname{grad}(F_{n})_{[\mu]}=0,
then
(\textnormal{M}_{\mu}).\mu\in\ker\pi_{*\mu}=\mathbb{C}\mu.

So
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=cI+D
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
D\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).
Clearly
[D,D^{*}]=[\textnormal{M}_{\mu}-cI,\textnormal{M}_{\mu}-\bar{c}I]=0,
we conclude by Corollary
3.2
that
D^{*}
is also a derivation of
\mu.
In particular,
(c-\bar{c})I=(D^{*}-D)\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu),
thus
c=\bar{c}\in\mathbb{R}.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

Let
[\mu]
be a critical point of
F_{n}
and
[\lambda]
be a critical point of
F_{m}
, then
[\mu\oplus c\lambda]
is a critical point of
F_{n+m}
for a suitable
c\in\mathbb{C}.
Indeed, assume that
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
,
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
, and
\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}I+D_{\lambda}
for some
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
,
D_{\lambda}\in\textnormal{Der}(\lambda)
. Noting that
\textnormal{M}_{t\lambda}=|t|^{2}\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}
for any

, we can choose
t_{0}
such that
c_{\mu}=|t_{0}|^{2}c_{\lambda}
, then it follows that
[\mu\oplus t_{0}\lambda]
is a critical point of
F_{n+m}.

In the frame of algebras, a remarkable result due to Ness can be stated as follows

Theorem 3.5

(
[
12
]
)
.

If
[\mu]
is a critical point of the functional

then

(i)

\left.F_{n}\right|_{\mathrm{G}L(n).[\mu]}
attains its minimum value at
[\mu]
.

(ii)

.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
be a critical point of

with

for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
. Then we have

(i)

c_{\mu}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}}{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{2}}<0.

(ii)

If

, then

and
\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}>0.

Proof.

Since

, by Lemma

and Corollary

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=(\textnormal{M}_{\mu},I)=2\langle\mu,I.\mu\rangle=-2\|\mu\|^{2}<0,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}(c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu})=c_{\mu}\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}.

So

If

, then

\displaystyle 0=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}D_{\mu}=c_{\mu}\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}+\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}^{2}.

So

and

∎

.

4.
The critical points of the variety of associative algebras

The space
\mathscr{A}_{n}
of all
n
-dimensional associative algebras is an algebraic set since it is given by polynomial conditions.
Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{n}
the projective algebraic variety obtained by projectivization of
\mathscr{A}_{n}
. Note that
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is
\textnormal{GL}(n)
-invariant, then by Theorem
3.3
, the critical points of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are precisely the critical points
of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that lie in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

4.1.
The Nikolayevsky derivation and the rationality

A derivation of
\phi
of an algebra
(\mu,\mathbb{C}^{n})
is called a
Nikolayevsky derivation
, if it is semisimple with all eigenvalues real, and
\operatorname{tr}\phi\psi=\operatorname{tr}\psi
for any
\psi\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).
This notion is motivated by
[
14
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\mu,\mathbb{C}^{n})
be an arbitrary algebra. Then

(1)

(\mu,\mathbb{C}^{n})
admits a Nikolayevsky derivation
\phi_{\mu}
.

(2)

The Nikolayevsky derivation
\phi_{\mu}
is determined up to automorphism of
\mu
.

(3)

All eigenvalues of
\phi_{\mu}
are rational numbers.

If moreover,
[\mu]
is a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
, then
-\frac{1}{c_{\mu}}D_{\mu}
is the Nikolayevsky derivation of
\mu
.

Proof.

(1) The complex Lie algebra
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
is algebraic. Let
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)=\mathfrak{s}\oplus\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
be its Levi-Mal’cev decomposition, where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple,
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
is the radical of
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
,
\mathfrak{n}
is the set of all nilpotent elements in
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
(and is the nilradical of
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
),
\mathfrak{t}
is an abelian subalgebra consisting of semisimple elements, and
[\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}]=0.

Define the bilinear form
B
on
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
by

B(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}):=\operatorname{tr}\psi_{1}\psi_{2},\quad\forall\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).

Then, in general,
B
is degenerate, and
\operatorname{Ker}B=\mathfrak{n}.
Since
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple, then
B(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})=B([\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{s}],\mathfrak{t})=B(\mathfrak{s},[\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}])=0.
Clearly,
B
is nondegenerate on
\mathfrak{t}.
Since
\mathfrak{t}
is reductive, we have
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{a}+\textnormal{i}\mathfrak{a}
, where
\mathfrak{a}
consists of semisimple elements with all eigenvalues real. It follows that there exists a unique element
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}
such that
B(\phi,\psi)=\operatorname{tr}\psi
for any
\psi\in\mathfrak{t}.
Thus
\operatorname{tr}\phi\psi=\operatorname{tr}\psi
for any
\psi\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).

(2) The subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
is a maximal fully reducible subalgebra of
\textnormal{Der}(\mu).
Since the maximal fully reducible subalgebras of
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
are conjugate by inner automorphism of
\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
(which corresponds to an automorphism of
\mu
), and then the center
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathfrak{s}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
, is defined uniquely, up to automorphism. So the Nikolayevsky derivation is determined up to automorphism of
\mu
.

(3) The case
\phi_{\mu}=0
is trivial. In the following, we assume that
\phi_{\mu}
is nonzero. Note that
\phi_{\mu}
is simisimple with all eigenvalues real, we have the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathfrak{l}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{l}_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{l}_{r},

where
\mathfrak{l}_{i}=\{X\in\mathbb{C}^{n}|\phi_{\mu}X=c_{i}X\}
are eigenspaces of
\phi_{\mu}
corresponding to eigenvalues
c_{1}<c_{2}<\cdots<c_{r}\in\mathbb{R},
respectively. Set
d_{i}=\dim\mathfrak{l}_{i}\in\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq r.
Since
\phi_{\mu}
is a derivation, we have the following relations

\displaystyle\mu(\mathfrak{l}_{i},\mathfrak{l}_{j})\subset\mathfrak{l}_{k}\quad\text{if}~c_{i}+c_{j}=c_{k},

for all
1\leq i,j,k\leq r.
Conversely, if we define a linear transformation
\psi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
by
\psi|_{\mathfrak{l}_{i}}=a_{i}\textnormal{Id}_{\mathfrak{l}_{i}}
, where
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
a_{i}+a_{j}=a_{k}
for all
1\leq i,j,k\leq r
such that
c_{i}+c_{j}=c_{k},
then
\psi
is a derivation of
\mu.
Clearly, all such derivations form a real vector space, which can be identified with

W:=\{(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}|w_{i}+w_{j}=w_{k}~\text{if}~c_{i}+c_{j}=c_{k}\}.
We endow
\mathbb{R}^{r}
with the usual inner product, i.e.

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i},

(4.1)

for any
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{r}),~y=(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

For any derivation
\psi\in W
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\phi_{\mu}-I)\psi=\operatorname{tr}\phi_{\mu}\psi-\operatorname{tr}\psi=0.

Then we see that

(d_{1}(c_{1}-1),d_{2}(c_{2}-1),\cdots,d_{r}(c_{r}-1))\perp W
relative to (
4.1
). Put
F:=W^{\perp}
, then by definition we have

\displaystyle F=\textnormal{span}_{1\leq i,j,k\leq r}\{e_{i}+e_{j}-e_{k}:c_{i}+c_{j}=c_{k}\},

where
e_{i}
belongs to
\mathbb{R}^{r}
having
1
in the
i
-th position and
0
elsewhere. Let
\{e_{i_{1}}+e_{j_{1}}-e_{k_{1}},\cdots,e_{i_{s}}+e_{j_{s}}-e_{k_{s}}\}
be a basis of
F,
then

\displaystyle(d_{1}(c_{1}-1),d_{2}(c_{2}-1),\cdots,d_{r}(c_{r}-1))=\sum_{p=1}^{s}b_{p}(e_{i_{p}}+e_{j_{p}}-e_{k_{p}}),

(4.2)

for some
b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{s}\in\mathbb{R}.

Put

\displaystyle E=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e_{i_{1}}+e_{j_{1}}-e_{k_{1}}\\
e_{i_{2}}+e_{j_{2}}-e_{k_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
e_{i_{s}}+e_{j_{s}}-e_{k_{s}}\\
\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{s\times r},

then
EE^{T}\in\textnormal{GL}(s,\mathbb{Z}),
and
(EE^{T})^{-1}\in\textnormal{GL}(s,\mathbb{Q}).
By (
4.2
) and the definition of
E
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d_{1}(c_{1}-1)\\
d_{2}(c_{2}-1)\\
\vdots\\
d_{r}(c_{r}-1)\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}=E^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
b_{s}\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1},~E\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{r}\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1},~\quad E\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1}.

By the left multiplication of
E
on (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1}-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1}=ED^{-1}E^{T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\\
\vdots\\
b_{s}\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1},

where
D=\textnormal{diag}(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r}).
It is easy to see that
(ED^{-1}E^{T})\in\textnormal{GL}(s,\mathbb{Q}).
Consequently

\displaystyle D\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c_{1}-1\\
c_{2}-1\\
\vdots\\
c_{r}-1\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}=-E^{T}(ED^{-1}E^{T})^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1},

and

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{r}\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{r\times 1}-D^{-1}E^{T}(ED^{-1}E^{T})^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{array}\right)_{s\times 1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{r}.

So all eigenvalues of
\phi_{\mu}
are rational.

For the last statement, by Corollary
3.2
we know that
0=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}\psi=c_{\mu}\operatorname{tr}\psi+\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}\psi
for any
\psi\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).
Since
D_{\mu}
is Hermitian, we conclude that
-\frac{1}{c_{\mu}}D_{\mu}
is the Nikolayevsky derivation of
\mu
.
∎

By Theorem
4.1
, it is easy to obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
be a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
such that the eigenvalues of
cD_{\mu}
are integers prime to each other, say
k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
with multiplicities
d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{N}.

Definition 4.3
.

The data set
(k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r};d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r})
in Theorem
4.2
is called the
type of the critical point
[\mu].

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n}
be a critical point of
F_{n}
with type
\alpha=(k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r};d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r}).
Then we have

(i)

If
\alpha=(0;n)
, then
F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{4}{n}.

(ii)

If
\alpha\neq(0;n)
, then
F_{n}([\mu])=4\left(n-\frac{(k_{1}d_{1}+k_{2}d_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}d_{r})^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}d_{1}+k_{2}^{2}d_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}^{2}d_{r}}\right)^{-1}.

Proof.

We suppose that

\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu},\|\mu\|=1.

Since
\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=-2\langle\mu,\mu\rangle=-2,
then

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}(c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu})=c_{\mu}\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=-2c_{\mu},

and

F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{4}}=\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}^{2}=-2c_{\mu}.

For
(i)
, we have
D_{\mu}=0
, so
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I
and
c_{\mu}n=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=-2.
Thus
c_{\mu}=-\frac{2}{n}.

F_{n}([\mu])=-2c_{\mu}=\frac{4}{n}.

For
(ii)
, we have
D_{\mu}\neq 0
, and
c_{\mu}=-\frac{\operatorname{tr}{D}_{\mu}^{2}}{\operatorname{tr}{D}_{\mu}}
by Lemma
3.6
and Remark
3.7
. Note that

\displaystyle F_{n}([\mu])=\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}(c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu})^{2}=c_{\mu}^{2}n+c_{\mu}\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4}F_{n}([\mu])^{2}n-\frac{1}{2}F_{n}([\mu])\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu},

so we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{F_{n}([\mu])}=\frac{1}{4}n-\frac{1}{2F_{n}([\mu])}\operatorname{tr}(D_{\mu})=\frac{1}{4}n+\frac{1}{4c_{\mu}}\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}=\frac{1}{4}\left(n-\frac{(\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu})^{2}}{\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}^{2}}\right).

It follows that
F_{n}([\mu])=4\left(n-\frac{(k_{1}d_{1}+k_{2}d_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}d_{r})^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}d_{1}+k_{2}^{2}d_{2}+\cdots+k_{r}^{2}d_{r}}\right)^{-1}.

∎

4.2.
The minima of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume
[\mu]\in\mathbb{P}V_{n},
then
[\mu]
is a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with type
(0;n)
if and only if
F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{4}{n}.
Moreover,
\frac{4}{n}
is the minimum value of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

For any
0\neq\mu\in V_{n},
we use
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the eigenvalues of
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}.

Note that
\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=-2\|\mu\|^{2}
, then we have

\displaystyle F_{n}([\mu])=\frac{\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}^{2}}{\|\mu\|^{4}}=4\frac{\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}^{2}}{(\operatorname{tr}{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}})^{2}}=4\frac{(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{n})^{2}}.

It is easy to see that
F_{n}([\mu])\geq\frac{4}{n}
with equality holds if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}.
So
[\mu]
is a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with type
(0;n)
if only if
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}
is a constant multiple of
I
, if and only
F_{n}
attains its minimum value
\frac{4}{n}
at
[\mu].

∎

Theorem 4.6
.

The functional
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its minimum value at a point
[\lambda]\in\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]
if and only if
\mu
is a semisimple associative algebra. In such a case,
F_{n}([\lambda])=\frac{4}{n}.

Proof.

Consider the simple associative algebra
\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
for an integer
m>0.
We endow
\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
with the following Hermitian inner product

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle:=\operatorname{tr}AB^{*},~A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C}).

(4.3)

Then
\{E_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq m\}
is an orthonormal basis, where
E_{ij}
denote the matrices having
1
in the
(i,j)
-position and
0
elsewhere. Set
\nu:=(\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Clearly

(L_{A}^{\nu})^{*}=L_{A^{*}},\quad(R_{A}^{\nu})^{*}=R_{A^{*}}

for any
A\in\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C}).
Thus by (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{M}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{ij}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}({L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}})^{*}-2\sum_{ij}({L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}})^{*}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}-2\sum_{ij}({R}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}})^{*}{R}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{ij}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ji}}-2\sum_{ij}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ji}}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}-2\sum_{ij}{R}^{\nu}_{E_{ji}}{R}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{ij}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}E_{ji}}-2\sum_{ij}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ji}E_{ij}}-2\sum_{ij}{R}^{\nu}_{E_{ij}E_{ji}}

\displaystyle=2m\sum_{i}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ii}}-2m\sum_{i}{L}^{\nu}_{E_{ii}}-2m\sum_{i}{R}^{\nu}_{E_{ii}}

\displaystyle=2m{L}^{\nu}_{I}-2m{L}^{\nu}_{I}-2m{R}^{\nu}_{I}

\displaystyle=2mI_{m^{2}}-2mI_{m^{2}}-2mI_{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=-2mI_{m^{2}}.

So
[\nu]
is a critical point of type

(0;m^{2}).
Since
\mu
is a complex semisimple associative algebra, by Theorem
2.6
,
\mu
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{M}_{n_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{M}_{n_{2}}(\mathbb{C})\times\cdots\times\mathbb{M}_{n_{s}}(\mathbb{C})
for some positive integers
n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{s}.
It follows from Remark
3.4
that there exists a point
[\lambda]\in\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]
such that
[\lambda]
is a critical point of type

(0;n)
. So the functional
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its minimum value at
[\lambda]
, and
F_{n}([\lambda])=\frac{4}{n}
by Lemma
4.5
.

Conversely, assume that
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its minimum value at a point
[\lambda]\in\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu].
The first part of the proof implies that
\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}I
with
c_{\lambda}<0.

To prove
\mu
is semisimple, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}=(\lambda,\mathbb{C}^{n})
is semisimple. Consider the following orthogonal decompositions: (i)
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the radical of
\lambda;
(ii)
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}=\{A\in\mathcal{N}:\lambda(A,\mathcal{N})=\lambda(\mathcal{N},A)=0\}
is the annihilator of
\mathcal{N}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{Z}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{L}
.
We have
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{V}\oplus\mathcal{Z}.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Z}\neq 0.
Let
\{H_{i}\},\{V_{i}\},\{Z_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V},
and
\mathcal{Z},
respectively. Put
\{X_{i}\}=\{H_{i}\}\cup\{V_{i}\}\cup\{Z_{i}\}.
For any
0\neq Z\in\mathcal{Z}
, by hypothesis we have

\displaystyle 0>\langle\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}Z,Z\rangle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(X_{i},X_{j}),Z\rangle|^{2}-2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(Z,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle|^{2}-2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(X_{i},Z),X_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}\left\{|\langle\lambda(Z_{i},H_{j}),Z\rangle|^{2}+|\langle\lambda(H_{i},Z_{j}),Z\rangle|^{2}\right\}+\alpha{(Z)}

\displaystyle-2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(Z,H_{i}),Z_{j}\rangle|^{2}-2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(H_{i},Z),Z_{j}\rangle|^{2},

where
\alpha{(Z)}=2\sum_{ij}|\langle\lambda(Y_{i},Y_{j}),Z\rangle|^{2}\geq 0,

\{Y_{i}\}=\{H_{i}\}\cup\{V_{i}\}.
This implies

\displaystyle 0>\sum_{k}\langle\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}Z_{k},Z_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k}\alpha{(Z_{k})}\geq 0,

which is a contradiction. So
\mathcal{Z}=0
, and consequently,
\mathcal{N}=0.
Therefore
\mathcal{L}
is a semisimple associative algebra.

This completes the proof of theorem.
∎

.

4.3.
The maxima of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

We say that an algebra
\lambda
degenerates to
\mu
, write as
\lambda\rightarrow\mu
if
\mu\in\overline{\textnormal{GL}(n).\lambda}
, the closure of
\textnormal{GL}(n).\lambda
with respect to the usual topology of
V_{n}
. The degeneration
\lambda\rightarrow\mu
is called
direct degeneration
if there are no nontrivial chains:
\lambda\rightarrow\nu\rightarrow\mu.
The
degeneration level
of an algebra is the maximum length of chain of direct degenerations.

Theorem 4.8

(
[
3
]
)
.

An
n
-dimensional associative algebra is of degeneration level one if and only if it is isomorphic to one of the following

(1)

\mu_{l}
:
\mu_{l}(X_{1},X_{i})=X_{i},~i=1,\cdots,n;

(2)

\mu_{r}
:
\mu_{r}(X_{i},X_{1})=X_{i},~i=1,\cdots,n;

(3)

\mu_{ca}
:
\mu_{s}(X_{1},X_{1})=X_{2}
,

where
\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\}
is a basis.

Theorem 4.9
.

The functional
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its maximal value at a point
[\mu]\in L_{n},

n\geq 3
if and only if
\mu
is isomorphic to the
commutative associative algebra
\mu_{ca}
. In such a case,
F_{n}([\mu])=20.

Proof.

Assume that
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its maximal value at a point
[\mu]\in\mathcal{A}_{n},

n\geq 3.
By Theorem
3.3
, we know that
[\mu]
is also a critical of
F_{n}:\mathbb{P}V_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
Then it follows Theorem
3.5
that
F_{n}|_{\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]}
also attains its minimum value at a point
[\mu]
, consequently
F_{n}|_{\textnormal{GL}.[\mu]}
is a constant, so

\displaystyle\textnormal{GL}(n).[\mu]=\textnormal{U}(n).[\mu]

(4.4)

The relation (
4.4
) implies that the only non-trivial degeneration of
\mu
is
0
(
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
), consequently the degeneration level of
\mu
is
1
.

It is easy to see that the critical points
[\mu_{l}]
,
[\mu_{r}]
are both of type
(0<1;1,n-1)
, and
[\mu_{ca}]
is of type
(3<5<6;1,n-2,1).

By Proposition

, we know

\displaystyle F_{n}([\mu_{ca}])=20>4=F_{n}([\mu_{l}])=F_{n}([\mu_{r}]).

So the theorem is proved.
∎

4.4.
The structure for the critical points of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

In the following, we discuss the structure for an arbitrary critical points of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
[\mu]
be a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
of type
(k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r};d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r})
, where
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
. Consider the orthogonal decomposition

\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathfrak{A}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{A}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{A}_{+},

where
\mathfrak{A}_{-},

\mathfrak{A}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{+}
denote the direct sum of eigenspaces of
D_{\mu}
with eigenvalues smaller than zero, equal to zero and larger than zero, respectively. Then the following conditions hold:

(i)

\textnormal{ann}(\mu)\subset\mathfrak{A}_{+},
where
\textnormal{ann}(\mu)
is the annihilator of
\mu

(ii)

\mathfrak{A}_{+}\subset N(\mu),
where
N(\mu)
is the radical of
\mu
.

(iii)

\mathfrak{A}_{-}\subset(C(\mu)\cap N(\mu))\setminus\textnormal{ann}(\mu),
where
C(\mu)
is the center of
\mu
.

(iv)

(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
for any
A\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
.
So the induced Lie algebra of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
is reductive.

Proof.

For (i), assume that
X\in\textnormal{ann}(\mu)
and
D_{\mu}X=cX
, then by (
3
)

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,X\rangle=2\sum_{i,j}|\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),X\rangle|^{2}\geq 0.

Since
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
, then
0\leq\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,X\rangle=(c_{\mu}+c)\langle X,X\rangle
. It follows from Lemma
3.6
that
c\geq-c_{\mu}>0.
This proves (i).

For (ii), it is an immediate consequence of (iii) by Remark
2.4
. Now, we prove (iii) as follows. Assume that
D_{\mu}X=cX
for some
c<0
. Since

cL_{X}^{\mu}=[D_{\mu},L_{X}^{\mu}],

cR_{X}^{\mu}=[D_{\mu},R_{X}^{\mu}],

then

\displaystyle c\operatorname{tr}(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})^{*}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}[D_{\mu},(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})](L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})^{*}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}[\textnormal{M}_{\mu},(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})](L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})^{*}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}[(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu}),(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})^{*}].

Noting that
(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
, by Corollary
3.2
we have

\displaystyle c\operatorname{tr}(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})^{*}\geq 0.

It follows that
(L_{X}^{\mu}-R_{X}^{\mu})=0
since
c<0
. So
X\in C(\mu).
By Remark
2.4
, it is easy to see that
X\in N(\mu)
.
Using (i), we conclude
\mathfrak{A}_{-}\subset(C(\mu)\cap N(\mu))\setminus\textnormal{ann}(\mu).
This proves (iii).

For (iv), we first note that

\displaystyle[D_{\mu},L_{A}^{\mu}]=L_{D_{\mu}A}^{\mu},\quad[D_{\mu},R_{A}^{\mu}]=R_{D_{\mu}A}^{\mu},

for any
A\in\mathfrak{A}.

If
A\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
, we have
[D_{\mu},L_{A}^{\mu}]=[D_{\mu},R_{A}^{\mu}]=0
, and so

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\textnormal{M}_{\mu}[(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu}),(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu})[(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu}),(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}D_{\mu}[(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu}),(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}[D_{\mu},(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})](L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}

\displaystyle=0.

By Corollary
3.2
, it follows that
(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})^{*}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
since
(L_{A}^{\mu}-R_{A}^{\mu})\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
. This proves (iv).
∎

In the sequel, we give a description of the critical points in terms of those which are nilpotent.
Let
[\lambda]
be a nilpotent critical point of
F_{m}:\mathcal{A}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Define

\displaystyle L(\lambda):

\displaystyle=\{\Phi\in\textnormal{End}(\mathbb{C}^{m}):\Phi(\lambda(X,Y))=\lambda(\Phi X,Y)\},

\displaystyle R(\lambda):

\displaystyle=\{\Psi\in\textnormal{End}(\mathbb{C}^{m}):\Psi(\lambda(X,Y))=\lambda(X,\Psi Y)\}.

Moreover, we set

\Gamma_{l}=\{\Phi\in L(\lambda):[\Phi,\Psi]=0,\forall\Psi\in R(\lambda)\},

\Gamma_{r}=\{\Psi\in R(\lambda):[\Phi,\Psi]=0,\forall\Phi\in L(\lambda)\}
, and

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda):

\displaystyle=\{(\Phi,\Psi)\in\Gamma_{l}\times\Gamma_{r}:\lambda(\cdot,\Phi(\cdot))=\lambda(\Psi(\cdot),\cdot)\}.

For any
(\Phi_{i},\Psi_{i})\in\Gamma(\lambda)
,
i=1,2
, we define
(\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1})(\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2}):=(\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2}\Psi_{1}).
Then it follows that
\Gamma(\lambda)
is an associative algebra.

Lemma 4.11
.

Assume that
\mathcal{S}\subset\Gamma(\lambda)
is a subalgebra such that
(\Phi^{*},\Psi^{*})\in\mathcal{S}
for any
(\Phi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathcal{S}
is a semisimple associative algebra.

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{S}
is an associative algebra of matrices, which are closed under conjugate transpose. Define an Hermitian inner product on
\mathcal{S}
by

\displaystyle\langle H_{1},H_{2}\rangle:=\operatorname{tr}{H_{1}H_{2}^{*}}=\operatorname{tr}{\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2}^{*}}+\operatorname{tr}{\Psi_{1}\Psi_{2}^{*}},~\forall H_{i}=(\Phi_{i},\Psi_{i})\in\mathcal{S},i=1,2.

Then it follows that
\langle HH_{1},H_{2}\rangle=\langle H_{1},H^{*}H_{2}\rangle
,
\langle H_{1}H,H_{2}\rangle=\langle H_{1},H_{2}H^{*}\rangle
for any
H,H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{S}.

Let
I
be an ideal in
\mathcal{S}
and
I^{\perp}
denote the orthogonal complement of
I
.
Then it is easy to see that
I^{\perp}
is also an ideal of
\mathcal{S}.
Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{R}\oplus\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the radical of
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
. It follows that
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{N}
are both ideals of
\mathcal{S}.
Moreover,
\mathcal{R}
is semisimple, and
\mathcal{N}
is the annihilator of
\mathcal{S}
(by considering the derived series). Since
\mathcal{S}
is an associative algebra of matrices which are closed under conjugate transpose, then
HH^{*}=0
for any
H\in\mathcal{N}
, hence
H=0
. So
\mathcal{N}=0
, and
\mathcal{S}
is semisimple.
∎

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
[\lambda]
be a nilpotent critical point of
F_{m}:\mathcal{A}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}I+D_{\lambda}
of type
(k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r};d_{2},\cdots,d_{r}),
where
c_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\lambda}\in\textnormal{Der}(\lambda)
. Assume that

\mathcal{S}\subset\Gamma(\lambda)
is a subalgebra of dimension
d_{1}
such that
(\Phi^{*},\Psi^{*})\in\mathcal{S}
,
[D_{\lambda},\Phi]=[D_{\lambda},\Psi]=0
for any
(\Phi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{S}.

Consider the following semidirect sum

\displaystyle\mu=\mathcal{S}\ltimes\lambda,

where

\displaystyle\mu((\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1})+X_{1},(\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2})+X_{2}):=(\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2}\Psi_{1})+\Phi_{1}(X_{2})+\Psi_{2}(X_{1})+X_{1}X_{2},

for any
(\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1}),(\Phi_{2},\Psi_{2})\in\mathcal{S}
,
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then
\mu
is an associative algebra. If we extend the Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
by setting

\displaystyle\langle H,K\rangle=-\frac{2}{c_{\lambda}}(\operatorname{tr}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{{K}^{*}}+\operatorname{tr}{H}{K}^{*}),~~H,K\in\mathcal{S},

then
[\mu]
is a critical point of type
(0,k_{2}<\cdots<k_{r};d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{r})
for the functional
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
n=d_{1}+m.

Proof.

For any
H=(\Phi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle L^{\mu}_{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}&0\\
0&\Phi\end{array}\right),\quad R^{\mu}_{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}R^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}&0\\
0&\Psi\end{array}\right),

where
L^{\mu}_{H}
,
R^{\mu}_{H}
(resp.
L^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
,
R^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
) denote the left and right multiplication by
H
of the algebra
\mu
(resp.
\mathcal{S}
), respectively. By Lemma
4.11
, we know that
\mathcal{S}
is a semisimple associative algebra. Then it follows that there is an orthonormal basis
\{H_{i}=(\Phi_{i},\Psi_{i})\}\subset\mathcal{S}
such that
{\Phi_{i}}^{*}=-\Phi_{i}
,
{\Psi_{i}}^{*}=-\Psi_{i}
, and
L^{\mu}_{H_{i}}
,
R^{\mu}_{H_{i}}
are skew-Hermitian for each
i.
Let
\{H_{i}\}\cup\{X_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathcal{S}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{m}.
Then for any
H=(\Phi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{S}
and
X\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,H\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X_{i},X),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},H),X_{j}\rangle}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(H,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\lambda(X_{i},X),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\Psi(X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\lambda(X,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\Phi(X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i}\langle\lambda(X_{i},X),\Psi(X_{i})\rangle-2\sum_{i}\langle\lambda(X,X_{i}),\Phi(X_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\operatorname{tr}\Psi^{*}R^{\lambda}_{X}-2\operatorname{tr}\Phi^{*}L^{\lambda}_{X}

\displaystyle=-2\operatorname{tr}R^{\lambda}_{\Psi^{*}(X)}-2\operatorname{tr}L^{\lambda}_{\Phi^{*}(X)}

\displaystyle=0,

where
L^{\lambda}_{X}
,
R^{\lambda}_{X}
denote the left and right multiplication by
X
of the algebra
\lambda
, respectively,
and the last two equalities follow from that
\lambda
is nilpotent and
(\Phi^{*},\Psi^{*})\in\mathcal{S}.
Moreover, since
{\Phi_{i}}^{*}=-\Phi_{i}
,
{\Psi_{i}}^{*}=-\Psi_{i}
for each
i
, then

[\Phi_{i},{\Phi_{i}}^{*}]=0
,
[\Psi_{i},{\Psi_{i}}^{*}]=0.
So by (
3
) we have

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,Y\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j}\overline{\langle\mu(H_{i},X_{j}),X\rangle}\langle\mu(H_{i},X_{j}),Y\rangle+2\sum_{i,j}\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},H_{j}),X\rangle}\langle\mu(X_{i},H_{j}),Y\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{i,j}\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),X\rangle}\langle\mu(X_{i},X_{j}),Y\rangle-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(H_{i},X),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(H_{i},Y),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X_{i},X),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},Y),X_{j}\rangle}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X,H_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(Y,H_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(Y,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\langle\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}X,Y\rangle+2\sum_{i}\langle[\Phi_{i},{\Phi_{i}}^{*}](X,Y\rangle+2\sum_{i}\langle[\Psi_{i},{\Psi_{i}}^{*}](X),Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}X,Y\rangle,

for any
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Therefore
{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}|_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\textnormal{M}_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}I+D_{\lambda}.
On the other hand, noting that
L^{\mu}_{H_{i}}
and
R^{\mu}_{H_{i}}
are skew-Hermitian for each
i
, then for any
H=(\Phi,\Psi)\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}H,H\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j}\overline{\langle\mu(H_{i},H_{j}),H\rangle}\langle\mu(H_{i},H_{j}),H\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(H_{i},H),H_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(H_{i},H),H_{j}\rangle}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(X_{i},H),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(X_{i},H),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(H,H_{i}),H_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(H,H_{i}),H_{j}\rangle}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle\mu(H,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle\overline{\langle\mu(H,X_{i}),X_{j}\rangle}

\displaystyle=-2(\operatorname{tr}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{{H}^{*}}+\operatorname{tr}{\Phi\Phi^{*}}+\operatorname{tr}{\Psi\Psi^{*}})

\displaystyle=-2(\operatorname{tr}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{H}L^{\mathcal{S}}_{{H}^{*}}+\operatorname{tr}{H}{H}^{*})

\displaystyle=c_{\lambda}\langle H,H\rangle.

So
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu},
where
c_{\mu}=c_{\lambda},
and

\displaystyle D_{\mu}=\left({\begin{array}[]{*{20}{c}}0&0\\
0&D_{\lambda}\\
\end{array}}\right)\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu).

This completes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Comments

By the previous discussion, we know that the critical types of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
n=2,3
, are necessarily nonnegative. So it is natural to ask the following question:

Let
[\mu]\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
be a critical point of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=c_{\mu}I+D_{\mu}
for some
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
and
D_{\mu}\in\textnormal{Der}(\mu)
. Are all the eigenvalues of
D_{\mu}
necessarily nonnegative?

On the other hand, it will be also interesting to construct or classify the critical points
[\mu]
of
F_{n}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
D_{\mu}
has negative eigenvalues if the above question does not hold. We note that
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebras are automatically associative algebras, so it follows from
[
13
, Example 1]
that there exist associative algebras whose Nikolayevsky derivations do admit negative eigenvalues.
