A central limit theorem for square ice
Wei Wu

Abstract
. We prove that the height function associated with the uniform six-vertex model (or equivalently, the uniform homomorphism height function from ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} to ℤ \mathbb{Z} ) satisfies a central limit theorem, upon some logarithmic rescaling.



1.
introduction

The six vertex model was initially proposed by Pauling
[
18
]
as an ideal model for ice. The square ice, which is the six vertex model on the two dimensional square lattice, can be defined using arrow configurations on the edges that satisfies the ice rule: at each vertex there is an equal number of incoming and outcoming arrows. This leads to six possible configurations at each vertex. The six-vertex model with weights
a,b,c
can be defined by giving the probability weight proportional to
a^{n_{1}+n_{2}}b^{n_{3}+n_{4}}c^{n_{5}+n_{6}}
, where
n_{i}
is the number of vertices of configuration type
i
, for every configuration that satisfies the ice rule.

Figure 1.
The six possible configurations, each has a weight
a,a,b,b
and
c,c
.

A six-vertex configuration naturally induces a height function, defined on the dual square lattice, by the following rule. Let
e^{*}:=(u,v)
be two adjacent vertices on the dual square lattice, define the height difference,
h(v)-h(u)
to be
+1
(respectively,
-1
), if the associated arrow of the primal edge
e
is crossed from left to right (resp. from right to left) when going from
u
to
v
. Thanks to the ice rule, the height function on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is well defined up to an additive constant. Alternatively, one may define the height function as an integer valued random surface
h:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}
, such that
|h(u)-h(v)|=1
for every adjacent
u,v
. Hence it is also called homomorphism height functions, as it takes even values on even sublattices and odd values on odd sublattices.

One may also define the six-vertex model and its associated height function in a finite domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, as long as the boundary condition is
admissible
, meaning that it extends to at least one six-vertex configuration (and homomorphism height function) in
D
.

The phase diagram of the six-vertex model (with respect to
a,b,c
) can be found in
[
4
]
. It is believed, that as long as
\Delta:=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}}{2ab}\in(-1,1)
, the model is disordered, such that the associated height function delocalizes, as long as we take the size of the domain to infinity. The conjectured delocalization was only proved in a few limited regions of the phase space. There are extensive studies at the free fermion point
a=b=1,c=\sqrt{2}
, where a bijection to a dimer model is available, and the scaling limit of the height function is given by a Gaussian free field
[
15
,
6
]
. The Gaussian free field scaling limit also extends to a neighborhood of the free fermion point, using renormalization group arguments
[
12
]
. Another special case of interest is
a=b=1,c=2
, where delocalization of the height function is obtained via a coupling to the random cluster model
[
10
,
13
]
.

The case
a=b=c=1
, which corresponds to the uniform square-ice model (and uniform homomorphism height function), has received great interest and is the subject of this paper. Considerable progress has been made in recent years and delocalization of the corresponding height function has been established in
[
7
,
5
,
20
]
. The long standing conjecture, that the height function has a Gaussian free field (GFF) scaling limit, still remains open. The proof in
[
7
]
is quantitative and obtains a logarithmic fluctuation of the height function, which is coherent with the GFF scaling limit. However, the exact asymptotics of the variance, and the Gaussianity in the limit, are still missing. Recently, the logarithmic fluctuations have been established for all values
a=b=1,c\in(1,2)

[
8
]
, and rotation invariance of any subsequential limit was obtained for
a=b=1,c\in(\sqrt{3},2)
in
[
9
]
. The GFF scaling limit were also expected in the whole region
a=b=1,c\in(1,2)
. Let us also mention that the uniform square-ice model has an explicit bijection to the uniform proper 3-colorings on the dual graph. For proper 3-colorings in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, an algebraic rate of mixing and Bernoullicity of the measure, which is suggested by the logarithmic fluctuation of the square-ice, was proved in
[
19
]
.

In this paper we give another input to the conjectured Gaussian free field scaling limit for the uniform square ice height function, by proving a central limit theorem.

To state the result, let a path (
\times
-path) be a sequence of vertices
v_{0},\cdots v_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, such that for every
0\leq i<n
,
v_{i}
and
v_{i+1}
are at a Euclidean distance
1
(resp.
\sqrt{2}
) of each other. A finite subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
whose boundary is a
\times
-circuit of even (resp. odd) vertices is called an even (resp. odd) domain. Let
\phi_{N}
be the uniform distribution on homomorphism height functions defined on an even domain
D_{N}
, where
[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq D_{N}\subseteq[-N-1,N+1]\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with zero boundary condition, we prove that

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}<\infty
and
\sigma_{N}
, such that
C_{1}(\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sigma_{N}\leq C_{2}(\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and such that
\phi_{N}(0)/\sigma_{N}
converges in distribution to a normal random variable.

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem
1.1
implies the Gaussianity of the scaling limit. It is, however, insufficient to give the asymoptotic convergence of the variance
\frac{\sigma_{N}}{\sqrt{\log N}}\to g
for some
g>0
, which we believe require new ideas. It is conceivable, that if such asymptotic convergence were established, then combining with the Gaussianity proof in this paper one can resolve the conjectured GFF scaling limit. See Section
5
for more discussions.

We also obtain a multivariate CLT, jointly for the height function
(\phi_{N}(x_{i,N}))_{i=1}^{k}
in the bulk of
D_{N}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x_{1},\cdots x_{k}\in(-1,1)\times(-1,1)
, and suppose
x_{1,N},\cdots,x_{k,N}
are points in
D_{N}
such that
\frac{1}{N}x_{i,N}\to x_{i}
as
N\to\infty
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}<\infty
and
\sigma_{1,N},\cdots,\sigma_{k,N},
, satisfying
C_{1}(\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\sigma_{i,N}\leq C_{2}(\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, for all
i\in{1,\cdots,k}
, and such that
\phi_{N}(x_{1,N})/\sigma_{1,N},\cdots,\phi_{N}(x_{k,N})/\sigma_{k,N}
converges in distribution to independent normal random variables.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
and
1.3
is based on an application of the martingale central limit theorem. The key inputs include a Russo-Seymour-Welsh estimate for the level line of the height function (established in
[
7
]
), the FKG inequality for the absolute value height function and the monotone coupling (used in
[
7
]
and
[
19
]
), and random walk estimates. We will summarize these tools in the next section, and the proof of Theorem
1.1
and
1.3
will be given in Section
3
and
4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

We prove Theorem
1.1
by constructing a martingale decomposition and applying a martingale difference CLT. We start by defining the geometric scales and the relevant martingale differences. For
k=1,\cdots\log N
, let
r_{k}=2^{-k}N
, and denote by
B_{k}(x)
an even domain centered at
x
and is nested between two boxes
[x-r_{k},x+r_{k}]^{2}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subseteq B_{k}(x)\subseteq[x-r_{k}-1,x+r_{k}+1]^{2}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. When
x=0
we simply denote
B_{k}(0)
by
B_{k}
, Also denote by
A_{r_{k},r_{l}}(x):=B_{l}(x)\setminus B_{k}(x)
, and
A_{r_{k},r_{l}}:=A_{r_{k},r_{l}}(0)
. We define a sequence of level loops
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1},\cdots
, of the height function such that
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\partial D_{N}
, and

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\text{ outermost loop contained in }\mathcal{L}_{k-1}\text{ such that }\left|\phi_{N}|_{\mathcal{L}_{k}}-\phi_{N}|_{\mathcal{L}_{k-1}}\right|=2.

We also define a sequence of level circuit
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\log N}
of the height function, such that

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\text{ outermost level loop }(\mathcal{L}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ contained in }B_{k}.

We may view
\mathcal{C}_{k}
as evaluating
(\mathcal{L}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
at random times. Notice that
\mathcal{C}_{k}
can be defined via a standard exploration process of the height function from
\partial B_{k}
.

Given a domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let
\text{int}(D):=D\setminus\partial D
, and we define
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma\{\phi(x),x\notin\text{int }{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\}
. Decompose
\phi_{N}(0)
into a telescoping sum:

\phi_{N}(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(0)|\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(0)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]

and denote by the martingale difference
\Delta_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(0)|\mathcal{F}_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(0)|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]
. We notice that
\Delta_{k}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
.

We recall the following martingale difference CLT stated in
[
14
]

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X_{k}
be a martingale difference array satisfying

•

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k\leq n}|X_{k}|\right]\to 0

•

\sum_{k=1}^{n}|X_{k}|^{2}\to 1
in probability,

then
S_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}
converges in distribution to a standard normal.

We will apply the martingale difference CLT with
X_{k}:=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
. This implies Theorem
1.1
with
\sigma_{N}:=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

The first assumption in Theorem
3.1
follows from the tail estimate for
\Delta_{k}
, which is a consequence of Proposition
2.6
. Apply Proposition
2.6
, we conclude that for all
k<\log N-200
, we have
\mathbb{P}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\Delta_{k}>n]\leq e^{-cn\log(n\wedge 2^{k})}
. A union bound (for
\log N
random variables with exponential tails) thus implies there is some
C<\infty
, such that

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}[\max_{k\leq\log N}|\Delta_{k}|]\leq\mathbb{E}[\max_{k\leq\log N-200}|\Delta_{k}|]+\mathbb{E}[\max_{\log N-200\leq k\leq\log N}|\Delta_{k}|]\leq C\log\log N.

Another application of Proposition
2.6
yields
c_{1}\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]\leq c_{2}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
. Therefore
c_{1}\log N\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]\leq c_{2}\log N
, which implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{k\leq n}|X_{k}|\right]\to 0
.

It suffices to check the second assumption in Theorem
3.1
. Since
c_{1}\log N\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]\leq c_{2}\log N
, it suffices to show

(3.2)

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}[\Delta_{k}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{2}]]\to 0

in probability.

Let
N(A_{r_{k+\log\log N},r_{k}})
be the number of level loops contained in
A_{r_{k+\log\log N},r_{k}}
. Consider the truncated random variable
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}:=\Delta_{k}1_{N(A_{r_{k+\log\log N},r_{k}})\geq 1}
, which is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{k+\log\log N}
, and notice that whenever
l-k>\log\log N
,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}
only depends on the information in
\sigma\{\phi(x),x\notin\text{int }{\mathcal{C}_{l}}\}
. We claim it suffices to show

(3.3)

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}]]\to 0

in probability. Indeed, it follows from Proposition
2.6
that for there exist
c>0
and
C<\infty
, such that for all
k\leq\log N-\log\log N-200
,
\mathbb{P}_{D_{N}}^{0}[N(A_{r_{k+\log\log N},r_{k}})=0]\leq C(\log N)^{-c}
. Thus

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[|\Delta_{k}^{2}-\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}|]\leq\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\Delta_{k}^{4}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{D_{N}}^{0}[N(A_{r_{k+\log\log N},r_{k}})=0]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(\log N)^{-c/2}.

The weak law of large numbers (

Lemma 3.2
.

There exist
C,K_{0}<\infty
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
, such that for every

, and

\cov[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2},\widetilde{\Delta}_{l}^{2}]\leq\frac{C}{(k-l-\log\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\log N)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}]]\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{(\log N)^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{\log N-K_{0}}\left(C\log\log N+\sum_{k=l+\log\log N+1}^{\log N-K_{0}}\frac{C}{(k-l-\log\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}\right)+\frac{CK_{0}^{2}}{(\log N)^{2}}

The estimates (

|h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}|

inside
B_{l^{*}}
. This gives rise to a natural monotone coupling of
h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}
and
h_{l^{*}}
inside
B_{l^{*}}

[
19
]

[
21
]
), which we now recall.

•

We explore the height function

B_{l^{*}}

in

is that of a uniform homomorphism height function in

•

The FKG for absolute value height function implies that
|h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}|
restricted in
B_{l^{*}}
stochastically dominates
|h_{l^{*}}^{0}|
. Consequently, we can construct a coupling between them inside
B_{l^{*}}
by simultaneously exploring and revealing the values of both
|h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}|
and
|h_{l^{*}}^{0}|
, vertex by vertex, starting from the boundary of
B_{l^{*}}
inwards, ensuring along the way that
|h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}|\geq|h_{l^{*}}^{0}|
. We stop the exploration when we discover the outermost height zero loop of
h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}
sourrounding
0
and inside
B_{l^{*}}
, and denote this loop by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

•

The stochastic domination and the domain Markov property implies that we can jointly sample
|h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}|
and
|h_{l^{*}}^{0}|
inside the domain enclosed by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, according to a single sample from their common distribution. In this case, we stop the exploration process and obtain a coupling of the two height functions that are equal inside
\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Suppose the level loop
\mathcal{L}^{*}
of
h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}
with height 0, constructed in the exploration process given above, satisfies
\mathcal{L}^{*}\subseteq A_{r_{k},r_{l^{*}}}
, then the coupling above implies
h_{\mathcal{C}_{l+}}^{0}=h_{l^{*}}^{0}=0
on
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, and we may couple the two fields so that they agree inside
\mathcal{L}^{*}
. This gives

(3.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}1_{\mathcal{L}^{*}\subseteq A_{r_{k},r_{l^{*}}}}|\mathcal{C}_{l+}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{B_{l^{*}}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}1_{\mathcal{L}^{*}\subseteq A_{r_{k},r_{l^{*}}}}]

To estimate the probability that the coupling fails, denote by
M
the total number of level loops contained in
\mathcal{C}_{l+}\setminus B_{k}
. We write
M=N+N^{\prime}
, where
N
is the total number of level loops contained in
A_{r_{k},r_{l^{*}},}
, and
N^{\prime}
is the number of level loops that intersects
\partial B_{l^{*}}
. Note that conditioned on
N^{\prime}
and
N
, the height function on the level loops forms a simple random walk in
2\mathbb{Z}
, and therefore we may use a last visit decomposition (conditioning on the last visit to zero in the first
N^{\prime}
steps) to obtain

(3.7)

\mathbb{P}[S_{N^{\prime}+1}S_{N^{\prime}+2}\cdots S_{M}\neq 0]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}[S_{N^{\prime}+1}S_{N^{\prime}+2}\cdots S_{M}\neq 0|S_{i}=0]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}\frac{C}{(M-i)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{CN^{\prime}}{N^{1/2}},

where the second inequality follows from the ballot theorem (Proposition
3.3
). Also notice, by Lemma
2.6
,
there exist
c>0
and
C<\infty
such that

(3.8)

\mathbb{P}[N^{\prime}>n]\leq e^{-cn\log(n\wedge 2^{K_{0}})}

and

(3.9)

\mathbb{P}[N<c(k-l-\log\log N)]\leq Ce^{-c(k-l-\log\log N)}

Combining these, we may choose
K_{0}=K_{0}(c,C)
large enough, and a small constant
\varepsilon>0
, such that the probability of coupling failure is bounded above by

(3.10)

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}^{*}\not\subseteq A_{r_{k},r_{l^{*}}}]\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\log(k-l-\log\log N)}{(k-l-\log\log N)^{1/2}}+\mathbb{P}[N^{\prime}>\log(k-l-\log\log N)]+\mathbb{P}[N<c(k-l-\log\log N)]\\
\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{(k-l-\log\log N)^{1/2-\varepsilon}}.

Since
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}
have all moments finite, which follows from Lemma
2.6
, we apply the Hölder inequality to obtain (
3.4
) (with a larger
\varepsilon
).
∎

4.
The Multivariate CLT

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
, by modifying the argument in the previous section. By definition, it suffices to show for any
a_{1},\cdots a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})/\sigma_{i,N}
converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}(0,\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2})
.

Let us set up some notations, similar to Section
3
. For each
x_{i,N}\in D_{N}
, we define a sequence of level loops
\mathcal{L}_{0}(x_{i,N}),\mathcal{L}_{1},(x_{i,N})\cdots
, of the height function such that
\mathcal{L}_{0}(x_{i,N})=\partial D_{N}
, and

\mathcal{L}_{m}(x_{i,N})=\text{ outermost loop contained in }\mathcal{L}_{m-1}(x_{i,N})\text{ such that }\left|\phi_{N}|_{\mathcal{L}_{m}(x_{i,N})}-\phi_{N}|_{\mathcal{L}_{m-1}(x_{i,N})}\right|=2

We also define, for each
1\leq i\leq k
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, a sequence of level circuit
\{\mathcal{C}_{m}(x_{i,N})\}_{m=1}^{\log N}
of the height function, such that

\mathcal{C}_{m}(x_{i,N})=\text{ outermost level loop }(\mathcal{L}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ contained in }B_{m}(x_{i,N}).

And we set
\mathcal{F}_{m}(x_{i,N})=\sigma\{\phi_{N}(x),x\notin\text{int }{\mathcal{C}_{m}(x_{i,N})}\}
. Decompose
\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})
into a telescoping sum:

\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})=\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})|\mathcal{F}_{m}(x_{i,N})]-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}(x_{i,N})]

Introduce the martingale difference
\Delta_{i,m}:=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})|\mathcal{F}_{m}(x_{i,N})]-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}(x_{i,N})]
, and we set
\sigma_{i,N}=\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Notice that, we may write

\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\phi_{N}(x_{i,N})/\sigma_{i,N}=\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}X_{i,m},

where
X_{i,m}:=\frac{\Delta_{i,m}}{\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}
.
We apply Theorem
3.1
to the martingale differences
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}X_{i,m}
.

The first moment condition in Theorem
3.1
follows from Proposition
2.6
as in (
3.1
), and the triangle inequality which gives

\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}\left[\max_{m\leq\log N}|\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}X_{i,m}|\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|a_{i}|\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}\left[\max_{m\leq\log N}|X_{i,m}|\right]\leq\frac{Ck\log\log N}{\log N}

which tends to
0
as
N\to\infty
.

To check the second moment condition of Theorem
3.1
, note that

\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}X_{i,m}\right)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\Delta_{i,m}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]}+2\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{a_{i}a_{j}\Delta_{i,m}\Delta_{j,m}}{\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{j,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

It follows from the proof of the one point CLT in Theorem
1.1
(in particular, the proof of (
3.2
)) that the diagonal terms

\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}a_{i}^{2}\frac{\Delta_{i,m}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]}\to a_{i}^{2}

in probability. We claim that the off-diagonal term will not contribute in the limit: for every
1\leq i<j\leq k
,

(4.1)

\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}\frac{a_{i}a_{j}\Delta_{i,m}\Delta_{j,m}}{\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{i,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{j,m})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}\to 0

in probability. Combine these estimates we conclude Theorem
1.3
.

Similar to the previous section, we define the truncated random variable
\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}:=\Delta_{i,m}1_{N(A_{r_{m+\log\log N},r_{m}}(x_{i,N}))\geq 1}
. It follows from Lemma
2.6
that all moments of
\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}
and
\Delta_{i,m}
are close. Therefore using
c_{1}\log N\leq\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[(\sum\Delta_{j,m})^{2}]\leq C_{2}\log N
it suffices to prove (
4.1
) for the random variables
\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}
:

(4.2)

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}a_{i}a_{j}\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}\to 0

in probability.

The estimate (
4.2
) follows from the decoupling estimate below. Define

m_{0}:=\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\text{ such that }\forall 1\leq i<j\leq k,B_{m}(x_{i,N})\cap B_{m}(x_{j,N})=\emptyset\}

being the first scale that separates the points
(x_{i,N})_{i=1}^{k}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
1\leq i<j\leq k
. There exists
C,K_{0}<\infty
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
, such that for every
m-m_{0}>\log\log N+1
, and
\log N-m>K_{0}
, we have

(4.3)

\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}]\leq\frac{C}{(m-m_{0}-\log\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}

Moreover, for every
m_{0}\leq l<m\leq\log N-K_{0}
such that
m-l>\log\log N+1
, we have

(4.4)

\cov[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m};\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,l}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,l}]\leq\frac{C}{(m-l-\log\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}

Assuming the lemma, we may estimate the second moment in (
4.2
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\log N)^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{\log N}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}^{2}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}^{2}]+\frac{1}{(\log N)^{2}}\sum_{l,m=1}^{\log N}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\cov[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m};\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,l}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,l}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{1}{\log N}+\frac{1}{\log N}\left(C_{2}\log\log N+\sum_{l=1}^{\log N-K_{0}}\sum_{m=l+\log\log N+2}^{\log N-K_{0}}\frac{C}{(m-l-\log\log N)^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{CK_{0}^{2}}{(\log N)^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{(\log N)^{{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}}

which tends to 0 as
N\to\infty
.

Finally we explain how Lemma
4.1
follows from a similar proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Denote by
m_{0}^{*}=m_{0}+\log\log N
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{m_{0}^{*}+}(x_{i,N})
be the innermost level loop outside
B_{r_{m_{0}^{*}}}(x_{i,N})
. We notice that,
\mathcal{C}_{m_{0}^{*}+}(x_{i,N})\subseteq A_{r_{m_{0}^{*}},r_{m_{0}}}(x_{i,N})
. We may write
\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}|\mathcal{C}_{m_{0}^{*}+}(x_{i,N})\right]\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}\right]
. Notice that, the same monotone coupling argument that leads to (
3.4
) gives that for every
m-m_{0}>\log\log N+1
, and
\log N-m>K_{0}
,

(4.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}|\mathcal{C}_{m_{0}^{*}+}(x_{i,N})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{B_{m_{0}^{*}}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}]+O(\frac{1}{(m-m_{0}-\log\log N)^{1/2-\varepsilon}})=O(\frac{1}{(m-m_{0}-\log\log N)^{1/2-\varepsilon}})

This yields (
4.3
).

To prove (
4.4
), denote by
l^{*}=l+\log\log N
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{l^{*}+}(x_{i,N})
be the innermost level loop outside
B_{r_{l^{*}}}(x_{i,N})
. We notice that,
\mathcal{C}_{l+}\subseteq A_{r_{l^{*}},r_{l}}(x_{i,N})
. We may write
\mathbb{E}_{D_{N}}^{0}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,l}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,l}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}|\mathcal{C}_{l^{*}+}(x_{i,N})\right]\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,m}\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,l}\widetilde{\Delta}_{j,l}\right]
.
The same argument that leads to (
3.4
) gives that for
m-l>\log\log N+1

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\Delta}_{i,m}|\mathcal{C}_{l^{*}+}(x_{i,N})\right]=O(\frac{1}{(m-l-\log\log N)^{1/2-\varepsilon}})

Combining this with (
4.3
) we obtain (
4.4
).
∎

5.
Open questions

Asymptotic variance.
As mentioned in the introduction, it remains open to prove
\frac{\sigma_{N}}{\log^{\frac{1}{2}}N}\to g
, for some
g>0
. From our point of view, this remains the main challenge to obtain the conjectured GFF scaling limit for the uniform square ice height function.

The proof presented in Section
3
suggests that asymptotic convergence of the variance would follow from

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=1}^{\log N}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}^{2}]\to g

Proving this claim requires new ideas, and essentially requires some approximate scale invariance.

Mixing rate for the height function.
We obtained an upper bound for the mixing rate for the gradients of the height function, which is algebraic in terms of the geometric scale (see Lemma
3.2
). We expect that the precise mixing rate is algebraic in the physical scale, similar to the gradients of DGFF.

Central limit theorem for generalised solid-on-solid models.
The (generalised) solid-on-solid models is an important class of random surface models which exhibit a roughening transition in two dimensions. To define it, let
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}
be integer valued height functions sampled from the Gibbs weight

d\mu(\phi)=\text{normalization}\times\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{x\sim y}V(\phi(x)-\phi(y))\right)

where
V:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is a symmetric convex potential. Prominent examples include
V(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}
(Discrete Gaussian) and
V(x)=|x|
. For
\beta
sufficiently small, it is conjectured that the scaling limit of the height function is given by a GFF. The conjecture is still widely open (except for the quadratic case). The seminal work of Fröhlich and Spencer
[
11
]
established the logarithmic fluctuation of the Discrete Gaussian and the absolute value potential model. There are extensive recent results on solid-on-solid models: the GFF scaling limit for the Discrete Gaussian case was proved in
[
2
,
3
]
, and the (qualitative) delocalization were established for general potential
[
16
,
17
,
1
,
22
]
.

It is conceivable that the method of this paper also extends to the Gaussianity of limit for generalised solid-on-solid models, which remains open except for the quadratic case. Indeed, the FKG inequality for the absolute value height function has been established for the solid-on-solid models (see e.g.,
[
17
]
). If the Russo-Seymour-Welsh estimate can be proved then one can carry out the arguments of this paper to obtain the analogues of Theorem
1.1
.
