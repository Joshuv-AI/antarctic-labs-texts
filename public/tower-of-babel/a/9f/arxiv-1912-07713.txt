Two examples of Wilf-collapse
Michael Albert, Vít Jelínek, Michal Opler

Abstract
Two permutation classes, the X-class and subpermutations of the increasing oscillation are shown to exhibit an exponential Wilf-collapse. This means that the number of distinct enumerations of principal subclasses of each of these classes grows much more slowly than the class itself whereas a priori , based only on symmetries of the class, there is no reason to expect this. The underlying cause of the collapse in both cases is the ability to apply some form of local symmetry which, combined with a greedy algorithm for detecting patterns in these classes, yields a Wilf-collapse.

Introduction

The coincidences that arise when two unrelated, or peripherally related collections of combinatorial structures have the same generating function have always attracted interest. In such cases one frequently seeks an explanation of this coincidence which might be an explicit size-preserving bijection between the classes, or parallel structural descriptions of the classes which frequently amount to an implicit description of such a bijection. In this paper we are concerned with certain families of such coincidences which are called
Wilf-equivalence
. These arise when the two classes in question both occur as subsets of some universal class carrying a partial order (always interpreted as containment of structures, and presumed to satisfy the obvious conditions that such an order should have). Specifically if the universe is
{\mathcal{U}}
and it carries a partial order
\leqslant
, then, to each
\alpha\in{\mathcal{U}}
we associate the subclass of structures
avoiding

\alpha
, i.e.,

\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)=\{\tau\in{\mathcal{U}}\,:\,\alpha\not\leqslant\tau\}.

We say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
Wilf-equivalent
(in
{\mathcal{U}}
) if
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
have the same generating function.

More particularly, we are interested in situations where Wilf-equivalence is the rule, rather than the exception. For two structures to be Wilf-equivalent they must have the same size (since, for any
\alpha
the lowest order term of the generating function for
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
that differs from that of
{\mathcal{U}}
is the term of degree
|\alpha|
(where
|\alpha|
denotes the size of
\alpha
). If, as
n\to\infty
the number,
w_{n}
, the number of equivalence classes of the Wilf-equivalence relation on structures of size
n
in
{\mathcal{U}}
is small relative to the total number,
u_{n}
of structures of size
n
in
{\mathcal{U}}
then we say that
{\mathcal{U}}
exhibits a
Wilf-collapse
. More specifically, a Wilf-collapse occurs if
w_{n}=o(u_{n})
. If, for some constant
c<1
,
w_{n}=o(c^{n}u_{n})
then we say that
{\mathcal{U}}
exhibits an
exponential Wilf-collapse
.

At the risk of some confusion we will refer to the equivalence classes of the Wilf-equivalence relation in a universe
{\mathcal{U}}
as
Wilf-equivalence classes
.

Here we will demonstrate an exponential Wilf-collapse in two permutation classes,
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{SIO}}
(defined below). Our results complement those of another paper by the same authors,
[
3
]
, which includes some sufficient conditions to establish exponential Wilf-collapse. The independent interest of these two particular examples is that neither
{\mathcal{X}}
nor
{\mathcal{SIO}}
satisfies those conditions. However, both admit a natural representation of structures as words over an infinite alphabet which combines with a greedy algorithm to decide the containment relation to allow “local” applications of symmetries in creating Wilf-equivalent structures. It is in exploring this central thesis with new examples that we believe the value of this paper lies.

1.1
Permutation classes

In this subsection we give the briefest possible introduction to the basics of permutation classes as required for the remainder of the paper. For a much more comprehensive introduction we recommend
[
11
]
.

Permutations of size
n
are bijections from
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
to itself, usually written in one line notation as
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\dots\pi_{n}
. A permutation
\pi
of size
k
is
contained in
another permutation
\tau
if there is a subsequence of
k
elements in
\tau
whose relative ordering is the same as that of
\pi
. For instance 321 is contained in 3714526 because the subsequence 742 is in decreasing order. In this situation we write
\pi\leqslant\tau
and also say that
\pi
is
involved in

\tau
. If
\pi\not\leqslant\tau
then we say that
\tau

avoids

\pi
.

The
symmetries
of the universe
{\mathcal{S}}
of all permutations are the order-automorphisms of
{\mathcal{S}}
. Concretely, there are eight of them generated by:

reverse

which simply reverses the one-line representation of each permutation,

complement

which, in a permutation of size
n
replaces each value
a
in its one line notation by
n+1-a
, and

inverse

the normal inverse operation on bijections.

These are most naturally understood as operating on the set of points
(i,\pi_{i})
representing a permutation by reflection through a vertical, horizontal, or diagonal axis (and compositions of such reflections of course).

The
sum
,
\sigma=\alpha\oplus\beta
, of two permutations
\alpha
and
\beta
is the permutation of size
|\alpha|+|\beta|
where:

\sigma(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha(i)&\text{for }1\leqslant i\leqslant|\alpha|\\
|\alpha|+\beta(i-|\alpha|)&\text{for }|\alpha|<i\leqslant|\alpha|+|\beta|\end{array}\right.

That is, “place
\alpha
to the left of and below
\beta
”. If we change “below” to “above”, then we will have defined the
skew-sum
,
\alpha\ominus\beta
. A permutation is sum- (resp. skew-) indecomposable if it cannot be written as a sum (skew-sum) of two permutations both of smaller size.

A
permutation class
(or just
class
),
{\mathcal{C}}
is a collection of permutations downward-closed under containment, i.e., if
\tau\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
\pi\leqslant\tau
, then
\pi\in{\mathcal{C}}
. When we speak about Wilf-collapse it will always be within a universe that is some permutation class (specifically, the classes
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{SIO}}
introduced below).

A class is
sum-closed
if
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
implies that
\alpha\oplus\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
. As above we use
\operatorname{Av}(\pi)
to denote the set of permutations avoiding
\pi
(possibly in a given class when the context is clear), and also
\operatorname{Inv}(\pi)
to denote the set of permutations involving
\pi
.

We repeat now the definitions of Wilf-equivalence and Wilf-collapse in the context of permutation classes in particular. Throughout, we consider a particular class
{\mathcal{C}}
to be given as our “universe” and all permutations mentioned are assumed to belong to
{\mathcal{C}}
and all concepts are relative to
{\mathcal{C}}
. For instance,
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)={\mathcal{C}}\cap\{\beta\,:\,\alpha\not\leqslant\beta\}
.

Two permutations
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
are
Wilf-equivalent
if the generating functions of
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
are the same. Equivalent conditions include:

•

the generating functions of
\operatorname{Inv}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Inv}(\beta)
are the same,

•

there is a size preserving bijection between
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
,

•

there is a size preserving bijection between
\operatorname{Inv}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Inv}(\beta)
, and

•

there is a size preserving bijection from
{\mathcal{C}}
to itself that sends
\operatorname{Av}(\alpha)
to
\operatorname{Av}(\beta)
.

In different contexts we have observed any one of these equivalent conditions to be the most natural one to prove. In
{\mathcal{X}}
it will turn out to be easiest to compute (and compare) generating functions for
\operatorname{Inv}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{Inv}(\beta)
, while in
{\mathcal{SIO}}
we demonstrate the existence of implicitly defined bijections.

Let
c_{n}
denote the number of permutations in
{\mathcal{C}}
of size
n
and
w_{n}
the number of Wilf-equivalence classes among those. Then we say that
{\mathcal{C}}
exhibits a
Wilf-collapse
if
w_{n}=o(c_{n})
, and an
exponential Wilf-collapse
if
w_{n}=o(r^{n}c_{n})
for some
0<r<1
.

In
[
3
]
we demonstrated some sufficient conditions for Wilf-collapse in sum-closed classes. One of these, Theorem 4.1, was:

Theorem
.

Any supercritical sum-closed class,
{\mathcal{C}}
, that contains an incompatible pair has an exponential Wilf collapse, unless
{\mathcal{C}}
is the class of increasing permutations.

A few words in the statement of that theorem require further explanation:
{\mathcal{C}}
is supercritical if the radius of convergence of the generating function of its sum-indecomposables is strictly greater than the radius of convergence of its generating function, and an incompatible pair is a pair of sum-indecomposable permutations
a
and
b
of
{\mathcal{C}}
such that neither
a\oplus b
nor
b\oplus a
is a subpermutation of any sum-indecomposable element of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

The examples we consider in this paper have an exponential Wilf collapse. However, the first one,
{\mathcal{X}}
, is not sum-closed and the second,
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is sum-closed and super-critical but contains no incompatible pair.

The X-class

The class
{\mathcal{X}}=\operatorname{Av}(2143,2413,3142,3412)
can be defined recursively as 1, together with all those permutations that start or finish with their maximum or minimum and that, when such an element is deleted, belong to
{\mathcal{X}}
. Alternatively, we can view it as the closure of
\{1\}
under the four operations:

\pi\mapsto 1\oplus\pi,\>\pi\mapsto 1\ominus\pi,\>\pi\mapsto\pi\oplus 1,\>\pi\mapsto\pi\ominus 1,

or as the set of permutations that can be drawn on the diagonals of an axis-aligned square. The class
{\mathcal{X}}
is closed under all the symmetries of
{\mathcal{S}}
. We will not explicitly state symmetrical variations of results below, but will feel free to use them as required.

Plainly,
{\mathcal{X}}
is not sum- or skew-sum-closed and so the results of
[
3
]
cannot provide a Wilf-collapse. But, as we shall see there is a natural way of representing the elements of
{\mathcal{X}}
as words over an (infinite) alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
and also a greedy method for determining when
\pi\leqslant\tau
for
\pi,\tau\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Together, these provide a factorisation theorem for the generating functions of
{\mathcal{X}}\cap\operatorname{Inv}(\pi)
in terms of the letters that occur in the word representing
\pi
. This is more than sufficient for an exponential Wilf-collapse.

The type of
\pi\in{\mathcal{X}}
is either
monotone
(if it is monotone increasing or decreasing) or
crossed
(otherwise). If
\pi
has size at least two and begins with its minimum or ends with its maximum we say it is
positive
(or

) while if it begins with its maximum or ends with its minimum it is
negative
. Note that, according to these definitions, the permutation
1
has no sign but every other permutation in
{\mathcal{X}}
has a uniquely defined sign. For a non-negative integer

we also use
a
to denote the monotone increasing permutation of size
a
, and
-a
to denote the monotone decreasing permutation of size

.

If
\pi\in{\mathcal{X}}
of size

either begins with 1 or ends with

Proposition 2.3
.

Let negative
\tau,\theta\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
(a,b)\in{\mathcal{A}}^{+}

a\oplus\theta\oplus b\leqslant c\oplus\tau\oplus d

if and only if

\max(0,a-c)\oplus\theta\oplus\max(0,b-d)\leqslant\tau.

Moreover, if
\tau

a\oplus\theta\oplus b\not\leqslant\tau

\tau=c\ominus\nu\ominus d

a\oplus\theta\oplus b\leqslant\tau

a\oplus\theta\oplus b\leqslant\nu

The first part follows immediately from the observation that

\frac{1-2x}{1-4x+2x^{2}}

\frac{x^{2}}{1-x}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{1-x^{i}}\right)^{\lceil(i+1)/2\rceil}.

After taking logarithms it is easy to verify that this converges for all

) and the bijection witnessing their Wilf-equivalence can be applied to it.

3
Sum closure of increasing oscillations

We now consider an important class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
(
{\mathcal{S}}
ubpermutations of the
{\mathcal{I}}
ncreasing
\mathcal{O}
scillation) to which the previous conditions do not apply and yet we can still demonstrate an exponential Wilf-collapse. This class consists of all finite permutations whose pattern can be found in the
infinite increasing oscillation
:

2,\,4,\,1,\,6,\,3,\,8,\,5,\,10,\,7,\,\dots

This sequence (and various minor modifications of it) arises in a wide variety of applications ranging from the recreational e.g., the theory of juggling
[
8
]
to the more serious in that it provides those sequences that are most difficult to sort by block reversals
[
6
]
. The class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
also plays a central role in the study of growth rates of permutation classes providing a fundamental building block for the construction of intervals in the set of achievable growth rates and other threshold phenomena
[
7
,
9
,
10
]
. The class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is invariant under four of the symmetries for permutations in general: the identity, reverse-complement, inverse, and the composition of reverse-complement with inverse.

The
inversion graph
of a sequence or permutation
\pi
is the graph on the indices of the sequence where, for
i<j
,
i
and
j
are adjacent if
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
, i.e., if the pair
(i,j)
forms an inversion in the sequence.
The inversion graph of the infinite increasing oscillation is a (one-ended) infinite path and the finite permutations in the class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
have sum decompositions whose components have inversion graphs that are paths (possibly consisting only of a single element). For each path of length at least three there are two permutations whose inversion graph is that path and both of these occur in
{\mathcal{SIO}}
. So the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
of sum-indecomposables for the class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
consists of: a symbol
\mathsf{a}
representing the singleton permutation (
\mathsf{a}
), a symbol
\mathsf{b}
representing 21, and for
k\geq 3
two symbols
\mathsf{w}_{k}
and
\mathsf{m}_{k}
representing the two different permutations of size
k
whose inversion graph is a path. The first few of these are shown explicitly in Figure

along with a convenient symbolic representation of each.

In the sum-indecomposables of size at least 3 we define the
Start
of that sum indecomposable to be “Up” (
\nearrow
) if the initial element of the permutation corresponds to an interior vertex of the inversion graph, and “Down” (
\searrow
) if it is a terminal vertex. The corresponding definition for
Finish
is that it is
\nearrow
if the final element of the permutation corresponds to an interior vertex of the inversion graph, and
\searrow
if it is a terminal vertex. Start and Finish are “Undefined” (
\bot
) for the permutations
1
or
21
.

Since every permutation in
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is uniquely represented as a sum of sum-indecomposables and all such sums belong to
{\mathcal{SIO}}
we freely identify
{\mathcal{SIO}}
with words over the alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
. We further define the Start of a word to be the Start of its first letter (possibly undefined) and its Finish to be the Finish of its terminal letter. The
type
of a word is the pair consisting of its Start and Finish.

Observation 3.1
.

The class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is supercritical since the generating function for its sum-closed permutations is:

x+x^{2}+2x^{3}/(1-x).

However, it has no incompatible pairs since for any pair of plus-indecomposable permutations

,
\pi\oplus\theta
occurs as a pattern in a sum-indecomposable of size at most
|\pi|+|\theta|+2
.

Define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
by

if and only if there is a size and type-preserving bijection
\Phi:{\mathcal{SIO}}\to{\mathcal{SIO}}
such that the image of
{\mathcal{SIO}}\cap\operatorname{Av}(X)

(where again as usual we make no distinction between the words
X
and

and the permutations they represent). In particular if

then
X\equiv_{{\mathcal{SIO}}}Y
. So, if we can demonstrate a significant collapse for the

{\mathcal{SIO}}

{\mathcal{SIO}}
as well.

.

X\sim\sigma(X)

(\nearrow,\searrow)

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
X\sim Y
and that
P,S\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
. Then
PXS\sim PYS
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi:{\mathcal{SIO}}\to{\mathcal{SIO}}
be a size and type-preserving bijection whose existence is guaranteed by the condition that
X\sim Y
. We will construct a size and type-preserving bijection
\Psi:{\mathcal{SIO}}\to{\mathcal{SIO}}
which witnesses
PXS\sim PYS
.

To this end, let
W\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
be given. If
PS\not\leqslant W
set
\Psi(W)=W
. So suppose that
PS\leqslant W
. Take a leftmost embedding of
P
into
W
and a rightmost embedding of
S
into
W
. If
P
is non-empty then the embedding of
P
ends in some letter
x
and there might remain some “usable” part of
x
(beginning with the next vertex but one after the end of
P
) call it
x^{\prime}
(if
P
is empty then
x
will just be the first character of
W
and
x^{\prime}=x
). Likewise
S
starts in some letter
y
and there might be some usable part of
y
before the beginning of
S
, call it
y^{\prime}
. Suppose first that
x\neq y
(not just as characters but as letters in the word
W
) and write
W=AxMyB
. Consider
W^{\prime}=x^{\prime}My^{\prime}
. Now
PXS\leqslant W
if and only if
X\leqslant W^{\prime}
. So
PXS\leqslant W
if and only if
Y\leqslant\Phi(W^{\prime})
. Since the Start and Finish of
\Phi(W^{\prime})
are the same as of
W^{\prime}
we can reattach the part of
x
before
W^{\prime}
and the part of
y
after
W^{\prime}
to
\Phi(W^{\prime})
in the same way as they were originally attached to
W
. Define
\Psi(W)
to be the concatenation of
A
, this reattachment, and
B
. If
P
is non-empty then
Ax
is non-empty and the Start of
\Psi(W)
is the same as the Start of
Ax
and hence of
W
. On the other hand if
P
is empty then the Start of
W
is just the Start of
x^{\prime}
(and so of
W^{\prime}
), and once again the Start of
\Psi(W)
is the same as that of
W
. Similar considerations show that the Finish of
\Psi(W)
is the same as the Finish of
W
and complete this case.

However, the case when
x
any
y
are the same character is essentially the same, but
W^{\prime}
just consists of a single letter which is the usable part of the letter in which
P
ends and
S
begins.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

The class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
has an exponential Wilf-collapse.

Proof.

As noted previously, the generating function for the plus indecomposable members of
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is

A(t)=t+t^{2}+\frac{2t^{3}}{1-t}

which is rational and of radius of convergence
1
and therefore the class
{\mathcal{SIO}}
is super-critical.

Proposition 3.10 of
[
3
]
implies that for some constant
c>0
, in all but an exponentially small proportion of the words in
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
of weight
n
there are at least
cn
disjoint occurrences of the factor
\mathsf{w}_{3}\mathsf{m}_{4}
. However
\mathsf{w}_{3}\mathsf{m}_{4}\sim\mathsf{w}_{4}\mathsf{m}_{3}
so the
\sim
(and hence Wilf) equivalence classes of all such words contain at least
2^{cn}
elements, which demonstrates an exponential Wilf-collapse.
∎

4
Conclusion

This paper provides more evidence for a thesis that has previously been explored in
[
1
,
5
,
3
]
. Put most broadly:

If the elements of a class
{\mathcal{C}}
admit a natural representation as words over some weighted alphabet in such a way that containment can be recognised at the word level in a greedy fashion, then global symmetries of the class can often be applied locally to provide Wilf-equivalent structures and this frequently implies a Wilf collapse.

However, we have no other general criteria that yield Wilf-collapse.

The class
\operatorname{Av}(321)
provides a testing ground for this thesis for a couple of reasons. Results in
[
4
]
show that there is a greedy form of testing for containment within this class. On the other hand, results in
[
