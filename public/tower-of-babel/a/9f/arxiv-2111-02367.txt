Multistage Estimators for Missing Covariates and Incomplete Outcomes
Daniel Suen

Abstract
We study problems with multiple missing covariates and partially observed responses. We develop a new framework to handle complex missing covariate scenarios via inverse probability weighting, regression adjustment, and a multiply-robust procedure. We apply our framework to three classical problems: the Cox model from survival analysis, missing response, and binary treatment from causal inference. We also discuss how to handle missing covariates in these scenarios, and develop associated identifying theories and asymptotic theories. We apply our procedure to simulations and an Alzheimer’s disease dataset and obtain meaningful results.



Keywords:

Missing covariates, missing not at random, multiple imputation, inverse probability weighting, multiply-robustness

1
Introduction

Missing data occurs across many different fields, and as the size of datasets becomes ever larger, it becomes increasingly unrealistic to expect fully observed datasets. In many statistical problems, we have outcome variables of interest
and a set of associated covariates. However, these outcome variables may not be completely observed. For example, in a survival problem, either the censoring time or the time-to-event are observed.
In a missing response problem, a response may be subject to missingness and only be observed for a subset of the sample.
In the binary treatment problem, only one of the two potential outcomes will be observed.
In a traditional setting, all the covariates are assumed to be completely measured
and there has been a vast amount of literature on how to estimate a parameter of interest.
However, little is known when the covariates could be missing.

Some early attempts to handle missing covariates rely on ignorability assumptions, utilize estimation procedures in which the assumptions are not well-specified, or assume that there is only one missing pattern (one covariate subject to missing). For example, many standard assumptions to identify an effect of interest take the form of a conditional independence statement, where the conditioning is done on the covariates
X
. In the case of missing covariates, strong ignorability assumptions have been proposed by conditioning on the observed covariates
X_{R}
and the missing covariate pattern
R
and using generalized propensity scores with propensity score matching
(
Rosenbaum and Rubin 1984
;
Rosenbaum and Rubin 1985
)
. Other approaches include augmenting the support of the covariates with a new category for missing data
\{\texttt{NA}\}

(
Mayer et al. 2020
;
Mayer et al. 2021
)
. One downside to both of these methods is that in some situations, the interpretation of these missing data assumptions may not be so natural as there are effectively different sets of covariates being conditioned on for different individuals. Various imputation methods have also been proposed in the Cox model such as those in
Paik 1997
;
White and Royston 2009
;
Beesley et al. 2016
, but those proposed methods rely on specific assumptions that may not extend to more general settings. The multiple imputation approach discussed in
Qu and Lipkovich 2009
;
Crowe et al. 2010
rely on missing data assumptions that are not clearly stated, which can lead to difficulty in interpretation.
Berg and Yu 2021
also recently examined estimation under missing-not-at-random (MNAR) but they only consider a single covariate that are subject to missingness.

In the case of multiple missing variables, the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption
becomes hard to interpret
(
Robins and Gill 1997
)
,
so a nonignorable/MNAR missing data assumption may be preferred.
There have been previous developments using nonignorable missing data assumptions in the literature. For example,
Robins and Gill 1997
proposed the permutation missingness process as a generalization of MAR processes. Also,
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. 2018
used discrete-choice models as a way to generate a class of MNAR assumptions.
Shpitser 2016
;
Sadinle and Reiter 2017
;
Malinsky et al. 2020
considered a “no self-censoring” or “itemwise conditionally independent nonresponse” assumption.
Chen 2020
introduced the idea of a pattern graph to generate further MNAR assumptions. Other approaches include various graphical ones:
Mohan et al. 2013
;
Mohan and Pearl 2014
;
Nabi et al. 2020
;
Mohan and Pearl 2021
.

In this paper, we build on this previous work by introducing a general framework for constructing a set of compatible assumptions on problems with missing covariates and incomplete outcomes. This framework has several advantages. First, one can construct identifying restrictions on the missing covariates that are compatible with common identifying restrictions for the outcome variables. This ensures that the final outcome of interest is nonparametrically identifiable
(
Robins et al. 2000
)
. Also, because we start with a clear set of assumptions and then develop an estimation method based on those assumptions, this framework provides a principled approach for estimating the final outcome of interest. Previous work on MNAR mechanisms can be utilized in our estimation procedure. Lastly, our framework can be applied to various settings including but not limited to: survival analysis with censoring, binary treatment from causal inference, and missing response.

.

2
Background and framework

In this section, we discuss the problem setup and introduce notation that will be helpful in describing our framework. We also discuss the setting of three classical problems from the statistics literature: the Cox model from survival analysis, missing response, and binary treatment from causal inference.

2.1
Problem Setup

In our problems, there are both covariate and outcome-related variables. Let
Z=(X,R)
represent the covariate-related variables, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set of covariate variables. Since
X
is multivariate, it can have an arbitrary missing pattern. We use the binary vector
R\in\{0,1\}^{d}
to represent the missing response for
X
. If
R_{j}=1
, then the variable
X_{j}
is observed; otherwise, it is missing. Let
1_{d}=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
denote the case where
X_{j}
is observed for all
j
. We denote
\bar{r}=1_{d}-r
as the flipped version of pattern
r
. Thus, the notation
X_{r}=(X_{j}:r_{j}=1)
and
X_{\bar{r}}=(X_{j}:r_{j}=0)
refer to observed and missing covariate variables, respectively.

Next, we define
S=(W,A)
as the outcome-related variables. Here
W\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a set of outcome variables, and
A\in\mathcal{A}
is a random variable over a discrete support
\mathcal{A}
that represents the observed pattern of the outcome variables. Note that
A
has a more general interpretation than the missing pattern
R
for
X
because
A
does not necessarily correspond to a missing pattern for
W
in the traditional sense. This idea will be made more precise in the following subsections. Similar to the previous case with the covariates, we will use the notation
W_{A}
to denote the set of outcome variables that are observed under the observed pattern
A
.

Therefore, with a slight abuse of notation, the observed dataset consists of
n
IID observations of the form
\{(X_{i,R_{i}},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Here
R_{i}
is the covariate missingness pattern for the
i
th individual. As
X_{i}
consists of the covariates for the
i
th individual, some of its entries may be subject to missingness; thus, we use
X_{i,R_{i}}
to denote the observed covariates for the
i
th individual. Similarly, the observed outcome variables may differ between individuals, so we use
W_{i,A_{i}}
to denote the observed outcome variables for the
i
th individual, where
A_{i}
is the observed pattern of the outcome variables for the
i
th individual.

To further describe our problem formulation, we provide a brief description of each of the three problems here (Cox model, missing response, and binary treatment).

2.2
Cox model

In survival analysis, we typically wish to determine the effect of covariates on a time-to-event variable. However, the event time is often not known for some individuals due to censoring. For example, people participating in a medical trial may dropout prematurely, which may mean that the event of interest is not observed for those individuals. Thus, the observed data is of the form

(X_{1,R_{1}},R_{1},Y_{1},\Delta_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n,R_{n}},R_{n},Y_{n},\Delta_{n}),

where
Y_{i}=\min\{T_{i},C_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}
is the observed time, and
\Delta_{i}=1(Y_{i}=T_{i})=1(C_{i}\geq T_{i})
is the status indicator for a time-to-event
T_{i}
and a censoring time
C_{i}
. Thus, the outcome variables are
W=(C,T)
and the observed outcome pattern corresponds to
A=\Delta
. We see that
W_{0}=C
and
W_{1}=T
, as the observed outcome pattern denotes whether or not the time-to-event variable was censored. One can see that the observed outcome pattern
A
is not associated with a missingness pattern. Importantly, the outcome variables
W=(C,T)
are never completely observed for any individuals.

The Cox proportional hazards model assumes that the effect of the covariates is multiplicative on
\lambda(t|x)
through a baseline hazard function

\lambda(t|x)=\lambda_{0}(t)\exp(\beta^{\top}x)
.
Under the Cox model and an independent censoring assumption,
the parameter of interest
\beta^{*}
satisfies the population estimating equation:

\displaystyle U(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)\right]=0,

(1)

\displaystyle s^{(1)}(t;\beta)=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X],

\displaystyle s^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)].

Our goal is to estimate
\beta^{*}
in this semiparametric setting. When the covariates
X
are fully observed, under suitable regularity conditions, it is known that finding a near-solution
\widehat{\beta}
to the estimating equation

\displaystyle U_{n}(\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left(X_{i}-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y_{i};\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y_{i};\beta)}\right)=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{n}),

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(1)}(t;\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(Y_{i}\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X_{i})X_{i},

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(Y_{i}\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X_{i})

provides a consistent and asymptotically normal estimator of
\beta^{*}
. Since
X
can now be subject to missingness, we need a way to estimate these nuisance functions.

2.3
Missing response

In the missing response problem, we assume that we have a single outcome variable of interest
Y\in\mathbb{R}
that is possibly subject to missingness. We use the binary random variable
A\in\{0,1\}
, which is
1
if and only if
Y
is observed. The observed data is either in the form of
(X_{i,R_{i}},R_{i},Y_{i},A_{i}=1)
or
(X_{i,R_{i}},R_{i},A_{i}=0)
.
In the second case (
Y_{i}
is missing), we can think of the data as
(X_{R_{i}},R_{i},\texttt{NA},A_{i}=0)
.
Using our aforementioned notations,
W_{0}=\texttt{NA}
and
W_{1}=Y
.
Since
Y
can be subject to missingness,
A
takes a traditional interpretation as a missing response pattern. Then,
Y_{A_{i}}
is the observed outcome variable for the
i
th individual.
In a typical case, the parameter of interest is the marginal mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
and
we often assume
Y\perp A|X

to identify the marginal mean.
Under this assumption,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{YA}{\pi_{A}(X)}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m_{1}(X)],

(3)

where
\pi_{A}(X)=P(A=1|X)
is the propensity score and
m_{1}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]
is the outcome regression. The expressions (
2
) and (
3
) are associated with the
inverse probability weighting
and
regression adjustment
estimators, respectively. When the covariates
X
are always observed, these quantities can be easily estimated from the observed data, but the situation becomes more complicated when the covariates are multivariate and subject to nonmonotone missingness.

2.4
Binary treatment

Under the Neyman-Rubin causal model
(
Rubin 1978
;
Splawa-Neyman et al. 1990
)
, we have a binary treatment
A\in\{0,1\}
that corresponds to potential outcomes
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
, respectively. Here the outcome variables are
W=(Y(0),Y(1))
. Similar to the survival analysis problem with censoring, only one of the outcome variables is observed for a given individual.
The observed outcome variables are
W_{0}=Y(0)
and
W_{1}=Y(1)
.

A common parameter of interest is the average treatment effect (ATE)

\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]

and under the classical assumptions (consistency, SUTVA, weak ignorability;
(
Rubin 1974
;
Rubin 1978
)
)
the ATE admits the following expression

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]-\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{YA}{\pi_{A}(X)}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Y(1-A)}{1-\pi_{A}(X)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m_{1}(X)]-\mathbb{E}[m_{0}(X)],

where
\pi_{A}(X)=P(A=1|X)
is the propensity score and
m_{a}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=a,X]
is the outcome regression.
Thus, this leads to the inverse probability weighting or regression adjustment estimators for inferring the ATE.

3
A multistage estimator

The intermediate quantities that we described in the previous section such as
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
and
\pi_{A}(X)
are calculated using the covariates
X
and the outcome-related variables
(W,A)
. Thus, due to this shared property, before we construct our multistage estimator, we first consider a general real-valued function
h
of the covariate-related variables
X
and the outcome-related variables
(W,A)
, as defined below.

Definition 1

(Observed-decomposable functions)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{j}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a function of interest.
Call
h
an observed-decomposable function (ODF) if

h(X,W,A)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a),

where
f_{a}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{\text{dim}(W_{a})}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a fixed (non-random) real-valued function for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

The interpretation of an ODF is that for each observed outcome pattern
a
, we have a separate real-valued function
f_{a}
that is dependent on all of the covariates
X
and the observed outcome variables
W_{a}
under pattern
a
. Since
h
has this decomposition, it is computable in the traditional setting with incomplete outcomes and completely observed covariates. As we will see in the construction of a multistage estimator, many quantities of interest can be written in this way, so this is a very natural decomposition. We will either typically be interested in estimating
\theta=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]
or
\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]=0
will be an estimating equation. We provide an illustrative example of
h
using the missing response problem.

Example 1

(Missing response)
.

In the traditional missing response problem without missing covariates where we commonly wish to estimate
\mathbb{E}[Y]
, there are 3 traditional estimators: regression adjustment on
Y
, inverse probability weighting on
Y
, and doubly robust on
Y
estimators. Because our goal is to estimate
\mathbb{E}[Y]
, the corresponding
h
,
f_{0}
, and
f_{1}
functions take the following forms in these three situations.
Note that in the case
A=0
(
Y
is missing), we simplify notation such that
f_{0}(X,W_{0})=f_{0}(X,\texttt{NA})=f_{0}(X)
since
W_{0}

can only take one possible outcome
NA
.

1.

Regression adjustment on
Y
. We have

h(X,W,A)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X],

which implies that
f_{0}(X)=f_{1}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]
.

2.

Inverse probability weighting on
Y
. We have

h(X,W,A)=\frac{1(A=1)Y}{P(A=1|X)},

which implies that
f_{0}(X)=0
and
f_{1}(X,Y)=\dfrac{Y}{P(A=1|X)}
.

3.

Doubly-robust on
Y
. We have

h(X,W,A)=\frac{1(A=1)Y}{P(A=1|X)}+\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]\left(1-\frac{1(A=1)}{P(A=1|X)}\right),

which implies that
f_{0}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]
and
f_{1}(X,Y)=\dfrac{Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]}{P(A=1|X)}+\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X]
.

Example 2

(Cox model)
.

In the Cox model,
\Delta=A
and
W_{0}=W_{1}=Y
and
there are three key equations as described in equation (
1
):
U(\beta),s^{(1)}(t;\beta),s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
.

1.

U(\beta)
. For this quantity,
U(\beta)=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]
, so
f_{0}(X,W_{0})=0
and
f_{1}(X,W_{1})=\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)
.

2.

s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
. In this case, by setting
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]
, we obtain
f_{0}(X,W_{0})=1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{T}X)X

and
f_{1}(X,W_{1})=1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{T}X)X
.

3.

s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
. This case is similar to
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
, so that
f_{0}(X,W_{0})=1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{T}X)

and
f_{1}(X,W_{1})=1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{T}X)
.

3.1
The IPW approach for missing covariates

To handle missing covariates, we start with an IPW procedure based on using the
complete odds
as defined below.

Definition 2

(Complete odds)
.

For all missingness patterns
r
and observed outcome patterns
a
, the complete odds is

O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})=\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)}.

Thus, these complete odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
are a function of all of the outcome variables completely observed under outcome pattern
a
and the covariates. With the complete odds,
\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]
can be decomposed via the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Complete odds decomposition)
.

The complete odds can be used to decompose the following two expectations as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=r)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d})\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d})\right].

(4)

This lemma shows that the expectation can be estimated using the complete cases since we have the indicator
1(R=1_{d})
in the expression on the RHS. Thus, we see that
\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]
is identifiable, provided that the complete odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
are identifiable for all
r
. So, it is clear what we need to show before estimation. Additionally, note that

\sum_{r}O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})=\sum_{r}\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)}=\frac{1}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)}

(5)

is the inverse propensity score, so we see that (
4
) recovers the form of the IPW estimator. In light of this, one can view the complete odds as an alternate version of a propensity score. In this situation, it is not required to use the complete odds approach since one may use the propensity score directly. However,
modeling via complete odds has several nice properties; we discuss this in Remark
1
.

In the following proposition, we show that a set of identifiable complete odds is equivalent to a set of identifiable selection probabilities. This connection offers one key insight: modeling the covariate missingness mechanism through the complete odds is equivalent to modeling the covariate missingness mechanism using the selection model
(
Little and Rubin 2002
)
. Specific missing data assumptions may make the former approach easier than the latter, so this can be a useful result.

Proposition 2

(Equivalence of complete odds and selection probabilities)
.

Fix an observed outcome pattern
a
. The complete odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})

are identifiable for all
r
if and only if the selection probabilities
P(R=r|X,W_{a},a)
are identifiable for all
r
.

Furthermore, another consequence of this proposition is that a set of identifiable complete odds implies that the distribution
p(x,r,w_{a},a)
is identifiable for all
a
. Marginalizing out
r
implies that the distribution
p(x,w_{a},a)
is nonparametrically identified
(
Robins et al. 2000
)
, which is sufficient for showing the effect of interest is identifiable under the traditional assumptions that assume fully observed covariates. This implies that with a set of identifiable complete odds, we can use existing problem-specific assumptions to identify the parameter of interest.

Remark 1

(Advantages to modeling the complete odds)
.

A modeling approach using the complete odds can be preferable to one using the selection probabilities directly. Suppose
r
and
a
are fixed. Then,
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
is variation independent of any
O_{r^{\prime},a}(X,W_{a})
for
r^{\prime}\neq r
. Indeed,
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
can take any value in
[0,\infty)
and is not constrained by any other odds function
O_{r^{\prime},a}(X,W_{a})
.
On the other hand, the selection probabilities
P(R=r|x,w_{a},a)
have an explicit sum to 1 constraint, so the selection probabilities for all missing patterns
r
need to be modeled at the same time. In principle, one can use different methods to model each odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
, provided it is identifiable under a missing data assumption for
X
. These allows for some flexibility because one can choose to have different binary classifiers for each pattern
r
. Due to the variation independence, they can all be compatible with each other.

Because of Proposition
2
,
we only need to identify the complete odds.
Here we consider a (conditional)
complete case missing value (CCMV) assumption
(
Little 1993
;
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. 2018
)
.

Definition 3

(CCMV missing data assumption)
.

Using a pattern mixture model factorization, the complete case missing value (CCMV) assumption equates the following two distributions

p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},w_{a},a)

(6)

for all covariate missingness patterns
r
and observed outcome patterns
a
.

Note that the above condition is a conditional CCMV in the sense that
we are using the CCMV for the missing
X

conditioned on the observed outcomes.
We call it CCMV for abbreviation.
Under Proposition
3
, this particular missing data assumption implies a simple closed form expression
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
for the odds.

Proposition 3

(Odds under CCMV)
.

Under the CCMV assumption for the covariates
X
, the odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
has the following simplified form in terms of observable quantities
X_{r}
and
W_{a}
, namely

O_{r,a}(X,W_{a}):=\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},a)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}\frac{P(R=r|X_{r},W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X_{r},W_{a},a)}=:Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a}).

(7)

Because each
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
is identifiable from the data,
Proposition
3
shows that the CCMV assumption allows us to estimate the parameter
of interest under missing covariates.
The modeling
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
is easy because it is comparing the proportions between
two missing patterns.

We provide some comments on generalizing the CCMV assumption to other assumptions in Appendix
C
. Other missing data assumptions can yield other identifiable and estimable expressions for the odds, but one would simply obtain a different equality than the one in (
7
). For example,
Chen 2020
exploits a pattern graph identifying restriction to present a recursive form for computing the odds.

Remark 2

(Relation to density ratio estimation)
.

Wang and Kim 2021
have examined the problem of estimating an inverse propensity score using a density ratio representation under a missing at random assumption. Interestingly, because they assume the variables can be partitioned into one set of always missing variables and one set of always observed variables, the missing at random assumption is equivalent to CCMV. Additionally, our complete odds can be expressed as a density ratio as well, so these problems are related. We elect to model our complete odds directly rather than reexpress it as a ratio of densities, but note that one can borrow methods from the density ratio literature to also tackle this problem. See
Sugiyama et al. 2012
for various approaches.

Theorem 4

(Nonparametric identification of CCMV)
.

The CCMV assumption in Definition
3
nonparametrically identifies the full-covariate distribution
p(x,r,w_{a},a)
.

With Theorem
4
, we are able to identify the full-covariate distribution
p(x,r,w_{a},a)
. This implies
that the problem reduces to scenarios where the covariates are observed.
Thus, we can estimate the parameter of interest as if all covariates are observed.
In what follows, we give an example of Cox model, and in Appendix
A
, we provide examples
of missing response problems.

3.1.1
Example: Cox model and survival problems

Recall that in the survival analysis problem,
\Delta=A
and
W_{0}=W_{1}=Y
. In the case of
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
, we have

f_{0}(X,W_{0})=f_{1}(X,W_{1})=1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X.

(8)

For any fixed time point
t
, a direct application of Lemma
1
yields

\displaystyle s^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X1(\Delta=1)]+\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X1(\Delta=0)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X1(\Delta=1)O_{r,1}(X,Y)1(R=1_{d})]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X1(\Delta=0)O_{r,0}(X,Y)1(R=1_{d})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,\delta}\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)Xe^{\beta^{\top}X}O_{r,\delta}(X,Y)1(\Delta=\delta)1(R=1_{d})],

where
O_{r,\delta}(X,Y)=P(R=r|X,Y,\delta)/P(R=1_{d}|X,Y,\delta)
are the complete odds. A similar expression can be obtained for
s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
. Next, we consider the estimating equation, which rewrites as

\displaystyle U(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)O_{r,1}(X,Y)1(R=1_{d})\right]

(9)

using Lemma
1
.

With Proposition
3
,
O_{r,\delta}(X,Y)=Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
, which provides an elegant way of computing the odds because the quantity
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
is a ratio of two conditional probabilities that no longer depend on all of the covariates but rather observed covariates
X_{r}
. We now discuss the construction of a multistage estimator under CCMV using the complete odds. Henceforth, we will use
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
rather than
O_{r,\delta}(X,Y)
because they are equal by Proposition
3
. This choice of an identifying restriction allows for us to compute the inverse propensity score in a fairly straightforward manner.

There are three steps to our multistage estimator, which we outline below.

1.

Stage 1 (Modeling the missingness of
X
using the complete odds).
We start by modeling
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
for every
r
and
\delta
. For example, we can impose a parametric model
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;\alpha_{r,\delta})
such as a logistic regression. Because this essentially becomes a binary classification problem (comparing the classes
R=r
and
R=1_{d}
based on the observed data
X_{r}
and
Y
), one may also appeal to various machine learning methods such as decision trees and support vector machines. Other parametric binary classification tools are also available.

2.

Stage 2 (Estimating intermediate quantities).
Next, we estimate the intermediate quantities of interest that are dependent on the potentially missing covariates
X
. Because of the model described in Stage 1, this estimation becomes possible. For example, assuming that the model for
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;\alpha_{r,\delta})
is correctly specified and
\widehat{\alpha}_{r,\delta}
is a consistent estimator of
\alpha_{r,\delta}^{*}
, under the suitable regularity conditions, the following is a consistent estimator for
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,\delta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(Y_{i}\geq t)X_{i}e^{\beta^{\top}X_{i}}Q_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};\widehat{\alpha}_{r,\delta})1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)1(R_{i}=1_{d})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggr(1(Y_{i}\geq t)X_{i}e^{\beta^{\top}X_{i}}\cdot\underbrace{\biggr[\sum_{r,\delta}Q_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};\widehat{\alpha}_{r,\delta})1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)1(R_{i}=1_{d})\biggr]}_{\lambda_{i}}\biggr)

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot 1(Y_{i}\geq t)X_{i}e^{\beta^{\top}X_{i}},

where
\lambda_{i}
is a weight for the
i
th individual, as defined in (
10
). Similarly, we can mimic the same construction to estimate
s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
, yielding

\widehat{s}^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot 1(Y_{i}\geq t)e^{\beta^{\top}X_{i}}.

3.

Stage 3 (Constructing the final estimator).
Lastly, we assemble the intermediate quantities of interest together to create the final estimator. Following the decomposition in (
9
), we have

\displaystyle U_{n}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,\delta}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left(X_{i}-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y_{i};\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y_{i};\beta)}\right)Q_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};\widehat{\alpha}_{r,1})1(R_{i}=1_{d})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggr(\Delta_{i}\left(X_{i}-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y_{i};\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y_{i};\beta)}\right)\cdot\underbrace{\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};\widehat{\alpha}_{r,1})1(\Delta_{i}=1)1(R_{i}=1_{d})}_{\lambda_{i,1}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot\Delta_{i}\left(X_{i}-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y_{i};\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y_{i};\beta)}\right),

where the second to last equality follows because
\Delta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. The last equality follows because
\lambda_{i}\Delta_{i}=\lambda_{i,1}\Delta_{i}
. Thus,
U_{n}(\beta)=0
is a reweighted estimating equation and can be solved to provide a consistent estimator
\widehat{\beta}
for
\beta^{*}
.

A nice property is that the weights
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{i,1}
need only to be computed from the data once and can be reused thereafter. We summarize the implementation of this reweighted Cox model in Algorithm
1
.

, fit the parametric model

.

3.2
Regression adjustment and imputation for missing covariates

In addition to the IPW approach for handling missing covariates,
we can use a regression adjustment approach as well.
Moreover, this regression adjustment naturally leads to an imputation procedure.
The following lemma is a key step to the regression adjustment (RA).

Lemma 5

(RA decomposition)
.

Regression adjustment can be used to decompose the following two expectations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=r)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)],

where
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]
is a regression function.

Thus, in order to estimate the expectation, it suffices to model the regression function
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
, which is a conditional expectation.

In practice, placing a parametric model for each regression function via
m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\beta_{r,a})
may be problematic because naively fitting models can lead to incompatible regression functions and uncongenial sources of input
(
Meng et al. 1994
)
. Instead, we recommend placing models on the extrapolation densities
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,a,w_{a})
for all
r
. This ensures that the modeled regression functions will be compatible with each other.

Under the CCMV assumption (equation
6
), we have

p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)=p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},w_{a},a),

which further implies

m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=1_{d},W_{a},A=a].

The RHS is identifiable using observations with complete covariates.
In this case, there are two ways of estimating the regression function:

1.

Method 1: Closed-form computation.

In some good scenarios, we may be able to derive the
the closed-form of the expectation
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]

from a parametric model of the extrapolation densities
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,a,w_{a})
. For instance, a multivariate Gaussian model implies that the conditional distributions will be conditional Gaussians.

2.

Method 2: Imputation (Monte Carlo approximation).

Alternatively, we can approximate the conditional expectation by
a Monte Carlo method. This is equivalent to the frequentist multiple imputation.
We generate from the extrapolation density
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,a,w_{a})

and then evaluate the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]
. Under CCMV (
6
), the extrapolation density
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,y,\delta)
is equated to the identifiable distribution
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},y,\delta)
. The latter distribution can be estimated using the complete cases.

3.2.1
Example: Cox model and survival problems

Using Lemma
5
with the functions defined in (
8
), we have

\displaystyle s^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,\delta}\mathbb{E}[m^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)1(R=r)1(\Delta=\delta)].

Thus, to estimate
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
, it suffices to model
m^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta):=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)X|X_{r},R=r,Y,\Delta=\delta]
for all
(r,\delta)
pairs. We obtain a similar expression for
s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
via

s^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\sum_{r,\delta}\mathbb{E}[m^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)1(R=r)1(\Delta=\delta)]

for
m^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta):=\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)\exp(\beta^{\top}X)|X_{r},R=r,Y,\Delta=\delta]
.

Applying Lemma
5
on the overall estimating equation, we obtain

\displaystyle U(\beta)=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\biggr|X_{r},R,Y,\Delta\right]\cdot 1(R=r)\cdot 1(\Delta=1)\right].

Therefore, the regression adjustment method is equivalent to finding a near-solution to the following finite sample estimating equation

\displaystyle U_{n}(\beta)=\sum_{r}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[X-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\biggr|X_{i,r},R_{i},Y_{i},\Delta_{i}\right]\right)\cdot 1(R_{i}=r)\cdot 1(\Delta_{i}=1)\right]=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{n}),

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(1)}(t;\beta)=\sum_{r,\delta}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{m}^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};t,\beta)1(R_{i}=r)1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)\right],

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\sum_{r,\delta}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{m}^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};t,\beta)1(R_{i}=r)1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)\right].

(11)

This can be achieved using a Monte Carlo procedure. We describe the multistage process below, and we summarize our sampling method in Algorithm
2
. This algorithm is the same as performing a frequentist (type B) multiple imputation approach described in
Wang and Robins 1998
and
Little and Rubin 2002
.

1.

Stage 1 (Modeling the missingness of
X
with extrapolation densities).
As previously discussed, instead of modeling the regression functions
m^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)
and
m^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)
for all
r
and
\delta
directly, we will instead focus on the extrapolation densities. Under CCMV, we simply need to model the joint distribution
p(x,R=1_{d},y,\delta)
. This will induce a model
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},y,\delta)
for any covariate missing pattern
r
. By CCMV, that conditional distribution equates exactly to the extrapolation distribution for the missing data.

2.

Stage 2 (Estimating intermediate quantities).
Next, we again outline how to estimate the nuisance functions
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)
and
s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
that are dependent on the potentially missing covariates
X
. We estimate these using a Monte Carlo procedure through iterative imputation of the missing covariates.

By estimating the regression functions using
M
draws from the extrapolation distribution, the following is a consistent estimator for
s^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle\widehat{s}^{(1)}(t;\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,\delta}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{m}^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};t,\beta)1(R_{i}=r)1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{r,\delta}\widehat{m}^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{i,r},Y_{i};t,\beta)1(R_{i}=r)1(\Delta_{i}=\delta)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}1(Y_{i}\geq t)\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}e^{\beta^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(Y_{i}\geq t)\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}e^{\beta^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}},

where
\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}=\left(X_{i,R_{i}},\widetilde{X}_{i,\bar{R}_{i}}^{(j)}\right)
. Here
\widetilde{X}_{i,\bar{R}_{i}}^{(j)}
denotes the
j
th imputed tuple of missing covariates for the
i
th observation, and
\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}
is the result of attaching the imputed variables to the observed covariates
X_{i,R_{i}}
. A similar construction applied to
s^{(0)}(t;\beta)
yields

\widehat{s}^{(0)}(t;\beta)=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(Y_{i}\geq t)e^{\beta^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}}.

3.

Stage 3 (Constructing the final estimator).
Lastly, we again merge everything together in the final stage. Following the decomposition in (
11
), we have

\displaystyle U_{n}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}\left(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}-\frac{\widehat{s}^{(1)}(Y_{i};\beta)}{\widehat{s}^{(0)}(Y_{i};\beta)}\right).

We solve the above estimating equation to obtain the final estimate
\widehat{\beta}
.

The terms we have derived suggest a simple procedure for the estimation. These estimators
\widehat{s}^{(1)}(t;\beta),\widehat{s}^{(0)}(t;\beta),
and
U_{n}(\beta)
are all formed by merging the
M
imputed datasets together to form one large completed dataset with
Mn
entries. Thus, this regression adjustment method with a Monte Carlo approximation is equivalent to a stacked multiple imputation approach utilized by
Robins and Wang 2000
;
Beesley and Taylor 2020
. Note that this approach differs from a traditional imputation approach where
M
imputed datasets are generated separately, analysis is performed on each one, and each analysis is pooled to form a final estimator. In contrast, in the stacked imputation approach, the pooling operation is done before the analysis such that
M
imputed datasets are pooled first and analysis is performed on all
Mn
observations to form a final point estimator.

An advantage of the traditional imputation approach is that the separate
M
analyses allows for parallelization. On the other hand, the stacked imputation does not exhibit this nice property, but a single analysis yields a more straightforward implementation, which can be helpful in situations where we perform the bootstrap.

Algorithm 2

For

For

using the estimated extrapolation distribution

.

Set the
j

th observation to be

, where

In a frequentist multiple imputation approach, we first use the observed data to estimate the extrapolation densities and thereby, perform the imputation. Therefore, all further analysis is based on the estimated extrapolation densities, and it is important to incorporate this uncertainty in the final variance calculation. Rubin’s estimator for the variance is commonly used in practice, but it will underestimate the variance in this situation. Specifically, there will be an additional term in the asymptotic variance related to the estimator for the extrapolation distribution that will not be included. See Sections 14.4 and 14.5 of
Tsiatis 2007
for more details.

To avoid this issue in practice, we recommend using the nonparametric bootstrap to estimate the variance. We bootstrap the data (including the missing values) many times and repeat the same analysis on each bootstrapped sample to obtain many point estimates. Each analysis on a bootstrapped sample will utilize a different estimated extrapolation distribution, so this uncertainty will be taken into account.
Chen and Sadinle 2019
provide additional related details in their work.

3.3
A multiply-robust estimator

In the previous two sections, we have described two approaches based on an inverse probability weighting and a regression adjustment. In the seminal paper by
Robins et al. 1994
, the authors introduced a general framework of an augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimating function under a MAR assumption for the covariates, and thus, the selection probability did not depend on any missing covariates.
Wang and Chen 2001
discussed further details on constructing an AIPW estimator with augmented functions for the Cox model under MAR. Additionally, in their paper, the covariates took the form of
(X_{\text{miss}},X_{\text{obs}})
, where
X_{\text{miss}}
was a covariate vector possibly missing for some individuals and
X_{\text{obs}}
was a covariate vector that was always observed, thereby inducing only two possible missingness patterns for the covariates. These AIPW methods rely on two parametric models for the selection probabilities and the missing covariates. To relax this parametric assumption,
Qi et al. 2005
outlined a fully augmented weighted estimator using a kernel regression approach for a one-dimensional covariate.
However, it is unclear how to handle the case with multiple covariates that may all be missing.

We extend the previous work by describing a multiply-robust estimator that allows missing not at random (MNAR) for a multidimensional covariate and arbitrary missing patterns. The following lemma shows that there exists an estimator that is robust to the misspecification of at most one of
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
or
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
for each
r
.

Lemma 6

(Doubly robust linear form under CCMV)
.

Fix a missing pattern
r
and an observed outcome pattern
a
. Define

\displaystyle g_{r}(X,W_{a})

\displaystyle=f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=1_{d})Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})

(12)

\displaystyle\quad+m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)

(13)

\displaystyle\quad-m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=1_{d})Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a}).

(14)

If
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
or
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
is correctly specified, then under a CCMV assumption of equation (
6
), we have

\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[g_{r}(X,W_{a})].

The expression of Lemma
6
shows an interesting connection to equation (10.95) of
Tsiatis 2007
.
In the case where the there is only two missing patterns of the covariates, i.e.,
the covariates can be expressed as
(X_{\text{miss}},X_{\text{obs}})
, where
X_{\text{miss}}
are possibly missing covariates and
X_{\text{obs}}
are completely observed covariates, the MAR and CCMV assumptions on the covariates can be shown to be equivalent. Our expression and equation (10.95) in
Tsiatis 2007
will be the same.

Theorem 7

(Multiply-robust Cox model)
.

Under CCMV, the estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{\text{mr}}
that solves the augmented estimating function is consistent provided either
Q_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
or
m_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y)
is correctly specified for each
r
.

Thus, this implies that we can construct a multistage estimator based on Lemma
6
that is multiply-robust since we have doubly robustness for each covariate pattern
r\neq 1_{d}
. However, this comes at a cost. We see that the doubly robust expression above contains the terms from Lemmas
1
and
5
along with an additional term. Term (
14
) is of interest because it shows that in order to compute it, we will have to perform imputation even for cases with completely observed covariates. Therefore, we can see explicitly that this doubly robust estimator is even more computationally intensive than the regression adjustment estimator in Lemma
5
.

Participating individuals have varying cognitive statuses from no cognitive impairment to mild impairment to severe impairment, and data is collected on a yearly basis with certain covariates measured at entry into the study.

We consider individuals who display mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and follow their trajectory until they either display evidence of dementia (mild, moderate, severe) or are censored. These individuals are binned into 5 age groups based on when they first display MCI: 60-64, 65-69, 70-74, 75-79, and 80-84. In each age group, we fit a Cox model, regressing on the binary variables: sex (female or not), race (white or not), education (have graduate degree or not), depression within last 2 years (yes or no), and anxiety (yes or no).

For the IPW estimator, we use the CCMV assumption using logistic models for the complete odds. 6
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a process for constructing a multistage estimator for problems with missing not at random covariates and incomplete outcomes. We discussed our method in the context of Cox regression from survival analysis and a missing response problem (in Appendix
A
). Although we did not show the specific details, the missing response problem provides a bridge to the binary treatment problem from causal inference and a more general longitudinal study problem with dropout. So, the arguments we provide for the missing response problem naturally extend to these two problems.

Figure

shows an interesting tradeoff between model complexity, computation, and efficiency in the special case of the CCMV assumption for the covariates.
The RA estimator is the easiest to model since we only need to place a model on
p(x|1_{d},w_{a},a)
for all
a
. But this comes at a cost of computation since we generally need to perform imputation to fill in the missing data. Also, RA does not achieve
the (semiparametric) efficiency bound.
The IPW estimator is easy to compute because it is a simple reweighting of the cases with completely observed covariates. But we need to model every
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
, which would require many models.
Also, IPW does not achieve the (semiparametric) efficiency bound and may be unstable if the weights are too large.
The DR estimator achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound by utilizing components from both the regression adjustment and inverse probability weighted estimator.
Due to the augmenting term, imputation must be performed even for the complete cases,
so the computational cost is high and we have to place models for every
Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
.
Thus, efficiency comes at the cost of complexity in modeling and high computation.

There are several possible areas for future work.
One direction is to study the problem of model compatibility issues between complete odds and regression functions.
When the assumption on missing covariates is not CCMV, the complete odds and regression function may again not be variationally
independent.
Thus, extra caution has to be taken
when placing models on them.
Another direction is to study the use of nonparametric models in complete odds and regression functions.
This would lead to a more flexible modeling strategy and avoid the problem of model compatibility, but
overcoming the slow convergence rate will be a challenge.

Appendix A
A multistage estimator for the missing response problem

We now turn our attention to the missing response problem and show how to construct a multistage estimator in this setting.
Note that this procedure can be easily generalized to the binary treatment effect in causal inference.
The parameter of interest is the marginal mean of the outcome variable
\mathbb{E}[Y]
. Let
\pi_{A}(X)=P(A=1|X)
and
m_{1}(X)
denote the propensity score and regression function for
Y
, respectively. We start with the classic DR estimator assuming fully-observed covariates for the missing response problem. The efficient influence function takes the form

\frac{1(A=1)Y}{\pi_{A}(X)}+m_{1}(X)-\frac{1(A=1)m_{1}(X)}{\pi_{A}(X)}.

The expectation of the above quantity equals
\mathbb{E}[Y]
as long as at least one of
\pi_{A}(X)
and
m_{1}(X)
is correctly specified.
Using empirical average of the above quantity, we obtain the DR estimator under the missing response problem.

One key takeaway is that for a fairly simple problem, the estimation procedure for the doubly robust estimator is rather complex. In particular, there are 12 terms in total. Corollary 9

(Multiply-robustness for missing response under CCMV)
.

Define the parameter of interest

\theta=\sum_{r}\theta_{r}=\sum_{r}\int y\ P(dx,r,dy,da)=\int y\ P(dx,dr,dy,da).

The uncentered influence function
\sum_{r}q_{r}(X,R,Y,A)
yields a consistent estimator of
\theta=\sum_{r}\theta_{r}
under the model
\bigcap_{r}(\mathcal{M}_{R}(r)\cup\mathcal{M}_{O}(r))
.

Corollary
9
provides a strong result in that the estimator is

is the number of missing covariate patterns. As shown in Theorem

or the distribution

.

Since the missing response and binary treatment problems are related and well-studied problems, we omit the construction of our multistage estimator for the binary treatment problem. The included arguments can easily be extended to the binary treatment problem and the problem of longitudinal data with monotone missingness in the response.

\sigma_{\text{RA},r,a}^{2}>0
.

(C2)

For a fixed covariate pattern
r
and observed-outcome pattern
a
, define

\displaystyle m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r}=x_{r},R=r,W_{a}=w_{a},A=a]

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})\ P_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a;\eta_{r,a}).

The following class of regression functions is Donsker:

\{m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a}):\eta_{r,a}\in\mathcal{H}_{r,a}\}.

(C3)

For all covariate patterns
r
and observed-outcome patterns
a
, assume that
m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})
has a nonzero first derivative with respect to
\eta_{r,a}
. Also, let

\int|\nabla_{\eta_{r,a}}m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})|\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)

be finite in all of its entries, and assume that

\int(m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a}^{*}))^{2}\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)=o_{p}(1).

These are fairly mild assumptions. Assumption
(C1)
says that the extrapolation distribution belongs to a parametric family and that the estimator
\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}
admits an asymptotically linear expansion. Provided the parametric model holds, the
\sqrt{n}
-consistency of
\widehat{\beta}_{r,a}
is a reasonable assumption since
\eta^{*}_{r,a}
is often estimated using
M
-estimators. Assumptions
(C2)
and
(C3)
are similar to assumptions
(B3)
and
(B4)
made in the IPW estimation theorem.

B.4
Asymptotic theory

We can estimate
\theta
using a regression adjustment approach. Using Lemma
5
, the parameter writes as

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{r}\theta_{\text{RA},r,a}.

Thus, the main strategy will be to show asymptotic results for every fixed
r
and
a
pair.

Theorem 12

(Regression adjustment)
.

Suppose the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A4)
and
(C1)
-
(C3)
hold. Then, the estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{a}\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\sum_{a}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})1(R_{i}=r)1(A_{i}=a)\right)

is consistent, and for some
\sigma^{2}_{\text{RA}}
, we have the following convergence in distribution

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA}}-\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}N(0,\sigma^{2}_{\text{RA}}).

Theorem
12
establishes that a population quantity expressible in the form (
B
) can be estimated via a regression adjustment approach. The asymptotic normality of the provided estimator can be used to construct a confidence interval for inference. Although it is possible to construct a sandwich estimator, we again recommend using the bootstrap to estimate the variance because writing a closed form expression may not be so elegant.

Appendix C
Beyond CCMV

We extend the work in the main body of the paper to see the analysis under an identifying restriction that is not CCMV.
We consider the case with 2 covariate variables
(X_{1},X_{2})
subject to missingness for a total of
2^{2}=4
possible patterns.
For simplicity, let
v=(w_{a},a).

The CCMV assumption (Definition
3
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=01,v)=p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=11,v),

(19)

\displaystyle p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=10,v)=p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,v),

\displaystyle p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00,v)=p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,v).

The last equality can be relaxed by other assumptions.
Here we consider the following identifying restriction:

\displaystyle p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=01,v)=p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=11,v),

(20)

\displaystyle p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=10,v)=p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,v),

\displaystyle p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00,v)=\kappa_{1}\cdot p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=01,v)+\kappa_{2}\cdot p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=10,v)+\kappa_{3}\cdot p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,v)

for nonnegative constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
, and
\kappa_{3}
satisfying
\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}+\kappa_{3}=1
.
When
\kappa_{3}=1
, equation (
20
) reduces to the CCMV in equation (
19
).
If there exist variables that are always observed, then the above identifying restrictions can be generalized to allow
\kappa_{1}
,
\kappa_{2}
, and
\kappa_{3}
to be real-valued functions of the observed variables.
Under suitable choice of
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}
(as a function of observed variables),
the last restriction in equation (
20
)
becomes

\displaystyle p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00,v)=p(x_{1},x_{2}|R\in\{01,10,11\},v),

which shows connections to the available-case missing value assumption in
(
Molenberghs et al. 1998
)
.

C.1
IPW estimators

We now show that the (complete) odds admits a nice identifiable representation.
Let
V=(W_{A},A)
.
The two odds

\frac{P(R=10|X,V)}{P(R=11|X,V)}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{P(R=01|X,V)}{P(R=11|X,V)}

are identifiable via the same formulas under CCMV. We now decompose the odds
P(R=00|X,V)/P(R\in\{01,10,11\}|X,V)
via

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R\in\{01,10,11\}|v)}+\kappa_{2}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=10,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R\in\{01,10,11\}|v)}

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa_{3}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R\in\{01,10,11\}|v)}.

The first term can be decomposed by

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R\in\{01,10,11\}|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=01,v)p(x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,v)p(x_{1},R=10|v)+p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=11|v)p(x_{2},R=01|v)+p(x_{1},x_{2},R=11|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{\dfrac{p(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,v)p(x_{1},R=10|v)}{p(x_{1}|x_{2},R=01,v)}+p(x_{2},R=01|v)+p(x_{2},R=11|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{\dfrac{p(x_{1},x_{2},R=11|v)p(x_{1},R=10|v)p(x_{2},R=01|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R=01|v)p(x_{1},R=11|v)}+p(x_{2},R=01|v)+p(x_{2},R=11|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{p(x_{2}|R=01,v)P(R=00|v)}{\dfrac{P(R=11|x_{2},v)}{P(R=01|x_{2},v)}\cdot\dfrac{P(R=10|x_{1},v)}{P(R=11|x_{1},v)}\cdot p(x_{2},R=01|v)+p(x_{2},R=01|v)+p(x_{2},R=11|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{P(R=00|v)/P(R=01|v)}{\dfrac{P(R=11|x_{2},v)}{P(R=01|x_{2},v)}\cdot\dfrac{P(R=10|x_{1},v)}{P(R=11|x_{1},v)}+\dfrac{P(R=11|x_{2},v)}{P(R=01|x_{2},v)}+1},

which is identifiable.

A similar argument for the second term yields

\kappa_{2}\cdot\frac{P(R=00|v)/P(R=10|v)}{\dfrac{P(R=11|x_{1},v)}{P(R=10|x_{1},v)}\cdot\dfrac{P(R=01|x_{2},v)}{P(R=11|x_{2},v)}+\dfrac{P(R=11|x_{1},v)}{P(R=10|x_{1},v)}+1}.

The third term yields

\displaystyle\kappa_{3}\cdot\frac{p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,v)P(R=00|v)}{p(x_{1},x_{2},R\in\{01,10,11\}|v)}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{3}\cdot\frac{P(R=00|v)/P(R=11|v)}{\dfrac{P(R=10|x_{1},v)}{P(R=11|x_{1},v)}+\dfrac{P(R=01|x_{2},v)}{P(R=11|x_{2},v)}+1},

which is identifiable.

Thus, this implies that
P(R=00|x,v)/P(R=11|x,v)
is identifiable. We can model the odds
P(R=r|x_{r},v)/P(R=11|x_{r},v)
, and then, plug them into the formulas above to obtain an identifiable expression for
P(R=00|x,v)/P(R=11|x,v)
.

C.2
RA estimator (imputation)

First, as the identifying restriction is expressed in terms of the extrapolation distribution, the regression adjustment estimator is fairly straightforward. Since the extrapolation distribution
p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00)
is a mixture of three identifiable distributions, we can again utilize a Monte Carlo approximation to construct the regression adjustment estimator. This approximation is essentially the same as under CCMV except we sample from this mixture distribution for pattern
R=00
.

Appendix D
Sensitivity analysis

We present details on the sensitivity analyses utilized in the paper.

D.1
Sensitivity analysis for the odds under logistic regression

We have showed with Proposition
3
that under CCMV, the odds satisfies

O_{r,a}(X,W_{a}):=\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},a)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}\frac{P(R=r|X_{r},W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X_{r},W_{a},a)}=:Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a}).

Thus, the complete odds depend only on the variables observed under pattern
R=r
. We can introduce a sensitivity parameter to perturb this CCMV assumption using an exponential tilting method with logistic regression
(
Kim and Yu 2011
;
Shao and Wang 2016
;
Zhao et al. 2017
)
. This results in

O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})=Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})e^{\rho_{\bar{r}}X_{\bar{r}}},

(21)

where
\rho_{\bar{r}}
is a sensitivity parameter that consists of a vector of real numbers (one for each missing covariate under pattern
r
).
In practice, we often let every element of
\rho_{\bar{r}}
to be the same for simplicity.

We now consider the special case of logistic regression. Under CCMV with an assumed logistic regression model, the complete odds satisfies

\log O_{r,a}(X,W_{a};\alpha_{r,a})=\log Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a};\alpha_{r,a})=\alpha_{r,a}^{\top}X_{r}.

Under the sensitivity model (equation (
21
)), this becomes

\log O_{r,a}(X,W_{a};\alpha_{r,a},\rho_{\bar{r}})=\alpha_{r,a}^{\top}X_{r}+\rho_{\bar{r}}^{\top}X_{\bar{r}}.

Note that the first term on the RHS is the traditional term obtained by fitting a logistic regression model, and the second term is the tilting factor to push the missing data assumption away from CCMV.

Varying the elements of
\rho_{\bar{r}}
will modify the missing data assumption and change the weights. This allows one to determine the robustness of the estimator to changes in this sensitivity parameter. This sensitivity parameter has a nice interpretation because it is the coefficient in a linear model, and the range can be specified based on one’s belief about the relation of impact of the missing variables to the impact of the observed variables. For example, if the coefficient of the
X_{1}
variable is
1
and if
X_{2}
is subject to missingness, then the sensitivity parameter corresponding to
X_{2}
can be chosen to be within the interval
[-1,1]
by assuming that
X_{2}
has at most the same impact in magnitude as
X_{1}
.

D.2
Sensitivity analysis for binary imputation via rejection sampling

We also present an approach for sensitivity analysis under imputation model.
We first consider the case where the variable to be imputed
X
is binary.
Suppose that under an identifying restriction (such as the CCMV), we will impute the variable
X

by drawing
X\sim\text{Binomial}(p)
, where
p

is the probability parameter from a particular imputation model.

To perturb the imputation model, we consider the following procedure:

•

Draw
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
.

•

Consider a sensitivity parameter
\zeta\in[0,1]
. We iterate the following steps until a draw is accepted:

–

If
X=1
, with probability
\zeta
, redraw
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
. Otherwise, accept the draw.

–

If
X=0
, with probability
1-\zeta
, redraw
X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)
. Otherwise, accept the draw.

We denote this new random variable by
Y
. We can see when
\zeta=1
, we force
Y
to be deterministically
0
, and when
\zeta=0
, we force
Y
to be deterministically
1
. When
\zeta=1/2
, we resample exactly half of the time, so we obtain
Y=X
. Namely, when
\zeta=1/2
, we are under the unperturbed model.
Every other value of
\zeta
interpolates between these extremes.
Thus, the parameter
\zeta
can be viewed as a preference toward
X=0
.

It turns out that the above procedure admits a simpler expression.
Given that the final output is a binary number, the accepted value
Y
follows from a Bernoulli random variable.
Since
Y
is binary,
\mathbb{E}[Y]
is the probability of success. On the initial iteration, we draw
1
and accept it with probability
p(1-\zeta)
. Otherwise, we redraw with probability
p\zeta+(1-p)(1-\zeta)
and return to the same state. This yields the following equation

\mathbb{E}[Y]=p(1-\zeta)+p\zeta\mathbb{E}[Y]+(1-p)(1-\zeta)\mathbb{E}[Y],

so we have
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\dfrac{p(1-\zeta)}{1-p\zeta-(1-p)(1-\zeta)}=\dfrac{p(1-\zeta)}{p+\zeta-2p\zeta}
.

Thus, we actually do not need to perform the rejection sampling procedure.
Instead, we can simply draw

Y\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(\dfrac{p(1-\zeta)}{p+\zeta-2p\zeta}\right).

D.3
Sensitivity analysis for continuous imputation via Gaussian tilting

Now, we discuss the case of sensitivity analysis on imputing a continuous random variable.
Suppose that without perturbations, we impute a continuous random variable
X
from a PDF
p(x)
.
We propose the following modified approach:

•

Draw
X\sim p(x)
.

•

Consider a sensitivity parameter
\xi\in[0,\infty)
. First, we sample
X\sim p(x)
. We iterate the following steps until a draw is accepted:

–

With probability
\exp(-\xi X^{2})
, accept
X\sim p(x)
.

–

Otherwise, redraw
X
.

First, note that as
\xi\to\infty
, we almost always resample, and as
\xi\to 0
, we almost never resample. We denote this new random variable by
Y
. The distribution of
Y
is given by

P(Y\leq y)=P(X\leq y\ |\ \text{accept }X)=P(X\leq y\ |\ U\leq\exp(-\xi X^{2}))

for a random variable
U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)
independent of
X
. We see that

\displaystyle P(Y\leq y)

\displaystyle=P(X\leq y\ |\ U\leq\exp(-\xi X^{2}))

\displaystyle\propto P(X\leq y,U\leq\exp(-\xi X^{2}))

\displaystyle=P(U\leq\exp(-\xi X^{2})\ |\ X\leq y)\cdot P(X\leq y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y}\frac{P(U\leq\exp(-\xi x^{2})\ |\ x)}{P(X\leq y)}\cdot p(x)\ dx\cdot P(X\leq y)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y}P(U\leq\exp(-\xi x^{2})\ |\ x)\cdot p(x)\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y}\exp(-\xi x^{2})p(x)\ dx.

Therefore, we see that this resampling method is equivalent to multiplying the original PDF
p(x)
by
\exp(-\xi x^{2})
and renormalizing. Since the multiplication factor
\exp(-\xi x^{2})
is an exponential, this action can be considered a form of
Gaussian tilting
.

p(x)
is Gaussian.
Suppose that the original imputation distribution is Gaussian such that
X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. A simple completing the square argument shows that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}-\xi x^{2}=-\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}+\xi\right)\left(x-\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^{2}\xi+1}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4\sigma^{4}\xi}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Therefore, Gaussian tilting by
\exp(-\xi x^{2})
results in a random variable

Y\sim N\left(\frac{\mu}{2\sigma^{2}\xi+1},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\xi+1}\right).

As
\xi\to\infty
, the distribution for
Y
degenerates to a point mass of
1
at
0
. As
\xi\to 0
, we approach the original distribution
p(x)
, which is what we expect as we almost never resample.

p(x)
is Exponential.
Suppose
p(x)
is Exponential
(\lambda)
such that
p(x)=\lambda\exp(-\lambda x)
. Then, Gaussian tilting by
\exp(-\xi x^{2})
results in a truncated Gaussian that is formed by taking the PDF over the positive support of a Gaussian with mean
-\lambda/(2\xi)
and variance
1/\xi
.
As
\xi\to\infty
, again, the distribution for
Y
degenerates to a point mass of
1
at
0
.

E.1
Details on Cox model simulation

Here we provide details on the Cox model simulation.
Note that the data generating process is described in the main paper.

E.1.1
Estimation of the imputation model

In our simulated Cox model problem,
there are two covariates
X=(X_{1},X_{2})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
.
In the complete case, the observed-data distribution can be decomposed into the following terms:

\displaystyle p(x,R=11,y,\delta)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{p(x,R=11)}_{\text{Empirical}}\cdot\frac{\overbrace{P(R=11|x,y,\delta)}^{\text{Propensity score}}}{\underbrace{P(R=11|x)}_{\text{
Integration}}}\cdot\underbrace{p(y,\delta|x)}_{\text{EM}}.

Each of the above four components in the RHS is estimated as follows:

•

Empirical.
This component can be directly estimated from the data using empirical proportion.

•

Propensity score.
This is estimated by combining estimators of the complete odds
and use equation (
5
).

•

Integration.
The term
P(R=11|x)
can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle P(R=11|x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta=0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}P(R=11|x,y,\delta)p(y,\delta|x)\ dy.

In practice, this integral does not have an analytic form, so we use numerical integration in R to circumvent the problem.

•

EM.

The estimation of this component is more involved. We use an EM-like method to estimate it.
We assume a parametric model
p_{Y,\Delta|X}(y,\delta|x)=[\nu_{1}\exp(x^{\top}\beta)]^{\delta}\nu_{2}^{1-\delta}e^{-y(\nu_{1}\exp(x^{\top}\beta)+\nu_{2})}
; this model is derived from our design of the simulation. Fitting
p(y,\delta|x)
is a simple maximum likelihood procedure when the covariates are fully observed. Consider a specific
\widetilde{x}
. The model becomes

\displaystyle p_{Y,\Delta|X}(y,\delta|\widetilde{x})

\displaystyle=[\nu_{1}\exp(\widetilde{x}^{\top}\beta)]^{\delta}\nu_{2}^{1-\delta}e^{-y(\nu_{1}\exp(\widetilde{x}^{\top}\beta)+\nu_{2})}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\widetilde{x}}^{\delta}\nu_{2}^{1-\delta}e^{-y(\gamma_{\widetilde{x}}+\nu_{2})}

for
\gamma_{x}=\nu_{1}\exp(x^{\top}\beta)
. The ML estimators are

\widehat{\gamma}_{\widetilde{x}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x})}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\nu}_{2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x})-\delta_{i}1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x})}.

In practice, we do not know the covariates for all of the individuals. So, we fit the models using an EM algorithm. We replace the indicators
1(x_{i}=\widetilde{x})
with weights
w_{i}
.

Suppose
\widetilde{x}=(1,1)
. The weights are as follows:

w_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $x_{1}=x_{2}=1$}\\
0&\text{ if $x_{1}=0$ or $x_{2}=0$}\\
\widehat{P}(X_{2}=1|X_{1}=1,R=11,y,\delta)&\text{ if $x_{1}=1$ and $x_{2}$ missing}\\
\widehat{P}(X_{1}=1|X_{2}=1,R=11,y,\delta)&\text{ if $x_{2}=1$ and $x_{1}$ missing}\end{cases}.

The weights depend on the fitted parameters. For the EM algorithm, we iterate between updating
w_{i}
(E-step) and computing the MLEs (M-step).

With the above procedure, we are able to estimate the distribution
p(x,R=11,y,\delta)
,
which describes all imputation models under the CCMV assumption.

We include the following sensitivity analysis as part of our simulations. We fix a sample size of
n=1000
with
M=50
imputations. For the IPW approach, we vary the exponential tilting sensitivity parameter
\rho
in the interval
[-3,3]
.
For the imputation/RA approach, we change the binary sensitivity analysis parameter
\zeta
in the interval
[0,1]
. For each corresponding value of the sensitivity parameter, we compute the IPW and RA estimators, respectively. The true value of the parameter is indicated with the red line.

First, we observe that the IPW estimator appears to be fairly stable as we vary the sensitivity parameter, and the size of the confidence interval does not change drastically. Therefore, we conclude that the
\beta_{1}
parameter is significant even as we perturb the estimator. On the other hand, the point estimates of the RA estimator do appear to change slightly as we vary the imputation model. The

E.2.2
Details on the estimation

Note that in the binary treatment problem, we have two estimators;
one estimator to account for the missing covariates (denoted as IPW-R or RA-R)
and the other estimator for the ATE (denoted as IPW-A, RA-A, DR-A).

Final estimators.

For ease of simulation, we can consider the following 6 estimators for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]
.
Note that here we describe the linear functional form of the estimator.
We take the empirical average of them to form the final estimate.

1.

IPW-R, IPW-A.

\frac{1(R=11)1(A=1)Y}{P(A=1|X)}\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

2.

IPW-R, RA-A.

\frac{1(R=11)m_{1}(X)}{P(A=1|X)}\sum_{r}\sum_{a}1(A=a)Q_{r,a}(X_{r},Y)

3.

IPW-R, DR-A.

\displaystyle\frac{1(R=11)1(A=1)(Y-m_{1}(X))}{P(A=1|X)}\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1(R=11)m_{1}(X)}{P(A=1|X)}\sum_{r}\sum_{a}1(A=a)Q_{r,a}(X_{r},Y)

4.

RA-R, IPW-A.

1(A=1)Y\sum_{r}1(R=r)M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)

5.

RA-R, RA-A.

\sum_{r}\sum_{a}1(R=r)1(A=a)M_{m,r,a}(X_{r},Y)

6.

RA-R, DR-A.

\displaystyle 1(A=1)Y\sum_{r}1(R=r)M_{m,r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{r}\sum_{a}1(R=r)1(A=a)M_{m,r,a}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad-1(A=1)\sum_{r}1(R=r)M_{m/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)

As a reminder, the notation
m_{1}
,
M_{m,r,0}
,
M_{m,r,1}
,
M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}
, and
M_{m/\pi_{A},r,1}
refer to the following regression functions:

\displaystyle m_{1}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=1,X],

\displaystyle M_{m,r,0}(X_{r})=\mathbb{E}[m_{1}(X)|X_{r},R=r,A=0],

\displaystyle M_{m,r,1}(X_{r},Y)=\mathbb{E}[m_{1}(X)|X_{r},R=r,Y,A=1],

\displaystyle M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\pi_{A}(X)}\biggr|X_{r},R=r,Y,A=1\right],

\displaystyle M_{m/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{m_{1}(X)}{\pi_{A}(X)}\biggr|X_{r},R=r,Y,A=1\right].

Similar estimators can be constructed for
\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]
. Merging estimators for
\mathbb{E}[Y(1)]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y(0)]
together, we can obtain
6
estimators similar to the ones above for the ATE. We choose not to construct the multiply-robust estimator for simplicity. As seen in Theorem
8
, there will be numerous terms, so the multiply-robust estimator will generally be considered a theoretical construct.

Here we describe how to estimate the components in the above estimators.

IPW-R component.

The above construction implies that the complete odds has the following form:

\displaystyle Q_{r,a}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(R=r|X_{r},Y,A)}{P(R=11|X_{r},Y,A)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x_{r}|r,y,a)p(r,y,a)}{p(x_{r}|11,y,a)p(11,y,a)}.

In the special case where covariances are assumed to be the same for a fixed
y
and
a
, one may simplify the complete odds accordingly

\displaystyle Q_{10,a}(x_{1},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x_{1}|10,y,a)p(r,y,a)}{p(x_{1}|11,y,a)p(11,y,a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-(x_{1}-\mu_{1,10})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\cdot p(10,y,a)}{\exp(-(x_{1}-\mu_{1,11})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\cdot p(11,y,a)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\mu_{1,10}-\mu_{1,11}}{\sigma^{2}}x_{1}+\frac{\mu_{1,11}^{2}-\mu_{1,10}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{p(10,y,a)}{p(11,y,a)},

\displaystyle Q_{01,a}(x_{2},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(x_{2}|01,y,a)p(r,y,a)}{p(x_{2}|11,y,a)p(11,y,a)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(-(x_{2}-\mu_{2,01})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\cdot p(01,y,a)}{\exp(-(x_{2}-\mu_{2,11})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\cdot p(11,y,a)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\mu_{2,01}-\mu_{2,11}}{\sigma^{2}}x_{1}+\frac{\mu_{2,11}^{2}-\mu_{2,01}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{p(01,y,a)}{p(11,y,a)},

\displaystyle Q_{00,a}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(00,y,a)}{p(11,y,a)}.

The probabilities
p(r,y,a)
can be estimated by the empirical frequencies, and the means and variances are estimated using the sample versions.
Finally, the propensity score of
R=11
is

\pi_{R}(x,y,a)=P(R=11|x,y,a)=\frac{1}{1+Q_{10,a}(x_{1},y)+Q_{01,a}(x_{1},y)+Q_{00,a}(y)}.

RA-R component.

We estimate the regression functions
M_{m,r,0}
,
M_{m,r,1}
,
M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}
, and
M_{m/\pi_{A},r,1}
using a Monte Carlo approach, described in Section
3.2
. Specifically, in the simulation procedure, we make the assumption that the distribution
p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)
is a multivariate Gaussian and that the missing mechanism for the covariates satisfies the CCMV assumption. This implies that the conditional distributions
p(x_{1}|x_{2},r,y,a)
and
p(x_{2}|x_{1},r,y,a)
are conditional Gaussians. Therefore, we first can estimate the distributions
p(x_{1}|R=10,r,y,a)
,
p(x_{2}|R=01,r,y,a)
, and
p(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,r,y,a)
with a maximum likelihood procedure directly (plug in the sample means and variances/covariances as parameters). Then, under the CCMV assumption, we can construct the estimates of the distribution
p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)
in the following way:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,y,a),

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=01,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{1}|x_{2},R=11,y,a)\widehat{p}(x_{2}|R=01,y,a),

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=10,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,y,a)\widehat{p}(x_{1}|R=10,y,a).

This further implies that we can use the estimated distributions

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=00,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|R=11,y,a),

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{1}|x_{2},R=01,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{1}|x_{2},R=11,y,a),

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(x_{2}|x_{1},R=10,y,a)=\widehat{p}(x_{2}|x_{1},R=11,y,a)

to complete our data. We use the above extrapolation densities as an estimated imputation model. We then sample the missing covariates
M=50
times to generate multiple complete data sets
\widetilde{D}^{(j)}=\{(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)},R_{i},Y_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}=\left(X_{i,R_{i}},\widetilde{X}_{i,\bar{R}_{i}}^{(j)}\right)
refers to the
j
th imputed missing covariates appended to the observed covariates corresponding to the
i
th observation.

We pool all
\widetilde{D}^{(j)}
data sets together to form one large data set
\check{D}
. This enables us to take the empirical average of the RA-
R
estimators directly using all of the completed data
\check{D}
. For example, for the (RA-
R
, IPW-
A
) estimator, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[1(A_{i}=1)Y_{i}\sum_{r}1(R_{i}=r)\widehat{M}_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{R_{i},i},Y_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[1(A_{i}=1)Y_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{A}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left[1(A_{i}=1)Y_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{A}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}\right].

Similar logic implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{r}\sum_{a}1(R_{i}=r)1(A_{i}=a)\widehat{M}_{m,r,a}(X_{R_{i},i},Y_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{a}1(A_{i}=a)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\widehat{m}_{1}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\widehat{m}_{1}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[1(A_{i}=1)\sum_{r}1(R_{i}=r)\widehat{M}_{m/\pi_{A},r,a}(X_{R_{i},i},Y_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[1(A_{i}=1)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{\widehat{m}_{1}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}{\widehat{\pi}_{A}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{Mn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{M}1(A_{i}=1)\frac{\widehat{m}_{1}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}{\widehat{\pi}_{A}(\widetilde{X}_{i}^{(j)})}.

With these equations, we can construct the (RA-
R
, RA-
A
) and (RA-
R
, DR-
A
) estimators.

IPW-A component.

The propensity score can be calculated via

\displaystyle\pi_{A}(x)=P(A=1|x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{r}\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,A=1)p(r,y,A=1)}{\sum_{r}\sum_{y=0}^{1}\sum_{a=0}^{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)p(r,y,a)}.

We describe how to estimate the distribution
\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)
in the previous discussion on the
RA-R component
.

RA-A component.

Since
Y
is binary, the regression function can be calculated via

\displaystyle m_{a}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|A=a,x_{1},x_{2}]

\displaystyle=P(Y=1|A=a,x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{r}p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,Y=1,A=a)p(r,Y=1,A=a)}{\sum_{r}\sum_{y=0}^{1}p(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)p(r,y,a)}.

We describe how to estimate the distribution
\widehat{p}(x_{1},x_{2}|r,y,a)

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let the observed outcome pattern
a
be fixed. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=r)]

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)1(r^{\prime}=r)\ p(x,r^{\prime},w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdr^{\prime}dw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)\ p(x,r,w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)\ \frac{p(x,r,w_{a},a^{\prime})}{p(x,1_{d},w_{a},a^{\prime})}\ p(x,1_{d},w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)\frac{P(R=r|x,w_{a},a^{\prime})}{P(R=1_{d}|x,w_{a},a^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\qquad\times 1(s=1_{d})\ p(x,s,w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdsdw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d})],

as desired.

Finally, note that summing over all patterns
r
yields the second decomposition

\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d})].

\square

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Fix an observed outcome pattern
a
. We start with the forward direction. Suppose the complete odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
is identifiable for all
r
. Then,

\sum_{r}O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})=\sum_{r}\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)}=\frac{1}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)}

is identifiable. The desired selection probabilities can be recovered because

P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)=P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)\cdot O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})

for any covariate missingness pattern
r
.

For the converse, an identifiable set of selection probabilities
P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)
for all
r
immediately implies that the complete odds can be formed and is identifiable since the complete odds is the exactly the ratio of two selection probabilities.

\square

Proof of Proposition
3
.

This result is best seen by showing that the CCMV formulation in (
6
) is equivalent to the CCMV formulation in (
7
), so we will proceed with a series of equivalences. We have

\displaystyle p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)=p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},w_{a},a)

\displaystyle\quad\iff\frac{p(x,r,w_{a},a)}{p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)}=\frac{p(x,1_{d},w_{a},a)}{p(x_{r},1_{d},w_{a},a)}

\displaystyle\quad\iff\frac{p(x,r,w_{a},a)}{p(x,1_{d},w_{a},a)}=\frac{p(x_{r},r,w_{a},)}{p(x_{r},1_{d},w_{a},a)}

\displaystyle\quad\iff\frac{P(R=r|X,W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},a)}=\frac{P(R=r|X_{r},W_{a},a)}{P(R=1_{d}|X_{r},W_{a},a)}

\displaystyle\quad\iff O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})=Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a}).

Thus, the CCMV missing data assumption on the covariates is equivalent through the pattern mixture model and complete odds formulations.

\square

Proof of Theorem
4
.

We wish to show that under the CCMV assumption, the full-covariate distribution
p(x,r,w_{a},a)
is nonparametrically identified. First, we factorize the target distribution using a pattern mixture model approach

p(x,r,w_{a},a)=p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)p(x_{r},w_{a},a).

Note that the distribution
p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)
is identifiable from the observed data, so the only part we need to handle is the extrapolation density
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)
. By the CCMV assumption, we have the equality

p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},w_{a},a).

Since the distribution
p(x,R=1_{d},w_{a},a)
is identifiable from the observed data (consider only the observations with fully-observed covariates and observed outcome pattern
A=a
), the conditional distribution
p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},R=1_{d},w_{a},a)
is identifiable. This means that the extrapolation density is equated to an identifiable quantity.

The distribution
p(x,r,w_{a},a)
derived from the CCMV identifying restriction does not conflict with the observed data
because after marginalizing out the unobserved variable
x_{\bar{r}}
,
we obtain
p(x_{r},w_{a},a)
, so we have nonparametric identification.

\square

Proof of Lemma
5
.

The expectation rewrites as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=r)]

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)1(r^{\prime}=r)\ p(x,r,w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdr^{\prime}dw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})\ p(x,r,w_{a},a)\ dxdw_{a}

\displaystyle=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{\bar{r}}\ p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}.

Recall that
m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a})\ p(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{\bar{r}}
is the regression function.
Substituting it into the previous expression yields

\displaystyle\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})\ p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)].

Finally, summing over all patterns
r
yields the second result

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)].

\square

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Fix the observed outcome pattern
a
, and suppose the CCMV assumption holds for

\mathbb{E}[g_{r}(X,W_{a})]=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=r)]

Case 1.
In the first case, we will assume that the complete odds
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
is correctly specified in that it equals
P(R=r|X,W_{a},A=a)/P(R=1_{d}|X,W_{a},A=a)
. Since term (

) has the desired expectation when the complete odds are correctly specified, it suffices to show that term (
13
) minus term (

\displaystyle m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)-m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=1_{d})O_{r,a}(X,W_{a}).

Taking the expectation yields

\displaystyle\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}

\displaystyle\quad-\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})1(a^{\prime}=a)1(s=1_{d})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a})\ p(x,s,w_{a},a^{\prime})\ dxdsdw_{a}da^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a})\ p(x,1_{d},w_{a},a)\ dxdw_{a}.

Since
O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})
is assumed to properly specified, this further simplifies to

\displaystyle\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}-\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x,r,w_{a},a)\ dxdw_{a}

\displaystyle=\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}-\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a})p(x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ dx_{r}dw_{a}

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[g(X,W_{a})]=\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]
when

is correctly specified.

Case 2.
We now consider the second case. Suppose that the regression function

is correctly specified. Since term (
13
) has the desired expectation under this assumption, it suffices to show that term (
12
) minus term (
14
) has mean 0.

Consider the expectation of (
12
). Using the law of total expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=1_{d})O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=1_{d},W_{a},A=a]|R=1_{d},A=a]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot P(R=1_{d},A=a)

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{CCMV}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]|R=1_{d},A=a]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot P(R=1_{d},A=a)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]\cdot Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})|R=1_{d},A=a]

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot P(R=1_{d},A=a).

m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})
is assumed to be correctly specified, this further simplifies as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})\cdot Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})|R=1_{d},A=a]\cdot P(R=1_{d},A=a)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})Q_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(A=a)1(R=1_{d})]

14

12

14
) has mean 0.

Since we showed the expectation is correct in these two cases, we can conclude this form is doubly robust.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

The heart of the proof lies with Lemma
6
. We will argue that the population estimating equation has mean
0
. Consider the multiple robust population estimating equation with

\displaystyle U_{r}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)1(R=1_{d})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\biggr|X_{r},R,Y,\Delta\right]\cdot 1(R=r)\cdot 1(\Delta=1)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\biggr|X_{r},R,Y,\Delta\right]\cdot 1(\Delta=1)\cdot 1(R=1_{d})\cdot Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)\right],

\displaystyle s^{(1)}_{r}(\beta)=\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)Xe^{\beta^{\top}X}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)1(\Delta=\delta)1(R=1_{d})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}[m^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)1(R=r)1(\Delta=\delta)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[m^{(1)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)\cdot 1(\Delta=1)\cdot 1(R=1_{d})\cdot Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)\right],

\displaystyle s^{(0)}_{r}(\beta)=\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}[1(Y\geq t)e^{\beta^{\top}X}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)1(\Delta=\delta)1(R=1_{d})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}[m^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)1(R=r)1(\Delta=\delta)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[m^{(0)}_{r,\delta}(X_{r},Y;t,\beta)\cdot 1(\Delta=1)\cdot 1(R=1_{d})\cdot Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)\right].

From Lemma
6
, the three above terms have expectation equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\left(X-\frac{s^{(1)}(Y;\beta)}{s^{(0)}(Y;\beta)}\right)1(R=r)\right],

is correctly specified. Thus,
\sum_{r}U_{r}(\beta)
has mean
0
, and the near-solution
\widehat{\beta}_{\text{mr}}
to the finite-sample estimating equation is consistent under standard regularity conditions.

\square

\displaystyle\quad+1(A=0)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=r)M_{m,r,0}(X_{r})-1(A=0)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=1_{d})M_{m,r,0}(X_{r})Q_{r,0}(X_{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1(A=1)Y}{P(A=1|X)}\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)1(R=1_{d})+1(A=1)Y\sum_{r}1(R=r)M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad-1(A=1)Y1(R=1_{d})\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)M_{1/\pi_{A},r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad-1(R=1_{d})1(A=1)\frac{m_{1}(X)}{\pi_{A}(X)}\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad-1(A=1)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=r)M_{m/\pi_{A},1}(X_{r},Y)+1(A=1)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=1_{d})M_{m/\pi_{A},1}(X_{r},Y)Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad+1(R=1_{d})1(A=0)m_{1}(X)\sum_{r}Q_{r,0}(X_{r})+1(R=1_{d})1(A=1)m_{1}(X)\sum_{r}Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad+1(A=1)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=r)M_{m,r,1}(X_{r},Y)-1(A=1)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=1_{d})M_{m,r,1}(X_{r},Y)Q_{r,1}(X_{r},Y)

\displaystyle\quad+1(A=0)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=r)M_{m,r,0}(X_{r})-1(A=0)\sum_{r\neq 1_{d}}1(R=1_{d})M_{m,r,0}(X_{r})Q_{r,0}(X_{r})

\displaystyle=q(X,R,Y,A).

This has mean equivalent to
\theta
, so the efficient influence function is
\text{EIF}(X,R,Y,A)=q(X,R,Y,A)-\theta
. This shows that the multiply-robust estimator for the missing response problem achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound.

\square

G.3
Asymptotic theory

This section contains proof of
Theorem
11
and Theorem
12
.
For the ease of derivation and referencing the literature (e.g. Chapter 19 of
van der Vaart 1998
),
we introduce notations from the empirical process theory.
We denote
P
as the true probability measure that generates our data
and
P_{n}
be the empirical measure.
Namely, in the case of observing IID random elements
Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n}\in\mathcal{Z}
,
P(z)
is the underlying distribution
that generates
Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{n}
and
P_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(Z_{i}\leq z)

is the corresponding empirical distribution.
For any function
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
we denote

Pf=\int f(z)P(dz)=\mathbb{E}(f(Z)),\quad P_{n}f=\int f(z)P_{n}(dz)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(Z_{i}).

Proof of Theorem
11
.

Following Lemma
1
, we rewrite the population quantity of interest as

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{r}\theta_{\text{IPW},r,a}.

Since we need to estimate
f_{a}
and
O_{r,a}
, our estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{r}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}_{a}(X_{i},W_{i,a})1(A_{i}=a)\widehat{O}_{r,a}(X_{i},W_{i,a})1(R_{i}=1_{d})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{r}\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r,a}.

We will show that
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r,a}
, as defined above, is an asymptotically linear estimator for
\theta_{\text{IPW},r,a}
. For ease of typesetting, define

\displaystyle q_{r,a}(X,R,W_{a},A):=f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)O_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d}),

\displaystyle\widehat{q}_{r,a}(X,R,W_{a},A):=\widehat{f}_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)\widehat{O}_{r,a}(X,W_{a})1(R=1_{d}).

Using the empirical process notations, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r,a}-\theta_{\text{IPW},r,a}

\displaystyle=P_{n}\widehat{q}_{r,a}-P_{0}q_{r,a}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(P_{n}-P_{0})q_{r,a}}_{(I)}+\underbrace{P_{0}(\widehat{q}_{r,a}-q_{r,a})}_{(II)}+\underbrace{(P_{n}-P_{0})(\widehat{q}_{r,a}-q_{r,a})}_{(III)}.

Therefore, there are 3 terms we need to consider to show asymptotic normality.

Term (I)
. Expanding this term, we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{n}-P_{0})q_{r,a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(q(X,R,W_{a},A)-\mathbb{E}[q(X,R,W_{a},A)]),

which is immediately asymptotically linear.

Term (II).
Collect the parameters
\tau_{a}
and
\eta_{r,a}
into a single parameter
\kappa_{r,a}
for simplicity. As the first derivative of
q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\kappa_{r,a})
exists and is nonzero (assumptions
(A4)
and
(B4)
), Term (II) equals

\displaystyle P_{0}(\widehat{q}_{r,a}-q_{r,a})

\displaystyle=\int[q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\widehat{\kappa}_{r,a})-q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\kappa^{*}_{r,a})]\ P(dx,dr,dw_{a},da)

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\kappa}_{r,a}-\kappa^{*}_{r,a})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\kappa_{r,a}}q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\kappa_{r,a})\ P(dx,dr,dw_{a},da)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+o_{p}(1/\sqrt{n}).

The interchange of the derivative and the integral follows from the Dominated Convergence Theorem. Since we assume the
\widehat{\kappa}_{r,a}
admits an asymptotically linear expansion (assumptions
(A1)
and
(B2)
), this further simplifies to

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r,a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,a},A_{i})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\kappa_{r,a}}q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\kappa_{r,a})\ P(dx,dr,dw_{a},da)\right)+o_{p}(1/\sqrt{n}),

where
\varphi_{r,a}
is the influence function for
\widehat{\kappa}_{r,a}
.

Term (III).
For the last term, we show that is negligible up to first order. We apply Lemma 19.24 from
(
van der Vaart 1998
)
. We verify the following two conditions:

1.

\mathcal{Q}:=\{q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a};\tau,\eta):\tau,\eta\}
is a
P
-Donsker class:

Since
\{f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau):\tau\}
and
\{O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta):\eta\}
are uniformly bounded
P
-Donsker classes, it follows that
\mathcal{Q}
is also
P
-Donsker.

The class of functions formed by the pairwise products of functions from two uniformly bounded
P
-Donsker classes is
P
-Donsker (this permanence property can be seen using a Lipschitz transformation argument; see Example 2.10.8 from
(
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
)
).

2.

q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\widehat{\tau}_{a},\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})
converges to some
q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\tau^{*}_{a},\eta^{*}_{r,a})
in
L_{2}(P)
:

We have

\displaystyle\int[q_{r,a}(x,s,w_{a},a^{\prime};\widehat{\tau}_{a},\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-q_{r,a}(x,s,w_{a},a^{\prime};\tau^{*}_{a},\eta^{*}_{r,a})]^{2}\ P(dx,ds,dw_{a},da^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad=\int 1(a^{\prime}=a)1(s=1_{d})\biggr[f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})

\displaystyle\qquad-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\biggr]^{2}\ P(dx,ds,dw_{a},da^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int[f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})]^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad P(dx,ds,dw_{a},da^{\prime}).

We can upper bound the
L_{2}(P)
norm via

\displaystyle\lVert f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})[O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})]\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\lVert[f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*})]O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\lVert O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\lVert f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*}_{a})\rVert_{2}\cdot\lVert O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\rVert_{\infty}

by Minkowski’s and Holder’s inequalities. By assumptions
(A2)
,
(A3)
,
(B1)
, and
(B4)
, we have
L_{2}(P)
convergence of
f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})
to the function
f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau^{*}_{a})O_{r,a}(x,w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})
.

Therefore, the sufficient conditions of the Lemma 19.24 of
(
van der Vaart 1998
)
are satisfied, and it follows that Term (III) is
o_{p}(1/\sqrt{n})
.

Merging terms.
Finally, putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{r,a}-\theta_{r,a})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(q(X,R,W_{a},A)-\mathbb{E}[q(X,R,W_{a},A)])

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{r,a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,a},A_{i})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\kappa_{r,a}}q_{r,a}(x,r,w_{a},a;\kappa_{r,a})\ P(dx,dr,dw_{a},da)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+o_{p}(1)

for some influence function
\varphi_{r,a}
for
\widehat{\kappa}_{r,a}
(this follows from the asymptotic linearity assumptions
(A1)
and
(B2)
. Next, we note that
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r,a}
is generally correlated with this
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r^{\prime},a^{\prime}}
for some
r\neq r^{\prime}
and
a\neq a^{\prime}
. Therefore, they do not typically converge jointly to a degenerate distribution. As we have provided the asymptotic linear forms for each of the estimators marginally, we have joint convergence of the
\{\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW},r,a}-\theta_{\text{IPW},r,a})\}_{r,a}
to a zero-mean multivariate Gaussian by the Central Limit Theorem. The asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{IPW}}
follows from the Continuous Mapping Theorem. Consistency follows from asymptotic normality since we have convergence at the standard
\sqrt{n}
-rate.

\square

Proof of Theorem
12
.
Using Lemma
5
, we can decompose
\theta
using a regression adjustment method as follows

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(X,W,A)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{r}\mathbb{E}[m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})1(R=r)1(A=a)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{r}\theta_{\text{RA},r,a},

where
m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a})=\ E[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r},R=r,W_{a},A=a]
is a regression function. Here
\theta_{\text{RA},r,a}=\int\mathbb{E}[f_{a}(X,W_{a})|X_{r}=x_{r},R=r,W_{a}=w_{a},A=a]\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)
.

To start, we fix a covariate pattern
r
and an observed outcome pattern
a
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})\ \widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ \widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ \widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ \widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a),

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{RA},r,a}=\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ P_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a).

Then, we can decompose the difference via

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}-\theta_{RA,r,a})=\underbrace{\sqrt{n}[\widehat{\theta}_{RA,r,a}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}]}_{(I)}+\underbrace{\sqrt{n}[\widetilde{\theta}_{RA,r,a}-\theta_{RA,r,a}]}_{(II)}.

Term (I).
First, we start with Term (I), which rewrites as

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{n}\int[f_{a}(x,w_{a};\widehat{\tau}_{a})-f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})]\ \widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},w_{a},a)\ \widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\tau}_{a}-\tau_{a}^{*})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\tau_{a}}\ f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a})\ \widehat{P}(dx,r,dw_{a},a)\right)+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\left(\int\nabla_{\tau_{a}}\ f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a})\ \widehat{P}(dx,r,dw_{a},a)\right)+o_{p}(1)

by the parametric assumption of
(A1)
.

Term (II).
Now, we consider Term (II), which requires slightly more work. Expanding it and grouping terms, we obtain the following decomposition.

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widetilde{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}-\theta_{\text{RA},r,a})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\int\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ \widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{n}\int\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ P_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sqrt{n}\int\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ \widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)(\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a))}_{\text{(A)}}

\displaystyle\quad-\underbrace{\sqrt{n}\int\int f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a}^{*})\ (\widehat{P}_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)-P_{ex}(dx_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a))P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)}_{\text{(B)}}

Term A of (I).
Term A rewrites as

\sqrt{n}\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)),

This is equal to

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\int(m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a}))\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a))

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{n}\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)).

Observe that the conditions of Lemma 19.24 of
(
van der Vaart 1998
)
are satisfied (namely,
\widehat{m}_{r,a}
belongs to a Donsker class by
(C2)
and we have
L_{2}(P)
convergence to the true regression function by
(C3)
). Therefore, it follows that the first term in the previous display is
o_{p}(1)
. Thus, for asymptotic analysis, we can replace
\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}
in Term A with the population version
\eta_{r,a}^{*}
. This means that Term A

\sqrt{n}\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a))

is asymptotically equivalent to (up to an
o_{p}(1)
term)

\sqrt{n}\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)).

Term B of (II).
We now consider the term B. It rewrites as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\int(m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\widehat{\eta}_{r,a})-m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a}^{*}))\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}-\eta_{r,a}^{*})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\eta_{r,a}}\ m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)\right)+o_{p}(1).

Since the first derivative of
m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})
is bounded, the interchange of the derivative and the integral follows from the Dominated Convergence Theorem.

Collecting Terms (I) and (II).
We combine Terms A and B of Term (II) and add them to Term (I), and it follows that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}-\theta_{\text{RA},r,a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\left(\int\nabla_{\tau_{a}}\ f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a})\ \widehat{P}(dx,r,dw_{a},a)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{n}\int m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta^{*}_{r,a})\ (\widehat{P}_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)-P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a))

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}-\eta_{r,a}^{*})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\eta_{r,a}}\ m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\left(\int\nabla_{\tau_{a}}\ f_{a}(x,w_{a};\tau_{a})\ \widehat{P}(dx,r,dw_{a},a)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}-\eta_{r,a}^{*})^{\top}\left(\int\nabla_{\eta_{r,a}}\ m_{r,a}(x_{r},w_{a};\eta_{r,a})\ P_{obs}(dx_{r},r,dw_{a},a)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggr[m_{r,a}(X_{i,R_{i}},W_{i,A_{i}};\eta_{r,a}^{*})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[1(R=r)1(A=a)m_{r,a}(X_{R},W_{A};\beta_{r,a}^{*})]\biggr]

\displaystyle\quad+o_{p}(1).

By Slutsky’s lemma and the WLLN, this is equivalent up to an
o_{p}(1)
term to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\cdot\mathbb{E}[1(R=r)1(A=a)\nabla_{\tau_{a}}\ f_{a}(X,W_{a};\tau_{a})]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{r,a}(X_{i},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i})\cdot\mathbb{E}[1(R=r)1(A=a)\nabla_{\beta_{r,a}}\ m_{r,a}(X_{r},W_{a};\eta_{r,a})]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggr[m_{r,a}(X_{i,R_{i}},R_{i},W_{i,A_{i}},A_{i};\beta_{r,a}^{*})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}[1(R=r)1(A=a)m_{r,a}(X_{R},R,W_{A},A;\beta_{r,a}^{*})]\biggr],

where
\psi_{r,a}
is the influence function of
\widehat{\eta}_{r,a}
since it is assumed to be an asymptotic linear estimator by Assumption
(C1)
.

Thus, we have showed that
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}
is an asymptotically linear estimator. Next, note that in standard missing data problems, different subsets of the observed data set are used to estimate the extrapolation distribution
P_{ex}(x_{\bar{r}}|x_{r},r,w_{a},a)
for different values of
r
and
a
, as this relies on the identifying restrictions used. Therefore,
|\text{Corr}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a},\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r^{\prime},a^{\prime}})|\neq 1
when
r\neq r^{\prime}
or
a\neq a^{\prime}
, so the joint convergence of all the
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA},r,a}
s to a multivariate normal distribution follows since marginally, they all admit an asymptotic linear expansion. Finally, applying the Continuous Mapping Theorem yields the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{RA}}
, and consistency follows.

\square
