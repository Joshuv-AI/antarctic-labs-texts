Convergence and concentration properties of constant step-size SGD through Markov chains
Ibrahim Merad, Stéphane Gaïffas

Abstract
We consider the optimization of a smooth and strongly convex objective using constant step-size stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and study its properties through the prism of Markov chains. We show that, for unbiased gradient estimates with mildly controlled variance, the iteration converges to an invariant distribution in total variation distance. We also establish this convergence in Wasserstein-2 distance in a more general setting compared to previous work. Thanks to the invariance property of the limit distribution, our analysis shows that the latter inherits sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential concentration properties when these hold true for the gradient. This allows the derivation of high-confidence bounds for the final estimate. Finally, under such conditions in the linear case, we obtain a dimension-free deviation bound for the Polyak-Ruppert average of a tail sequence. All our results are non-asymptotic and their consequences are discussed through a few applications. Keywords. stochastic approximation; Markov chains; Polyak-Ruppert averaging; generalization error

Introduction

We consider the following stochastic optimization problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta}[\ell(\theta,\zeta)],

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a smooth strongly convex objective only accessible through unbiased random gradient samples
G(\theta,\zeta)=\nabla\ell(\theta,\zeta)
which may be queried at any parameter value
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given an initial point
\theta_{0}
and a step-size
\beta,
problem (
1
) is commonly solved using the well-known stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm defined by the iteration

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\beta G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t}),\quad\text{ for }\quad t\geq 0.

(2)

We study the convergence properties of the Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by the above iteration as well as the concentration properties satisfied by a derived estimator
\widehat{\theta}
of the global optimum
\theta^{\star}=\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
based on the concentration of the gradient samples
G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t}).

Problem (
1
) is the common formulation for a large fraction of statistical learning problems where the objective
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is defined as the expectation of a loss function
\ell
over a random variable
\zeta
following an unknown distribution of samples. In a practical setting, the random gradients
G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t})
are computed using a dataset of independent and identically distributed samples
(\zeta_{i})_{i=1}^{n}.
The SGD algorithm is employed to solve (
1
) in two situations. Either the samples
(\zeta_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are available offline but in such a great amount that using the whole dataset at each gradient step incurs an excessive computational load, therefore, individual samples or small batches are used at each iteration instead. Or, the samples
\zeta_{i}
are received individually in an online fashion and optimization must be run using one instance at a time. Our framework covers both cases provided that each iteration uses new data which is independent from the past.

Thanks to its simplicity and efficiency, the SGD algorithm is widely adopted as the go-to approach for stochastic optimization problems in general. Since its first appearance in the seminal work of
[
73
]
the theoretical properties of SGD have been investigated in a series of pioneering works
[
13
,
80
,
22
]
. A notable milestone in these theoretical developments was the discovery of Polyak-Ruppert averaging
[
79
,
69
]
which allows to reduce the impact of noise and improve the convergence rate for certain cases of interest. The subject benefited from a growing attention with the advent of complex machine learning models such as neural networks and a rich literature has appeared to address the surfacing questions about SGD and its numerous variants and use cases
[
84
,
1
,
61
,
9
,
64
]
.

Although the basic definition of the SGD iteration (
2
) is quite simple, a great number of variations are possible by playing on various aspects among which the choice of step-size is critical. Early work
[
73
]
suggested a decaying step-size of order
t^{-1}
but this leads to poor dependence on problem conditioning
[
1
,
22
]
while other step-size schedules with slower decay of order
t^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(1/2,1]
combined with averaging achieve better practical and theoretical performance
[
79
,
69
,
61
]
. In this work, we consider constant step-size SGD which is also a commonly adopted choice due to its usually fast convergence
[
82
,
87
,
54
]
.

1.1
Main contributions

This paper studies constant step-size SGD as a Markov chain and makes the following contributions.

•

We state two convergence results of the Markov chain to an invariant distribution. The first ergodicity theorem states convergence in total variation distance and the second one in terms of the Wasserstein-
2
distance. While similar results exist in the literature, our version for the Wasserstein convergence mode holds in a more general setting than previous results.

•

We show that sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential concentration of the gradient samples imply the same property for the invariant limit distribution with
\Psi_{1}/\Psi_{2}
constant proportional to the step-size. Thanks to this property, we obtain high-confidence deviation bounds on the final SGD iterate.

•

Provide a slightly stronger concentration assumption on the gradient samples, we show similar but dimension-free high-confidence bounds on the last SGD iterate.

•

Finally, for the special case of a linear gradient, we obtain a high-confidence dimension-free bound for the Polyak-Ruppert average of a tail sequence of the SGD iterates. This is achieved, in part, thanks to a more generic concentration result which holds for any Lipschitz function applied to a stationary sequence.

All our results are non-asymptotic.

1.2
Related works

SGD as a Markov chain.

A fairly limited portion of the SGD literature adopts the Markov chain approach. Among the earliest,
[
68
]
studied the iteration in question for vanishing step-size, while
[
2
]
considers constant step-size averaged SGD for non-strongly-convex smooth objectives and shows
L_{p}
convergence of the excess risk for all
p\geq 1.
Although their analysis does not use Markov chain theory, they discuss properties of the invariant distribution which the iteration converges to, including a few properties we state in this paper. However, they do not derive high-confidence estimation bounds as we do. More recently, convergence in Wasserstein distance was established by
[
18
]
for constant step-size SGD applied to a strongly convex and smooth objective, albeit under a co-coercivity condition which is hard to establish in the nonlinear case. Further, an expansion of the asymptotic moments of averaged SGD is provided in
[
18
]
and the Richardson-Romberg extrapolation strategy is studied which allows to reduce the estimation error on the global optimum. Most recently,
[
91
]
studied SGD run on a non-convex, non-smooth but quadratically growing objective. Under such weakened conditions, they show that the generated Markov chain is geometrically ergodic (see
[
58
]
) and proceed to establish a CLT for the generated Markov iterates. They also state results controlling the bias of the limit distribution under additional assumptions such as convexity,
L_{4}
control of the gradient noise and a generalized Łojasiewicz condition
[
39
]
.

High probability bounds.

In addition to establishing the convergence of SGD in expectation, the works of
[
72
,
2
,
1
]
make the further step of stating high-confidence bounds on the final error. Still, sub-Gaussian concentration only holds under strong bounded gradient assumptions. High-confidence deviation results are also stated in
[
26
]
where an accelerated stochastic optimization method for strongly convex composite objectives is studied. However, the bounds are sub-exponential while the gradient is assumed to be sub-Gaussian.

In
[
38
]
, high probability bounds are proved for the PEGASOS algorithm using Freedman’s inequality for martingales
[
23
]
. A generalization of the said inequality was used by
[
32
,
31
]
to prove such bounds for SGD in the non-smooth strongly convex case. Most recently, for a careful choice of step-size,
[
36
]
obtained high-confidence results on the last SGD iterate. Unfortunately, both previous works require a deterministic bound to hold over the gradient or its noise which strongly constrains their applicability. Finally, a high probability analysis of Delayed AdaGrad with momentum was presented by
[
48
]
in the smooth non-convex setting.

Note that certain recent works design
robust
variants of SGD achieving sub-Gaussian deviation bounds on the last iterate with only a second moment assumption on the gradient
[
85
,
27
]
. Similar results were later obtained under even weaker gradient moment assumptions
[
81
,
67
]
. However, in this work, we focus on the
classical
SGD algorithm and the properties inherited by its iterates from the gradient samples.

Polyak-Ruppert averaging.

The averaging procedure introduced by
[
69
,
79
]
was also studied by
[
30
,
14
]
who proved asymptotic convergence properties. Non-asymptotic results and additional developments appeared in the works of
[
61
,
17
,
19
,
35
,
34
,
46
]
with particular attention to least-squares, logistic regression and kernel-based methods. In particular, non-asymptotic results of convergence in expectation were obtained for averaged SGD in
[
19
,
46
,
66
,
72
]
. Among such results, some demonstrate the advantages of special averaging schemes
[
84
,
45
]
. Finally, some relatively recent works obtained high probability concentration bounds for Polyak Ruppert averaging with and without sub-Gaussian assumptions on the data
[
60
,
49
]
.

2
Setting and notations

Let
\Theta
denote either a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
itself depending on context.
We refer to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

For any random variable
X,
we denote
\mathcal{D}(X)
its distribution. We refer to the space of square-integrable measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
We denote
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of probability measures over
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
For real numbers
a
and
b,
we denote
\min(a,b)=a\wedge b
and
\max(a,b)=a\vee b.
We denote
\Lip(\mathcal{X})
the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.
For
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*},
we denote
\|X\|_{L_{p}}=(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p})^{1/p}
the
L_{p}
norm of a random variable
X.

In the entirety of this work, we assume that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies

Assumption 1
.

There exist positive constants
0<\mu\leq L<+\infty
such that

\frac{\mu}{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})-\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}

for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
i.e.
\mathcal{L}
is
L
gradient-Lipschitz and
\mu
-strongly convex.

As an immediate consequence,
\mathcal{L}
admits a unique minimum
\theta^{\star}
which is a critical point:

\theta^{\star}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\quad\text{such that}\quad\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\star})=0.

(3)

For an initial
\theta_{0}\in\Theta,
step-size
\beta>0
and all
t\geq 0,
we recall the basic SGD iteration

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\beta G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t}).

(4)

In this work, we consider constant step-size SGD so that
\beta
is fixed along the iteration. We require some basic assumptions on the samples
G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t})
in order to prove the convergence of SGD. Namely,
G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t})
needs to be an unbiased estimator of the true gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})
with controlled variance as we formally state in

Assumption 2
.

Given any parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, the random gradient sample
G(\theta,\zeta)
can be written as

G(\theta,\zeta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta),

(5)

where
\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)
is a centered noise i.e.
\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)|\theta\big]=0
whose distribution can be written as
\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta))=\delta\nu_{\theta,1}+(1-\delta)\nu_{\theta,2}
with
\delta>0
and
\nu_{\theta,1},\nu_{\theta,2}
two probability distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\nu_{\theta,1}
admits a density
h(\theta,\cdot)
w.r.t. Lebesgue’s measure satisfying
:

\inf_{\omega\in S}h(\theta,\omega)>0\quad\text{for all $\theta$ and compact $S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Moreover
,
there are positive constants
L_{\sigma}
and
\sigma^{2}
such that for all
\theta
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|^{2}\>|\>\theta\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|G(\theta,\zeta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2}\>|\>\theta\big]\leq L_{\sigma}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

(6)

The additional assumptions on the distribution of the noise
\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)
are needed in order to establish the ergodicity of the resulting Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(Theorem
1
below) by ensuring that the associated transition kernel satisfies a
minorization
property implying that the chain will sufficiently explore the state space, see
[
58
]
for more details. Note also that these requirements are fairly mild: they only require that the noise distribution admits a diffuse component.

3
Markov Chain and Geometric Ergodicity

Before stating the convergence result for the SGD Markov chain we introduce some further useful notation. For a given step-size
\beta>0,
we will denote
P_{\beta}
the Markov transition kernel governing the Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
generated by iteration (
4
), so that for any
t\geq 0
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have:

\mathbb{P}(\theta_{t+1}\in A\>|\>\theta_{t})=P_{\beta}(\theta_{t},A).

The transition kernel
P_{\beta}
acts on probability distributions
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through the mapping
\nu\to\nu P_{\beta}
which is defined, for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by
\nu P_{\beta}(A)=\int_{A}P_{\beta}(\theta,A)d\nu(\theta)=\mathbb{P}(\theta_{t+1}\in A\>|\>\theta_{t}\sim\nu).
For
n\geq 1,
we similarly define the multi-step transition kernel
P_{\beta}^{n}
which is such that
P_{\beta}^{n}(\theta_{t},A)=\mathbb{P}(\theta_{t+n}\in A\>|\>\theta_{t})
and acts on probability distributions
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through
\nu P_{\beta}^{n}=(\nu P_{\beta})P_{\beta}^{n-1}.
Finally, we define the total-variation norm of a signed measure
\nu
as

2\|\nu\|_{\tv}=\sup_{f:|f|\leq 1}\int f(\theta)\nu(d\theta)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\nu(A)-\inf_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\nu(A).

In particular, we recover the total-variation
distance
between two probability distributions
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
d_{\tv}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|_{\tv}.
We are now ready to state the geometric ergodicity result for the SGD Markov chain.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,
the Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by iteration (
4
) with step-size

\beta<\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(\mu L\vee L_{\sigma})}

converges geometrically to a unique invariant measure
\pi_{\beta}
. Namely, for any initial
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
there exist
\rho<1
and
M<+\infty
such that

\big\|\delta_{\theta_{0}}P^{n}-\pi_{\beta}\big\|_{\tv}\leq M\rho^{n}\big(1+\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big),

(7)

where
\delta_{\theta_{0}}
is the Dirac measure located at
\theta_{0}.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
B.1
and is based on
[
58
, Theorem 15.0.1]
and a
drift
condition in terms of a Lyapunov function. A similar method was previously used in
[
91
]
to establish the convergence of SGD for non-convex, non-smooth objectives with quadratic growth. However, the focus in
[
91
]
is on proving a central limit theorem for the Markov sequence
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and bounding the invariant distribution’s bias under certain regularity conditions. In contrast, we focus on proving concentration properties of the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
which allow us to obtain non-asymptotic deviation bounds on the estimation of the optimum
\theta^{\star}.

One of the main questions which arise after the statement of Theorem
1
is regarding the speed of the claimed convergence. It is clear that the contraction factor
\rho
and the constant
M
mainly depend on the initial state
\theta_{0}
and the step-size
\beta,
however, a precise quantification is lacking.

The issue is closely related to renewal theory and can be traced back to Kendall’s theorem
[
40
]
and more generally concerns Markov chains satisfying a drift property. A rich literature
[
78
,
74
,
52
,
77
,
83
,
75
,
20
,
4
,
59
,
37
,
5
]
. Near optimal results were obtained for stochastically ordered Markov Processes
[
52
,
75
,
53
,
24
]
. Other examples especially amenable to such analysis include reversible Markov chains
[
16
,
15
,
37
,
76
]
and chains satisfying special assumptions on their renewal distribution
[
6
,
51
,
41
]
. However, the SGD Markov chain does not satisfy such criteria. An estimation of
\rho
may be obtained using results based on renewal theory and Kendall’s theorem
[
40
,
4
,
5
]
.
However, the resulting estimations are notoriously pessimistic
[
70
]
. Indeed, let
\alpha:=1-\beta\mu
be the contraction factor in the absence of gradient noise (i.e. simple gradient descent) so that we have

\|\theta_{t}-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-\theta^{\star}\|\leq\alpha\|\theta_{t}-\theta^{\star}\|\quad\text{for all }\quad t\geq 0,

then the worst-case bound on
\rho
obtained thanks to
[
4
,
5
]
is such that
1-\rho\sim(\beta\mu)^{3}
which is far worse than the intuitive expectation that
\rho\approx\alpha.
It is unclear whether the previous estimation can be improved through a careful study of the renewal properties of the SGD Markov chain or if a different approach such as the study of the spectral properties of the transition kernel
P_{\beta}
is more appropriate. Nevertheless, we will see in Section
5
below that
\rho
can be estimated close to
\alpha
under additional conditions by leveraging Wasserstein convergence.

4
Invariant Distribution Properties

This section states that concentration properties of the random gradient samples used for SGD
transfer
to the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
towards which iteration (
4
) converges as stated in Theorem
1
. We begin with a basic statement which holds without additional assumptions and bounds the variance of
\pi_{\beta}
and its bias w.r.t. the true optimum
\theta^{\star}.

Proposition 1
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
,
let
\pi_{\beta}
be the invariant measure and
\bar{\theta}_{\beta}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}}[\theta]
be its expectation. For
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}
, the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
satisfies the following properties.

(a)

\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}}\big[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\big]=0.
In particular
,
if the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is linear
(
see Assumption
6
below
)
then we have
\bar{\theta}_{\beta}=\theta^{\star}
.

(b)

The variance and the bias are bounded as follows
:

\Var_{\pi_{\beta}}(\theta)\leq\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{2\mu-\beta(\mu^{2}+L_{\sigma})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\big\|\bar{\theta}_{\beta}-\theta^{\star}\big\|\leq\sqrt{\Var_{\pi_{\beta}}(\theta)}.

Proposition
1
is proven in Section
B.2
and relies on the invariance property of
\pi_{\beta}
and the contraction property of the optimisation iteration. The second part of this statement reflects the known-fact that the iterates have an asymptotic magnitude of
\sqrt{\beta}

[
68
,
63
]
.

Before stating further results, we need to define sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential concentration properties for real random variables. Among the many known equivalent characterizations, we only introduce those required for the proofs of our results, see
[
89
, Chapter 2]
for other characterizations.

Definition 1
.

Let
X
be a real random variable. We say that
X
is
K
-sub-Gaussian for some
K>0
whenever

(i)

we have

\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda^{2}X^{2})\leq e^{\lambda^{2}K^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq\lambda\leq 1/K,

(8)

which we will denote
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}(K),

(ii)

or we have

\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda X)\leq\exp(\lambda^{2}K^{2})\quad\text{ for all}\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

(9)

which we will denote
X\in\Psi_{2}(K)
.

Definition 2
.

Let
X
be a real random variable. We say that
X
is sub-exponential if one of the two following conditions holds.

(i)

There exists
K_{1}>0
such that

\|X\|_{L_{p}}\leq K_{1}p\quad\text{for all}\quad p\geq 1,

(10)

in which case we write
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}(K_{1}).

(ii)

There exists
K_{2}
such that

\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda X)\leq\exp(\lambda^{2}K_{2}^{2})\quad\text{ for all}\quad|\lambda|\leq 1/K_{2},

(11)

in which case we write
X\in\Psi_{1}(K_{2}).

Note that, for a centered variable
X,
the two characterizations of Definition
1
are equivalent with the same constant
K.

Analogously, for centered
X,
we have that
X\in\Psi_{1}(K)
entails
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}(2eK)
and
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}(K)
entails
X\in\Psi_{1}(2eK).

Namely, the two characterizations of Definition
2
imply each other but with worse constants (see
[
89
, Section 2]
for a proof). Since the constants in Definition
2
degrade by switching between the two properties, we will specify which property is meant in each subsequent statement in order to minimize these degradations.

We first formulate a sub-Gaussian/sub-exponential concentration assumption on the norms of the gradient errors.

Assumption 3
.

There exists
\overline{K}<+\infty
such that one of the following holds
:

(a)

For all
\theta\in\Theta,
the gradient error satisfies
\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}(\overline{K}).

(b)

For all
\theta\in\Theta,
the gradient error satisfies
\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}(\overline{K}).

In combination with Assumptions
1
and
2
, the above pair of assumptions imply the following concentration properties for
\pi_{\beta}.

Proposition 2
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
,
for
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta},
the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
satisfies the following properties
:

(a)

If Assumption
3

(a)
holds then
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}\big(\overline{K}\sqrt{8\beta/\mu}\big).

(b)

If Assumption
3

(b)
holds and if
\beta\leq(2\mu)^{-1}
then
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}\big(2\overline{K}\sqrt{\beta/\mu}\big).

The proof of Proposition
2
is given in Section
B.3
. The most important aspect of this statement is that the quantity
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|
is sub-Gaussian/sub-exponential with a constant
depending
on the step-size
\beta.
Indeed, it is fairly easy to show, for example, that
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}(\overline{K}/\mu)
under Assumption
3

(a)
, however, this constant is too pessimistic since it fails to take advantage of a small step-size which leads to stronger concentration. The improved constants above are obtained by carefully leveraging the unbiased property of the gradient error (see Assumption
2
). Previous characterizations of
\pi_{\beta}
obtained bounds on the bias w.r.t.
\theta^{\star}

[
91
]
and moment expansions of
\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{\star}
for
\widehat{\theta}\sim\pi_{\beta}
or
\widehat{\theta}
equal to a Polyak-Ruppert average
[
18
]
, however, the sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian characterizations of Proposition
2
appear to be new.

Note that the constant
\overline{K}
obtained from Assumption
3
and appearing in Proposition
2
may hide a dependence on the dimension in
\sqrt{d}
since it is related to the Euclidean norm
\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|
of the gradient noise. In this respect, Proposition
2
resembles the results of
[
56
]
where similar hypotheses to Assumption
3
were used entailing the same dimension dependence. In order to avoid this shortcoming, one needs a stronger assumption which is stated along with the associated results further below. Note also that Assumption
3
considerably strengthens Assumption
2
by requiring that
\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|
admits a finite exponential moment.
In addition, the involved bound is uniform w.r.t.
\theta.
However, under a non-uniform finite
p
-moment assumption, it is still possible to show the following.

Lemma 1
.

Grant Assumptions
1
and
2
and assume that there is
K,\underline{K}>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that conditionally on any
\theta
we have

\big\|\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)\|\big\|_{L_{p}}\leq K\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|+\underline{K},

(12)

then for step-size
\beta
as in Theorem
1
and satisfying the additional condition
\beta\leq\frac{\mu}{j(\mu^{2}+K^{2})}
with
j\leq p,
the Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges to an invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
with at least
j
finite moments.

Lemma
1
is proved in Section
B.4
and shows that
\pi_{\beta}
can have as many finite moments as the gradient, provided that the step-size is small enough. This implies that even weaker concentration properties transfer to the invariant distribution. Note that a non-uniform sub-exponential (resp. sub-Gaussian) assumption would correspond to condition (
12
) with
K
replaced by
Kp
(resp.
K\sqrt{p}
) in which case the condition on
\beta
becomes at least
\beta\leq\mathcal{O}\big(\mu/(jK^{2}p)\big)
. This suggests that, for arbitrary
p
,
\pi_{\beta}\in L_{p}
may only hold in the limit
\beta\to 0
. Lemma
1
shares a similarity with
[
2
, Theorem 2]
which states an
L_{p}
convergence result under a resembling condition on the step-size. However,
[
2
, Theorem 2]
uses a uniform
L_{p}
condition on the gradient error whereas we allow the upperbound to depend on
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|
in Inequality (
12
).

We now introduce a stronger analog to Assumption
3
, which will enable later the proof of dimension-free deviation bounds.

Assumption 4
.

There is
K<+\infty
such that one of the following holds
:

(a)

For all
\theta\in\Theta
and all
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
we have
f(G(\theta,\zeta))-\mathbb{E}f(G(\theta,\zeta))\in\Psi_{2}(K).

(b)

For all
\theta\in\Theta
and all
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
we have
f(G(\theta,\zeta))-\mathbb{E}f(G(\theta,\zeta))\in\Psi_{1}(K).

As announced, the subtle difference with Assumption
3
is that the involved constants are, a priori, independent from the dimension. The so-called Bobkov-Götze theorem
[
7
]
states that Assumption
4

(a)
is equivalent to the fact that
\nu_{\theta}:=\mathcal{D}(G(\theta,\zeta))
satisfies the following
Transportation-Information
inequality

\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\nu,\nu_{\theta}\big)\leq\sqrt{2K^{2}D(\nu\|\nu_{\theta})}\quad\text{ for all }\quad\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(13)

where
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
D(\cdot\|\cdot)
are the Wasserstein-
1
distance
[
90
]
(see definition below) and the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
44
]
between probability measures respectively. An analogous equivalence may be established for the sub-exponential case of Assumption
4

(b)
(for instance, by adapting the proof given in
[
86
, Theorem 4.8]
).

By restricting the functions
f
in Assumption
4
to be linear, we recover the assumption that the vector
G(\theta,\zeta)
is sub-Gaussian/sub-exponential. An interesting question is then whether this weaker property implies Assumption
4
with a dimension independent constant. To the best of our knowledge of the current literature, this is only known to hold for Gaussian vectors (see for instance
[
86
, Theorem 3.25]
). In fact, Talagrand’s well-known transport inequality states that Gaussian vectors satisfy Inequality (
13
) for the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance rather than
\mathcal{W}_{1}
, which is an even stronger property. Since Inequality (
13
) involves two very different forms of distance between probability measures, a direct intuitive understanding of its meaning is elusive. However, the above inequality is related to a host of properties used to describe the concentration of measure phenomenon including Poincaré inequalities
[
8
,
28
]
, logarithmic Sobolev inequalities
[
7
,
47
]
and modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities
[
25
,
3
]
to mention only a few. A broad and comprehensive survey on transport inequalities and their consequences on concentration and deviation inequalities is available in
[
29
]
.

Using the previous assumption, we can show that the invariant distribution inherits similar properties.

Proposition 3
.

Grant Assumptions
1
and
2
and let
\pi_{\beta}
be the invariant distribution obtained for step-size
\beta
as in Theorem
1
. For
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}
, the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
satisfies the following properties.

(a)

If Assumption
4

(a)
holds then
f(\theta)-\mathbb{E}f(\theta)\in\Psi_{2}\big(K\sqrt{\beta/\mu}\big)
for all
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(b)

If Assumption
4

(b)
holds then
f(\theta)-\mathbb{E}f(\theta)\in\Psi_{1}\big(K\sqrt{\beta/\mu}\big)
for all
f\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proposition
3
is proven in Section
B.5
and will be used in Section
6
to derive dimension-free deviation bounds. Note that the
\Psi_{1}
/
\Psi_{2}
constants in Proposition
3
also display the crucial
\sqrt{\beta/\mu}
dependence as in Proposition
2
and without further degradation. Before proceeding to the statement of such results we explore another convergence mode of the SGD Markov chain.

5
Wasserstein Convergence

This section complements Theorem
1
with an additional convergence result w.r.t. the Wasserstein metric. We recall that, for
p\geq 1
and two distributions
\varpi,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
the Wasserstein-
p
distance is defined by

\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\varpi,\nu)=\inf_{\xi\in\Pi(\varpi,\nu)}\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\sim\xi}\|X-Y\|^{p},

where
\Pi(\varpi,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\varpi
and
\nu
i.e. distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with first and second marginals equal to
\varpi
and
\nu
respectively.

In order to show that the SGD iteration converges w.r.t. the Wasserstein-
2
distance, we require the following assumption.

Assumption 5
.

There is
L_{\mathcal{W}}<+\infty
such that for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime},
the gradient noise distributions
\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta))
and
\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta^{\prime}))
at
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
satisfy

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\big(\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)),\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta^{\prime}))\big)\leq L_{\mathcal{W}}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}.

In words, we assume that the change in the gradient noise distribution measured with the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
metric is controlled by the change in the parameter
\theta.
This assumption is discussed below and allows to obtain the following result.

Proposition 4
.

Grant Assumptions
1
,
2
and
5
. Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two initial distributions and let
\beta
be a step-size such that

\beta<\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(L_{\mathcal{W}}\vee\mu L)},

then we have the contraction

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{1}P_{\beta},\nu_{2}P_{\beta})\leq\big((1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\mathcal{W}}\big)\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

Consequently
,
for such a
\beta
and any initial
\theta_{0}\sim\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),
the Markov chain generated by iteration (
4
) converges to a unique stationary measure
\pi_{\beta}
in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance.

The proof of Proposition
4
is given in Section
B.6
. The intuition behind it is that, if the Markov chain evolves according to a locally similar dynamic when started from different points then, for small enough step-size, the contraction phenomenon coming from the optimization will prevail so that trajectories associated to different initializations join even before convergence. A similar result was previously stated in
[
18
, Proposition 2]
for smooth and strongly convex functions also.
In
[
18
]
, Wasserstein convergence is shown under the assumption that every random gradient
G(\theta,\zeta)
be almost surely co-coercive with fixed constant. Nonetheless, the proof also works when this property holds only in expectation (see
[
18
, Assumption A7]
), which translates to the following inequality for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}:

L\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle\geq\mathbb{E}\big[\|G(\theta,\zeta)-G(\theta^{\prime},\zeta)\|^{2}\big].

(14)

For the sake of illustration, we consider the simple example of least-squares linear regression in which, given a sample
\zeta=(X,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, a random gradient is computed as

G(\theta,\zeta)=XX^{\top}\theta-XY\quad\text{with}\quad Y=X^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi,

where
\xi
is an independent centered noise. In this particular case, Inequality (
14
) can be verified as long as
X
has a bounded fourth moment. Regarding Assumption
5
, we have
\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)=G(\theta,\zeta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=(XX^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XX^{\top})(\theta-\theta^{\star})-X\xi
and it is easy to couple the distributions of
\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta^{\prime})
and
\varepsilon_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})
by defining them with the same variables
\zeta=\zeta^{\prime}=(X,\xi)
so that we find

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\big(\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)),\mathcal{D}(\varepsilon_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime}))\big)\leq\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)-\varepsilon_{\zeta^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|XX^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XX^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the operator norm. We then recover the bounded fourth moment condition on
X
.

More generally, we can consider an objective
\mathcal{L}
defined by a linear learning task such that
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}[\ell(X^{\top}\theta,Y)]
for a convex loss
\ell
and random samples and labels
(X,Y)
so that the gradient samples are
G(\theta,(X,Y))=X\ell^{\prime}(X^{\top}\theta,Y)
with
\ell^{\prime}
the derivative in the first argument. By a similar computation as above, it is possible to verify Assumption
5
as soon as
\ell
is smooth in its first argument and
X
has a finite fourth moment. On the other hand, it is unclear how to establish (
14
) in this setting which makes Assumption
5
more generic.

In the same vein as Assumption
5
, it is possible to introduce a regularity condition on the transition kernel
P_{\beta}
in terms of the
\tv
distance which allows to obtain the following result.

Proposition 5
.

Let the assumptions of Proposition
4
hold and further assume that
:

•

For all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the probability measure
P_{\beta}(\theta,\cdot)
admits a density
p_{\beta}(\theta,\omega)
w.r.t. Lebesgue’s measure.

•

There exists
A<\infty
such that for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\|P_{\beta}(\theta,\cdot)-P_{\beta}(\theta^{\prime},\cdot)\|_{\tv}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|p_{\beta}(\theta,\omega)-p_{\beta}(\theta^{\prime},\omega)|d\omega\leq A\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|.

(15)

Then the ergodicity result of Theorem
1
holds with
\rho\leq\sqrt{(1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\mathcal{W}}}.

Proof.

Using
[
55
, Theorem 12]
(see also
[
55
, Lemma 13]
), the assumptions imply that for all
\varpi,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have:

\|\varpi P_{\beta}-\nu P_{\beta}\|_{\tv}\leq A\mathcal{W}_{1}(\varpi,\nu).

It then only remains to use Proposition
4
with
\varpi=\delta_{\theta_{0}}P_{\beta}^{n-1}
and
\nu=\pi_{\beta}=\pi_{\beta}P_{\beta}
along with the inequality:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\varpi,\nu)\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\varpi,\nu)}

valid for all
\varpi,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

∎

Proposition
5
uses the “Wasserstein-to-TV” method
[
71
,
55
]
in order to derive convergence in
\tv
distance from Proposition
4
which leads to an explicit estimate of
\rho
in Theorem
1
. Note however that although the necessary condition (
15
) is quite intuitive, its verification is not straightforward even for a toy example.

6
Confidence bounds

Using the convergence and concentration results formulated in the previous sections for the invariant distribution of the SGD Markov chain, we are ready to state confidence bounds on the estimation of the optimal
\theta^{\star}.
Recall that by Proposition
1
, the invariant distribution
\pi_{\beta}
may not be centered around
\theta^{\star}
unless the gradient is linear, which is a particular case. In general, the expectation of
\pi_{\beta}
may not be equal to
\theta^{\star}
but the bias is controlled by the step-size
\beta.
Therefore, two possibilities are available for the final estimator:

•

The last iterate
\theta_{T}
: with
T
the optimization horizon. In which case a small step size is appropriate.

•

A tail average
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=n_{0}+1}^{n_{0}+n}\theta_{j}
: in which case the step size may be chosen reasonably large within the convergence conditions.

6.1
Final iterate concentration bounds

When the expectation of the invariant measure
\overline{\theta}_{\beta}
differs from the true optimum
\theta^{\star}
, one may choose a small step-size
\beta
in order to obtain a precise estimator of
\theta^{\star}
through the final iterate
\theta_{T}
of iteration (
4
). In the event where Assumption
3
holds, a quick application of Proposition
2
combined with Theorem
1
leads to the following first deviation bounds.

Corollary 1
.

In the setting of Proposition
2
,
for a step-size
\beta
and horizon
T,
we have the following high-confidence bounds
:

(a)

Under Assumption
3

(a)
,
for
\delta>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|\theta_{T}-\theta^{\star}\big\|>\overline{K}\sqrt{8\beta\log(e/\delta)/\mu}\Big)\leq\delta+\rho^{T}M\big(1+\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big).

(b)

Under Assumption
3

(b)
,
for
\delta>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|\theta_{T}-\theta^{\star}\big\|>4e\overline{K}\log(2/\delta){\sqrt{\beta/\mu}}\Big)\leq\delta+\rho^{T}M\big(1+\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big),

where
\rho
and
M
are the constants obtained from Theorem
1
.

The proof of Corollary
1
is given in Section
B.7
. As discussed earlier, the constants
\overline{K}
drawn from Assumption
3
may have a poor dependence on the dimension in
\sqrt{d}
which leaves room for improvement in the above bounds. This can be achieved when the requirements of Assumption
4
are met leading to the following

deviation bounds.

Corollary 2
.

In the setting of Proposition
3
,
for a step-size
\beta
such that
\beta\leq\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+L_{\sigma}},
we have the following high-confidence bounds
:

(a)

Under Assumption
4

(a)
,
for
\delta>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\theta_{n}-\theta^{\star}\big\|>\sqrt{\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{\mu}}+2K\sqrt{\frac{\beta\log(1/\delta)}{\mu}}\bigg)\leq\delta+\rho^{n}M\big(1+\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big).

(16)

(b)

Under Assumption
4

(b)
,
for
\delta>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\theta_{n}-\theta^{\star}\big\|>\sqrt{\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{\mu}}+2K\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\beta\log(1/\delta)}{\mu}}\>\>\vee\>\>\beta\log(1/\delta)\bigg)\bigg)\leq\delta+\rho^{n}M\big(1+\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big),

(17)

and

are the constants obtained from Theorem

.

and is a consequence of Propositions

,

. Inequality (

) is an example of a sub-Gaussian deviation bound since the uncertainty term has no dependence on the dimension

]
.

The bounds given in Corollaries
1
and

can be made more explicit by granting the conditions of Proposition
5
which yields
\rho\approx 1-\beta\mu.
In this case, it is possible to set
\beta=\frac{\log(T)}{\mu T}
leading to a nearly optimal statistical rate in
T
albeit with a sub-optimal dependence in the problem conditioning
[
1
,
2
]
.

6.2
Polyak-Ruppert averaging

In this part, we consider the case where the step-size
\beta
is chosen as a constant order value satisfying the convergence criteria required in our previous results. Our goal is to obtain a high-confidence bound for the Polyak-Ruppert average
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=n_{0}+1}^{n_{0}+n}\theta_{j}
computed after a burn-in period of
n_{0}
iterations. This raises two challenges, the first of which is that, even for a very long burn-in period
n_{0},
the stationary regime is never reached in theory so that one cannot immediately use the concentration properties of
\pi_{\beta}.
The second challenge comes from the lack of independence of the Markov chain iterates. This prevents the adoption of certain approaches such as the entropy method as done in
[
56
]
for example.

Notice that, unless the gradient is linear, there is little hope to estimate
\theta^{\star}
using the Polyak-Ruppert average since it is bound to approach
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}}[\theta]=\overline{\theta}_{\beta}
which may differ from
\theta^{\star}
by up to
\sigma\sqrt{\beta/\mu}
in the non linear case. Nevertheless, the following initial statement holds without this assumption.

Theorem 2
.

Grant Assumptions
1
,

2
,

4

(a)
and
5
. Let
f:\Theta^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
1
-Lipschitz function in each of its parameters and
\vec{\theta}:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
be a sequence of SGD iterates with step size
\beta<\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(\mu L\vee L_{\mathcal{W}})}
started from stationarity i.e. such that
\theta_{1}\sim\pi_{\beta}
.
Then we have

f(\vec{\theta}\>)-\mathbb{E}f(\vec{\theta})\in\Psi_{2}\big(KC_{\mathcal{W}}\sqrt{\beta/\mu+(n-1)\beta^{2}}\big),

where
C_{\mathcal{W}}=\big(1-\sqrt{(1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\mathcal{W}}}\big)^{-1}
.
If Assumption
4

(a)
is replaced by Assumption
4

(b)
then

f(\vec{\theta}\>)-\mathbb{E}f(\vec{\theta})\in\Psi_{1}\big(KC_{\mathcal{W}}\sqrt{\beta/\mu+(n-1)\beta^{2}}\big).

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
B.9
and employs a hybrid martingale transportation method (see
[
10
,
57
,
21
,
12
]
for a reference) leveraging the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
convergence established in Proposition
4
in combination with
[
42
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Theorem
2
may be used in a variety of ways by plugging different choices of the function
f.
For instance, one may choose
f(\vec{\theta})=\sum_{i}g(\theta_{i})
for any
g\in\Lip(\mathbb{R}^{d}).
Instead, in what follows, we focus on the choice

f(\vec{\theta})=\Big\|\sum_{i=n_{0}+1}^{n_{0}+n}\theta_{i}-n\theta^{\star}\Big\|.

Before we proceed, we formalize the gradient linearity assumption.

Assumption 6
.

The gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}
is linear i.e. for all
\theta\in\Theta
it is equal to
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\Sigma(\theta-\theta^{\star})
for some symmetric positive definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Note that the positive definiteness of
\Sigma
in Assumption
6
is a consequence of strong convexity while its symmetry is a result of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}
being constant in this case and therefore continuous.We are now ready to state our non-asymptotic deviation bound for Polyak-Ruppert averaging.

Proposition 6
.

Grant Assumptions
1
,

2
,

4

(a)
,

5
and
6
. Let
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov sequence obtained by running SGD with step-size

\beta<\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+(\mu L\vee L_{\mathcal{W}})}\wedge\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+L_{\sigma}}

and initial distribution
\theta_{0}\sim\nu.
Then there exist
\rho<1
and
M<\infty
such that

\begin{split}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=n_{0}+1}^{n_{0}+n}\theta_{t}-\theta^{\star}\Big\|&\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Big(\alpha_{\mathcal{W}}^{n_{0}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\pi_{\beta})+\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{\mu}\Big)}\\
&\quad+\frac{2K\sqrt{\beta/\mu}}{1-\alpha_{\mathcal{W}}}\sqrt{\beta\mu+\frac{1}{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\end{split}

(18)

for
\delta>0
and
n,n_{0}>0
with probability at least
1-\Upsilon(\nu,n_{0})\delta,
where

\displaystyle\alpha=1-\beta\mu,\quad\alpha_{\mathcal{W}}=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\mathcal{W}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Upsilon(\nu,n_{0})=1+M\rho^{n_{0}}\Big\|\frac{d\nu}{d\pi_{\beta}}\Big\|_{\infty}.

If Assumption
4

(a)
is replaced by Assumption
4

(b)
then

\begin{split}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=n_{0}+1}^{n_{0}+n}\theta_{t}-\theta^{\star}\Big\|&\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Big(\alpha_{\mathcal{W}}^{n_{0}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\pi_{\beta})+\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{\mu}\Big)}\\
&\quad+\frac{2K\sqrt{\beta/\mu}}{1-\alpha_{\mathcal{W}}}\bigg(\sqrt{\beta\mu+\frac{1}{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\vee\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}\bigg)\end{split}

(19)

holds with the same probability.

The proof of Proposition
6
is given in Section
B.10
and takes advantage of the convergence both in total-variation distance and in the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
metric. Note that the given bounds are also dimension-free thanks to Assumption
4
. It is possible to derive a weaker result using only Assumption
3
but we omit it to avoid repetition. The variance terms in the upperbounds of (

) and (

) (those independent of
\delta
) are controlled thanks to a geometric decorrelation phenomenon which can be shown for the Markov chain iterates under Assumption
6
(see Lemma
6
in the Appendix). This phenomenon becomes weaker for smaller step-size
\beta,
therefore, it only makes sense to apply Proposition

with

of constant order to avoid excessive correlation between the averaged samples. Finally, the lack of stationarity of the involved Markov samples is tackled by taking advantage of a spectral gap property satisfied by the transition kernel

under the conditions of Theorem

(see
[

]
).

Proposition

may be compared to the works of
[

]
and
[

]
. The former derives a similar high probability bound for linear stochastic approximation under a generalized sub-Gaussianity assumption and uncorrelated noise. The latter considers a weaker finite

7
Applications

We discuss the consequences of our results for two common use-cases of SGD.

7.1
Linear regression

Linear regression is one of the most popular and most used standard models. The aim is to predict a real variable
Y
based on a random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to the linear model

Y=X^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\epsilon

where
\theta^{\star}
is an unknown parameter and
\epsilon
a centered noise. The estimation of
\theta^{\star}
may be carried out by minimizing the least-squares objective
\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big(X^{\top}\theta-Y\big)^{2}
with respect to
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
This may be done by running SGD with the random gradient
G(\theta,(X,Y))=X(X^{\top}\theta-Y).

Provided the previous gradient admits a finite second moment, Theorem
1
and Proposition
4
apply and guarantee the convergence of the SGD Markov chain in total-variation and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance. If the covariates
X
and the noise
\epsilon
are both Gaussian then the gradient
G(\theta,(X,Y))
is sub-exponential. However, note that Assumption
3
or
(b)
are not immediately satisfied since the associated
\Psi_{1}
constant may be unbounded for arbitrarily high values of
\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|.
This problem can be remedied thanks to the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and assume that gradient errors write
\varepsilon_{\zeta_{t}}(\theta_{t})=\Xi_{t}(\theta_{t}-\theta^{\star})+\xi_{t}
where the pairs
(\Xi_{t},\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are i.i.d in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\Xi_{t}
symmetric and such that for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|u\|=1
we have
\langle u,\Xi_{t}u\rangle\in\Psi_{1}(K_{\Xi})
and
\langle u,\xi_{t}\rangle\in\Psi_{1}(K_{\xi})
for
K_{\Xi},K_{\xi}>0.
Assume the following minibatch SGD iteration is run starting from
\theta_{0}
such that
\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{\star}\|\leq C
for some
C>0
for a finite horizon
T

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\beta\overline{G}_{N}(\theta_{t})\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{G}_{N}(\theta_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{tN+i})

with
N
the minibatch size. For a confidence level
\delta>0,
assume that
N
and
\beta
satisfy

\frac{N}{\log(4T/\delta)\!+\!3d}\geq 1\vee\Big(\frac{6}{\mu}\big(3K_{\Xi}\vee 4K_{\xi}/C\big)\Big)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta\leq\frac{\mu N}{54K_{\Xi}^{2}(\log(4T/\delta)\!+\!3d)}\wedge\frac{2}{\mu\!+\!L}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta,
we have
\max_{0\leq s\leq T}\|\theta_{s}-\theta^{\star}\|\leq C
.

Lemma
2
is proven in Section
B.11
and guarantees that, using a small step size and minibatching to reduce the gradient variance, with high probability, the iteration does not stray from the vicinity of the optimum during a finite horizon. This shows that the uniform aspect of Assumptions
3
and
4
does not prevent the application of the results given in the previous sections. Note that although Lemma
2
requires that
N=\Omega(d),
the constant
C
is arbitrary and may be taken dimension-free, for instance, by starting the iteration from a preliminary estimator
\theta_{0}=\widehat{\theta}.

For the example of linear regression with sub-Gaussian samples
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t},
Lemma
2
applies with
\Xi_{t}=X_{t}X_{t}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\top}]
and
\xi_{t}=-\epsilon_{t}X_{t}.
Thus, for finite horizon, one may consider the event where the bound of Lemma
2
holds to apply results from Sections
4
and
6
.

Alternatively, it is also possible to restrict the optimization to a convex and bounded subset
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta.
By letting
\Pi_{\Theta}(\cdot)
be the projection onto
\Theta
and replacing iteration (
4
) with

\theta_{t+1}=\Pi_{\Theta}\big(\theta_{t}-\beta G(\theta_{t},\zeta_{t})\big),

(20)

we obtain a Markov chain to which Proposition
2

(b)
applies and leads to the deviation bound (
17
). Indeed, it is easy to verify that these results still hold for iteration (
20
) thanks to the inequality

\big\|\Pi_{\Theta}\big(\theta-\beta G(\theta,\zeta)\big)-\theta^{\star}\big\|\leq\big\|\theta-\beta G(\theta,\zeta)-\theta^{\star}\big\|,

valid for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
since
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
which is convex. However, by considering the projected iteration (
20
), Proposition
1

(a)
may no longer hold so that
\overline{\theta}_{\beta}\neq\theta^{\star}
making Proposition
6
no longer applicable.

7.2
Logistic regression

Logistic regression corresponds to the model

1-\mathbb{P}(Y=-1|X)=\mathbb{P}(Y=+1|X)=\sigma(X^{\top}\theta^{\star}),

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function
\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x}).
For a parameter
\theta
and a sample
X,
the predicted probability is
\mathbb{P}(Y=+1|X)=\sigma(X^{\top}\theta)
and the model is trained using the log-loss
\ell(\theta,(X,Y))=-\log(\sigma(YX^{\top}\theta))
which yields the objective
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}\ell(\theta,(X,Y)).

In order to ensure the objective is strongly-convex, it is necessary to restrict the parameter
\theta
to a bounded convex set
\Theta.
This is commonly done by setting
\Theta=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>,\>\|\theta\|\leq R\}
for some radius
R>0

[
33
,
1
,
62
]
.

In this case, the projected iteration (
20
) may be used. In this setting, one may easily check that the gradient is sub-Gaussian/sub-exponential as soon as the covariates
X
satisfy one or the other of these properties. Therefore, the results of Propositions
2
and
3
apply in this context as well.

8
Conclusion and Discussion

The Markov chain point of view for SGD is very useful since it allows to draw conclusions and establish a number of characterizations for the invariant limit distribution. Convergence of the SGD Markov chain holds under fairly weak conditions
[
58
]
. As evidenced by our results, this opens doors for a better characterization of the limit distribution when the associated optimization iteration progresses at
geometric
speed, for instance, when strong convexity holds.
The precise determination of the speed of convergence in distribution constitutes a particular difficulty which more generally concerns Markov chains with a geometric drift property. However, this difficulty may be circumvented for SGD by leveraging Wasserstein convergence provided a regularity condition on the noise distribution and transition kernel. Obtaining such properties from generic assumptions on the gradient distribution represents an interesting perspective.
Finally, despite being quite productive, the Markov chain study of SGD remains limited to the constant step-size setting. This excludes the combination of a decreasing step-size with averaging which is known for its better dependence on problem conditioning
[
1
,
2
]
.

Appendix A
Preliminary lemmas

Lemma 3
.

Let
X
be a real random variable such that
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}(K)
then, with probability at least
\delta,
we have

|X|\leq K\sqrt{\log(e/\delta)}.

Proof.

Using Chernoff’s method, we find for
t>0
and
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|X|>t\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\lambda^{2}X^{2}>\lambda^{2}t^{2}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\exp(\lambda^{2}X^{2})>\exp(\lambda^{2}t^{2})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda^{2}X^{2})e^{-\lambda^{2}t^{2}}\leq\exp\big(\lambda^{2}(K^{2}-t^{2})\big).

Choosing
\lambda\!=\!1/K,
we have
\exp\big(1\!-\!(t/K)^{2}\big)\!\leq\!\delta\!\!\iff\!\!t\!\geq\!K\sqrt{\log(e/\delta)}
and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
X
be a real random variable such that
X\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{1}(K)
then, with probability at least
\delta,
we have

|X|\leq 2eK\log(2/\delta).

Proof.

Using Stirling’s approximation, we find for
|\lambda|<(eK)^{-1}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda|X|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{p}\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}}{p!}\leq 1+\sum_{p\geq 1}\frac{(\lambda Kp)^{p}}{p!}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{p\geq 1}\frac{(\lambda eK)^{p}}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\lambda eK}{1-\lambda eK}\leq\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\lambda eK}{1-\lambda eK}\Big),

where we used the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
in the last step. For
t>0,
using Chernoff’s method and choosing
\lambda=(2eK)^{-1}
, we find:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|X|>t\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\lambda|X|>\lambda t\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\exp(\lambda|X|)>\exp(\lambda t)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda|X|)e^{-\lambda t}\leq\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}-\frac{t}{2eK}\Big).

It only remains to choose
t=2eK\log(2/\delta)
to obtain the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 5

Grant Assumption

and

Proof.

For

we have

\displaystyle\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta^{2}\mu L)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}-\beta(2-\beta(\mu+L))\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta^{2}\mu L)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}-\beta(2-\beta(\mu+L))\mu\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}

where we used the inequalities

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\mu+L)\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle-\mu L\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}

(22)

\displaystyle\mu\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle,

(23)

valid for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
. Equation (

[

, Theoerem 2.1.12]
(see also
[

, Lemma 3.11]
and (

[

, Theorem 2.1.9]
).

Appendix B
Proof of Geometric Ergodicity

In the remaining part of this document, we make the dependences on
\zeta
in the gradient samples and errors implicit and write
G(\theta)
and
\varepsilon(\theta)
instead of
G(\theta,\zeta)
and
\varepsilon_{\zeta}(\theta)
respectively.

We show the geometric ergodicity of the SGD Markov chain
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by relying on
[
58
, Theorem 15.0.1]
. We will show that the following function:

V(\theta):=1+\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2},

is a
drift
function for this Markov chain. We define the action of the transition kernel
P
on integrable functions
f
through

\displaystyle Pf(\theta)=\mathbb{E}f(\theta-\beta G(\theta)).

We also define the variation operator

\Delta f(\theta):=Pf(\theta)-f(\theta).

B.1
Proof of Theorem
1

First, we establish that the Markov chain is aperiodic. Indeed, by Assumption
2
, for all
\theta,
the gradient is distributed according to an everywhere positive density, therefore, for all
\theta\in S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
S
a set with non zero Lebesgue measure we have
P(\theta,S)>0.
This implies that the greatest possible period for the chain is
1
which makes it aperiodic.

We also show that the Markov chain is
\psi
-irreducible (see
[
58
, Chapter 4]
). For any initial
\theta_{0},
its successor reads:

\theta_{+1}=\theta_{0}-\beta(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})+\varepsilon(\theta_{0}))

Given Assumption
2
, the distribution of
\varepsilon(\theta_{0})
is minorized by
\delta\nu_{\theta_{0},1}
where
\nu_{\theta_{0},1}
is a probability distribution which admits an everywhere positive density
h(\theta_{0},\cdot).
Consequently, for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with non zero Lebesgue measure, we have the following minorization:

\mathbb{P}(\theta_{+1}\in A|\theta_{0})=P(\theta_{0},A)\geq\frac{\delta}{\beta^{d}}\int_{A}h\Big(\theta_{0},\frac{\theta-\theta_{0}}{\beta}-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\Big)d\theta>0.

It follows that the Markov chain is irreducible w.r.t. Lebesgue’s measure and is thus
\psi
-irreducible.

For fixed
\theta,
and step-size
\beta<\frac{2}{\mu+L},
using Lemma
5
we find:

\displaystyle P\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\beta G(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle+\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta\mu)^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta\mu)^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\big(L_{\sigma}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\big((1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\sigma}\big)\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}

The previous inequality yields a contraction for step-size satisfying
0<\beta<\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{2}+L_{\sigma}}
and, as a consequence we have:

PV(\theta)\leq\underbrace{\big((1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\sigma}\big)}_{=:\widetilde{\lambda}}V(\theta)+\underbrace{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}+\big(1-\big((1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\sigma}\big)\big)}_{=:\widetilde{b}}

We now define the set
\mathcal{C}=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>,\>V(\theta)\leq 2\widetilde{b}/(1-\widetilde{\lambda})\big\}
which satisfies:

\Delta V(\theta)\leq-\frac{1-\widetilde{\lambda}}{2}V(\theta)+\widetilde{b}\mathbf{1}_{\theta\in\mathcal{C}}.

For such
\mathcal{C},
let
\underline{h}(\theta)=\inf_{\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{C}}h\Big(\theta_{0},\frac{\theta-\theta_{0}}{\beta}-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})\Big)
and define the probability measure
\nu_{\mathcal{C}}
by

\nu_{\mathcal{C}}(A)=\frac{\int_{A\cap\mathcal{C}}\underline{h}(\theta)d\theta}{\int_{\mathcal{C}}\underline{h}(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}}\quad\text{ for all }\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

It follows that for all
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{C},
we have the following minorization property:

P(\theta_{0},A)\geq\xi\nu_{\mathcal{C}}(A)\quad\text{ for all }\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\xi=\delta\int_{\mathcal{C}}\underline{h}(\theta)d\theta>0.
In words, the set
\mathcal{C}
is a
small
set and, thanks to
[
58
, Proposition 5.5.3]
, also a
petite
set (see definitions in
[
58
, Chapter 5]
).

We have shown that the Markov chain
(\theta_{t})
satisfies condition (iii) of
[
58
, Theorem 15.0.1]
. By the latter result, it follows that it admits an invariant probability measure
\pi_{\beta}
and there exist
r>1
and
M<\infty
such that:

\sum_{t\geq 0}r^{t}\|P(\theta_{0},\cdot)-\pi_{\beta}\|_{V}\leq MV(\theta_{0}).

(24)

Taking
\rho=r^{-1}
allows to conclude the proof.

B.2
Proof of Proposition
1

To prove
(a)
, let
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}
and simply compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta]=\mathbb{E}\big[\theta-\beta G(\theta)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\big]=\mathbb{E}[\theta]-\beta\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)]

since we know that
\mathbb{E}[\theta]<\infty
(this follows from (
24
) in the proof of Theorem
1
), this implies the first part of the claim. If we further assume the gradient to be linear, we have in addition

\mathbb{E}\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\nabla\mathcal{L}\big(\mathbb{E}\theta\big)=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bar{\theta})=0,

and the conclusion follows since
\theta^{\star}
is the unique critical point.

To prove
(b)
, let
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}
which implies that
\theta-\beta G(\theta)\sim\pi_{\beta}
as well. We compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\beta G(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\big((1-\beta\mu)^{2}+\beta^{2}L_{\sigma}\big)\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}

which implies

\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}}{1-(1-\beta\mu)^{2}-\beta^{2}L_{\sigma}}=\frac{\beta\sigma^{2}}{2\mu-\beta(\mu^{2}+L_{\sigma})}.

Moreover, by Jensen and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, we have

\|\bar{\theta}_{\beta}-\theta^{\star}\|=\|\mathbb{E}\theta-\theta^{\star}\|\leq\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}}.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
2

Let us prove
(a)
, we again use the fact that
\theta
and
\theta-\beta G(\theta)
have the same distribution when
\theta\sim\pi_{\beta}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda^{2}\|\theta-\beta G(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}(\|\theta\!-\!\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!-\!\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\!-\!2\beta\langle\theta\!-\!\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!-\!\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\!+\!\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2})\big)

Since we assume that
\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{2}(\overline{K}),
it is easy to check that for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit norm,
\langle u,\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\in\Psi_{2}(\overline{K})
because
\varepsilon(\theta)
is centered. Therefore, conditioning on
\theta,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\exp

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}(-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle+\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}))|\theta\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(-(2\lambda)^{2}\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle|\theta\big]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(2(\beta\lambda)^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2})|\theta\big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(8\lambda^{4}\beta^{2}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\overline{K}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\exp(8\lambda^{4}\beta^{2}(1-\beta\mu)^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\overline{K}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}),

where the last line uses that
\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|\leq(1-\beta\mu)\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|
. The previous inequality holds for
|\lambda|\leq\big(\sqrt{2}\beta\overline{K}\big)^{-1}
. We now restrict
\lambda
so that
|\lambda|\leq\big(\overline{K}\sqrt{8\beta/\mu}\big)^{-1}
and use Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}(1-\beta\mu)^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}(1+8\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2})\big)\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}(1-\beta\mu)^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}(1+\beta\mu)\big)\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}(1-\beta\mu)(1-(\beta\mu)^{2})\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big)\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\mathbb{E}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}\big)\Big]^{(1-\beta\mu)(1-(\beta\mu)^{2})}\exp\big(\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}\big)

and we arrive to the conclusion that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda^{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{2}\overline{K}^{2}}{\beta\mu+(\beta\mu)^{2}-(\beta\mu)^{3}}\Big)=\exp\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta\overline{K}^{2}}{\mu+\beta\mu^{2}-\beta^{2}\mu^{3}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta\overline{K}^{2}}{\mu}\Big)

where in the end we use that
\beta\mu^{2}\geq\beta^{2}\mu^{3}
since
\beta\leq 1/\mu
.

The task of proving
(b)
is more delicate. By assumption, we know that for all
\theta
, the gradient error
\varepsilon(\theta)
satisfies

\big\|\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|\big\|_{L_{p}}\leq\overline{K}p\quad\text{for }\quad p\geq 1.

(25)

We denote
M_{p}^{p}=\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{p}
. For
p=2,
we have through a similar computation to that for proving
(b)

M_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\beta(2\overline{K})^{2}}{\mu(2-\beta\mu)}\leq\frac{\beta(2\overline{K})^{2}}{\mu},

which immediately entails
M_{1}\leq 2\overline{K}\sqrt{\beta/\mu}.
We will show by induction that

M_{p}\leq K_{\pi}p\quad\text{for all }\quad p\geq 1,

(26)

with
K_{\pi}=C\overline{K}\sqrt{\beta/\mu}
for some
C\geq 2.
For
p\geq 2,
we assume (
26
) holds up to
2p-2
and consider
M_{2p}
, we compute

\displaystyle M_{2p}^{2p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\theta^{\star}\|^{2p}=\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\beta G(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle+\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}\big)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2k}\big(\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\big)^{p-k}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta\mu)^{2p}M_{2p}^{2p}+p\mathbb{E}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2p-2}(\beta\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\binom{p}{k}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2k}\big(\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\big)^{p-k},

where we isolated the two last terms of the sum in the last step and used that
\varepsilon(\theta)
is centered conditionally on
\theta.
Further, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\binom{p}{k}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{2k}\big(\beta^{2}\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|^{2}-2\beta\langle\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star},\varepsilon(\theta)\rangle\big)^{p-k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\!\binom{p}{k}\|\theta\!-\!\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!-\!\theta^{\star}\|^{2k}\sum_{j=0}^{p-k}\!\binom{p\!-\!k}{j}(\beta\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|)^{2j}(2\beta\|\theta\!-\!\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!-\!\theta^{\star}\|\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|)^{p-k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\sum_{j=0}^{p-k}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{j}\|\theta-\beta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\theta^{\star}\|^{p+k-j}(\beta\|\varepsilon(\theta)\|)^{p-k+j}2^{p-k-j}.

Now consider an index
l=p-k+j
, note that
2p-l=p+k-j
and we have
2\leq l\leq 2p.
We compute the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\sum_{j=0}^{p-k}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{j}2^{p-k-j}
for a fixed value of
l:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0,j=0\\
p-k+j=l\end{subarray}}^{k=p-2,j=p-k}

\displaystyle\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{j}2^{p-k-j}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{l-(p-k)}2^{2(p-k)-l}\mathbf{1}_{p-k\leq l\leq 2(p-k)}

(27)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\sum_{k=0\vee(p-l)}^{(p-2)\wedge(p-\lceil l/2\rceil)}\!\!\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{l\!-\!(p-k)}2^{2(p-k)-l}=\!\!\!\sum_{k=0}^{(\lfloor l/2\rfloor)\wedge(l-2)}\!\!\binom{p}{k,k\!+\!p\!-\!l,l\!-\!2k}2^{l-2k}

where
\binom{p}{k,k+p-l,l-2k}=\frac{p!}{k!(k+p-l)!(l-2k)!}
is the
trinomial
coefficient. Similarly we find that:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0,j=0\\
p-k+j=l\end{subarray}}^{k=p,j=p-k}\binom{p}{k}\binom{p-k}{j}2^{p-k-j}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor l/2\rfloor}\binom{p}{k,k\!+\!p\!-\!l,l\!-\!2k}2^{l-2k}.

In what follows, we set the convention that
\binom{p}{k,k+p-l,l-2k}=0
whenever
k\wedge(k+p-l)\wedge(l-2k)<0
which allows us to sum over all integer values without specifying the limits. For some variable
x,
we multiply by
x^{l},
sum over
l
and perform the change of variable
l\to l+2k
to find

Lemma 7
.

Let

be a matrix with positive entries such that there exists

and

such that

then we have

Proof.

Straightforward computations yield

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ii}+2\sum_{i<j}A_{ij}\leq nC+2\sum_{i<j}A_{ij},

uses the high probability bounds stated above and the conditions on
N
and
\beta
.

Using a union bound argument, we obtain (
37
) for
t+1
with probability at least
1-(t+1)\delta/T.
The induction argument is completed and implies the result for
t=T.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\Xi_{1},\dots,\Xi_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be random matrices and
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
random vectors as in Lemma
2
. Then denoting
\overline{\Xi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Xi_{i}
and
\overline{\xi}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i},
we have the high probability bounds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\overline{\Xi}\|_{2}>3K_{\Xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)+3d}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta,

(38)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\overline{\xi}\|>4K_{\xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)+2d}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta,

(39)

where
\phi(x)=\max(x,\sqrt{x}).

Proof.

We first prove (
38
). Denote
S^{d-1}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>\|u\|=1\}
and let
u\in S^{d-1}
and
|\lambda|\leq N/K_{\Xi},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda\langle u,\overline{\Xi}u\rangle)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\exp(\lambda\langle u,\Xi_{i}u\rangle/N)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{N}\exp(\lambda^{2}K_{\Xi}^{2}/N^{2})=\exp(\lambda^{2}K_{\Xi}^{2}/N),

so that for all
u\in S^{d-1}
we have
\langle u,\overline{\Xi}u\rangle\in\Psi_{1}(K_{\Xi}/\sqrt{N}).

Let
\Omega_{\epsilon}
be an
\epsilon
-net of
S^{d-1}.
By
[
88
, Lemma 5.2]
, there exists an
\epsilon
-net such that
|\Omega_{\epsilon}|\leq(1+2/\epsilon)^{d}
and for all
u\in S^{d-1}
there exists
v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
such that
\|u-v\|\leq\epsilon.
We write

\langle u,\overline{\Xi}u\rangle=\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle+2\langle u-v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle+\langle u-v,\overline{\Xi}(u-v)\rangle,

which allows us to deduce that

\|\overline{\Xi}\|_{2}=\sup_{u\in S^{d-1}}|\langle u,\overline{\Xi}u\rangle|\leq\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|+(2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2})\|\overline{\Xi}\|_{2}\implies\|\overline{\Xi}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|}{1-2\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}.

Let
v\in\Omega_{\epsilon},
using Chernoff’s method and the sub-exponential property of
\overline{\Xi}
(see also the proof of Corollary
2
), we find for
t>0:

\mathbb{P}(|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|>t)\leq\begin{cases}2\exp(-Nt^{2}/(4K_{\Xi}^{2}))&\text{ if }\quad t\leq 2K_{\Xi}\\
2\exp(-Nt/(2K_{\Xi}))&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Reformulating in terms of a failure probability
\delta,
we find that

\mathbb{P}\Big(|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|>2K_{\Xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta.

Replacing
\delta
with
\delta/(1+2/\epsilon)^{d}
and using a union bound argument over
\Omega_{\epsilon}
we find

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|>2K_{\Xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)+d\log(1+2/\epsilon)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta.

It only remains to set
\epsilon=1/8
and plug back into the inequality
\|\overline{\Xi}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\Xi}v\rangle|}{1-2\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}
in order to obtain (
38
).

To prove (
39
), we proceed similarly and first obtain for all
u\in S^{d-1}
and
|\lambda|\leq N/K_{\xi}:

\mathbb{E}\exp(\langle u,\overline{\xi}\rangle)\leq\exp(\lambda^{2}K_{\xi}^{2}/N).

For
u\in S^{d-1}
and
v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}
such that
\|u-v\|\leq\epsilon,
we write
\langle u,\overline{\xi}\rangle=\langle v,\overline{\xi}\rangle+\langle u-v,\overline{\xi}\rangle
which yields the inequality

\|\overline{\xi}\|\leq\frac{\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\xi}\rangle|}{1-\epsilon}.

As before, the sub-exponential property of
\overline{\xi}
yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(|\langle v,\overline{\xi}\rangle|>2K_{\xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta,

and using another union bound argument over
\Omega_{\epsilon}
we find

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{v\in\Omega_{\epsilon}}|\langle v,\overline{\xi}\rangle|>2K_{\xi}\phi\Big(\frac{\log(2/\delta)+d\log(1+2/\epsilon)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq\delta.

It only remains to set
\epsilon=1/2
to finish the proof of (
39
).
∎
