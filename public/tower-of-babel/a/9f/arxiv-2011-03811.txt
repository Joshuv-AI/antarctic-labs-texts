Geometric characterizations of the cross ratio in a pencil of conics
Alexander Thomas

Abstract
. We give geometric ways to determine the cross ratio of conics in a pencil using elementary projective geometry.



Introduction

In projective geometry the fundamental invariant is the cross ratio. On a line, the action of projective transformations is 3-transitive, i.e. a triple of points can be mapped to any other triple of points via a projective transform. For four points, the cross ratio is an invariant.

On the plane, the cross ratio is defined for four points on a common line, and by duality for four lines going through a common point. Collinear points and concurrent lines are examples of pencils. So the question raises
whether there is a notion of cross ratio in a pencil of conics
.

Abstractly, there is the following answer: denote by
Q(\mathbb{C}^{2})
the space of quadratic forms on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, which can be considered as the space of conics. A pencil of conics is precisely a line in the projective space
\mathbb{P}(Q(\mathbb{C}^{2}))
, see
[
Be87
]
, chapter 14. So there is a notion of cross ratio in a pencil of conics.

The question we address here is how to determine
geometrically
the cross ratio of four conics in a pencil.
After summarizing basic facts from elementary projective geometry in section
1
, we give six geometric characterizations for the cross ratio of conics in section
2
.
Notice that we work in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, but our pictures are drawn in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We work only with non-degenerate pencils of conics, the other cases can be easily obtained as limit cases.

References.

[

1.
Basic Facts on the cross ratio

We present here some facts on cross ratios in plane projective geometry which we will use in the sequel. For less known results we include proofs. Nothing in this section is new.

1.1.
First properties

The cross ratio is denoted by
(A,B,C,D)
. We use here the convention

(A,B,C,D)=\frac{(A-C)(B-D)}{(B-C)(A-D)}.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(a,b,c,d)
be four lines in a pencil (see Figure
1
). Any line not in the pencil intersects them in points
A,B,C,D
respectively. Then

(a,b,c,d)=(A,B,C,D).

Figure 1.
Cross ratio of four lines

Remark
.

This proposition gives the fundamental invariance of the cross ratio under elementary projective transformations.
The cross ratio of four concurrent lines can be expressed using the angles between the lines. Using the notation
\sphericalangle(a,b)
for the angle between lines
a
and
b
, we have

(a,b,c,d)=\frac{\sin\sphericalangle(a,c)\;\;\sin\sphericalangle(b,d)}{\sin\sphericalangle(b,c)\;\;\sin\sphericalangle(a,d)}.

Conics have the following fundamental property concerning the cross ratio:

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a conic and
A,B,C,D
be four points on it (see Figure
2
). Then for any point
P
on
\mathcal{C}
, the cross ratio
(PA,PB,PC,PD)
is independent of
P
.

Figure 2.
Cross ratio on a conic

We denote this cross ratio of four points on a conic by
(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}
. The previous proposition can actually be seen as a definition of a conic from five given points
A,B,C,D
and
P
: the set of points
Q
such that
(QA,QB,QC,QD)=(PA,PB,PC,PD)
.

Figure 3.
Angle equalities on the circle

Proof.
Among the multiple equivalent definitions of a conic, take the following one: a conic is the image of a circle under a projective transformation.

For a circle, we know that the cross ratio
(PA,PB,PC,PD)
is independent of
P
since all the angles between the four lines
PA,PB,PC
and
PD
are independent of
P
(angle property of the circle, see Figure
3
). Thus, since the proposition is only in terms of projective quantities, it remains true for a general conic by applying a projective transformation.

∎

The converse direction is also true, which explains the ubiquitous appearance of conics in geometry:

Proposition 3
.

Let
\alpha
be a projective transformation between two pencils of lines with base points
P
and
Q
respectively. Then the intersection of a line
d
through
P
with
\alpha(d)
describes a conic (when
d
varies) passing through
P
and
Q
.

Figure 4.
Chasles-Steiner theorem

The proof is simply the fact that a projective transformation between two pencils of lines is uniquely determined by the image of three lines. This gives three intersection points, and together with
P
and
Q
this gives a unique conic, so we can conclude with the previous Proposition
2
.

Both Proposition
2
and its converse
3
are known as the theorem of Chasles-Steiner (see Theorem 5.1 and 5.2 in
[
Si00
]
).

1.2.
Polars

For a point
P
in the plane and a given conic
\mathcal{C}
, we define the
polar
of
P
with respect to
\mathcal{C}
to be the line defined by all points
Q
such that denoting by
X
and
Y
the intersection of
PQ
with
\mathcal{C}
we have
(P,Q,X,Y)=-1
(see Figure
5
). The point
P
is called the
pole
of its polar.

Figure 5.
Definition of the polar

Geometrically, there is the following simple construction of the polar: take any two lines through
P
, each intersecting the conic in two points. Denote these points by
A,B,C,D
, see Figure
6
. Let
R
be the intersection of
AC
and
BD
and
S
be the intersection of
AD
and
BC
. Then the polar is given by
RS
. In particular if
P
is outside the conic, then the polar is given by the line through the two tangent points.

Figure 6.
Construction of the polar

There is a duality between poles and polars in the following sense: a point
B
lies on the polar of
A
iff
A
lies on the polar of
B
. This comes directly from the definition.

The duality between poles and polars is projective:

Proposition 4
.

Given four points
A,B,C,D
on a line and a conic
\mathcal{C}
, then the four polars
p_{A},p_{B},p_{C},p_{D}
are concurrent and
(A,B,C,D)=(p_{A},p_{B},p_{C},p_{D})
.

Figure 7.
Pole-polar duality

Figure 8.
Pole-polar duality is projective

Proof.

Denote by
d
the line of the four points
A,B,C,D
and by
P
the pole of
d
(see Figure
8
). All polars
p_{A},p_{B},p_{C}
and
p_{D}
go through
P
, so they are concurrent.

Denote by
R
and
S
the intersection of
d
with the conic
\mathcal{C}
(there is always such an intersection if we work in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
) and by
A^{\prime}
the intersection of
p_{A}
and
d
. By definition we have
(A,A^{\prime},R,S)=-1
, so the map
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
is projective (it is the involution on
d
with fixed points
R
and
S
).
Finally, the map
A^{\prime}\mapsto PA^{\prime}
is projective (by proposition
1
). Since
PA^{\prime}
is nothing but
p_{A}
, we see by composition that
A\mapsto p_{A}
is projective.

∎

The dual viewpoint gives the notion of a cross ratio of four tangent lines to a given conic
\mathcal{C}
(dual concept of
(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}
). Both notions are linked as follows:

Proposition 5
.

Consider four points
A,B,C,D
on a conic
\mathcal{C}
and denote by
t_{A},t_{B},t_{C},t_{D}
the four tangents to
\mathcal{C}
at these points. Then

(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}=(t_{A},t_{B},t_{C},t_{D})_{\mathcal{C}}.

Figure 9.
Tangents to a conic

Proof.

Take a fifth point
P
on
\mathcal{C}
, see Figure
9
. Denote by
A^{\prime}
the intersection of
t_{A}
with
t_{P}
and analogously for
B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
. By definition we have
(t_{A},t_{B},t_{C},t_{D})_{\mathcal{C}}=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})
. Thus we get

\displaystyle(t_{A},t_{B},t_{C},t_{D})_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})

by definition

\displaystyle=(PA,PB,PC,PD)

by Proposition
4

\displaystyle=(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}

by definition

∎

Using Proposition
1
, the cross ratio on a conic can be projected onto a line:

Proposition 6
.

Let
A,B,C,D
and
P
be points on a common conic
\mathcal{C}
and
d
be a line (see Figure
10
). The intersection of
PA
with
d
is denoted by
A^{\prime}
and analogously are defined
B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
. Then

(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime}).

Figure 10.
Conic to line

Every point in the plane determines an involution on a conic:

Proposition 7
.

Let
P
be a point in the plane. For a point
A
on a conic
\mathcal{C}
, denote the second intersection of
PA
with
\mathcal{C}
by
A^{\prime}
(see Figure
11
). Then the map
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
is projective, i.e. given four points
A,B,C,D
on
\mathcal{C}
, we have

(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})_{\mathcal{C}}.

Figure 11.
Conic to conic

Notice that the map
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
is involutive. A theorem due to Frégier asserts that any involution of a conic is induced by some point
P
in the plane (see Theorem 5.4 in
[
Si00
]
).

Proof.

We only indicate the idea of the proof: consider the involution of the plane with center
P
, axis
p_{P}
(the polar of
P
with respect to
\mathcal{C}
) and which takes
A
to
A^{\prime}
. The involution is uniquely determined by this. One easily shows that this involution preserves the conic
\mathcal{C}
and induces the involution described in the proposition.
∎

1.3.
Pencil of conics

Finally we need some preliminaries on pencils of conics.
Given two conics defined by equations
E_{1}=0
and
E_{2}=0
(quadratic polynomials in two variables), the pencil
\mathcal{F}
generated by them are all conics with equation
\lambda E_{1}+\mu E_{2}=0
.
Geometrically two conics intersect generically in four points, called the
base points
of the pencil. A conic belongs to their pencil
\mathcal{F}
iff it goes through the four base points.
Every pencil of conics contains three degenerate conics, consisting of pairs of lines through the base points. The double points of these degenerate conics are called
double points
of the pencil. In Figure
12
you see a pencil of conics with base points
A,B,C,D
and double points
R,S
and
T
.

Figure 12.
Pencil of conics

A pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
induces a projective involution on every straight line, due to a famous theorem of Desargues:

Proposition 8

(third theorem of Desargues)
.

Let
d
be a line not passing through any base point of
\mathcal{F}
. For
P\in d
there is a unique conic
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}
going through
P
. Denote by
P^{\prime}
the second intersection of
\mathcal{C}
with
d
. The map
P\mapsto P^{\prime}
is a projective involution on
d
.

This induced map on
d
is called
Desargues involution
. The proof can be found in
[
Si00
]
, Theorem 6.1.

Figure 13.
Desargues involution

Further, any pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
induces a transformation of the whole plane:

Proposition 9
.

Let
P
be a point in the plane, not a double point of the pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
. The set of all polars of
P
with respect to conics in
\mathcal{F}
are concurrent to a single point
P^{\prime}
.

We call
P^{\prime}
the
conjugated point
of
P
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
.

Figure 14.
Conjugated point

Proof.

Denote by
P^{\prime}
the intersection of the polars of
P
with respect to two conics
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
of the pencil
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
M_{i}
and
N_{i}
the intersection points of
PP^{\prime}
with
\Gamma_{i}
. The Desargues involution exchanges
M_{i}
and
N_{i}
and is uniquely determined by this. Furthermore, since
(P,P^{\prime},M_{i},N_{i})=-1
(by definition of the polar), we see that the fixed points of the Desargues involution are
P
and
P^{\prime}
(this means that the conic in
\mathcal{F}
passing through
P
is tangent to
PP^{\prime}
).
For any conic
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}
, denote by
M,N
the intersection of
PP^{\prime}
with
\Gamma
. Then
(P,P^{\prime},M,N)=-1
since
M
and
N
are exchanged by the Desargues involution. Therefore the polar of
P
with respect to
\Gamma
passes through
P^{\prime}
.
∎

Figure 15.
Proof of Proposition
9

Remark
.

There is also a simple algebraic proof: the equation of the polar of
P
with respect to a conic given by an equation which is a linear combination of polynomials
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
is itself a linear combination of the equations of the polars with respect to
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
. Thus, the set of all polars forms a pencil.
See
[
Be87
]
, 16.5.3, for more details.

The conjugation with respect to a pencil of conics is not projective. It is a
quadratic birational
transformation. The image of a line is given by the following proposition:

Proposition 10
.

The point-wise image of a straight line
d
under conjugation with respect to a pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
is a conic which passes through the double points of
\mathcal{F}
.
The same result is obtained by taking the set of all poles of
d
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
.

Figure 16.
Conjugated line gives a conic (two examples)

Proof.

Let
P
be a point on
d
. Denote by
R,S,T
the double points of the pencil of conics. The line
RP^{\prime}
is the polar of
P
with respect to the degenerate conic defining
R
. Hence by Proposition
4
the map
P\mapsto RP^{\prime}
is projective. In the same vain, the map
P\mapsto SP^{\prime}
is projective. Thus the map
RP^{\prime}\mapsto SP^{\prime}
is projective.

By the theorem of Chasles-Steiner, Proposition
4
, the image of
P^{\prime}
is a conic passing through
R
and
S
. Repeating the argument with
S
and
T
gives that this conic also passes through
T
.

To prove that this conic is the set of all polars of
d
with respect to the pencil
\mathcal{F}
, take again a point
P
on
d
. We show that there is a conic in
\mathcal{F}
such that the pole of
d
is
P^{\prime}
. Since
P^{\prime\prime}=P
all polars of
P^{\prime}
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
pass through
P
. We will see in Theorem
1
that the map
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}\mapsto p_{P^{\prime}}
is projective, so in particular surjective. Hence there is a conic whose polar is
d
which concludes the proof.
∎

Even if the conjugation with respect to a pencil of conics is not projective, it does preserve the cross ratio in some sense:

Proposition 11
.

Let
A,B,C,D
be four points on a common line
d
and
\mathcal{F}
be a pencil of conics. Denote by
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime}
the conjugated points and by
\mathcal{C}
the conic which is the image of
d
under conjugation. Then

(A,B,C,D)=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})_{\mathcal{C}}.

Figure 17.
Cross ratio preservation under conjugation (figure not realistic)

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be a conic in
\mathcal{F}
such that the pole of
d
with respect to
\Gamma
is a point
P^{\prime}
different from
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
(see Figure
17
). The polar of
A
with respect to
\Gamma
is then
P^{\prime}A^{\prime}
(every polar to a conic in
\mathcal{F}
passes through
A^{\prime}
) and the same holds true for the other points
B,C,D
.

Thus

\displaystyle(A,B,C,D)

\displaystyle=(P^{\prime}A^{\prime},P^{\prime}B^{\prime},P^{\prime}C^{\prime},P^{\prime}D^{\prime})

by Proposition
4

\displaystyle=(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime})_{\mathcal{C}}.

∎

2.
Cross ratio in a pencil of conics

In this section, we explore geometric ways to find the cross ratio of four conics in a common pencil.
Apart from the theorems
1
and
3
, all the others seem to be new.

For the whole section we fix a pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
with base points
A,B,C,D
, double points
R,S,T
and we consider four conics
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3}
and
\Gamma_{4}
in
\mathcal{F}
.

Probably the first thing one might try to do to determine the cross ratio of the four conics
\Gamma_{i}
is the following:

Theorem 1
.

Let
P
be a point in the plane, different from
R,S,T
, and denote by
p_{i}
the polar of
P
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
(
i=1,2,3,4
). Then

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}).

Notice that from Proposition
9
we know that the polars
p_{i}
are concurrent in
P^{\prime}
. This theorem is well-known, see for instance
[
Be87
]
16.5.3.3, or
[
Mi26
]
on page 41.

Figure 18.
Theorem
1
and its proof (figure not realistic)

Proof.

We prove that
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4})
is independent of
P
. The fact that it coincides with the double ratio of the four conics will be done using theorem
4
.

So take two points
P
and
Q
. Denote by
d
the line defined by these two points and by
\mathcal{C}
the conjugation of
d
with respect to the pencil
\mathcal{F}
(see Proposition
10
).
The polar of
P
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
intersects
\mathcal{C}
in
P^{\prime}
and in a point denoted by
P_{i}
.
The polar of
Q
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
is then given by
Q^{\prime}P_{i}
, since
P_{i}
is the pole of
d
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
.
Finally we get

(P^{\prime}P_{1},P^{\prime}P_{2},P^{\prime}P_{3},P^{\prime}P_{4})=(Q^{\prime}P_{1},Q^{\prime}P_{2},Q^{\prime}P_{3},Q^{\prime}P_{4})

by Proposition
2
.
∎

When
P
is one of the four base points of
\mathcal{F}
, we get:

Corollary 1
.

The cross ratio of four conics in a pencil is given by the cross ratio of the four tangents at a base point.

We can also determine the cross ratio of four conics using a line:

Theorem 2
.

Let
d
be a straight line. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
the conic which is the image of
d
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
. Denote further by
P_{i}
the pole of
d
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
. Then

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4})_{\mathcal{C}}.

Figure 19.
Proof of theorem
2
(figure not realistic)

Proof.

Let
Q
be on
d
such that its conjugated point
Q^{\prime}
is different from the
P_{i}
. The polar of
Q
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
is then given by
Q^{\prime}P_{i}
. Hence:

\displaystyle(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4})_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=(Q^{\prime}P_{1},Q^{\prime}P_{2},Q^{\prime}P_{3},Q^{\prime}P_{4})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})

by Theorem
1
.

∎

When
d
is the line at infinity, we get:

Corollary 2
.

The cross ratio of four conics in a pencil is the cross ratio of the four centers, taken on the conic of centers.

The geometric simplest way is probably the following:

Theorem 3
.

Consider a line
d
passing through one of the base points of the pencil
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
M_{i}
the second intersection of
d
with
\Gamma_{i}
. Then

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(M_{1},M_{2},M_{3},M_{4}).

We have not found this theorem in the literature, but its dual version is given without proof in
[

]
, chapter VIII, 3.29. So we believe that Theorem

is known.

The next characterization uses the following simple fact: a conic is uniquely determined by four points
A,B,C,D
and a complex number
k
. It is the conic
\mathcal{C}
passing through the four points such that
(A,B,C,D)_{\mathcal{C}}=k
.

Theorem 4
.

Denote by
k_{i}
the complex number which uniquely determines a conic
\Gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
as above. Then

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}).

Figure 23.
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

Let
d
be a line passing through
A
. Denote by
P_{i}
the intersection of
d
with
\Gamma_{i}
, by
Q_{i}
the intersection of
BP_{i}
with
CD
and by
E
the intersection of
d
with
CD
, see Figure
23
.

Hence we have
k_{i}=(P_{i}A,P_{i}B,P_{i}C,P_{i}D)=(E,Q_{i},C,D).

So we have projected everything on the line
CD
. By a projective transformation, we can get
D\mapsto 0
,
E\mapsto 1
and
C\mapsto\infty
. Denote the coordinate by
Q_{i}
after this transformation by
q_{i}
. We then have by definition of the cross ratio

k_{i}=(E,Q_{i},C,D)=(1,q_{i},\infty,0)=q_{i}.

Hence we get
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=(Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},Q_{4}).
By Proposition
1
we have
(Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},Q_{4})=(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4})
.
Therefore using Theorem
3
we conclude:

(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},P_{4})=(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4}).\qed

Remark
.

The following general statement is true: Given a pencil generated by two objects
P
and
Q
, then any other object in the pencil is of the form
tP+(1-t)Q
. For four objects
O_{i}
in the pencil, with parameters
t_{i}
, we have

(O_{1},O_{2},O_{3},O_{4})=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}).

This remark directly implies that the abstract definition of the cross ratio
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})
(as points in a line in the space of quadratic forms, see the Introduction) coincides with our geometric characterizations.

The cross ratio of four conics can be determined using another conic:

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a conic passing through two base points of the pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}
. The intersection points of
\mathcal{C}
with
\Gamma_{i}
, other then the two base points, are denoted by
M_{i}
and
N_{i}
. Then the lines
M_{i}N_{i}
are concurrent and

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(M_{1}N_{1},M_{2}N_{2},M_{3}N_{3},M_{4}N_{4}).

Figure 24.
Cross ratio using a conic

Proof.

First, let us prove that the lines
M_{i}N_{i}
all pass through a common point, which in addition lies on
CD
(we assume that
\mathcal{C}
passes through the base points
A
and
B
). This is a generalization of lemma
1
and we can adopt its proof.

Consider the pencil of conics
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
defined by the four base points
A,B,M_{1},N_{1}
. On the line
CD
this induces the Desargues involution
i
which exchanges
C
and
D
and the two intersection points of
\mathcal{C}
with
CD
. Thus
M_{1}N_{1}
passes through the image under
i
of
AB\cap CD
. This description is independent of
M_{1}N_{1}
.

Now we can show the second part:
Let
X^{\prime}
be the conjugated point to
X
with respect to
\mathcal{F}
(which also lies on
CD
),
p
be the polar of
X
with respect to
\mathcal{C}
and
Q_{i}
be the intersection of
p
and
XM_{i}
(see Figure
25
).

Figure 25.
Proof of Theorem
5

According to the definition of the polar
p
, we have

(X,Q_{i},N_{i},M_{i})=-1.

Thus, the point
Q_{i}
is also on the polar of
X
with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
. Hence this polar is given by
X^{\prime}Q_{i}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(M_{1}N_{1},M_{2}N_{2},M_{3}N_{3},M_{4}N_{4})

\displaystyle=(Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},Q_{4})

\displaystyle=(X^{\prime}Q_{1},X^{\prime}Q_{2},X^{\prime}Q_{3},X^{\prime}Q_{4})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})

where in the last line we used Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark
.

The first part of the theorem, the fact that
M_{i}N_{i}
goes through a fixed point on
CD
, is the ”three-conics theorem“. See for example
[
GSO16
]
, Theorem 7.3.2.

Remark
.

This theorem gives another proof of theorem
3
by taking for
\mathcal{C}
a degenerate conic consisting of two lines going through two base points of
\mathcal{F}
.

Remark
.

Together with Proposition
7
, we see that the pencil
\mathcal{F}
induces an involution on the conic
\mathcal{C}
.

Example
.

Consider the following special case: take for
\mathcal{F}
a pencil of circles and for
\mathcal{C}
any circle, see Figure
26
. In fact, a conic is a circle iff it passes through the two cyclic points
I
and
J
with homogeneous coordinates
[1:i:0]
and
[i:1:0]
(which are complex and lie at infinity!), so the base points of a pencil of circles are the two intersection points
A
and
B
, and the cyclic points
I
and
J
. The circle
\mathcal{C}
goes through
I
and
J
.
Then Theorem
5
gives directly the cross ratio of the circles.

Case of a pencil of circles

As corollary, we can consider the special case when the conic
\mathcal{C}
goes through three of the base points. This is our favorite geometric way to get the cross ratio of conics:

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a conic going through three base points of the pencil
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
M_{i}
the fourth intersection point of
\mathcal{C}
with
\Gamma_{i}
. Then

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(M_{1},M_{2},M_{3},M_{4})_{\mathcal{C}}.

Figure 27.
Characterization with a conic

Proof.

Take Theorem
5
with
X=N_{1}=N_{2}=N_{3}=N_{4}=C
. Then using Proposition
2
we conclude:

(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4})=(CM_{1},CM_{2},CM_{3},CM_{4})=(M_{1},M_{2},M_{3},M_{4})_{\mathcal{C}}.

∎

To finish, we prove that the conjugation with respect to a pencil of conics also preserves the cross ratio of lines in some sense:

Theorem 6
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}
four concurrent lines, passing through some point
P
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{i}
the conic which is the image of
d_{i}
by conjugation with respect to some pencil
\mathcal{F}
. Then the
\mathcal{C}_{i}
form a pencil of conics and

(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3},\mathcal{C}_{4}).

Proof.

First, all
\mathcal{C}_{i}
pass through the three double points of
\mathcal{F}
and through
P^{\prime}
. Hence they all belong to a common pencil.

Second, let
l
denote a line intersecting
d_{i}
in some point
Q_{i}
(see Figure
27
). The image of
l
under conjugation with respect to
\mathcal{F}
is a conic
\mathcal{C}
passing through the double points
R,S,T
of
\mathcal{F}
. Denote by
T_{i}
the fourth intersection point of
\mathcal{C}
with
\mathcal{C_{i}}
(other than
R,S,T
). We have that
T_{i}=Q_{i}^{\prime}
since
Q_{i}=d_{i}\cap l
.

Figure 28.
Proof of Theorem
6

Therefore

\displaystyle(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{3},\mathcal{C}_{4})

\displaystyle=(T_{1},T_{2},T_{3},T_{4})_{\mathcal{C}}

by Corollary
3

\displaystyle=(Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},Q_{4})

by Proposition
11

\displaystyle=(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})

by Proposition
1

∎

As final remark, we wish to share our impression with the reader that
in projective geometry, all reasonable possible equalities between cross ratios are true
.
