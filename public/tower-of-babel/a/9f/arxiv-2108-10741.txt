1.
Introduction

Let
J
be the
2n\times 2n
matrix

J=\begin{bmatrix}O&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&O\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.
Consider the antisymmetric bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
given by

(x,y)=\langle x,Jy\rangle,\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

We call this bilinear form to be the
symplectic inner product
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

A
2n\times 2n
real matrix that preserves the symplectic inner product is called a
symplectic matrix
, i.e.,
M
is a symplectic matrix if

\langle Mx,JMy\rangle=\langle x,Jy\rangle

for all
x,y
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
This is equivalent to saying that
M
is symplectic if and only if
M^{T}JM=J.

We denote by
Sp(2n)
the collection of all
2n\times 2n
real symplectic matrices. This is a Lie group under matrix multiplication.
A basic theorem in symplectic matrix theory, generally known as
Williamson’s theorem
tells us that for every
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix
A,

there exists a
2n\times 2n
symplectic matrix
M
such that

M^{T}AM=\begin{bmatrix}D&O\\
O&D\end{bmatrix},

(2)

where
D
is the
n\times n
positive diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
d_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(A).

These positive numbers are complete invariants for
A
under the action of the symplectic group. We call them the
symplectic eigenvalues
of
A.

Symplectic eigenvalues are important in different areas of mathematics and physics such as symplectic geometry, symplectic topology, both classical and quantum mechanics and quantum information. See, for instance,
[
2
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
15
]
.
In particular, applications of symplectic eigenvalues in the newer area of quantum information has driven much of the recent work on this topic
[
2
,
13
,
15
,
18
,
20
]
.

For over a century, there has been an extensive interest in the study of variational principles for eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices and the relationships between the eigenvalues of
A,

B
and
A+B.

We refer the reader to Chapter III of
[
3
]
, Chapter 5 of
[
12
]
and to the excellent exposition
[
4
]
for detailed accounts of these topics.
If
A
is a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix, then
\imath A^{1/2}JA^{1/2}
is a Hermitian matrix.
It can be seen that
A
has symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
if and only if
\imath A^{1/2}JA^{1/2}
has eigenvalues
\pm d_{1},\ldots,\pm d_{n}.

So, in principle, it could be possible to derive the properties of symplectic eigenvalues from the well-known properties of eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices.
But due to the complicated form of the matrix
\imath A^{1/2}JA^{1/2},

it is often not feasible to do so.
The theory of symplectic eigenvalues also differs from the eigenvalue theory because of the differences in the symplectic group and the orthogonal group.
It is very remarkable that inspite of some major differences, we can find symplectic analogues to many of the classical results in eigenvalue theory
[
6
,
10
,
13
,
14
]
.
Two versions of symplectic maxmin principles, analogous to the Courant-Fischer-Weyl minmax principle can be found in the literature. See
[
9
,
6
,
8
]
.
These principles are in turn used to give symplectic analogues of an interlacing theorem, monotonicity principle and Weyl’s inequalities.
In
[
10
]
, Hiroshima proved the inequalities
\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}(A+B)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(d_{j}(A)+d_{j}(B)\right),

1\leq k\leq n
;
and these inequalities were generalised to Lidskii’s type inequalities in
[
14
]
by using the analyticity of symplectic eigenvalues.
A symplectic analogue of the Ky Fan maximum principle was proved in
[
10
]
.
An important extremal principle that includes the Courant-Fischer-Weyl principle as well as the Ky Fan maximum principle
for eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices is the Wielandt extremal principle.
This played an important role in the development of the celebrated Horn’s conjecture.
It gives us an expression for arbitrary sum of
k
eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices.
If
A
is an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(A),

then for all
1\leq k\leq n
and
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n,

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{i_{j}}(A)={\underset{\underset{\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}=n-i_{j}+1}{\mathcal{W}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{W}_{k}}}{\max}}{\underset{\underset{\textrm{orthonormal}}{x_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}\langle x_{j},Ax_{j}\rangle.

We give a symplectic analogue of the Wielandt principle.
A subset
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is called
symplectically orthonormal
if

\langle x_{i},Jx_{j}\rangle=\langle y_{i},Jy_{j}\rangle=0\textrm{ and }\langle x_{i},Jy_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,k.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
be a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix with symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
and
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n.
Then

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}={\underset{\underset{\text{\rm dim}\,\mathcal{W}_{j}=2n-i_{j}+1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{W}_{k}}}{\max}}{\underset{\underset{\underset{\textrm{orthonormal}}{\textrm{symplectically}}}{x_{j},y_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\langle x_{j},Ax_{j}\rangle+\langle y_{j},Ay_{j}\rangle}{2}.

(3)

This gives us an alternative proof of the Lidskii type inequalities for symplectic eigenvalues that were first proved in
[
14
]
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrices.
Suppose that
A,

B
and
A+B
have symplectic eigenvalues

d_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(A),

d_{1}(B)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(B)
and
d_{1}(A+B)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(A+B),
respectively.
Then for all
k=1,\ldots,n
and
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n,

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}(A+B)\geq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}(A)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}(B).

(4)

We also study the multiplicative versions of Lidskii’s inequalities for symplectic eigenvalues.
For two positive definite matrices
A
and
B,
their
geometric mean
is the positive definite matrix

A\#B=A^{1/2}\left(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}\right)^{1/2}A^{1/2}.

This was introduced by Pusz and Woronowicz in
[
19
]
and have important connections with
various areas such as physics, computer science, matrix analysis and Riemannian geometry
[
5
]
.
Suppose
A
and
B
are two
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrices.
Let
d_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(A),

d_{1}(B)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(B)
and

d_{1}(A\#B)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(A\#B)
be the symplectic eigenvalues of
A,

B
and
A\#B,
respectively.
The following relationships were proved in Theorem 3 of
[
6
]
: For all
k=1,\ldots,n,

\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}^{2}(A\#B)\geq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}(A)d_{j}(B)

and

\prod\limits_{j=k}^{n}d_{j}^{2}(A\#B)\leq\prod\limits_{j=k}^{n}d_{j}(A)d_{j}(B).

As a consequence of our analysis, we can extend these inequalities to arbitrary products of symplectic eigenvalues
analogous to the multiplicative inequalities proved by Lidskii for eigenvalues of two positive definite matrices
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
A
and
B
be two
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrices.
Then for all
k=1,\ldots,n
and
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n

\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}(A)d_{j}(B)\leq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{2}(A\#B)\leq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}(A)d_{n-j+1}(B).

(5)

In Section 2, we recall some basic facts on symplectic spaces and symplectic eigenvalues, and introduce some terminology and notation.
We prove Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
in Section 3.
In the process we give a third version of the maxmin principle for symplectic eigenvalues.
Theorem 3 is proved in Section 4.

2.
Preliminaries

A subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is called a
symplectic space
if for every
x\in\mathcal{V},
there exists a
y\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\langle x,Jy\rangle\neq 0.
It can be verified that every symplectic space has even dimension. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a symplectic subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and let
S
be a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{V}.
Then the set

S^{\perp_{s}}=\{y\in\mathcal{V}:\langle x,Jy\rangle=0\textrm{ for all }x\in S\}

is called the
symplectic complement
of
S
in
\mathcal{V}.
For any given nonempty set
S,

S^{\perp_{s}}
is always a subspace of
\mathcal{V}.
If
S
is a subspace of
\mathcal{V},
then
\text{\rm dim}\,S+\text{\rm dim}\,S^{\perp_{s}}=\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{V}.
A subspace
\mathcal{U}
of a symplectic space
\mathcal{V}
is symplectic if and only if
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\perp_{s}}=\{0\}.
In this case,
\mathcal{V}
is the direct sum of
\mathcal{U}
and its symplectic complement. Two vectors
x
and
y
are said to be
skew-orthogonal
if their symplectic inner product
\langle x,Jy\rangle=0.
A nonempty set is called
skew-orthogonal
if all its vectors are mutually skew-orthogonal. A skew-orthogonal subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is called
isotropic
. A subspace
\mathcal{V}
is isotropic if and only if
\mathcal{V}^{\perp_{s}}\supseteq\mathcal{V}.
Clearly, the dimension of an isotropic subspace of a
2m
-dimensional symplectic space is always less than or equal to
m.
A pair of vectors
(x,y)
is said to be
normalised symplectic pair
if
\langle x,Jy\rangle=1,
and a set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
is said to be
symplectically orthonormal
if

\langle x_{i},Jx_{j}\rangle=0=\langle y_{i},Jy_{j}\rangle

and

\langle x_{i},Jy_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,k.
Every symplectically orthonormal set is linearly independent. If
\mathcal{V}
is a
2k
-dimensional symplectic space and
\{x_{1},\ldots,

x_{k},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
is a symplectically orthonormal subset of
\mathcal{V},
we call it to be a
symplectic basis
of
\mathcal{V}.
The columns of a
2n\times 2n
symplectic matrix form a symplectic basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
A basis of a symplectic space
\mathcal{V}
that is symplectic as well as orthonormal is called an
orthosymplectic basis
of
\mathcal{V}.
The standard basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is an orthosymplectic basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
For more details on symplectic spaces we refer the reader to
[
2
,
9
]
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a symplectic space and let
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
be a symplectic basis of
\mathcal{V}.
We define a new inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
on
\mathcal{V}
as follows, (see
[
8
]
): if
x
and
y
are two vectors of
\mathcal{V},
we can write
x
and
y
as

x=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}u_{i}+\beta_{i}v_{i}\right)\textrm{ and }y=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\gamma_{i}u_{i}+\delta_{i}v_{i}\right).

Then

\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}\gamma_{i}+\beta_{i}\delta_{i}\right).

We denote the norm of
x
in this inner product by
\|x\|_{\mathcal{B}}.
Clearly,
\mathcal{B}
is an orthosymplectic basis of
\mathcal{V}
under the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
and the symplectic inner product
(\cdot,\cdot).
We call this
\mathcal{B}
-orthosymplectic basis
of
\mathcal{V}.
We define the
\mathcal{B}
-complement
of
x
to be the vector

x^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(-\beta_{i}u_{i}+\alpha_{i}v_{i}\right).

One can see that
x^{\prime\prime}=-x
for every
x\in\mathcal{V},
and
u_{j}^{\prime}=v_{j}
and
v_{j}^{\prime}=-u_{j},

1\leq j\leq m.
Also

\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}=\langle x,Jy^{\prime}\rangle=\langle y,Jx^{\prime}\rangle=\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}.

(6)

So, if
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
is a skew-orthogonal,
\mathcal{B}
-orthonormal subset of
\mathcal{V},
then
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime}\}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-orthosymplectic (symplectic as well as
\mathcal{B}
-orthonormal) set.

For a subspace
\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{V},
the
\mathcal{B}
-complement
of
\mathcal{W}
is the set

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}:x\in\mathcal{W}\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
is also a subspace of
\mathcal{W}
and
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}.
We denote the intersection
\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
by
\mathcal{W}^{\sharp}.
It can be seen that

\mathcal{W}^{\sharp}=\{x\in\mathcal{W}:x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}\},

and
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}>\frac{1}{2}\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{V}
implies
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}^{\sharp}>0.

Let
A
be a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix and let
d
be a symplectic eigenvalue of
A.

We call a pair of vectors
(u,v)
to be a
symplectic eigenvector pair
of
A
corresponding to
d
if

Au=dJv\textrm{ and }Av=-dJu.

If
d_{1}(A),\ldots,d_{n}(A)
are the symplectic eigenvalues of
A,

then the set
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},

v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
is called a
symplectic eigenbasis
of
A
if

\mathcal{B}
is a symplectic basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and each
(u_{i},v_{i})
is a normalised symplectic eigenvector pair of
A
corresponding to
d_{i}(A).

The columns of the symplectic matrix
M
in (
2
) form a symplectic eigenbasis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
corresponding to the symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1}(A),\ldots,d_{n}(A)
of
A.

With this terminology, we can alternatively state Williamson’s theorem as follows:
Let
A
be a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix. Then there exist positive numbers
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}

and a symplectic basis
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}

such that
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
are the symplectic eigenvalues of
A
and
\mathcal{B}
is a corresponding symplectic eigenbasis.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1

We start with an inequality on symplectic eigenvalues that is analogous to the Poincaré inequality for eigenvalues. See Theorem III.1.1 of
[
3
]
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
A
be a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix with symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}.

Let
1\leq k\leq n.
Then for any
2n-k+1
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{M},

there exist two vectors
u,v
in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\langle u,Jv\rangle=1
and

d_{k}\geq\frac{\langle u,Au\rangle+\langle v,Av\rangle}{2}.

(7)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a symplectic eigenbasis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
corresponding to
the symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
of
A.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the space spanned by
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}.

Since
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{M}+\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{N}=3n+1,

\text{\rm dim}\left(\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{N}\right)\geq n+1.

Hence we can find a vector
u
in
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{N}
such that
\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}}=1

and the
\mathcal{B}
-complement
u^{\prime}
of
u
also belongs to
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{N}.

Since
u,u^{\prime}
both belong to
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{N}
is spanned by

u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},

we must have

u=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left(\alpha_{i}u_{i}+\beta_{i}v_{i}\right),

where
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}\right)=1.

We thus have

u^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left(-\beta_{i}u_{i}+\alpha_{i}v_{i}\right).

A straightforward calculation shows that

\langle u,Au\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left(\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}\right)d_{i}\leq d_{k}.

(8)

Similarly,
\langle u^{\prime},Au^{\prime}\rangle\leq d_{k}.

Since
\langle u,Ju^{\prime}\rangle=1,

u
and
u^{\prime}
are the required vectors that satisfy (
7
).

We next prove a maxmin principle for symplectic eigenvalues. Two versions of this principle can be found in
[
9
,
6
]
and
[
8
]
.
Though our present version is similar to that given in Theorem 1 of
[
8
]
,
the maxmin principle in its present form plays a key role in proving Theorem
1
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
A
be a
2n\times 2n
real positive definite matrix,
and let
d_{1}\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}
be the symplectic eigenvalues of
A.

Then

d_{k}={\underset{\underset{dim\mathcal{M}=2n-k+1}{\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}}}{\max}}{\underset{\underset{\langle x,Jy\rangle=1}{x,y\in\mathcal{M}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\frac{\langle x,Ax\rangle+\langle y,Ay\rangle}{2},\ \ 1\leq k\leq n.

(9)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
2n-k+1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

By Proposition
4

we can find a normalised symplectic pair
(u,v)
that satisfy (
7
).
Then

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\langle u,Au\rangle+\langle v,Av\rangle}{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\underset{\underset{\langle x,Jy\rangle=1}{x,y\in\mathcal{M}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\frac{\langle x,Ax\rangle+\langle y,Ay\rangle}{2}.

This shows that the right hand side of (
9
) is less than or equal to
d_{k}.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a symplectic eigenbasis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
corresponding to the symplectic eigenvalues
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
of
A.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{k}
be the subspace spanned by
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},

v_{k},\ldots,v_{n}\}.

Clearly
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{M}_{k}=2n-k+1.

Let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{i=k}^{n}\beta_{i}v_{i}
and
y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{i=k}^{n}\delta_{i}v_{i}

be any two elements of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
such that
\langle x,Jy\rangle=1.

We have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x,Jy\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\left(\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}-\beta_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}+\gamma_{i}^{2}+\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}.

The last inequality follows from the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality.
Now we have

\displaystyle\frac{\langle x,Ax\rangle+\langle y,Ay\rangle}{2}\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}+\gamma_{i}^{2}}{2}d_{i}+\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\beta_{i}^{2}+\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}d_{i}

(10)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{k}\sum\limits_{i=k}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}+\beta_{i}^{2}+\gamma_{i}^{2}+\delta_{i}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d_{k}.

Thus

d_{k}\leq{\underset{\underset{\langle x,Jy\rangle=1}{x,y\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\frac{\langle x,Ax\rangle+\langle y,Ay\rangle}{2}.

(11)

The two sides of (
11
) are equal for
x=u_{k}
and
y=v_{k}.

This gives the equality (
9
).

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a
2n
-dimensional symplectic space and let
\mathcal{B}
be a symplectic basis of
\mathcal{V}.

For
1\leq k\leq n,
let
\mathcal{W}_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal{W}_{k}
be a decreasing chain of subspaces of
\mathcal{V}

with
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}\geq n+k-j+1,

1\leq j\leq k.

Let
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k-1}\}
be a
\mathcal{B}
-orthonormal, skew-orthogonal set
such that
w_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}^{\sharp},

j=1,\ldots,k-1,

and let
\mathcal{U}
be the space spanned by
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k-1},w_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,w_{k-1}^{\prime}\}.

Then there exists an element
v\in\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\sharp}\cap\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k-1}\}^{\perp_{s}}

such that
\mathcal{U}+\text{\rm span}\{v,v^{\prime}\}
has a
\mathcal{B}
-orthosymplectic basis

\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime}\},

where
v_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}^{\sharp}
for all
j=1,\ldots,k.

Proof.

Since
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}\geq n+(k-j+1),

\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{\sharp}\geq 2(k-j+1),

1\leq j\leq k.

We prove the result by induction on
k.

Let
k=2,
and let
w_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\sharp}.

Then
\mathcal{U}=\text{\rm span}\{w_{1},w_{1}^{\prime}\}.

We know that
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\sharp}\geq 4
and
\text{\rm dim}\,\mathcal{U}^{\perp_{s}}=2n-2.

So, if
w_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\sharp},
then there exists a
v\in\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\sharp}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\perp_{s}}.

The set
\{v,w_{1},v^{\prime},w_{1}^{\prime}\}
is the required
\mathcal{B}
-orthosymplectic basis of
\mathcal{U}+\text{\rm span}\{v,v^{\prime}\}.

Now, suppose that
w_{1}\notin\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\sharp}.

Since
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\sharp}\geq 2,

\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{2}(A)={\underset{\underset{\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}=2n-i_{j}+1}{\mathcal{W}_{1}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{W}_{k}}}{\max}}{\underset{\underset{\underset{\textrm{ symplectically orthonormal}}{u_{j},v_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}}}{\mathcal{M}=\text{\rm span}\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}}}{\text{\rm min}}}\det(A_{\mathcal{M}}).

(22)

Proof of Theorem
3
:
Let
\mathcal{W}_{1}\supset\cdots\supset\mathcal{W}_{k}
be a decreasing chain of subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with
\text{\rm dim}\mathcal{W}_{j}=2n-i_{j}+1.

Suppose
u_{j},v_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{j}
are such that
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
is symplectically orthonormal.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the span of
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}.

For the convenience of notation, we denote
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
by
u_{k+1},\ldots,u_{2k},
respectively.
Let
U
be the orthogonal matrix such that
A\#B=A^{1/2}UB^{1/2}.

Now,

\displaystyle\left|\det(A\#B)_{\mathcal{M}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\langle u_{i}A^{1/2}UB^{1/2}u_{j}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right|^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\langle A^{1/2}u_{i},UB^{1/2}u_{j}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\langle A^{1/2}u_{i},A^{1/2}u_{j}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right|\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\langle UB^{1/2}u_{i},UB^{1/2}u_{j}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\det(A_{\mathcal{M}})\right|\left|\det(B_{\mathcal{M}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\det(A_{\mathcal{M}})\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(B),

where
\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(B),\ldots,\lambda_{2n}^{\downarrow}(B)
denote the eigenvalues of
B
arranged in decreasing order.
The last inequality in (
23
) follows from Theorem III.1.5 of
[
3
]
.
By using (
22
), we obtain

\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{4}(A\#B)\leq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{2}(A)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2k}\lambda_{j}(B).

Let
M
be a symplectic matrix such that
M^{T}BM=\text{\rm diag}(D(B),D(B)),

where
D(B)
is the positive diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
d_{1}(B)\leq\cdots\leq d_{n}(B).

By the congruence invariance property of geometric means,

M^{T}(A\#B)M=(M^{T}AM)\#(M^{T}BM),

and the fact that for all positive definite matrices
X,

d_{j}(M^{T}XM)=d_{j}(X),

1\leq j\leq n,

we can assume that
B
is the diagonal matrix
\text{\rm diag}(D(B),D(B)).

In this case,
\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(B)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{n-j+1}^{2}(B).

This gives the second inequality in (
5
).

Take
G=A\#B.
Then we have
A^{1/2}=GB^{-1/2}U^{*}.

Calculations similar to those in (
23
) give us the relation

\det(A_{\mathcal{M}})\leq\left(\det(G_{\mathcal{M}})\right)^{2}\det(B^{-1}_{U\mathcal{M}})\leq\left(\det(G_{\mathcal{M}})\right)^{2}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(B^{-1}).

Again, as in the previous paragraph, we can assume that
B
is the diagonal matrix
\text{\rm diag}(D(B),D(B)),
and by using (
22
) we can obtain

\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{2}(A)\leq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}d_{i_{j}}^{4}(G)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{d_{j}^{2}(B)}.

This gives the first inequality of (
5
).

We end the paper with an example which shows that unlike eigenvalues, the symplectic eigenvalues of
AA^{T}
and
A^{T}A
need not be equal.
Let
A
be the
4\times 4
matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&O\\
O&A_{2}\end{bmatrix},

A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

A_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

Then straightforward calculations show that the symplectic eigenvalues of

2

2

are
1
and
4.

Further, it can be easily seen from Matlab experiments that for any two
