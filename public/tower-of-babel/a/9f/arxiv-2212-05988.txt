Abstract
. In this paper we give a characterization of Avila’s quantized acceleration of the Lyapunov exponent via the number of zeros of the Dirichlet determinants in finite volume. As applications, we prove β \beta -Hölder continuity of the integrated density of states for supercritical quasi-periodic Schrödinger operators restricted to the ℓ \ell -th stratum, for any β < ( 2 ​ ( ℓ − 1 ) ) − 1 \beta<(2(\ell-1))^{-1} and ℓ ≥ 2 \ell\geq 2 . We establish Anderson localization for all Diophantine frequencies for the operator with even analytic potential function on the first supercritical stratum, which has positive measure if it is nonempty.



1.
Introduction

This paper studies the one-dimensional quasi-periodic Schrödinger operator:

(1.1)

\displaystyle(H_{\alpha,\theta}\phi)_{n}=\phi_{n+1}+\phi_{n-1}+f(\theta+n\alpha)\phi_{n},

with real-valued analytic potential
f(\theta)\in C_{\eta}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})
,
\eta>0
.
Here
\alpha
is called the frequency and
\theta
is called the phase, and
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is the one-dimensional torus,
and we shall use
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{T}}
to denote the torus norm.

We study the operator in the positive Lyapunov exponent regime.
The goal of this paper is to build a bridge between the large deviation estimates and avalanche principle developed in a series of papers by Bourgain-Goldstein
[
BG
]
, Goldstein-Schlag
[
GS1
,
GS2
]
on the one hand, with Avila’s quantized acceleration of the Lyapunov exponent
[
Av3
]
on the other hand.
In particular, we obtain a sharp characterization of the acceleration in terms of the number of zeros of the Dirichlet determinants, see Theorem
1.2
.
As applications, we improve the Hölder exponent of the integrated density of states in
[
GS2
]
(see Theorem
1.3
), and we prove Anderson localization of the operator on the first supercritical stratum, on which the acceleration equals
1
, and for all Diophantine frequencies (see Theorem
1.6
).

To be more specific, let

\displaystyle D_{n}(\theta,E):=\det(H_{\alpha,\theta}-E)|_{[0,n-1]}.

Since
f
is analytic, we can write
D_{n}(\theta,E)
as a function of
e^{2\pi i\theta}
. Replacing
e^{2\pi i\theta}
with a complex variable
z
, we shall also denote
D_{n}(\theta,E)
by
D_{n}(z,E)
.
We assume throughout that
\alpha
satisfies the Diophantine condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}:=\Big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}:\,\|n\alpha\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\frac{c}{n(\log n)^{a}},\text{ for all }|n|\geq 1,\Big\}

where
c>0
and
a>1
.
It is well-known that for any
a>1
,
\cup_{c>0}\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
is a full measure set.

For an energy
E
, let the Lyapunov exponent
L(E,\varepsilon)
of the complexified operator
H_{\alpha,\theta+i\varepsilon}
be defined as usual, see (
2.15
) below.
As a corner stone of his global cocycle theory
[
Av3
]
, Avila exhibited quantization of the slope of the Lyapunov exponent as a convex function of
\varepsilon
: the right derivative
\kappa(E,\varepsilon)
of
L(E,\varepsilon)/(2\pi)
in
\varepsilon
is always an integer, see Section
2.2
.
Using the quantized acceleration, Avila stratified the spectrum
\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})
into

\displaystyle\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})=\bigcup_{\ell\geq 1}\mathcal{S}_{\ell},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\ell}:=\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})\cap\{E:\,\kappa(E,0)=\ell-1\}.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
is called the
\ell
-th stratum of the spectrum.

For
E\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, it is necessary that
L(E,0)=0
since otherwise the corresponding Schrödinger cocycle is uniformly hyperbolic which leads to a contradiction with
E\in\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})
.
In fact,
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is refereed to as the subcritical regime.
This stratum is usually studied by KAM and reducibility methods, see e.g.
[
DS
,
E1
,
P
,
Av1
,
AJ
,
Av2
]
.

For each
\ell\geq 2
, we further divide

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\ell}=\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{+}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{0},

where
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{+}:=\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\cap\{E:\,L(E,0)>0\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{0}:=\mathcal{S}_{\ell}\cap\{E:\,L(E,0)=0\}
.
The energies in
\cup_{\ell\geq 2}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{0}
are referred to as the critical energies, and those in
\cup_{\ell\geq 2}\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{+}
are supercritical.

One of the central examples of quasi-periodic Schrödinger operator is the almost Mathieu operator:

(1.3)

\displaystyle(H^{\mathrm{AMO}}_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta}\phi)_{n}=\phi_{n+1}+\phi_{n-1}+2\lambda\cos 2\pi(\theta+n\alpha)\phi_{n}.

The supercritical, critical and subcritical regime corresponds to the regions where the coupling constant satisfies
|\lambda|>1
,
|\lambda|=1
, and
0<|\lambda|<1
, respectively.
A special feature for the almost Mathieu operator is that for
|\lambda|>1
,
\sigma(H^{\mathrm{AMO}}_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta})=\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
, hence the entire spectrum is contained in the first supercritical stratum.

Our analysis focuses on general analytic potentials in the supercritical regime.
We begin with an immediate corollary of
[
Av3
]
and
[
DGSV
]
on the Lebesgue measure of strata.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
for some
c>0
and
a>1
.
For any
k\geq 2
, if

(1.4)

\bigcup_{j=2}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{+}\neq\emptyset,\text{\ \ then }\Big|\bigcup_{j=2}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{+}\Big|>0

In particular, if
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}\neq\emptyset
, then
|\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}|>0
.

Indeed, let
E_{0}
be in the set in (
1.4
). Then by the upper semicontinuity and quantization of the acceleration
[
Av3
, Theorem 5]
, as well as the continuity of the Lyapunov exponent

I_{0}\subset\Big\{E\in\mathbb{R}\>:\>\kappa(E,0)<k+\frac{1}{2},\;L(E,0)>0\Big\}

for some interval
I_{0}=(E_{0}-\delta,E_{0}+\delta)
. By
[
DGSV
, Theorem H]
, we see that
|\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})\cap I_{0}|>\frac{1}{2}\delta
if
\delta
is small enough. By
[
Av3
, Theorem 6]
one has
\kappa(E,0)\geq 1
on
\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})\cap I_{0}
, hence
\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})\cap I_{0}=\cup_{j=2}^{k}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{+}
, whence the theorem.

We now turn to the main theorem of our work, which characterizes Avila’s acceleration via the number of zeros. Throughout we denote annuli by

A_{r}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,1/r<|z|<r\}
and

N_{n}(E,\varepsilon):=\#\{z\in\overline{A_{e^{2\pi\varepsilon}}}:\,D_{n}(z,E)=0\}

is the number of zeros relative to
z
of a determinant in finite volume.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
L(E)\geq\tau>0
and let
\varepsilon\in(0,\eta)
be such that

L(E,\varepsilon)=L(E,0)+2\pi\kappa(E,0)\varepsilon.

Then
for
n>N(\tau,E,f,\eta,\alpha,\gamma)
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2n}N_{n}(E,\varepsilon/2)-\kappa(E,0)\right|\leq\varepsilon^{-1}n^{-\gamma}.

This theorem gives a characterization of the acceleration
\kappa(E,0)
in terms of the number of zeros of
D_{n}(z,E)
in a neighborhood of the unit circle.
Theorem
1.2
is part of the more general Theorem
4.1
(see also Theorem
4.4
), where the characterization of
\kappa(E,\varepsilon)-\kappa(E,\varepsilon^{-})
, for non-zero
\varepsilon
, is given in terms of zeros of
D_{n}(z,E)
near the circle with radius
e^{2\pi\varepsilon}
.
An analogous result for the Riesz mass of
\log\|M_{n}(z,E)\|
, where
M_{n}
is the transfer matrix (
2.11
), is given in Theorem
5.1
.

A key new ingredient in the proof is a Riesz representation of subharmonic function on the annulus, compared to the one on disks that were used to cover the annulus in
[
GS1
,
GS2
]
.
It turns out the annulus version of the representation is more naturally connected to the Lyapunov exponent, hence to the acceleration.
As one of the applications of Theorem
1.2
, we improve on the known regularity of the integrated density of states (IDS) as a function of the energy.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume the potential function
f
is analytic and fix
\alpha\in\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
for some
c>0
and
a>1
. Then for any
\ell\geq 2
, on the open set
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{+}
the IDS of
H_{\alpha,\theta}
is
\beta
-Hölder continuous with any
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2(\ell-1)}
.

This result proves the conjecture by You in
[
Y
]
that the Hölder exponent for IDS is at least
1/(2\kappa(E,0))
(but we cannot decide equality of the exponent to this ratio). Note that
\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{+}
includes gaps in the spectrum on which the IDS is constant. But by Theorem
1.1
, if this set of energies is nonempty, then it contains a positive measure subset of the spectrum.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
is not self-contained. In fact, Theorem
1.3
follows by combining the proof of
[
GS2
, Theorem 1.1]
with the characterization of the acceleration (and thus the stratum) in Theorem
1.2
. Also, recall from
[
GS2
]
that the IDS is Lipschitz off a zero-measure set of energies.

Remark 1.4
.

In
[
GS2
, Theorem 1.1]
, it was proved that for
f(\theta)=\sum_{n=-k_{0}}^{k_{0}}c_{n}e^{2\pi in\theta}
, a trigonometric polynomial of degree
k_{0}\geq 1
and assuming
\omega\in{DC}_{c,a}
and

L(E,0)>0
the IDS is
\beta
-Hölder continuous for any
\beta<\frac{1}{2k_{0}}
.
Since for trigonometric
f
one has
\kappa(E,0)\leq k_{0}
, Theorem
1.3
is a refinement of
[
GS2
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Remark 1.5
.

For the almost Mathieu operator and
\alpha\in\mathrm{DC}
(see (
1.8
)), the IDS was proved to be
1/2
-Hölder if
|\lambda|\neq 0,1

[
AJ
]
.
The
1/2
-Hölder exponent was also proved for Diophantine
\alpha
and
f=\lambda\,g
with
g
being analytic and the coupling constant
|\lambda|
being small, first in the perturbative regime (smallness depends on
\alpha,g
) in
[
Am
]
, and then in the non-perturbative regime
[
AJ
]
(with dependence on
\alpha
removed).
For quasi-periodic long-range operators with large trigonometric polynomial potentials and Diophantine frequencies, the Hölder exponent in
[
GS2
]
was improved recently in the perturbative regime in
[
GYZ
]
.
These results are proved using the reducibility method.

Our next application concerns Anderson localization (pure point spectrum with exponentially decaying eigenfunctions).
Anderson localization for quasi-periodic operators was first studied perturbatively
[
FSW
,
Sin
,
E2
]
where
f=\lambda\,g
with large
\lambda
whose largeness depends on both
\alpha
and
g
.
The first non-perturbative Anderson localization was obtained by Jitomirskaya for the almost Mathieu operator
[
J1
,
J2
]
for any
|\lambda|>1
and all Diophantine frequencies.
In
[
BG
]
, Bourgain and Goldstein proved Anderson localization for the general analytic potential in the supercritical regime for an implicit full measure set of
\alpha
.
In this paper we show that quasi-periodic operators with even potentials in the first supercritical stratum
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
is always Anderson localized for all Diophantine frequencies. This result thus includes that of the supercricitical almost Mathieu operator as a special case.
Let

(1.6)

\displaystyle\Theta_{c^{\prime},b}:=\bigcap_{k\geq 1}\bigcup_{|n|\geq k}\Big\{\theta\in\mathbb{T}:\,\|2\theta+n\alpha\|_{\mathbb{T}}<\frac{c^{\prime}}{|n|^{b}}\Big\},

where
c^{\prime}>0
and
b>1
.
Clearly the complement
(\Theta_{c^{\prime},b})^{c}
is a full measure set.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume the potential function
f
is even. For
\alpha\in\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
for some
c>0
and
a>1
, and
\theta\in(\Theta_{c^{\prime},b})^{c}
for some
c^{\prime}>0
and
b>1
,
H_{\alpha,\theta}
has pure point spectrum with exponentially decaying eigenfunctions in
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
.

Recall that by Theorem
1.1
if
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
is non-empty, then this set has to be of positive measure.
As a corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we can prove Anderson localization for
analytic perturbations of the supercritical almost Mathieu operator, which has recently been studied by Ge-Jitomirskaya-Zhao in
[
GJZ
]
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let the perturbed almost Mathieu operator be defined as

(1.7)

\displaystyle(H_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta,g,\varepsilon}\phi)_{n}=\phi_{n+1}+\phi_{n-1}+(2\lambda\cos 2\pi(\theta+n\alpha)+\varepsilon g(\theta+n\alpha))\phi_{n},

where
g\in C^{\omega}_{\mathbb{T}_{\eta}}
is even and real-valued.
For
|\lambda|>1
, there exists
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}(\lambda,\|g\|_{\mathbb{T}_{\eta}})>0
such that if
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{1}
, and
\alpha\in\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
for some
c>0
and
a>1
, and
\theta\in(\Theta_{c^{\prime},b})^{c}
for some
c^{\prime}>0
and
b>1
,

H_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta,g,\varepsilon}
has pure point spectrum with exponentially decaying eigenfunctions.

This corollary follows from combining Theorem
1.6
with the following lemma of Avila.

Lemma 1.8

(Lemma 25,
[
Av3
]
)
.

For
|\lambda|>1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, there exists
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{1}(\lambda,\|g\|_{\mathbb{T}_{\eta}})>0
such that for
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{1}
,
\sigma(H_{\lambda,\alpha,\theta,g,\varepsilon})=\mathcal{S}_{2}^{+}
.

Remark 1.9
.

It is possible to prove Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7
for
\alpha
satisfying a weaker Diophantine condition:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathrm{DC}:=\bigcup_{c>0}\bigcup_{\rho>1}\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}:\,\|n\alpha\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\frac{c}{|n|^{\rho}},\text{ for any }n\neq 0\right\}.

Replacing our
\mathrm{DC}_{c,a}
with the weaker condition will lead to less sharp large deviation estimates in Section
2.3
, but these will still suffice for the proof of Anderson localization.

Next let us comment on the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
The key to prove Anderson localization is to eliminate “double resonance”, which roughly speaking occurs when both
D_{n}(\theta,E)
and
D_{n}(\theta+k\alpha,E)
are close to
0
for
k\simeq n^{C}
.
Jitomirskaya’s
[
J1
,
J2
]
argument for the almost Mathieu operator uses crucially that the potential is cosine.
She proved
D_{n}(\theta-\frac{n-1}{2}\alpha,E)
is a polynomial of
\cos(2\pi\theta)
of degree at most
n
, and used the Lagrange interpolation formula to eliminate double resonances.
Bourgain and Goldstein
[
BG
]
developed a large deviation estimate for the set on which the norm of the transfer matrix is small, and then used semi-algebraic tools to study the complexity of this set.
In order to eliminate double resonances, they then devised a “steep line” argument, which requires deleting an implicit zero measure of
\alpha
’s.
In this paper, on the one hand we appeal to the large deviation estimates in
[
GS2
]
for the sub-exponentially small measure of the deviation set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
(see (
6.79
)), on the other hand we combine the
\kappa=1
case of Theorem
1.2
with a Cartan set argument to show that
\mathcal{B}_{n}
consists of at most
2n+o(n)
many intervals.
Using that the potential is even, the
2n+o(n)
intervals form
n+o(n)
pairs under reflection
\theta\to-\theta
.
The arithmetic conditions on
\alpha,\theta
prevent that two points in the trajectory
\{\theta+j\alpha\}
fall into the same pair (at the appropriate scales).
This eliminates double resonances.
It is worth mentioning that although the
\kappa=1
case of Theorem
1.2
ensures that
D_{n}(z,E)
has at most
2n+o(n)
many zeros,
D_{n}(\theta-\frac{n-1}{2}\alpha,E)
is not a polynomial of degree at most
2n+o(n)
in
\cos(2\pi\theta)
, so the Lagrange interpolation argument as in
[
J1
,
J2
]
does not apply.

The evenness assumption on the potential is crucial for our argument. It is not clear if one can improve on
[
BG
]
without making this symmetry assumption in the sense that there is an explicit condition on the phases
\theta
which guarantees localization.
Recently, Forman and VandenBoom
[
FV
]
proved Anderson localization perturbatively for large cosine-like potentials without evenness assumption,
thus removing the symmetry requirement from the classical work by Fröhlich, Spencer, and Wittwer
[
FSW
]
. However, while
[
FSW
]
eliminate resonant phases via an arithmetic condition,
[
FV
]
do so by an implicit procedure.

Finally, let us mention that it is an interesting question as to whether the results in Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
hold for Liouville frequencies.
Related questions have been addressed for the almost Mathieu operator in e.g.
[
AD
,
Av1
,
AYZ
,
JL
]
.
For general analytic potentials, large deviation estimates have been developed for some Liouville
\alpha
’s in
[
YZ
,
HZ
]
, leading to the proof of Hölder continuity of the IDS, albeit with non-sharp exponent.
It would be interesting to see if one can combine the techniques in
[
YZ
,
HZ
]
with ours.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we collect some preliminary results; in Section
3
we develop the Riesz representation of subharmonic functions on an annulus; in Sections
4
and
5
we apply the Riesz representation to the functions
u_{n}
and

and establish the zero count of Theorem
1.2
. Finally, Anderson localization as in Theorem
1.6
is obtained in Section

, and Theorem
1.3
on Hölder continuity of the IDS is proved in Section

2.
Preliminaries

Notations: Let
A_{R}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,1/R<|z|<R\}
be the annulus and
\mathcal{C}_{R}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|=R\}
be the circle.
For a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
, let
|U|
be its Lebesgue measure.

2.1.
Determinant and transfer matrix

Since
f\in C_{\eta}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T})
, the determinant
D_{n}(z,E)
is holomorphic in the annulus
A_{e^{2\pi\eta}}
.
Moreover, as
f
is real-valued for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
D_{n}(z,E)=\overline{D_{n}(1/\overline{z},E)}
for
z\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
, hence

(2.9)

\displaystyle D_{n}(z,E)=\overline{D_{n}(1/\overline{z},E)},\text{ for }z\in A_{e^{2\pi\eta}}.

In particular, we note the following fact that will be used many time throughout the paper:

Fact 2.1
.

If
w\notin\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a zero of
D_{n}(z,E)
, then
1/\overline{w}
is also a zero.

Let

(2.10)

\displaystyle u_{n}(z,E):=\frac{1}{n}\log|D_{n}(z,E)|.

and as usual,

(2.11)

\displaystyle M(\theta,E)=\left(\begin{matrix}E-f(\theta)\ &-1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)

denotes the transfer matrix, and

\displaystyle M_{n}(\theta,E):=M(\theta+(n-1)\alpha,E)\cdots M(\theta,E)

be the n-step transfer matrix.
Let

(2.12)

\displaystyle v_{n}(z,E):=\frac{1}{n}\log\|M_{n}(z,E)\|.

Both
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
are subharmonic functions in the annulus
A_{R}
.
The starting point of our study is an effective Riesz representation of subharmonic functions in
A_{R}
, which we develop in Section
3
.

Recall the well-known connection between
D_{n}
and
M_{n}
, viz.

(2.13)

\displaystyle M_{n}(z,E)=\left(\begin{matrix}D_{n}(z,E)\,&-D_{n-1}(ze^{2\pi i\alpha},E)\\
D_{n-1}(z,E)&-D_{n-2}(ze^{2\pi i\alpha},E)\end{matrix}\right).

This implies that

(2.14)

\displaystyle u_{n}(z,E)\leq v_{n}(z,E),

which will be used multiple times in this paper.

2.2.
Lyapunov exponent and acceleration

Let

(2.15)

\displaystyle L_{n}(E,\varepsilon):=\int_{0}^{1}v_{n}(e^{2\pi i(\theta+i\varepsilon)},E)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

and

(2.16)

\displaystyle L(E,\varepsilon):=\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{n}(E,\varepsilon).

The Lyapunov exponent is even in
\varepsilon
due to
f
being real-valued, and is a convex function in
\varepsilon
by Hadamard’s three circle theorem. We define its derivative from the right as

(2.17)

\displaystyle\kappa(E,\varepsilon):=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{L(E,\varepsilon+\delta)-L(E,\varepsilon)}{2\pi\delta}.

The function
\kappa(E,\varepsilon)
is referred to as the
acceleration
of the Lyapunov exponent.

In
[
Av3
]
, Avila proved the following:

Theorem 2.2
.

[
Av3
, Theorem 5]

For any irrational
\alpha
and any
E,\varepsilon
, the acceleration
\kappa(E,\varepsilon)
is always an integer.

Using the quantized acceleration, Avila further introduced the stratification of the spectrum:
X_{1}:=\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta})
, and
X_{j}
with
j\geq 2
is defined as

\displaystyle X_{j}:=\{E\in X_{1}:\,\kappa(E,0)\geq j-1\}.

The set

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}:=X_{j}\setminus X_{j+1}=\{E\in\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta}):\,\kappa(E,0)=j-1\}

is called the
j
-th stratum of the stratification.
It is clear that
\{\mathcal{S}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise disjoint and

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j\geq 1}\mathcal{S}_{j}=X_{1}=\sigma(H_{\alpha,\theta}).

2.3.
Large deviation estimates

Our analysis depends crucially on the large deviation estimates (and their consequences) for
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
developed in a series of papers
[
BG
,
GS1
,
GS2
]
, in combination with the avalanche principle.
We list the results that are crucial to our analysis below.
Recall that the potential
f\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}_{\eta})
.
The first one is a uniform upper bound for
v_{n}
, the logarithm of the norm of the transfer matrix.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
GS2
, Proposition 4.3]

Assume
L(E,0)>\tau>0
.
Then for all
n\geq 1
, and
0\leq|\varepsilon|<\eta/2
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}v_{n}(e^{2\pi i(\theta+i\varepsilon)},E)\leq L_{n}(E,\varepsilon)+C\frac{(\log n)^{C_{0}}}{n}.

for some constants
C=C(\|f\|_{\eta},\eta,c,a,\tau,E)
and
C_{0}=C_{0}(a)
, with
c
,
a
as in (
1.2
).

The next is a quantitative convergence rate of
L_{n}(E,\varepsilon)
to
L(E,\varepsilon)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
GS1
, Theorem 5.1]

If
L(E,0)>\tau>0
, then for
n\geq 1
, one has that for
|\varepsilon|<\eta/2
,

\displaystyle 0\leq L_{n}(E,\varepsilon)-L(E,\varepsilon)\leq\frac{C}{n},

where
C=C(\tau,E,f,\eta,\alpha)
.

The next is an average lower bound for
u_{n}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
GS2
, Lemma 2.10]

If
L(E)>\tau>0
, there exists a positive constant
\gamma_{1}>0
such that for
|\varepsilon|<\eta/2
, and all
n>N(\tau,E,f,\eta,\alpha)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u_{n}(e^{2\pi i(\theta+i\varepsilon)},E)\,\mathrm{d}\theta>L_{n}(E,\varepsilon)-n^{-\gamma_{1}}.

Let

3.
Effective Riesz representation for the annulus

This section develops some basic potential theory on annuli. We begin with the Green’s function on an annulus, which is standard.

3.1.
Green’s function for the annulus.

It is worth pointing out that we never use the explicit expression of the Green’s function, rather only the fact that it can be decomposed as in (
3.22
) below. For the sake of completeness, we state the precise Green’s kernel, which can be derived by the method of images.

Lemma 3.1
.

The Green’s function on the annulus
A_{R}
is given by

(3.20)

\displaystyle G_{R}(z,w)=\frac{\log(|z|/R)\log(|w|/R)}{4\pi\log R}+K_{R}(z,w),

where

(3.21)

\displaystyle K_{R}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(|z/R-w/R|\cdot\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}|1-\frac{1}{R^{4k}}\frac{z}{w}|\cdot|1-\frac{1}{R^{4k}}\frac{w}{z}|}{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}|1-\frac{1}{R^{4k-2}}w\overline{z}|\cdot|1-\frac{1}{R^{4k-2}}\frac{1}{\overline{z}w}|}\right).

The Green’s function is symmetric and invariant under rotations:
G_{R}(z,w)=G_{R}(w,z)
and
G_{R}(z,w)=G_{R}(e^{i\phi}z,e^{i\phi}w)
.

Proof.

The symmetry properties are evident from the formula. Second, by inspection

(3.22)

\displaystyle G_{R}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|z-w|+H_{R}(z,w),\quad z\in A_{R},w\in\overline{A_{R}}

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}H_{R}(z,w)

\displaystyle=0

whence
\Delta_{z}G(z,w)=\delta_{0}(z-w)
.
Third, if
w=R
, then

G_{R}(z,w)=K_{R}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{|z/R-1|}{|1-\frac{1}{R}\overline{z}|}\right)=0

This holds for all
|w|=R
by rotational invariance of
G_{R}
. If
|w|=R^{-1}
, then

G_{R}(z,1/R)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(|z|/R)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{|z/R-1/R^{2}|}{|1-1/(R\bar{z})|}\right)=0

and this again holds for all
|w|=R^{-1}
by rotational invariance. The lemma follows from the uniqueness of the Green’s function.
∎

We evaluate the integral of the Green’s function along the circle
z\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
with
1/R\leq r\leq R
.
These integrals will be used later in Section
4
to estimate the number of zeros of
D_{n}(z,E)
, and the Riesz mass of
v_{n}(z,E)
in Section
5
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
1/R\leq r\leq R
and
w\in A_{R}
, we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle I(\log r,\log R,w)

\displaystyle:=2\pi\int_{0}^{1}G_{R}(re^{2\pi i\theta},w)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.24)

\displaystyle=(2\log R)^{-1}\begin{cases}\log(rR)\log|w/R|,\text{ if }|w|\geq r\\
\\
\log(r/R)\log|wR|,\text{ if }|w|<r.\end{cases}

Proof.

(
3.22
) yields

(3.25)

\displaystyle 2\pi\int_{0}^{1}G_{R}(re^{2\pi i\theta},w)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|re^{i\theta}-w|\,d\theta+\int_{0}^{2\pi}H_{R}(re^{i\theta},w)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=:J_{1}(w)+J_{2}(w)

where
J_{1}(w)=\log|w|
if
|w|\geq r
and
J_{1}(w)=\log r
if
|w|\leq r
.
J_{2}(w)
is harmonic in
w\in A_{R}
, radial, and continuous on
\overline{A_{R}}
. Thus,
J_{2}(w)=a\log|w|+b
. Setting
|w|=R
, respectively
|w|=R^{-1}
shows that

J_{2}(w)=\frac{\log(r/R)}{2\log R}\log(|w|/R)-\log R

which implies (
3.23
).
∎

3.2.
Effective Riesz representation

We now turn to the basic Riesz representation of subharmonic functions. In contrast to
[
GS1
,
GS2
]
, which analyzed supercricial cocycles by means of potential theory on small disks, here we conduct this analysis globally on annuli.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
v
be a subharmonic function in a neighborhood of
\overline{A_{R}}
, and assume
v|_{\partial A_{R}}
is a continuous function.
Let
G_{R}
be the Green’s function for
A_{R}
, as in (
3.20
).
There exists a positive finite measure
\mu
on
A_{R}
, and a harmonic function
h_{R}
on
A_{R}
, such that

\displaystyle v(w)=\int_{A_{R}}2\pi G_{R}(z,w)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}z)+h_{R}(w),

where

(3.26)

\displaystyle h_{R}(w)=\int_{\partial A_{R}}v(z)\,\nu(w,A_{R})(\mathrm{d}z),

where
\nu(w,A_{R})
is the harmonic measure of
A_{R}
with pole at
w
.
In particular,

(3.27)

\displaystyle h_{R}(z)=v(z),\text{ for }z\in\partial A_{R}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
v
is smooth. If this is not the case, we convolve
v
with
a radial nonnegative mollifier. The submean property then guarantees monotone convergence. We skip these technical details.

By Green’s second identify, with the Green’s function
G_{R}
defined in (
3.20
),

\displaystyle v(w)-\int_{A_{R}}G_{R}(z,w)\Delta v(z)\,m(\mathrm{d}z)=\int_{\partial A_{R}}v(z)\frac{\partial G_{R}}{\partial n_{z}}(z,w)\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}z),

where
m
is Lebesgue measure and
\sigma
is the (unnormalized) arclength measure on
\partial A_{R}
.
Since
v
is smooth and subharmonic,
\Delta v
is a non-negative, continuous function, and defines a positive measure
2\pi\mu=\Delta v\,m
. Therefore

(3.28)

\displaystyle v(w)=\int_{A_{R}}2\pi G_{R}(z,w)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}z)+h_{R}(w),

where

(3.29)

\displaystyle h_{R}(w):=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial A_{R}}v(z)\frac{\partial G_{R}}{\partial n_{z}}(z,w)\,\sigma(\mathrm{d}z)

(3.30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial A_{R}}v(z)\,\nu(w,A_{R})(\mathrm{d}z)

is the harmonic part.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

By the maximum principle, we have

(3.31)

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in A_{R}}h_{R}(w)\leq\max_{z\in\partial A_{R}}v(z).

The harmonic measure of the annulus
A_{R}
can be computed explicitly from
G_{R}
above, but we have no need for that.
We only require a basic well-known bound on the density of the harmonic measure, namely that it is controlled by the inverse distance to
\partial A_{R}
.

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha\in U_{j},\text{ and }\theta+\ell_{2}\alpha\in(-(n-1)\alpha-U_{j}).

Since
\theta\in(\Theta_{c^{\prime},b})^{c}
, for
k

\displaystyle\|2\theta+k\alpha\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\frac{c^{\prime}}{|k|^{b}}.

Using that

, we infer that

\displaystyle|U_{j}|\geq\|\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha-(-\theta-\ell_{2}\alpha-(n-1)\alpha)\|_{\mathbb{T}}=\|2\theta+(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+n-1)\alpha\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\frac{\tilde{c}}{n^{b}},

for some constant
\tilde{c}
depending on
c^{\prime}
. This contradicts with (
6.102
) again.
Thus the claimed results hold.
∎

Next, we show the following.

Lemma 6.6
.

For any
\ell\in I_{1}
, one has
\theta+\ell\alpha\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{N^{\prime\prime}}(U_{j}\cup(-(n-1)\alpha-U_{j}))

Proof.

Argue by contradiction.
Suppose there exists
\ell_{1}\in I_{1}
such that

\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha\notin\bigcup_{j=1}^{N^{\prime\prime}}(U_{j}\cup(-(n-1)\alpha-U_{j})).

By Lemma
6.2
, it is necessary that

, which implies

(6.106)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log|D_{n}(\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha)|=u_{n}(e^{2\pi i(\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha)})\geq L_{n}-n^{-\gamma_{2}/2}\geq L-\varepsilon,

where we used that
L_{n}\geq L
due to Theorem
2.4
.
Let
\ell_{2}:=\ell_{1}+n-1
.
By Lemma
2.3
, Theorem
2.4
and (
2.14
), we see that for
k>k(\varepsilon)
large enough

(6.107)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\log|D_{k}(\theta)|=u_{k}(e^{i\theta})\leq v_{k}(e^{i\theta})\leq L_{k}+C\frac{(\log k)^{C_{0}}}{k}\leq L+\varepsilon.

Combining (
6.106
), (
6.107
) with (
2.19
),

\displaystyle|\phi_{0}|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|D_{\ell_{2}}(\theta+\alpha)|}{|D_{n}(\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha)|}|\phi_{\ell_{1}-1}|+\frac{|D_{-\ell_{1}}(\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha)|}{|D_{n}(\theta+\ell_{1}\alpha)}|\phi_{\ell_{2}+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\ell_{1}(L-20\varepsilon)}|\phi_{\ell_{1}-1}|+e^{-\ell_{2}(L-20\varepsilon)}|\phi_{\ell_{2}+1}|

(6.108)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{\ell_{1}(L-20\varepsilon)}|\ell_{1}|+Ce^{-\ell_{2}(L-20\varepsilon)}|\ell_{2}|<\frac{1}{2},

invoking (
6.101
) and
\min(|\ell_{1}|,|\ell_{2}|)\geq[n/8]
.
Similarly, one shows that
|\phi_{-1}|<1/2
.
Hence we arrive at a contradiction with the assumption that
\max(|\phi_{0}|,|\phi_{-1}|)=1

∎

.

There exists
