Set-theoretical solutions of simplex equations
V. Bardakov, B. Chuzinov, I. Emel’yanenkov, M. Ivanov, T. Kozlovskaya, and V. Leshkov

Abstract
. The n n -simplex equation ( n n -SE) was introduced by A. B. Zamolodchikov as a generalization of the Yang–Baxter equation, which is the 2 2 -simplex equation in these terms. In the present paper we suggest some general approaches to constructing solutions of n n -simplex equations, describe some types of solutions, introduce an operation which under some conditions allows us to construct a solution of ( n + m + k ) (n+m+k) -SE from solution of ( n + k ) (n+k) -SE and ( m + k ) (m+k) -SE. We consider the tropicalization of rational solutions and discuss a way to generalize it. We prove that if a group G G is an extension of a group H H by a group K K , then we can find a solution of the n n -SE on G G from solutions of this equation on H H and on K K . Also, we find solutions of the parametric Yang-Baxter equation on H H with parameters in K K . For studying solutions of the 3-simplex equations we introduce algebraic systems with ternary operations and give examples of these systems which gives solutions of the 3 3 -SE. We find all elementary verbal solutions of the 3 3 -SE on free groups. Keywords: Yang-Baxter equation, tetrahedral equation, n n -simplex equation, set theoretical solution, groupoid, 2-groupoid, ternar, ternoid, group extension, virtual braid group. Mathematics Subject Classification 2010: 08A02, 16T25, 17A42, 20F36, 20F29.

Introduction

A solution of the quantum Yang–Baxter equation (YBE) is a linear map
R:V\otimes V\to V\otimes V
satisfying

(1.0.1)

R_{12}R_{13}R_{23}=R_{23}R_{13}R_{12},

where
V
is a vector space and
R_{ij}:V\otimes V\otimes V\to V\otimes V\otimes V
acts as
R
on the
i
-th and
j
-th tensor factors and as the identity on the remaining factor.
V. M. Bukhshtaber
[
8
]
called the map
R
, which satisfies the YBE, a Yang-Baxter map.

The Yang-Baxter equation, or the 2-simplex equation, or the triangle equation, is one of the basic equations in mathematical physics and in low dimension topology.
It lies in the foundation of the theory of quantum groups, solvable models of statistical mechanics, knot theory and braid theory.
At first, the YBE appears in the paper of C. N. Yang
[
41
]
on the many-body problem. Later R. J. Baxter
[
7
]
introduced this equation for the study of solvable vertex models in statistical mechanics as the condition of commuting transfer matrices. Another derivation of the YBE follows from the factorization of the
S
-matrix in a
1+1
dimensional Quantum Field Theory (see papers of A. B. Zamolodchikov
[
42
,
43
]
). The YBE is also essential in Quantum Inverse Scattering Method for integrable systems
[
39
,
40
]
.

The Yang-Baxter equation is equivalent to a system of
n^{6}
algebraic cubic equations for
n^{4}
variables, where
n=\dim V
. So even in the two-dimensional case, one has 64 equations for 16 variables. In
[
28
]
(see also
[
19
]
) one can find a complete description of solutions for the YBE in the case
n=2
.

V. G. Drinfeld
[
14
]
suggested studying set-theoretical solutions, i.e. solutions for which the vector space
V
is spanned by a set
X
, and
R
is the linear operator induced by a permutation
R:X\times X\to X\times X
. In this case, we say that
(X,R)
is a set-theoretical solution of the Yang–Baxter equation or simply a solution of the YBE. Set-theoretical solutions have connections for example with groups of I-type, Bieberbach groups, bijective 1-cocycles, Garside theory, etc. Involutive set-theoretical solutions for the YBE were studied in
[
16
]
.

It is easy to see that for any
X
the map
P(x,y)=(y,x)
gives a set-theoretical solution of the YBE. On the other side, if
R
is a solution of the YBE, then the map
S=PR
satisfies the braid relation

(S\times\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\times S)(S\times\mathrm{id})=(\mathrm{id}\times S)(S\times\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\times S).

The braid relation is a relation in the braid group
B_{n}
. Topologically, the braid relation is simply the third Reidemeister move of planar diagrams of links.
In 1980s, D. Joyce
[
21
]
and S. V. Matveev
[
33
]
introduced quandles as invariants of knots and links, and proved that any quandle gives an elementary set-theoretical solution to the braid equation.
A lot of papers is dedicated to set-theoretical solution of the Yang-Baxter equation and the braid equation (see, for example
[
8
,
15
,
31
]
).

In
[

]
solutions of a parametric YBE were constructed. In
[

there was suggested a method to construct linear parametric solutions to the YBE from non-linear Darboux transformations of some Lax operators.

In 2006, W. Rump introduced
[

36
]
braces to study involutive set-theoretical solutions to the Yang–Baxter equation, although the same algebraic objects were already considered by A. G. Kurosh

If

The solution

The tetrahedron equation is popular in electric nets. In particular, the famous transformation ‘star-triangle’ gives a rational solution to the tetrahedron equation. Also, the tetrahedron equation is used in the theory of 2-dimensional knots in a 4-sphere

The TE arrives in a problem of coloring 2-faces of a 4-dimensional cube by elements of some set

2.
Preliminaries and some basic results

In this section we give necessary preliminaries. We recall examples of solutions to the YBE, describe a connection of solutions with some groupoids (racks, quandles) and recall definitions of the braid group and the virtual braid group.

2.1.
Yang–Baxter equations and racks

The following lemma is evident.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set and
f,g:X^{2}\to X
are two maps. The map
R:X^{2}\to X^{2}
,
R(x,y)=(f(x,y),g(x,y))
is a solution of the YBE if and only if for any
x,y,z\in X
the following identities hold

\displaystyle f\left(f(x,y),z\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(f(x,g(y,z)),f(y,z)\right),

\displaystyle f\left(g(x,y),g(f(x,y),z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\left(f(x,g(y,z)),f(y,z)\right),

\displaystyle g\left(g(x,y),g(f(x,y),z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\left(x,g(y,z)\right).

Example 2.2
.

(1)

Let
A
be an abelian group and
a,b\in A
, then

R(x,y)=(x+a,y+b),~~x,y\in A,

is a solution of the YBE on
A
.

(2)

For arbitrary group
G
the map

R(x,y)=(xy^{2},xy^{-1}x^{-1}),~~x,y\in G,

is a solution of the YBE on
G
.

(3)

Let
G
be a group and
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
be its automorphism, then the map

R(x,y)=(\varphi(x),xy\varphi(x)^{-1}),~~x,y\in G,

is a solution of the YBE on
G
. It is easy to see that if the map
\varphi(x)=xy^{2}
is an automorphism of
G
, we get the solution from the previous example.

A groupoid is a non-empty set with one binary algebraic operation,
if
k
is a natural number,
k>1
, then a
k
-groupoid is a non-empty set with
k
binary algebraic operations.
Suppose that
(X,R)
is a set-theoretical solution of the YBE.
Writing
R(x,y)=(\sigma_{y}(x),\tau_{x}(y))
for
x,y\in X
, we say that the solution
R
is
non-degenerate
if
\sigma_{x}
and
\tau_{x}
are invertible for all
x\in X
, we says that the solution
R
is
square-free
if
R(x,x)=(x,x)
for all
x\in X
.

A
quandle
is a groupoid
Q
with a binary operation
(x,y)\mapsto x*y
satisfying the following axioms:

(Q1)

x*x=x
for all
x\in Q
,

(Q2)

for any
x,y\in Q
there exists a unique
z\in Q
such that
x=z*y
,

(Q3)

(x*y)*z=(x*z)*(y*z)
for all
x,y,z\in Q
.

If a groupoid satisfies (Q3) it is called a self-distributive groupoid, if it satisfies
(Q2) and (Q3) it is called a
rack
. Many interesting examples of quandles come from groups showing deep connection with group theory.

Example 2.3
.

(1)

If
G
is a group, then the binary operation
a*b=b^{-1}ab
turns
G
into the quandle
\operatorname{Conj}(G)
called the
conjugation quandle
of
G
.

(2)

A group
G
with the binary operation
a*b=ba^{-1}b
turns the set
G
into the quandle
\operatorname{Core}(G)
called the
core quandle
of
G
. In particular, if
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the cyclic group of order
n
, then it is called the
dihedral quandle
and denoted by
\operatorname{R}_{n}
.

(3)

Let
G
be a group and
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. Then the set
G
with binary operation
a*b=\phi(ab^{-1})b
forms a quandle
\operatorname{Alex}(G,\phi)
referred as the
generalized Alexander quandle
of
G
with respect to
\phi
.

A quandle
Q
is called
trivial
if
x*y=x
for all
x,y\in Q
. Unlike groups, a trivial quandle can have arbitrary number of elements. We denote the
n
-element trivial quandle by
\operatorname{T}_{n}
and an arbitrary trivial quandle by
\operatorname{T}
.

Notice that the axioms (Q2) and (Q3) are equivalent to the map
S_{x}:Q\to Q
given by

S_{x}(y)=y*x

being an automorphism of
Q
for each
x\in Q
. These automorphisms are called
inner automorphisms
, and the group generated by all such automorphisms is denoted by
\operatorname{Inn}(X)
. A quandle is said to be
connected
if it admits a transitive action by its group of inner automorphisms. For example, dihedral quandles of odd order are connected, whereas that of even order are disconnected. A quandle
X
is called
involutive
if
S_{x}^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{Q}
for each
x\in Q
. For example, all core quandles are involutive.

For a solution
(X,R)
,
R(x,y)=(\sigma_{y}(x),\tau_{x}(y))
, we can define a 2-groupoid
(X;\cdot,*)
with the operations
x\cdot y=\sigma_{y}(x)
and
y*x=\tau_{x}(y)
. If
\sigma_{y}=\mathrm{id}
for all
y\in X
or
\tau_{x}=\mathrm{id}
for all
x\in X
, then
the solution
(X,R)
is called by
elementary solution
. Any elementary solution defines a structure of self-distributive groupoid on
X
, if the elementary solution is non-degenerate, then this groupoid is a rack. On the other side, any self-distributive groupoid gives an elementary solution to the YBE and any rack gives a non-degenerate solution to the YBE, any quandle defines a non-degenerate square-free solution of the YBE.

Connections of solutions with 2-groupoids gives the next lemma, which follows from Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(X,\cdot,*)
be a 2-groupoid and
R:X\times X\to X\times X
given by
R(x,y)=(x\cdot y,y*x)
for
x,y\in X
. Then the following hold:

(1)

The pair
(X,R)
is a set-theoretical solution of the Yang–Baxter equation if and only if the equalities

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)\cdot z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\cdot(z*y))\cdot(y\cdot z),

\displaystyle(y*x)\cdot(z*(x\cdot y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y\cdot z)*(x\cdot(z*y)),

\displaystyle(z*(x\cdot y))*(y*x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(z*y)*x,

hold for all
x,y,z\in X
.

(2)

If
x\cdot y=y
for all
x,y\in X
, then the pair
(X,R)
is a set-theoretic solution of the Yang–Baxter equation if and only if the operation
*
is (right) self-distributive, i.e.

(z*x)*(y*x)=(z*y)*x

for all
x,y,z\in X
.

(3)

If
y*x=y
for all
x,y\in X
, then the pair
(X,R)
is a set-theoretic solution of the Yang–Baxter equation if and only if the operation
\cdot
is (right) self-distributive, i.e.

(z\cdot y)\cdot x=(z\cdot x)\cdot(y\cdot x)

for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Example 2.5
.

Let us define on the set of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
a binary operation

a*b=\max\{a,b\},~~~a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
(\mathbb{R},*)
is a self-distributive groupoid, which is not a rack, but any element
a\in\mathbb{R}
is idempotent, that is
a*a=a
, and it is commutative groupoid. The map
R(x,y)=(x,y*x)
gives a degenerate solution of the YBE on
\mathbb{R}
. If we take
\min
instead of
\max
we get a similar result.

A. Soloviev
[
37
, Theorem 2.3]
(see also L. Guarnieri, L. Vendramin
[
18
, Proposition 3.7]
and D. Bachiller
[
1

) proves that any non-degenerate solution of the YBE conjugates to an elementary solution.

Proposition 2.6
.

If
R(x,y)=(\sigma_{y}(x),\tau_{x}(y))
,
x,y\in X
, gives a left non-degenerate (i.e.
\sigma_{y}
is a bijection for any
y\in X
)
solution to the YBE, then it conjugates to an elementary solution:

R^{\prime}(x,y)=(\sigma_{x}(\tau_{\sigma^{-1}_{y}(x)}(y)),y).

If for all
a,b\in X
there exists a unique
x\in X
such that

(2.1.1)

\tau_{\sigma^{-1}_{x}(a)}(x)=\sigma^{-1}_{a}(b),

then this solution is non-degenerate.

2.2.
Braid group and virtual braid group

For
n\geq 2
the braid group
B_{n}
on
n
strands is defined as
the group generated by
(n-1)
elements

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}

with defining relations

(2.2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},

\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n-2,

(2.2.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i},

\displaystyle|i-j|\geq 2.

The
virtual braid group

VB_{n}
on
n
strands, introduced in
[
25
]
, is the group with the generators
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{n-1}
,
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots,\rho_{n-1}
, which is defined by the relations (
2.2.1
)-(
2.2.2
) and additional relations

(2.2.3)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1}\rho_{i}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i+1}\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1},

\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n-2,

(2.2.4)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=\rho_{j}\rho_{i},

\displaystyle|i-j|\geq 2,

(2.2.5)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n-1,

(2.2.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=\rho_{j}\sigma_{i},

\displaystyle|i-j|\geq 1,

(2.2.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i+1}\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1},

\displaystyle i=1,2,\ldots,n-2.

The elements
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{n-1}
generate a subgroup that is isomorphic to the braid group
B_{n}
. Relations (
2.2.3
)-(
2.2.5
) are defining relations of the symmetric group
S_{n}

n-1&\vline&&&&\\

The equation

where

Proposition 3.1
.

If

is a vector space and

is a linear map that is a solution of the quantum
{n}
-SE, and there is a map

The next proposition is a generalization of well-known result for the YBE.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

{n}
-SE.

(1)

If

-SE.

(2)

{n}
-SE.

(3)

If

\varphi^{\times n}\,R\,(\varphi^{-1})^{\times n}

{n}
-SE.

Proof.

(1) If we take inverse on both sides of
{n}
-SE, we get that

(2) It is easy to see that all functions

derived from such

Thus the left side of the

(3) Put

\widetilde{R}_{\bar{k}}=\varphi^{\times n}_{\bar{k}}\,R_{\bar{k}}\,({\varphi^{-1}})^{\times n}_{\bar{k}}=\varphi^{\times N}\,R_{\bar{k}}\,({\varphi^{-1}})^{\times N}

After cancellations we obtain an equation

\varphi^{\times N}{R}_{\overline{1}}R_{\overline{2}}\cdots R_{\overline{n+1}}({\varphi^{-1}})^{\times N}=\varphi^{\times N}R_{\overline{n+1}}\cdots R_{\overline{2}}R_{\overline{1}}\ ({\varphi^{-1}})^{\times N}.

To formulate the main result, we introduce some definitions and notations.

1
or all its coefficients are equal to

and the free term is equal to zero.
A rational solution

R(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}),~~~r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),

of the
{n}
-SE is said to be an
I
–rational solution
if all components
r_{i}
lie in
I_{n}
.

Example 5.1
.

It is easy to see that the so called
electric solution
of the TE,

\displaystyle R_{E}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{xy}{x+z+xyz},\ x+z+xyz,\ \frac{yz}{x+z+xyz}\right)

that appears in the theory of electric circuits
as the famous Y-
\Delta
transform of an electric network is an
I
–rational solution.

The map

\displaystyle R_{e}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{xy}{x+z},\ x+z,\ \frac{yz}{x+z}\right)

which is obtained from
R_{E}
by removing terms of degree three is also an
I
–rational solution.

Let us denote by
PL_{n}
the set of piecewise linear functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 5.2
.

The
tropicalization
is a map
(\cdot)^{t}:I_{n}\to PL_{n}
that is defined on
r=f/g\in I_{n}
, where

f=\sum_{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}>0}\alpha_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{i_{n}},~~~g=\sum_{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}\geq 0}\beta_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}x_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{j_{n}}

by the rule

r^{t}=\begin{cases}\underset{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}>0}{\max}\{i_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}x_{n}\}-\underset{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}>0}{\max}\{j_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}x_{n}\},&\text{for $g\not=1$;}\\
\underset{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}>0}{\max}\{i_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+i_{n}x_{n}\},&\text{for $g=1$.}\end{cases}

Note that
r^{t}
can be obtained from
r
using the following recursive procedure.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
r=r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
r_{1}=r_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
r_{2}=r_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be rational functions from
I_{n}
.
Then

(1)

if
r=x_{i}
, then
r^{t}=x_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
;

(2)

(r_{1}+r_{2})^{t}=\max\{r^{t}_{1},\ r^{t}_{2}\}
;

(3)

(r_{1}r_{2})^{t}=r_{1}^{t}+r_{2}^{t}
;

(4)

\left(\dfrac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)^{t}=r_{1}^{t}-r_{2}^{t}
.

In particular, tropicalization is correctly defined i.e. if
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are two equal rational functions, then their tropicalizations are also equal functions.

Let
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n})\in(I_{n})^{n}
be a rational vector-valued map of
n
variables.
Define the
tropicalization
of the rational map
R
componentwise:

R^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=(r_{1}^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,r_{n}^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

Example 5.4
.

The tropicalization of the electric solution
R_{E}
from Example
5.1
is

R_{E}^{t}(x,y,z)=(x+y-\max\{x,z,x+y+z\},\ \max\{x,z,x+y+z\},\ y+z-\max\{x,z,x+y+z\}).

The tropicalization of
R_{e}
from Example
5.1
is

R_{e}^{t}(x,y,z)=(x+y-\max\{x,z\},\ \max\{x,z\},\ y+z-\max\{x,z\}).

It is easy to see that
R_{E}^{t}
and
R_{e}^{t}
are piecewise linear solutions of the TE.

Remark 5.5
.

The solution
R_{E}^{t}
consists of 3 linear pieces:

R_{1}(x,y,z)=(y,x,y+z-x),

R_{2}(x,y,z)=(x+y-z,z,y),

R_{3}(x,y,z)=(-z,x+y+z,-x).

We can consider these maps as maps on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. One can check that
(\mathbb{R},R_{1})
and
(\mathbb{R},R_{2})
are solutions of the TE, but
(\mathbb{R},R_{3})

is not.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be two rational functions in
\mathbb{R}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and

1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. A
k
-
composition
of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
is a rational function
r_{1}\circ_{k}r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
defined as

(r_{1}\circ_{k}r_{2})(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=r_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},r_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Proposition 5.7
.

If
r_{1},r_{2}\in I_{n}
, then

(r_{1}\circ_{k}r_{2})^{t}=r_{1}^{t}\circ_{k}r_{2}^{t}
.

Proof.

The proof uses the recursive procedure given in Proposition
5.3
.
If
r_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{i}
is a variable, then the statement is obvious.
If

r_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})*g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),

where
f
and
g
are rational functions in
I_{n}

and
*~\in~\{+,\cdot,/\}
is a binary operation in rational function
r_{1}
, then the tropicalization

r^{t}_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})*^{t}g^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),

where
*^{t}~\in~\{\text{max},+,-\}
, and

(r_{1}\circ_{k}r_{2})(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(f\circ_{k}r_{2})(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})*(g\circ_{k}r_{2})(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Hence, the tropicalization

(r_{1}\circ_{k}r_{2})^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(f\circ_{k}r_{2})^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})*^{t}(g\circ_{k}r_{2})^{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

The needed statement could be obtained by induction on a recursion depth.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

Tropicalization preserves a composition of rational vector-valued maps of the form described above.

Theorem 5.9
.

If
(\mathbb{R}_{>0},R)
,
R\in(I_{n})^{n}
is a solution of the
n
-simplex equation on the set of positive real numbers,
then its tropicalization
(\mathbb{R}_{>0},R^{t})
is also a solution of the
n
-simplex equation.

Proof.

For any
n\geqslant 2
, the
n
-SE has the following form

R_{\overline{1}}R_{\overline{2}}\cdots R_{\overline{n+1}}=R_{\overline{n+1}}\cdots R_{\overline{2}}R_{\overline{1}}.

Apply the tropicalization procedure to both sides:

(R_{\overline{1}}R_{\overline{2}}\cdots R_{\overline{n+1}})^{t}=(R_{\overline{n+1}}\cdots R_{\overline{2}}R_{\overline{1}})^{t}.

Hence, according to Corollary
5.8
, the following relation holds:

R_{\overline{1}}^{t}R_{\overline{2}}^{t}\cdots R_{\overline{n+1}}^{t}=R_{\overline{n+1}}^{t}\cdots R_{\overline{2}}^{t}R_{\overline{1}}^{t}.

It means that
R^{t}
is a solution of the
n
-simplex equation.
∎

5.2.
Formal tropicalization of solutions on algebraic systems

The reasonable question that arises is whether we can make a replacement, analogous to a tropicalization, in solutions of the
{n}
-SE such that we again obtain a solution.
Obviously this question has a positive answer.
Such substitutions of functions in formulas of solutions that transform solutions into solutions we will call formal tropicalizations.
In order to give more formal definition we shall recall the notion of a partial algebra.
A partial algebra is a generalization of universal algebra that allows partial operations
[
17
, ch. 2]
.
A natural example of partial algebra is a field, a multiplicative inverse is not defined for element
0
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a partial algebra of signature
\Sigma
and
R:A^{n}\to A^{n}
be a partial function that can be
expressed as a realization of a collection
(f_{1},...\,,f_{n})
of
n
-ary functions–terms of a signature

\Sigma^{\prime}\subset\Sigma
such that
(A,R)
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a partial algebra of a signature
\Xi
,
and
g
is a mapping that match every
n
-ary functional symbol from
\Sigma^{\prime}
with
n
-ary function of the signature
\Xi
.
Then for every
f_{i}
we can define
g(f_{i})
as
n
-ary function of the signature
\Xi
where every functional symbol from

\Sigma^{\prime}
is replaced with corresponding
n
-ary function of the signature
\Xi
.
The function
R^{g}:B^{n}\to B^{n}
that is realization of a collection
(g(f_{1}),...\,,g(f_{n}))
will be called a

formal tropicalization
of the solution
R
if
(B,R^{g})
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE.

The sentence “
(A,R)
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE” could be understood as a system of
N
equations
\Phi_{R}
with
N
variables such that for any interpretation of variables every equation is either true or at least one side is undefined.
This is just an explicit form of the
{n}
-SE.

Those equations can be viewed as formulas of equational logic.
For some survey of equational logic in the context of algebras one can see
[
17
, appendix 4]
.

Following the notation above, let
T
be a set of equational formulas of signature
\Sigma^{\prime}
such that

\mathcal{A}
satisfies
T
and every formula
\Phi_{R}
has finite proof starting from
T
.
These formulae will be called
axioms
of the solutions
(A,R)
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
(A,R)
be a solution of the
{n}
-SE and
T
its set of axioms.
If
g
is a mapping such that
\mathcal{B}
satisfies
g(T):=\{g(\phi):\phi\in T\}

then
(B,R^{g})
is a formal tropicalization of the solution
(A,R)
, i.e. it is a solution of the
{n}
-SE.

Proof.

It is easy to see that the set
g(\Phi_{R})
coincide with the set
\Phi_{R^{g}}
.
Now since
\Phi_{R}
is derivable from
T
the set
g(\Phi_{R})
is derivable from
g(T)
.
From these two facts one can conclude that
\mathcal{B}
satisfies
\Phi_{R^{g}}
.
∎

Example 5.12
.

Let us consider the case of the tropicalization of rational functions described above.
We can interpret rational solution as a solution in algebraic system
(X,\cdot^{2},/^{2},+^{2},1^{0})
.
All those rational solutions can be proven from the following set of axioms:

\begin{gathered}a\cdot b=b\cdot a;\qquad a+b=b+a;\\
a\cdot(b\cdot c)=(a\cdot b)\cdot c;\qquad a+(b+c)=(a+b)+c;\qquad a\cdot(b+c)=a\cdot b+a\cdot c;\\
a/b=a\cdot(1/b);\qquad(1/a)\cdot(1/b)=1/(a\cdot b);\qquad a\cdot 1=a;\qquad a/1=a;\qquad a/a=1.\end{gathered}

Thus we can construct mapping
g
to signature of any algebraic system that meet those axioms.
One of such algebraic systems is
(X,+^{2},-^{2},\max^{2},0^{0}).

Note that as long as we consider multiplication by constant natural number as iterated operation of addition we can use the same
mapping
g
for rational functions with natural coefficients,
and with some formal accuracy even for rational functions with positive rational coefficients.

Corollary 5.13
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},R)
be a solution of the
{n}
-SE and
\mathcal{B}
be some subsystem of an algebraic system
\mathcal{A}

in the same class defined by the signature
\Sigma
.
Then
(\mathcal{B},R)
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE as well.

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},R)
be a solution of the
{n}
-SE and
\mathcal{B}
be a homomorphic image of an algebraic system

\mathcal{A}
under the homomorphism
h
.
Then
(\mathcal{B},R^{h})
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE.

One of the advantage of formal tropicalization is that it allows us to see solutions of the
{n}
-SE as some templates rather than fixed functions.
Consider the following example.

Example 5.15
.

We know that

(x,y)\mapsto(ax,by+(1-ab)x),\text{ where }a,b\in\mathbb{Z}

is a solution of the YBE over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
\mathbb{Z}
as a free left
\mathbb{Z}
-module is generic for the theory of left modules over commutative rings,
we can see that for any commutative ring
R

(x,y)\mapsto(ax,by+(1-ab)x),\text{ where }a,b\in R

is a solution of the YBE over any left
R
-module.

From the Proposition
5.14
we obtain the following result.

Corollary 5.16
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},R)
be a solution of the
{n}
-SE and
T
its set of axioms.
Consider the class
\mathfrak{K}
of algebraic systems defined by the subset of axioms
T^{\prime}\subset T
that contain variables.
For any system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathfrak{K}
if there is homomorphism

h:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
then
(\mathcal{B},R^{h})
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE.

Example 5.17
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be the class of commutative rings with partial operation of multiplicative inverse.
Consider a rational solution
(\mathbb{R},R)
of the
{n}
-SE of the form
(R_{1},...\,,R_{n})
with components

R_{i}=\frac{\sum a_{j}\cdot x_{1}^{\alpha_{j,1}}\cdot...\cdot x_{n}^{\alpha_{j,n}}}{\sum b_{k}\cdot x_{1}^{\beta_{k,1}}\cdot...\cdot x_{n}^{\beta_{k,n}}},\text{ where }\alpha_{j,m},\beta_{k,m}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\text{ for all }j,k,m.

In the class
\mathfrak{R}
there is an obvious homomorphism
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}[\mathbb{R}^{m}]

from the field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers with standard operations of addition and multiplication
to the linear space
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}[\mathbb{R}^{m}]
of generalized functions with compact support
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the standard operation of addition and the convolution
*
as the multiplication.
This homomorphism acts by

r\mapsto r\cdot\delta\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}[\mathbb{R}^{m}],~~~r\in\mathbb{R},

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta.
Thus
(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}[\mathbb{R}^{m}],R^{h})
is a rational solution of the
{n}
-SE.
Components of the function
R^{h}
are

R^{h}_{i}=\left(\sum a_{j}\cdot x_{1}^{\alpha_{j,1}}*...*x_{n}^{\alpha_{j,n}}\right)\left(\sum b_{j}\cdot x_{1}^{\beta_{j,1}}*...*x_{n}^{\beta_{j,n}}\right)^{-1},

where powers are repeated convolutions and the inverse is taken with respect to the convolution.

6.
Solutions of the parametric YBE

6.1.
Group extensions and the YBE

Suppose there exists a group extension

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

and a section
\varphi:K\to G
that is a map such that
j\circ\varphi=\mathrm{id}_{K}
.

In
[
34
]
was prove that if
K
is abelian, then the conjugacy quandle
Conj(G)
defines a solution of the parametric YBE,

R_{12}^{a,b}R_{13}^{a,c}R_{23}^{b,c}=R_{23}^{b,c}R_{13}^{a,c}R_{12}^{a,b},~~~a,b,c\in K,

on
H
with parameters in
K
.

In this subsection we generalize this result. To do it we recall some facts on group extensions. We will
present elements of
G
as the set of pairs
(x,a)\in H\times K
and assume that
i(x)=(x,1)
and
j((x,a))=a
. Then the multiplication on
G
is defined by the rule

(x,a)\circ(y,b)=(x\underset{a,b}{\circ}y,a\circ b)~~\mbox{for some}~x\underset{a,b}{\circ}y\in H,

where
a\circ b
is the multiplication on
K
. For the multiplication on
H
we use the same symbol
\circ
. These agree with the formula

(x,1)\circ(y,1)=(x\circ y,1).

Hence, we can consider
H
as algebraic system with the set of binary algebraic operations

\{\underset{a,b}{\circ}~|~a,b\in K\}.

From group axioms follows that these operations satisfies the following axioms

(1)

for any
x\in H
and
a\in K
there is unique element
x_{a,a^{-1}}^{-1}=x_{a^{-1},a}^{-1}\in H
such that

x_{a^{-1},a}^{-1}\underset{a^{-1},a}{\circ}x=x\underset{a,a^{-1}}{\circ}x_{a,a^{-1}}^{-1}=1;

(2)

for any
x,y,z\in H
and
a,b,c\in K
holds

(x\underset{a,b}{\circ}y)\underset{a\circ b,c}{\circ}z=x\underset{a,b\circ c}{\circ}(y\underset{b,c}{\circ}z).

Now we generalize this construction to the groups with a structure of a right distributive groupoid.
Suppose, that on the set
G
is defined a binary algebraic operation
*:G\times G\to G
such that
(G,*)
is a right distributive groupoid, the set
H
is closed under multiplication
*
and this multiplication defines a right distributive groupoid on
K
. As we know, the map

R(g,h)=(g,h*g),~~g,h\in G,

defines a solution of the YBE on
G
. For example, in
[
34
]
the operation
*
is
a*b=b^{-1}ab
,
a,b\in G
, it means that
(G,*)
is a conjugation quandle.

If we present elements of
G
as the set of pairs
(x,a)\in H\times K
, then

(x,a)*(y,b)=(x\underset{a,b}{*}y,a*b)~~\mbox{for some}~x\underset{a,b}{*}y\in H.

Hence, on
H
we have operation
*
and a set of operations
\{\underset{a,b}{*}~|~a,b\in K\}
. From the right distributivity of
*
follows

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
x,y,z\in H
and
a,b,c\in K
holds

(x\underset{a,b}{*}y)\underset{a*b,c}{*}z=(x\underset{a,c}{*}z)\underset{a*c,b*c}{*}(y\underset{b,c}{*}z).

Now we are ready to prove

Proposition 6.2
.

For any
a,b,c\in K
the following equality

R_{12}^{a,b}\,R_{13}^{a,c*b}\,R_{23}^{b,c}=R_{23}^{b*a,c*a}\,R_{13}^{a,c}\,R_{12}^{a,b}

holds in
H
, where

R^{u,v}(x,y)=(x,y\underset{v,u}{*}x),~~u,v\in K.

Proof.

Since
(G,R)
is a solution of the YBE, then for any
g,h,k\in G
holds

R_{12}\,R_{13}\,R_{23}(g,h,k)=R_{23}\,R_{13}\,R_{12}(g,h,k).

Let
g=(x,a)
,
h=(y,b)
,
k=(z,c)
. Then the left side,

R_{12}\,R_{13}\,R_{23}((x,a),(y,b),(z,c))=R_{12}\,R_{13}((x,a),(y,b),(z,c)*(y,b))=

=R_{12}\,R_{13}\left((x,a),(y,b),(z\underset{c,b}{*}y,c*b)\right)=R_{12}\left((x,a),(y,b),((z\underset{c,b}{*}y)\underset{c*b,a}{*}x,(c*b)*a)\right)=

=\left((x,a),(y\underset{b,a}{*}x,b*a),((z\underset{c,b}{*}y)\underset{c*b,a}{*}x,(c*b)*a)\right).

The right side,

R_{23}\,R_{13}\,R_{12}((x,a),(y,b),(z,c))=R_{23}\,R_{13}\left((x,a),(y\underset{b,a}{*}x,b*a),(z,c)\right)=

=R_{23}\left((x,a),(y\underset{b,a}{*}x,b*a),(z\underset{c,a}{*}x,c*a)\right)=

=\left((x,a),(y\underset{b,a}{*}x,b*a),((z\underset{c,a}{*}x)\underset{c*a,b*a}{*}(y\underset{b,a}{*}x),(c*a)*(b*a))\right).

If we restrict the action of
R
on
H\times H
and put

R^{u,v}(x,y)=(x,y\underset{v,u}{*}x),~~u,v\in K,

then we get the need equality.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

If
(K,*)
is a trivial right distributive groupoid, i.e.
u*v=u
for any
u,v\in K
, then for any
a,b,c\in K
the following equality

R_{12}^{a,b}\,R_{13}^{a,c}\,R_{23}^{b,c}=R_{23}^{b,c}\,R_{13}^{a,c}\,R_{12}^{a,b}

holds in
H
.

Example 6.4
.

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

be a group extension with
K
is a group of exponent 2. As it is well known, in this case

,

(g_{1},k_{1})\circ(g_{2},k_{2})=(g_{1}\underset{k_{1},k_{2}}{\circ}g_{2},k_{1}\circ k_{2}),

[a,b,c]_{1}=f(a,b,c),~~~[a,b,c]_{2}=g(a,b,c),~~~[a,b,c]_{3}=h(a,b,c),~~~a,b,c\in X.

For simplicity, we shall denote the different operations by different type of brackets and write

[a,b,c]_{1}=[a,b,c],~~~[a,b,c]_{2}=\langle a,b,c\rangle,~~~[a,b,c]_{3}=\{a,b,c\}.

Using direct calculations we get

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot],\langle\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\{\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\})
be a 3-ternoid.
Then it defines a solution of the TE if and only if the following six equalities hold

\begin{gathered}~[[x,\langle y,t,\{z,p,q\}\rangle,\langle z,p,q\rangle],[y,t,\{z,p,q\}],[z,p,q]]=[[x,y,z],t,p],\\
\langle[x,\langle y,t,\{z,p,q\}\rangle,\langle z,p,q\rangle],[y,t,\{z,p,q\}],[z,p,q]\rangle=[\langle x,y,z\rangle,\langle[x,y,z],t,p\rangle,q],\\
\{[x,\langle y,t,\{z,p,q\}\rangle,\langle z,p,q\rangle],[y,t,\{z,p,q\}],[z,p,q]\}=\\
=[\{x,y,z\},\{[x,y,z],t,p\},\{\langle x,y,z\rangle,\langle[x,y,z],t,p\rangle,q\}],\\
\langle x,\langle y,t,\{z,p,q\}\rangle,\langle z,p,q\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle x,y,z\rangle,\langle[x,y,z],t,p\rangle,q\rangle,\\
\{x,\langle y,t,\{z,p,q\}\rangle,\langle z,p,q\rangle\}=\langle\{x,y,z\},\{[x,y,z],t,p\},\{\langle x,y,z\rangle,\langle[x,y,z],t,p\rangle,q\}\rangle,\\
\{y,t,\{z,p,q\}\}=\{\{x,y,z\},\{[x,y,z],t,p\},\{\langle x,y,z\rangle,\langle[x,y,z],t,p\rangle,q\}\}\end{gathered}

for all
(x,y,z,t,p,q)\in X^{6}
.

The next three corollaries describe elementary solutions of the TE.

Corollary 9.2
.

Let
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot])
be a ternar.
The map
R:X^{3}\to X^{3}
,

R(a,b,c)=([a,b,c],b,c),~~~a,b,c\in X,

satisfies the TE if and only if

[[x,t,p],[y,t,q],[z,p,q]]=[[x,y,z],t,p],~~\mbox{for all}~x,y,z,t,p,q\in X.

Corollary 9.3
.

Let
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot])
be a ternar.
The map
R:X^{3}\to X^{3}
,

R(a,b,c)=(a,[a,b,c],c),~~~a,b,c\in X.

satisfies the TE if and only if

(9.1.1)

[[x,y,z],[x,t,p],q]=[x,[y,t,q],[z,p,q]],~~\mbox{for all}~x,y,z,t,p,q\in X.

Corollary 9.4
.

Let
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot])
be a ternar.
The map
R:X^{3}\to X^{3}
,

R(a,b,c)=(a,b,[a,b,c]),~~~a,b,c\in X.

satisfies the TE if and only if

(9.1.2)

[[x,y,z],[x,t,p],[y,t,q]]=[y,t,[z,p,q]],~~\mbox{for all}~x,y,z,t,p,q\in X.

Remark 9.5
.

One can see that the identity in Corollary
9.3
is more symmetric than identities in Corollary
9.2
and in Corollary
9.4
. In particular, the number of variables in the left side of this identity is the same as in the right side. In two other identities these numbers are different.

We will call a solution from Corollary
9.2
an
elementary solution of the first type
or, simply elementary
1
-solution; a solution from Corollary
9.3
an
elementary solution of the second type
or simply elementary
2
-solution; a solution from Corollary
9.4
an
elementary solution of the third type
or, simply elementary
3
-solution.

Let
P_{13}:X^{3}\to X^{3}
,
P_{13}(x,y,z)=(z,y,x)
, be the permutation of the first and the third components. If
R
is an elementary 1-solution, then
P_{13}RP_{13}
is an elementary 3-solution. Hence, we have to study only elementary 1- and 2-solutions.

9.2.
Elementary 1-solutions

For studying elementary 1-solutions of the TE let us introduce
an algebraic system
(X,\mathbin{\overline{\ast}},\mathbin{\overline{\circ}},\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}},\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}})
with four binary operations which satisfy axioms:

\begin{gathered}x\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}y=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}}z)\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}}z);\\
(x\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}y)\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}(z\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}w)=(x\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}z)\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}w);\\
(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}y)\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}z=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}z)\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}z);\\
(x\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}y)\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}}z=x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}z).\end{gathered}

We call it first tetrahedral 4-groupoid and denote it by
T_{1}
-groupoid.

Proposition 9.6
.

T_{1}
-groupoid gives an elementary 1-solution
(X,R)
of the TE if we put

R(x,y,z)=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}z),\;y,\;z),~~~x,y,z\in X.

Proof.

In order for
R
to be a solution it shall satisfy equality from corollary
9.2
.
The equality then takes the form

(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}((y\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}q))\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}(z\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(p\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}q)))=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}z))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p).

Applying axioms to the left side of this equality we get

\begin{gathered}(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}((y\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}q))\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}(z\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(p\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}q)))=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(((y\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}t)\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}}q)\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}((z\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}p)\mathbin{\overline{\triangleleft}}q))=\\
=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}((y\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}t)\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}(z\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}p))=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}((y\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}z)\mathbin{\overline{\ast}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p))=(x\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(y\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}z))\mathbin{\overline{\triangleright}}(t\mathbin{\overline{\circ}}p).\end{gathered}

∎

There are 2-groupoids, that give elementary 1-solutions.

Reduced

9.3.
Elementary 2-solutions

For studying elementary 2-solutions of the TE let us introduce an algebraic system
(X,\ast,\circ,\triangleleft,\triangleright)
with 4 binary operations which satisfies the following axioms:

\begin{gathered}x\triangleright(y\ast z)=(x\triangleright y)\ast(x\triangleright z),\\
(x\circ y)\triangleleft z=(x\triangleleft z)\circ(y\triangleleft z),\\
(x\ast y)\circ(z\ast w)=(x\circ z)\ast(y\circ w),\\
(x\triangleright y)\triangleleft z=x\triangleright(y\triangleleft z),\\
(x\ast y)\triangleleft z=x\triangleright(y\circ z).\end{gathered}

Further we shall call this algebraic system by second tetrahedral 4-groupoid and denote it by
T_{2}
-groupoid.

Proposition 9.10
.

Any
T_{2}
-groupoid
X
defines an elementary 2-solution
(X,R)
of the TE by the formula

R(x,y,z)=(x,\;x\triangleright(y\circ z),\;z),~~~x,y,z\in X.

Proof.

In order for
R
to be a solution it shall satisfy equality from Corollary
9.3
.
This equality then takes the form

x\triangleright((y\triangleright(t\circ q))\circ(z\triangleright(p\circ q)))=(x\triangleright(y\circ z))\triangleright((x\triangleright(t\circ p))\circ q).

Applying the axioms of the
T_{2}
-groupoid to the left side of this equality we get

\begin{gathered}x\triangleright((y\triangleright(t\circ q))\circ(z\triangleright(p\circ q)))=x\triangleright(((y\ast t)\triangleleft q)\circ((z\ast p)\triangleleft q))=\\
=x\triangleright(((y\ast t)\circ(z\ast p))\triangleleft q)=(x\triangleright((y\circ z)\ast(t\circ p)))\triangleleft q=\\
=((x\triangleright(y\circ z))\ast(x\triangleright(t\circ p)))\triangleleft q=(x\triangleright(y\circ z))\triangleright((x\triangleright(t\circ p))\circ q).\end{gathered}

∎

Now suppose that we have an elementary 2-solution
(X,R)
of the TE, where

R(x,y,z)=(x,[x,y,z],z),~~~x,y,z\in X.

We can formulate a question: Is it defines a
T_{2}
-groupoid ?

From the next proposition follows that under some assumptions on the ternar
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot])
the answer is positive.

Proposition 9.11
.

Suppose that a ternar
(X,[\cdot,\cdot,\cdot])
defines a solution of the TE, and there exist an element
c\in X
such that
[c,c,c]=c
, and a function
\{\cdot\}:X\to X
such that

\{[c,x,c]\}=[c,\{x\},c]=x,\quad[\{x\},\{y\},c]=\{[x,y,c]\}\quad\text{ and }\quad[c,\{x\},\{y\}]=\{[c,x,y]\}.

If we put

x\ast y=[x,y,c],\qquad x\circ y=[c,x,y],\qquad x\triangleright y=[x,\{y\},c],\qquad x\triangleleft y=[c,\{x\},y],

for any
x,y\in X
, then
(X,\ast,\circ,\triangleright,\triangleleft)
is a
T_{2}
-groupoid.

Proof.

It is easy to see that

\begin{gathered}(x\ast y)\circ(z\ast w)=[c,[x,y,c],[z,w,c]]=\qquad\qquad\\
\qquad\qquad=[[c,x,z],[c,y,w],c]=(x\circ z)\ast(y\circ w);\\
x\triangleright(y\ast z)=[x,\{[y,z,c]\},c]=[x,[\{y\},\{z\},c],[c,c,c]]=\qquad\qquad\\
\qquad\qquad=[[x,\{y\},c],[x,\{z\},c],c]=(x\triangleright y)\ast(x\triangleright z);\\
(x\circ y)\triangleleft z=[c,\{[c,x,y]\},z]=[[c,c,c],[c,\{x\},\{y\}],z]=\qquad\qquad\\
\qquad\qquad=[c,[c,\{x\},z],[c,\{y\},z]]=(x\triangleleft z)\circ(y\triangleleft z);\\
(x\triangleright y)\triangleleft z=[c,\{[x,\{y\},c]\},z]=\{[c,[x,\{y\},c],[c,z,c]]\}=\qquad\qquad\\
\qquad\qquad=\{[[c,x,c],[c,\{y\},z],c]\}=[x,\{[c,\{y\},z]\},c]=x\triangleright(y\triangleleft z);\\
x\triangleright(y\circ z)=[x,\{[c,y,z]\},c]=\{[[c,x,c],[c,y,z],c]\}=\qquad\qquad\\
\qquad\qquad=\{[c,[x,y,c],[c,z,c]]\}=[c,\{[x,y,c]\},z]=(x\ast y)\triangleleft z,\end{gathered}

it means that all axioms hold and we have a
T_{2}
-groupoid.
∎

Hence, using the operations, constructed in the last proposition, one can define the map

(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,\triangleright(y\circ z),z),~~~x,y,z\in X,

which gives a solution of the TE.

by the other words, is it true that
[x,y,z]=x\triangleright(y\circ z)
for all

10.
Verbal solutions

Let
G
be a group.
A
verbal solution

(G,R)
of the
{n}
-SE is a solution of the form

R(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n})=(w_{1}(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}),w_{2}(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}),\ldots,w_{n}(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n})),

where
w_{i}=w_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,
are reduced words in the free group
F_{n}=\langle x_{1},...\,,x_{n}\rangle
.
A verbal solution
R
of the
{n}
-SE is said to be verbal
elementary
k
-solution
if each word
w_{i}
is equal to
x_{i}
with the exception of
w_{k}
.

10.1.
Universal verbal solutions

If
(G,R)
is a verbal solution of the
{n}
-SE and
\varphi:G\to H
is a homomorphism, such that

(Ker(\varphi),R|_{Ker(\varphi)})
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE,
then it induces a solution
(\varphi(G),R^{\varphi})
, where

R^{\varphi}(\varphi(g_{1}),\varphi(g_{2}),\ldots,\varphi(g_{n}))=(\varphi(g^{\prime}_{1}),\varphi(g^{\prime}_{2}),\ldots,\varphi(g^{\prime}_{n})),

and

R(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n})=(g^{\prime}_{1},g^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,g^{\prime}_{n}).

As we know, for arbitrary group
G
there is a map
R:G^{2}\to G^{2}
what is an elementary solution to the YBE. For example, we can take some quandle on
G
, conjugacy quandle
Conj(G)
, or core quandle
Core(G)
and construct elementary solution on
G
. In general case, we can formulate

Question 10.1
.

Let
G
be a group. Is there a map
R:G^{n}\to G^{n}
,
n>2
, that gives a bijective non-trivial (elementary) solution to the
{n}
-SE?

By a trivial solution we mean a permutation of components.

In this subsection we are studying verbal elementary solutions of the TE on arbitrary group that is equivalent to study solutions on free non-abelian group
F_{6}
. At first recall some definitions from combinatorial group theory. We will consider
F_{n}
as the free product of
n
infinite cyclic groups, and present elements of
F_{n}
as reduced words

(10.1.1)

w=x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}},~~\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},~~i_{j}\not=i_{j+1}~\mbox{for}~j=1,2,\ldots,k-1.

Any subword
x_{i_{j}}^{\alpha_{j}}
is called a
syllable
. The number
k
is called the syllable length of
w
and is denoted by
l(w)
. The
i
-
syllable length
of
w
is the number of syllables which lie in
\langle x_{i}\rangle
, it is denoted by
l_{i}(w)
.
For example, if
w=x_{3}^{-5}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{-7}x_{1}x_{3}^{8}\in F_{3}
, then
l(w)=5
,
l_{1}(w)=l_{3}(w)=2
,
l_{2}(w)=1
.

Also we will use the cyclically reduced form of words. A word (
10.1.1
) is called
cyclically reduced
if
i_{1}\not=i_{k}
, or if
i_{1}=i_{k}
, then
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{k}>0
.
If
w
is not cyclically reduced, then
w\equiv u^{-1}w_{0}u
, where
w_{0}
is a cyclically reduced subword of
w
and
\equiv
means equality of words (letter by letter). Then
w^{m}\equiv u^{-1}w_{0}^{m}u
for any integer
m
. For example,
x_{3}^{-5}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{-7}x_{1}x_{3}^{8}=x_{3}^{-5}(x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{-7}x_{1}x_{3}^{3})x_{3}^{5}
and the subword
x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{-7}x_{1}x_{3}^{3}
is cyclically reduced.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a reduced word in the free group
F_{n}
, and
l_{j}(w)=k>0
for some
j
,
1\leq j\leq n
. Then
l_{j}(w^{m})\geq k
for any integer
m\neq 0
. Moreover, the first and the last symbols of
w
coincide with the first and the last symbols of
w^{m}
, respectively, for all positive integer
m
.

Proof.

We will assume that
m>0
. The proof for the case
m<0
is similar.

Suppose at first that
w
is cyclically reduced. If
l(w)=l_{j}(w)=1
, then
w=x_{j}^{\alpha}
for some non-zero integer
\alpha
and the need assertion is true. Suppose that
l(w)>1
and
w
has a form (
10.1.1
). If
i_{1}
or
i_{k}
is different from
j
, then
l_{j}(w^{m})=km
. If
i_{1}=i_{k}=j
,
then
k>1
and

l_{j}(w^{m})=km-(m-1)=m(k-1)+1.

Since
m\geq 2
, then
m(k-1)+1\geq 2(k-1)+1=2k-1\geq k
.

If
w
is not cyclically reduced, then
w\equiv u^{-1}w_{0}u
. If
k=l_{j}(w)=2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0})
, then
l_{j}(w^{m})=2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0}^{m})
. By the result above
l_{j}(w_{0}^{m})\geq l_{j}(w_{0})
. Hence,
l_{j}(w^{m})\geq 2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0})\geq k
. If
k=l_{j}(w)=2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0})-1
that it is possible if the last syllable of
u^{-1}
and the first syllable of
w_{0}
lies in
\langle x_{j}\rangle
, or the last syllable of
w_{0}
and the first syllable of
u
lies in
\langle x_{j}\rangle
(notice that both possibilities can not be realized). In this case

l_{j}(w^{m})=2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0}^{m})-1\geq 2l_{j}(u)+l_{j}(w_{0})-1\geq k.

The second part of the lemma is evident.

∎

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},x_{2})
and
g=g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
be reduced words in the free group
F_{5}
, such that
l(w)=k
,
l_{3}(g)=m>0
,
l(g)>1
. If
n=l_{3}(w(g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})))
, then
the following inequalities hold:

(10.1.2)

\displaystyle n\geq m,\quad

\displaystyle\text{if}~k=1,

(10.1.3)

\displaystyle n\geq 2(m-1)+(m-2)(k-2),\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }k\geq 2,~m\geq 2.

In the case
k\geq 2
and
g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}^{\alpha}g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
, if
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are non-trivial simultaneously then
n=k
.

Proof.

Suppose that

(10.1.4)

w=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\beta_{1}}x_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\ldots x_{1}^{\alpha_{s}}x_{2}^{\beta_{s}},~~\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},~\mbox{and only}~\alpha_{1}\,\mbox{and/or}~\beta_{s}~\mbox{can be zero}.

If
k=1
, then
w
is a power of
x_{1}
or a power of
x_{2}
. In this case the statement follows from Lemma
10.2
.

Let us prove (
10.1.3
). If
m\geq 2
, then in the word
g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{a}\,g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})^{b}
cancellation of
x_{3}
can occur only once, therefore,

l_{3}(g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{a}\,g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})^{b})\geq(m-1)+(m-1).

Further, in the word
g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{a}\,g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})^{b}\,g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{c}
cancellation of
x_{3}
can occur only twice, therefore

l_{3}(g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{a}\,g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})^{b}\,g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{c})\geq(m-1)+(m-2)+(m-1).

Applying this argument
k
times we get (
10.1.3
).
∎

Using the notations of the previous lemma we get

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
w=w(x_{1},x_{2})
,
l(w)=k\geq 2
and

g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\equiv g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}),~~g_{i}\not\equiv 1,~~\alpha\not=0.

If
n=l_{3}(w(g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3})))
, then
n=k
.

Proof.

Suppose that

(10.1.5)

w=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\beta_{1}}x_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}^{\beta_{2}}\ldots x_{1}^{\alpha_{s}}x_{2}^{\beta_{s}},~~\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},~\mbox{and only}~\alpha_{1}\,\mbox{and/or}~\beta_{s}~\mbox{can be zero}.

Since the words
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are not trivial, then

w(g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),g(x_{4},x_{5},x_{3}))=w(g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}),g_{1}(x_{4},x_{5})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{4},x_{5}))=

=\left(g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\,(g_{1}(x_{4},x_{5})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{4},x_{5}))^{\beta_{1}}\,(g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}))^{\alpha_{2}}\cdot

\cdot(g_{1}(x_{4},x_{5})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{4},x_{5}))^{\beta_{2}}\ldots(g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}))^{\alpha_{s}}\,(g_{1}(x_{4},x_{5})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{4},x_{5}))^{\beta_{s}}.

We can see that cancellations are possible only inside the words

\left(g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{i}}~\mbox{or}~(g_{1}(x_{4},x_{5})\,x_{3}^{\alpha}\,g_{2}(x_{4},x_{5}))^{\beta_{i}},

but not possible between these words.
Therefore
n=k
.

∎

A description of verbal elementary 3-solutions to the TE gives

Theorem 10.5
.

Let
R\colon G^{3}\to G^{3}
be a verbal elementary 3-solution of the TE for every group
G
, then one of following cases holds:

(1)

R(x,y,z)=(x,y,yx^{-1})
,

(2)

R(x,y,z)=(x,y,x^{-1}y)
,

(3)

R(x,y,z)=(x,y,w(y,z))
, where
R^{\prime}(y,z)=(y,w(y,z))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
.

Proof.

If

R(x,y,z)=(x,y,w(x,y,z)),~~x,y,z\in G,

is a solution of the TE, then by Corollary
9.2

(10.1.6)

\displaystyle w(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p),w(y,t,q))=w(y,t,w(z,p,q)).

Suppose at first, that
w
does not depend on
x
, i.e.
w(x,y,z)=w(y,z)
. In this case the equality (
10.1.6
) takes the form

w(w(t,p),w(t,q))=w(t,w(p,q)).

Therefore
R^{\prime}(y,z)=(y,w(y,z))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
.

Suppose further that
w(x,y,z)
depends on
x
. We will consider 3 cases:

Case 1
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,z)
.

In this case the equality (
10.1.6
) is rewriting as

(10.1.7)

\displaystyle w(w(x,z),w(y,q))=w(y,w(z,q)),

and it is easy to see that the left side of this equality depends on
x
. So this case is not possible.

Case 2
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,y,z)
, i.e.
w
depends on all generators.

In this case we can assume that

w(x,y,z)\equiv z^{\alpha_{1}}g_{1}(x,y)z^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots g_{n}(x,y)z^{\alpha_{n+1}},~~\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z},~\mbox{and only}~\alpha_{1}\,\mbox{and/or}~\alpha_{n+1}~\mbox{can be zero}.

So we can write the left side of (
10.1.6
):

(w(y,p,q))^{\alpha_{1}}g_{1}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))(w(y,p,q))^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots g_{n}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))(w(y,p,q))^{\alpha_{n+1}}.

Note that for every
g_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})
the word
g_{i}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))
depends on
t
and
z
. Moreover since
(w(y,p,q))^{\alpha_{i}}
depends on
q
for every nonzero
\alpha_{i}
, we obtain that the words
g_{i}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))
must not depend on
x
. It follows from Lemma
10.4
that if the word
g_{i}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))
does not depend on
x
, then
w=x^{\gamma}h(y,z)
or
w=h(y,z)x^{\gamma}
for some
0\neq\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h(x,y)\in F_{2}(x,y)
.

Suppose that
w=x^{\gamma}h(y,z)
. If there exist
g_{i_{0}}=y^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
g_{i_{0}}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p))
depends on
x
by Lemma
10.2
. We get the contradiction. Therefore
h(y,z)=y^{\alpha}z^{\beta}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
. So in this case we obtain

(x^{\gamma}y^{\alpha}z^{\beta})^{\gamma}(x^{\gamma}t^{\alpha}p^{\beta})^{\alpha}(y^{\gamma}p^{\alpha}q^{\beta})^{\beta}=y^{\gamma}t^{\alpha}(z^{\gamma}p^{\alpha}q^{\beta})^{\beta}.

Cancellation of
x
can occur only if
\gamma=-1,\alpha=1
, then

z^{-\beta}y^{-1}tp^{\beta}(y^{-1}pq^{\beta})^{\beta}=y^{-1}t(z^{-1}pq^{\beta})^{\beta}.

Since
\beta\neq 0
we get the contradiction.

By analogy we can show that the case
w=h(y,z)x^{\gamma}
also is not possible.

Case 3
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,y)
.

In this case equality (
10.1.6
) is rewriting as

(10.1.8)

\displaystyle w(w(x,y),w(x,t))=w(y,t).

We know that
l(w(x,y))=k\geq 2
, and at first we assume that
l_{x}(w(x,y))=m\geq 2
. Then

l_{x}(w(w(x,y),w(x,t)))\geq 2(m-1)+(m-2)(k-2)\geq 2(m-1)>0.

Therefore
w(w(x,y),w(x,t))
depends on
x
. So we obtain that
l_{x}(w(x,y))=m=1
or equivalently
w(x,y)\equiv y^{a}x^{b}y^{c}
, where
b\neq 0
.

It is not difficult to prove that
w(x,y)\equiv y^{a}x^{b}y^{c}
satisfies (
10.1.8
) if and only if
w\equiv x^{-1}y
or
w\equiv yx^{-1}
.
∎

Remark 10.6
.

If
P_{13}(x,y,z)=(z,y,x)
is a permutation of the first and the third components and
R(x,y,z)=(x,y,w(x,y,z))
is an elementary 3-solution to the TE, then

P_{13}RP_{13}(x,y,z)=(w(z,y,x),y,z)

is an elementary 1-solution to the TE.

A description of verbal elementary 2-solutions gives

Theorem 10.7
.

Let
R\colon G^{3}\to G^{3}
be a verbal elementary 2-solution of the TE for every group
G
, then one of the following cases holds:

(1)

R(x,y,z)=(x,w(x,z),z)
, where
w\equiv xz
or
w\equiv zx
,

(2)

R(x,y,z)=(x,w(x,y),z)
, where
R^{\prime}(x,y)=(x,w(x,y))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
,

(3)

R(x,y,z)=(x,w(y,z),z)
, where
R^{\prime}(y,z)=(y,w(z,y))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
.

Proof.

If

R(x,y,z)=(x,w(x,y,z),z),~~~x,y,z\in G,

is a solution of the TE, then by Corollary
9.3

(10.1.9)

\displaystyle w(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,p),q)=w(x,w(y,t,q),w(z,p,q)).

Suppose at first, that
w
dos not depend on
x
, i.e.
w(x,y,z)=w(y,z)
. In this case the equality (
10.1.9
) takes the form

w(w(t,p),q)=w(w(t,q),w(p,q)).

Therefore
R^{\prime}(y,z)=(y,w(z,y))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
.

Suppose further that
w(x,y,z)
depends on
x
. We will consider 3 cases:

Case 1
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,z)
.

In this case equality (
10.1.9
) is rewriting as

(10.1.10)

\displaystyle w(w(x,z),q)=w(x,w(z,q)).

Let
l_{2}(w(x_{1},x_{2}))=k\geq 1
and
l_{1}(w(x_{1},x_{2}))=m\geq 1
. From equality (
10.1.10
) and Lemma
10.2
we have

m=l_{x}(w(x,w(z,q)))=l_{x}(w(w(x,z),q))\geq(k-1)m.

Therefore
l_{2}(w(x_{1},x_{2}))\in\{1,2\}
. Similarly we get
l_{1}(w(x_{1},x_{2}))\in\{1,2\}
.
So, using direct calculations in this case we obtain two solutions:
w(x,z)\equiv xz
and
w(x,z)\equiv zx
.

Case 2
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,y,z)
.

Denote by
m=l_{1}(w(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))
and
n=l_{3}(w(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))
. Then

w\equiv\omega_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\,x_{3}^{\alpha_{1}}\,\omega_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\ldots x_{3}^{\alpha_{n}}\,\omega_{n+1}(x_{1},x_{2}).

Let us assume that
m\geq 3
. It is easy to see that

m=l_{x}(w(x,w(y,t,q),w(z,p,q)))=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}l_{x}(\omega_{i}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,r))).

Let us assume that there is a subword
\omega_{j}(x_{1},x_{2})
of
w
such that
k=l(\omega_{j}(x_{1},x_{2}))>1
. Then by Lemma
10.3
we have

m\geq l_{x}(\omega_{j}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,r)))\geq 2(m-1)+(m-2)(k-2)\geq 2m-2>m.

So, all
\omega_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})
have syllable length one i.e.
\omega_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv x_{1}^{\alpha_{i}}
or
\omega_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv x_{2}^{\beta_{i}}
. By Lemma
10.2
we have
l_{x}(\omega_{j}(w(x,y,z),w(x,t,r)))\geq m
. Therefore
l+1=1
in sum above, but the word
w
depends on
x_{3}
. Hence
m\in\{1,2\}
.

Similarly, it is established that
n\in\{1,2\}
. We get a finite number of possible variants of the word
w
. By checking it is easy to make sure that none of them is a solution.

Case 3
:
w(x,y,z)=w(x,y)
.

In this case the equality (
10.1.6
) is rewriting as

(10.1.11)

\displaystyle w(w(x,y),w(x,t))=w(x,w(y,t)).

Therefore
R^{\prime}(x,y)=(x,w(x,y))
is a solution of the YBE for every group
G
.
∎

From these results follows that it is not possible to find non-trivial invertible verbal elementary solutions to the TE on free non-abelian groups. We do not know are there exist non-elementary solutions of this type.
On the other side, I. G. Korepanov constructed non-trivial invertible solutions to the
4
-SE on free non-abelian (and, hence, on arbitrary) group.

Example 10.8

(I. G. Korepanov)
.

Let
G
be an arbitrary group and
a,b
are two fixed elements of
G
. Then the map

(x,y,z,w)\mapsto(yw^{-1}a,xbz,w,z),~~x,y,z,w\in G,

defines a solution to the
4
-SE on
G
. It is easy to see that if
a=b=1
, then this solution is a verbal one.

We studied verbal universal elementary solution for the TE. Also, it is possible to study solutions in some classes of groups, abelian, nilpotent, solvable and so on.

Definition 10.9
.

Let
R:F_{n}\to F_{n}
be an endomorphism of the free group that is defined on the generators,

R(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=(w_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),w_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,w_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})),

\mathcal{V}
be a variety of groups. We will say that
R
defines a
verbal universal

\mathcal{V}
-solution for
{n}
-SE, if
(G,R)
is a solution
for any group
G\in\mathcal{V}
. If
\mathcal{V}
is variety of abelian groups, we say on universal verbal abelian solution, if
\mathcal{V}
is variety of nilpotent groups, we say on universal verbal nilpotent solution, and so on.

10.2.
Verbal solutions in abelian groups

To find verbal universal abelian solutions for
{n}
-SE one can consider free abelian group of rank
n
.
Verbal solutions of the
{n}
-SE for the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are in one-to-one correspondence with linear solutions over the ring
\mathbb{Z}
. Any linear solution
R
over
\mathbb{Z}
may be written by matrix:

R(x)=Mx,\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\ M\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}).

Proposition 10.10
.

If a map
R
given by a matrix
M
is a solution of the
{n}
-SE, then a map, defined by a transposed matrix
M^{T}
is also a solution of the
{n}
-SE.

Fortunately in some cases we already know solutions of
{n}
-SE. For example, all linear bijective solutions for the
{2}
-SE,
{3}
-SE and
{4}
-SE
are listed in
[
20
]
.
Thus we can list all verbal universal abelian solutions in these cases.
