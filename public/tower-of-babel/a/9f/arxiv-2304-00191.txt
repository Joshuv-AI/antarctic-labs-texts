Partial Groupoid Representations and a relation with the Birget-Rhodes expansion
Wesley G. Lautenschlaeger and Thaísa Tamusiunas

Abstract
. We introduce partial representation of a finite groupoid 𝒢 \mathcal{G} on an algebra 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and we show that the partial representations of 𝒢 \mathcal{G} are in one-to-one correspondence with the representations of the algebra generated by the Birget-Rhodes expansion 𝒢 B ​ R \mathcal{G}^{BR} of 𝒢 \mathcal{G} .



2020 AMS Subject Classification:
Primary: 20L05, 18B40. Secondary: 20M30.

Keywords:
groupoid, partial representation, partial groupoid algebra, Birget-Rhodes expansion.

1.
Introduction

Many studies concerning actions and partial actions of groupoids as been investigate in the last few years. For instance, the relation between partial and global actions, Galois theory, generalizations of classic theorems of group theory, Morita theory, crossed products and duality theorems were research topics addressed in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
. The ideia of to classify something partial in terms of something global help us to understand the behavior of the partial theory.

The Birget-Rhodes expansion
\mathcal{G}^{BR}
of an ordered groupoid
\mathcal{G}
was constructed by Gilbert in
[
7
]
and it was proven that it has an ordered groupoid structure
[
7
, Proposition 3.1]
. Also, there is a one-to-one correspondence between partial actions of
\mathcal{G}
and actions of
\mathcal{G}^{BR}
, which can be viewed as a partial-to-global result achieved by enlarging the acting groupoid.

The construction of
\mathcal{G}^{BR}
can be used to improve the work developed by Exel, Dokuchaev and Piccione in
[
6
]
, regarding to partial representations of a group
G
. In that work, the authors presented the partial group algebra of a group
G
, called
K_{par}(G)
, which is the algebra whose representations correspond to the partial representations of
G
. The algebra
K_{par}(G)
was shown to be a groupoid algebra
K\Gamma(G)
, where
\Gamma(G)
is a determined finite groupoid associated to
G

[
6
, Corollary 2.7]
. But the groupoid
\Gamma(G)
has a very rich structure. It is, in fact, the Birget-Rhodes expansion of the group
G
. What is behind this is the Ehresmann-Schein-Nambooripad Theorem (or simply, “ESN Theorem”)
[
8
, Theorem 4.1.8]
, which proves that the category of the inverse semigroups is isomorphic to the category of inductive groupoids. Then, althought the Birget-Rhodes expansion
G^{BR}
of
G
is an inverse semigroup, it also has a structure of a groupoid, via the ESN Theorem.

Based on this, our major purpose in this paper is to complement and to extend the work of Exel, Dokuchaev and Piccione, establishing a relation between the partial representations of a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and representations of the algebra
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
generated by its Birget-Rhodes expansion
\mathcal{G}^{BR}
. Indeed, we shall prove that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the partial representations of
\mathcal{G}
and the representations of
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
. This agrees with the idea of to enlarge the groupoid
\mathcal{G}
to characterize partial representations in terms of  “global” representations.

The paper is organized as follows. We start by fixing some terminology concerning groupoids and hence we introduce the concept of partial representation of a finite groupoid
\mathcal{G}
on an algebra. Next we present the algebra
K_{par}(\mathcal{G})
, which the representations are in one-to-one correspondence with the partial representations of
\mathcal{G}
. The last section is destined to prove that the algebra
K_{par}(\mathcal{G})
is the algebra generated by the Birget-Rhodes expansion of
\mathcal{G}
.

Throughout, rings and algebras are associative and unital.

2.
Partial representations and the algebra
K_{par}(\mathcal{G})

2.1.
Partial representations

We recall that a
groupoid

\mathcal{G}
is a small category in which every morphism is an isomorphism. We denote by
\mathcal{G}_{0}
the set of objects of
\mathcal{G}
. Observe that
\text{id}:\mathcal{G}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
, given by
\text{id}(x)=\text{id}_{x}
, is an injective map and whence we identify
\mathcal{G}_{0}\subset\mathcal{G}
. Given
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the
domain
and the
range
of
g
will be denoted by
d(g)
and
r(g)
, respectively. Hence,
d(g)=g^{-1}g
and
r(g)=gg^{-1}
. For all
g,h\in G
, we write
\exists gh
whenever the product
gh
is defined. We fix the notation
\mathcal{G}_{2}:=\{(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}:\exists gh\}
.

For the rest of the paper, let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite groupoid,
K
be a field and
\mathcal{A}
be a
K
-algebra. We start this section by defining a partial groupoid representation on
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
partial groupoid representation
of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
is a map
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{A}
such that:

(i)

\pi(g)\pi(h)\pi(h^{-1})=\pi(gh)\pi(h^{-1})
,
\forall(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
;

(ii)

\pi(g^{-1})\pi(g)\pi(h)=\pi(g^{-1})\pi(gh)
,
\forall(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
;

(iii)

\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\pi(g)=\pi(g)
,
\forall g\in\mathcal{G}
;

(iv)

\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\pi(e)=1_{A}
and
\pi(e)\pi(f)=0
for
e,f\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
such that
e\neq f
.

Remark 2.2
.

Observe that
\pi(g)=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\pi(g)=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1}g)=\pi(g)\pi(d(g))
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Analogously,
\pi(g)=\pi(r(g))\pi(g)
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. So, if
(g,h)\notin\mathcal{G}_{2}
, then
\pi(g)\pi(h)=\pi(g)\pi(d(g))\pi(r(h))\pi(h)=\pi(g)0\pi(h)=0
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{G}
. It follows that:

(a)

\varepsilon(g)=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})
is an idempotent of
A
;

(b)

if
r(g)=r(h)
, then
\varepsilon(g)\varepsilon(h)=\varepsilon(h)\varepsilon(g)
.

Proof.

(a) It is straightforward.

(b) If
r(g)=r(h)
,

(1)

\begin{split}\pi(h^{-1})\varepsilon(g)&=\pi(h^{-1})\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})=\pi(h^{-1}g)\pi(g^{-1})=\pi(h^{-1}g)\pi(g^{-1}h)\pi(h^{-1}g)\pi(g^{-1})\\
&=\pi(h^{-1}g)\pi(g^{-1}h)\pi(h^{-1}gg^{-1})=\varepsilon(h^{-1}g)\pi(h^{-1}r(g))=\varepsilon(h^{-1}g)\pi(h^{-1}r(h))\\
&=\varepsilon(h^{-1}g)\pi(h^{-1}),\end{split}

from where it follows

\displaystyle\varepsilon(h)\varepsilon(g)=\pi(h)\pi(h^{-1})\varepsilon(g)=\pi(h)\varepsilon(h^{-1}g)\pi(h^{-1})=\varepsilon(r(h)g)\pi(h)\pi(h^{-1})=\varepsilon(g)\varepsilon(h).

∎

2.2.
The algebra
K_{par}(\mathcal{G})

As in the case of partial representations of a group G, which can be characterized by an algebra homomorphism defined on the partial group algebra
K_{par}(G)
, in the case of partial representations of a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
, it is possible to construct an algebra associated to
\mathcal{G}
which characterizes partial representations by algebra homomorphisms.

Definition 2.4
.

We define the partial groupoid
K
-algebra
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
as the universal
K
-algebra with unit
1_{K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})}
generated by the set of symbols
\{[g]:g\in\mathcal{G}\}
and relations:

(i)

[g^{-1}][g][h]=[g^{-1}][gh]
,
\forall(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
;

(ii)

[g][h][h^{-1}]=[gh][h^{-1}]
,
\forall(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
;

(iii)

[r(g)][g]=[g]=[g][d(g)]
,
\forall g\in\mathcal{G}
;

(iv)

[g][h]=0
,
\forall(g,h)\notin\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Notice that
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e]=1_{K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})}
. In fact,
\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e]\right)[g]=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e][g]=[r(g)][g]=[g]
. Similarly
[g]\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e]\right)=[g]
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{1}\cup\mathcal{G}_{2}
(disjoint union), where
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{g,g^{-1},r(g),d(g)\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{r(h),h\}
with
h=h^{-1}
. Then
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
has basis
\{[g],[g^{-1}],[r(g)],[d(g)],[g][g^{-1}],[g^{-1}][g],[h],

[r(h)],[h][h]\}
as a
K
-vector space. It is easy to see that
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\simeq K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\oplus K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
. More generally, if
\mathcal{G}
is a finite groupoid with connected components
\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{n}
, then
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\simeq K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G}_{n})
.

The next theorem shows that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between partial representations of
\mathcal{G}
and representations of
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{A}
be a partial representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
. Then there exists a unique homomorphism of
K
-algebras
\phi:K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\phi([g])=\pi(g)
. Conversely, if
\phi:K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{A}
is a homomorphism of
K
-algebras, then
\pi(g)=\phi([g])
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to A
be a partial representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
. Define

\displaystyle\phi:K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}[g_{i,j}]

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\pi(g_{i,j}),

where
k_{i}\in K
and
g_{i,j}\in\mathcal{G}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
and
1\leq j\leq n
. Then
\phi([g])=\pi(g)
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Furthermore, if
\exists gh
,

\displaystyle\phi([g][h])

\displaystyle=\phi([r(g)][g][h])=\phi([gg^{-1}][g][h])=\phi([g][g^{-1}][g][h])=\phi([g][g^{-1}][gh])

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\pi(gh)=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\pi(g)\pi(h)=\pi(g)\pi(h)=\phi([g])\phi([h]),

and
\phi([g][h])=0=\pi(g)\pi(h)
otherwise.

Moreover,
\phi(1_{K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})})=\phi\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e]\right)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\pi(e)=1_{A}
. Clearly,
\phi
is unique.

Conversely, let
\phi:K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{A}
be a homomorphism of
K
-algebras. Define
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
by
\pi(g)=\phi([g])
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. We shall prove that
\pi
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
. In fact,

(i): If
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\pi(g)\pi(h)\pi(h^{-1})

\displaystyle=\phi([g])\phi([h])\phi([h^{-1}])=\phi([g][h][h^{-1}])

\displaystyle=\phi([gh][h^{-1}])=\phi([gh])\phi([h^{-1}])=\pi(gh)\pi(h^{-1}).

(ii): Analogous to (i).

(iii):
\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\pi(g)=\phi([g])\phi([g^{-1}])\phi([g])=\phi([g][g^{-1}][g])=\phi([g])=\pi(g)
.

(iv): We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\pi(e)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\phi([e])=\phi\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}[e]\right)=\phi(1_{K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})})=1_{A}

and if
e,f\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
with
e\neq f
, then
\pi(e)\pi(f)=\phi([e])\phi([f])=\phi([e][f])=\phi(0)=0
.
∎

3.
The relation with the Birget-Rhodes expansion

In this section we shall describe
K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
in terms of the Birget-Rhodes expansion of
\mathcal{G}
.

Define
X_{g}=\{h\in\mathcal{G}:r(h)=r(g)\}
, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Observe that
X_{g}=X_{r(g)}
. Now let
S\subseteq X_{e}
be a subset, for some
e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
. We set:

(2)

\displaystyle P_{S}=\prod_{h\in S}\varepsilon(h)\prod_{h\in X_{e}\setminus S}(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(h)).

Using (
1
) it is easy to see that
\pi(\ell)P_{S}=P_{\ell S}\pi(\ell)
, for all
\ell\in\mathcal{G}
with
d(\ell)=e
and
S\subseteq X_{e}
.

Observe that if
e\notin S
, then
P_{S}=0
. Moreover, if
\ell\in X_{e}\setminus S
, then
P_{S}\pi(\ell)=0
, because
\varepsilon(\ell)\pi(\ell)=\pi(\ell)
, so
(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(\ell))\pi(\ell)=\pi(e)\pi(\ell)-\varepsilon(\ell)\pi(\ell)=\pi(r(\ell))\pi(\ell)-\pi(\ell)=\pi(\ell)-\pi(\ell)=0
.

Furthermore,

(3)

\displaystyle\pi(e)=\sum_{S\subseteq X_{e}}P_{S},

since we have the combinatorial formula

\displaystyle\pi(e)

\displaystyle=\prod_{h\in X_{e}}\pi(e)=\prod_{h\in X_{e}}(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(h)+\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq X_{e}}\left(\left(\prod_{h\in S}\varepsilon(h)\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{h\in X_{g}\setminus S}(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(h))\right)\right).

Now define
Y_{g}=\{h\in\mathcal{G}:r(h)=d(g)\}=X_{g^{-1}}
. We set the finite groupoid, constructed from
\mathcal{G}
,

\mathcal{G}^{BR}=\{(A,g):d(g),g^{-1}\in A\subseteq Y_{g}\}

as the groupoid with partial multiplication given by

\displaystyle(A,g)\cdot(B,h)=\begin{cases}(B,gh),\text{ if }(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}\text{ and }A=hB,\\
\text{undefined, otherwise.}\end{cases}

The inverse of the pair
(A,g)
is
(gA,g^{-1})
. Also
d(A,g)=(A,d(g))
and
r(A,g)=(gA,r(g))
. This groupoid is the Birget-Rhodes expansion of
\mathcal{G}
(see
[
7
, Proposition 3.1]
).

An easy calculation shows that the elements of the form
(A,e)
,
e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
, are idempotents in the groupoid algebra
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
, that is,
(A,e)^{2}=(A,e)
. Also, they are mutually orthogonal and their sum is
1_{K\mathcal{G}^{BR}}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Define the map
\lambda:\mathcal{G}\to K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
by
\lambda(g)=\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}(A,g)
. Then
\lambda
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
.

Proof.

(i) Given
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda(g^{-1})\lambda(g)\lambda(h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\ni g\\
C\ni g^{-1}\\
B\ni h^{-1}\end{subarray}}(A,g^{-1})(B,g)(C,h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
hC\ni g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(ghC,g^{-1})(hC,g)(C,h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(C,d(g)h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(C,r(h)h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(C,h).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\lambda(g^{-1})\lambda(gh)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\ni g\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(A,g^{-1})(C,gh)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(hC,g^{-1})(C,gh)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C\ni h^{-1}\\
C\ni h^{-1}g^{-1}\end{subarray}}(C,h).

Hence
\lambda(g^{-1})\lambda(g)\lambda(h)=\lambda(g^{-1})\lambda(gh)
, for all
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
. The equality
\lambda(g)\lambda(h)\lambda(h^{-1})=\lambda(gh)\lambda(h^{-1})
is proved similarly.

(iii) The equality
\lambda(g)\lambda(g^{-1})\lambda(g)=\lambda(g)
is equivalent to
\lambda(g)\lambda(d(g))=\lambda(g)
by Remark
2.2
. We shall show the second equality. For
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle\lambda(g)\lambda(d(g))

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{A\ni g}(A,g)\right)\left(\sum_{B\ni d(g)}(B,d(g))\right)=\sum_{A\ni g}(A,g)(A,d(g))=\sum_{A\ni g}(A,g)=\lambda(g).

(iv) We have
\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\lambda(e)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}(A,e)=1_{K\mathcal{G}^{BR}}
, and if
e,f\in\mathcal{G}_{0}
,
e\neq f
,

\lambda(e)\lambda(f)=\left(\sum_{A\ni e}(A,e)\right)\left(\sum_{B\ni f}(B,f)\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\ni e\\
B\ni f\end{subarray}}(A,e)(B,f)=0.\qed

Theorem 3.2
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the partial groupoid representations of
\mathcal{G}
and the representations of
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
. More precisely, if
\mathcal{A}
is any unital
K
-algebra, then
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{A}
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
if and only if there is an algebra homomorphism
\tilde{\pi}:K\mathcal{G}^{BR}\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\pi=\tilde{\pi}\circ\lambda
. Moreover, such a homomorphism
\tilde{\pi}
is unique.

Proof.

If
\tilde{\pi}:K\mathcal{G}^{BR}\to\mathcal{A}
is a homomorphism of
K
-algebras, then clearly
\pi=\tilde{\pi}\circ\lambda:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{A}
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{A}
.

Conversely, assume that
\pi:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{A}
is a partial groupoid representation of
\mathcal{G}
. For all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, denote by
\varepsilon(g)=\pi(g)\pi(g^{-1})\in\mathcal{A}
. Recall from Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.2
that
\varepsilon(g)\varepsilon(h)=\delta_{r(g),r(h)}\varepsilon(h)\varepsilon(g)
. Also, from (
1
), if
d(g)=r(h)
then
\pi(g)\varepsilon(h)=\varepsilon(gh)\pi(g)
, and if
d(g)\neq r(h)
, then
\pi(g)\varepsilon(h)=0
. Similarly, if
r(g)=r(h)
, then
\varepsilon(h)\pi(g)=\pi(g)\varepsilon(g^{-1}h)
, and
\varepsilon(h)\pi(g)=0
otherwise.

For
(A,g)\in\mathcal{G}^{BR}
, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(A,g)=\pi(g)\left(\prod_{h\in A}\varepsilon(h)\right)\left(\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))\right).

For
(A,g),(B,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{BR}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\cdot\prod_{k\in A}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(k))\cdot\pi(h)

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{\ell\in B}\varepsilon(\ell)\cdot\prod_{\ell\in Y_{h}\setminus B}(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(\ell)).

If
d(g)\neq r(h)
, then
\pi(d(g))\pi(h)=0
and
\varepsilon(k)\pi(h)=0
, for all
k\in Y_{g}\setminus A
, since
\varepsilon(k)=\pi(k)\pi(k^{-1})
and
d(k^{-1})=r(k)=d(g)\neq r(h)
. So in this case
\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)=0
.

Suppose now that
d(g)=r(h)
. Hence

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\pi(h)\cdot\prod_{k\in A}\varepsilon(h^{-1}k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(h^{-1}k))\cdot\prod_{\ell\in B}\varepsilon(\ell)\cdot\prod_{\ell\in Y_{h}\setminus B}(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(\ell))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\pi(h)\cdot\prod_{k\in h^{-1}A}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A}(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(k))\cdot\prod_{\ell\in B}\varepsilon(\ell)\cdot\prod_{\ell\in Y_{h}\setminus B}(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(\ell)).

If
h^{-1}A\neq B
, that is, if
A\neq hB
, then either there is
k\in h^{-1}A
such that
k\in Y_{h}\setminus B
or there is
k\in B
such that
k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A
. In both cases, the factor
\varepsilon(k)(\pi(d(h))-\varepsilon(k))=0
appears in the expression of
\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)
, from where it follows that
\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)=0
.

On the other hand, if
h^{-1}A=B
, then

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\pi(h)\cdot\prod_{k\in h^{-1}A}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A}(\pi(d(h)))-\varepsilon(k)

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\pi(h)\varepsilon(h^{-1})\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in h^{-1}A\\
k\neq h^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A}(\pi(d(h)))-\varepsilon(k)

\displaystyle=\pi(gh)\varepsilon(h^{-1})\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in h^{-1}A\\
k\neq h^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A}(\pi(d(h)))-\varepsilon(k)

\displaystyle=\pi(gh)\cdot\prod_{k\in h^{-1}A}\varepsilon(k)\cdot\prod_{k\in Y_{h}\setminus h^{-1}A}(\pi(d(h)))-\varepsilon(k)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\pi}(h^{-1}A,gh)=\tilde{\pi}(B,gh)=\tilde{\pi}((A,g)\cdot(B,h)).

Therefore, in all cases, we have
\tilde{\pi}(A,g)\tilde{\pi}(B,h)=\tilde{\pi}((A,g)\cdot(B,h))
. This shows that extending
\tilde{\pi}
linearly from
\mathcal{G}^{BR}
to
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
we obtain a homomorphism of
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
in
\mathcal{A}
.

Now recall that
1_{K\mathcal{G}^{BR}}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}(A,e)
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(1_{K\mathcal{G}^{BR}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\pi}\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}(A,e)\right)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}\tilde{\pi}(A,e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}\pi(e)\cdot\prod_{A\ni g}\varepsilon(g)\cdot\prod_{g\in Y_{e}\setminus A}(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(g))

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}\prod_{A\ni g}\varepsilon(g)\cdot\prod_{g\in Y_{e}\setminus A}(\pi(e)-\varepsilon(g))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{defps}}{=}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\ni e}P_{A}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\sum_{A\subseteq X_{e}}P_{A}\overset{\eqref{ps}}{=}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\pi(e)=1_{\mathcal{A}}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}\circ\lambda(g)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\pi}\left(\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}(A,g)\right)=\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}\tilde{\pi}(A,g)=\pi(g)\cdot\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}\sum_{h\in A}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\varepsilon(g^{-1})\cdot\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in A\\
h\neq g^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\cdot\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in A\\
h\neq g^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\cdot\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}\pi(d(g))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in A\\
h\neq g^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\cdot\sum_{A\ni g^{-1}}(\varepsilon(g^{-1})+\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(g^{-1}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in A\\
h\neq g^{-1}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus A}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\cdot\sum_{B}\prod_{h\in B}\varepsilon(h)\cdot\prod_{h\in Y_{g}\setminus B}(\pi(d(g))-\varepsilon(h))

\displaystyle=\pi(g)\tilde{\pi}\left(\sum_{B}(B,d(g))\right)=\pi(g)\tilde{\pi}(d(g))=\pi(g)\cdot\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\tilde{\pi}(e)\right)=\pi(g)1_{K\mathcal{G}^{BR}}=\pi(g).

Now it only remains to show the uniqueness of the homomorphism
\tilde{\pi}
. To this claim, we shall show that
\lambda(\mathcal{G})
generates
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
.

Let
(B,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{BR}
, where
B=\{b_{1}^{-1},b_{2}^{-1},\ldots,b_{k-1}^{-1},h^{-1}\}
is a subset of
Y_{h}
containing the
d(h)
. The set of such pairs forms a vector space basis for
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
. Let us denote by
\mathfrak{A}
the subalgebra of
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
generated by
\lambda(\mathcal{G})
. Let
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
be such that

\displaystyle g_{1}=b_{1},\quad g_{1}g_{2}=b_{2},\quad g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}=b_{3},\quad\ldots\quad g_{1}g_{2}\cdots g_{k-1}

\displaystyle=b_{k-1},\quad g_{1}\cdots g_{k}=h.

These elements are well-defined. In fact, from
g_{1}=b_{1}
,
d(b_{1}^{-1})=r(b_{1})=d(h)=r(b_{2})
and
g_{1}g_{2}=b_{2}
one obtain
g_{2}=b_{1}^{-1}b_{2}
. Inductively, we obtain
g_{i}=b_{i-1}^{-1}b_{i}
, for all
2\leq i\leq k-1
and
g_{k}=b_{k-1}^{-1}h
.

Consider the element

\displaystyle\lambda(g_{1})\cdots\lambda(g_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{1}\ni g_{1}^{-1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{k}\ni g_{k}^{-1}\end{subarray}}(A_{1},g_{1})\cdots(A_{k},g_{k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{k}\ni g_{k}^{-1}\\
g_{k}A_{k}\ni g_{k-1}^{-1}\\
\vdots\\
g_{2}\cdots g_{k}A_{k}\ni g_{1}^{-1}\end{subarray}}(A_{1},g_{1}\cdots g_{k})=\sum_{A\supseteq B}(A,h).

Thus, for all
(B,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{BR}
,
\sum_{A\supseteq B}(A,h)\in\mathfrak{A}
. Suppose that
Y_{g}\setminus B=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{A\supseteq B}(A,h)-\sum_{A\supseteq B\cup\{x_{1}\}}(A,h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\supseteq B\\
A\not\ni x_{1}\end{subarray}}(A,h),

from where it follows inductively that

\displaystyle(B,h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\supseteq B\\
A\not\ni x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\end{subarray}}(A,h)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\supseteq B\\
A\not\ni x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\end{subarray}}(A,h)\in\mathfrak{A},

ending the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The groupoid algebra
K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
is isomorphic to the partial groupoid algebra
K_{\text{\emph{par}}}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

The maps
[\,\,]:\mathcal{G}\to K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
,
g\mapsto[g]
, and
\lambda:\mathcal{G}\to K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
are partial representations of
\mathcal{G}
. By Theorems
2.6
and
3.2
, there exist
K
-algebra homomorphisms
\tilde{\pi}:K\mathcal{G}^{BR}\to K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})
and
\phi:K_{\text{par}}(\mathcal{G})\to K\mathcal{G}^{BR}
such that
\tilde{\pi}(\lambda(g))=[g]
and
\phi([g])=\lambda(g)
.

It is easy to check that
\tilde{\pi}
and
\phi
are each others inverses, since they are the identity on the generators; hence
\tilde{\pi}
and
\phi
are isomorphisms. ∎
