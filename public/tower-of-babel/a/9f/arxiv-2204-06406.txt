An isoperimetric inequality for surfaces formed from spherical polygons
Farhan Azad, Thomas Beck, Karolina Lokaj

Abstract
We give a new proof of an isoperimetric inequality for a family of closed surfaces, which have Gaussian curvature identically equal to one wherever the surface is smooth. These surfaces are formed from a convex, spherical polygon, with each vertex of the polygon leading to a non-smooth point on the surface. For example, the surface formed from a spherical lune is a surface of revolution, with two non-smooth tips. Combined with a straightforward approximation argument, this inequality was first proved by Bérard, Besson, and Gallot in [ 6 ] , where they provide a generalization of the Lévy-Gromov isoperimetric inequality. The inequality implies an isoperimetric inequality for geodesically convex subsets of the sphere, and, using a Faber-Krahn theorem, it also implies a lower bound on the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of a region of a given area on the closed surfaces. Via approximation, we convert this into a lower bound on the first Dirichlet-Neumann eigenvalue of domains contained in geodesically convex subsets of the sphere.



1
Introduction

An isoperimetric inequality on a Riemannian surface
S
provides a lower bound on the length of a closed curve enclosing a given surface area. Denoting
L
to be the length of the curve and
A
to be the enclosed surface area, a classical example of such an inequality is

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi A-KA^{2}.

This inequality holds when
S
is a simply-connected surface of constant sectional curvature
K
, and so it holds for the Euclidean plane (
K=0
), the sphere (
K>0
), and the hyperbolic plane (
K<0
), and was first proved in this generality in
[
25
]
. For all
K\in\mathbb{R}
, equality is obtained precisely when the region is a geodesic disc, and so these are the unique perimeter-minimizing regions.

In the variable curvature case, Benjamini and Cao
[
5
]
have proved an isoperimetric inequality for a class of rotationally symmetric curved planes, which include the paraboloid. For the paraboloid, the perimeter-minimizing region is a geodesic disc centered at the vertex of the paraboloid, where the Gaussian curvature is largest. The example of the paraboloid is also included in Theorem 1.2 in
[
21
]
, where they study rotationally symmetric planes with its Gaussian curvature a strictly decreasing function from an origin. In this case, the boundaries of the perimeter-minimizing regions are again circles, although in general the region that these circles bound may be the complement of a geodesic disc or an annulus. Using a geometric measure theory and calculus of variations approach, Ritoré
[
24
]
also proves an isoperimetric inequality for some complete surfaces, with rotational symmetry, including closed surfaces with equatorial symmetry, where the Gaussian curvature is monotonic away from the equator. Another general isoperimetric inequality for surfaces with variable curvature is given by Topping
[
26
]
, and see the comprehensive surveys by Osserman
[
23
]
and Howards, Hutchings, and Morgan
[
17
]
for many other results and references.

In this paper, we study surfaces which have constant positive Gaussian curvature, away from a finite number of singular, non-smooth points. These surfaces are formed from the double of a convex spherical polygon on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. A special case is when the polygon is a spherical lune, in which case the surface has an explicit isometric embedding as a surface of revolution (see Definition
1.1
and Lemma
1.1
below). This special case then formally fits into the framework of
[
21
]
and
[
24
]
: The surface has rotational symmetry with the Gaussian curvature identically equal to
1
except for two non-smooth tips where the curvature is undefined, but the tips can be thought of as providing an increase to the total Gaussian curvature. We use this framework to give a new proof of an isoperimetric inequality for these surfaces, with equality only attained when the surface is formed from spherical lunes and the region is a geodesic disc centered at a tip (see Theorem
1.1
). This inequality, and the case of equality, also follows directly by a straightforward approximation argument from the work of Bérard, Besson, and Gallot in
[
6
]
and Milman in
[
19
]
, where they prove generalizations of the Lévy-Gromov isoperimetric inequality
[
16
]
. See the discussion after Theorem
1.1
for more details.

An important application of many isoperimetric inequalities is the relationship with Dirichlet eigenvalues via the Faber-Krahn theorem,
[
12
]
,
[
18
]
. This is carried out in a very general setting by Chavel,
[
11
]
, where it is shown how combining an appropriate isoperimetric inequality with the co-area formula can provide a lower bound on the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of a region by that of a geodesic disc of the same area. Together with a careful analysis of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of spherical caps, this has been for example used in
[
4
]
to prove that two hemispheres provides the unique partition of the sphere that minimizes a certain function of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of each piece. This function is related to harmonic, homogeneous, functions supported on cones generated by the subsets of the sphere, and is of importance in the regularity theory for minimizers of two-phase free boundary problems,
[
1
]
. Since our surfaces are formed from two copies of a convex spherical polygon, a direct consequence of our isoperimetric inequality and a Faber-Krahn theorem is eigenvalue estimates for subsets of the polygon with mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions (see Corollary
1.2
).

Statement of Results

As mentioned above, we will study an isoperimetric inequality and Faber-Krahn eigenvalue estimates for surfaces formed by copies of spherical lunes, and more generally (geodesically) convex spherical polygons. For each
a
, with
0<a\leq 1
, we set
\Omega_{a}
to be a spherical lune on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}

\pi a

\Omega_{a}

has an isometric embedding given by a surface with boundary contained in a plane and equal to a convex curve, which is smooth except possibly at two points. The closed manifold is then given by gluing two copies of this embedding across this plane, and this will lead to a smooth surface of revolution, except for two non-smooth tips. See Lemma
1.1
and its proof for the details of this construction. In particular, in this case of the spherical lune
\Omega_{a}

Definition 1.1
.

For each

\displaystyle S_{a}=\left\{(g(u),h(u)\cos(v),h(u)\sin(v)):-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,0\leq v\leq 2\pi\right\}.

Here the functions

\displaystyle g(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\left(1-a^{2}\sin^{2}(t)\right)^{1/2}\,dt,\qquad h(u)=a\cos(u).

For each

\displaystyle U_{a,b}=\left\{(g(u),h(u)\cos(v),h(u)\sin(v)):b\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,0\leq v\leq 2\pi\right\}.

Note that when

Remark 1
.

By Lemma

Definition 1.2
.

Given

, the set of regions

is defined as follows: A set

We now state the isoperimetric inequality for the surfaces
S_{a}
and
\tilde{P}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Isoperimetric inequality)
.

Given
a
with
0<a\leq 1
, a spherical convex polygon
P\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
, and a region
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\tilde{P}}
, let
L
be the total perimeter of the boundary of
U
and
A
its surface area on
\tilde{P}
. Then, the isoperimetric inequality

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq A(4a\pi-A)

holds, with equality if and only if
\tilde{P}=S_{a}
, and
U
is a cap on
S_{a}
enclosing surface area
A
.

Remark 2
.

In the special case where
a=1
, then
P
is a hemisphere and
\tilde{P}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
. In this case, Theorem
1.1
reduces to the classical isoperimetric inequality for the sphere, stating that any perimeter-minimizing region must be a spherical cap. Therefore, from now on we will assume that
0<a<1
, and so in particular
S_{a}
will be non-smooth at precisely two points.

Remark 3
.

Let
\delta=\delta(P)
be the difference between the smallest interior angle of
P
and that of the lune
\Omega_{a}
of the same area. Since
P
is convex, note that
\delta(P)>0
whenever
P\neq S_{a}
. Then, in the course of proving Theorem
1.1
, we will show that, for regions
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\tilde{P}}
, the quantitative statement
L^{2}\geq(1+f(\delta))A(4a\pi-A)
holds, for a function
f
satisfying
f(\delta)>0
for
\delta>0
. Moreover, for sufficiently small

, depending on the distance between the vertices of the polygon
P
, the isoperimetric minimizers are caps centered around the tip corresponding to the smallest interior angle of
P
. In
[
20
]
, an analogous property is shown for polytopes in any dimension, where, for small volume, geodesic balls about some vertex minimize perimeter.

Theorem
1.1
also gives an isoperimetric inequality for geodesically convex subsets
W
of the sphere. In the corollary below
\mathcal{U}_{W}
is equal to those regions on
W
with boundary consisting of a finite number of disjoint, smooth, simple curves which are either closed or touch the boundary of

at two points.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
W
be a geodesically convex subset of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, with surface area
2a\pi
. For
U\in\mathcal{U}_{W}
, letting

be the total perimeter of the boundary of

, and

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq A(2\pi a-A)

, and

is the subset of the lune with boundary equal to a curve of constant latitude.

By approximation, Theorem
1.1
is also contained in earlier work for smooth surfaces: The Lévy-Gromov inequality
[
16
]
gives an isoperimetric inequality for compact, smooth manifolds with a lower bound on the Ricci curvature, by comparing to the model spaces

of constant curvature

. In particular, when
\kappa=1
, the model space is the unit sphere. In Theorem 2 in
[
6

holds. In our setting, since

is a convex polygon, the doubled surface

is not a lune. Moreover, by a direct construction and calculation, the surface

can be smoothly approximated by surfaces

with curvature bounded below by

(see Lemma

and Remark

[
6
]
therefore gives a strict isoperimetric inequality for each

, and by taking the limit, the strict inequality in Theorem

is thus also contained in this theorem. To further generalize and improve the Lévy-Gromov inequality and the inequality in
[
6
]
, sharp isoperimetric inequalities have been proved for metric measure spaces with a Curvature-Dimension-Diameter condition providing a lower bound on a (generalized) Ricci curvature tensor, by Milman (see Corollary 1.4 in
[

Application to eigenvalues on the polygon
P
and its double
\tilde{P}

We can use Theorem

2
Proof of the isoperimetric inequality

In this section, we will give a proof of Theorem
1.1
. We will split into two cases, by first proving the theorem for the surfaces
S_{a}
, and then extend to a general doubled polygon
\tilde{P}
. In both cases, key ingredients in the proof are the following isoperimetric inequalities. The first concerns a general inequality for simply-connected domains on surfaces with an upper bound on the Gaussian curvature.

Theorem 2.1

(Bol-Fiala inequality
[
7
]
,
[
13
]
, see Theorem 4.3 in
[
23
]
)
.

Let
D
be a simply-connected region on a surface
S
, contained in a region where the Gaussian curvature
K
satisfies
K\leq K_{0}
. Then, the perimeter
L
and area
A
of
D
satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi A-K_{0}A^{2}.

Note that when
K=K_{0}\equiv 1
, this is the isoperimetric inequality for the sphere. The other two results also concern isoperimetric inequalities for surfaces with prescribed information about their Gaussian curvature.

Theorem 2.2

(Morgan, Hutchings, Howards,
[
21
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

Let
S
be a smooth Riemannian surface (either with or without boundary), with a smooth, rotationally invariant metric, such that the Gauss curvature is a non-increasing function of the distance from an origin. Then, among disjoint unions of embedded discs of a given area, the geodesic disc centered at the origin minimizes the perimeter. Moreover, unless the Gaussian curvature of a region containing the origin is constant, this is the unique minimizer.

Theorem 2.3

(Morgan, Hutchings, Howards,
[
21
]
, Theorem 2.4)
.

Let
S
be a smooth Riemannian surface (either with or without boundary), and let
G(t)
be the supremum of the total Gaussian curvature over regions of area
t
on
S
. Let
L
be the length of the boundary of a finite number of embedded discs, enclosing total area
A
. Then,

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi A-2\int_{0}^{A}G(t)\,dt.

Moreover, one case of equality holds for a geodesic disc centered at the origin in a surface of revolution of non-increasing Gaussian curvature.

2.1
The proof for
S_{a}

We now prove Theorem
1.1
for the lune
P=\Omega_{a}
, with corresponding surface
\tilde{P}=S_{a}
. Since the surfaces
S_{a}
are non-smooth at the tips, when the region

contains some of these tips, we will see that it is often more convenient to work with a smoothed version of

. However, in order to apply Theorems

Lemma 2.1
.

Given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a smooth surface
S_{a,\epsilon}
with the following properties:

i)

S_{a,\epsilon}
is a surface of revolution

\displaystyle S_{a,\epsilon}=\left\{(g_{\epsilon}(u),h_{\epsilon}(u)\cos(v),h_{\epsilon}(u)\sin(v)):-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,0\leq v\leq 2\pi\right\},

with
g_{\epsilon}(u)=g(u)
,

for

.

ii)

There exists an absolute constant

, independent of

, such that for all

,

\displaystyle|g_{\epsilon}(u)-g(u)|+|h_{\epsilon}(u)-h(u)|\leq C\epsilon.

iii)

The surface
S_{a,\epsilon}
is symmetric about

, and for

, the Gaussian curvature is a non-increasing function of the distance from the tip
(g_{\epsilon}(\pi/2),0,0)

Remark 5
.

For each convex spherical polygon

, we also define corresponding smooth surfaces
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
: As described in Remark

, the surface

is equal to part of a surface

. Therefore, the smooth surfaces

can be defined using the same construction as for the surfaces

with an appropriate choice of

We will prove Lemma
2.1
with Lemma
1.1
at the end of this Section, and first use them to prove Theorem
1.1
. To prove Theorem
1.1
for
\tilde{P}=S_{a}
, we first assume that the region
U
on
S_{a}
is a single embedded disc with smooth boundary. To prove the inequality we split into four cases depending on the the number of tips contained in
U
.

1.

Suppose first that
U
does not contain either tip of
S_{a}
. Then,
U
is a simply connected region on
S_{a}
contained in a region where the Gaussian curvature
K
satisfies
K\equiv 1
. By the Bol-Fiala inequality, Theorem
2.1
, the perimeter of
U
satisfies

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq A(4\pi-A)>A(4\pi a-A),

since
a<1
, and so we obtain the desired strict inequality.

2.

If both tips are contained in the interior of
U
, then neither are contained in the complement
S_{a}\backslash U
. By Lemma
1.1
iii), this complement has area
4\pi a-A
, and the same perimeter as

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq(4\pi a-A)(4\pi-(4\pi a-A))=(4\pi a-A)(A+4\pi(1-a))>(4\pi a-A)A,

again using

, and this gives the strict inequality.

3.

Now suppose that exactly one tip is contained in the interior of
U
, say
(g(\pi/2),0,0)
. Let
U_{a,b}
be a cap of
S_{a}
, with

has the same area

. This cap has perimeter
L_{b}
satisfying
L_{b}^{2}=A(4a\pi-A)
, and so we need to show that
L\geq L_{b}
, with equality only when
U=U_{a,b}
. For
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, the boundaries of
U
and
U_{a,b}
are contained in the part of
S_{a}
with
-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi+\epsilon\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi-\epsilon
. So, replacing
S_{a}
by its smoothed version
S_{a,\epsilon}
from Lemma
2.1
, we obtain new sets
U_{\epsilon}
,
U_{a,b,\epsilon}
of the same area
A_{\epsilon}
, and with perimeters still equal to

and
L_{b}
respectively. Note that
U_{a,b,\epsilon}
is a geodesic disc centered at
(g_{\epsilon}(\pi/2),0,0)
on
S_{a,\epsilon}
. Therefore, applying Theorem
2.2
to a rotationally symmetric subset of
S_{a,\epsilon}
containing
U_{\epsilon}
,

and only the one tip

, with equality only when
U_{\epsilon}
(and hence also

[
24

in order to conclude that the isoperimetric domains of

are caps. However, the above method of proof using the results from
[
21

\tilde{P}
below.

4.

The final case is when the boundary of
U
passes through at least one of the two tips. Given
\delta>0

U
to form a new embedded disc
U_{\delta}
, containing neither tip, with area
A_{\delta}
and perimeter
L_{\delta}
satisfying

\displaystyle|A-A_{\delta}|<\delta,\quad|L-L_{\delta}|<\delta.

Then, by Case 1., we have

\displaystyle L_{\delta}^{2}\geq A_{\delta}(4\pi-A_{\delta})=A_{\delta}(4\pi a-A_{\delta})+4\pi(1-a)A_{\delta}.

Therefore, by choosing
\delta>0
sufficiently small, depending on
1-a>0
, we can ensure that
L^{2}>A(4\pi a-A).

This completes the case when
U
consists of a single embedded disc. To deal with the general case, we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given
a
, with
0<a<1
, and
m\geq 2
, let
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{m}>0
,
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{m}>0
be two sequences of positive numbers satisfying
L_{j}^{2}\geq A_{j}(4\pi a-A_{j})
for all
1\leq j\leq m
. Then, setting
L=L_{1}+\cdots+L_{m}
,
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle L^{2}>A(4\pi a-A).

Proof of Lemma
2.2
:

Using the inequalities
L_{j}^{2}\geq A_{j}(4\pi a-A_{j})
, we obtain

\displaystyle L^{2}=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{j}\bigg)^{2}>\sum_{j=1}^{m}L_{j}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}(4\pi a-A_{j})

\displaystyle=4\pi a\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle>4\pi aA-\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}\bigg)^{2}=4\pi aA-A^{2},

giving the required inequality.

.

\Box

Given
U\in\mathcal{U}_{S_{a}}
, suppose that
U
consists of a finite number of disjoint embedded discs, of areas
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
, with perimeters
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
. Then, by applying the single embedded disc argument we have
L_{j}^{2}\geq A_{j}(4\pi a-A_{j})
, for
1\leq j\leq m
, and so by Lemma
2.2
we have the strict inequality whenever
m\geq 2
.

Finally, for a general
U\in\mathcal{U}_{S_{a}}
let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{M}
be its connected components, each of area
A_{1},\ldots,A_{M}
and perimeter
L_{1},\ldots,L_{M}
. Each connected component
U_{j}
is either a single embedded disc, or else
U_{j}
is the complement of
m\geq 2
disjoint embedded discs. In the first case, we have already shown that
L_{j}^{2}\geq A_{j}(4\pi a-A_{j})
, with equality only when
U_{j}
is a cap. In the second case, let
A_{j,1},\ldots,A_{j,m}
be the areas of these discs (so that
A_{j,1}+\cdots+A_{j,m}=4\pi a-A_{j}
), with perimeters
L_{j,1},\ldots,L_{j,m}
(so that
L_{j,1}+\cdots+L_{j,m}=L
). Then, for
1\leq k\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle L_{j,k}^{2}\geq A_{j,k}(4\pi a-A_{j,k}).

Applying Lemma
2.2
once to
L_{j,1},\ldots,L_{j,m}
and
A_{j,1},\ldots,A_{j,m}
implies that

\displaystyle L_{j}^{2}>(4\pi a-A_{j})A_{j}.

Applying the lemma again to
L_{1},\ldots,L_{M}
and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{M}
, therefore implies that
L^{2}\geq A(4\pi a-A)
, and we have the strict inequality unless
M=1
, and the one connected component

is a cap. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
when the surface
U
is given by
U=S_{a}
.

2.2
The proof for
\tilde{P}

We now prove the isoperimetric inequality for the surface
\tilde{P}
coming from a general convex spherical polygon
P
of
3
or more sides. Let
\theta_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
, be the interior angles of
P
, with corresponding exterior angles
\psi_{i}=\pi-\theta_{i}
. Note that since
P
is convex, the exterior angles
\psi_{i}
are strictly positive. For
P\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
, the surface area of
P
is equal to
2\pi a
, and so the convexity of
P
ensures that all of the interior angles
\theta_{i}
satisfy

\displaystyle\theta_{i}>\pi a.

(5)

This is because the interior angles of the lune
\Omega_{a}
of the same area are
\pi a
. Moreover, by the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the exterior angles of
P
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}=2\pi-\text{ Area}(P)=2\pi(1-a).

(6)

As for
S_{a}
, rather than working with the non-smooth surface
\tilde{P}
, it will often be more convenient to work with the smooth surface
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
(see Lemma
2.1
and Remark
5
). This surface has
n
smoothed tips, corresponding to the interior angles
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
. In order to use Theorem
2.3
, we need control on the supremum of the total Gaussian curvature over regions of area
t
in
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
. The lemma below informally follows from the Gaussian curvature at each tip of
\tilde{P}
being a delta function of weight
2(\pi-\theta_{j})
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given
\epsilon>0
, let
V
be a region on
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
with smooth boundary, and containing the tips of
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
corresponding to the interior angles
\theta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{k}}
. Then, there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that the Gaussian curvature
K_{\epsilon}
of
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{V}K_{\epsilon}\leq\emph{Area}(V)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\pi-\theta_{i_{j}}\right)+C\epsilon.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
:

By breaking
V
into
k
pieces, it is sufficient to prove the lemma when
V
contains one tip, corresponding to an interior angle
\theta_{1}
. Let
V_{\epsilon}
be the part of
V\subset\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
contained in the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of this tip where
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
differs from
\tilde{P}
. Then, since the curvature of
K_{\epsilon}
equals
1
on
V\backslash V_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\int_{V}K_{\epsilon}=\int_{V\setminus V_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}+\int_{V_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}=\text{Area}(V\setminus V_{\epsilon})+\int_{V_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}\leq\text{Area}(V)+\int_{V_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}.

(7)

To bound the total curvature of
V_{\epsilon}
, we can work with the surface
S_{a_{1},\epsilon}
from Lemma
2.1
corresponding to the smoothed double of the spherical lune with interior angle
\theta_{1}
. Since
S_{a_{1},\epsilon}
is a smooth surface for all
\epsilon>0
, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem implies that

\displaystyle\int_{S_{a_{1},\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}=4\pi.

Moreover, the surface area of
S_{a_{1},\epsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon})-\text{Area}(S_{a_{1}})\right|=\left|\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon})-4\pi a_{1}\right|\leq C\epsilon.

(8)

Here and below
C
is an absolute constant (independent of
\epsilon>0
), which may change from line-to-line. The curvature
K_{\epsilon}
of
S_{a_{1},\epsilon}
is equal to
1
outside of the
\epsilon
-neighborhoods of each tip. Denoting these
\epsilon
-neighborhoods by
W_{\epsilon}
, we therefore have

\displaystyle 4\pi=\int_{W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}+\int_{S_{a_{1},\epsilon}\setminus W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}=\int_{W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}+\int_{S_{a_{1},\epsilon}\setminus W_{\epsilon}}1=\int_{W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}+\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon}\setminus W_{\epsilon}).

(9)

Moreover, there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon}\setminus W_{\epsilon})\geq\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon})-C\epsilon.

Thus, by (
9
)

\displaystyle\int_{W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=4\pi-\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon}\setminus W_{\epsilon})\leq 4\pi-\text{Area}(S_{a_{1},\epsilon})+C\epsilon\leq 4\pi-4\pi a+C\epsilon=4(\pi-\theta_{1})+C\epsilon.

Since
W_{\epsilon}
consists of
\epsilon
-neighborhoods of two tips, and
V_{\epsilon}
is contained in one of these neighborhoods, this implies that

\displaystyle\int_{V_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{W_{\epsilon}}K_{\epsilon}\leq 2(\pi-\theta_{1})+C\epsilon.

Using this inequality in (
7
) completes the proof of the lemma.

.

\Box

Before we prove Theorem
1.1
for
\tilde{P}
, we need one more lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\theta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{n}}
be the interior angles of
P
in a given order. Then, for any
m
, with
0\leq m\leq n
, at least one of the the following two inequalities holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{i_{j}}\right)-(m-1)\pi}{\pi}\geq a\quad\text{ or }\quad\frac{\left(\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\theta_{i_{j}}\right)-(n-m-1)\pi}{\pi}\geq a.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
:

Adding the left hand sides of the two inequalities, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_{i_{j}}\right)-n\pi+2\pi}{\pi}=\frac{-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{i_{j}}\right)+2\pi}{\pi}.

(10)

where
\psi_{i_{j}}=\pi-\theta_{i_{j}}
are the corresponding exterior angles. By (
6
), the right hand side of (
10
) equals
2a
, and so in particular at least one of the two inequalities in the statement of the lemma must hold.

.

\Box

We now proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\tilde{P}
. As for
S_{a}
, we start by considering the case where the region
U
is a single embedded disc on
\tilde{P}
, with smooth boundary not passing through any tips. Replacing
\tilde{P}
by
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, we then obtain a new region
U_{\epsilon}
on
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
with the same perimeter of
L
, and area
A_{\epsilon}
, with

\displaystyle\left|A-A_{\epsilon}\right|\leq C\epsilon.

(11)

Here
C
is an absolute constant, independent of
\epsilon
, which again may change from line-to-line. Let
\theta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{n}}
be the interior angles of
P
, so that the region
U
contains
m
tips of
\tilde{P}
, with
0\leq m\leq n
, and corresponding to interior angles
\theta_{i_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{m}}
. Recalling that
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
agrees with
\tilde{P}
outside of an
\epsilon-
neighborhood of each tip, let
V
be any region on
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
, which does not contain the
\epsilon-
neighborhoods of the tips corresponding to the angles
\theta_{i_{m+1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{n}}
. Then, by Lemma
2.3
, the total curvature of
V
on
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\text{Area}(V)+2\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\pi-\theta_{i_{j}})+C\epsilon.

Therefore, we can apply Theorem
2.3
to the resulting surface (with boundary) where the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of tips corresponding to the angles
\theta_{i_{m+1}},\ldots,\theta_{i_{n}}
have been removed, with

\displaystyle G_{\epsilon}(t)\leq t+2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\theta_{i_{j}}/\pi\right)+C\epsilon.

(12)

We obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi A_{\epsilon}-2\int_{0}^{A_{\epsilon}}G_{\epsilon}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi A_{\epsilon}-2\int_{0}^{A_{\epsilon}}t+2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\theta_{i_{j}}/\pi\right)+C\epsilon\,dt

\displaystyle=4\pi A_{\epsilon}-A_{\epsilon}^{2}-4\pi\left(m-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{i_{j}}}{\pi}\right)A_{\epsilon}-2A_{\epsilon}C\epsilon

\displaystyle=4\pi\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{i_{j}}}{\pi}-(m-1)\right)A_{\epsilon}-A_{\epsilon}^{2}-2A_{\epsilon}C\epsilon.

(13)

We can also apply the same argument to the complement
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}/U_{\epsilon}
, which has area
A^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=\text{ Area}(\tilde{P}_{\epsilon})-A_{\epsilon}
, and obtain

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi\left(\frac{\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\theta_{i_{j}}}{\pi}-(n-m-1)\right)A^{\prime}_{\epsilon}-(A^{\prime}_{\epsilon})^{2}-2A^{\prime}_{\epsilon}C^{\prime}\epsilon.

for another absolute constant
C^{\prime}
. Lemma
2.4
thus implies that either

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{i_{j}}}{\pi}-(m-1)\right)A_{\epsilon}-A_{\epsilon}^{2}-2A_{\epsilon}C\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi aA_{\epsilon}-A_{\epsilon}^{2}-2A_{\epsilon}C\epsilon.

or

\displaystyle L^{2}\geq 4\pi\left(\frac{\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\theta_{i_{j}}}{\pi}-(n-m-1)\right){A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}-({A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon})^{2}-2{A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}C^{\prime}\epsilon\geq 4\pi a{A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}-({A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon})^{2}-2{A}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}C^{\prime}\epsilon

holds. Letting
\epsilon
tend to
0
and using (
11
), in either case we get the desired lower bound of

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi aA-A^{2}.

Remark 6
.

If
U
(or its complement) only contains one tip. corresponding to an angle
\theta
, then from (
5
) and (
13
), we immediately get the strict inequality
L^{2}>A(4\pi a-A)
.

This completes the proof of the inequality when
U
is a single embedded disc, with boundary not passing through any tips. When the boundary passes through some of the tips, we can proceed as we did in Case 4) for
S_{a}
, by perturbing
U
in such a way that the sum of the angles in either the new set or its complement strictly satisfy one of the inequalities in Lemma
2.4
. Finally, for a general region
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\tilde{P}}
, we can proceed as for
S_{a}
using Lemma
2.2
in order to establish the inequality.

We are left to prove that when
\tilde{P}\neq S_{a}
, we always have the strict inequality
L^{2}>A(4\pi a-A)
. From Remark
6
, when
\tilde{P}\neq S_{a}
and
U
only contains one tip, then we do get the strict inequality. In fact, for sufficiently small area
A
(depending on the distance between the vertices of the polygon
P
), the minimizer can only contain at most one tip. By our isoperimetric inequality on
S_{a}
, the minimizer will therefore be a cap centered around the tip corresponding to the smallest interior angle, say
\theta_{1}
. Denoting
L(t)
to be the shortest perimeter of regions in
\mathcal{U}_{\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}}
enclosing area
t
, then for sufficiently small

, this ensures that

\displaystyle L(t)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq t\left(4\pi\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{\pi}-t\right)-C\epsilon>t\left(4\pi a-t\right),

\displaystyle L(t)L^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\geq 2\theta_{1}-t-C\epsilon=2\pi-\left(t+2\pi\left(1-\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{\pi}\right)\right)-C\epsilon>2\pi-\left(t+2\pi\left(1-a\right)\right).

(14)

Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
in
[
21
]
are proved by integrating the inequality

\displaystyle L(t)L^{\prime}_{L^{*}}(t)\geq 2\pi-G(t),

from

to

. Here

is the lower-left derivative of

(see (2) in
[

]
, page 4894) and

is the supremum of the total Gaussian curvature of a region of area

. Therefore, instead using (
14
) for small values of
t
, and the upper bound on

from (

) otherwise, we get the strict inequality

\displaystyle L^{2}>A_{\epsilon}(4\pi a-A_{\epsilon})-2A_{\epsilon}C\epsilon

where the difference between the two sides is bounded below by a constant independent of
\epsilon>0
. Letting

tend to

therefore gives the strict inequality

, and completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Note that using (

) in this way also ensures that the quantitative inequality given in Remark
3
holds.

Proof of Corollary
1.1
:

Given a geodesically convex set
W
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}_{W}
, if
W=\Omega_{a}
is a lune, then the corollary follows immediately from Theorem
1.1
by using
U
to form a region on the doubled closed surface
\tilde{W}=S_{a}
of twice the perimeter and area.

If
W
is not a lune, we first form a sequence of spherical polygons
P_{k}\subset W
, and corresponding closed surfaces
\tilde{P}_{k}
, such that
P_{k}
converges to
W
in Hausdorff measure. This leads to a sequence of regions
U_{k}
on
P_{k}
, with perimeters and surface area converging to that of

. Since
W
is convex but not a lune, it cannot contain antipodal points on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and thus the same is true for

. This gives a uniform lower bound of
\delta>0
on
\delta_{k}=\delta(P_{k})
, the difference between the smallest interior angle of

, and the lune
\Omega_{a_{k}}
of the same area. Applying the quantitative isoperimetric inequality in Remark
3
to each closed surface
\tilde{P}_{k}
and double of

, and then taking the limit as

tends to infinity therefore proves the Corollary.

.

We are left to prove the properties of
S_{a}
and its smoothed version given in Lemmas
1.1
and
2.1
.

Proof of Lemma
1.1
:

In Example 7.5 in Section 5.7 of
[
22
]
, all of the surfaces of revolution with constant positive curvature are constructed. These are given by

\displaystyle\textbf{r}_{c}(u,v)=\left(g_{c}(u),h_{c}(u)\cos(v),h_{c}(u)\sin(v)\right),

with

\displaystyle g_{c}(u)=\int_{0}^{u}\left(1-\tfrac{a^{2}}{c^{2}}\sin^{2}(t/c)\right)^{1/2}\,dt,\qquad h_{c}(u)=a\cos(u/c).

Here
a
,
c>0
are constants, and for all such
a
and
c
, the surfaces have constant curvature
K=\tfrac{1}{c^{2}}
. Moreover, as shown in
[
22
]
, these surfaces are only simply connected for
0<a\leq c
, and for this range of
a
, the variables
u
and
v
are defined for
-\tfrac{1}{2}c\pi\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}c\pi
and
0\leq v\leq 2\pi
. Therefore, setting
c=1
, and
g(u)=g_{1}(u)
,
h(u)=h_{1}(u)
, this ensures that the surfaces
S_{a}
from Definition
1.1
have constant Gaussian curvature equal to
1
everywhere except at
(\pm g(\pi/2),0,0)
, and are the only simply connected surfaces of revolution with this property. This proves part i) of the lemma.

Setting
c=1
and writing
\textbf{r}(u,v)=(g(u),h(u)\cos(v),h(u)\sin(v))
, a direction calculation gives the expressions

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{u}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{1-a^{2}\sin^{2}(u)},-a\sin(u)\cos(v),-a\sin(u)\sin(v)\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{v}

\displaystyle=\left(0,-a\cos(u)\sin(v),a\cos(u)\cos(v)\right),

\displaystyle|\mathbf{r}_{u}\times\mathbf{r}_{v}|

\displaystyle=a\cos(u).

This means that the surface
S_{a}
indeed has the metric
du^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}(u)\,dv^{2}
, giving part ii) of the lemma.

Moreover, the surface area of
S_{a}
is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}|\mathbf{r}_{u}\times\mathbf{r}_{v}|\,du\,dv=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}a\cos(u)\,du\,dv=4a\pi,

proving iii).

The lune
\Omega_{a}
has a parameterization given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{a}=\left\{(\sin(\tilde{u}),\cos(\tilde{u})\cos(\tilde{v}),\cos(\tilde{u})\sin(\tilde{v}))\,:\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\leq\tilde{u}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,0\leq\tilde{v}\leq a\pi\right\},

with metric
d\tilde{u}^{2}+\cos^{2}(\tilde{u})\,d\tilde{v}^{2}
. Therefore, using ii), the mapping
(u,v)\mapsto(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=(u,av)
provides an isometry from one half of
S_{a}
, given by,

\displaystyle\left\{(g(u),h(u)\cos(v),h(u)\sin(v)):-\tfrac{1}{2}\pi\leq u\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\pi,0\leq v\leq\pi\right\},

to the lune
\Omega_{a}
. In particular, the surface
S_{a}
is indeed an isometric embedding of the closed manifold formed by gluing two copies of
\Omega_{a}
, giving iv) in the lemma.

Finally, suppose that a cap
U_{a,b}
on
S_{a}
encloses area
A
and has boundary length
L
. By calculating the surface area of
U_{a,b}
, we see that
b
and
A
satisfy the equation

\displaystyle A=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{b}^{\pi/2}\ a\cos(u)\,du\,dv=2a\pi(1-\sin(b)).

Since every vertical cross-section (with
u
fixed) of
S_{a}
is a circle with radius
2a\pi\cos(u)
, the boundary length
L
of the cap
U_{a,b}
is therefore given by

\displaystyle L^{2}=(2a\pi\cos(b))^{2}=4a^{2}\pi^{2}(1-\sin^{2}(b))=4a^{2}\pi^{2}\left(1-\left(1-\tfrac{A}{2a\pi}\right)^{2}\right),

which simplifies to
L^{2}=4a\pi A-A^{2}
, and completes the proof of Lemma
1.1
.

.

\Box

We are left to prove Lemma
2.1
. We prove this lemma by smoothing out the left and right tips of
S_{a}
carefully in order to ensure that the Gaussian curvature is still an increasing function of the distance from the (smoothed) tips.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
:

By translating and reparameterizing the surface
S_{a}
, we can write the left half of it as

\displaystyle\textbf{r}(u,v)=\left(w(u),u\cos(v),u\sin(v)\right),

for
0\leq v\leq 2\pi
,
0\leq u\leq a
, and a function
w(u)
satisfying
0=w(0)\leq w(u)\leq g(\pi/2)
. For
0<a<1
, the right derivative of
w(u)
at
u=0
is non-zero, reflecting the non-smoothness of
S_{a}
at its two tips.

To obtain a smooth approximation of this part of
S_{a}
, we fix
\epsilon>0
and consider the surface

\displaystyle\textbf{r}_{\epsilon}(u,v)=\left(w_{\epsilon}(u),u\cos(v),u\sin(v)\right),

for
0\leq v\leq 2\pi
,
0\leq u\leq a
, and a
C^{2}
-smooth function
w_{\epsilon}(u)
with these two properties:

i)

w_{\epsilon}(u)=w(u)
for
\epsilon\leq u\leq a
;

ii)

w_{\epsilon}(u)=b_{0}+b_{1}u^{2}+b_{2}u^{4}
for
0\leq u\leq\epsilon
, and for coefficients
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
.

The coefficients
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are chosen so that

\displaystyle w(\epsilon)=w_{\epsilon}(\epsilon),\quad w^{\prime}(\epsilon)=w^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon),\quad w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)=w^{\prime\prime}_{\epsilon}(\epsilon),

(15)

to ensure that
w_{\epsilon}
is a
C^{2}
-smooth function. Solving these equations for
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
gives

\displaystyle b_{0}=\frac{8w(\epsilon)-5w^{\prime}(\epsilon)\epsilon+w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\epsilon^{2}}{8},\quad b_{1}=\frac{3w^{\prime}(\epsilon)-w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\epsilon}{4\epsilon},\quad b_{2}=\frac{-w^{\prime}(\epsilon)+w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)\epsilon}{8\epsilon^{3}}.

(16)

Note that by the properties of the surfaces
S_{a}
, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,
w(\epsilon)
and
w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)
are both positive and bounded by a constant multiple of
\epsilon
, while
w^{\prime}(\epsilon)
is bounded above and below by a positive constant. In particular, for
0\leq u\leq\epsilon
, we therefore have
|w_{\epsilon}(u)|\leq C\epsilon
for an absolute constant
C
. Defining the symmetric smoothed surface
S_{a,\epsilon}
to be formed by two copies of
\textbf{r}_{\epsilon}(u,v)
, this ensures that i) and ii) in Lemma
2.1
hold. These formulas also ensure that
b_{1}=b_{1}(\epsilon)>0
and
b_{2}=b_{2}(\epsilon)<0
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small.

To complete the proof of the lemma, we need to show that, for
v
fixed, the Gaussian curvature of the surface
\textbf{r}_{\epsilon}(u,v)
is a non-increasing function of
u
, with
0\leq u\leq a
. We know that the curvature is identically equal to
1
for
\epsilon\leq u\leq\epsilon
by the properties of
S_{a}
, and so we only need to consider
0\leq u\leq a
. For this range of
u
, the Gaussian curvature is given by

\displaystyle K(u)=\frac{u^{-1}w_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(u)w_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(u)}{(1+|w_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(u)|^{2})^{2}}=\frac{(2b_{1}+4b_{2}u^{2})(2b_{1}+12b_{2}u^{2})}{(1+(2b_{1}u+4b_{2}u^{3})^{2})^{2}},

(17)

and from (
15
) we know that
K(\epsilon)=1
, matching the constant Gaussian curvature of
S_{a}
away from the tips. By (
16
), there exists a constant
C
, independent of
\epsilon>0
, such that

\displaystyle\left|b_{1}-\tfrac{3}{4}\epsilon^{-1}w^{\prime}(\epsilon)\right|\leq C\epsilon,\qquad\left|b_{2}+\tfrac{1}{8}\epsilon^{-3}w^{\prime}(\epsilon)\right|\leq C\epsilon^{-1}.

(18)

These estimates guarantee that, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, the numerator in (
17
) is strictly decreasing for
0\leq u\leq\epsilon
, and since
K(\epsilon)=1\geq 0
, this numerator must therefore also be positive for this range of
u
.
The estimates in (
18
) also ensure that, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle w_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(u)>0,\quad w_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)<0,\quad w_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(u)\geq w_{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)=w^{\prime\prime}(\epsilon)>0

(19)

for
0\leq u\leq\epsilon
. Therefore, the denominator of
K(u)
in (
17
) is a strictly increasing, positive function. Putting everything together, we thus have that
K(u)
is indeed a positive, decreasing function for
0\leq u\leq\epsilon
, with
K(\epsilon)=1
.

.

\Box

3
Proof of the Faber-Krahn theorem and Corollaries
1.2
and
1.3

In
2
dimensions, the classical Faber-Krahn theorem states the following (see, for example,
[
11
]
, Theorem 2, page 87).

Theorem 3.1

(Faber
[
12
]
, Krahn
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}
be the complete, simply connected
n
-dimensional space of constant sectional curvature
\kappa
, and let
M
be a complete
2
-dimensional Riemannian surface. For each open set
U
consisting of a finite union of disjoint regular domains on
M
, let
D
be the geodesic disc in
\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}
of the same surface area. If for all such
U
, the boundary length of
U
is greater than or equal to that of
D
, with equality if and only if
U
and
D
are isometric, then

\displaystyle\lambda(U)\geq\lambda(D).

Here
\lambda(\cdot)
are the respective first Dirichlet eigenvalues, and equality holds if and only if
U
and
D
are isometric.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we follow the proof presented in
[
11
]
, with the surface
\mathbb{M}_{\kappa}
replaced by the surface
S_{a}
, chosen so that
S_{a}
has the same surface area as the given doubled polygon
\tilde{P}
: The geodesic discs of
S_{a}
, centered at a tip
(a^{*},0,0)
, are then precisely the caps
U_{a,b}
. Therefore, the isoperimetric inequality in Theorem
1.1
ensures that the hypothesis on the boundary length of
U
is satisfied. Theorem
1.2
then follows by exactly replicating the proof of Theorem 2 on page 87 of
[
11
]
by building a comparison function on
S_{a}
, with superlevel sets given by the caps
U_{a,b}
, and the use of the co-area formula. The quantitative eigenvalue lower bound in Remark
4
also follows by using the quantitative isoperimetric inequality in Remark
3
in the same argument.

We end by using Theorem
1.2
to prove Corollaries
1.2
and
1.3
.

Proof of Corollaries
1.2
and
1.3
:

We start by proving Corollary
1.2
, and first consider the case when the geodesically convex set
W
is a spherical polygon
P
. We let
U
be the double of
V
on the closed surface
\tilde{P}
. Then, by reflecting across the boundary of
W
, using the Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial W\cap\partial V
, the Dirichlet-Neumann eigenvalue
\mu(V)
is a Dirichlet eigenvalue of
U
on
\tilde{P}
. In particular,
\mu(V)\geq\lambda(U)
. Since the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the cap
U_{a,b}
is simple, it is rotationally symmetric, and so when
W
is equal to a lune
\Omega_{a}
, and
V=\Omega_{a,b}
, this process must lead to the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
U_{a,b}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu(\Omega_{a,b})=\lambda(U_{a,b})=\lambda(U_{1,b}).

The inequality in the corollary then follows from Theorem
1.2
. Moreover, if
\delta=\delta(P)\geq 0
is the difference between the smallest interior angle of
P
and that of
\Omega_{a}
, then

\displaystyle\mu(V)\geq(1+g(\delta))\mu(\Omega_{a,b}),

(20)

with
g(\delta)
the function from Remark
4
.

For a general geodesically convex set
W
, we approximate the first Dirichlet-Neumann eigenvalue of a subset
V\subset W
using this lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V_{k}\subset V
be subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, with boundaries of the following form: There exist sets
\partial V_{k}^{N}
,
\partial V_{k}^{D}
,
\partial V^{N}
, and
\partial V^{D}
, such that

\displaystyle\partial V_{k}=\partial V_{k}^{N}\cup\partial V_{k}^{D},\qquad\partial V=\partial V^{N}\cup\partial V^{D},

\partial V_{k}^{D}\subset\partial V^{D}
, and
\partial V^{N}
is Lipschitz. Then, denoting
\mu(V)
to be the first eigenvalue of
V
, with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial V^{D}
and Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial V^{N}
, and likewise for the eigenvalue
\mu(V_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle\mu(V_{k})\leq\mu(V)\left(1+C\emph{Area}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(V\backslash V_{k})\right).

The constant
C>0
depends only on the eigenvalue
\mu(V)
and the Lipschitz constant of
\partial V^{N}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
:

Denote
u
to be the corresponding first
L^{2}(V)
-normalized eigenfunction of
V
, and
\tilde{u}
to be the reflection of
u
across the Neumann boundary
\partial V^{N}
. Due to the Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial V^{N}
, and since
\partial V^{N}
is Lipschitz,
\tilde{u}
is then a solution of a non-degenerate elliptic equation in divergence form, with bounded, measurable coefficients on the double of
V
. These coefficients can be bounded in terms of
\mu(V)
and the Lipschitz constant of
\partial V^{N}
, and so elliptic estimates (see, for example, Theorem 8.25 in
[
15
]
) ensure that
\tilde{u}
and
u
are bounded in
L^{\infty}
.

To obtain the upper bound on
\mu(V_{k})
given in the statement of the lemma, we use the variational formulation of the first eigenvalue. The restriction of
u
to
V_{k}
, which we call
u_{k}
, is an admissible test function in this formulation, due to the structure of the boundaries and boundary conditions of
V_{k}
and
V
. Therefore, letting
g
be the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, with surface measure
\,d\sigma_{g}
,

\displaystyle\mu(V_{k})\leq\frac{\iint_{V_{k}}\left|\nabla_{g}u_{k}\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}}{\iint_{V_{k}}\left|u_{k}\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}}\leq\frac{\iint_{V}\left|\nabla_{g}u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}}{\iint_{V_{k}}\left|u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}}=\frac{\mu(V)}{\iint_{V_{k}}\left|u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}}.

Using

\displaystyle\iint_{V_{k}}\left|u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}=\iint_{V}\left|u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}-\iint_{V\backslash V_{k}}\left|u\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{g}=1-||{u}||_{L^{\infty}(V)}\text{Area}(V\backslash V_{k}),

and the boundedness of
u
, then gives the desired inequality.

.

\Box

To complete the proof of Corollary
1.2
, let
W_{k}\subset W
be a sequence of convex polygons on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, converging to
W
in Hausdorff measure, and let
V_{k}=V\cap W_{k}\subset V
. Since
V_{k}
is a subset of a convex spherical polygon, we have
\mu(V_{k})\geq\mu(\Omega_{a,b_{k}})
where
\Omega_{a,b_{k}}
has the same surface area as
V_{k}
. Applying Lemma
3.1
, and letting
k
tend to infinity, we therefore have the required inequality
\mu(V)\geq\mu(\Omega_{a,b})
. Moreover, if
W
is not a lune
\Omega_{a}
, then we can choose the approximating sequence
W_{k}
, so that the difference between the smallest interior angle of
W_{k}
and that of the lune
\Omega_{a_{k}}
of the same area is bounded below by a positive constant, independently of
k
. The strict inequality in (
20
) therefore ensures that
\mu(V)>\mu(\Omega_{a,b})
in this case.

This finishes the proof of Corollary
1.2
. With
\alpha(V)
and
\alpha(U_{1,b})
as in (
2
) and (
3
), Corollary
1.2
ensures that

\displaystyle\alpha(V)\geq\alpha(U_{1,b}),

with equality only when
W=\Omega_{a}
and
V=\Omega_{a,b}
. Corollary
1.3
therefore follows immediately from (
4
).

.

\Box
