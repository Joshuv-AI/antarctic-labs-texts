A virtual element method for the solution of 2D time-harmonic elastic wave equations via scalar potentials
Silvia Falletta, Matteo Ferrari, Letizia Scuderi

Abstract
In this paper, we propose and analyse a numerical method to solve 2D Dirichlet time-harmonic elastic wave equations. The procedure is based on the decoupling of the elastic vector field into scalar Pressure ( P P -) and Shear ( S S -) waves via a suitable Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition. For the approximation of the two scalar potentials we apply a virtual element method associated with different mesh sizes and degrees of accuracy. We provide for the stability of the method and a convergence error estimate in the L 2 L^{2} -norm for the displacement field, in which the contributions to the error associated with the P P - and S S - waves are separated. In contrast to standard approaches that are directly applied to the vector formulation, this procedure allows for keeping track of the two different wave numbers, that depend on the P P - and S S - speeds of propagation and, therefore, for using a high-order method for the approximation of the wave associated with the higher wave number. Some numerical tests, validating the theoretical results and showing the good performance of the proposed approach, are presented.



Keywords
time-harmonic elastic wave equation, virtual element method, Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition, scalar potentials.

1
Introduction

The numerical modelling of elastic waves propagation problems has undergone, in recent years, an increasing interest in many of the mathematical and engineering areas such as, for example, geophysics, acoustics and seismology.

We consider the Dirichlet vector time harmonic elastic equation defined in a 2D bounded homogeneous medium. For its solution, several numerical methods have been proposed and analysed, among which we mention the traditional finite differences, the finite element method and the more recent Virtual Element Method (VEM).

The aim of this paper is to propose a novel approach based on an Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition of the elastic vector field into two scalar potentials. It consists in reformulating the vector equation into a couple of scalar equations, that describe the propagation of
P
- and
S
-waves, respectively. These two equations are coupled by the Dirichlet boundary condition. This approach, originally proposed in
[
1
]
to solve interior soft-scattering elastodynamic problems by a finite element method, allows for using approximation spaces with different mesh sizes and degrees of accuracy, related to the
P
- and
S
- speeds of propagation. This turns out to be a great advantage in some applications, such as elastic propagation in soft tissues, in which
P
-waves propagate much faster than
S
- ones, an aspect that displacement-based methods are not able to exploit.
It is worth mentioning that, more recently, the same approach has been applied in
[
2
,
3
]
to traction-free interior elastodynamics problems, in
[
4
]
to solve exterior soft-scattering ones by means of their space-time BIE representations, and in
[
5
]
by means of the coupling of boundary and finite element methods. To the best of our knowledge, there are no other papers on such a topic which, according to its interesting properties, is worth to be investigated further.

The novelty of this paper consists in analyzing the potential approach for elastodynamic interior problems in the frequency domain, providing for stability of the variational formulation. Since in this case we cannot make use of the Fredholm theory, we perform the analysis by proving that the bilinear form associated with the variational formulation is T-coercive. This tool has been introduced in
[
6
,
7
]
and it is particularly suitable to treat problems with sign-changing coefficients.

For the numerical solution, we apply a VEM and we provide for a convergence error estimate in the
L^{2}
-norm for the displacement field. By a careful study, we show that the approximation error of the discrete solution can be split into two contributions, associated with the
P
- and
S
- waves. This aspect allows us to use different mesh grids and approximation orders and to retrieve a high accuracy of the global scheme with a low order VEM for the approximation of the
P
- waves which propagate faster than
S
-ones and, hence, are associated with a smaller wave number. The choice of using VEM relies in the possibility of considering meshes whose elements can be of general shape, and to use local discrete spaces of arbitrarily high order by maintaining the simplicity of implementation independent of it. Another advantage of the proposed procedure that we have exploited, is the possibility of using the same discrete spaces and bilinear forms associated with the VEM for the solution of scalar elliptic problems and, hence, of using the corresponding codes.
We mention that, in literature, VEMs have been already applied to solve elasticity problems: in
[
8
]
for compressible and nearly incompressible materials in two dimensions, and in
[
9
]
for three dimensions; in
[
10
]
with mixed formulation; in
[
11
,
12
]
for computational guidelines and in

[
13
]
for elastodynamics interior problems.
In the latter papers, details on the implementation of the vector version of VEM for elasticity are described, in particular for what concerns the virtual element projection of the strain tensor, which turns out to be the main challenge with respect to the scalar VEM. We remark that in our approach this issue is avoided since the proposed method involves the bilinear forms associated with the scalar Helmholtz equation, and hence only the guidelines to construct the classical VEM matrices (see
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
) are needed.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we present the model problem for the time-harmonic elastic equation and its reformulation based on the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition of the vector field. We introduce the variational formulation of the problem and we prove its stability by means of the T-coercivity property of the associated bilinear form. In Section
3
, for the approximation of the solution of the new problem, we apply a VEM and we prove the stability and the optimal convergence estimate in the
L^{2}
-norm for the displacement field. In Section
4
we describe the algebraic formulation of the numerical scheme. Finally, in the last section, we present some numerical tests which confirm the theoretical results. Even if the theoretical analysis is provided for the polygonal version of the VEM, to highlight the feasibility of the proposed approach when dealing with curved geometries, in the last example we apply the curvilinear version of the VEM to a problem defined in a curved domain. This allows us to avoid the approximation of the geometry and to retrieve the optimal convergence rate with high approximation orders.

.

, we denote by

the standard Sobolev space of order

, and by
\boldsymbol{H}^{s}(\Omega)=[H^{s}(\Omega)]^{2}.

Similarly, we denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
the scalar
L^{2}
-product in
\Omega
, and by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
the vectorial
L^{2}
-scalar product therein. Similarly, the corresponding Sobolev norms
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}
are defined as well.
If
\Gamma
is a Lipschitz curve, then we use the angled bracket

\langle\lambda,v\rangle_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Gamma}v(\boldsymbol{x})\lambda(\boldsymbol{x})\,\text{d}\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{x}}

to denote the

inner product and its extension as the
H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)\times H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)
duality product.
Denoting by
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=[u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),u_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})]^{T}
a vector field, and

a scalar function depending on the space variable
\boldsymbol{x}=[x_{1},x_{2}]^{T}
, we use the following notations for the differential operators

\begin{array}[]{lll}&\nabla v=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x_{1}}v\\
\partial_{x_{2}}v\end{bmatrix},\quad\bccurl v=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x_{2}}v\\
-\partial_{x_{1}}v\end{bmatrix},\quad\Delta v=\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}v+\partial^{2}_{x_{2}}v,\\
\\
&\ddiv\boldsymbol{u}=\partial_{x_{1}}u_{1}+\partial_{x_{2}}u_{2},\quad\ccurl\boldsymbol{u}=\partial_{x_{1}}u_{2}-\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1},\end{array}

and

\Delta\boldsymbol{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}u_{1}+\partial^{2}_{x_{2}}u_{1},\partial^{2}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+\partial^{2}_{x_{2}}u_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

2
The model problem

We consider the motion of a homogeneous, isotropic elastic solid occupying a bounded star shaped domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
. The displacement governing equation in the frequency domain, with the presence of a body force
\mathbf{f}
and with a prescribed Dirichlet condition
\mathbf{g}
on
\Gamma
, can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\ddiv\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})-\rho\kappa^{2}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{f}(\boldsymbol{x})&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{x})&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma,\end{cases}

(1)

where
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
are the Lamé constants,
\rho>0
is the material density and
\kappa>0
is the frequency. We assume
\mathbf{f}\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathbf{g}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)
.
To reformulate the vector problem in terms of a couple of scalar potential equations, following
[
1
]
and observing that

\Delta\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla(\ddiv\boldsymbol{u})-\mathbf{curl}(\ccurl\boldsymbol{u}),

we rewrite the partial differential equation in (
1
) as

-(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\ddiv\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))+\mu\mathbf{curl}(\ccurl\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\rho\kappa^{2}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{f}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2)

Hence, we split the displacement field
\boldsymbol{u}
by applying the following Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition

\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\varphi^{P}+\bccurl\varphi^{S},

(3)

in terms of some unknown scalar potentials
\varphi^{P},\varphi^{S}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. We point out that the decomposition (
3
) is not unique since, for any
\Phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\nabla\Phi+\bccurl\Phi=0
, the potentials
\widetilde{\varphi}^{P}=\varphi^{P}+\Phi,\widetilde{\varphi}^{S}=\varphi^{S}+\Phi
satisfy (
3
) as well.
The existence of a Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition is guaranteed by
Theorem 3.2 in
[
16
]
, that we report here for completeness.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be open connected, with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
, and denote by
\mathbf{n}
the unit normal vector on
\Gamma
pointing outside
\Omega
. Then, each
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
can be decomposed in a unique way with a Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition of the type
\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}
, with potentials
v^{P},v^{S}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying

\begin{cases}\Delta v^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})=\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
(\nabla v^{P}\cdot\mathbf{n})(\boldsymbol{x})=(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{n})(\boldsymbol{x})&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma,\\
\int_{\Gamma}v^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\text{d}\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{x}}=0,\end{cases}

(4)

and

\begin{cases}\Delta v^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
v^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})=0&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma.\end{cases}

(5)

In our case, the characterizations (
4
)-(
5
) can not be applied to determine
\varphi^{P}
and
\varphi^{S}
, the functions
\ddiv\boldsymbol{u}
,
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}
and
\ccurl\boldsymbol{u}
being unknown. Therefore, to construct a suitable decomposition, we start by recalling the following properties, for a sufficiently smooth potential
\Phi
:

\displaystyle\text{div}(\nabla\Phi)=-\text{curl}(\bccurl\Phi)=\Delta\Phi,\quad\text{div}(\bccurl\Phi)=\text{curl}(\nabla\Phi)=0,\quad\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla\Phi\cdot\mathbf{n}=\bccurl\Phi\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau},\quad\nabla\Phi\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=-\bccurl\Phi\cdot\mathbf{n},\hskip 97.59274pt\text{on $\Gamma$,}

\boldsymbol{\tau}
representing the clockwise oriented tangential directions. By properly applying (
6
), equation (
2
) can be reformulated, in terms of the potentials
\varphi^{P}
and
\varphi^{S}
, as

-(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\Delta\varphi^{P})-\mu\mathbf{curl}(\Delta\varphi^{S})-\rho\kappa^{2}\left(\nabla\varphi^{P}+\bccurl\varphi^{S}\right)=\mathbf{f},

(7)

and the associated Dirichlet boundary conditions can be equivalently rewritten as

(\nabla\varphi^{P}+\bccurl\varphi^{S})\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n},\quad(\nabla\varphi^{P}+\bccurl\varphi^{S})\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau},

i.e.,

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi^{P}-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{S}=\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathbf{n}=:{g}_{\mathbf{n}},\quad\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi^{S}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{P}=\mathbf{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}=:{g}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}.

To reformulate (
7
) in terms of a couple of scalar PDEs, according to Theorem
2.1
we decompose
\mathbf{f}=\nabla f^{P}+\bccurl f^{S}
with
f^{P},f^{S}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, and we easily obtain the following equivalent problem for the potentials (see Proposition 3.5.1 of
[
17
]
for details):

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{-\Delta\varphi^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{\rho\kappa^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}\varphi^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}f^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})},&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
\displaystyle{-\Delta\varphi^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{\rho\kappa^{2}}{\mu}\varphi^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{\mu}f^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})},&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})-\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})={g}_{\mathbf{n}}(\boldsymbol{x}),&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma,\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})+\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})={g}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(\boldsymbol{x}),&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma.\end{cases}

(8)

The first two equations of Problem (
8
) are Helmholtz equations associated with the longitudinal and transverse wave numbers

\kappa_{P}^{2}=\frac{\rho\kappa^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\kappa_{S}^{2}=\frac{\rho\kappa^{2}}{\mu},

(9)

respectively. Formulation (
8
) is of particular interest in many applications of physics, for example, when the problem source is a
P
-wave or a
S
-wave, and the knowledge of the propagation of the
P
- and
S
-waves generated by this source is required.

To obtain the variational formulation of Problem (
8
) we start by multiplying the first and second equations by the corresponding components of the vector function
\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{P},v^{S})\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, we apply the Green formula and we use the second two equations of (
8
) to rewrite the normal unknown derivatives on
\Gamma
in terms of the tangential ones. In particular, the weak form reads: find
(\varphi^{P},\varphi^{S})\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\begin{cases}\left(\nabla\varphi^{P},\nabla v^{P}\right)_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}-\left\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{S},v^{P}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}&-\kappa_{P}^{2}\left(\varphi^{P},v^{P}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(f^{P},v^{P}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\langle g_{\mathbf{n}},v^{P}\right\rangle_{\Gamma},\\
(\nabla\varphi^{S},\nabla v^{S})_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{P},v^{S}\rangle_{\Gamma}&-\kappa_{S}^{2}(\varphi^{S},v^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{\mu}(f^{S},v^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\langle{g}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},v^{S}\right\rangle_{\Gamma},\end{cases}

(10)

for all
(v^{P},v^{S})\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.

By introducing the bilinear forms
a:H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
and
m:L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}

a(u,v)=\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad m(u,v)=\left(u,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(11)

we define
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{K}:\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(\varphi^{P},v^{P})+a(\varphi^{S},v^{S})-\left\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{S},v^{P}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}+\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\varphi^{P},v^{S}\rangle_{\Gamma},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{P}^{2}m(\varphi^{P},v^{P})+\kappa_{S}^{2}m(\varphi^{S},v^{S}),

(13)

and the linear form
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}}:\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}}(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\left(f^{P},v^{P}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\langle g_{\mathbf{n}},v^{P}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{\mu}(f^{S},v^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\langle{g}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},v^{S}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}.

(14)

Hence, we rewrite (
10
) in the following operator notation: find
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}}(\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\text{for all~}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega).

By following
[
17
]
, we introduce the Hilbert space

\mathbf{V}(\Omega)=\{\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{P},v^{S})\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega):\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\},

endowed with the norm and semi-norm

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)},\quad|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}=\left\|\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}\right\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Observing that, for
\boldsymbol{v}=\left(v^{P},v^{S}\right)\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
, it holds

\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}\\
-\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix},

(15)

it is immediate to deduce that
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)=\boldsymbol{H}(\text{div},\Omega)\cap\boldsymbol{H}(\text{curl},\Omega)
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}(\text{div},\Omega)=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega):\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},\quad\boldsymbol{H}(\text{curl},\Omega)=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega):\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}

and that

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Following the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 1]
, it is easy to show that for all
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\left(\varphi^{P},\varphi^{S}\right),\boldsymbol{v}=\left(v^{P},v^{S}\right)\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, it holds

\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\nabla\varphi^{P}+\bccurl\varphi^{S},\nabla v^{P}+\bccurl v^{S}\right)_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)},

(16)

which entails that the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}
is well-defined also in the less regular space
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
. Moreover, combining (
15
) with (
16
), for all
\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
it is possible to rewrite

\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=(\ddiv\boldsymbol{\varphi},\ddiv\boldsymbol{v})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\ccurl\boldsymbol{\varphi},\ccurl\boldsymbol{v})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, since for
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
it holds
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})=|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}
, it appears natural to define the variational formulation of Problem (
8
) as follows: find
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}}(\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega).

(17)

It is worth to point out, since the canonical injection of
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
into
\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
is not compact (see
[
18
, Proposition 2.7]
), we can not assert that the operator
\mathcal{K}
is compact and, hence, we can not directly apply the well known Fredholm theory. To overcome such issue, in the next section we will introduce an isomorphism
\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
, that will allow us to prove the stability of the weak formulation (
17
). The strategy consists in showing that the new bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})
can be written as the sum of a coercive bilinear form and of a bilinear form associated with a compact operator. This procedure is the main idea of the so called
T-coercivity
, which has been coined in
[
6
]
, and turned out to be particularly useful for problems such as the Maxwell’s equations in the time-harmonic regime.

2.1
Stability of the weak formulation

In what follows, the notation
Q_{1}\apprle Q_{2}
(resp.
Q_{1}\apprge Q_{2}
) means that
Q_{1}
is bounded from above (resp. from below) by
c\,Q_{2}
, where
c
is a positive constant that may depend on
\kappa_{P}
and
\kappa_{S}
but, unless explicitly stated, does not depend on any other relevant parameter involved in the definition of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.

We start by providing for the basic definition of the T-coercivity, as given in
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space. A continuous bilinear form
b:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is
T-coercive
if there exists a linear isomorphism
T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

|b(u,Tu)|\geq\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad\forall\ u\in\mathcal{H}.

In
[
7
, Theorem 1]
, it has been shown that the T-coercivity is a necessary and sufficient condition to guarantee the well-posedness for a variational problem associated with the bilinear form
b
.

We remark that the T-coercivity can also be extended to allow for compact perturbations of coercive operators (see
[
19
]
). This will be the case analysed in this paper. In particular, we will show that there exists an invertible operator
\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
such that we can apply the Fredholm theory to the following transformed problem, equivalent to (
17
): find
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}.

(18)

To this aim, proceeding as in
[
20
]
, we introduce a projection
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
such that
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
is compact. This is achieved by defining
\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla\tilde{v}^{P}+\bccurl\tilde{v}^{S}
in terms of the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition of
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
, where
\tilde{v}^{P},\tilde{v}^{S}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
are solutions of the following Dirichlet Poisson problems

\begin{cases}\Delta\tilde{v}^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})=\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}),&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
\tilde{v}^{P}(\boldsymbol{x})=0,&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma,\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}\Delta\tilde{v}^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}),&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
\tilde{v}^{S}(\boldsymbol{x})=0,&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma.\end{cases}

By virtue of (
6
), the following properties hold

\text{div}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})=\ddiv\boldsymbol{v},\quad\text{curl}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})=\ccurl\boldsymbol{v},\quad\mathbf{P}^{2}=\mathbf{P}.

(19)

Moreover, due to the standard theory related to Poisson problems, from (
19
) we can easily deduce that

\|\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\text{curl}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\text{div}(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

Further, from standard regularity results for star-shaped domains (see e.g.
[
21
]
), it results that
\tilde{v}^{P},\tilde{v}^{S}\in H^{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
with
\varepsilon>0
. This latter entails
\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
and

\|\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\ddiv\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=|\boldsymbol{v}|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

According to (
19
), the operator
\mathbf{T}=2\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{I}
is an isomorphism in
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
; indeed, it satisfies
\mathbf{T}^{2}=\mathbf{I}
and

\text{div}(\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})=\ddiv\boldsymbol{v},\qquad\ccurl(\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})=\ccurl\boldsymbol{v}.

(20)

Proposition 2.3
.

The projection
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
is compact and Problem (
8
) is well-posed, assuming
-\kappa_{P}^{2}
and
-\kappa_{S}^{2}
are not eigenvalues of the associated homogeneous Laplace problem.

Proof.

From the compactness of the standard Sobolev embedding
\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
(see e.g.
[
22
, Theorem 7.1]
), and the continuity of
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{H}^{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
, it easily follows that
\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
is compact.

By using the relations (
20
) we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},(2\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})-\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}-(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v}-(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})+\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-2\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\kappa_{P}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-\kappa_{S}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})-\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v}),

where we have defined the auxiliary bilinear forms
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}},\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})+\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+2\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\kappa_{S}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-\kappa_{P}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt-\kappa_{S}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{v})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v})=2(\kappa_{P}^{2}+\kappa_{S}^{2})(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{v})_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}.

To show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
is coercive in
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
, we write

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\kappa_{P}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}\|((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\kappa_{P}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 55.48277pt+\kappa_{S}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}\|((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\kappa_{S}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 55.48277pt-\kappa_{P}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\hskip 55.48277pt-\kappa_{S}^{2}(((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}((\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S},((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\kappa_{P}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{P}^{2}\|((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{P}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}\|(\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa_{S}^{2}\|((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P})\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{S}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By using the relation

, the coercivity of

follows from the following inequality

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})

\displaystyle\geq\|\ddiv\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\ccurl\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\kappa_{P}^{2}}{2}\|\varphi^{P}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\kappa_{S}^{2}}{2}\|\varphi^{S}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}\apprge\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}^{2}.

From the coercivity of the bilinear form
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
, the invertibility of the associated operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)^{\prime}
follows. Moreover, by virtue of the compactness of

, we deduce that

, associated to the bilinear form

, is a compact operator (see e.g.
[

, Proposition 15.3]
).

Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
is a Fredholm operator, and so it is invertible if and only if it is injective that is if and only if the solution of (
18

-\kappa_{P}^{2}
and

are not eigenvalues for the associated homogeneous Laplace problem.
∎

3.1
Proof of the main result

Proposition 3.2
.

For all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)
it holds

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}.

(29)

Proof.

Following the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 1]
, it is easy to show that for
\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{P},v^{S})\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}v^{S},v^{P}\rangle_{\Gamma}-\langle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}v^{P},v^{S}\rangle_{\Gamma}.

Then, using the Hölder inequality and the trace theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\langle\partial_{\btau} v^S,v^P \rangle_{\Gamma}}+\absolutevalue{\langle\partial_{\btau} v^P,v^S\rangle_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}v^{S}\|_{H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}\|v^{P}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}+\|\partial_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}v^{P}\|_{H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}\|v^{S}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v^{S}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}\|v^{P}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}+\|v^{P}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}\|v^{S}\|_{H^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v^{S}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|v^{P}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the straightforward one
A^{2}+B^{2}+AB\leq\frac{3}{2}(A^{2}+B^{2})
for
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, the assertion (
29
) implies that the immersion
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
is continuous.
∎

Similarly, it is possible to prove that the immersion
\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}})\hookrightarrow\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}})

\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}})

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}})}\apprle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{h}})}.

(30)

From Proposition

(33)

Since the operator
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{K}
is invertible but not elliptic (and even, not Fredholm), we can not use standard tools to obtain stability results for its discrete counterpart
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
. Hence, we consider the slightly different operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{K}
, for which we are able to prove auxiliary properties that will allow us to carry out the theoretical analysis for our discrete operator. We start by showing that
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is elliptic in
\mathbf{V}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})=\|\ddiv\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\ccurl\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\kappa_{P}^{2}\|\varphi^{P}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\kappa_{S}^{2}\|\varphi^{S}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

Therefore,
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
being elliptic and continuous in the
\mathbf{V}
-norm, it is invertible with continuous inverse. To develop further our theoretical analysis, we introduce the following regularity assumption for the operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}

\bar{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}:\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to\boldsymbol{H}^{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\ \ \text{is continuous, for some}\ \ \varepsilon>0,

which is used in the next lemma for the discrete operator

Remark 3.5

The previous assumption is related to the modified bilinear form
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
, which is associated to the weak formulation of the time-harmonic Navier problem with the “good” sign. Our assumption is motivated by Section 4 of
[

, where such a modified operator is defined and analyzed to obtain wavenumber explicit estimates for the time-harmonic Maxwell equations.

Lemma 3.6

For any
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)

(34)

\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\apprle\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}\apprle(h_{P}^{\varepsilon}+h_{S}^{\varepsilon})\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

(36)

From the continuity of

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\bar{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\apprle\|\boldsymbol{w}-I_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\apprle(h_{P}^{\varepsilon}+h_{S}^{\varepsilon})\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1+\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}

(39)

Moreover, using the continuity of

and

\displaystyle II

In the following theorem we show the validity of the inf-sup condition for the discrete bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}

Theorem 3.7
.

For
h_{P}
and
h_{S}
small enough, it holds

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}\\
\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})}{\lVert\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}}\apprge\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}.

Proof.

Let us consider
\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
such that
\boldsymbol{q}={\mathcal{A}^{*}}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
, where
{\mathcal{A}^{*}}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)^{\prime}

{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathbf{J}:\mathbf{V}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{V}(\Omega)^{\prime}
denotes the canonical continuous map
(\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{w})(\boldsymbol{z})=(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z})_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}
. Hence we obtain

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{q})=(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{z})_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)},

(41)

for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{V}(\Omega)
, with

. Appealing to Lemma
3.6

satisfying

such that (
35
) holds for some
\varepsilon>0
. Now, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})+\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})-2\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})+\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})-2\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})-2\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})+2\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})-2\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})+2\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})-2\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}-\boldsymbol{q})+2(\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}})(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=:I+II+III.

From (
41
), it follows

and, from the continuity of
\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
and (

)

II\gtrsim-(h_{S}^{\varepsilon}+h_{P}^{\varepsilon})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

To estimate

24
), which implies the polynomial consistency of
\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
, and (
33
), we deduce that

\displaystyle III

\displaystyle\apprle\absolutevalue*{\mathcal{K}(\bw_{\bh} -\Pi_{\bk}^0 \bw_{\bh},\bq)}+\absolutevalue*{ (\mathcal{K}_{\bh}-\mathcal{K})(\Pi_{\bk}^{0} \bw_{\bh},\bq)}+\absolutevalue*{ \mathcal{K}_{\bh}(\bw_{\bh}-\Pi_{\bk}^0 \bw_{\bh},\bq)}

\displaystyle\apprle\|\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}-\Pi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{0}\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\boldsymbol{{L}}^{2}(\Omega)}\apprle(h_{P}+h_{S})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

Combining the estimates for

,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})\apprge(1-h_{S}^{\varepsilon}-h_{P}^{\varepsilon}-h_{S}-h_{P})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)},

from which, applying the first of (

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})}{\|\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}}\apprge\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}},\boldsymbol{q}_{\boldsymbol{h}})}{\|\boldsymbol{q}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}}\apprge(1-h_{S}^{\varepsilon}-h_{P}^{\varepsilon}-h_{S}-h_{P})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{\mathbf{V}(\Omega)}.

h_{S}
and

\displaystyle i\in\mathcal{S}_{S},j\in\mathcal{S}^{\Gamma}_{P}

and the right hand side vectors

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\diamond}=\left[\mathcal{F}_{h_{\diamond}}(\Phi^{\diamond}_{i})\right]_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\diamond}},\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{g}^{P}_{\mathbf{n}}=[g_{\mathbf{n}}^{P,j}]_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{P}},\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{g}^{S}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\left[g_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{S,j}\right]_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{S}}.

in the unknown vectors

. We refer to our approach as

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed a novel approach for the numerical simulation of two dimensional time-harmonic elastodynamics problems. It consists in reformulating the original PDE in terms of two coupled wave equations involving, as new unknowns, the
P-
and
S-
waves scalar potentials.

We have provided the stability analysis of the scalar system by means of the (non classical) T-coercivity of the bilinear form associated with the variational formulation and, for its approximation, we have applied a virtual element method. Convergence estimates have been derived and confirmed by numerical test.

This approach turns out to be a valid and competitive alternative to the vector displacement-based one. In particular, an advantage that we have highlighted for the scalar formulation is its feasibility in using different approximation orders and/or mesh sizes of the domain tessellation. This aspect revealed to be crucial when dealing with materials in which
P-
and
S-
waves are associated to different wave numbers, since it permits to tune the approximation parameters accordingly.
Furthermore, from the implementation view point, the proposed approach allowed us to use the well-established in-house software developed for standard Helmholtz problems, easily including curved geometries in the numerical investigation.

As a future development, we aim at extending the analysis of this approach to curved VEM as well as to exterior elastic problems, combining the interior VEM with a boundary one, both in the time-harmonic and in the space-time case. In particular, for the space-time case, we aim at making use of the numerical scheme for the classical two dimensional wave equation proposed in
[
38
]
.
