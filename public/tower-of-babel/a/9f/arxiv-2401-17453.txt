Flat, Uniformly Expanding Space in five Exercises
Bernd A. Berg

Abstract




Abstract

Exercises with solutions are presented which should allow advanced
undergraduate students to understand properties of a flat, uniformly
expanding space. No knowledge of general or special relativity is
needed besides that the speed of light
c
is a constant. The material
could be used stand alone or would fit well at the end of a treatment
of special relativity,

Using a reasonable value for the Hubble constant
H
, trajectories of
light are calculated within the model, followed by a calculation of the
Hubble horizon, and the synchronization of clocks is demonstrated.

Introduction

We consider flat space
(\vec{x},t)
, where a common time is defined
for all space points (synchronization of clocks can be achieved and is
discussed in exercise 5). Transformations to other coordinate systems
are not considered (the model is not invariant under general coordinate
transformations). Let us focus on two space points which are
initially, i.e., at common time
t=0
, located a distance
x_{0}>0

apart. The distance between them is assumed to increase according to

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)=x_{0}\exp(tH)\,

(1)

due to the Hubble expansion of space. Here
H
is the Hubble constant,
which is a constant in space, and we take it also to be a constant
in time. For sufficiently large time
t
the two points separate
faster than with the speed of light, which is often an issue of
confusion for students who just learned special relativity. Here
the resolution is that
c\,dt
, with
c
constant is only one of
two contributions to the infinitesimal motion of light.

It has to pointed out that our exercises aim at a qualitative
understanding, and we cannot expect quantitatively relevant estimates
of physical observables. Nevertheless we shall in numerical calculations
use physical values for
c
and
H
. Estimates for
H
are in the range

[
1
]
from 68 to 74
\ km/s
per
Mpc
. We use

\displaystyle H=71\ \rm km/s\ per\ Mpc\ .

(2)

In the following
we take the
x
-axis along the straight line which connects the two
points. The location of our first space point is by definition the
zero of the axis,
x=0
for all
t
. Our second space point lies at

x_{1}(t)
, given by Eq.(
1
), on the
x
-axis. At position
x_{0}=x_{1}(0)
we imagine a laser which emits at
t=0
an ultrashort photon
ray towards the first space point at
x=0
, where a photon detector is
located. There are no gravitational interactions as we are dealing
with a model of pure space.

2. Exercises and Solutions

Exercise 1: The Model.

Consider the infinitesimal motion
dx
of an ultrashort photon ray that
is at time
t=0
emitted at space point two towards space point one.
Find and verify the analytical solution for the travel path
x(t)

Solution:

For the position of the photon ray we derive a differential equation
from the infinitesimal motion:

\displaystyle dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c\,dt+x\,\{\exp[H\,(t+dt)]-\exp[H\,t]\}\ \ =\ -c\,dt+x\,\exp[H\,t]\,\{\exp[H\,dt]-1\}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c\,dt+x\,\exp[H\,t]\,H\,dt\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{dx}{dt}\ =\ -c+x\,H\,\exp[tH]\ ,

where
c
is the speed of light, and
H
the Hubble constant. The
solution is (Mathematica)

\displaystyle x(t)=\exp\left(e^{tH}\right)\,\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei\left(-e^{tH}\right)\right)\ ,

(4)

where
K_{1}
is an integration constant, and
Ei
is the exponential
integral

\displaystyle Ei(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{y}dt^{\prime}\,\frac{e^{t^{\prime}}}{t^{\prime}}=\gamma+\ln(|y|)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{k}}{k\,k!}\ .

(5)

Here
\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(-\ln(n)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k\right)

is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. In the following the solution
(
4
) is verified by differentiation. It is convenient to introduce
the variable
y=e^{tH}>0
and to break Eq.(
3
) up into

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}\ =\frac{dx}{dy}\frac{dy}{dt}\,,\ \ {\rm where}\ \ \frac{dy}{dt}=H\,e^{tH}=H\,y\ \ {\rm holds}.

(6)

It remains to calculate
dx/dy
. For
x(y)
we get from Eq.(
4
)

\displaystyle x(y)=e^{y}\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-y)\right)\ .

(7)

The
y
derivative of the exponential integral
Ei(-y)
is

\displaystyle\frac{dEi(-y)}{dy}=-\frac{dEi(-y)}{d(-y)}=-\frac{d\ }{d(-y)}\int_{-\infty}^{-y}dt^{\prime}\,\frac{e^{t^{\prime}}}{t^{\prime}}=-\frac{e^{-y}}{-y}=\frac{e^{-y}}{y}\ .

(8)

So we get

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dy}=e^{y}\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-y)\,\right)-\frac{c}{H\,y}\ .

(9)

Multiplying with
dy/dt=H\,y
yields

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dy}\frac{dy}{dt}=e^{y}\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-y)\right)\,H\,y-c\ .

(10)

Inserting
y=e^{tH}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=\exp\left(e^{tH}\right)\,\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-e^{tH})\right)\,H\,\exp(tH)-c=x\,H\,\exp(tH)-c\,,

(11)

where Eq.(
4
) has been used backwards for the last equal sign.

Exercise 2: Trajectories of Light.

Trajectories of light for
H=
71 km/s per Mpc and
various initial distances
x_{0}
.

Use numerical values for
c,\ H
and
x_{0}
to obtain the
x(t)

trajectories of Fig.(
1
), where the units are years
[y]

and light years
[ly]
.

Solution:

The integration constant
K_{1}
in Eq.(
4
) is determined by the
initial condition at time
t=0
,
x(0)=x_{0}>0
:

\displaystyle x_{0}=e^{1}\left(K_{1}-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-1)\right)\ \Rightarrow\ K_{1}=\frac{x_{0}}{e^{1}}+\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-1)\,,\ Ei(-1)=-0.219384\dots\ .

(12)

Inserting
K_{1}
, Eq.(
4
) becomes

\displaystyle x(t)=\exp\left(e^{tH}\right)\,\left(\frac{x_{0}}{e^{1}}+\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-1)-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei\left(-e^{tH}\right)\,\right)\ .

(13)

To plot
x(t)
we have to insert numbers for
c
and
H
, We measure
the time in units of years
[y]
. A light year
[ly]
is defined as
one year times the speed of light
c
. Therefore, the speed of light
is one in units of years and light years,

\displaystyle c=1\ [ly]/[y]\ .

(14)

Furthermore, we need to express
H
of Eq.(
2
) in units
of inverse years
[1/y]
. One Mpc is
3.262\times 10^{6}
[
ly
].
Converting
[ly]
to
[km]
, the distance unit
[km]

drops out of Eq.(
2
), and the value of
H
becomes

\displaystyle H=2.30222\times 10^{-18}\ [1/s]=7.2603\times 10^{-11}\ [1/y]\ ,

(15)

where we have converted seconds
[s]
to years
[y]
in the last step.
It is now straightforward to plot
x(t)
of Eq.(
13
) for various

x_{0}
values, and so that the results agree with Fig.(
1
).

Exercise 3: Hubble Horizon.

Find the largest value,
x_{0}^{\max}
, of the initial distance
x_{0}
,
so that the photon ray will reach its destination
x(t_{a})=0
for all
initial values
x_{0}<x_{0}^{\max}
. We call
x_{0}^{\max}
Hubble horizon
and
t_{a}
arrival time. Plot the relationship between initial condition

x_{0}
and arrival times
t_{a}
.

In particular, consider
t_{a}=13.7\times 10^{9}\ [y]
, i.e., the present
estimate
[
2
]
of the age of the universe, and calculate the
corresponding
x_{0}
. For these values indicate the vectors

(0,t_{a})\to(x_{0},ta)
and
(x_{0},t_{a})\to(x_{0},0)
in the figure.
What is the distance between point one and point two at time

t=t_{a}=13.7\times 10^{9}\ [y]
?

Solution:

In Fig.(
1
) we see that trajectories with initial values

x_{0}\leq 8.21\times 10^{9}\ [ly]
reach their destination
x(t_{a})=0
at
some arrival time
t_{a}
. To the contrary, for initial values
x_{0}\geq 8.22\times 10^{9}\ [ly]
the curves turn around before reaching
x=0
,
and an arrival time
t_{a}
does not exist. Hence, we have for the Hubble
horizon
8.21\times 10^{9}\ [ly]<x_{0}^{\max}<8.22\times 10^{9}\ [ly]
.
To get an accurate number for
x_{0}^{\max}
we set
t=t_{a}
and
x(t_{a})=0
in Eq.(
13
), and find the following relation between
t_{a}

and
x_{0}
:

\displaystyle Ei\left(-e^{t_{a}H}\right)=\frac{H\,x_{0}}{c\,e^{1}}+Ei(-1)\ .

(16)

The Hubble horizon is then obtained using the asymptotic behavior

\lim_{y\to\infty}Ei(-y)=0
:

\displaystyle x_{0}^{\max}=-\frac{c\,e^{1}\,Ei(-1)}{H}=8.2138\dots\times 10^{9}\ [ly]\ .

(17)

Initial distance
x_{0}
as function of the arrival time
t_{a}
,
where
x_{0}^{\max}
is the Hubble horizon. The arrows point from
t_{a}=13.7\times 10^{9}\ [y]
to
x_{0}=7.499\times 10^{9}\ [ly]
.

The relationship (
16
) between
x_{0}
and
t_{a}
is plotted
in Fig.(
2
). We see that for
t_{a}\to\infty
the value

x_{0}^{\max}
of Eq.(
17
) is rapidly approached.

The value
t_{a}=13.7\times 10^{9}\ y
corresponds to
x_{0}=7.499\times 10^{9}\ [ly]
. See the arrows in Fig.(
2
). During the travel
time the distance between the two space points increases according
to Eq.(
2
):

\displaystyle x_{1}(t_{a})=x_{0}\,\exp(t_{a}H)=20.473\ [ly]\ .

(18)

Exercise 4: Leading order small
t
expansion.

Towards small
t_{a}
it is seen in Fig.(
2
) how the relation

x_{0}=c\,t
becomes a good approximation. Let us discuss this quantitatively:
Expand
x(t)
of Eq.(
13
) to leading order in
t
. Transform the
result into a first-order correction to the zero-order arrival time

t_{a}=x_{0}/c
, and compute the correction for
x_{0}=1\,[ly]
.

Solution:

To leading order in
t
Eq.(
13
) reads

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(1+tH\right)\,\left(\frac{x_{0}}{e^{1}}+\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-1)-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei\left(-1-tH\right)\,\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{1}\,(1+tH)\,\left(\frac{x_{0}}{e^{1}}+\frac{c}{H}\,Ei(-1)-\frac{c}{H}\,Ei\left(-1-tH\right)\,\right)\ .

The derivative of the exponential integral is given by Eq.(
8
).
Defining
y=tH
and using the result of Eq.(
8
) we get

\displaystyle Ei(-1-y)=Ei(-1)-y\,\left.\frac{dEi(-y)}{d(-y)}\right|_{y=1}=Ei(-1)+tH\,e^{-1}\ .

(20)

The
Ei(-1)
terms cancel out in Eq.(
19
), and we arrive at

\displaystyle x(t)=x_{0}\,(1+tH)-c\,t\

(21)

Transforming Eq.(
21
) into a correction to
t_{a}
, we get

\displaystyle t_{a}=\frac{x_{0}}{c}+\frac{x_{0}H}{c^{2}}\ .

(22)

Assuming for
x_{0}
the distance of
1\,[ly]
, we obtain from the
first term
1\,[y]
(remember
c=1
in our units). Using
H

from Eq.(
15
), we obtain from the second term the correction

7.260^{-11}\ [y]=2.290^{-3}\ [s]
.

Exercise 5: Synchronization of clocks.

In our model the time is globally defined. This requires a procedure to
synchronize clocks at different space positions. The situation is as
follows: An observer A emits at time zero a photon ray in the direction
of a second observer B. The photon ray is reflected by B and arrives at
time
t_{a}>0
back at A. The entire information at hand is
t_{a}
from
which A has to calculate the time at which B reflected the signal. Show
that this is possible.

Conclude this section with the numerical example
t_{a}=5\times 10^{9}\ y
.
This is a value for which the
x_{0}(t_{a})
curve and the
x_{0}=c\,t_{a}
line
of Fig.(
2
) have clearly separated. Draw a diagram of the
exchange of signals.

Solution:

We denote by
t^{1}_{a}
the time the photon ray needs to travel from A to
B, and by
t^{2}_{a}
the time it needs to travel back from B to A. So we
have

Figure 3:
Signal exchange A
\to
B
\to
A
\to
B.

\displaystyle t_{a}\ =\ t^{1}_{a}+t^{2}_{a}\ .

(23)

Let us start with the guess:
t_{g_{1}}=t^{1}_{g_{1}}+t^{2}_{g_{1}}
with

t^{1}_{g_{1}}=t^{2}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}/2
. This is correct for
H=0
the situation of
special relativity. A would then tell B to set its clock to
t^{1}_{a}=t_{a}/2
plus whatever time has elapsed on B’s clock since receiving the
signal (A and B are machines).

However, for
H>0
the guess is false because
t^{1}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}/2

implies
t_{g_{1}}>t_{a}
. Namely, assume that the initial distance from
A to B is
x^{1}_{g_{1}}
, then the subsequent distance from B to A is
according to Eq.(
1
)
x^{2}_{g_{1}}=x^{1}_{g_{1}}\exp(t^{1}_{g_{1}}\,H)>x^{1}_{g^{1}}
. As long as
x^{2}_{g_{1}}<x_{0}^{\max}
there is a return
signal that needs a time
t^{2}_{g_{1}}>t^{1}_{g_{1}}
to travel from B
to A. See Fig.(
2
). As
t^{1}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}/2
it follows that

t_{g_{1}}=t^{1}_{g_{1}}+t^{2}_{g_{1}}>t_{a}
.

Now, with
t^{1}_{g_{1}}
given we can calculate
t^{2}_{g_{1}}
and, hence,

t_{g_{1}}
explicitly. As
t_{g_{1}}(t^{1}_{g_{1}})
is a monotonically
increasing function, we can compute
t^{1}_{a}
with the bisection method.
The relevant steps are outlined in the following.

Using Eq.(
16
), we find for the initial distance corresponding to
the travel time
t^{1}_{g_{1}}
:

\displaystyle x_{0}=x^{1}_{g_{1}}=\frac{c\,e^{1}}{H}\left[Ei\left(-\exp(t^{1}_{g_{1}}\,H)\right)-Ei(-1)\right]\ .

(24)

For the return signal from B to A the initial distance is then

x^{2}_{g_{1}}=x^{1}_{g_{1}}\exp(t^{1}_{g_{1}}\,H)
, which allows to calculate
the return time
t^{2}_{g_{1}}
using Eq.(
16
):

\displaystyle\exp(t^{2}_{g_{1}}\,H)=-Ei^{-1}\,\left(\frac{H\,x^{2}_{g_{1}}}{c\,e^{1}}+Ei(-1)\right)\,,

(25)

where
Ei^{-1}
is the inverse exponential integral.

Together with
t^{1}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}/2
any value
t^{1}_{g_{2}}>0
with

t_{g_{2}}=t^{1}_{g_{2}}+t^{2}_{g_{2}}<t_{a}
will give a starting point for the
bisection method. A suitable choice is
t^{1}_{g_{2}}=t^{1}_{g_{1}}-(t_{g_{1}}-t_{a})
, implying
t^{1}_{g_{2}}+t^{2}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}
. As
t^{1}_{g_{1}}<t^{1}_{g_{1}}

implies
t^{2}_{g_{2}}<t^{2}_{g_{1}}
, we have

t_{g_{2}}=t^{1}_{g_{2}}+t^{2}_{g_{2}}<t_{a}
, and the pair
t^{1}_{g_{2}}
,

t^{1}_{g_{1}}
provides staring values for the bisection method,
continuing with
t^{1}_{g_{3}}=(t^{1}_{g_{2}}+t^{1}_{g_{1}})/2
, replacing
in the next step either
t^{1}_{g_{2}}
(for
t_{g_{3}}<t_{a}
) or
t^{1}_{g1}

(for
t_{g_{3}}>t_{a}
) with
t^{1}_{g_{3}}
, and so on. After completing its
calculation A sends the result to B, who then sets its clock
accordingly.

For the numerical example we have
t^{1}_{g_{1}}=t_{a}/2=2.5\times 10^{9}\ [y]
, and get
t_{g_{1}}=5.5661\times 10^{9}\ [y]
,
t^{1}_{g_{2}}=t^{1}_{g_{1}}-(t_{g_{1}}-t_{a})=1.93391\times 10^{9}\ [y]
. With

t^{1}_{g_{1}}
and
t^{1}_{g_{2}}
we have starting values for our bisection
which proceeds with
t^{1}_{g_{3}}=(t^{1}_{g_{1}}+t^{1}_{g_{2}})/2=2.21695\times 10^{9}\ [y]\ \Rightarrow\ t_{g_{3}}=4.86586^{9}\ [y]
, then replacing

t^{1}_{g_{2}}
by
t^{1}_{g_{3}}
because of
t_{g_{3}}-t_{a}<0
, continuing
with a new
t^{1}_{g_{3}}=(t^{1}_{g_{1}}+t^{1}_{g_{2}})/2
, and so on, till
convergence is reached at
t_{a}=t_{g_{3}}=2.271888\times 10^{9}\ [y]
.
A signals this result for
t^{1}_{a}
to B, and B can set its clock
accordingly. Figure (
3
) illustrates the exchange of
signals. There are deviations from straight lines which are not
visible on the scale of this figure.

3. Summary

A simple model of an expanding universe is introduced. It allows for
a variety of analytical calculation. They are intended for qualitative,
pedagogical value and not of quantitative relevance. Results include:

Trajectories of light. See Fig.(
1
) for examples.

The initial distance
x_{0}
as function of the arrival time
t_{a}
(or
vice versa) and the Hubble horizon
x_{0}^{\max}
are drawn in Fig.(

2
).

Synchronization of clocks to a common time is outlined
in section 3, and a diagram of the corresponding exchange of signals
is given in Fig.(
3
