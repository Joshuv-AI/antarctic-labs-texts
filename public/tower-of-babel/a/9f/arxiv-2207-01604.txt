Lower bounds for adiabatic quantum algorithms by quantum speed limits
Jyong-Hao Chen

Abstract
We introduce a simple framework for estimating lower bounds on the runtime of a broad class of adiabatic quantum algorithms. The central formula consists of calculating the variance of the final Hamiltonian with respect to the initial state. After examining adiabatic versions of certain keystone circuit-based quantum algorithms, this technique is applied to adiabatic quantum algorithms with undetermined speedup. In particular, we analytically obtain lower bounds on adiabatic algorithms for finding k k -clique in random graphs. Additionally, for a particular class of Hamiltonian, it is straightforward to prove the equivalence between our framework and the conventional approach based on spectral gap analysis.

Formalism

Consider a quantum system characterized by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H^{\,}_{\lambda}

\lambda=\lambda(t)

For each
\lambda,

H^{\,}_{\lambda}|\Phi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle=E^{\,}_{\mathrm{GS},\lambda}|\Phi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle,

where

is the ground state energy.
On the other hand, the actual dynamics of the quantum system is described by the physical (or time-evolved) state
|\Psi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle

which is the solution to the scaled time-dependent Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\Gamma\partial^{\,}_{\lambda}|\Psi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle=H^{\,}_{\lambda}|\Psi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle

where
\Gamma\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\partial^{\,}_{t}\lambda(t)

Mathematically, adiabaticity can be quantified by the fidelity between the physical state

and the instantaneous ground state
|\Phi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle
,
namely, the
adiabatic fidelity

\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{\lambda}|\Psi^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle|^{2}.

In essence, the quantum adiabatic theorem
Born 1927
;
Born and Fock 1928
;
Kato 1950
;
Messiah 2014

ensures the following condition

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{F}(\lambda)\leq\epsilon,

(2)

provided that the driving rate
\Gamma
is small enough for any given allowance
\epsilon\in[0,1].

Building upon the seminal work of Ref.
Lychkovskiy et al. 2017

one of the main results of Ref.

that ties the adiabatic fidelity

,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(\lambda)-\mathcal{C}(\lambda)|\quad\leq\quad\sin\theta(\lambda)\quad\leq\quad\sin\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda),

(3)

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\min\left(\mathcal{R}(\lambda),\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\lambda)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\int^{\lambda}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\prime}}{|\Gamma(\lambda^{\prime})|}\delta E^{\,}_{0}(\lambda^{\prime}),

(4a)

\displaystyle\delta E^{\,}_{0}(\lambda)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\sqrt{\langle H^{2}_{\lambda}\rangle^{\,}_{0}-\langle H^{\,}_{\lambda}\rangle^{2}_{0}}.

Proof.

III.1
Example: Adiabatic Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm

Recall that in the problem of Deutsch-Jozsa

The task is to determine whether

is constant or balanced (i.e., equal number of output of 0’s and 1’s).
In the AQA proposed by Ref.

, the final state is chosen as (here,

)

\displaystyle|\Phi^{\,}_{1}\rangle=\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}|0\rangle+\frac{1-\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}}{\sqrt{N}}\sum^{N-1}_{i=1}|i\rangle,

(17)

\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,N^{-1}\left|\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{f(z)}\right|.

\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}=0

The corresponding final Hamiltonian can be constructed as a projector

16

The initial state is chosen as an equal weight superposition of all basis states [Eq. (
15

In Ref.

\lambda(t)=\frac{t}{T}

and

These results do not reach the well-known

Nevertheless, we shall demonstrate that using the formula Eq. (
14

obtain the lowest runtime found in Ref.

We compute the overlap

15

\displaystyle\left|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}+(1-\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}})\sqrt{N-1}\right)^{2},

(18)

and apply it to Eq. (
14

which is consistent with the result obtained by Ref.
Das et al. 2002
as mentioned above.

From the general expression of

[Eq. (
14
)],
it is clear that in order to reproduce the correct optimal runtime of the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm,

should be independent of

.
In light of this, the final state [Eq. (

Exactly this modification has been carried out in Ref.

by replacing the final state of Eq. (

with the one which is more symmetric in amplitudes:

\displaystyle|\Phi^{\,}_{1}\rangle=\frac{\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum^{N/2-1}_{i=0}|2i\rangle+\frac{1-\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum^{N/2-1}_{i=0}|2i+1\rangle.

\displaystyle\left|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{N/2}\left(\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}}\frac{N}{2}+(1-\mu^{\,}_{\mathrm{f}})\frac{N}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

This result should be compared with that of Eq. (

Bringing Eq. (

Therefore, the optimal runtime for the Deutsch-Jozsa problem is attained.

Das et al. 2002

Wei and Ying 2006

|f^{\,}_{\lambda}(z)\rangle^{\,}_{\mathrm{B}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|+\rangle^{\,}_{\mathrm{B}}+(-1)^{f^{\,}_{\lambda}(z)}|-\rangle^{\,}_{\mathrm{B}}\right).

Finally, it follows from Eq. (
14
) that

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(1)

as expected.

IV
Final Hamiltonian of type-II

For optimization problems with a cost function
h\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we seek a minimum of
h.

In the framework of adiabatic quantum computation
Farhi et al. 2000
;
Farhi et al. 2001
,
a final Hamiltonian can be defined to be diagonal in the computational basis, with cost function
h

being the diagonal element

\displaystyle H^{\,}_{1}=\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}h(z)|z\rangle\langle z|.

(23)

If the uniform superposition of all basis states [Eq. (
15
)] is chosen as the initial state,
the quantum uncertainty [Eq. (
9b
)] reads

\delta V^{\,}_{n}=(\overline{h^{2}}-\overline{h}^{2})^{1/2},

with the arithmetic average

\overline{h}\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,2^{-n}\sum^{\,}_{z}h(z)

and

\overline{h^{2}}\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,2^{-n}\sum^{\,}_{z}h^{2}(z).

Furthermore, if we impose the moments condition [Eq. (
13
)],
which amounts to requiring

\displaystyle\overline{h^{2}}\overset{!}{=}\overline{h},

(24)

then
the lower bound
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}
[Eq. (
14
)]

\displaystyle T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1/\delta V^{\,}_{n}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\delta V^{\,}_{n}=\sqrt{\overline{h}\left(1-\overline{h}\right)}

(25)

is completely determined by the arithmetic average of the cost function,
i.e.,
\overline{h}.

Note that

0\leq\overline{h}\leq 1

as a result of Cauchy-Schwarz
together with the moments condition [Eq. (
24
)].
Note also that the moments condition Eq. (
24
)
can be expressed using matrix analysis terminology
Bhatia 1996
;
Horn and Johnson 2012
as
\|H^{\,}_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\overset{!}{=}\mathrm{tr}H^{\,}_{1}
,
where
\|\cdot\|^{\,}_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm (or Hilbert-Schmidt norm).
If it is further assumed that the cost function
h
is non-negative, the moments condition [Eq. (
24
)]
can alternatively be written as

\|H^{\,}_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}\overset{!}{=}\|H^{\,}_{1}\|^{\,}_{\mathrm{tr}}
, where
\|\cdot\|^{\,}_{\mathrm{tr}}
represents the trace norm (or nuclear norm).

Example: Adiabatic Grover search

Taking the unstructured search problem of Grover
Grover 1996
;
Grover 1997
as an example,
it can be formulated as a combinatorial optimization problem with the following cost function
Farhi et al. 2001
;
van Dam et al. 2001
:

h(z)=0
for
M
marked items and
h(z)=1
otherwise.
For this case, one immediately finds
\overline{h^{2}}=\overline{h}=1-M/N

(here,
N\equiv 2^{n}
).
It then follows from Eq. (
25
) that a lower bound

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N/M}),

as expected
Bennett et al. 1997
;
Farhi and Gutmann 1998
;
Roland and Cerf 2002
.
We note that the final Hamiltonian of adiabatic Grover search can be equivalently written as
H^{\,}_{1}=\mathbb{I}-\sum^{\,}_{z\in\mathcal{M}}|z\rangle\langle z|
,
where
\mathcal{M}
is the space of solution (of size
M
).
Hence, this final Hamiltonian also belongs to the type-I final Hamiltonian [see Eq. (
16
)].
It is with this expression of projector final Hamiltonian that
Ref.
Lychkovskiy 2018

used a formula similar to Eq. (
10
)
to obtain the optimal runtime for adiabatic Grover search.
For completeness, we present a similar calculation using our formula in Appendix
B
.

Thus far, three adiabatic versions of keystone quantum algorithms have been examined.
We shall now apply our technique to the adiabatic algorithm proposed by Childs
et al.

Childs et al. 2002
for finding
k
-clique in random graphs.

V
Adiabatic algorithm for finding
k
-clique

Consider a random graph
G
where every pair of vertices is connected or disconnected with a probability of 1/2.
A
clique
is a subgraph of
G
in which every pair of vertices is connected by an edge.
The problem of finding cliques of
k
vertices (called
k
-clique
) in a random graph of
n
vertices
is an NP-complete problem
if both
n
and
k
are treated as inputs
Karp 1972
;
Moore and Mertens 2011
.

In the algorithm proposed by Ref.
Childs et al. 2002
, each vertex is associated with a qubit.
Hence, there are
n
qubits for a graph of
n
vertices.
As before, each qubit state is represented by
|z^{\,}_{i}\rangle
with
z^{\,}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for
i\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}.

A vertex
i
is included into a subgraph of
k
vertices only if
z^{\,}_{i}=1.

Although the full Hilbert space is
2^{n}
-dimensional,
we can focus only on the subspace spanned by quantum states with Hamming weight
k

since we are only interested in those quantum states that represent cliques of
k
vertices.
For notational convenience, we denote the set of
n
-bit binary strings of Hamming weight
k
as

\mathcal{S}^{\,}_{k}\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\left\{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}\colon|z|=k\right\},

where
|z|=\sum^{n-1}_{i=0}z^{\,}_{i}
is the Hamming weight of the
n
-bit binary string
z.

The size of
\mathcal{S}^{\,}_{k}
is
\binom{n}{k}\equiv C(n,k).

In the subspace generated by binary strings from
\mathcal{S}^{\,}_{k}
,
the initial state can be chosen as a
Dicke state
Dicke 1954
;
Tóth 2007
;
Bärtschi and Eidenbenz 2019

\displaystyle|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{C(n,k)}}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\mathcal{S}^{\,}_{k}}|z\rangle.

(26)

In Ref.
Childs et al. 2002
,
the final Hamiltonian is of the form
shown in Eq. (
23
)
with the cost function

\displaystyle h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\sum^{n-1}_{i,j=0:i>j}\left(1-G^{\,}_{ij}\right)z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j},

(27)

where
G^{\,}_{ij}
with
i,j\in\{0,\cdots,n-1\}
is the matrix element of the adjacency matrix for a graph
G.

As usual,
G^{\,}_{ij}=1
if the vertices
i
and
j
are connected by an edge;
otherwise,
G^{\,}_{ij}=0.

Observe that
the cost function
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)
takes values in
\{0,1,\cdots,L^{\,}_{k}\}
,
where
L^{\,}_{k}\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,C(k,2)
is the total number of edges for a graph of
k
vertices.

We proceed to obtain a lower bound on runtime using Eq. (
11
) for the algorithm defined above.
Since each
G^{\,}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
is a random variable,
an exact computation for the arithmetic average
\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}
and
\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}

is not possible without knowing an explicit instance of
G^{\,}_{ij}.

We attempt to consider a “mean-field” (or “randomized”) approach by replacing
G^{\,}_{ij}
with its (classical) expectation value
\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]=1/2,

and obtain (see Appendix
C
)

\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}]=L^{\,}_{k}/2

and

\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}]=(L^{\,}_{k}+1)L^{\,}_{k}/4.

It follows from Eq. (
11
) that a lower bound on runtime
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{rand},\mathrm{inf}}
reads

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{rand},\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{1/L^{\,}_{k}}),

which is independent of
n.

It is likely that this
n
-independent lower bound is considerably lower than the sufficient runtime obtained using the spectral gap analysis
(though it is not available).
We also notice that the moments condition
[Eq. (
24
)]
is not met for the cost function defined in
Eq. (
27
).
Nevertheless, we note that the
k
dependence in
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{rand},\mathrm{inf}}
seems consistent with the numerical data found in Ref.
Childs et al. 2002
,
saying that the median runtime for finding cliques of
k=5
is longer than that for finding cliques of
k=6
.
Specifically,
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{med}}(k=5)/T^{\,}_{\mathrm{med}}(k=6)=30.87/18.56\approx 1.66
found in Ref.
Childs et al. 2002
,
whereas our
result indicates

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{rand},\mathrm{inf}}(k=5)/T^{\,}_{\mathrm{rand},\mathrm{inf}}(k=6)=\sqrt{15/10}\approx 1.22.

To make better use of our formula Eq. (
25
),
we propose the following deformed cost function

\displaystyle h(z)\,\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\!\!=\,\frac{1}{2}\left(1+h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)-\left|1-h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)\right|\right).

(28)

This amounts to introduce

h(z)=\min(h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z),1),

where
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)
is defined in Eq. (
27
).
In other words, those
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)>1
are mapped to
h(z)=1.

Therefore, the deformed cost function
is Boolean-valued
h(z)\in\{0,1\}.

This deformed cost function is similar to that of adiabatic Grover search discussed previously.
One finds that the cost function defined in Eq. (
28
)
along with the initial state defined in Eq. (
26
) satisfies the moments condition
[Eq. (
24
)].
Explicitly,

\overline{h}=\overline{h^{2}}=\left(1-M/C(n,k)\right),

which renders
a lower bound from Eq. (
25
)

\displaystyle T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{C(n,k)/M}\right),

(29)

where
M
is the number of cliques of
k
vertices.
The result [Eq. (
29
)] is
consistent with that of a circuit-based algorithm of Ref.
Metwalli et al. 2020

in which the time complexity is related to the number of Grover iterations.
For the special case of
n\gg k
, Eq. (
29
) simplifies

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{k/2}\right)

for

n\gg k.

A well-studied case is
k=3,
i.e., the so-called
3-clique problem
(or
triangle-finding
problem);
the resulting lower bound is
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2}).

The exponent,
3/2
,
agrees with that found in a
quantum algorithm
Buhrman et al. 2005

for the triangle-finding problem
using a plain Grover search.

VI
Discussions

VI.1
Connection to spectral gap analysis

We shall now attempt to connect our approach with conventional spectral gap analysis.
An equivalence between the two approaches can be directly shown for a particular class of Hamiltonians
where both the initial and final Hamiltonians are of the projector form

H^{\,}_{0}=\mathbb{I}-|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\langle\Phi^{\,}_{0}|

and
H^{\,}_{1}=\mathbb{I}-|\Phi^{\,}_{1}\rangle\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|
.
It was then proved in Ref.
Aharonov and Ta‐Shma 2007
that the spectral gap of the full Hamiltonian
H^{\,}_{\lambda}
[Eq. (
1
)],
denoted as
g(H^{\,}_{\lambda}),
is bounded from below:

g(H^{\,}_{\lambda})\geq|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|\equiv g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}},

where
g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}}=\min^{\,}_{\lambda\in[0,1]}g(H^{\,}_{\lambda})
is the minimal spectral gap.
If the schedule function
\lambda(t)
is chosen simply as
\lambda(t)=t/T,

spectral gap analysis
Roland and Cerf 2002
;
Jansen et al. 2007
yields the scaling of runtime

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{gap}}\sim\mathcal{O}(1/g^{2}_{\mathrm{min}}).

Furthermore, it is possible to improve
Roland and Cerf 2002
;
Jansen et al. 2007
the error dependence on the minimal gap
by adopting a nonlinear schedule function; if so,
one obtains

T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gap}}\sim\mathcal{O}(1/g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}}).

On the other hand,
using our formalism, we directly obtain

\langle H^{\,}_{1}\rangle^{\,}_{0}=\langle H^{2}_{1}\rangle^{\,}_{0}=1-|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|^{2}=1-g^{2}_{\mathrm{min}}.

Consequently,
a lower bound on runtime follows from Eq. (
14
):

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(1/g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}}).

If the minimal gap
g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}}=|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|
is independent of
n
,
then
the two estimates
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{gap}}
and
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}

are equal:
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{gap}}=T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
This is the case for the adiabatic Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm and the adiabatic Bernstein-Vazirani algorithm
discussed previously.
For the case of adiabatic Grover search, the optimal runtime obtained from the spectral gap analysis
along with a nonlinear schedule function yields
Roland and Cerf 2002

T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{gap}}=\mathcal{O}(1/g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}})
,
which again agrees with our lower bound
T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(1/g^{\,}_{\mathrm{min}})
.

VI.2
Comparison with prior works

The performance of adiabatic quantum algorithms has been extensively investigated in a variety of settings.
Among the existing research on this topic, three groups of studies that utilize quantum speed limits are particularly relevant to our current work.
(i) A new class of quantum speed limits is presented in Refs.
Kieu 2019a
;
Kieu 2019b
and applied to adiabatic searches.
A lower bound on runtime is defined there as the minimum time it takes for the physical state to be orthogonal to the initial state.
This definition is, however, too restrictive.
4
4

4

Indeed, the final state of most adiabatic quantum algorithms is never orthogonal to the initial state, even though the overlap between the two is typically very small.

Additionally, it is unclear whether the results obtained in Refs.
Kieu 2019a
;
Kieu 2019b
are applicable beyond adiabatic searches.

(ii)
While the main result of Ref.
Lychkovskiy 2018
is an inequality similar to Eq. (
10
),
the present work makes several significant advances.
Our method for estimating lower bounds is more systematic and rigorous,
and we carefully clarified the applicability of Eq. (
10
).
In particular, we highlighted the importance of the asymptotic property [Eq. (
12
)]
and the irrelevance of the schedule function
\lambda(t)
in asymptotic analysis.
Furthermore, we explored a wider range of adiabatic quantum algorithms, including the adiabatic Grover algorithm examined in Ref.
Lychkovskiy 2018

as well as several additional adiabatic algorithms.
Notably, we obtained an analytical result on lower bounds for the adiabatic algorithm of Childs et al.
Childs et al. 2002
for
finding
k
-clique in random graphs, which is an algorithm with undetermined quantum speedup.

(iii)
It is also worth noting that
quantum speed limits and the formalism of shortcuts to adiabaticity
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Demirplak and Rice 2005
;
Berry 2009
;
Chen et al. 2010
;
Torrontegui et al. 2013
;
del Campo 2013
;
Jarzynski 2013
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019

are combined in Ref.
Suzuki and Takahashi 2020

to investigate the performance of adiabatic quantum computation.
However, the purpose and scope of Ref.
Suzuki and Takahashi 2020
is significantly different from ours.

VII
Concluding remarks

We have shown that for a wide class of adiabatic quantum algorithms (AQAs)
in which
the asymptotic property presented in
Eq. (
12
) holds,
nontrivial lower bounds on the runtime of adiabatic algorithms can be estimated by calculating
the quantum uncertainty of the final Hamiltonian with respect to the initial state
[see Eq. (
11
)].
A runtime estimation obtained by conventional spectral gap analysis is by no means smaller than the necessary runtime derived using our method.
The reason is that our formula provides a necessary condition that must be obeyed by the runtime of adiabatic quantum evolution.
Choosing a specific schedule function or initial Hamiltonian may cause the necessary runtime to be saturated.

Our findings may shed new light on the design of new adiabatic quantum algorithms.
For instance, if a potential quantum speedup of an adiabatic quantum algorithm
is undetermined,
one may attempt a deformation of the original adiabatic algorithm to fulfill the moments condition
[Eq. (
13
)]
and then apply
Eq. (
14
)
to obtain a lower bound on runtime.
The runtime of the deformed algorithm thus obtained may be utilized to estimate the spectral gap (and hence, a sufficient runtime)
of the original algorithm
by means of matrix inequalities such as Weyl’s inequalities
Bhatia 1996
;
Horn and Johnson 2012
.
For optimization problems, it is important to emphasize that the lower bound formula [Eq. (
25
)]
is not valid for every optimization problem but only for those in which the cost function meets the moments condition
[Eq. (
24
)].
Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate further the implications of the moments condition on general Boolean functions
using techniques from Boolean function analysis

O’Donnell 2014
.
Another future direction would be to derive bounds on the runtime of adiabatic quantum algorithms in open quantum systems.

Appendix B
Detail calculation for adiabatic Grover search

Consider a set of
N
items among which
M<N
items are marked,
the goal being to find marked items in minimum time.
We use
n
qubits to encode the
N
items.
Hence, the Hilbert space is of dimension
N=2^{n}
.
In this space, the basis states can be written as

|i\rangle
with
i\in\{0,\cdots,N-1\}.

The desired final state is

|\Phi^{\,}_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum^{\,}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}|m\rangle,

and the corresponding final Hamiltonian can be chosen as a projector

H^{\,}_{1}=\mathbb{I}-\sum^{\,}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}|m\rangle\langle m|,

where
\mathcal{M}
is the space of solution (of size
M
).
One can easily see that the ground state of
H^{\,}_{1}

is
|\Phi^{\,}_{1}\rangle

with eigenenergy zero.

The remaining task is to calculate the quantum uncertainty
\delta V^{\,}_{n}
[Eq. (
9b
)]
with
|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle
given by the uniform superposition state [Eq. (
15
)].
First, we calculate the overlap
|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|^{2},

\displaystyle|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum^{\,}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\langle m|\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum^{N-1}_{i=0}|i\rangle\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{MN}\left|\sum^{\,}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum^{N-1}_{i=0}\langle m|i\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{M}{N}.

(32)

We then obtain

\langle\Phi^{\,}_{0}|H^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle=1-|\langle\Phi^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle|^{2}=1-\frac{M}{N}.

Bringing this result to Eq. (
14
) yields

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N/M}).

Appendix C
Detail calculation for adiabatic algorithm of finding k-clique in random graphs

We want to calculate
\langle\Phi^{\,}_{0}|H^{\,}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\,=\!\!\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\,\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}

and
\langle\Phi^{\,}_{0}|H^{2}_{1}|\Phi^{\,}_{0}\rangle\,=\!\!\raisebox{0.85pt}{.}\hskip-2.78pt\raisebox{2.85pt}{.}\,\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}},

where

\begin{split}&\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z),\\
&\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}(z),\end{split}

(33)

with the cost function

h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)=\sum^{\,}_{i>j}(1-G^{\,}_{ij})z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}

as defined in Eq. (
27
).
To conduct the calculation, one encounters terms like
\sum^{\,}_{i>j}G^{\,}_{ij},

which cannot be done generically without knowing the explicit form of
G^{\,}_{ij}.

We attempt a “mean-field” approach by considering the
“average property” of
G^{\,}_{ij}
since each pair of vertices has probability
p
to be connected
and probability (
1-p
) otherwise.
Note that the value of
p
is
1/2
in the main text.

C.1
Mean-field approach

We shall approximate
G^{\,}_{ij}
by its expected value,
\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}].

Specifically, since each
G^{\,}_{ij}=1
with probability
p
and
G^{\,}_{ij}=0
with probability
1-p,

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]=p.

(34)

We now compute
\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}
(
33
):

\displaystyle\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}(1-G^{\,}_{ij})z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}(1-\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}])z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}.

(35)

There are
C(n,k)
states
|z\rangle
satisfy
|z|=k
that we have to consider.
For each such state, there are
k
’s
z^{\,}_{i}
for which
z^{\,}_{i}=1.

Therefore, the first term in the parenthesis of Eq. (
35
) is simply

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}=\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}C(k,2)

\displaystyle=C(n,k)\,C(k,2)=C(n,k)\,L^{\,}_{k}.

(36)

Here, for notational convenience, we define
L^{\,}_{k}\equiv C(k,2).

The second term in the parenthesis of Eq. (
35
)
can be calculated with the help of Eq. (
34
)

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}=pC(n,k)L^{\,}_{k}.

(37)

Bringing Eqs. (
36
)
and (
37
) back to
Eq. (
35
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\left(1-p\right)L^{\,}_{k}.

(38)

Next, we compute
\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}
(
33
):

\displaystyle\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\left(1-2G^{\,}_{ij}+G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}G^{\,}_{ij}\right)z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\Big(1-2\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]+\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}G^{\,}_{ij}\right]\Big)z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}.

(39)

There are three terms in the parenthesis of Eq. (
39
).
The first term is deterministic,

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\left(\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}\right)\left(\sum^{\,}_{i>j}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}\right)=\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}L^{\,}_{k}L^{\,}_{k}=C(n,k)\,L^{2}_{k}.

(40)

The second term in the parenthesis of Eq. (
39
) reads

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\left(-2\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]\right)z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}

\displaystyle=-2\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\left(\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}\right)\left(\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq: average of G})}}{{=}}-2\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\left(\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}\right)\left(\sum^{\,}_{i>j}pz^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}\right)=-2pC(n,k)L^{2}_{k}.

(41)

Before proceeding to calculate the third term in the parenthesis of Eq. (
39
),
we notice the following decomposition property

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}G^{\,}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{ij}G^{\,}_{ij}\right]+\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime},i>j:i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}G^{\,}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{2}_{ij}\right]+\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime},i>j:i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right]\mathbb{E}[G^{\,}_{ij}]

\displaystyle=\sum^{\,}_{i>j}p+\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime},i>j:i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}}p^{2}=L^{\,}_{k}p+\left(L^{2}_{k}-L^{\,}_{k}\right)p^{2}.

(42)

Finally, the third term in the parenthesis of Eq. (
39
) is

\displaystyle\sum^{\,}_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|=k}\left(\sum^{\,}_{i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}}\sum^{\,}_{i>j}\mathbb{E}\left[G^{\,}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}G^{\,}_{ij}\right]z^{\,}_{i^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{j^{\prime}}z^{\,}_{i}z^{\,}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq: average of G square})}}{{=}}pC(n,k)L^{2}_{k}\left(p+\frac{(1-p)}{L^{\,}_{k}}\right).

(43)

Upon using the above results,
Eq. (
39
)
reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\left(1-2p+p\left(p+\frac{1-p}{L^{\,}_{k}}\right)\right)L^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{2}L^{2}_{k}+p(1-p)L^{\,}_{k}.

(44)

Hence, the quantum uncertainty reads

\delta V^{\,}_{n}=\sqrt{p(1-p)L^{\,}_{k}}.

It then follows from Eq. (
11
) a lower bound on runtime

\displaystyle T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{1/L^{\,}_{k}}\right),

(45)

which is independent of
n.

Note that if the cost function
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)
is rescaled as
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)\to h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)/L^{\,}_{k},

one should find

T^{\,}_{\mathrm{inf}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{L^{\,}_{k}}\right).

C.2
Combinatorial approach

In this section, we use an explicit combinatorial method to reproduce the same result as we found in
Eq. (
45
).
The following observation is crucial:
If a binary string
z
of Hamming weight
k
represents a graph that is a
k
-clique after adding
\alpha
connected edges,
then
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)=\alpha,
where
\alpha\in\{0,1,\cdots,C(k,2)\}.

To simplify the notation, let us define
L^{\,}_{k}\equiv C(k,2)
for convenience.
Now the question is what is the multiplicity
m^{\,}_{\alpha}
for each possible value of cost function
h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}(z)=\alpha
.
Since each of the
L^{\,}_{k}
edges has probability
p
to be present when random graphs are generated uniformly, we expect

\displaystyle m^{\,}_{\alpha}=p^{L^{\,}_{k}-\alpha}(1-p)^{\alpha}C(L^{\,}_{k},\alpha)\,C(n,k),

(46)

for
\alpha\in\{0,1,\cdots,L^{\,}_{k}\}
.
One verifies that the sum of all multiplicity equals
C(n,k),
i.e., the number of binary strings of Hamming weight
k

\displaystyle\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}m^{\,}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=C(n,k)\underbrace{\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}p^{L^{\,}_{k}-\alpha}(1-p)^{\alpha}C(L^{\,}_{k},\alpha)}^{\,}_{=(p+1-p)^{L^{\,}_{k}}}

\displaystyle=C(n,k).

(47)

We proceed to compute
\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{\,}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}m^{\,}_{\alpha}\alpha=\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}p^{L^{\,}_{k}-\alpha}(1-p)^{\alpha}C(L^{\,}_{k},\alpha)\alpha

\displaystyle=(1-p)L^{\,}_{k},

(48)

which is the same as Eq. (
38
).
We then compute
\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{h^{2}_{\mathrm{C}}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C(n,k)}\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}m^{\,}_{\alpha}\alpha^{2}=\sum^{L^{\,}_{k}}_{\alpha=0}p^{L^{\,}_{k}-\alpha}(1-p)^{\alpha}C(L^{\,}_{k},\alpha)\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p)^{2}L^{2}_{k}+p(1-p)L^{\,}_{k},

(49)

which is identical to Eq. (
44
).
