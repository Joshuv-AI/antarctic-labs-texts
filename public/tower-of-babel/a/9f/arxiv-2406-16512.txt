Title
Philipp Jettkant

Abstract
We consider a control problem for the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation. This equation describes the evolution of the distribution of nonlocally interacting particles affected by a common source of noise. The system is directed by a controller that acts on the drift term with the goal of minimising a cost functional. We establish the well-posedness of the state equation, prove the existence of optimal controls, and formulate a stochastic maximum principle (SMP) that provides necessary and sufficient optimality conditions for the control problem. The adjoint process arising in the SMP is characterised by a nonlocal (semi)linear backward SPDE for which we study existence and uniqueness. We also rigorously connect the control problem for the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation to the control of the corresponding McKean–Vlasov SDE that describes the motion of a representative particle. Our work extends existing results for the control of the Fokker–Planck equation to nonlinear and stochastic dynamics. In particular, the sufficient SMP, which we obtain by exploiting the special structure of the Fokker–Planck equation, seems to be novel even in the linear deterministic setting. We illustrate our results with an application to a model of government interventions in financial systems, supplemented by numerical illustrations.

Introduction

In this article we study the optimal control of the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation

\mathrm{d}\langle\nu_{t},\varphi\rangle=\bigl\langle\nu_{t},\mathcal{L}\varphi(t,\cdot,\nu_{t},\gamma_{t})\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle\nu_{t},(\nabla\varphi)^{\top}\sigma_{0}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(1.1)

for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in[0,T]
. Here the second-order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is the generator of a nonlinear diffusion process, the control
\gamma
is a space-dependent progressively measurable process,
W
is a
d_{W}
-dimensional Brownian motion, and
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{W}}
. A controller chooses the control
\gamma
with the objective of minimising the cost functional

J(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nu_{t},f(t,\cdot,\nu_{t},\gamma_{t})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\psi(\nu_{T})\biggr]

over
\gamma
in some admissible set. The measure-valued solution
\nu
of the Fokker–Planck equation corresponds to the conditional law of a representative particle whose motion is described by a controlled McKean–Vlasov SDE with generator
\mathcal{L}
and a common noise, modelled by the Brownian motion
W
. The latter problem is referred to as McKean–Vlasov or mean-field control (MFC) and has received widespread attention in recent years
[
5
,
44
,
24
,
56
,
30
,
19
]
. While most of the literature studies MFC problems from the point of view of the McKean–Vlasov SDE, in our analysis we emphasise the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation (
1.1
). This perspective is particularly appropriate when the generator
\mathcal{L}
includes zeroth-order terms, in which case the measure
\nu_{t}
gains or loses mass over time. This change in mass can be interpreted as the insertion or killing of particles at a state-dependent rate in the associated McKean–Vlasov SDE. Models featuring such mechanisms were recently introduced in
[
33
]
and
[
27
]
. The insertion and killing mechanism manifests itself as an additional process in the McKean–Vlasov SDE, appended to the particle’s state, that tracks the cumulative insertion or killing intensity. As we prove in Theorem
A.2
, knowledge of this process is not necessary to optimally control the McKean–Vlasov SDE. Nonetheless, we cannot dispense with it in the specification of the representative particle. On the other hand, in the Fokker–Planck formulation of the control problem, the intensity is encoded as a zeroth-order term in the generator
\mathcal{L}
. No additional equations are required, leading to a cleaner description.

As indicated above, the goal of the present paper is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the control problem for the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation (
1.1
). First, we establish the well-posedness of this equation for any admissible control
\gamma
. Our next objective is to prove the existence of optimal controls through the use of tightness arguments. Then, we formulate both a necessary and a sufficient stochastic maximum principle (SMP) for the control problem. Lastly, we apply our results to the MFC model for government bailouts from
[
33
]
, illuminating the optimal range for capital injections by the government. We also discuss the equivalence between the optimal control of the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation and the optimal control of the associated McKean–Vlasov SDE to justify our approach (see Appendix
A.1
). In our analysis we exploit that the stochasticity in the Fokker–Planck equation can be removed by shifting the solution
\nu
of this stochastic PDE (SPDE) along the noise
W
. This requires a constant (or, more generally, time-dependent) diffusion coefficient
\sigma_{0}
. Through the shift, the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation is transformed into a PDE with random coefficients – a nonlinear random Fokker–Planck equation. This PDE captures the evolution of the distribution of a living representative particle whose state dynamics follow a McKean–Vlasov SDE with random coefficients. The same method was applied in
[
20
]
and, more recently, in
[
23
,
22
]
to study the existence of mean-field game (MFG) equilibria. (In a MFG, the particles described by the Fokker–Planck equation are in competition as opposed to being controlled simultaneously by a single agent.)

It is convenient to study the random Fokker–Planck equation as a stochastic evolution equation in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, assuming the initial measure
\nu_{0}
admits a density
\rho_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. A slight complication arises because, inspired by the correspondence with McKean–Vlasov SDEs, the nonlinearities in the measure argument, i.e. with respect to
\nu_{t}
, of the generator
\mathcal{L}
are assumed to be Lipschitz continuous with respect to the bounded Lipschitz distance on the space of measures. In general, such nonlinearities, when viewed as a function of the density of a measure, will not be continuous with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm. This leads us to consider a weighted
L^{2}
-space whose norm dominates the bounded Lipschitz distance in a suitable way. In this framework, the adjoint process arising in the necessary and the sufficient SMP is naturally an element of the dual of this weighted
L^{2}
-space. This dual can be identified as the weighted
L^{2}
-space whose weight is the multiplicative inverse of the original weight. While the shifted state process satisfies a random PDE, the adjoint process is nonetheless characterised through a stochastic equation. As for classical control problems, this equation runs backwards and to ensure adaptedness of the adjoint process, a martingale is introduced in the adjoint PDE, which removes randomness as one steps backwards in time. In other words, the adjoint process satisfies a linear backward SPDE (BSPDE). Since the tightness arguments employed to prove the existence of an optimal control force us to work in a general filtration, which may extend that of the Brownian motion
W
, the martingale driving the BSPDE may have merely càdlàg as opposed to continuous trajectories. In addition, the nonlinearities of the Fokker–Planck equation translate into nonlocalities for this BSPDE. In summary, the adjoint process is characterised by a nonlocal BSPDE with càdlàg martingale driver.

By inserting the necessary optimality condition from the SMP into the adjoint BSPDE, we derive a nonlocal semilinear BSPDE of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) type. We provide a general existence and uniqueness analysis of such nonlocal (semi)linear BSPDEs with càdlàg martingale driver. Together with the random Fokker–Planck equation, the stochastic HJB equation satisfied by the adjoint process forms a coupled forward-backward system of stochastic PDEs reminiscent of the classical MFG systems appearing in the pioneering work of Lasry & Lions
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. Indeed, our forward-backward SPDE, deduced from the optimality condition of the necessary SMP, characterises a MFG equilibrium with extended running and terminal cost functions deriving from
f
and
\psi
. This connection between MFC and MFGs was already established in
[
43
]
, but we also refer to the account in
[
25
, Section 6.2.5]
which resembles our setup more closely. Under a rather restrictive convexity assumption and if the minimiser of the Hamiltonian associated with the control of SPDE (
1.1
) is unique, it follows from the sufficient SMP that this forward-backward SPDE has a unique solution. This in turn implies that the associated MFG equilibrium is unique.

Let us note that the convexity assumptions required for the sufficient SMP imply linearity of the Fokker–Planck equation, though not that of the cost functions. However, even for the control of the linear Fokker–Planck equation our sufficient optimality condition seems to be new in the literature. This is likely to be due to the fact that the structure of the Fokker–Planck equation generically precludes joint convexity in the state and the control variable (except in pathological situations), which is crucially used to deduce the sufficient SMP for standard (stochastic) optimal control problems. Fortunately, this same structure can be exploited to establish sufficiency in the absence of joint convexity; an observation that seems to have been gone unnoticed so far. In particular, as soon as both the Fokker–Planck equation and the cost functionals are linear, the sufficient SMP holds.

1.1
Related Literature

The interest in controlling the Fokker–Planck equation stems from the fact that it describes the law of a particle whose motion is described by an SDE. Hence, controlling the Fokker–Planck equation corresponds to the control of the particle’s trajectories. A number of papers have studied the optimal control of deterministic Fokker–Planck equations, in the linear case
[
8
,
31
,
14
,
6
,
29
]
or where the equation has local
[
9
,
7
]
or nonlocal nonlinearities
[
12
,
28
]
. As in our case, the objective of many of the cited works, particularly those in the linear setting, is to establish the existence of optimal controls as well as the necessary SMP. In this article, we borrow from the exposition in
[
6
]
to construct an optimal control. However, due to the stochasticity present in our setup, the proof becomes more involved. Indeed, the tightness arguments we employ force us to introduce a weak formulation of the control problem, where the filtration underlying the admissible controls may be larger than that generated by the noise
W
. We must show that this relaxation does not change the optimal value of the control problem. We achieve this by adapting ideas of Djete, Possamaï & Tan
[
30
]
developed in the context of McKean–Vlasov control problems with common noise. Since our state equation only has random coefficients as opposed to a stochastic driver, we can simplify their arguments considerably.

For linear equations, the necessary SMP can be derived using the spike variation technique, without a need for a second-order adjoint process (cf.
[
14
,
6
]
). In our nonlinear setting, changing the control on a set of measure
\epsilon
leads to a variation in the state of order
\epsilon^{1/2}
, so a second-order adjoint would become necessary. To avoid this, we opted for a formulation of the necessary SMP with only a first-order adjoint at the price of convexity assumptions on the domain of the control and the Hamiltonian. Let us stress once more that we also establish a sufficient optimality condition for control of the Fokker–Planck equation. This result cannot be found in the aforementioned literature, even in the linear and deterministic setting.

Linear BSPDEs and their precursors were initially explored in the context of the optimal control of SPDEs by Pardoux
[
54
]
, Bensoussan
[
11
,
10
]
, Zhou
[
59
,
58
]
, among others. Their particular focus was on the Zakai equation, which arises in the filtering of partially observed diffusions. As in our case, the BSPDEs characterise the adjoint processes appearing in the necessary optimality condition. However, all of the cited articles consider linear SPDEs (such as the Zakai equation) and work in a Brownian filtration (in the context of filtering that would be the filtration of the Brownian motion driving the observation process). Consequently, they do not have to deal with nonlocalities or a càdlàg martingale driver. For the latter, we crucially draw on a generalisation of Itô’s formula for càdlàg semimartingales in
L^{2}
-spaces, see Theorem
3.2
.

The semilinear BSPDEs analysed in
[
37
,
55
,
22
]
are structurally closest to those in our work. Peng
[
55
]
studies a local stochastic HJB equation arising in the control of SDEs with random coefficients. The McKean–Vlasov SDE associated with the Fokker–Planck equation (
1.1
) takes this form if we view the dependence on the conditional law
\nu_{t}
in its coefficients as a fixed random input. However, unlike in
[
55
]
, this source of randomness
\nu_{t}
is not fixed and varies under perturbations of the control
\gamma_{t}
, which is the cause of the nonlocalities present in the BSPDEs in our setting. Cardaliaguet, Seeger & Souganidis
[
22
]
analyse a local stochastic HJB equation using an alternative solution theory based on the propagation of certain semiconcavity estimates, which does not require uniform parabolicity. Their arguments, however, cannot be straightforwardly adapted to nonlocal equations. Finally, let us mention that to the best of our knowledge the only articles that consider a similar class of BSPDEs in a non-Brownian filtration are
[
3
,
4
]
. However, these works explicitly assume that the filtration only generates continuous martingales in order to avoid jumps in the dynamics.

MFGs and – to a lesser extent – MFC have seen a surge of interest in the last decade and a half and have been studied both from a probabilistic and analytic perspective. Particularly for MFGs, the analytic approach, which leads to a coupled forward-backward PDE consisting of the Fokker–Planck equation and the HJB equation, has received considerable attention
[
41
,
43
,
21
,
32
,
20
,
23
,
22
]
. The corresponding system for MFC problems, see e.g.
[
12
,
2
,
25
]
, features less prominently in the literature. Its derivation as the necessary optimality condition of the SMP is rather different from the derivation of the MFG system, which proceeds via dynamic programming. Since the proof of the necessary SMP relies on the stability of the state equation under small perturbation of the control, it requires strict regularity conditions, such as uniform parabolicity of the generator
\mathcal{L}
. The derivation of the MFG system is less burdensome.
Moreover, in contrast to the MFG system, the HJB equation of the MFC system exhibits nonlocalities, so one cannot rely on the theory of viscosity solutions or the comparison principle to analyse the equation, unless the nonlocalities are restricted to zeroth-order terms as e.g. in
[
27
]
.

Several authors have established necessary and sufficient SMPs for the control of the McKean–Vlasov SDE underlying the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation (
1.1
) in the open-loop formulation. Andersson & Djehiche
[
5
]
treat coefficients that depend on moments of the population’s distribution and derive a necessary SMP for convex control spaces. A similar result was obtained by Li
[
46
]
for scalar interactions. Buckdahn, Djehiche & Li
[
15
]

derive the first SMP for McKean–Vlasov control with a general action space, requiring the application of spike variation techniques. General dependence on the population’s law are treated in the monographs
[
12
]
and
[
25
]
and by Carmona & Delarue
[
24
]
, who also provide a sufficient condition. Extended mean-field control, where interaction is through the joint law of the population and the control is covered in
[
1
]
. As for the control of standard SDEs, all of the above works require differentiability of the coefficients with respect to the state variable, which is not needed for our approach. Moreover, while in our approach the regularity of the coefficients and cost functions with respect to the measure argument is captured through the so-called linear functional derivative (cf. Definition
2.6
), when proceeding via the McKean–Vlasov SDE, the Lions derivative (see
[
25
, Definition 5.22]
) is the appropriate concept. For sufficiently regular function,
[
25
, Proposition 5.51]
shows that the Lions derivative coincides with the spatial derivative of the linear function derivative. So again, we require less regularity. Note, however, that our SMP characterises closed-loop as opposed to open-loop controls.

We should remark that our framework does not cover models where the killing of particles occurs at a fixed boundary. These have received increasing attention recently, both in an uncontrolled setting
[
48
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
52
]
and in the context of MFGs
[
17
,
18
,
16
,
53
]
. Instead of a zeroth-order term, killing at a boundary leads to a Dirichlet boundary condition for SPDE (
1.1
). Such nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equations with Dirichlet boundary condition were studied in
[
34
,
36
]
. To establish uniqueness, one has to perform a careful analysis of the regularity of the solution near the boundary. Embedding a control problem into this framework is an interesting topic for future research.

Finally, let us note here that the proof of Theorem
A.2
, which establishes the equivalence of the McKean–Vlasov control problem (in open-loop formulation) and the control problem for the corresponding stochastic Fokker–Planck equation with space-dependent control processes
\gamma
as introduced above, relies on a variation of the mimicking theorem from
[
40
]
combined with classical measurable selection arguments. The main difference to standard McKean–Vlasov control problems is that we must apply the measurable selection to an open-loop control of the McKean–Vlasov control problem in such a way that the resulting feedback control
\gamma
does not depend on the auxiliary cumulative intensity process. For otherwise, we cannot derive a stochastic Fokker–Planck equation for the conditional distribution
\nu_{t}
of the living particles. If the Hamiltonian of the McKean–Vlasov control problem is convex, such a selection is indeed possible. This yields a rather straightforward proof of the equivalence between both formulations of the control problem. A similar result was concurrently obtained by Carmona, Laurière & Lions
[
27
]
in the related context of the control of conditional processes. Their proof proceeds with a careful analysis of a nonlocal PDE of HJB type that characterises the optimal controls of the McKean–Vlasov control problem. Our approach simplifies the proof considerably.

1.2
Main Contributions and Structure of the Paper

In Section
2
we present the main results of the paper. We discuss the transformation of the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation to a Fokker–Planck equation with random coefficients, state the results concerning the well-posedness of the state equation and existence of optimal controls, and formulate the necessary and the sufficient SMP.

We present a general theory of nonlocal semilinear BSPDEs with càdlàg drivers in Section
3
. We first establish a generalisation of Itô’s formula for càdlàg semimartingales with values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This extension of Itô’s formula is a key tool that is used frequently throughout this work and also seems to be novel in this generality. Subsequently, we prove existence and uniqueness for the BSPDE based on a Galerkin approximation and fixed-point arguments.

The analysis of the control problem is the topic of Section
4
. We begin by establishing the equivalence between the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation and its transformation – the Fokker–Planck equation with random coefficients. For the latter, we prove existence and uniqueness given any control input, so the control problem is well-posed. Then, we construct an optimal control by combining compactness theorems from PDE theory with tightness arguments from stochastic analysis. Since the tightness arguments require us to change or, alternatively, enlarge the underlying probability space, we must verify that the optimal cost of the control problem does not depend on the underlying probability space or its filtration. We achieve this by adapting ideas from
[
30
]
. The next goal is to prove the necessary SMP. This requires an analysis of the infinitesimal variations of the state process under perturbations of the control, which is complicated by the nonlinearities of the Fokker–Planck equation. We combine these variations with the adjoint process to deduce an explicit expression for the Gateaux derivative of the cost functional. From this expression and standard convexity assumptions, we deduce the necessary condition of the SMP. We conclude the chapter by establishing the novel sufficient SMP. We conclude Section
4
by establishing the sufficient SMP.

In Section
5
, we give a concrete application of the results of this paper in the context of a MFC model of government bailouts in financial systems from
[
33
]
. In particular, we discuss the shape of the adjoint process, supplemented by numerical simulations for the noiseless case (i.e.
\sigma_{0}=0
), and the implications that can be drawn for the control problem.

2.1
The Control Problem

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space equipped with a complete filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
and a
d_{W}
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-Brownian motion
W=(W_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
for
d_{W}\geq 1

nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation

\mathrm{d}\langle\nu_{t},\varphi\rangle=\bigl\langle\nu_{t},\mathcal{L}\varphi(t,\cdot,\nu_{t},\gamma_{t})\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle\nu_{t},(\nabla\varphi)^{\top}\sigma_{0}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(2.1)

for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in(0,T]
with initial condition
\nu_{0}=v_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
generator

\mathcal{L}
acts on
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\mathcal{L}\varphi(t,x,v,g)=\lambda(t,x,v)\varphi(x)+b(t,x,v,g)\cdot\nabla\varphi+\bigl(a(t,x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}\sigma_{0}^{\top}\bigr)\colon\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)

(2.2)

for
(t,x,v,g)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times G
, where
G
is a nonempty, compact, and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}
with
d_{G}\geq 1
. The

are measurable functions
\lambda\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
,
b\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
a\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, while
\sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{W}}
. We will make suitable assumptions on the coefficients below. The
control

\gamma\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to G
is an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable random function with values in
G
. We denote the collection of such random functions by
\mathbb{G}
. Next, we introduce the
cost functional

J(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nu_{t},f(t,\cdot,\nu_{t},\gamma_{t})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\psi(\nu_{T})\biggr]

(2.3)

and denote the

of the control problem, i.e. the infimum of

over
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
, by

. The
running
and

are measurable functions
f\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times G\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\mathpunct{:}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Throughout this work, we make the following assumptions on the coefficients and cost functions.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume there exist constants
C>0
and
c>0
such that

(i)

for all
t
the map
x\mapsto a(t,x)
has a weak derivative, for all
(t,x)
and uniformly in
g
the maps
v\mapsto f(t,x,v,g)
and
v\mapsto\psi(v)
are continuous, and for all
(t,x,v,v^{\prime},g)
we have

\lvert b(t,x,v,g)-b(t,x,v^{\prime},g)\rvert+\lvert\lambda(t,x,v)-\lambda(t,x,v^{\prime})\rvert\leq Cd_{0}(v,v^{\prime});

(ii)

the coefficients
\lambda
,
b
,
a
, and
\partial_{x}a
as well as the costs
f
and
\psi
are bounded;

(iii)

for all
(t,x)
we have

.

Our goal is twofold: first, we will show that for a suitably weakened version of the control problem, an optimal control is guaranteed to exist. Secondly, we derive a necessary and a sufficient stochastic maximum principle (SMP). For both purposes, it is advantageous to transform the stochastic Fokker–Planck equation (
2.1
) to a Fokker–Planck equation with random coefficients by applying an appropriate shift along the noise
W
. More precisely, we set

\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,v)=\lambda\bigl(t,x+\sigma_{0}W_{t},(\id+\sigma_{0}W_{t})^{\#}v\bigr)

for
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and similarly define the shifted coefficient
\tilde{b}
and
\tilde{a}
as well as the shifted cost functions
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{\psi}
. Here
F^{\#}v
denotes the pushforward of
v\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
under a measurable functions
F\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and by
\id+\sigma_{0}W_{t}
we mean the random function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by
x\mapsto x+\sigma_{0}W_{t}
. No shift is applied to the control argument. Then we consider the
nonlinear random Fokker–Planck equation

\mathrm{d}\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle=\bigl\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi(t,\cdot,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t})\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

(2.4)

for
t\in(0,T]
and
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with initial condition
\mu_{0}=v_{0}
. The generator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
is obtained by replacing
\lambda
,
b
,
a
, and
\sigma_{0}
in the definition of

in (

) by
\tilde{\lambda}
,
\tilde{b}
,
\tilde{a}
, and
\tilde{\sigma}_{0}=0
.

Remark 2.2
.

If we interpret
\nu_{t}
as the conditional subprobability of the solution
X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of a McKean–Vlasov SDE (e.g. SDE (
A.1
)) given the common information
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, then the performed shift corresponds to subtracting
\sigma_{0}W_{t}
from the state
X_{t}
. The resulting process
Y_{t}=X_{t}-\sigma_{0}W_{t}
solves a McKean–Vlasov SDE without common noise, but where the coefficients depend on
\sigma_{0}W_{t}
. For example, if the drift coefficient of the original SDE was given by

, then the transformed coefficient is precisely provided by the shifted function
\tilde{b}
defined above. Moreover, the conditional law of the process

is simply
\mu_{t}
.

The
cost functional

\tilde{J}
for the control of PDE (
2.4
) is given by

\tilde{J}(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{f}(t,\cdot,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t})\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\tilde{\psi}(\mu_{T})\biggr]

(2.5)

for
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied, fix
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
, and define
\tilde{\gamma}\in\mathbb{G}
by
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}(x)=\gamma_{t}(x+\sigma_{0}W_{t})

Conversely, if
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}

-valued process that satisfies PDE (

) with control

) with control

Proposition

justifies studying the control problem for SPDE (
2.1
) from the point of view of PDE (
2.4

) we do not explicitly use that

is a Brownian motion and we could in principle replace it by an arbitrary

-progressively measurable stochastic process. For simplicity, we will not pursue this level of generality here.

To begin with, we will state a well-posedness result for PDE (
2.4
). We would like to analyse PDE (
2.4
) in a standard

-framework, studying the density of
\mu_{t}
as an element of

, as this facilitates the derivation of the SMP. However, in order to deal with the nonlinearities in the coefficients
\lambda
and

in the measure argument, which, in general, will not be continuous with respect to the

-norm of the density of

, we have to consider weighted
L^{2}
-spaces. Let us, therefore, fix the function
\eta\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\eta(x)=\eta_{0}\sqrt{1+\lvert x\rvert^{2}}
for some
\eta_{0}>0
and set
\bar{\eta}=-\eta
. Then the weighted space
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))
is embedded in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, for any

we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert v\rVert_{\eta}<\infty

and if

is another element of
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))
, then

d_{0}(v,v^{\prime})\leq\sup_{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\text{bLip}}\leq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\varphi(x)\rvert\lvert v(x)-v^{\prime}(x)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert v-v^{\prime}\rVert_{\eta}.

Here we identified
v
and
v^{\prime}
with the respective measures they induce. This embedding makes
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))
a convenient state space for our purpose. Note that we will be working with the specific choice for
\eta
detailed above throughout most of this article, we consider a more general
\eta
only in Section
3
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied and suppose that

has a density
\rho_{0}\in L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
, PDE (
2.4
) has a unique solution
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
with values in

. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. the measure
\mu_{t}
has a density
\rho_{t}
for all

such that
(\rho_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}\in C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\rho_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla\rho_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\lVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}

(2.6)

for a constant
C>0
depending only on the coefficients
\lambda
,
b
,
a
, and
\sigma
, the parameter
\eta_{0}
as well as the time horizon

.

From now on we will always assume that

has a density in
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and no longer distinguish between

and its density.

Before we state the existence result for optimal controls, let us make the following remark: it is possible to introduce a control problem analogous to the one discussed so far on any (complete) filtered probability space that carries a
d_{W}
-dimensional Brownian motion. In that case, the space of controls simply consists of the
G
-valued random functions that are progressively measurable with respect to the underlying filtration and the coefficients are shifted along the corresponding Brownian motion. If we wish to emphasise the
probabilistic setup

\mathbb{W}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P},W)
, we add it as a subscript to the symbols denoting the generator, the space of controls, and the cost functional. That is, we write
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{W}}
,
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}
and

instead of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
,

and

Theorem 2.5
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied. If for all
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the map
G\ni g\mapsto b(t,x,v,g)
is continuous and the map
G\ni g\mapsto f(t,x,v,g)
is upper semicontinuous, then the value
\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)
does not depend on the probabilistic setup
\mathbb{W}
.

Next, if for all
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the map
G\ni g\mapsto b(t,x,v,g)
is affine and the map
G\ni g\mapsto f(t,x,v,g)
is convex and continuous, then on some probabilistic setup
\mathbb{W}
there exists an optimal control.

Note that by
b
being affine in the control argument, we mean that there exist functions
b_{0}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b_{1}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}\times d}
such that
b(t,x,v,g)=b_{0}(t,x,v)+g^{\top}b_{1}(t,x,v)
. Theorem
2.5
tells us that under suitable assumptions on the coefficients and cost functions, we can always move to a probabilistic setup on which there exists at least one optimal control.

2.2
The Necessary Stochastic Maximum Principle

Let us now move on to the stochastic maximum principle (SMP). In order to formulate the SMP, we have to consider an appropriate adjoint process. The adjoint will solve a
backward SPDE
(BSPDE), involving the derivative of the Hamiltonian of the control problem with respect to the state, i.e. the measure
\mu_{t}
. We shall introduce these two objects next. The
Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\mathcal{H}(t,v,r,p,g)=\Bigl\langle v,\tilde{\lambda}(t,\cdot,v)r+\tilde{b}(t,\cdot,v,g)\cdot p+\tilde{f}(t,\cdot,v,g)\Bigr\rangle

(2.7)

for
(t,\omega,v,r,p,g)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{G}
. Here
\mathcal{G}
denotes the space of measurable functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to G
. Next, we define an appropriate notion of derivative for functions on the space of measures
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 2.6
.

A map
F\mathpunct{:}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is said to have a
linear functional derivative
if there exists a measurable and bounded function
DF\mathpunct{:}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
v
,
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds that

F(v)-F(v^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}DF\bigl(\theta v+(1-\theta)v^{\prime}\bigr)(x)\,\mathrm{d}(v-v^{\prime})(x)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(2.8)

The linear functional derivative satisfies the following type of product rule (see Lemma
A.4
for a proof): if
F\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable and bounded function such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto F(x,v)
has a linear functional derivative
DF(x,\cdot)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
DF\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable, bounded, and continuous in the measure argument, then the map
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto\langle v,F(\cdot,v)\rangle
possesses the linear functional derivative
F(x,v)+\langle v,DF(\cdot,v)(x)\rangle
. Consequently, assuming that the coefficients
\lambda
and
b
as well as the running cost
f
have linear functional derivatives that are bounded and continuous in the measure argument, we can compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}D\mathcal{H}(t,v,r,p,g)(x)&=\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,v)r(x)+\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,v,g(x)\bigr)\cdot p(x)+\tilde{f}\bigl(t,x,v,g(x)\bigr)\\
&\ \ \ +\Bigl\langle v,D\tilde{\lambda}(t,\cdot,v)(x)r+D\tilde{b}(t,\cdot,v,g)(x)\cdot p+D\tilde{f}(t,\cdot,v,g)(x)\Bigr\rangle.\end{split}

(2.9)

Note that the expression above as well as
\mathcal{H}
itself are also defined for
v\in L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))
,
r\in L_{\bar{\eta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
p\in L_{\bar{\eta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and we shall use this extension in what follows.

Now, let
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
and denote the corresponding solution to PDE (
2.4
) by
\mu
. Then the
adjoint process
solves the linear BSPDE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)=-\Bigl(D\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},u_{t},\nabla u_{t},\gamma_{t})(x)+\tilde{a}(t,x)\colon\nabla^{2}u_{t}(x)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}m_{t}(x)\end{split}

(2.10)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=D\tilde{\psi}(\mu_{T})(x)
. The process
m
is a càdlàg martingale, which takes values in the Hilbert space
L_{\bar{\eta}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It ensures that the process
u
stays
\mathbb{F}
-adapted as we step backwards in time.

Definition 2.7
.

A
solution
of BSPDE (
2.10
) is a pair
(u,m)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];H^{1}_{\bar{\eta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\times\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\bar{\eta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that
m
is started from zero and

\displaystyle\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle D\tilde{\psi}(\mu_{T}),\varphi\bigr\rangle+\int_{t}^{T}\Bigl(\bigl\langle D\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},u_{t},\nabla u_{t},\gamma_{t}),\varphi\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle\nabla u_{s},\nabla\cdot\bigl(\tilde{a}(t,\cdot)\varphi\bigr)\bigr\rangle\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\langle m_{T}-m_{t},\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
.

Note that
u
and
m
take values in function spaces which are weighted according to
\bar{\eta}=-\eta
instead of
\eta
itself. This ensures that the adjoint
u_{t}
can be integrated against the state
\mu_{t}
. This is of crucial importance for the SMP.

Naturally, we require additional assumptions to study the existence and uniqueness of solutions to BSPDE (
2.10
), in particular concerning the existence of linear functional derivatives for the coefficients
\lambda
and
b
as well as the cost functions
f
and
\psi
. We shall state them together with the assumptions for the SMP.

Assumption 2.8
.

We assume that

(i)

for all
(t,x,g)
the maps
v\mapsto\lambda(t,x,v)
,
v\mapsto h(t,x,v,g)
for
h\in\{b,f\}
, and

have measurable and bounded linear functionals derivatives

,

, and

which are continuous in the measure argument;

(ii)

there exists a measurable and bounded function
\nabla_{g}f\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}
such that for all
(t,x)
the map
(v,g)\mapsto\nabla_{g}f(t,x,v,g)
is continuous and for all
(t,x,v,g,g^{\prime})
we have

f(t,x,v,g)-f(t,x,v,g)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{g}f\bigl(t,x,v,\theta g+(1-\theta)g^{\prime}\bigr)\cdot(g-g^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(iii)

for all
(t,x,v)
the map
g\mapsto b(t,x,v,g)
is affine and the map
g\mapsto f(t,x,v,g)
is convex;

The slightly awkward phrasing of the differentiability condition for

in the control argument in Assumption
2.8

(ii)
is needed because the set

is not an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.8

(i)
be satisfied. Then for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
and corresponding state

, the BSPDE (

We are finally in a position to state the necessary SMP.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.8
be satisfied. Suppose further that
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
is an optimal control and let
\mu
and
(u,m)
be the corresponding solutions to PDE (
2.4
) and BSPDE (
2.10

\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)\cdot\nabla u_{t}(x)+\tilde{f}\bigl(t,x,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)=\inf_{g\in G}\Bigl(\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu_{t},g)\cdot\nabla u_{t}(x)+\tilde{f}(t,x,\mu_{t},g)\Bigr)

for

-a.e.

.

Note that owing to Theorem

Next, we wish to use the necessary SMP to derive a semilinear BSPDE from the linear BSPDE (
2.10
) satisfied by the adjoint process. Fix an optimal control
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
and let
\mu
denote the corresponding solution to PDE (
2.4

\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},u_{t},\nabla u_{t},\gamma_{t})=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},u_{t},\nabla u_{t},g),

which motivates us to introduce the function
\mathcal{H}_{\ast}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,v,r,p)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{H}(t,v,r,p,g)

for
(t,\omega,v,r,p)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As for
\mathcal{H}
, the functions
\mathcal{H}_{\ast}
and

are also defined for

,

, and

. Note that to guarantee that

exists, we have to make an additional assumption (see Assumption
2.11

\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)=-\Bigl(D\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,\mu_{t},u_{t},\nabla u_{t})(x)+a(t,x)\colon\nabla^{2}u_{t}(x)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}m_{t}(x)

(2.12)

with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=D\tilde{\psi}(\mu_{T})(x)
. We understand this BSPDE in the sense of Definition
2.7
.

Assumption 2.11
.

We assume there exist
(b_{0},f_{0})\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
and
(b_{1},f_{1})\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times G\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
such that for all
(t,x,v,g)
we have

b(t,x,v,g)=b_{0}(t,x,v)+b_{1}(t,x,g)\quad\text{and}\quad f(t,x,v,g)=f_{0}(t,x,v)+f_{1}(t,x,g).

In what follows Assumption
2.11
will typically be in force in conjunction with Assumption
2.8
, in which case the properties of
b
and
f
assumed in the latter transfer to the functions
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
,
f_{0}
, and
f_{1}
from the former. As for the remaining coefficients and cost functions, we will indicate the shifted versions of

,
b_{1}
,
f_{0}
, and
f_{1}
with a tilde. Under Assumption

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,v,r,p)

\displaystyle=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\Bigl\langle v,\tilde{\lambda}(t,\cdot,v)+\tilde{b}(t,\cdot,v,g)\cdot p+\tilde{f}(t,\cdot,v,g)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,r,p)+\Bigl\langle v,\inf_{g\in G}H_{1}(t,\cdot,p,g)\Bigr\rangle,

where
\mathcal{H}_{0}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,r,p)=\Bigl\langle v,\tilde{\lambda}(t,\cdot,v)r+\tilde{b}_{0}(t,\cdot,v)\cdot p+\tilde{f}_{0}(t,\cdot,v)\Bigr\rangle

H_{1}(t,x,p,g)=\tilde{b}_{1}(t,x,g)\cdot p+\tilde{f}_{1}(t,x,g)

for
(t,\omega,x,p,g)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times G
. Note that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and

, and

\displaystyle D\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,v,r,p)(x)=D\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,r,p)(x)+\inf_{g\in G}H_{1}\bigl(t,x,p(x),g\bigr).

Proposition 2.12
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.8

(i)

2.11
be satisfied. Then for any
\mu\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{\infty}([0,T];L_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

One might expect that if
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
is an optimal control with state
\mu

solves BSPDE (

). However, there is a slight technicality: Equation (

D\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,\mu_{t},\tilde{u}_{t},\nabla\tilde{u}_{t})(x)=D\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},\tilde{u}_{t},\nabla\tilde{u}_{t},\gamma_{t})(x)

for
\mu_{t}
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a priori it is not clear whether

for

\tilde{g}_{\ast}

\gamma^{\ast}\in\mathbb{G}
by

\gamma^{\ast}_{t}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{t}>0}\gamma_{t}(x)+\mathbf{1}_{\mu_{t}=0}\tilde{g}_{\ast}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x)).

\gamma

\gamma^{\ast}
in PDE (

\gamma^{\ast}

\gamma^{\ast}
and the corresponding adjoint process
(u^{\ast},m^{\ast})
. Plugging the equality

D\mathcal{H}_{\ast}(t,\mu_{t},u^{\ast}_{t},\nabla u^{\ast}_{t})(x)=D\mathcal{H}(t,\mu_{t},u^{\ast}_{t},\nabla u^{\ast}_{t},\gamma^{\ast}_{t})(x)

for
\mathrm{Leb}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle&=\bigl\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi\bigl(t,\cdot,\mu_{t},\tilde{g}_{\ast}(t,\cdot,\nabla u_{t})\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\

(2.13)

Let Assumptions

2.3
The Sufficient Stochastic Maximum Principle

Next, we will formulate a sufficient SMP which together with uniqueness of the minimum on the right-hand side of Equation (
2.11
) implies uniqueness for FBSPDE (
2.13
).

Theorem 2.14
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.8

(i)
, and
2.11
be satisfied. Fix
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
and let
\mu
and
(u,m)
be the corresponding solutions to PDE (
2.4
) and BSPDE (
2.10
), respectively. Suppose
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))\ni v\mapsto\psi(v)
is convex and
L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};[0,\infty))\ni v\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,u_{t},\nabla u_{t})
is convex for
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
. Then, if

H_{1}\bigl(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)=\inf_{g\in G}H_{1}\bigl(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),g\bigr)

(2.14)

for
\mu_{t}
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, the control
\gamma
is optimal.

Moreover, if the map
g\mapsto H_{1}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),g)
has a unique minimiser for
\mathrm{Leb}
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, then
\gamma
is the (up to modifications on null sets of
\mu_{t}
) unique optimal control.

In stark contrast to standard formulations of the sufficient SMP no joint convexity in the state and the control variable is required. This is due to the specific structure of the Fokker–Planck equation. Regarding the uniqueness part of Theorem
2.14
, a very natural situation in which the map
g\mapsto H_{1}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),g)
has a unique minimum is if Assumption
2.8

(iii)
is in place with strict convexity of the map
g\mapsto f_{1}(t,x,g)
.

Remark 2.15
.

Note that if
\lambda
,
b_{0}
, and
f_{0}
do not depend on
v
and
v\mapsto\psi(v)
is simply the integration of a bounded and measurable function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
against
v
, then the convexity requirements in Theorem
2.14
are automatically satisfied. Unfortunately,
\lambda
and
b_{0}
being independent of the measure argument also seems to be necessary in order for the convexity assumptions to hold. For example, suppose that
\lambda
only depends on the measure argument. Then the map
v\mapsto\langle v,\lambda(v)r\rangle
, which is one of the summands in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, is convex if and only if
D\lambda(v)(x)=r(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, the convexity condition from Theorem
2.14
would imply that
D\lambda(v)(x)=u_{t}(x)
, which in general can of course not be satisfied. Thus, there is little hope that
v\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,u_{t},\nabla u_{t})
is convex if
\lambda
or
b_{0}
depend on
v
. Consequently, Theorem
2.14
should be understood as a sufficient optimality condition for the linear Fokker–Planck equation (
2.4
) with possibly nonlinear cost functions.

If
(\mu,u,m)
is a solution to the FBSPDE (
2.13
) and the assumptions of Theorem
2.14
are satisfied, then the sufficient SMP implies that the control
\gamma
defined by
\gamma_{t}(x)=\tilde{g}_{\ast}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x))
is optimal. Moreover, under the additional assumption that the minimum of
g\mapsto H_{1}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),g)
is unique, if
(\mu^{\prime},q^{\prime},m^{\prime})
is another solution to FBSPDE (
2.13
) and we define
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}
by
\gamma^{\prime}_{t}(x)=\tilde{g}_{\ast}(t,x,\nabla u^{\prime}_{t}(x))
, uniqueness of optimal controls implies that
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma
. Consequently, it follows that
\mu^{\prime}=\mu
by Proposition
2.4
and, finally, by Proposition
2.12
, we get
(u^{\prime},m^{\prime})=(u,m)
. We have proved the following corollary.

Corollary 2.16
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.8
, and
2.11
be satisfied and let
(\mu,u,m)
be a solution to FBSPDE (
2.13
). If the convexity assumptions from Theorem
2.14
hold for
(\mu,u,m)
and the map
g\mapsto H_{1}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x),g)
has a unique minimum for
\mu_{t}
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
, then

is the unique solution of FBSPDE (

).

2.4
Practical Implementation of the Criticality Condition

Before we conclude this section, let us briefly comment on the practical implementation of the criticality condition of the necessary SMP. This condition gives rise to the forward-backward system (
2.13
). In general, this system may have multiple solutions
(\mu,u,m)
, not all of which necessarily determine an optimal control
\gamma_{t}
via

\gamma_{t}(x)=\tilde{g}_{\ast}(t,x,\nabla u_{t}(x)).

Optimality of
\gamma_{t}
can be guaranteed, however, under the additional convexity assumptions from the sufficient SMP, Theorem 2.14. In other words, solving FBSPDE (
2.13
) provides a candidate control, which is optimal under the conditions of Theorem 2.14. In practice, FBSPDE (
2.13
) will not be analytically solvable, except perhaps in certain special cases, so numerical methods must be deployed. One possibility is to discretise both the Fokker–Planck equation and the stochastic Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation from (
2.13
) by a finite-difference or Galerkin scheme. The resulting high-dimensional forward-backward SDE can be approximated, for example, by using the deep BSDE method of Jiequn, Jentzen & Weinan
[
38
]
. A numerical implementation of this procedure is beyond the scope of this paper.

Semilinear BSPDEs with Càdlàg Noise

In this section, we will study a more general version of the BSPDEs (
2.10
) and (
2.12
) appearing in Section
2
. In preparation, we will introduce an extension of Itô’s formula to
L^{2}
-valued semimartingales that will serve us throughout this paper.

3.1
Càdlàg Martingales with Values in Hilbert Spaces

We recall a few notions from the theory of martingales with values in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. For more information we refer to
[
51
, Section 26]
. We fix a separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
and induced norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}
. Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
denotes the Hilbert space of
\mathbb{H}
-valued
\mathbb{F}
-martingales
m
with a.s. càdlàg trajectories for which
\mathbb{E}\lVert m_{T}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}<\infty
. We denote the
quadratic variation
of a martingale
m\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
by
[m]
and its
tensor quadratic variation
by
[[m]]
. The tensor quadratic variation is a process with values in the trace class operators from
\mathbb{H}
to itself and is symmetric as such. Moreover, it holds that
[m]_{t}=\trace[[m]]_{t}
. We can define the
quadratic covariation

[m^{1},m^{2}]
and
tensor quadratic covariation

[[m^{1},m^{2}]]
of two martingales
m^{1}
,
m^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
through polarisation.

We say that two martingales
m^{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{1})
,
m^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{2})
with values in separable Hilbert spaces
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}
, respectively, are
very strongly orthogonal
if for all elements
h_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
,
h_{2}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
the real-valued càdlàg martingales
\langle m^{1},h_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{1}}
and
\langle m^{2},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{2}}
are strongly orthogonal, i.e.
\bigl[\langle m^{1},h_{1}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{1}},\langle m^{2},h_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{2}}\bigr]_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. If
\mathbb{H}_{1}=\mathbb{H}_{2}
, this is equivalent to saying that
[[m^{1},m^{2}]]=0
.

For
m\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
, let
\mathcal{P}_{m}^{2}
denote the space of
\mathbb{F}
-predictable
\mathbb{H}
-valued processes such that
\int_{0}^{T}\lVert h_{t}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[m]_{t}<\infty
almost surely. For
h\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{2}
we let
\int_{0}^{t}\langle h_{s},\mathrm{d}m_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
be the stochastic integral of
h
against
m
over the interval
(0,t]
, i.e. we exclude the left endpoint. The indefinite stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle h_{s},\mathrm{d}m_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
is a real-valued local martingale and its quadratic variation is given by
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle h_{s},h_{s}\cdot\mathrm{d}[[m]]_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. Here again the integral excludes the left endpoint, and for a bounded operator
A\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
and an element
h\in\mathbb{H}
we write
h\cdot A
for
Ah
.

Since the operator norm of a trace class operator
A
is bounded from above by its trace, it holds that
\langle h,h\cdot A\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\lVert h\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\trace A
. We can apply this inequality to simple integrands
h\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{2}
of the form
h=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_{i}\mathbf{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}
for
h_{i}
bounded and
\mathbb{F}_{t_{i}}
-measurable and
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}=T
to see
\int_{0}^{t}\langle h_{s},h_{s}\cdot\mathrm{d}[[m]]_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lVert h_{s}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[m]_{s}
. Next, we extend this inequality to arbitrary
h\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{2}
by density of the simple integrands. This implies the following Burkholder–Davis–Gundy type inequality.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
m\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H})
and
h\in\mathcal{P}^{2}_{m}
. Then for all
p\geq 1
and any stopping time
\tau
with values in
[0,T]
we have

\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\biggl\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\langle h_{s},\mathrm{d}m_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\biggr\rvert^{p}\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\biggl(\int_{0}^{\tau}\lVert h_{t}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[m]_{t}\biggr)^{p/2}

(3.1)

for a constant
C_{p}>0
independent of
m
,
h
, and
\tau
.

Proof.

By the classical Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequality we have

\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\biggl\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\langle h_{s},\mathrm{d}m_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\biggr\rvert^{p}\leq C_{p}\mathbb{E}\biggl(\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle h_{t},h_{t}\cdot\mathrm{d}[[m]]_{t}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\biggr)^{p/2}.

Then the bound
\int_{0}^{t}\langle h_{s},h_{s}\cdot\mathrm{d}[[m]]_{s}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lVert h_{s}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[m]_{s}
allows us to conclude the proof.
∎

3.2
Itô’s Formula for Càdlàg Hilbert Space-Valued Semimartingales

Our next goal is to derive a generalisation of Itô’s formula which is suitable for our analysis of càdlàg semimartingales with values in Hilbert spaces.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
i=1
, …,
k
, let
u^{i}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
\zeta^{i}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and
m^{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that

\langle u^{i}_{t},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i}_{s}(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}s+\langle m^{i}_{t},\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
u^{1}
, …,
u^{k}
lie in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(or
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
if
m^{1}
, …,
m^{k}
have continuous trajectories).

Next, let
F\mathpunct{:}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice continuously Fréchet differentiable function and assume that for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
it holds that

(i)

for each
h\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})
the derivative
D_{i}F(h)
is an element of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the map
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
h\mapsto D_{i}F(h)
is uniformly continuous on bounded subsets of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})
;

(ii)

for each
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})
the second derivative
D_{ij}^{2}F(h)
is an element of
B(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and the map
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})\to B(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
h\mapsto D_{ij}^{2}F(h)
is uniformly continuous on bounded subsets of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

Then for any
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(u_{t})&=F(m_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\biggl(\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i}_{s}(D_{i}F(u_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\langle D_{i}F(u_{s-}),\,\mathrm{d}m^{i}_{s}\rangle\biggr)\\
&\ \ \ +\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\trace\bigl(D^{2}_{ij}F(u_{s-})\,\mathrm{d}[[m^{i},m^{j}]]^{c}_{s}\bigr)\\
&\ \ \ +\sum_{0<s\leq t}\biggl(F(u_{s-}+\Delta m_{s})-F(u_{s-})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle D_{i}F(u_{s-}),\Delta m^{i}_{s}\rangle\biggr).\end{split}

(3.2)

Here
\Delta m_{s}
denotes the jump of
m
at time
s
.

Proof.

We proceed in two steps.

Step 1
: First, we assume that
\zeta^{i}
is an element of
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for
i=1
, …,
k
, so that
u^{i}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+m^{i}_{t}
, where
\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s
is understood as a Bochner integral. Then
u^{1}
, …,
u^{k}
are
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-valued càdlàg semimartingales, so we simply apply
[
51
, Theorem 27.2]
, which gives precisely (
3.2
). Note that
[
51
, Theorem 27.2]
is formulated for the case that the second derivative
D^{2}_{ij}F
is a uniformly continuous map from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{k})
to the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the Hilbert–Schmidt norm. However, since the tensor quadratic variation
[[m^{i},m^{j}]]
is a finite variation process with values in the trace class operators, the theorem can easily be extended to the situation where
D^{2}_{ij}F
only satisfies Assumption
(ii)
above.

Step 2
: Now, we let
\zeta^{i}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. We will reduce to the previous case by mollification. Let
\rho\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be the standard mollifier defined by
\rho(x)=c_{d}e^{-1/(1-\lvert x\rvert^{2})}
if
x
is in the interior of the centred ball of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and zero otherwise, and set
\rho_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d}}\rho(x/\epsilon)
. Here
c_{d}>0
is a normalisation constant which ensures that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
. For a function
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we let
(\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\epsilon}(x-y)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}y
. Then we set
u^{i,\epsilon}_{t}=\rho_{\epsilon}\ast u^{i}_{t}
, and
m^{i,\epsilon}_{t}=\rho_{\epsilon}\ast m^{i}_{t}
, and define
\zeta^{i,\epsilon}
via
\zeta^{i,\epsilon}_{t}(\varphi)=\zeta^{i}_{t}(\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi)
. With this we obtain

\mathrm{d}\langle u^{i,\epsilon}_{t},\varphi\rangle=\mathrm{d}\langle u^{i}_{t},\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi\rangle=\zeta^{i}_{t}(\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\langle m^{i}_{t},\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi\rangle=\zeta^{i,\epsilon}_{t}(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\langle m^{i,\epsilon}_{t},\varphi\rangle

(3.3)

for
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Note that for any
\xi\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds that
\lvert\xi(\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi)\rvert\leq\lVert\xi\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lVert\rho_{\epsilon}\ast\varphi\rVert_{H^{1}}\leq C_{\epsilon,d}\lVert\rho_{\epsilon}\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\lVert\xi\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{2}}
for some constant
C_{\epsilon,d}>0
. Hence, the mollification of
\xi
is a bounded linear functional on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so we may identify it with an element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Consequently, we can view
\zeta^{i,\epsilon}
as a process in
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Thus, by Step 1 it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(u^{\epsilon}_{t})&=F(m^{\epsilon}_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\biggl(\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i,\epsilon}_{s}(D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\langle D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}),\,\mathrm{d}m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rangle\biggr)\\
&\ \ \ +\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\trace\bigl(D^{2}_{ij}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})\,\mathrm{d}[[m^{i,\epsilon},m^{j,\epsilon}]]^{c}_{s}\bigr)\\
&\ \ \ +\sum_{0<s\leq t}\biggl(F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}+\Delta m^{\epsilon}_{s})-F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}),\Delta m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rangle\biggr).\end{split}

(3.4)

Our goal is to pass to the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in Equation (
3.4
). However, while the sequences
(u^{i,\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
,
(\zeta^{i,\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
, and
(m^{i,\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
,
i=1
, …,
k
, converge in their respective Hilbert spaces
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we do not yet know whether
(u^{i,\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
also converges as a sequence in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. But this is certainly necessary to pass to the limit in the stochastic integral term
\int_{0}^{t}\langle D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}),\,\mathrm{d}m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rangle
and to make sense of its limit
\int_{0}^{t}\langle D_{i}F(u_{s-}),\mathrm{d}m^{i}_{s}\rangle
.

To deduce the convergence in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we first apply (
3.4
) to
u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}=u^{i,\epsilon}-u^{i,\delta}
for the specific choice
F(\varphi)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\varphi^{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. This implies that for any
\epsilon
,
\delta>0
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\biggl(\lVert m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}2\zeta^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s}(u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}2\bigl\langle u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s-},\,\mathrm{d}m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle+[m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}]_{t}\biggr),

(3.5)

where
\zeta^{i,\epsilon,\delta}
and
m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}
are defined in the obvious way. We take the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
and then expectation on both sides. Further, we estimate with Proposition
3.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}2\biggl\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\langle u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s-},\,\mathrm{d}m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle\biggr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}\mathbb{E}\biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t-}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}[m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}]_{t}\biggr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}\mathbb{E}\biggl(\sup_{0<t\leq T}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t-}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}[m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}]_{T}\biggr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+8C_{1}^{2}\mathbb{E}[m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}]_{T}.

Inserting this into (
3.5
) and rearranging gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\biggl(

\displaystyle\lVert m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}2\lVert\zeta^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+(2C_{1}+1)\mathbb{E}[m^{i,\epsilon,\delta}]_{T}\biggr).

The right-hand side clearly tends to zero as
\epsilon
,
\delta\to 0
, which means that
(u^{i,\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is a Cauchy sequence in the space
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for
i=1
, …,
k
. Hence, it has a unique limit, which must coincide with
u^{i}
, since
u^{i,\epsilon}\to u^{i}
in
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Consequently, we have
\mathbb{E}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert u^{i,\epsilon}_{t}-u^{i}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0
and
u^{i}
lies in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Let us return to Equation (
3.4
). We can clearly pass to the limit in probability in the terms
F(u^{\epsilon}_{t})
,
F(m^{\epsilon}_{0})
, and
\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i,\epsilon}_{s}(D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s
resulting in the expressions
F(u_{t})
,
F(m_{0})
, and
\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{i}_{s}(D_{i}F(u_{s}))\,\mathrm{d}s
. Here we make use of Assumption
(i)
. Next, by Lemma
A.7
, the stochastic integrals and the quadratic variation terms converge to
\int_{0}^{t}\langle D_{i}F(u_{s-}),\mathrm{d}m^{i}_{s}\rangle
and
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\trace\bigl(D_{ij}^{2}F(u_{s-})\,\mathrm{d}[[m^{i},m^{j}]]^{c}_{s}\bigr)
, respectively, in probability as
\epsilon\to 0
. This leaves the jump terms. First note that the jumps of
m^{\epsilon}
and
m
align perfectly and, moreover, it holds that
\lVert\Delta m^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert\Delta m_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}
. We can use Taylor’s theorem to bound
F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}+\Delta m^{\epsilon}_{s})-F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}),\Delta m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rangle
by

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\bigl\langle\Delta m^{i,\epsilon}_{s},D_{ij}^{2}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})\Delta m^{j,\epsilon}_{s}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{\lVert D_{ij}^{2}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})\rVert}{2}\bigl(\lVert\Delta m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta m^{j,\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{\lVert D_{ij}^{2}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})\rVert}{2}\bigl(\lVert\Delta m^{i}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\Delta m^{j}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\bigr),

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the operator norm for elements of
B(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. From above we know that
\mathbb{E}\sup_{0<s\leq t}\lVert u^{\epsilon}_{s-}-u_{s-}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0
, so since
D_{ij}^{2}F
is bounded on bounded sets of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the operator norm by Assumption
(ii)
, we deduce that
\lVert D_{ij}^{2}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})\rVert
is bounded by some random constant
C>0
uniformly in
\epsilon
and
s\in[0,T]
. Hence, we can upper bound
F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}+\Delta m^{\epsilon}_{s})-F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle D_{i}F(u^{\epsilon}_{s-}),\Delta m^{i,\epsilon}_{s}\rangle
by the a.s. summable expression
Ck\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\Delta m^{i}_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Thus, we can apply the dominated convergence theorem to pass to the a.s. limit in the sum in the third line of Equation (
3.4
). Putting all convergences together yields the desired formula (
3.2
).
∎

We have the following corollary of Theorem
3.2
for weighted function spaces, where the exponent of the weight
\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has uniformly bounded derivative. Note that
\eta
is not necessarily equal to the exponent from Section
2
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
u^{\pm}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\pm\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
\zeta^{\pm}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];(H_{\mp\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{\ast})
, and
m^{\pm}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\pm\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that

\langle u^{\pm}_{t},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{\pm}_{s}(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}s+\langle m^{\pm}_{t},\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\mp\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
u^{\pm}
lies in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\pm\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(or
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\pm\eta}(\mathbb{R}))
if
m^{\pm}
has continuous trajectories) and for any
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\langle u^{+}_{t},u^{-}_{t}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{+}_{s}(u^{-}_{s})+\zeta^{-}_{s}(u^{+}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\langle u^{-}_{s},\mathrm{d}m^{+}_{s}\rangle+\langle u^{+}_{s},\mathrm{d}m^{-}_{s}\rangle\bigr)+[m^{+},m^{-}]_{t}.

(3.6)

Proof.

To see that
u^{\pm}
lies in
D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\pm\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(or
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\pm\eta}(\mathbb{R}))
if
m^{\pm}
has continuous trajectories), we simply apply Theorem
3.2
to
\tilde{u}^{\pm}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
defined by
\tilde{u}^{\pm}_{t}(x)=e^{\pm\eta(x)/2}u^{\pm}_{t}(x)
, which satisfies

\langle\tilde{u}^{\pm}_{t},\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\zeta}^{\pm}_{s}(\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}s+\langle\tilde{m}^{\pm}_{t},\varphi\rangle

for
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\tilde{\zeta}^{\pm}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\tilde{m}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
are given by
\tilde{\zeta}^{\pm}_{t}(\varphi)=\zeta^{\pm}(e^{\pm\eta/2}\varphi)
for
\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\tilde{m}^{\pm}_{t}(x)=e^{\pm\eta(x)/2}m^{\pm}_{t}(x)
, respectively. Next, we apply Itô’s formula from (
3.2
) to
\langle\tilde{u}^{+}_{t},\tilde{u}^{-}_{t}\rangle
, which implies that

\displaystyle\langle u^{+}_{t},u^{-}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{u}^{+}_{t},\tilde{u}^{-}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\zeta}^{+}_{s}(\tilde{u}^{-}_{s})+\tilde{\zeta}^{-}_{s}(\tilde{u}^{+}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\langle\tilde{u}^{-}_{s},\mathrm{d}\tilde{m}^{+}_{s}\rangle+\langle\tilde{u}^{+}_{s},\mathrm{d}\tilde{m}^{-}_{s}\rangle\bigr)+[\tilde{m}^{+},\tilde{m}^{-}]_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\zeta^{+}_{s}(u^{-}_{s})+\zeta^{-}_{s}(u^{+}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\langle u^{-}_{s},\mathrm{d}m^{+}_{s}\rangle+\langle u^{+}_{s},\mathrm{d}m^{-}_{s}\rangle\bigr)+[m^{+},m^{-}]_{t}

as required.
∎

3.3
Semilinear BSPDEs

In this subsection we study semilinear BSPDEs of a slightly more general type than BSPDEs (
2.10
) and (
2.12
) introduced in Section
2
. The existence and uniqueness of the latter BSPDEs (cf. Propositions
2.9
and
2.12
) then follows as a special case. This is further discussed at the end of this subsection. The BSPDE we consider here takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)=&-\Bigl(F_{t}(x,u_{t},\nabla u_{t},q_{t})+\alpha_{t}(x)\colon\nabla^{2}u_{t}(x)+\beta_{t}(x)\colon\nabla q_{t}(x)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+q_{t}(x)\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}m_{t}(x)\end{split}

(3.7)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=\Psi(x)
. Here
m
is a càdlàg
\mathbb{F}
-martingale with values in a suitable weighted function space and together with
q
ensures that the solution
(u,q,m)
is
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable. The coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
are
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable random functions on
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with values in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{W}}
, respectively. The map
F
is an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable random function
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a uniformly bounded derivative. Again we do not assume that
\eta
is the exponent from Section
2
. We define the function space

\mathcal{S}_{\eta}=L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\times L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}))\times\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Definition 3.4
.

A
solution
of BSPDE (
3.7
) consists of a triple
(u,q,m)\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
such that
m
is started from zero and is very strongly orthogonal to
W
, and

\displaystyle\langle u_{t},\varphi\rangle=\langle\Psi,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\Bigl(\bigl\langle F_{s}(\cdot,u_{s},\nabla u_{s},q_{s}),\varphi\bigr\rangle-\langle\nabla u_{s},\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{s}\varphi)\rangle-\bigl\langle q_{s},\nabla\cdot(\beta_{s}\varphi)\bigr\rangle\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{t}^{T}\langle q_{s},\varphi\rangle\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{s}-\langle m_{T}-m_{t},\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}_{\bar{\eta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
.

Note that Definition
3.4
implicitly assumes that the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
are differentiable in
x
. We shall always assume that this is the case.

Assumption 3.5
.

We assume that
\Psi\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},\mathbb{P};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and that there exist constants
c>0
and
C>0
such that

(i)

for all
(t,\omega)
the maps
x\mapsto\alpha_{t}(x)
and
x\mapsto\beta_{t}(x)
have a weak derivative;

(ii)

for all
(t,\omega)
and
(r,p,q)
,
(r^{\prime},p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\in L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})
we have

\displaystyle\bigl\lVert F_{t}(\cdot,r,p,q)-F_{t}(\cdot,r^{\prime},p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\bigr\rVert_{\eta}\leq C\bigl(\lVert r-r^{\prime}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert p-p^{\prime}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert q-q^{\prime}\rVert_{\eta}\bigr);

(iii)

the coefficients
\alpha
,
\nabla\cdot\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\nabla\cdot\beta
are bounded by
C
and for all
(t,\omega)
and
(r,p,q)\in L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert F_{t}(\cdot,r,p,q)\rVert_{\eta}\leq C\bigl(1+\lVert r\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert p\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert q\rVert_{\eta}\bigr);

(iv)

for all
(t,\omega,x)
we have
\alpha_{t}(x)-\frac{\beta_{t}(x)\beta^{\top}_{t}(x)}{2}\geq cI_{d}
.

We have the following existence and uniqueness result for BSPDE (
3.7
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let Assumption
3.5
be satisfied. Then BSPDE (
3.7
) has a unique solution
(u,q,m)
for which
u\in D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Moreover, it holds that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\lVert u_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\lVert m_{T}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla u_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\lVert q_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\bigr)

(3.8)

for a constant
C>0
that only depends on the coefficients, the exponent
\eta
, and
T
.

The remainder of this subsection is dedicated to proving Theorem
3.6
. First, we reduce to the case
\eta=0
. We observe that if
(u,q,m)\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
solves BSPDE (
3.7
) then
(\tilde{u},\tilde{q},\tilde{m})\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
defined by
\tilde{u}_{t}(x)=e^{\eta(x)/2}u_{t}(x)
,
\tilde{q}_{t}(x)=e^{\eta(x)/2}q_{t}(x)
, and
\tilde{m}_{t}(x)=e^{\eta(x)/2}m_{t}(x)
solves BSPDE (
3.7
) with
F
replaced by an appropriately chosen function
\tilde{F}\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying Assumption
3.5
with
\eta
replaced by the constant zero function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and terminal condition
\Psi
replaced by
\tilde{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
defined as
\tilde{\Psi}(x)=e^{\eta(x)/2}\Psi(x)
. The converse is also true. Thus, we may simply analyse the BSPDE satisfied by
(\tilde{u},\tilde{q},\tilde{m})
. Existence, uniqueness, and the estimate (
3.8
) then transfer to BSPDE (
3.7
). Hence, from now on we shall assume that
\eta=0
.

We obtain existence and uniqueness for BSPDE (
3.7
) through a fixed-point argument, so we first have to consider linear BSPDEs of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)=-\Bigl(h_{t}(x)+\alpha_{t}(x):\nabla^{2}u_{t}(x)+\beta_{t}(x):\nabla q_{t}(x)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+q_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}m_{t}(x)\end{split}

(3.9)

with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=\Psi(x)
. The process
h
lies in
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and fulfills the role of the coefficient
F
. A solution
(u,q,m)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
is understood in the sense of Definition
3.4
. We construct a solution to BSPDE (
3.9
) by virtue of a Galerkin approximation.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let Assumption
3.5
be satisfied and let
h\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then BSPDE (
3.9
) has a unique solution
(u,q,m)
for which
u\in D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Moreover, for any
t\in[0,T]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\lVert u_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\lVert m_{T}-m_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}&\int_{t}^{T}\lVert\nabla u_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert q_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C\biggl(\mathbb{E}\lVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}\lvert\langle u_{s},h_{s}\rangle\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s\biggr)\end{split}

(3.10)

for a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
t
.

Proof.

Existence
: Our arguments mirror those given in the proof of
[
50
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
. The main difference lies in the fact that BSPDE (
3.9
) is driven by a martingale with jumps. As mentioned above, we proceed via a Galerkin approximation: for any
n\geq 1
, we construct a Galerkin approximation
(u^{n},q^{n},m^{n})
on
n
basis elements, then we show that the resulting sequence is bounded in
\mathcal{S}_{0}
and that any weakly convergent subsequence yields a solution to BSPDE (
3.9
).

Let us fix an orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consisting of elements in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that the linear span of
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is dense in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
n\geq 1
we consider the BSDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}u^{ni}_{t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigl(\langle\alpha_{t}:\nabla^{2}e_{j},e_{i}\rangle u^{nj}_{t}+\langle\beta_{t}:\nabla e_{j},e_{i}\rangle q^{nj}_{t}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t-\langle h_{t},e_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+q^{ni}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}m^{ni}_{t}

with terminal condition
u^{ni}_{T}=\langle\Psi,e_{i}\rangle
for
1\leq i\leq n
. The existence of an
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable solution
u^{ni}\in D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T])
,
q^{ni}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})
,
m^{ni}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T])
,
i=1
,…,
n
is guaranteed by
[
26
, Example 1.20]
. Next, we define the random function
u^{n}\in D_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
by
u^{n}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{ni}_{t}e_{i}(x)
and, similarly,
q^{n}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}))
and
m^{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then, let
\pi_{n}
denote the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto the linear span of
e_{1}
, …,
e_{n}
, which keeps
u^{n}_{t}
,
q^{n}_{t}
, and
m^{n}_{t}
fixed. The projection allows us to rewrite the finite-dimensional linear BSDE above in the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}u^{n}_{t}(x)=-\pi_{n}\bigl(h_{t}+\alpha_{t}:\nabla^{2}u^{n}_{t}+\beta_{t}:\nabla q^{n}_{t}\bigr)(x)\,\mathrm{d}t+q^{n}_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathrm{d}m^{n}_{t}(x)\end{split}

(3.11)

with terminal condition
u^{n}_{T}(x)=(\pi_{n}\Psi)(x)
.

Now, we apply the generalisation of Itô’s formula from Theorem
3.2
for Hilbert space-valued semimartingales with jumps to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert u^{n}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}&=\lVert\pi_{n}\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\int_{t}^{T}2\Bigl(\bigl\langle\nabla u^{n}_{s},\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{s}u^{n}_{s})\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle q^{n}_{s},\nabla\cdot(\beta_{s}u^{n}_{s})\bigr\rangle\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}&\lVert\Delta_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\lvert\bigl\langle\Delta_{s},(\alpha^{\mu_{1}}_{s}+\lvert\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}\rvert)\Delta_{s}+\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}\cdot\nabla\Delta_{s}\bigr\rangle_{\eta}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\ \ \ +C\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\lvert\Bigl\langle\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s},\bigl(\alpha^{\mu_{1}}_{t}-\alpha^{\mu_{2}}_{t}+\lvert\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}-\beta^{\mu_{2}}_{s}\rvert\bigr)\Delta_{s}+(\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}-\beta^{\mu_{2}}_{s})\cdot\nabla\Delta_{s}\Bigr\rangle_{\eta}\Bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(4.3)

The integrand in the second line above is easily estimated by
\frac{2\epsilon(C_{\lambda}+C_{b})+C_{b}^{2}}{2\epsilon}\lVert\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\lVert\nabla\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
for an
\epsilon>0
that we shall choose later. Next, by Assumption
2.1

(i)
it holds that

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\Bigl\langle\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}

\displaystyle,\bigl(\alpha^{\mu_{1}}_{t}-\alpha^{\mu_{2}}_{t}+\lvert\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}-\beta^{\mu_{2}}_{s}\rvert\bigr)\Delta_{s}+(\beta^{\mu_{1}}_{s}-\beta^{\mu_{2}}_{s})\cdot\nabla\Delta_{s}\Bigr\rangle_{\eta}\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}\rVert_{\eta}d_{0}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t})\bigl((C_{\lambda}+C_{b})\lVert\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+C_{b}\lVert\nabla\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}(1+\epsilon)\lVert\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}}{2\epsilon}\lVert\mu^{1}_{s}-\mu^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\lVert\nabla\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}

for some constant
C_{0}>0
. Here we used that
d_{0}(v,v^{\prime})\leq\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert v-v^{\prime}\rVert_{\eta}
for all
v
,
v^{\prime}\in L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Inserting the previous two estimates into (
4.3
), choosing
\epsilon=\frac{1}{2C}
, rearranging, and then applying Grönwall’s inequality implies that

\lVert\Delta_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Delta_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\mu^{1}_{s}-\mu^{2}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

for
C_{1}>0
, where we used that
\lVert\Phi(\mu^{2})_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
is bounded uniformly over
(t,\omega)\in[0,T]\times\Omega
and
\mu^{2}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. We drop the integral on the left-hand side above, multiply both sides with
e^{-2C_{1}t}
, and then integrate over
t\in[0,T]
. This yields

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2C_{1}t}\lVert\Delta_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2C_{1}t}\lVert\mu^{1}_{t}-\mu^{2}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Clearly, the function

defines a norm on
L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, which is equivalent to the standard norm and turns
\Phi
into a contraction. Thus, by the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique fixed point
\mu\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Since
\mu=\Phi(\mu)
, it holds that
\mu\in C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
solves PDE (
2.4
). If

is another solution to PDE (
2.4
), then
\mu^{\prime}
is also a fixed point of
\Phi
, so by uniqueness of the fixed point of

we get
\mu^{\prime}=\mu
. In other words,
\mu
is the unique solution in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. The estimate (
2.6
) follows from (
4.2
).

Step 3
: Next, let us show that
\mu
actually takes values in
C([0,T];\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. First, we establish
\mu_{t}(x)\geq 0
for a.e.

and
\mathrm{Leb}\otimes\mathbb{P}
-a.e.

. For that we choose the function

in the generalisation of Itô’s formula from Theorem

to be

with

. Using the identities

and
x^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)=2\varphi(x)
it is easy to see that

F(\mu_{t})\leq F(\mu_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\biggl(2C_{\lambda}+\frac{C_{b}^{2}+C_{a}^{2}}{c_{a}}\biggr)F(\mu_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s,

where

is the uniform parabolicity constant from Assumption
2.1

(iii)
. Since
F(\mu_{0})=F(v_{0})=0
, Grönwall’s inequality implies that
F(\mu_{t})=0
, which means that
\mu_{t}(x)\geq 0
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By the discussion below the statement of Proposition
2.4
this shows that
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Continuity of the trajectories then follows from the fact that
\langle\mu_{s},\varphi\rangle\to\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle
as
s\to t
for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that
\lVert e^{\eta/2}\mu_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}}
is bounded uniformly over

.

Step 4
: So far we only proved uniqueness of solutions in the space

, but we need to establish uniqueness within the space of

-adapted

∎

4.2

In this subsection, we show that the value of the optimal control problem for PDE (

) and that there exists a probabilistic setup, which admits an optimal control. To establish the latter, we build on the compactness arguments from
[

]
. For the remainder of the subsection we will emphasise the probabilistic setup
\mathbb{W}
by adding it as a subscript to the symbols denoting the generator, the space of controls, and the cost functional.

We begin with some preliminary remarks on the parabolic function spaces appearing in our analysis. First, we know that for any
R>0
, the space
H^{1}_{\eta}(B_{R})
is compactly embedded in
L_{\eta}^{2}(B_{R})
. Here

to

by zero. Consequently, the Aubin–Lions lemma (see e.g.
[
57

) implies that the space

of functions

lies in
L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ast})
, is compactly embedded in

.

Now, let
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}
and denote the corresponding solution to PDE (
2.4
) by
\mu
. Then, it follows immediately from PDE (
2.4
) and the estimate (

\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\dot{\mu}_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ast}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\mu_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\lVert v_{0}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}

(4.4)

for some

. Together with estimate (

), this implies that every realisation of
\mu
for any control

on any probabilistic setup

lies in the same sufficiently large but bounded subset of

Using Proposition
4.1
, we can show that the value
\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)
of the control problem is independent of the underlying probabilistic setup

. We achieve this by showing that any control in

can be approximated by a sequence of controls that only depend on the information generated by the noise

Proposition 4.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satisfied and suppose that for all
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the map

. In particular,
\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)
does not depend on the probabilistic setup
\mathbb{W}

Proof.

First, we reduce to the case of piecewise constant controls. Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}
. By
[
49

there exists a sequence of partitions
0=t^{n}_{0}<t^{n}_{1}<\dots<t^{n}_{n}=T
for

as well as controls
(\gamma^{n})_{n}
, such that
\gamma^{n}_{0}
is a nonrandom element in

, the space of measurable functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to G
,
\gamma^{n}
is constant on the interval
[t^{n}_{i},t^{n}_{i+1})
for

, …,

, and for any

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{B_{R}}(\gamma^{n}_{t}-\gamma_{t})\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0

. Then Proposition
4.1

(ii)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{n})\leq\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)

. Consequently, minimising over the set of controls which are piecewise constant in time leads to the same value as minimising over all of
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}

\gamma_{0}
is a nonrandom element in

and
\gamma
is constant on the interval

for

, …,
n-1

The next step is to remove the randomness in
\gamma
beyond that provided by the noise

. To achieve this, we must first separate the stochasticity in
\gamma

\mathbb{F}
. By
[
39

, …,

-dimensional Brownian motion and

(B,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})

. Note that since

is adapted to
\mathbb{F}
and

is an

-Brownian motion, it follows that

. We can use this fact to successively replace the increments

by some external randomness. Indeed, let

, …,

be random variables on

and claim that

. Then, since

is independent of

Next, we choose a map
\Psi\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]^{n-1}\times C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})^{n-1}
such that

(\xi^{k}_{1},\dots,\xi^{k}_{n-1},\zeta^{k}_{1},\dots,\zeta^{k}_{n-1})=\Psi\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{s_{k}}}W_{s_{k}}\bigr)\sim(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1},\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}).

Finally, we define controls
\gamma^{k}\in\mathbb{G}
by
\gamma^{k}_{t}=g_{0}
if
t\in[0,t_{1})
and
\gamma^{k}_{t}=\Phi_{i}(W^{k}_{\cdot\land t_{i}},\zeta^{k}_{i},\xi^{k}_{1},\dots,\xi^{k}_{i})
for
t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1})
with
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, so that
(\gamma^{k},W^{k})\sim(\gamma,W)
. In particular, it holds that the sequence
(\gamma^{k},W)_{k}
converges weakly to
(\gamma,W)
on
L^{2}([0,T];L_{\text{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))\times C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})
, so using Proposition
4.1

(ii)
once more we conclude
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{k})\leq\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)
. Now, for any
\epsilon>0
we may choose
\gamma
such that
\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)\leq\inf_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{\prime})+\epsilon
, whence

\inf_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}^{0}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{\prime})\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{k})\leq\inf_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{\prime})+\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0
gives the desired identity.
∎

Let us now turn to the existence of optimal controls and the proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

The first statement of Theorem
2.5
follows immediately from Proposition
4.2
. Next, let
\mathbb{W}
be any probabilistic setup and fix a sequence of controls
(\gamma^{n})_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{n})=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb{G}}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma)=V
and denote the corresponding solutions to PDE (
2.4
) by
\mu^{n}
. By Proposition
4.1
, the sequence
(\mu^{n},\gamma^{n},W)_{n}
is tight on the space
L^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta,\textup{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\times(L^{2}([0,T];L_{\textup{loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}})),\textup{weak})\times C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}})
. Any subsequential limit
(\mu,\gamma,W^{\prime})
can be realised on some probabilistic setup
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
such that
W^{\prime}
is the Brownian motion underlying
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}
. Moreover, by Item
(i)
of Proposition
4.1
,
\mu
is a solution to PDE (
2.4
) corresponding to the control
\gamma
and

V=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}}(\gamma^{n_{k}})\geq\tilde{J}_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}(\gamma)\geq V

along the convergent subsequence

. Thus

is an optimal control on
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
.
∎

4.3
The Necessary Stochastic Maximum Principle

The objective of this subsection is to establish the necessary SMP, Theorem
2.10
. Throughout the subsection we shall assume that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.8
are in place.

The main step in the proof the necessary SMP is to derive a suitable expression of the Gateaux derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\tilde{J}(\gamma+\epsilon h)|_{\epsilon=0}
of the cost functional
\tilde{J}
at a control
\gamma\in\mathbb{G}
in some direction
h
in terms of the adjoint process. The necessary condition then follows by inserting the convexity of the Hamiltonian
H(t,x,v,p,g):=\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)\cdot p+\tilde{f}(t,x,v,g)
in the control argument, guaranteed by Assumption
2.8

(iii)
, into this expression. To compute the Gateaux derivative, we have to study the variation of
\mu
under small perturbations
\epsilon h
of the control
\gamma
. Let
h\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}
be a bounded
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable random function such that
\gamma^{\epsilon}=\gamma+\epsilon h
maps into
G
for all
\epsilon\geq 0
. Denote the unique solution to PDE (
2.4
) with control
\gamma^{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{G}
by
\mu^{\epsilon}
and define
\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\mu^{\epsilon}-\mu)
. We want to show that
\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}
converges in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
to the solution of the random linear PDE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t},\varphi\rangle&=\bigl\langle\mathcal{V}_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi(t,\cdot,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t})\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle\mu_{t},h_{t}^{\top}\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,\cdot,\mu_{t})\nabla\varphi\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ \ \ +\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},D\tilde{\lambda}(t,\cdot,\mu_{t})(x)\varphi+D\tilde{b}(t,\cdot,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t})(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi\Bigr\rangle\mathcal{V}_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(4.8)

for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with initial condition
\mathcal{V}_{0}=0
. Note that by Assumption
2.8

(iii)
, the function
g\mapsto\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)
is affine, so
\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)
does not depend on
g
and, therefore, we write
\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,v)
instead of
\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)
. We understand
\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}
as a function with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}\times d}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.8
be satisfied. Then
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
converges in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
to the unique solution
\mathcal{V}
of PDE (
4.8
).

Proof.

We will first show that the family
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon>0})_{\epsilon}
is Cauchy in the space
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L_{\eta}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H_{\eta}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. It is then straightforward to see that its limit
\mathcal{V}
satisfies PDE (
4.8
). To prove that
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is a Cauchy sequence, we derive a PDE for
\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}
. Then applying the estimate (
4.2
) to this PDE, we establish the boundedness of the family
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
from which we subsequently deduce the Cauchy property.

We set
\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)=\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)=\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\bigr)
for
(t,\omega,x)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon\geq 0
. Our first objective is to derive a PDE for
\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}
. It holds for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that

\mathrm{d}\langle\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon},\varphi\rangle=\bigl\langle\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi(t,\cdot,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma^{\epsilon}_{t})\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},(\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mathfrak{l}^{0}_{t})\varphi+(\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mathfrak{b}^{0}_{t})\cdot\nabla\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

(4.9)

Let us compute the last term on the right-hand side. We can decompose the difference
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\mathfrak{b}^{0}_{t}(x)
as

\bigl(\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)\bigr)+\bigl(\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)-\mathfrak{b}^{0}_{t}(x)\bigr).

The function
\tilde{b}
is linear in the control argument by Assumption
2.8

(iii)
, so it holds that
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma_{t}(x))=\epsilon(h_{t}(x))^{\top}\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})
. Next, by Assumption
2.8

(i)
, for
(t,x,g)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times G
the maps
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,v)
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)
have the linear functional derivatives
D\tilde{\lambda}
and

given by

\displaystyle D\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,v)(y)

\displaystyle=D\lambda\bigl(t,x+\sigma_{0}W_{t},(\id+\sigma_{0}W_{t})^{\#}v\bigr)(y+\sigma_{0}W_{t}),

\displaystyle D\tilde{b}(t,x,v,g)(y)

\displaystyle=Db\bigl(t,x+\sigma_{0}W_{t},(\id+\sigma_{0}W_{t})^{\#}v,g\bigr)(y+\sigma_{0}W_{t})

for
(t,x,v,g,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times G\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})-\tilde{\lambda}(t,x,\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl\langle\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu_{t},D\tilde{\lambda}\bigl(t,x,\theta\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}+(1-\theta)\mu_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl\langle\epsilon\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t},D\tilde{\lambda}\bigl(t,x,\theta\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}+(1-\theta)\mu_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(4.10)

and, similarly,

\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)-\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\mu_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl\langle\epsilon\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t},D\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\theta\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}+(1-\theta)\mu_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(4.11)

We set
\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(x,y)=D\tilde{\lambda}\bigl(t,x,\theta\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}+(1-\theta)\mu_{t}\bigr)(y)
and
B^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(x,y)=D\tilde{b}\bigl(t,x,\theta\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}+(1-\theta)\mu_{t},\gamma_{t}(x)\bigr)(y)
for
(t,\omega,x,y)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we insert the expressions
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma_{t}(x))=\epsilon(h_{t}(x))^{\top}\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})
, (
4.10
), and (
4.11
) into Equation (

), which yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\langle\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t},\varphi\rangle=&\bigl\langle\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t},\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\varphi(t,\cdot,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t},\gamma^{\epsilon}_{t})\big\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl\langle\mu_{t},h_{t}^{\top}\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})\nabla\varphi\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(\cdot,x)\varphi+B^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(\cdot,x)\cdot\nabla\varphi\Bigr\rangle\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\biggr)\mathrm{d}\theta\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(4.12)

for
\varphi\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Next, we derive a uniform bound on
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
. Clearly, the PDE (
4.12
) has the same form as PDE (
4.1
) with
\alpha
,
\beta
,
h^{0}
, and
h^{1}
replaced by
\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}
,
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}
,
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,0}
, and
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,1}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,0}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\langle\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(x,\cdot),\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,1}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}(x)(h_{t}(x))^{\top}\nabla_{g}\tilde{b}(t,x,\mu^{\epsilon}_{t})+\mu_{t}(x)\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\langle B^{\epsilon,\theta}_{t}(x,\cdot),\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

We will now apply the estimate (
4.2
) to
\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}

Inserting this into the bound provided by (

) yields

\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(1+\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+(1+\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta})\lVert\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.13)

for a constant

depending on
\mu
,
\eta_{0}
, the bounds on the coefficients
\lambda
and

, and
\lVert h\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sup_{y\in G}\lvert y\rvert
. Using the estimate
(1+\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta})\lVert\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\leq\frac{C}{2}(1+\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta})^{2}+\frac{1}{2C}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
, rearranging (

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C

for a possibly enlarged constant
C
independent of
\epsilon
.

Using the uniform bound on the family
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
, we can show that it is Cauchy in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Indeed, let us define
\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}=\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}-\mathcal{V}^{\delta}
. Note that again, the PDE satisfied by

is of the same form as PDE (
4.1
). This time with
\alpha
,
\beta
,

, and
h^{1}
replaced by
\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}
,
\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}
,
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,0}
, and
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,1}
, respectively, where

\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{t}(x)=\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{t}(x)\bigl(\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}_{t}(x)-\mathfrak{l}^{\delta}_{t}(x)\bigr)+\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,0}_{t}(x)-\mathfrak{h}^{\delta,0}_{t}(x)

and

Once more, we make us of the estimate (
4.2
) to show that the expression
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t
vanishes as
\epsilon
,
\delta\to 0
. The estimate reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s&\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\lvert\bigl\langle\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}(\mathfrak{l}^{\epsilon}_{s}+\lvert\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rvert)+\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{s}+\lvert\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s}\rvert,\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle_{\eta}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\ \ \ +C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl\lvert\bigl\langle\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\mathfrak{b}^{\epsilon}_{s}+\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s},\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle_{\eta}\bigr\rvert\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(4.14)

Let us first derive bounds on the functions
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,0}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,1}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,0}_{s}(x)-\mathfrak{h}^{\delta,0}_{s}(x)=\mu_{s}(x)\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\langle\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot),\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta+\mu_{s}(x)\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\langle\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot)-\Lambda^{\delta,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot),\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{s}\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

so that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{s}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C_{\lambda}d_{0}(\mu^{\epsilon}_{s},\mu^{\delta}_{s})\lvert\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{s}(x)\rvert+C_{\lambda}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\mu_{s}(x)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\mu_{s}(x)\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\bigl(\Lambda^{\epsilon,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot)-\Lambda^{\delta,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\bigr\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta

where

. Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\lvert\mathfrak{h}^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C_{b,G}\bigl(\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert\mu^{\epsilon}_{s}-\mu^{\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lvert\epsilon-\delta\rvert\bigr)\lvert\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{s}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\bigl(C_{b,G}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s}(x)\bigr)\mu_{s}(x)

with
I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\lVert e^{-\eta/2}\bigl(B^{\epsilon,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot)-B^{\delta,\theta}_{s}(x,\cdot)\bigr)\bigr\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. Plugging the previous two displays into (

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}

\displaystyle\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\bigr)\Bigl(\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert\mu^{\epsilon}_{s}-\mu^{\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lvert\epsilon-\delta\rvert\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\ \ \ +C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}+\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}\bigr)\bigl\lVert(I^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{s}+I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s})\mu_{s}\bigr\rVert_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{C+2C^{2}}{2}\Bigl(3\lVert\mu^{\epsilon}_{s}-\mu^{\delta}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+3\lvert\epsilon-\delta\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lVert(I^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{s}+I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{s})\mu_{t}\bigr\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

for a constant

independent of
\epsilon
,
\delta>0
. Again, we rearrange and apply Grönwall’s inequality, whereby

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\lVert\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu^{\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\lvert\epsilon-\delta\rvert^{2}+\bigl\lVert(I^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{t}+I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{t})\mu_{t}\bigr\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we enlarge

if necessary. We claim that the three expressions on the right-hand side vanish as

,

. Firstly, it holds that
\lVert\mu^{\epsilon}_{t}-\mu^{\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\leq\lVert\epsilon\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{t}-\delta\mathcal{V}^{\delta}_{t}\lVert_{\eta}^{2}
, which tends to zero due to the uniform in
\epsilon

\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\lVert\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon}_{s}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}
. The convergence of the second term is clear. For the third term, we note that owing to the continuity of

and

in the measure argument, we have

for a.e.

. Since

and

, the dominated convergence theorem implies that
\bigl\lVert(I^{\epsilon,\delta,0}_{t}+I^{\epsilon,\delta,1}_{t})\mu_{t}\bigr\rVert_{\eta}\to 0
. Hence,
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\nabla\Delta^{\epsilon,\delta}_{t}\rVert_{\eta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0
as
\epsilon
,
\delta\to 0
, so that
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
is Cauchy in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the limit of
(\mathcal{V}^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
in
C_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}([0,T];H^{1}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Using the convergence of

to

solves PDE (

) can be easily deduced from (

). This concludes the proof.

5
Application to a Model of Government Interventions in Financial Systems

In this final section we discuss the application of the theory developed in this article to the mean-field model for government interventions in financial markets from
[
33
]
. The model consists of an infinite system of banks, each represented by the capital it holds in excess of the regulatory capital requirements. The institutions have common exposures, modelled by the noise
W
, and mutual obligations. Whenever the excess capital of a bank is negative, so that it is in breach of the capital requirements, it has a positive default intensity, expressed by the coefficient
\lambda
. Through the mutual obligations, the default of an institution leads to losses at other banks, making them more likely to default as well. This contagion may precipitate systemic events, where a large portion of the system is wiped out in a short period of time. The controller, which should be thought of as a government or another central authority, can inject capital into the system to prevent defaults.

The excess capital
X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
of a representative bank in the mean-field system follows the McKean–Vlasov SDE

X_{t}=(\xi-c)+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sigma B_{t}+\sigma_{0}W_{t}-\kappa L_{t},

(5.1)

where
\xi
is the initial capital and
c>0
denotes the regulatory capital requirements,
\alpha_{s}\in[0,g_{\text{max}}]
is the amount of capital injected by the government at time
t\in[0,T]
,
B
and
W
are independent Brownian motions that represent the fluctuations of banks’ assets values correlated through the common noise
W
,
\kappa>0
measures the size of the mutual obligations, and
L_{t}
denotes the fraction of banks that have defaulted up to time
t\in[0,T]
conditional on the common noise
W
. In particular, we assume that the losses from default are proportional to the fraction of defaulted entities
L_{t}
. Let us try to give a more precise account of the latter quantity. As mentioned above, the representative institution’s instantaneous default intensity is
\lambda(X_{t})
, where we assume that
\lambda
is bounded and
\lambda(x)=0
for
x\geq 0
while
\lambda(x)>0
for
x<0
. Default occurs, when the cumulative intensity
\Lambda_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(X_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s
exceeds a standard exponentially distributed random variable
\theta
, which is independent of
X_{0}
,
B
,
W
, and
\alpha
. Hence,
L_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\theta\geq\Lambda_{t}|W)=1-\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Lambda_{t}}]=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nu_{s},\lambda\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s
, where
\nu_{t}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in\cdot,\,\theta>\Lambda_{t}|W)
is the conditional subprobability distribution of the solvent banks. The government’s objective is to minimise the cost functional

\alpha\mapsto\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{\theta>\Lambda_{t}}w\alpha_{t}+L_{T}\biggr]=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\Lambda_{t}}w\alpha_{t}+L_{T}\biggr]

over
\mathbb{F}^{\xi,B,W}
-progressively measurable
[0,g_{\text{max}}]
-valued processes
\alpha=(\alpha_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
. The weight
w>0
trades off the negative externalities caused by the bailout, such as moral hazard, measured by the size of the capital injections with the cost of a potential systemic crisis, captured by the fraction of defaulted entities

at time
T
.

If
\alpha
takes the form
\alpha_{t}=\gamma_{t}(X_{t})
for an
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-progressively measurable random function
\gamma\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,g_{\text{max}}]
, then one can show that
\nu=(\nu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfies the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation

\mathrm{d}\langle\nu_{t},\varphi\rangle=\Bigl\langle\nu_{t},-\lambda\varphi+\gamma_{t}\partial_{x}\varphi-\kappa\langle\nu_{t},\lambda\rangle\partial_{x}\varphi+\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{0}^{2}\bigr)\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+\langle\nu_{t},\sigma_{0}\partial_{x}\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(5.2)

for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with initial condition
\nu_{0}=\mathcal{L}(X_{0})
. This SPDE is a special case of SPDE (
2.1
) (note, however, the sign change of
\lambda
) and we can rewrite the cost functional in terms of

as

J(\gamma)=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int_{0}^{T}w\langle\nu_{t},\gamma_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t+1-\nu_{T}(\mathbb{R})\biggr].

This setup fits the framework introduced in Section
2
. Moreover, by Theorem
A.2
, the control of McKean–Vlasov SDE (
5.1
) over
\mathbb{F}^{X_{0},B,W}
-progressively measurable
[0,g_{\text{max}}]
-valued controls yields the same optimal value as the control of the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation (
5.2
) over
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-progressively measurable random functions
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,g_{\text{max}}]
. (Note that here we use that the weak formulation for the McKean–Vlasov control problem as introduced in Appendix
A.1
is equivalent to the strong formulation with
\mathbb{F}^{X_{0},B,W}
-progressively measurable controls considered here by
[

, and that the value for the control problem of the nonlinear stochastic Fokker–Planck equation is independent of the weak setup, cf. Theorem
2.5
.) The nonlinear random Fokker–Planck equation associated to SPDE (
5.2
) is

\mathrm{d}\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle=\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},-\tilde{\lambda}\varphi+\gamma_{t}\partial_{x}\varphi-\kappa\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{\lambda}\rangle\partial_{x}\varphi+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.3)

for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with initial condition
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{L}(X_{0})
, where
\gamma
is still an
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-progressively measurable random function
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,g_{\text{max}}]
.

After replacing
\mathbb{F}^{W}
with a larger filtration
\mathbb{F}
if necessary, Theorem
2.5
implies that there exists an optimal
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable control
\gamma\mathpunct{:}[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,g_{\text{max}}]
for
g_{\text{max}}>0
. Now, for
p\in\mathbb{R}
, the reduced Hamiltonian
g\mapsto H_{1}(p,g)=gp+wg=(p+w)g
is linear and, therefore, convex. Hence, the necessary SMP, Theorem
2.10

\gamma_{t}(x)=g_{\text{max}}\mathbf{1}_{\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)\leq-w}
, where we use that the minimiser of the map

over

is given by
p\mapsto g_{\text{max}}\mathbf{1}_{p\leq-w}
. The adjoint process
(u,m)
solves the semilinear BSPDE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}u_{t}(x)&=\Bigl(\tilde{\lambda}(x)u_{t}(x)+g_{\text{max}}(\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)+w)_{-}+\kappa\langle\mu_{t},\tilde{\lambda}\rangle\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)+\kappa\langle\mu_{t},\partial_{x}u_{t}\rangle\tilde{\lambda}(x)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ \ \ -\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}m_{t}(x)\end{split}

(5.4)

with terminal condition
u_{T}(x)=D(1-\nu_{T}(\mathbb{R}))=-1

p\mapsto-g_{\text{max}}(p+w)_{-}
arises from the minimisation of
(p+w)g
over

. As one would expect, the optimal control is of bang-bang type, i.e. either the controller injects the maximal amount of capital allowed (this happens when

) or none at all. Naturally, a smaller weight lowers the threshold at which the controller intervenes.

Let us assume for the moment, that
\kappa
and
\sigma_{0}
vanish, so that BSPDE (

-\partial_{t}u_{t}(x)+\lambda(x)u_{t}(x)+g_{\text{max}}(\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)+w)_{-}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}u_{t}(x)=0.

The functions which are constantly equal to

-1\leq u_{t}(x)\leq 0

is nonpositive, it is easy to see that

is a subsolution, whence

is nonincreasing, in other words
\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)\leq 0
. One may then conjecture that for each time

the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mathpunct{:}\partial_{x}u_{t}(x)\leq-w\}

in
\mathbb{R}
. That is, banks whose excess capital is above

are safe and do not obtain any capital injections while banks with an excess capital below

However, as for the local PDE we conjecture that the set

.

In conclusion, let us remark that the sufficient SMP, Theorem
2.14
, cannot be applied to the nonlinear model, because if
\kappa>0
, then the map
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})\ni v\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{0}(t,v,u_{t},\partial_{x}u_{t})=\bigl\langle v,-\tilde{\lambda}u_{t}-\kappa\langle v,\tilde{\lambda}\rangle\partial_{x}u_{t}\bigr\rangle

is unique (up to modifications on null sets of

).

G

\mathbb{F}

(iii)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}^{0}

\tilde{W}

\mathbb{F}

W

Some Properties of the Linear Functional Derivative

Lemma A.3
.

Let

\mathcal{F}\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(F(v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-F(v)\bigr)=\langle v^{\prime}-v,\mathcal{F}(v)\rangle

(A.2)

for all
v
,
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\mathcal{F}
is a linear functional derivative of
F
.

Proof.

For the first statement, it follows from the definition of the linear functional derivative that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(F(v+\epsilon\delta_{x})-F(v)\bigr)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\delta_{x},DF(v+\epsilon\theta\delta_{x})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta=DF(v)(x),

where we used the continuity of
DF
in the measure argument in the last step. Note that in fact much less than continuity with respect to
d_{0}
is required here.

To establish the second statement, let us fix
v
,
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define the function
f\mathpunct{:}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(\theta)=F(v+\theta(v^{\prime}-v))
. From (
A.2
), it follows that
f
is differentiable with
f^{\prime}(\theta)=\bigl\langle v^{\prime}-v,\mathcal{F}(v+\theta(v^{\prime}-v))\bigr\rangle
. Thus, by the fundamental theorem of calculus it holds that

F(v^{\prime})-F(v)=f(1)-f(0)=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\langle v^{\prime}-v,\mathcal{F}(\theta v^{\prime}+(1-\theta)v)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

This shows that
\mathcal{F}
is indeed a linear functional derivative of
F
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
F\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable and bounded function such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto F(x,v)
has a linear functional derivative
DF(x,\cdot)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
DF\mathpunct{:}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable, bounded, and continuous in the measure argument. Then the map
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni v\mapsto\langle v,F(\cdot,v)\rangle
possesses the linear functional derivative

.

Proof.

Set
\mathbb{F}(v)=\langle v,F(\cdot,v)\rangle
for
v\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By the second part of Lemma

it is enough to show that for any

,
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the limit
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(\mathbb{F}(v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-\mathbb{F}(v)\bigr)
exists and is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigl(F(x,v)+\langle v,DF(\cdot,v)(x)\rangle\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}(v^{\prime}-v)(x).

(A.3)

We compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(\mathbb{F}(v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-\mathbb{F}(v)\bigr)&=\bigl\langle v^{\prime}-v,F(\cdot,v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))\bigr\rangle\\
&\ \ \ +\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl\langle v,F(\cdot,v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-F(\cdot,v)\bigr\rangle.\end{split}

(A.4)

Now, since for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the map

has a linear functional derivative, its continuous. Consequently, the first term on the right-hand side above converges to

by the dominated convergence theorem. Next, for fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it follows from the continuity of
m\mapsto DF(x,m)(y)
for all

and the first part of Lemma

that

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(F(x,v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-F(x,v)\bigr)\to\langle v^{\prime}-v,DF(x,v)\rangle.

Applying the dominated convergence theorem once more we conclude that the second expression on the right-hand side of (
A.4
) tends to

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle v,DF(\cdot,v)(x)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}(v^{\prime}-v)(x).

Combining the two expression shows that
\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(\mathbb{F}(v+\epsilon(v^{\prime}-v))-\mathbb{F}(v)\bigr)
indeed tends to the expression in (
A.3
).
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
F\mathpunct{:}\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and assume

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\mu^{n}_{t},\varphi(t,\cdot)F\bigl(t,\cdot+w^{n}_{t},\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},g^{n}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},\varphi(t,\cdot)F\bigl(t,\cdot+w_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t},g_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t;

(iv)

if
(g^{n})_{n}
converges in the strong topology on
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
and the map
h\mapsto F(t,x,v,h)
is upper semicontinuous for all
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle\mu^{n}_{t},F\bigl(t,\cdot+w^{n}_{t},\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},g^{n}_{t}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle\mu_{t},F\bigl(t,\cdot+w_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t},g_{t}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

Let us define
\tilde{g}^{n}
,
\tilde{g}\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
by
\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}(x)=g^{n}_{t}(x-w^{n}_{t})
and
\tilde{g}_{t}(x)=g_{t}(x-w_{t})
and, similarly, set
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x-w^{n}_{t})
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x-w_{t})
. Note that, like
(\mu^{n}_{t})_{n}
, the sequence
(\tilde{\mu}^{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and converges to
\tilde{\mu}
in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\eta,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and, like
(g^{n})_{n}
, the sequence
(\tilde{g}^{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
\tilde{g}
in the space
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
. Thus, by Lemma
A.8
it holds that

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1}\langle\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{\mu}_{t},\varphi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0

(A.11)

and, in particular,
\int_{0}^{T}d_{0}^{2}(\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0
. Next, since
G
is convex and
(g^{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
g
in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
,
g^{n}_{t}(x)\in G
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
implies the same is true for
g
, i.e.
g_{t}(x)\in G
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the proof of the four statements, we use the decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}&\Bigl(\Bigl\langle\mu^{n}_{t},\varphi(t,\cdot)F\bigl(t,\cdot+w^{n}_{t},\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},g^{n}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\mu_{t},\varphi(t,\cdot)F\bigl(t,\cdot+w_{t},\tilde{\mu}_{t},g_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{t}F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}\bigr)\bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ \ \ +\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{t}-\tilde{\varphi}_{t})F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ \ \ +\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\Bigl(F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}\bigr)-F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\ \ \ +\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\Bigl(F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}\bigr)-F\bigl(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(A.12)

Note that for Item
(ii)
and
(iv)
, the appearances of
\varphi
and the functions derived from it should be removed, in which case the second term on the right-hand side above equals zero. Now, the first three terms on the right-hand side above vanish as
n\to\infty
independently of the specific assumptions made in Items
(i)
to
(iv)
. Indeed, the first expression vanishes by (
A.11
). Next, for the second term in (
A.12
) (relevant for Items
(i)
and
(iii)
), we have by assumption that

\Bigl\lvert(\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{t}(x)-\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(x))F\bigl(t,x,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}(x)\bigr)\Bigr\rvert\leq\lVert F\rVert_{\infty}\lvert\tilde{\varphi}^{n}_{t}(x)-\tilde{\varphi}_{t}(x)\rvert\to 0

since
w^{n}_{t}\to w_{t}
and by continuity of
\varphi
. So convergence follows from the dominated convergence theorem. Lastly, since
F
is continuous in the measure argument uniformly in the control, we have

\Bigl\lvert F\bigl(t,x,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t}(x)\bigr)-F\bigl(t,x,\tilde{\mu}^{n}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t}(x)\bigr)\Bigr\rvert\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. Thus, convergence of the third expression on the right-hand side of (
A.12
) is again a consequence of the dominated convergence theorem.

The behaviour as
n\to\infty
of the remaining terms on the right-hand side will be determined based on the specific assumptions made in Items
(i)
to
(i)
. We proceed in sequence.

Item
(i)
: The last term on the right-hand side of (
A.12
) vanishes as
n\to\infty
since
\tilde{\varphi}
has a compact support,
F
is affine in the control argument, and the sequence
(\tilde{g}^{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
\tilde{g}
in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
.

Item
(ii)
: Note that the convexity of
F
in the control argument implies convexity of the function
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};G))\ni h\mapsto\int_{0}^{T}\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},h_{t})\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t
and, therefore, its weak lower semicontinuity. Since
(\tilde{g}^{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
\tilde{g}
in
L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{G}}))
, we get that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t})-F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0.

Item
(iii)
: This is again a straightforward consequence of the dominated convergence theorem.

Item
(iv)
: Let us pick a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k}
that achieves the limit superior of the expression

\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t})-F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t.

By choosing a further subsequence if necessary (which we still denote by
(n_{k})_{k}
), we may assume that
\tilde{g}^{n_{k}}_{t}(x)
converges to
\tilde{g}_{t}(x)
for a.e.
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then upper semicontinuity of
F
in the control argument implies that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}F\bigl(t,x,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n_{k}}_{t}(x)\bigr)\leq F\bigl(t,x,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t}(x)\bigr).

Multiplying both sides by
\tilde{\mu}_{t}(x)
, integrating over
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and then applying the reverse version of Fatou’s lemma gives

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F

\displaystyle(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n}_{t})-F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n_{k}}_{t})-F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Bigl\langle\tilde{\mu}_{t},F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}^{n_{k}}_{t})-F(t,\cdot,\tilde{\mu}_{t},\tilde{g}_{t})\Bigr\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 0.

This establishes Item
(iv)
and concludes the proof.
