Abstract
We propose heavy ball neural ordinary differential equations (HBNODEs), leveraging the continuous limit of the classical momentum accelerated gradient descent, to improve neural ODEs (NODEs) training and inference. HBNODEs have two properties that imply practical advantages over NODEs: (i) The adjoint state of an HBNODE also satisfies an HBNODE, accelerating both forward and backward ODE solvers, thus significantly reducing the number of function evaluations (NFEs) and improving the utility of the trained models. (ii) The spectrum of HBNODEs is well structured, enabling effective learning of long-term dependencies from complex sequential data. We verify the advantages of HBNODEs over NODEs on benchmark tasks, including image classification, learning complex dynamics, and sequential modeling. Our method requires remarkably fewer forward and backward NFEs, is more accurate, and learns long-term dependencies more effectively than the other ODE-based neural network models. Code is available at https://github.com/hedixia/HeavyBallNODE .

Introduction

Neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs) are a family of continuous-depth machine learning (ML) models whose forward and backward propagations rely on solving an ODE and its adjoint equation
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
.
NODEs model the dynamics of hidden features
{\bm{h}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
using an ODE, which is parametrized by a neural network

f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with learnable parameters
\theta
, i.e.,

\frac{d{\bm{h}}(t)}{dt}=f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta).

(1)

Figure 1:
Contrasting NODE, ANODE, SONODE, HBNODE, and GHBNODE for CIFAR10 classification in NFEs, training time, and test accuracy. (Tolerance:
10^{-5}
, see Sec.
5.2
for experimental details.)

Starting from the input
{\bm{h}}(t_{0})
, NODEs obtain the output
{\bm{h}}(T)
by solving (
1
) for
t_{0}\leq t\leq T
with the initial value
{\bm{h}}(t_{0})
, using a black-box numerical ODE solver. The number of function evaluations (NFEs) that the black-box ODE solver
requires in a single forward pass is an analogue for the continuous-depth models
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
to the depth of networks in ResNets. The loss between NODE prediction
{\bm{h}}(T)
and the ground truth is denoted by
\mathcal{L}({\bm{h}}(T))
; we
update parameters
\theta
using the following gradient

(
Pontryagin 2018
)

\frac{d\mathcal{L}({\bm{h}}(T))}{d\theta}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{a}}(t)\frac{\partial f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)}{\partial\theta}dt,

(2)

where
{\bm{a}}(t):=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{h}}(t)
is the adjoint state,
which satisfies the
adjoint equation

\frac{d{\bm{a}}(t)}{dt}=-{\bm{a}}(t)\frac{\partial f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)}{\partial{\bm{h}}}.

(3)

NODEs are flexible in learning from
irregularly-sampled
sequential data and particularly suitable for learning complex dynamical systems
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Rubanova et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Norcliffe et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Kidger et al. 2020
)
, which can be trained by efficient algorithms
(
Quaglino et al. 2020
;
Daulbaev et al. 2020
;
Zhuang et al. 2021
)
. NODE-based continuous generative models have computational advantages over the
classical normalizing flows
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Grathwohl et al. 2019
;
Yildiz et al. 2019
;
Finlay et al. 2020
)
. NODEs have also been generalized to neural stochastic differential equations, stochastic processes,
and graph NODEs
(
Jia and Benson 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Poli et al. 2019
;
Tzen and Raginsky 2019
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Norcliffe et al. 2021
)
.
The drawback of NODEs is also prominent. In many ML tasks, NODEs require very high NFEs in both training and inference, especially in high accuracy settings where a lower tolerance is
needed.
The NFEs increase rapidly with training; high NFEs reduce computational speed and accuracy of NODEs
and can lead to blow-ups in the worst-case scenario

(
Grathwohl et al. 2019
;
Dupont et al. 2019
;
Massaroli et al. 2020
;
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
. As an illustration, we train NODEs for CIFAR10 classification using the same model and experimental settings as in
(
Dupont et al. 2019
)
, except using a tolerance of
10^{-5}
; Fig.
1
shows
both forward and backward NFEs and the training time of different ODE-based models; we see that NFEs and computational

(
Dupont et al. 2019
)
, and SONODE
(
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
. More results on the large NFE and degrading utility issues for different benchmark experiments are available in Sec.
5
. Another issue is that NODEs often fail to effectively learn long-term dependencies in sequential data
(
Lechner and Hasani 2020
)
,
discussed in Sec.
4
.

1.1
Contribution

We propose heavy ball neural ODEs (HBNODEs),
leveraging the continuous limit of the classical momentum accelerated gradient descent, to improve NODE training and inference. At the core of HBNODE is replacing the first-order ODE (
1
) with a heavy ball ODE (HBODE), i.e., a second-order ODE with an
appropriate damping term. HBNODEs
have two theoretical
properties that imply practical advantages over NODEs:

•

The adjoint equation
used for training a HBNODE is also a HBNODE
(see Prop.
1
and Prop.
2
), accelerating both forward and backward propagation, thus significantly
reducing
both forward and backward NFEs.
The reduction in NFE using HBNODE over existing benchmark ODE-based models becomes more aggressive as the error tolerance of the ODE solvers decreases.

•

The spectrum of the HBODE is well-structured (see Prop.
4
),
alleviating
the vanishing gradient issue in back-propagation and
enabling the model to effectively learn
long-term dependencies from sequential data.

To mitigate the potential
blow-up problem
in training
HBNODEs,
we further propose generalized HBNODEs (GHBNODEs) by integrating
skip connections
(
He et al. 2016
)
and gating mechanisms
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)

into the HBNODE. See Sec.
3
for details.

Organization

2
Heavy Ball Neural Ordinary Differential Equations

2.1
Heavy ball ordinary differential equation

Classical momentum method, a.k.a., the heavy ball method, has achieved remarkable success in accelerating gradient descent
(
Polyak 1964
)
and
has significantly improved the training of deep neural networks

(
Sutskever et al. 2013
)
.
As the continuous limit of the classical momentum method, heavy ball ODE (HBODE) has been studied in various settings and
has been used to analyze
the acceleration phenomenon of the momentum methods.
For the ease of reading and completeness, we derive the HBODE from the
classical momentum method.
Starting from initial points
{\bm{x}}^{0}
and
{\bm{x}}^{1}
, gradient descent with classical momentum searches a minimum of the function
F({\bm{x}})
through the following iteration

{\bm{x}}^{k+1}={\bm{x}}^{k}-s\nabla F({\bm{x}}^{k})+\beta({\bm{x}}^{k}-{\bm{x}}^{k-1}),

(4)

where
s>0
is the step size and
0\leq\beta<1
is the momentum hyperparameter.
For any fixed step size
s
, let

{\bm{m}}^{k}:=({{\bm{x}}^{k+1}-{\bm{x}}^{k}})/{\sqrt{s}},

and
let
\beta:=1-\gamma\sqrt{s}
,
where
\gamma\geq 0
is another hyperparameter.
Then we can rewrite (
4
) as

{\bm{m}}^{k+1}=(1-\gamma\sqrt{s}){\bm{m}}^{k}-\sqrt{s}\nabla F({\bm{x}}^{k});\ {\bm{x}}^{k+1}={\bm{x}}^{k}+\sqrt{s}{\bm{m}}^{k+1}.

(5)

Let

s\to 0
in (
5
); we obtain the following system of
first-order ODEs,

\frac{d{\bm{x}}(t)}{dt}={\bm{m}}(t);\ \frac{d{\bm{m}}(t)}{dt}=-\gamma{\bm{m}}(t)-\nabla F({\bm{x}}(t)).

(6)

This can be further
rewritten
as a
second-order heavy ball ODE (HBODE), which also models a damped oscillator,

\frac{d^{2}{\bm{x}}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\gamma\frac{d{\bm{x}}(t)}{dt}=-\nabla F({\bm{x}}(t)).

(7)

Figure 2:
Comparing the trajectory of ODE and HBODE when
F({\bm{x}})
is the Rosenbrock (left) and Beale (right) functions.

We compare the dynamics of HBODE (
7
) and the following ODE limit of the gradient descent (GD)

\frac{d{\bm{x}}}{dt}=-\nabla F({\bm{x}}).

(8)

In particular, we solve the ODEs (
7
) and (
8
) with
F({\bm{x}})
defined as a
Rosenbrock

(
Rosenbrock 1960
)
or
Beale

(
Gonçalves 1971
)
function (see Appendix
E.6
for experimental details). Fig.
2
shows that
with the same
numerical ODE solver, HBODE converges to the stationary point
(marked by stars)
faster than (
8
).
The fact that HBODE can accelerate the dynamics of the ODE for a gradient system motivates us to propose HBNODE to accelerate forward propagation of NODE.

2.2
Heavy ball neural ordinary differential equations

Similar to NODE, we parameterize
-\nabla F
in (
7
) using a neural network
f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)
, resulting in the following HBNODE
with initial position
{\bm{h}}(t_{0})
and
momentum
{\bm{m}}(t_{0}):=d{\bm{h}}/dt(t_{0})
,

\frac{d^{2}{\bm{h}}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\gamma\frac{d{\bm{h}}(t)}{dt}=f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta),

(9)

where
\gamma\geq 0
is the damping parameter, which can be set as a tunable or a learnable hyperparmater with positivity constraint. In the trainable case, we use
\gamma=\epsilon\cdot\text{sigmoid}(\omega)
for a trainable
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and a fixed tunable upper bound
\epsilon
(we set
\epsilon=1
below).
According to (
6
), HBNODE (
9
) is equivalent to

\frac{d{\bm{h}}(t)}{dt}={\bm{m}}(t);\quad\frac{d{\bm{m}}(t)}{dt}=-\gamma{\bm{m}}(t)+f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta).

(10)

Equation (
9
)
(or equivalently, the system (
10
))
defines the forward ODE for the HBNODE, and we can use either the first-order (Prop.
2
) or the second-order (Prop.
1
) adjoint sensitivity method to update the parameter
\theta

(
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
.

Proposition 1

(Adjoint equation for HBNODE)
.

The adjoint state
{\bm{a}}(t):=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{h}}(t)
for the HBNODE (
9
) satisfies the following HBODE with the same damping parameter
\gamma
as that in (
9
),

\frac{d^{2}{\bm{a}}(t)}{dt^{2}}-\gamma\frac{d{\bm{a}}(t)}{dt}={\bm{a}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta).

(11)

Remark 1
.

Note that we solve the adjoint equation (
11
) from time

t=T
to
t=t_{0}

in the backward propagation. By
letting
\tau=T-t
and
{\bm{b}}(\tau)={\bm{a}}(T-\tau)
, we
can
rewrite (
11
) as follows,

\frac{d^{2}{\bm{b}}(\tau)}{d\tau^{2}}+\gamma\frac{d{\bm{b}}(\tau)}{d\tau}={\bm{b}}(\tau)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(T-\tau),T-\tau,\theta).

(12)

Therefore, the adjoint of the HBNODE is also a HBNODE and they have the same damping parameter.

We can also employ (
10
) and its adjoint for the forward and backward propagations, respectively.

Proposition 2

(Adjoint equations for the first-order HBNODE system)
.

The adjoint states
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}(t)

:=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{h}}(t)
and
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t):=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{m}}(t)
for the first-order HBNODE system (
10
)
satisfy

\frac{d{\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}(t)}{dt}=-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta);\quad\frac{d{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)}{dt}=-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}(t)+\gamma{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t).

(13)

Remark 2
.

Let
\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t)=d{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)/dt
, then
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)
and
\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t)
satisfies the following first-order heavy ball ODE system

\frac{d{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)}{dt}=\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t);\quad\frac{d\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t)}{dt}={\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)+\gamma\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t).

(14)

Note that we solve this system backward in time in back-propagation.
Moreover, we have
{\bm{a}}_{{\bm{h}}}(t)=\gamma{\bm{a}}_{\bm{m}}(t)-\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{\bm{m}}(t)
.

Similar to
(
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
, we use the coupled first-order HBNODE system (
10
) and its adjoint first-order HBNODE system (
13
) for practical implementation, since the entangled representation permits
faster computation
(
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
of the gradients
of
the coupled ODE systems.

(Adjoint equations for GHBNODEs)

The adjoint states

4
Learning long-term dependencies – Vanishing gradient

It is known that the vanishing and exploding gradients are two bottlenecks for training recurrent neural networks (RNNs) with long-term dependencies
(
Bengio et al. 1994
;
Pascanu et al. 2013
)
(see Appendix
C
for a brief review on the exploding and vanishing gradient issues in training RNNs). The exploding gradients issue can be effectively resolved via gradient clipping, training loss regularization, etc
(
Pascanu et al. 2013
;
Erichson et al. 2021
)
. Thus in practice the vanishing gradient is the major issue for learning long-term dependencies
(
Pascanu et al. 2013
)
. As the continuous analogue of RNN,
NODEs as well as their hybrid ODE-RNN models, may also suffer from vanishing in the adjoint state
{\bm{a}}(t):=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{h}}(t)

(
Lechner and Hasani 2020
)
. When the vanishing gradient issue happens,
{\bm{a}}(t)
goes to
{\bf 0}
quickly as
T-t
increases, then

in (
2
) will be independent of these
{\bm{a}}(t)
. We have the following expressions for the adjoint states of the NODE and HBNODE (see Appendix
C
for detailed derivation):

•

For NODE, we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}\exp\Big\{-\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(s),s,\theta)ds\Big\}.

(17)

•

For GHBNODE

6
Related Work

Reducing NFEs in training NODEs.

Several techniques have been developed to reduce the NFEs for the forward solvers in NODEs, including weight decay
(
Grathwohl et al. 2019
)
, input augmentation
(
Dupont et al. 2019
)
, regularizing solvers and learning dynamics

(
Finlay et al. 2020
;
Kelly et al. 2020
;
Ghosh et al. 2020
;
Pal et al. 2021
)
,
high-order ODE
(
Norcliffe et al. 2020
)
, data control
(
Massaroli et al. 2020
)
, and depth-variance
(
Massaroli et al. 2020
)
. HBNODEs can
reduce
both forward and backward NFEs at the same time.

Second-order ODE accelerated dynamics.

It has been noticed in both optimization and sampling communities that second-order ODEs with an appropriate damping term, e.g., the classical momentum and Nesterov’s acceleration in discrete regime, can significantly accelerate the first-order gradient dynamics (gradient descent), e.g.,
(
Polyak 1964
;
Nesterov 1983
;
Chen et al. 2014
;
Su et al. 2014
;
Wilson et al. 2018
)
. Also, these second-order ODEs have
been discretized via some interesting numerical schemes to design fast optimization schemes, e.g.,
Shi et al. 2019
.

Learning long-term dependencies.

Learning long-term dependency is one of the most important goals for learning from sequential data. Most of the existing works
focus on
mitigating exploding or vanishing gradient issues in training RNNs, e.g.,
(
Arjovsky et al. 2016
;
Wisdom et al. 2016
;
Jing et al. 2017
;
Vorontsov et al. 2017
;
Mhammedi et al. 2017
;
Helfrich et al. 2018
;
Tan M. Nguyen and Richard G. Baraniuk and Andrea L. Bertozzi and
Stanley J. Osher and Bao Wang 2020
)
. Attention-based models are proposed for learning on sequential data concurrently with the effective accommodation of learning long-term dependency
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Devlin et al. 2018
)
. Recently, NODEs have been integrated with long-short term memory model
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)
to learn long-term dependency for irregularly-sampled time series
(
Lechner and Hasani 2020
)
. HBNODEs directly enhance learning long-term dependency from sequential data.

Momentum in neural network design.

As a line of orthogonal work, the momentum has also been studied in designing neural network architecture, e.g.,
(
Moreau and Bruna 2017
;
Tan M. Nguyen and Richard G. Baraniuk and Andrea L. Bertozzi and
Stanley J. Osher and Bao Wang 2020
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Sander et al. 2021
)
,
which can also help accelerate training and learn long-term dependencies. These techniques can be considered as changing the neural network
f
in (
1
).
We leave the synergistic integration of adding momentum to
f
with our work on changing the
left-hand side
of (
1
) as a future work.

ResNet-style models.

Interpreting ResNet as an ODE model has been an interesting
research direction which has lead to interesting neural network architectures and analysis from the numerical ODE solvers and differential equation theory viewpoints, e.g.,
Lu et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020b
.

7
Concluding Remarks

We proposed HBNODEs to reduce the NFEs in solving both forward and backward ODEs,
which also improve generalization performance over the existing benchmark models. Moreover, HBNODEs alleviate vanishing gradients in training NODEs, making HBNODEs able to learn long-term dependency effectively from
sequential data. In the optimization community, Nesterov acceleration
(
Nesterov 1983
)
is also a famous algorithm for accelerating gradient descent, that achieves an optimal convergence rate for general convex optimization problems. The ODE counterpart of the Nesterov’s acceleration corresponds to (
9
) with
\gamma
being replaced by a time-dependent damping parameter, e.g.,
t/3

(
Su et al. 2014
)
or with restart
Wang et al. 2020a
. The adjoint equation of the Nesterov’s ODE
(
Su et al. 2014
)
is no longer a Nesterov’s ODE. We notice that directly using Nesterov’s ODE cannot improve the performance of the vanilla neural ODE. How to integrate Nesterov’s ODE with neural ODE is an interesting future direction. Another interesting direction is connecting HBNODE with symplectic ODE-net
(
Zhong et al. 2020
)
through an appropriate change of variables.

Appendix A
Review of the Adjoint Equation for the First- and Second-order ODEs

The adjoint sensitivity method is the key to assuring constant memory usage in training neural ODEs
[
Chen et al. 2018
]
. In this section, we present two different proofs for
the first-order adjoint sensitivity equations. The differentiation proof in Appendix
A.1.1
is adapted from the proof by
Norcliffe et al. 2020
. We provide a new integral proof in Appendix
A.1.2
to extend theoretical support for the Lipschitz continuous functions. We also revisit the proof of the second-order adjoint sensitivity equations by
Norcliffe et al. 2020
.

A.1
First-order Adjoint Sensitivity Equation

A Neural ODE
for hidden features
{\bm{h}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
takes the form

\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial t}=f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta),\quad{\bm{h}}(t_{0})={\bm{h}}_{t_{0}},\quad{\bm{h}}(T)={\bm{h}}_{T},

(23)

where
f({\bm{h}}(t),t,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is a neural network with learnable parameters
\theta
.
The corresponding adjoint equation, with
\mathcal{L}
being
a scalar loss function,
is defined by the following ODE,

\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}(t)}{\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}},\quad{\bm{A}}(T)=-{\bm{I}},\quad{\bm{a}}(t)=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\bm{A}}(t).

(24)

For gradient-based optimization, we need to compute the following derivatives

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\theta}=\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d\theta},\quad\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}=\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}.

(25)

In the following sections, we show that

\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d\theta}=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}dt,\quad\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}=-{\bm{A}}(t_{0}).

(26)

By linearity, we immediately arrive at the following adjoint sensitivity equations

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\theta}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{a}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}dt,\quad\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}={\bm{a}}(t_{0}).

(27)

A.1.1
Proof of the First-Order Adjoint Sensitivity Equation: Differentiation Approach

We adapt the proof of the adjoint sensitivity equations from
Norcliffe et al. 2020
. Assume that
f\in C^{1}
,
\phi
is either
\theta
or
{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}
,
then the following equations hold

\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}(t)}{\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi\partial t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}}{d\phi},\quad\frac{\partial\big({\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\big)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}}{\partial t}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}+{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi\partial t}.

(28)

Combining the three equations in (
28
)
yields the
differential equation

\frac{\partial\big({\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\big)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}}{\partial t}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}+{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}+{\bm{A}}\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}}{d\phi}\Big)={\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}.

(29)

Integrating
both sides of (
29
) in
t
from
T
to
t_{0}
, we arrive at the
integral equation

\Big({\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\Big)\Big|_{T}^{t_{0}}=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}dt.

(30)

Using the conditions
{\bm{A}}(T)=-{\bm{I}}
,
{\bm{h}}(t_{0})={\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}
,
{\bm{h}}(T)={\bm{h}}_{T}
, we
rewrite the equation (
30
)
as

\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d\phi}=-{\bm{A}}(t_{0})\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}{d\phi}+\int_{T}^{t_{0}}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}dt.

(31)

Substituting
\phi={\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}
and
\phi=\theta
respectively in (
31
) leads to

\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}=-{\bm{A}}(t_{0}),\quad\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d\theta}=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}dt.

(32)

This proof is adapted from the proof provided by
Norcliffe et al. 2020
for general second-order neural ODEs by differentiation and this proof only holds for
f\in C^{1}
.

A.1.2
Proof of the First-Order Adjoint Sensitivity Equations: Integration Approach

The proof in Appendix
A.1.1
requires that
f\in C^{1}
. However,
with activation functions like ReLU,
f
may not be smooth enough to satisfy this requirement.
Meanwhile, the adjoint equation (
24
)
that
{\bm{A}}
satisfies may not have a continuous right hand side, which can fail the Picard-Lindelöf theorem that
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the adjoint equation.

To circumvent these deficiencies, we propose a new proof based on integration.
Assume that
f({\bm{h}},t,\theta)
is continuous in
t
and
Lipschitz continuous in
{\bm{h}},\theta
, and there exists
some open ball around
{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}={\bm{s}}_{0}
,
\theta=\theta_{0}
such that for every pair of initial condition and parameters in the open ball, there exists a unique solution for
t\in[t_{0},T]
. We denote the solution starting from
{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}={\bm{s}}_{0}
,
\theta=\theta_{0}
as
{\bm{h}}_{0}
. In order to avoid difficulties in proving the existence and uniqueness of the solution, we explicitly define the adjoint equation through the following matrix exponential

{\bm{A}}(t)=-\exp\Bigg\{-\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}_{0}(\tau),\tau,\theta_{0})d\tau\Bigg\}.

(33)

By definition,
{\bm{A}}
is Lipschitz continuous and satisfies the
differential equation almost everywhere

\frac{d{\bm{A}}(t)}{dt}=-{\bm{A}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}_{0}(t),t,\theta_{0}).

(34)

Since
{\bm{h}}\in C^{1}(t)
and
\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}\in L^{1}(t)
, we obtain the following using integration by parts,

{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}|_{t_{0}}^{T}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial t}dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}{\bm{h}}dt.

(35)

Taking partial derivatives with respect to
\phi
on both sides of (
35
), as
{\bm{A}}(t)
is only a function of
t
, we have

{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\Big|_{t_{0}}^{T}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial t}dt+\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}{\bm{h}}dt.

(36)

In order to exchange integral and derivatives, we use the dominated convergence theorem. Because
f
is Lipschitz continuous on
{\bm{h}}
,
{\bm{h}}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\phi
, and thus
\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}
is Lebesgue integrable. Therefore, by chain rule, the following equation holds almost everywhere,

\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial t\partial\phi}=\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi\partial t}=\frac{df}{d\phi}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}}{d\phi}.

(37)

Because
t
is bounded, the right hand side of equation (
37
) is Lebesgue integrable, and so is the left hand side. Because both
\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial t\partial\phi}
are Lebesgue integrable, by dominated convergence theorem, we have the following exchange of integrals and derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial t}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial t\partial\phi}dt,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}{\bm{h}}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}dt.

(38)

Combining equation (
36
) with (
38
) gives us

{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\Big|_{t_{0}}^{T}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial t\partial\phi}dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}dt.

(39)

By taking Lebesgue integral of equation (
37
), we have the equation

\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{h}}}{\partial t\partial\phi}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}}{d\phi}\Big)dt.

(40)

Meanwhile, at
{\bm{h}}_{0}
, we can integrate equation (
34
) to a similar form as

\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{d{\bm{A}}}{dt}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}dt=-\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}\frac{d{\bm{h}}}{d\phi}dt.

(41)

Consequently, at
{\bm{h}}_{0}
, we can sum up equations (
39
), (
40
), and (
41
) and arrive at

{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial\phi}\Big|_{t_{0}}^{T}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{A}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}dt,

(42)

which is the same integral equation
as equation (
30
) in the differentiation proof in Appendix
A.1.1
. Thus, plugging in the initial conditions provides us with the same result.

A.1.3
Corollary of the First-order Gradient Propagation

An immediate corollary of the above proof is that combining equations (
26
) and (
33
) results in

\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}=-{\bm{A}}(t_{0})=\exp\Bigg\{-\int_{T}^{t_{0}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}_{0}(\tau),\tau,\theta_{0})d\tau\Bigg\}.

(43)

As (
43
) is true for every choice of
t_{0}
, we can also generalize it to

\frac{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t}}=\exp\Bigg\{-\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}_{0}(\tau),\tau,\theta_{0})d\tau\Bigg\},

(44)

which shows the relative gradient between different
times
in integral.

A.2
Second-order Adjoint Sensitivity Equation

A SONODE satisfies the following equations

\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}}{\partial t}={\bm{v}},\quad\frac{\partial{\bm{v}}}{\partial t}=f({\bm{h}}(t),{\bm{v}}(t),t,\theta),\quad{\bm{h}}(t_{0})={\bm{h}}_{t_{0}},\quad{\bm{v}}(t_{0})={\bm{v}}_{t_{0}},

(45)

which can be viewed as a coupled first-order ODE system of the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}\\
{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{v}}\\
f({\bm{h}}(t),{\bm{v}}(t),t,\theta)\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}\\
{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix}(t_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}\\
{\bm{v}}_{t_{0}}\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

Denote
{\bm{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}\\
{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix}
and final state as

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}(T)\\
{\bm{v}}(T)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}_{T}\\
{\bm{v}}_{T}\end{bmatrix}={\bm{z}}_{T}.

(47)

Using the conclusions from Appendix
A.1
, then the adjoint equation
is given by

\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}(t)}{\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}(t)\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}&{\bm{I}}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}&\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{v}}}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{A}}(T)=-{\bm{I}},\quad{\bm{a}}(t)=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\bm{A}}(t).

(48)

By rewriting
{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{h}}&{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}\end{bmatrix}
, we have the following differential equations

\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}_{\bm{h}}(t)}{\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}},\quad\frac{\partial{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)}{\partial t}=-{\bm{A}}_{\bm{h}}(t)-{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{v}}},

(49)

with initial conditions

{\bm{A}}_{\bm{h}}(T)=-\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}\\
{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(T)=-\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}\\
{\bm{I}}\end{bmatrix},

(50)

and adjoint states

{\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}(t)=\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{h}}(t),\quad{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(t)=\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{z}}_{T}}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t).

(51)

The gradient equations becomes

\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d\theta}=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{a}}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\end{bmatrix}dt=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}dt,\quad\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}}={\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}(t_{0}),\quad\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{v}}_{t_{0}}}={\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(t_{0}).

(52)

In SONODE,
{\bm{h}}_{t_{0}}
is fixed, and thus
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{h}}
disappears in gradient computation. Therefore, we are only interested in
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}
.
Thus the adjoint
{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}
satisfies the
following
second-order ODE

\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}={\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}-\frac{\partial({\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{v}}})}{\partial t},

(53)

and thus

\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}={\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}-\frac{\partial({\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{v}}})}{\partial t},

(54)

with initial conditions

{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(T)=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{z}}}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{v}}(T)=\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{v}}_{T}},\quad\frac{\partial{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(T)}{\partial t}=-\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{d{\bm{h}}_{T}}-{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}(T)\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{v}}}(T).

(55)

This proves the second order adjoint equations for
{\bm{a}}_{\bm{v}}
.

Appendix C
Vanishing and Exploding Gradients in Training RNNs

Recurrent cells are the building blocks of RNNs. A recurrent cell can be mathematically written as

{\bm{h}}_{t}=\sigma({\bm{U}}{\bm{h}}_{t-1}+{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{t}+{\bm{b}}),\ {\bm{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\ \mbox{for}\ t=1,2,\cdots,T,

(69)

where
{\bm{h}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h}
is the hidden state,
{\bm{U}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h\times h},{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h\times d}
, and
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h}
are trainable parameters;
\sigma(\cdot)
is a nonlinear activation function, e.g., sigmoid. Backpropagation through time is a popular algorithm for training RNNs, which usually results in exploding or vanishing gradients
[
Bengio et al. 1994
]
. Thus RNNs may fail to learn
long term dependencies. As an illustration, let
{\bm{h}}_{T}
and
{\bm{h}}_{t}
be the state vectors at the timestamps
T
and
t
(
T\gg t
), respectively. Assume
\mathcal{L}
is the loss to minimize, then

{\small\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}\cdot\prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{k+1}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{k}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}\cdot\prod_{k=t}^{T-1}({\bm{D}}_{k}{\bm{U}}^{\top}),}

(70)

where
{\bm{D}}_{k}={\rm diag}(\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{U}}{\bm{h}}_{k}+{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{k+1}))
is a diagonal matrix with
\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{U}}{\bm{h}}_{k}+{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{k+1})
being its diagonal entries.

\|\prod_{k=t}^{T-1}({\bm{D}}_{k}{\bm{U}}^{\top})\|_{2}
tends to either vanish or explode
Bengio et al. 1994
.

When applying RNNs to sequence applications with

{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{T})

be
an input sequence of length
T
and
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{T})
be the sequence of labels,
we let
\mathcal{L}_{t}
be the loss at the timestamp
t
and the total loss on the whole sequence be

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t},

(71)

the vanishing or exploding issue can be shown following
(
70
).

For neural ODEs, note that the adjoint state
{\bm{a}}(t)
is defined as
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial{\bm{h}}(t)
, which also tends to explode or vanish during training.

C.1
Derivation of equation (
18
)

GHBNODE can be viewed as a system of higher dimensional NODE as in equation (
64
). With
{\bm{z}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{h}}\\
{\bm{m}}\end{bmatrix}
,
{\bm{z}}
satisfies NODE equation, and therefore it also satisfies the equation for relative gradient information as in (

\frac{d{\bm{z}}_{T}}{d{\bm{z}}_{t}}=\exp\Bigg\{-\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{z}}}({\bm{z}}_{0}(\tau),\tau,\theta_{0})d\tau\Bigg\}.

(72)

By definition of multivariate derivatives, we have

\frac{d{\bm{z}}_{T}}{d{\bm{z}}_{t}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{m}}_{t}}\\
\frac{\partial{\bm{m}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{m}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{m}}_{t}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(73)

and

\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{z}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf 0}&\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial{\bm{m}}}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}-\xi{\bm{I}}&-\gamma{\bm{I}}\end{bmatrix}.

(74)

With equations (
73
) and (
74
), we can rewrite equation (
72
) in terms of
{\bm{h}}
and
{\bm{m}}
as

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{h}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{m}}_{t}}\\
\frac{\partial{\bm{m}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{h}}_{t}}&\frac{\partial{\bm{m}}_{T}}{\partial{\bm{m}}_{t}}\\

Proof of Proposition
4

Proof.

Let
{{\bm{F}}}=\frac{1}{t-T}\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial f}{\partial{\bm{h}}}({\bm{h}}(s),s,\theta)ds-\xi{\bm{I}}
,
{{\bm{J}}}=\frac{1}{t-T}\int_{T}^{t}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial{\bm{m}}}({\bm{m}}(s))ds
, and
{{\bm{H}}}=\frac{1}{t-T}{\bm{M}}
, then we have the following equation

{{\bm{H}}}=\frac{1}{t-T}{\bm{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&{{\bm{J}}}\\
{{\bm{F}}}&-\gamma{\bm{I}}\end{bmatrix}.

(77)

As
(\lambda+\gamma){\bm{I}}
commutes with any matrix
{{\bm{F}}}
, the characteristics polynomials of
{{\bm{H}}}
and
{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}}
satisfy the
relation

ch_{{\bm{H}}}(\lambda)=\det(\lambda{\bm{I}}-{{\bm{H}}})=\det\begin{bmatrix}\lambda{\bm{I}}&-{{\bm{J}}}\\
-{{\bm{F}}}&(\lambda+\gamma){\bm{I}}\end{bmatrix}=det(\lambda(\lambda+\gamma){\bm{I}}-{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}})=-ch_{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}}(\lambda(\lambda+\gamma)).

(78)

Since the characteristics polynomial of
{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}}
splits in the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers, i.e.
ch_{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\lambda_{{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}},i})
, we have

ch_{{\bm{H}}}(\lambda)=-ch_{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}}(\lambda(\lambda+\gamma))=-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda(\lambda+\gamma)-\lambda_{{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}},i}).

(79)

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
{{\bm{H}}}
appear in
n
pairs with each pair satisfying
the
quadratic equation

\lambda(\lambda+\gamma)-\lambda_{{{\bm{J}}{\bm{F}}},i}=0.

(80)

By Vieta’s formulas,
the sum of these pairs are all
-\gamma
. Therefore, the eigenvalues of
{\bm{M}}
comes in
n
pairs and the sum of each pair is

-(t-T)\gamma
.
