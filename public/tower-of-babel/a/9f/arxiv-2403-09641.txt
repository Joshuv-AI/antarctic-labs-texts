Reformulating ϵ \epsilon - δ \delta Limits in a Pedagogically Cleaner Way
Joel Q. L. Chang

Abstract
. We provide a simple reformulation of the ϵ \epsilon - δ \delta limit definition introduced in undergraduate calculus courses that enhances its pedagogical value for conceptual understanding and computational skill.



1.
Introduction

During the advent of calculus, the renowned scientists Newton and Leibniz independently developed differential and integral calculus using infinitesimals
[
2
]
.
These notions of infinitesimals were logically questionable until the 20th century, when mathematicians such as Cauchy and Weierstrass
[
2
]
formulated the
\epsilon
-
\delta
limit definition as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a subset. We say that a function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
has a
limit

L
at
c
, denoted
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L
, precisely when

\forall\epsilon>0\quad\exists\delta>0:\quad 0<|x-c|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|f(x)-L|<\epsilon.

Many introductory calculus courses include this classical
\epsilon
-
\delta
limit definition as an introduction to undergraduate real analysis or as a means to rigorously discuss the concept of limits that are used to define continuity and differentiability
[
1
]
.

However, the
\epsilon
-
\delta
limit definition has its fair share of critics citing poor pedagogical value. Furthermore, the notion of “reverse-engineering” the choice of
\delta
given the goal of upper-bounding the output error
|f(x)-L|
seems counterintuitive to many undergraduates.

In this paper, we use the affine transformation
x=c+t
to reformulate the
\epsilon
-
\delta
definition of the limit, and demonstrate its pedagogical value for proof-writing students. To our knowledge, we are not aware of any other source that adopts this reformulation, due to the pervasive adoption of Definition
1
.

2.
The Reformulation

We first state the definition of the special case
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0
:

Definition 2
.

Let
f
be a real-valued function. Then

\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall\epsilon>0\quad\exists\delta>0:\quad 0<|t|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|f(t)|<\epsilon.

We call
\delta
the
input threshold
and
\epsilon
the
output threshold
. Furthermore,

\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0}|f(t)|=0

since
||x||=|x|
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The latter form is useful when we want to compute limits in terms of upper-bounding. We illustrate some basic examples in our
YouTube video
.

We can then formulate the usual definition of the limit in terms of this special case.

Theorem 1
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function. Then

\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0}(f(c+t)-L)=0.

Alternately phrased using the fizzle function vocabulary,

\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mbox{$f(c+\cdot)-L$ is a fizzle function}.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The substitution
x=c+t
ensures

0<|x-c|<\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<|t|<\delta

and

|f(x)-L|<\epsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|f(c+t)-L|<\epsilon.

∎

In the special case
c=0
, there is no difference between the two definitions apart from a relabeling
x\to t
. Furthermore, any limit proved using Definition
1
can in principle be proved via the equivalent formulation in Theorem
1
via the transformation
x=c+t
.

We therefore emphasise that the main advantage of this reformulation does not arise in mathematical novelty, but in pedagogical insight for students. This allows the heavy lifting of the theoretical
\epsilon
-
\delta
proofs to be carried out on the special case (Definition
2
), and derivation of the general limit theorems in terms of fizzle functions (see Section
4
).

Analytically, the transformation to
0<|t|<\delta
emphasises to the student that to prove
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0
, their two-fold goal for any
\epsilon>0
is to

()

upper-bound the input error
|t|
by their judiciously chosen input threshold
\delta
, so that they can

()

upper-bound the output error
|f(t)-L|
by the given output threshold
\epsilon
.

And finally, to prove general statements of the form
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L
, they simply need to prove

\lim_{t\to 0}(f(c+t)-L)=0

by Theorem
1
, which is familiar to them.
By letting the right-hand side of the limit to equal
0
, we have the added advantage of emphasising the goal of upper-bounding the error. Algebraically, this converts a problem involving factorisation into a problem involving expansion, which is easier though at the cost of tediousness. Furthermore, this notion is used to define asymptotes, which generalises the notion of

\lim_{x\to c}f(c)=L

when the right-hand side
L
is not a constant.

3.
Common Pedogogical Examples

To illustrate the point, we highlight several common examples of limits that students are taught to prove and demonstrate the veracity of the new formulation of the
\epsilon
-
\delta
approach.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}
be a nonzero polynomial. Then for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=p(c)
.

Remark 1
.

Most exercises will provide
p(x)
explicitly, for example,
p(x)=(x^{2}+3x)
. We prove this result in generality in Section
9
, and demonstrate the two ways of concretely obtaining
\delta
.

Example 1
.

Prove
\lim_{x\to 1}(x^{2}+3x)=4
using the
\epsilon
-
\delta
definition.

Solution to Example
1
.

By Theorem
1
, we equivalently prove that

\lim_{t\to 0}({({(1+t)}^{2}+3(1+t))}-4)=0.

By algebra,

\displaystyle({(1+t)}^{2}+3(1+t)-4)

\displaystyle=\cdots=t^{2}+5t.

Hence it suffices to prove that

\lim_{t\to 0}(t^{2}+5t)=0.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. We illustrate both choices of
\delta
.

()

Choose
\delta=\min\{\left({\epsilon/2}\right)^{1/2},{\left({\epsilon/10}\right)}^{1/2}\}
. Then for the input error
0<|t|<\delta
, we can bound the output error by

\displaystyle|t^{2}+5t|

\displaystyle\leqslant{|t|}^{2}+5|t|

\displaystyle<\delta^{2}+5\delta

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

()

Choose
\delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/6\}
. Then

\displaystyle|t^{2}+5t|

\displaystyle<\delta^{2}+5\delta

\displaystyle=\delta(\delta+5)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta\cdot(1+5)

\displaystyle=6\delta\leqslant 6\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{6}=\epsilon.

∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
p(x),q(x)
be nonzero polynomials. Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
q(c)\neq 0
. Then

\lim_{x\to c}{\frac{{{p(x)}}}{{{q(x)}}}}={\frac{{{p(c)}}}{{{q(c)}}}}.

Remark 2
.

Most exercises will provide
p(x),q(x)
explicitly, for example,
p(x)=x+1,q(x)=(x-1)(x^{2}+1)
. We prove this result in generality in Section
9
, and demonstrate the two ways of concretely how to obtain
\delta
.

Example 2
.

Prove
{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}{\frac{{{x+1}}}{{{(x-1)(x^{2}+1)}}}}}=\frac{3}{5}
using the
\epsilon
-
\delta
definition.

Solution to Example
2
.

By Theorem
1
, we equivalently prove that

\lim_{t\to 0}\left({{\frac{{{(2+t)+1}}}{{{((2+t)-1)({(2+t)}^{2}+1)}}}}-\frac{2}{5}}\right)=0.

By algebra,

\displaystyle{\frac{{{(2+t)+1}}}{{{((2+t)-1)({(2+t)}^{2}+1)}}}}-\frac{3}{5}

\displaystyle=\cdots={\frac{{{-22t-15t^{2}-3t^{3}}}}{{{5(1+t)({(2+t)}^{2}+1)}}}}.

Taking absolute signs,

\displaystyle\left|{{\frac{{{(2+t)+1}}}{{{((2+t)-1)({(2+t)}^{2}+1)}}}}-\frac{3}{5}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{|1+t|}\cdot\frac{1}{{(2+t)}^{2}+1}\cdot(22+15|t|+3{|t|}^{2})\cdot|t|

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Choose
\delta=\min\{1/2,\epsilon/4\}
.
Then for
0<|t|<\delta
, the reverse triangle inequality yields

|1+t|\geqslant 1-|t|>1-\delta\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\frac{{{1}}}{{{|1+t|}}}}<\frac{1}{2}.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

{(2+t)}^{2}+1\geqslant 0+1=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\frac{{{1}}}{{{{(2+t)}^{2}+1}}}}\leqslant 1.

Furthermore,

|t|<\delta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}<1\quad\Rightarrow\quad(22+15|t|+3{|t|}^{2})<22+15\cdot 1+3\cdot 1^{2}=40.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|{{\frac{{{(2+t)+1}}}{{{((2+t)-1)({(2+t)}^{2}+1)}}}}-\frac{3}{5}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{|1+t|}\cdot\frac{1}{{(2+t)}^{2}+1}\cdot(22+15|t|+3{|t|}^{2})\cdot|t|

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1\cdot 40\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{4}=\epsilon.

∎

4.
Implementing the Limit Theorems

This centre-at-zero approach can simplify many convoluted proofs, while maintaining the essence of why they work. In fact, this is arguably the last section (apart from the supplementary Section
9
) where we employ the
\epsilon
-
\delta
definition of a limit, since the other limit theorems are essentially algebraic derivations of these results.

Theorem 4
.

\lim_{t\to 0}t=0
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Choose
\delta=\epsilon
. Then

0<|t|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|t|<\delta\leqslant\epsilon.

∎

Consider the limit of a sum of functions.

Theorem 5
.

Let
f,g
be real-valued functions. If
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=\lim_{t\to 0}g(t)=0
, then

\lim_{t\to 0}(f+g)(t)=0.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We want to show that

\lim_{t\to 0}(f+g)(t)=0.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|(f+g)(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|f(t)|+|g(t)|.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.
Our goal is simply to upper bound both summands by
<\epsilon/2
each. For the real number
\epsilon/2>0
, since
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0
, there exists some
\delta_{1}>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad|f(t)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

For the real number
\epsilon/2>0
, since
\lim_{t\to 0}g(c+t)=0
, there exists some
\delta_{2}>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad|g(t)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Choose
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
. Then for
0<|t|<\delta
,

\displaystyle|(f+g)(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|f(t)|+|g(t)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

∎

We can similar extend this notion to show that taking limits is linear, and even allow students to prove it as a benign exercise.

Theorem 6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{R}
and
f
be a real-valued function. If
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0
, then

\lim_{t\to 0}(kf)(t)=0.

We can even show that
\lim_{t\to 0}(\cdot)
is multiplicative.

Theorem 7
.

Let
f,g
be real-valued functions. If
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=\lim_{t\to 0}g(t)=0
, then

\lim_{t\to 0}(f\cdot g)(t)=0.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

We want to show that

\lim_{t\to 0}(f\cdot g)(t)=0.

By algebra,

\displaystyle|(f\cdot g)(t)|

\displaystyle=|f(t)\cdot g(t)|=|f(t)|\cdot|g(t)|.

Since

\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0,\quad\lim_{t\to 0}g(t)=0,

there exist
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle 0<|t|<\delta_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad|f(t)|<\sqrt{\epsilon},

\displaystyle 0<|t|<\delta_{2}\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad|g(t)|<\sqrt{\epsilon}.

Choose
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
. Then

\displaystyle|(f\cdot g)(t)|

\displaystyle=|f(t)||g(t)|\leqslant\sqrt{\epsilon}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon}=\epsilon.

∎

By Theorem
1
, we can rewrite these theorems to centre around the point
c\in\mathbb{R}
rather than just
0
.
In particular, the proof for the continuity of
\sin(\cdot)
at
0
becomes strikingly obvious.

Theorem 8
.

\lim_{t\to 0}\sin(t)=0
.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

By a geometric argument, for
0<|t|<\frac{1}{2}\pi
,

|{\sin(t)}|<|t|.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.
Choose
\delta=\min\{\frac{1}{2}\pi,\epsilon\}
. Then for
0<|t|<\epsilon
,

|{\sin(t)}|<\epsilon.

∎

For limits of quotients, we need to be careful, since we cannot allow for zero-denominator errors. Thus, we will first consider the denominator having limit
1
, before generalising using our previous rules.

Theorem 9
.

Let
f
be a real-valued function. Suppose
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=1
. Then
\lim_{t\to 0}{\frac{{{1}}}{{{f(t)}}}}=1
.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

By algebra,

\left|{\frac{1}{f(t)}-1}\right|=\frac{|f(t)-1|}{|f(t)|}.

Since
\lim_{t\to 0}(f(t)-1)=0
, for the real number
1/2>0
, there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that for
0<|t|<\delta_{1}
,

\displaystyle|f(t)-1|<1/2\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad-1/2<f(t)-1<1/2

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad f(t)>1-1/2=1/2

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad{\frac{{{1}}}{{{|f(t)|}}}}=\frac{1}{f(t)}<\frac{1}{2}.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\lim_{t\to 0}(f(t)-1)=0
, for the real number
2\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that for
0<|t|<\delta_{1}
,

|f(t)-1|<2\epsilon.

Choose
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{1}{f(t)}-1}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|f(t)|}\cdot|f(t)-1|<\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\epsilon=\epsilon.

∎

The squeeze theorems also receives a more elegant proof.

Theorem 10
.

Let
f,g,h
be real-valued functions. Suppose
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=\lim_{t\to 0}h(t)=0
and
f(t)\leqslant g(t)\leqslant h(t)
for
t
near
0
. Then

\lim_{t\to 0}g(t)=0.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

Since
f(t)\leqslant g(t)\leqslant h(t)
for
t
near
0
, there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(t)\leqslant g(t)\leqslant h(t).

Fix
\epsilon>0
. We want to find
\delta>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|g(x)|<\epsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad-\epsilon<g(x)<\epsilon.

Since
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=L
, for the real number
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{2}>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta_{1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad|f(t)|<\epsilon/2\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(t)>-\epsilon/2.

Since
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=L
, for the real number
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{3}>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad|h(t)|<\epsilon/2\quad\Rightarrow\quad h(t)<\epsilon/2.

Choose
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3}\}
. Then

\displaystyle 0<|t|<\delta\quad

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad-\epsilon<f(t)\leqslant g(t)\leqslant h(t)<\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad-\epsilon<g(t)<\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad|g(t)|<\epsilon,

as required.
∎

5.
Generalising the Limit Theorems

We can now generalise these results for nonzero limits. In fact, since the
\epsilon
-
\delta
heavy lifting has been done in Section
4
, by assuming the results therein, we reduce their proofs to relatively benign high-school computational exercises.

Corollary 1
.

For any
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lim_{x\to c}x=c
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
, we equivalently want to show that

\lim_{t\to 0}((c+t)-c)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0}t=0,

which holds by Theorem
4
.
∎

We can furthermore generalise the limit theorems for nonzero limit points
c
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
f,g,h
be real valued functions,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose

\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{x\to c}h(x)=1.

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f+g)(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(kf)(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f\cdot g)(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}{\frac{{{1}}}{{{h(x)}}}}=1.

Furthermore, let
p
be a real-valued function such that
f(x)\leqslant p(x)\leqslant g(x)
for
x
near
c
. Then

\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=0.

Proof of Corollary
2
.

By Theorem
1
, the hypotheses are equivalent to

\lim_{t\to 0}f(c+t)=\lim_{t\to 0}g(c+t)=0,\quad\lim_{t\to 0}h(c+t)=1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f+g)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}(f+g)(c+t)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(kf)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}(kf)(c+t)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f\cdot g)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}(f\cdot g)(c+t)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}{\frac{{{1}}}{{{h(x)}}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}{\frac{{{1}}}{{{h(c+t)}}}}=1.

For the last result (i.e. the analog of Theorem
10
),
x=c+t
being near
c
is equivalent to
t
being near
0
, and
f(x)\leqslant p(x)\leqslant g(x)
is translated to

f(c+t)\leqslant p(c+t)\leqslant g(c+t).

By Theorem
10
,
\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=\lim_{t\to 0}p(c+t)=0
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
f,g
be real-valued functions,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L
and
\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=M
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f+g)(x)

\displaystyle=L+M,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(kf)(x)

\displaystyle=kL,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f-g)(x)

\displaystyle=L-M,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f\cdot g)(x)

\displaystyle=LM.

Furthermore, let
p
be a real-valued function such that
f(x)\leqslant p(x)\leqslant g(x)
for
x
near
c
. Suppose
M=L
. Then

\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=L.

Proof.

By Definition
1
, we are equivalently given that

\lim_{x\to c}(f(x)-L)=\lim_{x\to c}(g(x)-M)=0.

By Corollary
2
,

\lim_{x\to c}((f+g)(x)-(L+M))=\lim_{x\to c}((f(x)-L)+(g(x)-M))=0,

which is equivalent to

\lim_{x\to c}(f+g)(x)=L+M.

By Corollary
2
,

\lim_{x\to c}((kf)(x)-kL)=\lim_{x\to c}(k(f(x)-L))=0,

which is equivalent to

\lim_{x\to c}(kf)(x)=kL.

Writing
f-g=f+(-1)g
,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f-g)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}(f+(-1)g)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)+\lim_{x\to c}(-1)g(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)+(-1)\lim_{x\to c}g(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)-\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=L-M.

By Corollary
2
,

0=\lim_{x\to c}((f(x)-L)(g(x)-M))=\lim_{x\to c}(f(x)g(x)-Mf(x)-Lg(x)+LM).

By the linearity of
\lim_{x\to c}(\cdot)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}(f(x)g(x))

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}((f(x)g(x)-Mf(x)-Lg(x)+LM)+(Mf(x)+Lg(x)-LM))

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to c}(f(x)g(x)-Mf(x)-Lg(x)+LM)+M\cdot\lim_{x\to c}f(x)+L\cdot\lim_{x\to c}g(x)-LM

\displaystyle=0+ML+LM-LM=ML.

Finally, by Corollary
2
,
f(x)-L\leqslant p(x)-L\leqslant g(x)-L
for
x
near
c
allows for

\lim_{x\to c}(p(x)-L)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=L.

∎

Remark 3
.

The conventional
\epsilon
-
\delta
proofs require the obscure step of creating variables and having to reverse-engineer the choice of
\delta
. While this has value in and of itself, it may be an unnecessarily complicated and arguably extraneous barrier-to-entry to clearly communicating the
\epsilon
-
\delta
approach to proving limit statements. In this formulation, the mental gymnastics are essentially “covered” or “hidden” by the limit theorems of
\lim_{t\to 0}(\cdot)
and do not appear as prominently in the proofs.

Corollary 3
.

By Theorems
4
,
5
, and
7
, for any polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}
,
\lim_{x\to c}p(x)=p(c)
.

Furthermore, the proof for the continuity of
\sin(\cdot)
reduces to merely a matter of trigonometric manipulation.

Corollary 4
.

For
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lim_{x\to c}\sin(x)=\sin(c)
.

Proof of Corollary
4
.

For general
c\in\mathbb{R}
, trigonometric formulae yield

\displaystyle\sin(c+t)-\sin(c)

\displaystyle=\sin(c)\cos(t)+\cos(c)\sin(t)-\sin(c)

\displaystyle=-\sin(c)(1-\cos(t))+\cos(c)\sin(t)

\displaystyle=-\sin(c)\cdot 2\sin^{2}(t/2)+\cos(c)\sin(t).

Applying
\lim_{t\to 0}(\cdot)
on both sides, and applying the linearity and multiplicativity of
\lim_{t\to 0}(\cdot)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}(\sin(c+t)-\sin(c))

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}(-{\sin(c)}\cdot 2\sin^{2}(t/2)+\cos(c)\sin(t))

\displaystyle=-2\sin(c)\cdot\lim_{t\to 0}\sin^{2}(t/2)+\cos(c)\cdot\lim_{t\to 0}\sin(t)

\displaystyle=-2\sin(c)\cdot 0^{2}+\cos(c)\cdot 0=0.

∎

Corollary 5
.

Let
f,g
be real-valued functions. Suppose
\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L
,
\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=M\neq 0
. Then
\lim_{x\to c}(f/g)(x)=L/M
.

Proof of Corollary
5
.

By Theorem
11
,

\lim_{x\to c}\frac{g(x)}{M}=\frac{1}{M}\cdot M=1.

By Corollary
2
,

\lim_{x\to c}\frac{M}{g(x)}=1.

By Theorem
11
again,

\lim_{x\to c}\frac{1}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{1}{M}\cdot\frac{M}{g(x)}=\frac{1}{M}\cdot 1=\frac{1}{M}.

By Theorem
11
,

\lim_{x\to c}(f/g)(x)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\cdot\frac{1}{g(x)}=L\cdot\frac{1}{M}=\frac{L}{M}.

∎

6.
Reformulating Infinity-Related Limits

The conventional definition of limits at infinity is as follows.

Definition 3
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a subset. For any function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall\epsilon>0\quad\exists M>0:\quad x>M\quad\Rightarrow\quad|f(x)-L|<\epsilon.

We remark its equivalent form and demonstrate the usefulness of the latter version.

Theorem 12
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a subset. Then

\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}(f(1/t)-L)=0.

Proof of Theorem
12
.

Using
x=1/t
, set
\delta=1/M>0
and the proof follows the equivalence of

x>M>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<t<\delta.

∎

This allows us to derive the various limit theorems for when
x\to\infty
in terms of the formulation in Theorem
1
, since it follows from the vanilla limit theorems at finite
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
We illustrate the usefulness of the alternate formulation below.

Example 3
.

Prove that
{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{\frac{{{2x+1}}}{{{3x-4}}}}}=\frac{2}{3}
.

Proof of Example
3
.

By Theorem
12
, we equivalently prove that
{\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\left({{\frac{{{2(1/t)+1}}}{{{3(1/t)-4}}}}-\frac{2}{3}}\right)}=0
.
By algebra,

\displaystyle\frac{2(1/t)+1}{3(1/t)-4}-\frac{2}{3}

\displaystyle=\cdots=\frac{7t}{3(3-4t)}.

Taking absolute signs,

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{2(1/t)+1}{3(1/t)-4}-\frac{2}{3}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{|3-4t|}\cdot|t|.

Choose
\delta=\min\{3/8,9\epsilon/14\}
. Then for
0<t<\delta
,

0<\frac{3}{2}=3-4\cdot\frac{3}{8}=3-4\delta<3-4t<3\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{|3-4t|}=\frac{1}{3-4t}<\frac{2}{3}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|{\frac{2(1/t)+1}{3(1/t)-4}-\frac{2}{3}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{|3-4t|}\cdot|t|

\displaystyle<\frac{7}{3}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{9}{14}\epsilon=\epsilon.

∎

7.
Further Applications

Since the
derivative

f^{\prime}
of a function
f
is essentially defined as a limit, the above-mentioned techniques apply, since

f^{\prime}(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0}\left({\frac{f(c+t)-f(c)-f^{\prime}(c)t}{t}}\right)=0.

In fact, one uses the equivalent formulation to define
Frechét
derivatives, in the setting of normed spaces.

Definition 4
.

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
. A function
\left|\left|{\cdot}\right|\right|
is a
norm
if it satisfies the four conditions:

(1)

Non-negativity
For any
v\in V
,
\left|\left|{v}\right|\right|\geqslant 0
.

(2)

Non-degeneracy
For any
v\in V
,
\left|\left|{v}\right|\right|=0\Leftrightarrow v=0
.

(3)

Homogeneity
For any
v\in V,c\in\mathbb{K}
,
\left|\left|{cv}\right|\right|=|c|\left|\left|{v}\right|\right|
.

(4)

Triangle inequality
For any
u,v\in V
,
\left|\left|{u+v}\right|\right|\leqslant\left|\left|{u}\right|\right|+\left|\left|{v}\right|\right|
.

For instance, the absolute value function
\left|\left|{\cdot}\right|\right|:=|\cdot|
is a norm on
X:=\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 5
.

Let
f:V\to W
be a map between normed spaces, denoting the norms in
V,W
by
\left|\left|{\cdot}\right|\right|_{V},\left|\left|{\cdot}\right|\right|_{W}
respectively. For any open set
U\subset V
, the
Frechét
derivative at
v\in U
is the unique map
f^{\prime}(v):V\to W
such that

\lim_{\left|\left|{u}\right|\right|_{V}\to 0}\frac{\left|\left|{f(v+u)-f(v)-(f^{\prime}(v))(u)}\right|\right|_{W}}{\left|\left|{u}\right|\right|_{V}}=0.

This limit notion also generalise nicely to topological vector spaces, which are itself generalisations of normed spaces.

Definition 6
.

A
topological vector space
is a vector space
V
over a topological field
\mathbb{K}
equipped with a topology
\mathcal{T}
with the property that the addition and scalar multiplication maps

+:V\times V\to V,(u,v)\mapsto u+v,\quad\cdot:\mathbb{K}\times V\to V,(c,v)\mapsto c\cdot v

respectively are continuous.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a map between topological vector spaces.
Since topological vector spaces are completely regular
[
3
]
, they are Hausdorff, and limits therein are unique. We can then define limits as follows.

Definition 7
.

Denote
\lim_{t\to 0}f(t)=0
if for any neighbourhood
V
of
0\in Y
, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
0\in X
such that

f(U)\subseteq V.

Finally, we generalise to the limit at
c\in X
using continuity of addition and scalar multiplication by implementing

\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to 0}(f(c+t)-L)=0

where we verify the right-hand side via the function
g=f(c+\cdot)-L
.

We perform a quick check that this definition agrees with the conventional limit definition.

Theorem 13
.

The following equivalence holds:

\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\text{$\forall_{V\ni L}\quad\exists_{U\ni c}:\quad f(U)\subseteq V$}.

Proof.

For
(\Rightarrow)
, fix a neighbourhood
V
of
L
. By translation invariance, the set
V-L:=\{v-L:v\in V\}
is a neighbourhood of
0
. By the definition of the left-hand side, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
0
such that

g(U)\subseteq V-L.

The set
U+c:=\{u+c:u\in U\}
is a neighbourhood of
c
. Then one checks by linear algebra that

f(U+c)=g(U)+L\in V,

as required.
For
(\Leftarrow)
, fix a neighbourhood
V
of
0\in Y
. The set
V+L
is a neighbourhood of
L
. By the right-hand side, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
c
such that

f(U)\subseteq V+L.

One can then check that
U-c
is a neighbourhood of
0\in X
, and that

g(U-c)=f(U)-L\subseteq V,

as required.
∎

8.
Conclusion

The simple modification of defining limits in terms of the origin for vector spaces massively simplifies computational complications for undergraduate students and novices in real analysis, who are acclimatising to the required rigour. Furthermore, the core notions of neighbourhoods are sufficiently general to account for abstract topological vector spaces, which inevitably simplifies further computations. We hope this resource will edify university students of any mastery of real analysis and topology in their mathematical journey.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Theorem
1
it suffices to show that
\lim_{t\to 0}(p(c+t)-p(c))=0
. Equivalently, we want to prove the quantified statement

\forall\epsilon>0\quad\exists\delta>0:\quad 0<|t|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|p(c+t)-p(c)|<\epsilon.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Choose
\delta=\square
, where
\square>0
is to be determined from the analysis of our calculations. Assume
0<|t|<\delta
. Then

\displaystyle p(c+t)-p(c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}{(c+t)}^{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}{c}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}({(c+t)}^{k}-c^{k}).

By the vanilla binomial theorem,

{(c+t)}^{k}-c^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}t^{j}-c^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}t^{j}.

This simplifies
p(c+t)-p(c)
to

\displaystyle p(c+t)-p(c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\left({\sum_{j=1}^{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}t^{j}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}t^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=j}^{n}a_{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}t^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}t^{j},

where

b_{j}=\sum_{k=j}^{n}a_{k}{k\choose j}c^{k-j}

can be computed with relatively simple algebraic manipulation.
Applying
|\cdot|
and using the triangle inequality, we can bound the output error by

\displaystyle|p(c+t)-p(c)|

\displaystyle=\left|{\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}t^{j}}\right|\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{|t|}^{j}<\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{\square}^{j}.

There are two ways to determine the choices for
\square
. We illustrate them below.

()

To allow the right-hand side to be bounded above by
\epsilon
, simply set each
|b_{j}|\square^{j}\leqslant\epsilon/n
. That is, stipulate

\square\leqslant\left({\frac{\epsilon}{n|b_{j}|}}\right)^{\frac{1}{j}},\quad b_{j}\neq 0,

so that by the triangle inequality,

|p(c+t)-p(c)|<\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|\square^{j}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{n}=\epsilon.

Thus, the choice
\square=\min\left\{{(\epsilon/(n|b_{j}|))}^{1/j}:b_{j}\neq 0\right\}
works.

()

Alternatively, one could factor the
|t|
to get

|p(c+t)-p(c)|\leqslant|t|\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{|t|}^{j-1}<\square\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{\square}^{j-1}.

By first stipulating
\square\leqslant 1
, one could upper bound the sum by

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{\square}^{j-1}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|,

which is positive since
p(x)
is a nonzero polynomial. Further stipulating
\square\leqslant\epsilon/\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|
, one can obtain

|p(c+t)-p(c)|<\square\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|{\square}^{j-1}\leqslant\left({\frac{\epsilon}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|}}\right)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|=\epsilon.

Thus, the choice
\square=\min\left\{1,\epsilon/\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{j}|\right\}
works.

∎

Lemma 1
.

For any nonzero polynomial
p(x)
with
p(c)\neq 0
, there exists
r>0
and
\delta>0
such that for
0<|t|<\delta
,
|p(c+t)|>r

Proof.

Since
\lim_{t\to c}p(t)=p(c)
, equivalently,
\lim_{t\to 0}(p(c+t)-p(c))=0
. Since
p(c)\neq 0
,
|p(c)|/2>0
. Then for
\epsilon=|p(c)|/2>0
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that

0<|t|<\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad|p(c+t)-p(c)|<\frac{|p(c)|}{2}.

Expanding the inequality,

p(c)-\frac{|p(c)|}{2}<p(c+t)<p(c)+\frac{|p(c)|}{2}.

We consider the cases
p(c)>0
and
p(c)<0
.

()

If
p(c)>0
, then the first inequality yields

p(c+t)>p(c)-\frac{|p(c)|}{2}=|p(c)|-\frac{|p(c)|}{2}=\frac{|p(c)|}{2}=:r>0,

which gives

|p(c+t)|=p(c+t)>r
.

()

If
p(c)<0

p(c+t)<p(c)+\frac{|p(c)|}{2}=-|p(c)|+\frac{p(c)}{2}=-\frac{|p(c)|}{2}=:-r<0,

|p(c+t)|=-p(c+t)>-(-r)=r

∎
