Covariance estimation using h-statistics in Monte Carlo and multilevel Monte Carlo methods
Sharana Kumar Shivanand

Abstract
We present novel Monte Carlo (MC) and multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) methods to determine the unbiased covariance of random variables using h-statistics. The advantage of this procedure lies in the unbiased construction of the estimator’s mean square error in a closed form. This is in contrast to conventional MC and MLMC covariance estimators, which are based on biased mean square errors defined solely by upper bounds, particularly within the MLMC. The numerical results of the algorithms are demonstrated by estimating the covariance of the stochastic response of a simple 1D stochastic elliptic PDE such as Poisson’s model.



Keywords:

covariance estimator, Monte Carlo, multilevel Monte Carlo, h-statistics, uncertainty quantification

It is imperative to emphasize that our study, in contrast to the aforementioned research, does not encompass broader multifidelity frameworks concerning covariance matrices. Instead, it fundamentally centers on a Monte Carlo-based scalar covariance estimation.

Traditional MC covariance estimation relies on a closed-form yet biased sampling error

As a specific case, we consider a straightforward 1D steady-state heat equation, where uncertainty is incorporated into the material properties, particularly in the form of spatially constant random fields that affect the thermal conductivity. This study focuses exclusively on material uncertainties, assuming that all other model parameters are deterministic. However, it is important to note that our numerical approach is versatile and can be extended to handle various types of uncertainty. Both the MC and MLMC methods propagate this uncertainty, enabling the estimation of key statistics including the covariance, mean, and variance of the temperature field.

Consider a physical system that occupies the spatial domain

3
Monte Carlo method

As the analytical integration of Eq. (
4
) becomes a formidable challenge, numerical integration methods must be embraced. In this regard, the Monte Carlo (MC)
[
29
,
10
]
method has traditionally emerged as the most common choice.

3.1
Classical Monte Carlo estimator

To estimate the mean using the Monte Carlo procedure (MC), denoted as

\mathbb{E}(u^{h})\approx\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}u^{h}(x,\omega_{k}),

(7)

we rely on a set of
N>1
independent sample points
\left\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},...,\omega_{N}\right\}\in\varOmega
drawn from the random field
u^{h}(x,\omega)
. Consequently, the computation of the covariance in Eq. (
3
) is transformed into the following expression:

\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\frac{N}{N-1}\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}((u^{h}_{i}-\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i}))(u^{h}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{j}))),

(8)

where the expectation operator
\mathbb{E}
is replaced by
\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}
. Note that in the previous equation,
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})
serves as an unbiased estimator, implying that
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j}))=\mathrm{cov}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})
, owing to the presence of Bessel’s correction term
N/N-1
.

Subsequently, we express the mean squared error (MSE) of the MC covariance estimator as

\textrm{MSE}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j}))=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j}))+(\mathrm{cov}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})-\mathrm{cov}(u_{i},u_{j}))^{2}.

(9)

The first term reflects sampling or stochastic accuracy. The variance is defined as

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(Z)=\mathbb{E}((Z-\mathbb{E}(Z))^{2})=\int_{\varOmega}(Z-\mathbb{E}(Z))^{2}\mathbb{P}(\text{d}\omega),

(10)

where
Z=\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})
.
On the other hand, the second term in Eq. (
9
) represents the square of the deterministic discretization error, primarily in the spatial domain. Therefore, to attain an overall mean squared error of
\epsilon^{2}
, one may ensure that both terms are less than
\epsilon^{2}/2
. However, such an equal splitting of accuracy is not a requirement and can be set otherwise.

This study specifically focuses on analyzing the sampling error, which can be further expanded (as shown in
[
1
,
31
]
) as follows:

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j}))=\frac{\mu_{2,2}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})}{N}-\frac{(N-2)\mathrm{cov}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})^{2}}{N(N-1)}+\frac{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(u^{h}_{i})\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(u^{h}_{j})}{N(N-1)}.

(11)

Here
\mu_{2,2}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})
denotes the bivariate central moment:

\mu_{p,q}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\mathbb{E}((u^{h}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}_{i}))^{p}(u^{h}_{j}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}_{j}))^{q}),

(12)

with orders
p,q
belonging to a set of non-negative integers
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

It is noteworthy that the variance, as outlined in Eq. (
11
), remains analytically tractable. Consequently, to attain better accuracy in this estimation, the challenge lies in obtaining an unbiased estimator, potentially in a closed form.
Therefore, this study formulates an unbiased framework for the approximation of sampling errors in the MC covariance estimator using h-statistics
[
8
]
.

3.2
Monte Carlo estimation by h-statistics

3.2.1
h-statistics: unbiased estimation of central moments

The
p^{\text{th}}
univariate central moment of solution
u^{h}
can be expressed analytically as follows:

\mu_{p}(u^{h})=\mathbb{E}((u^{h}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}))^{p})=\int_{\varOmega}(u^{h}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}))^{p}\mathbb{P}(\text{d}\omega).

(13)

Here,
\mathbb{E}(u^{h})
represents the mean or first raw moment (not to be confused with the first central moment, which equals zero when
p=1
). When
p=2
,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(u^{h}):=\mathbb{E}\left((u^{h}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}))^{2}\right)
corresponds to the second central moment or variance as described in Eq. (
10
).

The h-statistics, denoted by
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}}\equiv\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h})
, serves as an unbiased estimator of the central population moments
\mu_{p}(u^{h})
in Eq. (
13
)
[
8
,
34
]
. The ‘MC’ superscript of
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}
signifies that these statistics are obtained through random Monte Carlo-based sampling. For example, the first two h-statistics can be defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\approx\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1}^{\text{MC}}

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\approx\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{2}^{\mathrm{MC}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Ns_{2}-s_{1}^{2}}{N(N-1)},

in which
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are the power sums, given as

s_{a}(u^{h}):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}u^{h}(x,\omega_{k})^{a},

(15)

for
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Using the power series representation of h-statistics, the authors in
[
34
]
developed a
Mathematica
-based package called
mathStatica
, which efficiently generates h-statistics for any value of
p
.

What distinguishes h-statistics from other unbiased estimators are its properties, such as
[
16
]
:

1.

Unbiasedness
: The expectation of the h-statistic equals the corresponding population central moment, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}})=\mu_{p}
.

2.

Symmetry
: Among all unbiased estimators of
\mu_{p}
,
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}}
is the only one that exhibits symmetry
2
2

2

A function or an estimate is considered symmetric when it is not influenced by the order in which observations are considered.
.

3.

Minimum variance
: Out of all unbiased estimators of
\mu_{p}
,
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}}
stands out for its minimal variance, denoted as
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p}^{\mathrm{MC}})
.

3.2.2
Monte Carlo estimation of covariance by h-statistics

Covariance can be defined as a bivariate central moment with orders
p,q=1
. Thus, Eq. (
12
) can be expressed as

\mu_{1,1}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\mathbb{E}((u^{h}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}_{i}))(u^{h}_{j}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h}_{j}))).

(16)

This equation is identical to that of Eq. (
3
),
and the corresponding
bivariate h-statistics estimation of covariance is
[
34
]

\mu_{1,1}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})\approx\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\frac{Ns_{1,1}-s_{0,1}s_{1,0}}{(N-1)N}.

(17)

Here,
s_{1,1}
,
s_{0,1}
, and
s_{1,0}
represent the bivariate power sum,

s_{a,b}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}u^{h}_{i}(x,\omega_{k})^{a}u^{h}_{j}(x,\omega_{k})^{b},

(18)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

As a result, the MSE of
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}\equiv\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})
can be rewritten from Eq. (
9
) as,

\textrm{MSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})+(\mu_{1,1}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})-\mu_{1,1}(u_{i},u_{j}))^{2},

(19)

where the sampling accuracy is recast as bivariate population central moments:

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})=\frac{\mu_{2,2}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})}{N}-\frac{(N-2)\mu_{1,1}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})^{2}}{N(N-1)}+\frac{\mu_{0,2}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})\mu_{2,0}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})}{N(N-1)}.

(20)

Here,
\mu_{0,2}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(u^{h}_{j})
denotes the variance of random variable
u^{h}_{j}
, and
\mu_{2,0}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(u^{h}_{i})
is the variance of
u^{h}_{i}
.

It is important to note that replacing the bivariate central moments with h-statistics in the previous equation does not directly yield an unbiased estimate because of the inherent nonlinear transformation of statistics. For simplicity and clarity, we drop the random variable pair notation

is considered.

In the interest of brevity, we express the previous equation as
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})=\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}/N
, which is equivalent to

\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}=N\,\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}).

(24)

This implies that the stochastic convergence of the MC covariance estimator follows the order
\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
. Consequently, the reduction in MC error occurs at a relatively slow rate, making it a computationally expensive method. Furthermore, the procedure becomes infeasible when implemented in systems and processes represented by high-fidelity models, which are commonly used in many science and engineering applications. For instance, decreasing the value of the semi-discretization parameter
h
increases the deterministic fidelity in Eq. (
2
), but also significantly increases the computational cost required to determine a single sample of the solution
u^{h}(x,\omega)
.

Following this, we outline the total computational cost of the MC covariance estimation for a given MSE of
\epsilon^{2}
in the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1

Let us consider
c_{\alpha}
,
c_{\gamma}
,
\alpha
, and
\gamma
to be positive constants. The error bounds are defined as follows:

\begin{split}(i)&\quad\text{The deterministic error decays as }|\mu_{1,1}(u^{h}_{i},u^{h}_{j})-\mu_{1,1}(u_{i},u_{j})|\leq c_{\alpha}h^{\alpha},\\
(ii)&\quad\text{The computational cost to determine one sample of $u^{h}(x,\omega)$ is given by}\\
&\qquad\qquad\mathcal{C}(u^{h})\leq c_{\gamma}h^{-\gamma}.\end{split}

Then, for any
0<\epsilon<e^{-1}
, the Monte Carlo (MC) covariance estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}
with
N=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/\alpha})
satisfies
\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})<\epsilon^{2}
. Therefore, the corresponding MC computational cost to estimate the covariance is bounded by

\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}})\leq c\epsilon^{-2-\gamma/\alpha},

where
c>0
.

4
Multilevel Monte Carlo method

To address the limitations of the MC method, we focus on the sampling-based multilevel Monte Carlo method (MLMC)
[
17
,
11
,
12
]
. This approach offers a promising solution by distributing the sampling strategy across a hierarchy of fidelity levels, primarily in a sequential increase in fidelity from low to high. Under appropriate conditions (to be discussed in Section
4.2
), the resulting reduction in the number of stochastic samples as fidelity increases marks a significant increase in overall computational efficiency.

4.1
Classical multilevel Monte Carlo estimator

Consider a sequence denoted as
\left\{l=0,1,2...,L\right\}
, which represents an increasing hierarchy of nested mesh grids
\mathcal{P}_{l}
within the context of a decreasing element size
h
. These grids form a regular nondegenerate partition of the computational domain
\mathcal{G}
, as defined in Eq. (
1
). Here,
l
corresponds to the mesh level and
L
represents the finest available mesh level.

The primary objective of the Multilevel Monte Carlo method (
MLMC
) is to ascertain statistical properties, such as covariance
[
18
,
31
]
, of the solution
u^{h_{L}}(\omega)
on the finest mesh level
L
:

\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}(u^{h_{L}}_{i},u^{h_{L}}_{j})_{\left\{N_{l}\right\}}:=\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})_{N_{l}}-\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}.

(25)

Here,
N_{l}
denotes the number of samples at each mesh level and
\left\{N_{l}\right\}:=\left\{N_{0},N_{1},...,N_{L}\right\}
represents the set of samples required to determine
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}
. In addition,
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}}
is the
MC
estimator of
\mathrm{cov}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})
at level
l=0
using
N_{0}
samples. Similarly,
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})_{N_{l}}
and
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}
represent the approximation of
\mathrm{cov}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})
and
\mathrm{cov}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})
with
l>0
and
N_{l}
samples, respectively.
To simplify the discussion, we introduce the following:

{Y}_{l}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}},\quad&l=0,\\
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})_{N_{l}}-\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}},\quad&l>0.\end{cases}

(26)

Following this, the covariance estimator
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}
from Eq. (
25
) can be expressed as

\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}=\sum_{l=0}^{L}{Y}_{l}.

(27)

It is important to note that each term
{Y}_{l},\ l\geq{0}
is sampled independently, and hence remains uncorrelated. However, for
l>0
, the quantities
(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})_{N_{l}}
and
(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}
in
{Y}_{l}
are strongly correlated and originate from the same random seeds.

A multilevel covariance estimator, represented by Eq. (
25
) or, in alternative terms, the sample multilevel covariance matrix
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{U}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{U}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{U}}^{h_{L}})
of the form shown in Eq. (
5
), has a limitation in that it does not guarantee the positive-definiteness property required for covariance matrices, as explained in Eq. (
6
).
This is because subtracting one positive-definite matrix from another does not guarantee that the resulting matrix is positive-definite, which is vital to the stability of the algorithm
[
18
,
27
]
.
To overcome this drawback, we follow a simple post-hoc procedure (as also depicted in
[
18
]
) by performing eigen-decomposition on

\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{T},

(28)

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the eigenvalues, and
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are the corresponding eigenvectors.
Positive definiteness is ensured by removing the zero- and negative-valued eigenvalues (
\lambda_{i}\leq 0
), that is

\mathrm{c}\widetilde{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\textrm{ML}}=\sum_{i=1;\lambda_{i}>0}^{M}\lambda_{i}\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{q}}_{i}^{T}.

(29)

As alternatives to spectral decomposition, which may involve less intrusiveness and/or reduced computational costs, various numerical techniques, including banding, shrinkage, thresholding, and localization (as outlined in
[
18
]
), offer the assurance of positive-definiteness of the covariance matrix, particularly in higher dimensions. In contrast,
[
27
]
introduced a multifidelity covariance estimator that enforces the definiteness constraint by construction, utilizing the log-Euclidean mapping of symmetric and positive definite (SPD) matrices.

As the multilevel Monte Carlo covariance estimate
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}
on the finest grid
L
is obtained as the telescopic sum of the difference of the
MC
covariance estimates on the coarser grids, the MSE corresponding to Eq. (
27
) takes the following form:

\textrm{MSE}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}})=\sum_{l=0}^{L}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}({Y}_{l})+(\mathrm{cov}(u^{h_{L}}_{i},u^{h_{L}}_{j})-\mathrm{cov}(u_{i},u_{j}))^{2}.

(30)

Similar to Eq. (
9
), the error estimate above is composed of two terms. The first denotes the variance of the covariance estimator
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}
; that is,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}})=\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\sum_{l=0}^{L}{Y}_{l})=\sum_{l=0}^{L}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}({Y}_{l})
. On the other hand, the second part represents the square of the spatial discretization error.
As emphasized by the authors in
[
31
]
, it is essential to determine the upper bound of the sampling error to ensure the convergence of the multilevel estimator. Therefore, it is derived as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{l=0}^{L}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}({Y}_{l})&\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=0}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}-1}\left(\sqrt{\mu_{4}(X^{{}^{-}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i}))\mu_{4}(X^{{}^{+}}(u^{h_{l}}_{j}))}+\right.\\
&\left.\sqrt{\mu_{4}(X^{{}^{+}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i}))\mu_{4}(X^{{}^{-}}(u^{h_{l}}_{j}))}\right),\end{split}

(31)

where
\mu_{4}
denotes the fourth-order central moment (see Eq. (
13
) for
r=4
). Furthermore,
X^{{}^{+}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i})=u^{h_{l}}_{i}+u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}
and
X^{{}^{-}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i})=u^{h_{l}}_{i}-u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}
.

As the previous equation is analytical, if one estimates the sample fourth-order moment as

\mu_{4}\approx\widehat{\mu}_{4}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{l}}(X_{k}-\widehat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}(X))^{4},

where
X
is
X^{{}^{-}}
or
X^{{}^{+}}
, we obtain the approximation for the sampling error. Meaning that,

\sum_{l=0}^{L}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}({Y}_{l})\approx\sum_{l=0}^{L}\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}({Y}_{l}),

(32)

which remains biased and worst-case bounded.

4.2
Multilevel Monte Carlo estimator using h-statistics

As discussed in Section
3
, the objective of this study is to obtain an unbiased estimate of the sampling error of the estimator
\mathrm{c}\widehat{\mathrm{o}}\mathrm{v}^{\mathrm{ML}}
, which is also expressed in a closed form. To this, we rewrite the classical multilevel covariance estimator from Eq. (
25
) using h-statistics as

\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}}(u^{h_{L}}_{i},u^{h_{L}}_{j})_{\left\{N_{l}\right\}}:=\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})_{N_{l}}-\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}.

(33)

Because the second part of the above equation (i.e., for
l>0
) is composed of variables
u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}
and
u^{h_{l-1}}_{j}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}}(u^{h_{L}}_{i},u^{h_{L}}_{j})_{\left\{N_{l}\right\}}=&\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1,0,0}^{\text{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}-\\
&\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{0,0,1,1}^{\text{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}.\end{split}

(34)

Here, the quadrivariate h-statistic
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{p,q,r,s}^{\text{MC}}
is an unbiased estimator of the corresponding central moment

with orders

(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}))^{r}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{j}-\mathbb{E}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{j}))^{s}).\end{split}

(35)

See Eq. (
12
) for the definition of bivariate central moment.
Similar to Eq. (
26
), one may simplify by introducing

{Z}_{l}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}(u^{h_{0}}_{i},u^{h_{0}}_{j})_{N_{0}},\quad&l=0,\\
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1,0,0}^{\text{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}}-\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{0,0,1,1}^{\text{MC}}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})_{N_{l}},\quad&l>0,\end{cases}

(36)

in Eq. (
34
), which transforms to

\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}}=\sum_{l=0}^{L}{Z}_{l}.

(37)

For
l=0
,
Z_{0}=\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}
is described by Eq. (
17
).
On the other hand,
{Z}_{l}

is defined

{Z}_{l}=\frac{N_{l}s_{1,1,0,0}-s_{0,1,0,0}s_{1,0,0,0}}{(N_{l}-1)N_{l}}-\frac{N_{l}s_{0,0,1,1}-s_{0,0,0,1}s_{0,0,1,0}}{(N_{l}-1)N_{l}}.

(38)

Here, the quadrivariate power sum

s_{a,b,c,d}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j},u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}u^{h_{l}}_{i}(\omega_{k})^{a}u^{h_{l}}_{j}(\omega_{k})^{b}u^{h_{l-1}}_{i}(\omega_{k})^{c}u^{h_{l-1}}_{j}(\omega_{k})^{d},

(39)

where

are non-negative integers.

4.2.2
Computational Complexity

If
\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
represents the computational cost of evaluating one MC sample of
u^{h_{l}}(x,\omega)
and
u^{h_{l-1}}(x,\omega)
in the difference term
{Z}_{l}
, then the overall cost of the MLMC covariance estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}}
is given by:

\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}}):=\sum_{l=0}^{L}N_{l}\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l}).

(45)

Subsequently, the optimum number of samples
N_{l}
on each mesh level
l
is determined to minimize the total computational cost
\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}})
under the constraint
\sum_{l=0}^{L}\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}({Z}_{l})<\epsilon^{2}/2
.
By re-expressing
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}({Z}_{l})=\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}/N_{l}
, which essentially is
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}=N_{l}\,\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\mathrm{ar}^{\mathrm{MC}}({Z}_{l})
, this translates into a constrained optimization problem, where the cost function

f(N_{l})=\text{arg}\min_{N_{l}}\sum_{l=0}^{L}\left(N_{l}\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l})+\tau\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}}{N_{l}}\right),

(46)

is minimized. Therefore, we can obtain the optimal number of samples
N_{l}
as:

N_{l}=\tau\left({\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}}{\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(47)

where
\tau
is the Lagrange multiplier defined by

\tau={\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}}}\sum_{l=0}^{L}\left({{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(48)

In addition, one can strategically choose the mesh levels
\left\{l=0,1,2,...,L\right\}
in an optimal manner, either following a geometric or non-geometric sequence, to minimize a specified deterministic error. For example, in PDE-based applications, the selection of the initial coarse mesh
l=0
is contingent on the regularity of the solution
u(x)
in Eq. (
1
)
[
6
]
. Subsequently, the choice of finer mesh grids is guided by an a priori mesh convergence analysis, where the finest mesh level
l=L
can either be set in advance or adaptively determined
[
12
]
during the computation of the MLMC estimation. For a deeper exploration of the optimal strategies for mesh hierarchy selection, refer to
[
14
]
.

The overall computational complexity of the h-statistics-based MLMC covariance estimator mirrors that of the classical MLMC procedure
of mean estimation in
[
6
,
12
]
. Furthermore, the notion of computational complexity of an arbitrary multilevel statistical estimator is detailed in
[
31
, Theorem 2.1]
, which also derives a special case for the multilevel covariance estimation in Theorem 2.5.
Therefore, we consider the following proposition:

Proposition 4.1

Consider the positive constants
c_{\alpha}
,
c_{\beta}
,
c_{\gamma}
,
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
with the constraint
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}\min(\beta,\gamma)
. We assume the following error bounds:

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\text{the deterministic error decays as}\ |\mu_{1,1}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})-\mu_{1,1}(u_{i},u_{j})|\leq c_{\alpha}{h_{l}}^{\alpha},

(49)

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\text{the variance decays as}\ \widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{l,1,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}\leq c_{\beta}h_{l}^{\beta},

\displaystyle(iii)

\displaystyle\text{the computational cost to determine one sample of}\ {Z}_{l}\ \text{is given by}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}({Z}_{l})\leq c_{\gamma}{h_{l}}^{-\gamma}.

Under these conditions, there exists a positive constant
c
such that, for any
0<\epsilon<e^{-1}
, the multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) mean square error estimator satisfies
\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}})<\epsilon^{2}
, and the total computational cost is bounded as follows:

\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathrm{h}}_{1,1}^{\mathrm{ML}})\leq\begin{cases}c\,{\epsilon}^{-2},&\beta>\gamma,\\
c\,{\epsilon}^{-2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\log{\epsilon})^{2}},&\beta=\gamma,\\
c\,{\epsilon}^{-2-(\gamma-\beta)/\alpha},&\beta<\gamma.\end{cases}

(50)

By considering the values of
\beta
and
\gamma
, we can determine the primary cost driver within the mesh grid sequence. If
\beta
is greater than
\gamma
, then the maximum cost is influenced by the coarsest level. Conversely, if
\beta
is less than
\gamma
, the finest level dictates the predominant cost. When
\beta
equals
\gamma
, the cost is generally evenly distributed across all levels

Furthermore, it is evident from the first relation in Eq. (
49
) that, as
l\rightarrow\infty
,

|\mu_{1,1}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})-\mu_{1,1}(u_{i},u_{j})|\rightarrow 0.

However,
\mu_{1,1}(u_{i},u_{j})
is analytical and unknown. Therefore, the deterministic error is defined using the triangle inequality as

|\mu_{1,1}(u^{h_{l}}_{i},u^{h_{l}}_{j})-\mu_{1,1}(u^{h_{l-1}}_{i},u^{h_{l-1}}_{j})|\leq c_{\alpha}{h_{l}}^{\alpha}.

(51)

5
Application to Poisson’s equation: 1D steady state heat equation

Deterministic Setting:
Consider a physical body defined in the domain
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{R}
, within a one-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
, with a smooth Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
. The objective is to determine the temperature
u({x})\in\mathbb{R}
(belonging to the Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
) at a spatial point
{x}\in\mathcal{G}
. This solution should satisfy the governing equations describing the steady-state heat conductivity:

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x)\nabla u(x))

\displaystyle=f(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{G},

(52)

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle={u}_{0},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\kappa(x)\nabla u(x)\cdot{n}(x)

\displaystyle={g}(x),\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{N},

where
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
represents the heat sink and source. In addition,
{g}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the surface heat flux on the Neumann boundary
\Gamma_{N}\subset\Gamma
and the homogeneous temperature on the Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{D}\subseteq\Gamma
, respectively. The vector
n(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
signifies the outward unit normal to the boundary
\Gamma_{N}
.
Furthermore, we assume that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
and
\Gamma_{D}\cap\Gamma_{N}=0
.

In this context,
\kappa(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
represents the thermal conductivity field. As Poisson’s equation encompasses a broader class of elliptic boundary value problems, describing physical phenomena such as electrostatic potential and chemical diffusion, the material coefficient
\kappa(x)
may also be considered to characterize intrinsic physics-based properties, such as electric permittivity and chemical diffusivity.

Uncertainty Modelling:
In accordance with Section
2
, we specifically model the coefficient
\kappa
as uncertain. For simplicity, this study only considers a spatially constant thermal conductivity such that
\kappa(x)\equiv\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Thus,
\kappa(x)
is depicted as a positive-definite random variable

\kappa(\omega):\varOmega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}

(53)

over probability space
(\varOmega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P})
.

For instance, in this study, we model
\kappa(\omega)
as a log-normal random variable

\kappa(\omega)=\exp(\psi(\omega))

(54)

on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Here,
\psi(\omega)
represents a Gaussian random variable,

\psi(\omega)=\log(\kappa(\omega))\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)

(55)

over
\mathbb{R}
, which is characterized by the mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and standard deviation
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The desired parameters, namely the mean
\mu_{\kappa}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\kappa}
of the log-normal variable
\kappa(\omega)
in Eq. (
54
), are related to the corresponding Gaussian random variable
\psi(\omega)
as established in
[
32
]
:

\mu=\ln\left(\frac{\mu_{\kappa}^{2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{\kappa}^{2}+\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}}}\right)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\sigma=\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\frac{\sigma_{\kappa}^{2}}{\mu_{\kappa}^{2}}\right)}.

(56)

Stochastic Setting:

By reformulating the deterministic model in Eq. (
52
) as a stochastic model,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(\omega)\nabla u(x,\omega))

\displaystyle=f(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{G},\omega\in\varOmega,

(57)

\displaystyle u(x,\omega)

\displaystyle={u}_{0},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\kappa(\omega)\nabla u(x,\omega)\cdot n(x)

\displaystyle={g}(x),\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{N},

which must be understood in a weak sense, both spatially and stochastically.
The objective is to determine the random temperature field
u({x},\omega):\mathcal{G}\times\varOmega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by assuming deterministic boundary conditions and a heat source.

As explained in Section
2
, given the weak form of Eq. (
57
), we employ the finite-element method to seek an approximate solution
u^{h}({x},\omega)
within a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{U}^{h}\subset\mathcal{U}
of the solution space
\mathcal{U}
, where
h
denotes the discretization parameter, which is an indicator of element size.
Subsequently, this study aims to determine the sample covariance of the solution
u^{h}({x},\omega)
using Monte Carlo and multilevel Monte Carlo estimators, as defined in Sections
3
and
4
.

7
Conclusion

A novel approach to estimate the unbiased covariance of random variables,
with minimal variance
, using both Monte Carlo (MC) and Multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) methods with h-statistics is introduced. Traditional MC covariance estimation relies on a closed-form, yet biased, sampling error; in contrast, MLMC covariance estimators depend on biased and upper-bounded stochastic accuracy. To enhance the overall accuracy and efficiency of covariance estimation, we derive unbiased and closed-form versions of the mean square error estimates using h-statistics within both the MC and MLMC algorithms.
This leads to potentially achieving sharper error estimates as compared to the error bounds of traditional estimators, particularly in MLMC estimation.

The proposed h-statistics-based procedures, along with the conventional MLMC method, are tested by applying them to Poisson’s equation in the form of a simple 1D steady-state heat equation. The analysis incorporates uncertainty in the material properties, specifically the thermal conductivity, modelled as a log-normal random variable. These methods propagate the uncertainty to estimate the covariance, as well as statistics, such as the mean and variance of the temperature field.

Finally, we compare the computational efficiencies of the MLMC and MC covariance estimators for different stochastic accuracies. The h-statistics-based MLMC estimator significantly outperformed the corresponding MC method in terms of the computational cost. It is also evident that the h-statistics-based MLMC method required fewer samples than the conventional MLMC, highlighting the computational advantages of the unbiased and closed-form sampling error-based MLMC covariance estimator over biased and upper-bounded MLMC estimates. Furthermore, the difference in accuracy between the MLMC and MC methods is small.
