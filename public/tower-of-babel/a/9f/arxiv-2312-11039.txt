1.
Introduction

1.1.

A system of vectors
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in a Banach space
X

is called a
Schauder basis
if every
x\in X
admits a unique series expansion

x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}x_{n}
where
\{c_{n}\}
are scalars.
In this case there exist
biorthogonal continuous linear functionals
\{x_{n}^{*}\}

such that the coefficients of the series expansion are given by

c_{n}=x_{n}^{*}(x)
(see e.g.
[
You01
, Section 1.6]
).
If the series converges unconditionally
(i.e. if it converges for any rearrangement of its terms)
for every
x\in X
, then
\{x_{n}\}

is said to be an
unconditional
Schauder basis.

Given a function
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we denote its translates by

(T_{\lambda}g)(x)=g(x-\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.1)

A long-standing problem which is still open, asks
whether the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
admits
a Schauder basis formed by translates of a single function.
The problem was first posed in the paper
[
OZ92
]
,
where it was also proved that no system of translates
can be an unconditional Schauder basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

A sequence
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of real numbers
is said to be
uniformly discrete
if

\inf_{n\neq m}|\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{n}|>0.

(1.2)

It was observed in
[
OZ92
]
that
the condition (
1.2
) is necessary for
a system of translates

\{T_{\lambda_{n}}g\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

to form a Schauder basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

1.2.

An example of a uniformly discrete real sequence
is the sequence of integers
\mathbb{Z}
.
It is well known that if
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
then a system

\{T_{\lambda}g\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

cannot even be
complete
in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
see e.g.
[
OU16
, Example 11.2]
.
Moreoever, it was conjectured (see
[
RS95
, Conjecture 1]
)
that the same should be true for any uniformly discrete
sequence
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
. However, this is not the case;
it was proved by Olevskii

[
Ole97
]
that for any “small perturbation” of the positive integers,
that is,

\lambda_{n}=n+\alpha_{n},\quad 0\neq\alpha_{n}\to 0\quad(n\to+\infty)

(1.3)

there exists
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

such that the system

\{T_{\lambda_{n}}g\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is complete in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
This shows that the arithmetic structure of the set
\Lambda

plays an important role in the problem.

1.3.

If
X
is a Banach space with dual space
X^{*}
, then a system

\{(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
X\times X^{*}
is called a

Schauder frame
(or a quasi-basis) if every
x\in X
has a series expansion

x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}^{*}(x)x_{n}.

(1.4)

If the series (
1.4
)
converges unconditionally for every
x\in X
, then

\{(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})\}
is called an
unconditional
Schauder frame.
We note that if
\{x_{n}\}
is a Schauder basis
with biorthogonal coefficient functionals
\{x_{n}^{*}\}
,
then
\{(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})\}
is a Schauder frame.
To the contrary, for a Schauder frame
\{(x_{n},x_{n}^{*})\}
the series expansion
(
1.4
) need not be unique and the coefficient functionals
\{x_{n}^{*}\}

need not be biorthogonal to
\{x_{n}\}
. Hence
Schauder frames form a wider class of representation systems than Schauder bases.

We proved recently
[
LT25
]
that in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

there do not exist unconditional Schauder frames
consisting of translates, i.e. of the form

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}
where
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\{\lambda_{n}\}
is a sequence of real numbers (discrete or non-discrete),
and
\{g_{n}^{*}\}
are continuous linear functionals on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

On the other hand, in
[
FPT21
, Section 4]
a
construction was given of (not unconditional) Schauder frames
of translates in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In fact, it was proved
that whenever a system of translates
\{T_{\lambda}g\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

is complete in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then there exists a Schauder frame

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

such that
\{\lambda_{n}\}\subset\Lambda
.
However the Schauder frames obtained by this construction
are
highly redundant
, as the sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}
is composed
of countably many blocks of finite size,
such that each block gets repeated a higher and higher
number of times. The sequence

\{\lambda_{n}\}
thus “runs back and forth” through the set
\Lambda
.

1.4.

The following question was posed in
[
OSSZ11
, Problem 4.4]
:
does there exist a Schauder frame in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
formed by
a
uniformly discrete
sequence of translates?
It is clear from the above that the problem cannot be settled by
combining the results from
[
Ole97
]
and
[
FPT21
, Section 4]
.

The main goal of the present paper is nevertheless to provide
the above problem with an affirmative solution.
We will prove the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist
a uniformly discrete sequence
\{\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\}\subset\mathbb{R}

and functions
g
and
\{g_{n}^{*}\}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
such that every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
admits a series expansion

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,g_{n}^{*}\rangle g(x-\lambda_{n})

(1.5)

convergent in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
norm.

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual scalar product
in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

The proof involves techniques
from the papers
[
Ole97
]
,
[
KO99
]
,
[
KO01
]
,
[
Ole02
]
.
It yields, similar to
[
Ole97
]
, a sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

that can be obtained from the positive integers
by a small perturbation.

In fact the technique allows to establish some stronger forms of Theorem
1.1
.
We present these extensions and give some
additional remarks in Section
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we fix notation and terminology, and
state some known facts that will be used in the proof
of the main result.

2.1.

By a
weight
we mean a function
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
w(t)>0
a.e.
For a weight
w
consider the space
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})

consisting of all functions
f
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|^{2}w(t)dt<+\infty
.
It is a separable Hilbert space endowed with the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\overline{g(t)}w(t)dt
.

The following result was proved in
[
Ole97
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
Ole97
]
)
.

Let
\sigma(n)=n+o(1)
,
\sigma(n)\neq n

(n=1,2,\dots)
.
Then for any weight
w_{0}
one can find another
weight
w
,
w(t)\leqslant w_{0}(t)
a.e., such that
the exponential system

\{e^{2\pi i\sigma(n)t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is complete in the space
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.2.

By a
trigonometric polynomial
we mean a finite sum of the form

P(t)=\sum_{j}a_{j}e^{2\pi i\sigma_{j}t}

(2.1)

where
\{\sigma_{j}\}
are distinct real numbers, and
\{a_{j}\}

are complex numbers. By the
spectrum
of
P
we mean the set

\operatorname{spec}(P):=\{\sigma_{j}:a_{j}\neq 0\}
.
If we have
\operatorname{spec}(P)\subset[0,+\infty)
then
we say that
P
is
analytic
.
We use the notation

\|\widehat{P}\|_{q}=(\sum_{j}|a_{j}|^{q})^{1/q}

and
\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty}=\max_{j}|a_{j}|
.

We also denote

\|P\|_{\infty}=\sup\{|P(t)|:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
,
and observe that

\|P\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|\widehat{P}\|_{1}
.

The (symmetric)
partial sum

S_{r}(P)
of a trigonometric polynomial
(
2.1
) is defined by

S_{r}(P)(t)=\sum_{|\sigma_{j}|\leqslant r}a_{j}e^{2\pi i\sigma_{j}t}.

(2.2)

We observe that if a trigonometric polynomial
P

has integer spectrum,
\operatorname{spec}(P)\subset\mathbb{Z}
,
then
P
is
1
-periodic, that is,

P(t+1)=P(t)
. In this case, we use

\{\widehat{P}(n)\}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, to denote the Fourier
coefficients of
P
.

The following lemma is taken from
[
Ole02
, Section 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given any
\varepsilon>0
and
q>2
, one can find
an analytic polynomial with integer spectrum

P(t)=\sum_{n>0}\widehat{P}(n)e^{2\pi int}

(2.3)

and a measurable set
F\subset[0,1]

satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

\|\widehat{P}\|_{q}<\varepsilon
;

(ii)

m([0,1]\setminus F)<\varepsilon
;

(iii)

|P(t)-1|<\varepsilon
on
F
;

(iv)

Any partial sum

S_{l}(P)(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{l}\widehat{P}(n)e^{2\pi int}

(2.4)

can be decomposed as a sum
A_{l}+B_{l}
of two analytic polynomials

A_{l}
,
B_{l}
with integer spectrum, such that

\|A_{l}\|_{L^{\infty}(F)}<2,\quad\|B_{l}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}<\varepsilon.

(2.5)

For the proof see
[
KO01
]
, Lemma 4.1 and Remark 2 on pp. 382–383.

2.3.

A system of vectors
\{x_{n}\}
in a separable Hilbert space
H

is called a
Riesz basis
if it can be obtained as
the image of an orthonormal basis
under a bounded and invertible linear map.
If
\{x_{n}\}
is a Riesz basis then it is
an unconditional Schauder basis, and
the biorthogonal system

\{x_{n}^{*}\}
is also a Riesz basis
(see
[
You01
, Section 1.8]
).

Lemma 2.3

(see
[
You01
, Section 1.10]
)
.

Let
\{e_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis in a Hilbert space
H
.
If
\{x_{n}\}\subset H
satisfies

\sum_{n}\|x_{n}-e_{n}\|^{2}<1
,
then
\{x_{n}\}
is a Riesz basis
in
H
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

3.1.

Following
[
Ole97
]
we begin with a reformulation of the main result
in terms of exponential systems in weighted
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
spaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any weight
w_{0}
we can find
a weight
w
,
w(t)\leqslant w_{0}(t)
a.e.,
a uniformly discrete sequence
\{\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
and a sequence of functions
\{e_{j}^{*}\}\subset L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
,
such that every
f\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
admits a series expansion

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle f,e_{j}^{*}\rangle\,e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}

(3.1)

convergent in the
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
norm.

We show that Theorem
3.1
implies Theorem
1.1
.
The mapping
U(f):=(f\sqrt{w})^{\wedge}

is a unitary operator
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
where
\widehat{\varphi}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)\exp(-2\pi ixt)dt

is the Fourier transform of
\varphi
.
We take
g:=U(1)
and observe that the exponential
system
\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}\}

is mapped by
U
onto the system of translates
\{T_{\lambda_{j}}g\}
.
Hence if we take
g_{j}^{*}:=U(e_{j}^{*})
then we obtain
Theorem
1.1
as a consequence of Theorem
3.1
.

3.2.

We now turn to prove Theorem
3.1
.
Assume that we are given a weight
w_{0}
. We use Lemma
2.1

to choose and fix another weight
u
satisfying

u(t)\leqslant\min\{w_{0}(t),\,\tfrac{1}{10}(1+t^{2})^{-1}\}

(3.2)

and such that there is an exponential system with real frequencies

\{e^{2\pi i\sigma(n)t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty},\quad\sigma(n)=n+o(1),\quad 0<|\sigma(n)-n|<\tfrac{1}{10}

(3.3)

which is complete in the space
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
.

For a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
we let
m(E)

be the Lebesgue measure of
E
, and

m_{u}(E):=\int_{E}u(t)dt.

(3.4)

Then
m_{u}
is a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
.
Due to (
3.2
), for any
F\subset[0,1]
we have

m_{u}(F+\mathbb{Z})\leqslant m(F).

(3.5)

Similarly, for any
1
-periodic function
g(t)
we have

\|g\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\|g\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}.

(3.6)

3.3.

The space
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
is a separable Hilbert space. Let us choose some
orthonormal basis
\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
for the space.
We construct by induction
a decreasing sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
of positive
numbers, an increasing sequence
\{M_{k}\}
of positive numbers,
and a sequence of functions
\{\gamma_{k}(t)\}
satisfying

1=\gamma_{0}(t)\geqslant\gamma_{1}(t)\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\gamma_{k}(t)>0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.7)

We begin by setting
\varepsilon_{0}:=1
and
M_{0}:=1
.

At the
k
’th step of the induction, we
observe that the weighted exponential system

\{\gamma_{k-1}(t)e^{2\pi i\sigma(n)t}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

(3.8)

is also complete in the space
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
. This can be easily
inferred from the completeness
of the unweighted exponential system (
3.3
) by duality.
Hence given any
\eta_{k}>0
we can find a trigonometric polynomial

Q_{k}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}d_{n,k}e^{2\pi i\sigma(n)t}

(3.9)

such that

\|\varphi_{k}-\gamma_{k-1}\cdot Q_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}<\eta_{k}.

(3.10)

We can then choose
\varepsilon_{k}>0
such that

\int_{S}(|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}+|Q_{k}(t)|^{2})u(t)dt<\eta_{k}^{2}

(3.11)

whenever
S\subset\mathbb{R}
is a measurable set satisfying

m_{u}(S)<\varepsilon_{k}
. By choosing

\varepsilon_{k}
small enough we can also assume that

\text{(a) \,}\varepsilon_{k}<\eta_{k}^{2};\quad\text{(b) \,}\varepsilon_{k}\|\widehat{Q}_{k}\|_{1}<\eta_{k};\quad\text{(c) \,}M_{k-1}\varepsilon_{k}<\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{k-1}.

(3.12)

By Lemma
2.2
we can now
find an analytic polynomial with integer spectrum

P_{k}(t)=\sum_{n>0}\widehat{P}_{k}(n)e^{2\pi int}

(3.13)

and a set
F_{k}\subset[0,1]
satisfying

(i)

\|\widehat{P}_{k}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon_{k}
;

(ii)

m([0,1]\setminus F_{k})<\varepsilon_{k}
;

(iii)

|P_{k}(t)-1|<\varepsilon_{k}
on
F_{k}
;

(iv)

Any partial sum
S_{l}(P_{k})

can be decomposed as a sum
A_{k,l}+B_{k,l}
such that

\|A_{k,l}\|_{L^{\infty}(F_{k})}<2,\quad\|B_{k,l}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}<\varepsilon_{k},

(3.14)

where
A_{k,l}
,
B_{k,l}
are analytic polynomials with integer spectrum.

We choose a large positive integer
\nu_{k}
(to be specified later) and set

E_{k}=\nu_{k}^{-1}(F_{k}+\mathbb{Z}),

(3.15)

then using
(
3.5
) we can deduce from
(ii)
that

m_{u}(E_{k}^{\complement})<\varepsilon_{k},

(3.16)

while due to
(iii)
we have

|P_{k}(\nu_{k}t)-1|<\varepsilon_{k},\quad t\in E_{k}.

(3.17)

We now set
M_{k}:=\max\{M_{k-1},\|P_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\|Q_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\}
,
and choose
\gamma_{k}
to satisfy

\gamma_{k}(t)=\gamma_{k-1}(t),\quad t\in E_{k},

(3.18)

and

0<\gamma_{k}(t)(1+\|P_{k}\|_{\infty}+\max_{l}\|A_{k,l}\|_{\infty})\leqslant\gamma_{k-1}(t),\quad t\in E_{k}^{\complement},

(3.19)

which completes the inductive step of the construction.

3.4.

Let now

D_{k}:=\bigcap_{j\geqslant k}E_{j}.

(3.20)

By (
3.12
)(c), (
3.16
) we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{u}(E_{k}^{\complement})\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}<+\infty,

(3.21)

hence the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that

\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D_{k}
is a set
of full measure in
\mathbb{R}
.

Define

\gamma(t):=\inf_{k}\gamma_{k}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.22)

It follows from (
3.18
) that

\gamma(t)=\gamma_{k-1}(t)>0,\quad t\in D_{k}.

(3.23)

As a consequence, we conclude that
\gamma(t)>0
a.e.

3.5.

Next we claim that the estimate

\|\varphi_{k}-\gamma\cdot\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}<4\eta_{k},\quad\tilde{P}_{k}(t):=P_{k}(\nu_{k}t),

(3.24)

holds for all
k
. Indeed, we have

\|\varphi_{k}-\gamma\cdot\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi_{k}-\gamma\cdot\tilde{P_{k}}\cdot Q_{k}|^{2}u\leqslant\int_{D_{k}}+\int_{E_{k}^{\complement}}+\int_{D_{k+1}^{\complement}}.

(3.25)

We estimate each of the three integrals separately.

First, due to (
3.17
), (
3.23
)
we have

\displaystyle|\varphi_{k}(t)-\gamma(t)\tilde{P}_{k}(t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}

(3.26)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\big(|\varphi_{k}(t)-\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)|+|\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)(1-P_{k}(\nu_{k}t))|\big)^{2}

(3.27)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2|\varphi_{k}(t)-\gamma_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}+2|Q_{k}(t)|^{2}\varepsilon_{k}^{2},\quad t\in D_{k},

(3.28)

hence using (
3.10
), (
3.12
)(b)
and the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\leqslant 1
we obtain

\int_{D_{k}}|\cdots|^{2}u\leqslant 2\|\varphi_{k}-\gamma_{k-1}\cdot Q_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+2\|Q_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\leqslant 4\eta_{k}^{2}.

(3.29)

Second, due to (
3.19
) we have

\displaystyle|\varphi_{k}(t)-\gamma(t)P_{k}(\nu_{k}t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}

(3.30)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}+2|\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}\|P_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}

(3.31)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}+2|Q_{k}(t)|^{2},\quad t\in E_{k}^{\complement},

(3.32)

so using (
3.11
), (
3.16
) we obtain

\int_{E_{k}^{\complement}}|\cdots|^{2}u\leqslant 2\eta_{k}^{2}.

(3.33)

Lastly, we have

\displaystyle|\varphi_{k}(t)-\gamma(t)P_{k}(\nu_{k}t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}

(3.34)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}+2|\gamma(t)P_{k}(\nu_{k}t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}

(3.35)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant 2|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}+2M_{k}.

(3.36)

Observe that by (
3.12
)(c) and (
3.16
) we have

m_{u}(D_{k+1}^{\complement})\leqslant\sum_{j>k}m_{u}(E_{j}^{\complement})\leqslant\sum_{j>k}\varepsilon_{j}\leqslant\varepsilon_{k}M_{k}^{-1},

(3.37)

so using (
3.11
), (
3.12
)(a) we obtain

\int_{D_{k+1}^{\complement}}|\cdots|^{2}u\leqslant 2\eta_{k}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{k}\leqslant 4\eta_{k}^{2}.

(3.38)

Combining (
3.29
), (
3.33
) and (
3.38
) we
arrive at the desired estimate (
3.24
).

3.6.

Suppose now that we
choose the sequence
\{\eta_{k}\}
to satisfy
16
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}<1
.
Then Lemma
2.3
and
the estimate (
3.24
) imply that
the system

\{\gamma\cdot\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

(3.39)

forms a Riesz basis in the space
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
.
Hence every
f\in L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
has a series expansion

f=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle\,\gamma\cdot\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}

(3.40)

where
\{\psi_{k}\}\subset L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
is the system biorthogonal
to (
3.39
) and the convergence of the series (
3.40
) is unconditional.
Moreover,
\{\psi_{k}\}
is also
a Riesz basis in
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
, hence

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant K\|f\|^{2}

(3.41)

where
K
is a constant not depending on
f
.

Here and below, by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and

\|\cdot\|
with no subindex, we mean the scalar product and the norm in
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.7.

Notice that we have

\tilde{P}_{k}(t)Q_{k}(t)=P_{k}(\nu_{k}t)Q_{k}(t)=\sum_{m>0}\widehat{P}_{k}(m)Q_{k,m}(t),

(3.42)

where
Q_{k,m}
are trigonometric polynomials defined by

Q_{k,m}(t):=Q_{k}(t)e^{2\pi im\nu_{k}t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}d_{n,k}e^{2\pi i(\sigma(n)+m\nu_{k})t}.

(3.43)

If we choose the sequence
\{\nu_{k}\}
growing sufficiently fast,
we can guarantee that the spectra
of the polynomials
Q_{k,m}
follow each other, in the sense that

\max\operatorname{spec}(Q_{k,m_{1}})<\min\operatorname{spec}(Q_{k,m_{2}}),\quad m_{1}<m_{2},

(3.44)

and moreover, the spectra
of the polynomials
\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}

also follow each other, that is,

\max\operatorname{spec}(\tilde{P}_{k_{1}}\cdot Q_{k_{1}})<\min\operatorname{spec}(\tilde{P}_{k_{2}}\cdot Q_{k_{2}}),\quad k_{1}<k_{2}.

(3.45)

(This property is referred to as “separation of spectra” in
[
KO01
, Section 2.3]
.)

We now define

\Lambda:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{spec}(\tilde{P}_{k}\cdot Q_{k}),

(3.46)

then each point
\lambda\in\Lambda
has a unique representation as

\lambda=\sigma(n)+m\nu_{k},\quad k\geqslant 1,\quad m\in\operatorname{spec}(P_{k}),\quad 1\leqslant n\leqslant N_{k}.

(3.47)

The set
\Lambda
is uniformly discrete due to (
3.3
).

3.8.

For
\lambda\in\Lambda
given by (
3.47
)
we define a function
h_{\lambda}^{*}\in L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
by

h_{\lambda}^{*}(t):=\overline{d_{n,k}\widehat{P}_{k}(m)}\,\psi_{k}(t).

(3.48)

We will prove that if the elements of
\Lambda

are enumerated
as
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

by increasing order, that is,
\lambda_{j+1}>\lambda_{j}
,
then each

f\in L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
admits a series expansion

f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle f,h_{\lambda_{j}}^{*}\rangle\gamma(t)e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}

(3.49)

convergent in the
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})
norm.

Indeed, using (
3.9
), (
3.13
),
(
3.44
), (
3.45
) and (
3.48
),
it follows that any partial sum of the series (
3.49
)
can be decomposed as
S^{\prime}+S^{\prime\prime}+S^{\prime\prime\prime}
where

S^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\langle f,\psi_{s}\rangle\gamma(t)\tilde{P}_{s}(t)Q_{s}(t),

(3.50)

S^{\prime\prime}(t)=\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)S_{l}(P_{k})(\nu_{k}t),

(3.51)

and

S^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle\gamma(t)\widehat{P}_{k}(l+1)S_{r}(Q_{k,l+1})(t)

(3.52)

for some
k
,
l
and
r
.

We have
\|f-S^{\prime}\|=o(1)
as
k\to\infty
due to (
3.40
).

To estimate
S^{\prime\prime}
we recall the property

(iv)
of the polynomial
P_{k}
, that is,
the partial sum
S_{l}(P_{k})

can be decomposed as
A_{k,l}+B_{k,l}
so that
(
3.14
) holds. Let

\tilde{A}_{k,l}(t)=A_{k,l}(\nu_{k}t),\quad\tilde{B}_{k,l}(t)=B_{k,l}(\nu_{k}t),

(3.53)

then from (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and using
(
3.6
) we obtain

\|\tilde{A}_{k,l}\|_{L^{\infty}(E_{k})}<2,\quad\|\tilde{B}_{k,l}\|_{L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})}<\varepsilon_{k}.

(3.54)

We now have

\|S^{\prime\prime}\|\leqslant|\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle|\cdot(\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\cdot\tilde{A}_{k,l}\|+\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\cdot\tilde{B}_{k,l}\|).

(3.55)

To estimate the first term we split the norm as the sum of two integrals

\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\cdot\tilde{A}_{k,l}\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)\tilde{A}_{k,l}(t)|^{2}u(t)dt=\int_{E_{k}}+\int_{E_{k}^{\complement}}.

(3.56)

Then using (
3.10
), (
3.54
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{E_{k}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\int_{E_{k}}|\gamma(t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}u(t)dt\leqslant 4\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\|^{2}\leqslant 4\|\gamma_{k-1}\cdot Q_{k}\|^{2}

(3.57)

\displaystyle\leqslant 4(\|\varphi_{k}\|+\eta_{k})^{2}=4(1+\eta_{k})^{2}<16,

(3.58)

while using (
3.19
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{E_{k}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{E_{k}^{\complement}}|\gamma_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)|^{2}u(t)dt\leqslant\|\gamma_{k-1}\cdot Q_{k}\|^{2}<4

(3.59)

(the last inequality is established in the same way as (
3.58
)).
Hence

\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\cdot\tilde{A}_{k,l}\|^{2}=\int_{E_{k}}+\int_{E_{k}^{\complement}}<20.

(3.60)

To estimate the second term in (
3.55
)
we use (
3.12
)(b), (
3.54
) to obtain

\|\gamma\cdot Q_{k}\cdot\tilde{B}_{k,l}\|^{2}\leqslant\|Q_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\|\tilde{B}_{k,l}\|^{2}\leqslant\|Q_{k}\|^{2}_{\infty}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}<\eta_{k}^{2}.

(3.61)

We conclude from
(
3.55
), (
3.60
), (
3.61
)
and (
3.41
) that

\|S^{\prime\prime}\|\leqslant 10|\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle|=o(1),\quad k\to\infty.

(3.62)

Finally we estimate
S^{\prime\prime\prime}
using the property

(i)
of the polynomial
P_{k}

and (
3.12
)(b), as

\displaystyle|S^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\psi_{k}(f)|\cdot\|\widehat{P}_{k}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|S_{r}(Q_{k,l+1})\|_{\infty}

(3.63)

\displaystyle\leqslant|\psi_{k}(f)|\cdot\varepsilon_{k}\cdot\|\widehat{Q}_{k}\|_{1}\leqslant|\psi_{k}(f)|\cdot\eta_{k}.

(3.64)

Hence, using again (
3.41
) and
the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\leqslant 1
, we obtain

\|S^{\prime\prime\prime}\|\leqslant|\psi_{k}(f)|\cdot\eta_{k}=o(1),\quad k\to\infty

(3.65)

as well.

We thus conclude that

\|f-(S^{\prime}+S^{\prime\prime}+S^{\prime\prime\prime})\|\leqslant\|f-S^{\prime}\|+\|S^{\prime\prime}\|+\|S^{\prime\prime\prime}\|=o(1),\quad k\to\infty

(3.66)

which shows that the partial sums of the series
(
3.49
) indeed converge to
f
in the norm.

3.9.

Now we can finish the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
Define
w(t):=u(t)\gamma(t)^{2}
, then
w
is
a weight and
w(t)\leqslant w_{0}(t)
a.e. due to (
3.2
).
The mapping
(Uf)(t):=f(t)/\gamma(t)

is a unitary operator
L^{2}_{u}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
,
which maps the weighted exponential system

\{\gamma(t)e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

onto the unweighted system

\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
Hence if we take
e_{j}^{*}:=U(h_{\lambda_{j}}^{*})
then
we can conclude that
for every
f\in L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
, the series expansion
(
3.1
) is valid with the
convergence in the
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
norm.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
,
and consequently Theorem
1.1
also follows.

4.
Remarks

In this section we present some extensions
of Theorem
1.1
and give additional remarks.

4.1.

As in
[
Ole97
]
, the function
g
in Theorem
1.1

may be chosen to be infinitely smooth. This can be achieved
by choosing the weight
u(t)
in (
3.2
) to be fast decreasing.
On the other hand, according to

[
OSSZ11
, Proposition 2.2]
, if a system of uniformly discrete translates
of a function
g
forms a Schauder frame in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
then
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g|=+\infty
, so that
g
cannot have fast decay.

4.2.

It follows from (
3.3
), (
3.47
)
that the sequence
\{\lambda_{j}\}
in Theorem
1.1

is obtained by a small perturbation of a certain subsequence
\{n_{j}\}

of the positive integers. In fact, we can
have
\lambda_{j}=n_{j}+o(1)
. This can be deduced from
the fact that in Lemma
2.1
, we may assume that any subsystem

\{e^{2\pi i\sigma(n)t}\}_{n>N}

is still complete in
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})

(see also
[
Lev25
]
).

On the other hand, note that the perturbations cannot decay arbitrarily fast.
In particular, one cannot have

\lambda_{j}=n_{j}+O(n_{j}^{-\alpha})
,
\alpha>0
,
see
[
KO03
, Section 4.1]
.

4.3.

As in
[
KO01
]
,
[
NO09
]
, the
sequence
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{j}\}
in Theorem
1.1

may be chosen to be not only
uniformly discrete, but even lacunary,
that is, it can satisfy the condition

\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\to+\infty,\quad j\to+\infty.

(4.1)

Moreover,
\Lambda
can have any “subexponential” growth,
i.e. it can satisfy
\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_{j}>1+\varepsilon_{j}

where
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}
is any pregiven positive sequence
tending to zero, no matter how slowly.
The latter condition is sharp, that is,
it cannot be replaced by the Hadamard lacunarity condition

\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_{j}>c>1

(see
[
OU16
, Section 11.4.1]
).

4.4.

We can add to the statement of Theorem
1.1
the condition
that the coefficients of the series expansion
(
1.5
) satisfy for every
q>2
the inequality

\big(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle f,g_{n}^{*}\rangle|^{q}\big)^{1/q}\leqslant K_{q}\|f\|,

(4.2)

where
K_{q}
is a constant which depends on
q

but does not depend on
f
. This strengthens the result
from
[
NO09
]
. The condition
is sharp, that is, (
4.2
) cannot hold for
q=2
.

Indeed, to get condition (
4.2
) it suffices
to fix a sequence
q_{k}>2
,
q_{k}\to 2
, and
choose the polynomial
P_{k}
in (
3.13
)
to satisfy

\|\widehat{P}_{k}\|_{q_{k}}\|\widehat{Q}_{k}\|_{q_{k}}<2^{-k}

using Lemma
2.2
(i)
.

To the contrary, if (
4.2
) was true with

q=2
, it would imply that the restriction mapping

f\mapsto f|_{[0,1]}
is a compact operator

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}([0,1])

(see the proof of
[
FOSZ14
, Proposition 5.1]
)
which is certainly not the case.
Hence (
4.2
) cannot hold for
q=2
.

4.5.

By the uniform boundedness principle,
if
\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is a Schauder frame in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

then the coefficient functionals
\{g_{n}^{*}\}

must be uniformly bounded. We observe that
the proof of Theorem
1.1
above, in fact yields a sequence

\{g_{n}^{*}\}
such that
\|g_{n}^{*}\|\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.
On the other hand, we note that it is also possible to choose

\{g_{n}^{*}\}
to be
seminormalized
, that is, to satisfy

A\leqslant\|g_{n}^{*}\|\leqslant B

where
A,B
are positive constants not depending on
n

(while at the same time satisfying (
4.2
)
for every
q>2
).

To this end we choose the weight
u(t)
in (
3.2
)
such that
u(t)\leqslant|\chi(t)|^{2}
, where
\chi

is the Fourier transform of a nonzero function supported
on
[-\frac{1}{10},\frac{1}{10}]
.
As in
[
NO09
]
this ensures that the exponentials

\{e^{2\pi i\lambda_{j}t}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

form a Bessel sequence in
L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
,
hence the translates
\{T_{\lambda_{n}}g\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

form a Bessel sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

(see e.g.
[
You01
, Section 4.2]
for the definition
of a Bessel sequence and its basic properties).
It then follows by an application of
[
BC20
, Lemma 2.4]

(where we take
\{g^{\prime}_{i}\}
to be any orthonormal system, say)
that the sequence
\{g_{n}^{*}\}
can be replaced with another
seminormalized sequence, such that

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
remains a Schauder frame.
It can be checked that (
4.2
) remains valid as well,
possibly with a different constant
K_{q}
.

4.6.

The statement of
Theorem
1.1
can be strengthened so that
not only the system

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

forms a Schauder frame in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
but also the system

\{(g_{n}^{*},T_{\lambda_{n}}g)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

(where the translates serve as the coefficient functionals)
is at the same time another Schauder frame in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

This can be achieved by choosing the sequences

\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
and
\{\nu_{k}\}
with a sufficiently
fast decay and growth respectively.

We note that the Schauder frame property of the system

\{(g_{n}^{*},T_{\lambda_{n}}g)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is not an automatic consequence, since the Schauder frame

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

is not unconditional
(compare with
[
FOSZ14
, Corollary 3.3]
and
[
LT25
, Theorem 4.2]
).

4.7.

Theorem
1.1
is true also in higher dimensions,
that is:

Theorem 4.1
.

There is a uniformly discrete sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and there are
functions
g
and
\{g_{n}^{*}\}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
such that every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
admits a series expansion
(
1.5
) where the convergence is in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm.

This can be deduced from
Theorem
1.1
using cartesian/tensor products.

4.8.

We conclude the paper with some remarks on

L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
spaces.

It is known

[
FOSZ14
]
that in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
p>2
,
there exist
unconditional
Schauder frames
of translates, i.e. of the form

\{(T_{\lambda_{n}}g,g_{n}^{*})\}
where
g\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,

\{\lambda_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and

\{g_{n}^{*}\}
are continuous linear functionals on
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover,
\{\lambda_{n}\}
may be chosen to
be an arbitrary unbounded sequence, and in particular,
it may increase arbitrarily fast.

To the contrary, we proved in
[
LT25
]
that
none of the spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
1\leqslant p\leqslant 2
,
admits an unconditional Schauder frame
consisting of translates.

In
[
FPT21
, Section 4]
,
Schauder frames (not unconditional)
formed by non-discrete translates
were constructed in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
1\leqslant p<\infty
.

The problem as to whether Schauder frames formed
by a
uniformly discrete
sequence of translates
may exist in the space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
1<p<2
, is addressed
in our subsequent paper
[
LT24
]
.
Theorem
1.1
solves the problem
affirmatively for the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Note that in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, a system of uniformly discrete
translates cannot even be
complete, see e.g.
[
OSSZ11
, Theorem 1.7]
.

It is also not known whether the space

L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
,
1<p<\infty
, admits
a Schauder basis formed by translates of a single function.
(If so, then the translates must be
uniformly discrete
[
OZ92
, Theorem 1]
).
It is known that there are no
unconditional Schauder bases of translates
in any of these spaces, see
[
FOSZ14
]
.
