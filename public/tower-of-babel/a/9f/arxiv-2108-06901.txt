Collective behaviour of swarmalators on a 1D ring
Kevin O’Keeffe, Steven Ceron, Kirstin Petersen

Abstract
We study the collective behavior of swarmalators, generalizations of phase oscillators that both sync and swarm, confined to move on a 1D ring. This simple model captures some of the essence of movement in 2D or 3D but has the benefit of being solvable: most of the collective states and their bifurcations can be specified exactly. The model also captures the behavior of real-world swarmalators which swarm in quasi-1D rings such as bordertaxic sperm and vinegar eels.

II
Model

We study a pair of modified Kuramoto models,

\displaystyle\dot{x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{i}+\frac{J}{N}\sum_{j}^{N}\sin(x_{j}-x_{i})\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j}^{N}\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})\cos(x_{j}-x_{i})

(2)

where
(x_{i},\theta_{i})\in(S^{1},S^{1})
are the position and phase of the
i
-th swarmalator for
i=1,\dots,N
and (
\nu_{i},\omega_{i}
),
(J,K)
are the associated natural frequencies and couplings. We consider identical swarmalators
(\omega_{i},\nu_{i})=(\omega,\nu)
and by a change of frame set
\omega=\nu=0
WLOG.

Eq. (
2
) models position-dependent synchronization. The familiar Kuramoto sine term minimizes swarmalators’ pairwise phase difference (so they sync) while the new cosine term strengthens the coupling between nearby swarmalators
K_{ij}=K\cos(x_{j}-x_{i})
(so the sync is position dependent). Eq. (
1
) is Eq. (
2
)’s mirror-image: it models phase-dependent swarming. Now the sine term minimizes swarmalators’ pairwise distances (so they swarm / aggregate) and the cosine term strengthens the coupling between similarly phased swarmalators
J_{ij}=J\cos(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})
. You can also think of Eqs.(
1
) and (
2
) as modelling synchronization on the unit torus.

Converting the trig functions to complex exponentials and rearranging makes the model even simpler

\displaystyle\dot{x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{2}\Big(S_{+}\sin(\Phi_{+}-(x_{i}+\theta_{i}))+S_{-}\sin(\Phi_{-}-(x_{i}-\theta_{i}))\Big)

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{2}\Big(S_{+}\sin(\Phi_{+}-(x_{i}+\theta_{i}))-S_{-}\sin(\Phi_{-}-(x_{i}-\theta_{i}))\Big)

(4)

where

W_{\pm}=S_{\pm}e^{i\Phi_{\pm}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}e^{i(x_{j}\pm\theta_{i})}.

(5)

The terms
x_{i}\pm\theta_{i}
occur naturally so we define

\displaystyle\xi_{i}=x_{i}+\theta_{i}

(6)

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=x_{i}-\theta_{i}

(7)

And find

\displaystyle\dot{\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=J_{+}S_{+}\sin(\Phi_{+}-\xi)+J_{-}S_{-}\sin(\Phi_{-}-\eta)

(8)

\displaystyle\dot{\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=J_{+}S_{+}\sin(\Phi_{+}-\xi)+J_{+}S_{-}\sin(\Phi_{-}-\eta)

(9)

where
J_{\pm}=(J\pm K)/2

3

(J,K)
appear

We see ring swarmalators obey a sum of two Kuramoto models where the traditional order parameter
Re^{i\Phi}:=(N)^{-1}\sum_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
has been replaced by a pair of new order parameters
W_{\pm}=S_{\pm}e^{i\Phi_{\pm}}=(N)^{-1}\sum_{j}e^{i(x_{j}\pm\theta_{j})}
. The new
W_{\pm}
measure the system’s total amount of ‘space-phase order‘.

What kind of order is this? The limiting cases are trivial: static sync,
(x_{i},\theta_{i})=(x^{*},\theta^{*})
, which produces maximal order
S_{\pm}=1
(which follows from substitution into Eq.(
5
)), and static async, in which positions

are fully uncorrelated with phases
\theta_{i}
, which produces minimal order

(S_{+},S_{-})=(0,1)
or
(S_{+},S_{-})=(0,1)

4

4

. If

, then

.
. What does

mean physically? Picture swarmalators on a ring as a group of fireflies flying around a circular track, flashing periodically. Let
\theta_{i}=0
be the beginning of their phase cycle (when they flash). Then

means each firefly flashes at the same point on its circular lap
x_{i}=c
. Plotting swarmalators as colored dots in space (where color represents phase) illustrates this behavior most evocatively. Then

corresponds to a colored splay state (Fig

(c), Fig
3
(c)). Since one of
S_{\pm}
are maximal in this rainbow-like state, we call

As we will show,

Motivation for model
. Before showing our analysis, we quickly show how the ring model connects to the original 2D model
O’Keeffe et al. 2017
. The 2D model is

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{att}}(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i})F(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})-\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{rep}}(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\Big],

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta_{i}}=\omega_{i}+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}H_{\mathrm{\mathrm{att}}}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})G(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i})

(11)

We pick out the rotational component of the swarming motion – since that part is analogous to movement on a ring – by converting Eqs.(
10

11

\displaystyle\dot{r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\nu}(r_{i})+\frac{J}{2}\Bigg[\tilde{S}_{+}\cos\Big(\Phi_{+}-\xi_{i}\Big)+\tilde{S}_{-}\cos\Big(\Phi_{-}-\eta_{i}\Big)\Bigg]

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\omega}(r_{i})+\tilde{J}_{+}(r_{i})\tilde{S}_{+}\sin\Big(\Psi_{+}-\xi_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\tilde{J}_{-}(r_{i})\tilde{S}_{-}\sin\Big(\Psi_{-}-\eta_{i}\Big)

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\omega}(r_{i})+\tilde{J}_{-}(r_{i})\tilde{S}_{+}\sin\Big(\Psi_{+}-\xi_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\tilde{J}_{+}(r_{i})\tilde{S}_{-}\sin\Big(\Psi_{-}-\eta_{i}\Big)

(14)

where we have switched to
(\xi_{i},\eta_{i})=(\phi_{i}+\theta_{i},\phi_{i}-\theta_{i})
coordinates and
\phi_{i}
is the spatial angle of the
i
-th swarmalator (which is analogous to
x_{i}
in the ring model). We put the derivation and definitions of the various new quantities in the Appendix because they are cumbersome and uninformative to display here.

Eqs. (
13
), (
14
) reveal the ring model hidden in the 2D model’s core. The
(\dot{\xi}_{i},\dot{\eta}_{i})
equations have the same form as the
(\dot{\xi_{i}},\dot{\eta_{i}})
equations in the 1D model (Eqs. (
8
), (
9
)); both are a summed pair of Kuramoto models. The only difference is that in the 2D model,

and
\tilde{J}_{\pm}(r)
depend on

. So the ring model is like the 2D model with the radial dynamics turned off. This is why we think studying the ring model will yield hints on how to study the 2D model (one of the paper’s aims).

IV
Match to real world swarmalators

Swarmalators are defined as entities with a two-way interaction between swarming and synchronization
O’Keeffe et al. 2017
. Below we list examples which (i) appear to meet this definition
9
9

9

We say ‘appear’ to meet these definition because what is means to prove a bidirectional space-phase coupling in an experimental system is somewhat ambiguous. Most experimental studies of swarmalators either infer a space-phase coupling exists based on the observations (like the microswimmers
Quillen
et al. 2021a
;
Yang et al. 2008
;
Yuan et al. 2014
we describe;
Yuan et al. 2014
do a particularly comprehensive study), but do not specify if the coupling bidirectional. Other studies demonstrate a two-way coupling in the sense that they encoding it in a model that produces behavior similar to observed data (like magnetic domain walls
Hrabec et al. 2018
or myxobacteria
Igoshin et al. 2001
). In other words, we’re trying to be conservative in any claims we make.
, (ii) swarm in ring-like geometries, and (iii) display collective behavior similar to the ring model.

Sperm
are a classic microswimmer which swarm in solution and sync their tail gaits
Yang et al. 2008
. Sperm collected from ram semen and confined to 1D rings bifurcate from an isotropic state (analogues to the static async state) to a vortex state in which sperm rotate either clockwise or anticlockwise
Creppy et al. 2016
which implies their positions and orientations are splayed just like the static phase wave (recall the static phase wave is really a state of uniform rotation and only static in the frame co-moving with the natural frequencies
\omega,\nu
)
Creppy et al. 2016
. Moreover, the transition has associated transient decay of rotation velocity (see Fig 4a in
Creppy et al. 2016
) consistent with a Hopf bifurcation, just like the ring model (
74
). Note unlike other studies of syncing sperm
Yang et al. 2008
, here the phase variable is the sperm’s orientation, not its tail rhythm.

Vinegar eels
are a type of nematode found in beer mats and the slime from tree wounds
Quillen
et al. 2021a
;
Quillen
et al. 2021b
. They can be considered swarmalators because they sync the wriggling of their heads, swarm in solution, and it seems likely based on their behavior said sync and swarming interact
Quillen
et al. 2021a
;
Quillen
et al. 2021b
(neighbouring eels sync more easily than distant eels, so sync interacts with swarming, and sync’d eels presumable affect each local hydrodynamic environment and thereby affect each other’s movements, so swarming interacts with sync). When confined to 2D disks, they seek out the 1D ring boundary forming metachronal waves in which the phase of their gait and their spatial positions around the ring are splayed similar to the static phase wave
Quillen
et al. 2021a
;
Quillen
et al. 2021b
(although note the winding number for the metachronal waves is
k>1
; a full rotation in physical space
x

k>1
rotations in phase
\theta

C. elegans
are another type of microswimmer which also swarm and sync the gait of their tails. When confined to 1D channels they form synchronous clusters analogous to the static sync state
Yuan et al. 2014
. Though not strictly consistent with the ring model, a channel being a 1D line as opposed to a 1D ring, we mention them here because it is natural to expect sync clusters would persist in a 1D ring too.

V
Discussion

Aim one of the paper was to model real-world swarmalators swarming in 1D. This was a success. The model captured the behavior of vinegar eels, sperm, and C. elegans. Even so, the model failed to capture the phenomenology of other 1D swarmalators such as the the two-cluster states of bordertaxic Japanese tree frogs
Aihara et al. 2014
