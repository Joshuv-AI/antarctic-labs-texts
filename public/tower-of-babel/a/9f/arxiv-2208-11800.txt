No ( ( , , , , , ) ) ((n,K,d<127)) code can violate the quantum Hamming bound
Emanuel Dallas, Faidon Andreadakis, Daniel Lidar

Abstract
It is well-known that non-degenerate quantum error correcting codes (QECCs) are constrained by a quantum version of the Hamming bound. Whether degenerate codes also obey such a bound, however, remains a long-standing question with practical implications for the efficacy of QECCs. We employ a combination of previously derived bounds on QECCs to demonstrate that a subset of all codes must obey the quantum Hamming bound. Specifically, we combine an analytical bound due to Rains with a numerical bound due to Li and Xing to show that no ( ( , , , , , ) ) ((n,K,d)) code with d < 127 d<127 can violate the quantum Hamming bound.



I
Introduction

The field of quantum computation has recently exploded with a wide range of exciting results regarding the practical applicability of quantum algorithms and the ability to realize ever-growing quantum processor sizes
Preskill 2018
.
Naturally, meaningful large-scale realizations of quantum algorithms depend on the ability to achieve fault tolerant computation
Campbell et al. 2017
.

Unfortunately for this endeavor, it is extremely difficult to implement quantum circuits with fidelities large enough to achieve useful computational tasks
Sanders et al. 2020
. Environmental noise and imperfect physical implementations of unitary gates lead to errors, which tend to propagate and grow throughout circuits
Suter and Álvarez 2016
.

Various methods have been developed to render functional and scaled up quantum circuitry possible in spite of this noise
Lidar and Brun 2013
.
Error correction requires redundantly encoding information to ensure that it is fully recoverable after being damaged by some particular set of errors.
Quantum error correction is a general and robust method for reducing the effects of noise
Shor 1995
;
Steane 1996
.
An
((n,K,d))
quantum error-correcting code (QECC) is a
K=q^{k}
dimensional subspace of the Hilbert space of
n

q
-level particles that is characterized by its distance
d
, the largest integer such that the code can detect any error that acts non-trivially on at most
d-1
such particles
Calderbank and Shor 1996
. The most common case is
q=2
, where each particle is a qubit.
In recent years, there has been significant progress in experimentally realizing many-qubit quantum error correction
Chiaverini et al. 2004
;
Reed et al. 2012
;
Andersen et al. 2020
;
Ryan-Anderson et al. 2021
;
Google Quantum AI et al. 2021
;
Krinner et al. 2022
;
Marques et al. 2022
.

For a given quantum information processing task, it would be desirable to maximize both the rate
k/n
and the distance
d
of the QECC one selects. In order to guide these efforts, it is imperative to have an understanding of the space of possible codes in terms of the parameters
((n,K,d))
. Since the advent of quantum error correction, there has existed an active field of research devoted to obtaining bounds on achievable codes for the parameter space of
((n,K,d))

Grassl 2007
. Such bounds quantify the inevitable tradeoff between the rate and the distance.

The quantum Hamming bound (QHB) is one such bound known to be satisfied by non-degenerate codes, which are codes for which linearly independent errors map the codespace to linearly independent subspaces
Ekert and Macchiavello 1996
. It is currently unknown for what set of degenerate codes the QHB holds.
Some results have shown violations of Hamming-like bounds for various codes
DiVincenzo et al. 1998
;
Shor and Smolin 1996
;
Kay 2014
;
Aly et al. 2006
;
Klappenecker and Sarvepalli 2007
;
Klappenecker and Sarvepalli 2008
(which do not immediately bear a clear resemblance to the QHB), but others conjecture that the QHB holds for all quantum codes
Sarvepalli and Klappenecker 2010
;
Calderbank et al. 1998
. Various methods have been employed to calculate constraints on
n
and
d
values for which the QHB may be violated
Rains 1999
;
Sarvepalli and Klappenecker 2010
;
Li and Xing 2010
;
Yu et al. 2010
.
Degenerate QHB-violating codes could be of great practical importance since they would more efficiently correct errors (achieving the same
k,d
for a lower
n
“cost”). However, research on the topic has progressed little in recent years. Most of the results have been negative, reducing the range of
((n,K,d))
values for which codes may exist that would violate the QHB.
In this work we contribute to this effort by combining an analytical bound derived by Rains
Rains 1999
with novel calculations of a numerical bound derived by Li and Xing
Li and Xing 2010
, to show that no
((n,K,d))
code can violate the QHB for
d<127
.

In
section II
, we introduce the statement of the QHB and describe the intuition behind its derivation. In
section III
, we introduce the key bounds we employ in this paper. In
section IV
, we combine these bounds to derive the impossibility of QHB-violating codes of distance
d<127
. Finally, we outline possible extensions of this work.

II
Preliminaries

Let

\mathcal{H}\cong\mathcal{H}_{C}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\perp}\cong\mathbb{C}^{q^{k}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{q^{n-k}}

(1)

be the Hilbert space associated with the quantum system. Here,
n
corresponds to the number of physical
q
-level particles and the codespace
\mathcal{H}_{C}
comprises
k
encoded particles. Vectors in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
correspond to code states. The state
\rho
of the quantum system is a positive semidefinite, unit trace element of the space of bounded linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
(
\rho\geq 0
,
\Tr[\rho]=1
,
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
).

Henceforth, unless otherwise noted, we consider only binary codes in this work, i.e., codes defined over qubits (
q=2
) as opposed to
q
-dimensional systems with
q>2
. Much of the theoretical background we present is easily generalizable to the higher-dimensional setting (e.g., see
section

III.2
), but our numerical results hold only for
q=2
.

For an arbitrary quantum circuit, let
\mathcal{N}
be the completely positive, trace-preserving (CPTP) map representing the noise process. We may write
\mathcal{N}
in the Kraus operator sum representation (OSR)
K. Kraus 1983
as

\mathcal{N}(\rho)=\sum_{j}E_{j}\rho E_{j}^{\dagger},

(2)

where
\{E_{j}\}
is a set of linearly independent “error operators” corresponding to different possible errors acting on the system of qubits
Knill and Laflamme 1997
. For a system of
n
qubits, we may represent the
E_{j}
in the
n
-qubit Pauli basis, spanned by operators of the form
\prod_{m=1}^{n}\sigma_{m}^{i_{m}}
, where
i_{m}\in\{x,y,z,0\}
and
\sigma^{0}\equiv I
, the identity operator. An error of weight
w
is one in which
i_{m}\neq 0
on strictly
w
of the
n
qubits. A detectable error is an error
E_{j}
which maps every state in
\mathcal{H}_{C}
to a state in
\mathcal{H}_{\perp}
. A correctable error is a detectable error which can be “undone” without knowing or destroying the quantum superposition state. Formally, a set of errors
\{E_{j}\}
is correctable by a code
C
iff it obeys the Knill-Laflamme condition
Knill and Laflamme 1997
:

P_{C}E_{i}^{\dagger}E_{j}P_{C}=\gamma_{ij}P_{C}\quad\forall i,j,

(3)

where
\gamma
is a Hermitian matrix. Here
P_{C}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{K}\ket{{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}}\!\bra{{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}}
is the codespace projector from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}_{C}
, and the set
\{\ket{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{C}
. Eq. (
3
) means that orthogonal code states remain orthogonal after the noise map, i.e., the encoded information is not corrupted. The condition for a detectable set of errors replaces Eq. (
3
) by
P_{C}E_{j}P_{C}=\gamma_{j}P_{C}

\forall E_{j}

Knill and Laflamme 1997
.

Consider a
non-degenerate
code
Calderbank et al. 1998
: a code for which different elements of the set of correctable errors map any code state to linearly independent states:

E_{i}\ket{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}\neq E_{j}\ket{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}\;\forall\alpha,\,i\neq j.

(4)

Non-degeneracy is equivalent to
\gamma
in Eq. (
3
) having full rank.

Since the number of linearly independent vectors in a Hilbert space is bounded by the dimension of the space itself,
non-degenerate

((n,K,d))
binary quantum codes obey the quantum Hamming bound
Ekert and Macchiavello 1996
;
Calderbank et al. 1998
:

\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{n}{j}3^{j}\leq 2^{n-k},

(5)

where
t=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor
is the highest error weight for which the code can correct all errors. The QHB can be derived from a simple combinatorial counting argument: it states that the number of linearly independent (LI) states with no errors (
2^{k}
) plus the number of LI states with correctable errors (
2^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\binom{n}{j}3^{j}
) cannot exceed the total number of LI states in the Hilbert space (
2^{n}
).

On the other hand, consider a
degenerate
code. For such a code, distinct correctable errors may have the same effect on the same state. This means that there exist some
i,j
such that:

E_{i}\ket{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}=E_{j}\ket{\bar{\psi}_\alpha}\forall\alpha.

(6)

Degeneracy is equivalent to
\gamma
in Eq. (
3
) being rank-deficient.

Since in the degenerate case the vectors resulting from the action of different errors need
not
be linearly independent, the number of correctable errors may exceed the bound set by the dimension of the Hilbert space. This leaves open the possibility that a degenerate code may violate the quantum Hamming bound.
1
1

1

There exists a related but different dichotomy, between pure and impure codes. Whereas non-degeneracy is concerned with linear independence, purity is about orthogonality. More precisely, if different errors
E_{i}
and
E_{j}
map
\mathcal{H}_{C}
to mutually orthogonal subspaces, we
say that
C
is pure with respect to
\{E_{j}\}
. A pure code must be non-degenerate. For the important class of additive (or stablizer
Gottesman 1997
) codes, i.e., quantum analogues to additive classical codes, derived from orthogonal geometry, “non-degenerate” and “pure” are
equivalent
Calderbank et al. 1998
. In general, a pure code is non-degenerate but the converse need not be true
Calderbank et al. 1998
. Indeed, there exists a non-additive
((7,2,3))
code that is non-degenerate but impure
Cao et al. 2022
.

It is important to note that code degeneracy and impurity are strictly quantum phenomena. Since any classical error is characterized by which bits it flips, all distinct classical errors map a given code state to distinct states. However, in quantum codes, the possibility of phase flips in conjunction with the Hilbert space structure of the code space, allows distinct errors to map to identical states. Interestingly, the first quantum error correcting code ever discovered was a degenerate code
Shor 1995
.

III
Bounds on quantum codes

We make use of two bounds in this work, which we review in this section. The first is the Shadow Enumerator Bound discovered by Rains, that sets a limit on how many errors an arbitrary
n
-qubit code can correct
Rains 1999
.
The second is a bound due to Li and Xing that allows us to calculate an integer
N>0
, given a distance
d
, for which all
((n,K,d))
codes with
n>N
satisfy the QHB
Li and Xing 2010
.

III.1
Rains bound

In
(
Rains 1999
, Theorem 15)
, Rains used shadow enumerator techniques to prove the following:

Theorem 1

If a ((6m-1+l,K,d)) quantum error-correcting code exists for
K>1
with
0\leq l\leq 5
, then

d\leq\begin{cases}2m+1&\text{if }l<5\\
2m+2&\text{if }l=5\end{cases}.

(7)

From the above theorem, by setting
n=6m-1+l
, we have
d\leq\frac{n+4-l}{3}\leq\frac{n+4}{3}
for
0\leq l<5
and
d\leq\frac{n+2}{3}<\frac{n+4}{3}
for
l=5
, i.e., for any code with
n
physical qubits we obtain the bound (which we refer to as the Rains bound):

n\geq 3d-4.

(8)

:
The distance versus number of physical qubits, for both the Rains bound
eq.

8
and the Li-Xing quantity
N(d)

eq.

. All codes that satisfy the Li-Xing bound

also satisfy the QHB. But every code must also satisfy the Rains bound, i.e., lie above the purple line. Therefore, for
d
values where the Rains bound is above
N(d)
, no codes of that distance can violate the QHB. Conversely, if there exists a
d
value where the Rains bound falls below
N(d)
then this would imply the existence of a code that can potentially violate the QHB; we have found no evidence for this up to
d<127
.

III.2
Li-Xing bound

Li and Xing have shown that there exists an integer
N(d,q)
such that any
((n,K,d))_{q}
code for which
n>N
must satisfy the QHB
Li and Xing 2010
, an idea posited earlier by Aly
Aly 2007
. Here
q
again refers to a
q
-level quantum system. As mentioned above, we assume we are dealing with qubits (
q=2
) for simplicity, but the results can be generalized to
q>2
.

Let
P_{i}(x;n)
denote the
i
-th Krawtchouk polynomial, defined as follows:

P_{i}(x;n)\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}\,3^{i-j}\binom{x}{j}\binom{n-x}{i-j},\;i=0,1,\dots

(9)

Here, the
3
comes from
q^{2}-1
, where we have used the above specification of
q=2
. Krawtchouk polynomials are ubiquitous in both classical
MacWilliams and Sloane 1977
and quantum coding theory
Shor and Laflamme 1997
. The following theorem was proved in Ref.
Li and Xing 2010
(which follows from a result in Ref.
Li and Xing 2021
).

Theorem 2

Given an
((n,K,d))
code, define S to be a non-empty subset of
\{0,...,d-1\}
and define
T\equiv\{0,...,n\}
. If the function
f(x;n)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i;n}\,P_{i}(x;n)
satisfies

1.

f_{g;n}>0\ \forall g\in S,\ f_{g;n}\geq 0
otherwise.

2.

f(g;n)\leq 0\ \forall g\in T\setminus S

then
2^{k}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\max\limits_{g\in S}\frac{f(g;n)}{f_{g;n}}
.

Select
f_{g;n}\equiv\big(\sum_{i=0}^{t}P_{i}(g;n)\big)^{2}
. Then
Li and Xing 2010
:

\frac{f(0;n)}{f_{0;n}}=\frac{2^{2n}}{\sum_{j=0}^{t}3^{j}\,\binom{n}{j}}.

(10)

Let us now choose
S\equiv\{0,\dots,d-1\}
. Considering large
n
expansions, Ref.
Li and Xing 2010
showed that

\displaystyle f_{g;n}=(3^{t}/t!)n^{2t}+o(n^{2t})\quad\text{for }g\in S

(11a)

\displaystyle f(g;n)=3^{2n}\,o(n^{t})\quad\text{for }g\in S

(11b)

\displaystyle f(g;n)=0\quad\text{for }g\in\{d,\dots,n\}

(11c)

and

f(0;n)=3^{2n}\left((3^{t}/t!)n^{t}+o(n^{t})\right).

(12)

The asymptotic behavior implies that there exists an integer
N(d)
such that
\forall n>N(d)
:
2
2

2

The same technique, with a different choice of
f_{t;n}
, was used in

\max\limits_{g\in S}\frac{f(g;n)}{f_{g;n}}=\frac{f(0;n)}{f_{0;n}}.

(13)

Li and Xing do not explicitly prove that the minimum
n
for which this condition holds is
N(d)
, but they take this to be true in their numerical calculations of

. We conjecture this in our work as well, and checked the conjecture numerically as explained below.
Succinctly:

\displaystyle N(d)=

(14)

\displaystyle\quad\min\left\{n_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}\bigg|\arg\max\limits_{g\in S}\frac{f(g;n)}{f_{g;n}}=0\;\;\forall n>n_{d}\right\}.

Then,
Theorem 2
ensures that for such

\displaystyle 2^{k}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\,\frac{2^{2n}}{\sum_{j=0}^{t}3^{j}\binom{n}{j}}=\frac{2^{n}}{\sum_{j=0}^{t}3^{j}\binom{n}{j}},

(15)

which
is
the QHB. In other words, by calculating
N(d)
, we are guaranteed that as long as
n>N(d)
, all QECCs with these
n,d
values satisfy the QHB. Also, in Ref.
Li and Xing 2010
it is shown that

N(2m)=N(2m+1)
for all
m\geq 1
. This allows us to limit our calculations to odd

only.

Equation (
14
) provides us with a means to compute

numerically, and the result is shown as the blue dots in
Figure 1

N(d)
values shown in
Figure 1
as follows: first, we generate a list of binomial coefficients with non-negative arguments using the recursive relationship

\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}.

(16)

Next, for a given
d
, we loop over increasing values of

, starting with
n=1
. For each
n
, we calculate
\frac{f(g)}{f_{g}}

\forall g\in S
. If
\arg\max\limits_{g\in S}\frac{f(g)}{f_{g}}=0
, we set

and end the loop for that

. Otherwise, we continue looping. Our method relies on the minimum

for which the condition
(
13
)
is satisfied to coincide with

. To gain confidence that such a hypothesis may have merit, we selected

and checked numerically that the condition (
13
) indeed holds
\forall n\in\{51,499\}
(for

The efficiency of our numerical method allowed us to compute
N(d)
for up to
d=125
, thereby demonstrating non-existence of QHB-violating codes for

. Data collection required one week on a high-performance computing cluster at the University of Southern California’s Center for Advanced
Research Computing (CARC). The scaling of time (

) required to numerically search for

is polynomial in

IV
Results and Discussion

The Rains bound Eq. (
8
) restricts the space of allowed QECCs by providing a lower bound on
n
. Simultaneously, for sufficiently large
n
the Li-Xing bound ensures that the QHB is satisfied. Due to these observations, we numerically find
N(d)
for odd
d
up to
125
by searching for the lowest
n
for which Eq. (
13
) is satisfied, recovering and extending previous results by Li and Xing, who computed
N(d)
up to
d=15

Li and Xing 2010
. Then, in combination with the Rains bound, we rule out the existence of QHB-violating codes with particular distances.
Figure 1
illustrates this point. From the Rains bound, the only possible codes are ones that lie
above
the solid purple line. From the Li-Xing bound, we know that any code lying above the dot-dashed blue line
must obey the QHB
. Therefore, if the Rains bound lies above the Li-Xing bound for a given distance
d
, all existing distance-
d
codes must obey the QHB.

One may worry about the fact that the graphs cross at
d=1
. However, recall that
the distance
d
is the lowest weight of those errors which the code cannot detect, so a code with
d=1
cannot detect (let alone correct) any error.

There is a wide range of possible future work with the above bounds. First, and most directly, higher-powered computers can continually calculate the Li-Xing
N(d)
for larger
d
values; our empirical observation is that the time required scales as
\sim d^{5}
, so this calculation is relatively benign. If
N(d)
exceeds the Rains bound for a particular
d
value, it remains undetermined by this method whether or not there exists a QHB-violating code of distance
d
. In this case, one may employ known linear programming bounds to determine if a given
((n,K,d))
code exists
Rains 1999
;
Gottesman 1997
;
Calderbank et al. 1998
.

Another approach would be to work with the Li-Xing bound analytically. To prove that the QHB holds for all QECCs, one must first rigorously prove the conjectured Eq. (
14
). It is then sufficient, but not necessary, to prove that the derivative of
N(d)
is less than
3
everywhere [recall Eq. (
8
)]. While this holds for our dataset, it is not clear that this should hold for all
d
. One could also attempt to directly analytically demonstrate that
N(d)
is less than the Rains bound for all
d
, regardless of its derivative. Both
N(d)
and its derivative are extremely complicated functions, and either of these results would be truly remarkable.
