Abstract
We design a nonadaptive algorithm that, given oracle access to a function f : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\} which is α \alpha -far from monotone, makes poly ( n , 1 / α ) (n,1/\alpha) queries and returns an estimate that, with high probability, is an O ~ ​ ( n ) \widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n}) -approximation to the distance of f f to monotonicity. The analysis of our algorithm relies on an improvement to the directed isoperimetric inequality of Khot, Minzer, and Safra (SIAM J. Comput., 2018). Furthermore, we rule out a poly ( n , 1 / α ) (n,1/\alpha) -query nonadaptive algorithm that approximates the distance to monotonicity significantly better by showing that, for all constant κ > 0 , \kappa>0, every nonadaptive n 1 / 2 − κ n^{1/2-\kappa} -approximation algorithm for this problem requires 2 n κ 2^{n^{\kappa}} queries. This answers a question of Seshadhri (Property Testing Review, 2014) for the case of nonadaptive algorithms. We obtain our lower bound by proving an analogous bound for erasure-resilient (and tolerant) testers. Our method also yields the same lower bounds for unateness and being a k k -junta.

1.1.1
Approximating the Distance to Monotonicity

We design a nonadaptive
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
-approximation algorithm for the distance to monotonicity
that runs in time polynomial in the number of dimensions,

and
1/\alpha

Our algorithm improves on the

Theorem 1.2

(Approximation Algorithm)
.

There is a nonadaptive (randomized) algorithm that, given a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and oracle access to a Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
which is
\alpha
-far from monotone, makes

\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
-approximation to
\varepsilon_{f}

Our algorithm works by estimating (in addition to the fraction
\nu_{f}
of decreasing edges) the size of a particular matching from a class of matchings parameterized by subsets

and consisting of decreasing edges along the dimensions in
S

, the size of a matching of decreasing edges, divided by

, is a lower bound for
\varepsilon_{f}
. This is because any monotone function

must disagree with
f
on at least one endpoint of each decreasing edge. The important feature of this class of matchings is that the
membership of a given edge in a specified matching can be verified locally by querying
f
on the endpoints of the edge and their neighbors. To analyze our algorithm, we use a slightly improved version of the (robust) directed isoperimetric inequality by Khot et al.
[
45
]
. Our improvements to isoperimetric inequalities of
[
45
]
are discussed in Section
1.1.2
and stated in Theorems
2.9
and
1.3

Intuitively, if the input function

is
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{n})
-close to monotone, then both the fraction of violated edges and the normalized sizes of all matchings considered by our algorithm are below some threshold
\widetilde{\Theta}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{n})
.
We use Theorem
2.9
to show (in Lemma
2.8
) that if
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone, then either
\nu_{f}
is above the threshold or our algorithm is likely to sample some set
S\subseteq[n]
where the corresponding matching
has normalized size
\widetilde{\Omega}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{n})
.
This allows us to get an
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
-approximation to the distance to monotonicity.

1.1.3
Lower Bounds for Monotonicity, Unateness, and Being a
k
-Junta

We show that a slightly better approximation for the distance to monotonicity, specifically, with a ratio of
n^{1/2-\kappa}
for an arbitrarily small constant
\kappa>0,
requires exponentially many queries in
n^{\kappa}
for every nonadaptive algorithm.

Theorem 1.4

(Approximation Lower Bound)
.

For all constant
\kappa\in(0,1/2),
there exist
\alpha=O(1/n^{1-\kappa})
and
\varepsilon=\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
(that is, with
\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}=\Omega(n^{1/2-\kappa})
), for which every nonadaptive algorithm requires more than
2^{n^{\kappa}}
queries to
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
to distinguish functions
f
that are
\alpha
-close to monotone from those that are
\varepsilon
-far
from monotone with probability at least 2/3.

This result, in combination with Theorem
1.2
, answers an open question on the problem of approximating the distance to monotonicity by Seshadhri
[
62
]
for the case of nonadaptive algorithms. It is the first lower bound for this problem, and it rules out nonadaptive algorithms that achieve approximations substantially better than
\sqrt{n}
with
\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\alpha)
queries, demonstrating that Theorem
1.2
is essentially tight.
This bound is exponentially larger than the corresponding lower bound in the standard property testing model and, in fact, than the running time of known algorithms for testing monotonicity. We elaborate on this point in the discussion below on separation.

To obtain Theorem
1.4
, we investigate a variant of the property testing model, called
erasure-resilient
testing. This variant, proposed by Dixit et al.
[
32
]
, is intended to study property testing in the presence of adversarial erasures. An erased function value is denoted by
\bot
. An
\alpha
-erasure-resilient
\varepsilon
-tester for a desired property gets oracle access to a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to{\{0,1,\bot\}}
that has at most an
\alpha
fraction of values erased. The tester has to accept (with probability at least 2/3) if the erasures can be filled in
to ensure that the resulting function has the property and to reject (with probability at least 2/3) if every completion of erasures results in a function that is
\varepsilon
-far from having the property. As observed in
[
32
]
, the query complexity of problems in this model lies between their complexity in the standard property testing model and the tolerant testing model. Specifically, a (standard)
\varepsilon
-tester that, given a parameter
\varepsilon,
accepts functions with the property and rejects functions that are
\varepsilon
-far from the property (with probability at least 2/3), is a special case of an
\alpha
-erasure-resilient
\varepsilon
-tester with
\alpha
set to 0. Importantly for us,
a tolerant tester that, given
\alpha,\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
with
\alpha<\varepsilon
, accepts functions that are
\alpha
-close and rejects functions that are
\varepsilon
-far (with probability at least 2/3) can be used to get an
\alpha
-erasure-resilient
\varepsilon
-tester. The erasure-resilient tester can be obtained by simply filling in erasures with arbitrary values and running the tolerant tester.
We prove a lower bound for erasure-resilient monotonicity testing.

Our method yields lower bounds for two other properties of Boolean functions: unateness, a natural generalization of monotonicity, and being a
k
-junta. A Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
unate
if, for every variable
i\in[n]
, the function is nonincreasing or nondecreasing in that variable.
A function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
k
-junta
if it depends on at most
k
(out of
n
) variables.

We prove the following result on erasure-resilient testing which implies Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 1.5

(Erasure-Resilient Lower Bound)
.

For all constant
\kappa\in(0,1/2),
there exist
\alpha=O(1/n^{1-\kappa})
and
\varepsilon=\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
(that is, with
\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}=\Omega(n^{1/2-\kappa})
), for which every nonadaptive
\alpha
-erasure-resilient
\varepsilon
-tester requires more than
2^{n^{\kappa}}
queries to test monotonicity of functions
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}

n/2
-junta property.

Theorem
1.5
directly implies lower bounds analogous to the one stated in Theorem
1.4
for unateness and being an
n/2
-junta. Lower bounds for approximating the distance to unateness and to being a

-junta have been investigated by Levi and Waingarten
[
50

\widetilde{\Omega}(n)

\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{3/2})

Our lower bounds are exponentially larger than those obtained by Levi and Waingarten
[

1.1.4
Separation

Our lower bounds provide natural properties for which erasure-resilient property testing (and hence, distance approximation) is exponentially harder than standard property testing with nonadaptive algorithms. Previously, such strong separation was only known for artificially constructed properties based on PCPs of proximity
[
37
,
32
]
. For testing monotonicity of Boolean function, the celebrated nonadaptive algorithm of Khot, Minzer and Safra
[
45
]
makes
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
queries. Unateness can be tested nonadaptively with

O(\frac{n}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
queries
[
3
]
whereas the property of being a
k
-junta can be tested nonadaptively with
\widetilde{O}(k^{3/2}/\varepsilon)
queries
[
11
]
. Our lower bound shows that, for all three properties, nonadaptive testers requires exponentially many queries when the ratio
\varepsilon/\alpha
is substantially smaller than
\sqrt{n}.
This stands in contrast to examples of many properties provided in
[
32
]
, for which erasure-resilient testers have essentially the same query complexity as standard testers.

1.2
Comparison to Potential Alternative Approaches to Approximating
\varepsilon_{f}

Chakrabarty and Seshadhri, in a personal communication, notified us of an alternative approach towards an

-approximation
of
\varepsilon_{f}
via estimating the size of a maximal matching of decreasing edges. Results in
[

Given Theorem

The problem is that this estimator could be just as high for functions that are close to monotone. For example, consider the random function

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}(x)=\begin{cases}1-\operatorname{Maj}(x)&\text{with probability }\varepsilon/n;\\
\operatorname{Maj}(x)&\text{with probability }1-\varepsilon/n,\end{cases}

where

denotes the majority function.
Then,
\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{f}}=\Theta(\varepsilon/n)
with high probability, yet the left-hand side of (

\Omega(\varepsilon)
with high probability.

Finally, as thoroughly explained in
[
55

Observation 2.4

For all functions

s\leq|E_{f}^{-}({x})|\leq 2s-1
and

|D_{f}^{-}({x^{(i)}})|\leq U_{f}^{-}(x)=|D_{f}^{-}({x})|\leq 2d-1

to get the second inequality,
d\geq s
to get the third inequality, and
(1-1/d)^{d-1}\geq 1/\mathrm{e}
for all
d\geq 2
to get the final inequality.
This concludes the proof of Claim
2.10
.
∎

By Claim
2.10
, for all
d\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\left\lfloor{\log_{2}n}\right\rfloor}\},

\displaystyle\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{S}\subseteq[n]\\
i\in\boldsymbol{S}\text{ w.p. }1/d\end{subarray}}\left[\mu_{f}(\boldsymbol{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{S}\subseteq[n]\\
i\in\boldsymbol{S}\text{ w.p.}\;1/d\end{subarray}}\Big[\mathop{\Pr}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left[\texttt{Capture}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{S},f)\right]\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\bigcup_{s}B_{d,s}}\ \ \mathop{\Pr}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{S}\subseteq[n]\\
i\in\boldsymbol{S}\text{ w.p.}\;1/d\end{subarray}}\left[\texttt{Capture}(x,{\boldsymbol{S}},f)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{s\in\{1,2,4,\dots,d\}}|B_{d,s}|\cdot\frac{s}{\mathrm{e}^{4}d}\;.

(7)

To conclude the proof of Lemma
2.8
,
we apply Titu’s Lemma, which
states that for all positive real numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\,,

\frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{b_{i}}\,.

Titu’s lemma follows directly from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality:

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{a_{i}}{\sqrt{b_{i}}}\cdot\sqrt{b_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{b_{i}}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}\right)\,.

By (
7
) and Titu’s Lemma,

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{\left\lfloor{\log_{2}n}\right\rfloor}\}}\mathop{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{S}\subseteq[n]\\
i\in\boldsymbol{S}\text{ w.p.}\;1/d\end{subarray}}[\mu_{f}(\boldsymbol{S})]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{d,s:d\geq s}|B_{d,s}|\cdot\frac{s}{\mathrm{e}^{4}d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{4}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,s:d\geq s\\
|B_{d,s}|\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{(|B_{d,s}|\sqrt{s})^{2}}{|B_{d,s}|d}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{4}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{n}}\frac{(\sum_{d,s:d\geq s}|B_{d,s}|\sqrt{s})^{2}}{\sum_{d,s:d\geq s}|B_{d,s}|d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\frac{\Omega((\varepsilon\cdot 2^{n})^{2})}{\varepsilon\sqrt{n/\log n}\cdot 2^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

where we used (
5
) and (
6
) to get the final line. By an averaging argument, since the summation on the left-hand side above has
O(\log n)
terms, at least one of the terms is
\Omega(\varepsilon/(\sqrt{n\log n}))
, completing the proof of Lemma
2.8
.

4
A Nonadaptive Lower Bound for Erasure-Resilient Testers

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.5
that gives a lower bound on the query complexity of erasure-resilient testers of monotonicity, unateness and the
k
-junta property. We prove the lower bound by constructing two distributions
\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\mathcal{D}^{-}
on input functions

that are hard to distinguish for every nonadaptive tester and then applying Yao’s Minimax principle
[
64
]
.

Recall that
\bot
denotes an erased function value. We say that a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1,\bot\}
is
\alpha
-erased
if at most an
\alpha
fraction of its values are erased. If
\alpha
is not specified, we call such a function
partially erased
. A
completion
of a partially erased function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1,\bot\}
is a function
f^{\prime}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
that agrees with
f
on all nonerased values, that is, for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, if
f(x)\neq\bot
then
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)
.
A partially erased function
has property
\cal P
if there exists
a completion of
f
that has
\cal P
. A partially erased function is
\varepsilon
-far from
having property
\cal P
if every completion of
f
is
\varepsilon
-far from
having property
\cal P
.

.

and
\mathcal{D}^{-}
on

-erased functions. Later, we show that

\mathcal{D}^{+}
is over monotone functions whereas most of the probability mass of

is on functions that are
\varepsilon
-far from monotone. Interestingly, the same distributions work to prove our lower bounds for unateness and
k
-juntas: all functions in the support of

are unate (because they are monotone) and also
n/2
-juntas whereas
\mathcal{D}^{-}
is mostly supported on functions that are
\varepsilon
-far from unate and
\varepsilon
-far from
n/2
-juntas. The core of the argument is demonstrating that the two distributions are hard to distinguish for nonadaptive testers that make too few queries.

4.2
Indistinguishability of the Hard Distributions

Next, we show that the distributions
\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
are hard to distinguish for nonadaptive testers.
Consider a deterministic tester that makes
q
queries. Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{q}(f)
be the sequence of query-answer pairs
(x,f(x))
obtained by the tester on input
f
. For every distribution
\cal D
on input functions, define
{\cal D}
-view to be the distribution on
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{f}^{+})
when
\boldsymbol{f}^{+}\sim{\cal D}
. We use the version of Yao’s principle stated in
[
58
]
that asserts that to prove a lower bound
q
on the worst-case query complexity of a randomized algorithm, it is enough to give two distributions
\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-},
on positive and negative instances, respectively, for which
the statistical distance between
\mathcal{D}^{+}
-view and
\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
-view is less than 1/3.

We start by analyzing how a tester can distinguish
\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\mathcal{D}^{-}
. The key point is the only way to do it is by querying a pair of points
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that fall in the same action subcube, but in different nonerased layers—one below the erased layers, the other above the erased layers. If the tester queries no such pair, then its view (that is, the distribution on the sequence of query-answer pairs it obtains) is identical for the two cases:
\boldsymbol{f}^{+}\sim\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{-}\sim\mathcal{D}^{-}
.

Let
BAD
be the event that one of the
\binom{q}{2}
pairs of points the tester queries ends up in the same action subcube, on different sides of erasures, as discussed above. Next, we show that conditioned on
BAD
not occurring, the view of the tester is the same for both distributions.

Claim 4.10
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{+}\text{-view}|_{\overline{\texttt{BAD}}}=\mathcal{D}^{-}\text{-view}|_{\overline{\texttt{BAD}}}.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary fixed set of control dimensions
M\subset[n]
of size
n/2
, and let
\Psi_{M}
be the set of control substrings. Partition the set of queries
x_{1},\dots,x_{q}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with nonerased values according to their control substrings in
\Psi_{M}
; namely, for
z\in\Psi_{M}
, let
Q_{z}\subset\{x_{1},\dots,x_{q}\}
be the set of queries

for which
x_{M}=z
and
x_{\overline{M}}
does not lie in the middle
2n^{\kappa}
layers of its action subcube. This partition depends only on
M
, and not on the set
\boldsymbol{P}_{M}
.

Suppose, furthermore, that any two queries
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
falling in the same action subcube (i.e., from the same part

) are either both above the erased layers or both below the erased layers, and notice that this implies
\boldsymbol{f}(x)=\boldsymbol{f}(y)
whenever
x,y\in Q_{z}
, for
\boldsymbol{f}
sampled from
\mathcal{D}^{+}
as well as
\mathcal{D}^{-}
. We will show that the distribution over answers to queries for a fixed
M
(and a random
\boldsymbol{P}_{M})
is exactly the same for
\mathcal{D}^{+}
and
\mathcal{D}^{-}
. In both cases, the answer to a query

is a function of the control substring

and is independent for two queries

with different control substrings. In fact, for each control substring

the value of every
\boldsymbol{f}(x)
with

is a uniformly random bit.

In order to see why, notice that for
\boldsymbol{f}^{+}\sim\mathcal{D}^{+}
, every
x\in Q_{z}
has value
\boldsymbol{f}^{+}(x)=1
if and only if
z\in\boldsymbol{P}_{M}
, which occurs with probability

. For
\boldsymbol{f}^{-}\sim\mathcal{D}^{-}
, we have two cases. The first case is when
x\in Q_{z}
has
|x_{\overline{M}}|\leq n/4-n^{\kappa}
; then,
\boldsymbol{f}^{-}(x)=1
if and only if
z\in\boldsymbol{P}_{M}
, which occurs with probability

. The second case is when
x\in Q_{z}
has
|x_{\overline{M}}|\geq n/4+n^{\kappa}
; then,
\boldsymbol{f}^{+}(x)=0
if and only if

, which occurs with probability
1/2
.
∎

Claim 4.11
.

For a deterministic nonadaptive tester making
q\leq 2^{n^{\kappa}}
queries,

\Pr[\texttt{BAD}]<1/8

over the draw of
\boldsymbol{M}\subset[n]
of size
n/2.

Proof.

We want to bound the probability over the draw of
\boldsymbol{M}
that any two queries
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
which have the same control substring are either both above the erased layers, or both below the erased layers in their action subcube. In particular, observe that the Hamming weights of any such
x
and
y
must differ by at least

2n^{\kappa}+2.

Consequently,
x
and
y
differ on at least

2n^{\kappa}+2

bits. Next, we upper bound the probability that two queries
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
which differ on at least
2n^{\kappa}+2
dimensions have the same setting of coordinates in
\boldsymbol{M}
.

Let
T=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}
denote the set of all coordinates on which the points
x
and
y
differ. Then
|T|\geq 2n^{\kappa}+2
. Observe that
x_{\boldsymbol{M}}=y_{\boldsymbol{M}}
iff
T\cap\boldsymbol{M}=\emptyset
. Since
\boldsymbol{M}
is a uniformly random subset of
[n]
of size

,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\boldsymbol{M}}[T\cap\boldsymbol{M}=\emptyset]=\frac{\binom{n-|T|}{n/2}}{\binom{n}{n/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{(n-|T|)!}{(n/2)!\cdot(n/2-|T|)!}}{\frac{n!}{(n/2)!\cdot(n/2)!}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n/2)!}{(n/2-|T|)!}\cdot\frac{(n-|T|)!}{n!}

\displaystyle=\frac{n/2\cdot(n/2-1)\cdots(n/2-|T|+1)}{n\cdot(n-1)\cdots(n-|T|+1)}\leq 2^{-|T|}.

Let
BAD
be the event that one of the
\binom{q}{2}
pairs of points the tester queries ends up in the same action subcube, on different sides of erasures, as discussed above. Then, by a union bound,

\Pr[\texttt{BAD}]<\frac{q^{2}}{2}\cdot 2^{-|T|}\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{2n^{\kappa}}\cdot 2^{-2n^{\kappa}-2}=\frac{1}{8}.

∎

Lemma 4.13

For a deterministic nonadaptive tester making

\mathcal{D}^{+}\text{-view}\approx_{9/28}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-}\text{-view}.

Proof.

5
From Tolerant Testing to Distance Approximation

The following theorem shows how to convert a tolerant tester to a distance approximation algorithm.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider input objects whose size is measured with respect to a parameter
n
, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a property of these objects.
Let
g(n)
be a polynomial in
n
such that
g(n)=\omega(1)
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a tolerant tester of the property
\mathcal{P}
that gets a parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and oracle access to an input object
f
, makes
Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,\varepsilon)
queries, and outputs
close
or
far
as follows:

1.

If
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{g(n)}
, then
\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon,f)
outputs
close
with probability at least
2/3
;

2.

If
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\geq\varepsilon
, then
\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon,f)
outputs
far
with probability at least
2/3
.

Suppose
Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,\varepsilon)
has at least linear dependence on
1/\varepsilon
. Then, there exists a distance approximation algorithm
\mathcal{B}
for the property
\mathcal{P}
that, given a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and oracle access to an input object
f
such that
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\geq\alpha
, returns an estimate that, with probability at least
2/3
, is a
2g(n)
-approximation to
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})
. The query complexity of
\mathcal{B}
is
O(Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,\alpha)\cdot\log\log(1/\alpha))
.

Proof.

The distance approximation algorithm
\mathcal{B}
is described in Algorithm
4
.

input :
minimum distance parameter
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
; oracle access to the input object
f
.

output :

2g(n)
approximation
\hat{\varepsilon}
to
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})
.

1

for

i=1
to
\left\lfloor{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\rfloor

do

2

Set
c\leftarrow 0
and
t\leftarrow\left\lceil{18\ln(3\log(1/\alpha))}\right\rceil
.

3

repeat

t

times

4

Run
\mathcal{A}(2^{-i},f)
. If it outputs
far
,
c\leftarrow c+1
.

5

if

c\geq t/2

then

return

\hat{\varepsilon}=2^{-i+1}
.

6

return

\hat{\varepsilon}=\alpha
.

Algorithm 4

Distance Approximation Algorithm
\mathcal{B}(\alpha,f)

For each
i\in\left[\left\lfloor{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right\rfloor\right]
, let
E_{i}
denote the event that
c
calculated by the algorithm in the
{i}^{\text{th}}
iteration of the
for
loop satisfies
c\geq t/2
when
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\geq 2^{-i}/g(n)
and
c<t/2
when
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\leq 2^{-i}
.
Event
\overline{E_{i}}
occurs when
\mathcal{A}
errs in at least
t/2
of its runs in Step
4
.
In each run,
\mathcal{A}
errs with probability at most
1/3
.
By Hoeffding bound, for each
i\in\left[\left\lfloor{\log({1}/{\alpha})}\right\rfloor\right]
, the probability
\Pr[\overline{E_{i}}]<1/(3\log(1/\alpha))
.
Let
E=\bigcap_{i\in[\left\lfloor{\log(1/\alpha)}\right\rfloor]}E_{i}
. By the union bound,
\Pr[\overline{E}]<1/3
, which implies that
\Pr[E]>{2}/{3}
.
For the rest of the proof, condition on the event
E
happening.
If
\hat{\varepsilon}=2^{-i+1}
, then it implies that for all
j<i
,
the algorithm determined that
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\leq 2^{-j}
. Substituting
j=i-1
yields
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\leq 2^{-i+1}=\hat{\varepsilon}
. Similarly, in the
{i}^{\text{th}}
iteration, the algorithm determined that
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\geq 2^{-i}/g(n)=\hat{\varepsilon}/(2g(n))
.
Hence, with probability at least
2/3
, the output
\hat{\varepsilon}
of the algorithm
\mathcal{B}
satisfies
\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})\leq\hat{\varepsilon}\leq 2g(n)\cdot\mathrm{dist}(f,\mathcal{P})
, completing the proof of the approximation guarantee.

The query complexity of
\mathcal{B}
is at most
t\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{\log(1/\alpha)}\right\rfloor}Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,2^{-i}).

Since
Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,\varepsilon)
has at least linear dependence on
1/\varepsilon
, the sum
is dominated by the last term. Therefore, the query complexity is

O(Q_{\mathcal{A}}(n,\alpha)\cdot\log\log(1/\alpha))
.
∎
