Profinite rigidity for free-by-cyclic groups with centre
Martin R. Bridson and Paweł Piwek

Abstract
. A free-by-cyclic group F N ⋊ ϕ ℤ F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z} has non-trivial centre if and only if [ ϕ ] [\phi] has finite order in Out ⁡ ( F N ) {\rm{Out}}(F_{N}) . We establish a profinite ridigity result for such groups: if Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} is a free-by-cyclic group with non-trivial centre and Γ 2 \Gamma_{2} is a finitely generated free-by-cyclic group with the same finite quotients as Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} , then Γ 2 \Gamma_{2} is isomorphic to Γ 1 \Gamma_{1} . One-relator groups with centre are similarly rigid. We prove that finitely generated free-by-(finite cyclic) groups are profinitely rigid in the same sense; the proof revolves around a finite poset 𝐟𝐬𝐜 ⁡ ( G ) {\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G) that carries information about the centralisers of finite subgroups of G G – it is a complete invariant for these groups. These results provide contrasts with the lack of profinite rigidity among surface-by-cyclic groups and (free abelian)-by-cyclic groups, as well as general virtually-free groups.

1.
Introduction

It is unknown whether free-by-cyclic groups
F\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
,
with
F
finitely generated,
can be distinguished from each other by their finite quotients.
Likewise, it is unknown whether
residually-finite 1-relator groups
can be distinguished from each other by their finite quotients.
In this article we will resolve these questions
in the case of groups with non-trivial centre.
For free-by-cyclic groups, the precise statement is as follows:

Theorem A
.

If a pair of free-by-cyclic groups

\Gamma_{1}=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
and
\Gamma_{2}=F_{M}\rtimes_{\phi_{2}}\mathbb{Z}

have the same finite quotients
and
[\phi_{1}]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
has finite order,
then
[\phi_{2}]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{M})
has finite order
and
\Gamma_{1}\cong\Gamma_{2}
.

In interpreting this theorem, it is important to note that

\Gamma_{1}\cong\Gamma_{2}
does not imply that
N=M
.
Moreover, when we restrict to the case
N=M
,
the existence of an isomorphism

F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi_{1}}\mathbb{Z}\cong F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi_{2}}\mathbb{Z}

does not imply that

\Phi_{1}=[\phi_{1}]
is conjugate to
\Phi_{2}=[\phi_{2}]

in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
,
nor does it imply that

\langle\Phi_{1}\rangle
is conjugate to
\langle\Phi_{2}\rangle
.
If
N=M
then we can deduce that

\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
have the same order in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

but if
N\neq M
then this too may fail.
These phenomena are discussed in
Section

7
.

In
Theorem

A
, the quotient of each group by its centre
will be free-by-(finite cyclic),
but it will not be a split extension in general.
The fact that these quotients are virtually free
is not sufficient to guarantee that
they are uniquely determined by their finite quotients,
but we shall prove that
free-by-(finite cyclic) groups are more rigid.
This is a key step in our proof of
Theorem

A

and it is of independent interest.

Theorem B
.

If a pair of finitely generated free-by-(finite cyclic) groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}

have the same finite quotients, then
G_{1}\cong G_{2}
.

A crucial idea in the proof of
Theorem

B

is that free-by-(finite cyclic) groups
G

are determined up to isomorphism by a natural poset
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)

built from the finite subgroups of
G
;
see
Theorem

D
.

Before discussing our approach to these results,
we review some of the context in which they sit.

A group
\Gamma
from a class
\mathcal{C}
is said to be

profinitely rigid among groups in
\mathcal{C}

if any group
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}

with the same finite quotients as
\Gamma

must be isomorphic to
\Gamma
.
If the groups in
\mathcal{C}
are finitely generated,
this can be rephrased as

\widehat{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}\Longrightarrow\Lambda\cong\Gamma
,
where
\widehat{\Gamma}
denotes the profinite completion of
\Gamma
,
i.e. the limit of the inverse system
formed by all of its finite quotients.
In recent years there have been a lot of advances
in the understanding of profinite rigidity
among groups that arise
in low-dimensional geometry and topology.
This provides motivation and context
for our study of free-by-cyclic groups,
in light of the fruitful and much-studied
analogies between automorphisms of free groups
and automorphisms of surface groups
and free-abelian groups –
see
[
Bes02
,
BV06
]
.

There are several noteworthy works on profinite rigidity for
surface bundles and
n
-torus bundles over the circle.
For
2
-torus bundles over the circle, it is known that
profinite rigidity can fail:
Funar
[
Fun13
]
pointed out that
classical results of Stebe
[
Ste72
]

provide infinitely many pairs of hyperbolic matrices

\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in{\rm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{Z})

such that the corresponding torus bundles over the circle
have non-isomorphic fundamental groups

\Gamma_{1}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes_{\phi_{1}}\mathbb{Z}
and
\Gamma_{2}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes_{\phi_{2}}\mathbb{Z}

with
\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
.
In contrast,
Bridson, Reid and Wilton
[
BRW17
]
proved that
the fundamental group of
every once-punctured torus bundle over the circle
is profinitely rigid in the class of 3-manifold groups.
And Liu proved that at most finitely many
hyperbolic 3-manifold groups
can share the same profinite completion
[
Liu23
]
,
adding weight to the expectation
that such groups are profinitely rigid, cf.

[
BMRS21
]
.

The lack of profinite rigidity
among groups of the form
\mathbb{Z}^{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}

was first revealed by Serre
in a seminal paper of 1964
[
Ser64
]
.
Baumslag
[
Bau74
]
stripped the phenomenon
identified by Serre down to its bare bones
and identified pairs of distinct
finite-by-cyclic groups that have the same finite quotients.
Forty years later,
Hempel
[
Hem14
]
adapted a key idea from these works
to construct pairs of 3-manifolds
M_{1},M_{2}

with
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
geometry such that
the groups
\pi_{1}M_{i}=\Sigma\rtimes_{\phi_{i}}\mathbb{Z}
are distinct
but have the same profinite completion;
these manifolds are closed-surface bundles over the circle,
each with finite holonomy and the same fibre.
Hempel also gave examples where
the surface-fibres have non-empty boundary,
but in this case he did not conclude that
the fundamental groups (which now have the form
F\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
)
were not isomorphic, but rather that
there was no isomorphism between them
that preserved the peripheral structure of the 3-manifold;
it was unclear at the time
whether this added restriction was necessary,
but
Theorem

A
shows that it is.

All groups of the form
F_{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
arise as
fundamental groups of punctured-torus bundles over the circle
and so are covered by
[
BRW17
]
,
but when the rank
N
of the free group is at least
3
,
the groups
F_{N}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
form a more complicated class
with more subtle geometry.
In particular, it is unclear what to expect from them
in terms of profinite rigidity.
In the case where
F_{N}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
is hyperbolic,
significant progress towards the analogue of Liu’s result
was made by Hughes and Kudlinska
[
HK23
]
,
but profinite rigidity remains open
even in the hyperbolic setting.
In the light of Hempel’s results, one might expect
profinite rigidity to fail for groups
F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}

where
\Phi=[\phi]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
has finite order,
but
Theorem

A
shows that this is not the case.

Another rich class of groups of geometric dimension 2
is formed by the
torsion-free 1-relator groups
.
It remains unknown whether
residually-finite 1-relator groups
can be distinguished from one another
by their profinite completions,
but
Theorem

C
will allow us to settle this question
in the case where the group has non-trivial centre.

Theorem C
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be residually finite groups
that admit 1-relator presentations.
If
\Gamma_{1}
has a non-trivial centre
and
\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
, then
\Gamma_{1}\cong\Gamma_{2}
.

We shall see in
Section

6

that this theorem would fail
if we did not assume that
\Gamma_{2}
was residually finite.
The passage from
Theorem

A
to
Theorem

C

is made straightforward
by a result of Baumslag and Taylor
[
BT67
]
,
who proved that all 1-relator groups with centre
are free-by-cyclic
F_{N}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
A further much-studied class
in which the members with centre are free-by-cyclic
is formed by the Generalised Baumslag-Solitar (GBS) groups,
i.e. the fundamental groups of
finite graphs of infinite cyclic groups
[
Lev15
]
, an the analogue of
theorem

C
holds in this
setting (Remark
5.13
).

GBS groups play a more important role in this article
than the tone of this last remark might suggest:
our proof of
Theorem

A
begins with a change of perspective
that replaces the visible semidirect decomposition

\Gamma=F\rtimes\mathbb{Z}

with a decomposition of
\Gamma

as a graph of infinite cyclic groups.
We will argue that the groups
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
in
Theorem

A

have the same profinite completion if and only if
the groups modulo their centres
have the same profinite completion.
The groups modulo their centres are free-by-(finite cyclic),
so
Theorem

B
will tell us that

\Gamma_{1}/Z(\Gamma_{1})
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{2}/Z(\Gamma_{2})

if these groups have the same finite quotients.
But we need more from
Theorem

B

than just its statement:
our proof of
Theorem

B
involves
an analysis of graph-of-finite-groups decompositions
of free-by-(finite cyclic) groups,
and what we prove is that
if the groups in
Theorem

B

have the same profinite completion,
then one can transform the graph of groups
representing the first into that representing the second
by a sequence of
slide moves
.
In the case
G_{i}=\Gamma_{i}/Z(\Gamma_{i})
,
these slide moves can be lifted
to the graph-of-groups decompositions
associated to the description of the
\Gamma_{i}
as GBS groups,
proving that
\Gamma_{1}\cong\Gamma_{2}
.

There are several features of this outline of proof
upon which we should expand.
First, although
Theorem

A
was our original motivation,

Theorem

B
emerges as a key result.
In the case where
the finite cyclic group is of prime-power order,

Theorem

B

was proved by Grunewald and Zalesskii –
see Theorem 3.12 of
[
GZ11
]
;
their proof relies on
the subgroup structure of cyclic
p
-groups
and uses a decomposition of
F_{n}
-by-
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{\alpha})
groups
that is not available in the general case.
It is important to note here that although one expects
a proof of
Theorem

B

to use arguments about graphs of finite groups,
it must avoid the fact that
virtually free groups are not profinitely rigid in general,
and hence one needs to use
graphs with special features or enhancements.

In our outline of proof,
we spoke about the graph-of-groups decompositions
of the groups
G_{i}

in a manner that might suggest they are reasonably canonical.
In fact, the uniqueness issue is somewhat delicate,
as readers familiar with
deformation spaces of group actions on trees will anticipate.
Correspondingly, the main object of study
in our proof of
Theorem

B
is
the entirely canonical
finite subgroups conjugacy poset
,
denoted by
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)
.
The elements of this poset
are the conjugacy classes of finite subgroups in
G

and these classes are labelled
by the first Betti number of the subgroup’s centraliser
as well as the size of the subgroup.
As one might expect with such a natural object,
versions of this poset have been studied elsewhere,
for example in
[
CHMV
]

where it is used to prove that graph products of finite groups
are profinitely distinguished from each other
(although in that setting it was not necessary
to retain information about centralisers).

Theorem D
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be finitely generated
free-by-(finite cyclic) groups.
Then
G_{1}\cong G_{2}
(equivalently,
\widehat{G}_{1}\cong\widehat{G}_{2}
)
if and only if there is a data-preserving isomorphism
of posets
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\cong{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})
.

Furthermore, the isomorphism
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\cong{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})

can be realised by a sequence of slide moves
transforming any realisation of
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})

as a reduced graph of finite cyclic groups
into a realisation of
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})
.

The fact that the isomorphism
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\cong{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})

can be exhibited through
slide moves

is crucial for the passage
from
Theorem

B
to
Theorem

A
.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Following some preliminaries,
in
Section

3
we study
the GBS decomposition of free-by-cyclic groups with centre,
explaining how the centre is encoded in this decomposition.
In
Section

4
we use this structure
to reduce
Theorem

A

to the study of
the profinite completions of free-by-(finite cyclic) groups,
whose decompositions we study in
Section

4.3
.
In
Section

5.1
we introduce a key technical device—the poset associated to a numbered graph—and prove a result that will enable us
to promote isomorphisms
to sequences of sliding moves in various contexts.
In
Section

5.2
we translate these results into
more algebraic terms, introducing
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)

and proving
Theorem

D
.
Following an interlude in
Section

5.3

gathering some conclusions from profinite Bass-Serre theory,
we are able to complete the proof of our main theorems
in
Section

5.4
.

Theorem

C
is proved in
Section

6

along with a companion result
that illustrates the need to restrict attention
to residually finite groups.
In the final section we discuss
some non-obvious isomorphisms
F_{N}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}\cong F_{M}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Profinite completions

The reader will require only
a rudimentary knowledge of profinite groups;
when we require non-elementary facts we will provide references.
The
profinite completion

\widehat{\Gamma}

of an abstract group
\Gamma

is the limit of the inverse system of finite quotients of
\Gamma
;
it is a compact topological group.
The natural map
\Gamma\to\widehat{\Gamma}
is injective
if and only if
\Gamma
is residually finite,
and we identify
\Gamma
with its image.
Every homomorphism of abstract groups
\phi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}

extends uniquely to a continuous homomorphism

\widehat{\phi}:\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\to\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
.

If
\Gamma
is finitely generated and
Q
is finite,
then every epimorphism
\widehat{\Gamma}\to Q
is continuous
by
[
NS03
]

and restriction to the dense subgroup
\Gamma
induces a bijection

{\rm{Epi}}(\widehat{\Gamma},Q)\to{\rm{Epi}}({\Gamma},Q)
.
For each natural number
d
,
there is a bijection between the subgroups of index
d
in
\Gamma

and those of index
d
in
\widehat{\Gamma}
;
this assigns to each
\Lambda<\Gamma

its closure
\overline{\Lambda}<\widehat{\Gamma}
,
and
\overline{\Lambda}\cong\widehat{\Lambda}
.
It follows that
if
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are finitely generated groups
with
\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}=\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}
,
then there is a 1-1 correspondence

\Lambda\leftrightarrow\overline{\Lambda}\cap\Gamma_{2}

between the subgroups of finite index in
\Gamma_{1}

and those in
\Gamma_{2}
,
such that corresponding subgroups have the same index
and the same profinite completion.

2.2.
Graphs of groups

We shall assume that the reader is familiar
with the ideas and terminology of
Bass-Serre theory

[
Ser80
]
,
and with the graph-of-spaces approach to the subject
developed by Scott and Wall
[
SW79
]
.
We will typically denote a graph of groups by
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
,
or simply
\mathbb{G}
.
The underlying graph
(V,E)
will always be connected.
The standard involution on
E
will be denoted

e\mapsto\overline{e}

and the endpoint maps are
o
and
\tau
;
so
\tau(\overline{e})=o(e)
.
We write
G_{e}=G_{\overline{e}}
and
G_{v}

for the local groups at
e\in E
and
v\in V
,
and
\iota_{e}:G_{e}\to G_{\tau(e)}

for the inclusion that is part of the data.
We will often assume that
\mathbb{G}
is
reduced
,
meaning that if
e
is an edge with distinct vertices
then
\iota_{e}
is not surjective.

2.3.
Culler realisation

Every element of finite order in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

admits a geometric realisation
[
Cul84
]
.
More precisely, if
\phi\in{\rm{Aut}}(F_{N})
is such that

[\phi]
has finite order
m
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
,
then there is a reduced graph
X

with an isomorphism
\iota:\pi_{1}X\to F_{N}

(involving an implicit choice of basepoint
x_{0}\in X
)
and an isometry
f:X\to X
of order
m

such that
[\iota f_{*}\iota^{-1}]=[\phi]
,
where
f_{*}\in{\rm{Aut}}(\pi_{1}X)
is any representative
of the outer automorphism determined by
f
.
These geometric realisations
will play a crucial role in this article.
They are not unique in general
– a point that is central to the discussion in
Section

7
.

2.4.
\ell_{2}
-Betti numbers

On occasion, we shall need some basic facts
about the first
\ell_{2}
-Betti numbers of groups.
In each case, the groups at hand will be
finitely presented and residually finite,
which means that the reader unfamiliar with these invariants
can treat Lück’s Approximation Theorem

[
Lüc94
]
as a definition:
if
\Gamma
is finitely presented and residually finite
then
\beta_{1}^{(2)}(\Gamma)
is the limit
of the numbers
\beta_{1}(S_{n})/[\Gamma\colon S_{n}]
,
where
(S_{n})
is any nested sequence
of finite-index subgroups in
\Gamma
with
\bigcap_{n}S_{n}=1

and
\beta_{1}(S_{n})
is the usual first Betti number,
i.e.
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}H_{1}(S_{n},\mathbb{Q})
.
If
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are finitely presented and there is
an injection
\Gamma_{2}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}
with dense image,
then
\beta_{1}^{(2)}(\Gamma_{2})\geq\beta_{1}^{(2)}(\Gamma_{1})

– see Proposition 3.2 in
[
BCR16
]
.
It follows that
\beta_{1}^{(2)}
is a profinite invariant
of finitely presented, residually finite groups.

3.
GBS Structures and Centres

In this section, we will be concerned mostly
with finite graphs of infinite cyclic groups
\mathbb{G}
,
each equipped with a choice of generator
for each of the local groups.
There is a 1-1 correspondence between these structures
and
integer-labelled graphs
– i.e.
finite graphs with an integer label at each end of each edge.
The correspondence is given by using the indices

\delta_{e}=[G_{\tau(e)}:\iota_{e}(G_{e})]
as labels,
altered by a sign
\varepsilon_{e}=\pm 1

that records whether the preferred generator of
G_{e}
is sent to
a positive or negative power
of the preferred generator of
G_{\tau(e)}
.
By definition, the fundamental group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}

of a finite graph of infinite cyclic groups
is a
Generalised Baumslag Solitar (GBS) group
.
If the decomposition of
\Gamma

corresponds to an integer-labelled graph
where all of the labels are positive,
then
\Gamma
is said to be a
positive
GBS group.

An important invariant of
\Gamma
is
the
modular homomorphism

\Delta_{\Gamma}:{\Gamma\to\mathbb{Q}^{\times}}
,
which maps the local groups trivially
and assigns to each circuit
\gamma=e_{1}e_{2}\ldots e_{k}
of edges
in the underlying graph of
\mathbb{G}

the rational number

(3.1)

\varepsilon_{\gamma}\frac{[G_{o(e_{1})}:\iota_{\overline{e}_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})]\cdot\ldots\cdot[G_{o(e_{k})}:\iota_{\overline{e}_{k}}(G_{e_{k}})]}{[G_{\tau(e_{1})}:\iota_{e_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})]\cdot\ldots\cdot[G_{\tau(e_{k})}:\iota_{e_{k}}(G_{e_{k}})]}

where
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
is the product of the signs
\varepsilon_{e_{i}}
.

The following proposition is not new; it is contained in
Proposition 4.1 of
[
Lev15
]
for example.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
be
a free-by-cyclic group
such that
[\phi]
has finite order in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{n})
.
Then,
\Gamma
is a positive GBS group
whose modular homomorphism

\Delta_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
is trivial.

Proof.

If
[\phi]
has finite order
m
,
then it can be realised as
an isometry of a reduced graph with fundamental group
F_{n}
;
see
[
Cul84
]
.
More precisely, there is a reduced graph
X

and an isomorphism
\iota:\pi_{1}X\to F_{n}

(involving an implicit choice of basepoint
x_{0}\in X
)
and an isometry
f:X\to X
of order
m

such that
[\iota f_{*}\iota^{-1}]=[\phi]
,
where
f_{*}\in{\rm{Aut}}(\pi_{1}X)
is
any representative of the outer automorphism determined by
f
.
Subdividing if necessary,
we may assume that
f
does not invert any edges.
The mapping torus of
f
is, by definition,

M_{f}=X\times[0,1]\ /\ (x,0)\sim(f(x),1).

The identifications made above induce an isomorphism

\pi_{1}M_{f}\cong F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
.
Observe that
M_{f}
is a graph of spaces
where all edge and vertex spaces are circles.
Applying the functor
\pi_{1}
,
we obtain a decomposition of
\pi_{1}M_{f}=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}

as a graph of infinite cyclic groups
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
,
where
V
is the set of
\langle f\rangle
-orbits in the 0-skeleton of
X

and
E
is the set of orbits of the 1-cells.

We choose generators of the local groups in this decomposition
so that the corresponding loops in
M_{f}

trace along
X\times(0,1)
in the positive direction.
With this choice, it is clear that for each edge
e\in E

the inclusion
G_{e}\to G_{\tau(e)}
sends
the preferred generator
t_{e}\in G_{e}
to
t_{\tau(e)}^{\delta_{e}}
,
where
t_{\tau(e)}
is the preferred generator of
G_{\tau(e)}

and the integer
\delta_{e}
is the number of 1-cells of
X

that lie in the
\langle f\rangle
-orbit
e
,
divided by the number of 0-cells in the
\langle f\rangle
-orbit
\tau(e)
.
Note that since
\delta_{e}>0
,
the labels in the corresponding integer-labelled graph
are all positive.

If
\gamma=e_{1}e_{2}\ldots e_{k}
is a circuit of edges in
(V,E)
,
then

(3.2)

\Delta_{\Gamma}(\gamma)=\frac{[G_{o(e_{1})}:\iota_{\overline{e}_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})]\cdot\ldots\cdot[G_{o(e_{k})}:\iota_{\overline{e}_{k}}(G_{e_{k}})]}{[G_{\tau(e_{1})}:\iota_{e_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})]\cdot\ldots\cdot[G_{\tau(e_{k})}:\iota_{e_{k}}(G_{e_{k}})]}=\frac{\delta_{\overline{e}_{1}}\dots\delta_{\overline{e}_{k}}}{\delta_{e_{1}}\dots\delta_{e_{k}}}.

The key point to observe now is that, for
i=1,\dots,k
, the fraction

\frac{[G_{o(e_{i})}:\iota_{\overline{e}_{i}}(G_{e_{i}})]}{[G_{\tau(e_{i})}:\iota_{e_{i}}(G_{e_{i}})]}

is the number of
0
-cells of
X
in the
\langle f\rangle
-orbit
o(e_{i})

divided by the number of 0-cells in the
\langle f\rangle
-orbit
\tau(e_{i})
,
and the latter is the same as
o(e_{i+1})
, with indices mod
k
.
Thus
\Delta_{\Gamma}(\gamma)=1
.
∎

The following corollary fails for automorphisms of surface groups,
and this is a key point in Hempel’s examples
[
Hem14
]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
[\phi]
has order
m
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
and
k
is coprime to
m
,
then
F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
and
F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi^{k}}\mathbb{Z}

are isomorphic.

Proof.

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
,
the graph
(V,E)
and the integer labels
\delta_{e}
depend only on the
\langle f\rangle
-orbits in
X
and not on the choice
of generator for
\langle f\rangle=\langle f^{k}\rangle
, so
F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
and
F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi^{k}}\mathbb{Z}
have the same GBS structure.
∎

Example 3.3
.

In the above construction, the Klein bottle group
\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}

with a non-positive GBS presentation

\langle x,y\mid x^{-1}yx=y^{-1}\rangle

emerges as the GBS associated to the labelled graph with
two vertices and one edge,
with both ends of the edge labelled
2

with a positive GBS presentation
\langle a,b\mid a^{2}=b^{2}\rangle
.

3.1.
Centres

It is well known that
the free-by-cyclic groups with non-trivial centre
are precisely those considered in the previous proposition;
we include a proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
be a free-by-cyclic group,
with
N\geq 2
, and write elements in the form
g=xt^{k}

with
x\in F_{N}
and
\langle t\rangle=1\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.

If
[\phi]
has infinite order in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

then the centre of
\Gamma
is trivial.
If
[\phi]
has order
m
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

then the centre of
\Gamma
is cyclic,
generated by an element of the form
xt^{m}
,
where
{\rm{ad}}_{x^{-1}}=\phi^{m}
.

Proof.

The centre of
\Gamma
injects into
\Gamma/F_{N}\cong\mathbb{Z}

and is therefore cyclic.
An element
xt^{k}
is central if and only if
for any element
yt^{l}

xt^{k}\cdot yt^{l}=x\phi^{k}(y)t^{k+l}=y\phi^{l}(x)t^{k+l}=yt^{l}\cdot xt^{k}.

In particular, taking
y
to be the identity and
l=1

we get
\phi(x)=x
.
Thus, for any
y\in F_{N}
we have

\phi^{k}(y)=x^{-1}yx
, i.e.
\phi^{k}={\rm{ad}}_{x^{-1}}
.
Hence the order of
\Phi=[\phi]
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

is finite and divides
k
.
Conversely, if
\Phi
has order
m
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
,
then
\phi^{m}={\rm{ad}}_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in F_{N}
,
and calculating as above we see that
x_{0}^{-1}t^{m}
is central.
∎

In the following statement,
we use the standard notation
G_{e}^{g}
for
the conjugate of the subgroup
G_{e}<\Gamma
by
g\in\Gamma
.
The careful reader may worry that
in order to make
G_{e}
a bona fide subgroup of
\Gamma=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}

one needs to make a choice of maximal tree in the underlying graph.
However, the conjugacy class of
G_{e}

is independent of all choices,
so the intersection in the proposition, taken over all conjugates,
is well defined;
it is the kernel of the action on the Bass-Serre tree.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
be a reduced graph of infinite cyclic groups
and suppose that
\Gamma=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}(V,E)
has a non-trivial centre.
Then, provided
\Gamma
is not virtually abelian, its centre is

Z(\Gamma)=\bigcap_{e\in E,\,g\in\Gamma}G_{e}^{g}.

Proof.

Let
z
be a nontrivial element of
Z(\Gamma)
.
There are two cases to consider:
in the action on the Bass-Serre tree
T
of
\mathbb{G}
,
either
z
fixes a point or else
it acts as a hyperbolic element.

The second case is impossible:
as
\mathbb{G}
is reduced, the action of
\Gamma
on
T
is minimal
(i.e. there are no proper invariant subtrees),
so the axis of
\langle z\rangle
, which is
\Gamma
-invariant,
would be the whole tree, contradicting the fact that

\Gamma
is not virtually abelian
and acts with cyclic arc stabilisers.

Thus each
z\in Z(\Gamma)

fixes a non-empty subtree
T_{0}\subseteq T
,
and since this is
\Gamma
-invariant,
we again use minimality to conclude
T_{0}=T
,
forcing
z
to be contained in

\bigcap\{G_{e}^{g}\mid e\in E,\,g\in\Gamma\}
,
the kernel of the action on
T
.

To see that
Z(\Gamma)
is the whole of the kernel,
first observe that the latter, being non-trivial and
an intersection of infinite cyclic groups,
is infinite cyclic.
It is also normal, so the action of
\Gamma
by conjugation on it
is either trivial or else some element acts
as multiplication by
-1
.
But this last case is impossible,
because the kernel contains
Z(\Gamma)\neq 1
.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}

where
[\phi]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
has finite order,
considered as a positive GBS group
\Gamma=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}(V,E)
as in
Proposition

3.1
.
Then, provided
\Gamma\not\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, its centre is

Z(\Gamma)=\bigcap_{e\in E,\,g\in\Gamma}G_{e}^{g}.

Proof.

Lemma

3.4
tells us that
Z(\Gamma)\neq 1
,
so the corollary is immediate
except in the case of the Klein bottle group,
which is covered by
Example

3.3
.
∎

We proved
Proposition

3.1
by realising
\Gamma

as the fundamental group of a graph
\mathbb{G}
of infinite-cyclic groups
with a choice of generator
t_{e}
for each edge group
and
t_{v}
for each vertex group
so that the inclusions were
\iota(t_{e})=t_{\tau(e)}^{\delta_{e}}
.
With
Corollary

3.6
in hand, we can describe
\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)

in a similar manner, but with finite cyclic groups
in place of the local groups in
\mathbb{G}
.
One should think of this graph of finite cyclic groups
\overline{\mathbb{G}}

as being obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by “reducing mod
m
”,
where
m
is the order of the holonomy.
More formally, it is described as follows.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\Gamma=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}(V,E)
be as in
Corollary

3.6

and equip the local groups of
\mathbb{G}

with generators
t_{e}\ (e\in E)
and
t_{v}\ (v\in V)
as above,
so that
\iota(t_{e})=t_{\tau(e)}^{\delta_{e}}
.
Let
d_{e}=[G_{e}\colon Z(\Gamma)]
, let
d_{v}=[G_{v}\colon Z(\Gamma)]
,
and let
\overline{\mathbb{G}}(V,E)
be the graph of groups
obtained from
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
by replacing

G_{e}\ (e\in E)
with
\overline{G}_{e}\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_{e}
generated by
\overline{t}_{e}

and by replacing
G_{v}\ (v\in V)
with
\overline{G}_{v}\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_{v}

generated by
\overline{t}_{v}
, defining
\iota_{e}(\overline{t}_{e})=\overline{t}_{v}^{\delta_{e}}
.
Then,

(1)

\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)=\pi_{1}\overline{\mathbb{G}}(V,E)
and

(2)

\delta_{e}=d_{\tau(e)}/d_{e}=[G_{\tau(e)}:\iota_{e}(G_{e})]=[\overline{G}_{\tau(e)}:\iota_{e}(\overline{G}_{e})]
.

Remark 3.8

(Comparison with Seifert fibred spaces)
.

Associated to any finite-order automorphism
f
of a surface,
one has the mapping torus
M_{f}
, a Seifert fibred 3-manifold.
The fundamental group of the 2-orbifold base
of this Seifert fibred space is
\pi_{1}M_{f}
modulo its centre.
The graph of finite cyclic groups
\mathbb{G}(V,E)

is the direct analogue of this base orbifold
in the setting of free-by-cyclic groups with finite holonomy.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
with
[\phi]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
of finite order, with
N\geq 2
.
Then,
\Gamma
is isomorphic to
F_{M}\times\mathbb{Z}
, for some
M
,
if and only if
\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)
is torsion-free
or, equivalently,
\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)
is free.

Proof.

Corollary

3.7
tells us
that
\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)
is the fundamental group
of a finite graph of finite cyclic groups,
so it has torsion unless all of the local groups are trivial,
in which case it is free
and
\Gamma
is a central
\mathbb{Z}
-by-
F_{M}
extension,
hence a direct product.
∎

In
Section

7.1
we shall describe
which free-by-cyclic groups are isomorphic to
F_{M}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

4.
Reduction to free-by-(finite cyclic) case

We will work with
slide moves
on graphs of groups,
which have played a prominent role in many works on GBS groups,
including Forester’s early work
[
For02
]
.

Definition 4.1

(slide move)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
be a graph of groups and
{e_{1},e_{2}\in E}

edges such that
\tau(e_{1})=o(e_{2})
.
A
slide move
can be performed when

\iota_{e_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})\leq\iota_{\bar{e}_{2}}(G_{e_{2}})
:
the move deletes
\{e_{1},\overline{e}_{1}\}
from
E

and replaces it with
\{e^{\prime}_{1},\overline{e}^{\prime}_{1}\}
,
where
o(e_{1}^{\prime})=o(e_{1})
and
\tau(e_{1}^{\prime})=\tau(e_{2})
;
the local group at
e_{1}^{\prime}
is
G_{e_{1}^{\prime}}=G_{e_{1}}

with
\iota_{e_{1}^{\prime}}=\iota_{e_{2}}\circ\iota_{\overline{e}_{2}}^{-1}\circ\iota_{e_{1}}

and
\iota_{\overline{e}_{1}^{\prime}}=\iota_{\overline{e}_{1}}
.

This gives a new graph of groups
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}(V,E^{\prime})

with
\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}\cong\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}^{\prime}
.

Note that in the case of graphs of cyclic groups,

{\iota_{e_{1}}(G_{e_{1}})\leq\iota_{\bar{e}_{2}}(G_{e_{2}})}

is equivalent to requiring that
the index of
G_{e_{1}}
in the common vertex group
divides the index of
G_{e_{2}}
.

4.1.
Slide moves lift

From
Corollary

3.7
we have:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be free-by-cyclic groups
with non-trivial centres and positive GBS decompositions

\Gamma_{i}=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}_{i}
as in
Proposition

3.1
.
If
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{1}
can be transformed into
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{2}

by slide moves,
then
\mathbb{G}_{1}
can be transformed into
\mathbb{G}_{2}
by slide moves,
and hence
\Gamma_{1}\cong\Gamma_{2}
.

Proof.

The graph of finite cyclic groups
\overline{\mathbb{G}}(V,E^{\prime})

obtained by performing a slide move on
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{1}(V,E)

inherits from
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{1}

a preferred set of generators for the local groups,
and the new edge-inclusion map
\iota_{e^{\prime}}

is calculated (in terms of these generators)
by the indices of the edge groups involved in the slide.
This data lifts uniquely to define a slide move
from
\mathbb{G}_{1}(V,E)
to a positive GBS structure
\mathbb{G}(V,E^{\prime})
,
with a preferred set of generators
for the local groups in
\mathbb{G}(V,E^{\prime})
.
At the end of the sequence of moves,
the positive GBS that we obtain
has the same underlying graph as
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{2}
,
which is the same as
\mathbb{G}_{2}
,
and the inclusions of the edge groups
are given by the correct indices

\delta_{e}=[G_{\tau(e)}:\iota_{e}(G_{e})]=[\overline{G}_{\tau(e)}:\iota_{e}(\overline{G}_{e})]
,
so this graph of infinite cyclic groups is
\mathbb{G}_{2}
.
∎

4.2.
Profinite equivalence passes to the central quotients

With the equivalence of
Lemma

4.2
in hand,
an obvious strategy emerges for deducing
Theorem

A

from
Theorem

B
:
given two free-by-(infinite cyclic) groups

\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
with finite monodromy,
prove that if
\widehat{\Gamma}_{1}\cong\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}

then the graphs of finite-cyclic groups for

\Gamma_{1}/Z(\Gamma_{1})
and
\Gamma_{2}/Z(\Gamma_{2})

are equivalent via slide moves,
then appeal to
Lemma

4.2
.
If this strategy is to succeed,

\widehat{\Gamma}_{i}
must determine
the profinite completion of
\Gamma_{i}/Z(\Gamma_{i})
,
so we begin by proving that.

As is standard, we shall regard
\Gamma

as a subgroup of
\widehat{\Gamma}
via the canonical embedding,
and we shall write
\overline{\Lambda}

for the closure in
\widehat{\Gamma}

of a subgroup
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
.

First we need a lemma which implies
that the order of any torsion element
[\phi]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

equals the order of
[\widehat{\phi}]\in{\rm{Out}}(\widehat{F}_{N})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

The canonical homomorphism
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})\to{\rm{Out}}(\widehat{F}_{N})

induced by the completion homomorphism

{\rm{Aut}}(F_{N})\to{\rm{Aut}}(\widehat{F}_{N})

is injective.

Proof.

If
\phi\in{\rm{Aut}}(F_{N})
is not an inner automorphism of
F_{N}
,
then by Lemma 1 of
[
Gro74
]

there exists
x\in F_{N}
such that

\phi(x)
is not conjugate to
x
in
F_{N}
.
As
F_{N}
is conjugacy separable,
there exists a finite quotient
\pi:F_{N}\to Q

(extending to
\widehat{\pi}:\widehat{F}_{N}\to Q
) such that

\pi(x)
is not conjugate to
\pi\phi(x)
in
Q
.
By definition,
\widehat{\pi}\widehat{\phi}(x)=\pi\phi(x)
and

\widehat{\pi}(x)=\pi(x)
,
so
x
is not conjugate to
\widehat{\phi}(x)=\phi(x)

in
\widehat{F}_{N}
,
and therefore
\widehat{\phi}
is not an inner automorphism.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

If
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
has non-trivial centre,
then
Z(\widehat{\Gamma})=\overline{Z(\Gamma)}

and hence
\widehat{\Gamma}/Z(\widehat{\Gamma})\cong\widehat{\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)}
.

Proof.

The action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
F_{N}
by
n\mapsto\phi^{n}

has finite image in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

and therefore extends to an action of
\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}

with the same image.
Thus
\widehat{F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}}\cong\widehat{F}_{N}\rtimes_{\widehat{\phi}}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
,
where the action of
\kappa\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
on
\widehat{F}_{N}
is

\kappa\cdot x=\widehat{\phi}^{(\kappa\mod m)}(x)
,
with
m
the order of
[\phi]
in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
,
which by
Lemma

4.3
is equal to
the order of
[\widehat{\phi}]
in
{\rm{Out}}(\widehat{F}_{N})
.

In the proof of
Lemma

3.4

we calculated the centre of
F_{n}\rtimes_{\phi}{\mathbb{Z}}

and that calculation works equally well in
\widehat{\Gamma}=\widehat{F}_{N}\rtimes_{\widehat{\phi}}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}

to show that

Z(\widehat{\Gamma})=\{{x_{0}}^{\kappa}t^{\kappa m}\mid\kappa\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\{(x_{0}t^{m})^{\kappa}\mid\kappa\in\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}\},

where
\langle t\rangle=1\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
and
\phi^{m}={\rm{ad}}_{{x_{0}}^{-1}}
.
Thus

Z(\widehat{\Gamma})=\overline{\langle x_{0}t^{m}\rangle}=\overline{Z(\Gamma)}
.
∎

4.3.
Decompositions of free-by-(finite cyclic) groups

By definition, a finitely generated free-by-(finite cyclic) group
is a group
G
that, for some
m>0
,
fits into a short exact sequence

(4.1)

1\to F_{N}\to G\to\mathbb{Z}/m\to 1.

(This sequence need not split.)
These are precisely the groups that arise as
fundamental groups of finite graphs of finite cyclic groups
that are
orientable
in the sense that
one can choose generators for the local groups
so that all inclusions are described by the index,
as in
Proposition

3.1
:

Proposition 4.5
.

A finitely generated group
G
is free-by-(finite cyclic)
if and only if there exists
a finite graph of finite cyclic groups
\mathbb{G}(V,E)
with

G_{v}\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_{v}\ (e\in V)
and

G_{e}\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_{e}\ (e\in E)
so that

(1)

G\cong\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}
,

(2)

d_{e}
divides
d_{\tau(e)}
for all
e\in E
,

(3)

there are generators
t_{v}
for
G_{v}\ (v\in V)

and
t_{e}
for
G_{e}=G_{\overline{e}}\ (e\in E)
so that

\iota_{e}(t_{e})=t_{\tau(e)}^{\delta_{e}}

for all
e\in E
, where
\delta_{e}=d_{\tau(e)}/d_{e}
.

Moreover, one can choose generators with these properties
for the local groups in any decomposition of
G

as a graph of finite groups.

Proof.

First assume that

G
fits into the short exact sequence (
4.1
).
As
G
is virtually free, it is the fundamental group
of a finite graph of finite groups
\mathbb{G}
.
The edge and vertex groups of
\mathbb{G}

intersect the torsion-free subgroup
F_{N}<G
trivially,
so they inject into
\mathbb{Z}/m
and are cyclic.
We fix a generator
\theta
for
\mathbb{Z}/m
and choose generators
for the non-trivial edge and vertex groups of
\mathbb{G}

that map to
\theta^{i}
with
1\leq i<m
.
Note that the map from
G
to
\mathbb{Z}/m

commutes with conjugation in
G
,
so this choice of generators is not subject to the ambiguity
inherent in regarding the local groups as subgroups of
G

(since this ambiguity is just a matter of
passing between conjugates).

For the converse, we assume that

G
is the fundamental group of
a finite graph of cyclic groups
\mathbb{G}

with generators as described and
fix an integer
M

that is divisible by the order of each of the local groups.
Recall that
G=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}
is generated by
the vertex groups
G_{v}=\langle t_{v}\rangle

together with a set of edge symbols
(see page 42 of
[
Ser80
]
).
We define a homomorphism
h:G\to\mathbb{Z}/M=\langle\theta\rangle

by mapping the edge symbols trivially
and sending
t_{v}
to
\theta^{\nu(v)}
where
\nu(v)=M/|G_{v}|
.
Condition (3) ensures that
h
is well-defined,
i.e. that it respects the defining relations of
G

(see page 43 of
[
Ser80
]
).
Every finite subgroup of
G

is conjugate to a subgroup of a vertex group,
so the kernel of
h
is torsion-free and hence free.
Thus
G
if free-by-(finite cyclic).
∎

Remark 4.6
.

We saw in the previous section that
if
\Gamma=F_{N}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
has non-trivial centre
then
G=\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)
is a free-by-(finite cyclic),
but not all free-by-(finite cyclic) groups
arise in this way.
One way to see this is to observe that
the commutator subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}

lies in the normal subgroup
F_{N}\rtimes 1
,
and this intersects the centre
Z(\Gamma)
trivially
(provided
\Gamma\neq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
),
as we saw in
Lemma

3.4
.
This forces the centre of
G=\Gamma/Z(\Gamma)
to be trivial:
if the image of
z\in\Gamma
in
G
were central,
then
[x,z]\in Z(\Gamma)\cap\Gamma^{\prime}=1
for all
x\in\Gamma
,
hence
z\in Z(\Gamma)
.
Arguing as in
Proposition

3.5
, it follows that
the orders
|G_{e}|
of the edge groups in any
graph-of-finite-groups decomposition of
G

must have greatest common divisor equal to
1
,
which is a non-trivial constraint.

5.
Solution of the reduced problem

5.1.
Numbered graphs, associated posets, and slide equivalence

In this section we introduce a key technical device –
the poset associated to a numbered graph,
and prove a result that will enable us to promote isomorphisms
to sequences of sliding moves in various contexts –

Proposition

5.6
.

All of the information about a graph of groups
of the type described in
Proposition

4.5

is captured by the sizes of the vertex and edge groups,
so we introduce the following data structure
to facilitate a concise calculus of manipulation for them.
We continue to use the word
graph
in the sense of Serre;
so
\{e,\overline{e}\}
corresponds to a 1-cell
in the topological realisation of the graph,
which need not be simplicial.

Definition 5.1

(Numbered graph)
.

A
numbered graph

(\mathcal{G},d)
consists of
a finite graph
\mathcal{G}(V,E)

and a labelling
d:V\sqcup E\to\mathbb{N}
such that

d_{e}=d_{\overline{e}}
and
d_{e}
divides
d_{\tau(e)}
for every
e\in E
.
A numbered graph is
reduced

if
d_{\tau(e)}=d_{e}
implies
\tau(e)=\tau(\overline{e})

(that is,
e
is a loop).

When there is no danger of ambiguity,
we will abbreviate
(\mathcal{G},d)
to
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 5.2

(Associated poset)
.

To each numbered graph
(\mathcal{G},d)
we associate
a finite poset
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
,
whose elements are the connected components
of certain subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}
.
Each element is assigned two pieces of
additional numerical data – a
level
and a
tag
.

•

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
a_{1,k},a_{2,k},\ldots

be the non-empty connected components
of the subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}

formed by the vertices and edges
to which
d
assigns a value divisible by
k
.
These
a_{i,k}
will be the elements of

level

k
.

•

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}=\coprod_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\{a_{1,k},a_{2,k},\ldots\}
.

•

The ordering: define
a_{i,k}\preceq a_{j,l}

if and only if

k\ |\ l
and
a_{i,k}\supseteq a_{j,l}
.

•

Each element
a_{i,k}
is
tagged

with its first Betti number
\beta_{1}(a_{i,k})
,
i.e. the number of edges
minus the number of vertices plus one.

A bijection
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G^{\prime}}}

is a
data-preserving isomorphism

if it is a poset isomorphism that preserves levels and tags.

Remarks 5.3
.

(1)

It is important to note that the labels (numbers)
on the edges of
\mathcal{G}

are
not
explicitly recorded
as part of the data of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}

– cf. Step 4 in the proof of

Proposition

5.6
.

(2)

If the labels on the edges of

a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}

are all divisible by
k^{\prime}>k
,
then
a_{i,k}=a_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}

as subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}_{k^{\prime}}
for some
i^{\prime}
,
but
a_{i,k}\neq a_{i^{\prime},k^{\prime}}

as elements of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
,
since their levels
k
and
k^{\prime}
are not equal.

(3)

The unique minimal element of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}

is
a_{1,1}=\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{G}
.
If all of the numbers in the labelling of
\mathcal{G}

are divisible by
r>1
,
then there will also be a unique element

a_{1,r}={\mathcal{G}_{r}}=\mathcal{G}

at level
r
,
and
a_{1,1}\prec a_{1,r}
.

Example 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}

Definition 5.5

(Slide moves on numbered graphs)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(V,E)
be a numbered graph
with distinct edges
e,f

such that
d_{e}
divides
d_{f}
and
\tau(e)=o(f)
.
Consider
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})

where
E^{\prime}
is obtained from
E

by replacing
\{e,\overline{e}\}
with
\{e^{\prime},\overline{e}^{\prime}\}

where
o(e^{\prime}):=o(e)
and
\tau(e^{\prime}):=\tau(f)
.
Number the new edge by
d_{e^{\prime}}:=d_{e}

and leave the numbering of the other edges unchanged.
This transformation
\mathcal{G}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

is called a
slide move
.

Note that slide moves take
reduced
numbered graphs
to reduced numbered graphs.
Crucially, the definitions have been framed so that
slide moves also induce isomorphisms of the associated posets.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

be reduced numbered graphs.
There is a data-preserving isomorphism
of the associated posets

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}

if and only if

\mathcal{G}
can be transformed into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

by a sequence of slide moves.

Proof.

The definitions have been framed
so that the “if” direction is clear,
so we concentrate on the converse.
Assume that we have a data-preserving isomorphism of posets

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}\cong\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}

carrying
a_{i,k}
to
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
.
Our proof that this isomorphism
can be realised by a sequence of slide moves
is broken into steps
with the following intermediate conclusions.

(1)

There is a bijection
v\mapsto v^{\prime}

between the vertex sets of
\mathcal{G}
and
{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}

that takes the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
to those of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{k}
for each level
k
.

(2)

This bijection restricts to a bijection
between the vertex sets of each pair of corresponding components

a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k},\ a_{i,k}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\prime}
,
and
a_{i,k}
has the same number of edges as
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
.

(3)

For each level
k
, the number of edges
in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{k}
is equal.

(4)

For each
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the components
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}

have the same number of edges labelled
l
.

(5)

Proceeding by top-down induction on
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
:
for each
a_{i,k}
, assuming that the bijection in (1)
extends to a label-preserving isomorphism
from the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
formed by edges with label greater than
k

to the corresponding subgraph of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
,
we argue that
a sequence of slide moves on
\mathcal{G}

involving only edges in
a_{i,k}
labelled
k

transforms
a_{i,k}
into
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
.
At the end of the induction, we have the desired transformation
of
\mathcal{G}=a_{1,1}
into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=a_{1,1}^{\prime}
.

Step 1.
For any reduced numbered graph
\mathcal{H}
with at least two vertices,

\mathcal{H}_{k}
(which is itself a reduced graph) is disconnected for some
k
. It follows that
the maximal elements of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
correspond to vertices of
\mathcal{G}
(some
of the maximal elements may correspond to one-vertex subgraphs of
\mathcal{H}
with loops, others
will simply be vertices). Thus an isomorphism of posets
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}\cong\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}

(without reference to level or tags) gives a bijection of vertex sets
v\leftrightarrow v^{\prime}
, and (1) follows from the
assumption that the isomorphism is level-preserving.

Step 2.

The number of maximal elements
\succeq a_{i,k}
in
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
is the
number of vertices in the subgraph
a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, and similarly for
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
,
so the isomorphism
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}\cong\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
tells that the connected subgraphs

a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
have the same number of
vertices. As the isomorphism is data-preserving, the tags on
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
are the same, and
these record the first Betti numbers of
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
. This proves (2).

Step 3.
The number of edges in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is the sum of the number of edges in
the components
a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}
, so (3) is an immediate consequence of (2).

Step 4.

Regarding
a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}

as a numbered graph in its own right,
we have a data-preserving inclusion of posets

\mathsf{P}_{a_{i,k}}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}

whose image is spanned by the elements comparable with
a_{i,k}
.
Thus, to prove that
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}

have the same number of edges with label
l

it is enough to prove the following:
for numbered graphs
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, if there is a data-preserving isomorphism

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}
, then
for each
l\in\mathbb{N}
the graphs
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

have the same number of edges labelled
l
.
We shall use Möbius inversion to count these edges.

We require some additional notation.
Let
e(\mathcal{H})
be
the number of edges of
\mathcal{H}

and let
e_{l}(\mathcal{H})
be
the number of edges labelled
l
.
As before,
\mathcal{H}_{m}
denotes
the subgraph of
\mathcal{H}

formed by vertices and edges
whose labels are divisible by
m
.
Recall that the Möbius function
\mu(n)

equals
0
if
n
is divisible by a square
and
(-1)^{\#\text{prime factors of }n}
otherwise.
We will show that

e_{l}(\mathcal{H})=\sum_{m\colon l|m}e(\mathcal{H}_{m})\mu(m/l).

In the light of (3), this will complete the proof.

Observing that
e(\mathcal{H}_{m})=\sum_{m|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{m})
and that

e_{n}(\mathcal{H})=e_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{m})
if
m|n
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\colon l|m}e(\mathcal{H}_{m})\mu(\frac{m}{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\colon l|m}\quad\sum_{n\colon m|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{m})\mu(m/l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\colon l|m}\quad\sum_{n\colon m|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H})\mu(m/l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\colon l|n}\quad\sum_{m\colon l|m|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H})\mu(m/l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\colon l|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H})\sum_{d\colon d|\frac{n}{l}}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\colon l|n}e_{n}(\mathcal{H})\cdot\mathbf{1}\big(\frac{n}{l}=1\big)

\displaystyle=e_{l}(\mathcal{H}).

Step 5.
At this stage in the proof, we can assume that
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}

are numbered graphs on the same set of vertices, with the same set of edges carrying a label greater than
k

and with the same number of edges of label
k
. We want to perform slide moves
involving only edges labelled
k
to transform
a_{i,k}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}
into
a_{i,k}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}^{\prime}
,
leaving the remainder of
\mathcal{G}
unchanged.
If we can do this, then we can inductively transform
\mathcal{G}
into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

by slide moves, arguing with an induction that assumes we have already adjusted
\mathcal{G}

so that
\mathcal{G}
into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
have the same set of edges numbered with higher labels.

For the base step of the induction, no slide moves are required: each
maximal element
a_{i,k}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
is a component with just one vertex and it may
have some loops whose label is
k
, the level of the vertex;
the number of these loops is the first Betti number, which is recorded as the tag on
a_{i,k}

and hence is the same for corresponding maximal element
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
.

For the inductive step, we first reduce to the case where

all
edges of
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
are labelled
k
. We do this by observing that
within each connected component
C
of the subgraph of
a_{i,k}

that would be obtained by deleting all edges labelled
k
,
we can freely slide the endpoints of each edge labelled
k
.
Moreover, by induction, each component
C
in
a_{i,k}
is identified with an identical component
C^{\prime}
in
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
.
So we can choose a vertex
v_{c}\in C
and assume that whenever an edge of
a_{i,k}
or
a_{i,k}^{\prime}

labelled
k
has an endpoint in
C
, that endpoint is
v_{C}
. We can then forget the remainder of
C
(it will
remain inertly attached to
v_{C}
while we perform slide moves on the connected subgraph where all edges are labelled
k
).

Finally, we observe that a numbered graph in which all edges are labelled
k
can be transformed by slide moves
into a simple normal form, namely the
octopus graph
shown in figure
3
.
In more detail, we fix a vertex
v_{0}
and note that for
every edge
e
, at least one of
o(e)
and
t(e)

is connected to
v_{0}
by a path which doesn’t include the edge
e
;
we slide
e
along the path until one of its endpoints is at
v_{0}
; proceeding one edge at a time, this
operation transforms our numbered graph into a graph where every edge has
v_{0}

as at least one of its endpoints and the vertices other than
v_{0}
are leaves (vertices of valence 1).
To complete the proof, we transform each of
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
into an octopus in this way,
and note that the octopus is determined by its number of vertices and edges, which is the same as the
number in
a_{i,k}
and
a_{i,k}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

There is a 1-1 correspondence
\mathbb{G}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{G}

between the finite graphs of finite groups described in Proposition
4.5
and
the set of finite numbered graphs. If
\mathbb{G}_{i}
corresponds to
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for
i=1,2
, then
there is a slide move transforming
\mathbb{G}_{1}
into
\mathbb{G}_{2}
if and only if there is a slide move
transforming
\mathcal{G}_{1}
into
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Proof.

Given a graph
\mathbb{G}
of finite cyclic groups, the corresponding numbered graph is obtained by replacing the
local groups with their order. One can recover
\mathbb{G}
from the numbered graph by taking edge and vertex groups to be
cyclic of the prescribed order, with preferred generators
t_{e}
and
t_{v}
, defining
\iota_{e}(t_{e})=t_{\tau(e)}^{\delta_{e}}

where
\delta_{e}
is the label on the vertex
t(e)
divided by the label on
e
. It is obvious from the definitions that
this correspondence respects slide moves.

5.2.
Finite subgroups, centralisers, and
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)

In this section, we translate the purely combinatorial arguments
of
Section

5.1
into group theory.

Definition 5.8

(The
f
inite
s
ubgroups
c
onjugacy poset)
.

Let
G
be a group.
The elements of the

finite subgroups conjugacy poset

{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)

are the conjugacy classes
[S]
of finite subgroups
S\leq G
,
with the ordering
[S_{1}]\preceq[S_{2}]

if a conjugate of
S_{1}
is contained in
S_{2}
.

Each element of
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)
is assigned two pieces
of additional numerical data:
its
level
, which is the cardinality
|S|
,
and its
tag
,
which is the first Betti number of the centraliser
C_{G}(T)
.
By definition, a
data-preserving isomorphism

{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\to{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})

is a poset isomorphism that preserves this numerical data.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated free-by-(finite cyclic) group,
let
\mathbb{G}
be a reduced graph of finite groups
with
G\cong\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}
,
and let
\mathcal{G}
be the numbered graph that encodes
\mathbb{G}

(as in
Lemma

5.7
).

Then, there is a data-preserving isomorphism

\Psi:{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G)\to\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof.

Consider the action of
G=\pi_{1}\mathbb{G}
on
the universal covering
T
of
\mathbb{G}
, which is the Bass-Serre tree of the splitting.
The fixed-point set
{\rm{Fix}}(S)\subseteq T
of each finite subgroup
S<G

is connected and non-empty, and its image in
\mathcal{G}=G\backslash T
, which we call
X_{S}
, is the same as the image of

{\rm{Fix}}(S^{g})
for every conjugate
S^{g}=g^{-1}Sg
. All of the edges and vertices in
X_{S}

have a copy of
S
in the local group, and hence the numbers labelling the edges and vertices of
X_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{G}

are all divisible by
k=|S|
; in other words
X_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}
. In fact,

X_{S}
is a connected component of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
: for each vertex
v\in X_{S}
and each
edge
e
with
\tau(e)=v
, if
e
is in
\mathcal{G}_{k}
then
k
divides
the label on
e
, which means that
G_{e}<G_{v}
contains a subgroup of cardinality
k
, and the only such subgroup in

G_{v}
(which is cyclic) is
S
, hence
S\leq G_{e}
and
e\subset X_{S}
. Thus
X_{S}=X_{S^{g}}
is one of the elements
of level
|S|
in
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
and we can define
\Psi([S]):=X_{S}
.
Note that
\Psi
is injective, because each of the local groups in
\mathbb{G}
contains a unique subgroup
of any given cardinality, so
X_{S_{1}}
and
X_{S_{2}}
are disjoint if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are non-conjugate subgroups
of the same size.

To see that
\Psi
is surjective, consider an element
a_{i,k}\in\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{G}}
. By definition,

a_{i,k}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, so the local group at each vertex
v
of
a_{i,k}
contains a subgroup of
cardinality
k
. If this subgroup is
S
, then
v\in X_{S}\cap a_{i,k}
.
But
a_{i,k}
and
X_{S}
are connected components of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, so having non-trivial intersection forces
a_{i,k}=X_{S}
,
in other words
a_{i,k}=\Psi([S])
. Thus
\Psi
is a bijection.

If
S_{1}\leq S_{2}
, then
{\rm{Fix}}(S_{1})\supseteq{\rm{Fix}}(S_{2})

and hence
X_{S_{1}}\supseteq X_{S_{2}}
.
Moreover,
|S_{1}|
divides
|S_{2}|
.
Thus
S_{1}\leq S_{2}
implies
\Psi([S_{1}])\preceq\Psi([S_{2}])
,
so
\Psi
is an isomorphism of posets.

It remains to prove that
\Psi
preserves tags, i.e. that the first Betti number of
C_{G}(S)
is equal to the
first Betti number of
X_{S}=\Psi([S])
. For this, the first thing to observe is that
while
N_{G}(S)
preserves the fixed-point set
{\rm{Fix}}(S)
in the Bass-Serre tree
T
,
the elements of
G
that are not in
N_{G}(S)
move
{\rm{Fix}}(S)
off itself entirely.
Indeed, if
v
and
gv
are both vertices of
{\rm{Fix}}(S)
, then
S
and
S^{g}
are both subgroups of the
stabiliser of
v
, which being cyclic has only one subgroup of size
|S|
, so
S=S^{g}
and
g\in N_{G}(S)
.
It follows that
N_{G}(S)\backslash{\rm{Fix}}(S)
injects into
G\backslash T=\mathcal{G}
with image
X_{S}
.
Thus
N_{G}(S)
is the fundamental group of a graph of finite groups with underlying graph
X_{S}
,
and therefore
N_{G}(S)
has the same first Betti number as
X_{S}
.
The final thing to observe is
N_{G}(S)=C_{G}(S)
, because in any (torsion-free)-by-abelian group,
the normaliser of a finite subgroup is the centraliser of that subgroup.
∎

.

Let

then

.
To this end, we decompose each

,
and encode

5.3.
Completions detect finite subgroups and centralisers

Several of our previous proofs have exploited the fact that Bass-Serre theory allows one to capture the
behaviour of finite subgroups of virtually free groups
G
. In this section, we shall exploit the fact that no additional
torsion appears when one passes from
G
to its profinite completion
\widehat{G}
; this is not true of finitely generated, residually finite groups in general. We continue to regard
G
as a subgroup of
\widehat{G}
and write
\overline{H}
for the closure of a
subgroup
H\leq\widehat{G}
.

Proposition 5.10
.

If a finitely generated group
G
is virtually free,
then every finite subgroup of
\widehat{G}
is conjugate to a finite subgroup of
G
,
and if two finite subgroups of
G
are conjugate in
\widehat{G}
, they are already conjugate in
G
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite graph of finite groups
such that
G
is its (discrete) fundamental group. Proposition 8.2.3 in

[
Rib17
]
shows that

\widehat{G}
is actually isomorphic
to the
profinite
fundamental group of
\mathcal{G}
,
which means that there is a profinite tree
\widehat{T}
on which
\widehat{G}
acts
with quotient
\mathcal{G}
.

As in the discrete setting, every finite subgroup
S
of
\widehat{G}

will fix some vertex of
\widehat{T}
, and therefore
S
is conjugate in
\widehat{G}

to a subgroup of one of the vertex groups of
\mathcal{G}
, which all lie in
G
.

The assertion about non-conjugate finite subgroups of
G
remaining non-conjugate in
\widehat{G}

is a special case of conjugacy separability for subgroups of virtually-free groups, which was
proved by Chagas and Zalesskii
[
CZ15
]
.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated free-by-(finite cyclic) group and
S<G
be a finite subgroup.
Then
C_{\widehat{G}}(S)\cong\widehat{C_{G}(S)}
.

Proof.

Ribes–Zalesskii in Theorem 2.6 of
[
RZ14
]

proved that for virtually-free groups

N_{\widehat{G}}(S)=\overline{N_{G}(S)}
.
And since both
G
and
\widehat{G}
are
(torsion-free)-by-abelian group,

N_{\widehat{G}}(S)=C_{\widehat{G}}(S)
and
N_{G}(S)=C_{G}(S)
.

In hyperbolic groups, the centralisers of finite subgroups
are always finitely generated,
and in virtually free groups all finitely generated subgroups
are closed in the profinite topology (the LERF property),
so
\overline{C_{G}(T)}\cong\widehat{C_{G}(T)}
.
∎

5.4.
Proof of the main theorems

We recall the statements of our main theorems,
for the convenience of the reader.

See
B

Proof.

With
Theorem

D
in hand,
it suffices to show that if
\widehat{G}_{1}\cong\widehat{G}_{2}

then there is a data-preserving isomorphism of posets

{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\cong{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})
.

To this end, we fix an identification
\widehat{G}:=\widehat{G}_{1}=\widehat{G}_{2}

and consider the canonical embeddings

G_{1}\hookrightarrow\widehat{G}
and
G_{2}\hookrightarrow\widehat{G}
.

Proposition

5.10
tells us that
for each conjugacy class of finite subgroups
S_{1}\leq G_{1}

there is a unique conjugacy class
of finite subgroups
S_{2}\leq G_{2}

such that
S_{1}
is conjugate to
S_{2}
in
\widehat{G}
.
Thus we obtain an isomorphism
{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{1})\cong{\rm{\bf{fsc}}}(G_{2})

and we will done if we can argue
that the first Betti numbers
of
C_{G_{1}}(S_{1})
and
C_{G_{2}}(S_{2})
are the same.

As
S_{1}
is conjugate to
S_{2}
in
\widehat{G}
,
they have isomorphic centralisers in
\widehat{G}
,
so
Proposition

5.11
tells us that

C_{G_{1}}(S_{1})
and
C_{G_{2}}(S_{2})
have
the same profinite completion.
It follows that
C_{G_{1}}(S_{1})
and
C_{G_{2}}(S_{2})

have the same first Betti number, because
the first Betti number of a group
G
is encoded in its finite quotients: it is the largest
integer
b
such that
G
maps onto
(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{b}
for every prime
p

Remark 5.13

7.
Isomorphisms of free-by-cyclic groups

In the introduction to this paper, we promised to give
examples illustrating various ways in which non-obvious isomorphisms
between free-by-cyclic groups with centre can arise.
In this section, we will fulfil these promises as follows:

•

We explain why, for each
M\geq 2
,
there is an automorphism
\phi\in{\rm{Aut}}(F_{N})
such that

F_{N}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}\cong F_{M}\times\mathbb{Z}

if and only if
M-1
divides
N-1
.
In each case,
the conjugacy class of
[\phi]\in{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is unique.

•

We describe elements

of finite order
m

with some powers
\Phi^{j}
, where

is coprime to
m
,
so that
\Phi

in
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

where

.

•

We also describe elements

of finite order so that

\langle\Psi\rangle

{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

known to experts, e.g. Khramtsov
[
Khr90
]
and Cashen–Levitt
[
CL16
]
.
This topic is also being explored in an ongoing work of Andrew and Patchkoria,
who obtained a version of

independently

]
.
