Abstract
. The behavior of objects associated with general extended affine Lie algebras is typically distinct from their counterparts in affine Lie algebras. Our research focuses on studying characters and Cartan automorphisms, which appear in the study of Chevalley involutions and Chevalley bases for extended affine Lie algebras. We show that for almost all extended affine Lie algebras, any finite order Cartan automorphism is diagonal, and its corresponding combinatorial map is a character.

17B67, 17B22, 16W10, 17B65

1.
Introduction

Extended affine Lie algebras are a class of Lie algebras that are defined by a set of axioms. They share many common features with finite and affine Lie algebras, but also possess unique characteristics that make them fascinating objects of study, see
[
12
]
,
[
1
]
and
[
15
]
. For instance, like finite and affine Lie algebras, an extended affine Lie algebra is equipped with a symmetric invariant non-degenerate bilinear form, and has a finite-dimensional Cartan subalgebra that splits the Lie algebra into a direct sum of root spaces.

The set of roots of an extended affine Lie algebra is called an extended affine root system. Similar to affine Lie algebras, a root is called isotropic if any integral multiple of it is also a root. The dimension of the vector space spanned by isotropic roots is known as the nullity of an extended affine Lie algebra or its root system.
The finite and affine Lie algebras correspond to nullities 0 and 1, respectively.

Concerning the structure theory, two of the most important objects that are associated with an extended affine Lie algebra are the core which is the subalgebra generated by non-isotropic root spaces, and the centerless core which is the quotient algebra of the core over its center. Similarly, associated with an extended affine root system
R
there are some objects which are essential in its study, we mention some here which are more related to our work.
The image
\bar{R}
of
R
in the quotient space of the real span of roots over the span of isotropic roots is an irreducible finite root system whose rank and type are turned to be isomorphism invariants of
R
. These are called the rank and the nullity of
R
, respectively. In this work, we assume that
R
is reduced. The root system
R
can be then described with three ingredients; an irreducible finite root system
\dot{R}
isomorphic to
\bar{R}
, and two discrete subsets
S
and
L
of isotropic roots. The interrelations between
S
and
L
, called semilattices, reflect the interaction of reflections based on short and long roots of
R
.

In this study, we investigate the concepts of ”characters” and “diagonal automorphisms” for extended affine Lie algebras. These concepts exhibit characteristics in higher nullities which are absent in finite or affine cases. Our study of characters stems from our interest in Chevalley involutions. These are finite order automorphisms that act as minus identity on the Cartan subalgebra.
Chevalley involutions are essential in the study of Chevalley bases, and more generally in the modular theory of extended affine Lie algebras, see
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
. The Composition of two Chevalley involutions results in an automorphism that stabilizes pointwise the Cartan subalgebra, which we refer to as a Cartan automorphism. Involutions, in a more broad context have been studied in the context of involutive Lie algebras, see for example
[
10
]
and
[
7
]
.

Let
E
be an extended affine Lie algebra with Cartan subalgebra
{\mathcal{H}}
and root system
R
.
Since a Cartan automorphism
\Psi
stabilizes each root space, it acts on the root space
E_{\alpha}
,
\alpha
non-isotropic, as
\psi(\alpha)\hbox{id}
where
\psi(\alpha)
is a non-zero complex number. If
\Psi
is diagonal, i.e., if it also acts as a scalar multiple of identity on each isotropic root space, then the map

\psi:R\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
is called a core-character associated with
\Psi
for
R
.
If
\psi(\alpha+\beta)=\psi(\alpha)\psi(\beta)
for
\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\in R
, then
\psi
is called a character, and if
\psi
is extendable to the root lattice
\langle R\rangle
, we say that
\psi
is a root-lattice character. While any character for
R
leads to a diagonal automorphism, the converse is not necessarily true. In this work, we investigate the interrelations between Chevalley involutions, Cartan automorphisms, and characters. The paper is organized as follows.

In Section 2, we provide the definition of an extended affine Lie algebra denoted by
E
and report some fundamental facts about
E
and its root system. In particular, we explain the concepts of “index” and “reflectable base” which are essential to extended affine root systems.

In Section
3
, we establish certain basic facts about Chevalley involutions. Specially, we prove that any finite order Chevalley involution has order
2
, as stated in Lemma
3.1.2
, and that a finite-order automorphism whose restriction to the core is a Chevalley involution for the core is also a Chevalley involution for
E
only if it stabilizes the Cartan subalgebra, see Corollary
3.1.5
. Furthermore, we show that the existence of Chevalley involutions does not depend on the choice of the Cartan subalgebra, see Lemma
3.1.6
.

In Section 4, the core part of the paper, we consider an extended affine Lie algebra
E
of type
X
and
\hbox{rank}>1
, with root system
R
; also consider a finite order Cartan automorphism
\Psi
of
E
and let
\psi:R\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
be the core-character associated with
R
, see Definition
4.1.7
. We proceed with a type-dependent combinatorial approach to show that if
X\not=B_{\ell}
, or if
X=B_{\ell}
of index
0
, then
\Psi
is diagonal and
\psi
is a character, see Propositions
4.2.1
,
4.3.4
,
4.4.5
, and
4.4.10
.
The connections among Chevalley involutions, Cartan automorphisms, and characters are summarized in Theorem
4.4.11
.

In Section 5, the final section, we show that in contrast to the affine case, not every character for an extended affine root system is expendable to a root-lattice character, see Definition
4.1.2
and Corollary
5.2.2
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this work
{\mathbb{K}}
is the field of complex numbers, and all vector spaces are over
{\mathbb{K}}
unless otherwise is specified. We set
{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}={\mathbb{K}}\setminus\{0\}
.
We denote the dual space of a vector space
{\mathcal{U}}
with
{\mathcal{U}}^{\star}
. The notation
R_{1}\uplus R_{2}
means
the union of two disjoint sets
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. We denote by
\langle S\rangle
, the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-span of a subset
S
of a vector space.

2.1.
Extended affine Lie algebras and root systems

We begin by recalling some terminologies and basic facts about extended affine Lie algebras and extended affine root systems. For more details, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
and
[
15
]
.

2.1.1
.

An
extended affine Lie algebra
is a triple
(E,(\cdot,\cdot),{\mathcal{H}})
where

E
is a Lie algebra over
{\mathbb{K}}
,
{\mathcal{H}}
is a non-trivial finite dimensional subalgebra of
E
, and
(\cdot,\cdot)
is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-valued symmetric bilinear form on
E
satisfying axioms (A1)-(A6) below:

(A1) The bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
is invariant and non-degenerate.

(A2)
{\mathcal{H}}
is a splitting Cartan subalgebra of
E
meaning that

E=\sum_{\alpha\in R}E_{\alpha}
with

E_{\alpha}=\{x\in E\mid[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\hbox{ for all }h\in{\mathcal{H}}\},

R=\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}\mid E_{\alpha}\not=\{0\}\}
,
and
E_{0}={\mathcal{H}}.
The set
R
is called the
root system
of
E
.

From axioms (A1)-(A2), we see that the form
(\cdot,\cdot)
on
E
restricted to
{\mathcal{H}}
is non-degenerate. For
\alpha\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}
let
t_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{H}}

be the unique element satisfying
\alpha(h)=(h,t_{\alpha})
,
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and consider the form on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}
defined by
(\alpha,\beta):=(t_{\alpha},t_{\beta})
.
The root
\alpha
is called
isotropic
if

(\alpha,\alpha)=0
and
non-isotropic
otherwise.
We denote by
R^{0}
the set of isotropic roots and by
R^{\times}
the set of non-isotropic roots of
R
. The subalgebra
E_{c}
of
E
generated by non-isotropic root spaces is called the
core
of
E
.

(A3) For
\alpha\in R^{\times}
and
x\in E_{\alpha}
,
\hbox{ad}(x)
is locally nilpotent on
E
.

(A4)
E
is
tame
, that is, the centralizer of
E_{c}
in
E
is contained in
E_{c}
.

(A5) The
{\mathbb{Z}}
-span of
R
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}
is a free abelian group of finite rank.

(A6)
R^{\times}
is
indecomposable
meaning that if
R^{\times}=R_{1}\uplus R_{2}
with
R_{1}\not=\emptyset
and
R_{2}\not=\emptyset,
then
R_{1}\not\perp R_{2}
.

Remark 2.1.2
.

In order to better achieve our goals, we have chosen to include the tameness condition (axiom (A4)) in the definition of an extended affine Lie algebra, despite it is typically not included in the definition. It follows from the tameness condition that isotropic roots are non-isolated, meaning that for
\sigma\in R^{0}
, there exists
\alpha\in R^{\times}
with
\alpha+\sigma\in R
.

Assume that
(E,(\cdot,\cdot),{\mathcal{H}})
is an extended affine Lie algebra.
The term

h_{\alpha}:=\frac{2t_{\alpha}}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\qquad(\alpha\in R^{\times}),

will be used frequently in the sequel.
It follows that for
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, there exists
x_{\pm\alpha}\in E_{\pm\alpha}
such that
(x_{\alpha},h_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha})
forms an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple.
Also we recall from
[
6
, Remark 1.5(ii)]
and
[
1
, Chapter I]
that

(2.1)

[E_{\alpha},E_{\beta}]\not=\{0\}\qquad(\alpha\in R^{\times},\;\beta,\alpha+\beta\in R),

(2.2)

[x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha}]=(x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha})t_{\alpha}\qquad(\alpha\in R,{x_{\pm\alpha}\in E_{\pm\alpha}}).

2.1.3
.

Next, we recall some facts about the root system
R
of
E
which is called an
extended affine root system
. Set
{\mathcal{V}}:=\hbox{span}_{{\mathbb{R}}}R
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{0}:=\hbox{span}_{{\mathbb{R}}}R^{0}
.
It turns out that the form on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\star}
restricted to
{\mathcal{V}}
can be assumed to be positive semidefinite,
2\alpha\not\in R^{\times}
for
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, and that the root-string property holds, namely for
\alpha\in R^{\times}
and
\beta\in R
, there exist non-negative integers
d,u
such that

(2.3)

\{n\alpha+\beta\in R\mid n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\{\beta-d\alpha,\ldots,\beta,\ldots\beta+u\alpha\}

with

2(\beta,\alpha^{\vee})/(\alpha,\alpha)=d-u.
Moreover,
the image
\bar{R}
of
R
in
\bar{\mathcal{V}}:={\mathcal{V}}/{\mathcal{V}}^{0}
is an irreducible finite root system in
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
. One can find a preimage
\dot{R}
of
\bar{R}
in
{\mathcal{V}}
, under
\bar{\;}
, such that
\dot{R}
is an irreducible finite root system in its real span, isomorphic to
\bar{R}
. Then
R\subseteq\dot{R}+\Lambda
, where
\Lambda=\langle R^{0}\rangle
is a free abelian group of rank equal to
\dim{\mathcal{V}}^{0}
.

The
rank
and the
type
of
E
or
R
is by definition the rank and the type of
\dot{R}
. The dimension of
{\mathcal{V}}^{0}
is called the
nullity
of
R
. We denote the rank and the nullity of
R
by
\hbox{rank}(R)
and
\hbox{null}(R)
, respectively.
We note that
\hbox{rank}\langle R\rangle=\hbox{rank}(R)+\hbox{null}(R)
.
In this work,
we assume that
E
is of reduced type
.

2.2.
Semilattices

The internal structure of an extended affine root system
R
can be described in terms the finite root system
\dot{R}
and certain subsets of
R^{0}
called ”semilattices”.

2.2.1
.

A
semilattice
in a
\nu
-dimensional real vector space
{\mathcal{U}}
is a discrete spanning subset
S
containing
0
with
S\pm 2S\subseteq S
. It turns out that
\Lambda:=\langle S\rangle
is a lattice in
{\mathcal{U}}
, and
S
is the union of a set of cosets of
2\Lambda
in
\Lambda
including the trivial coset
2\Lambda
. Moreover,
S=\uplus_{i=0}^{m}(\tau_{i}+2\Lambda)
, where
\tau_{i}
’s can be chosen such that,
\tau_{i}
’s represent distinct cosets of
2\Lambda
,
\tau_{0}=0
, and
\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}{\mathbb{Z}}\tau_{i}
.
The integer
m
,
the number of non-trivial cosets of
2\Lambda
in
S
, is called the
index
of
S
, denoted by
\hbox{ind}(S)
. We call
\nu
the
rank
of
S
. Note that
\nu\leq\hbox{ind}(S)\leq 2^{\nu}
.

2.2.2
.

Now, we can give the following description of
R
, in terms of the length of roots and two involved semilattices,

(2.4)

\begin{array}[]{c}R^{0}=S+S\quad\hbox{and}\quad R=R^{0}\cup(\dot{R}_{sh}+S)\cup(\dot{R}_{lg}+L),\\
S+L=S,\quad kS+L=L,\end{array}

where
\dot{R}_{sh}
and
\dot{R}_{lg}
are the sets of short and long roots of
\dot{R}
, respectively, and
k
is the maximum number of edges appearing between two consecutive roots in the Dynkin diagram of
\dot{R}
. Here if
\dot{R}_{lg}=\emptyset
, we ignore in (
2.4
) all terms in which
\dot{R}_{lg}
or
L
appear. It turns out that

- If
R
is simply laced of rank
>1
, or is of type
F_{4}
or
G_{2}
, then
S
and
L
are lattices.

- If
R
is of type
B_{\ell}
(respectively
C_{\ell}
),
\ell\geq 3
, the
L
(respectively
S
) is a lattice.

To have a more precise description of
R
when it is of one of non-simply laced types, we need to introduce another isomorphism invariant of
R
called the
twist number
, given by
k^{t}=|\langle L\rangle/\langle S\rangle|
. If
R
has twist number
t
, then there exists semilattices
S_{1}
of rank
t
and
S_{2}
of rank
\nu-t
, such that

(2.5)

S=S_{1}\oplus\langle S_{2}\rangle\quad\hbox{and}\quad L=k\langle S_{1}\rangle\oplus S_{2}.

Next, we explain briefly about the Weyl group of
R
. Set
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}:={\mathcal{V}}\oplus({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star}
, where
({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star}
is the dual space of
{\mathcal{V}}^{0}
. The form
(\cdot,\cdot)
on
{\mathcal{V}}
extends to a non-degenerate form on
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
by

(\dot{\mathcal{V}},({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star})=(({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star},({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star})=\{0\},\;(\gamma,\sigma):=\gamma(\sigma),\quad(\gamma\in({\mathcal{V}}^{0})^{\star},\sigma\in{\mathcal{V}}^{0}).

For
\alpha\in R^{\times}
the reflection
w_{\alpha}\in GL(\tilde{\mathcal{V}})
is defined by

w_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta-(2(\alpha,\beta)/(\alpha,\alpha))\alpha.
The
Weyl group

{\mathcal{W}}
of
R
is the subgroup of
GL(\tilde{\mathcal{V}})
generated by reflections
w_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R^{\times}.

2.2.3
.

For
\mathcal{P}\subseteq R^{\times}
, denote by
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{P}}
the subgroup of
{\mathcal{W}}
generated by reflections
w_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
. A
reflectable base
for
R
is a subset
\mathcal{P}
of
R^{\times}
such that
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{P}=R^{\times}
and no proper subset of
\mathcal{P}
has this property. According to
[
4
, Corollary 4.2.2]
, any reflectable base is finite.

We define
\hbox{refl}(R)
to be the smallest cardinality of a reflectable base for
R
, and
\hbox{ind}(R)
as
\hbox{refl}(R)-\hbox{rank}(R)-\hbox{null}(R)=\hbox{refl}(R)-\hbox{rank}\langle R\rangle
. These terms, along with
\hbox{rank}(R)
and
\hbox{null}(R)
, are all isomorphism invariants for
R
.
We may also describe the
\hbox{ind}(R)
in terms of rank
\ell
, nullity
\nu
, and semilattices
S,

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
given in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). In fact, depending on type
X
of
R
, we have

(2.6)

\hbox{ind}(R)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hbox{ind}(S)-\ell-\nu&X=A_{1},\\
0&X=A_{\ell}\;(\ell\geq 2),D_{\ell},E_{6,7,8},F_{4},G_{2},\\
\hbox{ind}(S_{1})+\hbox{ind}(S_{2})-\nu&X=B_{2},\\
\hbox{ind}(S_{1})-t&X=B_{\ell},\;\ell\geq 3,\\
\hbox{ind}(S_{2})-\nu-t&X=C_{\ell},\;\ell\geq 3.\end{array}\right.

Note that in type
B_{\ell}
,
\ell\geq 3
, if
\hbox{ind}(R)=0
, then
\hbox{ind}(S_{1})=t=\hbox{rank}(S_{1})
, so as we saw in
2.2.1
, in this case we have

S_{1}=\cup_{i=0}^{t}(\tau_{i}+2\langle S_{1}\rangle)
with
\langle S_{1}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}{\mathbb{Z}}\tau_{i}
.

Extended affine root systems of index
0
are more similar to finite and affine root systems than those with a non-zero index, as per
[
4
]
. We note from
[
5
, §4]
that
\hbox{ind}(R)=0
if
R
is finite or affine (i.e., if
\hbox{null}(R)=0
or
1
) or if
R
has one of the types
A_{\ell}
(
\ell\geq 2
),
D_{\ell}
(
\ell\geq 4
),
F_{4}
,
G_{2}
,
E_{6,7,8}
. Therefore, the extended affine root systems with a non-zero index occur only in types
A_{1}
,
B_{\ell}
, and
C_{\ell}
.

3.
Chevalley involutions

We begin the section by establishing some fundamental facts regarding Chevalley involutions for an extended affine Lie algebra
E=(E,(\cdot,\cdot),{\mathcal{H}})
.
We set
E_{c,\alpha}:=E_{c}\cap E_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in R
. When we refer to an involution on a vector space, we are referring to an automorphism of order
2
.

3.1.
Chevalley involutions

Although we have assumed that all extended affine Lie algebras in this work are reduced, the results in this subsection are also valid for non-reduced ones.

Lemma 3.1.1
.

Suppose
\tau
is a finite order automorphism of an extended affine Lie algebra. Then
\tau=\hbox{id}
if and only the restriction of
\tau
to the core is identity.

Proof.
Suppose
\tau_{|_{E_{c}}}=\hbox{id}_{E_{c}}
. Let
x\in E
and
y\in E_{c}
.
Since
E_{c}
is an ideal of
E
, we get
[x,y]=\tau[x,y]=[\tau(x),y]
.
Therefore,
\tau(x)-x
is in centralizer of the core. Since
E
is tame,
\tau(x)-x\in E_{c}
and so

\tau(\tau(x)-x)=\tau(x)-x,
for each
x\in E
. Thus
\tau
satisfies the polynomial
(t-1)^{2}=0
. Since

\tau
is of finite order, we get
\tau=\hbox{id}
.
\Box

Lemma 3.1.2
.

Let
\tau
be a finite order automorphism of
E
. Fix
\epsilon\in\{1,-1\}
.

(i) If
\tau(E_{\alpha})=E_{\epsilon\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, then

-
\tau_{|_{{\mathcal{H}}\cap E_{c}}}=\epsilon\hbox{id},

- if
\epsilon=-1
then
\tau^{2}=\hbox{id}
,

-
\tau_{|_{\mathcal{H}}}=\epsilon\hbox{id}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
mod
Z(E_{c})
.

(ii)
\tau(E_{\alpha})=E_{\epsilon\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in R
if and only if

\tau(h)=\epsilon h
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

(iii) If
\tau(E_{\alpha})=E_{\epsilon\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in R
, then
\tau
preserves the form.

Proof.
(i) Assume that
\tau(E_{\alpha})=E_{\epsilon\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in R^{\times}
. For
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, fix

x_{\pm\alpha}\in E_{\pm\alpha}
such that
[x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha}]=h_{\alpha}
. Then

\tau(x_{\alpha})=\mu_{\alpha}x_{\epsilon\alpha}
for some
\mu_{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
. So

\tau(h_{\alpha})=\tau[x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha}]=\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}[x_{\epsilon\alpha},x_{-\epsilon\alpha}]=\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}h_{\epsilon\alpha}=\epsilon\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}h_{\alpha}.

Now by applying
\tau
to the both sides of
2x_{\alpha}=[h_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}]
, we get

2\mu_{\alpha}x_{\epsilon\alpha}=\epsilon\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}[h_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha}x_{\epsilon\alpha}]=\epsilon^{2}2\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\mu_{-\alpha}x_{\epsilon\alpha}=2\mu_{\alpha}^{2}\mu_{-\alpha}x_{\epsilon\alpha}.

Thus

(3.1)

\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}=1\quad\hbox{and}\quad\tau(h_{\alpha})=\epsilon h_{\alpha},\hbox{ for each }\alpha\in R^{\times}.

Since
{\mathcal{H}}\cap E_{c}=\sum_{\alpha\in R^{\times}}h_{\alpha}
, we get

(3.2)

\tau_{|_{{\mathcal{H}}\cap E_{c}}}=\epsilon\hbox{id}.

Note that if
\epsilon=-1
then form (
3.1
) for
\alpha\in R^{\times}
we have

\tau^{2}(x_{\alpha})=\mu_{\alpha}\mu_{-\alpha}x_{\alpha}=x_{\alpha}
.
Since
E_{c}
is generated by
x_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, we get
\tau^{2}=\hbox{id}_{|_{E_{c}}}
, and so by Lemma
3.1.1
,
\tau^{2}=\hbox{id}
.

Next for
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, we have

\displaystyle[h-\epsilon\tau(h),x_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[h,x_{\alpha}]-\epsilon\tau[h,\mu_{\epsilon\alpha}^{-1}x_{\epsilon\alpha}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(h)x_{\alpha}-\epsilon\epsilon\mu_{\epsilon\alpha}^{-1}\alpha(h)\tau(x_{\epsilon\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(h)x_{\alpha}-\alpha(h)\mu_{\epsilon\alpha}^{-1}\mu_{\epsilon\alpha}x_{\alpha}=0.

Therefore,
h-\epsilon\tau(h)
is in the centralizer of
E_{c}
in
E
for each
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Since
E
is tame,

h-\epsilon\tau(h)\in Z(E_{c})
.

(ii) Assume first that
\tau(E_{\alpha})=E_{\epsilon\alpha}
for all
\alpha
; in particular,

\tau({\mathcal{H}})=\tau(E_{0})=E_{0}={\mathcal{H}}
. So from part (i), we have
h-\epsilon\tau(h)\in{\mathcal{H}}\cap Z(E_{c})
. Then from (
3.2
) we have

\tau(h-\epsilon\tau(h))=\epsilon(h-\epsilon\tau(h))
for each
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Thus
\epsilon\tau
restricted to
{\mathcal{H}}
satisfies the equation
(t-1)^{2}=0
. Since
\tau
is of finite order, it follows that
\tau_{|_{\mathcal{H}}}=\epsilon\hbox{id}
.

Conversely, assume that
\tau_{|_{\mathcal{H}}}=\epsilon\hbox{id}.
Then
for
\alpha\in R
,
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
x\in E_{\alpha}
, we have

[h,\tau(x)]=\tau[\epsilon h,x]=\epsilon\alpha(h)\tau(x),

and so
\tau(x)\in E_{\epsilon\alpha}.

(iii) By (ii), for
h,h^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}=E_{0}
, we have

(\tau(h),\tau(h^{\prime}))=(\epsilon h,\epsilon h^{\prime})=(h,h^{\prime})
. Assume now that
0\not=\alpha\in R
,
\beta\in R
.
Let

Definition 3.1.3
.

(i) We call a finite order automorphism

Remark 3.1.4

Let

.

Since

Lemma 3.1.6
.

The existence of Chevalley involutions for an extended affine Lie algebra does not depend on the choice of the Cartan subalgebra.

Proof.
Suppose

and

4.
Cartan automorphisms, and characters

Assume that
E=(E,(\cdot,\cdot),{\mathcal{H}})
is an extended affine Lie algebra with root system
R
. The composition of two Chevalley involutions for
E
is referred to as a ”Cartan automorphism” since it stabilizes pointwise the elements of the Cartan subalgebra
{\mathcal{H}}
and thus, stabilizes each root space. Moreover, the composition of a Cartan automorphism and a Chevalley involution results in another Chevalley involution. In turn, Cartan automorphisms can be constructed using the ”characters” of the root system, and vice versa, to each Cartan automorphism one associates a map that is perceivable to be generally a character. This section studies the relationships between Chevalley involutions, Cartan automorphisms, and characters.

4.1.
Characters

We begin by giving definitions of Cartan automorphisms, characters, and some related terms.

Definition 4.1.1
.

Let
(E,(\cdot,\cdot),{\mathcal{H}})
be an extended affine Lie algebra and
R
be an extended affine root system.

(i) A Cartan automorphism
\psi
for
E
is the one that satisfies
\psi(h)=h
for all
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. It is evident that Cartan automorphisms stabilize each root space.

(ii) We call a Cartan automorphism
diagonal
if it acts as a scalar multiple of identity when restricted to each root space.

Definition 4.1.2
.

(i) We call a map
\psi:R\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
, a
core-character
for
R
if

\psi(\alpha+\beta)=\psi(\alpha)\psi(\beta)
, for
\alpha\in R^{\times},

\beta,\alpha+\beta\in R
.
A core-character
\psi
is called of
finite-order
if there exists
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
with
\psi(\alpha)^{m}=1
for all
\alpha\in R
.
If
R
has type
X
, we call a core-character for
R
a
core-character of type

X
.

(ii) A core-character
\psi
is called a
character
if

\psi(\alpha+\beta)=\psi(\alpha)\psi(\beta)
for
\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\in R
.
A
root-lattice character
for
R
is a group homomorphism
\psi:\langle R\rangle\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
.

Example 4.1.3
.

(i) Given an extended affine Lie algebra
E
with root system
R
and a (finite-)order character
\Phi:R\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
, the linear map defined by
\bar{\Phi}(x)=\Phi(\alpha)x
, for
x\in E_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R
, defines a (finite order) diagonal automorphism for
E
.

(ii) Let
m
be a positive integer. Given an extended affine root system
R
, fix a basis
\mathcal{P}
for the free abelian group
\langle R\rangle
. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
fix an
m
-th root of unity
\eta_{\alpha}
.
Then the group homomorphism
\Phi:\langle R\rangle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
induced by
\Phi(\alpha)=\eta_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
, defines a finite-order root-lattice character for
R
.

Remark 4.1.4
.

(i) It is immediate that a Cartan automorphism stabilizes each root space.
Conversely, suppose
\psi
is a finite order automorphism of
E
that stabilizes each root space. Then by Lemma
3.1.2
(ii),
\psi
is a Cartan automorphism.

(ii) In
[
14
]
, the author classifies involutive automorphisms (automorphisms of order 2) for affine Kac-Moody Lie algebras by dividing them into two categories: first and second kind automorphisms. Chevalley involutions belong to the second kind, while Cartan involutions belong to the first kind.

(iii) By Lemma
3.1.2
(iii), any finite order Cartan automorphism preserves the form.

Previously, in Example
4.1.3
, we learned about producing non-trivial finite order diagonal automorphisms for the extended affine Lie algebra
E
using a finite-order character for the root system
R
. But does every finite order diagonal automorphism arise this way? We will find out in the following sections that the answer is ”almost” affirmative, with a few exceptions in types
A_{1}
and
B_{\ell}
.

From now on we assume that
E
is an extended affine Lie algebra with root system
R
, and we fix a Cartan automorphism
\Psi
. Hereafter, whenever we write
x_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in R
, we mean
x_{\alpha}
is a non-zero element of
E_{\alpha}
.

For
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, we define

\eta_{\alpha}
by

(4.1)

\Psi(x_{\alpha})=\eta_{\alpha}x_{\alpha},\quad(0\not=x\in E_{\alpha},\alpha\in R^{\times}).

Now let
\sigma\in R^{0}
. Since isotropic roots are non-isolated, there exists
\alpha\in R^{\times}
with

\alpha+\sigma\in R^{\times}
. We define

\eta_{\sigma}^{\alpha}:=\eta_{\alpha+\sigma}\eta_{-\alpha},\quad(\sigma\in R^{0},\alpha,\alpha+\sigma\in R^{\times}).

Convention 4.1.5
.

Whenever we use the notation

\alpha\in R^{\times}
,
\sigma\in R^{0}
,
\alpha+\sigma\in R^{\times}

Lemma 4.1.6
.

Let
\Psi
be a finite order Cartan automorphism. Then

is diagonal if and only if
\eta^{\alpha}_{\sigma}=\eta^{\beta}_{\sigma}
,

\alpha,\beta\in R^{\times}
,
\sigma\in R^{0}

Proof.
Assume first that
\Psi
is diagonal. Then

\Psi_{|_{E_{\alpha}}}=\eta_{\alpha}\hbox{id}_{|_{E_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\eta_{\sigma}[x_{-\alpha},[x_{\alpha},x_{\sigma}]]

\displaystyle\Psi([x_{-\alpha},[x_{\alpha},x_{\sigma}]])

\displaystyle[\Psi(x_{-\alpha}),\Psi[x_{\alpha},x_{\sigma}]]

\displaystyle\eta_{-\alpha}\eta_{\alpha+\sigma}[x_{-\alpha},[x_{\alpha},x_{\sigma}]]

\displaystyle{\eta_{\sigma}^{\alpha}[x_{-\alpha},[x_{\alpha},x_{\sigma}]].}

Conversely, assume that

Proposition 4.1.8

Let
\Psi

5.
Characters vs. root-lattice characters

In this section, we discuss the possibility of extending a character for an extended affine root system to a root-lattice character.

5.1.
Extendability in index zero

We begin with the following lemma for extended affine roots systems which resembles and
extends a familiar result for finite root systems, see
[
11

Lemma 5.1.1
.

Let
R
be an extended affine root system, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1}\in R^{\times}
. Then
w_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots w_{\alpha_{n}}(\alpha_{n+1})=\epsilon_{1}\alpha_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\epsilon_{m}\alpha_{i_{m}}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\alpha_{i_{j}}\in\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1}\}
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
, such that
\epsilon_{1}\alpha_{i_{1}}+\cdots\epsilon_{k}\alpha_{i_{k}}\in R
for
1\leq k\leq m
.

Proof.
Assume
\beta=w_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots w_{\alpha_{n}}(\alpha_{n+1})
. We use induction on
n
.
Assume first that
n=1
, namely
\beta=w_{\alpha_{1}}(\alpha_{2})
. If
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=0
, or
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are linearly dependent, we are clearly done. Otherwise, there exists
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
\{\epsilon_{1}\alpha_{1},\epsilon_{2}\alpha_{2}\}
is a base for the rank
2
finite root system
R\cap({\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{2})
.
Then the claim is known from finite dimensional theory, see
[
11
]
.
Assume next that
n>1
. Set
\gamma:=w_{\alpha_{2}}\cdots w_{\alpha_{n}}(\alpha_{n+1})
. By induction steps,

\gamma=\epsilon_{2}\alpha_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\epsilon_{p}\alpha_{i_{p}}
, where
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
\alpha_{i_{j}}\in\{\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n+1}\}
,
\epsilon_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
and

\epsilon_{2}\alpha_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\epsilon_{k}\alpha_{i_{k}}\in R
for
2\leq k\leq p.

Now,
\beta=w_{\alpha_{1}}(\gamma)
. If
(\gamma,\alpha_{1})=0
, or
\gamma
and
\alpha_{1}
are linearly dependent we are done. Otherwise, we consider the rank
2
finite root system

R\cap({\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}\gamma)
and we may assume that, up to a plus-minus sign of
\gamma
,
\{\alpha_{1},\gamma\}
is a base for this root system.
Then using the realization of rank
2
finite root systems, we have

\beta=w_{\alpha_{1}}(\gamma)\in\{\gamma\pm\alpha_{1},\gamma\pm 2\alpha_{1},\gamma\pm 3\alpha_{1}\}.

Correspondingly,
\beta=\gamma\pm\alpha_{1}
,
\beta=\gamma\pm\alpha_{1}\pm\alpha_{1}
or
\beta=\gamma\pm\alpha_{1}\pm\alpha_{1}\pm\alpha_{1}

Set

(5.2)

S=\cup_{i=0}^{m}(\tau_{i}+2\Lambda)\quad\hbox{and}\quad R=(S+S)\cup(\pm\dot{\alpha}+S).

Then
S
is a semilattice in
{\mathcal{V}}^{0}
and
R
is an extended affine root system of type
A_{1}
in
{\mathcal{V}}
with
R^{0}=S+S
, moreover, any extended affine root system of type
A_{1}
can be described this way, see
2.5
and
[
1
, Theorem II.2.37]
.

Lemma 5.2.1
.

Consider the extended affine root system
R=R^{0}\cup(\pm\dot{\alpha}+S)
of type
A_{1}
, where
S=\cup_{i=0}^{m}(\tau_{i}+2\Lambda)
is given by (
5.1
) and (
5.2
). Assume further that

(
\star
)

\tau_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\tau_{i_{k}}\not\equiv 0\hbox{ for }3\leq k\leq 6\hbox{ and distinct }1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leq m.

Define
\Phi:R^{\times}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
by

\Phi(\pm\dot{\alpha}+2\Lambda)=1,\;\Phi(\pm\dot{\alpha}+\tau_{i}+2\Lambda)=-1\hbox{ for }1\leq i\leq m,

and
\Phi:R^{0}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
by

\Phi(\sigma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{if }\sigma\in 2\Lambda\cup((S+S)\setminus S),\\
-1&S\setminus 2\Lambda.\end{array}\right.

Then
\Phi:R\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
is a character for
R
.

Proof.
First we note that the assumption (
\star
) implies that

(5.3)

\begin{array}[]{c}\tau_{i_{1}}+\tau_{i_{2}}\not\in S,\;\tau_{i_{1}}+\tau_{i_{2}}+\tau_{i_{3}}\not\in R^{0},\;\tau_{i_{1}}+\tau_{i_{2}}+\tau_{i_{3}}+\tau_{i_{4}}\not\in R^{0},\\
\hbox{ if $\tau_{i_{j}}$'s are distinct}.\end{array}

Now since
\tau_{i_{1}}+\tau_{i_{2}}\not\in S
for
1\leq i_{1}\not=i_{2}\leq m
, we get
R^{0}=(S+S)=2\Lambda\uplus(S\setminus 2\Lambda)\uplus((S+S)\setminus S).
In fact if
\sigma\equiv\tau_{i}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\equiv\tau_{j}
are elements of
S
, then

\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}\in(S+S)\setminus S&\hbox{if }1\leq i\not=j\leq m,\\
2\tau_{i}\in 2\Lambda&\hbox{if }i=j,\\
\tau_{j}\in S\setminus 2\Lambda&\hbox{if }i=0,\;j\not=0.\end{array}\right.

Therefore
\Phi:R\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
is a well defined map. From definition of
\Phi
it follows that
\Phi(\alpha)=\Phi(\beta)
if
\alpha\equiv\beta
.
We proceed to show that
\Phi
is a character. In the following, when there is symmetry in the indices, we present the argument for one and leave the others.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in R
with
\alpha+\beta\in R
.
If
\alpha,\beta\in R^{\times}
, then there exist
i,j
and
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
such that

\alpha\equiv\epsilon\dot{\alpha}+\tau_{i}
,
\beta\equiv-\epsilon\dot{\alpha}+\tau_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=\Phi(\tau_{i}+\tau_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{if }i=j\hbox{ or }1\leq i\not=j\leq m,\\
-1&i=0,\;j>0.\\
\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(\alpha)\Phi(\beta).

If
\alpha\in R^{\times}
and
\beta\in R^{0}
, then
\alpha\equiv\epsilon\dot{\alpha}+\tau_{i}
and

\beta\equiv\tau_{j}+\tau_{k}
for some
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
and some
0\leq i,j,k\leq m
.
If
|\{i,j,k\}|=3
, then as
\alpha+\beta\in R
and
\tau_{s}+\tau_{t}\not\in S
if
s\not=t
, we get
1\leq i,j,k\leq m
and

\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}+\tau_{k}\in S\setminus 2\Lambda
. Thus

\Phi(\alpha)=-1
,
\Phi(\beta)=1
and
\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1
.

Next, assume that
|\{i,j,k\}|=2
. We have the following possibilities, up to symmetry between
j,k
:

j=k,\;i=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

j=k,\;i\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

j\not=k,\;i=j=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=-1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

j\not=k,\;i=j\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1.

If
|\{i,j,k\}|=1
, we have the following possibilities:

i=j=k=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i=j=k\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1.

The above computations show that
\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=\Phi(\alpha)\Phi(\beta)
for
\alpha\in R^{\times}

\beta\in R^{0}
,
\alpha+\beta\in R
.

Finally, we consider the case
\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\in R^{0}=S+S
. We have
\alpha\equiv\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}
,
\beta\equiv\tau_{k}+\tau_{t}
for
some
0\leq i,j,k,t\leq m
. We have the following possibilities, up to symmetry between
i,j
or
k,t
:

i=j,\;k=t\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i=j=k\not=t=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\Phi(\beta)=-1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

i=j=k=0,\;t\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=-1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

i=j=0,\;k=t\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i=j=0,\;k,t\not=0,\;k\not=t\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i=0,\;j=k=t\not=0\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

i=0,\;j,k,t\not=0,\;j=k\not=t\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=-1,

i=0,\;j,k,t\not=0,\;|\{j,k,t\}|=3\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=-1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\alpha+\beta\equiv\tau_{j}+\tau_{k}+\tau_{t}\not\in R^{0},

see (
5.3
) for the conclusion that
\alpha+\beta\not\in R^{0}
in the last implication.
The cases we discussed exhaust situations in which
i=j,\;k=t
or at least one of
i,j,k,t
is zero.

Next we consider the remaining cases, namely
1\leq i,j,k,t\leq m
, and
|\{i,j,k,t\}|\geq 2
:

i=j=k\not=t\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i=j,k\not=t,\;\{i,j,k\}|=3\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

i\not=j=k\not=t,\;\{i,j,t\}|=3\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\Phi(\alpha+\beta)=1,

|\{i,j,k,t\}|=4\Rightarrow\Phi(\alpha)=1,\;\Phi(\beta)=1,\;\alpha+\beta\equiv\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}+\tau_{k}+\tau_{t}\not\in R^{0},

see (
5.3
) for the conclusion that
\alpha+\beta\not\in R^{0}
in the last implication.
\Box

We continue the section by showing that a character for
R
may fail to extend to a character for
\langle R\rangle
.

Corollary 5.2.2
.

There exist certain characters for extended affine root systems that are not extendable to root-lattice character.

Proof.
Let
R
be an extended affine root system of type
A_{1}
as in (
5.2
) with
\nu\geq 6
,
\tau_{i}=\sigma_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 6
and

\tau_{7}=\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{6}
. Note that with our choices of
\tau_{i}
’s, the conditions (
\star
) in the statement of Lemma
5.2.1
is fulfilled. Now if the character
\Phi
is extendable to a character
\bar{\Phi}
for
\langle R\rangle
, then we would have
\bar{\Phi}(\tau_{7})=\prod_{i=1}^{6}\bar{\Phi}(\sigma_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{6}\Phi(\sigma_{i})=1
. On the other hand
by definition of
\Phi
, we have
\bar{\Phi}(\tau_{7})=\Phi(\tau_{7})=-1
, a contradiction.
\Box
