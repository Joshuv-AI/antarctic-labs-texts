Abstract
. We establish existence, uniqueness and ergodicity results for Patterson–Sullivan measures for relatively Anosov groups. As applications we obtain an entropy gap theorem and a strict concavity result for entropies associated to linear functionals.



1.
Introduction

Anosov subgroups and relatively Anosov subgroups of semisimple Lie groups are respectively natural generalizations of convex cocompact subgroups and geometrically finite subgroups of rank one semisimple Lie groups to the higher rank setting. Patterson–Sullivan measures for Anosov subgroups have been extensively studied, see
[
18
,
38
,
12
,
33
]
.
They have also been studied for relatively Anosov subgroups of the projective general linear group which preserve a properly convex domain, see
[
4
,
5
,
9
,
42
,
10
]
. More generally, Patterson–Sullivan measures can be constructed for transverse subgroups, of which Anosov subgroups, relatively Anosov subgroups, and discrete subgroups of rank one semisimple Lie groups are examples, see
[
15
,
30
,
31
]
. For a more detailed historical discussion of Patterson–Sullivan measures for discrete subgroups of semisimple Lie groups see
[
15
, Sec. 1.1]
.

In this paper we study Patterson–Sullivan measures for relatively Anosov subgroups of semisimple Lie groups. The key new result here is that the Poincaré series associated to such a Patterson–Sullivan measure always diverges at its critical exponent if this critical exponent is finite. As a consequence, we establish uniqueness and ergodicity results for such Patterson–Sullivan measures. We then derive an entropy gap theorem and a strict concavity result for the entropy.

Delaying precise definitions until Sections
2
,
3
and
4
, we informally introduce the notation
necessary to state our main results.
For the entire paper,
\mathsf{G}
will be a connected semisimple Lie group without compact factors and with finite center. We fix a Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}+\mathfrak{p}

of the Lie algebra of
\mathsf{G}
, a Cartan subspace
{\mathfrak{a}}\subset{\mathfrak{p}}
, and a Weyl chamber
\mathfrak{a}^{+}\subset\mathfrak{a}
. Let
\Delta\subset{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}
be the corresponding system of simple restricted roots, and let
\kappa:\mathsf{G}\to{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
denote the associated Cartan projection.
Given a subset

\theta\subset\Delta
, we let
\Psf_{\theta}\subset\mathsf{G}
denote the associated parabolic subgroup and let
\Fc_{\theta}=\mathsf{G}/\Psf_{\theta}
denote the associated flag manifold. We will always assume that
\theta
is symmetric.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a non-elementary discrete subgroup which, as an abstract group, is relatively hyperbolic with respect to
a finite collection
\mathcal{P}
of subgroups in
\Gamma
. Let
\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
denote its associated Bowditch boundary.
Then
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
if
\alpha\circ\kappa
is proper on
\Gamma
for all
\alpha\in\theta
, its limit set
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
in

\Fc_{\theta}
consists of mutually transverse flags and there exists a continuous
\Gamma
-equivariant map

\xi:\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})\rightarrow\Fc_{\theta}

which is a homeomorphism onto
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
.

The action of
\mathsf{G}
on
\Fc_{\theta}
preserves a vector valued cocycle
B_{\theta}:\mathsf{G}\times\Fc_{\theta}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}
, called the
partial Iwasawa cocycle
, whose image lies in a subspace
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}\subset{\mathfrak{a}}
associated to
\theta
. If
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
, then the composition
\phi\circ B_{\theta}
is a real valued cocycle, which allows us to define Patterson–Sullivan measures associated to such
\phi
. More precisely, if
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
, then a
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure for
\Gamma
of dimension
\beta
is a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\Fc_{\theta}
where

(1)

{\rm supp}(\mu)\subset\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
,

(2)

for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
the measures
\gamma_{*}\mu
,
\mu
are absolutely continuous and

\frac{d\gamma_{*}\mu}{d\mu}(F)=e^{-\beta\phi(B_{\theta}(\gamma^{-1},F))}

for
\mu
-almost every
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
.

In the case when
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{SO}_{0}(d,1)
is the group of orientation-preserving isometries of real hyperbolic
d
-space
\Hb^{d}
, there is a single simple restricted root
\Delta=\{\alpha\}
and

\Fc_{\alpha}
naturally identifies with the geodesic boundary of
\Hb^{d}_{\Rb}
. Further (up to scaling),
\alpha\circ B_{\alpha}
identifies with the usual Busemann cocycle.
Since a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathsf{SO}_{0}(d,1)
is relatively
\Psf_{\alpha}
-Anosov if and only if it is geometrically finite, the above definition encompasses the classical notion of Patterson–Sullivan measures for geometrically finite Kleinian groups.

As in the classical theory, there is an associated Poincaré series and critical exponent. Let
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}
denote the
partial Cartan projection

defined in Section
2
. Then given
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
,
s>0
and a discrete group
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
, the
\phi
-Poincaré series
is

Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(s):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}.

The
\phi
-critical exponent
, denoted
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)\in[0,+\infty]
, is the critical exponent of the above series, that is
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(s)
converges
when
s>\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
and diverges when
0<s<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
.
Given
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
let
\bar{\phi}\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
be the unique functional where
\bar{\phi}(\kappa_{\theta}(g))=\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g^{-1}))
for all
g\in\mathsf{G}
.
Notice that
Q^{\phi}_{\Gamma}=Q^{\bar{\phi}}_{\Gamma}
and so
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)=\delta^{\bar{\phi}}(\Gamma)
.

The main result of this paper is that if
\Gamma
is relatively Anosov and its
\phi
-critical exponent is finite, then its
\phi
-Poincaré series diverges at its critical exponent.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
8.1
)
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
,
then
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}
diverges at its critical exponent.

Remark 1.2
.

As mentioned above, in this paper we assume that relatively Anosov groups are non-elementary. Theorem
8.1
holds for elementary transverse groups (see Remark
8.5
), however the non-elementary assumption is necessary for many of the applications of the theorem.

Later, we will discuss some important consequences of Theorem
1.1
, and also provide an outline of its proof (in Sections
1.1
and
1.2
respectively). Notice that Theorem
1.1
fails in the setting of transverse groups, since the Poincaré series of any finitely generated,
geometrically infinite discrete subgroup of
\mathsf{SO}(3,1)
whose limit
set is not all of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{3}
converges at its critical exponent (see
[
13
, Cor. 4.2]
and
[
40
, Thm. 2.17,  Cor. 2.18]
).

We also provide a characterization of the functionals with finite critical exponent, surprisingly the only requirement is that
\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\cdot))
converges to infinity along escaping sequences in the group. We also show that if
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
, then
\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\cdot))
has linear lower and upper bounds in terms of the distance

{\rm d}_{M}
on the Riemannian symmetric space
M
associated to
\mathsf{G}
.

Theorem 1.3

(see Section
10
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
and
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty
for every sequence of distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
.

(2)

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
.

Moreover, if
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
, then the above conditions are equivalent to:

(3)

If
x_{0}\in M
, there exist constants
c\geq 1
and
C>0
such that

\frac{1}{c}{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})-C\leq\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\leq c{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})+C

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

We will observe later, see Section
1.3
, that in Theorem
1.3
, Condition (3) is not equivalent to Conditions (1) and (2) without the assumption that
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
. Also, in the spirit of Sambarino’s analogous result for Anosov groups
[
38
]
, we observe that Theorem
1.3
implies that

\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
has finite critical exponent if and only if
\phi
is positive on the
\theta
-Benoist limit cone, see Section
10
.

A discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}

\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse

\alpha\circ\kappa

\Gamma

Theorem 1.4

(
[
15
, Thm. 1.4]
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a non-elementary
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse subgroup,
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\theta}
and

•

If

,
then there exists a unique
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure
\mu_{\phi}
for
\Gamma
of dimension
\delta
and there exists a unique
\bar{\phi}
-Patterson–Sullivan measure
\mu_{\bar{\phi}}
for
\Gamma
of dimension
\delta

(1)

\mu_{\phi}(\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma))=1=\mu_{\bar{\phi}}(\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma))
.

(2)

The action of
\Gamma
on

and

(3)

The action of
\Gamma
on
(\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)^{2},\mu_{\bar{\phi}}\otimes\mu_{\phi})

•

If
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(\delta)<+\infty
, then
\mu(\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma))=0
for any
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure

for
\Gamma
.

If
\Gamma
is a

-Anosov subgroup relative to

is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse. Thus, we obtain, as a consequence of Theorem

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}

(1)

There is a unique
\phi

In

15
]
, we also established a criterion for when the critical exponent of a subgroup of a transverse group is strictly less than that of the entire group.

(
[

1.2.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is inspired by earlier work of Blayac–Zhu
[
5
]
in the context of relatively hyperbolic groups preserving properly convex domains and Patterson–Sullivan measures defined using the Busemann functions associated to the Hilbert distance. The key technical result needed to prove Theorem
1.1
is that if
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
, then the
\phi
-Poincaré series of any peripheral
subgroup diverges at its critical exponent.

Theorem 1.11

(Theorem
7.1
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
.
If
P\in\mathcal{P}
, then
Q_{P}^{\phi}
diverges at its critical exponent.

Together, Theorems
1.6
and
1.11
imply that
\delta^{\phi}(P)<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. We may then adapt arguments of Dal’bo–Otal–Piegné
[
17
]
to our setting to conclude Theorem
1.1
.

The proof of Theorem
1.11
makes use of Hironaka’s famous result on the resolution of singularities. More precisely, in Section
7
, we relate the Poincaré series
Q_{P}^{\phi}(s)
associated to a peripheral subgroup
P
to an integral of the form

\int_{\Rb^{n}}\big(R_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots R_{m}^{\ell_{m}}\big)^{-s}d\lambda

(1)

where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure,
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}:\Rb^{n}\rightarrow\Rb
are positive rational functions that are defined everywhere and
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}
are real numbers such that
R_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots R_{m}^{\ell_{m}}
is a proper function. In Section
6
, we use Hironaka’s resolution of singularities to show that the integral in Equation (
1
) diverges at its critical exponent.
This in turn implies that the Poincaré series associated to any peripheral subgroup diverges at its critical exponent.

In Blayac and Zhu’s setting, the integral in Equation (
1
) involves a single rational function (i.e.
m=1
) and one can deduce that it diverges at its critical exponent from a result of Benoist–Oh
[
3
, Prop. 7.2]
(see the proof of
[
5
, Lem. 8.9]
).
The case when
m>1
is more technical and our use of Hironaka’s resolution of singularities to understand the integral at infinity is motivated by Benoist and Oh’s arguments.

1.3.
Conditions (1) and (2) are not equivalent to (3) in Theorem
1.3

We now give an example which shows that Condition (3) in Theorem
1.3

may fail to be equivalent to Conditions (1) and (2) when
\Psf_{\theta}
contains a simple factor of
\mathsf{G}
.

Example 1.12
.

Let
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)\times\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)
. Note that

\mathfrak{a}:=\left\{{\rm diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{4}):a_{1}+a_{2}=0=a_{3}+a_{4}\right\}

is a Cartan subspace of
\mathsf{G}
and
\Delta:=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}^{\prime}\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is a system of simple restricted roots, where

\alpha_{1}({\rm diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{4}))=a_{1}-a_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{1}^{\prime}({\rm diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{4}))=a_{3}-a_{4}.

Set
\theta:=\{\alpha_{1}\}
, and note that

{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}=\{{\rm diag}(a,-a,0,0):a\in\Rb\}

and

\kappa_{\theta}((g,h))=(\log\sigma_{1}(g),\log\sigma_{2}(g),0,0)

where
\sigma_{1}(g)\geq\sigma_{2}(g)
are the singular values of some (any) lift of
g
to
\SL(2,\Rb)
. Also,

\Psf_{\theta}=\Psf_{1}\times\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)

where
\Psf_{1}\subset\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)
stabilizes the line
\Rb\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
. In particular,
\Psf_{\theta}
contains a simple factor of
\mathsf{G}
.

Let
S
be a thrice punctured sphere equipped with a complete hyperbolic structure such that one of its ends is a cusp while the other two ends are hyperbolic funnels. Then there is a discrete subgroup
\Gamma_{0}\subset\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)
such that
S=\Gamma_{0}\backslash\Hb^{2}
. Let
h
be a hyperbolic element in
\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)
whose conjugacy class
[h]
corresponds to an oriented geodesic going once around one of the hyperbolic funnels of
S
, and let
u
be a unipotent element in
\mathsf{PSL}(2,\Rb)
whose conjugacy class
[u]
corresponds to an oriented closed curve going once around the cusp of
S
. We may assume that
\Gamma_{0}=\left\langle u\right\rangle*\left\langle h\right\rangle
. Then
\Gamma_{0}
is
\Psf_{1}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}_{0}:=\{\left\langle u\right\rangle\}
. Thus, if we set
\Gamma:=\left\langle\gamma,\eta\right\rangle\subset\mathsf{G}
, where

\gamma:=(u,h)\in\mathsf{G}\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta:=(h,\id)\in\mathsf{G},

then
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}:=\{\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle\}
. Furthermore,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{1}(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{n}))=+\infty

for every sequence of distinct elements
\{g_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
, so (1) in Theorem
1.3
holds.

On the other hand, the Riemannian symmetric space
M
associated to
\mathsf{G}
is the product

M=\Hb^{2}_{\Rb}\times\Hb^{2}_{\Rb}

where
\Hb^{2}_{\Rb}
is the real hyperbolic 2-space. Then for any
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in M
we have

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma^{n}(p_{0}),p_{0})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\rm d}_{\Hb_{\Rb}^{2}}(h^{n}(y_{0}),y_{0})>0

since
h
is hyperbolic and
u
is unipotent. However,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma^{n}))=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\sigma_{1}(u^{n})}{\sigma_{2}(u^{n})}=0

since
u
is unipotent. So (3) in Theorem
1.3
does not hold.

2.
Semisimple Lie groups

In this expository section we introduce some of the notation used throughout the paper. All the notation introduced is the same as in
[
15
]
.

As in the introduction (and the rest of the paper)
, let
\mathsf{G}
be a connected semisimple Lie group without compact factors and with finite center. Fix a Cartan decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{k}}\oplus{\mathfrak{p}}

of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
of
G
, a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{p}
, and a Weyl chamber by
{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}\subset{\mathfrak{a}}

\Sigma^{+}
be the set of positive restricted roots associated to
\mathfrak{a}^{+}
, let
\Delta\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
be the system of simple restricted roots associated to
\mathfrak{a}^{+}
, and let

denote the maximal compact Lie subgroup whose Lie algebra is
{\mathfrak{k}}

2.1.
Cartan projection

The
Cartan projection
is the map
\kappa:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
with the defining property that
\kappa(g)\in{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
is the unique element such that

g=me^{\kappa(g)}\ell

for some
m,\ell\in\Ksf
(in general
m
and
\ell
are not uniquely determined by
g
). Such a decomposition
g=me^{\kappa(g)}\ell
is called a
\mathsf{KAK}
-decomposition
of
g
, see
[
22
, Chap. IX, Thm. 1.1]
.

There is a unique involutive linear map
\iota:{\mathfrak{a}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}
, called the
opposition involution
, such that
\iota(-{\mathfrak{a}}^{+})={\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
and

\iota(\kappa(g))=\kappa(g^{-1})

for all
g\in\mathsf{G}
. The dual of
\iota
satisfies

\iota^{*}(\Delta)=\Delta.

As in the introduction we let
M
denote the standard Riemannian symmetric space associated to
\mathsf{G}
, and let
{\rm d}_{M}
denote the distance function on
M
induced by the Riemannian metric. As a
\mathsf{G}
-space,
M=\mathsf{G}/\Ksf
, and
{\rm d}_{M}
satisfies

{\rm d}_{M}(g\Ksf,h\Ksf)=\left\|\kappa(g^{-1}h)\right\|\quad\text{for all}\quad g,h\in\mathsf{G},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is the norm on
\mathfrak{a}
induced by the restriction of the Killing from to
\mathfrak{a}
. The Cartan projection satisfies the following estimates.

Lemma 2.1

(see e.g.
[
29
, Lem. 2.3]
)
.

If
g,h\in\mathsf{G}
, then

\left\|\kappa(gh)-\kappa(h)\right\|\leq\left\|\kappa(g)\right\|\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\kappa(gh)-\kappa(g)\right\|\leq\left\|\kappa(h)\right\|.

2.2.
Parabolic subgroups and flag manifolds

Given a subset
\theta\subset\Delta
, the
parabolic subgroup associated to
\theta
, denoted by
\Psf_{\theta}\subset\mathsf{G}
, is the normalizer of

{\mathfrak{u}}_{\theta}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}_{\theta}}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}

where
\Sigma^{+}_{\theta}:=\Sigma^{+}-{\rm Span}(\Delta-\theta)
. Then the
flag manifold associated to
\theta
is

\Fc_{\theta}:=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{P}_{\theta}.

In this paper will only consider the case when
\theta\subset\Delta
is
symmetric
, that is
\iota^{*}(\theta)=\theta
. In this case, there is a unique open
\mathsf{G}
-orbit in

\Fc_{\theta}\times\Fc_{\theta}

and we say that two flags
F_{1},F_{2}\in\Fc_{\theta}
are
transverse
if
(F_{1},F_{2})
is in this orbit. For a flag
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
, we let

\mathcal{Z}_{F}\subset\Fc_{\theta}

denote the set of flags that are not transverse to
F
.

Following the notation in
[
21
]
, we define a map

U_{\theta}:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\Fc_{\theta}

by fixing a
\mathsf{KAK}
-decomposition
g=m_{g}e^{\kappa(g)}\ell_{g}
for each
g\in\mathsf{G}
and then letting
U_{\theta}(g):=m_{g}\Psf_{\theta}
. One can show that if
\alpha(\kappa(g))>0
for all
\alpha\in\theta
, then
U_{\theta}(g)
is independent of the choice of
\mathsf{KAK}
-decomposition, see
[
22
, Chap. IX, Thm. 1.1]
, and hence
U_{\theta}
is continuous on the set

\left\{g\in\mathsf{G}:\alpha(\kappa(g))>0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\theta\right\}.

The action of
\mathsf{G}
on
\Fc_{\theta}
has the following north-south like dynamics.

Proposition 2.2

(see e.g.
[
15
, Prop. 2.3]
)
.

Suppose
\theta\subset\Delta
is symmetric,
F^{\pm}\in\Fc_{\theta}
and
\{g_{n}\}
is a sequence in
\mathsf{G}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

U_{\theta}(g_{n})\rightarrow F^{+}
,
U_{\theta}(g_{n}^{-1})\rightarrow F^{-}
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha(\kappa(g_{n}))=\infty
for every
\alpha\in\theta
,

(2)

g_{n}(F)\to F^{+}
for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}-\mathcal{Z}_{F^{-}}
, and this convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
\Fc_{\theta}-\mathcal{Z}_{F^{-}}
.

(3)

g_{n}^{-1}(F)=F^{-}
for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}-\mathcal{Z}_{F^{+}}
, and this convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
\Fc_{\theta}-\mathcal{Z}_{F^{+}}
.

(4)

there are open sets
\mathcal{U}^{\pm}\subset\Fc_{\theta}
such that
g_{n}(F)\to F^{+}
for all
F\in\mathcal{U}^{+}
and
g_{n}^{-1}(F)\to F^{-}
for all
F\in\mathcal{U}^{-}
.

2.3.
Fundamental weights and partial Cartan projections

For any
\alpha\in\Delta
, let
\omega_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}
denote the associated fundamental weight.

Given a subset
\theta\subset\Delta
, the
partial Cartan subspace associated to
\theta
is

{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}:=\{H\in{\mathfrak{a}}:\alpha(H)=0\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Delta-\theta\}.

Then there is a unique projection

p_{\theta}:{\mathfrak{a}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}

such that
\omega_{\alpha}(X)=\omega_{\alpha}(p_{\theta}(X))
for all
\alpha\in\theta
and
X\in\mathfrak{a}
. The
partial Cartan projection associated to
\theta
is

\kappa_{\theta}:=p_{\theta}\circ\kappa:\mathsf{G}\to\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}.

One can show that
\{\omega_{\alpha}|_{{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}}:\alpha\in\theta\}
is a basis of
{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and hence we will identify

{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}={\rm Span}\{\omega_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\theta\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}.

Note that
\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa_{\theta}(g))=\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa(g))
for all
\alpha\in\theta
and
g\in\mathsf{G}
and so

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))=\phi(\kappa(g))

(2)

for all
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and
g\in\mathsf{G}
.

2.4.
The partial Iwasawa cocycle

Let
\Usf:=\exp({\mathfrak{u}}_{\Delta})
. The
Iwasawa decomposition
states that the map

(k,a,u)\in\Ksf\times\exp({\mathfrak{a}})\times\Usf\mapsto kau\in\mathsf{G}

is a diffeomorphism, see
[
32
, Chap. VI, Prop. 6.46]
. Using this, Quint
[
37
]
defined the
Iwasawa cocycle

B:\mathsf{G}\times\Fc_{\Delta}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}

with the defining property that
gk\in\Ksf\cdot\exp(B(g,F))\cdot\Usf
for all
(g,F)\in\mathsf{G}\times\Fc_{\Delta}
, where
k\in\Ksf
is an element such that
F=k\Psf_{\Delta}
.

For any
\theta\subset\Delta
, note that
\Psf_{\Delta}\subset\Psf_{\theta}
, so the identity map on
\mathsf{G}
induces a surjection
\Pi_{\theta}:\Fc_{\Delta}\to\Fc_{\theta}
.
The
partial Iwasawa cocycle
is the map

B_{\theta}:\mathsf{G}\times\Fc_{\theta}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}

defined by
B_{\theta}(g,F)=p_{\theta}(B(g,F^{\prime}))
for some (all)
F^{\prime}\in\Pi_{\theta}^{-1}(F)
.
By
[
37
, Lem. 6.1 and 6.2]
, this is a well-defined cocycle, that is

B_{\theta}(gh,F)=B_{\theta}(g,hF)+B_{\theta}(h,F)

for all
g,h\in\mathsf{G}
and
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
.

2.5.
The Linear Case

We now briefly describe the above notations when
\mathsf{G}=\mathsf{PSL}(d,\Rb)
. Let
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\Rb)=\mathfrak{k}+\mathfrak{p}
denote the standard Cartan decomposition of
\mathfrak{sl}(d,\Rb)
, that is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{k}=\{X\in\mathfrak{sl}(d,\Rb):{{}^{t}X}=-X\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{p}=\{X\in\mathfrak{sl}_{d}(\Rb):{{}^{t}X}=X\}.

Also let

\mathfrak{a}:=\{{\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathfrak{sl}(d,\Rb):\ a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}=0\}\subset\mathfrak{p}

denote the standard Cartan subspace with the standard positive Weyl chamber

\mathfrak{a}^{+}:=\{{\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathfrak{a}:\ a_{1}\geq\dots\geq a_{d}\}\subset\mathfrak{p}.

Then the opposite involution is given by

\iota({\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}))={\rm diag}(-a_{d},\ldots,-a_{1})

and the Cartan projection is given by

\kappa(g)=(\log\sigma_{1}(g),\cdots,\log\sigma_{d}(g))

where
\sigma_{1}(g)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(g)
are the singular values of any lift of
g
to
\mathsf{SL}(d,\Rb)
.

The standard system of simple restricted roots is
\Delta:=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d-1}\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
where

\alpha_{j}({\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}))=a_{j}-a_{j+1}

for all
{\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathfrak{a}
. Then the fundamental weights are given by

\omega_{\alpha_{j}}({\rm diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}))=a_{1}+\dots+a_{j}

and so
\iota^{*}(\omega_{\alpha_{j}})=\omega_{\alpha_{d-j}}
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
denote the standard basis of
\Rb^{d}
and let
F_{0}^{j}:=\left\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{j}\right\rangle
be the subspace spanned by
e_{1},\dots,e_{j}
. Then given
\theta=\{\alpha_{j_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{j_{n}}\}\subset\Delta
with
j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{n}
, the parabolic subgroup
\Psf_{\theta}\subset\mathsf{PSL}(d,\Rb)
is the stabilizer of the flag

(F_{0}^{j_{1}},F_{0}^{j_{2}},\cdots,F^{j_{n}}_{0})

and
\Fc_{\theta}
is the partial flag manifold

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\left\{(F^{j_{1}},\dots,F^{j_{n}}):\ \mathrm{dim}\left(F^{j_{i}}\right)=j_{i}\text{ and }F^{j_{1}}\subset F^{j_{2}}\subset\cdots\subset F^{j_{n}}\right\}.

In the case when
\theta
is symmetric, two flags
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
are transverse if and only if they are transverse in the usual sense:
F_{1}^{j}\oplus F_{2}^{d-j}=\Rb^{d}
for all
\alpha_{j}\in\theta
.

In the linear case we often replace subscripts of the form
\theta=\{\alpha_{j_{1}},\dots,\alpha_{j_{n}}\}
with
j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}
. So for instance

\Fc_{1,d-1}=\Fc_{\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{d-1}\}}

denotes the line/hyperplane partial flag manifold.

2.6.
Properties of unipotent subgroups

Recall that a
d\times d
real matrix
A
is
unipotent
if 1 is the only eigenvalue of
A
(over
\Cb
) and a subgroup
\mathsf{U}\subset\mathsf{GL}(d,\Rb)
is called
unipotent
if every element in
\mathsf{U}
is unipotent. When
\mathsf{G}
has trivial center, a subgroup
\mathsf{U}\subset\mathsf{G}
is
unipotent
if
{\rm Ad}(\mathsf{U})\subset\SL(\mathfrak{g})
is unipotent. Given a Lie subgroup
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{G}
, the
unipotent radical
of
\mathsf{H}
is the maximal connected normal unipotent subgroup of
\mathsf{H}
.

Unipotent subgroups have the following well-known properties.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
Z(\mathsf{G})
is trivial and
\mathfrak{u}
is the Lie algebra of a connected unipotent subgroup
\mathsf{U}\subset\mathsf{G}
. Then:

(1)

The exponential map induces a diffeomorphism
\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\mathsf{U}
.

(2)

Let
\lambda
be a measure on
\mathfrak{u}
which is obtained by pulling back the Lebesgue measure on
\Rb^{\dim\mathfrak{u}}
via a linear isomorphism (notice that any two measures obtained this way are scalar multiplies of each other). Then the push-forward
\mu:=\exp_{*}\lambda
is a Haar measure on
\mathsf{U}
.

(3)

If
\Phi:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\SL(d,\Rb)
is a representation, then
\Phi(\mathsf{U})\subset\SL(d,\Rb)
is unipotent.

(4)

There exists
C>0
such that

\left\|\kappa(\exp(Y))\right\|\leq C+C\log(1+\left\|Y\right\|)

for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
.

(5)

For any
\alpha\in\Delta
there exist
M_{\alpha}\in\Nb
,
C_{\alpha}>1
and a positive everywhere defined rational function
R_{\alpha}:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\Rb
where

\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}\leq e^{\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa(\exp(Y)))}\leq C_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}

for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
.

We provide a proof of Proposition
2.3
in Appendix
A
.

3.
Relatively hyperbolic groups

In this expository section we recall one of the many equivalent definitions of a relatively hyperbolic group, for more background and other definitions see
[
8
,
19
,
35
,
41
]
.

3.1.
Relatively hyperbolic groups

Recall that the action, by homeomorphisms, of a group
\Gamma
on a
compact metrizable space
M
is said to be a (discrete)
convergence group action
if whenever

\{\gamma_{n}\}
is a sequence of distinct elements in
\Gamma
, then there are points
x,y\in M
and a subsequence
\{\gamma_{n_{j}}\}
so that
\gamma_{n_{j}}(z)
converges to
x
for all
z\in M-\{y\}
and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
M-\{y\}
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
acts on
M
as a convergence group, then:

•

A point
x\in M
is a
conical limit point
if there exist
a,b\in M
distinct and a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
such that
\gamma_{n}(x)\to a
and
\gamma_{n}(y)\to b
for all
y\in M-\{x\}
.

•

An element
\gamma\in\Gamma
is
parabolic
if it has infinite order and fixes exactly one point in
M
.

•

A infinite order subgroup
H\subset\Gamma
is
parabolic
if it fixes some point of
M
and each infinite order element in
H
is parabolic. The fixed point of a parabolic subgroup is called a
parabolic point
.

•

A parabolic point
x\in M
is
bounded
if the quotient
{\rm Stab}_{\Gamma}(x)\backslash(M-\{x\})
is compact.

Finally,
\Gamma
is called a
geometrically finite convergence group
if every point in
M
is either a conical limit point or a bounded parabolic point.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a finitely generated group
\Gamma
and a collection
\mathcal{P}
of finitely generated infinite subgroups, we say that
\Gamma
is
hyperbolic relative to
\mathcal{P}
, or that
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
is
relatively hyperbolic
, if
\Gamma
acts on a compact perfect metrizable space
M
as a geometrically finite convergence group and the maximal parabolic subgroups are exactly the set

\{\gamma P\gamma^{-1}:P\in\mathcal{P},\gamma\in\Gamma\}.

To avoid confusion with parabolic subgroups in the Lie group sense, we also sometimes call a maximal parabolic subgroup of
\Gamma
a
peripheral subgroup
.

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that by definition we assume that a relatively hyperbolic group is non-elementary (i.e.
M
is perfect and hence is infinite) and finitely generated.

By a theorem of Bowditch
[
8
, Thm. 9.4]
, given a relatively hyperbolic group
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, any two compact perfect metrizable spaces satisfying Definition
3.1
are
\Gamma
-equivariantly homeomorphic.
This unique topological space is then denoted by
\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
and called the
Bowditch boundary of
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
.

3.2.
The Groves–Manning cusp space

Given a relatively hyperbolic group
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
,
Groves and Manning
[
20
]
constructed a Gromov hyperbolic space which
\Gamma
acts on proper discontinuously so that subgroups
in
\mathcal{P}
preserve “combinatorial horoballs.” These spaces, now called Groves–Manning cusp spaces, are central tools in the study of relatively hyperbolic groups.
We recall their construction here.

Definition 3.3
.

Suppose
Y
is a graph with the simplicial distance
{\rm d}_{Y}
. The
combinatorial horoball

\mathcal{H}(Y)
is the graph, also equipped with the simplicial distance, that has vertex set
Y^{(0)}\times\Nb
and two types of edges:

•

vertical edges
joining vertices
(v,n)
and
(v,n+1)
,

•

horizontal edges
joining vertices
(v,n)
and
(w,n)
when
{\rm d}_{Y}(v,w)\leq 2^{n-1}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Suppose that
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
is relatively hyperbolic. A finite symmetric generating set
S\subset\Gamma
is
adapted
if
S\cap P
is a generating set of
P
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Given such an
S
, we let
{\mathcal{C}}(\Gamma,S)
and
{\mathcal{C}}(P,S\cap P)
denote the associated Cayley graphs. Then the associated
Groves–Manning cusp space
, denoted
{\mathcal{C}}_{GM}(\Gamma,\mathcal{P},S)
, is obtained from the Cayley graph
{\mathcal{C}}(\Gamma,S)
by attaching, for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
and each coset
\gamma P\in\Gamma/P
, a copy of the combinatorial horoball
\mathcal{H}(\gamma{\mathcal{C}}(P,S\cap P))
by identifying
\gamma{\mathcal{C}}(P,S\cap P)\subset{\mathcal{C}}(\Gamma,S)
with the
n=1
level of
\mathcal{H}(\gamma{\mathcal{C}}(P,S\cap P))
.

Theorem 3.5

(Groves–Manning
[
20
, Thm. 3.25]
)
.

If
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
is relatively hyperbolic and
S
is an adapted finite generating set, then

{\mathcal{C}}_{GM}(\Gamma,\mathcal{P},S)
is a proper geodesic Gromov hyperbolic space such that

(1)

\Gamma
acts properly discontinuously on
{\mathcal{C}}_{GM}(\Gamma,\mathcal{P},S)
by isometries,

(2)

every point in
X
is within a uniformly bounded distance of a bi-infinite geodesic, and

(3)

there exists a
\Gamma
-equivariant homeomorphism between
\partial_{\infty}{\mathcal{C}}_{GM}(\Gamma,\mathcal{P},S)
, the Gromov boundary of
{\mathcal{C}}_{GM}(\Gamma,\mathcal{P},S)
,
and
\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
.

4.
Discrete subgroups of semisimple Lie groups

In this expository section we introduce three classes of discrete subgroups in
\mathsf{G}
and state some of their basic properties.
In the rest of the paper
, we assume that
\theta\subset\Delta
is symmetric.

4.1.
Divergent groups

A discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is called
\Psf_{\theta}
-divergent
if

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\min_{\alpha\in\theta}\alpha(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty

whenever
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is a sequence of distinct elements in
\Gamma
. The
limit set

\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
of such a subgroup is the set of accumulation points of
\{U_{\theta}(\gamma):\gamma\in\Gamma\}
. We note that in the literature, divergent groups are sometimes called regular groups (e.g.
[
27
]
).

The limit set of a divergent group can be used to compactify it.

Lemma 4.1

(see e.g.
[
15
, Prop. 2.3]
)
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-divergent, then the set
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
has a topology that makes it a compactification of
\Gamma
. More precisely:

(1)

\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is a compact metrizable space.

(2)

If
\Gamma
has the discrete topology, then
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is an embedding.

(3)

If
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
has the subspace topology from
\Fc_{\theta}
, then
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is an embedding.

(4)

A sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
converges to
F
in
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
if and only if

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\min_{\alpha\in\theta}\alpha(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})\rightarrow F.

(5)

The natural left action of
\Gamma
on
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is by homeomorphisms.

4.2.
Transverse groups

A
\Psf_{\theta}
-divergent subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse
if
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is a transverse subset of
\Fc_{\theta}
, i.e. distinct pairs of flags in
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
are transverse. We note that in the literature, transverse groups are sometimes called regular antipodal groups (e.g.
[
27
]
).

One crucial feature of
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse groups is that they act as a convergence group on their limit sets.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
28
, Prop. 5.38]

If
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse, then
\Gamma
acts on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
as a convergence group.
In particular, if
\Gamma
is non-elementary, then
\Gamma
acts on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
minimally, and
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is perfect.

When
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse, the set of conical limit points for the action of
\Gamma
on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is called the
\theta
-conical limit set
and is denoted
\Lambda^{\rm con}_{\theta}(\Gamma)
.

4.3.
Relatively Anosov subgroups

There are several equivalent definitions of relatively Anosov groups. The definition we use comes from
[
26
]
.

A
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
, a finite collection of subgroups of
\Gamma
, if
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
is relatively hyperbolic with Bowditch boundary
\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
and there is a continuous
\Gamma
-equivariant map

\xi\colon\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})\to\Fc_{\theta}

which is a homeomorphism onto
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. Observe that such a
\xi
is unique, so we refer to it as the
limit map
of
\Gamma
.

The next result shows that this limit maps plays nicely with the Gromov boundary of a Groves–Manning cusp space.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
, with limit map
\xi:\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})\rightarrow\Fc_{\theta}
. Let
X
be a Groves–Manning cusp space for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, and let
b_{0}\in X
. If
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is a sequence in
\Gamma
and
\gamma_{n}(b_{0})\rightarrow x\in\partial_{\infty}X=\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, then
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})\rightarrow\xi(x)
.

Proof.

Since
\Fc_{\theta}
is compact, it suffices to show that every convergent subsequence of
\{U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})\}
converges to
\xi(x)
. Suppose
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n_{j}})\rightarrow F^{+}
. Passing to a further subsequence we can suppose that
\gamma_{n_{j}}^{-1}(b_{0})\rightarrow y\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
and
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n_{j}}^{-1})\rightarrow F^{-}
. Then, by properties of Gromov hyperbolic spaces,
\gamma_{n_{j}}(z)\rightarrow x
for all
z\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})-\{y\}
. Also, by Proposition
2.2
,

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n_{j}}(F)=F^{+}

for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
transverse to
F^{-}
.

Notice that Proposition
2.2
implies that
F^{-}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
and so
F^{-}=\xi(y^{\prime})
for some
y^{\prime}\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. Fix
z\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})-\{y,y^{\prime}\}
. Then

\xi(x)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\xi(\gamma_{n_{j}}(z))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n_{j}}\xi(z)=F^{+}

since
\xi(z)
is transverse to
F^{-}=\xi(y^{\prime})
.
∎

The following theorem was established in
[
43
]
when
\mathsf{G}=\SL(d,\Rb)
. In Appendix
B
we will explain why it is also true in the following setting. In the following theorem and elsewhere in the paper, given a Lie group
\mathsf{H}
let
\mathsf{H}^{0}
denote the connected component of the identity in
\mathsf{H}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
Z(\mathsf{G})
is trivial and
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
. Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a non-elementary
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
.

(1)

If
X

(2)

Helpful reductions

We first explain why one can often reduce to the case where the center

Decompose the Lie algebra

Proposition 4.5

(

.

(2)

5.
Multiplicative estimates

It is a general principle from linear algebra that if
U_{\theta}(A^{-1})
is uniformly transverse to
U_{\theta}(B)
, then
\kappa_{\theta}(AB)
is coarsely equal to

\kappa_{\theta}(A)+\kappa_{\theta}(B)
,
see, for example,
[
6
, Lem. A.7]
. We make use of two manifestations of this principle, the first in the context of transverse groups and the second in the context of relatively Anosov
groups. Previous instances of this principle in our work include
[
14
, Lem. 6.2]
and
[
15
, Prop. 6.3]
.

In the results of this section,
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denotes the norm on
\mathfrak{a}
induced by the restriction of the Killing from to
\mathfrak{a}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse and
{\rm d}_{\Fc_{\theta}}
is a distance on
\Fc_{\theta}
which is induced by a Riemannian metric. For any
\epsilon>0
there exists
C=C(\epsilon)>0
such that: if
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
and
{\rm d}_{\Fc_{\theta}}(U_{\theta}(\gamma^{-1}),U_{\theta}(\eta))>\epsilon
, then

\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma\eta)-\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma)-\kappa_{\theta}(\eta)\right\|\leq C.

Proof.

We use the following special case of
[
6
, Lem. A.7]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
denote the standard basis of
\Rb^{d}
. Suppose
g_{1},g_{2}\in\SL(d,\Rb)
have singular value decomposition
g_{1}=m_{1}a_{1}\ell_{1}
and
g_{2}=m_{2}a_{2}\ell_{2}
. Then

\sigma_{1}(g_{1})\sigma_{1}(g_{2})\sin(\theta)\leq\sigma_{1}(g_{1}g_{2})\leq\sigma_{1}(g_{1})\sigma_{1}(g_{2})

where
\theta:=\angle\left(m_{2}\left\langle e_{1}\right\rangle,\ell_{1}^{-1}\left\langle e_{2},\dots,e_{d}\right\rangle\right)
is the Euclidean angle between the subspaces
m_{2}\left\langle e_{1}\right\rangle
and
\ell_{1}^{-1}\left\langle e_{2},\dots,e_{d}\right\rangle
.

If the proposition fails, then for every
n\geq 1
there exist
\gamma_{n},\eta_{n}\in\Gamma
where

{\rm d}_{\Fc_{\theta}}(U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}),U_{\theta}(\eta_{n}))>\epsilon

and

\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}\eta_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\right\|\geq n.

Lemma
2.1
implies that
\{\gamma_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}
are escaping sequences in
\Gamma
.
So by passing to a subsequence we can suppose that
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1})\rightarrow F_{1}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
and
U_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\rightarrow F_{2}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
.
Then
{\rm d}_{\Fc_{\theta}}(F_{1},F_{2})\geq\epsilon
and hence
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are transverse (since
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse).

Since
\{\omega_{\alpha}|_{\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}}:\alpha\in\theta\}
is a basis for
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
, after passing to a subsequence there exists some
\chi\in\sum_{\alpha\in\theta}\Nb\cdot\omega_{\alpha}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\chi\Big(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}\eta_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\Big)\right|=\infty.

Let
N\in\Nb
,
\Phi:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\mathsf{PSL}(d,\Rb)
and
\xi:\Fc_{\theta}\rightarrow\Fc_{1,d-1}(\Rb^{d})
satisfy Proposition
4.6
for
\chi
.
Then by Proposition
4.6
Claim (2),

\log\sigma_{1}(\Phi(g))=N\chi(\kappa(g))=N\chi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))

for all
g\in\mathsf{G}
.

Let
\hat{\gamma}_{n}:=\Phi(\gamma_{n})
and
\hat{\eta}_{n}:=\Phi(\eta_{n})
. Since
\Gamma
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse,
\alpha(\kappa(\gamma_{n}))\rightarrow+\infty
for all
\alpha\in\theta
. So by Proposition
4.6
Claim (4),

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}U_{1,d-1}(\hat{\gamma}_{n}^{-1})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi(U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}))=\xi(F_{1}).

Likewise,
U_{1,d-1}(\hat{\eta}_{n})\rightarrow\xi(F_{2})
. Since
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are transverse, Proposition
4.6
Claim (1) implies that
\xi(F_{1})
and
\xi(F_{2})
are transverse. So Lemma
5.2
implies that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left|\log\sigma_{1}(\hat{\gamma}_{n}\hat{\eta}_{n})-\log\sigma_{1}(\hat{\gamma}_{n})-\log\sigma_{1}(\hat{\eta}_{n})\right|\leq C

for all
n\geq 1
. So,

\left|\chi\Big(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}\eta_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\Big)\right|\leq\frac{C}{N}

and we have a contradiction.
∎

Our result for relatively Anosov groups involves a choice of Groves–Manning cusp space.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov relative to
\mathcal{P}
and
X
is a Groves–Manning cusp space for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. There exists
C>0
such that: if
f:[0,T]\rightarrow X
is a geodesic with
f(0)=\id
and
f(T)\in\Gamma
, then

\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(f(T))-\kappa_{\theta}(f(t))-\kappa_{\theta}(f(t)^{-1}f(T))\right\|\leq C

whenever
t\in[0,T]
and
f(t)\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose not. Then for every
n\geq 1
there is a geodesic
f_{n}:[0,T_{n}]\rightarrow X
and some
t_{n}\in[0,T_{n}]
such that
f_{n}(0)=\id
,
f_{n}(t_{n})\in\Gamma
,
f_{n}(T_{n})\in\Gamma
, and

\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(T_{n}))-\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(t_{n}))-\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(t_{n})^{-1}f_{n}(T_{n}))\right\|\geq n.

Let
\gamma_{n}:=f(t_{n})
and
\eta_{n}:=f_{n}(t_{n})^{-1}f_{n}(T_{n})
. By Lemma
2.1
, both
\{\gamma_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}
are escaping sequences in
\Gamma
. So by passing to a subsequence we can suppose that
\gamma_{n}^{-1}\rightarrow x\in\partial_{\infty}X
and
\eta_{n}\rightarrow y\in\partial_{\infty}X
. Since
t\mapsto f_{n}(t_{n})^{-1}f_{n}(t)
is a geodesic in
X
passing through
\id
and joining
\gamma_{n}^{-1}
to
\eta_{n}
, we must have
x\neq y
.

Let
\xi:\partial_{\infty}X\rightarrow\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
be the limit map. Proposition
4.3
implies that
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1})\rightarrow\xi(x)
and
U_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\rightarrow\xi(y)
. Since
\xi(x)\neq\xi(y)
, Proposition
5.1
implies that there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(T_{n}))-\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(t_{n}))-\kappa_{\theta}(f_{n}(t_{n})^{-1}f_{n}(T_{n}))\right\|=\left\|\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}\eta_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})-\kappa_{\theta}(\eta_{n})\right\|\leq C

for all
n\geq 1
. So we have a contradiction.
∎

6.
Resolution of singularities

In this section we study the asymptotic behavior of proper positive functions which are products of powers of rational functions. This is the key technical step needed to prove that the Poincaré series of a peripheral subgroup of a relatively Anosov subgroup diverges at its critical exponent, see Theorem
7.1
.

Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
\Rb^{d}
, and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
the standard Euclidean norm on
\Rb^{d}
. We say that
R
is a rational function on
\Rb^{d}
if
R=\frac{f}{g}
where
f,g:\Rb^{d}\rightarrow\Rb
are polynomials and
R
has domain
\{g\neq 0\}
.

The main result of this section is the following theorem. Its proof is motivated by arguments of Benoist–Oh
[
3
, Prop. 7.2]
, which implies the special case when
m=1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose
R_{1},\dots,R_{m}
are rational functions on
\Rb^{d}
which are positive and everywhere defined, and let

R:=R_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots R_{m}^{\ell_{m}}

where
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\in\Rb
. If
R
is a proper function, then:

(1)

There exists
\delta=\delta(R)>0
such that

\int_{\Rb^{d}}R^{-s}d\lambda

converges when
s\in(\delta,+\infty)
and diverges when
s\in[0,\delta]
.

(2)

There exist
c=c(R)>0
and
\epsilon=\epsilon(R)>0
such that

R(x)\geq c(1+\left\|x\right\|)^{\epsilon}

for all
x\in\Rb^{d}
.

To prove Theorem
6.1
, we compactify
\Rb^{d}
by identifying it with the affine subspace

\Ab_{1}:=\{[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{d}]:x_{0}\neq 0\}\subset\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})

via the coordinate chart
\psi_{1}:\Ab_{1}\to\Rb^{d}
given by

\psi_{1}:[1:x_{1}:\cdots:x_{d}]\mapsto\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right).

It now suffices to prove the following lemma, which is the analog of Theorem
6.1
on “neighborhoods of infinity.”

Lemma 6.2
.

For each
p\in\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})-\Ab_{1}
, there exist
\delta_{p},c_{p},\epsilon_{p}>0
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{p}\subset\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})
of
p
such that:

(1)

The integral

\int_{\psi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{p}\cap\Ab_{1})}R^{-s}d\lambda

converges when
s\in(\delta_{p},+\infty)
and diverges when
s\in[0,\delta_{p}]
.

(2)

R(x)\geq c_{p}(1+\left\|x\right\|)^{\epsilon_{p}}
for all
x\in\psi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{p}\cap\Ab_{1})\subset\Rb^{d}
.

Assuming Lemma
6.2
, we prove Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Since
\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})-\Ab_{1}
is compact, there exist finitely many points
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
in
\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})-\Ab_{1}
such that

\Pb(\Rb^{d+1})-\Ab_{1}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}}.

Proof of (1). Let
\delta:=\max\{\delta_{p_{j}}:1\leq j\leq n\}
. Since
R
is positive,

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\int_{\psi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}}\cap\Ab_{1})}R^{-s}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Rb^{d}}R^{-s}d\lambda\leq\int_{\psi_{1}(\Ab_{1}-\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}})}R^{-s}d\lambda+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\psi_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}}\cap\Ab_{1})}R^{-s}d\lambda.

Since
\Ab_{1}-\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}}
is compact, the integral
\int_{\psi_{1}(\Ab_{1}-\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}})}R^{-s}d\lambda
is finite, so Claim (1) of Lemma
6.2
implies that (1) holds.

Proof of (2). Let
\epsilon:=\min\{\epsilon_{p_{j}}:1\leq j\leq n\}
. Since
R
is positive and continuous on the compact set
K:=\psi_{1}(\Ab_{1}-\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{p_{j}})\subset\Rb^{d}
, there exists
c_{0}>0
such that

R(x)\geq c_{0}(1+\left\|x\right\|)^{\epsilon}

for all
x\in K
. Thus, if we set
c:=\min\left(\{c_{0}\}\cup\{c_{p_{j}}:1\leq j\leq n\}\right)
, then Claim (2) of Lemma
6.2
implies that

R(x)\geq c(1+\left\|x\right\|)^{\epsilon}

for all
x\in\Rb^{d}
.
∎

To prove Lemma
6.2
, first note that we can assume that
p=[0:1:0:\dots:0]
by changing coordinates. Then
p
lies in the affine subspace

\Ab_{2}:=\left\{[x_{0}:\cdots:x_{d}]:x_{1}\neq 0\right\}\subset\Pb(\Rb^{d+1}).

Let
\psi_{2}:\Ab_{2}\to\Rb^{d}
be the coordinate chart given by

\psi_{2}:[y_{1}:1:y_{2}:\dots:y_{d}]\mapsto(y_{1},\dots,y_{d}).

Observe that
\psi_{2}(p)=0
and

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(\Ab_{2}-\Ab_{1})=\mathcal{Z}:=\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in\Rb^{d}:y_{1}=0\right\}.

(3)

Thus, the restriction

\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}|_{\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}}:\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}\to\Rb^{d}

is a well-defined embedding.

Since
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}|_{\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}}
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})=\left(\frac{1}{y_{1}},\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}},\dots,\frac{y_{d}}{y_{1}}\right),

(4)

for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

T_{j}:=R_{j}\circ\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}:\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}\to\Rb,

is a rational function that is well-defined and positive on
\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}
, and the assumption that
R
is proper implies that
T:=T_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\dots T_{m}^{\ell_{m}}
satisfies
\lim_{y\to z}T(y)=\infty
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Also, (
4
) implies that the Jacobian
D(\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})
of
\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}
satisfies

\left|\det D(\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})\right|=\frac{1}{\left|y_{1}\right|^{d+1}},

and

\left\|\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\right\|=\sqrt{\frac{1+y_{2}^{2}+\dots+y_{d}^{2}}{y_{1}^{2}}}.

Thus, to prove Lemma
6.2
, it now suffices to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose that
T_{1},\dots,T_{m}
are rational functions on
\Rb^{d}
which are positive and defined on
\Rb^{d}-\mathcal{Z}
, and let

T:=T_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\dots T_{m}^{\ell_{m}}

for some
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}\in\Rb
. If
\lim_{y\to z}T(y)=\infty
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, then there exist
\delta,c,\epsilon>0
and an open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\subset\Rb^{d}
of
0
such that:

(1)

The integral

\int_{\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{Z}}\frac{T^{-s}}{\left|y_{1}\right|^{d+1}}{\rm d}\lambda

converges when
s\in(\delta,+\infty)
and diverges when
s\in[0,\delta]
.

(2)

For all
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{Z}
, we have

T(y)\geq c\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{1+y_{2}^{2}+\dots+y_{d}^{2}}{y_{1}^{2}}}\right)^{\epsilon}.

For the remainder of this section, we will focus on the proof of Lemma
6.3
. An important tool used in the proof is the following version of Hironaka’s theorem
[
23
]
on the resolution of singularities (as stated in
[
1
, pg. 147]
).

Theorem 6.4

(Resolution theorem)
.

Let
F
be a real analytic function defined in a neighborhood of
0\in\Rb^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{Z}
be the set of zeroes of
F
. If
F
is not identically zero, then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
0
in
\Rb^{d}
, a real analytic manifold
M
and a proper real analytic map
\Phi:M\rightarrow U
such that

(1)

\Phi
restricts to a real-analytic diffeomorphism

M-\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{Z})\rightarrow U-\mathcal{Z}.

(2)

For every
q\in M
there exists an open neighborhood
V_{q}\subset M
of
q
and real analytic local coordinates
z_{q,1},\dots,z_{q,d}
on
V_{q}
centered at
q
where

F\circ\Phi(z_{q,1},\dots,z_{q,d})=z_{q,1}^{k_{q,1}}\cdots z_{q,d}^{k_{q,d}}\cdot\hat{F}_{q}(z_{q,1},\dots,z_{q,d})

for some
k_{q,1},\dots,k_{q,d}\in\Zb_{\geq 0}
and some nowhere-vanishing real analytic function
\hat{F}_{q}:V_{q}\to\Rb
.

The main idea of the proof of Claim (1) of Lemma
6.3
is to try to factor the integrand
\frac{T^{-s}}{\left|y_{1}\right|^{d+1}}
into the product of a bounded, nowhere vanishing function on
\mathcal{O}
, and power functions on
\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{Z}
that are “responsible" for how
\frac{T^{-s}}{\left|y_{1}\right|^{d+1}}
goes to infinity or zero near
\mathcal{Z}
. Doing so allows us to compare the required integral with the integral of a product of power functions, whose convergence or lack thereof is well-understood. Unfortunately, such a global factorization of
\frac{T^{-s}}{\left|y_{1}\right|^{d+1}}
on
\mathcal{O}
is not possible in general. However, Theorem
6.4
ensures that we do have such a factorization locally, but at the cost of pre-composing the integrand with a given real analytic function
\Phi
. We will show that the complications introduced to this strategy by using
\Phi
are surmountable, and that it can indeed be used to prove Lemma
6.3
.

We first write, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the rational function
T_{j}
as

T_{j}=\frac{f_{j}}{g_{j}}

where
f_{j},g_{j}:\Rb^{d}\to\Rb
are polynomials whose zeroes lie in the set
\mathcal{Z}
defined by (
3
). Then

\displaystyle F:=f_{1}\cdots f_{m}\cdot g_{1}\cdots g_{m}\cdot y_{1}:\Rb^{d}\to\Rb,

(5)

is a polynomial whose set of zeroes is
\mathcal{Z}
. Applying Theorem
6.4
to this
F
, we get an open neighborhood
U
of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a real analytic manifold
M
,
a proper real analytic map
\Phi:M\to U

\Psf^{\prime}_{\theta^{\prime}}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}:=\{p(P):P\in\mathcal{P}\}
. Also let
\phi^{\prime}\in({\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\prime})^{*}
be the unique functional where
\phi^{\prime}\circ{\rm d}p=\phi
. Then
Q_{p(P)}^{\phi^{\prime}}=Q_{P}^{\phi}
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. So by replacing
\mathsf{G}
with
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
, we may assume that
\mathsf{G}
has trivial center, and that
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
.

Fix
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Then by Theorem
4.4
there exists a closed subgroup
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{G}
with finitely many components such that:

(1)

P
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
.

(2)

\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{L}\ltimes\mathsf{U}
where
\mathsf{L}
is compact and
\mathsf{U}
is the unipotent radical of
\mathsf{H}
.

(3)

\mathsf{H}^{0}=\mathsf{L}^{0}\times\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{L}^{0}
is Abelian.

Let
\mathfrak{u}
denote the Lie algebra of
\mathsf{U}
.

Since
\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa_{\theta}(g))=\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa(g))
for all
\alpha\in\theta
and
g\in\mathsf{G}
, by Proposition
2.3
, for any
\alpha\in\theta

there exist
M_{\alpha}\in\Nb
,
C_{\alpha}>1
and a positive everywhere defined rational function
R_{\alpha}:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\Rb
where

\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}\leq e^{\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y)))}\leq C_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}

for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
. Write
\phi=\sum_{\alpha\in\theta}c_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
. Then define
R:=\prod_{\alpha\in\theta}R_{\alpha}^{\left|c_{\alpha}\right|/M_{\alpha}}
and
C_{\phi}:=\prod_{\alpha\in\theta}C_{\alpha}^{\left|c_{\alpha}\right|}
. Note that

C_{\phi}^{-s}R^{-s}(Y)\leq e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y)))}\leq C_{\phi}^{s}R^{-s}(Y)

for all
s\in\Rb
.

Lemma 7.3
.

R:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\Rb
is proper.

Proof.

Suppose that
\{Y_{n}\}
is an escaping sequence in
\mathfrak{u}
. Since
\exp:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\mathsf{U}
is a diffeomorphism,
\{\exp(Y_{n})\}
is an escaping sequence in
\mathsf{U}
(see Proposition
2.3
). Since
P
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
, there exists an escaping sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
P
such that
\{\gamma_{n}^{-1}\exp(Y_{n})\}
is relatively compact in
\mathsf{H}
. So there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

\left\|\kappa\left(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\exp(Y_{n})\right)\right\|\leq C_{0}

for all
n\geq 1
. Then by Lemma
2.1

R(Y_{n})\geq\frac{1}{C_{\phi}}e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y_{n})))}\geq\frac{1}{C_{\phi}}e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))}e^{-\left\|\phi\right\|C_{0}},

where
\left\|\phi\right\|
is the operator norm of the linear map
\phi:\mathfrak{a}\to\Rb
. Since
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
and
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is an escaping sequence, we must have
\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))\rightarrow+\infty
. Hence
R(Y_{n})\rightarrow+\infty
. So
R
is proper.
∎

Fix a measure
\lambda
on
\mathfrak{u}
which is obtained by pulling back the Lebesgue measure on
\Rb^{\dim\mathfrak{u}}
via some linear isomorphism
(notice that any two measures obtained this way are scalar multiplies of each other).
Then the push-forward
\mu:=\exp_{*}\lambda
is a Haar measure on
\mathsf{U}
, see Proposition
2.3
. By Theorem
6.1
there exists
\delta>0
such that

Q_{R}(s):=\int_{\mathfrak{u}}R^{-s}d\lambda

converges when
s\in(\delta,+\infty)
and diverges when
s\in[0,\delta]
. Hence to complete the proof of Theorem
7.1
it suffices to show the following.

Lemma 7.4
.

There exists a continuous function
A:\Rb_{\geq 0}\to\Rb_{>0}
such that

\frac{1}{A(s)}Q_{P}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{R}(s)\leq A(s)Q_{P}^{\phi}(s)

for all
s\geq 0
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma via a series of estimates. First let

P_{0}:=\mathsf{H}^{0}\cap P=\left(\mathsf{L}^{0}\times\mathsf{U}\right)\cap P.

Since
\mathsf{H}
has finitely many connected components,
P_{0}
has finite index in
P
. Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in P
such that
P/P_{0}=\{\gamma_{1}P_{0},\dots,\gamma_{n}P_{0}\}
, and let

D:=\left\|\phi\right\|\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\left\|\kappa(\gamma_{i})\right\|,

where
\left\|\phi\right\|
is the operator norm of the linear map
\phi:\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}\to\Rb
. Then by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-Ds}}{n}Q_{P}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{P_{0}}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{P}^{\phi}(s)

(12)

for all
s\geq 0
.

Next, let
\pi:\mathsf{L}^{0}\times\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathsf{U}
denote the projection and let
P_{1}:=\pi(P_{0})
. Since
P_{0}
is discrete and
\mathsf{L}^{0}
is compact, the kernel of
\pi|_{P_{0}}
is finite and
P_{1}
is discrete. Then by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-Cs}}{B}Q_{P_{0}}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{P_{1}}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{P_{0}}^{\phi}(s)

(13)

for all
s\geq 0
, where
B:=\#\ker(\pi|_{P_{0}})
and

C:=\left\|\phi\right\|\max_{g\in\mathsf{L}^{0}}\left\|\kappa(g)\right\|.

By (
12
) and (
13
), it now suffices to show that there exists a continuous function
A:\Rb_{\geq 0}\to\Rb_{>0}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A(s)}Q_{P_{1}}^{\phi}(s)\leq Q_{R}(s)\leq A(s)Q_{P_{1}}^{\phi}(s)

(14)

for all
s\geq 0
.

Since
P
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
, we see that
P_{1}
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{U}
. Fix compact neighborhoods
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\mathsf{U}
of the identity such that the left
P_{1}
-translates of
K_{1}
are pairwise disjoint and the left
P_{1}
-translates of
K_{2}
cover
\mathsf{U}
. Since
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
have non-empty interior,
both
\mu(K_{1})
and
\mu(K_{2})
are positive. For both
i=1,2
, define

E_{i}:=\left\|\phi\right\|\max_{h\in K_{i}}\left\|\kappa(h)\right\|.

Then by Lemma
2.1
,

e^{-sE_{i}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}\leq e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\leq e^{sE_{i}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}

for all
\gamma\in P_{1}
,
g\in\gamma K_{i}
and
s\geq 0
. So

\frac{e^{-sE_{2}}}{\mu(K_{2})}\int_{\gamma K_{2}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}d\mu(g)\leq e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\leq\frac{e^{sE_{1}}}{\mu(K_{1})}\int_{\gamma K_{1}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}d\mu(g)

for all
\gamma\in P_{1}
and
s>0
. Then

\displaystyle Q_{R}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{u}}R^{-s}(Y)d\lambda(Y)\leq C_{\phi}^{s}\int_{\mathfrak{u}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y)))}d\lambda(Y)=C_{\phi}^{s}\int_{\mathsf{U}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}d\mu(g)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\phi}^{s}\sum_{\gamma\in P_{1}}\int_{\gamma K_{2}}e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))}d\mu(g)\leq C_{\phi}^{s}\mu(K_{2})e^{sE_{2}}Q_{P_{1}}^{\phi}(s).

Likewise,

\displaystyle Q_{R}(s)

\displaystyle\geq C_{\phi}^{-s}\mu(K_{1})e^{-sE_{1}}Q_{P_{1}}^{\phi}(s),

so (
14
) holds.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

Notice that the proof implies that
\delta^{\phi}(P)
depends only on
\phi
and
\mathsf{U}
. One may concisely describe
\mathsf{U}
as the unipotent radical of the Zariski
closure of
P
.

8.
Divergence of the Poincaré series at its critical exponent

In this section we use an argument of Dal’bo–Otal–Peigné
[
17
]
and Corollary
7.2
to prove that the Poincaré series diverges at its critical exponent.
A similar argument was used by Blayac–Zhu
[
5
]
in their study of relatively Anosov subgroups of the projective general linear group which preserve a properly convex domain and Patterson–Sullivan measures defined using the Busemann functions associated to the Hilbert distance on the properly convex domain.

Theorem 8.1

(Theorem
1.1
)
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
, then
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}
diverges at its critical exponent.

The key result needed to prove Theorem
8.1
is the following lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

If
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
, then
there exists a
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure
\mu
for
\Gamma
of dimension
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
such that
\mu
has no atoms.

Assuming Lemma
8.2
, we prove Theorem
8.1
.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
assuming Lemma
8.2
.

By Lemma
8.2
there exists an atom-less
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure
\mu
of dimension
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
. Suppose for a contradiction that
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma))<+\infty
. Then Theorem
1.4
implies that
\mu(\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma))=0
. Since
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)-\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma)
is countable and
\mu
has no atoms,
\mu(\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma))=0
. Since
\mu
is a probability measure on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
this is impossible.
∎

We now give the proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Let
\delta:=\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)
. Endow
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
with the topology from Lemma
4.1
and for
x\in\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
denote the Dirac measure supported on
x
. By
[
36
, Lem. 3.1]
there exists a continuous non-decreasing function
h:\Rb^{+}\to\Rb^{+}
such that:

(1)

The series

\hat{Q}(s):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right)e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}

converges for
s>\delta
and diverges for
s\leq\delta
.

(2)

For any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\nu_{0}>0
such that: if
s>1
and
\nu>\nu_{0}
, then
h(\nu s)\leq s^{\epsilon}h(\nu)
.

(In the case when
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}
diverges at its critical exponent, we can choose
h\equiv 1
.)

For
s>\delta
consider the probability measure

\mu_{s}:=\frac{1}{\hat{Q}(s)}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right)e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}

on
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. By compactness, there exists
\{s_{n}\}\subset(\delta,\infty)
so that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}s_{n}=\delta
and

\mu:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{s_{n}}

exists in the weak-
*
. topology. In the proof of
[
15
, Prop. 3.1]
, we showed that
\mu
is a
\phi
-Patterson–Sullivan measure for
\Gamma
of dimension
\delta
.

We will now prove that
\mu
has no atoms. By
[
15
, Prop. 8.1]
, if
F\in\Lambda_{\theta}^{\rm con}(\Gamma)
, then
\mu(\{F\})=0
. So it suffices to fix a parabolic point
F_{0}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
and show that
\mu(\{F_{0}\})=0
. By the definition of the weak-
*
topology, it suffices to find a sequence of open sets
\{V_{m}\}
in
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
each of which contains
F_{0}
, and where

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\,\limsup_{s\searrow\delta}\,\mu_{s}(V_{m})=0.

(15)

Let
\xi:\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})\to\Fc_{\theta}
be the limit map of
\Gamma
. Let
P
be the stabilizer in
\Gamma
of
F_{0}
(it is a conjugate of a subgroup in
\mathcal{P}
), fix an enumeration
P=\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots\}
, and let
Q_{m}:=\{g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\}\subset P
for each positive integer
m
.

Fix a Groves–Manning cusp space
X
for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, and let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the set of elements
\gamma\in\Gamma
for which there is a geodesic ray
\sigma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow X
such that
\sigma(0)=\gamma
,
\sigma(T)=\id
for some
T\geq 0
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma(t)=\xi^{-1}(F_{0})\in\partial_{\infty}X=\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. The next lemma implies that for all positive integers
m
,

V_{m}:=(\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma))-Q_{m}\cdot\overline{\Gamma^{\prime}}

is an open neighborhood of
F_{0}
, where
\overline{\Gamma^{\prime}}
denotes the closure of
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 8.3
.

The closed subset
\overline{\Gamma^{\prime}}\subset\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
does not contain
F_{0}
. In particular, for all positive integers
m
,
V_{m}\subset\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
is an open neighborhood of
F_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that there exists a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma^{\prime}
converging to
F_{0}
. Then Lemma
4.1
implies that
U_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})\rightarrow F_{0}
. Hence, if we view
\{\gamma_{n}\}
as a sequence in
X
, then by Proposition
4.3
and taking a subsequence if necessary, we must have
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n}(\id)\rightarrow\xi^{-1}(F_{0})
.

For each
n
fix a geodesic ray
\sigma_{n}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow X
such that
\sigma_{n}(0)=\gamma_{n}
,
\sigma_{n}(T_{n})=\id
for some
T_{n}\geq 0
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}(t)=\xi^{-1}(F_{0})
. Passing to a subsequence we can suppose that the geodesics
\{\sigma_{n}(\cdot+T_{n})\}
converges locally uniformly to a geodesic
\sigma:\Rb\rightarrow X
. Then, since
\gamma_{n}\rightarrow\xi^{-1}(F_{0})
, we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\sigma(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}(0)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n}=\xi^{-1}(F_{0})=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\sigma(t),

which is impossible.

Since
Q_{m}
is finite for all
m
, the second claim follows immediately from the first.
∎

Since
P
is conjugate to a subgroup in
\mathcal{P}
, by Corollary
7.2
,

\epsilon:=\frac{\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)-\delta^{\phi}(P)}{2}>0.

To prove that Equation (
15
) holds, we prove the following lemma. This will be used to give an upper bound on
\mu_{s}(V_{m})
involving the tail of the Poincaré series
Q_{P}^{\phi}(\delta^{\phi}(P)+\epsilon)
.

Lemma 8.4
.

(1)

P\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma
.

(2)

There exists
C_{1}>0
such that

\left|\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g\gamma))-\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g))-\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\right|\leq C_{1}

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
g\in P
.

(3)

There exists
C_{2}>0
such that

h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}}\right)\leq C_{2}e^{\epsilon\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right)

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

Proof of (1). Fix
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Then fix a geodesic ray
\sigma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow X
with
\sigma(0)=\gamma
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma(t)=\xi^{-1}(F_{0})
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{P}\subset X
denote the combinatorial horoball associated to
P
. Notice that the geodesic ray
\sigma_{0}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow X
which satisfies
\sigma_{0}(n)=(\id,n)\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
for all
n\in\Nb
also limits to
\xi^{-1}(F_{0})
. Hence

\sup_{t\geq 0}\,{\rm d}_{X}(\sigma(t),\sigma_{0}(t))<+\infty.

Since

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rm d}_{X}(\sigma_{0}(t),X-\mathcal{H}_{P})=+\infty,

there exists
T\geq 0
such that
\sigma(T)\in P
and
\sigma(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{P}
for all
t\geq T
. Then
\sigma(T)^{-1}\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
. Since
\gamma\in\Gamma
was arbitrary, (1) holds.

Proof of (2). Suppose not. Then for each
n\geq 1
there exist
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
g_{n}\in P
such that

\left|\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{n}\gamma_{n}))-\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{n}))-\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))\right|\geq n.

Lemma
2.1
implies that
\{\gamma_{n}\}
and
\{g_{n}\}
are both escaping sequences in
\Gamma
. Since
\{g_{n}\}\subset P
, in the topology on
\Gamma\cup\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
we have
g_{n}^{-1}\rightarrow F_{0}
. Passing to a subsequence we can also assume that

F:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)

exists. By Lemma
8.3
,
F_{0}\neq F
. So by Lemma
4.1
and Proposition
5.1
, we have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{n}\gamma_{n}))-\Big(\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{n}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))\Big)\right|<+\infty

and hence a contradiction.

Proof of (3). Let
\nu_{0}>0
be a constant such that if
s>1
and
\nu>\nu_{0}
, then
h(\nu s)\leq s^{\epsilon}h(\nu)
. Let
C_{1}>0
be the constant from (2), and fix
C_{1}^{\prime}\geq C_{1}
such that

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+C_{1}^{\prime}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}^{\prime}>\log\nu_{0}

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}
and
k\geq 1
. Also let

\Gamma^{\prime\prime}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}:\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\leq\log\nu_{0}\}.

If
\gamma\notin\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, then

h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}}\right)\leq h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)\leq e^{\epsilon C_{1}^{\prime}}e^{\epsilon\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right).

On the other hand, if
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, then

h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}}\right)\leq h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+2C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)\leq e^{\epsilon C_{1}^{\prime}}e^{\epsilon\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}^{\prime}}\right).

So (3) holds with

C_{2}:=e^{\epsilon C_{1}^{\prime}}\max\left\{\frac{h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}{h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right)}:\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\right\},

which is finite since
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
is finite.
∎

If
s>\delta
, then by Lemma
8.4
,

\displaystyle\mu_{s}(V_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\hat{Q}(s)}\sum_{k>m}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}\gamma))}\right)e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}\gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{sC_{1}}}{\hat{Q}(s)}\sum_{k>m}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))+C_{1}}\right)e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}e^{sC_{1}}}{\hat{Q}(s)}\sum_{k>m}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}}e^{-(s-\epsilon)\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}h\left(e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}\right)e^{-s\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}e^{sC_{1}}\sum_{k>m}e^{-(\delta^{\phi}(P)+\epsilon)\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}.

Since
Q_{P}^{\phi}(\delta^{\phi}(P)+\epsilon)<+\infty
, it follows that

\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\,\limsup_{s\searrow\delta}\,\mu_{s}(V_{m})\leq C_{2}e^{\delta C_{1}}\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k>m}e^{-(\delta^{\phi}(P)+\epsilon)\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(g_{k}))}=0,

so Equation (
15
) holds.
∎

Remark 8.5

(The elementary case)
.

In this remark we sketch why the Poincaré series of an infinite elementary transverse groups diverges at its critical exponent.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is an infinite
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse subgroup,
\#\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)\leq 2
,
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
.

Case 1:
Suppose
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)=\{F^{+},F^{-}\}
. Then one can show that there is an infinite order element
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\langle\gamma\rangle
has finite index in
\Gamma
. Moreover, we can assume that
\gamma^{n}(F)\rightarrow F^{+}
for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
transverse to
F^{-}
. Then arguing as in Case 2 of the proof of Corollary
9.1
below, one can show that
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)=0
. Hence, since
\Gamma
is infinite, we have

Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}(0)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}1=+\infty.

Case 2:
Suppose
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)=\{F^{+}\}
. Using Proposition
4.5
we may assume that
\mathsf{G}
has trivial center, and that
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
. Then one can adapt the proof of Theorem
4.4
part (2) to show that there exists a closed subgroup
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{G}
with finitely many components such that:

(1)

\Gamma
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
.

(2)

\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{L}\ltimes\mathsf{U}
where
\mathsf{L}
is compact and
\mathsf{U}
is the unipotent radical of
\mathsf{H}
.

(3)

\mathsf{H}^{0}=\mathsf{L}^{0}\times\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{L}^{0}
is Abelian.

Finally, one can use the proof of Theorem
7.1
to show that
Q_{\Gamma}^{\phi}
diverges at its critical exponent.

9.
Relatively quasiconvex subgroups

Suppose that
\Gamma
is hyperbolic relative to
\mathcal{P}
. Given a subgroup
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
, the
limit set of
\Gamma_{0}
, denoted
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
,
is the set of all points
x\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
where there is a sequence
\{h_{n}\}
in
\Gamma_{0}
and
y\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})

such that
h_{n}(z)\rightarrow x
for all
z\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})-\{y\}
. Notice that the action of
\Gamma_{0}
on
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
is a convergence group action. Then
\Gamma_{0}
is
relatively quasiconvex
if
\Gamma_{0}
acts geometrically finitely on
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
, see
[
24
, Defn. 6.2]
. In this section we prove that infinite index, relatively quasiconvex subgroups of a relatively Anosov group
have strictly lower critical exponent.

Corollary 9.1

(Corollary
1.7
)
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
,
\phi\in{\mathfrak{a}}_{\theta}^{*}
and
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
.
If
\Gamma_{0}
is infinite index relatively quasiconvex subgroup of
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, then

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)>\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{0}).

Remark 9.2
.

Since we will be using results from Hruska
[
24
]
extensively in this section, we remark that the definition of relatively hyperbolic group used in
[
24
, Defn. 3.1]
is strictly weaker than our Definition
3.1
. To obtain our Definition
3.1
from Hruska’s definition, one needs to further require that
\Gamma

be non-elementary and finitely generated, and that every
P\in\mathcal{P}
is finitely generated. By
[
24
, Cor. 9.2]
, these additional conditions follow if
\Gamma
is non-elementary and every
P\in\mathcal{P}
is
slender
, i.e. any subgroup of
P
is finitely generated.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the two results needed to deduce Corollary
9.1
from Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
are stated as Lemma
9.3
and Proposition
9.4
below. They both follow from results of Hruska
[
24
]
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is hyperbolic relative to
\mathcal{P}
and
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
is relatively quasiconvex. If
\Gamma_{0}
has infinite index in
\Gamma
, then
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
is a closed proper subset of
\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
.

Proof.

We will prove the contrapositive: if
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})=\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, then
\Gamma_{0}
has finite index in
\Gamma
.

Let
X
be a Groves–Manning cusp space for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. Given a subset
A\subset X
, we will let
\mathcal{N}_{r}(A)
denote the closed
r
-neighborhood of
A
in
X
. Since
X
is Gromov hyperbolic, there exists
c>0
such that if
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}:\Rb\rightarrow X
are geodesics with

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\sigma_{1}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\sigma_{2}(t),

then

\sigma_{1}\subset\mathcal{N}_{c}(\sigma_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{2}\subset\mathcal{N}_{c}(\sigma_{1}).

Fix a geodesic
\sigma_{0}:\Rb\rightarrow X
. Then fix
r>0
such that

\id\in\mathcal{N}_{r}(\sigma_{0}).

An equivalent definition
[
24
, Defn. 6.6]
of relatively quasiconvex subgroups implies that there exists
R>0
such that: if
s:[0,T]\rightarrow X
is a geodesic segment with endpoints in
\Gamma_{0}
, then

s\cap\mathcal{N}_{c+r+1}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{N}_{R}(\Gamma_{0}),

see
[
24
, Prop. 7.5 and 7.6]
.

Fix
g\in\Gamma
and let
\sigma:=g\circ\sigma_{0}:\Rb\rightarrow X
. Since
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})=\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, there exist sequences
\{h_{n}^{-}\}
,
\{h_{n}^{+}\}
in
\Gamma_{0}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{n}^{\pm}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\sigma(t).

Let
\sigma_{n}
be a geodesic in
X
joining
h_{n}^{-}
to
h_{n}^{+}
. After possibly reparametrizing and passing to a subsequence we may suppose that
\sigma_{n}
converges locally uniformly to a geodesic
\sigma_{\infty}:\Rb\rightarrow X
. Then

\sigma_{\infty}\cap\mathcal{N}_{c+r}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{N}_{R}(\Gamma_{0}).

Further,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\sigma_{\infty}(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{n}^{\pm}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\pm\infty}\sigma(t),

and so
g\in\mathcal{N}_{r}(\sigma)\subset\mathcal{N}_{c+r}(\sigma_{\infty})
. Hence,

g\in\mathcal{N}_{R+c+r}(\Gamma_{0}).

Since
g\in\Gamma
was arbitrary,

\Gamma\subset\mathcal{N}_{R+c+r}(\Gamma_{0})

and thus
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
has finite index.
∎

Proposition 9.4
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
. Assume
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
is
non-elementary and relatively quasiconvex. Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
denote a set of representatives of the conjugacy classes in
\Gamma_{0}
of the intersection of the peripheral subgroups of
\Gamma
with
\Gamma_{0}
. Then:

(1)

(\Gamma_{0},\mathcal{P}_{0})
is relatively hyperbolic (in the sense of Definition
3.1
).

(2)

\Gamma_{0}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

Proof.

Proof of (1). Hruska
[
24
, Thm. 9.1]
proved that relatively quasiconvex subgroups of relatively hyperbolic groups in his weaker sense (see Remark
9.2
) are also relatively hyperbolic. Thus, it suffices to show that every
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
is slender.

Lemma 9.5
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
. If
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
Q\subset P
is a subgroup, then
Q
is finitely generated.

Proof.

Using Proposition
4.5
we may assume that
Z(\mathsf{G})
is trivial and
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
(notice that if
p:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
is as in the proposition, then
p|_{\Gamma}
has finite kernel and hence
Q
is finitely generated if and only if
p(Q)
is finitely generated). By Theorem
4.4
there exists a closed subgroup
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{G}
with finitely many components such that:

(1)

P
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
.

(2)

\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{L}\ltimes\mathsf{U}
where
\mathsf{L}
is compact and
\mathsf{U}
is the unipotent radical of
\mathsf{H}
.

Then Auslander’s theorem (see for instance
[
26
, Thm. 11.1]
) implies that
Q
is finitely generated.

∎

Proof of (2). By definition,
\Gamma_{0}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-transverse subgroup of
\mathsf{G}
. Also, by (1),
(\Gamma_{0},\mathcal{P}_{0})
is relatively hyperbolic, and so we may identify

\partial(\Gamma_{0},\mathcal{P}_{0})=\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})\subset\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P}).

Since
\Gamma
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
, there is a
\Gamma
-equivariant homeomorphism
\xi:\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})\rightarrow\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. Thus, to show that
\Gamma_{0}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, it suffices to show that
\xi(\Lambda(\Gamma_{0}))=\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma_{0})
.

Fix any
F^{+}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma_{0})
. Then by Proposition
2.2
there is
\{h_{n}\}
in
\Gamma_{0}
and
F^{-}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
such that
h_{n}(F)\rightarrow F^{+}
for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
transverse to
F^{-}
. By definition,
F^{\pm}=\xi(x^{\pm})
for some
x^{\pm}\in\partial(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. Also, since
\Gamma_{0}
acts on
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
as a convergence group, by passing to a subsequence, we can suppose that there exist
y^{\pm}\in\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
such that
h_{n}(z)\rightarrow y^{+}
for all
z\in\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})-\{y^{-}\}
. Since
\Gamma_{0}
is non-elementary, we may fix
z\in\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})-\{x^{-},y^{-}\}
. Then

F^{+}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{n}(\xi(z))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi(h_{n}(z))=\xi(y^{+}).

Since
F^{+}
was arbitrary, it follows that
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma_{0})\subset\xi(\Lambda(\Gamma_{0}))
. A very similar argument shows that
\xi(\Lambda(\Gamma_{0}))\subset\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma_{0})
.
∎

We may now give the proof of Corollary
9.1
.

Proof of Corollary
9.1
.

First, notice that if
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
is an elementary subgroup, then it is either finite (in which case
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
is empty), conjugate to a subgroup of a peripheral subgroup of
\Gamma

(in which case
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
is a single point)
or virtually a cyclic group generated by a hyperbolic element (in which case
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
consists of the the attracting and repelling fixed points of the hyperbolic element). If
\Gamma_{0}
is non-elementary,
then
\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})
is perfect. See the discussion in
[
24
, Sec. 3.1]
for more details.

Case 1:
Suppose
\Gamma_{0}
is non-elementary. Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
denote a set of representatives of the conjugacy classes in
\Gamma_{0}
of the intersection of the peripheral subgroups of
\Gamma
with
\Gamma_{0}
. Then Proposition
9.4
implies that
\Gamma_{0}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. By Lemma
9.3
,
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma_{0})
is a proper subset of
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
. By Theorem
1.1
,
Q^{\phi}_{\Gamma_{0}}
converges at its critical exponent. So, by Theorem
1.6
,

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{0})<\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma).

Case 2:
Assume
\#\Lambda(\Gamma_{0})=2
. Then there is an infinite order hyperbolic element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
such that
\langle\gamma\rangle
has finite index in
\Gamma_{0}
. Since
\Gamma
acts as a convergence group on
\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
, we can label the fixed
points
F^{+},F^{-}\in\Lambda_{\theta}(\Gamma)
of
\gamma
so that
\gamma^{n}(F)\rightarrow F^{+}
for all
F\in\Fc_{\theta}
transverse to
F^{-}
.
By Proposition
2.2
,
U_{\theta}(\gamma^{-n})\rightarrow F^{-}
and
U_{\theta}(\gamma^{n})\rightarrow F^{+}
. Then by Proposition
5.1
there exists

such that

for all

. This estimate implies that

, and hence that
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma_{0})=0
.

Since
\Gamma
is non-elementary, it contains a free subgroup of rank two and hence

Case 3:
Assume

. Then, after conjugating, there is a peripheral subgroup

with

. Then by Corollary

we have

Case 4:
Assume

.
So, as in Case 2,

10.
Characterizing linear functions with finite critical exponent

In this section, we give a complete analysis of which linear functionals in
\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
have associated Poincaré series with finite critical exponents. This generalizes the results of Sambarino
[
38
]
for Anosov groups.

Given a subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
, the
\theta
-Benoist limit cone of
\Gamma
, denoted
B_{\theta}(\Gamma)\subset\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}
, is the set of vectors
X\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}
for which there exists a sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
of distinct elements of
\Gamma
and a
sequence
\{r_{n}\}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
so that
r_{n}\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n})
converges to
X
.

As in Proposition
4.5
, let
p:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
denote the projection map of
\mathsf{G}
onto
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}:=\mathsf{G}/\mathsf{H}
, where
\mathsf{H}
is the product of
Z(\mathsf{G})
and the simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
contained in
\Psf_{\theta}
. Notice that the Benoist limit cones of a group
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
and its projection
\Gamma^{\prime}:=p(\Gamma)\subset\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
satisfy

{\rm d}p(B_{\theta}(\Gamma))=B_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Gamma^{\prime}).

We prove the following expanded version of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 10.1
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset\mathsf{G}
is a
\Psf_{\theta}
-Anosov subgroup relative to
\mathcal{P}
and
\phi\in\mathfrak{a}_{\theta}^{*}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty
for every sequence of distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
.

(2)

\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)<+\infty
.

(3)

If
x_{0}\in M^{\prime}:=\mathsf{G}^{\prime}/\Ksf^{\prime}
, where
\mathsf{K}^{\prime}:=p(\mathsf{K})
, then there exist constants
c\geq 1,C\geq 0
such that

\frac{1}{c}{\rm d}_{M^{\prime}}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})-C\leq\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\leq c{\rm d}_{M^{\prime}}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})+C

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
(where
{\rm d}_{M^{\prime}}
is the distance defined on
M^{\prime}
in Section
2
).

(4)

\phi(Y)>0
for all
Y\in B_{\theta}(\Gamma)-\{0\}
.

(5)

If
X
is a Groves–Manning cusp space for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
, then there exist constants
c,C>0
such that

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\geq c{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)-C

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Indeed, statements (1) and (2) of Theorem
1.3
are the same as statements (1) and (2) of Theorem
10.1
. Furthermore, when
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
, then
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}=\mathsf{G}/Z(\mathsf{G})
. Thus, in this case
M=M^{\prime}
and so statement (3) of Theorem
1.3
is equivalent to statement (3) of Theorem
10.1
.

We will now prove Theorem
10.1
. By part (3) of Proposition
4.5
, we may assume that
\mathsf{G}
has trivial center and
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
, in which case
M^{\prime}=M:=\mathsf{G}/\Ksf
. Then note that any one of (2), (3), (4) or (5) immediately imply (1), and (3) also immediately implies (4). Since
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}
, the equivalence of (3) and (5) follows from Theorem
4.4
. So it suffices to show that (3) implies (2) and (1) implies (5).

Proof of
(3)
\Rightarrow
(2): Without loss of generality we may assume that
x_{0}:=\Ksf\in M=\mathsf{G}/\Ksf
. By assumption, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\geq c{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})-C

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Then
\delta^{\phi}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{1}{c}\delta_{M}(\Gamma)
, where

\delta_{M}(\Gamma):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\log\#\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})<T\right\}}{T}.

Recall that the volume growth entropy of
M
is

h(M):=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{\rm Vol}_{M}(B_{T}(x_{0}))}{T}

where
{\rm Vol}_{M}
is the Riemannian volume on
M
and
B_{r}(x_{0})\subset M
is the open ball of radius
r>0
. Since
M
has bounded sectional curvature, volume comparison theorems imply that
h(M)<+\infty
.

Fix
r_{0}>0
and for
T>0
let
\Gamma_{T}:=\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma(x_{0}),x_{0})<T\right\}
. Then

\#\Gamma_{T}=\frac{1}{{\rm Vol}_{M}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{T}}{\rm Vol}_{M}(B_{r_{0}}(\gamma x_{0}))\leq\frac{\#\Gamma_{2r_{0}}}{{\rm Vol}_{M}(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0}))}{\rm Vol}_{M}(B_{T+r_{0}}(x_{0})).

Thus
\delta_{M}(\Gamma)\leq h(M)<+\infty
.
∎

The proof that (1) implies (5) is more technical, so we provide a brief outline. We first use Proposition
2.3
to provide a lower bound for
\phi\circ\kappa_{\theta}

on peripheral subgroups, see Lemma
10.2
. We then divide a geodesic joining
\id
to
\gamma\in\Gamma
in the Groves–Manning cusp space
X
into segments
\overline{\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i+1}}

with endpoints in
\Gamma
which either (a) have a pre-chosen size guaranteeing that
\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\rho(\gamma_{i+1}\gamma_{i}^{-1})))
is large enough, or (b) are at least
as long as the pre-chosen size and lie entirely in a cusped portion of
X
. We then apply Proposition
5.3
to show that the image
of the Cartan projections are roughly additive along the segment.

Proof of
(1)
\Rightarrow
(5): Suppose that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty

for every sequence of distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
.

Fix a Groves–Manning cusp space
X
for
(\Gamma,\mathcal{P})
. We first control the growth of peripheral elements.

Lemma 10.2
.

There exist
c_{1},C_{1}>0
such that: if
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
\upsilon\in P
, then

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\upsilon))\geq c_{1}{\rm d}_{X}(\upsilon,\id)-C_{1}.

Recall that
{\rm d}_{M}
was defined so that
{\rm d}_{M}(g\Ksf,\Ksf)=\left\|\kappa(g)\right\|
for all
g\in\mathsf{G}
. Then by Theorem
4.4
it suffices to find
c_{1},C_{1}>0
such that: if
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
\upsilon\in P
, then

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\upsilon))\geq c_{1}\left\|\kappa(\upsilon)\right\|-C_{1}.

Then, since
\mathcal{P}
is finite, it is enough to fix
P\in\mathcal{P}
and find constants
c_{P},C_{P}>0
such that: if
\upsilon\in P

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\upsilon))\geq c_{P}\left\|\kappa(\upsilon)\right\|-C_{P}.

By Theorem
4.4
there exists a closed subgroup
\mathsf{H}\subset\mathsf{G}
with finitely many components such that:

(1)

P
is a cocompact lattice in
\mathsf{H}
.

(2)

\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{L}\ltimes\mathsf{U}
where
\mathsf{L}
is compact and
\mathsf{U}
is the unipotent radical of
\mathsf{H}
.

Let
\mathfrak{u}
denote the Lie algebra of
\mathsf{U}
. By Proposition

,
\mathsf{U}=\exp(\mathfrak{u})
. By the same proposition, for any
\alpha\in\theta
there exist
M_{\alpha}\in\Nb
,

and a positive everywhere defined rational function

where

\frac{1}{C_{\alpha}}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}\leq e^{\omega_{\alpha}(\kappa(\exp(Y)))}\leq C_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(Y)^{1/M_{\alpha}}

for all

.

Write
\phi=\sum_{\alpha\in\theta}c_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
, and define
R:=\prod_{\alpha\in\theta}R_{\alpha}^{\left|c_{\alpha}\right|/M_{\alpha}}
and
C_{\phi}:=\prod_{\alpha\in\theta}C_{\alpha}^{\left|c_{\alpha}\right|}
. Then

\frac{1}{C_{\phi}}R(Y)\leq e^{\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y)))}\leq C_{\phi}R(Y)

for all
s\in\Rb
. We proved in Lemma
7.3
that

is positive and proper, so by Theorem

there exist
c_{2},\epsilon>0
such that

R(Y)\geq c_{2}(1+\left\|Y\right\|)^{\epsilon}

for all

. By Proposition
2.3
, there exist
A>0
such that

\left\|\kappa(\exp(Y))\right\|\leq A+A\log(1+\left\|Y\right\|)

for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
. Finally, let

M:=\max\{\left\|\kappa(\ell)\right\|:\ell\in\mathsf{L}\}.

If
\upsilon\in P
, then
\upsilon=\ell\exp(Y)
for some
\ell\in\mathsf{L}
and
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
. So by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\upsilon))

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\exp(Y)))-M\left\|\phi\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\log R(Y)-M\left\|\phi\right\|-\log C_{\phi}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon\log(1+\left\|Y\right\|)-M\left\|\phi\right\|-\log C_{\phi}+\log c_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{A}\left\|\kappa(\exp(Y))\right\|-\frac{\epsilon}{A}-M\left\|\phi\right\|-\log C_{\phi}+\log c_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{A}\left\|\kappa(\upsilon)\right\|-\frac{\epsilon}{A}M-\frac{\epsilon}{A}-M\left\|\phi\right\|-\log C_{\phi}+\log c_{2}.\qed

By Proposition
5.3
there exists
C_{0}>0
such that: if
f:[0,T]\rightarrow X
is a geodesic with
f(0)=\id
, and
f(t_{1}),f(t_{2})\in\Gamma
for some

, then

\left|\phi\big(\kappa_{\theta}(f(t_{2}))\big)-\phi\big(\kappa_{\theta}(f(t_{1}))\big)-\phi\big(\kappa_{\theta}(f(t_{1})^{-1}f(t_{2}))\big)\right|\leq C_{0}.

(16)

By hypothesis,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))=+\infty
for every sequence of distinct elements
\{\gamma_{n}\}
in
\Gamma
, so there exists

such that: if
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)\geq T_{0}

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))>1+C_{0}.

(17)

Then let

C_{2}:=\max\{\left|\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\right|:{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)<T_{0}\},

let

and let

and

as described above.

Fix
\gamma\in\Gamma
and let

be a geodesic with

and

.
If

, then

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\geq-C_{2}\geq c{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)-C.

If

, fix a partition

with the following properties:

(1)

t_{n+1}-t_{n}\geq T_{0}
for

,

(2)

\gamma_{n}:=f(t_{n})\in\Gamma
for

and

if

.

Then by Equation (

),

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\Big(\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n+1}))-\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}))\Big)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\Big(\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))-C_{0}\Big).

Thus to complete the proof it suffices to verify that

\displaystyle\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))\geq c(t_{n+1}-t_{n})+C_{0}

(18)

for each
n=0,\dots,m-1
. Indeed, if this were the case, then

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma))\geq c\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}(t_{n+1}-t_{n})=cT\geq c{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)-C.

We will now prove Equation (
18
). Fix
n\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
. If
t_{n+1}-t_{n}<(B+2)T_{0}
, then Equation (
17
) implies that

\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))\geq 1+C_{0}\geq c(t_{n+1}-t_{n})+C_{0}.

If
t_{n+1}-t_{n}>(B+2)T_{0}
, then by the maximality of the partition and the fact that
B+2>4
, there exist
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\eta\in\Gamma
and
a,b\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
such that:

(1)

a\in[t_{n},t_{n}+T_{0})
,
b\in(t_{n+1}-T_{0},t_{n+1}]
(hence
a<b
),

(2)

f(a),f(b)\in\eta P
, and

(3)

f|_{[a,b]}
is contained in the combinatorial horoball associated to
\eta P
.

Then by applying Equation (
16
) to the geodesic
t\mapsto\gamma_{n}^{-1}f(t)
,

\displaystyle\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}f(a)))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(f(a)^{-1}f(b)))+\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(f(b)^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))-2C_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(f(a)^{-1}f(b)))-2C_{0}-2C_{2},

where the last equality holds by the definition of
C_{2}
. Since
f(a)^{-1}f(b)\in P
and

{\rm d}_{X}(f(a)^{-1}f(b),\id)={\rm d}_{X}(f(a),f(b))>BT_{0},

by Lemma
10.2
, we then have

\displaystyle\phi(\kappa_{\theta}(\gamma_{n}^{-1}\gamma_{n+1}))

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}{\rm d}_{X}(f(a)^{-1}f(b),\id)-2C_{0}-C_{1}-2C_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c_{1}}{2}{\rm d}_{X}(f(a),f(b))+C_{0}+c_{1}T_{0}

\displaystyle\geq c(t_{n}-t_{n-1})+C_{0}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.3

In this appendix, we prove Proposition
2.3
. We start with an observation about the linear case.

Lemma A.1
.

If
\mathsf{U}\subset\SL(d,\Rb)
is a connected unipotent group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}
, then there exist
C_{0}>1
and a positive polynomial
P:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\Rb

such that

\frac{1}{C_{0}}P(Y)^{1/2}\leq\sigma_{1}(e^{Y})\leq C_{0}P(Y)^{1/2}

for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
.

Proof.

Define
P:\mathfrak{u}\rightarrow\Rb
by

P(Y)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left[e^{Y}\right]_{i,j}^{2}.

Since
\mathfrak{u}
is nilpotent, see
[
7
, Section 4.8]
,
P
is a polynomial. Observe that the Euclidean norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\rm Euc}:\mathrm{End}(\Rb^{d})\to\Rb
and the first singular value
\sigma_{1}:\mathrm{End}(\Rb^{d})\to\Rb
are both norms on the vector space
\mathrm{End}(\Rb^{d})
, so there exists
C_{0}>1
such that

\frac{1}{C_{0}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}(X)}{\left\|X\right\|_{\rm Euc}}\leq C_{0}

for all
X\in\mathrm{End}(\Rb^{d})
. Since
P(Y)^{1/2}=\left\|e^{Y}\right\|_{\rm Euc}
for all
Y\in\mathfrak{u}
, the lemma follows.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Proof of (1). Since

Proof of (4). Let
d:=\dim\mathfrak{g}

\mathfrak{g}=\Rb^{d}

\mathsf{GL}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathsf{GL}(d,\Rb)

\mathfrak{sl}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathfrak{sl}(d,\Rb)

Proof of Theorem

In this appendix we prove Theorem

Theorem B.1

Assume
Z(\mathsf{G})
is trivial and
\Psf_{\theta}
contains no simple factors of
\mathsf{G}

\Psf_{\theta}

X

\frac{1}{c}{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma\Ksf,\Ksf)-C\leq{\rm d}_{X}(\gamma,\id)\leq c{\rm d}_{M}(\gamma\Ksf,\Ksf)+C

(2)

If
P\in\mathcal{P}

P
is a cocompact lattice in a closed Lie group
\mathsf{H}
with finitely many components. Moreover,

(a)

\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{L}\ltimes\mathsf{U}
where
\mathsf{L}

\mathsf{U}
is the unipotent radical of
\mathsf{H}

(b)

\mathsf{H}^{0}=\mathsf{L}^{0}\times\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{L}^{0}
is Abelian.

Fix
\mathsf{G}
,
\Psf_{\theta}
,
\Gamma
, and
\mathcal{P}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.4

\chi:=\sum_{\alpha\in\theta}\omega_{\alpha}
, and let
N\in\Nb

\Phi:\mathsf{G}\rightarrow\SL(d,\Rb)
and
\xi:\Fc_{\theta}\rightarrow\Fc_{1,d-1}(\Rb^{d})

4.6
for

\mathsf{G}_{\star}

\Phi(\mathsf{G})

\SL(d,\Rb)

Lemma B.2
.

\Phi
is injective,
\mathsf{G}_{\star}
is semisimple and
\Phi(\mathsf{G})=\mathsf{G}_{\star}^{0}

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{G}

\ker\Phi

\mathsf{G}

\xi:\Fc_{\theta}\rightarrow\Fc_{1,d-1}(\Rb^{d})

\mathsf{G}

\ker\Phi
is normal, this implies that for any

, there is a continuous path in
\ker\Phi
between
h

ghg^{-1}
. The discreteness of
\ker\Phi
then implies that
ghg^{-1}=h
. Since both
g
and
h

\ker\Phi
is contained in the center of
\mathsf{G}

\ker\Phi
is trivial.

By construction
\Phi(\mathsf{G})\subset\SL(d,\Rb)
is irreducible and contains a proximal element and hence

11
