Erhan Bayraktar, Ibrahim Ekren, Xin Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we study a learning problem in which a forecaster only observes partial information. By properly rescaling the problem, we heuristically derive a limiting PDE on Wasserstein space which characterizes the asymptotic behavior of the regret of the forecaster. Using a verification type argument, we show that the problem of obtaining regret bounds and efficient algorithms can be tackled by finding appropriate smooth sub/supersolutions of this parabolic PDE.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study a zero-sum game between a forecaster and an adversary. At each round, the forecaster chooses an action between
K\geq 2
alternative actions based on his partial observations aiming at performing as well as the best constant strategy, while the adversary aims at maximizing the forecaster’s regret. Our problem is motivated by
prediction with expert advice
and
bandit problems
(see e.g.
[
13
,
9
]
), which are fundamental problems in online learning and sequential decision making. The main difference between prediction with expert advice and bandit problem is the information observed by the forecaster. In prediction with expert advice problems, the forecaster can monitor the outcomes of each alternative action, whereas in bandit problems, the forecaster can only observe the outcome of the action chosen. Thus, the former problem is a full information game whereas the latter is a bandit game (see e.g.
[
1
,
2
]
).

The most commonly used algorithm for decision making and prediction problem is the so-called
multiplicative weights algorithm
, which assigns initial weights to each expert, update these weights multiplicatively and iteratively based on their performance, and randomly choose experts according to their weights. This simple algorithm is widely used and has been proven efficient in practice. However, it cannot provide accurate regret bounds and best strategies for the forecaster. In
[
18
]
, techniques from partial differential equations were first employed to understand asymptotic behavior of prediction of expert advice problems. Since then, it became popular and has been proven powerful in certain problems, see e.g.
[
17
,
23
,
24
,
6
,
7
,
5
,
8
,
22
,
26
,
21
]
.

In full information games, these papers rely on the fact that the difference
(X^{i}_{t})_{i=1,\ldots,N}=(G^{i}_{t}-G_{t})_{i=1,\ldots,N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
between the gain
G_{t}
of the forecaster and the gain
G^{i}_{t}
of each action
i
is a natural state variable for the dynamic game between the forecaster and the adversary. Thus, the minimax regret of the forecaster satisfies a finite dimensional dynamic programming principle whose scaling limit is a parabolic partial differential equation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. For bandit games or in the presence of partial information such methodology cannot be applied. Indeed, due to partial information, the natural state variable for the dynamic programming principle is the set of probability distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
which encodes the distribution
m_{t}
of
X_{t}
conditional on the information of the forecaster. Thus, with partial information, the fundamental problem is to understand the dynamics of
m_{t}
and how these dynamics behave in the long-time regime.

Our main contribution consists in showing that the update of the conditional distribution between two consecutive time steps from
m_{t}
to
m_{t+1}
admits a scaling limit that can be described using partial differential equations in the Wasserstein space. The equations we obtain are fully nonlinear versions of the PDEs appearing in mean-field games and Mckean-Vlasov control problems, see e.g.
[
11
,
15
,
3
]
. This novel relation between the discrete-time bandit problem and the continuous-time equations comes from the fact that in the game we study, the updated measure
m_{t+1}
can be written as a push-forward operator on
m_{t}
, i.e.
m_{t+1}=(Id+Y_{t})\sharp m_{t}
where
Y_{t}
is a (random) function describing the feature learned by the forecaster on
[t,t+1]
. If the game is played
T
times and if we rescale the problem with its natural
\sqrt{T}
scaling, the update of the
m_{t}
can be written as
m_{t+\frac{1}{T}}=(Id+\frac{Y_{t}}{\sqrt{T}})\sharp m_{t}
. In the long-time regime, i.e., as
T\to\infty
, by the definition of the Wasserstein derivative (see
[
11
, Proposition 2.3]
), we obtain that for any smooth function
U
, we have the expansion

U\left(m_{t+\frac{1}{T}}\right)=U\left(m_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int D_{m}U(m_{t},x)Y_{t}(x)\,m(dx)+\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).

Thus, the impact of the Bayesian update of the distribution
m_{t}
can be characterized in the long-time regime using the Wasserstein derivative
D_{m}U
. In fact, we derive a second order expansion of
U(m_{t+\frac{1}{T}})
involving the derivatives
D_{x}D_{m}U
and
D_{mm}^{2}U
which allows us to heuristically exhibit a second order parabolic equation of type

(1.1)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}U(t,m)

\displaystyle+F\left(\int D_{m}U(t,m,x)m(dx),\int D_{x}D_{m}U(t,m,x)m(dx),\iint D^{2}_{mm}U(t,m,x,y)m(dx)m(dy)\right)

which is expected to govern the dynamics of the prediction problem in the long-time regime. In this equation, the unknown is the function
U
,
F
is a function that can be explicitly computed from the Bayes’ rule, and the derivatives are defined as in
[
11
,
15
]
.

The equation (
1.1
) gives simple methods to obtain algorithms and regret bounds for the long-time regime of the prediction problem with partial information. Indeed, using a verification type argument we show that the gradient
D_{m}U
of any smooth supersolution
\phi
of (
1.1
) satisfying some growth condition yields to an algorithm that guarantees an upper bound for regret of order
\phi(0,\delta_{0})\sqrt{T}
where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac mass at
0
. A similar result also holds for appropriate subsolutions.

Due to the nonlinearity on the second derivative term
D_{mm}^{2}U
, wellposedness of viscosity solutions for (
1.1
) is not available in the literature.
Hence, the questions of establishing appropriate comparison result for viscosity solutions and obtaining the exact growth of the regret as for example in
[
18
]
are left for future research.

The rest of paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we formulate our problem and show that the value function of the game depends only on the law
m_{t}
of
X_{t}
conditional on the information of the agents. Then, using Bayes’ rule, we compute explicitly the update of beliefs and prove a dynamic programming principle. In Section 3, by properly rescaling the value function and using differential calculus on the space of measures, we heuristically obtain a limiting PDE of type (
1.1
) on the Wasserstein space. In Section 4 and 5, using smooth supersolutions and subsolutions of the PDE, we construct strategies for the forecaster and the adversary, and find upper and lower bounds of expected regret.

2.
Formulation of the problem

Our online prediction problem with partial observation can be described as a
T
-round game, played by a forecaster in an adversarial environment. Suppose that there are
K
actions. At each round
t
, the forecaster chooses an action
I_{t}\in[K]
, and independently the adversary chooses the reward
J^{i}_{t}
of action
i
to be
0
or
1
, i.e.,
J_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
. Then the total gain of the forecaster
G_{t}
and the total gain
G_{t}^{i}
of action
i
evolve as

\displaystyle G_{t+1}-G_{t}={\mathbf{1}}_{I_{t}\in J_{t}},

\displaystyle G^{i}_{t+1}-G^{i}_{t}={\mathbf{1}}_{i\in J_{t}},\,\,\,i=1,\dots,K.

The goal of the forecaster is to design a robust strategy that performs as well as the best constant strategy under any adversarial environment, i.e., to minimize
\max\limits_{Adversary}{\mathbb{E}}[\max_{i}X_{T}^{i}]
, where
X_{t}^{i}:=G^{i}_{t}-G_{t}
is the state variable evolving as

\displaystyle X_{t+1}-X_{t}:=e_{J_{t}}-{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{t}\in J_{t}}e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}.

Both the forecaster and the adversary are allowed to adopt randomized strategies. At each round
t
, they decide on distributions
b_{t}
of
I_{t}
and
a_{t}
of
J_{t}
respectively. If we allow both agents to observe the outcomes of
I_{t}
and
J_{t}
, this problem is the classical prediction with expert advice problem in the adversarial setting, see for example
[
16
,
13
,
19
,
18
]
.

Let us now describe information observed by the forecaster and his admissible strategies in the partial information problem we aim to study. At initial time
t=0
, both the adversary and the forecaster get informed of the distribution
m_{0}
of
X_{0}
. For any
t\geq 0
, the random variable

\displaystyle Y_{t}:={\mathbf{1}}_{I_{t}\in J_{t}}{I_{t}}-{\mathbf{1}}_{I_{t}\not\in J_{t}}{I_{t}}\in\{\pm i\}

indicates whether the forecaster makes a good decision or not. Both players can observe the law of adversary’s control
a_{t-1}
and the indicator
y_{t-1}
. Their accumulated information is given by

\displaystyle h_{t}:=(m_{0},a_{0},y_{0}\dotso,a_{t-1},y_{t-1})\in\mathcal{H}_{t},\quad(h_{0}:=m_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}),

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}:={\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\times\left({\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})\times\{\pm i\}\right)^{t}.

The strategies of the forecaster and the adversary are measurable functions
\beta_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to{\mathcal{P}}([K])
and
\alpha_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
respectively. Define
\mathcal{A}
to be the set of all possible strategies
\alpha:=(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dotso,\alpha_{T-1})
, and
\mathcal{B}
similarly.

Suppose this game starts from time
t
with an initial distribution
m\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})
. Then given any strategies
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
,
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
, the regret for the forecaster is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{T}(t,m,\alpha,\beta):={\mathbb{E}}^{m,\alpha,\beta}[\max_{i}X^{i}_{T}\,|\,X_{t}\sim m].

From the perspective of the forecaster, we aim at solving a minimax problem

(2.1)

\displaystyle v_{T}(t,m):=\inf_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\gamma_{T}(t,m,\alpha,\beta),

and we denote this two player game by
\Gamma_{T}(t,m)
.

Remark 2.1
.

(i) This formulation is motivated by the classical bandit problems, see for example
[
13
,
9
]
. Similar to the bandit problems, both agents have access to the same partial information and they simultaneously choose their strategies to be played at each round. Before each round, our adversary has the same information as the non-oblivious adversary of
[
1
]
. However, unlike the bandit problems where the agents only observe
Y_{t}
, after each round, the forecaster also learns the strategy
a_{t}
chosen by the adversary in the previous round. Therefore, both players does not get access to state variable
X_{t}
, but still can compute the same update of its law conditional to a common filtration.

(ii) We note that the observation of
a_{t}
by the forecaster has a practical implication in terms of design of recommender systems. In the problem that we envision, the forecaster recommends successively one item among
K
alternatives to a sequence of users. The forecaster’s strategy is the choice of a randomization
b_{t}\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])
which leads to the realization of a random variable
I_{t}\in[K]
representing the recommendation of the forecaster. A sequence of users arrive and decide whether or not they accept the successive recommendations of the forecaster. We identify each user with
a_{t}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
so that the realization of random variable
J_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
represents the random choice that each successive user makes. Thus, unlike stochastic bandit problems,
a_{t}
is chosen adversarially at each round and learned by the forecaster after the round. In this context, the assumption that the forecaster learns
a_{t}
means that after proposing
I_{t}
, the forecaster learns the identity of the user
a_{t}
.

(ii) In our context, since the forecaster learns
a_{t}
, he can update
m_{t}
via Bayes’ rule. This update is impossible in the classical bandit problems. An interesting question that is left for future research is to extend our PDE tools to allow such an uncertainty on the update procedure of the conditional distribution.

2.1.
Dynamic programming principle

In this subsection, we establish the dynamic programming principle for the game (
2.1
), and reduce controls
\alpha,\beta
to functions of conditional distribution of the state
X
. Let us first compute the distribution of
X
, i.e., belief, given prior information. Suppose the current distribution is
m
and
X
is a random variable with distribution
m
. We denote
\Delta X
the change of
X
between two rounds. The players choose strategies
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
and
b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])
respectively, and receive signal
y\in\{\pm i\}
. We denote by
{\mathcal{L}}^{a,b}
the distribution of a random variable and by
{\mathbb{P}}^{a,b}
the probability of an event given the strategies of the agents. We omit the superscripts
a
or
b
if this dependence is clear from the context. We also denote by

l(m,a,y):=\mathcal{L}^{a}(X+\Delta X|X\sim m,Y=y)\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})

the Bayesian update of the distribution.

We will compute the explicit formula of
l(m,a,y)
in the next Lemma. For any
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
,
b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])
, denote

\displaystyle\hat{a}(i):=\sum_{j,i\in j}a(j),\quad\hat{a}(-i):=\sum_{j,i\notin j}a(j),\quad\forall\,i\in[K].

Proposition 2.1
.

Given
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
and the distribution
m\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})
, we have that

\displaystyle l(m,a,i)=\sum_{j,i\in j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}m\right)_{\sharp-e_{j^{c}}},\quad l(m,a,-i)=\sum_{j,i\notin j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}m\right)_{\sharp e_{j}}.

We make the convention in these expressions that
l(m,a,y)=\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})
whenever
\hat{a}(y)=0
.

Proof.

For
j\subset[K]
and
i\in[K]
, it can be easily verified that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta X=e_{j},Y=-i,X\in dx)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}a(j)b(i)m(dx)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta X=-e_{j^{c}},Y=i,X\in dx)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}a(j)b(i)m(dx)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y=i)

\displaystyle=b(i)\sum_{k,i\in k}a(k)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(Y=-i)

\displaystyle=b(i)\sum_{k,i\notin k}a(k)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta X=e_{j},X\in dx\,|\,Y=-i)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}a(j)m(dx)}{\sum_{k,i\notin k}a(k)}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta X=-e_{j^{c}},X\in dx\,|\,Y=i)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}a(j)m(dx)}{\sum_{k,i\in k}a(k)}.

Therefore, conditioning on
Y
, the distribution of
X+\Delta X
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[(X+\Delta X)\in dx\,|\,Y=i\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\in j}{\mathbb{P}}\left[X\in d(x+e_{j^{c}}),\Delta X=-e_{j^{c}}\,|\,Y=i\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\in j}\frac{a(j)m(d(x+e_{j^{c}}))}{\hat{a}(i)},

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\in j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}m\right)_{\sharp-e_{j^{c}}}(dx),

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[(X+\Delta X)\in dx\,|\,Y=-i\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\notin j}{\mathbb{P}}\left[X\in d(x-e_{j}),\Delta X=e_{j}\,|\,Y=-i\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\notin j}\frac{a(j)m(d(x-e_{j}))}{\hat{a}(-i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,i\notin j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}m\right)_{\sharp e_{j}}(dx).

∎

The following theorem proves a dynamic programming principle showing that one can solve (
2.1
) with a backward induction.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any distribution
m\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})
and
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have that

\displaystyle v_{T}(t,m)=

\displaystyle\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}b(i)\hat{a}(i)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,i))\right.

(2.2)

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{i=1}^{K}b(i)\hat{a}(-i)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,-i))\right),

where
b(i)\hat{a}(i)
,
b(i)\hat{a}(-i)
represent the probability of receiving signal
i
,
-i
respectively, and
l(m,a,\pm i)
is the update of beliefs.

Proof.

The equation (
2.1
) holds trivially for
t=T-1
. Suppose it is true for
t+1
. Let us prove it for
t
. Denote
by
v
the value of the right hand side of (
2.1
).
For any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
, denote
\alpha_{t+1:T}=\{\alpha_{t+1},\dotso,\alpha_{T-1}\}
,
\beta_{t+1:T}=\{\beta_{t+1},\dotso,\beta_{T-1}\}
.
It is clear that

\displaystyle\gamma_{T}(t,m,\alpha,\beta)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\in k}\beta_{t}(i)\alpha_{t}(k)\gamma_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},i),\alpha_{t+1:T},\beta_{t+1:T})

(2.3)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\notin k}\beta_{t}(i)\alpha_{t}(k)\gamma_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},-i),\alpha_{t+1:T},\beta_{t+1:T}),

where
l(m,\alpha_{t},\pm i)
is the conditional distribution of
X_{t+1}
. For the game

\gamma_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},\pm i))
, due to our induction hypothesis, the value of this game exists and is just
v_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},\pm i))
. Taking supremum over
\alpha
on both sides of (
2.1
), it can be easily seen that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha}\gamma_{T}(t,m,\alpha,\beta)\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha_{t}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\in k}\beta_{t}(i)\alpha_{t}(k)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},i))\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\notin k}\beta_{t}(i)\alpha_{t}(k)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,\alpha_{t},-i))\right).

Taking infimum over
\beta
, we conclude that
v_{T}(c,m)\geq v
.

Then we prove that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a robust strategy
\beta^{*}
of the forecaster such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha}\gamma_{T}(t,m,\alpha,\beta^{*})<v+2\epsilon.

Take
\beta_{t}^{*}\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])
with the property that

\displaystyle v+\epsilon>

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\in k}\beta_{t}^{*}(i)a(k)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,i))\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{k:i\notin k}\beta_{t}^{*}(i)a(k)v_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,-i))\right).

By induction hypothesis, for any belief
l(m,a,\pm i)
, the forecaster can choose a strategy
\beta_{t+1:T}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle v_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,\pm i))+\epsilon>\sup_{\alpha_{t+1:T}}\gamma_{T}(t+1,l(m,a,\pm i),\alpha_{t+1:T},\beta_{t+1:T}^{*}).

Taking
\beta^{*}=(\beta_{t}^{*},\beta^{*}_{t+1:T})
, clearly it is measurable and satisfies (
2.4
).
∎

3.
Heuristic expansion of the rescaled value function

Let us define the rescaled value functions

\displaystyle u^{T}(s,m):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}v_{T}\left({\lceil sT\rceil},m^{*\sqrt{T}}\right),

and equivalently

\displaystyle v_{T}({\lceil sT\rceil},m)=\sqrt{T}u^{T}\left(s,m^{*\sqrt{T^{-1}}}\right).

For any
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
and belief
m\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{K})
, denote

(3.1)

\displaystyle A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle=\left({\sum_{j,i\in j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}m^{*\sqrt{T}}\right)_{\sharp-e_{j^{c}}}}\right)^{*\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}},\,\,\,\,\,A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j,i\notin j}\left(\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}m^{*\sqrt{T}}\right)_{\sharp e_{j}}\right)^{*\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}}.

Then due to (
2.1
), it holds that

\displaystyle u^{T}\left(s-\frac{1}{T},m\right)=

\displaystyle\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\bigg(\sum_{i}b(i)\hat{a}(i)u^{T}\left(s,A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}\right)

(3.2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}b(i)\hat{a}(-i)u^{T}\left(s,A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}\right)\bigg),

with the terminal condition

\displaystyle u^{T}(1,m)=\int_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}}\max_{i}{x^{i}}\,m(dx).

Now we want to derive a limit for (
3
) as
T\to\infty
. This derivation requires us to take derivatives in the direction of
A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}-m
and
A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}-m
in the Wasserstein space. Let us introduce the differentiability of functions over the Wasserstein space as defined in
[
11
,
12
]
.

A function
u:{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be Fréchet differentiable if
there exists a continuous function

\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}:{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}

so that for all
(m,m^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{u(m+h(m^{\prime}-m))-u(m)}{h}=\int\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}(m,x)\,(m^{\prime}-m)(dx).

Whenever
\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}
is differentiable in
x
, we also define

\displaystyle D_{m}u(m,x)=D_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}(m,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}.

Similarly to
[
11
,
14
]
, we can also define
D_{x}D_{m}u(m,x)
,
D^{2}_{mm}u(m,x,y)
.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
u:{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
\mathcal{C}^{1}
if
D_{m}u(m,x)
is continuous and has at most quadratic growth in
x
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|D_{m}u(m,x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2}).

It is said to be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
if
D_{x}D_{m}u(m,x)
and
D^{2}_{mm}u(m,x,y)
are continuous, and have at most quadratic growth in
x
and
(x,y)
respectively.

It is shown in
[
11
, Proposition 2.3]
that
D_{m}u
can be understood as a derivative of
u
along push-forward directions, meaning that for all Borel measurable bounded vector field
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{K}

we have

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{u((Id+h\phi)_{\sharp}m)-u(m)}{h}=\int D_{m}u(m,x)\phi(x)\,m(dx).

Due to the expression of
A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}
and
A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}
, we need to take derivatives in the directions
\left(Id+\frac{e_{j}}{T}\right)
which are constant vector fields. However, the presence of terms
\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\sharp m
in (
3.1
) is a randomization among the directions of the vector fields. The following Proposition shows that at the leading order, we can simplify these perturbations by averaging over these different vector fields. We recall the notational convention that for all
m^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})

\displaystyle D_{m}u(m,[m^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=\int D_{m}u(m,x)\,m^{\prime}(dx)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K});{\mathbb{R}})
. Then for all
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
and
i\in[K]
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\sqrt{T}\left(u(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)\right)

\displaystyle=-{\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\sqrt{T}\left(u(A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)\right)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right),

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}:=\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}e_{j^{c}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K},\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}:=\sum_{j:i\notin j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}e_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that
-{\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
(resp.
{\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
) represents the increase in the expectation of
X_{t}
given the information that
Y=i
(resp.
Y=-i
) and the adversary’s strategy
a
.

Proof.

Let us only compute the derivative in the direction of
A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}-m
. By the definition of
\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}
, denoting
\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m}=m+s(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}-m)
we have that

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}(u(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{T}\int_{0}^{1}\int\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x\right)\,(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}-m)(dx)\,ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\sqrt{T}\int_{0}^{1}\int\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x\right)\,m(dx)\,ds,

and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\sqrt{T}\left(u(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)\right)=-\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\int e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,x\right)\,m(dx).

∎

We can now give the second order expansion along
T\mapsto u(A^{a,m}_{y,\sqrt{T}})
for all
y=\pm i
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
u\in C^{2}({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K});{\mathbb{R}})
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}T\left(u(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}{\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right)\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right)e_{j^{c}}

\displaystyle\,\,\,+{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k,j:i\in k,i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\frac{a(k)}{\hat{a}(i)}{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(m,[m],[m]\right)e_{k^{c}},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}T\left(u(A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}{\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right)\right)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j:i\notin j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}e_{j}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(m,[m]\right)e_{j}

\displaystyle+{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{k,j:i\notin k,i\notin j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}\frac{a(k)}{\hat{a}(-i)}{e_{j}^{\top}}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(m,[m],[m]\right)e_{k}

Remark 3.2
.

The Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
show that, at the leading orders, the impact of the scaled update
A^{a,m}_{y,\sqrt{T}}
of
m
on a smooth function
u
can be characterized by multiplication of
D_{m}u
,
D_{x}D_{m}u
, and
D_{mm}^{2}u
with some matrices depending only on
a
.

Proof.

Using the
[
11
, Equality (25)]
, we have

\displaystyle T\left(u(A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}})-u(m)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\int e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{m}u\left(m,x\right)dm(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}T\int_{0}^{1}\int\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\frac{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{T}}D_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}u\left(m,x\right)dm(x)ds.

Let us compute the limit of integrand on the right hand side. By Taylor expansion on
x
, it can ben seen that

\displaystyle T\left(\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},x\right)+\frac{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{T}}D_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}u\left(m,x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D^{2}_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},\widetilde{x}_{T}\right)e_{j^{c}}-\sqrt{T}{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}\left(D_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,T,m},x\right)-D_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}u\left(m,x\right)\right)

where
\widetilde{x}_{T}
is some point on the line segment joining
x
and
x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}
. Denoting
\widetilde{A}_{r,s,\sqrt{T},m}=m+r(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m}-m)
, the right hand side of the above equation equals to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D^{2}_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(\widetilde{A}_{s,\sqrt{T},m},\widetilde{x}_{T}\right)e_{j^{c}}-s\sum_{k:i\in k}\frac{a(k)}{\hat{a}(i)}\int_{0}^{1}\int

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}\bigg({e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}D_{x}\frac{\delta^{2}u}{\delta m^{2}}\left(\widetilde{A}_{r,s,\sqrt{T},m},x,x^{\prime}-\frac{e_{k^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}D_{x}\frac{\delta^{2}u}{\delta m^{2}}\left(\widetilde{A}_{r,s,\sqrt{T},m},x,x^{\prime}\right)\bigg)\,dm(x^{\prime})dx.

Letting

, it converges to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D^{2}_{x}\frac{\delta u}{\delta m}\left(m,x\right)e_{j^{c}}+s\sum_{k:i\in k}\frac{a(k)}{\hat{a}(i)}{e_{j^{c}}^{\top}}\int D^{2}_{x,x^{\prime}}\frac{\delta^{2}u}{\delta m^{2}}u\left(m,x,x^{\prime}\right)e_{k^{c}}\,dm(x^{\prime}),

and hence we obtain (
3.3
) by integrating over
x
. Similar computation yields to (
3.4
).
∎

We now use (
3
) to obtain a formal asymptotics for
u^{T}
as
T\to\infty
. Assuming
u^{T}
converges to a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function
u:[0,1]\times{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the dynamic programming principle yields to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sum_{i}b(i)\hat{a}(i)T\left(u^{T}\left(s,A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}\right)-u^{T}\left(s-\frac{1}{T},m\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+b(i)\hat{a}(-i)T\left(u^{T}\left(s,A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}\right)-u^{T}\left(s-\frac{1}{T},m\right)\right).

Using Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
for large enough

, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)=\partial_{t}u(t,m)+\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sqrt{T}\sum_{i}b(i)\left(\hat{a}(-i){\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}-\hat{a}(i){\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}\right)^{\top}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{b(i)}{\hat{a}(i)}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}^{\top}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(t,m,[m],[m]\right){\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}+\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)e_{j^{c}}\right)

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{b(i)}{\hat{a}(-i)}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}^{\top}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(t,m,[m],[m]\right){\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}+\sum_{j:i\notin j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}e_{j}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)e_{j}\right).

Notice that
A^{a,m_{\sharp\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}}}_{y,\sqrt{T}}=\left(A^{a,m}_{y,\sqrt{T}}\right)_{\sharp\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}}
for any
y\in\{\pm i\}
, and the final condition satisfies
u^{T}(1,m_{\sharp\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}})=u^{T}(1,m)+\epsilon
. Therefore by backward induction, we have
u^{T}(t,m_{\sharp\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}})=u^{T}(t,m)+\epsilon
for any
t\in[0,1]
, and also in its limit as

\displaystyle u(t,m_{\sharp\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}})=u(t,m)+\epsilon.

Thus, thanks to
[
11
, Proposition 2.3]
, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}D_{m}u(t,m,[m])=1.

Additionally, each component of
D_{m}u(t,m,[m])
is clearly non-negative, which implies that
D_{m}u(t,m,[m])\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
is simplex valued.
Denoting
u_{i}(t,m)
the
i
th component of
D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}b(i)\left(\sum_{j:i\notin j}{a(j)}e_{j}^{\top}-\sum_{j:i\in j}{a(j)}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}\right)D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}b(i)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}e_{j}^{\top}-\sum_{j:i\in j}{a(j)}{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\right)D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}{a(j)}\sum_{i\in j}u_{i}(t,m)-\sum_{i}b(i)\sum_{j:i\in j}{a(j)}=\sum_{i}(u_{i}(t,m)-b(i))\sum_{j:i\in j}{a(j)}.

Thus, in order to have the equality (
3.5
), the coefficients of the
\sqrt{T}
term must be zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sum_{i}b(i)\left(\hat{a}(-i){\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}-\hat{a}(i){\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}\right)^{\top}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)

\displaystyle=\inf_{b\in{\mathcal{P}}([K])}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sum_{i}(u_{i}(t,m)-b(i))\sum_{j:i\in j}{a(j)}.

Otherwise, the first order term explodes. Therefore the forecaster is forced to choose the strategy
b=D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)
, and we obtain the PDE

(3.7)

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}u(t,m)+\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sum_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{u_{i}(t,m)}{\hat{a}(i)}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}^{\top}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(t,m,[m],[m]\right){\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}+\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)e_{j^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{u_{i}(t,m)}{\hat{a}(-i)}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}^{\top}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(t,m,[m],[m]\right){\mathcal{V}}_{a,-i}+\sum_{j:i\notin j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(-i)}e_{j}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)e_{j}\right).

Remark 3.3
.

(i) We say
a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})
is a balanced strategy if
\sum\limits_{j:i\in j}a(j)
is independent of
i
, and denote by
\mathcal{E}
the set of all balanced strategies. According to (
3
), if we restrict

in (

) to be balanced, the first order term vanishes for any

.

(ii)The standard tool to show the convergence of

to the solution of (
3.7
) is to use the stability and comparison of viscosity solutions, see for example
[
17
,
4
]
in the finite dimensional cases. However, a comparison result for viscosity solution of second order PDEs on the Wasserstein space is not available in the literature in the generality we need, see for example
[
10
,
3
,
15
]
and the references therein.

(iii) Because the second derivative terms

and

are expected to explode as

, the generator of (

) is expected to become discontinuous as

. Thus, it is more convenient to use the equation

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}u(t,m)+\sup_{i,a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\hat{a}(i)}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}^{\top}D^{2}_{mm}u\left(t,m,[m],[m]\right){\mathcal{V}}_{a,i}+\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}D_{x}D_{m}u\left(t,m,[m]\right)e_{j^{c}}\right)

to obtain regret bounds. Indeed, any supersolution of (

) is clearly a supersolution of (
3.7
) and the generator of (
3.8
) is Lipschitz continuous on the derivatives of

. Thus, one can expect a simpler proof of comparison of viscosity solutions.

4.
Upper bound by smooth supersolution of the PDE

In this part, we design robust strategies of the forecaster using smooth supersolutions of (
3.7
). Note that (
3.7
) becomes simpler if
D^{2}_{mm}u=0
. This is the case if
u
is linear in
m
. The following Lemma uses this idea to generate simple supersolutions to (

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\phi
be a classical solution of

(4.1)

\begin{split}&0\geq\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}Tr\left(D^{2}_{xx}\phi\left(t,x\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)\\
&\phi(1,x)\geq\max_{i}x^{i},\,\,\,\phi(t,x+\lambda{\mathbf{1}})=\phi(t,x)+\lambda.\end{split}

Then, the function
\Phi:[0,1]\times{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{K})\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\Phi(t,m)=\phi(t,[m]):=\int\phi(t,x)\,m(dx)

is a smooth supersolution to (
3.7
)
with

(4.2)

D_{m}\Phi\left(t,m,x\right)=D_{x}\phi(t,x),\,\,D_{x}D_{m}\Phi\left(t,m,x\right)=D^{2}_{xx}\phi(t,x),\,\,D^{2}_{mm}\Phi\left(t,m,x,y\right)=0.

Proof.

Using (
4.2
) which can be easily verified, together with the supersolution property of
\phi
we have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\partial_{t}\phi(t,[m])+\frac{1}{2}\int\sup_{i,a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}Tr\left(D^{2}_{xx}\phi\left(t,x\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)dm(x)

\displaystyle\geq\partial_{t}\phi(t,[m])+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}Tr\left(D^{2}_{xx}\phi\left(t,[m]\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\partial_{t}\Phi(t,[m])+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{i,a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}Tr\left(D_{x}D_{m}\Phi\left(t,m,[m]\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\Phi_{i}(t,m)\,Tr\left(D_{x}D_{m}\Phi\left(t,m,[m]\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right),

where

denotes the

∎

Remark 4.1
.

It can be easily verified that smooth supersolutions of

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\sup_{a\in{\mathcal{P}}(\{0,1\}^{K})}\sum_{i}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\partial_{x^{i}}\phi(t,x)\,Tr\left(D^{2}_{xx}\phi\left(t,x\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)

We now show how we can use the Lemma

Fix a large time horizon
T
. Denote
\widetilde{m}:=m^{*\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}}

\displaystyle(\beta_{0}^{*},\dotso,\beta_{T-1}^{*})

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{*}(m):=D_{m}\Phi\left(t_{n},\tilde{m},\left[\widetilde{m}\right]\right).

Suppose that the initial belief is

\displaystyle v_{T}(m_{0})\leq\sup_{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[f([m_{T}])\right],

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\phi

\displaystyle|\partial^{2}_{tt}\phi(t,x)|\leq\frac{C}{(1-t)^{3/2}},\quad|\partial^{3}_{xxx}\phi(t,x)|+|\partial^{2}_{tx}\phi(t,x)|\leq\frac{C}{1-t},\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K},

for some positive constant

\beta^{*}

\sqrt{T}\phi(0,[\widetilde{m}_{0}])

Proof.

Our goal is to show that
\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[f([m_{T}])\right]-{\phi}\left(0,\left[\widetilde{m}_{0}\right]\right)\leq 0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[f([m_{T}])\right]-{\phi}\left(0,\left[\widetilde{m}_{0}\right]\right)=\sup_{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[f([\widetilde{m}_{T}])\right]-{\phi}\left(0,\left[\widetilde{m}_{0}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha}\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\left({\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[{\phi}(t_{n+1},[\widetilde{m}_{n+1}])\right]-{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[{\phi}(t_{n},[\widetilde{m}_{n}])\right]\right).

Conditioning on

, we have that

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},a}\left[{\phi}(t_{n+1},[\widetilde{m}_{n+1}])-\phi(t_{n},[\widetilde{m}_{n}])\,|\,\widetilde{m}_{n}=m\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\beta^{*}_{n}(m_{n})(i)\left(\hat{a}(i)\phi\left(t_{n+1},\left[A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}\right]\right)+\hat{a}(-i)\phi\left(t_{n+1},\left[A^{a,m}_{-i,\sqrt{T}}\right]\right)\right)-\phi(t_{n},[m]).

Using the linear structure of
\phi(t,[m])
, it can be seen that

\displaystyle\phi\left(t_{n+1},\left[A^{a,m}_{i,\sqrt{T}}\right]\right)-\phi(t_{n},[m])

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:i\in j}\frac{a(j)}{\hat{a}(i)}\int\left(\phi\left(t_{n+1},x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\phi(t_{n},x)\right)m(dx).

For any

, we have the equality

(4.7)

\displaystyle\phi\left(t_{n+1},x-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\phi(t_{n},x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\partial_{x}\phi(t_{n},x)+\frac{1}{T}\left(\partial_{t}\phi(t_{n},x)+\frac{1}{2}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}\partial^{2}_{xx}\phi(t_{n},x)e_{j^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\phi\left(t_{n}+\frac{s}{T},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{t}\phi(t_{n},x)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}\phi\left(t_{n}+\frac{s}{T},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{x}\phi\left(t_{n},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)ds

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)e_{j^{c}}^{\top}\left(\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi\left(t_{n},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi(t_{n},x)\right)e_{j^{c}}\,ds.

Using our assumption (
4.4
), we can estimate the last three terms in the equation above

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\phi\left(t_{n}+\frac{s}{T},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{t}\phi\left(t_{n},x\right)ds\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{1/T}\frac{\frac{1}{T}-s}{(1-t_{n}-s)^{3/2}}ds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}\phi\left(t_{n}+\frac{s}{T},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{x}\phi\left(t_{n},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)ds\right|\leq C\sqrt{T}\int_{0}^{1/T}\frac{\frac{1}{T}-s}{1-t_{n}-s}ds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)e_{j^{c}}^{\top}\left(\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi\left(t_{n},x-\frac{se_{j^{c}}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)-\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi(t_{n},x)\right)e_{j^{c}}\,ds\right|\leq\frac{C}{T^{3/2}(1-t_{n})}.

Let us define

(4.8)

O(T,n):=C\left(\int_{0}^{1/T}\frac{\frac{1}{T}-s}{(1-t_{n}-s)^{3/2}}ds+\sqrt{T}\int_{0}^{1/T}\frac{\frac{1}{T}-s}{1-t_{n}-s}ds+\frac{1}{T^{3/2}(1-t_{n})}\right).

Now plugging (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) into (
4.5
), we obtain that

(4.9)

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},a}\left[{\phi}(t_{n+1},[\widetilde{m}_{n+1}])-\phi(t_{n},[\widetilde{m}_{n}])\,|\,\widetilde{m}_{n}=m\right]\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{i}\beta^{*}_{n}(m_{n})(i)\left(\sum_{j,i\not\in j}a(j)e_{j}^{\top}-\sum_{j:i\in j}a(j)e_{j^{c}}^{\top}\right)\partial_{x}\phi(t_{n},[m])+\frac{1}{T}\times\\
&\left(\partial_{t}\phi(t_{n},[m])+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\beta_{n}^{*}(m_{n})(i)Tr\left(D^{2}_{xx}\phi\left(t_{n},[m]\right)\left(\sum_{j}{a(j)}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{i\in j}e_{j^{c}}e_{j^{c}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{1}}_{i\notin j}e_{j}e_{j}^{\top}\right)\right)\right)\right)\\
&+O(T,n).\end{split}

The first term on the right hand side vanishes due to our choice of
\beta^{*}
, the second term is non-positive due to the supersolution property of
\phi
, and thus we obtain that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},a}\left[{\phi}(t_{n+1},[\widetilde{m}_{n+1}])-\phi(t_{n},[\widetilde{m}_{n}])\,|\,\widetilde{m}_{n}=m\right]\leq O(T,n).

Summing up from
n=0
to
T-1
, taking supremum over
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, and letting
T\to\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sup_{\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}^{\beta^{*},\alpha}\left[f([m_{T}])\right]-{\phi}\left(0,\left[\widetilde{m}_{0}\right]\right)\leq\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}O(T,n)=0.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

Our main contribution in terms of regret bound is to extend the PDE based methodology of

]

\displaystyle 0
