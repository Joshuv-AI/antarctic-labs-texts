Active learning using region-based sampling
Sanjoy Dasgupta, Yoav Freund

Abstract
We present a general-purpose active learning scheme for data in metric spaces. The algorithm maintains a collection of neighborhoods of different sizes and uses label queries to identify those that have a strong bias towards one particular label; when two such neighborhoods intersect and have different labels, the region of overlap is treated as a “known unknown” and is a target of future active queries. We give label complexity bounds for this method that do not rely on assumptions about the data and we instantiate them in several cases of interest.



1
Introduction

In
active learning
, the starting point is a data set whose labels are hidden but can be obtained individually for a price. The goal is to label the data set, or to find a good classifier, at low cost.

We consider a formulation in which we have a collection of
n
points
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
that lie in a metric space
({\mathcal{X}},d)
. We can request the label of any of these points
x
, in which case we get a value
y\in\{-1,+1\}
with conditional distribution

\eta(x)\ =\ {\mathbb{E}}[y|x]\ =\ {\rm Pr}(y=1|x)-{\rm Pr}(y=-1|x)

for some unknown function
\eta:{\mathcal{X}}\to[0,1]
. The
Bayes-optimal
label for
x
, which we shall denote
g^{*}(x)
, is
-1
if
\eta(x)<0
and
+1
if
\eta(x)>0
; either label can be used if
\eta(x)=0
.
We wish to find the Bayes-optimal labels for the given data
X
.

More precisely, at the outset we have: the data
X
; two parameters
0<\gamma,\delta<1
; and a
query budget
, the number of label queries we can make. We want a procedure that chooses the next point, or batch of points, to query. This process will be applied repeatedly until the query budget is exhausted, whereupon labels
{\widehat{y}}(x)
must be provided for all
x\in X
, including those that were queried. Ideally, these will equal the Bayes-optimal labels
g^{*}(x)
, but we will only be judged on points
x\in X
with
|\eta(x)|\geq\gamma
. That is, the number of mistakes is taken to be:

\sum_{x\in X}{\bf 1}({\widehat{y}}(x)\neq g^{*}(x),\ |\eta(x)|\geq\gamma).

The overall procedure is allowed to fail with probability
\delta
, to account for sampling error.

1.1
Nonparametric active learning

In our setting there is no underlying assumption about
g^{*}
, for instance that it follows a linear model. Thus we adopt a nonparametric approach.

Existing proposals for nonparametric active learning can mostly be grouped according to the overall principle they follow: either (1) they seek to obtain the Bayes-optimal labels of a few well-positioned points, and then propagate these to the rest of the space
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
or (2) they estimate the biases (positive or negative) of entire regions at a time
[
3
,
8
]
. In this paper, we follow the second strategy because the only reliable and general-purpose way to assess the sign of an individual point—that is,
\mbox{sign}(\eta(x))
—is to query that point repeatedly; absent smoothness assumptions, the sign can change abruptly in an arbitrarily small neighborhood around
x
. On the other hand, the sign of a region
B
—that is, the sign of the average
\eta
value in
B
—is easy to determine, as long as
X
contains a reasonable number of points from that region.

1.2
Three key challenges

The overall active learning strategy is to define a large collection of regions, or
neighborhoods
,
{\mathcal{B}}
, of varying sizes, and to use label queries to estimate the signs associated with these regions. Ultimately we hope to cover
{\mathcal{X}}
by a patchwork of neighborhoods with
\eta(x)
values of uniform sign. Without strong smoothness conditions, these neighborhoods could be of very different sizes. The most beneficial for active learning are neighborhoods that contain a lot of
X
, but to get good coverage we might have to include smaller neighborhoods. To address this heterogeneity, we group the regions
{\mathcal{B}}
by size. We begin by estimating the signs of the largest of them, and then move on to progressively smaller neighborhoods as the need arises. When the label budget runs out, the process is stopped, and provisional labels are assigned to the individual points in
X
.

In giving shape to this scheme, there are three key challenges to be addressed.

The first challenge is deciding
where to query
. Suppose that a particular region
B\subset{\mathcal{X}}
has an average
\eta
-value close to zero. Earlier work has typically taken this as a sign that
B
is part of the “uncertainty” region and should be queried further. But it is important to distinguish two cases: (1) the
\eta
values are close to zero throughout
B
, and (2)
B
consists of two sub-regions, one of which is strongly positive while the other is strongly negative. In the first case, there is little merit in querying further. But in the second case, there is a lot to be gained.

To distinguish these two cases, we use a collection of neighborhoods that are
overlapping
. For instance, we might take
{\mathcal{B}}
to be
all
balls in
{\mathcal{X}}
, which is effectively a finite collection once the given data points
X
are taken into account. Case (2) can then be detected: we think of a point
x
as being in the uncertainty region if it is contained in a neighborhood
B
that is strongly positive as well as being in a neighborhood
B^{\prime}
that is strongly negative. Such points are “known unknowns”, and these are the targets of our active querying.

A second challenge is that the sign of
\eta(\cdot)
can change in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of any given point
x
. Thus, if we look at successively smaller neighborhoods containing
x
, say
B_{1}\supset B_{2}\supset B_{3}\supset\cdots
, the labels of these sets may keep changing. At first, when we are querying neighborhoods of the size of
B_{1}
, we might think
x
has label
+1
. When we move to neighborhoods of the size of
B_{2}
, this could change to
-1
. And then
+1
, and so on. We call these
mind changes
.

What this means is that we can
never
be sure of having correctly determined the label of
x
. Thus in addition to focused (active) querying, we also do background sampling of the entire space to pick up on possible mind changes. For simplicity, we make one background query per focused query.

The third challenge is
managing sampling
of overlapping neighborhoods. Recall that we are interested in detecting points, and thus neighborhoods, whose
\eta
-values are either
>\gamma
or
<-\gamma
. This suggests querying
k\approx 1/\gamma^{2}
points at random from each region. Now, suppose we have queried this many points from neighborhood
B
and then later want to query points from a different neighborhood
B^{\prime}
that overlaps
B
. How can we reuse the queries we have already made in
B\cap B^{\prime}
?

Poisson sampling
provides a clean solution. Rather than choosing
k
points at random from
B
, we pick each point in
B
with probability (roughly)
k/|B\cap X|
, independently. The specific way we implement this is to assign each
x\in X
a uniform-random value
T_{x}\in[0,1]
, at the outset. When sampling
B
, we choose to query
x
if
T_{x}\leq k/|B\cap X|
. And when it comes time to sample a different
B^{\prime}
that also contains
x
, we choose
x
if
T_{x}\leq k/|B^{\prime}\cap X|
; if its label has already been obtained, we are able to reuse it. In this way, the random querying of overlapping regions is seamlessly managed.

These three challenges go beyond earlier work in active learning, which was able to avoid problems like mind-changes by making smoothness assumptions on
\eta
. By tackling all three of them, we are able to give a general-purpose active learning scheme.

1.3
Results

In Section
2
, we present our active learning algorithm, and in Section
3
, we analyze it in generality, taking
X
to be an arbitrary point-set in the metric space and allowing any
\eta
function. We identify two
critical levels
for any
x\in X
: two scales (of neighborhood sizes) that control how many queries are sufficient for
x
to be correctly labeled (Theorem
7
). We instantiate these bounds in canonical one-dimensional settings (Theorems
26
and
8
) to get label complexities logarithmic in
|X|
.

In Section
4
, we consider the statistical setting where
X
is drawn from an underlying distribution
\mu
on
{\mathcal{X}}
. We give rates of convergence under distributional conditions (Theorem
15
), and instantiate these under assumptions that are common in learning theory and computational geometry.

1.4
Related work

There is a small body of work on the theory of nonparametric active learning. The early results of
[
1
]
established upper and lower bounds on label complexity in situations where the Bayes-optimal boundary is of a simple form: a single threshold for one-dimensional data or a “smooth boundary fragment” in higher dimension.

An algorithm for active learning based on hierarchical sampling was given by
[
3
]
and was analyzed under smoothness conditions by
[
7
]
. In these works, the idea is to begin with a hierarchical clustering of
X
, and to then use queries to discover a pruning of this tree whose leaf-clusters are almost-pure in their labels. The method is not well-suited to situations with significant noise levels. Another approach using dyadic partitions was given by
[
8
]
and analyzed under commonly-used smoothness, margin, and density assumptions—namely, that
\eta
is Holder-smooth, the fraction of points with
|\eta(x)|\leq t
is some polynomial in
t
, and the marginal density is close to uniform—along with an additional smoothness requirement on
\eta
.

A different strategy using nearest neighbors was explored by
[
6
]
. Their idea was to choose an appropriate scale
s
, find an
s
-cover of
X
, estimate the Bayes-optimal label for each point in this cover by querying its neighbors, and then use these cleanly-labeled points for 1-nearest neighbor classification. A somewhat more general approach was given by
[
5
]
and studied under the usual smoothness, margin, and density conditions, with resulting rates of convergence comparable to those found by
[
8
]
.

Finally,
[
2
]
suggested a graph-based method for active learning based on adaptively looking for the cut in the graph corresponding to the correct decision boundary. Their assumptions are based on properties of this cut and are not easily comparable with earlier work.

2
The active learning algorithm

Before giving a high-level overview of our algorithm, we recall some basic notation. We have a collection of points
X
lying in a metric space
({\mathcal{X}},d)
. The label of any
x\in X
can be requested and the value returned will be either
-1
or
+1
, according to the conditional probability function
\eta(x)={\mathbb{E}}(y|x)
. We wish to assign Bayes-optimal labels to all points in
X
with
|\eta(x)|\geq\gamma
.

2.1
A collection of sampling regions

In our active learning algorithm, sampling is organized around a collection
{\mathcal{B}}
of subsets of
{\mathcal{X}}
. These are the atomic sets on which we assess label bias and we call them “balls” or “neighborhoods”. There are no formal requirements on
{\mathcal{B}}
—for instance, the so-called balls can be of arbitrary shape—but the intention is that each
x\in X
is contained in balls of multiple sizes, including one that is so small as to exclude the rest of
X
.

For any ball
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
, let
X_{B}=X\cap B
be a shorthand for the data points that lie in it.
We group balls into levels by the number of points they contain. We put
B
at
level

\ell\geq 0
if

\frac{n}{2^{\ell+1}}\leq|X_{B}|<\frac{n}{2^{\ell}}.

(1)

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
consist of all balls in
{\mathcal{B}}
that are at level
\ell
. Thus
{\mathcal{B}}_{0},{\mathcal{B}}_{1},\ldots
is a partition of
{\mathcal{B}}
, with
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
consisting of highly-populated balls and subsequent
{\mathcal{B}}_{1},{\mathcal{B}}_{2},\ldots
consisting of successively smaller balls. We will use
{\mathcal{B}}_{\geq\ell}
to denote all balls at levels
\ell
or greater, and likewise
{\mathcal{B}}_{>\ell}
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}
, and so on.

Balls in lower levels contain more points, and thus their biases (average
\eta
values) are easier to estimate. Our sampling algorithm makes its way from easier to harder levels.

For any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, let
{\mathcal{B}}(x)\subset{\mathcal{B}}
denote the collection of balls that contain
x
and can thus be used in determining
x
’s label. We again partition these balls by sampling-level, so that
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)={\mathcal{B}}(x)\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
.

2.2
Estimating bias

We use label-queries to estimate the biases (average
\eta
values) of balls
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
. These are in turn used to estimate the labels of individual points.

The bias of a ball
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
is defined as

\eta_{X}(B)=\mbox{average}\{\eta(x):x\in X_{B}\}.

Rather than working with a numerical estimate, we assign each ball a
qualitative
bias estimate,

{\widehat{y}}(B)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}+1&\mbox{significant $+$ bias}\\
-1&\mbox{significant $-$ bias}\\
0&\mbox{no significant bias}\\
\bot&\mbox{not yet available}\end{array}\right.

The option
\bot
is used until sufficiently many points in
X_{B}
have been queried: the required number is
k=O(1/\gamma^{2})
, recalling that
\gamma
is the smallest bias that needs to be detected. Once this many labels are available,
{\widehat{y}}(B)
is set to a value in
\{-1,0,+1\}
and remains fixed thereafter. These bias estimates will with high probability be seen to satisfy the following guarantee.

Definition 1

For any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
, bias estimate
{\widehat{y}}(B)\in\{+1,-1,0\}
is
\gamma
-accurate
if:

•

{\widehat{y}}(B)=+1\implies\eta_{X}(B)>0

•

{\widehat{y}}(B)=-1\implies\eta_{X}(B)<0

•

{\widehat{y}}(B)=0\implies|\eta_{X}(B)|<\gamma

Pick any point
x
and any level
\ell
. Once qualitative bias estimates are available for all balls
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)
, the set of possible labels for
x
at level
\ell
, denoted
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)\subset\{-1,+1\}
, is defined thus:

•

{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
contains
+1
if there exists a
minimal
ball
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
(that is, which has no other
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
strictly contained with it) with
{\widehat{y}}(B)=+1
.

•

{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
contains
-1
under a symmetrical condition.

This is spelled out precisely in Equation (
2
) in in Figure
2
. The label-estimate for
x
at level
\ell
, denoted
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)
, is
+1
if
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)=\{+1\}
,
-1
if
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)=\{-1\}
,
0
if
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)=\{\}
, and
!
if
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)=\{-1,+1\}
(see Equation (
3

The label “!” can be interpreted as “known unknown”
[
10
]
or as “conflicting evidence”. Our active learning algorithm makes all of its
focused queries
in balls that contain known unknowns.

3
Analysis of algorithm: finite population setting

We now analyze the active learning procedure in a setting where
X\subset{\mathcal{X}}
is an arbitrary set of
n
points; that is, we make no distributional assumption on the manner in which
X
is generated.

3.1
Accuracy of bias estimates

Fix the set of balls
{\mathcal{B}}
and let
0<\delta<1
be a
predefined confidence parameter. We start with a uniform guarantee on the
bias estimates for all balls
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

In Figure
3
, we see that the query region for any ball
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
is
\{z\in X_{B}:T_{z}\leq\tau_{\ell}\}
, which from the definition of
\tau_{\ell}
has size
O(k)
. This is the number of queries we make to
B
before estimating its qualitative bias. Since we need to detect biases of magnitude greater than
\gamma
, we would expect
k
to be proportional to
1/\gamma^{2}
. This intuition is borne out by the following result, proved in the appendix.

Lemma 2

Suppose that
k\geq(192/\gamma^{2})\ln(4|{\mathcal{B}}|/\delta)
.
Let
{\widehat{y}}(B)
be defined as in Figure
4
. Then with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, all the
{\widehat{y}}(B)
, for
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
, are
\gamma
-accurate in the sense of Definition
1
.

In what follows, we will assume that
{\widehat{y}}(B)
is
\gamma
-accurate for all
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

3.2
Critical levels

The label assigned to a data point
x
at level
\ell
, denoted
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)
, can change as
\ell
increases; it may flip between
+1
and
-1
, with stretches of
0
or
!
in between. This
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)
is governed by
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)\subset\{-1,+1\}
, the “possible labels” for
x
given the information from balls at levels
0
through
\ell
. The value of
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
depends upon the random choices of the querying algorithm, but it is nonetheless possible to define two critical levels for each
x
: a level
L_{1}(x)
by which
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
will reliably contain the correct label of
x
, and a level
L_{2}(x)
by which
{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
will reliably omit the wrong label.

Definition 3
(Critical levels
L_{1},L_{2}
)

Pick any
x\in X
with
\eta(x)\neq 0
and let
s(x)={\rm sign}(\eta(x))
be its Bayes-optimal label. We define
L_{1}(x)
to be the smallest level
\ell
such that:

•

There exists some
B_{o}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)
with
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B_{o})\geq\gamma
.

•

Any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}(x)
with
X_{B}\subset X_{B_{o}}
also has
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B)\geq\gamma
.

We define
L_{2}(x)
to be the smallest level
\ell
such that:

•

For all
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\geq\ell}(x)
, we have
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B)\geq 0
.

•

For any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
with
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B)<0
, there exists
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
with
X_{B^{\prime}}\subset X_{B}
and
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B^{\prime})\geq 0
.

Take
L_{1}(x)
or
L_{2}(x)
to be
\infty
if no level meets the requirements.

The significance of these definitions is made clear by the following lemma.

Lemma 4

Pick any
x\in X
with
\eta(x)\neq 0
and let
s(x)\in\{+1,-1\}
denote its Bayes-optimal label. Then for any level
\ell
and any time at which
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)\neq\bot
:

(a)

If
\ell\geq L_{1}(x)
, then
s(x)\in{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
and thus
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)\in\{s(x),!\}
.

(b)

If
\ell\geq L_{2}(x)
, then
-s(x)\not\in{\mbox{\rm PL}}_{\ell}(x)
and thus
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)\in\{s(x),0\}
.

3.3
Boundary sets and label complexity

A common intuition about active learning is that successive queries gradually constrain the possible locations of the decision boundary. Let’s consider the state of affairs when all balls at level
\leq\ell-1
have been sampled. The “known unknowns” at level
\ell
are points
x
with
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell-1}(x)=\,!
; by Lemma
4
(b), such points have
L_{2}(x)\geq\ell
. Focused sampling at level
\ell
will be restricted to balls that contain these points. We can think of this region as the
boundary set
at level
\ell
.

Definition 5
(Boundary set
\bf\Delta_{\ell}
)

For any level
\ell
, define the
boundary set
at level
\ell
to be

\Delta_{\ell}=\bigcup_{x\in X:L_{2}(x)\geq\ell}\bigcup_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)}X_{B}.

(5)

Lemma 6

All focused samples at level
\ell
lie in
\{z\in\Delta_{\ell}:T_{z}\leq\tau_{\ell}\}
.

We can now give generic label complexity bounds in terms of
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, and
\Delta_{\ell}
. The bounds come in two equivalent forms: a
global
version that specifies what parts of
X
are correctly labeled after
m
queries and a
local
version that specifies the number of queries after which a particular

4
Analysis of algorithm: distributional setting

We now turn to a setting where the points
X
are sampled from a distribution
\mu
on
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The algorithm is unchanged, but we seek to bound label complexity in terms of properties of
\mu
and
\eta
.

For simplicity, we focus on the case where
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and thus admits a density. We take
{\mathcal{B}}
to consist of all open balls
B(x,r)=\{z:\|z-x\|<r\}
centered in
{\mathcal{X}}
, with
{\mathcal{B}}(x)
being balls that contain
x
.

4.1
A generic bound based on a probabilistic notion of distance

Our analysis rests upon a notion of distance based on the distribution
\mu
. We take the distance from a point
x
to a set
S
to be the probability mass of the smallest ball that contains
x
and touches
S
.

Definition 9

For any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
S\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, define

{\rm dist}(x,S)=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{B}}(x),B\cap S\neq\emptyset\}.

We will see that
L_{2}(x)
can be bounded in terms of such distances. In what follows, for
s\in\{-1,0,+1\}
, take
{\mathcal{X}}^{s}
to denote
\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:{\rm sign}(\eta(x))=s\}
.

Lemma 10

For any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
with
\eta(x)\neq 0
, let
s(x)={\rm sign}(\eta(x))
. Then

L_{2}(x)\leq\bigg\lceil\lg\frac{1}{{\rm dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)})}\bigg\rceil+1.

We will also use this probabilistic distance to define notions of boundary. We take the
p
-boundary to consist of points that are at distance
\leq p
from points of the opposite label.

Definition 11

For any
p>0
, define
\partial_{p}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:{\rm dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)})\leq p\}
.

Notice that this includes all
x
with
\eta(x)=0
.

The
(p,q)
-boundary consists of points at distance
\leq q
from the
p
-boundary.

Definition 12

For any
p,q>0
, define
\partial_{p,q}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:{\rm dist}(x,\partial_{p})\leq q\}
.

We can bound
\Delta_{\ell}
, the query region at level
\ell
, in terms of this second-order boundary.

Lemma 13

For any
\ell\geq 0
, we have
\Delta_{\ell}\subset\partial_{4/2^{\ell},2/2^{\ell}}
.

Theorem
7
thus continues to hold, but with
n\cdot\mu(\partial_{4/2^{\ell},2/2^{\ell}})
in place of
|\Delta_{\ell}|
; see Theorem
30
in the Appendix.

4.2
Bounds under three assumptions

In order to get concrete label complexity bounds in the distributional setting, we need to be able to bound the levels
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
, as well as the probability masses of boundary sets
\partial_{p,q}
. To do this, we introduce three assumptions.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:|\eta(x)|\geq\gamma\}
; thus
X\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
is the set of points on which our labels will be judged. We further divide this set by label: for
s\in\{-1,+1\}
, let
{\mathcal{X}}^{s}_{\gamma}=\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:s\cdot\eta(x)\geq\gamma\}
.

(A1)

There is an absolute constant
p_{o}>0
for which the following holds: for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
, there exists
B\in{\mathcal{B}}(x)
such that
B\subset{\mathcal{X}}^{s(x)}_{\gamma}
and
\mu(B)\geq p_{o}
.

As we will see, this assumption holds if the decision regions have bounded curvature. It allows us to bound
L_{1}(x)
easily.

Lemma 14

Under (A1), every
x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
has
L_{1}(x)\leq\lceil\lg(1/p_{o})\rceil
.

The second assumption is a variant of the Tsybakov margin condition.

(A2)

There are absolute constants
C>0
and
0<\sigma<1
for which the following holds: for any
p>0
, we have
\mu(\partial_{p,p})\leq Cp^{\sigma}
.

If, for instance,
\mu
were the uniform distribution over
[0,1]^{d}
, we would expect
\sigma\sim 1/d
.
The third assumption is similar in spirit, but for points of bias
\geq\gamma
. There are two options: (A3) or (A3’).

(A3)

There is an absolute constant
p_{1}>0
such that
\mu(\partial_{p}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})=0
for any
p<p_{1}
.

(A3’)

There are constants
C^{\prime}>0
and
0<\xi<1
such that
\mu(\partial_{p}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})\leq C^{\prime}p^{\xi}
for any
p>0
.

Under these assumptions, we can give concrete label complexity bounds.

Theorem 15

Assume (A1), (A2) and either (A3) or (A3’). There is an absolute constant
C^{\prime\prime}
for which the following holds. Pick
0<\delta<1
and take
k=O(((d\log n)+\log(1/\delta))/\gamma^{2})
. If the algorithm of Figure
2
makes
m
queries, for

\frac{128k}{p_{o}}\ \leq\ m\ \leq\ \frac{64C\cdot 8^{\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\,n^{1-\sigma}k^{\sigma},

then with probability at least
1-\delta
:

•

Under (A3), all of
X\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
get Bayes-optimal labels for
m>(512C/(1-\sigma))\cdot(1/p_{1})^{1-\sigma}\cdot k
.

•

Under (A3’), Bayes-optimal labels get assigned to all but
C^{\prime\prime}(k/m)^{\xi/(1-\sigma)}+(2/n)\log(4/\delta)

fraction of
X\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
.

Next, we’ll see two settings, here and in the Appendix, in which (A1)-(A3) follow from more commonplace assumptions in nonparametric estimation and computational geometry.

4.3
Example: Curvature and Massart noise

Consider the following two conditions. The first says that the distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}
is close (within a multiplicative factor) to uniform, while the second says that all of
{\mathcal{X}}
has bias
\geq\gamma
except for a
(d-1)
-dimensional decision surface of bounded curvature.

(C1)

[Strong density condition] Distribution
\mu
on
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
admits a density that is bounded below and above: there exist constants
c_{o},c_{1}>0
such that for all balls
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

c_{o}{\rm vol}(B)\leq\mu(B)\leq c_{1}{\rm vol}(B),

where
{\rm vol}(\cdot)
is
d
-dimensional volume.

(C2)

[Massart noise and boundary condition]
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{-}\cup{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
, where
{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
separates (intersects any line between)
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{-}
, and is a
(d-1)
-dimensional Riemannian submanifold of reach
r_{o}>0
.

The
reach
condition says that any point at Euclidean distance
<r_{o}
of
{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
has a unique nearest neighbor on the separator. It is a commonly-used notion of curvature in the computational geometry literature
[
4
,
9
]
and implies, for instance, that
{\mathcal{X}}^{+}
(resp.,
{\mathcal{X}}^{-}
) can be covered by open balls of radius
r_{o}
that do not touch
{\mathcal{X}}^{-}\cup{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
(resp.,
{\mathcal{X}}^{+}\cup{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
).

Lemma 16

Conditions (C1) and (C2) yield assumptions (A1), (A2), and (A3), with
p_{o}=r_{o}^{d}\cdot c_{o}\cdot v_{d}
, where
v_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\sigma=\xi=1/d
.

Theorem
15
then implies a convergence rate of
1/m^{1/(d-1)}
after
m
queries. This is an improvement over the usual
1/m^{1/d}
rate for random querying and has been observed previously in similar settings
[
1
]
.

A.1
Large deviation bounds for the discrete setting

Lemma 17

Fix a confidence parameter
0<\delta<1
and a positive integer
k\geq 6\ln(4/\delta)
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be any collection of points. Suppose that the labels
Y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
of these points are independent, with
{\mathbb{E}}Y_{i}=\eta(x_{i})
. Define

\eta_{o}=\frac{1}{m}\left(\eta(x_{1})+\cdots+\eta(x_{m})\right).

Now consider the following estimator
Z
of this quantity:

•

Each
x_{i}
is chosen with probability
q>0
, independently. Let
N
be the number of selected points.

•

If
N>0
, the labels
Y_{i}
of the selected points are obtained, and
Z
is their average.

If
qm\geq k+\sqrt{6k\ln(4/\delta)}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

(a)

N\geq k
, and

(b)

|Z-\eta_{o}|<\sqrt{(48/k)\ln(4/\delta)}
.

Proof:

Let’s start with (a). Define
c=\sqrt{3\ln(4/\delta)}
. We’ll take
qm=k+\sqrt{6k\ln(4/\delta)}=k+c\sqrt{2k}
since this is the worst case. By assumption,
k\geq 2c^{2}
and thus
qm\leq 2k
.

Now,
N
has a binomial(
m,q
) distribution. By a multiplicative Chernoff bound, for
0<\epsilon<1
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}(N\geq qm(1+\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq e^{-qm\epsilon^{2}/3}

\displaystyle{\rm Pr}(N\leq qm(1-\epsilon))

\displaystyle\leq e^{-qm\epsilon^{2}/2}

Take
\epsilon=c/\sqrt{qm}
; by the lower bound on
k
, we have
\epsilon\leq 1/2
. Recalling the choice of
c
, we see that with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, we get

(1-\epsilon)qm<N<(1+\epsilon)qm.

The lower bound implies
N>qm(1-\epsilon)=qm-c\sqrt{qm}=k+c\sqrt{2k}-c\sqrt{qm}\geq k
.

For (b), define
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\in\{0,1\}
as random variables indicating whether the corresponding points were selected; that is,
C_{i}={\bf 1}(\mbox{$x_{i}$ was selected})
. The sum of the obtained labels is then
S=C_{1}Y_{1}+\cdots+C_{m}Y_{m}
. Notice that these
C_{i}Y_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
are independent with
{\mathbb{E}}[C_{i}Y_{i}]=q\eta(x_{i})
and
{\mathbb{E}}[(C_{i}Y_{i})^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}[C_{i}]=q
. Thus their sum
S
has expectation

{\mathbb{E}}[S]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}[C_{i}Y_{i}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q\eta(x_{i})=qm\eta_{o}

and variance

\mbox{var}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mbox{var}(C_{i}Y_{i})\leq qm.

We can bound the concentration of
S
around its expected value using Bernstein’s inequality, by which

{\rm Pr}(|S-{\mathbb{E}}[S]|\geq t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2(\mbox{var}(S)+t/3)}\right).

Using
t=\epsilon qm
, we then have that
|S-qm\eta_{o}|\leq\epsilon qm
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
.

Combining with the high-probability bound on
N
above, we get

\frac{qm\eta_{o}-\epsilon qm}{qm(1+\epsilon)}<\frac{S}{N}<\frac{qm\eta_{o}+\epsilon qm}{qm(1-\epsilon)},

whereupon (recalling
Z=S/N
)

\eta_{o}\left(\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}-1\right)-\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}<Z-\eta_{o}<\eta_{o}\left(\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}-1\right)+\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}.

Since
|\eta_{o}|\leq 1
,

|Z-\eta_{o}|<\max\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon},\frac{2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\right)\leq 4\epsilon,

as claimed.

\Box

Of the

Now, for
\ell_{1}
as defined, we have that
\tau_{\ell_{1}}\leq m/8n
and thus from Lemma

m/4

are set.

It then follows from Lemma

(a) that the following hold for any

(a)

By the end of phase one,
{\widehat{y}}_{\ell_{1}}(x)\in\{g^{*}(x),!\}
.

(b)

For any

{\widehat{y}}_{\ell}(x)
becomes available, it lies in
\{g^{*}(x),!\}
.

(c)

If

during phase two, then its final label as defined in (
4

{\widehat{y}}(x)=g^{*}(x)
.

Now let’s move on to phase two. Let
A=\{x\in X:L_{1}(x)\leq\ell_{1},L_{2}(x)\leq\ell_{2}\}
. We will show that every point in
A
must leave the uncertainty region

We will break the argument into two cases.

Case 1:
Fewer than

focused queries are made in phase two. This can only happen if some round of sampling has an empty uncertainty set, meaning that all of

Case 2:
A full

\leq\ell_{1}
and by Lemma

\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}+1}^{\ell_{2}}|\{z\in\Delta_{\ell}:T_{z}\leq\tau_{\ell}\}|\ \leq\ \sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}+1}^{\ell_{2}}2|\Delta_{\ell}|\tau_{\ell}\ <\ \frac{m}{4},

where the first inequality is from Lemma

\Box

One-dimensional monotonic

\Box

We can now bound the size of the query region at each level and find that it shrinks exponentially with

on either side of
\lambda
.
\Box

We are now ready for the proof of Theorem

Setting

to
O((1/\gamma^{2})\ln(n/\delta))

A.7
One-dimensional data with Massart noise

We now turn to another one-dimensional setting. Once again,
X
consists of
n
arbitrarily-placed points in
{\mathcal{X}}=[0,1]
. This time, however, they are labeled according to a conditional probability function
\eta:{\mathcal{X}}\to[-1,1]
that satisfies the Massart noise condition:

•

There are
p
disjoint open intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{p}
, such that
{\mathcal{X}}
is (the closure of) their union, and

•

for each
j
, either
\eta(x)>\gamma
for all
x\in I_{j}
or
\eta(x)<-\gamma
for all
x\in I_{j}
. In the first case, we write
s(I_{j})=+1
and in the second case,
s(I_{j})=-1
.

Here
0<\gamma<1
is some constant. See Figure
5
(b) for an illustrative example. For concreteness, the intervals
I_{j}
can be written in the form
(\lambda_{j-1},\lambda_{j})
, where

0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{p-1}<\lambda_{p}=1.

Here
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p-1}
are the
boundary points
between intervals.

We will take
{\mathcal{B}}
to consist of all open intervals of
[0,1]
, with
{\mathcal{B}}(x)
denoting intervals that contain point
x
.

We begin with bounds on the
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
levels for each point.

Lemma 24

Pick any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
; suppose
x\in I_{j}
.

(a)

Let
n_{j}=|X\cap I_{j}|
. Then
L_{1}(x)\leq\lg(n/n_{j})
.

(b)

Let
r(x)
be the minimum number of data points that lie between
x
and a boundary point, counting
x
as well; this is either the number of points in the left-interval
(\lambda_{j-1},x]
(if
j>1
) or the right-interval
[x,\lambda_{j})
(if
j<p
), whichever is smaller. Then
L_{2}(x)\leq\lg(n/r(x))
.

Proof:

For (a), notice first that
I_{j}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)
for
\ell=\lceil(\lg n/n_{j})-1\rceil
. Moreover,
s(I_{j})\cdot\eta_{X}(I_{j})>\gamma
. Thus
I_{j}
belongs to
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)
and is strongly biased towards the correct label. This strong bias also holds for any subset of
I_{j}
.

For (b), consider any
\ell\geq\lg(n/r(x))
. Any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}(x)
with
\mbox{sign}(\eta_{X}(B))\neq s(I_{j})
must contain either the entire left-interval
(\lambda_{j-1},x]
or the entire right-interval
[x,\lambda_{j})
, and thus has at least
r(x)
points, which means that it is too large to be in
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
. Thus all intervals
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\geq\ell}(x)
have
s(I_{j})\cdot\eta_{X}(B)>0
. Also, for any interval
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{<\ell}(x)
there is some
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)
that is strictly contained within it.

\Box

With
L_{1}(x)
and
L_{2}(x)
under control, it is easy to bound the size of the focused query region
\Delta_{\ell}
at each level.

Lemma 25

For any
\ell\geq 0
, let
\Delta_{\ell}
denote the focused querying region at level
\ell
, as defined in (
5
). Then
|\Delta_{\ell}|\leq(p-1)n/2^{\ell-2}
.

Proof:

We have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{x\in X:L_{2}(x)\geq\ell}\bigcup_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)}(B\cap X)

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{x\in X:r(x)\leq n/2^{\ell}}\bigcup_{B\ni x:|B\cap X|<n/2^{\ell}}(B\cap X).

This includes at most
n/2^{\ell-1}
points from
X
on either side of each boundary point
\lambda_{j}
.

\Box

Notice that
|\Delta_{\ell}|
shrinks exponentially with
\ell
. With
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, and
|\Delta_{\ell}|
values in place, Theorem
7
can be applied directly to give the following label complexity bound.

Theorem 26

Pick any
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
. Suppose we run the algorithm of Figure
2
with
k=O((1/\gamma^{2})\ln(n/\delta))
. With probability at least
1-\delta
, after making

O\left(\frac{p-1}{\gamma^{2}}\ln\frac{p-1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{n}{\delta}\right)

queries, the algorithm will assign the correct label
g^{*}(x)
to at least
1-\epsilon
fraction of
X
, except possibly the
2k
points of either side of each boundary point.

Proof:

Setting
k
to
O((1/\gamma^{2})\ln(n/\delta))
satisfies the requirements of Theorem
7
. Here we are using the fact that although
{\mathcal{B}}
is infinite, we need only consider
O(n^{2})
distinct intervals since
|X|=n
.

Next, using Lemma
25
, we have that for any integers
0\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}
,

\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}+1}^{\ell_{2}}|\Delta_{\ell}|\tau_{\ell}\ \leq\ \sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}+1}^{\ell_{2}}\frac{(p-1)n}{2^{\ell-2}}\cdot\frac{2^{\ell+2}k}{n}\ =\ 16(p-1)k(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}).

to conclude that

with

\displaystyle\ell_{2}

Using Lemma

Appendix B
Technicalities: distributional setting

In the distributional setting,
X
is drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mu
on
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We assume
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and thus admits a density. Take
{\mathcal{B}}
to consist of all open balls
B(x,r)=\{z:\|z-x\|<r\}
, with
{\mathcal{B}}(x)
being balls that contain
x
.

B.1
Sampling level and probability mass

To begin with, we relate the number of points in a ball
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
(and thus the level of the ball) to its probability mass under the marginal distribution.

Lemma 27

With probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
n\mu(B)\geq 12\ln(2|{\mathcal{B}}|/\delta)
, we have

\frac{\mu(B)}{2}\leq\frac{|X_{B}|}{n}\leq 2\mu(B).

Proof:

It is an immediate consequence of the multiplicative Chernoff bound that with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

\frac{|X_{B}|}{n}=\mu(B)\left(1\pm\sqrt{\frac{3}{n\mu(B)}\ln\frac{2|{\mathcal{B}}|}{\delta}}\right).

\Box

Henceforth assume that this high-probability event holds. Next, we will see that balls of probability mass
p
belong to level
\ell\approx\lg(1/p)
.

Lemma 28

For any level
\ell\geq 0
and any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}
,

\left\lceil\lg\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\right\rceil-2\leq\ell\leq\left\lceil\lg\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\right\rceil.

Proof:

Recall the definition of level
\ell
:

B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \frac{n}{2^{\ell+1}}\leq|X_{B}|<\frac{n}{2^{\ell}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 2^{\ell}<\frac{n}{|X_{B}|}\leq 2^{\ell+1}.

By Lemma
27
,

\frac{1}{2\mu(B)}\leq\frac{n}{|X_{B}|}\leq\frac{2}{\mu(B)}.

Thus we must have
2^{\ell}<2/\mu(B)
and
1/(2\mu(B))\leq 2^{\ell+1}
. These translate into the stated bounds on
\ell
.

\Box

B.2
Bounding
L_{2}
using probabilistic distance: Proof of Lemma
10

Let
p={\rm dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)})
. If
p=0
, the statement is vacuous, so assume
p>0
.

Consider
\ell=\lceil\lg(1/p)\rceil+1
. For any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\geq\ell}(x)
, we have
\mu(B)\leq 2|X_{B}|/n<2/2^{\ell}\leq p
, using Lemma

, the definition of sampling levels, and the definition of
\ell
, in that order. It follows that
B
does not intersect
{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)}
, whereupon
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B)\geq 0
.

Next, pick any
B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
with
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B)<0
. Thus
B
must intersect
{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)}
and has probability mass
\geq p
. We will show that there exists
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\leq\ell}(x)
such that
B^{\prime}\subset B
and
B^{\prime}
does not intersect
{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)}
; whereupon
s(x)\cdot\eta_{X}(B^{\prime})\geq 0
. Indeed, take
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}(x)
to be a subset of

of
\mu

p-\epsilon
for some very small

; we can do this because of the absolute continuity of
\mu
. Then
B^{\prime}
does not touch
{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)}
and by Lemma
28
, for small enough

, it lies at level
\leq\ell
.

B.3
Uncertainty region in the distributional setting: Proof of Lemma
13

Recall from (
5
) that

\Delta_{\ell}=\bigcup_{x\in X:L_{2}(x)\geq\ell}\bigcup_{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell}(x)}X_{B}.

Consider any

x\in{\mathcal{X}}

B.4
A generic label complexity bound in the distributional setting

We will need to relate the size of the query region
\Delta_{\ell}
to the probability mass of the corresponding second-order boundary. For this, we provide an analog of Lemma
20
for the distributional setting.

Lemma 29

With probability at least
1-2(\lg(n/k))e^{-k/3}

(a)

.

(b)

Proof:

Part (a) is as in Lemma
20
.

For (b), pick any subset
S\subset{\mathcal{X}}
. If
X
consists of
n
independent draws from
\mu
, then
|\{x\in S:T_{x}\leq\tau_{\ell}\}|
has a
\mbox{binomial}(n,\mu(S)\tau_{\ell})
distribution with expectation
n\mu(S)\tau_{\ell}

e^{-n\mu(S)\tau_{\ell}/3}
. We will apply this to the various sets

and take a union bound over them. In each application, we will also see that

\Box

The proof is identical to that of Theorem

\left\lceil\lg\frac{1}{{\rm dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)})}\right\rceil+1>\ell_{2}\ \Longrightarrow\ {\rm dist}(x,{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)})<\frac{4}{2^{\ell_{2}}}\leq\left(\frac{512C}{1-\sigma}\right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}.

Call this quantity
p
; thus any such
x
lies in
\partial_{p}
.

Under (A3),
\partial_{p}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
has zero probability mass for
p<p_{1}
, that is, if

\left(\frac{512C}{1-\sigma}\right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}<p_{1}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ m>\frac{512C}{1-\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{p_{1}^{1-\sigma}}\cdot k.

Under (A3’),
\mu(\partial_{p}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})\leq C^{\prime}p^{\xi}
; we can then apply a Bernstein bound to assert that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|X\cap(\partial_{p}\cap{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma})|\ \leq\ \frac{3}{2}C^{\prime}np^{\xi}+2\log\frac{1}{\delta},

from which the bound in the theorem follows by defining
C^{\prime\prime}
appropriately.

B.7
Label complexity under curvature and Massart noise: Proof of Lemma
16

Lemma 31

Conditions (C1) and (C2) yield assumption (A1), with
p_{o}=r_{o}^{d}\cdot c_{o}\cdot v_{d}
, where
v_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof:

Pick any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{+}
(the negative case is similar), and let
r=\inf_{z\in{\mathcal{X}}^{0}}\|x-z\|
. If
r\geq r_{o}
, then
B=B(x,r_{o})
is entirely in
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{+}
. Otherwise, the reach condition (C2) implies the existence of a ball
B\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}^{+}
that contains
x
and has radius
r_{o}
. Either way,
\mu(B)\geq c_{o}{\rm vol}(B)=c_{o}v_{d}r_{o}^{d}
by (C1).

\Box

Lemma 32

Conditions (C1) and (C2) yield assumptions (A2) and (A3’) with
\sigma=\xi=1/d
.

Proof:

Under (C1), any ball of probability mass
\leq p
has volume
\leq p/c_{o}
and radius
\leq(p/(c_{o}v_{d}))^{1/d}
. Let’s call this latter quantity
r
. Thus, any point in
\partial_{p}
lies within distance
2r
of the boundary, while a point in
\partial_{p,p}
lies within distance
4r
.

To bound the volume of
\partial_{p,p}
, we can associate each point in this region with its nearest neighbor in
{\mathcal{X}}^{0}
; by condition (C1), this projection map is uniquely defined for
r<r_{o}/4
. The volume of the region is thus
O(r)
and under (C1), has probability mass
O(r)=O(p^{1/d})
.

\Box

B.8
Label complexity under smoothness and Tsybakov noise

Continuing from the Massart setting, we maintain (C1), but now replace (C2) by a trio of smoothness, margin, and curvature conditions. First, recall that
{\rm dist}(x,S)
denotes the probability-distance from
x
and set
S
. We will overload notation so that for
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

{\rm dist}(x,x^{\prime})={\rm dist}(x,\{x^{\prime}\})=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{B}}(x)\cap{\mathcal{B}}(x^{\prime})\},

that is, the probability mass of the smallest ball containing both
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

(C2’)

[Holder-smoothness of conditional probability function] There exist constants
L,\alpha
such that

|\eta(x)-\eta(x^{\prime})|\ \leq\ L\cdot{\rm dist}(x,x^{\prime})^{\alpha}

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

(C3’)

[Tsybakov margin condition] There exists constants
M,\beta
such that

\mu(\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:|\eta(x)|\leq\tau\})\leq M\tau^{\beta}

for all
\tau\in(0,1)
.

(C4’)

[Bounded curvature] The boundaries
\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\eta(x)=\gamma\}
and
\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:\eta(x)=-\gamma\}
are
(d-1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifolds of reach
r_{o}>0
.

Lemma
31
continues to hold, with condition (C4’) doing the job of (C2). This yields assumption (A1). For the remaining assumptions, we first obtain a consequence of the Holder condition.

Lemma 33

Under (C2’), for any
p,q>0
,

(a)

x\in\partial_{p}\implies|\eta(x)|\leq Lp^{\alpha}
.

(b)

x\in\partial_{p,q}\implies|\eta(x)|\leq L(p^{\alpha}+q^{\alpha})
.

Proof:

Pick any
x\in\partial_{p}
. Let
s(x)=\mbox{sign}(\eta(x))
. By definition of the
p
-boundary set, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{-s(x)}
such that
{\rm dist}(x,x^{\prime})<p+\epsilon
. By the Holder condition,
|\eta(x)-\eta(x^{\prime})|<L(p+\epsilon)^{\alpha}
and thus
|\eta(x)|<L(p+\epsilon)^{\alpha}
. Since this holds for any
\epsilon>0
, we get part (a).

For (b), pick
x\in\partial_{p,q}
and
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists
x^{\prime}\in\partial_{p}
with
{\rm dist}(x,x^{\prime})<q+\epsilon
. As before, we use the Holder condition to conclude that
|\eta(x)|<|\eta(x^{\prime})|+L(q+\epsilon)^{\alpha}
and then invoke (a).

\Box

Lemma 34

Conditions (C2’) and (C3’) yield assumptions (A2) and (A3) with
C=(2L)^{\beta}M
,
\sigma=\alpha\beta
, and
p_{1}=(\gamma/L)^{1/\alpha}
.

Proof:

By Lemma
33
,
\partial_{p,p}\subset\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:|\eta(x)|\leq 2Lp^{\alpha}\}
; the probability mass of this set can be bounded by (C3’).

Also by Lemma
33
,
\partial_{p}
is entirely contained in
\{x\in{\mathcal{X}}:|\eta(x)|\leq Lp^{\alpha}\}
. For
p<p_{1}
, this does not intersect
{\mathcal{X}}_{\gamma}
.

\Box
