Thomas-Fermi theory of out-of-plane charge screening in graphene
Vitaly Moroz, Cyrill B. Muratov

Abstract
. This paper provides a variational treatment of the effect of external charges on the free charges in an infinite free-standing graphene sheet within the Thomas-Fermi theory. We establish existence, uniqueness and regularity of the energy minimizers corresponding to the free charge densities that screen the effect of an external electrostatic potential at the neutrality point. For the potential due to one or several off-layer point charges, we also prove positivity and a precise universal asymptotic decay rate for the screening charge density, as well as an exact charge cancellation by the graphene sheet. We also treat a simpler case of the non-zero background charge density and establish similar results in that case.



1.
Introduction

Graphene is a classical example of a two-dimensional material whose
electronic properties give rise to a number of unusual characteristics
that make it a prime target for both fundamental research and multiple
applications

[
18
,
29
,
22
,
14
,
6
]
. A key
feature of the electrons in single layer graphene sheets is the
presence of the Dirac cone in their dispersion relation that makes the
elementary excitations (electrons and holes) of the ground state
behave as massless relativistic fermions

[
23
,
8
]
. This presents challenges in the
theoretical treatment of those excitations, as their kinetic energy,
which is on the order of
E_{K}\sim\hbar v_{F}/r
, where

v_{F}\simeq 1\times 10^{8}
cm/s is the Fermi velocity and
r
is the
radius of the wave packet containing a single charge, remains
comparable to the Coulombic interaction energy

E_{C}\sim e^{2}/(\epsilon_{d}r)
of two charges at distance
r

independently of the scale
r
(here
e
is the elementary charge, in
the CGS units,
\epsilon_{d}\sim 1
is the effective dielectric
constant, and it is noted that
e^{2}/(\hbar v_{F})\simeq 2.2
). As a
result, many-body effects need to be taken into consideration in the
studies of electronic properties of graphene. In particular, these
effects are significant in determining the way the massless
ultrarelativistic fermions screen the electric field of supercritical
charged impurities
[
23
]
.

The problem of characterizing the charged impurity screening by the
graphene sheet has been studied, using a number of theoretical
approaches
[
11
,
30
,
31
,
21
,
20
,
19
,
28
]
(this list is not
intended to be exhaustive). Note that a similar question arises in the
studies of the graphene based devices in the proximity of a conducting
electrode, or when a scanning tunneling microscope tip approaches a
graphene sheet
[
3
]
. In particular, in this situation the
electric charge the layer is exposed to may exceed the elementary
charge
e
by many orders of magnitude. Under such conditions, a fully
nonlinear treatment of the screening problem is, therefore, necessary.

In conventional quantum systems, a good starting point for the
analysis of electric field screening is the Thomas-Fermi theory, as it
yields an asymptotically exact response of a system of interacting
electrons to a large external charge
[
26
]
. Such a theory for
massless relativistic fermions was developed by Di Vincenzo and Mele
in the context of charged impurity screening in graphite intercalated
compounds
[
11
]
. They conducted numerical studies of the
resulting equations for the screening charge density and noted a
highly non-local character of the response. More recently, Katsnelson
carried out a formal analysis of the asymptotic behavior of the
screening charge density away from a single impurity ion in a graphene
monolayer
[
21
]
. His results were further clarified an
extended by Fogler, Novikov and Shklovskii, who also confirmed the
predictions about the decay of the screening charge density by
numerical simulations
[
16
]
. The nonlocal character of the
response and its dependence on the level of doping have been confirmed
by the direct experimental observations of the screening charge
density
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
. Note that these observations are
at variance with the prediction of a purely local dielectric response
at the Dirac point from the linear response theory for massless
relativistic fermions within the random phase approximation

[
20
]
.

This paper is a mathematical counterpart of the studies in

[
11
,
21
,
16
]
that provides a suitable
variational framework for the study of the charge screening problem
described by the Thomas-Fermi theory of graphene (for a closely
related Thomas-Fermi-von Weizsäcker model and some further
discussion, see
[
28
]
). The setting turns out to be rather
delicate, as the presence of a bare Coulombic potential from an
impurity leads to heavy tails in the potential term that are precisely
balanced with the Coulombic interaction term. Within our setting, we
prove existence, uniqueness, radial symmetry and monotonicity of the
minimizer of the graphene Thomas-Fermi energy for an off-layer
external point charge in a free-standing graphene sheet. More
generally, we provide existence, uniqueness, the Euler-Lagrange
equation that is understood in a suitable sense, and regularity of the
minimizer for a general class of external potentials arising as
Coulombic potentials of appropriate collections of external
charges. Back to a single off-layer charge in a free-standing graphene
sheet, we establish the precise asymptotic decay of the screening
charge density at infinity, which agrees with the one obtained by
Katsnelson using formal arguments.

The decay of the screening charge density turns out to be a borderline
power law decay modulated by a logarithmic factor that makes it barely
integrable. The latter presents a significant technical difficulty in
the handling of the appropriate barrier functions that control the
decay of the solution at infinity. In particular, we prove that the
decay indeed tuns out to be universal, independently of the strength
of the external charge and remains the same for a finite collection of
charges of the same sign.

As a by-product of our analysis, we also demonstrate the existence of
sign-changing minimizers in the case of positive fast decaying
potentials for the closely related Thomas-Fermi-von Weizsäcker model
studied in
[
28
]
in the regime when the latter is well
approximated by the Thomas-Fermi model. This gives a partial answer
to the question raised in
[
28
]
. Finally, we present the
corresponding results for the biased layer. The treatment of the
latter is significantly simpler due to the expected fast power law
decay of the screening charge density.

2.
Model and main results

Thomas-Fermi (TF) energy for massless relativistic fermions in a
free-standing graphene layer in the presence of the external
electrostatic potential
V
takes the following form, after a suitable
non-dimensionalization
[
21
]
:

(2.1)

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}(\rho)=\frac{2}{3}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\rho|^{3/2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)V(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x+\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

Here
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the
charge density
of charge carrying
fermionic quasiparticles (electrons and holes). The density
\rho
is
a sign–changing function with
\rho>0
corresponding to electrons and

\rho<0
to holes. The first,
Thomas–Fermi term
, is an
approximation of the kinetic energy of the uniform gas of
noninteracting particles. The exponent
3/2
can be deduced from
scaling considerations.
The last, nonlocal
Coulomb term

(2.2)

\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x)\rho(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y,

is the like-charged inter-particle repulsion energy which is inherited
from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The middle term is the
potential energy
due to the
interaction with the
external potential

V:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. In the
case of a
single
external point charge of magnitude
Z\in\mathbb{R}

located in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
at distance
d\geq 0
away from the graphene layer
the external potential is

(2.3)

V_{Z,d}(x):=\frac{Z}{2\pi\sqrt{d^{2}+|x|^{2}}},

but more general potentials
V(x)
could be considered, e.g., those
involving multiple point charge configurations:

(2.4)

V_{N}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_{Z_{i},d_{i}}(x-x_{i}),

for some
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
d_{i}\geq 0
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Importantly, for an unscreened system of uncompensated external
charges (i.e., when
\sum_{i}Z_{i}\not=0
in (
2.4
)) one has

V_{N}(x)\sim 1/|x|
as
|x|\to\infty
, since the
quasiparticle–charge interaction is according to Coulomb’s law in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For a more detailed discussion of various terms in the
energy and the non-dimensionalization, see
[
28
, Section
2]
. Notice that the energy in (
2.1
) is invariant
with respect to the transformation

(2.5)

\displaystyle\rho\to-\rho,\qquad V\to-V,

hence when dealing with the potential
V_{Z,d}
it is sufficient to
restrict attention to the case
Z>0
.

Our principal goal is to prove the existence of global minimizers of

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
and establish their fundamental properties, such as
regularity and decay estimates. At first glance the
Thomas–Fermi energy
{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
looks similar to its classical
three-dimensional (3D) atomic counterpart

[
27
]
[
26
]
[
5
]
.
However, there are fundamental differences within the variational
framework for graphene modelling:

•

Unlike in the classical TF-theory for atoms and molecules where

\rho\geq 0
, the density
\rho
in graphene is a sign–changing
function. As a consequence,
\mathcal{D}(|\rho|,|\rho|)\geq\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)

which means that oscillating profiles could be energetically more
favorable.

•

All three terms in
{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
with
V=V_{Z,0}
scale at
the same rate under the charge–preserving rescaling

\rho_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{2}\rho(\lambda x)
. Hence

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}(\rho_{\lambda})=c\lambda
when
d=0
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Physically, this is a manifestation of the non-perturbative role of
the Coulomb interaction in graphene. Mathematically, this reveals
the critical tuning of the three different terms in the energy.

•

The nonlocal term
\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)
is formally identical to the
usual Coulomb term in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. However, the integral kernel

|x-y|^{-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is associated with the Green function of the
fractional Laplacian operator
(-\Delta)^{1/2}
. As a consequence,
the Euler–Lagrange equation for
{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
transforms into
a fractional semilinear partial differential equation (PDE)
involving
(-\Delta)^{1/2}
, instead of the usual Laplace operator

-\Delta
of the classical 3D TF-theory.

Note that the total number of electrons and holes in the graphene
sheet is neither fixed nor bounded a priori. As a consequence, unlike
in the atomic and molecular 3D models, it is unclear if the minimizers
of
{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
should have a finite total charge, i.e. if they
are
L^{1}
–functions. This implies that regular distributions should
be included as admissible densities. Indeed, even if the density

\rho
is a sign–changing continuous function, it is not a priori
clear if
\rho
can be interpreted as a charge density in the sense of
potential theory (i.e., whether
d\mu=\rho\,dx
can be associated
to a signed measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, making the Coulomb energy

\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)
meaningful in the sense of the Lebesgue integration,
see
[
28
]
*Example 4.1 and further references therein. This
makes the analysis of the minimizers of
{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}

mathematically challenging.

We avoid these issues by identifying the Coulomb term
\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)

with one-half of the square of the
\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
norm of

\rho
. The energy we consider is then

(2.6)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle:=\frac{2}{3}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\rho|^{3/2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\rho,V\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho\right\|_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}^{2},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a duality pairing
between the function
V\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the linear
functional generated by
\rho
, to be specified shortly. Sometimes
we also write
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{V}
to emphasize the dependence on
V
. It is easy
to see that the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
in (
2.6
) agrees with that of

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
when
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\langle\rho,V\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V\rho\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x
.

The natural domain of definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is the class

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{0}:={\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Clearly,
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is a Banach space with the norm

\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}=\|\cdot\|_{L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\|\cdot\|_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Its dual
space
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\prime}
can be identified with the Banach space

{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
1
1

1

Recall that

{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):f=f_{1}+f_{2},\;f_{1}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},f_{2}\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\}

is a
Banach space with the norm

\|f\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}:=\inf(\|f_{1}\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}+\|f_{2}\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})})
,
where the infimum is taken over all admissible pairs
(f_{1},f_{2})
.

Therefore, one may define
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
as the
duality pairing between
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. More precisely, for every

\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and every
V=V_{1}+V_{2}
, where
V_{1}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and

V_{2}\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
we may define

(2.8)

\displaystyle\langle\rho,V\rangle:={}_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}\langle\rho,V_{1}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)V_{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x.

See Section
3
for further details and precise
definitions.

Our first result establishes the existence of a unique minimizer for

{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
there exists a unique
minimizer
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
such that

{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{V})=\inf_{\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho)
. The minimizer

\rho_{V}
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

(2.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\varphi,V\rangle+\langle\rho_{V},\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=0,\quad\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}.

Furthermore,

i)

if
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
then
\rho_{V}\geq 0
,

ii)

if
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
then
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\rho_{V}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
.

Note that the statements of this theorem, including the ones about the
positivity and Hölder continuity of the minimizer apply to

V=V_{Z,d}
for all
Z>0
and
d>0
. The positivity of the
minimizer follows from the well-known formula

(2.10)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}V_{Z,d}(x)=\left.-{d\over dt}V_{Z,t+d}(x)\right|_{t=0}={Zd\over 2\pi(d^{2}+|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

that is obtained from the interpretation of the half-Laplacian in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
via harmonic extension to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,\infty)
(see also
the direct calculations in
[
15
]
*p. 258 and (6.5)). From this
formula, it also follows that
V=V_{Z,d}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
for all
Z>0

and

, due to the fact that

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V_{Z,d}\in L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
in this
case.

If, e.g.,
\rho_{V}\in L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
then (
2.9
) implies that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sgn(\rho_{V}(x))|\rho_{V}(x)|^{1/2}-V(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho_{V}(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y=0\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$.}

However, (

) is not valid for a general

,
since the nonlocal term may not be well–defined as the Lebesgue
integral. Nevertheless, we show that for any

the
Euler–Lagrange equation (
2.9
) is equivalent to the
fractional semilinear PDE

(2.12)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u+|u|u=(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in ${\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$},

and

(2.13)

\displaystyle u_{V}:=\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

is the unique solution of (

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
Z>0
,

and let

be defined in (

). Then
the minimizer
\rho_{V_{Z,d}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}

]

]

]

Remark 2.1
.

The order of the estimate in (

–decay becomes clear if we note that
p=2

*(1.7) for

Remark 2.2
.

If

V_{Z,0}(x)=Z/(2\pi|x|)\not\in({\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

and
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}

. Then

(2.17)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{V_{Z,d}}(\rho_{V_{Z,d}})=d^{-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{V_{Z,1}}(\rho_{V_{Z,1}})\to-\infty,

as
d\to 0
. Note also that by scaling,

\|\rho_{V_{Z,d}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|\rho_{V_{Z,1}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that by (
2.10
) we have

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V_{Z,d}\to Z\delta_{0}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as

d\to 0
, so in the case
V=V_{Z,0}
equation (
2.12
)
formally becomes

(2.18)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u+u^{2}=Z\delta_{0}\quad\text{in
$\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Such an equation has no positive distributional solutions, see

[
9
]
*Theorem 4.2.

Lastly, as a corollary to Theorem
2.2
we have the following
result that is relevant to the experiments on ion cluster screening in
single graphene sheets
[
39
]
. This result is a direct
consequence of the universal decay estimate in (
2.15
) and
the comparison principle for (
2.12
).

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
N\geq 2
be an integer, let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
let
Z_{i}>0
and
d_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. Then the
minimizer
\rho_{V_{N}}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
of
\mathcal{E}_{0}
with

V=V_{N}
, where
V_{N}
is given by (
2.4
), satisfies the
conclusions of Theorem
2.2
.

In physical terms, this result implies that a cluster of
out-of-plane charges of the same sign exhibits the same universal
decay at infinity of the induced charge density in a graphene layer
as a single point charge and is independent of the charge
magnitude. Therefore, surprisingly, measuring the behaviour of the
induced charge density far from the cluster does not provide any
information about the cluster itself.

3.
Variational setting at the neutrality point

3.1.
Space
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Recall that the homogeneous Sobolev space
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

can be defined as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with respect
to the Gagliardo norm

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

By the fractional Sobolev inequality
[
25
, Theorem 8.4]
,
[
10
, Theorem
6.5]
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\geq\sqrt{\pi}\,\|u\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},\qquad\forall u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

In particular, the space
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a well-defined
space of functions and

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

The space
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is also a Hilbert space, with
the scalar product associated to (
3.1
) given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

Recall (cf.
[
17
]
) that if
u\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

then
u^{+},u^{-}\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

\|u^{\pm}\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\|u\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
Moreover,
\langle u^{+},u^{-}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 0
.

The dual space to
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is denoted

\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. According to the Riesz representation
theorem, for every
F\in\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
there exists a
uniquely defined
potential

U_{F}\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such
that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\langle U_{F},\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\langle F,\varphi\rangle\qquad\forall\varphi\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

where
\langle F,\cdot\rangle:\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
denotes
the bounded linear functional generated by
F
,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}
is the inner product in

{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}
will be similarly
defined as the inner product in
{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
Moreover,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|U_{F}\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|F\|_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

so the duality (
3.5
) is an isometry.

The potential
U_{F}\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfying
(
3.5
) is interpreted as the
weak solution
of
the linear equation

(3.7)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}U_{F}=F\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

and we recall that for functions
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the fractional
Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{1/2}
can be defined as

(3.8)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{2u(x)-u(x+y)-u(x-y)}{|y|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}),

cf.
[
10
, Proposition 3.3]
.

3.2.
Regular distributions in
{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and potentials

Recall that
\rho\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
means that
\rho
is a

regular distribution
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, i.e.

(3.9)

\displaystyle\langle\rho,\varphi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

and
\langle\rho,\varphi\rangle
is bounded by a multiple of

\|\varphi\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}
. Then
\langle\rho,\cdot\rangle
is understood
as the unique continuous extension of (
3.9
) to
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
Caution however is needed as not every regular distribution

\rho\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
admits an integral representation
(
3.9
) on all of
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. In other words,

\rho\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
does not necessarily imply that

\rho w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every
w\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Examples of this type go
back to H. Cartan (cf.
[
24
]
,
[
7
]
, or

[
28
]
*Remark 5.1 for an example from
{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and further references). As a consequence, the Coulomb energy term in

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}
may not be defined in the sense of Lebesgue’s integration
for all
\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and should be interpreted
in the
distributional sense
, i.e., in the definition of

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{0}
one should replace
\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)
with

\|\rho\|_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}^{2}
.
Recall however that every nonnegative distribution is a measure
[
25
, Theorem 6.22]
.

An alternative reinterpretation of
\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)
can be given in
terms of potentials. Given
\rho\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, let
U_{\rho}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

be the uniquely defined potential of
\rho
, defined as in (
3.9
)
by the Riesz’s representation theorem.
If
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},(1+|x|)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x)
then the potential
U_{\rho}

could be identified with the Riesz potential of the function

\rho
, so that

(3.10)

\displaystyle U_{\rho}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\quad\text{a.e.~in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$,}

(see
[
24
, (1.3.10)]
).
Furthermore, according to the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev (HLS) inequality (cf.
[
36
, Section 5.1, Theorem 1]
),
if
\rho\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
s\in(1,2)
then
U_{\rho}\in L^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\frac{1}{t}=\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{2}
, and

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|U_{\rho}\|_{L^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\|\rho\|_{L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Even if (
3.10
) is valid,
\rho U_{\rho}\not\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in
general. However, if
\varphi\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
then

\varphi U_{\rho}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by the HLS
inequality and

(3.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\rho(x)\varphi(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U_{\rho}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x=\langle U_{\rho},U_{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\langle\rho,\varphi\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}.

In particular,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\rho,\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U_{\rho}(x)\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x=\|U_{\rho}\|^{2}_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|\rho\|^{2}_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}},

which means that
L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and the Coulomb energy is
well–defined on
L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in the sense of Lebesgue’s
integration.

3.3.
Existence, uniqueness and regularity of the minimizers.

Consider the unconstrained minimization problem

(3.14)

\displaystyle E_{0}:=\inf_{\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho).

It is easy to prove the following.

Proposition 3.1

(Existence)
.

For every
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the TF–energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
admits
a unique minimizer
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{V})=E_{0}
.
The minimizer
\rho_{V}
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\varphi,V\rangle+\langle\rho_{V},\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=0\quad\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}.

Proof.

It is standard to conclude from
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that

{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is bounded below on
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
, i.e., that
E_{0}>-\infty
.

Consider a minimizing sequence
(\rho_{n})\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.
Clearly

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\|\rho_{n}\|_{L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C,\qquad\sup_{n}\|\rho_{n}\|_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C.

Using weak-
*
compactness of the closed unit ball in
{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, we may
extract a subsequence, still denoted by
(\rho_{n})
, such that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\rightharpoonup

\displaystyle\;\rho_{V}\qquad\text{in $L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$},

(3.18)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}

\displaystyle\;F\qquad\text{in ${\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$},

for some
\rho_{V}\in L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
F\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
By the definition, (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) mean that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{V}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\qquad\forall\varphi\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(3.20)

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{n},\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\to

\displaystyle\;\;\langle F,\varphi\rangle\qquad\forall\varphi\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Therefore, passing to the limit we obtain

(3.21)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{V}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x=\langle F,\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

In particular,
\rho_{V}\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
defines a regular distribution in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and we may identify
F=\rho_{V}
. This implies that

(3.22)

{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{V})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{n})=E_{0},

which follows from the weak lower semicontinuity of the

\|\cdot\|_{L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}
norms, and the
weak continuity of the linear functionals
\langle\cdot,V\rangle

on
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.

The uniqueness of the minimizer
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is a consequence of the strict convexity of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, which is the sum of the strictly convex kinetic energy, linear external potential energy, and positive definite quadratic Coulomb energy.

The derivation of the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.15
) is standard, we omit the details.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

As was already mentioned, if
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\cap L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

then (
3.15
) can be interpreted pointwise as the integral
equation (
2.11
). However, in general the
Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.15
) for
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
should be
interpreted as

(3.23)

\displaystyle\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}(x)|^{1/2}+U_{\rho_{V}}=V\quad\text{in
$\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$},

where
U_{\rho_{V}}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
is the potential of
\rho_{V}
defined
via (
3.5
). In particular, if

\rho_{V}\geq 0
then
U_{\rho_{V}}\geq 0
(see
[
12
, Theorem 3.14]
) which implies
V\geq 0
and

(3.24)

\displaystyle 0\leq\rho_{V}\leq V^{2}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Remark 3.2
.

The mapping
V\mapsto\rho_{V}
is a bijection between

{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{\prime}={\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. Indeed, the uniqueness of
the minimizer implies that
\rho_{V}
is injective. Further, it is
clear that for any
\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
,

(3.25)

\displaystyle V:=U_{\rho}+\sgn(\rho)|\rho(x)|^{1/2}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

which means that the mapping
\rho_{V}
is also surjective. In
particular, this shows that non–regular
at infinity
distributions in

{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
could occur amongst the minimizers. Simply choose a
regular distribution
\rho\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
such that
\rho\varphi\not\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\varphi\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
(see e.g.
[
28
, Example 4.1.]
for an explicit example) and generate the
corresponding potential
V
via (
3.25
).

While for a generic
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
the information

\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is optimal, under additional restrictions on the
potential
V
the regularity of the minimizer can be improved up to
the regularity of
V
.

Lemma 3.2

(Hölder regularity)
.

Assume that
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some

\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then the minimizer
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
additionally
satisfies
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and

\rho_{V}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
. Furthermore, the potential
U_{\rho}

could be identified with the Riesz potential of
\rho
as in (
3.10
) and

U_{\rho_{V}}\in C^{1/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

According to (
3.23
), the minimizer
\rho_{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
satisfies

(3.26)

\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}=V-U_{\rho_{V}}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Since

\rho_{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\subset L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
by the HLS-inequality (
3.11
) with
s=3/2
we have

(3.27)

U_{\rho_{V}}\in L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

and in particular, the potential
U_{\rho}

could be identified with the Riesz potential of
\rho
as in (
3.10
).

Also, by the Sobolev inequality (
3.2
),

(3.28)

V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset L^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

This implies

(3.29)

V^{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

In particular, both
V
and
V^{2}
are bounded and decay to zero as
|x|\to\infty
.
Note also that
U_{\rho_{V}}^{2}\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Hence,

(3.30)

|\rho_{V}|=(V-U_{\rho_{V}})^{2}=V^{2}-2VU_{\rho_{V}}+U_{\rho_{V}}^{2}\in L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Furthermore, by Hölder estimates on Riesz potentials, we conclude that
U_{\rho_{V}}\in C^{1/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, see
[
28
]
*Lemma 4.1 or
[
12
]
*Theorem 2. Then

(3.31)

|\rho_{V}|=(V-U_{\rho_{V}})^{2}\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

where
\beta=\min\{\alpha,1/3\}
and
\rho_{V}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
.
If
\alpha\leq 1/3
we are done. If
\alpha>1/3
then (
3.31
) implies
U_{\rho_{V}}\in C^{1,1/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, see
[
33
]
*Proposition 2.8. Therefore,
\rho_{V}
has at least the same Hölder regularity as
V
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Similarly, one can establish higher Hölder regularity of
\rho_{V}
assuming higher regularity of
V
.
For instance, using
[
33
]
*Proposition 2.8 we can conclude that if
V\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
then

\rho_{V}\in C^{1,\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where
\beta=\min\{\alpha,1/3\}
. However, in general the Hölder regularity of
\rho_{V}
can not be improved beyond the Hölder regularity of
V
.

4.
Positivity and decay

4.1.
Half-Laplacian representation, positivity and comparison

Let
\rho_{V}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
be the minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
. Introduce the substitution

(4.1)

\displaystyle u_{V}:=\sgn(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}.

Then
\rho_{V}=|u_{V}|u_{V}
and (
3.15
) transforms into

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{V}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\varphi,V\rangle+\langle U_{|u_{V}|u_{V}},\varphi\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}=0\quad\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}.

Proposition 4.1

(Equivalent fractional PDE)
.

Let
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
u_{V}
be defined by (
4.1
). Then

u_{V}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and is the unique solution of the problem

(4.3)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u+|u|u=(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in ${\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$.}

Proof.

Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then

(-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset L^{4/3}\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{0}

[
33
]
*Section 2.1. Test (
4.2
) with

\varphi=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi
and take into
account that in view of (
3.12
),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\langle|u_{V}|u_{V},\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=

\displaystyle\langle U_{|u_{V}|u_{V}},(-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u_{V}|u_{V}(x)\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\quad\forall\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Then (
4.2
) yields

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{V}(-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle(-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi,V\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u_{V}|u_{V}(x)\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x=0\quad\forall\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

or equivalently,

(4.7)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u_{V}-(-\Delta)^{1/2}V+|u_{V}|u_{V}=0\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

where
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
,
|u_{V}|u_{V}=\rho_{V}\in{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Hence
u_{V}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, and (
4.7
)
also holds weakly in
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
by density.

The uniqueness for (
4.3
) follows from the Comparison Principle of Lemma
4.3
below.
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Positivity)
.

Let
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Assume that
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then
u_{V}\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If, in addition
V\neq 0
then
u_{V}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Decompose
u_{V}=u_{V}^{+}-u_{V}^{-}
and recall that

u_{V}^{+},u_{V}^{-}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
\langle u_{V}^{+},u_{V}^{-}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 0
. Testing (
4.3
) by
u_{V}^{-}\geq 0
and taking into
account that
u_{V}|u_{V}|u_{V}^{-}\leq 0
, we obtain

(4.8)

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle V,u_{V}^{-}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\langle u_{V},u_{V}^{-}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{V}|u_{V}|\,u_{V}^{-}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\leq-\langle u_{V}^{-},u_{V}^{-}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 0.

We conclude that
u_{V}^{-}=0
.

Further, if
V\neq 0
then
u=0
is not a solution of (
4.3
) and hence
u_{V}\neq 0
.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Comparison Principle)
.

Let
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Assume that
u,v\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

are a super and a subsolution to (
4.3
) in a smooth domain

\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively, i.e.,

(4.9)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u+u|u|\geq(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega),

(4.10)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}v+v|v|\leq(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega).

If
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega\neq\varnothing
, we also assume
u\geq v
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bar{\Omega}
. Then
u\geq v
in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Subtracting one inequality from another, we obtain

(4.11)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}(v-u)+v|v|-u|u|\leq 0\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega).

Let
H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega)
denotes the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
wrt the Gagliardo’s norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}
, defined in (
3.1
). With this definition,
H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega)

is automatically a closed subspace of
H^{1/2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
By density, (
4.11
) is also valid in
H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega)
, in the sense that

(4.12)

\langle v-u,\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v|v|-u|u|)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\leq 0\quad\forall\,0\leq\varphi\in H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega).

Note that
(v-u)^{+}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. If

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Omega\neq\varnothing
then
u\geq v
in

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bar{\Omega}
and hence
(v-u)^{+}=0
in

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bar{\Omega}
. This implies
(v-u)^{+}\in H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega)
,
see e.g.
[
2
]
*Theorem 10.1.1. Testing (
4.12
)
by
(v-u)^{+}
, taking into account

\langle(v-u)^{-},(v-u)^{+}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 0
and monotone increase of the
nonlinearity, we obtain

(4.13)

0\geq\langle v-u,(v-u)^{+}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v|v|-u|u|)(v-u)^{+}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\\
\geq\langle(v-u)^{+},(v-u)^{+}\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|(v-u)^{+}\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}.

We conclude that
(v-u)^{+}=0
.
∎

The Comparison Principle immediately implies that (
4.3
) can
have at most one solution in
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Hence the solution
u_{V}

constructed from the minimizer
\rho_{V}
via (
4.1
) is the
unique solution of (
4.3
). A consequence of the uniqueness is
the following.

Corollary 4.4
.

Assume that
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a radially symmetric non-increasing function then
u_{V}
is also radially symmetric and
non-increasing.

Proof.

Note that
u_{V}
is the unique global minimizer of the convex energy

J_{V}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\|u\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{3}-\langle u,V\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

on
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Since
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\langle u_{V},V\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{V}(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x,

where the latter integral is finite by the HLS inequality.
Then the symmetric–decreasing rearrangement
u_{V}^{*}
is also a minimizer of

J_{V}
, by
[
25
]
*Theorem 3.4 and Lemma 7.17.
Hence the assertion follows from the uniqueness of the minimizer.
∎

Another straightforward, but important consequence of the Comparison
Principle is the following upper bound on
u_{V}
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Assume that
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
V\geq 0
. Then

(4.14)

\displaystyle u_{V}\leq V\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.}

Proof.

We simply note that
V
is a supersolution to
(
4.3
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.

(4.15)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V+V^{2}\geq(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Hence, (
4.14
) follows from the Comparison Principle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

The Comparison Principle can be used as an alternative tool to prove
the existence of the solution
u_{V}
of (
4.3
), via construction
of appropriate sub and supersolutions. In the next section we
construct an explicit barrier which later will be used to obtain lower
and upper solution with matching sharp asymptotics at infinity. This
will lead to the sharp decay estimates on
u_{V}
and
\rho_{V}
.

4.2.
Super-harmonicity of the potential is essential

We are going to show that the assumptions
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
is in a certain sense necessary for the positivity of the minimizer
\rho_{V}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Assume that
V\neq 0
and

(4.16)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|V(x)=0.

Then
\rho_{V}
changes sign in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Remark 4.1
.

The assumption (
4.16
) implicitly necessitates that

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V
can not be non-negative. Indeed, if

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
then
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|V(x)>0
(cf. (
4.18
) below), which is incompatible with (
4.16
).

Proof.

According to (
3.23
) and Lemma

3.2
, we know that
\rho_{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

U_{\rho}
could be identified with the Riesz potential of the function

\rho
as in (
3.10
),
U_{\rho_{V}}\in C^{1/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
and

(4.17)

\mathrm{sign}(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}(x)=V(x)-U_{\rho_{V}}(x)\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

Assume that
\rho_{V}\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(4.18)

\displaystyle U_{\rho}(x)\geq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{B_{2|x|}(x)}\frac{\rho(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\geq\frac{1}{4\pi|x|}\int_{B_{2|x|}(x)}\rho(y)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

In particular,

(4.19)

\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}|x|U_{\rho_{V}}(x)>0

and hence, in view of (
4.16
),

(4.20)

\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}|x|\mathrm{sign}(\rho_{V})|\rho_{V}|^{1/2}(x)=\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}|x|(V(x)-U_{\rho_{V}}(x))<0,

a contradiction. A symmetric argument shows that
\rho_{V}\leq 0
is also
impossible. ∎

Remark 4.2
.

For example, we can consider the dipole potential

W_{Z}(x)=\frac{Z}{2\pi(1+|x|^{2})^{3/2}}.

Note that
W_{Z}(x)=-\left.{d\over dt}V_{Z,t}(x)\right|_{t=1}
. While
W_{Z}>0
, it is not difficult to see,
using the harmonic extension of
W_{Z}
, that

(-\Delta)^{1/2}W_{Z}(|x|)=\frac{Z(2-|x|^{2})}{2\pi(1+|x|^{2})^{5/2}},

which is a sign–changing function.
Clearly,
W_{Z}
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
4.6
, so the minimizer
\rho_{W_{Z}}
changes sign for any
Z>0
.

4.3.
Sign-changing minimizer in TFW model

A density functional theory of Thomas-Fermi-Dirac-von Weizsäcker (TFW) type to describe the response of a single layer of graphene to a charge
V
was developed in
[
28
]
. For
\varepsilon>0
, and in the notations of the present paper, the TFW-energy studied in
[
28
]
has the form:

(4.21)

{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}(\rho):=\varepsilon\||\rho|^{-1/2}\rho\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho):{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}.

The existence of a minimizer for
{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}
with
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
was established in
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
.
We are going to show that if
V\geq 0
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
4.6
then for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
the TFW–energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}

admits a sign–changing minimizer
. This gives a partial answer to one of the questions left open in
[
28
]
(see discussions in
[
28
, Section 3]
).

To show the existence of a sign–changing minimizer for
{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}
,
assume that
V\geq 0
and the assumptions of Proposition
4.6
holds. Then the minimizer
\rho_{V}
of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
changes sign.
Let

E_{0}:=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}={\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{V}).

Similarly to Proposition
3.1
, we can also minimize convex energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
on the weakly closed set
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{+}
of nonnegative functions in
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
.
Let
\rho_{V}^{+}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{+}
be the minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{+}
and set

E_{0}^{+}:=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{+}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0}={\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho_{V}^{+}).

It is clear that
E_{0}^{+}<0
and hence
\rho_{V}^{+}\neq 0
(just take trial functions
0\leq\varphi\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\langle V,\varphi\rangle>0
). By an adaptation of arguments in
[
25
, Theorem 11.13]
, the minimizer
\rho_{V}^{+}
satisfies the Thomas–Fermi equation

(4.22)

(\rho_{V}^{+})^{3/2}=\big(V-U_{\rho_{V}^{+}}\big)^{+}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Observe that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho_{V}^{+})\neq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Indeed, assume that
\rho_{V}^{+}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then
\rho_{V}^{+}>0
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

(4.23)

(\rho_{V}^{+})^{3/2}=V-U_{\rho_{V}^{+}}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$},

which contradicts to the uniqueness, since (
4.23
) has a sign–changing solution
\rho_{V}
by Proposition
4.6
.
Crucially, by the strict convexity of
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
we can also conclude that

(4.24)

E_{0}<E_{0}^{+}.

Next, for
\varepsilon>0
consider the TFW-energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}
. Set

E_{0,\varepsilon}:=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}.

The existence of a minimizer for
E_{0,\varepsilon}
was established in
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Without loss of a generality, we may assume that
\rho_{V}
is regular
enough and
|\rho_{V}|^{-1/2}\rho\in\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(otherwise we may approximate
\rho_{V}
by smooths functions).
Then

E_{0,\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon\||\rho_{V}|^{-1/2}\rho_{V}\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}+E_{0}\to E_{0}\quad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0$}.

Similarly,

E_{0}^{+}\leq E_{0,\varepsilon}^{+}:=\inf_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}^{+}}{\mathcal{E}}_{0,\varepsilon}.

Taking into account the strict inequality (
4.24
), for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
we have

E_{0}<E_{0,\varepsilon}<E_{0}^{+}\leq E_{0,\varepsilon}^{+}.

In particular,
E_{0,\varepsilon}<E_{0,\varepsilon}^{+}
and we conclude that a minimizer for
E_{0,\varepsilon}
must change sign. For example, a dipole, or any compactly supported nonnegative potential should give rise to a sign–changing
global minimizer in the TFW model.

4.4.
Logarithmic barrier

Recall (cf.
[
15
]
*Theorem 1.1) that for a radial function

u\in C^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}})
such that

(4.25)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|u(r)|}{(1+r)^{3}}\,r\,\mathrm{d}r<\infty,

the following representation of the fractional Laplacian

(-\Delta)^{1/2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is valid:

(4.26)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}u(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi r}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(u(r)-u(r\tau)+\frac{u(r)-u(r/\tau)}{\tau}\right)\mathcal{K}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where

(4.27)

\mathcal{K}(\tau):=2\pi\tau^{-2}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},1,\tau^{-2}),

see
[
15
]
*p. 246. Note that
\mathcal{K}(\tau)>0
and

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tau)

\displaystyle\sim(\tau-1)^{-2}\qquad\text{as}\qquad\tau\to 1^{+},

(4.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tau)

\displaystyle\sim\tau^{-2}\qquad\text{as}\qquad\tau\to+\infty,

so the kernel
\mathcal{K}(\tau)
is integrable as

\tau\to+\infty
, but it is
singular
as
\tau\to 1^{+}
.

Denote

(4.30)

\Phi_{u}(r,\tau):=u(r)-u(r\tau)+\frac{u(r)-u(r/\tau)}{\tau}.

Clearly,
\Phi_{u}(r,1)=0
. A direct computation shows that

(4.31)

\partial_{\tau}\Phi_{u}(r,1)=0,\quad\partial^{2}_{\tau}\Phi_{u}(r,1)=-2r^{2}\mathscr{L}u(r),

where the differential expression

(4.32)

\mathscr{L}u(r):=u^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{2}{r}u^{\prime}(r)

acts on
u(r)
as the radial Laplacian in 3D. In particular, the
integral in (
4.26
) converges as
\tau\to 1^{+}
.

We now define a barrier function
U\in C^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}})
such
that
U(r)
is monotone decreasing and

(4.33)

U(r)=\frac{1}{r\log(er)}\qquad\forall r>1.

Clearly, if
u(x):=U(|x|)
then
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. By
interpolation between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

(cf.
[
4
]
*Proposition 1.52) we also conclude that

u\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Lemma 4.7
.

There exists
R>2
such that

(4.34)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}U(r)\sim-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}\quad\text{for all }\,r>R.

Proof.

Our strategy is to split the representation in (
4.26
) into
three parts
\int_{1}^{2}+\int_{2}^{r}+\int_{r}^{\infty}
and then either estimate
each part from above and below or compute the integrals
explicitly, see (
4.46
) and (
4.47
).

For
r>2
we compute

(4.35)

\mathscr{L}U(r)=\frac{\log(e^{3}r)}{(r\log(er))^{3}}>0.

Next we claim that for all
r>2
the following inequalities hold:

(4.36)

\displaystyle\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle<U(r)\quad

\displaystyle\forall

\displaystyle\tau\in[r,+\infty),

(4.37)

\displaystyle\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle\leq 0\quad

\displaystyle\forall

\displaystyle\tau\in[1,r],

(4.38)

\displaystyle\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle\geq-4r^{2}\mathscr{L}U(r)(\tau-1)^{2}\quad

\displaystyle\forall

\displaystyle\tau\in[1,2].

We begin by noting that by monotonicity and positivity of
U
we
have

(4.39)

\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)<U(r),

which yields (
4.36
). To deduce (
4.37
), observe that for

r>2
and
1\leq\tau\leq r
we have

(4.40)

\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)=\frac{1}{r}\left\{\frac{1}{\log(er)}-\frac{1}{\log(er/\tau)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\log(er)}-\frac{1}{\log(er\tau)}\right)\right\}.

It is elementary to see that (
4.37
) is equivalent to

(4.41)

\frac{\log(er\tau)}{\log(er/\tau)}\geq\frac{1}{\tau},

the latter is true for any
r>1
and
\tau\in[1,r]
(since in this
range the left hand side is bigger than one).

To derive (
4.38
), let
A:=\log(er)
and observe that for
r>2
and

\tau\in[1,2]
we have
A>1
and

(4.42)

r\,\left\{\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)+4\mathscr{L}U(r)r^{2}(\tau-1)^{2}\right\}=\\
=\frac{1}{\log(er)}-\frac{1}{\log(er)-\log(\tau)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\log(er)}-\frac{1}{\log(er)+\log(\tau)}\right)+\frac{4\log(e^{3}r)}{(\log(er))^{3}}(\tau-1)^{2}\\
=\frac{1}{A}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{A-\log(\tau)}+\frac{1}{\tau(A+\log(\tau))}\right)+\frac{4(2+A)}{A^{3}}(\tau-1)^{2}\\
\geq\frac{1}{A}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{A-\log(\tau)}+\frac{1}{\tau(A+\log(\tau))}\right)+\frac{4}{A^{2}}(\log(\tau))^{2},

where we used the fact that
\log(\tau)<\tau-1
for
\tau\geq 1
. It
is convenient to substitute
\tau=e^{x}
, where
x\in[0,\log(2)]
.
Then, taking into account that
A\geq\log(2e)>2x
we rewrite the
right-hand side of (
4.42
) as

(4.43)

\frac{1}{A}-\frac{1}{A-x}+e^{-x}\left(\frac{1}{A}-\frac{1}{A+x}\right)+\frac{4x^{2}}{A^{2}}={x\over A}\left\{-{1\over A-x}+{e^{-x}\over A+x}+{4x\over A}\right\}\\
\geq\frac{x}{A^{2}}\left\{-1-\frac{2x}{A}+(1-x)\left(1-\frac{x}{A}\right)+4x\right\}\\
\geq\frac{3x^{2}}{A^{2}}\left\{1-\frac{1}{A}\right\}\geq 0\quad\text{for all $x\in[0,\log(2)]$}.

Now, for
r>2
, we compute explicitly, using again the
substitution
\tau=e^{x}
and a standard asymptotic expansion of the
integral:

(4.44)

\int_{2}^{r}r\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)\tau^{-2}d\tau=\int_{\log(2)}^{z^{-1}}\left(\frac{xz^{2}e^{-x}}{(z+1)(xz+z+1)}+\frac{z}{(x-1)z-1}+\frac{z}{z+1}\right)e^{-x}dx\\
=-\frac{7+6\log(2)}{16}z^{2}+O(z^{3})\qquad\text{as}\qquad z\to 0^{+},

where we defined
z:=1/\log(r)
. Similarly, we have

(4.45)

\left|\int_{r}^{\infty}r\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)\tau^{-2}d\tau\right|\leq\int_{z^{-1}}^{\infty}\frac{ze^{-x}}{z+1}dx+U(0)e^{z^{-1}}\int_{z^{-1}}^{\infty}e^{-2x}dx\leq(1+\tfrac{1}{2}U(0))e^{-z^{-1}}.

Therefore, taking into account (
4.29
) and using (
4.36
),
(
4.37
) and (
4.44
), for
r>2
we estimate

(4.46)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}U(r)\lesssim r^{-1}\int_{2}^{r}\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)\tau^{-2}d\tau+r^{-1}U(r)\int_{r}^{\infty}\mathcal{\tau}^{-2}d\tau,\\
\sim-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}+\frac{1}{r^{3}\log(r)}\sim-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}\qquad\text{as}\qquad r\to\infty.

To deduce a lower estimate, we use (
4.38
), (
4.45
) and
(
4.44
) to obtain

(4.47)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}U(r)\gtrsim-r\mathscr{L}U(r)+r^{-1}\left(\int_{2}^{r}+\int_{r}^{\infty}\right)\Phi_{U}(r,\tau)\tau^{-2}d\tau,\\
\gtrsim-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{3}}\sim-\frac{1}{r^{2}(\log(r))^{2}}\qquad\text{as}\qquad r\to\infty,

which completes the proof.
∎

4.5.
Decay estimate

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Assume that
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
,
V\neq 0
, and for some
R>0
and
C>0
,

(4.48)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{2}(\log|x|)^{2}}\quad\text{for }|x|\geq R.

Then the unique solution
u_{V}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of (
4.3
) satisfies

(4.49)

0<u_{V}(x)\leq V(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

and

(4.50)

u_{V}(x)\sim\frac{1}{|x|\log|x|}\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

In particular,
u_{V}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Remark 4.3
.

We do not assume radial symmetry of
V
or
u_{V}
. The assumptions

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
and
V\neq 0
ensure the positivity of

u_{V}
, while the upper bound (
4.48
) controls the
logarithmic decay rate (
4.50
). The bound
(
4.48
) together with
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
implicitly
necessitates that
V
is positive in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

(4.51)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}2\pi|x|V(x)=\|(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

see Lemma
4.9
below.

Proof.

Note that
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
implies that
V\geq 0
(this
could be seen similarly to the argument in the proof of Proposition

4.2
but without the nonlinear term). Then the upper bound
in (
4.49
) follows by Corollary
4.5
. Next recall that

4.6.
Charge estimate

In the case of the standard Newtonian kernel
|x|^{-1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
it
is well–known that for a nonnegative
f\in L^{1}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

|x|^{-1}*f=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}|x|^{-1}+o(|x|^{-1})
as

|x|\to\infty
, cf.
[
32
]
for a discussion. The result becomes nontrivial when we
consider the convolution kernel
|x|^{-1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or more
generally the Riesz kernel
|x|^{-(N-\alpha)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with

\alpha\in(0,N)
. It is known that if
\alpha\in(1,N)
and

f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is positive radially symmetric then

|x|^{-(N-\alpha)}*f=O(|x|^{-(N-\alpha)})
, see
[
32
]
*Theorem
5(i). The same remains valid if
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
f
is in
addition monotone decreasing, see
[
13
]
*Lemma 2.2
(4). However, without assuming monotonicity of
f
,

|x|^{-(N-\alpha)}*f
with
\alpha\in(0,1]
could have arbitrary fast
growth at infinity
[
32
]
*Theorem 5.

We are going to show that if
f
is monotone non-increasing and decays
faster than
|x|^{-2}
then the sharp asymptotics of
|x|^{-1}*f
on

\mathbb{R}^{2}
is recovered. The proof is easily extended to Riesz kernels
with
N\geq 2
and
\alpha\in(0,N)
.

Lemma 4.9

(Asymptotic Newton’s type theorem)
.

Let
0\leq f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a function dominated by a radially
symmetric non-increasing function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that
satisfies

(4.58)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\varphi(x)|x|^{2}=0.

Then

(4.59)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y=\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}{|x|}+o(|x|^{-1})\quad\text{as
$|x|\to\infty$}.

Proof.

Fix
0\neq x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and decompose
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the union of

B=\{y:|y-x|<|x|/2\}
,
A=\{y\not\in B:|y|\leq|x|\}
,

C=\{y\not\in B:|y|>|x|\}
.

We want to estimate the quantity

(4.60)

\left|\int_{A\cup C}f(y)\Bigl(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\right|\leq\int_{A\cup C}f(y)\left|\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x|}\right|\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

Since
|x|/2\leq|x-y|\leq 2|x|
for all
y\in A
, by the Mean Value Theorem we have

(4.61)

\left|\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x|}\right|\leq\frac{4|y|}{|x|^{2}}\qquad(y\in A).

Thus

(4.62)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{A}f(y)\Bigl(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\right|&\leq\frac{4}{|x|^{2}}\int_{A}f(y)|y|\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.\end{split}

On the other hand, since
|x-y|>|x|/2
for all
y\in C
then

(4.63)

\left|\frac{1}{|x|}-\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right|\leq\frac{1}{|x|}\qquad(y\in C),

from which we compute that

(4.64)

\left|\int_{C}f(y)\Bigl(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|x|}\Bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\right|\leq\frac{1}{|x|}\int_{C}f(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y.

Then

(4.65)

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y-\frac{\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}{|x|}\right|\leq\\
\frac{4}{|x|^{2}}\int_{A}f(y)|y|\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y+\int_{B}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y+\frac{1}{|x|}\int_{B\cup C}f(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

Using (
4.58
), for
|x|\gg 2
we estimate

(4.66)

I_{1}=\frac{4}{|x|^{2}}\int_{|y|\leq|x|}f(y)|y|\,\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\leq\frac{8\pi}{|x|^{2}}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{|x|}\varphi(t)t^{2}\,dt}_{o(|x|)}=o(|x|^{-1})\qquad(|x|\to\infty).

Also using the monotonicity of
f
and
(
4.58
),
for
|x|\gg 2
we obtain

(4.67)

I_{2}=\int_{|y-x|\leq|x|/2}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}\,dy\leq\varphi(|x|/2)\int_{|z|\leq|x|/2}\frac{dz}{|z|}=\pi\varphi(|x|/2)|x|=o(|x|^{-1}).

Finally,
I_{3}=o(|x|^{-1})
as
|x|\to\infty
since
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
so the assertion follows.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume that the assumptions of Proposition
4.8
holds and

(4.68)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}2\pi|x|V(x)=Z>0.

Then
\|\rho_{V}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=Z
.

Proof.

According to (
3.23
), the minimizer
\rho_{V}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\cap C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies

(4.69)

\rho_{V}^{1/2}(x)=V(x)-U_{\rho_{V}}(x)\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

Taking into account (
2.15
), by Lemma
4.9

above we conclude that

(4.70)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}2\pi|x|U_{\rho_{V}}(x)=\|\rho_{V}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Then the assertion follows since
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|\rho_{V}^{1/2}(x)=0
.
∎

4.7.
Universality of decay

We next prove that in the case
V=V_{Z,d}
the behavior of

\rho_{V_{Z,d}}
for large
|x|
does not depend on the values of
Z

and
d
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
Z>0
,
d>0
and let
V=V_{Z,d}
as defined in (
2.3
).
Then

(4.71)

u_{V}(x)\simeq\frac{1}{|x|\log|x|}\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

Proof.

We start by noting that Proposition
4.8
applies to

V_{Z,d}
, see (
2.10
). To prove the sharp asymptotic
decay of the minimizer when
V=V_{Z,d}
, we use the idea in the
computation of Katsnelson
[
21
]
, also giving the latter
a precise mathematical meaning. To this end, we first note that since

\rho_{V}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have that
(
2.11
) holds. In terms of
u_{V}>0
defined in
(
2.13
), this equation reads

(4.72)

\displaystyle u_{V}(x)=V(x)-{1\over 2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{u_{V}^{2}(y)\over|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where we used the regularity of
u_{V}
and
V
. In turn, since

u_{V}(x)=u(|x|)
, applying Fubini’s theorem we obtain after an
explicit integration:

(4.73)

\displaystyle u(r)

\displaystyle={Z\over 2\pi\sqrt{d^{2}+r^{2}}}-{1\over 2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{u^{2}(r^{\prime})\,r^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\over\sqrt{r^{2}+{r^{\prime}}^{2}-2rr^{\prime}\cos\theta}}

(4.74)

\displaystyle={Z\over 2\pi\sqrt{d^{2}+r^{2}}}-{2\over\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}{r^{\prime}u^{2}(r^{\prime})\over r+r^{\prime}}K\left({2\sqrt{rr^{\prime}}\over r+r^{\prime}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}r^{\prime},

where
K(k)
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind
[
1
]
.

Proceeding as in
[
21
]
, we introduce a smooth bounded
function

(4.75)

\displaystyle F(t):=e^{t}u(e^{t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

which satisfies
F(\ln r)=ru(r)
. From (
4.50
) and the
boundedness of
u
we conclude that

(4.76)

\displaystyle F(t)\sim t^{-1}\quad\text{as $t\to+\infty$},

and
F(t)
decays exponentially as
t\to-\infty
. In particular,

F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then with the substitution
r=e^{t}
,
(
4.73
) written in terms of
F(t)
becomes

(4.77)

\displaystyle F(t)={Z\over 2\pi\sqrt{1+d^{2}e^{-2t}}}-{2\over\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{F^{2}(t^{\prime})\over 1+e^{t^{\prime}-t}}\,K\left({1\over\cosh{t^{\prime}-t\over 2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

We further introduce (with the opposite sign convention to that in
[
21
]
)

(4.78)

\displaystyle\phi(t):={2K\left({1\over\cosh{t\over 2}}\right)\over\pi(1+e^{-t})}-\theta(t),

where
\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function, and note that

\phi(t)
is a positive, exponentially decaying function as

t\to\pm\infty
, which is smooth, except for a logarithmic
singularity at
t=0
. Then, since
F(t)\to 0
as

t\to+\infty
by (
4.76
), passing to the limit using the
weak convergence of
\phi(t-\cdot)\rightharpoonup 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

t\to+\infty
and monotone convergence, we conclude that

(4.79)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}F^{2}(t)dt=\frac{Z}{2\pi}.

With this (
4.77
) becomes

(4.80)

\displaystyle F(t)={Z\left(1-\sqrt{1+d^{2}e^{-2t}}\right)\over 2\pi\sqrt{1+d^{2}e^{-2t}}}+\int_{t}^{\infty}F^{2}(t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(t-t^{\prime})F^{2}(t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}.

To conclude, we observe that in view of (
4.76
) we can estimate
the last term in (
4.80
) to be
O(t^{-2})
as
t\to+\infty
. Similarly, the first term gives an exponentially small
contribution for
t\to+\infty
and can, therefore, be absorbed into
the
O(t^{-2})
term as well. Thus we have

(4.81)

\displaystyle F(t)=G(t)+O(t^{-2}),\qquad G(t):=\int_{t}^{\infty}F^{2}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},

and it follows that
G(t)
satisfies for all
t
sufficiently large

(4.82)

\displaystyle{dG(t)\over dt}=-\left(G(t)+O(t^{-2})\right)^{2}.

In particular, using (
4.76
) we can further estimate for

t\gg 1
:

(4.83)

\displaystyle{dG(t)\over dt}=-G^{2}(t)\left(1+O(t^{-1})\right)^{2}.

Integrating this expression from some sufficiently large
t_{0}
then
gives

(4.84)

\displaystyle{1\over G(t)}-{1\over G(t_{0})}=t-t_{0}+O(\ln(t/t_{0})),\qquad t>t_{0}.

Finally, solving for
G(t)
and inserting it into (
4.81
)
results in

(4.85)

\displaystyle F(t)={1\over t+O(\ln t)}\qquad\text{as}\ t\to+\infty,

which yields the claim after converting back into the original variables.
∎

5.
Nonzero background charge

We now turn to the situation in which a net background charge
density
\bar{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}
is present, which is achieved
in graphene via back-gating. This leads to the modified TF-energy

[
28
]

{\mathcal{E}}_{\bar{\rho}}^{TF}(\rho)=\frac{2}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big(|\rho(x)|^{3/2}-|{\bar{\rho}}|^{3/2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\mathrm{sgn}({\bar{\rho}})|{\bar{\rho}}|^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big(\rho(x)-{\bar{\rho}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\\
-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\rho(x)-{\bar{\rho}})V(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x+\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(\rho(x)\!-\!{\bar{\rho}})(\rho(y)\!-\!{\bar{\rho}})}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y,

where
\rho(x)\to{\bar{\rho}}
sufficiently fast as

|x|\to\infty
. Since this energy is invariant with respect to

\rho\to-\rho,\quad{\bar{\rho}}\to-{\bar{\rho}},\quad V\to-V,

in the sequel we assume, without loss of generality, that
\bar{\rho}>0
.

5.1.
A representation of the energy functional

For a given charge density
\rho(x)
and
{\bar{\rho}}>0
, we
define

(5.1)

\displaystyle\phi:=\rho-\bar{\rho}.

Then, for
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the energy

{\mathcal{E}}^{TF}_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)
can be written as (with a slight abuse of
notation, in what follows we use the same letter to denote both the
energy as a function of
\rho
and that as a function of
\phi
)

(5.2)

{\mathcal{E}}_{\bar{\rho}}^{TF}(\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi(x))\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x+\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\phi(x)\phi(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y,

where

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi):=\tfrac{2}{3}|{\bar{\rho}}+\phi|^{3/2}-\tfrac{2}{3}{\bar{\rho}}^{3/2}-{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}\phi.

Clearly
\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex
C^{1}
–function of
\phi
with

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi)=|{\bar{\rho}}+\phi|^{1/2}\sgn({\bar{\rho}}+\phi)-{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2},

and
\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-{\bar{\rho}}\})
. The graphs of

\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)
and
\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi)
for
\bar{\rho}=1
are
presented in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1.
Plot of
\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)
and
\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi)
for
{\bar{\rho}}=1
.

Using elementary calculus one can see that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\frac{c|\phi|^{2}}{\sqrt{{\bar{\rho}}+|\phi|}}\leq\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)\leq\frac{C|\phi|^{2}}{\sqrt{{\bar{\rho}}+|\phi|}}\qquad(\phi\in\mathbb{R}),

for some universal
C>c>0
. This implies that for
{\bar{\rho}}>0
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Big\{\phi\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,:\,\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}<+\infty\Big\}=L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{\bar{\rho}}>0
. Then

\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}:L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}

is a strictly convex and weakly lower semi-continuous functional,
i.e.

(5.7)

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{n},\varphi\rangle\to\langle\phi,\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\implies\quad\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\liminf_{n}\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi_{n})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Proof.

The strict convexity of
\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
follows from the strict convexity of the function
\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Let
(\phi_{n})\subset L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a sequence that
converges strongly to
\phi
, i.e. there exist representations

\phi_{n}=f_{n}+g_{n}
and
\phi=f+g
such that

\|f_{n}-f\|_{L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\to 0
and
\|g_{n}-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\to 0
.
Then up to a subsequence
\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi_{n})\to\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)

a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. By Fatou’s lemma,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\liminf_{n}\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi_{n})\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

i.e., the sublevel sets of
\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
are
closed in the norm of
L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Using the
convexity of
\|\Psi_{\bar{\rho}}(\phi)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
, by Mazur’s theorem we conclude that all sublevels sets are also weakly
closed in
L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, i.e. (
5.7
)
holds.
∎

5.2.
Variational setup and the main result.

. The minimizer

\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

(5.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}(x))\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\varphi,V\rangle+\langle\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}},\varphi\rangle_{\mathring{H}^{-1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=0\qquad\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{\rho}}.

Proof.

The proofs of the existence and uniqueness of the minimizer (employing Lemma
5.1
), as well as
the derivation of the Euler–Lagrange equations (
5.12
) are small modifications of
the arguments in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, so we omit the
details. For the differentiability of the map
\Psi_{{\bar{\rho}}}
see

[
28
]
*Lemma 6.2.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

If, for instance,
\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{\rho}}\cap L^{4/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
then
(
5.12
) can be interpreted pointwise as

(5.13)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}(x))+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}(y)}{|x-y|}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y=V(x)\quad\text{a.e.~in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

However in general, the Euler–Lagrange equation for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\bar{\rho}}
should
be understood as

(5.14)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}})+U_{\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}}=V\quad\text{in
$\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$},

where

\phi_{\bar{\rho}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\bar{\rho}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+L^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and

U_{\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
is the potential of
\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}
defined
via (
3.5
).

In the rest of the section, under some additional assumptions on
V

we will use the equivalent half–Laplacian representation of
(
5.12
) to establish further regularity and decay properties of
the minimizer
\phi_{\bar{\rho}}
when
{\bar{\rho}}>0
. Our crucial observation is
that unlike in the case
{\bar{\rho}}=0
, for
{\bar{\rho}}>0
the minimizer
\phi_{\bar{\rho}}

has the same fast polynomial decay as the Green function of

(-\Delta)^{1/2}+1
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for all reasonably fast decaying
potentials
V
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
{\bar{\rho}}>0
,
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
\phi_{\bar{\rho}}
be the minimizer of

\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\rho}}
from Theorem
5.2
.

(i)

If
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
then

\phi_{\bar{\rho}}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(ii)

If additionally,
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
,
V\neq 0
, and for some

C>0
we have

(5.15)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\leq\frac{C}{(1+|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

then
\phi_{\bar{\rho}}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

(5.16)

\phi_{\bar{\rho}}(x)\sim\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

In particular,
\phi_{\bar{\rho}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

In the rest of this section we are going to sketch the proof of
Theorem
5.3
. We only emphasize the difference in the
asymptotic behaviour, other arguments that are similar to the case

{\bar{\rho}}=0
will be omitted.

5.3.
Half–Laplacian representation, regularity and decay

Let
{\bar{\rho}}>0
and
\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{\rho}}
be the minimizer of

{\mathcal{E}}_{\bar{\rho}}
. Introduce the substitution

(5.17)

\displaystyle u_{{\bar{\rho}}}:=\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi_{{\bar{\rho}}}).

Then (
5.12
) transforms into

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{{\bar{\rho}}}(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}x-\langle\varphi,V\rangle+\langle U_{S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u_{{\bar{\rho}}})},\varphi\rangle_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}}=0\qquad\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{\rho}},

where

(5.19)

\displaystyle S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u):=|{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}+u|({\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}+u)-{\bar{\rho}}\qquad(u\in\mathbb{R})

is the inverse function of
\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}
, so that

S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\Psi^{\prime}_{{\bar{\rho}}}(\phi))=\phi
, for all

\phi\in\mathbb{R}
. The graph of
S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u)
is shown in
Fig.
2
.

S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u)
for
{\bar{\rho}}=1

Proposition 5.4

(Equivalent PDE)
.

Let
{\bar{\rho}}>0
,
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
u_{\bar{\rho}}
be defined by
(
5.17
). Then
u_{\bar{\rho}}\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
and
u_{\bar{\rho}}
is the unique
solution of the equation

(5.20)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{1/2}u+S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u)=(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in ${\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$}.

Moreover,

(5.21)

\displaystyle-v_{-}\leq u_{{\bar{\rho}}}\leq v_{+},

where
v_{\pm}\geq 0
are solutions of

(-\Delta)^{1/2}v_{\pm}=((-\Delta)^{1/2}V)^{\pm}
in
{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
.
The uniqueness of the solution and the bound (
5.21
) follows from an extension of the comparison principle of Lemma
4.3
to the case of a monotone increasing function
S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u)
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
{\bar{\rho}}>0
and
V\in{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Assume that

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0
and

V\neq 0
. Then
u_{\bar{\rho}}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

u_{\bar{\rho}}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

(5.22)

u_{\bar{\rho}}(x)\gtrsim\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}\quad\text{as
}|x|\to\infty.

If, in addition, for some
C>0
,

(5.23)

(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\leq\frac{C}{(1+|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

then

(5.24)

u_{\bar{\rho}}(x)\sim\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty.

In particular,
u_{\bar{\rho}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

Represent (
5.20
) as

(5.25)

\displaystyle\big((-\Delta)^{1/2}+2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}\big)u_{{\bar{\rho}}}+s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u_{{\bar{\rho}}})=(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\quad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

where
s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(t)=S_{{\bar{\rho}}}(t)-2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}t
and observe that

s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(t)=t^{2}
for
|t|<{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}
small, while

s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(t)\sim|t|t
for
t
large.
In particular, in view of (
5.21
) we have
u_{{\bar{\rho}}}\geq 0

and
u_{{\bar{\rho}}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then for a sufficiently large
c>0
,

(5.26)

\displaystyle\big((-\Delta)^{1/2}+2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}+c\big)u_{{\bar{\rho}}}=c-s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(u_{{\bar{\rho}}})+(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\geq 0\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

This implies
u_{{\bar{\rho}}}\in H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
u_{\bar{\rho}}>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and additionally,

(5.27)

u_{\bar{\rho}}(x)\gtrsim\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}\quad\text{as }|x|\to\infty,

cf.
[
28
]
*Lemma 7.1 for a similar argument.

To derive the upper bound on
u_{\bar{\rho}}
, consider the dipole type family of barriers

W_{Z,\lambda}(|x|):=\frac{Z}{2\pi(1+|\lambda x|^{2})^{3/2}}

and note that using (
4.21
), scaling,
s_{\bar{\rho}}(W_{Z,\lambda})\geq 0
and (
5.24
), we obtain

(5.28)

\big((-\Delta)^{1/2}+2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}\big)W_{Z,\lambda}+s_{{\bar{\rho}}}(W_{Z,\lambda})-(-\Delta)^{1/2}V\\
\geq\frac{Z\lambda(2-|\lambda x|^{2})}{2\pi(1+|\lambda x|^{2})^{5/2}}+\frac{2Z{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}(1+|\lambda x|^{2})}{2\pi(1+|\lambda x|^{2})^{5/2}}-\frac{C}{(1+|x|^{2})^{3/2}}\geq 0\quad\text{in
$\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

provided that we choose
\lambda=2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}
and
Z\gg 1
sufficiently large.
Then
u_{\bar{\rho}}\leq W_{Z,2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by an extension of the comparison principle of Lemma
4.3
to the equation (
5.25
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Follows from Proposition

5.5
using the explicit representation

\phi_{\bar{\rho}}=S_{\bar{\rho}}(u_{\bar{\rho}})=2{\bar{\rho}}^{1/2}u_{\bar{\rho}}+u_{\bar{\rho}}^{2}
in (
5.19
),
which is valid since
u_{\bar{\rho}}>0
.
∎
