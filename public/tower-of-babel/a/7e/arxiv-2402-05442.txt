Triangular solutions to the reflection equation for U q ​ ( s ​ l n ^ ) U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})
Dmitry Kolyaskin, Vladimir V Mangazeev

Abstract
We study solutions of the reflection equation related to the quantum affine algebra U q ​ ( s ​ l n ^ ) U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}}) . First, we explain how to construct a family of stochastic integrable vertex models with fixed boundary conditions. Then, we construct upper- and lower-triangular solutions of the reflection equation related to symmetric tensor representations of U q ​ ( s ​ l n ^ ) U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}}) with arbitrary spin. We also prove the star-star relation for the Boltzmann weights of the Ising-type model, conjectured by Bazhanov and Sergeev, and use it to verify certain properties of the solutions obtained.



Keywords
: integrable systems, stochastic processes,
Yang-Baxter equation, reflection equation, star-star relation

2
Reflection equation and commuting transfer-matrices

We start with the Yang-Baxter equation

R_{12}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)R_{13}\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)R_{23}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)=R_{23}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)R_{13}\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)R_{12}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right),

(2.1)

whose solutions
R_{12}\left(x\right)\in\text{End}\left(V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\right)

act non-trivially in the tensor product of two
vector spaces and describe the behaviour of the system in the bulk.
We assume that the
R
-matrix satisfies the following properties:

•

R^{t_{2}}_{12}\left(x\right)
is non-degenerate, i.e. one can correctly define the matrix

\widetilde{R}_{12}\left(x\right)=\left(\left(R_{12}^{t_{2}}\left(x\right)\right)^{-1}\right)^{t_{2}},

(2.2)

where
t_{i}
denotes transposition with respect to
V_{i}
, i.e. in the matrix components
it has the form
\left[R_{12}^{t_{2}}\left(x\right)\right]_{i,j}^{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\left[R_{12}\left(x\right)\right]_{i,j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime},j}.

•

Regularity condition

R_{12}\left(1\right)=P_{12},

(2.3)

where we assume that
V_{1}\cong V_{2}\equiv V
. Here
P
is a permutation operator,
acting in a usual way
P(v\otimes w)=w\otimes v
, where
v,w\in V
.

•

Unitarity condition

R_{12}(x)R_{21}\left(x^{-1}\right)=f(x)I\otimes I,

(2.4)

where
f(x)
is a scalar function satisfying
f(x)=f(x^{-1})
. In fact, one can check that if
V_{1}\cong V_{2}
,
then the last property directly follows from the regularity condition (
2.3
) and the Yang-Baxter equation (
2.1
)
(see, for example,
[
29
]
).

•

Crossing unitarity condition

M_{1}R_{12}^{t_{1}}(x)M_{1}^{-1}R_{21}^{t_{1}}\left(\left(rx\right)^{-1}\right)=g(x)I\otimes I,

(2.5)

where
r\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
M\in\text{End}(V)
is a constant matrix. Note that the crossing unitarity
is a stronger condition than (
2.2
)
[
30
,
24
]
, since the expression

\widetilde{R}_{21}\left(x\right)=\dfrac{1}{g(1/(rx))}\left(M^{t_{1}}_{1}\right)^{-1}R_{12}\left(1/(rx)\right)M^{t_{1}}_{1}

(2.6)

directly follows from (
2.5
). In particular, if we consider the
R
-matrix as an intertwiner of
finite-dimensional
U_{q}(\widehat{\mathfrak{g}})
modules, then it follows from the quantum group arguments
that conditions of unitarity (
2.4
) and crossing unitarity (
2.5
) are satisfied
[
31
,
32
]
.
In this instance,
M=q^{-2\rho}
,
r=q^{h^{\vee}},
where
\rho
is a half-sum of positive roots, and
h^{\vee}

is a dual Coxeter number of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}.
In what follows, we will focus on

\mathfrak{g}=sl_{n}(\mathbb{C})
case, and as we will see in the next chapter, the matrix
M
is diagonal and
satisfies an additional property

\left[M\otimes M,R_{12}(x)\right]=0.

(2.7)

Now we come to describing the boundaries: for a given solution to the Yang-Baxter equation (
2.1
),
we define the so-called reflection equation

R_{12}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)K_{1}\left(x\right)R_{21}\left(xy\right)K_{2}\left(y\right)=K_{2}\left(y\right)R_{12}\left(xy\right)K_{1}\left(x\right)R_{21}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)

(2.8)

and the dual reflection equation

\widetilde{K}_{2}\left(y\right)\widetilde{R}_{21}(xy)\widetilde{K}_{1}\left(x\right)R_{21}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)=R_{12}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\widetilde{K}_{1}\left(x\right)\widetilde{R}_{12}(xy)\widetilde{K}_{2}\left(y\right),

(2.9)

where the matrix
\widetilde{R}_{12}(x)
is defined in (
2.2
). The solutions
K_{i}\left(x\right),\widetilde{K}_{i}\left(x\right)

act non-trivially on
V_{i}

(i=1,2)
and describe the right and left boundaries, respectively. Knowing the solution
to the reflection equation (
2.8
) and using the commutativity property (
2.7
), the solution to
the dual reflection equation (
2.9
) can be expressed in the form

\widetilde{K}_{1}(x)=M^{-1}K\left(\dfrac{1}{r^{1/2}x}\right).

(2.10)

Following
[
18
,
33
]
, we can also consider another reflection equation related to
the inverse
R
-matrix
R_{21}\left(x^{-1}\right)\cong R_{12}^{-1}\left(x\right)
:

R_{21}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\bar{K}_{1}\left(x\right)R_{12}\left(\frac{1}{xy}\right)\bar{K}_{2}\left(y\right)=\bar{K}_{2}\left(y\right)R_{21}\left(\frac{1}{xy}\right)\bar{K}_{1}\left(x\right)R_{12}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).

(2.11)

It is stated that if
\bar{K}(x)
is a solution to equation (
2.11
), then the expression

\widetilde{K}_{1}(x)=\operatorname{tr}_{0}\left(\bar{K}_{0}\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)\widetilde{R}_{01}\left(x^{2}\right)P_{01}\right)

(2.12)

solves the dual reflection equation (
2.9
). The proof of this fact is quite technical, and
we provide it in
A
for the reader’s convenience.
The mapping (
2.12
) defines an isomorphism between the solutions to
the dual reflection equation (
2.9
) and
the equation (
2.11
) (see
[
18
]
for this and other isomorphisms):
the inverse mapping has the form

\bar{K}_{1}(x)=\operatorname{tr}_{0}\left(\widetilde{K}_{0}\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)R_{01}\left(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\right)P_{01}\right),

(2.13)

which we also prove in
A
.

Substituting
y=1
into the reflection equation (
2.8
), we get

[R_{12}(x)K_{1}(x)R_{21}(x),K_{2}(1)]=0

(2.14)

which should be true for any
x
. Apparently, the only reasonable solution to (
2.14
) is
K(1)\sim I
.
In this paper we shall always assume the normalization

K(1)=\it{I}.

(2.15)

Solutions to the reflection equation (
2.8
) and the dual reflection equation (
2.9
)
allow us to construct a double-row transfer matrix

T(x)=\operatorname{tr}_{0}\left[\widetilde{K}_{0}\left(x\right)R_{01}\left(x\right)\dots R_{0N}\left(x\right)K_{0}\left(x\right)R_{N0}\left(x\right)\dots R_{10}\left(x\right)\right].

(2.16)

Transfer-matrices (
2.16
) form a one-parameter family of mutually commuting quantities:

\left[T(x),T(y)\right]=0,

(2.17)

and this property implies the integrability of the model. The proof of (
2.17
) can be found in
[
18
]
.

Taking into account (
2.3
) and (
2.15
), we obtain

T(1)=\operatorname{tr}{\bigl[}\widetilde{K}\left(1\right){\bigr]}\,\boldsymbol{\it{I}}.

(2.18)

Finally, we can calculate the Hamiltonian of the integrable system associated with the transfer-matrix (
2.16
)

H=-\dfrac{1}{4}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\ln T(x)\bigg|_{x=1}=B_{L}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N-1}H_{k,k+1}+B_{R},

(2.19)

where

B_{L}=\dfrac{1}{4}\dfrac{\bar{K}^{\prime}_{1}\left(1\right)}{\operatorname{tr}\widetilde{K}(1)},\quad H_{k,k+1}=-\dfrac{1}{2}R^{\prime}_{k,k+1}(1)P_{k,k+1},\quad B_{R}=-\dfrac{1}{4}{K}^{\prime}_{N}\left(1\right).

(2.20)

The operators
B_{L}
and
B_{R}
act only in the first and the
N
-th component of the Hilbert space. Let us notice that
the operator
B_{L}
at the left boundary is proportional to the derivative of the matrix
{\bar{K}}(x)

as can be easily seen from differentiating (
2.13
).

Now let us discuss under what conditions the Hamiltonian and the transfer-matrix define stochastic processes.

We say that an
M\times M
matrix
A_{ij}
is (left) stochastic if it satisfies the condition

\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_{ij}=1\mbox{ for any }j\>\mbox{ or }\>\langle\boldsymbol{1}|A=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|,\quad\langle\boldsymbol{1}|=\langle 1\ldots 1|,

(2.21)

that is,
\langle\boldsymbol{1}|
is a row vector whose components are all equal to 1.

If the transfer-matrix
T(x)
(
2.16
) is left stochastic,

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|T(x)=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|,

(2.22)

and matrix elements of
T(x)
are positive
then
T^{t}(x)
defines a discrete-time Markov process in a quantum space
V\otimes\ldots\otimes V
. The corresponding Hamiltonian
(
2.19
) satisfies the property

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|H=0

(2.23)

and the continuous-time Markov generator is defined by
M=-H^{t}
. The inverse statement is harder to establish and it is not always true.

First, we call matrices
R_{12}(x)
,
K(x)
and
\bar{K}(x)
stochastic if they are left stochastic

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|R_{12}(x)=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|,\quad\langle\boldsymbol{1}|K(x)=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|,\quad\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\bar{K}(x)=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|.

(2.24)

Differentiating (
2.24
) with respect to
x
and using (
2.19
-
2.20
), we conclude that

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|H=0.

(2.25)

Now let us remember that the Hamiltonian (
2.19
) commutes with the transfer-matrix

[T(x),H]=T(x)\,H-H\,T(x)=0.

(2.26)

Applying
\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|
from the left, we obtain

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|T(x)H=\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|H\,T(x)=0.

(2.27)

If the rank of the matrix
H
is
\mbox{dim}(V)^{N}-1
, then
\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|

is the only left zero vector of
H
and we conclude that

\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|T(x)=c(x)\langle\boldsymbol{1}|\otimes\ldots\otimes\langle\boldsymbol{1}|.

(2.28)

Dividing
T(x)
by a scalar factor
c(x)
, we obtain the condition (
2.22
). If the rank of
H
is smaller than

\mbox{dim}(V)^{N}-1
, we cannot say anything about the stochasticity of
T(x)
. Indeed, in the case of periodic boundary
conditions the hamiltonian may have additional zero modes and the transfer matrix is not stochastic in general.
However, in all cases with fixed boundary conditions, which we considered, the rank of the Hamiltonian
H
was
\mbox{dim}(V)^{N}-1

and this implies (
2.28
).

Let us also notice that we don’t know how to show the stochasticity of the transfer-matrix (
2.16
) directly from
(
2.24
). The main difficulty is that
T(x)
is defined in terms of
\widetilde{K}(x)
related to
\bar{K}(x)
via (
2.12
).

3
Stochastic
R
-matrix for
U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})

In this section we introduce the stochastic
R
-matrix
related to the symmetric tensor representations of the quantum affine algebra
U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})
and consider its basic properties.

First, we start by introducing index notations. We denote by
\boldsymbol{i}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}
, the
m
-component set of non-negative integers

i_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

k=1,\dots m
. We also define some operations on these sets:

|\boldsymbol{a}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k},\qquad(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}b_{k},\qquad Q(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{1\leq l<k\leq m}a_{l}b_{k},

(3.1)

where
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
. The permutation
\tau
is defined as

\tau\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\right\}=\left\{i_{m},i_{m-1},\ldots,i_{1}\right\}.

(3.2)

We will also need one more operation on indices: for a given
J\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m}
,
|\boldsymbol{i}|\leq J
,
we introduce the mapping

\sigma\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\right\}=\left\{i_{2},i_{3},\ldots,i_{m},J-|\boldsymbol{i}|\right\}.

(3.3)

For a positive integer
J\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 2
, we define a finite-dimensional vector space
V_{J}^{(n)}

with basis vectors having the form

|\boldsymbol{i}\rangle=|i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1}\rangle,\quad|\boldsymbol{i}|\leq J,

(3.4)

(do not confuse this notation with the row vectors from the previous section). The dimension of the space
V_{J}^{(n)}
is
\binom{J+n-1}{n-1}
.
We will also use an equivalent notation for the basis vectors of
V_{J}^{(n)}
:

|\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}\rangle=|i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1},i_{n}\rangle,\quad|\bar{\boldsymbol{i}}|=J,

(3.5)

where we extended the vector
|\boldsymbol{i}\rangle
by adding an additional component
i_{n}=J-|\boldsymbol{i}|
.
The introduced vector space naturally inherits the structure of finite-dimensional symmetric tensor representations
of the quantum affine algebra
U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})

[
14
]
. For further purposes, it will be essential for us
to consider a realization of the symmetric tensor representation of
sl_{n}(\mathbb{C})
Lie algebra on
V_{J}^{(n)}
:

e_{i}|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle=\alpha_{i+1}|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{i+1}\rangle,\quad f_{i}|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle=\alpha_{i}|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{i+1}\rangle,\quad h_{i}|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle=(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i+1})|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle,

(3.6)

where
e_{i}
,
f_{i}
and
h_{i}
(
i=1,\dots n-1
) are the standard
sl_{n}(\mathbb{C})
generators,

|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle=|\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}\rangle

as defined in (
3.5
) and
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
represents the vector with
1

at the
j
-th place and 0 otherwise. For what follows, we will need to find an expression for
the half-sum of positive roots
\rho
of
sl_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and a simple analysis shows that it has the form

2\rho|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle=\left(-2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\alpha_{k}+(n-1)J\right)|\boldsymbol{\bar{\alpha}}\rangle.

(3.7)

Now we discuss the stochastic
R
-matrix related to symmetric tensor representations,
see
[
14
,
27
]
for more details. For arbitrary positive integers
I,J\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce a linear operator

S_{I,J}(x)\in\operatorname{End}(V^{(n)}_{I}\otimes V^{(n)}_{J})
, whose action on the basis

\left|\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right\rangle\otimes\left|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}\right\rangle
(in terms of (
3.4
)) has the form

S_{I,J}(x)\left|\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\right\rangle\otimes\left|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}\left[S_{I,J}(x)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{i}\rangle\otimes|\boldsymbol{j}\rangle.

(3.8)

The matrix elements can be written in a compact way as

\displaystyle\left[S_{I,J}(x)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{j}}\Phi_{q^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{j}\mid\boldsymbol{m};\frac{q^{J-I}}{x^{2}},\frac{q^{-I-J}}{x^{2}}\right)\Phi_{q^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{n}\mid\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime};\frac{x^{2}}{q^{I+J}},q^{-2J}\right),

(3.9)

where we defined the function

\displaystyle\Phi_{q}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=q^{Q(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|}\frac{(\lambda;q)_{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|}\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda};q\right)_{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|-|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|}}{(\mu;q)_{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}}\prod_{s=1}^{n-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{l}\beta_{s}\\
\gamma_{s}\end{array}\right]_{q}

(3.10)

for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n-1}
and
\lambda
,
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, first introduced in
[
14
]
.
Here we use the standard notation for
q
-Pochhammer symbols

(x;q)_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(1-xq^{k}\right),&n>0,\\
1,&n=0,\\
\prod\limits_{k=0}^{-n-1}\left(1-xq^{n+k}\right)^{-1},&n<0,\end{cases}

(3.11)

and
q
-binomial coefficients

\left[\begin{array}[]{l}m\\
k\end{array}\right]_{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{(q;q)_{m}}{(q;q)_{k}(q;q)_{m-k}}.

(3.12)

Let us notice that the original parameter
q
of the quantum group
U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})
enters the expression for
the stochastic
R
-matrix only via
q^{2}
as indicated by the subscript of the function
\Phi_{q^{2}}
in (
3
).
Such notations were introduced in
[
14
]
and we prefer to follow them.

The introduced
R
-matrix was constructed in
[
27
]
from the corresponding 3D
R
-operators, where it was shown that
it satisfies the Yang-Baxter equation defined on
V^{(n)}_{I}\otimes V^{(n)}_{J}\otimes V^{(n)}_{K}

S_{I,J}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)S_{I,K}\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)S_{J,K}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)=S_{J,K}\left(\frac{y}{z}\right)S_{I,K}\left(\frac{x}{z}\right)S_{I,J}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right).

(3.13)

We can also obtain different symmetries of the
R
-matrix (
3
) from the symmetries of corresponding 3D
R
-matrix.
Before we describe them, we define a symmetric version of the
R
-matrix (
3
)

\left[\bar{R}_{I,J}(x)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}=x^{|\boldsymbol{i}|-|\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}|}q^{\left(\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}\right)-\left(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}\right)+J|\boldsymbol{i}|-I|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}|-Q(\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{i})+Q(\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime})}\left[S_{I,J}(x)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}.

(3.14)

One can check that in the case
n=2
and
I=J=1
, the
R
-matrix (
3
) corresponds
to the stochastic symmetric 6-vertex model

S_{12}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{q^{2}(x^{2}-1)}{q^{2}x^{2}-1}&\frac{(q^{2}-1)x^{2}}{q^{2}x^{2}-1}&0\\
0&\frac{(q^{2}-1)}{q^{2}x^{2}-1}&\frac{(x^{2}-1)}{q^{2}x^{2}-1}&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right).

(3.15)

It will be also convenient to represent the function
\Phi
from
(
3.10
) in another form

\Phi_{q^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda^{2},\mu^{2})=\dfrac{\mu^{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|}}{\lambda^{|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}}\dfrac{V_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},0)V_{\mu/\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{V_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\beta},0)},

(3.16)

where the function
V_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\right)
is defined as

V_{x}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})=q^{2Q(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})-Q(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a})-Q(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{b})}\left(\frac{q}{x}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{a}|-|\boldsymbol{b}|}\frac{\left(x^{2};q^{2}\right)_{|\boldsymbol{a}|-|\boldsymbol{b}|}}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(q^{2};q^{2}\right)_{a_{i}-b_{i}}}.

Another model

In this section we will not impose the requirement
I,J\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e the
R
-matrix (
3
)
acts in the tensor product of infinite dimensional modules with basis vectors having the form

|\boldsymbol{i}\rangle=|i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1}\rangle,\quad i_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(7.1)

The
R
-matrix (
3
) degenerates when we choose the spectral parameter as
x=q^{(J-I)/2}

[
27
,
14
]
:

\left[S_{I,J}\left(q^{(J-I)/2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}\Phi_{q^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{i}\mid\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime};q^{-2I},q^{-2J}\right),

(7.2)

which suggests to define a new stochastic non-difference type
R
-matrix

\left[S_{12}(x,y)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}\Phi_{q^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{i}\mid\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime};x,y\right).

(7.3)

Now we consider
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
as spectral parameters and the Yang-Baxter equation takes the form

S_{12}\left(x,y\right)S_{13}\left(x,z\right)S_{23}\left(y,z\right)=S_{23}\left(y,z\right)S_{13}\left(x,z\right)S_{12}\left(x,y\right),

(7.4)

which directly follows from (
3.13
).
It is easy to see that the internal sums in (
7.4
)
contain only a finite number of terms, so there is no problem with convergence.

For a given solution to the Yang-Baxter equation (
7.3
), we define
the reflection equation of a non-difference type

S_{12}(x,y)K_{1}(x,\bar{x})S_{21}(y,\bar{x})K_{2}(y,\bar{y})=K_{2}(y,\bar{y})S_{12}(x,\bar{y})K_{1}(x,\bar{x})S_{21}(\bar{y},\bar{x}).

(7.5)

In
[
24
]
it was suggested that this equation
admits an upper-triangular stochastic solution of the form

K(x,\bar{x})_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{l}}=\Phi_{q^{2}}({\boldsymbol{j}}\mid{\boldsymbol{l}};z^{2}x,z^{2}\bar{x}),

(7.6)

where
z\in\mathbb{C}
is an arbitrary parameter
and there is no constraint between spectral parameters
x
and
\bar{x}

(see the equation (5.10) from
[
24
]
).

Here we give the proof of this fact suggested in
[
38
]
, which is based on the star-star relation
(
4.1
) and an orthogonality property:

\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{m}}x^{|\boldsymbol{m}|-|\boldsymbol{b}|}y^{|\boldsymbol{m}|-|\boldsymbol{a}|}V_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{m}\right)V_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{b}\right)=V_{xy}\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\right).

(7.7)

The last equality directly follows from the relation
(
B.6
) and the
q
-Vandermonde summation formula (
B.2
). We start the proof
by writing the reflection equation (
7.5
) in index notation

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}}\left[S_{12}\left(x^{2},y^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}}}\left[K_{1}\left(x^{2},\bar{x}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}}\left[S_{21}\left(y^{2},\bar{x}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime\prime}}}\left[K_{2}\left(y^{2},\bar{y}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime\prime}}}=

(7.8)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}}\left[K_{2}\left(y^{2},\bar{y}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}}}\left[S_{12}\left(x^{2},\bar{y}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}}\left[K_{1}\left(x^{2},\bar{x}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}}\left[S_{21}\left(\bar{y}^{2},\bar{x}^{2}\right)\right]_{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}}^{\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime\prime}},\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime\prime}}},

where, as usual, the summation goes over repeated indices. Then we introduce new external indices

\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{a},\quad\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{b},\quad\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{a},\quad\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{e},

(7.9)

summation indices for the left-hand side

\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a},\quad\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{2}-\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{2}-\boldsymbol{e}

(7.10)

\boldsymbol{i^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{2}-\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{j^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{2}-\boldsymbol{b},\quad\boldsymbol{i^{\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{j^{\prime\prime}}=\boldsymbol{d}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}.

(7.11)

After canceling out similar factor on both sides,
the reflection equation in terms of the functions

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{d}_{2}}\frac{\bar{x}^{|\boldsymbol{c}|}\bar{y}^{\left|\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right|}}{x^{\left|\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right|}y^{|\boldsymbol{f}|}}\frac{V_{yz}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{e}\right)V_{\frac{\bar{x}}{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{c}\right)V_{\frac{\bar{y}}{y}}\left(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right)}{V_{z\bar{y}}(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{e})}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{d}_{1}}\frac{V_{\frac{y}{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}\right)V_{xz}\left(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)V_{\frac{\bar{x}}{y}}\left(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)}{V_{z\bar{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)}=

(7.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{d}_{2}}\frac{\bar{x}^{\left|\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right|}\bar{y}^{|\boldsymbol{c}|}}{x^{|\boldsymbol{f}|}y^{\left|\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right|}}\frac{V_{yz}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b})V_{\frac{\bar{x}}{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\right)V_{\frac{\bar{y}}{y}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{c}\right)}{V_{z\bar{y}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{b}\right)}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{d}_{1}}\frac{V_{\frac{\bar{y}}{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}\right)V_{xz}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)V_{\frac{\bar{x}}{\bar{y}}}\left(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)}{V_{z\bar{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\right)}.

and

which ends the proof.

Note that

is not invertible, and we cannot define

(7.15)

From (

8
Conclusion

In this paper we constructed triangular solutions for the reflection equation corresponding to symmetric
tensor representations of
U_{q}(\widehat{sl_{n}})
.
It is quite remarkable that all constructed solutions are built from the same function
\Phi_{q}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda,\mu)

defined in (
3.10
).
As a result, both reflection and dual reflection equations (
5.1
),
(
6.1
) reduce to multiple sum identities
among the
\Phi
-functions (or
V
-functions (
3.17
)). It would be natural to expect that
(
5.1
),
(
6.1
) can be proven by a repeated application of the star-star relation
(
4.1
). However,
we failed to find a correct sequence of the star-star relations which would prove these equations.
This is the reason why we introduced the
L
-operators (
3.31
) and (
3.32
) to prove that
the solution (
5.7
) solves the reflection equation (
5.12
