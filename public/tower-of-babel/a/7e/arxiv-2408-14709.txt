L 2 L^{2} -Sobolev space bijectivity and existence of global solutions for the matrix nonlinear Schrödinger equations
Yuan Li, Xinhan Liu, Engui Fan ∗,

Abstract
We consider the Cauchy problem to the general defocusing and focusing p × q p\times q matrix nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equations with initial data allowing arbitrary-order poles and spectral singularities. By establishing the L 2 L^{2} -Sobolev space bijectivity of the direct and inverse scattering transforms associated with a ( p + q ) × ( p + q ) (p+q)\times(p+q) matrix spectral problem, we prove that both defocusing and focusing matrix NLS equations are globally well-posed in the weighted Sobolev space H 1 , 1 ​ ( ℝ ) H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}) .

The matrix NLS equation (

Ablowitz et al.

We have to emphasize that if

\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\partial_{t}q_{1}+\partial_{x}^{2}q_{1}-2\sigma q_{1}\left(|q_{1}|^{2}+2|q_{0}|^{2}\right)-2\sigma q_{0}^{2}q_{-1}^{*}=0,\\
&i\partial_{t}q_{-1}+\partial_{x}^{2}q_{-1}-2\sigma q_{-1}\left(|q_{-1}|^{2}+2|q_{0}|^{2}\right)-2\sigma q_{0}^{2}q_{1}^{*}=0,\quad\sigma=\pm 1,\\

When

The
L^{2}
-Sobolev space bijectivity of the inverse scattering transform for the
(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix spectral problem is hard to establish because which involves a lot of matrix estimates and matrix calculations. The first obstacle is how to define the appropriate scattering data. If the scattering data are defined as scalar form like
2\times 2
or
3\times 3
case, then we would be dealing with relations between
(p+q)^{2}
scattering data, the difficulties of which are unimaginable. An efficient way to do this is to write the
(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix in the form of a
2\times 2
block matrix. However in this case the scattering data are matrix valued functions so how to guarantee the invertibility of the scattering data
\boldsymbol{A}(k),\boldsymbol{D}(k)
and build the unique solvable RH problem becomes another obstacle. We discuss these problems in two cases. (i) For the defocusing case, we find that the matrix scattering data
\boldsymbol{A}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{D}(k)
are naturally invertible by using the properties of self-adjoint operator and the symmetry of the block scattering matrix
S(k)
. Not only that, but the latter makes the jump matrix

satisfy the conditions of vanishing lemma
[
26
]
.
(ii) For the focusing case, the corresponding problems become very complicated. Unlike the defocusing case,
\boldsymbol{A}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{D}(k)
are not naturally invertible so we use Zhou’s techniques to define a new function
\boldsymbol{M}(x,k)
to avoid any spectral restriction on the initial data. However, the computation of the jump matrix
V(k)
along the contour
\Gamma
(Figure
1

(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix, which can neither be written directly (like
2\times 2

3\times 3

(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix as a block matrix and taking full advantage of the relation and symmetry of the eigenfunctions and the block scattering matrix. In addition, the contour
\Gamma
has self-intersections therefore we have to verify the matching conditions at the intersections involving the high-order triangular matrix
V_{\pm}(k)
.

1.3
Notations

Let us now recall the definition of the weighted Sobolev space mentioned above. The weighted Sobolev space
H^{k,j}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

H^{k,j}(\mathbb{R})=H^{k}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2,j}(\mathbb{R}),

(1.11)

where
H^{k}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the usual Sobolev space with the norm

\|f\|_{H^{k}}:=\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{k}\hat{f}\|_{L^{2}},\quad\langle\cdot\rangle=\sqrt{1+|\cdot|^{2}},

here
\hat{f}
is the Fourier transform of
f
,
and
L^{2,j}(\mathbb{R})
is the weighted
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
space with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{2,j}}:=\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{j}f\|_{L^{2}}.

The
L^{p}
matrix norm of the matrix function
U
are denoted as

\|U\|_{L^{p}}:=\left\|\left|U\right|\right\|_{L^{p}},

where
|U|=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(U^{{\dagger}}U)}
.

Next, we describe the definition of
H^{k}(\partial\Omega)
introduced by Beals and Coifman
[
3
]
, which also can be found in
[
26
]
.

(i)

If
\Omega
is a connected open region and
\partial\Omega
is the nonsmooth boundary of
\Omega
, then
f\in H^{k}(\partial\Omega)
means that
f
is
H^{k}
on each smooth piece and
f
matches from two sides to the order
k-1
at each nonsmooth point.

(ii)

If
\Omega
is a disconnected region, then
f\in H^{k}(\partial\Omega)
implies that
f\in H^{k}(\partial\Omega_{i})
for every connected component
\Omega_{i}
of
\Omega
.

(iii)

The weighted Sobolev space
H^{k,j}(\partial\Omega)
is defined by

H^{k,j}(\partial\Omega)=H^{k}(\partial\Omega)\cap L^{2,j}(\partial\Omega).

(1.12)

The corresponding Sobolev norms are defined in the obvious way.

Direct Scattering Map

In this section, we state some main results on the direct scattering transform associated with the Cauchy problem (

2.1
(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix spectral problem

Let us rewrite the system (
1.4
) in the following equivalent form

m_{x}=-ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(m)+Um,

(2.1)

where

m(x,k)=\psi(x,k)e^{ixk\sigma_{3}},

and
\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(m)=[\sigma_{3},m]=\sigma_{3}m-m\sigma_{3}
.
This system can be further rewritten in the form of an integral equation

\displaystyle m(x,k)=e^{-ixk\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}\beta(k)+\int_{\delta}^{x}e^{i(y-x)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}U(y)m(y,k)dy,

(2.2)

where
e^{-ixk\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}\beta(k)=e^{-ixk\sigma_{3}}\beta(k)e^{ixk\sigma_{3}}
. The matrix function
\beta(k)
is independent of
x
, and the lower limit
\delta
can be chosen differently for the different entries of the matrix.

If
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then for every
k\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist unique bounded solutions
m^{\pm}(x,k)
of (
2.1
) satisfying the boundary conditions

m^{\pm}(x,k)\rightarrow I_{p+q},\quad x\rightarrow\pm\infty,

(2.3)

here
I_{p+q}
is the
(p+q)\times(p+q)
identity matrix. Moreover, the bounded solutions
m^{\pm}(x,k)
can be expressed by the Volterra integral equations

m^{\pm}(x,k)=I_{p+q}+\int_{\pm\infty}^{x}e^{i(y-x)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}U(y)m^{\pm}(y,k)dy,

(2.4)

and satisfy
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\|m^{\pm}(x,k)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
.
It is more necessary to write the modified eigenfunctions
m^{\pm}
in the form of the block matrix, so we set

m^{\pm}=\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}^{\pm}&m_{12}^{\pm}\\
m_{21}^{\pm}&m_{22}^{\pm}\end{bmatrix},

where
m_{11}^{\pm}
,
m_{12}^{\pm}
,
m_{21}^{\pm}
and
m_{22}^{\pm}
are the
p\times p
,
p\times q
,
q\times p
and
q\times q
matrices, respectively. All
(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrices in this work are written as
2\times 2
block matrices in this way. In addition, we denote

\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}^{\pm}\\
m_{21}^{\pm}\end{bmatrix}=m_{1}^{\pm}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{bmatrix}m_{12}^{\pm}\\
m_{22}^{\pm}\end{bmatrix}=m_{2}^{\pm},

that is,
m_{1}^{\pm}
and
m_{2}^{\pm}
are
(p+q)\times p
and
(p+q)\times q
matrices, respectively. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
m_{1}^{-}(x,k)
and
m_{2}^{+}(x,k)
are analytic for
\text{Im}k\geq 0
, while
m_{2}^{-}(x,k)
and
m_{1}^{+}(x,k)
are analytic for
\text{Im}k\leq 0
. The above result for
m^{\pm}
can be derived by constructing the Neumann series.

To explore more properties of
m^{\pm}
, we define the integral operator
K
by

(Kf)(x,k):=\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{i(y-x)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}U(y)f(y,k)dy,

(2.5)

then the integral equation (
2.4
) for
m^{-}
can be rewritten
in the operator form

m^{-}(x,k)=I_{p+q}+Km^{-}(x,k),

(2.6)

furthermore, we have

m^{-}(x,k)-I_{p+q}=(I-K)^{-1}KI_{p+q}(x,k).

(2.7)

The existence of the inverse operator
(I-K)^{-1}
is guaranteed by the following Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then the integral operator
I-K
is invertible in the space
L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and satisfies the following estimate

\displaystyle\left\|(I-K)^{-1}\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}\rightarrow L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}}\leq e^{\|U\|_{L^{1}}}.

(2.8)

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{Q}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
(KI_{p+q})(x,k)\in L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|(KI_{p+q})(x,k)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}}=\left\|\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{i(y-x)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}U(y)dy\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}}\lesssim\left\|U\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(2.9)

These two estimates also hold in the space
L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{-},L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.

Proof.

Let
f(x,k)
be a matrix function belonging to the space
L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
with the norm

\displaystyle\left\|f(x,k)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\|f(x,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, an application of Minkowski inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|(Kf)(x,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x}|U(y)|\left\|f(y,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}dy

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in(-\infty,x)}\left\|f(y,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}|U(y)|dy.

By iteration we can further obtain

\displaystyle\left\|(K^{n}f)(x,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\|U\right\|_{L^{1}}^{n}}{n!}\left\|f(x,k)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}},

which implies that the operator
I-K
is invertible in the space
L_{x}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and (
2.8
) holds.

It follows from Plancherel formula that
\forall

x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|(KI_{p+q})(x,k)\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}=&\left\|\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{i(y-x)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}U(y)dy\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}\\
\lesssim&\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}|U(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left\|U\right\|_{L^{2}},\end{aligned}

(2.10)

thus, the estimate (
2.9
) holds. The proof of (
2.9
) can also be found in
[
17
]
.
∎

\displaystyle\left\|(KI_{p+q})(x,k)\right\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}L_{k}^{2}}e^{\left\|U\right\|_{L^{1}}}

(I-K)(\partial_{k}m^{-})=\partial_{k}(KI_{p+q})+(\partial_{k}K)(m^{-}-I_{p+q}),

,

Using the fact that

(2.13)

For
(\partial_{k}K)(m^{-}-I_{p+q})

m^{-}-I_{p+q}\in L_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{-};L_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))

Using the operator equation (

Proof.

The integral equation (

t\in[-T,T]
, we have

\|v_{\pm}(t,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{L^{2,1}}\lesssim\|v_{\pm}(0,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{L^{2,1}},

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{k}v_{\pm}(t,k)\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|-4itk\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}v_{\pm}(0,k)+\partial_{k}v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 4T\left\|v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2,1}}+\left\|\partial_{k}v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2}},

thus

\|v_{\pm}(t,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{H^{1,1}}\lesssim\|v_{\pm}(0,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{H^{1,1}}.

(3.22)

Next, we show that
v(t,k)
is uniformly continuous with respect to time
t
in the sense of the norm
H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
. An application of the Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem implies that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\gamma>0
such that

\|v_{\pm}(0,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{H^{1,1}(|k|>\gamma)}<\varepsilon.

Then, for every
t_{1},t_{2}\in[-T,T]
, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{\pm}(t_{1},k)-v_{\pm}(t_{2},k)\|_{H^{1,1}(|k|>\gamma)}\lesssim\|v_{\pm}(0,k)-I_{p+q}\|_{H^{1,1}(|k|>\gamma)}<\varepsilon.

When
|k|\leq\gamma
, we can obtain the estimates

\displaystyle\left|e^{\pm 2i(t_{1}-t_{2})k^{2}\sigma_{3}}-I_{p+q}\right|=\left|2ik^{2}\sigma_{3}\int_{0}^{t_{1}-t_{2}}e^{\pm 2isk^{2}\sigma_{3}}ds\right|\lesssim\gamma^{2}\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\left|e^{\pm 2it_{1}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}t_{1}-e^{\pm 2it_{2}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}t_{2}\right|\lesssim(\gamma^{2}T+1)\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|.

(3.24)

By the relation (
3.21
) and (
3.23
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|v_{\pm}(t_{1},k)-v_{\pm}(t_{2},k)\right\|_{L^{2,1}(|k|\leq\gamma)}\lesssim

\displaystyle\left\|v_{\pm}(0,k)\left(e^{2i(t_{1}-t_{2})k^{2}\sigma_{3}}-I_{p+q}\right)\right\|_{L^{2,1}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\left(e^{-2i(t_{1}-t_{2})k^{2}\sigma_{3}}-I_{p+q}\right)v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2,1}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|\left\|v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2,1}(|k|\leq\gamma)}.

Make use of the estimates (
3.23
) and (
3.24
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{k}(v_{\pm}(t_{1},k)-v_{\pm}(t_{2},k))\right\|_{L^{2}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left\|t_{1}k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}v_{\pm}(0,k)\left(e^{2it_{1}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}-e^{2it_{2}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\left(e^{-2it_{1}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}t_{1}-e^{-2it_{2}k^{2}\sigma_{3}}t_{2}\right)k\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle+\left\|\left(e^{-2it_{1}k^{2}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}-e^{-2it_{2}k^{2}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}}\right)\partial_{k}v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2}(|k|\leq\gamma)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(2\gamma^{2}T+1\right)\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|\left\|v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2,1}(|k|\leq\gamma)}+\gamma^{2}\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|\left\|\partial_{k}v_{\pm}(0,k)\right\|_{L^{2}(|k|\leq\gamma)}.

Therefore, in the sense of the norm
H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
,
v(t,k)
is uniformly continuous with respect to time
t
. The Lipschitz continuity follows from Proposition
2.4
.
∎

3.4
Unique solvability of the RH problem

For the case
\sigma=1
: defocusing matrix NLS equation, let us consider the following RH problem:

RH Problem 1
.

Find a
(p+q)\times(p+q)
matrix-valued function
M(x,k)
that satisfies the following conditions:

•

Analyticity:
M(x,k)
is analytic for
k\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

•

Jump condition:
M
has continuous boundary values
M_{\pm}(x,k)
as
k^{\prime}\rightarrow k
from
\mathbb{C}^{\pm}
to
\mathbb{R}
and

M_{+}(x,k)=M_{-}(x,k)v_{x}(k),\quad k\in\mathbb{R},

(3.25)

where

v_{x}(k)=e^{-ixk\operatorname{ad\sigma_{3}}}v(k)=e^{-ixk\operatorname{ad\sigma_{3}}}\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}-\boldsymbol{R}(k)\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}(k)&-\boldsymbol{R}(k)\\
\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}(k)&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.26)

•

Normalization:
M(x,k)=I_{p+q}+\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

In order to prove the Lipschitz continuity of the inverse scattering map, below we consider the solvability of the RH problems in a larger space.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
v_{\pm}(k)-I_{p+q}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})
,
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists a unique solution to the RH problem
1
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
v_{x}(k)
admits the triangular factorization

\displaystyle v_{x}(k)=v_{x-}^{-1}(k)v_{x+}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&-\boldsymbol{R}(k)e^{-2ixk}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&0_{p\times q}\\
\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}(k)e^{2ixk}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.27)

We define two nilpotent matrices
w_{x}^{+}
and
w_{x}^{-}
by

w_{x}^{+}:=v_{x+}-I_{p+q},\quad w_{x}^{-}:=I_{p+q}-v_{x-}.

(3.28)

According to the classical RH theory, it is sufficient for this proposition to prove that the following Beals-Coifman integral
equation can be solved uniquely

\nu(x,k)=I_{p+q}+(\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}}\nu)(x,k),

(3.29)

where

\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}}\nu=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}(\nu w_{x}^{-})+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}(\nu w_{x}^{+}),

(3.30)

and

\nu=M_{+}(I_{p+q}+w_{x}^{+})^{-1}=M_{-}(I_{p+q}-w_{x}^{-})^{-1}.

(3.31)

The matrices
w_{x}^{+}
and
w_{x}^{-}
allow the uniform rational approximations
since
H^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})\subset C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, for every
\varepsilon>0
.
Furthermore, the operator
(I-\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}})
is a Fredholm operator and has zero Fredholm index follow from the Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 in
[
26
]
.

From (
3.26
), we have

v(k)+v^{{\dagger}}(k)=2\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}-\boldsymbol{R}(k)\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}(k)&0_{p\times q}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.32)

By (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle I_{p}-\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=I_{p}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\boldsymbol{C})\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{{\dagger}}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{{\dagger}})\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{{\dagger}}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{{\dagger}})(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1},

then
v(k)+v^{{\dagger}}(k)
can be decomposed as

v(k)+v^{{\dagger}}(k)=2\begin{bmatrix}(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})^{{\dagger}}&0_{p\times q}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}&0_{p\times q}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.33)

Thus
v(k)+v^{{\dagger}}(k)
is positive definite on
\mathbb{R}
. It follows from Theorem 9.3 in
[
26
]
that
\ker_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}(I-\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}})=0
, then
(I-\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}})
is invertible in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by Fredholm theory. Moreover, the solution to the RH problem
1
is given by

\displaystyle M(x,k)

\displaystyle=I_{p+q}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\nu(w_{x}^{+}+w_{x}^{-})\right)(x,k)

(3.34)

\displaystyle=I_{p+q}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\nu(x,s)\left(w_{x}^{+}(s)+w_{x}^{-}(s)\right)}{s-k}ds.

∎

3.5
Estimates of the reconstructed potential

According to the fact that
M(x,k)
satisfies (
2.1
) and using the asymptotic expansion of
M(x,k)
it follows that the potential
\boldsymbol{Q}(x)
can be recovered by the following formula

\boldsymbol{Q}(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}2ikM_{12}(x,k),

(3.35)

where the “12" subscript denotes the (1,2) entry of the
2\times 2
block matrix. From (
3.34
), the above reconstruction formula can be rewritten as

\boldsymbol{Q}(x)=\left(-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(x,k)\left(v_{x+}(k)-v_{x-}(k)\right)dk\right)_{12}.

(3.36)

We rewrite the Beals-Coifman integral operator
\mathcal{C}_{w_{x}}
in (
3.30
) associated with (
3.36
) in the equivalent form

\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}\phi:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\left(\phi\left(I_{p+q}-v_{x-}\right)\right)+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\left(\phi(v_{x+}-I_{p+q})\right).

(3.37)

Denote
v\in Y
if
v_{\pm}(k)-I_{p+q}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})
and
v\in Z
if
v_{\pm}(k)-I_{p+q}\in H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
. In order to establish the boundedness of
\left\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}
and the Lipschitz continuity of the inverse scattering map, we give the following Lemma to characterize the uniform resolvent bound.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
v\in Z
, then for fixed
a\in\mathbb{R}
and any bounded subset
B
of
Z
, we have

\sup_{v\in B}\sup_{x\in[a,\infty)}\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\|_{\mathscr{B}\left(L^{2}\right)}<\infty,

(3.38)

and the map

v\mapsto\left\{x\mapsto(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\right\}

is Lipschitz continuous from
Z
to
C\left([a,\infty);\mathscr{B}\left(L^{2}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

The embedding

H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow H^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R})

is compact, so we only need to verify the three conditions of the Proposition B.1 in
[
10
]

(i) It clearly follows from (
3.37
) that the map
(v,x)\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}
is continuous from
Y\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathscr{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and the estimate

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}^{\prime}}\|_{\mathscr{B}(L^{2})}\leq\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{Y}

holds.

(ii) For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in Y
, the existence of the resolvent
(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}
is guaranteed by Proposition
3.2
.

(iii) Finally, let us prove that for each
v\in Y
, the estimate

\sup_{x\in[a,+\infty)}\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\|_{\mathscr{B}(L^{2})}<\infty

(3.39)

holds. Let
\breve{v}_{\pm}\triangleq(v_{\pm})^{-1}
, then an operator
\mathcal{C}_{\breve{v}_{x}}
similar to operator
\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}
can be defined as

\mathcal{C}_{\breve{v}_{x}}\phi=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\left(\phi\left(I_{p+q}-\breve{v}_{x-}\right)\right)+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\left(\phi\left(\breve{v}_{x+}-I_{p+q}\right)\right).

Further, we define operator
T_{v_{x}}
as

T_{v_{x}}\phi:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\phi(v_{x+}-v_{x-})\right)(I_{p+q}-\breve{v}_{x-})+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\phi(v_{x+}-v_{x-})\right)(\breve{v}_{x+}-I_{p+q}).

A direct calculation using the properties of the Cauchy projection operators
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{\pm}
yields

I-T_{v_{x}}=(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})(I-\mathcal{C}_{\breve{v}_{x}}),

then

(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}=(I-\mathcal{C}_{\breve{v}_{x}})(I-T_{v_{x}})^{-1}.

It follows from the rational approximation in
[
26
]
that

\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}\|T_{v_{x}}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}=0.

Choose
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\forall x\geq b
,
\|T_{v_{x}}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}<\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, the operator
I-T_{v_{x}}
is invertible for every
x\geq b
and a bound on the inverse operator is given by

\sup_{x\in[b,+\infty)}\|(I-T_{v_{x}})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

Clearly,
\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{\breve{v}_{x}})\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}
is bounded uniformly in
x
. Then for each
v\in Y
, we have

\sup_{x\in[b,+\infty)}\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}<\infty.

When
a<b
, the estimate (
3.39
) still holds because
\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}
is bounded uniformly in
x\in[a,b]
.
∎

Before giving the estimates of the reconstructed potential, we first show that
M(x,t,k)
is a fundamental solution of the Lax equations (
1.4
) and (
1.10
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
M(x,t,k)
solves the RH problem
1
,
U(x,t)
and
P(x,t,k)
are given by

\displaystyle U(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(x,t,k)\left(v_{x+}(t,k)-v_{x-}(t,k)\right)dk\right)

(3.40)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{p\times p}&\boldsymbol{Q}(x,t)\\
\boldsymbol{Q}^{{\dagger}}(x,t)&0_{q\times q}\end{bmatrix},

and

P(x,t,k)=2kU(x,t)+i\sigma_{3}U_{x}(x,t)-i\sigma_{3}U^{2}(x,t),

(3.41)

respectively, then

satisfies the Lax pair (
3.17
)-(
3.18
).

Proof.

Let us first define two linear operators
L
and
N
by

\displaystyle LM

\displaystyle:=M_{x}+ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M)-UM,

(3.42)

\displaystyle NM

\displaystyle:=M_{t}+2ik^{2}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M)-kP_{1}M-P_{2}M.

(3.43)

Differentiating both sides of (
3.25
) with respect to
x
and

respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M_{+}}{\partial x}+ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial M_{-}}{\partial x}+ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{-})\right)v_{x},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M_{+}}{\partial t}+2ik^{2}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial M_{-}}{\partial t}+2ik^{2}\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{-})\right)v_{x},

which imply that
L
and
N
satisfy (
3.25
), namely,

\displaystyle\left(LM\right)_{+}

\displaystyle=\left(LM\right)_{-}v_{x},

(3.44)

\displaystyle\left(NM\right)_{+}

\displaystyle=\left(NM\right)_{-}v_{x}.

(3.45)

Substituting the asymptotic expansion

M(x,t,k)=I_{p+q}+\frac{M_{1}(x,t)}{k}+\frac{M_{2}(x,t)}{k^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{k^{3}}),\quad k\rightarrow\infty,

(3.46)

into (
3.42
) and (
3.43
) respectively, we have

\displaystyle LM=i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{1})-U+O(\frac{1}{k}),

\displaystyle NM=k\left(2i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{1})-P_{1}\right)+\left(2i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{2})-P_{1}M_{1}-P_{2}\right)+O(\frac{1}{k}).

Actually, according to (
3.35
) and (
3.36
), we can rewrite (
3.40
) in the equivalent form

U=i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{1}),

(3.47)

then

as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Combining this result and (
3.44
), we see that
LM
satisfies the homogeneous version of the RH problem
1
, thus
LM=0
from the process of proving Proposition
3.2
.

Clearly, the matrix function
P(x,t,k)
given by (
3.41
) can be decomposed into

P(x,t,k)=kP_{1}(x,t)+P_{2}(x,t),

where
P_{1}(x,t)=2U(x,t)
and
P_{2}(x,t)=i\sigma_{3}U_{x}(x,t)-i\sigma_{3}U^{2}(x,t)
. It is straightforward to obtain

P_{1}(x,t)=2i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{1})

(3.48)

from equality (
3.47
). Substituting the expansion (
3.46
) into
LM=0
and considering the
O(\frac{1}{k})
term, we have

M_{1x}+ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{2})-UM_{1}=0.

(3.49)

Let
M_{1}=M_{1}^{(off)}+M_{1}^{(diag)}
, where the superscripts represent the off-diagonal and the diagonal parts of the block matrix
M_{1}
. From (
3.47
) and (
3.49
), we have

\displaystyle M_{1x}^{(off)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_{3}U_{x},

\displaystyle M_{1x}^{(diag)}

\displaystyle=UM_{1}^{(off)}=\frac{i}{2}\sigma_{3}U^{2},

then

can be rewritten in the form

P_{2}(x,t)=-2M_{1x}=2ik\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}(M_{2})-PM_{1}.

(3.50)

Therefore,
NM=O(\frac{1}{k})
as

by (

) and (
3.50
). Similarly, it follows from (
3.45
) and Proposition
3.2
that

. It is now obvious that the lemma holds.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

(see
[

]
).
If
\boldsymbol{R}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then for

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}(\boldsymbol{R}e^{-2ixk})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{H^{1}},

(3.51)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}(\boldsymbol{R}e^{2ixk})\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\|\boldsymbol{R}\|_{H^{1}}.

(3.52)

Proposition 3.6
.

If
\boldsymbol{R}\in H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\boldsymbol{Q}\in H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
and the map

to

Proof.

For
x\geq 0
, by
\nu(x,k)
satisfying the integral equation (

\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}

(3.53)

where

By the expression of

in (

3.3

\|g\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|(I-\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}})^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}\left\|\mathcal{C}_{v_{x}}I_{p+q}\right\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}.

(3.54)

Using Lemma
3.5
and (

), we can show that

\displaystyle\left|\left(f_{3}\right)_{12}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-}g(v_{x+}-I_{p+q})\right)(I_{p+q}-v_{x-})dk\right|

\displaystyle H_{1}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}-i\operatorname{ad}\sigma_{3}\left(\nu e^{-ixk\operatorname{ad\sigma_{3}}}k(v_{+}-v_{-})\right)dk,

The estimate for

Proof.

The uniqueness of the solution can be obtained from group theory as well as Gronwall’s inequality.
Suppose that the initial date
\boldsymbol{Q}_{01},\boldsymbol{Q}_{02}\in H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
, denote the corresponding solution by
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}
respectively, then we have

\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}(t)=S(t)(\boldsymbol{Q}_{01}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{02})+\int_{0}^{t}S(t-\tau)\left(F(\boldsymbol{Q}_{1})-F(\boldsymbol{Q}_{2})\right)(\tau)d\tau,

where

and

(

), we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{p\times q})}\leq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{01}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{02}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{p\times q})}

\displaystyle+C_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{p\times q})}d\tau,

here we use the fact that
H^{1}
is a Banach Algebra. From the Gronwall’s inequality, we have

\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{p\times q})}\leq\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{01}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{02}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C}^{p\times q})}e^{C_{T}t},

, then

∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that

are given by Proposition

and satisfy

2

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
4.3
that

Similarly, we only need to prove that the following Beals-Coifman integral equation for RH Problem
2
is uniquely solvable

\mu(x,k)=I_{p+q}+(\mathcal{C}_{W_{x}}\mu)(x,k),

(4.30)

where

\mathcal{C}_{W_{x}}\mu=\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}^{+}(\mu W_{x}^{-})+\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}^{-}(\mu W_{x}^{+}),

(4.31)

and

\mu=\boldsymbol{M}_{+}(I_{p+q}+W_{x}^{+})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{M}_{-}(I_{p+q}-W_{x}^{-})^{-1}.

Here,

are the Cauchy integral operators on

, see
[
26
]
. By the Proposition 4.1 and 4.2 in

]

and
W_{x}^{-}

Let us verify that jump matrix

given by Proposition
4.2
satisfies the conditions of Theorem 9.3 in
[

]
.
It is easy to show that
V_{2}(k)=V_{4}^{{\dagger}}(k^{*}),\ k\in\Gamma_{2}
from (

) and (
4.18
).
For
V_{1}(k)
, we have

\displaystyle V_{1}+V_{1}^{{\dagger}}=2\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}+\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}&-\boldsymbol{R}\\
-\boldsymbol{R}^{{\dagger}}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&-\boldsymbol{R}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&-\boldsymbol{R}\\
0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}^{{\dagger}},

clearly the matrix

0_{q\times p}&I_{q}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle V_{3}+V_{3}^{{\dagger}}=2\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&\boldsymbol{R}_{0}\\
