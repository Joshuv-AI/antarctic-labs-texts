Exact moments and re-entrant transitions in the inertial dynamics of active Brownian particles
Debasish Chaudhuri

Abstract
In this study, we investigate the behavior of free inertial Active Brownian Particles (ABP) in the presence of thermal noise. While finding a closed-form solution for the joint distribution of positions, orientations, and velocities using the Fokker-Planck equation is generally challenging, we utilize a Laplace transform method to obtain the exact temporal evolution of all dynamical moments in arbitrary dimensions. Our expressions in d d dimensions reveal that inertia significantly impacts steady-state kinetic temperature and swim pressure while leaving the late-time diffusivity unchanged. Notably, as a function of activity and inertia, the steady-state velocity distribution exhibits a remarkable re-entrant crossover from “passive” Gaussian to “active” non-Gaussian behaviors. We construct a corresponding “phase diagram” using the exact expression of the d d -dimensional kurtosis. Our analytic expressions describe steady states and offer insights into time-dependent crossovers observed in moments of velocity and displacement. Our calculations can be extended to predict up to second-order moments for run-and-tumble particles (RTP) and the active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (AOUP). Additionally, the kurtosis shows differences from AOUP.

The system eventually reaches a steady state in velocity but not displacement, with mean-squared displacement undergoing dynamic crossovers before reaching asymptotic diffusion with inertia-independent diffusivity.
In contrast, the kinetic temperature and swim pressure depend on inertia; with inertia, while the kinetic temperature increases to saturate eventually, the swim pressure decreases monotonically to vanish in the infinite mass limit.

Further, we propose approximate analytic forms of probability distributions of velocity that show reasonable agreement with numerical results at small and large inertia limits and can qualitatively capture the re-entrant active-passive crossovers.
Our calculations for ABP can be extended to predict up to second-order moments for RTPs and the AOUPs. Additionally, the non-zero kurtosis for intermediate inertia shows differences from AOUP.

2
Model

The underdamped motion of ABPs in
d
-dimensional space moving with a constant active speed
v_{a}
in the orientation
\hat{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{d})
is described by its position
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}=(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime},...,r_{d}^{\prime})
and velocity
{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}=(v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime},...,v_{d}^{\prime})
evolving with time
t^{\prime}
. We consider the dynamics in the presence of translational and orientational Brownian noise with diffusivities
D
and
D_{r}
, respectively.
The time and length scales are set by
\tau_{r}=D_{r}^{-1}
and
\ell=\sqrt{D/D_{r}}
, to give the unit of velocity
\bar{v}=\sqrt{DD_{r}}
. We use dimensionless position
{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}/\ell
, time
t=t^{\prime}/\tau_{r}
, and velocity
{\mathbf{v}}={\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}/\bar{v}
to express the Langevin equation of motion in
d
dimensions in the following Ito form
[
57
,
58
,
59
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{i}\mathrm{d}t,

(1)

\displaystyle M\mathrm{d}v_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(v_{i}-{\rm Pe}\,u_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{i}(t),

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}u_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta_{ij}-u_{i}u_{j})\,\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{j}(t)-(d-1)u_{i}\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

where we used the dimensionless mass
M=\tau/\tau_{r}
with inertial relaxation time
\tau=m/\gamma
,
m
denoting the translational inertia and
\gamma
the viscous damping, and Péclet number
{\rm Pe}=v_{a}/\sqrt{DD_{r}}
. The Gaussian noise in translation and rotation are uncorrelated, and their components obey
\langle\mathrm{d}B_{i}\rangle=0
and
\langle\mathrm{d}B_{i}\mathrm{d}B_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}t
. The first term in Eq. (
3
) denotes a projection operator for the noise onto a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface perpendicular to
\hat{\bm{u}}
. The second term ensures that the unit vector remains normalized after displacement. The equations can be directly integrated numerically using the Euler-Maruyama scheme.

3
Calculation of moments from the Fokker-Planck equation

Noting that the heading direction
\hat{\bm{u}}
undergoes an orientational diffusion on a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersurface and
{\mathbf{v}}
performs a drift-diffusion,
the probability distribution
P({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},t)
evolves following the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P=-\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\,P)-(1/M)\nabla_{v}\cdot[({\rm Pe}\,\hat{\bm{u}}-{\mathbf{v}})P]+(1/M^{2})\nabla_{v}^{2}P+\nabla_{u}^{2}P

(4)

where
\nabla
and
\nabla_{v}
denote the gradient operator in
d
-dimensional position and velocity space, respectively.
The Laplacian
\nabla_{u}^{2}
in
(d-1)
-dimensional orientation space can be expressed in terms of Cartesian coordinates
{\bf y}
as

\nabla_{u}^{2}=y^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{yi}^{2}-[y^{2}\partial_{y}^{2}+(d-1)y\partial_{y}]

by defining
u_{i}=y_{i}/y
with
y=|\bf{y}|
.
Using a Laplace transform
\tilde{P}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-st}\,P({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},t)
, the Fokker-Planck equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle-P({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},0)+s\tilde{P}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},s)=-\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\tilde{P})-\frac{1}{M}\nabla_{v}\cdot[({\rm Pe}\,\hat{\bm{u}}-{\mathbf{v}})\tilde{P}]+\frac{1}{M^{2}}\nabla_{v}^{2}\tilde{P}+\nabla_{u}^{2}\tilde{P}

Therefore, the mean of any observable
\langle\psi\rangle_{s}=\int d{\mathbf{r}}\,d{\mathbf{v}}\,d\hat{\bm{u}}\,\psi({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}})\tilde{P}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},s)
satisfies the moment equation

\displaystyle-\langle\psi\rangle_{0}+s\langle\psi\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}+\frac{{\rm Pe}}{M}\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\rangle_{s}-\frac{1}{M}\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\rangle_{s}+\frac{1}{M^{2}}\langle\nabla_{v}^{2}\psi\rangle_{s}+\langle\nabla_{u}^{2}\psi\rangle_{s},

(5)

where
\langle\psi\rangle_{0}=\int d{\mathbf{r}}\,d{\mathbf{v}}\,d\hat{\bm{u}}\,\psi({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}})P({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},0)
is given by the initial condition, which without any loss of generality, can be expressed as
P({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}},\hat{\bm{u}},0)=\delta({\mathbf{r}})\delta({\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{0})\delta(\hat{\bm{u}}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{0})
.
Equation (
5
) can be used to determine any dynamical moment of interest in arbitrary
d
-dimensions as a function of time by performing an inverse Laplace transform. In the following, we denote the steady-state values
\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\psi\rangle(t)\equiv\langle\psi\rangle_{st}=\lim_{s\to 0}s\langle\psi\rangle_{s}
. Our theoretical approach, offering a unified method for computing the precise time evolution of all dynamical variables in arbitrary dimensions, differs from the recent works on inertial ABPs
[
19
,
35
,
51
,
52
]
. Moreover, we present approximate analytic expressions for velocity distributions parallel and perpendicular to the heading direction in Sec.
4.9
.

4
Velocity moments, steady states, and “phase transitions”

We first employ the above equation to obtain the time evolution of several velocity moments, including mean, variance, quartic moment, and lag function towards the steady state. The dynamics of inertial passive particles lead to
the equilibrium Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of velocity. Activity changes this behavior
[
19
,
41
]
. Here, we calculate up to fourth order cumulant of velocity to identify such deviations and hence determine a “phase diagram” describing departures from the equilibrium Gaussian characteristic.

4.1
Mean velocity

Using
\psi={\mathbf{v}}
in equation (
5
) we get the velocity in the Laplace space

\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}=\frac{{\mathbf{v}}_{0}+{\rm Pe}\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\rangle_{s}/M}{s+1/M}

in terms of
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\rangle_{s}
.
Again, using
\psi=\hat{\bm{u}}
, equation (
5
) gives

\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\rangle_{s}=\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}/[s+(d-1)]
. Together, these two expressions lead to

\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}=\frac{{\mathbf{v}}_{0}}{s+M^{-1}}+\frac{{\rm Pe}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{M(s+d-1)(s+M^{-1})}.

(6)

Expressions of dynamical variables with
s
dependence
\prod_{i}(s+s_{i})^{-1}
lead to sum over exponentials in time after inverse Laplace transform. Thus,
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\rangle(t)=\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}e^{-(d-1)t}
and

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle(t)={\mathbf{v}}_{0}e^{-t/M}+\frac{{\rm Pe}\,\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{(d-1)M-1}\left(e^{-t/M}-e^{-(d-1)t}\right).

(7)

The mean velocity vanishes in the steady state,
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{st}=0
.

In the presence of inertia, the orientation of the velocity vector
{\mathbf{v}}
can be different from the heading direction
\hat{\bm{u}}
. Orientation fluctuation of the heading direction over the inertial relaxation time leads to this difference. In the limit of vanishing inertia, one expects
\langle v_{\parallel}\rangle={\rm Pe}
. Let us define instantaneous velocity components parallel and perpendicular to the heading direction,
{\mathbf{v}}_{\parallel}=({\mathbf{v}}\cdot\hat{\bm{u}})\hat{\bm{u}}
,
{\mathbf{v}}_{\perp}={\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{\parallel}
.
Using
\psi=v_{\parallel}=\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}
in equation(
5
)
we get

\displaystyle\langle v_{\parallel}\rangle_{s}\equiv\frac{(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})+{\rm Pe}/(sM)}{s+1/M+(d-1)}

(8)

Its inverse Laplace transform yields,

\displaystyle\langle v_{\parallel}\rangle(t)=\frac{{\rm Pe}}{(d-1)M+1}+\left((\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})-\frac{{\rm Pe}}{(d-1)M+1}\right)e^{-((d-1)+M^{-1})t}

(9)

Thus
\langle v_{\parallel}\rangle
starts from
\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
at
t=0
to asymptotically reach the steady-state value
\frac{{\rm Pe}}{(d-1)M+1}
(see Fig.
1
(
a
) ). As expected, in the limit of vanishing inertia,
\langle v_{\parallel}\rangle={\rm Pe}
, the velocity vector always remains oriented along the heading direction. The larger the inertia, the bigger the deviation from the heading direction (also see Fig.
5
in the Appendix). By symmetry
\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{\perp}\rangle=0
.

4.2
Lag function

While analyzing the motion of inertial vibrobots, a lag function was used in Ref.
[
19
]
,

{\cal{C}}({\mathbf{v}}(t),\hat{\bm{u}}(t))=\langle{\mathbf{v}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}(0)\rangle-\langle\hat{\bm{u}}(t)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}(0)\rangle

which quantifies the average difference between the projection of velocity to initial heading direction and the projection of heading direction to the initial velocity. In equilibrium, time-reversal symmetry ensures
{\cal{C}}({\mathbf{v}}(t),\hat{\bm{u}}(t))=0

We obtain the following closed-form expression for inertial ABPs,

\displaystyle{\cal{C}}({\mathbf{v}}(t),\hat{\bm{u}}(t))=\frac{{\rm Pe}}{1-[(d-1)M]^{-1}}\left(e^{-t/M}-e^{-(d-1)t}\right),

(10)

using
{\mathbf{v}}(0)={\rm Pe}\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}
.
In the absence of inertia,
{\cal{C}}({\mathbf{v}}(t),\hat{\bm{u}}(t))=0
. In the inertial system,
{\cal{C}}({\mathbf{v}}(t),\hat{\bm{u}}(t))
starts from zero at
t=0
to returns to zero as
t\to\infty
after reaching a maximum at an intermediate time
t_{m}=\frac{M}{M(d-1)-1}\ln[(d-1)M]
. Plots of this expression in
d=2,3
are shown in Fig.
1
(
b
), with a comparison against numerical simulations performed in two dimensions. A similar non-monotonic variation was observed in vibrobots
[

]
.

4.4
Effective temperature and violation of equilibrium fluctuation-dissipation relation

The expression of mean squared velocity can be used to obtain the steady-state kinetic temperature of the system

\displaystyle k_{B}T_{\rm kin}=\frac{M\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle_{st}}{d}=1+\frac{{\rm Pe}^{2}}{d}\frac{M}{(d-1)M+1}.

(15)

Remarkably, unlike in equilibrium, the kinetic temperature is a function of inertia. In the over-damped limit
k_{B}T_{\rm kin}=1
. The kinetic temperature increases with
M
linearly at a small
M
to saturate to
1+{\rm Pe}^{2}/[d(d-1)]
at large
M
. The kinetic temperature in the coexistence of phase separating ABPs
[
40
]
and in active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles in two dimensions
[
60
]
were calculated before. With a careful mapping of the active force and persistence time of ABPs to that of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particles, the above prediction of kinetic temperature agrees with that of Ref.
[
60
]
in
d=2
(see Sec.
6
).

At equilibrium
{\cal I}=D-\mu k_{B}T=0
, due to the equilibrium fluctuation-dissipation relation. Using the expression of effective diffusivity
D_{\rm eff}=1+{\rm Pe}^{2}/[d(d-1)]
derived later in equation (
40
) in Sec.
5
, we obtain the dimensionless form of the violation of equilibrium fluctuation-dissipation

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}=D_{\rm eff}-k_{B}T_{\rm kin}=\frac{{\rm Pe}^{2}}{d(d-1)}\left[1-\frac{(d-1)M}{(d-1)M+1}\right].

(16)

Clearly, the violation is maximum in the overdamped limit of
M=0
. It decreases linearly with
M
at small
M
, to vanish asymptotically as
M\to\infty
. Thus, with increasing
M
, ABPs can return to equilibrium-like behavior.

4.7
Kurtosis: deviations from the Gaussian process

The fourth moment
\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{4}\rangle
for a general Gaussian process with
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle=0
in terms of lower order moments can be expressed as
[
31
]

\displaystyle\mu_{4}:=\left(1+\frac{2}{d}\right)\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle^{2}

(24)

The departure from Gaussian behavior can be evaluated using the kurtosis

\displaystyle{\cal K}=\frac{\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{4}\rangle}{\mu_{4}}-1.

(25)

At steady-state, it has the form

\displaystyle{\cal K}=\frac{-2{\rm Pe}^{4}M^{2}((4d-1)M+3)}{(d+2)((d-1)M+3)(dM+1)\left(d^{2}M-dM+d+{\rm Pe}^{2}M\right)^{2}}.

(26)

Thus, kurtosis shows a non-monotonic variation with
M
(Fig.
2
(a) ). It vanishes in the two limits of small and large inertia – in the limit of
M\to 0
as

\displaystyle{\cal K}\approx-\frac{2{\rm Pe}^{4}}{3d^{2}(d+2)}\,M^{2}

(27)

and in the limit of
M\to\infty
as
{\cal{K}}\sim M^{-1}
,
indicating the two
passive
states with asymptotically Gaussian distributions of velocity. For intermediate
M
values,
{\cal K}
becomes negative and reaches a minimum. This suggests a re-entrance transition from passive to active to passive behavior with the increase in inertia.

On the other hand, with activity
{\rm Pe}
, kurtosis decreases monotonically to saturate (Fig.
2
(
b
) ). For
{\rm Pe}\to 0
, it vanishes as
{\cal{K}}\sim{\rm Pe}^{4}
. In the limit of large activity
{\rm Pe}\to\infty
, it saturates to

\displaystyle{\cal{K}}=-\frac{2(4dM-M+3)}{(d+2)(dM+1)((d-1)M+3)}.

(28)

The large amplitude of kurtosis characterizes a strongly non-Gaussian velocity distribution denoting an active state. As shown in Fig.
2
(
d
), the velocity distribution in two dimensions (2d) corresponding to the active state obtained from numerical simulations displays a distinct ring shape.

4.8
“Phase diagram” describing active-passive crossovers

A heat map of equation (
26
) in Fig.
2
(
c
) produces a “phase diagram” identifying crossovers between active (dark, large amplitude of
{\cal K}
) and passive (light,
{\cal K}\approx 0
) regions in 2d. It clearly shows a re-entrant crossover from passive (Gaussian) to active (non-Gaussian) to passive (Gaussian) with increasing inertia at a constant activity, e.g., near
{\rm Pe}\approx 100
. The “phase diagram” in
d=3
also shows similar behavior (figure not shown).

The re-entrant crossover can be understood using the following heuristic argument. The velocity distribution becomes Gaussian if the persistence of the heading direction is lost over the inertial relaxation time, i.e.,
\tau\gg\tau_{r}
, which is equivalent to
M\gg 1
. As a result, the active fluctuations can be approximated as additional ‘thermal noise.’ The “phase diagram” shows the actual boundary is near
M=1
. At
M<1
, i.e.,
\tau<\tau_{r}
, persistence dominates; therefore, the velocity distribution shows maximum on a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersphere of radius
v={\rm Pe}
, with the spherical shape emerging due to isotropy in the initial orientation. Also, the thermal noise thickens the hypersurface shell by
\langle\delta v^{2}\rangle\approx d/M
. The
v={\rm Pe}
peak of the distribution gets unrecognizable when the velocity fluctuation gets larger than the ring size,
\sqrt{\langle\delta v^{2}\rangle}\gg{\rm Pe}
, leading to the criterion
M<{\rm Pe}^{-2}
for a re-entrance to the passive Gaussian regime. This criterion is consistent with equation (
27
).

4.9
Probability distributions of velocity in 2d

While finding a closed-form solution for the joint distribution of positions, orientations, and velocities using the Fokker-Planck equation is generally challenging, it can be analyzed in the two limits of small and large
M
, particularly for the marginal distributions of velocities. The velocity fluctuations are governed by the kinetic temperature
k_{B}T_{\rm kin}
in Eq.(
15
). For
M\ll 1
, i.e.,
\tau_{r}\gg\tau
, the heading direction can be assumed to be fixed at some angle
\theta
. This regime is equivalent to the dynamics of a particle under external force. Moreover,
k_{B}T_{\rm kin}\approx 1
, as
M\ll 1
. Thus, the steady-state solution is

\displaystyle P(v_{x},v_{y},\theta)={\frac{M}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{M}{2}[(v_{x}-{\rm Pe}\cos\theta)^{2}+(v_{y}-{\rm Pe}\sin\theta)^{2}]}.

(29)

The velocity distribution
P(v_{x},v_{y})
can be obtained by integrating over all possible
\theta
,
P(v_{x},v_{y})=(1/2\pi)\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta P(v_{x},v_{y},\theta)
. The marginal distribution,

\displaystyle P(v_{x})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}~\sqrt{\frac{M}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{M}{2}(v_{x}-{\rm Pe}\cos\theta)^{2}},

(30)

by symmetry has the same form as
P(v_{y})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}~\sqrt{\frac{M}{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{M}{2}(v_{y}-{\rm Pe}\sin\theta)^{2}}
. As shown in Fig.
3
(
a
), this distribution already describes the small inertia crossover between the passive Gaussian to active non-Gaussian (bimodal symmetric) nature, capturing the numerical simulation results at
M=10^{-4},10^{-2}
for
{\rm Pe}=100
. Thus, the previously proposed perturbative calculation
[
35
]
is not necessary to qualitatively describe the non-Gaussian feature. Moreover, as the perturbative calculation is valid only for small
M
limits, it cannot capture the large
M
re-entrance to Gaussian behavior.

At large
M\gg 1
, orientational relaxation leads to effective equilibrium-like Gaussian distributions with fluctuations governed by the kinetic temperature
k_{B}T_{\rm kin}\approx 1+{\rm Pe}^{2}/[d(d-1)]
(see Eq.(
15
))

\displaystyle P(v_{x},v_{y})={\frac{M}{2\pi k_{B}T_{\rm kin}}}e^{-\frac{M}{2k_{B}T_{\rm kin}}(v_{x}^{2}+v_{y}^{2})},~P(v_{i})=\sqrt{\frac{M}{2\pi k_{B}T_{\rm kin}}}e^{\frac{-Mv_{i}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\rm kin}}}.

(31)

In the last expression,
v_{i}
denotes both
v_{x}
and
v_{y}
.
This expression captures the simulation results at
M=10^{2}
and
{\rm Pe}=100
, thereby describing the crossover to passive Gaussian behavior at large enough
M
. Thus, equations (
30
) and (
31
) together capture the re-entrance observed as a function of
M
as evidenced from the numerical simulations shown in Fig.

5
Displacement moments, active diffusivity, and swim pressure

In this section, we calculate moments of displacement vector to explore their time evolution. We begin by using
\psi={\mathbf{r}}
and the initial condition
\langle\psi\rangle_{0}=0
in equation(
5
) to get
\langle{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}=\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}/s
. Further using equation(

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}=\frac{{\mathbf{v}}_{0}}{s(s+1/M)}+\frac{({\rm Pe}/M)\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{s(s+1/M)(s+d-1)}.

(34)

Its inverse Laplace transform gives

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{r}}\rangle(t)=M{\mathbf{v}}_{0}+\frac{{\rm Pe}\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{(d-1)}+\frac{{\rm Pe}\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\,e^{-(d-1)t}}{(d-1)[(d-1)M-1]}-\left({\mathbf{v}}_{0}+\frac{{\rm Pe}\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{(d-1)M-1}\right)M\,e^{-t/M}

(35)

In the limit of
M\to 0
the above expression gives
\langle{\mathbf{r}}(t)\rangle=\frac{{\rm Pe}\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}}{(d-1)}[1-e^{-(d-1)t}]
in agreement with the evolution of overdamped ABPs
[
31
]
.

5.1
Mean squared displacement (MSD) and asymptotic diffusivity

Let us now consider
\psi={\mathbf{r}}^{2}
and the initial condition
{\mathbf{r}}(t=0)=0
in equation(
5
). It is easy to see
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=2\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
,
{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi=0
,
\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi=0
,
\nabla_{v}^{2}\psi=0
, and
\nabla_{u}^{2}\psi=0
. Thus, equation(
5
) leads to

\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\rangle_{s}=2\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}/s
.
The calculation of the position-velocity cross-correlation
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
proceeds in the same way using
\psi={\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}
in equation(
5
), using initial condition
\langle\psi\rangle_{0}=0
,
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle_{s}
,
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
,
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
,
\nabla_{v}^{2}\psi=0
, and
\nabla_{u}^{2}\psi=0
. This leads to

\displaystyle(s+1/M)\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}=\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle_{s}+{\rm Pe}\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}/M.

(36)

To complete the calculation, one needs to evaluate the position-orientation cross-correlation
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
, using
\psi=\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}
in equation(
5

\langle\psi\rangle_{s}=0
,
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla\psi\rangle_{s}=\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}
,
{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi=0
,
\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi=0
,
\nabla_{v}^{2}\psi=0
, and
\langle\nabla_{u}^{2}\psi\rangle_{s}=-(d-1)\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}
to obtain

\displaystyle(s+(d-1))\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{s}=\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}.

(37)

Note that
\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle_{s}
and
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{s}
were already calculated in equations (
11
) and (
8

Thus, we find

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\rangle_{s}=\frac{2}{s(s+1/M)}\left[\frac{1}{s+2/M}\left({\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{2}+\frac{2d}{sM^{2}}\right.\right.{\left.\left.+\frac{2{\rm Pe}\{(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})sM+{\rm Pe}\}}{sM\{1+[s+(d-1)]M\}}\right)\right.}

\displaystyle{\left.+\frac{{\rm Pe}\{(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})sM+{\rm Pe}\}}{sM(s+(d-1))\{1+(s+(d-1))M\}}\right].}

(38)

The inverse Laplace transform of equation(

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\rangle(t)=2t\left(d+\frac{{\rm Pe}^{2}}{d-1}\right)+\frac{2{\rm Pe}[(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})(d-1)M-{\rm Pe}]}{(d-1)^{2}[(d-1)M-1]}e^{-(d-1)t}

\displaystyle-\frac{2{\rm Pe}M\{(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})[(d-1)M+1]-{\rm Pe}\}}{(d-1)^{3}M^{2}-d+1}e^{-((d-1)+M^{-1})t}

\displaystyle+M\left(-(d-{\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{2}M)+\frac{2(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})M{\rm Pe}-M{\rm Pe}^{2}}{(d-1)M-1}\right)e^{-2t/M}

\displaystyle+M({\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{2}M-3d)+\frac{2(\hat{\bm{u}}_{0}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0}){\rm Pe}M}{d-1}-\frac{{\rm Pe}^{2}\{(d-1)M(3(d-1)M+4)+2\}}{(d-1)^{3}M+(d-1)^{2}}

5.2
Position orientation cross-correlation and swim pressure

While calculating
\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\rangle
, we computed the cross-correlation
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle
. Here, we consider its role in determining the swim pressure of ABPs. Note that the virial term for an ideal gas of
N
ABPs due to activity
\pi^{a}=(1/d\,V)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\gamma{\rm Pe}^{\prime}\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}\rangle
with
V
denoting an effective volume
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
. The dimensionless pressure has the form
{\cal P}=\rho({\rm Pe}/d)\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{st}
, where
\rho
denotes the dimensionless particle density and

\langle\hat{\bm{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}\rangle_{st}=\frac{{\rm Pe}}{(d-1)(1+(d-1)M)}
.
Thus, the swim pressure

\displaystyle{\cal P}=\rho\frac{{\rm Pe}^{2}}{d(d-1)}\frac{1}{[1+(d-1)M]}.

(43)

Unlike in passive particles or overdamped ABPs, the swim pressure turns out to be a function of inertia
M
.
Note that

Discussion: Comparison with RTP and AOUP

As the introduction mentions, the ABP model is closely related to the RTP and AOUP models. They differ only by the statistical properties of the active forces. However, up to the second moment, these properties are equivalent. The averages over the active forces over a time scale longer than the correlation time vanish. The two-time correlations for the active forces show exponential decay with characteristic correlation times. As a result, our predictions for ABPs up to second-order moments remain valid for these other two models and, with suitable mapping, can be used to predict exact results for them.

To illustrate this, let us denote the position and velocity vectors
({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{v}})
to express the dynamics of an active particle in
d
-dimensions,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}},

\displaystyle m\dot{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma({\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{a})+\gamma\sqrt{2D}\,{\bm{\eta}}(t),

where the only difference between the three models is in the dynamics of the active force
\gamma{\mathbf{v}}_{a}
. ABPs differ from RTPs in their rotational relaxation.
For the orientational diffusion of ABPs, the heading direction auto-correlation
\langle\hat{\bm{u}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=e^{-(d-1)D_{r}|t-t^{\prime}|}
using equation (
5
).
RTPs undergo discrete tumbling events with rate
\gamma_{r}

[
28
,
64
,
65
]
, such that the orientation propagator

P(\hat{\bm{u}},t|\hat{\bm{u}}_{0},0)=e^{-\gamma_{r}t}\delta(\hat{\bm{u}}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{0})+(1-e^{-\gamma_{r}t})\frac{1}{\Omega}
with
\Omega
the area of a
d
-dimensional unit sphere.
Thus, the orientational correlation controlling the active forces follows

\langle\hat{\bm{u}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{u}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=e^{-\gamma_{r}|t-t^{\prime}|}
.
In contrast, within the AOUP model,
using
\tau_{a}\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{a}=-{\mathbf{v}}_{a}+v_{ao}\sqrt{2\tau_{a}/d}~{\bm{\eta}_{v}}(t)

in
d
dimensions
with Gaussian white noise
{\bm{\eta}_{v}}(t)

[
60
,
31
]
, the steady-state auto-correlation follows

\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{a}(t)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{a}(t^{\prime})\rangle=v_{ao}^{2}e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau_{a}}
.
Thus, the active force in the three models is characterized by
\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{a}\rangle=0
and

\langle{\mathbf{v}}_{a}(t)\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{a}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{}l@{\qquad}l}v_{a}^{2}\,e^{-(d-1)D_{r}|t-t^{\prime}|}&&\textrm{for \, \, ABP,}\\[3.0pt]
v_{r}^{2}\,e^{-\gamma_{r}|t-t^{\prime}|}&&\textrm{for \, \, RTP,}\\[3.0pt]
v_{ao}^{2}e^{-|t-t^{\prime}|/\tau_{a}}&&\textrm{for \, \, AOUP}.\end{array}\right.

(46)

In the above relations, we used
v_{r}
and
v_{a}
to denote the amplitude of active velocity in the RTP and ABP models, respectively. Up to the second moment, all the models are equivalent with the mapping
v_{a}=v_{r}=v_{ao}
and
(d-1)D_{r}=\gamma_{r}=1/\tau_{a}
. The connection between ABP and RTP with
\gamma_{r}\leftrightarrow(d-1)D_{r}
was noted before in Ref.
[
28
]
.

The units of time and length are set in the three models by (i)
\tau_{r}=D_{r}^{-1},\,\ell=\sqrt{D/D_{r}}
(ABP),
(ii)
\tau_{r}=\gamma_{r}^{-1},\,\ell=\sqrt{D/\gamma_{r}}
(RTP),
(iii)
\tau_{r}=\tau_{a},\,\ell=\sqrt{D\tau_{a}}
(AOUP). Using these mappings and the definitions
M=m/\gamma\tau_{r}
, Pe
=v_{a}/\sqrt{D/\tau_{r}}
the expressions derived for
\langle{\mathbf{v}}\rangle
,
\langle{\mathbf{v}}^{2}\rangle
,
\langle{\mathbf{r}}\rangle
,
\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\rangle
within the ABP model remains valid for RTP and AOUP models. The same is true for the expressions of kinetic temperature, diffusivity, and pressure.
The ensuing results agree with, e.g., the earlier AOUP calculations of Ref.
[
60
]
in
d=2
.
Finally, the velocity distribution in RTP can show Gaussian-non-Gaussian crossovers, as in ABP. In contrast, given the inbuilt Gaussian nature, AOUP can not give rise to any non-Gaussian distribution, and all its higher-order dynamical moments can be calculated using the first two moments. Whether experimental observations demonstrate a non-Gaussian nature or not can be used to determine the potential application of the AOUP model in their analysis.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted a thorough analysis of the dynamics of free inertial Active Brownian Particles (ABP) in the presence of thermal noise.
To achieve this, we employed a method based on the Laplace transform of the Fokker-Planck equation, allowing us to obtain expressions of time-dependent dynamical moments of arbitrary observables in any
d
-dimensional space. We obtained explicit expressions for several moments of velocity and displacement and equal time cross-correlations. The analytical predictions were rigorously validated through direct numerical simulations in two dimensions.

Our study has uncovered several intriguing characteristics of inertial active particles, setting them apart from their overdamped and passive counterparts. Notably, the inertia-dependent characteristics manifested in the steady-state kinetic temperature and swim pressure while leaving the late-time diffusivity unchanged. Specifically, the kinetic temperature initially increased before saturation, while the pressure consistently demonstrated a monotonic decrease.

Furthermore, our study revealed fascinating re-entrant crossovers in the steady-state velocity distribution, transitioning from “passive” Gaussian to “active” non-Gaussian behaviors. To illustrate this phenomenon, we constructed a detailed “phase diagram” in the activity-inertia plane, utilizing the exact expression of
d
-dimensional kurtosis.
Additionally, we proposed approximate velocity distribution formulas that are closely aligned with numerical simulations, especially in small and large inertia limits. These formulations also captured the transition from low inertia passive to active states.
Moreover, our findings can be expanded to encompass related models such as RTP and AOUP, enabling predictions up to second moments of dynamic variables through suitable mappings.

Our exact expressions, beyond describing steady states, could be used to explain time-dependent crossovers observed in various moments of velocity and displacement. Overall, this study provides significant insight into the intricate interplay between activity, inertia, and thermal noise governing the behavior of inertial active particles.
