Certifying almost all quantum states with few single-qubit measurements
Hsin-Yuan Huang, John Preskill, Mehdi Soleimanifar

Abstract
Certifying that an n n -qubit state ρ \rho synthesized in the lab is close to the target state | ψ ⟩ |\psi\rangle is a fundamental task in quantum information science. However, existing rigorous protocols either require deep quantum circuits or exponentially many single-qubit measurements. In this work, we prove that almost all n n -qubit target states | ψ ⟩ |\psi\rangle , including those with exponential circuit complexity, can be certified from only 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2}) single-qubit measurements. This result is established by a new technique that relates certification to the mixing time of a random walk. Our protocol has applications for benchmarking quantum systems, for optimizing quantum circuits to generate a desired target state, and for learning and verifying neural networks, tensor networks, and various other representations of quantum states using only single-qubit measurements. We show that such verified representations can be used to efficiently predict highly non-local properties of ρ \rho that would otherwise require an exponential number of measurements on ρ \rho . We demonstrate these applications in numerical experiments with up to 120 120 qubits, and observe advantage over existing methods such as cross-entropy benchmarking (XEB).

Introduction

Our empirical knowledge of a quantum system often relies on statistical comparisons with a target model of its state.
This, for instance, can be achieved by certifying that an
n
-qubit quantum state
\rho
, which is synthesized in the lab and can be measured experimentally, is close to a target quantum state
|\psi\rangle
.
For many relevant applications, the description of this target state
|\psi\rangle
is provided to us through a query model
\Psi
.
When queried with
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the model
\Psi
returns the complex amplitude
\langle x|\psi\rangle
, up to an unimportant overall normalization factor. Here
\{|x\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
denotes a chosen orthonormal basis for the
n
qubits.

For systems of small size
n
, models with query access to the amplitudes may simply be obtained by storing the entire description of the state
|\psi\rangle
on a classical memory.
For larger systems, powerful models, such as neural network quantum states or tensor networks, can be employed instead.
These models have been the focus of many past and more recent works
[
CT17
,
VC06
,
SDV06
,
PSMF20
,
HAGH+20
,
CTMA19
,
MCCC19
,
TMC+18
,
IGPOM23
,
ZCV23
,
TMCM20
]
that explore their promising practical performance.
There are also many quantum states, including some highly entangled ones, which have simple classical descriptions and are therefore natively equipped with a query access model; examples include phase states, coherent Gibbs states, GHZ states, and W states.

We can query the model
\Psi
to obtain information about the target state
|\psi\rangle
.
We can also perform measurements on independent copies to gather information about the lab state
\rho
.

Quantum state certification
is a task that uses the results of the measurements to certify that the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
is sufficiently close to one.
To be most effective, this certification procedure should demand minimal experimental and computational resources.
Therefore we aim for the measurements on the state
\rho
to consist of simple single-qubit measurements.
These measurements are compatible with a wide range of experimental platforms and typically yield data that is easier to analyze computationally.
The primary question that we seek to address is:
how many copies of the state
\rho
do we need to measure in order to accurately determine if the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
is close to one or not?

Despite the considerable previous research, it has remained open whether a certification procedure exists that (a) relies solely on a few single-qubit measurements on separate copies of a general
n
-qubit state
\rho
and (b) can validate the overlap with a generic highly-entangled target state
|\psi\rangle
.
On the face of it, demanding both features (a) and (b) may seem contradictory.
After all, the relevant information in an entangled state is distributed non-locally among its constituent qubits, and it might appear that single-qubit measurements on
\rho
lack the capacity to probe global properties such as the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
with a highly-entangled target state
|\psi\rangle
.
Indeed prior results either require measurements involving deep quantum circuits
[
HKP20
,
OW16
,
OW17
,
HHJ+17
]
, need exponentially many single-qubit measurements
[
FL11
,
dSLCP11
,
AGKE15
]
, are limited to special families of target states
[
GKEA18
,
TM18
]
, or lack rigorous guarantee for certification
[
AAB+19
,
CSM+23
,
CMH+23
]
.
In the next section, we present our main result — a certification procedure, featuring a surrogate for the fidelity which we call the
shadow overlap
, that can certify almost all quantum states with few single-qubit measurements, achieving both (a) and (b).

2
Our main results

In this work, we devise a simple procedure (shown in Figure
1
and introduced in detail in Section
3
) for certifying the overlap
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
between a (possibly mixed) state
\rho
and a target pure state
|\psi\rangle
over
n

This certification procedure proceeds by performing single-qubit Pauli measurements on each qubit of the state
\rho
and outputs an estimate
\hat{\bm{\omega}}
.
The expectation
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\hat{\bm{\omega}}]
, which we refer to as the
shadow overlap
, satisfies the following relation with the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}[\hat{\bm{\omega}}]\geq 1-\epsilon

\displaystyle\,\,\,\text{implies}\,\,\,\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\tau\epsilon,

(1)

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon

\displaystyle\,\,\,\text{implies}\,\,\,\mathop{\bf E\/}[\hat{\bm{\omega}}]\geq 1-\epsilon.

(2)

Here, the parameter
\tau
corresponds to the relaxation time of a Markov chain for sampling from the measurement distribution
\pi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
induced by the state
|\psi\rangle
.
This Markov chain is introduced more formally in Section
3
.
When we neglect
\log(n)
factors, the relaxation time of a Markov chain is bounded by its mixing time and relates to the number of random bit flips needed to sample from the stationary distribution.
When the relaxation time
\tau
is bounded, the shadow overlap
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\hat{\bm{\omega}}]
offers a good surrogate for the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
.
Stated equivalently in the following theorem, we show that one can efficiently certify the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
for any target state
|\psi\rangle
with a polynomial relaxation (or mixing) time.
The theorem is proved in Appendix
C.2
.

Theorem 1

(Certification of quantum states, informal)
.

Given an
n
-qubit target pure state
|\psi\rangle
with a relaxation time
\tau\geq 1
.
There is a certification procedure that performs single-qubit Pauli measurements on
T=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
samples of an unknown
n
-qubit state
\rho

Failed
if the fidelity is low
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle<1-\epsilon

Certified
if the fidelity is high
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2\tau}
.

When one allows more general single-qubit measurements on the unknown
n
-qubit state
\rho
, the sample complexity can be improved to
T=\mathcal{O}(\tau/\epsilon)
.

The certification procedure in Theorem
1
uses
\mathcal{O}(T)

\Psi

|\psi\rangle
.
As we will explain soon, when

and the model

Although not all quantum states in a given basis exhibit a polynomial relaxation time, our analysis based on an intricate study of random walks on the Boolean hypercube

This result is established in Appendix
D

Together with Theorem

Theorem 2

(Certification of almost all quantum states, informal)
.

For all except an exponentially small
2^{-\Omega(n)}
fraction of
n
-qubit target pure states
|\psi\rangle
,
there is a certification procedure that performs single-qubit measurements on
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\epsilon)
samples of an unknown state
\rho
and, with high probability, outputs
Failed
if the fidelity is low
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle<1-\epsilon
and outputs
Certified
if the fidelity is high
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2\tau^{*}}
, where
\tau^{*}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.

While Theorem
2
establishes an efficient certification procedure for generic quantum states, we can also prove a polynomial bound
\tau\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
for a variety of structured quantum states, rendering our certification scheme efficient for such quantum states.
Quantum phase states and the GHZ-like states both have a relaxation time
\tau=\mathcal{O}(n)
as shown in Appendix
G
and Appendix
I
.
We also prove in Appendix
H
that
\tau=\mathcal{O}(n^{\kappa})
for a family of quantum states such that the probability distribution
|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
matches the distribution for the ground state of a gapped sign-free
\kappa
-local Hamiltonian but the phases
\tfrac{\langle x|\psi\rangle}{|\langle x|\psi\rangle|}
can be arbitrary.

Even though exhibiting fast mixing can be considered an assumption concerning the target state, in Appendix
E
, we also present a scheme that could be used to enforce this assumption by modifying a given query model
\Psi
of a target state
|\psi\rangle
.
This scheme runs efficiently and has the following feature:
If the model
\Psi
satisfies a sufficient condition for fast mixing, known as the
local escape property
defined formally in Definition
24
, then it remains unchanged by the enforcement procedure.
If not, a new model
\Psi^{\prime}
is induced which satisfies the local escape property and exhibits fast mixing.
However, this comes with the potential cost of
\Psi^{\prime}
being very different from the original model
\Psi
.
We show that local escape property holds for almost all quantum states, which is the essential ingredient in our proof of Theorem
2
.

This certification procedure has much broader applications that we also investigate in this work.
Here we briefly summarize these results, postponing a more detailed discussion until Section
4
.

ML tomography of quantum states:

In Section
4.1
, we demonstrate that the shadow overlap provides a theoretically backed yet practically feasible procedure for learning a machine learning model of a quantum state or certifying the fidelity of an already trained model.

We show in Section

Optimizing quantum circuits for state preparation:
In Section

Unlike fidelity, which faces the barren plateau phenomenon
[

,

]
, shadow overlap acts similarly to the Hamming distance when the target state has no global correlations and its amplitudes are spread across the hypercube

3
Certification procedure

In its simplest form, depicted in Figure
1
, our certification procedure consists of two steps.
In the first step, we acquire a single copy of the state
\rho
,
and randomly choose one of its
n
qubits, denoted by
\bm{k}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
We then measure all the qubits of
\rho
except for qubit
\bm{k}
in the Pauli
Z
-basis.
We denote the measurement outcomes collectively by
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
.
Following these measurements, we select a Pauli
X
,
Y
, or
Z
-basis measurement uniformly at random and measure the
\bm{k}
th qubit of
\rho
in that basis.
The post-measurement state of the
\bm{k}
th qubit is denoted by
|\bm{s}\rangle
.

Having obtained measurement results from the state
\rho
, the certification protocol then moves to the second phase where we query a model
\Psi
that represents the state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi(x)|x\rangle
by giving us access to the amplitudes
\psi(x)
of the state in the computational basis.
That is, upon receiving an input string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the model
\Psi
returns a complex number
\Psi(x)
. The numbers
\{\Psi(x)\}
are assumed to be proportional to the amplitudes
\{\psi(x)\}
, but we do not require the outputs to be normalized.
This extends the applicability of our results because computing the normalization constant
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\Psi(x)|^{2}
is in general intractable.

In the query phase of the certification procedure, the model
\Psi
is queried twice with binary strings
\bm{z}^{(0)}
,
\bm{z}^{(1)}
.
Here the string input
\bm{z}^{(a)}
equals
a\in\{0,1\}
on its
\bm{k}
th bit and matches the measurement outcome
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
from the first step of the protocol on the remaining
n{-}1
bits.
We use these queries to compute the single-qubit state
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle
defined by

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\Psi(\bm{z}^{(0)})\cdot|0\rangle+\Psi(\bm{z}^{(1)})\cdot|1\rangle}{\sqrt{|\Psi(\bm{z}^{(0)})|^{2}+|\Psi(\bm{z}^{(1)})|^{2}}}.

(3)

Finally, the measurement and the query data are used to compute the
local overlap

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|\cdot\left(3|\bm{s}\rangle\langle\bm{s}|-\mathds{1}\right)\cdot|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle.

(4)

If both queries
\Psi(\bm{z}^{(0)}),\Psi(\bm{z}^{(1)})=0
, we report
\bm{\omega}=0
.
We then repeat this two-step protocol for a total of
T
times on independent copies of the state
\rho
to obtain overlaps
\bm{\omega_{1}},\dots,\bm{\omega_{T}}
.
We report the empirical average
\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}
as our estimated shadow overlap between
\rho
and the state represented by model
\Psi
.

3.1
Overview of the analysis

We discuss the performance of this certification procedure in depth in Appendix
C
.
Here we explain the high-level idea behind why our protocol works.
Consider a modified version of this test where instead of randomized Pauli measurements, the qubit
\bm{k}
is measured in the orthogonal basis
\{|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|,\mathds{1}-|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|\}
where the single-qubit state
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle
is the state specified in Equation (
3
).
Upon measuring the outcome
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|
, we output value
\bm{\omega}=1
, and otherwise the measurement returns
\bm{\omega}=0
.
First, suppose
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
and assume that after measuring
n{-}1
qubits in the
Z
-basis, we obtain the outcome
\bm{z}
.
In this case, the post-measurement state of qubit
\bm{k}
equals
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|
.
Hence, all the local overlaps measured in this manner are
\bm{\omega}=1
.
This means that the estimated shadow overlap is

\hat{\bm{\omega}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle=1.

(7)

In Protocol
3
, however, we do not measure qubit
\bm{k}
in this orthogonal basis; instead we measure in a randomly chosen Pauli-operator basis, and compute the local overlap
\bm{\omega}
using expression (
4
). Fortunately, as briefly reviewed in Appendix
B
, the expression
\left(3|\bm{s}\rangle\langle\bm{s}|-\mathds{1}\right)
matches the measured state
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|
of qubit
\bm{k}
when averaged over the choice of Pauli basis and the measurement outcome. This feature is the key observation underlying the “classical shadow” protocol for learning properties of a quantum state
[
HKP20
]
; it ensures that if the lab state is
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, then the expectation value of
\omega
is 1. Moreover, the empirical average
\hat{\omega}
will be close to 1 if
\rho
is close to
|\psi\rangle
and the number of samples
T
is sufficiently large.
In summary, this means that if the lab state is close to the target state, our protocol will successfully certify that the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
is close to 1.

But can the protocol be fooled into certifying a lab state that does not have high fidelity with the target state? To address this question, we observe that the expectation of
\omega
is
\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]
for an observable
L
that can be constructed by querying the model
\Psi
of the state
|\psi\rangle
.
Ideally, we would want this observable to be the projector
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
onto the state
|\psi\rangle
.
What we show instead in Appendix
C
is that this observable satisfies
L|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
and
\langle\psi^{\perp}|L|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\leq 1-\frac{1}{\tau}
for any state
|\psi^{\perp}\rangle
orthogonal to
|\psi\rangle
,
where
\tau\geq 1
is a parameter that depends on
|\psi\rangle
.
In this sense, the observable
L
forms an approximate projector onto the target state
|\psi\rangle
. As a result, we can distinguish a lab state
\rho
that has high fidelity with
|\psi\rangle
from a lab state that has low fidelity with
|\psi\rangle
by measuring
T=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
samples of
\rho
.

An empowering fact, established in Appendix
C
, is that the observable
L
has the same eigenvalues as the (normalized) transition matrix
P
of a suitably-defined random walk (or Markov chain) on the
n
-dimensional hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Thus
1/\tau
is the eigenvalue gap of
P
, the difference between its largest and second largest eigenvalue; correspondingly,
\tau
is the relaxation time of the Markov chain. This is very useful, because we can draw on the extensive literature concerning relaxation times of Markov chains to infer upper bounds on
\tau
and hence on the sample complexity of our certification protocol.
We emphasize that this random walk defined by
P
is not itself part of the protocol. Rather, it is merely used in the analysis of the performance of the protocol.

The transition matrix
P
is determined by the measurement distribution
\pi(x)=|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
which is sampled when the state
|\psi\rangle
is measured in the computational basis. Specifically, for a state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle
, the corresponding walk transitions from vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to vertex
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with probability

\displaystyle P(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}&x\sim y,\\
\frac{1}{n}\cdot\sum_{x^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x^{\prime}\sim x}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)+\pi(x^{\prime})}&x=y,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(8)

where two vertices
x
and
y
are connected (denoted
x{\sim}y
) when they differ in exactly
1
bit.
The walk is designed such that in its unique stationary distribution vertex
x
is occupied with probability
\pi(x)
. When
\pi(x)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, this transition matrix defines a lazy random walk on the Boolean hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
, which remains at vertex
x
with probability
1/2
and moves to one of its
n
neighboring vertices, chosen equiprobably, with probability
1/2
.

We can leverage results concerning the relaxation times of random walks to analyze the performance of our certification test.
We consider various families of quantum states for which the relaxation time is
\tau\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
, and therefore, our certification protocol is efficient as well.
This includes generic quantum states drawn from the Haar measure in Appendix
D
, as well as various structured entangled states such as quantum phase states in Appendix
G
, ground states in Appendix
H
, and GHZ-like states in Appendix
I
.

Our analysis of the relaxation time for Haar random
n
-qubit states in Appendix
D
draws on the concept of multi-commodity flows.
In this framework, we distribute a unit flow from each vertex
x
to another vertex
y
, dividing it across multiple paths such that no edge is congested.
Stated more formally, given a set of simple directed paths connecting
x
to
y
denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{xy}
, a multi-commodity flow is a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\cup_{x\neq y}\mathcal{P}_{xy}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{xy}}f(p)=1
for all two distinct vertices
x\neq y
.
The resistance
R(f)
of a flow
f
is defined by

\displaystyle R(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{e}\frac{1}{Q(e)}\sum_{x,y}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{xy}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}p\ni e}\pi(x)\pi(y)f(p)|p|,

(9)

where the weight
Q(e)=\pi(e^{+})P(e^{+},e^{-})
of an edge
e=(e^{+},e^{-})
is the probability of the transition
(e^{+},e^{-})
occurring in the random walk.
It is well-known that the relaxation time of a Markov chain is bounded by
\tau\leq R(f)
for any flow
f

[
Sin92
]
.

The measurement distribution of random quantum states exhibits probabilities
\pi(x)
that can vary significantly, being either excessively small or large.
As outlined in the expression (
9
), this can lead to a large resistance
R(f)
.
To get around this and find a tighter upper bound on the relaxation time, the flow
f
needs to avoid such congested vertices.
We achieve this using the concept of
local escape property
introduced in Lemma
17
and Definition
24
.
We show that this property holds with high probability in random states and allows us to spread the flow from any vertex
x
to its neighbors using edges within some constant Hamming distance of
x
while avoiding congested vertices.
A similar approach has been followed before in
[
MSW17
]
, where instead of congested vertices, some of the edges are removed.

4
Applications

We stated in Theorem
2
that almost all quantum states can be certified using shadow overlaps, which can be reliably estimated with few single-qubit measurements.
In what follows, we give an overview of various interesting applications of the shadow overlap formalism.

4.1
Neural network quantum state tomography

To facilitate learning and simulation of quantum systems, we desire classical models that are expressive enough to capture essential features of intricate quantum states, which are also well-suited for predicting various properties of the systems.
A rich class of such models grant us direct access to the amplitudes of quantum states.
More precisely, given an
n
-qubit quantum state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi(x)|x\rangle
in a fixed basis, such models can provide us with
query access
: the ability to compute the amplitudes
\psi(x)\in\mathbb{C}
up to an overall normalization constant.

A family of models that provide query access are machine learning (ML) models of quantum states based on neural networks or tensor networks such as those considered in many prior works
[
CT17
,
PSMF20
,
HAGH+20
,
CTMA19
,
MCCC19
,
TMC+18
,
IGPOM23
,
ZCV23
,
TMCM20
,
SSC22
,
WRVC23
]
.
Neural networks with
\operatorname{poly}(n)
-bounded depth and width can compute the amplitudes
\psi(x)
of the represented
n
-qubit state efficiently.
Tensor networks that admit an efficient contraction method, such as matrix product states
[
VC06
]
and tree tensor networks
[
SDV06
]
, also yield efficient query access to the amplitudes.

Learning ML models via hypothesis selection:

Our certification scheme yields an algorithm for learning ML models of quantum states with rigorous sample complexity guarantees.
This is achieved using
learning by hypothesis selection
, which can generally be applied to a set of models
\{\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{M}\}
each describing an
n
-qubit state
|\psi_{i}\rangle
, for
i\in[M]
.
Our objective is to use the measurement data obtained from identical copies of a state
\rho
and learn a model
\Psi_{i}
among
i\in[M]
which achieves the highest overlap
\langle\psi_{i}|\rho|\psi_{i}\rangle
.
This approach to learning is relevant in applications where we either naturally have a set of
M
hypotheses (e.g., from different theories describing the physics of a quantum system) or where we can obtain such a discrete set by casting a covering net (or carrying out some form of coarse-graining) over a larger and more expressive family of models.

We show in Appendix
J
that assuming the fast mixing condition for the set of models
\{\Psi_{1},\cdots,\Psi_{M}\}
, we can use the shadow overlap to learn a model that achieves a high fidelity with the lab state
\rho
using
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
copies of
\rho
.
In Appendix
J
, we give a concrete application of this scheme for learning a feedforward neural network representation of a quantum state.
We show that the sample complexity of this problem scales as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(nL^{3}W^{3}s^{2L}\right)
for a network of depth
L
, width
W
, and spectral norm
s
that takes
n
-bit strings as input.
In Appendix
J.3
, we also discuss another application of this learning algorithm in the context of gapped ground states.

Training neural quantum states with shadow overlap:
Although hypothesis selection provides a learning scheme with a rigorous sample complexity, the run time of this algorithm scales linearly with the number of models
M
, rendering it inefficient for many applications where
M
grows exponentially with the number of qubits
n
.
In practice, though, as shown in Figure
2
and detailed in Appendix
L.1
, we can use the shadow overlap along with the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to efficiently train and certify an ML model of a quantum state.

To this end, we consider training a neural network representation of an
n
-qubit state
|\psi\rangle
.
Conventionally, such neural quantum states take as input an
n
-bit string
x
and directly output a complex value proportional to the amplitude
\langle x|\psi\rangle
.
To achieve an improved performance, we instead train a
dual-input
neural network that admits two inputs
x_{0},x_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
which differ only in one bit.
This neural network computes
\frac{\langle x_{0}|\psi\rangle}{\langle x_{1}|\psi\rangle}
as its output.
We will see in Appendix
L.1
that
n
applications of this neural network architecture allow us to compute the amplitude
\langle x|\psi\rangle
for a given
x
.

The dual-input neural quantum states can be trained using a shadow-based log loss, leveraging data acquired by single-qubit measurements as prescribed in Protocol
3
.
The log loss is minimized via stochastic gradient descent.
Figure
2
shows an application of this scheme to learning highly entangled phase states

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle

(10)

with random binary phases
\phi(x)
on
n=120
qubits, and training data consist of tuples
(x_{0},x_{1},|\phi(x_{0})-\phi(x_{1})|)
which represent the phase difference between two adjacent strings
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
.
Such quantum states have exponentially large circuit complexity
[
BaCHJ+21
]
, are indistinguishable from Haar-random states with polynomially many copies with high probability
[
JLS18
,
BS19
]
, and exhibit volume-law scaling of entanglement
[
ABF+24
]
.
The findings reported in Figure
2
indicate that beyond a certain training threshold, the model attains a fidelity of
1.00
with the target state.
As explained next, this performance can also be certified using the shadow overlap, as an efficient alternative to the fidelity.

Certifying ML models:
One drawback of machine learning models for quantum states is that their training usually relies on heuristic algorithms.
The absence of performance guarantees highlights the need for certification procedures capable of efficiently verifying the accuracy of the trained models.
The result of Theorem
1
can be restated in terms of certifying the overlap between an
n
-qubit state
|\psi\rangle
and its trained ML model with a relaxation time
\tau
.
This is achieved using single-qubit Pauli measurements performed independently on
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
copies of
|\psi\rangle
along with two queries to the trained ML model per each copy of
|\psi\rangle
.

Figure
2
shows a numerical implementation of this certification procedure for a dual-input neural network representation of a 120-qubit random phase state introduced before.
After training the neural net with
50,000
measurements using shadow-overlap-based stochastic gradient decent, we estimate and compare the shadow overlap of the resulting model with its fidelity.
We observe that the predicted shadow overlap closely mirrors the fidelity, serving as an effective proxy.

Estimating sparse observables:
The certified ML models of quantum states can be employed to statistically estimate many properties of interest
[
IGPOM23
,
TMCM20
]
if in addition to query access, we assume the models are also equipped with
sampling access:
the ability to sample from the measurement distribution corresponding to
|\psi(x)|^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
.
The sampling access can be obtained in various ways.
Once the lab state
\rho
has been certified to have large overlap with the target state
|\psi\rangle
, we may obtain sampling access to
|\psi\rangle
by measuring
\rho
in the computational basis.
Alternatively, we can use Markov chain sampling, running the random walk defined in (
8
) for a number of steps given by mixing time and then sampling from the walk.
This procedure is closely related to the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm conventionally used in ML applications. Another option is using autoregressive methods to obtain direct sampling access
[
SLW+20
]
.
We show in Appendix
K
how to apply a verified ML model of a quantum state with query and sampling access to estimate the expectation value of any sparse observable
G
, such as the energy of a local Hamiltonian, or highly non-local properties such as Rényi entanglement entropies, up to an error
\epsilon
with a number of samples that scales as
T=\mathcal{O}\left(\langle\psi|G^{2}|\psi\rangle/\epsilon^{2}\right)
.
When no certified ML model is available, estimating certain non-linear observables such as the subsystem purity
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{A}^{2}
) requires a number of samples exponential in the size of the subsystem
A
; e.g., see
[
CCHL22
]
for an exponential lower bound that applies to any single-copy measurements, and
[
HKP20
]
for an upper bound via the classical shadow formalism.
However, as shown in Appendix
K
, the same task can be conducted using a verified ML model with a sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(1/\epsilon^{2}\right)
, independent of the system size.

In Figure
2
, we demonstrate this feature with a numerical experiment on the trained neural network representation of the random phase state in equation (
10
).
The purity
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{A}^{2}
) of the phase state is estimated for subsystems of size
|A|\in\{1,\dots,120\}
, confirming that the state of the subsystem is close to maximally mixed for sufficiently large subsets
A
.

4.2
Benchmarking quantum devices

Certifying the fidelity between a state
\rho
prepared using a quantum device and a known quantum state
|\psi\rangle
offers a rigorous approach for benchmarking quantum machines.
However, the exponential resources and the high level of control needed for estimating fidelity limit the applicability of this approach in practice.
To address these challenges, a number of studies have proposed and deployed other statistical quantities that act as a form of
proxy
for the fidelity in the situations often encountered practically
[
BIS+18
,
AAB+19
,
MCS+22
,
CSM+23
]
.
For a proxy of fidelity to be the most informative, one may ask for features such as (1) minimal hardware requirements and easy statistical and computational evaluation, (2) close tracking of fidelity, and (3) being equipped with rigorous bounds.
The shadow overlap meets these requirements by providing a provable lower bound on the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
as stated in Theorem
1
.
As discussed earlier, Protocol
3
is also computationally efficient and experimentally feasible, requiring only minimal hardware control.

In Figure
3
, we compare the performance of the shadow overlap with that of fidelity and the cross entropy benchmark (XEB), a prominent metric employed in the evaluation of quantum supremacy experiments with local random quantum circuits
[
BIS+18
,
AAB+19
]
.
In this numerical experiment, the shadow overlap is normalized, as explained in Appendix
F
, such that the target state attains value
1
and the maximally mixed state attains value
1/2^{n}
.
We benchmark two families of states with
4
and
20
qubits: (1) Haar random states and (2) structured states which are phase states of the form
U_{\operatorname{phase}}\cdot\otimes_{i=1}^{n}|\psi_{i}\rangle
.
Here, each
|\psi_{i}\rangle
is a single qubit state with random real amplitudes, and
U_{\operatorname{phase}}
is diagonal with random complex phases.
We explore the effect of white noise (i.e. global depolarizing noise) as well as coherent noise realized as small Gaussian errors in both the magnitude and the phase of the probability amplitude, as discussed in Appendix
L.2
.

We observe that XEB performs well in Haar-random states but tends to overestimate fidelity for phase states, and has decreased effectiveness for smaller system size, likely due to reduced concentration effects.
In contrast, the shadow overlap closely matches the fidelity across different noise regimes and system sizes.

These findings suggest that, much like XEB or similar benchmarks
[
MCS+22
,
CSM+23
]
, an estimated shadow overlap may be accepted at face value, yielding a statistical figure of merit for the quality of the prepared states.
Otherwise, in the high-fidelity regime where the error
\epsilon\ll 1/\tau
, one can also apply Theorem
1
to uncover a provable lower bound on the actual fidelity of the prepared state.
As a concrete example, in Appendix
F
, we show that, in a quantum processor that prepares a family of quantum states with tunable circuit complexity
C
, one can benchmark the fidelity of the device using a number of single-qubit measurements that scales polylogarithmically with
C
.

4.3
Optimizing quantum circuits for state preparation

Many variational quantum algorithms use the fidelity between two quantum states as their cost function.
Such cost functions are known to suffer from exponentially vanishing gradients, known as barren plateaus
[
MBS+18
,
CSV+21
,
CHE+23
,
JGR+23
]
, and require a high sample complexity to be statistically estimated.
One may use the shadow overlap
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]
in place of the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
in some of these algorithms.
Besides demanding a substantially lower sample complexity, shadow overlaps may offer an improved optimization landscape with non-vanishing gradients.
In particular, the shadow overlap displays behavior similar to the Hamming distance in cases where the target state shows no global correlations and its probability amplitudes are well-distributed across the Boolean hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Indeed, for the special case of bit strings in the
X
-basis, the Hamming distance and the shadow overlap precisely coincide.
One can see this for the simple case of
|\psi\rangle=|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
where, as discussed in Appendix
C
, our protocol effectively measures the expectation
\operatorname{tr}(L\rho)
for the observable
L=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|+\rangle\langle+|_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\setminus i}
.
This observable is local and has favorable features when used as the cost function compared to the non-local observable
|+\rangle\langle+|^{\otimes n}
used in the fidelity estimation; see the discussion in
[
CSV+21
,
CHE+23
,
JGR+23
]
.

and discussed in Section
L.3
, we investigate this feature of the shadow overlap in the context of training quantum circuits to optimally prepare a target state.
We have access to the matrix product state (MPS) representation of the target state, which corresponds to the output of a one-dimensional IQP circuit
[
BJS11
]
infused with random
T

, and

gates to generate the target state, optimizing for maximum shadow overlap.
We then assess this method’s performance against fidelity-based training.

When employing
n=50
qubits, fidelity-based training encounters barren plateaus, whereas shadow overlap-based training successfully prepares the target state with a fidelity very close to
1
.
We also note that, akin to the linear decrease in Hamming distance between two binary strings as suitable bits are flipped, under shadow-overlap-based training the deviation of the shadow overlap from 1 decreases linearly as suitable gates are added to the circuit. This contrasts with the fidelity, which fails to exhibit a steady, gradual increase as the number of state construction steps increases.

5
Outlook

Further extending the reach of our certification protocol based on the shadow overlap raises many interesting open questions.

Quantum states with fast relaxation times
: What families of quantum states provably admit a
\operatorname{poly}(n)
relaxation time with respect to the Markov chain (
8
) introduced in our analysis?
We show that Haar random quantum states exhibits a relaxation time bounded by
\tau\leq O(n^{2})
.
Can our arguments for Haar random states be extended to “state
t
-designs” whose first
t
moments match that of the Haar measure?
Such quantum states can be efficiently prepared with random quantum circuits of size
\operatorname{poly}(n,t)

[
BaHH16
,
Haf22
]
.
More generally, can we show that states prepared with (random) quantum circuits of
arbitrary
depth satisfy a relaxation time
\tau\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
?

States that cannot be certified with few single-qubit measurements:
Are there concrete examples of target quantum states that cannot be certified using any protocol that only relies on a
\operatorname{poly}(n)
number of single-qubit measurements?

Mixed states
: Can a similar protocol be developed when the target state belongs to a certain family of
mixed
quantum states?
If we allow arbitrary mixed states, then known lower bounds from certifying maximally mixed state
[
BOW19
]
rule out a protocol with
\operatorname{poly}(n)
sample complexity even with entangled measurements.
Going beyond worst cases, the instance-optimal sample complexity of certifying mixed states has been studied in
[
CLO22a
]
, where approximately low-rank mixed states can be certified efficiently using highly-entangled measurements.
Can almost all approximately low-rank mixed states be certified with few single-qubit measurements?

Appendices

Appendix A
Related work

Many past works have studied the problem of certifying the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
between a quantum state
|\psi\rangle
and a general state
\rho
.
The full-blown tomography of a
2^{n}
-dimensional quantum state
\rho
is known to require
\Theta(4^{n}/\epsilon^{2})
many copies to achieve an
\epsilon
error in trace distance—or
\Theta(4^{n}/\epsilon)
copies for an
\epsilon
error measured by infidelity
[
OW16
,
OW17
,
HHJ+17
]
.
While this is, in principle, sufficient for estimating the fidelity
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
, it is also known that the same certification task can be achieved with a dramatically lower copy complexity of
\Theta(1/\epsilon^{2})
, independent of the system dimensions.
Achieving this scaling, however, entails performing certain quantum operations that are often contrary to the objective of the certification.
This includes starting from the description of a (potentially highly-entangled)
n
-qubit state
|\psi\rangle
and performing the two-outcome measurement
\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\mathds{1}-|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\}
) or preparing independent copies of the state
|\psi\rangle
(e.g., to perform the swap test)
[
MdW13
]
.
The recent framework of classical shadows
[
HKP20
]
improves this by performing Clifford measurements on independent copies of the state
\rho
.
Implementing randomized Clifford measurements, however, requires deep circuits that may be practically infeasible.
The efficiency of this protocol further depends on computing the overlap between the state
|\psi\rangle
and random stabilizer states, which for instance, may not be achievable for states with high stabilizer rank.

Provided that single-qubit Pauli measurements can be performed on the copies of the state
\rho
, a method known as the
direct fidelity estimation
introduced and analyzed in
[
FL11
]
(also see
[
dSLCP11
,
AGKE15
]
) can estimate the overlap
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
up to an additive error
\epsilon
using
\mathcal{O}(2^{n}/\epsilon^{4})
number of copies.
This scaling is an
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
improvement on a naive application of the full tomography scheme but still grows exponentially
\Omega(2^{n})
with the number of qubits.
A variant of classical shadows can also be applied by employing randomized Pauli measurements, where the number of samples required scales exponentially with the weight of the measured observable. This approach enables us to effectively investigate only small subsystems
[
HKP20
]
but cannot estimate fidelity with a highly-entangled state efficiently.

When the target state comes from certain special classes of states, such as stabilizer states or states generated by shallow quantum circuits, direct fidelity estimation
[
FL11
]
and classical shadow based on randomized Pauli measurements
[
HKP20
,
HLB+24
]
can efficiently certify the target states.
Other examples include hypergraph states, output states of IQP circuits
[
TM18
]
, bosonic Gaussian states
[
AGKE15
]
, and fermionic Gaussian states
[
GKEA18
]
.
For generic random target states,
cross entropy benchmarking (XEB)
provides a good estimate for fidelity under certain noise models as studied in
[
AAB+19
,
CSM+23
,
CMH+23
,
DHJB24
]
.

XEB and shadow overlap both use only single-qubit measurements and both require having access to
\langle x|\psi\rangle
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, which can be time consuming during classical postprocessing.
However, XEB does not solve the quantum state certification task rigorously because XEB only uses Z-basis measurement.
As a result, XEB can outputs a fidelity score of one even when the state
\rho
is a classical probability distribution with no quantum entanglement and is far from the target state
|\psi\rangle
.
In contrast, shadow overlap provably solves the certification task for almost all target states
|\psi\rangle
.
On a high level, shadow overlap can be seen as an enhancement of XEB using classical shadow based on randomized Pauli measurements on a randomly chosen qubit instead of all
Z
-basis measurements.

Appendix B
Review of randomized Pauli measurements

As part of our certification protocol, we use randomized Pauli measurements on pure
m
-qubit states.
These measurements provide sufficient statistical information for obtaining an unbiased estimator of the state.
At the same time, they are practically appealing and require minimal experimental capabilities to be performed in practice.

Such measurements are part of a broader framework for the statistical study of quantum systems, called classical shadows.
In this framework, measurements give us classical access to snapshots of the quantum state.
With sufficiently many randomized snapshots, one can efficiently and accurately estimate various properties of subsystems of a quantum state.

Suppose we perform randomized
X
,
Y
, or
Z
Pauli measurements on all
m
qubits of a state
|\varphi\rangle
.
As a result of these measurements, we obtain
m
single-qubit states denoted by

\displaystyle|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\bm{s_{m}}\rangle\quad\text{where}\quad|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle,\dots,|\bm{s_{m}}\rangle\in\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|+\rangle,|-\rangle,|i+\rangle,|i-\rangle\}.

(11)

Here,
\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}
,
\{|i+\rangle,|i-\rangle\}
, and
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
are eignestates of Pauli
X
,
Y
, and
Z
, respectively.
We use the collected data to compute the operator

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(3|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{1}}|-\mathds{1}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(3|\bm{s_{m}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{m}}|-\mathds{1}\right)

(12)

that when averaged sufficiently many times, gives an accurate approximation of the original state
|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|
.
This can be better seen by expressing the operator (
12
) in terms of Pauli operators.
Let
\bm{W_{1}},\dots,\bm{W_{m}}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
denote the randomly chosen Pauli operators that we measured on the state
|\varphi\rangle
,
and let
\bm{o_{1}},\dots,\bm{o_{m}}\in\{+1,-1\}
be the observed outcomes.
The measurements return

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathds{1}+3\bm{o_{1}}\bm{W_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\mathds{1}+3\bm{o_{n}}\bm{W}n\right)\rho\right)

(13)

with a probability that can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3^{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathds{1}+\bm{o_{1}}\bm{W_{1}}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(\mathds{1}+\bm{o_{m}}\bm{W_{m}}\right)\rho\right)

(14)

A direct calculation on this random ensemble (see
[
HKP20
]
) shows that in expectation, we have
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\sigma}]=|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|
.
Moreover, we have
{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\bm{\sigma}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{\infty}=2^{m}
.
We use these facts to establish concentration bounds for the certification protocol in Appendix
C.2
.

Appendix C
Performance guarantees of the certification protocol

In this section, we provide a detailed analysis of the certification protocol based on shadow overlaps.
We begin with a broad overview before discussing the technical details.

C.1
Technical overview

Our certification protocol is based on the notion of property testing.
Similar quantum state certification tasks were studied in a series of previous works with the a focus on certifying mixed states
[
BOW19
,
CHLL22
,
CLO22b
]
.
In our framework, we are given states
\rho
and
|\psi\rangle
and promised that either
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2\tau}
or
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle<1-\epsilon
.
The certification procedure outputs
Certified
in the first case and
Failed
in the second case.
The goal of our analysis, as stated in Theorem
6
in Appendix
C.2
, is to show that by using Protocol
3
and setting the number of samples to
T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log(\frac{1}{\delta})\right)
, we can correctly decide between the
Certified
and
Failed
instances with probability at least
1-\delta
.

The soundness of our protocol crucially relies on the ability to measure the state in both the Pauli-
X
and Pauli-
Z
bases.
This quantum mechanical feature is in sharp contrast to the classical testing of probability distributions, where, in effect, only Pauli-
Z
measurements are possible.
For instance, given a distribution
p(x)
, consider measuring the state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{p(x)}|x\rangle
only in the
Z
basis.
This gives us samples
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x_{T}}\sim p(x)
.
It is well-known
[
Pan08
]
that testing whether the distribution
p(x)
equals the uniform distribution or is far from the uniform distribution requires a number of samples that scales exponentially,
T=\Omega(2^{n/2})
.
We get around such grim scalings encountered classically by also allowing some complementary Pauli measurements (i.e. in
X
or
Y
-basis).
Indeed in the simple case of
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}|x\rangle
, measuring all
n
qubits in the
X
basis enables directly measuring
\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,\mathds{1}-|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\}
.
In contrast, our protocol shows that measuring just one of the
n
qubits in a randomized Pauli basis is sufficient for certifying generic quantum states
|\psi\rangle
.

We note that the notion of relaxation time used in our framework is basis-dependent.
In fact, a quantum state can mix slowly in one basis and mix rapidly in a locally rotated basis.
The GHZ state provides an example of such a state.
In the standard
Z
-basis, we have
|\mathrm{GHZ}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle^{\otimes n}+|1\rangle^{\otimes n})
.
Hence the distribution of this state is supported on two vertices
0^{n}
and
1^{n}
and does not mix under the walk defined by the transition matrix
P(x,y)
.
In the Hadamard basis, this state can be expanded as
H^{\otimes n}|\mathrm{GHZ}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n-1}}}\sum_{x\in\mathrm{even}}|x\rangle
and enjoys fast mixing, though for this purpose we need to change the protocol so that the observable
L
corresponds to a walk that jumps from a vertex to its next-to-nearest neighbor.
This is discussed in more depth in Appendix
I
.

A slightly modified version of the Protocol
3
offers an improved sample complexity
T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\tau}{\epsilon}\cdot\log(\frac{1}{\delta})\right)
with a quadratically better dependency on the error
\epsilon
and mixing time
\tau
.
This can be achieved if we replace the randomized Pauli measurement on qubit
\bm{k}
with a direct measurement in the orthogonal basis
\{|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|,\mathds{1}-|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|\}
and output
\bm{\omega}=0
or
\bm{\omega}=1
depending on the measurement outcome.
The sample complexity of this procedure is analyzed in Theorem
7
proved in Appendix
C.2
. However, the modified protocol has the disadvantage that the query model
\Psi
must be consulted in each measurement round to determine the measurement basis used. In contrast, using the original protocol we can measure all the copies of
\rho
first, and then query
\Psi
later on when we wish to estimate the fidelity. In fact, in the original protocol we can use the same data set repeatedly to estimate the fidelity with a variety of target states, each with its own query model.
This feature is further explored in Section
J.1
.

One enhancement to the certification Protocol
3
is obtained by allowing more than one qubit to be measured in a random Pauli bases.
This gives us a “hierarchy” of protocols (introduced more formally as Protocol
C.1
in Appendix
C.2
) where level
1
corresponds to Protocol
3
and level
m
, for a constant
m
, is constructed as follows:
(1) Choose
n-m
qubits uniformly at random and measure them in the Pauli-
Z
basis.
(2) The remaining
m
qubits are each measured in a random Pauli
X
,
Y
, or
Z
basis.
(3) As before, this allows us to construct the classical shadow
\bm{\sigma}
of the post-measurement state on
m
qubits.
(4) Based on the measurement outcomes on the
n-m
qubits, we query the model
\Psi
to compute an observable
L_{\bm{z_{k}}}
that we specify soon.
(5)
Instead of an overlap of the form in Equation (
4
), we compute
\bm{\omega}=\operatorname{tr}(L_{\bm{z_{k}}}\bm{\sigma})
. (6) Following
T
repetitions of the previous steps, we report the empirically estimated shadow overlap
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}
.

In this procedure, the observable
L_{\bm{z_{k}}}
is the transition matrix of a weighted random walk among
n
-bit strings that differ in at most
m
bits.
When
m=1
, this observable is simply the projector
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}|
.
More generally, for
m>1
, the observable
L_{\bm{z_{k}}}
is a sum of projectors onto states defined similarly to
|\Psi_{\bm{k,z}}\rangle
in Equation (
3
) and can be easily computed by querying the model
\Psi
.

In later sections, we show examples of states that can be verified with this generalized certification procedure.
This includes entangled states such as the GHZ state, as well as the ground states of gapped sign-problem-free Hamiltonians.

Protocol 2
Level-
m

\rho

|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|

T

\rho

\Psi

|\psi\rangle

0\leq\epsilon<1

Certify that the overlap
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon
.

\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle<1-\epsilon

Failed

1.

\bm{z_{k}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-\bm{r}}

3.

For each qubit

Compute the classical shadow

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=\left(3|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{1}}|-\mathds{1}\right)\otimes\left(3|\bm{s_{2}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{2}}|-\mathds{1}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(3|\bm{s_{r}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{r}}|-\mathds{1}\right).

(15)

\displaystyle|\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{\bm{z_{k}}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\Psi(\bm{z_{k}}^{(\ell_{1})})\cdot|\ell_{1}\rangle+\Psi(\bm{z_{k}}^{(\ell_{2})})\cdot|\ell_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{|\Psi(\bm{z_{k}}^{(\ell_{1})})|^{2}+|\Psi(\bm{z_{k}}^{(\ell_{2})})|^{2}}}.

C.2
Detailed analysis

Suppose we are provided with a representation of a many-body quantum state

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle

(18)

via query access to a model
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
. This model upon querying any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
returns the possibly un-normalized amplitudes
\Psi(x)
and
\Psi(y)
such that
\frac{\Psi(x)}{\Psi(y)}=\frac{\langle x|\psi\rangle}{\langle y|\psi\rangle}
.

We are also given multiple identical copies of a state
\rho
. Our goal is to use queries to
\Psi
along with performing unentangled simple local measurements on copies of
\rho
to certify that the state
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
is close or far from the state
\rho
.

In this section, we analyze the performance of a generalized version of Protocol
3
introduced in Section
1
.
In this version, which is stated in detail in Protocol
C.1
, we choose
m
qubits uniformly at random and measure each on a randomized basis.
This section includes the proof of Theorem
1
as well as the equivalent statement for the level-
m
protocol.

Fix a level
m
for the certification protocol. The measurement distribution
\pi(x)=|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
is a distribution on a graph
G=(V,E)
where the vertices
V=\{0,1\}^{n}
are
n
-bit strings,
and an edge
e=(x,y)
exists between vertices
x
and
y
when they differ in
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
bits.
Let
\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\pi(x)>0\}
denote the support of
\pi(x)
.
Let
N=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{n}{k}
be number of neighbors of each vertex.
Consider a weighted version of this graph where every edge
(x,y)
that connects vertices
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
is assigned a weight
W(x,y)
according to

\displaystyle W(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\pi(x)\pi(y)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}&(x,y)\in E,\\
\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(x^{\prime},x)\in E}\frac{\pi(x)^{2}}{\pi(x)+\pi(x^{\prime})}&x=y,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(19)

Given this weight matrix
W=\sum_{x,y}W(x,y)|x\rangle\langle y|
, there is a canonical way to define a random walk or a Markov chain on this graph.
The transition matrix
P=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}P(x,y)|x\rangle\langle y|
of this walk is defined by
P=S^{-1}W
where the scaling matrix is set to be

S=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\pi(x)|x\rangle\langle x|.

Therefore, the transition probability
P(x,y)
from a vertex
x\in\mathcal{S}
to
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle P(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}&(x,y)\in E,\\
\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(x^{\prime},x)\in E}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)+\pi(x^{\prime})}&x=y,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(20)

In our application, it is more convenient to consider a normalized version of the transition matrix
P
given by
S^{\frac{1}{2}}PS^{-\frac{1}{2}}=S^{-\frac{1}{2}}WS^{-\frac{1}{2}}
where
S^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}|x\rangle\langle x|
.
We now claim that the observable whose expectation is measured in Protocol
C.1
directly relates to this normalized transition matrix.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose the certification Protocol
C.1
is performed on copies of the state
\rho
and a model
\Psi
of the quantum state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle
.
Define the ‘phase matrix’ by
F=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle\langle x|
and let
L
be the Hermitian operator given by

\displaystyle L=F\cdot S^{\frac{1}{2}}PS^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot F^{\dagger}.

(21)

We have
L|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
and
\operatorname{tr}(L\rho)=\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathbf{\omega}]
, where
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathbf{\omega}]
denotes the expected output of the certification protocol.

Proof.

The entries of the observable
L
for any
x\in\mathcal{S}
are given by

\displaystyle\langle x|L|y\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi(x)\pi(y)}}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}\cdot e^{i(\phi(x)-\phi(y))}&(x,y)\in E,\\
\frac{1}{N}\cdot\sum_{x^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(x^{\prime},x)\in E}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)+\pi(x^{\prime})}&x=y,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(22)

For
x\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle x|L|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle x|L|x\rangle\cdot\langle x|\psi\rangle+\sum_{y\neq x}\langle x|L|y\rangle\cdot\langle y|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(y,x)\in E}\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}\cdot\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(y,x)\in E}\frac{\sqrt{\pi(x)\pi(y)}}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}\cdot e^{i(\phi(x)-\phi(y))}\cdot\sqrt{\pi(y)}e^{i\phi(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(y,x)\in E}\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}+\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}\right)\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}.

(23)

This shows that
L|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
.
Next we prove that
\operatorname{tr}(L\rho)=\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]
.
Consider subsets of qubits with size
r\leq m
.
There are
N=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{n}{k}
choices for the location of these qubits.
For any
r
, we enumerate the chosen qubits by
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}
and collectively denote them by
k=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}
.
For a fixed
k
, the set
\{z_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n-r}
} denotes all the possible bit strings on the remaining
n-r
bits.
Direct inspection reveals that the observable
L
corresponding to the model
\Psi
can be expressed as

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{r\in[m]}\sum_{k=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}}\sum_{z_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n-r}}|z_{k}\rangle\langle z_{k}|\otimes L_{z_{k}},

(24)

where
L_{z_{k}}
is an operator acting on
r
qubits
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}
, and is given by

\displaystyle L_{z_{k}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{r}\\
\mathrm{dist}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=r\end{subarray}}|\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{z_{k}}\rangle\langle\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{z_{k}}|\text{\quad with \quad}|\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{z_{k}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\Psi(z_{k}^{(\ell_{1})})\cdot|\ell_{1}\rangle+\Psi(z_{k}^{(\ell_{2})})\cdot|\ell_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{|\Psi(z_{k}^{(\ell_{1})})|^{2}+|\Psi(z_{k}^{(\ell_{2})})|^{2}}}.

(25)

In this expression, the binary string
z_{k}^{(\ell)}
equals
\ell\in\{0,1\}^{r}
on bits
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}
and equals
z_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n-r}
on the remaining
n-r
bits.

Let
\bm{\sigma}
denote the classical shadow obtained after performing randomized Pauli measurements on the post-measurement state of qubits
r
.
That is, if the
r
single Pauli measurements return states
|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle,\dots,|\bm{s_{r}}\rangle
,
we set
\bm{\sigma}=\left(3|\bm{s_{1}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{1}}|-\mathds{1}\right)\otimes\left(3|\bm{s_{2}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{2}}|-\mathds{1}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\left(3|\bm{s_{r}}\rangle\langle\bm{s_{r}}|-\mathds{1}\right)
.
It follows from the discussion in Appendix
B
that
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\mathrm{shadows}}[\bm{\sigma}]=\frac{\langle\bm{z_{k}}|\rho|\bm{z_{k}}\rangle}{\operatorname{tr}(\langle\bm{z_{k}}|\rho|\bm{z_{k}}\rangle)}
,
where the expectation is over Pauli measurements on qubits
\bm{k}
.

Using this, we can expand
\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]
as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[m]\\
k=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{z_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{n-r}}\operatorname{tr}(\langle z_{k}|\rho|z_{k}\rangle)\cdot\operatorname{tr}\left(L_{z_{k}}\cdot\frac{\langle z_{k}|\rho|z_{k}\rangle}{\operatorname{tr}(\langle z_{k}|\rho|z_{k}\rangle)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{k},\bm{z_{k}}}\operatorname{tr}\left(L_{\bm{z_{k}}}\cdot\frac{\langle\bm{z_{k}}|\rho|\bm{z_{k}}\rangle}{\operatorname{tr}(\langle\bm{z_{k}}|\rho|\bm{z_{k}}\rangle)}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{k},\bm{z_{k}}}\operatorname{tr}\left(L_{\bm{z_{k}}}\cdot\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\mathrm{shadows}}[\bm{\sigma}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{k},\bm{z_{k}}}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\mathrm{shadows}}[\operatorname{tr}\left(L_{\bm{z_{k}}}\bm{\sigma}\right)]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}].

In the last expression, the expectation is with respect to the location of the Pauli
Z
measurements, their outcomes, as well as the randomized measurements on the remaining qubits.

\sqcap
\sqcup

When we first average over the classical shadows, the shadow overlap
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\omega]
is equal to the average overlap between the postselected state on
\rho
and the postselected state on the target state
|\psi\rangle
. Hence
0\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[\omega]=\operatorname{tr}(L\rho)\leq 1
for any state
\rho
. This implies that
0\preceq L\preceq I
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\lambda_{1}=1-\frac{1}{\tau}
be the second largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix
P
defined with respect to the measurement distribution
\pi(x)
of the state
|\psi\rangle
.
The shadow overlap satisfies

\displaystyle\text{if\hskip 10.22217pt}\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]\geq 1-\epsilon\text{\hskip 10.22217ptthen we have \hskip 10.22217pt}\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\tau\epsilon;

(26)

\displaystyle\text{if\hskip 10.22217pt}\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon\text{\hskip 10.22217ptthen we have \hskip 10.22217pt}\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]\geq 1-\epsilon.

(27)

Proof.

We first study the spectrum of the observable
L
.
From the previous theorem and the fact that
0\preceq L\preceq I
, the eigenvalues of
L
are given by
1=\lambda_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
.
The two operators
P
and
L
are related by a similarity transformation. Hence, they have the same set of eigenvalues.
Let
|\lambda_{i}\rangle
denote the eigenstate of observable
L
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
We claim that the top eigenstate
|\lambda_{0}\rangle
of the operator
L
is the quantum state
|\psi\rangle
.
This can be seen by the direct calculation in Proposition
3
or by noting that the measurement distribution
\pi(x)
is the unique stationary distribution of
P
. Hence, we have
P|\pi\rangle=|\pi\rangle
,
where
|\pi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}|x\rangle
.
From this and the fact that
|\pi\rangle=F^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle
, we have
L|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle
, as claimed.

Now we prove the implication stated in (
26
).
From Proposition
3
, we know that
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathbf{\omega}]=\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]
. Assuming
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]\geq 1-\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathbf{\bm{\omega}}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle+\sum_{i\geq 1}\lambda_{i}\cdot\langle\lambda_{i}|\rho|\lambda_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{using\ }|\lambda_{0}\rangle=|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle+\lambda_{1}\cdot\sum_{i\geq 1}\langle\lambda_{i}|\rho|\lambda_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{definition of\ }\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle+\lambda_{1}\cdot(1-\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle)

\displaystyle\text{since\ }\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1.

By rearranging the two sides of the inequality, we arrive at the bound
\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\lambda_{1}}=1-\tau\epsilon
.
We next prove the implication stated in (
27
).

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}[\mathbf{\bm{\omega}}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}[L\rho]

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle+\sum_{i\geq 1}\lambda_{i}\cdot\langle\lambda_{i}|\rho|\lambda_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\text{using\ }|\lambda_{0}\rangle=|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle\geq 1-\epsilon.

(28)

This concludes the proof of this theorem.

\sqcap
\sqcup

It is worth mentioning that the fast mixing assumption used in our results in this section, in particular Theorem
4
, assumes a non-zero gap
\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1}\geq\frac{1}{\tau}
between the first and the second eigenvalues of the transition matrix
P
, and therefore a
unique
eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=1
.
There are, however, quantum states
|\psi\rangle
whose transition matrix possesses a degenerate eigenspace
\Pi_{0}
with
\lambda_{0}=1
.
Such states can occur when the support of the measurement distribution
\pi(x)
is a union of disjoint subsets of the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
As shown in Appendix
D
, these states are non-generic since Haar random states exhibit a non-zero gap
\tau\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)
with high probability.
A simple modification of the proof of Theorem
4
shows that in these cases, our protocol certifies the overlap
\operatorname{tr}[\Pi_{0}\rho]
between the lab state
\rho
and the degenerate subspace
\Pi_{0}
that includes the target state
|\psi\rangle
.
That is if the shadow overlap
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]\geq 1-\epsilon
then we have
\operatorname{tr}[\rho\Pi_{0}]\geq 1-\tau\epsilon
.
We discuss this in more depth in Appendix
I
in the context of the GHZ state.

Theorem 5

(Sample Complexity of level-
m
of Protocol
C.1
)
.

Using
T=2^{2m}\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
samples of the state
\rho
, the empirical average
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}
computed by the protocol is within
\epsilon
additive distance from the shadow overlap
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

This claim is a consequence of conventional tail bounds.
Note
\mathop{\bf E\/}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}\right)=\mathop{\bf E\/}(\bm{\omega})
.
Since subset sizes
r
satisfy
r\leq m
, it also holds that

\displaystyle{|\hskip-1.59016pt|L_{\bm{z_{k}}}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{1}\leq\max_{r\in[m]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{r}\\
\mathrm{dist}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})=r\end{subarray}}{|\hskip-1.59016pt||\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{\bm{z_{k}}}\rangle\langle\Psi^{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}_{\bm{z_{k}}}||\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{1}\leq 2^{m-1}

(29)

We have
{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\bm{\sigma}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{\infty}=2^{m}
from Appendix
B
.
From these bounds, we get
|\bm{\omega}|=|\operatorname{tr}(\bm{L_{z_{k}}}\bm{\sigma})|\leq 2^{2m-1}
.
Hence, an application of Hoeffding’s inequality shows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}-\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]\right|>\epsilon\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{T\epsilon^{2}}{2^{2m-1}}}.

(30)

Hence, when the number of samples
T
satisfies

T\geq 2^{2m}\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right),

the empirical average
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\omega_{t}}
is within
\epsilon
additive distance from the expected value
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\bm{\omega}]
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

\sqcup

Theorem 6

(Efficient certification using level-
m
of Protocol
C.1

Consider a probability distribution

Our goal is to analyze the spectral gap of the transition matrix

D.3
A tighter analysis

To obtain a tight analysis of the spectral gap, we consider a notion known as ‘resistance’ that improves and generalizes the path congestion method discussed in the warm-up analysis before.

The idea of this technique is based on the multi-commodity flow in which we route a unit flow from any vertex
x
to
y
by splitting it among several paths that connect
x
and
y
such that no edge is congested. This is in contrast to the path congestion method where the flow only follows one such path
\gamma_{xy}
.
More formally, let
\mathcal{P}_{xy}
be set of simple directed paths connecting
x
to
y
. Then a (multi-commodity) flow is a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\cup_{x\neq y}\mathcal{P}_{xy}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{xy}}f(p)=1
for all two distinct vertices
x\neq y
.
Given a flow
f
, we define resistance
R(f)
by

\displaystyle R(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{e\in E}\frac{1}{Q(e)}\sum_{x,y}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{xy}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}p\ni e}\pi(x)\pi(y)f(p)|p|

(53)

where as before the weight
Q(e)=\pi(e^{+})P(e^{+},e^{-})
of an edge
e=(e^{+},e^{-})
is the probability of the transition
(e^{+},e^{-})
occurring in the random walk.

Proposition 15

(Multi-commodity flows vs spectral gap, cf.
[
Sin92
]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{1}
be the second largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix
P
for a reversible Markov chain. For any flow
f
, it holds that

\displaystyle 1-\lambda_{1}\geq\frac{1}{R(f)}.

(54)

We apply this proposition to the random walk given in (

). Following (

), we can rewrite the resistance as

\displaystyle\bm{R}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{n}{\bm{S}}\max_{e=(e^{+},e^{-})}\frac{\bm{z}_{e^{+}}+\bm{z}_{e^{-}}}{\bm{z}_{e^{+}}\bm{z}_{e^{-}}}\sum_{x,y}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{xy}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}p\ni e}\bm{z}(x)\bm{z}(y)f(p)|p|.

We are going to divide the vertices into two sets of good

D.3.1
Geometric facts about Boolean hypercube

To bound the resistance

For now, we ignore the non-uniform weights

We would like to create a multi-commodity flow between every pair of vertices that have not been deleted.
The goal is to find a multi-commodity flow that does not congest at any particular edge.

We define a

that exists with high probability and allows us to spread the flow from any vertex

to its neighbors by using edges within some constant Hamming distance of

Even though the bad vertices are removed from the set of available vertices, we find it helpful to consider a

Lemma 17

(Local escape property)
.

There are constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that with probability at least

: (1) each vertex

\alpha n
good neighbors, and (2) there are at least
\alpha n
simple pairwise internally disjoint paths of length
\leq 5
connecting any two good vertices within Hamming distance

of each other.

Proof.

The proof of statement (1) follows immediately from the tail bound (

10

The proof of statement (2) uses the same construction of internally disjoint paths as in the proof of Proposition
11

to

It is not hard to see that because the starting and end vertices are within Hamming distance

of each other, the length of these paths is bounded by

Fix two good vertices

and

The bound (

.
From the tail bound (

) in Proposition

There are at most

D.4

Our final step in constructing a flow between vertices of the hypercube

is to insert the deleted vertices in

We achieve this simply by splitting a flow to or from a bad vertex between its good neighbors.
We then use the multi-commodity flow construction in the last section to move this flow to any other (good or bad) vertex.

We are now ready to put the previous steps together and prove Theorem

Consider an edge

The flow that we have developed has length

good neighbors of

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2})\cdot\frac{1}{\bm{S}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\bm{z}(e^{+})}{\bm{z}(e^{-})}\right)\cdot\sum_{y\in V}\bm{z}(y)\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha n}.

Appendix E
Enforcing fast mixing

As discussed in the Section
3.1
, the relaxation time
\tau
of the Markov chain associated with the target state
|\psi\rangle
provides an upper bound on the sample efficiency of our certification procedure.
If this relaxation time is superpolynomial in
n
, then we have no rigorous guarantee that our protocol can certify the lab state
\rho
efficiently.
What we can do, though, is modify the Markov chain (and hence the target state), replacing it by a new Markov chain that does mix rapidly.
Thus we can efficiently certify whether the lab state has high fidelity with this adjusted target state.
This new target state, however, could potentially be very different from the original state.

Our starting point is the notion of
local escape property
introduced formally in Definition
24
.
Examining this property offers a way to enforce fast mixing for a given query model
\Psi
of the target state
|\psi\rangle
.
To achieve this, each time the model
\Psi
is queried, we test the value
\pi(x)
of the queried vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
as well as its local neighborhood.
If these values satisfy local constraints mentioned in Definition
24
, the original value
\Psi(x)
is returned.
Otherwise, a value
\nu>0
is reported instead of
\Psi(x)
.
This effectively defines a modified query model
\Psi^{\prime}
(also shown in Figure
1
).

When the query model
\Psi
is known to return normalized values (i.e.
\sum_{x}|\Psi(x)|^{2}=1
), this parameter can be set as
\nu=2^{-n}
.
More generally, the normalization factor
\sum_{x}|\Psi(x)|^{2}
serves as a hyperparameter for this scheme and needs to be set in advance.
We show in Theorem
25
that this strategy indeed ensures that the local escape property is satisfied for all the vertices.
Hence, the modified model
\Psi^{\prime}
induces a random walk with a mixing time
\tau\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.

More formally, we start by restating the notion of local escape property, which was considered earlier in Appendix
D.3.1
in a slightly less general form.

Definition 24

(Local escape property, generalized)
.

Consider the random walk over
\{0,1\}^{n}
with stationary distribution
\pi(x)
and transition probabilities defined in Equation (
8
).
We say a vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is
(c_{\ell},c_{u})
-good when
c_{\ell}\cdot 2^{-n}\leq\pi(x)\leq c_{u}\cdot 2^{-n}
for some
c_{\ell}<1
and
c_{u}>1
.
This random walk is defined to satisfy the local escape property with parameters
(\alpha,c^{\prime}_{u},c_{u},c_{\ell})
when

(0)

Each vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has a probability bounded from above by
\pi(x)\leq c^{\prime}_{u}\cdot 2^{-n}
,

(1)

Each vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
has at least
\alpha n
neighbors that are
(c_{\ell},c_{u})
-good, and

(2)

There are at least
\alpha n
pairwise internally-disjoint paths of length
\leq 5
connecting any two
(c_{\ell},c_{u})
-good vertices within Hamming distance
3
of each other.

We now introduce a scheme based on this property that ensures the fast mixing of the Markov chain associated with a query model
\Psi
.
In this scheme, every time the model
\Psi
is queried with some vertex
x\in\{0,1\}
, we perform the following checks:

-

First, we check if
\pi(x)\leq c^{\prime}_{u}\cdot 2^{-n}
.

-

Next, we look at all the neighbors of vertex
x
.
For each neighbor, we check if it has at least
\alpha n
neighbors that are considered
(c_{\ell},c_{u})
-good.

-

Finally, starting from vertex
x
, we find all the vertex pairs within a Hamming distance
3
of each other. These vertices must be connected by internally-disjoint paths of length
\leq 5
such that one of these paths includes vertex
x
. For each such vertex pair, we check if there are at least
\alpha n
paths consisting entirely of
(c_{\ell},c_{u})
-good vertices.

If any of the above checks are not passed, we return the value
\nu=2^{-n}
for the vertex
x
. Otherwise, we output the original value
\Psi(x)
.
This value is chosen assuming
\sum_{x}|\Psi(x)|^{2}=1
.
We suppose the value of
\sum_{x}|\Psi(x)|^{2}
is given as input to this scheme.
For other normalization factors, the parameter
\nu
,
c^{\prime}_{u}
,
c_{u}
,
c_{\ell}
can be adjusted accordingly.

Note that the last condition can be efficiently checked by starting from the vertex
x
and creating all the internally-disjoint paths of length
\leq 5
that include
x
.
These paths are constructed before in the proof of Lemma
17
via the following procedure repeated for any
i\in[n]
:
Choose the
i
’th bit of the starting vertex and flip it.
Then moving rightward from bit
i+1
to
n
and back to

Appendix G
Quantum phase states

Quantum phase states are defined as
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle
for some function
\phi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.
These states include interesting families of quantum states such as graph states, which are resource states for measurement-based quantum computing, and a subset of Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial-time (IQP) circuits
[
BJS11
]
, which are the basis of some proposed quantum supremacy experiments.
For suitable choices of the function
\phi(x)
, phase states can form highly-entangled states that are indistinguishable from Haar random states.
This indistinguishability can be information theoretical or computational, as shown in
[
JLS18
,
BS19
]
, leading to a notion of quantum pseudorandom states with applications in quantum cryptography.
Due to their particular structure, these states may also be amenable to efficient tomography.

A recent work of
[
ABDY22
]
proves among other things that when the complex phases are given by
(-1)^{f_{d}(x)}
with
f_{d}(x)
being a degree-
d
Boolean polynomial over
n
variables, then this state can be learned using
\Theta(n^{d})
copies and using only separable measurements.
Here, we analyze our certification protocol for phase states with an
arbitrary
choice of the function
\phi(x)
by bounding the sample and query complexity as in Theorem
5
.

The measurement distribution of a phase state
|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}
is the uniform distribution over the
n
-dimensional Boolean hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Therefore, the transition matrix in Equation (
20
) is equivalent to the conventional (lazy) random walk on a degree-
n
graph.
In this walk, any vertex
x
with probability
1/2
does not change, or otherwise, it transitions to another vertex
y\neq x
with Hamming distance
1
from
x
.
The relaxation time of this walk is given by:

Lemma 27
.

The relaxation time of a lazy random walk on the
n
-dimensional hypercube with uniform edge weights is given by
\tau=n
.

Proof.

The proof is standard and, for example, can be found in
[
Roc15
, Spectral Methods]
.
Alternatively, we can show this from the following expression for the transition matrix
P
of this walk:

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

(78)

where
X_{i}
is the Pauli-
X
matrix acting on qubit
i\in[n]
.
We see that the top two eigenvalues of

are
\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2n}\cdot n=1
and
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2n}(n-2)=1-\frac{1}{n}
.
This implies that
\tau=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1}}=n
.

\sqcup

This relaxation time leads to the proof of the result in Section
2
, which claims an
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
sample complexity (or
\mathcal{O}(n/\epsilon)

Appendix H
Gapped ground states

In this section, we analyze the performance of Protocol
C.1
for a family of gapped ground states, known as stoquastic or sign-free ground states, as well as their “phase-shifted” versions.

Definition 28

(Stoquastic Hamiltonians)
.

A Hamiltonian
H
is sign-free (a.k.a stoquastic) in the standard basis
\{|x\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
if all the off-diagonal terms of the Hamiltonian are non-positive. That is

\displaystyle\langle x|H|y\rangle\leq 0\quad\text{for}\quad x\neq y.

(79)

Fact H.1
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be the unique ground state of a stoquastic Hamiltonian.
This state is a sign-free quantum state of the form
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{p(x)}|x\rangle
where the support of
p(x)
is a connected set.

Sign-free Hamiltonians are ubiquitous in quantum many-body physics.
They are also particularly significant due to their compatibility with classical simulations using Monte Carlo techniques
[
BGL22
]
.
The condition (
79
) on the Hamiltonian entries helps circumvent the well-known sign problem that emerges when attempting to simulate general Hamiltonians.

Definition 29

(phase-shifted version of a sign-free state)
.

We say that a state
|\psi\rangle
is the phase-shifted version a given sign-free ground state
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{p(x)}|x\rangle
if
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{p(x)}e^{i\phi(x)}|x\rangle
for some function
\phi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.

Quantum phase states, which we considered in Appendix
G
, are a particular example of states in Definition
29
.
These states are phase shifted with respect to the state
|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
, which is the ground state of the sign-free Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|-\rangle\langle-|_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\setminus i}
.

In what follows, we are interested in
n
-qubit stoquastic Hamiltonians that are gapped or mildly gapless with their energy gap
\gamma
lower bounded by
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
.
A recent result of
[
BGL22
]
shows that the unique ground state of such Hamiltonians admits a fast-mixing random walk based on the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.
We extend this result to show that the random walk (
20
) in our framework is also fast-mixing for such states.

Before formally stating the result, we set up some notations following
[
BGL22
]
.
Suppose the sign-free Hamiltonian
H
is
\kappa
-local (which may or may not be geometrically-local) with spectral (energy) gap
\gamma
.
We consider the level
m=\kappa
of the random walk in (
20
).
This means that the transitions are performed between vertices of Hamming distance
\leq\kappa
from each other.
Let

N=\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\binom{n}{i}=\mathcal{O}(n^{\kappa}).

Also define the sensitivity parameter

\displaystyle s=\max_{x\neq y}|\langle x|H|y\rangle|\frac{|\psi(x)|}{|\psi(y)|}.

(80)

It is shown in
[
BGL22
]
that for sign-free Hamiltonian, the sensitivity parameter is bounded by
s\leq\max_{y}\langle y|H|y\rangle-E_{0}
where
E_{0}
is the ground state energy of the system.

Assuming the ground state is unique, the random walk defined in (
20
) is irreducible. By design, it is also aperiodic and satisfies the detailed-balanced condition.
The following theorem establishes a
\operatorname{poly}(n)
bound on the parameter
\tau
in Protocol
C.1
.

Theorem 30

(cf.
[
BGL22
]
)
.

The relaxation time
\tau
of the random walk in (
20
) for the unique ground state of a sign-free Hamiltonian with gap
\gamma
and sensitivity
s
is bounded by

\displaystyle\tau\leq\frac{2Ns}{\gamma}.

(81)

Proof.

The statement can be obtained by minor changes to the proof of
[
BGL22
]
.
Their proof relies on the fact that
|\psi(x)\psi(y)\langle x|H|y\rangle|\leq 2Ns\pi(x)P(x,y)
.
This follows from
P(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)+\pi(y)}
, the definition of parameter
s
in Equation (
80
), and the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{|\psi(x)\psi(y)\langle x|H|y\rangle|}{\pi(x)P(x,y)}=N\cdot|\langle x|H|y\rangle|\cdot\left(\frac{|\psi(x)|}{|\psi(y)|}+\frac{|\psi(y)|}{|\psi(x)|}\right)\leq 2Ns.

(82)

This concludes the proof.

J.2
Learning neural quantum states

Neural quantum states are a family of parameterized quantum states

|\psi_{w}\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{w}(x)|x\rangle,

where the complex amplitudes
\psi_{w}(x)
are given by the output of
a neural network whose parameters are denoted by
w
.

The choice of the neural network architecture and how complex numbers
\psi_{w}(x)
are represented by the network result in different ansatz of quantum states.
For instance, past works have studied the expressiveness of neural quantum stated based on restricted Boltzmann machines
[
CT17
]
, convolutions neural networks
[
CNC19
]
, and recurrent neural networks
[
HAGH+20
]
.
In some cases, the neural network includes complex weights and directly outputs the amplitudes.
In other cases, networks with real weights
w
are used for the real
\mathrm{Re}(\psi(x))
and imaginary
\mathrm{Im}(\psi(x))
parts,
or the magnitude
|\psi(x)|
and phase
\phi(x)
parts of
\psi(x)=|\psi(x)|e^{i\phi(x)}
.
In this section, for a clearer exposition, we focus on (real) feedforward neural networks which represents the magnitude and phase components of the amplitudes. Arguments similar to the one used in this section apply to other cases as well.

More formally, a feedforward neural network, consists of various layers each including multiple “neurons”. The
j
’th neuron in the
i
’th layer is defined to be a function that maps the input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}
to

x\mapsto\sigma(\langle w_{ij}|x\rangle+b_{ij}).

This map consists of an affine part
\langle w_{ij}|x\rangle+b_{ij}
with some
|w_{ij}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}
and
b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and a non-linear part involving the
activation function

\sigma
which is often chosen to be rectified linear unit (ReLU)
z\mapsto\max\{0,z\}
or sigmoid
z\mapsto\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
; see
[
GBC16
]
for an introduction to deep learning.

The feedforward neural net, overall, corresponds to the mapping

\displaystyle f(x;w)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sigma_{L}\left(w_{L}\sigma_{L-1}\left(\cdots w_{2}\sigma_{1}(w_{1}x+b_{1})+b_{2}\cdots\right)+b_{L}\right),

(90)

where
w_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j\in[n_{i}]}|j\rangle\langle w_{ij}|\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}}
and
b_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j\in[n_{i}]}b_{ij}\cdot|j\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
. Each function
\sigma_{i}
is now a coordination-wise activation function.
We collectively denote these parameters by

w=(w_{1},b_{1},\dots,w_{L},b_{L}).

The number of layers
L
is called the
depth
and the maximum number of neurons across layers
W=\max_{i\in[L]}n_{i}
is known as the
width
of the neural network.

Given two feedforward neural networks
f(x;w_{\text{abs}})
and
g(x;w_{\text{phase}})
, we define a neural quantum state
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{w}(x)|x\rangle
with amplitudes
\psi_{w}(x)=|\psi(x)|e^{i\phi(x)}
and weights
w=(w_{\text{abs}},w_{\text{phase}})
by assigning

\displaystyle\log|\psi_{w}(x)|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=f(x;w_{\text{mag}})\text{\quad and \quad}\phi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=g(x;w_{\text{phase}}).

(91)

Going back to the learning framework in Appendix
J
, we assume that a quantum state
\rho
admits an accurate neural net representation
\psi_{w}(x)
for some weight parameters
w
.
Our goal is find the parameter
w
given independent copies of the state
\rho
.
In practice, this is often achieved by performing stochastic gradient descent or similar optimization subroutines,
where the objective function is e.g., the fidelity, log-likelihood, or KL divergence.
We now show that the sample complexity of the learning algorithm in Appendix
J
can be rigorously analyzed.

This is achieved by defining a covering net over the set of feedforward neural networks such that any neural quantum state
\psi_{w}(x)
is close to a point in the covering net.
The following lemma translates the approximation error in coarse-graining the set of neural nets to the corresponding error in the neural quantum state.

Lemma 34

(Approximation error for pure states, cf.
[
SSC22
]
)
.

Given two quantum states

\displaystyle|\psi(x)\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\psi(x)|e^{i\phi(x)}\cdot|x\rangle\text{\hskip 10.22217ptand \hskip 10.22217pt}|\psi^{\prime}(x)\rangle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\psi^{\prime}(x)|e^{i\phi^{\prime}(x)}\cdot|x\rangle,

(92)

suppose that for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, it holds that
|\log|\psi(x)|-\log|\psi^{\prime}(x)||\leq\varepsilon/2
and
|\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon/2
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle|^{2}}{|\langle\psi|\psi\rangle|\cdot|\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi^{\prime}\rangle|}\geq 1-\varepsilon^{2}.

(93)

The covering number of the set of feedforward neural networks has been found before in
[
BFT17
]
and depends on the spectral and
(2,1)
-norm of the weight matrices in the network.
The covering number in general scales exponentially with the depth of the network
L
.
Given a matrix
A
with entries
A_{ij}
, its
(2,1)
-norm is defined by the
\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}|A_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

Proposition 35

(Covering number for neural quantum states, cf.
[
BFT17
]
)
.

Consider neural quantum states represented, in the sense of Equation (
91
) by the set of feedforward neural networks with weight matrices that have bounded spectral norm
{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\cdot|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{2}
and
(2,1)

{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\cdot|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{2,1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\sigma_{L}\left(w_{L}\sigma_{L-1}\left(\cdots w_{2}\sigma_{1}(w_{1}x)\cdots\right)\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{|\hskip-1.59016pt|w^{\intercal}_{i}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{2}\leq s_{i},{|\hskip-1.59016pt|w_{i}^{\intercal}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{2,1}\leq r_{i}\},

(94)

We assume a Boolean input data
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and networks with depth
L
,
n_{i}
neurons in layer
i\in[L]
, and width
W=\max_{i\in[L]}n_{i}
.
The activation functions

have Lipschitz constants

\displaystyle\log\left(M(\mathcal{F},\varepsilon,{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\cdot|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{F})\right)\leq\frac{n\log(2W^{2})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{L}\rho^{2}_{j}s^{2}_{j}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(\frac{r_{i}}{s_{i}}\right)^{2/3}\right)^{3}.

(95)

Roughly speaking, if the spectral norm of all weight matrices is bounded by

The exponential scaling with respect to the depth

of the network is in contrast to the case of quantum circuits where the size of the covering net scales linearly with the size of the circuit.

Our learning algorithm in Theorem

relies on Promise (1) stated in Appendix
J.1
.
That is, we assume that one of the models obtained via casting the covering net in Proposition
35
has high overlap with the lab state

.
If this condition is satisfied for the set of neural quantum states prior to applying the covering net, then we claim that it also holds for the states in the covering net.
To see this, consider the neural quantum state
|\psi_{w^{*}}\rangle
such that
\langle\psi_{w^{*}}|\rho|\psi_{w^{*}}\rangle\geq 1-\theta

Let

.
We know from the approximation guarantee in Lemma

also requires that Promise (2) or (2’) hold for the neural quantum states in the covering net

.

To this end, suppose that the state

, as considered above, satisfies the local escape property with parameters

We know from the definition of the covering net for neural quantum states that the amplitudes

of the closest sate in the covering net are within

multiplicative error of

.

multiplicative error of the original constants

This means that if the relaxation time of the state

is bounded by

in the covering net.

\{\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{M}\}
obtained using Proposition

\rho

An illustrative application of the setup of Appendix

\rho

More concretely, suppose we have a family of
\kappa

E

{|\hskip-1.59016pt|h_{j}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{\infty}\leq 1

{|\hskip-1.59016pt|\frac{\partial h_{j}}{\partial a_{k}}(a)|\hskip-1.59016pt|}_{\infty}\leq c

a\in[-1,1]^{L}

\gamma
to be a lower bound on the gap of
H(a)
for
a\in[-1,1]^{L}

We assume that for one or more instantiations of parameters

\rho

We start with Proposition
37

This entails setting up a discretization of the set of parameters

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cover}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{-1+j\frac{2}{N_{c}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}j\in\{0,\dots,N_{c}\}\}^{\times L}.

(96)

b\in[-1,1]^{L}

Proposition 37

Consider the family of gapped Hamiltonians

Denote the ground state projector by

Define a path

It is shown in

with a relaxation time

and at least one ground state

such that

.
Then, there is a learning algorithm that uses

T=2^{2\kappa}\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\left(L\log\left(\frac{c^{\prime}LE}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right)+\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)

copies of the lab state

and outputs a Hamiltonian parameter

as in Proposition
37
, such that its ground state

satisfies

.

Appendix K
Estimating sparse observables

We have thus far developed the tools needed to certify a learned model
\Psi
of the quantum state
|\psi\rangle
which admits efficient query and sample access to amplitudes
\psi(x)
.
Having such a model, one can use Monte Carlo methods to efficiently estimate the expectation of any sparse observable such as the energy of a local Hamiltonian or low-degree polynomials of the reduced density operators such as the Rényi entanglement entropy.

We first show this for an observable
O
which is a
g
-sparse observable on
n
-qubits in the standard basis.
This means for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, there are at most
g
states
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\langle x|O|y\rangle\neq 0
.
For instance, a
\kappa
-local Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i=1}^{M}H_{i}
involving
M
local terms is
\mathcal{O}(2^{\kappa}M)
-sparse.

The expectation value of an observable
O
defined by
\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\psi|x\rangle\cdot\langle x|O|y\rangle\cdot\langle y|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}\cdot\langle x|O|y\rangle\cdot\frac{\langle y|\psi\rangle}{\langle x|\psi\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\sum_{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\langle\bm{x}|O|y\rangle\neq 0}\langle\bm{x}|O|y\rangle\cdot\frac{\langle y|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}.

(100)

Given a sampled bit string
\bm{x}
, the expression inside the expectation can be efficiently calculated when
g\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
and we have access to the (un-normalized) amplitudes via a query model
\Psi
.
By sampling
\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
, we can estimate the expression in (
100
) using empirical averaging.
To determine the number of samples needed to obtain an accurate estimate of
\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle
, we bound the variance of expression (
100
).
The random term in (
100
) equals
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\frac{\langle\bm{x}|O|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}
.
We first consider the real part of this term, with the imaginary part following a similar argument.
We have

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Var\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}|O|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}|O|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\left|\frac{\langle\bm{x}|O|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\bm{x}\sim|\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle|^{2}}\frac{\langle\psi|O|\bm{x}\rangle\langle\bm{x}|O|\psi\rangle}{|\langle\psi|\bm{x}\rangle|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\psi|O|x\rangle\langle x|O|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle

(101)

For practically relevant cases such as a
\kappa
-local Hamiltonians with bounded local terms, we have
\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle\leq{|\hskip-1.59016pt|O^{2}|\hskip-1.59016pt|}\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
.
It follows from the Chebyshev inequality that the number of samples required to estimate
\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle
up to
\epsilon
additive error with probability
\geq 1-\delta
is upper bounded by
\frac{8\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle}{\delta\cdot\epsilon^{2}}
.

As considered in
[
HKP20
]
, one could further boost this performance by using the median-of-the-means (MoM) estimator.
This estimator is robust to outliers and results in an exponential improvement with respect to the error probability
\delta
.
This entails collecting
K
batches of size
B
of samples denoted by
\bm{x_{1}},\dots,\bm{x_{BK}}
.
We then find the empirical averages
\bm{o_{k}}
of each batch
k\in[K]
and compute their median
\bm{o_{MoM}}
.
More formally—again focusing on the real part—we have

\displaystyle\bm{o_{\mathrm{MoM}}}=\mathbf{Median}\left\{\bm{o_{1}},\dots,\bm{o_{K}}\right\}\quad\textrm{where}\quad\bm{o_{k}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=B(k-1)+1}^{Bk}\mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x_{i}}|O|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x_{i}}|\psi\rangle}\right)

(102)

Let
B=\frac{34\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle}{\epsilon^{2}}
and
K=2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
.
Then, for all
\epsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{Pr}\left[\left|\bm{o_{\mathrm{MoM}}}-\operatorname{Re}\left(\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle\right)\right|\geq\epsilon\right]\leq\delta.

These findings are summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 38

(Estimating sparse observables)
.

For any sparse observable
O
and state
|\psi\rangle
,
one can estimate
\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle
up to an error
\epsilon
with probability
\geq 1-\delta

using the median-of-the-means estimator and

\displaystyle T=272\cdot\frac{\langle\psi|O^{2}|\psi\rangle}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)

(103)

samples
\bm{x}\sim|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}
, as well as, at most
gT
queries to the entries
O_{xy}
of observable
O
and
(g+1)T
queries to the amplitudes
\psi(x)
of the state
|\psi\rangle
.

A similar argument can be applied to estimate other non-linear functions such as Rényi entanglement entropies.
In this case, the expected value is given by
\operatorname{tr}\left(O|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes\ell}\right)
for some
\ell\geq 2
.
Here for simplicity, we only consider quadratic functions
\ell=2
.
We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(O|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\sim|\psi(x)|^{2}\\
\bm{x^{\prime}}\sim|\psi(x^{\prime})|^{2}\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{y,y^{\prime}}\langle\bm{x},\bm{x^{\prime}}|O|y,y^{\prime}\rangle\cdot\frac{\langle y|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}|\psi\rangle}\cdot\frac{\langle y^{\prime}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle}

(104)

As before, given samples
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x^{\prime}}
, the expectation can be efficiently computed if the observable
O
is sparse.

To empirically estimate this quantity, we use the median-of-the-means estimator with
K
batches of size
B
as before.
As an application, consider estimating the
purity
of the reduced density operator
\rho_{A}=\operatorname{tr}_{B}\left(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{AB}\right)
given by
\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho^{2}_{A}\right)
.
In this case, the observable

O=\mathrm{SWAP}_{AA^{\prime}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{BB^{\prime}}\text{\quad with \quad}\mathrm{SWAP}_{AA^{\prime}}\ |x\rangle_{A}|x^{\prime}\rangle_{A^{\prime}}=|x^{\prime}\rangle_{A}|x\rangle_{A^{\prime}}.

We indeed have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{A}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{SWAP}_{AA^{\prime}}\cdot\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{A^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|_{AB}\langle\psi|_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\cdot\mathrm{SWAP}_{AA^{\prime}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{BB^{\prime}}\cdot|\psi\rangle_{AA^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle_{BB^{\prime}}.

Plugging this in Equation (
104
) gives us

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{A}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\bm{x}_{A},\bm{x}_{B})\sim|\psi(x)|^{2}\\
(\bm{x^{\prime}}_{A},\bm{x^{\prime}}_{B})\sim|\psi(x^{\prime})|^{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\langle\bm{x}^{\prime}_{A}\bm{x}_{B}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x}_{A}\bm{x}_{B}|\psi\rangle}\cdot\frac{\langle\bm{x}_{A}\bm{x^{\prime}}_{B}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\bm{x^{\prime}}_{A}\bm{x^{\prime}}_{B}|\psi\rangle}\right)

(105)

A direct calculation similar to Equation (
101
) shows that the variance of this case is bounded by
\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}O^{2}|\psi\rangle^{\otimes 2}=1
.
Hence, the sample complexity of estimating this quantity scales as
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\log(\frac{1}{\delta})\right)
.
Following
[
HKP20
]
, we may also improve this variance by computing the empirical average in each batch a set of samples
\bm{x_{1}},\dots,\bm{x_{B}}
using the concept of U-statistics.

L.2
Benchmarking noisy quantum devices

In the numerical experiment in Figure
3
, we estimate fidelity, shadow overlap, and XEB of two families of noisy quantum states: Haar random and phase states.
We track these metrics as the strength of the noise is varied.
We explore both white noise and coherent noise, as detailed below.

Haar random states:

A Haar random state in a
d

In the white noise model, the state is subjected to a global depolarizing channel
(1-p)\cdot|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+p\cdot\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}
with the noise parameter
p
.
In the coherent noise model, the probability amplitudes are randomly changed according to
psi[b] = psi[b] + p * (normal(gen) + rmi * normal(gen)) / d

where
normal(gen) + rmi * normal(gen)
is a complex centered Gaussian with variance 1.

In the coherent noise model, the fidelity
fid
between the original state
psi
and the noisy state
psi_noisy
is calculated as the squared overlap between the two states.
We then estimates the shadow overlap and (XEB) using a Monte Carlo approach.
In each measurement round (for a total of
N
rounds), a computational basis state
outcome_b
is sampled from the noisy probability distribution
prob_noisy
.
The true log-probability
true_logp
of this outcome with respect to the original distribution
prob
is calculated, and the contribution to the XEB estimator is computed as
exp(true_logp) / N
.
This value is then normalized using

normalized_XEB = (XEB - 1 / d) / (normalization_XEB - 1 / d)
;
where
normalization_XEB
corresponds to the sum of squared probabilities of the ideal target distribution
prob
.

The shadow overlap (linearly shifted according to Appendix
F
) is evaluated by averaging over

1.0 * (d - 1) / d * 2 * (local_fid - 0.5) + (1.0 / d)

,

where the local overlaps

are calculated as follows:
First, two computational basis states
b0
and
b1
are randomly drawn to simulate the post-measurement state after measuring the qubits in positions other than
rand_i
.
Then the overlap

between the noisy and original post-measurement states are found.

When the white noise model is used, the fidelity is computed as

.
The local overlaps

p

The XEB estimation is done in the same way as before, by sampling from the original distribution

Phase states with non-uniform amplitudes:
The state in this case is generated by first preparing the product state

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\left(\cos(\hbox{\pagecolor{light-gray}{rotation[i]}})\cdot|0\rangle+\sin(\hbox{\pagecolor{light-gray}{rotation[i]}})\cdot|1\rangle\right),

where

In the next step complex phases

Two types of noise models are applied to this state: (1) white noise applied as a global depolarizing channel with parameter

More precisely, we apply a random Gaussian phase shift to each amplitude:

for the noise parameter

.
This is followed by adding a random complex term to each amplitude
psi[b] = psi[b] + 3 / 4 * p * (normal(gen) + rmi * normal(gen)) / sqrt(d)
,
where as before

The noisy state

is then normalized, and the fidelity, shadow overlap and XEB estimation procedures are similar to the ones explained in the white noise simulation.

L.3
Optimizing quantum circuits for state preparation

The goal of this simulation is to prepare a target state, which is the output of a 1D Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) circuit with additional
T

The target state is created by applying a sequence of Hadamard (

A greedy algorithm explores the action space sequentially.

The fidelity between the prepared state and the target state is estimated by repeatedly generating random bit strings and calculating their complex phases.
For each bit string, we keep track of

for a

After calculating

This phase difference is then used to calculate the fidelity by averaging over
10,000

If instead of the circuit representation, we use the matrix product representation (MPS) of the target state, we can obtain the complex phase of a given bit string by directly contracting the MPS.

To calculate the shadow overlap, we first generate a random bit string where all qubits except

are assigned random values (

We then simulate the sequence of actions (

) on this bit string, but only update the

In the query phase of estimating the shadow overlap, we find the post-measurement state of

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\exp\left(2\pi i(\hbox{\pagecolor{light-gray}{how\textunderscore many\textunderscore T\textunderscore true}}/8)\right)|1\rangle
