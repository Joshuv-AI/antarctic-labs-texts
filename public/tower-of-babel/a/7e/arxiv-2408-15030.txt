A generalization of Grünbaum’s inequality in RCD ( 0 , N ) (0,N) -spaces
Victor-Emmanuel Brunel, Shin-ichi Ohta, Jordan Serres

Abstract
We generalize Grünbaum’s classical inequality in convex geometry to curved spaces with nonnegative Ricci curvature, precisely, to RCD ⁡ ( 0 , N ) \mathrm{RCD}(0,N) -spaces with N ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) N\in(1,\infty) as well as weighted Riemannian manifolds of Ric N ≥ 0 \mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0 for N ∈ ( − ∞ , − 1 ) ∪ { ∞ } N\in(-\infty,-1)\cup\{\infty\} . Our formulation makes use of the isometric splitting theorem; given a convex set Ω \Omega and the Busemann function associated with any straight line, the volume of the intersection of Ω \Omega and any sublevel set of the Busemann function that contains a barycenter of Ω \Omega is bounded from below in terms of N N . We also extend this inequality beyond uniform distributions on convex sets. Moreover, we establish some rigidity results by using the localization method, and the stability problem is also studied.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

In the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is a well known fact that, given any probability measure, there exists a point such that any closed halfspace including this point has mass at least
1/(n+1)

[
26
, Theorem 1]
,
[
18
, Lemma 6.3]
.
This bound is tight since there are probability measures (e.g., the uniform distribution on the vertex set of a simplex) such that every point is in a closed halfspace with mass at most
1/(n+1)
.
Hence, this result becomes very little informative when the dimension
n
is very large because it does not allow to discriminate between points in general.
Nonetheless, the bound can be improved, provided that the measure satisfies some geometric properties.
For instance, for the uniform distribution on a bounded convex set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Grünbaum’s inequality
[
26
, Theorem 2]
states that any closed halfspace including the barycenter (centroid) of
\Omega
must have volume at least
(n/(n+1))^{n}
.
Grünbaum’s inequality remains informative in any dimension since
(n/(n+1))^{n}\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}
,
and it has been extended to log-concave distributions (among which uniform distributions on convex sets)
[
32
, Lemma 5.12]
.
Namely, for any log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, any closed halfspace containing the barycenter must have mass at least
\mathrm{e}^{-1}
, independently of the dimension
n
.
By virtue of the Prékopa–Leindler inequality, it is in fact sufficient to consider the one-dimensional case in order to prove the inequality for log-concave measures
[
32
]
.
Furthermore, as we will see in this work, a family of functional inequalities that generalize the Prékopa–Leindler inequality, namely the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequalities, allow to generalize the bound even further, to broader classes of distributions.

The problem described here is directly related to a notion that is central in descriptive statistics, called Tukey’s depth.
Given a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Tukey’s depth of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
relative to
\mu
is defined as
D_{\mu}(x):=\inf_{H}\mu(H)
, where the infimum is taken over all closed halfspaces
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
x
.
Then, the inequalities discussed above can be summarized as follows:

•

For any distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
D_{\mu}(x)\geq 1/(n+1)
; moreover, there exists
\mu
for which
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}D_{\mu}(x)=1/(n+1)
.

•

If
\mu
is the uniform distribution on a convex set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
D_{\mu}(x)\geq(n/(n+1))^{n}
and
x
can be chosen to be the barycenter of
\Omega
.

•

If
\mu
is a log-concave distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
D_{\mu}(x)\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}
and one can choose
x
to be the barycenter of
\mu
.

The function
D_{\mu}
is called a depth function
[
52
]
because it provides a measure of centrality relative to
\mu
.
Roughly speaking,
D_{\mu}(x)
is the amount of mass that can be separated from
x
by a hyperplane.
Therefore, the aforementioned results indicate that under a shape constraint on the measure
\mu
, even in large dimensions, there exist deep points.
Deep points are relevant in various applications:
In statistics, a deepest point (called Tukey median) provides a notion of center of a distribution that is robust to perturbations of that distribution
[
37
, Section 3.2.7]
, which is important when dealing with data from that distribution, that may have been corrupted. In numerical optimization, existence of deep points is essential for cutting plane methods
[
8
]
while Grünbaum’s inequality has also found applications in further convex optimization methods
[
5
]
. On the computational side, finding deep points is relevant in algorithmic geometry
[
15
]
.

In a non-Euclidean setup, a negative result was proved in
[
45
]
, showing that the above inequalities cannot be extended to nonpositively curved spaces in general.
Precisely,
[
45
, Theorem 2]
states that given any Hadamard manifold
M
, for any probability measure
\mu
on
M
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Riemannian volume measure (note that this additional restriction is only technical), there exists
x^{*}\in M
such that any closed halfspace
H
containing
x^{*}
must satisfy
\mu(H)\geq 1/(n+1)
, where
n=\dim M
.
Moreover, there are cases where
\mu
is the uniform distribution on a convex set and the bound is tight.
In this context, a closed halfspace is a subset of
M
of the form
\{y\in M\mid\langle v,\dot{\gamma}_{xy}(0)\rangle\geq 0\}
, for some
x\in M
and
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
, where
\gamma_{xy}
denotes the (unique) minimal geodesic from
x
to
y
.

In this article, we show that under a right framework, the above inequalities can be extended to non-Euclidean setups.
We work on metric measure spaces whose generalized Ricci curvature, in a synthetic sense, is nonnegative.
We appeal to Cheeger–Gromoll-type splitting theorems, which allow, as in the Euclidean case, to reduce the computations to a one-dimensional analysis.

1.2
Notations and definitions

We briefly recall some concepts necessary to explain our results.

1.2.1
Metric geometry.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
be a metric space.
Given
x,y\in X
, a (
minimal
)
geodesic
from
x
to
y
means a path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow X
such that
\gamma(0)=x
,
\gamma(1)=y
, and
\mathrm{d}_{X}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,y)
for all
s,t\in[0,1]
.
We call
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
a
geodesic space
if any pair
x,y\in X
can be connected by a geodesic.
A subset
\Omega\subset X
is said to be (
geodesically
)
convex
if, for any
x,y\in\Omega
, any geodesic between them is included in
\Omega
.
We say that a function
f\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is
convex
if it is convex along all geodesics, i.e.,
f(\gamma(t))\leq(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)
for all
x,y\in X
, all geodesics
\gamma\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow X
from
x
to
y
and all
t\in[0,1]
.
In particular,
\mathrm{supp}(f):=f^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
is a convex set.
We say that
f
is
concave
if
-f
is convex.

A
straight line
is a map
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow X
that satisfies
\mathrm{d}_{X}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, the
Busemann function
associated with
\gamma
is defined as

\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl\{t-\mathrm{d}_{X}\bigl(x,\gamma(t)\bigr)\bigr\},\quad x\in X.

(1.1)

The function
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
is
1
-Lipschitz and can be interpreted as a projection onto
\gamma
.
For instance, in the Euclidean case, we have
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)=\langle v,x-x_{0}\rangle
, where
\gamma(t)=x_{0}+tv
for some point
x_{0}
and unit vector
v
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}(X)
the set of Borel probability measures on
X
and, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, by
\mathcal{P}^{p}(X)
the subset consisting of probability measures with finite
p
-th moment, i.e., those for which the function
\mathrm{d}_{X}^{p}(\cdot,x_{0})
is integrable for some (and hence, all)
x_{0}\in X
.
For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(X)
, a point
x_{0}\in X
attaining

\inf_{z\in X}\int_{X}\mathrm{d}_{X}^{2}(z,x)\,\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)

is called a
barycenter
of
\mu
.
More generally, even when
\mu
only has finite first moment, we can define its barycenter as a point achieving

\inf_{z\in X}\int_{X}\bigl\{\mathrm{d}_{X}^{2}(z,x)-\mathrm{d}_{X}^{2}(z_{0},x)\bigr\}\,\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x),

where
z_{0}\in X
is an arbitrarily fixed point.
In Euclidean spaces, we have the unique barycenter
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\,\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
.

1.2.2
Curvature-dimension conditions.

The curvature-dimension condition for metric measure spaces is a synthetic geometric notion of lower Ricci curvature bound described with the help of optimal transport theory.
For brevity, we consider only the case of nonnegative curvature.

A metric measure space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
will mean a complete separable metric space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
equipped with a Borel measure
\mathfrak{m}
with
\mathfrak{m}(U)\in(0,\infty)
for each nonempty bounded open set
U\subset X
.

Given
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(X)
, the
L^{2}
-Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance
is defined by

W_{2}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1}):=\inf_{\pi}\biggl(\int_{X\times X}\mathrm{d}_{X}^{2}(x,y)\,\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)\biggr)^{1/2},

where
\pi
runs over all couplings of
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}

(i.e.,
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X)
with marginals
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
).
A geodesic
(\nu_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
with respect to
W_{2}
is regarded as an
optimal transport
from
\nu_{0}
to
\nu_{1}
.

For
\nu=\zeta\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathfrak{m}
, we define the
relative entropy

S_{\infty}(\nu):=\int_{X}\zeta\log\zeta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

(
S_{\infty}(\nu):=\infty
if
\int_{\{\zeta>1\}}\zeta\log\zeta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\infty
),
and the
Rényi entropy

S_{N}(\nu):=\begin{cases}-\int_{X}\zeta^{(N-1)/N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}&N\in(1,\infty),\\
\int_{X}\zeta^{(N-1)/N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}&N\in(-\infty,0).\end{cases}

We say that a metric measure space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
satisfies the
curvature-dimension condition

\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
(or
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
is a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space
) if the corresponding entropy
S_{N}
is convex with respect to
W_{2}
in the sense that,
for any absolutely continuous measures
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(X)
, there exists a geodesic
(\nu_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
between them with respect to
W_{2}
such that

S_{N}(\nu_{\lambda})\leq(1-\lambda)S_{N}(\nu_{0})+\lambda S_{N}(\nu_{1})

(1.2)

holds for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
More generally, one can define
\mathrm{CD}(K,N)
-spaces for any
K\in\mathbb{R}
, where the coefficients
1-\lambda
and
\lambda
in (
1.2
) are replaced with distorsion coefficients that depend on
\lambda
,
K
and
N

[
47
]
.

Consider an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
endowed with a measure
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}
for a smooth function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
, where
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
is the volume measure induced from
g
.
The
weighted Ricci curvature
(a.k.a.
Bakry–Émery–Ricci curvature
) of the weighted Riemannian manifold
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
is defined by

\mathrm{Ric}_{N}(v):=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(v)+\mathrm{Hess}\,\psi(v,v)-\frac{\langle\nabla\psi,v\rangle^{2}}{N-n}

(1.3)

for
v\in TM
and
N\in(-\infty,0]\cup(n,\infty)
(
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}
is the usual Ricci curvature of
g
).
We also define
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}
and
\mathrm{Ric}_{n}
as the limits. By definition, we have the monotonicity

\mathrm{Ric}_{n}\leq\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\leq\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\leq\mathrm{Ric}_{N^{\prime}}\leq\mathrm{Ric}_{0}

(1.4)

for
n<N<\infty
and
-\infty<N^{\prime}<0
(
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}
can be also regarded as
\mathrm{Ric}_{-\infty}
).
Thus, for example,
\mathrm{Ric}_{N^{\prime}}\geq 0
is a weaker condition than
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
.
Note also that
\mathrm{Ric}_{n}=\lim_{N\downarrow n}\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
can make sense only when
\psi
is constant.

A weighted Riemannian manifold
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
is a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space if and only if the weighted Ricci curvature
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}
is nonnegative
[
13
,
14
,
31
,
40
,
41
,
44
,
46
,
47
]
.

Moreover, the equivalence between
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
and
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
also holds true for Finsler manifolds
[
38
]
.
Then, to develop a genuinely Riemannian theory, the
Riemannian curvature-dimension condition

\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
was introduced as the combination of
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
and the so-called
infinitesimal Hilbertianity
(or, equivalently, the linearity of heat flow)
[
1
,
19
,
22
]
.
In
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-spaces, we can obtain much finer properties including a splitting theorem discussed below.
We refer to
[
48
]
for a recent survey.

1.2.3
Splitting theorems.

For a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of nonnegative Ricci curvature, Cheeger–Gromoll’s celebrated
splitting theorem

[
12
]
asserts that, if there is a straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
, then
M
is isometric to a product space
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is a Riemannian manifold of nonnegative Ricci curvature, and the Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
as in (
1.1
) coincides with the projection to
\mathbb{R}
.
The splitting theorem was generalized to
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-spaces by Gigli
[
22
, Theorem 1.4]
,
[
23
]
.
In short, it states that if
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-space for some
N\in(1,\infty)
including a straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow X
, then
X
is isometric to a product space
\mathbb{R}\times Y
where
(Y,\mathrm{d}_{Y},\mathfrak{n})
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N-1)
-space when
N\geq 2
, and
Y
is a singleton when
N\in(1,2)
.
(We will not consider the case of
N=1
, since
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
with
N>1
is weaker than
\mathrm{RCD}(0,1)
.)

In general, such an isometric splitting is false for
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-spaces, unless the infinitesimal Hilbertianity is assumed (see (
B
) in Section
7
).
This is why, in our main results, we consider only
\mathrm{RCD}
-spaces, although some intermediate results may be stated in more generality for
\mathrm{CD}
-spaces.

1.3
Main results

Theorem 1.1
(Main theorem;
N>1
).

Let
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-space with
N\in(1,\infty)
,
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(X)
with a measurable function
\rho\colon X\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
, and
x_{0}\in X
be any barycenter of
\mu
.
Suppose that there is a straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow X
.

(i)

If
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
is concave on
\rho^{-1}((0,\infty))
for some
\beta>N
, then the Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta},

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}.

(ii)

If
\log\rho\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is concave, then
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}.

(iii)

If
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is convex for some
\beta<-1
, then we have

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta},

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in X\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}.

Remark 1.2
.

Under the hypothesis in (
i
), it follows from Lemma
2.2
that
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mu)
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,\beta)
-space.
Then, by virtue of Lemma
2.1
, the support of
\mu
is necessarily bounded.
In particular,
\mu
is of finite second moment.

The condition on
\rho
in (
i
) is equivalent to the concavity of
\tilde{\rho}
by setting
\tilde{\rho}(x):=\rho(x)^{1/(\beta-N)}
if
\rho(x)>0
and
\tilde{\rho}(x):=-\infty
if
\rho(x)=0
.
Note that, on the one hand,
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
is convex in all the cases (
i
)–(
iii
).
On the other hand, given a convex set
\Omega\subset X
with
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\in(0,\infty)
, the uniform distribution
\mu_{\Omega}:=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)^{-1}\chi_{\Omega}\,\mathfrak{m}
on
\Omega
satisfies the hypothesis of (
i
) for any
\beta>N
, where
\chi_{\Omega}
is the indicator function of
\Omega
(with value
1
on
\Omega
and
0
on
X\setminus\Omega
).
Hence, taking the limit as
\beta\downarrow N
yields the following corollary, as a direct extension of Grünbaum’s inequality.
By a
barycenter
of
\Omega
, we will mean a barycenter of
\mu_{\Omega}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a convex set in an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
with
N\in(1,\infty)
such that
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\in(0,\infty)
,
and let
x_{0}\in X
be a barycenter of
\Omega
.
Then, for any straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow X
,
the associated Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\bigl(\{x\in\Omega\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N}\cdot\mathfrak{m}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\bigl(\{x\in\Omega\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N}\cdot\mathfrak{m}(\Omega).

We remark that a barycenter of
\Omega
may not be unique in this generality.
For instance, consider the cylinder
X=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
endowed with the
2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, which is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,2)
-space.
Then, for
\Omega=[-1,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we find that any point on the circle
\{0\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a barycenter of
\Omega
.
It seems unclear (to the authors) if every barycenter of
\Omega
lives in
\Omega
.

Remark 1.4
.

A subset of the form
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}((-\infty,r])
, for some straight line
\gamma
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
, is called a (closed)
horoball
.
Thus, Corollary
1.3
can be rephrased by saying that every horoball (or the closure of the complement of a horoball) including
x_{0}
has mass at least
(N/(N+1))^{N}\cdot\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
.
In Euclidean spaces, horoballs are simply closed halfspaces.
In general, horoballs are not geodesically convex, unless
X
is nonpositively curved.
See, for example,
[
29
]
concerning convex optimization on Hadamard spaces by means of horoballs.

We also study when equality holds in (
1.5
).
Roughly speaking, equality holds only when
\mu
has a cone structure (see Theorem
4.3
for the precise statement).
This kind of
rigidity
for geometric and analytic inequalities is one of the major problems in comparison geometry and geometric analysis (see, e.g.,
[
24
,
43
]
).
Cavalletti–Mondino’s
localization
(also called
needle decomposition
)
[
10
]
, together with a detailed one-dimensional analysis, plays a crucial role in our rigidity result.
We can even consider the
stability
problem in a similar way
(see Section
6
).
We refer to
[
25
]
for a stability result concerning Grünbaum’s inequality in the Euclidean setting, in terms of the volume of the symmetric difference from a cone.

The main ingredients of the proofs of our results are
Gigli’s splitting theorem for
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-spaces and Cavalletti–Mondino’s localization for essentially non-branching
\mathrm{CD}(K,N)
-spaces as we mentioned above.
In fact, the formulation of our results using a straight line is strongly inspired by the splitting theorem.
Both these ingredients are valid only for
N\in(1,\infty)
in this generality.

Nonetheless, in the smooth setting of weighted Riemannian manifolds, the isometric splitting is known by Lichnerowicz, Fang–Li–Zhang
[
30
,
20
]
(
N=\infty
) and Wylie
[
50
]
(
N\in(-\infty,1)
),
and the localization is also available by Klartag
[
27
]
.
Thus, we have the following counterparts to Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3
.

Theorem 1.5
(Main theorem;
N=\infty
,
N<-1
).

Let
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
,
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}
, be a complete weighted Riemannian manifold of
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
for
N=\infty
or
N\in(-\infty,-1)
, where
\psi\in C^{2}(M)
is bounded from above,
and
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}\in\mathcal{P}^{1}(M)
with
\rho\colon M\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
.

(i)

Suppose that
N=\infty
and
\log\rho\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is concave.
Then, for any barycenter
x_{0}\in M
of
\mu
and any straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
,
the associated Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in M\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1},

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in M\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

(ii)

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mu\bigl(\{x\in M\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})\}\bigr)

We remark that the upper boundedness of

2.1
Properties of
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-spaces

The next lemma may be a well known fact (cf.
[
51
, Theorem 7]
), but we could not find in the literature.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
be a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space with
N\in(1,\infty)
.
If
\mathfrak{m}(X)<\infty
, then the diameter of
X
is finite.

The proof is based on the following Bishop–Gromov volume comparison theorem
[
47
, Theorem 2.3]
:

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B(x,R))}{\mathfrak{m}(B(x,r))}\leq\biggl(\frac{R}{r}\biggr)^{N}\quad\ \text{for all}\ x\in X,\ 0<r<R,

(2.1)

where
B(x,r)
denotes the open ball with center
x
and radius
r
.
Fix
x_{0}\in X
and assume on the contrary that there is a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
X
such that
\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})\geq k
for all
k\geq 1
.
Then we infer from (
2.1
) that, for
k\geq 2
,

\mathfrak{m}\Bigl(B\bigl(x_{k},\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})+1\bigr)\Bigr)\leq\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})+1}{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1}\biggr)^{N}\mathfrak{m}\Bigl(B\bigl(x_{k},\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1\bigr)\Bigr).

Thus, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\bigl(B(x_{0},1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}\Bigl(B\bigl(x_{k},\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})+1\bigr)\Bigr)-\mathfrak{m}\Bigl(B\bigl(x_{k},\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\biggl\{\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})+1}{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1}\biggr)^{N}-1\biggr\}\cdot\mathfrak{m}\Bigl(B\bigl(x_{k},\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\biggl\{\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})+1}{\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},x_{k})-1}\biggr)^{N}-1\biggr\}\cdot\mathfrak{m}(X)

\displaystyle\to 0

as
k\to\infty
.
This implies
\mathfrak{m}(B(x_{0},1))=0
, a contradiction.
∎

In particular, every convex set
\Omega\subset X
with
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\in(0,\infty)
(as in Corollary
1.3
) is bounded,
since
(\Omega,\mathrm{d}_{X}|_{\Omega},\mathfrak{m}|_{\Omega})
is again a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space.
The above lemma can be applied to the space
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mu)
in Theorem
1.1
(
i
) thanks to the following fact.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-space with
N\in(1,\infty)
,
and
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}
be a measure with
\rho\colon X\longrightarrow[0,\infty)

such that
\mu(X)>0
and
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
is concave on
\rho^{-1}((0,\infty))
for some
\beta>N
.
Then,
(\mathrm{supp}(\mu),\mathrm{d}_{X}|_{\mathrm{supp}(\mu)},\mu)
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,\beta)
-space.

We give an outline of the proof for completeness
(we refer to
[
47
, Theorem 1.7(ii)]
for the smooth case).
Note that it is sufficient to show that
(\mathrm{supp}(\mu),\mathrm{d}_{X}|_{\mathrm{supp}(\mu)},\mu)
satisfies
\mathrm{CD}(0,\beta)
.
Since
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})
is non-branching by
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
,
in view of
[
47
, Proposition 4.2]
,

(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
being a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space is equivalent to the concavity of
\lambda\longmapsto\zeta_{\lambda}(\eta(\lambda))^{-1/N}
for almost every geodesic
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow X
along which an
L^{2}
-optimal transport
(\nu_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
is done,
where
\nu_{\lambda}=\zeta_{\lambda}\mathfrak{m}
.

When we consider
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}
instead of
\mathfrak{m}
, the corresponding density function of
\nu_{\lambda}
becomes
\zeta_{\lambda}\rho^{-1}
, provided
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{\lambda})\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
.
Indeed, if
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{0})\cup\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{1})\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, then
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{\lambda})\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
by the convexity of
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
.
We deduce from the concavity of
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
and the Hölder inequality that

\displaystyle[\zeta_{\lambda}\rho^{-1}]\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)^{-1/\beta}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\zeta_{\lambda}\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)^{-1/N}\Bigr)^{N/\beta}\Bigl(\rho\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}\Bigr)^{(\beta-N)/\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl((1-\lambda)\zeta_{0}\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{-1/N}+\lambda\zeta_{1}\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{-1/N}\Bigr)^{N/\beta}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigl((1-\lambda)\rho\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda\rho\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}\Bigr)^{(\beta-N)/\beta}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\lambda)[\zeta_{0}\rho^{-1}]\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{-1/\beta}+\lambda[\zeta_{1}\rho^{-1}]\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{-1/\beta}.

Therefore,
\lambda\longmapsto[\zeta_{\lambda}\rho^{-1}](\eta(\lambda))^{-1/\beta}
is concave, and
(\mathrm{supp}(\mu),\mathrm{d}_{X}|_{\mathrm{supp}(\mu)},\mu)
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,\beta)
-space.
∎

Next we summarize some key ingredients for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, based on Gigli’s splitting theorem and an observation related to the lemma above.
We remark that, though Lemma
2.2
could be also extended to include the cases of
\beta=\infty
and
\beta<-1
as in the next proposition, we restricted ourselves to
\beta>N
for simplicity.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(X,\mathrm{d}_{X},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-space for some
N\in(1,\infty)
and
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow X
be a straight line.
Then, there exists an
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N-1)
-space
(Y,\mathrm{d}_{Y},\mathfrak{n})
and an isometry
T\colon(X,\mathrm{d}_{X})\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}\times Y,d)
such that

•

d((s,y),(t,z))=\left(|s-t|^{2}+\mathrm{d}_{Y}(y,z)^{2}\right)^{1/2}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
y,z\in Y;

•

\Pi_{\mathbb{R}}(T(x))=\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)
for all
x\in X
, where
\Pi_{\mathbb{R}}\colon\mathbb{R}\times Y\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the projection
;

•

T\#\mathfrak{m}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\otimes\mathfrak{n}
, where
T\#\mathfrak{m}
denotes the push-forward of
\mathfrak{m}
by
T
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, let
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}
be a probability measure on
X
for some measurable function
\rho\colon X\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
and put
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\#\mu=w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
.

(i)

If
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
is concave on
\rho^{-1}((0,\infty))
for some
\beta>N
, then
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
is concave on
w^{-1}((0,\infty))
.

(ii)

If
\log\rho\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is concave, then
\log w
is concave.

(iii)

If
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is convex for some
\beta<1
, then
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
is convex.

The first part of the proposition comes from
[
22
, Theorem 1.4]
.
Then, using the product structure
X=\mathbb{R}\times Y
,
w
is explicitly given by

w(t)=\int_{Y}\rho(t,y)\,\mathfrak{n}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y).

(
i
)
Though we give a direct proof, one can also reduce the concavity of
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
to Lemma
2.2
, for it is equivalent to
\mathrm{CD}(0,\beta)
of the interval
(\mathrm{supp}(w),|\cdot|,w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
(see the beginning of Section
3
).
Take
a<b
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and define functions
f,g,h\colon Y\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

h(y):=\rho\bigl((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b,y\bigr),\qquad f(y):=\rho(a,y),\qquad g(y):=\rho(b,y).

For any minimal geodesic
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow Y
in
(Y,\mathrm{d}_{Y})
, observe that the curve

\lambda\,\longmapsto\,\bigl((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b,\eta(\lambda)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}\times Y=X

is also a minimal geodesic.
Thus, the assumed concavity of
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
implies

h\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}\geq(1-\lambda)f\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda g\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}.

Now, in order to apply the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality on
(Y,\mathrm{d}_{Y},\mathfrak{n})
in
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, one needs to check that
(Y,\mathrm{d}_{Y},\mathfrak{n})
is non-branching, which is the case thanks to
[
17
]
.
Hence, we obtain from the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality
\mathrm{BBL}(0,N-1)
with parameter
p=1/(\beta-N)>0
(as in
[
2
, Definition 1.1]
) that, since
p/(1+(N-1)p)=1/(\beta-1)
,

\int_{Y}h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\geq\Biggl((1-\lambda)\biggl(\int_{Y}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}+\lambda\biggl(\int_{Y}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}\Biggr)^{\beta-1}.

This yields the concavity of
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
.

(
ii
)
We similarly find

\log h\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)\geq(1-\lambda)\log f\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)+\lambda\log g\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr).

Then,
\mathrm{BBL}(0,N-1)
with
p=0
(in other words, the Prékopa–Leindler inequality) shows the claim

\int_{Y}h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\geq\biggl(\int_{Y}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1-\lambda}\biggl(\int_{Y}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{\lambda}.

(
iii
)
In this case, we have

h\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)\geq\Bigl((1-\lambda)f\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda g\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}\Bigr)^{\beta-N},

then the claim follows from
\mathrm{BBL}(0,N-1)
with
p=1/(\beta-N)>-1/(N-1)
.
∎

2.2
Euclidean case

In this subsection, we describe our results in the Euclidean setting.
We first remark that the curvature-dimension condition is intimately related to Borell’s
s
-concavity
[
6
]
.
For
s\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
a,b\geq 0
, the
s
-mean
is defined by

\mathcal{M}_{s}(a,b;\lambda):=\begin{cases}\bigl((1-\lambda)a^{s}+\lambda b^{s}\bigr)^{1/s}&\text{if }s>0,\text{ or if }s<0\text{ and }ab>0,\\
0&\text{if }s<0\text{ and }ab=0,\\
a^{1-\lambda}b^{\lambda}&\text{if }s=0,\\
\min\{a,b\}&\text{if }s=-\infty,\\
\max\{a,b\}&\text{if }s=\infty.\end{cases}

We say that a positive Radon measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
s
-concave
if, for any Borel measurable sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

\mu\bigl((1-\lambda)A+\lambda B\bigr)\geq\mathcal{M}_{s}\bigl(\mu(A),\mu(B);\lambda\bigr),

where
(1-\lambda)A+\lambda B:=\{(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\mid x\in A,\,y\in B\}
is the Minkowski sum.
Then, Borell’s classical result
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
shows the following.

Theorem 2.4
(Borell’s theorem).

•

If
s>1/n
, then there does not exist any
s
-concave measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

If
s=1/n
, then the only
s
-concave measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are constant multiplications of the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}
.

•

For
s\in(0,1/n)
, a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
s
-concave if and only if it has a density function
\rho
with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
\rho^{p}
is concave with
p=s/(1-sn)
.

•

For
s\in[-\infty,0]
, a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
s
-concave if and only if it has a density function
w
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on an affine subspace
V
and
w
satisfies

w\bigl((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\bigr)\geq\mathcal{M}_{q}\bigl(w(x),w(y);\lambda\bigr)

(2.2)

for all
x,y\in V
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, where
q:=s/(1-s\dim V)

(
which should be understood as
-1/\dim V
if
s=-\infty)
.

We remark that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
s\in(0,1/n]
, while
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
is a convex set in
V
for
s\in[-\infty,0]
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
s\in[-\infty,1/n]
and
\mu
be an
s
-concave measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, for any affine subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the orthogonal projection
\Pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow V
,

\Pi\#\mu
has a density function
w
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
V
, which satisfies (

).

It is readily seen that
\Pi\#\mu
is
s
-concave,
then we apply Theorem
2.4
.
∎

In particular, if
\dim V=1
, then

satisfies (
2.2
) with

, which corresponds to Proposition
2.3
in the Euclidean case (with

).

In view of Theorem
2.4
,
as a particular case of Theorem

, we obtain the following result.
Note that

since

.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
s\in(-1,1/n]
and

-concave in its support.
Then, every closed halfspace

, understood as

.
Moreover, as mentioned in the introduction, the case of log-concave distributions (

3
One-dimensional analysis

In this section, as a key step for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we first state Grünbaum’s inequality in the case of one-dimensional
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-spaces.

3.1
One-dimensional
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-spaces

We consider a one-dimensional space
((a,b),|\cdot|,\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
, where
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
,
|\cdot|
denotes the absolute value giving rise to the canonical distance on
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, and
\psi\colon(a,b)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function.
In this case, being a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space is equivalent to

\psi^{\prime\prime}-\frac{(\psi^{\prime})^{2}}{N-1}\geq 0

(3.1)

in the weak sense (recall the definition of
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}
from (
1.3
)).
By setting
w=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}
, this is equivalent to the concavity of
w^{1/(N-1)}
if
N\in(1,\infty)
, the concavity of
\log w
if
N=\infty
, and to the convexity of
w^{1/(N-1)}
if
N\in(-\infty,1)
.

When we assume that
\mu=w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
is a probability measure, on the one hand, for
N\in(1,\infty)
, observe from the concavity of
w^{1/(N-1)}
that
\mu
can only be supported on some bounded interval, thereby
a>-\infty
and
b<\infty
.
On the other hand, for
N=\infty
(resp.
N\in(-\infty,1)
), the concavity of
\log w
(resp. convexity of
w^{1/(N-1)}
) does not imply such boundedness, by letting
\log w=-\infty
(resp.
w^{1/(N-1)}=\infty
) outside the support of
\mu
.

For
N\in(1,\infty)
, the space
([0,\infty),|\cdot|,Nx^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
is a model
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space, that is, it enjoys equality in (
3.1
).
Moreover, normalized as a probability space,

([0,1],|\cdot|,Nx^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)

(3.2)

is a model space that also reaches the equality case in Grünbaum’s inequality.
Indeed, its barycenter is given by

\int_{0}^{1}x\cdot Nx^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{N}{N+1},

and we have

\int_{0}^{N/(N+1)}Nx^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\left(\frac{N}{N+1}\right)^{N}.

In the original Grünbaum’s inequality, this model corresponds to the projection of the uniform distribution over a finite cone along its axis.

3.2
Case of
N\in(1,\infty)

We first consider the case of
N\in(1,\infty)
.

Lemma 3.1
(Grünbaum’s inequality on intervals;
N>1
).

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<0<b
and
w\colon(a,b)\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
be a nonnegative function such that
w^{1/(N-1)}
is concave for some
N>1
and

\int_{a}^{b}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1,\qquad\int_{a}^{b}xw(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0.

Then, we have

\int_{a}^{0}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N},\qquad\int_{0}^{b}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N}.

(3.3)

Let
R(x):=\int_{a}^{x}w(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
be the cumulative distribution function, which satisfies
0\leq R\leq 1
,
R(a)=0
and
R(b)=1
by definition.
Note also that

\int_{a}^{b}R(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\Bigl[xR(x)\Bigr]_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}xw(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=b.

(3.4)

Since
w^{1/(N-1)}
is concave, the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality
[
6
,
7
]
with parameter
1/(N-1)
implies that
R^{1/N}
is concave.
Here we also give a detailed proof for later use in the rigidity problem.
Fix
x,y\in(a,b)
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
For
t\in(0,1)
, we take
\sigma(t)\in(a,x)
and
\tau(t)\in(a,y)
satisfying

\frac{1}{R(x)}\int_{a}^{\sigma(t)}w(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{R(y)}\int_{a}^{\tau(t)}w(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=t.

By differentiating in
t
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}w\bigl(\theta(t)\bigr)\theta^{\prime}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t&\geq\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl((1-\lambda)w\bigl(\sigma(t)\bigr)^{1/(N-1)}+\lambda w\bigl(\tau(t)\bigr)^{1/(N-1)}\Bigr)^{N-1}\\
&\qquad\times\biggl((1-\lambda)\frac{R(x)}{w(\sigma(t))}+\lambda\frac{R(y)}{w(\tau(t))}\biggr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\geq\int_{0}^{1}\bigl((1-\lambda)R(x)^{1/N}+\lambda R(y)^{1/N}\bigr)^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\bigl((1-\lambda)R(x)^{1/N}+\lambda R(y)^{1/N}\bigr)^{N}.\end{split}

is concave as we claimed.

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{1/c}R(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{1/c}^{b}R(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-N/c}^{1/c}R(0)\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+b-\frac{1}{c}

\displaystyle=R(0)\Biggl[\frac{N}{c(N+1)}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N+1}\Biggr]_{-N/c}^{1/c}+b-\frac{1}{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{R(0)}{c}\biggl(\frac{N+1}{N}\biggr)^{N}+b-\frac{1}{c},

In the other case of

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}R(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{-N/c}^{1/c}R(0)\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\biggl(\frac{1}{c}-b\biggr)

The second proof makes use of the localization (a.k.a. needle decomposition), and we additionally assume

The localization technique provides a decomposition of a space into a family of geodesics (called

Through this decomposition, one can reduce a problem into its one-dimensional counterpart.
We refer to
[
10

and
[
27
]
for the precise statement and more information.

Put
\Omega:=\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=\overline{X\setminus\rho^{-1}(0)}

; recall Remark
1.2

Thanks to Lemma
4.1

as a conditional function (as

in
[

We remark that

(equivalently,
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}_{X}(x_{0},\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mu)

since
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(X)
and
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
is

We denote the resulting decomposition by
\{(X_{q},\mathfrak{m}_{q})\}_{q\in Q}
,
where
\mathfrak{m}_{q}
is a probability measure on
X_{q}\subset X
.
The set
X_{q}
is the image of a minimal geodesic (so-called a needle) and carries the natural distance structure as the restriction of
\mathrm{d}_{X}
.
We also have a measure
\mathfrak{q}
on
Q

and
\mathfrak{q}
-almost every needle

satisfies
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)

\int_{X_{q}}\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}_{q}=0.

f=0

\mathfrak{m}
-almost everywhere in

In the current setting,

-negligible since

.
Then, for every

\int_{\Omega}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{Q}\int_{X_{q}}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}_{q}\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q).

We deduce from (

-almost every

(1)

\mathfrak{m}_{q}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)=0
;

(2)

\mathfrak{m}_{q}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)>0
and
X_{q}\subset\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(0)
;

(3)

\mathfrak{m}_{q}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)>0
,
X_{q}\not\subset\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(0)
,
and
X_{q}\cap\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(0)
is a singleton which is the unique barycenter of
\mu_{q}:=\rho\mathfrak{m}_{q}
.

We remark that
\mu_{q}
is not necessarily a probability measure and that the support of
\mu_{q}
is included in
\Omega

We infer from

\int_{Q}\mathfrak{m}_{q}\bigl(\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\bigr)\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)=\mathfrak{m}\bigl(\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega\bigr)=0

that the case (2) is
\mathfrak{q}
-negligible.
In the case (3), we can identify
X_{q}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(X_{q})\subset\mathbb{R}
via
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
.
Then, if we write

through this identification, it follows from Proposition

(or, more directly, from the Hölder inequality as in Lemma
2.2
; see also the proof of Theorem

below) that
w_{q}^{1/(\beta-1)}|_{w_{q}^{-1}((0,\infty))}
(
\beta>N
),
\log w_{q}
(
\beta=\infty
), or
-w_{q}^{1/(\beta-1)}
(
\beta<-1
) is concave.

Therefore, applying Lemmas
3.1
,
3.4
(with
N=\beta
) to each normalized needle
(X_{q},\mu_{q}(X_{q})^{-1}\mu_{q})
satisfying (3), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{q}\bigl(\{x\in X_{q}\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq 0\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\cdot\mu_{q}(X_{q}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{q}\bigl(\{x\in X_{q}\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq 0\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\cdot\mu_{q}(X_{q})

when
\beta>N
or
\beta<-1
, and

\displaystyle\mu_{q}\bigl(\{x\in X_{q}\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\leq 0\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}\cdot\mu_{q}(X_{q}),

\displaystyle\mu_{q}\bigl(\{x\in X_{q}\mid\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\geq 0\}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}\cdot\mu_{q}(X_{q})

when
\beta=\infty
.
Integrating these inequalities in
q
with respect to
\mathfrak{q}
completes the proof of Theorem

,
since
\int_{Q}\mu_{q}(A)\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)=\mu(A)
for measurable sets

.

Remark 4.2
.

On the one hand, the second proof provides a more detailed control at the level of needles (under
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}(X)
).
On the other hand, in the first proof, we have a direct connection between
X
and
\mathbb{R}
via
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
.
To consider the rigidity problem, we need both viewpoints to integrate the one-dimensional information on needles into a global picture of
X
.

4.3
Rigidity

Now, with the help of Lemma
3.2
, we study when equality holds in the generalized Grünbaum’s inequality (
1.5
) with
\beta>N
.
Put
\Omega:=\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
as in the previous subsection, and recall that
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})=0
.

Theorem 4.3
(Rigidity;
N>1
).

In the situation of Theorem
1.1
, let
(Q,\mathfrak{q})
,
(X_{q},\mathfrak{m}_{q})
, and
\mu_{q}=\rho\mathfrak{m}_{q}=w_{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
be the elements of the localization as in Subsection
4.2
.
Suppose
\beta>N
and that equality holds in (
1.5
).
Then, there exists
c>0
such that, for
\mathfrak{q}
-almost every needle
q\in Q
, we have

\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)=[-\beta/c,1/c]
and

\frac{1}{\mu_{q}(X_{q})}w_{q}(x)=c\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{\beta}\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)\biggr)^{\beta-1}

(4.4)

for all
x\in X_{q}\cap\Omega
.
Moreover, regarding
A_{t}:=\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}^{-1}(t)\cap\Omega
as a set in
Y
, we have

\mathfrak{n}(A_{t})=\biggl(\frac{\beta+ct}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{N-1}\mathfrak{n}(A_{1/c}),\qquad[\rho\mathfrak{n}](A_{t})=\frac{c\beta}{\beta+1}\biggl(\frac{\beta+ct}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta-1}

(4.5)

for all
t\in[-\beta/c,1/c]
.

We shall analyze by combining both of the two proofs above.
On the one hand, by the first proof in Subsection
4.1
and Lemma
3.2
(with
N=\beta
), we deduce that
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\#\mu=w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
satisfies
\mathrm{supp}(w)=[-\beta/c,1/c]
and

w(x)=c\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{\beta}x\biggr)^{\beta-1}

for some
c>0
.
On the other hand, in the second proof,
\mathfrak{q}
-almost every needle necessarily satisfies equality in (
4.3
), thereby, we obtain
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)=[-\beta/c_{q},1/c_{q}]
and

\frac{1}{\mu_{q}(X_{q})}w_{q}(x)=c_{q}\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\biggl(1+\frac{c_{q}}{\beta}x\biggr)^{\beta-1}

for some
c_{q}>0
and all
x\in X_{q}\cap\Omega
, where we identified
X_{q}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(X_{q})
via
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
as in the previous subsection.
For
\mathfrak{q}
-almost every
q\in Q
, since
[-\beta/c_{q},1/c_{q}]\subset[-\beta/c,1/c]
, we have
c_{q}\geq c
.
Moreover, by comparing the density at
0
and
1/c
, we infer that
c_{q}=c
necessarily holds.
Therefore, for
\mathfrak{q}
-almost every
q\in Q
,
we obtain
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(X_{q}\cap\Omega)=[-\beta/c,1/c]
and (
4.4
).
The latter equation in (
4.5
) is now straightforward by integrating (
4.4
) in
q\in Q
:

\displaystyle[\rho\mathfrak{n}](A_{t})

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}w_{q}(t)\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)=c\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{\beta}t\biggr)^{\beta-1}\int_{Q}\mu_{q}(X_{q})\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)

\displaystyle=c\biggl(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\biggr)^{\beta}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{\beta}t\biggr)^{\beta-1}.

Next, we have a closer look on the concavity of
w_{q}^{1/(\beta-1)}
.
Letting
\mathfrak{m}_{q}=\zeta_{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, since
(X_{q},\mathfrak{m}_{q})
is a
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-space, we know that
\zeta_{q}^{1/(N-1)}
is concave.
Combining this with the concavity of
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
along
X_{q}
and noting

, by a similar calculation to Lemma

, we obtain

\displaystyle w_{q}\bigl((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\bigr)^{1/(\beta-1)}

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl((1-\lambda)\rho(x)^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda\rho(y)^{1/(\beta-N)}\Bigr)^{(\beta-N)/(\beta-1)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigl((1-\lambda)\zeta_{q}(x)^{1/(N-1)}+\lambda\zeta_{q}(y)^{1/(N-1)}\Bigr)^{(N-1)/(\beta-1)}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\lambda)[\rho\zeta_{q}](x)^{1/(\beta-1)}+\lambda[\rho\zeta_{q}](y)^{1/(\beta-1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)w_{q}(x)^{1/(\beta-1)}+\lambda w_{q}(y)^{1/(\beta-1)}

for all
x,y\in X_{q}\cap\Omega
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Comparing this with (
4.4
),
we find that
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
and
\zeta_{q}^{1/(N-1)}
are both affine along
X_{q}\cap\Omega
and vanish (only) at
-\beta/c

(precisely,
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
and
\zeta_{q}^{1/(N-1)}
on

are the restrictions of such affine functions).
Therefore,
A_{t}
satisfies

\begin{split}\mathfrak{n}(A_{t})&=\int_{Q}\zeta_{q}(t)\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)=\biggl(\frac{t+(\beta/c)}{(1/c)+(\beta/c)}\biggr)^{N-1}\int_{Q}\zeta_{q}(1/c)\,\mathfrak{q}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q)\\
&=\biggl(\frac{ct+\beta}{1+\beta}\biggr)^{N-1}\mathfrak{n}(A_{1/c}),\end{split}

which is the former equation in (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.4

It seems plausible that, under the hypothesis in Theorem

5
Grünbaum’s inequality for
N\in(-\infty,-1)\cup\{\infty\}

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.5
and the associated rigidity result (Theorem
5.4
) concerning the cases of
N=\infty
and
N<-1
, in a similar manner to the case of
N>1
above.
Since the splitting theorem and localization for
\mathrm{RCD}(0,\infty)
-spaces are yet to be known, we restrict ourselves to weighted Riemannian manifolds.

Let
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
be an
n
-dimensional, complete, weighted Riemannian manifold with
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 0
or
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
for
N<-1
, where
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi}\,\mathrm{vol}_{g}
for some
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
.
By the monotonicity (
1.4
),
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 0
is a weaker condition than
\mathrm{Ric}_{N^{\prime}}\geq 0
with
N^{\prime}\geq n
, and
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
is even weaker.
In fact, under
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 0
, the boundedness of the diameter as in Lemma
2.1
does not hold true.
An archetypal example is the Gaussian space
(\mathbb{R},|\cdot|,\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
, which satisfies
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 1
.

We begin with a generalization of Proposition
2.3
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
be an
n
-dimensional complete weighted Riemannian manifold of
\mathrm{Ric}_{N}\geq 0
for
N=\infty
or
N<-1
.
Assume that
\psi
is bounded above and there is a straight line
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
.
Then, there exists an
(n-1)
-dimensional weighted Riemannian manifold
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\mathfrak{n})
with
\mathrm{Ric}_{N-1}\geq 0

(\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 0
if
N=\infty)
and an isometry
T\colon(M,g)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma,\tilde{g})
such that

•

\tilde{g}
is the product of the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}
and
g;

•

\Pi_{\mathbb{R}}(T(x))=\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x)
for all
x\in M;

•

T\#\mathfrak{m}=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\otimes\mathfrak{n}
.

Moreover, let
\mu=\rho\mathfrak{m}
be a probability measure on
M
for some measurable function
\rho\colon M\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
and put
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\#\mu=w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
.

(i)

If
N<-1
and
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
is concave on
\rho^{-1}((0,\infty))
for some
\beta\in(N,-1)
, then
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
is convex.

(ii)

If
N=\infty
and
\log\rho
is concave, then
\log w
is concave.

The first part follows from
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
for
N=\infty
and
[
50
, Corollary 1.3]
for
N<-1
(in fact, it is available for
N<1
).
We identify
M
with
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
, and then
w
is given by

w(t)=\int_{\Sigma}\rho(t,y)\,\mathfrak{n}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y).

As in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we fix
a<b
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and define

h(y):=\rho\bigl((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b,y\bigr),\qquad f(y):=\rho(a,y),\qquad g(y):=\rho(b,y)

for
y\in\Sigma
.
Recall also that, for any minimal geodesic
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow\Sigma
,

\lambda\,\longmapsto\,\bigl((1-\lambda)a+\lambda b,\eta(\lambda)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma=M

is a minimal geodesic.

(
i
)
In this case, we make use of a generalization of the Borell–Brascamp–Lieb inequality in
[
2
,
13
]
to
N<-1
(the estimate below also works for
N<\beta<1
).
We give an outline for thoroughness.
Set

\nu_{0}:=\frac{f}{\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\mathfrak{n},\qquad\nu_{1}:=\frac{g}{\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\mathfrak{n}.

Then, there is a unique
L^{2}
-Wasserstein geodesic
(\nu_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
from
\nu_{0}
to
\nu_{1}
, and we can write
\nu_{\lambda}=\zeta_{\lambda}\mathfrak{n}
.
On the one hand, it follows from
\mathrm{Ric}_{N-1}\geq 0
of
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\mathfrak{n})
that

\zeta_{\lambda}\bigl(T_{\lambda}(y)\bigr)^{1/(1-N)}\leq(1-\lambda)\biggl(\frac{f(y)}{\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}+\lambda\biggl(\frac{g(T_{1}(y))}{\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}

for
\nu_{0}
-almost every
y\in\Sigma

(by
[
40
, (4.7), (4.9)]
), where
T_{\lambda}
denotes the (unique) optimal transport map from
\nu_{0}
to
\nu_{\lambda}
(thereby
T_{\lambda}\#\nu_{0}=\nu_{\lambda}
).
On the other hand, the assumed concavity of
\rho^{1/(\beta-N)}
yields

h\bigl(T_{\lambda}(y)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}\geq(1-\lambda)f(y)^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda g\bigl(T_{1}(y)\bigr)^{1/(\beta-N)}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Sigma}\frac{h}{\zeta_{\lambda}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{\lambda}=\int_{\Sigma}\frac{h(T_{\lambda})}{\zeta_{\lambda}(T_{\lambda})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Sigma}\bigl((1-\lambda)f^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda g(T_{1})^{1/(\beta-N)}\bigr)^{\beta-N}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl\{(1-\lambda)\biggl(\frac{f}{\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}+\lambda\biggl(\frac{g(T_{1})}{\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}\biggr\}^{N-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\biggl\{(1-\lambda)\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}+\lambda\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}\biggr\}^{\beta-1}

by integrating the Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\biggl\{(1-\lambda)\biggl(\frac{f}{\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}+\lambda\biggl(\frac{g(T_{1})}{\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}}\biggr)^{1/(1-N)}\biggr\}^{1-N}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl((1-\lambda)f^{1/(\beta-N)}+\lambda g(T_{1})^{1/(\beta-N)}\bigr)^{\beta-N}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\biggl\{(1-\lambda)\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}+\lambda\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1/(\beta-1)}\biggr\}^{1-\beta}.

Since
\beta-1<0
, this yields the convexity of
w^{1/(\beta-1)}
.

(
ii
)
In this case, we can apply the Prékopa–Leindler inequality from
[
14
, Theorem 1.4]
(with
\lambda=0
).
From the concavity of
\log\rho
, for any minimal geodesic
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow\Sigma
, we obtain

h\bigl(\eta(\lambda)\bigr)\geq f\bigl(\eta(0)\bigr)^{1-\lambda}g\bigl(\eta(1)\bigr)^{\lambda}.

Hence, the Prékopa–Leindler inequality on
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\mathfrak{n})
(under
\mathrm{Ric}_{\infty}\geq 0
) implies

\int_{\Sigma}h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\geq\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{1-\lambda}\biggl(\int_{\Sigma}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{n}\biggr)^{\lambda},

which shows the concavity of
\log w
.
∎

Remark 5.3

As is natural from the monotonicity (

6
Stability estimates

This section is devoted to the stability problem for our Grünbaum’s inequality.
As a generalization of the rigidity, the stability is concerned with what happens when equality nearly holds.
Similarly to the previous sections, we first analyze the one-dimensional case, and use it to study the general case via the localization.
The localization has played a vital role in some stability results, e.g.,
[
9
,
35
,
36
]
on isoperimetric inequalities,
[
4
,
11
]
on the spectral gap (Poincaré inequality).
A stability result in the Euclidean setting can be found in
[
25
]
.

6.1
Case of
N\in(1,\infty)

We first consider (
3.3
) with
N>1
.
Let us begin with the following observation.
For
((a,b),|\cdot|,w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
as in Lemma
3.1
, an immediate application of
[
21
, Theorem 6]
, with
\phi(x)=x^{2}
and
n=N
, implies

2w(0)\int_{0}^{1/(2w(0))}x^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{N^{2}}{2(N+1)(N+2)}\frac{1}{w(0)^{2}},

which can be rewritten as

\frac{2(N+1)(N+2)}{N^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{w(0)^{2}}\leq 12\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(6.1)

Observe that, in the rigidity case (
3.8
), we have

\frac{1}{w(0)^{2}}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\biggl(\frac{N+1}{N}\biggr)^{2N},\qquad\int_{-N/c}^{1/c}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{N}{N+2}.

Remark 6.1
.

The right inequality in (
6.1
) is sharp for
N=\infty
.
Indeed, for the uniform distribution
w\equiv 1/(2\sqrt{3})
on
[-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3}]
, we have
\int_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1
and equality holds in the right inequality.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
((a,b),|\cdot|,w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
be as in Lemma
3.1
, and
put
c:=w(0)/R(0)
.
If

R(0)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N}

(6.2)

holds for some
\varepsilon>0
, then we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|R-F|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{4\sqrt{3}N^{N+1}}{(N+1)^{N}}(1+\varepsilon)\bigl(1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/N}\bigr)\biggl(\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\biggr)^{1/2},

(6.3)

where

F(x):=\biggl(\frac{N}{N+1}\biggr)^{N}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N}

on
[-N/c,1/c]
,
F(x):=0
on
(-\infty,-N/c)
and
F(x):=1
on
(1/c,\infty)
.

Note that
F
is the cumulative distribution function for the rigidity case (
3.8
).

Consider a probability density

u(x)=cR(0)\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N-1}

on
(-N/c,\bar{b})
, whose cumulative distribution function is given by

U(x)=R(0)\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}x\biggr)^{N}.

Note that
R(x)\leq U(x)
by (
3.7
), which implies
\bar{b}\leq b
.
Moreover, we find from
U(\bar{b})=1
that

\bar{b}=\frac{N}{c}\bigl(R(0)^{-1/N}-1\bigr),

and then (
3.3
) yields
\bar{b}\leq 1/c
.
One can also compute that the barycenter
\xi\in(-N/c,\bar{b})
of
u(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
is given by

\xi=R(0)\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}\bar{b}\biggr)^{N}\biggl(\bar{b}-\frac{1}{c}\frac{N}{N+1}\biggl(1+\frac{c}{N}\bar{b}\biggr)\biggr)=\frac{N}{N+1}\biggl(\bar{b}-\frac{1}{c}\biggr)\leq 0.

Similarly to
\int_{a}^{b}R(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=b
in (
3.4
), we can calculate

\xi=\int_{-N/c}^{\bar{b}}xU^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\bar{b}-\int_{-N/c}^{\bar{b}}U(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Then, recalling
a\geq-N/c
from the proof of Lemma
3.1
and
\bar{b}\leq b
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|R-U|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-N/c}^{a}U\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{\bar{b}}(U-R)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\bar{b}}^{b}(1-R)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-N/c}^{\bar{b}}U\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{a}^{b}R\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+b-\bar{b}=-\xi.

Next, observe from (
3.3
) that
U(x)\geq F(x)
, and
\int_{-N/c}^{1/c}F(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1/c
holds similarly to (
3.4
).
Combining these, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|U-F|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-N/c}^{\bar{b}}(U-F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\bar{b}}^{1/c}(1-F)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-N/c}^{\bar{b}}U\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{-N/c}^{1/c}F\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{c}-\bar{b}=-\xi.

Now, if
w
almost attains the Grünbaum bound in the sense of the hypothesis (
6.2
), then we have

\bar{b}\geq\frac{1}{c}\bigl((N+1)(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/N}-N\bigr),

and hence

\xi\geq\frac{N}{c}\bigl((1+\varepsilon)^{-1/N}-1\bigr).

Thus, we deduce from the right inequality in (
6.1
) and the hypothesis (
6.2
)
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|R-F|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq-2\xi\leq\frac{2NR(0)}{w(0)}\bigl(1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/N}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{3}(1+\varepsilon)\frac{N^{N+1}}{(N+1)^{N}}\bigl(1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-1/N}\bigr)\biggl(\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\biggr)^{1/2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.1
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.3
.

In the situation of Theorem
1.1
(

) with

, suppose that

holds for some

.
Then
w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}\#\mu
satisfies (
6.3
) with
\beta
in place of

.

We next consider another immediate consequence of Lemma
6.2
via the localization.
We use the same notations as Subsection
4.2
:

,

, and

Proposition 6.4
.

In the situation of Theorem
1.1
(

) with
\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})=0
, suppose that

\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}(x_{0})=0

6.2
Case of
N\in(-\infty,-1)\cup\{\infty\}

We saw in Subsection
6.1
that a key ingredient to derive a stability estimate is the right inequality in (
6.1
), which gives a lower bound of
w(0)
in term of the second moment for centered distributions.
For
N=\infty
, log-concave probability densities have finite moment of any order (by the fact that they have sub-exponential tails; see, e.g.,
[
28
, Section 2.2]
) and (

) is still available.
For
N<-1

\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-probability densities may not have finite second moment (recall Remark
3.6
).
Moreover, even if the second moment is finite, (
6.1
) for negative
N
seems not known.
Thus, in the following counterpart of Lemma
6.2
, we can use (
6.1
) only when
N=\infty
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
((a,b),|\cdot|,w(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)

(

7
Further problems

We close the article with some further comments and problems.

(A)

The results in Section
5
could be generalized to
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
-spaces with
N=\infty
or
N\in(-\infty,-1)
.
We remark that the curvature-dimension condition for
N<0
was defined in
[
40
]
(see also
[
41
]
for the case of
N=0
).
However, both the splitting theorem and localization are not known even for
N=\infty
, thereby we need to generalize them or consider a different method.

(B)

For
\mathrm{CD}(0,N)
-spaces with
N\in(1,\infty)
, though the localization is known by
[
10
]
under the essential non-branching condition, the isometric splitting does not hold in general.
Indeed,
n
-dimensional normed spaces endowed with the Lebesgue measure satisfy
\mathrm{CD}(0,n)
but do not isometrically split off the real line.
Furthermore, without the essential non-branching condition, even the topological splitting may fail; we refer to
[
33
]
for a counter-example.

(C)

Finsler manifolds provide examples of
\mathrm{CD}
-spaces with possibly asymmetric distance structures (see
[
38
,
42
]
).
In this setting, the localization is known by
[
10
,
41
]
.
Moreover, a weak splitting theorem can be found in
[
39
]
;
for example, there is a one-parameter family of isometric translations (generated by
\nabla\mathbf{b}_{\gamma}
) in the Berwald case.
Since this splitting is not isometric, we do not have an exact formula as in (
4.1
) (consider the case of normed spaces), and it is unclear if Lemma
4.1
can be generalized.

(D)

Our rigidity results (Theorems
4.3
,
5.4
) show that equality in generalized Grünbaum’s inequalities is attained only when the measure
\mu
possesses a cone structure.
As we discussed in Remark
4.4
, we also expect that the set
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
is also a cone, as in the Euclidean setting.
To achieve this goal, we would need a more geometric argument, possibly with the help of
[
3
,
16
]
.

(E)

Once the rigidity as above is established, it is natural to expect a corresponding stability estimate (in a more geometric way than Section
6
), bounding the volume of the symmetric difference from a cone in a certain sense, akin to
[
25
]
.
