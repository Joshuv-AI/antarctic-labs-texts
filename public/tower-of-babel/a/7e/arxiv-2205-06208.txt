Example of 4 4 -pt non-vacuum 𝒲 3 \mathcal{W}_{3} classical block
Mikhail Pavlov

Abstract
In this note, we study a special case of the 4 4 -pt non-vacuum classical block associated with the 𝒲 3 \mathcal{W}_{3} algebra. We formulate the monodromy problem for the block and derive monodromy equations within the heavy-light approximation. Fixing the remaining functional arbitrariness using parameters of the 4 4 -pt vacuum 𝒲 3 \mathcal{W}_{3} block, we compute the 4 4 -pt non-vacuum 𝒲 3 \mathcal{W}_{3} block function.

c
contribution of the operator
\psi(y)
into the classical conformal block. Due to the condition
(
2.21
) the conformal block
\Psi_{W}(y,z_{i})
satisfies the BPZ equation
[
27
]
, which can be rewritten as a differential equation for the function
\psi(y,z_{i})

\left[\frac{d^{3}}{dy^{3}}+4T(y,z_{i})\frac{d}{dy}+\frac{2dT(y,z_{i})}{dy}-4W(y,z_{i})\right]\psi(y,z_{i})=0\;.

(3.6)

The functions
T(y,z_{i})
and
W(y,z_{i})
read

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle T(y,z_{i})=\sum^{4}_{i=1}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{i}}{(y-z_{i})^{2}}+\frac{c_{i}}{(y-z_{i})}\right)\;,\\
\\
\displaystyle W(y,z_{i})=\sum^{4}_{i=1}\left(\frac{q_{i}}{(y-z_{i})^{3}}+\frac{a_{i}}{(y-z_{i})^{2}}+\frac{b_{i}}{(y-z_{i})}\right)~,\end{array}

(3.7)

where we use notations
[
28
]

a_{i}=\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac{6}{c}\frac{W^{(i)}_{-1}\Psi(y,z_{i})}{\Psi(y,z_{i})}\;,\qquad b_{i}=\lim_{c\to\infty}\frac{6}{c}\frac{W^{(i)}_{-2}\Psi(y,z_{i})}{\Psi(y,z_{i})}\;,\qquad c_{i}=\partial_{i}f(z_{i})\;.

(3.8)

A few comments are in order.
First, (
3.6
) is the Fuchsian-type equation
with
4
singular regular points. Second, assuming
q_{i}=0
and
a_{i}=b_{i}=0
we see that the BPZ equation
(
3.6
) reduces to the equation associated with the degenerate operator
V_{(3,1)}
of the Virasoro algebra (see Appendix
A
for details). Third, the conditions (
2.12
) allow fixing out 8 of 12 parameters
(a_{i},b_{i},c_{i})
. Here we leave
(c_{{}_{2}},a_{1},a_{2},b_{2})
independent. Simultaneously setting
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})\rightarrow(1,z,0,\infty)
the expressions (
3.7
) become

T(y,z)=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{(y-1)^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{(y-z)^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon_{3}}{y^{2}}+\frac{c_{2}}{y-z}+\frac{c_{2}\left(z+y-1\right)+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}}{(1-y)y}\;,

(3.9)

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle W(y,z)=\frac{q_{1}}{(y-1)^{3}}+\frac{q_{2}}{\left(y-z\right)^{3}}+\frac{q_{3}}{y^{3}}+\frac{a_{1}}{(y-1)^{2}}+\frac{a_{2}}{\left(y-z\right)^{2}}+\frac{b_{2}}{y-z}\\
\\
\displaystyle-\frac{a_{2}\left(2z+y-1\right)+a_{1}(y+1)+b_{2}\left(z\left(z+y-1\right)+(y-1)y\right)+q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}+q_{4}}{(y-1)y^{2}}\;.\end{array}

(3.10)

The monodromy problem for classical
\mathcal{W}_{3}
blocks.

Let a string
\;\psi_{a}(y,z),\;a=1,2,3
denote three independent solutions of (
3.6
). These solutions of the Fuchsian-type differential equation have non-trivial monodromy along contours enclosing singular points. Here we consider the contour
\Gamma
bypasses the points
(1,z)
. The corresponding monodromy matrix
M_{ab}(\Gamma|z)
is defined by

\psi_{a}(\Gamma\circ y,z)=\psi_{b}(y,z)M_{ab}(\Gamma|z)\;,

(3.11)

where
\Gamma\circ y
denotes a traversal along the contour
\Gamma
. The matrix (
3.11
) also depends on the functions
(c_{{}_{2}},a_{1},a_{2},b_{2})
as parameters.

On the other hand, the main contribution to the monodromy matrix of the auxiliary
5
-pt block along the contour
\Gamma
comes from the term
(z-y)^{\Delta_{\beta}-\Delta_{\alpha}-\Delta_{\psi}}
, where

\Delta_{\alpha}
and
\Delta_{\beta}
are intermediate dimensions of the
5
-pt block. Hence, the block must have
the monodromy matrix

\tilde{M}_{ab}(\Gamma)=\exp[2\pi i\gamma_{a}]\delta_{ab}\;,

(3.12)

in a diagonal basis. The coefficients
\gamma_{a}
are given by (
2.24
). In what follows, we always use the fusion rules (
2.26
) for simplicity.

The monodromy problem is to find solutions of the BPZ equation (
3.6
) with the monodromy matrix (
3.12
). In other words, the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrices
M_{ab}
(
3.11
) and
\tilde{M}_{ab}
(
3.12
) must coincide. This imposes algebraic restrictions on the parameters
(c_{{}_{2}},a_{1},a_{2},b_{2})
, the so-called monodromy equations. Solving these equations and integrating the accessory parameter
c_{{}_{2}}=\partial_{z}f_{W}(z)
, we obtain the classical
\mathcal{W}_{3}
block. It is important to mention that monodromy equations are generally not enough to determine all 4 parameters, which is consistent with the functional arbitrariness of the block.

3.2
Solution of the monodromy problem

HL approximation.

Since the solutions of the equation (
3.6
) with arbitrary classical dimensions and charges are not generally known, we consider the heavy-light (HL) approximation.
More precisely, we assume that the classical dimensions and charges of the operators
\mathcal{O}_{3}
and
\mathcal{O}_{4}
are much larger than the remaining ones
8
8

8

For the case of the Virasoro algebra, more than two heavy operators were also considered, see
[
29
,
30
]
.

\begin{array}[]{c}\epsilon_{1,2},\tilde{\epsilon}\ll\epsilon_{H}\;,\qquad q_{1,2}\ll q_{H}\;.\end{array}

(3.13)

By virtue of (
2.15
), in the zeroth order of the HL approximation the classical dimensions and charges of heavy operators are related by
\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon_{4}
and
q_{3}=-q_{4}
, and henceforth will be denoted by
\epsilon_{H},q_{H}
respectively. For the sake of simplicity, we also set
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}\equiv\epsilon
and
q_{1}=-q_{2}\equiv q
.

Now, we implement the heavy-light expansion

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi(y,z)=\psi^{(0)}(y)+\psi^{(1)}(y,z)+...\;,\qquad T(y,z)=T^{(0)}(y)+T^{(1)}(y,z)+...\;,\\
\\
W(y,z)=T^{(0)}(y)+W^{(1)}(y,z)+...\;,\qquad f(z)=f^{(0)}(z)+f^{(1)}(z)+...\,,\end{array}

(3.14)

where zeroth-order parameters
a^{(0)}_{1,2},b^{(0)}_{2},c^{(0)}_{2}
associated with heavy operators and the classical block
f^{(0)}(z)
are equal to 0. Because of that, we omit the superscript of the parameters, always referring to them in the first order of the HL approximation.

The monodromy matrix along contour
\Gamma
is also expanded up to the first order in the HL approximation

M_{ab}(\Gamma|z)=M_{ab}^{(0)}(\Gamma)+M_{ab}^{(1)}(\Gamma|z)+...\;.

(3.15)

Monodromy matrix in the zeroth order.

In zeroth order, the equation (
3.6
) has the form

D^{(0)}\psi^{(0)}(y)=0\;,\qquad D^{(0)}=\left[\frac{d^{3}}{dy^{3}}+4T^{(0)}\frac{d}{dy}+\frac{2dT^{(0)}}{dy}-4W^{(0)}\right]\;,

(3.16)

where

T^{(0)}=\frac{\epsilon_{H}}{y^{2}}\;,\qquad W^{(0)}=\frac{q_{H}}{y^{3}}\;.

(3.17)

There are three branches of solutions

\psi_{a}^{(0)}(y)=y^{1+p_{a}},\qquad a=1,2,3\;,

(3.18)

where the exponents satisfy the characteristic equation of the third degree

p^{3}_{a}-\alpha^{2}p_{a}-4q_{H}=0\;,\qquad\alpha=\sqrt{1-4\epsilon_{H}}\;.

(3.19)

In order not to clutter up the calculations, we do not write explicit expressions for the roots of
p_{a}
. However, due to the Vieta’s theorem for the cubic equation the exponents
p_{i}
are satisfied by several relations, which are used below. For instance,
p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}=0
and
p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}=4q_{H}
.

The contour
\Gamma
does not encircle the branch points of the solutions (
3.18
) hence the monodromy matrix in the zeroth order is given by the identity matrix,
M_{ab}^{(0)}(\Gamma)=\delta_{ab}
. The monodromy matrix (
3.12
) in the zeroth order of the HL approximation coincides with
M_{ij}^{(0)}(\Gamma)
.

First-order monodromy matrix and monodromy equations.

In the first order of the HL approximation, we have

D^{(0)}\psi^{(1)}(y)=-D^{(1)}\psi^{(0)}(y)\;,\qquad D^{(1)}=\left[4T^{(1)}\frac{d}{dy}+\frac{2dT^{(1)}}{dy}-4W^{(1)}\right]\;,

(3.20)

where

T^{(1)}=\frac{c_{2}(z-1)+2\epsilon}{y-1}-\frac{c_{2}z+2\epsilon}{y}+\frac{c_{2}}{y-z}+\frac{\epsilon}{(z-y)^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{(y-1)^{2}}\;,

(3.21)

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle W^{(1)}=\frac{q}{(y-1)^{3}}-\frac{q}{\left(y-z\right){}^{3}}+\frac{a_{1}}{(y-1)^{2}}+\frac{a_{2}}{\left(y-z\right){}^{2}}+\frac{b_{2}}{y-z}\\
\\
\displaystyle+\frac{\left(2a_{2}z-a_{2}-a_{1}+b_{2}z(z-1)\right)(z-1)}{y^{2}}-\frac{2a_{2}\left(z-1\right)-2a_{1}+b_{2}\left(z-1\right){}^{2}}{y-1}\;.\end{array}

(3.22)

Note that equation (
3.20
) is the inhomogeneous differential equation with the same operator as (
3.16
) in the left hand side. Therefore, the first-order solutions can be expressed in terms of zeroth-order solutions (
3.18
) and take the form
[
8
]

\psi^{(1)}_{a}(y,z)=\psi_{b}^{(0)}(y)\int T_{ab}(y,z)dy\;,

(3.23)

where

T_{ab}(y,z)=2\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{2y\left(yW^{(1)}-p_{1}T^{(1)}\right)}{p_{12}p_{13}}\;\;&\displaystyle\frac{y^{1-p_{12}}\left(p_{3}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{12}p_{13}}\;\;&\displaystyle\frac{y^{1-p_{13}}\left(p_{2}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{12}p_{23}}\\
\\
\displaystyle-\frac{y^{1+p_{12}}\left(p_{3}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{12}p_{23}}\;\;&\displaystyle-\frac{2y\left(yW^{(1)}-p_{2}T^{(1)}\right)}{p_{12}p_{13}}\;\;&\displaystyle-\frac{y^{1-p_{23}}\left(p_{1}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{12}p_{23}}\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{y^{1+p_{13}}\left(p_{2}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{13}p_{23}}\;\;&\displaystyle\frac{y^{1+p_{23}}\left(p_{1}T^{(1)}+2W^{(1)}y\right)}{p_{13}p_{23}}\;\;&\displaystyle\frac{2y\left(yW^{(1)}-p_{3}T^{(1)}\right)}{p_{13}p_{23}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.24)

and we introduce
p_{ij}\equiv p_{i}-p_{j}
. Hence, the monodromy matrix
M^{(1)}_{ab}
has the form

M^{(1)}_{ab}=\int_{\Gamma}dyT_{ab}\equiv I_{ab}\;.

(3.25)

Calculating the contour integrals using residues, one can obtain the elements of the monodromy matrix
M^{(1)}_{ab}
. In particular, the diagonal elements turn out to be 0,
M^{(1)}_{aa}=0
. Other elements are linear functions of
a_{1,2},b_{2}
and
c_{2}
and have the form

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{I_{12}}{2\pi i}=\displaystyle\frac{2z^{-p_{12}}\left(z\left(2a_{2}\left(2-p_{12}\right)+c_{2}p_{3}\right)+2b_{2}z^{2}-\left(p_{12}-1\right)\left(p_{3}\epsilon+\left(p_{12}-2\right)q\right)\right)}{p_{12}p_{13}}\\

\\
\displaystyle\frac{I_{21}}{2\pi i}=-\frac{2p_{12}\left(2a_{1}+\epsilon p_{3}+\left(p_{12}+3\right)q\right)-4a_{2}z-2b_{2}z^{2}-c_{2}p_{3}x_{2}-\epsilon p_{3}+2q}{p_{12}p_{23}}-\\

Since the monodromy matrix of zero-order solutions is given by the identity matrix, in the first order the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrix

are given

\tilde{M}^{(1)}_{ab}=\gamma^{(1)}_{a}\delta_{ab}\;,\qquad\gamma^{(1)}_{1,2}=\pm 4\pi i\tilde{\epsilon}\;,\qquad\gamma^{(1)}_{3}=0\;.

one finds the block function

,

\begin{array}[]{c}f^{(v)}_{W}(z)=\displaystyle\frac{\left(3q-\epsilon\right)}{2}\log\left(p_{3}\left(2z^{2p_{2}+p_{3}}-z^{p_{2}+2p_{3}}-1\right)+p_{2}\left(z^{2p_{2}+p_{3}}-2z^{p_{2}+2p_{3}}+1\right)\right)-\epsilon(1+p_{1})\log z\\

-\displaystyle\frac{\left(\epsilon+3q\right)}{2}\left(\log\left(-p_{3}\left(z^{p_{2}}-2z^{p_{3}}+z^{2\left(p_{2}+p_{3}\right)}\right)+p_{2}\left(-2z^{p_{2}}+z^{p_{3}}+z^{2\left(p_{2}+p_{3}\right)}\right)\right)\right)\;.\end{array}

The 4-pt vacuum Virasoro block is obtained by imposing the conditions

,
p_{2}=-p_{3}=\alpha

3.3
4
-pt non-vacuum
\mathcal{W}_{3}

There are several ways to exclude functional arbitrariness for the
4
-pt non-vacuum block. For example, one can fix the parameters
a_{2}=0,\;b_{2}=0
and solve (
3.28

c_{2}
and
a_{1}
.

9

More precisely, any pair of parameters can be fixed equal to 0, trivial configurations correspond to
c^{(n)}_{2}=0

Since the integrals
I_{ab}
are linear in
a_{1}
and
c_{2}
, the monodromy system contains one third-order equation and one second-order equation. Isolating the accessory parameter
c_{2}
, we end up with a sixth order equation, solutions of which cannot be found in radicals. On the other hand, one can try to construct the solution as follows: for example, suppose that we fix

I_{12}=I_{23}=I_{21}=0\;,

(3.30)

in the first equation of (
3.28
) so the second one has the form

I_{13}I_{31}=-16\pi^{2}\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}.

(3.31)

Solving (
3.30

a_{1,2},b_{2}
, which are linear functions of
c_{2}
and substitute to the equation above. It results in the quadratic equation in
c_{2}
which can be solved but having a
too complicated form.

In this section, we consider another way of capturing functional arbitrariness. Since for the vacuum block all parameters
c_{2},a_{1,2},b_{2}
are known, we consider the following ansatz for the
4
-pt non-vacuum block

c_{2}=c^{(v)}_{2}+c^{(n)}_{2}\;,\qquad a_{1,2}=a^{(v)}_{1,2}\;,\qquad b_{2}=b^{(v)}_{2}\;,

where for the accessory parameter
c_{2}
the superscript

refers to a vacuum block and

to a non-vacuum part of the block. Fixing the functional arbitrariness in this way, we assume that the intermediate operator with the classical dimension
\tilde{\epsilon}
contributes only to the classical conformal block and does not change parameters
a_{1,2},b_{2}
.

Despite such a fixation of three parameters instead of two, one can see that under (
3.32
) the first equation in (
3.28
) becomes trivial. The second equation is a quadratic equation in
c^{(n)}_{2}
and its solution with the correct asymptotic takes the simple form

c^{(n)}_{2}=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}\displaystyle}{2z}\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq 3}p^{-1/2}_{ij}\left(\frac{p^{2}_{3}\left(z^{-p_{12}/2}-z^{p_{12}/2}\right)^{2}}{p_{12}}-\frac{p^{2}_{2}\left(z^{-p_{13}/2}-z^{p_{13}/2}\right)^{2}}{p_{13}}-\frac{p^{2}_{1}\left(z^{-p_{23}/2}-z^{p_{23}/2}\right)^{2}}{p_{23}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(3.33)

where
p_{i}
are defined by (
3.19
).

Although we have not calculated this integral analytically, one can expand in
q_{H}/\alpha^{2}\ll 1
. This approximation can be viewed as a deformation of the heavy-light Virasoro block
[

,
31
]
by the classical spin-3 charge
q_{H}

and

denotes the Lerch transcendent function (see

for review)

\Phi(x,s,a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{(a+k)^{s}}\;,

(3.37)

which generalise the polylogarithm given by

can be found in exactly the same way.
Thus, the result for the non-vacuum 4-pt

\begin{array}[]{c}f_{W}=-\epsilon(1-\alpha)\log z-2\epsilon\log\left(z^{-\alpha/2}-z^{\alpha/2}\right)+\tilde{\epsilon}f_{0}+\\

Few comments are in order. First, we have highlighted the

-pt non-vacuum Virasoro block in the first line, and the corrections to it in the second line, where
f_{0}
and
f_{2}
are determined by (
3.36
). For

we get the vacuum block (

are determined by the vacuum block (

), which does not contribute to the second order. Third, the block (
3.38
) can be rewritten as a function

,
which indicates the presence of holographic variables

]
.

The study of the non-vacuum block was partly motivated by the connection between the
\mathcal{W}_{N}
vacuum block and the Virasoro blocks (both vacuum and non-vacuum)
[
20
]
. More precisely, the basis states formed the
\mathcal{W}_{N}
module can be rewritten in terms of Virasoro states. However, based on (
3.38
), it is still not entirely clear whether the
\mathcal{W}_{3}
non-vacuum block can be organized in the same way.

4
Conclusion

In this work, the monodromy method for the
4
-pt non-vacuum classical block associated with the
\mathcal{W}_{3}
algebra was considered. Using the fusion rules for the auxiliary
5
-pt block, we have obtained monodromy equations that partially define the
4
-pt classical block. Having fixed the functional arbitrariness in the way mentioned above, we have found the
4
-pt non-vacuum block. We also studied the reduction of the monodromy method for the
\mathcal{W}_{3}
algebra to one for the Virasoro algebra and showed that the results obtained within its framework go over to the well-known expressions for the
4
-pt non-vacuum Virasoro block.

It would be of interest to apply the monodromy method to conformal blocks, where the functional arbitrariness is fixed by the choice of special primary operators
[
11
,
9
]
or within the framework of the isomonodromic approach
[
34
]
. Another interesting topic is to consider blocks where one or two primary operators are replaced by degenerate operators with a highest-weight in the fundamental representation of
sl(3)
.

In this work, we did not touch upon the holographic interpretation of the results obtained. It would be interesting to study dual objects in terms of Wilson lines and establish a correspondence similar to the examples given in
[
35
]
.
