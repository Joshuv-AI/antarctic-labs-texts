Transport via Partial Galois Connections and Equivalences (Extended Version)
Kevin Kappelmann

Abstract
Multiple types can represent the same concept. For example, lists and trees can both represent sets. Unfortunately, this easily leads to incomplete libraries: some set-operations may only be available on lists, others only on trees. Similarly, subtypes and quotients are commonly used to construct new type abstractions in formal verification. In such cases, one often wishes to reuse operations on the representation type for the new type abstraction, but to no avail: the types are not the same. To address these problems, we present a new framework that transports programs via equivalences. Existing transport frameworks are either designed for dependently typed, constructive proof assistants, use univalence, or are restricted to partial quotient types. Our framework 1. is designed for simple type theory, 2. generalises previous approaches working on partial quotient types, and 3. is based on standard mathematical concepts, particularly Galois connections and equivalences. We introduce the notions of partial Galois connection and equivalence and prove their closure properties under (dependent) function relators, (co)datatypes, and compositions. We formalised the framework in Isabelle/HOL and provide a prototype. 1 1 1 Non-peer reviewed, extended version of “Transport via Partial Galois Connections and Equivalences”, 21st Asian Symposium on Programming Languages and Systems (APLAS), 2023 [ 15 ]

Introduction

Computer scientists
often write programs and proofs in terms of representation types
but provide their libraries
in terms of different, though related, type abstractions.
For example,
the abstract type of finite sets
may be represented by the type of lists:
every finite set is related to every list
containing the same elements and, conversely,
every list is related to its set of elements.
As such, every function on lists respecting this relation
may be reused for a library on finite sets.
To be more explicit, consider the following example
in simple type theory:

A Simple Example

Take the types of lists,
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}
,
and finite sets,
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{fset}
.
There is a function
\mathsf{to\_fset}:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{fset}

that turns a list into its set of elements.
This allows us to define the relation

\mathsf{LFS}\,xs\,s\coloneqq\mathsf{to\_fset}\,xs=s

that identifies lists and finite sets,
e.g.
\mathsf{LFS}\,[1,2,3]\,\{1,2,3\}

and

\mathsf{LFS}\,[3,1,2]\,\{1,2,3\}
.
Our goal is to use this identification to
transport programs between these two types.

For instance, take the function
\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs\coloneqq\mathsf{foldr}\,{\mathsf{max}}\,xs\,0
of type

\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{list}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}

that returns the maximum natural number contained in a list.
After some thinking, one recognises that
\mathsf{max\_list}

respects the relation
\mathsf{LFS}
in the following sense:
if two lists correspond to the same set,
then applying
\mathsf{max\_list}
to these lists returns equal results.
Formally,

\forall xs\,ys.\,\mathsf{to\_fset}\,xs=\mathsf{to\_fset}\,ys\longrightarrow\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs=\mathsf{max\_list}\,ys.

(1)

Despite this insight,
we still cannot directly compute the maximum
of a finite set
s:\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsf{fset}
using
\mathsf{max\_list}
;
the term
\mathsf{max\_list}\,s
does not even typecheck (for good reasons).
But there is an indirect way
if we are also given an “inverse” of
\mathsf{to\_fset}
, call it

\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{fset}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}
,
that returns an arbitrary list containing the same elements as the given set.
The functions
\mathsf{to\_fset}
and
\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}
form an equivalence between
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}
and
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{fset}

that respects the relation
\mathsf{LFS}
:

\forall xs.\,\mathsf{LFS}\,xs\,(\mathsf{to\_fset}\,xs)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall s.\,\mathsf{LFS}\,(\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}\,s)\,s.

(2)

Thanks to this equivalence,
we can compute the maximum of
s
by simply transporting
s
along the equivalence:

\mathsf{max\_fset}\,s\coloneqq\mathsf{max\_list}\,(\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}\,s).

(3)

The correctness of this transport is guaranteed by
1
,
2
, and
3
:

\forall xs\,s.\,\mathsf{LFS}\,xs\,s\longrightarrow\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs=\mathsf{max\_fset}\,s.

(4)

We can now readily replace
any occurrence of
\mathsf{max\_fset}\,s

by

\mathsf{max\_list}\,(\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}\,s)

and, vice versa,
any occurrence of
\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs

by
\mathsf{max\_fset}\,(\mathsf{to\_fset}\,xs)
.
This process can be extended
to many other functions,
such as
\mathsf{map}
,
\mathsf{filter}
,
\mathsf{intersect}
,
by introducing new terms
\mathsf{map\_fset}
,
\mathsf{filter\_fset}
,
\mathsf{intersect\_fset}

and proving their respectfulness theorems.
Indeed, it is a very repetitive task begging for

automation
.

State of the Art

There are various frameworks
to automate the transport
of terms along equivalences.
Most of them are designed for
dependently typed, constructive proof assistants
and are based on
type equivalences

[
9
,

In a nutshell, type equivalences are pairs of functions

that are mutually inverse (i.e.
g\,(f\,x)=x
and
f\,(g\,y)=y
)
together with a compatibility condition.
They cannot solve our problem since

\mathsf{to\_fset}
and
\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}}
are not mutually inverse.

Angiuli et al.
[
1
]

note and address
this issue
in Cubical Agda
[
32
]
.
Essentially, they first quotient both types
and then obtain
a type equivalence between the quotiented types.
Their approach supports a restricted variant of

quasi-partial equivalence relations

[
16
]

but also uses univalence
[
33
]
,
which is unavailable in major proof assistants
like Isabelle/HOL
[
24
]

and Lean 3
[
22
]
/Lean 4
[
23
]
.

Another existing framework is Isabelle’s

Lifting package

[
13
]
,
which transports terms via
partial quotient types
:

Definition 1
.

A partial quotient type
(T,Abs,Rep)
is given by
a right-unique and right-total relation

T

and two functions
Abs
,

Rep

respecting
T
, that is

T\,x\,y\longrightarrow Abs\,x=y

and

T\,(Rep\,y)\,y
,
for all
x,y
.

In fact,
(\mathsf{LFS},\mathsf{to\_fset},\mathsf{to\_list^{fin}})
forms a partial quotient type.
The Lifting package can thus transport our list library to
finite sets
2
2

2

The Lifting package
is indeed used pervasively for such purposes.
At the time of writing, Isabelle/HOL and the
Archive of Formal Proofs

(
www.isa-afp.org
) contain more than 2800 invocations of the package.
.
However, the package also has its limitations:

Limitations of the Lifting Package

Consider the previous example with one modification:
rather than transporting
\mathsf{max\_list}
to finite sets,
we want to transport it to the type of (potentially infinite) sets,
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{set}
.
We cannot build a partial quotient type from
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}

to
\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{set}
because the required relation

T:\,\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{set}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}

is not right-total (we can only relate finite sets to lists).
The Lifting package is stuck.
But in theory, we can (almost) repeat the previous process:
There is again a function
\mathsf{to\_set}:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{set}
.
We can define a relation

\mathsf{LS}\,xs\,s\coloneqq\mathsf{to\_set}\,xs=s
.
We can again prove that
\mathsf{max\_list}
respects
\mathsf{LS}
:

\forall xs\,ys.\,\mathsf{to\_set}\,xs=\mathsf{to\_set}\,ys\longrightarrow\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs=\mathsf{max\_list}\,ys.

(5)

There is a function

\mathsf{to\_list}:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{set}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}
,
and we obtain a
partial
equivalence:

\forall xs.\,\mathsf{LS}\,xs\,(\mathsf{to\_set}\,xs)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall s.\,\mathsf{finite}\,s\longrightarrow\,\mathsf{LS}\,(\mathsf{to\_list}\,s)\,s.

(6)

We can define the function

\mathsf{max\_set}\,s\coloneqq\mathsf{max\_list}\,(\mathsf{to\_list}\,s)
.
And we again obtain a correctness theorem:

\forall xs\,s.\,\mathsf{LS}\,xs\,s\longrightarrow\mathsf{max\_list}\,xs=\mathsf{max\_set}\,s
.
While this process looks rather similar, there is one subtle change:
the second part of
Eq.

6
only holds conditionally.
As a contribution of this paper,
we show that these conditions are not showstoppers,
and that we can transport via such partial equivalences in general.

Now one may argue that
we could still use partial quotient types to transport
from lists to sets:
First obtain a right-unique, right-total relation
T
by
building a subtype of the target type.
Then transport to the new subtype
and then inject to the original type.
In spirit, this is close to the approach suggested by Angiuli et al.
[
1
]
.
But the author finds this unsatisfactory from a practical and a conceptual perspective:
From a practical perspective, it introduces unnecessary
subtypes to our theory.
And conceptually,
the process for sets and lists
was almost identical to the one for
finite sets and lists –
there was no detour via subtypes.

A second limitation of the Lifting package
is that it does not support
inter-argument dependencies
.
For example,
take the types of natural numbers,
\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}
,
and integers,
\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We can construct a partial quotient type

(\mathsf{ZN},\mathsf{to\_nat},\mathsf{to\_int})
,
where

\mathsf{to\_int}:\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}

is the standard embedding,

\mathsf{to\_nat}:\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}
is its inverse (a partial function),
and

\mathsf{ZN}\,i\,n\coloneqq i=\mathsf{to\_int}\,n
.
It then seems straightforward
to transport subtraction

(-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}}):\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}

from integers
to natural numbers in the following way:

n_{1}-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}}\,n_{2}\coloneqq\mathsf{to\_nat}\,\big(\mathsf{to\_int}\,n_{1}-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathsf{to\_int}\,n_{2}\big).

(7)

And of course, we expect a correctness theorem:

\forall i_{1}\,n_{1}\,i_{2}\,n_{2}.\,\mathsf{ZN}\,i_{1}\,n_{1}\land\mathsf{ZN}\,i_{2}\,n_{2}\longrightarrow\mathsf{ZN}\,(i_{1}-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}}i_{2})\,(n_{1}-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}}n_{2}).

(8)

But alas, the theorem does not hold:
we need an extra dependency
between the arguments of the respective subtractions,
e.g.
i_{1}\geq i_{2}
or
n_{1}\geq n_{2}
.
Unfortunately,
the Lifting package’s theory
[
13
]

cannot account for such dependencies,
and as such,
the transport attempt for
(-_{\mathsf{\mathbb{Z}}})
fails.

In a similar way,
the list index operator

\mathsf{(!!)}:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}

can only be transported
to the type of arrays for indices that are in bounds
(cf.
Section

5
,
Example

2
).
While solutions for dependently typed
environments
[
9
,
8
,
26
,
29
,
28
,
1
]

typically handle such examples by
encoding the dependencies in a type,
e.g.
(xs:\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{list})\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\{0,\dotsc,\mathsf{length}\,xs-1\}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}
,
it is unclear how to support this
in a simply typed environment.
As a contribution of this paper,
we show how to account for such dependencies
with the help of
dependent function relators
.

We introduce a new transport framework – simply called

.
Our framework

The Essence of Transport

Existing frameworks, although beneficial in practical contexts,
are unapplicable to our introductory examples.
We hence first want to find
the essence of transport

To avoid confusion,

We focus on the general task of transporting a term

To find this essence, we have to answer the following question:

What are the minimum expectations when we transport terms via equivalences?

In this section, we argue that Galois connections are the right notion to cover this essence.

The Essence

Abstracting from these approaches, we note some commonalities:

•

As input, they take base equivalences,
which are then used to build more complex equivalences.

•

The equivalences include
a
left transport function

l:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}

and
a
right transport function

r:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}
.
They can be used to move terms
from one side of the equivalence to a “similar”
term on the other side of the equivalence.

•

Terms
t:\mathsf{\alpha}
and
t^{\prime}:\mathsf{\beta}
that are “similar” stand in particular relations:
in the case of type equivalences,
t=r\,t^{\prime}
and
l\,t=t^{\prime}
;
in the case of Lifting,
t\approx r\,t^{\prime}
and
l\,t=t^{\prime}
.
More abstractly,

L\,t\,(r\,t^{\prime})
and
R\,(l\,t)\,t^{\prime}

for some
left relation

L:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}

and
right relation

R:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}
.
5
5

5

The choice of

L\,t\,(r\,t^{\prime}),R\,(l\,t)\,t^{\prime}

may seem arbitrary – why not pick

L\,t\,(r\,t^{\prime}),R\,t^{\prime}\,(l\,t)
instead?
In the end, the choice does not matter:
While the former leads us to (monotone) Galois connections,
the latter leads us to antitone Galois connections.
Using that
L,R
form a Galois connection if and only if

L,R^{-1}
form an antitone Galois connection,
every result in this paper
can be transformed to its corresponding result on
antitone Galois connections
by an appropriate instantiation of the framework.

•

More generally,

L
and
R

specify how terms ought to be related in
\mathsf{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{\beta}

and determine which terms can be meaningfully transported using
l
and
r
.

•

L,R,l,r
are compatible: if
terms are related on one side (e.g.
L\,t_{1}\,t_{2}
), their transports are related on the other side (e.g.
R\,(l\,t_{1})\,(l\,t_{2})
).

Based on these commonalities,
we can formulate six minimum expectations:

00

We want to specify how terms in
\mathsf{\alpha}
and
\beta

are related using relations
L,R
.

(’)

Transports should be possible by means of functions
l:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta},r:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}
.

(’)

The notion of equivalence should be closed under common relators,
particularly those for functions and (co)datatypes.

(’)

Terms related on one side have transports that are related on the other side.

(’)

Transporting a term should result in a term that is “similar” to its input.

(’)

“Similar” terms

t:\mathsf{\alpha}
and
t^{\prime}:\mathsf{\beta}

are related with each other’s transports,
i.e.
L\,t\,(r\,t^{\prime})
and
R\,(l\,t)\,t^{\prime}
.

(’)

Applying
Section

2

to
Section

2

then yields the requirements

L\,t\,(r\,(l\,t))
,

(’)

R\,(l\,(r\,t^{\prime}))\,t^{\prime}
.

(’)

At this point,
one may notice
the similarity to
Galois connections
.
A Galois connection between two preorders

({\leq_{L}})

and
({\leq_{R}})

consists of two functions

l
and

r

such that

•

l
is monotone, that is
x_{1}\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}x_{2}\longrightarrow l\,x_{1}\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}l\,x_{2}
for all
x_{1},x_{2}
,

•

r
is monotone, that is
y_{1}\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y_{2}\longrightarrow r\,y_{1}\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y_{2}
for all
y_{1},y_{2}
, and

•

x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,(l\,x)
and
l\,(r\,y)\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y
for all
x,y
.
6
6

6

These two conditions are equivalent to requiring

x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y\longleftrightarrow l\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y
for all
x,y
.

The final conditions correspond
to

and
2
above,
while the monotonicity conditions on

and

Other Motivations

A second motivation to base our framework
on Galois connections
comes from category theory.
There, an equivalence between two categories
L,R
is given
by two functors

l:L\to R
and

r:R\to L

and two natural isomorphisms

\mathsf{\eta}:Id_{L}\to r\circ l
and

\mathsf{\epsilon}:l\circ r\to Id_{R}
.
Applied to preorders
({\leq_{L}}),({\leq_{R}})
and monotone functions
l,r
,
this translates to the four conditions

x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,(l\,x)
,

(’)

l\,(r\,y)\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y
,

(’)

r\,(l\,x)\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}x
,

(’)

y\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}l\,(r\,y)
.

(’)

A related categorical concept is that of an
adjunction
.
When applied to preorders and monotone functions,
an adjunction is similar to an equivalence but is only required to satisfy

Sections

2
and
2
.
In fact, while Galois connections are not categorical equivalences,
they are adjunctions.
From this perspective,
a Galois connection can be seen as a weak form of an (order) equivalence.

A final motivation is the applicability and wide-spread use
of Galois connections.
They are fundamental in the closely related field
of abstract interpretation
[
7
,
5
]
,
where they are used to relate concrete to abstract domains.
Moreover, they are pervasive throughout mathematics.
In the words of Saunders Mac Lane:

The slogan is “Adjoint functors arise everywhere”.

(Categories for the Working Mathematician)

We hope
our exposition convinced the reader that
Galois connections are
a suitable notion to cover the essence of transport.
The remaining challenges are

•

to bring the notion of Galois connections to a partial world – the relations

L,R

may only be defined on a subset of
\mathsf{\alpha},\mathsf{\beta}
– and

•

to check the closure properties of our definitions under common relators.

3.1
(Order) Basics

We work in a polymorphic, simple type theory

A

A

Composition of two relations

A

,
if
\forall x\,y.\,R\,x\,y\longrightarrow S\,x\,y
.
It will be convenient to interpret relations as infix operators.
For every relation
R
, we hence introduce an infix operator
({\leq_{R}})\coloneqq R
,
that is
x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y\longleftrightarrow R\,x\,y
.
We also write
({\geq_{R}})\coloneqq({\leq_{R}})^{-1}
.
The
field predicate on a relation
is
defined as

\mathsf{in\_field}\,R\,x\coloneqq\mathsf{in\_dom}\,R\,x\lor\mathsf{in\_codom}\,R\,x
.

We use relativised versions of well-known order-theoretic concepts.
For example,
given a predicate
P
,
we define
reflexivity on
P
and
R
as

\mathsf{reflexive\_on}\,P\,R\coloneqq\forall x.\,P\,x\longrightarrow R\,x\,x
.
We proceed analogously for other standard order-theoretic concepts, such as transitivity, preorders, etc. (see
Section

0.A.1
).

3.2
Function Relators and Monotonicity

We introduce a generalisation of the well-known function relator (see e.g.
[
25
]
).
The slogan of the function relator is
“related functions map related inputs to related outputs”.
Our generalisation –
the
dependent function relator
–
additionally allows its target relation to depend on both inputs:

\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)\,f\,g\coloneqq\forall x\,y.\,R\,x\,y\longrightarrow S\,(f\,x)\,(g\,y),

(10)

where
x,y
may occur freely in
S
.
The well-known
(non-dependent) function relator
is given as a special case:

(R\Rrightarrow S)\coloneqq\big([\_\,\_::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)
.
A function is
monotone from
R
to
S
if it maps
R
-related inputs to
S
-related outputs:

\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}S\big)\,f\coloneqq\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)\,f\,f,

(11)

where
x,y
may occur freely in
S
.
A
monotone function relator
is like a function relator but additionally requires its members to be monotone:

\displaystyle\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow^{\oplus}S\big)\,f\,g\coloneqq

\displaystyle\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)\,f\,g

(12)

\displaystyle\land\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}S\big)\,f\land\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}S\big)\,g,

where
x,y
may occur freely in
S
.
In some examples, we have to include conditionals in our relators.
For this, we define the
relational if conditional

\mathsf{rel\_if}\,B\,S\,x\,y\coloneqq B\longrightarrow S\,x\,y

and set the following notation:

\displaystyle\big([x\,y::R\mid B]\Rrightarrow S\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow\mathsf{rel\_if}\,B\,S\big),

(13)

where
x,y
may occur freely in
B,S
.

3.3
Galois Relator

In
Section

2
of
Section

2
,
we noted
that
“similar” terms

t,t^{\prime}

are related with each other’s transports,
i.e.
L\,t\,(r\,t^{\prime})
and
R\,(l\,t)\,t^{\prime}
.
We now define this relation formally,
calling it the
Galois relator
:

\mathsf{Galois}\,({\leq_{L}})\,({\leq_{R}})\,r\,x\,y\coloneqq\mathsf{in\_codom}({\leq_{R}})\,y\land x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y

(14)

When the parameters are clear from the context,
we will use the infix notation

({{}_{L}{\lessapprox}})\coloneqq\mathsf{Galois}\,({\leq_{L}})\,({\leq_{R}})\,r
.
It is easy to show that Galois relators
generalise the transport relations
of partial quotient types:

Lemma 1

For every partial quotient type
(T,l,r)
with induced
left relation

({\leq_{L}})
,
we have

T=\mathsf{Galois}\,({\leq_{L}})\,(=)\,r
.

3.4
Partial Galois Connections and Equivalences

In their standard form,
Galois connections are defined on preorders

({\leq_{L}}),({\leq_{R}})
,
where
every
x:\mathsf{\alpha}

is in the domain of
({\leq_{L}})

and
every
y:\mathsf{\beta}

is in the domain of
({\leq_{R}})
.
But as we have seen,
this is not generally the case
when
transporting terms
.

We hence lift the notion of Galois connections to a partial setting.
We also do not assume any order axioms on
({\leq_{L}}),({\leq_{R}})

a priori but add them as needed.
In our formalisation,
we moreover break the concept
of Galois connections
down into smaller pieces
that, to our knowledge,
do not appear as such in the literature.
This allows us to obtain very precise results
when deriving the closure properties for our definitions
(
Section

4
).
But for reasons of brevity,
we only state the main definitions and results here.
Details can be found in
Section

0.A.4
.

The
(partial) Galois property
is defined as:

\displaystyle\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq

\displaystyle\forall x\,y.\,\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{L}})\,x\land\mathsf{in\_codom}({\leq_{R}})\,y\longrightarrow{}

(15)

\displaystyle(x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y\longleftrightarrow l\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y).

If

and

are also monotone,
we obtain a
(partial) Galois connection
:

\displaystyle\big(({\leq_{L}})\dashv({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq

\displaystyle\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r

(16)

\displaystyle\land\big(({\leq_{L}})\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\land\big(({\leq_{R}})\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}({\leq_{L}})\big)\,r.

We omit the qualifier “partial” when referring to these
definitions, unless we want to avoid ambiguity.

\big(({\leq_{L}})\equiv_{\mathsf{PER}}(=)\big)\,l\,r
.

4
Closure Properties

We now explore the closure properties of
partial Galois connections and equivalences,
as well as standard order properties,
such as reflexivity and transitivity.
We will derive closure conditions
for the dependent function relator,
relators for (co)datatypes, and composition.
In each case, we will also derive conditions
under which the Galois relator
aligns with the context-dependent notion

of “similarity”
.

For reasons of brevity,
we only show that our framework is robust
under Galois equivalences on preorders and PERs here.
The results for Galois connections (and proof sketches)
can be found in
Section

0.B.1
.

4.1
(Dependent) Function Relator

In the field of abstract interpretation,
it is well-known that Galois connections,
as usually defined in the literature,
are closed under the non-dependent, monotone function relator
(see for example
[
7
]
).
We generalise this result to partial Galois connections
and to dependent function relators.

Remark 1
.

The relations and functions
we use are often non-dependent in practice.
The following definitions
and theorems are considerably simpler in this case.
The reader hence might find instructive
to first consult the results for this special case
in
Section

.

The Setup

In

4.2
(Co)datatypes

Different proof assistants ground (co)datatypes in
different ways.
For instance, Coq and Lean introduce them axiomatically,
whereas Isabelle/HOL proves their existence
using the theory of
bounded natural functors

[
30

.
As our formalisation takes place in Isabelle/HOL,
we use the latter theory.
Nonetheless,
the results presented in this section
are relatively straightforward
and can likely be adapted to
other “reasonable” definitions of (co)datatypes.

In this section, we derive closure properties
for arbitrary
natural functors
.
A natural functor is a bounded natural functor without cardinality constraints.
The exact axioms
can be found elsewhere
[
30
]
.
For our purposes,
it suffices to say that natural functors are equipped with a
mapper
and a
relator
.
More precisely, for every
n
-ary natural functor
(\mathsf{\alpha}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{\alpha}_{n})\,F
,
there are two functions:

\displaystyle\mathsf{map}_{F}:\;

\displaystyle(\mathsf{\alpha}_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{1})\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\dotsb\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}(\mathsf{\alpha}_{n}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{n})\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}(\mathsf{\alpha}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{\alpha}_{n})\,F\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}(\mathsf{\beta}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{\beta}_{n})\,F

\displaystyle\mathsf{rel}_{F}:\;

\displaystyle(\mathsf{\alpha}_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{1}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool})\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\dotsb\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}(\mathsf{\alpha}_{n}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{n}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool})\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}{}

\displaystyle(\mathsf{\alpha}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{\alpha}_{n})\,F\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}(\mathsf{\beta}_{1},\dotsc,\mathsf{\beta}_{n})\,F\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}

The former lifts functions on the functor’s type arguments
to the functorial structure,
the latter lifts relations on the functor’s type arguments
to the functorial structure.
Using the mapper and relator,
it is straightforward
to define appropriate target relations and
transport functions.
First we fix the following variables for
1\leq i\leq n
:

L_{i}:\mathsf{\alpha}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad l_{i}:\mathsf{\alpha}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{i},\quad R_{i}:\mathsf{\beta}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad r_{i}:\mathsf{\beta}_{i}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}_{i}.

\displaystyle\begin{split}L&\coloneqq\mathsf{rel}_{F}\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,\dotso\,({\leq_{L_{n}}}),\\
R&\coloneqq\mathsf{rel}_{F}\,({\leq_{R_{1}}})\,\dotso\,({\leq_{R_{n}}}),\end{split}\begin{split}l&\coloneqq\mathsf{map}_{F}\,l_{1}\,\dotso\,l_{n},\\

4.3
Compositions

It is well-known that Galois connections,
as defined in the literature,
are closed under composition
in the following sense:
given Galois connections between
({\leq_{L_{1}}}),({\leq_{R_{1}}})

and
({\leq_{L_{2}}}),({\leq_{R_{2}}})
with
({\leq_{R_{1}}})=({\leq_{L_{2}}})
,
we can build a Galois connection between

({\leq_{L_{1}}}),({\leq_{R_{2}}})
.
This result readily generalises
to our partial setting (see
Section

0.B.2
).
However,
({\leq_{R_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{L_{2}}})

usually do not coincide in our context.
We need a more general result.

The Setup

Our goal is to define a notion of composition that works
even if

({\leq_{R_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{L_{2}}})
do not coincide.
For this, we fix the variables

\displaystyle L_{1}:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad

\displaystyle l_{1}:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta},\quad

\displaystyle R_{1}:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad

\displaystyle r_{1}:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha},

\displaystyle L_{2}:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad

\displaystyle l_{2}:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\gamma},\quad

\displaystyle R_{2}:\mathsf{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool},\quad

\displaystyle r_{2}:\mathsf{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}.

Intuitively, we are in a situation where

1.

we are given an equivalence between
({\leq_{L_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{R_{1}}})

2.

we are given an equivalence between
({\leq_{L_{2}}})
and
({\leq_{R_{2}}})

l_{2}
and

3.

({\leq_{R_{2}}})

This particularly means that we can apply the transport functions

The question is: how do we find those parts and how can we make them compatible?
The solution we propose is inspired by and generalises
the approach of Huffman and Kunčar
[

]
.
We provide details and intuitions for the constructions in

.
The resulting target relations and transport functions are defined as follows (where

\displaystyle\begin{split}L&\coloneqq({{}_{L_{1}}{\lessapprox}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({{}_{R_{1}}{\lessapprox}}),\\
R&\coloneqq({{}_{R_{2}}{\lessapprox}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{R_{1}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({{}_{L_{2}}{\lessapprox}}),\end{split}\begin{split}l&\coloneqq l_{2}\circ l_{1},\\

Application Examples

As all our results are formalised in Isabelle/HOL,
we can directly use them to manually transport terms in said environment.
But that would be rather tiresome.
We thus implemented a prototype in Isabelle/ML to

automate transports
.

The Prototype

The method
trprover

uses registered base equivalences,
along with the closure theorems from
Section

4
,
to construct more complex equivalences.
The prototype is currently
restricted to equivalences on partial equivalence relations (PERs) for pragmatic reasons:
their closure theorems have fewer
assumptions and are hence simpler to apply.
Providing automation for weaker equivalences is future work.
The current prototype also does not build
composition closures (
Section

4.3
)
and automates only a fragment of
dependent function relators for simplicity reasons.
Again, these extensions are future work.

The prototype provides a command
trp
.
As input, it takes a term
t:\mathsf{\alpha}
(the term to be transported)
and two optional target relations

L:\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}
,

R:\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}
.
This is unlike other transport frameworks
[
29
,
26
,
9
,
13
]
,
which only take the term
t:\mathsf{\alpha}
and a target type
\mathsf{\beta}
.
This design decision is crucial since we
can neither assume a unique correspondence
between types and target relations in practice
(cf.
Example

3
),
nor can we express dependencies in types,
but we express them using dependent relators
(cf.
Example

2
).
The command then opens two goals.
The first one asks for an equivalence

\big(({\leq_{L}})\equiv_{\mathsf{PER}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r
,
the second one for a proof that
\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{L}})\,t
.
On success, it registers a new term
t^{\prime}
and a theorem that
t\mathrel{{}_{L}{\lessapprox}}t^{\prime}
.
It also registers a second theorem where the relator
({{}_{L}{\lessapprox}})
has been rewritten
to its desired form
as described in
Theorems

4.2
,
4.4
, and
4.6
.

The following examples are best explored interactively in our formalisation.
We define the
restricted equality relation on predicates
as

x=_{P}y\coloneqq P\,x\land x=y

and
the
restricted equality relation on sets
as

x=_{S}y\coloneqq x\in S\land x=y
.

Related Work

Transport in Proof Assistants

Our work was chiefly inspired by Isabelle’s
Lifting package
[
13
,
17
]
,
which transports terms via partial quotient types.
All closure theorems in this work
generalise the ones in
[
13
]
.
Besides this source of inspiration,
the theory of automated transports
has seen prolific work in recent years:

Tabareau et al.
[
28
]

proved a strengthened relational parametricity result,
called
univalent parametricity
,
for the Calculus of Inductive Constructions.
Their approach ensures
that all relations are compatible with type equivalences.
One can then use univalence
[
33
]
to seamlessly transport
terms between related types.
The framework is implemented using Coq’s
typeclass mechanism
[
27
]
.

Tabareau et al.
[
29
]
extended their
work to integrate what they call

“white-box transports”
.
White-box transports
structurally rewrite a term
t
to
t^{\prime}
using
user-specified correspondences.
In contrast,

“black-box transports”

transport
t
without looking at its syntactic structure.
For instance,
given an equivalence between unary and binary numbers

(\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\simeq\mathsf{Bin})\,l\,r
,
black-box transporting the term
0+_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}}0
results
in
l\,(0+_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}}0)
.
In contrast,
given correspondences
between the functions
{(+)}_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}},{(+)}_{\mathsf{Bin}}

and constants
0,0_{\mathsf{Bin}}
,
white-box transporting the term results
in
0_{\mathsf{Bin}}+_{\mathsf{Bin}}0_{\mathsf{Bin}}
.
These modes can also be mixed:
given just the equivalence

(\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}\simeq\mathsf{Bin})\,l\,r

and correspondence between
{(+)}_{\mathsf{\mathbb{N}}},{(+)}_{\mathsf{Bin}}
,
we obtain
(l\,0)+_{\mathsf{Bin}}(l\,0)
.
Isabelle’s Lifting package also supports white-box transports
via the
transfer
method
[
17
]
.
While our work is concerned with
black-box transports,
our prototype also contains
experimental support for white-box transports.
This integration will be further polished in future work.

Angiuli et al.
[
1
]

establish representation independence results
in Cubical Agda
[
32
]
.
Their approach
applies to a restricted variant of
quasi-partial equivalence relations
[
16
]
.
Essentially, they quotient two types by a given correspondence to obtain
a type equivalence between the quotiented types.

Dagand et al.
[
9
,
8
]
introduce what they call
“type-theoretic partial Galois connections”,
which are essentially partial type equivalences
on an enriched

\mathsf{\alpha}\,\mathsf{option}
type.
They allow for partiality on one
side of the equivalence but not the other.
Their framework is designed for effective
program extraction and implemented
using Coq’s typeclass mechanism.

Ringer et al.
[
26
]

developed a Coq plugin
to transport proof terms via type equivalences
for inductive types.
Their theory shares similarities
with
[
29
,
28
]
,
but it directly transforms proof terms.
This way, one can remove all references to the old datatype
once the proof terms have been transported to the new target type.
This is not readily achievable using other mentioned frameworks,
including ours.

Type equivalences enjoy the property
of having total and mutually inverse transport functions.
This is not the case for partial Galois connections,
which makes the transport of proofs harder.
For example, the parametricity law for equality

\big(T\Rrightarrow T\Rrightarrow(\longleftrightarrow)\big)\,(=)\,(=)

holds only if
T
is left-unique and injective.
This is the case if
T
is described by a type equivalence
but not in general by a Galois connection.
Kunčar
[
17
]
provides parametricity rules
for all prominent logical connectives.
These rules also apply to our setting and
will be crucial when we polish the integration
of white-box transports in our prototype.

The works mentioned above
all transport terms via certain notions of equivalences.
But there are also other approaches,
particularly in the field of data refinement.
An example is the CoqEAL framework
[
4
]
,
which automatically derives parametricity results using typeclass search.
Another one is Isabelle’s Autoref framework
[
18
]
,
which derives relational parametricity results using white-box transports.
The core inspiration in both cases goes back to

[
21
,
25
,
34
]
.
A comprehensive comparison of these frameworks
can be found in
[
19
]
.

Galois Connections in Computer Science

Galois connections are fundamental
in the field of abstract interpretation.
Cousot and Cousot’s recent book
[
5
]

provides an overview of their applications.
The closure of Galois connections under non-dependent function relators
goes back to at least
[
6
]
.
We generalised this result
to partial Galois connections and dependent function relators
in
Section

4.1
.
Most work in abstract interpretation
does not consider partially defined Galois connections
and assumes partial orderings on relations.
The work of Miné
[
20
]

is an exception,
allowing for partiality on one side of the connection but not the other.
Darais and Van Horn
[
10
]
formalise Galois connections
constructively and apply it to tasks in abstract interpretation.
An early
application of Galois connections
was by Hartmanis and Stearns
[
12
]
.
Though they did not use Galois connections,
they introduced an equivalent notion of
pair algebras

[
11
]
.
Our Galois relator
indeed
describes the pair algebra induced by a Galois connection.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We explored existing notions of equivalences used for automatic transport.
Based on this exploration,
we identified a set of minimal expectations when transporting terms via equivalences.
This essence led us to introduce a new class of equivalences,
namely partial Galois connections.
Partial Galois connections generalise (standard) Galois connections
and apply to relations
that are only defined on subsets of their types.
We derived closure conditions for partial Galois connections and equivalences,
and typical order properties
under (dependent) function relators, relators for (co)datatypes,
and composition.
Our framework applies to simple type theory and –
unlike prior solutions for simple type theory –
can handle inter-argument dependencies.
We implemented a prototype in Isabelle/HOL based on our results.
The prototype needs to be further polished,
but it can already handle relevant examples that
are out of scope for existing tools.

Future work

As our theory subsumes the one of Isabelle’s Lifting package,
one goal is to replace the package by a more general tool.
To this end,
we have to integrate our results into Isabelle’s (co)datatypes package
[
2
]
,
extend our prototype to automate the construction of compositions,
and polish the support of white-box transports (cf.
Section

6
).

Finally,
based on our formalisation insights,
we conjecture that one can adopt our theory
to constructive logics,
but only a formalisation in a constructive prover
will give a definite answer.

Appendix 0.A
Partial Galois Connections, Equivalences, and Relators

0.A.1
(Order) Basics

Given types
\mathsf{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{\beta}
,
the type of functions from
\mathsf{\alpha}
to
\mathsf{\beta}

is written
\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}
.

The composition of functions is defined as
(f\circ g)\,x\coloneqq f\,(g\,x)
.

A
predicate on a type
\mathsf{\alpha}
is a function of type

\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}
.

The predicate mapping all inputs to
\mathsf{True}
is denoted by
\top\coloneqq\lambda\,\_.\,\mathsf{True}
.

A
relation on
\mathsf{\alpha}
and
\mathsf{\beta}
is a function of type

\mathsf{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{\beta}\mathrel{\mathsf{\Rightarrow}}\mathsf{bool}
.

For every relation
R
, we introduce an infix operator
({\leq_{R}})\coloneqq R
,
that is
x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y\longleftrightarrow R\,x\,y
.

The
inverse
of a relation is defined as

R^{-1}\,x\,y\coloneqq R\,y\,x
.

The
composition of two relations
R,S

is defined as
(R\mathbin{{\circ}}S)\,x\,y\coloneqq\exists z.\ R\,x\,z\land S\,z\,y
.

A
relation
R
is finer than another relation
S
, written
R\leq S
,
if
\forall x\,y.\,R\,x\,y\longrightarrow S\,x\,y
.

The
domain
,
codomain
, and
field
predicates on a relation
R

are defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{in\_dom}\,R\,x

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exists y.\,R\,x\,y

\displaystyle\mathsf{in\_codom}\,R\,y

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exists x.\,R\,x\,y

\displaystyle\mathsf{in\_field}\,R\,x

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{in\_dom}\,R\,x\lor\mathsf{in\_codom}\,R\,x

A relation
R
is
right-total
if

\forall y.\;\exists x.\;R\,x\,y

and
right-unique
if

\forall x,y,y^{\prime}.\;R\,x\,y\land R\,x\,y^{\prime}\to y=y^{\prime}
.

Given a predicate
P
and relation
R
,
we define
reflexivity
,
transitivity
,
and
symmetry on
P
and
R
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{reflexive\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\forall x.\,P\,x\longrightarrow R\,x\,x

\displaystyle\mathsf{transitive\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\forall x\,y\,z.\,P\,x\,\land P\,y\land P\,z\land R\,x\,y\land R\,y\,z\longrightarrow R\,x\,z

\displaystyle\mathsf{symmetric\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\forall x\,y.\,P\,x\,\land P\,y\land R\,x\,y\longrightarrow R\,y\,x

Preorders
and
partial equivalence relations
(PERs)
are then defined in the expected way:

\displaystyle\mathsf{preorder\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{transitive\_on}\,P\,R\land\mathsf{reflexive\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\mathsf{partial\_equivalence\_rel\_on}\,P\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{transitive\_on}\,P\,R\land\mathsf{symmetric\_on}\,P\,R

For all relativised concepts, we introduce their unrelativised analogue:

\displaystyle\mathsf{reflexive}\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{reflexive\_on}\,\top\,R

\displaystyle\mathsf{transitive}\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{transitive\_on}\,\top\,R

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}\coloneqq{}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle\mathsf{partial\_equivalence\_rel}\,R

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{partial\_equivalence\_rel\_on}\,\top\,R

Given a predicate
P
and relation
R
,
we say that
f
is
inflationary (sometimes also called
extensive
) on
P
and
R
,
written
\mathsf{inflationary\_on}\,P\,R\,f
,
if
\forall x.\,P\,x\longrightarrow x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}f\,x
.
Similarly, we say that
f
is
deflationary on
P
and
R
,
written as
\mathsf{deflationary\_on}\,P\,R\,f
,
if
\forall x.\,P\,x\longrightarrow f\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}x
.
If
f
is inflationary and deflationary on
P
and
R
,
it is a
relational equivalence on
P
and
R
:

\mathsf{rel\_equivalence\_on}\,P\,R\,f\coloneqq\mathsf{inflationary\_on}\,P\,R\,f\land\mathsf{deflationary\_on}\,P\,R\,f.

0.A.2
Function Relators and Monotonicity

The
dependent function relator
is defined as

\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)\,f\,g\coloneqq\forall x\,y.\,R\,x\,y\longrightarrow S\,(f\,x)\,(g\,y),

where
x,y
may occur freely in
S
.
The
(non-dependent) function relator
is given as a special case:

(R\Rrightarrow S)\coloneqq\big([\_\,\_::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)
.
A function is
monotone from
R
to
S
if it maps
R
-related inputs to
S
-related outputs:

\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}S\big)\,f\coloneqq\big([x\,y::R]\Rrightarrow S\big)\,f\,f,

where
x,y
may occur freely in
S
.
The non-dependent variant is given as a special case:

0.A.4
Partial Galois Connections and Equivalences

Typically, Galois connections are defined on preorders,
distinguished by the characteristic property

x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y\longleftrightarrow l\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y
for all
x,y
.
We break the concept down into smaller pieces
and lift it to a partial setting.
The
(partial) half Galois property on the left
is defined as

\big(({\leq_{L}})\,{}_{\mathsf{h}}{\unlhd}\,({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq\forall x\,y.\,x\mathrel{{}_{L}{\lessapprox}}y\longrightarrow l\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R}}}y

and dually, the
(partial) half Galois property on the right
as

\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd_{\mathsf{h}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq\forall x\,y.\,x\mathrel{{\lessapprox}{}_{R}}y\longrightarrow x\mathrel{{\leq_{L}}}r\,y,

Both halves combined constitute the
(partial) Galois property
:

\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq\big(({\leq_{L}})\,{}_{\mathsf{h}}{\unlhd}\,({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\land\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd_{\mathsf{h}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r.

If
l
and
r
are also monotone,
we obtain a
(partial) Galois connection
:

\big(({\leq_{L}})\dashv({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq\big(({\leq_{L}})\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\land\big(({\leq_{R}})\Rrightarrow_{\mathsf{m}}({\leq_{L}})\big)\,r\land\big(({\leq_{L}})\unlhd({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r.

({\leq_{L}}),({\leq_{R}})

to be transitive nor reflexive.
An example Galois connection can be found in

By requiring a two-sided Galois connection,
we obtain a
(partial) Galois equivalence

\big(({\leq_{L}})\equiv_{\mathsf{G}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\coloneqq\big(({\leq_{L}})\dashv({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r\land\big(({\leq_{R}})\dashv({\leq_{L}})\big)\,r\,l

An example of a Galois equivalence can be found in
Fig.

Since the relations

are preorders or partial equivalence relations

\displaystyle\big(({\leq_{L}})\equiv_{\mathsf{G}}({\leq_{R}})\big)\,l\,r

\displaystyle\land\mathsf{preorder\_on}\,(\mathsf{in\_field}\,({\leq_{L}}))\,({\leq_{L}})

Construction Idea

As mentioned in
Section

4.3
,
our construction is inspired by Huffman and and Kunčar’s construction in
[
13
]
.
Unfortunately, they
do not provide any intuition about their constructions,
nor does Kunčar
[
17
]
in his thesis.
We try our best to fill this gap:
In the following,
we call
({\leq_{L_{1}}})
the
leftmost relation
,

({\leq_{R_{1}}}),({\leq_{L_{2}}})
the
middle relations
, and

({\leq_{R_{2}}})
the
rightmost relation
.
We will explain the definition of
({\leq_{L}})
.
The case for
({\leq_{R}})
is symmetric.

Fix some
x:\mathsf{\alpha}
of the leftmost type.
We want to

(a)

make sure that applying
l=l_{2}\circ l_{1}
on
x

does not leave the domain/codomain of our equivalences, and

(b)

find all elements
x^{\prime}:\mathsf{\alpha}
that are greater
or equal than
x
while doing so.

We make a first approximation to satisfy these conditions using three “chase” steps:

00

check whether
\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,x
and find some
y
such that
l_{1}\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R_{1}}}}y
,

(’)

find some
y^{\prime}
such that
y\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{2}}}}y^{\prime}
, and

(’)

check whether
\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{R_{1}}})\,y^{\prime}

and find some
x^{\prime}
such that
r_{1}\,y^{\prime}\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{1}}}}x^{\prime}
.

(’)

These steps are not enough:
we may have
l_{1}\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R_{1}}}}y\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{2}}}}\,y^{\prime}

but not necessarily
l_{1}\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{2}}}}y\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{2}}}}\,y^{\prime}
,
as required for Property
(a)
and
Section

0.B.2
.
But if we further require that
({\leq_{R_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{L_{2}}})

commute
,
that is
\big(({\leq_{R_{1}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\big)=\big(({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{R_{1}}})\big)
,
the steps become sufficient.
Finally note that

•

x\mathrel{{}_{L_{1}}{\lessapprox}}y\longleftrightarrow\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,x\land l_{1}\,x\mathrel{{\leq_{R_{1}}}}y
whenever
(({\leq_{L_{1}}})\unlhd({\leq_{R_{1}}}))\,l_{1}\,r_{1}
, and

•

y^{\prime}\mathrel{{}_{R_{1}}{\lessapprox}}x^{\prime}\longleftrightarrow\mathsf{in\_dom}\,({\leq_{R_{1}}})\,y^{\prime}\land r_{1}\,y^{\prime}\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{1}}}}x^{\prime}

whenever
(({\leq_{R_{1}}})\unlhd({\leq_{L_{1}}}))\,r_{1}\,l_{1}

due to
Lemma

3
.
For Galois equivalences
\big(({\leq_{L_{1}}})\equiv_{\mathsf{G}}({\leq_{R_{1}}})\big)\,l_{1}\,r_{1}

it is thus sufficient to search for a chain

,

\big(({{}_{L_{1}}{\lessapprox}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}({{}_{R_{1}}{\lessapprox}})\big)\,x\,x^{\prime}

Hence the definition of
({\leq_{L}})
.

Remark 2
.

A Galois connection

\big(({\leq_{L_{1}}})\dashv({\leq_{R_{1}}})\big)\,l_{1}\,r_{1}

would not be sufficient due to
Section

0.B.2
:
We are given some
y^{\prime}:\mathsf{\beta}
and
x^{\prime}:\mathsf{\alpha}

and need to check whether
y^{\prime}
is “smaller” than
x^{\prime}
.
We may check this by either transporting
y^{\prime}
to the left
(i.e.
r_{1}\,y^{\prime}\mathrel{{\leq_{L_{1}}}}x^{\prime}
)
or
x^{\prime}
to the right (i.e.
y^{\prime}\mathrel{{\leq_{R_{1}}}}l_{1}\,x^{\prime}
).
However, right adjoints only preserve infima
while left adjoints only preserve suprema.
Hence the need for

\big(({\leq_{R_{1}}})\dashv({\leq_{L_{1}}})\big)\,r_{1}\,l_{1}
.

Now it is not to be excluded that there is an alternative way
that avoids the need of a Galois equivalence.
But at least thus far,
it has eluded the author.

Remark 3
.

As noted, the relations

({\leq_{L}})
and
({\leq_{R}})

may not be equal to

({\leq_{L_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{R_{2}}})

but, in some sense, describe those parts
that were made “compatible” with respect to

and

While our formalisation includes conditions

they do not apply to all practical examples.
It is indeed a challenge on its own to find
particular conditions under which the relations

({\leq_{L}})
and
({\leq_{R}})

may be rewritten to a simpler form.
In this direction, the
thesis of Kunčar
[

]

includes ideas applicable to total quotients and partial subtypes.

Comparison To Isabelle’s Lifting Package

As mentioned, our definitions are
inspired by
[
13
]
:
Let
(T_{1},l_{1},r_{1})
and
(T_{2},l_{2},r_{2})

be two partial quotient types
with induced left relations
({\leq_{L_{1}}})
and
({\leq_{L_{2}}})
.
Huffman and and Kunčar then construct the composition

(T_{1}\mathbin{{\circ}}T_{2},l_{2}\circ l_{1},r_{1}\circ r_{2})
.
Moreover, they prove that the induced left relation
({\leq_{L}})

of this composed partial quotient type satisfies

({\leq_{L}})=T_{1}\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}T_{1}^{-1}
.
This insight sparked the idea of our definitions.

Indeed, we can show that our definitions
faithfully generalise their work.
Just as
Lemma

1

shows that

T_{1}=\mathsf{Galois}\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,(=)\,r_{1}
,
we can show that

T_{1}^{-1}=\mathsf{Galois}\,(=)\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,l_{1}
.
It then follows that

\big(T_{1}\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}T_{1}^{-1}\big)=\big(\mathsf{Galois}\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,(=)\,r_{1}\mathbin{{\circ}}({\leq_{L_{2}}})\mathbin{{\circ}}\mathsf{Galois}\,(=)\,({\leq_{L_{1}}})\,l_{1}\big).

Moreover, it is easy to show that the compatibility condition
is vacuously true for partial quotient types.
