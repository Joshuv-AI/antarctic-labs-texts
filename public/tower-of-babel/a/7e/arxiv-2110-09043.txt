Stroboscopic quantum nondemolition measurements for enhanced entanglement generation between atomic ensembles
Manish Chaudhary, Yuping Mao, Manikandan Kondappan, Amiel S. P. Paz, Valentin Ivannikov, Tim Byrnes

Abstract
We develop a measurement operator formalism to handle quantum nondemolition (QND) measurement induced entanglement generation between two atomic gases. We first derive how the QND entangling scheme reduces to a positive operator valued measure (POVM), and consider its limiting case when it can be used to construct a projection operator that collapses the state to a total spin projection state. We then analyze how a stroboscopic sequence of such projections made in the x x and z z basis evolves the initial wavefunction. Such a sequence of QND projections can enhance the entanglement between the atomic ensembles and makes the state converge towards a highly entangled state. We show several mathematical identities which greatly simplify the state evolution in the projection sequence, and allows one to derive the exact state in a highly efficient manner. Our formalism does not use the Holstein-Primakoff approximation as is conventionally done, and treats the spins of the atomic gases in an exact way.

II.1
Definitions

First consider the states of the atomic gas clouds (see Fig.
1
). We consider each atom within the ensembles to be occupied by one of two internal states. For example, the states may be two hyperfine ground states of an atom (e.g.
F=1,m=-1
and
F=2,m=+1
for
87
Rb). The motional degrees of freedom of the atom are decoupled from the spin and may be neglected. In the case of a BEC, we may define bosonic annihilation operators
e_{j},g_{j}
for the two internal states respectively, and
j\in\{1,2\}
label the two atomic ensembles
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021
. For a collection of
N
atoms in each BEC, the initial state of the atomic cloud can be described by

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{N}\psi_{k_{1}k_{2}}|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle,

(1)

where we have defined the Fock states on the
j
th atomic ensemble as

\displaystyle|k\rangle=\frac{(e_{j}^{\dagger})^{k}(g_{j}^{\dagger})^{N-k}}{\sqrt{k!(N-k)!}}|\text{vac}\rangle,

(2)

and

\displaystyle|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle=|k_{1}\rangle\otimes|k_{1}\rangle.

(3)

Here the Fock states obey
\langle k|k^{\prime}\rangle=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
and the coefficients
\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}|\psi_{k_{1}k_{2}}|^{2}=1
are normalized.

For uncondensed thermal atomic gases, in general there are
2^{N}
possible spin configurations per ensemble, instead of the
N+1
states as defined in (
2
). However, if the initial state and all applied Hamiltonians are completely symmetric under particle interchange on a single ensemble, there is a mathematical equivalence between the BEC description and the thermal ensemble
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021
. Since we will work in the completely symmetric subspace, our results will be equally valid for the thermal atomic case, despite using the bosonic notation.

The collective spin operators on the
j
th ensemble are defined by

\displaystyle S^{x}_{j}

\displaystyle=e_{j}^{\dagger}g_{j}+e_{j}^{\dagger}g_{j}

\displaystyle S^{y}_{j}

\displaystyle=-ie_{j}^{\dagger}g_{j}+ig_{j}^{\dagger}e_{j}

\displaystyle S^{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=e_{j}^{\dagger}e_{j}-g_{j}^{\dagger}g_{j},

(4)

obeying commutation relations
[S^{j},S^{k}]=2i\epsilon_{jkl}S^{l}
, where
\epsilon_{jkl}
is the completely anti-symmetric Levi-Civita tensor.
Spin coherent states, which are completely polarized spin configurations with Bloch sphere angles
\theta,\phi
are defined as

\displaystyle|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\left(e^{\dagger}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}+g^{\dagger}e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{N}|\text{vac}\rangle.

(5)

Expanding the spin coherent state we may equally write this in terms of Fock states

\displaystyle|\theta,\phi\rangle\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sqrt{N\choose k}e^{i(N-k)\phi}\cos^{k}\frac{\theta}{2}\sin^{N-k}\frac{\theta}{2}|k\rangle.

(6)

II.2
QND entangled wavefunction

The QND entangling scheme is shown in Fig.
1
.
Here, coherent light is arranged in a Mach-Zehnder configuration and the two atomic gases are placed in each arm of the interferometer. Preparing the atoms in the initial state (
1
), the light interacts with the atomic spins via the QND Hamiltonian
Ilo-Okeke and Byrnes 2014
,

\displaystyle H=\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2})(a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1}-a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{2}),

(7)

where
a_{1},a_{2}
denote the bosonic annihilation operators of the light in the two arms of the interferometer. After interacting with the atoms, the two modes are interfered via the second beam splitter and the photons are detected.

The above sequence modulates the quantum state of the atoms due to the atom-light entanglement that is generated by the QND interaction. This can be evaluated exactly due to the diagonal form of (
7
). We refer the reader to Ref.
Aristizabal-Zuluaga
et al. 2021
for further details and present only the final result. The final unnormalized state after detection of
n_{c},n_{d}
photons in modes

respectively is

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\psi}_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{N}\psi_{k_{1},k_{2}}C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2})\tau]|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle,

(8)

where we defined the function

\displaystyle C_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\chi)=\frac{\alpha^{n_{c}+n_{d}}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{n_{c}!n_{d}!}}\cos^{n_{c}}(\chi)\sin^{n_{d}}(\chi).

(9)

Here,
\alpha
is the amplitude of the coherent light entering the first beamsplitter in Fig.
1
and
\tau=\Omega t
. The probability of obtaining a photonic measurement outcome
n_{c},n_{d}
is

\displaystyle p_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\tau)|\widetilde{\psi}_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{N}|\psi_{k_{1}k_{2}}C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2})\tau]|^{2}.

(10)

We note that the
C
-functions are normalized according to

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{c},n_{d}=0}^{\infty}|C_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\chi)|^{2}=1.

(11)

The most likely photon number counting outcomes are centered around

\displaystyle n_{c}+n_{d}\approx|\alpha|^{2},

(12)

since the input coherent state has an average photon number of
|\alpha|^{2}
and the remaining operations are photon number conserving.

The
C
-functions can be approximated for bright coherent light regime
|\alpha|\gg 1
as

\displaystyle C_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\chi)\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{n_{c}+n_{d}}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{(n_{c}+n_{d})!}}\frac{\text{sgn}(\cos^{n_{c}}(\chi)\sin^{n_{d}}(\chi))}{(\frac{\pi}{2}(n_{c}+n_{d})\sin^{2}2\chi)^{1/4}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\Big[-\frac{(n_{c}+n_{d})}{\sin^{2}2\chi}\big(\sin^{2}\chi-\frac{n_{d}}{n_{c}+n_{d}}\big)^{2}\Big],

(13)

for
n_{d}>0
. In the case of
n_{d}=0
, the
C
-function is better approximated as

\displaystyle C_{n_{c}n_{d}=0}(\chi)\approx\frac{\alpha^{n_{c}}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{n_{c}!}}e^{-\frac{n_{c}}{2}\sin^{2}\chi}.

(14)

Example

To see how entanglement is generated by the QND scheme, let us choose an initial state for the atoms that is polarized in the

-direction

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\frac{\pi}{2},0\rangle\rangle|\frac{\pi}{2},0\rangle\rangle

III
Quantum measurement theory for QND induced entanglement

In quantum mechanics, a measurement is represented by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
, of which projective measurements are a special case. The QND entangling procedure may be viewed as a particular way of measuring the atomic states such that it collapses the state onto an entangled state. In this section, we introduce a POVM based theory of QND measurements, and its associated relations to connect the photonic readouts to particular projection operators.

III.1
QND POVM operators

According to the QND entangling protocol described in the previous section, the initial wave function (
1
) is modulated by an extra factor of
C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2})\tau]
and the final state becomes (
8
).
An efficient way of summarizing this procedure is to define the measurement operator

\displaystyle M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}C_{n_{c},n_{d}}[(k_{1}-k_{2})\tau]|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle\langle k_{1},k_{2}|.

(17)

According to the theory of quantum measurements, the resulting state after the measurement is

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\psi}_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)\rangle=M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)|\psi\rangle

(18)

and the probability of this outcome is

\displaystyle p_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)=\langle\psi|M_{n_{c}n_{d}}^{\dagger}(\tau)M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)|\psi\rangle

(19)

in agreement with (
8
) and (
10
) respectively. Since
M_{n_{c}n_{d}}^{\dagger}(\tau)M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)
is a positive operator and we can evaluate

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{c},n_{d}}M^{\dagger}_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)M_{n_{c},n_{d}}(\tau)=I

(20)

11

M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau)
satisfies the definition of being a POVM.

III.2
QND projection operators

In the limit that the intensity of coherent light
|\alpha|^{2}
is very bright such that
|\alpha\tau|^{2}>1
, the Gaussian function in (
13
) is sharply defined and strongly suppresses values of
k_{1}-k_{2}
away from (
21
).
In this limit, when two such measurements are made in succession, the net result is a projection operator. Taking the interaction time
\tau=\pi/2N
, there are two values of
k_{1}-k_{2}
where the projections occur (Fig.
2
(d)), as given by (
23
). We may then approximate the POVM according to

\displaystyle M_{n_{c}n_{d}}(\tau=\frac{\pi}{2N})\approx\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}\hskip 28.45274pt(|\alpha\tau|^{2}\gg 1)

(24)

where the
\Delta
and
n_{c},n_{d}
are related according to (
23
), and we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\delta_{\Delta}}}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{N-\Delta}|k,k+\Delta\rangle\langle k,k+\Delta|

\displaystyle\pm\sum_{k^{\prime}=\Delta}^{N}|k^{\prime},k^{\prime}-\Delta\rangle\langle k^{\prime},k^{\prime}-\Delta|\Big).

A double sequence of such POVMs then gives the projection operator

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}\mathcal{M}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\delta_{\Delta}}}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{N-\Delta}|k,k+\Delta\rangle\langle k,k+\Delta|

\displaystyle+\sum_{k^{\prime}=\Delta}^{N}|k^{\prime},k^{\prime}-\Delta\rangle\langle k^{\prime},k^{\prime}-\Delta|\Big).

(25)

Here
\delta_{\Delta}
is the Kronecker delta which is 1 if
\Delta=0
and 0 otherwise.

The above projection operators are defined with respect to Fock states that are in the
S^{z}
basis. We may equally define the Fock states in a different basis
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021

\displaystyle|k\rangle^{(\theta,\phi)}={\cal U}(\theta,\phi)|k\rangle^{(z)}

(26)

where

\displaystyle{\cal U}(\theta,\phi)=e^{-i(S^{z}_{1}+S^{z}_{2})\phi/2}e^{-i(S^{y}_{1}+S^{y}_{2})\theta/2}

(27)

and
|k\rangle^{(z)}
are the same Fock states as defined in (
2
). The projection operators may then be defined with respect to Fock states in a different basis

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{(\theta,\phi)}={\cal U}(\theta,\phi)P_{\Delta}^{(z)}{\cal U}^{\dagger}(\theta,\phi)

(28)

where
P_{\Delta}^{(z)}
is the same projector as in (
25
), but we explicitly specified the basis with the
(z)
label. For the case that
\theta=\pi/2
and
\phi=0
, the unitary rotation transforms the
S^{z}
eigenstates to

eigenstates and we define

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{(x)}={\cal U}(\frac{\pi}{2},0)P_{\Delta}^{(z)}{\cal U}^{\dagger}(\frac{\pi}{2},0).

(29)

III.3
Properties of projection operators

Here we list the properties of projection operators (
28
). These also apply to the specific cases (
25
) and (
29
).

1.

The projection operators are idempotent and orthogonal

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{(\theta,\phi)}P_{\Delta^{\prime}}^{(\theta,\phi)}=\delta_{\Delta\Delta^{\prime}}P_{\Delta}^{(\theta,\phi)}.

(30)

2.

Projection operators are Hermitian

\displaystyle(P^{(\theta,\phi)}_{\Delta})^{\dagger}=P^{(\theta,\phi)}_{\Delta}.

(31)

3.

Projection operators are complete

\displaystyle\sum_{\Delta}(P^{(\theta,\phi)}_{\Delta})^{\dagger}P^{(\theta,\phi)}_{\Delta}=\sum_{\Delta}P^{(\theta,\phi)}_{\Delta}=I.

(32)

4.

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of projection operators are

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{(\theta,\phi)}|k,k+\Delta^{\prime}\rangle^{(\theta,\phi)}=\delta_{\Delta\Delta^{\prime}}|k,k+\Delta^{\prime}\rangle^{(\theta,\phi)}.

(33)

The proofs of these properties are straightforward and can be verified by substituting the definitions.

III.4
Example

We show that the projection operator can generate entanglement between the two atomic ensembles by applying it to the initial state (
15
). For example, for the outcome
\Delta=0
we have

\displaystyle P_{\Delta=0}^{(z)}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N}{N\choose k}|k,k\rangle.

(34)

Similarly to that obtained in Sec.
II.3
, we obtain an entangled state between the two BECs.

We point out that the entanglement generation depends upon the preparation of a suitable initial state. From (
33
) it is evident that the projection operator applied to a product state of two Fock states leaves it unchanged. Since a product state does not possess entanglement between the BECs, the operation does not produce entanglement in this case. In this sense, a single QND measurement should not be considered an equivalent of Bell measurement, for which any initial state and measurement outcome results in an entangled state. It is better described as a projective operation which can result in an entangled state for suitable initial states. In the next sections we show how a sequence of projections can drive an arbitrary initial state into an entangled state.

IV
Sequential QND projections: pure state analysis

In Ref.
Aristizabal-Zuluaga
et al. 2021
a two-pulse scheme for improved entanglement generation was analyzed. In the scheme, first the initial state (
15
) is prepared, and a QND measurement is performed, to yield a state similar to (
34
). Then a unitary rotation
e^{iS^{x}_{1}\pi/4}e^{-iS^{x}_{2}\pi/4}
was performed and the QND measurement was repeated. This was found to produce a more strongly entangled state than using only a single QND measurement. Such a sequence of pulses have been used experimentally in several studies to enhance the entanglement in single atomic ensembles
Behbood
et al. 2013a
;
Behbood
et al. 2013b
;
Behbood et al. 2014
;
Vasilakis et al. 2015
. We show in this section that the technique is equally applicable in the two-ensemble case, and develop a theory to evaluate the result of multiple projections.

IV.1
Multiple QND measurements

First let us define the multiple QND measurement scheme in terms of the formalism we have introduced so far. We work in the regime
|\alpha\tau|^{2}>1
such that the QND measurements can be described using the projection operators introduced in Sec.
III.2
. Let us define a particular QND projection sequence as

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}

\displaystyle=P_{\Delta^{x}_{L}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{L}}^{(z)}\dots P_{\Delta^{x}_{2}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{2}}^{(z)}P_{\Delta^{x}_{1}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{1}}^{(z)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Big[P_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{l}}^{(z)}\Big].

(35)

where the projections in the
z
- and
x
-basis are defined in (
25
) and (
29
) respectively. In the product operator, we take the convention that the order of the projectors is arranged from right to left, for labels running from the lower index to the upper index. Each projection is made in an alternating basis switching between
z
and
x
.
The sequence consists of
L
repetitions of projections in the
z
and
x
basis. We take the convention that a sequence always starts with a projection in the
z
-basis. Equation (
35
) is an example where there are an even number of projections (a total of
2L
projections) in total. Thus in this case the last projector will be in the
x
-basis. The outcomes are specified by an ordered list

\displaystyle\vec{\Delta}_{2L}=(\Delta_{1}^{z},\Delta_{1}^{x},\Delta_{2}^{z},\Delta_{2}^{x},\dots,\Delta_{L}^{z},\Delta_{L}^{x}).

(36)

where the subscript
2L
shows the number of projections that are made. Physically, these can be interpreted as the random outcomes associated with a particular projection sequence.

For an odd number of projections, we have instead

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}

\displaystyle=P_{\Delta^{z}_{L+1}}^{(z)}P_{\Delta^{x}_{L}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{L}}^{(z)}\dots P_{\Delta^{x}_{2}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{2}}^{(z)}P_{\Delta^{x}_{1}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{1}}^{(z)}

\displaystyle=P_{\Delta^{z}_{L+1}}^{(z)}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Big[P_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{l}}^{(z)}\Big].

(37)

Here a particular sequence is specified by
2L+1
parameters

\displaystyle\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}=(\Delta_{1}^{z},\Delta_{1}^{x},\Delta_{2}^{z},\Delta_{2}^{x},\dots,\Delta_{L}^{z},\Delta_{L}^{x},\Delta_{L+1}^{z}).

(38)

We shall see that the parity of the number of projections (i.e. whether it is even or odd) will make a large difference to the final state. When discussing a property without any parity dependence, we will drop the subscript on
\vec{\Delta}
for brevity.

Starting from an initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, after a particular projection sequence we obtain an unnormalized state (denoted by the tilde)

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}_{\vec{\Delta}}\rangle

\displaystyle=T_{\vec{\Delta}}|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(39)

The probability of this particular outcome labeled by
\vec{\Delta}
occurring is

\displaystyle p_{\vec{\Delta}}=\langle\tilde{\psi}_{\vec{\Delta}}|\tilde{\psi}_{\vec{\Delta}}\rangle,

(40)

where the probabilities satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\Delta}}p_{\vec{\Delta}}\equiv\sum_{\Delta_{1}^{z}}\sum_{\Delta_{1}^{x}}\dots\sum_{\Delta_{L+1}^{z}}p_{\vec{\Delta}}=1.

(41)

We may visualize the sequence of projections as shown in Fig.
4
. Each projection occurs randomly and yields in general a different state. Successive projections yield new states that depend upon the past projection outcomes.

Figure 4:
Tree diagram corresponding to the multiple QND projection sequence
T_{\vec{\Delta}}

).

We note that the sequence of projection operators
T_{\vec{\Delta}}
is not itself a projection operator. Specifically, the property of idempotence and orthogonality (
30
) does not hold. They are however complete and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{\Delta}}T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{\dagger}T_{\vec{\Delta}}=\sum_{\vec{\Delta}}T_{\vec{\Delta}}=I,

(42)

which may be used to show (
41
).

IV.2
Example: convergence to maximally entangled state

To see the effect of the multiple QND projections, it is illustrative to see a simple example. Let us consider the particular outcome sequence with an odd number of projections

\displaystyle\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}=(0,0,\dots,0).

(43)

The final state in this case is

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}\rangle=P_{0}^{(z)}\Big(P_{0}^{(x)}P_{0}^{(z)}\Big)^{L}|\psi_{0}\rangle.

The effect of the projector

is to remove all elements from the wavefunction except

in (

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{kk}|k,k\rangle.

(45)

IV.3
Singular value decomposition of two projectors

The example in the previous section illustrates the general behavior of multiple QND projections. The basic behavior is that after a number of QND measurements, the state converges to a fixed state. This was a relatively simple example, where some known properties of the states could be used to deduce the final state. We now examine the more general case for an arbitrary
\vec{\Delta}
.

The following key mathematical result greatly simplifies the subsequent analysis:

Lemma 1
.

Consider a sequence of two projections

\displaystyle T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}=P_{\Delta^{x}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}

(52)

as defined in (
35
). Its singular value decomposition can be written

\displaystyle T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}=U\Lambda_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}V^{\dagger}

(53)

where
U,V
are unitary matrices that are independent of
\Delta^{z},\Delta^{x}
, and
\Lambda_{\Delta^{z},\Delta^{x}}
is a diagonal matrix up to a permutation of rows and columns, i.e. a matrix with only one non-zero element in each row and column.

Proof.

The full proof is given in Appendix
A
. Here we provide the main steps of the proof. First define the Hermitian matrix

\displaystyle R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}

\displaystyle\equiv T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}^{\dagger}T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}

(54)

\displaystyle=P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{\Delta^{x}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}

(55)

From Appendix
A
, it follows that for arbitrary choices of the index
\Delta^{z},\Delta^{x}
we have

\displaystyle[R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}},R_{{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}]=0.

(56)

For any two commuting Hermitian matrices, it is possible to write down a common unitary transformation that diagonalizes both the operators.
Since all
R
’s mutually commute, there is a common unitary transformation for all the
R
’s. Hence

\displaystyle R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}=VD_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}V^{\dagger},

(57)

where
D
is a diagonal matrix. Substituting (
53
) in (
54
) we have

\displaystyle R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}=V\Lambda_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}\Lambda_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}.

(58)

Proving (
56
) for all possible
\Delta^{z}
and
\Delta^{x}
demonstrates that a common set of eigenvectors diagonalizes the Gram matrix
R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}
, and the resultant eigenvector matrix therefore forms a consistent set of left singular vectors for
T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}
.
For the
U
unitary matrix, the argument is identical except that one starts with the definition
R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}=T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}^{\dagger}
.
∎

An example of Lemma 1 is shown in Appendix
B
for illustration.

IV.4
Evaluation of multiple projectors

With the aid of Lemma 1, we may write a more general sequence of projections in simpler form.

Lemma 2
.

The matrix product for a sequence of projections with an odd number of projectors as in (
37
) can be written

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}

\displaystyle=V\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}\right)V^{\dagger},

(59)

and for an even number of projectors as in (
35
),

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}

\displaystyle=U\Lambda_{\Delta_{L}^{x}\Delta_{L}^{z}}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}\right)V^{\dagger},

(60)

for
L\geq 1
.
Here the unitary matrices
U,V
and the matrices

\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}
are the same as given in (
53
).

Proof.

Using the idempotence relation we can write (
37
) as

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Big[P_{\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}^{(z)}P_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}_{l}}^{(z)}\Big]

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}T_{\Delta_{l+1}^{z}\Delta_{l}^{x}}^{\dagger}T_{\Delta_{l}^{z}\Delta_{l}^{x}}.

(61)

Substituting (
53
) into (
61
), the unitary rotations
U,V
at all intermediate steps can be removed by
U^{\dagger}U=V^{\dagger}V=I
since they are common for all
\Delta^{x},\Delta^{z}
. This yields (
59
). For (
60
), use (
59
) with
L\rightarrow L-1
and apply (
53
) once.
∎

From Lemma 2, we see that in a sequence of projections, there is a particular basis where the evolution of the state becomes particularly simple. The
\Lambda
matrices are diagonal up to a permutation of rows and columns, which means that the computation can be performed efficiently.

To illustrate this, let us now use Lemma 2 to determine the effect of applying the sequential projection for the particular case of an initial state in the
V
-basis

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle=|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}\equiv V|\bm{k}\rangle^{(z)},

(62)

where
|\bm{k}\rangle^{(z)}
is defined in (
3
), and we have defined

\displaystyle\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2}).

(63)

Considering the case with an odd number of projectors first, apply (
59
) to (
62
). This results in the unnormalized state

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}=V\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}\right)|\bm{k}\rangle^{(z)},

(64)

where we take the case with an odd number of projections. Since the matrix
\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}
is a permutation matrix up to some coefficients, the effect of each application of the matrix will be to change the labels
\bm{k}\rightarrow\bm{k}^{\prime}
, and adjust the normalization of the state. Let us define the permutation function associated with the matrix
\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}

\displaystyle\bm{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pi_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}(\bm{k}).

(65)

For the matrix
\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}^{\dagger}
, we have the inverse function

\displaystyle\bm{k}

\displaystyle=\pi_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}^{-1}(\bm{k}^{\prime}).

(66)

Then for the
l
th pair of
\Lambda
matrices in (
64
), the labels shift by

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{l}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\pi_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}(\bm{k}_{l})

\displaystyle\bm{k}_{l+1}

\displaystyle=\pi^{-1}_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}(\bm{k}_{l}^{\prime}).

(67)

Here
\bm{k}_{l}^{\prime}
are the state labels after application of
\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}
and
\bm{k}_{l+1}
are the labels after the
\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}
. The initial state label is
\bm{k}_{1}=\bm{k}
in (
62
). This is a recursive relation by which we can evaluate the final state indices.

The amplitude of (
64
) can be evaluated by
defining the matrix element for this permutation as

\displaystyle a_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}(\bm{k})=\langle\bm{k}^{\prime}|\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}|\bm{k}\rangle.

(68)

Application of each
\Lambda
matrix gives a factor as given in (
68
). Thus the final coefficient is

\displaystyle A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Big[a_{\Delta^{x}_{l}\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}(\bm{k}_{l+1})a_{\Delta^{x}_{l}\Delta^{z}_{l}}(\bm{k}_{l})\Big],

(69)

and the
\bm{k}_{l}
are evaluated by the recursion relation (
67
). We also define the result of
L
rounds of recursion of the relation (
67
) as

\displaystyle r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})=\bm{k}_{L+1}.

(70)

Then (
64
) can be evaluated to give the unnormalized state

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}=A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})|r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})\rangle^{(V)}.

(71)

For the case with an even number of projectors, we have

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}=U\Lambda_{\Delta_{L}^{x}\Delta_{L}^{z}}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}}\right)|\bm{k}\rangle^{(z)},

(72)

using (
60
). The result of the recursion relation in this case is

\displaystyle r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})=\bm{k}_{L}^{\prime}.

(73)

and the coefficient is

\displaystyle A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})=a_{\Delta^{x}_{L}\Delta^{z}_{L}}(\bm{k}_{L})\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\Big[a_{\Delta^{x}_{l}\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}(\bm{k}_{l+1})a_{\Delta^{x}_{l}\Delta^{z}_{l}}(\bm{k}_{l})\Big].

(74)

The final unnormalized result in this case is

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}=A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})|r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})\rangle^{(U)},

(75)

note that the final result is in the
U
-basis in this case because of the
U
in (
72
).

For (
71
) and (
75
), the normalized state is simply
|r_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})\rangle^{(V)}
, unless
A_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})=0
in which case the projection sequence is an outcome with zero probability.

We are now ready to state our first main result.

Theorem 1
.

Applying a sequence of projections
T_{\vec{\Delta}}
on an arbitrary initial state
|\phi\rangle
results in the unnormalized state

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}|\phi\rangle=\sum_{\bm{k}}A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})\phi_{\bm{k}}^{(V)}|r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}(\bm{k})\rangle^{(V)}

(76)

for an odd number of projectors as in (
37
) and

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}|\phi\rangle=\sum_{\bm{k}}A_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})\phi_{\bm{k}}^{(V)}|r_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L}}(\bm{k})\rangle^{(U)}

(77)

for an even number of projectors as in (
35
). Here,
\phi_{\bm{k}}^{(V)}=\langle\bm{k}|V^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle
is the amplitude of
|\phi\rangle
in the
V
-basis,
L\geq 1
,

r_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})
is the result of the recursion relation (
70
) and (
73
) and
A_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})
is defined in (
69
) and (
74
).

Proof.

Inserting the resolution of the identity in the
V
-basis

\displaystyle T_{\vec{\Delta}}|\phi\rangle=T_{\vec{\Delta}}\sum_{\bm{k}}|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}\langle\bm{k}|^{(V)}|\phi\rangle

(78)

and using the results (
71
) and (
75
) give (
76
) and (
77
).
∎

It follows straightforwardly from (
76
) that the probability of a particular sequence of projections is given by

\displaystyle p_{\vec{\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi|T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{\dagger}T_{\vec{\Delta}}|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}|A_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})\phi_{\bm{k}}^{(V)}|^{2}.

(79)

The states (
76
) and (
77
) can then be normalized by simply dividing by
\sqrt{p_{\vec{\Delta}}}
.

Equations (
76
) and (
77
) allows for a highly efficient way to evaluate the result of a sequence of projections. In words, the procedure is as follows. Given an arbitrary initial state
|\phi\rangle
, first expand the state in the
V
-basis, defined by (
62
). Then for each state labeled by
\bm{k}
, we can recursively apply (
67
)
until we find
\bm{k}_{L}
. This results in obtaining the sequence of
\bm{k}_{l},\bm{k}_{l}^{\prime}
, from which the overall coefficient
A
can be found through (
69
). Multiplying the coefficient by the associated term in the superposition gives (
76
). This procedure is far more efficient than evaluating
2L+1
matrix multiplications directly, each with an overhead of
(N+1)^{6}
.

Example: stochastic evolution

While (
76
) and (

) gives the general result for an arbitrary sequence of projections, for a typical sequence the resulting dynamics is often rather simple.
Figure

shows the probability distribution of evolving a random initial state with the sequence of QND projections

\displaystyle p(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\langle\bm{k}|{{}^{(V)}}T_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}|\phi\rangle|^{2}}{p_{\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}}},

(80)

where we consider an odd number of projections, such that the output state is in the
V
-basis. Here the measurement outcomes
\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}
are chosen randomly according to their measurement probabilities. We see that the state quickly becomes dominated by a single state in the
V
-basis
|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}
. Depending upon the measurement outcomes and the initial state, the state can remain fixed in a particular state (Fig.

67
). However, at any given step in the projection sequence, the state is typically in only only of the

The reason for this can be seen from (
76
). After a sequence of projections is applied, the state becomes modified by the factor

. Since this is a product of matrix elements according to (

), depending upon the particular
\vec{\Delta}
that occurs, one of the coefficients of
|\bm{k}\rangle^{(V)}
becomes dominant, due to the exponential increase of a dominant factor. Similar results occur for an even number of projections, with the main difference being that the final state is in the

-basis.

We summarize the effect of multiple QND projections by the following basic rule-of-thumb:
it collapses the state to one of the states in the
V
-basis for an odd number of projections, and one of the
U
-basis states for an even number of projections
.

V
Sequential QND projections: Mixed state analysis

In the previous section we considered the effect of applying a sequence of projections
T_{\vec{\Delta}}
to a pure state. This results in a stochastic evolution of the state, as illustrated in Fig.
7
. This corresponds to a single run of a particular experiment. It is also useful to analyze this in a statistical sense, where we deal with a probabilistic evolution of the state. The natural language for this are density matrices, and in this section we adapt the results of the previous section to the mixed state case.

V.1
Sequence of QND projections on a mixed state

Let us first generalize the results of the previous section to the case when the initial state is a mixed state with density matrix
\rho_{0}
. After performing a projection sequence the resulting unnormalized density matrix is

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\vec{\Delta}}=T_{\vec{\Delta}}\rho_{0}T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{\dagger}

(81)

occurring with probability

\displaystyle p_{\vec{\Delta}}=\text{Tr}(T_{\vec{\Delta}}^{\dagger}T_{\vec{\Delta}}\rho_{0}).

(82)

Instead of a single shot outcome as we considered in the last section, we now wish to obtain the average over many possible runs of the QND projector sequence. Let us here restrict ourselves to the case that there is an odd number of projections
\vec{\Delta}=\vec{\Delta}_{2L+1}
. Averaging over all possible outcomes the density matrix is

\displaystyle\rho^{(V)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{\Delta}}(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}})\rho^{(V)}_{0}(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l+1}^{z}}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Delta_{l}^{x}\Delta_{l}^{z}})^{\dagger}

(83)

where we used (
59
) and we defined density matrices rotated into the
V
-basis as

\displaystyle\rho^{(V)}=V^{\dagger}\rho V.

(84)

Alternatively, we may use (
76
) to write

\displaystyle\rho^{(V)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{\Delta}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}A_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})A_{\vec{\Delta}}^{*}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\rho_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{(V)}|r_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k})\rangle\langle r_{\vec{\Delta}}(\bm{k}^{\prime})|

(85)

where
\rho_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{(V)}=\langle\bm{k}|V^{\dagger}\rho_{0}V|\bm{k}^{\prime}\rangle
.

V.2
Example: probabilistic evolution

We now numerically evaluate the density matrix evolution to illustrate the effect of the multiple projections. To evaluate the density matrix, rather than the expressions (
83
) or (
85
)
which contain a large number of summations over
\vec{\Delta}
, it is more efficient to evaluate the density matrices iteratively.
Consider the application of an even number of projectors (
35
) and averaging over all outcomes we have

\displaystyle\rho_{l}^{\prime}=\sum_{\Delta^{z}_{1},\Delta^{x}_{1},\dots,\Delta^{x}_{l}}P^{(x)}_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}\dots P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{z}_{1}}\rho_{0}P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{x}_{1}}\dots P^{(x)}_{\Delta^{z}_{l}}.

(86)

For an odd number of projectors we have instead

\displaystyle\rho_{l+1}=\sum_{\Delta^{z}_{1},\Delta^{x}_{1},\dots,\Delta^{x}_{l},\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}P^{(x)}_{\Delta^{x}_{l}}\dots P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{z}_{1}}\rho_{0}P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{x}_{1}}\dots P^{(x)}_{\Delta^{z}_{l}}P^{(z)}_{\Delta^{z}_{l+1}}.

(87)

Then using property (
30
) of projection operators, we may relate the two density matrices by

\displaystyle\rho_{l+1}=\sum_{\Delta^{x},\Delta^{z}}T^{\dagger}_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}\rho_{l}^{\prime}T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}.

(88)

Similarly, we may transform a density matrix with an odd number of projectors to an even one according to

\displaystyle\rho_{l}^{\prime}=\sum_{\Delta^{x},\Delta^{z}}T_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}\rho_{l}T^{\dagger}_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}.

(89)

Applying (
53

\displaystyle{\rho_{l}^{\prime}}^{(U)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Delta^{x},\Delta^{z}}\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}^{\dagger}{\rho_{l}}^{(V)}\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}

(90)

\displaystyle\rho_{l+1}^{(V)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\Delta^{x},\Delta^{z}}\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}{\rho_{l}^{\prime}}^{(U)}\Lambda_{\Delta^{x}\Delta^{z}}^{\dagger}.

(91)

where we defined

\displaystyle\rho^{(U)}=U^{\dagger}\rho U.

(92)

From (
90
) and (
91
) we may find the resulting density matrix iteratively, by starting with the initial state

.

We now show a method for finding the steady-state distribution of the density matrix, as illustrated in Fig.

VII
Summary and Conclusions

We have developed a theory of entangling QND measurements between atomic ensembles based on measurement operators. We first formulated the theory as developed in Ref.
Aristizabal-Zuluaga
et al. 2021
in terms of POVMs. We then examined a limiting case of the theory with
|\alpha\tau|^{2}>1
where two POVMs reduce to projection operators. This clarifies the way in which entanglement is generated in the system, but also reveals why non-maximal entanglement is generated with a single QND projection (see for e.g. (
34
)). We then proceeded to analyze the multiple projection case, where a sequence of projections in alternating
z
and
x
bases are made. Using a key mathematical observation (Lemma 1), the multiple projections can be simplified greatly. This resulted in Theorem 1, where it was found that it is possible to evaluate an arbitrary sequence of projections very efficiently, using only a set of recursion relations of the state labels and associated amplitudes. The same situation can be analyzed in a statistical sense using density matrices, where the convergent state can be obtained for a large number of projections.

The main difference between our results and past works is to firstly formulate the QND entangling operation as an exact POVM or projection operator. Past methods rely on using various approximation to the spins (e.g. a Holstein-Primakoff approximation). Since our theory is based upon Ref.
Aristizabal-Zuluaga
et al. 2021
which treats the spin dynamics exactly,
the POVM formulation is an exact solution of the QND dynamics. The projection
operator is a particular limiting case of this theory, but should be very accurate for the large photon regime that experiments are typically carried out in. Secondly, we have shown that a sequence of multiple QND measurements (i.e. the stroboscopic measurements) has a very simple mathematical structure that can be exploited. This yields a method to obtain the state after a number of measurements in a very efficient way. Normally, the projection sequence requires a calculation that scales as
(2L+1)(N+1)^{6}
, and there is little intuition to the procedure. We have shown that there is a particular basis in which the states collapse to (the
U
and
V
-basis), and makes clear the nature of the state that is obtained in the procedure.

The projected sequence to prepare a maximally entangled state as shown in Sec.
IV.2
is very attractive as a fundamental state preparation protocol for use in quantum information applications. It allows for a way of enhancing the spin correlations and can generate a highly entangled state of atomic ensembles. A prime application is spinor quantum computing
Byrnes et al. 2012
;
Byrnes et al. 2015
, where one deals with a many-body wavefunction to encode the logical states, and a regime away from the conventional
Holstein-Primakoff approximated states are used. Hence it is important to know
the effect of such entangling operations such that they can be used for quantum information applications. However, it should be pointed out that this is not deterministic, and represents merely one particular measurement outcome. This is similar to the one-ensemble counterpart as experimentally demonstrated in Ref.
Behbood et al. 2014
where post-selection was used to target the entangled singlet state. Due to the large Hilbert space of atomic ensembles, a more desirable scheme would be deterministic preparation of a target entangled state. For the one-ensemble case, feedback approaches were examined to achieve this
Behbood
et al. 2013b
. We leave investigation of such deterministic schemes as future work.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma 1

In this section we show that the commutative property (
56
) holds. First note that for
\Delta^{z}\neq{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle R_{\Delta^{z}\Delta^{x}}R_{{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}=P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}^{(x)}P_{{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}}^{(z)}=0

(104)

from the orthogonality of projection matrices. Thus for
\Delta^{z}\neq{\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}
the commutative relation (
56
) holds trivially. Thus we only need to prove (
56
) for the
\Delta^{z}={\Delta^{z}}^{\prime}
case, and our task will be to prove the equivalent relation

\displaystyle P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime\prime}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}{=}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime\prime}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}P_{{\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}^{(x)}P_{\Delta^{x}}^{(x)}.

(105)

Next let us write the relation (
105
) in an alternative form by defining new projection operators

\displaystyle\Pi_{\delta}^{(l)}=\sum_{k}|k,k+\delta\rangle\langle k,k+\delta|^{(l)}

(106)

where
\delta
can be a positive or negative integer, and
l\in\{x,z\}
indicates the basis. Then our original projection operators (
25
) are

\displaystyle P_{\Delta}^{(l)}=\Pi_{\Delta}^{(l)}+\Pi_{-\Delta}^{(l)}=\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\Pi_{\sigma\Delta}^{(l)}.

(107)

The relation that we wish to prove (
105
) can be rewritten in terms of the new projection operators as

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}\sigma_{5}}\Big(\Pi_{{\sigma_{1}\Delta^{z}}}^{(z)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{2}\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}^{(x)}\Pi_{\sigma_{3}\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{4}\Delta^{x}}^{\prime\prime}}^{(x)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{5}\Delta^{z}}}^{(z)}

\displaystyle-\Pi_{{\sigma_{1}\Delta^{z}}}^{(z)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{4}\Delta^{x}}^{\prime\prime}}^{(x)}\Pi_{\sigma_{3}\Delta^{z}}^{(z)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{2}\Delta^{x}}^{\prime}}^{(x)}\Pi_{{\sigma_{5}\Delta^{z}}}^{(z)}\Big)=0.

(108)

it is possible to relabel the
\sigma_{i}
variables since they are dummy indices, and we have made a convenient choice for the purposes of our proof.
We will show that the quantity inside parentheses is always zero, proving the desired relation.

The key insight to proving the desired relation is to notice that the
projectors (
106

S^{z}_{1}-S^{z}_{2}
, such that the spin difference between the BECs is fixed. It will be convenient to transform the projectors such that instead of a spin difference, the projectors (
106

S^{z}_{1}+S^{z}_{2}

\displaystyle e^{iS^{y}_{2}\frac{\pi}{2}}|k\rangle=(-1)^{k}|N-k\rangle.

(109)

We can therefore define the rotated projection operator

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}

\displaystyle=e^{iS^{y}_{2}\frac{\pi}{2}}\Pi_{\Delta}^{(z)}e^{-iS^{y}_{2}\frac{\pi}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}|k,N-k-\Delta\rangle\langle k,N-k-\Delta|,

(110)

which consists of states with total spin
S^{z}_{1}+S^{z}_{2}=-2\Delta
.

Let us now change the notation of the Fock states such that they are instead interpreted as angular momentum eigenstates. For a single BEC, we may write
Byrnes and Ilo-Okeke 2021

\displaystyle|k\rangle=\Big|j=\frac{N}{2},m=k-\frac{N}{2}\Big\rangle

(111)

where ‘

’ is the angular momentum quantum number and ‘
m
’ is the quantum number associated with the spin along the
z
-direction. Then the rotated projectors can be written in this notation

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}=\sum_{m}

\displaystyle|j_{1}=\frac{N}{2},m;j_{2}=\frac{N}{2},-m-\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle\langle j_{1}=\frac{N}{2},m;j_{2}=\frac{N}{2},-m-\Delta|.

(112)

Now we may rewrite the states in (
112
) in terms of the total angular momentum basis
|J,M\rangle
, consisting of two individual spins of equal angular momenta

. The total spin operator is defined

\displaystyle\bm{J}=\bm{j}_{1}+\bm{j}_{2}=\frac{\bm{S}_{1}+\bm{S}_{2}}{2}.

(113)

The conversion between the bases is achieved by Clebsch-Gordan coefficients

\displaystyle|j_{1},m_{1};j_{2},m_{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{J=|j_{1}-j_{2}|}^{j_{1}+j_{2}}|J,M=m_{1}+m_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\times\langle J,M=m_{1}+m_{2}|j_{1},m_{1};j_{2},m_{2}\rangle.

(114)

Substituting this into (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}=\sum_{J}|J,-\Delta\rangle^{(z)}\langle J,-\Delta|^{(z)}.

(115)

Here we used the

-sum unitarity relation for Clebsch-Gordan coefficients
Thompson 2008
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}

\displaystyle\langle J^{\prime},M^{\prime}|j_{1},m_{1};j_{2},m_{2}\rangle\langle j_{1},m_{1};j_{2},m_{2}|J,M\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{MM^{\prime}}.

(116)

The sum in (

Similarly, we have the projectors defined in the

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta^{x}}^{(x)}

\displaystyle=e^{-iJ^{y}\frac{\pi}{4}}\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}e^{iJ^{y}\frac{\pi}{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J}|J,-\Delta\rangle^{(x)}\langle J,-\Delta|^{(x)}

Let us now evaluate the sequence of projectors

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}\tilde{\Pi}_{{\Delta_{2}}}^{(x)}\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta_{3}}^{(z)}\tilde{\Pi}_{{\Delta_{4}}}^{(x)}\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}=\sum_{J}|J,-\Delta\rangle^{(z)}\langle J,-\Delta|^{(z)}

where we used the fact that a rotation of the angular momentum states preserves

\displaystyle\text{}^{(z)}\langle J,M|J^{\prime},M^{\prime}\rangle^{(x)}

We may also evaluate the sequence with the ordering of the

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}\tilde{\Pi}_{{\Delta_{4}}}^{(x)}\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta_{3}}^{(z)}\tilde{\Pi}_{{\Delta_{2}}}^{(x)}\tilde{\Pi}_{\Delta}^{(z)}=\sum_{J}|J,-\Delta\rangle^{(z)}\langle J,-\Delta|^{(z)}

The right hand sides of (
