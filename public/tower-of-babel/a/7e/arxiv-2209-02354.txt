A Generic Type System for Higher-Order Ψ \Psi -calculi
Alex Rønning Bendixen, Bjarke Bredow Bojesen, Hans Hüttel, Stian Lybech

Abstract
The Higher-Order Ψ \Psi -calculus framework (HO Ψ \Psi ) is a generalisation of many first- and higher-order extensions of the π \pi -calculus. It was proposed by Parrow et al. who showed that higher-order calculi such as HO π \pi and CHOCS can be expressed as HO Ψ \Psi -calculi. In this paper we present a generic type system for HO Ψ \Psi -calculi which extends previous work by Hüttel on a generic type system for first-order Ψ \Psi -calculi. Our generic type system satisfies the usual property of subject reduction and can be instantiated to yield type systems for variants of HO π \pi , including the type system for termination due to Demangeon et al.. Moreover, we derive a type system for the ρ \rho -calculus, a reflective higher-order calculus proposed by Meredith and Radestock. This establishes that our generic type system is richer than its predecessor, as the ρ \rho -calculus cannot be encoded in the π \pi -calculus in a way that satisfies standard criteria of encodability.



1
Introduction

Process calculi are formalisms for modelling and reasoning about concurrent and distributed computations; a prominent example being the
\pi
-calculus of Milner et al.
[
20
,
26
]
, which models computation as communication between processes, by passing messages on named channels.
Since its inception, a multitude of variants of the
\pi
-calculus have appeared; e.g. D
\pi

[
12
]
, the calculus of explicit fusions
[
10
]
, the spi-calculus with correspondence assertions
[
2
]
and the
\prescript{e}{}{\pi}
-calculus
[
7
]
.
These calculi are all
first-order
, in the sense that only atomic channel names can be passed around, not processes themselves.
Bengtson et al.
[
4
,
5
]
created
\Psi
-calculi
as a generalisation of these first-order variants and extensions, allowing a range of calculi, including all of the aforementioned, to be expressed as
instances
of the
\Psi
-calculus framework through appropriate settings of a small number of parameters.
However, there also exist
higher-order
variants of the
\pi
-calculus, such as the Higher-Order
\pi
-calculus, HO
\pi
,
[
25
,
24
]
, that also allow
processes
to be sent across channels.
Parrow et al.
[
22
]
have extended the
\Psi
-calculus framework with a construct for higher-order communication, creating the
Higher-Order

\Psi
-calculus, HO
\Psi
.
Calculi such as HO
\pi
and CHOCS
[
27
]
can now be represented as HO
\Psi
-instances, as well as every calculus that the ‘first-order’
\Psi
-calculus framework can represent.

One of the techniques for reasoning about processes is that of
type systems
.
The first type system for a process calculus is due to Milner
[
20
]
and deals with the notion of correct usage of channels in the
\pi
-calculus:
In a well-typed process only names of the correct type can be communicated.
Pierce and Sangiorgi
[
23
]
later described a type system that uses subtyping and capability tags to control the use of names as input or output channels; and also many of the aforementioned first-order extensions of the
\pi
-calculus have been given type systems to capture such properties as secrecy, authenticity and access control.

In
[
13
]
, Hüttel noted that these type systems, despite arising in different settings, share certain characteristics:
The type judgments for processes
P
are all of the form
\Gamma\vdash P
where
\Gamma
is a type environment recording the types of the free names in
P
, so processes are only classified as being either well-typed or not.
On the other hand, terms
M
are given a type
T
, so type judgements for terms are of the form
\Gamma\vdash M:T
.
Based on these shared characteristics, Hüttel then created a generic type system for the first-order
\Psi
-calculus framework, that generalises several of the type systems for the
\pi
-calculus and its variants.
This generic type system can similarly be instantiated through parameter settings to yield both well-known and new type systems for the calculi that are representable as first-order
\Psi
-calculi.
An important advantage of this approach is that a general result of type system soundness can be formulated, which is then inherited by all instances of the type system.

There has been some other works on generic type systems, notably those of König
[
17
]
, Caires
[
6
]
and Igarashi and Kobayashi
[
16
]
.
However, these are formulated for variants of the first-order
\pi
-calculus, which thus limits their applicability to languages that can be represented in the first-order paradigm.
Stated otherwise, they exclude languages such as the aforementioned
\prescript{e}{}{\pi}
-calculus, which cannot be encoded into the first-order
\pi
-calculus, as shown in
[
7
]
, but which nevertheless can be given a type system using the generic approach of Hüttel, indicating that the latter is a more general framework for first-order calculi.

However, the generic type system of Hüttel can only type
first-order
calculi; it cannot be instantiated to yield type system for
higher-order
calculi, such as HO
\pi
or CHOCS.
Both of these higher-order calculi can be encoded into the first-order
\pi
-calculus, as shown by Sangiorgi in
[
25
]
, and may therefore also be represented in just the first-order
\Psi
-calculus.
Not surprisingly, there is therefore little work on type systems for higher-order calculi, since these encodings allow us to disregard the higher-order behaviour and instead just type the first-order translations.
One exception is the type system for termination in variants of HO
\pi
, due to Demangeon et al.
[
8
]
.
As these authors argue, it may not always be desirable (or even possible) to type a higher-order language through a first-order representation, if the language contains features that are difficult (or impossible) to encode.
For example, higher-order behaviour may alternatively be viewed as a special case of
reflection
; i.e. the ability of a program to turn code into data, modify or compute with it, and reinstantiate it as running code; and process mobility here appears as a special case where data (code/processes) are transmitted without modification.

This reflective capability is inherent in the Reflective Higher-Order (RHO or
\rho
) calculus of Meredith and Radestock
[
19
]
, and this calculus cannot be uniformly encoded in the
\pi
-calculus, as shown in
[
18
]
.
This calculus therefore gives us an example of a language that cannot easily be represented in the first-order paradigm, thus making it difficult (or impossible) to adapt any of the existing first-order type systems to this language.
Yet it
can
be instantiated as a HO
\Psi
-calculus, as we shall show in the following.

The goal of the present paper is therefore to extend the aforementioned generic type system by Hüttel, to create a generic type system for the HO
\Psi
-calculus framework that will allow us to capture typability in the higher-order paradigm.
It allows us to identify what should be required of type systems for higher-order process calculi that are instances of the HO
\Psi
-calculus, and these requirements here take the form of a number of assumptions that must hold for each instance.
Like its predecessor, our generic type system also satisfies a general subject reduction property that is inherited by all instances.
We use this to formulate simple type systems for HO
\pi
, and we show that the type system for termination by Demangeon et al. also can be captured as an instance of our type system.
Lastly, we show that our generic type system can be instantiated to yield a type system for the
\rho
-calculus, which establishes that our type system is richer than the first-order type systems.
To our knowledge, no type system has hitherto been published for this calculus, so we regard this instance as a further contribution of the present paper.
A technical report with full proofs of most results is available in
[
3
]
.

2
The higher-order
\Psi
-calculus

The Higher-Order
\Psi
-calculus extends the original
\Psi
-calculus
[
4
]
with primitives for higher-order communication, i.e. process mobility.
In this section we first review the syntax of HO
\Psi
as given in
[
22
]
, and then proceed to give a reduction semantics for the calculus.

2.1
Syntax

The Higher-Order
\Psi
-calculus is a general framework, which is intended to allow many different calculi to be obtained as instances, by setting a small number of parameters which takes the form of definitions of three (not necessarily disjoint) sets of
terms, conditions
and
assertions
.
To allow the framework to be as general and flexible as possible, the authors of
[
4
,
22
]
identify only a few restrictions that must be imposed on these sets: they must be
nominal datatypes
.
Informally, a
nominal set
, in the sense of Gabbay and Pitts
[
9
]
, is a set whose members can be affected by names being bound or swapped.
If
a,b
are names and
X
is an element of a nominal set, then the
transposition
of
a
and
b
on
X
, written
\left(a,b\right)\kern-2.0pt\boldsymbol{\cdot}\kern-2.0ptX
, swaps all occurrences of
a
for
b
in
X
and vice versa.
A function on a nominal set is
equivariant
, if it is unaffected by name swapping; and a
nominal datatype
is a nominal set together with a set of equivariant functions on it.
This requirement is very mild and allows e.g. non-well-founded sets to be used in an instantiation.
The utility of this shall become apparent later, when we create a HO
\Psi
-calculus instance where the set of processes (which itself contains terms) is included in the set of terms.

Another important notion is that of support: if
X
is an element of a nominal set, the
support
of
X
, written
\supn\kern-2.0pt\left(X\right)
, is the set of names that occur in
X
.
Conversely, a name
a
is
fresh for
X
, written
a{\syntaxfont{\#}}X
, if
a\notin\supn\kern-2.0pt\left(X\right)
; and we extend this to sets of names
A
such that
A{\syntaxfont{\#}}X
if it is the case that
\forall a\in A.a\notin\supn\kern-2.0pt\left(X\right)
.
This is pointwise extended to lists of elements
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, so we write
A{\syntaxfont{\#}}X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
for
A{\syntaxfont{\#}}X_{1}\land\ldots\land A{\syntaxfont{\#}}X_{n}
.

As mentioned above, any
\Psi
-calculus instance requires a specification of three nominal datatypes: the terms, conditions and assertions.
The datatype of
terms
, ranged over by
M,N\in\mathds{T}
, contains the terms that can be communicated and used as channels.
These could be e.g. single names, as in the monadic
\pi
-calculus, vectors of names as in
\prescript{e}{}{\pi}
and the polyadic
\pi
-calculus; or elements of a composite datatype (e.g. the integers).
The datatype of
conditions
, ranged over by
\varphi\in\mathds{C}
contains the conditions that can be used in conditional process expressions.
Finally, and importantly, we have the nominal datatype of
assertions
, ranged over by
\Psi\in\mathds{A}
.
Each of the datatypes
\mathds{T}
,
\mathds{C}
and
\mathds{A}
must include an equivariant
substitution function
, written
\left(\cdot\right)[\widetilde{a}:=\widetilde{M}]
, substituting tuples of terms
\widetilde{M}
for tuples of names
\widetilde{a}
of equal arity.
It must be defined such that it satisfies the following
substitution laws:

1.

If
\widetilde{a}\subseteq\supn\kern-2.0pt\left(X\right)
and
b\in\supn\kern-2.0pt\big(\widetilde{Y}\big)
then
b\in\supn\kern-2.0pt\left(X[\widetilde{a}:=\widetilde{Y}]\right)

2.

If
\widetilde{u}{\syntaxfont{\#}}X,\widetilde{v}
then
X[\widetilde{v}:=\widetilde{Y}]=\big(\left(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\right)\kern-2.0pt\boldsymbol{\cdot}\kern-2.0ptX\big)[\widetilde{u}:=\widetilde{Y}]

The requirements are quite general and should be satisfied by any ordinary definition of substitution: The first law states that names cannot be lost in substitution, i.e. the names present in
\widetilde{Y}
must also be present when the substitution has been performed; whilst the second law states that substitution cannot be affected by transposition.

Since the calculus allows arbitrary terms to be used as channels, any
\Psi
-calculus instance requires a definition of two equivariant operators,
channel equivalence

\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
and
assertion composition

\otimes
, a
unit element

\boldsymbol{1}
of assertions, and an
entailment relation

\Vdash
, defined on the respective nominal datatypes and with the following signatures:

\begin{array}[]{ r @{~} l }\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\;:\mathds{T}\times\mathds{T}\to\mathds{C}&\text{channel equivalence}\\
\otimes:\mathds{A}\times\mathds{A}\to\mathds{A}&\text{assertion composition}\end{array}\hskip 14.22636pt\begin{array}[]{ r @{~} l }\boldsymbol{1}\in\mathds{A}&\text{assertion unit}\\
\Vdash\subseteq\mathds{A}\times\mathds{C}&\text{entailment relation}\end{array}

We write the entailment relation as
\Psi\Vdash\varphi
instead of
(\Psi,\varphi)\in\;\Vdash
to denote that the condition
\varphi
holds, given the assertions
\Psi
.
Note that comparison by channel equivalence
M_{1}\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}M_{2}
is itself a condition, which may or may not be entailed by some assertions
\Psi
, according to the definition of the entailment relation.

The set of HO
\Psi
-calculus
processes

is generated by the formation rules:

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} c @{~} l}P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}&::=&\mathbf{0}~~\big|~~~P_{1}~\syntaxfont{|}~P_{2}~~\big|~~~\overline{M}N.P~~\big|~~~\underline{M}(\lambda\widetilde{x}:\widetilde{T})N.P&~~\big|&{\textbf{run~}M}~~\big|~~~{\textbf{case~}}\widetilde{\varphi}:\widetilde{P}~~\big|~~~\left(\mathbf{\nu}x:T\right)P~~\big|~~~{\syntaxfont{!}}P~~\big|~~~\llparenthesis\Psi\rrparenthesis\end{array}

Most of these constructs are similar to those of the
\pi
-calculus; the input and output prefixes generalise those of the
\pi
-calculus, since here both subject and object are
terms
rather than just names.
Thus

Here,
\widetilde{x}

\widetilde{x}
within the continuation

.
Unlike the presentation in
[
22
]
, we here use a typed version of the language: thus the types of the pattern variables are found in the list
\widetilde{T}
where
|\widetilde{x}|=|\widetilde{T}|
, and likewise, in the restriction
\left(\mathbf{\nu}x:T\right)P
, we annotate the name
x
bound in
P
with its type
T

The selection construct
{\textbf{case~}}\widetilde{\varphi}:\widetilde{P}
is a shorthand for a list of cases and is to be understood as saying: If condition
\varphi_{i}
is entailed by the assertions
\Psi
, we continue as
P_{i}
.
If more than one condition is entailed, the process is chosen non-deterministically.
This construct thus generalises the choice and matching operators of the
\pi
-calculus.

Higher-order communication is handled by representing processes as terms, thus allowing them to be communicated.
We assume the existence of assertions of the form
M\Leftarrow P
.
By writing such an assertion,
M
becomes a
handle
of the process
P
, and we can then send
P
by sending its handle.
Thence
M
may be used to activate the process
P
, and for this we use the only construct that is new to the higher-order setting, the invocation construct
{\textbf{run~}M}
.
Note that the set of processes may itself be included in the set of terms, thus allowing assertions of the form
P\Leftarrow P
whereby a process becomes a handle for itself.

Lastly, an assertion
\llparenthesis\Psi\rrparenthesis
is said to be
guarded
, if it occurs as a subterm of an input or output, and
unguarded
otherwise.
The authors in
[
22
]
impose the restriction that no assertion may occur unguarded in the processes in a conditional expression
{\textbf{case~}}\widetilde{\varphi}:\widetilde{P}
, nor in a replicated process
{\syntaxfont{!}}P
, nor in processes spawned by a
{\textbf{run~}M}
operator.
We say that processes conforming to this criterion are
well-formed
, and we shall only consider well-formed processes in the following.

3
The generic type system

The goal of our generic type system is to be able to instantiate it such that we obtain a sound type system for a given HO
\Psi
-calculus instance.
As in other type systems, we need to describe when processes are well-typed, but since we in the HO
\Psi
-calculus also have terms, conditions and assertions, we shall therefore also need a way to decide when
they
are well-typed.
However, since these nominal datatypes are parameters to the HO
\Psi
-calculus, we cannot specify a set of type rules for them, as we can with processes.
Instead, such rules must likewise be provided as parameters to create an instance of the generic type system, and these rules must then satisfy a number of requirements, here denoted
instance assumptions
, which we shall need in the proof for subject reduction.
We describe them in detail below, in section
3.3
.

3.1
Types and type judgements

Types can contain names, and we assume that the set of types
Types
is a nominal datatype ranged over by
T
; however, we do not allow substitution of terms for names
inside
types.
1
1

1

As we shall see in our examples of instantiations of the type system, a type
T
may itself contain a type environment
\Gamma
, which thus may contain names with type annotations.
By this requirement, we disallow that such names may be substituted for terms.

Furthermore, we need the concept of a type environment
\Gamma
to record the types of free names; thus
\Gamma
is a partial function with finite support
\Gamma:\mathcal{N}\rightharpoonup\textbf{{Types}}
.
We can think of
\Gamma
as a set of tuples
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{N}\times\textbf{{Types}}
where
(x,T)\in\Gamma

3.2
Channel compatibility

When we type an input or output prefix term, the type of the subject
M
and the type of the object (the term transmitted on channel
M
) must be compatible w.r.t. a
compatibility predicate

\looparrowleft
that describes which types of values can be carried by channels of a given types.
Thus,
T_{1}\looparrowleft T_{2}
denotes that channels of type
T_{1}
can carry terms of type
T_{2}
, and we require that the set of types be defined such that this holds.
Furthermore, we distinguish between output compatibility
\looparrowleft^{+}
, and input compatibility
\looparrowleft^{-}
, and we write
T_{1}\looparrowleft T_{2}
if both
T_{1}\looparrowleft^{+}T_{2}
and
T_{1}\looparrowleft^{-}T_{2}
.

As an example, consider the channel types in the sorting system by Milner
[
20
]

Here, a name has type

, if it is a channel that can be used to transmit names of type
T
, so in that case we would therefore require that
\text{ch}(T)\looparrowleft T
.

In our definition of compatibility, we assume given a subtype ordering
\leq
on types.
If
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then a term of
T_{1}
can be used wherever a term of type
T_{2}
is needed.
Thus we require the usual subsumption rule for types, namely that a term of a given type
T_{1}
can also be typed with a supertype
T_{2}
:

[
subsume
]

\dfrac{\Gamma,\Psi\vdash M:T_{1}\quad T_{1}\leq T_{2}}{\Gamma,\Psi\vdash M:T_{2}}

The compatibility predicate for a type
T
must further satisfy the following requirements w.r.t. the subtyping relation:

1.

If a channel type can carry two distinct types, then the types have to be related by the subtype ordering.
That is, if
d\in\{+,-\}
,
T\looparrowleft^{d}T_{1}
and
T\looparrowleft^{d}T_{2}
with
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
, then
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
or
T_{2}\leq T_{1}
.

2.

Output compatibility is
contravariant
.
That is, if
T\looparrowleft^{+}T_{2}
and
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then also
T\looparrowleft^{+}T_{1}
.
This requirement mirrors that of
[
23
]
.
If
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then a term of type
T_{1}
can be used where ever a term of type
T_{2}
is needed, and a channel that outputs terms of the more general type
T_{2}
can therefore be used, where ever a channel of the specialized type
T_{1}
is required.

3.

Input compatibility is
covariant
.
That is, if
T\looparrowleft^{-}T_{1}
and
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, then also
T\looparrowleft^{-}T_{2}
.
This requirement, too, mirrors that of
[
23
]
.
Here, if
T_{1}\leq T_{2}
, a channel that accepts terms of type
T_{1}
can also be used to accept terms of type
T_{2}
.

4
Properties of the generic type system

Type systems normally ensure two properties of well-typed programs: a
subject reduction
property guarantees that a well-typed program remains well-typed under reduction; and a
safety
property ensures that if a program is well-typed then a certain safety predicate holds.
The latter will depend on the particular instance of the type system and must therefore be shown individually, for each instance, but subject reduction can be shown for the generic type system.
We establish this through a series of lemmas, beginning with the usual results of
weakening
and
strengthening
of the type environment:

Lemma 1

(Weakening and strengthening)
.

•

If
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P
then
\Gamma,x:T,\Psi\vdash P

•

If
\Gamma,x:T,\Psi\vdash P
and
x{\syntaxfont{\#}}{P,\Psi}
then

\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P

A similar result holds for assertions.
Any process that is well-typed remains well-typed after a composition of any assertion in the assertion environment, so long as all names in the new assertion environment are in the support of the type environment:

Lemma 2

(Assertion environment weakening)
.

If
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P
,
\names(\Psi^{\prime})\subseteq\dom(\Gamma)
and
\Psi\leq\Psi^{\prime}
then
\Gamma,\Psi^{\prime}\vdash P
.

This lemma is necessitated by the syntax of the HO
\Psi
-calculus itself, which allows guarded assertions in continuations to become unguarded after a reduction.
It is in the proof of this result that the instance assumptions
,
and
become important.

As we here use reduction semantics with an asymmetric evaluation relation to handle unfolding of
case
and
run
expressions, we shall also need two results that describe how frames can evolve during evaluation.
The former, given in Lemma
3
is used in the proof of subject reduction (Theorem
1
) to find any new assertions that may have become composed onto the pre-existing assertion environment after a reduction.
The latter, given in Lemma
4
states that the assertions in a process are unaltered by an evaluation:
This is mainly ensured by the criterion for well-formed processes, asserting that all processes under replication or in a
case
expression, and all processes spawned by a
{\textbf{run~}M}
operator, may not contain unguarded assertions.
The proof then establishes that the property of being assertion-guarded is preserved by the evaluation relation
\mathop{\underline{\ggg}}
.

Lemma 3

(Frame post reduction)
.

If
\Psi\vartriangleright P\rightarrow P^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{F}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\kern-2.0pt\left(P\right)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\kern-2.0pt\left(P^{\prime}\right)

Lemma 4

(Frame post evaluation)
.

If
\Psi\vartriangleright P\mathop{\underline{\ggg}}P^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{F}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\kern-2.0pt\left(P\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\kern-2.0pt\left(P^{\prime}\right)
.

The above lemmas can now be used to prove that a well-typed process remains well-typed after an evaluation:

Lemma 5

(Subject evaluation)
.

If
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P\land\Psi\vartriangleright P\mathop{\underline{\ggg}}P^{\prime}
then
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P^{\prime}
.

Lastly, we need a standard result of
substitution
, which states that a well-typed process remains well-typed after a well-typed substitution.
The proof of this lemma requires the instance assumptions
and
.

Lemma 6

(Subject substitution)
.

If
\Gamma,\widetilde{x}:\widetilde{T},\Psi\vdash P
and
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash\widetilde{L}:\widetilde{T}
then
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P[x:=\widetilde{L}]
.

This, at last, gives us our main result:

Theorem 1

(Subject reduction)
.

If
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P\land\Psi\vartriangleright P\rightarrow P^{\prime}
then
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P^{\prime}
.

Outline.

Induction in the reduction rules.
In many of the cases, the instance assumptions are needed.
An example is that in the case of the
rule, the substitution assumption
is needed to ensure that the substitution of the received message can be well-typed and the weakening assumptions
and
are needed to ensure that the resulting process can be typed within the same type environment as before.
∎

The subject reduction theorem holds for all valid instances of the generic type system.
This is all that we can guarantee in our generic setting, as a notion of
safety
will also depend on a definition of runtime error, which will be specific to each instance.
Safety must therefore be proved individually for each instance.

\Gamma,\Psi\vdash\overline{a}P
, we want
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P:\text{drop}(\Gamma)
.
In this way, when we run the process, we can recall the bound variables and their types at the time when the process was sent.
To implement this, we shall use the (previously unused) assertions
\Psi
as type environments for processes.
Thus we redefine
\mathds{A}
as follows:

\mathds{A}\triangleq\wp\kern-2.0pt\left(\left\{\,P:T~~\big|~~~P\in\mathcal{P}\land T\in\textbf{{Types}}\,\right\}\right)

We can now show safety for the type system instance:

Theorem 2
.

If
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P
then
P\not\rightarrow\textbf{WRONG}
.

The proof is by induction in the rules of
\Gamma,\Psi\vdash P
.
Details are given in
[
3
]
.

5.2
A type system for termination

We now turn our attention to an instance of the generic type system that captures a liveness property.
Demangeon et al.
[
8
]
present a type system for checking termination in variants of the HO
\pi
-calculus: for any well-typed process
P
we have that
P\rightarrow\kern-4.0pt^{*}~P^{\prime}\not\rightarrow
.
These authors study
\textrm{HOpi}_{2}
, a higher-order process calculus in which only processes can be communicated.
The syntax of
\textrm{HOpi}_{2}
is given by the formation rules

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} c @{~} l}P&::=&\mathbf{0}~~\big|~~~a\syntaxfont{(}X\syntaxfont{)}.P~~\big|~~~\overline{a}\syntaxfont{<}Q\syntaxfont{>}.P~~\big|~~~P_{1}~\syntaxfont{|}~P_{2}~~\big|~~~\left(\mathbf{\nu}a:T\right)P~~\big|~~~X\end{array}

In this type system, processes
P
are typed with a type
n
, where
n
is a natural number called the
level
of
P
.
Names
a
have types of the form
\text{ch}^{k}(\diamond)
, where
\diamond
denotes the type of processes and
k
is a natural number, the level of
a
.
This is interpreted as saying that
a
is only used to carry processes whose level
n
is less than
k
.
Type judgements are of the form
\Gamma\vdash P:n
.
The type rules, shown below, ensure that the level of processes that are sent on any channel
a
will be strictly smaller than that of
a
.

\displaystyle{[\textsc{In}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\begin{aligned} \Gamma,X:(k-1)\vdash&P:n\\
\Gamma(a)=\text{ch}^{k}(\diamond)\end{aligned}}{\Gamma\vdash a(X).P:n}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Out}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\begin{aligned} \Gamma\vdash Q:m\quad\Gamma\vdash P:n\\
\Gamma(a)=\text{ch}^{k}(\diamond)\quad m<k\end{aligned}}{\Gamma\vdash\overline{a}\syntaxfont{<}Q\syntaxfont{>}.P:\max(k,n)}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Nil}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{}{\Gamma\vdash\mathbf{0}:0}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{New}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma,a:\text{ch}^{k}(\diamond)\vdash P:n}{\Gamma\vdash\left(\mathbf{\nu}a:T\right)P:n}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Par}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma\vdash P:m\quad\Gamma\vdash Q:n}{\Gamma\vdash P~\syntaxfont{|}~Q:\max(m,n)}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Var}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma(X)=n}{\Gamma\vdash X:n}

It is straightforward to represent the
\textrm{HOpi}_{2}
calculus as an instance of the Higher-Order
\Psi
-calculus, using a variant of the parameter setting described in section
5.1
.
In order to represent the type system, we introduce assertions of the form

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} c @{~} l}\Psi&::=&n~~\big|~~~n^{-}~~\big|~~~n^{+}\end{array}

We use assertions to indicate in which way a channel is to be used; an input use can only be typed in the presence of an assertion
n^{-}
and output use must be used with an assertion
n^{+}
.
We have that
n\otimes n^{-}=n^{-}\otimes n=n
; that
n\otimes n^{+}=n^{+}\otimes n=n
; and that
n_{1}\otimes n_{2}=\max(n_{1},n_{2})
.
We distinguish explicitly between input uses (
\text{ch}^{k}_{-}(\diamond)
) and output uses (
\text{ch}^{k}_{+}(\diamond)
) of channels:

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} c @{~} l}T\in\textbf{{Types}}&::=&n~~\big|~~~\text{ch}^{k}_{-}(\diamond)~~\big|~~~\text{ch}^{k}_{+}(\diamond)\end{array}

and we let
\text{ch}^{n}(\diamond)\curvearrowleft(n-1)
and
\text{ch}^{n}(\diamond)\curvearrowleft k
whenever
k<n
.
Type judgements are of the form
\Gamma,m\vdash M:T
for terms and
\Gamma,m\vdash P
for processes.
We represent the judgement
\Gamma\vdash P:n
as
\Gamma,n\vdash P
.
The type rules for channels are thus:

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Ch-in}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma(a)=\text{ch}^{k}_{-}(\diamond)}{\Gamma,n^{-}\vdash a:\text{ch}^{k}_{-}(\diamond)}

\displaystyle{[\textsc{Ch-out}]}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma(a)=\text{ch}^{k}_{+}(\diamond)}{\Gamma,n^{+}\vdash a:\text{ch}^{k}_{+}(\diamond)}

5.3
The
\rho
-calculus

The Reflective Higher-Order calculus of Meredith and Radestock
[
19
]
is less well-known than e.g. CHOCS and HO
\pi
, so we recall it in some detail.
Unlike other calculi, the
\rho
-calculus does not assume an infinite set of names: instead, names and processes are both built from the same syntax, so names are structured terms, rather than atomic entities.
The syntax for both processes and names is given by the formation rules:

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} c @{~} l}P&::=&\mathbf{0}~~\big|~~~P~\syntaxfont{|}~P~~\big|~~~x\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|P\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle~~\big|~~~x\syntaxfont{(}y\syntaxfont{)}.P~~\big|~~~\urcorner x\ulcorner x,y&::=&\ulcorner P\urcorner\end{array}

where the syntax for names is simply
\ulcorner P\urcorner
, pronounced
quote
P
.
Names can be passed around as in the
\pi
-calculus, as well as un-quoted (called
drop
), and thus higher-order behaviour becomes an inherent property of the calculus, rather than just an extension on top of an already computationally complete language.

The parallel, and the input construct
x\syntaxfont{(}y\syntaxfont{)}.P
, are similar to their
\pi
-calculus counterparts.
The
lift
operation,
x\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|P\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle
is an output construct that quotes the process
P
, thereby creating the
name

\ulcorner P\urcorner
, and sends it out on
x
; thus the calculus can generate new names at runtime without the need of a
\nu
-operator.
The converse of lift is the
drop
operation,
\urcorner x\ulcorner
: it is a request to run the process within a name, by removing the quotes around it.
This is not performed by a reduction, but rather by a form of substitution

\urcorner x\ulcorner\left\{\ulcorner P\urcorner/x^{\prime}\right\}=P\qquad\text{if }x\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}x^{\prime}

where the entire
process

\urcorner x\ulcorner
is replaced with the process
P
found within the substituted name, similar to how process variables are replaced by processes in e.g. HO
\pi
.
Notably, this means that if
x
is a
free
name, then
\urcorner x\ulcorner
will be a
deadlock
, since
x
can never be touched by a substitution at runtime.
Otherwise, substitution is the standard, capture-avoiding substitution of names for names, and note in particular that substitution does
not
recur into processes under quotes; i.e.
\ulcorner P\urcorner\left\{x/y\right\}=\ulcorner P\urcorner
if
y\not\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}\ulcorner P\urcorner
regardless of whether the name
y
exists somewhere within
\ulcorner P\urcorner
.

The reduction semantics is given by the standard rules for parallel composition and structural congruence (as in e.g. the
\pi
-calculus) plus the following rule for communication:

[
\rho
-com
]

\dfrac{x_{1}\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}x_{2}}{x_{1}\syntaxfont{(}y\syntaxfont{)}.P~\syntaxfont{|}~x_{2}\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|Q\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle\rightarrow P\left\{\ulcorner Q\urcorner/y\right\}}

One subtlety of this calculus concerns the notion of structural congruence,
\equiv
.
It is the usual least congruence on processes, containing
\alpha
-equivalence,
\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\alpha}
, and the abelian monoid rules for parallel composition with
\mathbf{0}
as the unit element.
However, with
structured
terms as names,
\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\alpha}
in turn requires a notion of
name equivalence
, written
\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}
, that is also used for comparing subjects in the
rule above.
It is defined as the smallest equivalence relation on
quoted
processes, closed forward under the rules:

[
\rho
-Nameeq
1
]

\dfrac{P\equiv Q}{\ulcorner P\urcorner\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}\ulcorner Q\urcorner}

[
\rho
-Nameeq
2
]

\dfrac{}{\ulcorner\urcorner x\ulcorner\urcorner\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}x}

This yields a mutual recursion between name equivalence, structural congruence and
\alpha
-equivalence, albeit one that always terminates as proved in
[
19
]
, because both the sets of names and processes are well-founded; their smallest elements being
\mathbf{0}
(the inactive process) and
\ulcorner\mathbf{0}\urcorner
respectively.

5.3.1
Instantiation as a
\Psi
-calculus

The
\rho
-calculus is interesting in the present setting, because it cannot be encoded in the
\pi
-calculus in a way that satisfies a number of generally accepted criteria of encodability, similar to those of
[
11
]
.
This has been established by one of the authors in
[
18
]
.

The key reason for this impossibility lies in the ability of the
\rho
-calculus to generate new,
free
, and hence observable, names at runtime, whilst this is not possible in the
\pi
-calculus; and, dually, its use of name equivalence, which will equate more names than strict syntactic equality.
However, the
\rho
-calculus
can
be represented in the HO
\Psi
-framework as follows.
We define

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} l}\mathds{T}&\triangleq\mathcal{N}\cup\left\{\,\ulcorner P\urcorner\;\mid\;P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\,\right\}\cup\left\{\,\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle\;\mid\;P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\,\right\}\\
\mathds{C}&\triangleq\big\{\,M\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}N\;\mid\;M,N\in\mathds{T}\,\big\}\cup\;\left\{\,P_{1}\equiv P_{2}\;\mid\;P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\,\right\}\\
&\cup\;\left\{\,M\Leftarrow P\;\mid\;M\in\mathds{T}\land P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\,\right\}\end{array}

and (initially) with
\mathds{A}\triangleq\left\{\,\emptyset\,\right\}
,
\otimes\triangleq\cup
and
\boldsymbol{1}\triangleq\emptyset
as before.
Note the two different kinds of terms: we use terms of the form
\ulcorner P\urcorner
to represent a
statically
quoted name in the
\rho
-calculus, which can never be dropped and never substituted into.
Conversely, we use
\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle
for the equivalent of the object of a
x\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|P\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle
, which in the
\rho
-calculus is a
process
that therefore
can
be substituted into, and which later may be dropped.
Furthermore, we shall assume that all
bound names
are implemented as distinct atomic names
x\in\mathcal{N}
; this is a trivial conversion, since their structure has no semantic meaning in the
\rho
-calculus.
The encoding is then given by the translation:

\begin{array}[]{r @{~} l}\llbracket\mathbf{0}\rrbracket&=\mathbf{0}\\
\llbracket P_{1}~\syntaxfont{|}~P_{2}\rrbracket&=\llbracket P_{1}\rrbracket~\syntaxfont{|}~\llbracket P_{2}\rrbracket\\
\llbracket{n\syntaxfont{(}x\syntaxfont{)}.P}\rrbracket&=\underline{\llbracket n\rrbracket}(\lambda x)\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|x\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.\llbracket P\rrbracket\\
\llbracket n\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|P\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle\rrbracket&=\overline{\llbracket n\rrbracket}\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.\mathbf{0}\end{array}\hskip 28.45274pt\begin{array}[]{r @{~} l}\llbracket\urcorner x\ulcorner\rrbracket&={\textbf{run~}x}\\
\llbracket\urcorner\ulcorner P\urcorner\ulcorner\rrbracket&=\mathbf{0}\\
\llbracket\ulcorner P\urcorner\rrbracket&=\ulcorner\mathcal{N}\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner\\
\llbracket x\rrbracket&=x\end{array}

where
\mathcal{N}\llbracket P\rrbracket
is similar to
\llbracket P\rrbracket

except
that
\mathcal{N}\llbracket\urcorner\ulcorner P\urcorner\ulcorner\rrbracket={\textbf{run~}\ulcorner\mathcal{N}\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner}
.

Note the two translations of drop for processes: the process
\urcorner\ulcorner P\urcorner\ulcorner
has no reduction in the
\rho
-calculus and is therefore behaviourally equivalent to
\mathbf{0}
; but its counterpart
{\textbf{run~}\ulcorner P\urcorner}

might
have a reduction, since
{\textbf{run~}M}
is not evaluated eagerly in the HO
\Psi
-calculus.
For the purpose of preserving operational correspondence, we therefore translate the drop of a
free name

\ulcorner P\urcorner
as
\mathbf{0}
, and the drop of an atomic name
x
as
{\textbf{run~}x}
, since atomic names are bound by construction.
However, we cannot do this within
names
, since name equivalence is determined by the structure, rather than the behaviour of the process within quotes.
Thus we use the second level translation
\mathcal{N}\llbracket P\rrbracket
for statically quoted names, since these can never be dropped.

Lastly, we shall define entailment such that it contains the rule
\Psi\Vdash\ulcorner P\urcorner\Leftarrow P
, making every term
\ulcorner P\urcorner
a handle for the process
P
within, to mirror the duality of names and processes in the
\rho
-calculus.
We furthermore include the following rules for entailment of
channel equivalence

\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
, mirroring the rules
and
for concluding name equivalence:

[
Chaneq
1
]

\dfrac{}{\Psi\Vdash\ulcorner{\textbf{run~}M}\urcorner\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}M}

[
Chaneq
2
]

\dfrac{\Psi\Vdash P_{1}\equiv P_{2}}{\Psi\Vdash\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\ulcorner P_{2}\urcorner}

including the symmetric and transitive closure of
\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}
.
We then let the entailment relation for conditions of
structural congruence

\equiv
be defined such that
\equiv
contains
\alpha
-equivalence; that
(\mathcal{P}_{/\equiv},~\syntaxfont{|}~,\mathbf{0})
is an abelian monoid; and containing the four congruence rules derived from the above translation:

[
Par
]

\dfrac{\Psi\Vdash P_{1}\equiv P_{2}}{\Psi\Vdash P_{1}~\syntaxfont{|}~R\equiv P_{2}~\syntaxfont{|}~R}

[
In
]

\dfrac{\Psi\Vdash M_{1}\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}M_{2}\qquad\Psi\Vdash P_{1}\equiv P_{2}}{\Psi\Vdash\underline{M_{1}}(\lambda x_{1})\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|x_{1}\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.P_{1}\equiv\underline{M_{2}}(\lambda x_{2})\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|x_{2}\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.P_{2}}

[
Run
]

\dfrac{\Psi\Vdash M_{1}\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}M_{2}}{\Psi\Vdash{\textbf{run~}M_{1}}\equiv{\textbf{run~}M_{2}}}

[
Out

\dfrac{\Psi\Vdash M_{1}\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}M_{2}\qquad\Psi\Vdash P_{1}\equiv P_{2}}{\Psi\Vdash\overline{M_{1}}\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P_{1}\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle\equiv\overline{M_{2}}\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P_{2}\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle}

\simeq

Theorem 3

(Operational correspondence)
.

Let
\simeq
be a notion of behavioural equivalence for processes of the

\Psi
-instance of the
\rho
-calculus, that includes at least structural congruence and the axiom
{\textbf{run~}\ulcorner\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner}\simeq\llbracket P\rrbracket
.

P\rightarrow P^{\prime}\iff\llbracket P\rrbracket\rightarrow\simeq\llbracket P^{\prime}\rrbracket
.

Outline.

The proof requires a number of steps.
First we show that the translation preserves name equivalence; i.e.
x_{1}\equiv_{\kern-2.0pt\mathcal{N}}x_{2}\iff\boldsymbol{1}\Vdash\llbracket x_{1}\rrbracket\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}\llbracket x_{2}\rrbracket

Then we show that substitution can be moved out of the translation; i.e.
\llbracket P\sigma\rrbracket\simeq\llbracket P\rrbracket\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket

\boldsymbol{1}\Vdash\llbracket(n)\sigma\rrbracket\xleftrightarrow{\kern-1.5pt\mathchoice{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\raisebox{-2.1097pt}{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}(\llbracket n\rrbracket)\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket

and
\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket\triangleq[\llbracket x\rrbracket:=\llbracket\ulcorner Q\urcorner\rrbracket]
, by induction in the clauses of the translation function.
This step relies on our assumption about
\simeq
.
Lastly we can show soundness and completeness w.r.t. operational correspondence by induction in the two semantics.
In both cases, the interesting clauses are the communication rules, which require the aforementioned substitution and name equivalence preservation results.
Details are available in
[
3
]
.

5.3.2
A type system for reflection

Other higher-order calculi such as CHOCS and HO
\pi
can be encoded in the
\pi
-calculus and may thus be typable through translation, but as we noted above there cannot be such an encoding of the
\rho
-calculus into the
\pi
-calculus.
Thus, we cannot hope to create a type system for the
\rho
-calculus by adapting an existing first-order type system.
In fact, we are not aware of any type system for the
\rho
-calculus, so we shall now create one by instantiating our generic type system.
We let types for names be of the form

T\in\textbf{{Types}}::=\left<T,\Gamma\right>~~\big|~~~\left<B,\Gamma\right>

where
B
is a basis type, and
\Gamma
is a type environment representing the possibility of executing the process within the name.
Furthermore we shall use assertions as type environments for processes
as we previously did with HO
\pi
, so we update the definition
accordingly.

\displaystyle\mathds{A}

\displaystyle\triangleq\wp\kern-2.0pt\left(\left\{\,\ulcorner P\urcorner:T\mid P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\land T\in\textbf{{Types}}\,\right\}\cup\left\{\,\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle:T\mid P\in\mathcal{P}_{\kern-2.0pt\Psi}\land T\in\textbf{{Types}}\,\right\}\right)

with assertion unit and composition as
\boldsymbol{1}\triangleq\emptyset
and
\otimes\triangleq\cup
respectively.
Note that by construction
\forall x\in\mathcal{N}.x{\syntaxfont{\#}}{\ulcorner P\urcorner}
, so substitution can only occur in terms of the form
\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner P\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle:T
.
We then append an assertion to the encoding of input and output:

\displaystyle\llbracket\ulcorner R\urcorner\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|P\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle\rrbracket

\displaystyle\triangleq\overline{\ulcorner\llbracket R\rrbracket\urcorner}\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.\mathbf{0}~\syntaxfont{|}~\llparenthesis\left\{\,\ulcorner\llbracket R\rrbracket\urcorner:T,\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|\ulcorner\llbracket P\rrbracket\urcorner\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle:T^{\prime}\,\right\}\rrparenthesis

\displaystyle\llbracket{\ulcorner R\urcorner\syntaxfont{(}x\syntaxfont{)}.P}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\triangleq\underline{\ulcorner\llbracket R\rrbracket\urcorner}(\lambda x)\left\langle\kern-2.0pt\left|x\right|\kern-2.0pt\right\rangle.\llbracket P\rrbracket~\syntaxfont{|}~\llparenthesis\left\{\,\ulcorner\llbracket R\rrbracket\urcorner:T\,\right\}\rrparenthesis

Lastly, we also need to take the type information into account when concluding channel equivalence, to ensure that two terms with initially dissimilar types cannot become channel equivalent after a substitution.
Thus we redefine the entailment rule
as follows:

6
Conclusions and future work

We have presented a generic type system for higher-order
\Psi
-calculi, which extends a previous type system for first-order
\Psi
-calculi.
Like its predecessor, type judgements for processes are of the form
\Gamma\vdash P
and are given by a fixed set of rules.
Terms, assertions and conditions are assumed to form nominal datatypes, and only a few requirements on type rules are imposed.

The generic type system allows us to identify what should be required of type systems for higher-order process calculi that are instances of the
\Psi
-calculus; these requirements take the form of instance assumptions.
Thus it may also yield important insights into the general structure of type systems for higher-order calculi, and it may therefore also be taken as a starting point for developing more advanced type systems for any language that can be shown to be an instance of higher-order
\Psi
-calculi.

Our type system satisfies a general subject-reduction property and can be instantiated to yield type systems with a notion of channel safety for higher-order calculi such as CHOCS, HO
\pi
and also the
\rho
-calculus.
The latter in particular is interesting, as there is no valid encoding of the
\rho
-calculus into the
\pi
-calculus, and thus we cannot capture higher-order typability in a purely first-order setting.
This establishes that our generic type system is richer than first-order type systems.
However, typability in the
\rho
-calculus comes at the cost of necessitating that we include type information directly in the definition of channel equivalence.
This amounts to saying that the programmer must know (and specify) in advance the type of all names that will be generated during the course of program evaluation.
We do not know whether it is possible to create other (non-trivial) type systems for the
\rho
-calculus without such a restriction.

There are two important lines of future work in this direction:
In

[
15

he also considers
session types
for psi-calculi.
Both of these extensions are formulated for first-order
\Psi
-calculi only, and they would therefore be relevant to also consider in the higher-order setting.
