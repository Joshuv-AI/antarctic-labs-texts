Asymptotics of non-local perimeters Thanks: Research supported by National Science Centre (Poland), grant no. 2019/33/B/ST1/02494
Wojciech Cygan, Tomasz Grzywny

Abstract
. We introduce a notion of non-local perimeter which is defined through an arbitrary positive Borel measure on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} which integrates the function 1 ∧ | x | 1\wedge|x| . Such definition of non-local perimeter encompasses a wide range of perimeters which have been already studied in the literature, including fractional perimeters and anisotropic fractional perimeters. The main part of the article is devoted to the study of the asymptotic behaviour of non-local perimeters. As direct applications we recover well-known convergence results for fractional perimeters and anisotropic fractional perimeters

1.
Introduction

The concept of non-local perimeter of a given Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure which corresponds to the fractional Laplacian was proposed in
[
7
]
. This is the so-called
\alpha
-perimeter and it is defined via the following integral formula

(1.1)

\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E)=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
0<\alpha<1
and
E^{c}
is the complement of
E
. By
|x|
we denote the Euclidean norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This object is strongly related to the fractional Sobolev norm and it has been intensively studied over last years, see
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,

[
18
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
39
]
, and
[
26
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. We also refer to
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
for the case of fractional norms related to Feller generators.

The main motivation for the present article was an another interesting variant of non-local perimeter which is defined through a given non-singular kernel. More precisely, if
J\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a radially symmetric and integrable function then the corresponding
J
-perimeter of a set
E
is given by

(1.2)

\mathrm{Per}_{J}(E)=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}J(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

In
[
31
]
the authors established basic properties and convergence results for
J
-perimeters, see also
[
32
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
36
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
35
]
.
For a treatment on a unified framework for non-local perimeters and curvatures we refer to
[
14
]
.

Our principal goal is to introduce a notion of non-local perimeter which is defined with the aid of a given positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If we look at (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) from a probabilistic perspective then it is evident that the
\alpha
-perimeter is linked with an
\alpha
-stable Lévy process while
J
-perimeter is associated with a compound Poisson process. We thus aim to develop a unified approach that encompasses the both concepts as special cases. We emphasize that our methods are, however, purely analytical.

Before we define the object of our study we recall the definition of the classical perimeter.
The perimeter of a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be defined in the variational language as the total mass of the total variation measure of the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{E}
.
More precisely, the distributional gradient
Du
of a function
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a vector-valued Radon measure and its total variation is a positive measure defined as

\displaystyle|Du|=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)\,\mathrm{div}\,v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x:\,v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|v\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}.

The perimeter of
E
is then given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}(E)=|D\mathbf{1}_{E}|(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

It is known that for a set
E
of finite Lebesgue measure,
\mathrm{Per}(E)
is finite if, and only if,
\mathbf{1}_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where the
space of functions of bounded variations is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\,|Du|(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty\}.

The space
\mathrm{BV}
is endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{\mathrm{BV}}=\|u\|_{L^{1}}+|Du|
and it holds
W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Our main reference for functions of bounded variation is
[
3
]
.

For any positive Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

(1.4)

\displaystyle\int(1\wedge|x|)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\nu(\{0\})=0

we consider
the corresponding non-local
\nu
-perimeter of a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}-x}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

It was recently observed in
[
16
]
that
such perimeters appear as limit objects in the asymptotics of the heat content related to Lévy processes of bounded variation. It was proved in
[
16
, Lemma 1]
that
for a set
E
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter,
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)
is finite as well.
To the non-local
\nu
-perimeter we attach the space

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x+y)-u(y)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\}.

It is equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}}=\|u\|_{L^{1}}+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)
,
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x+y)-u(y)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y.

In Section
2
we show that
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Banach space and
\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For sets of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter their
\nu
-perimeter can be computed through the formula
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)=\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\mathbf{1}_{E})
. Furthermore, we observe that for the
\nu
-perimeter a version of isoperimetric inequality holds in the case when the measure
\nu
is given by a radially increasing kernel. We also find a co-area formula for the
\nu
-perimeter.

There has been a vivid interest in asymptotic behaviour and convergence results for fractional perimeters in recent years.
The asymptotics as
\alpha\uparrow 1
were found in
[
37
]
(see also
[
6
]
and
[
17
]
; and recent paper
[
13
]
for second order asymptotics). In this case the following convergence holds for any set
E
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E)=\frac{K_{1,d}}{2}\,\mathrm{Per}(E),

where

(1.7)

\displaystyle K_{1,d}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|e\cdot\theta|\sigma(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad|e|=1.

Here,
\sigma

stands for the usual surface measure on the unit sphere.
One can show (see e.g.
[
27
]
) that
K_{1,d}=2\varpi_{d-1}
, where

\varpi_{d}=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)

is the Lebesgue measure of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
On the other hand, if
\alpha\downarrow 0
, the asymptotic result was found in
[
33
]
(see also
[
18
]
for a more detailed treatment) and it asserts that for any bounded set
E
of finite perimeter,

(1.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\alpha\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E)=\kappa_{d-1}|E|,

where
|E|
stands for the Lebesgue measure of
E
and
\kappa_{d-1}=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=d\varpi_{d}
. For
J
-perimeters it was found in
[
31
]
that under the assumption that
J
has compact support and for bounded sets
E
of finite perimeter the following convergence holds

(1.9)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{J_{\varepsilon}}(E)=C_{J}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}(E),

where
J_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}J(x/\varepsilon)
and
C_{J}=2(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(x)|x_{d}|\,\mathrm{d}x)^{-1}
(here
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
).

In order to obtain asymptotics of non-local
\nu
-perimeters we first extend
[
37
, Theorem 2]
to the current setting (see Theorem
3.1
) and with this result at hand we show that, for any family of measures
\{\nu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfying (
1.4
) and
such that the mass of normalized measures
(1\wedge|x|)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)
concentrates at zero, there is a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}
converging to zero such that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}C_{\varepsilon_{j}}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(E)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\mathbf{1}_{E}\cdot\theta|\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Here,
C_{\varepsilon_{j}}
are normalizing constants and
\mu
is a probability measure on the unit sphere which is constructed through the family
\{\nu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
, see Section
3
for details. Clearly, if the limit measure
\mu
happens to be the (normalized) uniform measure on the unit sphere then the right-hand side of (
1.10
) is equal to the right-hand side of (
1.6
) divided by
\kappa_{d-1}
.
It turns out that this approach applies to fractional perimeters and
J
-perimeters and we not only recover results in (
1.6
) and (
1.9
) but we also abandon the assumption that
J
is compactly supported.

A fruitful observation in this context is the fact that the non-local perimeter of
E
can be represented with the aid of the so-called covariance function related to the set
E
, see (
3.16
). This enables us to
investigate the case when the mass of the normalized measures
(1\wedge|x|)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)
concentrates at infinity and as an application we recover (
1.8
). We also find a corresponding result for
J
-perimeters. We show that under the assumption that the function
\ell(s)=\int_{|x|>s}J(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is slowly varying at zero, for any set
E
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\ell(\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\widetilde{J}_{\varepsilon}}(E)=|E|,

where
\widetilde{J}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{d}J(\varepsilon x)
. Recall that
\ell
is slowly varying at zero if
\lim_{s\to 0}\ell(\lambda s)/\ell(s)=1
, for
\lambda>0
, see
[
5
]
.

The notion of non-local
\nu
-perimeter can be also successfully exploited in the framework of anisotropic perimeters.
Anisotropic perimeter related to a given convex body is a natural generalization of the classical perimeter and it is defined via a norm whose unit ball is equal to a given convex body, see
[
21
]
and references therein.
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex compact set of non-empty interior (so-called convex body) and such that it is origin-symmetric. Let
\|\cdot\|_{K}
denote the unique norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit ball equal to
K
, that is

\displaystyle\|x\|_{K}=\inf\{\lambda>0:\lambda^{-1}x\in K\},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let
K^{*}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sup_{x\in K}y\cdot x\leq 1\}
be the polar body of
K
. Anisotropic perimeter of a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to
K
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}(E,K)=\int_{\partial^{*}E}\|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\|_{K^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Here
\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)
denotes the measure theoretic outer unit normal vector of
E
at
x\in\partial^{*}E
, where
\partial^{*}E
is the reduced boundary of
E
, see
[
3
, Section 3.5]
.
Similarly as the classical perimeter is linked with Sobolev norm, the anisotropic perimeter is related to anisotropic Sobolev (semi)norms which have been intensively studied, see
[
1
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
34
, Appendix by M. Gromov]
,
[
29
]
and
[
30
]
.

There also exists a fractional counterpart of the anisotropic perimeter. For
0<\alpha<1
, the anisotropic
\alpha
-perimeter of
E
with respect to
K
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}}\frac{1}{\|x-y\|^{d+\alpha}_{K}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

In
[
28
]
it was proved that for any bounded set of finite perimeter the following results hold

(1.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}(1-\alpha)\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=\mathrm{Per}(E,ZK)

and

(1.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\alpha\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=d|K||E|,

where
ZK
is the so-called moment body of
K
, see (
3.10
) for the definition.
Through the methods of the present paper we are able to recover convergence in (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) and show that they are actually valid for all sets of finite measure and of finite perimeter. Similarly, we establish the corresponding convergence of anisotropic Sobolev norms given in
[
28
, Theorem 8]
and show that the assumption of compact support is superfluous.

2.
Basic properties of the non-local perimeter

In this section we establish a few essential properties of the non-local perimeter such as isoperimetric inequality and co-area formula. We start with the following symmetry property.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)=\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E^{c}).

Proof.

Indeed, by Fubini’s theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\int_{E^{c}-x}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\int\mathbf{1}_{E}(x)\mathbf{1}_{E^{c}-y}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int|E\cap(E-y)^{c}|\nu(dy).

Since
|E\cap(E-y)|=|E\cap(E+y)|
, we have

|E\cap(E-y)^{c}|=|E|-|E\cap(E-y)|=|E\cap(E+y)^{c}|=|E^{c}\cap(E-y)|.

Hence,
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)=\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E^{c})
, as desired.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

(1) Lemma
2.1
evidently does not hold if
|E|=\infty
. Indeed, the equality
|E\cap(E-y)^{c}|=|E\cap(E+y)^{c}|
fails already for
(0,\infty)
.

(2) Without loss of generality we could assume that the measure
\nu
appearing in the definition of the
\nu
-perimeter is symmetric in the sense that
\nu(A)=\nu(-A)
, for any Borel set
A
. This follows from the fact that
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(A)=\mathrm{Per}_{\widetilde{\nu}}(A)
, where
\widetilde{\nu}(A)=\frac{1}{2}(\nu(A)+\nu(-A))
.

The following result is an isoperimetric inequality for the non-local perimeter in the case when the measure
\nu
is given by a radial kernel
j
. We emphasize that the function
j
does not have to be integrable. Recall that for a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
|A|
its Lebesgue measure. We omit the proof as it is the same as that of
[
12
, Proposition 3.1]
, see also
[
31
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Proposition 2.3

(Isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=j(x)\mathrm{d}x
where
j\geq 0
is a radially non-increasing function. Then for any
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)\geq\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(B),

where
B
is an open ball centred at the origin such that
|B|=|E|
.

We further claim that under condition (
1.4
) the space
\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is contained in
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In particular, by Lemma
2.1
we easily obtain that

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\mathbf{1}_{E})=\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E),

for any
E
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter.
For any vector-valued measure
\Lambda
we use notation
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Lambda=\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For any
u\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds

(i)

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)\leq C_{\nu}\|u\|_{\mathrm{BV}}
, where
C_{\nu}=\int(1\wedge|x|)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
.

(ii)

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)\leq\tilde{C}_{\nu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Du|
, where
\tilde{C}_{\nu}=\int|x|\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\infty
.

Proof.

It is known
[
3
, Thm. 3.9]
that for any
u\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists a sequence
u_{n}\in C^{\infty}\cap W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle u_{n}\to u\ \mathrm{in}\ L^{1}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u_{n}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Du|\,.

We prove that for sequence
u_{n}
it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u_{n})=\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u).

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x+y)-u_{n}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq|x|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}|\nabla u_{n}(y+tx)|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}y

(2.1)

\displaystyle=|x|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u_{n}(w)|\,\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C|x|\,|Du|.

Further, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x+y)-u_{n}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}}.

Thus, we may apply the dominated convergence theorem to arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x+y)-u_{n}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x+y)-u(y)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,dy=\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u).

Hence, it suffices to operate on
u\in C^{\infty}\cap W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then inequality (i) follows easily by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). Similarly, (ii) is a consequence of (
2.1
).
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
|E|<\infty
and
\mathrm{Per}(E)<\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)\leq C_{\nu}(\mathrm{Per}(E)+|E|).

We next show for completeness that
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a Banach space.

Lemma 2.6
.

The space
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}}=\|u\|_{L^{1}}+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)
is a Banach space.

Proof.

The function
u\mapsto\|u\|_{\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}}
is evidently a norm and thus we only need to show completeness. Let
\{u_{n}\}
be a Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
u
be its
L^{1}
limit. By Fatou’s lemma we have

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x+y)-u_{n}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{n}(x+y)-u_{n}(x)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x=2\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u_{n})

and as the sequence
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded in
\mathrm{BV}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we infer the result.
∎

Proposition 2.7

(Co-area formula)
.

For
u\in L^{1}
we set
S_{t}(u)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,u(x)>t\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(S_{t}(u))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{-\infty}^{0}(1-\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(x))\,\mathrm{d}t,

we have

\displaystyle|u(x)-u(y)|=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(y)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thus, by Tonelli’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(x+y)-\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(y)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}t.

In view of Lemma
2.1
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(x+y)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{1}_{S_{t}(u)}(y)|\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{t}(u)}\int_{S_{t}(u)^{c}-y}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{S_{t}(u)^{c}}\int_{S_{t}(u)-y}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(S_{t}(u))+\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(S_{t}(u)^{c})=2\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(S_{t}(u))

and the result follows.
∎

3.
Asymptotics of the non-local perimeter

In this section we show that the non-local perimeter which we introduce in the present article converge towards the classical perimeter (or to the Lebesgue measure) if we use an appropriate scaling procedure. We then apply our results to establish convergence of fractional perimeters,
J
-perimeters and anisotropic fractional perimeters.

3.1.
Convergence towards the classical perimeter

We first formulate an approximation result in the space of functions of bounded variation which can be seen as a generalization of the result by Ponce
[
37
]
, see also
[
17
]
and
[
6
]
. By
B_{R}
we denote the closed ball centred at zero and of radius
R>0
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\{\lambda\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\{0\})=0
. Suppose that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})=0,\quad\mathrm{for\ any}\ R>0.

Further, let
\mu_{\varepsilon}
be a probability measure on the unit sphere given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}(E)=\lambda_{\varepsilon}((0,\infty)E),\quad E\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

where
(0,\infty)E=\{re:\,r>0\ \text{and}\ e\in E\}
is a cone determined by
E
.
Then there exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}
converging to zero such that for any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathrm{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{R}^{d}}\frac{\left|f(x+y)-f(x)\right|}{|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),

where
\mu
is a probability measure on the unit sphere which is equal to the weak limit of the sequence
\{\mu_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}
.

The proof is postponed to the Appendix given in Section
4
.

Remark 3.2
.

In many applications we are able to conclude the weak convergence of the whole sequence
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}
towards the limit measure
\mu
. In such cases convergence in (
3.3
) holds for
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and not only along a subsequence. This fact follows directly from the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We apply Theorem
3.1
in the case when the probability measure
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
is absolutely continuous with respect to a Lévy measure. We start by giving a general result and then present a few examples.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\{\nu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of Lévy measures satisfying (
1.4
) and let

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\left(R_{\varepsilon}\wedge|x|\right)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x),

where
C_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(R_{\varepsilon}\wedge|x|\right)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)
and
R_{\varepsilon}\in[1,\infty]
.
Let
\mu_{\varepsilon}
be the corresponding probability measure on the unit sphere defined in (
3.2
).
Under condition (
3.1
), there exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}
converging to zero such that
for any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}C_{\varepsilon_{j}}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),

where
\mu
is the weak limit of the sequence
\{\mu_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}
.

Proof.

We split the integral as follows

(3.5)

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon_{j}}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}^{c}}\right)\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}\wedge|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}x.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}x=\!\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!-\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}^{c}}\right)\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}x

and the first integral converges by Theorem
3.1
to (two times) the right hand side of (
3.4
) while the second integral converges to zero as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}^{c}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{1}}\frac{\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(B_{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}^{c})}{R_{\varepsilon_{j}}}\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{1}}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(B_{1}^{c})\to 0.

The second integral in (
3.5
) is negligible by the argument from the previous line.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

In the notation of Theorem
3.3
, let
f=\mathbf{1}_{E}

where
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter (i.e.
\mathbf{1}_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). Then

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}C_{\varepsilon_{j}}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(E)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\mathbf{1}_{E}\cdot\theta|\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

In particular, if
\mu
is the (normalized) uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
then

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}C_{\varepsilon_{j}}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon_{j}}}(E)=\frac{K_{1,d}}{2\kappa_{d-1}}\mathrm{Per}(E),

where
K_{1,d}
is the constant from (
1.7
).

We next illustrate Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
by a few examples.
We start with the following result which is an application of Theorem
3.3
to stable Lévy measures.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\nu_{\alpha}
be an
\alpha
-stable Lévy measure with its spectral decomposition given by

(3.6)

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(A)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(r\theta)r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r\,\eta(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\alpha\in(0,1),

where
\eta
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then, for any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}(1-\alpha)\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta),

where
\mu
is the measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
constructed according to Theorem
3.3
. In particular, if the measure
\eta
is uniform on the unit sphere then,
for any set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure and such that
\mathrm{Per}(E)<\infty
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)=\frac{K_{1,d}}{2\kappa_{d-1}}\mathrm{Per}(E).

Proof.

We aim to apply Theorem
3.3
. We define measures
\lambda_{\alpha}
as follows

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}(A)=C_{\alpha}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge r)\mathbf{1}_{A}(r\theta)r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r\,\eta(\theta),\quad C_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge r)r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r.

We first observe that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{1}r^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sim\frac{1}{1-\alpha},\quad\alpha\uparrow 1.

Further, for any
R>0
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}(B_{R}^{c})=C_{\alpha}^{-1}\cdot\begin{cases}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{\alpha}R^{-\alpha},\quad R>1;\\
\int_{R}^{1}r^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r+\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}(1-R^{-\alpha+1})+\frac{1}{\alpha},\quad R\leq 1.\end{cases}

We easily infer that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\lambda_{\alpha}(B_{R}^{c})=0,\quad R>0.

The measures
\mu_{\alpha}
are given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(E)=\eta(E),\quad E\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

which evidently implies
\mu_{\alpha}\xrightarrow[\alpha\uparrow 1]{w}\eta

and the result follows.
∎

Anisotropic fractional perimeters

We can utilize Theorem
3.3
also in a more general context of anisotropic fractional perimeters.
We start by recalling an another (equivalent) definition of the classical perimeter. For a set
E
such that
\mathbf{1}_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
one can define its perimeter as

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}(E)=\int_{\partial^{*}E}|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)|\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\partial^{*}E
is the reduced boundary of
E
,
\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)
denotes the measure theoretic outer unit normal vector of
E
at
x\in\partial^{*}E
and
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
is the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. For a detailed treatment on the fine structure of the classical perimeter we refer to
[
3
, Section 3.5]
and
[
21
]
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex compact set of non-empty interior (so-called convex body) and such that it is origin-symmetric.
Anisotropic perimeter of a Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with respect to
K
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}(E,K)=\int_{\partial^{*}E}\|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\|_{K^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Let
ZK
be the so-called moment body of
K
. It is defined as the unique convex body satisfying

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|y\|_{Z^{*}K}=\frac{d+1}{2}\int_{K}|y\cdot x|\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad y\ \in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
Z^{*}K
is the polar body of
ZK
. Our aim is to show (through the methods of the present paper) that convergence in (
1.11
) holds actually for all sets of finite perimeter.

Proposition 3.9
.

For any Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}(1-\alpha)\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=\mathrm{Per}(E,ZK).

Proof.

Let
\nu_{\alpha}(A,K)
be
a measure (with respect to the convex body
K
) given by

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(A,K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(r\theta)r^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}}.

We easily verify that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)}(E).

Clearly,
\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)
is (up to a normalising constant) a special case of (
3.6
) and thus we are in the scope of Theorem
3.3
. We easily find that the measure
\lambda_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)
appearing in Theorem
3.3
satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}(B_{R}^{c},K)=\frac{\int_{R}^{\infty}(1\wedge r)r^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}r}{\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge r)r^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}r}

and we show similarly as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
that
\lambda_{\alpha}(B_{R}^{c},K)

converges to
0
. The corresponding measure
\mu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)
is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(S,K)=C_{\alpha}(K)^{-1}\int_{S}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}},\qquad S\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

where
C_{\alpha}(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}}
.
Since
\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\|\theta\|_{K}^{-d-\alpha}=\|\theta\|_{K}^{-d-1}
for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we infer that

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)\xrightarrow[\alpha\uparrow 1]{w}\mu(\cdot,K),

where

\mu(S,K)=C(K)^{-1}\int_{S}\|\theta\|^{-d-1}_{K}\mathrm{d}\theta
and
C(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\theta\|^{-d-1}_{K}\mathrm{d}\theta
. We obtain that for any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}C_{\alpha}^{-1}C_{\alpha}(K)^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta,K),

where
C_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge r)r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r
. Hence, by taking
f=\mathbf{1}_{E}\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\mathbf{1}_{E}\cdot\theta|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}.

Further, by employing (
3.9
) together with Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\mathbf{1}_{E}\cdot\theta|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial^{*}E}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\cdot\theta|\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}x).

Finally, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{K}|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\cdot y|\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\|\theta\|_{K}}}|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\cdot\theta|r^{d}\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{d+1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\cdot\theta|\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|D\mathbf{1}_{E}\cdot\theta|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}=\int_{\partial^{*}E}\|\mathsf{n}_{E}(x)\|_{Z^{*}K}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathrm{Per}(E,ZK)

and the result follows.
∎

Anisotropic Sobolev norms

We finally show that similar methods apply in the context of anisotropic Sobolev norms. We aim to prove a stronger version of
[
28
, Theorem 8]
as we abandon the assumption of compact support. We recall that for any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we define its anisotropic Sobolev semi-norm (with respect to a given convex body
K
which is origin-symmetric) as

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathrm{BV},ZK}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\right\|_{Z^{*}K}\!\mathrm{d}|Df|.

Here the vector
Df/|Df|
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued vector measure
Df
with respect to the positive measure
|Df|
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(3.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{\|y\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=2\|f\|_{\mathrm{BV},ZK}.

Proof.

We observe that by (
3.12
)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\,(1-\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{\|y\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}.

We are thus left to identify the limit. We first note that, in view of
[
3
, Proposition 1.23]
,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}|Df\cdot\theta|=\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\cdot\theta\right|\mathrm{d}|Df|.

This implies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Df\cdot\theta|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\cdot\theta\right|\,\mathrm{d}|Df|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\cdot\theta\right|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}\,\mathrm{d}|Df|.

We then proceed similarly as in (
3.13
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\cdot\theta\right|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+1}}=(d+1)\int_{K}\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\cdot y\right|\,\mathrm{d}y=2\left\|\frac{Df}{|Df|}\right\|_{Z^{*}K}

and the result follows.
∎

3.2.
Convergence towards the Lebesgue measure

In this paragraph we focus on convergence of non-local perimeters towards the Lebesgue measure in the case when the mass of the underlying Lévy measure concentrates at infinity. In the rest of the paper we make use of a covariance function of a set and thus we briefly recall its definition and basic properties.

For any
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure its covariance function
g_{E}
is given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle g_{E}(y)=|E\cap(E+y)|=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathbf{1}_{E}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{E}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

It is a symmetric and uniformly continuous function tending to zero at infinity. If
E
is of finite perimeter then it is Lipschitz continuous. For more details we refer to
[
25
]
. According to
[
16
, Lemma 1]
, if
E
is of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter then
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu}(E)<\infty
for any measure
\nu
satisfying (
1.4
).

We first aim to prove the following result.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
\{\nu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of measures satisfying (
1.4
) and let

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\left(1\wedge R_{\varepsilon}|x|\right)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)

where
C_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1\wedge R_{\varepsilon}|x|\right)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)
and
R_{\varepsilon}\in[1,\infty]
.
We assume that, for any
R>0
,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})=1.

Then for any set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter it holds

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}(E)=|E|.

Furthermore, if
\{\nu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is a family of finite measures then for any set
E
of finite Lebesgue measure it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}(\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}(E)=|E|.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|E\cap(E^{c}-y)|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=|E|\nu_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})-\int_{B_{R}^{c}}g_{E}(y)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y).

Since
R_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
, for any
R>1
we have
C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\nu_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})=\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})
which tends to one as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.
If
|E|<\infty
then
g_{E}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
1
1

1

By
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote the space of all continuous functions that vanish at infinity.
. Thus we can choose
R
big enough so that
g_{E}(y)
is smaller than any given
\epsilon>0
for
|y|>R
. We are left with the last integral in the formula above. We have

(3.19)

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq CC_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\int_{B_{R}}(1\wedge|y|)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\leq C\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R})\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0,

where we used the fact that
g_{E}
is Lipschitz continuous if
\mathrm{Per}(E)<\infty
. If
\nu_{\varepsilon}
is a finite measure then we simply choose
R_{\varepsilon}=\infty
and repeat the same reasoning as above. In (
3.19
) we use the fact that
g_{E}
is bounded.
∎

We present an analogous result for stable Lévy measures.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\nu_{\alpha}
be the
\alpha
-stable Lévy measure given by

(3.20)

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(A)=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(\varrho\theta)\varrho^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\varrho\,\eta(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\alpha\in(0,1),

where
\eta
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For any set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
|E|<\infty
and
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha_{0}}}(E)<\infty
, for some
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
it holds

(3.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)=|E|.

Proof.

Representation (
3.20
) yields
\nu_{\alpha}(B_{R}^{c})=R^{-\alpha}
, for any
R>0
. As in the proof of Theorem
3.11
we have

(3.22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)

\displaystyle=|E|R^{-\alpha}-\int_{B_{R}^{c}}g_{E}(y)\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)

and the first integral is negligible as
g_{E}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To estimate the second integral we observe that

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{R}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(\varrho\theta))\varrho^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\varrho\,\eta(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Since for
0<\varrho\leq R
and
0\leq\alpha<\alpha_{0}
,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\varrho^{-1-\alpha}\leq R^{\alpha_{0}}\varrho^{-1-\alpha_{0}},

we can apply the dominated convergence theorem in the last equation and this implies

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\alpha^{-1}\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{R}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(\varrho\theta))\varrho^{-1}\mathrm{d}\varrho\,\eta(\mathrm{d}\theta).

The last expression is finite in view of (
3.23
) used for
\alpha=0
and combined with the assumption that the perimeter
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha_{0}}}(E)
is finite.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

We could prove convergence in (
3.21

\eta

given in (

and converges weakly towards some measure on the unit sphere, see e.g. (

Let the measure

Corollary 3.14

(Convergence of
\alpha
-perimeters as

Let

\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha_{0}}}(E)<\infty

This follows directly from Proposition

In the following example we show that the assumption of finite perimeter in Theorem
3.11
cannot be weakened.

Example 3.15
.

We first present an example of a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
of finite Lebesgue measure and such that its classical perimeter is infinite whereas its
\alpha
-perimeter is finite for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We consider the one-dimensional case for simplicity’s sake.
Let

(3.24)

\displaystyle E=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\,[n,n+2^{-n}].

Clearly
|E|=1
and
\mathrm{Per}(E)=\infty
. In order to compute
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)
we apply formula (
3.22
) which leads to

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)

\displaystyle=|E|-\int_{|y|>1}g_{E}(y)\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{|y|\leq 1}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y).

The first integral is clearly finite so it suffices to handle the last integral. We easily show that for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))=(n-1)y+2^{-n+1},\quad\mathrm{for}\ y\in(2^{-n},2^{-n+1}).

This implies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left((n-1)\int_{2^{-n}}^{2^{-n+1}}\!\!\!y^{-\alpha}\mathrm{d}y+2^{-n+1}\int_{2^{-n}}^{2^{-n+1}}\!\!\!\!y^{-\alpha-1}\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{1-2^{\alpha-1}}+\frac{2^{\alpha}(1-2^{-\alpha})}{1-2^{\alpha-1}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha},\quad\alpha\uparrow 1.

In particular, we infer that
\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)<\infty
and
\lim_{\alpha\uparrow 1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}}(E)=\infty
.

Further, we consider a family of stable Lévy measures given by

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\nu_{\frac{1}{n}}(\mathrm{d}x)+c_{n}\nu_{1-\frac{1}{n}}(\mathrm{d}x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\nu_{\frac{1}{n}}
(resp.
\nu_{1-\frac{1}{n}}
) is the
\frac{1}{n}
-stable (resp.
(1-\frac{1}{n})
-stable) Lévy measure defined at (
3.20
) and
c_{n}
is a sequence of positive numbers to be specified later. Using (
3.25
) and the above calculation we obtain for set
E
given in (
3.24
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{n}}(E)\sim\frac{1}{(1-\frac{1}{n})^{2}}+c_{n}n^{2}\sim c_{n}n^{2},\quad n\to\infty.

If
c_{n}\sim n^{-1}
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{n}}(E)=\infty
whereas for
c_{n}\sim n^{-2}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{n}}(E)<\infty
.
Moreover, if we choose
c_{n}=o(n)
then the corresponding sequence of measures

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=C_{n}^{-1}(1\wedge|x|)\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x),

with
C_{n}=\int(1\wedge|x|)\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)
, satisfies condition
\lambda_{n}\xrightarrow{w}\delta_{\infty}
of Theorem
3.11
. Indeed, we easily find that

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>R}\nu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\begin{cases}R^{-1/n}+c_{n}R^{1/n-1},\ R>1;\\
\frac{1/n}{1-\frac{1}{n}}(1-R^{-1/n+1})+\frac{1-\frac{1}{n}}{1/n}(1-R^{1/n})+c_{n}+1,\ R<1\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle C_{n}=\frac{1/n}{1-\frac{1}{n}}+c_{n}\frac{1-\frac{1}{n}}{1/n}+c_{n}+1\to 1,\quad n\to\infty.

It follows that under condition
c_{n}n\to 0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}(B_{R}^{c})=1,\quad R>0,

which yields
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{n}}(E)=1
.

As a further application of Theorem
3.11
we obtain asymptotics for the perimeter given through rescaled measures under the assumption that the tail of the original measure is slowly varying at zero.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
\nu
be a given measure and let
\nu_{\varepsilon}(A)=\nu(\varepsilon A)
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that
\nu_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
1.4
) for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose that the function
\ell(s)=\int_{|x|>s}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
is slowly varying at zero. Then, for any set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}C_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}(E)=|E|,

where
C_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1\wedge|x|)\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.11
with
R_{\varepsilon}=1
. It is enough to show condition (
3.17
). For any
R\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{|x|<R}(1\wedge|x|)\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1\wedge|x|)\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)}=\frac{\int_{|x|<1}|x|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)+\int_{1<|x|<R}\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int_{|x|<1}|x|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)+\int_{|x|>1}\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)}.

Further,

\displaystyle\int_{|x|<1}|x|\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{|x|<1}\int_{0}^{|x|}\mathrm{d}u\,\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{u<|x|<1}\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\varepsilon u<|x|<\varepsilon}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{1}(\ell(\varepsilon u)-\ell(\varepsilon))\,\mathrm{d}u.

This implies

(3.26)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\ell(\varepsilon u)\,\mathrm{d}u-\ell(\varepsilon R)}{\int_{0}^{1}\ell(\varepsilon u)\,\mathrm{d}u}.

We set
L(w)=\ell(1/w)
, for
w>0
. By a change of variable we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}\ell(\varepsilon u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{1/\varepsilon}^{\infty}w^{-2}L(w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

According to
[
5
, Proposition 1.5.10]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{1/\varepsilon}^{\infty}w^{-2}L(w)\,\mathrm{d}w\sim L(1/\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon\downarrow 0,

which yields
\int_{0}^{1}\ell(\varepsilon u)\,\mathrm{d}u\sim\ell(\varepsilon)
when
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and thus the expression in (
3.26
) tends to zero as
\ell
is slowly varying.
∎

We finish this paragraph with two results for
J
-perimeters.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
J\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
be a given kernel and let
J_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{d}J(\varepsilon x)
, for

. Assume that
(1\wedge|x|)J_{\varepsilon}(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\varepsilon>0
and that
\ell(s)=\int_{|x|>s}J(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\ell(\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{Per}_{J\varepsilon}(E)=|E|.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.16
if we choose
\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=J_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x
and notice that in this case

The following easy observation is a consequence of Theorem
3.11
if we choose
\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=J_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x
and observe that
\nu_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\|J\|_{L^{1}}
. We remark, however, that it can be proved directly if we use the definition of the

-perimeter together with the dominated convergence theorem.

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
J\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
be a kernel such that
J\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and let
J_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{d}J(\varepsilon x)
, for

Convergence of anisotropic fractional perimeters

We finally present how to deduce convergence of anisotropic fractional
\alpha
-perimeters when
\alpha\downarrow 0
. We actually slightly improve on
[
28
, Theorem 6]
as we do not require the set
E
to be bounded.

Proposition 3.19
.

For any
E
of finite Lebesgue measure and of finite perimeter it holds

(3.27)

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\alpha\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K)=d|K||E|.

Proof.

We consider the following measure

(3.28)

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(A,K)=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(\varrho\theta)\varrho^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\varrho\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}},\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

We observe that

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)}(E)=\alpha\mathrm{Per}_{\alpha}(E,K).

Proceeding similarly as in (
3.22
) we find that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)}(E)=C_{\alpha}(K)R^{-\alpha}|E|-\int_{B_{R}^{c}}g_{E}(y)\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y,K)+\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y,K),

where
C_{\alpha}(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d+\alpha}}
. The integral over
B^{c}_{R}
may be done arbitrarily small if we choose
R
big enough.
Since
\mathrm{Per}(E)<\infty
, we know that
g_{E}(x)
is a Lipschitz function. This and the fact that

\|\theta\|^{-d-\alpha}_{K}
is bounded for
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
allows us to apply the dominated convergence theorem and we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\alpha^{-1}\int_{B_{R}}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(y))\nu_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}y,K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{R}(g_{E}(0)-g_{E}(r\theta))r^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d}}.

Further, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}C_{\alpha}(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\|\theta\|_{K}^{d}}=d|K|

and we infer that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\mathrm{Per}_{\nu_{\alpha}(\cdot,K)}(E)=d|K||E|,

which in view of (
3.29
) implies (
3.27
).
∎

Appendix: Proof of Theorem
3.1

We aim to establish a more general version of
[
37
, Theorem 2]
which is included in Theorem
3.1
. We follow closely the approach of
[
37
]
. In the remaining part of the text we make use of mollifiers. Let
j\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}j(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
. For any
\delta>0
let
j_{\delta}(x)=\delta^{-d}j(x/\delta)
. Clearly,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}j_{\delta}(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
. For any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we set

\displaystyle f_{\delta}(x)=j_{\delta}\ast f(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We start with the following series of lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any probability measure
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
f\in\mathrm{BV}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big|Df\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\Big|\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y).

Proof.

For any
R>0
and
\delta>0
, by a standard argument basing upon the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{|f_{\delta}(x+y)-f_{\delta}(x)|}{|y|}

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\nabla f_{\delta}(x+\rho y)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\mathrm{d}\rho\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla f_{\delta}(x+\rho y)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla f_{\delta}(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\mathrm{d}x\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}j_{\delta}(x-z)\,Df(\mathrm{d}z)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\mathrm{d}x\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}j_{\delta}(x-z)\frac{Df}{|Df|}(z)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\,|Df|(\mathrm{d}z)\right|\mathrm{d}x\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}j_{\delta}(x-z)\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}(z)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\mathrm{d}x\,|Df|(\mathrm{d}z)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{Df}{|Df|}(z)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right||Df|(\mathrm{d}z)\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|Df\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\right|\lambda(\mathrm{d}y),

where we used (
3.15
) together with
[
3
, Proposition 1.23]
. We finally take
\lambda\downarrow 0
and then
R\to\infty
and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For any probability measure
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f_{\delta}(x+y)-f_{\delta}(x)|}{|y|}\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

We clearly have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f_{\delta}(x+y)-f_{\delta}(x)|}{|y|}

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(x+y-z)-f(x-z)|}{|y|}\,j_{\delta}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(w+y)-f(w)|}{|y|}\,j_{\delta}(x+w)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}w\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|f(w+y)-f(w)|}{|y|}\,\mathrm{d}w\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)

and the proof is finished.
∎

Recall that
\{\lambda\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\{0\})=0
and, for any
R>0
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(B_{R}^{c})=0.

We consider
\mu_{\varepsilon}(E)=\lambda_{\varepsilon}((0,\infty)E)
, where
(0,\infty)E=\{re:\,e\in E\ \text{and}\ r>0\}
is a cone spanned by
E\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Since the family
\{\mu_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is bounded in the space of Radon measures on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{j}
converging to zero and a probability measure
\mu
on the unit sphere such that
\mu_{\varepsilon_{j}}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
. We observe that in view of the definition, for any continuous function
F\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F\Big(\frac{y}{|y|}\Big)\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F(\theta)\mu_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\varepsilon>0.

It evidently follows that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F\Big(\frac{y}{|y|}\Big)\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F(\theta)\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arbitrary Euclidean ball of radius
R>0
. Then, for any
f\in C^{2}(B_{R})
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{|y|}\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla f(x)\cdot\theta|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Proof.

We start with the following easy inequality

\displaystyle\left|\frac{|f(x+y)-f(y)|}{|y|}-\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\big|f(x+y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|}{|y|}

\displaystyle\leq C|y|,\quad C>0.

This implies

\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\left|\frac{|f(x+y)-f(y)|}{|y|}-\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\right|\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq|B_{R}|\left(C\int_{|y|\leq 1}|y|\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)+2\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\int_{|y|>1}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\right).

By (
4.1
), the second integral converges to zero as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. For the second we write

\displaystyle\int_{|y|\leq 1}|y|\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\leq 1}\int_{0}^{|y|}\mathrm{d}t\,\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{t\leq|y|\leq 1}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{|y|>t}\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}t\to 0,\quad\mathrm{as}\ \varepsilon\downarrow 0.

Thus we are left to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{B_{R}}|\nabla f(x)\cdot\theta|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

We have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}}\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{B_{R}}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\big|\nabla f(x)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}\big|\lambda_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathrm{d}x.

As the last integral clearly tends to zero, for
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we infer the result with the aid of (
4.3
) and the dominated convergence theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

By Lemma
