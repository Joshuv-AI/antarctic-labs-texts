Nonlinear codes with low redundancy
Shu Liu, Chaoping Xing

Abstract
. Determining the largest size, or equivalently finding the lowest redundancy, of q q -ary codes for given length and minimum distance is one of the central and fundamental problems in coding theory. Inspired by the construction of Varshamov-Tenengolts (VT for short) codes via check-sums, we provide an explicit construction of nonlinear codes with lower redundancy than linear codes under the same length and minimum distance. Similar to the VT codes, our construction works well for small distance (or even constant distance). Furthermore, we design quasi-linear time decoding algorithms for both erasure and adversary errors.



1.
Introduction

Given a code alphabet, determining the largest size
A_{q}(n,d)
of
q
-ary block codes
\mathcal{C}
with length
n
and Hamming minimum distance
d
has been a long-standing problem in coding theory.
There are a large amount of papers in literatures to study the quantity
A_{q}(n,d)
. When
d
is small or even a constant, BCH codes and Hamming codes usually have large size in this parameter regime. The other way to see whether a code has good parameters, one can simply look into its redundancy. Thus, we can define the smallest possible redundancy by
r_{q}(n,d)=n-\log_{q}A_{q}(n,d)
for given alphabet size
q
, length
n
and minimum distance
d
. Of course, one would like to have this redundancy
r_{q}(n,d)
as small as possible.
In this paper, we mainly focus on
r_{q}(n,d)
(or equivalently
A_{q}(n,d)
) with small
d
.

1.1.
Known results

There are various upper bounds on
A_{q}(n,d)
(see
[
3
]
) such as the Hamming bound (a relatively tight bound for small distance like
d=3
), the Plotkin bound (a relatively tight bound for large distance such as
d>(q-1)n/q
), the Grismer bound (a bound only for linear codes) and the linear programming bound. A benchmark for a good code is the Gilbert-Varshamov bound–a lower bound on
A_{q}(n,d)
. There are two versions of the Gilbert-Varshamov bound, one is called the weak version and the other is called strong version (see Section 2 for the detail). The strong version is applicable only for linear codes (see
[
3
]
).

Apart from some well-known families of codes such as Hamming codes, Reed-Solomon codes, BCH codes, Reed-Muller codes, Goppa codes and algebraic geometry codes, etc, there are also various constructions of linear and nonlinear codes that provide lower bounds on
A_{q}(n,d)
for some specific parameters. As there are too many such constructions in literatures, we are not going to mention these constructions one by one. The reader may refer some books
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
and the online table
[
8
]
for some of these constructions. We would like to particularly discuss Hamming codes. Hamming codes have minimum distance
3
and achieve the Hamming bound. Therefore, Hamming codes are optimal in the sense that the codes achieve the maximal size
A_{q}(n,3)
. However, there are some constraints on parameters for Hamming codes. Firstly, as Hamming codes are linear codes, thus we require that
q
is a prime power. Secondly, the code length of Hamming codes are of the form
\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
for
r\geqslant 2
. Thus, except for some specific parameters, in general we do not know exact values of
A_{q}(n,3)
if
q
is not a prime power; or
n
is not of the form
\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
. We do not even know if
r_{q}(n,3)<n-k_{q}(n,3)
for a prime power
q
, where
k_{q}(n,d)
is the maximum dimension
k
such that there exists a
q
-ary
[n,k,3]
-linear code (this means that
n-k_{q}(n,3)
is the smallest redundancy for
q
-ary linear codes of length
n
and distance
3
).

1.2.
Our results

In this paper, we present a construction of nonlinear codes with low redundancy that is inspired by the construction of Varshamov-Tenengolts codes via check-sums. As a result, in general our codes have lower redundancy than linear codes for given code length and distance. In particular, when minimum distance
d
is fixed, one can show that our codes have smaller redundancy than linear codes if the code length
n
lies in some intervals.

Although it is generally difficult to design efficient algorithms for nonlinear codes, we present quasi-linear time decoding algorithms for our codes for both erasure and adversary errors. More precisely speaking, the decoding complexity is
O(n\log^{4}n)
bit operations for both erasure and adversary errors. In addition, if distance
d
is constant, the decoding complexity is
O(\log^{2}n)
and
O(n\log^{2}n)
bit operations for erasure and adversary errors, respectively.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present some preliminaries on codes including definitions of
A_{q}(n,d)
,
r_{q}(n,d)
, the main MDS conjecture and the relation between distance and erasure error correcting capability. In Section 3, we provide an explicit construction of the nonlinear code with low redundancy. Furthermore, some numerical examples are given in Section 3 as well. In the last section, decoding algorithms of our nonlinear codes constructed in Section 3 are presented.

2.
Preliminary

2.1.
Some notations on codes

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q
elements.
For two integers
a,b
with
a\leqslant b
, denote by
[a,b]
the set
\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
. In particular, denote by
[n]
the set
[1,n]
. A
q
-ary code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
is a subset of
[0,q-1]^{n}
. If the size of
\mathcal{C}
is
M
, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
q
-ary
(n,M)
-code or an
(n,M)_{q}
-code. Furthermore, if the Hamming distance of
\mathcal{C}
is
d
, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
q
-ary
(n,M,d)
-code. It is well known that a code with minimum Hamming distance
d
can correct
\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\rfloor
adversary errors.
It is clear that if there is a
q
-ary
(n,M,d_{1})
-code with
d_{1}>d
, then there is also a
q
-ary
(n,M,d)
-code. This is because we can turn a
q
-ary
(n,M,d_{1})
-code into a
q
-ary
(n,M,d)
-code by changing every codeword of a fixed set of
d_{1}-d
positions to
0
.
For a
q
-ary linear code
\mathcal{C}
with length
n,
dimension
k
and minimum distance
d
, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is a
q
-ary
[n,k,d]
-linear code.

2.2.
Optimal linear and nonlinear codes

In coding theory, it is a great challenge to determine the maximal size of
q
-ary codes for given length
n
and minimum distance
d
. The following quantity characterizes this maximum size:

(2.2.1)

A_{q}(n,d)=\max\{M\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\;\mbox{there is a $q$-ary $(n,M,d)$ code}\}.

An
(n,M,d)_{q}
-code is called optimal if
M=A_{q}(n,d)
.

Similarly, when
q
is a prime power, we can define maximal size of
q
-ary linear codes for given length
n
and minimum distance
d
as follows:

(2.2.2)

K_{q}(n,d)=\max\{M\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\;\mbox{there is a $q$-ary $(n,M,d)$-linear code}\}.

An
[n,k,d]_{q}
-code is called optimal if
k=\log_{q}K_{q}(n,d)
.

By the simple prorogation rules mentioned above (also see
[
3
, Chapter 6]
), we know that
A_{q}(n,d)
is a decreasing function of
d
for given
q
and
n
, while
A_{q}(n,d)
is an increasing function of
n
for given
q
and
d
. Usually the quantities
A_{q}(n,d)
and
K_{q}(n,d)
are large integers. Thus, we define the following two normalized quantities

(2.2.3)

a_{q}(n,d)=\log_{q}A_{q}(n,d);\quad k_{q}(n,d)=\log_{q}K_{q}(n,d).

Note that
a_{q}(n,d)
may not be an integer, while
k_{q}(n,d)
is always an integer which is the dimension of a code.

Recall,
r_{q}(n,d)=n-a_{q}(n,d)
is the smallest redundancy of
q
-ary codes of length
n
and minimum distance
d
. Now we define
r^{L}_{q}(n,d)=n-k_{q}(n,d)
to be the smallest redundancy of
q
-ary linear codes of length
n
and minimum distance
d
. Then it is clear that
r_{q}(n,d)\leqslant r_{q}^{L}(n,d)
. It is not clear where
r_{q}(n,d)
is strictly less than
r_{q}^{L}(n,d)
in general.

2.3.
Defect

In this subsection, we assume that
q
is a prime power. Then by the Singleton bound, we know that every
q
-ary
[n,k,d]
-linear code
\mathcal{C}
obeys

(2.3.1)

k\leqslant n-d+1.

When the equality in (
2.3.1
) holds,
\mathcal{C}
is called a maximum distance separable (MDS for short) code.

One of the main problems for MDS codes is to determine the maximum length of an MDS code. The following is an important conjecture.

Main Conjecture on MDS codes.
For a nontrivial
q
-ary
[n,k,n-k+1]
-MDS code, we have

n\leqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q+2&\mbox{ if $2|q$ and $k\in\{3,q-1\}$};\\
q+1&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

A
q
-ary
[n,k,d]
-linear code satisfying

(2.3.2)

k=n-d+1-r

is said to have defect
r
. It is clear that codes with defect
0
are MDS codes. A code with defect
1
is called a almost MDS code. We denote by
N_{q}(d,r)
the largest length
n
of
q
-ary
[n,n-d+1-r,d]
-linear codes.

By generalized Reed-Solomon codes and their extended codes, we know that
N_{q}(d,0)\geqslant q+1
. On the other hand, the main MDS conjecture tells us that
N_{q}(d,0)\leqslant q+2
.

The values
N_{q}(3,0)
and
N_{q}(3,1)
are completely determined
[
1
]
. For completeness, we provide a short proof below.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
r\geqslant 0
, one has

N_{q}(3,0)=q+1,\quad N_{q}(3,1)=q^{2}+q+1.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an
[n,n-2,3]
-MDS code, then its parity-check matrix has size
2\times n
. As the distance of
\mathcal{C}
is
3
, any two coloumns are linearly independent. This means that one-dimensional spaces spanned by columns of
H
are pairwise distinct. As there are
\frac{q^{2}-1}{q-1}=q+1
one-dimensional spaces in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, the desired result follows.

The similar arguments can be used to show
N_{q}(3,1)=\frac{q^{3}-1}{q-1}=q^{2}+q+1
. In this case, we consider one-dimensional spaces in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3}
.
∎

2.4.
Erasure errors and minimum distance

It is well known that minimum distance of a code determines erasure error correcting capability. Informally, we say that a
q
-ary code
C\subseteq[0,q-1]^{n}
can correct
\tau
erasure errors if any
\tau
positions of a codeword are erased, we can still recover this codeword. Precisely speaking, a
q
-ary code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq[0,q-1]^{n}
can correct
\tau
erasure errors if for any subset
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=n-\tau
and a codeword
{\bf c}\in\mathcal{C}
, no other codewords
{\bf b}\in\mathcal{C}
satisfy
{\bf b}_{S}={\bf c}_{S}
, where
{\bf b}_{S}
is the projection of
{\bf b}
at
S
. The following lemma follows immediately.

Lemma 2.2
.

A code
\mathcal{C}
has minimum distance at least
d
if and only if it can correct
d-1
erasure errors.

2.5.
Generalized Reed-Solomon codes

Let
F
be a field and choose nonzero elements
\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}\subseteq F^{*}
(
v_{i}
are not necessarily distinct) and pairwise distinct elements
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}\subseteq F
. Put
{\bf v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})\in(F^{*})^{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in F^{n}.
For
0\leqslant k\leqslant n,
the generalized Reed-Solomon is defined by

{\rm GRS}_{n,k}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\bf v})=\{v_{1}f(\alpha_{1}),\cdots,v_{n}f(\alpha_{n}):f\in F[x]_{<k}\}.

Here,
F[x]_{<k}
denotes the set of polynomial in
F[x]
of degree less than
k
.

Lemma 2.3
.

{\rm GRS}_{n,k}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\bf v})
is an
[n,k,d]
-linear code over
F
with length
n\leqslant|F|.
If
0<k<n,
then
d=n-k+1.
In particular, a generalized Reed-Solomon code is an MDS code.

The dual of the generalized Reed-Solomon code
{\rm GRS}_{n,k}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\bf v})
is given by

{\rm GRS}_{n,k}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\bf v})^{\perp}={\rm GRS}_{n,n-k}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\bf u}),

where
{\bf u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
with
u_{i}^{-1}=v_{i}\Pi_{j\neq i}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}).
Thus, the dual code of a generalized Reed-Solomon code is also an MDS code.

Decoding of generalized Reed-Solomon codes is of both practical and theoretical importantance. The most widely known decoding is the syndrome-based Reed-Solomon codes decoding, in which the key equation is solved using either the Berlekamp-Massey algorithm, the Euclidean algorithm or fast Fourier tranform. For an
[n,k]
-generalized Reed-Solomon code, the computational complexity of syndrome-based decoding is
O(n\log n+(n-k)\log^{2}(n-k))
operations of field elements
[
2
]
.

3.
Construction

In this section, we provide an explicit construction of nonlinear code with low redundancy. Our construction follows the idea of VT codes with additional parity-check from
a
RS code. Some numerical examples are provided to show that our codes have lower redundancy than linear codes for given length
n
and minimum distance
d
.

Let
q
be an integer greater than
1
and let
d,n
be two positive integers greater than
2
. Let
\ell
be the smallest prime satisfying
\ell\geqslant\max\{n,q\}
. For
{\bf x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,q-1]^{n}
, define the functions

(3.0.1)

\quad t_{j}({\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{j}x_{i}

for
j\geqslant 0
.

Choose
b_{0},b_{1},b_{1},\dots,b_{d-2}
with
b_{0}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]
and
b_{i}\in[0,\ell-1]
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-2
and put
{\bf b}=(b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{d-2})
.
Define the code

(3.0.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})=\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in[0,q-1]^{n}:\;t_{j}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\equiv b_{j}\pmod{\ell}

\displaystyle\mbox{ for $1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2$};\;t_{0}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\equiv b_{0}\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}\}

Now we show that the minimum distance of the code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
is at least
d
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
d\geqslant 3
, the code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
given in (
3.0.2
) has Hamming distance at least
d
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, it suffices to show that the code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
can correct
d-1
erasure errors. Let
S:=\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{d-1}\}
be a subset of
[n]
with
k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{d-1}
. Assume that a codeword
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
are erased at positions of
S
, i.e.,
(\alpha_{k_{1}},\alpha_{k_{2}},\dots,\alpha_{k_{d-1}})
is erased. Put
c=\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus S}\alpha_{i}
. Then we can compute

\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus S}\alpha_{i}\equiv b_{0}-c\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1},

since
t_{0}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})=b_{0}
. Let
c_{0}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]
with
c_{0}\equiv b_{0}-c\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}
. Then we have
\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}=c_{0}
since
\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}\leqslant|S|(q-1)=(d-1)(q-1)
.
Hence, we have
\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}\pmod{\ell}=c_{0}
.

For
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2
, we can also compute

c_{j}:=b_{j}-\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus S}i^{j}\alpha_{i}\pmod{\ell}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^{j}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus S}i^{j}\alpha_{i}\pmod{\ell}=\sum_{i\in S}i^{j}\alpha_{i}\pmod{\ell}.

This means that the vector
(\alpha_{k_{1}},\alpha_{k_{2}},\dots,\alpha_{k_{d-1}})
is the unique solution of the following equation

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
k_{1}&k_{2}&\cdots&k_{d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
k_{1}^{d-2}&k_{2}^{d-2}&\cdots&k_{d-1}^{d-2}\end{pmatrix}{\bf x}^{T}={\bf c}^{T}\pmod{\ell},

where
{\bf c}=(c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{d-2})
. Note that the above matrix is a
(d-1)\times(d-1)
Vandermonde matrix which is invertible.
This completes the proof.
∎

The following lower bound on
A_{q}(n,d)
can be easily derived from the above Lemma.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
d\geqslant 3
, let
\ell
be the smallest prime satisfying
\ell\geqslant\max\{q,n\}
. Then
one has

A_{q}(n,d)\geqslant\frac{q^{n}}{((d-1)(q-1)+1)\ell^{d-2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that there exists a vector
{\bf b}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]\times[0,\ell-1]^{d-2}
such that the code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
is an
(n,M)
code with

M\geqslant\frac{q^{n}}{((d-1)(q-1)+1)\ell^{d-2}}.

It is clear that

\bigcup_{{\bf b}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]\times[0,\ell-1]^{d-2}}\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})=[0,q-1]^{n}.

This gives

q^{n}=\left|[0,q-1]^{n}\right|=\left|\bigcup_{{\bf b}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]\times[0,\ell-1]^{d-2}}\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})\right|\leqslant\sum_{{\bf b}\in[0,(d-1)(q-1)]\times[0,\ell-1]^{d-2}}|\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})|.

This implies that when
{\bf b}
runs through
[0,(d-1)(q-1)]\times[0,\ell-1]^{d-2}
, the average size of
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
is at least
\frac{q^{n}}{((d-1)(q-1)+1)\ell^{d-2}}
. The proof is completed.
∎

q^{2}+q+1<n\leqslant\frac{q^{4}}{4q-2}
, we have

r_{q}(n,3)<r_{q}^{L}(n,3).

Proof.

for

(3.0.4)

~A_{q}(n,3)\geqslant\frac{q^{n}}{(2q-1)\ell}>\frac{q^{n}}{(2q-1)\times 2n}\geqslant q^{n-4}\geqslant K_{q}(n,3).

So,

r_{q}(n,3)=n-\log_{q}A_{q}(n,3)<n-\log_{q}K_{q}(n,3)=r_{q}^{L}(n,3).

The proof is completed.
∎

4.
Decoding Algorithms

In this section, we discuss decoding algorithm of the codes given in Section 3. Let us consider erasure error decoding algorithm and adversary error decoding algorithm separately. For erasure errors, the decoding algorithm mainly consists of solving an equation system. On the other hand, for adversary errors with larger error-correcting capability, the decoding algorithm is based on the decoding algorithm of generalized Reed-Solomon codes.

Firstly, we introduce the erasure error decoding algorithm.
The code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
has distance
d
. Hence, it can correct
d-1
erasure errors. From our proof of Lemma
3.1
, we find that to decode
d-1
erasure errors, it is sufficient to solve an equation system with
d-1
variables over
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
. Thus the decoding complexity is
O(d^{3}\log^{2}\ell)=O(d^{3}\log^{2}n)
. In case
d
is a constant, the decoding algorithm requires
O(\log^{2}n)
bit operations. If
d
is not a constant, we can use the following decoding algorithm for adversary errors to get a quasi-linear time
O(n\log^{4}n)
for erasure errors.

To correct adversary errors, let
\tau=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor
. We distinguish two cases:
\tau=1
and
\tau>1
. Let
{\bf y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})\in[0,q-1]^{n}
be a received word.

\blacksquare

Case
1

\tau=1
.

Step 1:

Compute
t_{0}({\bf y})\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}

and
t_{j}({\bf y})\pmod{\ell}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2
. If

t_{0}({\bf y})\equiv b_{0}\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1},\quad t_{j}({\bf y})\equiv b_{j}\pmod{\ell}

for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2,
output
{\bf y}
. Otherwise, go to Step 2.

Step 2:

For every position
k\in[n]
, replace
y_{k}
by an element
\alpha\in[0,q-1]\setminus\{y_{k}\}
and compute
t_{0}({\bf z})=t_{0}({\bf y})+(\alpha-y_{k})\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}

and
t_{j}({\bf z})=k^{j}(\alpha-y_{k})\pmod{\ell}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2
, where
{\bf z}
is obtained by
{\bf y}
by replaying
y_{k}
with
\alpha
. If

(4.0.1)

t_{0}({\bf z})\equiv b_{0}\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1},\quad t_{j}({\bf z})\equiv b_{j}\pmod{\ell}

for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2
, output
{\bf z}
. Otherwise, we try other elements of
[0,q-1]
and position
k\in[n]
until we find
k
and
\alpha
that satisfy (
4.0.1
).

Note that the complexity of the above algorithm in Case
1
is
O(n\log^{2}n)
bit operations.

\blacksquare

Case
2

\tau>1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the dual code of the generalized Reed-Solomon code over
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
with evaluation points
1,2,\dots,n
and dimension
d-1
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is also a generalized Reed-Solomon code with parameters
[n,n-d+1,d]
. Assume that
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is the codeword that was transmitted for the code
\mathcal{C}_{d}({\bf b})
. Then
{\bf y}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is the error vector when we transmit the codeword
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

Step 1:

Compute
t_{0}({\bf y})\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}

and
t_{j}({\bf y})\pmod{\ell}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant d-2
. View
t_{0}({\bf y})\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1}
as an element of
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
. Compute the vector
{\bf s}:={\bf b}-(t_{0}({\bf y})\pmod{(d-1)(q-1)+1},t_{1}({\bf y})\pmod{\ell},\cdots,t_{d-2}({\bf y})\pmod{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}^{d-1}
. Then
{\bf s}
is the syndrome of the received word
{\bf y}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
for the generalized Reed-Solomon code
\mathcal{A}
.

Step 2:

Find the error vector
{\bf y}-{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
through the syndrome
{\bf s}
via a decoding of the generalized Reed-Solomon code
\mathcal{A}
.

The decoding complexity depends on the decoding algorithm of generalized Reed-Solomon codes. The fastest decoding algorithm of Reed-Solomon codes has complexity
O(n\log(d-1)+(d-1)\log^{2}(d-1))
bit operations
[
2
]
. Thus, it takes
O((n\log(d-1)+(d-1)\log^{2}(d-1))\log^{2}n)=O(n\log^{4}n)
bit operations. If
d
is a constant, the the decoding complexity is in fact
O(n\log^{2}n)
bit operations.
