Compressed Sensing of Compton Profiles for Fermi Surface Reconstruction: Concept and Implementation
Junya Otsuki, Kazuyoshi Yoshimi, Yoshinori Nakanishi-Ohno, Michael Sekania, Liviu Chioncel, Masaichiro Mizumaki

Abstract
Compton scattering is a well-established technique that can provide detailed information about electronic states in solids. Making use of the principle of tomography, it is possible to determine the Fermi surface from sets of Compton-scattering data with different scattering axes. Practical applications, however, are limited due to long acquisition time required for measuring along enough number of scattering directions. Here, we propose to overcome this difficulty using compressed sensing. Taking advantage of a hidden sparsity in the momentum distribution, we are able to reconstruct the three-dimensional momentum distribution of bcc-Li, and identify the Fermi surface with as little as 14 directions of scattering data with unprecedented accuracy. This compressed-sensing approach will permit further wider applications of the Compton scattering experiments.

Introduction

The Compton scattering comprises the collision events in which photons (usually X-rays) are inelastically scattered by electrons in materials.
Since these electrons are in motion, the scattered radiation is Doppler-broadened and its measurement provides information on the electron momentum density (EMD) projected along the scattering direction

The Compton scattering experiment measures the double differential cross section

Within the so-called impulse approximation

\displaystyle J_{\zeta}(p_{z})=\iint\rho(\bm{p})\,{\mathrm{d}}p_{x}{\mathrm{d}}p_{y}.

(1)

This principle has been applied to the Compton scattering to reconstruct the three-dimensional function

;
Mijnarends 1969
;

Compared with the original CT,
the reconstruction of
\rho(\bm{p})

involves practical difficulties for following two reasons.
Firstly, the reconstruction of
\rho(\bm{p})
requires recovering of two axes eliminated by the double integral in Eq. (

Secondly,

is limited

Because of these difficulties, recent applications recover only one axis and employ the two-dimensional EMD projected onto a plane (e.g.,

;

Following recent development in data-science techniques,
we are now able to improve the inversion process.
In this paper, we propose a method using compressed sensing to reconstruct the three-dimensional EMD.
Compressed sensing, first applied to MRI, is a data-processing technique that reduces required measurement data for obtaining a certain given precision of the density map

;

;

;
Lustig et al. 2007
;
Lustig et al. 2008

The key idea is that the final image of the density map is compressible, and the information, hence the number of measured Fourier signals, can be less than the number of pixels in the final image.
The success of the compressed sensing indicates that using characteristics of the EMD, there is a chance to carry out the Fermi-surface reconstruction with a much fewer number of scattering axes than it was required so far.

II
Compressed sensing

In this section, we review the fundamentals of the compressed sensing
Candes and Wakin 2008
;
Elad 2010
;
Eldar and Kutyniok 2012
;
Krzakala et al. 2012
;
Otsuki et al. 2020
as a preliminary to its application to Compton profiles.
We consider a situation where a set of experimental data
\bm{y}
is related to a physical quantity of interest,
\bm{x}
, by a linear equation
{\bm{y}=A\bm{x}}
.
Here, the sizes of vectors
\bm{y}
and
\bm{x}
are
M
and
N
, respectively, and
A
is an
{(M\times N)}
matrix.
If
M<N
, namely, if the number of equations is less than the number of unknown variables, a solution for
\bm{x}
is not uniquely determined (underdetermined systems).
Experimental errors further expand the set of possible solutions that satisfy
{\bm{y}=A\bm{x}}
within error bars.
Finding physical solutions for
\bm{x}
thus involves practical difficulties in realistic applications.

Compressed sensing solves
{\bm{y}=A\bm{x}}
for
\bm{x}
, assuming
sparsity
in the solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
.
This can be carried out by solving the optimization problem called generalized least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)
Tibshirani 1996
;
Tibshirani and Taylor 2011
.
In this case, the function
\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})
to minimize is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-A\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|B\bm{x}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},

(2)

where
B
is a non-square matrix,
and
\|\cdot\|_{\gamma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}
represents the
L_{\gamma}
norm defined by

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\|_{\gamma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i}|x_{i}|^{\gamma}\right)^{1/\gamma}.

(3)

The first term in Eq. (
2
) yields a least-square fitting,
whereas the second term imposes a penalty for the absolute value of each component of
B\bm{x}
.
This penalty imposes solutions to have more zeros in
B\bm{x}
.
A selected solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
, thus, acquires sparsity in its linear combination
B\bm{x}^{\ast}
.

Clearly, the choice of the matrix
B
is essential in the generalized LASSO.
Applications to MRI take advantage of the sparsity in the spatial variations of an expected image
Candès et al. 2006
;
Candes et al. 2006
;
Donoho 2006
;
Lustig et al. 2007
;
Lustig et al. 2008
.
The matrix
B
in this case describes differences of intensities between neighboring pixels, which is called total variation
Rudin et al. 1992
.
With the aid of LASSO, measurement time required to obtain a certain resolution in the final result has shown to be reduced considerably.
The compressed sensing based on the
L_{1}
-norm regularization has been widely applied to various measurements
Moravec et al. 2007
;
Newton 2012
;
Honma et al. 2014
;
Nakanishi-Ohno et al. 2016
;
Matsushita 2016
;
Akai et al. 2018
;
Miyama and Hukushima 2018
;
Tanaka et al. 2019
;
Yokoyama et al. 2019
and even to theoretical calculations
Nelson et al. 2013a
;
Nelson et al. 2013b
;
Zhou et al. 2014
;
Seko et al. 2014
;
Tadano and Tsuneyuki 2015
;
Otsuki et al. 2017
;
Yoshimi et al. 2019
.

The regularization parameter
\lambda
plays a crucial role in LASSO.
How to determine the optimal value of
\lambda
will be demonstrated using explicit data in Section
IV
.

Finally, a comment on the matrix
A
is in order.
For successful applications of compressed sensing,
A
should be a
dense
matrix as discussed below.
If
A
is not dense, the matrix
A
connects an element of
\bm{x}
to only a few elements of input
\bm{y}
.
Hence, if some of these elements of
\bm{y}
are missing, the corresponding element of
\bm{x}
cannot be reproduced with accuracy. This might lead to a complete failure of the procedure, resulting in entirely wrong solution of
\bm{x}
.
If
A
is a dense matrix, on the other hand, the lack of knowledge of some elements of
\bm{y}
, has only a diffuse effect over all elements of
\bm{x}
and might lead to only minor errors in the outcome.
Moreover, a dense
A
will cause a large number of degeneracies of possible solutions of
\bm{x}
, and the
L_{1}
-norm regularization will effectively work in choosing a sparse solution.

III
Reconstruction Method

III.1
Overview of reconstruction methods

The Radon transform and the equivalent inverse formula found by Cormack in early
{}^{\prime}60

Cormack 1963
;
Cormack 1964
are the seminal works which allowed the development of current CT.
Mijnarends applied the method of Cormack to the problem in the angular correlation of positron annihilation radiation
Mijnarends 1967
;
Mijnarends 1969
, which involves the same inversion problem as Eq. (
1
).
He represented
J_{\zeta}(p_{z})
and
\rho(\bm{p})
in terms of the spherical harmonics,
J_{lm}(p)
and
\rho_{lm}(p)
, respectively.
Equation (
1
) then forms an integral equation consisting of
J_{lm}(p)
, which is represented around the scattering axis, and
\rho_{lm}(p)
, which is represented in the crystal coordinate.
This complicated equation has been solved analytically.
Therefore, once
J_{lm}(p)
are obtained from experimental data, they are immediately converted into
\rho_{lm}(p)
, and thus
\rho(\bm{p})
, using the analytical solution.
This method has also been applied to the reconstruction of two-dimensional projected EMD
Kontrym-Sznajd et al. 2003
;
Kontrym-Sznajd et al. 2004
;
Ketels et al. 2021
.

An alternative approach uses the Fourier transform as is now common in practical appliations of CT.
Tanaka
et al.
applied the direct Fourier transform method to the Compton profiles with elaborate consideration of the error propagation
Tanaka et al. 2001
.
They demonstrated reconstruction of the three-dimensional EMD from experimentally measured Compton profiles of a lithium metal.
There is a room for improvement in the fact that the truncation of the Fourier series results in artificial oscillations in the final result of
\rho(\bm{p})
, which make it difficult to identify the discontinuity in
\rho(\bm{p})
(Fermi surface).

From the point of view of the compressed-sensing technique, the direct Fourier transform method is more suitable than the Cormack’s method for the following reasons.
As described in Sec.
II
, successful applications of the compressed sensing require the transformation matrix
A
to be
dense
.
The Cormack’s method is represented in the polar coordinate, in which different radial coordinates are decoupled. Therefore, the matrix
A
is sparse.
In the direct Fourier transform method, on the other hand, the matrix
A
corresponds to the Fourier basis
e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}
, in which each real-space component is represented with the whole Fourier components. Therefore, the matrix
A
is dense and satisfies the requirement of the compressed sensing.

III.2
Direct Fourier transform method

Schematic figure of the distribution of lines on which
B(\bm{r})
is obtained by the Fourier transform of the Compton profiles

We review the direct Fourier transform method by Tanaka
et al.
in Ref.
Tanaka et al. 2001
.
We define the Fourier transform of the momentum density
\rho(\bm{p})
by
B(\bm{r})
:

\displaystyle B(\bm{r})=\iiint\rho(\bm{p})e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\bm{p}.

(4)

Substituting
{\bm{r}=(0,0,z)}
in a coordinate system with
z
axis being parallel to the scattering direction
\zeta
, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{\zeta}(0,0,z)=\int J_{\zeta}(p_{z})e^{ip_{z}z}\,{\mathrm{d}}p_{z}.

(5)

Here, we used Eq. (
1
) to replace
\rho(\bm{p})
with
J_{\zeta}(p_{z})
.
The subscript
\zeta
for
B
is to indicate the direction of the
z
axis.
Compton profiles
J_{\zeta}(p_{z})
measured on several scattering directions
\zeta
yield
B(\bm{r})
on the corresponding lines in the real space as shown in Fig.
2
.
The inverse transformation of Eq. (
4
) is given by

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{p})=(2\pi)^{-3}\iiint B(\bm{r})e^{-i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\bm{r}.

(6)

In order to perform this integral, we need
B(\bm{r})
in the whole
\bm{r}
space.
In Ref.
Tanaka et al. 2001
,
B(\bm{r})
obtained on several lines (Fig.
2
) is interpolated for arbitrary
\bm{r}
, and then the inverse transformation is carried out to reconstruct
\rho(\bm{p})
.

III.3
Application of compressed sensing

In the inverse Fourier transform in Eq. (
6
), missing information in
B(\bm{r})
was filled by interpolation, which could result in a reduction of accuracy.
In the following, we directly solve Eq. (
4
) for
\rho(\bm{p})
without an interpolation by applying the compressed-sensing technique.

We represent the integral in Eq. (
4
) with a discrete sum over
\bm{p}_{j}
on a uniformly spaced grid,
\Delta p_{\mathrm{calc}}
, within a cube of volume
(2P_{\mathrm{max}})^{3}
.
The cube is taken to be sufficiently large so that the whole region where
\rho(\bm{p}_{j})
is finite is covered.
Then, Eq. (
4
) is represented as

\displaystyle B_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\rho_{j},

(7)

where
{B_{i}\equiv B(\bm{r}_{i})}
,
{\rho_{j}\equiv\rho(\bm{p}_{j})\Delta p_{\mathrm{calc}}^{3}}
, and
{A_{ij}\equiv e^{i\bm{p}_{j}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}}
.

\rho_{j}
is defined on a dense grid that covers the whole region, whereas
B_{i}
is given only on lines that are computed from several Compton profiles in Eq. (
5
).
Therefore, this linear equation forms an underdetermined system that has a fewer number of equations than the number of unknown variables.
Filling interpolated values in
B(\bm{r})
is a way to supply additional equations to make the system of linear equations solvable.

Instead of increasing the number of equations, we
reduce
the number of variables that need to be determined.
To this end, we suppose that
\rho(\bm{p})
is constant, i.e.,
{\nabla\rho(\bm{p})=0}
, in an extensive region.
This is true away from the Fermi surface, where the energy bands are either fully occupied or empty.
Such a solution can be obtained by minimizing the following function of the form of the generalized LASSO (see Section
II
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}(\{\rho_{j}\})&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\text{measured}}\left[B_{i}-\sum_{j}A_{ij}\rho_{j}\right]^{2}\\
&+\lambda\sum_{j}\sum_{\xi=p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}}\left|\sum_{j^{\prime}}(D_{\xi})_{jj^{\prime}}\rho_{j^{\prime}}\right|.\end{split}

(8)

Here, the summation in the first term is taken over
B_{i}
computed from the measured Compton profiles.

D_{\xi}
is a matrix that represents the derivative
{\partial/\partial\xi}
. With the first-order forward difference, its explicit expression is given by

{(D_{\xi}\rho)_{j}=\rho_{j(+\xi)}-\rho_{j}}
, where the index
{j(+\xi)}
denotes the coordinates one-point ahead of
\bm{p}_{j}
to the direction
\xi

1
1

1

We omitted the factor
{1/\Delta p}
because it only changes the scale of
\lambda
.
.
The second term in Eq. (
8
) forces the solution to have
{\partial\rho(\bm{p})/\partial\xi=0}
, keeping the first term within a certain range.
To what extent the second term affects the solution
is controlled by the regularization parameter
\lambda
, which will be discussed in details in Section
IV.4
.

There are two additional relations that
\rho(\bm{p})
should fulfill.
One is non-negativity

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{p})\geq 0,

(9)

and the other is the sum rule

\displaystyle\iiint\rho(\bm{p})\,{\mathrm{d}}\bm{p}=\int J_{\zeta}(p_{z})\,{\mathrm{d}}p_{z}\equiv n,

(10)

which is obtained by integrating Eq. (
1
) over
p_{z}
.
Here, the value
n
represents the total number of electrons in a unit cell.
In the discrete representation, the above two relations are written as

\displaystyle\rho_{j}\geq 0\,,\qquad\sum_{j}\rho_{j}=n,

(11)

Our goal is to minimize the function
\mathcal{F}
in Eq. (
8
) with respect to
\rho_{j}
under the constraints, Eq. (
11
).

IV.3
Solving LASSO

The momentum points
\bm{p}_{j}
for representing
\rho(\bm{p})
is constructed with a linear mesh between
-P_{\mathrm{max}}
and
P_{\mathrm{max}}
for each axis.
We set
{P_{\mathrm{max}}=3}
a.u. in this paper.
The number of points,
L
, for each axis is fixed at
{L=121}
.
The momentum step size is thus
{\Delta p_{\mathrm{calc}}=0.05}
a.u.

We apply the symmetry operations against a set of
{N=L^{3}}
momenta,
\{\bm{p}_{j}\}
, to reduce the number of points.
The crystals of elemental alkali metals have
O_{h}
point-group symmetry.
There are 48 symmetry operations and therefore only
{N_{\mathrm{irr}}\simeq N/48}
momenta are inequivalent.
With this property, we can reduce the number of grid points.
For details of how to find equivalent points and how to integrate the symmetry features into the optimization problem in Eq. (
8

For determining an optimal value of

An application to LASSO is presented, for example, in Ref.

The input data

is split into
K

{K=5}

{(K-1)}

\bm{y}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\mathrm{T}}
, and the rest one group of data is denoted by
\bm{y}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\mathrm{V}}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{V}}=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{V}}}\|\bm{y}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\mathrm{V}}-\mathcal{P}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\mathrm{V}}A\bm{x}^{\ast}\|_{2}^{2},

At

2{,}103

VI
Summary

The inverse problem for reconstruction of the three-dimensional EMD,
\rho(\bm{p})
, is underdetermined in nature, because the number of experimentally measured scattering directions is limited.
We employed the compressed sensing, which can deal precisely with undertermined systems.
The compressed sensing uses the limited information for determination of EMD in a specific regions, that is, around the Fermi surface.
This is accomplished by the sparsity condition for
\nabla\rho(\bm{p})
implemented as an optimization problem called the generalized LASSO.

We tested this new technique on the reconstruction of
\rho(\bm{p})
of bcc-Li from the Compton profiles computed by DFT.
The compressed-sensing technique allows us to reconstruct
\rho(\bm{p})
from 14 projections and to characterize the shape of the Fermi surface.
We also investigated the noise dependency in the reconstruction problem.
We show that even if the Compton profiles are perturbed by the noise (assumed errors of experimental measurements),
our method stably captures the feature around the Fermi surface.

The demonstration with bcc-Li will lead to further applications to more complicated materials.
We believe that out method based on the compressed sensing will contribute to accelerate the research into fermiology and stimulate development on the experiment side as well.

The symmetry property is integrated into computations to have only

We introduce notations
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}

\bm{\rho}

By definition,
\bm{\rho}
is obtained by upfolding
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
by

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}=F\tilde{\bm{\rho}},

(15)

where
F
is
{(N\times N_{\mathrm{irr}})}

The matrix-vector product

is then evaluated as

\displaystyle A\bm{\rho}=AF\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\equiv\tilde{A}\tilde{\bm{\rho}},

(16)

where
\tilde{A}
is a matrix that is downfolded from
A
by
{\tilde{A}\equiv AF}
.
The size of the original matrix

is
{(M\times N)}
, while
\tilde{A}
is
{(M\times N_{\mathrm{irr}})}
.
Using
\tilde{A}
, an actual evaluation of the

term,
{\|\bm{y}-A\bm{\rho}\|_{2}^{2}}
, is done with
{\|\bm{y}-\tilde{A}\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}}
, and the solution for
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
is evaluated.
Finally,
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
is upfolded into
\bm{\rho}
using Eq. (
15
).

The evaluation of
L_{1}

{(3N\times N)}
matrix
D
to
{(3N\times N_{\mathrm{irr}})}
matrix
(DF)
, which still has the scale
N
.
In order to eliminate the
N

\|D\bm{\rho}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

has the same symmetry property as
\bm{\rho}
, since

represents the derivative, which preserves symmetry.
We can therefore sum up over equivalent elements in
\bm{\rho}^{\prime}
before its

\|\bm{\rho}^{\prime}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

This leads the equality
{\|D\bm{\rho}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}=\|F^{\mathrm{T}}(D\bm{\rho})\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}

Substituting
\bm{\rho}
with
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
using Eq. (
15

\displaystyle\|D\bm{\rho}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}=\|F^{\mathrm{T}}DF\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\equiv\|\tilde{D}\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}},

(17)

where the matrix
\tilde{D}
is defined by
{\tilde{D}\equiv F^{\mathrm{T}}DF}
.
The size of
\tilde{D}
is
{(3N_{\mathrm{irr}}\times N_{\mathrm{irr}})}

Appendix C
ADMM for generalized LASSO with constraints

We consider a generalized LASSO problem with additional constraints.
The function to minimize is
\mathcal{F}(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
2
).
Two constraints, non-negativity and a sum-rule, are generalized into

\displaystyle P\bm{x}\geq 0,\quad\langle S\bm{x}\rangle=s,

(18)

where the bracket stands for
\langle S\bm{x}\rangle\equiv\sum_{j}(S\bm{x})_{j}
, and
s
is a constant.
The matrices,
A
,
B
,
P
, and
S
, have the same column size
N
, but their row sizes are, in general, all different.

We solve this optimization problem using ADMM by Boyd
et al.

Boyd 2010
.
A situation similar to the present case with constraints is considered in Refs.
Otsuki et al. 2017
;
Otsuki et al. 2020
.
Here, we generalize them to includes general four matrices
A
,
B
,
P
, and
S
.

Introducing auxiliary vectors
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
, we rewrite the function
\mathcal{F}
in Eq. (
2
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{x},\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-A\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\nu(\langle S\bm{x}\rangle-s)\\
&+\lambda\|\bm{z}\|_{1}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}+\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\gamma\sum_{j}\Theta(-z^{\prime}_{j}),\end{split}

(19)

where
\nu
is a Lagrange multiplier that enforces the sum-rule constraint.
With the conditions

\displaystyle\bm{z}=B\bm{x},\quad\bm{z}^{\prime}=P\bm{x},

(20)

Eq. (
19
) is reduced to Eq. (
2
) plus the constraints in Eq. (
18
).
The advantage of the latter form,
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(\bm{x},\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})
, is that the minimization with respect to
\bm{x}
,
\bm{z}
, and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
can be done using analytical formulas.
Therefore, our task is to make
\bm{x}
,
\bm{z}
, and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
satisfy the condition, Eq. (
20
), keeping minimizing
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
.

In the ADMM approach, the constraints, Eq. (
20
), is imposed by the augmented Lagrange multiplier method.
We here quote the update formulas from Ref.
Otsuki et al. 2020
with generalization to the four-matrices representation:

\displaystyle\bm{x}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\left(A^{\mathrm{T}}A+\mu B^{\mathrm{T}}B+\mu^{\prime}P^{\mathrm{T}}P\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(A^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{y}+\mu B^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{z}-\bm{u})+\mu^{\prime}P^{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{z}^{\prime}-\bm{u}^{\prime})+\nu S^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{d}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv\bm{\xi}_{1}+\nu\bm{\xi}_{2},

(21)

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathcal{S}_{\lambda/\mu}(B\bm{x}+\bm{u}),

(22)

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\bm{u}+B\bm{x}-\bm{z},

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathcal{P}_{+}(P\bm{x}+\bm{u}^{\prime}),

(24)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\bm{u}^{\prime}+P\bm{x}-\bm{z}^{\prime},

(25)

where
\bm{d}
is a vector with all elements being 1,

\mathcal{P}_{+}
is define by
{\mathcal{P}_{+}(x)=\max(x,0)}
,
which truncates negative values to zero,
and
\mathcal{S}
is the element-wise soft-thresholding function, which is defined for each element by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(x)=\begin{cases}0&(|x|\leq\lambda)\\
x-\mathrm{sgn}(x)\lambda&(|x|>\lambda)\end{cases}.

(26)

The Lagrange multiplier
\nu
is determined by

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{s-\langle S\bm{\xi}_{1}\rangle}{\langle S\bm{\xi}_{2}\rangle}.

(27)

The parameter
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
are penalty parameters,
which will be explained later.
As an initial condition, all vectors
\bm{x}
,
\bm{z}
,
\bm{u}
,
\bm{z}^{\prime}
, and
\bm{u}^{\prime}
are set to zero.

The most expensive computation in this calculation is the matrix inversion in Eq. (
21
).
We compute the LU decomposition of the matrix
M\equiv A^{\mathrm{T}}A+\mu B^{\mathrm{T}}B+\mu^{\prime}P^{\mathrm{T}}P
before starting the iteration
2
2

2

The Cholesky decomposition can be applied instead of the LU decomposition, because the matrix
M
is real symmetric. However, we confirmed that the LU decomposition was faster in our implementation using SciPy.
.
Using this result, linear equations are solved in each iteration to update
\bm{x}
.
The cost for the LU decomposition is
O(N^{3})
, while the cost for the updates is
O(N^{2})
, where
N
is the dimension of
\bm{x}
(
N
should be replaced with
N_{\mathrm{irr}}
when the symmetry is applied as presented in Appendix
B
).
Therefore, the computational cost and the memory storage required for the LU decomposition determine the upper limit of the system size.

Convergence of the iteration should be checked in two perspectives.
One is the residual error of the constraint (
20
), namely,
{r\equiv\|\bm{z}-B\bm{x}\|_{2}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}
.
The other is convergence of the variables, e.g.,
{s\equiv\|\bm{z}_{k+1}-\bm{z}_{k}\|_{2}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}}
, where
k
indicates the quantity at the
k
-th iteration.
A fast convergence is achieved when
r
and
s
are of the same order.
A relative magnitude between
r
and
s
depends on
\mu
:
Larger values of
\mu
reduce
r
, since
\mu
is the penalty against the constraints (
20
).
Therefore, if convergence of
r
is slower than
s
, one should increase
\mu
, and vise versa.
See Ref.
Boyd 2010
for more details.
