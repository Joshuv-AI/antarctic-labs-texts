On The Problem Of Entire Functions That Share a Small Function With
Their Difference Operators

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we study uniqueness problems for
an entire function that shares small functions of finite order with their
difference operators. In particular, we give a generalization of results in
[2,3,13]
.

2010
Mathematics Subject Classification
:30D35, 39A32.

Key words
: Uniqueness, Entire functions, Difference
operators.

1
Introduction and Main Results

In this paper, by meromorphic functions we will always mean
meromorphic functions in the complex plane. In what follows, we assume that
the reader is familiar with the fundamental results and the standard
notations of the Nevanlinna’s value distribution theory of meromorphic
functions
(\left[9\right],

\left[11\right],

\left[17\right])
. In
addition, we will use
\rho\left(f\right)
to denote the order of growth
of
f
and
\lambda\left(f\right)
to denote the exponent of convergence
of zeros of
f
, we say that a meromorphic function
\varphi\left(z\right)
is a small function of
f\left(z\right)
if
T\left(r,\varphi\right)=S\left(r,f\right),
where
S\left(r,f\right)=o\left(T\left(r,f\right)\right),
as
r\rightarrow\infty
outside of a possible exceptional set
of finite logarithmic measure, we use
S\left(f\right)
to denote the
family of all small functions with respect to
f\left(z\right)
. For a
meromorphic function
f\left(z\right),
we define its shift by
f_{c}\left(z\right)=f\left(z+c\right)
and its difference operators by

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=f\left(z+c\right)-f\left(z\right),\text{ \ \ }\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}^{n-1}\left(\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\right),\text{ }n\in\mathbb{N},\text{ }n\geq 2.

In particular,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\Delta^{n}f\left(z\right)
for the case
c=1.

Let
f
and
g
be two meromorphic functions and let
a
be
a finite nonzero value. We say that
f
and
g
share the value
a
CM
provided that
f-a
and
g-a
have the same zeros counting multiplicities.
Similarly, we say that
f
and
g
share
a
IM provided that
f-a
and
g-a

have the same zeros ignoring multiplicities. It is well-known that if
f

and
g
share four distinct values CM, then
f
is a Möbius
transformation of
g
. In
\left[15\right],
Rubel and Yang proved that if
an entire function
f
shares two distinct complex numbers CM with its
derivative
f^{\prime}
, then
f\equiv f^{\prime}
. In 1986, Jank et al.
\left(\text{see }\left[10\right]\right)
proved that for a nonconstant
meromorphic function
f
, if
f
,
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}

share a finite nonzero value CM, then
f^{\prime}\equiv f
. This result
suggests the following question:

Question 1
\left[17\right]
Let
f
be a nonconstant meromorphic function, let
a
be a finite nonzero
constant, and let
n
and
m

\left(n<m\right)
be positive integers. If
f
,
f^{\left(n\right)}
and
f^{\left(m\right)}
share
a
CM, then
can we get the result
f^{\left(n\right)}\equiv f
?

The following example
\left(\text{see }\left[18\right]\right)

shows that the answer to the above question is, in general, negative. Let
n

and
m
be positive integers satisfying
m>n+1
, and let
b
be a constant
satisfying
b^{n}=b^{m}\neq 1
. Set
a=b^{n}
and
f\left(z\right)=e^{bz}+a-1
. Then
f
,
f^{\left(n\right)}
and
f^{\left(m\right)}

share the value
a
CM, and
f^{\left(n\right)}\not\equiv f
. However,
when
f
is an entire function of finite order and
m=n+1
, the answer to
Question 1 is still positive. In fact, P. Li and C. C. Yang proved the
following:

Theorem A

\left[14\right]

Let
f
be a nonconstant entire function, let
a
be a finite nonzero
constant, and let
n
be a positive integer. If
f
,
f^{\left(n\right)}
and
f^{\left(n+1\right)}
share
the value
a
CM, then
f\equiv f^{\prime}.

Recently several papers have focussed on the Nevanlinna
theory with respect to difference operators see, e.g.
[1],

[5],\left[7\right],\left[8\right]
. Many authors started to investigate the
uniqueness of meromorphic functions sharing values with their shifts or
difference operators. In
\left[2,3\right],
B. Chen et al. proved a
difference analogue of result of Jank et al. and obtained the following
results:

Theorem B
\left[2\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let

a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)
share
a\left(z\right)
CM,
then
\Delta_{c}f\equiv\Delta_{c}^{2}f.

Theorem C
\left[3\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let

a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 2\right)

share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f.

Theorem D
\left[3\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=C\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant.

Recently in
\left[13\right],
Z. Latreuch et al. proved
the following results:

Theorem E
\left[13\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order,
and let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and

\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)

share
a\left(z\right)

CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right).

Theorem F
\left[13\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM,
then
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)=C\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant.

For the case
n=1,
A. El Farissi and others gave the
following improvement.

Theorem G
\left[6\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a non-periodic entire function of finite order, and
let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)
share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right).

Remark 1.1
The Theorem G is essentially known in [6]. For
the convenience of readers, we give his proof in the Lemma 2.4.

It is naturally now to ask the following question:
Under the hypotheses of Theorem E, can we get the result
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right)
?
The aim of this
paper is to answer this question and to give a difference analogue of result
of P. Li and C. C. Yang in
\left[14\right]
. In fact we obtain the
following results:

Theorem 1.1

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
, and let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic
entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)

share
a\left(z\right)

CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right).

Remark 1.2
The condition

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
is necessary. Let’s take for example
the entire function
f\left(z\right)=1+e^{2\pi iz}
and
c=a=1,
then
f-a

and
\Delta^{n}f-a=\Delta^{n+1}f-a=-1
have the same zeros but
\Delta f\neq f.
On the other hand, under the conditions of Theorem 1.1
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
can not be a periodic entire function
because
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
(Theorem E,
\left[13\right]
).

Example 1.1
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{z\ln 2}
and
c=1.

Then, for any
a\in\mathbb{C},
we notice that
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
a
CM for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we can easily see that
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right).
This example satisfies Theorem 1.1.

Theorem 1.2
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
, and let
a\left(z\right),

b\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
such that
b\left(z\right)

is a periodic entire function with
period
c

and
\Delta_{c}^{m}a\left(z\right)\equiv 0

\left(1\leq m\leq n\right)
. If
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
and

\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right)+b\left(z\right)+\Delta_{c}a\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right).

Remark 1.3
The condition
b\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0

is necessary in the proof of Theorem 1.2, for the case
b\left(z\right)\equiv 0,
please see Theorem 1.4.

Remark 1.4
The condition
\Delta_{c}^{m}a\left(z\right)\equiv 0
in Theorem 1.2 is more general than the condition ”periodic entire
function of period
c
”.

For the case
m=1,
we deduce the following result.

Corollary 1.1
Let
f\left(z\right)

be
a nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
, and let
a\left(z\right),

b\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be periodic entire functions with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right)+b\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right).

Example 1.2
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{z\ln 2}-2,

a=-1

and
b=1.
It is clear that
f\left(z\right)-a,

\Delta^{n}f\left(z\right)-b
and
\Delta^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-b
share
0
CM. Here, we
also get
\Delta f\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)+b-a.

Example 1.3
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{z\ln 2}+z^{3}-1,

a\left(z\right)=z^{3}
and
b=1.
It is clear that
f\left(z\right)-z^{3},

\Delta^{4}f\left(z\right)-1
and
\Delta^{5}f\left(z\right)-1
share
0
CM. On the other hand, we can verify that
\Delta f\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)+1+\Delta z^{3}-z^{3}
which satisfies Theorem
1.2.

Theorem 1.3
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv Cf\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant.

Example 1.4
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{az}
and
c=1

where
a\neq 2k\pi i

\left(k\in\mathbb{Z}\right),
it is clear that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\left(e^{a}-1\right)^{n}e^{az}
for any integer
n\geq 1.
So,
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we can easily see that
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv Cf\left(z\right)
where
C=e^{a}-1.
This example satisfies Theorem 1.3.

Corollary 1.2
Let
f\left(z\right)

be
a nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

\left(\not\equiv 0\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)

share
0
CM. If there exists a
point
z_{0}

and

an integer

m\geq 1

such that
\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z_{0}\right)=f\left(z_{0}\right)\neq 0,

then
\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right).

By combining Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 we can prove the
following result.

Theorem 1.4
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order such that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
, and let
a\left(z\right)\in S\left(f\right)
such that
\Delta_{c}^{m}a\left(z\right)\equiv 0

\left(1\leq m\leq n\right)
. If
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM,
then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv Cf\left(z\right)+\Delta_{c}a\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),
where
C
is a
nonzero constant.

2
Some lemmas

Lemma 2.1
\left[5\right]

Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}

be two arbitrary complex numbers such that
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}

and let
f\left(z\right)

be a finite order
meromorphic function. Let
\sigma

be the order of
f\left(z\right)
,
then for each
\varepsilon>0,

we have

m\left(r,\frac{f\left(z+\eta_{1}\right)}{f\left(z+\eta_{2}\right)}\right)=O\left(r^{\sigma-1+\varepsilon}\right).

By combining Theorem 1.4 in
\left[4\right]
and Theorem
2.2 in
\left[12\right],
we can prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
Let
a_{0}\left(z\right),a_{1}\left(z\right),\cdots,a_{n}\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right),

F\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)
be finite order
meromorphic functions,
c_{k}
(
k=0,\cdots,n
) be
constants, unequal to each other. If
f
is a finite order
meromorphic solution of the equation

a_{n}\left(z\right)f\left(z+c_{n}\right)+\cdots+a_{1}\left(z\right)f\left(z+c_{1}\right)+a_{0}\left(z\right)f\left(z+c_{0}\right)=F\left(z\right)

(2.1)

with

\max\left\{\rho\left(a_{i}\right),\left(i=0,\cdots,n\right),\rho\left(F\right)\right\}<\rho\left(f\right),

then
\lambda\left(f\right)=\rho\left(f\right).

Proof.

By
\left(2.1\right)
we have

\frac{1}{f\left(z+c_{0}\right)}=\frac{1}{F}\left(a_{n}\frac{f\left(z+c_{n}\right)}{f\left(z+c_{0}\right)}+\cdots+a_{1}\frac{f\left(z+c_{1}\right)}{f\left(z+c_{0}\right)}+a_{0}\right).

(2.2)

Set
\max\left\{\rho\left(a_{j}\right)\text{ }\left(j=0,\cdots,n\right),\rho\left(F\right)\right\}=\beta<\rho\left(f\right)=\rho.
Then, for any given
\varepsilon

\left(0<\varepsilon<\frac{\rho-\beta}{2}\right)
, we have

\overset{n}{\underset{j=0}{\sum}}T\left(r,a_{j}\right)+T\left(r,F\right)\leq\left(n+2\right)\exp\left\{r^{\beta+\varepsilon}\right\}=o\left(1\right)\exp\left\{r^{\rho-\varepsilon}\right\}.

(2.3)

By
\left(2.2\right)
,
\left(2.3\right)
and Lemma 2.1, we obtain

T\left(r,f\right)=T\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O\left(1\right)=m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O\left(1\right)

\leq N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{F}\right)+\overset{n}{\underset{j=0}{\sum}}m\left(r,a_{j}\right)+\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}m\left(r,\frac{f\left(z+c_{j}\right)}{f\left(z+c_{0}\right)}\right)+O\left(1\right)

\leq N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+T\left(r,\frac{1}{F}\right)+\overset{n}{\underset{j=0}{\sum}}T\left(r,a_{j}\right)+\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}m\left(r,\frac{f\left(z+c_{j}\right)}{f\left(z+c_{0}\right)}\right)+O\left(1\right)

\leq N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O\left(r^{\rho-1+\varepsilon}\right)+o\left(1\right)\exp\left\{r^{\rho-\varepsilon}\right\}.

(2.4)

By
\left(2.4\right)
, we obtain that
\rho\left(f\right)\leq\lambda\left(f\right)
and since
\lambda\left(f\right)\leq\rho\left(f\right)
for every meromorphic function, we deduce that
\lambda\left(f\right)=\rho\left(f\right).

Remark 2.1
Recently, Shun-Zhou Wu and Xiu-Min Zheng (see
\left[16\right]
) obtained Lemma 2.2 by using a different proof.

Lemma 2.3
\left[17\right]

Suppose
f_{j}\left(z\right)

(j=1,2,\cdots,n+1)
and
g_{j}\left(z\right)

(j=1,2,\cdots,n)

(n\geq 1)
are entire functions satisfying the following conditions:

\left(\text{i}\right)

\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}f_{j}\left(z\right)e^{g_{j}\left(z\right)}\equiv f_{n+1}\left(z\right);

\left(\text{ii}\right)
The order of
f_{j}\left(z\right)
is less than the order of
e^{g_{k}\left(z\right)}
for
1\leq j\leq n+1,

1\leq k\leq n.
And
furthermore, the order of
f_{j}\left(z\right)
is less than the
order of
e^{g_{h}\left(z\right)-g_{k}\left(z\right)}
for
n\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq n+1,

1\leq h<k\leq n.

Then
f_{j}\left(z\right)\equiv 0,

\left(j=1,2,\cdots n+1\right).

Lemma 2.4
\left[6\right]

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a non-periodic entire function of finite order, and
let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)
share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\equiv f\left(z\right).

Proof.
Suppose that
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv f\left(z\right).
Since
f,

\Delta_{c}f
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f
share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then we have

\frac{\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=e^{P\left(z\right)}

and

\frac{\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=e^{Q\left(z\right)}

where
P

\left(e^{P}\not\equiv 1\right)
and
Q
are polynomials. By
using Theorem B, we obtain that
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\equiv\Delta_{c}f
, which
means that

\alpha\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)-f\left(z\right)

(2.5)

is entire periodic function of period
c.
By
\left(2.5\right)
we have

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)+\alpha\left(z\right),

then

\frac{\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=1+\frac{\alpha\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=e^{P\left(z\right)},

which is equivalent to

f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)=\frac{\alpha\left(z\right)}{e^{P\left(z\right)}-1}.

(2.6)

Since
\alpha\left(z\right)
and
a\left(z\right)
are periodic
functions of period
c
, then we have

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=\alpha\left(z\right)\Delta_{c}\left(\frac{1}{e^{P\left(z\right)}-1}\right)

(2.7)

and

\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)=\alpha\left(z\right)\Delta_{c}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{e^{P\left(z\right)}-1}\right).

(2.8)

We have the two following subcases:

\left(\text{i}\right)
If
P\equiv K

\left(K\neq 2k\pi i,\text{ }K\in\mathbb{Z}\right)
, then by
\left(2.7\right)
we have
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=0.
On the other hand, by using
\left(2.5\right),

\left(2.6\right)

and
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=0,
we deduce that

f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)=\frac{-f\left(z\right)}{e^{K}-1},\text{
}K\in\mathbb{C}-\left\{2k\pi i,k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

So,

f\left(z\right)=\frac{e^{K}-1}{e^{K}}a\left(z\right).

Hence

T\left(r,f\right)=S\left(r,f\right),

which is a contradiction.

\left(\text{ii}\right)
If
P
is nonconstant and since
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right),
then

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=Cf\left(z\right).
Thus, the
proof of Theorem 1.3 is completed.

Proof of Corollary 1.2.
By Theorem 1.3 we have
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=Cf\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant.
Then

\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)=C\Delta_{c}^{m-1}f\left(z\right)=C^{m}f\left(z\right),\text{ }m\geq 1.

(3.27)

On the other hand, for
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z_{0}\right)=f\left(z_{0}\right).

(3.28)

By
\left(3.27\right)
and
\left(3.28\right)
we deduce that
C^{m}=1.

Hence
\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)
.

Proof of the Theorem 1.4.
Setting
g\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),
we can remark that

g\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}g\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)

and

\Delta_{c}^{n+1}g\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right),\text{ }n\geq 2.

Since
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
g\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}g\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}g\left(z\right)
share
0
CM. By using Theorem 1.3, we
deduce that
\Delta_{c}g\left(z\right)\equiv Cg\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant
,
which leads to

and the proof of Theorem 1.4 is completed.
