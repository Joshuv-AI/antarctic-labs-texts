Mean Field Analysis of Hypergraph Contagion Models Thanks:
Desmond John Higham, Henry-Louis de Kergorlay

Abstract
We typically interact in groups, not just in pairs. For this reason, it has recently been proposed that the spread of information, opinion or disease should be modelled over a hypergraph rather than a standard graph. The use of hyperedges naturally allows for a nonlinear rate of transmission, in terms of both the group size and the number of infected group members , as is the case, for example, when social distancing is encouraged. We consider a general class of individual-level, stochastic, susceptible-infected-susceptible models on a hypergraph, and focus on a mean field approximation proposed in [Arruda et al., Phys. Rev. Res., 2020]. We derive spectral conditions under which the mean field model predicts local or global stability of the infection-free state. We also compare these results with (a) a new condition that we derive for decay to zero in mean for the exact process , (b) conditions for a different mean field approximation in [Higham and de Kergorlay, Proc. Roy. Soc. A, 2021], and (c) numerical simulations of the microscale model.



keywords

compartmental, collective contagion, epidemiology, spectral analysis, susceptible-infected-susceptible.

Motivation and Background

Biological and social contagion processes
can be used to model the way that opinions, rumours, ideas
or diseases propagate through a community

[
9
,
16
]
.
Traditionally a graph, or network,
is used to represent the
possible routes for
person-to-person
transmission

[
11
,
13
,
19
,
24
]
.
Recent work has suggested that
it is beneficial to
account directly for the
higher-order group structures
that
arise in human-to-human interactions, using hypergraphs

[
7
,
14
,
18
]

or simplicial complexes
[
15
,
21
,
23
]
.
Indeed, beyond-pairwise interactions are also relevant
in many other social, economic and technological settings

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
10
]
.

In the context of opinion dynamics, an individual may
be affected differently if multiple members of the same
group (such as a workplace or household) express a view
than if the same number of contacts from different groups
express that view
[
15
]
; this is an example of
a
majority effect

[
17
]
.
Similarly,
in the spread of a disease,
having multiple infected contacts in the same group
may lead to a different infection rate
than having
the same number of contacts across independent groups

[
18
]
.
For example,
(unknowingly)
sharing a photocopier
with four
infected colleagues may not be four times as risky as
sharing it with
one infected colleague, if the item is cleaned regularly.
On the other hand, if there is a viral load threshold

[
8
]

then sharing a car with
four infected colleagues may be more than four times as risky
as sharing a car with one infected colleague.
Moreover the overall group size may have an effect—for a fixed classroom space, there may be a cutoff on the number students beyond which attempts at social distancing become ineffective.

For these reasons, it is natural to consider
a model of spreading that (a) uses information about the
groups present, rather than
simply the resulting pairwise interactions, and
(b) allows for the transmission rate to be
a nonlinear function of the number of
active individuals.

Particular nonlinearities of interest are the
concave, or

collective suppression
, case
[
14
]

and the threshold, or

collective contagion
, case

[
7
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
.
This leads to the
hypergraph-based model
that we describe in section
2
, and
the mean field approximation from
[
7
]
that we describe in section
3
.
Sections
4
and
5

give stability analysis for
the exact and mean field processes, respectively.
In section
6
we compare
results with those for an alternative mean field model
of
[
14
]
.
An unusual feature of the mean field model in

[
7
]
is that although it takes the form of
a deterministic ODE system
with real-valued components,
it evaluates
the nonlinear infection rate
function only at non-negative integer arguments,
just as the exact stochastic model does.
This feature complicates the analysis, but we show that it
offers concrete benefits when the nonlinearity is concave.
Illustrative computational experiments are
described in
section
7
.
Corresponding results for a more general and flexible version of the
hypergraph-based model are given in
section
8
, and conclusions appear in section
9
.

To be concrete, we describe the models
and analysis in the language of
epidemiology,
but we emphasize that the concepts and results
are relevant in other scenarios.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

for the exact model: a condition that guarantees decay to zero in mean of the infection level
(Theorem
4.3
)
and, for concave nonlinearity, a condition that guarantees

(Theorem

),

for the mean field model of

[

),

8.1
,

8.2
and

8.3
).

2
Notation and Individual-level Model

Before describing the model, we first introduce some definitions and notation.

A
hypergraph

[
6
]

is a generalization
of graph in which an edge, now called a
hyperedge
,
may join any number of vertices.
More formally,
a hypergraph is a
is a tuple
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(V,E)
, where

\displaystyle V
is a set of vertices and
\displaystyle E

is a set of nonempty
subsets of
\displaystyle V
which specifies the hyperedges.

We denote
the number of nodes
and hyperedges by

\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle m
, respectively; that is,

\displaystyle|V|=n
and
\displaystyle|E|=m
.
Assuming that the vertices and hyperedges have been ordered in some (arbitrary) way,
we use

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}

to denote the
corresponding

incidence matrix
;
here

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

has

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}=1
if node
\displaystyle i
belongs to hyperedge
\displaystyle h
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}=0
otherwise.

In our context the vertices represent individuals in a population of size
\displaystyle n
, and the hyperedges
record group interactions. For example,
a set of vertices may form a hyperedge if the
corresponding individuals live in the same household,
work in the same office or sing in the same choir.

Following the original idea in

[
5
]
,
which has also been
studied in

[
7
,
15
]
,
we use a continuous time Markov process
to track the propagation of disease through the population

in a susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) framework
.
The state vector
\displaystyle X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is such that

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=1
if vertex
\displaystyle i
is infected at time

\displaystyle t
and

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=0
otherwise.

We assume that the instantaneous recovery rate is given by a constant
\displaystyle\delta>0
, and we let

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(X(t))
denote the state-dependent instantaneous infection rate for vertex
\displaystyle i
, given
\displaystyle X(t)
; that is,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}(t+\varepsilon)=1\,|\,X(t)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}(X(t))\,\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon),&\text{\penalty\ if\penalty\ }X_{i}(t)=0,\\
1-\delta\,\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon),&\text{\penalty\ if\penalty\ }X_{i}(t)=1,\end{cases}

(1)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}(t+\varepsilon)=0\,|\,X(t)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\delta\,\varepsilon+o(\gamma),&\text{\penalty\ if\penalty\ }X_{i}(t)=1,\\
1-\lambda_{i}(X(t))\,\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon),&\text{\penalty\ if\penalty\ }X_{i}(t)=0.\end{cases}

(2)

In this way, specifying the model reduces to
defining the infection rates,
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(X(t))
.
We mention that in the standard graph setting

[
11
,
19
,
24
]
, where interactions involve only pairs of vertices,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(X(t))
is taken to be proportional to the
number of infected neighbours of vertex
\displaystyle i
at time
\displaystyle t
.
Hence, in that case, the infection rate is linear in the number of infected neighbours.
As discussed in section
1
, we are interested in the setting of group interactions
and possibly nonlinear
infection rates.

Now,
writing
\displaystyle X_{j}
rather than
\displaystyle X_{j}(t)
for convenience,
we will assume that for a given vertex
\displaystyle i
,
the contribution to the overall
infection rate from a given hyperedge

\displaystyle h
is

\beta\,{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}\,f(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{I}}_{jh}X_{j}).

(3)

Here, when
\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}=1
, so that
\displaystyle i
is a member of the hyperdge,
the argument
passed to the function
\displaystyle f

is the number of infected individuals
to which
\displaystyle i
is exposed in this hyperedge.
Hence

\displaystyle f
describes the
dependence of the infection rate
on the number of infected individuals.
The disease cannot spread unless there is at least one infected individual in the hyperedge, so we may assume throughout that
\displaystyle f(0)=0
.
The factor

\displaystyle\beta

in (
3
)
represents the inherent
infectiousness of the disease.

For example, consider a one-hour meeting
between a predefined group of co-workers
(forming a hyperedge)
that takes place in a
dedicated meeting
room.
Suppose further that, for this size of meeting room,
five
infected individuals, but no fewer,
generate sufficient viral load to pass on the infection
(perhaps through
airborne microdroplets
or through indirect contact).
Then a
suitable nonlinearity in
(
3
)
could be

\displaystyle f(x)=c\,\max\{0,x-4\}

or

\displaystyle f(x)=c\,\mathbbm{1}(x\geq 5)
,
for some constant
\displaystyle c
; these are of
collective contagion
form

[
7
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
.
Now suppose that the meeting room is in continual use,
for different groups (hyperedges) within the workforce,
and that vertex
\displaystyle i
may
participate in several meetings.
We may then take
the sum of (
3
) over all
groups (hyperedges).

For the purpose of analysis, it will be useful to
categorize these hyperedges according to their size,
so we have categories

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{2},\dots,{\mathcal{C}}_{K}

with

\displaystyle h\in{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\Leftrightarrow|h|=k
.

Then the overall infection rate for vertex
\displaystyle i
may be written

\lambda_{i}(X(t))=\beta\,\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}\,f(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{I}}_{jh}X_{j}).

(4)

It is natural to generalize the expression
(
4
) to incorporate
different types of hyperedge;
for example these may correspond
to groups that congregate in various sizes of
classroom, workspace,
residence, or
vehicle,
and groups that interact
through various kinds of
sports or leisure activities.
Each different type of hyperedge
may be given its own
function

\displaystyle f
to quantify the
dependence of the infection rate
on the number of infected individuals in that setting, and

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(X(t))

in (
4
) would generalize to include the sum over all contributions.
The analysis below extends readily to this case, at the expense of
notational complexity.
For the sake of clarity, we therefore state and prove results for the
one-type model (
4
), and in Section
8

we explain how the results extend to the multi-type model.

In
[
7
]
the authors considered a model of the form
(
1
), (
2
), (
4
)
with a particular collective contagion nonlinearity
\displaystyle f
.
(More precisely, the model in
[
7
]
is covered by the multi-type
setting of Section
8
.)
A first order, or mean field, approximation to the individual-level
model
was derived in

[
7
]
, and the dynamical behaviour of the
resulting ODE system was investigated numerically.
In the next section we
describe this mean field approach for a general
nonlinearity,
\displaystyle f
.

Later, in section
6
, we compare the performance of this model with another, simpler, mean field approximation
that was derived and studied in
[
14
]
based on the
idea of commuting the order of
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}
and
\displaystyle f
.

3
Mean Field Hypergraph Models

The rate of infection expressed in (
4
) is random.

To make large-scale simulations tractable, and to
facilitate analysis, it is natural to focus on
the evolution of the the expected processes
\displaystyle(p_{i}(t))_{t\geq 0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}(t)])_{t\geq 0}
,
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
Substituting the random rates of infection by their expectation,
gives

\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(X(t))](1-p_{i})-\delta p_{i}.

(5)

Taking expected values in (
4
), the expected rate of infection may be written

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(X(t))]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathbb{E}[f(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})]

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\mathbb{P}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh}=l).

This expression defines the expected rate exactly, but it does not
appear to be amenable to numerical simulation.
In
[
7
]
, an approximation was introduced by
assuming independence of the
\displaystyle X_{j}
, giving

\mathbb{P}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh}=l)\approx\Psi(h,l)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{J_{l}\subset h}\prod_{j\in J_{l}}p_{j}\prod_{j\in h\setminus J_{l}}(1-p_{j}),

(7)

where
\displaystyle J_{l}
runs over all possible subsets of nodes of hyperedge
\displaystyle h
, of size
\displaystyle l
.
To avoid cumbersome notation, we do not explicitly denote the dependence of
\displaystyle p_{j}
on
\displaystyle t
or the dependence
of
\displaystyle\Psi(h,l)
on the
\displaystyle p_{j}
.
With this approximation, the expected processes
\displaystyle P(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(p_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{n}
satisfy the deterministic ODE system

\frac{dP(t)}{dt}=g(P(t)),

(8)

where
\displaystyle g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

g_{i}(P(t))\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}(\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\Psi(h,l))(1-p_{i}(t))-\delta p_{i}(t).

(9)

We emphasize that, with a slight abuse of notation,
\displaystyle p_{i}(t)
is now being used to
denote a mean field approximation to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{i}(t)]
. We also note that the factors

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ih}
in (
9
) implicitly depend on

\displaystyle k
through the hyperedge
constraint
\displaystyle h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
To make the model physically reasonable we
assume that the initial conditions satisfy

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{i}(0)\leq 1
for
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n
,
and we note that

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{i}(t)\leq 1
for
\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n
then follows for all
\displaystyle t>0
.

This mean field ODE was derived and studied numerically
in
[
7
]
with
an emphasis on first-and second-order transitions,
bistability and
hysteresis.
Our aim in this work is to
derive analytical results that address a more fundamental question: under what conditions will the disease will die out?
We do this by studying the local and global stability of the
disease-free state.
In the next section, we show that it is possible to analyse the
exact expected process, and in section
5

we move on to the
mean field approximation
(
8
)–(
9
).

4
The Exact Expected Process

Here we show that, while the exact equation describing the dynamics of the expected processes (
5
) does not seem to be amenable to numerical simulation, an upper bound argument allows us to derive vanishing conditions. In the following analysis, and throughout the remaining sections, we define the symmetric matrix

\displaystyle W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
by

W_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{jh},

so that
\displaystyle W_{ij}

records the number of hyperedges containing both nodes
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
. Given a symmetric matrix
\displaystyle A
, we let
\displaystyle\lambda(A)
denote its largest eigenvalue.

Recall from (
5
) and
(
6
)
that we have, for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=\beta(1-p_{i})\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})]-\delta p_{i}.

(10)

We also define the constant
\displaystyle c_{f}
as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

c_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{x\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}}\frac{f(x)}{x}.

With this definition, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathbb{E}[f(\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh})]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\beta\,c_{f}\sum_{k=2}^{K}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\mathcal{I}_{jh}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\beta\,c_{f}\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}p_{j}.

Hence, from
(
10
),
we have the following differential inequalities, for
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,

\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}\leq c_{f}\,\beta\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}p_{j}(1-p_{i})-\delta p_{i}.

(11)

This system of differential inequalities can be analyzed after invoking the result below.

Theorem 4.1
(
[
20
]
).

Suppose that
\displaystyle u
satisfies
\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)\leq f(u(t),t)
and
\displaystyle y
satisfies
\displaystyle y^{\prime}(t)=f(y(t),t)
, with boundary condition
\displaystyle u(t_{0})=y(t_{0})
. Then

\begin{cases}\forall\ t<t_{0},\ u(t)\geq y(t)\\
\forall\ t>t_{0},\ u(t)\leq y(t).\end{cases}

This result readily extends to a system of differential inequalities as in
\displaystyle(\ref{eq: ODI})
, as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfies

\forall\ i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\},\ \forall\ t\in\mathbb{R},\ u_{i}^{\prime}(t)\leq f_{i}(u(t),t),

that
\displaystyle\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfies

\forall\ i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\},\ \forall\ t\in\mathbb{R},\ y_{i}^{\prime}(t)=f_{i}(y(t),t),

and that for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
\displaystyle u_{i}(t_{0})=y_{i}(t_{0})
.
Then for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}

\begin{cases}\forall\ t<t_{0},\ u_{i}(t)\geq y_{i}(t)\\
\forall\ t>t_{0},\ u_{i}(t)\leq y_{i}(t).\end{cases}

For our purposes, we have the following.

Corollary 1
.

If for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
\displaystyle u_{i}^{\prime}(t)\leq g_{i}(u(t))
,
\displaystyle y_{i}^{\prime}(t)=g_{i}(y(t))
and
\displaystyle u_{i}(0)=y_{i}(0)
, then for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and all
\displaystyle t\geq 0,

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)\leq y_{i}(t).

We are interested in finding conditions under which the spread of the disease predicted by
\displaystyle(\ref{eq: exact mean field model})
vanishes as
\displaystyle t\to\infty
. By Corollary
1
and
\displaystyle(\ref{eq: ODI})
, it suffices to find such conditions for the following, more simple, model

\frac{dP(t)}{dt}=\widetilde{g}(P(t)),

(12)

where
\displaystyle\widetilde{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\widetilde{g}_{i}(P(t))\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\,c_{f}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}p_{j}(t)(1-p_{i}(t))-\delta p_{i}(t).

(13)

This system can be analysed
by appealing to
[
14
, Theorem
\displaystyle 6.4
]
in the case where the infection function is the identity (which, in particular, is concave)
, and we deduce the following result.

Theorem 4.3
(Extinction in mean for the exact process).

If

\frac{\beta\,c_{f}\,\lambda(W)}{\delta}<1,

(14)

then
\displaystyle 0
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium for
\displaystyle(\ref{eq:concave upper bound dynamical system})
and hence for
\displaystyle(\ref{eq: exact mean field model})
,
that is, for all
\displaystyle i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
and all initial conditions,
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}p_{i}(t)=0
in
\displaystyle(\ref{eq: exact mean field model})
.

Considering the collective suppression case, where
\displaystyle f
is concave and
\displaystyle f(0)=0
, we have for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle f(x)\leq f(1)x
; hence
\displaystyle c_{f}=f(1)
.

For a collective contagion model
of the form
\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=c_{2}\,\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1})
for some constants

\displaystyle c_{1}\geq 1
and
\displaystyle c_{2}>0
, we have for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{N}
,
\displaystyle f(x)\leq c_{2}\,x/c_{1}
, hence
\displaystyle c_{f}\leq c_{2}/c_{1}
.

Theorem
4.3
gives a practical condition for the exact model that guarantees
decay to zero in mean of the infection level of every component.
In the next section we seek similar results for the mean field
approximation (
8
)–(
9
).
This allows us (a) to judge the accuracy of this
mean field approximation in terms of
a corresponding spectral threshold, and
(b) to get insights into the behaviour of a system that
can be
simulated directly. Also,
in section
6

we use this analysis to
compare predictions against those of
the alternative mean field model from
[
14
]
.

5
Analysis of Mean Field Hypergraph Model

Here we analyze the mean field model described in (
8
)–(
9
). We find conditions for local and global asymptotic stability of the disease-free state of the process, considering various assumptions on the infection function
\displaystyle f
, including collective contagion and collective suppression cases.
Our first result is a spectral condition for local asymptotic stability.

Theorem 5.1
(General condition for local asymptotic stability).

If

\frac{\beta\,f(1)\,\lambda(W)}{\delta}<1,

(15)

then
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium for (
8
)–(
9
).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle g(0)=0

in
(
8
)–(
9
), so
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an equilibrium.
From a standard linearization
result
[
22
]
,
local asymptotic stability
follows if every eigenvalue of the Jacobian matrix
\displaystyle\nabla g(0)
has a negative real part.
For
\displaystyle j_{0}\neq i
we compute

\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}=\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}(h,l)(1-p_{i}),\\

(16)

and along the diagonal

\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial p_{i}}=\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{i}}(h,l)(1-p_{i})-\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\Psi(h,l)-\delta.

(17)

We see that

\displaystyle\nabla g(0)=B-\delta I
, where

B_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{jh}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{j}}(h,l)|_{P=0}.

Let
\displaystyle h\in E
be a hyperedge, let
\displaystyle j_{0}\in V=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
be a node of the hypergraph, and let
\displaystyle\tilde{h}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=h\setminus\{j_{0}\}.
Using (
7
), we find

\frac{\partial\Psi(h,l)}{\partial p_{j_{0}}}=\mathcal{I}_{j_{0}h}\left(\sum_{J_{l-1}\cup\{j_{0}\}\subset h}\prod_{j\in J_{l-1}}p_{j}\prod_{j\in\tilde{h}\setminus J_{l-1}}(1-p_{j})-\sum_{J_{l}\subset\tilde{h}}\prod_{j\in J_{l}}p_{j}\prod_{j\in\tilde{h}\setminus J_{l}}(1-p_{j})\right)

and hence

with Kronecker delta notation, so that
\displaystyle\delta_{xy}=1
if
\displaystyle x=y
, and
\displaystyle\delta_{xy}=0
otherwise.
It follows that
\displaystyle B_{ij}=\beta f(1)\sum_{k=2}^{K-1}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{jh}=\beta f(1)W_{ij}
.

We see that
\displaystyle\nabla g(0)
is symmetric and

\displaystyle\lambda(\nabla g(0))=\lambda(B-\delta I)=\lambda(W)f(1)\beta-\delta
. So it suffices for local
asymptotic stability that
\displaystyle\lambda(W)f(1)\beta/\delta<1
, as stated.

The above result has the advantage that it
does not require specific assumptions on the infection model. However, it is relevant only when
the initial proportion of infected individuals is sufficiently small. We now consider particular infection models, with the aim of
constructing a global asymptotic stability result.

As discussed in section

Lemma 2
.

Let
\displaystyle\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
be a set of independent Bernoulli random variables such that for each

z_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ with probability }p_{i}\\

For every

\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{K}z_{i}=l)=\sum_{J_{l}\subset[K]}\prod_{j\in J_{l}}p_{j}+\sum_{k=l+1}^{K}(-1)^{k-l}{l+k\choose l}\sum_{J_{k}\subset[K]}\prod_{j\in J_{k}}p_{j},

where
\displaystyle J_{l}
runs over all possible subsets of
\displaystyle[K]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{1,2,\dots,K\}
of size
\displaystyle l

Proof.

Letting
\displaystyle A_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{z_{i}=1\}
for every
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,K\},
we have for
\displaystyle l\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}

\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{K}z_{i}=l)=\sum_{J_{l}\subset[K]}\mathbb{P}\left((\cap_{i\in J_{l}}A_{i})\cap(\cap_{j\in[K]\setminus J_{l}}A_{j}^{c})\right),

(18)

where
\displaystyle A^{c}
denotes the complement of event
\displaystyle A
.

Let us estimate
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{l+1}^{c}\cap\dots\cap A_{K}^{c})
, where
\displaystyle A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A_{1}\cap\dots\cap A_{l}
, thus considering without loss of generality the case
\displaystyle J_{l}=\{1,2,\dots,l\}
. Define the induced probability measure from
\displaystyle A
by

\mathbb{P}_{A}(B)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(A)}.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{l+1}^{c}\cap\dots\cap A_{K}^{c})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}_{A}(A_{l+1}^{c}\cap\dots\cap A_{K}^{c})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A)\left(1-\sum_{i=l+1}^{K}\mathbb{P}_{A}(A_{i})+\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left.\sum_{l+1\leq i<j\leq K}\mathbb{P}_{A}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})+\dots+(-1)^{K-l}\mathbb{P}_{A}(A_{l+1}\cap\dots\cap A_{K})\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A)-\sum_{i=l+1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{i})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\sum_{l+1\leq i<j\leq K}\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{i}\cap A_{j})+\dots+(-1)^{K-l}\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{l+1}\cap\dots\cap A_{K}).

Spanning over all
\displaystyle J_{l}
, we see that for each
\displaystyle i\in\{l+1,\dots,K\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{i})
is substracted in (
18
) exactly
\displaystyle l+1
times. Indeed it is counted once for each
\displaystyle J_{l}
satisfying

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exists\ i_{0}\in[K]\setminus J_{l},\ (\cap_{j\in J_{l}}A_{j})\cap A_{i_{0}}=A\cap A_{i}=A_{1}\cap\dots\cap A_{l}\cap A_{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\exists\ i_{0}\in[K]\setminus J_{l},\ J_{l}\cup\{i_{0}\}=\{1,2,\dots,l,i\},

which yields
\displaystyle{l+1\choose l}=l+1
possible choices for
\displaystyle J_{l}
. Likewise, for every
\displaystyle 1\leq i<j\leq K
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{i}\cap A_{j})
is added exactly
\displaystyle{l+2\choose l}
times in (
18
), and more generally every
\displaystyle(-1)^{k-l}\mathbb{P}(A\cap A_{j_{l+1}}\cap\dots A_{j_{k}})
is added in (
18

\displaystyle{l+k\choose l}
times. This, together with the independence of the
\displaystyle z_{i}

Using Lemma
2
, we deduce the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

We have

\sum_{l=1}^{K}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{K}}(h,l)=f(1)+\sum_{k=2}^{K-1}x_{k}(\sum_{J_{k-1}\subset[K-1]}\prod_{j\in J_{k-1}}p_{j}),

where, for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, we let

x_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=f(k)+\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{l}{2k-l\choose k-l}f(k-l).

(19)

Proof.

By Lemma
2
, we can rewrite
\displaystyle\Psi(h,l)
as

\Psi(h,l)=\sum_{J_{l}\subset[K]}\prod_{j\in J_{l}}p_{j}+\sum_{k=l+1}^{K}(-1)^{k-l}{l+k\choose l}\sum_{J_{k}\subset[K]}\prod_{j\in J_{k}}p_{j}.

(20)

From
\displaystyle(\ref{estimated prob})
, we find for
\displaystyle K\geq 2
(for
\displaystyle K=1

\displaystyle 1
)
that
\displaystyle\partial\Psi(h,l)/\partial p_{K}

takes the form

\begin{cases}1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(-1)^{k}{l+k+1\choose l}\sum_{J_{k}\subset[K-1]}\prod_{j\in J_{k}}p_{j},&\text{ if }l=1\\
\sum_{J_{l-1}\subset[K-1]}\prod_{j\in J_{l-1}}p_{j}+\sum_{k=l}^{K-1}(-1)^{k-l+1}{l+k+1\choose l}\sum_{J_{k}\subset[K-1]}\prod_{j\in J_{k}}p_{j},&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Multiplying the above by
\displaystyle f(l)
, summing over
\displaystyle l
and grouping the terms according to each

that

\sum_{l=1}^{K}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{K}}(h,l)

f(1)\ +\ \sum_{k=2}^{K-1}(f(k)+\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{l}{2k-l\choose k-l}f(k-l))(\sum_{J_{k-1}\subset[K-1]}\prod_{j\in J_{k-1}}p_{j}).

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f
is concave, then
\displaystyle x_{k}\leq 0

in (
19

for all
\displaystyle k\geq 2.

Proof.

It is clear that
\displaystyle x_{2}\leq 0
, so it remains to show that
\displaystyle x_{k}\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle k\geq 3
.
Letting
\displaystyle C_{l}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={2k-l\choose k-l}
for fixed
\displaystyle k\geq 3

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{l}C_{l}f(k-l)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\left((C_{1}f(k-1)-C_{2}f(k-2))+\dots+C_{k-1}f(1)\right),&k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2\\
-\left((C_{1}f(k-1)-C_{2}f(k-2))+\dots+(C_{k-2}f(2)-C_{k-1}f(1))\right),&k\equiv 1\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2.\end{cases}

Since
\displaystyle C_{l}f(k-l)
is decreasing in
\displaystyle l

\displaystyle k\geq 3

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(k)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}C_{k-1}f(1)=(k+1)f(1),&k\equiv 0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2\\

By the concavity of
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle f(0)=0

\displaystyle f(k)/k
are decreasing in
\displaystyle k\geq 1

\displaystyle f(k)\leq(k+1)f(1)

\displaystyle k\geq 3

f(k)\leq{k+2\choose 2}f(2)-(k+1)f(1).

Dividing both sides of the above inequality by
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle k
, while the RHS increases in
\displaystyle k
; hence it suffices to show the inequality for
\displaystyle k=3

By the concavity of
\displaystyle f
,

\displaystyle f(3)\leq 2f(2)-f(1),

hence
\displaystyle{5\choose 2}f(2)-4f(1)\geq 4f(3)

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

From the global asymptotic stability result in

\displaystyle 1^{\prime}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle P\neq 0

From (
16

using Lemma
\displaystyle 6.3

[

with diagonal matrix given by
\displaystyle\Lambda_{ii}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\Psi(h,l)\geq 0

\lambda(\nabla g(P)^{(S)})\leq\lambda(B^{(S)}-\delta I),

where
\displaystyle B_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{k}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\mathcal{I}_{jh}\sum_{l=1}^{k}f(l)\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial p_{j}}(h,l)(1-p_{i})

\displaystyle f

Comparison with Alternative Mean Field Model and Exact Model

As mentioned in section
2
,
an alternative mean field approximation model was
introduced and studied in
[
14
]
. This is given by

\frac{dP(t)}{dt}=\widehat{g}(P(t)),

(22)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\widehat{g}_{i}(P(t))\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\beta\sum_{h\in E}\mathcal{I}_{ih}f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t)\mathcal{I}_{jh}\right)(1-p_{i}(t))-\delta p_{i}(t).

(23)

The key approximation in the derivation of this model is to take
the expectation operation
inside the function
\displaystyle f
. Comparing
(
8
)–(
9
) and
(
22
)–(
23
),

one major difference is that while the infection function
\displaystyle f
is only
evaluated
over integers in
\displaystyle g
, it is
evaluated
on a continuous domain in
\displaystyle\widehat{g}
.

This leads to different factors in the
spectral bounds.

Indeed, suppose that
\displaystyle f
is concave.

Theorem
5.2
tells us that
the solution of mean field approximation model given by
\displaystyle g

in (
8
)–(
9
).
vanishes if
\displaystyle\beta\,f(1)\,\lambda(W)/\delta<1
.
For the model defined by
\displaystyle\widehat{g}
in
(
22
)–(
23
),
[
14
, Theorem 6.4]

gives the condition

\frac{\beta\,f^{\prime}(0)\,\lambda(W)}{\delta}<1

(24)

for global asymptotic stability.
In this concave setting, the slopes
\displaystyle x\mapsto(f(x)-f(0))/(x-0)
are decreasing in
\displaystyle x>0
. Since
\displaystyle f(0)=0
, we deduce that
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0}f(x)/x\geq f(1)
always holds true.
Hence, for the mean field model
(
8
)–(
9
)
we have

a less restrictive sufficient condition
for vanishing of the disease.
Moreover, the following theorem shows that a similar condition
controls the behavior of the exact solution, and hence, in this sense,
(
8
)–(
9
)
gives a more accurate approximation
than
(
22
)–(
23
)
in the concave case.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle f
is concave in the
mean field
model given by (
8
)–(
9
).

Also assume for simplicity that each node has the same,

by
\displaystyle i_{0}
; that is, for
\displaystyle j=1,2,\ldots,n

Then

Proof.

This result may be proved using the arguments in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem
\displaystyle 8.1
]
, noticing that we can substitute
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)
by
\displaystyle f(1)
.

The following corollary also holds, analogously to
[
11
]
and
[
14
, Corollary
\displaystyle 8.2
]
,

where

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle f(1)
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f

7.3
Collective contagion model: sensitivity to the initial condition

An interesting working assumption
is that only hyperedges of size three or greater are present,
and hence there are no pairwise interactions.
This circumstance may arise, for example, if we restrict attention to a workplace or school environment.

Here we look how this assumption may impact the predictive performance of the two mean field models, in the case of a collective contagion model.
We used the same infection rate functions as in
Figure
4
.
In Figures
6
,
7
, and
8

\displaystyle T=100
averaged over
\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle i_{0}
of infected individuals. We observe that the mean field model given by (
8
)–(
9
) remains relatively stable, while the behaviour of the mean field model given by (
22
)–(
23
) appears to be sensitive to the initial condition
\displaystyle i_{0}
, its predictive performance degrading if
\displaystyle i_{0}
is small (e.g., red dots in Figure

8
)–(

\displaystyle P\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while the model in (

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}(\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{I}_{ih}\leq c_{1})

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle P(0)=(i_{0})_{i=1}^{n}
sufficiently small that the threshold conditions of the above step functions are not satisfied, the infection rate expressed by (

\displaystyle 0

8
Multi-type Model

In the above results, we assumed for simplicity that a fixed infection function
\displaystyle f
applies for all hyperedges. The results, however, readily extend to a
multi-type partition model
,
where the infection rate function may depend on the category and size of the hyperedge.
As we discussed in section
2
,
the categories of hyperedge may correspond to locations, such as
households, schools, offices, shops and public transport vehicles, and
hyperedge size may have an impact on transmission if individuals
are attempting to mutually distance.
We will therefore explain how the main results change when we extend
the infection rate model.
Let us partition the hyperedges of the hypergraph into
\displaystyle S
disjoint families
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{F}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, such that to each family
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}
corresponds an infection function
\displaystyle f_{s}
. For each
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,\dots,S\}
we may further partition the hyperedges in
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}
into disjoint categories
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(s)},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{K_{s}}^{(s)}
, where a hyperedge
\displaystyle h\in\mathcal{F}_{s}
belongs to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(s)}
if and only if
\displaystyle|h|=k
.
The infection rate model
(
4
) may then be extended to

\lambda_{i}(X(t))=\beta\,\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=2}^{K_{s}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(s)}}{\mathcal{I}}_{ih}^{(s),(k)}\,f_{s}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{I}}_{jh}^{(s),(k)}X_{j}),

(26)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(s),(k)}
is the incidence matrix inducing the subhypergraph spanned by the hyperedges of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(s)}\subset\mathcal{F}_{s}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(s),(k)}_{ih}=1
if
\displaystyle h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(s)}\subset\mathcal{F}_{s}
and
\displaystyle i\in h
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(s),(k)}_{ih}=0
otherwise.

We then have the following generalization of the ODE system in (
8
)–(
9
)

\frac{dP(t)}{dt}=g(P(t)),

(27)

where
\displaystyle g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

g_{i}(P(t))=\beta\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=2}^{K_{s}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{C}_{k}^{(s)}}\mathcal{I}^{(s),(k)}_{ih}(\sum_{l=1}^{k}f_{s}(l)\Psi(h,l))(1-p_{i}(t))-\delta p_{i}(t).

(28)

Define also
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(s)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=2}^{K_{s}}\mathcal{I}^{(s),(k)}
to be the incidence matrix inducing the subhypergraph spanned by the hyperedges in
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}
, and let
\displaystyle W^{(s)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathcal{I}^{(s)}(\mathcal{I}^{(s)})^{T}
, so that
\displaystyle W^{(s)}_{ij}
records the number of hyperedges in
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}
containing both
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
. We then have the following results for the generalized partition model, which are extensions of Theorems
5.1
,
5.2
and
5.3
.

Theorem 8.1
(General condition for local asymptotic stability).

If

\frac{\beta\,\lambda\left(\sum_{s=1}^{S}f_{s}(1)W^{(s)}\right)}{\delta}<1,

(29)

then
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally asymptotic stable equilibrium for (
27
)–(
28
).

Theorem 8.2
(Global asymptotic stability for a collective suppression model).

Suppose that
\displaystyle f_{s}
is concave for all
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,\dots,S\}
.

If
(
29
) holds,

then
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally asymptotically stable for (
27
)–(
28
).

Theorem 8.3
(Global asymptotic stability for a collective contagion model).

Suppose that for each
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,\dots,S\}
,
\displaystyle f_{s}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=c_{2,s}\mathbbm{1}(x\geq c_{1,s})
, where

\displaystyle c_{1,s}\geq 2
and
\displaystyle c_{2,s}>0
.
If

\frac{\beta\,\lambda\left(\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{c_{2,s}}{c_{1,s}}W^{(s)}\right)}{\delta}<1,

then
\displaystyle 0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally asymptotically stable for (
27
)–(
28
).

9
Summary and Conclusions

Hypergraphs offer more flexibility and realism than
pairwise, graph-based models and they are relevant to many spreading processes where members of a
population form groups.
In the pairwise setting, with linear infection rates,
graph-based models have been widely studied,
and spectral stability bounds derived
[
11
,
13
,
19
,
24
]
.
Spectral analysis for
the hypergraph case was initially developed
in
[
14
]
, both for an exact individual-level
stochastic model and a deterministic mean field approximation.
In this work we focused on a more sophisticated mean field approximation that was proposed in
[
7
]
and
requires a more detailed analysis.
Although this ODE system produces real-valued trajectories,
it has the unusual feature of evaluating the
nonlinear infection rate function only at integer
arguments.
Intuitively, since the infection function is zero at the origin,
this feature is likely to make the approximation more accurate than the version in

[
14
]

in the case of concave
nonlinearity and small infection levels.
This behaviour was observed in
our computational tests
(Figures
1
–
3
and
Figure
5
)
and is backed by our theoretical
analysis—in the concave case, this mean field model produces a locally
asymptotically stable disease-free state
under the same condition as the exact model
(see Theorem
4.3
with
\displaystyle c_{f}=f(1)
and Theorem
5.2
).
However, for other types of nonlinear infection rate, it is possible for the mean field model in

[
14
]
to give a better approximation
(Figure
4
).
Hence the main conclusion from this work is that both
mean field models can be analysed
rigorously and both can provide useful information.

It is notable that the spectral conditions for
decay of the disease level
appearing in our results have the form

\frac{\beta\,c\,\lambda(W)}{\delta}<1,

for some constant
\displaystyle c
that is determined by the
type of nonlinear infection rate
(with generalized versions in section
8
).
This expression separates out different aspects of the process in a natural manner and offers a means to
inform mitigation strategies.
The parameters
\displaystyle\beta
and
\displaystyle\delta
quantify the inherent infectiousness
and recovery rate for the disease, respectively.
The constant
\displaystyle c
is affected by the way that the chance of a new infection
depends on the number of infected people in a group. This
could be controlled by changing
behavioural patterns; for example, through
face-covering or social distancing.
The factor
\displaystyle\lambda(W)
, which
summarizes the interaction structure, could be reduced by lockdown measures that restrict movement and therefore
limit physical encounters.
Hence, it would be of interest to calibrate
a hypergraph model against real data and investigate the
predictive power of these spectral bounds.
