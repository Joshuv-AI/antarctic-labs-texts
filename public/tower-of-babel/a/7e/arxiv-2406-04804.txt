Mitigation of DESI fiber assignment incompleteness effect on two-point clustering with small angular scale truncated estimators

Abstract
We present a method to mitigate the effects of fiber assignment incompleteness in two-point power spectrum and correlation function measurements from galaxy spectroscopic surveys, by truncating small angular scales from estimators. We derive the corresponding modified correlation function and power spectrum windows to account for the small angular scale truncation in the theory prediction. We validate this approach on simulations reproducing the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Data Release 1 (DR1) with and without fiber assignment. We show that we recover unbiased cosmological constraints using small angular scale truncated estimators from simulations with fiber assignment incompleteness, with respect to standard estimators from complete simulations. Additionally, we present an approach to remove the sensitivity of the fits to high k k modes in the theoretical power spectrum, by applying a transformation to the data vector and window matrix. We find that our method efficiently mitigates the effect of fiber assignment incompleteness in two-point correlation function and power spectrum measurements, at low computational cost and with little statistical loss.



1
Introduction

Contemporary galaxy spectroscopic surveys, such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)
[
1
]
, use thousands of optical fibers to collect spectra from millions of galaxies. Due to the increasing size of the collected data, cosmological measurements are becoming ever more precise and the impact of systematic effects is of growing importance. In particular, due to instrumental effects, spectroscopic surveys collect redshifts for only a fraction of the full set of targets available. For a given telescope pointing, two fibers cannot be positioned infinitely close to each other. In previous surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), this limitation was imposed by the size of the fiber mechanical holder. In the case of DESI, the patrol area of each fiber positioner is fixed by design. This limitation imposes a lower threshold on the angular separation between two observed galaxies. This induces a fiber assignment incompleteness effect that is uneven across the survey footprint and mostly affects regions with high density of targets. As a result, if not accounted for, missing galaxy pairs introduce a bias into the estimations of standard two-point statistics, namely the two-point correlation and two-point power spectrum, especially on small scales
[
2
]
. The overall effect of fiber assignment is reduced by having multiple visits at each position on the sky. In the final data release of the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS)
[
3
]
, 5% of targets were left unobserved as a result of fiber collisions
[
4
]
. In DESI, the fiber incompleteness depends on the density of each target type, its priority in the fiber assignment process and the number of passes at each point of the survey. For the initial year of DESI observations (DESI DR1)
[
5
]
, the completeness ranges between 10% in the Southern Galactic Cap to almost 100% in the equatorial region of the Northern Galactic Cap.

Various methods have been developed to correct the effect of missing observations in galaxy clustering measurements
[
6
,
7
,
2
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. The nearest neighbour method
[
6
]
assigns the number of galaxies with missing redshift as a weight to the nearest observed galaxy in angular separation. This allows us to reconstruct the underlying two-point statistics on scales much larger than the fiber collision scale, but fails at smaller scales. Pairwise-inverse-probability (PIP) weighting, introduced in
[
12
,
10
,
11
]
, weights each galaxy pair by its inverse probability of being observed. It requires running the fiber assignment algorithm hundreds of times. This is perfectly feasible for the DESI samples but it can be challenging for processing thousands of mocks for systematic studies. Furthermore, this method provides unbiased clustering measurements only in regions of the survey footprint where pairs of galaxies have a non-zero probability of being observed and techniques based on angular up-weighting were developed to circumvent this issue
[
13
,
14
]
. Other techniques propose to null purely angular modes
[
9
,
15
]
that are affected either by fiber assignment incompleteness or by photometric systematics across the survey footprint. However, these techniques null all angular modes while, in the case of fiber assignment, we expect simple completeness weighting to recover large scale angular modes leaving only small angular scales to be corrected.
[
16
]
proposed to subtract transverse modes from the power spectrum estimator, which we show does not sufficiently reduce the effect of fiber assignment incompleteness in appendix
D
.

Another approach consists in approximating the effect of fiber collisions in two-point measurements by a top-hat function as a function of transverse separation, and convolving it with the true power spectrum in Fourier space to get a power spectrum model that includes the effect of missing observations
[
2
]
. This technique was successfully applied to eBOSS ELG
[
17
]
and quasar
[
18
]
samples. This is however an average correction that does not account for the local impact of the density of targets on the fraction of missing pairs. Working in configuration space,
[
19
]
proposed to remove all pairs for which the angle between the line connecting the two galaxies of a pair and the line-of-sight is small. This was further investigated in
[
20
]
for the clustering analysis of eBOSS quasars, where it improves upon nearest neighbor up-weighting in reducing the systematic bias. Such approach has the advantage of not relying on observed galaxies to infer properties of unobserved ones, at a very low statistical cost.

In this paper, we adopt a modified version of the previous method by removing all pairs of galaxies with angular separation below the fiber collision angular scale in configuration space for the correlation function estimator and in Fourier space for the power spectrum estimator (section
2
). We modify windows (relating the aforementioned estimators to the theory prediction) correspondingly in configuration and Fourier space to account for this truncation (section
3
). Finally, we validate our procedure with full-shape fits (section
4
) and we discuss and reduce the sensitivity of the modified Fourier-space window to high-
k
modes (section
5
). Our methodology is used in the full-shape analysis of DESI DR1 data
[
21
,
22
]
.

2.3
Effect of fiber assignment incompleteness on the clustering of DESI DR1 samples

Fiber assignment incompleteness has two main effects on observed clustering in DESI. First, on angular scales larger than the patrol diameter, the density of targets that can be observed with one tile is capped in a way that depends on the details of fiber assignment (requirements on sky fibers, standard stars, subpriorities, etc.). To correct for that, we apply completeness weights, calculated at the level of each fiber and target class, that are presented in
[
34
]
and
[
43
]
. Second, below the patrol diameter angular scale, there are missing pairs that cannot be recovered with completeness weights. The aim of the method we are presenting in this paper is to mitigate this effect. Figure
2
shows the fraction of galaxy pair counts in altMTL mocks with respect to complete mocks, together with that of fiber-assigned targets with respect to all targets, as a function of angular separation
\theta

2
, SGC is less complete than NGC as a consequence of the survey strategy and observational conditions during the first year of DESI operations. Moreover, the ELG sample has the lowest completeness, with its ratio of fiber-assigned to complete pair counts dropping to about 20% at small
\theta
in SGC. In the following, we restrict ourselves to the ELG sample to test our method to mitigate the effect of fiber assignment incompleteness. In addition, we consider the
1.1<z<1.6
redshift bin, as used in the DESI DR1 fiducial analysis
[
34
]
.

From figure
2
we see that fiber assignment incompleteness affects clustering below
\theta\sim 0.05^{\circ}
for all samples. This is consistent with the DESI fiber positioner patrol diameter mentioned in section
2.1

Thus, for the remainder of this paper, we choose a threshold

to discard pair counts at

We note some difference between altMTL mocks and fiber-assigned data, further discussed in

(1)

where
s
is the comoving pair separation,
\mu
is the cosine of the angle between the vector formed by the pair of galaxies
\mathbf{s}
and the mid-point line-of-sight
(\mathbf{r}_{1}+\mathbf{r}_{2})/2

,

, and

are data - data, data - random, random - random weighted pair counts in a given

and
\mu
-bin, respectively. Pairs are weighted by the product of the individual weights of the two
objects in the pair, which themselves are the product of systematic correction weights (see
[
34
]
) and FKP weights
[
46
]
.
In practice,
\int_{\mu,RR\neq 0}
denotes a (finite) sum over
\mu
-bins for which
RR(s,\mu)\neq 0
,
|\{\mu,RR\neq 0\}|
is the sum of the width of corresponding
\mu
-bins, and
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)d\mu
is taken as the integral of Legendre multipole of order
\ell
over each
\mu
-bin.

2.5
\theta
-cut power spectrum

Let us write an estimator for the
\theta
-cut power spectrum. The standard two-point power spectrum estimator
[
48
]
is based on the FKP field:

F(\mathbf{r})=n_{D}(\mathbf{r})-\alpha_{R}n_{R}(\mathbf{r}).

(3)

where
n_{D}(\mathbf{r})
and
n_{R}(\mathbf{r})
are
the densities at comoving position
\mathbf{r}
of the observed galaxies and random catalog, respectively.
Both densities include weights to correct for various systematic effects (see
[
34
]
) and to optimize variance (FKP weights
[
46
]
):
w_{D}
,
w_{R}
.
\alpha_{R}
is the ratio of the overall weight of galaxies to that of randoms. The power spectrum multipoles are given by
[
49
]
:

P_{\ell}(k)=\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}F_{0}(\mathbf{k})F_{\ell}(-\mathbf{k})-\mathcal{N}_{\ell}

(4)

with:

F_{\ell}(\mathbf{k})=\int d\mathbf{r}F(\mathbf{r})\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\eta})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

(5)

where hat quantities denote vectors normalized to unity. The line-of-sight
\hat{\eta}
is taken to be
\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
, the position of the first galaxy of the pair with respect to the observer. The normalization term is estimated as
A=\alpha_{R}\int d\mathbf{r}n_{R}(\mathbf{r})n_{D}(\mathbf{r})
.
\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}
denotes the spherical average in a
k
-bin, and
\int d\mathbf{r}
are to be understood as a sum over a 3D-mesh onto which
n_{R}(\mathbf{r})
,
n_{D}(\mathbf{r})
and thus
F(\mathbf{r})
are estimated. The shot noise term
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
is only non-zero for the monopole:

\mathcal{N}_{0}=\frac{1}{A}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{D}}w_{D,i}^{2}+\alpha_{R}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{R}}w_{R,i}^{2}\right]

(6)

where the
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{D}}
(resp.
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{R}}
) runs over objects in the data (resp. randoms) catalog.
The
\theta
-cut power spectrum estimator is obtained by subtracting, from this estimator, the contribution from low-
\theta

\widehat{P}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{cut}}(k)=\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)-\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}(k).

(7)

To estimate
\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}(k)
, we use a direct pair counting approach (similarly to equation 26 in
[
11
]
), appropriately scaling randoms to data density, and removing contributions from self-pairs in case of autocorrelation:

\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}(k)=\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}^{DD}(k)-\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}^{DR}(k)-\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}^{RD}(k)+\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}^{RR}(k)

(8)

with:

\widehat{\Delta P}_{\ell}^{XY}(k)=i^{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\sum_{i,j,\;\theta\leq\Lambda_{\theta}}w_{X,i}w_{Y,j}j_{\ell}(k|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{i}|)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{i}})

(9)

where
w_{X,i}
,
w_{Y,j}
are particle weights, the sum
\sum_{i,j,\;\theta\leq\Lambda_{\theta}}
runs over pairs of particles with angular separation
\theta\leq\Lambda_{\theta}
, and

are spherical Bessel functions of the first kind. We compute the power spectrum with
pypower

4
4

4

https://github.com/cosmodesi/pypower

3

3
Window matrix for
\theta
-cut estimator modelling

When comparing the correlation function or power spectrum measurement from a survey to their theoretical prediction, one needs to properly model the survey footprint and selection function in order to get unbiased cosmological constraints. This is usually done through a so-called window matrix that includes the geometrical features of the survey and relates the expected value of the measurement to the theory. With
\theta
-cut statistics, we are adding a geometrical effect that needs to be modelled in the window matrix. This section presents how we modify standard windows to incorporate this cut.

3.1
Correlation function window

In general, the window matrix links the theory correlation function multipoles to the expected value of observed correlation multipoles through:

\left\langle\widehat{\xi}_{\ell}(s)\right\rangle=W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(s)\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}(s).

(10)

Let us write the
\theta
-cut window matrix
W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\rm cut}(s)
. The expected value for our implementation of the Landy-Szalay estimator in equation (
1
) is:

\begin{split}\left\langle\widehat{\xi}_{\ell}^{\rm cut}(s)\right\rangle&=\frac{2\ell+1}{|\{\mu,RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0\}|}\\
&\times\int_{RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0}d\mu\left\langle\frac{DD^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)-DR^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)-RD^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)+RR^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)}{RR^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)}\right\rangle\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)\\
&\simeq\frac{2\ell+1}{|\{\mu,RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0\}|}\int_{RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0}d\mu\frac{RR^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)\xi(s,\mu)}{RR^{\rm cut}(s,\mu)}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)\\
&=\frac{2\ell+1}{|\{\mu,RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0\}|}\int_{RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0}d\mu\xi(s,\mu)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)\end{split}

(11)

where
\xi(s,\mu)
is the theory correlation function.

Then, expanding
\xi(s,\mu)
into Legendre multipoles, we get:

\left\langle\widehat{\xi}_{\ell}^{\rm cut}(s)\right\rangle=\frac{2\ell+1}{|\{\mu,RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0\}|}\int_{RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0}d\mu\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)

(12)

i.e.:

\left\langle\widehat{\xi}_{\ell}^{\rm cut}(s)\right\rangle=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\rm cut}(s)\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}(s)

(13)

with:

W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\rm cut}(s)=\frac{2\ell+1}{|\{\mu,RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0\}|}\int_{RR^{\rm cut}\neq 0}d\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu).

(14)

In addition to the pure
\mu
-window above, we also account for the binning of the correlation function, effectively rebinning the theory
\xi_{\ell}(s)
computed with

-theory bins of

to the measurement binning of
4\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
, with weights

3.2
Power spectrum window

Now let us write the
\theta
-cut power spectrum theory. Analogous to the correlation function, the observed power spectrum is linked to the theory through its window matrix. Injecting equation (
5
) into (
4
), we get:

\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)=\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\int d\mathbf{r}\int d\mathbf{s}F(\mathbf{r})F(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{k}})-\mathcal{N}_{\ell}.

(15)

and using that
[
46
]
(with
w(\mathbf{r})
particle weights):

\left\langle F(\mathbf{r})F(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\right\rangle=\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\xi(\mathbf{s})+w(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\delta_{D}^{(3)}(\mathbf{s}),

(16)

the power spectrum expectation value reads (
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
given by equation (
6
) cancelling the above shot noise contribution
w(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\delta_{D}^{(3)}(\mathbf{s})
):

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\int d\mathbf{r}\int d\mathbf{s}\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\xi(\mathbf{s})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(17)

Using the Rayleigh plane wave expansion:

e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{s}}=\sum_{q=0}^{+\infty}i^{q}(2q+1)j_{q}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{q}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})

(18)

and (for sufficiently fine
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
sampling of the power spectrum estimator mesh):

\int\frac{d\Omega_{k}}{4\pi}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\mathcal{L}_{q}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{\delta_{\ell q}}{2\ell+1}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}),

(19)

we find:

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=i^{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int d\mathbf{r}\int d\mathbf{s}\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\xi(\mathbf{s})j_{\ell}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})

(20)

i.e.

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=i^{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int d\mathbf{r}\int s^{2}ds\int d\phi\int d\mu\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\xi(\mathbf{s})j_{\ell}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)

(21)

with
\mu=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}
and
\phi
the azimuthal angle of
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
around
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
. Injecting the multipole expansion for
\xi(\mathbf{s})
we have:

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=i^{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int d\mathbf{r}\int s^{2}ds\int d\phi\int d\mu\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)j_{\ell}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu).

(22)

In the previous equations, we have made the approximation that theory multipoles
\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}(s)
are uniquely defined by the line-of-sight chosen as
\mathbf{r}
, the position of the first galaxy of the pair with respect to us, which is only valid in the local plane-parallel approximation,
s\ll r
. This assumption leads to so-called wide-angle effects, that appear in wide-angle surveys where the maximum separation between two galaxies can be of the same order of magnitude as their distance to the observer
r
. To correct for that,
\xi(\mathbf{s})
can be expanded around in powers of
s/r

[
51
]
:

\xi(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{\ell,n}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n}\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})

(23)

where:

\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)=\frac{(-i)^{\ell}}{2\pi^{2}}\int dkk^{2}(ks)^{-n}P_{\ell}^{(n)}(k)j_{\ell}(ks)

(24)

where
P_{\ell}^{(n)}
(with
n=1
) is defined in appendix
B
(see below). Eventually the expected value of the power spectrum estimator reads:

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=i^{\ell}\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\int d\mathbf{r}\int s^{2}ds\int d\phi\int d\mu\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\sum_{n,\ell^{\prime}}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n}\xi_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)j_{\ell}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)

(25)

i.e.:

\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=4\pi\int{k^{\prime}}^{2-n}dk^{\prime}\sum_{n,\ell^{\prime}}W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k,k^{\prime})P_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k^{\prime})

(26)

where:

W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k,k^{\prime})=\frac{2\ell+1}{(2\pi)^{3}A}i^{\ell}(-i)^{\ell^{\prime}}\int d\mathbf{r}\int s^{2}ds\int d\phi\int d\mu\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})r^{-n}j_{\ell^{\prime}}(k^{\prime}s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)j_{\ell}(ks)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu).

(27)

We can rewrite
W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k,k^{\prime})
:

W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k,k^{\prime})=\frac{i^{\ell}(-i)^{\ell^{\prime}}}{2\pi^{2}}\int s^{2}ds\sum_{p}\frac{2\ell+1}{2p+1}A_{p\ell\ell^{\prime}}W_{p}^{(n)}(s)j_{\ell^{\prime}}(k^{\prime}s)j_{\ell}(ks)

(28)

with
A_{p\ell\ell^{\prime}}
defined by:

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}}(\mu)=\sum_{p}A_{p\ell\ell^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}_{p}(\mu)

(29)

and where
W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
is the configuration space window function, defined by:

W_{p}^{(n)}(s)=\frac{2p+1}{4\pi A}\int d\mathbf{r}\int d\phi\int d\mu r^{-n}\bar{n}(\mathbf{r})\bar{n}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})\mathcal{L}_{p}(\mu).

(30)

Equation (
30
) is equal to the Hankel transform of the power spectrum of the random catalog, that can be estimated through the standard power spectrum estimator from equation (
15
) (see appendix E.1 in
[
52
]
). Equations (
26
) to (
30
) hold in the standard case, with no
\theta
-cut.

To obtain the
\theta
-cut window function, we just remove the contribution
\Delta W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
of pairs at small angular separation:

W_{p}^{\rm cut,(n)}(s)=W_{p}^{(n)}(s)-\Delta W_{p}^{(n)}(s)

(31)

where
\Delta W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
can be estimated with a direct pair counting approach:

\Delta W_{p}^{(n)}(s)=\frac{2\ell+1}{A}\sum_{i,j,\;\theta\leq\Lambda_{\theta}}r_{i}^{-n}w_{R,i}w_{R,j}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{i}})

(32)

Eventually, one can relate theory
P_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}(k^{\prime})
of order
n=1
that enters equation (
26
) to the plane-parallel multipoles
P_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})
linearly through
P_{\ell}^{(1)}(k)=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{WA},(1)}(k)P_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(0)}(k)

with (see appendix
B
):

W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{WA},(1)}(k)=-i\frac{\ell\left(\ell-1\right)}{2\left(2\ell-1\right)}\left[\left(\ell-1\right)-k\partial_{k}\right]\delta_{\ell-1,\ell^{\prime}}-i\frac{\left(\ell+1\right)\left(\ell+2\right)}{2\left(2\ell+3\right)}\left[\left(\ell+2\right)+k\partial_{k}\right]\delta_{\ell+1,\ell^{\prime}}

(33)

where
\partial_{k}
is the derivative operator w.r.t.
k
. We can thus include wide-angle correction into the window:

W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime})=W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}^{(0)}(k,k^{\prime})+\frac{1}{k^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime\prime}}W_{\ell\ell^{\prime\prime}}^{(1)}(k,k^{\prime})W_{\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{WA},(1)}(k^{\prime})

(34)

such that
\left\langle\widehat{P}_{\ell}(k)\right\rangle=4\pi\int{k^{\prime}}^{2}dk^{\prime}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}}W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime})P_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})
.

In the following, plots of the

3.3
Practical computation of the window

The window function,
W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
, written in equation (
30
), is computed using
pypower
. The Fourier space window function (i.e. the Hankel transform of equation (
30
)) is computed from the random catalog using three meshes with different side lengths, to capture both small and large scales: one equal to that used to compute the power spectrum, i.e.
9\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
, one of
45\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
and one of
180\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
. The mesh size for the three windows is fixed to that of the power spectrum estimation, i.e.
1500^{3}
. Each window is computed similarly to the power spectrum in equation (
2.5
), with a fine
k
-binning of
\Delta k=2\pi/180000=3.5\;\times 10^{-5}\;h/\rm Mpc
. The three windows are then concatenated together: the
k
-range is first taken from the
180\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
window, and is then
extended at higher
k
with the
45\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
and
9\;\mathrm{Gpc}/h
windows, such that in each
k
-bin, the window with the larger number of modes in the bin is selected. This ensures that the concatenated window has a high
k
resolution in a wide range of
k
values (up to the Nyquist frequency of the smaller box, here
0.52\;h/\rm Mpc
). The Fourier space window is then Hankel-transformed to get the configuration space window
W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
(
n=0,1
), which is used to compute the window matrix
W_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(k,k^{\prime})
from equation (
34
). The contribution from small angular pairs
\Delta W_{p}^{(n)}(s)
(equation (
32
)) is computed the same way as for the power spectrum estimation, with the same binning in separation
s
(see appendix
A
). We demonstrate the validity of the window computation in the next section.

In this section we demonstrate the validity of the power spectrum window matrix computation. In order to approximate the underlying theory

of the mocks, we use the average power spectrum of the 25 cubic AbacusSummit simulations from which the ELG

4
Application to full-shape analysis

In this section we test the ability of our method to yield unbiased cosmological constraints from fiber-assigned mocks by doing a full-shape fit — as opposed to Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) analyses that only extract the BAO information from two-point measurements — of the correlation function and power spectrum multipoles with
\theta
-cut. We sample cosmological parameters posterior distributions from the average correlation function and power spectrum multipoles of the 25 AbacusSummit ELG cut-sky mocks, as well as from each individual mock, in four configurations: complete mocks, complete mocks with
\theta
-cut, altMTL mocks and altMTL mocks with
\theta
-cut. We show that, for complete mocks, the
\theta
-cut by itself introduces a negligible bias in cosmological parameter estimates with respect to DR1 statistical uncertainty. Moreover, although constraints on cosmological parameters from altMTL mocks are biased with respect to that from complete mocks when no correction is applied, we show that the
\theta
-cut allows to reduce this bias.

4.1
Mock-based covariance matrix

To generate the covariance matrix of the data, we use 1000 approximate simulations based on the Zel’dovich approximation
[
53
]
, called EZmocks, that have clustering properties matching
N
-body simulations in terms of one-point, two-point and three-point statistics
[
54
]
. The set of EZmocks we use are built from a cubic snapshot at
z=1.325
simulating ELG clustering, and sampled to match the footprint and redshift density distribution

of the DESI DR1 ELG sample. In addition, because the altMTL procedure would be too computationnally expensive to run on the 1000 EZmocks, EZmocks are processed through a fast fiber assignment algorithm
[
55

, that emulates the fiber assignment process based on shallow learning.

The power spectrum of each EZmock is computed in 0.005
h
/Mpc wide
k
-bins in the range
k\in[0,0.4]\;h/\rm Mpc
, with 15 times as many randoms as data, otherwise with the same settings as for the 25 AbacusSummit mocks in section
2.5
.
Power spectrum estimates are made with and without
\theta
-cut.
We apply the Hartlap correction
[
56

to account for the finite number of mocks used to estimate the covariance matrix. The correlation function covariance matrix is obtained similarly.

5
Removing sensitivity to high theory
k
modes

We showed in section
4.4
that using the model described in section
4.2
, fits with
\theta
-cut power spectra give unbiased constraints on cosmological parameters, suggesting that non-zero terms at high theory modes
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
in
\mathrm{W}\mathbf{t}
(coming from the non-zero columns at high
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
in
\mathrm{W}
), truncated by our theory cut
k\textsubscript{\rm t}<0.35\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
, are absorbed by stochastic parameters. While these terms are apparently not problematic in our specific case, in general we do not want the window matrix to have non-zero contributions from the theory at
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
values above our fitting range, that is the maximum
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
where the theory is reliable. An idea to avoid having diverging tails at high
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
would be to apply a smooth cut instead of the sharp truncation we have been doing. This idea of apodization is explored in appendix
C
, where we show that we would need to remove galaxy pairs at transverse separations of up to
\sim 30\;\rm Mpc/h
to get a vanishing window at
k\textsubscript{\rm t}\sim 0.3-0.4\;h/\mathrm{Mpc}
. In this section, we apply an alternative method developed in
[
62
]
that consists in performing a change of basis that forces the non-zero elements of the window to be concentrated in a
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
range as narrow as possible, while leaving the likelihood unchanged.

5.1
Transforming the window matrix to make it more compact in
k\textsubscript{\rm t}

In this subsection, we describe our method to find a transformation matrix
\mathrm{M}
to apply to the window matrix
\mathrm{W}
such that
\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}
converges towards zero at high theory modes
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
, while conserving the likelihood of the data. The log-likelihood is proportional to the
\chi^{2}
:

\chi^{2}=\left(\mathbf{d}-\mathrm{W}\mathbf{t}\right)^{T}\mathrm{C}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}-\mathrm{W}\mathbf{t}\right)

(40)

where
\mathbf{d}
is the data vector,
\mathbf{t}
is the theory, and
\mathrm{C}
the covariance matrix. Provided that M is invertible, we can write:

\chi^{2}=\left(\mathrm{M}\mathbf{d}-\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}\mathbf{t}\right)^{T}(\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}^{T})^{-1}\left(\mathrm{M}\mathbf{d}-\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}\mathbf{t}\right).

(41)

We found that when applying only the rotation M, removing the contribution of high
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
modes in the window came at the cost of highly distorting the data vector, because the sum over the
\theta
-cut window matrix columns (i.e. the integral of the window matrix over
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
) is close to zero. This motivated the addition of a corrective term to allow the window columns to have a non-zero sum. Indeed, we can modify the window matrix by:
\mathrm{W}\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{W}-\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}^{T}
, noting that for any theory
\mathbf{t}
and any vector
\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}
,
\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}^{T}\mathbf{t}
is a scalar. This means that, provided that we marginalize over
\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}
in the data vector, i.e. if we add a term
s\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}
with
s
free, we are removing sensitivity to
\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}
in the fit, and thus the additional
\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}^{T}
term in the window matrix has no impact on our results. So the
\chi^{2}
is now:

\chi^{2}=\left(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}-\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\right)^{T}\mathrm{C}^{\prime\;-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\prime}-\mathrm{W}^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\right)

(42)

with the transformed data vector, window matrix, and covariance matrix:

\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\mathrm{M}\mathbf{d}-s\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},

(43)

\mathrm{W}^{\prime}=\mathrm{M}\mathrm{W}-\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}^{T},

(44)

\mathrm{C}^{\prime}=\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}^{T},

(45)

with
s
a free parameter.

We want to find
\mathrm{M}
,
\mathbf{m}_{\rm o}
and
\mathbf{m}_{\rm t}
such that
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
is as compact as possible in k
o
, and
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}
as diagonal as possible. We define a loss function to minimize:

L(\mathrm{M},\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},\mathbf{m}_{\rm t})=L_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathrm{M},\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},\mathbf{m}_{\rm t})+L_{\mathrm{C}}(\mathrm{M},\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},\mathbf{m}_{\rm t})+L_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathrm{M})

(46)

with:

L_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathrm{M},\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},\mathbf{m}_{\rm t})=\frac{\sum_{ij}w_{ij}\left|\mathrm{W}^{\prime}_{ij}\right|}{\sum_{ij}\left|\mathrm{W}^{\prime}_{ij}\right|},

(47)

where
i
and
j
indices are spanning the different values of
\ell_{\mathrm{o}}
,
k\textsubscript{\rm o}
and
\ell_{\mathrm{t}}
,
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
respectively, and

w_{ij}=\min\left\{\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{o},i}-k_{\mathrm{t},j}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{2},\alpha\right\}+\beta\delta_{\ell_{i}\ell_{j}},

(48)

where we chose
\alpha=5
and
\beta=10
, and
\sigma_{k}
is defined to be the width at half maximum of the window peak at
k_{\mathrm{o}}=0.1\;h/\rm Mpc
.
L_{\mathrm{C}}
is defined as:

L_{\mathrm{C}}(\mathrm{M},\mathbf{m}_{\rm o},\mathbf{m}_{\rm t})=\frac{\sum_{ij}r_{ij}\left|\mathrm{R}^{\prime}_{ij}\right|}{\sum_{ij}\left|\mathrm{R}^{\prime}_{ij}\right|},

(49)

where
\mathrm{R}^{\prime}
is the correlation matrix:

\mathrm{R}^{\prime}_{ij}=\frac{\mathrm{C}^{\prime}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{C}^{\prime}_{ii}\mathrm{C}^{\prime}_{jj}}},

(50)

and:

r_{ij}=\min\left\{\left(\frac{k_{\mathrm{o},i}-k_{\mathrm{o},j}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{2},\alpha\right\}+\beta\delta_{\ell_{i}\ell_{j}}.

(51)

Finally, we define
L_{\mathrm{M}}
:

L_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathrm{M})=10\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\mathrm{M}_{ij}-1\right)^{2}

(52)

to regulate the fit by maintaining the original normalization of the window. We minimize the loss function
L

jax

6
Conclusion

We have presented a method to mitigate the effect of fiber assignment incompleteness on two-point measurements, by cutting out small angular separations from two-point estimators. We present truncated two-point estimators implementing the cut in configuration and Fourier space, and we derive the corresponding modified windows allowing to model this cut. We apply our method to high-accuracy N-body mocks designed to reproduce the clustering of the DESI DR1 ELG sample in the redshift bin
1.1<z<1.6
. We find that the truncation of small angular scales from two-point statistics allows us to recover unbiased cosmological constraints from mocks reproducing the fiber assignment incompleteness of DESI DR1, with respect to mocks with no fiber assignment incompleteness. The small size of the cut (
\theta<0.05^{\circ}
) ensures that negligible statistical power is lost while removing all galaxy pairs affected by small-scale fiber assignment incompleteness.

Although the unbiased constraints obtained when cutting out small angular separations from the power spectrum suggests that any non-zero term at high-
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
in the window matrix should be absorbed by stochastic parameters varied in the fit, we propose a method to remove such high-
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
term arising from the truncation of small scales in the power spectrum window. Our approach consists in applying some rotation to the data vector, window matrix and covariance matrix, designed to suppress large-
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
terms from the transformed window while leaving the likelihood unchanged. We define a loss function tailored to this end and fit the coefficients of the transformation matrix to minimize that loss function. We find that the transformation successfully suppresses high-
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
terms while only slightly modifying the power spectrum. This transformation ensures that the window matrix, even when truncated at
k\textsubscript{\rm t}<0.2h/\mathrm{Mpc}
, correctly models the relationship between the underlying theory power spectrum and the observed power spectrum with survey geometry, imaging masks, and fiber assignment incompleteness corrections.

Appendix A
Numerical computation of the contribution of small-
\theta
pairs

The straightforward implementation of equation (
9
) sums spherical Bessel functions (times an appropriate Legendre polynomial) for each pair of galaxies
i,\;j
within
\theta<\Lambda_{\theta}
and each mode
k
. With this implementation, the estimation of all pair counts takes more than one hour for one mock with randoms about
150
times the size of the data on one Perlmutter node. To reduce computation time, we can rather bin pairs in separation
s
(bin width
ds=0.1\;\rm Mpc/h
), and make the Bessel integral outside of the pair counts. Then the direct sums take approximately
80
seconds, while the computation of the standard mesh power spectrum estimator (
15
) typically takes about
190
seconds. Besides, when
s
increases, the total volume of the bin grows like
s^{2}ds
while the volume of pairs with
\theta<\Lambda_{\theta}
scales with
ds
, so the fractional contribution of these pairs decreases. As a result we can limit ourselves to separations
s<s_{\mathrm{max}}
. Restricting to
s_{\mathrm{max}}=100\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
helps us decrease the computing time for these direct sums to approximately
50
seconds, with minimal changes to power spectrum estimates, as shown in figure
20
.

,
\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)
and
P_{\ell}^{(n)}(k)
coefficients in the wide-angle expansion are non-vanishing only if
\ell
,
n
are either both odd or both even. Here we are computing the wide-angle expansion up to first order (
n=1
), so let us compute odd correlation function and power spectrum poles. First, let us note that, by symmetry, we must have
\xi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\left\langle\delta(\mathbf{x}_{1})\delta(\mathbf{x}_{2})\right\rangle=\left\langle\delta(\mathbf{x}_{2})\delta(\mathbf{x}_{1})\right\rangle=\xi(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{1})
, such that, starting from equation (
23
):

\xi(\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{r},\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s})=\sum_{n,\ell}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{n}\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{n,\ell}\left(\frac{s}{\left|r+s\right|}\right)^{n}\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(-\widehat{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}).

(53)

At zeroth order in
s/r
, we have:

\sum_{\ell}\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{\ell}\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(-\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})

(54)

so for
\ell
odd,
\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)=-\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)=0
. Now, at first order in
s/r
,
\widehat{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}=\mu+s/r\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)
with
\mu=\hat{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}
. Hence, expanding
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}})
in Taylor series around
\mu
, and discarding second order terms in
s/r
, we get:

\sum_{\ell}\frac{s}{r}\xi_{\ell}^{(1)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)=\sum_{\ell}\frac{s}{r}\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)(-1)^{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mu)\left(1-\mu^{2}\right)+\sum_{\ell}\frac{s}{r}\xi_{\ell}^{(1)}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(-\mu).

(55)

By differentiating the Legendre polynomials’ generating function:

g(\mu,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\mu t+t^{2}}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)t^{\ell}

(56)

with respect to
\mu
, we get that:

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)+2\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mu)=\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1}^{\prime}(\mu)+\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}^{\prime}(\mu).

(57)

Besides, Bonnet recurrence relation:

(\ell+1)\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1}(\mu)=(2\ell+1)\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)-\ell\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}(\mu)

(58)

can be differentiated and combined with equation (
57
), to obtain:

\left(\mu^{2}-1\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mu)=\ell\left[\mu\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)-\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}(\mu)\right].

(59)

Using equation (
58
) again, we get:

\left(\mu^{2}-1\right)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{\ell\left(\ell+1\right)}{2\ell+1}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1}(\mu)-\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}(\mu)\right].

(60)

Plugging this relation into equation (
55
):

\sum_{\ell}\frac{s}{r}\xi_{\ell}^{(1)}(s)\left[\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(-\mu)\right]=\sum_{\ell}\frac{s}{r}\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)(-1)^{\ell+1}\frac{\ell\left(\ell+1\right)}{2\ell+1}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1}(\mu)-\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}(\mu)\right].

(61)

Equalizing terms of same
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mu)
, odd poles read:

\xi_{\ell}^{(1)}(s)=-\frac{\ell\left(\ell-1\right)}{2\left(2\ell-1\right)}\xi_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(s)+\frac{\left(\ell+1\right)\left(\ell+2\right)}{2\left(2\ell+3\right)}\xi_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(s).

(62)

Now let’s derive the formula for power spectrum multipoles. Let us assume that:

P_{\ell}^{(1)}(k)=-\frac{\ell\left(\ell-1\right)}{2\left(2\ell-1\right)}A_{\ell}(k)+\frac{\left(\ell+1\right)\left(\ell+2\right)}{2\left(2\ell+3\right)}B_{\ell}(k)

(63)

and find
A_{\ell}(k)
and
B_{\ell}(k)
. Starting from the definition of
P_{\ell}^{(n)}(k)
:

P_{\ell}^{(n)}(k)=4\pi i^{\ell}\int dss^{2}(ks)^{n}\xi_{\ell}^{(n)}(s)j_{\ell}(ks)

(64)

injecting (
62
) (for
n=1
), and using that:

\xi_{\ell}^{(0)}(s)=\frac{(-i)^{\ell}}{2\pi^{2}}\int dkk^{2}P_{\ell}^{(0)}(k)j_{\ell}(ks),

(65)

we find:

A_{\ell}(k)=\frac{2i}{\pi}k\int dss^{3}\int dk^{\prime}k^{\prime 2}P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})j_{\ell-1}(k^{\prime}s)j_{\ell}(ks).

(66)

Following appendix F1 of
[
64
]
, let us define the following integrals:

\mathcal{I}_{p,q,n}(a,b)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int dss^{2+2n+p+q}j_{p}(as)j_{q}(bs).

(67)

We can thus rewrite:

A_{\ell}(k)=ik\int dk^{\prime}k^{\prime 2}P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})\mathcal{I}_{\ell-1,\ell,1-\ell}(k^{\prime},k).

(68)

Using equations (F4) and (F10), we find:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\ell-1,\ell,1-\ell}(k^{\prime},k)

\displaystyle=k^{\prime-\ell-1}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\left[k^{\prime\ell+1}\mathcal{I}_{\ell,\ell,-\ell}(k^{\prime},k)\right]

(69)

\displaystyle=k^{\prime-\ell-1}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\left[k^{\prime\ell+1}\frac{\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k)}{k^{\prime 2}}\right]

(70)

\displaystyle=\left(\ell-1\right)k^{\prime-3}\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k)+k^{\prime-2}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k),

(71)

hence:

A_{\ell}(k)=i\left[\left(\ell-1\right)P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k)-k\partial_{k}P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k)\right].

(72)

We follow the same procedure for
B_{\ell}(k)
:

\displaystyle B_{\ell}(k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2i}{\pi}k\int dss^{3}\int dk^{\prime}k^{\prime 2}P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})j_{\ell+1}(k^{\prime}s)j_{\ell}(ks)

(73)

\displaystyle=-ik\int dk^{\prime}k^{\prime 2}P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k^{\prime})\mathcal{I}_{\ell+1,\ell,-\ell}(k^{\prime},k),

(74)

and using equations (F6) and (F10) from
[
64
]
, we find:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\ell+1,\ell,-\ell}(k^{\prime},k)

\displaystyle=-k^{\prime\ell}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\left[k^{\prime-\ell}\mathcal{I}_{\ell,\ell,-\ell}(k^{\prime},k)\right]

(75)

\displaystyle=-k^{\prime\ell}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\left[k^{\prime-\ell}\frac{\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k)}{k^{\prime 2}}\right]

(76)

\displaystyle=\left(\ell+2\right)k^{\prime-3}\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k)+k^{\prime-2}\partial_{k^{\prime}}\delta_{D}(k^{\prime}-k).

(77)

Hence:

B_{\ell}(k)=-i\left[\left(\ell+2\right)P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k)+k\partial_{k}P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k)\right].

(78)

In conclusion, we get the odd multipoles of the wide-angle expansion of
P_{\ell}(k)
, for
n=1
:

\begin{split}P_{\ell}^{(1)}(k)=&-i\frac{\ell\left(\ell-1\right)}{2\left(2\ell-1\right)}\left[\left(\ell-1\right)P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k)-k\partial_{k}P_{\ell-1}^{(0)}(k)\right]\\
&-i\frac{\left(\ell+1\right)\left(\ell+2\right)}{2\left(2\ell+3\right)}\left[\left(\ell+2\right)P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k)+k\partial_{k}P_{\ell+1}^{(0)}(k)\right].\end{split}

(79)

Appendix C
Apodization

An idea to make the window matrix more compact in theory space is to apply a smooth cut instead of the hard cut we have applied.

For simplicity in this section we consider a cut in separation transverse to the line-of-sight
r_{\perp}
rather than a
\theta
-cut, as the two are equivalent in the small-angle approximation.
We estimate the
r_{\perp}
value that corresponds to a
\Lambda_{\theta}=0.05^{\circ}
cut by computing the fraction of missing galaxy pairs shown in figure
2
as a function of
r_{\perp}
.
Instead of a top-hat cut, i.e. a sharp truncation of all transverse separations below
\Lambda_{r_{\perp}}
, we explore a smoother cut using a tukey window, which is defined by:

W_{\mathrm{tukey}}(r_{\perp})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if \;}0\leq r_{\perp}<\Lambda_{r_{\perp}}\\
\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\cos\left(\pi\frac{(r_{\perp}^{0}-r_{\perp})}{(r_{\perp}^{0}-\Lambda_{r_{\perp}})}\right)\right]&\text{if \;}\Lambda_{r_{\perp}}\leq r_{\perp}<r_{\perp}^{0}\\
1&\text{if \;}r_{\perp}\geq\ r_{\perp}^{0}\end{cases}

(80)

with
\Lambda_{r_{\perp}}=2.1\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
and with two different values of
r_{\perp}^{0}
:
10\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
and
30\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
. Figure
21
shows the three windows (top-hat, tukey with
r_{\perp}^{0}:10\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
, and tukey with
r_{\perp}^{0}:30\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
) as a function of
r_{\perp}
. We then multiply the uncut configuration space window function with each of these windows, and compute the Fourier space window matrix. Figure
22
shows the resulting matrices at a fixed
k\textsubscript{\rm o}=0.1\;h/\mathrm{Mpc}
. We see that the small-scale cut induces oscillations in Fourier space that have an amplitude decreasing with
k\textsubscript{\rm t}
. The wider the cut is in configuration space, the higher the frequency of the oscillations are in Fourier space. We see that we need to apodize the cut up to scales of
\sim 30\;\mathrm{Mpc}/h
in order to get the Fourier space window to vanish above
k\textsubscript{\rm t}\sim 0.3-0.4\;h/\mathrm{Mpc}
, which implies discarding information at scales much larger than the original cut we meant to do.

(dark red).

Appendix D
Chained multipoles

Another idea to deal with spectroscopic systematics such as fiber assignment incompleteness effect, introduced in
[
16
]
, consists in subtracting transverse
\mu\sim 0
modes with so-called chained multipoles. Assuming that fiber assignment incompleteness can be modelled as a power injection (or depletion)
X(k)
at
\mu=0
, the observed power spectrum can be written:

\hat{P}(k,\mu)=\hat{P}^{\mathrm{complete}}(k,\mu)+X(k)\delta_{D}(\mu)

(81)

with
\hat{P}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{complete}}(k,\mu)
the ”clean” power spectrum (without fiber assignment incompleteness effect).
Taking the multipole of order
\ell
(see
[
16
]
):

\hat{P}_{\ell}(k)=\hat{P}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{complete}}(k)+\frac{2\ell+1}{2}X(k)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(0),

(82)

the next multipole order reads:

\hat{P}_{\ell+2}(k)=\hat{P}_{\ell+2}^{\mathrm{complete}}(k)+\frac{2\ell+5}{2}X(k)\mathcal{L}_{\ell+2}(0).

(83)

Chained multipoles are thus defined as:

\hat{Q}_{\ell}(k)=\hat{P}_{\ell}(k)-\frac{(2\ell+1)\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(0)}{(2\ell+5)\mathcal{L}_{\ell+2}(0)}\hat{P}_{\ell+2}(k)

(84)

such that the contamination
X(k)
is removed. Figure
23
show the comparison between the two first power spectrum multipoles and corresponding chained multipoles, averaged over the 25 ELG AbacusSummit cut-sky mocks. We see that the residuals between fiber-assigned and complete mocks is reduced but still significant in chained multipoles, suggesting that this procedure is not sufficient to remove fiber assignment incompleteness effect on the power spectrum.

Bottom
: blue lines show the difference between chained multipoles in altMTL and complete mocks, while red lines show the difference between power spectrum multipoles in altMTL and complete mocks.
