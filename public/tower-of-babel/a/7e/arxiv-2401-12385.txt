Abstract
. The class of type-two basic feasible functionals ( 𝙱𝙵𝙵 2 {\mathtt{BFF}_{2}} ) is the analogue of 𝙵𝙿 \mathtt{FP} (polynomial time functions) for type-2 functionals, that is, functionals that can take (first-order) functions as arguments. 𝙱𝙵𝙵 2 {\mathtt{BFF}_{2}} can be defined through Oracle Turing Machines with running time bounded by second-order polynomials. On the other hand, higher-order term rewriting provides an elegant formalism for expressing higher-order computation. We address the problem of characterizing 𝙱𝙵𝙵 2 {\mathtt{BFF}_{2}} by higher-order term rewriting. Various kinds of interpretations for first-order term rewriting have been introduced in the literature for proving termination and characterizing first-order complexity classes. In this paper, we consider a recently introduced notion of cost–size tuple interpretations for higher-order term rewriting and see second order rewriting as ways of computing type-2 functionals. We then prove that the class of functionals represented by higher-order terms admitting polynomially bounded cost–size interpretations exactly corresponds to 𝙱𝙵𝙵 2 {\mathtt{BFF}_{2}} .

1.
Introduction

Computational complexity classes — and in particular those relating to
polynomial time and space
[
32
,
18
]
—
capture the concept of a feasible problem, and as
such have been scrutinized with great care by the scientific community in the
last fifty years.
The fact that even apparently simple problems
(such as nontrivial separation between those classes)
remain open today has highlighted the need for a comprehensive study aimed at investigating the deep nature of
computational complexity.
The so-called implicit computational
complexity
[
13
,
48
,
54
,
21
,
8
]

fits into this picture, and is concerned with characterizations of complexity
classes based on tools from mathematical logic and the theory of programming
languages.

One of the areas involved in this investigation is certainly that of
term rewriting
[
55
]
, which has proved useful as a tool for the
characterization of complexity classes. In particular, the class
\mathtt{FP}
(i.e., of first-order functions computable in polynomial time) has been
characterized through variations of techniques originally
introduced for
termination
, e.g., the interpretation method
[
50
,
47
]
,
path orders
[
24
]
, or dependency pairs
[
27
]
.
Some examples of such characterizations can be found in
[
12
,
15
,
17
,
4
,
7
]
.

After the introduction of
\mathtt{FP}
,
it became clear that the study of computational complexity
also applies to
higher-order functionals
,
which are functions that take not only data but also other functions as inputs.
The pioneering work of
Constable
[
19
]
,
Mehlhorn
[
53
]
,
and Kapron and Cook
[
37
]

laid the foundations of the so-called higher-order complexity,
which remains a prolific research area to this day.
Some motivations for this line of work can be found for instance
in computable analysis
[
39
]
,
NP search problems
[
11
]
,
and programming language theory
[
23
]
.

There have been several proposals for a class of type-2
functionals that generalizes
\mathtt{FP}
.
However, the most widely accepted one is the class
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}

of
type-two basic feasible functionals
.
This class can be characterized based on function algebras,
similar to Cobham-style,
but it can also be described using Oracle Turing Machines.
The class
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
was then the object of study by the research community,
which over the years has introduced
a variety of characterizations, e.g.,
in terms of programming languages with
restricted recursion schemes
[
34
,
23
]
,
typed imperative languages
[
29
,
30
]
,
and restricted forms of iteration in OTMs
[
38
]
.

The studies cited above present structurally complex programming languages and
logical systems, precisely due to the presence of higher-order functions.
It is not currently known whether it is possible to give a characterization of

{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
in terms of mainstream concepts of rewriting theory,
although the latter has long been known to provide tools
for the modeling and analysis of functional programs with higher-order
functions
[
40
]
.

This paper goes precisely in that direction by showing that the interpretation
method in the form studied by
Kop and Vale
[
43
,
41
]

provides the right tools to characterize
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
More precisely,
we consider a class of higher-order rewrite systems admitting cost–size tuple interpretations
(with some mild upper-bound conditions on their cost and size components)
and show that this class contains exactly the functionals in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
Such a characterization could not have been obtained employing classical
integer interpretations as e.g. in
[
15
]

because
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
crucially relies on some conditions both on
size
and on
time
.
We believe that a benefit of this characterization is that it opens the way to effectively handling programs
or executable specifications implementing
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
functions, in full generality.
For instance,
we expect that such a characterization could be integrated into rewriting-based
tools for complexity analysis of term rewriting systems
such as e.g.,
[
5
]
.

Contributions

We consider simply-typed term rewriting systems (with partial application but without

\lambda
-abstraction), denoted as
STRS
. The contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We provide a compatibility theorem for cost-size interpretations of STRSs with respect to
innermost reduction, whose proof is simpler than that of
[
41
]
; this captures the fact
that cost-size interpretations provide safe upper bounds on the length of reduction sequences, and that the size interpretation of a term cannot increase.

•

We propose a natural definition of computation of type-2 functionals by an STRS, which can be of more general interest than the specific characterization of the class
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
that we focus on in this paper;
this is purely operational in nature and revolves around the use of rewrite rules modeling the oracle.

•

We prove a soundness result, stating that any (orthogonal) STRS with a polynomially bounded cost-size interpretation computes a type-2 functional in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
; this proof uses, in particular, a term-graph rewriting argument.

•

Conversely,
we define an encoding of polynomial time Oracle Turing Machines in an STRS
which shows that any type-2 functional in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
can be computed
by an STRS with a polynomially bounded cost-size interpretation.

Related Work

We describe here some related works in the topics addressed by this paper,
namely implicit computational complexity,
higher-order complexity classes,
higher-order rewriting systems,
and interpretations.

Implicit computational complexity refers to a line of work
aiming at characterizing complexity classes without
reference to machine models and explicit bounds on resources,
but instead by relying on logical systems and programming language restrictions.
It goes back to early work by Leivant
[
48
]
and
Bellantoni and Cook
[
13
]

and has used various methods coming in particular from
recursion theory
[
49
,
54
]
, programming language restrictions
[
35
,
42
]
,
linear logic
[
28
,
45
,
6
]
and
type systems
[
33
,
46
]
.
In the setting of term-rewriting it has taken advantage of contributions
in the area of polynomial interpretations
[
14
]

and has provided a variety of characterizations for
first-order complexity classes such as

\mathtt{FP}
and
\mathtt{PSPACE}

[
51
,
16
,
12
,
15
,
17
,
4
]
.

The class of Basic Feasible Functionals
\mathtt{BFF}
was introduced by
Cook and Kapron
[
20
]
by means of bounded typed loop programs,
and they showed that its type-2 restriction
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
coincides with a class that
had been defined by Melhorn
[
53
]
.
They later provided a machine characterization of type-2
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}

by polynomial time Oracle Turing Machines
(OTM)
[
36
,
37
]

which gave more confidence in the naturalness of this class.
Several works then provided alternative characterizations of
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
,
in particular by restricted recursion schemes in some
functional languages
[
34
,
23
]
,
or typed imperative languages with insights coming from
non-interference
analysis
[
29
,
30
]
,
or by restricted forms of iteration in OTMs
[
38
]
.

Higher-order interpretations have been introduced and investigated
in
[
57
]
in relation to termination issues
but not to complexity classes.
In
[
7
,
9
]

a notion of higher-order polynomial interpretations was proposed
which allowed to provide a characterization of the (first-order)
class
\mathtt{FP}
of polynomial time computable functions.
However, the codomain considered was the domain
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers,
not tuples, and this approach did not consider higher-order complexity.
An investigation of higher-order complexity classes employing the higher-order
interpretation method in the context of a pure higher-order functional language
was proposed in
[
31
]
.
However, this paper did not provide a characterization of the standard
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
class.
Instead, it characterized a newly proposed class
\mathtt{SFF}_{2}

(Safe Feasible Functionals) which is defined as the restriction of
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}

to argument functions in
\mathtt{FP}

(see Sect. 4.2 and the conclusion in
[
31
]
).
Another related line of work is that of
[
26
]
: the authors consider a first order
functional stream language, which allows to implement second order functionals and they study (first-order) interpretations for this language.
In this way they define a subclass of
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
but do not characterize
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
itself.
Closer to the present work, the paper
[
43
]
introduced the notion of
tuple interpretation for higher-order rewriting systems
and in particular the cost-size interpretations that we use here.
Then
[
41
]

adapted this notion to the weak call-by-value strategy,
which allowed for tighter bounds
but also relaxed the approach by separating the cost and the size components.

Technical Overview.

In this paper we see
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
(
)
as the set of those type-2 functionals
computed by an Oracle Turing Machine in polynomial time.
We recall basic definitions of such theory in
.
The main result of this paper is a characterization of the
class
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
via higher-order term rewriting.

In order to formally give a statement to this result
we need to first establish some important notions
such as the rewriting counterpart of an oracle
(see
definition

4.2
)
and a computability notion for higher-order rewriting
(see
definition

4.3
).
We state the main result in
theorem

4.5
.
It is proved in two parts.
We first prove that if any term rewriting system in this class computes
a higher-order functional, then this functional has to be in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
(
soundness
).
Conversely,
we prove that all functionals in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
are computed by this class of rewriting systems
(
completeness
).
We argue that the key ingredient towards achieving this characterization
is the ability to split the dual notions of
cost
and
size
given by
the usage of tuple interpretations.

Soundness at first seems straightforward.
From Kop and Vale
[
41
]

we know that (call-by-value) higher-order rewrite systems that admit polynomial
interpretations (with certain conditions on the interpretation of data constructors)
satisfy the property that their runtime complexity is polynomially bounded.
We could temptingly say if a term rewrite sytem computes
(in the sense of
definition

4.3
) a type-2 functional,
it must do so in a polynomial number of steps,
and hence said functional must be in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
However, this is not generally the case due to the size-explosion problem,
i.e., in a polynomial number of steps we could iterate over copied data.
We solve this issue by restricting the interpretation of data constructors
in
definition

4.4
and
by employing term graph rewriting (see
section

5.3
).
Additionally, we need to guarantee that polynomial interpretations alone are
capable of controlling the size of the calls to the oracle.
More precisely, such interpretations do not allow for unbounded repeated iteration of oracle
calls and the size of the resulting oracle call
is polynomially related to the size of its given input.
This is established by the
Oracle Subterm Lemma
(see
lemma

5.4
).
We then prove soundness in
theorem

5.16
.

To prove completeness we work on an encoding of polynomial time Oracle
Turing Machines (OTMs) in STRSs.
We proceed as follows:
we encode machine configurations as terms and machine transitions as rewriting
rules that rewrite such configuration terms.
With such encoding,
we can faithfully simulate transitions on an OTM as one or more rewriting steps
on the corresponding rewrite system.
The correctness of such simulation is the subject of
lemma

6.2
.
Notice however that simulating OTMs by STRSs is not enough.
We need to do it in polynomially many steps.
For this we provide a rewrite system that can fully simulate a run of an OTM
in polynomially many steps, which is given by
theorem

6.4
.

2.1.
Higher-Order Rewriting

We roughly follow the definition of
simply-typed term rewriting system
[
44
]
(STRS):
types are simple so they can be either a base or functional type;
terms are applicative meaning terms can be partially applied and there are no lambda expressions.
We limit our interest to second-order STRSs where all rules have base type
(which in practice means the left- and right-hand side of rules are fully applied terms).
Reduction follows an innermost evaluation strategy.
We make this intuition precise below.

First, let us define our notion of a type.
Let
\mathbb{B}
be a nonempty set of
base types

which are ranged over by
\iota,\kappa,\nu
.
The set

of

over

Types from

The
\Rightarrow

\sigma\Rightarrow\tau\Rightarrow\rho

{(\sigma\Rightarrow(\tau\Rightarrow\rho))}
.
Hence, every type
\sigma
can be written as

\sigma_{1}\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow\sigma_{n}\Rightarrow\iota
with

We may write such types as
\vec{\sigma}\Rightarrow\iota

The

of a type is:
\mathtt{ord}(\iota)=0
for

and

\mathtt{ord}(\sigma\Rightarrow\tau)=\max(1+\mathtt{ord}(\sigma),\mathtt{ord}(\tau))

Then, signatures are sets of function symbols, each of them equipped with a type.
Formally, a
signature

where

is a set of base types,

\Sigma
is a nonempty set of symbols,
and

rewriting systems that characterizes ${\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}$ --- \emph{not} to find the most general class of
term rewriting systems that may do so.
Indeed, we have already derived a variation of the results in this work for call-by-value systems
of unrestricted type order following the definitions of cost--size{} interpretation in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{kop:vale:23}{}{}]}
(see~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{val:24}{}{}, Chapter 6]}).
Hence, we have every reason to believe that the type restriction could be dropped also in the
current innermost setting, provided the ``main'' function $\mathsf{F}$ that we will identify
in \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{def:type-2-rw-computability} remains second-order, i.e., the oracle functions considered
are still first-order in such an envisioned extension.
We have not done so in this work because extending the definition of ``interpretation'' to
innermost systems of higher type orders would introduce a lot of complexity (as it also does for
call-by-value reduction), and inhibit understanding of the methodology, for arguably little gain.
\par We also \emph{suspect} that the results will extend if lambda-abstraction and beta reduction are
permitted, but we have not confirmed this as it would require a larger change, primarily in the
construction of \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{subsec:graphs}. This is also not a major restriction, since abstractions in
the right-hand side of rules can be replaced by fresh function symbols.

3.
Cost–Size Interpretations

In this section
we define cost–size interpretations for the syntax of
types and terms in the STRS format.
The interpretations we develop differ from those
in
[
41
]
in two key aspects:
here we limit ourselves to second-order systems and
the rewriting strategy is innermost instead of call-by-value.
As a consequence,
the notion of
cost
here is explicitly expressed as a function

\mathsf{cost}(\cdot)
that inductively computes the total cost of reduction.
Furthermore,
these changes require that we prove a higher-order version of the innermost
compatibility theorem.

3.1.
The Interpretation of Types and Terms

In cost–size interpretations, each base-type term is mapped to two different components:
a
size
and a
cost
. Intuitively, the size component imposes a restriction
on the shape of the normal form of a term; for example, by limiting the total number of
function symbols in the normal form, or a weighted total where some symbols are weighed
more heavily than others.
Meanwhile, the cost component is an integer that bounds the number of steps needed to
reach a normal form.
By separating these two notions, we can obtain a more fine-grained complexity bound
compared to the classical interpretation methods that map to for instance natural
numbers
[
9
,
15
,
31
]
.
For non-base type terms, the cost and size interpretations are represented by

functions
.

In this paper both the cost and size components must be elements of quasi-ordered sets,
which we define formally as follows.

Size interpretation

For sets
A
and
B
,
we write
A\longrightarrow B
for the set of functions from
A
to
B
.
A
quasi-ordered set

(A,\sqsupseteq)
consists of a nonempty set
A

and a reflexive and transitive relation
\sqsupseteq
on
A
.
For quasi-ordered sets
(A_{1},\sqsupseteq_{1})
and
(A_{2},\sqsupseteq_{2})
,
we write
A_{1}\Longrightarrow A_{2}
for the set of functions

f\in A_{1}\longrightarrow A_{2}
such that
f(x)\sqsupseteq_{2}f(y)
whenever

x\sqsupseteq_{1}y
, i.e.,

A_{1}\Longrightarrow A_{2}
is the space of
weakly monotonic
functions:
functions that preserve the quasi-ordering.

For every
\iota\in\mathbb{B}
, let a quasi-ordered set

(\mathcal{S}_{\iota},\sqsupseteq_{\iota})
be given.
We extend this to
{\mathbb{T}(\mathbb{B})}
by defining

\mathcal{S}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}=(\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\tau},\sqsupseteq_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau})

where
f\sqsupseteq_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}g

iff
f(x)\sqsupseteq_{\tau}g(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
.
So
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}
is a set of weakly monotonic functionals,
using pointwise comparison.

We assume given a fixed
size interpretation function

\mathcal{J}^{\mathsf{s}}
,
which maps each
\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma
to some
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{s}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathtt{typeOf}(\mathsf{f})}
.
If we are additionally given a valuation
\alpha
that maps each

x\in\mathbb{X}_{\sigma}
to
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}
, we can map each term

s\mathrel{:}\sigma
to an element of
\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}

naturally as follows:
(a)
{\llbracket{}x\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}=\alpha(x)
;
(b)
{\llbracket{}\mathsf{f}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{s}}
;
(c)
{\llbracket{}s\,t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}={\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}({\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})
.

(Note that the superscript
s
always indicates
size
, while we will use

c
for
cost
.)

Cost interpretation

For every type
\sigma
with
\mathtt{ord}(\sigma)\leq 2
, we define

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
as follows:

(a)

\mathcal{C}_{\kappa}=\mathbb{N}
for
\kappa\in\mathbb{B}
;

(b)

\mathcal{C}_{\iota\Rightarrow\tau}=\mathcal{S}_{\iota}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
for
\iota\in\mathbb{B}
, and

(c)

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}=\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
if
\mathtt{ord}(\sigma)=1
.

For the weak monotonicity, we again use pointwise comparison: for
f,g\in\mathcal{C}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}
we say
f\geq g
if
f(x)\geq g(x)

for every
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
.

We assume given a fixed
cost interpretation function

\mathcal{J}^{\mathsf{c}}
, which maps each
\mathsf{f}\in\Sigma
to some

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathtt{typeOf}(\mathsf{f})}
;
and for each type
\sigma
with
\mathtt{ord}(\sigma)\leq 1
, we assume given

valuations

\alpha:\mathbb{X}_{\sigma}\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{\sigma}

and
\zeta:\mathbb{X}_{\sigma}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}
.
We then define, for terms of type order
\leq 1
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\llbracket{}x\,s_{1}\cdots s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}&=&\zeta(x)({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\dots,{\llbracket{}s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})\\
{\llbracket{}\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\,t_{1}\cdots t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}&=&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta},{\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta},{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},{\llbracket{}t_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})\\
\end{array}

This is well-defined under our assumptions that all variables have a type of order

0
or
1
(so the arguments to a variable have type order
0
), and that we
can write

\mathsf{f}\mathrel{:}(\vec{\iota_{1}}\Rightarrow\kappa_{1})\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow(\vec{\iota_{k}}\Rightarrow\kappa_{k})\Rightarrow\nu_{1}\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow\nu_{l}\Rightarrow\iota
for each function symbol
\mathsf{f}
.
Observe also that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}
, when fully applied, maps
to a natural number. As the left- and right-hand sides of rules have base type,
this definition assigns a natural number as the cost interpretation for both sides of
each rule.

Intuitively, for a ground base-type term
\mathsf{f}\ s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\ t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
,

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\,t_{1}\cdots t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

expresses the cost of the reduction to normal form
not including
the cost of
first normalising each
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
. To compute the full reduction cost, we let

\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}=\sum\{{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}\mid s\mathrel{\unrhd}t\text{ and $t$ is a non-variable term of base type}\}

In addition, in our proofs it is very convenient to have a second cost measure:

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}=\sum\{{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}\mid s\mathrel{\unrhd}t\text{ and }t\text{ is a non-variable base-type term not in normal form}\}

The difference between
\mathsf{cost}
and
\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}
is that subterms
that are already in normal form may still contribute a non-zero part to the

\mathsf{cost}
measure of a term, but not to
\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}
.

Compatibility

A
cost–size interpretation

\mathcal{F}
for a second order signature

\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{B},\Sigma,\mathtt{typeOf})

is a choice of a quasi-ordered set
\mathcal{S}_{\iota}
,
for each
\iota\in\mathbb{B}
,
along with cost- and size-interpretation functions

\mathcal{J}^{\mathsf{c}}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\mathsf{s}}
defined as above.
Let
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an STRS over
\mathbb{F}
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
is
compatible
with a cost–size interpretation
if for any valuations
\alpha
and
\zeta
, we have
(a)
{\llbracket{}\ell\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}>\mathsf{cost}(r)_{\alpha,\zeta}

and
(b)
{\llbracket{}\ell\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}r\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}
,
for all rules
\ell\to r
in
\mathcal{R}
.
In this case we say such cost–size interpretation
orients
all rules
in
\mathcal{R}
.

As in all notions of interpretation where the underlying domain is that of the
natural numbers, the compatibility check is in general bound to be undecidable,
even if sound but complete techniques exist. In any case, a study about the possibility
of automating our technique is outside the scope of this paper and is thus
left to future work.

To avoid heavy notation, we will often omit the valuations
\alpha
and
\zeta

from subscripts when quantifying over them, and keep the quantification implicit.
For example, instead of writing

\forall\alpha,\zeta.\ {\llbracket{}\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,(\mathsf{s}\,x)\,y\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}>\mathsf{cost}(\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,x\,(\mathsf{mult}\,y\,(F\,x)))_{\alpha,\zeta}

we will use:

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,(\mathsf{s}\,x)\,y\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}>\mathsf{cost}(\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,x\,(\mathsf{mult}\,y\,(F\,x)))

When we do this, we will also leave
\alpha
and
\zeta
out of concreate
interpretations; for example, instead of

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,x\,y\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}=3*\alpha(x)*\alpha(y)*{\max(\alpha(F)(\alpha(x)),1)}^{\alpha(x)+1}+\alpha(x)*\zeta(F)(\alpha(x))+2*\alpha(x)+1

we will use:

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{funcProd}\,F\,x\,y\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}=3*x*y*{\max(F^{\mathsf{s}}(x),1)}^{x+1}+x*F^{\mathsf{c}}(x)+2*x+1

Where
\alpha(x)
is simply denoted as
x
for type-0 variables, and as

x^{\mathsf{s}}
for type-1 variables;
\zeta(x)
is always denoted as

x^{\mathsf{c}}
(note that, when considering the cost interpretation of rules,

\zeta(x)
only occurs for type-1 variables).

Example 3.2
.

Let us consider the signature of the STRS given in
.
We can interpret the base type
{\rm Nature}
as the natural quasi-order set

\mathcal{S}_{{\rm Nature}}=(\mathbb{N},\geq)
.
We need to give interpretations for the constructors of
{\rm Nature}
.
Let us start by size.

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{0}}^{\mathsf{s}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{s}}^{\mathsf{s}}

{\llbracket{}\ulcorner\mathsf{n}\urcorner\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}=n
for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{0}}^{\mathsf{c}}

3.2.
The Compatibility Theorem

We now prove the Innermost Compatibility Theorem for STRSs.
The proof is analogous to that in
[
41
]
with some adaptations
to the simpler definitions of
section

3.1
, which we could
make because here, terms are applicative (i.e., we do not use lambdas) and
second-order only; we also make some adaptations to the evaluation strategy
being innermost rather than call-by-value, in particular through the use of the

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(\cdot)
function.

Theorem 3.4

(Innermost Compatibility)
.

Suppose
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
is an STRS compatible with a cost–size interpretation
\mathcal{F}

(following
definition

3.1
).
Then for any valuations
\alpha
and
\zeta

we have

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}>\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(t)_{\alpha,\zeta}
and

{\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}

whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
.

Hence, if we find a suitable cost-size interpretation, which orients all the rules,
then we have a
bound
on the derivation height of any given term: that is, the
number of reduction steps we need to take to reduce any given term to normal form.
For example, suppose
s_{0}\to_{\mathcal{R}}s_{1}\to_{\mathcal{R}}\dots\to_{\mathcal{R}}s_{n}
.
Let
\alpha
and
\zeta
map all variables either to
0
or to a constant
function
\lambda\!\!\!\lambda\vec{x}.0
. Then
\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s_{0})_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s_{1})_{\alpha,\zeta}+1\geq\dots\geq\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s_{n})_{\alpha,\zeta}+n
— and therefore, necessarily

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s_{0})_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq n
.
This holds whether
s_{0}
has base type or not, since
\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(\cdot)

always maps to a natural number.
Observing that always
\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}
:

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
is an STRS compatible with a cost–size interpretation
\mathcal{F}
,
and let
s
be a term with derivation height
n
.
Then for any valuations
\alpha
and
\zeta
,

\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq n
.

In order to prove
theorem

3.4
,
we first stablish some useful lemmas.
Recall that in this paper,
all rules are of base type, i.e., they are fully applied.
Since reduction is innermost,
a rule may only be fired if the matching substitution
(i.e., the substitution
\gamma
on the base case
\ell\gamma\to r\gamma
),
maps all variables to irreducible terms.
So in the lemmas below, without loss of generality,
we restrict ourselves to this type of substitutions and notice that

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(x\gamma)=0
for any variable
x
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all terms
s\,t
with
t
of base type:

{\llbracket{}s\,t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}={\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}({\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})
and

{\llbracket{}s\,t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}={\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}({\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})

for all
\alpha,\zeta
.

Proof.

The former statement holds by definition.
The latter holds by a straightforward case analysis on the form of
s

it holds both if
s=x\,s_{1}\cdots s_{n}

and

s=\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\,t_{1}\cdots t_{n}

For the
\mathsf{f}

\mathsf{f}\mathrel{:}(\vec{\iota_{1}}\Rightarrow\kappa_{1})\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow(\vec{\iota_{k}}\Rightarrow\kappa_{k})\Rightarrow\nu_{1}\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow\nu_{l}\Rightarrow\iota

as the general type for function symbols
\mathsf{f}
, and since

has base

Hence,
{\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}
is indeed well-defined.
∎

Given a valuation
\alpha
and substitution
\gamma

\gamma

With that in mind we start with some substitution lemmata.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\gamma
be a substitution mapping all variables to irreducible terms
and
\alpha

Then, for any term

{\llbracket{}s\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}={\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

By induction on the structure of
s
.

•

If
s
is a variable,
we have
{\llbracket{}x\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}=\alpha^{\gamma}(x)={\llbracket{}x\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}

•

If

we have

\displaystyle{\llbracket{}(t\,u)\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}

\displaystyle={\llbracket{}t\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}({\llbracket{}u\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\overset{IH}{=}{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}({\llbracket{}u\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}})={\llbracket{}t\,u\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}

∎

We can now prove the

3.4

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an STRS compatible with a cost–size interpretation following

,

{\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}

.

Proof.

By induction on the form of
s
.

In the base case, we have

by
\ell\gamma\to r\gamma
.
Then we combine the substitution lemma (
lemma

3.7
) with the compatibility

{\llbracket{}\ell\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}r\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle{\llbracket{}\ell\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}={\llbracket{}\ell\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}r\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}}={\llbracket{}r\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}

In the application case,

In the second case,

{\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}

∎

Lemma 3.9

\alpha,\gamma

By induction on the size of
s
. We consider two cases:

•

For the first case, we get

and

–

If

\displaystyle{\llbracket{}(x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{n})\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

\displaystyle{\llbracket{}(\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\ldots s_{k}\,t_{1}\ldots t_{n})\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{IH, \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lemma:subst-size}}}{=}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}},{\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}},{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}},{\llbracket{}t_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}},\ldots,{\llbracket{}t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma}})

\displaystyle={\llbracket{}\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\ldots s_{k}\,t_{1}\ldots t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

∎

Next, we connect the relationship between the two cost functions

Lemma 3.10
.

For any term

of type order

or

, and any

\gamma

Proof.

We again consider two cases:

•

For the first case, let
s=x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{n}
.
If
n=0
then
\mathsf{cost}(x)_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}=0

by definition, and since we assumed that
\gamma(x)
is in normal form, also

\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(x\gamma)_{\alpha,\zeta}=0
.
If

and
s
has base type, then

\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}=\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}(x)({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}},\dots,{\llbracket{}s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}})+\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost}(s_{i})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}={\llbracket{}\gamma(x)\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}({\llbracket{}s_{1}\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\dots,{\llbracket{}s_{n}\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})+\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost}(s_{i})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}
by Lemmas

and

By Lemma
3.6
and the induction hypothesis this

\geq{\llbracket{}(x\gamma)\,(s_{1}\gamma)\cdots(s_{n}\gamma)\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}+\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost^{\prime}}(s_{i}\gamma)_{\alpha,\zeta}

Since

, or

and we are done regardless.
If

does not have base type, we complete quickly with the induction hypothesis.

•

For the second case, let

We have two cases whether

is in normal form or not.

\displaystyle\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{cost}(\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\ldots s_{k}\,t_{1}\ldots t_{n})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle={\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{cost}(s_{i})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost}(t_{j})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:cost-subst-base}}}{=}{\llbracket{}s\gamma\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{cost}(s_{i})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost}(t_{j})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{cost}(s)_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{cost}(s_{i})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{cost}(t_{j})_{\alpha^{\gamma},\zeta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}}

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an STRS compatible with a cost–size interpretation following
definition

3.1

and
s,t
be type-1 terms of the same type, such that

s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
.

Then
{\llbracket{}s\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq{\llbracket{}t\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}
.

Proof.

By induction on the size of
s
. There are two cases.

•

First,
s=x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{n}
.
We can write
x\mathrel{:}\iota_{1}\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow\iota_{k}\Rightarrow\kappa
and,

s
is type-1 (so

.
Then
t=x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{i}^{\prime}\ldots s_{n}

s_{i}\to_{\mathcal{R}}s_{i}^{\prime}
.
Hence, by

3.8
and monotonicity of

\displaystyle{\llbracket{}x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{i}\ldots s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

\displaystyle=\zeta(x)({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{i}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\sqsupseteq\zeta(x)({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{i}^{\prime}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle={\llbracket{}x\,s_{1}\ldots s_{i}^{\prime}\ldots s_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

•

For the second part,

,
recall that rules are of base type, and therefore reduction does not occur at head position.

,

In the first case,

\begin{array}[]{ll}&{\llbracket{}\mathsf{f}\,s_{1}\ldots s_{i}\ldots s_{k}\,t_{1}\ldots t_{n}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}\\

\end{array}

because
{\llbracket{}s_{i}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq{\llbracket{}s_{i}^{\prime}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}
by

lemma

3.8
,

{\llbracket{}s_{i}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}\geq{\llbracket{}s_{i}^{\prime}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta}

by the induction hypothesis,
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}
is weakly monotonic.
For the second case, we only need
lemma

3.8
and monotonicity, not the induction hypothesis.

Proof.

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}({\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta},{\llbracket{}s_{1}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{i}^{\prime}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta},{\llbracket{}s_{i}^{\prime}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha}\ldots,{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\alpha,\zeta},{\llbracket{}s_{k}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha},{\llbracket{}\vec{t}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}_{\alpha})

4.
From Higher-Order Rewriting to

{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
and Back

The main result of this paper roughly states that
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}

consists exactly of those type-2 functionals computed by
an STRS compatible with a cost–size tuple interpretation, whose main function is
polynomially bounded.
To formally state this result,
we must first define what it means for an STRS to compute a type-2 functional
and define precisely the class of cost–size interpretations we are interested in.
The challenge is that such an STRS is required to do what it is supposed to do

for every
input, and the inputs not only consist of words, but also
of functions on words. We thus have to find a way to encode type-1 functions
seen as inputs to a type-2 program.

Indeed,
let us start by encoding words in
W
as terms.
We let
\mathsf{bit},\mathsf{word}\in\mathbb{B}
and introduce symbols

\mathsf{o},\mathsf{i}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{bit}
and
\mathsf{[]}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word},\ \mathsf{::}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{bit}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
(where
\mathsf{::}
will be used in
infix notation).
Then for instance
001
is encoded as the term

\mathsf{o}\mathsf{::}(\mathsf{o}\mathsf{::}(\mathsf{i}\mathsf{::}\mathsf{[]}))
.
In practice, we usually employ the cleaner list-like notation

[\mathsf{o};\mathsf{o};\mathsf{i}]
.
Let
\underline{\mathsf{w}}
denote the
term encoding of a word
w
.

(Implementing Binary Addition)
.

\mathsf{[]}

The size of a binary word is its length.
The cost of adding two numbers is bounded by
2+7*\langle\text{the length of
the longest}\rangle

Next, we encode type-1 functions as a possibly infinite set of one-step
rewrite rules.

Definition 4.2
.

Consider a type-1 function
f:W\longrightarrow W
and
let
\mathsf{S}_{f}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
be a fresh function symbol.
A set of rules
\mathcal{R}_{f}

defines
a function

f:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
by way of the symbol
\mathsf{S}_{f}

if and only if

\mathcal{R}_{f}
contains exactly the rules

\mathsf{S}_{\mathsf{f}}\,\underline{\mathsf{n}}\to\underline{\mathsf{m}}

where
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
m=f(n)
.

Intuitively,
this infinite set of rules is the rewriting counterpart of an oracle
f
.
Indeed, in a single rewrite step
\mathsf{S}_{\mathsf{f}}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}

rewrites to the value
\underline{\mathsf{f(x)}}
.
Henceforth,
we assume given that our STRS
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
at hand
is such that
\mathbb{F}
contains

\mathsf{o},\mathsf{i},\mathsf{[]},\mathsf{::}
typed as above
and a
distinguished
symbol
\mathsf{F}\mathrel{:}{(\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word})}^{k}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}^{l}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
.
Given type-1 functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
,
we write
\mathbb{F}_{\vec{f}}
for
\mathbb{F}
extended with function
symbols
\mathsf{S}_{f_{i}}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
, with
1\leq i\leq k
,
and let
\mathcal{R}_{+\vec{f}}=\mathcal{R}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{R}_{f}
.
Now we can define the notion of type-2 computability for such STRSs.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an STRS.
We say that
\mathsf{F}

computes
the type-2 functional

\Psi

in
(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})
iff
for all type-1 functions

f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
and all
w_{1},\dots,w_{l}\in W
,

\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{S}_{f_{k}}\,\underline{\mathsf{w_{1}}}\cdots\underline{\mathsf{w_{l}}}\to_{\mathcal{R}_{+\vec{f}}}^{+}\underline{\mathsf{u}}
, where
u=\Psi(f_{1},\dots,f_{k},w_{1},\dots,w_{l})
.

Next,
we define what we mean by polynomially bounded interpretation.

Definition 4.4
.

We say that an STRS
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}

admits
a polynomially bounded interpretation
iff
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
is compatible with a cost–size interpretation such that:

•

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{word}}=(\mathbb{N},\geq)
;

•

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{o}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{i}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{[]}}^{\mathsf{c}}=0
,

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{::}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda xy.0
,
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{::}}^{\mathsf{s}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda xy.x+y+c

for some
c\geq 1
;

•

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathsf{c}}
is bounded by a polynomial
in
{\mathtt{Pol}^{2}_{\mathbb{N}}[F_{1}^{\mathsf{c}},F_{1}^{\mathsf{s}},\ldots,F_{k}^{\mathsf{c}},F_{k}^{\mathsf{s}};x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}]}
.

Note that the condition on
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{::}}^{\mathsf{s}}
corresponds to what is called
additive
quasi-interpretation in
[
17
]
.

Finally, we can formally state our main result.

Theorem 4.5
.

A type-2 functional

\Psi

is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}

if and only if
there exists a finite orthogonal STRS

{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}

such that the distinguished symbol
\mathsf{F}
computes
\Psi
in
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}

and
\mathcal{R}
admits a polynomially bounded cost–size interpretation.

Note that the polynomial bound
only
refers to the cost interpretation function
for
\mathsf{F}
; there are no requirements on the interpretations for other function
symbols (except for
\mathsf{o}
,
\mathsf{i}
,
\mathsf{[]}
and
\mathsf{::}
).

We will prove this result in two parts.
First, we prove soundness in Section
5

which states that every type-2 functional computed by an STRS as above
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
Then in Section
6
we prove completeness,
which states that every functional in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
can be computed by such an STRS.
In order to simplify proofs,
we only consider type-2 functions of rank (1,1).
We claim that the results can be easily generalized, but the proofs become more
tedious when handling multiple arguments.

5.
Soundness

In order to prove soundness,
let us consider a fixed finite orthogonal STRS
\mathcal{R}
with a distinguished
function symbol
\mathsf{F}
which admits a polynomially bounded cost–size
interpretation, that computes a type-2 functional
\Psi
.
We proceed to show that
\Psi
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
roughly as follows.

(1)

Since
\mathcal{R}
computes
\Psi
and admits a polynomially bounded
interpretation,
we show that so does the extended system
\mathcal{R}_{+f}

(Definition
4.3
).
The restriction on
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{::}}^{\mathsf{s}}

(Definition
4.4
) implies that

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}

is bounded by a second-order polynomial over
|f|,|w|
.
We show this in Lemma
5.1
.
By compatibility (Corollary
3.5
),
we can do at most polynomially many steps when reducing
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}
.

(2)

The cost polynomial restricts the size of any input that the
function variable
F
is applied to (e.g., a cost bound
of
3+F^{\mathsf{c}}(m)
implies that
F
is never called on a term with
size interpretation
>m
).
This is the subject of Lemma
5.4
.

(3)

Using the observations above,
we then show that by graph rewriting we can simulate
\mathcal{R}_{+f}

and compute each
\mathcal{R}_{+f}
-reduction step in polynomial time on an OTM.
This guarantees that
\Psi
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
, Theorem
5.16
.

5.1.
Interpreting The Extended STRS, Polynomially

Our first goal is to provide a polynomially bounded cost–size interpretation
to the extended system
\mathcal{R}_{+f}
.
We start with the observation that
the size interpretation of words in
W
is proportional to their length.
Indeed, since
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{::}}^{\mathsf{s}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda xy.x+y+c

(Definition
4.4
)
let

\mu:=\max(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{o}}^{\mathsf{s}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{i}}^{\mathsf{s}})+c

and

\nu:=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{[]}}^{\mathsf{s}}
.
Consequently, for all
w\in W
:

|w|\leq{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}\leq\mu*|w|+\nu

(1)

Recall that by Definition
4.2
the extended system

\mathcal{R}_{+f}
has possibly infinitely many rules of the form

\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to\underline{\mathsf{f(w)}}
.
Such rules
\mathsf{S}_{f}
represent calls for an oracle to compute
f
in a single step.
Thus, we set their cost to 1.
The size should be given by the length of the oracle output, taking the overhead
of interpretation into account.
Hence, we obtain:

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.1\quad\quad\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.\mu*|f|(x)+\nu

Recall that
|f|(n)=\max\limits_{{|y|}\leq n}|f(y)|
.
Hence,
|f|
is weakly monotonic, and therefore so is
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}}
.
This orients the rules in
\mathcal{R}_{f}
because

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{c}}=1>0=\mathsf{cost}(\underline{\mathsf{f(w)}})
, and

{\llbracket{}\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}=\mu*|f|({\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})+\nu\geq\mu*|f|(|w|)+\nu\geq\mu*|f(w)|+\nu
by definition of

|f|
, which is superior or equal to
{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{f(w)}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a second-order polynomial
D
so that

D(|f|,|w|)
bounds the derivation height of
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}
for any
f\in W\longrightarrow W
and
w\in W
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathsf{c}}
is bounded by a second-order polynomial

\lambda\!\!\!\lambda F^{c}F^{s}x.P
, we can let
D(F,n):=P(\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.1,\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.\mu*F(x)+\nu,\mu*n+\nu)
. Then
D
is a
second-order polynomial, and
D(|f|,|w|)\geq\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathsf{c}}(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}},{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})=\mathsf{cost}(\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}})

(we omit
\alpha
and
\zeta
here because there are no variables in

\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}
, and therefore the valuations are
not relevant to the
\mathsf{cost}
value).
By
corollary

3.5
, this serves as a bound on the derivation height
of
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}
.
∎

Notice that this lemma does not imply that
\Psi
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
It only guarantees that there is a polynomial bound to the

number of rewriting steps
for such systems.
However, it does not immediately follow that this number is a reasonable bound
for the actual computational cost of simulating a reduction on an OTM.
Consider for example a rule
\mathsf{f}\,(\mathsf{s}\,n)\,t\to\mathsf{f}\,n\,(\mathsf{c}\,t\,t)
. Every step doubles the size of the
term. A naive implementation – which copies the duplicated term in each step
– would take exponential time.
Moreover, a single step using the oracle can create a very large output, which
is not considered part of the cost of the reduction, even though an OTM would be
unable to use it without first fully reading it.
Therefore,
in order to prove soundness,
we show how to realize a
reasonable implementation of rewriting w.r.t. OTMs.
In essence, we will show that
(1) oracle calls are not problematic in the presence of polynomially bounded interpretations, and
(2) we can handle duplication with an appropriate representation of rewriting. This is very
much in the style of what has been done for first-order rewriting and the
\lambda
-calculus
in the past
[
3
,
22
,
1
]
.

5.2.
Bounding The Oracle Input

We first show that calling the oracle along a computation does not introduce
an exponential overhead along the way.
More precisely,
we will show that there
exists a second-order polynomial
B
such that if an oracle call

\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}
occurs anywhere along the reduction
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\mathrel{\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}}\underline{\mathsf{v}}
, then
|x|\leq B(|f|,|w|)
.
From this, we know that the growth of the overall term size during
an oracle call is at most
|f|(B(|f|,|w|))
.

Let
P
again be any polynomial bounding
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathsf{c}}
.
Since
P
is a second-order polynomial, each occurrence of a sub-expression

F^{c}(E)
in
P
is itself a second-order polynomial, and so is
E
.
Let us enumerate these arguments as
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
.
We can then construct the new polynomial
Q
as follows:

Q\coloneq\sum\limits_{i}E_{i}\quad\text{where occurrences of}\ F^{c}(E_{j})\ \text{inside}\ E_{i}\ \text{are replaced by}\ 1

The idea here is that the polynomial
Q
sums up all those expressions
E_{i}

(with
1\leq i\leq n
) given to
F^{\mathsf{c}}
as argument. We do this because
intuitively, if during the computation a word
\underline{\mathsf{v}}
is ever given to the oracle,
then the cost for the oracle computation must be accounted for in
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{F}}^{\mathsf{c}}
.
Hence,
{\llbracket{}v\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}}
must be bounded by some
E_{i}
, so certainly by the sum of all

E_{i}
. We can safely replace occurrences of
F^{\mathsf{c}}(E_{j})
that occur inside another

E_{i}
by
1
because
F^{\mathsf{c}}
will be instantiated by
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.1
(or, in Lemma
5.3
, by a function that maps
specific input to
1
).

Due to this replacement,
F^{\mathsf{c}}
does not occur in
Q
, so
Q
is only
parametrized by
F^{\mathsf{s}}
and some type-0 variable
x
.
It is also possible for
Q
to be a constant polynomial, or to be parametrized
only by a type-0 variable.
Its final shape depends on the arguments provided
to the
F^{\mathsf{c}}
in
P
.
We let
B(G,y):=Q(\lambda\!\!\!\lambda z.\mu*G(z)+\nu,\mu*y+\nu)
.

Example 5.2
.

Let us illustrate the construction of such polynomial
Q
.
Consider the following polynomial
P
.

P=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda F^{c}F^{s}x.x*F^{\mathsf{c}}(3+F^{\mathsf{s}}(9*x))+F^{\mathsf{c}}(12)*F^{\mathsf{c}}(3+x*F^{\mathsf{c}}(2))+5,

then
Q
is built by adding each of those arguments given to
F^{\mathsf{c}}
.
We get

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=3+F^{\mathsf{s}}(9*x)+12+3+x*1+2

\displaystyle=20+F^{\mathsf{s}}(9*x)+x

Finally, we construct the polynomial
B
.

\displaystyle B(G,x)

\displaystyle=20+\mu*G(9*(\mu*x+\nu))+\nu+(\mu*x+\nu)

\displaystyle=20+2*\nu+G(9*\mu*x+9*\nu)+\mu*x

Now
B
gives an upper bound to the argument values for
F^{\mathsf{c}}
that
are considered: if a function differs from
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}

only on argument values greater than
B(|f|,|w|)
, then we can use it in
P
and
obtain the same result. Formally:

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
f,w
.
Let
G:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
with
G(z)=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}}(z)
if

z\leq B(|f|,|w|)
.
Then
P(G,\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}},{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})=P(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}},{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})
.

This is proved by induction on the form of
P
, using that
G
is never
applied on arguments larger than
B(|f|,|w|)
.
Lemma
5.3
is used in the following key result:

Lemma 5.4

(Oracle Subterm Lemma)
.

Let
f:W\longrightarrow W
be a type-1 function and

w\in W
.
If
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to_{\mathcal{R}_{+f}}^{*}C[\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}]
for some context

C
, then
|x|\leq B(|f|,|w|)
.

Proof.

By way of a contradiction, suppose there exist
f,w
, and
x
such that

\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to_{\mathcal{R}_{+f}}^{*}C[\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}]
for some context
C
, and
|x|>B(|f|,|w|)
.
Let us now construct an alternative oracle:
let
\mathsf{0}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature},\mathsf{s}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature},\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
and
\mathsf{helper}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}
, and for
N:=D(|f|,|w|)
, let
\mathcal{R}_{f,w}^{\prime}
be
given by:

\begin{array}[]{rcllcrcl}\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}&\to&\underline{\mathsf{f(x)}}&\text{if}\ |x|\leq B(|f|,|w|)&&\mathsf{helper}\,\mathsf{0}\,y&\to&y\\
\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}&\to&\mathsf{helper}\,\ulcorner\mathsf{N}\urcorner\,\underline{\mathsf{f(x)}}&\text{otherwise}&&\mathsf{helper}\,(\mathsf{s}\,x)\,y&\to&\mathsf{helper}\,x\,y\\
\end{array}

Where
\ulcorner\mathsf{N}\urcorner
is the unary number encoding of
N
,
as introduced in Section
2.1
.
Notice that by definition the rules for
\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}
will produce

\underline{\mathsf{f(x)}}
in a single step if

but they will take
N+2
steps otherwise.
Also observe that
\mathsf{S}_{f}
and
\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}
behave the same; that is,

\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}
and
\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}
have the
same normal form on any input
\underline{\mathsf{x}}
.
We extend the interpretation function of the original signature with:

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if}\ x\leq B(|f|,|n|)\\
N+2&\text{if}\ x>B(|f|,|n|)\\
\end{array}\right.\quad\quad\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{s}}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}}

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclcrclcrcl}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{helper}}^{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda xy.x+1&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{helper}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda xy.y&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{0}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&0&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{s}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.x+1\\
\end{array}

We easily see that this orients all rules in
\mathcal{R}_{f,w}
.
Then, by Lemma

,
\mathsf{cost}(\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}})\leq P(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{c}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{s}},{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})=P(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{c}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{S}_{f}}^{\mathsf{s}},{\llbracket{}\underline{\mathsf{w}}\rrbracket}^{\mathsf{s}})\leq D(|f|,|w|)=N
.
Yet, as we have
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to_{\mathcal{R}_{+f}}^{*}C[\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}]
, we also have
\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{f,w}^{\prime}}C^{\prime}[\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}]
,
where
C^{\prime}
is obtained from
C
by replacing all occurrences of

\mathsf{S}_{f}
by
\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}
.
Since
|x|>B(|f|,|w|)
by assumption, the reduction

\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{f,w}^{\prime}}^{*}C[\mathsf{S}_{f}^{\prime}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}]\to_{\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{R}_{f,w^{\prime}}}^{*}C[\underline{\mathsf{f(x)}}]
takes strictly more than
N
steps,
contradicting Corollary
3.5
.
∎

5.3.
Graph Rewriting

Lemma

Definition 5.6
.

Let

Definition 5.7
.

A

Let

5.4.
Bringing Everything Together

We are now ready to complete the soundness proof following the recipe at the
start of the section. Towards the third bullet point, we make the following
observation.

Lemma 5.14
.

There is a constant
a
such that, whenever
G\rightsquigarrow H

by a rule in
\mathcal{R}
, then
|H|\leq|G|+a
, where

|G|
denotes the total number of nodes in the graph
G
.

Proof.

In a step using a rule
\ell\to r
, the number of nodes in the graph
can be increased at most by
|[r]_{\mathbb{G}}|
. As there are only finitely
many rules in
\mathcal{R}
, we can let
a
be the number of nodes in the
largest graph for a right-hand side
r
.
∎

To see that graph rewriting with
\mathsf{S}_{f}
can be implemented in an
efficient way, we observe that the size of any intermediate graph in the
reduction
[\mathsf{G}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}]_{\mathbb{G}}\mathrel{\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}}[q]_{\mathbb{G}}
is
polynomially bounded by a second-order polynomial over
|f|,|w|
:

Lemma 5.15
.

There is a second-order polynomial
Q
such that
if

[\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}]_{\mathbb{G}}\rightsquigarrow^{*}H
,
then
|H|\leq Q(|f|,|w|)
.

Proof.

Let
Q(F,x):=x+D(F,x)*(a+F(B(F,x)))
, where

D
is the polynomial from Lemma
5.1
,

a
is the constant from Lemma
5.14
, and

B
is the polynomial from Section
5.2
.
This suffices, because there are at most
D(|f|,|w|)
steps
(Lemma
5.1
, Corollary
5.13
),
each of which increases the graph size by at most
\max(a,|f|(B(|f|,|w|)))

according to
lemmas

5.14
and
5.4
.
(Note that, by the Oracle Subterm lemma,
B
takes the size
|w|
of the
original input
w
as its second argument,
not
the current size of
the term.)
∎

All in all, we are finally ready to prove the
soundness
side of the
main theorem:

Theorem 5.16

(Soundness)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite orthogonal STRS admitting a
polynomially bounded interpretation.
If
\mathsf{F}
computes a type-2 functional
\Psi
,
then
\Psi\in\mathtt{BFF}
.

Proof.

Given
{(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{R})}
, we can construct an OTM
M
so that for a given

f\in W\longrightarrow W
, the machine
M_{f}
executed
on
w\in W
computes the normal form of

\mathsf{F}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{w}}
under
\to_{\mathcal{R}_{+f}}

using graph rewriting.
We omit the details of the construction, but observe:

•

that we can represent each graph in polynomial space in the size of the
graph; simply, we can make use of an adjacency-matrix representation of
the graph;

•

that we simulate any rewriting step that does not call the oracle (so using a
rule in
\mathcal{R}
) following the contraction
algorithm we defined in Definition
5.11
, which
is clearly feasible to do in polynomial time in the size of the graph; of course
this could in principle involve the creation or new nodes, but and edges, but the
amount of them is statically bounded by the size of the underlying set or
rewrite rules (excluding those involving the oracle).

•

and that each oracle call (implemented in rewriting by a

\mathcal{R}_{f}
-step
\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}\to\underline{\mathsf{y}}
) is resolved by copying
\underline{\mathsf{x}}
to the query
tape, transitioning to the query state, and from the answer state copying

\underline{\mathsf{y}}
from the answer tape to the main tape.
By Lemma
5.4
this is doable in polynomial time
in
|f|,|w|
and the graph size.

By Lemma

, graph sizes are bounded by a
polynomial over
|f|,|w|
, so using the above reasoning, the same
holds for the cost of each reduction step.
In summary: the total cost of

bounded by a second-order polynomial in terms of
|f|
and
|w|
.
As
M_{f}
simulates
\mathcal{R}_{+f}
via graph rewriting and

\mathcal{R}_{+f}
computes
\Psi

also computes
\Psi
.
By Definition
,
\Psi
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
.
∎

6.
Completeness

Recall from Section
4
that
to prove completeness we have to show the following:
if a given
type-2
functional
\Psi
is in
{\mathtt{BFF}_{2}}
,
then there exists an orthogonal STRS computing
\Psi

and admits a polynomially bounded interpretation.
In this section,
we prove this implication by providing an encoding of Oracle Turing Machines
as STRSs that admits a polynomially bounded interpretation.

The encoding is divided into three steps.
In Section
6.1
, we define the function symbols
that allow us to encode any possible machine configuration as terms.
In Section
6.2
, we encode machine transitions as reduction
rules that rewrite configuration terms.
This allows us to simulate one transition in an OTM as one or more rewriting steps
on the corresponding rewrite system.
Lastly, we design an STRS which simulates a complete execution of an OTM in
polynomially many steps.
Achieving this polynomial bound is non-trivial and is done in
Section
6.3
and Section
6.4
.

Henceforth, we assume given a fixed OTM
M
, and a second-order
polynomial
P_{M}
, such that
M
operates in time
P_{M}
.
For simplicity, we assume the machine has only three tapes (one input/output
tape, one query tape, one answer tape); that each non-oracle transition only
operates on one tape (i.e., reading/writing and moving the tape head); and
that we only have tape symbols
\{0,1,\mathtt{B}\}
.

6.1.
Encoding Machine Configurations as Terms

Recall from

To represent a (partial) tape, we also introduce

.

6.2.
Encoding Machine Transitions as Rules

A single step in an OTM can either be an oracle call (a transition from the

\mathtt{query}
state to the
\mathtt{answer}
state), or a traditional step: we
assume that an OTM
M
has a fixed set
\mathcal{T}
of

transitions

q\xRightarrow[t]{r/i,\ d}l
where

q
is the
input state
,

l
the
output state
,

t\in\{1,2,3\}
the tape considered (recall that we have assumed that a
non-oracle transition only operates on one tape),

r,i\in\{0,1,\mathtt{B}\}
respectively the symbol being read and the symbol
being written, and

d\in\{L,R\}
the direction for the read head of tape
t

to move.
We will model the computation of
M
as rules that simulate the small
step semantics of the machine.
Let us describe such rules as follows:

•

To encode a single transition, let

\mathsf{step}\mathrel{:}(\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word})\Rightarrow\mathsf{config}\Rightarrow\mathsf{config}
.
For any transition of the form
q\xRightarrow[1]{r/i,\ L}l
(so
a transition operating on tape 1, and moving left), we introduce a rule
(where we write
\underline{\mathsf{0}}=\mathsf{o},\ \underline{\mathsf{1}}=\mathsf{i},\ \underline{\mathsf{\mathtt{B}}}=\mathsf{b}
):

\mathsf{step}\,F\,(\mathtt{q}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,(x\mathsf{::}y))\,(\mathsf{R}\,(\underline{\mathsf{r}}\mathsf{::}z)))\,u\,v)\to\mathtt{l}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,y)\,(\mathsf{R}\,(x\mathsf{::}\underline{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{::}z)))\,u\,v\\

Moreover, for transitions
q\xRightarrow[1]{\mathtt{B}/w,\ L}l
(so
where
\mathtt{B}
is read), we add a rule:

\mathsf{step}\,F\,(\mathtt{q}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,(x\mathsf{::}y))\,(\mathsf{R}\,\mathsf{[]}))\,u\,v)\to\mathtt{l}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,y)\,(\mathsf{R}\,(x\mathsf{::}\underline{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{::}\mathsf{[]})))\,u\,v

These rules respectively handle the steps where a tape configuration is changed
from
u_{1}\ldots u_{p-1}u_{p}{\#}ru_{p+2}\ldots u_{k}
to

u_{1}\ldots u_{p-1}{\#}u_{p}iu_{p+2}\ldots u_{k}
, and where a tape
configuration is changed from
u_{1}\ldots u_{k}{\#}
to

u_{1}\ldots{\#}u_{k}i
.

•

Transitions where
d=R
, or on the other two tapes, are encoded
similarly.

•

Next, we encode oracle calls.
Recall that to query the machine for the value
of
f
at
u
, we write
u
on the second tape,
move its head to the leftmost position, and enter the query state. Then,
the content of this tape is erased and the image of
f

over
u
is written in the third tape.
Visually, this step is represented as:

(\mathtt{query},\langle\text{tape}_{1}\rangle,v_{1}\ldots v_{p}{\#}\underline{\mathsf{u}}\mathtt{B}\ldots,\langle\text{tape}_{3}\rangle)\rightsquigarrow(\mathtt{answer},\langle\text{tape}_{1}\rangle,{\#}\mathtt{B},{\#}\underline{\mathsf{f(u)}})

This is implemented by the following rules:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsf{step}\,F\,(\mathtt{query}\,t_{1}\,(\mathsf{split}\,x\,(\mathsf{R}\,y))\,t_{3})&\to&\mathtt{answer}\,t_{1}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,\mathsf{[]})\,(\mathsf{R}\,\mathsf{[]}))\\
\lx@intercol\hfil(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,\mathsf{[]})\,(\mathsf{R}\,(F\,(\mathsf{clean}\,y))))\lx@intercol\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclcrcl}\mathsf{clean}\,(\mathsf{o}\mathsf{::}x)&\to&\mathsf{o}\mathsf{::}(\mathsf{clean}\,x)&&\mathsf{clean}\,(\mathsf{b}\mathsf{::}x)&\to&\mathsf{[]}\\
\mathsf{clean}\,(\mathsf{i}\mathsf{::}x)&\to&\mathsf{i}\mathsf{::}(\mathsf{clean}\,x)&&\mathsf{clean}\,\mathsf{[]}&\to&\mathsf{[]}\\
\end{array}

Here,
\mathsf{clean}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
turns a word that may have
blanks in it into a bitstring, by reading until the next blank; for instance
replacing

[\mathsf{o};\mathsf{i};\mathsf{b};\mathsf{i}]
by

[\mathsf{o};\mathsf{i}]
.

The various
\mathsf{step}
rules and the
\mathsf{clean}
rules defined above are
non-overlapping because we consider
deterministic
OTMs.
Additionally, they are also left-linear.
We can orient such rules as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{clean}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.x&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{clean}}^{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.x+1\\
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{step}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda Fx.x+1&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{step}}^{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda F^{c}F^{s}x.F^{c}(x)+x+2\\
\end{array}

Note that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{step}}^{\mathsf{s}}
is so simple because the size
of a configuration does not include the size of the answer tape.
From these simulation rules, the following result is straightforward.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
M_{f}
be an OTM and
C,D
be machine configurations of

M_{f}
such that
C\rightsquigarrow D
.
Then
\mathsf{step}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,{[C]}\mathrel{\to_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}}{[D]}
,
where
{[C]}
is the term encoding of
C
.

6.3.
A Bound on the Number of Steps

To generalize from performing a single step of the machine to tracing a full
computation on the machine level, the natural idea would be to
define rules such as:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\mathsf{execute}\,F\,(\mathtt{q}\,x\,y\,z)&\to&\mathsf{execute}\,F\,(\mathsf{step}(\mathtt{q}\,x\,y\,z))&\text{for}\ \mathtt{q}\neq\mathtt{end}\\
\mathsf{execute}\,F\,(\mathtt{end}\,(\mathsf{split}\,(\mathsf{L}\,x)\,(\mathsf{R}\,w))\,y\,z)&\to&\mathsf{clean}\,w\\
\end{array}

Then, reducing
\mathsf{execute}\,\mathsf{S}_{f}\,\mathsf{initial}(w)
to normal form
simulates a full OTM execution of
M_{f}
on input
w
.
Unfortunately, this rule does not admit an interpretation, as it may be
non-terminating.
A solution could be to give
\mathsf{execute}
an additional argument

\ulcorner\mathsf{N}\urcorner

suggesting an execution in at most
N
steps; this argument would ensure
termination, and could be used to find an interpretation.

The challenge, however, is to compute a bound on the number of steps in the OTM:
the obvious thought is to compute
P_{M}(|f|,|w|)
, but this cannot in
general be done in polynomial time because the STRS does not have access to

|f|
: since
|f|(i)=\max\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid|x|\leq i\}
, there
are exponentially many choices for
x
.

To solve this, and following
[
37
, Proposition 2.3]
, we observe that
it suffices to know a bound for
f(x)
for only those
x
on
which the oracle is actually questioned.
That is, for
A\subseteq W
, let
|f|_{A}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda n.\max\{|f(x)|\mid x\in A\wedge|x|\leq n\}
. Then:

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose an OTM
M_{f}
runs in time bounded by
P_{M}(|f|,|w|)
on
input
w
.
If
M_{f}
transitions in
N
steps from its initial state to some
configuration
C
, calling the oracle only on words in
A\subseteq W
, then
N\leq P_{M}(|f|_{A},|w|)
.

Proof sketch.

We construct
f^{\prime}
with
f^{\prime}(x)=0
if
x\notin A
and
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)
if
x\in A
. Then
|f^{\prime}|=|f|_{A}
, and
M_{f^{\prime}}
runs the same on input
w
as
M_{f}
does.
∎

Now, for
A
encoded as a term
\mathsf{A}
(using symbols
\mathsf{\emptyset}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{set},\ \mathsf{setcons}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{set}\Rightarrow\mathsf{set}
), we can compute
|f|_{A}
using the rules below, where we use
unary integers as in Example
(
\mathsf{0}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature},\mathsf{s}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}
), and defined symbols
\mathsf{len}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature},\ \mathsf{max}\mathrel{:}{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature},\ \mathsf{limit}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word},\ \mathsf{retif}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word},\ \mathsf{tryapply}\mathrel{:}(\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word})\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature},\ \mathsf{tryall}\mathrel{:}(\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word})\Rightarrow\mathsf{set}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}
.
By design,
\mathsf{retif}\,\underline{\mathsf{x}}\,\ulcorner\mathsf{n}\urcorner\,\underline{\mathsf{y}}

if

\mathsf{[]}

and

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclrcl}\mathsf{len}\,\mathsf{[]}&\to&\mathsf{0}&&\mathsf{len}\,(x\mathsf{::}y)&\to&\mathsf{s}\,(\mathsf{len}\,y)\\

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{len}}^{\mathsf{s}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.x&&\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{len}}^{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lambda\!\!\!\lambda x.x+1\\

=&1+tl*(F^{c}(n)+2*F^{s}(n)+2*n+6)\\
&\phantom{ABi}+1*(F^{c}(n)+2*F^{s}(n)+2*n+6)\\

\mathsf{limit}

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{tryall}}^{\mathsf{s}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda Fan.F(n)

\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{tryall}}^{\mathsf{c}}=\lambda\!\!\!\lambda F^{c}F^{s}an.1+a+F^{c}(n)+2*F^{s}(n)+2*n+6

Now, for a given second-order polynomial

,

over

.

6.4.
Finalising Execution

Now, we can define execution in a way that can be bounded by a polynomial
interpretation.
We let
\mathsf{execute}\mathrel{:}(\mathsf{word}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word})\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow\mathsf{nnat}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow\mathsf{set}\Rightarrow\mathsf{config}\Rightarrow\mathsf{word}
and will define rules to reduce expressions
\mathsf{execute}\,F\,n\,m\,z\,a\,c
where

•

F
is the function to be used in oracle calls.

•

n-1
is a bound on the number of steps that can be done before
the next oracle call (or until the machine completes execution).

•

m
is essentially a natural number that represents the number of
steps that have been done so far. We use a new sort
\mathsf{nnat}
with
function symbols
\mathsf{o}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{nnat}
and
\mathsf{n}\mathrel{:}\mathsf{nnat}\Rightarrow\mathsf{nnat}
because we will let
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{nnat}}=(\mathbb{N},\leq)
,
so ordered in the other direction. This will be essential to find an
interpretation for
\mathsf{execute}
.

•

z
is a unary representation of
|w|
, where
w
is the
input to the OTM.

•

c
is the current configuration.

Using helper symbols

7.
Conclusion

This paper gives the first characterization of the class of type-2 basic feasible functionals based on the interpretation method and in particular on polynomial cost-size interpretations.

Surely, it would make sense to try to go beyond types of order 2 and try to characterize classes of basic feasible functionals of order at least 3. Such classes are less well understood than their order-2 counterparts and perhaps an analysis based on tools from rewriting could help to shed some light on their nature (see, e.g., Hugo Feree’s work on this subject
[
25
]
).
