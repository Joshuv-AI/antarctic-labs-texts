A unifying framework for ADI-like methods for linear matrix equations and beneficial consequences
Jonas Schulze, Jens Saak

Abstract
We derive the \ac ADI method based on a commuting operator split and apply the results \chadded in detail to the continuous time algebraic Lyapunov equation with low-rank constant term and approximate solution, \chadded giving pointers for the Sylvester case. Previously, it has been mandatory to start the low-rank ADI \chreplaced for Lyapunov equations (CF-ADI, LR-ADI, G-LR-ADI) or Sylvester equations (fADI, G-fADI)(LR-ADI) with an all-zero initial value. Our approach \chreplaced extendsretains the known efficient iteration schemes of low-rank increments and residuals to arbitrary low-rank initial values for \chreplaced all these methodsthe LR-ADI method. We further generalize \chreplaced two properties of the low-rank Lyapunov ADI to the generic ADI applied to arbitrary linear equations using a commuting operator split, namely the invariance of iterates under permutations of the shift parameters, and the efficient handling of complex shift parameters.some of the known properties of the LR-ADI for Lyapunov equations to larger classes of algorithms or problems. We investigate the performance of arbitrary initial values using two outer iterations in which \chreplaced the low-rank Lyapunov ADILR-ADI is typically called. First, we solve an algebraic Riccati equation with the Newton method. Second, we solve a differential Riccati equation with a first-order Rosenbrock method. Numerical experiments confirm that the proposed new initial value of the \ac ADI can lead to a significant reduction in the total number of \ac ADI steps, while also showing a 17 % 17\text{\,}\% and 8 × 8\times speed-up over the zero initial value for the two equation types, respectively.

(3)

We call an operator split

M_{k}N_{k}=N_{k}M_{k}\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{-}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{+}}x^{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=b-\mathop{\mathcal{V}_{k}^{-}}x^{k}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathcal{V}_{k}^{+}}x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=b-\mathop{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{-}}x^{k+\frac{1}{2}}.

As a splitting scheme (
4

the
\ac

\displaystyle:={(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k})}^{-1}\mathop{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{+}}\mathop{\mathcal{V}_{k}^{+}}

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle:={(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k})}^{-1}\mathop{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{-}}\mathop{\mathcal{V}_{k}^{-}}.

Theorem 3.1

\ac

\mathcal{V}

Lemma 4.2

(Consistent Lyapunov Residual)
.

There exists a factorization of the Lyapunov
\ac
ADI residual
r^{k}=R_{k}TR_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}

that is consistent with the initial residual
r^{0}=R_{0}TR_{0}^{\mathsf{H}}
.
That is, the inner factor
T
does not depend on the iteration
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
r^{k}=R_{k}T_{k}R_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Substitute the split operators (
16
) into
the residual update of
2.1

M_{k}r^{k+1}=N_{k}r^{k}
,
to obtain

(A+\alpha_{k}I)R_{k+1}T_{k+1}R_{k+1}^{\mathsf{H}}{(A+\alpha_{k}I)}^{\mathsf{H}}=(A-\overline{\alpha_{k}}I)R_{k}T_{k}R_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}(A-\overline{\alpha_{k}}I)^{\mathsf{H}}.

(18)

By choosing the outer factor according to

(A+\alpha_{k}I)R_{k+1}=(A-\overline{\alpha_{k}}I)R_{k},

(19)

the inner factor does not need to change;
that is,
T_{k+1}=T_{k}=T

∎

Moreover, we may utilize the definition of the increment

for a more efficient evaluation of the residual factors.
The formulation

Using the Lyapunov operator split (

Remark 5.2

(Order of Shifts and Eigenvalues)
.

Benner, Kürschner, and Saak require that complex conjugated pairs remain adjacent,

\mathcal{R}(Z_{\ell}Y_{\ell}Z_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})=RTR^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

where

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}&E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(q+2z_{\ell})}

(32)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&Y_{\ell}\\
\cdot&Y_{\ell}&-Y_{\ell}Z_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}BB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}Y_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{(q+2z_{\ell})\times(q+2z_{\ell})},

compare, e.g.,
[
14
]
.
We consider the Newton method to have converged once

\lVert\mathcal{R}(X_{\ell+1})\rVert<\mathrm{reltol}_{\mathrm{Newton}}\lVert C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C\rVert

(33)

for some user-provided
\mathrm{reltol}_{\mathrm{Newton}}
.
Next,
we describe how to configure the
\ac
ADI convergence criterion (
24
)
based on three different variations of the Newton method.
First, for the classical Newton method we use

\mathrm{reltol}_{\textup{\ac{ADI}}}=\mathrm{reltol}_{\textup{Newton}}/10.

(34a)

Second,
to speed up the early Newton iterations,
we use an inexact Newton method
with variable forcing term
\eta\in\mathbb{R}

described by Dembo, Eisenstat, and Steihaug
[
22
]
,
resulting in

\mathrm{abstol}_{\textup{\ac{ADI}}}=\eta\lVert\mathcal{R}(X_{\ell})\rVert.

(34b)

Third,
to speed up the later Newton steps with already small
\lVert\mathcal{R}(X_{\ell})\rVert
,
we switch back to the classical Newton method
if the inexact bound (
34b
) becomes smaller than the classical bound (
34a
).
This “hybrid” Newton method results in

\mathrm{abstol}_{\textup{\ac{ADI}}}=\max\big\{\eta\lVert\mathcal{R}(X_{\ell})\rVert,\kern 5.0pt\tfrac{1}{10}\mathrm{reltol}_{\textup{Newton}}\lVert\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(0)\rVert\big\}.

(34c)

Furthermore,
to keep the potential overshoot of the Riccati residual in control,
which refers to an increasing Riccati residual, as usually observed in the first Newton iterations,
we employ an Armijo line search.

4
4

4

Our implementation is rather naive in that it does not utilize the connection between
\mathcal{R}(X)

and
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(X)
described in
[
7
, Equation (5.2b)]
.
However, the overall runtime cost of the line search was negligible (less than 1% of the total runtime).

As described by Benner et al.
[
7
]
,
the line search is applied if
\lVert\mathcal{R}(\hat{X}_{\ell+1})\rVert>0.9\lVert\mathcal{R}(X_{\ell})\rVert
,
where
\hat{X}_{\ell+1}
denotes the solution to the classical Newton step (
30
).

5.2
Differential Riccati Equation

We apply a first-order Rosenbrock scheme, the implicit Euler method,
to the
\acf
DRE

E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\dot{X}E=\mathcal{R}(X):=C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C+A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}XE+E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}XA-E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}XBB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}XE,\qquad E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X(t_{0})E=C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C,

(35)

where, again,

E,A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\quad C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n},

(36)

and
m,q\ll n
.
As
C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}C
and
BB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
are positive semi-definite,
equation (
35
) has a unique solution;
see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 4.1.6]
.
Motivated by Stillfjord
[
43
]
and following Lang, Mena, and Saak
[
31
]
,
we factorize the solution according to

X_{\ell}=Z_{\ell}Y_{\ell}Z_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\approx X(t_{0}+\ell\tau)

with

Z_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times z_{\ell}},\quad Y_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{z_{\ell}\times z_{\ell}},

(37)

where
z_{\ell}\ll n
.
Thus, every Rosenbrock step reads

\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(X_{\ell+1}):=G_{\ell}S_{\ell}G_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+A_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X_{\ell+1}E+E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X_{\ell+1}A_{\ell}=0

(38)

where

\displaystyle A_{\ell}

\displaystyle=A-\tfrac{1}{2\tau}E-BB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X_{\ell}E

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(39)

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(q+z_{\ell})}

\displaystyle S_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
&Y_{\ell}Z_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}BB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}Y_{\ell}+\tfrac{1}{\tau}Y_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{(q+z_{\ell})\times(q+z_{\ell})}.

Note, again, that
A_{\ell}
has the structure of a sparse matrix updated by a low-rank matrix product.
This time, however, the inner dimension of the right-hand side
G_{\ell}S_{\ell}G_{\ell}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
,
and therefore the corresponding Lyapunov residual
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(X_{\ell+1,k})

both depend on the rank of the current Rosenbrock iterate
X_{\ell}
.
More precisely,
the rank is at most
(q+z_{\ell})+2z_{\ell}
;
see formula (
17
)

\chadded
with
g=q+z_{\ell}
and
z=z_{\ell}
.
Again, we do not yet utilize the common
E^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Z_{\ell}
in the outer low-rank factor,
which would allow for a factorization having inner dimension
q+2z_{\ell}<q+3z_{\ell}
and, thus, reduced compression times.

Conclusion

We introduced the notion of fully commuting splitting schemes to solve arbitrary linear systems,
and derived the
\ac
ADI method in that context.
This allowed us to extend the low-rank Lyapunov
\ac
ADI for complex data to non-zero initial values.
Furthermore, we generalized the permutation invariance of
\ac
ADI iterates
to arbitrary fully commuting splitting schemes (
2.4
),
as well as the existence of a real-valued
\ac
ADI double-step for complex-conjugated shifts
to arbitrary linear systems (
Theorem 3.2
).

We applied the extended low-rank Lyapunov
\ac
ADI to a Newton and a Rosenbrock method
to solve an algebraic and a differential Riccati equation, respectively.
For the Newton method we observed a
4\text{\,}\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}\times\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}
lower total number of
\ac
ADI steps for heuristic Penzl shifts,
but more expensive linear systems at every
\ac
ADI step.
Overall, for this application, our implementation only showed a modest
17\text{\,}\%
run-time improvement over the old
\ac
ADI.

\chdeleted
Therefore, at the moment we doubt that our modifications to the Newton-ADI for
\acp
ARE
suffice to make the method competitive with the
\ac
RADI
[
BenBKetal18
]
.

For the Rosenbrock method, however, we observed a
26\text{\,}\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}\times\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}
lower total number of
\ac
ADI steps,
depending on the shifts,
whose linear systems have fewer columns,
resulting in an
8\text{\,}\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}\times\mkern 0.0mu\mathrm{-}\mathrm{2}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{u}

\rho(\mathcal{C}(A,\alpha))=\max\left\{\frac{\lvert\lambda-\overline{\alpha}\rvert}{\lvert\lambda+\alpha\rvert}:\lambda\in\Lambda(A)\right\}.

(44)
