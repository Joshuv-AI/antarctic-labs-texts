Information Leakage in Index Coding With Sensitive and Non-Sensitive Messages
Yucheng Liu † , Lawrence Ong † , Phee Lep Yeoh § , Parastoo Sadeghi ‡ , Joerg Kliewer ∗ , and Sarah Johnson † , † The University of Newcastle, Australia (emails: {yucheng.liu, lawrence.ong, sarah.johnson}@newcastle.edu.au) § University of Sydney, Australia (email: phee.yeoh@sydney.edu.au) ‡ University of New South Wales, Canberra, Australia (email: p.sadeghi@unsw.edu.au) ∗ New Jersey Institute of Technology, USA (email: jkliewer@njit.edu)

Abstract
Information leakage to a guessing adversary in index coding is studied, where some messages in the system are sensitive and others are not. The non-sensitive messages can be used by the server like secret keys to mitigate leakage of the sensitive messages to the adversary. We construct a deterministic linear coding scheme, developed from the rank minimization method based on fitting matrices (Bar-Yossef et al. 2011). The linear scheme leads to a novel upper bound on the optimal information leakage rate, which is proved to be tight over all deterministic scalar linear codes. We also derive a converse result from a graph-theoretic perspective, which holds in general over all deterministic and stochastic coding schemes.



I
Introduction

Index coding
[
1
,
2
]
is a canonical problem in network information theory, where a server tries to efficiently broadcast messages to multiple receivers with side information.
In this work, we study the information leakage in index coding when the broadcast codeword is eavesdropped by a guessing adversary.
The adversary tries to maximize the probability of correctly guessing its messages of interest within a single trial.
Our goal is to minimize the leakage to this adversary, which is defined as the ratio between the adversary’s probability of successful guessing
after and before
observing the codeword
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

Our previous work
[
6
]
studied the information leakage with an underlying assumption that the server aims to protect
all
of the messages against the adversary.
Indeed, such assumption holds in most existing works
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
where the security and privacy aspects of index coding are investigated.
Nevertheless, in many practical circumstances, some messages may be sensitive while the others are non-sensitive, and thus secrecy loss should be measured over only those sensitive messages.
For example, consider a data storage system with a number of messages. Some messages are private and needs to be protected from any adversary, while the other messages are non-private and thus need not be protected. There are a number of clients, each of which may request access to some messages, whether private or non-private, and may have stored some other messages already as side information. The goal is to satisfy the clients’ requirements while keeping the private messages as safe as possible from the adversary.
A similar setting can also be motivated from the adversary’s perspective as it may only be interested in a subset of messages and thus these messages should be considered as sensitive by the server.
This distinction between sensitive and non-sensitive messages enables the server to treat the non-sensitive messages like secret keys and design a smart way of coding to simultaneously satisfy the receivers and mitigate information leakage to the adversary.

serves as a toy example showing how the server can reduce the information leakage by smartly designing coding schemes.

We study the problem of both achievability and converse.
In Section
II
, we describe the system model and provide necessary preliminaries.
In Section
III
, we propose a practical linear coding scheme based on the rank minimization method over fitting matrices
[
2

. The scheme is proved to yield optimal (minimum) information leakage rate over all deterministic scalar linear codes.
In Section

To show the lower bound, we use the confusion graph representation of index coding problems

II-C

Information Leakage Metric

We assume the broadcast codeword is eavesdropped by a guessing adversary, knowing a subset of messages
X_{K}
as side information.
The rest of the messages
X_{K^{c}}
are divided into two groups, where the sensitive messages are denoted by
X_{S}
and the non-sensitive ones are denoted by
X_{U}
.
The information leakage from
Y
to the adversary will be measured only over the sensitive messages
X_{S}
.
Upon observing the broadcast codeword, the adversary makes a single guess on the value of
X_{S}
according to the maximum likelihood rule.
Note that
K,S,U
are non-overlapping and
[n]=K\cup S\cup U
. Let
k,s,u
denote the cardinality of sets
K,S,U
, respectively.

Consider any valid
(t,M,f,{\bf g})
index code.
Before eavesdropping the codeword
Y
, the expected probability of the adversary successfully guessing
x_{S}
is

\displaystyle{\rm P_{\rm s}}(X_{K})={\mathbb{E}}_{X_{K}}\left[\max_{x_{S}}P_{X_{S}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{K}}(x_{S}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{K})\right]=\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}^{-ts},

and the expected successful guessing probability after observing
Y
is

\displaystyle{\rm P_{\rm s}}(X_{K},Y)={\mathbb{E}}_{Y,X_{K}}\left[\max_{x_{S}}P_{X_{S}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{K}}(x_{S}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{K})\right].

The leakage
L
is defined as the logarithm of the ratio between the expected probabilities of the adversary successfully guessing
x_{Q}
after and before observing
Y

[
3
,
4
]
. That is,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\doteq\log_{q}\frac{{\rm P_{\rm s}}(X_{K},Y)}{{\rm P_{\rm s}}(X_{K})}

(3)

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\sum_{x_{K},y}\max_{x_{S}}P_{Y,X_{K}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{S}}(y,x_{K}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x_{S}).

(4)

The leakage rate of the code is
\mathcal{L}=t^{-1}L
and the optimal leakage rate can then be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}

\displaystyle\doteq\lim_{t\to\infty}\inf_{\text{$(t,M,f,{\bf g})$ codes}}\mathcal{L}.

(5)

Remark 2

The idea of measuring leakage as the ratio of the adversary’s successful guessing probabilities has been introduced and explored in various contexts
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
The leakage
L
is equal to the maximal leakage
[
5
]
from
X_{S}
to
Y
given side information
X_{K}
, which is also equal to the maximum min-entropy leakage
[
4
]
from
X_{S}
to
Y
given
X_{K}
.

III
A Deterministic Linear Index Code

In the following, we construct a deterministic linear index code based on the minrank method and fitting matrices, which were developed for the original index coding problem without adversary
[
2
]
.
We then show that the proposed scheme achieves the optimal leakage rate over all valid deterministic scalar linear index codes.
Throughout the section, we set
t=1
as we are considering only scalar linear codes.

Unless otherwise stated, we use bold-faced capital letters to denote matrices and vectors, e.g.,

{\bf X}_{[n]}=[X_{1}\enskip\ldots\enskip X_{n}]^{T}
.
Let
r(\cdot)
denote the rank of a matrix over the Galois field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

Note that for any receiver
i
requiring more than one messages (i.e.
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}W_{i}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\geq 2
), we can transform the problem into a new equivalent problem by removing the receiver
i
and adding
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}W_{i}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}
new receivers, where every new receiver has the same side information set
A_{i}
and each receiver wants a unique message in set
W_{i}
.
Therefore, we can, without loss of generality, always assume
W_{i}=\{w_{i}\}
being a singleton set for every receiver
i\in[m]
.

For any given problem, a size
m\times n
fitting matrix
{\bf M}
of Galois field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is a matrix such that for any receiver
i\in[m]
,

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{ij}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\text{for $j=w_{i}$,}

(6)

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{ij}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{for any message $j\in[n]\setminus(W_{i}\cup A_{i})$}.

(7)

As
{\bf M}_{ij}
can be any element in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
if
j\in A_{i}
, there can be multiple fitting matrices for a given problem.
For example, for the problem with
n=5
messages and
m=3
receivers, where
W_{i}=\{i\},\forall i\in[3]
,
A_{1}=\{3,4\}
,
A_{2}=\{1,4,5\}
,
A_{3}=\{2,5\}
, any matrix of the form below is a fitting matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0&?&?&0\\
?&1&0&?&?\\
0&?&1&0&?\end{bmatrix}

(8)

where “?” means that the entry can be any element in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

If the server generates codeword
{\bf Y}
by multiplying a fitting matrix
{\bf M}
by the message vector
{\bf X}_{[n]}=[X_{1}\enskip X_{2}\enskip\ldots\enskip X_{n}]^{T}
, every receiver
i\in[m]
can recover its wanted message because the
i
-th element of
{\bf Y}
is a linear combination of only
X_{w_{i}}
and some of its side information.
Moreover, note that any row of
{\bf M}
can be generated by
r({\bf M})
independent rows of
{\bf M}
. Thus,
to satisfy the decoding requirements at the receivers, the server needs only to transmit the linear combination of the messages with coefficients from
r({\bf M})
independent rows of
{\bf M}
.
In this way, for a given problem, the minimum rank over all the fitting matrices, namely, the
minrank
value, establishes an upper bound on its broadcast rate, which has been proved to be optimal over all deterministic scalar linear codes
[
2
]
.

For analysis of information leakage based on the fitting matrix framework,
we can split
{\bf M}
into three submatrices formed by different groups of columns in
{\bf M}
according to sets
K
,
S
, and
U
. For brevity, we simply write

\displaystyle{\bf M}=[{\bf K}\quad{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}].

(9)

The following theorem characterizes the minimal leakage rate among all codes based on the fitting matrix framework.

Theorem 1

For any index coding problem, there exists a deterministic scalar linear index code that yields the following leakage rate,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\min_{{\bf M}}(r([{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}])-r({\bf U})).

(10)

Furthermore, this result is leakage-wise rate optimal for all deterministic scalar linear codes.

Proof:

We first show the achievability.
Consider any fitting matrix
{\bf M}=[{\bf K}\quad{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}]
and any encoding matrix
{\bf E}
formed by a set of row vectors of
{\bf M}
such that the row space of
{\bf M}
is the same as that of
{\bf E}
and thus by receiving
{\bf Y}={\bf E}{\bf X}
every receiver can decode its wanted message.
As
t=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\sum_{x_{K},y}\max_{x_{S}}P_{Y,X_{K}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{S}}(y,x_{K}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x_{S})

\displaystyle=\log_{q}\sum_{x_{K},y}\max_{x_{S}}\sum_{x_{U}}P_{Y,X_{K},X_{U}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{S}}(y,x_{K},x_{U}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x_{S})

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\log_{q}\sum_{x_{K},y}\max_{x_{S}}\sum_{x_{U}}P_{Y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{[n]}}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x_{[n]})\cdot P_{X_{K\cup U}}(x_{K\cup U})

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\log_{q}\big(q^{-k-u}\sum_{x_{K}}\sum_{y}\max_{x_{S}}\sum_{x_{U}}{\mathbbm{1}}({\bf Y}={\bf E}{\bf X})\big)

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\log_{q}\big(q^{-k-u}\sum_{x_{K}}\sum_{y}q^{u-r({\bf U})}\big)

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}r([{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}])-r({\bf U}),

where (a) is due to the messages being independent,
(b) is due to the code being deterministic, where
{\mathbbm{1}}(\cdot)
is the indicator function,
(c) follows from the fact that given any fixed
y
and
x_{K\cup S}
, there are
q^{u-r({\bf U})}
possible
x_{U}
values that satisfy
{\bf Y}={\bf E}{\bf X}
,
and (d) follows from the fact that given any fixed
x_{K}
, there are
q^{r([{\bf S}\hskip 8.19447pt{\bf U}])}
possible
y
values.
Therefore, by minimizing over all fitting matrices, the leakage rate in (
10
) can be achieved.

Now we prove the converse part of the theorem.
Suppose a
\ell\times n
matrix
{\tilde{\bf E}}
of Galois field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

is the encoding matrix of an arbitrary valid deterministic scalar linear index code.
Note that
{\tilde{\bf E}}
need not be a fitting matrix.
We split
{\tilde{\bf E}}
into three submatrices formed by different groups of columns according to sets
K
,
S
, and
U
as

\displaystyle{\tilde{\bf E}}=[{\tilde{\bf K}}\quad{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}].

(11)

Following a similar argument as in the achievability proof of the theorem, we can show that the leakage rate caused by the codeword
{\bf Y}={\tilde{\bf E}}{\bf X}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{\bf E}}}=r([{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}])-r({\tilde{\bf U}}).

(12)

It remains to show that
L_{{\tilde{\bf E}}}
is lower bounded by (
10
).

According to the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 1]
, there exists some fitting matrix
{\bf M}=[{\bf K}\quad{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}]
of the problem such that the row vectors of
{\bf M}
lie in the row space of
{\tilde{\bf E}}
.
In other words, there exists some
n\times\ell
matrix
{\bf B}
such that
{\bf B}{\tilde{\bf E}}={\bf M}
, or, if we only consider the submatrices according to sets
S
and
U
,
{\bf B}[{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}]=[{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}]
.
Also, there exists some matrix
{\bf D}
such that
{\tilde{\bf U}}=[{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}]{\bf D}
,
{\bf U}=[{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}]{\bf D}={\bf B}[{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}]{\bf D}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle r([{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}])+r({\tilde{\bf U}})

\displaystyle=r({\bf B}[{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}])+r([{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}]{\bf D})

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{\leq}\limits}}r([{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}])+r({\bf B}[{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}]{\bf D})

\displaystyle=r([{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}])+r({\bf U}),

where
(a) is due to Frobenius inequality

\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{\bf E}}}=r([{\tilde{\bf S}}\quad{\tilde{\bf U}}])-r({\tilde{\bf U}})\geq r([{\bf S}\quad{\bf U}])-r({\bf U}),

which, together with the fact that

is a fitting matrix, completes the proof.

The following fitting matrix

{\tilde{\bf M}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1&0\\
0&1&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

gives
\mathcal{L}=r({\tilde{\bf M}})-r({\tilde{\bf U}})=2-2=0
,
indicating that zero leakage (i.e., perfect secrecy) can be achieved for the problem.
However,
{\tilde{\bf M}}
leads to a suboptimal compression rate of

.
In fact, in the following we use Shannon-type inequalities
[

to show that the compression rate of any index code that attains zero leakage is at least
2
.
Recall that we are considering binary uniform messages that are independent to each other. Thus,
t=1
and
H(X_{J})=\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}J\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{},\forall J\subseteq[4]
.
Consider any
(t,M,f,{\bf g})
index code that achieves zero leakage, i.e.,
L=I(X_{\{1,2\}};Y)=0
.
We have
2=H(X_{\{1,2\}})=H(X_{\{1,2\}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y)=H(X_{1}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y)+H(X_{2}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{1})=H(X_{2}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y)+H(X_{1}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{2})
,
which implies that
H(X_{2}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{1})=H(X_{1}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{2})=1
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle H(X_{3}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{\{1,2\}})

\displaystyle=H(X_{3}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{2})-H(X_{1}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{2})+H(X_{1}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{\{2,3\}})

where (a) follows since that

and

due to the decoding requirement that receiver

X_{\{2,3\}}

As entropy is always non-negative, we have

.
Similarly, we can show that

Thus, we have

\displaystyle t^{-1}\log_{q}M

\displaystyle\geq H(Y)

\displaystyle\geq I(Y;X_{\{3,4\}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{\{1,2\}})

\displaystyle=H(X_{\{3,4\}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}X_{\{1,2\}})-H(X_{\{3,4\}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y,X_{\{1,2\}})

That is, the compression rate of any index coding attaining zero leakage must be at least

.
Therefore,
\beta=1
and

can never be simultaneously achieved for this problem.

IV
A General Lower Bound on
\mathcal{L}^{*}

We derive the following lower bound on the leakage rate that holds generally over any valid index codes.

Theorem 2

For any index coding problem
\Gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}(\Gamma)\geq\beta({\tilde{\Gamma}}(S\cup U))-u,

(14)

where
{\tilde{\Gamma}}
denotes the index coding problem constructed by adding an extra receiver, indexed by
m+1
, to the original problem, which knows side information
A_{m+1}=K\cup S
and wants messages
W_{m+1}=U
.

Proof:

Consider any valid
(t,M,f,{\bf g})
index code.
For any
(x_{K},y)
value and any
J\subseteq S\cup U
,
let
X
_J(x_K,y)={ x_J∈
X
_J:P_Y,X_K∪J(y,x_K∪J)¿0 } denote the set of
X_{J}
values jointly possible with
(x_{K},y)
.

Now consider the problem
{\tilde{\Gamma}}
, which is constructed from
\Gamma
by adding an extra receiver
m+1
with
A_{m+1}=K\cup S
and
W_{m+1}=U
.
Notice that by adding a receiver we are essentially adding more edges into the confusion graph (i.e.,
E(\Gamma_{t})\subseteq E({\tilde{\Gamma}_{t}})
).
We first show the following inequality:

\displaystyle\max_{x_{K},y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\leq\alpha({\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U)),

(15)

where
\alpha(\cdot)
denotes the independence number of a graph.

We prove the inequality via contradiction.
Assume there exists some
(x_{K},y)
value such that
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}>\alpha({\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U))
.
Consider a subset
\mathcal{I}
of the vertex set
\mathcal{X}_{S\cup U}(x_{K},y)
in the subgraph
\Gamma_{t}(S\cup U)
such that for every
x_{S}\in\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)
, there is exactly one vertex in
v_{S\cup U}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
v_{S}=x_{S}
.
Thus
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{I}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}=\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}
.
For a visualization of the construction of
\mathcal{I}
, see the schematic graph in Figure
3
.

\mathcal{X}_{S\cup U}(x_{K},y)

We partition the nodes into subgroups according to their

values, and each subgroup is denoted by a dashed circle with its corresponding
x_{S}

For example,

The number of distinct

values in
\mathcal{X}_{S\cup U}(x_{K},y)

\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}

\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{I}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}

By the definition of the confusion graph,

Since

\mathcal{I}
is also an independent set in

Thus, any two vertices in
\mathcal{I}
are not confusable at any receiver

\Gamma(S\cup U)

Note that the extra receiver
m+1

X_{S}

Since any two vertices in
\mathcal{I}

In conclusion, any two vertices in
\mathcal{I}
are not confusable at any receivers in the subproblem

In other words, any two vertices in
\mathcal{I}
are not adjacent in
{\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U)
, and hence
\mathcal{I}
is also an independent set of
{\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U)
.
Therefore, we must have
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{I}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\leq\alpha({\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U))
, which contradicts with the assumption that
\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{X}_{S}(x_{K},y)\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}=\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\mathcal{I}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}>\alpha({\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U))

Having proved (
15

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{K},y}\max_{x_{S}}P_{Y,X_{K,S}}(y,x_{K,S})

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log_{q}\chi_{\rm f}({\tilde{\Gamma}}_{t}(S\cup U))-u

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\beta({\tilde{\Gamma}}(S\cup U))-u,

where (c) follows from (

Remark 6

It can be shown using the generalized maximal acyclic induced subgraph (MAIS) bound
