On the diameter of a super-order-commuting graph
Janko Bračič, Bojan Kuzma

Abstract
. We answer a question about the diameter of an order-super-commuting graph on a symmetric group by studying the number-theoretical concept of d d -complete sequences of primes in arithmetic progression.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 05C25, Secondary: 05C69, 11B13, 11A67, 11N13.

1.
Introduction

In a recent paper
[
5
]
the authors studied the properties of super graphs on finite groups. These graphs are based
on already well-studied commuting, power, and enhanced power graphs (we refer to
[
3
]
for a nice survey and the current
development), but with an equivalence relation thrown in. More precisely, if
\sim
is an equivalent relation on a graph
\Gamma
, then

\sim
-super-
\Gamma
has the same vertex set as
\Gamma
however, its edge set is enlarged, whereby distinct
x,y
form an edge if there
exist
u\sim x
and
v\sim y
with either
u=v
or
(u,v)\in E(\Gamma)
.
In particular, with the order relation on a finite group
\mathcal{G}
(i.e.,
x\sim y
if
x
and
y
have the same order), the
order-super-commuting graph,
\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{G})
, of a group
\mathcal{G}

is a simple graph

\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{G})

with the vertex set equal to

\mathcal{G}
and
where two disjoint vertices
x,y
form an edge if there exists commuting
u,v
with
|u|=|x|
and
|v|=|y|
(here, we also allow
u=v
, so
in particular, each conjugacy class forms an induced complete graph). We should caution that in
[
5
]
the central
elements and in particular, the identity also belong to the vertex set of the commuting graph, but we do not allow the loops. Notice that
this contrasts with a similar definition in some of the existing literature
[
1
,
7
]
, where the central elements are
removed. It was shown in
[
5
]
that, for
n\geq 4
, the identity, that is, the center of the group, is the only
dominant vertex of
\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
, the order-super-commuting graph of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
on
n
elements. By deleting all the
dominant vertices, one obtains the reduced graph,
\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{\ast}
. This is connected if and only if neither
n-1
nor
n
is a prime
number; moreover, if it is disconnected, then it has exactly two components, and if it is connected, then its diameter is bounded above by

3
, see
[
5
, Proposition 4.9 and Theorem 4.11]
. Whether its diameter is
3
or smaller was
not determined for all values of
n\geq 4
, but it was shown
[
5
, Proposition 4.12]
that
the following holds.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 4
. If neither
n
nor
n-1
is a prime number, then
\diam\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{\ast}=3
if and only if there exist
nonempty disjoint subsets
\mathscr{T}_{1},\mathscr{T}_{2}
consisting of primes smaller or equal to
n
, such that, for some positive
integers
\alpha_{p}
and
\beta_{q}
we have

M^{\alpha}_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}:=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{T}_{1}}p^{\alpha_{p}}\in\{n-1,n\},\qquad M^{\beta}_{\mathscr{T}_{2}}:=\sum_{q\in\mathscr{T}_{2}}q^{\beta_{q}}\leq n,

and
p+M^{\beta}_{\mathscr{T}_{2}}>n
, for every
p\in\mathscr{T}_{1}
.∎

With this proposition, the authors managed to prove that the diameter is
3

if
n
or
n-1
is a nontrivial power of a prime or a sum of two prime
powers, with both primes distinct and greater or equal to
5
. The latter
provided that the strong Goldbach conjecture is true, immediately yields that

\diam\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{\ast}=3
, for every even integer
n\geq 4
. This paper
aims to give a complete solution, without relying on the strong Goldbach
conjecture and proves that
\diam\Delta^{o}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{\ast}=3
for every
n\geq 4

such that neither
n
nor
n-1
is a prime number. Our main ingredient is the
fact that the sequences consisting of primes congruent to
\pm 1
modulo
4

are complete (see
Theorem

2.5
and its consequences).

An infinite sequence of distinct positive integers
\{a_{n};\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is
called complete if every sufficiently large positive integer is a sum of
distinct
a_{i}
(sometimes such sequences are called weakly
complete, while the term complete sequence is reserved for the case when
every integer is a sum of distinct
a_{i}
). Erdős and Lewin
[
6
]

call a complete sequence
d
-complete if every sufficiently large integer is
a sum of distinct
a_{i}
such that no one divides the other. In
[
6
]
,
there are given several examples of
d
-complete sequences. For instance, it
is proved that, for positive integers
p
and
q
, the sequence
\{p^{a}q^{b};\;a,b\in\mathbb{N}\}
is
d
-complete if and only if
\{p,q\}=\{2,3\}
. Bruckman
[
2
]
has proved that the sequence
\mathscr{P}=\{2,3,5,\ldots\}
of all prime numbers is
d
-complete. We adjust Bruckman’s
proof and show that the sequences of all prime numbers congruent to
1
,
respectively to
3
, modulo
4
are
d
-complete.

2.
Results

2.1.
Generating polynomials

Let
1\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<\dots
be a sequence of integers. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

f_{n}(x)=\bigl(1+x^{q_{1}}\bigr)\cdots\bigl(1+x^{q_{n}}\bigr).

It is clear that
f_{n}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
S_{n}=q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}
.
Denote the coefficient of
f_{n}(x)
at power
x^{m}
by
\gamma_{m}(n)
; we
also let
\gamma_{m}(n)=0
if
m\geq S_{n}+1
. Then

f_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n}}\gamma_{m}(n)x^{m}.

Since

\begin{split}\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n+1}}\gamma_{m}(n+1)x^{m}&=f_{n+1}(x)=f_{n}(x)\cdot(1+x^{q_{n+1}})=\biggl(\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n}}\gamma_{m}(n)x^{m}\biggr)\bigl(1+x^{q_{n+1}}\bigr)\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n}}\gamma_{m}(n)x^{m}+\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n}}\gamma_{m}(n)x^{m+q_{n+1}}\end{split}

the comparison of the coefficients gives

(2.1)

\gamma_{m}(n+1)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\gamma_{m}(n),&\quad 0\leq m<q_{n+1};\\
\gamma_{m}(n)+\gamma_{m-q_{n+1}}(n),&\quad q_{n+1}\leq m\leq S_{n};\\
\gamma_{m-q_{n+1}}(n),&\quad S_{n}<m\leq S_{n+1}.\end{array}\right.

It follows from (
2.1
) that
\gamma_{m}(n+1)\geq\gamma_{m}(n)\geq 0
. On the other hand,
let
m\geq 0
be arbitrary but fixed. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
m<q_{n+1}
. Then, by (
2.1
),

\gamma_{m}(n+1)=\gamma_{m}(n)
. Since
m<q_{n+1}<q_{n+2}
we have

\gamma_{m}(n+2)=\gamma_{m}(n+1)

and therefore
\gamma_{m}(n+2)=\gamma_{m}(n)
. By induction,

\gamma_{m}(k)=\gamma_{m}(n)
, for all
k>n
.
Thus, we may define
\Gamma_{m}=\max\{\gamma_{m}(n);\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Note that
\Gamma_{m}>0
if and only if there exist
n
such that
\gamma_{m}(n)>0
which is equivalent
to the fact that there exist distinct sequence members
q_{j_{1}},\ldots,q_{j_{\ell}}

where
\ell\geq 1
, such that
m=q_{j_{1}}+\cdots+q_{j_{\ell}}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
1\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots
be a sequence of integers with partial sums
S_{n}=q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}
.
Consider

f_{n}(x)=(1+x^{q_{1}})\cdots(1+x^{q_{n}})=\sum_{m=0}^{S_{n}}\gamma_{m}(n)x^{m}.

If there exist positive integers
k_{0}
and
n_{0}
such that

2k_{0}+q_{n+1}\leq S_{n},\quad\text{for}\;\;n\geq n_{0},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{m}(n_{0})\geq 1,\quad\text{for}\;\;k_{0}\leq m\leq S_{n_{0}}-k_{0},

then every integer
n\geq k_{0}
is a sum of (one or more) different members of
\{q_{1},q_{2},\dots\}
.

Proof.

We will prove by induction that, for
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

\gamma_{m}(n)\geq 1\quad\text{if}\;\;k_{0}\leq m\leq S_{n}-k_{0}.

It is obvious that this will imply
\Gamma_{m}\geq 1
, for all
m\geq k_{0}
, and the statement will follow.

Let
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
denote the set of all integers
n\geq n_{0}
such that
\gamma_{m}(n)\geq 1
if

k_{0}\leq m\leq S_{n}-k_{0}
. By the hypothesis,
n_{0}\in\mathscr{N}
.
Assume that
n\in\mathscr{N}
. If
k_{0}\leq m\leq S_{n}-k_{0}
, then
\gamma_{m}(n+1)\geq\gamma_{m}(n)\geq 1
,
by the inductive hypothesis. Similarly, if
m
is such that
k_{0}\leq m-q_{n+1}\leq S_{n}-k_{0}
, then

\gamma_{m-q_{n+1}}(n)\geq 1
, again by the inductive hypothesis, and therefore
\gamma_{m}(n+1)\geq 1
,
by (
2.1
). Clearly,
k_{0}\leq m-q_{n+1}\leq S_{n}-k_{0}
is equivalent to

k_{0}+q_{n+1}\leq m\leq S_{n}+q_{n+1}-k_{0}=S_{n+1}-k_{0}
. Also, by the assumptions,
k_{0}+q_{n+1}\leq S_{n}-k_{0}

so that the intersection of the intervals
[k_{0},S_{n}-k_{0}]
and
[k_{0}+q_{n+1},S_{n+1}-k_{0}]
is nonempty.
We conclude that
\gamma_{m}(n+1)\geq 1
, for all
m
such that
k_{0}\leq m\leq S_{n+1}-k_{0}
. Hence,
n+1\in\mathscr{N}
.
∎

We next show that there always exists
k_{0}
which satisfies the first condition in
Theorem

2.1
, provided that
the sequence of integers grows at most exponentially.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
1\leq q_{1}<q_{2}<\dots
be a sequence of integers satisfying
q_{n+1}<2q_{n}
and let
k_{0}\geq 0
be a given integer.
Then,
S_{n}-q_{n+1}\geq 2k_{0}
, for every
n\geq q_{1}+2k_{0}+1
.

Proof.

Notice first that
q_{i}<q_{i+1}\leq 2q_{i}-1
, so that
q_{i}-q_{i+1}\geq q_{i}-(2q_{i}-1)=-q_{i}+1
.
Then, proceeding backward, we get

\begin{split}S_{i}&-q_{i+1}=q_{1}+q_{2}+\cdots+(q_{i}-q_{i+1})\geq q_{1}+q_{2}+\cdots+(q_{i-1}-q_{i})+1\geq\\
&\geq q_{1}+q_{2}+\cdots+(q_{i-2}-q_{i-1})+1+1\geq\dots\geq q_{1}-q_{2}+(i-2)\geq-q_{1}+(i-1).\end{split}

Hence, with
i=q_{1}+2k_{0}+1
we get that
S_{i}-q_{i+1}\geq 2k_{0}
. This was the start of the induction.
To finish the proof, let
\mathscr{M}
denote the set of all integers
n\geq q_{1}+2k_{0}+1
such that the
statement of the lemma is valid. If
n\in\mathscr{M}
, then

S_{n+1}-q_{n+2}=S_{n}-q_{n+1}+2q_{n+1}-q_{n+2}\geq S_{n}-q_{n+1}\geq 2k_{0},

and therefore
n+1\in\mathscr{M}
.
∎

.

Let

1.1
. The remaining integers
