Real zeros of random trigonometric polynomials with ℓ \ell -periodic coefficients
Ali Pirhadi

Abstract
. The case r = 0 r=0 is marked as a special one since in such a case T n T_{n} asymptotically obtains the largest possible number of real zeros.

1.
Introduction

The study of zeros and level crossings of random functions, which is an interesting topic in mathematics and statistics, has a long history of almost a century.
Bloch and Pólya
[
3
]
were the first to study the expected number of real zeros of a polynomial with random coefficients.
Later, Littlewood and Offord gave explicit bounds for the number of real zeros of random (algebraic) polynomials with uniform, standard normal, and Bernoulli coefficients, see
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Ever since, there has been continual interest in the study of asymptotic estimate of the number of real zeros of random algebraic/trigonometric polynomials and the way these roots are distributed in the complex plane.
In the case of random polynomials, the pioneering work of Kac
[
15
]
showed that the expected number of real zeros of polynomials with i.i.d. standard normal coefficients is asymptotically
(2/\pi)\log n
. More precisely,

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}({\mathbb{R}})]=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\log n+o(\log n),\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty,

(1.1)

where
N_{n}(S)
, in general, denotes the number of real roots of a random function (in this case a random polynomial) in a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}
.
The asymptotic relation (
1.1
) was later studied and enhanced by other mathematicians, including Hammersley
[
12
]
, Ibragimov and Maslova
[
13
,
14
]
, Wilkins
[
32
]
, Edelman and Kostlan
[
7
]
, and recently H. Nguyen, O. Nguyen and Vu
[
20
]
.

A key step in investigating the expected real zeros of random functions (in general) with centered Gaussian entries has been the renowned Kac-Rice formula, first introduced in the 1940’s by Kac
[
15
]
and independently by Rice
[
29
]
, while studying random polynomials.
In this article, we stick to one out of many different, but more or less equivalent, versions of Kac-Rice’s formula, which was proved by Lubinsky, Pritsker and Xie
[
19
]
.

Proposition

(Kac-Rice Formula)
.

Let
[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{R}},
and consider real-valued functions
f_{j}(x)\in C^{1}[a,b]
,
0\leqslant j\leqslant n
. Define the random function

F_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}f_{j}(x),

where the
a_{j}
are i.i.d. random variables with Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle A_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(f_{j}(x))^{2},

\displaystyle B_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j}(x)f^{\prime}_{j}(x),

and

\displaystyle C_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(f^{\prime}_{j}(x))^{2}.

If
A_{n}(x)>0
on
[a,b],
and there is
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
F^{\prime}_{n}(x)

has at most
M
zeros in
(a,b)
for all choices of
coefficients, then the expected number of real zeros of
F_{n}(x)
in the interval
(a,b)
is given by

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(a,b)]=\frac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\dfrac{\sqrt{A_{n}(x)C_{n}(x)-B_{n}(x)^{2}}}{A_{n}(x)}\,dx.

(
\ast
)

Among the class of random functions, of great importance are random trigonometric polynomials

T_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)+b_{j}\sin(jx),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),

and random cosine polynomials of the form

V_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),

where the coefficients are real and chosen at random. It is quite easy to show that these polynomials have
2n
complex roots, counted with multiplicity. When it comes to the number of real zeros of these polynomials, the famed work of Dunnage
[
6
]
showed that for a random cosine polynomial with i.i.d. standard Gaussian coefficients,

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]=\frac{2n}{\sqrt{3}}+o(n),\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

(1.2)

Further results on the real zeros of random trigonometric polynomials and their asymptotic behavior can be found through the works of Das
[
5
]
, Qualls
[
25
]
, Wilkins
[
33
]
, Flasche
[
10
]
, and extensively in the book of Farahmand
[
8
]
and the references therein.

1.1.
Random trigonometric polynomials with dependent coefficients

There are two main approaches to make the coefficients of a given random trigonometric (cosine) polynomial dependent. One is through the non-trivial correlated coefficients, and the other is to identify suitable, often large enough, number of the coefficients.
Herein, we give some examples for each direction and discuss the resulted asymptotic that has been established in each model. Let us assume that
(a_{j})_{j\geqslant 0}
is a sequence of identically distributed random variables with standard Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
a_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Since
\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}=0
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sigma_{j}^{2}=1
, it is trivial that the correlation between the coefficients satisfies

\rho_{ij}:=\mathrm{corr}(a_{i},a_{j})=\dfrac{\mathrm{cov}(a_{i},a_{j})}{\sigma_{i}\,\sigma_{j}}={\mathbb{E}}[a_{i}a_{j}].

We can indeed correlate the coefficients through a function known as correlation function. Assume that
\rho:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a suitable function and set
{\mathbb{E}}[a_{i}a_{j}]=\rho(\left\lvert i-j\right\rvert)
,
i\neq j
.
It is obvious that
\rho\not\equiv 0
corresponds to the case of dependent Gaussian coefficients. The first works in this direction (non-trivial correlation functions) are due to Sambandham
[
31
]
and Renganathan and Sambandham
[
28
]
.
The authors showed that the asymptotic relation (
1.2
) still remains valid for random cosine polynomials whose coefficients are correlated through either the constant correlation function
\rho\equiv r
or the geometric one defined as
\rho:k\mapsto r^{k}
,
r\in(0,1)
.

Angst, Dalamo and Poly
[
1
]
considered a normalized type of random trigonometric polynomials, also known as Qualls’ ensemble, of the form

f_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)+b_{j}\sin(jx),\quad x\in(0,2\pi),

where the
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are standard Gaussian with the same correlation function
\rho
, and they satisfy the independence condition
{\mathbb{E}}[a_{i}b_{j}]=0
.
They proved that under mild assumptions on the spectral function
\psi_{\rho}
associated with
\rho
, (
1.2
) indeed holds.
The first example in this direction that provides an asymptotic expected number of real zeros of
f_{n}
remarkably deviating from the universal asymptotic (
1.2
) is the work of Pautrel
[
21
]
.
His work concentrates on the expected number of real zeros of
f_{n}
whose coefficients satisfy the correlation function
\rho:k\mapsto\cos(k\alpha)
,
\alpha\geqslant 0
. He proves that for all
\varepsilon>0
and
l\in(\sqrt{2},2]
, there exist
\alpha=\alpha(l)\geq 0
, and infinitely many
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\left\lvert\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]}{n}-l\right\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon.

A recent paper of Angst, Pautrel and Poly
[
2
]
has generalized the main result of
[
1
]
.
The authors have considered Qualls’ ensemble where the coefficients are standard Gaussian, correlated through the same
\rho
with its associated spectral measure
\mu_{\rho}
whose density component (with
respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
) is
\psi_{\rho}
. Under these assumptions, they have proved that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]}{n}=\dfrac{\lambda\{\psi_{\rho}=0\}}{\pi\sqrt{2}}+\dfrac{2\pi-\lambda\{\psi_{\rho}=0\}}{\pi\sqrt{3}},

provided that
\psi_{\rho}
is
C^{1}
with
Hölder derivative on an open set of full measure. As an outcome of this result, see
[
2
, Corollary 2.1]
, it is also shown that as long as
\psi_{\rho}>0
a.e., one obtains

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(a,b)]}{n}\geqslant\dfrac{b-a}{\pi\sqrt{3}}.

The above lower bound in fact supports the conjuncture made in
[
23
]
by this article’s author, which claims that
2n/\sqrt{3}
is the least asymptotic number of real zeros one can expect for high degree random trigonometric polynomials with dependent Gaussian coefficients.

One may adopt quite a different approach to the class of dependent coefficients. One reasonable way to make the coefficients of a given random trigonometric (cosine) polynomial dependent is to identify certain number of coefficients while taking advantage of a broad range of choices of identification models.
The first model we would like to mention is palindromic random cosine polynomials studied by Farahmand and Li
[
9
]
.
They assumed that the first half of the coefficients of
V_{n}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian, and the rest are just their copies identified in a palindromic way, that is,
a_{j}=a_{n-j}
.
Their result that has been corrected in
[
24
, Appendix, pp. 101–107]
reveals that a random cosine polynomial with palindromic Gaussian coefficients obtains approximately
36\%
more expected real zeros than the exemplary one with i.i.d. Gaussian coefficients.
One can identify
blocks
of Gaussian coefficients (of the same length) instead and still study the number of real zeros of random trigonometric polynomials.
Two cases of random cosine polynomials with identified blocks were considered in
[
22
]
.
The first model discussed (identified adjacent blocks) is another example of random cosine polynomials with weakly dependent coefficients meaning that even though the coefficients are not independent,
the expected number of real zeros coincides with the universal asymptotic (
1.2
).
On the other hand, the second model, which involves only two identical block of a significantly large size, provides an example of random cosine polynomials with strongly dependent coefficients, namely the expected number of real zeros deviates from the universal asymptotic (
1.2
).
More recently, the author has investigated the mean number of real zeros of
V_{n}
whose Gaussian coefficients are grouped in blocks of a fixed length
\ell>1
and identified in a palindromic fashion, see
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
.
It turns out that in this model, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]=\frac{2n}{\sqrt{3}}\ \mathrm{K}_{\ell}+{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3/4}),\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty,

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1+\dfrac{3(1-u_{\ell}^{2}(s))}{(1+u_{\ell}(s)\cos(t))^{2}}}\,ds\,dt,

and

u_{\ell}(s):=\sin(\ell s)/(\ell\sin(s)).

In this paper, our main goal is to give another example that goes in the direction of the second approach to dependent coefficients while noting that in this case the asymptotic expected number of real zeros still exceeds
2n/\sqrt{3}
.
This article is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce the class of
\ell
-periodic coefficients and discuss the expected number of real zeros of high degree random trigonometric polynomials with
\ell
-periodic Gaussian coefficients. Section
3
is devoted to the proofs of the results stated in Section
2
.

2.
Random trigonometric polynomials with
\ell
-periodic coefficients

Our motivation behind studying the number of real zeros of random trigonometric (cosine) polynomials with
\ell
-periodic coefficients is the classical work of Szegő
[
27
, p. 260]
as stated below.

Theorem

(Szegő)
.

Let
f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}z^{j}
be a power series with only finitely many distinct coefficients. Then either
{\mathbb{D}}
(the open unit disk) is the domain of holomorphy of
f
or
f
can be extended to a rational function
\hat{f}(z)=p(z)/(1-z^{k}),
where
p(z)\in{\mathbb{C}}[z]
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Proving the above theorem requires showing that if
{\mathbb{D}}
is not the domain of holomorphy of
f
, then from some coefficient on all coefficients are periodic, i.e., there exist
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\lambda<\mu
such that

a_{\lambda+j}=a_{\mu+j}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}.

Therefore, defining
P(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{\lambda-1}a_{j}z^{j}
and
Q(z):=\sum_{j=\lambda}^{\mu-1}a_{j}z^{j}
,
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,
we may write, while setting
k=\mu-\lambda
,

f(z)=P(z)+Q(z)+Q(z)z^{k}+Q(z)z^{2k}+\cdots=P(z)+\dfrac{Q(z)}{1-z^{k}},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

To the contrary, it turns out that trigonometric (cosine) series with only finitely many distinct coefficients behaves quite differently.

Proposition 2.1
.

V_{\infty}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\cos(jz)
with only finitely many distinct coefficients diverges in the entire complex plane.

Proof.

If we set
w=e^{iz}
, we see that

V_{\infty}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\cos(jz)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}w^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}w^{-j}\bigg).

We note that
\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert^{1/j}=1
since we have only finitely many distinct coefficients.
Therefore,
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}w^{j}
diverges in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
, so does
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}w^{-j}
in
{\mathbb{D}}
. Equivalently,
V_{\infty}
diverges in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
.
Note that
V_{\infty}
with only finitely many distinct coefficients diverges in
[0,2\pi)
by the Divergence Test.
This could be shown by assuming to the contrary that
\lim_{j\to\infty}a_{j}\cos(jx_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,M/2)
, where
M:=\min\{\left\lvert a_{j}\right\rvert:a_{j}\neq 0\}
.
Thus, there exists
J\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\left\lvert a_{j}\cos(jx_{0})\right\rvert<\varepsilon
for all
j\geqslant J
.
In particular,
\left\lvert\cos(Jx_{0})\right\rvert<\varepsilon/M<1/2
, and

\displaystyle\left\lvert a_{2J}\cos(2Jx_{0})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geqslant M\left\lvert\cos(2Jx_{0})\right\rvert=M(1-2\cos^{2}(Jx_{0}))>M(1-2\varepsilon^{2}/M^{2})>\varepsilon,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
2\varepsilon^{2}+M\varepsilon-M^{2}<0
, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,M/2)
. Thus,
V_{\infty}
with only finitely many distinct coefficients diverges in
[0,2\pi)
, and by periodicity in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
∎

The above proposition still holds if we replace
V_{\infty}
with
T_{\infty}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\cos(jz)+b_{j}\sin(jz)
.
However, we are still interested in the number of real zeros of partial sums of these infinite series whenever the coefficients are
\ell
-periodic, which is indeed a special case of the coefficients being of finitely many.
To state our desired results, we require the following definition:

Definition 2.1
.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We say
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are
\ell
-periodic if
a_{i+\ell}=a_{i}
for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-\ell
.

For a fixed
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any large enough
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, It is trivial that
n=\ell m-1+r
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.
In what follows, we first investigate the roots of polynomials
P_{n}
,
T_{n}
, and
V_{n}
with
\ell
-periodic coefficients when
r=0
.
We note that in this case,
r=0
, these polynomial asymptotically obtain the largest possible number of zeros.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and set
n=\ell m-1,\ m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}
, where
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are
\ell
-periodic, i.e.,
a_{i+\ell}=a_{i}
for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant n-\ell
.
Then

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z^{\ell m}-1}{z^{\ell}-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}a_{k}z^{k}.

Remark 2.1
.

From the above lemma, it is clear that
P_{n}
has at least
\ell(m-1)=n-\ell+1
zeros that are all unimodular.
We also note that if
\ell=1
, then all zeros are the
m
-th roots of unity other than 1.

Theorem 2.1
.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and set
n=\ell m-1
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{1\}
.
Let
T_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)+b_{j}\sin(jx)
,
x\in(0,2\pi)
.
Assume
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\cup\{b_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\ell-1}
is a family of i.i.d. random variables with Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
We also assume that the coefficients are
\ell
-periodic.
Then

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]=n+1-\ell+\sqrt{n^{2}+\dfrac{\ell^{2}-1}{3}}.

Remark 2.2
.

If
\ell=1
, then all the zeros of
T_{n}
happen to be real. In other words, all the roots of the associated (complex-valued) algebraic polynomial are unimodular, which coincides with the remark on the previous lemma and Proposition 2.1 of
[
21
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and set
n=\ell m-1
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
and let
V_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)
,
x\in(0,2\pi)
.
Assume
A_{0}
, defined as above, is a family of i.i.d. random variables with Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
We further assume that the
a_{j}
are
\ell
-periodic.
Then

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]=2n+{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2/3}),\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty,

where the implied constant (in big
{\mathcal{O}}
notation) depends only on
\ell
.

The case when
r\neq 0
, which is of a more delicate setting, is considered only for a random trigonometric polynomial
T_{n}
due to the similarity of the computations and their estimates. In the following theorem, we give a closed form of the mean number of real zeros of
T_{n}
when the coefficients are
\ell
-periodic and under the assumption that
r\neq 0
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Fix
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\{1\}
, and set
n=\ell m-1+r
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
r\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.
Let
T_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)+b_{j}\sin(jx)
,
x\in(0,2\pi)
.
Assume
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\cup\{b_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\ell-1}
is a family of i.i.d. random variables with Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
We suppose that the
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are
\ell
-periodic and define

\displaystyle{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,r}:=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{1+\dfrac{r(\ell-r)\sin^{2}(s)}{[(\ell-r)\sin^{2}(t)+r\sin^{2}(s+t)]^{2}}}\,ds\,dt.

Then

{\mathbb{E}}[N_{n}(0,2\pi)]=n\,{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,r}+{\mathcal{O}}(n^{4/5}),\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty,

where the implied constant depends only on
\ell
.

Remark 2.4.
Even though
r
is assumed nonzero, setting
r=0
(equivalently
{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,0}=1
) implies that the average number of
probabilistic
zeros of
T_{n}
is asymptotically
n
. Then, considering
n

deterministic
roots of
T_{n}
in one period, the asymptotic expected number of real zeros of
T_{n}
is
2n
, which is now in accordance with the result of Theorem
2.1
.

The following lemma gives a lower bound and an upper bound for the
{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,r}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
0<r<\ell
, we have
{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,r}\in(\sqrt{2},2]
.

3.
Proofs

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

With the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
, one can write

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}z^{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}a_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z^{k+\ell j}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z^{\ell j}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}a_{k}z^{k}\bigg)=\dfrac{z^{\ell m}-1}{z^{\ell}-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}a_{k}z^{k},

which gives us at least
\ell(m-1)=n-\ell+1
deterministic roots with modulus one.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

It follows from
[
11
, 1.341(1,3), p. 29]
that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\cos(2pj+q)x=\frac{\sin(rpx)\cos((r-1)p+q)x}{\sin(px)},

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\sin(2pj+q)x=\frac{\sin(rpx)\sin((r-1)p+q)x}{\sin(px)}.

(3.2)

For
x\in(0,2\pi)
, we apply (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) and observe that

\displaystyle T_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\cos(jx)+b_{j}\sin(jx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}a_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\cos(k+\ell j)x+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}b_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sin(k+\ell j)x

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sin(m\ell x/2)}{\sin(\ell x/2)}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\big[a_{k}\cos(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x+b_{k}\sin(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x\big]

\displaystyle=:\dfrac{\sin(m\ell x/2)}{\sin(\ell x/2)}\,T_{n}^{*}(x).

We first estimate the expected number of real zeros of
T_{n}^{*}
.
We observe that, for
x\in(0,2\pi)
,

\displaystyle A_{n}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\big[\cos^{2}(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x+\sin^{2}(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x\big]=\ell>0,

\displaystyle B_{n}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}[k+(m-1)\ell/2]\sin(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x\,\cos(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}[k+(m-1)\ell/2]\sin(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x\,\cos(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x=0,

and

\displaystyle C_{n}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\big[k+(m-1)\ell/2\big]^{2}\sin^{2}(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\big[k+(m-1)\ell/2\big]^{2}\cos^{2}(k+(m-1)\ell/2)x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\bigg(k+\dfrac{\ell(m-1)}{2}\bigg)^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\bigg(k^{2}+\ell(m-1)k+\dfrac{\ell^{2}(m-1)^{2}}{4}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(\ell-1)\ell(2\ell-1)}{6}+\dfrac{(\ell-1)\ell^{2}(m-1)}{2}+\dfrac{\ell^{3}(m-1)^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ell(3\ell^{2}m^{2}-6\ell m+\ell^{2}+2)}{12}=\dfrac{\ell\big[3(\ell m-1)^{2}+(\ell^{2}-1)\big]}{12}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ell\big[3n^{2}+(\ell^{2}-1)\big]}{12}.

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Phi(f(s))\,ds>\Phi\bigg(\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f(s)\,ds\bigg).

Let us define

f_{\ell,r}(s,t):=\frac{\sqrt{r(\ell-r)}\sin(s)}{(\ell-r)\sin^{2}(t)+r\sin^{2}(s+t)},\quad(s,t)\in(0,\pi)\times(0,\pi).

Hence by Jensen’s inequality we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{C}}_{\ell,r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{1+f_{\ell,r}^{2}(s,t)}\,ds\,dt>\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{1+\bigg[\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f_{\ell,r}(s,t)\,ds\bigg]^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\geqslant\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\bigg[\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}f_{\ell,r}(s,t)\,ds\,dt\bigg]^{2}}=\sqrt{1+J_{\ell,r}^{2}}=\sqrt{2},

which concludes the proof.
