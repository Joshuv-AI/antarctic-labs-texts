Functional Principal Subspace Sampling for Large Scale Functional Data Analysis
Shiyuan He, Xiaomeng Yan

Abstract
Functional data analysis (FDA) methods have computational and theoretical appeals for some high dimensional data, but lack the scalability to modern large sample datasets. To tackle the challenge, we develop randomized algorithms for two important FDA methods: functional principal component analysis (FPCA) and functional linear regression (FLR) with scalar response. The two methods are connected as they both rely on the accurate estimation of functional principal subspace. The proposed algorithms draw subsamples from the large dataset at hand and apply FPCA or FLR over the subsamples to reduce the computational cost. To effectively preserve subspace information in the subsamples, we propose a functional principal subspace sampling probability, which removes the eigenvalue scale effect inside the functional principal subspace and properly weights the residual. Based on the operator perturbation analysis, we show the proposed probability has precise control over the first order error of the subspace projection operator and can be interpreted as an importance sampling for functional subspace estimation. Moreover, concentration bounds for the proposed algorithms are established to reflect the low intrinsic dimension nature of functional data in an infinite dimensional space. The effectiveness of the proposed algorithms is demonstrated upon synthetic and real datasets.

Introduction

The modern era brings us the opportunities and challenges of big data, where both sample size and dimension increase out of the capacity of classical statistical methods. Examples of the large scale datasets include: millions of spectra collected by astronomical surveys (e.g. LAMOST of
Zhao et al. 2012
and SDSS of
Eisenstein et al. 2011
); petabyte amounts of hyperspectral images recorded by airborne or satellite remote sensing
(
Liu et al. 2018
)
; brain medical images through the functional magnetic resonance imaging
(
Tian 2010
;
Turkbrowne 2013
, fMRI)
. A common characteristic of these datasets is that their observations can be viewed as functions over some continuous domain (e.g. temporal or spatial domain), and functional data analysis
(
Kokoszka and
Reimherr 2017
, FDA,)
can serve as a designated toolbox. FDA treats functional observations as realizations of some stochastic process with low intrinsic dimension in an infinite dimensional Hilbert space. This means observations can be effectively approximated by splines, wavelets or linear combinations of a few functional principal components. In contrast to classical multivariate methods, FDA takes these features into consideration to achieve optimal convergence rates. Despite their demonstrated value in theory and application, the current FDA methods lack the scalability for modern large scale datasets
(
Hadjipantelis and
Müller 2018
)
.

Among the current approaches to making statistical methods scalable to large datasets, randomized algorithms have recently gained popularity. The randomized algorithms approach the problem by constructing a substantially smaller sketch of the large scale data at hand. Then, existing methods are applied to the sketch to reduce computation cost. When the sketch keeps the most relevant information, the result computed from the sketch should remain close to the result from the original dataset. There are a few ways to construct a sketch of a large data set. One approach is to draw subsamples with respect to some carefully designed probability, which will select informative samples with larger probability. These sampling probabilities include the importance sampling in matrix multiplication
(
Drineas, Kannan and
Mahoney 2006a
)
, the leverage sampling for least squares regression
(
Drineas et al. 2012
;
Ma, Mahoney and Yu 2015
)
, the subspace sampling for low rank matrix construction
(
Drineas, Mahoney and
Muthukrishnan 2006
)
, etc. Effective sampling probabilities have also been proposed for logistic regression
(
Wang, Zhu and Ma 2018
;
Wang 2019
)
and generalized linear models
(
Zhang, Ning and
Ruppert 2021
)
to minimize the asymptotic variance of the estimator. An alternative way towards sketching is to mix the original data with a random projection and draw samples from the projected data
(
Drineas et al. 2011
;
Wang, Gittens and
Mahoney 2017
)
.

Since the seminal works of
Drineas, Kannan and
Mahoney 2006a
;
Drineas, Kannan and
Mahoney 2006b
;
Drineas, Kannan and
Mahoney 2006c
for matrix multiplication and approximation, the idea of randomized algorithm has been successfully applied to optimization
(
Pilanci and
Wainwright 2015
;
Pilanci and
Wainwright 2017
)
, low rank matrix estimation
(
Halko, Martinsson and
Tropp 2011
;
Drineas, Mahoney and
Muthukrishnan 2006
)
, least squares regression
(
Drineas et al. 2012
)
, nonparametric kernel regression
(
Yang et al. 2017
)
, etc. These algorithms are able to yield comparatively accurate results at reduced computational and storage costs. See the references
Drineas and
Mahoney 2018
;
Woodruff 2014
for an overview. Most theoretical analysis of these randomized algorithms is conducted from the algorithmic perspective, where the analysis is carried out conditionally on an arbitrarily fixed dataset. Some recent works
(
Ma, Mahoney and Yu 2015
;
Raskutti and Mahoney 2016
;
Wang, Gittens and
Mahoney 2017
)
also draw analysis from the statistical perspective, where statistical properties such as bias and average prediction error are considered.

The current literature of randomized algorithms focuses on multivariate statistical methods which are not directly applicable to the functional data setting. Their theoretical results also does not naturally extend to functional data in an infinite dimensional space.
He and Yan 2020
recently studied randomized estimation of functional covariance operator. Still, most functional data methods remain to be explored via the randomized algorithm approach for scalability. Among these methods, functional principal component analysis (FPCA)

(
James, Hastie and
Sugar 2000
;
Yao, Müller and
Wang 2005
;
Peng and Paul 2009
, e.g.)
is one of the most important. Like the classical principal component analysis, FPCA identifies a few orthonormal functional principal components (FPC) explaining most of the variability of a dataset. The computation and theoretical analysis of functional data in an infinite-dimensional space are usually facilitated by FPCA. Indeed, a large amount of FDA methods have been proposed based on FPC. For example, when fitting functional linear regression,
(
Hall et al. 2007
;
Kato 2012
)
first identify a few FPCs of the predictor, and then the model is fitted via the leading FPCs. For functional two-sample test, the test statistic can be constructed based on FPCs
(
Horváth, Kokoszka and
Reeder 2013
)
. The work of
Delaigle et al. 2010
relies on FPCs to define probability density for random functions. More examples can be found in
Horváth and
Kokoszka 2012
.

Due to the fundamental role of FPCA, we study a randomized FPCA algorithm and extend it to functional linear regression (FLR) with scalar response in this work. Our work focuses on the fully observed functional data case, where the function value is known at any point of its domain. In practical applications, for example, these could be functions recorded with a fixed high frequency over a time interval or recorded over a dense spatial grid. This type of data has been widely collected by astronomical spectral surveys, remote sensing, etc, as discussed at the beginning of this section. Specifically, we will view functional observations as indivisible elements in a Hilbert space, and the sketched data is constructed based on subsampling. Our basic algorithm is motivated by the work of
He and Yan 2020
for which we now provide a review.

1.1
Review of the Importance Sampling

Let
\mathscr{H}_{X}
be a Hilbert space equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and an induced norm
\|\cdot\|
.
For example, when the space
\mathscr{H}_{X}=L_{2}(T)
is the set of all square integrable functions over a compact interval
T
, the inner product is
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{T}x(t)x^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
and the norm is
\|x\|=\big(\int_{T}x^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\big)^{1/2}
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
. Given a random element
x\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
with zero mean and finite second moment
\mathbb{E}\|x\|^{2}
, its
covariance operator

\mathscr{C}_{XX}

\mathscr{H}_{X}\mapsto\mathscr{H}_{X}
is a mapping such that
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
is mapped to
\mathbb{E}\langle u,x\rangle x
. Equivalently, we can write
\mathscr{C}_{XX}=\mathbb{E}\left(x\otimes x\right)
, where
\otimes
is tensor product such that
\left(x\otimes x\right)u=\langle x,u\rangle\times x
for any
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
. Covariance operator generalizes the concept of covariance matrix in multivariate statistics.
For any
u,v\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
, it holds that
\langle\mathscr{C}_{XX}u,v\rangle=\mathbb{E}(\langle x,u\rangle\langle x,v\rangle)
. We can see that
\langle\mathscr{C}_{XX}u,v\rangle
quantifies the correlation between the random scalars
\langle x,u\rangle
and
\langle x,v\rangle
. A comprehensive introduction to these FDA concepts can be found in
Hsing and Eubank 2015
.

Algorithm 1

Randomized Covariance Operator Estimation

Suppose we have
N
(not necessarily independent) realizations
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of the random element
x
. The covariance operator
\mathscr{C}_{XX}
can be estimated by the
empirical covariance operator

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\otimes x_{n}
. The empirical covariance operator involves the calculation of
N
tensor products and their summation. This computational cost is overwhelming for large sample size. For example, when each
x_{n}\in L_{2}(T)
is digitally stored as a high dimensional vector of length
L
over a dense grid of
T
, the cost of computing
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
scales as
O(NL^{2})
. In a typical astronomical survey,
L
is in the order of thousands, and
N
has the magnitude of millions. Under this scenario, computing a covariance operator using all available data for scientific research is unrealistic.

Based on the randomized matrix multiplication of
Drineas, Kannan and
Mahoney 2006a
,
the algorithm of
He and Yan 2020
estimates the covariance operator from a sketch of much smaller size. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
. Given a full dataset
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and a sampling probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, it draws with replacement a subsample
\{\tilde{x}_{c}\}_{c=1}^{C}
of size
C
from
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Then, the subsampled empirical covariance operator is computed as

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\tilde{x}_{c}\otimes\tilde{x}_{c}.

(1.1)

Suppose
c
-th subsample is indexed by
i_{c}
in the original dataset, we have set
\tilde{x}_{c}=x_{i_{c}}
and
\tilde{p}_{c}=p_{i_{c}}
correspondingly in the above expression. It is obvious that, conditional on the full dataset
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, the subsampled
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
is an unbiased estimator of the full sample covariance operator
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
with any strictly positive probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. It is shown in
He and Yan 2020
that the optimal sampling probability, which minimizes the expected squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the subsampling error
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
, is of the form
p_{n}\propto\|x_{n}\|^{2}
. This sampling probability will be referred as the
importance sampling
(IMPO) throughout this work.

1.2
Limitation of the Importance Sampling

Recall the purpose of this work is to develop randomized functional principal component analysis (FPCA) and its extension to functional linear regression (FLR).
At first glance, the importance sampling (IMPO) of
He and Yan 2020
is already a plausible sampling strategy. By direct application of perturbation theory, the eigenfunctions (functional principal components) of
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
should not be far away from each other, provided the related eigengap is positive and the difference
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
is small. However, for FPCA and FLR, this importance sampling probability is far from being optimal.

To see the reasons, consider a toy principal component analysis example in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. We generate
N=1000
observations by
\mathbf{x}_{n}=\sigma_{1}\xi_{n1}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}+\sigma_{2}\xi_{n2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}+\sigma_{3}\xi_{n3}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3}
, where the principal component directions
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}=(1,1,0,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}=(1,-1,0,0)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{3}=(0,0,1,1)^{T}/\sqrt{2}
are scaled by
\sigma_{1}=10
,
\sigma_{2}=2
,
\sigma_{3}=0.1
, respectively. The scores
\xi_{n1},\xi_{n2},\xi_{n3}\in\mathbb{R}
are drawn from the standard normal distribution. Figure
1
plots the generated points by their first two coordinates. Suppose we want to estimate the first two principal components
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
from subsamples, and the sampling is taken with respect to the IMPO sampling probability
(
He and Yan 2020
)
. To illustrate the effect of IMPO, we color points with larger
p_{n}\propto\|x_{n}\|^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}\xi_{n1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\xi_{n2}^{2}+\sigma_{3}^{2}\xi_{n3}^{2}
by darker blue in the left penal of Figure
1
. It is evident this probability over-emphasizes the observations along the first direction
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
while neglects the observations along
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
. When
C
subsamples are drawn according to IMPO, the resulting subsamples will likely contain abundant information for estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
, but fail to determine
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
accurately.

In the above example, the first two scaling factors only have a moderately large ratio
\sigma_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{2}^{2}=25
, but their effect in determining
p_{n}
is not negligible. To resolve the issue, we may consider to remove the scaling factor to get a new sampling probability
p_{n}\propto\xi_{n1}^{2}+\xi_{n2}^{2}
. The points in the right penal of Figure
1
are colored accordingly. Notice this probability puts equal sampling weights for observations along
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
. The subsamples, which are drawn by this probability, are more likely to contain balanced information for estimating the first two principal components. Moreover, this idea can be generalized to infinite dimensional space to estimate the leading
R(>0)
functional principal components.

points
\mathbf{x}_{n}=(x_{n1},x_{n2},x_{n3},x_{n4})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

. Points with larger sampling probability has darker blue color.

1.3
Contributions of This Work

This work develops randomized algorithms for functional principal component analysis (FPCA) and functional linear regression (FLR) with scalar response. Our methodological and theoretical contributions are summarized in the following paragraphs.

In Section
2
, a randomized algorithm is developed for FPCA. Motivated by the example from Section
1.2
, we propose the
functional principal subspace sampling probability
(FunPrinSS) to effectively preserve subspace information in subsamples. The FunPinSS probability removes the eigenvalue scale effect inside the functional principal subspace; and at the same time, it properly weights the complementary subspace residual. The latter part is necessary because we need to control the interaction between the leading and the remaining eigenfunctions in the infinite dimensional space. As the exact value of the FunPrinSS probability is unknown unless we execute full sample computation, a fast and theoretically justifiable algorithm is proposed for practical implementation.

For the theoretical analysis of the randomized FPCA algorithm, the task of precisely estimating the leading
R
eigenfunctions is translated into estimating the projection operator of their spanned subspace. The analysis is conducted from an algorithmic perspective, which is conditional on the full dataset and imposes minimal assumption (on eigengap). With operator perturbation theory, we are able to decompose the subsample estimation error into a first order term and a higher order term. The proposed FunPrinSS probability is then justified from two aspects:

(i)

From a heuristic aspect, we show the proposed probability enjoys the property of precisely controlling the first order error. In fact, the FunPrinSS probability can be interpreted as an importance sampling for the first order error. This means FunPrinSS for FPCA plays a role similar to that of the IMPO sampling
(
He and Yan 2020
)
in minimizing the difference
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
for covariance operator.

(ii)

From a rigorous aspect, we develop concentration bounds for the proposed algorithm. The constants of the derived bounds reflect the low intrinsic dimension nature of functional data. That is, the subsample uncertainty is characterized by the dimension of the functional principal subspace and the intrinsic dimension of the residual covariance operator. These dimension parameters are usually small for functional data, and as a result, our theory indicates the subsample size need not be very large to control the subsample error within a desired accuracy level.

The randomized FPCA algorithm and its analysis naturally extend to the functional linear regression setting in Section
4
. For FLR as an inverse problem in the Hilbert space, the involved operator inverse is not well defined due to its infinitely many eigenvalues decreasing to zero. We study an estimator whose operator inverse is truncated after its leading
R
functional principal components. It follows that the accuracy of the FLR estimator relies on the precision of the subsampled estimates of functional principal components. Based on the FunPrinSS probability, a randomized algorithm for FLR is proposed.

For the theoretical justification the randomized FLR algorithm, we conduct a careful analysis of the subsample prediction error and decompose it into three components: the functional principal subspace estimation error, the truncated inverse operator estimation error, and an orthogonal residual error. The error decomposition reveals distinct sources of uncertainty during subsampling. Like FPCA, our FLR theoretical bound is further established via the operator perturbation expansion of the related error components.

The proposed randomized algorithms are closely related to the survey based functional data analysis methods
(
Cardot and
Josserand 2011
;
Cardot, Degras and
Josserand 2013
;
Degras 2014
;
Lardin-Puech, Cardot and
Goga 2014
, see e.g.)
. These works study the estimation of the mean (or total) function, covariance function, or eigenfunctions for a population (with possibly unknown size). Classical survey sampling strategies (e.g. simple sampling without replacement and stratified sampling) are employed to draw functional samples. Most of the existing works focus on estimating the mean or total function. When auxiliary variables are available,
Cardot, Goga and
Lardin 2013
construct a model-assisted estimator of the mean function. For eigenfunction estimation,
Cardot et al. 2010
establish the asymptotic design unbiasedness and asymptotic variance of the estimator for general survey sampling probabilities. However, no specific strategy is proposed to reduce the asymptotic variance of the eigenfunction estimator. To our best knowledge, functional linear regression has not been considered and analyzed yet in the survey based functional data analysis framework.

\mathscr{J}:\mathscr{H}_{X}\mapsto\mathscr{H}_{X}
be a bounded linear operator defined on
\mathscr{H}_{X}
. The

is defined by
\|\mathscr{J}\|:=\sup_{u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}:\,\|u\|\leq 1}\|\mathscr{J}u\|
. Assume that
\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a complete orthonormal system for
\mathscr{H}_{X}
, the
Hilbert–Schmidt norm
for

is defined by
\|\mathscr{J}\|_{HS}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\mathscr{J}e_{j},e_{j}\rangle^{2}
. Besides, its

is computed via
\text{intdim}(\mathscr{J}):=\mathrm{tr}(\mathscr{J})/\|\mathscr{J}\|
, where the
trace
of
\mathscr{J}
is defined as
\mathrm{tr}(\mathscr{J}):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle\mathscr{J}e_{j},e_{j}\rangle
. In this work, the
N
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is equipped with a normalized inner product
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{N}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{i}
for any vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

2
Functional Principal Subspace Sampling for FPCA

Functional principal component analysis
(
Horváth and
Kokoszka 2012
;
Ramsay 2004
, FPCA,)
is the cornerstone for many functional data analysis methods. To reduce the computation cost of FPCA over a large dataset, we develop a randomized algorithm for FPCA in Section
2.1
. To retain the most relevant principal subspace information after subsampling, we propose the functional principal subspace sampling (FunPrinSS) probability in Section
2.2
. A practical implementation of our algorithm is discussed in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Randomized FPCA

FPCA generalizes the classical multivariate principal component analysis to identify the modes of variations among a dataset. Classical multivariate principal components can be computed from the eigen-decomposition of a covariance matrix. Similarly, functional principal components can be estimated via the
eigenvalue-eigenfunction decomposition
of the empirical covariance operator
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
, where
\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}\geq\cdots
are non-negative eigenvalues, and

\hat{\theta}_{r}
’s are the corresponding eigenfunctions (or called
functional principal components
). According to the Karhunen-Loève (KL) expansion, each sample
x_{n}
can be represented by

x_{n}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}\hat{\xi}_{nr}\hat{\theta}_{r},

(2.1)

where
\hat{\xi}_{nr}
is the
r
-th
principal component score
of the
n
-th sample. It is computed by
\hat{\xi}_{nr}=\langle x_{n},\hat{\theta}_{r}\rangle/\hat{\sigma}_{r}
when
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}>0
, and we set
\hat{\xi}_{nr}=1
when
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}=0
. Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}=(\hat{\xi}_{1r},\cdots,\hat{\xi}_{Nr})^{T}
denote the vector of the
r
-th score for all samples. It satisfies the normalizing property
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}\|_{N}=1
. From the KL expansion and the orthonormality of the eigenfunctions, we can also find that
\|x_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{r}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{nr}^{2}
.

Randomized FPCA

When the eigenvalues

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\approx\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
for some
R
. Besides, each sample can be approximated by
x_{n}\approx\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\sigma}_{r}\hat{\xi}_{nr}\hat{\theta}_{r}
. In practice, obtaining the leading
R

\hat{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\theta}_{R}
relies on the computation and decomposition of the empirical
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}

1
, computing
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
is an expensive procedure for a large dataset. We propose to estimate the full sample
\hat{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\theta}_{R}
based on a subsampled dataset. In particular, we can apply Algorithm
1
to get a subsampled covariance operator
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
, and perform eigenvalue-eigenfunction decomposition of
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
instead of
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
. This is summarized in Algorithm
2
for some sampling probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where the probability satisfies
p_{n}\geq 0
and
\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}=1
. The algorithm will output subsampled eigenfunction estimates
\tilde{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\theta}_{R}
. The effectiveness of Algorithm
2
relies on suitably choosing the sampling probability, which will be discussed in details in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Functional Principal Subspace Sampling Probability

Algorithm
2
leaves the sampling probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
unspecified. As noted in Section
1.2
, though
p_{n}\propto\|x_{n}\|^{2}
is optimal in controlling the error
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
in terms of Hilbert-Schmidt norm, it is far from being optimal for the principal subspace estimation. The problem arises because the eigenvalues
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}
’s place unequal weights on distinct principal component directions for
p_{n}\propto\|x_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{r}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{nr}^{2}
. To remove the influence of eigenvalues in determing the sampling probability, we propose the
functional principal subspace sampling (FunPrinSS) probability
, which is

p_{n}^{\mathrm{Exact}}=\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{nr}^{2}+\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}\|^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\big[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{mr}^{2}+\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{m}\|^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\big]},

(2.2)

where
I
is the identity operator (i.e.,
Ix=x
for any
x\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
), and

\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
is the projection operator of the subspace spanned by
\hat{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\theta}_{R}
.
For the
n
-th sample, the numerator of (
2.2
) includes two parts: (i) the sum of squared scores in the principal subspace
\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{nr}^{2}
; and (ii) the squared norm of
(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}
, which is the observation projected out of the principal subspace. The second part of the numerator is necessary because, in addition to the first
R
scores, all the remaining scores will affect the operator perturbation
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
(see Lemma
6
of Section
3.2
and the followed discussion). However, it is not practical to compute all the remaining scores in the infinite dimensional function space for a large dataset.
For this reason, we adopt the squared norm of the projected residual
\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}\|^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}
in (
2.2
) to account for the effect of the remaining scores.

The proposed sampling probability (
2.2
) is denoted as “Exact” because its value is determined by the full sample eigenfunctions
\hat{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\theta}_{R}
and the related scores
\hat{\xi}_{nr}
. In practice, its exact value is unknown unless we carry out full sample FPCA computation. Fortunately, practitioners only need an approximate value of (
2.2
) as input for Algorithm
2
. A probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is regarded as an approximation to (
2.2
) if

p_{n}\geq p_{n}^{\mathrm{Exact}}/\beta

(2.3)

for some
fixed

\beta\geq 1
. We call a sampling probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
satisfying (
2.3
)
nearly exact FunPrinSS probability
. Section
2.3
will develop a fast algorithm to obtain an estimate
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
fulfilling the lower bound requirement (
2.3
) with high probability. Our theoretical analysis of the randomized FPCA will also be based on any sampling probability satisfying (
2.3
).

The normalizing constant for the sampling probability (
2.2
) has an interesting interpretation. Define
\mathscr{R}:=\sum_{r=R+1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\big(\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}\big)
as the residual operator obtained by removing the first
R
eigenpairs from
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
, i.e.
\mathscr{R}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\big(\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}\big)
. Let
\Delta_{R}
be the intrinsic dimension of
\mathscr{R}
such that

\Delta_{R}:=\mathrm{intdim}(\mathscr{R})=\mathrm{tr}(\mathscr{\mathscr{R}})/\|\mathscr{R}\|=\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2},

(2.4)

if
\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}>0
; and
\Delta_{R}=0
if
\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}=0
.
Then, for the normalizing constant in the denominator of (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}\big[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{mr}^{2}+\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})

\displaystyle x_{m}\|^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\big]=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\big[\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{mr}^{2}+\sum_{s=R+1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\hat{\xi}_{ms}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=N\big(R+\sum_{s=R+1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\big)\leq N\big(R+\Delta_{R}\big),

(2.5)

where the second equality uses the score normalizing property
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}\|_{N}=1
for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}=(\hat{\xi}_{1r},\cdots,\hat{\xi}_{Nr})^{T}
. The last inequality uses that
\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}
. The above states that the normalizing constant in (
2.2
) is upper bounded by
N(R+\Delta_{R})
. In particular, the quantity
R+\Delta_{R}
is a summation of the principal subspace dimension
R
and the residual intrinsic dimension
\Delta_{R}
.

: The dataset
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
;
the subsample size
C
; the number

.

.

for each

Apply Algorithm
2
with

\{p^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
C
to get the pilot estimates
(\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{r})^{2}
and
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
for
r\leq R
.

for

do

Algorithm 3

Estimating the Functional Principal Subspace Probability

Recall the exact value of (
2.2
) is not available unless we obtain
\hat{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\theta}_{R}
with full sample computation. This would contradict the goal of reducing computational cost via subsampling. In practice, a two-step procedure can be carried out:

Step 1.

Obtain an estimate
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of the exact sampling probability (
2.2
) via the randomized procedure in Algorithm
3
.

Step 2.

Plug the estimated probability
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
into Algorithm
2
, which will gives the final estimator of the functional principal components.

Specifically, in the first step, Algorithm
3
estimates the sampling probability (
2.2
) as follows. At first (Line 1–2), it executes Algorithm
2
with the sampling probability

p_{n}^{\prime}=\alpha(1/N)+(1-\alpha)\|x_{n}\|^{2}/\Big(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}\Big),

for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The probability
\{p_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n=1}^{N}
is a mixture of the uniform sampling probability and the importance sampling probability
(
He and Yan 2020
)
. This provides a pilot estimate of the first
R
eigenvalues
(\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{r})^{2}
and eigenfunctions
\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
in Line 2 of Algorithm
3
. At the same time, a pilot estimation of the projection operator
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}^{\prime}_{R}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
is obtained. With these pilot estimates, we compute the score estimates
\hat{\xi}^{\prime}_{nr}=\langle\tilde{\theta}^{\prime}_{r},x_{n}\rangle/\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}_{r}
for each sample in Line 4 of Algorithm
3
.
Then, the probability estimator
\hat{p}_{n}\propto\big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat{\xi}_{nr}^{\prime})^{2}+\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}^{\prime}_{R})x_{n}\|^{2}/(\hat{\sigma}^{\prime}_{R})^{2}\big)
is computed for each sample in Line 5. Algorithm
3
will output the normalized
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. In Section
3.3
, it will be shown that the probability
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
computed by Algorithm
3
satisfies the lower bound requirement (
2.3
) with high probability.

Remark 1
.

Two-step procedures can often be found in the literature of randomized algorithms. In
Drineas et al. 2012
, the leverage scores are approximately computed by sketching the design matrix. In the work of
Wang, Zhu and Ma 2018
;
Zhang, Ning and
Ruppert 2021
for generalized linear regression, the sampling probabilities are also computed from pilot regression estimators.

Remark 2
.

The computational complexity of the above two-step procedure can be analyzed when each functional observation is digitally recorded as a high dimensional vector of length
L
. In this case, the eigenfunction can be obtained by directly applying SVD to the data matrix. The full sample computational complexity is
\mathscr{O}(NL\min\{N,L\})
. On the other hand, the proposed Algorithm
2
costs a complexity of order
\mathscr{O}(NRL+CL\min\{C,L\})
, where computing the sampling probabilities via Algorithm
3
costs
\mathscr{O}(NRL)
.

Compared with the full sample computation, the proposed algorithm has much smaller complexity when
C\ll N
and
R\ll L
. Specifically, the relation
R\ll L
is a characteristic of functional data, whose intrinsic dimension is much smaller than the ambient dimension. Recall from Section
1
, in practical applications such as astronomical spectral processing,
L
is in the order of thousands, and
N
has the magnitude of millions. Some work
(
Connolly et al. 1994
)
has found
R=3
principal components are enough to capture most variability of the dataset. Theorem
8
suggests the subsample size
C
need not to be very large for accurate subsample estimation, due to the small dimension parameter
R+\Delta_{R}
for functional data.

3
Theoretical Analysis of Randomized FPCA with FunPrinSS

This section develops a theoretical justification of Algorithm
2
when the employed sampling probability satisfies (
2.3
). The derivation is based on the operator perturbation theory reviewed in Section
3.1
, and the main results follow in Section
3.2
. After that, the theoretical justification of Algorithm
3
is presented in Section
3.3
. We will show its estimated probability satisfies the lower bound requirement (
2.3
) with high probability. Finally, we discuss practical choice of the parameter
R
in Section
3.4
.

Our analysis formulates estimating the leading
R
eigenfunctions as estimating the projection operator
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
. Algorithm
2
delivers a randomized estimate
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{\theta}_{r}\otimes\tilde{\theta}_{r}
, which is computed from the sketched data. We will focus on bounding their difference
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
. The theoretical analysis is carried out from an algorithmic perspective. In other words, the concentration bounds are established conditional on the full dataset, and the derivation exploits the independence of the subsampling draws within our algorithms. The full dataset can be almost arbitrary and the theoretical bounds provide worst-case guarantee. Neither specific distribution nor independence assumption is imposed on the full dataset. Only a single condition on the eigenvalues is required.

Condition 3
.

The empirical eigenvalues of
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
satisfy the following relation:

\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}>\hat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}>\cdots>\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}>\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{R+2}^{2}\geq\cdots.

Remark 4
.

A strictly positive eigengap
g_{R}=\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}>0
is the minimal assumption for the identifiability of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
. To simplify presentation, we further assume the leading
R
eigenvalues
\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2},\cdots,\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}
are distinct with multiplicity one. If there exists repeated eigenvalues, the same theoretical results can be obtained using a similar argument but with complications in notation. Lastly, note we always have
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}=0
for
r>N
, because the empirical
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
is computed from
N
samples, i.e.
\text{rank}\big(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\big)\leq N
.

3.1
Operator Perturbation Theory

We set up the tools from the operator perturbation theory
(
Hsing and Eubank 2015
)
for further analysis of the randomized FPCA. For the operator
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
, its
resolvent
is defined as
\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta):=(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\eta I)^{-1}
, where
I
is the identity mapping. Let
\Gamma_{R}:=\{\eta\in\mathbb{C}:\ \mathrm{dist}(\eta,\,[\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}])=g_{R}/2\}
represent the boundary of a disk in a complex plane. Every point on
\Gamma_{R}
has equal distance
g_{R}/2=(\hat{\sigma}_{R}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1})/2
to the interval
[\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2},\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}]\subset\mathbb{R}
on the positive real axis.
Then, the subspace projection operator can be expressed as a contour integration

\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{R}}\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta
.

Denote
\mathscr{E}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
as the covariance operator approximation error. Then, using the result of
Koltchinskii
et al. 2016
, the difference of the projection operators can be decomposed as
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=L_{R}(\mathscr{E})+S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
, where

\displaystyle L_{R}(\mathscr{E}):

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{R}}\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\mathscr{E}\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\mathrm{d}\eta,\,

(3.1)

\displaystyle S_{R}(\mathscr{E}):

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-L_{R}(\mathscr{E}).

(3.2)

Notice the first term
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
is linear in
\mathscr{E}
, while
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
is the residual error. Because
\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{E})=0
where the expectation is taken with respect to the subsampling procedure, the error
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
in (
3.1
) also has zero expectation
\mathbb{E}L_{R}(\mathscr{E})=0
.

Recall
g_{R}=\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}(>0)
is the eigengap.
In the case
\|\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\|<g_{R}/3
, it holds that

\max_{r}|\tilde{\sigma}_{r}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}|\leq\|\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\|<g_{R}/3.

This implies that the first
R
eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
are strictly in the interior of the disk
\Gamma_{R}
. Therefore, the relation
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{R}}\mathscr{R}_{\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta
holds for the subsampled estimator with
\mathscr{R}_{\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)=(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\eta I)^{-1}
. In this case, the residual approximation error
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
has the integral expression

S_{R}(\mathscr{E})=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma_{R}}\sum_{k\geq 2}[-\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\mathscr{E}]^{k}\mathscr{R}_{\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}}(\eta)\mathrm{d}\eta.

From the above, we can see that
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
can be regarded as the first order approximation error for

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
, while
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
as the higher order error. In fact, according to Lemma 2 of
Koltchinskii
et al. 2016
, the two terms
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
and
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
can be bounded as in the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

For the full sample covariance operator
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
and its subsampled counterpart
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
, denote
\mathscr{E}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
as their difference. Consider the decomposition
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=L_{R}(\mathscr{E})+S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
with
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
and
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), respectively. It holds that

(i)\quad\|L_{R}(\mathscr{E})\|\leq\big[1+(\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2})/(\pi g_{R})\big]\times\|\mathscr{E}\|;\quad(ii)\quad\|S_{R}(\mathscr{E})\|\leq K_{R}\left(\|\mathscr{E}\|/g_{R}\right)^{2}.

where
K_{R}:=15[1+2(\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2})/(\pi g_{R})]
.

3.2
Main Results

We are now ready to derive our main theorem for the randomized FPCA in Algorithm
2
with the nearly exact sampling probability (
2.3
). Before presenting the formal result, we provide a heuristic justification of the proposed functional principal subspace sampling probability (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). The decomposition in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) suggests that, to minimize the loss
\|\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}\|
, we need to have precise control over the first order error
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
; at the same time, the magnitude of
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
should also be contained with high probability. Our proposed FunPrinSS probability backs up this intuition. In fact, the probability (
2.2
) or (
2.3
) can be interpreted as an importance sampling probability to control the first order error
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
. The argument is based on the following fact.

Lemma 6
.

The first order error term
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
can be expressed as
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})=(1/C)\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathscr{Z}_{c}/(N\tilde{p}_{c})
. Each
\mathscr{Z}_{c}
is related to one subsampled
\tilde{x}_{c}
and that

\mathscr{Z}_{c}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}\hat{\sigma}_{s}}{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}}\times(\tilde{\xi}_{cr}\tilde{\xi}_{cs})\times\big[\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{s}+\hat{\theta}_{s}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}\big].

In the above,
\tilde{\xi}_{cs}=\langle\tilde{x}_{c},\hat{\theta}_{s}\rangle/\hat{\sigma}_{s}
when
\hat{\sigma}_{s}>0
and
\tilde{\xi}_{cs}=1
when
\hat{\sigma}_{s}=0
.

Remark 7
.

Note the quantity
\tilde{\xi}_{cs}
associated with
\tilde{x}_{c}
is computed with the full sample eigenvalue
\hat{\sigma}_{s}
and the full sample eigenfunction
\hat{\theta}_{s}
. Suppose the
c
-th subsample is the
i_{c}
-th observation in the original dataset (i.e.,
\tilde{x}_{c}=x_{i_{c}}
), and the original
x_{i_{c}}
has full sample scores
\hat{\xi}_{i_{c},1},\hat{\xi}_{i_{c},2},\cdots
. Recall we have set
\tilde{p}_{c}=p_{i_{c}}
as the sampling probability of the original
i_{c}
-th observation. Similarly, we denote for the
c
-th subsample
\tilde{\xi}_{cs}=\hat{\xi}_{i_{c},s}
(
s=1,2,\cdots
) as the corresponding full sample score.

Lemma
6
reveals that
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
can be expressed as a summation of
C
terms, and each summand
\mathscr{Z}_{c}
is related to one subsampled
\tilde{x}_{c}
. For the importance sampling,
the sampling probability is proportional to the size of each summand.
We can show the proposed probability (
2.2
) or (
2.3
) serve this purpose by computing the squared norm of each summand
\mathscr{Z}_{c}
in Lemma
6
as

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{Z}_{c}\|_{HS}^{2}=2\sum_{r=1}^{R}

\displaystyle\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}}{[\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}]^{2}}\cdot\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}\tilde{\xi}_{cs}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad 2\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{4}}{[\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}]^{2}}}_{f_{rs}^{2}}\cdot\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}(\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\tilde{\xi}_{cs}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}).

(3.3)

Define
f_{rs}:=(1-q_{r,s})^{-1}
with
q_{r,s}=\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}
for the above equation.
For
r<s
, it is obvious that
q_{r,s}\leq 1
and
f_{rs}\geq 1
.
Consider the special case where the leading
R
principal components dominate the overall signal, i.e.,
\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\gg\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}
. In this case, we have the approximation
f_{rs}\approx 1
for
r(\leq R)
and
s(>R)
. It follows approximately that

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{Z}_{c}\|_{HS}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx 2\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}\big(\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\tilde{\xi}_{cs}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}2\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\tilde{\xi}_{cs}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}+\sum_{s=R+1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\tilde{\xi}_{cs}^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}\tilde{\xi}_{cr}^{2}+\|(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})\tilde{x}_{c}\|^{2}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\Big)^{2}.

(3.4)

In the above, (i) uses that
\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\geq\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}
for
r\leq R
, and (ii) uses the inequality
4ab\leq(a+b)^{2}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. As expected, the last line of (
3.4
) indicates the approximate proportional relation
\tilde{p}_{c}^{\mathrm{Exact}}\propto\|\mathscr{Z}_{c}\|_{HS}
for the FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
). This resembles the importance sampling
\tilde{p}_{c}\propto\|\tilde{x}_{c}\otimes\tilde{x}_{c}\|_{HS}=\|\tilde{x}_{c}\|^{2}
for the covariance operator estimation (
1.1
) in
He and Yan 2020
. From here, we find the proposed FunPrinSS probability can be viewed as an importance sampling probability to control
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
when
\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\gg\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}
.

The above heuristic justification is developed in the case
\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}\gg\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}
. Under the general eigenvalue setting of Condition
3
, to control the first order term
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
, we define

G_{R}:=(1-q_{R,R+1})^{-1}=\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}/(\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}),

and find
it as an upper bound for the ratio factor
f_{rs}
in (
3.2
). That is, for
r\leq R<s
, we have

f_{rs}=(1-q_{r,s})^{-1}\leq(1-q_{R,R+1})^{-1}=G_{R}.

This relation holds because
f(q)=1/(1-q)
is an increasing function for
q\in(0,1)
. Theorem
8
below formalizes this idea and provides theoretical guarantees for the subsampled FPCA in Algorithm
2
with the nearly exact sampling probability (
2.3
). Note all the probability bounds in this work are derived conditional on the full dataset and with respect to the subsampling.

Theorem 8
.

Under Condition
3
, define
G_{R}=\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}/(\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2})
and define

V=\max\{G_{R}^{2}Z^{2}/(2\beta),\,Z\}\quad\text{and}\quad L=\max\{G_{R}Z/\sqrt{2},\,Z+1\},

with

Z=\beta(R+\Delta_{R})
. Then, for sampling probability

\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
satisfying
p_{n}\geq p_{n}^{\text{Exact}}/\beta

with
\beta\geq 1
, and for
\epsilon
satisfying
\epsilon\cdot C\geq\sqrt{CV}+L/3
, it holds that

\|\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}\|\leq\epsilon+K_{R}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}\epsilon^{2}/g_{R}^{2},

(3.5)

with probability at least
1-12(R+\Delta_{R})\exp\Big(-\frac{C\epsilon^{2}/2}{V+L\epsilon/3}\Big).

In the above theorem,
G_{R}
can be viewed as a measurement of the eigengap
\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{R+1}^{2}
. The dimension
R+\Delta_{R}
discussed in (
2.5
) also appears in multiple places above. On the right hand side of the error bound (
3.5
), the first term
\epsilon
bounds the first order error
L_{R}(\mathscr{E})
in (
3.1
), while the second term
K_{R}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}\epsilon^{2}/g_{R}^{2}
bounds the high order
S_{R}(\mathscr{E})
in (
3.2
).

According to (
3.5
) of Theorem
8
, the subsample error
\|\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}\|
can be controlled within a precision level
\epsilon
with high probability. For a large enough
C
and with probability at least
0.9
, the bound (
3.5
) holds for
the precision level
\epsilon
of magnitude order
\mathscr{O}\big((R+\Delta_{R})\log^{1/2}[120(R+\Delta_{R})]/C^{1/2}\big)
. The precision level
\epsilon
is inversely proportional to
C^{1/2}
, and is proportional to the dimension parameter
R+\Delta_{R}
with an additional logarithm factor. The nature of functional data usually indicates the dimension parameter
R+\Delta_{R}
is small, though the ambient space dimension is infinite. This means the subsample size
C
need not be very large to control the subsample error toward a small accuracy level
\epsilon
.

3.3
Theoretical Justification of Algorithm
3

Algorithm
3
can be justified for providing the pilot probability estimate. We show that its estimated probability
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
remains close to the target (
2.2
) in the sense of (
2.3
). Its proof is derived in Section
E
in the Appendix.

Proposition 9
.

Set
\Delta_{0}=\mathrm{intdim}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX})
,

\gamma_{0}=\sqrt{(\Delta_{0}+1)\log(120\Delta_{0})}
and
G_{R}=(1-q_{R,R+1})^{-1}
. For large enough subsample size
C
,
with probability at least
0.9
, the pilot sampling probability
\{\hat{p}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
computed by Algorithm
3
with
\alpha=0.5
satisfy

\min\Big\{\frac{p^{\mathrm{Exact}}}{\hat{p}_{n}},\,\frac{\hat{p}_{n}}{p^{\mathrm{Exact}}}\Big\}\,\geq\,\frac{1-\gamma_{1}C^{-1/2}-\gamma_{2}C^{-1}}{1+\gamma_{1}C^{-1/2}+\gamma_{2}C^{-1}},

where
\gamma_{1}=35(1+G_{R})(R+\Delta_{R})+8\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\gamma_{0}/\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}

and
\gamma_{2}=32K_{R}\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{6}G_{R}^{2}/(g_{R}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2})
.

One implication of Proposition
9
is that, when the subsample size
C
is large enough, it holds with high probability that
\hat{p}_{n}\geq p^{\mathrm{Exact}}/\beta
for
\beta=\frac{1+\gamma_{1}C^{-1/2}+\gamma_{2}C^{-1}}{1-\gamma_{1}C^{-1/2}-\gamma_{2}C^{-1}}
. This corresponds to our definition of the nearly exact FunPrinSS probability (
2.3
).
Besides, the value of
\beta(\geq 1)
will converge to one as
C
increases to infinity.

3.4
Choice of
R

For functional principal component analysis, the subspace dimension
R
is usually chosen based on
the fraction of the variance explained (FVE) by the leading
R
eigenfuctions. That is,
R
is chosen such that the FVE is greater than a pre-specified threshold value (e.g.
90\%
).
Given the full sample estimate
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
, its fraction of variance explained can be computed by

\widehat{\text{FVE}}=\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}}{\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}x_{n}\|^{2}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}};

and correspondingly, the fraction of variance explained by the subsampled estimate
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
is

\widetilde{\text{FVE}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}x_{n}\|^{2}/\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}
.
As a direct consequence of Theorem
8
, we can bound the difference between the
\widehat{\text{FVE}}
of the full sample estimator and the

\widetilde{\text{FVE}}
of the subsampled estimator.

Corollary 10
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
8
, it holds that

\big|\widehat{\mathrm{FVE}}-\widetilde{\mathrm{FVE}}\big|\leq\epsilon+K_{R}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}\epsilon^{2}/g_{R}^{2},

(3.6)

with probability at least
1-12(R+\Delta_{R})\exp\big(-\frac{C\epsilon^{2}/2}{V+L\epsilon/3}\big).

Proof.

With Theorem
8
, the result (
3.6
) is straightforward by observing

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}x_{n}\|^{2}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}}-\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}x_{n}\|^{2}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle x_{n},(\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}\rangle}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{n}\|^{2}}\Big|\leq\epsilon+K_{R}\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}\epsilon^{2}/g_{R}^{2}.

The last inequality employs
|\langle x_{n},(\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}\rangle|\leq\|\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}\|\cdot\|x_{n}\|^{2}
.
∎

Corollary
10
indicates
\widetilde{\text{FVE}}
is a reliable estimate of

\widehat{\text{FVE}}
. When applying the randomized algorithm, we can use the criterion
\widetilde{\text{FVE}}
to select
R
. After obtaining the subsampled estimate
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
, computing

\widetilde{\text{FVE}}
requires one additional scan over the whole dataset and have a computational complexity
\mathscr{O}(NRL)
. This complexity is no more than that of Algorithm
3
, see Remark

.

4
Extenstion: Randomized Functional Linear Regression

A considerable number of functional data models rely on functional principal components for dimension reduction.
One of these models is functional linear regression (FLR) with scalar response. In this section, we extend the results from Section
2
and Section
3
to FLR and develop its theoretical guarantees.

4.1
Randomized FLR

FLR is a generalization of the classical linear regression to the functional setting. For a functional predictor
x_{n}\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
and a scalar response
Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, FLR assumes the relation
Y_{n}=\alpha+\langle x_{n},\Psi\rangle+\epsilon_{n}
for
n=1,\cdots,N
. In this model,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the intercept term,
\Psi\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
is the regression function and
\epsilon_{n}
is some random error with zero mean. When both
x_{n}
and
\Psi
belong to
L_{2}(T)
for some compact interval
T
, the regression model is written as
Y_{n}=\alpha+\int_{T}x_{n}(t)\Psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\epsilon_{n}
.
Given any estimator
\widehat{\Psi}
, the intercept is directly available via
\hat{\alpha}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}(Y_{n}-\langle x_{n},\widehat{\Psi}\rangle)
. In the following, we focus on the estimation of the regression function
\Psi
by assuming that
Y_{n}
and
x_{n}
have been centered with zero mean, such that
\hat{\alpha}=0
.

Considerable amount of works has been proposed for estimating
\Psi

(
Yao et al. 2005
;
Yuan and Cai 2010
, e.g.)
. One of the commonly used estimators for
\Psi
is based on the truncated inverse of the empirical covariance operator
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\otimes x_{i}
; see
Hall et al. 2007
;
Cardot, Mas and Sarda 2007
. Suppose the covariance operator admits the eigenvalue-eigenfunction decomposition
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}\cdot\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
. Its rank-
R

truncated inverse
is defined as
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}^{+}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}(1/\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2})\cdot\hat{\theta}_{r}\otimes\hat{\theta}_{r}
. Based on this, the regression function is estimated by
\widehat{\Psi}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}^{+}\hat{z}
with
\hat{z}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}x_{n}
.

Input
: Dataset
\{(x_{n},Y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
; sampling probability
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
; subsample size
C
.

Output
: the regression function
\widetilde{\Psi}

1:

for

c=1,\cdots,C

do

2:

Sample
(\tilde{x}_{c},\tilde{Y}_{c})
from
\{(x_{n},Y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
and get

\tilde{p}_{c}
with
\mathbb{P}\big((\tilde{x}_{c},\tilde{Y}_{c},\tilde{p}_{c})=(x_{n},Y_{n},p_{n})\big)=p_{n}.

3:

end

for

4:

Compute
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\tilde{x}_{c}\otimes\tilde{x}_{c}
and
\tilde{z}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\tilde{Y}_{c}\tilde{x}_{c}
.

5:

Compute
\widetilde{\Psi}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}\tilde{z}

Algorithm 4

Base Randomized FLR

To reduce the cost of computing
\widehat{\Psi}
from the full sample,
we adapt the randomized algorithm to estimate it based on a subset of the data. The algorithm will sample a subset
\{(\tilde{x}_{c},\tilde{Y}_{c})\}_{c=1}^{C}
of observation pairs from the full sample
\{(x_{n},Y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
. The sampling is taken with replacement and according to some probability distribution
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Given the subsampled pairs, we can compute two quantities,

\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\tilde{x}_{c}\otimes\tilde{x}_{c}
and
\tilde{z}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\tilde{Y}_{c}\tilde{x}_{c}
, which are unbiased estimators of
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}
and
\hat{z}
, respectively. Finally, the full sample regression function is estimated by its subsampled counterpart
\widetilde{\Psi}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}\tilde{z}
. This procedure is listed in Algorithm
4
.

In Algorithm
4
, notice the truncated inverse
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}
is computed from the eigenvalue-eigenfunction decomposition of the subsampled
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
. It is evident that the accuracy of the randomized estimator
\widetilde{\Psi}
relies on the precision of the principal subspace estimated by
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}
. Our proposed FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
) is a proper choice for the randomized FLR to capture the principal subspace information.

In practice, the FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
) is unknown without full sample computation. We can carry out a two-step procedure similarly to that of Section
2.3
. Firstly, the FunPrinSS probability is estimated via Algorithm
3
. Secondly, the estimated probability is supplied to Algorithm
4
to obtain
\widetilde{\Psi}
. As noted in Proposition
9
, the probability obtained in the first step is close to the target FunPrinSS probability with high probability when the subsample size
C
is large enough.

Application of the randomized FLR algorithm also requires the specification of the principal subspace dimension
R
. Practitioners can choose
R
such that the fraction of variance explained (FVE) by the principal subspace is close to
100\%
. In this way, most of the variability of the predictor
x
is captured and the remaining variability can be regarded as being negligible.

Remark 11
.

When the functional observation is recorded as a high dimensional vector of length
L
,
the computational complexity of such two-step procedure can be similarly analyzed as in Remark
2
.
In this case, the computational complexity of FLR with full sample is
\mathscr{O}(NL\min\{L,N\}+L(R+N))
. The proposed two-step procedure for randomized FLR requires
\mathscr{O}(CL\min\{C,L\}+(C+NR)L)
. Compared with the full sample computation, the proposed algorithm has much smaller complexity when
C\ll N
and
R\ll L
.

4.2
Analysis of Randomized FLR with FunPrinSS

We continue to theoretically analyze the subsampled regression estimator
\widetilde{\Psi}
obtained from Algorithm
4
with the nearly exact sampling probability (
2.3
). Toward this target, we express the full sample estimator
\widehat{\Psi}
and the subsampled estimator
\widetilde{\Psi}
in compact expressions. These expressions resemble the commonly used ones for the classical linear regression. First of all, define an operator
\mathscr{T}:\mathscr{H}_{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that an element
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
is mapped to

\mathscr{T}u=(\langle x_{1},u\rangle,\cdots,\langle x_{N},u\rangle)^{T}.

(4.1)

Let
\mathbf{Y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{N})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{N})^{T}
be
N
-dimensional vectors of the responses and the residuals, respectively. Then, the regression model for all
N
observations can be expressed as
\mathbf{Y}=\mathscr{T}\Psi+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. The operator
\mathscr{T}
plays a similar role as the design matrix for classical linear regression.

Recall from the end of Section
1
, the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is equipped with the normalized inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{N}
. We denote
\mathscr{T}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathscr{H}_{X}
as
the adjoint operator of
\mathscr{T}
. For any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
, it holds that

\langle\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{a},u\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\langle\mathbf{a},\mathscr{T}u\rangle_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\langle x_{n},u\rangle=\big\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}x_{n}/N,\ u\big\rangle.

In the above, (i) uses the definition of adjoint operator, and (ii) employs (
4.1
).
From the above equation, we find that the adjoint operator
\mathscr{T}^{*}
maps any
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to
(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}x_{n}
. Moreover, for any
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
, we can find that

\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{T}u=\mathscr{T}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}\langle x_{1},u\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle x_{N},u\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle x_{n},u\rangle x_{n}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}u.

The above equation implies that
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{T}
. Similarly, we can verify
\hat{z}=\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{Y}
. Combining these results, the full sample estimator of the regression function can be equivalently expressed as
\widehat{\Psi}=\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}^{+}\hat{z}=(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{T})^{+}(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{Y})
.

To find a closed form expression for the subsampled
\widetilde{\Psi}
,
we represent the subsampling process by an operator
\mathscr{D}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}
. As a mapping between Euclidean spaces,
\mathscr{D}
can be represented by a matrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times N}
. The
(c,n)
-th entry of the matrix
\mathbf{D}
is
D_{cn}=1/\sqrt{Np_{n}}
if
x_{n}
is selected as the
c
-th subsample; all the other entries are zero. The adjoint operator of
\mathscr{D}
is denoted as

\mathscr{D}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Note the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{C}
is also equipped with the normalized inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{C}
, such that
for
\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
we have
\langle\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\rangle_{C}=(1/C)\sum_{c=1}^{C}a^{\prime}_{c}b^{\prime}_{c}
. Now for any
\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}

and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

\langle\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{D}\mathbf{b}\rangle_{C}=\frac{1}{C}\langle\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{D}\mathbf{b}\rangle_{2}=\frac{1}{C}\langle\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{2}=\langle(N/C)\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{N},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
is the classical (unnormalized) inner product for Euclidean space, which is the summation of element-wise products. From the above equation, we can see that the matrix representation of
\mathscr{D}^{*}
is
(N/C)\mathbf{D}^{T}
, which is the transpose of
\mathbf{D}
and multiplied by a ratio factor
N/C
. With some computation, we can verify that for any
u\in\mathscr{H}_{X}

\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathscr{T}u=(N/C)\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{D}\begin{pmatrix}\langle x_{1},u\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle x_{N},u\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{N\tilde{p}_{c}}\langle\tilde{x}_{c},u\rangle\tilde{x}_{c}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}u.

It follows that
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}=\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathscr{T}
, and

\tilde{z}=\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathbf{Y}
can be verified due to a similar reasoning.
This leads to a compact expression for the randomized regression estimator

\widetilde{\Psi}=\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}\tilde{z}=(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathscr{T})^{+}(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathbf{Y})
. The above results are summarized in Lemma
12
below, which gives an alternative expression of the full sample estimator and the subsampled estimator obtained via Algorithm
4
.

Lemma 12
.

The full sample regression estimator can be expressed as

\widehat{\Psi}=(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{T})^{+}(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{Y}),

and
the subsampled estimator can be expressed as

\widetilde{\Psi}=(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathscr{T})^{+}(\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D}\mathbf{Y}).

Now we show that the subsampled estimator provides a good approximation of the full sample estimator under suitable criterion. Our aim is to characterize the closeness between the full sample estimator
\widehat{\Psi}
and the subsampled estimator
\widetilde{\Psi}
. In the literature of functional linear regression, strong consistency of the estimated regression function is not generally possible. The estimators are only guaranteed to converge in some weak norm such as the prediction error on a new sample
(
Cardot, Mas and Sarda 2007
;
Yuan and Cai 2010
, see)
.
We choose to measure the difference
\widetilde{\Psi}-\widehat{\Psi}
based on the full sample prediction error
\|\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle x_{n},\widetilde{\Psi}-\widehat{\Psi}\rangle^{2}
. When only a fraction of the whole dataset is employed to estimate
\widetilde{\Psi}
, this metric partially gauges the quality of

\widetilde{\Psi}
by the samples out of the subsampled (training) dataset.
Our theoretical target boils down to the analysis of the error
\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}
. Lemma
13
below provides a decomposition of the subsampling error, the proof of which can be found in Section
D
in the Appendix. Recall that
x_{n}
can be represented as
x_{n}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\hat{\sigma}_{r}\hat{\xi}_{nr}\hat{\theta}_{r}
due to the Karhunen-Loève expansion (
2.1
). The following decomposition involves an operator
\mathscr{T}^{+}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathscr{H}_{X}
, which maps
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
to
\mathscr{T}^{+}\mathbf{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}x_{n}^{+}\in\mathscr{H}_{X}
, with

x_{n}^{+}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\xi}_{nr}\hat{\theta}_{r}/\hat{\sigma}_{r}
corresponding to each
x_{n}
.

Lemma 13
.

The subsampling error in full sample prediction has the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}=\underbrace{\mathscr{T}(\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})\mathscr{T}^{+}\mathbf{Y}}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathscr{T}(\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX})\mathscr{T}^{*}(\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D})\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}}_{\mathrm{II}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\mathscr{T}\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}\mathscr{T}^{*}(\mathscr{D}^{*}\mathscr{D})\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}}_{\mathrm{III}}.

(4.2)

In the above,
\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}=\mathbf{Y}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}

is the residual of the full sample estimator. In addition,
we have that

\mathscr{T}\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}^{+}\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}.

(4.3)

Lemma
13
shows that the subsampling error
\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}
can be decomposed into three interpretable terms. The first term (I) accounts for the subsampling estimation error of the principal subspace
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
. The second term (II) arises due to the difference between
the truncated inverses
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}
.
The third term (III) can be interpreted as follows.
Notice we have
\mathscr{T}\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}^{+}\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}=\mathbf{0}
by the last conclusion of Lemma
13
. This relation holds due to the similar reason that the residual is orthogonal to the column space of the design matrix for classical linear regression. In fact, (
4.3
) holds because our
\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}
is
orthogonal to the space spanned by the first
R
score vectors , i.e.,
\text{span}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{R})

with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}=(\hat{\xi}_{1r},\cdots,\hat{\xi}_{Nr})^{T}
. This orthogonality is violated due to subsampling, which explains the appearance of the last term (III) in (
4.2
). With Lemma
13
and the operator perturbation expansion techniques in Section
3.1
, we can establish the following result for the randomized FLR.

Theorem 14
.

Under Condition
3
,
set
Z_{1}=\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{6}K_{R}/(g_{R}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{R}^{2})
and
Z_{2}=\beta(R+\Delta_{R})
. In addition, let
V=2+(2+G_{R}^{2})Z_{2}^{2}/\beta
and

L=\sqrt{2R}+(\sqrt{2}+G_{R}/\sqrt{2})Z_{2}
.
Suppose
C
subsamples are obtained according to the probability

\{p_{n}\}
satisfying

p_{n}\geq p_{n}^{\mathrm{Exact}}/\beta

with some
\beta\geq 1
. Then with probability at least

\displaystyle 1-16(R+\Delta_{R})\exp\Big(-\frac{C\epsilon^{2}}{V+L\epsilon/3}\Big)-\frac{3Z_{2}}{\epsilon^{2}C},

(4.4)

it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\big(\epsilon+\hat{\sigma}_{R}Z_{1}/\hat{\sigma}_{1}\epsilon^{2}\big)\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{N}}_{a}+\underbrace{4\big(\epsilon+Z_{1}\epsilon^{2}\big)\|\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}\|_{N}}_{b}.

(4.5)

In the above,
\epsilon
satisfy
\epsilon\geq\sqrt{V/C}+L/(3C)
and
\epsilon\leq\min\{1,g_{R}/3\}
.

Theorem
14
directly implies that, given a subsample size
C
, we can control
\|\mathscr{T}\widetilde{\Psi}-\mathscr{T}\widehat{\Psi}\|_{N}
within a precision level
\epsilon
in (
4.5
) with high probability. For a large enough
C
and with probability at least
0.9
, the bound (
4.5
) holds for the precision level
\epsilon
of magnitude order
\mathscr{O}\big((R+\Delta_{R})\log^{1/2}[320(R+\Delta_{R})]/C^{1/2}\big)
.

The right hand side of (
4.5
) contains two parts. The first term (a) bounds the subspace difference (I) in (
4.2
), and it is an error term relative to
\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{N}
. The second term (b) bounds (II) and (III) in (
4.2
) together, and its magnitude is relative to
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}\|_{N}
.

To help get a better understanding of the two terms on the right hand side of (
4.5
), we can relate them to similar quantities in finite dimensional problems. For the randomized classical linear regression
(
Drineas et al. 2011
)
, the error bound can be simplified to one term containing
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}\|_{N}
. This is because their corresponding predictors
x_{n}
span a finite dimensional space. Their work agrees with our result when
\mathrm{dim}(\mathscr{H}_{X})
is finite and relatively small, and we directly take
R
to be
\mathrm{dim}(\mathscr{H}_{X})
without truncation. Indeed, with large enough subsample size
C
, the finite dimensional space can be easily recovered, and
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R}=\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
is guaranteed with high probability. This implies that the term (I) will vanish in (
4.2
) for linear regression, and consequently the first term (a) will disappear in (
4.5
). The result (
4.5
) would become an error bound whose magnitude is only related to the size of the residual,
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}\|_{N}
.

When
\mathrm{dim}(\mathscr{H}_{X})
is finite and
R<\mathrm{dim}(\mathscr{H}_{X})
, our randomized FLR corresponds to randomized multivariate principal component regression (PCR). In the literature, additive error bound relative to
\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{2}
has been established for the randomized PCR
(
Mor-Yosef and Avron 2019
, see. Theorem 5 of)
. Our theoretical bound (
4.5
) reveals different sources of uncertainties whose sizes are proportional to
\|\mathbf{Y}^{\perp}\|_{N}
or
\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{N}
. This is a consequence of the subsample error decomposition in Lemma
13
.

Notice the precision parameter
\epsilon
has an upper bound
g_{R}/3
in the theorem. This is because we need to control the difference between the truncated inverses
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{+}_{XX}
. The difference is generally unbounded unless we require
\|\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\|
to be smaller than the eigengap
g_{R}
. Thereby, the theorem is derived conditional on the event

\|\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}-\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{XX}\|\leq(1/3)g_{R}
, which holds at least with the stated probability (
4.4
).

This section conducts a simulation study for assessing the randomized functional principal component analysis in Algorithm
2
and the randomized functional linear regression in Algorithm
4
. Specifically, for these randomized algorithms, we will consider three sampling probabilities: 1) the uniform sampling probability (UNIF),
p_{n}=1/N
; 2) the importance sampling probability
(
He and Yan 2020
, IMPO,)
,
p_{n}=\|x_{n}\|^{2}/\sum_{m=1}^{N}\|x_{m}\|^{2}
; 3) the proposed functional principal subspace sampling probability (FunPrinSS) estimated by Algorithm

with
\alpha=0.5
. These sampling probabilities will be supplied to the randomized algorithms, and the accuracy of the subsampled estimation will be compared.

5.1
Data Generation

In each replication of the simulation study, we will generate one synthetic dataset
\{x_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
as follows. Each observation
x_{n}
is a continuous function defined over the compact interval
T=[0,1]
. In particular, it is generated according to
x_{n}(t)=\sum_{r=1}^{50}\sigma_{r}\xi_{nr}\theta_{r}(t)
, where the
\theta_{r}
is set as the Fourier function which is
\theta_{r}(t)=\sqrt{2}\sin(2\pi rt)
when
r
is odd, and
\theta_{r}(t)=\sqrt{2}\cos(2\pi rt)
when
r
is even. The scores
\xi_{nr}
’s are independent random variables with zero mean and unit variance. For the random function
x_{n}
, its covariance operator
\mathscr{C}_{XX}
is determined by the integral
(\mathscr{C}_{XX}u)(t^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}k(t,t^{\prime})u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
for any
u\in L_{2}([0,1])
, with integral kernel
k(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sigma_{r}^{2}\theta_{r}(t)\theta_{r}(t^{\prime})
. We can see the
r
-th eigenvalue and eigenfunction of
\mathscr{C}_{XX}
are
\sigma_{r}^{2}
and
\theta_{r}
, respectively.

The settings to specify the eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{r}^{2}\}_{r=1}^{50}
and the distribution of
\xi_{nr}
is described in Table
1
.
The eigenvalues
\{\sigma^{2}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{50}
are specified as two cases:
Exponential Decay (ED)
and
Polynomial Decay (PD)
. The decline rate of the PD eigenvalues is slower than the ED eigenvalues. Hence, the eigengap for the PD setting is smaller, and the estimation of the subspace spanned by the first R eigenfunctions is more difficult.

Similar to the simulation setting of
Ma, Mahoney and Yu 2015
, the random scores,
\xi_{nr}
’s, are independently drawn from one of the three distributions:
Nearly Uniform (NU)
distribution, for which
\xi_{nr}
’s are drawn independently from standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
;
Moderately Nonuniform (MN)
distribution, where the scores
\xi_{nr}
’s are independently generated by t-distribution with three degree of freedom and scaled to unit variance; and
Very Nonuniform (VN)
distribution, where
\xi_{nr}
follows t-distribution with one degree of freedom and scaled to unit variance. Notice among these three distributions, the score distribution for
NU
has the lightest tail, while has the heaviest tail for
VN
. As a result, the functions in a synthetic dataset from
NU
will behave more homogeneously. On the other hand, the synthetic datasets from
MN
or
VN
are likely to contain some observations with extreme magnitude.
Ma, Mahoney and Yu 2015
note observations in the VN setting could have drastically different leverages, adversely affecting the naive uniform sampling.

A
Comparison with sampling probabilities in the multivariate context

There are some underlying connections between the FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
) for functional data and some sampling probabilities for multivariate statistics in the literature. In this section, we discuss some of the similarities and differences.

The FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
) is closely related to the leverage sampling probability
(
Drineas et al. 2012
)
for classical linear regression. Let
\mathbf{X}=\left(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\right)^{T}
be a
N\times p
fixed design matrix with
N
observations and
p
variables (with
N>p
).
Suppose the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal matrix with singular values, and the columns of
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
contain the left and right singular vectors, respectively. Denote
U_{nj}
as the
(n,j)
-th element of
\mathbf{U}
.
The leverage sampling probability
p_{n}
is proportional to the
statistical leverage

h_{nn}
of
n
-th observation, i.e.

p_{n}\propto h_{nn}=\mathbf{x}_{n}^{T}\left(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-}\mathbf{x}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}U_{nj}^{2}.
When the samples
\mathbf{x}_{n}
’s have zero mean,
U_{nj}
can be interpreted as multivariate principal component scores, and the statistical leverage
h_{nn}
is the summation of all squared scores in the
p
-dimensional space. In contrast, our FunPrinSS probability (
2.2
) makes a rank-
R
truncation to the Karhunen-Loève expansion (
2.1
) of the infinite dimensional functional data, and takes into account the effect of the remaining scores via the projected residual
(I-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}_{R})x_{n}
.

The FunPrinSS probability is also connected to the subspace sampling of
Drineas, Mahoney and
Muthukrishnan 2006
. To perform the CUR decomposition for a matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
, they compute the row sampling probabilities as follows. A matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times q}
is formed by sampling columns from
\mathbf{X}

B
Concentration Inequality for Compact Operators

Theorem 7.3.1 of
Tropp et al. 2015
developed intrinsic matrix Bernstein concentration inequality. Together with the techniques in Section 3.2 of
Minsker 2017
, it can be easily extended to compact operators.
Similar results can also be found in Lemma 5 of
Dicker et al. 2017
.
In the below,
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}
are two Hilbert spaces.

Lemma 15
.

For a finite sequence of random operators
\mathscr{Z}_{i}
mapping from
\mathscr{H}_{1}
to
\mathscr{H}_{2}
, they satisfies
\mathbb{E}\mathscr{Z}_{i}=0
and
\|\mathscr{Z}_{i}\|\leq L
.
Suppose their summation is
\mathscr{S}=\sum_{i}\mathscr{Z}_{i}
and
\mathscr{S}^{*}
is its adjoint. Let
\mathscr{V}_{1}
and
\mathscr{V}_{2}
be semidefinite upper bounds for
\mathrm{Var}_{1}(\mathscr{S})
and
\mathrm{Var}_{2}(\mathscr{S})
, respectively. That is,

\mathscr{V}_{1}\succeq\mathrm{Var}_{1}(\mathscr{S})=\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{S}\mathscr{S}^{*})=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{Z}_{i}\mathscr{Z}_{i}^{*})
, and
\mathscr{V}_{2}\succeq\mathrm{Var}_{2}(\mathscr{S})=\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{S}^{*}\mathscr{S})=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{Z}_{i}^{*}\mathscr{Z}_{i})
. Define an intrinsic bound
d=\mathrm{intdim}(\mathscr{V}_{1})+\mathrm{intdim}(\mathscr{V}_{2})
and a variance bound

v=\max\{\|\mathscr{V}_{1}\|,\|\mathscr{V}_{2}\|\}
, then for
t\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
,

\mathbb{P}(\|\mathscr{S}\|\geq t)\leq 4d\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{v+Lt/3}\right).

The next lemma provides upper bound for self-adjoint operator.

Lemma 16
.

For a finite sequence of random self-adjoint operators
\mathscr{Z}_{i}
mapping from
\mathscr{H}_{1}
to
\mathscr{H}_{1}
, they satisfies
\mathbb{E}\mathscr{Z}_{i}=0
and
\|\mathscr{Z}_{i}\|\leq L
. Suppose their summation is
\mathscr{S}=\sum_{i}\mathscr{Z}_{i}
, and
\mathscr{V}
is the semidefinite upper bound for

\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{Z}_{i}^{2})
.
Define an intrinsic bound
d=\mathrm{intdim}(\mathscr{V})
and a variance bound

v=\|\mathscr{V}\|
, then for
t\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
,

\mathbb{P}(\|\mathscr{S}\|\geq t)\leq 4d\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{v+Lt/3}\right).
