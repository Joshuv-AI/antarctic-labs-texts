Implicit Shape Completion via Adversarial Shape Priors
Abhishek Saroha, Marvin Eisenberger, Tarun Yenamandra, Daniel Cremers

Abstract
We present a novel neural implicit shape method for partial point cloud completion. To that end, we combine a conditional Deep-SDF architecture with learned, adversarial shape priors. More specifically, our network converts partial inputs into a global latent code and then recovers the full geometry via an implicit, signed distance generator. Additionally, we train a PointNet++ discriminator that impels the generator to produce plausible, globally consistent reconstructions. In that way, we effectively decouple the challenges of predicting shapes that are both realistic, i.e. imitate the training set’s pose distribution, and accurate in the sense that they replicate the partial input observations. In our experiments, we demonstrate state-of-the-art performance for completing partial shapes, considering both man-made objects (e.g. airplanes, chairs, …) and deformable shape categories (human bodies). Finally, we show that our adversarial training approach leads to visually plausible reconstructions that are highly consistent in recovering missing parts of a given object.



1
Introduction

In recent times, neural implicit models experienced a steep increase in popularity as a tool for 3D shape modeling.
The high flexibility and representation power of such approaches make them often preferable to other, alternative 3D representations like voxel grids, point clouds, polygonal meshes, or grid-based SDFs.
For once, neural implicit methods are grid-free and can, in theory, represent objects up to an arbitrary resolution. In practice, this enables them to adapt the level of precision to different regions of a given surface – the resulting encoding is, at the same time, compact and precise.
In comparison to explicit surface representations like meshes, they can represent geometries of an arbitrary topology, only requiring the target surface to be watertight.
Moreover, implicit surfaces can be converted to other familiar representations in a straightforward manner, e.g. voxel grids by thresholding, or triangle meshes via the classical marching cubes algorithm
[
20
]
.

A common approach in this line of work is to devise encoder-decoder architectures
[
21
,
24
,
25
,
6
]
to convert various types of input data into high-fidelity 3D surfaces. The decoder, in this context, predicts the occupancy probability or signed distance values for a set of xyz-point coordinates. This methodology has been used successfully to convert point clouds into watertight surfaces, reconstruct the 3D geometry from image observations or perform voxel super-resolution
[
40
,
6
,
28
]
.
On the other hand, while such encoder-decoder approaches excel at reproducing objects from the training distribution, they often lack robustness when generalizing to unseen test poses.

One of the key insights of our work is that we can extend the generalization capacity of neural implicit generative models by utilizing adversarial shape priors. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
10
]
are a ubiquitous tool for generative modeling on the 2D image domain. By and large, the popularity of GANs is based on their ability to generate novel data instances that imitate the style of the training distribution. While standard encoder-decoder architectures merely replicate the exact training samples, GANs are able to generate truly novel datapoints. Motivated by this observation, in this work we leverage the power of adversarial shape priors, in combination with a neural implicit architecture
[
24
,
17
]
.

A central open problem in the context of 3D generative models is shape completion from partial input observations. Common use-cases include shape reconstruction from range sensors like LIDAR, or completing partially occluded inputs.
Many existing methods define shape completion as filling in local information, completing the backside of a front-facing human
[
6
]
, or completing halves of symmetric objects (e.g. left side of airplanes)
[
24
]
.
In contrast, we consider shape completion of point clouds that show truly arbitrary views of an object with varying degrees of partiality. By definition, this necessitates a holistic view on a given input observation: Completing a given snippet requires (1) encoding and categorizing the partial input point cloud (2) inferring the appropriate global geometry and (3) reconstructing the full shape, while taking both (1) and (2) into account. In an encoder-decoder architecture, (1)-(3) are strongly correlated. In our experiments, we demonstrate that, by including adversarial shape priors, our method is able to disentangle (1) and (2) to a certain extent. Overall, this leads to significantly more robust predictions on partial snippets of unseen test poses. There are a number of recent works that aim at increasing the fidelity of neural implicit surface decoders by leveraging more local information
[
25
,
6
]
. Our results indicate that this is not always preferable for shape completion, which requires modeling semantic information about a given object on the level of the global shape pose.

Contribution

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.

We devise a novel neural network architecture based on implicit surfaces that predicts complete, watertight meshes from partial input point cloud observations.

2.

We learn enhanced shape priors via generative adversarial learning. In that manner, our model effectively decouples the tasks of reconstructing meaningful shapes and fitting them to partial inputs.

3.

Our method achieves state-of-the-art performance for completing partial point cloud observations of both man-made objects (lamps, chairs, …) and non-rigidly deformable shapes (human bodies).

Related work

The field of 3D generative models experienced a surge in popularity over the last few years. In the following, we provide an overview of approaches most closely related to ours. We focus on related neural implicit models and methods that are specifically designed for 3D shape completion.

While point cloud generation models have similar advantages

The initial works

,

sparked countless follow-up works that build up on the idea of generating 3D shapes with neural implicits. There are a number of works combine neural implicit models with local, grid-based convolutions for different applications like creating human avatars

, representing 3D scenes

Another related line of work

Shape completion

The central task we consider in this work is shape completion, i.e. reconstructing a full object from a partial observation. A standard approach to that end is learning shape priors via an encoder-decoder architecture and then predicting optimal reconstructions by fixing the learned decoder. This approach has been explored individually for both voxelized, man-made objects

3
Method

The overall objective of our method is to perform shape completion of partial point cloud observations. Specifically, the goal is to map a partial input point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
to a full, reconstructed triangular mesh
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{rec}}:=\bigl(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{rec}},\bm{F}_{\mathrm{rec}}\bigr)
, where
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{rec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 3}
and
\bm{F}_{\mathrm{rec}}\subset\bm{V}_{\mathrm{rec}}\times\bm{V}_{\mathrm{rec}}\times\bm{V}_{\mathrm{rec}}
are the sets of vertices and faces of
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{rec}}
, respectively. We analogously write
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}:=\bigl(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{full}},\bm{F}_{\mathrm{full}}\bigr)
for the full, watertight input training shapes. Furthermore, we denote
\bm{U}=(\bm{u}_{i})_{i=1,\dots,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times 3}
as a set of randomly sampled, uniform points from the unit sphere
\bm{u}_{i}\in\bigl\{\bm{x}:\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq 1\bigr\}
. During training, the points
\bm{u}_{i}
are used as query points to train the Deep-SDF signed distance prediction network. In the following, we define our neural network architecture in
Section

3.1
and loss function in
Section

3.2
. We further provide additional implementation details in
Section

3.3
.

Figure 1
:
Overview
. Our model takes a partial point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
and generates signed distance predictions
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}
for a uniform set of points
\bm{U}
, conditioned on the encoding
\bm{z}:=\mathcal{E}(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}})
. Similarly, it predicts distances for the input points
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
in order to generate a shape that matches the partial observations. At test time, the resulting distance field
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}
is converted to the full, reconstructed shape
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}
.
The discriminator incentivizes meaningful output shapes by learning to distinguish between generated distance fields
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}
and corresponding real samples
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}
.

3.1
Network

The core of our method is an adversarial
[
10
]
, implicit surface neural network architecture
[
21
,
24
]
, see
Figure

1
for an overview. In the following, we define its individual components:

Encoder

The encoder
\mathcal{E}
is responsible for converting the partial input point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
into a high-dimensional latent code
\bm{z}
.

We can write this mapping
\mathcal{E}
as

\mathcal{E}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}^{512}\\
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}\mapsto\bm{z},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
is the partial input point cloud and
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{{512}}
is the
512
-dim predicted latent encoding.
More specifically, we choose
\mathcal{E}
as a point cloud architecture based on the classification network of PointNet
[
26
]
. In comparison to more sophisticated point cloud processing networks
[
27
,
39
,
33
]
, PointNet has no local feature detection mechanism and mainly focuses on the global geometric structure of a considered object.
In fact, the main objective of the sub network
\mathcal{E}
is to encode the global pose context of
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
whereas, detecting local features is of secondary importance. Additionally, this makes our method robust to varying input sampling densities, which we demonstrate in
Figure

5
. Apart from being a straightforward choice, we therefore found empirically that the bias of PointNet towards global geometric features serves our purpose best in this context.

Generator

The generator
\mathcal{G}
(or decoder) is responsible for generating 3D surfaces that match the underlying training distribution. The network architecture of
\mathcal{G}
is based on existing work on Deep-SDF decoders
[
17
,
24
]
, see our supplementary material for more details. For a given set of query points
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}
, which are provided to the network as the input, the generator
\mathcal{G}
predicts signed distance values to the target surface.

In summary, we can define
\mathcal{G}
as

\mathcal{G}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{512}\to\mathbb{R}\\
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{z})\mapsto d_{i}:=\mathrm{SDF}_{\mathcal{G},\bm{z}}(\bm{x}_{i}),\end{cases}

(2)

where
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{{k\times 3}}
are the query points with the corresponding, predicted signed distance values
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{{512}}
is a
512
-dim latent vector predicted by the encoder
\mathcal{E}
.

During training, we consider two different types of query points
\bm{x}_{i}
as inputs to
\mathcal{G}
: uniformly sampled points
\bm{x}_{i}:=\bm{u}_{i}
and partial input surface points
\bm{x}_{i}:=\bm{v}_{\mathrm{part},i}
. The former represents the predicted output shape
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{rec}}
and the latter is used to train the reconstruction loss, see
Section

3.2
for more details.
By construction of
Equation

2
, the generator
\mathcal{G}
processes each input point
\bm{x}_{i}
independently.
For convenience, we therefore simply concatenate both of these signals
\bm{U}=(\bm{u}_{i})_{i=1,\dots,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times 3}
and
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}=(\bm{v}_{\mathrm{part},i})_{i=1,\dots,n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
in practice and obtain a single input point cloud
(\bm{U},\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}})\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+n)\times 3}
with which we query
\mathcal{G}
during training. This corresponds to the orange/blue matrices in
Figure

1
.

Discriminator

The final component of our method is the discriminator network
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
, playing the role of the critic that classifies generated point clouds into real/fake:

\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}:~\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R},~~\bm{d}\mapsto c.

(3)

The mapping
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
takes a signed distance field
\bm{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
as input. During training, we consider either the distances
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}:=\mathcal{G}(\bm{U},\bm{z})
predicted by the generator or the ground-truth field
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}
corresponding to the full training shapes
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}
, see
Section

3.2
for more details. The subscript
\bm{U}
of the discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
highlights its implicit dependency on the uniform query point cloud
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times 3}
. In practice, we concatenate
\bm{U}
and
\bm{d}
to a 4D input point cloud
(\bm{U},\bm{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times 4}
within the first layer of
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
.
The output score
c\in\mathbb{R}
is used subsequently used to compute the GAN loss, see
Section

3.2
for more details.

Following the common methodology of GANs on images, the
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
acts as a critic that encourages
\mathcal{G}
to imitate the training shape distribution and synthesize more realistic samples. To that end, we chose the PointNet++ architecture
[
27
]
as the basis for the discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
. Compared to the standard PointNet network
[
26
]
, PointNet++ decomposes a given input point cloud into multiple subregions in a hierarchical manner. Hence, it is much more effective at detecting local features, which is a major prerequisite for obtaining realistic reconstructions. Note, that this is in stark contrast to the encoder network
\mathcal{E}
, whose main objective is detecting the global pose features of a considered object. To further illustrate this point, we show a comparison of the two different discriminators, in an ablation study, see our supplementary material.

3.2
Loss function

To train our model, we define the following loss function:

\ell\bigl(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}\bigr):=\ell_{\mathrm{GAN}}\bigl(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}\bigr)+\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\ell_{\mathrm{rec}}\bigl(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}\bigr)+\lambda_{\mathrm{norm}}\ell_{\mathrm{norm}}\bigl(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}\bigr).

(4)

Motivated by the standard GAN methodology, our generator
\mathcal{G}
and discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
are trained in an alternating manner, playing the role of each other’s adversary. The discriminator tries to distinguish samples generated by the generator from real training samples, which in turn drives
\mathcal{G}
to synthesize more realistic shapes.

\min_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}}\max_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}}\ell\bigl(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}\bigr).

(5)

In practice, we optimize
Equation

5
in an alternating manner, taking single update steps of
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
for a single batch, respectively. In the remainder of this section, we define the individual components referred to in
Equation

4
.

GAN loss

We devise the GAN loss component of our method by following the popular image model Wasserstein-GAN (WGAN)
[
2
]

\ell_{\mathrm{GAN}}={\mathbb{E}_{\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}}}\bigl[\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}})\bigr]-{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}}}\bigl[\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}\bigl(\mathcal{G}(\bm{U},\mathcal{E}(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}))\bigr)\bigr]+\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}GP

(6)

where we sample from both the training distributions of real samples
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}
and partial conditional inputs
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
. Here,
GP
denotes a gradient penalty term, defined as

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)}\biggl[\bigl(\bigl\|\nabla_{\bm{d}}\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}\bigl((1-\lambda)\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}+\lambda\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}\bigr)\bigr\|_{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\biggr],

(7)

where
\mathcal{U}(0,1)
denote the uniform distribution on the interval
(0,1)
. Furthermore,
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full}}
and
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{rec}}=\mathcal{G}(\bm{U},\mathcal{E}(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}))
are defined as the signed distances of the full input training shapes and the distance field predicted by the generator, respectively. Main idea of this gradient penalty term is to enforce the approximate Lipschitz property
\nabla_{\bm{d}}\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}(\cdots)\approx 1
on the predictions of the
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
as a soft version of gradient clipping, see
[
2
]
for further details.

Reconstruction loss

In addition to the adversarial loss, we employ a reconstruction loss on the outputs of the generator.

\ell_{\mathrm{rec}}={\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}}}\bigl[\bigl\|\mathcal{G}\bigl(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}},\mathcal{E}(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}})\bigr)\bigr\|_{2}\bigr],

(8)

where
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
are the surface points of the partial input point cloud. The purpose of this loss is to enforce the predicted signed distances of points on the input surface
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
to be as close as possible to zero.

Note, that
\ell_{\mathrm{GAN}}
prevents the generator to opt for the degenerate solution
\mathcal{G}(\cdot,\cdot)\equiv 0
and instead take a sample from the learned prior distribution, for which the points
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
are as close as possible to the surface of the predicted shape.

Normal loss

We further utilize the outer normals of the partial input point clouds
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}

\ell_{\mathrm{norm}}={\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}}}\bigl[\bigl\|\nabla_{\bm{V}}\mathcal{G}\bigl(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}},\mathcal{E}(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}})\bigr)-\bm{N}_{\mathrm{part}}\bigr\|_{2}\bigr],

(9)

where
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{part}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n)\times 3}
denotes the surface normals corresponding to the point set
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
. As demonstrated in
[
11
]
, the loss in
Equation

9
encourages the generator
\mathcal{G}
to replicate small scale features present in the training shape distribution. In practice, we extract
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{part}}
from the full training mesh before sampling the partial point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
.

3.3
Implementation details

Training protocol

To train our model for the task of shape completion, we consider a training set, defined as a collection of full, watertight triangular meshes
\bigl\{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(N)}\bigr\}
, where
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)}=\bigr(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)},\bm{F}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)}\bigr)
, see the purple shape in
Figure

1
.
In each training iteration, we sample a random half-space that removes parts of the full input shape to generate the partial inputs
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
, see the blue point cloud in
Figure

1
. The relative ratio of retained points after cut-off is chosen randomly from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}(0.5,1)
. In that way, the model learns to complete shapes with varying degrees of partialities. At test time, we consider cut-off ratios sampled from
\mathcal{U}(0.5,0.55)
to focus on the challenging case of
\approx 50\%
retained points. We show additional partiality settings at test time in our ablation study, see
Table

2
.

Our model is trained in a coarse-to-fine manner: We start training the network with
n=m=1024
sampled points for both
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
. After every 300 epochs, we double the number of points in
\bm{U},\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
to
n=m=2048,4096,\dots,32768
points. For refinement, we append another 1000 epochs on the highest resolution
n=m=32768
. This progressive training schedule is motivated by previous works
[
16
,
17
]
which show that it improves the training stability and overall synthesis quality.

Data preparation

As specified in
Section

4
, we consider datasets that focus on both man-made objects and deformable human bodies, respectively.
For data preparation, we follow the protocol described by
[
17
,
24
]
. The input to our method is a training set of triangular meshes
\bigl\{\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(N)}\bigr\}
. We first scale the vertices of the meshes
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)}
to a unit sphere for data normalization. For each training shape, we then uniformly sample a set of
100,000
points
\bm{u}_{j}
in the ambient domain and compute the ground-truth signed distance values
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{full},j}
of each point
\bm{u}_{j}
from the surface boundary of
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)}
. To convert the mesh
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}^{(i)}
into a point cloud, each mesh is rendered from multiple camera angles to obtain depth buffers, which are projected back into the object space, see
[
17
]
for more details.
We then sample the partial inputs
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
, as well as their corresponding normals
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{part}}
, as described in the previous paragraph.
Shapes which had either less than 1% of the samples points inside the shape, or a discontinuous SDF, are discarded from the respective training and test sets.

In summary, for every processed mesh we get two input point clouds. the first is the point cloud
\bm{U}
, signified by the orange box in
fig.

1
, which contains randomly sampled points around the input object.
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
and
\bm{N}_{\mathrm{part}}
, on the other hand, are the surface points and corresponding outer normals sampled from the meshes
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}
.

Parameter specifications

During training, we use RMSProp
[
34
]
as the optimizer for updating the parameters of the various modules of our model. For the discriminator optimization, the optimizer has a learning rate of 1e-5, while for the encoder and the generator, the learning rate was set to 1e-3. We also set the relative weight of the reconstruction loss to
\ell_{\mathrm{rec}}=8e-3
, while the weight of the normal loss component is set to be
\ell_{\mathrm{norm}}=0.01
. The Gradient Penalty defined (
7
) is weighed by a factor of
\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}=10
.

Experiments

4.1
Datasets

We choose two dataset to asssess our method’s ability to complete shape from both rigid, man made object classes and non-rigidly deformable shape categories. Specifically, we consider the following datasets:

ShapeNet

The ShapeNet dataset
[
4
]
is a large collection of man-made 3D models. Its objects are classified into various different categories, such as airplanes, sofas, chairs, cars, etc. Beyond the objects’ geometries, the ShapeNet dataset also provides annotations such as correspondences, keypoints, various orientation vectors, and more. More specifically, we use ShapeNetCore (v1), which is a subset of the ShapeNet dataset, containing approximately 51,300 models that span 55 different categories. For our method, we specifically focus on three different classes from the full dataset, which were lamps, benches, and displays.

SURREAL Dataset

SURREAL
[
36
]
is a large-scale dataset which contains about 6 million 3D models of synthetic humans. In addition to the pose information, they also provide details such as optical flow, depth, normals, segmentation of body parts etc. For our purposes, we use a subset of
1886
shapes for training and
536
different poses as our test set.

4.2
Evaluation

Metric

We evaluate different methods by measuring the Chamfer Distance (CD) between the ground truth models
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{full}}
and the predicted shapes
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{rec}}
. The CD measures the distance between two point clouds, defined as the mean distance of each individual point on the first point cloud to the second point set:

\mathrm{CD}\left(\bm{V}_{1},\bm{V}_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\left|\bm{V}_{1}\right|}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\bm{V}_{1}}\min_{\bm{y}\in\bm{V}_{2}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\left|\bm{V}_{2}\right|}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\bm{V}_{2}}\min_{\bm{x}\in\bm{V}_{1}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

(10)

5
Conclusion

We presented a novel approach to shape completion that combines a neural implicit surface architecture with learnable, adversarial shape priors. In that manner, our network effectively decouples the task of shape completion into (1) reconstructing objects that are meaningful and (2) fitting shapes from the learned distribution to a conditional, partial observation. We demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on both ShapeNet and SURREAL, highlighting our network’s flexibility of completing both rigid, man-made objects and non-rigidly deformable shape categories. Our method yields robust predictions for various degrees of partiality and different sampling densities. Finally, we show a variety of qualitative comparisons that highlight the superior visual quality of the shape prior learned by our adversarial model.

Appendix 0.A
Network architecture details

Encoder

The encoder
\mathcal{E}
converts an input point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
with
n
points into a high-dimensional latent encoding
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}
.
The specific architecture is based on PointNet
[
26
]
. It comprises 4 linear layers followed by leaky ReLU non-linearities and a max-pool operation over the points
n
after the last linear layer. The linear layers change the feature dimension from 3, representing the spatial coordinates, to 64, 128, 256, and finally to 512 dimensions. The max pool operation after the last linear layer reduces the entire feature tensor to a single latent vector
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}
per input shape.

Generator

The generator
\mathcal{G}
is responsible for predicting the SDF values for a set of query points
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{{k\times 3}}
. Apart from the points
\bm{x}_{i}
, the second input to
\mathcal{G}
is a vector
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}
, containing a latent representation of the partial input point cloud
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{part}}
obtained from the encoder
\mathcal{E}
.
\mathcal{G}
comprises 8 linear layers, all operating on 128 dimension features, where each linear layer is followed by layer normalization
[
3
]
and non-linear activation. In each layer, we use leaky ReLU as the non-linearity, since these were shown to increase the generation robustness in the context of generative adversarial networks. It furthermore contains a linear layer that maps the input latent code of the partial shape
\bm{z}
from a 512 (output size of the encoder defined in
3.1
) to a 128 dimensional vector. Similarly, the points
\bm{x}_{i}
are converted from
3
input dimensions to
128
latent dimensions in a linear layer. The two 128-dim latent vectors produced by the initial input layers are added up before being passed to the second layer.

To facilitate gradient flow and increase training stability, we use skip connections
[
14
]
between the input and the 5th layer. Similar to the first layer,
\bm{z}
is first passed through a linear layer before being added to the output of the fourth layer.

Discriminator

We use a standard PointNet++
[
27
]
classification architecture without batch normalization layers, as our discriminator
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{U}}
. The module is composed of what the authors call ’set abstraction layers’, where the points are recursively sampled, grouped and passed through individual PointNet layers. The purpose of this scheme is to learn the features in a hierarchical way, which allows it to extract fine grained details. After a series of abstraction layers and subsequent pooling, the resulting feature tensor is passed through three linear layers, the final one of which outputs a scalar value corresponding to the discriminator’s output classification of true/false.
