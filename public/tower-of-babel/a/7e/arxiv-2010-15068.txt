Quantum control with a multi-dimensional Gaussian quantum invariant
Selwyn Simsek, Florian Mintert

Abstract
The framework of quantum invariants is an elegant generalization of adiabatic quantum control to control fields that do not need to change slowly. Due to the unavailability of invariants for systems with more than one spatial dimension, the benefits of this framework have not yet been exploited in multi-dimensional systems. We construct a multi-dimensional Gaussian quantum invariant that permits the design of time-dependent potentials that let the ground state of an initial potential evolve towards the ground state of a final potential. The scope of this framework is demonstrated with the task of shuttling an ion around a corner which is a paradigmatic control problem in achieving scalability of trapped ion quantum information technology.



1
Introduction

The development of hardware for quantum information processing has reached a stage in which tens of qubits can be accurately controlled
[
Friis et al.(2018)Friis,
Marty, Maier, Hempel,
Holzäpfel, Jurcevic, Plenio,
Huber, Roos, Blatt, and Lanyon
,
Wei et al.(2020)Wei,
Lauer, Srinivasan, Sundaresan, McClure, Toyli, McKay, Gambetta, and Sheldon
]
.
In order to control such a large register of qubits, it is advisable to arrange them into several smaller units that are coherently interconnected.
Such interconnections can be realised in a multitude of different ways
[
Monroe et al.(2014)Monroe,
Raussendorf, Ruthven, Brown,
Maunz, Duan, and Kim
,
Lekitsch et al.(2017)Lekitsch, Weidt, Fowler, Mølmer, Devitt, Wunderlich, and Hensinger
]
.
In the case of trapped ions, one of the most prominent envisioned architectures is based on segmented traps, in which ions can be moved,
i.e.
shuttled, between different segments
[
Kielpinski et al.(2002)Kielpinski, Monroe, and Wineland
,
Lekitsch et al.(2017)Lekitsch, Weidt, Fowler, Mølmer, Devitt, Wunderlich, and Hensinger
]
of a segmented trap.

Since trapped ion quantum logic usually requires the motional states of ions to be
close to their quantum mechanical ground state
[
Mølmer and Sørensen(1999)
]
,
it is desirable that any such shuttling process should transfer ions to their motional ground state with high fidelity.
Performing all operations of a quantum algorithm within the time-scale imposed by the system’s coherence times, requires fast shuttling
[
Kielpinski et al.(2002)Kielpinski, Monroe, and Wineland
]

implying that the transient motional states during shuttling are far away from the motional ground state.
Any shuttling process thus needs to end with a stage of deceleration in which an originally rapidly moving ion is being transferred to its quantum mechanical motional ground state.

In practice, shuttling is realised in terms of time-dependent trapping potentials resulting from voltages applied to trap electrodes
[
Rowe et al.(2002)Rowe,
Ben-Kish, Demarco, Leibfried,
Meyer, Beall, Britton,
Hughes, Itano, Jelenković,
Langer, Rosenband, and Wineland
,
Hensinger et al.(2006)Hensinger, Olmschenk, Stick, Hucul, Yeo, Acton, Deslauriers, Monroe, and Rabchuk
]
.
Describing the quantum dynamics of a particle through such a time-dependent potential landscape is generally a formidable numerical challenge,
but in the present context the problem can be substantially simplified.
The initial ground state is a Gaussian wavepacket characterised by the expectation values of position and momentum and the corresponding covariances of those operators.
For realistic trapping potentials, the potential can be approximated to be harmonic in the spatial domain occupied by such a wavepacket
[
Leibfried et al.(2003)Leibfried, Blatt, Monroe, and Wineland
]
.
Within this approximation, an initial Gaussian wavepacket remains Gaussian under time evolution, and thus the state is characterised in terms of expected position in phase space and covariances,
i.e.
a small number of parameters
[
Heller(1975a)
,
Heller(1975b)
,
Heller(1976)
,
Heller(1977)
,
Heller(1981)
]
.

Identifying time-dependent gate voltages that realise a shuttling protocol that transfers one or several ions from their ground state in the initial potential to the ground state of their final potential, typically requires testing a large number of different potential solutions.
As such, the reduction of numerical effort resulting from the Gaussian approximation is invaluable.

An equally important reduction in effort can be achieved with the framework of quantum invariants
[
Lewis(1968)
]
,
since those allow one to directly construct shuttling protocols that end with the ions in their motional ground state
[
Chen et al.(2011)Chen,
Torrontegui, and Muga
]
.
The framework of quantum invariants and its use for control of wave-packets via
invariant-based inverse engineering is well-established for one-dimensional problems
[
Chen et al.(2011)Chen,
Torrontegui, and Muga
,
Torrontegui et al.(2011)Torrontegui, Ibáñez, Chen, Ruschhaupt, Guéry-Odelin, and Muga
,
Levy et al.(2018)Levy,
Kiely, Muga, Kosloff, and Torrontegui
,
Torrontegui et al.(2013)Torrontegui, Ibáñez, Martínez-Garaot,
Modugno, del Campo, Guéry-Odelin, Ruschhaupt, Chen, and Muga
,
Tobalina et al.(2017)Tobalina, Palmero, Martínez-Garaot, and Muga
]
.

Invariant-based inverse engineering has been also suggested to realise the transport of cold atoms in optical lattices
[
Lu et al.(2020)Lu,
Ruschhaupt, Martínez-Garaot, and Muga
]

or optical tweezers
[
Zhang et al.(2016)Zhang,
Muga, Guéry-Odelin, and Chen
]
as well as atom clouds with a corresponding experimental implementation
[
Ness et al.(2018)Ness,
Shkedrov, Florshaim, and Sagi
]
. Invariant-based inverse engineering has also motivated the study of fast transport of spin-orbit-coupled Bose-Einstein condensates
[
Chen et al.(2018)Chen,
Jiang, Li, Ban, and Sherman
]
, and quantum invariants have been used as a theoretical tool to compute topological phases in planar waveguides
[
Rosu and Romero(1999)
]
and minispace quantum cosmologies
[
Espinoza(2000)
]
. They may also be constructed for light beam propagation in nonlinear inhomogeneous media
[
Goncharenko et al.(1991)Goncharenko, Logvin, Samson, Shapovalov, and Turovets
]
.

Processes in which an ion is being shuttled across a junction in a more complicated trap geometry, however, cannot yet be approached with invariant-based inverse engineering,
because the development of invariants for systems with more than one translational degree of freedom
[
Tobalina et al.(2020)Tobalina, Torrontegui, Lizuain,
Palmero, and Muga
]
is still in its infancy.
Here, we develop a framework of quantum invariants that is suitable for optimal control of Gaussian wave-packets in any number of spatial dimensions via invariant-based inverse engineering.

In Sec.
2
, invariant-based inverse engineering and Gaussian states are summarised. Sec.
3
introduces the invariant that is at the core of this paper and discusses how this invariant can be used to design time-dependent potentials that result in ground-state-to-ground-state shuttling.
This section is followed by explicit examples of such protocols discussed in Sec.
4
,
while the actual derivation of the invariant is in Sec.
5
, and Sec.
6
contains a discussion of how well-established one-dimensional invariants are contained as special case in the present framework.
A reader who is more interested in the underlying framework than in its application to a specific control problem, can skip Sec.
4
or read Secs.
5
and
6
in any order.

2
Invariant-based control

In this section several elements of Gaussian quantum states and quantum invariants upon which the present work builds will be reviewed.

In order to ease notation, from hereon in the momentum variables
p_{i}
will include a scaling factor depending on the mass of each particle, such that the kinetic energy reads

\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}^{2}/(2m)
instead of the usual expression
\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}^{2}/(2m_{i})
that explicitly reflects the possibility of having different ions with different masses.
A corresponding scaling of the position variables
x_{i}
ensures that
\hat{x}_{i}
and
\hat{p}_{i}
, as well as their classical counterparts, are conjugate to each other.

2.1
Gaussian dynamics

The framework uses the Gaussian approximation of wavepackets that simplifies the description in terms of general quantum states to a description in terms of classical trajectories and covariances.
If
\hat{X}
denotes the
2d
-dimensional vector comprised of the phase space operators of a
d
-dimensional system,
i.e.

d
position operators and
d
momentum operators

\hat{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},...,\hat{r}_{n},\hat{p}_{1},\hat{p}_{2},...,\hat{p}_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(1)

then any Gaussian wavepacket is completely characterised in terms of the expectation value of
\hat{X}
, denoted in the following by the vector
X
,
and by the
2d\times 2d\ -
dimensional covariance matrix
\Sigma
with the elements

\Sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{X}_{i}\hat{X}_{j}+\hat{X}_{j}\hat{X}_{i}\rangle-\langle\hat{X}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{X}_{j}\rangle\ ,

(2)

where the symbol
\langle\ \rangle
denotes the expectation value with respect to the Gaussian quantum state.

In a potential that is not always harmonic, an initially Gaussian quantum state will lose its Gaussian character,
but if the wavepacket is sufficiently well localised in real-space, then the potential can be approximated by its second-order Taylor expansion around the center of the wavepacket, and the quantum state remains Gaussian
[
Heller(1975b)
]
.

The center of the wavepacket,
X=\langle\hat{X}\rangle
, follows the classical trajectory,
i.e.
it satisfies the equation of motion

\dot{X}={\cal S}\vec{V}\ ,

(3)

where

\vec{V}=\nabla H_{c}

(4)

is the vector containing the first derivatives of the
classical
Hamiltonian
H_{c}
with respect to the phase space variables, evaluated at the centre of the wavepacket
X
, and the symplectic matrix

{\cal S}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}\mathbbm{O}&\mathbbm{1}\\
-\mathbbm{1}&\mathbbm{O}\\
\end{array}\right]\ ,

(5)

is defined in terms of the
d\times d-
dimensional identity matrix
\mathbbm{1}
and zero-matrix
\mathbbm{O}
.

The covariance matrix satisfies the equation of motion
[
Mintert and Heller(2009)
]

\dot{\Sigma}=\Sigma\Omega{\cal S}-{\cal S}\Omega\Sigma\\

(6)

in terms of the matrix
\Omega
containing the second derivatives

\Omega_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}H_{c}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}

(7)

of the classical Hamiltonian
H_{c}
with respect to the phase space variables evaluated at
X
.

From hereon in it is assumed that the anharmonic component of
H
, which is to say the Taylor expansion of
H
beyond the second order, is negligible, in which case the approximation holds and the system Hamiltonian can be expressed as

H=\frac{1}{2}\hat{X}^{T}\Omega\hat{X}+\vec{V}\hat{X}\ .

(8)

2.2
Quantum invariants

The problem at hand lies in finding
\Omega
and
\vec{V}
such that
X(T)
matches the desired phase space position at the final instance in time
T
of the shuttling protocol, and such that
\Sigma(T)
corresponds to the covariances of the quantum mechanical ground state of
H(T)
.
Quantum invariants allow one to derive straightforwardly expressions for
\Omega
and
\vec{V}
that ensure that the boundary conditions at
t=0
and
t=T
are satisfied
[
Tobalina et al.(2017)Tobalina, Palmero, Martínez-Garaot, and Muga
]
.

A quantum invariant is any operator
{\cal I}(t)
satisfying the equation of motion
[
Lewis(1967)
]

\frac{\partial{\cal I}(t)}{\partial t}=i[{\cal I}(t),H(t)]\ .

(9)

Crucially, the instantaneous eigenstates of an invariant, are solutions of the Schrödinger equation with the time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)

[
Lewis(1968)
]
.
A quantum invariant with non-degenerate spectrum that commutes with the Hamiltonian at the beginning and the end of a time interval of interest,

i.e.

[{\cal I}(0),H(0)]=0
and
[{\cal I}(T),H(T)]=0
, can thus be used to identify shuttling protocols that ensure that the system ends up in an eigenstate of the final Hamiltonian
H(T)
, if it is initialised in an eigenstate of
H(0)

[
Chen et al.(2011)Chen,
Torrontegui, and Muga
]
.
If the system is initialised in the ground state of
H(0)
, then it will be transferred to the ground state of
H(T)
since the only eigenstate of the quadratic operator
H(T)
that is Gaussian is the ground state. This is similar to the idea of adiabatic transitions, but in contrast, the invariant based method is exact and holds also under fast changes in the Hamiltonian,
and the system state remains an eigenstate of the invariant
{\cal I}(t)
rather than an eigenstate of the system Hamiltonian
H(t)
.

2.3
Control with quadratic invariants

In the case of quadratic, time-dependent, Hamiltonians,
quantum invariants can be constructed from which one
can deduce quadratic Hamiltonians
H(t)
that correspond to those invariants.
This, however, is not very useful in practice since,
the resulting Hamiltonians contain in general bilinear terms of phase space operators;
in particular, the part corresponding to the kinetic energy will be of the form
\sum_{ij}h_{ij}(t)\hat{p}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}
, which is not necessarily equal to the desired term
\sum_{i}\hat{p}_{i}^{2}/2m
.

Since, in practice, shuttling must be realised in terms of modulation of only the trapping potential,
it is crucial to ensure that Hamiltonians resulting from the invariant-based framework are of the form

H(t)=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\sum_{ij}M_{ij}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}-\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\hat{x}_{i}\ ,

(10)

with tuneable parameters
\vec{F}
and
M
,
and no Lorentz-type terms of the form
\hat{x}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}+\hat{x}_{j}\hat{p}_{i}
, or terms linear in the momenta
\hat{p}_{i}
.

In one-dimensional systems, there does exist a class of quantum invariants
[
Chen et al.(2011)Chen,
Torrontegui, and Muga
]
that ensures the Hamiltonians are of this form and gives a way to deduce
M
and
\vec{F}
from the invariant. Despite the utility and applicability of these one-dimensional invariants, generalisations to higher dimensional systems have not been found to date.

3
Optimal control with high-dimensional quadratic invariants

As the present work deals with quadratic Hamiltonians, it is most useful to work with an invariant that contains the phase space operators

\displaystyle=\Omega{\cal S}\vec{W}-\Gamma{\cal S}\vec{V},

3.2
Boundary conditions

As stated in Sec.
2.2
, a prerequisite for invariant-based control is that the invariant and Hamiltonian commute at initial and final times,
i.e.

[{\cal I}(0),H(0)]=[{\cal I}(T),H(T)]=0\ .

(22)

Since the initial and final Hamiltonians
H(0)
and

are determined by the choice of problem at hand,
this commutativity must be ensured only by imposing boundary conditions on the invariant
{\cal I}(t)
, and not
H(t)
.
Since the invariant
{\cal I}(t)
is parametrised in terms of
\vec{L}
and

via Eqs. (

) and (

this implies boundary conditions for

and

The choices

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}R(t)&=&M(t)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\ ,&&\dot{R}(t)&=&0\ ,\\
\vec{L}(t)&=&(mM(t))^{-1}\vec{F}(t)\ ,&\mbox{and}&\dot{\vec{L}}(t)&=&0\end{array}

directly result in commutativity of

Imposing the conditions of Eq. (

An additional set of conditions arise from the requirement that

\displaystyle\{\ddot{R},R\}+\{R^{2},M\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2[\dot{R},R]_{A}-2RA^{2}R

\displaystyle\ddot{\vec{L}}+M\vec{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\vec{F}}{m},

In practice, however, some choices of

can result in trajectories

It is thus desirable to relate the variables

and

Since the invariant

is quadratic, this is most elegantly done via the fact that the time-dependent quantum state is the instantaneous ground state of

(given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\{R,\dot{R}\}+{{\cal J}}}{2}&m\left(\dot{R}^{2}+\Re([\dot{R},R]_{A}-RA^{2}R)\right)\end{array}\right]\end{array}

That is,

,
which means that the trajectory can be easily chosen at will.

It is of course desirable to attribute an equally clear physical interpretation to the quantity

as found for

in the sense of the trajectory.
This, however, seems feasible only in the adiabatic limit, in which the time-derivative

4
Shuttling protocol

With the general framework for optimal control based on high-dimensional invariants established,
its practical use can be demonstrated with the problem of shuttling an ion through a two-dimensional potential landscape,
such as the transfer from an initial trapping potential

V_{i}=\frac{1}{2}m\left(\omega_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{2}}}x^{2}+\omega_{r}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{2}}}(y-r)^{2}\right)

(30)

centered around the initial position
[0,r]

to a final potential

V_{f}=\frac{1}{2}m\left(\omega_{r}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{2}}}(x-r)^{2}+\omega_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{2}}}y^{2}\right)

(31)

centered around the final position
[r,0]
.
In addition to the change in position, in particular, also the shape of the potential will change during the shuttling process with the (typically strong) radial confinement (characterized by the frequency
\omega_{r}
) initially in the
y
-direction and finally in the
x
-direction,
and the (typically weak) axial confinement (characterized by the frequency
\omega_{t}
) initially in the
x
-direction and finally in the
y
-direction.

As discussed above in the context of Eq. (
28
), the trajectory of the ion can be chosen at will, as long as it satisfies the required boundary conditions.
We are thus free to chose an arc

\vec{L}(t)=r\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}p(\tau)\right)\\
\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}p(\tau)\right)\end{array}\right]

(32)

with
\tau=t/T
, radius

, and a function

satisfying the boundary conditions

and

.
Motivated by its successful use in one-dimensional problems
[
Torrontegui et al.(2011)Torrontegui, Ibáñez, Chen, Ruschhaupt, Guéry-Odelin, and Muga
,
Palmero et al.(2013)Palmero, Torrontegui, Guéry-Odelin, and Muga
,

\dot{\vec{L}}(0)=\dot{\vec{L}}(T)=\ddot{\vec{L}}(0)=\ddot{\vec{L}}(T)=0

For the quadratic component

\end{array}\right]=:R_{i}\ ,

5
Construction of the invariant

Since the derivation of the invariant introduced in Sec.
3.1
is rather involved,
it is deferred to this Section, with some technical steps discussed in
Appendices
A
and
B
.

The goal is not to find an invariant for a given Hamiltonian; instead, the time-dependent invariant is the starting point, and the quest is for a Hamiltonian such that Eqs. (
12
), (
13
) and (
14
) are satisfied for the given invariant
{\cal I}(t)
.
This can be done in subsequent steps, first determining the quadratic component of the invariant, parametrized in terms of
\Gamma
, and subsequently determining the linear component of the invariant, parametrized in terms of
\vec{W}
in Eq. (
11
).

5.1
A suitable invariant – the quadratic component

As stated in Eq. (
15
),

\Omega
must take the form

\Omega=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}mM&\mathbbm{O}\\
\mathbbm{O}&\frac{1}{m}\mathbbm{1}\end{array}\right]\ ,

(41)

where
M
is a real symmetric matrix.
The lower right sub block
\frac{1}{m}\mathbbm{1}
is associated with the kinetic energy while the term
M
is associated with the potential energy, and is the unknown to be deduced from any proposed invariant.

For a general matrix
\Gamma
, the resultant quadratic part
\Omega
of the system Hamiltonian will not be of the desired form given in Eq. (
15
), but the parametrization

\Gamma=\Re\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}m\dot{P}^{\dagger}\dot{P}&-\dot{P}^{\dagger}P\\
-P^{\dagger}\dot{P}&\frac{1}{m}P^{\dagger}P\end{array}\right]\ ,

(42)

with four sub-blocks defined in terms of a
d\times d
-dimensional,
complex
matrix
P
will result in the desired form.
This can be seen by expressing the equation of motion for
\Gamma
(Eq. (
12
)) in terms of
P
,
resulting in

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}m\Re(\dot{P}^{\dagger}{\cal D}+{\cal D}^{\dagger}\dot{P})&-\Re({\cal D}^{\dagger}P)\\
-\Re(P^{\dagger}{\cal D})&0\end{array}\right]=0\

(43)

where
{\cal D}=\ddot{P}+PM
.
Any choice of
P
satisfying the differential equation

\ddot{P}+PM=0\,

(44)

thus results in a quadratic term
\Gamma
for the invariant
{\cal I}
that is consistent with the initial ansatz in Eq. (
15
).

With the current parametrisation of
\Gamma
in terms of
P
,
one could construct

M
as

M=-P^{-1}\ddot{P}\ .

(45)

Usually, however, this does not result in the identification of a suitable matrix
M
,
because
M
defined in this fashion would not necessarily be symmetric or real.
It is thus necessary to find additional restrictions on
P
that guarantee the symmetry and reality of
M
.

To this end, it is helpful to express
P
in terms of its polar decomposition

P=UR\ ,

(46)

where
U
is unitary and
R
is Hermitian and positive semi-definite.
It is possible to leave the factor
R
as a free parameter and determine
U
through the requirement that
M
be real-symmetric.

In practice, this task can be formulated in terms of the anti-Hermitian operator

A=U^{\dagger}\dot{U}\ .

(47)

Since
A
satisfies the differential equation
\dot{U}=UA
, the unitary factor
U
is uniquely determined in terms of
A(t)
and the initial condition
U(0)=\mathbbm{1}
.

As derived explicitly in Sec.
A
, requiring
M
to be Hermitian requires
A
to be of the form

A=iR^{-2}+\frac{1}{2}[R^{-1},\dot{R}]+\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}{{\cal J}}R^{-1}\ ,

(48)

with

{{\cal J}}=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ [M(\tau),R(\tau)^{2}]\ .

(49)

With this specification of
A
in terms of
\dot{R}
,
R
and
M
,
one obtains the differential equation

\{\ddot{R},R\}+\{R^{2},M\}=2[\dot{R},R]_{A}-2RA^{2}R

(50)

for
R
.

So far, it is only imposed that
M
ought to be Hermitian, and not that it should be real as well.
As shown in Sec.
B
, however,
M
is indeed real-symmetric for all times, if
R
is chosen such that it is real and positive at all times and that

\dot{R}(0)=0\ .

(51)

Even though Eq.(
50
), together with Eqs.(
48
) and (
49
) may be used to determine
M(t)
,
they are unwieldy in practice, since they relate
M(t)
to the integral
{{\cal J}}
that depends on the entire history of
M
.
Since
M
is real-symmetric, however, one can – as discussed in detail in Appendix
A
arriving at Eq. (
78
) – specify
{{\cal J}}
explicitly as

{{\cal J}}=\sum_{ij}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}\langle\Phi_{i}|\dot{R}|\Phi_{j}\rangle\ket{\Phi_{i}}\bra{\Phi_{j}}

(52)

in terms of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
and eigenvectors
\ket{\Phi_{i}}
of
R
.
With this explicit form of
{{\cal J}}
, in terms of
R
and
\dot{R}
, but without dependence on
M
, one can finally solve Eq. (
50
) for
M
, which yields

M=\sum_{ij}\frac{\langle\Phi_{i}|B|\Phi_{j}\rangle}{\lambda_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}\ket{\Phi_{i}}\bra{\Phi_{j}}\ ,

(53)

with

B=2[\dot{R},R]_{A}-2RA^{2}R-\{\ddot{R},R\}\ .

(54)

Any real positive matrix
R
that satisfies the initial condition
\dot{R}(0)=0
(Eq. (
51
)) thus yields the quadratic term
\Gamma
of the invariant via Eq. (
42
) that is consistent with the desired form for
\Omega
specified in Eq. (
15
) with a real-symmetric potential matrix
M
.

Substituting for
P=UR
and
A=U^{\dagger}\dot{U}
in the Ansatz for
\Gamma
(Eq. (
42
)),
results in the form of
\Gamma
that is quoted in Eq. (
3.1
) in Sec.
3.1
.

5.2
A suitable invariant – the linear component

The goal of this section is to show that the ansatz

\vec{W}=-\Gamma\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{L}\\
m\dot{\vec{L}}\end{array}\right]\ ,

(55)

of Eq.(
18
)
results in a linear part
\vec{V}
of the Hamiltonian that is of the form

\vec{V}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-\vec{F}\\
0\end{array}\right]\ ,

(56)

specified in Eq. (
16

\vec{V}
can be expressed in terms of
\vec{W}
as

\vec{V}={{\cal S}}^{-1}\Gamma^{-1}\left(\Omega{{\cal S}}\vec{W}-\dot{\vec{W}}\right)

(57)

via the equation of motion for
\vec{W}
given in Eq. (
13
).
Rather than expressing the right-hand-side in terms of
\vec{W}
, it will turn out helpful to express it

\vec{Z}
defined via the relation
\vec{W}=-\Gamma\vec{Z}

Together with the equation of motion for

given in Eq. (
12

\vec{V}={{\cal S}}^{-1}\dot{\vec{Z}}\ -\Omega\vec{Z}.

(58)

With the explicit form of the quadratic term
\Omega
of the Hamiltonian specified in Eq. (
15
)
this results in the explicit form

\vec{V}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-mM\vec{Z}_{x}-\dot{\vec{Z}}_{p}\\
\dot{\vec{Z}}_{x}-\frac{1}{m}\vec{Z}_{p}\end{array}\right]

(59)

for
\vec{V}
, where the vector
\vec{Z}
is decomposed into spatial and momentum parts

\vec{Z}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\vec{Z}_{x}\\

(60)

Finally, choosing

and the identification of
\vec{L}
with
\vec{Z}_{x}

(Eq. (

The resulting equation of motion

\ddot{\vec{L}}+M\vec{L}=\frac{\vec{F}}{m},

permits the determination of
\vec{F}
in terms of
\vec{L}
and

5.3
A suitable invariant – the scalar component

The scalar part

Strictly speaking, it does not need to be constructed, since the eigenstates of the invariant that are the relevant objects are independent of this phase term.
For the sake of completeness, however, the solution

6
The one-dimensional case

The invariant derived in Sec.
5
does not seem to share many similarities with the invariant that are routinely used in the one-dimensional case
[
Lewis and Leach(1982)
]
.
The regular one-dimensional framework is, however, naturally contained as special case in the present framework, as we will show in the following.

Since all matrices such as
M
and
R
reduce to scalars
M_{s}
and
R_{s}
in the one-dimensional case,
the commutators and generalized commutators vanish in the one-dimensional case.
In particular, the object
{\cal J}
defined in terms of a commutator in Eq. (
49
) vanishes,
so that Eq. (
48
) simplifies to

A_{s}=iR_{s}^{-2}.

(63)

Substituting Eq. (
63
) into Eq. (
50
) and exploiting the fact that all objects commute, gives that

\ddot{R}_{s}R_{s}+M_{s}R_{s}^{2}=R_{s}^{-2}\ .

(64)

The quadratic part
\Gamma
defined in Eq. (
3.1
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{s}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle m\left(\dot{R}_{s}^{2}+R_{s}^{-2}\right)&\displaystyle-R_{s}\dot{R}_{s}\\
\displaystyle-R_{s}\dot{R}_{s}&\frac{1}{m}R_{s}^{2}\end{array}\right]\ ,

in the case of commutativity.
From Eqs. (
18
), (
19
) and (
6
), it is possible to write the invariant
{\cal I}
out explicitly in terms of
\hat{r}
and
\hat{p}
,

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\left(R_{s}(\hat{p}-m\dot{L})-m\dot{R}_{s}(\hat{r}-L)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}m\left(\frac{\hat{r}-L}{R_{s}}\right)^{2},

(68)

which is the Ermakov-Lewis invariant
[
Lewis(1967)
]
with additional terms linear in position and momentum
[
Lewis and Leach(1982)
]
.
Dividing both sides of Eq. (
64
) by
R_{s}
gives

\ddot{R}_{s}+R_{s}M_{s}=R_{s}^{-3},

(69)

which is the Ermakov equation that appears in the original treatment
[
Lewis(1967)
]
,
where the symbol
\rho
is used instead of
R_{s}
,
and the symbols
\omega^{2}
and
\alpha
are used instead of
M_{s}
and
\vec{L}
.

7
Outlook

The extension of the framework of quantum invariants to systems with quadratic potentials of more than one translational degree of freedom
opens up a wide perspective for the control of quantum systems.
While the present work is motivated by control of trapped ions, none of the features of the present framework are specific to this system,
and the only precondition for the use of the presented invariant is the validity of the Gaussian approximation.
Since also shuttling of cold neutral atoms has been experimentally realised in one-dimensional optical lattice potentials
[
Lu et al.(2020)Lu,
Ruschhaupt, Martínez-Garaot, and Muga
]
with time-dependent potential designed with the Ermakov-Lewis invariant, the presently derived framework shall open up possibilities to extend such tasks to higher-dimensional optical lattices.

The ability to systematically devise time-dependent potentials that realise ground-state to ground-state transfer permits to identify those potentials that are optimized with respect to additional desirable properties.
Typical examples might include shuttling protocols with particularly weak forcing or potentials that suit best the geometry of the electrodes that generate the potentials.
As such, the present framework promises to substantially advance the shuttling of trapped ions, which is a central step towards achieving scalability of quantum information technology with trapped ions.

The specific choice of invariant discussed here, does not necessarily need to be seen as the unique generalization of one-dimensional invariants,
but rather as evidence that such generalizations are possible.
It thus seems conceivable that different generalization can be found, and that different restrictions of the achievable Hamiltonians can be found.
One may, for example, envision the Lorentz force caused by a homogeneous magnetic field and aim at finding an invariant that ensures that no inhomogeneities in the magnetic field arise.

In addition to the extension of experimental tasks such as noise-resilient shuttling
[
Levy et al.(2018)Levy,
Kiely, Muga, Kosloff, and Torrontegui
,
Lu et al.(2014)Lu,
Muga, Chen, Poschinger,
Schmidt-Kaler, and Ruschhaupt
,
Lu et al.(2018)Lu,
Ruschhaupt, and Muga
]
to higher dimensional settings,
the present work can also initiate new steps in the conceptual developments of quantum invariants.

Appendix A
Derivation of the invariant equations

This section contains the explicit derivation of Eqs. (
48
),(
49
) and (
50
).

The equation of motion
\ddot{P}+PM=0
(Eq. (
44
))
with
P
expressed in terms of its polar decomposition
P=UR
(Eq. (
46
)) results in

K:=\dot{A}R+A^{2}R+2A\dot{R}+\ddot{R}+RM=0\ .

(70)

Since
M
is Hermitian, and the relation
RK=K^{\dagger}R
holds because
K
vanishes,
this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}RAR=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left([\ddot{R},R]+[M,R^{2}]\right)\ .

(71)

The right hand side of Eq. (
71
) can be rewritten in terms of a total derivative to obtain

\frac{d}{dt}RAR=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left([\dot{R},R]+{{\cal J}}\right),

(72)

with

{{\cal J}}=\int^{t}_{0}d\tau\ [M(\tau),R(\tau)^{2}]\ .

(73)

This can be directly integrated, resulting in the explicit solution

A=R^{-1}CR^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}[R^{-1},\dot{R}]+\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}{{\cal J}}R^{-1},

(74)

for
A
.

The matrix
C
is the constant of integration that can be chosen freely.
With the choice
C=i\mathbbm{1}
motivated by the Ermakov equation in the one-dimensional case
[
Lewis(1967)
,
Leach and Andriopoulos(2008)
]
,
this results in Eq. (
48
).

Using again the fact that
K
vanishes, we can form the relation
RK+K^{\dagger}R=0
and rearrange to derive Eq. (
50
).

Appendix B
Reality of
M

This section contains the proof that
M(t)
determined by Eq.(
50
) is indeed real and symmetric for all times, provided that the initial condition
\dot{R}(0)=0
is satisfied.

Eq. (
50
) reads explicitly

\{\ddot{R},R\}+\{R^{2},M\}=2[\dot{R},R]_{A}-2RA^{2}R\ ,

(75)

and there is indeed no evident reason why
M
determined from this relation together with Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
) should be real and symmetric.

In the following, explicit, closed-form expressions for
{{\cal J}}
,
A
and
M
are derived, that hold under the assumption that
M
is real and symmetric.
It is then demonstrated that such closed-form expressions form indeed a solution of Eqs. (
48
), (
49
) and (
50
), which not only completes the proof that
M
is real symmetric, but gives a direct way to calculate
M
.

B.1
Necessary conditions for the reality of
M

Under the assumption that
M
is real and symmetric,
the imaginary part of the equation of motion for
R
(Eq. (
50
)) reads

\{{{\cal J}},R^{-2}\}=[\dot{R},R^{-1}]+[R,R^{-2}]_{\dot{R}}\ .

(76)

This determines
{{\cal J}}
in terms of
R
and
\dot{R}
, and, in contrast to the defining relation (Eq. (
49
)) for
{{\cal J}}
it is time-local.

In order to solve Eq. (
76
) for
{{\cal J}}
, it is helpful to consider the eigen-decomposition

R=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}|\Phi_{j}\rangle\langle\Phi_{j}|

(77)

of the real and positive semi-definite matrix
R
.

In terms of matrix elements with respect to the eigenstates
\ket{\Phi_{j}}
of
R
,
Eq. (
76
) can directly be solved, yielding

{{\cal J}}_{jk}=\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{k})^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\dot{R}}_{jk}\ ,

(78)

where

{O}_{jk}=\langle\Phi_{j}|O|\Phi_{k}\rangle

(79)

is a short hand notation for the matrix elements of operator
O
,
and

\dot{O}_{jk}=\langle\Phi_{j}|\dot{O}|\Phi_{k}\rangle

(80)

does generally
not
coincide with
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}O_{jk}
because of the time-dependence of the eigenstates
\ket{\Phi_{j}}
.

Similarly to Eq.(
78
), also Eq. (
50
) can be solved for
M_{jk}
, resulting in

{M}_{jk}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\left(2\sum_{l}\left(\lambda_{k}{\dot{R}}_{jl}A_{lk}-\lambda_{j}A_{jl}\dot{R}_{lk}-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}A_{jl}A_{lk}\right)-{\ddot{R}}_{jk}\left(\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{k}\right)\right)\ .

(81)

Eq. (
48
) expressed similarly in terms of matrix elements reads

{A}_{jk}=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}}\left(2i\delta_{jk}-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}){\dot{R}}_{jk}+{{\cal J}}_{jk}\right)\ .

(82)

Substituting Eq. (
78
) into Eq. (
82
) yields

A_{jk}=\frac{i}{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}}\delta_{jk}+\frac{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\dot{R}_{jk}.

(83)

Substituting Eq. (
83
) into Eq. (
81
) yields

{M}_{jk}=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{\lambda_{j}^{4}}-\frac{(\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{k})}{\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}}{\ddot{R}}_{jk}+2\sum_{l}{\dot{R}}_{jl}{\dot{R}}_{lk}p(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k},\lambda_{l})\ ,

(84)

with

p(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k},\lambda_{l})=\frac{\lambda_{l}^{3}\left(\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{k}\right)-\lambda_{l}^{2}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}\right)-\lambda_{j}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}}{(\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{k}^{2})(\lambda_{j}^{2}+\lambda_{l}^{2})(\lambda_{k}^{2}+\lambda_{l}^{2})}.

(85)

The closed-form expressions for

{{\cal J}}
,
A
and ultimately
M
, in terms of their matrix elements, given in Eqs, (
78
), (
83
) and (
84
), hold if
M
is real symmetric.

Conversely, if Eqs. (
78
), (
83
) and (
84
) hold, then
M
is guaranteed to be real symmetric since Eq. (
84
) is manifestly symmetric and defined solely using real quantities.
As a consequence,
M
is real symmetric if and only if these expressions hold.

B.2
Direct proof of the reality of
