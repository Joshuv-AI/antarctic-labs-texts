Abstract
. In this note, three 2003 problems of Nathanson and two 2007 problems of Chen on unique representation bases for the integers are resolved.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11B13, 11B34, 11B05

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{N}
be the set of natural numbers and
A
a subset of
\mathbb{N}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

r_{A}(n)=\#\big\{(a,a^{\prime}):n=a+a^{\prime},a\leq a^{\prime},a,a^{\prime}\in A\big\}.

One of the most attractive unsloved problems in additive combinatorics is the Erdős-Turán conjecture on additive bases
[
2
]
posed in 1941, which states that if
r_{A}(n)\geq 1
for all sufficiently large
n
, then
r_{A}(n)
cannot be unbounded.

However, things get changed if one replaces the natural set
\mathbb{N}
by the integer set
\mathbb{Z}
. A set
A
is called a
unique representation basis
of
\mathbb{Z}
if
r_{A}(n)=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In a fairly original article, Nathanson
[
4
]
showed that there is a unique representation basis of
\mathbb{Z}
. Nathanson further proved that a unique representation basis can be arbitrarily sparse. In addition, Nathanson proved that every unique representation basis
A
for the integers satisfies
A(-x,x)\leq\sqrt{8x}
, where
A(y,x)=\big|A\cap[y,x]\big|
. Nathanson himself constructed a unique representation basis
A
such that

A(-x,x)\geq(2/\log 5)\log x+0.63.

Based on the results above, Nathanson asked whether there exists a number
\theta<1/2
so that
A(-x,x)\leq x^{\theta}
for every unique representation basis
A
and for all sufficiently large
x
?
Nathanson
[
4
]
also posed the following three open problems:

Problem 1
.

For each number
c>2/\log 5
, does there exist a unique representation basis
A
such that
A(-x,x)>c\log x
for all sufficiently large
x
?

Problem 2
.

Does there exist a unique representation basis
A
such that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(-x,x)}{\log x}=\infty?

Problem 3
.

Does there exist a number
\theta>0
and a unique representation
A
such that
A(-x,x)\geq x^{\theta}
for all sufficiently large
x
?

As was already shown by Chen
[
1
]
, the classical results on finite Sidon sets (see e.g.
[
5
]
) can be used to construct unique representation basis
A
with large growth of the number of its elements infinitely often. Precisely, Chen proved that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a unique representation basis
A
for the integers such that
A(-x,x)\geq x^{1/2-\varepsilon}
for infinitely many positive integers
x
, which gave a negative answer to the problem of Nathanson mentioned above.
Chen
[
1
]
then posed some open problems. Two of them are as follows:

Problem 4
.

Does there exist a real number
c>0
and a unique representation basis
A
such that

A(-x,x)\geq c\sqrt{x}
for infinitely many positive integers
x
?

Problem 5
.

Does there exist a real number
c>0
and a unique representation basis
A
such that

A(-x,x)\geq c\sqrt{x}
for all real numbers
x\geq 1
?

In this article, we shall solve the Problems
1
to
5
above.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a unique representation
A
such that
A(-x,x)\geq\frac{1}{8}x^{1/3}
for all sufficiently large
x
.

Theorem
1
answers affirmatively Problems
1
,
2
and
3
from Nathanson.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitraily small number. Then there is a unique representation basis
A
such that

A(-x,x)\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\sqrt{x}
for infinitely many
x
.

Theorem
2
answers affirmatively Problem
4
of Chen.

Theorem 3
.

Let
A
be a unique representation basis. Then

\liminf_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(-x,x)}{\sqrt{x/\log x}}\leq 4\sqrt{7}.

Theorem
3
answers negatively Problem
5
of Chen.

At present, I cannot solve the following problem of Chen
[
1
]
.

Does there exist a real number
\theta<1/2
such that for any unique representation basis
A
there are infinitely many positive integers
x
with
A(-x,x)\leq x^{\theta}
?

Now, let
\mathscr{A}
be set of all unique representation bases
A
such that there exists some real number
c>0
so that
A(-x,x)>cx^{1/2}
for infinitely many
x
. For any
A\in\mathscr{A}
, let

c_{A}:=\limsup_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{A(-x,x)}{\sqrt{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\mathscr{A}}:=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}c_{A}.

It is clear from Theorem
2
that
\mathscr{A}
is nonempty
and

c_{\mathscr{A}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.

As we mentioned above, Nathanson’s result implies that

c_{\mathscr{A}}\leq 2\sqrt{2}.

Thus, it would be of some interests to ask ‘
what is the exact value of
c_{\mathscr{A}}
’? It seems safe to guess
c_{\mathscr{A}}\geq 1
.

2.
Proofs

The idea leading to the solutions of Nathanson’s problems benefits from an application of the greedy algorithm together with the inductive process of Chen
[
1
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Firstly, we will construct by inductive process a series of sets

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset A_{i}\subset\cdots

satisfying the following five requirements

I.
r_{A_{h}}(n)\leq 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

II.
r_{A_{h}}(n)=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
|n|\leq h-1
,

III.
\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|<\big|a^{*}_{h+1}\big|\leq 64\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|
,

IV.
A_{h}\big(-\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|,\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)^{1/3},

V.
0\not\in A_{h}

for any positive integer
h
, where
a^{*}_{h}
is the element of
A_{h}
with maximum absolute value.

Let
A_{1}=\{-1,1\}
and
x_{1}=1
. Clearly,
A_{1}
satisfies I, II, IV, V. Assume that we have already constructed the sets
A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset A_{h}
satisfying conditions I to V. Let
m
be the integer with minimum absolute value so that
r_{A_{h}}(m)=0
. Then by inductive hypothesis II and the definition of
m
we have

\displaystyle h\leq|m|\leq 2\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|+1.

(1)

Take

\displaystyle b=4\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|+|m|

(2)

and

B=A_{h}\cup\big\{-b,b+m\big\}.

Note that
m=(b+m)+(-b)\in B+B
and the following four sets

2A_{h},\quad A_{h}-b,\quad A_{h}+b+m,\quad\big\{m,-2b,2b+2m\big\}

are disjoint, where

A_{h}-b:=\big\{a-b:a\in A_{h}\big\}

and

A_{h}+b+m:=\big\{a+b+m:a\in A_{h}\big\}.

So, we have
r_{B}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r_{B}(m)=1
and
0\not\in B
. If
r_{B}(-m)=0
, then take

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}=4b+5|m|

(3)

and

\widetilde{B}=B\cup\big\{-\widetilde{b},\widetilde{b}-m\big\}.

It can be seen that
r_{\widetilde{B}}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
r_{\widetilde{B}}(-m)=1
and
0\not\in\widetilde{B}
via similar discussions above.
Let

\displaystyle D_{h+1}=\begin{cases}B,&\text{if~}r_{B}(-m)=1;\\
\widetilde{B},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then we know that
\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|<\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\leq 64\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|
from (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
), where
d^{*}_{h}
is the element of
D_{h+1}
with maximum absolute value.
Now, we will add a few new elements into
D_{h+1}
to form
A_{h+1}
, making it satisfied with the dense condition, i.e., inductive hypothesis IV. This can be done via a greedy algorithm which will be put in the next paragraph.

If

\displaystyle\big|D_{h+1}\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\big)^{1/3},

(4)

then we stop here and let
A_{h+1}=D_{h+1}
. Clearly, we have

\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|<\big|a^{*}_{h+1}\big|=\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\leq 64\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|

and hence

A_{h+1}\big(-\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|,\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)=\big|D_{h+1}\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)^{1/3}.

Therefore, the inductive hypotheses I to V are satisfied with this
A_{h+1}
.
Now, suppose the contrary of (
4
), we consider the following set

\displaystyle W_{h+1}:=\left\{d_{1}+d_{2}-d_{3},~\frac{d_{4}+d_{5}}{2}:d_{j}\in D_{h+1},~1\leq j\leq 5\right\}.

We add integers
|n|\leq\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|/2
step by step. First, we choose a nonzero integer
n_{1}
with minimum absolute value which does not belong to
W_{h+1}
and add it into
D_{h+1}
to form a new set
D^{1}_{h+1}
. We claim that
D^{1}_{h+1}
satisfies
r_{D^{1}_{h+1}}(n)\leq 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Actually, if
r_{D^{1}_{h+1}}(n)\geq 2
for some
n
, then we must have

n_{1}+d_{3}=d_{1}+d_{2}\quad\text{or}\quad 2n_{1}=d_{4}+d_{5}

for some
d_{j}\in D_{h+1}~(1\leq j\leq 5)
which contradicts the selection of
n_{1}
.
Next, we
consider the following set

\displaystyle W^{1}_{h+1}:=\left\{d_{1}+d_{2}-d_{3},~\frac{d_{4}+d_{5}}{2}:d_{j}\in D^{1}_{h+1},~1\leq j\leq 5\right\}.

Again, we choose a nonzero integer
n_{2}
with minimum absolute value which does not belong to
W^{1}_{h+1}
and add it into
D^{1}_{h+1}
to form a new set
D^{2}_{h+1}
. We continue this process and stop it until the
g
-th step when we have

\big|D^{g}_{h+1}\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\big)^{1/3}.

This can be done because otherwise

\big|W^{g}_{h+1}\big|\leq\big|D^{g}_{h+1}\big|^{3}+\big|D^{g}_{h+1}\big|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{8}\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|+\frac{1}{4}\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|^{2/3}<\frac{1}{2}\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|,

which can be continued again by the process above. Suppose now that the process stops at the
g
-th step, then we take
A_{h+1}=D^{g}_{h+1}
. We still have

\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|<\big|a^{*}_{h+1}\big|=\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\leq 64\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|.

Moreover, we have

\big|A_{h+1}\big|=\big|D^{g}_{h+1}\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|d^{*}_{h+1}\big|\big)^{1/3}.

It is clear from the constructions above that the new set
A_{h+1}
satisfies the inductive hypotheses I to V.

Let

\displaystyle A=\bigcup_{h=1}^{\infty}A_{h}

By the inductive hypothesis I and II, we have
r_{A_{h}}(n)=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which means that
A
is a unique representation basis for the integers. Let
x
be a sufficiently large number. Then there exists some
h
so that

\displaystyle\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\leq x<\big|a^{*}_{h+1}\big|<64\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|,

from which it follows that

\displaystyle A(-x,x)\geq A_{h}(-x,x)\geq A_{h}\big(-\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|,\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\big(\big|a^{*}_{h}\big|\big)^{1/3}\geq\frac{1}{8}x^{1/3},

where the last but one inequality comes from inductive hypothesis IV.
∎

Before presenting the proof of Theorem
2
, we introduce an important object from additive combinatorics.

A set

S=\{s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdot\cdot\cdot<s_{t}\}\subset\{1,2,\cdots,n\}

is called a
Sidon set
if all the sums

s_{i}+s_{j}~(i\leq j)

are different. Let
F_{2}(n)
be the largest cardinality of a Sidon set in
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.
The second lemma is a standard result involving Sidon sets
[
5
, Theorem 7, page 88]
.

Lemma 1
.

We have

F_{2}(n)=n^{1/2}+O\big(n^{1/3}\big).

The error term in Lemma
1
is not the best known one at present (see e.g.
[
3
]
), but the relation
F_{2}(n)\sim n^{1/2}
is actually applicable enough for our purpose.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We will firstly construct by inductive process a series of sets

A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset A_{i}\subset\cdots

and a series of positive integers

x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{i}<\cdots

satisfying the following four constraints

I.
r_{A_{h}}(n)\leq 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

II.
r_{A_{2h}}(n)=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
with
|n|\leq h
,

III.
A_{2h-1}(-x_{h},x_{h})\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\sqrt{x_{h}},

IV.
0\not\in A_{h}

for any positive integer
h
.

Let
A_{1}=\{-1,1\}
and
x_{1}=1
. Assume that we have already constructed the sets
A_{1}\subset A_{2}\subset\cdots\subset A_{2h-1}
and
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{h}
satisfying conditions I to IV. Let
m
be the integer with minimum absolute value so that
r_{A_{2h-1}}(m)=0
. We claim that
|m|\geq h
. In fact, if
h=1
, then
|m|=1=h
. For
h>1
, we have
r_{A_{2h-2}}(m)=0
since
A_{2h-2}\subset A_{2h-1}
. Then by inductive hypothesis II we have
|m|\geq h
. Similarly to the constructions of

B
and
\widetilde{B}
in the proof of Theorem
1
,
we let

B=A_{2h-1}\cup\big\{-b,b+m\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{B}=B\cup\big\{-\widetilde{b},\widetilde{b}-m\big\},

where
b=4\big|a^{*}_{2h-1}\big|+|m|
and
\widetilde{b}=4b+5|m|
. Next, we let

\displaystyle A_{2h}=\begin{cases}B,&\text{if~}r_{B}(-m)=1;\\
\widetilde{B},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, the set
A_{2h}
satisfies inductive hypotheses I, II and IV. It remains to construct a set
A_{2h+1}
and an integer
x_{h+1}>x_{h}
so that
A_{2h}\subset A_{2h+1}
and the hypotheses I, III, IV hold. Improving the former one given by Chen
[
1
, Lemma 2]
, we shall make use of the full strength of Lemma
1
and we will put it in the next paragraph.

Denote by
a^{*}_{2h}
the element of
A_{2h}
with maximum absolute value. Now, let
y
be a (positive) parameter to be decided later. By Lemma
1
there is a set
S\subset\big[0,\big(1/2-\varepsilon/2\big)y\big)
with

\displaystyle\big|S\big|\geq\sqrt{\left(1/2-\varepsilon/2\right)y}+O\big(y^{1/3}\big)>\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\sqrt{y}+O\big(y^{1/3}\big)

(5)

such that
r_{S}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\varepsilon
is a sufficiently small number. Let

\widetilde{S}=S+y/2:=\big\{s+y/2:s\in S\big\}.

Then
\widetilde{S}\subset\left[y/2,\big(1-\varepsilon/2\big)y\right)
satisfies
r_{\widetilde{S}}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{S}\big|=\big|S\big|\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\sqrt{y}+O\big(y^{1/3}\big)

(6)

from (
5
). Noting that

A_{2h}-A_{2h}\subset\big[-2\big|a^{*}_{2h}\big|,2\big|a^{*}_{2h}\big|\big],

we know that the number of pairs
s_{1}\neq s_{2}\in\widetilde{S}
so that

s_{1}-s_{2}\in A_{2h}-A_{2h}

is not exceeding
4\big|a^{*}_{2h}\big|
since
r_{\widetilde{S}}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now, we remove all such pairs
s_{1},s_{2}
from
\widetilde{S}
to form a new subset
S^{*}\subset\big[y/2,\left(1-\varepsilon/2\right)y\big)
. Clearly, from (
6
) we have

\displaystyle\big|S^{*}\big|\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\sqrt{y}+O\big(y^{1/3}\big)-8a^{*}_{2h}\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\sqrt{y},

(7)

provided that
y
is sufficiently large (in terms of
\varepsilon
and
a^{*}_{2h}
). From now on, suppose that
y>x_{h}
is a sufficiently large given number (in terms of
\varepsilon
and
a^{*}_{2h}
),
then both of the following two equations

a_{1}+a_{2}=s_{1}+s_{2},\quad(a_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2h},~s_{1},s_{2}\in S^{*})

and

a_{1}+a_{2}=a_{3}+s_{1},\quad(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in A_{2h},~s_{1}\in S^{*})

have no solutions. Note further that we have

s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}\geq y/2+y/2-\big(1-\varepsilon/2\big)y=\varepsilon y/2>a

for any
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in S^{*}
and any
a\in A_{2h}
, which clearly means that

s_{1}+s_{2}=s_{3}+a,\quad(a\in A_{2h},~s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\in S^{*})

has no solutions. Now, let

A_{2h+1}=A_{2h}\cup S^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{h+1}=y.

Then we have
r_{A_{2h+1}}(n)\leq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{h+1}>x_{h}
from the discussions above. Moreover, from (
7
) we have

\frac{A_{2h+1}\big(-x_{h+1},x_{h+1}\big)}{\sqrt{x_{h+1}}}>\frac{\big|S^{*}\big|}{\sqrt{y}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\varepsilon.

Thus, so far we have finished the constructions of
\{A_{h}\}
and
\{x_{h}\}
.

Let

\displaystyle A=\bigcup_{h=1}^{\infty}A_{h}

By the inductive hypotheses I and II, we have
r_{A_{h}}(n)=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence,
A
is a unique representation basis for the integers. By inductive hypothesis III, we have

A(-x_{h},x_{h})\geq A_{h}(-x_{h},x_{h})\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\sqrt{x_{h}},

which completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
3
is motivated by the idea of Erdős
[
6
]
, where he communicated to Stöhr the argument for an old claim of Erdős and Turán
[
2
]
on infinite Sidon sets.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
n
be a sufficiently large integer. For any positive integer
\ell
, let
N_{\ell}
(resp.
M_{\ell}
) be the number of elements of
A
in the interval

\big((\ell-1)n,\ell n\big]\quad\left(\text{resp.}~\big[-\ell n,(-\ell+1)n\big)\right).

Suppose that
N_{\ell}\geq 2
and
a,a^{\prime}\in A\cap\big((\ell-1)n,\ell n\big]
with
a<a^{\prime}
, then

0<a^{\prime}-a<n.

Since
A
is a unique basis, the differences
a^{\prime}-a
are all different. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n,~N_{\ell}\geq 2\end{subarray}}\binom{N_{\ell}}{2}<n,

which means that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n,~N_{\ell}\geq 2\end{subarray}}N_{\ell}^{2}\leq 4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n,~N_{\ell}\geq 2\end{subarray}}\binom{N_{\ell}}{2}<4n,

(8)

Moreover, we clearly have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n,~N_{\ell}=1\end{subarray}}N_{\ell}^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n,~N_{\ell}=1\end{subarray}}1\leq n.

(9)

Hence, from (
8
) and (
9
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}N_{\ell}^{2}<5n.

(10)

Similarly to the proof of (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}M_{\ell}^{2}<5n.

(11)

Now, we make a further observation which would be the main novelty of the proof. For

a\in A\cap\big((\ell-1)n,\ell n\big]\quad\text{and}\quad b\in A\cap\big[-\ell n,(-\ell+1)n\big),

we have

-n<a+b<n.

Since
A
is a unique basis, the sums
a+b
are all different. So we obtain

\displaystyle N_{\ell}M_{\ell}<2n.

(12)

Collecting together (
10
), (
11
) and (
12
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}\left(N_{\ell}+M_{\ell}\right)^{2}<14n.

(13)

It is clear that

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{N_{\ell}+M_{\ell}}{\ell^{1/2}}\bigg)^{2}\leq\bigg(\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{1}{\ell}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}(N_{\ell}+M_{\ell})^{2}\bigg)<(1+\log n)14n

(14)

from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. By partial summations, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{N_{\ell}+M_{\ell}}{\ell^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\Big(A\big(-\ell n,\ell n\big)-A\big(-(\ell-1)n,(\ell-1)n\big)\Big)\frac{1}{\ell^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}A\big(-\ell n,\ell n\big)\left(\frac{1}{\ell^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{(\ell+1)^{1/2}}\right)+\frac{A\big(-n^{2},n^{2}\big)}{(n+1)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{A\big(-\ell n,\ell n\big)}{\sqrt{\ell n/\log(\ell n)}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\sqrt{\frac{\ell n}{\log(\ell n)}}\left(\frac{1}{\ell^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{(\ell+1)^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle>\min_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{A\big(-\ell n,\ell n\big)}{\sqrt{\ell n/\log(\ell n)}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\log n}}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{1}{2(\ell+1)},

(15)

where we used the estimates

\sqrt{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\ell^{1/2}}-\frac{1}{(\ell+1)^{1/2}}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\ell+1}-\sqrt{\ell}}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}(\sqrt{\ell+1}+\sqrt{\ell})}>\frac{1}{2(\ell+1)}

in the last inequality. Inserting (
15
) into (
14
), we clearly have

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{A\big(-\ell n,\ell n\big)}{\sqrt{\ell n/\log(\ell n)}}<\frac{4\sqrt{7}\sqrt{(1+\log n)\log n}}{\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n}\frac{1}{\ell+1}}<4\sqrt{7}+\delta

(16)

for any
\delta>0
providing that
n>n_{\delta}
, from which our theorem clearly follows.
∎
