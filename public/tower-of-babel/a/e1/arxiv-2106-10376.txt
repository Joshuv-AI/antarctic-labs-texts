The scaling limit of fair Peano paths
Nathan Albin, Joan Lind, Pietro Poggi-Corradini

Abstract
. We study random Peano paths on planar square grids that arise from fair random spanning trees. These are trees that are sampled in such a way as to have the same (if possible) edge probabilities. In particular, we are interested in identifying the scaling limit as the mesh-size of the grid tends to zero. It is known [ 4 ] that if the trees are sampled uniformly, then the scaling limit exists and equals SLE 8 {\rm SLE}_{8} . We show that if we simply follow the same steps as in [ 4 ] , then fair Peano paths have a deterministic scaling limit.



1.
Introduction

It was shown in
[
4
]
that the scaling limit of random Peano paths arising from uniform spanning trees of planar grids exists and equals
{\rm SLE}_{8}
, which is a random Schramm-Loewner evolution process. In this paper, we study random Peano paths which are generated by random spanning trees that are not necessarily uniform. In particular, we are interested in studying the limiting behavior of laws on spanning trees that arise in the context of spanning tree modulus. The latter trees are called fair trees, because rather than having the same probability of being sampled, they are sampled in such a way as to yield the same (if possible) edge probabilities. In particular, we will be drawing on the two papers
[
1
,
2
]
that initiated the study of fair trees.

In order to state our main result, we must first set the stage.
Let
Q=(0,1]\times[0,1)
be the unit square (including the bottom and right sides).
For the lattice
\mathcal{L}_{n}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}\times\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z}\right]\cap Q
,
consider the spanning trees that contain the edges along the bottom and right sides of
Q
.
Let
\Gamma_{n}
be the set of fair trees, which are identified in Lemma
2.2
(after contracting the bottom and right sides).
For each element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
,
there is a unique spanning tree
\hat{\gamma}
of the dual grid (i.e.
\left[(\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z})\times(\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{Z})\right]\cap Q
) so that
\gamma\cap\hat{\gamma}=\emptyset
.
Further we can define a path
\zeta_{\gamma}
in
Q
from 0 to
1+i
that winds between
\gamma
and
\hat{\gamma}
, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Except for near 0 and
1+i
, we take
\zeta_{\gamma}
to be on the lattice
(\frac{1}{4n}+\frac{1}{2n}\mathbb{Z})\times(\frac{1}{4n}+\frac{1}{2n}\mathbb{Z})
.
We call

the
Peano path
associated with
\gamma
,
and we define
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{\zeta_{\gamma}\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}\}

to be the collection of fair Peano paths.

(in black) and
\hat{\gamma}
(in gray) and the corresponding Peano path

(in red) for

.

We consider an element
\zeta
of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
to be a family of the growing traces
\zeta[0,t]
for
t\in[0,1]
modulo reparametrization. For
\zeta,\xi\in\cup\mathcal{A}_{n}
, define the distance

(1.1)

d(\zeta,\xi)=\inf\sup_{t\in[0,1]}d_{H}\left(\zeta[0,t],\xi[0,t]\right),

where the infimum is over all possible parametrizations of
\zeta,\xi,
and

is the Hausdorff distance.
Let

be the closure of

with respect to the distance

.
Also, let
\mathcal{P}(X)
be the set of probability measures on
(X,\mathcal{B}(X))

where
\mathcal{B}(X)
is the Borel

on

.

Note that elements of

are growing families of subsets of
\overline{Q}
, modulo reparametrization (i.e. elements have the form
\kappa=\{\kappa_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
with
\kappa_{s}\subseteq\kappa_{t}
for
s\leq t
).
Further,
X
contains elements that are no longer traces of curves.
For instance,
define the growing family of triangles

(1.2)

\lambda_{t}:=\{x+iy\in\overline{Q}\,:\,x+y\leq 2t\}.

We will see in Section

that
\lambda=\{\lambda_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is an element of

. Moreover, the following is our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be the probability measure that is uniform on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
Then
\mu_{n}
converges weakly to the Dirac measure
\delta_{\lambda}
as

, where
\lambda\in X
is defined by (
1.2
).

Note in particular that we obtain a deterministic scaling limit.
There are other possible choices for fair probability measures. However, we choose to study
\mu_{n}
since it has the largest support and the largest entropy out of all these possibilities.
In this setting, one can relate the fair trees to a system of coalescing random walks under isotropic scaling.
(This is in contrast to the diffusive scaling that would lead to the Brownian web, which originated with Arratia
[
3
]
. See the survey paper
[
7
]
for more on the Brownian web and related objects.)

In Section
2
, we summarize the background regarding fair trees that is relevant for our purposes.
In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
in two steps: we first show the existence of subsequential limits of

, using Prohorov’s Theorem, and then we show that the only possible subsequential limit is

Finally in Section

we identify the fair trees in the case of both the modified and standard grids, and we give evidence supporting an alternate approach.

2.
Background on fair trees and modified grids

2.1.
Random spanning trees

Consider a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, with vertex-set
V
and edge-set
E
that are both finite. We allow multi-edges, but not self-loops. Let
\Gamma_{G}
be the set of all spanning trees of
G
. The set of all probability mass functions (pmf’s)
\mu
on
\Gamma_{G}
is denoted with
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{G})
. A pmf
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{G})
determines random trees
\underline{\gamma}
that satisfy the law:

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(\underline{\gamma}=\gamma\right)=\mu(\gamma)\hskip 23.49976pt\forall\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}.

The
\sigma
-weighted uniform spanning trees
, write
\sigma
-WUST for short, are random spanning trees obtained by considering some fixed edge-weights
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E}
.
These are random trees
\underline{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{G}
whose probability
\mu_{\sigma}(\gamma)
is proportional to the product:

\prod_{e\in\gamma}\sigma(e).

If the edge weights
\sigma
are constant, then we recover uniform spanning trees and write UST .
It is well known, see
[
5
, Chapter 4]
, that
\sigma
-WUST can be sampled using weighted random walks as in the Aldous-Broder algorithm, or loop-erased weighted random walks as in Wilson’s algorithm. Moreover, a celebrated result of Kirchhoff states that the edge-probabilities for
\sigma
-WUST are related to the effective resistance
\textrm{eff}\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}
for the associated electrical network with edge-conductances given by
\sigma
. More precisely, if
e\in E
, then

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{\sigma}}\left(e\in\underline{\gamma}\right)=\sigma(e)\textrm{eff}\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(e).

2.2.
Fair edge usage

As we have seen, the edge-probabilities of UST coincide with the effective resistance of each edge and can therefore vary from edge to edge. Instead of requiring that each spanning tree be equally likely as in the case of UST, we ask for laws that sample spanning trees so as to equalize the edge-probabilities as much as possible. Ideally, we would like all the edge-probabilities to be the same, but this is not always possible. When this happens we say the graph is
homogenous
. More precisely, the graph
G
is said to be homogeneous, if it admits a pmf
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{G})
with constant edge probabilities, in which case by
[
1
, Corollary 4.5]
, the edge-probabilities are uniquely determined as

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{*}}\left(e\in\underline{\gamma}\right)\equiv\frac{|V|-1}{|E|},\hskip 23.49976pt\forall e\in E.

In general, the Fairest Edge Usage (FEU) problem, introduced in
[
1
]
, tries to minimize the variance of the edge-probabilities:

(2.3)

\begin{array}[]{lll}\underset{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{G})}{\text{minimize}}&&\operatorname{Var}(\eta)\\
&&\\
\text{subject to}&&\eta(e)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(e\in\underline{\gamma}\right),\hskip 11.74988pt\forall e\in E.\end{array}

A pmf
\mu
that is optimal for the FEU problem (
2.3
) is called
fair
, and a spanning tree
\gamma
is
fair
if it is in the support of some fair pmf.
In
[
1
]
it was shown that fair pmf’s for FEU always exist, but they are in general not unique. On the other hand, the edge probabilities
\eta^{*}(e):=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{*}}\left(e\in\underline{\gamma}\right)
corresponding to a fair pmf
\mu^{*}
, are uniquely determined. In fact, the optimal
\eta^{*}
in (
2.3
) can be computed using the spanning tree modulus algorithms and is related to the family of feasible partitions via Fulkerson duality. More details about these connections can be found in
[
1
]
.

We write
\Gamma_{G}^{f}
for the set of all fair trees. Trees in
\Gamma_{G}\setminus\Gamma_{G}^{f}
are called
forbidden
. In particular, if a
\sigma
-WUST pmf
\mu_{\sigma}
is fair, then
G
does not have any forbidden trees, because
\mu_{\sigma}
is supported on all of
\Gamma_{G}
by definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A graph
H
has the
all fair
property if every spanning tree of
H
is fair:
\Gamma_{H}=\Gamma_{H}^{f}
.

2.3.
Planar modified grids

The scaling limit for UST was established in
[
4
]
. The idea there is to consider a modified grid, see Figure
2
, so that each spanning tree gives rise to a normalized Peano path. The authors in
[
4
]
show that as the mesh size of the grid goes to zero, these random Peano paths converge to a known stochastic process called
SLE_{8}
.

A
standard
m
-by-
n
planar grid
is a squared-grid with vertex set

\left(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\right)\cap\left([0,m]\times[0,n]\right).

A modified grid
G
consists of a standard
m
-by-
n
grid
G_{0}
, together with an extra node
v_{0}
connected via an edge to each node on the bottom and the right hand-side of
G_{0}
. In particular, the bottom-right corner of
G_{0}
is connected by two edges to
v_{0}
. Alternatively, we can think of
G
as the graph obtained from an
(m+1)
-by-
(n+1)
grid by identifying every node on the bottom and right hand-sides and removing all the resulting self-loops.

Figure 2.

We associate to every vertex
v\in V(G_{0})=V(G)\setminus\{v_{0}\}
in the grid an edge set
E_{v}\subset E(G)
, consisting of the edge connecting
v
to its neighbor on the right and the one below
v
. Note that if
v
belongs to the bottom or the right-side edge of the grid
G_{0}
, then the neighbor to the right, or the one below
v
, may have to be the special node
v_{0}
. Then, the family of sets
\{E_{v}\}_{v\in G_{0}}
forms a partition of
E
:

E(G)=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)\setminus\{v_{0}\}}E_{v}\hskip 23.49976pt\text{and}\hskip 23.49976ptE_{v}\cap E_{v^{\prime}}=\emptyset,\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for $v\neq v^{\prime}$}.

Consider the collection of all subgraphs of
G
that use exactly one edge from each
E_{v}
:

(2.4)

\bar{\Gamma}:=\{T\subset E:\ |T\cap E_{v}|=1,\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for all $v\in V(G_{0})$}\}.

For instance, the tree on the left in Figure
3
is in
\bar{\Gamma}
, while the tree on the right is not, because the latter tree connects the central node of the grid to both its neighbor on the right and its neighbor below.

Figure 3.

Suppose we are given a set
T\in\bar{\Gamma}
. Then, starting from any node
v\in V(G_{0})
one can follow an edge of
T
and either move to the right or down and eventually reach
v_{0}
. Thus, every
T\in\bar{\Gamma}
is connected and spans
V(G)
.
Moreover, any such
T
satisfies

(2.5)

|E(T)|=\sum_{v\in V(G_{0})}|E_{v}\cap T|=\sum_{v\in V(G_{0})}1=|V(T)|-1.

Therefore, every such
T
is a spanning tree of
G
, namely
\bar{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma_{G}

Let
\bar{\mu}
be the uniform pmf conditioned on
\bar{\Gamma}
:

\bar{\mu}(T):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\left|\bar{\Gamma}\right|}&\text{if $T\in\bar{\Gamma}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

A random tree in
\bar{\Gamma}
with law
\bar{\mu}
can be sampled by tossing independent fair coins, one for each node
v\in V(G_{0})
, and picking either the edge to the right of
v
or below
v
. In particular, notice that every tree
T\in\bar{\Gamma}
has a
partner tree
, i.e., the tree that can be obtained by substituting every edge in
T\cap E_{v}
with the other edge in
E_{v}\setminus T
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The family
\bar{\Gamma}
is the family of all fair trees for the modified grid, i.e.,

\Gamma^{f}_{G}=\bar{\Gamma}
. Also,
\bar{\mu}
is optimal for the
FEU
problem on
G
.

Moreover,
\bar{\Gamma}
consists exactly of all the trees
\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}
that have the partner tree property, i.e., the trees
\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}
such that the complement
E\setminus\gamma
is also a tree.

For the moment, we will assume Lemma
2.2
, and defer its proof to Section
4.5
.

3.
The scaling limit of fair Peano paths

We are interested in the behavior of fair Peano paths as
n\to\infty
.
Since any fair Peano path
\zeta_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
passes within distance
\frac{1}{4n}
of every vertex in the lattice
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, the limit of the full trace
\zeta_{n}[0,1]
will converge to
\overline{Q}
.
However, we are interested in a more detailed understanding. In particular, is the limit a spacefilling curve or not, and is the limit a random or deterministic object?
Theorem
1.1
answers both of these questions, showing that the limit is not a spacefilling curve and is deterministic.

\phi[0,T]=\overline{Q}

Since the image of the path fills up the entire square, a spacefilling curve is best visualized by an approximation. For an example, see Figure

We can view a spacefilling curve as a way to grow

Note that

Proof of Theorem

.

Our first step is to show that the set

and let
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
32N^{-1/4}<\epsilon
.
Set
B_{n}=\big\{\zeta\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\,:\,d_{H}\left(\beta(\zeta),\,L_{n}\right)<\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-1/4}\big\}
and define

K_{\epsilon}=\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{A}_{n}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}B_{n}\right)\cup\{\lambda\}.

Then
\mu_{n}(K_{\epsilon})=1
for
n<N
,
and by Lemma
3.1
,
\mu_{n}(K_{\epsilon})\geq 1-32n^{-1/4}\geq 1-\epsilon
for
n\geq N
.
It remains to show that
K_{\epsilon}
is compact, by showing that every sequence in
K_{\epsilon}
has a subsequence converging to an element of
K_{\epsilon}
.
A sequence in
K_{\epsilon}
must satisfy one of two cases. In the first case, there is a subsequence contained in

\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{A}_{n}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{n=N}^{M}B_{n}\right)\cup\{\lambda\}
for some
M>N
.
Since this is a finite set, there must be a constant subsequence.
In the second case, there is a subsequence
\zeta_{n_{k}}
with
\zeta_{n_{k}}\in\bigcup_{n=k}^{\infty}B_{n}
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
d(\zeta_{n_{k}},\lambda)\leq\frac{5}{2}k^{-1/4}
,
which shows that
\zeta_{n_{k}}
converges to
\lambda\in K_{\epsilon}
.
This completes our first step.

Let
\mu
be the limit of a subsequence
\mu_{n_{k}}
under weak convergence. To finish the proof, we must show that
\mu=\delta_{\lambda}
.
Set

\tilde{B}_{M}=\left(\bigcup_{n=M}^{\infty}B_{n}\right)\cup\{\lambda\},

and note that
\tilde{B}_{M}
is a closed set by the reasoning above.
Then by weak convergence and Lemma
3.1

\mu(\tilde{B}_{M})\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mu_{n_{k}}(\tilde{B}_{M})\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}1-32n_{k}^{-1/4}=1,

implying that
\mu(\tilde{B}_{M})=1
.
Since
\tilde{B}_{M}
is a decreasing sequence of sets with limit
\{\lambda\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\{\lambda\})=\lim_{M\to\infty}\mu(\tilde{B}_{M})=1.

This shows that
\mu=\delta_{\lambda}
, completing the proof.
∎

4.
Fair trees for modified grids and for standard grids

In this section we give more details about the FEU problem and then prove Lemma
2.2
. Also, we show that standard grids might be better suited for this kind of scaling limit.

4.1.
The
1
-densest subgraph

Define the measure of
1
-
density
for a graph
G
as:

\theta(G):=\frac{|E|}{|V|-1}.

Hence, by (
2.2
), when
G
is homogeneous, the edge probabilities are all equal to
\theta(G)^{-1}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}(G)
be the family of all subgraphs
H\subset G
such that
H
is:

(i)

non-trivial, meaning that it has at least one edge;

(ii)

connected;

(iii)

vertex-induced.

Note that we will often identify a subset of edges in
E(G)
with the corresponding edge-induced subgraph of
G
.
Consider the following
1
-densest subgraph problem
:
Find
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
such that

(4.1)

\theta(H)=\max_{H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}(G)}\theta(H^{\prime}).

In
[
1
]
, it was shown that the spanning tree modulus algorithm can provide a solution to this combinatorial problem. Namely, the modulus algorithm computes the optimal density
\eta^{*}
in (
2.3
). Next, if
H
is a connected component of the set of edges in
E
where the minimum value of
\eta^{*}
is attained, then
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
,
H
is homogeneous by construction and
H
solves the
1
-densest subgraph problem. Such
H
is called a
homogeneous core
of
G

4.2.
Reducibility and the deflation process

A proper subgraph
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
has the
restriction property
with respect to
G
, if for every fair tree
\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}^{f}
, the restriction
\gamma\cap H
is a spanning tree in
\Gamma_{H}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The graph
G
is said to be
reducible
, if it admits a proper subgraph
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
with the restriction property with respect to
G
. A graph that is not reducible is called
irreducible
.

When the homogeneous core
H
defined in Section
4.1
is a proper subgraph of
G
(and hence
G
is
inhomogeneous
), then
H
also has the restriction property, see
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
. Moreover, by
[
1
, Theorem 5.7]
, in this case, not only will the restriction of a fair tree
\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}
to
H
be a spanning tree of
H
, but it will also be a fair tree of
H
. Secondly, the forest
\gamma\setminus H
is mapped to a fair tree of the quotient graph
G/H
by the quotient map. And finally, given a fair tree for
H
and a fair tree for
G/H
, they can be glued together to yield a fair tree of
G
.
This is the essence of the deflation process, namely that arbitrary fair trees of
G
can be built from fair trees of homogeneous graphs.
We summarize and rephrase the main results of
[
1
]
as follow.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with multi-edges, but no loops. Then, either

(i)

G
is homogeneous, and
\theta(H)\leq\theta(G)
for all
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
; or

(ii)

G
is inhomogeneous, and hence admits a homogeneous
core

H_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
,
H_{0}\subsetneq G
, that solves the
1
-densest subgraph problem (
4.1
) with
\theta(H_{0})>\theta(G)
, and that has the restriction property with respect to
G
.

Moreover, for any fair tree
\gamma
of
G
, the projection of
\gamma\setminus H_{0}
onto
G/H_{0}
is a fair spanning tree of
G/H_{0}
. And, fair trees of the core
H_{0}
can be coupled with fair trees of the quotient graph
G/H_{0}
to produce fair trees of
G
.

4.3.
Minimal
1
-densest subgraphs

We say
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
solves the
minimal
1
-densest subgraph problem
, if
H
is a
1
-densest subgraph
and no proper subgraph of
H
is also a
1
-densest subgraph. In other words, we look for a solution of the
1
-densest subgraph problem that has the following property:

Definition 4.3
.

A graph
H
has the
strict
1
-density
property, if

\theta(H^{\prime})<\theta(H)

for all proper subgraphs
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}(H)
.

When
H\in\mathcal{H}(G)
solves the minimal
1
-densest problem, we say that
H
is a
minimal core
for
G
.
In the next result, we show that the deflation process described in Theorem
4.2
can also be phrased in terms of minimal cores instead.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with multi-edges, but no loops. Then, either

(i)

G
has the strict
1
-density property (and hence is homogeneous), or

(ii)

G
admits a
minimal core
, namely, a subgraph
H_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
,
H_{0}\subsetneq G
, that is a minimal
1
-densest subgraph. Also, in this case,

(a)

H_{0}
has the restriction property with respect to
G
;

(b)

for any fair tree
\gamma
of
G
, the restriction
\gamma\cap H_{0}
onto
H_{0}
is a fair spanning tree of
H_{0}
;

(c)

for any fair tree
\gamma
of
G
, the projection of
\gamma\setminus H_{0}
onto
G/H_{0}
is a fair spanning tree of
G/H_{0}
;

(d)

moreover, fair trees of the core
H_{0}
can be coupled with fair trees of the quotient graph
G/H_{0}
to produce fair trees of
G
.

Note that if
G
satisfies Theorem
4.4
(ii), then
G
may be homogeneous or not.
Indeed, if
G
is homogeneous and not strictly
1
-dense, then it must admit a proper subgraph with the same
1
-density.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

First note that if
G
is inhomogeneous, then by
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
,
G
admits a homogeneous core
H_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
, such that
H_{0}\subsetneq G
, and by
[
1
, Theorem 5.8]
,
\theta(H_{0})>\theta(G)
, so
G
is not strictly
1
-dense. Thus, if
G
is strictly
1
-dense, then
G
must be homogeneous. This is the (i) alternative.

Now, assume
G
is not strictly
1
-dense. Let
H_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(G)
solve the minimal
1
-densest subgraph problem for
G
. Then,
H_{0}
is a proper subgraph of
G
and
\theta(H_{0})\geq\theta(G)
. By minimality,
H_{0}
has the strict
1
-density property. Therefore, by Theorem
4.4
(i),
H_{0}
is homogeneous.

Let
H
be a homogeneous core of
G
, as described in Theorem
4.2
, so that
H
solves the
1
-densest subgraph problem and satisfies
\theta(H)^{-1}=\min_{e\in G}\eta^{*}(e)
.
Since both
H
and
H_{0}
solve the
1
-densest subgraph problem, we have
\theta(H)=\theta(H_{0})
.

To prove (a) in Theorem
4.4
(ii), assume that
H_{0}
does not have the restriction property with respect to
G
. Then there is a fair tree
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma_{G}^{f}
, such that
\gamma_{0}\cap H_{0}
is not a spanning tree of
H_{0}
. Since
\gamma_{0}\cap H_{0}
is acyclic, it must be a forest and we must have
|\gamma_{0}\cap H_{0}|<|V(H_{0})|-1
. Also, since
\gamma_{0}
is a fair tree of
G
, it must be in the support of some fair pmf
\mu^{*}
. For every other
\gamma^{\prime}
in the support of
\mu^{*}
, we also have that
\gamma^{\prime}\cap H_{0}
is acyclic, so we at least get the bound
|\gamma^{\prime}\cap H_{0}|\leq|V(H_{0})|-1
.
Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle\theta(H_{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\theta(H)^{-1}=\min_{e\in G}\eta^{*}(e)\leq\min_{e\in H_{0}}\eta^{*}(e)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{e\in H_{0}}\eta^{*}(e)=\frac{1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{e\in H_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{*}}(e\in\underline{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{e\in H_{0}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}}\mu^{*}(\gamma)\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)=\frac{1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}}\mu^{*}(\gamma)\sum_{e\in H_{0}}\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}}\mu^{*}(\gamma)|\gamma\cap H_{0}|<\frac{|V(H_{0})|-1}{|E(H_{0})|}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{G}}\mu^{*}(\gamma)=\theta(H_{0})^{-1}.

The last inequality is strict, because it is strict when
\gamma=\gamma_{0}
. This leads to a contradiction. Therefore
H_{0}
has the restriction property with respect to
G
. This proves part (a) in Theorem
4.4
(ii). Moreover, the same calculation shows that equality must hold everywhere. In particular, the minimum of
\eta^{*}
on
H_{0}
must coincide with the the average of
\eta^{*}
on
H_{0}
. Thus,

(4.2)

\eta^{*}(e)\equiv\theta(H_{0})^{-1}\hskip 23.49976pt\forall e\in H_{0}.

Finally parts (b), (c), and (d) follow from part (a), because Theorem 5.5, Lemma 5.6 and Theorem 5.7, in
[
1
]
only depend on the fact that
H_{0}
has the restriction property with respect to
G
.
∎

4.4.
Irreducibility, the all fair property, and uniformizability

Finally we make the following definition.

Definition 4.5
.

A graph
H
is
uniformizable
, if there is a set of edge-weights
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E(H)}
so that the corresponding
\sigma
-WUST pmf
\mu_{\sigma}
is fair.

The results in
[
2
]
show that the fair trees of an arbitrary graph
G
can be built from the fair trees of uniformizable graphs. In particular, one can use the Aldous-Broder or the Wilson algorithms to sample them. We summarize the results from
[
2
]
in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.6

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, possibly with multi-edges, but no loops. Assume
G
is biconnected. Then, the following are equivalent:

(a)

G
is uniformizable (see Definition
4.5
);

(b)

G
is irreducible (see Definition
4.1
);

(c)

G
has the strict
1
-density property (see Definition
4.3
);

(d)

G
has the all fair property (see Definition
2.1
).

Moreover, in this case the weights
\sigma
are unique and the corresponding
\sigma
-
WUST

\mu_{\sigma}
is the fair pmf of maximal entropy among all the fair pmf’s.

In the next two sections we use this theory to describe the fair trees for modified grids and for regular grids.

4.5.
Fair trees for modified planar grids

We begin by computing the fair trees for the modified grids, by proving Lemma
2.2
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

To see that
\bar{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma^{f}_{G}
, it is enough to show that
\bar{\mu}
is optimal for the
FEU
problem on
G
. Notice that, when
T\in\bar{\Gamma}
, each edge
e\in E(T)
lies in
T\cap E_{v}
for some vertex
v\in V(G_{0}).
Also, by construction, exactly half of the trees of
\bar{\Gamma}
use any given
e
. Therefore, the edge-usage probabilities for
\bar{\mu}
are:

\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\mu}}(e\in\underline{T})=\sum_{T\in\bar{\Gamma}}\bar{\mu}(T)\mathcal{N}(T,e)=\frac{1}{|\bar{\Gamma}|}\sum_{T\in\bar{\Gamma}}\mathcal{N}(T,e)=\frac{1}{2},

where
\mathcal{N}(T,e)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in T\}}
gives the usage of
e
by
T
.
Coincidentally, this also proves that
G
is homogeneous.

Before proving the other inclusion, we examine the reducibility of
G
.
Note that
G
has
mn+1
vertices and
2mn
edges. Therefore, it has
1
-density

(4.3)

\theta(G)=2.

Let
H
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the special vertex
v_{0}
and the bottom-right corner of the standard grid
G_{0}
. Thus,
H
consists of two nodes connected by a double edge. We refer to such graphs as
digons
. In particular,
\theta(H)=\theta(G)=2
. By Theorem
4.4
,
G
is not strictly
1
-dense and hence
G
is reducible. Moreover, since
G
is homogeneous, Theorem
4.2
shows that every subgraph in
\mathcal{H}(G)
has
1
-density at most
2
. This shows that
H
is a solution of the Minimal
1
-Densest Subgraph (MDS) problem for
G
and by Theorem
4.4
(ii), we can deflate
G
with respect to
H
.

If we do that, the resulting quotient graph
G/H
still maintain the structure whereby there is a special node
v_{0}
and the edge-set
E
can be partitioned as
\cup_{v\in V\setminus\{v_{0}\}}E_{v}
with
|E_{v}|=2
, as before. In particular, since the edge count decreases by
2
and the node count decreases by
1
,
G/H
still has
1
-density equal to
2
. Moreover, this creates additional digons and
G/H
is again homogeneous, so these digons solve the MDS problem, which means that we can repeat the deflation process iteratively. For instance, we can apply the deflation process going from right to left, row by row, until we are left with a single digon graph. Finally, observe that at each step the two edges that get contracted are exactly the pairs of edges in the sets
E_{v}
for
v\in V(G)\setminus\{v_{0}\}
defined above.

Now assume that
T\in\Gamma_{G}\setminus\bar{\Gamma}
. Then, by definition of
\bar{\Gamma}
, and the Pigeonhole principle, there exists vertices
v^{*},v_{*}\in V(T)
, such that
|T\cap E_{v^{*}}|=2
and
|T\cap E_{v_{*}}|=0
. Indeed,
T
must choose
mn
edges from
E
, which is partitioned into
mn
pairs of edges of the form
E_{v}
.
However, this means that in the deflation process described above, we are guaranteed to get to a point where the two edges in, say,
E_{v_{*}}
connect
v_{*}
to a single other node
u_{*}
, so as to form a subgraph
H_{*}
of
1
-density two in a larger homogeneous graph
G_{*}
of the same
1
-density. Let
T_{*}
be the tree obtained from
T
by removing all the pairs
E_{v}
that have been involved in the deflation thus far. Then,
T_{*}
does not restrict to a spanning tree of
H_{*}
, and thus
T
is forbidden.
∎

4.6.
Fair trees for regular planar grids

One reason the scaling limit of the Peano pathss in the case of modified grids turned out to be deterministic is because the family of fair spanning trees is much smaller than the family of all spanning trees.
To explore a setting that may have a richer collection of spanning trees, we return to regular planar grids.

Recall the strict
1
-denseness property as defined in Definition
4.3
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The
m
-by-
n
planar grid
G_{m,n}
has the strict
1
-density property.

In particular, by Lemma
4.7
, Theorem
4.6
, and Definition
4.5
,
there is a unique set of edge weights
\sigma>0
on
E(G_{m,n})
so that the corresponding
\sigma
-WUST
\mu_{\sigma}
is fair. This suggests a different approach to the scaling limit of fair Peano paths. Namely, sample fair
\sigma
-WUST trees from the grid
G_{m,n}
and then condition on such trees containing the bottom and right hand-side of the grid. This should give rise to a much richer family of spanning trees that may have a non-deterministic scaling limit. We will pursue these considerations in a separate paper.

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

First note that:

|V(G_{m,n})|=mn\hskip 23.49976pt\text{and}\hskip 23.49976pt|E(G_{m,n})|=(m-1)n+m(n-1)=2mn-m-n

So

\theta(G_{m,n})=\frac{2mn-m-n}{mn-1}=2-\frac{m+n+2}{mn-1}

In particular,

\frac{\partial\theta(G_{m,n})}{\partial m}=-\frac{(mn-1)-n(m+n+2)}{(mn-1)^{2}}=\frac{(n+1)^{2}}{(mn-1)^{2}}>0,

and by symmetry the same holds for the derivative in
n
, meaning that the
1
-density of rectangular grids is strictly increasing with the size of the grid, is always less than
2
, and in fact tends to
2
as
m,n\rightarrow\infty
.

Now let
H
be a proper connected vertex-induced subgraph of
G=G_{m,n}
. We first consider the smallest rectangular grid containing
H
. Namely, let

i_{min}:=\min\{i:\ \exists j,(i,j)\in V(H)\}

and similarly define
i_{max},j_{min}
, and
j_{max}
. Then, define the translated grid

\tilde{G}:=(i_{min},j_{min})+G_{i_{max}-i_{min},j_{max}-j_{min}}.

If
H=\tilde{G}
, then
\theta(H)=\theta(\tilde{G})<\theta(G)
and we are done. If
H
is a proper subgraph of
\tilde{G}
, then since
\theta(\tilde{G})\leq\theta(G)
, it will be enough to show that
\theta(H)<\theta(\tilde{G})
. In particular, without loss of generality we can assume that
\tilde{G}=G_{m,n}
.

As an embedded planar graph
G_{m,n}
has
(m-1)(n-1)
bounded faces. Whenever such a face has all four vertices in
V(H)
we fill it in so that
H
becomes a connected compact set
\hat{H}
in the plane. In particular, the complement of
\hat{H}
is an open set consisting of finitely many bounded components
\Omega_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
, and one unbounded component
\Omega_{0}
. The boundary of each one of these components can be parametrized by a curve
\Gamma_{j}
,
j=0,\dots,k
. This can be seen by thickening
\hat{H}
a little bit, for instance by defining

\hat{H}_{\epsilon}:=\bigcup_{z\in\hat{H}}\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w-z|\leq\epsilon\}.

For each
\epsilon
, the boundary of
\hat{H}_{\epsilon}
consists of
C^{1}
-smooth Jordan curves
\Gamma_{j,\epsilon}
,
j=0,\dots,k
. If we parametrize each
\Gamma_{j,\epsilon}
with its arc-length parametrization, then, as
\epsilon
tends to
0
, they converge uniformly to the curves
\Gamma_{j}
.

We begin by looking at the boundary of the unbounded component
\Omega_{0}
and think of it as being parametrized counter-clockwise. In particular,
\Gamma_{0}
can be taken to be piecewise linear so that the derivative
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}
is well defined away from the nodes of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Since
\Gamma_{0,\epsilon}
bounds the simply connected domain
\Omega_{0,\epsilon}\cup\{\infty\}
, by the argument principle, the change in argument for the derivative
\Gamma_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}
, as the curve completes one full loop, is
2\pi
. As
\epsilon
tends to
0
, this property is inherited by
\Gamma_{0}
, as long as the argument of the derivative
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}
is properly defined.
At any moment when
\Gamma_{0}
is not at a node of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the right hand of a walker traveling along with
\Gamma_{0}
will always be touching
\Omega_{0}
. In particular, the only changes in the argument of
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}
that are allowed when
\Gamma_{0}
passes through a node of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
are

0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\text{ and, }-\frac{\pi}{2}.

In words, either the walker goes straight, turns
90^{\circ}
left, does a
180^{\circ}
turn in the positive direction, or turns
90^{\circ}
right. This can be verified by looking at the unbounded component
\Omega_{0,\epsilon}
of the thickened
\hat{H}_{\epsilon}
. Indeed, if
\Gamma_{0}
were to do a
180^{\circ}
turn in the negative direction, then while walking along
\Gamma_{0,\epsilon}
the right hand would be touching the thickened neighborhood of an edge, and this neighborhood would disappear as
\epsilon
tends to
0
, so that edge would not be part of the boundary of
\Omega_{0}
, which leads to a contradiction.

Now assume
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a node where
\Gamma_{0}
makes a right turn (
90^{\circ}
in the negative direction), and let
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be the node visited by
\Gamma_{0}
just before
v
, and
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
the one visited just after
v
.
Then,
u,v,w
bound a face
f
of
G_{m,n}
and the fourth corner
t
does not belong to
H
. To see why, assume by contradiction that all four corners are in
V(H)
, then the face would be contained in
\hat{H}
. But then the right hand would not be touching the unbounded component
\Omega_{0}
in this case.
Also, note that since
H
is vertex-induced the two sides of
f
incident at
t
also do not belong to
E(H)
.

Now, if we add
t
to
V(H)
, to get a new vertex-induced graph
H^{\prime}
. Since one of the coordinates of
t
is equal to one of the coordinates of either
u
or
w
, we see that
H^{\prime}
is still a subgraph of
G_{m,n}
. Moreover, we are guaranteed that

E(H^{\prime})\geq E(H)+2.

In particular,

\theta(H^{\prime})=\frac{E(H^{\prime})}{V(H^{\prime})-1}\geq\frac{E(H)+2}{V(H)}

Since
H
is a subgraph of the grid
G_{m,n}
, we always have
\deg_{H}(x)\leq 4
. Moreover, at least one vertex of
H
has
\deg_{H}(x)<4
. Therefore, by the Handshake Lemma,

2E(H)=\sum_{x\in V(H)}\deg_{H}(x)<4V(H).

This implies that
\theta(H^{\prime})>\theta(H)
because

\displaystyle\frac{E(H)+2}{V(H)}>\frac{E(H)}{V(H)-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(E(H)+2)(V(H)-1)>E(H)V(H)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow 2V(H)>E(H).

If
H^{\prime}=G_{m,n}
, we are done. Otherwise, we can repeat the same argument with
H
replaced by
H^{\prime}
. This process has to end, so without loss of generality, we can assume that
\Gamma_{0}
never makes any right turns. In particular, the argument of
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}
can only change by
0
,
\pi/2
, or
\pi
. So since there has to be an even number of argument changes that are positive, and the sum must be
2\pi
, we either get two changes by
\pi
or four changes by
\pi/2
. In either case, we see that
\Gamma_{0}
describes the boundary of a rectangular grid, and by assumption this grid has to be
G_{m,n}
.

Finally, consider a bounded component
\Omega_{j}
with
j\geq 1
. Assume that the boundary of
\Omega_{j}
is described in the clockwise direction by a curve
\Gamma_{j}
, constructed as
\Gamma_{0}
above, using the thickened set
\hat{H}_{\epsilon}
. Once again the allowed argument changes at a grid node are
0,\pi/2,\pi
, and
-\pi/2
. Also, when walking along
\Gamma_{j}
the right-hand touches
\Omega_{j}
and the total argument change must equal
-2\pi
. In particular, there must always be at least four right turns with argument change
-\pi/2
. By repeating the argument above we see that each right turn identifies a vertex in
\Omega_{j}
that can be added to
H
in a way to increase the
1
-density, and therefore after finitely many steps we get that the component
\Omega_{j}
has been filled in. Hence, after finitely many steps,
H=G_{m,n}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

5.
Appendix

For the convenience of the reader we include a proof of needed result about the running maximum
M_{k}
of
|S_{k}|
for the 1-dimensional simple random walk
S_{k}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}\geq r)\leq 2k/r^{2}
for
r>0
.

Proof.

Let
r>0
and assume that
r\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Set
M_{k}^{+}=\max\{S_{0},S_{1},\cdots,S_{k}\}
and
M_{k}^{-}=\min\{S_{0},S_{1},\cdots,S_{k}\}
.
Then for
v\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\mathbb{P}(M_{k}^{+}\geq r\text{ and }S_{k}=v)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=v)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{ if }v\geq r\\
\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=2r-v)\;\;\text{ if }v<r\end{cases}.

(Reason for second case: The probability of going up to
r
and then down to
v
is the same as going up to
r
and continuing up to
2r-v
. In other words, think of reflecting the future of the simple random walk over the line
y=r
at the point it first reaches height
r
.)
Now summing over
v
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M_{k}^{+}\geq r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=r}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=v)+\sum_{v=-\infty}^{r-1}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=2r-v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=r)+2\sum_{v=r+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(S_{k}=r)+2\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\geq r+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\geq r)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|S_{k}|\geq r).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M_{k}\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{k}^{+}\geq r)+\mathbb{P}(M_{k}^{-}\leq-r)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{P}(M_{k}^{+}\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{P}(|S_{k}|\geq r)

\displaystyle\leq 2k/r^{2},

where the last step is from Chebyshev’s Inequality.
This proves the result for integer
r
, but since
M_{k}
is integer-valued, we are able to extend this to all
r>0
.
∎
