Permutations With A Given X X -Descent Set
Mohamed Omar

Abstract
. Motivated by the work of Grinberg and Stanley, we investigate the enumeration of permutations with a given X X -descent set, a generalization of the classical notion of a descent set. Our work expands on recent developments of Diaz-Lopez, Harris, Insko, Omar and Sagan and has interesting connections to Hamiltonian paths in directed graphs.

In this article, we look at a broader class of permutations that have restricted relations between consecutive elements at prescribed indices. This is motivated by work of Grinberg and Stanley
[
13
]
who developed a rich combinatorial understanding of quasisymmetric functions intimately related to permutations of these types. The key definition is the following (and throughout this article,
\mathbb{N}
is the positive integers):

Definition 1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
. For a positive integer
n
, and a set
I\subseteq[n-1]
, define

D_{X}(I;n)=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\ :\ (\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})\in X\ \mbox{ if and only if }i\in I\}.

The permutations in
D_{X}(I;n)
are referred to as permutations with
X
-descent set
I
. We further denote by
d_{X}(I;n)
the number of these permutations; that is
d_{X}(I;n)=|D_{X}(I;n)|
.

The notion of an
X
-descent set appears throughout related literature. When
X=\{(i,j)\ :\ i>j\}
we have
D_{X}(I;n)=D(I;n)
, the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with descent set
I
. If
I\subset[n-1]
and
\mathcal{P}(I;n)
is the set of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with peak set
I
, then again letting
X=\{(i,j)\ :\ i>j\}
, we have
\mathcal{P}(I;n)=D_{\overline{X}}(I-1;n)\cap D_{X}(I,n)
where,
I-1
is a shift of
I
by one to the left, and
\overline{X}
is the complement of
X
. This follows since a peak at an index is an ascent directly before the index followed by a descent at that index. Other classes of permutations even outside of our relevant literature can be cast as permutations with a given
X
-descent set as well. For instance, reverse succession-free permutations are those permutations
\pi
for which
\pi_{j}-\pi_{j+1}\neq 1
for any
j
, and so permutations that are reverse succession-free at prescribed indices
I
are precisely those in the set
D_{X}(I;n)
where
X=\{(i+1,i)\ :\ i\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

This article is a first foray into understanding the enumerative aspects of the sets
D_{X}(I;n)
, in particular understanding the numbers
d_{X}(I;n)
. In Section 2, we develop recursive formula for
d_{X}(I;n)
based on properties of
X
and
I
. We also proceed as much of the literature on this topic does by considering what conditions on
X
and
I
guarantee that
d_{X}(I;n)
is a polynomial in
n
when
I
is fixed. In these investigations, we will see how determining
d_{X}(I;n)
is intimately related to the enumeration of directed Hamiltonian paths in directed graphs. Section 3 is dedicated to the explicit enumeration of
d_{X}(I;n)
for various prescribed sets
X
as
I
varies. We conclude with open problems in Section 4.

2.
Recurrence Formulae and Polynomiality

Like many of the related constructions in the literature we can develop recursive formulas to compute
d_{X}(I;n)
. The first two are similar in nature to Proposition 2.1 and its consequence Theorem 2.3 in
[
8
]
. Throughout our arguments, for a nonempty set of indices
I
we denote by
I^{-}
the set
I
with its maximum element removed.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
I\neq\emptyset
. Let
m
be the maximum element in
I
. Then

d_{X}(I;n)=\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(I^{-};m)d_{X}(\emptyset;n-m)-d_{X}(I^{-};n).

Proof.

Consider the set
P
of permutations
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that can be written as
\pi^{\prime}\pi^{\prime\prime}
where

(1)

|\pi^{\prime}|=m
,
|\pi^{\prime\prime}|=n-m

(2)

\mbox{XDes}(\pi^{\prime})=I^{-}
,
\mbox{XDes}(\pi^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset

Here, for a permutation
\sigma
,
\mbox{XDes}(\sigma)
is the set of positions where
\sigma
has an
X
-descent, that is
\{i\in[n]:(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1})\in X\}
.

The number of such
\pi
that have an
X
-descent at
m
is
d_{X}(I;n)
and the number that do not is
d_{X}(I^{-};n)
so
|P|=d_{X}(I;n)+d_{X}(I^{-};n).
On the other hand the permutations in
P
can be constructed as follows. Select
\binom{n}{m}
labels for the entries in
\pi^{\prime}
. For that fixed set of entries, there are
d_{X}(I^{-};m)
choices for
\pi^{\prime}
, for a total of
\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(I^{-},m)
choices for
\pi^{\prime}
. Finally, the labels for
\pi^{\prime\prime}
are fixed since those of
\pi^{\prime}
are selected and the number of choices for them is
d_{X}(\emptyset,n-m)
. Altogether then,
|P|=\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(I^{-};m)d_{X}(\emptyset,n-m).
The result then follows from equating the two expressions for
|P|
.
∎

Notice in the case of descents, when
X=\{(i,j):i>j\}
, that
d_{X}(\emptyset;n-m)=1
for any
n>m
because there is only one permutation with no descents. It is a quick consequence in this case, as stated in Corollary 2.2 of
[
8
]
, that
d_{X}(I;n)
is a polynomial in
n
. We can generalize this for generic
X
in the case that
d_{X}(\emptyset;\cdot)
is constant.

Corollary 3
.

For
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
, if
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
is constant with respect to
n
, then
d_{X}(I;n)
is a polynomial in
n
of degree
m=\max(I)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from induction on
|I|
. If
|I|=0
then
d_{X}(I;n)=1
for all
n
and this has degree
0
. Now for induction, by Theorem
2
,
d_{X}(I;n)=C\cdot\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(I^{-};m)-d_{X}(I^{-};n)
,
where
C=d_{X}(\emptyset;\cdot)
. We notice
C\cdot\binom{n}{m}
is a polynomial of degree
m
and
d_{X}(I^{-};m)
is a constant. Furthermore,
d_{X}(I^{-};n)
is inductively a polynomial of degree
\max(I^{-})<m
. Consequently
d_{X}(I;n)
is a polynomial of degree
m
.
∎

One can use Theorem
2
to develop a formula for
d_{X}(I;n)
in terms of the numbers
\{d_{X}(\emptyset;k)\}_{k\geq 0}
that mimics those found in
[
3

[

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}

d_{X}(I;n)

\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot d_{X}(\emptyset,n-i_{j_{r}})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r}-1})\cdots d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}}).

We can argue inductively on
|I|
. If
|I|=1
then
I=\{m\}
and
I^{-}=\emptyset
. The given formula predicts

\displaystyle d_{X}(I;n)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1-1}\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(\emptyset,n-m)d_{X}(\emptyset,m)+(-1)^{1-0}d_{X}(\emptyset,n)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{m}d_{X}(I^{-},m)d_{X}(\emptyset;n-m)-d_{X}(\emptyset;n)

. For arbitrary

I^{-}=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k-1}\}
and

\displaystyle d_{X}(I;n)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{i_{k}}d_{X}(I^{-};i_{k})d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{k})-d_{X}(I^{-};n)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}}(-1)^{k-1-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt\cdot d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{j_{r}})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r-1}})\cdots d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{r}<k\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-(r+1)}\binom{n}{i_{k}}\binom{i_{k}}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt\cdot d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{k})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{k}-i_{j_{r}})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r-1}})\cdots d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt\cdot d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{j_{r}})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r-1}})\cdots d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt\cdot d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{j_{r}})d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r}-1})\cdots d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}}).

the last equality coming from the fact that the summands on the left hand side are appropriately partitioned by those that contain
i_{k}
and those that do not.
∎

It is clear then that the crux to enumerating
d_{X}(I;n)
is determining
d_{X}(\emptyset,k)
for all
k
. We will see that this reduces to a problem which is itself notoriously intractable. Despite this, we can still infer much about
d_{X}(\emptyset,\cdot)
in general cases (as we do in Section 2) and in combinatorially relevant situations (as we do in Section 3).

Note that the recursive formula in Theorem
4
unfortunately has cancellation. We can develop a cancellation-free expression though for particular sets
X
and particular values of
n
. This generalizes a recursive formula in
[
14
]
and is of the same ilk as a recursive formula in
[

.

Let
I\neq\emptyset
, with
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
. For notational ease, given
1\leq r\leq k
, define
I_{k}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r-1},i_{r}-1,\ldots,i_{k}-1\}-\{0\}
and
\hat{I}_{k}=\{i_{1},\cdots,i_{r-1},i_{r+1}-1,\cdots,i_{k}-1\}
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
I\neq\emptyset
and suppose
n
is such that
(n+1,i)\in X
for every
i\leq n
. Then

d_{X}(I;n+1)=d_{X}(I;n)+\sum_{i_{r}\in I^{\prime\prime}}d_{X}(I_{r};n)+\sum_{i_{r}\in I^{\prime}}d_{X}(\hat{I}_{r};n).

Proof.

Partition
D_{X}(I;n+1)
depending on the position of
n+1
. The set of permutations
\pi\in D_{X}(I;n+1)
with
\pi_{n+1}=n+1
is precisely the set of permutations in
D_{X}(I;n)
. If
\pi_{r}=n+1
with
i_{r}>1
and
(\pi_{i_{r}-1},\pi_{i_{r}+1})\in X
, the possible
i_{r}
where this occurs are the elements of
I^{\prime\prime}
. Similarly, if
\pi_{i_{r}}=n+1
with
i_{r}=1
, or
i_{r}>1
and
(\pi_{i_{r}-1},\pi_{i_{r}+1})\notin X
then the corresponding
\pi^{\prime}
are counted by the second sum.
∎

[
3
]

[
5
]
,
[

a primary concern is when the focal enumeration problems are counted by polynomials. The benefit of these expressions being polynomials is that it makes them amenable to an expansion in terms of binomial bases, which gives potential for unlocking a better enumerative understanding of the counting functions at hand. In this light, we investigate conditions which gives rise to
d_{X}(I;n)
being a polynomial in
n
for fixed
I
. To start, we need to re-visit the question of how to calculate
d_{X}(I;n)
at all. Corollary
10
and Theorem
2
make clear that determining
d_{X}(I;n)
hinges heavily on the values
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
for positive integers
n
. This turns out to be related to counting directed Hamiltonian paths in directed graphs. We need a pertinent definition to begin.

Definition 6
.

For
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
, define
G(X)
to be the directed graph whose vertex set is
\mathbb{N}
, and whose edge set is
\{(i,j)\ :\ (i,j)\notin X,\ i\neq j\}
. For a positive integer
n
, define
G_{n}(X)
to be the subgraph of
G(X)
induced by the set of vertices
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

The following is a graph theoretic interpretation for determining
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
:

Proposition 7
.

For a positive integer
n
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
,
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
is the number of directed Hamiltonian paths in
G_{n}(X)
.

Proof.

Writing the permutations in
D_{X}(\emptyset,n)
in one-line notation we have
D_{X}(\emptyset,n)=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\ :\ (\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})\notin X\mbox{ for any }i\}.
There is a bijection then between
D_{X}(\emptyset,n)
and directed Hamiltonian paths in
G_{n}(X)
which sends
\pi\in D_{X}(\emptyset,n)
to the directed path

(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}),(\pi_{2},\pi_{3}),\ldots,(\pi_{n-1},\pi_{n}).

∎

We turn to an example to illustrate Theorem
2
using Proposition
7
. Consider the set
X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup X_{3}
where

X_{1}=\{(i,i)\ :\ i\in\mathbb{N}\},\ \ X_{2}=\{(i+1,i)\ :\ i\in\mathbb{N}\},\ \ X_{3}=\{(i,j)\ :\ i+1<j,\ i,j\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Then for any positive integer
n
, the graph
G_{n}(X)
has edge set

E(G_{n}(X))=\{(i,i+1)\ :\ 1\leq i\leq n-1\}\ \cup\ \{(i,j)\ :\ j+1<i,\ 1\leq i,j\leq n\}.

This graph has exactly
n
directed Hamiltonian paths. Indeed suppose a directed Hamiltonian path begins at vertex
i<n
. Since the vertices
i,i+1,\ldots,n-1
have outdegree
1
in
G_{n}(X)
, the path must have the directed edges
(i,i+1),(i+1,i+2),\ldots,(n-1,n)
in that order. Moreover, the vertices
1,2,\ldots,i-2
have outdegree
1
so on this directed path we must have the edges
(1,2),(2,3),\ldots,(i-2,i-1)
. The only way to complete the directed path is either to have the directed edge
(i-1,i)
or the directed edge
(n,1)
to be present. This gives rise to a total of
n
directed Hamiltonian paths in
G_{n}(X)
. Consequently,
d_{X}(\emptyset,n)=n
for any
n
, and by Theorem
2
, we get that if
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n}\}
,

d_{X}(I;n)=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot(n-i_{j_{r}})(i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r}-1})\cdots i_{j_{1}}.

Proposition
7
and the previous example indicate that computing
d_{X}(I;n)
for generic
X
is in fact quite difficult because it relies on computing the number of Hamiltonian paths in directed graphs
G_{n}(X)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a problem that in turn is generally quite difficult.

We can, however, enumerate such paths theoretically for many classes of directed graphs, and subsequently infer the values
d_{X}(\emptyset,n)
when
G_{n}(X)
are in such a class. For instance, this can be done for tournaments by using a formula for the number of Hamiltonian paths in a tournament due to Grinberg and Stanley
[
13
]
.

Theorem 8

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let
D
be a tournament on vertex set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, meaning for each
i\neq j
, exactly one of
(i,j)
or
(j,i)
is a directed edge in
D
. Then the number of Hamiltonian paths in
D
is
\sum_{w}2^{nsc(w)}

where
w
ranges over all permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of odd order such that every nonsingleton cycle of
w
is a (directed) cycle of
D
and where
\mbox{nsc}(w)
denotes the number of nonsingleton cycles of
w
.

This translates directly for our purposes to the following:

Theorem 9
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
n
be a positive integer. Then

d_{X}(\emptyset;n)=\sum_{w}2^{nsc(w)}

where
w
ranges over all permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of odd order such that every nonsingleton cycle
w
is a directed cycle in
G_{n}(X)
and where
nsc(w)
denotes the number of nonsingleton cycles of
w
.

We see from this that we can find a large class of sets
X\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
for which
d_{X}(I;n)
is a polynomial in
n
for fixed
I
, and furthermore find a large class of sets
X
where
d_{X}(I;n)
is exactly the number of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with descent set
I
.

Corollary 10
.

Fix
I\neq\emptyset
. If
G(X)
is a tournament with no odd directed cycles then
d_{X}(I;n)=d(I;n)
for every
n
. Moreover, if
G(X)
has finitely many directed odd cycles, then there is a polynomial
p(x)
and a positive integer
N
such that
d_{X}(I;n)=p(n)
for all
n\geq N
.

Proof.

First, suppose
G(X)
has no directed cycles. Then
G_{i}(X)
has no directed cycles for any
i
. From this, for any
i
,
w=id
is the only permutation of odd order such that every nonsingleton single of
w
is a directed cycle of
G_{i}(X)
. By the previous theorem, this implies
d_{X}(\emptyset;i)=2^{0}=1
for all
i
. Now by Theorem
2
,
d_{X}(I;n)
and
d(I;n)
satisfy the same recurrence with the same base cases and so they are equal for all
n
.

For the remainder of the claim, let
M
be larger than the largest element of
\mathbb{N}
in any cycle of
G(X)
. The sum
nsc(w)
in the summation from Theorem
9
will only have contribution from summands involving vertices at most
M
. Because of this,
\sum_{w}2^{nsc(w)}
is constant in
G_{M}(X),G_{M+1}(X),\cdots
and thus there is a constant
C
for which
C=d_{X}(\emptyset,M)=d_{X}(\emptyset,M+1)=\cdots
. Now select
N
so that
N\geq M+i_{k}
. Then for any
1\leq j\leq k
we have
N-i_{j}\geq N-i_{k}\geq M
, so
d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{j})=C
for all
n\geq N
. By Theorem
4
this implies
d_{X}(I;n)
is polynomial as each of the contribution
d_{X}(\emptyset;n-i_{j_{r}}),d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{r}}-i_{j_{r-1}}),\ldots,d_{X}(\emptyset;i_{j_{1}})
is constant.

∎

One might ask whether the condition of having finitely many directed odd cycles in

is not only sufficient but is also necessary for deeming

to be polynomial in

. It may be difficult to discern this because the expression in Theorem

that

does not. By Theorem

, for every

d_{X}(\emptyset;n_{i+1})-d_{X}(\emptyset;n)\geq 2^{nsc(w)}
where

is the permutation in

given by
w=(j_{1}^{i}\ j_{2}^{i}\ \cdots\ j_{r_{i}}^{i})
. As a consequence
d_{X}(\emptyset;n_{i})<d_{X}(\emptyset;n_{i+1})
and so

d_{X}(\emptyset,n_{1})<d_{X}(\emptyset,n_{2})<d_{X}(\emptyset,n_{3})<\cdots

3.
Computing
d_{X}(I;n)

This section is dedicated to explicit computations of
d_{X}(I;n)
for various sets
X\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
. As we saw in Section 2 through Theorem
9
and its consequences, the key is computing
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
for all
n
. A colloquial way to put this is, if we have a counting phenomenon on permutations in which we are interested in counting the number of permutations that have the phenomenon occurring at a prescribed index set
I
, we can perform this count if we know the number of permutations that do not have the phenomenon occurring at all.

For ease of notation, throughout this section when we have an index set
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
we add an additional index
i_{0}=0
for our summations.

3.1.
Succession

We look at
d_{X}(I;n)
where

X=\{(2,1),(3,2),(4,3),\ldots\},

so that
d_{X}(I;n)
counts permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that have reverse successions only at the positions in
I
. For this
X
, we can get an explicit formula for
d_{X}(I;n)
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
X=\{(2,1),(3,2),(4,3),\ldots\}
,
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
and set
i_{0}=0
. Then

d_{X}(I;n)=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot(n-i_{j_{r}})!\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-i_{j_{r}}}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}-i+1}{i!}\right)\cdot\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}C_{\ell}

where

C_{\ell}=(i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}})!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-i+1}{i!}\right)

Proof.

The permutations
D_{X}(\emptyset;n)
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are referred to as
reverse succession free
and it is classically known that the exponential generating function for
\{d_{X}(\emptyset;n)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is given by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{X}(\emptyset;n)\cdot\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{e^{-x}}{(1-x)^{2}}.

A direct calculation then shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{X}(\emptyset;n)\cdot\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)\cdot x^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}\cdot(n-i)!(n-i+1)\right)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

which simplifies to
\displaystyle d_{X}(\emptyset;n)=n!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{n-i+1}{i!}\right).
The result then follows by applying Theorem
4
.
∎

We can argue similarly for permutations that have successions at specific indices. For an index set
I
, this the set
D_{X}(I;n)
where
X=\{(1,2),(2,3),\ldots\}
.

Theorem 12
.

If
X=\{(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),\ldots\}
and
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
then

d_{X}(I;n)=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot(n-i_{j_{r}}-1)!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-i_{j_{r}}-1}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}-i}{i!}\right)\cdot\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}C_{\ell}

where

C_{\ell}=(i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-1)!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-1}(-1)^{i}\cdot\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-i}{i!}\right)

Proof.

Again appealing to Theorem
4
, we need to determine
d_{X}(\emptyset;n)
for various
n
. We can do this with a direct inclusion-exclusion argument. Let
A_{i}
be the set of permutations that have a succession at index
i
and
A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
. It is clear that for
\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k}\}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
that
\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}A_{\ell_{j}}
is in bijection with
\mathfrak{S}_{n-k}
by truncating the two consecutive terms in any succession into one term, so

d_{X}(\emptyset;n)=n!-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{k+1}(n-k)!\binom{n-1}{k}=(n-1)!\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\cdot\frac{n-k}{k!}.

The result then follows by applying Theorem
4
.
∎

We now consider successions where an odd number follows an odd number at specific positions.

Theorem 13
.

If
X=\{(2n+1,2m+1)\ :\ n,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
then

d_{X}(I;n)=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}}{2}\right\rfloor\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}}{2}+1\right\rfloor\cdot\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}C_{\ell}

where

C_{\ell}=\left\lfloor\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}}{2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}}{2}+1\right\rfloor!

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for any
n
we have

d_{X}(\emptyset;n)=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+1\right\rfloor!

First suppose
n
is odd. The permutation given must alternate between odd and even numbers, starting and ending with an odd number. There are
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+1\right\rfloor!
ways to permute the
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+1\right\rfloor
odd numbers and
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!
to permute the
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
even numbers for a total of
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}+1\right\rfloor!
such permutations. A similar analysis leads to the same result when
n
is even.
∎

Finally we consider a similar succession where an even number follows and even number at specific positions. We omit the proof as the analysis is similar to the previous theorem.

Theorem 14
.

If
X=\{(2n,2m)\ :\ n,m\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
then

d_{X}(I;n)=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}}(-1)^{k-r}\binom{n}{i_{j_{r}}}\binom{i_{j_{r}}}{i_{j_{r-1}}}\cdots\binom{i_{j_{2}}}{i_{j_{1}}}\cdot(n-i_{j_{r}})\left\lfloor\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}-1}{2}\right\rfloor\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n-i_{j_{r}}-1}{2}+1\right\rfloor\cdot\prod_{\ell=1}^{r-1}C_{\ell}

where

C_{\ell}=(i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}})\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-1}{2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{i_{j_{\ell}}-i_{j_{\ell-1}}-1}{2}+1\right\rfloor!

Proof.

This proof is similar to the proof of the previous theorem.
∎

Open Problems

One of the first questions of interest when considering the sets

X,I

n

X

is
not

2

then

is admissible and in fact
D_{X}(I;n)=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. These two examples can be seen as two extremes to the question of admissibility, which a full characterization of would help understand the numeration of
D_{X}(I;n)
for various

and
