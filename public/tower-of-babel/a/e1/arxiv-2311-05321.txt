Finite Element Analysis of the Oseen eigenvalue problem Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Felipe Lepe, Gonzalo Rivera, Jesus Vellojin

Abstract
We propose and analyze a finite element method for the Oseen eigenvalue problem. This problem is an extension of the Stokes eigenvalue problem, where the presence of the convective term leads to a non-symmetric problem and hence, to complex eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. With the aid of the compact operators theory, we prove that for inf-sup stable finite elements the convergence holds and hence, error estimates for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are derived. We also propose an a posteriori error estimator which results to be reliable and efficient. We report a series of numerical tests in two and three dimension in order to assess the performance of the method and the proposed estimator.



keywords

Oseen equations, eigenvalue problems, error estimates, mixed problems

Introduction

In fluid mechanics, the knowledge of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions associated to spectral problems are important since they are the core in the analysis of stability of certain systems. Since several partial differential equations are difficult to solve analytically, the development of numerical techniques to approximate accurately the solutions is such equations play an important role in mathematics and engineering. In the case of the Stokes eigenvalue problem, the literature is abundant, where several formulations and numerical methods have emerged. On this subject we mention
[
1
,
19
,
9
,
8
,
10
,
15
,
18
,
21
,
16
,
22
]
and the references therein.

The Oseen problem appears as a linearization of the Navier-Stokes equations. For the best of the author’s knowledge, the eigenvalue problem associated to the Ossen system has not been analyzed, at least from a numerical point of view. This problem, contrary to the Stokes eigenvalue problem, results to be non-selfadjoint due to the presence of the convective term. Hence, the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues must be found naturally on complex spaces, which clearly is a difference with the Stokes eigensystem. This is of course the theoretical implication since in practice, particularly on the computational tests, it is possible to recover real solutions instead of complex eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The relation between the viscosity and the convective velocity of the system promotes the appearance of complex eigenvalues. However, for real applications of Stokes and Oseen, the Reynolds number is assumed to be small, and therefore it is more feasible to recover real eigenvalues
[
12
]
.

The non-symmetric eigenvalue problems need a different treatment for analysis compared with the symmetric problems, particularly in the incorporation of the dual problems. This is needed for convergence in norm and error analysis for numerical methods as is shown in
[
4
,
17
,
24
,
23
,
28
]
. Clearly the research on non-symmetric eigenvalue problems is in ongoing progress where the results for different partial differential equations and methods to solve the spectral problems are emerging.

Our contribution is a conforming finite element method to approximate the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Oseen eigenvalue problem. The Oseen problem is clearly different from the Stokes problem due to the presence of the convective term. Hence, the natural question is if the convective term goes to zero on the eigenvalue problem, the spectrum of Oseen approaches to the spectrum of the Stokes eigensystem as it happen in other eigenvalue problems like in
[
11
]
, where the mixed elasticity eigenvalue problem converge to the Stokes problem when the corresponding Lamé constant explodes. In the case of the Oseen eigenvalue problem, we focus when the convective vector goes to zero in norm and the convergence in the limit to the Stokes spectrum. This must be reflected on the computational experiments.

Related to the numerical method, we focus on inf-sup stable conforming finite elements for the Stokes problem. In particular, we consider in our method two families: the mini element and Taylor-Hood finite elements. Both families are suitable choices for the approximation and our intention is to compare their performance on our problem. With these finite elements we derive a priori error estimates which we derive according to the compact operators theory presented in
[
2
,
3
]
.

The outline of our manuscript is as follows: In Section
2
we present the Oseen eigenvalue problem and its variational formulation written in terms of the velocity and pressure. We recall key properties of the problem such as stability and regularity of the eigenfunctions, leading to the introduction of the corresponding solution operator. Since our problem is non-selfadjoint, we also introduce the adjoint problem and its properties. For completeness, we present a result that relates the spectrum of the Oseen eigenvalue problem with the Stokes one. In Section
3
we present the finite element method in which our analysis is supported. This numerical scheme consists in the inf-sup stable families for the mini element and Taylor-Hood elements. We report convergence and a priori error estimates for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. In Section
4
we introduce an a posteriori error estimator for the primal and adjoint eigenvalue problems that result to be reliable and efficient. Finally, in Section
5
we report a series of numerical tests to analyze the performance and accuracy of the methods in two and three dimensions. We also perform several tests in order to analyze the behavior of the a posteriori estimator, where two and three dimensional domains with singularities are considered.

1.1
Notations and preliminaries

Throughout this work, we will use notations that will allow a smoother reading of the content. Let us set these notations. Given
d\in\{2,3\}
, we denote
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
as the space of square real matrices of order
d
, where
\mathbb{I}:=\left(\delta_{ij}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
denotes the identity matrix. Given
\boldsymbol{A}:=\left(A_{ij}\right),\boldsymbol{B}:=\left(B_{ij}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we define

\boldsymbol{A}:\boldsymbol{B}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}A_{ij}\overline{B}_{ij},

as the tensorial product between
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
. The entry
\overline{B}_{ij}
represent the complex conjugate of
B_{ij}
. Similarly, given two vectors
\boldsymbol{s}:=(s_{i}),\boldsymbol{r}:=(r_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we define the products

\boldsymbol{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\overline{r}_{i},\qquad\boldsymbol{s}\otimes\boldsymbol{r}:=\boldsymbol{s}\overline{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\texttt{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{i}\overline{r}_{j},

as the dot and dyadic product in
\mathbb{C}
, respectively, where
(\cdot)^{\texttt{t}}
denotes the transpose operator.

In turn, in what follows we will resort to a standard simplified terminology for Sobolev spaces and norms. Let
\mathcal{O}
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
. For
r\geq 0
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, we denote by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{W}^{r,p}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
the usual Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces of maps from
\mathcal{O}
to
\mathbb{C}
, and endowed with the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{W}^{r,p}(\mathcal{O})}
, respectively, where
\mathrm{W}^{0,p}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
. If
p=2
, we write
\mathrm{H}^{r}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
instead of
\mathrm{W}^{r,2}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
, and denote the corresponding Lebesgue and Sobolev norms by
\|\cdot\|_{0,\mathcal{O}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{r,\mathcal{O}}
, respectively. As usual, we write
|\cdot|_{r,\mathcal{O}}
to denote the seminorm. In particular, for
p=\infty
, we will denote
\|\cdot\|_{\infty,\mathcal{O}}
as the induced norm over the space
\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
. We define
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
as the space of functions in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
with vanishing trace on
\partial\mathcal{O}
, and
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
as the space of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{C})
functions with vanishing mean value over
\mathcal{O}
.

2
The model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\in\{2,3\}
, be an open bounded polygonal/polyhedral domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The problem of interest in this study is based on the Oseen equations, whose main characteristic is to model incompressible viscous fluids at small Reynolds number. The corresponding strong form of the equations are given as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p&=&\lambda\boldsymbol{u},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}&=&\boldsymbol{0},&\text{in}\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{u}
is the displacement,
p
is the pressure and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a given vector field, representing a
steady flow velocity
such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
is divergence free and
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity. Over this last parameter, we assume that there exists two positive numbers
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
such that
\nu^{-}<\nu<\nu^{+}
.

The standard assumptions on the coefficients are the following (see
[
12
]
)

•

\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\sim 1
if
\nu\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}
,

•

\nu\sim 1
if
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}<\nu
,

where the first point is the case more close to the real applications. Now we need some regularity assumptions on the convective coefficient. In two dimensions, le us assume that there exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2+\varepsilon_{1}}(\Omega,,\mathbb{C})^{d}
and in three dimensions
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
. With these regularity assumptions at hand, we have the skew-symmetry property of the convective term (see
[
12
, Remark 5.6]
) which claims that for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
, there holds

\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}.

(2)

Now we introduce a variational formulation for (
1
). To simplify the presentation fo the material, let us define the spaces
\mathcal{X}:=\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\times\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
and its dual space
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
together with the space space
\mathcal{M}:=\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\times\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
and its dual
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. For the space
\mathcal{X}
we define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}:=\|\cdot\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\cdot\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}
whereas for
\mathcal{M}
the norm will be
\|(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}
, for all
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathcal{M}
.

We follow the usual approach as in the Stokes model and we multiply such a system with suitable tests functions, integrate by parts, and use the boundary conditions in order to obtain the following bilinear forms
a:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

and
b:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{and}\quad b(\boldsymbol{v},q):=-\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}.

Observe that the resulting eigenvalue problem will be non-symmetric since the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is precisely a non-symmetric bilinear form and, rigorously speaking, the eigenvalues are complex.
With these sesquilinear forms at hand, we write the following weak formulation for problem (
1
): Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathcal{X}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)&=&\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).\end{array}\right.

(3)

Let us define the kernel
\mathcal{K}
of
b(\cdot,\cdot)
as follows

\mathcal{K}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\,:\,b(\boldsymbol{v},q)=0\,\,\,\,\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})\}.

With this space at hand, is easy to check with the aid of (
2
) that
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is
\mathcal{K}
-coercive. Moreover, the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following inf-sup condition

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\tau},q)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\beta\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).

(4)

Hence, we introduce the so-called solution operator, which we denote by
\boldsymbol{T}
and is defined as follows

\boldsymbol{T}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},

(5)

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathcal{X}
is the solution of the following well posed source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p})&=&(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
b(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}),\end{array}\right.

(6)

implying that
\boldsymbol{T}
is well defined due to the Babuŝka-Brezzi theory. Moreover,
we have the following estimates for the velocity and pressure
[
12
, Lemma 5.8]

\displaystyle\nu\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

(7)

where
C_{p}>0
represents the constant of the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality.
whereas for the pressure we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{p}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left(C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\nu^{1/2}\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\left(\nu^{1/2}+\dfrac{C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu^{1/2}}\right)\right),

where
\beta
is the constant associated with the inf-sup condition (
4
).

We observe that
(\lambda,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{X}
solve (
3
) if and only if
(\kappa,\boldsymbol{u})
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T},i.e.,\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{u}=\kappa\boldsymbol{u}
with
\kappa:=1/\lambda
.

Using the fact that the convective term is well defined and taking advantage of the well known Stokes regularity results (see
[
6
,
25
]
for instance), we have the following additional regularity result for the solution of the source problem (
6
) and consequently, for the generalized eigenfunctions of
\boldsymbol{T}
.

Theorem 1
.

There exists
s>0
that for all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
, the solution
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}},\widehat{p})\in\mathcal{X}
of problem (
6
), satisfies for the velocity
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
, for the pressure
\widehat{p}\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
, and

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widehat{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where
C:=\dfrac{C_{p}}{\beta}\max\left\{1,\dfrac{C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\right\}
.

Hence, because of the compact inclusion
\mathrm{H}^{1+s}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
, we can
conclude that
\boldsymbol{T}
is a compact operator and we obtain the following spectral characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}
holds.

Lemma 2
.

(Spectral Characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}
).
The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}
is such that
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})=\{0\}\cup\{\kappa_{k}\}_{k\in{N}}
where
\{\kappa_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
is a sequence of complex eigenvalues that converge to zero, according to their respective multiplicities.

2.1
Relation between the Oseen and the Stokes eigenvalue problems

From (
1
) we observe that if
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
we obtain the Stokes eigenvalue problem. Hence, a natural question to answer is related to the relation between the spectrums of the Oseen and the Stokes eigenvalue problems. More precisely, the convergence of the solution operator (
5
) and the solution operator associated to the Stokes spectral problem. Let us begin by introducing the Stokes spectral problem: Find
\lambda^{S}\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}^{S},p^{S})\in\mathcal{X}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a^{S}(\boldsymbol{u}^{S},\boldsymbol{v})+b^{S}(\boldsymbol{v},p^{S})&=&\lambda^{S}(\boldsymbol{u}^{S},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
b^{S}(\boldsymbol{u}^{S},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}),\end{array}\right.

(8)

where

a^{S}(\boldsymbol{u}^{S},\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{S}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{and}\quad b^{S}(\boldsymbol{v},p^{S}):=-\int_{\Omega}p^{S}\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}.

Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{T}^{S}
the solution operator associated to (
8
), defined by

\boldsymbol{T}^{S}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}^{S}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S},

(9)

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S},\widehat{p}^{S})\in\mathcal{X}
is the solution of the following well posed source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a^{S}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S},\boldsymbol{v})+b^{S}(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{p}^{S})&=&(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
b^{S}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S},q)&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}),\end{array}\right.

(10)

The following result establish that the operator
\boldsymbol{T}
converge to
\boldsymbol{T}^{S}
as
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}
tends to zero.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{T}
be the operator defined in (
5
) and
\boldsymbol{T}^{S}
the operator defined in (
9
). Then, for any
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
the following estimate holds

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}^{S})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
. Subtracting (
10
) from (
6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S}):\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}(\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}^{S})\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S})q

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).

Testing the above system with
\boldsymbol{v}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S}
and
q=\widehat{p}-\widehat{p}^{S}
, and using the incompressibility conditions that the velocity fields of the Oseen and Stokes source problem satisfy, we obtain

\nu\|\nabla(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S}\|_{0,\Omega}.

(11)

On the other hand, from Poincaré inequality there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
such that
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\|\nabla(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S})\|_{0,\Omega}
. Replacing this in (
11
) we obtain

\|\nabla(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{S})\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the last estimate is consequence of (
7
). This concludes the proof.

We conclude this section by redefining the spectral problem (
3
) in order to simplify the notations for the forthcoming analysis. With this in mind, let us introduce the sesquilinear form
A:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

A((\boldsymbol{u},p);(\boldsymbol{v},q)):=a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)-b(\boldsymbol{u},q),\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X},

which allows us to rewrite problem (
3
) as follows: Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathcal{X}
such that

A((\boldsymbol{u},p);(\boldsymbol{v},q))=\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}.

(12)

Since a part of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is elliptic,
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies an inf-sup condition and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is divergence free, we prove the following stability result holds which will be useful for the a posteriori error analysis.

Lemma 4
.

The sesquilinear form
A(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following inf-sup conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\inf_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r))}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\|}=\gamma,

\displaystyle\inf_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r))}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\|}=\gamma,

where
\gamma
is a positive constant, uniform with respect to
\nu
. Consequently, given
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a pair
(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}
and a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|r\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C,

(13)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\leq A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r)).

Proof.

Let
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}
. With this pair at hand we define

\mathbb{S}:=\sup_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r))}{\|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\|}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is divergence free, we have that
(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}=0
, which together with the Poincaré inequality allows us to have

C_{p}\nu\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\leq a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{v},q))\leq\mathbb{S}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|.

(14)

In turn, we have that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies an inf-sup condition. Hence, there exists
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=-q
together with a constant
C>0
satisfying
\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C\|q\|_{0,\Omega}
. From this we obtain

\displaystyle\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}=-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}=-\int_{\Omega}q\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}-a(\boldsymbol{v},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}})+a(\boldsymbol{v},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=-A((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}},0))+a(\boldsymbol{v},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{S}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\nu\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{S}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\mathbb{S}^{1/2}C_{p}^{-1/2}\left(\nu^{1/2}+\nu^{-1/2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\right)\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|^{1/2}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega},

where we have used the continuity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and (
14
). Exploiting the bound for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{v}}
and using Young’s inequality we obtain

\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq 2C\left[\mathbb{S}^{2}+\mathbb{S}C_{p}(\nu+\nu^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega})^{2}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\right].

(15)

Gathering (
14
) and (
15
) allows to have

\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|^{2}\leq\nu^{-1}C_{p}^{-1}\mathbb{S}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|+2C\left[\mathbb{S}^{2}+\mathbb{S}C_{p}(\nu+\nu^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega})^{2}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\right].

Using now the following version of the Young’s inequality
ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{4}+b^{2}
we obtain

\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|^{2}\leq\nu^{-2}C_{p}^{-2}\mathbb{S}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|^{2}+2C\mathbb{S}^{2}+2C\mathbb{S}^{2}C_{p}^{2}(\nu+\nu^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega})^{4}+\frac{1}{4}\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|^{2}.

The resulting bound is then

\gamma\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\leq\mathbb{S},

with

\gamma:=\frac{1}{2\left\{(\nu)^{-2}C_{p}^{-2}+2C\left[1+C_{p}^{2}(\nu+\nu^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega})^{4}\right]\right\}^{1/2}}.

Finally the bounds (
13
) follows by noting that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega})\leq(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})^{1/2}.

This concludes the proof.

2.2
The adjoint eigenvalue problem

An important aspect of the Ossen eigenvalue problem is the lack of symmetry due to the presence of the convective term. This is an important fact that leads to a solution operator that is non-selfadjoint. Hence, the dual eigenvalue problem must be considered in order to have complete information of the convergence of the finite element approximation of the spectrum.

The strong form of the dual equations are given as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla p&=&\lambda^{*}\boldsymbol{u}^{*},&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}^{*}&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p^{*}&=&0,&\text{in}\,\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}&=&\boldsymbol{0},&\text{in}\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(16)

The dual weak variational formulation from (
16
) reads as follows: Find
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
and the pair
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*})\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\widehat{a}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})-b(p^{*},\boldsymbol{v})&=&\lambda^{*}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
-b(q,\boldsymbol{u}^{*})&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).\end{array}\right.

(17)

where
\widehat{a}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{*}+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta}):\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
. From (
3
), (
17
) and integration by parts we obtain

a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})-\widehat{a}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})=\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{*}=0,

hence, we readily find that
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
.

Now we introduce the adjoint of (
5
) defined by

\boldsymbol{T}^{*}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},

(18)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
is the adjoint velocity of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
and solves the following adjoint source problem: Find

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\widehat{p}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\widehat{a}(\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*})-b(\widehat{p}^{*},\boldsymbol{v})&=&(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{f})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\\
-b(q,\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*})&=&0&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).\end{array}\right.

(19)

Similar to Theorem
1
, we have that the dual source and eigenvalue problems are such that the following estimate holds.

Lemma 5
.

There exist
s^{*}>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
, the solution
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\widehat{p}^{*})
of problem (
19
), satisfies
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\in\mathrm{H}^{1+s^{*}}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
and
\widehat{p}^{*}\in\mathrm{H}^{s^{*}}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
, and

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\|_{1+s^{*},\Omega}+\|\widehat{p}^{*}\|_{s^{*},\Omega}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the constant
C
is the same as in Theorem
1
.

Hence, with this regularity result at hand, the spectral characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
is given as follows.

Lemma 6
.

(Spectral Characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
).
The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
is such that
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}^{*})=\{0\}\cup\{\kappa_{k}^{*}\}_{k\in{N}}
where
\{\kappa_{k}*\}_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
is a sequence of complex eigenvalues that converge to zero, according to their respective multiplicities.

It is easy to prove that if
\kappa
is an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}
with multiplicity
m
,
\bar{\kappa}^{*}
is an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
with the same multiplicity
m
.

Let us define the sesquilinear form
\widehat{A}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

\widehat{A}((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*})):=\widehat{a}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})-b(p^{*},\boldsymbol{v})+b(q,\boldsymbol{u}^{*}),

which allows us to rewrite the dual eigenvalue problem (
17
) as follows: Find
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
and the pair
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*})\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that

\widehat{A}((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*}))=\lambda^{*}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{*})\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}.

The dual counterpart of Lemma
4
is given below, where we have that
\widehat{A}
is stable.

Lemma 7
.

The form
\widehat{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the inf-sup conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\inf_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\frac{\widehat{A}((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r))}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\|}=\gamma^{*},

\displaystyle\inf_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\sup_{(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\frac{\widehat{A}((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r))}{\|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\|\|(\boldsymbol{w},r)\|}=\gamma^{*}.

where
\gamma^{*}
is a positive constant, uniform with respect to
\nu
. Consequently, given
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, there exists
(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|r\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\widehat{A}((\boldsymbol{v},q);(\boldsymbol{w},r)).

In the forthcoming we will introduce the finite element discretization of (
3
) and (
19
), and hence, of
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
.

3
The finite element method

In this section our aim is to describe a finite element discretization of problem (
3
). To do this task, we will introduce two families of inf-sup stable finite elements for the Oseen load problem whose properties also hold for the eigenvalue problem. We begin by introducing some preliminary definitions and notations to perform the analysis.

3.1
Inf–sup stable finite element spaces

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{T\}
be a conforming partition of
\overline{\Omega}
into closed simplices
T
with size
h_{T}=\text{diam}(T)
. Define
h:=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}
. Given a mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}\in\mathbb{T}
, we denote by
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}
the finite element spaces that approximate the velocity field and the pressure, respectively. In particular, our study is focused in the following two elections:

(a)

The mini element
[
5
, Section 4.2.4]
: Here,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\oplus\mathbb{B}(T)]^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}),

where
\mathbb{B}(T)
denotes the space spanned by local bubble functions.

(b)

The lowest order Taylor–Hood element
[
5
, Section 4.2.5]
: In this case,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{2}(T)]^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}\}\cap\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C}).

The discrete analysis will be performed in a general manner, where the both families of finite elements, namely Taylor-Hood and mini element, are considered with no difference. If some difference must be claimed, we will point it out when is necessary. To make more simple the presentation of the material, let us define the space
\mathcal{X}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{P}_{h}
.

3.2
The discrete eigenvalue problem

With our finite element spaces at hand, we are in position to introduce the FEM discretization of problem (
3
) which reads as follows: Find
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})&=&\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})&=&0&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

(20)

We introduce the discrete solution operator
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
defined as follows

\boldsymbol{T}_{h}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widehat{p}_{h})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
is the solution of the following well posed source discrete problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})&=&(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})&=&0&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

(21)

The choices of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}
make that (
21
) is well posed since the following discrete inf-sup condition holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\bar{\beta}\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.

(22)

implying that
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
is well defined due to the Babuŝka-Brezzi theory. Moreover,
we have the following estimates for the velocity and pressure
[
12
, Lemma 5.13]

\displaystyle\nu\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

whereas for the pressure we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{p}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\left(C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}+\nu^{1/2}\|\nabla\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\left(\nu^{1/2}+\dfrac{C_{p}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}}{\nu^{1/2}}\right)\right),

where
\bar{\beta}>0
is the constant given by the discrete inf-sup condition (
22
).

As in the continuous case,
(\lambda_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))
solves Problem (
20
) if and only if
(\kappa_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h})
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
.
The discrete counterpart of (
12
) is defined from (
20
) as: Find
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{C}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p)\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
such that

A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{v},q))=\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}_{h},

(23)

where
A((\cdot,\cdot);(\cdot,\cdot))
is the same as (
12
).

The dual discrete eigenvalue problem reads as follows: Find
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
and the pair
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p_{h}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})-b(p_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=&\lambda_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
-b(q_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})&=&0&\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{h},\end{array}\right.

(24)

and let us introduce the discrete version of (
18
) which we define by

\boldsymbol{T}^{*}_{h}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{h},\qquad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*},

where the pair
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*},\widehat{p}_{h}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
is the solution of the following adjoint discrete source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*})-b(\widehat{p}_{h}^{*},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&=&(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
-b(q_{h},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*})&=&0&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h},\end{array}\right.

(25)

Now, due to the compactness of
\boldsymbol{T}
, we are able to prove that
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
converge to
\boldsymbol{T}
as
h
goes to zero in norm. This is contained in the following result.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
be such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}:=\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}
. Then, there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{s}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}:=\max\left\{1+\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\min\left\{\dfrac{C_{p}}{\nu},\dfrac{1}{\nu^{1/2}}\right\},\dfrac{1}{\nu}\right\}
.
Moreover, we have the improved estimate

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{s+1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the constant
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}
is the same defined above.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
be such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}.=\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}
. Then, the following Céa estimate holds

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1,\Omega}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}.

Now, the proof of the lemma follows from Theorem 5.14, Theorem 5.15, Theorem C.13, Corollary 5.16 of
[
12
]
and Theorem
1
. Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is divergence free, the improved estimate follows similar to the
\mathrm{H}^{1}
estimate, but with the aid of the additional regularity from Theorem
1
together with
[
12
, Theorem 6.32, Theorem 6.34, Corollary 6.33]
.

Also for the adjoint problem, we have the following convergence result. Since the proof is essentially identical to Lemma
8
we skip the steps of the proof.

Lemma 9
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\|(\boldsymbol{T}^{*}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{s*}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Moreover, we have the improved estimate

\|(\boldsymbol{T}^{*}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{s^{*}+1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where the constant
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}
is the same as in Lemma
8
.

The key consequence of the previous results is that we are in position to apply the well established theory of
[
13
]
to conclude that our numerical methods does not introduce spurious eigenvalues. This is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set containing
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})
. Then, there exists
h_{0}>0
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\subset V
for all
h<h_{0}
.

3.3
Error estimates

The goal of this section is deriving error estimates for the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues. We first recall the definition of spectral projectors. Let
\mu
be a nonzero isolated eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}
with algebraic multiplicity
m
and let
\Gamma

be a disk of the complex plane centered in
\mu
, such that
\mu
is the only eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{T}
lying in
\Gamma
and
\partial\Gamma\cap\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})=\emptyset
. With these considerations at hand, we define the spectral projections of
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{*}
, associated to
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
, respectively, as follows:

1.

The spectral projector of
\boldsymbol{T}
associated to
\mu
is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T})^{-1}\,dz;

2.

The spectral projector of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
associated to
\bar{\mu}
is
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{*}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}^{*})^{-1}\,dz,

where
I
represents the identity operator. Let us remark that
E
and
E^{*}
are the projections onto the generalized eigenvector
R(\boldsymbol{E})
and
R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})
, respectively.
A consequence of Lemma
8
is that there exist
m
eigenvalues, which lie in
\Gamma
, namely
\mu_{h}^{(1)},\ldots,\mu_{h}^{(m)}
, repeated according their respective multiplicities, that converge to
\mu
as
h
goes to zero. With this result at hand, we introduce the following spectral projection

\boldsymbol{E}_{h}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Gamma}(z\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})^{-1}\,dz,

which is a projection onto the discrete invariant subspace
R(\boldsymbol{E}_{h})
of
\boldsymbol{T}
, spanned by the generalized eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
corresponding to

\mu_{h}^{(1)},\ldots,\mu_{h}^{(m)}
.
Now we recall the definition of the
gap

\widehat{\delta}
between two closed
subspaces
\mathfrak{X}
and
\mathfrak{Y}
of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
:

\widehat{\delta}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}):=\max\big\{\delta(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}),\delta(\mathfrak{Y},\mathfrak{X})\big\},\text{ where }\delta(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y}):=\sup_{\underset{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}\right\|_{0,\Omega}=1}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathfrak{X}}}\left(\inf_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathfrak{Y}}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\|_{0,\Omega}\right).

We end this section proving error estimates for the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues.

Theorem 11
.

The following estimates hold

\widehat{\delta}(R(\boldsymbol{E}),R(\boldsymbol{E}_{h}))\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h\quad\text{and}\quad|\mu-\mu_{h}|\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}\widehat{C}^{s+s^{*}},

where the constant
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}
is the same as in Lemma
8
and

\widehat{C}:=\max\left\{2C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}},\dfrac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\max\left\{\widetilde{C}^{2},1+\dfrac{1}{\bar{\beta}}+\dfrac{1}{\beta_{h}\nu^{1/2}}\right\}\right\},

with
\widetilde{C}:=\left(1+\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\min\left\{\dfrac{C_{p}}{\nu},\dfrac{1}{\nu^{1/2}}\right\}\right)
and
\bar{\beta}
is the constant given by the discrete inf-sup condition (
22
).

Proof.

The proof of the gap between the eigenspaces is a direct consequence of the convergence in norm between
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
as
h
goes to zero.
We focus on the double order of convergence for the eigenvalues. Let
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be such that
\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=\mu\boldsymbol{u}_{k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,m
. A dual basis for
R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})
is
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{m}
. This basis satisfies
A((\boldsymbol{u}_{k},p);(\boldsymbol{u}_{l}^{*},p^{*}))=\delta_{k.l},

where
\delta_{k.l}
represents the Kronecker delta.
On the other hand, the following identity holds

|\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{h}|\lesssim\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|\langle(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\rangle|+\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})|_{R(\boldsymbol{E})}\|\|(\boldsymbol{T}^{*}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*})|_{R(\boldsymbol{E}^{*})}\|.

For the first term on the right-hand side we note that

\langle(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}\rangle=A((\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},p-p_{h});(\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*},p^{*}))\\
=A((\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k},p-p_{h});(\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*},p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}))\\
\leq\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}\|_{1,\Omega}\\
+\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\|_{1,\Omega}\|p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Then, Theorem
11
follows from the above estimates, the approximation properties of discrete spaces, in addition to Lemmas
8
and
9
.

3.4
Reduced solution operators

With the above result at hand, we are allowed to define the following operators:
for any
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}
we introduce the linear and compact operator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}
defined by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\,\,\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\mapsto\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},

where the pair
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})
is the solution of (
6
).
Also, we introduce
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}
as the discrete linear counterpart of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}
, defined by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{H}_{h},\,\,\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\mapsto\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},

where the pair
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widetilde{p}_{h})
is the solution of (
21
). On the other hand, we introduce the respective adjoint reduced operators defined by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}^{*}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\,\,\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\mapsto\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},

for the continuous counterpart where the pair
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\widetilde{p}^{*})
solves problem (
19
), whereas the discrete version of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}^{*}

is defined by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}^{*}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})^{d},\,\,\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\mapsto\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}^{*}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*}
, and the pair
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*},\widetilde{p}_{h}^{*})
is the solution of (
25
).
As a consequence of Lemmas
8
and

, the following is ensured convergence in operators’ norm, i.e:

\begin{split}\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h})\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}&\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega},\\
\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}^{*}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h})^{*}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}&\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s*}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}.\end{split}

(26)

Hence we guarantee a spectral convergence result for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}
as
h\rightarrow 0
. Now we are in a position to guarantee the following estimate.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
be an eigenfunction of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
associated with the eigenvalue
\mu_{k,h}
, with
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}
=1. Then, there exists an eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{u}
of
\boldsymbol{T}
associated with
\mu
such that, for all
s>0
, there exists
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}
as in Lemma
8
such that

\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s}.

Proof.

Thanks to (
26
),
[
2
, Theorem 7.1]
yields spectral convergence of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}
to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}
as
h\rightarrow 0
. Specifically, due to the connection between the eigenfunctions of
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
and the eigenfunctions of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h}
, respectively, we have that
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{E}_{h}
, and there exits
\boldsymbol{u}\in\boldsymbol{E}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C\sup_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{E}}:\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}=1}\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h})\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}.

Conversely, thanks to Lemma
8
, for any function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{E}}
, if
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{E}
is such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}=\boldsymbol{f}
, then

\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{h})\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{0,\Omega}=\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}.

which together with the above estimate allow us to conclude the proof.

Furthermore, in the context of the adjoint problem, we have the following convergence result.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}
be an eigenfunction of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}^{*}
associated with the eigenvalue
\mu_{k,h}^{*}
, with
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}
=1. Then, there exists an eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
of
\boldsymbol{T}^{*}
associated with
\mu^{*}
such that, for all
s^{*}>0
, there exists
C>0
such that

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s^{*}}.

Remark 14
.

We conclude this section by summarizing the a priori error estimates obtained. If
(\lambda_{k},\boldsymbol{u}_{k})
is a solution of (
17
),
then there exists
(\lambda_{k,h},\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h})
satisfying (
20
) such that :

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}h^{s},

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}h^{1+s},

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}h^{s^{*}},

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{k,h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}}h^{1+s^{*}},

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{k,h}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta},C_{p}\widehat{C}}h^{s+{s^{*}}},

where

,

are as in Theorem

4
A posteriori analysis

The aim of this section is to introduce a suitable
residual-based error estimator for the Oseen
eigenvalue problem which is fully computable,
in the sense that it depends only on quantities available from the FEM solution. Then, we will show its equivalence with the error.
Moreover, on the forthcoming analysis we will focus only on eigenvalues with simple multiplicity. With this purpose, we introduce the following definitions and notations. For any element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{T}
the set of faces/edges of
T

and

\mathcal{E}_{h}:=\bigcup_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\mathcal{E}_{T}.

We decompose
\mathcal{E}_{h}=\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}
,
where
\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}:=\{\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{h}:\ell\subset\partial\Omega\}

and
\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}:=\mathcal{E}\backslash\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}
.
For each inner face/edge
\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}
and for any sufficiently smooth function

\boldsymbol{v}
, we define the jump of its normal derivative on
\ell
by

\left[\!\!\left[\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{n}}}\right]\!\!\right]_{\ell}:=\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}|_{T})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{T}+\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}|_{T^{\prime}})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{\prime}},

where
T
and
T^{\prime}
are the two elements in
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
sharing the
face/edge
\ell
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{\prime}}
are the respective outer unit normal vectors.

4.1
Local and global indicators

In what follows we introduce a suitable residual-based error estimator for the Oseen eigenvalue problem. To do this task, for an element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
we introduce the following local error indicator:

\eta_{T}^{2}:=h_{T}^{2}\|\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}\\
+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}+\frac{h_{e}}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\llbracket(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\|_{0,e}^{2},

and the global estimator for the primal problem

\eta:=\left(\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\eta_{T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

On the other hand, we define the local indicator for the dual problem as follows

\eta_{T}^{*,2}:=h_{T}^{2}\|\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{h}+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})+\nabla p_{h}^{*}\|_{0,T}^{2}\\
+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,T}^{2}+\frac{h_{e}}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\llbracket(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}+p_{h}^{*}\mathbb{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\|_{0,e}^{2},

and the global estimator for the dual problem is

\eta^{*}:=\left(\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\eta_{T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

It is important to remark that it is possible to define a global estimator as the sum of the contributions given by the primal and dual problems as follows

\theta:=\left(\eta^{2}+(\eta^{*})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Now the aim is to prove that the proposed estimator is reliable and efficient. In the following, for the sake of simplicity, sometimes we will denote the errors between the continuous and discrete eigenfunctions as

\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\quad\texttt{e}_{p}:=p-p_{h},\quad\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}:=\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},\quad\texttt{e}_{p}^{*}:=p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}.

4.2
Reliability

Let us begin with the reliability analysis of the proposed estimators. The task here is to prove that the error is upper bounded by the estimator, together with
the high order terms. This is stated in the following result.

Theorem 15
(Reliability).

The following statements hold

1.

Let
(\lambda,(\boldsymbol{u},p))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{X}
be a solution of the spectral problem (
3
) and let
(\lambda_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{X}_{h}
be the finite element approximation of
(\lambda,(\boldsymbol{u},p))
given as the solution of (
20
). Then, for every
h_{0}\geq h
there holds

\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C(\eta+|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}).

(27)

2.

Let
(\lambda^{*},(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*}))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
be a solution of the spectral problem problem (
17
) and let
(\lambda_{h}^{*},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p_{h}^{*}))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{X}_{h}
be the finite element approximation of
(\lambda^{*},(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},p^{*}))
given as the solution of (
24
). Then, for every
h_{0}\geq h
there holds

\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C(\eta^{*}+|\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{h}^{*}|+\lambda^{*}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}),

(28)

where in each estimate the constant
C>0
depends on
\nu
, but is independent of the mesh size and the discrete solutions.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the reliability for the primal formulation. The dual reliability follows by similar arguments. Given
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
, we subtract the continuous formulation (cf. (
12
) ) and its discrete counterpart (cf. (
23
)) in order to obtain the error equation

A((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p});(\boldsymbol{v},q))=(\lambda\boldsymbol{u}-\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}\quad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\mathcal{X}_{h}.

(29)

Thanks to Lemma
4
, we have that for
(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists
(\boldsymbol{w},r)\in\mathcal{X}
, with

\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|r\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C,

(30)

such that

\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq A((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p});(\boldsymbol{w},r)).

(31)

Let us define
\boldsymbol{w}_{I}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
as the Clément interpolant (see
[
14
, Chapter 2]
) and
r_{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}
as the usual
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection of
r
. Then, from (
29
) and (
31
) we obtain

\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}-A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w},r))\\
=(\lambda\boldsymbol{u}-\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}+\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I})_{0,\Omega}-A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I},r-r_{I}))\\
=\underbrace{-A((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h});(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I},r-r_{I}))+(\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I})_{0,\Omega}}_{\Lambda_{1}}\\
+\underbrace{(\lambda\boldsymbol{u}-\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{w})_{0,\Omega}}_{\Lambda_{2}}.

(32)

The task is to control
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
. For
\Lambda_{1}
, we use integration by parts to obtain

\Lambda_{1}=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\left\{\int_{T}\left(\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nabla p_{h}+\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I})\right.\\
\left.+\int_{T}(r-r_{I})\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\int_{e}\llbracket\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\cdot(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{I}).

(33)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
33
), followed by Clément interpolation properties,
\mathrm{L}^{2}
projection properties, (
30
) and the estimator definition, we obtain

\Lambda_{1}\leq C\eta.

For
\Lambda_{2}
we follow
[
21
, Theorem 3.1]
and obtain the estimate

\Lambda_{2}\leq|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

The proof of (
27
) is completed by adding the estimates
\Lambda_{1}
and

\Lambda_{2}
, together with (
32
). The proof of (
28
) is obtained in a similar way.

Now we are in position to establish the estimator reliability for the eigenvalues, whose proof follows immediately by squaring the estimates from Theorem
15
, Remark
14
and
[
7
, Remark 2.1]
we have the following eigenvalue estimate.

Proposition 16
.

The eigenvalue approximation is such that the estimate

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{h}| \leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\theta^{2}+|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|^{2}+\lambda^{2}(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\right),

holds, where the constant
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0
is independent of the meshsize and the discrete solutions.

Proof.

First we note that

\displaystyle(\lambda_{h}-\lambda)(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})_{0,\Omega}=A((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h});(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}))

\displaystyle-\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})_{0,\Omega}.

Observe that the term
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})_{0,\Omega}
is needed to be lower bounded (see for instant
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
). Then, there exists
C>0
such that
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})_{0,\Omega}>C
. Then, taking modulus and applying triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|\leq|A((\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p-p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}))|+|\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*})_{0,\Omega}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|p^{*}-p_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\lambda(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\eta^{2}+(\eta^{*})^{2}+|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|^{2}+\lambda^{2}(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2})\right).

This concludes the proof.

It is important to note that, thanks to Remark
14
, the errors
\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}
(resp.
\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}
),
|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|^{2}
and
\lambda^{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}
(resp.
\lambda^{2}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}
) are high-order terms in the estimations of the above results.

4.3
Efficiency

We begin by introducing the bubble functions for two dimensional elements. Given
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
e\in\mathcal{E}(T)
, we let
\psi_{T}
and
\psi_{e}
be the usual triangle-bubble and edge-bubble functions, respectively. See
[
26
]
for further details and properties about these functions.

4.4
Upper bound

In order to simplify the presentation of the material, let us define
\textbf{R}_{1,T}:=\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nabla p_{h}+\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
. Also, we define
\boldsymbol{v}_{T}:=\psi_{T}\textbf{R}_{1,T}
, where
\psi_{T}
is the bubble that satisfies the classical properties of bubbles. Now we compute a bound for the term
\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}
. To do this task, let us recall that
the continuous problem satisfies
-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p-\lambda\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Hence

\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}^{2}\leq\int_{T}(\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nabla p_{h}+\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
=\int_{T}(-\nu\Delta\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla\texttt{e}_{p}+\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\lambda\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
=\underbrace{\int_{T}(-\nu\Delta\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla\texttt{e}_{p})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}}_{\textrm{T}_{1}}+\underbrace{\int_{T}(\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\lambda\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{T}}_{\textrm{T}_{2}}.

Now our task is to estimate the terms
\textrm{T}_{1}
and
\textrm{T}_{2}
. Hence we have

\textrm{T}_{1}=\nu\int_{T}\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{T}+\int_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}_{T}-\int_{T}\texttt{e}_{p}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\\
\leq\max\{\nu,\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,T},1\}(\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,T})\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{T}\|_{0,T}\\
\leq\max\{\nu,\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty,T},1\}(\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,T})h_{T}^{-1}\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}.

Now for
\textrm{T}_{2}
is enough to follow the arguments on
[
21
, Theorem 3.2]
to obtain

\textrm{T}_{2}\lesssim h_{T}^{2}(|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\lambda\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T})\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}.

Then

h_{T}\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\|_{0,T}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,T}\right.\\
\left.+h_{T}(|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T})\right).

(34)

The control of the term
\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}
is direct:

\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}=\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T},

where we have used the incompressibility condition
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}=0
in
\Omega
.

The following step is to estimate the boundary term of the estimator
\eta
. Given
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
, let us define
\textbf{R}_{e}:=\llbracket\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket
. Using the extension operator
L
defined by
L:\mathcal{C}(e)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(T)
with
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}
being the spaces of continuous functions defined on
e
and
T
, respectively, and using the properties of
\psi_{e}
the edge-bubble function, we have that

||\textbf{R}_{e}||_{0,e}^{2}=\int_{e}\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e})\cdot\llbracket\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\\
=\sum_{T\in\omega_{e}}\int_{\partial e}\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e})\cdot\llbracket\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket,

where
\omega_{e}:=\{T^{\prime}\in CT_{h}:e\in\mathcal{E}_{T^{\prime}}\}
.
Now, using
\llbracket\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbb{I}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket=0
, and integrating by parts, we get

||\textbf{R}_{e}||_{0,e}^{2}=\sum_{T\in\omega_{e}}\left(\int_{T}\nu\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}:\nabla(\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e}))+\int_{T}\texttt{e}_{p}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e}))\right.\\
\left.+\int_{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e})+\int_{T}(\lambda\boldsymbol{u}-\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h})\cdot\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e})+\int_{T}\textbf{R}_{1,T}\cdot\psi_{e}L(\textbf{R}_{e})\right)\\
\leq\sum_{T\in\omega_{e}}C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,T}+h_{T}\|\textbf{R}_{1,T}\|_{0,T}+h_{T}(|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|\right.\\
\left.+\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}\right)h_{e}^{-1/2}||\textbf{R}_{e}||_{0,e},

where we used that
h_{e}\leq h_{T}
. Combining the above result with (
34
) we have that

h_{e}^{1/2}||\textbf{R}_{e}||_{0,e}\leq\sum_{T\in\omega_{e}}C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,T}+h_{T}(|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}\right).

In summary we have proof that

\eta_{T}^{2}\leq\sum_{T\in\omega_{e}}C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}\left(\|\nabla\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}^{2}+\|\texttt{e}_{p}\|_{0,T}^{2}+h_{T}^{2}(|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|^{2}+\|\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{0,T}^{2}\right).

Now, we are in a position to establish the efficiency
\eta
, which is stated in the following result.

Lemma 17
.

(Efficiency) The following estimate holds

\eta\leq C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+h.o.t),

where the constant
C_{\nu,\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0
is independent of meshsize,
\lambda
, and the discrete solution.

Finally, the efficiency for the
\eta^{*}
estimator is analogous to that shown for
\eta
, so the proof is omitted.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section we carry out several numerical experiments to visualize the robustness and performance of the proposed schemes. The discrete eigenvalue problem have been implemented using FEniCS
[
20
]
. After computing the eigenvalues, the rates of convergence are calculated by using a least-square fitting. In this sense, if
\lambda_{h}
is a discrete complex eigenvalue, then the rate of convergence
\alpha
is calculated by extrapolation and the least square fitting

\lambda_{h}\approx\lambda_{\text{extr}}+Ch^{\alpha},

where
\lambda_{\text{extr}}
is the extrapolated eigenvalue given by the fitting. For convenience in handling the two- and three-dimensional plots, the representation of eigenfunctions is done using Taylor-Hood elements.

In what follows, we denote the mesh resolution by
N
, which is connected to the mesh-size
h
trough the relation
h\sim N^{-1}
. We also denote the number of degrees of freedom by
dof
, namely
\texttt{dof}=\dim(\boldsymbol{V}_{h})+\dim(\mathcal{P}_{h})
. The relation between
dof
and the mesh size is given by
h\sim\texttt{dof}^{-1/n}
, with
n\in\{2,3\}
.

Let us define
\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})
as the error on the
i
-th eigenvalue, with

\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i}):=\frac{|\lambda_{i,h}-\lambda_{i}|}{|\lambda_{i}|},

where
\lambda_{i}
is the extrapolated value. Similarly, the effectivity indexes with respect to
\eta
,
\eta_{*}
and
\theta
and the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i,h}
is defined, respectively, by

\texttt{eff}(\lambda_{i}):=\frac{\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})}{\eta^{2}},\qquad\texttt{eff}_{*}(\lambda_{i}):=\frac{\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})}{(\eta^{*})^{2}},\qquad\texttt{eff}_{\theta}(\lambda_{i}):=\frac{\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})}{\theta^{2}}.

In order to apply the adaptive finite element method, we shall generate a sequence of nested conforming triangulations using the loop

solve
\rightarrow
estimate
\rightarrow
mark
\rightarrow
refine,

based on
[
27
]
:

1.

Set an initial mesh
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
.

2.

Solve (
20
) (resp. (
24
)) in the actual mesh to obtain
\lambda_{h}
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})
(resp.
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p_{h}^{*})
).

3.

Compute
\eta_{T}
(resp.
\eta_{T}^{*}
) for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
using the eigenfunctions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})
(resp.
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*},p_{h}^{*})
).

4.

Use blue-green marking strategy to refine each
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
whose indicator
\zeta_{T^{\prime}}
satisfies

\zeta_{T^{\prime}}\geq 0.5\max\{\zeta_{T}\,:\,T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

where
\zeta_{T}\in\{\eta_{T},\eta_{T}^{*},\theta_{T}\}
.

5.

Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
as the actual mesh and go to step 2.

The refinement algorithm is the one implemented by FEniCS through the command
refine
, which implements Plaza and Carey’s algorithms for 2D and 3D geometries. The algorithms use local refinement of simplicial grids based on the skeleton.

For the study of the estimators, we comprise each contribution of the global residual terms as

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}:=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{T}^{2}\|\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{h})-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}:=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}:=\frac{h_{e}}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\llbracket(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\|_{0,e}^{2}.

for the case of
\eta
, while each contribution of
\eta^{*}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{*}:=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{T}^{2}\|\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{h}+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\otimes\boldsymbol{\beta})+\nabla p_{h}^{*}\|_{0,T}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{*}:=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}\|_{0,T}^{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{*}:=\frac{h_{e}}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\llbracket(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}+p_{h}^{*}\mathbb{I})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\|_{0,e}^{2}.
