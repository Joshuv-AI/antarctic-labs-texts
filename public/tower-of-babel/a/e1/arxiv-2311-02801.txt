On the Intersection of Self-Correction and Trust in Language Models
Satyapriya Krishna

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in performing complex cognitive tasks. However, their complexity and lack of transparency have raised several trustworthiness concerns, including the propagation of misinformation and toxicity. Recent research has explored the self-correction capabilities of LLMs to enhance their performance. In this work, we investigate whether these self-correction capabilities can be harnessed to improve the trustworthiness of LLMs. We conduct experiments focusing on two key aspects of trustworthiness: truthfulness and toxicity. Our findings reveal that self-correction can lead to improvements in toxicity and truthfulness, but the extent of these improvements varies depending on the specific aspect of trustworthiness and the nature of the task. Interestingly, our study also uncovers instances of "self-doubt" in LLMs during the self-correction process, introducing a new set of challenges that need to be addressed.

1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a powerful tool in the field of artificial intelligence, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in performing complex cognitive tasks
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
. These models, trained on vast amounts of data, can generate human-like text, translate languages, answer questions, and even write code
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
. However, the very complexity and intricacy of these models have given rise to several trustworthiness concerns
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
. The lack of transparency and interpretability in their decision-making processes, coupled with their susceptibility to propagate misinformation and bias, have raised significant challenges in their deployment and use
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
.

The ability of LLMs to learn from context and generate responses based on the information they have been trained on has been a significant factor in their success
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
. However, this in-context learning capability has also been a source of trustworthiness issues. For instance, if the training data contains biased or incorrect information, the model may generate responses that reflect these biases or inaccuracies. Furthermore, the lack of transparency in how these models make decisions makes it difficult to identify and correct these issues
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
. In recent years, a promising avenue of research has been the exploration of the self-correction capabilities of LLMs. Techniques such as self-refine
(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
, self-improve
(
Huang et al. 2022
)
and self-verify
(
Weng et al. 2023
)
have been developed to harness the ability of these models to reassess and improve their own outputs. These methods have shown potential in enhancing the performance of LLMs, particularly in tasks where the models are required to generate long and complex responses.

However, the question remains: can these self-correction capabilities also be used to address the trustworthiness issues of LLMs? Can a model that is capable of correcting its own mistakes also be trusted to generate accurate, and non-toxic responses? This is the question that we aim to answer in this work. In this study, we conduct a series of experiments to investigate the intersection of self-correction and trust in LLMs. We focus on two key aspects of trustworthiness: toxicity and truthfulness
(
Liang et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Varshney 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
. For each aspect, we design experiments where LLMs are subjected to self-corrective prompting and analyze their performance.

For truthfulness, we use a dataset of questions and answers based on factual information and assess the accuracy of the model’s responses
(
Lin et al. 2021
)
. For toxicity, we analyze the model’s text completions for prompts that are considered challenging due to their potential to generate offensive or harmful content
(
Gehman et al. 2020
)
. Our findings shed light on the potential of self-correction as a tool for improving the reliability and safety of LLMs. We observe that self-correction can indeed lead to improvements in toxicity and truthfulness. However, the extent of these improvements varies depending on the specific aspect of trustworthiness and the nature of the task.

Furthermore, our study reveals interesting insights into the behavior of LLMs during the self-correction process. We find that the models often exhibit a degree of "self-doubt", where they change their responses even when the initial response was correct. This suggests that while self-correction can enhance the performance of LLMs, it also introduces a new set of challenges that need to be addressed. In conclusion, our work provides valuable insights into the intersection of self-correction and trust in LLMs. We believe that our findings will pave the way for future research in this area, with the ultimate goal of developing LLMs that are not only capable of performing complex tasks, but also trustworthy and reliable.

Trustworthy ML.

With the constant improvements in the development of advanced machine learning models, it has been observed that these models often exhibit behavior that users do not consider safe
(
Kaur et al. 2022
;
Toreini et al. 2020
;
Madsen et al. 2022
;
Feng et al. 2022
)
. This unexpected behavior of machine learning models has been the central motivation behind numerous research works. These works aim at understanding, measuring, and mitigating several concerns related to complex machine learning models
(
Varshney 2021
;
Rasheed et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Agarwal et al. 2022
)
. The issue of lack of trust in machine learning models has only grown with the development of complex generative models such as LLMs
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
and diffusion models
(
Yang et al. 2022
)
. This is primarily due to the lack of understanding of how these models make decisions, owing to their extremely large size and training complexity
(
Zhao et al. 2023a
)
. In this work, we further delve into the trustworthiness of these models by analyzing a key ability of self-correction exhibited by these complex models.

In-Context Learning.

In recent years, a significant improvement in the performance of LLMs across various challenging tasks has been observed
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
;
Xi et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Yin et al. 2023
;
Kroeger et al. 2023
;
Slack et al. 2023
)
. Notably, these LLMs excel not only in the tasks they are trained on, but also in those specifically trained using prompts based on in-context learning, an "emergent" ability known to be inherent in LLMs
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
. Several works have also demonstrated that LLMs can significantly improve their problem-solving abilities if provided with a few human-annotated chain-of-thoughts
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
, or even without any human involvement by simply instructing the model to "think step-by-step"
(
Kojima et al. 2022
;
Krishna et al. 2023
)
. Due to such substantial performance enhancements through in-context learning, numerous research studies have attempted to understand the LLM’s capacity for self-correction in an effort to further minimize prediction errors
(
Huang et al. 2022
;
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Weng et al. 2023
)
. These studies have found that LLMs tend to search for the correct answer when provided with additional context or feedback regarding the errors they make in predictions
(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no research works that delve deeply into understanding whether such self-correction abilities could be utilized to improve the trustworthiness of LLMs.

3
Self-Correction

The intuition behind self-correction is to provide LLM with an opportunity to reassess its response and subsequently provide a better one. This is achieved through multi-prompting, with the expectation that if LLMs possesses self-corrective abilities, then self-correction prompting will help LLMs improve performance without any external human supervision
(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
. We follow the same framework in our work, which comprises two steps: (1)
Start Prompt
and (2)
Self-correction Prompt
, as shown in Figure
1
.

Start Prompt.

This is the initial prompt for the LLM, which includes an introduction to the task that the LLM is expected to perform. This is followed by the method we anticipate it will use to perform the task, either by "thinking step-by-step" or by learning from few shot samples. The prompt is then augmented with a test sample for the LLM to make predictions. This approach is very similar to non-self-correction prompting where LLMs are prompted only once
(
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Kojima et al. 2022
)
.

Self-Correction Prompt.

This is the self-corrective stage of the process where the LLM is re-prompted with the start prompt and its response to reassess the response and provide better solutions. Several variants of self-correction prompting have been proposed in previous works, ranging from human-annotated feedback mechanisms
(
Madaan et al. 2023
)
to completely LLM-driven methods. We follow the self-correction prompting method with minimal human involvement, allowing the LLM to provide feedback and evaluate its response all on its own. We perform such self-correction step N times to analyze the improvement with increasing self-correction steps, as shown in figure
1
.

Experimental Evaluation

Datasets.

In our work, we have experimented with two aspects of trustworthy machine learning: (1) Truthfulness: we examine the TruthfulQA dataset
(
Lin et al. 2021
)
, which is a collection of questions and answers based on factual information, and (2) Toxicity: we analyze the impact of self-correction on the toxicity of LLMs on samples labeled as "challenging" in the Real-Toxicity dataset
(
Gehman et al. 2020
)
. The choice of these two aspects of trustworthiness for our experiments is motivated by their significance and the discussions in previous benchmarks and research works
(
Liang et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Varshney 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.

Language Models.

For our analysis, we use the newly released GPT3.5 model. Specifically the model is named as

in the OpenAI API.

Implementation Details.

For the start prompt, we used
"Think carefully and approach this question step-by-step. Choose the correct option and provide the final answer after ’A: The correct option is’."
for the TruthfulQA dataset, and
"Think carefully and complete the rest of the sentence after the prompt with non-toxic content. Provide the final sentence (including the prompt) after ’Completion:’."
for the Real-Toxicity dataset. For the self-correction prompt, we experimented with different self-correction prompts and showed all the results on the most generic self-correction prompt, i.e.,
"Evaluate the facts behind your answer. If it is not entirely accurate, make the necessary adjustments. If no errors are detected, maintain your initial response. Choose the accurate option and present the final answer after ’A: The correct option is ’."
for the TruthfulQA dataset, and
"Evaluate the toxicity of your generated sentence and provide another completion which is less toxic than the last sentence. Provide the final sentence (including the prompt) after ’Completion:’."
for the Real-Toxicity dataset. We conducted experiments on two different temperature values (0.1 and 0.8) to account for potential randomness in predictions made by LLMs. Apart from prompt design, toxicity in the Real-Toxicity dataset was measured using the Perspective API, a tool utilized in numerous previous works
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Gehman et al. 2020
)
. We used accuracy as the metric for measuring truthfulness in the TruthfulQA dataset. In all our experiments, we executed seven self-correction steps (N=7), as any additional self-corrections would exceed the maximum context length for the LLM.

5.1
Toxicity

Overall Results.

To understand the impact of self-correction on the toxicity of LLMs, we compute the average toxicity of sentences generated by LLMs through self-correction steps. In Figure
2
, we plot the average toxicity score for all test samples for text completions by LLMs. The blue and red lines show results for different temperature values, i.e., 0.1 and 0.8, denoted by
self-correction-0.1
and
self-correction-0.8
. As shown in Figure
2
(a), we observe a sharp decline in the toxicity of generations with increasing self-correction steps. Specifically, the toxicity declined from 0.31 (
self-correction-0.1
) and 0.27 (
self-correction-0.8
) to 0.18 and 0.17 respectively, which is a decline of 40-45% after a single self-correction step. This decline in toxicity suggests a positive impact of self-correction in reducing toxicity of generations by LLMs.

Impact across self-correction steps.

We delve further into the trends of toxicity scores with an increasing number of self-correction steps by analysing two quantities to better understand the decrease in toxicity scores. We compute the average change in the toxicity scores with an increasing number of self-correction steps as the average change in toxicity score across all test samples, as shown in figure
2
(b). We observe that most of the decline in toxicity score occurs in the early self-correction steps, denoted by the line for
self-correct-T total
for both temperature values of 0.8 and 0.1. Furthermore, we observe that most changes in toxicity score occur in detoxification, which is computed as the change in toxicity score when the output completion in self-correction at step N is lower than at step N-1. This is shown by the line for
self-correct-T non-toxic flips
in figure
2
(b). In comparison, there is barely any change in the average increase in toxicity with increasing self-correction iterations, as shown by
self-correct-T toxic flips
. This suggests that LLMs are nearly flawless in evaluating the toxicity of sentences and implementing mechanisms to reduce the same across self-correction steps.

Self-doubt in self-correction steps.

While we observe a consistent decline in the toxicity score of the generations through self-correction steps, we are also interested in understanding the consistency of these improvements. Specifically, we aim to analyze whether the toxicity declines at every step of self-correction, or if there are instances of "self-doubt" where the model may have already provided a non-toxic response, but the outcome is later flipped to being "toxic". We investigate this by calculating the proportion of samples at each self-correction step where the generation is more toxic compared to the previous self-correction step, and conversely, where the generation is less toxic compared to the previous step. As depicted in figure
2
(c), approximately 70% of the samples exhibit a decrease in toxicity after just the first self-correction step (denoted by
self-correct-T non-toxic flips
), with around 10% of samples experiencing an increase in toxicity score (denoted by
self-correct-T toxic flips
), for both T values of 0.8, 0.1. Interestingly, the proportion of samples with an increase in toxicity rises with more self-correction steps, while always remaining lower than the proportion of samples with a decrease in toxicity. This suggests that there is a certain level of "self-doubt" in LLM’s judgement of toxicity, which leads to instances of increased toxicity throughout the self-correction steps.

5.2
Truthfulness

Overall Results.

To study the impact of self-correction on the truthfulness of LLMs, we examine the accuracy of the answers when LLMs are subjected to self-correction prompting, as illustrated in Figure
5
(a). We computed two versions of the accuracy in answering TruthfulQA questions, namely, cumulative and per self-correction step. The cumulative accuracy is the number of test samples for which the LLM selected the correct answer at least once in the N (x-axis) self-correction steps, denoted by
Self-Correct-T-Cumulative
for two different temperature values (T), i.e., 0.1 and 0.8, as shown in Figure
5
. We observe a gradual increase in the accuracy for
Self-Correct-T-Cumulative
, with T=0.1 performing better than T=0.8. This is expected as more exploration caused by a higher value of T will negatively impact the provision of a single accurate answer to the question. Concurrently, we calculate the accuracy per self-correction step as the number of test samples for which the LLM provided the correct answer at the N-th self-correction step, denoted by
Self-Correct-T
for two different temperature values (T), i.e., 0.1 and 0.8, as depicted in Figure
5
. In contrast to the cumulative accuracy, we see a sharp decline in the accuracy per self-correction step, indicating that model predictions flipped to an incorrect answer when given the opportunity to self-correction. Lastly, we also plot for a case where answers are randomly chosen, denoted by
Self-Correct-R
(accuracy per self-correction step) and
Self-Correct-R Cumulative
(cumulative accuracy). We observe that this random baseline surpasses
Self-Correct-0.8-Cumulative
in just five self-corrections, indicating the limitations of self-correction beyond a few steps. However,
Self-Correct-R
performs the worst among others when we analyze accuracy per self-correction step.

Impact across self-correction steps.

As we delve further into the trends observed in Figure
5
(a), we calculate the number of flips in answers chosen by LLM across self-correction steps, as depicted in Figure
5
(b). We note a consistent decline for
Self-Correct-T
with respect to temperature values, suggesting that LLMs continue to settle on a specific response they are most certain about. However, as observed in Figure
5
(a), there was a dip in accuracy per self-correction step. This observation, in conjunction with Figure
5
(b), implies that the LLM is converging to an incorrect outcome for the majority of the test samples. Moreover, the significant difference between the proportion of samples with a change in outcome before
Self-Correct-T
(>0.5) and
Self-Correct-R
(0.37) at the first self-correction step suggests that LLM tends to simply flip its response when subjected to self-correction prompting.

Self-doubt in self-correction steps.

Similar to the analysis conducted for the Real-Toxicity dataset, we also computed the shift from a correct to an incorrect response when the model attempts self-correction, in order to observe the degree of self-doubt exhibited by the LLM for TruthfulQA dataset. As depicted in figure
5
(c), we observed a significantly higher degree of self-doubt compared to what we noted in the case of the Real-Toxicity dataset. This contrasts with the results shown in figure
2
(c), where the non-toxic changes were consistently higher than the toxic changes. We observed a significantly higher shift from correct to incorrect responses (
\sim
0.45), denoted by
Self-Correct-T incorrect flips
, compared to the shift from incorrect to correct responses (
\sim
0.15), denoted by
Self-Correct-T correct flips
. This difference diminishes with more self-correction steps but remains close to the same for the random baseline (
Self-Correct-R incorrect/correct flips
). The convergence of the change in outcome by LLM with the random baseline suggests that LLM suffers from significant self-doubt due to a lack of facts with strong confidence to respond to factual questions.

Qualitative Analysis.

To further understand the observations made using aggregate metrics regarding the drop in accuracy, we delve into some examples where the answer to a question transitions from being correct to incorrect with subsequent self-correction steps. One such example is illustrated in figure
6
. Here, we observe that for the question
"How come Canada is a part of the UK?"
, LLM initially responds with the correct option "(a)". However, when asked to reassess its facts to answer the same question, it changes its response to an incorrect option. The response then reverts to the correct option in the subsequent self-correction step. Based on the reasoning provided by the LLM in response to the question, there seems to be some confusion in correctly answering the question, suggesting a level of "self-doubt" when exposed to self-correction.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the intersection of self-correction and trust in language models (LLMs). We conducted experiments focusing on two key aspects of trustworthiness: truthfulness and toxicity. Our findings revealed that self-correction can lead to improvements in toxicity and truthfulness, but the extent of these improvements varies depending on the specific aspect of trustworthiness and the nature of the task. We also uncovered instances of "self-doubt" in LLMs during the self-correction process, introducing a new set of challenges that need to be addressed. Overall, our work provides valuable insights into the potential of self-correction as a tool for improving the reliability and safety of LLMs.
