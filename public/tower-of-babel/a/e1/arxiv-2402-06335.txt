Abstract
. In this article, we discuss the local exact controllability to trajectories of the following convective Brinkman-Forchheimer (CBF) equations (or damped Navier-Stokes equations) defined in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ d \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} ( d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 ) with smooth boundary: ∂ 𝒖 ∂ t − μ Δ 𝒖 + ( 𝒖 ⋅ ∇ ) 𝒖 + α 𝒖 + β | 𝒖 | 2 𝒖 + ∇ p = 𝒇 + ϑ , ∇ ⋅ 𝒖 = 0 , \displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}+\beta|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\ \ \ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0, where the control ϑ \boldsymbol{\vartheta} is distributed in a subdomain ω ⊂ Ω \omega\subset\Omega , and the parameters α , β , μ > 0 \alpha,\beta,\mu>0 are constants. We first present global Carleman estimates and observability inequality for the adjoint problem of a linearized version of CBF equations by using a global Carleman estimate for the Stokes system. This allows us to obtain its null controllability at any time T > 0 T>0 . We then use the inverse mapping theorem to deduce local results concerning the exact controllability to the trajectories of CBF equations.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

(d=2,3)
be a bounded domain with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
(for example of class
\mathrm{C}^{2}
). Let
\omega\subset\Omega
be a non-empty, small, open subset, and let
T>0
. We will use the notations
Q:=\Omega\times(0,T)
,
Q_{\omega}:=\omega\times(0,T)
, and
\Sigma:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
, and denote by
\boldsymbol{n}(x),
the outward unit normal to
\Omega
at the point
x\in\partial{\Omega}
. The convective Brinkman-Forchheimer (CBF) equations describe the motion of an incompressible viscous fluid through a rigid, homogeneous, isotropic, porous medium (cf.
[
4
]
). Let us consider the following controlled CBF equations:

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}+\beta|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{u}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{u}(0)&=\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here
\boldsymbol{u}(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the velocity vector field of the fluid,
p(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the pressure in the fluid,
\boldsymbol{f}(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given external forcing acting on the fluid,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a control distributed in some arbitrary fixed subdomain
\omega
of the physical domain
\Omega
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x)
is a given initial vector field, and
\mathds{1}_{\omega}
is the characteristics function of the set
\omega
:

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\omega}(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\text{for }x\in\omega,\\
0,&\ \ \text{ for }x\in\Omega\backslash\omega.\end{array}}}\right.

The positive constant
\mu
denotes the
Brinkman coefficient
(effective viscosity), while
\alpha
and
\beta
stand for the
Darcy
(permeability of porous medium) and
Forchheimer
(proportional to the porosity of the material) coefficients, respectively. Setting
\alpha=\beta=0
yields the classical
d
-dimensional controlled Navier-Stokes equations (NSE). This accounts to refer the problem (
1.1
) as a controlled damped NSE also. The global solvability results of the uncontrolled problem (
1.1
) (that is,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=\mathbf{0}
) is discussed in
[
18
,
39
]
, etc.

1.1.
Statement of the problem

The main objective of this work is to discuss the local exact controllability to trajectories of the CBF equations (
1.1
). To state the problem precisely and present the main result, we require some function spaces which are given below (
[
46
]
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\ \ \text{in}\ \Omega\ \ \text{and}\ \ \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\ \ \text{on}\ {\partial\Omega}\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}:\ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\ \ \text{in}\ \Omega\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\bigg\{q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\ \int_{\Omega}q(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x=0\bigg\},

where
\boldsymbol{n}
is the unit outward drawn normal to the boundary and
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0
should be understood in the sense of trace (
[
46
, Section 1.3, Chapter 1]
). The following notations are also used repeatedly in the article:
\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)^{d}:=\mathrm{L}^{p}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)^{d})
, for
p\in[1,\infty]
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\omega)^{d})
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}:=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d^{2}})
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(Q):=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega))
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q):=\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
. Let
(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the inner product in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represent the duality pairing between a Banach space
\mathbb{X}
and its dual
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}
. Note that
\mathbb{H}
can be identified with its own dual
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
. We will denote a generic constant (usually depending on
\Omega
and
\omega
) by
C
throughout this article.

We cannot expect the exact controllability for CBF equations with an arbitrary target function due to the dissipative and irreversible characteristics of the system (
[
45
]
). Therefore, we consider an “ideal” trajectory
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})
of the following uncontrolled CBF equations:

(1.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\beta|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla\widetilde{p}&=\boldsymbol{f},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)&=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}}}\right.

with the same right-hand side
\boldsymbol{f}
as in (
1.1
). We additionally suppose that
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})
satisfies the following regularity property:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}.

Let us now define the notion of local exact controllability to trajectories of the CBF equations (
1.1
). The task is to find a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that at least one solution of (
1.1
) verifies that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,T)=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}(\cdot,T),\ \text{ in }\Omega.

If we are able to obtain such a control, we can switch it off after time
T
and the system will follow the “ideal” trajectory
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})
of (
1.2
).

More precisely, we say that the system (
1.1
) is
locally exactly controllable to trajectories
if for a suitable trajectory
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p}),
solution of (
1.2
), there exists a
\delta>0
such that if

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\delta,

we can find a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that the corresponding solution to (
1.1
) satisfies:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,T)=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}(\cdot,T),\ \text{ in }\Omega.

1.2.
Main result and application

The main result of the present article concerns the local exact controllability to trajectories of the CBF equations (
1.1
). We now state our main result in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let us assume that
\omega
is a non-empty open subset of
\Omega
and
T>0
. We suppose that the solution
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})
of the system (
1.2
) satisfies (
1.3
). Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}\cap\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{d}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\delta,

there exists a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
and a solution
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
of the system (
1.1
) that satisfies (
1.4
).

Motivated by the ideas developed in
[
8
,
35
,
41
]
, we prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
5
and the proof is based on an application of the inverse mapping theorem.

Remark 1.2
.

Under the regularity hypotheses described in (
1.3
), J.-P. Puel
[
41
]
established local exact controllability to the trajectories of the Navier-Stokes system. Therefore, it will be proven in this article that the presence of a cubic nonlinearity requires no additional regularity on the trajectories than the one described in (
1.3
).

Let us provide an application of the above controllability result (for more details, see
[
25
,
41
]
, etc).

Stabilizability of unstable stationary flows:
Let
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger},{p}^{\dagger})
be a stationary solution of the following CBF equations in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(1.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\mu\Delta{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}+({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}\cdot\nabla){\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}+\alpha{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}+\beta|{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}|^{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}+\nabla{p}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{f}(x),\ \text{ in }\Omega,\\
&\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}=0,\ \text{ in }\Omega,\ \ \ \ {\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

The global solvability results (
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})
, where
\mathrm{A}
is the Stokes operator) of the system (
1.5
) can be obtained from
[
40
]
. Suppose that this stationary solution is unstable.
Thus, for each
\delta>0,
there is an initial condition
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\{\boldsymbol{u}:\|\boldsymbol{u}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d}}<\delta\}
such that the solution
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
of the CBF equations (
1.1
) with
\boldsymbol{f}(x,t)\equiv\boldsymbol{f}(x)
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}(x,t)\equiv\mathbf{0}
would diverge from
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger},{p}^{\dagger})
as
t\rightarrow\infty
and

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,t)-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{d}}\nrightarrow 0\ \ \text{as}\ \ t\rightarrow\infty.

One can stabilize the unstable stationary solution
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger},{p}^{\dagger})
by replacing the first equation of (
1.1
) by the distributed control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
. Indeed, by Theorem
1.1
, for any initial
condition
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}\cap\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d},
there is a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
supported in
\omega\times(0,T)
, such that the solution
(\boldsymbol{u},p)
of the CBF equations (
1.1
) satisfies the condition

\boldsymbol{u}(x,T)\equiv{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger}(x).

This says that we are able to perform a strong stabilization of the unstable stationary flows
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{\dagger},{p}^{\dagger})
of solution of the system (
1.5
) by an interior control.

1.3.
Strategy and literature review

Several steps will be required to prove the main Theorem
1.1
. Let us now sketch the strategy which we employ to solve our problem.

•

First of all if we write

\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\ \ \ q=p-\widetilde{p},\ \ \boldsymbol{v}_{0}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},

we obtain a new controlled system:

(1.6)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla q&=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{v}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{v}(0)&=\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}}}\right.

where

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\boldsymbol{v}+2\beta(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}-\beta|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{v}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})-2\beta(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\boldsymbol{v}.

Consequently, it is easy to see that the local exact controllability of the trajectories of the system (
1.1
) is equivalent to the local null controllability of the system (
1.6
), that is, we want to find the control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that at time
T
, we have

\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0}.

More precisely, we say that the system (
1.6
) is
null controllable
if, for any initial data
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}\cap\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d}
, there exists a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that the associated solution to the system (
1.6
) satisfies:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0},\ \ \ \text{in }\Omega.

Accordingly, we will focus on proving the local null controllability of the nonlinear system (
1.6
).

•

In order to prove this local result, we employ an inverse mapping argument, developed in
[
8
,
41
]
, etc., in two steps.

Given an appropriate choice of tensor
\boldsymbol{g}
, a function
\boldsymbol{h}
(in a functional class that will be specified later), and given an initial data
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, we prove the null controllability of the following linearized system:

(1.10)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla q&=\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{g}+\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{v}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{v}(0)&=\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}}}\right.

where the operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined in (
1.7
).

The null controllability of (
1.10
) is based on the attainment of the so-called
observability inequality
for a backward system associated with (
1.10
). Let us introduce the adjoint state
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\pi)
associated with (
1.10
) that satisfies the system:

(1.11)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\nabla\pi&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{\varphi}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{\varphi}(T)&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T),\ \text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{L}
define by

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi}=-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}-(D\boldsymbol{\varphi})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\beta|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+2\beta(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},

\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
is the solution of the optimal control problem (cf. subsection
4.1
)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg),

such that
\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
is the solution of the system (
1.10
) associated to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, and
D\boldsymbol{\varphi}
stands for the symmetrized gradient
D\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{\top}
.

At present, the most powerful tool to prove the null controllability of the linearized system (
1.10
) is establishing global Carleman estimates for the solutions of its adjoint system (
1.11

1.11

•

2.
Well-posedness of the Linearized CBF Equations

In this section, we provide the well-posedness result for the linearized CBF equations (
1.10
), which is crucial in obtaining the controllability result for the CBF equations.

2.1.
Well-posedness

Let us prove the existence, uniqueness, and stability results for the linearized system (
1.10
). To do this, we first derive formal energy estimates for the solution of the problem (
1.10
). The following theorem establishes the well-posedness of the system (
1.10
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
,
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}
,
\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})
, and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
be given. Then, there exists a unique weak solution
(\boldsymbol{v},q)
to the system (
1.10
) satisfying

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}),\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(Q),

and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}+\|q\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}+\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\big),

and

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})}+\|\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}+\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg\{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(0)-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\boldsymbol{g}_{1}-\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}+\|\boldsymbol{h}_{1}-\boldsymbol{h}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\bigg\},

where
C
depends on the input data,
\mu,\alpha,\beta,T
, and
\Omega
.

Proof.

Existence:
The following calculations can be made rigorous by using a Faedo-Galerkin approximation technique. As the system (
1.10
) is linear, weak convergence of a Faedo-Galerkin approximated subsequence is enough to pass to the limit. Taking the inner product with
\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot)
to the first equation in (
1.10
) and using the fact that
((\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=0
and
(\nabla q,\boldsymbol{v})=0
, we obtain

3.
Carleman Estimates and observability inequality

The exact controllability problem for a linear evolution system can often be proven by examining the observability inequality of the associated adjoint system.

In this section, we derive a Carleman estimate and an observability inequality for the adjoint system (
1.11
) corresponding to the linearized system (
1.10
), which is necessary to study the exact controllability of the trajectories of the CBF equations. The only way we know to obtain such an estimate and inequality is to use a global Carleman estimate for the Stokes system. We begin this section by addressing the weight functions that will be used throughout the article. Other weight classes will be presented in the sequel as needed.

3.1.
Weight functions

We proceed to construct appropriate weight functions. Let us recall that
\omega\subset\Omega
is a non-empty open set.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\omega
be an arbitrary fixed subdomain of
\Omega
. Then, there exists a function
\eta\in\mathrm{C}^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

\displaystyle\eta(x)>0,\ \ \forall\ x\in\Omega,\ \ \ \ \eta|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\ \ \ \ |\nabla\eta(x)|>0,\ \ \forall\ x\in\overline{\Omega\backslash{\omega}}.

A proof of this lemma can be found in
[
22
, Lemma 1.1]
. Let us define
\ell\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}([0,T])
be such that

\displaystyle\ell(t)=t\ \ \text{on}\ \bigg[0,\frac{T}{4}\bigg],\ \ \ell(t)=T-t\ \ \text{on}\ \bigg[\frac{3T}{4},T\bigg],\ \ \ell(t)\geq\frac{T}{4}\ \ \text{on}\ \bigg[\frac{T}{4},\frac{3T}{4}\bigg].

Using the function
\eta
constructed in Lemma
3.1
, we introduce the weight functions as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\xi(x,t)-\frac{e^{\lambda(\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+m_{2})}}{\ell^{4}(t)},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \xi(x,t)=\frac{e^{\lambda(\eta(x)+m_{1})}}{\ell^{4}(t)},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\check{\psi}(t)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\psi(x,t)=\psi(t)|_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{e^{\lambda m_{1}}-e^{\lambda(\|\eta\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}+m_{2})}}{\ell^{4}(t)},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\check{\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\xi(x,t)=\xi(t)|_{\partial\Omega}=\frac{e^{\lambda m_{1}}}{\ell^{4}(t)},

where
\lambda\geq 1
and the constants
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are chosen such that
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
(see
[
41
, Lemma 4.1]
), and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\bigg|\leq C\xi^{\frac{5}{4}},\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t^{2}}\bigg|\leq C\xi^{\frac{3}{2}}.

We notice that

(3.4)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\check{\xi}(t)^{\frac{1}{4}}}\frac{\partial\check{\psi}(t)}{\partial t}\leq C\xi(t),\ \ \ \frac{1}{\check{\xi}(t)^{\frac{5}{4}}}\frac{\partial\check{\xi}(t)}{\partial t}\leq C,\ \ \text{for all}\ t\in[0,T],\\
&\check{\psi}(t)\leq\psi(x,t),\ \ \ \check{\xi}(t)\leq\xi(x,t),\ \ \ \check{\xi}(t)\geq C_{0}>0,\ \text{for all}\ (x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}}}\right.

We define the space
\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma)
using a classical notation as

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{4}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega))\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)).

We remark that Fursikov and Imanuvilov first introduced functions of this type in
[
22
]
to obtain Carleman inequalities for the heat equations. We also state that these weight functions were already defined by J.-P. Puel in the work
[
41
]
in order to obtain a Carleman estimate for the Stokes system.

3.2.
Carleman estimates

In the following subsection, we deduce a Carleman estimate for the adjoint system (
1.11
) associated with the linearized system (
1.10
). This will provide a null controllability result for the linearized CBF equations (
1.10
). We closely follow similar ideas of Carleman estimates for the Stokes system with Dirichlet conditions, which were proved by J.-P. Puel in
[
41
]
. To this end, let us consider the following Stokes system:

(3.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial t}-\Delta\boldsymbol{y}+\nabla q&=\boldsymbol{k},\ \text{ in }Q\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{y}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

It is well known (see
[
46
]
) that if
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}\big)
, where
\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
is the dual space of
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, there exist a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{y},q)
of the system (
3.5

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}),\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ q\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(Q).

Moreover, from the classical regularity result, if
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}
and
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{V}),\ \ \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial t}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}),\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \nabla q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d},

and in particular

(3.6)

\displaystyle\bigg\|\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial t}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{H}^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla q\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d}}\leq C\big(\|\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{V}}+\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d}}\big).

Let us define

. Taking the curl of the equation (

(3.7)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{z}}{\partial t}-\Delta\boldsymbol{z}&=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{k},\ \text{ in }Q,\\

As
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}=0

(3.8)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\nabla\times\boldsymbol{z}(t),\ \text{ in }\Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

We notice that
\boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{0}\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega
implies

{\nabla\boldsymbol{y}=(\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\boldsymbol{n},\ \text{ on }\ \partial\Omega.}

Thus,
\boldsymbol{z}_{|_{\partial\Omega}}
can be expressed in terms of
\nabla\boldsymbol{y}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
.

The vector function
\boldsymbol{z}
verifies a system of non-homogeneous heat equations (
3.7
). We can use the global Carleman estimate from
[
37
]
or an improvement established in
[
38
]
. For non-homogeneous parabolic equations, the following result of the global Carleman estimate has been established in
[

]

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem 2.2,
[

]
)
.

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\xi^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{s\psi}\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\int_{Q}e^{2s\psi}|\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+Cs\lambda^{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}e^{2s\psi}\xi|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t.

The vector function

is a solution of the elliptic equation (

\displaystyle\gamma(x)=e^{\lambda(\eta(x)+m_{1})}.

(Theorem A.1,

]
)
.

Let

Combining the estimates (

3.3
), we immediately get

\displaystyle\int_{T/2}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\xi}^{2}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{T/2}^{T}\int_{\Omega}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{0}^{T/2}\int_{\omega}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{T/2}^{T}\int_{\omega}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{0}^{T/2}\int_{\omega}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{T/2}^{T}\int_{\omega}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

(3.32)

\displaystyle=Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{Q_{\omega}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,

which combined with (
3.3
) leads to

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q}\widetilde{\xi}^{2}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{Q}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq Cs^{3}\lambda^{4}\int_{Q_{\omega}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,

and we get the desired observability inequality (
3.6
).
∎

4.
Null Controllability of the Linear System

In this section, we prove the null controllability of the linearized CBF equations (
1.10
) as a consequence of the observability inequality (see Theorem
3.6
). This result will be helpful to determine the local exact controllability of the system (
1.1
) in the next section.

Later discussion of the null controllability of the linearized CBF equations is motivated by the works
[
8
,
41
]
, in which the authors established the null controllability of the linearized Navier-Stokes system in both two and three dimensions.

4.1.
Penalty method

With the help of observability inequality (
3.6
), one can prove the null controllability of the linearized system (
1.10
). We establish the following null controllability result for the system (
1.10
).

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and the functions
\boldsymbol{g}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty,

respectively, then there exists a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
and a solution
\boldsymbol{v}
of the system (
1.10
) such that

\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0}.

Proof.

Let us consider, for each
\varepsilon>0
, the following optimal control problem:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}),

where

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,

and
\boldsymbol{v}
is the solution of the system (
1.10
) associated to
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
.

Step 1.

Existence of an optimal control:
From the definition of the cost functional
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
defined in (
4.2
), we can see that
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)\geq 0
, there exists an infimum
I
of
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
over
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
, that is,

\displaystyle 0\leq I:=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})<+\infty.

Since,
0\leq I<+\infty
, there exists a minimizing sequence
\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bigg(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}^{n}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)=I,

where
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}(\cdot)
is the unique solution of the linearized problem (
1.10
) with the control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
and initial data
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}(0)\in\mathbb{H}
. Since,
\mathbf{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n})\leq\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{0})
, where
\boldsymbol{v}
is the unique solution of (
1.10
) corresponding to the control
\mathbf{0}
. Using the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}^{n}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}<+\infty.

From the above expression, it is clear that, there exists a constant
R>0
, large enough such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq R<+\infty.

Using the energy estimate obtained in Lemma
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}+\|\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}+\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}+\|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}+\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}+R^{\frac{1}{2}}\big).

We know that the dual of
\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T,\mathbb{H})
is
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T,\mathbb{H})
and
\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T,\mathbb{H})
is separable. Also the spaces
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T,\mathbb{V})
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
are reflexive. By using the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, we can extract subsequences still denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\}
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\xrightharpoonup{w^{\ast}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T,\mathbb{H}),\\
&\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}),\\
&\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Moreover,
\boldsymbol{v}^{n}_{t}\xrightharpoonup{w}{\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}}_{t}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}^{\prime})
(cf. (
2.6
)).
Now, we show that
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
satisfies the system (
1.10
). Since,
\mathcal{L}
is a linear operator defined in (
1.7
), from the above convergences, one can easily deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}^{n}\xrightharpoonup{w}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}^{\prime}).

On passing to limit
n\rightarrow\infty
in the equation satisfied by
(\boldsymbol{v}^{n},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n})
, we find that the limit
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
satisfies (
1.10
) in the weak sense.

Finally, we show that
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
is a minimizer, that is,
I=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
. Since the
cost functional
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
is continuous and convex on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
and
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})
, it follows that
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
is weakly lowersemicontinuous. That is, for a sequence
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d},

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n}).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle I\leq\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{n})=I,

and hence
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
is a minimizer of the problem (
4.1
).

Step 2.

Uniqueness of the optimal control:

The optimal control problem (
4.1
) has a solution
(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
, and by the convexity of the cost functional and since the system (
1.10
) is linear, this solution is even unique.

Step 3.

First order necessary conditions of optimality:
The necessary condition of minimum yields

(4.4)

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T),\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(T)\bigg)+\int_{Q_{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t=0,\ \ \text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{w}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d},

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is, together with certain pressure
q_{\boldsymbol{w}}
, a solution of

(4.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}+\nabla q_{\boldsymbol{w}}&=\boldsymbol{w}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}&=0,\ \text{ in }Q,\\
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ on }\Sigma,\\
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(0)&=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Here, the (affine) map’s derivative as
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
at the point
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}
in the direction

\boldsymbol{w}
is represented by
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Then, the duality properties between
\boldsymbol{v}
(see (
1.10
)) and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
(see (
1.11
)) yield

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T),\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(T)\bigg)=\int_{Q_{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,\ \ \text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{w}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d},

which combined with (
4.4
), provides

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{\omega}}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}+\boldsymbol{\varphi})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t=0,\ \ \text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{w}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}.

Consequently, we can identify
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}
:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}+\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\mathbf{0},\ \text{ in }\ Q_{\omega}.

Step 4.

Null controllability of the linearized system:

We are now ready to pass the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. To this end, by multiplying the state equation for
\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}
(see (
1.10
)) by
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}+\nabla q_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big)=\big(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{g}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big)+\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle.

On simplification, it is immediate that

\displaystyle\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon},\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\big(\mathcal{L}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}\big)=\big(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{g}+\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big)+\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle.

Integrating the above equation with respect to time from
0
to
T
, we find

\displaystyle\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T),\boldsymbol{\varphi}(T)\big)-\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0)\big)=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{g}_{ij}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{Q_{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/t.

The optimality condition (
4.6
) and (
1.11
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t=\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0)\big)-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{g}_{ij}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/t.

Using the Cauchy-Schwartz and Young’s inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3C}{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{3C}{2}\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\frac{3C}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t

(4.7)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{6C}\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{3C}\int_{Q}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,

where
\widetilde{\psi}
and
\widetilde{\xi}
are the weight functions defined in (
3.23
) and
C
is the constant appearing in the observability inequality (see (
3.6
)). From the observability inequality (
3.6
) and optimality condition (
4.6
), we deduce

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q}\widetilde{\xi}^{2}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{Q}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{Q_{\omega}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq C\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t=C\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t.

If we assume that
\boldsymbol{g}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty,

then by using (
4.1
) in (
4.1
), we have the following estimate:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q_{\omega}}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq C\bigg(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg).

We can easily see that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)
and
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
, respectively, and independent of
\varepsilon
.
Using the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, we can extract subsequences, still denoted by
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}
, such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon}\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\ \ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\varepsilon})\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\ \text{ in }\ \mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)\xrightharpoonup{w}\boldsymbol{v}(T)\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{H}.

Using the weakly lowersemicontinuity property of the norm, we have

\|\boldsymbol{v}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{\varepsilon}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

Thus, we must have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0},

which completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Exponentially decreasing controls and solutions

In the following subsection, our aim is to find a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta},
which is exponentially decreasing as
t
approaches
T
, such that not only
\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0}
, but also
\boldsymbol{v}
is exponentially decreases as
t
approaches
T
. This result will be crucial to deduce the controllability properties for the nonlinear system (
1.1
) in the next section. Motivated by the works
[
8
,
41
]
, we have the following result:

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and the functions
\boldsymbol{g}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty,

respectively, then there exists a control
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
and a solution
{\boldsymbol{v}}
of (
1.10
) such that

{\boldsymbol{v}}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0},

and

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty\ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \int_{Q_{\omega}}\frac{e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}}{\xi^{3}}|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty.

Proof.

Let us remark that we adapt the ideas from
[
8
,
28
,
41
]
to prove our result.
Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\bigg\{(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\overline{Q})^{d+1},\ \ \nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}=0,\ \text{in}\ Q,\ \bar{\boldsymbol{v}}=\mathbf{0},\ \text{on}\ \Sigma,\ \int_{\omega}\bar{q}(t)\/\mathrm{d}\/x=0,\ \text{ a.e.}\ \text{in}\ (0,T)\bigg\}.

Let us consider the bilinear form
\mathfrak{B}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}\big((\breve{\boldsymbol{v}},\breve{q}),(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}(\mathcal{L}^{*}\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla\breve{q})\cdot(\mathcal{L}^{*}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla\bar{q})\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

(4.9)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{Q_{\omega}}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t,\ \ \text{for all}\ (\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\mathcal{X},

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{L}
(cf. (
1.12
) and (
1.7
)).

Since
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
, because of the Carleman estimate (
3.4
), we can see that
\mathfrak{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a scalar product on
\mathcal{X}
. Let us define
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
as the completion of
\mathcal{X}
for the norm associated to scalar product
\mathfrak{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
(which we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\overline{\mathcal{X}}}
). Then,
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
is a Hilbert space for this scalar product. Thus, the bilinear form
\mathfrak{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is well-defined, continuous, and coercive on
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
, and observe that the observability inequality (
3.6
) holds for all
(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
, that is,

\displaystyle\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\int_{Q}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}\big(|\nabla\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\widetilde{\xi}^{2}|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\big)\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{Q_{\omega}}e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathfrak{B}\big((\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q}),(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\big),\ \ \text{for all}\ (\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}.

Let us now consider the linear form
\mathfrak{L}:\overline{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(4.11)

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{L},(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\rangle=(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}(0))_{\mathbb{H}}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{Q}\boldsymbol{g}_{ij}\frac{\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\boldsymbol{h},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/t,\ \ \text{for all}\ (\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}},

with

\displaystyle|\langle\mathfrak{L},(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\big\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{g}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}\big\|e^{s\widetilde{\psi}}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}

\displaystyle\quad+\big\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}\big\|e^{s\widetilde{\psi}}\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\big\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{g}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d^{2}}}+\big\|e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\boldsymbol{h}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d})}}}\right)\|(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\|_{\overline{\mathcal{X}}},

for all
(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
and we have used the estimate (
4.2
). Therefore, we can see that the linear form
\langle\mathfrak{L},(\cdot)\rangle
is well-defined and continuous on
\overline{\mathcal{X}}
. Consequently, in view of Lax-Milgram Theorem (see
[
16
, Chapter 6]
), there exists a unique solution
(\breve{\boldsymbol{v}},\breve{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
of the variational problem

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}\big((\breve{\boldsymbol{v}},\breve{q}),(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\big)=\langle\mathfrak{L},(\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\rangle,\ \ \text{for all}\ (\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\bar{q})\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}.

Let us define

(4.14)

{\boldsymbol{v}}=e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}(L^{*}\breve{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla\breve{q}),\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ {\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=-e^{2s\widetilde{\psi}}\widetilde{\xi}^{3}\mathds{1}_{\omega}\breve{\boldsymbol{v}},

and one can observe that
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
verifies

\displaystyle\int_{Q}e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\int_{Q_{\omega}}\frac{e^{-2s\widetilde{\psi}}}{\xi^{3}}|{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/t<+\infty

and
\boldsymbol{v}
is solution of the system in (
1.10
) for certain pressure
q
.

Note that,
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q)^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(Q_{\omega})^{d}
. Then, we introduce the weak solution

5.3.
Local exact controllability to trajectories

In this subsection, we prove our main result of local exact controllability to trajectories of the CBF equations (
1.1
) (Theorem
1.1
) by using the inverse mapping theorem (or epimorphism theorem) given below. In order to achieve this, we establish the null controllability of the nonlinear system (
1.6
) which leads to the require result. We mainly follow the ideas outlined in
[
8
,
41
]
and references therein.

Theorem 5.9

(Inverse mapping theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{G}
be two Banach spaces and let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}
satisfy
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\mathcal{E};\mathcal{G})
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{e}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0})=\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0}):\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}
is surjective. Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for every
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathcal{G}
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{b}-\boldsymbol{b}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{G}}<\delta
, there exists a solution of the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{e})=\boldsymbol{b},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{e}\in\mathcal{E}.

This theorem can be viewed as a simple corollary of the generalized implicit function theorem, as demonstrated in the work of V. Alekseev, V. Tikhomirov, and S. Fomin
[
1
]
.

Let us define the operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}
by

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\big(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}-\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})+\nabla q-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\boldsymbol{v}(0)\big),\ \ \ \text{for all}\ (\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{E},

where the operators
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})
are defined in (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), respectively and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{d}
and
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{d}
for
d=2,3
.

The next lemma provides the continuity of some multilinear forms which will be required to apply Theorem
5.9
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Assume that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
. Let us consider the bilinear and trilinear forms
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}:\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Tri}}:\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{W}
, respectively, given by

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{2})+\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}+2\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\boldsymbol{v}_{2},

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Tri}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})

\displaystyle=\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\boldsymbol{v}_{3},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2}),&\ \ \text{ if }d=2,\\
\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathrm{W}^{-1,6}(\Omega)^{3}),&\ \text{ if }d=3.\end{array}}}\right.

Then, there exists
C>0
such that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{E}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathcal{E}},

(5.12)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Tri}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})\|_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{E}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathcal{E}}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathcal{E}}.

Proof.

Let us first prove the continuity of the bilinear form
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}
. In fact, for
d=2
, we can use

for any
(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{E}
and obtain from (

) that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2})}+\|\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|2\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathbb{H})}+C\|(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathbb{H})}

\displaystyle\quad+C\|(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(e^{-s{\psi}}(0,T);\mathbb{H})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(Q)^{2}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(Q)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{2}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(Q)^{2}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(Q)^{2}}.

d=2
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Tri}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s{\psi}}\beta(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-s{\psi}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{12}{11}}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}}}\right)\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}}}\right)\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{3}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{-\frac{s}{3}{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{4}}^{6}\/\mathrm{d}\/t\bigg)^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}\hat{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}\hat{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}}}\right)\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}\hat{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}\hat{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}\hat{\psi}}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{6}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|e^{\frac{s}{4}\hat{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3s}{4}{\hat{\psi}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}\|e^{\frac{s}{4}\hat{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3s}{4}{\hat{\psi}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{4}{3}}}}\right)\|e^{\frac{s}{4}\hat{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3s}{4}{\hat{\psi}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\|e^{\frac{s}{4}\hat{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3s}{4}{\hat{\psi}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{2}{3}}\|e^{\frac{s}{4}\hat{\psi}}e^{-\frac{3s}{4}{\hat{\psi}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{6}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{4}(\Omega)^{2})}^{2},

where we have used the embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{12}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\frac{12}{11}}_{\sigma}(\Omega)^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{2}
, Gagliardo-Nirenberg’s and Hölder’s inequalities. Using (
5.13
), we deduce (
5.12
), and the proof of Lemma
5.10
is completed.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

Let us assume that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
. Then,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\mathcal{E};\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

From (
5.9
) and (
5.10
), we can see that the operator
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})
defined in (
1.8
) can be written as

\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Bi}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{Tri}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}).

Thus, (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) imply that

\|\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{\mathcal{G}}\leq C(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{3}).

Therefore, we infer that
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}
defined in (
5.8
) is well-defined.

The first component of the operator
\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})
is linear (and consequently
\mathrm{C}^{1}
), except
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v})
. However, the bilinear and trilinear forms given by (
5.9
) and (
5.10
), respectively, are continuous (cf. Lemma
5.10
) and consequently, we deduce that
\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathrm{C}^{1}
map from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{G}
, which proves the lemma.
∎

As a consequence of this lemma, we can prove our main result (Theorem
1.1
). For the proof, we follow the strategy introduced in
[
8
,
35
,
41
]
, etc.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us take
\boldsymbol{e}_{0}=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\in\mathcal{E}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\in\mathcal{G}
. It follows that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0}):\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0})[\boldsymbol{v}_{*},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{*}]=\big(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{v}_{*}+\nabla q_{*}-\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{*}\mathds{1}_{\omega},\boldsymbol{v}_{*}(0)\big),\ \ \ \text{for all}\ (\boldsymbol{v}_{*},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{*})\in\mathcal{E},

and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0})
is a surjective linear map from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{G}
, as implied by the null controllability result for the linearized system (
5.1
) (see also Remark
5.8
), or equivalently,
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{e}_{0}))=\mathcal{G}
. An application of the inverse mapping theorem (Theorem
5.9
) gives exactly the conclusions of Theorem
1.1
if we take
\boldsymbol{b}=(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{0})\in\mathcal{G}
, that is, it gives the existence of
\delta>0
such that, if

\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2d-2}(\Omega)^{d}}\leq\delta,

then we can find a control
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
such that the associated solution to (
1.6
) satisfies
\big(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\big)\in\mathcal{E}
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,T)=\mathbf{0},\ \ \ \text{in}\ \Omega,

and we reach at the desired result. Thus the proof of Theorem
1.1
is completed.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

We could have taken in the equation for
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
an external force
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
different from
\boldsymbol{f}
(cf. (
1.2
)). We can set

\boldsymbol{f}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}+\boldsymbol{f}^{*},

where
\widetilde{\xi}^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{-s\widetilde{\psi}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
is sufficiently small and finite, and obtain the local exact controllability to trajectories of the CBF equations (
