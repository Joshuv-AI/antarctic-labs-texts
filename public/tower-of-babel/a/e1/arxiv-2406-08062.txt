Abstract
. The combinatorial Loewner property was introduced by Bourdon and Kleiner as a quasisymmetrically invariant substitute for the Loewner property for general fractals and boundaries of hyperbolic groups. While the Loewner property is somewhat restrictive, the combinatorial Loewner property is very generic – Bourdon and Kleiner showed that many familiar fractals and group boundaries satisfy it. If X X is quasisymmetric to a Loewner space, it has the combinatorial Loewner property. Kleiner conjectured in 2006 that the converse to this holds for self-similar fractals – the hope being that this would lead to the existence of many exotic Loewner spaces. We disprove this conjecture and give the first examples of spaces which are self-similar, combinatorially Loewner and which are not quasisymmetric to Loewner spaces. In the process we introduce a self-similar replacement rule, called iterated graph systems (IGS), which is inspired by the work of Laakso. This produces a new rich class of fractal spaces, where closed form computations of potentials and their conformal dimensions are possible. These spaces exhibit a rich class of behaviors from analysis on fractals in regards to diffusions, Sobolev spaces, energy measures and conformal dimensions. These behaviors expand on the known examples of Cantor sets, gaskets, Vicsek sets, and the often too difficult carpet-like spaces. Especially the counterexamples to Kleiner’s conjecture that arise from this construction are interesting, since they open up the possibility to study the new realm of combinatorially Loewner spaces that are not quasisymmetric to Loewner spaces.

1.2.
Main theorem

The theory of quasiconformal mappings was revealed in settings of increasing generalities: first developed on surfaces
[
1
]
, then higher dimensional Euclidean spaces
[
72
,
82
]
, followed by certain Carnot groups
[
70
]
and ultimately Loewner spaces
[
38
]
; see
[
12
,
53
,
36
,
20
,
47
]
for nice surveys. This generalization was partly motivated by quasi-isometric rigidity problems in geometric group theory (e.g. Mostow’s rigidity theorem,
[
64
,
16
,
47
]
). Indeed, it was observed that whenever the boundary of a hyperbolic group possessed a Loewner structure in its conformal gauge, this implied rigidity properties for the group
[
53
,
36
,
20
]
. The conformal gauge consists of all metrics quasisymmetric to a given (visual) reference metric. While successful, this approach suffered a somewhat serious limitation: there are only very few hyperbolic groups whose boundaries are known to possess a Loewner structure. An essentially exhaustive list is given by the following families of examples: boundaries of groups acting by isometries co-compactly on a rank-one symmetric space
[
70
,
63
]
or a Bourdon-Pajot building
[
18
,
19
]
. Prompted by this, it was asked by several authors
[
53
,
36
,
30
,
21
,
47
]
if more hyperbolic group boundaries would admit a Loewner structure.

Towards an answer, Bourdon and Kleiner observed in
[
17
]
that a seemingly similar combinatorial Loewner property (CLP) was much more generic than the Loewner property, and was much easier to establish in given examples. Indeed, they and Clais
[
31
]
showed that far more group boundaries and fractals satisfy it: the Sierpińki carpet, the Menger curve, boundaries of certain higher dimensional hyperbolic buildings, and boundaries of certain Coxeter groups. None of these examples were known to possess a Loewner metric in their conformal gauge. Given the amplitude of such examples, Kleiner conjectured in
[
53
]
the following.

Conjecture 1.2
.

All approximately self-similar (or: group boundary) combinatorially Loewner metric spaces are quasisymmetric to a Loewner space.

Kleiner’s conjecture, and related problems, have attracted considerable attention, see e.g.
[
30
,
66
,
44
,
27
,
56
,
24
]
, and its statement is quite natural. Due to a lack of counterexamples, and hopes of finding new exotic Loewner spaces, there have been several attempts at proving the conjecture true, and an expectation by many that the conjecture would have a positive answer. However, a negative answer also would be quite intriguing: it opens up the possibility to study the combinatorial Loewner porperty as something strictly weaker and more general than the Loewner property.

As we will discuss below, when we introduce the terminology in detail, this problem is also equivalent to the attainment problem for the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension. This attainment problem has also been studied extensively, and is known to be quite difficult. In a different disguise, the same problem has also been studied for conductively homogeneous spaces, see
[
66
,
52
]
. In that setting, it is closely related to the attainment problem of other critical dimensions, such as the conformal walk dimension – whose attainment problem is much better understood thanks to
[
44
]
.

Our main contribution is to answer the main part of Kleiner’s conjecture to the negative by giving the first examples of approximately self-similar combinatorially Loewner metric spaces which are not quasisymmetric to Loewner spaces.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists a compact metric space
X
, which is approximately self-similar and combinatorially
Q
-Loewner for some
Q\in(1,\infty)
, but which is not quasisymmetric to a Loewner space. This space also does not attain its Ahlfors regular conformal dimension.

The counterexamples are obtained by a new construction of fractal spaces that we call
iterated graph systems (IGS)
. An IGS consists of a given graph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
together with an iteration procedure that produces a sequence of graphs
G_{m}=(V_{m},E_{m})
. The graphs
G_{m+1}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, are obtained recursively by replacing each edge of
G_{m}
by a copy of
G_{1}
and identifying neighboring copies by a given rule. The resulting graphs
G_{n}
are equipped with a re-scaled path metric
d_{n}
. Under some easily checkable conditions the sequence of metric spaces
(G_{n},d_{n})
Gromov-Hausdorff converge to a limit space
X
. (At this juncture, an eager reader may check Definition
3.2
.) We note that a slightly similar idea has appeared in
[
61
, Definition 2.1]
.

With specific graphs, see e.g. Figure
1.5
and Example
4.23
, such IGSs produce variants of fractals called Laakso-spaces
[
58
]
, although the construction is distinct from the quotient-construction of Laakso and the identifications used are different. These can also be realized by an inverse limit construction, see

[
29
]
, and as we will see in Example
5.14
they are Loewner spaces. Our key insight is that such constructions can be modified by adding edges, which we call
removable edges
, to produce spaces that do not arise as inverse limits. Such spaces are still combinatorially Loewner. It is one of these modified examples that are depicted in Figure
1.1
which yields a counterexample to Kleiner’s conjecture. Indeed, the example in this figure shows the following explicit version of Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 1.4
.

The space
X
from Example
3.8
is
\log(9)/\log(4)
-Ahlfors regular,
3/2
-combinatorially Loewner and approximately self-similar. Its conformal dimension is equal to
3/2
, and it is not attained. In particular, the space
X
is not quasisymmetric to a Loewner space.

Our result will be more general, and we will indicate which assumptions and mechanisms lead to the failure of being quasisymmetric to a Loewner space. In particular, we give a fairly large family of examples, all of which share crucial features and all of which are fairly simple to describe using the substitution procedure above. Indeed, by relatively minor assumptions, see Assumptions
5.1
, an IGS produces an approximately self-similar and combinatorially Loewner limit space. While we do not resolve the full question of Kleiner in all settings (in particular group boundaries or the Sierpiński carpet), these examples indicate mechanisms and tools by which one can prove that other examples are also not quasisymmetric to Loewner spaces. Further work is needed to determine which combinatorially Loewner fractals attain their conformal dimensions. Next, we shall briefly discuss relationships to recent work.

1.3.
Background

Until the present work, not much definitive was known about Kleiner’s question. Due to the work of
[
49
,
25
,
74
,
65
,
51
,
63
]
it was known how to construct metrics in the conformal gauge of
X
. The construction was powerful enough to lead to a characterization of the conformal dimension as a critical exponent for certain discrete modulus problems,
[
17
,
25
]
. It was also known, that the hypothetical Loewner metric would be a minimizer for the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension, see e.g.
[
27
, Introduction]
. These insights suggested that, if a Loewner metric were to exist in the conformal gauge of a metric space, it could be obtained by studying minimizers for discrete modulus problems
{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Gamma,G_{m})
with
Q
equal to the conformal dimension.

By applying discretization, certain structures from this hypothetical Loewner metric can be gleaned form the original undeformed space. Versions of Sobolev spaces were constructed in
[
15
,
66
,
62
]
, which would coincide with the Sobolev space associated to the Loewner space in the sense of
[
26
,
73
,
3
]
, if the space was quasisymmetric to a Loewner space. Also, discrete modulus at the critical exponent would be comparable to the continuous modulus of the associated Loewner space
[
34
,
69
,
80
]
. Finally, Murugan and Shimizu constructed an energy measure associated to the Sobolev spaces mentioned before, that would yield a measure with respect to which the Hausdorff
Q
-measure of the Loewner structure would be mutually absolutely continuous
[
66
]
. Their work built upon the work of Kigami
[
52
]
and Shimizu
[
76
]
.

Indeed, the work of Murugan and Shimizu presented a strategy by which the existence of a Loewner metric in the conformal gauge could be determined in general. This was inspired by earlier work of Kajino and Murugan
[
44
]
, where they observed that a similar strategy could be used for
p=2
to resolve a problem on the attainment of a related conformal walk dimension. In this latter work the energy was associated with the exponent
p=2
, and in the first,
p
is chosen to coincide with the conformal dimension. In both works, one observes that if the hypothesized structure were to exist, the measure and metric could be obtained by considering Sobolev functions on the space and their energy measures. Our argument also is based on computing minimizers for modulus, but the logic is more direct, and we do not discuss energy measures or the associated Sobolev space in this work.

1.4.
New Fractal Spaces

Finally, we note that the fractal spaces that we construct using the framework of IGS, may have broader interest. Indeed, there has been much work on analysis on fractals, especially regarding diffusions see e.g.
[
7
,
50
,
77
,
78
]
and conformal geometry
[
63
]
. Some major examples in this realm have been the Cantor sets, Sierpiński gasket, general gaskets, Sierpiński carpets, Menger curve and the Vicsek set. Indeed, these classes of spaces are often presented as the prototypical examples of fractals and all arise from iterated function systems of Euclidean space. Often, the Cantor sets, gasket and Vicsek cases have been solved first, and then (with considerably more work) the Sierpiński carpet/Menger curve case is pursued. Cantor sets are quantitatively totally disconnected and, in the gasket and Vicsek spaces, cut points give significant simplifications for calculations. In the cases with local cut-points many problems have been fully resolved, such as determining the conformal dimension and its attainment
[
63
,
81
]
, and the construction of diffusions
[
50
]
. For carpets, the lack of cut points is reflected in significant difficulty in performing calculations. Consequently, most of the same questions such as the attainment problem of conformal dimension are not known for carpets (e.g.
[
63
]
), while others, such as the existence of a diffusion
[
8
,
9
]
and construction of a Dirichlet form
[
55
]
are quite involved.

The reason to study these example spaces is that they are “toy models” used to develop tools to approach much more difficult problems, such as: the behavior of random walks on percolation clusters (e.g.
[
7
, Introduction]
), or Cannon’s, Kleiner’s and Kapovitch’s conjectures in geometric group theory
[
27
, Introduction]
. This is natural for the reason that connected components of supercritical percolation clusters in the plane, and planar group boundaries, are homeomorphic to carpets (see e.g.
[
48
]
). It has been suggested that the next step in difficulty from gasket-type spaces is carpet-like spaces. However, at present, beyond some intriguing work in
[
17
,
52
,
76
,
66
]
, there is not much progress in the study of carpets. Indeed, the difficulties in the analysis of carpet-like spaces has stymied development of the theory.

The class of fractals that we present here are a new class of natural spaces where to study analysis and which do not arise as attractors of iterated function systems in Euclidian spaces. (Indeed, the metrics on these spaces often do not bi-Lipschitz embed into Euclidean space, see e.g.
[
59
]
for an argument for the example given in Figure
3.4
). Similar to carpet’s, the examples (often) do not have cut points. Despite this, the computations in these examples are even somewhat simpler than those for gaskets. (This is especially true for exponents
p\neq 2
. For
p=2
the computations for gaskets are also very tractable, see e.g.
[
52
,
77
]
and compare these to
[
40
,
79
,
23
]
, which showcase the
p\neq 2
theory for gaskets.) Thus, in a sense, the spaces lie in difficulty between gaskets and carpets, and offer a new setting to explore prototypical behaviors of random walks and conformal geometry. The examples have some common features: they all have topological and Assouad-Nagata dimension equal to
1
, see Remark
3.38
. Further, as explained in Example
3.8
and as follows from
[
4
,
5
]
, the constructions often yield spaces homeomorphic to the Menger curve.

Often analysis on fractals hinges upon potential theory and computing minimizers of energy. In general this is quite difficult - especially for carpet-like spaces (see e.g.
[
56
]
). The inability to perform exact computations has left many interesting questions unanswered. The distinguished feature of the graphs arising from iterated graph systems is the ability to effectively compute minimizers of energy. This explicit computability and richness of the family suggest that studying the class further could yield crucial insights and may allow one to answer further questions in analysis on fractals that have heretofore resisted effort. Answering Conjecture
1.2
seems to be just the first example of these insights. Another example is understanding when the so called
p
-walk dimension
d_{w,p}
is strictly grater than
p
, which we define in our framework in Definition
6.10
. It has been observed in multiple different works (see e.g.
[
46
,
66
,
33
]
) that this problem is closely related to the structure of Sobolev spaces. See also
[
52
, Problem 4 in Section 6.3]
. In Subsection
6.3
we present a simple characterisation in our framework of when
d_{w,p}\geq p
is a strict inequality and present examples for both cases.

Next, we will go over each of the main concepts: conformal dimension, the Loewner property and the combinatorial Loewner property. We will also explain the proof of the counterexamples using them. The iterated graph systems are described and studied in Section
3
. A reader mostly interested in the construction of such fractal spaces and graphs can read this section independent of most of the other sections, and this section does not require most of the notions from quasiconformal geometry. Modulus on graphs is presented in Section
4
and the combinatorial Loewner property for certain IGSs is proven in Section
5
. In Section
6
we study porosity and prove the main theorems. The concepts that we use are mostly standard, and their classic theory can be read from textbooks such as
[
35
,
63
,
39
]
.

2.
Core concepts

2.1.
Conformal dimension

Let
Q\in(1,\infty)
and let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
A compact metric space
X
is said to be
Q
-
Ahlfors regular
, if there exists a constant
C>1
so that the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure satisfies
C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mathcal{H}^{Q}(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}
for all
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X)]
and all
x\in X
. Here, the
Hausdorff measure
is given by

\mathcal{H}^{Q}(A):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\delta}(A),

where the
Hausdorff content
’s are defined by

\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\delta}(A):=\inf\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm diam}(A_{i})^{Q}:A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},{\rm diam}(A_{i})\leq\delta\}.

The value
Q
is equal to the
Hausdorff dimension
for a
Q
-Ahlfors regular space. In general, if
\mu
is any Radon measure on
X
, we say that
(X,d,\mu)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular if
C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}
for all
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X)]
and
x\in X
. In this case there exists a constant
C
for which
\frac{1}{C}\mu\leq\mathcal{H}^{Q}\leq C\mu
, and the metric space
X
is also
Q
-Ahlfors regular.

Our focus will be on self-similar spaces with self-similairity defined as in
[
17
]
. See
[
71
]
for a more detailed discussion on this, and related, notions.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a metric space
X
is
approximately self-similar
, if there exists a constant
L\geq 1
so that for every
x\in X
and every
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X)]
, there exists an open set
U_{x,r}\subset X
and a
L
-biLipschitz map
f_{x,r}:(B(x,r),d/r)\to U_{x,r}
, where the ball
B(x,r)
is equipped with the scaled metric
d/r
.

The maps
f_{x,r}
in the statement will be refered to as scaling maps. A map
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is a
L
-
biLipschitz map
(or embedding) if for all
x,y\in X
, we have

\frac{1}{L}d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq Ld_{X}(x,y).

Let
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous homeomorphism. A homeomorphism
f:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is said to be
\eta
-
quasisymmetric
if for every
x,y,z\in Z
with
x\neq z
, we have

\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{Y}(f(x),f(z))}\leq\eta\left(\frac{d_{X}(x,y)}{d_{X}(x,z)}\right).

We say that
f:X\to Y
is a
quasisymmetry
, if it is an
\eta
-quasisymmetry for some
\eta
. In these cases, we say that
X
is quasisymmetric to
Y
. For more background on these mappings and the notions below, see
[
35
]
. At this juncture, we note that we will usually omit the subscript of a metric
d
, where the space is clear from context.

The
conformal gauge
of a metric space
X
is given by

\mathcal{G}(X,d):=\{\hat{d}:\hat{d}\text{ is a metric on }X\text{ s.t. }{\rm id}:(X,d)\to(X,\hat{d})\text{ is a quasisymmetry}\}.

Here
{\rm id}
is the identity map. This gauge is generally quite big – see
[
25
]
for a description of metrics in this gauge (also,
[
65
,
74
]
). We will focus mostly on those metrics, which are Ahlfors regular. This gives the
Ahlfors regular conformal gauge
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d):=\{\hat{d}:

\displaystyle\ \ {\rm id}:(X,d)\to(X,\hat{d})\text{ is a quasisymmetry and }

\displaystyle(X,\hat{d})\text{ is $\widehat{Q}$-Ahlfors regular for some }\widehat{Q}>0\}.

Where the metric
d
is clear from context, we will drop it in the notation and simply write
\mathcal{G}(X),\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X)
. Among all such metrics, one may wish to find an “optimal metric”, which satisfies some nice analytic or symmetry properties. A frequently considered problem is to minimize the Hausdorff dimension among metrics in the (Ahlfors regular) conformal gauge: the
(Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension
is given by

{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X):=\inf\{\widehat{Q}:\hat{d}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X,d)\text{ and }(X,\hat{d})\text{ is $\widehat{Q}$-Ahlfors regular}\}.

A version of this invariant, the conformal Hausdorff dimension was first considered by Pansu
[
69
]
, and the present definition was given in
[
21
]
. Further variants of this conformal dimension, such as conformal Assouad, have alse been studied. See
[
63
]
for an introduction to conformal dimension and
[
27
,
25
]
for some further work on these dimensions. The various definitions are equivalent in self-similar settings, see
[
32
]
. Since all the spaces considered in this paper are self-similar, we will mostly speak of just conformal dimension to refer to these notions.

If the infimum in the definition of the (Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension is attained, we say that
X
attains its (Ahlfors regular) conformal dimension. The question to determine if this happens for a given space, or a class of spaces, is called the
attainment problem
. Determining the conformal dimension and the attainment problem are notoriously hard. The value of the conformal dimension can, however, be numerically estimated. Numerical computations of the conformal dimension of the Sierpiński carpet were performed in
[
56
]
. These calculations are based on characterizations of the conformal dimension in terms of combinatorial modulus, see
[
25
,
17
]
and the related work
[
65
,
74
,
51
]
. In order to describe these characterizations, we will next define combinatorial and continuous moduli.

2.2.
Continuous and combinatorial modulus

Out of the two notions of moduli, the continuous one is easier to define, and is much more classical, see e.g.
[
83
,
39
]
. We will define it only in the case when
X
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular for some
Q\in(1,\infty)
. While modulus can be defined with respect to any measure on
X
, we will only consider Hausdorff
Q
-measures in the present paper. Let
\Gamma
be a family of curves. We say that a Borel measurable
\rho:X\to[0,\infty]
is admissible for
\Gamma
if for all rectifiable curves
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have
\int_{\gamma}\rho ds\geq 1
, where the integration is with respect to the length measure. If
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
p
-modulus of a curve family
\Gamma
is defined as

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Gamma):=\inf\left\{\int\rho^{p}d\mathcal{H}^{Q}:\rho\text{ is admissible for }\Gamma\right\}.

For a quick overview of modulus and its properties, see
[
83
,
39
]
. A crucial issue with this definition is that it is not a quasisymmetry invariant. Indeed, different metric in the conformal gauge do not yield mutually absolutely continuous Hausdorff-measures, nor do they agree on the class of rectifiable curves. The only setting, where invariance can be obtained, is if
f:X\to Y
is a quasisymmetry between two
Q
-Ahlfors regular metric spaces – see
[
80
]
.

In order to get full invariance, the notion of modulus needs to be discretized. There are a variety of approaches to do this,
[
34
,
80
,
37
,
49
,
52
,
17
,
32
,
66
,
62
]
. For some comparisons between different approaches, see
[
32
]
. In each approach the space and curves are discretized in different ways. We will follow
[
17
]
since the combinatorial Loewner property was originally expressed in terms of it. (It may be possible to express the combinatorial Loewner property also in terms of other notions of modulus and by employing other graphical approximations, but this is not essential for the present work.)

Let
\alpha,L_{*}>1
. An
\alpha
-approximation to a compact metric space
X
is a sequence of incidence graphs
\{G_{m}=(V_{m},E_{m})\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, where for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
the set of vertices
V_{m}
is a collection of subsets of
X
, which forms a covering of
X
, and which satisfies the following conditions

(1)

For every
v\in V_{m}
there exists a
z_{v}\in v
for which

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-m})\subset v\subset B(z_{v},\alpha L_{*}^{-m}).

(2)

For every pair of distinct
v,w\in V_{m}
we have

B(z_{v},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-m})\cap B(z_{w},\alpha^{-1}L_{*}^{-m})=\emptyset.

Here, an incidence graph
G=(V,E)
of
X
is a collection
V
of subsets of
X
, which cover
X
, where
\{v,u\}\in E
is an edge if and only if
u\cap v\neq\emptyset
. For a given subset
F\subseteq X
we write

G[F]:=\{v\in V:X_{v}\cap F\neq\emptyset\}.

If
\Gamma
is a family of subsets of
X
we define

G[\Gamma]:=\{G[\gamma]:\gamma\in\Gamma\}.

In
[
17
]
, the value
L_{*}=2
is used exclusively, but the results apply just the same with a different

is a limit space under Gromov-Hausdorff convergence. A posteriori, the graphs

can also be identified with an

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N},p\geq 1
and let

, and
\gamma\subset X
be a continuous curve identified with its image. We define the
\rho

L_{\rho}(\gamma):=\sum_{v\in G_{m}[\gamma]}\rho(v).

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
. We define the

Given a family of continuous curves

\delta>0

\mathcal{M}_{\delta,p}^{(m)}:={\rm Mod}_{p}^{D}(\Gamma_{\delta},G_{m}).

Proposition 2.3

(Corollary 1.4

Let

{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)=\inf\left\{p\geq 1:\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{M}_{\delta,p}^{(k)}=0\right\}.

This characterization is very powerful, but difficult to use since the moduli in question are quite challenging to compute. At present, for most spaces, this can only be done via numerical approximation. See e.g.

2.3.
Loewner condition

Given the work of Heinonen and Koskela
[
38
]
, and given its implications for the study of quasisymmetric maps, one special property of a metric in the conformal gauge is the Loewner property. This property is expressed using the concept of a continuous modulus. Now, through its connection to capacity, see e.g.
[
52
,
83
]
, it is particularly relevant to consider the moduli of path families connecting sets. Let
E,F\subset X
be closed sets, and let
\Gamma(E,F)
be the collection of rectifiable curves connecting
E
to
F
. The Loewner condition is given by a lower bound for the modulus of curves between two continua. If
E,F\subset X
are two non-degenerate continua (i.e. compact connected subsets with
{\rm diam}(E),{\rm diam}(F)\neq 0
), then their relative distance is given by
\Delta(E,F)=\frac{d(E,F)}{\min\{{\rm diam}(E),{\rm diam}(F)\}}
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is said to be
Q
-Loewner, if it is
Q
-Ahlfors regular and

{\rm Mod}_{Q}(\Gamma(E,F))\geq\phi(\Delta(E,F)^{-1}),

for some increasing function
\phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
and for all non-degenerate continua
E,F\subset X
.

The problems of minimizing Hausdorff dimension in the conformal gauge, and that of finding a Loewner metric in the conformal gauge are very closely related. First, if
(X,d^{\prime})
is
Q^{\prime}
-Loewner for some metric
d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X)
, then
Q^{\prime}={\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)
, and thus the metric
d^{\prime}
attains the infimum in the definition of the conformal dimension. This follows from
[
80
,
69
]
. Indeed, the Loewner property is somewhat excessive here, since it suffices that
{\rm Mod}_{Q^{\prime}}(\Gamma)>0
for any family of rectifiable curves.

Conversely, if
(X,d^{\prime})
is
Q^{\prime}
-Ahlfors regular,
d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X)
and
Q^{\prime}={\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)
, then
X
almost admits a positive modulus family of curves. Indeed, Keith and Laakso showed that a weak tangent of
X
admits such a positive modulus family of curves
[
49
]
. If
X
is further assumed to be self-similar, then this family of curves can be elevated to the space
X
, see e.g.
[
14
, Proof of Corollary 1.6]
.

The Loewner property is, however, stronger than a mere positivity of modulus for some family of curves. It involves a quantitative, and scale-invariant, lower bound for the connecting modulus between any pair of continua. However, it was already observed in
[
14
]
, that further symmetry in the space elevates having positive modulus to a Loewner property. Thus, the Loewner property can be seen as involving three ingredients: positive modulus, scale invariance and symmetry. Further, as a consequence, at least for group boundaries, the attainment of conformal dimension is equivalent to the existence of a Loewner metric in the conformal gauge,
[
14
]
. How can one then recognize the existence of a Loewner metric in the conformal gauge? Bourdon and Kleiner proposed a discrete version of the Loewner property, the combinatorial Loewner property as a potential condition for this
[
17
]
.

Combinatorial Loewner property

By discretizing the continuous modulus, Bourdon and Kleiner gave a quasisymmetrically invariant combinatorial Loewner property,

Definition 2.7

(CLP)
.

Let
Q>1
and

.
We say that a compact metric space satisfies the

on

(CLP1)

If

If

and

2.5.
Proof of counterexamples

There are two key insights that lead to our counterexamples.

(1)

Explicit computability:
There is a family of spaces,

\{X:X\text{ is a limit of an iterated graph system}\},

similar to those introduced by Laakso in
[
58
]
, for which discrete moduli are “easy” to compute. Consequently for these examples, we can compute their conformal dimensions explicitly, see Section
4
and Theorem
5.2
.

(2)

Porosity:
There are pairs of spaces
(Y,X)
in the given family where
Y\subset X
is a porous subset, and where using the first insight we can find
{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)={\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(Y)
.

We say that
Y\subset X
is a (uniformly)
porous
subset of
X
, if there exists an
r_{0}>0
and
c>0
so that for every
y\in Y
, and every
r\in(0,r_{0})
there exists
x\in B(y,r)\subset X
for which
B(x,cr)\cap Y=\emptyset
. Porous sets of Ahlfors regular spaces have strictly smaller Assouad dimension, and the conformal dimension is bounded from above by the Assouad dimension of any quasisymmetric image. These facts together with the quasisymmetric invariance of porosity yield the following proposition. See Section
6.1
for definitions and a detailed proof.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a porous subset. If
{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(Y)={\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)
, then
X
does not attain its conformal dimension.

In our case,
X
will be the space arising from the IGS in Figure
1.1
, and
Y
the Laakso-type space arising from Figure
1.5
. A direct computation will show that their conformal dimensions are equal. The fact that the IGS for
Y
arises from a sub-graph of that of
X
yields that
Y
is porous in
X
. From these, we see that
X
can not attain its conformal dimension, and thus by Proposition
2.8
can not contain a Loewner metric in its conformal gauge. This, together with the fact that
X
is combinatorially Loewner and approximately self-similar yields the proof of the main theorem.

3.
Iterated graph systems

3.1.
General notation

Simple graphs (without loops) are pairs
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is some finite set of vertices and
E\subset\{\{x,y\}:x,y\in V,x\neq y\}
is a finite set of edges. An edge
e
with end points
x,y
is denoted
\{x,y\}
. Notice that we do not permit loops in our graphs. If
\{v,w\}\in E
we say that
w
is a neighbor of
v
. The degree of a vertex
v\in V
is the number
{\rm deg}(v)
of edges to which it is adjacent. The degree of the graph
G
is defined as
{\rm deg}(G)=\max_{v\in V}{\rm deg}(v)
.

Remark 3.1
.

For simplicity of notation and to avoid some tedious case analysis, we restrict attention to simple graphs. Our results can also be stated for multi-graphs, and these require mostly small changes to the statements and arguments, e.g. in Proposition
3.11
.

It will be convenient to identify
G
with its geometric realization, which is obtained by gluing copies of unit edges indexed by the edges of
G
along their end points, whenever two edges are adjacent. This makes
G
into a simplicial complex. If this geometric realization is connected, we call
G
connected.

A vertex path in
G
is a sequence
\theta=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
, where
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\in E
for each
i=1,\dots,k-1
. Here
{\rm len}(\theta)=k-1
is the length of the vertex path
\theta
, and we say that the path connects
v_{1}
to
v_{n}
. The path metric
d_{G}(v,w)
in a connected metric space
G
between two vertices
v,w\in V
is the smallest
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that there is a vertex path of length
n
connecting
v
to
w
. If
A,B\subseteq V
then
\Theta(A,B)
is the set of paths connecting a point
v\in A
to a point
u\in B
. Further if
d
is a metric on
V
and
A\subseteq V
is a set of vertices, then
{\rm diam}(A,d):=\max_{a,b\in A}d_{G}(a,b)
is the diameter in the metric
d
. Where the metric
d
is clear from context, we may drop it from the notation.

3.2.
Definition of IGS

We now define iterated graph systems based on edge replacements. A construction similar to the following definition has appeared earlier in
[
61
, Definition 2.1]
. For iterated graph systems based on replacing vertices, see
[
6
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

An
iterated graph system (IGS)
consists of a graph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
, together with the following data.

(1)

A finite set
I
called the
gluing set
.

(2)

Each edge
e=\{v,w\}
and endpoint point
v\in e
is associated with an injective mapping
\phi_{v,e}:I\to V_{1}
, whose image
I_{v,e}:=\phi_{v,e}(I)
is an independent set of
V_{1}
- that is, no edges between vertices in
I_{v,e}
.

(3)

We have
I_{v,e}\cap I_{w,e}=\emptyset
for each edge
e=\{v,w\}
.

The set
I
together with the maps
\phi_{v,e}
will be referred to as the gluing rules. In general, a graph
G=(V,E)
is said to be labeled by
G_{1}
if for every edge
e
of
G
and any
v\in e
, there is an associated injective mapping
\phi_{v,e}:I\to V_{1}
, whose image is an independent set. Given a labeled graph
G
we can form a replacement graph
\hat{G}=(\hat{V},\hat{E})
, whose vertices are
\hat{V}=V_{1}\times E/\sim
, where we identify

(\phi_{x,e}(v),e)\sim(\phi_{x,f}(v),f)

for every
e,f\in E
which share an end point
x\in V
and
v\in I
. Further, we define edge sets

\hat{E}=\{[(v,e)],[(w,e)]:\{v,w\}\in E_{1},e\in E\}.

This amounts to replacing each edge in
G
by a copy of
G_{1}
, which are glued along the images of the mappings
\phi_{v,e}
. We can also define a labeling for
\hat{G}
by

\phi_{[(v,e)],\{[(v,e)],[(w,e)]\}}=\phi_{v,\{v,w\}}

This
replacement rule
can be applied recursively to
G_{1}
to produce a sequence of graphs
G_{k}
: Set
G_{k+1}:=\hat{G_{k}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The edges and vertices of
G_{k}=(V_{k},E_{k})
can be described as follows.

(1)

Let
V_{k+1}=V_{1}\times E_{k}/\sim
, where we identify vertices with the relationships
(\phi_{x,e}(v),e)\sim(\phi_{x,f}(v),f)
for every
e,f\in E
which share an end point
x
and
v\in I
.

(2)

\{[v,e],[w,e]\}\in E_{k+1}
if
\{v,w\}\in E_{1}
.

(3)

\phi_{[v,e],\{[v,e],[w,e]\}}=\phi_{v,\{v,w\}}
.

Notice that in the notation of equivalence classes
[(v,e)]
, we drop the parenthesis and write
[v,e]
. When
(\phi_{x,e}(v),e)\sim(\phi_{x,f}(v),f)
, we say that the equivalence relation is given by the vertex
x
. We call the graphs
G_{k}
thus constructed
replacement graphs.

Remark 3.3
.

We have chosen the above fairly general framework in order to encompass a wide variety of examples. In all relevant examples of this paper, we will focus on a significantly simpler subclass of iterated graph systems. In our case, the graph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
is a fixed graph, and we have just two maps
\phi_{\pm}:I\to V_{1}
. The choice of mapping corresponds to a choice of orientation for an edge. An orientation for edges is a choice of an ordered pair
(v,w)
(or
(w,v)
), for each edge
e=\{v,w\}
, in which case we say that
e
is positively oriented from
v
and to
w
(or
w
to
v
). Given such an orientation, we define
\phi_{v,e}=\phi_{-}
if
e=\{v,w\}
is positively oriented from
v
, and
\phi_{w,e}=\phi_{+}
if
e
is positively oriented towards
w
. We call these types of constructions
oriented iterated graph systems
. One simple way to obtain an orientation for edges is by ordering vertices with a well-ordering and declaring
\{v,u\}
positively ordered if
v<u
. This will be the primary way we introduce an orientation in our examples.

This simplification reduces the number of different maps
\phi_{v,e}
one would need to consider below. Another simplification, which will hold in many, but not all, of our examples, is that there is a graph isomorphism
\eta_{e}:G_{1}\to G_{1}
with
\eta_{e}\circ\phi_{\mp}=\phi_{\pm}
. If an oriented IGS satisfies this condition, we say that the IGS is
symmetric
. For symmetric IGS, it does not matter how the edges are oriented to define maps
\phi_{v,e}
. Indeed, in such cases, the sequence of graphs produced with different orientations are isomorphic.

Given this independence on the orientation for symmetric IGSs, we have striven to use notation that avoids the use of orientations wherever possible. Indeed, the only instance where we need these orientations is in defining the explicit maps
\phi_{v,e}
in our examples. The desire to avoid orientations leads to the framework above and considering most of the time the maps
\phi_{v,e}
, instead of using the maps
\phi_{\pm}
together with an (arbitrary) orientation.

3.4.
Basic properties

Our assumptions on the iterated graph systems guarantee that equivalence relations are simple to describe.

Lemma 3.9
.

The relation
(\phi_{x,e}(v),e)\sim(\phi_{x,f}(v),f)
for every
e,f\in E
which share an end point
x
is an equivalence relation.

Proof.

The relation is clearly reflexive and symmetric. We check transitivity. If
(v_{1},e_{1})\sim(v_{2},e_{2})\sim(v_{3},e_{3})
, then
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
share an end point
x
, and
e_{2}
and
e_{3}
share an end point
y
. If
x=y
, then
v_{i}=\phi_{x,e_{i}}(v)
for
i=1,2,3
and
(v_{1},e_{1})\sim(v_{3},e_{3})
. If
x\neq y
, then
v_{2}=\phi_{x,e_{2}}(v)
and
v_{2}=\phi_{y,e_{2}}(w)
for some
v,w\in I
, but then
I_{x,e_{2}}\cap I_{y,e_{2}}\neq\emptyset
, which by (3) in Definition
3.2
is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

We define maps
\pi_{k+1}:V_{k+1}\cup E_{k+1}\to V_{k}\cup E_{k}
as follows. For each vertex
[(v,e)]\in V_{k+1}
define
\pi_{k+1}([(v,e)])=e
if

for any
w\in e
, and otherwise set
\pi_{k+1}([(v,e)])=w
if
v\in I_{w,e}
. In Figure
3.4
, the vertices marked with squares are mapped to vertices, and vertices marked with circles are mapped to edges. In other words, vertices in gluing sets are mapped to vertices, and all other vertices are mapped to the edges which gave rise to them.

The map
\pi_{k+1}
is easier to define for edges. Each edge in the replaced graph arises from some edge. Indeed, we set
\pi_{k+1}(\{[(v,e)],[(w,e)]\})=e
for every edge

. Because

is an independent set for each

, the mapping

is well defined.

Lemma 3.10
.

The mapping
\pi_{k}
is well-defined, and
\pi_{k}:E_{k}\to E_{k-1}
for all
k\geq 1
, and
\pi_{k}^{-1}(V_{k-1})\subset V_{k}
.

Proof.

First, we check the equivalence class for vertices. For the well-definedness it suffices to verify that if

, for

, then the map is well defined. In this case,

and

share a unique end point

, and thus in both cases

∎

3.5.

For each

Proposition 3.11
.

For every

and

, the image of

then
\{v,u\}\in E_{m}
if and only if
\{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\sigma_{e,m}(u)\}\in E_{n+m}
and

\phi_{v,\{v,u\}}=\phi_{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\sigma_{e,m}(u)\}}.

(SM2)

For distinct edges

the subsets

and
f\cdot G_{m}
intersect if and only if
e,f
have a common vertex
v
.
Moreover, their intersection is

\sigma_{e,m}\left(I_{v,e}^{(m)}\right)=\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(v)=\sigma_{f,m}\left(I_{v,f}^{(m)}\right)

the set of ancestors of
v
.

(SM3)

For every

we have
e\cdot E_{m}=\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(e)
.
In particular,
\{e\cdot E_{m}\}_{e\in E_{n}}
is a partition of
E_{n+m}
.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we will prove the existence of the mapping
\sigma_{e,m}
by induction on
m
. The case
m=1
follows by setting
\sigma_{e,1}:V_{1}\to V_{n+1},z\mapsto[z,e]
for all

and
e\in E_{n}
.

Assume that
m\geq 1
and we have constructed
\sigma_{e,m}:V_{m}\to V_{n+m}
for all

that satisfies
(SM1)
,
(SM2)
and
(SM3)
. We define
\sigma_{e,m+1}:V_{m+1}\to V_{n+m+1}
by

\sigma_{e,m+1}([z,\{v,u\}])=[z,\{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\sigma_{e,m}(u)\}],

where each vertex of

can be represented by an equivalence class
[z,\{v,u\}]
for some edge

and some vertex

. By the induction hypothesis (IH) we get
\phi_{v,\{v,u\}}=\phi_{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\{\sigma_{e,m}(v),\sigma_{e,m}(u)\}}

is a well-defined. The fact that

is an injection can be seen as follows. Suppose that
\sigma_{e,m+1}([z_{1},\{v_{1},u_{1}\}])=\sigma_{e,m+1}([z_{2},\{v_{2},u_{2}\}])

\displaystyle V_{n+m+1}

3.6.

We will next consider the problem of defining natural metrics on the sequence of graphs produced by an IGS. There are many ways to do this, but with the following assumption the construction becomes easier to describe.

x\in I_{v,e},y\in I_{u,e}
we have
d_{G_{1}}(x,y)=L_{*}
.

It is easy to construct examples of IGSs with this property. Indeed, all examples in this paper satisfy it, and the value of
L_{*}
is readily computable.

Throughout this paper,
C_{{\rm diam}}
denotes the diameter of the graph
G_{1}
equipped with the path metric
d_{G_{1}}
and
C_{\deg}:=\deg(G_{1})
denotes the maximum degree the graph
G_{1}
.
Clearly
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property implies
L_{*}\leq C_{{\rm diam}}
, however, in general, we may have
L_{*}<C_{{\rm diam}}
.
We equip the set
V_{n}
with the metric
d_{n}:=L_{*}^{-n}\cdot d_{G_{n}}
which is the path metric on
G_{n}
scaled by
L_{*}^{-n}
.
Furthermore define the semi-metric
d_{n}
on
E_{n}
by

d_{n}(e,f):=\min_{x\in e,y\in f}d_{n}(x,y).

We also write
d_{n}(e,y)=d_{n}(y,e):=\min_{x\in e}d_{n}(x,y)
for
y\in V_{n}
and
e\in E_{n}
.

Lemma 3.18
.

Assume that the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property and fix
n\in\mathbb{N},m\geq 0
.
If
x_{n+m},y_{n+m}\in V_{n+m}
are ancestors of vertices
x_{n},y_{n}\in V_{n}
respectively, then the following hold.

(DL1)

If
x_{n}\neq y_{n}
then
d_{n+m}(x_{n+m},y_{n+m})=d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})
.

(DL2)

If
x_{n}=y_{n}
then
d_{n+m}(x_{n+m},y_{n+m})\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
.

(DL3)

Suppose
e_{n}\in E_{n}
so that
x\in e_{n}\cdot G_{m}
.
If
x_{n}\in e_{n}
and
\widehat{x}_{n}\in V_{n+m}
is an ancestor of
x_{n}\in V_{n}
then

d_{n+m}(x,\widehat{x}_{n})\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
.

Proof.

We will first consider
(DL1)
.
It is sufficient to prove the case
m=1
.
Let
\theta_{n+1}=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
be a path in
G_{n+1}
from
x_{n+1}
to
y_{n+1}
.
By Proposition
3.16
, there is a path
[u_{1},\dots u_{l}]
from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
and for
i=1,\dots,l-1
pairwise disjoint sub-paths
\hat{\theta}_{i}
from
\pi_{n}^{-1}(u_{i})
to
\pi_{n}^{-1}(u_{i+1})
which are contained in
\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}\cdot G_{1}
.
By unifrom scaling property,
{\rm len}(\hat{\theta}_{i})\geq L_{*}
for all
i
, which gives

{\rm len}(\theta)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}{\rm len}\left(\hat{\theta}_{i}\right)\geq L_{*}(l-1)\geq L_{*}^{1+n}\cdot d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n}).

Since the path
\theta
was arbitary, we have
d_{n+1}(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\geq d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})
.

To prove the other inequality, let
\theta_{n}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{l}]
be a shortest path from
x_{n}
to
y_{n}
.
For every
1\leq i\leq l
fix an ancestor
v^{(i)}
of
u_{i}
so that
v^{(1)}=x_{n+1}
and
v^{(l)}=y_{n+1}
.
By uniform scaling property, for each
1\leq i\leq l-1
, there is a path
\theta^{(i)}
from
v^{(i)}
to
v^{(i+1)}
of length
L_{*}
.
By joining these paths, we obtain a path
\theta_{n+1}
from
x_{n+1}
to
y_{n+1}
of length

{\rm len}(\theta_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}{\rm len}\left(\theta^{(i)}\right)=L_{*}\cdot(l-1)=L_{*}^{n+1}\cdot d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})

which yields
d_{n+1}(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})\leq d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})
.

Next we move to
(DL2)
. Without loss of generality assume
x_{n+m}\neq y_{n+m}
.
Let
1\leq k\leq m
be the smallest integer so that

x_{n+k}:=\pi_{n+m,n+k}(x_{n+m})\neq y_{m+k}:=\pi_{n+m,n+k}(y_{n+m}).

Since
\pi_{n+k}(x_{n+k})=\pi_{n+k}(y_{n+k})
,
x_{n+k},y_{n+k}\in e\cdot G_{1}
for some
e\in E_{n+k-1}
. Hence

d_{n+m}(x_{n+m},y_{n+m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{=}}d_{n+k}(x_{n+k},y_{n+k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{SM1}}}{{\leq}}C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n+k)}\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}

Lastly we prove
(DL3)
.
We choose edges
e_{n+i}\in E_{n+i}
for
0\leq i\leq m-1
so that
x\in e_{n+i}\cdot G_{m-i}

and
\pi_{n+i+1}(e_{n+i+1})=e_{n+i}
.
For every
0\leq i\leq m-1
we also choose
x_{n+i}\in e_{n+i}
.
Then we fix the ancestors
\widetilde{x}_{n+i}\in V_{n+i+1}
of
x_{n+i}
and their ancestors
\widehat{x}_{n+i}\in V_{n+m}
.
For every
0\leq i\leq m-1
, as
x_{n+i+1},\widetilde{x}_{n+i}\in e_{n+i}\cdot G_{1}
, there is a path of length at most
C_{{\rm diam}}
between these two vertices. Therefore
d_{n+i+1}(x_{n+i+1},\widetilde{x}_{n+i})\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n+i+1)}
.
By
(DL1)
and
(DL2)
we have

d_{n+m}(\widehat{x}_{n+i},\widehat{x}_{n+i+1})\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{(-n+i+1)}.

Similarly
d_{n+m}(x,\widehat{x}_{n+m-1})\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n+m)}
.
We now obtain

\displaystyle d_{n+m}(x,\widehat{x}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq d_{n+m}(x,\widehat{x}_{n+m-1})+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}d_{n+m}(\widehat{x}_{n+i},\widehat{x}_{n+i+1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n+i+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}.

∎

Corollary 3.19
.

If
n>m\in\mathbb{N}
then the following hold.

(DL4)

d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f))\leq d_{n}(e,f)
.

(DL5)

d_{n}(e,f)\leq d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f))+2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}
.

(DL6)

1\leq{\rm diam}(G_{n},d_{n})\leq 2\cdot C_{{\rm diam}}
.

Proof.

(DL4)
is clear if
\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f)
share a common vertex.
Otherwise, let
x\in e
and
y\in f
be such that
d_{n}(e,f)=d_{n}(x,y).
If
\theta
is a path connecting
x
to
y
, then by Proposition
3.16
,
\theta
contains a sub-path from an ancestors
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{y}
of some
x_{e}\in\pi_{n,m}(e)
and
x_{f}\in\pi_{n,m}(f)
respectively.
Hence

d_{n}(e,f)=d_{n}(x,y)\geq d_{n}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{=}}d_{m}(x_{e},x_{f})\geq d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f)).

To prove
(DL5)
, suppose
x\in\pi_{n,m}(e),y\in\pi_{n,m}(f)
so that

d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f))=d_{m}(x,y).

If
x=y
, then the claim follows from
(DL3)
.
Otherwise, pick
\widehat{x},\widehat{y}
ancestors of
x,y
respectively.
Then

\displaystyle d_{n}(e,f)

\displaystyle\leq d_{n}(e,\widehat{x})+d_{n}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})+d_{n}(\widehat{y},f)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (3)}}}{{\leq}}2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}+d_{n}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{=}}2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}+d_{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}+d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(e),\pi_{n,m}(f)).

Lastly, we prove
(DL6)
. The lower bound follows from
(DL1)
. Let
x,y\in V_{n}
and choose
e,f\in E_{1}
so that
x\in e\cdot G_{n-1}
and
y\in f\cdot G_{n-1}
. Then choose
\tilde{x}\in e,\tilde{y}\in f
and a path
[z_{1},\dots,z_{k}]
from
\tilde{x}
to
\tilde{y}
in
G_{n}
of length at most
C_{{\rm diam}}
. By choosing ancestors of
\widehat{z}_{i}\in V_{n}
of
z_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{n}(x,\widehat{z}_{1})+d_{n}(\widehat{z}_{k},y)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_{n}(\widehat{z}_{i},\widehat{z}_{i+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{=}}d_{n}(x,\widehat{z}_{1})+d_{n}(\widehat{z}_{k},y)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d_{1}(z_{i},z_{i+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (3)}}}{{\leq}}2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}^{-1}+C_{{\rm diam}}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}.

∎

3.7.
Doubling, regularity and limit space

A metric space
(X,d)
is said to be
metrically doubling
if there exists a constant
N
so that for each
x\in X
and every
r>0
, there exists points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
so that
B(x,r)\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i},r/2)
. We say that a sequence
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is
uniformly doubling
, if each space in the sequence if
N
-doubling for a fixed
N
. The following definition gives a simple necessary and sufficient condition for an IGS to produce a uniformly doubling sequence of graphs.

Definition 3.20
.

An iterated graph system is
doubling
, if for every

e=\{v,u\}\in E_{1}
and
z\in I_{v,e}
we have
{\rm deg}(z)=1
.
For such
z\in I_{v,e}
we set
\mathfrak{n}(z)
to be the unique neighbour of
z
and for the corresponding edge we use the notation
\mathfrak{e}_{z}
.

Lemma 3.21
.

If the iterated graph system is doubling, then
\deg(G_{n})=C_{\deg}
for all
n
.
Furthermore, if the iterated graph system also satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property, then the metric spaces
(G_{n},d_{n})
are doubling with the doubling constant
N_{D}=N_{D}(C_{\deg},C_{{\rm diam}},L_{*})
.

Proof.

It follows from
(SM1)
that
\deg(G_{n})\geq C_{\deg}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We prove by induction, that
\deg(G_{n})=C_{\deg}
.
The base case
n=1
is trivial so we assume it holds for
n
.
If
x\in V_{n+1}
and
x
is not an ancestor of any
x_{n}\in V_{n}
, by
(SM1)
and
(SM2)
,
\deg(x)\leq\deg(G_{1})=C_{\deg}
.
On the other hand, if
x
is an anscestor of
x_{n}\in V_{n}
, then, by the doubling property of the IGS,
\deg(x)=\deg(x_{n})\leq C_{\deg}
.

Next we prove the metric doubling property of
d_{n}
.
Fix
x\in V_{n}
and
r>0
.
First assume
r\leq 4C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
.
Then every vertex
y\in B(x,r)
can be reached from
x
by a path of length at most
4C_{{\rm diam}}
.
Hence
\lvert B(x,r)\rvert\leq(C_{\deg})^{4C_{{\rm diam}}}
so in this case we may set
N_{D}=(C_{\deg})^{4C_{{\rm diam}}}
. On the other hand, if
r\geq 4C_{{\rm diam}}
, then
B(x,r/2)=V_{n}
so we can choose
N_{D}=1
. Hence we may assume that

4C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}<r\leq 4C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m+1}

for some
m=1,\dots,n
.
Fix
\widetilde{x}\in V_{n}
so that it has an ancestor in
V_{m}
and

d_{n}(x,\widetilde{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (3)}}}{{\leq}}C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}<r/4.

Similarly, for each
y\in B(x,r)
, we choose
\widehat{y}\in V_{n}
having an ancestor in
V_{m}
and

d_{n}(y,\widehat{y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (3)}}}{{\leq}}C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}<r/4.

Then

d_{m}(\pi_{n,m}(\widetilde{x}),\pi_{n,m}(\widehat{y}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{\leq}}d_{n}(\widetilde{x},\widehat{y})\leq r/2+d_{n}(x,y)<2r

which gives

S:=\{\pi_{n,m}(\widehat{y})\in V_{m}:y\in B(x,r)\}\subseteq B(\pi_{n,m}(\widetilde{x}),2r).

Since
2r\leq 8C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}
,
S
contains at most
(C_{\deg})^{8C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}}
vertices.
Lastly, for every
v\in S
we choose
x_{v}\in\pi_{n+m}^{-1}(v)
.
Then for any
y\in B(x,r)
, we have

d_{n}(\widehat{y},x_{\pi_{n,m}({\widehat{y}})})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (2)}}}{{\leq}}C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}<r/4

and we finally obtain

B(x,r)\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in B(x,r)}B(\widehat{y},r/4)\subseteq\bigcup_{v\in S}B(x_{v},r/2)

so we may set
N_{D}=(C_{\deg})^{8C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}}
.
∎

Next, we will show that there is a fractal space
X
which can be obtained as a Gromov-Hausdorff limit of the sequence
(G_{n},d_{n})
. This limit space has a symbolic description, which we now give.

Definition 3.22
.

Given an iterated graph system, we define

\Sigma:=\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}:e_{i}\in E_{i}\text{ and }\pi_{m}(e_{m})=e_{m-1}\}.

For
e\in E_{n}
, we define the subsets
\Sigma_{e}\subseteq\Sigma
by

\Sigma_{e}:=\left\{(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma:e_{n}=e\right\}.

We give
\Sigma
the natural metric

d_{\Sigma}((e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }e_{i}=f_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}\\
2^{-\max\left\{k\ :\ (e_{i})_{i=1}^{k}=(f_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\right\}}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and the Radon measure
\mu_{\Sigma}
, for which

\mu_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{e})=\frac{1}{\lvert E_{1}\rvert^{n}}

for all
e\in E_{n}
.

Remark 3.23
.

The measure is well-defined by Kolmogorov’s extension theorem and Proposition
3.11
. There is also an alternative way to construct
\mu_{\Sigma}
. The space
\Sigma
and can be identified as
(E_{1})^{\mathbb{N}}
by identifying
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
, with
\left(\hat{e}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}\in(E_{1})^{\mathbb{N}}
, where

(1)

e_{1}=\hat{e}_{1}

(2)

\hat{e}_{n+1}=\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
, where
e_{n+1}=\{[z_{1},e_{n}],[z_{2},e_{n}]\}
.

Let
T:(E_{1})^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Sigma
be the map
T(\left(\hat{e}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty})=(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, and let
\nu
be the Bernoulli measure on
(E_{1})^{\mathbb{N}}
where each element in
E_{1}
has equal measure
\lvert E_{1}\rvert^{-1}
. Then the measure can also be constructed as the push-forward
\mu_{\Sigma}=(T)_{*}(\nu)
, where
T_{*}
is the push-forward operation for measures.

Definition 3.24
.

The
limit space
of an iterated graph system is

X:=\Sigma/\sim,\text{ where }(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\sim(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\iff e_{i}\cap f_{i}\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }i

and is given the metric

(3.25)

d_{X}([(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]):=\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{n}(e_{n},f_{n}).

The canonical projection
\Sigma\to X
is denoted by
\chi

and the push-forward measure on
X
is denoted as
\mu_{X}:=\chi_{*}(\mu_{\Sigma})
.
We also denote
X_{e}:=\chi(\Sigma_{e})
.

The limit in (
3.25
) exists and it defines a metric by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.26
.

The function
d_{X}
is a well-defined metric on
X
and
\chi:(\Sigma,d_{\Sigma})\to(X,d_{X})
is a continuous function.

Proof.

Given any pair of sequences
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
, by
(DL4)
and
(DL6)
,
the sequence
d_{n}(e_{n},f_{n})
converges as it is increasing and bounded.
Moreover,
d_{n}
defines a semi-metric on
E_{n}
so that
d_{n}(e,f)=0
if and only if
e
and
f
share a vertex. Therefore
d_{X}
is a well-defined metric on
X
.
The continuity of
\chi
follows from
(DL5)
.
∎

The following lemma is immediate from
(DL4)
and
(DL5)
.

Lemma 3.27
.

Let
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],y=[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
be points in
X
. Then the following hold.

(DL7)

d_{X}(x,y)\geq d_{n}(e_{n},f_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(DL8)

If
e_{m}
and
f_{m}
have a common vertex then

d_{X}(x,y)\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}.

In particular,
{\rm diam}(X_{e})\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}
for all
e\in E_{m}
.

In summary, we can now show that the spaces
(G_{n},d_{n})
Gromov-Hausdorff converge to
X
. First, we give the relevant definitions. An
\epsilon
-isometry is an isometry (i.e. a map which preserves distances) between metric spaces
f:X\to Z
, for which
Z\subset\bigcup_{x\in X}B(\iota_{X}(x),\epsilon)
.
The
Gromov-Hausdorff metric
between
X,Y
is defined as

\displaystyle d_{GH}(X,Y):=\inf\{\epsilon>0:

\displaystyle\exists\text{ a metric space }Z

\displaystyle\text{ and $\epsilon$-isometries }\iota_{X}:X\to Z,\iota_{Y}:Y\to Z\}.

A metric space
X
is said to be
geodesic
, if for all
x,y\in X
there exists a rectifiable curve
\gamma
connecting
x
to
y
with
d(x,y)=\ell(\gamma)
, where
\ell(\gamma)
is the length of
\gamma
. See
[
22
]
for background on these notions.

Proposition 3.28
.

If the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling- and the doubling property, then the sequence of metric spaces
\{(G_{n},d_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
Gromov-Hausdorff converges to
X
. Moreover,
X
is compact, geodesic and satisfies the metric-doubling property.

Proof.

Consider the metric spaces
(G_{n},d_{n})
together with their geometric realizations, which are obtained by gluing edges of length
L_{*}^{-n}
between adjacent vertices. Notice that the Gromov-Hausdorff distance of
(G_{n},d_{n})
to its geometric realization is
L_{*}^{-n}
, since we can choose
Z
as the geometric realization and use the inclusion maps in the definition of the Gromov-Hausdorff metric. Thus, if the metric space
(G_{n},d_{n})
converge to
X
, so do the geometric realizations. On the other hand, the geometric realizations are compact, geodesic and uniformly doubling (by Lemma
3.21
), so it follows that if
\{(G_{n},d_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
Gromov-Hausdorff converges to
X
, then
X
is compact, geodesic, and satisfies metric doubling property; see e.g.
[
22
, Theorem 7.5.1]
.

We will construct
\epsilon(n)
-isometries
i_{G_{n}}:V_{n}\to X
so that
\epsilon(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
First for every
x\in V_{m}
we choose ancestors
\widehat{x}\in V_{m}
of
x
and define the map
i_{m,n}:V_{m}\to V_{n},i_{m,n}(x)=\widehat{x}
.
By
(DL1)

i_{m,n}
is an isometry and,
moreover, we can choose the ancestors so that for all
m>n>l
we have

(3.29)

i_{n,l}\circ i_{m,n}=i_{m,l}\text{ and }\pi_{l,n}\circ i_{m,l}=i_{m,n}.

Next we construct maps
i_{m,n}^{E}:V_{m}\to E_{n}
for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
n\leq m
we define
i_{n,m}^{E}:V_{n}\to E_{m}
by choosing an edge
e_{x}
for every
x\in V_{n}
containing
i_{n,m}(x)
.
For
n>m
we define
i_{n,m}^{E}:V_{n}\to E_{m}
by

i_{n,m}^{E}(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{n,m}(x)&\text{ if }\pi_{n,m}(x)\in E_{m}\\
e_{x}&\text{ if }\pi_{n,m}(x)\in V_{m},\end{cases}

where
e_{x}\in E_{m}
is an edge containing
\pi_{n,m}(x)\in V_{m}
.
By (
3.29
) we may choose the edges
e_{x}
so that

(3.30)

\pi_{n,k}\circ i_{m,n}^{E}=i_{m,k}^{E}.

We now define the mappings
i_{G_{n}}:V_{n}\to X
by
i(x)=[(i_{n,i}(x))_{i=1}^{\infty}]
.
Indeed, by (
3.30
),
(i_{n,i}(x))_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
for all
x\in V_{n}
so
i_{G_{n}}
is well-defined.
We will prove that
i_{G_{n}}
is a
2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
-isometry.
Choose distinct vertices
x,y\in V_{n}
.
Since the edges
i_{n,m}^{E}(x),i_{n,m}^{E}(y)\in E_{m}
contain the ancestors
i_{n,m}(x),i_{n,m}(y)\in V_{m}
of
x,y
respectively, we have by
(DL3)

\displaystyle d_{m}(i_{n,m}(x),i_{n,m}(y))-2C_{\rm diam}\cdot L_{*}^{-m}

\displaystyle\leq d_{m}(i_{n,m}^{E}(x),i_{n,m}^{E}(y))

\displaystyle\leq d_{m}(i_{n,m}(x),i_{n,m}(y))

\displaystyle=d_{n}(x,y).

By letting
m\to\infty
, we have

d_{X}(i_{G_{n}}(x),i_{G_{n}}(y))=\lim_{m\to\infty}d_{m}(i_{n,m}(x),i_{n,m}(y))=d_{n}(x,y).

Lastly, if
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X
and
x_{n}\in e_{n}
, then

d_{X}(x,i_{G_{n}}(x_{n}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{lemma: properties of d_X (2)})}}{{\leq}}2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}.

Hence
i_{G_{n}}
is a
2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
-isometry. This gives
d_{GH}((G_{n},d_{n}),X)\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
and thus
X
is the Gromov-Hausdorff limit of the spaces
G_{n}
.
∎

We can also compute that
X
is Ahlfors regular, and identify the Hausdorff dimension of

.

Lemma 3.31
.

If the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling- and doubling properties, then the metric measure space
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})

is
Q
-Ahlfors regular with

Q:=\frac{\log(\lvert E_{1}\rvert)}{\log(L_{*})}.

Proof.

Fix
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X
and
r\in(0,{\rm diam}(X)]
. Then

r\leq{\rm diam}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{corollary: Distance between edges (3)}}}{{\leq}}2C_{{\rm diam}}

so that for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}<r\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n-1)}
.
Now if
y=[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]
so that
f_{n}=e_{n}
,
then, by
(DL8)
,
d_{X}(x,y)\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}
.
Hence
X_{e_{n}}\subseteq B(x,r)

which yields

\mu_{X}(B(x,r))\geq\mu_{X}(X_{e_{n}})\geq\mu_{\Sigma}(\Sigma_{e_{n}})=\frac{1}{\lvert E_{1}\rvert^{n}}\geq(2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*})^{-Q}\cdot r^{Q}.

Next we prove the upper bound. Let
f_{n}^{(1)},\dots,f_{n}^{(k)}\in E_{n}
be the edges

so that
\chi^{-1}(B(x,r))\cap\Sigma_{f}\neq\emptyset
.
Choose
y=[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in B(x,r)
. Then

d_{n}(e_{n},f_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{lemma: properties of d_X (1)}}}{{\leq}}d_{X}(x,y)<r\leq 2C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{-(n-1)}=2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}\cdot L_{*}^{-n}.

Hence
f_{n}
can be reached from
e_{n}
by a path of length at most
2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}
.
This yields
k\leq 2(C_{\deg})^{2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}}
and further

\displaystyle\mu_{X}(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{\Sigma}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\Sigma_{f_{n}^{(i)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\deg})^{2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}}\cdot\frac{1}{\lvert E\rvert^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\deg})^{2C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}}(2C_{{\rm diam}})^{Q}\cdot r^{Q}.

∎

3.8.
Approximate Self-similarity

Our spaces are constructed by a self-similar procedure. Thus, beyond a minor combinatorial issue, it is not too difficult to obtain approximate self-similarity; recall Definition
2.1
. Our proof utilizes a condition similar to that of the finite type condition of an iterated function system. This condition is also called a finite intersection condition.

Definition 3.32
.

Suppose the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling- and doubling properties.
The
fundamental neighbourhood
of
e\in E_{n}
is the induced sub-graph
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)=(V(e),E(e))\subseteq G_{n}
so that

V(e)=\left\{x\in V_{n}:d_{G_{n}}(x,e)\leq 2\right\}.

Notice that in above the distance is the un-scaled path metric.
Furthermore, we define the
extended fundamental neighbourhood
of
e\in E_{n}
to be the induced sub-graph
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)=\left(\widehat{V}(e),\widehat{E}(e)\right)\subseteq G_{n}
so that

\widehat{V}(e)=\left\{x\in V_{n}:d_{G_{n}}(x,e)\leq 10C_{{\rm diam}}\right\}.

We say that the fundamental neighbourhoods
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e),{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)
of
e=\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\in E_{n}
and
f=\{x_{2},y_{2}\}\in E_{m}
are
equivalent
if there is a graph isomorphism
\beta:\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)\to\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(f)
between the extended fundamental neighbourhoods satisfying

(1)

\{x_{2},y_{2}\}=\{\beta(x_{1}),\beta(y_{1})\}
and

(2)

\phi_{x,\{x,y\}}=\phi_{\beta(x),\{\beta(x),\beta(y)\}}
for all
\{x,y\}\in\widehat{E}(e)
.

We also say that
\beta
is an isomorphism between the fundamental neighbourhoods
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
and
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)
.
The set of equivalence classes of equivalent fundamental neighbourhoods is denoted as
\mathcal{N}
.

The idea behind equivalent fundamental neighborhoods is their ”similarity” on all scales. Indeed, if
e\in V_{n}
, we define the sub-graphs
\mathcal{N}_{k}(e)=(V_{k}(e),E_{k}(e))
and
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{k}(e)=\left(\widehat{V}_{k}(e),\widehat{E}_{k}(e)\right)
of
G_{n+k}
where

E_{k}(e)=\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(E(e))\text{ and }V_{n}(e)=\bigcup_{f\in V(e)}f\cdot G_{k}

and similarly

\widehat{E}_{k}(e)=\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}\left(\widehat{E}(e)\right)\text{ and }\widehat{V}_{k}(e)=\bigcup_{f\in\widehat{V}(e)}f\cdot G_{k}.

Now given an isomorphism
\beta:\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)\to\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(f)
between the fundamental neighbourhoods of
e
and
f
, we define the mappings
\beta_{k}:\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}_{k}(e)\to\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}_{k}(f)
inductively:

(1)

\beta_{0}=\beta
.

(2)

If
\beta_{k}
is defined then
\beta_{k+1}([z,\{x,y\}])=[z,\{\beta_{k}(x),\beta_{k}(y)\}]
.

The corresponding map between edges
\widehat{E}_{k}(e)\to\widehat{E}_{k}(f)
is denoted as
\beta_{k}^{E}
.

In the following lemma, recall that
d_{G_{n}}
was the un-scaled path metric on
G_{n}
, and
d_{n}=L_{*}^{-n}d_{G_{n}}
.

Lemma 3.33
.

If
\beta:\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)\to\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(f)
is an isomorphism between the fundamental neighbourhoods of
e\in E_{n}
and
f\in E_{m}
then the mappings
\beta_{k}
are graph isomorphisms. These maps satisfy

\pi_{m+k+1}\circ\beta_{k+1}^{E}=\beta_{k}^{E}\circ\pi_{n+k+1}

for all
k\geq 0
. Furthermore, if the IGS satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property and the doubling property, then the restriction
\beta_{k}:{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(e)\to{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(f)
are isometries with respect to the (un-scaled) path metrics
d_{G_{n+k}}
and
d_{G_{m+k}}
respectively.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
3.11
, it is a straightforward inductive argument that
\phi_{x,\{x,y\}}=\phi_{\beta_{k}(x),\{\beta_{k}(x),\beta_{k}(y)\}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{x,y\}\in E_{k}(e)
. This observation yields that
\beta_{k}
is a graph isomorphism. Moreover, the commutativity with the projections follows from

\displaystyle\pi_{m+k+1}(\beta_{k+1}^{E}(\{[z_{1},e],[z_{2},e]\}))

\displaystyle=\pi_{m+k+1}(\{[z_{1},\beta_{k}^{E}(e)],[z_{2},\beta_{k}^{E}(e)]\})

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}^{E}(e)

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}^{E}(\pi_{n+k+1}(\{[z_{1},e],[z_{2},e]\})).

To prove that its restritcions
\beta_{k}:{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(e)\to{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(f)
are isometries, we will show that a shortest path in
G_{n+k}
between vertices in
{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(e)
is contained in
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}_{k}(e)
. By applying the same argument to the vertices in
{\rm\mathcal{N}}_{k}(f)
, we would be done.

First, if
e^{\prime}\in E(e)
, then
d_{G_{n}}(e,e^{\prime})\leq 2
, which gives
{\rm diam}(\mathcal{N}_{k}(e),d_{G_{n+k}})\leq 8C_{{\rm diam}}L_{*}^{k}
by
(DL5)
and the fact that
d_{n+k}=L_{*}^{-(n+k)}d_{G_{n+k}}
.
Next, suppose
\theta
is a path in
G_{n+k}
containing a vertex
x\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(e)
with
{\rm diam}(\theta)\leq 8C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{k}
. We will show that indeed
\theta
is contained in
\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)
. Suppose not. Then
\theta
contains a vertex in
e^{\prime}\cdot G_{k}
where
d_{G_{n}}(e,e^{\prime})\geq 10C_{{\rm diam}}
. This, by Proposition
3.16
, gives that there are
y\in e,y^{\prime}\in e^{\prime}
and a sub-path
\theta^{\prime}
from
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(y)
to
\pi_{n+k,n}^{-1}(y^{\prime})
. Hence

{\rm len}(\theta)\geq{\rm len}(\theta^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref*{lemma: Distance of ancestors (1)}}}{{\geq}}L_{*}^{n+k}\cdot d_{n}(y,y^{\prime})=L_{*}^{k}\cdot d_{G_{n}}(y,y^{\prime})\geq 10C_{{\rm diam}}\cdot L_{*}^{k}>{\rm diam}(\theta).

This yields the contradiction so we are done.
∎

The previous lemma has the following immediate corollary. A map
h:(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
between metric spaces is called a homothety if there exists a constant
s
so that
d_{Y}(h(x),h(y))=s\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

Corollary 3.34
.

Let
\beta:\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(e)\to\widehat{{\rm\mathcal{N}}}(f)
be an isomorphism between the fundamental neighbourhoods of
e\in E_{n}
and
f\in E_{m}
.
Then the mapping

\beta_{\infty}:\bigcup_{e^{\prime}\in{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)}X_{e^{\prime}}\to\bigcup_{f^{\prime}\in{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)}X_{f^{\prime}},\quad[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\mapsto[(f_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}],

where

f_{i}=\begin{cases}\beta_{k}^{E}(e_{n+k})&\text{ if }i=m+k\text{ for }k\geq 0,\\
\pi_{m,i}(\beta(e_{n}))&\text{ if }i<m\end{cases}

is a homothety with the constant
L_{*}^{n-m}
.

Proof.

Let
\left(e_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty},\left(e_{i}^{\prime}\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Sigma
so that
e_{n},e_{n}^{\prime}\in{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
.
By Lemma
3.33
we have

d_{n+k}(e_{n+k},e_{n+k}^{\prime})\cdot L_{*}^{n+k}=d_{m+k}(\beta_{k}^{E}(e_{n+k}),\beta(e_{n+k}^{\prime}))\cdot L_{*}^{m+k}

which proves that
\beta_{\infty}
is a homothety.
∎

Proposition 3.35
.

If the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling- and doubling properties, then the limit space

is approximately self-similar.

Proof.

It follows from the doubling property of the IGS that the set
{\rm\mathcal{N}}
of equivalence classes of equivalent fundamental neighbourhoods is finite.
In particular, there is
m_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
so that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
e\in E_{n}
, the fundamental neighbourhood
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e)
is equivalent to
{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)
for some
f\in E_{m}
so that
m\leq m_{*}
.

Let
x=[(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}]\in X,r>0
and set
B=B(x,r)
. If

then we choose the identity
{\rm id}_{B}:B(x,r)\to B(x,r)
as our scaling map. Otherwise, suppose
L_{*}^{-(n+1)}<r\leq L_{*}^{-n}
where

. Let
f\in E_{m}
so that
m\leq m_{*}
and
\beta:{\rm\mathcal{N}}(e_{n})\to{\rm\mathcal{N}}(f)
an isomorphism between fundamental neighbourhoods of
e_{n}
and

. If
\beta_{\infty}
is as in Corollary
3.34
then, by
(DL7)
-
(DL8)
, we have

and

4.
Modulus on graphs

4.1.
Edge- and vertex modulus

Ultimately, we will be interested in establishing the combinatorial Loewner property for the limit spaces
X
. We already defined in Subsection
2.2
a notion combinatorial moduli for
X
with respect to an
\alpha
-approximations. In Proposition
3.37
we saw that
G_{m}
can be identified with such an
\alpha
-approximation. For computational reasons, it will be useful to consider two additional notions of modulus on
G_{m}
: the edge modulus and the vertex modulus. We will shortly see that these notions of moduli are comparable.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
G=(V,E)
a graph. If
\theta=[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]
is a non-constant path in
G
and
\rho:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we write

L_{\rho}(\theta):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho(\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\})\text{ and }\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho):=\sum_{e\in E}\rho(e)^{p}.

Given a family of paths
\Theta
in
G
, we say that
\rho
is
\Theta
-admissible
if
L_{\rho}(\theta)\geq 1
for all
\theta\in\Theta
. Furthermore, we denote the
(edge) modulus
of
\Theta
by

\displaystyle{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G):=\inf_{\rho}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)

where the infimum is over all
\Theta
-admissible densities
\rho:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
For any density
\rho:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
the
support
of
\rho
is
{\rm supp}(\rho):=\{e\in E:\rho(e)\neq 0\}
.

In the following definition, we say that a subset
\theta\subset G
is connected, if its induced sub-graph is connected. Most of the time we will perform calculations for the edge modulus. However, in relating the estimates to the modulus of Bourdon and Kleiner, we need to momentarily also discuss vertex modulus.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
G=(V,E)
a graph. If
\theta\subseteq V
is a non-empty connected set in
G
and
\rho:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we write

L_{\rho}(\theta):=\sum_{v\in\theta}\rho(v)\text{ and }\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho):=\sum_{v\in V}\rho(v)^{p}.

Given a family of paths
\Theta
in
G
, we say that
\rho
is
\Theta
-admissible
if
L_{\rho}(\theta)\geq 1
for all
\theta\in\Theta
. Furthermore, we denote the
(vertex) modulus
of
\Theta
by

\displaystyle{\rm Mod}_{p}^{V}(\Theta,G):=\inf_{\rho}\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)

where the infimum is over all
\Theta
-admissible densities
\rho:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Given a density
\rho^{E}:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, one can easily define a density
\rho^{V}:\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by considering the density of the edges adjacent to a given vertex and vice versa. This standard argument gives the comparability of edge- and vertex modulus. Given a path
\theta=[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]
in
G
, we call
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
the corresponding set in
V
. For simple paths
\theta
we use
\theta
also to denote the corresponding set.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\Theta
a non-empty family of non-constant paths so that each path in
\Theta
contains a simple sub-path in
\Theta
, and let
\hat{\Theta}
be the corresponding subsets for each path. Then

(4.4)

C^{-1}\cdot{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)\leq{\rm Mod}_{p}^{V}(\hat{\Theta},G)\leq C^{-1}\cdot{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)

where
C=C(p,\deg(G))
. In particular, (
4.4
) holds if
\Theta=\Theta(A,B)
for non-empty disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq V
.

Proof.

Let
\rho^{E}:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be
\Theta
-admissible.
Define
\widehat{\rho}^{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\widehat{\rho}^{V}(v)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
v\in e\end{subarray}}\rho^{E}(e).

Let
\theta\in\hat{\Theta}
and let
\theta^{\prime}=\left[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right]\in\Theta
be a simple sub-path of
\theta
.
Then

\sum_{v\in\theta}\widehat{\rho}^{V}(v)\geq\sum_{v\in\theta^{\prime}}\widehat{\rho}^{V}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\rho}^{V}(v_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho^{E}(\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\})\geq 1

and

\sum_{v\in V}\widehat{\rho}^{V}(v)^{p}\leq\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
v\in e\end{subarray}}\rho^{E}(e)^{p}\leq 2\cdot\sum_{e\in E}\rho^{E}(e)^{p}.

Then let
\rho^{V}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be
\hat{\Theta}
-admissible.
Define

\widehat{\rho}^{E}(\{v,u\})=2\cdot\max\{\rho^{V}(v),\rho^{V}(u)\}.

Let
\theta\in\Theta
, and let
\hat{\theta}
be the corresponding subset.
Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\widehat{\rho}^{E}(\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho^{V}(v_{i})\geq\sum_{v\in\hat{\theta}}\rho^{V}(v)\geq 1

and

\sum_{\{v,u\}\in E}\widehat{\rho}^{E}(\{v,u\})^{p}\leq 2^{p}\cdot\sum_{\{v,u\}\in E}\left(\rho^{V}(v)^{p}+\rho^{V}(u)^{p}\right)\leq 2^{p}\deg(G)\cdot\sum_{x\in V}\rho^{V}(x)^{p}.

∎

Roughly dual to the notion of edge modulus is the notion of a flow. We will use the exponent
p\in[1,\infty)
for modulus, and the exponent
q\in[1,\infty)
for flows. Here,
q
is called
the dual exponent
of
p
if
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. In all that follows,
q
will always denote this dual exponent.
A flow
from
A\subset V
to
B\subset V
is a map
\mathcal{F}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
for which

(1)

\mathcal{F}(x,y)=-\mathcal{F}(y,x)
and
\mathcal{F}(x,y)=0
unless
\{x,y\}\in E
;

(2)

\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{F}(x,y)=0
unless
x\in A\cup B
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a flow from
A
to
B
we also write that
\mathcal{F}
is a flow
A\to B
.
The
support
of a flow
\mathcal{F}
is
{\rm supp}(\mathcal{F}):=\{\{x,y\}\in E:\mathcal{F}(x,y)\neq 0\}
.
The
total flow
is given by

I(\mathcal{F}):=\sum_{x\in A}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{F}(x,y).

A
unit flow
from
\mathcal{F}
is one for which
I(\mathcal{F})=1
.
We also write the amount of flow from
x
as

{\rm div}\mathcal{F}(x):=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{F}(x,y).

If
e=\{x,y\}\in E
, we also write
|\mathcal{F}(e)|=|\mathcal{F}(x,y)|
, since this does not depend on the orientation of the edge.
Define the
energy
of the flow by

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}):=\sum_{e\in E}|\mathcal{F}(e)|^{q}.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
q\in[1,\infty)
. If
A,B\subset V
, then let

\mathcal{E}_{q}(A,B,G):=\inf\{\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}):\mathcal{F}\text{ is a unit flow from }A\text{ to }B\}.

Both optimal flows and optimal admissible functions are unique. We will use this lemma frequently without additional mention.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
A,B\subseteq G
be disjoint and non-empty. If
p>1
, and
q
is the dual exponent of
p
, then there is a unique
\mathcal{E}_{q}
-minimizing flow
\mathcal{F}
for
\mathcal{E}_{q}(A,B,G)
. Further for all collections
\Theta
of paths (or connected subsets) in Definition
4.1
(or in
4.2
) there is a unique optimal admissible function
\rho
.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from the fact that the optimization problem is finite dimensional and strictly uniformly convex.
∎

The following proposition for edge modulus is fairly well known, and was first shown in
[
68
, Theorem 5.1]
. See also
[
75
]
for another presentation of this argument, and
[
56
]
for a different approach. More recently, a variant involving probability measures on curves was shown in
[
2
, Theorem 2.8]
. Such probability measures can be identified with unit flows via superposition and by using the flow decomposition theorem.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
A,B\subseteq V
be non-empty disjoint subsets. Then for every
p\in(1,\infty)
and its dual exponent
q\in(1,\infty)

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(A,B,G)^{\frac{1}{q}}=1.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{F}
is a unit flow from
A
to
B
and
\rho
is
\Theta(A,B)
-admissible so that

(4.8)

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F})^{\frac{1}{q}}=1,

then

{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta(A,B),G)=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho)\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{q}(A,B,G)=\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}).

We introduce one more useful quantity that is equivalent to the edge modulus: the capacity.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
p>1,G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\emptyset\neq A,B\subseteq V
be disjoint subsets of
V
.
The
p
-capacity
from
A
to
B
is

{\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B,G):=\inf\left\{\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert^{p}:U_{\mid A}=0\text{ and }U_{\mid B}=1\right\}

We now state a few classical definitions and lemmas. The minimizers for capacity exist and are
p
-harmonic.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
p>1,G=(V,E)
be a graph and
\emptyset\neq V^{\prime}\subseteq V
.
For
p>1
we say that
U:V\to\mathbb{R}
is
p
-harmonic in
V^{\prime}
if for every
x\in V^{\prime}

\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}{\rm sgn}(U(x)-U(y))\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert^{p-1}=0.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
p>1,G=(V,E)
be a connected graph and
\emptyset\neq A,B\subseteq V
be disjoint subsets of
V
.
There is a unique function
U:V\to\mathbb{R}
so that
U|_{A}=0,U|_{B}=1
and

{\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B,G)=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert^{p}.

Furthermore, this function is
p
-harmonic in
V\setminus(A\cup B)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from uniform convexity and the variational principle, see e.g.
[
41
, Theorems 3.5 and 3.11]
for details.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
p>1,G=(V,E)
be a finite graph and
\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq V
a connected subset. If
U:V\to\mathbb{R}
is
p
-harmonic in
A
then

\max_{x\in\overline{A}}U(x)=\max_{x\in\partial{A}}U(x).

where

\partial{A}=\{x\in V\setminus A:\{x,y\}\in E\text{ for some }y\in A\}\text{ and }\overline{A}=A\cup\partial A.

Moreover, if
U(y)=\max_{x\in\overline{A}}U(x)
for some
y\in A
then
U
is constant in
\overline{A}
.

Proof.

The definition of
p
-harmonic functions yields by a standard argument the maximum principle, for details see
[
41
, Theorem 3.14]
.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
p>1,G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph and
\emptyset\neq A,B\subseteq V
be disjoint subsets of
V
.
Then

(4.14)

{\rm Mod}_{p}(A,B,G)={\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B,G).

Moreover, if
\widehat{\rho}:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the optimal
\Theta(A,B)
-admissible density,
\mathcal{F}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
is the optimal flow from
A
to
B
with respect to
\mathcal{E}_{q}
and
\widehat{U}:V\to\mathbb{R}
so that

\widehat{U}|_{A}=0,\widehat{U}|_{B}=1
and

{\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B,G)=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\lvert\widehat{U}(x)-\widehat{U}(y)\rvert^{p}.

then

\widehat{\rho}(\{x,y\})=\lvert\widehat{U}(x)-\widehat{U}(y)\rvert

and

\mathcal{F}(x,y)={\rm Mod}_{p}(A,B,G)^{-1}{\rm sgn}(\widehat{U}(y)-\widehat{U}(x))\lvert\widehat{U}(y)-\widehat{U}(x)\rvert^{p-1}

for all
\{x,y\}\in E
.

Proof.

Suppose
\rho:E\to\mathbb{R}
is
\Theta(A,B)
-admissible and we define
U:V\to\mathbb{R}
by

U(x):=\min_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho(\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\})

where the minimum is over all paths
\gamma=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
so that
x_{1}=x
and
x_{n}\in A
.
For
U^{\prime}=U\land 1
, we have
U^{\prime}|_{A}=0,U^{\prime}|_{B}=1
and

\lvert U^{\prime}(x)-U^{\prime}(y)\rvert\leq\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert\leq\rho(\{x,y\})

for all
\{x,y\}\in E
. This proves

{\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B,G)\leq{\rm Mod}_{p}(A,B,G).

On the other hand, if
U|_{A}=0,U|_{B}=1
, then
\rho(\{x,y\})=\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert
is, by the triangle inequality,
\Theta(A,B)
-admissible.
Hence (
4.14
) holds.
Furthermore, if we define the density
\widetilde{\rho}(\{x,y\})=\lvert\widehat{U}(x)-\widehat{U}(y)\rvert
, then

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\widetilde{\rho})={\rm Mod}_{p}(A,B,G).

By the uniqueness of the optimal density,
\widehat{\rho}=\widetilde{\rho}
.

Finally, let
\mathcal{F}
be the optimal flow function from
A
to
B
. By
[
68
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have

1=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}(U(y)-U(x))\mathcal{F}(x,y).

Thus, by Hölder’s inequality, we have

1\leq\left(\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}|U(y)-U(x)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathcal{F}(x,y)^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

By duality, Proposition
4.7
, the inequality is an equality. This is only possible if
\mathcal{F}(x,y)
is proportional to
{\rm sgn}(\widehat{U}(y)-\widehat{U}(x))\lvert\widehat{U}(y)-\widehat{U}(x)\rvert^{p-1}
. The propotionality constant can be computed using Proposition
4.7
again.
∎

The previous proposition also encodes the so called Ohm’s law for optimal potential functions and flows; see
[
56
, Section 2]
.

In what follows, we will often drop the graph
G
from the notation of moduli, capacity and flows, where it is clear from context and write e.g.
{\rm Mod}_{p}(A,B)
,
{\rm Cap}_{p}(A,B)
and
\mathcal{E}_{q}(A,B)
.

4.2.
Moduli computations on replacement graphs

The biggest advantage of the framework of IGS is the simplicity of edge modulus computations. Indeed, all modulus problems that we study in this paper can be reduced to the modulus problem over the paths between the gluing sets
\Theta(I_{v,\{v,u\}},I_{u,\{v,u\}})
, for
\{u,v\}\in E_{1}
. These modulus problems are explicitly and effectively computable.

First we introduce the basic notations. For
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
we will write
\Theta_{v,e}^{(m)}=\Theta\left(I_{v,e}^{(m)},I_{u,e}^{(m)}\right)
for the path family in
G_{m}
.
We denote the optimal
\Theta_{v,e}^{(m)}
-admissible density (for both edge- and vertex
p
-modulus) as
\rho_{e,m}
. Observe that, by symmetry,
\rho_{e,m}
is the optimal
\Theta^{(m)}_{u,e}
-density. Hence we omit the vertex
v,u
from the notation of the optimal function
\rho_{e,m}
. For
m=1
we simply write
\Theta_{v,e}^{(1)}=\Theta_{v,e}
and
\rho_{e,1}=\rho_{e}
. We denote the
\mathcal{E}_{q}
-minimizing unit flow from
I_{v,e}
to
I_{u,e}
as
\mathcal{F}_{v,e}
.
Lastly we define the family of paths

\Theta_{m}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E_{1}\\
v\in e\end{subarray}}\Theta_{v,e}^{(m)}

and the
\Theta_{m}
-admissible density
\widetilde{\rho}_{m}:V_{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

(4.15)

\widetilde{\rho}_{m}(v):=\max_{e\in E_{1}}\rho_{e,m}(v).

Remark 4.16
.

Observe that, in general,
\rho_{e}
and
\mathcal{F}_{v,e}
depend on
p
and
q
. Moreover, for
p=1
,
\rho_{e}
is not unique. Since in most arguments we deal with only one
p>1
and
q>1
is the dual exponent of
p
, we omit
p,q
from the notation.

Next we introduce conductively uniform-condition, under which the moduli computations are extremely simple and straightforward.

Definition 4.17

(Conductively uniform)
.

We say that an iterated graph system satisfying doubling property is
conductively uniform

if for every
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
the value

(4.18)

\mathcal{F}_{a}:=\mathcal{F}_{v,e}(\phi_{v,e}(a),\mathfrak{n}(\phi_{v,e}(a)))

only depends on
a\in I
and the value

(4.19)

\mathcal{R}_{p}:=\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{v,e})

does not depend on
e
or
v
.

The first of these means that the optimal flows in
G_{1}
between the gluing sets are distributed in a way that is independent of the gluing used. This is particularly important when defining flows at higher level graphs, since we need compatibility across the gluing sets. The second of these properties means that the modulus “accross” an edge is independent of the edge. This allows us to compute optimal moduli at the
n
’th level in a simple way. As we will shortly see, optimality of such flows and potential functions is shown using Proposition
4.7
. But, first, we shall give a few examples.

.

Let

v_{(k,i)}
, where
v_{(k,i)}
is the
k

for

[v_{(k,i)}]

[v_{(k,j)}]<[v_{(l,m)}]

Lemma 4.24
.

For every
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
we have

(4.25)

\mathcal{F}_{v,e}=-\mathcal{F}_{u,e}.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{E}_{q}(-\mathcal{F}_{v,e})=\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{v,e})
and
-\mathcal{F}_{v,e}
is a unit flow from
I_{u,e}
to
I_{v,e}
, the claim follows from the uniqueness of the optimal flow.
∎

Lemma 4.26
.

If the iterated graph system is doubling and conductively uniform then

(4.27)

\mathcal{M}_{p}:=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{e})

does not depend on
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
.
In particular,

(4.28)

(\mathcal{M}_{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot(\mathcal{R}_{p})^{\frac{1}{q}}=1.

Proof.

Directly follows from Proposition
4.7
and (
4.19
).
∎

Proposition 4.29
.

If the iterated graph system satisfies the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property for
L_{*}\geq 2
, the doubling property and is conductively uniform, then
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is continuously strictly decreasing in
p\in[1,\infty)
.
Furthermore, if
G_{1}
contains two edge-wise disjoint paths
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta_{v,e}
for some
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{1}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{1}>1
and there is a unique
Q_{*}\in(1,\infty)
so that
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}=1
.

Proof.

In order to avoid confusion with the
p
-dependence of the optimal density, we will write
\rho_{e,p}
as the optimal
\Theta_{v,e}
-admissible density.

If
\rho
is admissible for
\Theta
, then
\min(\rho,1)
is also admissible. Thus, for the optimal admissible density function we have
\rho_{e,p}(f)\leq 1
for all
f\in E.
From this, it is direct to verify that for any non-empty family of paths
\Theta
in a finite graph
G
,
{\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta,G)
is continuously decreasing in
p\in[1,\infty)
.
To prove that
\mathcal{M}_{p}={\rm Mod}_{p}(\Theta_{v,e})
is strictly decreasing, it is sufficient to prove that for all
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f\in E_{1}
we have
\rho_{e,p}(f)<1
. Indeed then

\mathcal{M}_{p}=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{e,p})>\mathcal{M}_{p+\epsilon}(\rho_{e,p})\geq\mathcal{M}_{p+\epsilon}(\rho_{e,p+\epsilon})=\mathcal{M}_{p+\epsilon}

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Fix
p\in(1,\infty)
. By Lemmas
4.11
and
4.13
there is a function
U:V_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is
p
-harmonic in
V_{1}\setminus(I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e})
and
\rho_{e,p}(\{x,y\})=\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert
for all
\{x,y\}\in E_{1}
. Now if
\{x,y\}\in E_{1}
, by the uniform scaling property and the fact that
I_{v,e},I_{u,e}
are independent sets, at most one of the vertices
x,y
is contained in
I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e}
. Furthermore, by the doubling property,
V_{1}\setminus(I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e})
is connected and
\overline{V_{1}\setminus(I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e})}=V_{1}
. By the strong maximum principle, Lemma
4.12
, we have

\rho_{e,p}(\{x,y\})=\lvert U(x)-U(y)\rvert<1

for all
\{x,y\}\in E_{1}
and so we conclude that
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is strictly decreasing.

We proceed to the second part. Let
\theta_{1}=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}],\theta_{2}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{l}]\in\Theta_{v,e}
be two edge-wise disjoint paths. By taking a sub-path, we may assume that both are simple paths.
Now if
\rho
is
\Theta_{v,e}
-admissible we have

\mathcal{M}_{1}(\rho)=\sum_{e\in E_{1}}\rho(e)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\rho(\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\})+\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\rho(\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\})\geq 2

Therefore
\mathcal{M}_{1}>1
.
On the other hand, by the
L_{*}
-uniform scaling property, the density
\widetilde{\rho}\equiv L_{*}^{-1}
is
\Theta_{v,e}
-admissible.
Hence

\mathcal{M}_{p}\leq\mathcal{M}_{p}(\widetilde{\rho})=\lvert E_{1}\rvert\cdot\frac{1}{L_{*}^{p}}<1

for large enough
p>1
.
By continuity of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, there must be
Q_{*}\in(1,\infty)
so that
\mathcal{M}_{Q_{*}}=1
.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is strictly decreasing, this
Q_{*}
is unique.
∎

For the rest of this section we assume that the IGS is doubling and conductively uniform.

Proposition 4.30
.

Let
A,B\subseteq V_{n}
be non-empty disjoint subsets and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given a
\Theta(A,B)
-admissible density
\rho_{n}:E_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
there is a
\Theta\left(\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(A),\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(B)\right)
-admissible density
\rho_{n+m}:E_{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with

(4.31)

\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n+m})=\mathcal{M}_{p}(\rho_{n})\cdot\mathcal{M}_{p}^{m}\text{ and }{\rm supp}(\rho_{n+m})\subseteq\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}({\rm supp}(\rho_{n})).

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the case
m=1
. Define

\rho_{n+1}(\{[z_{1},e],[z_{2},e]\})=\rho_{n}(e)\cdot\rho_{e}(\{z_{1},z_{2}\}).

By
(SM3)

\rho_{n+1}
is well-defined and, by (
4.27
), it is sufficient to prove that
\rho_{n+1}
is
\Theta\left(\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A),\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)\right)
-admissible.
Let
\theta\in\Theta\left(\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A),\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)\right)
and
\theta=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
.
By Proposition
3.16
there is a path
\hat{\theta}=\left[u_{1},\dots,u_{l}\right]\in\Theta(A,B)
and disjoint sub-paths
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{l-1}
of
\theta
where

\theta_{i}\in\Theta(\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(u_{i}),\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(u_{i+1}))\text{ and }e_{i}=\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}

and
\theta_{i}
is contained in
e_{i}\cdot G_{1}
where
e_{i}=\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}
for all
i=1,\dots,l-1
.
By
(SM1)
,
\hat{\theta}_{i}:=\sigma_{e_{i}}^{-1}(\theta_{i})\in\Theta_{e_{i}}
,
so we have

\displaystyle L_{\rho_{n+1}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}L_{\rho_{n+1}}(\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\rho_{n}\left(e_{i}\right)\cdot L_{\rho_{e_{i}}}\left(\hat{\theta}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\rho_{n}\left(e_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1.

∎

Proposition 4.32
.

Let
A,B\subseteq V_{n}
be non-empty disjoint subsets.
Given any unit flow
\mathcal{F}_{n}
from
A
to
B
there is a unit flow

\mathcal{F}_{n+m}
from
\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(A)
to
\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}(B)

with

(4.33)

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{n+m})=\mathcal{E}_{q}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\cdot\mathcal{R}_{p}^{m}\text{ and }{\rm supp}(\mathcal{F}_{n+m})\subseteq\pi_{n+m,n}^{-1}({\rm supp}(\mathcal{F}_{n})).

Proof.

Sufficient to prove the case
m=1
.
We define
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
as follows:
For
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in V

we set

\mathcal{F}_{n+1}([z_{1},\{v,u\}],[z_{2},\{v,u\}])=\mathcal{F}_{n}(v,u)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{v,e}(z_{1},z_{2}).

The above definition is well-defined in the sense that it does not depend on the orientation of
e=\{v,u\}
.
Indeed, by Lemma
4.24
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n+1}([z_{1},\{u,v\}],[z_{2},\{u,v\}])

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{n}(u,v)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{u,e}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: Reversing flow}}}{{=}}(-\mathcal{F}_{n}(v,u))\cdot(-\mathcal{F}_{v,e}(z_{1},z_{2}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{n}(v,u)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{v,e}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{n+1}([z_{1},\{v,u\}],[z_{2},\{v,u\}]).

Also (
4.33
) follows from (
4.19
) and the the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
so we only need to prove that
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
is a unit flow from
\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)
to
\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)
. Let
[z,\{v,u\}]\in V_{n+1}
for some
e=\{v,u\}\in E_{n}
and
z\in V
. Assume that
[z,\{v,u\}]\not\in\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)\cup\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)
. There are two cases to consider.
First, assume that
z\notin I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e}
. Then, by
(SM1)
and
(SM2)
, we have

{\rm div}(\mathcal{F}_{n+1})([z,\{v,u\}])={\rm div}(\mathcal{F}_{v,e})(z)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{n}(v,u)=0.

Next consider
z\in I_{v,e}\cup I_{u,e}
. By symmetry we may assume
z=\phi_{v,e}(a)\in I_{v,e}
for some
a\in I
.
Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
be the edges in
E_{n+1}
containing
[z,\{v,u\}]
and write those as
e_{i}=\{[z,\{v,u\}],[z_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}]\}
.
Then, by definition,
\{v,u\}
and
\{v_{i},u_{i}\}
have a common vertex, which must be
v
since otherwise
z\in I_{v,e}\cap I_{u,e}
.
Up to relabeling, we may assume
v_{i}=v
so we have
z_{i}=\mathfrak{n}(\phi_{v_{i},e_{i}}(a))
and
v=v_{i}=\phi_{v_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}}(a)
.
We get

\displaystyle{\rm div}(\mathcal{F}_{n+1})([z,\{v,u\}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{F}_{n+1}([z,\{v,u\}],[z_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{F}_{n+1}([\phi_{v_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}}(a),\{v_{i},u_{i}\}],[z_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{F}_{n}(v_{i},u_{i})\cdot\mathcal{F}_{v_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}}(\phi_{v_{i},\{v_{i},u_{i}\}}(a),z_{i})

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: Flows agree on boundary}}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}_{a}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}F_{n}(v_{i},u_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{a}\cdot{\rm div}(F_{n})(v).

Recall that
[z,\{v,u\}]\not\in\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)\cup\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)
and
z\in I_{v,\{v,u\}}
. Since
[z,\{v,u\}]\notin\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)\cup\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)
, we must have
v\notin A_{n}\cup B_{n}
. This concludes showing that
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
is a flow from
\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)
to
\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(B)
.

Finally, if
v\in A_{n}
and
z\in I_{v,\{v,u\}}
then
[z,\{v,u\}]\in\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)
.
Hence

\displaystyle I(\mathcal{F}_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{[z,\{v,u\}]\in\pi_{n+1,n}^{-1}(A)}{\rm div}(\mathcal{F}_{n+1})([z,\{v,u\}])

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{a\in I}\mathcal{F}_{a}\cdot\sum_{v\in A_{n}}{\rm div}(\mathcal{F}_{n})(v)

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{a\in I}\mathcal{F}_{a}\cdot I(\mathcal{F}_{n})

\displaystyle=\quad 1.

Thus
\mathcal{F}
is a unit flow, and the claim follows.
∎

5.

Assumption 5.1

L_{*}
-uniform scaling property,

(2)

Doubling property,

(3)

Conductively uniform property and

contains at least two edge-wise disjoint paths in
\Theta_{v,e}
for some

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2

If the iterated graph system satisfies Assumption
5.1
then

X
satisfies the combinatorial
Q_{*}
-Loewner property for
Q_{*}=\dim_{{\rm AR}}(X)>1
.

Recall the notation from Subsection
2.4
. Throughout this section we assume that the IGS satisfy (1)-(3) in Assumption
5.1
. The condition (4*) will only be used to ensure that the conformal dimension of the limit space is strictly greater than
1
and is only used in the proof of Theorem
5.2
. Furthermore, for the rest of the paper,
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is always the moduli constant in (
4.27
).

During the moduli computations, we will adopt the notation
A\lesssim B
(resp.

) if there is a constant

, which depends on at most

and dependence on

(resp.

and

Lemma 5.3

. Moreover, if

Proposition 5.4

To prove the other inequality, let
\rho:V_{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\Gamma(F_{1},F_{2})
-admissible.

{\rm Mod}_{p}^{V}(\Theta(G_{m}[F_{1}],G_{m}[F_{2}]),G_{m})\leq(C_{\deg})^{p}\cdot{\rm Mod}_{p}^{D}(\Gamma(F_{1},F_{2}),G_{m})

6.1.
Porous sets and Assouad dimension

We will prove Proposition
2.9
in this section and give a natural condition for porosity of IGSs. First, we introduce some additional terminology that is just needed in this section.
For a subset
A\subset X
le

N(A,r):=\inf\left\{N:A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i},r)\right\}

be the smallest number of balls of radius
r
needed to cover the set
A
. The Assouad dimension of a metric space is then given by

{\rm dim}_{\rm A}(X):=\inf\left\{s>0:\exists C\geq 1,\forall 0<r<R,\forall x\in X,N(B(x,R),r)\leq CR^{s}r^{-s}\right\}.

A standard volume counting argument shows that if
X
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular, then
{\rm dim}_{\rm A}(X)=Q
.
We need the following result which is from
[
35
, Proposition 14.14]
. In that reference, connectivity is replaced by the weaker condition of uniform perfectness. For us, this weaker statement suffices.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
X
is connected, then for every
Q>{\rm dim}_{A}(X)
there exists a metric
d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X)
which is
Q
-Ahlfors regular. In particular,
{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)\leq{\rm dim}_{\rm A}(X)
.

We now prove Proposition
2.9
.

Proof of Proposition
2.9
.

Let
Y\subset(X,d)
be a porous subset with
{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)={\rm dim}_{AR}(Y)
. Let
d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\rm AR}(X)
be
Q
-Ahlfors regular. Then,
{\rm dim}_{\rm A}((X,d^{\prime}))=Q
. Since
(X,d^{\prime})
is quasisymmetric to
(X,d)
, via the identity map, it is direct to see that
(Y,d^{\prime}|_{Y})
is a porous subset of
(X,d^{\prime})
, and that
(Y,d|_{Y})
is quasisymmetric to
(Y,d^{\prime}|_{Y})
and
d^{\prime}|_{Y}\in\mathcal{G}(Y)
.

Porosity implies that the Hausdorff
Q
-measures of neighborhoods of
Y
decay geometrically in their thickness. This, together with Ahlfors regularity and a volume counting argument yields an Assouad dimension bound. This fairly direct counting argument is classical, and yields that Assouad dimension of a porous subset of a
Q
-regular space is strictly less than the Assouad dimension of the space see e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 3.12]
, (result also contained in many other places, e.g.
[
42
, Proposition 3.5]
):

{\rm dim}_{\rm A}(Y,d^{\prime}|_{Y})<Q.

Thus, by Lemma
6.1
, we get

{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(Y)={\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(Y,d^{\prime}|_{Y})\leq{\rm dim}_{\rm A}(Y,d^{\prime})<Q.

Thus,
Q>{\rm dim}_{\rm AR}(X)
, and
X
does not attain its conformal dimension.
∎

In probability theory, the walk dimension determines the order of the typical distance a diffusion travels in time

. For its natural generalization, the

(see e.g.
[

For many fractals it has been observed that the 2-walk dimension is strictly greater than 2 (see e.g.
[
43
,
10
,
9

[

]

for all

[
66
,
46
,
33
]
. See also
[

.

In our framework, whenever (1)-(3) in Assumption

is satisfied, we are able to compute the exact value of

-walk dimension. This is essentially due to Corollary

and Theorem

. We also present in Proposition

If the iterated graph system satisfies (1)-(3) in Assumption
