Introduction

Let
\ell_{\infty}
be the space of all bounded sequences
endowed with the usual norm

\|x\|=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x_{n},

(1)

where
\mathbb{N}
denotes set of all positive integers,
and let
c_{0}
be the subspace of null-sequences.
The famous result of Phillips
[
1
]

states that
c_{0}
is not complemented in
\ell_{\infty}
:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

There is no bounded linear operator
P:\ell_{\infty}\to c_{0}
such that for every

x\in c_{0}
the equality
Px=x
holds.

Theorem
1.1
gives the very first example of a non-complemented subspace.
Later, more such subspaces were found;
for further discussion we refer the reader to
[
2
]
.

The notion of almost convergence is a generalization of the notion of convergence.
Before we proceed to it, we need to introduce the concept of Banach limits,
which (expectably) provide a way to generalize the concept of the limit.

Definition 1.2
.

A linear functional
B\in\ell_{\infty}^{*}
is said to be a
Banach limit
if

(i)

B\geq 0
, that is
Bx\geq 0
for
x\geq 0
,

(ii)

B\mathbb{I}=1
, where
\mathbb{I}=(1,1,\ldots)
,

(iii)

B(Tx)=B(x)
for
x\in\ell_{\infty}
,
where
T
is the shift operator
T(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)=(x_{2},x_{3},\ldots)
.

The set of all Banach limits is denoted by
\mathfrak{B}
.
The existence of Banach limits was announced by Mazur
[
3
]
and proved by Banach
[
4
]
.

Definition 1.3
.

A sequence
x\in\ell_{\infty}
is said to be
almost convergent
to
t\in\mathbb{R}

if for every
B\in\mathfrak{B}
the equality

Bx=t

(2)

holds.

The set of all sequences that are almost convergent to
t
is denoted by
ac_{t}
;
the space of all almost convergent sequences is denoted by
ac
.

Theorem 1.4

(Lorentz,
[
5
]
)
.

A sequence
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...)
is almost convergent to
t\in\mathbb{R}
iff

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=m+1}^{m+n}x_{k}=t

(3)

uniformly by
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

[

is not complemented in
\ell_{\infty}
.
The proof is based on the original Phillips’s proof of Theorem

and uses some lemmas from there.
For more details on Banach limits, we refer the reader to

The following lemma is a classical result, and we prove it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists an uncountable family of subsets

\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,
S_{i}\subset\mathbb{N}
,
such that each
S_{i}
is countable and for every
i\neq j
the intersection
S_{i}\cap S_{j}
is finite.

Proof.

Consider a bijection
\mathbb{N}\leftrightarrow\mathbb{Q}

and let
I=\mathbb{R}
.
For
i\in I
we set
S_{i}=\{q_{n}\}
,
where
\{q_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{Q}
is a sequence which converges monotonically to
i
.
∎

The following lemma is inspired by
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For each linear operator
Q:ac_{0}\to ac_{0}
such that
c_{0}\subseteq\ker Q
,
there exists infinite subset
S\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\forall(x\in ac_{0}:\supp x\subset S)[Qx=0].

(7)

Proof.

Notice first that for any infinite subset
S\subset\mathbb{N}

there exists
x\in ac_{0}\setminus c_{0}
with
\supp x\subseteq S
.
Indeed, we can find such
x
with
0<x\leq\chi_{S}

that contains infinitely many ones, and the distance between the ones
is enough for Lorentz’s criterion (
3
) to be hold.

Let
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of subsets of
\mathbb{N}

that satisfy the conditions of Lemma
2.1
.
Suppose to the contrary that

\forall(\mbox{infinite }S\subset\mathbb{N})\exists(x\in ac_{0}:\supp x\subset S)[Qx\neq 0].

(8)

In particular,

\forall(i\in I)\exists(x_{i}\in ac_{0}:\supp x_{i}\subset S_{i})[Q(x_{i})\neq 0].

(9)

Note that
x_{i}\notin c_{0}
because
c_{0}\subseteq\ker Q
.
Without loss of generality we can assume that
\|x_{i}\|=1
for all
i\in I
.

Consider
I_{n}=\{i\in I\,:\,(Qx_{i})_{n}\neq 0\}
,
then
I=\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}
.
Thus, we can find
n
such that
I_{n}
is also uncountable
(otherwise
I
would be countable as a countable union of countable sets,
which contradicts to conditions of Lemma
2.1
).

Сonsider now
I_{n,k}=\{i\in I_{n}\,:\,|(Qx_{i})_{n}|\geq 1/k\}
,
then
I_{n}=\bigcup\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n,k}
.
Applying the same argument as above, one can easily see that the set
I_{n,k}
is uncountable for some
k
.
Let us choose such
I_{n,k}
and proceed with it.

So, we have an uncountable set
I_{n,k}
and

\forall(i\in I_{n,k})\exists(x_{i}\in ac_{0}:\supp x_{i}\subset S_{i})\Bigl[\|x_{i}\|=1\mbox{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }|(Qx_{i})_{n}|\geq 1/k\Bigr].

(10)

Consider a finite set
J\subset I_{n,k}
with
\#J>1

(here
\#J
stands for the cardinality of the set
J
).
Take

y=\sum_{j\in J}\operatorname{sign}{(Qx_{j})_{n}}\cdot x_{j}.

(11)

Since the intersection
S_{i}\cap S_{j}
is finite for any
i\neq j
and

\supp x_{j}\subset S_{j}
,
the intersection
\bigcap\limits_{j\in J}\supp x_{j}
is also finite.
Hence,
y=f+z
,
where
\supp f
is finite and
\|z\|\leq 1
.

On the other hand,

(Qy)_{n}=\sum_{j\in J}(\operatorname{sign}(Qx_{j})_{n})\cdot(Qx_{j})_{n}\geq\frac{\#J}{k}.

(12)

Note, that
f\in c_{0}
and we have
Qf=0
, because
c_{0}\subseteq\ker Q
.
Thus,
Qy=Q(f+z)=Qf+Qz=Qz
and

\frac{\#J}{k}\leq(Qy)_{n}\leq\|Qy\|=\|Qz\|\leq\|Q\|\cdot\|z\|\leq\|Q\|.

(13)

Due to (
13
), we obtain
\#J\leq\|Q\|k
for every
J\subset I_{n,k}
.
This contradicts the fact that
I_{n,k}
is uncountable,
and we are done.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

The subspace
c_{0}
is not complemented in
ac_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
there exists a continuous projection
P:ac_{0}\to c_{0}
.
Applying Lemma
2.2
to
I-P

we can find infinite subset
S\subset\mathbb{N}

such that
\forall(x\in ac_{0}:\supp x\subset S)[(I-P)x=0]

(such

exists even if
\chi_{S}\notin ac_{0}
).
But then
Px\notin c_{0}
,
which contradicts the fact that
P
is a projection onto
c_{0}
.
∎
