Magneto-nonlinear Hall effect in time-reversal breaking system
Anwei Zhang, Jun-Won Rhim

Abstract
Magneto-nonlinear Hall effect is known to be intrinsic and requires time-reversal symmetry. Here we show that a new type of magneto-nonlinear Hall effect can occur in the time-reversal breaking materials within the second-order response to in-plane electric and vertical magnetic fields. Such a Hall response is generated by the oscillation of the electromagnetic field and has a quantum origin arising from a geometric quantity associated with the Berry curvature and band velocity. We demonstrate that the massive Dirac model of LaAlO3/LaNiO3/LaAlO3 quantum well can be used to detect this Hall effect. Our work widens the theory of the Hall effect in the time-reversal breaking system by proposing a new kind of nonlinear electromagnetic response. Keywords: quantum transport, nonlinear Hall effect, electromagnetic response

II. Model

In this work, we consider a clean system and apply an electric field polarized in
y
-direction and propagating along
x
-direction, i.e.,
\mathbf{E}=E_{y}\hat{y}e^{iqx-i\omega t}
, and a magnetic field polarized along
z
-direction, i.e.,

\mathbf{B}=B_{z}\hat{z}e^{iqx-i\omega t}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Here,
q
and
\omega
are the moduli of the momentum and frequency of the fields, respectively.
Under the presence of such external fields, the system can be described by the vector potential

\mathbf{A}=A_{y}\hat{y}e^{iqx-i\omega t},

(2)

where
A_{y}
satisfies the relations
A_{y}=E_{y}/i\omega=B_{z}/iq
.

The full Hamiltonian of the system in the presence of the external fields can be written as
H=H_{0}+H^{{}^{\prime}}
. Here
H_{0}
is the Hamiltonian of the sample and
H^{{}^{\prime}}
is the perturbed Hamiltonian which is given by Taylor series of the Hamiltonian in minimal coupling scheme
morimoto2016semiclassical

H^{{}^{\prime}}=e\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A}+\frac{e^{2}}{2}\partial_{{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{A},

(3)

where
-e
is the charge of the electron and
\mathbf{\hat{v}}=\partial_{\mathbf{k}}H_{0}
is the velocity operator. Since we consider second-order response in this paper, we expand the Hamiltonian up to second-order.
As a result, the current operator in our system will be described by

\hat{j}_{x}=-e\partial_{k_{x}}H=-e\hat{v}_{x}-e^{2}\partial_{k_{x}}(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A})-\frac{e^{3}}{2}\partial_{k_{x}}[\partial_{{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A})\cdot\mathbf{A}].

(4)

Note that we set
\hbar=1
in the paper.

III
III. Second-order nonlinear response

Now we consider the second-order harmonic response with respect to the vector potential.
By using second quantization and Fourier transformation, the expectation value of the current
\langle\hat{j}_{x}(2\mathbf{q},2i\omega)\rangle
in momentum-frequency space can be written as (see Appendix A)

j_{x}(2\mathbf{q},2i\omega)=\frac{1}{\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{j}_{x}G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n};\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega)],

(5)

where
V
denotes the volume of a three-dimensional system or the area of a two-dimensional system,
\beta=1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature,
\mathbf{q}=q\hat{x}
,
\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi/\beta
and

\omega=2m\pi/\beta
are the fermionic and bosonic Matsubara frequencies, respectively.
According to Dyson equation, the exact
Green’s function
G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n};\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega)
can be expanded as a perturbation series of the unperturbed Green’s function
G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})
.

For second-order response, the average value of the current Eq. (
5
) should have two vector potentials. If the current operator
\hat{j}_{x}
takes the first term in Eq. (
4
), the exact Green’s function should contain two first term or one second term in Eq. (
3
). Similarly, if
\hat{j}_{x}
takes the second term in Eq. (
4
), the exact Green’s function should contain the first term in Eq. (
3
), and if
\hat{j}_{x}
takes the third term in Eq. (
4
), the exact Green’s function does not contain any term of the interacting Hamiltonian.

For simplicity,
in this paper we consider a generic linear continuum Hamiltonian targeting various Dirac and Weyl systems.
Then, the corresponding Hamiltonian of the sample satisfies the restriction
\partial_{k_{i}}\partial_{k_{j}}H_{0}=\partial_{k_{i}}\hat{v}_{j}=0
.
As a result, the perturbed Hamiltonian becomes

H^{{}^{\prime}}=e\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A},

(6)

the current operator is

\hat{j}_{x}=-e\partial_{k_{x}}H=-e\hat{v}_{x},

(7)

and the exact
Green’s function is
given by the term

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n};\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})G(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-i\omega)H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})G(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega),

where the unperturbed Green function has the form

G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})=\sum_{a}\frac{|u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle\langle u_{a}(\mathbf{k})|}{i\omega_{n}+\mu-\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})}.

(9)

Here
|u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle
is the periodic part of Bloch wave functions for band
a
,
\mu
is the chemical potential, and
\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})
is the energy dispersion of band
a
.
The matrix element of the interaction Hamiltonian can be obtained from

\displaystyle\langle u_{a}(\mathbf{k})|H^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})|u_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{a}(\mathbf{k})|H^{\prime}|\psi_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{a}(\mathbf{k})|\frac{e}{2}(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{v}})|\psi_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{a}(\mathbf{k})|\frac{e}{2}A_{y}\big[\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})+\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\big]|u_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\rangle,

where
|\psi_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}|u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle
is the Bloch wave function and
\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\hat{v}_{y}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
. Here we have symmetricized the perturbed Hamiltonian.
For linear Hamiltonian, due to the fact that
\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})=\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})-q\partial_{k_{x}}\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})=\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})
, we have

;
shi2007quantum
;
zhang2008theory

H^{\prime}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})=\frac{e}{2}A_{y}\big[\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})+\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\big]=eA_{y}\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k}).

(11)

Note that we omit the time-dependent factor
e^{-i\omega t}
in eq. (
10
), which disappears in frequency space.
The above second-order nonlinear response forms a triangle diagram, as shown in Fig.
2

Besides, unlike the previous treatment of the second-order response

;

By taking the Matsubara sum over

j_{x}(2\mathbf{q},2\omega)=\frac{-2e^{3}}{V}\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)A_{y}A_{y},

(12)

with

\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a,b,c}M_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})F_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\omega).

(13)

Here
M_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})
is the correlation function with band indices
a
,
b
, and
c
:

\displaystyle M_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle u_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})|\hat{v}_{x}(\mathbf{k})|u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle\langle u_{a}(\mathbf{k})|\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})|u_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})\rangle\langle u_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})|\hat{v}_{y}(\mathbf{k})|u_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})\rangle,

(14)

and

\displaystyle F_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\omega}\bigg[\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-2\omega}-\frac{f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\omega}\bigg]

(15)

is the Matsubara summation with
f(\varepsilon_{i}(\mathbf{k}))=1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{i}(\mathbf{k})-\mu)}+1)
being the Fermi-Dirac distribution function.

IV
IV. Electromagnetic response

Here we are interested in the dependence of the second harmonic response on the electric and magnetic field, i.e.,
j_{x}(2\omega)\propto E_{y}B_{z}
.
To derive the response to the electromagnetic fields, we need to expand
\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
12
) to first order in
q
as

\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\Pi(0,\omega)+q\Pi(\omega)
, where
\Pi(\omega)=\partial_{q}\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)|_{q=0}
.
As a result, Eq. (
12
) becomes

j_{x}(2\omega)=\frac{2e^{3}}{\omega V}\Pi(\omega)E_{y}B_{z}.

(16)

Note that the
E^{2}
and
B^{2}
terms for the second harmonic generation can also be derived by expanding
\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
to the zero-order and second-order in
q
, respectively.

In the low-frequency limit, the above current can be expanded in terms of the frequency
\omega
, i.e.,
\Pi(\omega)/\omega=\Pi(0)/\omega+\partial_{\omega}\Pi(\omega)|_{\omega=0}+\dots
.
The second term has no dependence on
\omega
, it should generate an intrinsic nonlinear Hall current as shown in Ref.
gao2014field
.
Here we are interested in the leading order term, i.e.,

j_{x}(2\omega)=\frac{2e^{3}}{\omega V}\Pi(0)E_{y}B_{z}.

(17)

We note that the function
\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
satisfies the following relation (see Appendix B)

\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\Pi^{*}(-\mathbf{q},-\omega).

(18)

Then one can get

\Pi(0)=-\Pi^{*}(0)=i\mathrm{lm}\Pi(0).

(19)

The leading term of the current thus becomes

j_{x}(2\omega)=\frac{2ie^{3}}{\omega V}\mathrm{lm}\Pi(0)E_{y}B_{z}.

(20)

For two-band system, the energy bands
a,b,c
in
M_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})
and
F_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\omega)
can only be upper or lower bands. If the bands
a,b,c
are the same band,
\mathrm{lm}M_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},0)
and
F_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},0,\omega)
will all be zero. Then
\mathrm{lm}\Pi(\omega)=\partial_{q}\mathrm{lm}M_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})|_{q=0}F_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},0,\omega)+\mathrm{lm}M_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},0)\partial_{q}F_{aaa}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\omega)|_{q=0}=0
, i.e., the full intraband term’s contribution to the second-order response vanishes.
Note that for the linear response in the uniform limit, the intra-band terms also give zero contributions
chang2015chiral
;
zhong2016gyrotropic
.
For nonzero response, the bands
a,b,c
should take
a,b,b
;
a,b,a
;
a,a,b
.

One can decompose
\Pi(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
13
) into the intraband part
\Pi_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
and interband part

\Pi_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
.
The intraband part
\Pi_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is further split into three terms such that

\Pi_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\Pi^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
,
where

\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{-M_{abb}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\omega}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\omega},

(21)

\Pi^{(2)}_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{M_{aba}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\omega}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-2\omega},

(22)

and

\Pi^{(3)}_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{M_{aab}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\omega}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\omega}.

(23)

From these terms, we expand the imaginary part of
\Pi_{\mathrm{intra}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, take the uniform limit, then obtain (see Appendix C)

\mathrm{lm}\Pi_{\mathrm{intra}}(0)=-\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}F^{ab}_{xy}v_{ay}\partial_{k_{x}}f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})),

(24)

where
F^{ab}_{xy}=-2\mathrm{lm}\langle\partial_{k_{x}}u_{a}(\mathbf{k})|u_{b}(\mathbf{k})\rangle\langle u_{b}(\mathbf{k})|\partial_{k_{y}}u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\rangle
is the Berry curvature which is the imaginary part of quantum geometric tensor
provost1980riemannian
;
zhang2022revealing
and
v_{iy}=\partial_{k_{y}}\varepsilon_{i}(\mathbf{k})
is the band velocity along
y
-axis.

The interband part
\Pi_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is also composed of three terms such that

\Pi_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{3}\Pi^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
.
Since the interband part is irrespective of the order of limits
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
and
\omega\rightarrow 0
, we take
\omega=0
before
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
and have

\Pi^{(1)}_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},0)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{M_{abb}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})},

(25)

\Pi^{(2)}_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},0)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{-M_{aba}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})},

(26)

and

\Pi^{(3)}_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},0)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}\frac{-M_{aab}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})}.

(27)

We expand
\mathrm{lm}\Pi_{\mathrm{inter}}(\mathbf{q},0)
and get (see Appendix D)

\mathrm{lm}\Pi_{\mathrm{inter}}(0)=\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))\partial_{k_{x}}\big[F^{ab}_{xy}(v_{ay}-v_{by})\big].

(28)

Combining the contribution of the intraband and interband terms, the Hall current becomes

j_{x}(2\omega)=\frac{2ie^{3}}{\hbar\omega V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},a\neq b}f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))\partial_{k_{x}}\big[F^{ab}_{xy}(2v_{ay}-v_{by})\big]E_{y}B_{z}.

(29)

Here we have taken integration by parts for the Fermi surface term in Eq. (
24
).
Eq. (
29
) is the main result of this paper.
It shows that in the Hall device, there is a second-order Hall response which is proportional to a dipole, i.e.,

D=\int[d\mathbf{k}]\sum_{a\neq b}f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))\partial_{k_{x}}\big[F^{ab}_{xy}(2v_{ay}-v_{by})\big].

(30)

Here
[d\mathbf{k}]=d^{n}\mathbf{k}/(2\pi)^{n}
denotes the integration measure of a
n
-dimensional system. Under time-reversal symmetry, such a geometric quantity vanishes, since the partial differential, the Berry curvature, and the band velocity are all odd functions of momentum in such a case. The dipole can be written in another form with the term
\partial_{k_{x}}f(\epsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))
, which shows that its physical origin is the Fermi surface.
For simplicity, we only consider the clean limit
where the relaxation time
\tau
approaches infinity.
Here the response depends on the electromagnetic field frequency
\omega
, which is similar to the extrinsic nonlinear Hall response
sodemann2015quantum
. Note that in the clean limit, the factor
-\tau/(1+i\omega\tau)

in the extrinsic nonlinear Hall current is actually the factor
i/\omega
.

V. Massive Dirac model

The result can be applied to Dirac and Weyl systems. Here, as an example, let us consider a two-dimensional Dirac Hamiltonian with broken time-reversal symmetry motivated by the LaAlO
3
/LaNiO
3
/LaAlO
3
quantum well system

.
The low-energy physics of the material around Dirac points can be described by the Hamiltonian

H_{0}(\mathbf{k})=\alpha(k_{x}-k_{y})\sigma_{x}+\beta(k_{x}+k_{y})\sigma_{z}+\Delta\sigma_{y}.

(31)

Here
\mathbf{k}
is momentum defined near the Dirac point,
(\alpha,\beta)
are expansion coefficients determined by the band parameters, and
\Delta
is the gap at the Dirac points.
The energy dispersion of upper band
(+)
and lower band
(-)
are respectively given by
\varepsilon_{\pm}(\mathbf{k})=\pm\varepsilon_{0}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}=\sqrt{k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{x}+k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{y}+\Delta^{2}}
,
k^{{}^{\prime}}_{x}=\alpha(k_{x}-k_{y})
, and
k^{{}^{\prime}}_{y}=\beta(k_{x}+k_{y})
.
Then the band velocity is
v_{\pm y}=\pm(-\alpha k^{{}^{\prime}}_{x}+\beta k^{{}^{\prime}}_{y})/\varepsilon_{0}
and
the Berry curvature is given by
F^{-+}_{xy}=-\alpha\beta\Delta/\varepsilon_{0}^{3}
. In the case of zero temperature and the chemical potential
\mu<-\Delta
, the dipole density, i.e., the integrand function in the dipole, becomes
3\alpha\beta\Delta[k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{x}(3\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})-k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{y}(\alpha^{2}+3\beta^{2})+\Delta^{2}(\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2})]/(4\pi^{2}\varepsilon_{0}^{6})
,
dk_{x}dk_{y}=dk^{{}^{\prime}}_{x}dk^{{}^{\prime}}_{y}/(2\alpha\beta)
.
and the integral is over the region
\varepsilon_{-}(\mathbf{k})<\mu
, i.e.,
k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{x}+k^{{}^{\prime}2}_{y}>\mu^{2}-\Delta^{2}
. By using polar coordinate, the dipole is found to be

D=\frac{3(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})(\mu^{2}-\Delta^{2})\Delta}{8\pi\hbar^{2}\mu^{4}}.

(32)

and

VI. Concluding remarks

Let us estimate the magnitude of this nonlinear Hall response. Under a driving magnetic field of
1

\hbar\omega=0.1

|j_{x}(2\omega)/E_{y}|=2e^{3}DB_{z}/\hbar\omega
, can reach

A/V if we take
\alpha/2=\beta
=1eVÅ,

. As a comparison, the magnitude of the conductivity for the quantum Hall effect is about

Here we mainly restrict our system to be linear. If there is a correction to the linear Hamiltonian, we should consider the second term in Eq. (
3

Appendix A. The expectation value of the current operator

The expectation value of the current operator can be expressed by Green’s function
araki2021spin
;
vlasiuk2023cavity
. For readers to follow, here we give the derivation.

By using the second quantization method, the expectation value of the current operator can be written as

\langle\hat{j_{i}}(\mathbf{r},\tau)\rangle=\sum_{m,n}j^{mn}_{i}\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}(\mathbf{r},\tau)\hat{a}_{n}(\mathbf{r},\tau)\rangle.

(33)

Here
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}(\mathbf{r},\tau),\hat{a}_{n}(\mathbf{r},\tau)
are fermion creation and annihilation operators, respectively.
Note that using the Fourier transformation and Matsubara frequency, we have

\hat{a}_{n}(\mathbf{r},\tau)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\int d\mathbf{k}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-i\omega_{n}\tau}\hat{a}_{n}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}),

(34)

where
\beta=1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature and
\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi/\beta
is the fermionic Matsubara frequency.
In momentum-frequency space, the expectation value of the second-order current is

\langle\hat{j_{i}}(2\mathbf{q},2i\omega)\rangle=\int d\mathbf{r}d\tau e^{-2i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}+2i\omega\tau}\langle\hat{j_{i}}(\mathbf{r},\tau)\rangle.

(35)

Substituting Eq. (
33
) into Eq. (
35
) and using Eq. (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\hat{j_{i}}(2\mathbf{q},2i\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta V}\sum_{m,n,\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}}j^{mn}_{i}\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega)\hat{a}_{n}(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n})\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{j_{i}}G(\mathbf{k},i\omega_{n};\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},i\omega_{n}-2i\omega)].

Here we have used the substitution
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\omega\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}
and
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int d\mathbf{k}\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Appendix B. The proof of the relation:

Here we show the details of the proof of the relation:

We start from the expressions of
M_{abc}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})
and

\displaystyle\langle u_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})|\hat{v}_{y}|u_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})\rangle

and

F_{abc}(\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{q},-\omega)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})+\omega}\bigg[\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{q})+2\omega}-\frac{f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{q}))}{\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}+2\mathbf{q})+\omega}\bigg].

Transferring the momentum from
\mathbf{k}
to
\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}

(42)

and

\displaystyle F_{abc}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q},-\omega)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})+\omega}\bigg[\frac{f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}))}{\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k})+2\omega}-\frac{f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}))}{\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k})+\omega}\bigg].

(43)

Now exchanging the indexes
a
and
c
, i.e.
a\leftrightarrow c

\sum_{\mathbf{k},a,b,c}M^{*}_{cba}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q})F_{cba}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q},-\omega)

(44)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})+\omega}\bigg[\frac{f(\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))}{\varepsilon_{c}(\mathbf{k}-2\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})+2\omega}-\frac{f(\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}))-f(\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k}))}{\varepsilon_{b}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})-\varepsilon_{a}(\mathbf{k})+\omega}\bigg]

\displaystyle F_{abc}(\mathbf{q},\omega).
