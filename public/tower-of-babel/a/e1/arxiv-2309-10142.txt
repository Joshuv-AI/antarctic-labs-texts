Controlling Unknown Linear Dynamics with Almost Optimal Regret
J. Carruth, M. F. Eggl, C. Fefferman, C. W. Rowley

Abstract
Continuing from the companion paper [ 7 ] , we consider a simple control problem in which the underlying dynamics depend on a parameter a a that is unknown and must be learned. In this paper, we assume that a a can be any real number and we do not assume that we have a prior belief about a a . We seek a control strategy that minimizes a quantity called the regret. Given any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , we produce a strategy that minimizes the regret to within a multiplicative factor of ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) .

Many works in adaptive control theory attempt to control a system whose underlying dynamics are initially unknown and must be learned from observation. The goal is then to bound
Regret
, a quantity defined by comparing our expected cost with that incurred by an opponent who knows the underlying dynamics. Typically one tries to achieve a regret whose order of magnitude is as small as possible after a long time. Adaptive control theory has extensive practical applications; see, e.g.,
[
4
,
8
,
21
,
29
]
.

In some applications, we don’t have the luxury of waiting for a long time. This is the case, e.g., for a pilot attempting to land an airplane following the sudden loss of a wing, as in
[
5
]
. Our goal, here and in
[
7
]
, is to achieve the absolute minimum possible regret over a fixed, finite time horizon. This poses formidable mathematical challenges, even for simple model systems.

We will study a one-dimensional, linear model system whose dynamics depend on a single unknown parameter
a
. When
a
is large positive, the system is highly unstable. (There is no “stabilizing gain” for all
a
.) We suppose that the unknown
a
may be any real number and we don’t assume that we are given a Bayesian prior probability distribution for it.

Modulo an arbitrarily small increase in regret, we reduce the problem to a Bayesian variant in which the unknown
a
is confined to a finite set and governed by a prior probability distribution.

For the Bayesian problem, our task is to find a strategy that minimizes the expected cost. This leads naturally to a PDE (a Bellman equation).

In
[
7
]
, we prove that the optimal strategy for Bayesian control is indeed given in terms of the solution of the Bellman equation, and that any strategy significantly different from that optimum incurs a significantly higher cost. We proceed modulo assumptions about existence and regularity of the relevant PDE solutions, for which we lack rigorous proofs. (However, we have obtained numerical solutions, which seem to behave as expected.)

Let us now explain the above in more detail.

1.1
The Model System

Our system consists of a particle moving in one dimension, influenced by our control and buffeted by noise. The position of our particle at time
t
is denoted by
q(t)\in\mathbb{R}
. At each time
t
, we may specify a “control”
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, determined by history up to time
t
, i.e., by
(q(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
. A “strategy” (aka “policy”) is a rule for specifying
u(t)
in terms of
(q(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
for each
t
. We write
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{*},\text{etc.}
to denote strategies. The noise is provided by a standard Brownian motion
(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

The particle moves according to the stochastic ODE

dq(t)=\big(aq(t)+u(t)\big)dt+dW(t),\qquad q(0)=q_{0},

(1.1)

where
a
and
q_{0}
are real parameters. Due to the noise in (
1.1
),
q(t)
and
u(t)
are random variables.

Over a time horizon
T>0
, we incur a
Cost
, given
2
2

2

By rescaling, we can consider seemingly different cost functions of the form
\int_{0}^{T}(q^{2}+\lambda u^{2})
for
\lambda>0
.
by

\textsc{Cost}=\int_{0}^{T}\big\{(q(t))^{2}+(u(t))^{2}\big\}dt.

(1.2)

This quantity is a random variable determined by
a,q_{0},T
and our strategy
\sigma
. Here, the starting position
q_{0}
and time horizon
T
are fixed and known.

We would like to keep our cost as low as possible. We examine several variants of the above control problem, making successively weaker assumptions regarding our knowledge of the parameter
a
. Those variants are as follows.

Variant I: Classical Control

We suppose first that the parameter
a
is known. We write
\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})
to denote the expected
Cost
incurred by executing a given strategy
\sigma
. Our task is to pick
\sigma
to minimize
\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})
. As shown in textbooks (e.g.,
[
3
]
), the optimal strategy
\sigma
is given by

u(t)=-\kappa(T-t,a)q(t)

for a known elementary function
\kappa
; see also Section
2
below. We denote this strategy by
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
. It will be important later to note that
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
satisfies the inequality

|u(t)|\leq C\max\{a,1\}\cdot|q(t)|\;\text{for an absolute constant }C.

(1.3)

Variant II: Bayesian Control

Next, suppose that the parameter
a
is unknown, but is subject to a given prior probability distribution
d\text{Prob}(a)
supported in an interval
[-A,A]
. Our goal is now to pick a strategy
\sigma
to minimize our expected cost, given by

\int_{-A}^{A}\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})\ d\text{Prob}(a).

(1.4)

To solve this problem, we first note a major simplification: In principle, a strategy
\sigma
is a one-parameter family of functions on an infinite-dimensional space, because it specifies
u(t)
in terms of the path
(q(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
for each
t
. However, one computes that the posterior probability distribution for the unknown
a
, given past history
(q(s))_{s\in[0,t]}
, is determined by the prior
d\text{Prob}(a)
, together with the two observable quantities

\zeta_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}q(s)\ dq(s)-\int_{0}^{t}q(s)u(s)\ ds,\qquad\zeta_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(q(s))^{2}\ ds.

(1.5)

Therefore, it is natural to suppose that our optimal strategy
\sigma_{\text{Bayes}}(d\text{Prob})
takes the form

u(t)=\tilde{u}(q(t),\zeta_{1}(t),\zeta_{2}(t),t)

(1.6)

for a function
\tilde{u}
on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

So, instead of looking for a one-parameter family of functions on an infinite-dimensional space, we merely have to specify a function
\tilde{u}
of four variables. It isn’t hard to derive a PDE (the Bellman equation) for the function
\tilde{u}(q,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},t)
that, according to heuristic reasoning, minimizes our expected cost. We have produced approximate solutions
\tilde{u}
to the Bellman equation in numerical simulations, but we don’t have rigorous proofs of existence or regularity. We proceed by imposing the

PDE Assumption
: The Bellman equation has a smooth solution, and the resulting control strategy satisfies the estimate

|u(t)|\leq C_{0}A^{m_{0}}[|q(t)|+1],

(1.7)

for constants
C_{0},m_{0}
independent of
A
.

Since our prior distribution
d\text{Prob}(a)
is supported in
[-A,A]
, a glance at (
1.3
) suggests that the optimal Bayesian strategy should satisfy

|u(t)|\leq C\max\{A,1\}\cdot|q(t)|.

Our numerical simulations appear to confirm this belief. Accordingly, (
1.7
) seems to be a very safe assumption.

Under the above PDE Assumption, we prove in
[
7
]
the natural result that the strategy
\sigma_{\text{Bayes}}(d\text{Prob})
arising from the Bellman equation indeed minimizes the expected cost given by (
1.4
). Moreover, any strategy that differs significantly from
\sigma_{\text{Bayes}}(d\text{Prob})
incurs a significantly higher expected cost.

Our results on Bayesian control pave the way for our analysis of agnostic control.

Variant III: Agnostic Control for Bounded
a

We suppose that our parameter
a
is confined to a bounded interval
[-A,A]
but is otherwise unknown. In particular, we don’t assume that we are given a Bayesian prior probability distribution
d\text{Prob}(a)
. Consequently, we cannot define a notion of expected cost by formula (
1.4
).

Instead, our goal will be to minimize
worst-case regret
, defined by comparing the performance of our strategy with that of the optimal known-
a
strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
. We will introduce several variants of the notion of regret.

Let us fix a starting position
q_{0}
, a time horizon
T
, and an interval
[-A,A]
guaranteed to contain the unknown
a
. To a given strategy
\sigma
, we associate the following functions on
[-A,A]
:

•

Additive Regret
, defined as

\text{AR}(\sigma,a)=\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})-\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a),a;T,q_{0})\geq 0.

•

Multiplicative Regret
(aka “competitive ratio”), defined as

\text{MR}(\sigma,a)=\frac{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})}{\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a),a;T,q_{0})}\geq 1.

•

Hybrid Regret
, defined in terms of a parameter
\gamma>0
by setting

\text{HR}_{\gamma}(\sigma,a)=\frac{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})}{\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a),a;T,q_{0})+\gamma}

Writing
\textsc{Regret}(\sigma,a)
to denote any one of the above three functions on
[-A,A]
, we define the
worst-case regret

\textsc{Regret}^{*}(\sigma)=\sup\{\textsc{Regret}(\sigma,a):a\in[-A,A]\}.

(1.8)

We seek a strategy
\sigma
that minimizes worst-case regret.

The above notions are useful in different regimes. If we expect to pay a large cost, then we care more about multiplicative regret than about additive regret. (If we have to pay
10^{9}
dollars, we are unimpressed by a saving of
10^{5}
dollars.) Similarly, if our expected cost is small, then we care more about additive regret than about multiplicative regret. (If we pay only
10^{-5}
dollars, we don’t care that we might instead pay
10^{-9}
dollars.) If we fix
\gamma
to be a cost we are willing to neglect, then hybrid regret
\text{HR}_{\gamma}(\sigma,a)
provides meaningful information regardless of the order of magnitude of our expected cost.

So far, we have defined three flavors of worst-case regret, and posed the problem of minimizing that regret. The solution to our agnostic control problem is given by the following result, proved in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
[-A,A]
,
q_{0}
,
T
(and
\gamma
if we use hybrid regret). Assume the
PDE Assumption
. Then

(I)

There exists a probability measure
d\emph{Prob}_{*}
on
[-A,A]
for which the optimal Bayesian strategy
\sigma_{\emph{Bayes}}(d\emph{Prob}_{*})
minimizes the worst-case regret among all possible strategies.

Moreover,

(II)

The measure
d\emph{Prob}_{*}
is supported on a finite set
E\subset[-A,A]
, where

(III)

E
is precisely the set of points
a\in[-A,A]
at which the function
[-A,A]\ni a\mapsto\textsc{Regret}(\sigma_{\emph{Bayes}}(d\emph{Prob}_{*}),a)
achieves its maximum.

So, for optimal agnostic control, we should pretend to believe that the unknown
a
is confined to a finite set
E
and governed by the probability distribution
d\text{Prob}_{*}
, even though in fact we know nothing about
a
except that it lies in
[-A,A]
.

It is easy to see that a probability distribution
d\text{Prob}_{*}
satisfying (II) and (III) gives rise to a Bayesian strategy satisfying (I). The hard part of Theorem
1.1
is the assertion that such a probability measure exists.

Theorem
1.1
lets us search for optimal agnostic strategies: We first guess a finite set
E
and a probability measure
d\text{Prob}
concentrated on
E
. By solving the Bellman equation, we produce the strategy
\sigma=\sigma_{\text{Bayes}}(d\text{Prob})
, which allows us to compute the function
[-A,A]\ni a\mapsto\textsc{Regret}(\sigma,a)
. If the maxima of that function occur precisely at the points of
E
, then
\sigma
is the desired optimal agnostic strategy. We have carried this out numerically for several
[-A,A],q_{0},T
.

This concludes our discussion of agnostic control for bounded
a
. Finally, we pass to the most general case.

Variant IV: Fully Agnostic Control

We make no assumption whatever regarding the unknown
a
; our
a
may be any real number, and we are not given a Bayesian prior distribution for it.

Our goal is again to find a strategy that minimizes the
worst-case regret
, defined as in (
1.8
), except that the
\sup
is now taken over all
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

The main result of this paper is that, with negligible increase in regret, we can reduce matters to agnostic control for bounded
a
. More precisely, we will prove the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a time horizon
T
and a nonzero starting position
q_{0}
, as well as constants
C_{0},m_{0}
(for which, see (
1.7
)). Then given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
A>0
for which the following holds.

Let
\sigma
be a strategy for the starting position
q_{0}
and time horizon
T+\varepsilon
. Suppose
\sigma
satisfies estimate (
1.7
) for the given
C_{0},m_{0},A
.

Then there exists a strategy
\sigma_{*}
for the starting position
q_{0}
and time horizon
T
, satisfying the following estimates.

(A)

For
a\in[-A,A]
we have

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})\leq\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\sup\{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a^{\prime};T+\varepsilon,q_{0}):|a^{\prime}-a|\leq\varepsilon|a|\}.

(B)

For
a\notin[-A,A]
we have

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})\leq\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\emph{opt}}(a),a;T,q_{0}).

So, if
a\in[-A,A]
, then
\sigma_{*}
performs almost as well as
\sigma
; and if
a\notin[-A,A]
, then
\sigma_{*}
performs almost as well as the optimal known-
a
strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
.

To apply Theorem
1.2
, we take
\sigma
to be an optimal agnostic strategy for the starting position
q_{0}
and the time horizon
T+\varepsilon
, assuming
a
to be confined to the interval
[-(1+\varepsilon)A,+(1+\varepsilon)A]
.

If our
PDE Assumption
holds, then
\sigma
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
. It’s easy to deduce from Theorem
1.2
that the worst-case hybrid regret of the strategy
\sigma_{*}
(for fully agnostic control) is at most
O(\varepsilon)
percent worse than that of
\sigma
(for agnostic control with
a
confined to
[-(1+\varepsilon)A,(1+\varepsilon)A]
).

For any
\gamma>0
and any strategy
\sigma
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
we let
\text{HR}_{\gamma}^{*}(\sigma;T)
denote the worst-case hybrid regret (over all
a\in\mathbb{R}
) of
\sigma
. The above discussion then implies the following.

Corollary 1.1
.

Fix constants
T>0
,
\eta>0
,
q_{0}\neq 0
. Assume the
PDE Assumption
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we can construct a strategy
\sigma_{\emph{Ag}}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
satisfying

\emph{HR}_{\gamma}^{*}(\sigma_{\emph{Ag}};T)\leq(1+C\varepsilon)\cdot\emph{HR}_{\gamma}^{*}(\sigma;T+\varepsilon)

for any strategy
\sigma
for time horizon
T+\varepsilon
and starting position
q_{0}
.

There are variants of Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.1
for the case of starting position
q_{0}=0
; see Corollary

.

Recap

Let us summarize what has been achieved.

Our goal is to minimize worst-case regret in the setting where
a
may be any real number. Modulo an arbitrarily small increase in regret, we may reduce matters to the case in which
a
is confined to a bounded interval
[-A,A]
. We then look for a probability measure
d\text{Prob}_{*}
living on a finite subset
E\subset[-A,A]
such that the regret of the optimal Bayesian strategy for
d\text{Prob}_{*}
is maximized precisely on
E
. We can calculate the optimal Bayesian strategy for a given prior probability measure by solving a Bellman equation. However, our results are conditional; we have to make an assumption on the existence, size, and smoothness of solutions to the Bellman equation. In numerical simulations, we have produced evidence for our
PDE Assumption
, and we have produced optimal agnostic strategies for cases in which the unknown
a
is confined to a bounded interval.

1.2
Ideas from the Proof of Theorem
1.2

Recall that in Theorem
1.2
we are given a strategy
\sigma
, a small positive
\varepsilon
, and a large positive
A
depending on
\varepsilon
. The strategy
\sigma
applies to a starting position
q_{0}\neq 0
, and a time horizon
T+\varepsilon
. Our task is to find a strategy
\sigma_{*}
satisfying conditions (A) and (B).

In this introduction, we allow ourselves some inaccuracy in the interest of simplicity. See Sections
5
–
7
for correct details.

A recurring theme in the proof of Theorem
1.2
is that because the unknown
a
may be arbitrarily large and positive, the system may be arbitrarily unstable. Consequently, disasters of exponentially small probability may lead to exponentially large expected cost.

To prepare the way for the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we first examine two regimes in which we can do almost as well as if we knew the value of
a
.

The Large
q
Regime

Suppose
q_{0}
is very large. A glance at (
1.1
) suggests that the noise
dW(t)
has only a small effect compared to that of the term
(aq+u)dt
. Therefore, after initially setting
u\equiv 0
and observing
q(t)
for small
t
, we quickly arrive at a guess
\bar{a}
for the unknown
a
. That guess is probably highly accurate. Moreover, the larger
a
is, the sooner we can arrive at the guess
\bar{a}
.

Once we have found
\bar{a}
, we can simply play the known-a strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\bar{a})
until the end of the game at time
T
. (If
\bar{a}
is large positive, then in place of
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\bar{a})
, we use the strategy in which
u(t)=-2\bar{a}q(t)
, which is equivalent to
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\bar{a})
asymptotically for
\bar{a}\gg 1
.)

This “Large
q
strategy” incurs an expected cost almost as small as that of an opponent who knows
a
.

A crucial point is that for large positive
a
, the probability of a significantly inaccurate guess
\bar{a}
is
O(\exp(-cq_{0}^{2}a))
, while the expected cost if such an error occurs is
O(q_{0}^{2}\exp(CTa))
. Hence, for large
q_{0}
, the exponentially tiny probability of disaster overwhelms the exponentially large resulting cost.

The Large
a
Regime

As in our discussion of the Large
q
strategy, a glance at (
1.1
) suggests that the nosie
dW(t)
will have negligibly small effect compared to that of the term
(aq+u)dt
, provided
a
is large positive, say,
a\geq A
. This leads to a “naïve large
a
strategy” in which we initially set
u\equiv 0
, observe
q(t)
for small
t
, arrive at a guess
\bar{a}
for the unknown
a
, and then play the known-a strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\bar{a})
until the game ends at time
T
.

This time, however, the exponentially small probability of an error of the form
\bar{a}\ll a
is dominated by the exponentially large cost of the ensuing disaster. Consequently, the naïve large
a
strategy fails. The cure is to pick some
q_{0}^{*}
, big but not too big, and execute the naïve large
a
strategy only until the first moment we encounter
|q(t)|=q_{0}^{*}
. At that moment, we switch over to the Large
q
strategy. If we never encounter
|q(t)|=q_{0}^{*}
, then we continue with the naïve large
a
strategy until the end of the game.

The above modification limits the damage arising from the event
\bar{a}\ll a
. We have thus produced a “Large
a
strategy” whose expected cost is close to that of the optimal known-a strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
whenever
a\geq A
. When
a<A
, the expected cost of our Large
a
strategy is
O(A^{2})
. That’s bigger than we’d like, but it isn’t exponentially large.

Strengthening the Given Strategy

Next, we consider the strategy
\sigma
given in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. Recall that
\sigma
is assumed to satisfy the estimate (
1.7
). From that estimate we see at once that
\sigma
disastrously undercontrols in case
a\gg A^{m_{0}}
, leading to exponentially large expected cost. We can remedy this defect, by modifying
\sigma
the same way we modified the naïve large
a
strategy. We pick a
q_{0}^{*}
, large but not too large, and switch over from
\sigma
to the Large
q
strategy as soon as we encounter
|q(t)|=q_{0}^{*}
. Thus, we obtain a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
that performs almost as well as
\sigma
for
a\in[-A,A]
, and avoids exponentially large expected cost if
a>A
.

Like
\sigma
, the strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
starts at position
q_{0}
. By rescaling
\tilde{\sigma}
slightly, we arrive at a strategy
\bar{\sigma}
with starting position
(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
. Like
\tilde{\sigma}
, the strategy
\bar{\sigma}
performs almost as well as
\sigma
when
a\in[-A,A]
, and avoids exponentially large expected cost when
a>A
.

Armed with the Large
q
and Large
a
strategies, and the modified strategy
\bar{\sigma}
, we can now describe the strategy
\sigma_{*}
whose existence is asserted by Theorem
1.2
. Without loss of generality, we suppose that our nonzero starting position
q_{0}
is positive.

In the strategy
\sigma_{*}
there are two epochs, a Prologue and a Main Act. During the Prologue, we set
u\equiv 0
. The Prologue ends as soon as we encounter one of the three events

(a)

q(t)=(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}

(b)

q(t)=-q_{\text{rare}}
for a suitable
q_{\text{rare}}>0
(big but not too big)

(c)

the end of the game at time
T
(in which case there is no Main Act).

Event (b) occurs with small probability, regardless of the unknown
a
. If it does occur, then during the Main Act we play a slight variant of the Large
q
strategy to bound our losses.

If instead we enter the Main Act via case (a), then by observing how long it took to pass from the initial position
q_{0}
to the position
(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
, we obtain a guess
\bar{a}
for the unknown
a
. If
a
is large positive, then as in our discussion of the Large
a
strategy, our guess
\bar{a}
is probably highly accurate. Otherwise,
\bar{a}
is likely not so close to
a
, but at least
\bar{a}
probably won’t be large positive. Therefore, for large
A
, our guess
\bar{a}
will at least tell us whether
a\gtrsim A
or
a\ll A
. Accordingly, in case (a) we proceed as follows during the Main Act.

•

If
\bar{a}>A
, then during the Main Act we execute the Large
a
strategy.

•

If
\bar{a}\leq A
, then during the Main Act we execute
\bar{\sigma}
, the improved version of the given strategy
\sigma
.

The Main Act lasts until the end of the game at time
T
.

This completes our description of the strategy
\sigma_{*}
. We hope the reader finds it plausible that our
\sigma_{*}
satisfies conditions (A) and (B) in Theorem
1.2
.

We again warn the reader that our discussion in this introduction is somewhat oversimplified. For instance, our basic stochastic ODE (
1.1
) isn’t obviously well-defined if our strategy allows
u(t)
to be a discontinuous function of
t
. We haven’t even given a rigorous definition of a strategy. Our rigorous discussion starts from scratch in Section
2
.

Survey of Prior Literature

Literature that considers adaptive control of a simple linear system similar to the one considered in this paper commonly consists of one or more of the following features: (
i
) unknown governing dynamics, (
ii
) unknown cost function and (
iii
) adversarial noise. Examples of such work include
[

,

,

,

Initial work in obtaining regret bounds in the infinite time horizon for the related LQR (linear-quadratic regulator) problem was undertaken in
[
1
]
, which proved that under certain assumptions, the expected additive regret of the adaptive controller is bounded by
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
. Further progress was made on this problem in
[
9
]
. Assuming controllability of the system, the authors gave the first efficient algorithm capable of attaining sublinear additive regret in a single trajectory in the setting of online nonstochastic control. See also the related
[
28
]
, which obtained sublinear adaptive regret bounds, a stronger metric than standard regret and more suitable for time-varying systems. Additional adaptive control approaches include
[
12
,
13
]
using the system level synthesis. This expands on ideas in
[
31
]
, which showed that the ordinary least-squares estimator learns a linear system nearly optimally in one shot. Other work uses Thompson Sampling
[
2
,
23
]
or deep learning
[
10
]

[
22

In
[
16
]
, the third and fourth authors of the present paper, along with B. Guillén Pegueroles and M. Weber, found regret minimizing strategies for a problem with simple unknown dynamics (a particle moving in one-dimension at a constant, unknown velocity subject to Brownian motion). In
[
20
]
, along with D. Goswami and D. Gurevich, they generalized these results to an analogous, higher-dimensional system with the addition of sensor noise. In
[
16
]
, they also posed the problem of finding regret minimizing strategies for the more complicated dynamics (
1.1
). In
[
6
]
, the authors of the present paper, along with M. Weber, took the first steps toward resolving this problem. Specifically, we exhibited a strategy for the dynamics (
1.1
) with bounded multiplicative regret.

Historically, significant work has been undertaken in the closely related “multi-armed bandit” problem; see, for instance, the classic papers
[
30
,
32
]
. Recent work considering this paradigm includes
[
34
]
, which used reinforcement learning to obtain dynamic regret whose order of magnitude is optimal, and
[
15
]
, which studied the more general Generalized Linear Bandits (GLBs) and obtains similar regret bounds.

We finally want to point out the parallel field of adversarial control, where the noise profile is chosen by an adversary instead of randomly. This includes
[
27
]
, which attained minimum dynamic regret and guaranteed compliance with hard safety constraints in the face of uncertain disturbance realizations using the system level synthesis framework, and
[
19
]
, which studied the problem of competitive control.

As this list of references is by no means exhaustive and does not do justice to the wealth of studies in the literature, we point the reader to the book
[
21
]
and the references therein for a more thorough overview of online control.

We emphasize that our approach in
[
16
]
,
[
6
]
, and the present paper differs from the other work cited above in that:

•

We seek strategies that minimize the worst-case regret for a fixed time horizon
T
, whereas the literature is mainly concerned with
T\rightarrow\infty
.

•

Typically in the literature one assumes either that the dynamics are bounded or that one is given a stabilizing control. We make no such assumptions and so we must control a system that is arbitrarily unstable.

•

However, we achieve the above ambitious goals only for a simple model system.

We introduce a

(S.1)

For every

(S.5)

For every

We remark that any strategy for time horizon

For any smooth function

\displaystyle q^{\sigma_{v}}(t,a)=q_{0}+W(t)+\int_{0}^{t}(a-v(\tau))q^{\sigma_{v}}(\tau,a)\ d\tau,

Preliminary results on strategies

We begin this section by stating some properties of strategies.

Remark 3.1
.

By symmetry, any strategy
\sigma
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
gives rise to a strategy
\sigma_{-}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
-q_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle q^{\sigma_{-}}(t,a)=-q^{\sigma}(t,a),\qquad u^{\sigma_{-}}(t,a)=-u^{\sigma}(t,a)

and satisfying

\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})=\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{-},a;T,-q_{0}).

For the remainder of this paper we assume, without loss of generality, that
q_{0}>0
; this is justified by Remark
3.1
.

Remark 3.2

(Rescaling property)
.

Let
\sigma
be a strategy for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
with particle trajectories
q^{\sigma}(t,a)
and control variables
u^{\sigma}(t,a)
. Let
\lambda>0
and define the rescaled random variables

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(t,a)=\lambda\cdot q^{\sigma}\bigg(\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}},\lambda^{2}a\bigg),\qquad\tilde{u}(t,a)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot u^{\sigma}\bigg(\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}},\lambda^{2}a\bigg),

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(t)=\lambda\cdot W\bigg(\frac{t}{\lambda^{2}}\bigg).

Note that
\tilde{W}(t)
is a standard Brownian motion and that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,\lambda^{2}T]
we have

\tilde{q}(t,a)=\lambda q_{0}+\tilde{W}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}[a\tilde{q}(\tau,a)+\tilde{u}(\tau,a)]\ d\tau

with probability 1. It follows that
\tilde{q}(t,a)
,
\tilde{u}(t,a)
determine a strategy for time horizon
\lambda^{2}T
and starting position
\lambda q_{0}
. Denoting this strategy by
\sigma_{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\lambda},a;\lambda^{2}T,\lambda q_{0})

\displaystyle=\emph{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\lambda^{2}T}\big(\tilde{q}^{2}(t,a)+\tilde{u}^{2}(t,a)\big)dt\bigg]

\displaystyle=\emph{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\big(\lambda^{4}\cdot(q^{\sigma}(t,\lambda^{2}a))^{2}+(u^{\sigma}(t,\lambda^{2}a))^{2}\big)dt\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\lambda^{4},1\}\cdot\mathcal{J}(\sigma,\lambda^{2}a;T,q_{0}).

We say that
\sigma_{\lambda}
is a
rescaling
of the strategy
\sigma
. Note that the rescaled strategy
\sigma_{1}
is equal to the original strategy
\sigma
.

Remark 3.3

(Branching strategies)
.

We will often decide to switch from one strategy to another.

To explain how we do that, recall that we denote strategies by
\sigma
,
\sigma^{\prime}
,
\hat{\sigma}
, etc. We introduce the notion of a
parametrized strategy
, denoted
\sigma(\cdot)
. For each parameter value
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(some
d\geq 0
),
\sigma(\alpha)
is a strategy. (When
d=0
, a parametrized strategy is just a strategy.) For instance, the strategy
\sigma_{\emph{\text{opt}}}(\beta)
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
is a parametrized strategy;
\beta,T,q_{0}
are the parameters. We remark that the parameter
\alpha
will often include our guess for the unknown
a
.

Now suppose we are given parametrized strategies
\sigma_{0}(\cdot)
,
\sigma_{1}(\cdot)
,…,
\sigma_{N}(\cdot)
. We will combine the
\sigma
’s into a new parametrized strategy
\sigma^{\#}
(a
branching strategy
). Suppose we are given a parameter value
\alpha
and that
\sigma_{0}(\alpha)
is a strategy for some time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
. The strategy
\sigma^{\#}(\alpha)
is then also a strategy for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
.

For each
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we pick a stopping time
\tau(a,\alpha)\leq T
depending on
\alpha
and satisfying the following: for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,T]
, and
\omega\in\Omega
, if
q^{\sigma_{0}(\alpha)}(s,a,\omega)=q^{\sigma_{0}(\alpha)}(s,b,\omega)
for all
s\in[0,t]
then
\tau(a,\alpha,\omega)>t
if and only if
\tau(b,\alpha,\omega)>t
. Note that this condition ensures that the stopping times are defined only in terms of
q
and cannot use knowledge of the unknown
a
. The strategy
\sigma^{\#}(\alpha)
proceeds as follows.

Until time
\tau(a,\alpha)
, we execute the strategy
\sigma_{0}(\alpha)
. If
\tau(a,\alpha)=T
, then we are done.

If
\tau(a,\alpha)<T
, then we will pick a new parametrized strategy
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
and a new parameter
\hat{\alpha}
. The
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
will be one of our given parametrized strategies
\sigma_{1}(\cdot)
, …,
\sigma_{N}(\cdot)
. Our choice of
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\alpha}
is determined by
\alpha
, the history up to time
\tau(a,\alpha)
, and the requirement that
\hat{\sigma}(\hat{\alpha})
is a strategy for starting position
\hat{q}_{0}=q(\tau(a,\alpha))
and some time horizon
\hat{T}\geq(T-\tau(a,\alpha))
.

Once we have picked
\hat{\sigma}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\alpha}
, we forget the past, regard
t=\tau(a,\alpha)
as if we were at
t=0
, and execute the strategy
\hat{\sigma}(\hat{\alpha})
, starting at position
\hat{q}_{0}
. We stop playing at time
T
.

Thus, we have combined our parametrized strategies
\sigma_{0}(\cdot)
,
\sigma_{1}(\cdot),
…,
\sigma_{N}(\cdot)
into a branching (parametrized) strategy
\sigma^{\#}(\cdot)
.

We may then combine our
\sigma^{\#}(\cdot)
with additional parametrized strategies to form further branching strategies.

We note that the strategies
\sigma_{*}
and
\tilde{\sigma}
constructed in Section
5
, the strategy
\text{L}q\text{S}
constructed in Section
6
, and the strategy
\text{L}a\text{S}
constructed in Section
7
are all examples of branching strategies.

\text{for any}\;a\leq-2A,|a-a^{\prime}|<\delta|a|.

(3.16)

Note that (

) and (
3.16
) imply that for a sufficiently large number
\tilde{A}>A
depending on
\varepsilon
,
T
,

, we have

|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a^{\prime};T^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})|\\
<\varepsilon\cdot\mathcal{J}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;|a|\geq 2\tilde{A},|a-a^{\prime}|<\delta|a|.

(3.17)

Next, we note that (
3.3
) implies that
\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})
is of the form

\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})=f_{1}(a,T)q_{0}^{2}+f_{2}(a,T)

for smooth functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
independent of
q_{0}
. Therefore (since
\tilde{A}
is determined by
\varepsilon
,
T
,
q_{0}
), we have

|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a^{\prime};T^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})|<C_{\varepsilon,T,q_{0}}\cdot\delta\\
\text{for any}\;|a|,|a^{\prime}|\leq 3\tilde{A}\;\text{and}\;|a-a^{\prime}|<\delta|a|

and

\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})>c_{\varepsilon,T,q_{0}}\;\text{for any}\;|a|\leq 3\tilde{A}.

Combining the last two inequalities gives

\begin{split}|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a^{\prime};T^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})|<C_{\varepsilon,T,q_{0}}&\cdot\delta\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\\
&\text{for any}\;|a|\leq 2\tilde{A},|a-a^{\prime}|<\delta|a|.\end{split}

(3.18)

Combining (
3.15
), (
3.16
), (
3.18
), and taking
\delta
sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon,T,q_{0}
proves (
3.10
).

We summarize the above discussion (specifically (

), (
3.8
), (
3.9
), and (
3.10
)) as a lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The optimal expected cost for known
a
,
\mathcal{J}_{0}
, has the following properties:

1.

\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})=\kappa(T,a)\cdot q_{0}^{2}+K(T,a).

2.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
A>0
depending on
\varepsilon
and
T
such that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})-2a(q_{0}^{2}+T)|<\varepsilon a(q_{0}^{2}+T)\;\text{when}\;a>A,\;\text{and}

(3.19)

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})-\frac{1}{2|a|}(q_{0}^{2}+T)\bigg|<\frac{\varepsilon}{|a|}(q_{0}^{2}+T)\;\text{when}\;a<-A.

(3.20)

3.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\delta>0
be sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon
,
T
,
q_{0}
, and suppose that
T^{\prime}>0
and
q_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
|T-T^{\prime}|<\delta
and
|q_{0}-q_{0}^{\prime}|<\delta
. Then for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
we have

|\mathcal{J}_{0}(a^{\prime};T^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})|<\varepsilon\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;|a-a^{\prime}|<|a|\delta.

The next lemma says that the strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
is indeed optimal for known
a
, i.e., for fixed
a\in\mathbb{R}
the strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
minimizes the quantity
\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\sigma
be an arbitrary strategy for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
. Then

\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T,q_{0})\geq\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

We suppose that
N\gg 1
is a sufficiently large integer depending on
a,T
; we write
c,c^{\prime},C,C^{\prime},\dots
to denote constants determined by
a,T
. The symbols
c,c^{\prime},C,C^{\prime},\dots
may denote different constants in different occurrences.

We set
h=T/N
and for
\nu=0,\dots,N
we set
t_{\nu}=\nu h
.

By downward induction on
\nu
, we will show that

\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{T}((q(s))^{2}+(u(s))^{2})\ ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu}}\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
\geq\kappa(T-t_{\nu},a)(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+K(T-t_{\nu},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu}).

(3.21)

Once we prove (
3.21
), we take
\nu=0
and let
N\rightarrow\infty
to derive the conclusion of the lemma. So our task is to prove (
3.21
). We begin our induction on
\nu
.

In the base case
\nu=N
, (
3.21
) holds since
\kappa(0,a)=K(0,a)=0
.

For the induction step, we fix
\nu
(
0\leq\nu<N)
, and assume the inductive hypothesis

\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu+1}}^{T}((q(s))^{2}+(u(s))^{2})\ ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu+1}}\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
\geq\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)\cdot(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}+K(T-t_{\nu+1},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1}).

(3.22)

Our goal is then to prove (
3.21
) assuming (
3.22
).

Recall that our Brownian motion is denoted by
(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

We set

\displaystyle\Delta W_{\nu}=W(t_{\nu+1})-W(t_{\nu}),

(3.23)

\displaystyle\omega(\nu)=\sup\{|W(t)-W(t_{\nu})|:t\in[t_{\nu},t_{\nu+1}]\},

(3.24)

\displaystyle||u||_{\nu}=\bigg(\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}u^{2}\ ds\bigg)^{1/2},

(3.25)

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{\nu}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}u\ ds.

(3.26)

For the rest of the proof, we condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu}}
, and we write
\text{E}[\cdots]
to denote the expectation conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu}}
.

We have

\text{E}[\Delta W_{\nu}]=0,\quad\text{E}[(\Delta W_{\nu})^{2}]=h,\quad\text{E}[(\omega(\nu))^{2}]\leq Ch,

(3.27)

and

h|\bar{u}_{\nu}|^{2}\leq||u||_{\nu}^{2};

(3.28)

more generally,

\bigg|\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t}u\ ds\bigg|\leq h^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}\;\text{for}\;t\in[t_{\nu},t_{\nu+1}].

(3.29)

By the definition of a strategy, we have

q(t)-q(t_{\nu})=[W(t)-W(t_{\nu})]+aq(t_{\nu})(t-t_{\nu})+\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t}a[q(s)-q(t_{\nu})]\ ds\\
+\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t}u\ ds\;\text{for}\;t\in[t_{\nu},t_{\nu+1}].

(3.30)

Setting

\text{osc}(\nu)=\sup\{|q(t)-q(t_{\nu})|:t\in[t_{\nu},t_{\nu+1}]\},

(3.31)

we deduce that

\text{osc}(\nu)\leq\omega(\nu)+|aq(t_{\nu})|h+|a|\text{osc}(\nu)\cdot h+h^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}.

Since
h
is less than a small enough constant determined by
a
, we may absorb the term
|a|\text{osc}(\nu)h
into the left-hand side above, to conclude that

\text{osc}(\nu)\leq C\omega(\nu)+Ch\cdot|aq(t_{\nu})|+Ch^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}.

(3.32)

From (
3.31
), (
3.32
), we see that

|q(t)-q(t_{\nu})|\leq C\omega(\nu)+Ch|q(t_{\nu})|+Ch^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}\;\text{for}\;t\in[t_{\nu},t_{\nu+1}],

hence

\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}q^{2}\ ds\geq(1-Ch^{1/10})(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}h-Ch^{9/10}(\omega(\nu))^{2}-Ch^{19/10}||u||_{\nu}^{2}.

(3.33)

Also, (
3.30
), (
3.31
), (
3.32
) yield

\displaystyle q(t_{\nu+1})=(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h+\text{ERR}(\nu),\;\text{with}

(3.34)

\displaystyle|\text{ERR}(\nu)|\leq Ch\omega(\nu)+Ch^{2}|q(t_{\nu})|+Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}.

(3.35)

Using (
3.34
), (
3.35
), we estimate

(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}=[(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h]^{2}+(\text{ERR}(\nu))^{2}\\
+2[(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h]\cdot(\text{ERR}(\nu)).

(3.36)

We have

\begin{split}|[(1+a&h)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h]\cdot(\text{ERR}(\nu))|\\
\leq&C[|q(t_{\nu})|+\omega(\nu)+h^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}]\cdot[h\omega(\nu)+h^{2}|q(t_{\nu})|+Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}]\\
\leq&Ch^{2}|q(t_{\nu})|^{2}+Ch(\omega(\nu))^{2}+Ch^{2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}+Ch^{3/2}|q(t_{\nu})|\cdot||u||_{\nu}\\
&+Ch|q(t_{\nu})|\omega(\nu)+Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}\omega(\nu).\end{split}

(3.37)

Into (
3.37
) we substitute the estimates

\displaystyle h^{3/2}|q(t_{\nu})|\cdot||u||_{\nu}\leq Ch^{3/2}|q(t_{\nu})|^{2}+Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2},

\displaystyle h|q(t_{\nu})|\omega(\nu)\leq Ch^{5/4}|q(t_{\nu})|^{2}+Ch^{3/4}(\omega(\nu))^{2},\;\text{and}

\displaystyle h^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}\omega(\nu)\leq Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}+Ch^{3/2}(\omega(\nu))^{2}.

We find that

|[(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h]\cdot\text{ERR}(\nu)|\\
\leq Ch^{5/4}|q(t_{\nu})|^{2}+Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}+Ch^{3/4}(\omega(\nu))^{2}.

Consequently, (
3.36
) implies the estimate

\begin{split}(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}\geq&[(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})+\Delta W_{\nu}+\bar{u}_{\nu}h]^{2}-Ch^{5/4}|q(t_{\nu})|^{2}\\
&-Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}-Ch^{3/4}(\omega(\nu))^{2}\\
=&\{(1+ah)^{2}-Ch^{5/4}\}(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+(\Delta W_{\nu})^{2}+\bar{u}_{\nu}^{2}h^{2}\\
&+2(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\Delta W_{\nu}+2(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\bar{u}_{\nu}h+2\Delta W_{\nu}\bar{u}_{\nu}h\\
&-Ch^{3/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}-Ch^{3/4}(\omega(\nu))^{2}.\end{split}

(3.38)

Since

\displaystyle|\Delta W_{\nu}\bar{u}_{\nu}h|

\displaystyle\leq|\Delta W_{\nu}|\cdot h^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{1/2}|\Delta W_{\nu}|^{2}+Ch^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{1/2}(\omega(\nu))^{2}+Ch^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2},

estimate (
3.38
) implies the following:

\begin{split}(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}\geq&\{(1+ah)^{2}-Ch^{5/4}\}(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+(\Delta W_{\nu})^{2}\\
&+2(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\Delta W_{\nu}+2(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\bar{u}_{\nu}h-Ch^{1/2}||u||_{\nu}^{2}\\
&-Ch^{1/2}(\omega(\nu))^{2}.\end{split}

(3.39)

Recall that we are conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu}}
, so that
q(t_{\nu})
is deterministic. From (
3.27
) and (
3.39
) we therefore learn that

\begin{split}\text{E}[(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}]\geq&(1+2ah-Ch^{5/4})(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+h+2h(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\text{E}[\bar{u}_{\nu}]\\
&-Ch^{1/2}\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}]-Ch^{3/2},\end{split}

(3.40)

while (
3.27
) and (
3.33
) yield

\begin{split}\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}q^{2}\ ds\bigg]\geq&h(1-Ch^{1/10})(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}-Ch^{19/10}\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}]\\
&-Ch^{19/10}.\end{split}

(3.41)

We bring the inductive assumption (
3.22
) into play. Thanks to (
3.22
), (
3.40
), (
3.41
), we have

\begin{split}\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{T}&(q^{2}+u^{2})\ ds\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
=&\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}q^{2}\ ds\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})+\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{t_{\nu+1}}u^{2}\ ds\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
&+\text{E}\bigg[\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu+1}}^{T}(q^{2}+u^{2})\ ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu+1}}\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\bigg]\\
\geq&\{h(1-Ch^{1/10})(1+h^{1/100})(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}-Ch^{19/10}\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}]-Ch^{19/10}\}\\
&+\{\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}](1+h^{1/100})\}\\
&+\{\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)\text{E}[(q(t_{\nu+1}))^{2}]+K(T-t_{\nu+1},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1})\}\\
\geq&h\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}h^{1/100}\Big)(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+\text{E}\Big[||u||_{\nu}^{2}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}h^{1/100}\Big)\Big]-Ch^{19/10}\\
&+\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)\Big\{(1+2ah-Ch^{5/4})(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+h+2h(1+ah)q(t_{\nu})\text{E}[\bar{u}_{\nu}]\\
&\qquad-Ch^{1/2}\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}]-Ch^{3/2}\Big\}+K(T-t_{\nu+1},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1}),\end{split}

and consequently,

\begin{split}\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{T}(q^{2}+&u^{2})\ ds\bigg](1+h^{1/100})\\
\geq&\Big\{\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)+h[2a\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)+1]+\frac{1}{4}h^{101/100}\Big\}(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}\\
&+\text{E}[||u||_{\nu}^{2}+2h(1+ah)\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)q(t_{\nu})\bar{u}_{\nu}]\\
&+\{K(T-t_{\nu+1},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1})+\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)h-Ch^{3/2}\}\end{split}

(3.42)

Now, recalling (
3.28
), we see that

\begin{split}||u||_{\nu}^{2}+2h(1+ah)\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},&a)q(t_{\nu})\bar{u}_{\nu}\\
\geq&\bar{u}_{\nu}^{2}h+2h(1+ah)\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)q(t_{\nu})\bar{u}_{\nu}\\
=&h(\bar{u}_{\nu}+(1+ah)\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)q(t_{\nu}))^{2}\\
&-h(1+ah)^{2}\kappa^{2}(T-t_{\nu+1},a)(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}\\
\geq&-h(1+ah)^{2}\kappa^{2}(T-t_{\nu+1},a)(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, from (
3.42
), we have

\begin{split}\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{T}&(q^{2}+u^{2})\ ds\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
\geq&\Big\{\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)+h[1+2a\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)\\
&\qquad-(1+ah)^{2}\kappa^{2}(T-t_{\nu+1},a)]+\frac{1}{4}h^{101/100}\Big\}(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}\\
&+\{K(T-t_{\nu+1},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1})\\
&\qquad+\kappa(T-t_{\nu+1},a)h-Ch^{3/2}\}.\end{split}

(3.43)

Recall that

\frac{d}{dt}[\kappa(T-t,a)]=-[1+2a\kappa(T-t,a)-\kappa^{2}(T-t,a)]

and that

\frac{d}{dt}[K(T-t,a)]=-\kappa(T-t,a).

Therefore the expressions in curly brackets on the right in (
3.43
) are bounded below, respectively, by
\kappa(T-t_{\nu},a)
and by

K(T-t_{\nu})-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1})-Ch^{3/2}.

Consequently, (
3.43
) implies the estimate

\begin{split}\text{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{\nu}}^{T}(q^{2}+&u^{2})\ ds\bigg]\cdot(1+h^{1/100})\\
\geq&\kappa(T-t_{\nu},a)\cdot(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+K(T-t_{\nu},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu+1})-Ch^{3/2}\\
\geq&\kappa(T-t_{\nu},a)\cdot(q(t_{\nu}))^{2}+K(T-t_{\nu},a)-h^{1/100}(T-t_{\nu}).\end{split}

(3.44)

Recalling that
\text{E}[\cdots]
here denotes expectation conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{\nu}}
, we see that (
3.44
) is precisely our desired inequality (
3.21
).

This completes our downward induction on
\nu
, proving the lemma.

3.2
The expected cost of simple feedback strategies

Let
v:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and let
\sigma_{v}
denote the simple feedback strategy for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
with gain function
v:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(see Section
2
for the definition of a simple feedback strategy). We fix some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and let
q
denote the particle trajectory
q^{\sigma_{v}}(\cdot,a)
. Note that
q
solves the stochastic ODE

dq=(a-v)qdt+dW,\qquad q(0)=q_{0}

(3.45)

and that

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{v},a;T,q_{0})=\text{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}q^{2}(\tau)\big(1+v^{2}(\tau)\big)\ d\tau\bigg].

(3.46)

Define a (smooth) function
\phi:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\phi(t)=\int_{t}^{T}\big(1+v^{2}(\tau)\big)\exp\bigg(2\int_{t}^{\tau}\big(a-v(s)\big)\ ds\bigg)d\tau;

\phi
solves the ODE

-\phi^{\prime}(t)=2\phi(t)(a-v(t))+1+v^{2}(t),\qquad\phi(T)=0.

(3.47)

By Itô’s Lemma we have

d(q^{2}\phi)=[\phi^{\prime}q^{2}+\phi]dt+(2q\phi)dq.

Combining this with (
3.45
) and (
3.47
) gives

d(q^{2}\phi)=[-(1+v^{2})q^{2}+\phi]dt+(2\phi q)dW.

(3.48)

With probability 1, we have

\int_{0}^{T}d(q^{2}\phi)=q^{2}(T)\phi(T)-q^{2}(0)\phi(0)=-q_{0}^{2}\phi(0);

combining this with (
3.48
) gives

\int_{0}^{T}q^{2}(\tau)(1+v^{2}(\tau))\ d\tau=\phi(0)q_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\phi(\tau)\ d\tau+\int_{0}^{T}2\phi(\tau)q(\tau)\ dW_{\tau}.

(3.49)

Taking expectation and using (
3.46
), we get

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{v},a;T,q_{0})=\phi(0)q_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\phi(\tau)\ d\tau.

We summarize the above result as a lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
v:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and let
\sigma_{v}
be the simple feedback strategy with gain function
v
. Then

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{v},a;T,q_{0})=\phi(0)\cdot q_{0}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\phi(s)ds,

where

\phi(t)=\int_{t}^{T}(1+v^{2}(s))\exp\bigg(2\int_{t}^{s}(a-v(r))dr\bigg)ds.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
be a real number. We define the
constant gain strategy

\text{CG}(\alpha)
to be the simple feedback strategy with the constant gain function
v(t)\equiv\alpha
. We remark that
\text{CG}(\alpha)
is independent of the time horizon
T
and the starting position
q_{0}
.

Lemma
3.3
then implies that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{J}(\text{\text{CG}}(\alpha),a;T,q_{0})=(1+\alpha^{2})\Big[\frac{1}{2(a-\alpha)}\Big[\Big(\frac{e^{2T(a-\alpha)}-1}{2(a-\alpha)}\Big)-T\Big]\\
+\Big(\frac{e^{2T(a-\alpha)}-1}{2(a-\alpha)}\Big)q_{0}^{2}\Big]\;\text{for any}\;\alpha\geq 0,\alpha\neq a,

(3.50)

and for any
a\geq 0
we have

\mathcal{J}(\text{CG}(a),a;T,q_{0})=(1+a^{2})T\Big(q_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}T\Big).

(3.51)

Using (
3.50
) and (
3.51
) we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

For
\alpha\geq 0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{J}(\emph{CG}(\alpha),a;T,q_{0})\leq\begin{cases}C_{T}(1+\alpha^{2})(1+q_{0}^{2})e^{2T(a-\alpha)}&\text{when}\;(a-\alpha)>1/T,\\
C_{T}(1+\alpha^{2})(1+q_{0}^{2})&\text{when}\;|a-\alpha|\leq 1/T,\\
\frac{(1+\alpha^{2})}{2|a-\alpha|}(q_{0}^{2}+T)&\text{when}\;(a-\alpha)<-1/T.\end{cases}

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
the strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(\alpha)
is a simple feedback strategy with gain function
\kappa(T-t,\alpha)
. Note that this strategy depends on
T
but is independent of
q_{0}
. By Lemma
3.1
, and because
\kappa(T-t,a)\leq C\max\{1,a\}
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,T]
, we have
\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\leq C\max\{1,a\}(q_{0}^{2}+T)
. We combine this with Lemma
3.3
to deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
the following holds.

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\emph{opt}}(\alpha),a;T,q_{0})\leq\begin{cases}C\max\{1,a\}(q_{0}^{2}+T)&\text{if}\;\alpha=a,\\
C_{T}(1+\alpha^{2})e^{2|a|T}(1+q_{0}^{2})&\text{for any}\;\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

We remark that the first upper bound in Corollary
3.2
(for
\alpha=a
) is sharp when
a
is large. The second upper bound is far from sharp unless
a\gg\alpha\gg 1
.

4
The uncontrolled system

Let
T>0
,
q_{0}>0
, and let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary. In this section we consider the dynamics

dq=(aq)dt+dW_{t},\qquad q(0)=q_{0}.

(4.1)

We define a random process

X_{t}=e^{-at}q(t)-q_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-as}dW_{s}.

(4.2)

If
a=0
, then
X_{t}
is standard Brownian motion. If
a\neq 0
, then
X_{t}
is a normal random variable with

\text{E}[X_{t}]=0,\qquad\text{Var}[X_{t}]=\frac{1-e^{-2at}}{2a}.

(4.3)

For the remainder of this paper, we adopt the convention that
(1-e^{-2at})/2a
is equal to
t
when
a=0
. With this convention in place, (
4.3
) holds for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
. As a consequence of the above, for any
\delta>0
we have

\text{Prob}(|X_{t}|>\delta)\leq\begin{cases}C\exp(-c\delta^{2}a)\;\text{for any}\;a\geq 0,t\geq 0,\\
C\exp(-c\delta^{2}/t)\;\text{for any}\;|a|t\leq 1/10.\end{cases}

(4.4)

Note that these two cases are not mutually exclusive.

We also note that
X_{t}
satisfies the reflection principle, i.e., for any
M>0
we have

\text{Prob}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}X_{s}\geq M\right)=2\cdot\text{Prob}(X_{t}\geq M).

(4.5)

This is true because (1)
X_{t}
has almost surely continuous paths, (2)
X_{t}
satisfies the strong Markov property, and (3) the random variable
(X_{t+t^{\prime}}-X_{t})
is equal in distribution to
e^{-at}X_{t^{\prime}}
, which is symmetric. Examining the proof of the Reflection Principle for Brownian motion in
[
17
]
, one sees that properties (1)–(3) are sufficient to prove (
4.5
).

if such a time exists and equal to

•

Claim 1
.

Let
a\neq 0
and let
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
, where
\eta_{0}>1

Suppose that
t_{0}<T
and that

. Then, provided
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small depending on
T
, the following hold.

(i)

If
a>0
, then

\emph{Prob}(\tau_{+}>t_{0})\leq C\exp(-c\varepsilon\eta q_{0}^{2}a).

(ii)

If
a<0
, then

\emph{Prob}(\tau_{-}>t_{0})\leq C\exp(-c\varepsilon\eta q_{0}^{2}|a|).

Proof.

We will only prove (i); the proof of (ii) is nearly identical. Assume that
a>0
. By our assumption that
t_{0}<T
, we have

\begin{split}\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}>t_{0})=&\text{Prob}(q(t)<(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}\;\text{for all}\;t\in[0,t_{0}])\\
\leq&\text{Prob}(q(t_{0})<(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}).\end{split}

(4.6)

Let
X_{t}
be as in (
4.2
). If
q(t_{0})<(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
, then (since we assume

) we have

\displaystyle X_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle<e^{-at_{0}}(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}-q_{0}

\displaystyle\leq(1-c\eta\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}-q_{0}.

\displaystyle\leq-c^{\prime}\eta\varepsilon q_{0}

provided
\eta
is larger than an absolute constant and
\varepsilon
is smaller than an absolute constant. This shows that

\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}>t_{0})\leq\text{Prob}(X_{t_{0}}\leq-c^{\prime}\eta\varepsilon q_{0});

applying (
4.4
) (and again using the assumption

) proves (i).
∎

(observe that our choice of
N
ensures that
\Delta t|a|\leq\frac{1}{10}
) and
I_{j}=[j\Delta t,(j+1)\Delta t]
for
j=0,\dots,N-1
. Note that

\text{Prob}((\tau_{+}^{*}<T)\;\text{OR}\;(\tau_{-}^{*}<T))\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\text{Prob}(\exists t\in I_{j}:|q(t)|\geq q_{0}^{*}).

(4.41)

We claim that

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in I_{j}:|q(t)|\geq q_{0}^{*})\leq C\exp(-c_{\rho}q_{0}^{2}|a|)\;\text{for any}\;j=0,\dots,N-1.

(4.42)

Combining (
4.40
)-(
4.42
) proves the lemma. Therefore, it just remains to establish (
4.42
).

Since
a<0
, we have
e^{-at}\geq e^{-aj\Delta t}
for all
t\in I_{j}
. Therefore, in the event that
q(t)\geq q_{0}^{*}
,

X_{t}=q(t)e^{-at}-q_{0}\geq q_{0}^{*}e^{-at}\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho}e^{at}\right)\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}

(4.43)

(we’ve used that
\rho>1
and
e^{at}\leq 1
). In the event that
q(t)\leq-q_{0}^{*}
,

X_{t}=q(t)e^{-at}-q_{0}\leq-q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}.

(4.44)

Combining (
4.43
) and (
4.44
) implies that

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in I_{j}:|q(t)|\geq q_{0}^{*})\leq\text{Prob}(\exists t\in I_{j}:|X_{t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}).

(4.45)

Since
I_{j}\subset[0,(j+1)\Delta t]
, we have

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in I_{j}:|X_{t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t})\\
\leq\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,(j+1)\Delta t]:|X_{t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}).

(4.46)

Equation (
4.5
) implies that

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,(j+1)\Delta t]:|X_{t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t})\\
=2\cdot\text{Prob}\left(|X_{(j+1)\Delta t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}\right).

(4.47)

Recall that
X_{(j+1)\Delta t}
is a normal random variable with mean
0
and standard deviation

\left(\frac{e^{2|a|(j+1)\Delta t}-1}{2|a|}\right)^{1/2}

Recall also that our choice of
N
ensures that
\Delta t|a|<\frac{1}{10}
. Therefore
c<e^{-|a|\Delta t}<1
, and we have

\frac{c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{|a|j\Delta t}|a|^{1/2}}{(e^{2|a|(j+1)\Delta t}-1)^{1/2}}=\frac{c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-|a|\Delta t}|a|^{1/2}}{(1-e^{-2|a|(j+1)\Delta t})^{1/2}}\geq c_{\rho}^{\prime}q_{0}|a|^{1/2}.

Thus, when
|X_{(j+1)\Delta t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}
, the normal random variable
X_{(j+1)\Delta t}
is at least
c_{\rho}^{\prime}q_{0}|a|^{1/2}
standard deviations from its mean of 0. Therefore

\text{Prob}\left(|X_{(j+1)\Delta t}|\geq c_{\rho}q_{0}^{*}e^{-aj\Delta t}\right)\leq C\exp(-c_{\rho}^{\prime}q_{0}^{2}|a|).

(4.48)

Combining (
4.45
) - (
4.48
) proves (
4.42
), finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.2
Estimating the parameter
a

We remain in the setting of the previous section.

In the event that
\tau_{+}<T
we define a random variable

\bar{a}_{+}=\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{\tau_{+}}.

Note that
\bar{a}_{+}
is an estimate for the parameter
a
in (
4.1
). Similarly, in the event that
\tau_{-}<T
we define a random variable

\bar{a}_{-}=\frac{\log(1-\varepsilon)}{\tau_{-}}.

Let
X\subset(0,\infty)
be a subset. We will be interested in estimating the probability of events of the form “
\tau_{+}<T
and
\bar{a}_{+}\in X
”. We will abbreviate such events to “
\bar{a}_{+}\in X
”; note that when
\tau_{+}=T
the random variable
\bar{a}_{+}
is undefined and so the event
\bar{a}_{+}\in X
is meaningless. Similarly, for
Y\subset(-\infty,0)
we let “
\bar{a}_{-}\in Y
” denote the event “
\tau_{-}<T
and
\bar{a}_{-}\in Y
”.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
A^{\#}>1
. Then provided
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small depending on
T
we have

\emph{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>A^{\#})\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon}q_{0}^{2}A^{\#})

for any
|a|\leq A^{\#}/10
.

Proof.

Define

t_{2}=\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{A^{\#}}.

(4.49)

By taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small depending on
T
we ensure that
t_{2}<T
. Therefore

\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>A^{\#})=\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}<t_{2}).

(4.50)

Suppose that
0\leq a\leq A^{\#}/10
. Note that for any
t\in[0,t_{2})
we have

ta<\frac{a}{A^{\#}}\log(1+\varepsilon)<\frac{\varepsilon}{10},

and thus

e^{-at}\geq(1-\varepsilon/10).

Consequently, if
q(t)>(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
for any
t\in[0,t_{2})
, then

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-at}q(t)-q_{0}

\displaystyle>e^{-at}(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}-q_{0}

\displaystyle\geq c\varepsilon q_{0}.

Now suppose that
-A^{\#}/10\leq a<0
. If
q(t)>(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
for some
t\in[0,t_{2})
, then

X_{t}=e^{|a|t}q(t)-q_{0}>\varepsilon q_{0}.

We have shown that

\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}<t_{2})\leq\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,t_{2}):X_{t}\geq c\varepsilon q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;|a|\leq\frac{A^{\#}}{10}.

Combining this with (
4.50
) and (
4.5
), we deduce that

\begin{split}\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}\geq A^{\#})&\leq\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,t_{2}):X_{t}\geq c\varepsilon q_{0})\\
&=2\cdot\text{Prob}(X_{t_{2}}\geq c\varepsilon q_{0}).\end{split}

(4.51)

Since
t_{2}|a|<C\varepsilon
, we apply (
4.4
) to get

\text{Prob}(X_{t_{2}}\geq c\varepsilon q_{0})\leq C\exp(-c\varepsilon^{2}q_{0}^{2}/t_{2})\leq\exp(-c_{\varepsilon}q_{0}^{2}A^{\#}).

Combining this with (
4.51
) proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\delta>0
. Then provided
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small depending on
T
the following hold.

(i)

\emph{Prob}(|a-\bar{a}_{+}|>\delta a)\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}^{2}a)
for any
a\geq\varepsilon^{1/2}
.

(ii)

\emph{Prob}(|a-\bar{a}_{-}|>\delta|a|)\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}^{2}|a|)
for any
a\leq-\varepsilon^{1/2}
.

Proof.

We will just prove (i), as essentially the same argument can be used to prove (ii).

Without loss of generality we assume that
\delta<1/10
. Note that

\text{Prob}(|a-\bar{a}_{+}|>\delta a)=\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>(1+\delta)a)+\text{Prob}(0<\bar{a}_{+}<(1-\delta)a).

(4.52)

Now define

t_{3}=\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{(1+\delta)a}.

By taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small depending on
T
we ensure that
t_{3}<T
, and therefore

\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>(1+\delta)a)=\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}<t_{3}).

For any
t\in[0,t_{3})
, we have

at\leq at_{3}\leq\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{1+\delta}.

Therefore, in the event that
q(t)>(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
for some
t\in[0,t_{3})
, we have

X_{t}=e^{-at}q(t)-q_{0}\geq q_{0}\big((1+\varepsilon)^{1-1/(1+\delta)}-1\big)=c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}.

This implies that

\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>(1+\delta)a)\leq\text{Prob}(\exists t\in(0,t_{3}):X_{t}>c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}).

We use (
4.5
) to deduce that

\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>(1+\delta)a)\leq 2\cdot\text{Prob}(X_{t_{3}}>c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0});

we then note that
t_{3}a<C\varepsilon^{1/2}<1/10
and apply (
4.4
) to get

\text{Prob}(\bar{a}_{+}>(1+\delta)a)\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}^{2}a).

(4.53)

Now define

t_{4}=\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{(1-\delta)a}.

By taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small depending on
T
we ensure that
t_{4}<T
, and therefore

\text{Prob}(0<\bar{a}_{+}<(1-\delta)a)\leq\text{Prob}(\tau_{+}>t_{4}).

If
q(t_{4})<(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}
, then

\displaystyle X_{t_{4}}

\displaystyle<e^{-at_{4}}(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}-q_{0}

\displaystyle=q_{0}((1+\varepsilon)^{-\delta/(1-\delta)}-1)

\displaystyle<-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}.

Therefore

\text{Prob}(0<\bar{a}_{+}<(1-\delta)a)\leq\text{Prob}(X_{t_{4}}<-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0});

applying (
4.4
) gives

\text{Prob}(0<\bar{a}_{+}<(1-\delta)a)\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon,\delta}q_{0}^{2}a).

(4.54)

Combining (
4.52
), (
4.53
), (
4.54
) proves (i).
∎

5
The almost optimal strategy

Throughout this section we fix a time horizon
T>0
and a starting position
q_{0}>0
.

We fix constants
C_{0},m_{0}
as in the definition of an
A
-bounded strategy in Section
2
. Recall that a strategy
\sigma
(for time horizon
T
) is
A
-bounded for some
A>0
if

|u^{\sigma}(t,a)|\leq C_{0}A^{m_{0}}[|q^{\sigma}(t,a)|+1]\;\text{for all}\;a\in\mathbb{R},t\in[0,T].

Throughout this section, we allow all constants and parameters to depend on
C_{0},m_{0}
.

In this section we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then for
\varepsilon_{0}>0
sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon
and
A>0
sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}
, the following holds.

Let
\sigma
be an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and starting position
q_{0}
. Then the strategy
\sigma_{*}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
specified in Section
5.2
satisfies the following.

1.

If
a\in[-A,A]
, then

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})<\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\sup\{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,b;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0}):|a-b|<\varepsilon|a|\}.

2.

If
|a|>A
, then

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})<\varepsilon+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0}).

We now show that Theorem
5.1
implies Theorem
2.1
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be sufficiently small (depending on
\varepsilon
) and let
A
be sufficiently large (depending on
\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon_{0}
) so that the conclusion of Theorem
5.1
holds. Note that we can assume that
\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon
.

Let
\sigma
be an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon
and starting position
q_{0}
. Since
\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon
,
\sigma
is also an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and starting position
q_{0}
. Moreover,

\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0})\leq\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T+\varepsilon,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.1)

Let
\sigma_{*}
denote the strategy defined in Section
5.2
. Observe that Part (B) of Theorem
2.1
follows immediately from Part (B) of Theorem
5.1
. To prove Part (A) of Theorem
2.1
we simply combine equation (
5.1
) with Part (B) of Theorem
5.1
. This proves Theorem
2.1
.

The remainder of Section
5
is devoted to proving Theorem
5.1
. For the remainder of Section
5
we assume that we are given
\varepsilon>0
smaller than some constant. We let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon
, and
A>0
be sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}
.

In Section
5.2
we define the strategy
\sigma_{*}
. First, in Section
5.1
, we establish some preliminary results. In Section
5.3
we prove Theorem
5.1
.

5.1
Preliminaries

5.1.1
Averting disaster when
a
is large

Let
\sigma
be an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and starting position
q_{0}
. Because
\sigma
is
A
-bounded, if
a\gg A
then with high probability the control
u^{\sigma}
will fail to control the particle and so the expected cost of
\sigma
will be much larger than
\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})
(see
[
7
]
). We now show how to use Theorem
2.2
to obtain a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
that performs almost as well as
\sigma
when
|a|\leq A
and much better than
\sigma
when
a\gg A
.

By Theorem
2.2
, there exists a number
q_{0}^{*}>\max\{1,2|q_{0}|\}
depending on
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}
,
T
, and
q_{0}
, but independent of
A
, such that for any
T^{\prime}\in[\varepsilon_{0},2T]
there exists a strategy
\text{L}q\text{S}(T^{\prime})
for time horizon
T^{\prime}
and starting position
q_{0}^{*}
satisfying

\mathcal{J}(\text{L}q\text{S}(T^{\prime}),a;T^{\prime},q_{0}^{*})<(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T^{\prime},q_{0}^{*})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.2)

We now define the strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
.

We execute the strategy
\sigma
from time 0 until time
t^{*}
, where
t^{*}
is equal to the first time
t\in(0,T)
for which
|q^{\sigma}(t)|=q_{0}^{*}
if such a time exists and
t^{*}
is equal to
T
if no such time exists. Note that
t^{*}
is a stopping time.

If
t^{*}=T
, then
\tilde{\sigma}
exercises the same control variable as
\sigma
at all times
t\in[0,T]
.

If
t^{*}<T
, then we execute the strategy
\text{L}q\text{S}(T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*})
(from (
5.2
)) starting from time
t^{*}
and position
|q^{\tilde{\sigma}}(t^{*})|=q_{0}^{*}
. For any
s\in(0,T)
, we have
(T+\varepsilon_{0}-s)>\varepsilon_{0}
. By taking
\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small depending on
T
we ensure that
(T+\varepsilon_{0})<2T
and therefore that the strategy
\text{L}q\text{S}(T+\varepsilon_{0}-s)
is well-defined for any
s\in(0,T)
.

This concludes the definition of the strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
. We now estimate its expected cost.

If we never encounter
|q^{\tilde{\sigma}}|=q_{0}^{*}
, i.e., if
t^{*}=T
, then the strategies
\sigma
and
\tilde{\sigma}
exercise the same control from time 0 to time
T
, hence they incur the same cost.

Suppose instead that we first encounter
|q^{\tilde{\sigma}}|=q_{0}^{*}
at some time
t^{*}<T
. Until time
t^{*}
, the strategies
\sigma
,
\tilde{\sigma}
exercise the same control and incur the same cost. Let
S
denote the expected cost of
\sigma
starting from position
q_{0}^{*}
and time
t^{*}
and continuing until time
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and let
\tilde{S}
denote the expected cost of
\tilde{\sigma}
starting from position
q_{0}^{*}
and time
t^{*}
and continuing until time
T
. Note that

\displaystyle\tilde{S}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(\text{L}q\text{S}(T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*}),a;T-t^{*},q_{0})

\displaystyle<\mathcal{J}(\text{L}q\text{S}(T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*}),a;T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*},q_{0}).

Therefore, by (
5.2
),

\tilde{S}<(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*},q_{0}).

By Lemma
3.2
,

\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*},q_{0})\leq S,

and therefore
\tilde{S}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot S
.

As a consequence of the above discussion, we have

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma},a;T,q_{0})<(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.3)

We now derive another estimate on the expected cost of
\tilde{\sigma}
—this one is much sharper than (
5.3
) when
|a|\gg A
.

From time
0
until time
t^{*}
, we execute the strategy
\sigma
and, by construction, have
|q^{\tilde{\sigma}}(t)|\leq q_{0}^{*}
for all
t\in[0,t^{*}]
. Since
\sigma
is
A
-bounded, we therefore have

|u^{\tilde{\sigma}}(t)|<C_{0}A^{m_{0}}(|q(t)|+1)\;\text{for any}\;t\in[0,t^{*}).

Therefore, from time
0
until time
t^{*}
we incur a cost of at most
C_{T}A^{2m_{0}}(q_{0}^{*})^{2}
with probability 1.

In the event that
t^{*}<T
, then from time
t^{*}
until time
T
we execute the strategy
\text{L}q\text{S}(T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*})
(starting from position
\pm q_{0}^{*}
). Therefore, from time
t^{*}
until time
T
we incur an expected cost that is at most
(1+\varepsilon)
times the expected cost of the optimal known-
a
strategy
\sigma_{\text{opt}}(a)
for time horizon
(T+\varepsilon_{0}-t^{*})
and starting position
q_{0}^{*}
. By Corollary
3.2
, this is at most
C_{T}(q_{0}^{*})^{2}\max\{a,1\}
. We therefore have

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma},a;T,q_{0})<C_{T}(q_{0}^{*})^{2}\max\{A,a\}^{2m_{0}}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
q_{0}^{*}
is determined by
\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon_{0}
,
T
, and
q_{0}
we have

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma},a;T,q_{0})<C_{T,q_{0},\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}\max\{A,a\}^{2m_{0}}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.4)

5.1.2
Rescaling strategies

3.2

Moreover, since

,

, and we have

(5.6)

Combining (
5.5
), (
5.6
), we deduce that

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{\lambda},a;T,\lambda q_{0})\leq\lambda^{4}\cdot\mathcal{J}(\sigma,\lambda^{2}a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.7)

Let
\sigma
be an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and starting position
q_{0}
.

By the results of the previous section (specifically, see (
5.3
) and (

)), there exists a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}
for time horizon

and starting position
q_{0}
satisfying

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma},a;T,q_{0})<(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}(\sigma,a;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}

(5.8)

and

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma},a;T,q_{0})<C_{T,q_{0},\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot\max\{A,a\}^{2m_{0}}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.9)

By the discussion above, there then exists a strategy
\tilde{\sigma}_{1+\varepsilon_{0}}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0}
satisfying

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1+\varepsilon_{0}},a;T,(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})<(1+C\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}(\sigma,(1+\varepsilon_{0})^{2}a;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0})\\
\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}

(5.10)

(recall that
\varepsilon_{0}
is sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon
; in particular, to deduce (

) we assume that
\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon
) and

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1+\varepsilon_{0}},a;T,(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})<C_{T,q_{0},\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot\max\{A,a\}^{2m_{0}}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.11)

From equation (
5.10
), we deduce that

\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1+\varepsilon_{0}},a;T,(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})\\
<(1+C\varepsilon)\cdot\sup\{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,b;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0}):|a-b|<\varepsilon|a|\}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R}

(5.12)

(again taking
\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon
).

5.2
Definition of the almost optimal strategy

Let
\sigma
be an
A
-bounded strategy for time horizon
T+\varepsilon_{0}
and starting position
q_{0}
.

To prove the Main Theorem, we must exhibit a strategy
\sigma_{*}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
q_{0}
satisfying

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})<\varepsilon+(1+C\varepsilon)\cdot\sup\{\mathcal{J}(\sigma,b;T+\varepsilon_{0},q_{0}):|b-a|<\varepsilon|a|\}\\
\text{for any}\;a\in[-A,A]

(5.13)

and

\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{*},a;T,q_{0})<\varepsilon+(1+C\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;|a|>A.

(5.14)

The definition of
\sigma_{*}
requires a bit of setup.

By Theorem
2.3
, provided
A
is sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon,T,q_{0}
there exists a strategy
\text{L}a\text{S}
for time horizon
T
and starting position
(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0}
such that

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(\text{L}a\text{S},a;T,(&1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})\\
&<(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a>\frac{1}{100}A,\end{split}

(5.15)

and

\mathcal{J}(\text{L}a\text{S},a;T,(1+\varepsilon_{0})q_{0})<C_{T,q_{0}}A^{2}\;\text{for any}\;a<\frac{1}{100}A.

(5.16)

By Lemma
3.1
, provided
\varepsilon_{0}
is sufficiently small depending on
\varepsilon,T,q_{0}
we have

We remark that

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}]=\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}}].

Suppose that we enter the Disaster Mitigation Epoch at some time
\tau_{D}\in(0,T)
and suppose that
\bar{a}=\tilde{a}
for some
\tilde{a}>0
. Starting from position
|q|=q_{0}^{*}
and time
\tau_{D}
until time
T
we execute the strategy
BR
. Therefore, by (
7.1
), we have

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}|\mathcal{D}\;\text{AND}\;\bar{a}=\tilde{a}]\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R},\tilde{a}>0.

(7.28)

We remark that the RHS of (
7.28
) is independent of
\tilde{a}
, and so we have

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}|\mathcal{D}(X)]\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R},X\subset(0,\infty).

Combining this with Corollary
3.2
gives

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}|\mathcal{D}(X)]\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\max\{a,1\}\;\text{for any}\;a\in\mathbb{R},X\subset(0,\infty).

(7.29)

In particular, taking
X=(0,\infty)
implies that

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}]\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot A\;\text{for any}\;a\leq A.

(7.30)

We now show that the probability of reaching the Disaster Mitigation Epoch is small when
a\geq A
.

Assume
a\geq A
. Observe that

\text{Prob}(\mathcal{D}((0,3a/4)))\leq\text{Prob}(0<\bar{a}<3a/4).

Provided
A
is sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small depending on
T
, Lemma
4.5
implies that

\text{Prob}(\mathcal{D}((0,3a/4)))\leq C\exp(-c_{\varepsilon}q_{0}^{2}a).

(7.31)

Now suppose that the event
\bar{a}=\tilde{a}
occurs for some
\tilde{a}>3a/4
. In this case, the probability that we enter the Disaster Mitigation Epoch is less than or equal to

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,T]:|\bar{q}(t)|\geq q_{0}^{*}),

where
\bar{q}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is governed by

d\bar{q}=(a-2\tilde{a})\bar{q}dt+dW_{t},\qquad\bar{q}(0)=(1+\varepsilon)q_{0}.

Since
a-2\tilde{a}<0
, we apply Lemma
4.3
to get

\text{Prob}(\exists t\in[0,T]:|\bar{q}(t)|\geq q_{0}^{*})\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\exp(-c_{T,q_{0}}a).

This holds for any
\tilde{a}>3a/4
; we deduce that

\text{Prob}(\mathcal{D}((3a/4,\infty)))\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\exp(-c_{T,q_{0}}a).

(7.32)

Combining (
7.29
), (
7.31
), (
7.32
) gives

\begin{split}\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}]&=\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}((0,3a/4))}]+\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}((3a/4,\infty))}]\\
&\leq C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot a\cdot\exp(-c_{\varepsilon}q_{0}^{2}a)+C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot a\cdot\exp(-c_{T,q_{0}}a)\end{split}

for any
a\geq A
. Taking
A
sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon,T,q_{0}
gives

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}]<\varepsilon\;\text{for any}\;a\geq A.

Combining this with (
7.2
) gives

\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}]<\varepsilon\cdot C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\geq A.

(7.33)

7.4
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Note that

\mathcal{J}(\text{L}a\text{S},a;T,q_{0})=\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{T}]+\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{C}]+\text{E}[\textsc{Cost}_{D}].

(7.34)

By (
7.4
), (
7.26
), and (
7.33
) we have

\mathcal{J}(\text{L}a\text{S},a;T,q_{0})<(1+C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4})\cdot\mathcal{J}_{0}(a;T,q_{0})\;\text{for any}\;a\geq A.

By (
7.3
), (
7.27
), and (
7.30
), we have

\mathcal{J}(\text{L}a\text{S},a;T,q_{0})<C_{T,q_{0}}\cdot A^{2}\;\text{for any}\;a\leq A.

This proves Theorem
2.3
.
