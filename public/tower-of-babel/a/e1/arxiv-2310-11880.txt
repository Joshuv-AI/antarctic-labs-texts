Online Convex Optimization with Switching Cost and Delayed Gradients
Spandan Senapati, Rahul Vaze

Abstract
We consider the online convex optimization (OCO) problem with quadratic and linear switching cost in the limited information setting, where an online algorithm can choose its action using only gradient information about the previous objective function. For L L -smooth and μ \mu -strongly convex objective functions, we propose an online multiple gradient descent (OMGD) algorithm and show that its competitive ratio for the OCO problem with quadratic switching cost is at most 4 ​ ( L + 5 ) + 16 ​ ( L + 5 ) μ 4(L+5)+\frac{16(L+5)}{\mu} . The competitive ratio upper bound for OMGD is also shown to be order-wise tight in terms of L , μ L,\mu . In addition, we show that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm is max ⁡ { Ω ⁡ ( L ) , Ω ⁡ ( L μ ) } \max\{\Omega(L),\Omega(\frac{L}{\sqrt{\mu}})\} in the limited information setting when the switching cost is quadratic. We also show that the OMGD algorithm achieves the optimal (order-wise) dynamic regret in the limited information setting. For the linear switching cost, the competitive ratio upper bound of the OMGD algorithm is shown to depend on both the path length and the squared path length of the problem instance, in addition to L , μ L,\mu , and is shown to be order-wise, the best competitive ratio any online algorithm can achieve. Consequently, we conclude that the optimal competitive ratio for the quadratic and linear switching costs are fundamentally different in the limited information setting.

Introduction

Online convex optimization (OCO) is a very popular optimization paradigm where decisions have to be made with unknown utility functions.
With OCO, at each time
t
,
the goal of an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is to choose an action
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
that minimizes a convex objective function
f_{t}
, however
f_{t}
is revealed after
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has been chosen. In most settings with OCO, once
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
is chosen both
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
and
\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
are revealed.
Knowing all the
f_{t}
’s,
t=1,\dots,T
ahead of time, where
T
is the time horizon, an optimal offline algorithm (
\mathsf{OPT}
) chooses action
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}:=\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, and the
static
regret for
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

Sr\coloneqq\max_{f_{t},t=1,\dots,T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}),
i.e., an adversary can choose the functions
f_{t}
. The name static comes from the
fact that
\mathsf{OPT}
is constrained to use a single action.

Large body of work is known for static regret
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, where if the functions
f_{t}
’s are convex, the optimal regret is
\Theta(\sqrt{T})
, while if the functions
f_{t}
’s are strongly convex
then algorithms with regret at most
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
are known. When the functions
f_{t}
’s are chosen
by an adversary, but the arrival order is randomized, algorithms with better guarantees are also
known
[
3
]
.

Natural generalization of the static regret is the dynamic regret
[
6
,
7
]
, where the regret for a comparator sequence
{\boldsymbol{u}}=({\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{T})
is defined as

Dr({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\max_{f_{t},t=1,\dots,T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}).

For this general dynamic regret definition, sub-linear (in
T
) regret is not always possible, unless some structure is enforced on the comparator sequence.
For example, for a sequence
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, defining
\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\sum_{t=2}^{T}||{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t-1}||
,
the online gradient descent (OGD) algorithm was shown to achieve dynamic regret
Dr({\boldsymbol{u}})=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T}(1+\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}))

[
6
]
, which has been improved to

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T(1+\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}))})
in
[
8
]
, matching the lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{T(1+\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}))})

[
8
]
.

A special case of
Dr({\boldsymbol{u}})
that is popularly studied
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
is by restricting
{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}=({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}^{\star})
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\coloneqq\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the local minimizer of
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
For this case, the best known bound on the dynamic regret has been shown to be
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}))
in
[
12
]
by using the online gradient descent (OGD) algorithm, when the functions
f_{t}
’s are strongly convex and smooth.
Under the special case that the minimizers
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}
lie in the interior of the feasible set,
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}))
regret can be
achieved when the functions
f_{t}
’s are just convex and smooth
[
11
]
.
For strongly convex and smooth functions, defining
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}:=\sum_{t=2}^{T}||{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}||^{2}
,
[
13
]
showed that
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}),\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}\})
is also
achievable as long as at each time
t
, the gradient of
f_{t}
at multiple points is available. Most recently,
[
16
]
improved this guarantee to

\mathcal{O}(\min\{\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}),\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star},FV\})
, where

FV:=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}|f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})-f_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{x}})|
denotes the functional variation.

The constrained version of OCO has also been considered recently in
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, where at each time
t
, the objective is to minimize the loss function
f_{t}
subject to a constraint
g_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq 0
.
Compared to OCO, there are two performance measures, the usual dynamic regret and in addition the constraint violation penalty
\max_{f_{t},g_{t},t=1,\dots,T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
.

One generalization of OCO that has applications in data centers is the OCO with switching cost (OCO-S) problem, where in addition to the penalty paid for choosing sub-optimal decisions, there is a cost for changing actions in successive time slots.
In particular, the cost of an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for OCO-S is

C_{\mathcal{A}}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}),

(1)

where
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})
is a non-negative function that denotes the cost of switching from
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}
to
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
. The goal of
\mathcal{A}
is to either minimize the
dynamic regret

R_{d}(\mathcal{A}):=\max_{f_{t}}(C_{\mathcal{A}}-C_{\mathsf{OPT}}),

(2)

or the competitive ratio

\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\max_{f_{t}}\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}}}{C_{\mathsf{OPT}}},

(3)

where
C_{\mathsf{OPT}}
denotes the cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
, that knows all the
f_{t}
’s ahead of time, for solving (
1
), i.e.

C_{\mathsf{OPT}}:=\min_{\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}\}_{t=1}^{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}).

Two of the most common forms of the switching cost that have been considered in the literature are (a) quadratic switching cost, where
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}
, and (b) linear switching cost, where
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})=\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|
. In this paper, we consider both the competitive ratio (
3
) as well as the dynamic regret (
2
) for (a) and competitive ratio for (b).

The OCO with switching cost model is a versatile framework that captures a number of important problems in real-life. To build motivation, we consider the following problems that can be formulated within the framework.

1.

Server Management in Data Centers: The pioneering work on the OCO problem with switching cost was
[
21
]
that considered the problem of the dynamic right sizing for power-proportional data centers. A data center is modelled as an ensemble of servers, and it involves two important determinations: (i) determining
x_{t}
, the count of the active servers at time
t
, and (ii)
assigning jobs to servers, i.e., determining
r_{i,t}
which represents the arrival rate to server
i
at time
t
. Let
r_{t}
denote the total job arrival rate at time
t
. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{x_{t}}r_{i,t}=r_{t}
must hold, to ensure that all the jobs are processed.
The goal is to choose the number of active servers
x_{t}
and assign jobs to each active server
r_{i,t}
, such that the total cost incurred by the data center across
T
time slots is minimized.

At each time
t
, the data center’s cost is modelled as a combination of (i) operational cost, representing the cost of running an active server, and (ii) the switching cost to model the cost of toggling a server on/off. The costs of operation are modelled by a convex function
\phi(r_{i,t})
, where the function
\phi
is the same for all the servers. This assumption is quite standard and captures a number of commonly used server models. We refer interested readers to the discussion in
[
21
]
for examples of
\phi
. The switching cost
c(x_{t},x_{t-1})
denotes the cost of changing the number of active servers.

With the cost model as mentioned above, the goal of the data center is to choose
x_{t}
and
r_{i,t}
to solve the following convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{x_{t}}\phi(r_{i,t})+\beta c(x_{t},x_{t-1})\bigg),

\displaystyle\text{subject to }r_{i,t}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{x_{t}}r_{i,t}=r_{t}\text{ for all }t,

where the switching cost
c(x_{t},x_{t-1})
can take many forms, for example
(x_{t}-x_{t-1})^{2}
or
(x_{t}-x_{t-1})^{+}
.

2.

Trajectory Tracking: Given a target trajectory
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, a robot positioned at
x_{t}
at time
t
aims to chase the target while maintaining proximity to it, meaning the distance
\left\|x_{t}-{\theta}_{t}\right\|
is small. Additionally, the robot wishes to minimize the energy expended in changing its position from
x_{t-1}
to
x_{t}
. This energy expenditure is influenced by the rate of change between positions and is proportional to the kinetic energy

\big(\frac{\left\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\right\|}{\Delta}\big)^{2}
, where
\Delta
denotes the time taken by the robot to move from
x_{t-1}
to
x_{t}
. Therefore, the problem that we wish to solve for the robot can be formulated as (
1
) with
f_{t}(x_{t})=\left\|x_{t}-\theta_{t}\right\|
and switching cost
c(x_{t},x_{t-1})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\right\|^{2}
, or equivalently
\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}\right\|
, for a certain
\gamma>0
.

3.

Economic Power Dispatch: We consider the problem of economic power dispatch as outlined in
[
22
]
. This scenario involves a set of
k
generators with non-negative outputs
(x_{t}^{(1)},\dots,x_{t}^{(k)})
(collectively represented as a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
) at a given time
t
. The main objective here is to minimize the overall generation cost
\sum_{i=1}^{k}g(x_{t}^{(i)})
, all the while ensuring a balance between the changing power demand
d_{t}
and the availability of renewable energy supply
s_{t}
. To achieve this, a cost function
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
is introduced, which accounts for the generation cost and also incorporates a penalty for any deviation from the demand-supply equilibrium. The cost function is given by

\displaystyle f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}(x_{t}^{(i)})+\lambda_{t}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{t}^{(i)}+s_{t}-d_{t}\bigg)^{2},

where
\lambda_{t}>0
governs the penalty imposed when the demand-supply gap is not adequately maintained. Within the context of power systems, altering the power output of generators incurs significant operational costs. These are known as ramp costs and similar to
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, we model the switching cost as a quadratic function
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}=\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(x_{t,i}-x_{t-1,i})^{2}
.
Therefore, the goal is to minimize the total generation cost, including the imbalance penalty and the ramp costs summed across
T
time slots. This can be formulated as (
1
) with
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
and
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})
as discussed above.

For the OCO-S problem with quadratic switching cost (OCO-SQ), if
f_{t}
’s are convex, then one can show that
the competitive ratio (
3
) of any online algorithm is unbounded. Thus, starting with
[

, to solve (

The online balanced descent (OBD) algorithm, initially proposed by
[

3+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mu})
for

\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}})
as

, which was also shown to be the best possible (order-wise with respect to

) competitive ratio for all possible online algorithms in
[
27
]
.

OCO-S with linear switching cost (OCO-SL), where

C_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|
,
has also been considered. When
f_{t}
’s are convex, the competitive ratio of any online algorithm has a lower bound
\Omega(\sqrt{d})

[

. OBD was the first algorithm to achieve a dimension free competitive ratio upper bound of
3+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\alpha})

. When

’s are

-strongly convex and

\mathcal{O}(({L/\mu})^{\frac{1}{2}})

[

showed that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm is
\Omega(({L}/{\mu})^{\frac{1}{3}})

The main limitation of most prior work on OCO-S for minimizing the competitive ratio with quadratic or linear switching cost is that before the action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
is chosen the full function
f_{t}
is completely revealed, referred to as the
full information setting
in the rest of the paper.
Recall that the information structure in the OCO problem is fundamentally different; action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
at time
t
is chosen without any information about
f_{t}
, and once the action is chosen, one or more gradients
\nabla f_{t}(.)
are revealed. We will refer to this as the limited information setting hereafter.
For minimizing the dynamic regret (
2
) for OCO-S, a
delayed information
setting has also been considered in
[
33
]
, where at time
t
, an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
takes action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
with complete information about
f_{1},\dots,f_{t-1}
. However, a limitation of
[
33
]
is that it completely ignores the switching cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
and trivially bounds the switching cost of its proposed algorithm. This leads to a large gap between the upper and lower bound on the dynamic regret with respect to
L,\mu
for certain problem instances.

The full information setting (as well as the delayed information setting to some extent) is quite imposing for many applications
and difficult to realize in practice. Thus, in this paper, we study the OCO-S problem under the limited information setting, similar to the OCO problem. In particular, the limited information setting we consider is as follows: At time
t
,
\mathcal{A}
takes action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
with only gradient information about
f_{t-1}
at multiple points. Moreover, given that the limited information setting is far
less imposing than the widely considered full information setting, our proposed algorithms are readily applicable to practical settings relevant to
the performance modelling of computer systems and communication networks,
where obtaining large amount of sequential information may not be viable.

Moving from the full information setting to the limited information setting to solve (
3
) is challenging on account of the following reasons.
Most algorithms to solve (
3
) are of the following flavour: given the previous action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}
for algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, choose the new action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
such that
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=\beta c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})
, for some constant
\beta>0
. This algorithm critically needs to know
f_{t}
at all points before choosing
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
. Moreover the analysis using a potential function argument proceeds by comparing the hitting cost
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
of
\mathcal{A}
with that of the
\mathsf{OPT}
, and that is sufficient since the hitting cost and the switching cost for
\mathcal{A}
are multiples of each other. An alternative approach to construct an algorithm in the full information setting is to simply use
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}

[
34
]
and use certain structural properties of strongly convex functions.

With the limited information setting, neither can one directly connect the hitting and the switching costs for an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, since
\mathcal{A}
is never aware of
f_{t}
before making the decision at time
t
, nor use
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}
to make decisions at time
t
. This is the primary challenge we overcome in this paper, where we depart from the potential function approach and proceed as follows:
We propose a simple online gradient descent algorithm that at each time slot makes
\mathcal{O}(L/\mu)
number of gradient descent steps in the limited information setting, and upper bound its cost (
1
). Next, we separately lower bound the cost (
1
) of the
\mathsf{OPT}
for any problem instance. Taking the ratio of the two bounds we get our result. As far as we know, this approach has not been used for solving (
3
). A byproduct of our approach is that we also get an upper bound on the dynamic regret (
2
) which is also shown to be order-wise (with respect to the path length
\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})
) optimal when the switching cost is quadratic.
Similar approach also works for the linear switching cost case as well.

Contributions

2.1
Quadratic Switching Cost

Our contributions for solving the OCO-SQ problem, when all
f_{t}
’s are
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, in the limited information setting are as follows.

1.

We consider an online multiple gradient descent (OMGD) algorithm that takes
K
gradient descent steps (if it can) at time slot
t
using the gradients
\nabla f_{t-1}
at
K
distinct points with step size equal to
\frac{1}{L}
.

OMGD algorithm essentially tries to choose action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
that is close to the optimizer of the function
f_{t-1}
. It is well-known that for strongly convex functions, one iteration of the usual gradient descent algorithm with appropriate step size has the contraction property that
||{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}||\leq c||{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}||
for some
c<1
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}
is the optimal point and
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}
are the iterates. Given that at time
t
, OMGD is trying to get close to the optimizer
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}
of
f_{t-1}
, using the contraction property and the triangle inequality it is possible to bound the sum of squared distances of the action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
produced by OMGD and the optimizer
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}
of
f_{t}
, i.e., the quantity
{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|}^{2}
in terms of the squared path length
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}={\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|}^{2}
, a fundamental quantity for OCO. Finally using the
L
-smoothness property, this can be translated to bound the sub-optimality gap
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})
. This is the usual recipe to bound the cost of OCO with a gradient descent based algorithm in the limited information setting.

With the additional switching cost present in (
1
), analysing OMGD is lot more challenging, since by its very definition OMGD disregards the presence of switching cost. It turns out that the contraction property can be leveraged for bounding the switching cost as well. In the analysis, we show that choosing

K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
, the competitive ratio of OMGD algorithm is at most

4(L+5)+\frac{16(L+5)}{\mu}.

(4)

To derive this competitive ratio bound, we separately upper bound the cost of OMGD and lower bound the cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
. Lower bounding the cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
is a non-trivial exercise and as far as we know has not been accomplished before. We also show that a similar competitive ratio guarantee holds for OMGD even against an arbitrary comparator sequence in Remark
6
.

2.

By constructing a sequence of
f_{t}
’s, we show that the competitive ratio of OMGD is at least
1+\frac{L+1}{4\mu}+\frac{L+1}{8}
, showing that order-wise (with respect to
L,\mu
), the competitive ratio (
4
) for OMGD cannot be improved. The analysis to derive the lower bound on the competitive ratio for the OMGD algorithm also implies that any online algorithm that at time
t
tries to ‘chase’
1
1

1

Defined after Lemma
6
.
the optimizer
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}
also has the same competitive ratio lower bound as the OMGD algorithm.

3.

We also show that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm is at least
\max\{\Omega\left(L\right),\Omega\big(\frac{L}{\sqrt{\mu}}\big)\}
. These bounds follow rather easily by porting prior results from
[
27
]
on the full information model to the limited information model. We in fact conjecture that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm in the limited information setting is at least
\Omega(\frac{L}{{\mu}})
, which will show a fundamental difference in the dependence of
\mu
on the order-wise optimal competitive ratio in the full information setting versus the limited information setting.

4.

Our results to bound the competitive ratio of OMGD directly also provide an upper bound on its dynamic regret (
2
) which is shown to be order-wise optimal (with respect to the path length
\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})
) when the feasible set has a finite diameter. Compared to
[

, our upper bound on the dynamic regret is order-wise tighter in the regime when
L\rightarrow 0
and
\frac{L}{\mu}
is finite, and matches in all other regimes.

2.2
Linear Switching Cost

Our contributions for solving the OCO-SL problem, when all
f_{t}
’s are
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, in the limited information setting are as follows.

1.

Similar to the quadratic switching cost case, we consider the OMGD algorithm with
K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
, and derive an upper bound on its cost. To lower bound the cost of
\mathsf{OPT}
, we reduce any instance of the linear switching cost problem to a quadratic switching cost problem, and use the lower bound derived for the quadratic switching cost case.
As a result, we get the following upper bound on the competitive ratio of OMGD

\displaystyle L(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|)+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\big(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{8L}{\mu}+\frac{12}{\mu}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}},

where we use the shorthand
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
for
\mathcal{P}_{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})
throughout, and
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
. Different from the quadratic switching cost problem, where the competitive ratio depends only on
L,\mu
, the competitive ratio for the linear switching cost problem depends on problem specific quantities
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
, in addition to
L
,
\mu
.

2.

By constructing a sequence of
f_{t}
’s that are
\mu
-strongly convex and
\mu
-smooth, we show that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm can be lower bounded by

\displaystyle\Omega\bigg(\mu(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|)+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\big(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{12}{\mu}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}\bigg),

which order-wise (with respect to
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
) matches with the upper bound on the competitive ratio of OMGD.

Recall that with the full information setting, algorithm with competitive ratio of
2

[
31
]
is known even when the functions
f_{t}
’s are just convex and
d=1
. Thus, our result shows that the limited information setting is fundamentally different than the full information setting when the switching cost is linear, and the competitive ratio of any online algorithm is a function of the path length
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
or the squared path length
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
.

2.3
Comparison with prior work

2.3.1
Quadratic Switching Cost

For
\mu
-strongly convex functions
f_{t}
’s, in the full information setting, the optimal competitive ratio for (
3
) is
\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}})
as
\mu\to 0

[
27
]
. Our results show that the limited information setting is fundamentally different and any online algorithm has to have a competitive ratio growing linearly in
L
and
\frac{L}{\sqrt{\mu}}
.
Surprisingly, when
L=c\mu
, for some finite constant
c\geq 1
(that is independent of
T
and the problem parameters, i.e.,
L,\mu
, etc.), the competitive ratio of the OMGD algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mu})
as
\mu\to 0
similar to the OBD algorithm
[
25
,
26
]
that requires full information about the functions
f_{t}
’s, which is rather remarkable.

For the delayed information setting,
[
33
]
considered the problem of minimizing dynamic regret (
2
) over a compact feasible set. The online gradient descent algorithm that takes only one gradient step achieves a regret of
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{G}{1-\sqrt{1-\mu/L}}\big(1+\frac{1}{L}\big)(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+D)\big)
, while the lower bound on the regret for
\mu
-strongly convex and
\mu
-smooth

\Omega\left(\zeta D(\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|+\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star})\right)
, where
\zeta=\frac{\mu^{3}(1-\rho)^{2}}{32(\mu+1)^{2}},\rho=\frac{\sqrt{\mu+4}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\mu+4}+\sqrt{\mu}}

is the diameter of the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
,
\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right\|\leq G
, and
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{*}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|
. Thus, when
L\rightarrow 0
and
\frac{L}{\mu}
is finite, the upper and lower bounds of
[
33
]
are not close. This gap is primarily the result of
[
33
]
’s analysis that ignores the switching cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
and bounds the switching cost of its proposed algorithm trivially. In comparison to
[
33
]
, we show by a tighter analysis that the OMGD algorithm achieves a dynamic regret which is order-wise (in terms of
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
) the same as the lower bound of
[
33
]
and has a superior dependence of
L,\mu
than the factor
\frac{G}{1-\sqrt{1-\mu/L}}\big(1+\frac{1}{L}\big)
of
[
33
]
, even in the limited information setting.

In comparison to the most related prior work
[
25
,
27
,
34
]
on the competitive ratio analysis of OCO-SQ, our analysis strategy for deriving an upper bound on the competitive ratio (
3
) for the OMGD algorithm is novel. We separately upper bound the cost of the OMGD algorithm and lower bound the cost of
\mathsf{OPT}
, while the known analysis methods use a potential function argument
[
25

27
]
or compare the relative costs of the algorithm against the
\mathsf{OPT}
using structural properties of strongly convex functions
[

. Lower bounding the cost of
\mathsf{OPT}
for the quadratic switching cost is non-trivial while taking into account the quadratic switching cost and as far as we know has not been accomplished before.

3
Problem Setting

OCO with Switching Cost (OCO-S) Problem:
Consider that at time
t=1,2,\dots,T
, a convex function
f_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is going to be revealed and an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
’s objective is to choose action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
so as to the minimize the overall cost

C_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}),

(5)

which comprises of the
hitting cost

f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
and a non-negative
switching cost

c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})
.
Compared to OCO, there is an additional penalty in (
5
) if
\mathcal{A}
changes its decisions between time slots
t-1
and
t
.
The goal for
\mathcal{A}
is to either minimize the
dynamic regret

R_{d}(\mathcal{A})=\max_{f_{t}}(C_{\mathcal{A}}-C_{\mathsf{OPT}}),

(6)

or the competitive ratio

\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}}=\max_{f_{t}}\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}}}{C_{\mathsf{OPT}}},

(7)

where
C_{\mathsf{OPT}}
denotes the cost of the
\mathsf{OPT}
, that knows all
f_{t}
’s ahead of time, for solving (
5
).
In this paper, we consider two forms of the switching cost (a) quadratic switching cost, where
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}
, and (b) linear switching cost, where
c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})=\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|
. For (a) and (b) both, we primarily focus on finding online algorithms with small competitive ratios. Moreover, for (a), we also show how to bound the dynamic regret using the corresponding analysis for the competitive ratio.

Throughout the paper we shall assume that
\mathcal{X}
is a non-empty closed convex set and contains the point
\mathbf{0}
. An additional assumption about the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
is made in Sec.
5.3
while bounding the dynamic regret.
Moreover, for bounding the cost attained by our proposed algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{o}
,
C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}
(
5
), we assume that the optimizers
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
. Discussion about this assumption can be found in Remark
2
.
Note that all algorithms for (
5
), start from the same starting point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
.

For solving (
7
), as far as we know only the
full information setting
is assumed, where
f_{t}
is revealed completely
before

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
is chosen. Because of the switching cost, even in the full information setting, solving (
7
) remains non-trivial.
The full information setting requires incredibly large amount of information compared to the limited information setting in which OCO is studied, and is not reasonable to assume for many applications. Thus, in this paper we intend to solve (
7
) under the limited information setting, where in particular we make the following assumption.

A1
.

At time
t
,
\mathcal{A}
takes action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
with only gradient information about
f_{t-1}
at multiple points.

From
[
27
]
, we know that if functions
f_{t}
’s are just convex and the switching cost is quadratic, the competitive ratio (
7
) of any online algorithm even in the full information setting is unbounded. Similarly, from
[
36
]
, we know that the competitive ratio of any online algorithm when the switching cost is linear in the full information setting is
\Omega(\sqrt{d})
and is therefore arbitarily large for high problem dimensionality. Thus, similar to prior work for solving (
7
), we assume the following:

A2
.

For all
1\leq t\leq T
, the function
f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex. Mathematically, this implies
\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|^{2}\leq f_{t}({\boldsymbol{y}})-f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\langle\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}),{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|^{2},

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Note that with the full information setting,
L
-smoothness is not necessary, however, we need it since we are working with the limited information setting.

We next propose a simple algorithm and bound its competitive ratio for both the quadratic switching cost as well as linear switching cost separately. The challenge in proposing algorithms and analysing them in the limited information setting compared to the full information setting has been discussed in the Introduction
1
.

4
Algorithm

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}\right):=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|
be the projection of point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
onto set
\mathcal{X}
.
We consider the online multiple gradient descent (OMGD) algorithm (Algorithm
1
) for minimizing the cost (
5
) that was originally proposed for minimizing the dynamic regret for OCO. The OMGD algorithm is a very simple algorithm that takes
K
gradient descent steps (if it can) with respect to the objective function
f_{t-1}
at each time
t
. Notably, it is disregarding the presence of the switching cost in (
5
). The presence of the switching cost with OCO-S makes the analysis of OMGD different from
[
13
]
.

Algorithm 1

Online Multiple Gradient Descent (OMGD) for OCO-S

1:

Input:
Problem parameters, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}(\text{starting point)},\mathcal{X}(\text{feasible set}),L(\text{smoothness parameter})
;

2:

Choose
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
;

3:

for

t=2,\ldots,T

4:

Set
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(0)}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}
;

5:

for

k=1,\ldots,K

6:

Query
\nabla f_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k-1)})
;

7:

Update
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k)}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k-1)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k-1)})\right)
;

8:

end

9:

Choose
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(K)}
;

10:

end

Remark 1
.

Eventually, we are going to select
K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
for the OMGD algorithm thus requiring the knowledge of both
L,\mu
. Note that for any gradient descent algorithm, the step size is typically a function of
L
. Thus, over and above the usual assumption of the knowledge of
L
, we are also assuming that
\mu
is known ahead of time.

Remark 2
.

We discuss the reason to assume that the optimizers
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
. We require this assumption in Theorem
1
in order to set
\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})=\mathbf{0}
, and get an upper bound on the cost
{C}_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}
(
5
) (where
\mathcal{A}_{o}
is the OMGD algorithm) that is independent on the norm of the gradient
\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})
. If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\notin\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
, we show in
E
that as long as the quantity

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}
is sufficiently small, i.e.,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star})
, we can get the same order-wise (with respect to
L,\mu
) bound as Theorem
1
.
It is worth noting that for deriving a lower bound in Lemma
5
on the cost (
5
) incurred by
\mathsf{OPT}
, we do not make the assumption that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
.

5
Quadratic Switching Cost

In this section, we consider (
5
) in the context of the quadratic switching cost, i.e., the cost of an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is given by

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}.

(8)

Note that in (
8
), we can also include a scaling factor
\delta>0
for the switching cost
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}
that can tradeoff the relative importance of the two costs. However, that is not necessary since equivalently,
f_{t}
’s can be scaled by
\frac{1}{\delta}
, changing their strong convexity parameter appropriately. Since our guarantees will be a function of the strong convexity parameter, we just consider (
8
).

5.1
Competitive Ratio

5.1.1
Upper Bounding the Cost of OMGD

Let
\mathcal{A}_{o}
be the OMGD algorithm.
We now proceed towards upper bounding the total cost (
8
) of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
. Let, the action chosen by
\mathcal{A}_{o}
at time
t
be
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
. The following Lemma establishes a bound on the quantity
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, which can be interpreted as the cumulative progress of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
, measured as squared distance from the optimizers
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. This quantity will be useful in bounding both the total hitting cost and the total switching cost of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
.

Lemma 1
.

Under assumption
A2
, for
\mathcal{A}_{o}
(Algorithm
1
) with

K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+4{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star},

(9)

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
.

{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}
is the squared path length that captures the squared distance between consecutive optimizers and is a quantity that
naturally controls the cost of any algorithm.

Proof.

At time
t\geq 2
, upon taking a gradient descent step from
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k-1)}
to
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k)}
, the following contraction holds for
\mathcal{A}_{o}
from Lemma
16
(refer
A
) with
\eta=\frac{1}{L}
:

||{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k)}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}\leq\bigg(\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\bigg)||{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k-1)}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}.

Therefore, the net contraction after the end of
k
gradient steps at time
t
is

||{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(k)}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}\leq\bigg(\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\bigg)^{k}||{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(0)}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}.

Since,
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(0)}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}^{(K)}
, the contraction for
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
compared to
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}
is given by
||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}\leq\big(\frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}\big)^{K}||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}.

Since
e^{x}\geq 1+x,
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, choosing
K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
, we get

\displaystyle\bigg(1-\frac{2\mu}{L+\mu}\bigg)^{K}\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{2\mu}{L+\mu}\left\lceil{\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4}\right\rceil\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{2\mu}{L+\mu}{\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4}\bigg)=\frac{1}{4}.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}}||^{2}.

(10)

It is important for further exposition that the constant in the contraction (
10
) is a constant. If
K=1
in the OMGD algorithm, then the constant will depend on
\mu
and
L
and will limit further analysis.
Using (
10
), we have the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(2\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}),

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{one_fourth_0}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}+2{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}+2{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star},

(11)

where to get
(a)
we have used the Triangle inequality followed by
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\leq 2(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2})
.

Rearranging (
11
), we obtain
\sum_{t=2}^{T}||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}}||^{2}\leq||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}||}^{2}+4{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}\implies\sum_{t=1}^{T}||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}}||^{2}\leq 2||{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}}||^{2}+4{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3
.

From the discussion above, we know that at time
t
, OMGD can obtain
\frac{1}{4}
-th contraction by performing gradient descent, using at most
K=\mathcal{O}(\frac{L}{\mu})
gradient steps. However, by using Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG), i.e., at time
t
, instead of performing
K
gradient steps, execute
K
steps of NAG, we can show that the number of steps can be substantially reduced. In
B
, we show that
K={\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{\frac{L}{\mu}}\log{\frac{L}{\mu}})
gradient steps are sufficient to obtain
\frac{1}{4}
-th contraction (
10
).

The next Lemma bounds the total hitting cost
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
.

Lemma 2
.

Under assumption
A2
, the total hitting cost
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
with
K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
is bounded by

\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}+(L+\alpha)(\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+2{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}),

for any
\alpha>0
.

The proof of Lemma
2
follows from the smoothness of
f_{t}
’s and can be found in
C
. Note that for the OMGD algorithm, the contraction obtained in (
10
) is with respect to the preceding optimizer
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}
, and the fact that all functions
f_{t}
’s have Lipschitz continuous gradients, it is not surprising that we can upper bound the total hitting cost of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
. What is not obvious is that it can also be used
to upper bound the total switching cost of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
which we do in the following manner in Lemma
3
: (a) By applying the Triangle inequality and the contraction result (
10
), we relate
\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}
with
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
; (b) Finally, applying the result of Lemma
1
, we relate
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
with
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
.

Recall that the OMGD algorithm by itself does not have any feature to control the switching cost.

Lemma 3
.

Under assumption
A2
, for
\mathcal{A}_{o}
with
K=\lceil\frac{L+\mu}{2\mu}\ln 4\rceil
, the total switching cost is bounded by

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\leq 5\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+10{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality followed by
\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\leq 2(\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2})
, we obtain the following for all
t\geq 2
:

\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{one_fourth_0}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{5}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}.

Hence, the total switching cost of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\frac{5}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{actual_squared_bound}}}}{{\leq}}5\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+10{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star},

where
(a)
follows since
\mathcal{A}_{o}
chooses
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
,
(b)
follows from
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{5}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
established above. This completes the proof.
∎

\mathcal{A}_{o}
.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
A2
, the total cost (

\mathcal{A}_{o}
with

for any

.

5.1.2
Lower bounding the cost of
\mathsf{OPT}

In this section, we lower bound the cost of
\mathsf{OPT}
(
8
).
Without loss of generality, the starting location for all algorithms is
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. For deriving Lemma

, we do not need the assumption that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}({\mathcal{X}})

Lemma 5
.

Under assumption
A2
, the cost incurred by the
\mathsf{OPT}
(
8
) satisfies

\displaystyle{C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{t})+\frac{\mu}{2(\mu+4)}\big({\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}\big).}

(12)

To prove Lemma
5
, we do the following: Using the strong convexity of
f_{t}
’s, first, we lower bound
C_{\mathsf{OPT}}
by an optimization problem of the following quadratic form:

C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\min_{\{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}\}_{k=1}^{d}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\psi(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}),

where we perform a change of variable by
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}=[x_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,x_{T}^{(k)}]^{{\mathsf{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, where
T
is the time horizon. The function
\psi(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k})
is defined as

\psi(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}):=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\frac{\mu}{2}(x_{t}^{(k)}-x_{t}^{\star(k)})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(x_{t}^{(k)}-x_{t-1}^{(k)})^{2}\big)=\frac{\mu}{2}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}^{\star})^{{\mathsf{T}}}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}^{{\mathsf{T}}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k},

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}^{\star}=[x_{1}^{\star(k)},\dots,x_{T}^{\star(k)}]
, and the matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
given by (
41
) is
of dimension
T\times T
.
Clearly,
\psi(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k})
is a quadratic function in
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{k}
. Further, we define a matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}:={\boldsymbol{B}}+\mu{\boldsymbol{I}}
and bound the eigenvalues of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
using the Gershgorin’s Circle Theorem
[
38
, Thm. 6.1.1]
by exploiting the special structure of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. In particular, we show that

\lambda_{i}\big(\mu{\boldsymbol{A}}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\mu+4}{\boldsymbol{A}}\big)\leq 1+\frac{\mu}{\mu+4},
for all
1\leq i\leq T
. Combining these results, we get the required lower bound (
12
). The full proof is rather long and can be found in
D
.

f_{t}=f,

5.1.3
Bounding the Competitive Ratio of OMGD

Combining Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
, we upper bound the competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}_{o}
(the OMGD algorithm) as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Under assumption
A2
, and assuming that the minimizers
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
, the competitive ratio of the OMGD algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{o}
satisfies

{\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}=\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}}{C_{\mathsf{OPT}}}\leq 4(L+5)+\frac{16(L+5)}{\mu}.}

Proof.

Using the result of Lemma
5
and
\frac{\mu}{2(\mu+4)}<1
, we obtain
C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq\frac{\mu}{2(\mu+4)}\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}\big).

From Lemma
4
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})\right\|^{2}+(L+\alpha+5)(\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+2{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}).

(13)

Since
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\in\text{int}(\mathcal{X})
, we have
\nabla f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})=\mathbf{0}
. Taking
\lim\alpha\to 0
in (
13
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+(L+5)(\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+2{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star})\leq(2L+10)\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}\big).

Taking the ratio of the obtained bounds on
C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}
and
C_{\mathsf{OPT}}
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 6
.

In this remark, we generalize the competitive ratio attained by the OMGD algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{o}
against the
\mathsf{OPT}
(
7
), to an arbitrary comparator sequence chosen by an user. Formally, let
{\boldsymbol{u}}=({\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{T})
be an user-specified comparator sequence. The competitive ratio of an online algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes an action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
at time
t
, with respect to
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}}^{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{t})+c({\boldsymbol{u}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t-1})}.

(14)

Note that if the comparator sequence corresponds to the sequence of optimal actions
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\mathsf{OPT}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}^{\mathsf{OPT}})
chosen by the
\mathsf{OPT}
, (
14
) corresponds to the competitive ratio against the
\mathsf{OPT}
(
7
). When the switching cost is quadratic, from the proof of Theorem
1
, we have
C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}\leq(2L+10)\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\right\|^{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2,T}^{\star}\big)
. Therefore,
\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}^{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}^{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\leq(2L+10)\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}4(L+5)\bigg(1+\frac{4}{\mu}\bigg),

where we have defined
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}^{\star}\coloneqq\mathbf{0}
and
(a)
follows from
[
34
, Thm. 2]
, which states that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{4}{\mu},

for any
{\boldsymbol{u}}
. Therefore, our result on the total cost
C_{\mathcal{A}_{o}}
attained by OMGD, naturally allows us to generalize the notion of competitive ratio to arbitrary comparator sequences.

Discussion
: It is instructive to compare our result in Theorem
1
with that of algorithms proposed in
[
26
]
, called OBD, and
[
27
]
, called Regularized OBD, under the full information setting. When all functions
f_{t}
’s are assumed to be strongly convex with strong convexity parameter
\mu
, the competitive ratio of OBD is at most
3+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mu})

[
26
]
while the competitive ratio of Regularized OBD is at most
\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4}{\mu}}\big)

[
27
]
.
Note that both these algorithms require full information about the function
f_{t}
before choosing action
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
for their implementation.
A lower bound of
\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}})
as
\mu\to 0
on the competitive ratio of all algorithms under the full information setting has also been derived in
[
27
]
, showing that Regularized OBD is orderwise optimal.

Our result in Theorem
1
shows that as
\mu\to 0
, when
L=c\mu
, for some finite constant
c\geq 1
(that is independent of
T
and all other problem parameters), the competitive ratio of OMGD is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mu})
which matches the bound of OBD
[
26
]
which is surprising, since OBD requires full information while OMGD works with limited information. As a function of
\mu
, there is an orderwise gap between the lower bound
\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}})

[
27
]
that is applicable under the full information setting and the competitive ratio of OMGD. Therefore, a natural question is: can the lower bound be improved in the limited information setting.
In the next section, we show that the upper bound obtained in Theorem
1
is orderwise tight for the OMGD algorithm.

Also note that Theorem
1
shows that the competitive ratio of OMGD algorithm depends on both
L,\mu
in contrast to OBD or Regularized OBD whose bounds only depend on
\mu
. This is a manifestation of the limited information setting. In the next section, we also show that the competitive ratio of any algorithm scales linearly with
L
in the limited information setting.

We begin by showing that the upper bound on the competitive ratio of the OMGD algorithm obtained in Theorem

Lemma 7

Since we are interested in bounds depending on

Lemma 8

In the limited information setting, there exists a sequence of functions that satisfy assumption

\text{cr}_{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}}}{{C_{\mathsf{OPT}}}}\geq\max\big\{\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right),\Omega\left(L^{2}\right)\big\}.

Lemma 9

Under assumptions

A3

6.2
Lower Bound on the Competitive Ratio

To explicitly bring out the dependence of the lower bound for any online algorithm

Lemma 15
.

In the limited information setting, there exists a sequence of functions that satisfy assumption

\displaystyle\Omega\bigg(\mu(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|)+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\big(\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|\big)^{2}}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}+\frac{12}{\mu}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|}{\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}\bigg).

(23)

Remark 7
.

In contrast to the quadratic switching cost case, the lower bound (Lemma
15
) on the competitive ratio of any online algorithm depends on both the path length and the squared path length. An easy explanation for this can be generated by restricting
the strongly convex functions to the class of strongly convex quadratic functions of the form
f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}\right\|^{2}
. In this case, when the switching cost is linear, choosing

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}
,
\mathsf{OPT}
only incurs a cost of

\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
, which however cannot be matched by an online algorithm which ends up incurring a cost that is both a function of
\mathcal{P}_{T}^{\star}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
. When the switching cost is quadratic, however, choosing

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{\star}
,
\mathsf{OPT}
also incurs a cost of

\mathcal{P}_{2,T}^{\star}
, which is similar to that of an online algorithm.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the OCO-S problem with quadratic and linear switching cost in the limited information setting and proposed an algorithm whose competitive ratio was shown to be order-wise (in terms of
L,\mu
) tight when the objective functions are
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, and the switching cost is quadratic. Lower bounds on the competitive ratio of any online algorithm for the quadratic switching cost are also derived, that show that the linear dependence of
L
on the competitive ratio is unavoidable. The proposed algorithm is also shown to achieve the optimal order-wise (with respect to the path length) dynamic regret in the limited information setting when the feasible set is bounded and the switching cost is quadratic. We also upper bounded the dynamic regret of the proposed algorithm when the feasible set is unbounded, and showed that it can be smaller than the lower bound for the case when the feasible set is bounded under a certain regime. Finally, we showed that the linear switching cost problem is fundamentally different from the quadratic switching cost problem, since the dependence of the squared path length and path length on the competitive ratio is unavoidable for any online algorithm in this case.

\displaystyle\mu(\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{2})+(\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1})=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\implies-\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}+(\mu+1)\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}=\mu{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{2}.

The optimal solution is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}\\
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu+2&-1\\
-1&\mu+1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mu{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}\\
\mu{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}\begin{bmatrix}(\mu+1){\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}+{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\star}\\
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star}+(\mu+2){\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}.

Also, since

, we can express

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}=\bigg(\frac{\mu(\mu+1)}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}+\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{2}+\frac{\mu+1}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}\mathbf{0}\bigg)\in\mathcal{X},

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}=\bigg(\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{1}+\frac{\mu^{2}+2\mu}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}_{2}+\frac{1}{\mu^{2}+3\mu+1}\mathbf{0}\bigg)\in\mathcal{X}.

From the considered special case, we observe that the optimal solution

. Although, the coefficients corresponding to

are positive, they don’t form a convex combination (
\because
they don’t add up to
1

\mathbf{0}
. This makes
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}
a convex combination of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\star},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\star}
, and
\mathbf{0}
each of which belongs to
\mathcal{X}
. Similar reasoning can be applied to
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}
. Therefore, the unconstrained optimization is equivalent to the constrained optimization for
T=2
. Next, we proceed towards proving the Lemma for any general

Notice that the optimal solution

Definition 1

A

\displaystyle\lvert x_{ii}\rvert\geq\sum_{j\neq i}\lvert x_{ij}\rvert,\forall i.

(55)

If a strict inequality is used in (

is said to be strictly diagonally dominant.

Theorem 7

[

Assume a

Lemma 19

The elements of
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}=[\tilde{h}_{ij}]
satisfy

, for all

1\leq j\leq T

Proof.

Recall from (

\displaystyle w_{t}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho^{t}}-\rho^{t}\bigg),v_{t}=c_{1}\frac{1}{\rho^{T-t}}+c_{2}\rho^{T-t},v_{T}=\frac{1}{(\xi-1)w_{T}-w_{T-1}},

(57)

\displaystyle c_{1}:=v_{T}\bigg(\frac{(\xi-1)\rho-\rho^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}\bigg),c_{2}:=v_{T}\frac{1-(\xi-1)\rho}{1-\rho^{2}},

and
\rho:=\frac{\sqrt{\mu+4}-\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\mu+4}+\sqrt{\mu}},\xi:=\mu+2
. Note that since
{\boldsymbol{H}}
is symmetric,
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}
is symmetric as well. Therefore, to prove that all entries

, it is sufficient to consider the elements
\tilde{h}_{t,t+\tau}

, for all

.

To prove the Lemma, it is sufficient to show that

, for all
1\leq t\leq T
. To prove this, we first show that

. From (

),

and

which are given by

\displaystyle w_{T}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho^{T}}-\rho^{T}\bigg),w_{T-1}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho^{T-1}}-\rho^{T-1}\bigg).

(58)

Consider the function

that is monotonically decreasing in

\zeta^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{x^{2}}-1<0
. Since

increases as

increases. Therefore,

. This implies that

, since

. Next we show that

for all

Since

, we have

. Note that, we have substituted

to obtain

. Similarly,

. To prove that

, it is sufficient to prove that the quantity

. Note that

.

Since

and

, for all
1\leq t\leq T-1
.
With

From (

Lemma 20

If the functions

are identically equal to a function

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2(\mu+1)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2},

where

Proof.

Continuing from step (

5

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\min_{\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{t=1}^{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\big),

Define

to be the following:

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{OPT}}(j):=Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\min_{\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{t=1}^{j}}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\big(\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}\big),

It is trivial to notice that

is a monotonically increasing sequence. To obtain a lower bound on

, we can use

and obtain the following:

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq C_{\mathsf{OPT}}(T)\geq C_{\mathsf{OPT}}(1)=Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\right\|^{2}\big),

(59)

The unconstrained minimizer of

\displaystyle\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\right\|^{2}\big)=\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}.

\displaystyle C_{\mathsf{OPT}}\geq Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}}\|^{2}=Tf({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2(\mu+1)}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎
