Non-equilibrium steady states as saddle points and EDP-convergence for slow-fast gradient systems
Alexander Mielke

Abstract
The theory of slow-fast gradient systems leads in a natural way to non-equilibrium steady states, because on the slow time scale the fast subsystem stays in steady states that are controlled by the interaction with the slow system. Using the theory of convergence of gradient systems depending on a small parameter ε \varepsilon (here the ratio between the slow and the fast time scale) in the sense of the energy-dissipation principle shows that there is a natural characterization of these non-equilibrium steady states as saddle points of a so-called B B -function where the slow variables are fixed. We give applications to slow-fast reaction-diffusion systems based on the so-called cosh-type gradient structure for reactions. It is shown that two binary reaction give rise to a ternary reaction with a state-dependent reaction coefficient. Moreover, we show that a reaction-diffusion equation with a thin membrane-like layer convergences to a transmission condition, where the formerly quadratic dissipation potential for diffusion convergences to a cosh-type dissipation potential for the transmission in the membrane limit.

Introduction

1.1
Dissipative evolution equations and gradient systems

We consider systems that are characterized by a state
u
in the state space

X
(a convex subset of a Banach space or a manifold). The evolution process
of the system is assumed to be slow enough such that inertial effects can be
neglected. Moreover, we assume that the dynamics can be described by a balance
of friction forces
\xi
and a potential restoring forces
-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)
,
where the energy potential
{\mathcal{E}}
can be a free energy or a negative
entropy. The friction forces are given by a kinetic relation

\xi=K(u,\dot{u}),

where the mapping
K(u,\cdot):X\to X^{*}
is assumed to satisfy
K(u,0)=0
and

\langle K(u,v),v\rangle\geq 0
for thermodynamic reasons. Thus, the evolution
equations of our interest are
given by

0=K(u,\dot{u})+{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u).

Classical viscous friction leads to a linear kinetic relation
\xi=K(u,\dot{u})={\mathbb{K}}(u)\dot{u}
, while Coulomb friction satisfies
\xi=\mu(u)\,\mathrm{Sign}(\dot{u})
. Along solutions we obtain decay of the (free) energy via

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{E}}(u(t))=\langle{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u(t)),\dot{u}(t)\rangle=-\langle K(u(t),\dot{u}(t)),\dot{u}(t)\rangle\leq 0.

For general nonlinear kinetic relations, the proper generalization of Onsager’s
symmetry
{\mathbb{K}}(u)={\mathbb{K}}(u)^{*}\geq 0
for linear kinetic relations (see

[
Ons31
]
) is the existence of a so-called
dissipation
potential

{\mathcal{R}}
such that
K(u,v)={\mathrm{D}}_{v}{\mathcal{R}}(u,v)
, see the discussion in

[
MRP16
]
. A function
{\mathcal{R}}:X{\times}X\to[0,\infty]
is called a
dissipation potential, if for all
u\in X
, the function

{\mathcal{R}}(u,\cdot):X\to[0,\infty]
is lower semi-continuous, convex and
satisfies
{\mathcal{R}}(u,0)=0
. The dual dissipation potential

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}:X{\times}X^{*}\to[0,\infty]
is defined via

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi):=\sup\big\{\,\langle\xi,v\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(u,v)\>\big|\>v\in X\,\big\}.

By classical duality theory (the so-called Fenchel equivalences

[
Fen49
]
) we have

\xi={\mathrm{D}}_{v}{\mathcal{R}}(u,v)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathcal{R}}(u,v)+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)\leq\langle\xi,v\rangle.

The triple
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
will shortly be called a gradient system (GS), and
the gradient-flow equation associated with the GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
can be
written in two equivalent forms, namely

\text{(I) \ }0={\mathrm{D}}_{v}{\mathcal{R}}(u,\dot{u})+{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{(II) \ }\dot{u}={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big).

(1.1)

Throughout this work we assume
for simplicity that
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
are differentiable; however most of
the theory carries over to the general case of convex functions where

{\mathrm{D}}_{v}{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
are replaced by the set-valued convex
subdifferentials, see e.g.
[
Mie23a
]
.

The restriction to evolution processes given by GS restricts the dynamics to
dissipative processes only, but leads to several favorable thermodynamical
principles. A first observation is that all steady states
u_{*}
of
(
1.1
) are equilibria, i.e.
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*})=0
. One purpose
of this work is to show that in slow-fast GS one can have non-equilibrium
phenomena such as Non-Equilibrium Steady States (NESS) if the slowly changing
components of the system allow the very fast changing components to relax into
a steady state but not into an equilibrium.

Next, we will show how gradient-flow equations can be reformulated in a
thermodynamic variational form, and then explain how the limit of slow-fast
systems can be derived. On this way, NESS will appear naturally and a new
saddle-point characterization of NESS will emanate.

1.2
Energy-dissipation principle for slow-fast systems

Under suitable technical assumptions, the gradient-flow equation
(
1.1
) can be reformulated in a thermodynamical way by the so-called

energy-dissipation principle
(EDP), see

[
RMS08
,
Mie23a
]
. If
u:[0,T]\to X
satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}\big[{\mathcal{R}}(u,\dot{u}){+}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big)\big]\;\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty
, then (I) and (II) hold a.e. in
[0,T]
if and only if the

energy-dissipation inequality
(EDI) holds, namely

{\mathcal{E}}(u(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big({\mathcal{R}}(u,\dot{u}){+}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}(u(0)).

(1.2)

If we now have a family
(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
of GS with a small
parameter
\varepsilon>0
, we say that this family converges in the sense of the EDP
to the limiting GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
if we have the following

\Gamma
-convergences

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\text{ in }X\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}\mathfrak{D}_{0}\text{ \ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}([0,T];X),

(1.3)

where the dissipation functionals
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}:{\mathrm{L}}^{2}([0,T];X\to[0,\infty]
are
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}\big(u(\cdot)\big):=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big({\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}(u,\dot{u}){+}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\big(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u)\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\text{for }\varepsilon>0,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{0}\big(u(\cdot)\big):=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big({\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(u,\dot{u}){+}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(u)\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\text{for }\varepsilon=0.

We refer to
[
LM∗17
]
for the first discussion of this concept, to

[
MMP21
]
for refinements, and to
[
DFM19
,
Fre19
,
MPS21
,
FrL21
,
PeS22
]
for various applications of this
approach.

We emphasize two important properties of EDP-convergence: The first one simply
states that if
u_{\varepsilon}
are solutions to
(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
, we have
convergence
u_{\varepsilon}(t)\to u(t)
in a suitable way, and the initial conditions

u_{\varepsilon}(0)
are well-prepared, i.e.
{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(0))\to{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(u(0))
, then
u
is a solution of the
effective GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
. The second property states that

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be different to a potentially existing
\Gamma
-limit

{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
, i.e.
{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
. The point is that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}

involves a nonlinear construction for the pair
({\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
, which
allows for a transfer of microscopic information of the energy (encoded in

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}{-}{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
) into the dissipation
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
. We will see this
below in Section
5.2
where
{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}^{*}(u,\cdot)
is
quadratic and has a quadratic limit
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{0}
but
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{*}
contains a
cosh-type membrane part for the transmission through the membrane.

The slow-fast GS under consideration are assumed to be of the following form

X=X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}},\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(U,w)=E(U)+\varepsilon\,e(w),\quad{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(U,w;\Xi,\xi)=\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,w;\Xi,\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\;\!\xi\big),

which provides only one class of GS where slow-fast effects can be studied (see

[
MiS20
,
MPS21
]
for other scalings). The associated
gradient-flow equation reads

\binom{\displaystyle\dot{U}}{\displaystyle\varepsilon\;\!\dot{w}}=\partial_{\Xi,\zeta}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big),

(1.4)

which shows nicely the slow-fast structure, because
\varepsilon
only appears once,
namely in front of the time derivative
\dot{w}
of the fast variable

w\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}
.

As in
[
Kue15
]
the above two-scale system gives rise
to the “slow system” (by setting formally
\varepsilon=0
)

\binom{\displaystyle\dot{U}}{0}=\partial_{\Xi,\zeta}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big),

(1.5)

and to the “fast system” (on the short time scale
s=t/\varepsilon
with
{}^{\prime}={\mathrm{d}}/{\mathrm{d}}s=\varepsilon{\mathrm{d}}/{\mathrm{d}}t
)

U=\text{constant},\quad w^{\prime}={\mathrm{D}}_{\zeta}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big).

(1.6)

The steady states
w=\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U)
of (
1.6
) can be seen as NESS
that are nontrivial because of the given fixed values of
U
. In Section

2
we discuss our first main goal, namely a variational,
thermodynamical characterization of NESS as saddle points. The second main
goal is to show that the reduced slow system, obtain by inserting

w=\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U)
into (
1.5
) is again a gradient-flow equation for
a GS
(X_{\mathrm{slow}},{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}=E
and a suitable
effective dissipation potential
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, see Section

3
.

As a simple example we may consider the quadratic GS
(X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}},{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
with

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(U,w)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U,U\rangle+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{f}}w,w\rangle\ \text{ and }\ {\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(\Xi,\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{s}}}\Xi,\Xi\rangle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}\zeta,\Xi\rangle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\langle{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{f}}{\mathrm{f}}}\zeta,\zeta\rangle.

The associated slow system reads

\binom{\dot{U}}{0}=\binom{{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U+{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{f}}w}{{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U+{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{f}}{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{f}}w}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\dot{U}={\mathbb{K}}_{\mathrm{eff}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U\text{ with }{\mathbb{K}}_{\mathrm{eff}}={\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{s}}}{-}{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{f}}{\mathrm{f}}}^{-1}{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}\\
\text{and }w=-({\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{f}}{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{f}})^{-1}{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U.\end{cases}

For this example, it is easy to find the NESS

w=\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U)=-({\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{f}}{\mathrm{f}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{f}})^{-1}{\mathbb{K}}_{{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{f}}}^{*}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U
as well as the effective GS
(X_{\mathrm{slow}},{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
with

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}U,U\rangle
and

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Xi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\Xi,{\mathbb{K}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Xi\rangle
, see Section

4.1
for more details. The purpose of
this work is to generalize these trivial observations to more general settings with

state-dependent
and
nonlinear
kinetic relations.

1.3
Reduction via B-functions and NESS

To see where the motivation for out theory comes from, we return to the
two-scale equation (
1.4
) and apply the EDP convergence theory
as in (
1.3
). We observe that
\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}
takes the
simple form

\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}\big(U,w\big):=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big(\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}(U,w;\dot{U},\varepsilon\!\;\dot{w}){+}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,w;{-}{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t,

and it is tempting to drop the term
\varepsilon\dot{w}
and minimize the integrand
for each
t\in[0,T]
with respect to
w
. However, we will see that this
approach is not correct because we have to find the correct

non-equilibrium steady states
which create a nontrivial flux as a limit
of
\varepsilon\dot{w}
.

We follow the approach in
[
LM∗17
]
and estimate
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}
from below
via

\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}(U,w;\dot{U},\varepsilon\!\;\dot{w})\geq\Big\langle\binom{\Xi}{\zeta},\binom{\dot{U}}{\varepsilon\!\;\dot{w}}\Big\rangle-\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta),

where
(\Xi,\zeta):[0,T]\to X_{\mathrm{slow}}^{*}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}}^{*}
are smooth test
functions. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{\varepsilon}(U,w)\geq\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\Big\langle\binom{\Xi}{\zeta},\binom{\dot{U}}{\varepsilon\!\;\dot{w}}\Big\rangle-{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}(U,w)=E(U)+e(w)
. For general GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
we
call

{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}:\mathrm{dom}({\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}})\subset X{\times}X^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}};\ (u,\xi)\mapsto{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big)

the associated “B-function”.

It turns our that we can now pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
by omitting the term

\varepsilon\!\;\dot{w}
, because it multiplies the given smooth test function

\zeta
. Then, we can maximize with respect to
\zeta
and
minimize with respect to
w
for each individual
t\in[0,T]
. Hence, in terms of the
function
{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}
we are lead to the following sup-inf
problem:

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(U,\Xi):=\sup_{w\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}}\inf_{\zeta\in X^{*}_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta).

(1.7)

We could have equally well defined
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
in terms of the
corresponding inf-sup problem, which in general would produce a larger
function, see (
2.6a
). However, our theory is based on the
existence of global saddle points, which imply that the sup-inf equals the
inf-sup. Our choice of taking the infimum first is motivated by the simplicity
of minimizing the convex functional

\zeta\mapsto{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)
over

\zeta\in X^{*}_{\mathrm{fast}}
. By construction, the function

(\Xi,\zeta)\mapsto{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)
is always
convex. The classical existence theory for global saddle points works under the
additional assumption that

(U,w)\mapsto{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)
is concave. This
condition is valid for Otto’s gradient structure for diffusion (cf. Section

5.2
), but not for nonlinear chemical reaction systems as
treated in Section
4.2
.
For our general theory, we do not need to assume concavity of

(U,w)\mapsto{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)
.

We say that the reduced B-function
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
has
BER
structure
if there exists an effective dissipation potential

{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}:X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{slow}}\to[0,\infty]
such that it can be written as

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(U,\Xi)={\mathfrak{B}}_{E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}}(U,\Xi)={\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U,\Xi)-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big(U,{-}{\mathrm{D}}E(U)\big).

(1.8)

Using the EDP backwards, we see that the effective GS

(X_{\mathrm{slow}},E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
with the gradient-flow equation

\dot{U}=\partial_{\Xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big(U,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(U)\big)

indeed describes the limiting dynamics.

Thus, the main point in applying this theory successfully is to show the
existence of the BER structure
(E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
for the reduced B-function

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
. And it is here were the theory of NESS comes into play. The
definition of NESS in the above context means that we fix
\overline{U}\in X_{\mathrm{slow}}
and
want to find the NESS
\overline{w}\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}
such that

\binom{0}{0}=\binom{{\mathrm{D}}_{\Xi}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,\overline{w};-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(\overline{w})\big)}{{\mathrm{D}}_{\zeta}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\!\big(U,\overline{w};-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(\overline{w})\big)}-\binom{\overline{y}}{0},\quad U=\overline{U}\in X_{\mathrm{slow}},\quad\overline{y}\in X_{\mathrm{slow}}.

(1.9)

We refer to (
2.2
) for the general case involving port mappings

P:X\to Y
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}:X^{*}\to Y^{*}
, which reduce to (
1.9
) if we
choose
Y=X_{\mathrm{slow}}
,
P(U,w)=U
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}(\Xi,\zeta)=\Xi
. We observe that
fixing
U=\overline{U}
artificially generates a flux
\overline{y}
which is generated by
the NESS
\overline{w}
associated with
\overline{U}
, i.e. one can think of
\overline{y}
as a
Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
U=\overline{U}
.

1.4
Global saddle points provide NESS

The first major link between the theory of NESS and the above saddle-point
reduction for B-functions is the fact that a NESS
\overline{w}
solving

) gives rise to a
global null-saddle

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}\big(\overline{U},w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(\overline{U}),-{\mathrm{D}}e(\overline{w})\big)\leq 0

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}\big(\overline{U},\overline{w};-{\mathrm{D}}E(\overline{U}),-{\mathrm{D}}e(\overline{w})\big)\leq{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}\big(\overline{U},\overline{w};-{\mathrm{D}}E(\overline{U}),\zeta\big).

discusses conditions under which null-saddles

automatically satisfy

, where

is
NESS solving (

). There seem to exist a number of different

[

,

,

]
,

[

, Cha. V]

[

The second important link arises from the fact that the existence of

Applications in reactions and diffusion

,

2
NESS and constrained saddle points

We first provide our definition of NESS for general
port gradient
systems
and derive a few fundamental properties. Next we collect some
basic facts about unconstrained saddle points, then introduce the notion of
constrained saddle points using linear port mappings
P:X\to Y
and

{\mathbb{P}}^{*}:X^{*}\to Y^{*}
and show that under a suitable additional condition that
these constrained saddle points are indeed NESS. Section

2.4
shows a further characterization of NESS as
null-minimizers of a suitable auxiliary functional. Section

2.5
provides the main result concerning the BER structure
for reduced B-functions
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
if the associated NESS are null-saddles.

2.1
Port gradient systems and NESS

For the definition of NESS, we generalize the setting from slow-fast GS to
general port gradient systems (PGS)
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
, where the linear
port mapping
{\mathbb{P}}:Y\to X
maps the port space
Y
to the (tangent space of)

X
. The dual mapping
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}:X^{*}\to Y^{*}
maps the thermodynamical driving forces
in
X^{*}
to the reduced ones in
Y^{*}
. In the slow-fast GS we choose

Y=X_{\mathrm{slow}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}y=(y,0)\in X=X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}}
. The “fast system”
(
1.6
) generalizes to the port gradient-flow equation

\dot{u}={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big)-{\mathbb{P}}y\quad\text{with }y\in Y\ \text{ and }\ {\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta\in Y^{*}.

(2.1)

Like in the theory of port-Hamiltonian systems (cf.
[
EMV07
,
VaM23
]
), one can consider
y(t)\in Y
as an input
(possibly time-dependent) and
\eta(t)\in Y^{*}
as the output, or vice versa. A
simple calculation gives, along solutions, the power balance

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\underbrace{\big\langle{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u),{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\big\rangle_{X}}_{\geq 0}+\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y},

see e.g.
[
EMV07
, Eqn. 18]
.

Definition 2.1
(NESS for a PGS)

A state
\overline{u}\in X
is called a NESS for the PGS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
if
there exist a constant pair
(y,\eta)\in Y{\times}Y^{*}
such that
u(t)=\overline{u}
is a solution of
(
2.1
) and
\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}\neq 0
. This means that
\overline{u}
is a NESS if and only if it satisfies the NESS equations

0={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))-{\mathbb{P}}y,\qquad{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})=-\eta\in Y^{*},\qquad\overline{y}\in Y.

(2.2)

Defining the dissipation function
\Phi_{*}(u,\xi):=\langle\xi,{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)\rangle_{X}\geq 0
we see that every NESS dissipates energy

\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}=\Phi_{*}(\overline{u},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))>0.

This is in contrast to equilibrium solutions
\widetilde{u}\in X
satisfying

0={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\widetilde{u},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u}))
.

Under the assumption that for all
\eta\in Y^{*}
there exists a unique NESS

\overline{u}_{\eta}
of (
2.2
) with Lagrange parameter
y=\overline{y}_{\eta}
, we
can define the
port relation

\mathfrak{P}:Y^{*}\to Y;\ \eta\mapsto\overline{y}_{\eta}.

Such port relations which will play a crucial role in the sequel. As a
first result we observe, that in the case that
{\mathcal{R}}
is independent of the
state, the port relation can be obtained easily from
{\mathcal{R}}
, it is independent
of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
, and it is given as the differential of an effective
potential
\mathsf{R}_{Y}
. We refer to Section
4.1
for a simple
and explicit case.

Proposition 2.2
(Port relation for state-independent dissipation)

If
{\mathcal{R}}:X\to[0,\infty]
is a state-independent dissipation potential, then
the port relation
\mathfrak{P}
is given by

y=\mathfrak{P}(\eta)=\partial\mathsf{R}^{*}_{Y}(\eta)\quad\text{with }\ \mathsf{R}_{Y}(v)={\mathcal{R}}({\mathbb{P}}v).

Equivalently,
\mathsf{R}^{*}_{Y}
is characterized via
\mathsf{R}^{*}_{Y}(\eta):=\inf_{\xi:\,{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)
.

Proof.
By Fenchel’s equivalence we have
\xi\in\partial\Psi(y)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ y\in\partial\Psi^{*}(\xi)
. Hence, the NESS equation
(
2.2
) can be rewritten as

{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{R}}\big(\overline{u}_{\eta},{\mathbb{P}}\overline{y}_{\eta}\big)=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta}),\quad{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta})=-\eta,\quad\overline{y}_{\eta}\in Y.

Hence, we have the relation

\eta={\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{R}}\big(\overline{u}_{\eta},{\mathbb{P}}\overline{y}_{\eta}\big)={\mathrm{D}}_{y}\mathsf{R}_{Y}(\overline{u}_{\eta},y)
. Only if
{\mathcal{R}}
and hence
\mathsf{R}_{Y}
are independent
of
u
, we obtain an explicit relation between
\eta
and
\overline{y}_{\eta}
.
Applying Fenchel’s equivalence once again, we arrive at the assertion

\overline{y}_{\eta}={\mathrm{D}}_{\eta}\mathsf{R}_{Y}^{*}(\eta)
.

The second characterization of
\mathsf{R}_{Y}^{*}
follows by an
application of Lemma
2.13
.

2.2
Classical saddle points

For a gradient system
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
we consider the B-function

{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(u,\xi)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)),

(2.3)

which is defined on
X{\times}X^{*}
. It will be the source of a series of results
concerning NESS. We will simply write
{\mathfrak{B}}
in place of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}

if the relevant GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
is clear.

Remark 2.3
(Slope dissipation term)

In the definition of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
, we use the formula

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))
to denote the so-called
{\mathcal{R}}
-
slope
,
which should properly be defined by its weak lower semicontinuous hull,
namely

{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{R}}(u):=\inf\Big\{\>\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u_{n},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{n}))\;\Big|\;u_{n}\rightharpoonup u,\ u_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}({\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}})\>\Big\}.

(2.4)

For example the linear diffusion equation
\dot{u}=\Delta u
with no-flux
boundary conditions is the gradient-flow equation associated with the Otto
gradient system
({\mathrm{P}}(\Omega),{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathrm{B}}{\mathrm{z}}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{Otto}}^{*})
with

{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathrm{B}}{\mathrm{z}}}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}(u)\;\!\mathrm{d}x
, and

{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{Otto}}^{*}(u,\xi)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\xi|^{2}u\;\!\mathrm{d}x
. We
obtain the Fisher information

{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{R}}(u)=\int_{\Omega}2|\nabla\sqrt{u}|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x
, which is well defined even
when
u=0
in a set of positive measure, whereas
u\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}({\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}})
needs

u>0
a.e.

Subsequently, we will still write
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))
to emphasize the
structure of the problem; but whenever analysis is done, one has to replace
this term by
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Obviously, for all
u\in X
the functions
{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\cdot):X^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are
convex, and in some cases we have concavity of
{\mathfrak{B}}(\cdot,\xi)
for all

\xi\in X^{*}
. In the case of quadratic energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{Q}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}u,u\rangle-\langle\ell,u\rangle
and a quadratic dual dissipation potential

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{Q}}(u,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,{\mathbb{K}}\xi\rangle
we obtain the
simple quadratic B-function

{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{Q}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{Q}}}(u,\xi)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{quad}}(u,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\xi,{\mathbb{K}}\xi\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\big\langle{\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell,{\mathbb{K}}({\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell)\big\rangle.

(2.5)

which has the above-mentioned concave-convex property on
X{\times}X^{*}
. However,
our theory does not need concavity in
u
.

Definition 2.4
(Global saddle points)

Given two Banach spaces
X
and
Y
and a functional

{\mathscr{B}}:X{\times}Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we call a point
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in X{\times}Y
a
(global)
saddle point
for
{\mathscr{B}}
if

{\forall\,x\in X,\ y\in Y:\quad{\mathscr{B}}(x,\overline{y})\leq{\mathscr{B}}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\leq{\mathscr{B}}(\overline{x},y).}

Thus, we are in the situation of classical saddle-point theory, see

[
EkT74
]
and Appendix
A
, which collects the most
important facts. In particular, we will use that the infimum over

\xi\in X
and the supremum over
u\in X
can be interchanged if a saddle point
exists, see Lemma
A.1
:

(a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SI}_{{\mathscr{B}}}:=\sup_{u\in X}\inf_{\xi\in X^{*}}{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)\ \leq\ \inf_{\xi\in X^{*}}\sup_{u\in X}{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi):=\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathscr{B}}},

(2.6a)

(b)

\displaystyle\text{saddle point $(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})$ exists}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \mathrm{SI}_{{\mathscr{B}}}=\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathscr{B}}}={\mathscr{B}}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi}).

(2.6b)

For
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{quad}}
in (
2.5
) with invertible
{\mathbb{A}}
we see
that
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
is a saddle point if and only if
\overline{\xi}=0
(use

{\mathbb{K}}>0
) and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})={\mathbb{A}}\overline{u}{-}\ell=0
, viz.
\overline{u}={\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\ell
. We then have
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{quad}}(\overline{u},0)=0
. If
{\mathbb{A}}
is not
invertible, we have multiple saddle points, namely all
\overline{u}
minimizing

u\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\big\langle{\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell,{\mathbb{K}}({\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell)\big\rangle
. Then, one has

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{quad}}(\overline{u},0)=-\min\big\{\,\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell,{\mathbb{K}}({\mathbb{A}}u{-}\ell)\rangle\>\big|\>u\in X\,\big\}
.

As a second example we consider

X={\mathbb{R}}^{i_{*}},\quad{\mathcal{E}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{A}}u,u\rangle,\quad\text{and }\ {\mathcal{R}}(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{*}}\big(\sigma|v_{i}|+\frac{\nu}{2}|v_{i}|^{2}\big)=\sigma|v|_{1}+\frac{\nu}{2}|v|_{2}^{2}.

(2.7)

Now we have
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{*}}\frac{1}{2\nu}\big(\max\{|\xi_{i}|{-}\sigma,0\}\big)^{2}
such that
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)=0
for

|\xi|_{\infty}\leq\sigma
. Hence, we have many saddle points
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
, namely all pairs with
|\overline{\xi}|_{\infty}\leq\sigma
and
|{\mathbb{A}}\overline{u}|_{\infty}\leq\sigma
. Again all saddle points satisfy
{\mathfrak{B}}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=0
.

In the following, we give a general characterization of saddle points and
complement the result with a discussion of critical points
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\xi})
of

{\mathscr{B}}
satisfying
\widetilde{\xi}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u})
.

Theorem 2.5
(Unconstrained saddle points)

Consider a GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
and set
{\mathscr{B}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
as in
(
2.3
).

(a) A pair
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
is a (global) saddle point of
{\mathscr{B}}
if and only
if

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=0\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))=\min_{u\in X}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)).

(b) If there exists
u_{*}\in X
with
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*})=0
, then
all saddle points satisfy the relation

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}\big(\overline{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})\big)=0
, and hence

{\mathscr{B}}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=0
.

(c) If in addition to the condition in (b), the dual dissipation potentials

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\cdot):X^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are strictly convex, then all saddle points
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
satisfy
\overline{\xi}=0
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})=0
.

Proof.
Part (a).
Minimizing
{\mathscr{B}}
with respect to
\xi\in X^{*}
and using
use
0={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,0)\leq{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)
yields

\mathrm{SI}_{{\mathscr{B}}}=\sup_{u\in X}\big({-}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\big)=:\overline{S}\ \leq 0.

Moreover, choosing
\xi=0
we obtain an upper bound for
\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathscr{B}}}
, namely

\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathscr{B}}}\leq\overline{S}
. Thus, with (
2.6a
) we conclude

\mathrm{SI}_{{\mathscr{B}}}=\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathscr{B}}}=\overline{S}
.

Hence, we conclude that a saddle point
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
must satisfy

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))=-\overline{S}
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=0
,
which is the desired result (a).

Part (b).
We obtain
\overline{S}=0
and the result follows.

Part (c).
This is an immediate consequence of the implication

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \xi=0
and of Part (b).

The following result will not be used in the sequel, but it gives a first
insight why the saddle-point theory for
{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
is useful. The
point is that there is a certain redundancy in the Euler-Lagrange equation for
critical points
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\xi})
of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
, when the critical
point satisfies
\widetilde{\xi}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u})
.

Lemma 2.6
(Euler-Lagrange equations if
\widetilde{\xi}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u})
)

The pair
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\xi})=(\widetilde{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u}))

\in X{\times}X^{*}
is
a critical point of
{\mathscr{B}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
if and only if

{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\widetilde{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u}))=0\in X
, i.e.
\widetilde{u}
is a steady state
for the gradient system
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
.

Proof.
We have
{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)[\widetilde{\xi}]={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)[\widetilde{\xi}]
and

{\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)[\widetilde{u}]={\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)[\widetilde{u}]-{\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))[\widetilde{u}]+{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\big[{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u)[\widetilde{u}]\big].

Inserting
(u,\xi)=(\widetilde{u},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{u}))
we see a
cancellation and the two equations for a critical point reduce to

0={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathscr{B}}(u_{*},\xi_{*})[\widetilde{\xi}]={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u_{*},\xi_{*})[\widetilde{\xi}],\quad 0={\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathscr{B}}(u_{*},\xi_{*})[\widetilde{u}]={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u_{*},\xi_{*})\big[{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*})[\widetilde{u}]\big].

Thus, we see that it is necessary and sufficient to satisfy

\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u_{*},\xi_{*})=0
.

Remark 2.7
(Perturbed gradient systems)

If a the gradient-flow equation is perturbed by a
general vector field
V
in the form

\dot{u}=V(u)+\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)),

(2.8)

then steady states can still be obtained as stationary points of a B-function

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}
, namely

\widetilde{{\mathscr{B}}}(u,\xi)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)-\langle\xi,V(u)\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))-\langle{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u),V(u)\rangle.

Assume that
u_{*}
is a steady state for (
2.8
), namely

0=V(u_{*})+\partial_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*})),

(2.9)

then
(u,\xi)=(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*}))
is a stationary point for

\widetilde{{\mathscr{B}}}
and obviously the critical value is
0
,
i.e.
\widetilde{{\mathscr{B}}}(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*}))=0
.

To see the stationarity we observe

{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)-V(q)
,
and (
2.9
) yields
{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*}))=0
as desired.
For the derivative with respect to
u
we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{D}}_{u}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)[w]={\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)[w]-\langle\xi,{\mathrm{D}}V(u)[w]\rangle-{\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))[w]

\displaystyle\qquad-{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))[{-}{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u)[w,\cdot]\rangle-{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u)[w,V(u)]-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\big[{\mathrm{D}}V(u)[w]\big].

Inserting
\xi={-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)
the first term cancels
the third, and the second term cancels the last. Moreover, the forth
and the fifth terms cancel if we additionally use
(
2.9
). Hence,
{\mathrm{D}}_{u}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*}))=0
,
and
(u_{*},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{*}))
is indeed a stationary point for
\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}
.

2.3
Constrained saddle points

We now study the constrained case generated by a port GS

(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
, where the linear port mapping
{\mathbb{P}}:Y\to X

is used to drive the GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
. We start by introducing a
constrained saddle-point problem and then relate the existence of constrained
saddle points to the existence of NESS.

Problem 2.8
(Constrained saddle-point problem (CSPP))

Given the port GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
with B-function

{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
and port mapping
{\mathbb{P}}:Y\to X
, the

constrained saddle-point problem
for
\eta\in Y^{*}
consists in finding a
saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\in X{\times}X^{*}
for

\displaystyle\forall\;u\in X\text{ with }{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta

\displaystyle\forall\;\xi\in X^{*}\text{ with }{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta:

(2.10)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(u,\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\leq{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\leq{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(\overline{u}_{\eta},\xi).

The saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\in X{\times}X^{*}
is called a

null-saddle
if
{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})=0
.

In light of our theory, it will be important to know whether a constrained
saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})
gives rise to a NESS. For
this we need the following preliminary result.

Lemma 2.9
(Null-saddles)

If a constrained saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\in X{\times}X^{*}

satisfies
\overline{\xi}_{\eta}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta})
, then it is a
null-saddle. Vice versa, if
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u}_{\eta},\cdot):X^{*}\to[0,\infty]
is
strictly convex and
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})\in X{\times}X^{*}
is a null-saddle,
then it satisfies
\overline{\xi}_{\eta}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta})
.

Proof.
The first statement follows directly from

{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))=0
for all
u\in X
.

For the opposite implication we start from a null-saddle
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
. From
0={\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})\leq{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(\overline{u},\xi)
for all
\xi
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta
we see that
\xi=\overline{\xi}
and
\xi=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})
are global minimizers. By strict convexity the minimizer is
unique, which proves the assertion.

We recall the example in (
2.7
) where
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is not strictly
convex, because of
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)=0
for
|\xi|_{\infty}\leq\sigma
. The saddle
points
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
are characterized by
|{\mathbb{A}}\overline{u}|_{\infty}\leq\sigma

and
|\overline{\xi}|_{\infty}\leq\sigma
and all of them are null-saddles. However,
only some satisfy
\overline{\xi}=-{\mathbb{A}}\overline{u}
. This shows that the result does not
hold without a further condition like our strict convexity.

The next result shows that constrained saddle points of the form

(\overline{u}_{\eta},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta}))
satisfies a simplified Euler-Lagrange
equation, and hence are NESS. Of course, without strong further global
assumptions one cannot expect that all NESS can be obtained as constrained
saddle points.

Proposition 2.10
(Euler-Lagrange equations for NESS)

If the constrained saddle point in (
2.8
) has the form

(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=(\overline{u}_{\eta},-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta}))
, then
\overline{u}_{\eta}

is a NESS for the port GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
with the constraint
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta
.

Proof.
In (
2.10
) we may consider variations
\widehat{\xi}
and
\widehat{u}
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\widehat{\xi}=0
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u)[\widehat{u}]=0
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)[\widehat{\xi}]={\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)[\widehat{\xi}]=\langle\widehat{\xi},{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)\rangle_{X},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)[\widehat{u}]={\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)[\widehat{u}]-{\mathrm{D}}_{u}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))[\widehat{u}]+{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\big[{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(u)[\widehat{u}]\big].

Inserting
\overline{\xi}={-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})
we obtain a cancellation in the second
line leading to

0=\langle\widehat{\xi},{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))\rangle_{X}\quad\text{and}\quad 0=\langle{\mathrm{D}}^{2}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})[\widehat{u}],{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})\rangle_{X}.

However, by the choice of admissible variations, we see that the second
relation follows from the first. Hence we have

{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))\in\big(\mathrm{ker}({\mathbb{P}}^{*})\big)^{\perp}
.

To conclude, we simply use Fredholm’s
alternative (theorem):

\big(\mathrm{ker}({\mathbb{P}}^{*})\big)^{\perp}:=\big\{\,x\in X\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=0\ \Rightarrow\ \langle\xi,x\rangle_{X}=0\,\big\}\;=\;\mathrm{ran}({\mathbb{P}}):=\big\{\,{\mathbb{P}}y\>\big|\>y\in Y\,\big\}.

With this we have

{\mathrm{D}}_{\xi}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}))\in\big\{\,{\mathbb{P}}y\>\big|\>y\in Y\,\big\}
, which gives
\overline{y}\in Y
such that (
2.2
) holds.

We now provide a general existence result for constrained saddle points and for
NESS. For this we use the following additional assumptions on

{\mathfrak{B}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}:Y\to X
:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{aligned} \forall\,u\in X,\ \xi\in X^{*}:&\quad{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\cdot):X^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}\ \text{ and }\ {-}{\mathfrak{B}}(\cdot,\xi):X\to{\mathbb{R}}\ \text{ are}\\
&\text{lower semi-continuous, strictly convex, and coercive};\end{aligned}\right\}

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\forall\,\eta\in Y^{*}:\quad\big\{\,u\in X\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta\,\big\}\text{ is nonempty, closed, and convex}.

(2.11b)

Theorem 2.11
(Existence of constrained saddle points)

Assume that
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
satisfies
(
2.11
). Then, for each
\eta\in Y^{*}
there exists a unique
constrained saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})
for
{\mathfrak{B}}
(in the
sense of (
2.10
)).

If additionally the mapping
X\ni u\mapsto{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)\in X^{*}
is
surjective, then these saddles points are NESS satisfying

\overline{\xi}_{\eta}=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta})

and (
2.2
).

Proof.
The existence follows by applying Proposition

A.2
with
{\bm{U}}=\big\{\,\xi\in X^{*}\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta\,\big\}
and

\widetilde{\bm{V}}=\big\{\,u\in X\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta\,\big\}
, where we extend
{\mathfrak{B}}
by

-\infty
outside of
\widetilde{\bm{V}}
if it is not a linear space. Thus, we find a
unique constrained saddle point
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u}_{\eta})=-\eta
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\overline{\xi}_{\eta}=\eta
.

Using Lemma
2.9
it is sufficient to show that
(\overline{u}_{\eta},\overline{\xi}_{\eta})
is a null-saddle. Because we already have a saddle point,
it is sufficient to show
\mathrm{SI}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\leq 0\leq\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}
.

For the lower estimate we simply use
\inf_{\xi\in{\bm{U}}}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)\leq{\mathfrak{B}}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))=0
. Taking the supremum over
u\in\widetilde{\bm{V}}
we find

SI_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\leq 0
.

For the upper estimate we start from a general
\xi\in{\bm{U}}
such that the
surjectivity of
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}
provides a
u_{\xi}\in{\bm{V}}
with

\xi=-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u_{\xi})
. With this we have

\sup_{u\in{\bm{V}}}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)\geq{\mathfrak{B}}(u_{\xi},\xi)=0
. Now taking the infimum
over
\xi\in{\bm{U}}
yields
\mathrm{IS}_{{\mathfrak{B}}}\geq 0
as desired.

2.4
NESS as minimizers

The main observation of the last section is that the equation
(
2.2
) does not have a simple variational structure. Its
characterization via the above saddle-point theory provides some kind of
variational structure, but needs a doubling of variables. Moreover, in
nonlinear problems (non-quadratic
{\mathfrak{B}}
) the saddle-point theory for solving
infinite-dimensional problem like PDEs is technically rather demanding.

The naive way of treating the CSPP
(
2.10
) would be to minimize first with
respect to
\xi
providing
\xi=\Xi_{B}(z,u)
and such that it remains to study
the minimization problem

u\ \mapsto\ {\mathcal{R}}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\Xi_{B}(z,u))\quad\text{subject to }B{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=z.

This approach is doable but has the disadvantage that it is difficult to keep
enough control on the mapping
u\mapsto\Xi_{B}(z,u)
to tackle the final
minimization problem.

The following result shows that the saddle point can be turned into a
minimization problem by applying a suitable Legendre transformation with
respect to the constrained variable
\xi
, but keeping a dual parameter

\Lambda\in Z^{*}
. Thus, the minimization formulation stays explicit in terms
of the constituents of the GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
. Moreover, it is more
directly related to the Euler-Lagrange equations (
2.2
) and
the original thermodynamical functions.

Proposition 2.12
(NESS as minimizers)

For all
\eta\in Y^{*}
any global minimizer
(\overline{u},\overline{y})\in X{\times}Y
of the
constrained minimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize }

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{R}}(u,{\mathbb{P}}y)+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))+\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\text{over }

\displaystyle(u,y)\in X{\times}Y\quad\text{subject to }\ {\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta

gives rise to a constrained saddle points
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})\in X{\times}X^{*}
for
(
2.10
) where we can choose any
\overline{\xi}\in\mathrm{Argmin}\big\{\,{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\xi)\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta\,\big\}
. Vice versa,
if
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
is a constrained saddle point for (
2.10
), then

(\overline{u},\overline{y})
with
\overline{y}\in\mathrm{Argmax}\big\{\,\langle\eta,y\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(\overline{u},{\mathbb{P}}y)\>\big|\>y\in Y\,\big\}
is a global minimizer for (
2.12
).

Moreover, if
(\overline{u},\overline{y})
is a null-minimizer, then
(\overline{y},\overline{\xi})
is a
null-saddle, and under the additional assumption of strict convexity of

{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\cdot)
it defines a NESS solving (
2.2
).

Proof.
We define the auxiliary dissipation potentials

\Psi_{u}:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\infty};\,y\mapsto{\mathcal{R}}(u,{\mathbb{P}}y)
and can now apply
Lemma
2.13
below. This gives

\displaystyle\inf_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.91673pt}\xi:{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)

\displaystyle=\Psi^{*}_{u}(\eta)=\sup_{y\in Y}\big(\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}{-}{\mathcal{R}}(u,{\mathbb{P}}y)\big).

(2.13)

With this we obtain the following chain of identities:

\displaystyle\mbox{}\hskip-10.00002pt\sup_{u\in X\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta}\ \inf_{\xi\in X^{*}\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(u,\xi)=\sup_{u\in X\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta}\Big(\big[\inf_{\xi\in X^{*}\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,\xi)\big]-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\eqref{eq:MaaMieRelation}}}{=}\sup_{u\in X\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta}\Big(\ \Big[\sup_{y\in Y}\big(\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}-{\mathcal{R}}(u,{\mathbb{P}}y)\big)\Big]-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\Big)

\displaystyle=-\inf_{u:{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta\atop y\in Y}\Big({\mathcal{R}}(u,{\mathbb{P}}y)+\langle\eta,y\rangle_{Y}+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(u,{-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\Big).

This shows that the minimization problem (
2.12
) is equivalent to
the CSPP (
2.10
) if we choose
\xi=\overline{\xi}\in X^{*}
in
(
2.13
) optimally, i.e.
\overline{\xi}=\mathrm{Argmin}\big\{\,{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\overline{u},\xi)\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta\,\big\}
.

Moreover, the values are the same up to a minus sign. Hence, null-minimizers

(\overline{u},\overline{y})\in X{\times}Y
correspond to null-saddles
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})\in X{\times}X^{*}
, and the remaining statement follows from Lemma

2.9
.

In the above proof the relation in (
2.13
) relies on the
following general result.

Lemma 2.13

For a lower semi-continuous and convex
\Psi:X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\infty}
and linear bounded
operator
B:X^{*}\to Z
we have

\inf_{\rule{0.0pt}{3.27773pt}\xi\in X^{*}:\;B\xi=z}\Psi^{*}(\xi)=\sup_{\Lambda\in Z^{*}}\Big(\langle\Lambda,z\rangle_{Z}-\Psi(B^{*}\Lambda)\Big).

Proof.
Consider a dissipation potential
\Psi:X\to[0,\infty]
and a
bounded linear mapping
A:Y\to X
and define the dissipation potential

\widetilde{\Psi}:Y\to[0,\infty];\ y\mapsto\Psi(Ay)
. In

[
MaM20
, Prop. 6.1]
the identity
(\widetilde{\Psi}){}^{*}(\eta)=\inf\big\{\,\Psi^{*}(\xi)\>\big|\>A^{*}\xi=\eta\,\big\}
is established.
Applying this with
Y=Z^{*}
and
A=B^{*}:Z^{*}\to X
the
assertion follows.

A much simpler case occurs if the dissipation potential
{\mathcal{R}}
is independent
of the state
u\in X
. Then, the minimization in (
2.12
) with
respect to
u\in X
subject to
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta
and with respect to

y\in Y
decouple completely. In particular, if
{\mathcal{E}}
is uniformly convex,
all

\overline{u}\in\mathrm{Arg\!\;min}\big\{\,{\mathcal{R}}^{*}({-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))\>\big|\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=-\eta\,\big\}

are NESS, see
[
Mie23b
]
for more details. This relates to Prigogine’s
principle that states that NESS are minimizers of the dissipation, i.e. of

u\mapsto\Phi_{*}({-}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u))
, where
\Phi_{*}\geq{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is defined after
(
2.2
). For quadratic dissipation potentials (linear kinetic
relations) we have
\Phi_{*}(\xi)=2{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(\xi)
, such that the principle becomes
exact. For general nonlinear kinetic relations the result is an approximation
only, which works well close to equilibrium, see the discussions in

[
DeM84
, Cha. V]
and
[
Mie23b
]
.

2.5
Constrained B-functions, BER structure, and NESS

When doing reduction or
\Gamma
-limits of B-functions, we may end up with a
general function
\mathscr{K}:Y{\times}Y^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and may then ask the question whether
this function can be written as a B-function
{\mathfrak{B}}_{\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}}
.

Definition 2.14
(BER structure)

We say that a function
\mathscr{K}:Y{\times}Y^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}

has the
BER structure

(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R})
, if
(Y,\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R})
is a
gradient system and

{\mathscr{K}={\mathfrak{B}}_{\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}},\quad\text{namely }\forall\,(y,\eta)\in Y{\times}Y^{*}:\ \mathscr{K}(y,\eta)=\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)-\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)).}

We observe that for a given
\mathscr{K}
the dissipation functional
\mathsf{R}
and its
dual
\mathsf{R}^{*}
are uniquely determined by

\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)=\mathscr{K}(y,\eta)-\mathscr{K}(y,0)
. Hence, we have the following
necessary and sufficient conditions of a BER structure. The third
condition (
2.14c
) provides the important link to
null-saddles.

Proposition 2.15
(Conditions for BER structure)

Given an energy
\mathsf{E}:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the function
\mathscr{K}:Y{\times}Y^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}
has a BER
structure
(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R})
if and only if

\displaystyle\forall\,(y,\eta)\in Y{\times}Y^{*}:\quad\mathscr{K}(y,\eta)\geq\mathscr{K}(y,0),

(2.14a)

\displaystyle\forall\,y\in Y:\quad\mathscr{K}(y,\cdot):Y^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}\ \text{ is convex},

(2.14b)

\displaystyle\forall\,y\in Y:\quad\mathscr{K}(y,{-}{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))=0.

(2.14c)

Then,
\mathsf{R}
is given by
\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)=\mathscr{K}(y,\eta)-\mathscr{K}(y,0)
.

Proof.
It is obvious that
\mathscr{K}
satisfies (
2.14
) if
it has the BER structure
(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R})
.

To show the opposite, we observe that

\mathsf{R}^{*}_{\mathscr{K}}:(y,\eta)\mapsto\mathscr{K}(y,\eta){-}\mathscr{K}(y,0)
is a (dual)
dissipation potential because of (
2.14a
) and
(
2.14b
). Inserting the formula for
\mathsf{R}^{*}_{\mathscr{K}}
into the
condition
0={\mathfrak{B}}_{\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}}(y,\eta)-\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)+\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))
defining BER structures, we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathscr{K}(y,\eta)-\mathsf{R}^{*}_{\mathscr{K}}(y,\eta)+\mathsf{R}^{*}_{\mathscr{K}}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))

\displaystyle=\mathscr{K}(y,\eta)-\big(\mathscr{K}(y,\eta){-}\mathscr{K}(y,0)\big)+\big(\mathscr{K}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))-\mathscr{K}(y,0)\big)=\mathscr{K}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)).

Hence, (
2.14c
) guarantees that this
(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}_{\mathscr{K}})
is
the desired BER structure.

We return to our constrained saddle-point problems by generalizing it in a
crucial way. For this we use a second port function
P:X\to Y
which allows
us to impose direct conditions
Pu=y
on the state variable, whereas

{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)=\eta
does this indirectly. Nevertheless, we always
assume there is an energy
\mathsf{E}:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
, such that

Pu=y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)={\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y).

(2.15)

An important point for understanding the reduced or effective kinetic
relation generated by the port GS
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathbb{P}})
is to study the
reduced B-function
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}:Y{\times}Y^{*}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined via

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,\eta):=\sup_{u\in X\atop Pu=y}\inf_{\xi\in X^{*}\atop{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathfrak{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}}}(u,\xi).

(2.16)

In contrast to the previous analysis, we are now using two independent
constraints
y\in Y
and
\eta\in Y^{*}
, whereas in Section

2.3
we always assumed the compatibility

\eta=-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)
, cf. (
2.15
).
However, assuming there are null-saddles under these constraints means that

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))=0
holds, i.e. the necessary
(
2.14c
) holds. The next result provides the
fundamental link between null-saddles and a BER structure for

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
.

Theorem 2.16
(BER structure for
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
)

Consider a gradient system
(X,{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{R}})
with port mappings
P:X\to Y
and

{\mathbb{P}}^{*}:X^{*}\to Y^{*}
and a compatible energy
\mathsf{E}
as in (
2.15
).
Assume that for all
y\in Y
the CSPP (
2.10
) with

\eta=-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)=-{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(u)
has a null-saddle. Then, the
reduced B-function
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
defined in (
2.16
) has
the BER structure
(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{E})
with

\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)={\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,\eta)-{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,0)
, namely

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,\eta){={\mathfrak{B}}_{\mathsf{E},\mathsf{R}}(y,\eta)}=\mathsf{R}^{*}(y,\eta)-\mathsf{R}^{*}\big(y,-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)\big).

(2.17)

Proof.
The proof follows by checking the conditions
(
2.14
) in Proposition
2.15
.

Part (a):
Since
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}
is a dual dissipation potential we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\xi:\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=0}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)={\mathfrak{B}}(u,0)\leq\inf_{\xi:\>{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi).

Taking the supremum over
u
with
Pu=y
gives
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,0)\leq{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,\eta)
as desired.

Part (b):
Defining
{\mathcal{N}}(u,\eta)=\inf_{\xi:\,{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)
we can easily check that each
{\mathcal{N}}(u,\cdot)
is still
convex, because
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}
is linear map. Indeed, for
\eta_{0},\eta_{1}\in Y
and
\theta\in[0,T]
set

\eta_{\theta}=(1{-}\theta)\eta_{0}+\theta\eta_{1}
. For
\varepsilon>0
pick

\xi_{0},\xi_{1}\in X^{*}
with
{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi_{j})\leq{\mathcal{N}}(u,y_{j})+\varepsilon
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(u,\eta_{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\xi:\,{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=\eta_{\theta}}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\xi)\leq{\mathfrak{B}}\big(u,(1{-}\theta)\xi_{0}+\theta\xi_{1}\big)

\displaystyle\overset{{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\cdot)\text{ cvx}}{\leq}(1{-}\theta){\mathfrak{B}}\big(u,\xi_{0})+\theta{\mathfrak{B}}\big(u,\xi_{1})\leq(1{-}\theta){\mathcal{N}}(u,\eta_{0})+\theta{\mathcal{N}}(u,\eta_{1})+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the convexity of
{\mathcal{N}}(u,\cdot)
is
established.

Because
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,\cdot)
is the supremum of the family

\big({\mathcal{N}}(u,\cdot)\big)_{u:\,Pu=y}
it is again convex.

Part (c):
For every
y\in Y
there exists a null-saddle
(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})
with
P\overline{u}=y
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\overline{\xi}=-{\mathbb{P}}^{*}{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(\overline{u})=-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)
. Hence, we have

\sup_{u:\,Pu=y}{\mathfrak{B}}(u,\overline{\xi})\leq{\mathfrak{B}}(\overline{u},\overline{\xi})=0\leq\inf_{\xi:\,{\mathbb{P}}^{*}\xi=-{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y)}{\mathfrak{B}}(\overline{u},\xi).

Comparing with the definition of
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
we find

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(y,{-}{\mathrm{D}}\mathsf{E}(y))=0
as desired.

3
EDP-convergence for slow-fast GSs via NESS

We consider a family of Gs
(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
where

\varepsilon>0
is the small parameter modeling the ratio between fast and slow
relaxation times. We consider two distinguished cases: in the first the state
space can be decomposed in the form
u=(U,w)\in X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}}=X
and in
the second we have

X=\big\{\,u=(U,w)\in X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}}\>\big|\>{\mathbb{Q}}(U,w):=P_{\mathrm{slow}}U-P_{\mathrm{fast}}w=0\,\big\}

where
P_{\mathrm{slow}}:X_{\mathrm{slow}}\to Y
and
P_{\mathrm{fast}}:X_{\mathrm{fast}}\to Y
are suitable port
mappings. Here we consider
U\in X_{\mathrm{slow}}
as the slow macroscopic part of the state variables, while
w\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}
is the fast microscopic part, that one wants to eliminate in the limit

\varepsilon\to 0
.

In both setting we assume that the scaling in
\varepsilon
is very particular, but
nevertheless we are able to treat a number of prototypical cases. In
particular, we assume
{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(U,w)=E(U)+\varepsilon\,e(w)
.

3.1
Case 1: product space
X=X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}}

The precise assumptions on the scaling with
\varepsilon>0
are the following:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(U,w)

\displaystyle=E(U)+\varepsilon\,e(w)

\displaystyle\text{additive split of energy},

(3.1a)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(U,w;\Xi,\mu)

\displaystyle=\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}(U,w;\Xi,\tfrac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\varepsilon}\mu\big)

\displaystyle\text{fast relaxation of $w$},

(3.1b)

where
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}:X{\times}X^{*}\to[0,\infty]
is a general dual dissipation
potential independent of
\varepsilon
.

The associated gradient-flow equation takes a simple form, because
the appearance of
\varepsilon
is chosen in a particular way.

\displaystyle\dot{U}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{D}}_{\Xi}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big),

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\dot{w}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{D}}_{\mu}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big).

(3.2b)

Thus, on the formal level, we can drop the term
\varepsilon\dot{w}
, because
w

relaxes into a NESS on the time scale
\varepsilon
which is much faster than the
evolution of
U
which happens on time scales of order
1
. The microscopic
variable
w
moves into the NESS
w=\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U)
satisfying

0={\mathrm{D}}_{\mu}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big).

(3.3)

Inserting the limiting relation
w=\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U)
into the first equation of
(
3.2a
) we obtain the reduced macroscopic problem

\dot{U}={\mathrm{D}}_{\Xi}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U);-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(\widetilde{\mathsf{w}}(U))\big).

(3.4)

The disadvantage of the above approach is that we lose control over
the gradient structures. As we have started with the GSs

(X,{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
, it is natural to ask whether the effective
equation (
3.4
) has a natural gradient structure inherited
from
E
,
e
, and
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}
.

This question can be answered by the notion of EDP-convergence, which provides
a tool to stay on the level of gradient systems. We follow here the approach
developed in
[
LM∗17
]
which forms the basis of the further developments
of EDP-convergence in
[
DFM19
,
MMP21
]
. The abbreviation
“EDP” stand for the
energy-dissipation principle
(cf.
[
Mie16
, Thm. 3.3.1]
) that shows that under suitable technical
assumptions a curve
u_{\varepsilon}=(U_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon}):[0,T]\to X
is a solution of the
gradient-flow equation (
3.2
) if and only if it satisfies the
energy-dissipation inequality

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big({\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}\big(u_{\varepsilon};\dot{u}_{\varepsilon}\big)+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\big(u_{\varepsilon};-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(0)).

The idea in
[
LM∗17
,
MaM20
]
is to replace the primal
dissipation
{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}(u,\dot{u})
by the lower bound
\langle\xi,\dot{u}\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}(u,\xi)
for an arbitrary test function
\xi:[0,T]\to X^{*}
. Then, the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
is performed and finally one maximizes with
respect to
\xi
to recover the limiting energy-dissipation balance again.

Thus, for a general smooth function
\xi:[0,T]\to X^{*}
we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big(\big\langle\xi,\dot{u}_{\varepsilon}\big\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\big(u_{\varepsilon};\xi\big)+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\big(u_{\varepsilon};-{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(0)).

Using the explicit
\varepsilon
-dependence of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}
imposed in
(

) and choosing
\xi=(\Xi,\varepsilon\zeta)
we arrive
at

{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big(\big\langle(\Xi,\varepsilon\zeta),\dot{u}_{\varepsilon}\big\rangle-\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(u_{\varepsilon};\Xi,\zeta\big)+\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(u_{\varepsilon};-{\mathrm{D}}E(U_{\varepsilon}),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w_{\varepsilon})\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon}(0)).

Now passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the term

\langle\varepsilon\zeta,\dot{w}_{\varepsilon}\rangle
and the terms
\varepsilon e(w_{\varepsilon}(t))

vanish. Assuming
(U_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})\to(U,w)
we arrive at the inequality

\displaystyle E(U(T))+\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\Big(\big\langle\Xi,\dot{U}\big\rangle-{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq E(U(0))\ \text{ for all }(\Xi,\zeta)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}([0,T];X^{*}),

where
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}(U,w)=E(U){+}e(w)
and hence

{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta)=\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,w;\Xi,\zeta\big)-\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}^{*}\big(U,w;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U),-{\mathrm{D}}e(w)\big).

Since
w
appears in the integral only via
w(t)
, but not with a derivative

\dot{w}(t)
we can eliminate
w(t)
by taking the infimum pointwise in

t\in[0,T]
. Similar, we can
eliminate
\zeta
by a pointwise supremum. Hence, defining

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}:X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X^{*}_{\mathrm{slow}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
via

{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(U,\Xi):=\sup_{w\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}}\inf_{\zeta\in X_{\mathrm{fast}}^{*}}\;\!{\mathfrak{B}}_{\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(U,w;\Xi,\zeta).

(3.5)

we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle E(U(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big(\big\langle\Xi,\dot{U}\big\rangle-{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(U,\Xi)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq E(U(0)).

(3.6)

Now it remains to show that
{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}
has a BER structure

(E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
in the sense of Definition
2.14
, i.e. it
has the form

\displaystyle{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}(U,\Xi)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U;\Xi)-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U)),\ \ \text{ i.e. }{\mathscr{B}}_{\mathrm{red}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}}

(3.7)

for a suitable effective dissipation potential
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

If this is the case, we can insert this into (
3.6
) and
reverse the Legendre transform with respect to
\Xi
to obtain the
energy-dissipation inequality

\displaystyle E(U(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\Big({\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U;\dot{U})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}\big(U;-{\mathrm{D}}E(U)\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq E(U(0)).

(3.8)

Applying the energy-dissipation principle once again, we see that
U
is a
solution of the gradient-flow equation

\dot{U}={\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{R}}^{*}_{\mathrm{eff}}(U,{-}{\mathrm{D}}E(U))

for the reduced gradient system
(X_{\mathrm{slow}},E,{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}})
. Clearly, this
equation must equal (
3.4
), but now we have a truly
thermodynamical structure.

To achieve this goal it remains to establish the BER structure
(
3.7
). The following result is the analogue of Theorem

2.16
.

Theorem 3.1
(

has BER structure)

For a GS
(X_{\mathrm{slow}}{\times}X_{\mathrm{fast}},\overline{{\mathcal{E}}},\overline{{\mathcal{R}}})
with

define

It seems that the above theory can be generalized to an arbitrary number of

5.2
EDP-convergence in the Otto gradient structure

We reconsider the above linear equation, but now we strict to the
scalar case
i_{*}=1

u(t,x)\in{[0,\infty[}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1}

to do the EDP-limit
\varepsilon\to 0
as in the previous subsection, but now
for the so-called Otto gradient structure, also called gradient-flow in the
Wasserstein space. The gradient system is the triple

(\mathrm{Prob}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}),{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{Otto}})
, where
the function space is

\mathrm{Prob}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}):=\big\{\,u\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})\>\big|\>u\geq 0,\ \int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u\;\!\mathrm{d}y=1\,\big\},

the energy is Boltzmann’s relative entropy

{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}\big(A_{\varepsilon}(y)u(y)\big)\frac{\;\!\mathrm{d}y}{A_{\varepsilon}(y)},

and the dual dissipation functional reads

{\mathcal{R}}^{\mathrm{Otto\>*}}_{\varepsilon}(u,\xi)=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\frac{K_{\varepsilon}(y)}{2}|\partial_{y}\xi(y)|^{2}u(y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle{\mathbb{K}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{Otto}}(u)\xi,\xi\rangle,

which is quadratic in
\xi
and dependent on the state
u\in\mathrm{Prob}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})
. Here
{\mathbb{K}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{Otto}}(u)
can be understood as
the self-adjoint nonnegative differential operator

{\mathbb{K}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{Otto}}(u)\,\xi=-\partial_{y}\big(K_{\varepsilon}u\partial_{y}\xi\,\big)\quad\text{with }K_{\varepsilon}u\partial_{y}\xi\big|_{y=\pm(1{+}\varepsilon)}=0.

The associated gradient-flow equation is the Fokker-Planck equation

\dot{u}=\partial_{y}\big(K_{\varepsilon}u\partial_{y}(A_{\varepsilon}u)\big)=\partial_{y}\big(K_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{y}u+uV^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})\big),

if we define the driving potential

.

of

Theorem 5.1
(Membrane reduction)

(5.6)

where

Proof.

However, it is advantageous to do a transformation first. We set

It will be particularly useful, that

is linear in

If

As the prefactor

Because of

, this is enough to evaluate

{\mathcal{I}}(v,\eta_{*})

Using the port conditions

In summary, we obtain the effective gradient system

We see that the effective contribution of the membrane is of cosh-type, and in

Another way of understanding this transition involves the theory of
Markov processes. The pure diffusion problem can be interpreted as a particle
performing a Brownian motion with state-dependent mobility. In the membrane
region the mobility is very low (of order
\varepsilon

is
2\varepsilon

Theorem 5.2
(Membrane with reaction and diffusion)

For the fast gradient system

\displaystyle=M_{\mathrm{eff}}\sqrt{\overline{A}(-1)w_{-}\overline{A}(1)w_{+}}\,\mathsf{C}^{*}(\zeta_{+}{-}\zeta_{-})

(5.9)

\displaystyle\quad+M_{-}\sqrt{\overline{A}(-1)w_{-}}\,\mathsf{C}^{*}(\zeta_{-})+M_{+}\sqrt{\overline{A}(1)w_{+}}\,\mathsf{C}^{*}(\zeta_{+}),

where

In the case of constant coefficients we have

Proof.

Appendix A
Classical existence theory for saddle points

We recollect the basic result from saddle point theory as contained in

[
EkT74
, Cha. VI]
(La dualité par les minimax).

We consider a functional
\mathscr{L}:{\bm{U}}{\times}{\bm{V}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}=[-\infty,\infty]
,
called “Lagrangian” in
[
EkT74
, Cha. VI]
, where we now want
to minimize with respect to
x\in{\bm{U}}
and maximize with respect to

y\in{\bm{V}}
. This means that for applying the theory below to the
B-functions used above we have to set
{\bm{U}}=X
,
{\bm{V}}=X^{*}
, and

\mathscr{L}(u,\xi)=-{\mathscr{B}}(u,\xi)
. Now, a point
(x_{*},y_{*})
is called a
saddle
point
of
\mathscr{L}
if

\forall\,x\in{\bm{U}},\ y\in{\bm{V}}:\quad\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y)\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y_{*})\leq\mathscr{L}(x,y_{*}).

Thus, we minimize with respect to
x\in{\bm{U}}
, and we maximize with
respect to
y
.

The aim is to find a saddle point from general principles. For this one looks
at
\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y)
and

\inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y)
. We obviously always have a one-sided
estimate, and the major question in constructing saddle points is when we have
equality.

Lemma A.1
(Simple facts on saddles points)

(a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}:=\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y)\ \leq\ \inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y):=\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}

(A.1)

(b)

\displaystyle\text{saddle point $(x_{*},y_{*})$ exists}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}=\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}.

(A.2)

In the latter case, we have
\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y_{*})=\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}=\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}
.

Proof.
To show (a), we start from
\mathscr{L}(x,y)\leq\sup_{\overline{y}}\mathscr{L}(x,\overline{y})
. Taking the infimum over
x
we obtain
\inf_{x}\mathscr{L}(x,y)\leq\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}
. Now taking the supremum over
y
in the left-hand side
leads to the desired estimate
\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}\leq\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}
.

To show (b) simply note that the saddle-point property implies

\inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y_{*})=\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y_{*})=\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y).

Thus, we find
\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}\geq\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y_{*})\geq\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}
. With (a)
this implies the desired equality.

The quantity
\delta_{\mathscr{L}}=\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}-\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}\geq 0
is called the
duality
gap
. The function
\mathscr{L}(x,y)=\tanh(x{-}y)
on
{\mathbb{R}}{\times}{\mathbb{R}}
shows that

\delta_{\mathscr{L}}
can be positive. Indeed,
\mathrm{SI}_{\tanh}=-1
and
\mathrm{IS}_{\tanh}=+1
such
that
\delta_{\tanh}=2
.

The opposite implication in (
A.2
) is not
valid. To see this consider
{\bm{U}}={\bm{V}}={\mathbb{R}}
and

\mathscr{L}(x,y)=\mathrm{e}^{x}-\mathrm{e}^{-y}
. Clearly,
\inf_{x}\mathscr{L}(x,y)=-\mathrm{e}^{-y}

and hence,
\mathrm{SI}_{\mathscr{L}}=0
and similarly
\mathrm{IS}_{\mathscr{L}}=0
. However, no
saddle-point exists. Even in cases where no saddle-point exists it is an interesting
question under what conditions the duality gap is
0
, see e.g.
[
EkT74
, Ch. III, Prop. 2.3]
.

If two saddle points
(x_{j},y_{j})
with
j=1,2
exist, we have

\mathscr{L}(x_{1},y_{2})\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{1},y_{1})\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{2},y_{1})\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{2},y_{2})\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{1},y_{2}),

which means that all four points have the same value. If each

\mathscr{L}(\cdot,y_{j})
is convex and each
\mathscr{L}(x_{j},\cdot)
concave,
then we conclude
\mathscr{L}(x,y)=\mathscr{L}(x_{1},y_{1})
for all

x=(1{-}s)x_{1}+sx_{2}
and
y=(1{-}r)y_{1}+ry_{2}
with arbitrary
r,s\in[0,1]
.

A standard existence result for saddle points can be found in

[
EkT74
, Ch. VI, Prop. 2.1]
. We provide a variant that is
adjusted to our purposes.

Proposition A.2
(Existence of saddle points)

Consider reflexive Banach spaces
{\bm{U}}
and
{\bm{V}}
and assume that the
following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}:\quad x\mapsto\mathscr{L}(x,y)\text{ is convex and lsc},

(A.3a)

\displaystyle\forall\,x\in{\bm{U}}:\quad y\mapsto-\mathscr{L}(x,y)\text{ is convex and lsc},

(A.3b)

\displaystyle\exists\,y_{0}\in{\bm{V}}:\quad\mathscr{L}(\,\cdot\,,y_{0})\text{ is coercive},

(A.3c)

\displaystyle\exists\,x_{0}\in{\bm{U}}:\quad\!{-}\mathscr{L}(x_{0},\,\cdot\,)\text{ is coercive}.

(A.3d)

Then, a saddle point
(x_{*},y_{*})
exists and

\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y_{*})=\min_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\sup_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y)=\max_{y\in{\bm{V}}}\inf_{x\in{\bm{U}}}\mathscr{L}(x,y).

If moreover, in (
A.3a
) and (
A.3b
) we have strict
convexity, then the saddle point is unique.

Proof.
Step 1: Saddle points on balls using strict convexity.

We additionally impose that

\forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}:\quad\mathscr{L}(\,\cdot\,,y):{\bm{U}}\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}\text{ is strictly convex}.

(A.4)

For
R\geq R_{0}:=\max\{\|x_{0}\|_{\bm{U}},\|y_{0}\|_{\bm{V}}\}
we consider the closed and convex balls

{\bm{U}}_{R}=\{\,x\in{\bm{U}}\>|\>\|x\|_{\bm{U}}\leq R\,\}
and similarly
{\bm{V}}_{R}
.

For all
R
we obtain a saddle point
(x_{R},y_{R})
as follows. For all

y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
the direct method of the calculus of variations provides a
minimizer
x=\widehat{x}_{R}(y)\in{\bm{U}}_{R}
for
\mathscr{L}(\cdot,y)|_{{\bm{U}}_{R}}
, i.e.
\mathscr{L}(\widehat{x}_{R}(y),y)=\min_{x\in{\bm{U}}_{R}}\mathscr{L}(x,y)=:\lambda_{R}(y)
. By the strict
convexity in (
A.4
)
\widehat{x}_{R}(y)
is uniquely determined.

We first observe that
-\lambda_{R}:{\bm{V}}_{R}\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
is convex and lsc, as it is
the supremum of the convex and lsc functions
-\mathscr{L}(x,\cdot)
. Moreover, by
(
A.3d
) the function
-\lambda_{R}
is
bounded from below by the proper, lsc, convex function
-\mathscr{L}(x_{0},\cdot)
.
Hence,
\lambda_{R}
attains its maximum in a point
y^{R}\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
.

Our aim is now to show that
(\widehat{x}_{R}(y^{R}),y^{R})
is a saddle point of
\mathscr{L}

on
{\bm{U}}_{R}{\times}{\bm{V}}_{R}
. For this we choose arbitrary
y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
and
\theta\in[0,1]

and set
x_{\theta}(y):=\widehat{x}_{R}((1{-}\theta)y^{R}{+}\theta y)
and obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}(y^{R})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{R}\big((1{-}\theta)y^{R}{+}\theta y\big)=\mathscr{L}\big(x_{\theta}(y),(1{-}\theta)y^{R}{+}\theta y\big)

\displaystyle\overset{-\mathscr{L}(x_{\theta}(y),\cdot)\text{ cvx}}{\geq}(1{-}\theta)\mathscr{L}(x_{\theta},y^{R})+\theta\mathscr{L}(x_{\theta}(y),y)\geq(1{-}\theta)\lambda_{R}(y^{R})+\theta\mathscr{L}(x_{\theta}(y),y).

In particular, for
\theta\in{]0,1]}
and all
y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
we conclude

\lambda_{R}(y^{R})=\mathscr{L}(\widehat{x}_{R}(y^{R}))\geq\mathscr{L}(x_{\theta}(y),y)\quad\text{ for
all }y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}.

(A.5)

Choosing
\theta=1/k
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain
x_{k}:=x_{1/k}(y)\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
and
may select a weakly convergent subsequence (not relabeled) with
x_{k}\rightharpoonup x^{R}
. We claim that
x^{R}=\widehat{x}_{R}(y^{R})
and hence is independent of

y
. Indeed, for our fixed
y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
and arbitrary
\widetilde{x}\in{\bm{U}}_{R}
we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\eqref{eq:Cond.a},lsc}}{\leq}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathscr{L}(x_{k},y^{R})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\eqref{eq:Cond.b},cvx}}{\leq}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{1{-}\frac{1}{k}}\Big(\mathscr{L}(x_{k},(1{-}\tfrac{1}{k})y^{R}{+}\tfrac{1}{k}y)-\frac{1}{k}\mathscr{L}(x_{k},y)\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def.\,$\lambda_{R},\widehat{x}_{R}$}}{\leq}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Big(\frac{k}{k{-}1}\mathscr{L}(\widetilde{x},(1{-}\tfrac{1}{k})y^{R}{+}\tfrac{1}{k}y)-\frac{1}{k{-}1}\lambda_{R}(y)\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda_{R}(y)<\infty}{\leq}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathscr{L}(\widetilde{x},(1{-}\tfrac{1}{k})y^{R}{+}\tfrac{1}{k}y)\ \overset{\text{\eqref{eq:Cond.b},lsc}}{\leq}\ \mathscr{L}(\widetilde{x},y^{R}),

where we used
(1{-}\tfrac{1}{k})y^{R}{+}\tfrac{1}{k}y\to y^{R}
in the last step. Since

\widetilde{x}\in{\bm{U}}_{R}
was arbitrary we obtain
\lambda_{R}(y^{R})\leq\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})\leq\min_{\widetilde{x}\in{\bm{U}}_{R}}\mathscr{L}(\widetilde{x},y^{R})=\lambda_{R}(y^{R})
. Hence,
{\bm{U}}^{R}
is a
minimizer of
\mathscr{L}(\cdot,y^{R})
and hence coincides with
\widehat{x}_{R}(y^{R})
because
of the strict convexity (
A.4
).

Because of the uniqueness of the limit we conclude that for all
y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}
we
have
x_{\theta}(y)\rightharpoonup x^{R}=\widehat{x}_{R}(y^{R})
for
\theta\to 0^{+}
. Thus, taking the
limit
\theta\to 0^{+}
in (
A.5
) and exploiting the lsc from
(
A.3a
) we obtain

\forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}\ \forall\,\widetilde{x}\in{\bm{U}}_{R}:\quad\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y)\leq\lambda_{R}(y^{R})=\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})\leq\mathscr{L}(\widetilde{x},y^{R}).

This shows that
(x^{R},y^{R})
is a saddle point for
\mathscr{L}
restricted to
{\bm{U}}_{R}{\times}{\bm{V}}_{R}
.

Step 2: Saddle points on balls without strict convexity.
If we only have
convexity we consider

\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\mathscr{L}(x,y)+\varepsilon\|x\|^{2}\quad\text{with }\varepsilon>0,

where we can choose a strictly convex norm
\|\cdot\|
on the reflexive space

{\bm{U}}
. By Step 1 we obtain a saddle point
(x^{R}_{\varepsilon},y^{R}_{\varepsilon})\in{\bm{U}}_{R}{\times}{\bm{V}}_{R}
. Hence, we have

\forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}\ \forall\,x\in{\bm{U}}_{R}:\quad\mathscr{L}(x^{R}_{\varepsilon},y)+\varepsilon\|x^{R}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leq\mathscr{L}(x^{R}_{\varepsilon},y^{R}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\|x^{R}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leq\mathscr{L}(x,y^{R}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\|x\|^{2}.

(A.6)

We may choose a subsequence (not relabeled) with
(x^{R}_{\varepsilon},y^{R}_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup(\overline{x}^{R},\overline{y}^{R})
in
{\bm{U}}{\times}{\bm{V}}
. Dropping the middle term in
(
A.6
) we can pass to the limit using the lsc in (
A.3a
) and
(
A.3b
) and arrive at

\forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}_{R}\ \forall\,x\in{\bm{U}}_{R}:\quad\mathscr{L}(\overline{x}^{R},y)\leq\mathscr{L}(x,\overline{y}^{R}).

Thus,
(\overline{x}^{R},\overline{y}^{R})
is indeed a saddle point for
\mathscr{L}
restricted to
restricted to
{\bm{U}}_{R}{\times}{\bm{V}}_{R}
.

Step 3: Unbounded case.
We now consider the limit
R\to\infty
.
Using the coercivities (
A.3c
) and (
A.3d
). For
R\geq R_{0}

the saddle points
(x^{R},y^{R})
from Step 2 satisfy

\mathscr{L}(x_{R},y_{0})\leq\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})\leq\mathscr{L}(x_{0},y^{R}).

(A.7)

Since
\mathscr{L}(\cdot,y_{0})
and
-\mathscr{L}(x_{0},\cdot)
are lsc and coercive (cf. (
A.3c
) and (
A.3d
)), they are bounded from below:

\exists\,M>0\ \forall\,x\in{\bm{U}}\ \forall\,y\in{\bm{V}}:\quad\mathscr{L}(x,y_{0})\geq-M\ \text{ and }\ \mathscr{L}(x_{0},y)\leq M.

Combining this with (
A.7
), we have

\forall\,R\geq R_{0}:\quad\text{(i) }\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y_{0})\geq-M\ \text{ and }\ \text{(ii) }\mathscr{L}(x_{0},y^{R})\leq M.

With (
A.7
) we obtain
|\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})|\leq M
.
Using the coercivity (
A.3c
) and (ii) we find
\|y^{R}\|\leq C_{\bm{V}}
, and
similarly (
A.3d
) and (i) give
\|x^{R}\|\leq C_{\bm{U}}
. Thus, using the
reflexivity of
{\bm{U}}
and
{\bm{V}}
we find a subsequence
(x^{R},y^{R})
(not relabeled) such that

\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})\to\lambda_{*},\quad x^{R}\rightharpoonup x_{*}\text{ in }{\bm{U}},\quad y^{R}\rightharpoonup y_{*}\text{ in }{\bm{V}}.

For arbitrary
x\in{\bm{U}}
we choose
R>\max\{R_{0},\|x\|\}
and obtain

\mathscr{L}(x,y^{R})\geq\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})
. Taking the limit
R\to\infty
(along the
subsequence) and using the lsc of
-\mathscr{L}(x,\cdot)
we arrive at

\mathscr{L}(x,y_{*})\geq\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathscr{L}(x,y^{R})\geq\limsup_{R\to\infty}\mathscr{L}(x^{R},y^{R})=\lambda_{*},

where
x\in{\bm{U}}
was arbitrary. Similarly, we obtain
\mathscr{L}(x_{*},y)\leq\lambda_{*}

which gives the desired saddle-point property for
(x_{*},y_{*})\in{\bm{U}}{\times}{\bm{V}}
:

\forall\,x\in{\bm{U}},\ y\in{\bm{V}}:\quad\lambda(x_{*},y)\leq\lambda_{*}=\lambda(x_{*},y_{*})\leq\mathscr{L}(x,y_{*}).

Step 4: Uniqueness under strict convexity.
This was shown already in Step
1.

This completes the proof of Proposition
A.2
.
