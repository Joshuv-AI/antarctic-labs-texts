SoK: A Defense-Oriented Evaluation of Software Supply Chain Security
Eman Abu Ishgair, Marcela S. Melara, Santiago Torres-Arias

Abstract
The software supply chain comprises a highly complex set of operations, processes, tools, institutions and human factors involved in creating a piece of software. A number of high-profile attacks that exploit a weakness in this complex ecosystem have spurred research in identifying classes of supply chain attacks. Yet, practitioners often lack the necessary information to understand their security posture and implement suitable defenses against these attacks. We argue that the next stage of software supply chain security research and development will benefit greatly from a defense-oriented approach that focuses on holistic bottom-up solutions. To this end, this paper introduces the AStRA model, a framework for representing fundamental software supply chain elements and their causal relationships. Using this model, we identify software supply chain security objectives that are needed to mitigate common attacks, and systematize knowledge on recent and well-established security techniques for their ability to meet these objectives. We validate our model against prior attacks and taxonomies. Finally, we identify emergent research gaps and propose opportunities to develop novel software development tools and systems that are secure-by-design.

2
Systematization Model and Methodology

To reason about different defense approaches’ ability to meet specific
software supply chain security objectives, we must first develop a holistic conceptual framework (towards
RQ1
).
Thus, we begin by defining the core elements of a software supply chain, and use these to describe a model that represents these elements
as well as their relationships in a supply chain.

2.1
Core Software Supply Chain Elements

Fundamentally, a software supply chain can be described in terms of four core elements:

1) Principals:
Broadly, any organization, institution or individual involved with any aspect of the software supply chain.
Human principals are often identified via
digital credentials
such as cryptographic keys, access tokens, or certificates.
For the sake of simplicity, we consider a principal and her credentials to be equivalent.

2) Artifacts:
A software artifact refers to a unit of digital information that is
required for the creation, configuration and evaluation of a piece of software.

An artifact is typically represented as a file, immutable object, or a collection thereof, such as Debian package, a Docker image, a source code file, or a series of translation files.
Artifacts are created either manually (e.g., source code) or generated through software (e.g., Docker image).

3) Resources:
A resource is any software component, service or hardware component used in the creation, configuration, or evaluation of an artifact.
Resources may be implemented or configured by first-party principals or third-party service providers.
Examples of resources include source control management systems like Git
[
129
]
,
CI/CD services such as GitHub Actions
[
59
]
, or the operating system on a machine used to build
an artifact.

4) Steps:
A step refers to a specific task or operation
that creates, evaluates or distributes a software artifact (per Torres Arias
et al.

[
69
]
).
In other words, we consider steps to be the logical units in the software supply chain.
Individual steps are usually performed within a given administrative domain, i.e., a single organization.
In practice, principals use resources to carry out steps.

2.2
AStRA model

Identifying the fundamental elements of a software supply chain allows us to describe
an abstract model which enables principled reasoning about the security of individual elements.
Yet, evaluating each element in isolation is not sufficient to understand how the
causal relationships between these elements impact the overall security of a software supply chain.

To reason about software supply chain defenses, we introduce a the AStRA model, a conceptual framework
that includes a representation of supply chain
topology
.
That is, our model represents a specific software supply chain structure as a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
in which Artifacts, Step, Resources and principAls are vertices
and the edges between them represent their relationships.

Fig.
1
shows a simple example.
Our model captures three types of causal relationships between supply chain elements:

1.

Principals, identified by credentials,
use
resources;

2.

Resources
carry out
steps;

3.

Steps
consume
and
produce
artifacts.

These abstract elements of the AStRA model allow us to capture two intrinsic
properties of the software supply chain that have an effect on risk.
First, this model makes explicit all dependency relationships between
the participants, components, and operations in the software supply chain.
Including these dependencies in our analysis of security objectives for the individual AStRA elements then enables us to identify the scope and limitations of possible defenses.

Second, the AStRA model enables us to express the causal nature of the software supply chain.
That is, by including ingress and egress relationships between artifacts and
steps, the model allows us to make explicit not only the transformations of
material artifacts into products, but also to capture the
transitions over time of
stateful
artifacts (e.g., version-controlled code repositories).

Note that our model treats a artifacts that evolve over time, such as a source code repository under version control,
as two distinct artifacts each capturing its state at time
t
before and
t+1
after
a step (e.g., a commit step).
Making such causal properties explicit enables us to preserve the acyclic property
of the supply chain DAG.

3
Defending Principals

Incidents like the first stage of the SolarWinds hack
[
55
]
,
targeted the principal element of a software supply chain by exploiting compromised credentials. Ultimately, this allowed an attacker to plant backdoors in the networks of thousands of companies and government agencies
[
18
]
.

Thus, a primary objective for defending principals is to address weaknesses in
authorization
– the process that uses credentials to grant a principal the permissions to trigger a step.
That is, assuming authorization is in place, defenses aim to make it more difficult to abuse credentials.

3.1
Principal Security Objectives

P1 - Credential reuse limited:
Limits how often a principal can use the same credential
to perform a step. This is achieved by ensuring credentials are used within a time frame or count,
which reduces the window for compromise.

P2 - Credential compromise detected:
Provides mechanisms to detect compromised credentials.
This reduces the impact of a compromise by providing means to check whether credentials were misused.

P3 - Credential authority limited:
Reduces the level of authority a single credential can give to a principal.
This increases the effort needed to compromise a step, and can be accomplished by requiring multiple
credentials

to authorize a principal or by restricting the types of operations a credential may be used for.

P4 - Multiple principals required:
Requires multiple principals to carry out a step. This is
stricter than
P3
as it reduces the risk of misbehaving principals.

P5 - Minimum security properties preserved:
Within a certain threshold of compromise,
core security properties are maintained. That is, this can be met if a sufficiently large
set of authorized principals behave honestly.

3.2
Defense Approach Evaluation

To support the evaluated principal defense approaches, we assume an underlying
authentication
mechanism
to verify the validity of a credential.

3.2.1
Restrictions on compromise impact

Defenses in this category aim to contain the impact of compromising a principal or a credential.

Usage-limited credentials
restrict the number of times a given credential may be used
to authorize a principal. This implies that an attacker has a limited number of opportunities
to exploit a given credential once compromised (
P1
).

A commonly implemented mechanism is one-time passwords, or OTP, (e.g.,
[
114
,
52
]
),
which are only valid for a single authorization session. Implemented via combinations of
pseudorandom functions, cryptographic hash functions, and encryption schemes
[
52
,
114
,
82
]
,
this technique has been in use for about four decades
[
82
]
to address user authentication
and authorization risks in distributed systems.

Ephemeral credentials

constrain the window of vulnerability by limiting
the time frame during which a credential is valid. In the case of the SolarWinds hack, this approach might have restricted how long the attackers had access to the system.
One form of ephemeral credentials are cryptographic keys, referred to as
“ephemeral keys”
[
95
]
or “session keys”
[
38
]
,
which are newly generated for each key exchange or communication process.

Another form are short-lived certificates
[
133
,
113
]
, digital certificates
used to authenticate a remote party that have a short validity period, i.e., they expire after
a short period of time (e.g., a few minutes
[
29
]
to a few days
[
133
]
).
To address
P1
in the software supply chain, Fulcio
[
88
]
provides a framework for generating
short-lived X.509 certificates to curb the impact of compromised credentials.
In implementations such as SPIFFE
[
131
]
,
short-lived credentials may also describe the purpose of the credential
constraining the authority granted to a principal (
P3
).

Credential transparency
enables principals and steps to detect whether a credential
compromise has occurred (
P2
). This approach typically relies on a database backed by a Merkle tree
[
93
]

referred to as a
transparency log
, which makes any changes to the contents of the log easily detectable.
Many implementations also preserve some security properties so long as there is one honest principal,
providing additional reliability in the face of compromise (
P5
).

This approach was first realized through certificate transparency
[
84
]
, a framework and protocol
for publicly logging and auditing the validity of web certificates.
CONIKS
[
91
]
was the first credential transparency approach tailored specifically for
end-user encryption keys. Similar efforts building upon these lines of work
have emerged since (e.g.,
[
83
,
115
,
67
,
34
]
).

Role separation
reduces the authority of any given principal (and their credentials)
to a specific set of steps or operations a principal can perform (
P3
). Taking a more preventative approach
than the other approaches in this category of principal defenses, the principles of
least common mechanism

[
110
]

and
least privilege

[
116
]
underpin role separation. The former reduces the functions
shared between different principals to reduce the risks of interference, while the latter reduces the authority of a given principal to the minimum needed
degree
for her to perform a desired step.

In practice, these principles are used to group principals by the type of function they fulfill in an application
[
39
]
.
Common roles in open- and closed-source software development settings include project leads, maintainers,
and software developers and contributors, as well as project managers, product security engineers, system administrators, policy administrators,
software testers and project auditors, and customers (i.e., product consumers).

2
2

2

We derive this set of common software supply chain roles from academic
[
103
,
80
,
33
]

and industry
[
39
,
125
]
sources.

To achieve role separation, many software supply chain resources and tools provide mechanisms for
assigning users to different roles and assigning different levels of authority and control
over a given step or resource
[
61
,
100
,
101
]
. One such implementation is GitHub’s
repository roles
[
61
]
, that allow organization owners to grant members and outside collaborators different levels
of access permissions to code repositories based on their role in the organization.

3.2.2
Redundancy in authorization

Such defenses increase the efforts an attacker must spend to gain unauthorized access to a step.
This can be accomplished by requiring multiple credentials or principals to successfully
carry out a step.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
relies on a principal using
at least two different
categories
of credentials:
something a principal knows (e.g., a password), something they have (e.g., a
hardware token), or something they are (e.g., their physical fingerprint) (
P3
).
Service providers for software supply chain resources such as package managers
[
111
]
and version control systems
[
108
]
have begun to adopt this approach. This approach could have made it harder for SolarWinds attackers to impersonate principals upon accessing the build system.
Many MFA implementations leverage OTPs (e.g.,
[
147
,
46
,
48
]
),
providing additional risk mitigation (
P1
).

Separation of principal privilege
requires multiple separate principals to authorize
a step
[
116
]
, preventing the authority for a given step from lying in a single principal (
P4
).
As an important security mechanism in various types of systems (see §
5
),
this approach may show promise as a software supply chain defense as well.

One notable application of this approach in the software supply chain is two-person code review,which requires at least two independent reviewers with sufficient privilege and expertise
to authorize source code changes before merging them into the main codebase
[
39
]
.
This practice helps improve the reliability of human code reviews by reducing the
risks of undetected problems in a codebase,
especially through contributions made by untrusted principals
[
64
]
.

Threshold authorization
increases the difficulty of an attacker gaining
unauthorized access by dividing the credential needed to
perform a step amongst
n
authorized principals (
P3-P5
). This approach might have helped narrowing the impact of SOLARBURST, or prevented the attackers from performing building steps. Threshold authorization has its roots in
(t,n)
threshold cryptography, where
t
out of
n
credential
shares are needed to decrypt data (e.g.,
[
120
]
).

Threshold schemes have also been applied in other use cases such private information
retrieval (e.g.,
[
141
]
) and peer-to-peer membership control (e.g.,
[
94
]
).
However, to our knowledge, the application of threshold schemes in the software supply chain has yet to be studied.

3.3
Discussion

Table
1
reveals that no one approach is sufficient for reducing
the possible risks to principals.
Practical defenses have combined multiple approaches to provide stronger
security properties.
One interesting combination of approaches are usage-limited credentials (e.g., OTPs
[
114
]
) with
multi-factor authentication (e.g., two-factor authentication
[
108
]
) reducing risks in two key areas (
P1
and
P3
). Despite the highly collaborative, multi-party nature of today’s software development practices,
this widely adopted defense combination primarily reduces risks for individual principals, who represent
single points of failure. Providing additional security properties requires layering this approach with others,
to make it more difficult to compromise an entire supply chain through a single principal.

Another promising combination of approaches are short-lived credentials like those provided by Sigstore’s Fulcio
[
88
]

and credential transparency: indeed, Fulcio relies on CT
[
4
]
for compromise detection and added reliability for issued
short-lived credentials
[
29
]
, reducing risks in three key areas (
P1-P3
, and
P5
).
To simplify usability, Fulcio additionally relies on OpenID Connect
[
15
]

and timestamping services (RFC 3161)
[
21
]
for credential generation and discovery.
Based on our evaluation, layering this defense combination with an additional approach to address
P4
would potentially
not significantly impact adoption costs.

Research gaps.

Several approaches in our evaluation have potential for
addressing software supply chain risks, although they have been studied only in other application domains.
Threshold schemes, for instance, meet several principal security
objectives (
P3-P5
); their applicability to improving the reliability
of authorization in the software supply chain seems a worthwhile direction for future research.

Further, credential transparency is a promising approach for active detection of credential
compromise. However, its limited adoption for software supply chain security warrants further study into its
practical applicability to address the problem. Similarly, understanding how other principal defenses
may be combined with credential transparency remains an open question.

4
Defending Software Artifacts

Artifacts are the unit of exchange in the software supply chain.
As a result, supply chain attacks targeting artifacts often focus on surreptitiously modifying or replacing an authentic artifact with a compromised one.

4.1
Security Objectives

A1 - Artifact changes detected:
Tampering with artifacts at rest, in transit or in memory can be detected.

A2 - Artifact non-equivocation:
All principals have the same view of the artifacts produced, and consumed by a given step.

A3 - Expected artifact behavior enforced:
Ensures that artifacts exhibit the expected functionality or behavior.

4.2
Defense Approach Evaluation

4.2.1
Detection of artifact tampering.

Such defenses provide mechanisms for detecting tampering with the integrity of an artifact in transit or at rest.

Hashing
relies on a cryptographically-strong hash function such as SHA-256
[
96
]
to ensure that any bit-level changes to
an artifact are detected. The central property of a cryptographically strong hash function that makes it suitable for artifact tamper detection is that the likelihood of
two different input artifacts having the same resulting checksum is extremely low.
In the software supply chain, hashing is used to enable artifact content matching in settings such as source code repositories
[
57
]
,
packages published online
[
43
]
, and even credentials
[
145
]
.

However, hashing alone only partially meets
A1
as this approach has security limitations
[
135
,
23
]
.
For instance, an attacker that is able to replace a published artifact on a website is likely able to also replace the checksum.
In fact, given that these checksums are distributed through the same channel as the software artifact, any compromise of such a channel subverts the integrity guarantees of hashing.

Artifact signing
consists of generating a digital signature over the contents of a software artifact (e.g., a package)
[
43
]
.
In practice, a variety of signing algorithms and protocols are used to sign artifacts.
Microsoft Authenticode
[
127
]
employs x.509 certificates, while Debian and Maven packages are signed with GPG
[
43
,
56
]
.
Similarly, version controlled source code (e.g., GitHub-hosted code) also commonly relies on GPG signing
[
58
,
71
]
.

Thus, the added authenticity properties provided by crytographic signatures overcome many of the limitations of hashing,
providing stronger tamper detection properties because the trust information (i.e., signing key) is generally delivered through a separate channel.
Nevertheless, studies like Cappos et al.
[
33
]
and Torres-Arias et al.
[
135
]

have shown how selectively tampering with artifact metadata can alter the integrity of a software artifact (or collection thereof) even if it is signed. This approach therefore only partially meets
A1
.

Metadata signing
applies the same principles to defend artifact metadata (e.g., file creation time)
against tampering as artifact signing. Such defenses prevent the use of metadata as an additional vector to attack signed software artifacts.

A widely used artifact metadata signing implementation is The Update Framework (TUF)
[
117
]
, which is used to sign package repository metadata. At the core of this design, separation of privilege (through “
signing roles
”) among metadata types provides both tamper detection as well as
a degree of non-equivocation through publicly visible “freshness claims”
[
117
]
.

Similarly, signing Docker container manifests help ensure a container’s authenticity and integrity
[
70
]
.
Indeed, this mechanism transitively signs it contents (by means of a cryptographic hash) as well as its metadata, such as container layers (i.e., dependent artifacts), size, and checksum.
Metadata signing together with artifact signing therefore achieves
A1
and conditionally
A2
.

Artifact transparency
leverages transparency logs to provide a verifiable
history of an artifact’s changes over time. This makes changes to artifacts as well as surreptitious or compromised releases
detectable (
A1
).
Since we consider credentials to be a special type of artifact,
we view this approach as a generalization of credential transparency (see §
3.2
).

One example is Firefox’s Binary Transparency

Another notable example is Sigstore

4.2.2
Enforcing Normal Behavioral Properties

Such defenses typically follow one of two general strategies: studying an artifact’s normal behavior to distinguish it from the malicious case;
or identifying abnormal behavior.

Static analysis
approaches are widespread in computer security.
In the context of software supply chain, these are often used to identify suspicious artifact components such as installed packages
(e.g., by detecting a vulnerable dependency), or to detect vulnerabilities that may be introduced transitively (e.g., a statically compiled vulnerable library).
At the core of these approaches lies a mechanism
for evaluating an abstract representation of an artifact, such as its abstract syntax tree (AST) or bytecode.

Techniques such as
[
77
]
trace so-called “taints”
throughout PHP web applications to identify critical code execution paths
that may lead to abnormal behavior. Feature-based analysis mechanisms such as Jarhead
[
119
]

for Java and Bandit
[
31
]
for Python trace through an artifact to evaluate and collect
information about abnormal program features, which can be later used to rate or classify an artifact’s behavior.
Since static analysis techniques tend to be very domain-specific and require a separate enforcement
mechanism, however, this approach only partially achieves
A3
.

Dynamic analysis
evaluates an artifact’s behavior during its execution.
The execution may be emulated or performed in a controlled environment to reduce
the influence of external factors in the analysis.
In the context of software supply chains, this approach is used to detect maliciously behaving artifacts
before their utilization as a resource (see §
5
).
The goal of this approach is twofold: to avoid maliciously-injected malicious behavior, and to reduce the number of vulnerabilities introduced as dependencies in the software supply chain.

TaintCheck
[
37
]
, for instance, employs taint analysis to
identify and mitigate specific vulnerabilities in x86 binaries through binary rewriting.
Solutions like Aftersight
[
99
]
perform a two-stage analysis in which full VM system behavior
is recorded, and then nondeterministic features (e.g., file system interactions) are analyzed separately.

Machine learning (ML) based approaches are emerging in this space as well.
Jarhead
[
119
]
, for instance, combines static analysis with ML to classify Java applets
as benign or malicious. HARP
[
138
]
uses Active Learning and Regeneration (ALR) to
detect application behavior with potentially abnormal side-effects (e.g., file system changes, global variables, modules, process arguments, etc).
To detect these, HARP models an artifact’s functional behavior in a controlled environment such that malicious behaviors are not learned during inference.
The inferred model can then regenerate vulnerability-free versions of string libraries in JavaScript and C/C++.

While all of these mechanisms may achieve
A3
, rewriting or regenerating binaries as in TaintCheck and HARP may directly
conflict with other objectives such as
A1
.

4.3
Discussion

Much like Principals, Table

shows that there is no one-stop-shop to achieve all desired objectives,
nor are several single approaches enough to achieve individual properties.
Artifact and metadata signing, for instance, are both needed to fully achieve
A1
.
Static and dynamic analysis techniques are often combined for improved results to identify
and enforce expected artifact behavior (
A3
).

While composing systems to achieve all artifact security objectives may be feasible,
(e.g., by means of dynamic analysis and metadata signing),
there is no established best practice for doing so, nor have the security implications of
such combined systems been studied.
This is a stark contrast with other elements of the model (e.g., see resources below), which have
garnered significant industry interest in establishing guidance in the software supply chain.

Another salient point is that of
conflicting properties
in dynamic analysis approaches, which may provide a particular
A3
at the expense of other objectives.
This highlights the need for an approach to establish a cross-property strategy.

Research gaps.
Some of the evaluated approaches have seen varied degrees of adoption.
This is evidenced by the low uptake in solutions such as artifact and metadata signing in critical areas
such as package management
[
78
,
16
]
.
For hashing, for instance, research on mechanisms such as subresource integrity html tags
[
35
]

may address known usability issues
[
36
]
, but more usability studies
on a wider variety of artifact defenses are needed to understand barriers to adoption.

Establishing better mental models about trust in artifacts like packages through the properties above is also lacking.
In particular, security policies for software trust in different ecosystems are ad-hoc, and do not follow a principled definition based on white or gray literature.
That is, systems such as GPG have provided mechanisms for developers to sign packages, but they rarely develop infrastructure and processes to verify that the right developer signed for the right package.
Further, there is very little software engineering research exploring the usability and process challenges to appropriately define and enforce these policies.

Approaches such as ledger-backed systems face performance and efficiency tradeoffs introduced by consensus
algorithms used to help achieve
A2
.
Given that these approaches can provide availability and privacy properties
[
32
,
24
]
in addition to meeting objectives (
A1, A2
), and these mechanisms provide a strong deterrent to attackers, more research is needed to
reduce the deployment costs of defenses.

5
Defending Resources

As described in §
2.1
, resources are themselves software components, i.e., they are created
through their own software supply chains. This means that many security objectives that apply to resources
may be met by artifact defenses (see §
4
).
At the same time, because resources are used to carry out (parts of) a particular supply chain step,
we identify additional security objectives specific to resources, which pertain to mitigating
execution-time risks.

For a resource to operate, it typically requires auxiliary resources and artifacts,
which we generally refer to as the
execution environment
.
Depending on the type of resource, it execution environment may be defined at various
levels of granularity ranging from an intra-process unit (e.g., thread or domain), to an application, to a virtualized environment (e.g., container or VM), to an entire system running on a hardware platform.
Reducing risks to resources thus involves protecting different aspects of execution
environments.

The Log4j vulnerability highlight the software supply chain risks associated with widespread use of open-source dependencies as resources. In December 2021, security researchers discovered a vulnerability that enables remote attackers to inject and remotely execute code through the messages that are logged by Log4j utility. After its discovery, millions of attempted exploits, many of which turned into successful denial-of-service (DoS) attacks
[
12
,
136
]
.

5.1
Resource Security Objectives

R1 - Resource privilege limited:
Limits the operating privileges a
resource is granted to only those needed to carry out a step
within

its execution environment. This objective is equivalent to
P3

aiming to reduce risk within the context of an authorized step.

R2 - Environment interfaces restricted:
Restricts the access points to
and from a resource’s execution environment.

R3 - Environment boundaries enforced:

Explicitly defines and enforces the boundaries of a resource’s execution environment.
This objective aims to prevent any interference between different resource
environments, even in the face of any fault or compromise.

5.2
Defense Approach Evaluation

5.2.1
Restrictions to resources

At a high level, such defenses regulate which resources can interact with which parts
of their operating environment.
This goal is commonly achieved by enforcing the
least privilege

[
116
]
property.
That is, these approaches limit the authority or permissions a given resource
has to perform specific operations, such as accessing local files,
communicating over a network, or calling certain functions.

Discretionary Access Control (DAC)
relies on the notion of ownership of artifacts
to determine which operations a resource is allowed to perform on them
[
116
]
.
In other words, DAC mechanisms (e.g., Linux file permissions
[
140
]
)
restrict access by resources based on the identity of their owner and the access permissions the owners
of target artifacts or resources have granted other users (
R1
).
A typical usage of DAC in software supply chain resources is in container and VM images used for CI/CD operations
[
60
,
62
]
,
which provide software developers and maintainers with a legacy, highly customizable operating environment.

Mandatory Access Control (MAC)
enforces a non-discretionary,
global access policy which enforces multiple levels of security for operations resources perform
[
116
]
.
As such, MAC can be used to meet objective (
R1
) as a compromised resource
can be prevented from performing an unauthorized operation by default.

One way these defenses may help reduce software supply chain risk is by running a kernel-level MAC system
like AppArmor
[
1
]
or SELinux
[
17
]
to control which operations (e.g.,
read()
,
mount()
)
a CI/CD or build resources may perform on artifacts
[
39
]
.

Other access control approaches.

For brevity, we focus on the two main models that meet resource security objectives.
Specific access control strategies mostly vary by granularity and types of policy rules,
but they may be used to enforce DAC or MAC models. Examples include Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)
[
53
]
,
Attribute Based Access Control (ABAC)
[
19
]
, and History-Based Access Control
[
51
]
.

5.2.2
Limiting compromises in execution environments

Defenses in this category aim to limit external access out of
or into an execution environment, or completely prevent interactions altogether.

Filtering
techniques intercept accesses at a particular
environment interface controlling the level of interactions between internal
and external resources.
At one end of the range, approaches such as the netfilter
[
142

restricts the network connections that a resource like a CI/CD system may establish with
remote resources (
R2
)
[
39
]
.

Defenses such as eBPF
[
49

provide programmable filtering of a
variety of kernel-level interfaces (e.g., network, system calls), which also enables
enforcement of MAC policies for specific resources running on a system (
R1
,
R2
).
3

3

While eBPF
[
49
]
executes within an isolated environment within the kernel, we do not consider this sufficient to meet

One example usage of eBPF in the software supply chain is for monitoring compiler execution during builds according to a security policy
[
74

.

Virtualization
creates an abstract execution environment in which resources share
common underlying hardware and software components without needing to be aware of other environments
[
50

.
Software virtualization techniques establish boundaries based on specific common properties or conditions shared by resources that
are typically enforced by privileged software (

). For example, Linux namespaces
[

and cgroups

are
used to implement container environments, in which the kernel enforces boundaries between named groups of processes based on hardware resource access constraints,
while sharing the same underlying kernel resources.Hardware virtualization approaches (e.g.,

Virtual machines are very commonly used in the software supply chain to implement strongly isolated execution environments

5.3
Discussion

As with prior supply chain elements, defense approaches for resources are complementary.
Yet, unlike other areas of the software supply chain, we argue that the longstanding history of
security research on computer systems has led to better compatibility between the evaluated approaches.

For instance, access control mechanisms are capable of operating at various levels of granularity
depending on a resource’s operating environment. While implementations such as AppArmor
[
1
]

provide application-level MAC, DAC can provide system-level defenses.
At the same time, filtering mechanisms such as netfilter
[
142
]
can provide
system-level protection in addition to DAC and MAC within the system.
Virtualization then provides an additional level of isolation
on top of access control and filtering.

We observe that resource defense approaches more commonly appear in industry security best
practices documents that have been authored in response to recent supply chain compromises.
Most notably, the Cloud Native Compute Foundation’s Software Supply Chain Best Practices
[
39
]
and
the Secure Software Development Framework (SSDF) by NIST
[
125
]
both describe a set of recommendations for configuring and implementing more secure software supply chain resources that rely on
defense approaches including MAC, filtering and virtualization. The Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts (SLSA)
[
124
]
, which provides a framework for incrementally
implementing a hardened build pipeline, also includes requirements for resource isolation to ensure that build steps are performed without any external influence to meet higher security levels.

Research gaps.
Academic research analyzing the security of software supply chain resources thus far taken
primarily an attack-oriented view (e.g.,
[
76
,
47
]
).
While the design and application of access control and resource isolation techniques have been widely
studied in the academic systems community, research dedicated to applying these defense
mechanisms in context of software supply chain security is still uncommon;
we see ample opportunities to build upon defense-oriented academic studies such as Gruhn
et al.

[
65
]

and Ohm
et al.

[
104
]
.

For brevity, we do not extensively evaluate resource defense approaches such as bounds checking (e.g.,
[

or more recent hardware-based techniques such as trusted execution environments (e.g.,
[

Yet, we observe in existing best practices guides that such approaches are under-explored in supply chain settings.

The focus on reducing risks to build and packaging resources

,

leaves an opportunity for future research to explore existing and novel approaches for other software supply chain use cases.
Further research is also needed around the adoption of industry best practices.

6
Defending Steps

Steps represent software development actions or operations that principals carry out on
artifacts with the help of resources.
Generally, step risks depend on whether risks to the principals, resources and artifacts
involved in a particular step have been addressed.
For example, compromise of a principal who is authorized to carry out a step leads to
a compromised step.

Yet, even with reduced risks to principals, artifacts and resources, steps may still face
risks related to the invocation or operation of the step.

6.1
Step Security Objectives

S1 - Expected step behavior enforced:
The expected step behavior is well-defined
and cannot be altered once triggered. Ensuring correct step behavior may also be achieved
by the correctness of the underlying resource(s) used to carry out a step.

S2 - Step operation recorded:
The identities of involved principals, artifacts used
and produced, and resources used, are recorded. Further, a step provides faithful
information about its operation.

S3 - Interference with step detected:
Detects attempts to tamper with or
otherwise interfere with the expected behavior of a step. Detecting attempts to present different
versions of the same step to different principals provide the stronger global non-equivocation property.

6.2
Defense Approach Evaluation

Several mechanisms exist to reduce risks to carrying out steps.
While most of the approaches we evaluate target build and packaging steps,
their design principles and properties can be applied to other steps in the software supply chain.
These mechanisms fall within two major categories: Preventative and detection approaches.

6.3
Limiting step risk through resources

Preventative approaches aim to ensure the correct behavior of a step from
within, proactively reducing risks to steps through careful choice or
configuration of the underlying
resources
used to carry out a step.

Formal verification
mechanisms leverage mathematical principles, such as discrete logic,
to check whether an algorithm or design meet specific properties or behavior. Typically, this approach
may be used to reduce risks to steps by checking the behavioral correctness of the resources used
to carry out a step (
S1
).

One instance is CompCert
[
86
]
, a functionally verified optimizing C compiler.
The use of verifiable and formal languages also fall within this category.
Work such as Rustan and Leino’s dafny
[
85
]
, for example, enable software developers to validate
the behavioral correctness of the programs they write at the source development step.

Deterministic steps
follow configuration patterns that prevent sources
of entropy from influencing the behavior or outcome of a step (
S1
).
For instance, Courtès
et al.

[
40
]
, describes how GUIX ensures correctness by using
source-to-binary mapping.

Navarro-Leija et al. proposed DetTrace
[
97
]
, a reproducible container abstraction that
enables reproducible software builds, among other use cases.
To this end, DetTrace controls the influence of a system’s behavior, such as naturally noisy system calls
(e.g.,
clock_gettime
) that would introduce nondeterminism into a step (
S1
).

“This works well for large companies with leverage (e.g. forcing
third-party developers to use analysis tools, specific languages, specific compiler flags, and other elements). Mandates can also come from management within a company (e.g., forcing all developers to run a static analysis tool). Clients, system vendors, and others can also mandate the use of security tools.”

6.4
Enabling step risk detection

Techniques that facilitate detection of compromises, while being less proactive,
enable relying parties to evaluate and make decisions about the risk of a step after-the-fact.

Step transparency
provides information about any aspect of a step’s operation to provide
visibility into all aspects of a step (
S2
).
An increasingly prominent type of step metadata are Software Bills of Materials
[
128
]

for their use in software supply chain compliance
[
146
]
. Their main goal, while not authenticated,
is to convey information about artifact composition, software “pedigree”, and quality,
complementing other step operational metadata such as SLSA Provenance
[
124
]
.

One of the earliest proposals to capture step operational metadata was in-toto
[
134
]

via a primitive referred to as Links. in-toto Links are
authenticated
documents describing
the input and output artifacts to a step, the command executed during a step, and any step
byproducts such as return value or error messages. Using principal and artifact defense mechanisms
allows in-toto to also detect tampering with any aspect of the step (
S3
).

Step consensus
relies on the repeatability and/or visibility of a step to enable multiple parties to determine jointly if a step has been tampered with
(
S3
). That is, if different parties obtain different metadata or results for a given
step, then the step is assumed to have been tampered with.

Approaches such as the IETF Supply Chain Integrity Transparency and Trust (SCITT
[
32
]
) framework
and Ferraiuolo
et al.

[
54
]
provide global (
S2
) and (
S3
) properties
using mechanisms like distributed ledgers and transparency logs to expose policy decisions as part of the
metadata recording and publication process.
Bandara
et al.

[
30
]
propose a similar mechanism based on a blockchain as a
backend data store to provide a globally-visible representation of the state of the software supply chain.

Nikitin
et al.
proposed CHAINIAC
[
6
]
, for instance, provides strong tamper-detection guarantees
for software updates through a novel data structure called a skipchain (i.e., the merging of a skiplist and a blockchain).
CHAINIAC detects step tampering in two stages. First, a group of build services re-runs
a particular build step. Then, they validate the resulting binary, and only commit it into the skipchain
if the builders reach consensus over the binary.

As CHAINIAC skipchains contain a cryptographically verifiable
log of all released binaries, the approach also helps different parties ensure that they all
have the same view of the software updates over time (the stronger non-equivocation property of
S3
).
Similar approaches were proposed by Hof
et al.

[
66
]
and Linderud
[
87
]
.

6.5
Discussion

The majority of step defenses developed and studied thus far has focused on mechanisms for
recording step metadata to detect tampering.
Xia
et al.

[
144
]
, for instance, conducted a study that focuses on current SBOM practices, tooling and concerns
from SBOM practitioners’ perspectives.
The study shows lack of SBOM adoption and problems in enabling SBOMs benefits, due to various concerns
regarding SBOM generation, distribution, consumption processes.

Given these concerns, the study suggests three prerequisites to increase SBOM adoption and SBOM enabled benefits: higher quality SBOM generation, clearer benefits and use cases in SBOM consumption, and lower barriers in SBOM sharing.

Research gaps.

In both academic and gray literature, we observe a general lack of studies on
preventing or reducing risks that affect the correct execution or operation of software supply chain steps.
Formal verification, for instance, has been widely explored by the programming language and
software engineering communities, though, its application in the context of
the software supply chain remains under-explored.
Furthermore, the adoption of formally verifiable systems as resources for steps presents
an opportunity for further research.

While the design of consensus-based step defense mechanisms has been the focus of
an increasing number of academic studies, their practical applicability and adoption
costs remain relatively under-explored. Similarly, the design, development and adoption
of step containment approaches at large, is an area that warrants further study.

7
Defending Supply Chain Topology

While the previous sections cover risks and defenses for individual elements
in a software supply chain graph, these defenses most often do not capture the federated and transitive
properties inherent
across
a series of interdependent steps.
These effects may become more pronounced depending on the number of organizations
and principals involved in carrying out these steps using a multitude of resources which
may not always be cross-compatible. The left-pad incident is a good example of the transitivity of the software supply chain risk, such that unpublishing the package, and removing it from the topology, broke the build steps that use it as a dependency, which broke many topologies that uses this step.

Since attacks that modify a supply chain topology at large impact downstream steps and artifacts,
we separately evaluate risks and defenses that affect a topology in order to reason about
transitive software supply chain risk.

7.1
Topology Security Objectives

Software supply chain topology defenses usually focus on providing
improved integrity to identify and detect topology changes,
ensuring that a set of
steps
executed in a specific order
perform consistently, or remediating changes to improve reliability of a software supply chain.

Thus, defenses can meet the following security objectives:

T1 - Topology changes detected:
Provides a mechanism for detecting changes to the expected structure of the software supply chain graph, i.e., additions, removals or reordering of
individual supply chain elements. This property validates the integrity of parts or whole software supply chains, essentially detecting modified or compromised edges between two elements in the graph.

T2 - No single point of failure:
Reduces the risks of failure in a software supply chain due to a missing step or resource.
This is achieved through
redundancy
mechanisms
[
45
,
28
]
.

T3 - Attack propagation prevented:
Limits the risks to a particular step, resource or principal by blocking or avoiding
an interaction or transitive relationship between two vertices in a software supply chain graph.

T4 - Topology non-equivocation:
All principals have the same view of the topology at a given point in time, this includes the set and order of steps in the topology.

T5 - Consistent results:
Provides a process for ensuring or checking that a set of the steps performed on given artifacts
in a specific order always produces consistent artifacts. This property reflects the correctness of an entire supply chain graph topology.
This property is stricter than
T4
, we consider an exact match in final artifacts to be the strongest form of consistency for the sake of this evaluation.

7.2
Defense Approach Evaluation

Reducing risk over an entire topology requires knowledge about
parts or all of the steps and resources in a software supply chain. Thus, to enable defenses
for validation and detection of tampering or other topology changes, we assume
mechanisms are in place to establish a good-known topology configurations a priori.
Similarly, for defenses that improve the reliability of a software supply chain to prevent attacks
made possible through topology changes, these defenses typically rely on a mechanism for
detecting problematic configurations, and recovering from these dynamically.

Supply chain layout integrity
enables principals to detect whether all steps in a software supply chain were performed in the
expected order (
T4
), whether they were carried out by the expected principals, and whether expected artifacts were modified between steps.
This approach first formally introduced in Torres Arias
et al.

[
134
]
reduces the risks of added, removed, or reordered steps (
T1
).

Using a primitive called layouts, in-toto
[
134
]
implements this approach by requiring project owners or maintainers
(a principal role) to pre-define the set of steps to be performed, the principals authorized to perform each step, and the expected consumed and produced materials for each step, a priori.
Given an in-toto layout document authenticated by the expected project owner, and step metadata is available (see §
6
),
interested principals are able to validate that a given artifact was produced following the expected in-toto layout.

Resource replication
addresses risks in a step caused by a single point of failure such as a removed or
problematic resource that the particular step in the software supply chain graph depends on. For instance, in the Debian or Ubuntu package ecosystem, principals
may configure their dependency fetching steps to use “mirrors”
[
2
]
, which replicate and regularly synchronize with a central package
registry to provide a fallback mechanism in case that a chosen package registry becomes unavailable or otherwise inaccessible (
T2
). In the case of left-pad incident , this technique could have helped the impacted builds to succeed.

Dependency reduction
is a defense technique that intentionally modifies a supply chain DAG itself in order to
block or avoid transitive relationships between two vertices, such as principals, resources and steps. One solution specifically developed
to address compromises in artifacts that are built using a compiler (i.e., a resource) such as GCC is Bootstrappable builds
[
41
,
3
]
.
The key idea behind Bootstrappable builds is that the smaller the so-called “seed” compiler is for a project, the lower the risks of
compromise are for the project since a seed can be individually inspected, increasing the transparency of the dependencies.
Reducing a resource dependency to a seed ultimately limits the transitive relationships between a step and a resource (
T3
).

Supply chain reproducibility
is broadly a mechanism for detecting whether a particular sequence of steps in a software supply chain always produces
the same results. In practice, reproducibility relies on the chaining of techniques for individual steps like deterministic builds
(discussed in §
6
), which ensure that a particular step, such as build, always produces the same result (
T5
). By enabling
downstream principals to repeat a sequence of upstream steps, upstream producers enable their consumers to validate the integrity of a
software supply chain for their product artifact. This guarantees that all principals have the same view
of the set and order of steps and of the topology (
T4
).

7.3
Discussion

Drawing further from systems research on redundancy, we observe that while some mechanisms for
replication are applicable in the software supply chain, the stronger property of
fault tolerance
, in which
a system may tolerate or even recover from unexpected changes such as modifications to the supply chain DAG
topology
[
45
,
28
]
remain unexplored. One possible means to achieving
this property may be by substituting an individual compromised or faulty software supply chain element
with an equivalent element before re-attempting to execute a given step, though further research and engineering
are necessary to better understand the impact of such fault recovery mechanisms to the security, usability,
and performance of a software supply chain.

Research gaps.
while data-based approaches have been used to model risks in the software supply chain, there has been no studies on best software supply chain practices using topological data.
Using topology information from different data sources, it is possible to shed light on the correlation between different supply chain practices and their security outcomes to provide empirical models to harden the software ecosystem.
Similarly, interventions and human-aspects research to understand the way that software supply chains are topologically configured (beyond package dependency networks) will aid in understanding how individual structural risks surface.
This will allow to introduce stronger, data informed levels of replication and reduction (
T2-3
), as well as define ideal topologies that are based on empirical data (
T1
).
Further, mechanisms to provide a transparent, tight binding between policies and their evaluation will allow for stronger insights on the security posture of an artifact in context (
T4
).
Lastly, the use and exploration of software reproducibility is widely explored in practice but overlooked in academia.
This includes work developing
tools and systems
that increase, leverage, or generate insights reproducibility, as well as human-centered studies on the day-to-day challenges in achieving and maintaining step reproducibility to pave the way to a more stable software supply chain topology (
T5
).

To explore the utility of the AStRA model for software supply chain security assessment, we need to ensure
two properties:
generality
and
completeness
.
We define generality as the ability of the model to represent any software supply chain.
This property is important because missing or misrepresenting a particular supply chain topology
may lead to security gaps.
Completeness extends the concept of generality and is defined as the ability to capture every

Future Research Opportunities

The AStRA model allows us to evaluate different security mechanisms in the context of software supply chain
defenses to reveal future research opportunities (towards
RQ4
).

Reducing Barriers to Adoption.
Originally, we intended to evaluate the
tradeoffs and costs to adopt mechanisms for software supply chain security.
Unfortunately, we found there are very little literature and formal studies on how to deploy a particular approach.
These studies should cover human factors (e.g., training), usability, and system requirements (e.g., extra servers).
Thus, we envision that a green field for research is that of
Usable software supply chain security
.

Moving Beyond Modeling Threats.

Our objective-driven model is a first step towards systematically mapping threats to defenses.
We see an opportunity to expand upon our work to design novel adoption-ready software supply chain defenses.
Beyond our preliminary work, we envision future research that incorporates holistic
cost-benefit analysis frameworks for supply chain risk mitigation with the goal of increasing adoption of
supply chain defenses.

Understanding the Impact & Security of AI-Generated Software.
Generative AI
is playing an increasingly central role in software development. According to a recent survey

122
]
,
about 92% of developers in the U.S. rely on AI-based tools. This rapid shift presents ample future research
opportunities to study the security implications of AI-generated code and address software and supply chain security
in this emerging space.
