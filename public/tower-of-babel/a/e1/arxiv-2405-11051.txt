Darboux transformation of diffusion processes
Alexey Kuznetsov, Minjian Yuan

Abstract
Darboux transformation of a second order linear differential operator is a well-known technique with many applications in mathematics and physics. We study Darboux transformation from the point of view of Markov semigroups of diffusion processes. We construct a Darboux transform of a diffusion process through a combination of Doob’s h h -transform and a version of Siegmund duality. Our main result is a simple formula that connects transition probability densities of the two processes. We provide several examples of Darboux transformed diffusion processes related to Brownian motion and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. For these examples we compute explicitly the transition probability density and derive its spectral representation.



Keywords
: diffusion process, killed Brownian motion, Darboux transform, Sturm-Liouville theory, Markov semigroup, Doob’s transform, Siegmund duality

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
: Primary 60J60, Secondary 60J35

1
Introduction

Darboux transformation is a well-known technique for studying second order linear differential operators that goes back to the late 19th century
[
7
]
.
To introduce this transformation, we start with a second order linear differential operator

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-c(y),

(1)

where
y\in(l,r)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. We assume that
c(y)
is continuous on
(l,r)
and that
{\mathcal{L}}
acts on
C^{2}
functions on
(l,r)
. Let
h
be a positive function on
(l,r)
that satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. It is easy to check that the second order differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}-\lambda
can be factorized as a product of two first order differential operators

{\mathcal{L}}-\lambda=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{\frac{1}{h}}{\mathcal{D}}_{h},

(2)

where we denoted

{\mathcal{D}}_{h}:=\partial_{y}-\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}=h(y)\partial_{y}\frac{1}{h(y)},\;\;\;{\mathcal{D}}_{\frac{1}{h}}:=\partial_{y}+\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}=\frac{1}{h(y)}\partial_{y}h(y).

(3)

Now we introduce the Darboux transform of
{\mathcal{L}}
as a second order linear differential operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
defined via

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}-\lambda=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{D}}_{\frac{1}{h}}.

(4)

Thus, the Darboux transform of a second order linear operator is obtained by factorizing it (or, more precisely, its shift by
\lambda
) as a product of two first order differential operators and then interchanging the order of the two factors. It is an easy exercise to verify that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-\tilde{c}(y),

(5)

where

\tilde{c}(y):=c(y)-\partial_{y}^{2}\ln h(y)=\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}\Big)^{2}-c(y)-2\lambda.

(6)

The key property of the Darboux transformation, which makes it so useful in applications, is the following intertwining relation

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{L}},

(7)

which follows from (
2
) and (
4
). In particular, if
f
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}f=\mu f
then

g={\mathcal{D}}_{h}f
solves
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g=\mu g
. Note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(1/h)=\lambda(1/h)
(this follows immediately from
(
4
)), therefore the Darboux transformation is invertible: starting from the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and applying the Darboux transformation with the function
\tilde{h}(y)=1/h(y)
we will recover the original operator
{\mathcal{L}}
. The Darboux transformation can be iterated several times – these are usually called Darboux-Crum transformations (see
[
6
]
and
[
12
]
[Section 2.4]).

A vast literature exists on the applications of the Darboux transformation in applied mathematics and physics. For example, the
Darboux transformation is used to construct exactly solvable potentials for Schrödinger equation
[
1
]
and it plays a crucial role in the study of exceptional orthogonal polynomials
[
9
,
10
]
. Many other applications in integrable systems are described in
[
13
]

and
[
18
]
. However, it seems that the Darboux transformation has not yet been studied in the context of diffusion processes, and this is what we intend to do in the present paper.

Assuming that the functions
c(y)
and
\tilde{c}(y)
in (
1
) and (
5
) are positive, we can consider the operators
{\mathcal{L}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
as the infinitesimal generators of killed Brownian motion processes
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
with state space
(l,r)
. When a boundary point
l
or
r
is non-singular for one of these processes (see Section
3
for a review of Feller’s boundary classification), we have many different ways to construct a diffusion process with a given infinitesimal generator by specifying different boundary conditions (reflecting/killing/elastic/etc.). Instead of first specifying the boundary conditions and then studying the resulting processes, we will proceed in the opposite direction. We will first set a goal for what kind of process we want to construct, and then we will see what boundary conditions and other ingredients are needed for this construction to work. What follows next is an informal description of what we plan to do. Let
Y
be a killed Brownian motion on
(l,r)
with the Markov semigroup
\{{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
(where we denoted
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}f(y):={\mathbb{E}}_{y}[f(Y_{t})]
) and
with the infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}
given by (
1
). Assume that we found another killed Brownian motion process

\widetilde{Y}
with the Markov semigroup
\{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

and the infinitesimal generator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
given by (
5
). Moreover, we assume that the intertwining relationship for generators (
7
) carries over to a similar relationship for semigroups, so that
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
. We want to use this intertwining relationship to express the transition operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
in terms of
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
. One way to define operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
which satisfy such intertwining relationship is via

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{P}}_{t}{\mathcal{I}}^{(z)}_{h},

(8)

where
z
is some fixed point in
(l,r)
and the integral operator
{\mathcal{I}}^{(z)}_{h}
is defined as follows

{\mathcal{I}}^{(z)}_{h}f(y):=h(y)\int_{z}^{y}\frac{f(u)}{h(u)}{\textnormal{d}}u.

Assume that
h
is a positive
\lambda
-invariant function for
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
, meaning that it satisfies
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}h=e^{\lambda t}h
. This condition implies
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
. Then the operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
defined in (
8
) would indeed satisfy the intertwining relationship
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{t}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
, since
{\mathcal{I}}^{(z)}_{h}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}f(y)=f(y)-f(z)h(y)/h(z)
for
C^{1}
functions
f
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{h}{\mathcal{P}}_{t}h=0
(due to
\lambda
-invariance of
h
). A similar argument shows that the operators defined in (
8
) would not depend on the choice of
z\in(l,r)
. If it is indeed true that the Markov semigroups
of
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
satisfy (
8
), then it is easy to verify that the transition probability densities of
\widetilde{Y}
and
Y
should satisfy:

p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\frac{h(x)}{h(y)}\partial_{x}\Big[\frac{1}{h(x)}\int_{y}^{r}p_{t}^{Y}(x,u)h(u){\textnormal{d}}u\Big].

(9)

We added a question mark in the above equation to emphasize that this is only a hypothesis at this stage.
Indeed, the above discussion is non-rigorous, and this construction of the process
\widetilde{Y}
has many gaps. For example, it is not clear why the operators
(
8
) would indeed define a Markov semigroup, in particular, why the right-hand side of (
9
) should be positive. It is unclear whether we should impose the boundary conditions on
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
in some specific way for
(
9
) to be true. However, this informal reasoning is essentially correct, as we will demonstrate in this paper.
Our main result is the construction of a killed Brownian motion process
\widetilde{Y}
such the transition probabilities of
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
satisfy an analogue of
(
9
) (the difference is that we will allow adding a constant to
\tilde{c}(y)
that would make the resulting function positive, and this will introduce an extra exponential factor in (
9
)). We will show that the process
\widetilde{Y}
can be constructed in three steps, starting from a killed Brownian motion
Y
and a
\lambda
-invariant
function
h
: Doob’s
h
-transform followed by a version of Siegmund duality
[
23
]
followed by another Doob’s
h
-transform.

The primary motivation for constructing Darboux transformed diffusion processes is that this would greatly expand the number of diffusions for which the transition probability density can be computed in closed form. There are only a few examples (Brownian motion, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, Bessel process, square root diffusion process, and several others) for which the transition probability is known in closed form, and all of these processes are very useful in applications, particularly in Mathematical Finance. Expanding the number of such examples is, therefore, a worthwhile task.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall some basic facts about diffusion processes, including Feller’s classification of boundary points for diffusion processes. In Section
3
we discuss Siegmund duality
[
3
,
23
]
and present a new version of it (which can be applied to processes with reflecting instead of absorbing boundary conditions). In Section
4
we
construct the Darboux transform of a killed Brownian motion and present an example that illustrates the necessity of
\lambda
-invariance of
h
: we show that if
h
solves
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
but is not
\lambda
-invariant for the process
Y
, then the right-hand side of (
9
) may be negative. Section
5
provides five examples of the Darboux transformation applied to various diffusion processes. In the first four examples we start with the Brownian motion process on an interval with various boundary conditions, and in the last example we apply the Darboux transformation to the Brownian motion killed at rate
y^{2}/2
(this latter process is closely related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process). In all examples we describe the boundary behavior and derive explicit formulas for the transition probability density of the transformed process. In four of these examples we also provide the spectral representation of the transition probability density. At the end of Section
5
we discuss connections between our results and those obtained in the physics literature on propagators for the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce notation and recall several basic facts about diffusion processes, which will be useful later. We will mostly follow
[
2
]
[Chapter II]; other good references are
[
14
,
17
]
.
Let
X
be a regular diffusion process on an interval
(l,r)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. We assume that the infinitesimal generator of
X
has the form

{\mathcal{L}}_{X}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\partial_{x}^{2}+b(x)\partial_{x}-c(x),

(10)

where the functions
b
,
c
and
\sigma
are continuous on
(l,r)
and
\sigma(x)>0
and
c(x)\geq 0
for
x\in(l,r)
.
The operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
is acting on the class of
C^{2}
functions on
(l,r)
with appropriate boundary conditions, which will be described below. We denote by
s(x)
the scale function of
X
and by
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)
and
{\textnormal{d}}k(x)
the speed measure and the killing measure of
X
. The derivative of
s
and the densities of measures
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)
and
{\textnormal{d}}k(x)
can be found via

s^{\prime}(x)=e^{-B(x)},\;\;m^{\prime}(x)=2\sigma^{-2}(x)e^{B(x)},\;\;\;\;k^{\prime}(x)=c(x)m^{\prime}(x),

(11)

where
B^{\prime}(x):=2\sigma^{-2}(x)b(x)
, see
[
2
]
[Section II.9].
We denote

f^{\pm}(x)=\lim\limits_{h\to 0^{\pm}}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{s(x+h)-s(x)}.

Let us now recall Feller’s boundary classification (see
[
2
]
[Section II.6]). We fix a point
z\in(l,r)
and denote

R(x):=(m(z)-m(x)+k(z)-k(x))s^{\prime}(x),\;\;\;Q(x):=(s(z)-s(x))(m^{\prime}(x)+k^{\prime}(x)).

(12)

The left boundary
l
is called
exit
if
R\in L_{1}((l,z))
and
entrance
if
Q\in L_{1}((l,z))
. The conditions for the right boundary
r
are the same, except for an obvious change of the interval
(l,z)\mapsto(z,r)
. A boundary point that is both not-entrance and not-exit is called
natural
while a boundary point that is both entrance and exit is called
non-singular
. If
l
(or
r
) is a non-singular boundary point for
X
, we impose one of the following boundary conditions

(i)

elastic
boundary condition:
f(l+)=\alpha f^{+}(l+)
for
\alpha>0
(or
f(r-)=-\beta f^{-}(r-)
for
\beta>0
).

(ii)

reflecting
boundary condition:
f^{+}(l+)=0
(or
f^{-}(r-)=0
);

(iii)

killing
boundary condition:
f(l+)=0
(or
f(r-)=0
).

In this paper we do not consider sticky boundary conditions or traps (in the terminology of
[
2
]
[Section II.7]) – these are boundary conditions involving the second derivative at a point.

Let
\lambda>0
and denote by
\psi_{\lambda}(x)
and
\varphi_{\lambda}(x)

the fundamental solutions
to the second order ODE
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}f=\lambda f
. We recall that the fundamental solutions are unique (up to multiplication by a constant) positive solutions to
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}f=\lambda f
such that
\psi_{\lambda}
is increasing and
\varphi_{\lambda}
is decreasing
(see
[
2
]
[Section II.10]). Also, if
l
is a non-singular boundary point, then
\psi_{\lambda}
must satisfy one of the boundary conditions stated above (and the same applies for
\varphi_{\lambda}
at the right boundary point
r
). Their Wronskian is defined as follows

{\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\lambda};\psi_{\lambda}]:=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\psi_{\lambda}(x),

(13)

and it is known that the quantity
\omega_{\lambda}:={\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\lambda};\psi_{\lambda}]/s^{\prime}(x)
is independent of
x
. Let
p^{X}_{t}(x,y)
be the transition probability density of the process
X
with respect to the speed measure, that is

{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)=\int_{A}p^{X}_{t}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}m(y),

for all Borel sets
A\subset(l,r)

The function
p^{X}_{t}(x,y)
is symmetric in
x
and
y
, (see
[
2
]
[Section II.4]). The Green function of the process
X
has the following expression in terms of the fundamental solutions
\psi_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}
:

G^{X}_{\lambda}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}p^{X}_{t}(x,y){\textnormal{d}}y=\begin{cases}\omega_{\lambda}^{-1}\psi_{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{\lambda}(y),\;\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}x\leq y,\\
\omega_{\lambda}^{-1}\psi_{\lambda}(y)\varphi_{\lambda}(x),\;\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}y\leq x,\end{cases}

(14)

as stated in
[
2
]
[Section II.11].

We also mention the following fact: if a process
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is mapped into a process
Y=\{y(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
via an increasing differentiable function
y=y(x)
, then the scale function and speed measure are transformed as follows:

s_{Y}(y(x))=s_{X}(x),\;\;\;m_{Y}^{\prime}(y(x))=m_{X}^{\prime}(x)/y^{\prime}(x).

The state space for process
Y
is
(y(l),y(r))
, and the boundary behavior of
Y
at points
y(l)
and
y(r)
is the same as the boundary behavior of
X
at
l
and
r
.

3
Siegmund duality

Let
Z
and
\widetilde{Z}
be two Markov processes on
[0,\infty)
. We say that
\widetilde{Z}
is a Siegmund dual of
Z
if

{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(Z_{t}\geq x)={\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}\leq y),

(15)

for all
x,y,t\geq 0
. This concept was introduced by Siegmund
[
23
]
in 1976 and was later studied in
[
3
,
5
,
15
]
. Diffusion processes satisfying (
15
) were called conjugate diffusions in
[
25
]
. The existence of a dual process
\widetilde{Z}
requires the process
Z
to be
stochastically monotone
, see
[
3
,
23
]
. The latter property can be stated as follows: for any
t>0
and
x>0
the function
y\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(Z_{t}\geq x)
is non-decreasing.

Siegmund duality is applied when one of the processes has an absorbing boundary. For example, assuming that
Z
is conservative and taking the limit of (
15
) as
x\to 0^{+}
, we see that the process
\widetilde{Z}
must have an absorbing boundary at zero. Thus, in this case, the distribution of
\widetilde{Z}_{t}
will have an atom at zero, and assuming that this distribution has a density on
(0,\infty)
, it must be given by

p_{t}^{\widetilde{Z}}(x,y)=\partial_{y}{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(Z_{t}\geq x),

(16)

which follows from (
15
) by taking derivative in
y
. Our goal in this section is to construct a version of Siegmund duality, which works for processes with reflecting/killing boundary conditions and where we would still have a result similar to (
16
).

For the rest of this section, we assume that
X
is a conservative diffusion process on the interval
(l,r)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
having the infinitesimal generator

{\mathcal{L}}_{X}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\partial_{x}^{2}+b(x)\partial_{x}=\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}m(x)}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}s(x)},

(17)

where the scale function
s(x)
and the speed measure
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)
are determined via
(
11
). We assume that
\sigma(x)
is a positive and continuously differentiable function of
x\in(l,r)
.
If some boundary points are non-singular for
X
, we impose a
reflecting
boundary condition at that point.

We want to construct a new diffusion process
\widetilde{X}
on
(l,r)
, which has
the speed measure/scale function equal to the scale function/speed measure of
X
(see Section 4 in
[
5
]
). In other words, the infinitesimal generator of
\widetilde{X}
should have the form

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}=\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}s(x)}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}m(x)}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)\partial_{x}^{2}+\big(\sigma(x)\sigma^{\prime}(x)-b(x)\big)\partial_{x}.

(18)

Note that the operators
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}
satisfy the following intertwining relation

\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}s(x)}{\mathcal{L}}_{X}={\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}\;\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}s(x)},

(19)

which will play an important role later.

To complete our construction of the process
\widetilde{X}
, we need to discuss boundary behavior at
l
and
r
. The type of the boundary behavior depends on the functions
R
and
Q
defined via (
12
), and when we compute their analogues
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{Q}
for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}
we find that

\widetilde{Q}\equiv R
and
\widetilde{R}\equiv Q
(because the killing measure is zero and

m_{X}(s)=s_{\widetilde{X}}(s)
and
s_{X}(x)=m_{\widetilde{X}}(x)
). Note that
l
can not be exit-not-entrance for
X
since we assumed that
X
is conservative. From the boundary classification presented on page
12
, it follows that

(i)

if
l
is a non-singular reflecting boundary for
X
, then it is also a non-singular boundary for
\widetilde{X}
, and in this case we impose a
killing
boundary condition
\tilde{f}(l+)=0
for
\widetilde{X}
;

(ii)

if
l
is a natural boundary for
X
, then it stays a natural boundary for
\widetilde{X}
;

(iii)

if
l
is an entrance-not-exit, it becomes an exit-not-entrance boundary for
\widetilde{X}
.

The same applies to the right boundary point
r
. Note that in cases (ii) and (iii) we do not need to impose boundary conditions (as they are already determined by the character of the boundary point, see
[
2
]
[Section II.8]). This discussion of the boundary behavior completes our construction of the process
\widetilde{X}
.

The following is our main result in this section, which connects the transition probabilities of
X
and
\widetilde{X}
.

Theorem 1
.

For
t>0
and
x_{1},x_{2},y\in(l,r)
such that
x_{1}<x_{2}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{1}}(X_{t}\leq y)={\mathbb{P}}_{x_{2}}(X_{t}\leq y)+{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(x_{1}<\widetilde{X}_{t}\leq x_{2}).

(20)

Proof.

We will first establish this result in the special case when
b(x)\equiv 0
. In this case the process
X
is in the natural scale, that is
s_{X}(x)\equiv x
. Let
\lambda>0
and
\psi_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}
be the fundamental solutions to
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}f=\lambda f
. We claim that the fundamental solutions to
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}f=\lambda f
are given by

\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x)=\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x),\;\;\;\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda}(x)=-\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x).

(21)

To prove this, we note first that if
f
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}f=\lambda f
, then
\tilde{f}=f^{\prime}
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}\tilde{f}=\lambda\tilde{f}
, as follows from the intertwining relation (
19
). It is clear that the functions
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda}
are positive on
(l,r)
(since
\psi_{\lambda}
is increasing
and
\varphi_{\lambda}
is decreasing). Next we note that
\lambda>0
and
\psi_{\lambda}
is a positive solution to
\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda f(x)
, which implies that
\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(x)>0
, so that
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}=\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}
is increasing. The same argument shows that
\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda}
is decreasing. If
l
is a natural or entrance-not-exit boundary for
X
, it becomes a natural or exit-not-entrance boundary for
\widetilde{X}
. In these cases we do not need to impose a boundary condition for
\psi_{\lambda}
at
l
(the increasing solution to
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f=\lambda f
is unique, up to a multiplicative constant, as follows from see
[
2
]
[Section II.10]). If
l
is a non-singular reflecting boundary for
X
, then necessarily
\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(l+)=0
, which implies the correct killing boundary condition
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(l+)=0
. The same considerations apply to the boundary condition of
\varphi_{\lambda}
at
r
. This ends the proof of (
21
).

Next, we check that if
\omega_{\lambda}={\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\lambda};\psi_{\lambda}]
then

{\textnormal{Wr}}[\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda};\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}]=-\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(x)+\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda}{\sigma^{2}(x)}{\textnormal{Wr}}[\psi_{\lambda};\phi_{\lambda}]=\lambda\omega_{\lambda}s_{\widetilde{X}}^{\prime}(x),

(22)

where in the second step we used the fact that
\psi_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}
are solutions to
\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda f(x)
.
Using (
14
) and (
22
) we can write the Green function of the process
\widetilde{X}
as

G^{\widetilde{X}}_{\lambda}(x,y)=\begin{cases}-(\lambda\omega_{\lambda})^{-1}\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y),\;\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}x\leq y,\\
-(\lambda\omega_{\lambda})^{-1}\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y)\tilde{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x),\;\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}y\leq x.\end{cases}

(23)

Now we have all the ingredients to complete the proof of (
20
). We rewrite
(
20
) in the following form:

\int_{l}^{y}p^{X}_{t}(x_{1},u){\textnormal{d}}u=\int_{l}^{y}p^{X}_{t}(x_{2},u){\textnormal{d}}u+\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}p^{\widetilde{X}}_{t}(y,v){\textnormal{d}}v.

(24)

We need to prove that (
24
) holds for all
t>0
and
x_{1},x_{2},y
in
(l,r)
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
. We take
\lambda>0
, multiply both sides of
(
29
) by
\exp(-\lambda t)
and integrate in
t
over
(0,\infty)
. After applying Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

2\int_{l}^{y}G^{X}_{\lambda}(x_{1},u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u=2\int_{l}^{y}G^{X}_{\lambda}(x_{2},u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u+\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}G^{\widetilde{X}}_{\lambda}(y,v){\textnormal{d}}v.

(25)

By the uniqueness of Laplace transform, to prove (
24

25

\lambda>0
and
x_{1},x_{2},y\in(l,r)
such that
x_{1}<x_{2}
. Identity (
25
) is true if

2\partial_{x}\int_{l}^{y}G^{X}_{\lambda}(x,u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u+G^{\widetilde{X}}_{\lambda}(x,y)=0,

(26)

for
x,y\in(l,r)
. We will prove first that (
26
) holds when
l<x<y
. Using (
14
) and (
23
)
we rewrite (
26
) in the form

\frac{2}{\omega_{\lambda}}\partial_{x}\Big[\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\int_{l}^{x}\psi_{\lambda}(u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u+\psi_{\lambda}(x)\int_{x}^{y}\varphi_{\lambda}(u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u\Big]-\frac{1}{\lambda\omega_{\lambda}}\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y)=0.

(27)

Since
\psi_{\lambda}
is a solution to
\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\lambda f(x)
, we have

2\int_{l}^{x}\psi_{\lambda}(u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{l}^{x}\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u){\textnormal{d}}u=\frac{1}{\lambda}\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x),

since
\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(l+)=0
due to boundary conditions on the process
X
(see

[
2
]
[Section II.10]). Similarly

2\int_{x}^{y}\varphi_{\lambda}(u)\sigma^{-2}(u){\textnormal{d}}u=\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y)-\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\big).

With the help of the above two equations, we can rewrite (
27
) as follows

\frac{1}{\lambda\omega_{\lambda}}\partial_{x}\big({\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\lambda};\psi_{\lambda}]+\psi_{\lambda}(x)\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y)\big)-\frac{1}{\lambda\omega_{\lambda}}\psi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\varphi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(y)=0,

and this identity is true since
{\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\lambda};\psi_{\lambda}]=\omega_{\lambda}
does not depend on
x
. Thus, we have established (
26
) when
l<x<y
. The proof that (
26
) holds when
l<y<x
is done in the same way, and the details are omitted.

This ends the proof of (
26
) in the driftless case, when
b(x)\equiv 0
. The general case follows by considering the driftless process
Y=\{s(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
and constructing the transformed process
\widetilde{Y}
and defining
\widetilde{X}_{t}=s^{-1}(\widetilde{Y}_{t})
. The fact that
identity (
26
) holds for processes
Y
and
\widetilde{Y}
implies that it also holds for
X
and
\widetilde{X}
. We leave it as an exercise to check that the process
\widetilde{X}
thus constructed has the infinitesimal generator as in
(
18
).

Condition (

the function

x\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\leq y)-{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(\widetilde{X}_{t}>x)

(28)

we obtain the following result:

Corollary 1
.

The transition probability densities of

and
\widetilde{X}

p^{\widetilde{X}}_{t}(x,y)=\partial_{y}{\mathbb{P}}_{y}(X_{t}>x)=\partial_{y}\int_{x}^{r}p^{X}_{t}(y,u){\textnormal{d}}u.

(29)

4
Darboux transform of killed Browian motion

We start with a diffusion process
Y
on the interval
(l,r)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, which is a Brownian motion killed at rate
c(y)
, where
c(y)
is a positive continuous function for
y\in(l,r)
. The infinitesimal generator is
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-c(y)
. We take the speed measure to be the Lebesgue measure
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)={\textnormal{d}}x
, in this case the scale function becomes
s(x)=2x
(see (
11
)). If
l=-\infty
, then
l
is a natural boundary. If
l
is finite, then the boundary classification conditions
R\in L_{1}((l,z))
and
Q\in L_{1}((l,z))
from
2
are equivalent to

R\in L_{1}((l,z))\Leftrightarrow\int_{l}^{z}c(y)(y-l){\textnormal{d}}y<\infty\;{\textnormal{ and }}\;Q\in L_{1}((l,z))\Leftrightarrow\int_{l}^{z}c(y){\textnormal{d}}y<\infty

Thus, if a boundary is an entrance, it must also be an exit, and we have the following three possibilities for boundary behavior of the process
Y
: natural, exit-not-entrance and non-singular (entrance-not-exit boundary is not possible for a killed Brownian motion). We will only consider killed Brownian motion processes that have one of elastic/reflecting/killing boundary conditions at each non-singular boundary point (see page
2
).

Next, we assume that for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have found a positive function
h(y)
that is
\lambda
-invariant for the process
Y
, that is

{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[h(Y_{t})]=e^{\lambda t}h(x),\;\;\;x\in(l,r),\;t>0.

(30)

This
\lambda
-invariant function
h
must satisfy the following properties:

(i)
h
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
on
(l,r)
;

(31)

(ii)
h
satisfies appropriate boundary conditions at each non-singular boundary point.

(32)

The first property follows from the fact that the process
\exp(-\lambda t)h(Y_{t})
is a martingale. To check the second property, we take
\mu>\max(0,\lambda)
, multiply both sides of (
30
) by
e^{-\mu t}
, integrate in
t\in(0,\infty)
and obtain

(\mu-\lambda)\int_{l}^{r}G^{Y}_{\mu}(x,y)h(y){\textnormal{d}}y=h(x),

(33)

where
G^{Y}_{\mu}(x,y)
is the Green function of the process
Y
. Thus, if
h
is bounded on
(l,r)
(which happens, for example, if both boundaries are non-singular), it must be in the domain of the infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}
(because it lies in the image of the resolvent operator). Therefore,
h
must satisfy the appropriate boundary conditions at each non-singular boundary (see
[
2
]
[Section II.7]). The proof of (
32
) in the general case, when
h
can be unbounded, is given in Appendix
B
.

In what follows, we will require one additional assumption on
h
:

{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}:=\sup\limits_{y\in(l,r)}\Big[c(y)-\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}\Big)^{2}\Big]<\infty.

(34)

Remark 1.

Note that
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}
is finite when
c(y)
is bounded on
(l,r)
. In all our examples in Section
5
, we actually have
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}=0
, though it is easy to construct examples with
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}
equal to any positive number (one could take Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}
killed at a positive constant rate and a

\lambda
-invariant function
h(y)=\cosh(y)
, see Section
5.1
). It could be true that
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}
is always finite, but we could not prove this or find a counterexample.

As the first step of constructing the Darboux transform of
Y
, we introduce a new Markov process
X
via Doob’s
h
-transform:

{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(X_{t}\in A)=\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{h(x)}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[{\mathbf{1}}_{\{Y_{t}\in A\}}h(Y_{t})].

(35)

The process
X
is a conservative Markov process, since
h
is
\lambda
-invariant for
Y
. Moreover, it is a diffusion process on
(l,r)
with the infinitesimal generator given by

{\mathcal{L}}_{X}=\frac{1}{h}{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h-\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}+\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}\partial_{y}.

(36)

The derivatives of the scale function and the speed measure of the process
X
are
s^{\prime}(x)=2h^{-2}(x)
and
m^{\prime}(x)=h^{2}(x)
(this follows from (
11
)), thus the functions
R
and
Q
in (
12
) are given by

R_{X}(x)=2h^{-2}(x)\int_{x}^{z}h^{2}(y){\textnormal{d}}y,\;\;\;Q_{X}(x)=2h^{2}(x)\int_{x}^{z}h^{-2}(y){\textnormal{d}}y.

(37)

The above two functions
R_{X}
and
Q_{X}
can be used to find the type of the boundary points
l
and
r
for the process
X
(see Section
2
).

For the rest of this paper we assume that if
l
or
r
is a non-singular boundary point for the process
X
, then it is a reflecting boundary.
The following proposition will be useful for our examples in Section
5
.

Proposition 1
.

If a non-singular boundary point of
Y
has a reflecting or elastic boundary condition, then this boundary point is also
non-singular for
X
with a reflecting boundary condition.

Proof.

The Green function of
X
(with respect to the speed measure
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)=h^{2}(x){\textnormal{d}}x
) is given by

G^{X}_{\mu}(x,y)=\frac{G^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x,y)}{h(x)h(y)},

where
\mu>\max(0,-\lambda)
and
x,y\in(l,r)
. Comparing the above formula and (
14
), we see that the fundamental increasing and decreasing solutions
\psi^{X}_{\mu}
and
\varphi^{X}_{\mu}
for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{X}
must be given by

\psi^{X}_{\mu}(x)=\frac{\psi^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x)}{h(x)},\;\;\;\varphi^{X}_{\mu}(x)=\frac{\varphi^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x)}{h(x)}.

Assume that
l
is a non-singular boundary point for
Y
with reflecting or elastic boundary condition, which can be written as
g^{\prime}(l+)=\gamma g(l+)
for some
\gamma\geq 0
(here we used the fact that
Y
is in the natural scale, so that
g^{+}(y)=g^{\prime}(y)
). The function
h
, being
\lambda
-invariant for
Y
, must satisfy equation
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
and the corresponding boundary condition at
l
. The fundamental solution
\psi^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x)
must satisfy the same boundary condition at
l
. From here, it is easy to check that

\lim\limits_{x\to l+}h^{2}(x)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}x}\psi^{X}_{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\lim\limits_{x\to l+}\Big[h(x)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}x}\psi^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x)-\psi^{Y}_{\lambda+\mu}(x)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}x}h(x)\Big]=0.

Thus, for
\mu>\max(0,-\lambda)
the function
f(x)=\psi^{X}_{\mu}(x)
satisfies
f^{+}(l+)=0
, which is a reflecting boundary condition. The values of the Green function
G^{X}_{\mu}(x,y)
for
\mu>\max(0,-\lambda)
uniquely determine the transition probability density of the process
X
(by the uniqueness of the Laplace transform), and we conclude that
l
is indeed a reflecting boundary for
X
.

When
r
is a non-singular boundary point for
Y
, the proof proceeds along the same lines, except that now we focus on the boundary behavior of the decreasing fundamental solution
\varphi^{X}_{\mu}(x)
.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

The process
X
thus constructed is a conservative diffusion process with reflecting boundary condition at every non-singular boundary point. We construct its Siegmund transform
\widetilde{X}
as described in Section

3

\widetilde{X}
is

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{X}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-\frac{h^{\prime}(y)}{h(y)}\partial_{y}.

(38)

The process
\widetilde{X}
can have only natural, exit-not-entrance, or non-singular killing boundaries. Now we want to turn it into a killed Brownian motion by “removing the drift term”. We achieve this through another Doob’s
h
-transform. First we establish that the function
h
is
\nu
-excessive for the process
\widetilde{X}
with
\nu={\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+\lambda
.

Proposition 2
.

For
x\in(l,r)
and
t>0
we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}[h(\widetilde{X}_{t})]\leq e^{({\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+\lambda)t}h(x)
.

Proof.

The process
\widetilde{X}
is a diffusion process solving the stochastic differential equation

{\textnormal{d}}\widetilde{X}_{t}=-\frac{h^{\prime}(\widetilde{X}_{t})}{h(\widetilde{X}_{t})}{\textnormal{d}}t+{\textnormal{d}}W_{t},\;\;\;t<\zeta,

where
\zeta
is the first time the process reaches the boundary of
(l,r)
. When both boundaries are natural, we have
\zeta=+\infty
, and if one of the boundaries is exit-not-entrance or non-singular killing boundary, then the process is killed when it hits that boundary (in other words, the process at time
\zeta
is sent to a cemetery state
\widetilde{X}_{\zeta}=\Delta
).
We apply Ito’s formula to
\ln h(\widetilde{X}_{t})
:

\ln h(\widetilde{X}_{t})=\ln h(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{h^{\prime}(\widetilde{X}_{s})}{h(\widetilde{X}_{s})}{\textnormal{d}}\widetilde{X}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}v(\widetilde{X}_{s}){\textnormal{d}}s,\;\;\;t<\zeta,

(39)

where

v(x):=\frac{{\textnormal{d}}^{2}}{{\textnormal{d}}x^{2}}\ln h(x)=\frac{h^{\prime\prime}(x)}{h(x)}-\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(x)}{h(x)}\Big)^{2}=2(c(x)+\lambda)-\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(x)}{h(x)}\Big)^{2}.

In the last step we used the fact that
h
is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
.
Let us denote

U_{t}:=\frac{h^{\prime}(\widetilde{X}_{t})}{h(\widetilde{X}_{t})},\;\;\;Z_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}U_{s}\;{\textnormal{d}}W_{s},\;\;\;t<\zeta.

After rearranging the terms in (
39

h(\widetilde{X}_{t})=h(\Delta)=0
for
t\geq\zeta

h(\widetilde{X}_{t})\leq h(x)\times e^{Z_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\langle Z\rangle_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(v(\widetilde{X}_{s})-U_{s}^{2}){\textnormal{d}}s},

(40)

which is now valid for all
t\geq 0
.

Next, we observe the following two facts. The process
\exp\big(Z_{t}-(1/2)\langle Z\rangle_{t}\big)
is a positive local martingale; thus, it is a supermartingale, and its expected value is less than one. We also have an upper bound

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(v(\widetilde{X}_{s})-U_{s}^{2}){\textnormal{d}}s=\int_{0}^{t}\Big[c(\widetilde{X}_{s})+\lambda-\Big(\frac{h^{\prime}(\widetilde{X}_{s})}{h(\widetilde{X}_{s})}\Big)^{2}\Big]{\textnormal{d}}s\leq({\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+\lambda)t,

which follows from (
34

40
) gives us the desired result.

Next, we present our main result in this section, which connects transition probability densities of the process
Y
and its Darboux transform
\widetilde{Y}

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the two conditions (
44
) and
(

\widetilde{Y}
be the Darboux transform of

. Then

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=e^{-({\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+2\lambda)t}\frac{h(x)}{h(y)}\partial_{x}\Big[\frac{1}{h(x)}\int_{y}^{r}p^{Y}_{t}(x,u)h(u){\textnormal{d}}u\Big].

(46)

Proof.

Since
X
(respectively,
\widetilde{Y}
) is the Doob’s
h
-transform of
Y
(respectively,
\widetilde{X}
), their transition probability densities satisfy

\displaystyle p^{X}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{h(x)}p_{t}^{Y}(x,y)h(y),\;\;\;p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{e^{-({\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+\lambda)t}}{h(x)}p_{t}^{\widetilde{X}}(x,y)h(y).

The transition probability densities of
\widetilde{X}
and
X
are related via (
29
). Combining these identities and using the symmetry of
p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x,y)
with respect to
x
and

, we obtain (
46
).

Next, we present an example demonstrating the importance of our assumption (
44
) of
\lambda
-invariance of
h
. As we discussed in the introduction, to define the Darboux transform of operator
{\mathcal{L}}
we need a positive function
h
that satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
. If
h
is
\lambda
-invariant for the process
Y
, that is
{\mathbb{E}}_{y}[h(Y_{t})]=\exp(\lambda t)h(y)
, then
h
necessarily satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
. However, the converse is not true. For example, consider a process
Y
, which is a Brownian motion on
(0,\infty)
reflected at zero. The transition probability of
Y
(with respect to Lebesgue measure) is

p_{t}^{Y}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big(e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}+e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y+x)^{2}}\Big),\;\;\;t,x,y>0.

The infinitesimal generator is
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}
. The point
l=0
is a non-singular boundary and we have a boundary condition
f^{\prime}(0+)=0
. Consider a function
h(y)=y
. This function is a solution to
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=0
, but it is not an invariant function for
Y
, since
h
does not satisfy the reflecting boundary condition at zero. If we take the above expression for
p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)
and compute the expression in the right-hand side of (

), we would obtain

\frac{h(x)}{h(y)}\partial_{x}\Big[\frac{1}{h(x)}\int_{y}^{r}p^{Y}_{t}(x,u)h(u){\textnormal{d}}u\Big]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big[e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{t}{xy}\Big)-e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y+x)^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{t}{xy}\Big)\Big].

The expression in the right-hand side is negative for small values of
y
, thus it can not be a transition probability density. This example confirms the necessity of assumption (
44
).

Finally, we would like to point out that the second step in our construction of the Darboux transformed process (see Table
1
) is closely related to the concept of Krein dual strings, see
[
4
,
16
]
. To demonstrate this connection, we introduce two increasing functions
u(x)
and
v(x)
via

u^{\prime}(x)=h^{2}(x)
and
v^{\prime}(x)=2h^{-2}(x)

and define diffusion processes
V_{t}=v(X_{t})
and

. These are processes in natural scale (the functions
v
and
u
are scale functions for
X
and
\widetilde{X}
), and their infinitesimal generators can be written as follows:

{\mathcal{L}}_{U}=\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}M(u)}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}u},\;\;\;{\mathcal{L}}_{V}=\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}m(v)}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}}{{\textnormal{d}}v},

where

and
m
are speed measures of
U
and
V
(see (

\frac{{\textnormal{d}}m}{{\textnormal{d}}v}=\frac{1}{2}h^{4}(x(v)),\;\;\;\frac{{\textnormal{d}}M}{{\textnormal{d}}u}=2h^{-4}(x(u)).

Here
x(u)
and
x(v)
are the inverse functions of
u(x)
and
v(x)
. One can check that

\frac{{\textnormal{d}}M}{{\textnormal{d}}m}\frac{{\textnormal{d}}m}{{\textnormal{d}}v}=1,

which means that
m(v)
is the inverse function of
M(u)
. This shows that

is the Krein dual string of

, in the terminology of
[
4

16
]

is closely related to Krein duality.

5
Examples

In this section we present five examples of Darboux transformed processes. In the first four examples, we take the process
Y
to be the Brownian motion on an interval
(l,r)
with various boundary conditions, and our last example is related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. For all killed Brownian motion processes in this section we take the speed measure
{\textnormal{d}}m(x)={\textnormal{d}}x
, so that the transition probability density and the Green function for each process are given with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Our goal in this section is to compute the transition probability density of the transformed process
\widetilde{Y}
and (in most cases) to provide its spectral expansion. Computing
p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x,y)
via (
46
)
is very time-consuming if done by hand. Instead, we obtained all expressions for
p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x,y)
in this section using symbolic computations, and then we verified numerically that our formulas were correct. The Matlab programs for verifying these formulas via symbolic computations can be found at
kuznetsov.mathstats.yorku.ca/code/
.

Some computations in this section will require the following simple result (which is probably well known, but we could not find it in the literature in this exact form).

Lemma 1
.

Consider a second order linear differential operator
{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}-c(y)
, where
c
is a continuous function of
y\in(l,r)
. Assume that
h
,
f
and
g
are
C^{2}
functions on
(l,r)
such that

(i)

h
is positive and satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
for
y\in(l,r)
;

(ii)

g
satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}g=\mu g
for
y\in(l,r)
.

Denote
\tilde{f}={\mathcal{D}}_{h}f
and
\tilde{g}=\mathcal{D}_{h}g
. Then

\int\tilde{f}(y)\tilde{g}(y){\textnormal{d}}y=f(y)\tilde{g}(y)+2(\lambda-\mu)\int f(y)g(y){\textnormal{d}}y.

(47)

If
f
also satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}f=\mu f
for
y\in(l,r)
, then

{\textnormal{Wr}}[\tilde{f},\tilde{g}]=2(\lambda-\mu){\textnormal{Wr}}[f,g].

(48)

The proof of (
48
) is obtained by calculating
{\textnormal{Wr}}[\tilde{f},\tilde{g}]
and using the fact that
g^{\prime\prime}=2(c+\mu)g
and
h^{\prime\prime}=2(c+\lambda)h
. Formula (
47
) is derived in a similar way, first by taking derivative in
y
of both sides and then simplifying the result using the above identities for
g^{\prime\prime}
and
h^{\prime\prime}
.

We also record here the following useful fact (which follows from the intertwining relation (
7
) and our construction of Darboux transformed process
\widetilde{Y}
):

\;{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}f=(\mu+{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}+2\lambda)f\;\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\;{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{Y}}({\mathcal{D}}_{h}f)=\mu({\mathcal{D}}_{h}f).

(49)

5.1
Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}

We take
Y
to be a standard Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}
. In this case we have
l=-\infty
,
r=+\infty
, both boundaries are natural and the transition probability density of
Y
is

p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}},t>0,\;\;x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The infinitesimal generator is
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}
, thus the killing term
c(x)
is zero.
We set
\lambda=1/2
and take
h(x)=\cosh(x)
. Note that
h
solves the equation
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
. To check that
h
is indeed
\lambda
-invariant for
Y
, we verify that

\int^{r}_{l}p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)h(y){\textnormal{d}}y=e^{\lambda t}h(x).

(50)

The integral in the left-hand side can be easily computed explicitly. We also provide the Matlab code for symbolic verification of this result. Another way to prove that
h
is
\lambda
-invariant is via
[
8
]
[Theorem 2.7] or
[
19
]
[Corollary 2.2].

Next, we verify that condition (
44
) holds with
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}=0
and condition (
45
) is also satisfied since both boundaries are natural for the process
X
with the generator given in (
36
). Thus, we can construct the Darboux transformed process
\widetilde{Y}
and identify it as a Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}
killed at rate
\tilde{c}(y)=\tanh(y)^{2}
(this latter expression follows from (
43
)).
The transition probability density of
\widetilde{Y}
is given by

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-t-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}+\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{2}}}{2\cosh(x)\cosh(y)}\Big[\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x+t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x-t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)\Big],

(51)

where
\Phi(x)
is the CDF of standard normal distribution. Formula (
51
) was obtained from
(
46
) by symbolic computation (and then verified numerically).

Now we turn our attention to the Green function of
\widetilde{Y}
. The fundamental increasing/decreasing solutions of
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}f=\mu f
(with
\mu>0
) are
\psi_{\mu}^{Y}(y)=\exp(\sqrt{2\mu}y)
and

\varphi_{\mu}^{Y}(y)=\exp(-\sqrt{2\mu}y)
. Let us denote

F(z,y)={\mathcal{D}}_{h}e^{zy}=e^{zy}(z-\tanh(y)).

(52)

The functions

\psi_{\mu}^{\widetilde{Y}}(y):=F(\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},y),\;\;\;\varphi_{\mu}^{\widetilde{Y}}(y):=F(-\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},y)

are the increasing/decreasing fundamental solutions to
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{Y}}f=\mu f
(this follows from (
49
)). With the help of
(
48
) we compute
{\textnormal{Wr}}[\varphi_{\mu}^{\widetilde{Y}};\psi_{\mu}^{\widetilde{Y}}]=-2(1+2\mu)\sqrt{2(1+\mu)}
and using (
14
) we find the Green function of
\widetilde{Y}
in the form

G^{\widetilde{Y}}_{\mu}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+\mu)}(1+2\mu)}\times\begin{cases}F(\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},x)F(-\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},y),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;x<y,\\
F(-\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},x)F(\sqrt{2(1+\mu)},y),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;y<x.\end{cases}

(53)

We claim that the transition probability density of
\widetilde{Y}
can be written in the following spectral representation form:

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{e^{-\frac{t}{2}}}{2\cosh(x)\cosh(y)}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-(1+\frac{z^{2}}{2})t}F({\textnormal{i}}z,x)F(-{\textnormal{i}}z,y)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}z}{1+z^{2}}.

(54)

There are three ways how one can obtain this result. The first method is to use general Sturm-Liouville theory (see Section 2.2 in
[
24
]
). The second is to write the transition probability density as the inverse Laplace transform of the Green function in
(
53
)

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{c+{\textnormal{i}}{\mathbb{R}}}G^{\widetilde{Y}}_{\mu}(x,y)e^{\mu t}{\textnormal{d}}\mu,\;\;\;c>0,

and transform the contour of integration to a Hankel-type contour, which goes around the interval
(-\infty,-1]
in the counterclockwise direction (starting at
-\infty
). While transforming this contour of integration we will collect a residue at
\mu=-1/2
, which will give us the first term in (
54
), and the integral over Hankel’s contour will give the integral term in (
54
). The third method is probably the simplest (though the least enlightening): we compute the integral in the right-hand side of (
54
) and transform the resulting expression into the form
(
51
). More details on this last method are provided in Appendix
A
.

The formula (
54
) shows that the effect of Darboux transformation on the spectral representation of the transition semigroup of the diffusion process
Y
is that we have shifted the spectrum by
-1
and inserted a new eigenvalue at
-1/2
(with the corresponding eigenfunction
1/h(x)=1/\cosh(x)
).

(0,\infty)

Now we take

We check that condition (
44

{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}=0
and that condition (
45

X
with the generator given in (
36
). The process
\widetilde{Y}
(the Darboux transform of
Y
) is a killed Brownian motion on
(0,\infty)
with the killing rate
\tilde{c}(y)=\coth^{2}(y)
. Both boundaries
0
and
+\infty
are natural for
\widetilde{Y}

\widetilde{Y}

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big(e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}+e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y+x)^{2}}\Big)

(55)

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{-t/2}}{2\sinh(x)\sinh(y)}\Big[\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x-t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)+\Phi\Big(\frac{y+x+t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{y+x-t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x+t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)\Big].

The fundamental increasing/decreasing solutions to
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{Y}}f=\mu f
are

\psi^{\widetilde{Y}}_{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{z^{2}-1}\Big(z\cosh(zx)-\sinh(zx)\coth(x)\Big),\;\;\;\varphi^{\widetilde{Y}}_{\mu}(x)=e^{-zx}(z+\coth(x)),

where we denoted
z=\sqrt{2(\mu+1)}
. The Green function for the process
\tilde{Y}
is

G^{\widetilde{Y}}_{\mu}(x,y)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2(\mu+1)}}\times\begin{cases}\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{\mu}(x)\tilde{\varphi}^{-}_{\mu}(y),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;x<y,\\
\tilde{\varphi}^{-}_{\mu}(x)\tilde{\psi}^{+}_{\mu}(y),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;y<x.\end{cases}

The spectral representation for
\tilde{p}^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)
is given by

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(1+\frac{z^{2}}{2})t}f(z,x)f(z,y)\frac{{\textnormal{d}}z}{1+z^{2}},

(56)

where we denoted
f(z,y):=z\cos(zx)-\sin(zx)\coth(x)
. In this case the Darboux transformation has resulted in shifting the spectrum by
-1
, but no new eigenvalues are created. Intuitively this can be explained by noting that the function
1/h(x)=1/\sinh(x)
(which would be the candidate eigenfunction with the eigenvalue
-1/2
for the transition semigroup of
\tilde{Y}

5.3
Brownian motion on
(0,\infty)
, killed elastically at

Next, we consider the example when
Y
is a Brownian motion on
(0,\infty)
killed elastically at zero,
see page 125 in
[

{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{y}^{2}
, the killing term
c(x)
is zero. The point
l=0
is a non-singular boundary for
Y
and we have an elastic boundary condition
f^{\prime}(0^{+})=\gamma f(0+)
, where
\gamma>0
. The transition probability is

p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big[e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}+e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y+x)^{2}}\Big]-2\gamma e^{\gamma(x+y)+\frac{\gamma^{2}t}{2}}\Phi\Big(-\frac{x+y+\gamma t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big).

We set
\lambda=1/2
and solving the equation
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}h=\lambda h
with the boundary condition
h^{\prime}(0^{+})=\gamma f(0+)
we find a solution

h(y)=e^{y}+\beta e^{-y},

(57)

where
\beta:=(1-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)
and then we check that
h
is indeed
\lambda
-invariant for
Y
by verifying that (
50

Condition (
44
) holds with
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}=0

45

1

Y
is a process
\widetilde{Y}

(0,\infty)

\tilde{c}(y)=(h^{\prime}(y)/h(y))^{2}

l=0
is a non-singular boundary for
\widetilde{Y}
, and we have a killing bondary condition at this point. In order to simplify the expression for
\tilde{c}
, we introduce a positive parameter
\alpha

|\beta|=\exp(-2\alpha)
and with the help of this parameter we can express
h
in the following form:
h(y)=2e^{-\alpha}\cosh(y+\alpha)
if
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
h(y)=\exp(y)
if
\gamma=1
and
h(y)=2e^{-\alpha}\sinh(y+\alpha)
if
\gamma>1
. Thus, the killing rate of
\widetilde{Y}
is given by

\tilde{c}(y)=\begin{cases}\tanh^{2}(y+\alpha),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;\gamma\in(0,1),\\
1,\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;\gamma=1,\\
\coth^{2}(y+\alpha),\;\;&{\textnormal{ if }}\;\gamma>1.\end{cases}

The transition probability density of
\widetilde{Y}
is

\displaystyle p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big[e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y-x)^{2}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2t}(y+x)^{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle-\frac{8\gamma\beta}{h(x)h(y)}e^{\gamma(x+y)+(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-1)t}\sinh(x)\sinh(y)\Phi\Big(-\frac{y+x+\gamma t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)

(58)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\beta e^{-\frac{t}{2}}}{h(x)h(y)}\Big[\Phi\Big(\frac{y+x-t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)+\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x+t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{y+x+t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{y-x-t}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)\Big],

where

is given by (
57

(0,\infty)
killed at rate one and killed at the first time it hits zero. The simplicity of this result suggests that it may have a pathwise explanation, but we were not able to find it.

For the process
\tilde{Y}
in this section, we did not derive the spectral representation of the semigroup, as the resulting expressions were rather complicated.

Corollary 2

Let

Using the representation of
\widetilde{Y}

\int_{0}^{y-\alpha}p_{t}^{\widetilde{Y}}(x-\alpha,u){\textnormal{d}}u={\mathbb{P}}_{x-\alpha}(\widetilde{Y}_{t}\leq y-\alpha)={\mathbb{E}}_{x-\alpha}\Big[e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\kappa(B_{s}+\alpha){\textnormal{d}}s}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{B_{t}\leq y-\alpha,\min\limits_{0\leq s\leq t}B_{s}>0\}}],

where

and use translation invariance of Brownian motion.

\sqcap\kern-8.0pt\hbox{$\sqcup$}

Taking derivatives with respect to

and of its running minimum. This extends the results of Sections

constructed in those sections.

Remark 2.
As discussed in the introduction, the Darboux transformation of second order linear differential operators is invertible. Starting with an operator
{\mathcal{L}}
and a positive formal eigenfunction
h
such that
{\mathcal{L}}h=\lambda h
, we obtain Darboux transformed operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, for which
1/h
is also a formal eigenfunction. Applying Darboux transformation to
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
with the seed function
1/h
, we recover the original operator
{\mathcal{L}}
. However, this inversion does not work on the level of transition semigroups of diffusion processes. Consider the process
Y
from this section and the function
h
given by (
57
). The function
f=1/h
is positive and satisfies the equation
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{Y}}f=\lambda f
(with
\lambda=1/2
), but it is not a
\lambda
-invariant function for
\widetilde{Y}
, since it does not satisfy the boundary condition at
0
(recall that the process
\widetilde{Y}
has a killing boundary condition at zero).

5.4
Brownian motion on
(0,1)
, killed at
0
or
1

Let
Y
be a Brownian motion on
(0,1)
killed at both boundaries, see page 122 in
[
2
]
. The transition probability density is

p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)=2\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}n^{2}t}\sin(\pi nx)\sin(\pi ny).

The above formula can be seen as a spectral representation of the transition density of
Y
in
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
. Using orthogonality of
\{\sin(n\pi y)\}_{n\geq 1}
on the interval
(0,1)
, it is easy to check that
h(y)=\sin(\pi y)
is
\lambda
-invariant function for
Y
with
\lambda=-\pi^{2}/2
. We check that condition (
44
) holds with
{\mathfrak{m}}_{h}=0
and condition (
45
) is also satisfied, since both boundaries are natural for the process
X
with the generator given in (
36
). The Darboux transformed process
\widetilde{Y}
is a Brownian motion on
(0,1)
, killed at rate

\tilde{c}(x)=\pi^{2}\cot^{2}(\pi x)
. Both boundaries are natural for
\widetilde{Y}
. The transition probability density is obtained using (
46
) and has the form

p^{\widetilde{Y}}_{t}(x,y)=2\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n^{2}-2)t}}{n^{2}-1}f_{n}(x)f_{n}(y),

(60)

where we denoted

f_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\pi}{\mathcal{D}}_{h}\sin(n\pi x)=n\cos(\pi nx)-\sin(\pi nx)\cot(\pi x).

The functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
are orthogonal in
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
:

\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}(x)f_{m}(x){\textnormal{d}}x=\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}-1)\delta_{n,m},\;\;\;n,m\geq 2.

This result follows by applying (
47
) with
f=\sin(n\pi x)
and
g=\sin(m\pi x)
. Thus formula (
60
) gives a spectral representation of the transition probability density of
\widetilde{Y}
in
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
. In this case, the Darboux transformation removes the first eigenvalue from the spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}
and shifts all eigenvalues by
-2\lambda
.

Now we can repeat this construction. We note that
f_{2}(x)=-2\sin^{2}(\pi x)
, thus
h_{1}(y)=-f_{2}(y)=2\sin^{2}(\pi y)
is a positive
\lambda
-invariant function for
\widetilde{Y}
, with
\lambda=-\pi^{2}
. Applying Darboux transformation to
\widetilde{Y}
with this
\lambda
-invariant function
\tilde{h}
, we obtain a new diffusion process, which we denote by
Y^{(2)}
. This process is a Brownian motion on
(0,1)
killed at rate

c^{(2)}(x)=3\pi^{2}\cot^{2}(\pi x)
and having transition probability density

p^{(2)}_{t}(x,y)=2\sum\limits_{n\geq 3}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n^{2}-6)t}}{(n^{2}-1)(n^{2}-4)}f^{(2)}_{n}(x)f^{(2)}_{n}(y),

(61)

where

f^{(2)}_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{\pi}{\mathcal{D}}_{h_{1}}f_{n}(x)=-(2+n^{2})\sin(\pi nx)+3\sin(\pi nx)\csc^{2}(\pi x)-3n\cos(\pi nx)\cot(\pi x).

The formula (
61
) again gives us a spectral representation of the transition probability density of the process
Y^{(2)}
in
L_{2}((0,1),{\textnormal{d}}x)
.

This process can be repeated indefinitely. The first eigenfunction of the transition semigroup must have a constant sign on
(0,1)
, thus we can take
\lambda
-invariant function
h
to equal to this first eigenfunction or its negative. After performing Darboux transform
m
times, we will obtain a process
Y^{(m)}
, which is a Brownian motion on
(0,1)
, killed at rate
c^{(2)}(x)=\frac{1}{2}m(m+1)\pi^{2}\cot^{2}(\pi x)
. The transition probability can be written in the following form

p^{(m)}_{t}(x,y)=2\sum\limits_{n\geq m+1}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\pi^{2}(n^{2}-m(m+1))t}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}(n^{2}-j^{2})}f^{(m)}_{n}(x)f^{(m)}_{n}(y),

(62)

where the functions
f^{(m)}_{n}
can be given explicitly in terms of Gegenbauer polynomials and also in terms of certain Wronskian determinants. The proof of (
62
) will appear in the forthcoming paper
[
26
]
.

5.5
Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}
killed at rate
y^{2}/2

Let
Y
be a Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}
killed at rate
c(y)=y^{2}/2
. It is known that its transition probability density function is given by
Mehler’s kernel

\displaystyle p^{Y}_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sinh(t)}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\coth(t)(x^{2}+y^{2})+\frac{xy}{\sinh(t)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})}\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}\frac{e^{-(n+\frac{1}{2})t}}{2^{n}n!}H_{n}(x)H_{n}(y).

Here
H_{n}(x)
denote Hermite polynomials.
The process
Y
is closely related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
Z
, which has infinitesimal generator
{\mathcal{L}}_{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}^{2}-z\partial_{z}
.
It is known (and easy to check) that
Y
is Doob’s
h
-transform of
Z
:

{\mathbb{P}}_{z}(Y_{t}\in A)=e^{-\frac{t}{2}-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{z}\Big[e^{\frac{1}{2}Z_{t}^{2}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{Z_{t}\in A\}}\Big].

Next, we will discuss how to find a
\lambda
-invariant function for the process
Y
. It is known (see Example 2.3 in
[
12
]
) that the functions
f_{n}(y)=e^{-y^{2}/2}H_{n}(y)
and
g_{n}(y)={\textnormal{i}}^{n}e^{y^{2}/2}H_{n}({\textnormal{i}}y)
are formal eigenfunctions of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{y}^{2}-y^{2})
. Hermite polynomials
H_{n}(y)
have real zeros if
n\geq 1
and the functions
H_{n}({\textnormal{i}}y)
have a zero at
y=0
when
n
is odd, so these are not good candidates for
a positive
\lambda
-invariant function and we should take
h
as a multiple of
f_{0}
or
g_{2n}
. Taking
h(y)=\exp(\pm y^{2}/2)
does not lead to a new process, as
\tilde{c}(y)
given by (
43
) is equal to
c(y)
up to a constant. Thus, the first non-trivial example can be obtained when we take
