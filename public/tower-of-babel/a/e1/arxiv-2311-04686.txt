Robust and Communication-Efficient Federated Domain Adaptation via Random Features
Zhanbo Feng 1, Yuanjie Wang 1, Jie Li, Fan Yang, Jiong Lou, Tiebin Mi, Robert. C. Qiu, Zhenyu Liao 2

Abstract
Modern machine learning (ML) models have grown to a scale where training them on a single machine becomes impractical. As a result, there is a growing trend to leverage federated learning (FL) techniques to train large ML models in a distributed and collaborative manner. These models, however, when deployed on new devices, might struggle to generalize well due to domain shifts. In this context, federated domain adaptation (FDA) emerges as a powerful approach to address this challenge. Most existing FDA approaches typically focus on aligning the distributions between source and target domains by minimizing their (e.g., MMD) distance. Such strategies, however, inevitably introduce high communication overheads and can be highly sensitive to network reliability. In this paper, we introduce RF-TCA, an enhancement to the standard Transfer Component Analysis approach that significantly accelerates computation without compromising theoretical and empirical performance. Leveraging the computational advantage of RF-TCA, we further extend it to FDA setting with FedRF-TCA. The proposed FedRF-TCA protocol boasts communication complexity that is independent of the sample size, while maintaining performance that is either comparable to or even surpasses state-of-the-art FDA methods. We present extensive experiments to showcase the superior performance and robustness (to network condition) of FedRF-TCA.

II-A
2
Transfer learning and domain adaptation

Transfer learning, and in particular, domain adaptation, aims to “align” the distribution of source and target data representations in a common feature space.
Different feature alignment strategies have been explored in the literature:
TCA
[
30
]
and JDA
[
31

propose to minimize the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) distance between the source and target marginal/joint distributions.
GFK
[
32
]
performs subspace feature alignment by exploiting the intrinsic low-dimensional structures of the data.
CORAL
[
33
]
proposes to align data representations via their second-order statistics.
DAN
[
34
]
, DaNN
[
15
]
, and DDC
[
35
]
leverage the strong expressive power of deep neural networks to align source and target representations in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, based on MMD principle
[
12
,
13
]
.
In contrast, DANN
[
36
]
addresses the domain shift through adversarial learning, upon which CDAN
[
37
]
introduces conditioning strategies to further improve the performance.

II-A
3
Federated learning and federated domain adaptation

Federated learning (FL) is a decentralized ML paradigm that facilitates model training on local data while preserving privacy through the sharing of model updates across a network of devices.
Notwithstanding the remarkable progress achieved in the field of FL
[
9
,
38
,
39
]
, the resulting trained models often falter in generalizing to data at novel devices, primarily due to
domain shift
, where the feature distribution in target domain diverges from that in the source domain.
To address this challenge, federated domain adaptation (FDA) techniques have been proposed to mitigate domain shift by aligning the feature distributions of both source and target domains.
While many FDA methods have demonstrated considerable empirical success, they often come at the cost of prohibitively high computational and communicational overheads
[
14
,
40
]
.
To alleviate the computational and communicational challenges inherent associated with FDA, recent work
[
16
,
41
]
propose to compute MMD distance using a less resource-intensive feature extractor, thereby significantly reducing both computational and communicational overheads.

[
41
]
take this optimization a step further by introducing an approximate version of the original MMD loss, resulting in even more pronounced reductions in communication costs.

In this work, we address the issue of domain shift challenge within FL and introduce the FedRF-TCA protocol.
The proposed FedRF-TCA approach enjoys a much lower (in fact a data-size-
independent
) communication complexity compared to existing FDA methods, and is, in addition, robust to network reliability and possible malicious (source) clients, all while delivering commendable performance.

II-B

Maximum Mean Discrepancy Principle and Transfer Component Analysis

The maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) was first introduced as a pivotal test statistic in the seminal works of
[
12
,
13
]

It serves as a tool to determine whether data points are drawn i.i.d. from the same underlying distribution, via an evaluation of their features in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).
It quickly gains popularity as the preferred objective function for the purpose of “aligning” different feature distributions, from different domains, of particular interest to DA
[
42
]

This further gave rise to numerous highly efficient transfer learning methods
[

[

TCA proposes to align the distributions of source

(1)

II-C

Federated Averaging

Model aggregation is a key enabler in federated learning (FL) that allows for collaborative training of large-scale ML models by, e.g., combining the model parameters from different clients.
In this respect, FedSGD and federated averaging (FedAvg)
[
10
]
allows users to collaboratively benefit from shared models trained on diverse data without central storage.
FedMA
[
46
]
, on the other hand, proposes to construct a shared ML model (e.g., a deep neural network) in a layer-by-layer manner by “matching” and averaging the hidden elements.

In the proposed FedRF-TCA protocol, we utilize federated averaging to update the model parameters in both the classifier
{\mathbf{C}}
and the linear adaptive layer
{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}
in
Figure

1
, due to its simplicity and low communication cost.
Federated averaging (FedAvg) was introduced in
[
10
]
to train a ML model in a decentralized way by iteratively aggregating locally computed updates.
Consider a FL scenario where the training data are available at
K
clients, the FedAvg protocol proposes, in each iteration, to first locally update the model parameter at each client and then send the updated parameter to a central server.
The server then aggregates the model parameters
{\mathbf{W}}_{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
from the selected clients. FedAvg allows one to collectively reap the benefits of shared models trained on a large number of data partitioned over
K
clients, without centrally storing these data in a server.

II-D

Federated Domain Adaptation

Federated Domain Adaptation (FDA) aims to resolve the non-i.i.d. and domain shift issues of data in a FL context
[
14
,
41
]
.
Due to the different origin of datasets at different clients in a FL system, there may exist domain shift between them
[
47
]
.
FDA proposes to address this domain shift issue by transferring knowledge from the source clients to a novel target client, in an unsupervised fashion, as follows.

Definition 1

(Unsupervised Federated Domain Adaptation, UFDA)
.

Consider a federated learning system having
K
clients.
Denote
\mathcal{D}_{S}^{(i)},i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
the source domains at these
K
clients, in the sense that the data and label pairs satisfy
({\mathbf{X}}_{S}^{(i)},{\mathbf{Y}}_{S}^{(i)})\sim\mathcal{D}_{S}^{(i)}
.
The problem of Unsupervised Federated Domain Adaptation (UFDA) aims to decide on (e.g., the label
{\mathbf{Y}}_{T}
of) the unlabelled data
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
at a novel target client in the system from a different domain
({\mathbf{X}}_{T},{\mathbf{Y}}_{T})\sim\mathcal{D}_{T}
, via federated information exchange in the system.

III
A Random Features Approach to TCA

In this section, we propose RF-TCA, a random features-based computationally efficient approach to TCA.
Under standard assumptions, we demonstrate in
1
that RF-TCA substantially mitigates the computational overhead associated with vanilla TCA, while exhibiting (virtually) no degradation in transfer learning performance compared to vanilla TCA.

III-A

RF-TCA: A Random Features Approach to Efficient TCA

Here, we present RF-TCA, a random features-based approach to computationally efficient TCA, by focusing on random Fourier features (RFFs) and Gaussian kernel.
We first recall the definition of RFFs as follows.

Definition 2

(Random Fourier features for Gaussian kernels,
[
19
]
)
.

For data matrix
{\mathbf{X}}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
of size
n
, the random Fourier feature (RFF) matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times n}
of
{\mathbf{X}}
is given by
1
1

1

Extensions of RFFs to other shift-invariant kernels (such as the Laplacian and Cauchy kernels) also exists, see
[
19
]
.

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{X}})\\
\sin(\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{X}})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times n},

(5)

with
N
the number of random features,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
a random matrix having i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries with mean zero and variance
1/\sigma^{2}

[\boldsymbol{\Omega}]_{ij}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1/\sigma^{2})
, and nonlinear functions
\cos(\cdot),\sin(\cdot)
applied entry-wise on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{X}}
.

Denote
\dim({\mathbf{K}})\equiv\tr{\mathbf{K}}/\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2}

{\mathbf{K}}={\mathbf{K}}_{\Gauss}
, it is known, e.g., per
2

N=O(\dim({\mathbf{K}})\log(n))
RFFs suffices to well approximate the Gaussian kernel matrix
{\mathbf{K}}
in a spectral norm sense
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\approx{\mathbf{K}}
.
This allows one to obtain an
effective low-rank approximation
of

, and can be further exploited to propose RF-TCA that significantly reduces the computational burden of vanilla TCA.

Precisely, following the idea of vanilla TCA in (
TCA

{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times m}
that “projects” the RFFs

onto an

Such projection
{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times m}}

\displaystyle\quad\tr({\mathbf{W}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}})+\gamma\tr({\mathbf{W}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}})

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbf{W}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}}={\mathbf{I}}_{m},

(RF-TCA)

with regularization
\gamma

{\mathbf{H}}

Assume
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}

\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\equiv(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times m}

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\quad\tr\left(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\sf T}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{L}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}+\gamma{\mathbf{I}}_{2N})(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\sf T}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}={\mathbf{I}}_{m},

(6)

IV
Robust and Communication-efficient Federated Domain Adaptation via RF-TCA

The majority, if not all, of the DA methods based on MMD, while known for their effectiveness in aligning feature distributions between source and target data, impose a substantial computational burden.
This computational complexity primarily arises from the necessity of operations involving the
large
kernel matrices of both source and target data, as highlighted in prior works
[
34
,
35
]
.
This makes them unsuitable for use in an FL context, for which low communication and computational costs are desired
[
16
]
.

In response to this challenge, we propose Federated Random Features-based Transfer Component Analysis (FedRF-TCA), a novel FDA approach designed to alleviate the computational and communicational demands and enhance privacy protection.
FedRF-TCA achieves this by employing compression and randomization techniques during the (asynchronous) training procedure, leveraging the RF-TCA methodology introduced in
Section

III
, to optimize information exchange within the MMD-based FDA scheme.
In comparison to existing MMD-based FDA protocols, FedRF-TCA offers significant reductions in both communication and computational overheads while maintaining commendable performance.

Specifically, we are primarily focused on a federated multi-source classification task, which involves multiple source clients coexisting alongside one target client.
Each source client is characterized by a distinct domain, comprising its own dataset and corresponding labels.
In contrast, the target client solely possesses data without accompanying labels.
The overarching goal of FDA is to train a model that can effectively classify the target data while also addressing concerns such as data leakage.

The FedRF-TCA protocol comprises a structured framework for each client, encompassing three distinct local components, as illustrated in
Figure

1
:

(i)

Feature Extractor
: For each source and target domain, an dedicated local feature extractor is trained by fine-tuning a pretrained model, such as ResNet-50.
These feature extractors are denoted as
{\mathbf{G}}_{S}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{G}}_{S}^{(K)}
for the
K
source domains
\mathcal{D}_{S}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{S}^{(K)}
, and
{\mathbf{G}}_{T}
for the target domain
\mathcal{D}_{T}
in
Figure

1
.
This training process yields local and “unaligned” source features
{\mathbf{X}}_{S}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n_{S}^{(1)}},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{S}^{(K)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n_{S}^{(K)}}
and target features
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n_{T}}
, respectively.

(ii)

RF-TCA Transfer Module
: In this module, the random feature map in
2
is applied on
{\mathbf{X}}_{S}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{S}^{(K)}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
to intermediate representations
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S}^{(K)}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{T}
.
Subsequently, an
adaptive
linear layer
{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}
is applied to these RFFs, aligning the source and target feature distributions, by minimizing their MMD distance.

(iii)

Classifier
: Finally, source classifiers
{\mathbf{C}}_{S}^{(i)}
are trained on the “aligned” source features from RF-TCA module, and their outputs are aggregated to obtain the target classifier, serving the desired classification purpose.

In the remainder of section, we discuss in
Section

IV-A
how, for a given pair of source and target domain
(\mathcal{D}_{S}^{(i)},\mathcal{D}_{T})
, features are extracted from data in distinct domains, and “transferred” into a common domain via random feature maps and a trainable linear layer

;
and then in
Section

IV-B
how, in a multi-source FDA setting, the local models of source clients are updated and subsequently aggregated to construct the target model.
Finally, in
Section

, we undertake a comprehensive analysis of the advantages conferred by the proposed FedRF-TCA protocol, in terms of its computational and communication overhead, in addition to its enhanced robustness as well as privacy protection mechanisms.

Analysis of communication overhead and robustness

The communication overhead of the proposed FedRF-TCA protocol depends on the dimension
N
of the random features (which, according to
1
, suffices to be of order
\log(n)
and thus depends on the sample size
n
, but
only
in a logarithmic fashion), the dimension of the projected feature space
m
, and the number of the source clients
K
.
During training, each pair of source and target client will need to exchange the compressed features
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
to compute the MMD loss in (
11
), the communication overhead of which is
O(KN)
.
Then, the server collects an aggregation of the linear layer weights
{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times m}
from all source and target clients.
This leads to a communication overhead of
O(KNm)
.
As such, the total communication complexity of the proposed FedRF-TCA is
O(KN+KNm)
, and is, in particular,
independent
of the sample size
n
.

Additional privacy guarantee

Note from
Figure

and the discussion above in
Algorithm

–
1
that with the proposed FedRF-TCA protocol, the messages exchanged between different clients are either in form of “compressed” random features
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
of linear layer weights
{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times m}
and/or the classifier
{\mathbf{C}}
.
In the following, we discuss the additional privacy guarantee offered by FedRF-TCA.

Remark 2

(Privacy protection via random features)
.

FL and federated DA methods are originally proposed to perform decentralized ML so as to mitigate many of the systemic privacy risks
[
17
]
.
In addition to the decentralized storage of data at different local clients, many other techniques such as secure aggregation, noise addition, and update clipping have been proposed for additional privacy consideration, against, e.g., a malicious server.
We argue that the proposed FedRF-TCA protocol offers additional privacy protection on top of that of FL and FDA, in the following sense:

(i)

it is
impossible
for a malicious server to infer the sample size
n_{S,T}
, nor the (random) features
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S,T}
involved in training from a mere observation of the messages exchanged between clients during FDA training, since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S,T}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{S,T}=\pm\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{S,T}{\mathbf{1}}_{n_{S,T}}/n_{S,T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
, the dimension of which is
independent
of the sample size
n_{S,T}
; and

(ii)

it is
impossible
to reveal the raw data
{\mathbf{X}}
from a mere observation of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, since
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{X}})
by
2
for
periodic
function
\sigma(t)=\cos(t)
or
\sin(t)
and Gaussian random matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, and the solution to infer
{\mathbf{X}}
is
not
unique, unless additional constraints are imposed, see also a formal argument in
[
52
]
.

VI
Conclusion and Perspectives

In this paper, we propose FedRF-TCA, a robust and communication-efficient federated DA approach based on the improved random features-based TCA (RF-TCA) approach.
The proposed FedRF-TCA approach has a communication complexity that is (theoretically and practically) nearly
independent
of the sample size, and is robust to messages and/or clients dropouts in the network.
We further provide extensive experiments demonstrating the numerical efficiency and advantageous performance of FedRF-TCA.

By leveraging the block matrix structure inherent in the random feature maps in
2
, FedRF-TCA can be readily extended vertical FL
[
38
]
.
More generally, the FedRF-TCA strategy holds the potential for broader use in other federated MMD-based DA methods.
Importantly, it accomplishes this while delivering substantial reductions in communication and computational complexity, all without compromising the performance typically associated with vanilla MMD-based methods.

Lemma 3

(Sherman–Morrison)
.

For
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
invertible and
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
{\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\sf T}
is invertible if and only if
1+{\mathbf{v}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}\neq 0
and

({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\sf T})^{-1}={\mathbf{A}}^{-1}-\frac{{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}}{1+{\mathbf{v}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}{\mathbf{u}}}.

Theorem 3

(Weyl’s inequality,
[
61
, Theorem 4.3.1]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be symmetric matrices and let the respective eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{A}}
,
{\mathbf{B}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{B}}
be arranged in non-decreasing order, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{p-1}\leq\lambda_{p}
. Then, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{i+j}({\mathbf{A}})+\lambda_{p-j}({\mathbf{B}}),\quad j=0,1,\ldots,p-i,

\displaystyle\lambda_{i-j+1}({\mathbf{A}})+\lambda_{j}({\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{B}}),\quad j=1,\ldots,i.

In particular, taking
i=1
in the first equation and
i=p
in the second equation, together with the fact
\lambda_{j}({\mathbf{B}})=-\lambda_{p+1-j}(-{\mathbf{B}})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
, implies

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|\lambda_{j}({\mathbf{A}})-\lambda_{j}({\mathbf{B}})|\leq\|{\mathbf{A}}-{\mathbf{B}}\|_{2}.

Theorem 4

(Davis–Kahan theorem,
[
62
]
)

Under the same notation as in Theorem

\sin\Theta\left({\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{B}}),{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}})\right)\leq\frac{2\|{\mathbf{B}}\|_{2}}{\min\{|\lambda_{i-1}({\mathbf{A}})-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}})|,|\lambda_{i+1}({\mathbf{A}})-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}})|\}},

(13)

where we denote ‘

’ the alignment between two vectors

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}+{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}+{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|(\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}){\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}({\mathbf{K}}-\hat{\mathbf{K}}){\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}(\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}})\right\|_{2}}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|\hat{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}({\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})({\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}{(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\hat{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})(\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}\right]\leq 3\varepsilon.

Putting together, this gives, for
\mathbb{E}\left\|{\mathbf{K}}-\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{K}}-\frac{{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}}{\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}-\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\right)\right\|_{2}\right]\leq 5\varepsilon,

(29)

which, together with Theorem
2
, concludes the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎

With
1
at hand, we are ready to present the proof of
1
as follows.

Proof of
1
.

Note from (
22
) and (
23
) that the columns of
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{K}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\rm R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are in fact the top
m
eigenvectors
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R}),{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\RF})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, of
{\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R}\equiv{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{K}}(\gamma{\mathbf{K}}+{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}})^{-1}{\mathbf{K}}{\mathbf{H}}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{\RF}\equiv{\mathbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}(\gamma{\mathbf{I}}_{2N}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\mathbf{H}}
, respectively.
As such, the Frobenius norm error satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}-{\mathbf{K}}{\mathbf{W}}_{\rm R})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\RF})-{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(30)

It then follows from Davis–Kahan theorem
[
62
]
, Theorem
4
in Appendix
A
, that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\RF})-{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\sin\Theta\left({\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\RF}),{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\left\|{\mathbf{A}}_{\RF}-{\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R}\right\|_{2}}{\min\{|\lambda_{i-1}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})|,|\lambda_{i+1}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})-\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})|\}},

(31)

with ‘
\sin\Theta({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2})
the “sine similarity” between two vectors
{\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\Theta({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2})\equiv\arccos\left(\frac{{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}}{\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\|_{2}}\right)
that satisfies
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\Theta({\mathbf{u}}_{1},{\mathbf{u}}_{2})
.

Further note that for
\mathbb{E}\left\|{\mathbf{K}}-\frac{{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}}{\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}-(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\gamma+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}})\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2}
, one has

\mathbb{E}\left\|{\mathbf{A}}_{\RF}-{\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R}\right\|_{2}\leq\|{\mathbf{H}}\|_{2}\cdot\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\mathbf{H}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2},

(32)

where we use the fact that
\|{\mathbf{H}}\|=1
, and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{W}}_{\RF}-{\mathbf{K}}{\mathbf{W}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\RF})-{\mathbf{u}}_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{8m\varepsilon^{2}}{(\Delta_{\lambda}/\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2})^{2}}=\frac{8m\varepsilon^{2}}{\tilde{\Delta}_{\lambda}^{2}},

(33)

where we recall that
\Delta_{\lambda}\equiv\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\left|\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})-\lambda_{i+1}({\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R})\right|>0
denotes the
eigen-gap
of
{\mathbf{A}}_{\rm R}\equiv{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{K}}(\gamma{\mathbf{K}}+{\mathbf{K}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\sf T}{\mathbf{K}})^{-1}{\mathbf{K}}{\mathbf{H}}
, as well as the
relative eigen-gap

\tilde{\Delta}_{\lambda}\equiv\Delta/\|{\mathbf{K}}\|_{2},

(34)

as in the statement of
1
.
Applying Corollary
1
, we concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎
