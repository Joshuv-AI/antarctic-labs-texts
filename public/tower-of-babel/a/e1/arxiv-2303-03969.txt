Abstract
. We present an introduction to modern continuous model theory with an emphasis on its interactions with topics covered in this volume such as C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras and von Neumann algebras. The role of ultraproducts is highlighted and expositions of definable sets, imaginaries, quantifier elimination and separable categoricity are included.

1.
Introduction

It is not a new idea to have more truth values than just true and false. For example, Łukasiewicz and Tarski suggested a real-valued logic in the 1930’s
[
38
]
. The modern understanding of continuous model theory has other antecedents as well. Chang and Keisler wrote a book
[
15
]
in which they consider a logic with truth values in arbitrary compact Hausdorff spaces. This logic had many quantifiers and did not catch on; see Ben Yaacov
[
5
]
for further commentary. Henson introduced positive bounded logic in
[
30
]
, mostly in the service of the model theory of normed vector spaces with additional structure. Ben Yaacov introduced the very general setting of cats (short for compact abstract theories) in
[
6
]
in order to capture expansions of first order logic that would allow the seamless use of hyperimaginaries as introduced in
[
31
]
; this line of research built on earlier work around Robinson theories by Hrushovski, Pillay and others. In particular, it was realized that Hausdorff cats interpreted a metric. Ben Yaacov, Berenstein, Henson and Usvyatsov then introduced a logic for metric structures which is the subject of this paper. This logic is first presented in
[
12
]
, which strangely was published after the de facto standard
[
10
]
.

The history of the ultraproduct is very interesting and more colourful than most model theorists are aware. A short history is contained in
[
41
]
. The thumbnail sketch is that an ultraproduct-like construction in the operator algebra context was introduced by Kaplansky and Wright in the early 1950’s. Sakai also used an ultraproduct in the early 1960’s, again in the service of operator algebras. McDuff’s systematic use of ultraproducts in her paper
[
39
]
highlighted how important the construction had become in the operator algebra world. In parallel, the seminal introduction of the ultraproduct in model theory by Łoś
[
37
]
and its use by Robinson in the development of non-standard analysis were key early moments in model theory. It does not seem that anyone at that time saw anything more than a formal connection between the two uses of ultraproducts. Keisler
[
34
]
provides a brief history on the model theory side in which he gives the nod for a precursor to the ultraproduct to Skolem and interestingly to Hewitt
[
33
]
, who was working on rings of continuous functions. Although there was prior work done by Krivine, Stern and others, the first systematic connection between the analytic ultraproduct construction and model theory, particularly for Banach spaces, was the work of Henson
[
30
]
, in which he also introduces his positive bounded logic. A general exposition of this logic appears with Iovino in
[
32
]
.

The ultraproduct plays a slightly different role in the development of continuous model theory than it does in classical logic. While the connection between the ultraproduct construction and the compactness theorem is the same for both logics, it is the exploratory nature of the ultraproduct which is different, by which we mean that there are classes of structures appearing in analysis where an ultraproduct is used but without a formal language being present. The fact that mathematicians are using ultraproducts in certain settings "in the wild" often leads one to examine whether the class can in fact be presented in a model theoretic framework. Examples relevant to this volume where this has been done successfully include Banach spaces,
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, tracial von Neumann algebras, and W
∗
-probability spaces.

As we will see in Example
3.8
, every classical elementary class can be seen as a continuous elementary class. Continuous logic, however, is a
proper
extension of classical first order logic. While there are a number of ways to see this, here is one suggestion based on a Borel complexity argument due to Farah, Toms and Törnquist. In
[
21
]
, they show that the isomorphism relation on the class of separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is turbulent and not Borel-reducible to any class of countable classical structures (see their article for the relevant definitions). Of course, the isomorphism relation for every class of countable classical structures is Borel-reducible to itself, so the isomorphism relation for the class of separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is not equivalent to any class of countable classical structures. As we will see in this article, the class of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is an elementary class (Example
5.18
) and by this complexity argument, this class is not equivalent to any classical elementary class.

We now give a brief outline of the paper. After a quick reminder of the basics of ultrafilters in Section
2
, we introduce the syntax and semantics of continuous logic in Sections
3
and
4
.
Basic continuous model theory is then developed in Section
5
, where a generalized notion of formula is introduced.
As was indicated above, ultraproducts play a crucial role in continuous model theory and the ultraproduct of metric structures is introduced in Section
6
. Section
7
introduces the notion of type in continuous model theory and the type space is used to give another characterization of general formulas. A critical section is Section
8
, where definable sets are discussed and contrasted with the notion of definable set from classical logic. Section
9
highlights the formulation of quantifier elimination in continuous logic and gives several examples. In Section
10
, imaginary elements in the continuous setting are considered and the conceptual completeness theorem is stated in this context. We end the article with a discussion of omitting types in Section
11
and separable categoricity in Section
12
.

Inevitably, there are topics that we left out. In particular, we do not include a discussion of a proof system for continuous logic. This topic is covered in
[
11
]
and is important for the topic of decidability in continuous logic (see, for example, Section 3 of Goldbring’s paper in this volume). Another topic not discussed here is stability theory in continuous logic; one can find the basics of this theory in
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
.

2.
Preliminaries on ultrafilters and ultraproducts

Ultrafilters and ultraproducts will play an important role in continuous model theory. We cover the requisite facts in this section.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose that
X
is a set and
F\subset P(X)
, where
P(X)
is the power set of
X
.

(1)

F
is a (proper)
filter
on
X
if:

(a)

\emptyset\not\in F
,

(b)

A,B\in F
implies
A\cap B\in F
, and

(c)

A\subset B\subset X
and
A\in F
implies
B\in F
.

(2)

F
is an
ultrafilter
on
X
if it is a filter on
X
and, for every
A\subset X
, either
A\in F
or
X\setminus A\in F
(but not both).

The following is an important characterization of ultrafilters. Note that we are assuming the axiom of choice throughout this paper.

Exercise 2.2
.

Suppose
F\subset P(X)
.

(1)

F
is an ultrafilter on
X
if and only if
F
is a maximal (under inclusion) filter on
X
.

(2)

F
is contained in an ultrafilter on
X
if and only if
F
has the finite intersection property, that is, for any finitely many
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in F
,
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\neq\emptyset
.

Example 2.3
.

(1)

If
X
is any infinite set and
F
is the collection of co-finite subsets of
X
, then
F
is a filter on
X
, called the
Fréchet filter
on
X
. Note that the Fréchet filter is not an ultrafilter.

(2)

If
X
is any set,

is any element, and
F
is the collection of subsets of
X
which contain
a
, then
F
is an ultrafilter on
X
, called the

generated by

.

Exercise 2.4
.

If
X
is an infinite set, then an ultrafilter on
X
is non-principal if and only if it contains the Fréchet filter on
X
. Consequently, non-principal ultrafilters on

exist.

The notion of an ultralimit will be important throughout this paper.

Exercise 2.5
.

Suppose that
(r_{i}:i\in I)
is a sequence of real numbers and
{\mathcal{U}}
is an ultrafilter on

. Further suppose that there is a real number

\{i\in I:|r_{i}|<B\}\in{\mathcal{U}}

Definition 2.6
.

In the context of the previous exercise, we say that

is the

(r_{i}:i\in I)
along
{\mathcal{U}}
, written

.

We now introduce the notion of the metric ultraproduct for bounded metric spaces. Suppose that
(X_{i},d_{i})

and define, for

. It is easy to see that

is a pseudometric on

with respect to

\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X_{i}

\bar{x}\in X

[\bar{x}]_{\mathcal{U}}
denote the equivalence class of
\bar{x}
in the quotient. If
X_{i}
is some fixed

of

{\mathcal{U}}

(x:i\in I)
.

Exercise 2.7
.

(1)

Suppose that
(X_{i}:i\in I)
is a collection of

is an ultrafilter on

(2)

Suppose that
(X_{n}:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
is a collection of uniformly bounded metric spaces (which are not necessarily assumed to be complete) and
{\mathcal{U}}
is a non-principal ultrafilter on
{\mathbf{N}}

\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X_{n}
is complete.

Exercise 2.8
.

Suppose that
X
is a compact bounded metric space. Show that for any index set
I
and ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
on

is surjective (and thus an isometry of metric spaces).

Throughout this article, we will be dealing with various metric spaces equipped with additional structure. We highlight here a key point that will come up when we have to deal with such additional structure. To keep things simple, suppose we have a complete, bounded metric space
(X,d)
and, in addition, we have a unary function
f
on
X
. We can naturally define the product
X^{\mathbf{N}}
with the
\sup
-metric and view
f
as being defined co-ordinatewise on
X^{\mathbf{N}}
. We ask: what constraints must there be on
f
if we wish to form an ultrapower of
(X,d,f)
as a quotient of the product structure? Notice in the classical case, there are no restrictions (one can always form the discrete ultrapower). We claim that it is necessary and sufficient that
f
be uniformly continuous. Sufficiency is straightforward and is left as an exercise to the reader. To verify necessity, suppose that there is
\epsilon>0
such that, for every
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
(see the footnote

1

1

We will adopt the convention that when
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
is being treated as a number and not an index then
n\neq 0
. This will avoid issues with division by 0.
on the use of
{\mathbf{N}}
in this article), one can find
a_{n},b_{n}\in X
such that
d(a_{n},b_{n})<1/n
and yet
d(f(a_{n}),f(b_{n}))\geq\epsilon
. Set
\bar{a}=(a_{n}:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
and
\bar{b}=(b_{n}:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
. If
{\mathcal{U}}
is a non-principal ultrafilter on
{\mathbf{N}}
, then
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}d(a_{n},b_{n})=0
and so
[\bar{a}]_{\mathcal{U}}=[\bar{b}]_{\mathcal{U}}
as elements of the ultrapower

. However,
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}d(f(a_{n}),f(b_{n}))\geq\epsilon
and so
[f(\bar{a})]_{\mathcal{U}}\not=[f(\bar{b})]_{\mathcal{U}}
, where
f(\bar{a})=(f(a_{n})\ :\ n\in{\mathbf{N}})
and likewise for

cannot be defined on the ultrapower as a quotient of the product structure.

The conclusion we draw from the previous paragraph is that if we want to extend an ultraproduct construction to additional structure on a metric space, we are going to have to assume some form of uniform continuity. In fact, a similar argument to the one just given shows that if one wants to construct an ultraproduct of uniformly bounded metric spaces
(X_{n},d_{n})
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
each of which is equipped with a uniformly continuous unary function
f_{n}
, the uniform continuity must be uniform along the sequence of structures, that is, for every
\epsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
so that for any
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
and any
a,b\in X_{n}
, if
d_{n}(a,b)<\delta
, then
d_{n}(f_{n}(a),f_{n}(b))\leq\epsilon
(see the discussion at the beginning of Section
3
where we address the asymmetry in the above inequalities).

3.
Metric structures and languages

3.1.
Continuous languages and their interpretation

We now introduce the structures we are interested in studying and the languages of the intended logic at the same time. A
metric structure

\mathcal{M}
, the continuous analog of a classical structure, will be multi-sorted. The language will have a collection of sorts
\mathfrak{S}
. For each sort
S\in\mathfrak{S}
, the language specifies a positive real number
B_{S}
. The intended interpretation of
S
in a metric structure
\mathcal{M}
is a bounded, complete, non-empty metric space
S^{\mathcal{M}}
whose diameter is at most
B_{S}
. There is also a relation symbol
d_{S}
in the language which is to be interpreted as the metric on the space
S^{\mathcal{M}}
.

A language also contains a set of function symbols
\mathfrak{F}
and relation symbols
\mathfrak{R}
(these are sometimes called predicate symbols in other presentations). Each of these symbols comes with some additional information; we first treat function symbols. If
f\in\mathfrak{F}
, then
f
has a domain and co-domain as specified by the language. The domain of
f
, denoted
dom(f)
, is a product of sorts
S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{n_{f}}
for some
n_{f}\in{\mathbf{N}}
, and the co-domain of
f
, denoted
cod(f)
, is a sort
S_{f}
. In addition, the language specifies an increasing, continuous function

\delta_{f}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfying
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\delta_{f}(\epsilon)=0
;
\delta_{f}
is called the
uniform continuity modulus
of
f
. The function
\delta_{f}
is specified by the language (and motivated by the discussion in the previous section).

Comment 3.1
.

It may seem strong to insist that the uniform continuity modulus above is a continuous function. As it turns out, it always can be arranged:

Proposition 3.2
.

[
10
, Proposition 2.10 and Remark 2.12]

Let
F,G\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
be such
that

(\forall\epsilon>0)(\exists\delta>0)(\forall x\in X)(F(x)<\delta\text{ implies }G(x)\leq\epsilon).

Then there exists an increasing, continuous function
\alpha\colon[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]

such that
\alpha(0)=0
and such that,
for all
x\in X
, we have
G(x)\leq\alpha(F(x))
. In fact, one may choose
\alpha
so that it only depends on the map
\epsilon\mapsto\delta
and not on the particular functions
F
and
G
.

Proposition
3.2
is quite useful throughout continuous model theory. For example, it is useful in expressing certain implications (which are otherwise difficult to express given that continuous logic is a “positive” logic); see
[
10
, Section 7]
.
We shall have occasion to use this proposition in Section
8
below.

In a metric structure
\mathcal{M}
for this language, the function symbol
f
is interpreted as a uniformly continuous function

f^{\mathcal{M}}:S_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}\times\ldots\times S_{n_{f}}^{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow S_{f}^{\mathcal{M}}

where the uniform continuity of
f^{\mathcal{M}}
is witnessed by
\delta_{f}
, that is, for all
\epsilon>0
and
a_{i},b_{i}\in S_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying
d_{S_{i}}(a_{i},b_{i})<\delta_{f}(\epsilon)
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have

d_{S_{f}}(f^{\mathcal{M}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n_{f}}),f^{\mathcal{M}}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n_{f}}))\leq\epsilon.

The careful reader will notice the asymmetry between strict inequality and weak inequality in this definition of uniform continuity. The short explanation for the asymmetry is that we want this definition to pass easily through the ultraproduct construction. Nevertheless, the definition here is equivalent to one where we have strict inequalities on both sides.

Constant symbols will be thought of as function symbols with an empty sequence of sorts as domain and some sort as co-domain. The interpretation in a metric structure will be a single element in the chosen co-domain.

For the relation symbols, if
R\in\mathfrak{R}
, the language specifies a domain, denoted
dom(R)
, which is a product of sorts
S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{n_{R}}
for some
n_{R}\in{\mathbf{N}}
. The language also provides a bound
B_{R}>0
and a uniform continuity modulus
\delta_{R}
. In a metric structure
\mathcal{M}
for this language,
R
is interpreted as a uniformly continuous function
R^{\mathcal{M}}:S_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}\times\ldots\times S_{n_{R}}^{\mathcal{M}}\to[-B_{R},B_{R}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with uniform continuity witnessed by
\delta_{R}
as above.

To summarize, a metric language
{\mathcal{L}}
will contain:

•

a set of sorts
\mathfrak{S}
together with, for every
S\in\mathfrak{S}
, a relation symbol
d_{S}
and number
B_{S}>0
,

•

a set of function symbols
\mathfrak{F}
together with, for every
f\in\mathfrak{F}
, a product of sorts
dom(f)
, a sort
cod(f)
, and a uniform continuity modulus
\delta_{f}
, and

•

a set of relation symbols
\mathfrak{R}
together with, for every
R\in\mathfrak{R}
, a product of sorts
dom(R)
, a number
B_{R}>0
, and a uniform continuity modulus
\delta_{R}
.

A metric structure
\mathcal{M}
for
{\mathcal{L}}
(also called an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure) specifies the following:

•

for every sort
S\in\mathfrak{S}
, a complete non-empty metric space
S^{\mathcal{M}}
, with metric
d_{S}^{\mathcal{M}}
of diameter bounded by
B_{S}
,

•

for every
f\in\mathfrak{F}
, a uniformly continuous function
f^{\mathcal{M}}
whose uniform continuity is witnessed by
\delta_{f}
and with domain
S_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}\times\cdots\times S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
and co-domain
S^{\mathcal{M}}
, where
dom(f)=S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
and
cod(f)=S
, and

•

for every
R\in\mathfrak{R}
, a real-valued, bounded, uniformly continuous function
R^{\mathcal{M}}
with uniform continuity modulus
\delta_{R}
, domain
S_{1}^{\mathcal{M}}\times\cdots\times S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
, where
dom(R)=S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
, and co-domain
[-B_{R},B_{R}]
.

For
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, we say that
\mathcal{N}
is a
substructure
of
\mathcal{M}
if

•

for every
S\in\mathfrak{S}
,
S^{\mathcal{N}}
is a closed subspace of
S^{\mathcal{M}}
,

•

for every
f\in\mathfrak{F}
,
f^{\mathcal{N}}
is the restriction of
f^{\mathcal{M}}
to
S_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}\times\ldots\times S_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}
, where
dom(f)=S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
, and

•

for every
R\in\mathfrak{R}
,
R^{\mathcal{N}}
is the restriction of
R^{\mathcal{M}}
to
S_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}\times\ldots\times S_{n}^{\mathcal{N}}
, where
dom(R)=S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
.

If
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
are two languages and
\mathcal{M}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure, then by
\mathcal{M}\restriction{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
we mean the
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-structure obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by only interpreting those sorts, relations and functions arising from
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. We call
\mathcal{M}\restriction{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
the
reduct
of
\mathcal{M}
to
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
and we refer to
\mathcal{M}
as an
expansion
of
\mathcal{M}\restriction{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
to
{\mathcal{L}}
.

We want to consider several categories of
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures. All of these categories will have
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures themselves as objects; the difference between these categories will be the morphisms. For now, we will consider two choices for maps between two
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures,
\rho:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
, by which we mean a family of maps
(\rho_{S}:S\in\mathfrak{S})
such that
\rho_{S}:S^{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow S^{\mathcal{N}}
. Below, we will write
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
to mean that
\bar{a}
is a tuple from the union of the interpretations of the sorts of
\mathcal{M}
and by
\rho(\bar{a})
we will mean
\rho_{S}^{\mathcal{M}}
applied to the elements of the tuple
\bar{a}
for the appropriate
S
’s.

•

\rho
is a
homomorphism
if:

–

for all function symbols
f
of
{\mathcal{L}}
and appropriately sorted
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
\rho(f^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a}))=f^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho(\bar{a}))
;

–

for all relation symbols
R
in
{\mathcal{L}}
and appropriately sorted
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
R^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})\geq R^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho(\bar{a}))
.

•

\rho
is an
embedding
if it is a homomorphism which furthermore satisfies, for all relation symbols
R
in
{\mathcal{L}}
and appropriately sorted
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
,
R^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=R^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho(\bar{a}))
. Notice that if
\rho
is an embedding, then
\rho_{S}
is an isometric embedding for all sorts
S
of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

In all categories we consider,
\rho
is an
isomorphism
if it is an embedding and
\rho_{S}
is surjective for all
S
.
If
\rho:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is an embedding, then by
\rho(\mathcal{M})
we will mean the substructure of
\mathcal{N}
induced on the image of the maps
\rho_{S}
for all sorts
S
.

3.2.
Examples

Example 3.3
.

A toy example of a metric structure is a bounded complete metric space. The language has one sort and one binary relation symbol for the metric. There are no uniform continuity requirements here.

A slightly more involved situation arises if one wants to consider an
unbounded
complete metric space
(X,d)
. While there is no canonical way to deal with this situation, the following approach is helpful, particularly when there is more structure as we will see later. Fix a basepoint
\star\in X
. Introduce in the language sorts
S_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
\{a\in X:d(a,\star)\leq n\}
is the intended interpretation of the sort
S_{n}
. The metric on
S_{n}
will just be the restriction of
d
to this sort. For
m<n
, we also introduce function symbols
i_{m,n}
with domain
S_{m}
, co-domain
S_{n}
, and the identity function for modulus of uniform continuity. The intended interpretation of
i_{m,n}
is the natural inclusion of
S_{m}
into
S_{n}
.

Example 3.4
.

A particularly interesting bounded metric space from the model-theoretic perspective is the Urysohn sphere
U
. In
[
42
]
, Urysohn constructed a separable complete
1
-bounded metric space which is both
universal
, that is, every other separable 1-bounded metric space isometrically embeds into
U
, and
ultrahomogeneous
, that is, if
A,B\subset U
are finite and
f:A\rightarrow B
is an isometric bijection, then
f
can be extended to an automorphism of
U
.
U
is the unique separable complete
1
-bounded metric space which is both universal and ultrahomogeneous. Since
U
is bounded, it is automatically an example of a metric structure.

One way to build
U
is via a Fraïssé-like construction using the class
\mathcal{C}
of finite metric spaces with rational distances at most 1. (To see how Fraïssé constructions are done in the continuous setting, see Vignati’s article in this volume.) There are only countably many objects in
\mathcal{C}
and so, besides bookkeeping, one is led to the following problem: given
A,B,C\in\mathcal{C}
with
A\subset B
and

For any

We henceforth assume that, for each

we have added function symbols

\mathrm{C}^{*}

will be the (naïve) co-domain of

Suppose that

4.
Formulas

4.1.
Basic Formulas

We now introduce the syntax of formulas for continuous logic. Fix a continuous language
{\mathcal{L}}
with sorts
\mathfrak{S}
, function symbols
\mathfrak{F}
, and relation symbols
\mathfrak{R}
.

For each sort
S\in\mathfrak{S}
, we introduce variables
x_{n}^{S}
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. As in classical logic, we need to define terms before formulas.

Terms
in
{\mathcal{L}}
, along with their domain, co-domain, and associated uniform continuity modulus, are defined inductively as follows:

(1)

If
x
is a variable of sort
S
, then
x
is a term. Its domain and co-domain are
S
and its uniform continuity modulus is the identity function.

(2)

If

is a function symbol with domain
S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
and co-domain
S
and
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
are terms with co-domains
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
respectively, then
f(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})
is a term with co-domain
S
and domain determined by composition. The uniform continuity modulus of this term is also determined by composition; for instance, one can take

\epsilon\mapsto\min\{\delta_{1}(\delta_{f}(\epsilon)/2),\ldots,\delta_{n}((\delta_{f}(\epsilon)/2)\},

where
\delta_{f}
is the uniform continuity modulus of
f
and
\delta_{k}
is the uniform continuity modulus of
\tau_{k}
for every
k=1,\ldots,n
.

The
basic formulas
of

are now defined by induction. We use the term basic formula because, as we will see later in this article, we will want to introduce a more general notion of formula to accommodate the approximate nature of continuous logic.

3

We hope there is no confusion with this use of the term basic formula and its various uses in classical logic.
Each basic formula will have a domain, bound, and uniform continuity modulus as is the case for relation symbols.

(1)

Suppose that

and

(2)

If

\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are basic formulas, then
\psi:=f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})

B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
are the bounds of
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}

\psi
is the minimum

f(\prod_{i=1}^{n}[-B_{i},B_{i}])\subseteq[-B,B].

The domain of
\psi

is also determined by composition from the uniform continuity moduli of the
\varphi_{i}
’s and a fixed uniform continuity modulus of

restricted to
\prod_{i=1}^{n}[-B_{i},B_{i}]

(3)

Suppose that
x
is a variable and
\varphi
is a formula. We can then form two
quantified
basic formulas
\sup_{x}\varphi
and
\inf_{x}\varphi
. The bound and uniform continuity modulus of these formulas is the same as
\varphi
. The domain is only effected by the possible removal of the sort associated with

.

The basic formulas arising from the first clause are called

. Basic formulas arising from the first two clauses are called

Comment 4.2
.

In the second clause above, we have chosen to be very generous about the functions - the connectives - we allow. It is convenient theoretically to allow all continuous functions (which are actually uniformly continuous since we are only really interested in their values on some hypercube in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
). It is sometimes worthwhile to work with a smaller collection of connectives. The expressive power of continuous logic would not change if we used a collection of connectives which were dense in the set of all continuous functions. For instance, we could restrict ourselves to using only polynomials with rational coefficients as connectives. Other restricted sets of connectives have been suggested (see
[
10
, Section 6]
for more details). As we will see, it is the density character of the set of formulas which matters the most in continuous logic and not the cardinality of the set of formulas.

Another set of basic formulas to highlight are the
prenex
formulas, which are those basic formulas beginning with a block of quantifiers followed by a quan-tifier-free basic formula. In a natural (pseudo)metric defined on the space of all basic formulas (see Subsection
5.1
), the prenex formulas are dense. We will come back to prenex formulas in Subsection
7.2
.

{\mathcal{L}}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
.

A term
\tau

\mathcal{M}

Some examples of formulas

Since it takes some getting used to writing expressions in continuous logic, let us look at some standard ways of expressing certain statements.

Example 4.5

Suppose we have two terms

We sometimes wish to assert that one formula is less than or equal to another in value. We do this with the help of the truncated substraction function

\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ y:=\max\{0,x-y\}

to

. Notice that for an

-structure

-)theory. If

5.2.
General Notion of Formula

We now generalize the notion of basic formula. It is important to note that this general notion of formula is relative to a background theory. The general notion of formula follows from introducing the following semi-norm on the set of basic formulas. Fix an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory
T
and a fixed set of variables
\bar{x}
. Given a basic formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
, define
\|\varphi(\bar{x})\|_{T}=\sup\{|\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})|\colon\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}\models T\}
. Let

be the completion of the set of basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas in free variables
\bar{x}
with respect to the pseudometric induced by the semi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{T}
(that is, the distance between
\varphi
and

\|\varphi-\psi\|_{T}
). We say
\varphi
and
\psi
are

-equivalent
if
\|\varphi-\psi\|_{T}=0
. Thus,

is obtained by first identifying

-equivalent formulae and then completing the resulting metric space. Elements of

will be called

-formulas
and are limits of basic formulas in the sense of the semi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{T}
. If

, we will refer to elements of

simply as
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas
. If
\varphi
is a
T
-formula which is the limit of basic formulas
\varphi_{n}
, then we can interpret
\varphi
in a model
\mathcal{M}
of

by setting
\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}=\lim_{n}\varphi_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

5.3.
First model-theoretic results

Proposition 5.5

(Tarski-Vaught test)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{N}\preceq\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

For all basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas
\varphi(y,\bar{x})
and all
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{N}
,

\inf\{\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(b,\bar{a})\colon b\in\mathcal{N}\}=\inf\{\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(b,\bar{a})\colon b\in\mathcal{M}\}.

Proof.

(1) implies (2) by the definition of elementary substructure. For (2) implies (1), we argue by induction on the formation of basic formulas. The interesting case is that of an
\inf
quantifier. Thus, suppose
\varphi(\bar{x})=\inf_{y}\psi(y,\bar{x})
and
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{N}
. By induction, we always have
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})\geq\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
. To prove the reverse inequality, fix
\epsilon>0
and take
b\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(b,\bar{a})<\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})+\epsilon
. By condition (2), we can find
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(b^{\prime},\bar{a})<\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})+\epsilon
, and so by induction and letting
\epsilon
tend to 0, we have
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})\leq\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
. ∎

Theorem 5.6

(Downward Löwenheim-Skolem)
.

Given
X\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, there is
\mathcal{N}\preceq\mathcal{M}
such that
X\subseteq N
and
\chi(\mathcal{N})\leq\chi(X)+\chi({\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

We accomplish the creation of
\mathcal{N}
by iteratively closing off in order to satisfy the Tarski-Vaught test; we only sketch of the details.
Fix a set of formulas
\{\varphi_{i}(y,\bar{x}_{i})\colon i\in I\}
which is dense in

with
|I|=\chi({\mathcal{L}})
. We create an increasing sequence
(X_{n})
of subsets of
\mathcal{M}
such that:

(1)

X_{0}=X
,

(2)

for each
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
,
i\in I
, and
\bar{a}\in X_{n}
, there is a sequence
(b_{n})
contained in
X_{n+1}
such that

\textstyle{(\inf_{y}\varphi(y,\bar{a}))^{\mathcal{M}}=\inf\{\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(b_{n},\bar{a}):n\in{\mathbf{N}}\}},

and

(3)

\chi(X_{n})\leq\chi(X)+\chi({\mathcal{L}})
.

We let

be the closure (in

. ∎

5.4.
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games

We now introduce the continuous version of Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games which provide a way of recognizing elementary equivalence. These games have been useful in the study of theories of various operator algebras (see for example
[
25
,
28
]
).

Fix a set of basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas
\Delta
in the variables
\bar{x}=(x_{i}\colon 1\leq i\leq n)
, as well as an
\epsilon>0
. Suppose
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are two
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures. We describe a game, the
EF(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N},\Delta,\epsilon)
-game, between two players, Player I and Player II. The game has
n
rounds. In the i
th
round, Player I chooses either
a_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
or
b_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
of whatever sort the variable
x_{i}
belongs to and then Player II replies with
b_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
or
a_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
respectively. After
n
rounds, there are two sequences
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
and
\bar{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
. Player II wins the game if for every
\varphi(\bar{x})\in\Delta
, we have

|\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})-\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{b})|<\epsilon.

Theorem 5.12
.

For two
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathcal{N}
.

(2)

For every finite set
\Delta
of atomic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas and every
\epsilon>0
, Player II has a winning strategy for the

-game.

(3)

For every finite set
\Delta
of basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas and every
\epsilon>0
, Player II has a winning strategy for the
EF(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N},\Delta,\epsilon)
-game.

Example 5.14

(The Urysohn sphere)

Example 5.16

(Hilbert space)
.

We now write sentences (or axioms) that are true in all (dissections of) Hilbert spaces using the language
{\mathcal{L}}_{HS}
. These sentences will in fact axiomatize the theory of (dissections of) Hilbert spaces.

The first set of axioms will express that we are dealing with a complex vector space. Due to the presence of the sorts
S_{n}
, we will need infinitely many sentences to express the usual axioms. For instance, for each
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, we will have a sentence

\sup_{x,y\in S_{n}}d_{n}(\lambda_{n}(x+_{n}y),\lambda_{n}(x)+_{m}\lambda_{n}(y)),

where
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
and
S_{m}
is the co-domain of
\lambda_{n}
. Of course, if these sentences hold in our structure, then we will know that
\lambda(x+y)=\lambda x+\lambda y
for all
x
and
y
. We leave it as an exercise to write out in a similar fashion all other sentences needed to guarantee that we have a complex vector space.

The second set of axioms expresses that we have an inner product and expresses its relationship with the metric on each sort. For instance, we have the sentences

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}|d_{n}(x,0)^{2}-Re\langle x,x\rangle|.

We leave it as an exercise to express the other axioms related to the inner product.

We need a third set of axioms which tell us that the inclusion maps between the sorts are isometric embeddings that preserve the algebraic operations. First, we need to express that the inclusion maps themselves are compatible: for
n<m<k
, we include sentences

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}d_{k}(i_{m,k}i_{n,m}(x),i_{n,k}(x)),

which assert that
i_{m,k}i_{n,m}=i_{n,k}
An instance of the inclusion maps preserving the algebraic structure are the sentences, for
n<m
and
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
,

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}d_{l}(i_{k,l}(\lambda_{n}x),\lambda_{m}i_{n,m}(x)),

where
S_{k}
is the co-domain of
\lambda_{n}
and
S_{l}
is the co-domain of
\lambda_{m}
.

Finally, we want to express that the sorts are interpreted correctly, that is,
S_{n}
should be the ball of radius
n
. We express this fact with two axioms:

\sup_{x\in S_{1}}(d_{1}(x,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ 1)

and

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}\min\{1\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ \|x\|,\inf_{y\in S_{1}}d_{n}(y,i_{1,n}(y)\}.

The first axiom says that everything in
S_{1}
should have norm at most 1 and by scaling, everything in
S_{n}
should have norm at most
n
. The second axiom says that if something in
S_{n}
has norm less than
1
, then it is in the closure of the image of the inclusion map from
S_{1}
. Using the completeness of the sorts, we obtain that anything of norm at most 1 in
S_{n}
arises from something in
S_{1}
. Consequently, if these two axioms are satisfied (along with all the others above), then
S_{1}
is the unit ball of a Hilbert space, and the direct limit of the sorts, via the embeddings, is a Hilbert space.

If we denote by
T_{HS}
the set of sentences listed above, then we conclude:

Theorem 5.17
.

T_{HS}
axiomatizes the class of dissections of Hilbert spaces. Moreover, models of
T_{HS}
with homomorphisms as morphisms and the category of Hilbert spaces with homomorphisms as morphisms are equivalent via the dissection functor.

Example 5.18

(
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras)
.

As in the previous example, we now write out sentences that axiomatize the class of (dissections of)
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras in the language
{\mathcal{L}}_{C^{*}}
from Example

First of all we, need to express that the structure is a complex *-algebra. This requires writing out a number of equations in the manner described above in the Hilbert space example. We will not write out all the equations, but here is an example:

\sup_{x,y\in S_{n}}d_{n}((xy)^{*},y^{*}x^{*}).

There is one such sentence for every
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. Of course, this set of sentences is expressing the relationship between multiplication and the adjoint in a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, namely that
(xy)^{*}=y^{*}x^{*}
for all
x
and
y
.

We also want to say that the underlying structure is a normed algebra. Again, we only write out a sample and leave it as an exercise to write out the rest. It is tempting to write the triangle inequality as

\forall_{x,y\in S_{n}}\|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|

but more formally it is written as

\sup_{x,y\in S_{n}}\left(\|x+y\|\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ (\|x\|+\|y\|)\right),

where
\|z\|
is shorthand for
d(z,0)
. The latter sentence evaluates to
0
in a
{\mathcal{L}}_{C^{*}}
-structure precisely when the triangle inequality holds.

We also want our Banach *-algebra to be a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. The
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-identity is written as

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}\left|\|x^{*}x\|-\|x\|^{2}\right|,

where we use the above convention for
\|\cdot\|
. We include one such sentence for every
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
although, in light of the other axioms, it would be enough to include this axiom only for
n=1
.

As in the case of Hilbert spaces, there are now some sentences which must be included in order to make the formalism of continuous logic match with the normal operator algebraic setting. To start with, we have the embedding maps
i_{m,n}
. We can write out sentences which will express that these maps are isometric embeddings and they preserve the relevant algebraic structure (addition, multiplication and the adjoint). We leave the formulation of these sentences to the reader.

We also want the sorts
S_{n}
to be interpreted as the operator norm balls of radius
n
. We need two sets of sentences. First, we include sentences

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}(\|x\|\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ n),

which says that every element of the sort
S_{n}
has norm at most
n
. Finally, we record sentences which will guarantee that all the elements of norm at most
n
lie in
S_{n}
. Recall from Example
3.10
the introduction of function symbols
\mathbf{q^{n}_{k}}
with domain
S_{n}
and co-domain
S_{1}
. They are to represent polynomials
q^{n}_{k}
and so we write sentences

\sup_{x\in S_{n}}d_{m}(i_{1,m}(\mathbf{q^{n}_{k}}(x)),q^{n}_{k}(x)),

where
S_{m}
is the co-domain of the term
q^{n}_{k}
. We will call the set of sentences that we have outlined in this example
T_{C^{*}}
.
By the argument after Lemma
3.11
, we see that the
{\mathcal{L}}_{C^{*}}
-structures which satisfy
T_{C^{*}}
are precisely those of the form
D(A)
for some
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
. We conclude:

Theorem 5.19
.

T_{C^{*}}
axiomatizes the class of dissections of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. Moreover the category of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and the category of models of
T_{C^{*}}
(with homomorphisms as morphisms) are equivalent via the dissection functor.

6.
Ultraproducts

6.1.
Ultraproducts of metric structures

Fix a metric language
{\mathcal{L}}=(\mathfrak{S,F,R})
, an index set
I
, an ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
on
I
, and
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}_{i}
for
i\in I
. We construct the
(metric) ultraproduct

\mathcal{N}:=\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
as follows:

•

For each sort
S\in\mathfrak{S}
, we let
S^{\mathcal{N}}
be the metric space
\prod_{\mathcal{U}}(S^{\mathcal{M}_{i}},d_{i})
, where
d_{i}
is the metric on
S^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}
. We interpret the metric on
S^{\mathcal{N}}
as
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}d_{i}
. Recall that this is well-defined since all the metric spaces involved are uniformly bounded by the bound
B_{S}
.

•

For each
f\in\mathfrak{F}
whose domain is
S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{n}
, we define
f^{\mathcal{N}}
by

f^{\mathcal{N}}([(a^{1}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}},\ldots,[(a^{n}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}})=[(f(a^{1}_{i},\ldots,a^{n}_{i}))]_{\mathcal{U}},

where
[(a^{j}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in S^{\mathcal{N}}_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
. Again, since all interpretations of
f
satisfy the same uniform continuity modulus, this definition of
f^{\mathcal{N}}
is well-defined and
f^{\mathcal{N}}
satisfies the uniform continuity modulus specified by the language.

•

For each
R\in\mathfrak{R}
, we define
R^{\mathcal{N}}
by

R^{\mathcal{N}}([(a^{1}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}},\ldots,[(a^{n}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}})=\lim_{\mathcal{U}}R^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(a^{1}_{i},\ldots,a^{n}_{i}),

where
[(a^{j}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in S^{N}_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
. As in the case of function symbols, since the interpretation of
R
is uniformly bounded in all
\mathcal{M}_{i}
with the same uniform continuity modulus, this definition of
R^{\mathcal{N}}
is well-defined and
R^{\mathcal{N}}
satisfies the uniform continuity modulus specified by the language.

Exercise 6.1
.

Verify that
\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
is indeed an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure.

If all the
\mathcal{M}_{i}
are the same
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
, then we call the resulting ultraproduct the
ultrapower
and denote it by
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{U}}
.

As in classical logic, it is important to be able to evaluate formulas in the ultraproduct. Here is the appropriate version of
Łoś’ Theorem
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure for all
i\in I
and
{\mathcal{U}}
is an ultrafilter on
I
. Set
\mathcal{N}=\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{M}_{i}
. For an
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
and
\bar{a}=[(\bar{a}_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathcal{N}
sorted the same as the variables
\bar{x}
, we have

\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})=\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i}).

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the formulation of basic formulas. We only treat the case of
\varphi(\bar{x})=\inf_{y}\psi(y,\bar{x})
.

Suppose that
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i})>r
for some
r\in\mathbf{R}
. Set
X=\{i\in I:\varphi^{M_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i})>r\}
and note that
X\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Fix
b=[(b_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathcal{N}
. For each
i\in X
, we have
\psi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(b_{i},\bar{a}_{i})>r
. By induction, we have
\psi^{\mathcal{N}}(b,\bar{a})\geq r
. Since this was true for all
b\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})\geq r
and thus
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})\geq\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i})
.

Now suppose that
s=\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{a})>r
but
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i})\leq r
. Pick
\epsilon>0
small enough so that
r+\epsilon<s
and let
X=\{i\in I\colon\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(\bar{a}_{i})<r+\epsilon\}\in{\mathcal{U}}
. For
i\in X
, choose
b_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}
so that
\psi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(b_{i},\bar{a}_{i})<r+\epsilon
. Set
b=[(b_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}
. By induction we have
\psi^{\mathcal{N}}(b,\bar{a})\leq r+\epsilon<s
, which is a contradiction. ∎

.

6.2.
Applications of the ultraproduct

We say that a set of sentences
\Gamma
is
finitely satisfiable
if every finite subset of
\Gamma
is satisfiable. We say that
\Gamma
is
approximately finitely satisfiable
if, for every finite subset
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
and every
\epsilon>0
, there is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
so that
|\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}|\leq\epsilon
for every
\varphi\in\Gamma_{0}
. Equivalently,
\Gamma
is approximately finitely satisfiable if the set of sentences
\{|\varphi|\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ \epsilon\colon\varphi\in\Gamma,\ \epsilon>0\}
is finitely satisfiable.

Theorem 6.4

(Compactness Theorem)
.

For a metric language
{\mathcal{L}}
and a set of
{\mathcal{L}}
-sentences
\Gamma
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\Gamma
is satisfiable.

(2)

\Gamma
is finitely satisfiable.

(3)

\Gamma
is approximately finitely satisfiable.

Proof.

Clearly the conditions decrease in strength. To show that (3) implies (1), consider
I
to be the set of finite subsets of
\Gamma\cup{\mathbf{N}}
(disjoint union) which have non-empty intersection with both
\Gamma
and
{\mathbf{N}}
. If
i=F\cup X
is an element of
I
with
F\subset\Gamma
and
X\subset{\mathbf{N}}
, let
M_{i}
be an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure such that
|\varphi^{M_{i}}|\leq 1/m
for all
\varphi\in F
, where
m=\max\{n\colon n\in X\}
. For
\varphi\in\Gamma
and
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, let
O_{\varphi,n}
be the set of
i\in I
which contain both
\varphi
and
n
. Notice that
Y=\{O_{\varphi,n}\colon\varphi\in\Gamma,n\in{\mathbf{N}}\}
has the finite intersection property. Let
{\mathcal{U}}
be an ultrafilter on
I
which contains
Y
. Consider the ultraproduct
\mathcal{N}=\prod_{\mathcal{U}}M_{i}
. For every
\varphi\in\Gamma
and every
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, we have
O_{\varphi,n}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, so by the Łoś Theorem, we have
|\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}|\leq\frac{1}{n}
for all
n
and hence
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}=0
. It follows that
\mathcal{N}
satisfies
\Gamma
. ∎

We can use the compactness theorem and the method of expansion by constants to give a version of the upward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem in the continuous setting. We say that a metric structure
\mathcal{M}
is
compact
if
S^{\mathcal{M}}
is a compact metric space for each sort
S
in the language.

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is a non-compact
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure and
\chi(Th(\mathcal{M}),{\mathcal{L}})\leq\lambda
. Then
\mathcal{M}
has an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\chi(\mathcal{N})=\lambda
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}
is not compact, there is a sort
S
and an
\epsilon>0
such that the collection of
\epsilon
-balls of
S^{\mathcal{M}}
does not have a finite subcover. Now consider the language
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
obtained from
{\mathcal{L}}
by introducing new constant symbols
c_{\alpha}
of sort
S
for
\alpha<\lambda
. Consider the
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-theory
T^{\prime}
made up of
Elem(\mathcal{M})
together with

\{d(c_{\alpha},c_{\beta})\geq\epsilon:\alpha<\beta<\lambda\}.

Note that
T^{\prime}
is finitely satisfiable and
\chi(T^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})=\lambda
. By the compactness theorem and the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem,
T^{\prime}
has a model with density character
\lambda
. Taking the reduct of this model to
{\mathcal{L}}
, we get the required model of
T
of density character
\lambda
. ∎

We begin this section with an exercise.

-structures

-structure
\mathcal{P}

(Keisler-Shelah)
.

For
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}
and

and

[

.
It is important to notice that in the Keisler-Shelah theorem, the index sets for the ultrafilters need not be countable.

In the continuous setting, it is often difficult to explicitly write out a sentence that distinguishes the theories of two structures. The approach of showing that no two ultrapowers are isomorphic has been used to good effect in separating theories of II

[

{\mathcal{L}}
is separable. Given two
{\mathcal{L}}

\mathcal{M}
and

\omega

in

and

\mathcal{N}

{\mathcal{L}}
-substructures of
\mathcal{M}
and

.

Assume

{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}
and

(1)

\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathcal{N}
.

(2)

For some (equiv. any) non-principal ultrafilter

6.4.
Elementary classes

We now give a characterization of elementary classes which parallels a result from classical first order logic. Fix a class
\mathcal{C}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
closed under ultraroots
if for any ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
, whenever
\mathcal{N}^{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}
.

Theorem 6.10
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is a class of
{\mathcal{L}}
-structures. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{C}
is an elementary class.

(2)

\mathcal{C}
is closed under isomorphisms, ultraproducts, and elementary submodels.

(3)

\mathcal{C}
is closed under isomorphisms, ultraproducts, and ultraroots.

Proof.

Clearly (1) implies both (2) and (3). We first prove (2) implies (1) and then modify the proof to get (3) implies (1). Thus, assume (2) and let
T
be the theory of
\mathcal{C}
, that is, let
T
be the set of all sentences
\varphi
such that
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}=0
for all
\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{C}
. We show that
\mathcal{C}
is the collection of models of
T
. It is clear that all elements of
\mathcal{C}
model
T
. On the other hand, fix a model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
. By (2), it suffices to elementarily embed
\mathcal{M}
into an ultraproduct of structures from
\mathcal{C}
. To this end, suppose
\varphi(c_{\bar{m}})
is in
Elem(\mathcal{M})
for some sequence of constants
c_{\bar{m}}
with
\bar{m}\in\mathcal{M}
. Now consider
\inf_{\bar{x}}|\varphi(\bar{x})|
, an
{\mathcal{L}}
-sentence whose value in
\mathcal{M}
is 0. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there is
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi,\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}
which satisfies
\inf_{\bar{x}}|\varphi(\bar{x})|<\epsilon
(for otherwise the sentence
\epsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ \inf_{\bar{x}}|\varphi(\bar{x})|
belongs to
T
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{M}\models T
). By taking an ultraproduct over all
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi,\epsilon}
as
\varphi
and
\epsilon
vary, we can elementarily embed
\mathcal{M}
into an ultraproduct of elements of
\mathcal{C}
, as desired.

To obtain (3) implies (1), notice that the previous argument showed (assuming that
\mathcal{C}
is merely closed under isomorphisms and ultraproducts) that
\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathcal{N}
for some
\mathcal{N}
in
\mathcal{C}
(namely an ultraproduct of structures from
\mathcal{C}
). By the Keisler-Shelah theorem,
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{U}}\cong\mathcal{N}^{\mathcal{V}}
for some ultrafilters
{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
. Assuming (3), we have that
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{C}
. ∎

Although the semantic characterization of elementary class is the same in continuous logic as in classical logic, it plays a somewhat bigger role in the continuous setting as it is often difficult to explicitly write out sentences that axiomatize a given class of metric structures. For instance, although the class of W
∗
-probability spaces (see
[
2
]
for the definition) was axiomatized in a complicated language in
[
17
]
, it was relatively easy to show using the above semantic characterization that this class was elementary in a more natural language
[
26
]
; an explicit axiomatization in this language will only finally appear in
[
3
]
.

7.
Types

7.1.
The type space

Suppose that
T
is a theory in some language
{\mathcal{L}}
. Fix a sequence of sorted variables
\bar{x}
. The
space of
\bar{x}
-types of
T
, denoted
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
, is the set of functions of the form
tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})(\varphi(\bar{x})):=\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a model of
T
,
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
(sorted in the same way as the variables in
\bar{x}
), and
\varphi
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula whose free variables are among
\bar{x}
.
tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
is called the
complete type of
a
in
\mathcal{M}
(complete meaning that the value of every formula
\varphi
is completely specified). We use letters such as
p
and
q
to denote complete types. If
p\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)
and
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
is such that
tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=p
, then we say that
\bar{a}

realizes

p
and that
p
is
realized in
\mathcal{M}
. Just as with complete theories, complete types are determined by knowing the kernel of the type functional:

tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})(\varphi(\bar{x}))=r\text{ iff }\varphi(\bar{x})-r\in ker(tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})).

It is convenient to talk about partial types as well. Suppose
\Sigma
is a set of
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas in free variables
\bar{x}
and
p
is a complete type in the variables
\bar{x}
. Then the corresponding
partial (
\Sigma
)-type
is the function
p\restriction\Sigma
.
If
\Sigma
is the set of all quantifier-free formulas in the free variables
\bar{x}
, we call the
\Sigma
-type a
quantifier-free type
.

As in the classical case, the type space
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
carries a natural topology. For
\varphi\in Form_{\bar{x}}(T,{\mathcal{L}})
and real numbers
r<s
, set

O^{T}(\varphi,(r,s))=\{p\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)\colon p(\varphi)\in(r,s)\}.

Exercise 7.1
.

(1)

Prove that the set of all
O^{T}(\varphi,(r,s))
is a base for a Hausdorff topology on
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
, called the
logic topology
.

(2)

Prove that the logic topology on
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
is compact. (Hint: use the compactness theorem.)

(3)

Prove that if
\pi(\bar{x})
is a partial type, then the set
\{p\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)\ :\ p\text{ extends }\pi\}
is a closed subset of
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
.

(4)

In fact, show that every non-empty closed in the logic topology on
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
has the form
\{p\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)\ :\ p\text{ extends }\pi\}
, where
\pi
is a partial type. (Hint: Show that those
p\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)
for which
p(\varphi)\leq r
are the same as those which extend the partial type
\pi
where
\pi(\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ r)=0
.)

If the theory
T
is complete, the type space also has a natural
metric topology
: for
p,q\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)
, we set
d(p,q)
to be the infinum of
d(\bar{a},\bar{b})
, where
p=tp^{\mathcal{M}}({\bar{a}})
and
q=tp^{\mathcal{M}}({\bar{b}})
for some model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
and some
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
. The completeness of the theory guarantees that
d(p,q)
is defined (not infinite) for every pair of types
p
and
q
. The symmetry and reflexivity of
d(p,q)
are clear. To see that the triangle inequality holds, fix
p,q,r\in S_{\bar{x}}(T)
. By compactness, we can find models
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
of
T
and
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
,
\bar{b}^{\prime},\bar{c}\in\mathcal{N}
with
p=tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
,
q=tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b})=tp^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{b}^{\prime})
,
r=tp^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{c})
,
d(p,q)=d(\bar{a},\bar{b})
and
d(q,r)=d(\bar{b}^{\prime},\bar{c})
. We leave it as an exercise that by taking a common elementary extension of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, we can assume that
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{N}
and
\bar{b}=\bar{b}^{\prime}
. By the triangle inequality (in
\mathcal{M}
),
d(p,q)+d(q,r)\geq d(\bar{a},\bar{c})\geq d(p,r)
.

Exercise 7.2
.

(1)

Show that the metric topology refines the logic topology on
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
.

(2)

Show that
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
is complete with respect to the metric topology.

We will see later that there are model theoretic consequences when these two topologies agree (even locally).

7.2.
Some uses of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem in continuous logic

To understand the extension from basic formulas to more general formulas in a more abstract fashion, we remind the reader of the background necessary for the Stone-Weierstrass theorem.

Suppose that
X
is a topological space and set
C(X,\mathbf{R})
to be the set of continuous functions from
X
to
\mathbf{R}
.
C(X,\mathbf{R})
has a natural real algebra structure by considering pointwise addition and multiplication together with scalar multiplication by elements of
\mathbf{R}
. It is also naturally topologized by uniform convergence, that is, for

and

is metrizable by the

-norm. For

, we say that

if for all

.

Theorem 7.3

(Stone-Weierstrass theorem)
.

Suppose that
X
is a compact, Hausdorf space and
A
is a real subalgebra of
C(X,\mathbf{R})
. If the constant function
1
belongs to

, then
A
is dense in
C(X,\mathbf{R})
if and only if
A
separates points.

If
\varphi(\bar{x})
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula and
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory, then there is a natural evaluation map
f_{\varphi}\colon S_{\bar{x}}(T)\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
given by
f_{\varphi}(p)=p(\varphi)
. With the logic topology on the type space
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
, it is easy to see that
f_{\varphi}
is continuous. We want to use the Stone-Weierstrass theorem to give another understanding of formulas in continuous model theory. In fact, this alternative perspective is really the reason behind extending the class of formulas.

Theorem 7.4
.

Suppose that
T
is a theory,
\bar{x}
a sequence of variables, and
X=S_{\bar{x}}(T)
. Then the evaluation map

from

to

given by

is an isometry which commutes with all continuous real-valued functions, that is, if

is a continuous function and
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in Form_{\bar{x}}(T,{\mathcal{L}})
, then

f\circ(ev(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,ev(\varphi_{n}))=ev(f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})).

Proof.

Note that the range of the evaluation map restricted to basic formulas is a real subalgebra containing 1 and which separates points. It follows then from the Stone-Weierstrass theorem that the evaluation map is onto. The map is norm-preserving since if
\varphi(\bar{x})
is a basic formula such that
\|\varphi\|_{T}=r
, then on the one hand,
\|ev(\varphi)\|\leq r
in

, while by compactness, we can find some

such that

, hence
\|ev(\varphi)\|=r
. Commutation with continuous functions is immediate. ∎

We need a slight strengthening of the previous theorem for real algebra systems (see Definition
5.3
). The proof follows from compactness and the Stone-Weierstrass theorem.

7.3.
Saturation

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure and
A\subset\mathcal{M}
. We say that

is a
(partial) type over
A
if
p
is a (partial) type in
{\mathcal{L}}_{A}
with respect to the theory of
\mathcal{M}_{A}
. Consequently, if
p
is a partial type over
A
, there is an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
and
\bar{b}\in\mathcal{N}
realizing
p
, that is, if
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{a})
is an
{\mathcal{L}}_{A}
-formula (in the domain of
p
), then
p(\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{a}))=\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}(\bar{b},\bar{a})
.

Definition 7.7
.

For
\kappa
an infinite cardinal, we say that an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure
\mathcal{M}
is
\kappa
-saturated
if, whenever
A\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is such that
\chi(A)<\kappa
and
p
is a type over
A
, then
p
is realized in
\mathcal{M}
.
\mathcal{M}
is said to be
saturated
if it is
\chi(\mathcal{M})
-saturated.

As in the case of classical first order logic, saturated models typically only exist under additional set theoretic assumptions. For instance, if
2^{\kappa}=\kappa^{+}
, then the following proposition yields that if
\chi(\mathcal{M})=\kappa
, then
\mathcal{M}
has a saturated elementary extension of density character
\kappa^{+}
.

Proposition 7.8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure and
\chi({\mathcal{L}})\leq\chi(\mathcal{M})=\kappa
. Then there is an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{N}
is
\kappa^{+}
-saturated and
\chi(\mathcal{N})\leq 2^{\kappa}
.

Proof.

We construct
\mathcal{N}
as a union of an increasing chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\kappa^{+}}
such that:

(1)

M_{0}=\mathcal{M}
,

(2)

if
\delta<\kappa^{+}
is a limit ordinal, then
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
is the completion of
\bigcup_{\alpha<\delta}\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
(that is, we take the direct limit of the
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
’s as described in Proposition
5.10
),

(3)

\chi(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})\leq 2^{\kappa}
for all
\alpha<\kappa^{+}
, and

(4)

for every
\alpha<\kappa^{+}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\prec M_{\alpha+1}
and for every
A\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
with
\chi(A)\leq\kappa
, every type
p
over
A
is realized in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha+1}
.

Only the last point needs comment. There are
2^{\kappa}
many subsets of size
\kappa
of a set of size
2^{\kappa}
and at most
2^{\kappa}
many types over a set of density character
\kappa
. Consequently, we need to realize
2^{\kappa}
many types, which one can do one at a time with a chain of length
2^{\kappa}
since any individual type can be realized in an elementary extension of density character at most
2^{\kappa}
. ∎

One model-theoretic reason for working with ultraproducts is that they are always somewhat saturated:

7.4.
Beth definability

We now prove the Beth Definability theorem in continuous logic. This theorem gives us an abstract criterion for recognizing relations that can be expressed by a formula. As we will see, this theorem plays a wider role in continuous logic than in classical logic due to the difficulty in identifying which relations are exactly formulas in the continuous setting.

Theorem 7.11

(Beth Definability)
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
are two continuous languages with the same sorts. Further, suppose
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory. If the forgetful functor

F:Mod(T)\rightarrow Str({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})
given by
F(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{M}\restriction{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
is an equivalence of categories onto the image of
F
, then every
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula is
T
-equivalent to an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formula.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varphi(\bar{x})
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula. For
\epsilon>0
, consider the set of sentences
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
in the language
{\mathcal{L}}
with two tuples of constants
\bar{a}
and
\bar{b}
added consisting of
T
together with

\{|\psi(\bar{a})-\psi(\bar{b})|\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ \frac{1}{n}:\psi(\bar{x})\text{ is an ${\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}$-formula, }n\in{\mathbf{N}}\}\cup\{\epsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ |\varphi(\bar{a})-\varphi(\bar{b})|\}.

If
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
is unsatisfiable for all
\epsilon
, then by compactness,
\varphi
defines a continuous real-valued function on the
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-restrictions of
{\mathcal{L}}
-types in the variables
\bar{x}
and so by Proposition
7.5
applied to the real algebra of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formulas,
\varphi
is
T
-equivalent to an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formula, which is the desired conclusion.

Based on the previous paragraph, we now suppose, towards a contradiction, that
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
is satisfiable for some
\epsilon>0
. We can thus find a model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
and
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\bar{a}
and
\bar{b}
have the same
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-type but
\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})\neq\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b})
.

Take an
|M|^{+}
-saturated elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
be the reducts of
\mathcal{M}
and
N
to
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is also
|\mathcal{M}^{\prime}|^{+}
-saturated, we can find an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-elementary map
\rho\colon\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\rightarrow N^{\prime}
such that
\rho(\bar{b})=\bar{a}
. Since
F
is an equivalence of categories,
\rho
is also an
{\mathcal{L}}
-elementary map, which contradicts that
\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})\neq\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b})
. ∎

8.
Definable Sets

8.1.
Introducing definable sets

We will now look at one of the biggest differences between continuous model theory and classical model theory. In the classical setting, it is often advantageous to be able to quantify over a subset of the universe which is described by a formula. For instance, if
\varphi(x)
is a formula expressing some property, it is straightforward to also express "for all
x
, if
\varphi(x)
then …" or "there exists
x
such that
\varphi(x)
and …". In the continuous setting, where formulas take on real values, the subsets over which one can quantify is a delicate matter.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure and
\varphi(\bar{x})
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula. The
zero-set of
\varphi
in
\mathcal{M}
, written
Z^{\mathcal{M}}(\varphi)
, is the set
\{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}\colon\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=0\}
. Naively one might think that one can quantify over zero sets of formulas, but as we will soon see, that is not quite correct.

Suppose that
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory and
\bar{S}
is a tuple of sorts from
{\mathcal{L}}
. A
T
-functor (for
\bar{S}
)
is a functor
X\colon Mod(T)\rightarrow Met
, where
Met
is the category of metric spaces with isometric embeddings as morphisms,
X(\mathcal{M})
is a closed subspace of
\bar{S}(\mathcal{M})
, and if
\rho:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is an elementary map in
Mod(T)
, then
X(\rho)
is the restriction of
\rho
to
X(\mathcal{M})
.

Example 8.1
.

A natural example of a
T
-functor is
X_{\varphi}
for some formula
\varphi
, where
X_{\varphi}(\mathcal{M})=Z(\varphi^{\mathcal{M}})=\{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}\colon\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=0\}
, the zero set of
\varphi
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Suppose that
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory and
X
is a
T
-functor for
\bar{S}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There is a
T
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
such that for any model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
and any
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=d(\bar{a},X(\mathcal{M})).

(2)

For any
T
-formula
\psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, there is a
T
-formula
\chi(\bar{y})
such that for any model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
and any
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\chi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})=\inf\{\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b},\bar{a})\colon\bar{b}\in X(\mathcal{M})\}

(or equivalently the analogous statement replacing
\inf
by
\sup
).

(3)

For every
\epsilon>0
, there is a
T
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
and
\delta>0
such that, for all models
\mathcal{M}
of
T
:

•

X(M)\subseteq Z(\varphi^{\mathcal{M}})
, and

•

For all
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, if
\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})<\delta
, then
d(\bar{a},X(\mathcal{M}))\leq\epsilon
.

(4)

For every
\epsilon>0
, there is a basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
and
\delta>0
such that, for all models
\mathcal{M}
of
T
:

•

X(\mathcal{M})\subseteq Z(\varphi^{\mathcal{M}})
, and

•

For all
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, if
\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})<\delta
, then
d(\bar{a},X(\mathcal{M}))\leq\epsilon
.

(5)

For any set

{\mathcal{L}}
-structures
\mathcal{M}_{i}
for

, and ultrafilter
{\mathcal{U}}
on
I

X(\mathcal{N})=\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X(\mathcal{M}_{i}),\text{ where }\mathcal{N}=\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{M}_{i}.

Comment 8.3
.

Before we begin the proof of Theorem
8.2
, we want to address one small point. What do we do with the empty functor, that is, the functor
X
which satisfies
X(\mathcal{M})=\emptyset
for all models
\mathcal{M}
of
T
? The empty functor certainly satisfies condition (5) above; what about the other four conditions? In condition (1) we must decide what the distance is to the empty set - it should be the maximum possible value of the metric (and hence is given by a formula). In (2), the
\inf
is taken over an empty set hence equals the maximum possible value of
\psi
- again, a formula. In (3) and (4), we are measuring the distance to the empty set and so we again take this to be the upper bound on the metric. Consequently, the empty functor satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
8.2
when conditions (1)-(4) are suitably interpreted. Moreover, if
X
satisfies any of the conditions of the theorem and
X(\mathcal{M})
is empty for some model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
, then it is empty for all models
\mathcal{M}
of
T
(and thus satisfies all of the other conditions in the theorem).

Proof of Theorem
8.2
.

Based on the previous comment, we can assume, without loss of generality, that
X(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
for all models
\mathcal{M}
of
T
.

The proof of (1) implies (2) can be found in
[
10
, Theorem 9.17]
. We only remark that if
d_{X}(x)
is a formula which expresses the distance from
x
to
X
in any model of
T
and
\alpha
is a uniform continuity modulus for the formula
\psi
, then in any model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
(remembering that
X(\mathcal{M})
is not empty),
\inf\{\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b},\bar{a})\colon\bar{b}\in X(\mathcal{M})\}
equals

\inf_{\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}}(\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{b},\bar{a})+\alpha(d^{\mathcal{M}}_{X}(\bar{b}))).

It is easy to see that (2) implies (3). For (3) implies (4), it is enough to remember that
T
-formulas are uniformly approximated by basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas. To see that (4) implies (5), we first show that
\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X(\mathcal{M}_{i})\subseteq X(\mathcal{N})
. To this end, fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\varphi
and
\delta
be as in (4). If
[(a_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X(\mathcal{M}_{i})

\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(a_{i})=0
for all

, hence
\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}([a_{i}]_{\mathcal{U}})=0
and thus

was arbitrary, we have that
[(a_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in X(\mathcal{N})

[(a_{i})]_{\mathcal{U}}\in X(\mathcal{N})\setminus\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X(\mathcal{M}_{i})

\prod_{\mathcal{U}}X(\mathcal{M}_{i})
is closed in
\mathcal{N}
, there is
\epsilon>0
such that

\varphi
and
\delta>0
be as in (4). Since

\varphi^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}(a_{i})<\delta

-almost all

It remains to show that (5) implies (1). Let
\mathcal{C}
be the class of structures

\{(\mathcal{M},d_{X}^{\mathcal{M}})\colon\mathcal{M}\models T\},

where

measures the distance from an element to

. Note that measuring the distance to a closed set is indeed an allowable relation in continuous logic: the bound is the maximum of the bounds on the metrics in
\bar{S}
and the modulus of uniform continuity is the identity function. By Theorem

\mathcal{C}
is an elementary class. The forgetful functor from
\mathcal{C}
onto

is an equivalence of categories since any model of

is in fact equivalent to a
T
-formula. ∎

If
X
is a
T
-functor (for
\bar{S})
satisfying any of the equivalent conditions of the previous theorem, then we say that

is a

-definable set
. By condition (2) of the previous theorem,
(T
-)definable sets are exactly those sets in metric structures over which one can quantify.

8.2.
Examples and non-examples

We begin with some examples of definable sets.

Example 8.4
.

Any sort is a definable set relative to any theory as is any finite product of sorts. Perhaps less trivially, the range of any term is definable, since if we are given a term
\tau(\bar{x})
and we wish to quantify over the range of
\tau
, for instance
\inf\{\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}(y)\colon y\in\text{range}(\tau^{\mathcal{M}})\}
, we would evaluate
\inf_{\bar{x}}\varphi(\tau(\bar{x}))
. This fact already pays dividends in the case of operator algebras, where we can express any self-adjoint element as
(x+x^{*})/2
and any positive element as
x^{*}x
, hence both of these sets of elements are definable relative to theory of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

Example 8.5
.

A slightly less trivial example is that the set of projections is definable relative to the class of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. Recall that the element
p
in a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is a projection if it is self-adjoint and satisfies
p^{2}=p
. To see that the set of projections is definable, we use some functional calculus. Suppose we have a self-adjoint element
b
in some
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
satisfying
\|b^{2}-b\|<\epsilon
, where
\epsilon<1/4
. We know that the subalgebra
B
of
A
generated by
b
and
1_{A}
is isomorphic to
C(\sigma(b))
by an isomorphism sending
b
to the identity function and
1_{A}
to the constant function
1
, where
\sigma(b)
is the spectrum of
b
and
C(\sigma(b))
is the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra of complex-valued continuous functions on
\sigma(b)
. By applying the isomorphism, in
C(\sigma(b))
, we have
\|x^{2}-x\|<\epsilon
and so
\sigma(b)
can be partitioned into two disjoint open sets
O_{0}
and
O_{1}
with
i\in O_{i}
for
i=0,1
. If we let
\pi
be the function which is constantly
i
on
O_{i}
, then this is a continuous function on
\sigma(b)
. Additionally,
\pi
is a projection and
\|\pi-x\|<\epsilon
. Pulling the isomorphism from
C(\sigma(b))
back into
B
, we find a projection
p\in B
such that
\|b-p\|<\epsilon
. By Theorem
8.2
(3), the set of projections is definable relative to the theory of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

We now give two non-examples to demonstrate that the notion of definable set is quite distinct from the notion of zero set. The first is a somewhat abstract example.

Example 8.6
.

The structure
\mathcal{M}
has a single sort with underlying set
[0,1]
equipped with the discrete metric and a single relation symbol
R
which is interpreted as
R(x)=x
. Let
T=Th(\mathcal{M})
and consider the
T
-functor
X
, for
\mathcal{N}\in Mod(T)
, defined by
X(\mathcal{N})=\{a\in\mathcal{N}\colon R^{\mathcal{N}}(a)\leq 1/2\}
. There are a variety of ways to see that
X
is not definable. We will use the ultraproduct characterization. Notice that if
{\mathcal{U}}
is a non-principal ultrafilter on
{\mathbf{N}}
, then the sequence
(1/2+1/n:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
has

1/2
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{U}}
but the distance from this element to every element in

is 1. This shows that
X(\mathcal{M})^{\mathcal{U}}\neq X(M^{\mathcal{U}})
and so

is not
T
-definable.

Example 8.7
.

An example that appears in
[
18
]
in the context of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras is the set of normal elements. In a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, the element

is said to be normal if it commutes with its adjoint, hence we are looking at the zero set of
\|a^{*}a-aa^{*}\|
. The set of normal elements in a

-algebra is not definable. (See
[

for a proof.)

Principal types

For emphasis, we record the following remark made during Comment

Proposition 8.8

If

9.
Quantifier elimination

We say that a theory
T
in a language
{\mathcal{L}}
has
quantifier elimination
if, for every
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
, there is a quantifier-free
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\psi(\bar{x})
which is
T
-equivalent to
\varphi(\bar{x})
. In terms of basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas, this says: for every
\epsilon>0
and every basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
, there is a quantifier-free basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\psi
such that
\|\varphi(\bar{x})-\psi(\bar{x})\|_{T}<\epsilon
.

We will give three characterizations of quantifier elimination. The first is syntactical.

Proposition 9.1
.

Suppose that
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory. Then
T
has quantifier-elimination if and only if every formula of the form
\inf_{x}\varphi(x,\bar{y})
, where
\varphi(x,\bar{y})
is a basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula, is
T
-equivalent to a quantifier-free
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula.

Proof.

The forward direction is immediate and the backward direction is proven by induction on the formation of basic
{\mathcal{L}}
-formulas, the quantifier case being taken care of by the backwards assumption and the connective case being immediate.
∎

Proposition 9.2
.

Suppose that
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory. Then
T
has quantifier-elimination if and only if whenever
\mathcal{M}
is a model of
T
and
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
have the same values for basic atomic formulas, then they satisfy the same complete type.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
7.5
applied to the real algebra system of quantifier-free formulas. ∎

Example 9.3
.

We can use Proposition
9.2
to see that the theory
T
of the Urysohn sphere

has quantifier elimination. Indeed, suppose that
\mathcal{N}
is a model of
T
and
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\mathcal{N}
have the same quantifier-free type. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mathcal{N}
is separable and thus we may further assume that
\mathcal{N}
is

. Since
\bar{a}
and

sending

is an isometry. Since

is ultrahomogeneous,

extends to an automorphism of

\bar{a}
and
\bar{b}
have the same complete type in

A more semantic characterization of quantifier elimination is given by the following:

Proposition 9.4

Suppose

{\mathcal{L}}

\{|\psi(\bar{c})-\psi(\bar{d})|\colon\psi(\bar{y})\text{ is a basic quantifier-free ${\mathcal{L}}$-formula}\}\\

\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ |\inf_{x}\varphi(x,\bar{c})-\inf_{x}\varphi(x,\bar{d})|\},

\bar{c}

of

10.
Imaginaries

In classical model theory, imaginary elements play an important role. The construction of the theory
T^{eq}
from
T
creates the largest expansion of
T
for which the category of models is equivalent. Although somewhat more complicated in the continuous setting, one can define imaginaries for metric structures which have similar properties. We consider them in the next three subsections.

10.1.
Products

Fix a continuous language
{\mathcal{L}}
and an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory
T
. Suppose that
\bar{S}=(S_{n}:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
is a sequence of sorts from
{\mathcal{L}}
. We add one new sort
S
to
{\mathcal{L}}
to form
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. The intention is that we will expand models of
T
to include as the interpretation of
S
the product of the sorts
S_{n}
. We expand every model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
by interpreting
S
as
\prod_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
. We need a metric for this sort. The choice of metric is not unique but for definiteness we use, for
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
,

d(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\sum_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\frac{d_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}(x_{n},y_{n})}{B_{n}2^{n}},

where
d_{n}
is the metric symbol for
S_{n}
and
B_{n}
is the bound on that metric. The key property that
d
has is that for every
\epsilon>0
, there is an
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
such that if
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\prod_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
x_{i}=y_{i}
for every
i<n
, then
d(\bar{x},\bar{y})<\epsilon
. The only other symbols we add to the language
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
are maps
\pi_{n}
from
S
to
S_{n}
, and in the canonical expansion of an
{\mathcal{L}}
-structure to an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-structure, we interpret
\pi_{n}
as the projection onto the
n^{th}
coordinate. The uniform continuity modulus for
\pi_{n}
can be taken to be
\epsilon/B_{n}2^{n}
.

We record here three families of sentences that hold in all expanded structures.
First, for any
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, we want to state that

\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{d_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(x_{i},y_{i})}{B_{i}2^{i}}\leq d(x,y)\leq\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{d_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(x_{i},y_{i})}{B_{i}2^{i}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}

holds for all
x,y\in S
; we can express this fact in continuous logic by requiring that the following two sets of sentences evaluates to
0
:

\sup_{x,y\in S}\left(d(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ (\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{d_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(\pi_{i}(x),\pi_{i}(y))}{B_{i}2^{i}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}})\right).

and

\sup_{x,y\in S}\left(\sum_{i\leq n}\frac{d_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(\pi_{i}(x),\pi_{i}(y))}{B_{i}2^{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{
\vrule height=3.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}{\smash{-}}}\limits^{\textstyle\ .}}\ d(x,y)\right).

We would also like the finite products to be dense in
S
. We can achieve this by satisfying the following sentences, one for each
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
:

\sup_{x_{i}\in S_{i},i<n}\inf_{x\in S}\max_{i<n}\{d_{i}(\pi_{i}(x),x_{i})\}.

Let
T^{\prime}
be the
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-theory consisting of
T
together with the three preceding sets of sentences. If
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a model of
T^{\prime}
, then it has the form
(\mathcal{M},Y)
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a model of
T
and
Y
is the interpretation of the sort
S
. We wish to see that
S
is essentially
\prod_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
. Fix
x_{i}\in S_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}
. The third set of axioms guarantees that, for every
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
, there is
y^{n}\in Y
such that
d(\pi_{i}(y^{n}),x_{i})<\frac{1}{2^{n}}
for every
i<n
. The first set of axioms assures us that the sequence
(y^{n}:n\in{\mathbf{N}})
converges to some
y\in Y
(since
Y
is complete). For this
y
, we have that
d(\pi_{n}(y),x_{n})=0
for all
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. If there is another
y^{\prime}\in Y
with the same property, then using the first set of sentences again, we have that
d(y,y^{\prime})=0
and so
y=y^{\prime}
. In this way we see that
Y
can be identified with
\prod_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}S_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
via the projection maps. This identification is an isometry since the first two sets of axioms guarantees that the metric on
Y
is given by

d(x,y)=\sum_{n}\frac{d_{n}(\pi_{n}(x),\pi_{n}(y))}{B_{n}2^{n}}.

What we have achieved is the following:

Theorem 10.1
.

The forgetful functor from
Mod(T^{\prime})
to
Mod(T)
is an equivalence of categories.

X

{\mathcal{L}}
-theory

.

The forgetful functor from

in the article by Berenstein and Henson in this volume) in an expanded language
{\mathcal{L}}^{eq}
which is closed under taking finite and countable products of sorts, and adding definable sets and canonical parameters. (In fact, one can be economical and first close under products, then add definable sets and finally add canonical parameters.)

In order to state the main theorem regarding
T^{eq}
, we need to say what interpretation means in continuous logic.

Definition 10.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
be continuous languages and suppose that
T
and
T^{\prime}
are theories in
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
respectively. An
interpretation

I
of
T
in
T^{\prime}
assigns:

(1)

to every sort
S
of
{\mathcal{L}}
, a sort
I(S)
of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
,

(2)

to every function symbol
f\colon\prod S_{i}\rightarrow S
an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formula
I(f)
with free variables in
\prod S_{i}\times S
, such that

T^{\prime}\models\text{``}I(f)(\bar{x},y)=0\text{ is the graph of a total function of }\bar{x}\text{''}

(3)

to every relation symbol
R
with domain
S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{n}
in
{\mathcal{L}}
, an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formula
I(R)
with domain
I(S_{1})\times\cdots\times I(S_{n})
,

such that, for any
{\mathcal{L}}
-sentence
\varphi
,
T\models\varphi
iff
T^{\prime}\models I(\varphi)
(where by
I(\varphi)
one means the result of applying the interpretation of the language inductively to
\varphi
).

While this definition is not the most general definition of interpretation, it is sufficient to prove the following theorem, whose proof can be found in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 10.5

(Conceptual completeness)
.

Suppose that
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory. Then
T^{eq}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

The forgetful functor from
Mod(T^{eq})
to
Mod(T)
is an equivalence of categories.

(2)

If
T^{\prime}
is an expansion of
T
in some language
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
such that the forgetful functor from
Mod(T^{\prime})
to
Mod(T)
is an equivalence of categories, then
T^{\prime}
can be interpreted in
T^{eq}
.

11.
Omitting types

We say that a structure
\mathcal{M}

omits
a (partial) type
p
if
p
is not realized in
\mathcal{M}
. Criteria in the classical case for the omission of types is well understood. The situation in the continuous case is more problematic; see the discussion at the end of this section. We will see here that the role of definability is pivotal in the omitting types theorem for continuous logic.

Throughout this section, we work in a theory
T
in a separable language
{\mathcal{L}}
. As mentioned before, by Proposition
8.8
, if
p
is omitted in some model, then
p
is not principal. The converse is the omitting types theorem for continuous logic:

Theorem 11.1

(Omitting types theorem)
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose that
p_{n}
is a complete type which is not principal. Then there is a separable model of
T
which omits each
p_{n}
.

Proof.

This proof provides an opportunity to show what a Henkin construction looks like in the continuous setting. We will sketch the proof where we omit a single complete type
p
in one variable and leave it to the reader to handle the more general case. To this end, we consider the language
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
which is
{\mathcal{L}}
together with countably many new constants symbols
c_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. The intention is that we will construct an
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-theory
T^{\prime}
extending
T
and a model of
T^{\prime}
in which the interpretations of the
c_{n}
’s are dense.
Recall the notation for open sets in the logic topology: a basic open set relative to the theory
T
has the form

\{\bar{T}\colon T\subseteq\bar{T}\text{ is a complete ${\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}$-theory and }\bar{T}\models a<\psi<b\}

for some
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-sentence
\psi
and interval
(a,b)
. We denote this open set by
O^{T}(\psi,(a,b))
.

We construct
T^{\prime}
inductively as the intersection of a decreasing sequence of non-empty basic logical open sets
O_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. We set
O_{0}
to be the set of all complete
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-theories extending
T
. Now, fix a countable dense set of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-sentences
\psi_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
and a countable dense set of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-formulas in one free variable
\varphi_{n}(x)
for
n\in{\mathbf{N}}
. We will want the set
O_{n}
to satisfy the following conditions:

First, for every
m
and every rational number
r
, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
and rational interval
(a,b)
with
b-a<r
such that

O_{n}\subseteq O^{T}(\psi_{m},(a,b)).

Second, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and rational number
r
, if for some
n
we have

O_{n}\subseteq O^{T}(\inf_{x}\varphi_{m}(x),(-\infty,r)),

then for some
k>n
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
we have

O_{k}\subseteq O^{T}(\varphi(c_{l}),(-\infty,r)).

It is not hard to see that if we can succeed in obtaining these properties, then
\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}O_{n}
is a complete
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
-theory such that in any model, the closure of the interpretations of the constants
c_{n}
is the underlying set of a model of
T
.

In order for the aforementioned model of
T
to omit
p
, we need to demand more from the open sets
O_{n}
. In the classical case, it is enough to require that none of the new constants satisfy
p
. Here, since the new constants are merely dense in the model of
T
we have constructed, we will instead need to keep the constants some fixed distance away from the set of realizations of
p
. To this end, recall that since
p
is not principal, by Proposition
8.9
, there is some
\epsilon>0
such that the open
\epsilon
-ball around
p
does not contain any logical open set. Suppose that we are at some stage of the construction and we have constructed the logical open set
O_{n}
, which we may assume, after some manipulation, has the form
O^{T}(\theta(c_{k},\bar{d}),(-\infty,0))
, where
c_{k}
is the new constant we are currently interested in keeping away from
p
and
\bar{d}
is the rest of the new constants in
\theta
.
Now consider the logical open set
O^{T}(\inf_{\bar{y}}\theta(x,\bar{y}),(-\infty,0))
. By the assumption that
p
is not principal, there is some
q\in S_{x}(T)
such that
q
belongs to
O^{T}(\inf_{\bar{y}}\theta(x,\bar{y}),(-\infty,0))
and
d(p,q)>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. By compactness, we can find some
{\mathcal{L}}
-formula
\psi(x)
such that:

•

for all
\mathcal{M}\models T
and
a\in\mathcal{M}
with
\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(a)<0
, we have
(\inf_{\bar{y}}\theta(a,\bar{y}))^{\mathcal{M}}<0
, and

•

for any
\mathcal{M}\models T
and
a,b\in\mathcal{M}
such that
a
realizes
p
and
\psi^{\mathcal{M}}(b)<0
, we have
d(a,b)>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

We now let
O_{n+1}=O_{n}\cap O^{T}(\psi(c_{k}),(-\infty,0))
and note that this will guarantee that no interpretation of
c_{k}
will be within
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
of a realization of
p
in any model of
T
. Since we can achieve this for all of the new constants, no Cauchy sequence of the new constants will approach a realization of
p
and so the model we construct will omit
p
. ∎

The situation regarding omitting partial types in continuous logic is quite problematic as evidenced by the results of Farah and Magidor from
[
20
]
. There they show the following:

(1)

There is a complete theory
T
in a separable language and countably many partial types such that every finite subset is omitted in some model of
T
but no model of
T
simultaneously omits all of them.

(2)

There is a complete theory
T
in a separable language and partial types
s
and
t
, each one omitted in some model of
T
, but no model omits both.

A variant of the omitting types theorem used to omit certain kinds of partial types has proven useful in the operator algebra context; for example, see
[
18
]
,
[
24
]
, and Goldbring’s article in this volume.

12.
Separable categoricity

For an infinite cardinal
\lambda
, we say that a theory
T
is
\lambda
-categorical
if
\lambda\geq\chi(T,{\mathcal{L}})
and whenever
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are models of
T
such that
\chi(\mathcal{M})=\chi(\mathcal{N})=\lambda
, then
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{N}
. We will focus on the case where
\lambda
is
\aleph_{0}
and we will typically say
separably categorical
instead of
\aleph_{0}
-categorical. As in the classical case, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 12.1
.

Suppose
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory which has only models of density character at least
\chi(T,{\mathcal{L}})
and is
\lambda
-categorical. Then
T
is complete.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are models of
T
. If
\mathcal{M}
has density character at least
\lambda
, then by the Downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, we can find
\mathcal{M}_{0}\prec\mathcal{M}
with
\chi(\mathcal{M}_{0})=\lambda
. If
\chi(\mathcal{M})<\lambda
, then by the upward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem applied to
Elem(\mathcal{M})
, we can find
\mathcal{M}_{0}
with
\chi(\mathcal{M}_{0})=\lambda
and
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Applying the same reasoning to
\mathcal{N}
, we can find
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{N}_{0}
so that

\mathcal{M}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{0}\cong\mathcal{N}_{0}\equiv\mathcal{N}.

From this we conclude that
T
is complete. ∎

We now state the Ryll-Nardewski Theorem for continuous logic; the proof, done originally in the positive bounded case, is due to Henson.

Theorem 12.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{L}}
is separable and
T
is an
{\mathcal{L}}
-theory with no compact models. Then
T
is separably categorical if and only
T
is complete and every complete type is principal.

Proof.

If
T
is separably categorical then it is complete. If there is a complete type
p
which is not principal, then by the omitting types theorem, there is some separable model of
T
which omits
p
. However, there is also a separable model of
T
which realizes
p
; since these two models of
T
cannot be isomorphic,
T
is not separably categorical; contradiction.

Now suppose that
T
is complete and every complete type is principal. Consider two separable models
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
of
T
. We need to show that
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{N}
.

We construct two sequences

a_{0}^{0},a^{1}_{0}a^{1}_{1},a^{2}_{0}a^{2}_{1}a^{2}_{2},\ldots
in
\mathcal{M}
and

b_{0}^{0},b^{1}_{0}b^{1}_{1},b^{2}_{0}b^{2}_{1}b^{2}_{2},\ldots
in
\mathcal{N}
such that
all initials segments of the same length have the same type, that is, for any
k
, there is a fixed type for

a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{k}
and
b^{n}_{0}\ldots b^{n}_{k}
independent of
n\geq k
.
We will also want to arrange that for every
k
,
\langle a^{n}_{k}:n\geq k\rangle
and
\langle b^{n}_{k}:n\geq k\rangle
form Cauchy sequences converging to
\bf a_{k}
and
\bf b_{k}
respectively so that

\{\bf a_{k}:k\in{\mathbf{N}}\}
and
\{\bf b_{k}:k\in{\mathbf{N}}\}
are dense in
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
respectively.
If we can achieve this, then the map sending
\bf a_{k}
to
\bf b_{k}
extends to an isomorphism from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{N}
.

To start, we enumerate countable dense subsets in
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
; call them respectively
\langle c_{k}:k\in N\rangle
and
\langle d_{k}:k\in N\rangle
.
At stage 0, let
a^{0}_{0}=c_{0}
. By assumption,
tp^{\mathcal{M}}(c_{0})
is principal and hence realized in
\mathcal{N}
by some
b^{0}_{0}
. In general, at each step we alternate by either choosing a
c_{k}
or
d_{k}
and we revisit each
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
infinitely often in the construction.
Assume we have chosen
a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{n}
already and we consider whatever
c_{k}
is given to us at this stage.
Let
p(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
be the type of
a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{n}
in
\mathcal{M}
and
q(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+1})
be the type of
a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{n}c_{k}
in
\mathcal{M}
.
Since
q
is principal, we have a formula
d_{q}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n+1})
expressing the distance to realizations of
q
.
Since
d_{q}^{\mathcal{M}}(a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{n},c_{k})=0
, we have that
\inf_{y}d_{q}^{M}(a^{n}_{0}\ldots a^{n}_{n},y)=0
.
Since
b^{n}_{0}\ldots b^{n}_{n}
satisfies
p
by assumption, we have
\inf_{y}d_{q}^{\mathcal{N}}(b^{n}_{0}\ldots b^{n}_{n},y)=0
.
This means we can find
b^{n+1}_{0}\ldots b^{n+1}_{n+1}
realizing
q
and such that
d(b^{n}_{i},b^{n+1}_{i})\leq 1/2^{n}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
.
This guarantees we have the required Cauchy sequences and we have the required density as well. To see the latter fact, fix
\epsilon>0
. Choose
N
large enough so that
\sum_{n\geq N}1/2^{n}<\epsilon
. If we visit
c_{k}
at stage
t>N
,
then
\bf a_{t}
is within
\epsilon
of
c_{k}
and so the
\bf a_{k}
’s are dense in
M
. Similarly, the
\bf b_{k}
’s are dense in
N
, finishing the proof.
∎

Note that the condition “every complete type is principal” in Theorem
12.1
is equivalent to the statement that the logic and metric topologies on
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
agree for all finite tuples
\bar{x}
of variables.

Exercise 12.3
.

Prove that a complete theory
T
in a separable language with no compact models is separably categorical if and only if
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
is compact in the metric topology for all finite tuples
\bar{x}
of variables.

There are a number of nontrivial examples of separably categorical continuous theories:

Example 12.4
.

The following theories are separably categorical:

(1)

The theory of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

(2)

The theory of the Urysohn sphere.

(3)

The theory of atomless probability algebras.

The fact that the theory of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces is separably categorical follows from the fact that any two separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces are isomorphic to
\ell^{2}
while the separable categoricity of the theory of the Urysohn sphere follows from the argument presented in Example
5.14
. The separable categoricity of the theory of atomless probability algebras is discussed in Berenstein and Henson’s article in this volume
[
4
]
.

In the context of operator algebras, there are very few examples of separably categorical theories. For instance, for any separable II
1
factor
N
, there are continuum many nonisomorphic separable II
1
factors elementarily equivalent to
N
(see Theorem 4.1 in the author’s article with Goldbring in this volume). One notable example of a separably categorical
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra is the algebra
C(X)
for
X
a totally disconnected compact Hausdorff space; in this case,
C(2^{\mathbb{N}})
is the unique separable model. The proof of this fact follows from Stone duality together with the fact that there is a unique countable atomless Boolean algebra.

We end this section with some related considerations. We say that a model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
is
atomic
if
tp^{\mathcal{M}}(\bar{a})
is principal for every finite tuple
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
. Theorem
12.1
(and its proof) yields the following:

Theorem 12.5
.

(1)

Any two separable atomic models of
T
are isomorphic.

(2)

If
T
is a complete theory in a separable language, then
T
is separably categorical if and only if all separable models of
T
are atomic.

In the remainder of this section, for simplicity, we assume that
T
is a complete theory in a separable language.

We say that a model of
T
is
prime
if it embeds elementarily into all other models of
T
.

Exercise 12.6
.

Prove that a model
\mathcal{M}
of
T
is prime if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is separable and atomic. Moreover,
T
can have at most one prime model up to isomorphism. (Hint: To show that a separable atomic model of
T
is prime, use a “forth only” version of the back and forth argument appearing in the proof of Theorem

.)

Exercise 12.7
.

Prove that
T
has a prime model if and only if the principal types are dense in
S_{\bar{x}}(T)

\bar{x}

We say that
T
is
small
if
S_{\bar{x}}(T)
is separable in the metric topology for all finite tuples
\bar{x}
of variables. The following is Proposition 1.17 in
[

]

Proposition 12.8
.

If
T
