Convergence of the Riemannian Langevin Algorithm
Khashayar Gatmiry, Santosh S. Vempala

Abstract
We study the Riemannian Langevin Algorithm for the problem of sampling from a distribution with density ν \nu with respect to the natural measure on a manifold with metric g g . We assume that the target density satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with respect to the metric and prove that the manifold generalization of the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm converges rapidly to ν \nu for Hessian manifolds. This allows us to reduce the problem of sampling non-smooth (constrained) densities in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} to sampling smooth densities over appropriate manifolds, while needing access only to the gradient of the log-density, and this, in turn, to sampling from the natural Brownian motion on the manifold. Our main analytic tools are (1) an extension of self-concordance to manifolds, and (2) a stochastic approach to bounding smoothness on manifolds. A special case of our approach is sampling isoperimetric densities restricted to polytopes by using the metric defined by the logarithmic barrier.

Introduction

Sampling is a fundamental problem with connections to many areas of mathematics and algorithms. A natural approach to sampling from a desired distribution is
diffusion

When does the rapid convergence of a diffusion process result in an efficient sampling algorithm?

In recent years, progress has been made on this via the Langevin Diffusion and associated Langevin algorithm, both in Euclidean space. For a target density proportional to

1.

Under what conditions does the discretization of RLD, the Riemannian Langevin Algorithm, converge?

2.

Can non-smooth and/or constrained distributions be sampled by mapping to appropriate manifolds and using RLA? This is analogous to optimization where non-smooth convex optimization can be mapped to smooth convex optimization via self-concordant barriers (the Interior-Point Method

The discretization of Mirror Langevin Diffusion has been an active research topic in recent years. It was first proposed as a sampling algorithm by
[

They also require the function of interest to be smooth and strongly convex. Even with these assumptions, convergence to the target distribution with vanishing bias was established very recently

A natural candidate for the condition on the metric is self-concordance, as usually defined in optimization, i.e., the third directional derivative of the convex function can be bounded by a constant times the second directional derivative to the power of
3/2

[

Here we prove a general theorem based on self-concordance parameters of the metric
g
defining the manifold and smoothness parameters of
F
. We define
second-order self-concordance
for a metric, an extension of the standard definition to one higher derivative.
Our main result can be interpreted as a reduction from the problem of sampling a general density on a manifold to sampling Brownian motion on the manifold. As a special case of this theorem, it follows that for smooth functions

satisfying LSI with respect to the log barrier metric, RLA can sample from the density proportional to
e^{-f}
restricted to polytopes with the Langevin algorithm. Previous work was able to sample smooth, strongly logconcave densities or smooth, unconstrained densities satisfying isoperimetry, or convex and Lipshitz densities. To describe the results more precisely, we first define manifold self-concordance. Recall that the local norm of a vector

.

\displaystyle-\gamma_{3}^{2}\|v\|_{g}\|w\|_{g}g\preceq D^{2}g(v,w)\preceq\gamma_{3}^{2}\|v\|_{g}\|w\|_{g}g.

1.1
Approach

Langevin diffusion, even for Riemannian manifolds, converges exponentially fast to the target distribution in KL divergence for any target satisfying LSI. To turn this into an algorithm, we bound the “discretization error”, i.e., how much the time-discretized algorithm deviates from the continuous process in terms of relative entropy. The discretization proceeds by taking a step defined by the gradient of
F
at the current point for some positive time, and then performing a Brownian motion for the same length of time.

In the Euclidean setting, the error of discretization can be bounded using smoothness of the target (gradient of
f
is Lipshitz) and LSI itself
[
VW19
]
. In the Riemannian setting, there is a new source of error, namely the gradient step is along the geodesic with initial velocity determined by the gradient of
F
. The error analysis of
[
VW19
]
was cleanly generalized by
[
LE20
]
with an additional Riemannian discretization error term. They then focused on the manifold given by a product of spheres, where quantities of interest can be written quite explicitly.

The general case presents formidable further challenges. This is already illustrated in the papers on Mirror Langevin, which introduce the modified self-concordance condition making the applicability of the analysis rather restricted. Our goal here is to avoid such a condition and preserve the affine-invariance of the process in the analysis.

The discretization error (Lemma
7
) can be viewed as follows for
h(x)=\log(\rho_{t}(x)/\nu(x))

\E_{x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}}\left(\E_{x\sim\rho_{t|0}}\langle\nabla_{x}h(x),\nabla F(x)\rangle-\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}h(x),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\right)

(5)

where
\rho_{t|0}
is the distribution obtained by running Brownian motion for time
t
starting at
\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
, the point obtained by going along the geodesic starting at
x_{0}
with initial velocity
-\nabla F(x_{0})
for time
t
.
The vector
b(t,x_{0})
is the parallel transport of
\nabla F(x_{0})
from
x_{0}
to
\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
. In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it would simply be
\nabla F(x_{0})
and could be factored. Here we will have to bound its change along the geodesic.

Next, note that in the above expression, it would be useful to exchange the gradient and the inner expectation in the second term. In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can write
\nabla\E(h(x))=\E(\nabla h(x))
for an arbitrary function
h
, but this is false in general on a manifold, since the (i) gradient is locally defined and (ii) expectation doesn’t make sense as
\nabla h(x)
is a point in the tangent space at
x
.

To address this, we consider a map
H_{t}:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
,
H_{t}(x_{0})=X_{t}^{x_{0}}
is a (random) map from
x_{0}
to
X_{t}
, the value of the Brownian process started at
x_{0}
at time
t
. Recall that

dX_{t}=A(X_{t})dB_{t}+Z(X_{t})dt

where
A(x)=\sqrt{2g^{-1}(x)}
and
Z(x)=\nabla\cdot(g^{-1}(x))
are defined by the metric.

Then we write

d\E_{\rho_{t|0}}(h(x))=\E_{\rho_{t|0}}dh(x)DH_{t}(x_{0}),

where
DH_{t}(x)
is the Jacobian of the random map
H
. To analyze this, we use an extension of a theorem due to
[
EL94
]
, which will imply that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}h(x),b(t,x_{0})\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{V_{t}}\langle\nabla h(x),V_{t}\rangle,

for a stochastic process
V_{t}
with SDE

\displaystyle dV_{t}=DA(X_{t})(V_{t})dB_{t}+DZ_{t}(V_{t})dt.

(6)

where
V_{0}=b(t,x_{0})
.

Here
V_{t}
will be the action of the Jacobian of the random map
H_{t}
on
b(t,x_{0})
.

Applying this, we get that the discretization error is bounded by

\displaystyle\E_{\rho_{0}}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\langle\nabla h(X_{t}),V(X_{t})-\nabla F(X_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\rho_{t}}\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla h(X_{t})\|^{2}+\E_{\rho_{t}}\|V(X_{t})-\nabla F(X_{t})\|^{2}

using Cauchy-Schwarz and the AM-GM inequality. The first term, with our choice of
h(x)=\log(\rho_{t}(x)/\nu(x))
will be a quarter of the relative Fisher information, which is good — the continuous process decreases the relative entropy by the relative Fisher information and so we still get a decrease overall. Now the second term needs to be bounded, this takes up most of our effort, and will crucially use the self-concordance of
g
as well as the smoothness of
F
. We will do this by showing that the squared norm of
V
grows slowly and so does that of
\nabla F
.

Abstract Manifold and Charts.

A manifold

For each point

\{\partial x_{i}\}
is denoted by
\{dx_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Differential.

For a map
f:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
between two manifolds, the differential
df_{p}
at some point
p\in\mathcal{M}
is a linear map from
T_{p}(\mathcal{M})
to
T_{f(p)}(\mathcal{N})
with the property that for any curve
\gamma(t)
on
\mathcal{M}
with
\gamma(0)=p
, we have

\displaystyle df(\frac{d}{dt}\gamma(0))=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma)(0).

(8)

. As a special case, for a function
f
over the manifold, the differential
df
at some point
p\in\mathcal{M}
is a linear functional over
T_{p}(\mathcal{M})
, i.e. an element of
T^{*}_{p}(\mathcal{M})
. Writing (
8
) for curve
\gamma_{i}
with
\frac{d}{dt}\gamma_{i}(0)=\partial x_{i}
, testing property (
8
), we see

\displaystyle df(\partial x_{i})=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma_{i}(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(\gamma_{i}(0)).

for every
i
, one can see
df=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}dx_{i}
.

Vector field.

A vector field
V
is a smooth choice of a vector
V(p)\in T_{p}(\mathcal{M})
in the tangent space for all
p\in\mathcal{M}
.

Metric and inner product.

A metric tensor on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
is simply a smooth choice of a symmetric bilinear map over
\mathcal{M}
. Alternatively, the metric or dot product
\langle,\rangle
can be seen as a bilinear map over the space of vector fields with the tensorization property, i.e. for vector fields
V,W,Z
and functions
\alpha,\beta
over
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\langle V+W,Z\rangle=\langle V,Z\rangle+\langle W,Z\rangle,

(9)

\displaystyle\langle\alpha V,\beta W\rangle=\alpha\beta\langle V,W\rangle.

(10)

2.3
Manifold Derivatives, Geodesics, Parallel Transport

2.3.1
Covariant derivative

Given two vector fields
V
and
W
, the covariant derivative, also called the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla_{V}W
is a bilinear operator with the following properties:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\alpha_{1}V_{1}+\alpha_{2}V_{2}}W=\alpha_{1}\nabla_{V_{1}}W+\alpha_{2}\nabla_{V_{2}}W,

\displaystyle\nabla_{V}(W_{1}+W_{2})=\nabla_{V}(W_{1})+\nabla_{V}(W_{2}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{V}(\alpha W_{1})=\alpha\nabla_{V}(W_{1})+V(\alpha)W_{1}.

Importantly, the property that differentiates the covariant derivative from other kinds of derivaties over manifold is that the covariant derivative of the metric is zero, i.e.,

\nabla_{V}g=0
for any vector field
V
. In other words, we have the following intuitive rule:

\displaystyle\nabla_{V}\langle W_{1},W_{2}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{V}W_{1},W_{2}\rangle+\langle W_{1},\nabla_{V}W_{2}\rangle.

Moreover, the covariant derivative has the torsion free property, meaning that for vector fields
W_{1},W_{2}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{W_{1}}W_{2}-\nabla_{W_{2}}W_{1}=[W_{1},W_{2}],

where
[W_{1},W_{2}]
is the Lie bracket of
W_{1},W_{2}
defined as the unique vector field that satisfies

\displaystyle[W_{1},W_{2}]f=W_{1}(W_{2}(f))-W_{2}(W_{1}(f))

for every smooth function
f
.

In a local chart with variable
x
, if one represent
V=\sum V_{i}\partial x_{i}
, where
\partial x_{i}
are the basis vector fields, and
W=\sum W_{i}\partial x_{i}
, the covariant derivative is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{V}W

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}V^{i}\nabla_{i}W=\sum_{i}V^{i}\sum_{j}\nabla_{i}(W^{j}\partial x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}V^{i}\sum_{j}\partial_{i}(W^{j})\partial x_{j})+\sum_{i}V^{i}\sum_{j}W^{j}\nabla_{i}\partial x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}V(W^{j})\partial x_{j})+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}V^{i}W^{j}\sum_{k}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial x_{k})=

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(V(W^{k})+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}V^{i}W^{j}\Gamma_{ij}^{k})\partial x_{k}.

where Christoffel symbols
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
are the representations of the Levi-Cevita derivatives of the basis
\{\partial x_{i}\}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\partial x_{j}}\partial x_{i}=\sum_{k}\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial x_{k}

and are given by the following formula:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}g^{km}(\partial_{j}g_{mi}+\partial_{i}g_{mj}-\partial_{m}g_{ij}).

Above,
g^{ij}
refers to the
(i,j)
entry of the inverse of the metric.

2.3.2
Parallel Transport

The notion of parallel transport of a vector
V
along a curve
\gamma
can be generalized from Euclidean space to a manifold. On a manifold, parallel transport is a vector field restricted to
\gamma
such that
\nabla_{\gamma^{\prime}}(V)=0
. By this definition, for two parallel transport vector fields
V(t),W(t)
we have that their dot product
\langle V(t),W(t)\rangle
is preserved, i.e.,
\frac{d}{dt}\langle V(t),W(t)\rangle=0
.

2.3.3
Geodesic

A geodesic is a curve
\gamma
on
\mathcal{M}
is a “locally shortest path”, i.e., the tangent to the curve is parallel transported along the curve:
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}=0
(
\dot{\gamma}
denotes the time derivative of the curve
\gamma
.) Writing this in a chart, one can see it is a second order nonlinear ODE
which locally has a unique solution given initial location and speed.

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\gamma_{k}}{dt^{2}}(t)=-\sum_{i,j}\frac{d\gamma_{i}}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{j}}{dt}\Gamma_{ij}^{k},\ \ \forall k.

(11)

2.3.4
Exponential Map

The exponential
\exp_{p}(v)
at point
p
is a map from
T_{p}(\mathcal{M})
to
\mathcal{M}
, defined as the point obtained on a geodesic starting from
p
with initial speed
v
, after time
1
. We use
\gamma_{t}(x)
to denote the point after going on a geodesic starting from
x
with initial velocity
\nabla F
, after time
t
.

2.3.5
Gradient

For a function
F
over the manifold, it naturally acts linearly over the space of vector fields: given a vector field
V
, the mapping
V(F)
is linear in
V
. Therefore, by Riesz representation theorem, there is a vector field
W
where

\displaystyle V(F)=\langle W,V\rangle

for any vector field
V
. This vector field
W
is defined as the gradient of
F
, which we denote here by
\nabla F
or
\nabla F
. To explicitly derive the form of gradient in a local chart, note that

\displaystyle V(F)=(\sum_{i}\partial x_{i}V_{i})(F)=\sum_{i}V_{i}\partial_{i}F=\sum_{i}V_{i}\sum_{j,m,i}V_{j}g_{jm}g^{mi}\partial_{i}F=\langle V,\sum_{i}g^{mi}\partial_{i}F\rangle.

The above calculation implies

\displaystyle\nabla F=\sum_{m}(\sum g^{mi}\partial x_{i}F)\partial x_{m}.

So
g^{-1}DF
is the representation of
\nabla F
in the Euclidean chart with basis
\{\partial x_{i}\}
.

2.3.6
Manifold Divergence

For the divergence of a vector field
F
on a manifold, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot F

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}dx_{i}(\nabla_{i}F)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}dx_{i}(\sum_{j}\partial_{i}F_{j}\partial x_{j})+\sum_{i}dx_{i}((\sum_{j}F_{j}\Gamma^{k}_{ij})\partial x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}F_{i}+\sum_{i}(\sum_{j}F_{j}\Gamma^{i}_{ij}).

Note that by the formula for the Christoffel symbol
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
for
k=i
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\Gamma^{i}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}g^{im}\partial_{j}g_{im}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\textrm{Tr}(g^{-1}\partial_{j}g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial_{j}\det g}{\det g}.

Putting this back in the formula for divergence, we get

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot F

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}F_{i}+\sum_{j}F_{j}(\sum_{i}\Gamma^{i}_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{i}\left(\sqrt{|g|}\,\partial_{i}F_{i}+\frac{\partial_{i}|g|}{2\sqrt{|g|}}F_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{i}\partial_{i}(\sqrt{|g|}\,F_{i}).

2.3.7
Higher order differentiated tensors

As we described in the previous part, gradient of function
F
can be regarded as an element of the cotangent space
T^{*}_{x}(\mathcal{M})
. Now one can differentiate this tensor w.r.t the Levi-Civita connection to the Hessian which is a 0-2 tensor:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}F(V_{1},V_{2})=\text{Hess}(F)(V_{1},V_{2})=V_{1}(\langle\nabla F,V_{2}\rangle)-\langle\nabla F,\nabla_{V_{1}}V_{2}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{V_{1}}\nabla F,V_{2}\rangle.

Repeating the same process, one can differentiate the Hessian of
F
to obtain. a 0-3 order tensor, which can be regarded as the manifold analog of the third order tensor of partial derivatives of
F
in the Euclidean space:

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}F(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=X_{1}(\nabla^{2}F(X_{2},X_{3}))-\nabla^{2}F(\nabla_{X_{1}}X_{2},X_{3})-\nabla^{2}F(X_{2},\nabla_{X_{1}}X_{3}).

In general, we think of
F
as a function on the manifold, and we assume bounds on the tensors
\nabla F
,
\nabla^{2}F
, and
\nabla^{3}F
as for all vector fields
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
:

\displaystyle|\nabla F(V_{1})|\leq L_{1}\|V_{1}\|,

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}F(V_{1},V_{2})|\leq L_{2}\|V_{1}\|\|V_{2}\|

\displaystyle|\nabla^{3}F(V_{1},V_{2},V_{3})|\leq L_{3}\|V_{1}\|\|V_{2}\|\|V_{3}\|.

Note that the above norms are all the norm of a vector in the tangent space of the manifold, hence taking into account the metric.

2.4
Brownian Motion on Manifold

The metric
g
in the Euclidean chart defines a manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The Brownian motion over
\mathcal{M}
is formally defined in a local chart as the solution to the following Itô Stochastic differential Equation:

\displaystyle dX_{t}=Z(X_{t})dt+A(X_{t})dB_{t},

(12)

for

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2g^{-1}(x)},

\displaystyle Z(x)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(g^{-1}).

dX_{s}=\sqrt{2g^{-1}}dB_{s}+\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})dt
is the Brownian motion over the manifold. Note that Brownian motion on
\mathcal{M}
is just the RLD for
F=const
.
The symbol
\nabla\cdot
above refers to divergence over the manifold applied to each row of the metric
g
,
which in the Euclidean chart, for any vector field
V=\sum_{i}V_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
, is given by

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{j}(\sqrt{|g|}V_{j})

(13)

where
|g|=|\det g|
. Applied to
g^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})_{i}=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{j}(\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij}).

(14)

The drift term
Z(x)
can alternatively be written as
[
Hsu88
]

\displaystyle Z(x)=\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})=-\sum_{ij}\Gamma_{i,j}^{k}g^{ij},

(15)

The symbols
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
refer to the Christoffel symbols regarding the covariant derivative over the manifold that are discussed in the preliminary section.

Brownian motion over the manifold is closely related to the heat equation over the manifold; namely, the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations for this process both reduces to the heat equation over
\mathcal{M}
. We should note that the conventional way to define the Brownian is in a way that the infinitesimal operator of the process becomes half of the Laplace Beltrami operator in
\mathcal{M}
, which differs from our definition by removing the factor
\sqrt{2}
and adding a factor of
1/2
to the drift. In particular, the infinitesimal operator of this process is the Laplace-Beltrami over
\mathcal{M}
, which is stated and proved in the following Lemma.

We start by recalling the definition of infinitesimal operator.

Definition 4
.

For a Markov chain with semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, one can associate an operator
L
, called the infinitesimal operator, which is the generalization of the Laplacian in the discrete case, defined on function
f
as

\displaystyle Lf=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}(P_{t}f-f).

The domain of the operator
L
is exactly the set functions that we are working, in domain of
Q_{t}
(usually bounded functions in some
\ell_{p}
space) for which the above limit is defined. It is often the case that the domain of
L
does not include the whole domain of
Q_{t}
. Also, the operator
L
is usually unbounded, while
(Q_{t})_{t}
are bounded. As an example, the infinitesimal generator of Brownian motion is the Laplacian operator (we skip the detailed discussion on the domain of
L
).

Now we state a lemma about the infinitesimal operator of Brownian motion on a manifold.

Lemma 5
.

The infinitesimal operator of the above process is the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
over the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta f=\nabla\cdot(\nabla f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{i}\partial_{i}(\sum_{j}\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij}\partial_{j}f),

(16)

where
|g|
is the determinant of
g
.

Proof.

Using Itô’s Lemma, for a twice-differentiable function
h
, we have

\displaystyle dh(X_{t})

\displaystyle=DhdX_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}D^{2}h(X_{t})_{ij}[{X}_{i},{X}_{j}]_{t}dt

\displaystyle=(Dh)^{T}dB_{t}+(Dh)^{T}\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})dt+\langle g^{-1},D^{2}h(X_{t})\rangle dt,

which by taking expectation implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\E h(X_{t})\Big|_{t=0}=\Delta h(x).

Hence,
\Delta
is the infinitesimal operator.
∎

Lemma 6

(RLD in the Euclidean chart)
.

Riemmanian Langevin diffusion in the Manifold sense for the distribution
e^{-F}dv_{g}(x)
is the same as it is in the Euclidean definition (
2
) for the distribution
e^{-f}
for

\displaystyle f=F-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(g(x)).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{j}(\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(g^{ij})+\sum_{j}g^{ij}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{j}(\sqrt{|g|})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(g^{ij})+\sum_{j}g^{ij}\langle\partial_{j}g,g^{-1}\rangle.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle g^{-1}Df=g^{-1}(DF-\frac{1}{2}D\mathrm{logdet}(g(x)))=g^{-1}DF-g^{-1}(\frac{1}{2}D\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))).

Noting that

\displaystyle g^{-1}D\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))_{i}=\sum_{j}g^{ij}\partial_{j}(\mathrm{logdet}(g(x)))=\sum_{j}g^{ij}\langle g^{-1},\partial_{j}g\rangle

completes the proof.

∎

2.5
Itô calculus

Given continuous semimartingales
X_{t}
and
A_{t}
, one can integrate one with respect to another to obtain a new semimartingale, namely

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\int_{s_{0}}^{t}X_{s}dA_{s},

which uses the Itô’s construction for stochastic integration. The Itô integral obeys a slightly different chain rule compared to the ordinary calculus. Namely, if
X_{t}
is a vector of
n
semimartingales and given a continuously differentiable function
f
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle f(X_{t})=\sum_{i}\int\partial_{i}f(X_{t})d{{X}_{t}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\int\partial_{i,j}f(X_{t})d[{X}_{i},{X}_{j}]_{t},

where
[{X_{t}}_{i},{X_{t}}_{j}]
is the quadratic variation of
{X_{t}}_{i}
and
{X_{t}}_{j}
.

2.6
Isoperimetry

Definition 5
.

We say the distribution
\nu
satisfies a Log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\alpha
with respect to the metric
g

if for every measure with density
\rho
with respect to
\nu
:

\displaystyle H_{\nu}(\rho)\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}I_{\nu}(\rho),

where
H_{\nu}(\rho)
is the KL divergence between
\rho
and
\nu

and
I_{\nu}(\rho)
is the Fisher information of the measure
\rho
with respect to
\nu
. Note that the metric
g
only affects the Fisher information
I_{\nu}(\rho)
.

Log-Sobelov inequalities in manifolds are a well-studied topic, see e.g.,
[
BGL14
]
for the compact setting and
[
Wan97
]
for the the noncompact Riemannian setting. We note however that LSI in Euclidean space does not translate automatically to LSI with a manifold metric; for example, the uniform density over the unit interval does not have a bounded LSI constant with respect to the metric defined by the log barrier over the interval.

3
The discretization error and a related stochastic process

We use the same notation as e.g. in Erdogdu et al.
[
LE20
]
: We denote the current step of the algorithm by
x_{0}
and its distribution by
\rho_{0}
.
\rho_{t}
is the distribution after taking one discrete step with time parameter
t
.

For a point
x\in\mathcal{M}
, let
\gamma_{t}(x)
be the exponential map at point
x
applied to the vector
\nabla F(x)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}(x)=\exp_{x}(\nabla F).

Let
b(t,x)\in T_{\gamma_{t}(x)}(\mathcal{M})
be the parallel transport of the vector
\nabla F(x)
from
x
to
\gamma_{t}(x)
, i.e.

\displaystyle b(t,x)=P_{x}^{\gamma_{t}(x)}(\nabla F).

Given
x_{0}
, let
\rho_{t|0}
be the density of the Brownian motion on manifold at time
t
with respect to
v_{g}dx
, starting from
\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
, and let
\rho_{t0}
be the joint density of
x_{0}
and
x_{t}
.

To derive the discretization error, we first compute the derivative of the density
\rho_{t}
with respect to time in the following lemma, similar to
[
LE20
]
.

Lemma 7
.

For the derivative of the relative entropy,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H_{\nu}(\rho_{t})=-I_{\nu}(\rho_{t})+\E_{\rho_{0}}\E_{x\sim\rho_{t|0}}\langle\nabla_{x}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),\nabla F(x)\rangle-\E_{\rho_{0}}\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Denoting the density of Brownian motion by
p_{t}(x_{0},x)
, from the chain rule

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t|0}(x)=\frac{d}{dt}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x)

\displaystyle=dp_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x)(\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),\partial_{t})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x),\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}(x_{0})\rangle+\partial_{t}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x)

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x),b(t,x_{0})\rangle+\Delta_{x}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x).

(17)

Now taking the time derivative of the KL divergence to the stationary distribution at time
t
, applying Equation (
17
), and integrating by parts, we have:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H_{\nu}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}(x)\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})dv_{g}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\int\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t|0}(x)\rho_{0}(x_{0})\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})dv_{g}(x)dv_{g}(x_{0})

(by definition of
\rho_{t|0}
)

\displaystyle=\int\int\Delta_{x}\rho_{t|0}(x)\rho_{0}(x_{0})\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})dv_{g}(x)dv_{g}(x_{0})

(Chain rule and Fokker Plank Equation)

\displaystyle\indent-\int\int\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}p_{t}(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}),x),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})dv_{g}(x)dv_{g}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\int\int\langle\nabla\rho_{t|0}(x),\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})dv_{g}(x)dv_{g}(x_{0})

(integration by parts)

\displaystyle\indent-\int\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})dv_{g}(x_{0})

(Leibniz rule)

\displaystyle=-\int\langle\rho_{t}(x)\nabla\log(\rho_{t}),\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})\rangle dv_{g}(x)

(Leibniz rule, Chain rule)

\displaystyle\indent-\int\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})dv_{g}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\int\langle\rho_{t}(x)\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\nu})+\rho_{t}\nabla\log(\nu),\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})\rangle dv_{g}(x)

(Add and Subtract)

\displaystyle\indent-\int\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})dv_{g}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\int\rho_{t}(x)\|\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}(x)}{\nu})\|^{2}dv_{g}(x)+\int\langle\nabla F,\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu})\rangle\rho_{t}(x)dv_{g}(x)

\displaystyle\indent-\int\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle\rho_{0}(x_{0})dv_{g}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-I_{\nu}(\rho_{t})+\E_{\rho_{0}}\E_{x\sim\rho_{t|0}}\langle\nabla_{x}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),\nabla F(x)\rangle-\E_{\rho_{0}}\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle.

(18)

∎

Therefore, defining the discretization error as

\displaystyle\textrm{DE}_{t}

\displaystyle=\E_{\rho_{0}}\E_{x\sim\rho_{t|0}}\langle\nabla_{x}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),\nabla F(x)\rangle-\E_{\rho_{0}}\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{\rho_{t|0}}\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\nu}),b(t,x_{0})\rangle

(19)

we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}H_{\nu}(\rho_{t})=-I_{\nu}(\rho_{t})+\textrm{DE}.

(20)

To bound the discretization error, note that for a fixed
x_{0}
, the distribution
\rho_{t|0}
is a time
t
Brownian increment on the manifold starting from
\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
. Therefore, we consider the SDE for Brownian motion on
\mathcal{M}
starting from
x_{0}
, which is given in Itô form as

\displaystyle dX_{s}=A(X_{s})dB_{s}+Z(X_{s})dt.

(21)

Recall from (
12
),
A=\sqrt{2g^{-1}}
and
Z(x)=\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})=\Gamma_{i,j}^{k}g^{ij}
,
and
\nabla\cdot
is the row-wise divergence over the manifold, defined as
\nabla\cdot(g^{-1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij}).

Taking a stochastic analysis point of view, we wish to employ a formula from
[
EL94
]
which enables us to write the discretization error as an expectation over the randomness of the underlying Brownian motion. Using the notation
h=\log(\rho_{t}/\nu)
, our aim is to write the first term of the discretization error as:

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\gamma_{t}(x_{0})}\E_{x\sim\rho_{t|0}}h(x),b(t,x_{0})\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{X_{t},V_{t}}\langle\nabla h(X_{t}),V_{t}\rangle,

(22)

for some process
V_{s}
, called “the derivative flow,” which obeys the following joint stochastic differential equation with
X_{t}
:

\displaystyle dV_{t}=DA(X_{t})(V_{t})dB_{t}+DZ_{t}(V_{t})dt.

(23)

with initial condition
V_{0}=b(t,x_{0})
, where we define
b(t,x_{0})
to be
\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
. We think of
V_{t}
as an element in the tangent space
T_{X_{t}}(\mathcal{M})
written in the Euclidean chart.
Note that
b(t,x_{0})
is the same as the parallel transport of
\nabla F(x_{0})
from
x_{0}
to
\gamma_{t}(x)
.

To introduce this process, we use the notation
H_{t}(x)
to denote the solution flow of the process
X_{t}
over the manifold; namely,

4
Bounding the discretization error

4.1
Overview

Note that
g^{-1/2}
is the term appearing in the Langevin equation. Hence, because the time derivative of the evolving density w.r.t to the stationary measure
(\rho_{t}/\nu)
is equal to
L
(the infinitesimal operator of the Markov chain), and
L
involves the term
\langle g^{-1/2},D^{2}(\rho_{t}/\nu)\rangle
, one might hope to control this term to bound the discretization error. However, this is not possible for two reasons: First,
g^{-1/2}
is not self-concordant in general; in fact, its self-concordance itself is not an affine-invariant property. Second, the matrix
D^{2}(\rho_{t}/\nu)
is not necessarily positive definite.
Instead of directly trying to control the derivative of the evolving density, we observe that the second and third terms in Equation (
18
) are similar but different in the fact that the order of computing the gradient and taking the expectation with respect to the conditional distribution
\rho_{t|0}
are switched. In the Euclidean space, this change of derivation and integration could be easily handled, but here we need to use a more sophisticated machinery, in its core is the process
V(t)
that we define. As we discuss in section
3
, this allows us to write the discretization term as the expected norm squared between
V
and
\nabla F
in Equation (
26
).

The overall approach from here onward is to bound the discretization error
\E_{\rho_{0}}\E_{X_{t}}\|V(X_{t})-\nabla F(X_{t})\|^{2}
.

Notably, we bypass the argument about self concordance of
g^{-1/2}
in Lemma

by a weaker argument which turns out to be sufficient for our needs.
We start by bounding the norm of
V(t)
for small enough times

V_{t}
process as a stochastic integral along the stochastic curve
X(t)
. More specifically, we use Lemma

which is directly Itô’s rule.

Lemma 15
.

Given stochastic vector field
V(t)
on the stochastic curve

\displaystyle d\langle V,V\rangle_{g}(t)

\displaystyle=2\langle dV(t),V\rangle_{g}+\sum_{i,j,p}V_{i}\partial_{p}g_{ij}V_{j}dX_{p}+\sum_{i,j,p,r}\frac{1}{2}V_{i}V_{j}\partial_{p,r}g_{ij}d[X_{p},X_{r}]+\sum_{i,j,p}\partial_{p}g_{ij}d[V_{i},X_{p}]V_{j}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}d[V_{i},V_{j}].

Proof.

Using Itô’s rule,

\displaystyle d\langle V,V\rangle_{g}(t)

\displaystyle=2\langle dV(t),V\rangle_{g}+\sum_{i,j}V_{i}dg_{ij}V_{j}+\sum_{i,j}d[V_{i},g_{ij}]V_{j}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}d[V_{i},V_{j}]

\displaystyle=2\langle dV(t),V\rangle_{g}+\sum_{i,j,p}V_{i}\partial_{p}g_{ij}V_{j}dX_{p}+\sum_{i,j,p,r}\frac{1}{2}V_{i}V_{j}\partial_{p,r}g_{ij}d[X_{p},X_{r}]+\sum_{i,j,p}\partial_{p}g_{ij}d[V_{i},X_{p}]V_{j}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}d[V_{i},V_{j}]

\displaystyle=2\langle dV(t),V\rangle_{g}+(*).

Our job is to then bound its growth using the self-concordance of the metric. We will see that our generalized self-concordance plays nicely with the terms that we obtain in the stochastic integral using Itô’s rule. Notably, here we also prove a regularity result on the derivative of the square-root of the metric based only on the self-concordance assumption for the metric itself, proved in Section

Lemma 21

On a Hessian manifold, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{i}g_{qj}+\partial_{j}g_{qi}-\partial_{q}g_{ij}=\partial_{q}g_{ij}=(Dg)_{qij},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|_{g}\sqrt{Tr(X_{2}^{T}gX_{2})}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|_{g}\|X_{2}\|_{g}.

Now from the first term we get

\displaystyle|\langle X_{3},\nabla_{X_{1}}\nabla_{X_{2}}J\rangle|\leq L_{3}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\|X_{3}\|

which implies

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{X_{1}}\nabla_{X_{2}}J\|\leq L_{3}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

Now we expand this double covariant derivative:

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{1}}\nabla_{X_{2}}J

\displaystyle=DJ(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))+D^{2}J(X_{1},X_{2})+\frac{1}{2}g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})DJ(X_{2})-\frac{1}{2}g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})J

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}g^{-1}Dg(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))J+\frac{1}{2}g^{-1}Dg(X_{1},X_{2})J

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})DJ(X_{1})+\frac{1}{4}g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})J.

(59)

Therefore

\displaystyle\|D^{2}J(X_{1},X_{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})DJ(X_{2})\|+\frac{1}{4}\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})J\|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1},X_{2})J\|+\frac{1}{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})DJ(X_{1})\|+L_{3}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|

\displaystyle+\|DJ(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))\|+\frac{1}{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))J\|.

(60)

For the first term, using Lemma
29
and again self-concordance

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})DJ(X_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|Dg(X_{1})DJ(X_{2})\|_{g^{-1}}=DJ(X_{2})^{T}g^{\frac{1}{2}}(g^{-\frac{1}{2}}Dg(X_{1})g^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{2}g^{\frac{1}{2}}DJ(X_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|\|DJ(X_{2})\|_{g}\leq\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|(L_{2}+\gamma_{1}\|J\|)\|X_{2}\|.

For the second term, using a similar technique

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})J\|=\|Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})J\|_{g^{-1}}\leq\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|\|Dg(X_{2})J\|_{g^{-1}}\leq\gamma_{1}^{2}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\|J\|.

For the third term:

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1},X_{2})J\|=\|Dg(X_{1},X_{2})J\|_{g^{-1}}\leq\gamma_{3}^{2}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\|J\|.

The fourth term is similar to the first term.
For the fifth term, using Lemma
29
, self concordance and Equation (
58
):

\displaystyle\|DJ(DX_{2}(X_{1}))\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{2}+\gamma_{1}\|J\|)\|DX_{2}(X_{1})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}(L_{2}+\gamma_{1}\|J\|)\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

For the sixth term, similarly

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))J\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{J^{T}g^{1/2}(g^{-1/2}Dg(D(X_{2})(X_{1}))g^{-1/2})^{2}g^{1/2}J}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|D(X_{2})(X_{1})\|\sqrt{J^{T}gJ}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}^{2}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\|J\|.

So overall, ignoring constants, we get

\displaystyle\|D^{2}J(X_{1},X_{2})\|\leq(\gamma_{1}L_{2}+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\|J\|+L_{3})\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

(61)

∎

Lemma 34
.

Given a point
x_{0}
and its mapped version
\gamma_{t}(x_{0})
on a geodesic starting with initial speed
\nabla F(x_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\|b(t,x_{0})-\nabla F(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}))\|\leq tL_{2},

where recall that
b(t,x_{0})
is the parallel transport of
\nabla F(x_{0})
along the geodesic up to time
t
.

Proof.

We can write using fundamental theorem of calculus

\displaystyle\|b(t,x_{0})-\nabla F(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}))\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{t}\partial_{s}\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s=0}^{t}\partial_{s}\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{t}\Big\langle\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0}),\nabla_{\gamma_{s}(x_{0})}F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-\nabla_{\gamma_{s}(x_{0})}b(s,x_{0})\Big\rangle ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s=0}^{t}\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|\|\nabla_{\gamma_{s}(x_{0})}F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))\|ds

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{t}\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|\|\text{Hess}F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}),\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))\|ds

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}\int\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|ds.

(116)

Taking derivatives from both sides implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\|\nabla F(\gamma_{s}(x_{0}))-b(s,x_{0})\|\leq L_{2},

which implies

\displaystyle\|\nabla F(\gamma_{t}(x_{0}))-b(t,x_{0})\|\leq tL_{2}.

(117)

∎

5.2
Square root matrix Regularity: proofs of Lemmas

Here we prove Lemma

Lemma 37
.

Suppose the metric
g
is
\gamma_{1}
normal self-concordant. Then, for the square root matrix
A(x)=\sqrt{2g^{-1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle g,(DA(V))^{2}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{4}n\gamma_{1}^{2}\|V\|_{g}^{2},

\displaystyle\langle g^{1/2},DA(V)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}n\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}.

Proof.

A general trick that we use here is that we reduce the problem to the case where
A
is diagonal at the fixed point
x
for which we want to prove the inequality. We start with the first term. To show this reduction, let
g(x)=U\Gamma U^{T}
be the eigendecomposition of the metric
g
at point
x
. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\langle g,(DA(V))^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=Tr(g(DA(V))^{2})

\displaystyle=Tr(gDA(V)DA(V))

\displaystyle=Tr(U^{T}gUU^{T}DA(V)UU^{T}DA(V)U)

\displaystyle=Tr(\Lambda(U^{T}DA(V)U)^{2}).

But note that because
U
is a fixed matrix, it can go inside the differentiation:

\displaystyle\langle g,(DA(V))^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=Tr(\Lambda(D(U^{T}AU)(V))^{2}).

On the other hand, note that

A=\sqrt{g}=U\sqrt{\Lambda}U^{T},

which means

U^{T}AU=\sqrt{\Lambda}.

Hence, if we define the metric
g^{\prime}(y)=U^{T}g(y)U
for every
y
in the domain of our Euclidean chart (note that
U
is fixed to an orthonormal eigendecomposition of
g
at point
x
), then
g^{\prime}
is diagonal and equal to
\Lambda
at
x
, and further

\displaystyle\langle g,(DA(V))^{2}\rangle=Tr(\Lambda(Dg^{\prime}(V))^{2}).

The above equality means it is enough to bound the RHS instead of LHS, which means without loss of generality, we can assume the metric
g
is some diagonal matrix. For ease of notation later on, let
\Lambda=g^{-1}(x)
be the diagonal inverse matrix of
g
at
x
. This way,
A=\sqrt{\Lambda}
. The point of having diagonal
g
at
x
is that the closed form of
DA(V)
at
x
now takes a simpler form. To compute it, it is enough to take derivative from the sides of

\displaystyle A^{2}=g^{-1},

which implies

\displaystyle DA(V)A+ADA(V)=Dg^{-1}(V)=H,

where
H
need not to be diagonal. Solving the above for
A
gives for every
1\leq i,j\leq n
:

\displaystyle DA(V)_{ij}=H_{ij}/(\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j}).

Now note that the amtrix
\Big((\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j})^{-1}\Big)_{ij}
is PSD. This is because it can be written as the integral over a class of PSD matrices as

\displaystyle\Big((\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j})^{-1}\Big)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\Big(e^{-(\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j})t}\Big)_{ij}dt

\displaystyle\int_{t=0}^{\infty}\text{vec}(e^{-t\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}})\text{vec}(e^{-t\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}})^{T}dt\succeq 0.

On the other hand, using the self concordance of
g
, we can write

\displaystyle H=Dg^{-1}(V)=-g^{-1}Dg(V)g^{-1}\preceq\|V\|_{g}g^{-1}gg^{-1}\leq\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}g^{-1}\leq\|V\|_{g}\Lambda,

and similarly

\displaystyle H\succeq-\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\Lambda.

Hence, applying the Schur product theorem, we see that

\displaystyle DA(V)=\Big((\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j})^{-1}\Big)_{ij}\odot H\preceq\|V\|_{g}\Big((\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}+\sqrt{\Lambda}_{j})^{-1}\Big)_{ij}\odot\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\sqrt{\Lambda},

and similarly

\displaystyle DA(V)\succeq-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\sqrt{\Lambda}.

Now if we denote the
i
th eigenvalue and singular value of a matrix by
\lambda_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
respectively, then the above inequalities imply

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(DA(V))\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i},

\displaystyle\lambda_{(}DA(V))\geq-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i},

which implies for all

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(DA(V))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\gamma_{1}^{2}\|V\|_{g}^{2}\Lambda_{i}.

(122)

Next, we apply the von Neumann’s trace inequality, which states that for two PSD matrices
M
and
N

\displaystyle\langle M,N\rangle\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(M)\sigma_{i}(N).

Applying this inequality for the matrices
M=g^{-1}
and
N=(DA(V))^{2}
at point
x
, we get

\displaystyle\langle g^{-1},(DA(V))^{2}\rangle\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(\Lambda^{-1})\sigma_{i}((DA(V))^{2})\leq\sum\Lambda_{i}^{-1}\frac{1}{4}\gamma_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{i}\|V\|_{g}^{2}=\frac{n\gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}\|V\|_{g}^{2}.

where we used the fact that
g(x)=\Lambda^{-1}
. This completes the proof of the first inequality.

For the second and third inequality, we use a similar trick.
For the third inequality, we apply the von Neumann trace inequality and use (
122

\displaystyle\langle g^{1/2},DA(V)\rangle\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(g^{1/2})\sigma_{i}(DA(V))\leq\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}^{-1/2}\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\Lambda_{i}^{1/2}=\frac{n\gamma_{1}}{2}\|V\|_{g}.

For the third one, we can use the three matrix version of the von Neumann trace inequality:

\displaystyle Tr(g^{1/2}(DA(V))^{2}g^{1/2})\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(g^{1/2})^{2}\sigma_{i}((DA(V))^{2})\leq\|V\|_{g}^{2}\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}^{-1/2}\gamma_{1}^{2}\Lambda_{i}^{-1/2}\Lambda_{i}/4\leq\frac{n\gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}\|V\|_{g}^{2}.

For the second inequality, we use a similar trick. To prove that it is enough to show the problem for when

is diagonal at

\displaystyle\langle g^{1/2},DA(V)\rangle

\displaystyle=Tr(g^{1/2}DA(V))

\displaystyle=Tr(U^{T}g^{1/2}UU^{T}DA(V)U)

\displaystyle=Tr(\Lambda^{1/2}D(U^{T}AU)(V))

\displaystyle=Tr(\Lambda^{1/2}D\sqrt{g^{\prime-1}}(V)).

Now assuming that
g
is diagonal at

\displaystyle Tr(g^{1/2}DA(V))\leq\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(g^{1/2})\sigma_{i}(DA(V))\leq\sum_{i}{\Lambda_{i}}^{-1/2}\|V_{g}\|\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}\leq\frac{n\gamma_{1}}{2}\|V\|_{g}.

where we used that

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}(DA(V))\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{1}\|V\|_{g}\sqrt{\Lambda}_{i}.

∎

5.3
Properties of the Riemann Tensor

Here, our goal is to show a bound on some terms obtained from the Riemann tensor of our self-concordant manifold
\mathcal{M}
, including the Ricci tensor and the sectional curvature. The lower bound on the Ricci curvature is required to guarantee that the process does not blow up in finite time. The bound on sectional curvature is required when we wish to apply Lemma
42
.

We start by the following lemma which illustrates a bound on the subtensor of the Riemann tensor obtained by setting
\gamma^{\prime}
on the second and third slots, for some normal vector
\|\gamma^{\prime}\|=1
:

Lemma 39
.

We have

\displaystyle\|R(v,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}\lesssim(\gamma_{1}^{4}+\gamma_{3}^{4})\|v\|^{2}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}.

Proof.

Expressing the Riemman tensor in the following index form:

\displaystyle R^{\rho}_{\sigma\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=dx^{\rho}(R(\partial_{\mu},\partial_{\nu})\partial\sigma),

we have the following formula:

\displaystyle R^{\rho}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\Gamma^{\rho}_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_{\nu}\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\sigma}+\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\nu\sigma}-\Gamma^{\rho}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\mu\sigma}.

Using this formula, one can see that by extending
\gamma^{\prime}
to a fixed vector field over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can write
R(v,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle R(v,\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=D(g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime})(v)-D(g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v)(\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle+g^{-1}Dg(v)g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}-g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v.

But for the first term, using triangle inequality

\displaystyle\|D(g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime})(v)\|_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g^{-1}Dg(v)g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}+\|g^{-1}D^{2}g(\gamma^{\prime},v)\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\prime T}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})g^{-1}Dg(v)g^{-1}Dg(v)g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}+\gamma^{\prime T}D^{2}g(\gamma^{\prime},v)g^{-1}D^{2}g(\gamma^{\prime},v)\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{2}\|v\|_{g}^{2}\gamma^{\prime T}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}+\|v\|^{2}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}\gamma^{\prime T}g\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma_{1}^{4}\|v\|_{g}^{2}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|_{g}^{4}.

Similarly for the second term:

\displaystyle\|D(g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v)(\gamma^{\prime})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v\|^{2}+\|g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma_{1}^{2}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}\|g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v\|^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{4}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}\|v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma_{1}^{4}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}\|v\|^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{4}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}\|v\|^{2}.

For the third term

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(v)g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})\gamma^{\prime}\|^{2}\leq\gamma_{1}^{4}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}\|v\|^{2}.

For the forth term:

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})g^{-1}Dg(\gamma^{\prime})v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{4}\|\gamma^{\prime}\|^{4}\|v\|^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 40
.

For the sectional curvature, we have

\displaystyle K(e_{i},e_{j})\lesssim(\gamma_{1}^{4}+\gamma_{3}^{4}).

Proof.

Using Lemma
39
:

\displaystyle K(e_{i},e_{j})=\langle R(e_{i},e_{j})e_{j},e_{i}\rangle\leq\|R(e_{i},e_{j})e_{j}\|\lesssim\gamma_{1}^{4}+\gamma_{3}^{4}.

∎

Lemma 41
.

For the Ricci curvature, we have

\displaystyle|Ricci(v,v)|\leq n(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\|v\|_{g}^{2}.

Proof.

Using the previous Lemma

\displaystyle|Ricci(v,v)|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{i}\langle R(v,e_{i})e_{i},v\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}|\langle R(v,e_{i})e_{i},v\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\|R(v,e_{i})e_{i}\|\|v\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i}(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\|v\|_{g}^{2}=n(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\|v\|_{g}^{2}.

(133)

(134)

∎

.

Function
F

3\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+2\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+3\gamma_{1}\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega}+\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}}{\omega\sqrt{\omega}}+\gamma_{3}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}
hessian Lipshitz on the manifold, i.e.

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}F(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})\|\leq 3\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+2\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+3\gamma_{1}\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega}+\gamma^{\prime}\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}}{\omega\sqrt{\omega}}+\gamma_{3}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}.

Proof.

Using the result of Lemma

, all of the terms in Equation (

, which with our current value of

and our bounds on the gradient

implies the coefficient

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega})+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}.

(135)

Hence, it is enough to bound

. To compute it, building upon (

:

\displaystyle D^{2}J(X_{1},X_{2})=D\Big[-g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}DF+g^{-1}D^{2}F(X_{1})\Big](X_{2}).

For the first part, we have

\displaystyle D(-g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}DF)(X_{2})

\displaystyle=g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}DF-g^{-1}D^{2}g(X_{1},X_{2})g^{-1}DF

\displaystyle+g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})g^{-1}DF-g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}D^{2}FX_{2}.

For the second part:

\displaystyle D(g^{-1}D^{2}F(X_{1}))(X_{2})=-g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})g^{-1}D^{2}F(X_{1})+g^{-1}D^{3}F(X_{1},X_{2}).

Now we bound all of these terms one by one. For the first term, using Lemma

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{2})g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}DF\|

\displaystyle\leq\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}DF\|\gamma_{1}\|X_{2}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|g^{-1}DF\|\gamma_{1}^{2}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\leq\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

(137)

For the second term, using Lemma

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}D^{2}g(X_{1},X_{2})g^{-1}DF\|_{g}\leq\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\gamma_{3}^{2}\|DF\|_{g^{-1}}=\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\gamma_{3}^{2}\|J\|_{g}\leq\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|\gamma_{3}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}.

(138)

Third term is similar to the first term. For the forth term, using the result of the derivation in (

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}Dg(X_{1})g^{-1}D^{2}FX_{2}\|\leq\gamma_{1}\|X_{1}\|_{g}\|g^{-1}D^{2}FX_{2}\|_{g}\leq\gamma_{1}\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega}\|X_{1}\|_{g}\|X_{2}\|_{g}.

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}D^{3}F(X_{1},X_{2})\|\leq L_{3}^{\prime}\|X_{1}\|_{2}\frac{1}{\omega}\|X_{2}\|\leq\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}}{\omega\sqrt{\omega}}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}F(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+2\gamma_{1}\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega}+\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}}{\omega\sqrt{\omega}}

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1}(\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}\gamma_{1}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega})+(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{3}^{2})\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}

\displaystyle=3\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+2\gamma_{1}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}}+3\gamma_{1}\frac{L_{2}^{\prime}}{\omega}+\frac{L_{3}^{\prime}}{\omega\sqrt{\omega}}+\gamma_{3}^{2}\frac{L_{1}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\omega}},

Lemma 49
.

For a polytope

with the geometry of log barrier, we have

\displaystyle\big|\langle Dg(v),Dg^{-1}{w}\rangle\big|\leq 4\|v\|_{g}\|w\|_{g}.

(143)

Plugging this into (

∎

7.2
Smoothness

Next, note that for the uniform distribution in the polytope (uniform dist. in the Euclidean chart) we have

Lemma 51
.

The function
\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))
over any
\gamma_{1}
self-concordant Hessian manifold has bounded gradient. Specifically

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))\|_{g}\leq\gamma_{1}n\sqrt{n}.

Proof.

Let
g^{-1}=\sum u_{i}u_{i}^{T}
be a Choleskey factorization of
g^{-1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))\|_{g}

\displaystyle=\|g^{-1}D\log\det(g)\|

\displaystyle=\|g^{-1}\langle g^{-1},Dg\rangle\|

\displaystyle=\|\langle g^{-1},Dg\rangle\|_{g^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum\langle g^{-1},Dg(u_{i})\rangle^{2}}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\sqrt{\langle g^{-1},g\rangle^{2}\sum\|u_{i}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}n\sqrt{n}.

∎

Lemma 52
.

For the geometry of log barrier in the polytope

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{X_{1}}\nabla\mathrm{logdet}(g(x))\|_{g}\leq(n(2\sqrt{n}+1)+18)\|X_{1}\|_{g},

\displaystyle\|DJ(X_{1})\|_{g}\leq(n+20)\|X_{1}\|_{g}.

For brevity, we drop the

Therefore, it is enough to bound the first term, i.e.

. First, for the case of log barrier, we claim for the following is the representation of

7.2

\displaystyle D(J)(X_{1})=D(g^{-1}A^{T}\sigma)(X_{1})

\displaystyle-g^{-1}A^{T}4s_{X_{1}}.\sigma-g^{-1}A^{T}P^{(2)}s_{X_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|s_{X_{2}}\|_{2}\|s_{X_{1}}\|_{2}=\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

For the fifth term

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}A^{T}\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}\big)s_{X_{1}}\|

\displaystyle=\|P(P\odot PS_{X_{2}})s_{X_{1}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|P^{(2)}S_{X_{2}}s_{X_{1}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|S_{X_{2}}s_{X_{1}}\|\leq\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|.

For the last term,

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}A^{T}\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}P\big)s_{X_{1}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|P\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}P\big)s_{X_{1}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}P\big)s_{X_{1}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{s_{X_{1}}^{T}\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}P\big)^{2}s_{X_{1}}}.

(151)

But note that

\displaystyle-\|S_{X_{2}}\|_{2}P\leq PS_{X_{2}}P\leq\|S_{X_{2}}\|_{2}P.

On ther other hand, using the Schur product theorem, we know that if
A\geq 0
and
B\geq C
, then

A\odot B\geq A\odot C
. Applying above, we have

\displaystyle-\|s_{X_{2}}\|_{2}P^{(2)}\leq P\odot(PS_{X_{2}}P)\leq\|s_{X_{2}}\|_{2}P^{(2)},

which implies

\displaystyle-\|s_{X_{2}}\|_{2}I\leq P\odot(PS_{X_{2}}P)\leq\|s_{X_{2}}\|_{2}I.

This in turn implies

\displaystyle(P\odot(PS_{X_{2}}P))^{2}\leq\|X_{2}\|^{2}I.

Applying this to (
151
):

\displaystyle\|g^{-1}A^{T}\big(P\odot PS_{X_{2}}P\big)s_{X_{1}}\|\leq\|X_{2}\|\|s_{X_{1}}\|_{2}=\|X_{2}\|\|X_{2}\|.

∎

Combining all these equations and applying them to (
150
), we have

\displaystyle D^{2}J(X_{1},X_{2})\leq C\sqrt{n}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|,

for some universal constant
C
. Therefore, we showed

\displaystyle|\nabla^{3}\ \mathrm{logdet}(g(x))(X_{1},X_{2})|\leq C_{2}n\sqrt{n}\|X_{1}\|\|X_{2}\|,

for some universal constant
C_{2}
.

7.3
Smallest eigenvalue of the log barrier metric

Here, we show that given a bound on the smallest eigenvalue of the metric imposed by the log barrier, given that the polytope
Ax\geq b
is inside a ball of radius
R
.

Lemma 54
.

For polytope
Ax\geq b
with diameter
R
, we have that the smallest eigenvalue of the metric is at least
1/R
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that for any
x
inside the polytope and unit vector
v
we have
v^{T}A_{x}^{T}A_{x}v\geq\frac{1}{R^{2}}
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\Big(\frac{a_{i}^{T}v}{b_{i}-a_{i}^{T}x}\Big)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{R^{2}}.

Let
I_{i}(x,v)
be the intersection point of the line passing through
x
in direction
v
with the facet
a_{i}^{T}x=b
. Note that the quantity
r_{i}=\frac{b_{i}-a_{i}^{T}x}{a_{i}^{T}v}
is nothing but the length of the segment
[x,I_{i}(x,v)]
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\Big(\frac{a_{i}^{T}v}{b_{i}-a_{i}^{T}x}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{r_{i}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\min_{i}r_{i}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{R^{2}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
R
is the diameter of the polytope.
∎

In the following lemma, we check the strong 1-completeness assumption for the log barrier geometry:

Lemma 55
.

Given a polytope
Ax\geq b
with the log barrier geometry, the heat diffusion defined in (
21
) is strongly 1-complete.

Proof.

First, we check if the SDE (
21
) is complete. Using our lower bound for the Ricci curvature of the log barrier. The first implication of this is that the Brownian motion is non-explosive. Moreover, using Theorem 8.2 in
[
Li94

(note the the drift vector

there is zero in our case, since (

) is complete. Next, we check if it is strongly 1-complete. Using Lemma A.2 in

8
Discussion

We highlight two future research directions.

1.

The first is whether there is a simple modulation method that allows one to convert LSI in one metric to LSI in another metric, e.g., by smoothly and rapidly decaying the target density for points “near” constraints (i.e., with high values of the barrier function). A potential approach is multiplying the target density by a fixed density that is almost constant inside the polytope and rapidly decaying close to the boundary. One natural candidate for the fixed density in the log barrier case is
e^{-e^{\phi}}
, where
\phi
is the log barrier. In the appendix, we verify that this distribution satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality.
[
NT02
]
gives several examples self-concordant Riemannian metrics which can also be adapted.

2.

The second is the following algorithmic problem: Given a metric
g
, an initial point
x_{0}
and a time interval
t
, sample from the distribution of Brownian motion on the manifold starting at
x_{0}
for time
t
. The question of sampling according to the natural manifold measure is a special case of this Brownian increment sampling problem for a sufficiently large time increment.

Appendix: Log-Sobolev Inequality for log barrier metric

Lemma 58
.

Define the probability measure
\nu
with support
(0,1)
with density w.r.t to the Lebesgue measure as
d\nu(x)\coloneqq p(x)dx\sim\frac{dx}{e^{1/(x(1-x))}}
. Then,
\nu
satisfies a Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality with respect to the log barrier with constant
\Omega(1)
.

Proof.

It is enough to check LSI for intervals of the form
S\coloneqq(0,y)
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{y}\frac{1}{e^{1/(y(1-y))}}\lesssim\frac{y^{2}}{e^{1/(y(1-y))}}\sim y^{2}p(y).

(The notation
\sim
implies equality up to constants).
On the other hand, we write the definition of the log-Sobolev constant using the
\epsilon
neighborhood of the set
S
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha}=\sup_{S}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\nu(S^{+\epsilon})}{d\nu(S)\sqrt{\log(1/\nu(S))}},

where
S^{+d}
is the set of points outside
S
within distance at most
d
of it.
Now since the metric at point
y
is
g(y)=1/y^{2}
, we get that the length of the interval
S^{+d}
is
\Theta(y)
, hence

\displaystyle\nu(S^{+d})=\Theta(\epsilon yp(y)).

(155)

Overall, this implies

\displaystyle\sqrt{\alpha}=\Omega(\frac{\epsilon yp(y)}{\epsilon y^{2}p(y)\sqrt{\log(y^{2}p(y))}}).

Also note that

\displaystyle\log(1/(y^{2}p(y)))\lesssim\log(1/y)+\log(1/e^{1/(y(1-y))})\lesssim\log(1/y)+1/y\lesssim 1/y,

which means

\displaystyle\alpha=\Omega(1).

∎
