Quantum dynamics of Bose-polaron in a d \displaystyle d -dimensional Bose Einstein condensate
M. Miskeen Khan, H. Terças, J. T. Mendonça, J. Wehr, C. Charalambous, M. Lewenstein, M. A. Garcia-March

Abstract
We study the quantum motion of an impurity atom immersed in a Bose Einstein condensate in arbitrary dimension. The Bogoliubov excitations of the Bose Einstein condensate act as a bosonic bath for the impurity. We present a detailed derivation of the d \displaystyle d -dimensional Langevin equations that describe the quantum dynamics of the system, and of the associated generalized tensor that describes the spectral density in the full generality. When the impurity is not trapped, we calculate the mean square displacement, showing that the motion is super diffusive. We obtain also explicit expressions for the super diffusive coefficient in the small and large temperature limits. We find that, in the latter case, the maximal value of this coefficient is the same in all dimensions. We study also the behaviour of the average energy and compare the results for various dimensions. In the trapped case, we study squeezing and find that the stronger position squeezing can be obtained in lower dimensions. We quantify the non-Markovianity of the particle’s motion, and find that it increases with dimensionality.

Introduction

The concept of a quasiparticle plays a fundamental role in physics, allowing to greatly simplify the description of numerous complex phenomena. A paradigmatic classical problem, in which quasiparticles appear, is the study of an electron interacting with a surrounding dielectric crystal. Its dynamics can be approximated by a much simpler dynamics of an electron with a different mass, called polaron, traveling through free space. This classical theory (see historical note in
Dykman and Rashba 2015
) keeps inspiring new developments in physics. In particular, it plays an important role in the recent studies of the Bose polaron – the quasiparticle associated with an impurity immersed in a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC).

Bose polarons were investigated in diverse experiments on impurities immersed in bosonic gases. To begin with, the quantum dynamics of impurities in Bose gases were examined in
Catani et al. 2012
;
Spethmann et al. 2012

Hohmann et al. 2015

Fukuhara et al. 2013

Ospelkaus et al. 2006

Jørgensen et al. 2016

Hu et al. 2016

Yan et al. 2020

II
Hamiltonian and Bogoliubov Modes
\displaystyle-

We start by considering an impurity atom with mass
\displaystyle m_{\rm I}
immersed in a
\displaystyle d
-dimensional ultracold gas of
\displaystyle N
bosons. The interaction between the bosons occurs through the scattering potential
\displaystyle V_{\rm B}(\bf{r})
. We denote by
\displaystyle\Psi(\bf{r})
(
\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})
) the annihilation (creation) field operator of the atoms at the position
\displaystyle\bf{r}
, which fulfills canonical bosonic commutation relations
\displaystyle[\Psi(\mathbf{r}),\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})]=\delta(\bf{r}-\bf{r}{{}^{\prime}})
. The bosonic density therefore takes the form
\displaystyle n_{\rm B}=\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Psi(\mathbf{r})
. The total Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle H=H_{\rm I}+H_{\rm B}+H_{\rm BB}+H_{\rm IB}.

(1)

Here, the four terms represent the Hamiltonians of the impurity being kept in an external potential
\displaystyle U_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})
, bosons in a potential
\displaystyle V_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})
, the boson-boson atomic interaction and the impurity-boson atomic interaction, respectively. Within the second quantization formalism, their explicit forms are
Lampo et al. 2017

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm I}=\frac{\mathbf{P}^{2}}{2m_{\rm I}}+U_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r}),

(2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm B}=\int d^{d}\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\left(\frac{\mathbf{P}_{\rm B}^{2}}{2m_{\rm B}}+V_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})\right)\Psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}},

(3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm BB}=g_{\rm B}\int d^{d}\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Psi(\mathbf{r})\Psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 19.91684pt=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k{{}^{\prime}}},\mathbf{q}}V_{\rm B}(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{k{{}^{\prime}}-q}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k+q}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k{{}^{\prime}}}}a_{\mathbf{k}},

(4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm IB}=g_{\rm IB}n_{\rm B}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}V_{\rm IB}\rho_{\rm I}(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{k-q}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}.

(5)

In the above expressions,
\displaystyle V_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})
denotes the external potential experienced by the Bosons which are contained in a (box of) volume
\displaystyle V
of the hyperspace. From now on, we assume a homogenous BEC, that is,
\displaystyle V_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})=0
along the direction of the impurity motion. For the impurity, the external potential is
\displaystyle U_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})
, and we will study two cases: a free or a parabolically trapped impurity. The bosonic operators
\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger})
destroy (create) a boson of mass
\displaystyle m_{\rm B}
having wave vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{k}
and energy
\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\hbar k\right)^{2}/(2m_{B})-\mu
, measured from its chemical potential
\displaystyle\mu
. In addition, the quantities
\displaystyle V_{\rm B}
and
\displaystyle V_{\rm IB}
represent the Fourier transform
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q}}[.]
of the impulsive (contact) boson-boson and impurity-boson interactions respectively. Their explicit expressions are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\rm B}(\mathbf{q})=g_{\rm B}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q}}[\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}{{}^{\prime}})],

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\rm IB}(\mathbf{q})=g_{\rm IB}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q}}[\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}{{}^{\prime}})].

(7)

Here, the respective coupling strengths are
\displaystyle g_{\rm B}
and
\displaystyle g_{\rm IB}
. They are mainly determined by the corresponding scattering lengths and densities
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003
;
Lewenstein et al. 2012
and their explicit expressions will be given later. We assume that the impurity density is low enough, which allows us to neglect the terms describing the interaction between impurities. The (dimensionless) density of the impurity in the momentum space is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho_{\rm I}(\mathbf{q})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\mathbf{r}{{}^{\prime}}\penalty\ e^{-i\mathbf{q}.\mathbf{r}{{}^{\prime}}}\delta(\mathbf{r}{{}^{\prime}}-\mathbf{r}).

(8)

Next, for the sake of completeness, we review how to construct the Fröhlich Hamiltonian, which describes the linear interaction between the motional position quadrature of the impurity and the Bogoliubov bosonic modes of BEC. The goal is to show that such linear regime allows us to model the impurity as a quantum Brownian particle which experiences an effective environment formed by the Bogoliubov Bosonic modes of the BEC (as derived in
Lampo et al. 2017
).

Given that the Hamiltonian of the Bosonic interaction is not in bilinear form, we linearize it and replace the creation and annihilation operators by their average values
\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{0}}
. Below a critical temperature, the atoms mainly occupy the ground state forming a BEC, however, we neglect terms proportional to
\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{k}}
(
\displaystyle\mathbf{k}\neq 0
) i.e. the number of particles out of the ground state. In order to diagonalise the bath modes, we further apply the following Bogoliubov transformation

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{k}}=u_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}-v_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{-k}}^{\dagger}\penalty\ ,\quad a_{\mathbf{-k}}=u_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{-k}}-v_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}.

(9)

The transformation coefficients are

\displaystyle\displaystyle u_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{0}V_{\rm B}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}+1\right),

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle v_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+n_{0}V_{\rm B}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}-1\right),

(11)

where
\displaystyle n_{0}
is the (constant) density of particles in the ground state of the homogeneous gas and the Bogoliubov energy spectrum is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{k}}=\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{k}}=\hbar c\left|\mathbf{k}\right|\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\xi\mathbf{k}\right)^{2}},

(12)

with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\xi=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2g_{\rm B}m_{\rm B}n_{0}}}\penalty\ ,\quad c=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2}m_{\rm B}\xi},

(13)

representing the coherence length and the speed of sound, respectively. The effective bath Hamiltonian under such transformation reads
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm B}+H_{\rm BB}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}E_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{k}}.

(14)

Here, we have neglected the non-operator terms which simply shift the energy level of the atoms in BEC. We approximate the bosons-impurity interaction in a similar way. Further, we only keep the terms proportional to
\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{0}}
, where the macroscopic occupation of the condensate holds as expressed by the condition
\displaystyle N_{i\neq 0}\ll N_{0}
. By discarding the terms which might cause non-physical instabilities and those bilinear in
\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{0}}
, we obtain the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm IB}=n_{0}V_{\rm IB}+\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}}{V}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\rho(\mathbf{k})V_{\rm IB}(a_{\mathbf{k}}+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}).

(15)

The first term represents simply the constant mean field energy and provides the shift of the energy of polaron. For the purposes here, it can be neglected. By further invoking the transformation from Eq. (
9
) into Eq. (
15
), one gets

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm IB}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}}{V}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\rho(\mathbf{k})V_{\rm IB}\left(u_{\mathbf{k}}-v_{\mathbf{k}}\right)(b_{\mathbf{k}}+b_{\mathbf{-k}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}}{V}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\rho(\mathbf{k})V_{\rm IB}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}}(b_{\mathbf{k}}+b_{\mathbf{-k}}^{\dagger}),

(16)

where once again we have discarded the non-operator terms. Since the density is dependent on the position of the impurity, we insert its expression into Eq. (
16
), which results in the interaction between impurity position and bath variables given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm IB}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}V_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}(b_{\mathbf{k}}+b_{\mathbf{-k}}^{\dagger}).

(17)

Importantly,
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{k}}
contains the impurity-Boson coupling coefficient, and takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{k}}=g_{\rm IB}\sqrt{\frac{n_{0}}{V}}\left[\frac{(\xi k)^{2}}{(\xi k)^{2}+2}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(18)

The interaction in the Eq. (
17
) is the interaction part of the Fröhlich Hamiltonian. Under the assumption that one restricts the calculation to the limit
\displaystyle\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\ll 1
, the interaction reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm IB}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}V_{\mathbf{k}}\left(1+i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}\right)(b_{\mathbf{k}}+b_{\mathbf{-k}}^{\dagger}).

(19)

We further simplify it by redefining the Bogoliubov modes operator
\displaystyle b_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow b_{\mathbf{k}}-v_{\mathbf{k}}/E_{k}\mathbf{1}
, to absorb terms proportional to identity operator. After all these simplifications, the final form of the Hamiltonian of the impurity in a BEC reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle H=H_{\rm I}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}E_{\mathbf{k}}b_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{k}}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\hbar\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \mathbf{\pi_{\mathbf{k}}},

(20)

with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathbf{k}}=\mathbf{k}V_{\mathbf{k}}/\hbar\penalty\ ,\quad\mathbf{\pi_{\mathbf{k}}}=i\left(b_{\mathbf{k}}-b_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\right).

(21)

The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
20
) describes a linear interaction between the impurity center of mass motion and a bath of the Bogoliubov modes of a BEC. It thus has a form of the QBM Hamiltonian, in which the impurity plays the role of a Brownian particle while the modes of BEC act as an effective Bosonic environment as represented by its (dimensionless) momenta
\displaystyle\mathbf{\pi_{\mathbf{k}}}
.

III
d-Dimensional Spectral Density
\displaystyle-

The Hamiltonian derived in the previous section allows us to study the quantum dynamics of an impurity, taking advantage of the analogy with the QBM model. To characterise the bath, we write its self-correlation function as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathcal{C}}}(\tau)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\hbar\underline{\underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}}\left\langle\mathbf{\pi_{\mathbf{k}}}(\tau)\mathbf{\pi_{\mathbf{k}}}(0)\right\rangle.

(22)

Here
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}}=\underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}\penalty\ \underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}^{T}
is the coupling tensor. The environment is made of bosons whose state at finite temperature
\displaystyle T
follows the Bose-Einstein statistics. Therefore, the mean number of bosons in each of the modes reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle b_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\exp(\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{k}}/k_{\rm B}T)-1}.

(23)

In order to calculate the correlation, we invoke the expression for the dimensionless momenta and make use of Eq. (
23
) and Eq. (
22
) which results in

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathcal{C}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}\hbar\underline{\underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}}\left[\coth\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathbf{k}}}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right)\cos(\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)-i\sin(\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\tau)\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\underline{\underline{\nu}}(\tau)-i\underline{\underline{\lambda}}(\tau),

(24)

where the real and imaginary part of the self-correlation function are given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\nu}}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)\coth\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right)\cos(\omega\tau)d\omega,

(25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\lambda}}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)\sin(\omega\tau)d\omega=-m_{\rm I}\underline{\underline{\dot{\Gamma}}}(\tau).

(26)

Moreover, the damping kernel
\displaystyle\Gamma(t)
can be obtained from

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(t)=(1/m_{\rm I})\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega(1/\omega)\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)\cos(\omega t).

(27)

In the above expressions we have introduced the spectral density
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)
, which fully characterises the effects of the bath on the system. This information is contained in the coupling strengths of the various modes of the bath with the system. The spectral density is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k\neq 0}}\hbar\underline{\underline{g_{\mathbf{k}}}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathbf{k}}).

(28)

In the present case, the couplings of the impurity (system) and bosons (bath) interaction can be derived from first principles. It is therefore possible to obtain the exact expression for the spectral density. This scenario is in contrast to various complicated system-bath interactions, where it is hard to get an exact form of the spectral densities, such as in bulk mechanical structure akin to opto-mechanical setup
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
;
Khan et al. 2016
;
Gröblacher et al. 2015
. While the case of spectral density in
\displaystyle 1d
has been studied in
(
Lampo et al. 2017
)
, here we derive it systematically in
\displaystyle d=\{1,2,3\}
dimensions of the quasi momentum space. In appendix
A
, we derive the expression for the spectral density tensor, which is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\underline{J_{\rm d}}}(\omega)=d^{-1}\left[\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)\right]\underline{\underline{I}}\penalty\ _{\rm d\times d},

(29)

where
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{I}}\penalty\ _{\rm d\times d}
is the identity matrix and the scalar function
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)
in d dimensions is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)=\left(\frac{S_{\rm d}\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{d}(\eta_{\rm d})^{2}(\Lambda_{\rm d})^{d+2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{\left[\left(\frac{m_{\rm B}}{\left[g_{\rm B,d}\right]^{\left[\frac{d}{d+2}\right]}n_{0,\rm d}}\right)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\Lambda_{\rm d})^{2}}+1}-1\right)\right]^{(\frac{d+2}{2})}}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\Lambda_{\rm d})^{2}}+1}\right)}\right).

(30)

For
\displaystyle d=1,\penalty\ 2
and
\displaystyle 3
we have
\displaystyle S_{1}=2,\penalty\ S_{2}=2\pi
and
\displaystyle S_{3}=4\pi
respectively. Moreover, we have defined the
\displaystyle d-
dependent characteristic frequency
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm d}=(g_{\rm B,d}n_{0,{\rm d}})/\hbar
because the boson-boson coupling and the density differ in various dimensions. We also write the impurity-boson coupling in the units of the boson-boson coupling as
\displaystyle\eta_{\rm d}=(g_{\rm IB,d}/g_{\rm B,d})
. Such characterization allows us to study the long-time dynamics of the impurity in the following sense: one can identify two opposite limits in the above expression i.e.
\displaystyle\omega\ll\Lambda_{\rm d}
and
\displaystyle\omega\gg\Lambda_{\rm d}
in which
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm d}
appears naturally as the characteristic cut-off frequency which distinguishes between the low and the high frequencies of the bath. The low-frequency behaviour is attributed to the linear part of the Bogoliubov spectrum
Lampo et al. 2017
. From the Tauberian theorem
Nixon 1965
, one can obtain the long-time behaviour of a function which is determined by the low frequency response of its Laplace transform. The above low-frequency choice is therefore a natural way of studying the dynamics perturbed by the bath that acts beyond the very short transient regime. Note that, for
\displaystyle d=1
the above expression reduces to the one dimensional spectral density used in
(
Lampo et al. 2017
)
. To the lowest order of
\displaystyle\omega/\Lambda_{\rm d}
, the expression for the spectral density with the low frequency response of the bath is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)\simeq\frac{S_{\rm d}\left(\eta_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}{2(2\pi)^{d}}\left(\frac{m_{\rm B}}{\left[g_{\rm B,d}\right]^{\left[\frac{d}{d+2}\right]}n_{0,\rm d}}\right)^{(\frac{d+2}{2})}\times\omega^{d+2}.

(31)

This expression gives the scaling of the frequency for the spectral density function in all dimensions. We point out that due to the spherical symmetry of the bath, the spectral density tensor is a diagonal matrix. As a consequence, the noise and damping kernels given by Eq. (
25
) and Eq. (
27
) are also diagonal. We now give further details about the other parameters involved. In dimension
\displaystyle d
, the coupling constant
\displaystyle g_{\rm B,{\rm d}}
and boson density
\displaystyle n_{0,{\rm d}}
have the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\rm B,{\rm d}}=\frac{S_{\rm d}\hbar^{2}a_{3}}{m_{\rm B}\left(\sqrt{\hbar/m_{\rm B}\omega_{\rm d}}\right)^{3-d}},\penalty\ \quad n_{0,{\rm d}}=\left(n_{0,{\rm 1}}\right)^{d},

(32)

their units being
\displaystyle{\rm J\cdot m}^{d}
and
\displaystyle{\rm m}^{-d}
respectively. Here we have written these expressions in terms of the three-dimensional scattering length
\displaystyle a_{3}
and one-dimensional density
\displaystyle n_{0,1}
. We have further assumed transverse confinement of the boson gas with a harmonic trap having a Gaussian ground state
Alexander et al. 2012
, which makes the cases
\displaystyle d<3
to be the quasi one- and two-dimensional. We emphasize that the parabolic potential is introduced only in the direction transverse to the direction under investigation. The dynamics we study is thus still confined to a box potential, making the homogeneity of Boson gas to be a valid approximation. Moreover, the zero point fluctuations of the condensate are characterised by the trapped frequencies
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm d}=\left\{\omega_{1}=\omega_{\perp},\omega_{2}=\omega_{z},\omega_{3}=0\right\}
. For instance, when we consider one, two- and three-dimensional condensate to be confined in the
\displaystyle x
direction, in the
\displaystyle x-y
plane or in the volume
\displaystyle x-y-z
respectively, the explicit form of the potential may be given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r})=\begin{cases}(1/2)m_{\rm B}\omega_{\perp}^{2}\left(y^{2}+z^{2}\right),\quad\text{for }d=1\\
(1/2)m_{\rm B}\omega_{z}^{2}\left(z^{2}\right),\quad\quad\text{for }\penalty\ d=2\\
0\penalty\ ,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{for }\penalty\ d=3.\\
\end{cases}

(33)

Note that for
\displaystyle d=3
there is no parabolic confinement and therefore the expressions are independent of the trapping frequency. It is then possible to define a characteristic time
\displaystyle\tau_{\rm d}
which is raised to the power
\displaystyle d
in the expression for the spectral function, which is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)=m_{\rm I}\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\omega^{d+2}\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\equiv\frac{S_{\rm d}\left(\eta_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}{2(2\pi)^{d}m_{\rm I}}\left(\frac{m_{\rm B}}{\left[g_{\rm B,d}\right]^{\left[\frac{d}{d+2}\right]}n_{0,\rm d}}\right)^{(\frac{d+2}{2})}.

(34)

It is evident that the spectral density has a super-ohmic dependence on the frequency in all dimensions. It is therefore expected that the bosonic bath would induce a non-Markovian dynamics of the impurity. Moreover, it can be shown that the increasing nature of the spectral density makes certain quantities, such as momentum dispersion, diverge. It is therefore customary to define the ultraviolet cut-off
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\omega,\Lambda_{\rm d})
in order to suppress the contribution of high frequencies. After this, the expression for the spectral density reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm d}(\omega)=m_{\rm I}\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\omega^{d+2}\mathcal{K}(\omega,\Lambda_{\rm d}).

(35)

In the following we study the impurity dynamics, varying the dimension and d-dependent cut-off function in the expression of the spectral density.

IV
Dynamics and Control
\displaystyle-

To study the quantum dynamics of the impurity atom, we write down the equations of motion in the Heisenberg picture. The impurity, which is immersed in a bath of dimension
\displaystyle d
, is further trapped by a harmonic potential. In dimensions
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 2
and
\displaystyle 3
, the potential is
\displaystyle U_{\rm ext}(x)=(1/2)m_{\rm I}\Omega^{2}\left(x^{2}\right)
,
\displaystyle U_{\rm ext}(x,y)=(1/2)m_{\rm I}\Omega^{2}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)
and
\displaystyle U_{\rm ext}(x,y,z)=(1/2)m_{\rm I}\Omega^{2}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\right)
respectively. Here, we have assumed equal trapping frequency in all the directions available to the impurity dynamics. The free QBM is therefore characterised by setting
\displaystyle\Omega=0
in all the cases. We write the equation of motion in vectorial form as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\dot{X}}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H,\underline{X}(t)\right]=\frac{\underline{\dot{P}}(t)}{m_{\rm I}},

(36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\dot{P}}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H,\underline{P}(t)\right]=-m_{\rm I}\Omega^{2}\underline{\dot{X}}(t)-\hbar\sum_{k}\underline{g_{k}}\pi_{k}(t),

(37)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{b}_{k}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H,b_{k}(t)\right]=-i\omega_{k}b_{k}(t)-\underline{g_{k}}^{T}\underline{X}(t),

(38)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H,b_{k}^{\dagger}(t)\right]=-i\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}(t)-\underline{g_{k}}^{T}\underline{X}(t).

(39)

Here
\displaystyle H
represents the Hamiltonian of the system given by Eq. (
20
). In general, the dimension of the vectors in the above equations is
\displaystyle d
, the dimension of the bath.
In appendix
B
, we combined these equations to obtain an equation of motion for the impurity position vector:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\ddot{X}}(t)+\Omega^{2}\underline{X}(t)+\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(t-s)\underline{X}(s)ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1/m_{\rm I})\underline{B}(t).

(40)

Here, the quantum Brownian stochastic force
\displaystyle\underline{B}(t)
stands for

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{B}(t)=\sum_{k}i\hbar\underline{g_{k}}(b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{k}t}-b_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}t}).

(41)

In any given dimension, the diagonal damping kernel when equal weighting for all the directions is taken, will suffice to study the motion along any one of the coordinate axis. However for different dimensions, the spectral density will bring different level of
super-ohmicity
, as stated by Eq. (
35

As a result, the impurity motion is different for different dimensions, despite being studied along one particular coordinate axis. One can then aim to study such a motion by constructing a unit vector
\displaystyle\left(1,0,0\right)
(i.e. along the x direction) and taking dot product with Eq. (
40

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ddot{x}(t)+\Omega^{2}x(t)+\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{\rm d}^{xx}(t-s)x(s)ds=\left(\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}}\right)B^{x}(t).

(42)

Note that the tensor components satisfy
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm d}^{xy}=\Gamma_{\rm d}^{xz}=0
. Additionally, from the structure of the integral term in the Eq. (
42

\displaystyle\omega
) the memory damping kernel reduces to a Dirac delta function and describes the time-local dynamics of the standard damped harmonic oscillator. The time local behaviour is violated in similar experimental configuration

;
Reitz et al. 2019

Zhang et al. 2016

The formal solution to Langevin-like Eq. (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times G_{\rm 2,d}(t-s)B^{x}(s),

(43)

where the Green’s functions

(44)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},{\rm d}}\left[G_{\rm 2,d}(t)\right]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{S}^{2}+\Omega^{2}+\mathcal{S}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},{\rm d}}\left[\Gamma_{\rm d}^{xx}(t)\right]}.

(45)

Moreover, they satisfy the following initial conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{\rm 1,d}(0)=1,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \dot{G}_{\rm 1,d}(0)=0,

(46)

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{\rm 2,d}(0)=0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \dot{G}_{\rm 2,d}(0)=1.

(47)

IV.1
Untrapped Case

Let us first study the free QBM, that is the untrapped case
\displaystyle\Omega=0
. The quantities of interest in this case are the mean squared displacement
\displaystyle{\rm MSD_{d}}(t)
(see definition below, Eq. (
53
)) and the average kinetic energy
\displaystyle E_{\rm d}(t)
of the impurity. The motion is fully characterised by the functions
\displaystyle G_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
which are the inverse Laplace transform of Eq. (

45
) respectively. Exact analytical expressions for these functions are hard to obtain. Note however, that the Laplace transform of both of these functions are expressed in terms of the Laplace transform of the damping kernel. In the regime of interest
\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}|\ll\Lambda_{\rm d}
, which characterises the low frequency response, we have approximately

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},{\rm d}}\left[\Gamma^{xx}_{\rm d}(t)\right]=d^{-2}(\Lambda_{\rm d}\tau_{\rm d})^{d}\mathcal{S}.

(49)

We therefore obtain the asymptotic expressions for the Laplace transforms of the position and momentum propagators

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S},\rm d}\left[G_{1,\rm d}(t)\right]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\rm d}\mathcal{S}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\left[G_{2,\rm d}(t)\right]=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\rm d}\mathcal{S}^{2}},

(50)

where
\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm d}=1+d^{-2}(\Lambda_{\rm d}\tau_{\rm d})^{d}
. Their time domain representations read

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{1,\rm d}(t)=1/\alpha_{\rm d}\penalty\ ,\quad G_{2,\rm d}(t)=t/(\alpha_{\rm d}).

(51)

We note that such expressions do not satisfy the boundary conditions stated in Eq. (
46
) and Eq. (
47
). However, this is justified since the above solution refers to long-time behaviour. Several algorithms exist for the numerical computation of the inverse Laplace transform of an arbitrary function. Here we employ the Zakian method

to compute the inverse Laplace transform of Eq. (
44
) and Eq. (
45
). This method approximates the inverse Laplace transform
\displaystyle f(t)
of a function
\displaystyle F(\mathcal{S})
through

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(t)\simeq\frac{2}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\Re}\left[k_{j}F(\Xi_{j}/t)\right],

(52)

with the values of the complex parameters
\displaystyle k_{j}
and
\displaystyle\Xi_{j}
given in Ref.
Wang and Zhan 2015
. In order to check the equivalence between the asymptotic form of
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
and its Zakian approximation, we plot both in Fig.

IV.1.1
Mean Square Displacement

In this section we discuss the mean squared displacement (MSD) of the impurity motion which is a measurable quantity in cold-atom experiments
Catani et al. 2012
. The MSD is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm MSD}_{x,\rm d}(t)=\left\langle\left[x(t)-x(0)\right]^{2}\right\rangle.

(53)

The expression of the MSD for the generalised Heisenberg-Langevin equations is given in the Appendix
C
. For the system at hand, we employ the asymptotic expressions of the Green’s functions to evaluate the MSD in different dimensions. Its dynamical part is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm MSD}_{x,\rm d}(t)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{\rm d}}\right)^{2}\langle\dot{x}(0)^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2(\alpha_{\rm d}m_{\rm I})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}du\int_{0}^{t}dv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times(t-u)(t-v)\langle\{B^{x}(u),B^{x}(v)\}\rangle.

(54)

Additionally, using the diagonal form of the noise tensor given in Eq. (
25
), one can obtain the relation between the correlation characterised by the positive commutator
\displaystyle\langle\{B^{x}(u),B^{x}(v)\}\rangle
and the component
\displaystyle\nu^{xx}(t)
of the noise kernel (fluctuation-dissipation relation)
Lampo et al. 2019
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\{B^{x}(u),B^{x}(v)\}\rangle=2\hbar\nu^{xx}(u-v).

(55)

Substituting the
\displaystyle d
-dimensional spectral function with a sharp cutoff into the noise component we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm MSD}_{x,\rm d}(t)=\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{\rm d}}\right)^{2}\langle\dot{x}(0)^{2}\rangle+\frac{\hbar\penalty\ d^{-1}\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}}{m_{\rm I}(\alpha_{\rm d})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}du\int_{0}^{t}dv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Lambda_{\rm d}}d\omega(t-u)(t-v)\coth{\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right)}\cos{\left[\omega\left(u-v\right)\right]}\omega^{d+2}.

(56)

By performing the two-dimensional integration over the time variables
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
, followed by an integration over the variable
\displaystyle\omega
, we evaluate the expression for the low temperature regime, where
\displaystyle\coth\left(\hbar\omega/2k_{\rm B}T\right)\rightarrow 1
holds. In the long time limit, the resulting expression for the MSD is dominated by the terms proportional to
\displaystyle t^{2}
and its explicit expression turns out to be

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm MSD}_{x,\rm d}^{\rm LT}(t)=\left[\langle\dot{x}(0)^{2}\rangle+\frac{\hbar\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{d+1}}{m_{\rm I}d(d+1)}\right]\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{\rm d}}\right)^{2}

(57)

In the regime which fulfills the conditions stated above, the MSD is proportional to the square of the time for all dimensions. In the normal diffusion scenario, the MSD shows a linear dependence on time. If, on the contrary, the MSD is non-linear in time, proportional to
\displaystyle t^{\alpha}
with an exponent higher than one, the diffusion is called anomalous and the motion is called superdiffusive. In the present case, superdiffusion is a consequence of the super-ohmic spectral density in every dimension. The coefficient in the second term is called the superdiffusion coefficient
\displaystyle D_{x,\rm d}
and can be interpreted as the average of the square of the speed with which the impurity runs away. We thus have

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x,\rm d}^{\rm LT}=\frac{\hbar\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{d+1}}{m_{\rm I}d(d+1)\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}.

(58)

One can perform a similar analysis of the high temperature regime, which is followed by the approximation
\displaystyle\coth\left(\hbar\omega/2k_{\rm B}T\right)\rightarrow\left(2k_{\rm B}T/\hbar\omega\right)
. We remark that the condition
\displaystyle k_{\rm B}T\geqslant{\rm Max}\left[\hbar\Lambda_{\rm d}\right]
implies the high temperature regime in any dimension. Here
\displaystyle{\rm Max}[.]
is the maximum of the cut-off frequencies of different dimensions. This means that all the Bogoliubov modes of the bath will be thermally populated in any of the considered dimensions. However, while the cut-off frequency in dimension
\displaystyle d
scales as
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm d}\sim\left(n_{0,1}\right)^{d}
, it also depends on the boson coupling constant in the corresponding dimension and therefore on the transverse confinement of the boson gas [cf. Eq. (

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}>\Lambda_{3}>\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle k_{\rm B}T\geqslant\hbar\Lambda_{2}
. In this regime, the MSD again scales with the square of the time. The dimension-dependent superdiffusion coefficient takes the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x,\rm d}^{\rm HT}=\frac{2k_{\rm B}T\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{d}}{m_{\rm I}d^{2}\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}.

(59)

It is clear from this expression that the superdiffusion coefficient is proportional to the temperature of bath and inversely proportional to the mass of the impurity.

Here, we have defined the quantities

(61)

In dimension
\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\displaystyle\eta_{\rm max,d}=\frac{d}{\sqrt{\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\tau_{\rm d,s}\right)^{d}}}.

IV.1.2
Average Energy

We now turn to the average kinetic energy
\displaystyle E(t)
of the impurity, corresponding to the observed coordinate. This can be computed from the variance of the corresponding momentum operator reading

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d}(t)=\frac{\langle p^{2}_{x,\rm d}(t)\rangle}{2m_{\rm I}}

(64)

The generalized expression for the variance of the momentum is given in the appendix
C
. Using the dimension-dependent asymptotic expressions for
\displaystyle G_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d}(t)=\frac{\langle p^{2}_{x,\rm d}(0)\rangle}{2m_{\rm I}\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\hbar}{2m_{\rm I}\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!du\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dv\penalty\ \nu^{xx}_{\rm d}(u-v).

(65)

For any arbitrary temperature, it is difficult to obtain from here an analytic expression. Here we are mainly interested in the ultracold regime. This means that all the bath modes are now in a collective vacuum state. Therefore, in the zero-temperature limit, the above expression further reduces to

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d}^{\rm LT}(t)=\frac{\langle p^{2}(0)\rangle}{2m_{\rm I}\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\hbar\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{d+1}\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}}{\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}d(d+1)}+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{d+1}\left(\tau_{\rm d}\right)^{d}}{\left(\alpha_{\rm d}\right)^{2}d(d+1)}\left(\,{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2},\frac{d}{2}+\frac{3}{2};-\frac{1}{4}\left(\Lambda_{\rm d}\right)^{2}t^{2}\right)\right).

(66)

Here, the first term represents the initial mean energy of the impurity determined by its initial momentum variance. Additionally, there is a rescaling of the mass of the impurity due to the interaction with the bath. The additional mass term depends on the dimensionality of the bath through
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm d}
and
\displaystyle\tau_{\rm d}
. The second term is the steady state mean energy of the impurity which is determined by the impurity-bath coupling and density of the bath, again through the same parameters. The last term of the expression contains information about the energy variation in time. We plot the energy function in Fig.
3
a-c) for different dimensions. In all dimensions, the energy oscillates in time. This clearly shows the energy exchange between the system and the bath. Where the energy increases, it is due to an energy absorbed from the bath. The back flow of energy is a manifestation of memory effects in the QBM
Guarnieri et al. 2016
. Moreover, deep inside the weak coupling regime, the bath perturbs the system more strongly as the coupling strength increases. In any dimension, this results in the higher initial increasing peak for a larger coupling constant. The overall profiles of all the energy functions tend to approach their asymptotic steady state values.

A similar analysis can be performed for the high temperature case, as was done in the previous section for the MSD. We treat the problem classically, meaning that the the symmetrised noise correlation function (cf. Eq. (
55
) and Appendix
C
) would act as the classical analogue in the present quantum formulation
Clerk et al. 2010
. As for the dynamical part of the energy expression, the classical regime is further obtained by requiring the conditions
\displaystyle t\to\infty,\\
\hbar\to 0
and
\displaystyle k_{\rm B}T\gg\hbar\omega
. In these limits, the energy
\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d}^{\rm cl,ss}
becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d}^{\rm cl,ss}=2k_{\rm B}T\left[\frac{\beta_{\rm d}\eta^{2}}{\left(\beta_{\rm d}\right)^{2}d^{-2}\eta^{4}+2\beta_{\rm d}\eta^{2}+d^{2}}\right].

(67)

The above expression is once again maximised at
\displaystyle\eta_{\rm max,d}
giving an upper bound to the kinetic energy of the impurity reading

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{x,\rm d,max}^{\rm cl,ss}=k_{\rm B}T/2.

(68)

Remarkably, this is the familiar equipartition theorem that holds in any dimension. It follows from these results that
\displaystyle\eta_{\rm max,d}
is the value of the system-bath coupling at which the impurity reaches thermal equilibrium with the Bogoliubov bath.

IV.2
Trapped Case

In recent years, there has been an increased interest on trapped impurities within cold atomic media. For instance, the bound states of the trapped impurities provide a platform to test the existence of synthetic vacuum of the hosting medium by witnessing the induced Lamb-shifts
Rentrop et al. 2016
. Additionally, trapped impurities in BECs can serve as highly controlled phononic q-bits
Scelle et al. 2013
;
Shaukat et al. 2017
. Hence, theoretical study of a trapped impurity as an open quantum system, as in this work, can be valuable in all of the aforementioned cases. In the trapped case, we confine the impurity into a harmonic trap with frequency
\displaystyle\Omega
. We compute the functions
\displaystyle G_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
by employing the Zakian method. Their time dynamics is shown in Fig.
4
(a-b). In all dimensions, both of these functions oscillate out of phase by
\displaystyle\pi/2
. This reflects the fact that position and momentum are the two quadratures of the impurity motion. Note that the information of the initial position and the momentum variances is carried by the functions
\displaystyle G_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
respectively (see appendix
C
). The decay of these functions provides insights into the system dynamics. First of all, such decay shows that the impurity dynamics is stable. In general, the stability analysis of the dynamics can be performed more rigorously, e.g. through the Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion
Morris 1962
. However, given the absence of the analytical form of
\displaystyle G_{1}(t)
and
\displaystyle G_{2}(t)
(or their Laplace transforms), we rely on a numerical evaluation of their profiles.
In fact, both of these functions approach to zero as
\displaystyle t\rightarrow\infty
. In effect, the system dynamics becomes independent of its initial conditions and its behaviour is completely determined by the bath state. It can be seen that in the long time limit, each one of them collapses to a single curve for all the cases displayed in Fig. 4. On the contrary at initial short times, their amplitudes and phases are mismatched for different initial conditions. The differences coming from different coupling parameters and dimensions also vanish in the long time limit. This leads to the insight that the steady state regime is completely determined by the bath state and the system tends to equilibrate with the local state of the bath.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\nu_{\rm d}^{xx}(u-v).

(69)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\hbar}{m_{\rm I}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J_{\rm d}^{xx}\left(\omega\right)\coth\left(\hbar\omega/2k_{\rm B}T\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}du\int_{0}^{t}dvG_{\rm 2,d}(t-u)G_{\rm 2,d}(t-v)\cos\left[\omega\left(u-v\right)\right].

We now define the collective function
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm d}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{Q_{\rm d}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left(1/2\right)\left(\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}du\int_{0}^{t}dv

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times G_{\rm 2,d}(t-u)G_{\rm 2,d}(t-v)\left[e^{i\omega u}e^{-i\omega v}+c.c.\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}^{2}}\right)\left(1/2\right)\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}d\tilde{u}e^{-i\omega\tilde{u}}G_{\rm 2,d}(\tilde{u})\int_{0}^{t}d\tilde{v}e^{i\omega\tilde{v}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad\quad G_{\rm 2,d}(\tilde{v})+c.c.

(71)

so that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}=\hbar\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J_{\rm d}^{xx}\left(\omega\right)\coth\left(\hbar\omega/2k_{\rm B}T\right)\mathcal{Q_{\rm d}}(\omega).

(72)

In the second equality of Eq. (

), we have defined new variables
\displaystyle\tilde{u}=t-u
and
\displaystyle\tilde{v}=t-v
. It turns out that each term is the product of two copies of the Laplace transform of
\displaystyle G_{\rm 2,d}(\tilde{u})
, evaluated at
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=-i\omega
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=i\omega
. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{Q_{\rm d}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}^{2}}\right)\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}=-i\omega,\rm d}\left[G_{\rm 2,d}(t)\right]\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}=i\omega,\rm d}\left[G_{\rm 2,d}(t)\right].

We now introduce the frequency dependent functions

and
\displaystyle\theta^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)\equiv\Im\left\{\mathcal{L}_{\bar{\mathcal{S}},{\rm d}}\left[\Gamma\left(t\right)\right]\right\}
which are respectively the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier domain tensor component of the damping kernel. They are obtained by the analytic continuation of Eq. (

) with
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}=-i\omega+0^{+}
. This allows us to write the function in Eq. (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{Q_{\rm d}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\left[\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}-\omega\theta^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)\right]^{2}+\left[\omega\xi^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)\right]^{2}}\right).

Additionally, from the relation between the damping kernel and the spectral density tensor components given in the Eq. (

Weiss 2008

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{\rm d}^{xx}(\omega)=\frac{m_{\rm I}\omega\xi^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)}{\pi}.

(75)

By inserting Eq. (

) and Eq. (
75

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\coth\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{\rm B}T}\right)\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\omega),

(76)

where the function
\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\omega)
equals

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{m_{\rm I}}\frac{\xi_{\rm d}^{xx}(\omega)\omega}{\left[\omega\xi_{\rm d}^{xx}(\omega)\right]^{2}+\left[\Omega^{2}-\omega^{2}+\omega\theta_{\rm d}^{xx}\left(\omega\right)\right]^{2}}.

(77)

From the direct inspection of Eq. (
43
), it turns out that this function is the imaginary part of the susceptibility
\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}=\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}^{{}^{\prime}}+i\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
. The susceptibility function can be obtained by extracting the linear response function from Eq. (
43

\displaystyle|\tilde{\chi}_{\rm d}(\omega)|^{2}=\mathcal{Q_{\rm d}}(\omega)
.

It follows from these results that the steady state position variance is fully determined by the functions
\displaystyle\xi^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)
and
\displaystyle\theta^{xx}_{\rm d}(\omega)
. In addition, note that the upper limit of the Eq. (
76
) is reduced to the cut-off frequency in every dimension due to the unit step function involved in spectral function. We perform an analytic continuation of Eq. (
48
) to get these functions in the low frequency limit
\displaystyle\omega\ll\Lambda_{\rm d}
(up to first few orders of
\displaystyle\omega
) for each of the dimensions. Such a process is straightforward for
\displaystyle d=1
and
\displaystyle d=3
, since both can be expanded as polynomials of
\displaystyle\omega
. This is not the case for
\displaystyle d=2
, since in this case the expression contains a logarithmic transcendental function of the frequency. In order to study this function for low frequencies, we evaluate it numerically below the cut-off, and use this for a numerical evaluation of the position variance. We pass to the dimensionless (scaled) position quadrature by introducing
\displaystyle\acute{x}\equiv x/x_{zpf}
, where
\displaystyle x_{zpf}\equiv\sqrt{\hbar/2m_{\rm I}\Omega}
is the zero-point fluctuation (ground state width) of the harmonically bound impurity. The Heisenberg uncertainty relation, for the standard deviations of the conjugate position and momentum operators, in the scaled variables becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta\acute{x}_{\rm d}\Delta\acute{p}_{\acute{x},\rm d}\geqslant 1.

(78)

On the other hand, if one of these standard deviations falls below unity, it is said to have achieved a squeezed state. The squeezing is a pure quantum effect where quantum noise is driven below its ground state uncertainty for one of the conjugate observables. The mechanical squeezed states are of great significance in high precision displacement sensing
Wollman et al. 2015
.
One may also express such standard deviations in the scaled coordinates. For the position and momentum, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta\acute{x}_{\rm d}=\sqrt{\frac{2m_{\rm I}\Omega\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}}{\hbar}},\quad\Delta\acute{p}_{\acute{x},\rm d}=\sqrt{\frac{2\left\langle p_{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}}{\hbar m_{\rm I}\Omega}}.

(79)

From here on, we focus on the position quadrature. Before proceeding to study position squeezing, we comment on the equipartition theorem in our system. In the present case, we have a thermostat with the large number of modes of the Bogoliubov bosonic bath. Additionally, one can tune the temperature of the bath sufficiently high for all the modes to be thermally populated. In the long time limit, the bath achieves a thermal equilibrium steady state. An immersed impurity would therefore tend to equilibrate too, once the temperature of the bath is kept above (or close to) its trap frequency. In thermal equilibrium, equipartition theorem states that the amount of energy
\displaystyle(1/2)k_{\rm B}T
is distributed per degree of freedom. We thus have

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1/2)k_{\rm B}T=(1/2)m_{\rm I}\Omega^{2}\left\langle x^{2}\right\rangle_{\rm d}.

(80)

and thus the standard deviation in the dimensionless coordinate reads

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta\acute{x}_{\rm d}=\sqrt{2\acute{T}},\quad\text{where}\quad\acute{T}\equiv(k_{\rm B}T)/(\hbar\Omega).

(81)

By checking that the position squeezing parameter asymptotically approaches the equipartition profile as stated in the last equation, one can verify that impurity motion follows the equipartition theorem. This is indeed the case, as shown in Fig.
5
(a-c). As the thermal energy
\displaystyle k_{\rm B}T
becomes equal to the quantum energy
\displaystyle\hbar\Omega
, all the cases approach the equipartition profiles. Note that this holds in all dimensions
\displaystyle d=1,2,3
, despite the fact that the tensor component of the bath spectral density
\displaystyle J^{xx}
, which is responsible for the
\displaystyle x
-directed motion, scales as
\displaystyle d^{-1}
[cf. Eq. (
29
)].

On the other hand, the differences between the profiles corresponding to different dimensions are apparent when one examines the magnitude of the position squeezing achieved. Although a direct comparison is not possible due to different ranges of coupling compatible with the Fröhlich regime, it is apparent that squeezing is more pronounced in lower dimensions. The amount of squeezing is proportional to the coupling strength and it is achieved at quite low temperature. The squeezing effect is purely due the interaction with the bath and it occurs without an external control of the impunity motion. It is referred to as genuine position squeezing
Lampo et al. 2017
.

In this section, we briefly analyse the memory effects. In general, one expects to see Makovian dynamics when the spectral density of the bath is Ohmic, and for sufficiently high temperature and weak coupling, such as that obtained with a Dirac delta like damping kernel in Eq. (
42
). On the contrary, the super-Ohmic nature of the bath is generally assumed to lead to non-Markovian dynamics of the system, i.e. to memory effects. We will use the super-Ohmic spectral densities of the bath we obtained for different dimensions to study the relation between the expected non-Markovian effects and dimensionality. In order to quantify the amount of non-Markovianity, a number of measures have been proposed so far
Breuer et al. 2016
. Here, we apply the criteria related to the back flow of information. It has been shown that such back flow of information can be expressed in terms of the trace distance
Breuer et al. 2009
and the fidelity
Vasile et al. 2011
of two states. For Gaussian states, the latter has an analytical form. More specifically, the non-Markovianity
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm d}
based on the back flow of information through the fidelity criterion is explicitly related to the noise kernel and is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm d}=\int_{\Delta_{\rm d}<0}\Delta_{\rm d}(t)dt\quad\text{with}

(82)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm d}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\nu^{xx}_{\rm d}(s)\cos(\Omega s).

(83)

In our case, the noise kernel is given by Eq. (
25
) which depends on the dimension of the system. The above measure can be obtained by first calculating the definite integrals over the variables
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle s
, and then integrating the resulting function
\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm d}(t)
over the time region where it is negative. In the study of non-Markovianity through this measure, we focus on the comparison between the cases of
\displaystyle d=1
and
\displaystyle d=2
dimensions. In Fig.
6
a), we plot the measure
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm d}
for the zero temperature case (i.e. when the cotangent in Eq. (
25
) equals
\displaystyle 1
) for a range of values of the system-bath coupling. Clearly the non-Markovianity measure is showing a monotonically increasing behaviour on the logarithmic scale, as we enhance the system-bath coupling. In addition, as we move from low to higher dimension with the corresponding higher level of super-Ohmicity, larger non-Markovian effects are witnessed: the non-Markovianity is at least an order of magnitude larger in
\displaystyle d=2
than in
\displaystyle d=1
.

Another measure of memory effect is the
\displaystyle J
-distance
\displaystyle\rm J\mathcal{D}
, which has been introduced in Ref.
Lampo et al. 2018
. This is a quantitative measure of the relative position variance between the case where the spectral density is super-Ohmic and the case where it is Ohmic. The Ohmic case corresponds to the time-local form of the damping kernel and has spectral density
\displaystyle J_{\rm Ohm}=m_{\rm I}\gamma\omega
, where we have introduced the phenomenological damping constant
\displaystyle\gamma
. We may explicitly write the
\displaystyle J
-distance as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rm J\mathcal{D}_{\rm d}=\left|\frac{\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\rm d}-\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\rm Ohm,d}}{\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\rm d}+\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\rm Ohm,d}}\right|.

(84)

Here
\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle_{\rm Ohm,d}
is the position variance that would be obtained, had we assumed an Ohmic Spectral density with the same cut-off function. In Fig.
6
(b), we plot this quantity for different dimensions as a function of temperature. It turns out that for a similar system-bath coupling strength
\displaystyle\rm J\mathcal{D}_{\rm d}
assumes largest values for
\displaystyle d=3
. This is supported by the argument that in a higher dimension super-ohmicity affects the dynamics stronger, leading to a larger deviation from the ohmic case. In addition, the non-Markovian effects are more pronounced near zero temperature i.e. a vacuum bath. As the temperature grows, dynamics tends to achieve the thermal steady state both for Markovian and non-Markovian scenarios. The relative difference in Eqn. (
84
) then approaches zero.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we studied from the quantum open systems perspective the dynamics of an impurity immersed in a
\displaystyle d
-dimensional BEC. In particular, we offered a detailed derivation of the Langevin equations and the associated generalized
\displaystyle d
-dimensional spectral density. We derived an expression for the tensor that describes this spectral density in full generality. Particular attention was given to the case of a spherically symmetric bath, which implied a diagonal form for this tensor. In addition, the tensors for the noise and damping kernels were calculated; these tensors enter the vectorial Langevin-like equations of motion. All these technical aspects, allowed to study in detail the dependence of the dynamics of the impurity on the dimensionality.

We considered both untrapped and trapped scenarios for the impurity. In the untrapped case, we performed in all dimensions the calculation of the mean square displacement, showing that the motion is superdiffusive. We derived explicit expressions for the superdiffusivity coefficient in the low and high temperature limits. In the latter limit we found that this coefficient has a maximum as a function of the impurity-boson coupling. The maximal value of the coefficient is equal in all dimensions, but the value of the coupling at which it occurs is dimension-dependent. It lies within the limits of validity of the Fröhlich model in one dimension only. We calculated also the average energy, for which we obtained the generalized expression for the variance of the momentum; the expression of average energy in the ultracold regime was calculated explicitly. These results confirm the expected rescaling of the mass of the impurity as a consequence of its interaction with the bath, whose specific value depends on the dimensionality. The behaviour of the energy in all dimensions is oscillatory, with a backflow of the energy between the bath and the impurity—a memory effect. We performed a similar analysis for the large temperature limit, finding the expressions for very large times and in the classical limit. These expressions exhibit maximum at certain value of the impurity-boson coupling and, amazingly, at this value of the coupling, the equipartition theorem is fulfilled in all dimensions, so the impurity is in a thermal equilibrium with the Bogoliubov bath.

In the trapped case, we obtained the steady state and characterized it with its covariance matrix. To this end, we obtained the expressions of the position and momentum variances. We identified the position variance is connected to the the imaginary part of the susceptibility. To calculate the position variance explicitly is possible in one and three dimensions, but in two dimensions it involves a logarithmic transcendental function of the frequency, so we proceeded numerically. We saw that one can find squeezing in all dimensions, which is an important result as it can be used for applications in quantum technologies, such as quantum sensing and metrology. Here we evaluated it in any dimensions, which is relevant for many experimental set-ups. In the high temperature limit we calculated the equipartition profile and showed that in all dimensions the variances tend to this limit as temperature is increased. We found that, although a direct comparison among dimensions is not possible within our framework, due to different ranges of admissible coupling strengths within the Fröhlich regime, the magnitude of the position squeezing achieved is different, and at low dimensions one can obtain stronger position squeezing.

Finally, we also computed the amount of non-Markovianity in all dimensions via two quantifiers: the backflow of energy and the, so called,
\displaystyle J
-distance. Again, the direct comparison among dimensions is not possible, due to different parameter regimes. For the back flow of energy, we only perform the calculation in one and two dimensions, as for increased dimensions more and more cumbersome functions appear in the expressions, which complicate the analysis. Nevertheless this suffices to show that non-Markovianity grows with interactions and increases with dimensionality. The latter effect is also apparent in the calculation of the
\displaystyle J
-distance, which shows that the non-Markovian effects are sizeable in higher dimensions. As an outlook, we foresee that this work will permit to continue the explorations of the Bose polaron problem in different quantum technologies, where the role of squeezed states and of non-Markovian effects may be important. Finally, we also hope that in the future, this work will provide the starting point for investigations of more complex trapping settings, both for the impurity as well as for the bath itself.

(103)

we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{\ddot{X}}(t)+\tilde{\Omega}^{2}\underline{\underline{I}}\penalty\ \underline{X}(t)-\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(t-s)\underline{X}(s)ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(1/m_{\rm I})\underline{B}(t),

(104)

where we have introduced a renormalized frequency of the impurity:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}^{2}\underline{\underline{I}}=\Omega^{2}\underline{\underline{I}}-\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(0).

(105)

From here on, we will neglect such frequency re-normalisation contributed by the term
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(0)
. This term grows as the interaction strength between the impurity and the bath increases, and could potentially lead to a negative re-normalised frequency for the harmonically trapped impurity. This, in practice, would correspond to effectively having an impurity trapped in an inverse parabolic potential, for which no stable solution in the long time limit exists. In view of this, as in
Lampo et al. 2017
,
we make sure that we always consider values of the parameters for which this renormalized frequency is positive. This issue could have equivalently been solved by artificially introducing a counter-term in the Hamiltonian that would guarantee positivity of the Hamiltonian and translational invariance, but we prefer to use the physical Hamiltonian that we obtained directly from the Hamiltonian describing the original Bose polaron.
