A new family of translating solitons in hyperbolic space
Antonio Bueno, Rafael López

Abstract
. If ξ \xi is a Killing vector field of the hyperbolic space ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} whose flow are parabolic isometries, a surface Σ ⊂ ℍ 3 \Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{3} is a ξ \xi -translator if its mean curvature H H satisfies H = ⟨ N , ξ ⟩ H=\langle N,\xi\rangle , where N N is the unit normal of Σ \Sigma . We classify all ξ \xi -translators invariant by a one-parameter group of rotations of ℍ 3 \mathbb{H}^{3} , exhibiting the existence of a new family of grim reapers. We use these grim reapers to prove the non-existence of closed ξ \xi -translators.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable smooth surface and
\Psi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{H}^{3}
an isometric immersion in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. The mean curvature flow (MCF in short) is a differentiable map
{\bf\Psi}:\Sigma\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{H}^{3}
such that if
{\bf\Psi}_{t}={\bf\Psi}(-,t)
, then
{\bf\Psi}_{0}=\Psi
and
\frac{\partial{\bf\Psi}_{t}}{\partial t}=H({\bf\Psi}_{t})N({\bf\Psi}_{t})
, where
H({\bf\Psi}_{t})
and
N({\bf\Psi}_{t})
are the mean curvature and the unit normal of
{\bf\Psi}_{t}
respectively. See
[
1
,
3
]
. Our interest are those surfaces whose shape evolves along the MCF by translations along a direction of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. These surfaces are called translating solitons of the MCF, or translators for short. In Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, translators
\Sigma
along a direction
\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are characterized by
H=\langle N,\textbf{v}\rangle
, where
H
and
N
are the mean curvature and the unit normal of
\Sigma
, respectively. Translators also appear in the singularity theory of the MCF after a blow-up near type II singularities, according to Huisken and Sinestrari
[
8
]
.

In the hyperbolic space, the same notion of translator can be stated by taking the translations of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
determined from a Killing vector field
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
. Geometrically, the shape of any such translator does not change during the evolution by motion along the one-parameter group of translations generated by
X
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
be a Killing vector field whose flow are translations of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. An immersed surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is an
X
-translator if its mean curvature
H
satisfies

H=\langle N,X\rangle.

(1.1)

In hyperbolic space there are parabolic translations and hyperbolic translations. Translators of the MCF whose shape evolves by hyperbolic translations have been recently studied in
[
10
]
. In this paper, we investigate
\xi
-translators, where
\xi
is a Killing vector field that generates parabolic translations. We point out that these translators in the MCF theory of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
have not been previously studied in the literature.

Consider the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, that is
(\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+},\langle,\rangle)
, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z>0\}
and
\langle,\rangle
is the hyperbolic metric

\langle,\rangle=\frac{1}{z^{2}}\langle,\rangle_{e},

where
\langle,\rangle_{e}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}
is the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The ideal boundary
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
is the one-compactification of the plane of equation
z=0
. Define the Killing vector field

\xi=a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y},\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.2)

The flow of isometries generated by
\xi
are parabolic translations of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, which in the upper half-space model correspond to horizontal Euclidean translations in the direction of
(a,b,0)
.

In this paper we classify all
\xi
-translators invariant by a one-parameter group of rotations. In hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
there are three types of group of rotations. Following Do Carmo and Dajczer
[
5
]
, there are parabolic rotations (a double point of
\mathbb{H}^{3}_{\infty}
is fixed), hyperbolic rotations (two points of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
are fixed) and spherical rotations (a geodesic is pointwise fixed). Consequently, there are three types of rotational surfaces called, respectively, parabolic, hyperbolic and spherical rotational surfaces. In the literature, parabolic and hyperbolic rotations are also called parabolic and hyperbolic translations.

The first result gives a classification for hyperbolic and spherical rotational
\xi
-translators.

Theorem 1.2
.

(1)

Let
\Sigma
be a hyperbolic rotational
\xi
-translator. Then,
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic plane containing the two points at
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
fixed by the hyperbolic rotations.

(2)

There are no spherical rotational
\xi
-translators.

More interesting is the family of parabolic
\xi
-translators. After an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, and from the Euclidean viewpoint, a parabolic rotational surface is a cylindrical surface of
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
whose rulings are horizontal lines. Such a surface can be parametrized as
\Psi(s,t)=(x(s),t,z(s))
, where
(x(s),0,z(s))
is the generating curve. By analogy with the Euclidean case, we give the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

A
\xi
-grim reaper is a parabolic
\xi
-translator.

The main result in this paper is the following classification of the
\xi
-grim reapers.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let be
\xi=a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y},\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\Sigma
a
\xi
-grim reaper.

•

If
a=0
, then
H=0
and
\Sigma
is either a vertical plane parallel to
\xi
, or belongs to a one-parameter family
\mathcal{G}^{0}(z_{0})
. The generating curve of each
\mathcal{G}^{0}(z_{0})
is a strictly concave graph on
x
-axis, intersecting orthogonally the
x
-axis at two points, and whose maximum height to the
x
-axis is
z_{0}
.

•

If
a\neq 0
, then
\Sigma
belongs to a one-parameter family
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
. The generating curve of each
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
is a bi-graph on the
x
-axis, its maximum height to the
x
-axis is
z_{0}
, and both graphical components converge to the
x
-axis as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
we prove Th.
1.2
, while in Sec.
3
we prove Th.
1.4
. It is of special relevance the proof of the case
a\neq 0
, for which we study the qualitative properties of the generating curve of a
\xi
-grim reaper by means of a phase plane analysis. In Sec.
4
, and under mild hypothesis, we prove the non-existence of
\xi
-translators contained in the convex side of Killing cylinders and consequently, there are no closed
\xi
-translators. Due to the variety of translators and grim reapers that can be defined in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, an Appendix has been added to summarize the literature concerning it.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
there is a relation between the mean curvature
H
of a surface
\Sigma
and its Euclidean mean curvature
H_{e}
, when
\Sigma
is regarded as surface isometrically immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
. This relation is

H(x,y,z)=zH_{e}(x,y,z)+(N^{e})_{3}(x,y,z),\quad(x,y,z)\in\Sigma,

(2.1)

where
N^{e}
is the Euclidean unit normal of
\Sigma
and the subindex
(\cdot)_{3}
denotes the third coordinate of the vector. To prove Th.
1.2
we distinguish between hyperbolic and spherical rotational surfaces.

First, assume that
\Sigma
is a hyperbolic rotational surface. From the Euclidean viewpoint,
\Sigma
is invariant under the homotheties from a point of the plane
z=0
. After an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, this point can be fixed to be the origin
O
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
. Then,
\Sigma
can be parametrized as the radial graph of a curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s),1)
, that is,

\Psi(s,t)=t\alpha(s)=t(x(s),y(s),1),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

Suppose that
s
is the Euclidean arc-length parameter. The Euclidean mean curvature
H_{e}
and unit normal
N^{e}
are

H_{e}=\frac{|\alpha|_{e}^{2}\langle\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha,\alpha^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{e}}{2t(|\alpha|_{e}^{2}-\langle\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\rangle_{e}^{2})^{3/2}},\quad N^{e}=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha}{(|\alpha|_{e}^{2}-\langle\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\rangle_{e}^{2})^{1/2}}.

Since
N=zN^{e}
, then
\langle N,\xi\rangle=\frac{1}{t}\langle N^{e},\xi\rangle_{e}
. Thus (
1.1
) is

\frac{|\alpha|_{e}^{2}\langle\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha,\alpha^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{e}}{2(|\alpha|_{e}^{2}-\langle\alpha^{\prime},\alpha\rangle_{e}^{2})}+(\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha)_{3}=\frac{\langle\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha,\xi\rangle_{e}}{t}.

(2.2)

As this equation holds for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we deduce that the left hand-side of (
2.2
) vanishes identically, that is,
H=0
. The same occurs for the right hand-side of (
2.2
), so
\langle\alpha^{\prime}\times\alpha,\xi\rangle_{e}=0
, which implies that
\alpha
is a horizontal line parallel to the vector
(a,b,0)
. This proves that
\Sigma
is a plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
through
O
. In hyperbolic geometry,
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic vertical plane if the curve
s\mapsto(x(s),y(s),0)
passes through
O
, or
\Sigma
is an equidistant plane in other case. Because the mean curvature of an equidistant plane is not zero, the result holds.

Now, we assume that
\Sigma
is a spherical rotational surface, which up to an isometry can be then parametrized by

\Psi(s,t)=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},\ t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
is the generating curve of
\Sigma
. We assume that
s
is the Euclidean arc-length parameter, hence
\alpha^{\prime}(s)=(\cos\theta(s),0,\sin\theta(s))
for some smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
. A straightforward computation shows that Eq. (
1.1
) writes as

\frac{z}{2}\left(\theta^{\prime}+\frac{z^{\prime}}{x}\right)+x^{\prime}=-\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}(a\cos t+b\sin t).

Since this equation holds for any
t
, the functions
\{1,\cos t,\sin t\}
are linearly independent and
a,b
are not both identically zero, we deduce that the left hand-side is zero, that is,
H=0
, and from the right hand-side, we have
z^{\prime}(s)=0
for all
s\in I
. This means that
\alpha
is a horizontal Euclidean straight line, which yields that
\Sigma
is a horosphere. However, the mean curvature of a horosphere is
H=1
, obtaining a contradiction.

3.
Classification of the
\xi
-grim reapers

In the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, a parabolic rotational surface
\Sigma
can be parametrized as a cylindrical surface of
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
whose rulings are horizontal lines. After a rotation about the
z
-axis, which only changes the constants
a
and
b
in (
1.2
), we can suppose that the direction of the rulings is
(0,1,0)
. Then, a parametrization for
\Sigma
is

\Psi(s,t)=(x(s),t,z(s)),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},t\in\mathbb{R},

(3.1)

where
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
is parametrized by the Euclidean arc-length. Then
x^{\prime}=\cos\theta
,
z^{\prime}=\sin\theta
for some smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
. The Euclidean mean curvature and unit normal of
\Sigma
are
H_{e}=\theta/2
and
N^{e}=(-z^{\prime},0,x^{\prime})
. If
\Sigma
is a
\xi
-grim reaper, according to (
2.1
), Eq. (
1.1
) is

z\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{2}+x^{\prime}=-a\frac{z^{\prime}}{z}.

(3.2)

At this point, we distinguish if
a
vanishes or not.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
: case
a=0

In Th.
1.4
we assume
a=0
, hence Eq. (
3.2
) implies
H=0
. Parabolic rotational surfaces of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with

were classified in
[
5
,
7
]
. For completeness, we describe these surfaces. If
x^{\prime}=0
at some point, then the solution of (
3.2
) is
\theta(s)=0
,
\alpha
is a vertical line and
\Sigma
is a totally geodesic plane parallel to
(0,1,0)
. Suppose now
x^{\prime}(s)\not=0
for all

. Then
\alpha
is a graph
z=z(x)
on the
x
-axis, and (
3.2
) is

\frac{z^{\prime\prime}}{1+z^{\prime 2}}=-\frac{2}{z}.

(3.3)

In particular, the curve
\alpha
is a strictly concave graph. Multiplying (
3.3
) by

and integrating, there is a positive constant

such that
1+z^{\prime 2}=cz^{-4}
. Hence, it can be deduced that
\alpha
is symmetric about a vertical line and that
\alpha
intersects orthogonally the

-axis at two points. See Fig.
1
. We denote this

-grim reaper by
\mathcal{G}^{0}(z_{0})
indicating the maximum height

of

to the

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Now, we suppose

1.4
. After a reflection about a vertical plane and a hyperbolic translation we assume
a=1
, hence
\xi=\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y}
. From (

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=\cos\theta\\
z^{\prime}&=\sin\theta\\
\theta^{\prime}&=\displaystyle{-\frac{2}{z^{2}}\left(\sin\theta+z\cos\theta\right)}.\end{split}\right.

(3.4)

Note that the first equation of (
3.4
) can be obtained from the second and third ones, and that the parameter

does not appear. The phase plane of (
3.4

\Theta=\{(z,\theta)\colon z>0,\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)\}

z

\Theta

. We point out that every orbit defines a generating curve
\alpha

\xi

The following properties of the phase plane hold.

(1)

If

.

(2)

The curve
\Gamma:=\Theta\cap\{z=\Gamma(\theta)\}
, where
\Gamma(\theta)=-\tan\theta
, corresponds to points of
\alpha
with vanishing Euclidean curvature. It only appears for
\theta\in(-\pi/2,0]\cup(\pi/2,\pi]
and has the lines
\theta=0
and
\theta=\pi/2
as asymptotes.

(3)

The curve
\Gamma
and the line
\theta=0
divide
\Theta
into

The first result depicts the global behavior of the orbits and of the corresponding generating curves.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
z_{0}>0
there exists a unique orbit
\gamma_{z_{0}}
passing through
(z_{0},0)
, which converges to
z=0
as
|s|
increases. The corresponding generating curve
\alpha_{z_{0}}
converges to
z=0
and has Euclidean height
z_{0}
.

Proof.

Given
z_{0}>0
, consider the solution
\alpha_{z_{0}}
of (
3.4
) with initial conditions
x(0)=0
,
z(0)=z_{0}
and
\theta(0)=0
, and let
\gamma_{z_{0}}
denote the corresponding orbit. The initial conditions yield that at
s=0
the height function of
\alpha_{z_{0}}
attains a local maximum. Indeed,
\theta^{\prime}(0)=-2/z_{0}^{2}<0
,
z^{\prime}(0)=0
and for
s>0
close enough to
s=0
the orbit
\gamma_{z_{0}}
lies in the region
\theta<0
and
z>-\tan\theta
, hence
\theta^{\prime}(s)<0
. By continuity,
\gamma_{z_{0}}
ends up intersecting
\Gamma
at some instant
s_{0}>0
where
\theta^{\prime}(s_{0})=0
, hence
\alpha_{z_{0}}
has vanishing curvature at
s=s_{0}
. Then,
\theta^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s>s_{0}
since
\gamma_{z_{0}}
lies in the region
z<-\tan\theta
. Consequently,
\gamma_{z_{0}}
ends up converging to the boundary component
z=0
, and the motion of the orbits in the phase plane forbids
\gamma_{z_{0}}
to converge to
(0,-\pi/2)
. On the other hand, for
s<0
close enough to
s=0
,
\gamma_{z_{0}}
lies in the region
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
and when
s<0
further decreases it can behave in two ways: either
\gamma_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(0,\theta_{*})
with
\theta_{*}\in(0,\pi/2]
, or
\gamma_{z_{0}}
intersects the line
\theta=\pi/2
at some
s_{1}<0
and then ends up converging to
(0,\theta_{*})
with
\theta_{*}\in(\pi/2,\pi]
.

In the first case,
\alpha_{z_{0}}
is always a graph on the
x
-axis since
x^{\prime}=\cos\theta
never vanishes. In the second case,
\alpha_{z_{0}}
fails to be a graph precisely at

since
x^{\prime}(s_{1})=0
, but it can be expressed as a vertical bi-graph whose both components are smoothly joined at
x(s_{1})
. In both cases, the maximum height of
\alpha_{z_{0}}
is
z_{0}
at
z=0
.
∎

In this proposition, we know that when
s
diverges, any orbit of
\Theta
must end up converging to some
(0,\theta_{*})
. The following result restricts the possible values of
\theta_{*}
and the behavior of the parameter
s
, proving that
\alpha_{z_{0}}
converges to
z=0
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\gamma(s)
be an orbit and assume that
\gamma(s)\rightarrow(0,\theta_{*})
as
s\rightarrow s_{*}
. Then
\theta_{*}=0
and
s_{*}=\infty
, or
\theta_{*}=\pi
and
s_{*}=-\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{z_{0}}
be the orbit passing through
(z_{0},0)
at
s=0
. Arguing by contradiction, assume that
\gamma_{z_{0}}
converges to some
(0,\theta_{*})
with
\theta_{*}\neq 0,\pi
.

First, assume that
s
increases from
s=0
, hence
\theta_{*}\in(-\pi/2,0)
. Thus
z^{\prime}(s)=\sin\theta(s)\rightarrow\sin\theta_{*}<0
as
s\rightarrow s_{*}\leq\infty
. In fact,
s_{*}<\infty
since otherwise
\alpha_{z_{0}}
, which is arc-length parametrized, would eventually cross the line
z=0
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\alpha_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(x_{*},0)
as
s\rightarrow s_{*}
, with
x_{*}=x(s_{*})
and
z(s_{*})=0
. Fix some
\hat{x}\in(0,x_{*})
with
x_{*}-\hat{x}<1
and let
\hat{s}
such that
\hat{x}=x(\hat{s})
. In
s\in(\hat{s},s_{*})
we write
\alpha_{z_{0}}(s)
as a graph
x\mapsto(x,0,u(x))
with
x\in(\hat{x},x_{*})
. Then, Eq. (
1.1
) becomes

\frac{u^{\prime\prime}}{1+u^{\prime 2}}=-\frac{2}{u}-\frac{2u^{\prime}}{u^{2}}.

(3.5)

Integrating from
\hat{x}
to
x
, we have

\arctan u^{\prime}(x)=-2\int_{\hat{x}}^{x}\frac{1}{u(t)}dt+\frac{2}{u(x)}+A(\hat{x}),\qquad A(\hat{x})=\arctan u^{\prime}(\hat{x})-\frac{2}{u(\hat{x})}.

By the mean value theorem, there is
c_{x}\in(\hat{x},x)
such that

\arctan u^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{2}{u(c_{x})}(x-\hat{x})+\frac{2}{u(x)}+A(\hat{x}).

(3.6)

Now, let
x_{n}\nearrow x_{*}
,
x_{n}\in(\hat{x},x_{*})
and name
c_{n}=c_{x_{n}}
. Bearing in mind that
u(c_{n})>u(x_{n})
because
u
is strictly decreasing, we get from (
3.6
)

\arctan u^{\prime}(x_{n})=\frac{-2}{u(c_{n})}(x_{n}-\hat{x})+\frac{2}{u(x_{n})}+A(\hat{x})>\frac{-2(x_{n}-\hat{x})+2}{u(x_{n})}+A(\hat{x}).

Letting
x_{n}\rightarrow x_{*}
, we obtain

\arctan u^{\prime}(x_{*})>\frac{-2(x_{*}-\hat{x})+2}{u(x_{*})}+A(\hat{x}),

which is a contradiction since the left-hand side is negative while the right-hand side diverges to
\infty
. This contradiction ensures us that
\gamma_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(0,0)
as
s
increases. In fact,
s\rightarrow\infty
since otherwise
s\rightarrow s_{*}<\infty
and then
\alpha_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(x_{*},0)
, arriving to the same contradiction.

To prove that
\gamma_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(0,\pi)
as
s\rightarrow-\infty
, we follow a similar argument. If
\theta_{*}\in(0,\pi/2]
then
x^{\prime}=\cos\theta\neq 0
, and consequently there exists
x_{*}<0
such that
\alpha_{z_{0}}
is a graph
x\mapsto(x,0,u(x))
for
x\in(x_{*},0)
, with
u^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
and
u^{\prime}(x)>0
. Note that if
\theta_{*}=\pi/2
, then
\alpha_{z_{0}}
would intersect orthogonally
z=0
, failing to be a graph at this intersection point. However, we just need to express
\alpha_{z_{0}}
as a graph in the open interval
x\in(x_{*},0)
. An integration of (
3.5
) from
x
to
0
and the mean value theorem for integrals yields

\arctan u^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{2x}{u(c_{x})}+\frac{2}{u(x)}-\frac{2}{u(0)},\qquad c_{x}\in(x,0).

The left-hand side is a bounded function. However, if
x\rightarrow x_{*}
the right-hand side diverges to
\infty
, a contradiction. If
\theta_{*}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
, now
\alpha_{z_{0}}
is a bi-graph whose lower component is again a strictly convex graph that converges to
z=0
at a finite point. The contradiction is the same as the one exposed in the proof of
\theta_{*}\in(-\pi/2,0)
, concluding that
\gamma_{z_{0}}(s)\rightarrow(0,\pi)
as
s\rightarrow-\infty
. This concludes the proof of Prop.
3.3
.
∎

4.
Non-existence of closed
\xi
-translators

In this section, we will use the properties of
\xi
-grim reapers to prove the non-existence of certain
\xi
-translators. For this, we will employ the comparison principle of solutions of elliptic equations for Eq. (
1.1
). In contrast to the Euclidean space, it is not known if
\xi
-translators are minimal surfaces in a weighted space in the sense of Ilmanen
[
9
]
(a similar situation occurs for
\chi
-translators, see
[
10
]
).

Lemma 4.1

(Tangency principle)
.

Let
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
be two connected
\xi
-translators and assume that they are tangent at some
p\in\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}
, around. If
\Sigma_{1}
lies at one side of
\Sigma_{2}
, then
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}
in the largest neighborhood of
p
in
\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose that a
\xi
-translator
\Sigma
writes locally as
z=u(\bar{x})
, where
\bar{x}=(x,y)\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then Eq. (
1.1
) becomes

\mbox{div}\left(\frac{Du}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\right)=-\frac{1}{u^{2}\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}(u+au_{x}+bu_{y}).

As usually, we write this equation as
\sum a_{ij}(\bar{x},u,Du)D_{ij}u+{\bf b}(\bar{x},u,Du)=0
. Here

{\bf b}(\bar{x},u,Du)=\frac{1+|Du|^{2}}{2u^{2}}(u+au_{x}+bu_{y}).

Since
{\bf b}(\bar{x},u,Du)
is non-increasing on the variable
u
for each fixed
(\bar{x},Du)
, the comparison principle of quasilinear equations can be applied: see
[
6
, Th. 9.2]
. The proof concludes by realizing that a change of the orientation keeps Eq. (
1.1
) invariant, hence preserves the property of being a
\xi
-translator.
∎

We apply the tangency principle to exhibit the non-existence of certain
\xi
-translators contained in Killing cylinders. Recall that a
Killing cylinder

\mathcal{C}_{L}
around a geodesic
L
is the set of points that lie at a fixed distance of
L
. The convex side of
\mathcal{C}_{L}
is the component of
\mathbb{H}^{3}-\mathcal{C}_{L}
that contains
L
.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\xi=a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y}
, then there do not exist properly immersed
\xi
-translators in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
without boundary contained in the convex side of a Killing cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{L}
. As a consequence, there are no closed (compact and without boundary)
\xi
-translators.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that
\Sigma
is a properly immersed
\xi
-translator without boundary, contained in the convex side of a Killing cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{L}
. The geodesic
L
is a vertical line or a halfcircle intersecting orthogonally the plane
z=0
. We will assume the first case, say
L
is the
z
-axis, and a similar argument can be done if
L
is a halfcircle.

Consider the case
a\neq 0
in (
1.2
). Fix some
z_{0}>z_{*}=\inf_{p\in\Sigma}z(p)
and let
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
the
\xi
-grim reaper given by Th.
1.4
whose maximum height is
z_{0}
. After parabolic translations
t\mapsto(x+t,y,z)
with
t
sufficiently large, the grim reaper
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
does not intersect the convex side of
\mathcal{C}_{L}
. Let us move back
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
by parabolic translations
t\mapsto(x+t,y,z)
with
t\searrow-\infty
. Then there exists a first contact interior point between
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
because
\Sigma
is proper and has points with height less than
z_{0}
. Consequently the tangency principle would imply that
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
and
\Sigma
coincide because both surfaces are complete. This is a contradiction because no
\mathcal{G}(z_{0})
is contained in the convex side of any Killing cylinder.

Suppose now
a=0
in (
1.2
). Notice that there must be points
p\in\Sigma
with
x(p)\not=0
since on the contrary,
\Sigma
would be with the vertical plane
x=0
, which it is not contained in the convex side of
\mathcal{C}_{L}
. Let
p^{*}\in\Sigma
be a point with
x(p^{*})\neq 0
, say
x(p^{*})>0
, and let
x_{1}=x(p^{*})/2
. Consider the solutions
z_{\lambda}=z_{\lambda}(x)
of (
3.3
) where
z(x_{1})=z(x_{1}+\lambda)=0
,
\lambda>0
, in particular,
z^{\prime}(\frac{x_{1}+\lambda}{2})=0
. If
\lambda
is sufficiently small, then the graphic of
z_{\lambda}
is outside
\mathcal{C}_{L}
, or equivalently,
\mathcal{G}^{0}(z(x_{\lambda}))\cap\mathcal{C}_{L}=\emptyset
. Letting
\lambda\nearrow\infty
the graph of
z_{\lambda}
is asymptotic to the vertical plane of equation
x=x_{1}
(see Rem.
3.1
; also
[
4
]
). Since
x(p^{*})>x_{1}
, there there is a first time
\lambda_{0}
such that
\mathcal{G}^{0}(z(x_{\lambda_{0}}))
touches
\Sigma
. The tangency principle implies that both surfaces coincide, a contradiction.

∎
