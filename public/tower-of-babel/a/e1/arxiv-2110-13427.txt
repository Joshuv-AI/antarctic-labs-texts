Optimal Bayesian Estimation of a Regression Curve, a Conditional Density and a Conditional Distribution
A.G. Nogales

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper several related estimation problems are addressed from a Bayesian point of view and optimal estimators are obtained for each of them when some natural loss functions are considered. Namely, we are interested in estimating a regression curve. Simultaneously, the estimation problems of a conditional distribution function, or a conditional density, or even the conditional distribution itself, are considered. All these problems are posed in a sufficiently general framework to cover continuous and discrete, univariate and multivariate, parametric and non-parametric cases, without the need to use a specific prior distribution. The loss functions considered come naturally from the quadratic error loss function comonly used in estimating a real function of the unknown parameter. The cornerstone of the mentioned Bayes estimators is the posterior predictive distribution. Some examples are provided to illustrate these results.

1
Introduction.

In Statistics, the expression
the probability of an event

A
(written
P_{\theta}(A)
) is, in general, ambiguous as it depends of the unknown parameter
\theta
. Before conducting the experiment, a Bayesian statistician, provided with the prior distribution, possesses a natural candidate: the prior predictive probability of
A
since it is the prior mean of the probabilities of
A
. However, according to Bayesian philosophy, after performing the experiment and having observed the data
\omega
, a reasonable estimation is the posterior predicitive probability of
A
given
\omega
because it is the posterior mean of the probabilities of
A
given
\omega
. It can be shown not only that this is the Bayes estimator of the probability
P_{\theta}(A)
of
A
for the squared error loss function but also that the posterior predictive distribution is the Bayes estimator of the sampling probability distribution
P_{\theta}
for the squared variation total loss function and that the posterior predictive density is the Bayes estimator of its density for the
L^{1}
-squared loss function. Notice that these loss functions should be considered natural in the sense that they are derived directly from the quadratic error loss function commonly used in the estimation of a real function of the parameter.
Nogales (2021) contains accurate statements and proofs of these results which are nothing but a functional generalization of Theorem 1.1 (more specifically of its Corollary 1.2.(a)) of Lehmann et al. (1998, p. 228) which obtains the Bayes estimator of a real function of the parameter for the squared error loss function.

This article is about estimating a regression curve and some other related problems like the estimation of a conditional density or a conditional distribution function or even of the conditional distribution itself, ever from a Bayesian point of view.
Therefore, this paper should be considered as the conditional counterpart of Nogales (2021) and the results below become the functional extension of Lehmann et al. (1998, Theorem 1.1) for the conditional case. So it is not surprising that the posterior predictive distribution is the keystone in the estimation problems to be discussed below. Some examples are presented in Section
7
to illustrate the results. See Nogales (2021), and the references therein, for other examples of determination of the posterior predictive distribution. But in practice, the explicit evaluation of the posterior predictive distribution could be cumbersome and its simulation may become preferable. Gelman et al. (2014) is also a good reference for such simulation methods and, hence, for the computation of the Bayes estimators of the conditional density and the regression curve.

The posterior predictive distribution has been presented as the base for Predictive Inference, which seeks to make inferences about a new unknown observation from the previous random sample (in contrast to the greater emphasis that statistical inference makes on the estimation and contrast of parameters since its mathematical foundations in the early twentieth century, see Geisser (1993) or Gelman et al. (2014)).
With that idea in mind, it has also been used in other areas such as model selection, testing for discordancy, goodness of fit, perturbation analysis or classification (see addtional fields of application in Geisser (1993) and Rubin (1984)), but never as a possible solution for the Bayesian problems of estimating an unconditional or conditional density. The reader is referred to the references within Nogales (2021) for other uses of the posterior predictive distribution in Bayesian statistics.

In what follows we place ourselves in a general framewok for Bayesian inference, as described in Barra (1971).

2
The framework.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\{P_{\theta}\colon\theta\in(\Theta,\mathcal{T},Q)\})
be a Bayesian statistical experiment and
X_{i}:(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\{P_{\theta}\colon\theta\in(\Theta,\mathcal{T},Q)\})\rightarrow(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{A}_{i})
,
i=1,2
, two statistics. Consider the Bayesian experiment image of
(X_{1},X_{2})

(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2},\{P_{\theta}^{(X_{1},X_{2})}\colon\theta\in(\Theta,\mathcal{T},Q)\}).

In the next, we shall suppose that
P^{(X_{1},X_{2})}(\theta,A_{12}):=P_{\theta}^{(X_{1},X_{2})}(A_{12})
,
\theta\in\Theta,\ A_{12}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}

Let us write
R_{\theta}=P_{\theta}^{(X_{1},X_{2})}

The Bayesian experiment corresponding to a sample of size
n

(X_{1},X_{2})

\big((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n},\big\{R_{\theta}^{n}\colon\theta\in(\Theta,\mathcal{T},Q)\big\}\big)\qquad\hbox{(1)}

We write
R^{n}(\theta,A^{\prime}_{12,n})=R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})
for
A^{\prime}_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}
and

\Pi_{12,n}:=Q\otimes R^{n},

for the joint distribution of the parameter and the sample, i.e.

\Pi_{12,n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n}\times T)=\int_{T}R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dQ(\theta),\quad A^{\prime}_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n},T\in\mathcal{T}.

The corresponding prior predictive distribution
\beta_{12,n,Q}^{*}
is

\beta_{12,n,Q}^{*}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})=\int_{\Theta}R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dQ(\theta),\quad A^{\prime}_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}.

The posterior distribution is a Markov kernel

R_{n}^{*}:((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n})\mbox{$\succ$\hskip-4.30554pt$\longrightarrow$}(\Theta,\mathcal{T})

such that, for all
A^{\prime}_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}
and
T\in\mathcal{T}
,

\Pi_{12,n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n}\times T)=\int_{T}R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dQ(\theta)=\int_{A^{\prime}_{12,n}}R_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},T)d\beta_{12,n,Q}^{*}(x^{\prime}).

Let us write
R_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(T):=R_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},T)
.

The posterior predictive distribution on
\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
is the Markov kernel

{R_{n}^{*}}^{R}:((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n})\mbox{$\succ$\hskip-4.30554pt$\longrightarrow$}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})

defined, for
x^{\prime}\in(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}
, by

{R_{n}^{*}}^{R}(x^{\prime},A_{12}):=\int_{\Theta}R_{\theta}(A_{12})dR_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta)

It follows that, with obvious notations,

\int_{\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}}f(x)d{R_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}}^{\!\!\!\!R}(x)=\int_{\Theta}\int_{\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}}f(x)dR_{\theta}(x)dR_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta)

for any non-negative or integrable real random variable (r.r.v. for short)
f
.

We can also consider the posterior predictive distribution on
(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}
defined as the Markov kernel

{R_{n}^{*}}^{R^{n}}:((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n})\mbox{$\succ$\hskip-4.30554pt$\longrightarrow$}((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n})

such that

{R_{n}^{*}}^{R^{n}}(x^{\prime},A^{\prime}_{12,n}):=\int_{\Theta}R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dR_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta)

According to Theorem 1 of Nogales (2021), it is the Bayes estimator of the distribution

for the squared total variation function, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}\times\Theta}\sup_{A_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}}\left|{R_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}}^{\!\!\!\!R^{n}}(A_{12,n})-R_{\theta}^{n}(A_{12,n})\right|^{2}d\Pi_{12,n}(x^{\prime},\theta)\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}\times\Theta}\sup_{A_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}}\left|M(x^{\prime},A_{12,n})-R_{\theta}^{n}(A_{12,n})\right|^{2}d\Pi_{12,n}(x^{\prime},\theta),

for every Markov kernel
M:(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}\mbox{$\succ$\hskip-4.30554pt$\longrightarrow$}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}
.

It can be readily checked that

\left[{R_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}}^{\!\!\!\!R^{n}}\right]^{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}={R_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}}^{\!\!\!\!R},

where
\pi^{\prime}_{1}(x^{\prime}):=x^{\prime}_{1}:=(x^{\prime}_{11},x^{\prime}_{21})
for

. So, Theorem 2 of Nogales (2021) shows that

\displaystyle\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}\times\Theta}\sup_{A_{12}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\left|M(x^{\prime},A_{12})-R_{\theta}(A_{12})\right|^{2}d\Pi_{12,n}(x^{\prime},\theta),

for every Markov kernel
M:(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}\mbox{$\succ$\hskip-4.30554pt$\longrightarrow$}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})

We introduce some notations for

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},x,\theta):=x^{\prime},\quad\pi^{\prime}_{i}(x^{\prime},x,\theta):=x^{\prime}_{i}:=(x^{\prime}_{i1},x^{\prime}_{i2}),\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle\pi(x^{\prime},x,\theta):=x,\quad\pi_{i}(x^{\prime},x,\theta):=x_{i},\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle q(x^{\prime},x,\theta):=\theta.

Let us consider the probability space

\displaystyle((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}\times(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})\times\Theta,(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}\times(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})\times\mathcal{T},\Pi_{n}),\qquad\hbox{(2)}

where

\Pi_{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n}\times A_{12}\times T)=\int_{T}R_{\theta}(A_{12})R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dQ(\theta),

when
A^{\prime}_{12,n}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}
,
A_{12}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}
and

.

So, for a r.r.v.
f
on

\int fd\Pi_{n}=\int_{\Theta}\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}}\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}f(x^{\prime},x,\theta)dR_{\theta}(x)dR_{\theta}^{n}(x^{\prime})dQ(\theta)\qquad{\rm(3)}

provided that the integral exists.
Moreover, for a r.r.v.
h
on
((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})\times\Theta,(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})\times\mathcal{T})

The following proposition is straightforward.

.

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{(\pi^{\prime},q)}(A^{\prime}_{12.n}\times T)=\Pi_{12,n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n}\times T)=\int_{T}R_{\theta}^{n}(A^{\prime}_{12,n})dQ(\theta)=\int_{A^{\prime}_{12,n}}R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(T)d\beta_{12,n,Q}^{*}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{q}=Q,\quad\Pi_{n}^{(\pi^{\prime},q)}=\Pi_{12,n},\quad\Pi_{n}^{\pi^{\prime}}=\beta_{12,n,Q}^{*},\quad\Pi_{n}^{(\pi,q)}=\Pi_{12,1},\quad\Pi_{n}^{\pi}=\beta_{12,1,Q}^{*}

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{\pi^{\prime}|q=\theta}=R_{\theta}^{n},\quad\Pi_{n}^{\pi|q=\theta}=R_{\theta},\quad\Pi_{n}^{q|\pi^{\prime}=x^{\prime}}=R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}},\quad\Pi_{n}^{q|\pi=x}=R^{*}_{1,x^{\prime}}

In addition, it becomes the natural framework to address the estimation problems of this paper, as we can see in the next.

Bayes estimator of the conditional distribution.

An estimator of the conditional distribution
P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}}
from a
n

(X_{1},X_{2})

4
Bayes estimator of the conditional density.

When the joint distribution
R_{\theta}=P_{\theta}^{(X_{1},X_{2})}
has a density
f_{\theta}
with respect to the product of two
\sigma
-finite measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
on
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, resp., the conditional density is

f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2}):=\frac{f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})}{f_{\theta,X_{1}}(x_{1})}

for almost every
x_{1}
, where
f_{\theta,X_{1}}(x_{1})
stands for the marginal density of
X_{1}
.

An estimator of the conditional density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}}
from a
n
-sized sample of the joint distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is a map

m:((\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}\times\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2},(\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})^{n}\times\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2})\rightarrow(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R})

so that, being observed
x^{\prime}=((x^{\prime}_{11},x^{\prime}_{21}),\dots,(x^{\prime}_{1n},x^{\prime}_{2n}))\in(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{n}
,
m(x^{\prime},x_{1},\cdot)
is considered as an estimation of the conditional density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}
of
X_{2}
given
X_{1}=x_{1}
.

It is well known (see, for instance, Ghosal et al. (2017, p.126)) that, given two probability measures
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
having densities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
with respect to a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
,

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|P_{1}(A)-P_{2}(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|p_{1}-p_{2}|d\mu.

So the Bayesian estimation of the conditional distribution
P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}=R_{\theta}^{\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}
for the squared total variation loss function corresponds to the Bayesian estimation of its density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}
for the
L^{1}
-squared loss function.
Hence, according to Theorem
1
, the Bayes estimator of the conditional density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}
for the
L^{1}
-squared loss function is the
\mu_{2}
-density
{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}
of the conditional distribution

\left[{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!R}\right]^{\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}.

Notice that

\displaystyle{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!R}(A_{1}\times A_{2})=\int_{\Theta}R_{\theta}(A_{1}\times A_{2})dR^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\int_{A_{1}\times A_{2}}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})d(\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2})(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)=

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}\times A_{2}}\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)d(\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2})(x_{1},x_{2})

where
r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)
denotes the
Q
-density of the posterior distribution
R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}
. So
r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)
is of the form
K(x^{\prime})f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})
, where

f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x^{\prime}_{i})

is the
(\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2})^{n}
-density of
R_{\theta}^{n}
. Hence the

-density of the posterior predictive distribution

f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta),

and its first marginal is

f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime},1}(x_{1}):=\int_{\Omega_{2}}\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},t)r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)d\mu_{2}(t).

.

for any estimator
F
of the conditional distribution function
F_{\theta}
.

A natural candidate is the conditional distribution function for the posterior predictive distribution as it is stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 3
.

The posterior predictive conditional distribution function

F_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},x_{1},t):=\left({R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!R}\right)^{\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}\!\!(]-\infty,t])

is the Bayes estimator of the conditional distribution function
F_{\theta}
for the
L^{\infty}
-squared loss function.

6
Bayes estimator of a regression curve.

Now suppose that
X_{2}
is an squared-integrable r.r.v. So
(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{A}_{2})=(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R})
. The regression curve of
X_{2}
given
X_{1}
is the map
x_{1}\in\Omega_{1}\mapsto r_{\theta}(x_{1}):=E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1})
. An estimator of the regression curve
r_{\theta}
from a sample of size
n
of the joint distribution of
(X_{1},X_{2})
is a statistic

m:(x^{\prime},x_{1})\in(\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}\times\Omega_{1}\longmapsto m(x^{\prime},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R},

so that, being observed
x^{\prime}\in(\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}
,
m(x^{\prime},\cdot)
is the estimation of
r_{\theta}
.

From a frequentist point of view, the simplest way to evaluate the error in estimating an unknown regression curve is to use the expectation of the quadratic deviation (see Nadaraya (1989, p. 120)):

\displaystyle E_{\theta}\left[\int_{\Omega_{1}}(m(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}dP_{\theta}^{X_{1}}(x_{1})\right]=

\displaystyle\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}(m(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}dR_{\theta}^{\pi_{1}}(x_{1})dR_{\theta}^{n}(x^{\prime}).

From a Bayesian point of view, the Bayes estimator of the regression curve
r_{\theta}
should minimize the Bayes risk (i.e. the prior mean of the expectation of the quadratic deviation)

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\int_{(\Omega_{1}\times\mathbb{R})^{n}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}(m(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}dR_{\theta}^{\pi_{1}}(x_{1})dR_{\theta}^{n}(x^{\prime})dQ(\theta)=

\displaystyle E_{\Pi_{n}}\big[(m(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}\big].

The following result solves the problem of estimation of the regression curve from a Bayesian point of view.

Theorem 4
.

The regression curve of
\pi_{2}
on
\pi_{1}
with respect to the posterior predictive distribution
{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!R}

m_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},x_{1}):=E_{{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!R}}(\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1})

is the Bayes estimator of the regression curve
r_{\theta}(x_{1}):=E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1})
for the squared error loss function, i.e.,

E_{\Pi_{n}}[(m_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}]\leq E_{\Pi_{n}}[(m_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})-r_{\theta}(x_{1}))^{2}]

for any other estimator
m_{n}
of the regression curve
r_{\theta}
.

Remark
.

(Estimation of the regression curve when densities are available) According to the previous results, when
R_{\theta}
has density
f_{\theta}
with respect to the product
\mu_{1}\times\mu_{2}
of two
\sigma
-finite measures, the
\mu_{2}
-density
{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}
of the conditional distribution
\left[{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!R}\right]^{\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}
is

{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2}):=\frac{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2})}{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime},1}(x_{1})}=\frac{\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},t)r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)d\mu_{2}(t)},

which is the Bayes estimator of the conditional density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}}
. Hence, the Bayes estimator of the regression curve can be computed as

\displaystyle m_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},x_{1}):=E_{{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!R}}(\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{2}\cdot{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2})d\mu_{2}(x_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{2}\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)d\mu_{2}(x_{2})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\Theta}f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)d\mu_{2}(x_{2})}.

7
Examples.

Example 1
.

Let us suppose that, for
\theta,\lambda,x_{1}>0
,
P_{\theta}^{X_{1}}=G(1,\theta^{-1})
,
P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}=G(1,(\theta x_{1})^{-1})
and
Q=G(1,\lambda^{-1})
, where
G(\alpha,\beta)
denotes the gamma distribution of parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
. Hence the joint density of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is

f_{\theta}(x_{1},x_{2})=\theta^{2}x_{1}\exp\{-\theta x_{1}(1+x_{2})\}I_{]0,\infty[^{2}}(x_{1},x_{2}).

So, the density of
R_{\theta}^{n}
is

f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})=\theta^{2n}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}\cdot\exp\big\{-\theta\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})\big\}\cdot I_{]0,\infty[^{2n}}(x^{\prime}),

and the posterior
Q
-density given
x^{\prime}
is

\frac{dR^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)}{dQ}=:r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)=K(x^{\prime})f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})

where
K(x^{\prime})=[\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})dQ(\theta)]^{-1}
.

So, the posterior predictive density given
x^{\prime}\in]0,\infty[^{2n}
is

\displaystyle f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\theta}(x)r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)=

\displaystyle\lambda K(x^{\prime})x_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta^{2n+2}\exp\left\{-\theta[\lambda+x_{1}(1+x_{2})+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]\right\}d\theta\cdot I_{]0,\infty[^{2}}(x).

Since

\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta^{n}\exp\{-a\theta\}d\theta=\frac{n!}{a^{n+1}},

we have that

f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{(2n+2)!\lambda K(x^{\prime})x_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}}{[\lambda+x_{1}(1+x_{2})+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{(2n+3)}}

and its first marginal is

f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime},1}(x_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2})dx_{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{A}{(Bt+C)^{m}}dt=\frac{A}{(m-1)BC^{m-1}}

where

m=2n+3,\quad A=(2n+2)!\lambda K(x^{\prime})x_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1},\quad B=x_{1},\quad\hbox{and}\quad C=\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2}).

So

f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime},1}(x_{1})=\frac{(2n+2)!\lambda K(x^{\prime})x_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}}{(2n+2)x_{1}[\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{2n+2}}.

The Bayes estimator of the conditional density
f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2})=\theta x_{1}\exp\{-\theta x_{1}x_{2}\}I_{]0,\infty[}(x_{2})
is, for
x_{1},x_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2}):=\frac{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2})}{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime},1}(x_{1})}=

\displaystyle(2n+2)x_{1}\frac{[\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{2n+2}}{[\lambda+x_{1}(1+x_{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{2n+3}}=

\displaystyle\frac{(2n+2)x_{1}a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})^{2n+2}}{(x_{1}x_{2}+a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1}))^{2n+3}}

where

a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})=\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2}).

The Bayes estimator of the conditional distribution function

F_{\theta}(x_{1},t):=P_{\theta}(X_{2}\leq t|X_{1}=x_{1})

is, for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{n}^{*}(x^{\prime},x_{1},t)&=\int_{0}^{t}(2n+2)x_{1}\frac{[\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{2n+2}}{[\lambda+x_{1}(1+x_{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})]^{2n+3}}dx_{2}\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(2n+2)x_{1}a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})^{2n+2}}{(x_{1}x_{2}+a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1}))^{2n+3}}dx_{2}\\
&=a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})^{2n+2}\left(\frac{1}{a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})^{2n+2}}-\frac{1}{(x_{1}t+a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1}))^{2n+2}}\right)\\
&=1-\left(1+\frac{x_{1}t}{a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})}\right)^{-2n-2}.\end{split}

The Bayes estimator of the regression curve
r_{\theta}(x_{1}):=E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1})=\frac{1}{\theta x_{1}}
is, for
x_{1}>0
,

m^{*}_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}x_{2}\cdot{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2})dx_{2}=\frac{\lambda+x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i1}(1+x^{\prime}_{i2})}{(2n+1)x_{1}}=\frac{a_{n}(x^{\prime},x_{1})}{(2n+1)x_{1}}.

Example 2
.

Let us suppose that
X_{1}
has a Bernoulli distribution of unknown parameter
\theta\in]0,1[
(i.e.
P_{\theta}^{X_{1}}=Bi(1,\theta)
) and, given
X_{1}=k_{1}\in\{0,1\}
,
X_{2}
has distribution
Bi(1,1-\theta)
when
k_{1}=0
and
Bi(1,\theta)
when
k_{1}=1
, i.e.
P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=k_{1}}=Bi(1,k_{1}+(1-2k_{1})(1-\theta))
. We can think of tossing a coin with probability
\theta
of getting heads (
=1
) and making a second toss of this coin if it comes up heads on the first toss, or tossing a second coin with probability
1-\theta
of making heads if the first toss is tails (
=0
). Consider the uniform distribution on
]0,1[
as the prior distribution
Q
.

So, the joint probability function of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{\theta}(k_{1},k_{2})&=\theta^{k_{1}}(1-\theta)^{1-k_{1}}[k_{1}+(1-2k_{1})(1-\theta)]^{k_{2}}[1-k_{1}-(1-2k_{1})(1-\theta)]^{1-k_{2}}\\
&=\begin{cases}\theta(1-\theta)\hbox{ if }k_{2}=0,\\
(1-\theta)^{2}\hbox{ if }k_{1}=0,k_{2}=1,\\
\theta^{2}\hbox{ if }k_{1}=1,k_{2}=1.\end{cases}\end{split}

The probability function of
R_{\theta}^{n}
is

f_{n,\theta}(k^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(k^{\prime}_{i})=\theta^{a_{n}(k^{\prime})}(1-\theta)^{b_{n}(k^{\prime})}

for
k^{\prime}=(k^{\prime}_{1},\dots,k^{\prime}_{n})=(k^{\prime}_{11},k^{\prime}_{12},\dots,k^{\prime}_{n1},k^{\prime}_{n2})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
, where

a_{n}(k^{\prime})=n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{11}(k^{\prime}),\quad b_{n}(k^{\prime})=n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime}),

being
n_{j_{1}j_{2}}(k^{\prime})
the number of indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
(k^{\prime}_{i1},k^{\prime}_{i2})=(j_{1},j_{2})
and
n_{+j}=n_{0j}+n_{1j}
for
j=0,1
. Notice that
a_{n}(k^{\prime})+b_{n}(k^{\prime})=2n
.

Hence, the posterior
Q
-density given
k^{\prime}
is

r^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}(\theta)=\frac{dR^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}}{dQ}(\theta)=K(k^{\prime})f_{n,\theta}(k^{\prime})=K(k^{\prime})\theta^{a_{n}(k^{\prime})}(1-\theta)^{b_{n}(k^{\prime})}

where

K(k^{\prime})=\left[\int_{0}^{1}f_{n,\theta}(k^{\prime})dQ(\theta)\right]^{-1}=\frac{1}{B(a_{n}(k^{\prime})+1,b_{n}(k^{\prime})+1)},

where
B(\alpha,\beta)
stands for the Beta function.

So, the posterior predictive density given
k^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}f^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})&=\int_{0}^{1}f_{\theta}(k_{1},k_{2})r^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)\\
&=K(k^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\theta^{a_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{1}}(1-\theta)^{b_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{2}}d\theta\\
&=K(k^{\prime})B(a_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{1}+1,b_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{2}+1),\end{split}

and its first marginal is

\displaystyle f^{*}_{n,k^{\prime},1}(k_{1})=K(k^{\prime})[B(a_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{1}+1,b_{n}(k^{\prime})+1)+B(a_{n}(k^{\prime})+k_{1}+1,b_{n}(k^{\prime})+2)].

The Bayes estimator of the conditional probability function

f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=k_{1}}(k_{2})=[k_{1}+(1-2k_{1})(1-\theta)]^{k_{2}}[1-k_{1}-(1-2k_{1})(1-\theta)]^{1-k_{2}}

is

\displaystyle{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=k_{1}}(k_{2}):=\frac{f^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}(k_{1},k_{2})}{f^{*}_{n,k^{\prime},1}(k_{1})}=\begin{cases}\frac{2n+2}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+3}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=k_{2}=0,\\
\frac{n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+1}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+3}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=0,k_{2}=1,\\
\frac{2n+3}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+4}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=1,k_{2}=0,\\
\frac{n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+1}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+4}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=k_{2}=1.\end{cases}

The Bayes estimator of the conditional mean
r_{\theta}(k_{1}):=E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=k_{1})=\theta^{k_{1}}(1-\theta)^{1-k_{1}}
is, for
k_{1}=0,1
,

m^{*}_{n}(k^{\prime},k_{1})={f^{*}_{n,k^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!X_{2}|X_{1}=k_{1}}(1)=\begin{cases}\frac{n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+1}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+3}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=0,\\
\frac{n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+1}{2n+n_{+0}(k^{\prime})+2n_{01}(k^{\prime})+4}&\hbox{ if }k_{1}=1.\end{cases}

Example 3
.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2})
have bivariate normal distribution with density

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}[(x_{1}-\theta)^{2}-2\rho(x_{1}-\theta)(x_{2}-\theta)+(x_{2}-\theta)^{2}]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}[x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2\rho x_{1}x_{2}-2(1-\rho)(x_{1}+x_{2})\theta+2(1-\rho)\theta^{2}]\right\},

where
\sigma>0
and
\rho\in]-1,1[
are supposed to be kown.
So

\displaystyle R_{\theta}=N_{2}\left(\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\theta\\
\theta\end{array}\right),\sigma^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{array}\right)\right),\qquad X_{1},X_{2}\sim_{\theta}N(\theta,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}=N((1-\rho)\theta+\rho x_{1},\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}),\qquad E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1})=(1-\rho)\theta+\rho x_{1}.

Hence, for
x^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})=(x^{\prime}_{11},x^{\prime}_{12},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n1},x^{\prime}_{n2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}
,

\displaystyle f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\theta}(x^{\prime}_{i})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(x^{\prime}_{i1}-\theta)^{2}-2\rho(x^{\prime}_{i1}-\theta)(x^{\prime}_{i2}-\theta)+(x^{\prime}_{i2}-\theta)^{2}\right]\right\}.

Let us consider the prior distribution
Q=N(\mu,\tau^{2})
whose density is

g(\theta)=\frac{1}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}(\theta-\mu)^{2}\right\}.

The posterior
Q
-density is

\displaystyle r^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta):=\frac{dR^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}{dQ}(\theta)=K_{1}(x^{\prime})f_{n,\theta}(x^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{K_{1}(x^{\prime})}{[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}[2n(1-\rho)\theta^{2}-2(1-\rho)s_{1}(x^{\prime})\theta+s_{2}(x^{\prime})-2\rho p(x^{\prime})]\right\}

where

\displaystyle s_{1}(x^{\prime}):=\sum_{i}(x^{\prime}_{i1}+x^{\prime}_{i2}),\quad s_{2}(x^{\prime}):=\sum_{i}({x^{\prime}_{i1}}^{2}+{x^{\prime}_{i2}}^{2}),\quad p(x^{\prime})=\sum_{i}x^{\prime}_{i1}x^{\prime}_{i2},

\displaystyle K_{1}(x^{\prime})=\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)\right]^{-1}.

Note that, writing
c_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)=[\tau\sqrt{2\pi}(2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}})^{n}]^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n,x^{\prime}}(\theta)dQ(\theta)=

\displaystyle c_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}[2n(1-\rho)\theta^{2}-2(1-\rho)s_{1}(x^{\prime})\theta+s_{2}(x^{\prime})-2\rho p(x^{\prime})]-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}(\theta-\mu)^{2}]\right\}d\theta

\displaystyle=c_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\{-(A_{1}\theta^{2}-B_{1}(x^{\prime})\theta+C_{1}(x^{\prime}))\}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\left(C_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{1}^{2}(x^{\prime})}{4A_{1}}\right)\right\}}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left\{-A_{1}\left(\theta-\frac{B_{1}(x^{\prime})}{2A_{1}}\right)^{2}\right\}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\left(C_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{1}^{2}(x^{\prime})}{4A_{1}}\right)\right\}}{\tau\sqrt{2A_{1}}[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n}}

where

\displaystyle A_{1}=\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)}+\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}},\quad B_{1}(x^{\prime})=\frac{s_{1}(x^{\prime})}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)}+\frac{\mu}{\tau^{2}},\quad C_{1}(x^{\prime})=\frac{s_{2}(x^{\prime})-2\rho p(x^{\prime})}{2{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}.

Hence

K_{1}(x^{\prime})=\tau\sqrt{2A_{1}}[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n}\exp\left\{C_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{1}^{2}(x^{\prime})}{4A_{1}}\right\}

The posterior predictive density given
x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}
is

\displaystyle f_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{\theta}(x)r_{n,x^{\prime}}^{*}(\theta)g(\theta)d\theta=\frac{K_{1}(x^{\prime})}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n+1}}\cdot

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left\{-\frac{[2(n+1)(1-\rho)\theta^{2}-2(1-\rho)(x_{1}+x_{2}+s_{1}(x^{\prime}))\theta+(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+s_{2}(x^{\prime}))-2\rho(x_{1}x_{2}+p(x^{\prime}))]}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{(\theta^{2}-2\mu\theta+\mu^{2})}{2\tau^{2}}\right\}d\theta=

\displaystyle K_{2}(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\{-(A_{2}\theta^{2}-B_{2}(x,x^{\prime})\theta+C_{2}(x,x^{\prime}))\}d\theta=

\displaystyle K_{2}(x^{\prime})\exp\left\{-\left(C_{2}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{2}^{2}(x,x^{\prime})}{4A_{2}}\right)\right\}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left\{-A_{2}\left(\theta-\frac{B_{2}(x,x^{\prime})}{2A_{2}}\right)^{2}\right\}d\theta=

\displaystyle K_{2}(x^{\prime})\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{A_{2}}}\exp\left\{-\left(C_{2}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{2}^{2}(x,x^{\prime})}{4A_{2}}\right)\right\}

where

\displaystyle K_{2}(x^{\prime})=\frac{K_{1}(x^{\prime})}{\tau\sqrt{2\pi}[2\pi\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}]^{n+1}}=\frac{\sqrt{A_{1}}\exp\left\{C_{1}(x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{1}^{2}(x^{\prime})}{4A_{1}}\right\}}{2\pi^{3/2}\sigma^{2}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}},\quad A_{2}=\frac{(n+1)(1+\rho)}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle B_{2}(x.x^{\prime})=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+s_{1}(x^{\prime})}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)}+\frac{\mu}{\tau^{2}},\quad C_{2}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+s_{2}(x^{\prime})-2\rho(x_{1}x_{2}+p(x^{\prime}))}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{\tau^{2}}

We can write

\displaystyle C_{2}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{B_{2}^{2}(x,x^{\prime})}{4A_{2}}=A_{3}x_{1}^{2}+A_{3}x_{2}^{2}+B_{3}x_{1}x_{2}+C_{3}(x^{\prime})(x_{1}+x_{2})+D_{3}(x^{\prime})

where

\displaystyle A_{3}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}-\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}[4(n+1)(1+\rho)\tau^{2}+2\sigma^{2}]},

\displaystyle B_{3}=-\frac{\rho}{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}-\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}[2(n+1)(1+\rho)\tau^{2}+\sigma^{2}]},

\displaystyle C_{3}(x^{\prime})=-\frac{\tau^{2}s_{1}(x^{\prime})+\mu\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)}{\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}[2(n+1)(1+\rho)\tau^{2}+\sigma^{2}]},

\displaystyle D_{3}(x^{\prime})=\frac{\tau^{2}s_{2}(x^{\prime})-2\rho\tau^{2}p(x^{\prime})+2\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}{2\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}-\frac{\tau^{4}s_{1}(x^{\prime})^{2}+\mu^{2}\sigma^{4}(1+\rho)^{2}+2\mu\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)s_{1}(x^{\prime})}{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\rho)^{2}[4(n+1)(1+\rho)\tau^{2}+2\sigma^{2}]}

It is readily shown that
A_{3}>0
. It follows that the posterior predictive density
f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}
is the density of a normal bivariate distribution
N_{2}(m(x^{\prime}),\Sigma)
where

m(x^{\prime})=\binom{m_{1}(x^{\prime})}{m_{2}(x^{\prime})},\quad\Sigma=\sigma_{1}^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\rho_{1}\\
\rho_{1}&1\end{array}\right)

being

\rho_{1}=\frac{B_{3}}{2A_{3}},\quad\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{2A_{3}}{4A_{3}^{2}-B_{3}^{2}},\quad m_{1}(x^{\prime})=m_{2}(x^{\prime})=-\frac{C_{3}(x^{\prime})}{2(1-\rho_{1})}.

It is easy to see that, as can be expected,
|\rho_{1}|<1
and
\sigma_{1}^{2}>0
.
If we denote

a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau):=2(n+1)(1+\rho)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau^{2}}

We can write

\displaystyle\rho_{1}=-\frac{a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)+\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}{a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)-\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\cdot\rho,\quad\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)}{a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)-\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}}\cdot\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle m_{1}(x^{\prime})=m_{2}(x^{\prime})=\frac{s_{1}(x^{\prime})+(1+\rho)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\mu}{2(1-\rho_{1})(1+\rho)^{2}\sigma^{2}a_{n}(\rho,\sigma,\tau)}

It follows that the conditional distribution

\left({R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!R}\right)^{\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}\!\!:=N\big((1-\rho_{1})m_{1}(x^{\prime})+\rho_{1}x_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2}(1-\rho_{1}^{2})\big)

is the Bayes estimator of the conditional distribution

P_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}=N\big((1-\rho)\theta+\rho x_{1},\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})\big)

for the squared total variation function,
and its density
{f^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1}}
is the Bayes estimator of the conditional density

f_{\theta}^{X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}}(x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^{2})}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}[x_{2}-(1-\rho)\theta-\rho x_{1}]^{2}\right\}

for the
L^{1}
-squared loss function.

Moreover, its mean

E_{{R^{*}_{n,x^{\prime}}}^{\!\!\!\!\!R}}(\pi_{2}|\pi_{1}=x_{1})=(1-\rho_{1})m_{1}(x^{\prime})+\rho_{1}x_{1}

is the Bayes estimator of the regression curve

E_{\theta}(X_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1})=(1-\rho)\theta+\rho x_{1}

for the squared error loss function.
