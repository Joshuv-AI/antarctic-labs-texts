Abstract
. For given posets P P and Q Q and an integer n n , the generalized Turán problem for posets, asks for the maximum number of copies of Q Q in a P P -free subset of the n n -dimensional Boolean lattice, 2 [ n ] 2^{[n]} . In this paper, among other results, we show the following: (i) For every n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 , the maximum number of 2 2 -chains in a butterfly-free subfamily of 2 [ n ] 2^{[n]} is ⌈ n 2 ⌉ ​ ( n ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ ) \left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} . (ii) For every fixed s s , t t and k k , a K s , t K_{s,t} -free family in 2 [ n ] 2^{[n]} has O ⁡ ( n ​ ( n ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ ) ) O\left(n\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right) k k -chains. (iii) For every n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , the maximum number of 2 2 -chains in an N -free family is ( n ⌊ n / 2 ⌋ ) \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} , where N is a poset on 4 distinct elements { p 1 , p 2 , q 1 , q 2 } \{p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\} for which p 1 < q 1 p_{1}<q_{1} , p 2 < q 1 p_{2}<q_{1} and p 2 < q 2 p_{2}<q_{2} . We say that
P

Denote

For positive integers

has

For integers

and let

\displaystyle\binom{[n]}{k}=\left\{S\subseteq[n]:|S|=k\right\}.

We will sometimes use

The expression

denotes the largest subposet of the
n
-dimensional Boolean lattice that does not have

The study of

[
21
]
, who proved that
{\rm La}(n,P_{2})=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.
This was extended by Erdős
[

]
in 1945, who established

, and in fact determined

The systematic study of

[

in 1983, who studied the case where

is a fork poset, in which the

with

s and the

s forming an antichain.

In 2005, De Bonis, Katona, and Swanepoel
[

]
proved that for all

.

Determining
{\rm La}(n,P)
is known as the
poset Turán problem
. The basic results of Turán theory in graphs are well-known
[
22
,
7
,
8
]
, whereas the asymptotic value of
{\rm La}(n,P)
is not known for most posets

. Famously, even if

, the 4-element diamond poset, the asymptotic value of
{\rm La}(n,\Diamond)
is unknown
[

]
.

1.1.
Previous Results

One extension of Turán theory is when instead of determining the maximum number of edges in an
H
-free graph, one finds the maximum number of copies of
F
in an
H
-free graph, see for example Alon and Shikhelman
[
1
]
.

There is an obvious analogue of generalized Turán theory to posets, introduced by Gerbner, Keszegh, and Patkós
[
9
]
:

Definition 1
.

The maximum number of copies of
Q
in a
P
-free subfamily of the
n
-dimensional Boolean lattice is denoted by
{\rm La}(n,P,\#Q)
.

We note that the notation is slightly different, but we add the “
\#
” symbol to make it clear which poset is being counted.

Denote by
P_{k}
the chain on
k
elements.
Clearly,
{\rm La}(n,P)={\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{1})
.
The primary problem in generalized Turán theory on posets is determining
{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{\ell})
, where usually,
\ell
is less than the height of
P
.

The function
{\rm La}(n,P_{3},\#P_{2})
was determined by Katona
[
17
]
and reproved independently in
[
20
]
.
The function
{\rm La}(n,P_{k},\#P_{\ell})
was determined for every pair of integers
k>\ell\geq 1
.

Theorem 2

(Gerbner-Patkós
[
11
]
)
.

For any pair of integers
k>\ell\geq 1
,

\displaystyle{\rm La}(n,P_{k},\#P_{\ell})=\max_{0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k-1}\leq n}f(n,\ell,i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k-1}),

where
f(n,\ell,i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k-1})
denotes the number of
\ell
-chains in
\binom{[n]}{i_{1}}\cup\binom{[n]}{i_{2}}\cup\dots\cup\binom{[n]}{i_{k-1}}
.
If
k=\ell+1
, then
f(n,\ell,i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k-1})=\binom{n}{n-i_{k-1},i_{k-1}-i_{k-2},\dots,i_{2}-i_{1},i_{1}}
and the above maximum is attained when the integers
i_{1},i_{2}-i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1}-i_{k-2},n-i_{k-1}
differ by at most one. Consequently,

\displaystyle{\rm La}(n,P_{3},\#P_{2})=(1+o(1))\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2\pi n}3^{n}.

The general case of
P
, where
P
has height at least
3
, has also been resolved.

Theorem 3
.

[Gerbner-Methuku-Nagy-Patkós-Vizer
[
10
]
]

(a)

If
P
is a poset of height at least
3
, then

\displaystyle{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{2})=\Theta\left({\rm La}(n,P_{3},\#P_{2})\right).

(b)

If
T
is a poset
T
of height
2
whose Hasse diagram is a tree, then

\displaystyle{\rm La}(n,T,\#P_{2})=\Theta\left(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right).

(c)

If
P
is a poset of height
2
that has at least three elements, then

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)={\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{2})=O\left(n\cdot 2^{n}\right).

Since
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\Theta\left(2^{n}/\sqrt{n}\right)
, there is a gap in the asymptotic value of
{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{2})
for height
2
posets
P
.

1.2.
New results

We compute
{\rm La}(n,\bowtie,\#P_{2})
, and determine the extremal families.

Theorem 4
.

For all
n\geq 5
,

\displaystyle{\rm La}(n,\bowtie,\#P_{2})=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Moreover, if
n\geq 7
, then equality is only achieved by either
\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\cup\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}
or
\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}\cup\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil}
.

Remark 5
.

For
n\leq 4
, the expression in Theorem
4
is false.
For
n=2
, it is clear that
{\rm La}(2,\bowtie,\#P_{2})=4>2=\binom{2}{1}
.
For
n=3
, the family
\{\emptyset,1,2,3,123\}
witnesses that
{\rm La}(3,\bowtie,\#P_{2})\geq 7>6=2\binom{3}{2}
.
For
n=4
, the family
\{\emptyset,12,13,14,23,24,34,1234\}
witnesses that

.

It is not difficult to see that
{\rm La}(3,\bowtie,\#P_{2})=7
but we did not choose to go through the case analysis to compute
{\rm La}(4,\bowtie,\#P_{2})
or to determine all extremal families for

when

.

Theorem 6
.

Fix

,

\displaystyle\textstyle{\rm La}(n,K_{s,t},\#P_{k})=O\left(n\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)=O\left(\sqrt{n}\cdot 2^{n}\right).

Because every finite poset

of height

is a subposet of

If

We start with the proof of Theorem

For Theorem

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
be a
K_{s,t}
-free family. We color the sets of
2^{[n]}
in the following way:
If a set
G
is strictly contained by (that is, “below”) at least
t
elements of
{\mathcal{F}}
then
G
is blue, otherwise it is red.
Note that the blue sets form a downset and the red sets form an upset.
Furthermore, each blue set contains at most
s-1
sets from
{\mathcal{F}}
(including, possibly, itself), otherwise a
K_{s,t}
would be formed.
Define a
critical pair
to be a pair of sets
(G,G^{\prime})
where
G\subsetneq G^{\prime}
,
|G^{\prime}|=|G|+1
,
G
is blue and
G^{\prime}
is red.

Let us count copies of
P_{k}
of the form
F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq F_{k}
, according to the colors of
F_{1},F_{k}
.
Suppose first that
F_{k}
is blue. By definition, a blue set contains at most
s-1
other members of
{\mathcal{F}}
,
therefore the number of such
P_{k}
s is at most
\binom{s-1}{k-1}2^{n}
.
Now assume that
F_{1}
is red.
By the definition of the coloring, a red set is below at most
t-1
other members of
{\mathcal{F}}
, hence the number of such
P_{k}
s is at most
\binom{t-1}{k-1}2^{n}
.
This gives that the number of monochromatic
P_{k}
s is at most
\left[\binom{s-1}{k-1}+\binom{t-1}{k-1}\right]2^{n}<2^{\max\{s+t\}+n}=O\left(\sqrt{n}\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
.

Finally, suppose that
F_{1}
is blue and
F_{k}
is red. For every
k
-chain of the form
F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq F_{k}
, there is a pair
F_{1}\subseteq G\subsetneq G^{\prime}\subseteq F_{k}
such that
(G,G^{\prime})
is a critical pair and each of
G
and
G^{\prime}
are comparable with each set
F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Because the blue sets form a downset and the red sets form an upset, we have that each maximal chain
{\mathcal{C}}
contains at most one critical pair
(C,C^{\prime})
with
C,C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

Now let us count the triples
(G,G^{\prime},{\mathcal{C}})
with
G,G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the pair
(G,G^{\prime})
a critical pair, and the existence of
F_{1}\subsetneq F_{2}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq F_{k}
such that
F_{1}\subseteq G,G^{\prime}\subseteq F_{k}
and each
F_{i}
is comparable with
G,G^{\prime}
.
Each such pair
(G,G^{\prime})
is contained in
|G|!(n-|G|-1)!
maximal chains, and as we remarked earlier, every chain contains at most one such pair.
So we obtain
\sum_{(G,G^{\prime})}|G|!(n-|G|-1)!\leq n!
.
Since
k!(n-k-1)!
is minimized when
k=\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, we obtain that the number of summands, and thus the number of pairs
(G,G^{\prime})
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor!\cdot\left(n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\right)!}=\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Finally, we claim that the number of
k
-chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
to which every pair
(G,G^{\prime})
belongs is at most
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\binom{s-1}{i}\binom{t}{k-i}
.
Indeed, because
G
is blue, it contains (allowing for itself) at most
s-1
sets of
{\mathcal{F}}
and because
G^{\prime}
is red, it is contained (allowing for itself) in at most
t
sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
.
So,
\binom{s-1}{i}\binom{t}{k-i}
gives a bound on the number of
k
-chains such that the first
i
sets of which are contained in
G
and the remaining
k-i
sets of which contain
G^{\prime}
.

Therefore, the number of
k
-chains with
F_{1}
blue and
F_{k}
red is at most

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\binom{s-1}{i}\cdot\binom{t}{k-i}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}<2^{s+t-1}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

So the total number of pairs in containment is at most
2^{\max\{s,t\}+n}+2^{s+t-1}\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=O\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

The set system
\binom{[n]}{\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil-1}\cup\binom{[n]}{\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil}
is
\bowtie
-free and has
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}

proves the lower bound.

To prove the upper bound, consider a
\bowtie
-free family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
.
Our plan is as follows:
We will partition the set of containments and associate to each part a disjoint subset of full chains.
Then for each part in this partition, we will bound the ratio of the size of the associated subset of full chains to the size of that part.

Formally, suppose
{\mathcal{P}}_{1},{\mathcal{P}}_{2},\dots,{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
is a partition of
{\mathcal{P}}=\{(F,F^{\prime}):F\subsetneq F^{\prime}\mbox{ for }F,F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}\}
and there is a function
h:\{{\mathcal{P}}_{1},{\mathcal{P}}_{2},\dots,{\mathcal{P}}_{m}\}\rightarrow 2^{\mathbf{C}_{n}}
such that
1\leq i\neq j\leq m
implies
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})\cap h({\mathcal{P}}_{j})=\emptyset
.
Denote by
\alpha
the minimum of
\frac{|h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})|}{|{\mathcal{P}}_{i}|}
over
1\leq i\leq m
.
By averaging,

|{\mathcal{P}}|=|{\mathcal{P}}_{1}|+|{\mathcal{P}}_{2}|+\ldots+|{\mathcal{P}}_{m}|\leq\frac{\mathbf{C}_{n}}{\alpha}=\frac{n!}{\alpha}

We define a function
h
with the above properties for which
\alpha
is at least
\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil-1\right)!
because then

|{\mathcal{P}}|\leq\frac{n!}{\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil-1\right)!}=\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Denote
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
(and
{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
) the set of inclusion-wise minimal (maximal) sets of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
Note that we may ignore any set in
{\mathcal{F}}
that is both minimal and maximal because such sets will not contribute to the family of containments.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}:={\mathcal{F}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{1}-{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
. Note that
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
is an antichain, otherwise we would have a
4
-chain, which forms a
\bowtie
poset.

First consider all sets
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{s}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
.
Since
{\mathcal{F}}
is
\bowtie
-free, for every
1\leq i\leq s
there are two unique sets
X_{i},Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
X_{i}\subset Y_{i}\subset Z_{i}
, where
X_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
and
Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
. Greedily set
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i}),~(Y_{i},Z_{i}),~(X_{i},Z_{i})\}
, except if the pair
(X_{i},Z_{i})
was already used for some
j<i
, then we set
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i}),~(Y_{i},Z_{i})\}
.
In either case, we let
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})=\mathbf{C}_{Y_{i}}
, the set of chains that contain
Y_{i}
.
Clearly,
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!
and
|{\mathcal{P}}_{i}|\leq 3
and so

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{|h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})|}{|{\mathcal{P}}_{i}|}\geq\frac{1}{3}\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!\geq\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil-1\right)!\qquad\mbox{ for every $1\leq i\leq s$.}

The last inequality comes from the condition that
n\geq 5
.

We still have to consider those pairs
F\subset F^{\prime}
for which
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1},~F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
and there is no set
Y\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
F\subset Y\subset F^{\prime}
.
Let such pairs be
\left\{(F_{1},F^{\prime}_{1}),(F_{2},F^{\prime}_{2}),\dots,(F_{t},F^{\prime}_{t})\right\}
.
We set
{\mathcal{P}}_{s+j}=\{(F_{j},F^{\prime}_{j})\}
and so
|{\mathcal{P}}_{s+j}|=1
.
In order to define
h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})
for all
1\leq j\leq t
, we set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{H\in 2^{[n]}:F_{j}~\text{is the unique subset (allowing for itself) of}\ H~\text{in}\ {\mathcal{F}}\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{H^{\prime}\in 2^{[n]}:F^{\prime}_{j}~\text{is the unique superset (allowing for itself) of}\ H^{\prime}~\text{in}\ {\mathcal{F}}\}.

Since
F_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{3}
, we have
F_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
.
Furthermore, for any set
F_{j}\subset H\subset F_{j}^{\prime}
, we have
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\cup{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
, otherwise
H
would have two proper subsets and two proper supersets in
{\mathcal{F}}
, forming a
\bowtie
.
Here we used that there is no
H\in{\mathcal{F}}
for which
F_{j}\subset H\subset F^{\prime}_{j}
.

Observe that if
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
, then every set between
F_{j}
and
H
is in
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
and if
H^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
, then every set between
H^{\prime}
and
F_{j}^{\prime}
is in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
.
Thus, we can find two sets
G_{j}
and
G_{j}^{\prime}
such that
F_{j}\subseteq G_{j}\subset G_{j}^{\prime}\subseteq F_{j}^{\prime}
,
G_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
,
G^{\prime}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}^{\prime}
and
|G^{\prime}_{j}|=|G_{j}|+1
.
We let
h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})=\mathbf{C}_{G_{j},G_{j}^{\prime}}
for an arbitrary such pair, and thus

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{|h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})|}{|{\mathcal{P}}_{s+j}|}=|h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})|=|G|!\cdot(n-|G|-1)!\geq\left\lceil{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right\rceil!\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right\rfloor!=\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil-1\right)!,

as claimed.

We still need to show that the images are pairwise disjoint.
It is clear that
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})\cap h({\mathcal{P}}_{j})=\mathbf{C}_{Y_{i}}\cap\mathbf{C}_{Y_{j}}=\emptyset
where
1\leq i,j\leq s
, because
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
is an antichain.
Now consider
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})\cap h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})
where
1\leq i\leq s
and
1\leq j\leq t
.
If a chain contains
G_{j}
and
G_{j}^{\prime}
, then it cannot contain any member of
{\mathcal{F}}
other than
F_{j}
and
F^{\prime}_{j}
since
G_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
and
G_{j}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{j}
.
In particular such a chain cannot contain any
Y_{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
and so
h({\mathcal{P}}_{i})\cap h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+j})=\emptyset
.

Finally, suppose that a chain belongs to
h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+a})\cap h({\mathcal{P}}_{s+b})
via the pairs
(G_{a},G_{a}^{\prime})
and
(G_{b},G_{b}^{\prime})
, respectively, where
1\leq a,b\leq t
.
Let
S=G_{a}\cup G_{b}
.
Since both
G_{a}
and
G_{b}
are on the same chain, either
S=G_{a}
or
S=G_{b}
.
Thus,
S
is in both
{\mathcal{H}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{b}
and must contain only one member of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
As a result,
F_{a}=F_{b}
.
Analogously, using
S^{\prime}=G_{a}^{\prime}\cap G_{b}^{\prime}
, we may conclude that
F_{a}^{\prime}=F_{b}^{\prime}
.
Consequently,
a=b
as desired.

It remains to establish equality in the case where
n\geq 7
.
In order for equality to hold, both (
1
) and (
2
) must hold with equality for each
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
.
Since
n\geq 7
implies (
1
) never holds with equality,
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
is empty and
s=0
.
Moreover, in order for (
2
) to hold with equality, every pair
(G_{j},G_{j}^{\prime})
must have sizes in
\left\{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor,\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor+1\right\}
or in
\left\{\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil,\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil+1\right\}
.

Next observe that if
{\mathcal{F}}
is extremal, then for every pair
(F_{j},F^{\prime}_{j})
we must have
F_{j}=G_{j},F^{\prime}_{j}=G^{\prime}_{j}
. Indeed, if
F_{j}\subsetneq G_{j}\subsetneq G^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq F^{\prime}_{j}
, then there exists at least one other pair
(G^{*}_{j},G^{**}_{j})\neq(G_{j},G^{\prime}_{j})
with
F_{j}\subseteq G^{*}_{j}\subseteq G^{**}_{j}\subseteq F^{\prime}_{j}
,
G^{*}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
,
G^{**}_{j}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{j}^{\prime}
and
|G^{*}_{j}|+1=|G^{**}_{j}|
. So the mapping
h
could be defined as
h(F_{j},F^{\prime}_{j})=\mathbf{C}_{G_{j},G^{\prime}_{j}}\cup\mathbf{C}_{G^{*}_{j},G^{**}_{j}}
, and then (
2
) could not hold with equality.

So
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
is empty, for every pair
(F_{j},F^{\prime}_{j})
we have
F_{j}=G_{j},F^{\prime}_{j}=G^{\prime}_{j}
, and the sizes are in
\left\{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor,\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor+1\right\}
or in
\left\{\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil,\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil+1\right\}
.

If
n
is odd, then equality can only occur if
{\mathcal{F}}=\binom{[n]}{(n-1)/2}\cup\binom{[n]}{(n+1)/2}
, as needed.

If
n
is even, then each pair
(G,G^{\prime})
must contain an element from
\binom{[n]}{n/2}
and an element from
\binom{[n]}{n/2-1}\cup\binom{[n]}{n/2+1}
.
Because
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}=\emptyset
, then no member of
\binom{[n]}{n/2}
can have a member of
{\mathcal{F}}
both above and below it.
So in order for equality to hold, each
F\in\binom{[n]}{n/2}
must be in
{\mathcal{F}}
and either
F
has
n/2
members of
{\mathcal{F}}
above it (an
under-element
) or
n/2
members of
{\mathcal{F}}
below it (an
over-element
).
If there are both under elements and over elements, there must be a pair
F_{1},F_{2}
whose symmetric difference is 2,
F_{1}
is an under-element and
F_{2}
is an over-element.
In that case,
F_{1}\cup F_{2},F_{1}\cap F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, which would put
F_{1},F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
, a contradiction to that set being empty.
Thus, in order for equality to hold, either
{\mathcal{F}}=\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\cup\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}
or
{\mathcal{F}}=\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}\cup\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil}
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

Definition 12
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a set system. The
comparability graph
of
{\mathcal{F}}
is a simple graph
G
whose vertices correspond to the sets of
{\mathcal{F}}
. The vertices representing two sets
A
and
B
are connected by an edge if
A\subset B
or
B\subset A
holds.

Definition 13
.

The convex hull of a set system
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is

conv({\mathcal{F}})=\{F\in 2^{[n]}~\big|~\exists F_{1},F_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}},~F_{1}\subseteq F\subseteq F_{2}\}.

Definition 14
.

Two families
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
in
2^{[n]}
are
incomparable
if neither
F\subseteq F^{\prime}
nor
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
holds for every pair
F\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
F^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
.

Note that if
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
are incomparable then
conv({\mathcal{H}})
and
conv({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime})
are also incomparable. Therefore no full chain can intersect both
conv({\mathcal{H}})
and
conv({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
A\subset B\subseteq[n]
. The number of full chains meeting the set
\{F~:~A\subset F\subset B\}
is
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-|B|+|A|}{|A|}}
.

Proof.

A full chain meets the set
\{F~:~A\subset F\subset B\}
if and only if all elements of
A
appear before the elements of
[n]\setminus B
. This is true for a random chain with probability
\binom{n-|B|+|A|}{|A|}^{-1}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

To show
{\rm La}(n,N,\#P_{2})\geq\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, consider the family
\{\emptyset\}\cup{\binom{[n]}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}}
. It has
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
containments and avoids the subposet
N
.

To prove the upper bound, consider an
N
-free family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
, and let
G
be its comparability graph. We will classify the possible components of
G
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}
contains the poset
P_{3}
(three sets
A\subset B\subset C
), then there can be no fourth set comparable to any of these three, otherwise an
N
would be formed. A component corresponding to a
P_{3}
is a triangle.

If a component contains no
P_{3}
, then all its paths must be alternating.
Since we cannot have
N
(the
3
-edge alternating path), there is no path with at least three edges.
Therefore, a component consists of either a minimal element and some of its supersets forming an antichain or a maximal element and some its subsets forming an antichain.
In either case, the corresponding component in
G
is a star.

Note that a full chain can meet the convex hull of at most one of the components. We will show that if a component has
r
containments (edges in
G
), then at least
r\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!
full chains meet its convex hull. Since there are
n!
full chains, this means that the number of containments is at most
n!/\left(\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!\right)=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

If a
P_{3}
component is formed by the sets
A\subset B\subset C
then its convex hull is the set
\{F~:~A\subset F\subset C\}
. By Lemma
15
, it meets
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-|C|+|A|}{|A|}}
full chains. Since
|C|-|A|\geq 2
, this is at least
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}=n(n-1)\cdot\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)!\cdot\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1\right)!
, which is at least
3\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!
when
n\geq 3
.

A
P_{3}
-free component of size
r+1
has
r
containments. It also has
r
sets forming an antichain. Each of them meets at least
\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!
full chains, and these are different due to the antichain property.
∎

.

Lemma 16

(Katona, Nagy
[
18
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a family of
t
subsets of
[n]
. Then the number of full chains meeting at least one element of
{\mathcal{H}}
is at least

\left(t-\frac{t(t-1)}{n}\right)\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

For
n\geq 2
, consider the following family:

{\mathcal{F}}:=\left\{F\subset[n]~:~|F\cap[n-2]|=\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

This family can be divided into
\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}
pairwise incomparable
4
-tuples. Each of these are isomorphic to the Boolean lattice
B_{2}
, and has
5
containments. Therefore the number of containments in
{\mathcal{F}}
is
5\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}
.

To prove that for
n
large enough a
{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
-free family
{\mathcal{F}}
has at most
5\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}
containments, we use the same strategy as in the proof of Theorem
8
. We will describe all possible components of the comparability graph
G
, and show that if a component has
c
containments then its convex hull meets at least

\frac{c\cdot n!}{5\cdot\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}=\left(\frac{4}{5}+o(1)\right)\cdot c\cdot\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil!

full chains. There are
n!
full chains and each of them meets the convex hull of at most one component, therefore the number of containments is at most
5\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}
.

Before finding all possible components, we describe two types for which the statement is easy to verify. These two types will cover most of our cases.

If a component has at most
c\leq 100
containments, and its convex hull contains at least
c
elements, then we call it
type I
. Choose
c
sets from the convex hull. By Lemma
16
, the number of full chains meeting them is at least
(c-o(1))\cdot\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil!
, which is more than
\left(\frac{4}{5}+o(1)\right)\cdot c\cdot\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil!
for large enough
n
.

If a component has
c
containments (for
c
of any size) and at least
\frac{5}{6}c
of its members form an antichain then we call it
type II
. Each of the sets in the antichain meets at least
\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil!
full chains, and these are pairwise different. Therefore, a type II component meets at least
\frac{5}{6}c\cdot\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil!
chains, which is more than
\left(\frac{4}{5}+o(1)\right)\cdot c\cdot\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor!\cdot\left\lceil{n/2}\right\rceil!
for large enough
n
.

Now let us list all possible components of
G
and the corresponding set systems in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that
{\mathcal{F}}
being
{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
-free means that
G
has no
5
-vertex path.

First, consider a tree component in
G
. As we work with
{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
-free posets, it contains no
4
-edge path.
It is easy to see that this implies that there must be an edge that shares a vertex with all other edges. Let
A\subset B
be the sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
corresponding to the endpoints of this special edge. If a third set
C\in{\mathcal{F}}
would be a subset of
A
or a superset of
B
then
A,B
and
C
would span a triangle in
G
, which is not allowed in this case. Therefore all other sets of
{\mathcal{F}}
are either supersets of
A
or subsets of
B
, so the component is a star. If this component has
t
sets, then the number of containments is
t-1
and there is an antichain of size
t-2
, formed by all sets of the component, except for
A
and
B
. If
t<7
then the component is of type I. If
t\geq 7
then it is of type II, since
t-2\geq\frac{5}{6}(t-1).

From now on, we assume that the component has a cycle. It cannot be a cycle of length
5
or more since it would contain a
5
-vertex path. First, assume that the component has a triangle, but no
4
-cycle. A triangle in
G
corresponds to three sets
A\subset B\subset C
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. If there are no other sets in this component then it is of type I.

If there is a fourth set
D\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
B\subset D
or
D\subset B
, then
\{A,C,B,D\}
is a
4
-cycle, which is not allowed in this case. Therefore, any set of
{\mathcal{F}}
comparable to
\{A,B,C\}
must be either a superset of
A
or a subset of
C
. If there are sets
D,E\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
A\subset D
and
E\subset C
, then
D,A,B,C,E
form an
S\in{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
. Note that
D=E
would create a
4
-cycle, which is not allowed in this case.

We have only two subcases remaining to handle. Either there are some sets
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
A\subset D_{i}
for all
i
and
\{D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}\}
is incomparable to
\{B,C\}
, or there are some sets
E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
E_{j}\subset C
for all
j
and
\{E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{k}\}
is incomparable to
\{A,B\}
. By symmetry, it is enough to consider the first subcase. The sets
\{D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}\}
must form an antichain, since
D_{i}\subset D_{j}
would mean that
\{B,C,A,D_{j},D_{i}\}
form an
M\in{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
. Therefore the component has
k+3
sets,
k+3
containments, and contains the antichain
\{B,D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{k}\}
of size
k+1
. If
k<9
then the component is of type I. If
k\geq 9
then it is of type II, since
k+1\geq\frac{5}{6}(k+3)
.

Finally, we handle the case when a component contains a
4
-cycle. If there were a fifth set in
{\mathcal{F}}
comparable to any of the four sets in the cycle in any way then these sets would form a poset in
{\mathcal{P}}_{5}
. Therefore, we can assume that this component consists of only four sets. If there are only four containments, then the component is of type I. If there are six containments among the four sets then they must form a chain
A\subset B\subset C\subset D
. The convex hull is the set
\{F~:~A\subset F\subset D\}
. By Lemma
15
, it meets
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-|D|+|A|}{|A|}}
chains. Since
|D|-|A|\geq 3
, this is at least
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-3}{\left\lfloor{(n-3)/2}\right\rfloor}}
, which is more than
\frac{6\cdot n!}{5\cdot\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}
for
n\geq 3
.

The last subcase has five containments among the four sets. This can be achieved in three different ways:

•

A\subset B\subset C,D
. In this case, the size of the convex hull is at least five since it also contains at least one set
E
such that
E\not=B
and
A\subset E\subset D
. With five containments and a convex hull of size at least five, the component is of type I.

•

A,B\subset C\subset D
. This case can be handled similarly to the previous one.

•

A\subset B,C\subset D
. The convex hull of this component is the set
\{F~:~A\subset F\subset D\}
.
By Lemma
15
, it meets
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-|D|+|A|}{|A|}}
full chains. Since
|D|-|A|\geq 2
must hold, the minimum of this quantity is
\frac{n!}{\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}=\frac{5\cdot n!}{5\cdot\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}
.

Thus, every component with
c
containments has a convex hull that meets at least
\frac{c\cdot n!}{5\cdot\binom{n-2}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor-1}}
full chains, as desired.

∎

.

Theorem 17

(Bukh
[
2
]
)
.

For every poset
T
with a tree Hasse diagram, we have
La(n,T)=(h(T)-1+o(1))\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, where
h(T)
denotes the height of
T
.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

To prove the first inequality, let
{\mathcal{F}}=\{\emptyset\}\cup{\binom{[n]}{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}}\cup\{[n]\}
. This family contains neither
M
nor
W
. The number of containments in
{\mathcal{F}}
is
2\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+1
.

To prove the second inequality, assume that we have six pairwise different sets
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{6}
such that
A_{i}\subset A_{i+1}
or
A_{i}\supset A_{i+1}
holds for all
1\leq i\leq 5
. It can be checked that one can select five of these sets forming an
M
or a
W
. Indeed, let
m
be the most number of consecutive
A_{i}
s that form a chain, so
A_{i}\subset A_{i+1}\subset\ldots\subset A_{i+m-1}
or
A_{i}\supset A_{i+1}\supset\ldots\supset A_{i+m-1}
.
If
m\geq 5
, then a
5
-chain is both an
M
and a
W
.
If
m=2
, then one of
\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{5}\}
and
\{A_{2},A_{3},\dots,A_{6}\}
forms an
M
and the other forms a
W
. If
m=4
, then by symmetry we can assume
A_{i}\subset A_{i+1}\subset A_{i+2}\subset A_{i+3}
. If
i\geq 2
, then
\{A_{i-1},A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+1},A_{i+3}\}
forms a
W
, otherwise
\{A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+1},A_{i+3},A_{i+4}\}
forms an
M
. Finally, if
m=3
, then again we can assume
A_{i}\subset A_{i+1}\subset A_{i+2}
. If
i\leq 2
then we have
A_{i+2}\supset A_{i+3}
by the maximality of the chain
A_{i}\subset A_{i+1}\subset A_{i+2}
. If
A_{i+3}\subset A_{i+4}
then
\{A_{i+1},A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+3},A_{i+4}\}
forms a
W
. If
A_{i+3}\supset A_{i+4}
then
\{A_{i+1},A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+4},A_{i+3}\}
forms a
W
. If
i\geq 3
then we can similarly conclude that
A_{i-1}\supset A_{i}
and either
\{A_{i-2},A_{i-1},A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+1}\}
or
\{A_{i-1},A_{i-2},A_{i},A_{i+2},A_{i+1}\}
forms an
M
.
This means that a family that avoids both
M
and
W
will avoid all posets in
{\mathcal{P}}_{6}
, proving the inequality.

To prove the last relation, assume that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is a
{\mathcal{P}}_{6}
-free family. It means that its comparability graph
G
contains no
6
-vertex path. A theorem of Erdős and Gallai
[
6
]
states that in this case
|E(G)|\leq 2|V(G)|
. Therefore the number of containments in
|{\mathcal{F}}|
is at most
2|{\mathcal{F}}|
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}
avoids the
6
-element alternating path poset (a tree poset of height two), Theorem
17
implies
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq(1+o(1))\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, completing the proof.
∎

4.
Open problems

Determining the exact or asymptotic value of any
{\rm La}(n,P,\#Q)
would be interesting, but there are two natural problems that arise from our current results and previous findings from
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
.

Problem 18
.

Determine the largest possible order of magnitude of
{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{k})
over all posets of height at most
\ell\leq k
.
The case
\ell=2
is settled for arbitrary values of
k
: Theorem
6
gives the upper bound
La(n,P,\#P_{2})=O_{k}\left(n\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
, while the family

{\mathcal{F}}_{k}:=\left\{F\in 2^{[n]}:\left|F\cap[n-k+2]\right|=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}~\text{or}\ \left|F\cap[n-k+2]\right|=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}+1\right\}

is
K_{s,s}
-free for large enough
s
and contains
\Omega_{k}\left(n\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
chains of length
k
.

Let us repeat some observations on posets of height
2
and chains of size
2
. It is clear that if the family consisting of the two middle levels does not contain any copy of
P
, then we have
{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{2})\geq\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil\cdot\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. As mentioned in the introduction,
{\rm La}(n,T,\#P_{2})=O_{T}\left(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
was proved in
[
10
]
.

Problem 19
.

Does there exist a poset
P
contained in
\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\cup\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}
such that
{\rm La}(n,P,\#P_{2})=\omega\left(\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\right)
?

Observe that
\bowtie
is a poset for which the value of
{\rm La}(n,\bowtie,\#P_{k})
is determined for all values of
k
. The ordinary forbidden subposet problem, case
k=1
, was solved by DeBonis, Katona, and Swanepoel
[
3
]
. The case
k=2
is settled by Theorem
4
, the case
k=3
is an easy proposition by Gerbner, Keszegh, and Patkós
[
9
]
, while for all
k\geq 4
the value is zero as a 4-chain contains
\bowtie
. It would be interesting to find other posets
P
for which all values
La(n,P,\#P_{k})
can be determined.

Problem 20
.

Determine
{\rm La}(n,\Diamond_{3},\#P_{k})
for
k=2,3,4
, where
\Diamond_{3}
is the poset on 5 elements
a,b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},c
with
a<b_{i}<c
for all
i=1,2,3
. The case
k=1
was solved by Griggs, Li, and Lu
[
14
]
.
