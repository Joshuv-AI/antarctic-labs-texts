Fluctuation theorems in general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
Yifan Cai, Tao Wang, Liu Zhao

Abstract
Based on the recently proposed framework of general relativistic stochastic mechanics [ J. Stat. Phys. , 190:193, 2023; J. Stat. Phys. , 190:181, 2023] and stochastic thermodynamics [ SciPost Physics Core 7, 082, 2024] at the ensemble level, this work focuses on general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics at the trajectory level. The first law of stochastic thermodynamics is reformulated and the fluctuation theorems are proved on this level, with emphasis on maintaining fully general covariance and on the choice of observers. Keywords: Langevin equation, fluctuation theorem, time-reversal symmetry, general relativity



1
Introduction

One of the central problems in modern statistical physics is the origin of irreversibility
in macroscopic and mesoscopic systems. This problem can be traced back to Boltzmann’s
efforts in proving the second law of thermodynamics starting from deterministic
mechanics, which has resulted in the well-known H-theorem
[
1
]
.
However, the debates about the validity of (the assumptions of) the H-theorem
have lasted for more than a century. The most acute criticism of the H-theorem is
reflected by the Loschmidt paradox
[
2
,
3
]
,
which roughly states that the macroscopic arrow of time cannot possibly arise from the
underlying microscopic mechanics obeying time reversal symmetry (TRS). This paradox remained
unresolved until the 1990s, when numerous works
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
emerged, revealing that the forward
and reversed processes are not probabilistically equally likely, provided a certain
dissipative effect exists on the mechanical level. These results, known as
fluctuation theorems, largely resolved the debates and paradoxes related to the H-theorem.
In particular, the most questioned molecular chaos hypothesis adopted in proving
the H-theorem is completely avoided in proving the fluctuation theorems.

Almost at the same time, Sekimoto
[
7
]
established the first
law of stochastic thermodynamics on the trajectory level by use of the Langevin equation.
This formulation establishes a connection between stochastic mechanics and fluctuation
theorems. In 2005, Seifert
[
8
]
presented a version of
fluctuation theorem based on the overdamped Langevin equation. Subsequently,
several fluctuation theorems based on non-relativistic stochastic mechanics were presented

[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
,
making it clear that stochastic mechanics provides an ideal starting point
for constructing fluctuation theorems and interpreting the origin of irreversibility.

Most of the works mentioned above were carried out in the non-relativistic regime.
Nowadays, it is widely acknowledged that thermodynamics and relativity are
both concentrated on the universal principles that every physical
system must obey. It is important to establish fluctuation theorems based on
these universal principle theories. However, since the spacetime
symmetry in relativity imposes stronger protection of time-reversal invariance,
the extension of fluctuation theorems to the relativistic regime proves to be
more difficult. The central difficulty lies in
how to incorporate the breaking of time reversal invariance while still maintaining
relativistic covariance. Refs.
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]

considered the extension of fluctuation theorems to the special relativistic regime.
However, the long awaited general relativistic extension is still beyond our
ability to understand.

Recently, we developed a framework for dealing with stochastic mechanics on
curved spacetime, and we investigated the general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
based on this framework
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
Meanwhile, we also established a version of a fluctuation theorem on an arbitrary
curved Riemannian manifold
[
12
]
. The aim of the present work is to
employ the framework established in
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
and make use of the technique introduced in

[
12
]
to construct a version of a fluctuation theorem based
on a fully general relativistic description of stochastic mechanics and
stochastic thermodynamics.

In our framework of relativistic stochastic mechanics
[
19
,
20
]
, it is important to liberate the observer from the coordinate
system. Rather than fixing the zeroth component of the coordinate system, we utilize
the observer’s proper time
t
to label the configuration space
\mathcal{S}_{t}

and the space of microstates
\Sigma_{t}
, enabling our theory to possess
general covariance. For this purpose, we first clarify the geometry of the space of

\mathcal{S}_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}
in Sec.
2
. To make the construction
more self-contained, we provide a brief review of the basics of our framework
of relativistic stochastic mechanics and relativistic stochastic thermodynamics in
Sec.
3
, and the first law of relativistic
stochastic thermodynamics at the trajectory level is established in this section also.
The separation of the observer from the coordinate system is also important
in describing the time reversal transformation (TRT) in curved spacetime.
In contrast to the usual practice in special relativistic theories (including
special relativistic field theories) in which the TRT is often described as a
coordinate transformation
(t,x^{i})\mapsto(-t,x^{i})
, our general covariant framework
calls for interpreting the TRT as a transformation from a future-directed observer
to a past-directed observer. This concept is elucidated in detail in
Sec.
4
. Reference
[
19
]
presents a method
to establish the covariant relativistic Langevin equation from the perspective
of the observer. Based on this approach, the forward and reversed processes are defined,
respectively, as stochastic processes from the perspectives of the
future-directed and past-directed observers. In Sec.
5
,
we demonstrate that the forward and reversed processes possess distinct
probabilities, thus breaking the TRS and leading to
a version of a fluctuation theorem on a curved spacetime manifold.
Finally, in Sec.
6
, we provide brief concluding remarks.

We maintain the notations and conventions consistent with Refs.

[
19
,
20
]
. To distinguish random
variables from their realizations, the former are labeled with extra tildes.
For instance,
\tilde{x}
represents a random variable, while
x
denotes its realization.
Several manifolds of different dimensions will be relevant in our discussion. These
include the
(d+1)
-dimensional spacetime manifold
\mathcal{M}

with metric
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
of signature
(-,+,\cdots,+)
,
its tangent bundle
T\mathcal{M}
of dimension
(2d+2)
, and certain submanifolds
within the tangent bundle. To distinguish tensors on these different manifolds,
we introduce distinct indices. Lower-case Greek letters, such as
\alpha,\beta,\mu,\nu,\rho,\ldots
, are used as concrete indices and range from
0
to
d
.
Lower-case Latin letters, such as
i,j,k,l,m,\ldots
are also used as concrete indices,
which range from
1
to
d
. Lastly, lower-case Latin letters
a,b,c,\ldots

are used as abstract indices. This paper is intended to be as mathematically rigorous
as possible. A more concise summary of the main results can be found in
Ref.
[
22
]
.

2
Geometry of the space of microstates

Statistical physics is built on top of the space of microstates.
For systems consisting of classical massive particles,
the space of microstates can be subdivided into configuration space
and momentum space. In a relativistic context, configuration space
is a subspace of the spacetime manifold

\mathcal{M}
consisting of simultaneous events at a given instance of time,
while the momentum space for each individual particle is a subspace of the
tangent (or cotangent) space of the
spacetime at a given event which obeys the mass shell condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(x,p):=g_{\mu\nu}(x)p^{\mu}p^{\nu}+m^{2}=0.

(1)

It is important to remember that,
due to the non-degeneracy of the spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}(x)
,
the tangent and cotangent spaces are dual to each other, and both can
describe the momentum space equally well. In this work, we adopt the tangent space description.
When cotangent space variables appear, they are considered to be
linear functions of the tangent space variables,
e.g.
p_{\mu}=g_{\mu\nu}(x)p^{\nu}
.

In principle, the space of microstates for a single particle
should be considered as a submanifold of
the mass shell bundle

\displaystyle\Gamma_{m}:=\{(x,p)\in T\mathcal{M}|\mathcal{H}(x,p)=0\},

(2)

with the configuration space taken to be a subspace of the spacetime
manifold
\mathcal{M}
consisting of
simultaneous events
. To clarify the
concept of simultaneous events, we need to introduce an arbitrary observer field
that is encoded by a normalized timelike vector field
Z^{\mu}

obeying
g_{\mu\nu}Z^{\mu}Z^{\nu}=-1
. For convenience, we shall refer to this arbitrary observer field as
Alice
.
When considering the motion of a Brownian particle inside a heat reservoir,
there is a particular observer field that comoves with the reservoir.
This particular observer field will be referred to as
Bob
.

Consider the worldline
x_{\tau}
of a massive relativistic particle of which
\tau
is
its proper time. If the time orientations of Alice and the particle align,

i.e.
g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}_{\tau}Z^{\nu}<0
, where
p^{\mu}_{\tau}:=m{\rm d}x^{\mu}_{\tau}/{\rm d}\tau
,
the part of the mass shell bundle in which the phase trajectory lies is defined as
the
future mass shell bundle relative to Alice
and is denoted as

\Gamma^{+}_{m}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{+}_{m}:=\{(x,p)\in T\mathcal{M}|\mathcal{H}(x,p)=0,\ g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}Z^{\nu}<0\}.

(3)

For notational convenience, the phase trajectory is denoted as
X_{\tau}=(x_{\tau},p_{\tau})
,
which is the uplift of the particle’s worldline
x_{\tau}
into the
bundle
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
.

Figure 1:
The worldline of a particle is lifted to different regions of
the mass shell bundle by different observers.

If we consider another observer field with proper velocity
C^{\mu}=-Z^{\mu}
, referred
to as
Carol
, whose time orientation is opposite to that of Alice,
the definition of the future mass shell bundle
relative to Carol will be opposite to that relative to Alice (see Fig.
1
).
To avoid confusion,
we will designate Alice as the future-directed observer and Carol as the
past-directed observer, and throughout this paper,
the future and past mass shell bundles are always defined relative to Alice.
This designation is arbitrary because, in the presence of TRS,
the future- and the past-directed observer fields are indistinguishable
on the level of deterministic mechanics.

The future mass shell bundle is also a fiber bundle based on
\mathcal{M}
.
Its fiber, denoted by
(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}
, is the momentum space of the
relativistic particle. We use calligraphy letters, such as
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{R}

and
\mathcal{K}
, to denote tensors on
(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}
, and the cursive letters,
such as
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{Z}
and
\mathscr{L}
, to denote
tensors on
\Gamma_{m}^{+}
.

The future mass shell bundle is still not the space of microstates,
because the base manifold
\mathcal{M}
is not the configuration space.
There are different ways to drop the temporal dimension of
\mathcal{M}
, e.g.
either by fixing the zeroth component
x^{0}
of coordinates or by
fixing the proper time
t
of Alice. The first approach lacks apparent general
covariance. The core idea of our framework for relativistic stochastic
mechanics is to keep the manifest general
covariance and emphasize the role of observer choice. The details can be found
in Ref.
[
19
]
. Here we list some of the key
points:

1.

The proper time
t
of Alice can be extended into a scalar field
t(x)

on
\mathcal{M}
;

2.

The configuration space relative to Alice is defined as a
constant proper time slice of
\mathcal{M}
,
i.e.
a spacelike hypersurface

\mathcal{S}_{t}:=\{x\in\mathcal{M}|t(x)=t\}
in
\mathcal{M}
. Moreover,
the proper velocity field
Z^{\mu}
of Alice is the unit normal vector
field of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
;

3.

The space of microstates is defined as a hypersurface in
\Gamma^{+}_{m}

as a constant time slice,
i.e.
\Sigma_{t}^{+}:=\{(x,p)\in\Gamma^{+}_{m}|t(x)=t\}
;

4.

Since the gradient of
t
must be a normal co-vector of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
,

Z_{\mu}
must be collinear with
\nabla_{\mu}t
.
Denoting
|\nabla_{\mu}t|
as
\lambda
, we have
\partial_{\mu}t=-\lambda Z_{\mu}
.

Since
Z
is the unit future-directed normal vector field of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
,
the volume element on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
can be written as the interior derivative
of the volume element on
\mathcal{M}
along
Z^{\mu}
:

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}:=\iota_{Z}\eta_{\mathcal{M}},

(4)

where
\iota
represents interior derivative, and
\eta_{\mathcal{M}}=g^{1/2}{\rm d}x^{0}\wedge{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}
is the volume element on
\mathcal{M}
,
with
g=|{\rm det}(g_{\mu\nu})|
.
Since
\iota_{Z}({\rm d}x^{\mu})=Z^{\mu}
and
{\rm d}x^{0}=-\partial_{i}t{\rm d}x^{i}/\partial_{0}t

on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}

\displaystyle=g^{1/2}\iota_{Z}({\rm d}x^{0}\wedge{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d})

\displaystyle=g^{1/2}\sum_{\rho=0}^{d}(-1)^{\rho}\iota_{Z}({\rm d}x^{\rho}){\rm d}x^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{\rho-1}\wedge{\rm d}x^{\rho+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}

\displaystyle=g^{1/2}Z^{0}{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}-g^{1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Z^{i}{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}

\displaystyle=g^{1/2}\left[Z^{0}+\frac{1}{\partial_{0}t}\partial_{i}tZ^{i}\right]{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda g^{1/2}}{\partial_{0}t}{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}.

(5)

We shall also need to make use of the volume elements on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
and
\Sigma_{t}^{+}
.
Since both of them are submanifolds of
T\mathcal{M}
, it is appropriate to begin
from the geometry of
T\mathcal{M}

[
23
]
. The non-degenerate
metric on
T\mathcal{M}
is known as the Sasaki metric

[
24
]

\hat{g}_{ab}
, which is determined by the
metric
g_{\mu\nu}
of the base manifold
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{ab}:=g_{\mu\nu}{\rm d}x^{\mu}{}_{a}{\rm d}x^{\nu}{}_{b}+g_{\mu\nu}\theta^{\mu}{}_{a}\theta^{\nu}{}_{b},\quad\hat{g}^{ab}:=g^{\mu\nu}e_{\mu}{}^{a}e_{\nu}{}^{b}+g^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\nu}}\right)^{b},

(6)

where

\theta^{\mu}:={\rm d}p^{\mu}+\varGamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}p^{\alpha}{\rm d}x^{\beta},\quad e_{\mu}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}-\varGamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\beta}p^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\alpha}},

and
\varGamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}
is
the Christoffel connection associated with
g_{\mu\nu}
.
The corresponding volume element reads

\displaystyle\eta_{T\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=g~{\rm d}x^{0}\wedge{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}\wedge\theta^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta^{d}

\displaystyle=g~{\rm d}x^{0}\wedge{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}\wedge{\rm d}p^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}p^{d}.

(7)

As a hypersurface in
T\mathcal{M}
,
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
has
the unit normal (co)vector

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{a}=\frac{1}{|{\rm d}\mathcal{H}|}{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{a}=\frac{p_{\mu}}{m}\theta^{\mu}{}_{a},\qquad\hat{N}^{a}=\hat{g}^{ab}\hat{N}_{b}=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}\right)^{a},

(8)

giving rise to the induced metric

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{ab}

\displaystyle:=\hat{g}_{ab}+\hat{N}_{a}\hat{N}_{b}=g_{\mu\nu}{\rm d}x^{\mu}{}_{a}{\rm d}x^{\nu}{}_{b}+\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)\theta^{\mu}{}_{a}\theta^{\nu}{}_{b},

(9)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}p_{\mu}p_{\nu},

(10)

where the second term is the induced metric on
(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}
:

\displaystyle h_{ab}:=\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)\theta^{\mu}{}_{a}\theta^{\nu}{}_{b}.

(11)

Consequently, we get the volume element

\displaystyle\eta_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}

\displaystyle:=\iota_{\hat{N}}\eta_{T\mathcal{M}}=-\frac{m}{p_{0}}g~{\rm d}x^{0}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{d}\wedge{\rm d}p^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}p^{d}.

(12)

It is easy to see that the volume element
\eta_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}
can be factorized
into the wedge product of
\eta_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}
,

\displaystyle\eta_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}=\eta_{\mathcal{M}}\wedge\eta_{(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}},

(13)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}:=-\frac{m}{p_{0}}g^{1/2}{\rm d}p^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}p^{d}

(14)

is the volume element on the fiber space
(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}
.
Finally, since
\Sigma^{+}_{t}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}
, the volume element
on
\Sigma^{+}_{t}
also has a factorized form,

\displaystyle\eta_{\Sigma^{+}_{t}}=\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}\wedge\eta_{(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}}=\frac{m\lambda g}{p_{0}\partial_{0}t}{\rm d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots{\rm d}x^{d}\wedge{\rm d}p^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}p^{d}.

(15)

3
General relativistic Langevin systems

This section is intended for a brief review of the framework for general
relativistic stochastic mechanics and thermodynamics
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
in order to fix the notations and
make the forthcoming presentation for the
proof of fluctuation theorem self-contained.

3.1
Covariant Langevin equations

Let us consider a relativistic Brownian particle carrying an electric charge
q
and moving
in a heat reservoir residing in the curved spacetime
\mathcal{M}
and subjected to
an external electromagnetic field
F=F_{\mu\nu}{\rm d}x^{\mu}\wedge{\rm d}x^{\nu}
.
We assume that the heat reservoir has already reached thermal equilibrium,
hence there is no difference between the Eckart frame and the Landau frame in
defining the proper velocity
U^{\mu}
of the reservoir.
A version of the corresponding general covariant Langevin equation
(referred to as LE
τ
) that takes the proper time
\tau
of the particle
as an evolution parameter reads

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{x}_{\tau}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mu}}{m}{\rm d}\tau,

(16)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\mu}_{\tau}{\rm d}\tau+\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{dp}}{\rm d}\tau+\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}}{\rm d}\tau-\frac{1}{m}\varGamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\alpha}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\beta}{\rm d}\tau,

(17)

where
\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{dp}}:=\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}U_{\nu}

is the damping force with
\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}
being the damping coefficient
which transforms as a tensor under general coordinate transformations,

\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}}:=\frac{q}{m}F^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\tilde{p}^{\nu}_{\tau}

is the electromagnetic force, and

\displaystyle\xi_{\tau}^{\mu}:=\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\circ_{S}{\rm d}\tilde{w}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}/{\rm d}\tau+\mathcal{F}_{\text{add}}^{\mu}

(18)

is the stochastic force, which consists of a random force term

\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\circ_{S}{\rm d}\tilde{w}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}/{\rm d}\tau
encoding the
Stratonovich type coupling between the stochastic amplitudes
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}

with a set of independent Gaussian noises
{\rm d}\tilde{w}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}

obeying the probability distribution

\displaystyle\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{w}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tau}={\rm d}w^{\mathfrak{a}}]=\frac{1}{(2\pi{\rm d}\tau)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}{\rm d}w^{\mathfrak{a}}{\rm d}w^{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\rm d}\tau}\right),

(19)

and an additional stochastic force term

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{add}}^{\mu}=\frac{\delta^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}}{2}\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\nabla^{(h)}_{i}\mathcal{R}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{b}}

(20)

in which
\nabla^{(h)}_{i}
denotes the spatial components of the covariant derivative
associated with the metric (
11
) on the momentum space.
Both the random force and the additional stochastic force terms arise
from the interaction of the Brownian particle with the heat reservoir.
The additional stochastic force is required in order for the Brownian
particle to be able to reach equilibrium
in the long time limit
[
25
]
.
Each component of
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is assumed to be smoothly dependent
on the coordinates on
\Sigma_{t}^{+}
, and
for each fixed
\mathfrak{a}=1,2,\cdots d
,
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
transforms as a
vector under general coordinate transformations.
As was done in Refs.
[
19
,
20
]
,
we use tilded and un-tilded symbols to denote the random variables
and their realizations.

Although LE
τ
is perfectly generally covariant and encodes all necessary
factors that affect the motion of the Brownian particle, there are still some
drawbacks that call for an alternative version of the covariant Langevin equation.
The problem is connected to the choice of evolution parameter
\tau
.
Since

\displaystyle{\rm d}t=\partial_{\mu}t{\rm d}\tilde{x}^{\mu}=-\lambda Z_{\mu}{\rm d}\tilde{x}^{\mu}=-\lambda Z_{\mu}\frac{{\rm d}\tilde{x}^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\tau}{\rm d}\tau=-\lambda\frac{Z_{\mu}\tilde{p}^{\mu}}{m}{\rm d}\tau=\gamma(\tilde{x},\tilde{p}){\rm d}\tau,

(21)

we have
{\rm d}\tau=\gamma^{-1}(\tilde{x},\tilde{p}){\rm d}t
. Therefore,
from the perspective of the observer Alice, the proper time

\tau
of the Brownian particle becomes a random variable. To avoid this inconvenience,
a reparametrization scheme is adopted in
[
19
]
, with

\tilde{X}_{\tau}=(\tilde{x}_{\tau},\tilde{p}_{\tau})~\mapsto~\tilde{Y}_{t}=(\tilde{y}_{t},\tilde{k}_{t}),

where

\tilde{y}_{t}:=\tilde{x}_{\tilde{\tau}_{t}},\quad\tilde{k}_{t}:=\tilde{p}_{\tilde{\tau}_{t}}.

This leads to the following alternative version of covariant Langevin equation
which is referred to as LE
t
for short,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{y}_{t}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{k}^{\mu}_{t}}{m}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t,

(22)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{k}_{t}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\hat{\xi}_{t}^{\mu}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t+\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{dp}}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t+\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{m}\varGamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{k}^{\alpha}_{t}\tilde{k}^{\beta}_{t}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t.

(23)

The new stochastic force
\hat{\xi}^{\mu}_{t}
reads

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}^{\mu}_{t}:=\gamma^{1/2}\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\circ_{S}{\rm d}\tilde{W}_{t}^{\mathfrak{a}}/{\rm d}t+\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{add}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\mu i}\gamma^{1/2}\nabla^{(h)}_{i}\gamma^{-1/2},

(24)

in which

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{t}=\gamma^{1/2}(\tilde{Y}_{t}){\rm d}\tilde{w}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{\tilde{\tau}_{t}}

are still Gaussian noises but with the variance changed from
{\rm d}\tau
to
{\rm d}t
,
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu}:=\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\delta^{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}}\mathcal{R}^{\nu}{}_{\mathfrak{b}}
is the diffusion tensor.

In this work, we assume
that the diffusion tensor has rank
d
, so that
\mathcal{D}^{ij}
is a full-rank symmetric
matrix. This also requires that
\mathcal{R}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is a
d\times d
full-rank matrix.
The above assumption is necessary and sufficient to ensure
that the Brownian particle couples to the heat reservoir in every spatial direction.
Please keep in mind that the choice for the stochastic amplitudes
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}

is non-unique. Different choices correspond to different Langevin systems.
The result of the present work does not require the explicit values for the
stochastic amplitudes and should be valid for any choices obeying the above assumption.

3.2
Reduced Fokker-Planck equation

Using the diffusion operator method
[
26
]
,
the reduced Fokker-Planck equation (RFPE) associated with LE
τ
or LE
t

is obtained in Ref.
[
20
]
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{m}\mathscr{L}_{F}(\varphi)=\nabla_{i}^{(h)}\mathcal{I}^{i}[\varphi],

(25)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{F}:=p^{\mu}e_{\mu}+qF^{\mu}{}_{\nu}p^{\nu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}

(26)

is the Liouville vector field for a charged particle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[\varphi]=\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{ij}\nabla^{(h)}_{j}\varphi-\mathcal{K}^{i\nu}U_{\nu}\varphi\right]\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}

(27)

is a vector field which is connected to the heat transfer rate from the heat
reservoir to the Brownian particle via
[
19
,
21
]

\displaystyle Q[\varphi]:=\int_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}Z_{\nu}\mathcal{I}^{\nu}[\varphi],

(28)

and the definition for the derivative operator

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}
is provided in
Appendix
A
.
The round and square brackets around
\varphi
have different meanings:

\mathscr{L}_{F}(\varphi)
represents the action of the vector field
\mathscr{L}_{F}
on
the scalar
\varphi
, while
\mathcal{I}^{i}[\varphi]
implies that the
vector field
\mathcal{I}^{i}
is dependent on
\varphi
. Such convention will be used
throughout this paper.

It is important to point out that
the one particle distribution function (1PDF)
\varphi
appearing in the RFPE
is
not
a probability distribution in
\Sigma^{+}_{m}
. To see this,
we recall that the probability current associated with the RFPE (
25
) is

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}[\varphi]=\frac{\varphi}{m}\mathscr{L}_{F}-\mathcal{I}[\varphi].

(29)

Therefore, the probability distribution function on
\Sigma^{+}_{t}
should be

\displaystyle f:=-{\mathscr{Z}_{a}}\mathscr{J}^{a}[\varphi]=-\frac{1}{m}p^{\mu}Z_{\mu}\varphi=\gamma\lambda^{-1}\varphi,

(30)

where
\mathscr{Z}=Z^{\mu}e_{\mu}
is the unit normal vector of
\Sigma_{t}^{+}
.

Since
\mathcal{I}[\varphi]
is proportional to
the heat transfer rate, the condition for the
Brownian particle to reach detailed thermal equilibrium with the reservoir is

\mathcal{I}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]=0
, which yields the equilibrium distribution

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{eq}}=\e^{-\alpha+\beta_{\mu}p^{\mu}},\quad\beta_{\mu}:=\beta U_{\mu},

(31)

provided that the covariant Einstein relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu}=2\beta^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}

(32)

holds and that
\alpha
and
\beta_{\mu}
obey the following equations

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\alpha+q\beta^{\nu}F_{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad\nabla_{(\mu}\beta_{\nu)}=0,

(33)

which are simple consequences of the Liouville equation

\mathscr{L}_{F}(\varphi_{\text{eq}})=0
. Eq. (
33
) implies that
\beta_{\mu}

is a Killing vector field, while the relation
\beta_{\mu}=\beta U_{\mu}
implies
that it is timelike. Therefore,
\beta_{\mu}
must be timelike Killing. This leads to the
conclusion that the existence of the equilibrium distribution (
31
)
requires the spacetime to be at least stationary.

As we have argued in Ref.
[
20
]
,
the equilibrium state is intrinsic to the system which
is independent of the choice of observer. However, the parameters that characterize
the equilibrium state is indeed observer-dependent. It has been shown

[
27
,
20
]
that the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are
related to the chemical potential and the temperature observed by Bob via

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}},\qquad\alpha=-\frac{\mu_{\rm B}}{T_{\rm B}}.

(34)

The equilibrium distribution (
31
) is recognized to be
precisely the Jütnner distribution which is also obeyed by
particles of the heat reservoir.

3.3
Thermodynamic relations

The definition of the energy of a charged relativistic particle is non-unique.
For instance, both the kinematic momentum
p^{\mu}
and the canonical momentum

P^{\mu}:=p^{\mu}+qA^{\mu}
can be used for defining the energy
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle E:=-Z_{\mu}p^{\mu},\qquad H:=-Z_{\mu}P^{\mu}.

(35)

As an analogy of the non-relativistic case,
E
can be viewed as kinematic energy
and
H
can be viewed as the sum of kinematic energy and electromagnetic potential
energy
-qZ_{\mu}A^{\mu}
. The non-uniqueness for the definition of energy also
appeared in the non-relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
[
12
]
.
However, such non-uniqueness does not affect the description
of heat in the first law of stochastic thermodynamics. We will show that
the same situation also occurs in the relativistic case.

Since the microstate of the Brownian particle is described by a set of random variables,
the energy of the Brownian particle also depends on the same set of random variables,

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{\tau}:=E(\tilde{x}_{\tau},\tilde{p}_{\tau}),\qquad\tilde{H}_{\tau}:=H(\tilde{x}_{\tau},\tilde{p}_{\tau}).

(36)

Since LE
τ
is a system of Stratonovich-type
stochastic differential equations, the chain rule is available

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{E}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial E}{\partial x^{\mu}}{\rm d}\tilde{x}^{\mu}_{\tau}+\frac{\partial E}{\partial p^{\mu}}{\rm d}\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=-Z_{\mu}\left[\xi_{\tau}^{\mu}+\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}}^{\mu}\right]{\rm d}\tau-\frac{\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mu}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\nu}}{m}\nabla_{\nu}Z_{\mu}{\rm d}\tau-Z_{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\text{em}}^{\mu}{\rm d}\tau.

(37)

Similarly,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{H}_{\tau}

\displaystyle={\rm d}\tilde{E}_{\tau}-\frac{q}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\nu}}(Z_{\mu}A^{\mu})\tilde{p}^{\nu}_{\tau}{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle={\rm d}\tilde{E}_{\tau}+Z_{\mu}\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}}{\rm d}\tau-\frac{q}{m}[A_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}Z^{\mu}+Z^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}A_{\nu}]\tilde{p}^{\nu}_{\tau}{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=-Z_{\mu}\left[\xi_{\tau}^{\mu}+\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}}^{\mu}\right]{\rm d}\tau-\frac{\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mu}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\nu}}{m}\nabla_{\nu}Z_{\mu}{\rm d}\tau-\frac{q}{m}\pounds_{Z}A_{\mu}\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{\tau}{\rm d}\tau,

(38)

where
\pounds_{Z}A_{\mu}
is the Lie derivative of
A^{\mu}
along the vector field
Z^{\mu}
.
If the electromagnetic field is controlled by an external protocol denoted by
\sigma
,
the last term of the above equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm d}_{\sigma}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}:=-\frac{q}{m}\pounds_{Z}A_{\mu}\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{\tau}{\rm d}\tau.

(39)

In the realm of stochastic thermodynamics, the energy exchange between
the Brownian particle and the heat reservoir is considered as heat,
while the other part of the change of energy of the Brownian particle
is considered as work. Therefore, the heat received by the Brownian particle
from the heat reservoir is identified to be

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}:=-Z_{\mu}\left[\xi_{\tau}^{\mu}+\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}}^{\mu}\right]{\rm d}\tau,

(40)

and gravitational
[
29
]
and electromagnetic works are identified respectively as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\tau}:=-\frac{\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\mu}\tilde{p}_{\tau}^{\nu}}{m}\nabla_{\nu}Z_{\mu}{\rm d}\tau,

(41)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau}:=-Z_{\mu}\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}}{\rm d}\tau.

(42)

Therefore, the first law of relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
can be realized either as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{E}_{\tau}={\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}+{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\tau}+{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{\tau},

(43)

or as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{H}_{\tau}={\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\tau}+{\rm d}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\tau}+{\rm d}_{\sigma}\tilde{\mathcal{U}}.

(44)

The energy currents associated with the above two definitions of energy
are presented as follows,

\displaystyle E^{\mu}[\varphi]

\displaystyle:=\int_{{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\varphi E=-Z_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi],

(45)

\displaystyle H^{\mu}[\varphi]

\displaystyle:=\int_{{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\varphi H=E^{\mu}[\varphi]-qZ_{\nu}A^{\nu}N^{\mu}[\varphi],

(46)

wherein the energy-momentum tensor
T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi]
reads

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi]:=\int_{{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}\frac{p^{\mu}p^{\nu}}{m}\varphi.

(47)

In relativistic kinetic theory, the entropy current
associated with classical non-degenerate particles
is defined as
[
30
]

\displaystyle S^{\mu}[\varphi]=-\int_{{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\varphi\left(\ln\varphi-1\right).

(48)

In the equilibrium distribution (
31
), we have the following
relation
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle S^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]

\displaystyle=\beta^{\mu}P-T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]\beta^{\nu}+\alpha n^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}],

(49)

where
\displaystyle P:=\frac{1}{d}\Delta_{\mu\nu}(U)T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
is the pressure
and
n^{\mu}[\varphi]
is the particle current. Following a standard procedure as did
in
[
27
]
, it can be shown that
T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
and

n^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
can both be expressed in terms of the proper velocity
U^{\mu}

of Bob:
n^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
is simply proportional to
U^{\mu}
, while

T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
takes the form of the energy-momentum tensor of
a perfect fluid.

Now we would like to reinterpret eq.(
49
) in terms of the
densities of various physical quantities as measured by Alice. This can be achieved by
contracting each term in eq.(
49
) with
Z_{\mu}
. Recalling the
fact that
\beta^{\mu}=\beta U^{\mu}
, the relationship between the proper velocities of
Alice and Bob, can be written as

\displaystyle U^{\mu}=\gamma_{\rm A}(Z^{\mu}+z^{\mu}),\qquad\gamma_{\rm A}=-U^{\mu}Z_{\mu},\qquad z^{\mu}Z_{\mu}=0,

(50)

where
\gamma_{\rm A}
represents the
local Lorentz factor arising from the relative motion between Alice and Bob.
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle e_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1}s_{\rm A}-\alpha(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1}n_{\rm A}-P+{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A},

(51)

or alternatively

\displaystyle h_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1}s_{\rm A}-[qZ_{\mu}A^{\mu}+\alpha(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1}]n_{\rm A}-P+{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A},

(52)

where

\displaystyle e_{\rm A}:=T^{\mu\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]Z_{\mu}Z_{\nu},\quad h_{\rm A}:=e_{\rm A}-qZ_{\mu}A^{\mu}n_{\rm A},\quad n_{\rm A}:=-Z_{\mu}n^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}],

(53)

\displaystyle s_{\rm A}:=-Z_{\mu}S^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}],\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A}:=-T^{\rho\nu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]z_{\mu}Z_{\nu}.

(54)

e_{\rm A}
is the density of the energy
E
,
h_{\rm A}
is
the density of the energy
H
,
n_{\rm A}
is the particle number density,

s_{\rm A}:=-Z_{\mu}S^{\mu}[\varphi_{\text{eq}}]
is the entropy density, and

{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A}
is the relative kinematic energy density arising
from the relative motion between
Alice and Bob. The subscript
\rm A
in the notation for all these
density quantities indicates that
they are all defined with respect to the observer Alice.
Equation (
51
) can be viewed as the localized version of the Euler relation
in which the coefficients of
s_{\rm A}
and
n_{\rm A}
should be interpreted as
the temperature and chemical potential as measured by Alice. Therefore we have

\displaystyle T_{\rm A}:=(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1},\qquad\mu_{\rm A}:=-\alpha(\gamma_{\rm A}\beta)^{-1}.

(55)

Similarly, from eq. (
52
), we can read off

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\rm A}:=-[qZ_{\mu}A^{\mu}+\alpha(\gamma_{\rm AB}\beta)^{-1}]=\mu_{\rm A}-qZ_{\mu}A^{\mu},

(56)

which is recognized to be the electrochemical potential. Inserting eq.(
34
) into
eq. (
55
), the transformation rule for the temperature and chemical potential
presented in Ref.
[
27
]
can be easily recovered,

T_{\rm A}:=(\gamma_{\rm A})^{-1}T_{\rm B},\qquad\mu_{\rm A}:=(\gamma_{\rm A})^{-1}\mu_{\rm B}.

We can also substitute eqs. (
55
) and (
56
)
into eqs. (
51
)-(
52
) to make the appearance of the Euler relation
simpler,

\displaystyle e_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=T_{\rm A}s_{\rm A}+\mu_{\rm A}n_{\rm A}-P+{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A},

(57)

\displaystyle h_{\rm A}

\displaystyle=T_{\rm A}s_{\rm A}+\hat{\mu}_{\rm A}n_{\rm A}-P+{\mathcal{T}}_{\rm A}.

(58)

The different choices of definition for the energy induce different chemical potentials,
while such choices have no influence on the temperature. Putting these thermodynamic
quantities into eq. (
31
), the equilibrium distribution
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{eq}}={\rm e}^{(E-\mu_{\rm A}-z_{\nu}p^{\nu})/T_{\rm A}}={\rm e}^{(H-\hat{\mu}_{\rm A}-z_{\nu}p^{\nu})/T_{\rm A}}.

(59)

It remains to introduce the
trajectory entropy
for the Brownian particle,
which plays an important role in the formulation of the fluctuation theorem.
Denoting the phase trajectory of the Brownian particle as

\tilde{Y}_{t}=(\tilde{y}_{t},\tilde{k}_{t})
, the trajectory entropy
is defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}=-\ln\varphi(\tilde{Y}_{t}),

(60)

The entropy production of the Brownian particle in the time interval

[t_{I},t_{F}]
is related to the trajectory entropy via

\displaystyle\Delta S=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{t_{F}}}\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{t_{F}}}Z_{\mu}S^{\mu}[\varphi]-\int_{\mathcal{S}_{t_{I}}}\eta_{\mathcal{S}_{t_{I}}}Z_{\mu}S^{\mu}[\varphi]=\langle\tilde{S}_{t_{F}}\rangle-\langle\tilde{S}_{t_{I}}\rangle.

(61)

This part of the entropy production is also referred to as the trajectory
entropy production.

4
Time-reversal symmetry and its breaking

Before delving into the construction of fluctuation theorems in the framework of
general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics, it is crucial to accurately describe
what the TRS is meant on curved spacetime.

In ordinary textbooks on special relativistic field theories,
the time-reversal transformation (TRT) is often represented by a coordinate
transformation
(x^{0},x^{i})\mapsto(-x^{0},x^{i})
with different transformation rules
for temporal and spatial components of the coordinate bases

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}\mapsto-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}.

(62)

Since
x^{0}
serves as the evolution parameter, the spatial components
p^{i}
of
the momentum should reverse their signs, while the temporal component of the
momentum remains unchanged, which corresponds to the particle’s energy.
Consequently, the transformation under TRT for the contraction of momentum
components and the basis obeys

\displaystyle p=p^{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}+p^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\mapsto-p^{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}-p^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}=-p.

(63)

To maintain general covariance in our construction, it is better to treat the TRT
as a change of the observer’s time orientation,
i.e.
from the perspective
of Alice to that of Carol, rather than treating it as a coordinate transformation.
Meanwhile, we adopt the scalar expression like
-Z_{\mu}p^{\mu}
for defining
the energy of a particle instead of using the coordinate dependent definition
p^{0}
.
Therefore, we employ an alternative representation of the TRT,
which reverses the sign of full momentum (
i.e.
p^{\mu}\mapsto-p^{\mu}
)
while keeping the spacetime coordinates intact.
Such a representation implies the reversal of the particle’s proper time derivative:

{\rm d}/{\rm d}\tau\mapsto-{\rm d}/{\rm d}\tau
. If Alice observes a particle evolving from

\tau_{I}
to
\tau_{F}
in its own proper time during the time interval
t_{I}\rightarrow t_{F}
,
then Carol will observe the proper time of the charged particle evolving from
\tau_{F}

to
\tau_{I}
.

Since the momentum is defined only in the local tangent space on curved spacetime,
the TRT can be realized as an automorphism of
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
(see Fig.
2
):

\displaystyle I_{x}:p\mapsto-p,\qquad\text{for }\forall p\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}.

(64)

Furthermore,
I_{x}
induces a homeomorphism
between the future and past mass shell bundles,

\displaystyle I:\Gamma^{+}_{m}\to\Gamma^{-}_{m},

(65)

\displaystyle I:(x,p)\mapsto(x,I_{x}(p))=(x,-p).

(66)

Therefore, the phase trajectories lifted by Alice and Carol are on two disconnected
regions of the mass shell bundle. For convenience, the trajectory lifted by Alice
will be referred to as the
forward trajectory
, and the one lifted by Carol
will be referred to as the
reversed trajectory
.
For later reference, we also list the induced action (
i.e.
pushforward)
of the map
I
on the coordinate basis for the vector field on
T\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle I^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}},\qquad I^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}.

(67)

Figure 2:
The evolution directions of the particle under the perspectives of Alice and Carol.

If we discretize the time interval
[t_{I},t_{F}]
into a sequence of
N+1

equal-distance nodes
t_{I}=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=t_{F}
, then the forward trajectory
can be written as a sequence
Y_{[t]}:=(Y_{0},Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{N})
with
Y_{n}:=Y_{t_{n}}
.
Consequently, the reversed trajectory can also be written as a sequence

Y_{[t]}^{-}:=(Y_{0}^{-},Y_{1}^{-},\cdots,Y_{N}^{-})
in which

\displaystyle Y^{-}_{n}=I(Y_{N-n}).

(68)

The probability distribution
f=-p^{\mu}Z_{\mu}\varphi/m
describes the distribution of
the intersections of the stochastic trajectories with the space of microstates
\Sigma^{+}_{t}
,
and it can be easily extended to the past mass shell bundle by use of the relationship
between the forward and the reversed trajectories. Additionally,
as a metric-preserving transformation, the TRT
I
preserves the volume
elements on every submanifolds of the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{M}
mentioned
in Sec.
2
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle I^{*}\varphi=\varphi,\qquad I^{*}\eta_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}=\eta_{\Gamma^{-}_{m}},\qquad I^{*}\eta_{(\Gamma^{+}_{m})_{x}}=\eta_{(\Gamma^{-}_{m})_{x}},\qquad I^{*}\eta_{\Sigma^{+}_{t}}=\eta_{\Sigma_{t}^{-}},

(69)

where
\Sigma_{t}^{-}
is the space of microstates associated with the past directed
observer, Carol.

It remains to describe the TRT for the electromagnetic field. Consider the motion of a
massive charged particle subjected to the electromagnetic field
F^{\mu\nu}
.
The equation of motion reads

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}p^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\tau}=\frac{q}{m}F^{\mu}{}_{\nu}p^{\nu}.

(70)

The TRS implies that the above equation of motion remains invariant.
Since
p^{\mu}
is odd and hence
\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}p^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\tau}
is even under the TRT,
the invariance of the equation of motion requires either

\displaystyle q\mapsto q,\qquad F^{\mu\nu}\mapsto-F^{\mu\nu}

(71)

or

\displaystyle q\mapsto-q,\qquad F^{\mu\nu}\mapsto F^{\mu\nu}.

(72)

We will adopt the second convention which is known as Feynman’s convention in the
literature
[
31
]
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle I^{*}q=-q,\qquad I^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\text{em}}^{\mu}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{em}}^{\mu}.

(73)

Using the definition (
26
), it is not difficult to check that the
induced action of the TRT on the Liouville vector field is given by

\displaystyle I^{*}\mathscr{L}_{F}=-\mathscr{L}_{F}.

(74)

Bringing all the above conventions together, we will see that the equations for
the phase trajectory of a massive charged particle moving in a generic curved spacetime
and subjected to an external electromagnetic field remains invariant under the TRT,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}x^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m},

(75)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}p^{\mu}}{{\rm d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{m}F^{\mu}{}_{\nu}p^{\nu}-\frac{1}{m}\varGamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}.

(76)

Let us now bring the above system into a broader picture. Consider
a scenario in which there are a great number of massive charged particles of different
masses and charges moving together. The electromagnetic field is produced by the
particles themselves and the spacetime geometry is
determined by the masses and charges of the particles.
We assume that the system consists of different species of particles,
and each species carries different mass
m_{s}
and charge
q_{s}

and obeys a different distribution
\Phi_{s}
, which are differentiated from each other by
the suffix
s
. We also assume that the distributions
\Phi_{s}

are invariant under the TRT,
i.e.
I^{*}\Phi_{s}=\Phi_{s}
. Then the total electric current

\displaystyle J^{\mu}=\sum_{s}\int\eta_{(\Gamma_{m_{s}}^{+})_{x}}\frac{q_{s}p^{\mu}_{s}}{m_{s}}\Phi_{s}

(77)

as well as the energy-momentum tensor
contributed by the particles

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{pa}}=\sum_{s}\int\eta_{(\Gamma_{m_{s}}^{+})_{x}}\frac{p^{\mu}_{s}p^{\nu}_{s}}{m_{s}}\Phi_{s}

(78)

should both be TRT invariants. Consequently, the Maxwell equation

\displaystyle\nabla_{\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=J^{\mu}

(79)

that determines the electromagnetic field as well as the Einstein equation

\displaystyle R^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}R=8\pi G\left(T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{pa}}+T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{em}}\right)

(80)

that determines the spacetime geometry should all be invariant under the TRT,
wherein

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{em}}=F^{\mu\rho}F^{\nu}{}_{\rho}-\frac{1}{4}g^{\mu\nu}F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}

(81)

is the energy-momentum tensor of the electromagnetic field, whose TRT invariance
is self-evident.

Stochastic mechanics can be regarded as an effective theory for describing a
complete mechanical system within specific spatial and temporal
scales
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
Let us consider a heavy particle in the above system, assuming that the
remaining particles constitute a heat reservoir that has already reached
equilibrium. The electromagnetic interaction acting on the heavy particle
can be divided into two parts: the coarse-grained averaging effects at larger
spatial and temporal scales, and the stochastic remnants at smaller scales.
Consequently, eqs. (
75
)-(
76
) can be approximated by LE
τ

[
19
]
, and this heavy particle is the Brownian particle.

However, such a coarse-grained description violates the TRS. Let us first
consider the damping force. If the forward and reversed trajectories
simultaneously satisfy the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}p^{\mu}=\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}U_{\nu},

(82)

the damping tensor must reverse its sign under the TRT, since the velocity of
the heat reservoir should reverse its sign. The sign change in
\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}
implies
that the damping force reverses its role from a decelerating force to an accelerating
force, which makes a difference between future- and past-directed observers.
On the other hand, the Einstein relation
[
20
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu}=2T_{\rm B}\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}

(83)

requires that the damping tensor should be kept invariant under the TRT,
because the diffusion tensor
\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\mathcal{R}^{\nu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is a quadratic form
in
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
whose eigenvalues must be non-negative.
Therefore, the assumption that eq.(
82
) is invariant under the TRT
has to be wrong. The correct behaviors of the damping and diffusion tensors
and the stochastic amplitudes are provided as follows,

\displaystyle I^{*}\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{K}^{\mu\nu},\qquad I^{*}\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{D}^{\mu\nu},\qquad I^{*}\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\pm\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}.

(84)

The sign of the stochastic amplitude
\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is of little
significance, since the probability distribution of the Gaussian noise is an even
function. For convenience, we adopt
I^{*}\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mathcal{R}^{\mu}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
.
For the sake of mathematical consistency, we define a vector field
V^{a}
on

T\mathcal{M}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle V^{a}|_{(x,p)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle U^{\mu}|_{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}\bigg|_{(x,p)}&(x,p)\in\Gamma^{+}_{m}\\
\displaystyle-U^{\mu}|_{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}\bigg|_{(x,p)}&(x,p)\in\Gamma^{-}_{m}\end{cases}.

(85)

Then the damping force and the right-hand side of eq. (
27
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}}^{a}=\mathcal{K}^{ab}V_{b},\qquad\mathcal{I}^{a}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{ab}\nabla^{(h)}_{b}\varphi-\mathcal{K}^{ab}V_{b}\varphi,

(86)

and it is easy to check that their transformation rules under the TRT are

\displaystyle I^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{dp}},\qquad I^{*}\mathcal{I}[\varphi]=\mathcal{I}[\varphi].

(87)

Therefore, the probability current (
29
) of the
Brownian particle can be divided into the even and odd parts under the TRT,
i.e.

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}_{\text{r}}[\varphi]=\dfrac{\varphi}{m}\mathscr{L}_{F},\qquad\mathscr{J}_{\text{d}}[\varphi]=-\mathcal{I}[\varphi],

(88)

with

\displaystyle I^{*}\mathscr{J}_{\text{r}}[\varphi]=-\mathscr{J}_{\text{r}}[\varphi],\qquad I^{*}\mathscr{J}_{\text{d}}[\varphi]=\mathscr{J}_{\text{d}}[\varphi].

(89)

It is evident that
\mathscr{J}_{\text{d}}[\varphi]
violates the TRS.
The entropy production is always closely related to the breaking of the TRS,
and we have proved
[
21
]
that, on the ensemble level,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}S^{\mu}[\varphi]=-\int\eta_{(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}}\varphi^{-1}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial p^{\mu}}\mathscr{J}^{\mu}_{\mathrm{d}}[\varphi],

(90)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}S_{R}^{\mu}=\int\eta_{(\Gamma_{m}^{+})_{x}}\beta_{\mu}\mathscr{J}^{\mu}_{\text{d}}[\varphi],

(91)

where
S^{\mu}[\varphi]
and
S_{R}^{\mu}
respectively denote the entropy currents
of the Brownian particle and of the heat reservoir.
Eq. (
91
) is actually the relativistic version of the celebrated Clausius’ identity

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}S^{\mu}_{R}=-\frac{Q_{\rm B}[\varphi]}{T_{\rm B}}=\frac{Q_{R}}{T_{\rm B}},

(92)

where
Q_{\rm B}[\varphi]
is the heat transfer rate
from Bob’s perspective, and
Q_{R}
is that of the heat reservoir.
It is worth noticing that Clausius’ identity holds only from the perspective of Bob.

5
Fluctuation theorem

Unlike the case of ordinary differential equations, there is no deterministic
solution for stochastic differential equation. The best one can do is to
determine the probability for a certain trajectory to be realized. Consequently,
the breaking of the TRS of the Langevin equation could be described
in terms of the non-equal probabilities for the
forward and reversed trajectories,

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=Y_{[t]}]\neq\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}^{-}=Y_{[t]}^{-}],

(93)

where
\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=(\tilde{Y}_{0},\tilde{Y}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{Y}_{N})
and

\tilde{Y}_{[t]}^{-}=(\tilde{Y}^{-}_{0},\tilde{Y}_{1}^{-},\cdots,\tilde{Y}_{N}^{-})
denote
the forward and reversed processes,
and
Y_{[t]}
and
Y^{-}_{[t]}
denote the forward and reversed trajectories
which are already described in Sec.
4
. Notice that
“forward process” and “forward trajectory” are different concepts:
the latter is a concrete realization of the former.
Unlike the relation between forward and reversed
trajectories, the only requirement in the reversed process is that its initial state
is identical to the TRT of the final state of the forward process,
i.e.

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}^{-}_{0}=I(\tilde{Y}_{N}).

(94)

Using the above conventions, we are now in the right
position to present the precise form of the detailed and integral fluctuation theorems
in the context of general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
and formulate their proofs.

Theorem 5.1
(Detailed fluctuation theorem)

The ratio between the probabilities for the forward and reversed
general relativistic stochastic trajectories
Y_{[t]}
and
Y^{-}_{[t]}

to be realized is equal to the exponential of total entropy production
\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}

along the trajectory
Y_{[t]}
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=Y_{[t]}]}{\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{[t]}=Y^{-}_{[t]}]}={\rm e}^{\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}}.

(95)

Theorem 5.2
(Integral fluctuation theorem)

The statistical expectation value of the total trajectory entropy production
must be non-negative,
i.e.

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\left\langle\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}\right\rangle}\leq 1,\qquad\left\langle\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}\right\rangle\geq 0.

(96)

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the above theorems.

The Lorentz factors relative to Alice and Carol can be related via the TRT

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm A}=-\lambda Z_{\mu}p^{\mu}/m=\lambda C_{\mu}p^{\mu}/m=I^{*}\gamma_{C}.

(97)

These Lorentz factors can be merged into a single scalar field on the complete
mass shell bundle,

\displaystyle\gamma:=\begin{cases}\gamma_{\rm A}|_{Y}&Y\in\Gamma^{+}_{m}\\
\gamma_{C}|_{Y}&Y\in\Gamma^{-}_{m}\end{cases},

(98)

which is even under the TRT,
I^{*}\gamma=\gamma
.
Accounting for the transformation rules under the TRT,
we can rearrange the spatial components of the LE
t
presented in
eqs. (
22
)-(
23
) into the form

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{y}_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{k}^{i}_{t}}{m}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t,

(99)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{k}_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\circ_{S}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}+F^{i}{\rm d}t+\bar{F}^{i}{\rm d}t,

(100)

where
\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\gamma^{-1/2}\mathcal{R}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}
and

\displaystyle F^{i}:=\gamma^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{em}}-\frac{1}{m}\varGamma^{i}{}_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{k}^{\alpha}\tilde{k}^{\beta}\right),\quad\bar{F}^{i}

\displaystyle:=\gamma^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\text{add}}^{i}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{ij}\gamma^{1/2}\nabla^{(h)}_{j}\gamma^{-1/2}+\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{dp}}\right),

(101)

which have opposite behaviors under the TRT,

\displaystyle F^{i}|_{Y}=F^{i}|_{I(Y)},\qquad\bar{F}^{i}|_{Y}=-\bar{F}^{i}|_{I(Y)}.

(102)

In order to prove the fluctuation theorems at the trajectory level,
we need a discretized version of LE
t
, which reads

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{y}_{n}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{k}^{i}_{n}}{m}\gamma^{-1}|_{\tilde{Y}_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t,

(103)

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{k}_{n}^{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}|_{\tilde{Y}_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}+F^{i}|_{\tilde{Y}_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t+\bar{F}^{i}|_{\tilde{Y}_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t,

(104)

where

which comes from the Stratonovich
coupling. We also need to introduce the measures on the space of

which are defined to be the successive wedge products of the volume element of the

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[Y_{[t]}]:=\eta_{\Sigma_{0}}\wedge\eta_{\Sigma_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\eta_{\Sigma_{N}},\qquad\mathcal{D}[Y^{-}_{[t]}]:=\eta_{\Sigma^{-}_{0}}\wedge\eta_{\Sigma^{-}_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\eta_{\Sigma^{-}_{N}}.

\displaystyle I^{*}\mathcal{D}[Y_{[t]}]=\mathcal{D}[Y^{-}_{[t]}].

(106)

In other words,
Y_{[t]}\mapsto Y^{-}_{[t]}
is a volume preserving map.

Since both the forward and reversed processes are Markovian, the trajectory probabilities
can be written as products of transition probabilities with the initial probability,

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=Y_{[t]}]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}=Y_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]\bigg)\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{0}=Y_{0}],

(107)

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{[\bar{t}\,]}=Y^{-}_{[t]}]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{n+1}=Y^{-}_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{n}=Y^{-}_{n}]\bigg)\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{0}=Y^{-}_{0}]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{n+1}=I(Y_{N-n-1})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{n}=I(Y_{N-n})]\bigg)\Pr[I(\tilde{Y}_{N})=I(Y_{N})]

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n}=I(Y_{n})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n-1}=I(Y_{n+1})]\bigg)\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{N}=Y_{N}].

(108)

volume preserving property of
I
is used in the third line.
Appendix

proves the following continuum limit

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}=Y_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]}{\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n}=I(Y_{n})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n-1}=I(Y_{n+1})]}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{Y_{0}}}{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{Y_{N}}}\exp\left[\int^{t_{F}}_{t_{I}}\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}({m}a^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}\right].

(109)

Under the perspective of Bob, the complete differential of the energy is

\displaystyle{\rm d}E_{\rm B}=-\frac{p^{\nu}}{m}\nabla_{\nu}(p^{\mu}U_{\mu}){\rm d}\tau=-ma^{\mu}U_{\mu}{\rm d}\tau+{\rm d}\mathcal{P},

(110)

or

\displaystyle{\rm d}H_{\rm B}=-\frac{p^{\nu}}{m}\nabla_{\nu}(P^{\mu}U_{\mu}){\rm d}\tau=-ma^{\mu}U_{\mu}{\rm d}\tau+{\rm d}\mathcal{P}+{{\rm d}_{\sigma}\mathcal{U}}-{\rm d}\mathcal{W}.

(111)

Comparing with eq. (

\displaystyle\int\gamma^{-1}{\rm d}t\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}=-\int\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{Q}}{T_{\rm B}}=\Delta S_{R},

(112)

where the second equality encodes the Clausius equality at the level of trajectories.
Let us stress that, although the Clausius equality holds only from the perspective of
Bob, the total increase of the entropy of the heat reservoir is actually
observer-independent. This fact enables us to make use of eq. (

addressing the fluctuation theorem from the perspective of an arbitrary observer.

In the continuum limit, the logarithm of the ratio of the probabilities of
the forward and reversed trajectories is the sum of the trajectory entropy production of

\displaystyle\ln\frac{\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=Y_{[t]}]}{\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{[t]}=Y^{-}_{[t]}]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln\frac{\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}=Y_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]}{\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n}=I(Y_{n})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n-1}=I(Y_{n+1})]}

\displaystyle\qquad+\ln\frac{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{X_{0}}\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{0}=Y_{0}]}{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{Y_{N}}\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{N}=Y_{N}]}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{Q}}{T_{\rm B}}+\ln\frac{\varphi(Y_{0})}{\varphi(Y_{N})}

\displaystyle=\Delta S_{R}+\Delta S{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}~:=\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}}.

(113)

It is important to remember that the relation eq. (

different distribution functions and the definition (

the trajectory entropy production are used here.
Exponentiating the above relation yields Theorem
5.1
.
Moreover, Theorem
5.2
follows immediately by use of Theorem
5.1

and taking an integration in the space of trajectories, together with the aid of
Jensen inequality,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\left\langle\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}\right\rangle}\leq\left\langle{\rm e}^{-\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle:=\int\mathcal{D}[Y_{[t]}]\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{[t]}=Y_{[t]}]~{\rm e}^{-\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[Y^{-}_{[t]}]\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{[t]}=Y^{-}_{[t]}]=1,

(114)

which implies
\left\langle\Sigma_{Y_{[t]}}\right\rangle\geq 0
.

6
Concluding remarks

We have thus proved the detailed and integral fluctuation theorems in the context of
general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics.
In our construction, it is important to decouple the choice of observers from
the choice of coordinate systems, which has several important consequences.
First, it allows us to parametrize the stochastic trajectory using the
proper time of the observer field, which helps to get rid of the
random clock carried by the Brownian particle. Second, it helps to
properly describe the TRT in a coordinate independent manner. Lastly,
it is precisely such decoupling that makes the
construction fully general covariant.

Although the values of
many thermodynamic quantities are observer dependent, including
e.g.
the energy and its density, the temperature and chemical potential,
and even the Clausius’ identity
etc.
,
the total entropy production is not among the observer dependent quantities.
Meanwhile, the trajectory probability is a pure mathematical entity
that is also observer independent. Therefore, it is not surprising that
the form of the fluctuation theorems obtained in this work is identical to that
obtained from non-relativistic stochastic thermodynamics.

Fluctuation theorems are not only important in understanding the theoretical origin of
irreversibility, but they have also found various applications
in diverse areas ranging from macroscopic to mesoscopic systems, and even to
active matter. For example, there are some studies

[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]

utilizing the non-relativistic fluctuation theorem to explore the concept of
Maxwell’s demon. Some of them argue that the change of the mutual information
between Maxwell’s demon and the Brownian particle should be considered as a part of
the entropy production. When considering the event horizon within curved spacetime,
which serves as a causal boundary, the causal connection between Maxwell’s demon and
the Brownian particle may be lost over time. Consequently, behaviors different
from those predicted by non-relativistic fluctuation theorems, similar to
the black hole information paradox, may emerge.
Another possible scenario in which our general relativistic fluctuation theorems
may find application is in black hole physics. In recent years, due to the
observational progresses brought about by the event horizon telescope
[
39
]
,
a considerable amount of theoretical works on the images and shadows of black holes
have appeared
1
1

1

A quick search of the literature
[
43
]
has
found 3222 results. It is impossible to list them all, therefore we simply refer to

[
40
,
41
,
42
]
as prototypes of related works.
.
In most of those works, the light-emitting substance is assumed
to be particles undergoing regular geodesic motion, rendering the corresponding images
also regular and containing rich patterns. In reality, however,
since a large amount of particles accrete around the black holes, they effectively form
a heat reservoir with a very high temperature, and consequently they make the trajectories of
individual particles moving inside the accretion disk probabilistic. The corresponding
images should also be obscured as a consequence of such probabilistic motion.
The quantitative nature of the detailed fluctuation theorem may help to understand
to what extent the black hole images should be obscured. In summary,

the research carried out in the present work opens a new area
for the potential applications of fluctuation theorems which embodies
relativistic gravity, as in typical cases of cosmological processes, astrophysical
processes and black hole physics. We believe that much more sophisticated
applications of the new version of the fluctuation theorems presented in this work and
more generally of the framework of general relativistic stochastic thermodynamics
will emerge as we look more clearly and deeply into them. We are working hard on
related problems.

Appendix A
Coordinate bases for tangent vectors

In our description of the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{M}
, we employ
2d+2

independent coordinates
(x^{\mu},p^{\mu})
with the corresponding
coordinate basis
\displaystyle\left(\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}},\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}\right)

for tangent vectors on
T\mathcal{M}
.
However, due to the mass shell constraint, the above coordinates are no longer
independent of the mass shell bundle
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
,
rendering the coordinate basis also redundant. The same also happens when
we restrict ourselves from the mass shell bundle to the
space of microstates
\Sigma_{t}^{+}
. The aim of this appendix is to
resolve the redundancies in the coordinate bases on various useful submanifolds
of
T\mathcal{M}
.

To resolve the coordinate redundancy on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
, we take the coordinates
on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
to be
(x^{\mu},p^{i})
and view
p^{0}
as a function

\displaystyle p^{0}(x^{\mu},p^{i})=\frac{g_{0i}(x)p^{i}\pm\sqrt{\left[g_{0i}(x)p^{i}\right]^{2}-g_{00}(x)\left[m^{2}+g_{ij}(x)p^{i}p^{j}\right]}}{-g_{00}(x)}.

(115)

This leads to the partial derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p^{0}}{\partial x^{\mu}}=-\frac{1}{2p_{0}}\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^{\mu}}p^{\alpha}p^{\beta},\qquad\frac{\partial p^{0}}{\partial p^{i}}=-\frac{p_{i}}{p_{0}}.

(116)

It is essential to recognize that the coordinate basis for tangent vectors on

\Gamma^{+}_{m}
is different from the corresponding subset of coordinate basis
on
T\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}\neq\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}},\quad\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}\neq\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}.

(117)

To demonstrate this difference, let us consider a scalar field
f(x^{\mu},p^{\mu})
on

T\mathcal{M}
and its restriction on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
,
i.e.

\displaystyle\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}(x^{\mu},p^{i}):=f(x^{\mu},p^{0}(x^{\mu},p^{i}),p^{i}).

(118)

The action of the coordinate basis vectors on
\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}
reads

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f+\frac{\partial p^{0}}{\partial p^{i}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{0}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f,

(119)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f+\frac{\partial p^{0}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{0}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f,

(120)

where the “breved” partial derivatives are defined as

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}:=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}-\frac{p_{i}}{p_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{0}}\right]_{T\mathcal{M}},\qquad\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}:=\left[e_{\mu}+\varGamma^{i}{}_{\beta\mu}p^{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}\right]_{T\mathcal{M}}.

(121)

Therefore, the partial derivatives on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
and
T\mathcal{M}
are related via

\displaystyle i^{*}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}},\qquad i^{*}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{\mu}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}},

(122)

where
i:\Gamma^{+}_{m}\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
is the embedding map.
From the exterior geometric point of view, we can safely identify

\{\partial/\partial\breve{x}^{\mu},\partial/\partial\breve{p}^{i}\}

as the coordinate basis on the mass shell bundle, omitting the subscripts.

The tangent vector
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}^{\mu}(\partial/{\partial p^{\mu}})

of the momentum space must be orthogonal to the normal vector

\hat{N}=p^{\mu}(\partial/\partial p^{\mu})/m
. The orthogonality
condition
\mathcal{V}^{\mu}p_{\mu}=0
implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}=\mathcal{V}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{i}}-\frac{\mathcal{V}^{i}p_{i}}{p_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{0}}=\mathcal{V}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}.

(123)

Therefore, the tangent vectors of the momentum space have two different
representations under the above two bases. We will use them interchangeably.
There is a similar property for the vectors on the
mass shell bundle. Let
\mathscr{V}
be a tangent vector of the phase
trajectory
X_{\tau}:=(x_{\tau},p_{\tau})
.
As a vector on a tangent bundle,
\mathscr{V}
can be expanded as

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+m\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}}=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}e_{\mu}+ma^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p^{\mu}},

(124)

where

is the
coordinate acceleration
and

is the

covariant acceleration
. The mass shell condition implies that

\mathscr{V}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}e_{\mu}+ma^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}=\frac{p^{\mu}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{\mu}}+m\mathcal{A}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{p}^{i}}.

(125)

The two representations for vectors on the mass shell bundle under the
bases
\{\partial/\partial\breve{x}^{\mu},\partial/\partial\breve{p}^{i}\}

and
\{\partial/\partial x^{\mu},\partial/\partial p^{\mu}\}

The space of microstates
\Sigma^{+}_{t}
is also an embedding submanifold of

to be a constant.
Therefore, the coordinates on
\Gamma^{+}_{m}
become redundant once again on
\Sigma^{+}_{t}

(x^{i},p^{i})
as a coordinate

\Sigma^{+}_{t}

can be
evaluated to be

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{\Sigma_{t}^{+}}\left.f\right|_{\Sigma_{t}^{+}}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}+\frac{\partial x^{0}}{\partial x^{i}}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{0}}\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}\left.f\right|_{\Gamma^{+}_{m}}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{i}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f-\frac{\partial_{i}t}{\partial_{0}t}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{x}^{0}}\right|_{T\mathcal{M}}f,

(127)

Appendix B
Continuum limit

This appendix provides the details for proving eq. (
5

First, we introduce two mathematical lemmas.

1. Let
A

B
be an arbitrary matrix

(135)

(136)

where

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\det[A(t_{n})+B(t_{n}){\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})]}{\det[A(t_{n})+C(t_{n}){\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})]}=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{-1}(B-C)]{\rm d}t\right],

where

Let
\mathscr{O}
be a function on the mass shell bundle, and let

be a
phase trajectory parametrized by the proper time of Alice. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathscr{O}(Y_{t_{F}})}{\mathscr{O}(Y_{t_{I}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{F}})}{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{I}})}=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{n+1}})}{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{n}})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{n}})+\mathscr{V}(\mathscr{O}){\rm d}\tau+o({\rm d}\tau^{2})}{\mathscr{O}(X_{\tau_{n}})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{\tau_{I}}^{\tau_{F}}\mathscr{O}^{-1}\mathscr{V}(\mathscr{O}){\rm d}\tau\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\mathscr{O}^{-1}\mathscr{V}(\mathscr{O}){\rm d}t\right],

(138)

where
\mathscr{V}
is the tangent vector along
Y_{t}
,
as presented by eq. (
125
) and/or
eq. (
133
).
It is worth noting that in this appendix, we will use
(y,k)
to
represent the coordinates of the tangent bundle, rather than
(x,p)
.
This choice does not imply a coordinate transformation. It simply emphasizes
that the discretized time corresponds to Alice’s proper time
t
.

Eq. (
B
) can be used to evaluate the continuum limit of certain
ratios which are useful in proving the fluctuation theorem. For instance,

\displaystyle\frac{k_{0}|_{Y_{N}}}{k_{0}|_{Y_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}\left\{\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\mu}}k_{0}+m\mathcal{A}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial k^{\mu}}k_{0}\right\}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}\left\{\frac{1}{m}\partial_{\mu}g_{0\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}+m\mathcal{A}_{0}\right\}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}\left\{\frac{1}{2m}\partial_{0}g_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}+ma_{0}\right\}{\rm d}t\right],

(139)

\displaystyle\frac{g|_{Y_{0}}}{g|_{Y_{N}}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}g^{-1}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\mu}}g{\rm d}t\right],

(140)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{0}t|_{Y_{N}}}{\partial_{0}t|_{Y_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{\partial_{0}t}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{\mu}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{0}}t{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{1}{\partial_{0}t}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{0}}\left(\gamma^{-1}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\partial_{\mu}t\right)-\partial_{\mu}t\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{0}}\left(\gamma^{-1}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\right)\right\}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{1}{\partial_{0}t}\left\{\gamma^{-2}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}\partial_{\mu}t\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{0}}\gamma-\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{m}\partial_{0}t\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{y}^{0}}k^{0}\right\}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\gamma{\rm d}t\right]\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}\frac{1}{2m}\partial_{0}g_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu}k^{\nu}{\rm d}t\right].

(141)

Eq. (
103
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\tilde{y}^{i}_{n+1}=h^{i}(\tilde{Y}_{n+1},\tilde{Y}_{n})=\frac{\tilde{k}^{i}_{\bar{n}}}{m}\gamma^{-1}|_{\tilde{Y}_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t+\tilde{y}^{i}_{n},

(142)

which implies that the realization of
\tilde{y}^{i}_{n+1}
is determined by the
realization of
\tilde{Y}_{n}
and
\tilde{k}^{i}_{n+1}
. The probability
distribution of a random variable
\tilde{x}
obeying the constraint
\tilde{x}=f(\tilde{x})

is given by

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{x}=x]=(1-f^{\prime}(x))\delta(x-f(x)).

(143)

The generalization of this equation to general dimension is straightforward,
showing that the probability distribution of
\tilde{y}^{i}_{n+1}

under the conditions
\tilde{k}^{i}_{n+1}={k}^{i}_{n+1},\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{y}_{n+1}=y_{n+1}|\tilde{k}_{n+1}={k}_{n+1},\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]

\displaystyle\qquad=\det\left[\delta^{i}{}_{j}-\frac{\partial h^{i}}{\partial\hat{y}_{n+1}^{j}}\right]\left|\frac{\partial_{0}t}{\lambda\sqrt{g}}\right|_{y_{n+1}}\delta^{d}(y_{n+1}-h({Y_{n+1},Y_{n}}))

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[1+\gamma^{-2}\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{{y}}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}\left|\frac{\partial_{0}t}{\lambda\sqrt{g}}\right|_{y_{n+1}}\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n}),

(144)

where
y_{n}
denotes the sequence
(y_{n}^{1},\cdots,y_{n}^{d})
and we will also use the notation

k_{n}=(k^{1}_{n},\cdots,k_{n}^{d})
.
\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})
is defined as

\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n}):=\delta^{d}(y_{n+1}-h(Y_{n+1},Y_{n}))
,
which has the property

\displaystyle\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})=\Delta(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1})).

(145)

The appearance of
\partial/\partial\hat{y}^{i}
in the second line of
eq. (
B
) is due to the fact that
\tilde{y}^{i}_{n+1}
only takes
values in the configuration space, and its probability density is a scalar field
on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. The factor
|\partial_{0}t/(\lambda\sqrt{g})|
arises from the change
of coordinate volume element into an invariant volume element,
as indicated by eq. (
2
).

The single step transition probability can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}]=\Pr[\tilde{y}_{n+1}|\tilde{k}_{n+1},\tilde{Y}_{n}]\Pr[\tilde{k}_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}],

(146)

where the second factor remains to be evaluated.
Defining the function

\displaystyle{\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})

\displaystyle:=(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}\left[k^{i}_{n+1}-k^{i}_{n}-F^{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t-\bar{F}^{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}\left[\gamma^{-1}m\mathcal{A}^{i}_{n}-F^{i}-\bar{F}^{i}\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t,

(147)

where
\mathcal{A}^{i}_{n}:=\gamma|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}(k^{i}_{n+1}-k^{i}_{n})/(m{\rm d}t)
is
the coordinate acceleration, eq. (
104
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(\tilde{Y}_{n+1},\tilde{Y}_{n}).

(148)

In the case in which the realization of
\tilde{Y}_{n}
is given,

\tilde{y}_{n+1}
is determined by
\tilde{k}_{n+1}
. Therefore,
eq. (
148
) gives the relation between
{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}

and
\tilde{k}^{i}_{n+1}
, which can be used to calculate the conditional probability

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{k}_{n+1}=k_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]=\left|\frac{k_{0}}{m\sqrt{g}}\right|_{Y_{n+1}}\det\left[T^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})\right]\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})],

(149)

where

\displaystyle T^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n}):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}_{n+1}}{\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n}),

(150)

and
y_{n+1}
appearing on the right hand side is regarded as a function

y_{n+1}(k_{n+1},Y_{n})
determined by eq. (
142
).
Using the implicit relationship (
142
) between
y_{n+1}
and
k_{n+1}
,
we can get the following relation by use of a differentiation with respect to

k_{n+1}^{i}
,

\displaystyle\left[\delta^{i}{}_{\ell}-\frac{\partial\gamma^{-1}}{\partial\hat{{y}}_{n+1}^{\ell}}{\rm d}t\right]\frac{\partial y_{n+1}^{\ell}}{\partial\breve{k}_{n+1}^{j}}=\left[\frac{1}{2m}\delta^{i}{}_{j}\gamma^{-1}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}+\frac{k^{i}_{\bar{n}}}{2m}\frac{\partial\gamma^{-1}}{\partial\breve{{k}}^{j}_{n+1}}\right]{\rm d}t,

(151)

which indicates that
\partial y^{\ell}_{n+1}/\partial\breve{k}^{j}_{n+1}\sim o({\rm d}t)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle T^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}({Y_{n+1},Y_{n}})=\frac{\partial{\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}_{n+1}}+\frac{\partial y^{k}_{n+1}}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}_{n+1}}\frac{\partial{\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}}{\partial\hat{y}^{k}_{n+1}}=\frac{\partial{\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}_{n+1}}+o({\rm d}t^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad=(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{m\gamma^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{n}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}F^{j}]-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\bar{F}^{j}]\right\}_{Y_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t,

(152)

where terms of order
o({\rm d}t^{2})
and higher have been omitted.
Finally, the transition probability of the forward process can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}=Y_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[1+\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\gamma^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}\left|\frac{k_{0}\partial_{0}t}{m\lambda g}\right|_{Y_{n+1}}\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})\det\left[T^{a}{}_{i}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}{(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})}].

(153)

The single step transition probability in the reversed process can be
evaluated following a similar procedure, yielding

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n}=I(Y_{n})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n-1}=I(Y_{n+1})]

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[1+\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\gamma^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{{y}}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{I(Y_{\bar{n}})}\left|\frac{k_{0}\partial_{0}t}{m\lambda g}\right|_{I(Y_{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\Delta(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))\det\left[T^{a}{}_{i}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))\right]\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))]

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[1-\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\gamma^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}\left|\frac{k_{0}\partial_{0}t}{m\lambda g}\right|_{Y_{n}}\Delta(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})\det\left[T^{a}{}_{i}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))].

(154)

The Jacobian matrix
T^{a}{}_{i}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))
in the reversed process reads

\displaystyle T^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))

\displaystyle\qquad=(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}|_{I(Y_{\bar{n}})}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{m\gamma^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{n}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}F^{j}]-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\bar{F}^{j}]\right\}_{I(Y_{\bar{n}})}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{i}|_{Y_{\bar{n}}}+\frac{1}{2}\left\{-m\gamma^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{n}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}F^{j}]-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\bar{F}^{j}]\right\}_{Y_{\bar{n}}}{\rm d}t.

(155)

In the continuum limit, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\det[T^{a}{}_{i}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n+1})]}{\det[T^{a}{}_{i}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))]}

\displaystyle\qquad=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\left\{m\gamma^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}{j}\mathcal{A}^{j}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}[(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}\bar{F}^{j}]\right\}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{\mathfrak{a}}{}_{j}[ma^{j}-\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\text{em}}]{\rm d}t\right]\exp\left[-\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}F^{i}{\rm d}t\right],

(156)

where the exponent in the last term can be expanded as

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}F^{i}=

\displaystyle-\left(\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{em}}-\frac{1}{m}\varGamma^{i}{}_{\alpha\beta}k^{\alpha}k^{\beta}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}\gamma^{-1}+\gamma^{-1}\frac{k^{\mu}}{m}g^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{\mu}}g

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\partial_{0}g_{\alpha\beta}k^{\alpha}k^{\beta}+(\mathcal{F}_{\text{em}})_{0}\right].

(157)

Meanwhile, we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left[1+\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\gamma^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}}{\left[1-\frac{k^{i}}{2m}\gamma^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t+o({\rm d}t^{2})\right]_{Y_{\bar{n}}}}=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-2}\frac{k^{i}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma{\rm d}t\right].

(158)

Moreover, the continuum limit of the ratio of the probabilities of the
Gaussian increments reads

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(Y_{n+1},Y_{n})]}{\Pr[{\rm d}\tilde{W}^{\mathfrak{a}}_{n}={\rm d}W^{\mathfrak{a}}(I(Y_{n}),I(Y_{n+1}))]}=\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}2\gamma^{-2}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-1})_{ij}(m\mathcal{A}^{i}-\gamma F^{i})\gamma\bar{F}^{j}{\rm d}t\right],

(159)

where the integrads can also be expanded as

\displaystyle 2\gamma^{-2}(\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{-1})_{ij}(m\mathcal{A}^{i}-\gamma F^{i})\gamma\bar{F}^{j}

\displaystyle\qquad=2\gamma^{-1}(\mathcal{D}^{-1})_{ij}(ma^{i}-\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{em}})\left(\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\text{dp}}+\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\text{add}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{jk}\gamma^{1/2}\nabla_{k}^{(h)}\gamma^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}+\gamma^{-1}(\mathcal{R}^{-1})^{a}_{j}\nabla_{i}^{(h)}\mathcal{R}^{i}{}_{a}(ma^{j}-\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\text{em}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{-2}(ma^{i}-\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{em}})\nabla_{i}^{(h)}\gamma

\displaystyle\qquad=\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}+\gamma^{-1}(\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1})^{a}{}_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{i}{}_{a}(ma^{j}-\mathcal{F}^{j}_{\text{em}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\gamma^{-2}(ma^{i}-\mathcal{F}^{i}_{\text{em}})\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}\gamma-\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{k_{0}}(ma_{0}-(\mathcal{F}_{\text{em}})_{0}).

(160)

In the end, by combining all the continuum limit together, we obtain the ratio of the
conditional probabilities for the forward and reversed processes,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{n+1}=Y_{n+1}|\tilde{Y}_{n}=Y_{n}]}{\Pr[\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n}=I(Y_{n})|\tilde{Y}^{-}_{N-n-1}=I(Y_{n+1})]}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\lambda|_{Y_{0}}}{\lambda|_{Y_{N}}}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-p0}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-g}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-t0}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-T}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-gamma}\times\text{eq.~}\eqref{rate-dw}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\lambda|_{Y_{0}}}{\lambda|_{Y_{N}}}\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-2}\left(\frac{k^{i}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}^{i}}\gamma+\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\gamma+m\mathcal{A}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\breve{k}^{i}}\gamma\right){\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{\lambda|_{Y_{0}}}{\lambda|_{Y_{N}}}\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}{\rm d}t\right]\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-2}\mathscr{V}(\gamma){\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{Y_{0}}}{(\lambda\gamma^{-1})|_{Y_{N}}}\exp\left[\int_{t_{I}}^{t_{F}}\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{\rm B}}(ma^{\mu}-\mathcal{F}^{\mu}_{\text{em}})U_{\mu}{\rm d}t\right],

(161)

which is exactly eq. (
5
) in the main text.
