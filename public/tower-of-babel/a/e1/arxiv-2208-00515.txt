Random zeros entire functions
Yuri Kondratiev Dragomanov University, Kyiv, Ukraine

Abstract
. We propose the construction of entire functions with a given random collection of zeros. There are considered two particular cases. In the first one we are dealing with simple zeros. And the second corresponds to random zeros with random multiplicity. Keywords : entire functions, zeros, Weierstrass products, Poisson random fields, marked configurations AMS Subject Classification 2010 : 60J65, 47D07, 35R11, 60G52.



1.
Introduction

A classical problem in complex analysis consists in a construction
of an entire function with a prescribed set of zeros. For a finite set
of zeros the answer is given by a proper polynomial. More generally,
let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots
be a given sequence of complex numbers, non of which is zero,
with the point at infinity as the only limit point. We would like construct an entire function
whose set of zeros is precisely this sequence, see, e.g.,
[
3
]
for the detailed discussion.

Definition 1.1
.

Put
E_{0}(z)=1-z
, and for
p\in{\mathbb{N}}

E_{p}(z)=(1-z)\exp\{z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\dots+\frac{z^{p}}{p}\}.

These functions are called elementary factors.

We form an infinite product

(1)

\Pi(z)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{p_{n}}(\frac{z}{a_{n}}).

There we have chosen a sequence of natural numbers
p_{n}
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|z|^{p_{n}+1}}{|a_{n}|^{p_{n}+1}}<\infty.

Such sequence always exists
[
3
]
.

Of course, the sequence
a_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the set of zeros for

\Pi(z)
. Representation similar to (
1
) is possible for any entire
function that is stated by the Weierstrass theorem. If all
p_{n}=p\in{\mathbb{N}},n\in{\mathbb{N}}

then we are in the framework of the Hadamar theorem
[
3
]
.

The aim of this short note is to show that the results above may be extended to
the case of random zeros. For the more transparent presentation we consider
only the case of Poisson random fields on
{\mathbb{C}}
. But the obtained results may be
stated for several other random point fields as, e.g., Gibbs point processes.
Actually, as is may be seeing from the proofs below, only information we need
is the density of a random point process we use.

We will consider two particular cases. In the first one we are dealing with simple random zeros.
In the second are considered random zeros with random multiplicity.

2.
Poisson zeros

We will use Poisson random fields over the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
. To this end we introduce the space
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
of all locally
finite subsets
\gamma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
which are called configurations. For a detailed description of topological and metrical
properties of
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
see, e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
. Let
\sigma
be a Radon measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular
we will use the case of the Lebesgue measure
m(dz)=dxdy,\;\;z=x+iy
.

For
f\in C_{0}({\mathbb{C}})
(the set of continuous fuctions with compact supports)
introduce a lifted function on
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
by

\left\langle f,\gamma\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in\gamma}f(x),\;\;\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}}).

The Poisson measure
\pi_{\sigma}
on
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
is defined via the Laplace transform

\int_{\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})}e^{\left\langle f,\gamma\right\rangle}d\pi_{\sigma}(\gamma)=\exp(\int_{\mathbb{C}}\{e^{f(x)}-1)\}d\sigma(x)).

As a corollary of this definition we may show for any

f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{C}},\sigma)

(2)

\int\left\langle f,\gamma\right\rangle d\pi_{\sigma}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}f(z)d\sigma(z).

For any
R>0
denote
B_{R}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;|\;|z|\leq R\}
and
B_{R}^{c}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;|\;|z|>R\}.

Then
{\mathbb{C}}=B_{R}\cup B_{R}^{c}
and

\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})=\Gamma(B_{R})\times\Gamma(B_{R}^{c}),\;\gamma=\gamma_{R}\cup\gamma_{R}^{c}.

Note that
\Gamma(B_{R})
contains only finite configurations as
B_{R}
is compact.
For the decomposition
{\mathbb{C}}=B_{R}\cup B_{R}^{c}
we have the decomposition of the measure

\sigma=\sigma_{R}+\sigma_{R}^{c}

and corresponding Poisson measures product

\pi_{\sigma}=\pi_{\sigma_{R}}\otimes\pi_{\sigma_{R}^{c}}.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

For
|z|<1

(3)

|1-E_{p}(z)|\leq|z|^{p+1}.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}

(4)

\ln|E_{p}(z)|<A_{p}\frac{|z|^{p+1}}{1+|z|}.

Our aim is the following: for fixed
p
and a configuration
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
we consider a heuristic expression

\Pi(z,\gamma)=\prod_{x\in\gamma,x\neq 0}E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}).

Assume we take
z\in B_{R}
. We can decompose

\Pi(z,\gamma)=\prod_{x\in\gamma,x\neq 0}E_{p}(\frac{z}{x})=\prod_{x\in\gamma_{R},x\neq 0}E_{p}(\frac{z}{x})\times\prod_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}E_{p}(\frac{z}{x})

and the existence of this function depends only on the second (infinite product) factor.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
p\geq 2
the function
\Pi_{p}(z,\gamma)
is defined for
\pi_{m}
-a.a.
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})
as
an entire function of
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

We rewrite

\Pi_{p}^{R}(z,\gamma)=\prod_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}E_{p}(\frac{z}{x})=\prod_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}(1-(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))

and the convergence of the infinite product is equivalent to the convergence of the sum

\sum_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x})).

Now we will use relation (
2
) with
f(z)=(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))
. We have

\int_{\Gamma(B_{R}^{c})}\sum_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))d\pi_{R}^{c}(\gamma_{R}^{c})=

\int_{B_{R}^{c}}(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))dm_{R^{c}}(z)\leq\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\frac{|z|^{p+1}}{|x|^{p+1}}dm_{R^{c}}(x)=C|z|^{p+1}\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{p}}d\rho

where we used bound (
3
). For
p\geq 2
last integral is finite that means
the integrability of
\sum_{x\in\gamma_{R}^{c}}(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))
and, therefore, this sum is
finite
\pi_{m_{R}^{c}}
almost surely. Then

\sum_{x\in\gamma}(1-E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}))

converges
\pi_{m}
almost surely and uniformly w.r.t.
z\in B_{R}
.

Using Theorem 15.6 from
[
8
]
we conclude that
E_{p}(z,\gamma)
is well defined for a.a.
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})

and is holomorphic on
B_{R}
. But it is true for all
R>0
that means
E_{p}(z,\gamma)
is entire.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

An alternative proof of this theorem is based on the analysis of

\log\Pi_{p}(z,\gamma)=\sum_{x\in\gamma,x\neq 0}\log E_{p}(\frac{z}{x}).

Using bound (
2.2
) we again arrive in the necessity to estimate the convergence of

\sum_{x\in\gamma,x\neq 0}\frac{1}{|x|^{p+1}}

and then we proceed as above.

Remark 2.5
.

We have a straightforward generalization of this theorem to the case when
instead of the Lebesgue measure
m
we use another intensity measure

\sigma
for the Poisson random field.

Remark 2.6
.

It clear that the function
\Pi_{p}(z,\gamma)
has
\gamma
at the set of simple zeros.

3.
Marked Poisson zeros

Now we will consider the case of random zeros with random multiplicity.
Technically, this situation may be described by means of marked random point fields.

Let us introduce the marked configuration space
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{N}})
with marks from

{\mathbb{N}}
. For
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
we consider a pair
(x,n_{x})
with
n_{x}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The set of all such pairs
\hat{\gamma}=\{(x,n_{x})|x\in\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}})\}
we denote

\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{N}})
. Obviously,

\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{N}})\subset\Gamma({\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{N}}).

For a given probability measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{N}}
consider the Poisson measure
\pi_{m\otimes\lambda}
on
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{N}})

and denote
\pi_{m}^{\lambda}
its restriction on
\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{N}})
. It is possible because the last set has the full measure, see

[
7
]
for detailed study of marked random fields.

For a marked configuration
\hat{\gamma}
consider

\Pi_{p}(z,\hat{\gamma})=\prod_{x\in\gamma,x\neq 0}\left(E_{p}(x,\frac{z}{x}\right)^{n_{x}}

that takes into account the multiplicity
n_{x}
for each zero
x\in\gamma
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that

\int_{\mathbb{N}}nd\lambda(n)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}n\lambda(\{n\})<\infty.

For each
p\geq 2
and
\pi_{m}^{\lambda}
-a.a.
\hat{\gamma}\in\Gamma({\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{N}})

we have that

\Pi_{p}(z,\hat{\gamma})
exists as an entire function
of
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

Consider
z\in B_{R},R>0
. For

\Pi_{p}^{R}(z,\hat{\gamma})=\prod_{x\in\gamma,|x|>R}E^{n_{x}}_{p}(z/x)

holds

\log\Pi_{p}^{R}(z,\hat{\gamma})=\sum_{x\in\gamma,|x|>R}n_{x}\log E_{p}(z/x)

and then

\int\log\Pi_{p}^{R}(z,\hat{\gamma})d\pi_{m}^{\lambda}(\hat{\gamma})=\int\left(\sum_{x\in\gamma,|x|>R}n_{x}\log E_{p}(z/x)\right)d\pi_{m}^{\lambda}(\hat{\gamma})=

\int_{\mathbb{N}}\int_{|x|>R}nE_{p}(z/x)d\lambda(n)dm(x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{N}}nd\lambda(n)\int_{|x|>R}\frac{|z|^{p+1}}{|x|^{p+1}}dm(x)=

C\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{p}}d\rho<\infty.

We have used the bound (
2.2
). Therefore,

\log\Pi_{p}(z,\hat{\gamma})=\sum_{x\in\gamma}n_{x}\log E_{p}(z/x)

is finite
\pi_{m}^{\lambda}
almost surely and converges uniformly in
z\in B_{R}
. As above it gives the statement
of the theorem.

∎
