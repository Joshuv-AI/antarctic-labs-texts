Defect Fusion and Casimir Energy in Higher Dimensions


Abstract
We study the operator algebra of extended conformal defects in more than two spacetime dimensions. Such algebra structure encodes the combined effect of multiple impurities on physical observables at long distances as well as the interactions among the impurities. These features are formalized by a fusion product which we define for a pair of defects, after isolating divergences that capture the effective potential between the defects, which generalizes the usual Casimir energy. We discuss general properties of the corresponding fusion algebra and contrast with the more familiar cases that involve topological defects. We also describe the relation to a different defect setup in the shape of a wedge. We provide explicit examples to illustrate these properties using line defects and interfaces in the Wilson-Fisher CFT and the Gross-Neveu(-Yukawa) CFT and determine the defect fusion data thereof.

Introduction and Summary

Impurities in many-body systems embody a vast array of rich physical phenomena, such as phase transitions, symmetry breaking and universality under renormalization group (RG) flows. Interestingly this is the case even when the bulk system is weakly coupled or free.
One famous example is the Kondo effect
[
80
]
, that describes a strongly coupled spin impurity in a sea of essentially free electrons (see
[
8
]
for a review). Quantum Field Theory (QFT) provides a general and powerful framework to explain the dynamics of impurities in the continuum limit. For the prototypical Kondo model, this is achieved by modeling the impurity, in the so-called s-wave limit, by a boundary condition for the
d=2
theory of free fermions. Under RG, it flows to a certain strongly coupled conformal boundary condition in the infra-red (IR) which can be identified by Conformal Field Theory (CFT) methods
[
8
,
7
,
6
,
5

. More generally, under mild assumptions such as locality and reflection positivity, universality classes of isolated impurities that extend in
p\geq 1
spacetime directions are modeled at low energies by conformal defects that preserve the
\mathfrak{so}(p+1,1)
conformal symmetry. We will focus on the case where the bulk system is gapless and described by a CFT. If the bulk is gapped instead, a
p
-dimensional conformal defect therein is not much different from a conventional
p
-dimensional local CFT coupled to certain topological backgrounds (in dimensions
d>p
).

In recent years, a lot of progress has been made in the study of conformal defects in CFT, especially thanks to the bootstrap philosophy. The bootstrap approach has proven to be extremely successful in constraining and solving the local operator data of CFT based on very general principles such as unitarity, locality and conformal symmetry (see
[
98
,
74
,
99
]
for recent reviews). The challenge is to bring that success to the more general CFT observables that incorporate defects. Thus far most of the works on conformal defects have focused on the setup with a single defect insertion. This has already produced a large body of interesting results that capture the physics of isolated impurities, in particular from the operator-product-expansion (OPE) between a single defect and one or more local (point) operators
[
24
]
. However impurities also interact with one another in a nontrivial way, especially when immersed in an interacting bulk system. In particular, one expects a generalization of the OPE between the corresponding conformal defects, which capture the physical signature of a collection of interacting impurities observed from far away. Such an OPE for conformal defects would also be important for formulating bootstrap-type constraints with multiple defects in CFT
[

Here we consider the simplest nontrivial setting that consists of a pair of parallel

We consider two parallel line defects and use coordinates

Let us first analyze the contributions in (

\begin{split}&\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle=-\frac{h_{1,b}a_{D}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}-\frac{h_{2,b}a_{D}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(\vec{x}^{2}+(z-r)^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}+\\

where the coefficients

I_{2}&=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)^{4}}{(4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}})^{4}}\int\frac{d^{d-2}\vec{x}^{\prime}dz^{\prime}}{((\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+z^{\prime 2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z^{\prime}-r)^{2})^{d-3}}\,.\end{split}

(3.6)

It is easy to see that the above integrals are finite as long as
r>0
, and only diverge when
r\rightarrow 0
. So the leading divergences in (
3.5
) (at
r>0
) come from diagrams that only involve one of the defects rather than both. From these divergences we obtain the following relations between the renormalized and the bare couplings at the leading order in
\epsilon

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}\lambda_{b}=\lambda+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,,\\
h_{1,b}=h_{1}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\lambda h^{3}_{1}}{32\pi^{2}}(\Lambda_{T}a_{D})^{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,,\\
h_{2,b}=h_{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\lambda h^{3}_{2}}{32\pi^{2}}(\Lambda_{T}a_{D})^{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})\,.\end{matrix}\right.

(3.7)

Note that here we are using a scheme where
\Lambda_{T}
and
a_{D}
are independent scales.

We now resum diagrams that involve a single defect using the familiar Callan-Symanzik equation for the one-point function

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{D}_{1}\phi^{1}(x)}\equiv x_{\perp}^{\frac{2-d}{2}}\Phi\left(\Lambda_{D}x_{\perp},h\right)\,,

(3.8)

that we consider as a function of the renormalized coupling constant
h
, perpendicular distance
x_{\perp}=\sqrt{\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}}
to the defect and a defect width
\Lambda_{D}=a^{-1}_{D}
. The Callan-Symanzik equation states that physical observables should not depend on defect width
\Lambda^{-1}_{D}
and renormalized coupling constant
h
,

\displaystyle\left(\Lambda_{D}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda_{D}}+\beta(h)\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\right)\Phi\left(\Lambda_{D}x_{\perp},h\right)=0\,,

(3.9)

as long as we change
h
accordingly to its beta function,

\displaystyle\beta(h)\equiv\Lambda_{D}\frac{dh}{d\Lambda_{D}}=-\frac{4-d}{2}h+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{2}}h^{3}\,.

(3.10)

The general solution to (
3.9
) is given by the method of characteristics.
Namely we look for
\bar{h}(x_{\perp})
that satisfies the following equation and boundary condition,

\displaystyle-\frac{d\bar{h}}{d\log x_{\perp}}=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\bar{h}+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{2}}\bar{h}^{3}\,,\ \ \ \bar{h}\left(x_{\perp}=a_{D}\right)=h\,,

(3.11)

and then
\Phi(\Lambda_{D}x_{\perp},h)=\bar{\Phi}(\bar{h}(x_{\perp}))
for general
\bar{\Phi}
produces the desired solution to (
3.9
).
Solving (
3.11
) explicitly, we have then (assuming
h>0
)

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{D}_{1}\phi^{1}(x)}=\frac{1}{x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\bar{\Phi}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{8\pi^{2}(4-d)}+\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{8\pi^{2}(4-d)}\right)\left(\Lambda_{D}x_{\perp}\right)^{d-4}}}\right)\,.

(3.12)

Now, requiring that to the leading order in the
h
expansion the solution should match the perturbative calculation in (
3.5

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{D}_{1}\phi^{1}(x)}=-\frac{ha_{D}^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}x_{\perp}^{d-3}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3},\lambda)\,,\quad\quad

(3.13)

we can fix the unknown function
\bar{\Phi}
in (
3.12
) and the final result takes the following form,

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{D}_{1}\phi^{1}(x)}=-\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\frac{1}{(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{8\pi^{2}(4-d)}+\left(\frac{1}{h^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{8\pi^{2}(4-d)}\right)(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}a_{D}^{4-d}}}\,.

(3.14)

Taking the limit
a_{D}\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the one-point function for the conformal defect (see (
3.3
)),

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{D}_{1}\phi^{1}(x)}=-\dfrac{h_{*}}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,.

(3.15)

Thus after resumming the diagrams that are attached to a single defect, we obtain
that the two defects would individually flow to a conformal defect,

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{h_{1*}}{(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{h_{2*}}{(\vec{x}^{2}+(z-r)^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}+\text{cross-terms}\,,

(3.16)

where
h_{1*},h_{2*}=\pm h_{*}
(see (
3.3
)) depending on which of the two conformal lines
{\mathcal{D}}(\pm\hat{n})
describes the fixed point. The “cross-terms” above refers to contributions from all the diagrams that involve both of the defects (such as the last two diagrams in Figure
3
). In the following we will resum such contributions to fully determine
\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle
for
x_{\perp}\gg r
to the leading order in
\epsilon
.

Naively, if we omit these diagrams, the one-point function measured far away (i.e.
x_{\perp}^{2}=\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}\gg r^{2}
) would simply be the sum of the one-point functions from a single defect, already at zeroth order in
\epsilon
,

\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle=-\frac{(h_{1*}+h_{2*})}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,.

(3.17)

In particular, if both of the defects are described by the same conformal defect
{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})
at large distance, the resulting effective coupling for
\phi^{1}
on the defect would be doubled. This is in contradiction with the RG equation which admits one unique conformal line defect (up to a sign). This mismatch happens because we have to take into account the infinite contributions coming from diagrams that involve both defects to obtain the correct result even at the leading order in
\epsilon
. To show this, let us take a closer look at the cross-terms in (
3.16
), coming from the diagrams involving both defects (for example the last two diagrams in Figure
3
). Since we have already resummed all diagrams attached to a single defect, the integrals
I_{1,2}
should be modified accordingly. Consequently, the leading pieces in the cross-terms take the following form

\begin{split}\hat{I}_{1}&=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\int\frac{d^{d-2}\vec{x}^{\prime}dz^{\prime}}{((\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+z^{\prime 2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z^{\prime}-r)^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\,,\\
\hat{I}_{2}&=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\int\frac{d^{d-2}\vec{x}^{\prime}dz^{\prime}}{((\vec{x}-\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z-z^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+z^{\prime 2})^{\frac{d-2}{4}}((\vec{x}^{\prime})^{2}+(z^{\prime}-r)^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,.\end{split}

Let us estimate their contributions in the limit
x_{\perp}=\sqrt{x^{2}+z^{2}}\gg r
,

\begin{split}3\lambda(h_{1*}^{2}h_{2*}\hat{I}_{1}+h_{1*}h_{2*}^{2}\hat{I}_{2})\sim\frac{3\lambda\left(h_{1*}^{2}h_{2*}+h_{2*}h_{1*}^{2}\right)}{4-d}\frac{1}{x_{\perp}^{\frac{3d-10}{2}}}\gg\frac{2h_{*}}{x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,,\end{split}

(3.18)

which dominates over the naive solution (
3.17
) at large distances and modifies the effective coupling to
\phi^{1}
on the defect. To nail down the right value of this effective coupling, we have to resum the contributions that come from diagrams involving both of the defects. In particular, we have to sum up all the diagrams that
behave as
(\lambda/\epsilon)^{n}
when the separation
r\rightarrow 0
but are finite otherwise. Once we plug in the fixed point value of
\lambda
in (
3.3
), all of these contribution will add up to cancel the naive factor of two we encounter in (
3.17
) when
h_{1*}=h_{2*}=h_{*}
. We could perform this resummation explicitly using again the Callan-Symanzik equation, but instead we would like to present another method for resummation of these contributions in the spirit of
[
100
]
using the Schwinger-Dyson equation.

In the following, to ease the notation we will simply use
\phi^{1}(x)
to denote the one-point function
\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle
.
We notice that to leading order in
\lambda
this resummation is equivalent to solving the classical equation for the field
\phi^{1}
,

\displaystyle\Box\phi^{1}-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{x_{\perp}^{4-d}}(\phi^{1})^{3}=0\,,

(3.19)

where
\Box
is the scalar Laplacian,
with specific boundary conditions determined by the insertion of defects,

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{h_{1*}}{4\pi\left(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\,,\quad\vec{x},z\to 0\,,\\
-\frac{h_{2*}}{4\pi\left(\vec{x}^{2}+(z-r)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\,,\quad\vec{x},(z-r)\to 0\,.\end{cases}

(3.20)

Note that in (
3.19
) we have taken into account that at the fixed point of the bulk theory the coupling constant must depend on the distance.

We first consider the case when both defects are aligned (e.g.
{\mathcal{D}}_{1,2}
are both described
{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})
in the conformal limit) so that both defect coupling constants are tuned to the same value
h_{1*}=h_{2*}=h_{*}
. Then one can check that near both of the defects, the corrections are small and we can trust the approximation

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(x)\approx-\frac{h_{*}}{2\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,,\quad|\vec{x}|,|z|\lesssim r\,

(3.21)

On the other hand, in the limit
x_{\perp}\gg r
, we expect that the field
\phi(x)
should depend only on
x_{\perp}
and satisfy the following equation

\displaystyle(\phi^{1})^{\prime\prime}(x_{\perp})+\frac{d-2}{x_{\perp}}(\phi^{1})^{\prime}(x_{\perp})-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{x_{\perp}^{4-d}}(\phi^{1})^{3}(x_{\perp})=0\,,

(3.22)

subject to the behavior (
3.21
) for small
x_{\perp}
.

To solve this equation we perform the following change of variables

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(x_{\perp})=-\frac{g(\epsilon\log(x_{\perp}))}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,,

(3.23)

that leads to the following equation for the function
g(\epsilon\log(x_{\perp}))
,

\displaystyle\dot{g}-\epsilon\ddot{g}=\frac{1}{4}(d-2)g-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{16\epsilon\pi^{2}}g^{3}\,.

(3.24)

Taking into account that we work in the limit
d\to 4
and
\lambda_{*}\propto(4-d)
, we can neglect the term containing
\ddot{g}
in the above equation and obtain the following

\displaystyle\epsilon\dot{g}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}g-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{16\pi^{2}}g^{3}\,.

(3.25)

Notice that this coincides with the RG equation for the renormalized coupling constant
h
(
3.10
) and consequently we conclude that in the limit
x_{\perp}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(x_{\perp})\to-\frac{h_{*}}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,.

(3.26)

Next, we consider the case when the two defects are anti-aligned and the coupling constants have the opposite fixed point values
h_{1}=-h_{2}=h_{*}
. Close to the defects, we have the following approximation

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(\vec{x},z)=\phi_{(0)}(\vec{x},z)=\frac{zh_{*}r}{4\pi\left(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}\right)^{\frac{d+2}{4}}}\,,\quad|\vec{x}|,|z|\lesssim r\,.

(3.27)

Let us compute the first correction to this behaviour for large
x_{\perp}
treating the interaction term
\lambda_{*}
in (
3.19
) perturbatively as in

\displaystyle\phi^{1}=\phi_{(0)}+\lambda_{*}\phi_{(1)}+\ldots\,.

(3.28)

The solution is

\displaystyle\phi_{(1)}=\frac{h_{*}^{3}r^{3}}{7(4\pi)^{3}(4-d)x_{\perp}^{\frac{3}{2}d+1}}\left(z^{3}+\frac{6}{5(d-6)}zx_{\perp}^{2}\right)\ll\phi_{(0)}\,,\quad x_{\perp}\to\infty\,,

(3.29)

therefore we can conclude that the profile of
\phi^{1}
does not receive large corrections in
x_{\perp}\to\infty
, and we can trust the solution
\phi^{1}(\vec{x},z)=\phi_{(0)}(\vec{x},z)
even far away from the pair of defects. Since
\phi_{(0)}
vanishes in the limit
r\rightarrow 0
, we conclude that the fused defect is trivial.

Notice that vanishing of the one-point function of
\phi^{1}
does not rule out the possibility that the fused defect
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}\circ{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
could be a direct sum
{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})\oplus{\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})
for
\hat{n}=(1,0,\dots,0)
.
13
13

13

We thank Zohar Komargodski for a question and discussions on this point.
To exclude this possibility, we need to show that the one-point function of the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
even operator
(\phi^{1})^{2}
also vanishes (and
\langle{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})(\phi^{1})^{2}(x)\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})(\phi^{1})^{2}(x)\rangle
is nonzero). However, to the order we are working at, the one-point function of
(\phi^{1})^{2}
is proportional to the square of that for
\phi^{1}
and in particular
\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(\phi^{1})^{2}(x)\rangle
is given by the square of (
3.15
) and
\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}(\phi^{1})^{2}(x)\rangle
is given by the square of (
3.27
) in the limit
r\rightarrow 0
(the corrections to this start at order
\lambda^{2}
). This implies the fused defect is indeed trivial.

To sum up, if
h_{1*}+h_{2*}=0
, then the one-point function of
\phi^{1}
and
(\phi^{1})^{2}
(similarly for the
O(N)
singlet
(\phi^{I})^{2}
) fall off faster than
1/x_{\perp}^{d-2\over 2}
and
1/x_{\perp}^{d-2}
respectively. This implies that no defect is detectable in the limit of long distances, resulting in a trivial defect. On the other hand, if we start with
h_{1*}+h_{2*}\neq 0
then
\phi^{1}(x_{\perp})
would flow back to the conformal solution described by
h=h_{*}\operatorname{sgn}(h_{1*}+h_{2*})
.
Thus we have established the following fusion rules

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})\circ{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})={\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})\,,\quad{\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})\circ{\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})={\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})\,,\quad{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{n})\circ{\mathcal{D}}(-\hat{n})=\mathds{1}\,,

(3.30)

confirming (
3.1
) for the special case where the two defects are aligned or anti-aligned in the
O(N)
directions.

3.2
Symmetry Enhancement

We can generalize the above computations to a situation when the two original defects are coupled to different components of
\phi^{I}
, for instance
\phi^{2}
and
\phi^{1}
. We have the same action as in (
3.2
), but now
h_{2,b}
couples to
\phi^{2}
instead. In that situation, we start with having only an unbroken
O(N-2)
symmetry. But as we show now, at long distances (or equivalently, in the conformal limit for the fused defect), it gets enhanced back to
O(N-1)
. The resulting one-point function of
\phi^{1}
to leading order in the coupling constant
\lambda
is computed by the following,

\begin{split}&\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{1}(x)\rangle\\

As discussed in the previous subsection, when we study these defects at finite transverse distance
r\gg a_{D}
, the coupling
h_{1,b}
gets resummed to
h_{\pm}
(when we sum over all leading diagrams stemming from the same defect). This yields again naively

(3.32)

and the same holds for the other component

\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{1}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}\phi^{2}(x)\rangle=-\frac{h_{2*}}{4\pi\sqrt{\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}}}=\mp\frac{\sqrt{N+8}}{4\pi\sqrt{\vec{x}^{2}+(z-r)^{2}}}\,,

(3.33)

which are valid close to either one of the defects but corrections are important for more general

including when
x_{\perp}\gg r
.
To see this, note that we have the following RG equations for
h_{1}
and

\begin{split}\mu\frac{dh_{1}}{d\mu}&=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}h_{1}+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{2}}h_{1}(h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2})\,,\\
\mu\frac{dh_{2}}{d\mu}&=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}h_{2}+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^{2}}h_{2}(h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2})\,.\end{split}

(3.34)

This implies that at the fixed point for the coupled two-defect system
h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}=h_{*}^{2}
to leading order in
\epsilon
and the naive solution from
h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}=2h_{*}^{2}
is not allowed. Thus we have to take into account diagrams that involve both of the defects.

As in the previous subsection, we will resum these diagrams using the Schwinger-Dyson equation with boundary conditions specified by the defects. In fact, we can tackle the more general case when
h_{1}
couples to
\hat{n}
direction of the field
\vec{\phi}
while
h_{2}
couples to the component along
\hat{m}
. In this case, the equation reads

\displaystyle\Box\vec{\phi}-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{x_{\perp}^{4-d}}(\phi^{I}\phi^{I})\vec{\phi}=0\,,

(3.35)

with the following boundary behavior near the defects,

\displaystyle\vec{\phi}(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{h_{*}\hat{n}}{4\pi\left(\vec{x}^{2}+z^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\,,~\vec{x},z\to 0\,,\\
-\frac{h_{*}\hat{m}}{4\pi\left(\vec{x}^{2}+(z-r)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{4}}}\,,~\vec{x},(z-r)\to 0\,.\end{cases}

(3.36)

We can solve the above equation following the similar steps as the previous section. Far away from the defects, equation (
3.35

\displaystyle(\vec{\phi})^{\prime\prime}(x_{\perp})+\frac{d-2}{x_{\perp}}(\vec{\phi})^{\prime}(x_{\perp})-\frac{\lambda_{*}}{x_{\perp}^{4-d}}(\phi^{I}\phi^{I})\vec{\phi}=0\,,

(3.37)

with the following boundary condition in the near defects region,

\displaystyle\vec{\phi}(x)=-\frac{h_{*}\left(\hat{n}+\hat{m}\right)}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,,\quad x_{\perp}\to 0\,.

(3.38)

We can then argue as we have done in the previous subsection that at large distances,

\displaystyle\vec{\phi}(x)\to-\frac{h_{*}(\hat{n}+\hat{m})}{4\pi x_{\perp}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\sqrt{2(1+\hat{n}\cdot\hat{m})}}\,.

(3.39)

This establishes the fusion rules as in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\hat{n})\circ\mathcal{D}(\hat{m})=\mathcal{D}\left(\frac{\hat{n}+\hat{m}}{\sqrt{2(1+\hat{n}\cdot\hat{m}})}\right)\,.

(3.40)

Note that even though we started from a defect configuration which only preserves
O(N-2)
symmetry, at distances larger than the separation of the two defects, there is a symmetry enhancement and we recovers
O(N-1)
symmetry.

4.1.2

We start by considering the bulk-wedge two-point function of the bulk field

\displaystyle\Box\langle\phi^{I}(\rho,\varphi,\vec{y})O_{n}^{J}(\vec{y}_{1})\rangle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\left(\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta_{n}\right)^{2}+\partial_{\varphi}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{2\Delta_{n}\left(2\Delta_{\phi}-d+2\right)}{(\vec{y}-\vec{y}_{1})^{2}+\rho^{2}}\right)\frac{\delta^{IJ}f(\varphi)}{\rho^{\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta_{n}}\left((\vec{y}-\vec{y}_{1})^{2}+\rho^{2}\right)^{\Delta_{n}}}\,,

4.2.2
Interacting Wedge in
\epsilon

We are now ready to determine the Casimir energy of fusion for conformal boundaries in the Gross-Neveu model. As before, the wedge free energy receives contributions from the free fermions and the interaction term. Using the hyperbolic space picture, we may write it as,

\displaystyle F(\theta)=F_{0}(\theta)+I(\theta)

(4.88)

\displaystyle=-\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\sum_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\frac{|\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}\log\left(\lambda^{2}+\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right)}{|\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}}-\frac{g_{*}}{2}\int d^{d}x\langle\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}\right)^{2}\rangle\,.

The sum in the above equation is over

for
++/--

+-/-+

\displaystyle\mu(\lambda)=\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\frac{|\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}}{|\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}}\,.

(4.89)

Indeed, working on
S^{1}_{\theta}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d-1}

\psi

\begin{split}\psi(x)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\theta}}\frac{1}{\rho^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}}\hat{P}_{\pm}\left(n,\varphi\right)\chi_{n}(\rho,\vec{y})\hskip 28.45274pt+-/-+\\
\psi(x)&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\theta}}\frac{1}{\rho^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\hat{P}_{\pm}\left(0,\varphi\right)\chi_{0}(\rho,\vec{y})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\theta}}\frac{1}{\rho^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{P}_{\pm}\left(n,\varphi\right)\chi_{n}(\rho,\vec{y})\hskip 28.45274pt++/--\\
\end{split}

(4.90)

After performing a Kaluza-Klein reduction on
S^{1}_{\theta}
and using that
\bar{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{i}
and

for the

\begin{split}S&=-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}}\int\frac{d\rho d\vec{y}}{\rho^{d-1}}\bar{\chi}_{n}\left[\rho\left(\gamma^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\vec{\gamma}\cdot\vec{\partial}\right)-\frac{d-2}{2}\gamma^{\rho}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right]\chi_{n}\,,\end{split}

(4.91)

while for

\begin{split}S&=-\int\frac{d\rho d\vec{y}}{\rho^{d-1}}\bar{\chi}_{0}\left[\rho\left(\gamma^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\vec{\gamma}\cdot\vec{\partial}\right)-\frac{d-2}{2}\gamma^{\rho}\right]\left[\dfrac{1\pm\gamma^{\varphi}}{2}\right]\chi_{0}\\
&-\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\int\frac{d\rho d\vec{y}}{\rho^{d-1}}\bar{\chi}_{n}\left[\rho\left(\gamma^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\vec{\gamma}\cdot\vec{\partial}\right)-\frac{d-2}{2}\gamma^{\rho}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right]\chi_{n}\,,\end{split}

(4.92)

which includes the fermion zero mode. The formula for the free energy (
4.88

To proceed with the evaluation of (
4.88

F_{0}
, which we determine in the small
\theta
expansion and the result is given in (
4.97

We then study the interaction piece
I(\theta)

Applying the same trick as we have done in the scalar case, we differentiate

with respect to

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial F_{0}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\lambda\frac{|\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}}{|\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\lambda\right)|^{2}}\dfrac{1}{\left(\lambda^{2}+\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\theta^{2}}\right)}\dfrac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\,,

(4.93)

where

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial F_{0}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n}\dfrac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\bigg[\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)}{\frac{n\pi}{\theta}}\cos{\frac{n\pi^{2}}{\theta}}

\displaystyle-\cot{\left(\frac{\left(d-1\right)\pi}{2}\right)}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)}{\frac{n\pi}{\theta}}\sin{\frac{n\pi^{2}}{\theta}}\bigg]\,,

where the first term in the square brackets comes from the residues at

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial F_{0}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})\Gamma\left(\frac{3-d}{2}\right)}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n}\dfrac{n\pi}{\theta^{2}}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3-d}{2}+\frac{n\pi}{\theta}\right)}\,.

\displaystyle F_{0}(\theta)=\frac{N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1-d}{2}\right)\dfrac{1}{2}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n}\dfrac{\left(n\pi\right)^{d-1}}{\theta^{d-1}}\,.

(4.96)

which can be evaluated using zeta regularization,

\displaystyle

\displaystyle F_{0}^{++}(\theta)=F_{0}^{--}(\theta)=N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})\frac{\pi^{-\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\theta^{d-1}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\zeta(d)\,,

(4.97)

\displaystyle F_{0}^{+-}(\theta)=F_{0}^{-+}(\theta)=-N\textrm{Vol}({\mathbb{H}}^{d-1})\frac{\pi^{-\frac{d}{2}}}{2^{d}\theta^{d-1}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\left(1-2^{1-d}\right)\zeta(d)\,.

For the case of

\displaystyle E^{(1)}_{0}=\frac{g_{*}}{2}\left(N_{f}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int d^{d}x\bar{\chi}_{0}\chi_{0}\langle\bar{\psi}{\psi}(x)\rangle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\,,

Since we are only interested in the wedge free energy to leading orders in

To determine the contribution of the interaction term

\displaystyle\frac{g_{*}}{2}\int d^{d}x\langle\bar{\psi}{\psi}(x)^{2}\rangle=\frac{g_{*}}{2}N_{f}\left(N_{f}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int d^{d}x\langle\bar{\psi}{\psi}(x)\rangle^{2}\,,

4.3
Numerical Treatment of the Large
N
Model

In this section we will consider the critical
O(N)
model in the wedge using lattice regularization. We will tackle this problem numerically directly at large
N
(in the strict
N\rightarrow\infty
limit), which should provide insights on the analytical treatment of the problem at the physical dimension
d=3
. Such an approach also serves as a complementary counterpart to what was discussed in the previous subsection.

Let us briefly review the critical large
N
vector models in flat space (see
[
94
]
for a more detailed review). We start with the following action as before

\displaystyle S[\phi^{I}]=\int d^{d}x\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi^{I}\right)^{2}+m_{0}^{2}(\phi^{I})^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{0}}{4N}\Lambda^{4-d}\left(\phi^{I}\phi^{I}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(4.103)

where
m_{0},\lambda_{0}
are bare mass and coupling constants, which should be fine-tuned to bring the theory to criticality. We introduce the Hubbard-Stratonovich (HS) field
\sigma
and integrate over the fields
\phi^{I}
to obtain the following effective action for
\sigma
,

\displaystyle S[\sigma]=N\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\log\left[-\Delta+\sigma\right]+\int d^{d}x\left(\mu_{0}\sigma+\Lambda^{d-4}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\lambda_{0}}\right)\right]\,,\quad\mu_{0}=\frac{m_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\,,

(4.104)

which can be evaluated by saddle-point function in the large
N
limit. The saddle-point equation for
\sigma
, also known as the gap equation, reads

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{-\Delta+\sigma}\right]_{\mathcal{R}}(x,x)=\mu_{0}+\Lambda^{d-4}\frac{\sigma}{4\lambda_{0}}\,,

(4.105)

after choosing a regularization scheme
{\mathcal{R}}
. The notation
[..](x,x)
above represents the trace over the matrix operator
[..]
. To reach criticality, we solve for
\mu_{0}=\mu_{\rm crit}
by requiring that
\sigma=0
in the flat space time. One can check that if
2<d<4
the second term on the RHS of (
4.105
) is irrelevant in the IR and can be neglected. Thus to the leading order in the large
N
limit, it boils down to solving the following equation,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{-\Delta+\sigma}\right]_{\mathcal{R}}(x,x)=\mu_{\rm crit}\,.

(4.106)

Now we can apply this technique to our setup which involves the vector model on a wedge geometry. We work with the physical dimension
d=3
. Note that the metric and the HS field
\sigma
on such a background take the following form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=dy^{2}+d\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}d\varphi^{2}\,,\quad\varphi\in[0,\theta]\,,\quad\sigma(\rho,\varphi)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\sigma(\varphi)\,,

(4.107)

where
\sigma(\varphi)
is some unknown function and to be determined by minimizing the effective action (free energy) for
\sigma
. Once
\sigma(\varphi)
is fixed, then in principle we can determine any correlator in this geometry to leading non-trivial order in the large
N
limit. Below, we will calculate the wedge free energy by taking the coincident point limit of the two-point function as we have done in the previous subsections using
\epsilon
expansion. We will encounter some divergences in the process which we will regulate by working on a lattice. We will then minimize this free energy to determine the profile of
\sigma
and hence get the free energy at the critical point.

The scalar propagator of
\phi^{I}
satisfies the following equation,

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{y}^{2}-\partial_{\rho}^{2}-\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{\rho}+\frac{-\partial_{\varphi}^{2}+\sigma(\varphi)}{\rho^{2}}\right)G(y,\rho,\varphi,y^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\rho}\delta(\rho-\rho^{\prime})\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})\delta(y-y^{\prime})\,.

Note that the angular part is completely decoupled and we can find the eigenfunctions
\xi_{n}
and the corresponding eigenvalues
E_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{\varphi}^{2}+\sigma(\varphi)\right)\xi_{n}(\varphi)=E_{n}^{2}\xi_{n}(\varphi)\,,\quad\sum_{n}\xi^{*}_{n}(\varphi)\xi_{n}(\varphi^{\prime})=\delta(\varphi-\varphi^{\prime})\,.

(4.108)

Then using these eigenfunctions, we can expand the propagator into the following sum,

\displaystyle G(y,\rho,\varphi,y^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\sum_{n}\xi^{*}_{n}(\varphi)\xi_{n}(\varphi^{\prime})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho\rho^{\prime}}}G_{n}(\rho,\rho^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\,,

(4.109)

where
G_{n}
is the propagator for a massive scalar field with mass
\sqrt{E_{n}^{2}-1/4}
on
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\left(-\rho^{2}(\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{\rho}^{2})+\left(E_{n}^{2}-1/4\right)\right)G_{n}(\rho,\rho^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=\rho^{2}\delta(\rho-\rho^{\prime})\delta(y-y^{\prime})\,.

(4.110)

The explicit solutions are given by Legendre
Q
-functions
[
101
]

\displaystyle G_{n}(r,r^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\equiv G_{n}(Z)=Q_{-\frac{1}{2}+E_{n}}(Z)\,,\quad Z\equiv 1+\frac{(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{2}+(y-y^{\prime})^{2}}{2\rho\rho^{\prime}}\,.

(4.111)

Expanded near
Z=1
, the above becomes
[
19
]

\displaystyle Q_{-\frac{1}{2}+E_{n}}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}\log 2-\gamma-\psi\left(E_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log(Z-1)+\mathcal{O}(Z-1)\,,

(4.112)

where
\psi(x)
is the digamma function. We will adopt the
\rm\overline{MS}
scheme to regularize the propagator and obtain at coincident points,

\displaystyle\rho G(\rho,\varphi)=-\sum_{n}\xi_{n}^{*}(\varphi)\xi_{n}(\varphi)\psi\left(E_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(4.113)

This sum is still divergent, which we will handle with a lattice regularization. We first consider the case when there is no wedge (i.e.
\theta=2\pi
in the metric (
4.107
) with periodic boundary conditions) to benchmark the numerics. We approximate this circle by a lattice of
{\mathcal{N}}
sites located at the angles
\varphi_{i}=\frac{2\pi i}{{\mathcal{N}}}
. In this situation, we can approximate the Schrödinger operator in (
4.108
) by the following finite dimensional matrix
{\mathcal{N}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
,

\displaystyle H=-\left[\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\right]_{{\mathcal{N}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}+[\sigma(\varphi)]_{{\mathcal{N}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}\,,

(4.114)

with

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\right]_{{\mathcal{N}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}=\frac{{\mathcal{N}}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&\ldots&-1\\
-1&2&-1&\ldots&0\\
0&-1&2&\ldots&0\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
-1&0&0&\ldots&2\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad[\sigma(\varphi)]_{{\mathcal{N}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}=\operatorname{diag}\left[\sigma\left(\frac{2\pi i}{{\mathcal{N}}}\right)\right]^{\mathcal{N}}_{i=1}\,.

(4.115)

Such a matrix can be diagonalized using Matlab to find energies
\hat{E}_{n}
and eigenfunctions
\hat{\xi}_{n}(\varphi_{i})
, which are normalized as
\sum\limits^{\mathcal{N}}_{n=1}\hat{\xi}_{n}(\varphi_{i})\hat{\xi}^{*}_{n}(\varphi_{j})={\mathcal{N}}\delta_{ij}
. The regularized propagator at coincident points then follows from

\displaystyle\rho G(\rho,\varphi_{i})=-\sum^{\mathcal{N}}_{n=1}\hat{\xi}_{n}^{*}(\varphi_{i})\hat{\xi}_{n}(\varphi_{i})\psi\left(E_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(4.116)

In the large
N
vector models, this regularized one-point function by the virtue of the equations of motions is equal to the parameter
\mu

\displaystyle-\sum^{\mathcal{N}}_{n=1}\hat{\xi}_{n}^{*}(\varphi_{i})\hat{\xi}_{n}(\varphi_{i})\psi\left(E_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\mu\,,

(4.117)

and by setting
\mu
to

5
Discussion

In this paper, we pursue a general investigation on the fusion of extended defect operators in conformal field theory of dimension
d>2
, which generalizes the more familiar operator product expansion for point operators. We define a fusion product for a pair of parallel defects after subtracting divergences at small transverse separation and explain general properties of the corresponding fusion algebra for defects. The aforementioned divergences are themselves physical observables that keep track of the interactions between the pair of defect. In particular, the leading divergence captures the Casimir energy due to the defect insertion. Furthermore we explain the relation between parallel defect fusion and a wedge-like defect configuration in CFT. The latter together with the utilization of hyperbolic spaces provides an efficient tool to extract the Casimir energy between interfaces (boundaries).
To supplement the general discussion, we
have determined these defect fusion data (e.g. fusion algebra and Casimir energy) for a variety of concrete examples including line defects and interfaces in the scalar
O(N)
CFT and the fermionic Gross-Neveu(-Yukawa) CFT.

The ideas described here can be used to study the fusion of more general defects in CFT. One obvious target is the monodromy defect for global symmetries, such as
the
O(N)
monodromy defect studied in
[
64
]
. In the free theory of a single complex scalar
\Phi
, the
U(1)
monodromy defect
{\mathcal{D}}_{\vartheta}
is defined by demanding that the scalar
\Phi
picks up a phase as we go around the defect (using the same coordinates as in (
2.7
) with the defect at
\rho=0
),

\Phi(\vec{y},\rho,\varphi)=e^{i\vartheta}\Phi(\vec{y},\rho,\varphi+2\pi)\,,\quad\vartheta\sim\vartheta+2\pi\,.

(5.1)

Intuitively, the fusion product for such a monodromy defect is
{\mathcal{D}}_{\vartheta_{1}}\circ{\mathcal{D}}_{\vartheta_{2}}={\mathcal{D}}_{\vartheta_{1}+\vartheta_{2}}
. However the Casimir energy is much less obvious. Another interesting example in this class of CFTs is the magnetic line defect in the
O(N)^{3}
tensor model studied in
[
102
]
. In this case, there are a large zoo of conformal line defects corresponding to different, inequivalent choices of the source term
J_{abc}
under the action of
O(N)^{3}
, and because of that we expect the structure of defect fusion in the tensor models to be a lot more intricate.
For the fusion of codimension-one defects, it would be interesting to consider cases with interactions localized on the boundary
[
68
,
20
,
21
]
, which can define a nontrivial boundary condition even when the bulk theory is free.
Another interesting phenomena to study will be the fusion of defects of different co-dimensions, for instance, the fusion of line defects with boundaries. Apart from exploring examples for defect fusion, it would also be important to understand general constraints on the fusion algebra and the Casimir energy from basic principles such as unitarity and locality.

We have used the wedge geometry to analyze the fusion of conformal boundaries (also factorized interfaces) and in particular to extract the Casimir energy in Section
4
. However, studying CFT on a wedge geometry is an interesting problem in its own right, which has received some recent attention
[
25
,
12
]
. In particular, as was pointed out in
[
12
]
, one can write a crossing equation by demanding that the one-point function of bulk operators, when expanded in terms of operators on two different boundaries agree with each other. It will be interesting to exploit this crossing equation in explicit interacting examples such as the large
N
critical
O(N)
model or Gross-Neveu model. We expect the hyperbolic space method we have employed here to calculate the wedge free energy will be useful in this context. There is also potentially one additional channel where the bulk operator is expanded into operators at the edge which could lead to one more bootstrap equation for the wedge setup. It is worth exploring if this approach provides additional constraints on the wedge CFT data.

Finally, most of our calculations here in the explicit models utilized the
\epsilon
expansion. It will be useful to develop complimentary approaches such as large
N
expansion which may give us direct access to defect fusion in the strongly coupled CFT at
d=3
. It is possible to study this numerically directly in
d=3
as we have done in Section
4.3
for the fusion of ordinary boundary conditions in
d=3
critical
O(N)
model. But it will be desirable to develop an analytic approach at large
N
in arbitrary dimensions which can serve as a cross-check of our results in
d=4-\epsilon
which apply to arbitrary
N
.
