A Short Simple Proof of Closedness of Convex Cones and Farkas’ Lemma
Wouter Kager

Abstract




Farkas’ lemma is a cornerstone of the theory of linear inequalities, and is
often the starting point for proving the duality theorem of linear programming
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
*Ch. 6). Suppose
A
is a real

m\times\penalty n
matrix with column vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, and
b
is a
vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. To avoid trivial cases, assume
A\neq\penalty 0
and (with no
loss of generality)
a_{1}\neq\penalty 0
. Let
K
be the convex cone
\{Ax\colon x\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\}
, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the set of non-negative reals. Then

Farkas’ lemma
.

Either
b
lies in
K
, or there exists a vector
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\langle a_{1},y\rangle\geq\penalty 0,\dots,\langle a_{n},y\rangle\geq\penalty 0
and
\langle b,y\rangle<\penalty 0
(but not both).

In
[
6
]
, Komornik gave a simple proof of Farkas’ lemma, and stated as
a difficulty with “intuitive” proofs that
“the proof of the
closedness of
K
is not obvious”
. Broyden in fact calls this
“the
most difficult part of a geometric proof”

[
4
]
. But here we show
that closedness of
K
follows simply from the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem
once we have introduced the notion of an
optimal
vector in
[0,1]^{n}
.
Farkas’ lemma follows from this by standard means.

To illustrate why it is not obvious that
K
is closed, let
C
be the set of
points
(x,y)
such that
-1<\penalty x<\penalty 1
and
y\leq\ln(1-x^{2})
. Then
C

is convex and closed in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but the convex cone generated by
C
, i.e.,
the set
\{\lambda z\colon\lambda\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+},z\penalty\in C\}
, is the open
lower half-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
plus the point 0, which is not closed. Also, the
linear map
f\colon(x,y)\mapsto\penalty x
maps
C
to the open interval
(-1,1)
. So it is not true that a set is closed simply because it is the convex
cone generated by a closed set of vectors, or because it is the linear image
of such a set. We need a more refined argument.

We call a vector
u=(u^{1},\dots,u^{n})

optimal
if
u
lies
in
[0,1]^{n}
, has length
\lVert u\rVert=\penalty 1
, and there is no vector
z

in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
which satisfies
Az=\penalty Au
and has fewer non-zero components
than
u
. Note that
Au=\penalty 0
is impossible if
u
is optimal, but any
vector
v=\penalty Ax
with
x\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
can be written in the form
\lambda\,Au
with
\lambda\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
u
optimal: we can take
\lambda=\penalty 0
,
u=\penalty(1,0,\dots,0)
if
v=\penalty 0
, and if
v\neq\penalty 0
we can first
choose from the set
\{z\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\colon v=\penalty Az\}
a vector
z
with a
minimal number of non-zero components, and then take
\lambda=\penalty\lVert z\rVert
,

u=\penalty\lVert z\rVert^{-1}z
. We use this to prove closedness of
K
.

Lemma
.

Let
K
be the convex cone defined above, and suppose
\{v_{k}\}
is a
sequence of points in
K
that converges to some limit
v
. Then
v
lies
in
K
.

Proof.

Write
v_{k}=\lambda_{k}\,Au_{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\in\penalty\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
u_{k}=(u_{k}^{1},\dots,u_{k}^{n})
an optimal vector. Then the sequence
\{u_{k}\}
has a
subsequence
\{u_{\smash{k_{j}}}\}
that converges to some limit
u
in
[0,1]^{n}
of
length 1. Let
I
be the set of indices
i
for which
u^{i}>\penalty 0
, and
choose
k
in
\{k_{j}\}
so that
u_{k}^{i}>\penalty 0
for each
i
in
I
. Define

\mu:=\min_{i\in I}\,(\,u_{k}^{i}/\penalty u^{i}\,)
and
z:=u_{k}-\penalty\mu u
. Then
\min_{i\in I}\,(\,z^{i}/\penalty u^{i}\,)=\penalty 0
and
z^{i}=\penalty u_{k}^{i}
if
i\notin\penalty I
, so
z
lies in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and has at least one
non-zero component less than
u_{k}
. Since
u_{k}
was optimal, this implies

Az\neq\penalty Au_{k}
, hence
Au\neq\penalty 0
. But we know that
\lVert Au_{\smash{k_{j}}}\rVert\to\penalty\lVert Au\rVert
and
\lambda_{\smash{k_{j}}}\,\lVert Au_{\smash{k_{j}}}\rVert=\lVert v_{\smash{k_{j}}}\rVert\to\penalty\lVert v\rVert
. It follows that
\lambda_{\smash{k_{j}}}\to\penalty\lambda:=\lVert Au\rVert^{-1}\lVert v\rVert
, hence
v=\penalty\lambda\,Au
, which lies in
K
.
∎

We do not claim complete originality of the ideas used in the proof, but are
not aware of other publications that prove the lemma in exactly the same way.
In particular, while this note was under review it was brought to the author’s
notice that Bonnans and Shapiro
[
3
]
* Prop. 2.41 use optimal
vectors in a similar way, yet their proof is more involved than necessary and
not as direct as the one presented here. Other closely related but distinct
proofs can be found for instance in
[
2
,
5
]
and
[
7
]
*
pp. 96–97. We close with a proof of Farkas’ lemma:

Proof of Farkas’ lemma.

Suppose
b
does not lie in
K
. Set
\delta:=\inf_{v\in K}\,\lVert v-\penalty b\rVert
and for each
k
in
\mathbb{N}
, choose a point
v_{k}
in
K
such that

\lVert v_{k}-\penalty b\rVert<\delta+\penalty k^{-1}
. Then
\lVert v_{k}\rVert\leq\lVert b\rVert+\penalty\delta+\penalty 1
, so the sequence
\{v_{k}\}
is bounded and hence has a
subsequence
\{v_{\smash{k_{j}}}\}
which converges to some
v
. By the lemma,
v\in\penalty K
, hence
\delta=\lVert v-\penalty b\rVert>\penalty 0
. Now write
y=v-\penalty b
, and let
w
be one of the vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
or
-v
. Note that
then
\lVert y\rVert=\penalty\delta
, and
\lVert y+\penalty\lambda w\rVert\geq\penalty\delta

for
\lambda
in
(0,1)
because
v+\penalty\lambda w\in K
.
Therefore,

0\leq\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\bigl(\,\lVert y+\lambda w\rVert^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}\,\bigr)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert w\rVert^{2}+\langle w,y\rangle=\langle w,y\rangle.

This gives
\langle a_{1},y\rangle\geq\penalty 0,\dots,\langle a_{n},y\rangle\geq\penalty 0
and

-\langle b,y\rangle=\langle-v,y\rangle+\penalty\lVert y\rVert^{2}\geq\penalty\delta^{2}
.

If
b

does
lie in
K
, then
b=\penalty Ax
for some
x
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.
It follows that for any
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\langle b,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{i}\,\langle a_{i},y\rangle
. But then
\langle b,y\rangle<\penalty 0
implies
\langle a_{i},y\rangle<\penalty 0
for some
a_{i}
.
∎

Remark 1
.

The point
v
is actually the unique point in
K
closest to
b
. To see
this, recall that
y=v-\penalty b
, let
w
be in
K
, and write
z=w-\penalty b
. Then the point
\tfrac{1}{2}\,(v+\penalty w)
lies in
K
, so
\lVert y+\penalty z\rVert=\lVert v+\penalty w-\penalty 2b\rVert\geq\penalty 2\delta
. Hence,
v\neq\penalty w
implies

2\,\lVert z\rVert^{2}=\lVert y-z\rVert^{2}+\lVert y+z\rVert^{2}-2\,\lVert y\rVert^{2}\geq\lVert v-w\rVert^{2}+2\delta^{2}>2\delta^{2}.

Remark 2
.

Let
c
be the infimum of
\lVert Au\rVert
over all optimal vectors
u
, and
choose a sequence
\{u_{k}\}
of optimal vectors such that
\lVert Au_{k}\rVert\to\penalty c
. Then the argument from the proof of our lemma yields
c>\penalty 0
, which is a key step in Bonnans and Shapiro.
