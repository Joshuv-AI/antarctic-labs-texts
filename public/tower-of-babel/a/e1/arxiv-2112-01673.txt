Coherent states for equally spaced, homogeneous waveguide arrays
Julio Guerrero, Héctor M. Moya-Cessa

Abstract
Abstract Coherent states for equally spaced, homogeneous waveguide arrays are defined, in the infinite, semiinfinite and finite cases, and resolutions of the identity are constructed, using different methods. In the infinite case, which corresponds to Euclidean coherent states, a resolution of the identity with coherent states on the circle and involving a nonlocal inner product is reviewed. In the semiinfinite case, which corresponds to London coherent states, various construction are given (restricting to the circle with a non-local scalar product, rescaling the coherent states, modifying them, or using a non-tight frame). In the finite case, a construction in terms of coherent states on the circle is given, and this construction is shown to be a regularization of the infinite and semiinfinite cases.



I
Introduction

Many efforts have been directed towards the engineering of nonclassical states of quantum mechanical systems, in which certain observables exhibit less fluctuations than that of a coherent state, the so-called standard quantum limit (SQL). In this context, coherent states for the electromagnetic field, introduced by Glauber
Glauber63a
;
Glauber63b
and Sudarshan
Sudarshan63
, are of importance because their relatively easy way of generation in the laboratory and their classical-like wave behaviour. These states may be obtained by
different mathematical definitions: a) As eigenstates of the annihilation operator b) by the application of the displacement operator on the vacuum state of the harmonic oscillator, c) as states for which their time-evolving wave function shape does not change in time and whose centroid follows the motion of a classical point particle in a harmonic oscillator potential and d) coherent states that have a resolution to the identity
Klauder63-I
;
Klauder94
.

By using harmonic oscillator algebras, one may obtain the same coherent states from the three definitions above. However, for more complicated dynamical systems, beyond the harmonic oscillator, there is a need for new methods to generalize the idea of coherent states. Nieto and Simmons
Nieto78-I
;
Nieto79-II
;
Nieto79-III
have constructed coherent states for potentials whose energy spectra have unequally spaced energy levels, such as the Morse potential, the Poschl-Teller potential and the harmonic oscillator with centripetal barrier.

Man’ko
et al.

Manko97
introduced coherent states of an
f
-deformed algebra as eigenstates of a generalized annihilation operator,
i.e.,
an annihilation operator composed by the annihilation operator for the harmonic oscillator times a function of the number operator. A remarkable result is that such states may present nonclassical properties as squeezing and antibunching
Vogel96
.

Coherent states may be modelled in classical systems
LeonMontiel11a
;
LenMontiel11b
by injecting classical light in equally spaced waveguide arrays and monitoring its propagation properties. In particular it has been shown that Susskind-Glogower (or London) coherent states may be produced in semi-infinite arrays of waveguides when light is injected in the first waveguide (that corresponds to the vacuum state)
LeonMontiel11a
;
LenMontiel11b
(see also

Curado21
;
Zelaya21
). In the case of an infinite array (also equally spaced) coherent states of the group E(2) may be generated, whose amplitudes are given by Bessel functions that depend on the propagation distance
Jones65
(see also
Hacyan08
for other coherent states containing Bessel functions). On the other hand, a finite system of equally spaced waveguide arrays may be solved analytically with the use of Chebyshev polynomials
Makris06
;
SotoEguibar11
.

In this paper we shall focus on coherent states in equally spaced, homogeneous waveguide arrays, either infinite, semi-infinite and finite, identifying the obstacles in their construction and proposing different possibilities for a resolution of the identity. In particular, it will be shown that in all cases it is possible to construct a resolution of the identity with coherent states restricted to the circle, as it is has been already done for the infinite case
Guerrero21
. In fact, both the infinite and semiinfinite cases are easily recovered from the finite case when the number of waveguides grows to infinite, in this sense the finite case can be used as a regularization procedure for the infinite and semiinfinite cases.

III
E(2) coherent states

Coherent states for the case of an infinite-dimensional array of waveguides have been constructed in
Guerrero21
. Here we shall resume the main results in order to fix notation an to better compare with the semiinfinite and finite cases.

In the setting of Sec.
II.1
,
we have that the step operators
\hat{V}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{V}
satisfy the following unitarity property:

\hat{V}\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{V}=\hat{I}_{\cal H}

(18)

Therefore these operators, together with the number operator
\hat{n}
, constitute
a realization of the Euclidean algebra
E(2)
, with commutators:

\displaystyle\left[\hat{n},\hat{V}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\hat{V}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{n},\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\dagger}

(19)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{V},\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

It is clear that the unitarity property (
18
) is the eigenvalue equation for the cuadratic Casimir of the Euclidean algebra
E(2)

\hat{C}_{2}=\hat{V}\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{V}
.

Perelomov-type coherent states

can be introduced as the action of the Displacement operator:

\hat{D}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}}\,,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\,,

, then we have that
\hat{D}(\alpha)=\hat{U}_{\theta}(r)
.

The Displacement operator satisfies:

\hat{D}(\alpha)\hat{D}(\beta)=\hat{D}(\alpha+\beta)\,,\qquad\hat{D}(\alpha)^{\dagger}=D(-\alpha)

We define

coherent states as:

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha)|0\rangle=e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}}|0\rangle=e^{r\left(e^{i\theta}\hat{V}^{\dagger}-\left(e^{i\theta}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}\right)}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(2r)e^{in\theta}\hat{V}^{\dagger n}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha^{n}2^{n}k_{n}(2|\alpha|)|n\rangle\equiv\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}(r,\theta)|n\rangle

where we have used the generating function of Bessel functions

Using the asymptotic behaviour of Bessel functions for large

Particular cases for the overlap are:

The issue of coherent states of the Eucliean

Isham91

The key point is that coherent states of the E(2) group satisfy Helmholtz equation

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\,}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\,}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\,}{\partial\theta^{2}}+k^{2}\right)|\alpha\rangle=0

, which was derived from the Casimir condition and a differential realization for the E(2) algebra generators

. Coherent states

belong to the Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{osc}
of oscillatory solutions of Helmholtz equaiton
SphereMomentum20
.

Seen the complex number
\alpha=re^{i\theta}=x+i\>y
as a vector
\vec{\alpha}=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Eq. (
29
) is written as

\left[\Delta_{\vec{\alpha}}+k^{2}\right]|\alpha\rangle=0\,.

(30)

In Ref.
SphereMomentum20

Using the fact that Helmholtz equation is separable in polar coordinates, and that any regular solution (i.e. without
Y_{n}
terms) can be obtained from its values at a circle centered at the origin, we can try to obtain a resolution of the identity involving only coherent states on a circle.

In fact, if
\psi(r,\theta)
is a solution of (
29
) regular at the origin, then

\psi(r,\theta)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta^{\prime}\Delta_{r_{0}}(\theta-\theta^{\prime},r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\psi}}(\theta^{\prime})

\Delta_{r_{0}}(\theta,r)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{J_{n}(kr)}{J_{n}(kr_{0})}e^{in\theta}

Choosing

Using the asymptotic expression of Bessel functions (

The situation is similar to that of standard coherent states, when any function in phase space is expressed in terms of coherent states on a circle (see

In the present case there is no need to restrict the Hilbert space

of the Helmholtz equation have Fourier coefficients decaying fast enough to compensate the divergence of the propagator, and thus (

Let us introduce

|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}\rangle=|r_{0}e^{i\theta}\rangle.

The overlap of two such states is:

\langle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}{}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_{n}(2r_{0}){}^{2}e^{in(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)}=J_{0}(4r_{0}\sin\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}-\theta}{2}\right)

Note that with the choice of

The fact that

\langle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}|\hat{A}_{p}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}{}^{\prime}\rangle=J_{0}(4r_{0}\sin\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime}-\theta}{2}\right))

, and therefore are commuting operators.
.

These operators are known as London-Susskind-Glogower

;

phase operators, and do not close a finite-dimensional Lie algebra, therefore their treatment will be more complicated that in other cases where the ladder operators close a finite-dimensional Lie algebra, as is the case for the operators
\hat{V}
and
\hat{V}^{\dagger}
, which close the Euclidean algebra.

It is clear that the partial isometry property (

\hat{V}_{+}\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}=\hat{I}_{\cal H_{+}}
, is the eigenvalue equation (with eigenvalue 1) for the quadratic
Casimir

\hat{C}^{+}_{2}=\hat{V}_{+}\hat{V}_{+}^{\dagger}
i.e.
\hat{C}_{2}^{+}=\hat{I}_{\cal H_{+}}
. Although there is no finite-dimensional Lie algebra,
\hat{C}^{+}_{2}
commutes with
\hat{n}
,
\hat{V}_{+}
and
\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}
, for this reason we still call it a

.

We define the displacement operator for London-Susskind-Glogower phase operators
\hat{V}_{+},\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}
as:

\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}_{+}}

(40)

This operator can be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}_{+}}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}_{+}}e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}J_{n+2}(2r)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e^{i(n-2k)\theta}|n-k\rangle\langle k|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}e^{i(l-p)\theta}J_{l-p}(2r)|l\rangle\langle p|+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}J_{n+2}(2r)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}e^{i(n-2k)\theta}|n-k\rangle\langle k|\,.

Although the expression obtained here is slightly different from that of the operator
U_{+}^{\theta}(r)
in Eq. (
13

They satisfy
\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha)^{\dagger}=\hat{D}_{+}(-\alpha)

\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha)\hat{D}_{+}(\beta)=\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha+\beta)

(42)

if

\alpha=re^{i\theta}
and

. For
different arguments of
\alpha
and

the product does not have a simple expression since the London-Susskind-Glogower phase operators do not close a finite-dimensional Lie algebra.

If
\alpha=re^{i\theta}

We define London coherent states as

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{+}(\alpha)|0\rangle=e^{\alpha\hat{V}^{\dagger}_{+}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}_{+}}|0\rangle

With the standard construction of a resolution of the identity for Perelomov-type coherent states, we can try with:

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{+}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle_{+}\langle\alpha|

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}rdr\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\mu(re^{i\theta})|re^{i\theta}\rangle_{+}\langle re^{i\theta}|

This a difficult problem related to the

is at least invertible with a bounded inverse, (c) reescaling the coherent states or (d) modifying the London displacement operator

IV.1

\hat{D}_{LN}(\alpha)^{\dagger}=\hat{D}_{LN}(-\alpha)
and

\hat{D}_{N}(\alpha)\hat{D}_{N}(\beta)=\hat{D}_{N}(\alpha+\beta)

(95)

if
\alpha/\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
, i.e.
\alpha=re^{i\theta}
and
\beta=r^{\prime}e^{i\theta}
. For
different arguments of
\alpha
and
\beta
the product does not have a simple expression since the finite London phase operators do not close a finite-dimensional Lie algebra.

If
\alpha=re^{i\theta}
, then we have that
\hat{D}_{N}(\alpha)=\hat{U}^{\theta}_{N}(r)
.

We define the finite London coherent states as

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{N}(\alpha)|0\rangle=e^{\alpha\hat{V}_{N}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{V}_{N}}|0\rangle

(96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{in\theta}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}i^{-(2N+2)l}\left(J_{n-(2N+2)l}(2r)+J_{n+2-(2N+2)l}(2r)\right)|n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{in\theta}\left(\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{(N+1)l}(n+1-2(N+1)l)J_{n+1-2(N+1)l}(2r)\right)|n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}B^{N}_{n}(r)e^{in\theta}|n\rangle

with

B^{N}_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{(N+1)l}(n+1-2(N+1)l)J_{n+1-2(N+1)l}(2r)\,,\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1

(97)

The functions
B^{N}_{n}(r)
are well defined as can be checked using eq. (
24
), or noting that they can be expressed as a finite sum (from 0 to
N-1
) of products of Chebyshev polynomials (see
SotoEguibar11
). As in the case of E(2) and London coherent states, finite London coherent states are defined on the entire complex plane.

The overlap of two coherent states is given by:

{}_{N}\langle re^{i\theta}|r^{\prime}e^{i\theta^{\prime}}\rangle_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}B^{N}_{n}(r)B^{N}_{n}(r^{\prime})e^{i(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)}\,.

(98)

As in the previous cases, let us define the coherent states on the circle:

|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}\rangle_{N}=|r_{0}e^{i\theta}\rangle_{N}\,,

(99)

with
r_{0}>0
to be determined. The overlap of two states is:

{}_{N}\langle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}{}^{\prime}\rangle_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}B^{N}_{n}(r_{0})^{2}e^{in(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)}\,.

(100)

Note that the overlap is a trigonometric polynomial in the variable
\theta^{\prime}-\theta
. Let us fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and choose
r_{0}>0
such that
B^{N}_{n}(r_{0})\neq 0
for
n=0,1,\ldots,N-1
. Define the trigonometric polynomial

K_{N}(\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{B^{N}_{n}(r_{0})^{2}}e^{-in\theta}\,.

(101)

Contrary to the cases of
E(2)
and London coherent states,
K_{N}(\theta)
is an ordinary function. Using it we can construct the resolution of the identity

\hat{A}_{N}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta^{\prime}K_{N}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}\rangle_{N}\langle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\,\,\circ}}{{\theta}}{}^{\prime}|=\hat{I}_{{\cal H}_{N}}\,.

(102)

It should be stressed that under the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
all the construction reproduces that of London coherent states (only the the term
l=0
survives in the infinite sums). In this limit
K_{N}\rightarrow K_{+}
,
thus
K_{N}
can be seen as an approximation of the distribution
K_{+}
.

The infinite case can also be recovered, with a little more effort, shifting the state
|0>
to the middle of the finite array, which will be labelled from
-\frac{N-1}{2}
to
\frac{N-1}{2}
(in the case of
N
odd). In the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
E(2) coherent states are recovered. In this limit
K_{N}\rightarrow K
,
thus
K_{N}
can also be seen as an approximation of the distribution
K
.

Appendix A
Appendix: A primer on Frames

Let us review the construction of non-tight frames (see for instance
AAG14
).
Consider a set of vectors
\mathcal{F}=\{|\alpha\rangle\,/\,\alpha\in S\}\subset{\cal H}
, where

S=\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\ldots
, and
{\cal H}
is a Hilbert space.

Suppose that

\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|=\hat{A}\,,

(103)

where
\mu(\alpha)
is an appropriate measure and
\hat{A}
is an invertible operator with bounded inverse

\hat{A}^{-1}
(this equalities should be understood in the weak sense). This means that

m||\phi||^{2}\leq\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle|^{2}\leq M||\phi||^{2}\,,\qquad\forall|\phi\rangle\in{\cal H}\,,

(104)

with
0<m\leq M
.

If
m=M>1
the frame is
tight
, and in this case
\hat{A}=m\hat{I}
. If
m=M=1
we have a Parseval frame (like the set
of standard coherent states).

The frame condition (
104
) can be written as

m\hat{I}\leq\hat{A}\leq M\hat{I}

(105)

We shall normally consider
m=inf(Spec(\hat{A}))
and
M=sup(Spec(\hat{A}))
.

A.1
Dual Frame

Since
\hat{A}^{-1}
is bounded, we can define the new set of vectors

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}=\{\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}\,/\,\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}=\hat{A}^{-1}|\alpha\rangle\,,\alpha\in S\}
.
Then, it can be proven (see, for instance,
AAG14
) that
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
is also
a frame with

\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}\widetilde{\langle\alpha|}=\hat{A}^{-1}

(106)

and frame bounds

M^{-1}||\phi||^{2}\leq\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\widetilde{\langle\alpha|}\phi\rangle|^{2}\leq m^{-1}||\phi||^{2}\,,\qquad\forall|\phi\rangle\in{\cal H}

(107)

The most important result is that we can reconstruct the orginal
state
|\phi\rangle
from its
frame
coefficients
\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle
using
the dual frame vectors:

|\phi\rangle=\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}\,,\qquad\forall|\phi\rangle\in{\cal H}

(108)

The frame
\mathcal{F}
and the dual frame
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
are orthogonal to each
other in the sense that:

\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle\widetilde{\langle\alpha|}=\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}\langle\alpha|=\hat{I}

(109)

Expression (
109
) is the equivalent to the resolution of the identity
for standard coherent states, where in this case
\hat{A}=\hat{A}^{-1}=\hat{I}
and thus

\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}=|\alpha\rangle
.

A.2
Series expansion for the reconstructed states

Since the reconstruction formula (
108
) involves computing the
inverse
\hat{A}^{-1}
of
\hat{A}
, it is useful to obtain power series expansions for the inverse
and the reconstruction formulae with control of the size of the different terms of the series.

Let us suppose that the frame
\mathcal{F}
is
almost
tight, in the sense that
M-m
is small
and then
m\approx M
. Then
\hat{A}\approx\frac{m+M}{2}\hat{I}
,
\hat{A}^{-1}\approx\frac{2}{m+M}\hat{I}
and
therefore
\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}\approx\frac{2}{m+M}|\alpha\rangle
. More precisely,

\widetilde{|\alpha\rangle}=\frac{2}{m+M}|\alpha\rangle+(\hat{A}^{-1}-\frac{2}{m+M}\hat{I})|\alpha\rangle

(110)

and plugging this into the reconstruction formula (
108
):

\displaystyle|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle(\frac{2}{m+M}|\alpha\rangle+(\hat{A}^{-1}-\frac{2}{m+M}I)|\alpha\rangle)

(111)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m+M}\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle|\alpha\rangle+(\hat{A}^{-1}-\frac{2}{m+M}I)\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m+M}\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle|\alpha\rangle+(\hat{A}^{-1}-\frac{2}{m+M}I)A|\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{m+M}\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle|\alpha\rangle+\hat{B}|\phi\rangle

where
\hat{B}=\hat{I}-\frac{2}{m+M}\hat{A}
. Solving for
|\phi\rangle
, and denoting

|\phi\rangle_{0}\equiv\hat{A}|\phi\rangle=\int_{S}d\alpha\mu(\alpha)\langle\alpha|\phi\rangle|\alpha\rangle
, we have:

|\phi\rangle=\frac{2}{m+M}(\hat{I}-\hat{B})^{-1}|\phi\rangle_{0}=\frac{2}{m+M}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{B}^{k}|\phi\rangle_{0}

(112)

Using (
105
), the operator
\hat{B}
verifies:

-\frac{M-m}{m+M}\hat{I}\leq\hat{B}\leq\frac{M-m}{m+M}\hat{I}

(113)

and therefore the operator norm is bounded by
||\hat{B}||\leq\frac{M-m}{m+M}=w(\mathcal{F})
where
w(\mathcal{F})
is
the
frame width
.

The size of each term of the series expansion is bounded by:

||\hat{B}^{k}|\phi\rangle_{0}||\leq||\hat{B}||^{k}|||\phi\rangle_{0}||\leq w(\mathcal{F})^{k}|||\phi\rangle_{0}||

(114)

and therefore its size goes to zero faster than a geometric series of ratio

w(\mathcal{F})=\frac{M-m}{m+M}
. Also,

|||\phi\rangle||\leq\frac{2}{m+M}\frac{1}{1-w(\mathcal{F})}|||\phi\rangle_{0}||=\frac{1}{m}|||\phi\rangle_{0}||

(115)
