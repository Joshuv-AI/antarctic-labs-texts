Lyman- 𝜶 \boldsymbol{\alpha} feedback prevails at Cosmic Dawn: Implications for the first galaxies, stars, and star clusters
Olof Nebrin , Aaron Smith , Kevin Lorinc , Johan Hörnquist , Åsa Larson , Garrelt Mellema , and Sambit K. Giri

Abstract
Radiation pressure from Lyman- α \alpha (Ly α \alpha ) scattering is a potentially dominant form of early stellar feedback, capable of injecting up to ∼ 100 × \sim 100\,\times more momentum into the interstellar medium (ISM) than UV continuum radiation pressure and stellar winds. Ly α \alpha feedback is particularly strong in dust-poor environments and is thus especially important during the formation of the first stars and galaxies. As upcoming galaxy formation simulations incorporate Ly α \alpha feedback, it is crucial to consider processes that can limit it to avoid placing Λ \Lambda CDM in apparent tension with recent JWST observations indicating efficient star formation at Cosmic Dawn. We study Ly α \alpha feedback using a novel analytical Ly α \alpha radiative transfer solution that includes the effects of continuum absorption, gas velocity gradients, Ly α \alpha destruction (e.g. by 2 ​ p → 2 ​ s 2p\rightarrow 2s transitions), ISM turbulence, and atomic recoil. We verify our solution for uniform clouds using extensive Monte Carlo radiative transfer (MCRT) tests, and resolve a previous discrepancy between analytical and MCRT predictions. We then study the sensitivity of Ly α \alpha feedback to the aforementioned effects. While these can dampen Ly α \alpha feedback by a factor ≲ few × 10 \lesssim\textrm{few}\times 10 , we find it remains ≳ 5 − 100 × \gtrsim 5-100\,\times stronger than direct radiation pressure and therefore cannot be neglected. We provide an accurate fit for the Ly α \alpha force multiplier M F M_{\rm F} , suitable for implementation in subgrid models for galaxy formation simulations. Our findings highlight the critical role of Ly α \alpha feedback in regulating star formation at Cosmic Dawn, and underscore the necessity of incorporating it into simulations to accurately model early galaxy evolution.

Radiation from stars, stellar winds, and supernovae (SNe) inject copious amounts of momentum and energy into the interstellar medium (ISM), capable of destroying star-forming clouds. These stellar feedback processes are crucial for understanding why star formation in the local Universe is, on average, observed to be inefficient, with only
\sim 1\%
of the total (stellar and gas) mass of giant molecular clouds (GMCs) in our Galaxy being in the form of stars
(
Lee et al., 2016
;
Grudić et al., 2019
, e.g.)
. A key goal for theoretical astrophysics is to predict and understand the star formation efficiency from first principles, which requires detailed modelling of these feedback processes.

This issue has become increasingly important with the recent launch of the
JWST
and subsequent discoveries of surprisingly massive galaxies
(
Labbé et al., 2023
;
Chemerynska et al., 2024
;
Xiao et al., 2024
, e.g.)
and very dense star clusters
(
Vanzella et al., 2023
;
Adamo et al., 2024
)
during the Epoch of Reionization and Cosmic Dawn, when the first stars and galaxies formed (at redshifts of
z\sim 5
–
30
)
(
Bromm & Yoshida, 2011
;
Klessen & Glover, 2023
, e.g.)
. These discoveries of highly efficient star formation at high redshifts could indicate that our understanding of stellar feedback in the early Universe is incomplete, or that the standard model of cosmology (
\Lambda
CDM) needs modification
(
Boylan-Kolchin, 2023
, e.g.)
. Addressing the former (among other things) is necessary before we can conclude the latter. Still, the recent observations highlight potential discrepancies between theoretical models and inferred stellar masses, constraining the efficacy of feedback in these primeval galaxies.

To address the nature of feedback at high redshifts, it is prudent to consider the unique environments of star-forming clouds during Cosmic Dawn, which are expected to be qualitatively different from typical local GMCs. In particular, they exhibit the following characteristics:

1.

Very dense
: High-resolution cosmological simulations and models have consistently predicted large gas densities (
\sim 10^{4}
–
10^{9}\,\rm cm^{-3}
) and surface densities (
\sim 10^{3}
–
10^{6}\,\text{M}_{\astrosun}~\rm pc^{-2}
) to be common in star-forming clouds at Cosmic Dawn
(
Oh & Haiman, 2002
;
Safranek-Shrader et al., 2014
;
Kimm et al., 2016
;
Ricotti et al., 2016
;
Latif et al., 2022
;
Nebrin, 2022
;
Garcia et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. This prediction is broadly in line with recent detections of bound star clusters with stellar surface densities
\sim 10^{5}\,\text{M}_{\astrosun}~\rm pc^{-2}
at
z=6
–
10

(
Vanzella et al., 2023
;
Adamo et al., 2024
)
. Similarly, a significant fraction of the so-called ‘Little Red Dots’ may be extremely compact galaxies with stellar densities
\sim 10^{4}-10^{8}\,\text{M}_{\astrosun}~\rm pc^{-3}

(
Guia et al., 2024
)
.

2.

Dust-poor
: The ISM of the first galaxies has not undergone many episodes of metal enrichment by SNe. Consequently, the gas is expected to be dust-poor. Additionally, dust may be driven out of the first galaxies by radiation pressure
(
Fukushima et al., 2018
;
Hirashita & Inoue, 2019
)
. For the very first stars, there is no dust at all.

3.

Abundant atomic hydrogen
: The formation of H
2

(

;

;

. Strong Lyman-Werner (LW) feedback from young stars can also suppress H

)
. As a result, even dense and cool clouds can have substantial

i

These characteristics have significant implications for the nature of stellar feedback at high redshifts. Due to their relatively high densities, many if not most of the star-forming clouds at Cosmic Dawn will have short free-fall timescales
t_{\rm ff}<\textrm{few Myrs}
. As a result, massive stars do not have time to explode as SNe before the cloud is converted into stars, and star formation ‘overshoots’ and becomes bursty
(
Kimm et al., 2016
;
Faucher-Giguère, 2018
;
Nebrin, 2022
, e.g.)
. Thus, only so-called ‘early’ (pre-SNe) stellar feedback can regulate the star formation efficiency in such starbursts. Indeed, this holds true even for many local GMCs, which have estimated dispersal timescales of
<3~\textrm{Myrs}
, consistent with disruption by early stellar feedback
(
Kruijssen et al., 2019
;
Chevance et al., 2022
;
Chevance et al., 2023
)
.

Early stellar feedback from photoionization, stellar winds, and radiation pressure on dust has been extensively explored in the literature and incorporated into many state-of-the-art cloud-scale star formation simulations
(
Raskutti et al., 2016
;
Grudić et al., 2018
;

;

;

;

;

;
Hopkins et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. At Cosmic Dawn, radiation pressure on dust becomes increasingly irrelevant because of the lack of dust. Photoionization feedback is also expected to be weaker since very dense clouds, with escape velocities
\gtrsim 10\,\rm km~s^{-1}
, can avoid disruption after being photoheated to

(
Dale et al., 2012
;
Dale et al., 2013
;

Smith, 2014

. Thus, while important in certain contexts, these processes likely play a smaller role in regulating star formation in the early Universe.

The resonant scattering of Ly

By fitting Monte Carlo Ly

Tomaselli & Ferrara (2021)
studied Ly
\alpha

implemented their Ly

)

Recently,

simulated the formation of Pop III stars and found that

On paper, the above studies strongly suggest the importance of Ly
\alpha
feedback. However, as upcoming simulations and models start to incorporate Ly
\alpha
feedback in the pursuit of greater realism, we anticipate a looming problem. Namely, the
risk of severely underpredicting the star formation efficiency at Cosmic Dawn compared to recent JWST observations
. As alluded to earlier, this is a potential issue already facing current simulations that do not incorporate Ly
\alpha
feedback, although models often employ arbitrary momentum boosting calibrations to account for uncertain factors such as the high-mass
end of the IMF, numerical overcooling, and missing feedback processes
(
Rosdahl et al., 2018
, e.g.)
. Adding an additional dominant feedback process could exacerbate this problem, potentially sparking a new ‘small-scale challenge’ for
\Lambda
CDM
(
Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin, 2017
)
. We also note that Ly
\alpha
feedback is immune to the arguments of
Dekel et al. (2023)
against the importance of feedback from SNe, winds, and radiation pressure on dust at Cosmic Dawn, which we largely agree with in a qualitative sense
(
Nebrin, 2022
, see the above discussion and)
.

This motivates a careful study of Ly
\alpha
feedback and ways it could be dampened. It is well-known that dust absorption of Ly
\alpha
photons can set an upper limit to Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure
(
Cox, 1985
;
Bithell, 1990
;
Henney & Arthur, 1998
;
Oh & Haiman, 2002
;

;

(
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021

. For lower dust abundances, most relevant to Cosmic Dawn, dust alone cannot prevent Ly
\alpha

A less explored suppression mechanism for Ly
\alpha
feedback comes from
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions in hydrogen (Fig.
1
), which can destroy Ly
\alpha
photons if followed by two-photon emission to the ground state. Collisions with electrons and protons can induce such transitions
(
Pengelly & Seaton, 1964
;
Guzmán et al., 2017b
, e.g.)

2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions on Ly
\alpha
feedback
(
Chiu & Draine, 1998
;
McKee & Tan, 2008

. However, collisions with neutral atoms and molecules (e.g.
H
i
,
He
i
, H
2
) can also induce
2p\rightleftharpoons 2s
transitions
(
Slocomb et al., 1971
;
Ryan et al., 1977
;
Vassilev et al., 1990
, e.g.)
, the rates of which were not available or known during early discussions of
2p\rightleftharpoons 2s
transitions in astrophysical contexts
(
Spitzer & Greenstein, 1951
;
Pengelly & Seaton, 1964
, e.g.)
. The importance of this suppression mechanism has not been quantified.

Furthermore, most studies of Ly
\alpha
feedback have assumed uniform and/or static clouds
(
Dijkstra & Loeb, 2008
;
Smith et al., 2017a
;
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Lao & Smith, 2020
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
, e.g.)
. Ly
\alpha
photons can escape more easily from clouds with velocity gradients
(
Bonilha et al., 1979
;
Seon & Kim, 2020
)
. Similarly, cloud inhomogeneity and anisotropy can create low-density channels through which Ly
\alpha
photons can escape
(
Behrens et al., 2014
;
Zheng & Wallace, 2014
;
Smith et al., 2019
;
Kakiichi & Gronke, 2021
;
Almada Monter & Gronke, 2024
;
Yuan et al., 2024
, e.g.)
. Whether these factors can significantly suppress Ly
\alpha
feedback remains unknown but is crucial to assess in detail

Thompson & Heckman, 2024
)
.

Finally, there is also an unexplained factor
\sim 2
–
3
discrepancy between the estimated Ly
\alpha

(

;

)
compared to Monte Carlo methods
(

)
.

suggested that this could be due to the inclusion of atomic recoil by the latter, which slowly drains energy from Ly
\alpha
photons, pushing them to the red wing of the Ly

line and facilitating escape
(
Adams, 1971
;
Smith et al., 2017a
)
. Unfortunately, there are no analytical solutions with recoil,

In this paper we study Ly
\alpha
feedback with the goal of quantifying the importance of various suppression mechanisms, and derive an accurate estimate for the Ly
\alpha
force multiplier

, for use in subgrid models in galaxy and star formation simulations. The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
2.2

\alpha

\alpha
photon destruction (e.g. by
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions and dust absorption), velocity gradients, atomic recoil, and density fluctuations from supersonic turbulence. In Sec.
2.3
we use the solution to study Ly
\alpha

2.4
we carefully examine processes that can destroy Ly
\alpha

transitions in collisions with hydrogen (see Appendix

\alpha
feedback around the first stars. We discuss the implications of our results for star and galaxy formation at Cosmic Dawn in Sec.

2.2
Radiative transfer solution

Ly
\alpha
radiative transfer is notoriously complicated, and therefore only a few analytical solutions of the radiative transfer equation exist.
Harrington (1973)

(
Unno, 1955
, building on work by)
and
Neufeld (1990)
were the first to find analytical solutions of resonant Ly
\alpha
scattering in slab geometries. These authors allowed for the potential destruction of Ly
\alpha
photons by e.g.
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions, and dust absorption. Later on,
Dijkstra et al. (2006)
and
Tasitsiomi (2006)
found the corresponding solution for uniform static spherical and cubic clouds, respectively, with a central point source of Ly
\alpha
photons (e.g. a star), but ignoring Ly
\alpha
destruction
(
Seon & Kim, 2020
;
McClellan et al., 2022
, see also e.g.)
. More recently,
Lao & Smith (2020)
and
Tomaselli & Ferrara (2021)
generalized the earlier solutions for spherical clouds to consider density gradients and different source distributions. However, these studies neglected all destruction mechanisms besides dust absorption, and they did not consider the effects of velocity gradients, atomic recoil, or turbulent density fluctuations.

In this paper we derive a novel analytical solution describing resonant Ly
\alpha
scattering in a spherical, potentially turbulent, cloud. We then use this solution to estimate the momentum input from Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure, and its sensitivity to various effects (e.g.
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions, cloud expansion). New ingredients in this solution are:

1.

Generalizing the solutions of
Dijkstra et al. (2006)
and
Lao & Smith (2020)
for a spherical geometry to include a finite Ly
\alpha
destruction probability (e.g. by
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions, Sec.
2.4
). This effect had only been considered earlier by
Harrington (1973)
and
Neufeld (1990)
for a slab geometry.

2.

For the first time we allow for velocity gradients, as long as they are constant on cloud scales, as in, e.g., a cloud undergoing Hubble-like expansion or contraction. This generalizes the only known previous analytical solution by
Loeb & Rybicki (1999)
of a point source in an infinite expanding medium that had assumed zero gas temperature
(
Higgins & Meiksin, 2012
, see also)
. We explore the effect of velocity gradients on Ly
\alpha
RT (e.g. spectra) in greater detail in a separate paper
(
Smith et al., 2025
)
.

3.

We include atomic recoil, i.e. the (small) energy loss during each Ly
\alpha
scattering event, which can help push Ly
\alpha
photons to the red side of line centre, and hence make cloud escape easier. Recoil leads, on average, to a frequency jump of
-(h\Delta\nu_{\rm D}/2k_{\rm B}T)\Delta\nu_{\rm D}\equiv-\bar{x}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}

(see e.g.
Chuzhoy & Shapiro, 2006
;
Rybicki, 2006
, and Appendix
A.1
)
. Numerically we get:

\bar{x}=2.54\times 10^{-3}\left(\dfrac{T}{100\,\rm K}\right)^{-1/2}\hskip 1.0pt.

(3)

4.

We include continuum absorption/scattering, allowing us to model the effects of dust absorption/scattering or other continuum absorption effects (e.g. Raman scattering by H
2
). Dust absorption and isotropic scattering of Ly
\alpha
photons had only been considered analytically before for a slab geometry by
Neufeld (1990)
. As discussed in Appendix
A.1
, we allow for anisotropic scattering using the
\delta
-Eddington approximation
(
Joseph et al., 1976
;
Wiscombe & Joseph, 1977
)
, often employed in climate modelling
(
Briegleb, 1992
, e.g.)
. However, we find that there is no dependence on the scattering asymmetry as long as the Ly
\alpha
opacity is much larger than the continuum opacity.

5.

We generalize the solution further in Sec.
2.2.2
to include an approximate treatment of (potentially large) turbulent density fluctuations. This is achieved using the theory of asymptotic homogenization
(
Zwillinger, 1992
;
Holmes, 2013
, e.g.)
, which has been used in several other non-astrophysical contexts to derive effective diffusion coefficients in media with small-scale fluctuations. This allows us to study, analytically, the sensitivity of Ly
\alpha
feedback to Ly
\alpha
escape along low-density channels in turbulent media
(
Kimm et al., 2019
;
Kakiichi & Gronke, 2021
)
.

6.

Finally, while our main focus is on applying the solution to study Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure, we also derive analytical expressions for other quantities of interest – e.g. the predicted emergent spectrum, and the escape fraction of Ly
\alpha
photons. We will explore these applications of the solution in more detail in future work.

\alpha

\dot{R}_{\rm cl}=0
) and no Ly

), Eq. (

(

J(x,\tau)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}T_{n}(\tau)f_{n}(x)\hskip 1.0pt.

It is also convenient to follow

and introduce a new frequency variable

y(x)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\int_{0}^{x}\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\mathcal{H}(u)}\simeq\begin{cases}\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{3}}x&\quad\lvert x\rvert<x_{\rm crit}\\

(9)

Using the above Ansatz and variable change in Eq. (

) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}T_{n}}{\textrm{d}\tau^{2}}+\dfrac{2}{\tau}\dfrac{\textrm{d}T_{n}}{\textrm{d}\tau}\right)f_{n}=~

\displaystyle-\dfrac{3\mathcal{H}j_{\rm s}}{n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}}+\sum_{n}3(p_{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{2}+\mathcal{H}\epsilon)f_{n}T_{n}

where

(11)

for some eigenvalue

from the boundary condition that there is no incoming Ly
\alpha

I^{-}=J-\sqrt{3}H

I^{-}(\tau_{\rm cl})=0

\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}\mathcal{H}(x)}\dfrac{\textrm{d}T_{n}}{\textrm{d}\tau}\bigg|_{\tau_{\rm cl}}+T_{n}(\tau_{\rm cl})=0\hskip 1.0pt.

\lambda_{n}\simeq n\pi/\tau_{\rm cl}

\int_{0}^{\tau_{\rm cl}}\textrm{d}\tau\hskip 1.0pt\tau^{2}T_{n}(\tau)T_{n^{\prime}}(\tau)\propto\delta_{n^{\prime},n}

\displaystyle\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y^{2}}+\gamma\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}+\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}y}\left(\mathcal{H}f_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=~\lambda_{n}^{2}f_{n}+3(p_{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{2}+\epsilon\mathcal{H})f_{n}

\displaystyle-~4\pi\int_{0}^{\tau_{\rm cl}}\textrm{d}\tau\hskip 1.0pt\tau^{2}\dfrac{3\mathcal{H}j_{\rm s}T_{n}}{n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}}\hskip 1.0pt.

(14)

\mathcal{H}^{2}

pointed out that such terms are sharply peaked around

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}y\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{H}^{2}(y)=\sqrt{2/3}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}x\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{H}(x)=\sqrt{2\pi/3}

2.2.2
Approximate solution for a spherical turbulent

Real clouds in the ISM are not perfectly uniform. Supersonic turbulence, for instance, can generate large fluctuations in volume densities, column densities, and velocity gradients
(
Federrath et al., 2010
;
Federrath, 2013
;
Thompson & Krumholz, 2016
;
Buck et al., 2022
, e.g.)
. As illustrated in Fig.
4
, Ly
\alpha
photons are expected to escape more easily via low-density channels, and spatially fluctuating velocity gradients could induce large Doppler shifts of Ly
\alpha
photons and further aid in their escape
(
Munirov & Kaurov, 2023
)
. To assess the corresponding impact on Ly
\alpha
feedback, we have to consider Ly
\alpha
RT in non-uniform clouds.

\ell\ll R_{\rm cl}

\alpha

\alpha
RT, is to find the
effective
spatial diffusion term on scales
\gtrsim\ell

\lesssim\ell
) fluctuations in a rigorous manner.

If we allow for spatial dependence of the gas density and velocity, the Ly
\alpha

\displaystyle\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}J\right)}_{\textrm{Spatial diffusion}}+\underbrace{\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla\cdot u})}{b\alpha_{0}}\mathcal{H}\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial x}}_{\textrm{Doppler shift}}~

\displaystyle=~-\underbrace{\dfrac{3\mathcal{H}j_{\rm s}}{\alpha_{0}}}_{\textrm{Emission}}+\underbrace{3(p_{\rm d}\mathcal{H}^{2}+\epsilon\mathcal{H})J}_{\textrm{Ly}\alpha\textrm{ destruction}}

\displaystyle~-

\displaystyle~\underbrace{\dfrac{3}{2}\mathcal{H}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\mathcal{H}\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial x}+2\bar{x}\mathcal{H}J\right)}_{\textrm{Frequency diffusion \& recoil}}\,,

(28)

where
\alpha_{0}(\boldsymbol{r})\equiv n_{\rm HI}(\boldsymbol{r})\sigma_{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{r})
is the gas velocity. There is no general exact analytical solution of Eq. (
28
).
6
6

6

Indeed, similar problems of radiative diffusion in inhomogeneous clouds arise in atmospheric physics, where only limited analytical progress has been made
(
Davis & Marshak, 2001
, for a review, see)
.
However, we can render Eq. (
28

(
Zwillinger, 1992
;
Holmes, 2013
, for good overviews, see e.g.)
. HT is a powerful technique that can approximately solve diffusion equations with diffusion coefficients that exhibit large spatial fluctuations, provided these fluctuations occur on relatively small scales. With HT applied to Ly
\alpha
RT (Eq.
28
), we make the following assumptions:

1.

We assume that the gas density and properties (
\alpha_{0}
,
p_{\rm d}
, and
\epsilon
), and the gas velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
fluctuate on some formally small scale
\ell\ll R_{\rm cl}
(see Fig.
4
for a schematic sketch). Most often it is assumed that the fluctuations are periodic over ‘cells’ of volume
\sim\ell^{3}
, which can make the derivation more convenient
(
Zwillinger, 1992
;
Holmes, 2013
, e.g.)
. However, HT can also be applied for random fluctuations
(
Kozlov, 1993
;
Hanasoge et al., 2013
, e.g.)
. Here, we model the gas density as randomly fluctuating on small scales.

2.

Because of the first assumption, we assume there is a separation of scales, such that we can generally write
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi})
, and similarly for
\boldsymbol{u}
,
p_{\rm d}
, and
\epsilon
. Here
\boldsymbol{\xi}\equiv\boldsymbol{r}/\varepsilon
is the ‘fast’ spatial variable, characterizing fluctuations on small scales
\ll R_{\rm cl}
. For simplicity, we assume that the cloud is approximately uniform on large scales, so that
\alpha_{0}
,
p_{\rm d}
, and
\epsilon
are only functions of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

The goal is now to find the
effective
Ly
\alpha
RT equation, valid on scales
>\ell
. To do so, we expand
J(x,\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi})
as a power series in
\varepsilon\equiv\ell/R_{\rm cl}
, and use the chain rule for the gradient
\boldsymbol{\nabla}
:

\displaystyle J(x,\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=~J_{(0)}(x,\boldsymbol{r})+\varepsilon J_{(1)}(x,\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi})+\varepsilon^{2}J_{(2)}(x,\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\xi})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}

\displaystyle=~\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}+\varepsilon^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,,

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\equiv\partial/\partial\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\equiv\partial/\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}
are the gradients for the ‘slow’ and ‘fast’ variations, respectively. The assumption that
J_{(0)}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be understood intuitively to capture the fact that
J
should look uniform on scales
\gg\ell
(but smaller than
R_{\rm cl}
), which can also be derived self-consistently in HT
(
Zwillinger, 1992
;
Holmes, 2013
, e.g.)
. Finally, we note that we do not expand
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{u}
, but rather treat it as a given function of
\boldsymbol{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, as done for
\alpha_{0}
. We now use the above expansions in Eq. (
28
). The spatial diffusion term becomes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}J\right)

\displaystyle=~\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}J_{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}J_{(1)}\right)\right]\varepsilon^{-1}

\displaystyle+~\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{2}J_{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}J_{(1)}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle+~\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}J_{(1)}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}J_{(2)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,.

If we insert this result into Eq. (
28

\varepsilon
, we find that to order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})

0=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}J_{(0)}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}J_{(1)}\right)\,,

(31)

This can be solved using separation of variables. One finds that
J_{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{r}}J_{(0)}
, where the components of the vector

(
Zwillinger, 1992

Holmes, 2013
)
:

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{j}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\dfrac{\partial\Lambda_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\right)=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{i}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\,,

(32)

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm p})

7

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}J\right)\right\rangle

Isothermal turbulence is well-known to produce an approximately log-normal distribution of densities

(38)

Dagan (1993)

\sigma_{s}^{2}\lesssim 7-8

(

;
Neuman & Orr, 1993
;

)

8
8

8

5\%

in the simulations of

and

, it seems likely that the LLM formula can be extrapolated up to at least

, corresponding to

which in our context (Eq.

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}J\right)\right\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle\equiv~\dfrac{1}{\tilde{\alpha}_{0}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}}^{2}J_{(0)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,,

where we have used

\displaystyle\dfrac{\nabla^{2}J}{\tilde{\alpha}_{0}\langle\alpha_{0}\rangle}+\dfrac{\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla\cdot u}\rangle}{b\langle\alpha_{0}\rangle}\mathcal{H}\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial x}

\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}T_{n}}{\textrm{d}r^{2}}+\dfrac{2}{r}\dfrac{\textrm{d}T_{n}}{\textrm{d}r}=-\lambda_{n}^{2}\langle\alpha_{0}\rangle T_{n}\,,\quad\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\rm cl}}\textrm{d}^{3}\boldsymbol{r}\,\langle\alpha_{0}\rangle T_{n^{\prime}}T_{n}=\delta_{n^{\prime}n}\,,

\langle\alpha_{0}\rangle T_{n}(r)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y^{2}}+\gamma\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}+\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}y}\left(\mathcal{H}f_{n}\right)~

\displaystyle=~(\lambda_{n}^{2}+3\langle\epsilon\rangle_{m}\mathcal{H})f_{n}

(53)

\displaystyle+~\left[\sqrt{6\pi}\langle p_{\rm d}\rangle_{m}f_{n}-\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{s},n}\right]\delta_{\rm D}(y)\hskip 1.0pt,

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{3\sqrt{6}L_{\rm Ly\alpha}}{4\pi\Delta\nu_{\rm D}R_{\rm cl}}\dfrac{[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})-n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\cos(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})]}{\sqrt{2\pi\langle\tau_{\rm cl}\rangle}\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}^{3}(n\pi)^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle J(x,\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{3\sqrt{6}L_{\rm Ly\alpha}}{(4\pi)^{2}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}R_{\rm cl}^{2}}\dfrac{\tau_{\rm cl}^{\star}}{\langle\tau_{\rm cl}\rangle}

\displaystyle\times~\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})-n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\cos(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})]}{\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}^{3}(n\pi)^{2}(\mathcal{P}+n\pi\mathcal{D}_{n})}\dfrac{\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau})}{\bar{\tau}}\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt,

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{n}

Having derived a new Ly
\alpha
RT solution in the previous section, we can now estimate the Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure in the cloud, and study its sensitivity to various effects (e.g. Ly
\alpha
destruction, velocity gradients, and turbulence). As shown in Appendix
A.5
, the acceleration due to Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure is
\boldsymbol{a}_{\rm Ly\alpha}=(1/c\rho)\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\hskip 1.0ptn_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}\mathcal{H}\boldsymbol{F}
, where the flux and cross-section are evaluated in the
comoving
frame. We will focus on non-turbulent (i.e. uniform) spherical clouds first, and discuss the effect of turbulence in Sec.
2.3.6
.

Because of the assumed spherical symmetry, the flux, and hence the acceleration, is radially outward. The net radiation pressure force
\dot{p}_{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}
on the cloud is then

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\textrm{Ly}\alpha}

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{4\pi}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\hskip 1.0pt\int_{0}^{R_{\rm cl}}\textrm{d}r\hskip 1.0ptr^{2}n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}\mathcal{H}F

\displaystyle\simeq~-\dfrac{(4\pi)^{2}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}}{3c(n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0})^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}x\hskip 1.0pt\int_{0}^{\tau_{\rm cl}}\textrm{d}\tau\hskip 1.0pt\tau^{2}\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial\tau}

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{2(4\pi)^{2}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}\tau_{\rm cl}^{2}}{3c(n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0})^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}x\hskip 1.0pt\int_{0}^{1}\textrm{d}\bar{\tau}\hskip 1.0pt\bar{\tau}J\hskip 1.0pt.

(62)

On the second line the Ly
\alpha
flux has been expressed as
F=4\pi H=-(4\pi/3\mathcal{H})(\partial J/\partial\tau)
(from the Eddington approximation), and the third line reflects an expansion using integration by parts, along with adopting the variable
\bar{\tau}\equiv\tau/\tau_{\rm cl}
, and the fact that
[\tau^{2}J]_{0}^{\tau_{\rm cl}}=0
.
11
11

11

As noted by
McClellan et al. (2022)
, the series solution has
J=0
at the cloud edge, which stems from our approximate treatment of the boundary condition there. These authors propose a more complicated but rigorous solution to enforce the boundary condition at the cloud edge. With this they found that for
(a_{\rm v}\tau_{\rm cl})^{1/3}\gg 1
the series solution is fairly accurate, with the the flux
\propto\partial J/\partial\tau
even being accurate at the cloud edge. Thus, no large errors are expected from using the series solution in the cases of interest to us.

As a measure of the strength of Ly
\alpha
feedback, it is customary to define the dimensionless Ly
\alpha
force multiplier,
M_{\rm F}\equiv\dot{p}_{\rm Ly\alpha}/(L_{\rm Ly\alpha}/c)

(
Dijkstra & Loeb, 2008
;
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Smith et al., 2019
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
, e.g.)
. A value of
M_{\rm F}\gg 1
corresponds to strong Ly
\alpha
feedback, whereas
M_{\rm F}\sim 1
is akin to the single-scattering limit characteristic of radiation pressure on dust. Physically,
M_{\rm F}
is expected to be proportional to the time
t_{\rm trap}
that Ly
\alpha
photons remain trapped in the cloud, implying
M_{\rm F}\sim t_{\rm trap}/(R_{\rm cl}/c)
. In the case of static uniform clouds and ignoring Ly
\alpha
destruction, simple physical arguments and more detailed analytical and MCRT calculations predict that
t_{\rm trap}\sim(a_{\rm v}\tau_{\rm cl})^{1/3}(R_{\rm cl}/c)

(
Adams, 1975
;
Bonilha et al., 1979
;
Spaans & Silk, 2006
;
Smith et al., 2018
;
Lao & Smith, 2020
, e.g.)
. Thus, we expect in this limit that
M_{\rm F}\sim(a_{\rm v}\tau_{\rm cl})^{1/3}\sim 100
–
10^{3}
, and therefore that Ly
\alpha
feedback is very strong. Below we calculate
M_{\rm F}
and its dependence on various effects using the full Ly
\alpha
RT solution.

Using the series solution (Eq.
25

\displaystyle M_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\times~\int_{0}^{1}\textrm{d}\bar{\tau}\hskip 1.0pt\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau})\beta_{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}z\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt.

We see from Eq. (

1.

In the case of a slab geometry, the general solution for an arbitrary

2.

A spherical cloud with uniform Ly
\alpha

H
ii

\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}=\tau_{\rm s}/\tau_{\rm cl}
. The Ly
\alpha

\tau_{\rm cl}=5.88\times 10^{8}\,T_{100}^{-1/2}(N_{\rm HI}/10^{21}\,\textrm{cm}^{-2})

H

18

\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\ll 1
, even if the
H
ii
region is of comparable size to the cloud.

3.

In the opposite case of a small
H
ii

R_{\rm s}\ll R_{\rm cl}
, we must have
\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\ll 1
, regardless of whether it is defined in physical or optical depth space.

Thus, we conclude that
\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\simeq\tau_{\rm s}/\tau_{\rm cl}

H

\alpha

19

2.4

In Sec.

2.4.1
Ly
\alpha

2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions

If Ly
\alpha

2p\rightarrow 2s
transition (with probability
p_{2p,2s}
), and then a
2s\rightarrow 1s
two-photon decay transition (with probability
p_{2\gamma}
), the original Ly
\alpha

p_{\rm d,HI}
associated with this process is given by

\displaystyle p_{\rm d,HI}

\displaystyle=~p_{2p,2s}p_{2\gamma}+p_{2p,2s}(p_{2s,2p}p_{2p,2s})p_{2\gamma}

\displaystyle+~p_{2p,2s}(p_{2s,2p}p_{2p,2s}p_{2s,2p}p_{2p,2s})p_{2\gamma}+...

\displaystyle=~p_{2p,2s}p_{2\gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(p_{2s,2p}p_{2p,2s})^{k}\hskip 1.0pt.

(94)

The terms with parentheses allow for multiple transitions between the
2p
and
2s
levels before the final decay to
1s

being the probability of a

p_{\rm d,HI}=\frac{p_{2p,2s}p_{2\gamma}}{1-p_{2s,2p}p_{2p,2s}}\hskip 1.0pt.

The probabilities that enter this expression are:

\displaystyle p_{2p,2s}

\displaystyle=~\frac{\mathcal{R}_{2p,2s}}{A_{\rm Ly\alpha}+\mathcal{R}_{2p,2s}}\hskip 1.0pt,

\displaystyle p_{2s,2p}

\displaystyle=~\frac{\mathcal{R}_{2s,2p}}{A_{2\gamma}+\mathcal{R}_{2s,2p}}\hskip 1.0pt,

(97)

\displaystyle p_{2\gamma}

\displaystyle=~\frac{A_{2\gamma}}{A_{2\gamma}+\mathcal{R}_{2s,2p}}\hskip 1.0pt.

(98)

2.4.2
Spontaneous emission rates

A_{2p,2s}=8.78\times 10^{-7}~\rm s^{-1}

2.4.6
Neglected processes that contribute to the destruction probability

There are other processes that can promote transitions between
2p
and
2s
, or that contribute to the destruction probability more generally, that we have neglected. This is partially in the interest of keeping the subgrid model as simple as possible, and also because we think it is likely that they are of secondary importance compared to the processes that we do include. We briefly comment on some of the neglected processes:

•

Absorption of Ly
\alpha
by other neighbouring lines
: Besides the H
2
lines, there are also other lines near Ly
\alpha
. One of particular interest is the
1
S
0
–
1
P
{}_{1}^{\rm o}

1217.65~\textrm{\r{A}}
line of
O
i

(
Forsman & Clark, 1973
;
Kramida et al., 2023
)
. Very preliminary estimates by ourselves indicate that this could potentially suppress Ly
\alpha
feedback by a factor
\lesssim\textrm{few}
in some dense metal-enriched star-forming clouds. However, a more comprehensive study would need to couple the relevant level populations of
O
i
to the Ly
\alpha
and continuum backgrounds, as well as consider oxygen chemistry in star-forming clouds
(
Omukai et al., 2005
;
Hollenbach et al., 2009
, e.g.)
, making it considerably more complicated. Even if included, suppression of Ly
\alpha
feedback by
O
i
line absorption is likely to be of minor importance compared to dust absorption.

•

Maser amplification of the CMB
:
Dijkstra et al. (2016)
has noted that since Ly
\alpha
will pump the
2p
levels, we will get a
2s_{1/2}-2p_{3/2}
population inversion. This in turn can lead to a maser effect, where the CMB is exponentially amplified around
\lambda=3~\rm cm
. It is conceivable that this could boost
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions enough and suppress Ly
\alpha
feedback. Preliminary estimates by ourselves, however, suggest that (i) the effect is only potentially significant in very dense star-forming clouds where a massive star cluster can provide a strong enough Ly
\alpha
background, and (ii) the effect is strongly self-regulated, such that any boost in
2p\rightarrow 2s
by this mechanism will suppress
J
, and hence the
2p\rightarrow 2s
rate. A detailed analysis would need to couple the
2p
and
2s
levels to the Ly
\alpha
and 3-cm backgrounds self-consistently to find the equilibrium rate of
2p\rightarrow 2s
.

•

Other processes
: Besides the previously mentioned processes, we have also neglected: (i) collisional ionization and excitation/de-excitation (to
n\neq 2
), (ii) photoionization, and (iii)
\textrm{H}(2s)+\textrm{H}(2s)\rightleftharpoons\textrm{H}(2p)+\textrm{H}(2p)
. The rates of (i) are negligible in cold clouds compared to the other collisional processes included in this paper
(
Omukai, 2001
;
McKee & Tan, 2008
, e.g.)
, and (ii) can be neglected since the rate is negligible in self-shielded clouds, and because another Ly
\alpha
photon is likely to be produced following recombination
(
Dijkstra et al., 2006
;
McKee & Tan, 2008
)
. While the rate coefficient of (iii) is large,
\sim 10^{-6}~\rm cm^{3}~s^{-1}

(
Forrey et al., 2000
)
, the level population in
n=2
is many orders of magnitude smaller than
n=1
in cold gas (
T<10^{4}\,\rm K
). As a result, (iii) can be safely neglected.

Future work should revisit these processes with more detailed modelling, but we suspect that this will only lead to second-order corrections to the estimated
p_{\rm d}
in this paper.

\alpha

\alpha

M_{\rm F}
in colder star-forming clouds.

•

Gas density and Ly
\alpha
destruction
: To estimate the Ly
\alpha
destruction probability (Eq.

destruction in local star-forming clouds.

•

Velocity gradients
: We have assumed a fixed cloud-scale velocity
\lvert\dot{R}_{\rm cl}\rvert/b=10
. This would correspond to a cloud undergoing large-scale collapse or dispersal (e.g. by feedback) at a rate
\simeq 13~\rm km\,s^{-1}

\sim 2

underestimating

In the plots, we also assume an effective point source (

3.1
Implications for the first galaxies

The first galaxies are expected to have formed in the smallest low-mass haloes able to to host cool gas, and retain metals from the first stars
(
Nebrin et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. Because they formed in very low-mass haloes (
M_{\rm vir}\sim 10^{6}-10^{9}\,\text{M}_{\astrosun}
), they are particularly susceptible to bursty stellar feedback. Candidate ‘fossils’ of these galaxies are observed around the Milky Way, and known as Ultra-Faint Dwarf (UFD) galaxies
(
Simon, 2019
)
. UFDs have a wide range of stellar masses
\sim 10-10^{5}\,\text{M}_{\astrosun}
, and half-light radii
\sim\textrm{few}-\textrm{few}\times 100~\,\rm pc
, and
average
metallicities
-3\lesssim\langle[\textrm{Fe/H}]\rangle\lesssim-2

(
Simon, 2019
;
Fu et al., 2023
;
Smith et al., 2024
, e.g.)
.
31
31

31

The individual stars in a given UFD typically span a wide range of metallicities,
-4\lesssim[\textrm{Fe/H}]\lesssim-1

(
Fu et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. For this reason, and to be conservative regarding Ly
\alpha
feedback, we have shown a wider range of metallicities in Fig.
18
.
Most, or a significant fraction, of their stars formed at redshifts
z\gtrsim 6

(
Brown et al., 2014
;
Simon et al., 2021
;
McQuinn et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, consistent with quenching of star formation in low-mass haloes by reionization feedback
(
Ricotti & Gnedin, 2005
;
Jeon et al., 2017
;
Wheeler et al., 2019
;
Benitez-Llambay & Frenk, 2020
;
Nebrin et al., 2023
, e.g.)
.

Several recent high-resolution galaxy formation simulations have studied the formation of UFDs
(
Ricotti et al., 2016
;
Jeon et al., 2017
;
Wheeler et al., 2019
;
Agertz et al., 2020
;
Applebaum et al., 2021
;
Gutcke et al., 2022
)
. None of these simulations include Ly
\alpha
feedback. In Fig.
18
we plot the expected region in parameter space of the star-forming clouds in which UFDs formed. It is seen that Ly
\alpha
feedback is predicted to be strong in UFDs, contributing
\gtrsim 75-97\%
of the total radiation pressure, even under our conservative assumptions.

Furthermore, since UFDs are likely to have formed as gravitationally unbound star clusters
(
Ricotti et al., 2016
;
Nebrin, 2022
;
Garcia et al., 2023
)
, this suggests that their birth clouds were sensitive to disruption by strong feedback. As a result, Ly
\alpha
feedback may have an important effect on the predicted properties of UFDs. This was indeed found in
Nebrin (2022)
, where starbursts in low-mass haloes were modelled semi-analytically in detail. Several feedback processes were implemented, including photoionization feedback, stellar winds, direct and indirect radiation pressure on dust, and Ly
\alpha
feedback
(
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
, following)
. Ly
\alpha
feedback was found to be important in reproducing the abundance and properties of UFDs, especially at the low-mass end.
32
32

32

The effect of turning off Ly
\alpha
feedback in this model is the overproduction of very compact (
<1\,\rm pc
), gravitationally bound star clusters in low-mass haloes, and too few UFD-like galaxies. This leads to the (wrong) prediction that a significant fraction of local globular clusters formed in low-mass haloes at Cosmic Dawn. In contrast, with Ly
\alpha
feedback, such bound clusters are still predicted to have formed, with properties similar to those recently found by
Adamo et al. (2024)
, but at lower abundance. The star clusters that become unbound following gas expulsion by Ly
\alpha
feedback instead form low-mass galaxies, with properties consistent with UFDs around the Milky Way.
More detailed modelling and simulations should revisit the effect of Ly
\alpha
feedback on UFD formation. Until this is done, great caution should be taken in interpreting simulation results as bona fide
\Lambda
CDM predictions, since they are likely missing a significant fraction, if not majority, of the momentum input from early stellar feedback.
33
33

33

There are, of course, additional uncertainties related to generating realistic UFD galaxies in simulations. For instance, the UFDs in the FIRE-2 simulations of
Wheeler et al. (2019)
have too low metallicity, which the authors suggest may indicate the importance of enrichment by Pop III stars.

It is also likely that the implementation of Ly
\alpha
feedback in simulations and models could alter the predicted abundance of globular clusters (GCs). Indeed, this was found in the simulations of
Kimm et al. (2018)
, who found that Ly
\alpha
feedback reduced the number of star clusters formed in a metal-poor dwarf galaxy by a factor
\sim 5
. Similarly,
Nebrin (2022)
found that the abundace of bound star clusters formed in low-mass haloes at Cosmic Dawn is sensitive to Ly
\alpha

Abe & Yajima (2018)
had argued that the apparent metallicity floor of GCs,
\textrm{[Fe/H]}\gtrsim-2.5
, could be explained by Ly

However, we also note that these earlier studies had various shortcomings when it comes to estimating
M_{\rm F}
. For example, velocity gradients and turbulence were not taken into account in these estimates of
M_{\rm F}
. Furthermore, as discussed in Sec.
2.3
,
Kimm et al. (2018)
systematically underestimated
M_{\rm F}
for uniform, static clouds by a factor
\sim 2-3
.
Abe & Yajima (2018)

M_{\rm F}
by dust absorption. As shown in this paper, and by
Kimm et al. (2018)
and

at some specific metallicity. This conclusion is also consistent with more recent detections of GCs below the apparent metallicity floor

(

.

Additional observational constraints on the properties of high-

\alpha

3.3
Implications for the first stars

The very first stars (Pop III stars) formed in zero-metallicity environments, where there is no dust on which H
2
can form. Because of this, and the extremely high predicted gas densities, large
H
i
column densities are guaranteed to be found. As a result, several authors have argued that Ly
\alpha
feedback could be important in regulating formation and growth of Pop III protostars
(
Doroshkevich & Kolesnik, 1976
;
McKee & Tan, 2008
;
Jaura et al., 2022
;
Nebrin, 2022
)
.
McKee & Tan (2008)
generalized, in an approximate manner, the analytical slab solution found by
Neufeld (1990)
to study Ly
\alpha
feedback from Pop III protostars. These authors found that Ly
\alpha
feedback could potentially clear gas in the polar directions, after which Ly
\alpha
photons would easily escape, and Ly
\alpha
feedback would be suppressed.

More recently,
Jaura et al. (2022)
studied Pop III star formation in high-resolution simulations. These authors found that the
H
ii
regions remained trapped in the very dense gas on extremely small scales (
\lesssim\rm AU
). To facilitate
H
ii
region breakout, Ly
\alpha
feedback was suggested as a key missing ingredient in current simulations. Similarly,
Nebrin (2022)
studied starbursts in low-mass haloes using an early version of the (semi-)analytical model
Anaxagoras
, and also found that Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure is capable of regulating the total number of Pop III stars formed in minihaloes.

However, these studies have several limitations. For instance,
Nebrin (2022)
used the solution for the force multiplier by
Tomaselli & Ferrara (2021)
, which did not include, e.g., velocity gradients, Ly
\alpha
destruction by
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions and Raman scattering, and atomic recoil.
McKee & Tan (2008)
did consider some of these effects, but mainly relying on order-of-magnitude scaling relations, and not including the rate of
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions in collisions with
H
i
, which we have found to dominate over collisions with protons and electrons in most cases (see Fig.
14
).

There are two separate basic questions we can ask about the role of Ly
\alpha
feedback during Pop III star formation:

1.

Is Ly
\alpha
feedback important compared to other feedback processes?

2.

If the answer is
yes
to (i), is it strong enough to regulate Pop III star formation?

In Fig.
18
it is evident that Ly
\alpha
feedback dominates over direct radiation pressure. However, in Fig.
18
we had assumed an average
H
i
density
\langle n_{\rm HI}\rangle=10^{6}\,\rm cm^{-3}
for all parameter space. In contrast, simulations and models of Pop III star formation in minihaloes find extremely dense discs, with temperature
T\sim 1000\,\rm K
and (total) gas number densities
n\sim 10^{8}-10^{11}\,\rm cm^{-3}

(
Jaura et al., 2022
;
Sugimura et al., 2023
, e.g.)
. As long as not all the gas is converted into H
2
, this would imply
H
i
densities of a similar magnitude. With higher gas densities and temperatures like these, we estimate that the Ly
\alpha
destruction probability is
p_{\rm d}\sim 4\times 10^{-9}-10^{-8}
for
f_{\rm H_{2}}\lesssim 0.01
, in which case the destruction probability is mainly determined by
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions in collisions with
H
i
(see Figs.
14
and
16
).

If the
H
i
column density around Pop III protostars is
N_{\rm HI}\sim 10^{25}\,\rm cm^{-2}
, as estimated by
Jaura et al. (2022)
, we expect Ly
\alpha
destruction to suppress
M_{\rm F}
by a factor
\sim\textrm{few}\times 10
(see Fig.
7
). Velocity gradients near the protostar, anisotropic Ly
\alpha
escape, turbulence, and Raman scattering off H
2
, could further suppress
M_{\rm F}
by a factor
\sim\textrm{few}
. All in all, suppression of this magnitude could render Ly
\alpha
feedback too weak to help
H
ii
regions break out from the protostellar discs around Pop III stars, in contrast to the suggestion of
Jaura et al. (2022)
. This conclusion, however, is sensitive to the assumed Ly
\alpha
output – if Pop III stars grow more massive, their increased ionizing and Ly
\alpha
output (Table
1
) could, at least partially, counteract the suppression of
M_{\rm F}
by Ly
\alpha
destruction.

The key point here is that the presence of large
H
i
column densities and no dust, by itself, does not to ensure that Ly
\alpha
feedback will be efficient.
36
36

36

A similar point has been made by
Smith et al. (2019)
regarding the importance of Ly
\alpha
feedback, and by
Krumholz (2018)
regarding radiation pressure on dust. In both cases, gas expulsion can only occur if feedback is strong enough to overcome the gravitational binding force of the cloud. A feedback process may be dominant, but still, in some circumstances, be too weak to have a significant dynamical effect on the gas.
The answers to questions (i) and (ii) above may very well be
yes
and
no
, respectively. More detailed analytical and numerical modelling should revisit the role of Ly
\alpha
feedback during Pop III star formation, taking into account all major processes that effect Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure.

4
Summary & conclusion

Recent studies have emphasized the importance, and even dominance, of Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure feedback during star and galaxy formation, especially in high-redshift and dust-poor environments
(
Smith et al., 2017a
;
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Kimm et al., 2019
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
;
Nebrin, 2022
;
Kapoor et al., 2023
;
Thompson & Heckman, 2024
, e.g.)
. In contrast, recent
JWST
results indicate surprisingly efficient star formation in high-redshift galaxies. As simulations and models of the first stars and galaxies start to incorporate Ly
\alpha
feedback in the pursuit of greater realism, there is an urgent need to understand under what conditions Ly
\alpha
feedback is expected to be strong, or risk underpredicting the star formation efficiency.

In this paper, we have addressed Ly
\alpha
feedback using a multifaceted approach. We have:

1.

Derived a novel analytical Ly
\alpha
radiative transfer (RT) solution
: To study Ly
\alpha
feedback in detail, we have greatly generalized previous analytical Ly
\alpha
RT solutions for spherical clouds to incorporate the effects of continuum absorption, finite Ly
\alpha
destruction probability (e.g. by
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions), gas velocity gradients, atomic recoil, and low-density channels from ISM turbulence. We have verified the solution for uniform clouds against extensive Monte Carlo Ly
\alpha
RT (MCRT) experiments with
colt

(
Smith et al., 2015
)
, showing excellent agreement. This solution, and the framework used to, e.g., handle ISM turbulence, has broader applications beyond Ly
\alpha
feedback, such as predicting and understanding observed Ly
\alpha
spectra. We explore the effects of velocity gradients in greater detail in a companion study
(
Smith et al., 2025
)
, and also plan to generalize analytical solutions further to study other effects (e.g. different cloud geometries).

2.

Studied suppression mechanisms for Ly
\alpha
feedback
: We have conducted a comprehensive study of processes that can suppress Ly
\alpha
feedback. We have identified and studied several processes that can be particularly important in star-forming clouds. These include (1) continuum absorption by dust and Raman scattering off H
2
, (2)
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions in collisions with
H
i
,
He
i
, and H
2
, (3) velocity gradients, (4) ISM turbulence, and (5) atomic recoil. In the case of (2) we perform, to our knowledge, the first
ab initio
calculation of the rate for
H
i
at relevant temperatures (Sec.
2.4.4
and Appendix
B
), and show it to be very important in regulating Ly
\alpha
feedback around Pop III stars. The various suppression mechanisms covered in this paper are shown to be capable of suppressing Ly
\alpha
feedback by a factor
\sim\textrm{few}-\textrm{few}\times 10
, depending on the precise conditions in the star-forming cloud.

3.

Resolved the discrepancy between analytical and MCRT results
: We resolve an earlier unexplained factor
\sim 2-3
discrepancy between analytical and MCRT estimates for the Ly
\alpha
force multiplier
M_{\rm F}

(
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Lao & Smith, 2020
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
)
. Instead of missing physics (e.g. recoil), as speculated by
Tomaselli & Ferrara (2021)
, we found that numerical errors in earlier MCRT experiments by
Kimm et al. (2018)
caused the discrepancy. We find that our analytical solution is in excellent agreement with our MCRT results. As a result, despite finding Ly
\alpha
feedback to be very important,
Kimm et al. (2018)
may have systematically underestimated it, highlighting the urgency of implementing this feedback process in upcoming simulations.

4.

Developed an accurate analytical fit to the Ly
\alpha
force multiplier
: We derived a computationally efficient, accurate, and easily implemented fit to the Ly
\alpha
force multiplier
M_{\rm F}
, covering all major suppression mechanisms. This fit can be incorporated into a subgrid model for Ly
\alpha
feedback in both star and galaxy formation simulations. Such a subgrid model greatly improves upon earlier modelling that lacked several processes considered in this paper.

We also discuss some preliminary implications of our results:

•

Ly
\alpha
feedback is dynamically important
: Despite considering a multitude of suppression mechanisms of Ly
\alpha
feedback, we find that it prevails as an important, if not dominant, early feedback mechanism. This conclusion holds true for a wide range of metallicities and gas conditions. As a result, Ly
\alpha
feedback cannot anymore be ignored, and should be implemented in star and galaxy formation simulations and models, even with approximate prescriptions until more accurate treatments can be widely adopted.

•

Implications for the first galaxies
: Ly
\alpha
feedback is predicted to be very strong, if not dominant, in the first faint dwarf galaxies that formed in low-mass haloes at Cosmic Dawn (Sec.
3.1
). Ly
\alpha
feedback is therefore likely to have a significant impact on the predicted properties and abundance of Ultra-Faint Dwarf galaxies in high-resolution galaxy formation simulations. This conclusion is consistent with earlier findings from semi-analytical modelling, which was less comprehensive with respect to Ly
\alpha
feedback
(
Nebrin, 2022
)
. This could also have significant implications for testing
\Lambda
CDM using the faintest satellite galaxies of the Milky Way
(
Nadler et al., 2020
)
.

•

Implications for the first star clusters
: Ly
\alpha
feedback is likely a dominant early feedback process during the formation of star clusters, as found by several recent authors
(
Abe & Yajima, 2018
;
Kimm et al., 2018
;
Nebrin, 2022
)
. We find that strong Ly
\alpha
feedback imposes a lower limit on the stellar surface densities of young, gravitationally bound star clusters. We compare this constraint to recently discovered star clusters at redshifts
z\simeq 6-10

(
Vanzella et al., 2023
;
Adamo et al., 2024
;

•

Implications for the first stars
: Ly
\alpha
feedback is likely to dominate over direct (photoionizing) radiation pressure from Pop III protostars. However, whether it is sufficiently strong to aid in the breakout of
H
ii
regions around these stars, as suggested by
Jaura et al. (2022)
, remains unclear. This is primarily due to the process of
2p\rightarrow 2s
transitions in collisions with
H
i
, that we have identified and studied in this paper. We find that this process can suppress Ly
\alpha
feedback by a factor
\sim\textrm{few}\times 10
, potentially making it too weak to expel gas from the extremely dense protostellar discs around Pop III protostars. However, this conclusion depends to some extent on the ionizing output, and hence masses, of Pop III stars. Regardless of its ultimate importance, Ly
\alpha
feedback should be implemented in simulations of the first stars for physical completeness, as it is significantly stronger than the already implemented feedback from direct radiation pressure
(
Jaura et al., 2022
;
Latif et al., 2022
, e.g.)
.

The present study highlights that Ly
\alpha
feedback constitutes a rich astrophysical problem, where details of both resonant line RT and atomic/molecular physics can have a strong impact on one of the most dominant stellar feedback processes at high redshifts. While we believe that we have made significant strides towards realistic modelling of Ly
\alpha
feedback, our work has also opened several new avenues for future exploration. Among other things, these include further studies of Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure in clouds with complicated geometries, investigating the potential impact of Ly
\alpha
destruction by neighbouring lines (e.g. in
O
i
), more accurate calculations of cross-sections at low temperatures for key processes (e.g.
2s\rightarrow 2p
collisional excitation by
He
i
and H
2
), and studying the impact of deuterium scattering of Ly
\alpha

(
Dijkstra et al., 2006
;
Michel-Dansac et al., 2020
)
. State-of-the-art numerical and analytical methods will continue to address these questions and help us better understand how star formation is regulated in the early Universe. We therefore urge the simulation community to prioritize numerical implementations and benchmarks of Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure.

In the main text, we saw that the Ly
\alpha
mean specific intensity could be written as
J(x,\tau)=\sum_{n}T_{n}(\tau)f_{n}(x)
, with the frequency dependence
f_{n}
being the solution to the following differential equation (Eq.
15
):

\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y^{2}}+\gamma\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}+\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}y}\left(\mathcal{H}f_{n}\right)-(\lambda_{n}^{2}+3\epsilon\mathcal{H})f_{n}=\left[\sqrt{6\pi}p_{\rm d}f_{n}-\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{s},n}\right]\delta_{\rm D}(y)\hskip 1.0pt.

(139)

Here we solve Eq. (
139
). In the absence of recoil and dust absorption (i.e.
\bar{x}=\epsilon=0
), Eq. (
139
) can be solved by a simple exponential Ansatz (see Eq.
18
). With dust and/or recoil, the equation becomes harder to solve, as there are no known closed-form solutions, and a WKB approximation is inapplicable for a wide range of parameters
(
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
)
. Instead we can proceed using a power series Ansatz, following
Neufeld (1990)
who considered the case with dust absorption (but not recoil and cloud expansion/contraction). Eq. (
139
) is more straightforward to solve in terms of
x
. Using the wing limit
\mathcal{H}\simeq a_{\rm v}/\sqrt{\pi}x^{2}
gives the following equation away from line centre (i.e.
x\neq 0
),

\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}x^{2}}+\left(2\bar{x}+\gamma\sqrt{\dfrac{2\pi}{3}}\dfrac{x^{2}}{a_{\rm v}}-\dfrac{2}{x}\right)\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}x}-\left(\dfrac{2\lambda_{n}^{2}\pi x^{4}}{3a_{\rm v}^{2}}+\dfrac{2\sqrt{\pi}\epsilon x^{2}}{a_{\rm v}}+\dfrac{4\bar{x}}{x}\right)f_{n}=0\hskip 1.0pt.

(140)

To reduce clutter we can make a variable change,
x\equiv z\beta_{n}
, with
\beta_{n}=(3a_{\rm v}^{2}/2\pi\lambda_{n}^{2})^{1/6}
. We then get

\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}z^{2}}+\left(\tilde{x}_{n}+\tilde{\gamma}_{n}z^{2}-\dfrac{2}{z}\right)\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}-\left(z^{4}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}z^{2}+\dfrac{2\tilde{x}_{n}}{z}\right)f_{n}\equiv\mathcal{L}[f_{n}(z)]=0\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 5.0pt\textrm{where}~\tilde{x}_{n}\equiv 2\beta_{n}\bar{x}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 2.0pt\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\equiv\gamma\sqrt{2\pi/3}\beta_{n}^{3}/a_{\rm v}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 2.0pt\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\equiv 2\sqrt{\pi}\beta_{n}^{4}\epsilon/a_{\rm v}\hskip 1.0pt.

(141)

To avoid clutter in the following derivation of the solution to Eq. (
141
), we will temporarily drop the subscript
n
for
\tilde{x}_{n}
,
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
, and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
in this Appendix. Thus, for example,
\tilde{x}
should be understood to mean
\tilde{x}_{n}
below.

Eq. (
141
) is a linear differential equation with a regular singular point at
z=0
. It can be solved using Frobenius method
(
Arfken et al., 2013
, e.g.)
. The indicial equation for Eq. (
141
) is
r(r-1)-2r=0
, which has the two roots
r=3
and
r=0
. Since the roots differ by an integer we will have two linearly independent solutions
S(z)
and
P(z)
of the form

S(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k}z^{k+3}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 5.0ptP(z)=\mathcal{A}S(z)\ln z+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}z^{k}\hskip 1.0pt,

(142)

for some constant
\mathcal{A}
to be determined (we show it to be zero below). Plugging in a power series of the form
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k}
(to represent
S
and the power series part of
P
) in Eq. (
141
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k}~

\displaystyle=~\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(r+k)(r+k-1)c_{k}z^{r+k-2}+(\tilde{x}+\tilde{\gamma})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(r+k)c_{k}z^{r+k-1}-2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(r+k)c_{k}z^{r+k-2}

\displaystyle-~\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k+4}-\tilde{\epsilon}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k+2}-2\tilde{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k-1}

\displaystyle=~\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(r+k)(r+k-3)c_{k}z^{r+k-2}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}[\tilde{x}(r+k-2)+\tilde{\gamma}(r+k)]c_{k}z^{r+k-1}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k+4}-\tilde{\epsilon}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k+2}\hskip 1.0pt.

(143)

Expanding, collecting terms, and shifting indices gives us

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{r+k}~

\displaystyle=~\underbrace{[r(r-1)-2r]}_{=~0}c_{0}z^{r-2}+[(r+1)(r-2)c_{1}+\tilde{x}(r-2)c_{0}]z^{r-1}+[(r+2)(r-1)c_{2}+\tilde{x}(r-1)c_{1}]z^{r}

\displaystyle+~[(r+3)rc_{3}+\tilde{x}rc_{2}+\tilde{\gamma}rc_{0}]z^{r+1}+[(r+4)(r+1)c_{4}+\tilde{x}(r+1)c_{3}+\tilde{\gamma}(r+1)c_{1}-\tilde{\epsilon}c_{0}]z^{r+2}

\displaystyle+~[(r+5)(r+2)c_{5}+\tilde{x}(r+2)c_{4}+\tilde{\gamma}(r+2)c_{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}c_{1}]z^{r+3}

\displaystyle+~\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\{(r+k+6)(r+k+3)c_{k+6}+\tilde{x}(r+k+3)c_{k+5}+\tilde{\gamma}(r+k+3)c_{k+3}-c_{k}-\tilde{\epsilon}c_{k+2}\}z^{r+k+4}\hskip 1.0pt.

(144)

We can now derive the coefficients for
S(z)
, which had
r=3
. Since
\mathcal{L}[S(z)]=0
we find, after fixing
s_{0}=1
:

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle=1\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0pts_{1}=-\dfrac{1}{4}\tilde{x}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0pts_{2}=\dfrac{1}{5\times 4}\tilde{x}^{2}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0pts_{3}=-\dfrac{1}{6\times 5\times 4}\tilde{x}^{3}-\dfrac{1}{6}\tilde{\gamma}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0pts_{4}=-\dfrac{1}{7}\tilde{x}s_{3}-\dfrac{1}{7}\tilde{\gamma}s_{1}+\dfrac{1}{7\times 4}\tilde{\epsilon}\hskip 1.0pt,

\displaystyle s_{5}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{8}\tilde{x}s_{4}-\dfrac{1}{8}\tilde{\gamma}s_{2}+\dfrac{1}{8\times 5}\tilde{\epsilon}s_{1}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0pts_{k+6}=-\dfrac{\tilde{x}s_{k+5}}{(k+9)}-\dfrac{\tilde{\gamma}s_{k+3}}{(k+9)}+\dfrac{s_{k}+\tilde{\epsilon}s_{k+2}}{(k+9)(k+6)}\hskip 5.0pt\textrm{for }k\geq 0\hskip 1.0pt.

(145)

If we ignore recoil and consider a static cloud (i.e.
\tilde{x}=\tilde{\gamma}=0
) we recover the series solution found by
Neufeld (1990)
(see his eq. B9). Next we consider
P(z)
. We first note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[S(z)\ln z]~

\displaystyle=~\underbrace{\mathcal{L}[S(z)]}_{=~0}\ln z+\dfrac{2}{z}\dfrac{\textrm{d}S}{\textrm{d}z}-\dfrac{S}{z^{2}}+\left(\tilde{x}+\tilde{\gamma}z^{2}-\dfrac{2}{z}\right)\dfrac{S}{z}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}[(2k+3)s_{k}z^{k+1}+\tilde{x}s_{k}z^{k+2}+\tilde{\gamma}s_{k}z^{k+4}]\hskip 1.0pt.

(146)

Using Eqs. (
146
) and (
144
) with
r=0
then yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[P(z)]=0~

\displaystyle=~\mathcal{A}(3s_{0}z+\tilde{x}s_{0}z^{2}+\tilde{\gamma}s_{0}z^{4})+\mathcal{A}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}[(2k+3)s_{k}z^{k+1}+\tilde{x}s_{k}z^{k+2}+\tilde{\gamma}s_{k}z^{k+4}]

\displaystyle+~(-2p_{1}-2\tilde{x}p_{0})z^{-1}+(-2p_{2}-\tilde{x}p_{1})+0\times z+(4p_{4}+\tilde{x}p_{3}+\tilde{\gamma}p_{1}-\tilde{\epsilon}p_{0})z^{2}+(10p_{5}+2\tilde{x}p_{4}+2\tilde{\gamma}p_{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}p_{1})z^{5}

\displaystyle+~\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}[(k+6)(k+3)p_{k+6}+(k+3)\tilde{x}p_{k+5}+(k+3)\tilde{\gamma}p_{k+3}-\tilde{\epsilon}p_{k+2}-p_{k}]z^{k+4}

(147)

We see from the
\mathcal{O}(z)
coefficients that
\mathcal{A}=0
, and so there is no logarithmic contribution in the solution
P(z)
. Setting each coefficient of
z^{k}
to zero then yields (after fixing
p_{0}=1
,
p_{3}=0
):

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=1\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0ptp_{1}=-\tilde{x}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0ptp_{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}\tilde{x}^{2}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0ptp_{3}=0\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0ptp_{4}=\dfrac{1}{4}\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{x}+\dfrac{1}{4}\tilde{\epsilon}\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 8.0ptp_{5}=\dfrac{1}{10}\tilde{\epsilon}p_{1}-\dfrac{1}{5}\tilde{\gamma}p_{2}-\dfrac{1}{10}\tilde{x}p_{4}\hskip 1.0pt,

\displaystyle p_{k+6}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{p_{k}+\tilde{\epsilon}p_{k+2}}{(k+6)(k+3)}-\dfrac{\tilde{x}p_{k+5}+\tilde{\gamma}p_{k+3}}{(k+6)}\hskip 5.0pt\textrm{for }k\geq 0\hskip 1.0pt.

(148)

In summary so far, we have found that the general solution for
f_{n}(z)
is

f_{n}(z)=\mathcal{C}_{1}P(z)+\mathcal{C}_{2}S(z)=\mathcal{C}_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}z^{k}+\mathcal{C}_{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k}z^{k+3}\hskip 1.0pt,

(149)

where the coefficients
p_{k}
and
s_{k}
are given by Eqs. (
148
) and (
145
), respectively, and where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are constants. To get the physically relevant solution we impose the boundary condition
f_{n}(\lvert z\rvert=\infty)=0
(no intensity very far from line centre). This fixes one of the constants, and we can therefore write the solution away from line centre as

f_{n}(z>0)=\mathcal{C}[P(z)-S(z)\mathcal{R}_{+}]\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 5.0ptf_{n}(z<0)=\mathcal{C}[P(z)-S(z)\mathcal{R}_{-}]\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 5.0pt\textrm{where }\mathcal{R}_{\pm}\equiv\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}\dfrac{P(z)}{S(z)}\hskip 1.0pt.

(150)

The final constant
\mathcal{C}
can be determined from the jump in derivative imposed from the source term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
139
). Integrating Eq. (
139
) from
y=-y_{\rm max}
to
y=y_{\rm max}
and taking the limit
y_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0
yields for the left-hand side:

\displaystyle\lim_{y_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0}\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}\bigg|_{y_{\rm max}}+\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\mathcal{H}f_{n}\big|_{y_{\rm max}}-\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}\bigg|_{-y_{\rm max}}-\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\mathcal{H}f_{n}\big|_{-y_{\rm max}}\right)~

\displaystyle=~\dfrac{a_{\rm v}}{\sqrt{\pi}\beta_{n}^{2}}\lim_{z_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{z_{\rm max}^{2}}\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{2}}\dfrac{1}{\beta_{n}}\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}\bigg|_{z_{\rm max}}-\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}\bigg|_{-z_{\rm max}}\right)

\displaystyle+~\dfrac{a_{\rm v}}{\sqrt{\pi}\beta_{n}^{2}}\lim_{z_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0}\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{2}}\dfrac{\tilde{x}}{\beta_{n}z_{\rm max}^{2}}\left(f_{n}\big|_{z_{\rm max}}-f_{n}\big|_{-z_{\rm max}}\right)\hskip 1.0pt.

(151)

Expanding the result using Eq. (
150
), with the coefficients from Eqs. (
148
) and (
145
), then gives us after some simplification:

\displaystyle\lim_{y_{\rm max}\rightarrow 0}\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}\bigg|_{y_{\rm max}}+\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\mathcal{H}f_{n}\big|_{y_{\rm max}}-\dfrac{\textrm{d}f_{n}}{\textrm{d}y}\bigg|_{-y_{\rm max}}-\sqrt{6}\bar{x}\mathcal{H}f_{n}\big|_{-y_{\rm max}}\right)~

\displaystyle=~-3\lambda_{n}(\mathcal{R}_{+}-\mathcal{R}_{-})\mathcal{C}\equiv-2\lambda_{n}\mathcal{D}_{n}\mathcal{C}\hskip 1.0pt.

(152)

In the last step we have introduced
\mathcal{D}_{n}\equiv(3/2)(\mathcal{R}_{+}-\mathcal{R}_{-})
, which is equal to unity for the special case of no dust, expansion/contraction, or recoil – i.e. the special case considered by several earlier authors
(
Dijkstra et al., 2006
;
Tasitsiomi, 2006
;
Lao & Smith, 2020
;
Tomaselli & Ferrara, 2021
;
McClellan et al., 2022
, e.g.)
. More generally,
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\mathcal{D}_{n}(\tilde{x}_{n}.\tilde{\gamma}_{n},\tilde{\epsilon}_{n})
(where we have now restored the subscript
n
for the dimensionless parameters
\tilde{x}_{n}
,
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
, and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
). Equating Eq. (
152
) to the integral over
y
of the right-hand side of Eq. (
139
), solving for
\mathcal{C}
, and then using Eq. (
150
) gives us the following solution:

f_{n}(z)=\dfrac{\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{s},n}\tau_{\rm cl}}{2(\mathcal{P}+n\pi\mathcal{D}_{n})}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt,\hskip 5.0pt\textrm{where }\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)=\begin{cases}P(z)-S(z)\mathcal{R}_{+}~~\textrm{for }z>0\\
P(z)-S(z)\mathcal{R}_{-}~~\textrm{for }z<0\end{cases},

(153)

where, as usual,
\mathcal{P}\equiv\sqrt{6\pi}p_{\rm d}\tau_{\rm cl}/2
, and we have used
\lambda_{n}\simeq n\pi/\tau_{\rm cl}
. In Fig.
20
we plot
\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)
,
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and relevant integrals of
\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)
, for different values of the recoil and continuum absorption parameters
\tilde{x}_{n}
and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
, respectively. The series solution for velocity gradients can be written in closed form, given in Eq. (
18
) in the main text.

A.3
Emergent Ly
\alpha
spectrum

The emergent Ly
\alpha
spectrum at
r=R_{\rm cl}
is
J(x,\tau_{\rm cl})
. Naively, one would predict no escaping emission at all from Eq. (
25
), which would be wrong. The issue has to do with the approximation that the eigenvalues are exactly
\lambda_{n}=n\pi/\tau_{\rm cl}
, which is only an approximate solution of Eq. (
12
). To get the correct result at the cloud edge one could look at the first-order corrections to the eigenvalues, as done by
Dijkstra et al. (2006)
and
Seon & Kim (2020)
. Another approach to get the same result, which we take here, is to use the fact that the boundary condition at the cloud edge yields
J=\sqrt{3}H
(which was used to derive the eigenvalues in the first place). Using
H=-(1/3\mathcal{H})(\partial J/\partial\tau)
(from the Eddington approximation) and
J
from Eq. (
25
) then yields:

\displaystyle J(x,\tau_{\rm cl})

\displaystyle=~-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}\mathcal{H}(x)\tau_{\rm cl}}\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial\bar{\tau}}\bigg|_{\bar{\tau}=1}

\displaystyle\simeq~-\dfrac{\sqrt{18}L_{\rm Ly\alpha}}{(4\pi)^{2}\tau_{\rm cl}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}R_{\rm cl}^{2}\mathcal{H}(x)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})-n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\cos(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})]}{\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}^{3}(n\pi)(\mathcal{P}+n\pi\mathcal{D}_{n})}\hskip 1.0pt(-1)^{n}\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt,

(154)

where we have used
\partial/\partial\bar{\tau}[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau})/\bar{\tau}]=n\pi(-1)^{n}
at
\bar{\tau}=1
. In Fig.
2
we compare predicted spectra using Eq. (
154
) to MCRT results for the same setups using
colt
.

A.4
The Ly
\alpha
escape fraction

The Ly
\alpha
escape fraction
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
from a spherical uniform cloud is given by
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}=4\pi R_{\rm cl}^{2}\Delta\nu_{\rm D}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}x\hskip 1.0ptF(x,\tau_{\rm cl})/L_{\rm Ly\alpha}
, where
F(x,\tau_{\rm cl})=4\pi H(x,\tau_{\rm cl})
is the specific flux at the cloud edge. From the boundary condition at the cloud edge we have
F(x,\tau_{\rm cl})=4\pi J(x,\tau_{\rm cl})/\sqrt{3}
. Thus, with Eq. (
154
) for the emergent spectrum, the Ly
\alpha
escape fraction becomes:

f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}=-\dfrac{\sqrt{6}}{\tau_{\rm cl}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})-n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\cos(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})]}{\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}^{3}(n\pi)(\mathcal{P}+n\pi\mathcal{D}_{n})}(-1)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{\textrm{d}x}{\mathcal{H}(x)}\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt.

(155)

In the general case where recoil and dust are included, it is more convenient to convert the integral into an integral over
z
. Using the wing limit
\mathcal{H}\simeq a_{\rm v}/\sqrt{\pi}x^{2}
, and
\textrm{d}x=\beta_{n}\textrm{d}z
, where
\beta_{n}=(3/2\pi^{3})^{1/6}(a_{\rm v}\tau_{\rm cl})^{1/3}n^{-1/3}
, yields

f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}=-3\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{[\sin(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})-n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}\cos(n\pi\bar{\tau}_{\rm s})]}{\bar{\tau}_{\rm s}^{3}(n\pi)^{2}(\mathcal{P}+n\pi\mathcal{D}_{n})}(-1)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d}z\hskip 1.0ptz^{2}\mathcal{F}_{n}(z)\hskip 1.0pt.

(156)

For a given source distribution in a static cloud, and assuming
p_{\rm d}=0
, the Ly
\alpha
escape fraction
f_{\rm esc,Ly\alpha}
is only a function of the dimensionless variable
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
. In Fig.
3
we plot the predicted Ly
\alpha
escape fraction from dusty clouds using Eq (
156
) and compare it to MCRT results from
colt
.

A.5
Ly
\alpha
radiation hydrodynamics

In this Appendix we give a short derivation of the Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure force on gas. The momentum equation for the gas in the lab frame is given by
(
Mihalas & Auer, 2001
;
Castor, 2004
;
Smith et al., 2017a
, e.g.)
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho\boldsymbol{u})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\rho\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})~

\displaystyle=~-\boldsymbol{\nabla}P-\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\rm LF}-\dfrac{\boldsymbol{u}}{c}\mathcal{G}_{\rm LF}^{0}\,,

(157)

where
\boldsymbol{u}
is the gas velocity,
P
the gas pressure, and
\Phi
the gravitational potential. As for the two last terms on the right,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\rm LF}
and
c\mathcal{G}_{\rm LF}^{0}
are the rates of momentum and energy deposition from radiation, as computed in the lab frame (LF). They are related to the corresponding comoving frame quantities
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathcal{G}^{0}
via a Lorentz transformation
(
Mihalas & Auer, 2001
)
:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{\rm LF}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}+\gamma\dfrac{\boldsymbol{u}}{c}\left(\mathcal{G}^{0}+\dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{u}}{c}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\right)\,,\hskip 10.0pt\mathcal{G}_{\rm LF}^{0}=\gamma\left(\mathcal{G}^{0}+\dfrac{\boldsymbol{u}}{c}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\right)\,,

(158)

where
\gamma\equiv 1/\sqrt{1-\lvert\boldsymbol{u}\rvert^{2}/c^{2}}
is the Lorentz factor. To first order in
\boldsymbol{u}/c
, Eq. (
157
) then simplifies to

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho\boldsymbol{u})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\cdot}(\rho\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})~

\displaystyle=~-\boldsymbol{\nabla}P-\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\,,

(159)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
, the Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure force per unit volume, given by
-4\pi/c
times the right-hand side of Eq. (
129
), integrated over frequency:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}=\dfrac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\,[\alpha+\alpha_{\rm c}(1-\omega g)]\boldsymbol{F}\simeq\dfrac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\,n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}\mathcal{H}\boldsymbol{F}\hskip 1.0pt,

(160)

where in the last step we have used
\alpha\gg\alpha_{\rm c}(1-\omega g)
for the Ly
\alpha
line, as shown in Appendix
A.1
, and
\alpha=n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}\mathcal{H}
. The acceleration due to Ly
\alpha
radiation pressure is simply
\boldsymbol{a}_{\rm Ly\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}/\rho
, or:

\boldsymbol{a}_{\rm Ly\alpha}=\dfrac{1}{c\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\,n_{\rm HI}\sigma_{0}\mathcal{H}\boldsymbol{F}\simeq-\dfrac{4\pi}{3c\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{d}\nu\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}J\,,

(161)

where the last step follows from the Eddington approximation (see Appendix
A.1
).

Appendix B
The cross-section for quenching by collisions with H(1s)

In this Appendix we outline how the cross-section for
\textrm{H}(2s)+\textrm{H}(1s)\rightarrow\textrm{H}(2p)+\textrm{H}(1s)
was calculated. New structure calculations of the adiabatic potential energy curves and non-adiabatic couplings of
{}^{3}\Sigma^{+}_{g}
states associated with the
n=2
and
n=3
asymptotic limits are performed. The multi reference configuration interaction (MRCI) method is used with the basis set described in
Stenrup et al. (2009)
. Molecular orbitals are generated using a state-averaged complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) calculation. The active space both in MRCI and CASSCF contains (7
\sigma_{g}
,3
\pi_{g}
,1
\delta_{g}
,7
\sigma_{u}
,3
\pi_{u}
,1
\delta_{u}
) and 5 states of
{}^{3}\Sigma^{+}_{g}
are computed.

The method used to calculate the
\textrm{H}(2s)+\textrm{H}(1s)\rightarrow\textrm{H}(2p)+\textrm{H}(1s)
cross-section has been described in detail in previous papers
(
Stenrup et al., 2009
;
Hörnquist et al., 2022
, see e.g.)
, and we will therefore only repeat the main steps here. For convenience, atomic units are used in this Appendix. The molecular wave function is expanded using adiabatic electronic states,
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
, as

\displaystyle\Psi(\boldsymbol{R})=\dfrac{1}{R}\sum_{i,l,m}Y_{lm}(\hat{R})F_{il}(R)\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle\hskip 1.0pt,

(162)

where
R
and
\hat{R}
are the radial and angular components of the internuclear coordinate
\boldsymbol{R}
, respectively. The radial nuclear wave functions are denoted by
F_{il}(R)
and the functions
Y_{lm}(\hat{R})
are spherical harmonics, where
l
denotes the orbital angular momentum quantum number. The radial nuclear wave functions are solutions to the adiabatic coupled Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{2\mu}\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}}{\textrm{d}R^{2}}+V^{\rm ad}_{i}(R)+\dfrac{l(l+1)}{2\mu R^{2}}-E\bigg]F_{il}(R)-\dfrac{1}{\mu}\sum_{j}\bigg[2f_{ij}(R)\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}R}+g_{ij}(R)\bigg]F_{jl}(R)=0\hskip 1.0pt,

(163)

where
\mu
is the reduced mass of the nuclei, and where

\displaystyle f_{ij}(R)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{i}\rvert\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}R}\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle\hskip 1.0pt,

(164)

\displaystyle g_{ij}(R)

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{i}\rvert\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}}{\textrm{d}R^{2}}\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle\hskip 1.0pt,

(165)

are the first and second derivative non-adiabatic coupling elements, respectively. We have here neglected the angular components of the non-adiabatic couplings.

The adiabatic coupled Schrödinger equation is then transformed to a strict diabatic representation. The adiabatic-to-diabatic transformation matrix is obtained by numerically solving the equation
(
Mead & Truhlar, 1982
)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}R}\textbf{{T}}+\textbf{{f}}\textbf{{T}}=\textbf{{0}}\hskip 1.0pt,

(166)

where
f
is the anti-symmetric matrix containing the first derivative non-adiabatic coupling elements. The non-adiabatic couplings are assumed to be zero at large internuclear distances, and the adiabatic-to-diabatic transformation matrix is therefore equal to the identity matrix asymptotically. The diabatic potential matrix is obtained by the similarity transformation
\textbf{{V}}^{\rm d}=\textbf{{T}}^{T}\textbf{{V}}^{\rm ad}\textbf{{T}}
. The diabatic nuclear wave functions, which we denote by
\tilde{F}_{il}(R)
, are solutions to the diabatic coupled Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2\mu}\dfrac{\textrm{d}^{2}}{\textrm{d}R^{2}}+\dfrac{l(l+1)}{2\mu R^{2}}-E\bigg)\tilde{F}_{il}(R)+\sum_{j}V_{ij}^{\rm d}(R)\tilde{F}_{jl}(R)=0\hskip 1.0pt.

(167)

This equation is solved numerically by using Johnson’s log-derivative method
(
Johnson, 1973
)
, in which the equation is transformed into the matrix Riccati equation for the logarithmic derivative of the nuclear wave function. The matrix Riccati equation is then numerically integrated. From the logarithmic derivative, the scattering matrix
S_{ij,l}
can be extracted by matching the solution with the appropriate boundary conditions of the open or closed covalent or ionic channels. The cross-section going from state
j
to state
i
is then calculated from the open-open partition of the scattering matrix by

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(E)=\frac{\pi}{k_{j}^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)|S_{ij,l}-\delta_{ij}|^{2}\hskip 1.0pt.

(168)

For each partial wave, the matrix Riccati equation is numerically integrated from
0.6\hskip 1.0pta_{0}
to
50\hskip 1.0pta_{0}
using an integration step size of
0.005\hskip 1.0pta_{0}
. The total cross-section is obtained by summing the contributions from the partial waves. When the ratios of the partial cross-sections and the accumulated cross-sections remain less than
10^{-5}
for 25 terms in succession, the summation of Eq. (
168
) is terminated.

In the present formulation, we use a molecular description of the electronic states. The excited states correlating with
n\geq 2
are degenerate in the asymptotic region and are linear combinations of atomic states. In order to know which potential energy curve that corresponds to
\mathrm{H}(1s)+\mathrm{H}(2s)
or
\mathrm{H}(1s)+\mathrm{H}(2p)
in the asymptotic region, these linear combinations need to be determined. This can be achieved by using the method proposed by
Belyaev (2015)
. The molecular electronic wave function is thus written as a linear combination of the atomic states

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm mol}=\textbf{{B}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{\rm at}\hskip 1.0pt,

(169)

where
B
is an orthogonal matrix. In order to determine the matrix
B
, one can utilize the
ab initio
calculated first derivative non-adiabatic couplings. It can be shown
(
Grosser et al., 1999
;
Belyaev et al., 2001
, see e.g.)
that the following relation holds for the asymptotic values of the non-adiabatic couplings written in an atomic basis:

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{\rm at}_{i}\rvert\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}R}\lvert\phi^{\rm at}_{j}\rangle_{\infty}=\gamma[V_{i}(\infty)-V_{j}(\infty)]\langle\phi^{\rm at}_{i}\rvert z\lvert\phi^{\rm at}_{j}\rangle\hskip 1.0pt,

(170)

where
\langle\phi^{\rm at}_{i}\rvert z\lvert\phi^{\rm at}_{j}\rangle
are atomic transition dipole moment that can be calculated analytically using the eigenstates of atomic hydrogen. In the present case,
\gamma=1/2
. The
B
matrix is parameterized by blocks of
n\times n
orthogonal matrices, which is possible since the molecular states are linear combinations of atomic states with the same
n
. In the present case, we neglect any mixing between the
n=4
states. Therefore, we parameterize the
B
matrix using block matrices composed of
2\times 2
and
3\times 3
rotation matrices while the rest of the
B
matrix contains ones along the diagonal and zeroes otherwise. By using Eq. (
169
), one can show that

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}^{\rm mol}_{ij}=\sum_{k,l}B_{ik}\overline{\tau}^{\rm at}_{kl}B_{jl}\hskip 1.0pt,

(171)

where
\overline{\tau}^{\rm mol}_{ij}
are the asymptotic values of the non-adiabatic couplings in a molecular basis and
\overline{\tau}^{\rm at}_{ij}
the same quantities expressed in an atomic basis. This set of equations can be further simplified by using the selection rules for the transition dipole moments. To obtain the relevant elements of the
B
matrix, the set of equations was minimized using least squares. The optimization was performed separately for each electronic symmetry. For the
2\times 2
block of
B
, which is written as

\displaystyle\textbf{{B}}_{2\times 2}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\beta\\
\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\hskip 1.0pt,

(172)

the values obtained were
\alpha=0.9992
and
\beta=0.0410
for
{}^{1}\Sigma^{+}_{g}
,
\alpha=-0.1122
and
\beta=-0.9937
for
{}^{1}\Sigma^{+}_{u}
and
\alpha=-0.0022
and
\beta=0.9999
for
{}^{3}\Sigma^{+}_{g}
. A comparison of the
ab initio
calculated non-adiabatic couplings of
{}^{3}\Sigma^{+}_{u}
symmetry with the couplings calculated using Eq. (
170
) shows that there is no mixing among the
n=2
states in that symmetry and the corresponding
B
matrix will therefore be equal to the identity matrix. Once the
B
matrix has been determined, the cross-section can be calculated in the usual way using the transformed scattering matrix
\overline{\textbf{{S}}}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\textbf{{S}}}=\textbf{{B}}^{T}\textbf{{S}}\textbf{{B}}\hskip 1.0pt.

(173)

However, since the mixing between the
n=2
states is weak (i.e. they correspond almost entirely to either
2s
or
2p
), we approximate the
S
matrix by neglecting the smallest component of
\textbf{{B}}_{2\times 2}
for each symmetry, which should be sufficient for the present purpose.
