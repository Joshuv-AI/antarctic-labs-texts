Approximate control of parabolic equations with on-off shape controls by Fenchel duality
Camille Pouchol, Emmanuel Trélat, Christophe Zhang

Abstract
We consider the internal control of linear parabolic equations through on-off shape controls, i.e. , controls of the form M ⁡ ( t ) ​ χ ω ⁡ ( t ) \displaystyle M(t)\chi_{\omega(t)} with M ⁡ ( t ) ≥ 0 \displaystyle M(t)\geq 0 and ω ⁡ ( t ) \displaystyle\omega(t) with a prescribed maximal measure. We establish small-time approximate controllability towards all possible final states allowed by the comparison principle with nonnegative controls. We manage to build controls with constant amplitude M ⁡ ( t ) ≡ M ¯ \displaystyle M(t)\equiv\overline{M} . In contrast, if the moving control set ω ⁡ ( t ) \displaystyle\omega(t) is confined to evolve in some region of the whole domain, we prove that approximate controllability fails to hold for small times. The method of proof is constructive. Using Fenchel-Rockafellar duality and the bathtub principle, the on-off shape control is obtained as the bang-bang solution of an optimal control problem, which we design by relaxing the constraints. Our optimal control approach is outlined in a rather general form for linear constrained control problems, paving the way for generalisations and applications to other PDEs and constraints.

Main results

As our results require different sets of hypotheses and in order to give a quick glance at the main ideas, we first present them in the simplified context of the heat equation (
1

Given a constraint set
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}\subset\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\,u\geq 0\}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}
such that the corresponding solution to (

On-off shape control.

For our first main result, we focus on nonnegative approximate controllability with on-off shape controls: for a fixed
\displaystyle L\in(0,1)
, we consider the constraint set

\mathcal{U}_{L}^{\mathrm{shape}}:=\{M\chi_{\omega},\quad\omega\subset\Omega,\quad|\omega|\leq L|\Omega|,\,M>0\}\subset L^{2}(\Omega).

Within the above class of on-off shape controls, we establish nonnegative approximate controllability in arbitrary time (see Theorem
3.1

\displaystyle L\in(0,1)

Theorem A
.

For any
\displaystyle L\in(0,1)
,
\displaystyle T>0
, system (
1

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{shape}}^{L}
in time
\displaystyle T

To establish this result, we draw from the Lions strategy in
[
26
]
, which develops a constructive approach in studying the approximate controllability of a linear wave equation.
The idea is to consider the requirement
\displaystyle\|y(T)-y_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon
as a constraint. With
\displaystyle L_{T}u:=\textstyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{(T-t)\Delta}u(t)\,dt
and since
\displaystyle y(T)=L_{T}u+e^{T\Delta}y_{0}
, Lions considers the constrained optimal control problem

\pi:=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{2},\ \|e^{T\Delta}y_{0}+L_{T}u-y_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

The infimum satisfies
\displaystyle\pi<+\infty
if and only if there exists
\displaystyle u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
steering
\displaystyle y_{0}
to a closed
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-ball around
\displaystyle y_{f}
.
To find minimisers,
i.e.
, to build controls, note that

\pi=\inf_{u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{2}+G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u)=\displaystyle\inf_{u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}F_{T}(u)+G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u),

with
\displaystyle F_{T}(u)=\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{2}
and

G_{T,\varepsilon}(y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 0\quad&\text{if}\quad\|e^{T\Delta}y_{0}+y-y_{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\varepsilon,\\
+\infty\quad&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

From this optimisation problem, one computes its Fenchel dual optimisation problem, which reads

d:=-\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})+G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}(-p_{f})=-\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\|L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{2}+G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}(-p_{f}),

where
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
(
\displaystyle=F_{T}
) and
\displaystyle G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}
are the Fenchel conjugates of
\displaystyle F_{T}
and
\displaystyle G_{T,\varepsilon}
, respectively, and
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}
is the adjoint of the linear bounded operator
\displaystyle L_{T}:L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
. Recall that for a given
\displaystyle p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle p=L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
is the solution to the adjoint equation ending at
\displaystyle p_{f}
,
i.e.
, it solves

\left\{\begin{aligned} p_{t}+\Delta p&=0,\\
p=0\ &\textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\\
p(T)=p_{f}\ &\textrm{on}\ \Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

Under suitable conditions, the Fenchel-Rockafellar theorem
[
42
]
ensures that
\displaystyle\pi=d
.
As a result, one can then study the dual functional to establish that
\displaystyle\pi=d<+\infty
, and that its minimum is attained. Typically, one proves that it is coercive, as a consequence of a unique continuation property. Furthermore, the cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
is differentiable in this case and the first order optimality condition for the (unique) variable
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
minimising the dual functional then reads
\displaystyle L_{T}L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}^{\star}=y_{f}-e^{T\Delta}y_{0}-\varepsilon\textstyle\frac{p_{f}^{\star}}{\|p_{f}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}
. The optimal control
\displaystyle u^{\star}:=L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}^{\star}
is thus constructed from the minimiser of the dual problem
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
.

Accordingly, in this paper we reframe constrained approximate controllability as an optimal control problem, replacing
\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)}^{2}
of
[
26
]
with a suitable cost functional
\displaystyle F_{T}
. This constitutes a novel generalisation of the Lions method.

One can choose between two different sufficient conditions for the equality
\displaystyle\pi=d
to hold. One regards the primal problem, and the other the dual problem. Importantly, they are not symmetric (although the primal and dual problems are). These hypotheses when used on the primal problem are useless when it comes to proving controllability: they amount to assuming that controllability holds. We here crucially use these hypotheses in terms of the dual problem, see subsection
2.2
and Appendix
A.4

As the detailed statements in Theorem

and Proposition
3.8

•

Instead of using the

•

1.4
Proof strategy and related works

In the unconstrained case, approximate controllability of the heat equation is a consequence of the unique continuation property, thanks to a general property of linear control problems (see for example
[
13
, Section 2.3]
).
In the case of heat equations, the latter property can be obtained by the Holmgren Uniqueness Theorem
[
3
]
.
In contrast to these existence results, the variational approach developed in
[
26
]
(see Section
1.2
), handles approximate controllability in a
constructive manner
.

Our strategy consists in extending this approach to the constrained case: the main idea is to find a suitable cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
such that optimal controls must satisfy the constraint
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}_{L}^{\mathrm{shape}}
. A remarkable feature of our strategy lies in how we design the cost function: we do so by building an adequate Fenchel dual function, instead of trying to find the cost function directly.

Constrained controllability.

Constrained control problems in infinite dimension have been studied in papers such as
[
20
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
16
]
.
In
[
16
]
, sufficient conditions (in the form of unique continuation properties) for controllability results are derived when the control and states are constrained to some prescribed subspaces, but at the expense of controlling only a finite-dimensional subpart of the final state.
In
[
20
]
, the authors deal with a form of approximate controllability of the heat equation akin to ours, focusing on minimal time problems. They derive bang-bang type necessary optimality conditions for minimal time controls, and then build such controls using an auxiliary optimisation problem.

The papers
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
address constrained exact controllability through modified observability inequalities, thus giving abstract necessary and/or sufficient conditions. One key difference with our work is that constraint sets are assumed to be convex. In fact, all examples handled by
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
feature isotropic constraints, that is, constraints that are symmetrical with respect to
\displaystyle 0
, or more generally, are expressed using radial functions (such as norms). This precludes, for instance, any type of positivity constraint.

It is noteworthy that all the above references introduce so-called dual functionals, drawing from the variational formulation of the Hilbert Uniqueness Method. However, the formalism of Fenchel-Rockafellar duality in itself, as developed in
[
26
]
, has increasingly been abandoned in the literature. Some notable exceptions are
[
46
]
in the context of stabilisation and
[
23
]
for parameterised problems, both in the unconstrained case. To some extent, the work
[
5
]
uses Fenchel duality to study (constrained) null-controllability in some specific settings.

We fully exploit the ideas hinted at in the latter paper by choosing a different type of functional, which allows us to handle anisotropic, non-convex constraints.
In contrast with the aforementioned trend in the literature, we work with Fenchel duality, but in a rather unusual way, in that we will focus mainly on the dual problem. The nature of the actual primal problem (optimal control problem) being solved follows effortlessly.
To perform the necessary computations, we will make extensive use of convex analysis. Doing so bypasses many technical difficulties thanks to properties of subdifferentials and Fenchel conjugates, among others, and allows for the use of costs which are not differentiable but still smooth in the convex analytic sense.

Bathtub principle for appropriate costs.

The second main idea is what underlies our choice of cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
, forcing optimal controls to satisfy the required the on-off shape constraint.
As the set of on-off shape controls is a non-convex cone, we are led to relaxation,
i.e.
, to consider the closure of its convex hull. In order to build relevant costs, we then rely on the so-called bathtub principle (actually, a relaxed version of it)
[
25
]
.

For a given function
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the latter principle solves

\sup_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx,\qquad\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;0\leq u\leq 1\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}u\leq L|\Omega|\right\}.

This optimisation problem comes up naturally in some control problems similar to ours
[
22
,
30
]
, or in shape optimisation problems
[
40
]
.

Interpreting the bathtub principle as a Fenchel conjugate leads us to design the unusual cost functional (
4
).
This allows us to design dual problems such that optimal controls exist, and are characterised as maximisers of some bathtub principle. Then, using analyticity properties for solutions of the dual problem, we prove their uniqueness and hence their extremality, thereby uncovering that they are on-off shape controls.

Bang-bang property of optimal controls.

Bang-bang controls (
i.e.
, controls that saturate their constraints) are a common feature in time optimal control problems. A growing literature on the heat equation alone
[
45
,
33
,
31
,
47
,
49
]
shows that this property extends well to some infinite-dimensional systems. In our case, we will see that the on-off shape controls we have constructed can be understood as time-optimal controls. As these controls are bang-bang, this yields another occurrence of the bang-bang property in the time-optimal control of the heat equation.

Note, however, that in the references cited above, the controls are constrained to lie in balls of specific function spaces, whereas we consider non-negative constraints on the controls, which is an anisotropic constraint. Moreover, the bang-bang property is usually established separately using optimality conditions, having established controllability at the onset. In our case, the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality approach allows to do all those things simultaneously.

1.5
Extensions and perspectives

Operator, boundary conditions.

The (
GUC
) property and analytic-hypoellipticity are two key sufficient properties for nonnegative approximate controllability by on-off shape controls. We have highlighted second-order elliptic operators with analytic coefficients Dirichlet boundary conditions as an example. Our results apply to such operators with Robin boundary conditions of the form
\displaystyle a(x)y+b(x)\partial_{\nu}y=0
over
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
(with
\displaystyle a,b
analytic) as soon as the function
\displaystyle a
does not vanish on the whole of
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
(more generally, as soon as
\displaystyle a
is nontrivial on any connected component of
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
).
This excludes the important case of Neumann boundary conditions, which remains open.

Our approach also accommodates subelliptic operators. This includes a large class of Hörmander operators,
i.e.
, operators of the form
\displaystyle A=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}+X_{0}+V\mathrm{Id}
with vector fields
\displaystyle X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
generating a Lie algebra that equals
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
on the whole of
\displaystyle\Omega
. Under general regularity assumptions and boundary conditions, such an operator and its adjoint generate a strongly continuous semigroup on
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
, satisfy the comparison principle
[
7
]
, all three conditions sufficient for the (
GUC
) property, and are analytic-hypoelliptic for instance if the characteristic manifold is an analytic symplectic manifold (see
[
34
]
).

Finally, going beyond the linear setting is a completely open problem, since our approach fundamentally relies on the Fenchel-Rockafellar theorem which itself requires a bounded linear operator (the role played by
\displaystyle L_{T}
in our setting).

Control operator.

Our results have been stated with the identity control operator.
They extend to the nonnegative control of

\left\{\begin{aligned} y_{t}-Ay&=\varphi u,\\
y(0)=&y_{0}\ \textrm{in}\ \Omega\end{aligned}\right.

where
\displaystyle\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is positive, analytic.

An interesting perspective is to follow our proof strategy with boundary control operators, where on-off shape controls now refer to characteristic functions over the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
.

Other notions of controllability.

In the case of unconstrained controllability with a control acting in some fixed subset
\displaystyle\omega
, any function that can be reached exactly is (at least) analytic in
\displaystyle\Omega\setminus\omega
, preventing exact controllability to hold true.

On the one hand, this argument for (non)-exact controllability by on-off shape controls fails since the control may act everywhere. On the other hand, our approach heavily relies on targeting a ball
\displaystyle\overline{B}(y_{f},\varepsilon)
with
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. As a result, exact nonnegative controllability by on-off shape controls is an open and seemingly difficult question.

A related matter is that of the cost of approximate controllability as a function of
\displaystyle\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Although our focus has been on
\displaystyle L^{2}
-approximate controllability, we mention that one may extend the same methodology to
\displaystyle L^{p}
-approximate controllability for
\displaystyle 1<p<+\infty
, by working in duality within the appropriate spaces:
the bounded operator underlying the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality is now
\displaystyle L_{T}\in L(L^{2}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega)),L^{p}(\Omega))
, meaning that the dual functional is defined on
\displaystyle L^{q}(\Omega)
with
\displaystyle q
the dual exponent to
\displaystyle p
.

Controllability in large time.

As evidenced by Theorem B, we provide obstructions for small times
\displaystyle T
. We do not know whether nonnegative approximate controllability holds for sufficiently large times.

Abstract constrained control.

The strategy of proof developed in this article hints at generalisations, where the method is applied to abstract linear control problems with abstract constraint sets
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
.

In particular, we expect it to lead to necessary and sufficient conditions for controllability when
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
is convex. When
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
is not convex as is the case for on-off shape controls, this requires to study the convex hull of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
, following the relaxation approach. This abstract setting should allow us to discern how one can design a cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
, analogous to (
4
), tailored to a given
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
.

Further sufficient conditions should be derived to ensure that optimal controls in the convex hull of
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
actually are in the original constraint set
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
. In the present work, analytic-hypoellipticity and the (
GUC
) property play that role in the case of on-off shape controls.

This will be the subject of an ulterior article.

Regularity of the sets
\displaystyle\omega(t)
.

Another problem is to analyse the complexity of the sets
\displaystyle\omega(t)
occupied by optimal controls over time. For instance, how smooth (
\displaystyle BV
regularity, number of connected components, etc) are the sets
\displaystyle\omega(t)
achieving approximate controllability?

In view of applications, these are important issues for the controls to be implementable in practice. For example, if the sets
\displaystyle\omega(t)
are constrained to depend on a few parameters restricting their geometry, or if they are restricted to rigid movements, controllability is a totally open question.

Homegenisation approach.

We acknowledge that an homogenisation approach to establishing nonnegative approximate controllability by on-off shape controls could certainly be pursued. The underlying idea would be to "atomise" the sets
\displaystyle\omega(t)
(see
[
2
]
).
Contrarily to our technique, however, this approach would not be constructive.

Numerical approximation of optimal controls.

Optimal controls are given explicitly in terms of optimisers of the dual problem: the constructive nature of our approach means that optimal controls may be numerically computed, at least on paper.

Providing reliable and efficient methods to compute optimal controls is a difficult issue which has been studied in the case of Lions’ cost functional with
\displaystyle\varepsilon=0
(
i.e.
, exact controllability)
[
21
,
8
]
. Similar results in a generalised setting with our Fenchel-Rockafellar-based approach would be valuable.

Contrary to Lions’ cost functional, we note that ad hoc algorithms are required in order to cope with functions that are not necessarily differentiable, as is the case in the present paper. Recent primal-dual algorithms designed for optimisation problems with objective functions of the form
\displaystyle F(u)+G(L_{T}u)
are likely to be good candidates
[
11
]
.

Outline of the paper.

First, Section
2
lays out the convex analytic framework, that of Fenchel-Rockafellar duality, and how it may be applied to constrained approximate controllability. We then introduce the bathtub principle and interpret it in terms of Fenchel conjugation in order to design a relevant optimal control problem for our purposes.
Section
3
is dedicated to the proof of our nonnegative approximate controllability result given by Theorem
3.1
, and Section
4
to that of the obstruction result, Theorem
4.1
.
Finally, Section
5
gathers our results about further obstructions when the control amplitude is bounded, along with our analysis of the corresponding minimal time control problem.

2
Building the optimal control problem

2.1
Convex analytic framework

Let
\displaystyle H
be a Hilbert space. We let
\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(H)
be the set of functions from
\displaystyle H
to
\displaystyle\mathopen{]}-\infty,+\infty]
that are convex, lower semicontinuous (abbreviated lsc) and proper (
i.e.
, not identically
\displaystyle+\infty
).
For
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
, we let

\mathrm{dom}(f)=\{x\in H,\,f(x)<+\infty\}

be its
domain
.

Fenchel conjugate.

For a proper function
\displaystyle f:H\rightarrow\mathopen{]}-\infty,+\infty]
, we denote
\displaystyle f^{\ast}:H\rightarrow\mathopen{]}-\infty,+\infty]
its convex conjugate, given by the convex lsc function

f^{\ast}(y):=\sup_{x\in H}\;\big(\langle y,x\rangle-f(x)\big),\quad\forall y\in H.

Support and indicator functions.

Given a subset
\displaystyle C\subset H
, the
indicator function
of
\displaystyle C
is the function defined by

\delta_{C}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in C\\
+\infty&\text{if }x\notin C\end{cases}\;,\quad\forall x\in H,

and the
support function
of
\displaystyle C
is defined by

\sigma_{C}(p):=\sup_{x\in C}\langle p,x\rangle=\delta_{C}^{\ast}(p),\quad\forall p\in H,

i.e.
, the Fenchel conjugate function of the indicator function of
\displaystyle C
.

Subdifferentials.

For
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
, we let

\partial f(x):=\{p\in H,\,\forall y\in H,f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle\},

be its subdifferential at a point
\displaystyle x\in H
.

Various common properties of Fenchel conjugates, support functions and subdifferentials are used throughout the article. These are all recalled in Appendix
A
, where a few additional lemmas are proved.

2.2
Approximate controllability by Fenchel duality (
[
26
]
)

Let us explain how the approximate controllability problem is reformulated in the context of Fenchel-Rockafellar duality
[
42
]
(see
A.4
for a general presentation), following the strategy introduced by Lions in
[
26
]
. We work with the control problem (
5
), with the control space
\displaystyle E:=L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and the state space
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
.

By Duhamel’s formula
\displaystyle y(T)=S_{T}y_{0}+L_{T}u
, the inclusion
\displaystyle y(T)\in\overline{B}(y_{f},\varepsilon)
(where the closed ball of center
\displaystyle y_{f}
and radius
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is with respect to the
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm) can equivalently be written as
\displaystyle L_{T}u\in\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
.

Given some cost functional
\displaystyle F_{T}:E\rightarrow[0,+\infty]\in\Gamma_{0}(E)
, consider the optimal control problem (which we will refer to as the
primal
problem)

\pi:=\inf_{u\in E}F_{T}(u)+G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u).

where

G_{T,\varepsilon}:=\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}\in\Gamma_{0}(L^{2}(\Omega)).

Now consider the Fenchel
dual
to the above problem, which writes

d=-\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f}),\quad J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f}):=F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})+G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}(-p_{f}).

(8)

Thanks to the formulae for conjugates, we find

G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}(z)=\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},z\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|z\|_{L^{2}},

leading to

J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})=F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})-\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},p_{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}.

We recall that
\displaystyle p=L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
solves the adjoint equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} p_{t}+A^{\ast}p&=0,\\
p(T)=p_{f}\ &\textrm{in}\ \Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

Strong duality.

The weak duality
\displaystyle\pi\geq d
always holds. According to the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality theorem recalled in Appendix
A.4
, the existence of
\displaystyle p_{f}\in\mathrm{dom}(G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast})
such that
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
is a sufficient condition for the strong duality
\displaystyle\pi=d
to hold. Since
\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast})=L^{2}(\Omega)
, this condition reduces to the existence of a point of continuity of the form
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
for
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
.
In the cases covered here, we shall check that the chosen
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle 0
. When strong duality holds, it is therefore equivalent to work with the dual problem, which is easier to handle especially when it has full domain,
i.e.
, its objective function is finite everywhere.

We note that an alternative to establish strong duality is to find
\displaystyle u\in E
such that
\displaystyle G
is continuous at
\displaystyle L_{T}u
and
\displaystyle F(u)<+\infty
. This approach is bound to fail here since it would require finding a control achieving the target ball,
i.e.
, assuming that controllability holds.

Non-trivial strong duality.

Furthermore, the primal value
\displaystyle\pi
is attained if finite,
i.e.
, if this equality is not the trivial
\displaystyle+\infty=+\infty
(the uncontrollable case). Thus, if
\displaystyle d
is finite,
\displaystyle\pi
is finite as well and attained: we may speak of optimal controls.

This requirement that
\displaystyle d
be finite is by far the subtlest one. It may be tackled by proving that the functional
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
underlying the dual problem (written in infimum form
\displaystyle\textstyle\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})
) has a minimum. In practice, we will always find this to be the case, as the dual problem is usually unconstrained (depending on the choice of
\displaystyle F_{T}
), unlike the primal problem.
Hence, both
\displaystyle\pi
and
\displaystyle d
will be attained and, from Proposition
A.8
, any optimal dual variable
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
is such that any optimal control
\displaystyle u^{\star}
satisfies

u^{\star}\in\partial F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}^{\star}).

(10)

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that, for any
\displaystyle y_{0},y_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle y_{f}\geq S_{T}y_{0}
and any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
,

•

there exists
\displaystyle p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
,

•

\displaystyle d\neq+\infty
.

If for any dual optimal variable
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
, the controls characterised by (
10
) are in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}
, then the control system (
5
) is nonnegatively approximately controllable with controls in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{+}
in time
\displaystyle T
.

This shows how the choice of the cost
\displaystyle F_{T}
impacts the existence and properties of optimal controls. More precisely, it must be pointed out that all the hypotheses of Proposition
2.1
are formulated with respect to the dual problem. Accordingly, from the next section onwards, our strategy will be to determine an adequate optimal control problem by designing its dual problem.

Finally, we emphasise that (
10
) is only a necessary condition for the optimality of
\displaystyle u^{\star}
. It becomes sufficient only when
\displaystyle\partial F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}^{\star})
is reduced to a singleton, which will occur in our case.

2.3
Convex analytic interpretation of the bathtub principle

Starting from the set of on-off shape controls of amplitude
\displaystyle 1
,

\mathcal{U}_{L}:=\{\chi_{\omega},\quad\omega\subset\Omega,\quad|\omega|\leq L|\Omega|\},

(11)

where
\displaystyle|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure, we define the closure of its convex hull (which is also its weak-
\displaystyle\ast
closure for the
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega)
-topology)

\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;0\leq u\leq 1\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}u\leq L|\Omega|\right\}.

(12)

Given a fixed
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we consider the (static) maximisation problem

\sup_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx.

(13)

This a relaxed version of the so-called bathtub principle, which gives the maximum value as well as a characterisation of maximisers
[
25
]
. For the sake of readability, we introduce the necessary results for what follows, but refer to Appendix
B
for a more detailed statement.
For a given
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we let

\Phi_{v}(r):=\left|\{v>r\}\right|.

(14)

and its pseudo-inverse function

\Phi^{-1}_{v}(s):=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\Phi_{v}(r)\leq s\right\}=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{|\{v>r\}|\leq s\right\}.

(15)

Finally, we set

h(v):=\max(0,\Phi^{-1}_{v}(L|\Omega|)).

(16)

Remark 2.2
.

The function
\displaystyle\Phi_{v}^{-1}
is the Schwarz rearrangement of
\displaystyle v
, see
[
19
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(relaxed bathtub principle)
.

Let
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. The maximum in (
13
) equals

\int_{0}^{\min(\Phi_{v}(0),L|\Omega|)}\Phi_{v}^{-1}.

Furthermore, if all the level sets of the function
\displaystyle v
have measure zero, the maximum equals
\displaystyle\textstyle\int_{\{v>h(v)\}}v
and is uniquely attained by

u^{\star}:=\chi_{\{v>h(v)\}},

We refer to Lemma
B.2
for the comprehensive statement of the relaxed bathtub principle.
We may interpret the above results as a formula for the support function of
\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
:

\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)=\sup_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\left(\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}-\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(u)\right))=\sup_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\min(\Phi_{v}(0),L|\Omega|)}\Phi_{v}^{-1}.

(17)

First, using the characterisation of the subdifferential given in Appendix
A
, we arrive at the following characterisation for the solutions to the maximisation problem given in Lemma
2.3
:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. The maximisers of the relaxed bathtub problem are given by the elements of
\displaystyle\partial\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)
.

Proof.

Using
\displaystyle(\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}})^{\ast}=\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}
along with (
46
) given in Appendix
A.1
, we have, for
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

\argmax_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}=\argmax_{u\in L^{2}}\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}-\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(u)=\left\{u\in L^{2},\quad\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}-\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(u)=\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)\right\}=\partial\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

Proposition
2.4
implies that for any maximiser
\displaystyle u
of the relaxed bathtub problem,

v\in\partial\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(u).

Proposition
A.4
in Appendix
A.2
shows that this implies
\displaystyle u\in\partial\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
. Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
characterise exactly which elements of the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
are involved.

2.4
From the static bathtub principle to the dual problem and its corresponding cost

Following Section
2.2
and
recalling Proposition
2.1
and (
10
), we are looking for a cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
such that the corresponding optimal controls are on-off shape controls, and we have established that it suffices to find a conjugate functional
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
satisfying two key properties. First, if there exists
\displaystyle p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}
, and if we can provide the existence of a minimiser
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
of
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
, then
\displaystyle\pi
is attained and there exists at least one optimal control. Second, any optimal control
\displaystyle u^{\star}
should satisfy (
10
)
so
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
should be chosen so that the subdifferential
\displaystyle\partial F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}^{\star})
contains only characteristic functions. Given Proposition
A.4
and Section
2.3
, elements of

\partial\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v),\quad v\in L^{2}(\Omega)

are bang-bang, in the sense that they are characteristic functions, under some mild conditions that
\displaystyle v
must satisfy.

To go from the static optimisation problem to the adequate dual problem, we add a time dependency. Moreover, to ensure coercivity of the dual problem, we add a quadratic exponent. All in all, we choose the following conjugate:

F_{T}^{\ast}(p):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(p(t))\,dt\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{\min(\Phi_{p(t)}(0),L|\Omega|)}\Phi_{p(t)}^{-1}(s)\,ds\,dt\right)^{2},\quad\forall p\in E.

(18)

Since the approximate controllability problem corresponds to
\displaystyle G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}:=\sigma_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}
, this defines a dual problem of the form (
8
).
As pointed out in Section
2.2
, we are now dealing with an unconstrained optimisation problem (
i.e.
, the domain of the functions involved is the whole space
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega
)).

We can now derive the corresponding constrained optimisation problem, by computing the actual cost
\displaystyle F_{T}
associated to the choice (
18
) for
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
. We find, as announced by (
4
) in the introduction:

Lemma 2.6
.

The function
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
defined by (
18
) satisfies
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\star}\in\Gamma_{0}(E)
. Defining

\mathcal{M}(u):=\max\left(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}},\frac{\|u\|_{L^{1}}}{L|\Omega|}\right),\quad\forall u\in L^{2}(\Omega),

its Fenchel conjugate
\displaystyle(F_{T}^{\ast})^{\ast}=F_{T}
is given for
\displaystyle u\in E
by

F_{T}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{M}^{2}(u(t,\cdot))\bigg)+\delta_{\{u\geq 0\}}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max\Big(\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}},\frac{\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}}{L|\Omega|}\Big)^{2}\bigg)+\delta_{\{u\geq 0\}}(u).

Proof.

Lemma
A.5
in Appendix
A.3
shows that
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}\in\Gamma_{0}(E)
.
We proceed by computing
\displaystyle(F_{T}^{\ast})^{\ast}
. We have
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}H^{2}
, with
\displaystyle H(p):=\textstyle\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(p(t,\cdot))\,dt
. Since
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\in\Gamma_{0}(L^{2}(\Omega))
, the definition of the support function together with Lemma
A.5
in Appendix show that
\displaystyle H\in\Gamma_{0}(E)
with

H^{\ast}(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}^{\ast}(u(t,\cdot))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(u(t,\cdot))\,dt.

Furthermore, we find the conjugate of
\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{2}H^{2}
by using (
45
) in Appendix
A.1
, which leads to

\left(\frac{1}{2}H^{2}\right)^{\ast}(u)=\min_{\alpha>0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}+\alpha H^{\ast}\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)\right),

where we used that
\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(H)=E
.
Clearly,

H^{\ast}\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)=0\quad\text{if}\quad u\geq 0\;\text{ and }\;\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{M}(u(t,\cdot))\leq\alpha,

and is
\displaystyle+\infty
otherwise.

We end up with

\left(\frac{1}{2}H^{2}\right)^{\ast}(u)=\min_{\alpha>0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}+\delta_{\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{M}(u(t,\cdot))\leq\alpha\right\}}(u)\right)+\delta_{\{u\geq 0\}}(u)=F_{T}(u).

The lemma is proved.
∎

Note that
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is
(positively)-homogeneous of degree
\displaystyle 2
. Indeed,
\displaystyle v\mapsto\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}
is positively-homogeneous of degree
\displaystyle 1
,
i.e.
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(\lambda v)=\lambda\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)
for all
\displaystyle\lambda>0
,
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We end this subsection by establishing a crucial property satisfied by
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
. It will play a crucial role in proving that the dual functional is coercive, akin to that of the unique continuation property in the Lions strategy described in Section
1.2
.

Lemma 2.7
.

For all
\displaystyle p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, if
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})=0
, then
\displaystyle p_{f}\leq 0
.

Proof.

It is easily seen that
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\geq 0
, and, for
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)>0
as soon as
\displaystyle v>0
on a set of positive measure, by taking the scalar product of
\displaystyle v
against a well chosen element of
\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
. Consequently, if
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)=0
, then
\displaystyle v\leq 0
.

Now recall that

F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}(t,\cdot))\,dt\right)^{2}.

If
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})=0
, then, as
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\geq 0
, for almost every
\displaystyle t\in(0,T)
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})=0
. Thus,
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}(t,\cdot)\leq 0
. In particular, since
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, this implies that
\displaystyle p_{f}\leq 0.

∎

3
Approximate controllability results

In this section, we state and prove our main result on approximate controllability. The full statement for our Theorem
A
is given with more details below, for general linear operators, satisfying the properties given in Section
1.3
.

We are considering the following optimal control problem:

\pi=\inf_{u\in E}F_{T}(u)+G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u)=\inf_{u\in E}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max\left(\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}},\frac{\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}}}{L|\Omega|}\right)^{2}+\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(L_{T}u)\right\},

(19)

whose dual problem is

d=-\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})=-\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f}(t))\,dt\right)^{2}-\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},p_{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\right\}.

(20)

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
satisfies the (
GUC
) property and that
\displaystyle\partial_{t}-A^{\ast}
is analytic-hypoelliptic. Then for the cost function
\displaystyle F_{T}
defined by (
4
),

•

the strong duality
\displaystyle\pi=d
holds,

•

the dual problem (
20
) is attained at a unique minimiser
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

•

there exists a unique optimal control
\displaystyle u^{\star}\in E
for the primal problem (
19
).

Furthermore,
the optimal control is given by

u^{\star}(t,\cdot)=\overline{M}\,\chi_{\{p^{\star}(t,\cdot)>h(p^{\star}(t,\cdot))\}},\quad\overline{M}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{p^{\star}(t,\cdot)>h(p^{\star}(t,\cdot))\}}p^{\star}(t,x)\,dt\,dx,

(21)

where
\displaystyle h
is defined by (
16
), and where
\displaystyle p^{\star}=L_{T}^{\star}p_{f}^{\star}
is the solution of the adjoint equation (
9
) satisfying
\displaystyle p^{\star}(T)=p_{f}^{\star}
.

Remark 3.2
.

In fact, if
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
is such that
\displaystyle y_{f}\in\overline{B}(S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
, we prove that
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}=0
and the formula above returns
\displaystyle u^{\star}=0
, which obviously does steer the system to the target ball.

Remark 3.3
.

As mentioned in the introduction, Theorem
3.1
holds for uniformly elliptic operators of the form (
7
) with analytic coefficients, and in particular the classical heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions, on a bounded, open, connected domain with
\displaystyle C^{2}
boundary.

Throughout this section, we assume the hypotheses sufficient for Theorem

,
i.e.
, that
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
satisfies the (
GUC
) property and that
\displaystyle\partial_{t}-A^{\ast}
is analytic-hypoelliptic. The proof is then scattered into the section as follows:

•

First, we establish that strong duality holds.

•

Second, we prove that the corresponding dual functional is coercive: hence, the dual functional attains its minimum (the dual problem attains its maximum).

•

Third
we prove (
21
).

•

Finally, we investigate the uniqueness of optimal variables.

Remark 3.4
.

As the proofs show, the first two steps and the uniqueness of dual optimal variables are valid for any operator
\displaystyle A

satisfy the (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}-A^{\ast}

3.1
Strong duality

Lemma 3.5
.

\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle 0=L_{T}^{\ast}0
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\forall u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L},\quad\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}\|v\|_{L^{2}},

which leads to

\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}(v)=\int_{0}^{\min(\Phi_{v}(0),L|\Omega|)}\Phi_{v}^{-1}\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}\|v\|_{L^{2}}.

As a result, we may bound with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality again

0\leq F_{T}^{\ast}(p)\leq\frac{1}{2}|\Omega|\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|p(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\,dt\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}T|\Omega|\,\|p\|_{E}^{2},

hence the continuity of
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
at
\displaystyle 0=L_{T}^{\ast}0
.
∎

The above lemma shows that the first condition of Proposition
2.1
is satisfied,
i.e.
, strong duality holds.

3.2
Coercivity of
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
, nonnegative approximate controllability

Proposition 3.6
.

The functional
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
defined by

J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{\overline{\mathcal{U}_{L}}}(L_{T}^{\ast}p_{f})dt\right)^{2}-\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},p_{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}.

(22)

is coercive on
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})\xrightarrow[\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\to\infty]{}\infty,

and thus attains its minimum.

Proof.

Since we know that
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
is convex, proper, strongly lsc, if
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
is coercive then
\displaystyle\textstyle\inf_{p_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)}J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})\neq-\infty
, and that it is actually attained.

We will actually prove a stronger condition than coercivity, namely

\liminf_{\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}\to\infty}\frac{J_{T,\varepsilon}(p_{f})}{\|p_{f}\|_{L^{2}}}>0.

Our method of proof follows that of
[
32
,
20
]
. Take a sequence
\displaystyle\|p_{f}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\to\infty
. We denote
\displaystyle\textstyle q_{f}^{n}:=\frac{p_{f}^{n}}{\|p_{f}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}}
.
By positive homogeneity of
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
(of degree
\displaystyle 2
), we have

\frac{J_{T,\varepsilon}(p^{n}_{f})}{\|p^{n}_{f}\|_{L^{2}}}=\|p^{n}_{f}\|_{L^{2}}F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f}^{n})-\left\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},q_{f}^{n}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon

and hence if
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f}^{n})>0
, then

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{J_{T,\varepsilon}(p^{n}_{f})}{\|p^{n}_{f}\|{L^{2}}}=+\infty.

Let us now treat the remaining case where
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f}^{n})=0
. Since
\displaystyle\|q_{f}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}=1
, upon extraction of a subsequence, we have
\displaystyle q_{f}^{n}\rightharpoonup q_{f}
weakly in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
for some
\displaystyle q_{f}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Since
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}\in L(L^{2}(\Omega),E)
, we have
\displaystyle L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f}^{n}\rightharpoonup L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f}
weakly in
\displaystyle E
.

Now, since
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}
is convex and strongly lsc on
\displaystyle E
, it is (sequentially) weakly lsc and taking the limit we obtain
\displaystyle F_{T}^{\ast}(L_{T}^{\ast}q_{f})=0
.
By Lemma
2.7
, we infer that
\displaystyle q_{f}\leq 0
.

Then, recalling that the target satisfies
\displaystyle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0}\geq 0\Leftrightarrow y_{f}\geq S_{T}y_{0}
, we end up with

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{J_{T,\varepsilon}(p^{n}_{f})}{\|p^{n}_{f}\|_{L^{2}}}\geq-\langle y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},q_{f}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon>0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Characterisation of the minimisers

In this subsection and the subsequent one, it will be convenient to discuss depending on the assumption

y_{f}\notin\overline{B}(S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon),

(23)

in which case the target is not reached with the trivial control
\displaystyle u=0
.
Note that, if (
23
) is not satisfied, the control
\displaystyle u=0
steers
\displaystyle y_{0}
to the target, and is indeed a control in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\textrm{shape}}^{L}

We first remark the following fact:

.

) holds if and only if any minimiser
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
of (

\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}\neq 0

Proof.

Suppose that
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}=0

\displaystyle d=0

\displaystyle\pi=0

\displaystyle u^{\star}

This implies that
\displaystyle F_{T}(u^{\star})=G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u^{\star})=0

On the one hand, this leads to
\displaystyle u^{\star}(t,\cdot)=0

\displaystyle u^{\star}=0

Conversely, if Assumption (

\displaystyle u=0

Remark 3.9
.

As evidenced by the proof, a weaker (but less workable) property than analytic-hypoellipticity is sufficient to infer that optimal controls are on-off shape controls. Indeed, it suffices to require either one of the following conditions (in decreasing order of strength):

(i)

All solutions
\displaystyle t\mapsto p(t)
of the adjoint equation such that
\displaystyle p(T)\neq 0
have zero-measure level sets.

(ii)

For all solutions
\displaystyle t\mapsto p(t)
of the adjoint equation such that
\displaystyle p(T)\neq 0
, the level sets
\displaystyle\{p(t,\cdot)=h(p(t,\cdot))\}
(see (
16
) for the definition of
\displaystyle h(p)
) have measure
\displaystyle 0
.

(iii)

For all solutions
\displaystyle t\mapsto p(t)
of the adjoint equation such that
\displaystyle p(T)\neq 0
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &|\{p(t,\cdot)=h(p(t,\cdot))\}|=L|\Omega|-|\{p(t,\cdot)>h(p(t,\cdot))\}|,\quad&\text{if}\ h(p(t,\cdot))\neq 0\\
&|\{p(t,\cdot)=h(p(t,\cdot))\}|=0,\quad&\text{if}\ h(p(t,\cdot))=0\end{aligned}\quad\mathrm{for\ a.e.}\ t\in[0,T].\right.

Note that requirement
\displaystyle(iii)
is minimal (see Lemma
B.2
and Remark
B.3
).

Finally, an even weaker requirement would be to restrict any of the above
\displaystyle(i)
,
\displaystyle(ii)
or
\displaystyle(iii)
to a single solution
\displaystyle t\mapsto p^{\star}(t)
of the adjoint equation, namely that with
\displaystyle p^{\star}(T)=p_{f}^{\star}
where
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
is the unique dual optimal variable (see below for the uniqueness of optimal variables).

3.4
Uniqueness

Our first uniqueness statement below (
i.e.
, that of the dual optimal variable) is a consequence of Fenchel-Rockafellar duality, and the fact that we work with a Hilbert space, rather than specific properties of the evolution equation under consideration.

Remark 3.10
.

Still applying Proposition
A.8
, we get

L_{T}u^{\star}\in\partial G_{T,\varepsilon}^{\ast}(-p_{f}^{\star})=\partial\sigma_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(-p_{f}^{\star}).

Using the Legendre-Fenchel identity (
47

\displaystyle-p_{f}^{\star}\in\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(L_{T}u^{\star})
.
Thanks to Proposition

, this means that
\displaystyle L_{T}u^{\star}
lies at the boundary of the closed ball
\displaystyle\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, the primal-dual optimal pairs
\displaystyle(u^{\star},p_{f}^{\star})
are unique.

Proof.

Uniqueness of the dual optimal variable.

First note that if Assumption (
23
) does not hold, then
\displaystyle 0
is the unique optimal control,
i.e.

\{L_{T}u^{\star},\,u^{\star}\text{ is optimal}\}=\{0\}.

(24)

On the other hand, if Assumption (
23
) holds, according to Remark
3.10
, and since the set of minimisers of a convex function is convex, the set
\displaystyle\{L_{T}u^{\star},\,u^{\star}\text{ is optimal}\}
is a convex subset of the sphere
\displaystyle S(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
. The closed ball being strictly convex since we are working in the Hilbert space
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists some
\displaystyle y^{\star}\in\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
with
\displaystyle\|y^{\star}-(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0})\|_{L^{2}}=\varepsilon
such that

\{L_{T}u^{\star},\,u^{\star}\text{ is optimal}\}=\{y^{\star}\}.

(25)

Thus, in any case, the set of targets reached by optimal controls is always reduced to a single point.

Now, let
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}
be a dual optimal variable, and
\displaystyle u^{\star}
an optimal control. Then, as strong duality holds, Proposition
A.7
implies that the pair
\displaystyle(u^{\star},p_{f}^{\star})
satisfies the two optimality conditions from (
25

p_{f}^{\star}\in-\partial G_{T,\varepsilon}(L_{T}u^{\star})=-\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(L_{T}u^{\star}).

If Assumption (
23
) does not hold, then (
26
) and (
24
) imply
\displaystyle p_{f}^{\star}\in-\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(0)
.
If
\displaystyle\|y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0}\|_{L^{2}}<\varepsilon
, then
\displaystyle 0\in B(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
and

p_{f}^{\star}\in-\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)}(0)=\{0\}.

Otherwise,
\displaystyle 0\in\partial B(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0},\varepsilon)
and (

Restricting the function
\displaystyle J_{T,\varepsilon}
defining the dual problem (

\displaystyle\|y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=\varepsilon

\gamma_{0}(\lambda):=J_{T,\varepsilon}(\lambda(y_{f}-S_{T}y_{0}))=a_{0}\lambda^{2},\quad\lambda\geq 0.

(28)

It is clear that
\displaystyle 0
is the unique minimiser of
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}

From (

\displaystyle 0
is the unique dual optimal variable if (

If Assumption (

is an optimal control on
\displaystyle[0,T]
whenever
\displaystyle u_{T_{\ell}}
is an optimal control on
\displaystyle[0,T_{\ell}]
.

We now establish the relationship between the optimal control problem (
31
) and the minimal time control problem.

Proposition 5.10
.

Assume that
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
satisfies the comparison principle (
6
). Then, for all
\displaystyle T\in(0,T_{\ell})
, any optimal control for (
31
) on
\displaystyle[0,T]
is a minimal time control, that is,

T^{\star}(\Pi(T))=T.

Moreover, for any
\displaystyle\lambda>\mu_{-}
,

\Pi(T^{\star}(\lambda))=\lambda.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
\displaystyle T^{\star}(\Pi(T))<T.

Then, there exists
\displaystyle\delta>0
and a control
\displaystyle u_{\delta}\in L^{2}(0,T-\delta;L^{2}(\Omega))
such that

\displaystyle F_{T}(u_{\delta})\leq\Pi(T).

Now, any optimal control
\displaystyle u^{\star}_{\delta}
(in the sense of optimal control problem (
31
) in time
\displaystyle T-\delta
) satisfies
\displaystyle F_{T}(u^{\star}_{\delta})\leq F_{T}(u_{\delta})
(the inequality is not necessarily strict, as
\displaystyle u_{\delta}
could be an optimal control),

i.e.
,
\displaystyle\Pi(T-\delta)=F_{T}(u^{\star}_{\delta})\leq F_{T}(u_{\delta})\leq\Pi(T),

which contradicts the fact that
\displaystyle T\mapsto\Pi(T)
is a decreasing function on
\displaystyle(0,T_{\ell})
. Thus, (
5.10
) holds.

Now, let
\displaystyle\lambda>\mu_{-}
. From Corollaries
5.3
,
5.6
and Proposition
5.1
, there exists
\displaystyle T\in(0,T_{\ell})
such that

\displaystyle\Pi(T)=\lambda.

Applying
\displaystyle T^{\star}
to the above and using (
5.10
), we get

\displaystyle T^{\star}(\lambda)=T^{\star}(\Pi(T))=T.

Then, applying
\displaystyle\Pi
to the above yields

\displaystyle\Pi(T^{\star}(\lambda))=\Pi(T)=\lambda.
∎

We can also formulate the above result in the following way: for all
\displaystyle\lambda>\mu_{-}
,

T^{\star}(\lambda)=\inf\{T>0,\quad\Pi(T)\leq\lambda\},

that is,
\displaystyle T^{\star}
is the pseudo-inverse of
\displaystyle\Pi
on
\displaystyle(\mu_{-},+\infty)
.

In terms of the time optimal control problem, we now have a complete characterisation of time optimal controls for (
37
):

Theorem 5.11
.

Assume that
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
satisfies the comparison principle (
6
).

For any
\displaystyle\lambda>\mu_{-}
,
\displaystyle T^{\star}(\lambda)<+\infty
, and
\displaystyle T^{\star}(\lambda)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to\infty]{}0
,
\displaystyle T^{\star}(\lambda)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to\mu_{-}]{}+\infty
.
As a consequence, the domain of definition of
\displaystyle T^{\star}
is
\displaystyle(\mu_{-},+\infty)
, and on its domain of definition,
\displaystyle T^{\star}
is continuous and decreasing.

Moreover, if
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
satisfies the (
GUC
) property and
\displaystyle\partial_{t}-A^{\ast}
is analytic-hypoelliptic, there exists a unique minimal time control for (
37
), given by the optimal control problem (
19
), and it lies in
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{L}_{\textrm{shape}}
.

Appendix A
Convex analysis

A.1
Core properties of Fenchel conjugation

A fundamental property of conjugation is involution (over
\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(H))
:

Theorem A.1

(Fenchel-Moreau)
.

Given any
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
, there holds
\displaystyle f^{\ast}\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
and
\displaystyle f^{\ast\ast}=f
.

Analogously to the classical gradient, the subdifferential can be used to study optimality:

Proposition A.2

(Fermat’s rule)
.

Let
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
.
\displaystyle f
attains a finite global minimum over
\displaystyle H
in
\displaystyle x^{\star}
if and only if

0\in\partial f(x^{\star}).

We now list further useful properties of the Fenchel conjugate:

•

multiplication by a real number: for
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

(\alpha f)^{\ast}(y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \alpha f^{\ast}\left(\frac{y}{\alpha}\right)\qquad&\textrm{if}\quad\alpha\neq 0,\\
\sigma_{\operatorname{dom}(f)}(y)\qquad&\textrm{if}\quad\alpha=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(44)

•

the (suitably normalised) squared norm is its own conjugate:

\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{H}^{2}\right)^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{H}^{2}.

(45)

Let us also mention a result about composition
[
18
]
.
First, let
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
and
\displaystyle g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R})
be non-decreasing. Then,

(g\circ f)^{\ast}(y)=\min_{\alpha\geq 0}\left(g^{\ast}(\alpha)+\alpha f^{\ast}\Big(\frac{y}{\alpha}\Big)\right).

Following (
44
), the convention for
\displaystyle\alpha=0
is
\displaystyle 0\,f^{\ast}\Big(\frac{y}{0}\Big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{dom}(f)}(y).

Link with the subdifferential.

We now give another characterisation of the subdifferential set, which illustrates the link with convex conjugation: for
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
,

\displaystyle\partial f(x)=\{p\in H,\,\langle p,x\rangle_{H}-f(x)=f^{\ast}(p)\}=\{p\in H,\,\langle x,p\rangle_{H}-f^{\ast}(p)=f(x)\}

(46)

Essentially, the subdifferential is the set of linear forms on which the convex conjugate is attained.

Using this characterisation, we then get the Legendre-Fenchel identity, which allows us to “flip” subdifferentials:

p\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^{\ast}(p),\quad f\in\Gamma_{0}(H),\;\forall x,p\in H.

(47)

A.2
Some properties of indicator and support functions

Indicator functions are a crucial tool to encode constraints in convex optimisation problems. Their properties are closely linked to topological properties of their indicated sets:

Proposition A.3
.

We have
\displaystyle\delta_{C},\sigma_{C}\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
as soon as
\displaystyle C
is non-empty, convex and closed.

The characterisation (
46
) of the subdifferential yields a useful result on indicator functions:

Proposition A.4
.

Let
\displaystyle C\subset H
be a closed convex set with nonempty interior. Then, for
\displaystyle x\in H
we have the following:

x\in\partial C\quad\iff\quad\partial\delta_{C}(x)\ \textrm{is a nontrivial cone}.

Equivalently, by convex conjugation,

\exists p\neq 0,\;x\in\argmax_{v\in C}\langle v,p\rangle\ \iff\exists p\neq 0,\;\ x\in\partial\sigma_{C}(p)\ \iff\ x\in\partial C.

Indicator function of a ball in a Hilbert space.

Consider the closed unit ball
\displaystyle\overline{B}(0,1)
of
\displaystyle H
. We have seen before that

\sigma_{\overline{B}(0,1)}(y)=\big(\delta_{\overline{B}(0,1)}\big)^{\ast}(y)=\|y\|_{H}.

Using (
46
), we get the following: for
\displaystyle x\in\overline{B}(0,1)
,

\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(0,1)}(x)=\{p\in H,\quad\langle p,x\rangle_{H}=\sigma_{\overline{B}(0,1)}(p)\}=\{p\in H,\quad\langle p,x\rangle_{H}=\|p\|_{H}\}.

From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we know that

\displaystyle\langle p,x\rangle_{H}\leq\|p\|_{H}\|x\|_{H},

it follows that

\displaystyle\langle p,x\rangle_{H}=\|p\|_{H}
if and only if

\displaystyle x=\textstyle{\frac{p}{\|p\|_{H}}}
.
This implies that

\partial\delta_{\overline{B}(0,1)}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{0\}&\qquad\textrm{if}\quad\|x\|_{H}<1,\\
\{\lambda x,\quad\lambda\geq 0\}&\qquad\textrm{if}\quad\|x\|_{H}=1.\end{array}\right.

(48)

A.3
Technical lemmas

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\displaystyle f\in\Gamma_{0}(H)
be such that

F:u\in L^{2}(0,T;H)\longmapsto\int_{0}^{T}f(u(t))\,dt,

is well-defined and proper. Then
\displaystyle F\in\Gamma_{0}(L^{2}(0,T;H))
, and its Fenchel conjugate and subdifferential are given by

\forall p\in L^{2}(0,T;H),\quad F^{\ast}(p)=\int_{0}^{T}f^{\ast}(p(t))\,dt,

\partial F(u)=\left\{p\in L^{2}(0,T;H),\quad p(t)\in\partial f(u(t)),\ \text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T)\right\},\quad\forall u\in L^{2}(0,T;H).

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle F
is obviously convex, we only need to justify that
\displaystyle F
is lsc to infer
\displaystyle F\in\Gamma_{0}(L^{2}(0,T;H))
.
We let
\displaystyle u_{n}\rightarrow u
be in
\displaystyle L^{2}(0,T;H)
and must show that
\displaystyle F(u)\leq\liminf F(u_{n})
.
Upon extraction of a subsequence, we may assume that
\displaystyle F(u_{n})\rightarrow\liminf F(u_{n})
, and that
\displaystyle u_{n}(t)\rightarrow u(t)
in
\displaystyle H
for a.e.
\displaystyle t\in(0,T)
.
Then, using successively the lsc of
\displaystyle f
and Fatou’s lemma, we find

F(u)=\int_{0}^{T}f(u(t))\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\liminf f(u_{n}(t))\,dt\leq\liminf\int_{0}^{T}f(u_{n}(t))\,dt=\liminf F(u_{n}).

For
\displaystyle p\in L^{2}(0,T;H)
, we compute

F^{\ast}(p)=\sup_{u\in L^{2}(0,T;H)}\langle p,u\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T;H)}-\int_{0}^{T}f(u(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\sup_{u\in H}\langle p(t),u\rangle_{H}-f(u(t))\right)\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}f^{\ast}(p(t))\,dt.

Using the characterisation given in (
46
), and Lemma
A.5
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\partial F(u)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{p\in L^{2}(0,T;H)}\{\langle p,u\rangle-F^{\ast}(p)\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{p\in L^{2}(0,T;H)}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\langle p(t),u(t)\rangle dt-\int_{0}^{T}f^{\ast}(p(t))dt\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{p\in L^{2}(0,T;H)}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\left(\langle p(t),u(t)\rangle-f^{\ast}(p(t))\right)dt\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in L^{2}(0,T;H),\quad p(t)\in\argmax_{p\in H}\{\langle p,u(t)\rangle-f^{\ast}(p)\}\right\},

and the result follows by the same characterisation of the subdifferential set
\displaystyle\partial f(u(t))
.
∎

A.4
Fenchel-Rockafellar duality

Let
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle F
be two Hilbert spaces. Let
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
be functions in
\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(E)
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(F)
, respectively, and
\displaystyle A:E\rightarrow F
be a bounded operator.
Consider the (primal) optimisation problem

\pi=\inf_{x\in E}\left(f(x)+g(Ax)\right).

(
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
)

and its dual problem

d=\sup_{z\in F}\left(-f^{\ast}(A^{\ast}z)-g^{\ast}(-z)\right)=-\inf_{z\in F}\left(f^{\ast}(A^{\ast}z)+g^{\ast}(-z)\right)

(
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
)

With the above notations, weak duality always holds,
i.e.
, we always have
\displaystyle\pi\geq d
.
The Fenchel-Rockafellar theorem states when and how the strong duality holds,
i.e.
, when
\displaystyle d=\pi

[
42
]
.

Theorem A.6
.

If there exists
\displaystyle\bar{x}\in E
such that
\displaystyle g
is continuous at
\displaystyle A\bar{x}
and
\displaystyle f(\bar{x})<+\infty
, then

\pi=d\quad\text{and}\quad d\text{ is attained {if finite}}.

Symmetrically, if there exists
\displaystyle\bar{z}\in F
such that
\displaystyle f^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle A^{\ast}\bar{z}
and
\displaystyle g^{\ast}(-\bar{z})<+\infty
, then

d=\pi\quad\text{and}\quad\pi\text{ is attained {if finite}}.

The second part of the theorem is obtained by applying the first part to (

D

),
\displaystyle\inf_{z\in F}\left(f^{\ast}(A^{\ast}z)+g^{\ast}(-z)\right),

seen as a primal problem, and (

C

), rewritten as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in E}\left(-f(x)-g(Ax)\right),

seen as its dual problem.
This yields

\displaystyle-d\geq-\pi,

with equality under the corresponding assumptions.

Lagrangian and saddle-point interpretation.

Let us now define the Lagrangian for
\displaystyle(x,y)\in E\times F
by

\mathcal{L}(x,y):=\langle y,Ax\rangle+f(x)-g^{\ast}(y).

If
\displaystyle(x^{\star},y^{\star})
is a saddle point of the Lagrangian,
i.e.
,

x^{\star}\in\argmin_{x\in E}\mathcal{L}(x,y^{\star})\;\text{ and }\;y^{\star}\in\argmax_{y\in F}\mathcal{L}(x^{\star},y),

then
\displaystyle(x^{\star},z^{\star})
(with
\displaystyle z^{\star}=-y^{\star}
) is a pair of primal and dual optimal variables, and strong duality holds.

What matters is the converse: if
\displaystyle(x^{\star},z^{\star})
is a pair of primal and dual optimal variables and if strong duality holds, then
\displaystyle(x^{\star},y^{\star})
(with
\displaystyle y^{\star}=-z^{\star}
) is a saddle point of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
.

Whenever
\displaystyle(x^{\star},y^{\star})
is a primal-dual optimal pair, Fermat’s rule and the Legendre-Fenchel identity yield

x^{\star}\in\argmin_{x\in E}\mathcal{L}(x,y^{\star})\quad\iff\quad-A^{\ast}y^{\star}\in\partial f(x^{\star})\quad\iff\quad x^{\star}\in\partial f^{\ast}(-A^{\ast}y^{\star}),

as well as

y^{\star}\in\argmax_{y\in F}\mathcal{L}(x^{\star},y)\quad\iff\quad Ax^{\star}\in\partial g^{\ast}(y^{\star})\quad\iff\quad y^{\star}\in\partial g(Ax^{\star}),

Summing up, we have the following proposition:

Proposition A.7
.

Let
\displaystyle(x^{\star},z^{\star})
be a pair of primal and dual optimal variables. If strong duality holds, then

x^{\star}\in\partial f^{\ast}(A^{\ast}z^{\star}),\quad Ax^{\star}\in\partial g^{\ast}(-z^{\star}),

(49)

z^{\star}\in-\partial g(Ax^{\star}),\quad A^{\ast}z^{\star}\in\partial f(x^{\star}).

Reinterpreting the Fenchel-Rockafellar theorem with the above and in a way that is useful for controllability issues, we end up with

Proposition A.8
.

Under the assumption that there exists
\displaystyle\bar{x}\in E
such that
\displaystyle g
is continuous at
\displaystyle A\bar{x}
and
\displaystyle f(\bar{x})<+\infty
, if
\displaystyle\pi
is finite, and attained at
\displaystyle x^{\star}\in E
, then
\displaystyle d
is attained at
\displaystyle z^{\star}\in F
satisfying

z^{\star}\in-\partial g(Ax^{\star}),\quad A^{\ast}z^{\star}\in\partial f(x^{\star}).

(50)

Conversely, if
\displaystyle(x^{\star},z^{\star})
satisfies (
50
)
\displaystyle,(x^{\star},z^{\star})
is a pair of primal and dual optimal variables.

Similarly, under the assumption that there exists
\displaystyle\bar{z}\in E
such that
\displaystyle f^{\ast}
is continuous at
\displaystyle A^{\ast}\bar{z}
and
\displaystyle g^{\ast}(-\bar{z})<+\infty
, if
\displaystyle d
is finite, and attained at
\displaystyle z^{\star}\in F
, then
\displaystyle\pi
is attained at
\displaystyle x^{\star}\in E
satisfying

x^{\star}\in\partial f^{\ast}(A^{\ast}z^{\star}),\quad Ax^{\star}\in\partial g^{\ast}(-z^{\star}).

(51)

Conversely, if
\displaystyle(x^{\star},z^{\star})
satisfies (
51
)
\displaystyle,(x^{\star},z^{\star})
is a pair of primal and dual optimal variables.

A.5
Parametric convex optimisation

Lemma A.9
.

Let
\displaystyle H
be a Hilbert space,
\displaystyle Z
be a metric space,
\displaystyle f:H\times Z\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup{\{+\infty\}}
.
Assume that

•

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in Z
,
\displaystyle f(\cdot,\alpha)
is convex on
\displaystyle H
,

•

\displaystyle\forall x\in H
,
\displaystyle f(x,\cdot)
is continuous on
\displaystyle Z
,

•

\displaystyle f
is sequentially weak-strong lower semicontinuous on
\displaystyle H\times Z
, i.e.,

\forall x_{n}\rightharpoonup x,\;\forall\alpha_{n}\rightarrow\alpha,\quad f(x,\alpha)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}f(x_{n},\alpha_{n}),

•

there exists a unique
\displaystyle x_{\alpha}\in H
such that
\displaystyle\inf_{x\in H}f(x,\alpha)=f(x_{\alpha},\alpha)
.

Then the mapping

\alpha\in Z\longmapsto\inf_{x\in H}f(x,\alpha)

is continuous on
\displaystyle Z
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\rightarrow\alpha
. Denoting

\displaystyle m(\alpha)=\inf_{x\in H}f(x,\alpha)=f(x_{\alpha},\alpha)
, let us show that
\displaystyle m(\alpha_{n})
converges to
\displaystyle m(\alpha)
.

Upper semicontinuity.

For
\displaystyle x\in H
fixed, thanks to the continuity of
\displaystyle f(x,\cdot)
, we pass to the limit in
\displaystyle f(x,\alpha_{n})\geq m(\alpha_{n})
and find

m(\alpha)=\inf_{x\in H}f(x,\alpha)\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}m(\alpha_{n}).

Lower semicontinuity.

We denote
\displaystyle x_{n}=x_{\alpha_{n}}
. Let us for the moment admit that
\displaystyle(x_{n})
is bounded. Upon extraction, we may assume that
\displaystyle x_{n}\rightharpoonup\bar{x}
for some
\displaystyle x\in H
. By sequential weak-strong lower semicontinuity,

f(\bar{x},\alpha)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}f(x_{n},\alpha_{n})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}m(\alpha_{n}).

Since the left-hand side is bounded from below by
\displaystyle m(\alpha)
, we have proved lower semicontinuity (and in fact
\displaystyle\bar{x}=x_{\alpha}
).

We are left to proving the boundedness of
\displaystyle(x_{n})
to conclude the proof. Assume that
\displaystyle(x_{n})
is not bounded. Upon extraction, we may assume that

y_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}\rightharpoonup y,

for some
\displaystyle y\in H
. For any fixed
\displaystyle\lambda>0
, we shall prove that
\displaystyle x_{\alpha}+\lambda y
minimises
\displaystyle f(\cdot,\alpha)
, which contradicts the fourth assumption that there exists a single minimum point.

Indeed, we notice that

\Big(1-\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}\Big)x_{\alpha}+\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}x_{n}\rightharpoonup x_{\alpha}+\lambda y.

Hence, by weak-strong lower semicontinuity, convexity, the fact
\displaystyle x_{n}
minimises
\displaystyle f(\cdot,\alpha_{n})
and continuity,

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(x_{\alpha}+\lambda y,\alpha)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}f\Big(\Big(1-\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}\Big)x_{\alpha}++\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}x_{n},\alpha_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\Big(1-\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}\Big)f(x_{\alpha},\alpha_{n})+\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}f(x_{n},\alpha_{n})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\Big(1-\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}\Big)f(x_{\alpha},\alpha_{n})+\frac{\lambda}{\|x_{n}\|_{H}}f(x_{\alpha},\alpha_{n})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}f(x_{\alpha},\alpha_{n})=f(x_{\alpha},\alpha).

∎

Appendix B
The classical bathtub principle

The classical bathtub principle characterises the maximisers, and gives the maximum value, of the constrained scalar product maximisation:

\sup_{u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\ast}_{L}}\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx,

(52)

where
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is arbitrary, and

\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{L}^{\ast}:=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\;0\leq u\leq 1\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}u=L|\Omega|\right\},

is the convex hull (and
\displaystyle L^{\infty}
weak-
\displaystyle\ast
closure) of the set of characteristic functions whose support has the corresponding fixed measure:

\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{L}:=\{\chi_{\omega},\quad\omega\subset\Omega,\quad|\omega|=L|\Omega|\}.

Recalling the notations (
14
) and (
15
) introduced in Section
2.3
, the classical bathtub principle reads (we refer to
[
25
]
):

Lemma B.1

(classical bathtub principle)
.

Let
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Denote
\displaystyle\rho(v):=\Phi^{-1}_{v}(L|\Omega|)
. The maximum in (
52
) equals

\left(\int_{v>\rho(v)}v\right)+\rho(v)(L|\Omega|-|\{v>\rho(v)\}|)=\int_{0}^{L|\Omega|}\Phi_{v}^{-1},

and the maximisers are given by

u^{\star}:=\chi_{\{v>\rho(v)\}}+c\chi_{\{v=\rho(v)\}},

where
\displaystyle c
is any measurable function such that
\displaystyle 0\leq c\leq 1
and

\int_{\{v=\rho(v)\}}c=L|\Omega|-|\{v>\rho(v)\}|.

In particular, if all the level sets of the function
\displaystyle v
have zero measure, then the maximum is uniquely attained by

u^{\star}:=\chi_{\{v>\rho(v)\}},

and the maximum hence equals
\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\{v>\rho(v)\}}v
.

Now, recall that we defined
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{L}
by (
11
) and its convex hull
\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
by (
12
) in Section
2.3
. They are respective relaxations of

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{L}
, and its convex hull

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}^{\ast}_{L}
.

For
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we consider the relaxed version of (
52
):

\sup_{u\in\overline{\mathcal{U}}_{L}}\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,dx.

(53)

Then, the complete solution of Lemma
2.3
is given by the following:

Lemma B.2

(relaxed bathtub principle)
.

Let
\displaystyle v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Denote
\displaystyle h(v)=\max(0,\Phi_{v}^{-1}(L|\Omega|))=\max(0,\rho(v))
. Then, the maximum in (
53
) equals

\int_{0}^{\min(\Phi_{v}(0),L|\Omega|)}\Phi_{v}^{-1},

and the maximisers are given by

u^{\star}:=\chi_{\{v>h(v)\}}+c\chi_{\{v=h(v)\}},

where
\displaystyle c
is any measurable function such that
\displaystyle 0\leq c\leq 1
and

\left\{\begin{aligned} \int_{\{v=h(v)\}}c=L|\Omega|-|\{v>h(v)\}|\ &\textrm{ if }\ h(v)>0\\
\int_{\{v=h(v)\}}c\leq L|\Omega|-|\{v>h(v)\}|\ &\textrm{ if }\ h(v)=0\end{aligned}\right.

Remark B.3
.

In particular, if
\displaystyle h(v)>0
, there is a unique maximiser in (
53
) if and only if

|\{v>h(v)\}|+|\{v=h(v)\}|=L|\Omega|.

Indeed, when the above does not hold,
\displaystyle c
can be chosen to have values in
\displaystyle(0,1)
on a set of nonzero measure, and the maximisers are no longer unique.

On the other hand, if
\displaystyle h(v)=0
, as soon as
\displaystyle|\{v=h(v)\}|>0
the maximisers are no longer unique.

Proof.

Let us first note that the supremum exists and is attained, as (
53
) consists in maximising a continuous function on a weak-
\displaystyle\ast
compact set. Also note that any maximiser
\displaystyle u^{\star}
obviously satisfies
\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}u^{\star}\subset\{v\geq 0\}
.

To prove the relaxed bathtub principle, we distinguish two cases:

1.

We first consider the case where
\displaystyle|\{v>0\}|\geq L|\Omega|
.

Let

\displaystyle u^{\star}
be a maximiser.

Suppose

\int_{\Omega}u^{\star}<L|\Omega|.

(54)

If
\displaystyle|\{u^{\star}<1\}\cap\{v>0\}|>0
, then there exists a set
\displaystyle\omega\subset\{u^{\star}<1\}\cap\{v>0\}
of nonzero measure with
\displaystyle|\omega|\leq L|\Omega|
, so that

0<\int_{\omega}1-u^{\star}\leq L|\Omega|-\int_{\Omega}u^{\star}.

Then,
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{\star}+\chi_{\omega}(1-u^{\star})\leq L|\Omega|
and

\int_{\Omega}(u^{\star}+\chi_{\omega}(1-u^{\star}))v>\int_{\Omega}u^{\star}v,

which contradicts the fact that
\displaystyle u^{\star}
