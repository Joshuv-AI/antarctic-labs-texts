Multipartite Entanglement Distribution in Quantum Networks using Subgraph Complementations
Aniruddha Sen ∗, Kenneth Goodenough, Don Towsley

Abstract
Quantum networks are important for quantum communication and allow for several tasks such as quantum teleportation, quantum key distribution, quantum sensing, and quantum error correction. Graph states are a specific class of multipartite entangled states that can be represented by graphs. We propose a novel approach for distributing graph states across a quantum network. We show that the distribution of graph states can be characterized by a system of subgraph complementations , which we also relate to the minimum rank of the underlying graph and the degree of entanglement quantified by the Schmidt-rank of the quantum state. We analyze resource usage for our algorithm and show that it improves on the number of qubits, bits for classical communication, and EPR pairs utilized, as compared to prior work. In fact, the number of local operations and resource consumption for our approach scales linearly in the number of vertices. This produces a quadratic improvement in completion time for several classes of graph states represented by dense graphs, which translates into an exponential improvement by allowing parallelization of gate operations. This leads to improved fidelities in the presence of noisy operations, as we show through simulation in the presence of noisy operations. Common classes of graph states are classified along with their optimal distribution time using subgraph complementations. We find a close to optimal sequence of subgraph complementation operations to distribute an arbitrary graph state, and establish upper bounds on distribution time along with providing approximate greedy algorithms.

Motivation

The theory of multipartite entanglement has been extensively used to study the distribution of graph states in a quantum network
[
1

3

. Creating entangled graph states efficiently is important for the development of quantum networks, having applications in areas such as quantum metrology

In this paper, we first consider a quantum network in ideal conditions with no noisy communication and a limited number of perfect quantum memories. We address the problem of distributing a graph state under such conditions and present an algorithm to do so for a network having an arbitrary topology. We are in particular interested in gaining a better understanding of the limitations in resource consumption (of resources such as EPR pairs and number of qubits) and runtime associated with distributing graph states, and how they can be optimized. Secondly, we show that our algorithm leads to improved fidelities of the graph state in the presence of noisy operations.

I-C

Summary of Contributions

We propose a novel graph state distribution algorithm both with and without parallelization and analyze it first for the noiseless case. We then examine the effects of noise through simulations on various graph states. Our protocol is based on a specific operation on graph states, termed a
subgraph complementation
(
SC
), which should not be confused with local complementation. We then show that any graph state can be distributed in the network through a sequence of
SC
operations. Along with introducing this protocol, we make the following contributions, improving upon prior work.

•

We show that our graph state distribution algorithm utilizes
(n-1)
Bell pairs and requires at most
(n-1)
qubits at the central node. The number of Bell pairs is clearly optimal and the number of qubits at the central node approximately scales with the average degree of the vertices in the graph.

•

We show that our algorithm requires
O(n)
time steps and
O(n)
bits of classical communication to distribute sparse graphs, as well as several relevant classes of dense graphs such as complete graphs (GHZ states) and complete bipartite graphs, but may take up to
O(n^{2})
resources for some dense graphs.

•

By parallelizing gate operations and using auxiliary qubits, we show that the distribution time improves to
O(\log n)
time steps for the same classes of dense graphs. We obtain a general upper bound of
O(n\log n)
time steps for arbitrary dense graphs and
O(n)
for sparse graphs, as well as
O(n)
time steps for random graphs (sparse and dense) with high probability.

•

We establish a relation between the number of
SC
operations needed to distribute a graph state
\ket{G}
and the entanglement of
\ket{G}
(quantified by its average Schmidt-rank over all possible bipartitions).

•

We also carry out simulations (with and without parallelization) of our algorithm in the presence of gate operations characterized by depolarizing noise, and show that our algorithm almost always increases fidelity compared to other approaches.

II
Background

Graph states are a class of multipartite entangled states that can be represented by graphs, with each vertex representing a qubit and each edge representing a
CZ
(controlled-
Z
) gate. A graph state
\left|G\right\rangle
corresponding to the undirected graph
G=(V,E)
is defined as

\left|G\right\rangle=\prod_{(a,b)\in E}CZ_{ab}\left|+\right\rangle^{|V|}\,,

where
CZ_{ab}\left|+\right\rangle
denotes an edge between two vertices
a,b\in V
, each of them initially in the
\left|+\right\rangle
state. Applying a
CZ
gate twice removes an edge, i.e.
CZ^{2}=I
. Note that
CZ
gates commute with each other and are also symmetric, so edges are undirected.

We utilize the following graph operations corresponding to quantum gates or measurements to modify a graph state in this paper — edge addition/deletion (
CZ
gate), local complementation of a vertex (
LC
) and measurement of a qubit in either the
Y
or
Z
basis. An
LC
operation on a vertex complements the induced subgraph formed by the neighborhood of a vertex, whereas a
Z
-basis measurement removes a vertex from the graph
[
1
]
. A
Y
-basis measurement is equivalent, up to single qubit Clifford gates, to an
LC
operation followed by removal of the vertex
[
1
]
.

We also need an operation denoted as
subgraph complementation

[
10
]
(or
SC
) on some subset of vertices
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
of the graph. Subgraph complementation of
V^{\prime}
corresponds to complementing the induced subgraph on vertices
V^{\prime}
of the graph
G
. That is, for each pair of vertices
v_{i},v_{j}\in V^{\prime}
, we either add or remove the corresponding edge
(v_{i},v_{j})
.

An
SC
operation is similar to an
LC
operation since an
LC
on a particular vertex
v
is equivalent to an
SC
carried out on the neighborhood of
v
. However,
SC
operations do not correspond to
LC
operations since they can be on any arbitrary induced subgraph of
G
. The one exception is when the subgraph is the neighborhood of
v
for some
v\in V
not in the subgraph. Also, note that
SC
operations commute.

III
Subgraph complementation protocol

We describe the protocol for the setting of no noise and deterministic Bell pair generation. This section will be followed by a discussion of
SC
operations as part of our algorithm to distribute an arbitrary graph state.

III-A

Single subgraph complementation

We first present a protocol for performing a single
SC
operation on any subset of nodes
V_{\alpha}\subseteq V
of the network, requiring
O(|V_{\alpha}|)
time steps. This will serve as a building block for our graph state distribution algorithm.

The protocol by Avis et al.
[
8
]
distributes a state by locally creating it and then teleporting it, which is costly in memory usage. Roughly speaking, we get around this by constructing the desired graph state step by step through
SC
operations which consist of local CZ gates (acting only on
n
qubits as opposed to the
2n
qubits in Avis et al.
[
8
]
) and local complementations.

More formally, consider a network with a central node
A
that contains
k-1
qubits
\{a_{i}:0\leq i<k-1\}
. The network also contains a set of
k-1
nodes containing one qubit each
\{c_{i}:0\leq i<k-1\}
. Our setup assumes that
(k-1)
Bell pairs of the form
(a_{i},c_{i}),0\leq i<(k-1)
, are already distributed between the nodes. Note that this defines two network graphs corresponding to the nodes (the physical graph) and qubits (the graph state) respectively.

The following protocol describes how to distribute a
k-clique
, or a fully connected graph of
k
vertices, among the qubits in the set
\{a_{i}\}\bigcup\{c_{i}\}
where qubits
a_{i}
are in the central node
A
and qubits
c_{i}
are in the other end nodes. An example operation for
k=4
can be found in Fig.
1
.

a_{1}
and
a_{2}

SC

\forall a_{i}:i\neq 0\implies CZ(a_{i},a_{0})

\forall a_{i}:i\neq 0\implies LC(a_{i})

3.

\forall a_{i}:i\neq 0\implies CZ(a_{i},a_{0})

If a

III-B

Sequence of subgraph complementations

In order to carry out another
SC
operation after obtaining a state similar to state (iv) in Fig.
1
, we need to restore direct connections
(a_{i},c_{i})
between the central node and each of the end nodes, similar to the connections in state (i) in Fig.
1

operation. This simply involves carrying out steps 1–3 in the subgraph complementation protocol in reverse order. This is illustrated in Fig.
2
. The only difference between the new state (v) and state (i) is that the end nodes form a clique. The
SC
protocol can then be carried out on a new set of vertices to add another clique, but since
CZ^{2}=I
, the edges of the clique get added modulo 2 (every two additions of the same edge removes it). More specifically, applying the sequence of operations 1–4 on any set of vertices
V_{\alpha}\subseteq V
corresponds to performing another
SC
operation on
V_{\alpha}
. This can be used to successively perform several
SC
operations.

Finally, after performing some sequence of
SC
operations, we would like to select which connections (edges) to keep or not to keep between the central node
A
and the other nodes. Since
A
is also part of the network, simply perform step 1 followed by step 2 of the
SC
protocol to add any edges between
A
(resp.
a_{i}
) and
C_{i}
(
c_{i}
), and measure out all the other qubits.

III-C

Analysis

In order to complement a subgraph of
k
vertices in a network, this protocol utilizes
k-1
Bell pairs and
k-1
qubits in the central node. A total of
(2k-2)

CZ
operations and
k

LC
operations are required by the basic protocol. This will be followed by a linear number of
Z
-measurements (maximum
k
),
LCs
and
CZs
(maximum
k-1
in conjunction with each other). Thus, the number of time steps required to carry out this protocol is
O(k)
.

IV
Graph State Distribution Algorithm

In this section, we describe an algorithm for distributing an arbitrary graph state, which uses
SC
operations as a primitive. Specifically, we show that any graph state can be generated through a sequence of
SC
operations, and also analyze the distribution time.

IV-A

Subgraph complementation system

Any arbitrary graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices can be represented by a subgraph complementation system
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{d}\}

[
10
]
where
V_{i}
denotes a single
SC
operation on
V_{i}\subseteq V
and
d=|\mathcal{SC}_{sys}|
. Note that
SC
operations can be carried out in any order, and that this decomposition is not unique. Let the subgraph complementation number
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
denote the minimum cardinality
d
(over all possible
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
for a graph
G
) of such a set
[
10
]
. For instance, consider the case of building the wheel graph
W_{5}
as shown leftmost in Fig.
3
. For one possible
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
, all 8 edges (which are 2-cliques) are added one by one, corresponding to one
SC
operation each, so
d=8
.

Fig. 3
:
Subgraph complementation system for
W_{5}

[
10
]
.

In the example shown in the figure, only three
SC
operations (solid lines) are required and
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}=\{\{1,2,4,5\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,3,5\}\}
, where each subset of
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
contains the vertices in an
SC

, thus

. In fact, we can show that this is the minimum number of

IV-B

Time for subgraph complementation

For our analysis, we assume that Bell pairs are distributed in advance. We consider first the gate-based model of computation with perfectly noiseless quantum channels for communication (see Section
VII
for a numerical analysis in the presence of noise). Let
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})
denote the time required by our protocol to distribute a graph state
\left|G\right\rangle
according to subgraph complementation system
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
. Denote by
t(k)
the time required for a single
SC
operation on
k
vertices,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows from the prior analysis section that
t(k)=\gamma k=O(k),
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R^{+}}
, since time is proportional to the number of
CZ
gates performed. This directly leads to an expression for
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})
as stated below.

Theorem IV.1
.

Given a graph
G
with
n
vertices and the corresponding system
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
with minimal size
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
such that each
SC
operation on
V_{i}
is performed on
k_{i}
vertices, then
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\cdot t(\overline{k})\,,
where
\overline{k}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}
.

Proof.

Given a graph
G
and an
SC
system
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
,

T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}t(k_{i})\,,

where
k_{i}
denotes the number of vertices in
V_{i}
.
By linearity of
t
,

\overline{k}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}\implies t(\overline{k})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}t(k_{i})=\frac{1}{d}T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys}).

Also assuming that
d
is minimum,

T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\cdot t(\overline{k})\,.

∎

It is possible that
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})
can be minimized by some
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
that is not of minimum size
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
. However, the case
d=\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
provides an upper bound
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\cdot t(\overline{k})
on distribution time, which we conjecture is a tight bound for most graph states.

Given a particular
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
, we can also optimize the ordering. Note that we do not always have to do a complete edge reset, there are some edges that we could potentially keep, minimizing both the number of gates and time steps needed.

Another useful result
[
11
]
is as follows.

Theorem IV.2
.

For any graph
G
with
n
vertices,

\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\leq n-1\,.

If
c_{2}(G)
is constant with respect to some class of graphs, then the graph states corresponding to that class could be efficiently distributed in a linear number of time steps since
t(\overline{k})\leq n
, so
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=O(n)
(from
IV.1
). It turns out we can characterize the class of graphs with a constant bounded value of
c_{2}(G)
. Formally,
\{G|\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\leq d\}
can be characterized by a finite number of
forbidden induced subgraphs
(i.e. there is no subgraph in
G
isomorphic to any graph in some finite set) for all
d\in\mathbb{N}

[
10
]
. Dense structured graphs often have a bounded
d
, such as cliques (
d=1
) and complete bipartite graphs
(d=3)
. Specifically, the
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}
with minimum
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})
for cliques just corresponds to one
SC
on all vertices. Similarly, for complete bipartite graphs with vertex partitions
P_{1},P_{2}
the corresponding optimal subgraph complementation system is given by
\mathcal{SC}_{sys}=\{P_{1}\cup P_{2},P_{1},P_{2}\}
.

It is also worth noting that
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
is related to another graph attribute – the
minimum rank

[
10
]
of
G
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}

A
denote the adjacency matrix of a graph
G
. The minimum rank over
\mathbb{F}_{2}

, is defined as
mr(G,\mathbb{F}_{2})=min_{D}

over all binary diagonal matrices

\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)=mr(G,\mathbb{F}_{2})

\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)=mr(G,\mathbb{F}_{2})+1\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Buchanan2021SubgraphComp}{}{}]}

Calculating the optimal number of time steps taken to distribute a graph state and the corresponding

appears to be a hard problem in general, but we observe some common characteristics of entanglement distribution using subgraph complementations. There is often a trade-off between

IV-C

Entanglement of the graph state

An interesting property of
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
is its relation to the Schmidt-rank
r_{G}(X)
, a common entanglement measure of pure states. For a graph state
\left|G\right\rangle
,
r_{G}(X)
is defined over some bipartition
(X,V(G)\setminus X)
of the vertices of
G
. The Schmidt-rank quantifies the number of superpositions of a pure state into factorizable states
[
13
]
—- a higher Schmidt-rank of a state implies it has higher entanglement. Define the average Schmidt-rank
\mathbb{E}_{r}(G)
to be the expectation of
r
for a uniformly chosen random subset of
V
.

Theorem IV.3
.

For any graph
G
,
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)>\mathbb{E}_{r}(G)\,.

Proof.

This can be inferred from a relation between
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
and the cut-rank of a graph
[
14
]
, which equals the Schmidt-rank
[
15
]
, as described below.
Define the cut-rank
\rho_{G}(X)
of a set
X
of vertices in a graph
G
as the rank of the
X\times(V(G)\setminus X)
matrix over the binary field whose
(i,j)
-entry is 1 if vertex
i
in
X
is adjacent to vertex
j
in
V(G)\setminus X
and 0 otherwise.
Define the expected cut-rank
\mathbb{E}\rho(G)
as the cut-rank for a uniformly chosen random subset
X
of
V(G)

[
14
]
.
Now, consider the following two results:

\displaystyle\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)

\displaystyle>\mathbb{E}\rho(G)\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\rho_{G}(X)

\displaystyle=r_{G}(X)\,,

(2)

where (
1
) comes from
[
14
]
and (
2
) from
[
15
]
. From these, we can directly conclude that
\mathbb{E}\rho(G)=\mathbb{E}_{r}(G)
, and thus,

\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)>\mathbb{E}_{r}(G)\,.

∎

Consequently, graph states with a high degree of entanglement will have large values of the subgraph complementation number
\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)
, and therefore may be more expensive to distribute.

V
Comparison to prior work

Our approach utilizes
(n-1)
EPR pairs,
O(\overline{k}n)
bits of classical communication (CC), and a maximum of
n-1
qubits at the central node, all of which improve on prior work
[
2
,
3
,
8
]
. Our approach requires one time step to distribute an arbitrary graph state, if we follow early work to implement our cost model
[
2
,
16
]
, which make the assumption that local gates at multiple nodes and the same Clifford operations on a single central node commute, so all the operations can be performed in one time step. We instead assume a gate-based model of computation, which implies
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})
number of time steps is required. Other work
[
8
]
utilizes an approach with a central network node that takes
O(n)
time steps if local operations are free and
O(|E|)=O(n^{2})
time steps if not (accounting for local gates) to build a graph state corresponding to a dense graph. In the presence of memory decoherence, local operations are not free. The fidelity of the state is directly dependent on the number of time steps required. Our approach may also utilize
O(n^{2})
number of gates and time steps to distribute certain dense graph states but not for most of the common classes since
T(G,\mathcal{SC}_{sys})=O(n)
even for several classes of dense graphs. This presents a quadratic improvement in the number of time steps, gates required, and potential fidelity of the state in the presence of noise.

VI
Incorporating Parallelization

VI-A

Log depth GHZ state generation

We first describe a log depth circuit to generate a GHZ state, that is the same up to local rotations as the construction proposed by Cruz et al.
[
17
]
. This will be useful as a subroutine in reducing the time to create a star graph in steps 1 and 3 of the subgraph complementation protocol from
III-A
.

Let
n
qubits be denoted by
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2}...q_{n-1}
. Begin with a GHZ state (star graph state with center
q_{0}
) of a constant size of
c
qubits s.t.
c<n/2
which can be created in constant depth through the application of
c

CZ
gates. We claim that the size of the GHZ state can be doubled in constant depth. This can be done as follows.

1.

\forall q_{i}:1\leq i\leq c\implies CZ(q_{i},q_{c+i})
. The gates can be carried out in parallel, taking one time step.

2.

\forall q_{i}:1\leq i\leq c\implies LC(q_{i})
. Also takes one time step.

3.

\forall q_{i}:1\leq i\leq c\implies CZ(q_{i},q_{c+i})
. This is identical to step 1, thus it also takes one time step.

The above protocol is equivalent to applying
CZ(q_{0},q_{c+i}),\forall q_{i}:1\leq i\leq c
but only takes three time steps (instead of
c
time steps). Thus, the size of the GHZ state is doubled from
c
to
2c
in constant time. Repeated application of this protocol clearly leads to the creation of an
n
-qubit GHZ state in
O(\log(n))
depth.

VI-B

Application to subgraph complementation

Now, the above protocol can be utilized to reduce the times for steps 1 and 3 of the subgraph complementation protocol from Section
III-A
. We first observe that in both of those steps, a star graph is being added (modulo 2) on the induced subgraph of the qubits
\forall i:a_{i}
with
a_{0}
as the center.

Let there be
n
qubits in the central node. Naively, it takes
n-1

CZ
gates (and time steps) to add the star graph. We claim that by using
k
auxiliary qubits
q_{0},q_{1}...q_{k-1}
in the central node, the star graph can be added in
O(n/k+\log(k))
time steps. Since steps 2 and 4 of the subgraph complementation protocol both take one time step, this reduces the asymptotic runtime for carrying out an
SC
operation to the time required to add a star graph (in steps 1 and 3). The protocol is described as follows.

Create a

. This takes

(b)

Add
(k-1)

through the doubling protocol described in section

. This can be done on any subset of size
(k-1)
of qubits
a_{i}
.

(c)

Repeat step (b)
n/(k-1)

LC(q_{0})
instead of
LC(a_{0})
.

VI-C

Analysis

Let
T(G)
denote the time required by the parallelized protocol to distribute a graph state
\left|G\right\rangle
. Denote by
t(n)
the time required for a single subgraph complementation on
n
vertices,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Steps 1 and 4 of the above protocol take
O(\log n)
time. Steps 2 and 3 take
O(n/k)
time. The other steps in the original subgraph complementation protocol take constant time. Thus, with
(n+k)
qubits in the central node,

t(n)=O\left(\frac{n}{k}+\log(k)\right).

Choosing
k=n/\log(n),

t(n)=O(\log(n)).

Consequently,

T(G)=\mathbb{c}_{2}(G)\cdot t(\overline{n})=O(n\log(n))\,.

(3)

We can show that a tighter bound holds with high probability for the distribution of
random
graphs. The minimum rank of random graph over the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is bounded according to the following theorem
[
18
]
.

Theorem VI.1
.

For an Erdős-Rényi random graph
\mathcal{G}_{n,p}
, and any constant
0<p<1
, it holds with high probability that

\mathbb{c}_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{n,p})=\Theta\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right).

Thus, since
t(n)=O(\log n)
by the prior result, we have with high probability that

T(\mathcal{G}_{n,p})=\mathbb{c}_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{n,p})\cdot t(\overline{n})=O\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)\cdot O(\log n)=O(n)\,.

(4)

for a random graph
\mathcal{G}_{n,p}
.

VII
Graph State Generation in the Presence of Noise

In this section, we present numerical results comparing our graph state distribution algorithm to the algorithm proposed by Avis et al.
[
8
]
, which is closest to ours in terms of problem setting and resource consumption
1
1

1

All the code used to carry out simulations, including programs to create the graphs in this sections, are publicly available at
https://github.com/Anisen123/graph-state-simulator
.
.

To carry out our simulations, we model noise through depolarizing channels. The action of a depolarizing channel on a state
\rho
can be expressed by
[
19
]
:

\mathcal{E}(\rho)\xrightarrow{}(1-p)\rho+\frac{p}{3}(X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z)\,.

(5)

This is equivalent to applying
X,Y
or
Z
gates on
\rho
with equal probability
p/3
and the identity
I
with probability
(1-p)
. Each time an operation (
LC
or
CZ
) is carried out on some qubit
q_{i}
with state
\rho_{i}
, we model noise by applying
5
on the qubit. Thus, the graph state
\left|G\right\rangle
is transformed as follows:

\left|G\right\rangle\xrightarrow{}P_{i}\left|G\right\rangle\,,

where the Pauli operator
P_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z,I\}
acts on the state
\rho_{i}
and
P_{i}
is selected according to probabilities described above. We can also express
P_{i}\left|G\right\rangle
as

P_{i}\left|G\right\rangle=Z^{b}\left|G\right\rangle\,,

for some bitstring
b=b_{1}b_{2}...b_{n}
, where
Z
is applied on the
i
’th qubit iff
b_{i}=1
, since the action of any Pauli operator on
q_{i}
can be mapped to
Z
gates acting on some set of qubits in the graph state
[
20
]
. Thus, we can now express all noise acting on
\left|G\right\rangle
as a single bitstring of
Z
operators acting on
\left|G\right\rangle
since
Z
gates commute. This makes the noise easier to characterize and store in a list data structure. We estimate the fidelity by running this simulation several times (between 5000 and 10000 times) and finding the fraction of states where
b
is the all zero bitstring. Note that the probability that
b
is the all zero bitstring corresponds to the fidelity of the total mixed state. This is because the set of all possible states of the form
Z^{b}\left|G\right\rangle
corresponding to
2^{n}
possible linearly independent
Z
-bitstrings are all orthogonal to each other.

The red line in each simulation graph indicates an arbitrarily chosen fidelity of 0.75, which serves as a reference line or a visual aid to compare fidelities between different graphs.

VIII
Conclusion

We have presented a novel and efficient protocol for distributing an arbitrary graph state across a quantum network by drawing upon the mathematical theory of
subgraph complementations
, which we show to be strongly linked to the entanglement and structure of the graph state. We show, through simulations, that this protocol generates graph states having high fidelity in the presence of noisy operations. This algorithm also provides a natural framework to create a graph state in a distributed fashion, since subgraph complementations commute and it may be possible to implement several
SC
operations in parallel (since their order does not matter) and merge the subgraphs at the end.

This could also be extended to a procedure for distributing arbitrary stabilizer states, since graph states are equivalent to stabilizer states up to local operations. We anticipate that the framework of subgraph complementations will be useful for other applications as well, for instance quantum error correction and secret sharing. Further work includes incorporating error correction in estimating runtime and fidelity through noisy simulations of our algorithm, considering the detailed impact of memory decoherence, as well as optimizing our protocol for more domain-specific purposes.
