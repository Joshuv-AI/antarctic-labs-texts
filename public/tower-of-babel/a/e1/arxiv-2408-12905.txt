On the relation between likelihood ratios and p p -values for testing success probabilities of Bernoulli trials
Wouter Kager, Ronald Meester

Abstract
It is well known that there is no direct one-to-one relation between p p -values and likelihood ratios or Bayes factors, since their relation crucially involves the sample size n n . We investigate their (asymptotic) relation in a coin-tossing context where the hypotheses of interest address the success probability of the coin, and where detailed computations are possible. This leads to useful insights in the nature of p p -values and likelihood ratios. Our results imply, for instance, that under mild conditions, a p p -value of 0.05 0.05 cannot correspond to a likelihood ratio larger than 7.5 7.5 , for any hypothesis versus a null hypothesis that the success probability has a specific value. We also show it is unlikely one can obtain a large likelihood ratio by tossing a fair coin until the number of heads deviates from the mean by several standard deviations.



Key words and phrases
:
p
-value; likelihood ratio; Bayesian
analysis; five sigma; evidential value; asymptotics.

1
Introduction

It is well known that
p
-values cannot be easily interpreted as a
quantification of statistical evidence, despite the fact that this is still
common practice. In
Briggs, (2016)
;
Meester and Slooten, (2020)
;
Meester and Slooten, (2021)
;
Royall, (1996)
, and in
many other references therein, it is argued that a (very) small
p
-value does
not at all rule out that the null hypothesis is in fact the best explanation
of the observed data. Indeed, the fact that the
p
-value is small only means
that the data (or more extreme outcomes) have a small probability under that
hypothesis, but this could be the case for any rivaling hypotheses as well. It
is therefore logically unjustified to dismiss the null hypothesis only because
the
p
-value is small. Moreover, the mere fact that
p
-values depend on the
probabilities of unobserved outcomes means they cannot be a proper measure of
the strength of the evidence against the null hypothesis, as is for instance
pointed out in
Meester and Slooten, (2021)
;
Royall, (1996)
.

A likelihood paradigm is often suggested as an alternative (
Royall, (1996)
). In
that paradigm, one compares the probabilities of the observed data under two
competing hypotheses. The ratio of these values is called the likelihood
ratio, or Bayes factor, depending on the circumstances and one’s philosophy.
The likelihood ratio (or Bayes factor) tells us which of the two hypotheses
explains the data best, and by what factor. As such, it quantifies the
evidence the data contains in favor of one hypothesis over another, and is a
genuinely relative notion.

Before we continue and to avoid confusion, a brief discussion of our
terminology is called for. We use the term
hypothesis
both for a
‘point’ hypothesis which fixes the value(s) of the parameter(s), and for the
situation in which the parameters are thought to follow a certain probability
distribution. This is consistent with, e.g.,
Wagenmakers, (2022)
and the
general use of hypotheses in forensic science, see
Meester and Slooten, (2020)
.
For example, in a coin-flip context with unknown success probability
\theta
, we
will speak of the hypothesis that
\theta
equals
\frac{1}{2}
, but also of the
hypothesis that
\theta
follows a uniform distribution on
(0,1)
. This is a
matter of nomenclature only, and our results could be formulated in other
statistical philosophies as well.

We use the term
likelihood ratio
not only for the case where all
parameters in the hypotheses are fixed, but also when these are described by a
probability distribution and integrated over under either hypothesis. The
latter is often called a
Bayes factor
in the statistical literature,
but
Edwards et al., (1963)
for instance use the term
likelihood ratio
for
both cases as well. Again, this is a matter of convention only. From our point
of view, the likelihood ratio obtained by an observer will depend on the
observer’s knowledge and understanding of the parameters and is conditional on
that, rather than on the parameters themselves. The case in which this
knowledge leads to a point distribution is in this philosophy a special case.
We refer to
Royall, (1996)
and
Meester and Slooten, (2021)
for gentle introductions to the use
of likelihood ratios.

Our investigation was initially inspired and motivated by the habit of
theoretical particle physicists to claim a particle discovery if the data lead
to a “five-sigma”
p
-value (see, e.g.,
Lyons, (2013)
and further references
therein). The “five sigma” here refers to a
p
-value that would be obtained
if an observation of a random variable with a normal distribution were at
least five standard deviations away from the mean. This boils down to a
numerical value of roughly
3\times 10^{-7}
.

However, even a five-sigma
p
-value does not necessarily imply that the
relevant likelihood ratios are convincingly large or small. It is very
important how this five sigma was obtained. To be more precise, it is
essential to know how many experiments were done, and also why. These warnings
are well established, of course, but here we look at them from the perspective
of the asymptotic relation between
p
-values and likelihood ratios.

We do not work in the extremely complicated world of elementary particle
physics here, but in the conveniently controlled environment of coin tosses,
where explicit asymptotic results can be derived. That is, we consider
n

coin tosses using a coin with unknown success probability
\theta
, and we want to
make an inference about the value of
\theta
.

In this article, we derive explicit (asymptotic) relations between likelihood
ratios and
p
-values in a coin-tossing context. It is well known that there
is no direct one-to-one correspondence between
p
-values and likelihood
ratios, as their relation involves the sample size
n
, see, e.g.,

Wagenmakers, (2022)
;
Sellke et al., (2001)
, and the many references therein. We
investigate their relation in various situations, varying the hypotheses
involved and also the experimental design. Some of our results are known (we
give references in such cases), but are deduced in a new way and in a
different context. Others (the three theorems and some other results) are new
as far as we know.

Our analysis also yields some interesting numerical results. For example, we
show that if
n
is not too small, a
p
-value of
0.05
cannot correspond to
a likelihood ratio larger than
7.5
for
any
hypothesis versus a null
hypothesis that
\theta
has a specific value.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present our
main results. We defer the technical proofs of those results to
Section
3
, and we close with a brief discussion of our
conclusions in Section
4
.

2
Main results

2.1
Uniform versus fair success probability

We start by comparing the hypothesis
H_{1}
that the unknown success
probability
\theta
is uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
to the hypothesis
H_{2}:\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}
that the coin is fair. Suppose we toss the coin
n
times and
obtain
k
heads and
n-k
tails. The likelihood ratio of the data, relative
to the two hypotheses, is the ratio of the probabilities of the data under
each hypothesis, namely

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}=\frac{P(\text{data}\mid H_{1})}{P(\text{data}\mid H_{2})}=\frac{\binom{n}{k}\,\int_{0}^{1}\theta^{k}\,(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,d\theta}{\binom{n}{k}\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}}.

(1)

Indeed, under
H_{1}
the unknown success probability
\theta
is claimed to be
uniformly distributed over
(0,1)
, so we condition on
\theta
and integrate over
the density (which is identically equal to 1) to arrive at the expression in
the numerator. We remark that it is not important whether our data consist of
the precise sequence of coin tosses or only of the number of heads and tails.
In the former case, the factor
\binom{n}{k}
disappears from both the
numerator and the denominator, which leads to the same likelihood ratio.

Under
H_{2}
, the number of heads has mean
\tfrac{1}{2}n
with a standard deviation
of
\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}
. We now implicitly define a new parameter
u
, given
n

and
k
, via

k=\tfrac{1}{2}n-\tfrac{1}{2}u\sqrt{n},\qquad n-k=\tfrac{1}{2}n+\tfrac{1}{2}u\sqrt{n}.

The hereby introduced parameter
u
measures how many standard deviations our
observation is away from the mean under
H_{2}
. This will turn out to be a
useful parametrization. Our interest lies in the behavior of the likelihood
ratio as a function of
n
and
u
, with an emphasis on large
n
.

In Section
3.1
we prove the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Choose
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. If the inequality
\lvert u\rvert\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{n}

holds, then

-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{11}{10n}\leq\ln\biggl(\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}\,\sqrt{\frac{2n}{\pi}}\,\,\biggr)-\frac{u^{2}}{2}-\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{12}\leq\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}{9}.

The takeaway from Theorem
1
is that if
n
is sufficiently
large given the outcome
u
of the experiment, then a good approximation
for
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
is

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}.

(2)

Here, what one considers a “good” approximation depends on personal taste
and is reflected in one’s choice of the parameter
\varepsilon
in the theorem. We
see from Equation (
2
) that the relation between
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
and
u

(and therefore between
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
and the
p
-value under
H_{2}
, as we explain
below) crucially involves
n
. We need both
n
and
u
to compute
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
, and
hence to assess the relative evidence the data contain for either hypothesis.

We now provide some intuition behind Equation (
2
). As we show in
Section
3.1
, every outcome
k
is equally likely under a
uniformly distributed parameter
\theta
. Therefore,
u
is roughly uniformly
distributed over
(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}\,)
under
H_{1}
, while under
H_{2}
,
u

is approximately standard normal. Dividing the uniform density
over
(-\sqrt{n},\sqrt{n}\,)
by
\varphi(u)
, the standard normal density in
the point
u
, reproduces Equation (
2
). For fixed
n
, the
likelihood ratio increases with
u
because the outcome becomes increasingly
unlikely under
H_{2}
. Conversely, if we fix
u
, then the likelihood ratio
decreases with
n
because the outcome becomes increasingly unlikely
under
H_{1}
.

Next we consider
p
-values. The 2-sided
p
-value under
H_{2}
depends only
on
u
(approximately, for large enough
n
). It is given by

p\approx 2\,\Phi(-\lvert u\rvert\,),

(3)

where
\Phi
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard
normal distribution. We show in Section
3.1
that

p\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi u^{2}}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}},

(4)

and that this leads to the following relation between
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
and
p
:

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}\approx\frac{1}{p\,\sqrt{-2n\ln p}}.

(5)

Clearly, there is no direct one-to-one correspondence between the likelihood
ratio and the
p
-value, since Equation (
5
) involves the
sample size
n
.

\theta

\tfrac{1}{2}
. However, we

2.3

) is not new. It also arises in the context of a

(where one must take the constant

be be

. Based on this formula, Wagenmakers proposed to use

3p\sqrt{n}
as an approximation to the inverse likelihood ratio
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}^{-1}
.

) suggests that this “
3p\sqrt{n}
rule” ought
to be replaced by the rule
p\sqrt{-2n\ln p}
, and an even better

. Figure

Remark
.

Equations (
2
)–(
5
) are only asymptotically
accurate, in the sense that the ratios of the left- and right-hand sides
of these equations tend to 1 in the limit
n\to\infty
, followed in the
case of Equations (
4
) and (
5
) by the limit

u\to\infty
or
p\downarrow 0
. The reader desiring better approximations
for moderate values of
n
and
u
can apply the usual continuity
correction for the binomial distribution by setting
v=\lvert u\rvert-\penalty n^{-1/2}
and replacing Equations (
3
)–(
5
) by

p\approx 2\,\Phi(-v)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi v^{2}}}\,\frac{v^{2}+1}{v^{2}+2}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}};

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}\approx\frac{\exp\bigl(\sqrt{-\ln(\pi p^{2}/2)/n}\,\bigr)}{p\sqrt{-n\ln(\pi p^{2}/2)}}.

2.2
Comparing two point hypotheses

In this section we turn to the case of comparing two given values of the
unknown success probability. Let
\theta_{0}\in(0,1)
be given, and let
H_{0}
be
the hypothesis that
\theta
equals
\theta_{0}
. Set
\bar{\theta}_{0}=1-\penalty\theta_{0}
and
c_{0}=(\theta_{0}\bar{\theta}_{0})^{1/2}
. Suppose we toss the coin
n
times, and obtain

k
successes. We write
k=\theta_{0}n-\penalty u\sigma_{n}
, where
\sigma_{n}=c_{0}\sqrt{n}
is the standard deviation corresponding to
\theta_{0}
. Then we have

n-\penalty k=\bar{\theta}_{0}n+\penalty u\sigma_{n}
, and
u
measures how many
standard deviations the outcome is away from the mean if the success
probability were in fact
\theta_{0}
.

We would like to compare
H_{0}
with the hypothesis that
\theta
has some other
specific value. To do this, it turns out to be computationally convenient to
reparametrize the situation, and consider the likelihood ratio

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}=\frac{P(\text{data}\mid H_{x})}{P(\text{data}\mid H_{0})}

relative to the hypotheses
H_{0}
and

H_{x}:\theta=(k-x\sigma_{n})/n,

for varying values of
x
. In Section
3.2
we prove two
principal results concerning the likelihood ratio
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}
, which we formulate
as Theorems
2
and
3
below.

In Theorems
2
and
3
,
m_{0}
denotes the
largest of the two parameters
\theta_{0}
and
\bar{\theta}_{0}
. The fact that
\theta_{0}
need not
be
\tfrac{1}{2}
introduces a fundamental asymmetry in the problem. To deal with
this, we state the two theorems under the assumption that
u
is positive.
This simplifies the formulation of the results without losing generality, as
the case of negative
u
can be turned into that of a positive
u
by
interchanging the roles of heads and tails.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
u
is positive and choose
\varepsilon\in(0,2/5)
. If the inequality

m_{0}u\leq\varepsilon\sigma_{n}
holds, then for all
x
in the
range
(-\tfrac{3}{2}u,\tfrac{3}{2}u)
we have

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}\leq\exp\Bigl(\,\frac{u^{2}-x^{2}}{2}+\lvert\bar{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0}\rvert\,\frac{25u^{2}\varepsilon}{12m_{0}}+\frac{1}{12}\,\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\,\Bigr);\kern 10.00002pt

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}\geq\exp\Bigl(\,\frac{u^{2}-x^{2}}{2}-\lvert\bar{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0}\rvert\,\frac{25u^{2}\varepsilon}{12m_{0}}-\frac{297}{32}\,\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{2-5\varepsilon}\,\Bigr).

Moreover, if either
\theta_{0}\leq\bar{\theta}_{0}
or else
250\,(\theta_{0}-\bar{\theta}_{0})\,\varepsilon\leq 12m_{0}
holds as well, then we have
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}\leq\exp(-u^{2}/2)
for all
x

outside the range
(-\tfrac{3}{2}u,\tfrac{3}{2}u)
.

This theorem tells us that if
n
is sufficiently large given the values of

u
and
\theta_{0}
, then for all practical purposes we may use the following
approximation for
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}
as a function of
x
:

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}\approx\exp\bigl(\,\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}\,\bigr).

(6)

In particular, the theorem gives us conditions under which we can use this
approximation to integrate
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}
with respect to a probability distribution
for
x
, which is what we will do in the next section. These conditions are
relatively mild when
\theta_{0}
is close to
\tfrac{1}{2}
, but can require a very large
sample size for extreme values of
\theta_{0}
. It may appear at first sight that
Equation (
6
) itself does not contain
n
and
\theta_{0}
, but this
is due to the parametrization we used. Indeed,
x
measures the deviation from
the rescaled outcome
k/n
in terms of
\sigma_{n}
(which depends on
\theta_{0}

and
n
) and
n
itself.

Expression (
6
) is rather elegant, and illustrates the
usefulness of the chosen parametrization. It makes mathematically precise a
phenomenon that was mentioned already in
Edwards et al., (1963)
, namely that the
likelihood ratio as a function of
\theta
becomes sharply peaked around the
observation of the experiment. Indeed, via
\theta=(k-\penalty x\sigma_{n})/n
, we
see that in terms of
\theta
, the graph of the likelihood ratio is shaped like a
normal distribution with mean
k/n
and standard deviation
\sigma_{n}/n=c_{0}\,/\sqrt{n}
. The likelihood ratio is of course maximal for
x=0
(or
equivalently,
\theta=k/n
), because this provides the best explanation of the
data, per construction.

Equation (
6
) also seems to suggest that
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}
is bounded
above by
\exp(u^{2}/2)
for all values of
x
. It was shown in

Edwards et al., (1963)
that this is indeed a uniform upper bound on the likelihood
ratio in the case of a normally distributed observation, but in the binomial
case we are dealing with here this is only approximately true. Our next
theorem provides a uniform upper bound in our context which is sharper than
the one in Theorem
2
:

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
u
is positive and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. If
m_{0}u\leq\varepsilon\sigma_{n}

holds, then

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}\leq\exp\Bigl(\,\frac{u^{2}}{2}+(\bar{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\,\frac{u^{2}\varepsilon}{6m_{0}}+\frac{u^{2}}{12}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\,\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all~$x$}.

says in this case that

,

.

2.3
Generic likelihood ratio bounds

The likelihood ratio for any alternative hypothesis versus
H_{0}
can in
principle be computed by integrating the ratio
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{x}
with respect to a
distribution for
x
. As we have explained in the previous section,
Theorem
2
gives us conditions under which we may use the
approximation in Equation (
6
) in such computations. These
conditions boil down to making sure
n
is sufficiently large given the values
of
u
and
\theta_{0}
, and throughout this section we work under the assumption
that this is the case and hence that Equation (
6
) can be used.

In practice, the alternative hypothesis will typically be formulated in terms
of a distribution for the parameter
\theta
rather than for
x
. So suppose
H_{f}

is the hypothesis that
\theta
has density
f(\theta)
. We show in Section
3.3
that via
\theta=(k-\penalty x\sigma_{n})/n
and
\sigma_{n}=c_{0}\sqrt{n}
, and using Equation (
6
), it then follows that the
likelihood ratio for
H_{f}
versus
H_{0}
can be computed via

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{f}\approx\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}\int f\Bigl(\,\frac{k}{n}-x\,\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\Bigr)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\,dx.

(7)

For the uniform distribution on
(0,1)
, for example, this gives

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{f=1_{(0,1)}}\approx\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\,dx=c_{0}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}},

(8)

which for
\theta_{0}=c_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}
reproduces Equation (
2
), as it
should.

We will now study generic bounds on the likelihood ratio that follow from
Equation (
7
) by restricting our attention to certain classes of
distributions
f
. To begin, we consider those densities
f
under which the
probability that the distance between
\theta
and the outcome
k/n
is at most

c_{0}\lvert u\rvert\,/\sqrt{n}
, is bounded above by
C\,\lvert u\rvert\,/\sqrt{n}
for
some constant
C>0
. Note that this includes all densities which are bounded
above by the constant
C/(2c_{0})
in the mentioned interval around
k/n
. We
show in Section
3.3
that for all such densities we have

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{f}\lesssim 1+C\,\frac{\lvert u\rvert}{\sqrt{n}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}.

(9)

This confirms that for a wide range of alternative hypotheses, the likelihood
ratio is in general much smaller than the value
\exp(u^{2}/2)
shown in
Table
3
, as we claimed in the previous section. It once again
points out the danger of interpreting the
p
-value as a measure of evidence
against
H_{0}
.

Next we consider the family of alternative hypotheses under which
\theta
follows
a normal distribution with mean
\theta_{0}
and a fixed standard deviation
s
.
Here, we assume
s
is allowed to vary over a reasonable range given the value
of
\theta_{0}
, such as the range
(0,1-\penalty\tfrac{1}{2}m_{0})
. As we show in
Section
3.3
, for the normal density
f
with
parameters
(\theta_{0},s^{2})
, Equation (
7
) yields

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{s}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tau_{n}^{2}}}\,\exp\Bigl(\,\frac{u^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}}{1+\tau_{n}^{2}}\,\Bigr),

(10)

where
\tau_{n}^{2}=ns^{2}/c_{0}^{2}
. One thing we can now do is maximize the
right-hand side of Equation (
10
) over
s
. This reproduces what
we call the
Edwards bound

\mathit{EB}
, first derived in

Edwards et al., (1963)
for a normal observation, namely

\sup_{s}\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{s}\approx\mathit{EB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{eu^{2}}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}.

(11)

Using the same argument which lead from Equation (
2
) to
Equation (
5
), we show in Section
3.3

that in terms of the
p
-value, the Edwards bound satisfies

\mathit{EB}\approx-\frac{1}{p\ln(\pi p^{2}/2)\sqrt{\pi e/2}}

(12)

for small
p
. A similar generic upper bound on the likelihood ratio is the
bound
(-ep\ln p)^{-1}
proposed by
Sellke et al., (2001)
and
Benjamin and Berger, (2019)
,
and considered first by
Vovk, (1993)
. Equation (
12
) shows that the
two bounds are of similar magnitude, the Edwards bound being the smaller of
the two. In fact, our analysis can be used to prove that the ratio of the two
bounds converges to the value
\sqrt{e/(2\pi)}\approx 0.66
in the limit

p\downarrow 0
.

Researchers should treat these upper bounds with the same wariness as

p
-values. Indeed, the likelihood ratio bounds and the
p
-value carry the
same information, as the relation between them is bijective and does not
involve the sample size
n
. Hence, large values of these bounds must not be
mistaken for a sign of strong evidence against the null hypothesis.

An example makes this clear. We consider two researchers A and B who each toss

The reason this happens is the maximization procedure used to derive the

We conclude this section with a brief digression to an amusing example leading
to a Bayesian interpretation of the
p
-value. Fix
\theta_{0}\in(0,1)
as before,
and choose
\alpha
in
(0,1-\penalty m_{0})
. Let

and
H^{-}_{\alpha}

be the respective hypotheses that
\theta
is uniform in
(\theta_{0},\theta_{0}+\penalty\alpha)
or in
(\theta_{0}-\penalty\alpha,\theta_{0})
. We show in Section

that

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{\alpha}=\frac{P(\text{data}\mid H^{+}_{\alpha})}{P(\text{data}\mid H^{-}_{\alpha})}\approx\frac{\Phi(-u)}{1-\Phi(-u)}.

(13)

Here, for

, we recognize
\Phi(-u)
as the 1-sided

-value for testing
the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. So we have found a one-to-one relation, which does
not involve

, between the
p
-value and a likelihood ratio, after all. But
this particular likelihood ratio does not consider the null hypothesis at all,
and provides a rather unexpected Bayesian interpretation of the
p
-value.

2.4
Likelihood ratio upon optional stopping

In this section we investigate how likely it is that by optional stopping we
can obtain a small
p
-value, or a large likelihood ratio against the null
hypothesis that the coin is fair, if the coin is in fact fair. That is, in
order to obtain a small
p
-value, we want to toss the coin repeatedly until
the number of heads deviates from the mean by, say, 3 to 5 standard
deviations. The first question we ask is: How likely is it that after a finite
number of tosses
n
the number of heads
k
will satisfy
\lvert k-\penalty\tfrac{1}{2}n\rvert\geq\tfrac{1}{2}c\,\sqrt{n}
, for some given
c\geq 1
measuring the
deviation from the mean in standard deviations?

We must be careful here. It is well known that runs of consecutive heads or
tails will occur. As a consequence, we may actually get lucky and achieve the
desired deviation already after a small number of coin flips. To avoid such
“beginner’s luck”, we assume that the coin is always tossed at least a
decent number of times (several hundred, say). We call that number
m
. The
question then becomes how likely it is we achieve a “
c
-sigma” deviation
(i.e.,
c
standard deviations from the mean) within a finite number of tosses

after
first having tossed the coin
m
times.

This is not a difficult question. The classical Law of the Iterated Logarithm
tells us that we are
certain
to obtain a
c
-sigma deviation (and hence
a
p
-value as small as we like) at some point, for any positive
c
. However,
the expected number of coin flips required is infinite for any
c\geq 1
, as
was shown several decades ago; see
Breiman, (1967)
;
Shepp, (1967)
. This raises
the question what we can say about likelihood ratios upon reaching a
c
-sigma
deviation.

To answer this question, we consider the likelihood ratio from Section
2.1
addressing the hypothesis that
\theta
is distributed uniformly
on
(0,1)
and the null hypothesis that the coin is fair. The value of this
likelihood ratio at the moment we first reach
c
standard deviations from the
mean is a random quantity, because it depends on the random number of coin
tosses required. In Section
3.4
we argue that the
expectation of this quantity is given approximately by the surprisingly simple
formula

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{1+c^{2}}{c}.

(14)

We see that the expected likelihood ratio, at the moment we achieve a

c
-sigma deviation, is quite small. This should not come as a surprise given
that we worked under the assumption that the coin is fair. It is reassuring,
however, that even this optional stopping procedure is unlikely to produce a
large likelihood ratio against the null hypothesis, even though it is
guaranteed to achieve a
c
-sigma deviation, and hence a very small
p
-value.

3.1
Proofs for Section
2.1

We start by deriving some preliminary bounds.
Robbins, (1955)
has proven
the following version of Stirling’s formula, valid for all
n\geq 1

\sqrt{n}\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{12n+1}\Bigr)\leq\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{e^{n}}{n^{n}}\leq\sqrt{n}\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{12n}\Bigr).

Since by Equation (
15
) we also have

\frac{1}{2}\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{5n}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{5n+1}\geq\frac{1}{12n}\quad\text{if $n\geq 1$},

it follows that for all
n
(including
n=0
if we put

),

satisfies

\sqrt{\vphantom{/}n}\leq\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{e^{n}}{n^{n}}\leq\sqrt{n+1/5}.

(16)

Next, consider the function

f(z)=\begin{cases}(1-z)\ln(1-z)+(1+z)\ln(1+z)&z\in(-1,1);\\
2\ln 2&z=-1,1.\end{cases}

From the MacLaurin series of

we obtain

f(z)=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\,\frac{1}{2k-1}-\frac{1}{2k}\,\Bigr)z^{2k}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2k}}{2k\,(2k-1)}.

Hence, using Abel’s theorem, we conclude that

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle\geq z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}z^{4};

(17)

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle\leq z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}z^{4}+\bigl(2\ln 2-1-\tfrac{1}{6}\bigr)z^{6}\leq z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}z^{4}+\tfrac{2}{9}z^{6}.

(18)

We are now ready to move on to the proof proper of Theorem
1

Using the fact that the beta function

Substituting this result into Equation (
1

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}=\frac{k!\,(n-k)!}{n!}\,\frac{2^{n}}{n+1}.

(19)

Applying the bounds from Equation (
16
) to the factorials in
Equation (

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
which are of

\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{n+1}\,\sqrt{\frac{(k+a)(n-k+a)}{n+b}}\,\frac{2^{n}}{n^{n}}\,k^{k}\,(n-k)^{n-k}.

To be more precise, this expression is an upper bound on
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}
if we fill in

a=1/5
,
b=0
, and a lower bound if we take
a=0
,
b=1/5
.

We now set

, so that

This allows us to rewrite our bounds in terms of

and the function

as

\sqrt{\frac{\pi\vphantom{()}}{2n\vphantom{()}}}\,\sqrt{\frac{(1+2a/n)^{2}-z^{2}}{(1+1/n)^{2}\,(1+b/n)}}\,\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}nf(z)\bigr).

For

and

, the middle square-root term is bounded above by 1,
hence by Equation (

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\,\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}nz^{2}\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{6}z^{2}+\tfrac{2}{9}z^{4}\bigr)\bigr).

For the lower bound we substitute
a=0
,
b=1/5
and obtain

\begin{split}\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}&\geq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\,\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}nf(z)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-z^{2})-\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\ln\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{5n}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&\geq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\,\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}nz^{2}\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{6}z^{2}\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}}-\frac{11}{10n}\Bigr),\end{split}

where the second step uses Equations (
15
) and (
17
).
Theorem
1
follows.

. We work under the assumption that

m_{0}u/\sigma_{n}\leq\varepsilon<2/5
holds. Then the upper bound in

g(y)\leq g\bigl(\tfrac{5}{2}u\bigr)\leq h\bigl(\tfrac{5}{2}u\bigr)+\frac{u^{2}}{45}.

From Equation (

than
-\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}
when

is at least
\theta_{0}
. Furthermore, for
\bar{\theta}_{0}

smaller than
\theta_{0}
we have

g(y)\leq-\frac{5u^{2}}{8}+(\theta_{0}-\bar{\theta}_{0})\,\frac{25u^{2}\varepsilon}{12m_{0}}+\frac{u^{2}}{45}.

If the second term on the right is at most

, then the whole right-hand

Let

3.4
Proofs for Section
2.4

Let
N
denote the random number of coin tosses (including the first, fixed
number of
m
tosses) until the number of heads first deviates by at least
c

standard deviations from its mean. If we stop tossing at that point, then
according to Equation (
2
) we have obtained a likelihood ratio
given by

\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{N}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}c^{2}}.

This is a random variable determined by
N
, and we are interested in its
mean, and therefore in computing
E(N^{-1/2})
.

The discrete nature of the problem makes this computation hard, but we can
obtain a decent approximation (if
m
is large) by considering the analogous
stopping problem for Brownian motion. That is, let
W_{t}
be standard Brownian
motion and set

T:=\inf\bigl\{\,t\geq m:\lvert W_{t}\rvert\geq c\sqrt{t}\,\bigr\}.

Then
E(T^{-1/2})
should be a good approximation to
E(N^{-1/2})
. The
Brownian motion case was studied in
Shepp, (1967)
. Shepp showed that

E(\,T^{\mu}\mid W_{m}=0)=\frac{m^{\mu}}{M\bigl(-\mu,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}c^{2}\bigr)},

where
M(a,b,z)
is Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric function.

This result unfortunately involves conditioning on
W_{m}
being zero, which in
our coin-tossing setting means conditioning on the number of heads after the
initial
m
tosses being equal to its mean. However, it turns out that Shepp’s
calculation can be modified to compute the unconditional
expectation
E(T^{\mu})
as well. We omit the details, but we claim that by
following the steps of Shepp it can be shown that

E(T^{\mu})=m^{\mu}\,\biggl(\,2\,\Phi(-c)+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{M\bigl(1-\mu,\frac{3}{2},\frac{1}{2}c^{2}\bigr)}{M\bigl(-\mu,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}c^{2}\bigr)}\,c\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}}\,\biggr).

(33)

For positive
\mu
the function
M(-\mu,\tfrac{1}{2},z)
has a positive root, which
means that
E(T^{\mu})
is finite only if
c
is sufficiently small. To be
precise, it follows from tabulated values of the smallest positive root
of
M(a,b,z)
that
E(T)
is finite only if
c<1
, while
E(T^{1/2})
is
finite only if
c<1.3069

(
Abramowitz and Stegun,, 1965
, Table 13.2)
. The same
conclusions then hold for
E(N)
and
E(N^{1/2})
if
T
is comparable to the
number of coin tosses
N
in our discrete setting. Justification for this is
given in
Breiman, (1967)
, where it is shown that in the conditional case
mentioned above, the random variables
T
and
N
do have the same tail
behavior.

We conclude that for
c
larger than 1.3069, the inverse likelihood
ratio
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{N}^{-1}
has infinite mean. As for the mean of
\mathit{L\mskip-1.0muR}_{N}
itself, we
note that in the case
\mu=-\tfrac{1}{2}
, the confluent hypergeometric functions
in Equation (
33
) simplify considerably
(
Abramowitz and Stegun,, 1965
, Section 13.6)
, which leads to

E(T^{-1/2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\Bigl(\,2\,\Phi(-c)+\sqrt{2/\pi}\,c\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}}\,\Bigr).

If we now assume
E(N^{-1/2})\approx E(T^{-1/2})
and approximate the term

2\,\Phi(-c)
using Equation (
4
), we obtain the expression in
Equation (
14
).

4
Discussion and conclusion

This contribution reinforces the well-known thesis that reporting
p
-values
without context is statistically rather dangerous. It has been observed, for
instance in
Ioannidis, (2005)
, that using
p
-values in an evidential role
easily gives rise to wrong results and claims. In the current article, we have
analyzed the relation between
p
-values and likelihood ratios in detail, and
we have concluded that they display very different asymptotic behavior in
terms of the observation
u
and sample size
n
. Our results not only confirm
that this is the case, but also shed some light on the underlying reasons.

Of course, our results were only obtained in the controlled environment of
coin flips, and they do not verbatim apply to other circumstances. However, we
note two things: First, the point we want to make is also philosophical in
nature. Although in different circumstances it may no longer be reasonable to
expect that exact expressions such as ours can be obtained, the fact that

p
-values and likelihood ratios scale differently will probably still hold;
there is no reason why they would not. Second, especially in applied sciences
like sociology, psychology, and medicine, the context is quite often one of
repeated experiments for which the coin-tossing context is entirely
appropriate.

There are therefore both philosophical and practical reasons to be very
cautious with the use of
p
-values. In particular, the fact that in the
coin-tossing context a
p
-value of 0.05 cannot correspond to a likelihood
ratio larger than 7.5 under mild conditions on the sample size should make all
researchers very suspicious about this methodology. The same caution should be
observed regarding the likelihood ratio (or Bayes factor) bounds that have
been proposed in the literature. As we have demonstrated, the connection
between the strength of the evidence as measured by a likelihood ratio and a

p
-value is non-trivial and rather complicated.
