Introduction

We first review some standard notation, and then describe our results.
The
Fibonacci numbers
are defined by

F(n)\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n=0\\
1&n=1\\
F(n-1)+F(n-2)&\ n\geq 2,\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

and have a closed form given by
Binet’s formula

F(n)\ =\ \frac{\varphi^{n}-\psi^{n}}{\sqrt{5}},

(1.2)

where

\varphi\ =\ \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\ =\ \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}\ =\ -\frac{1}{\varphi}.

This is the simplest depth two constant-coefficient recurrence to study (the simpler depth one recurrences are just pure geometric sequences). They satisfy numerous interesting identities and arise in various areas; see for example
[
Ko
]
. We consider the Fibonacci numbers and other recursively defined sequences of numbers. In particular, we are interested in
Pell numbers
and their generalizations; we state these sequences and then our results.

Definition 1.1
.

Classical Pell numbers
are defined by the following recurrence and initial conditions:

P(n)\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&n=0\\
1&n=1\\
2P(n-1)+P(n-2)&\ n\geq 2.\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

Definition 1.2
.

The
Pell-Lucas sequence
or the
Companion Pell sequence
is defined by

Q(n)\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}2&n=0\\
2&n=1\\
2Q(n-1)+Q(n-2)&\ n\geq 2.\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

Definition 1.3
.

The
Lucas sequence
is defined by

L(n)\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}2&n=0\\
1&n=1\\
L(n-1)+L(n-2)&\ n\geq 2.\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.5)

Definition 1.4
.

Every sequence defined by a homogeneous linear recurrence with constant coefficients has a closed-form expression. If the terms of such a sequence can be expressed in a closed form like (
1.2
), this is called
a generalized Binet formula
(see
[
BBILMT
,
Le
]
).

Example
.

Let

a\ :=\ 1+\sqrt{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b\ :=\ 1-\sqrt{2}\ =\ -\frac{1}{a}.

(1.6)

Then the
n
{\rm th}
Pell Number is given by the generalized Binet formula:

P(n)\ =\ \frac{a^{n}-b^{n}}{2\sqrt{2}}.

(1.7)

Definition 1.5
.

Throughout the paper, we let

1.1
Motivation and Results

We analyze the relationships between sums of consecutive numbers in recurrence sequences. The first theorem below is a generalization of an observation made by the third named author to the sixth named author (for a problem for the Pi Mu Epsilon Journal) for sums of eight consecutive terms.

Theorem 1.6
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the sum of
4N
consecutive Pell numbers is equal to a constant (depending on
N
) multiplied by the
(2N+1)
st
term of the consecutive terms. In particular, we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{4N-1}P(n+i)\ =\ \frac{(a^{2N}-b^{2N})}{\sqrt{2}}P(n+2N),

(1.8)

where

a\ =\ 1+\sqrt{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b\ =\ 1-\sqrt{2}\ =\ -\frac{1}{a}.

Proof.

The
n
{\rm th}
Pell Number is given by (
1.7
):

P(n)\ =\ \frac{a^{n}-b^{n}}{2\sqrt{2}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{4N-1}P(n+i)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{4N-1}\frac{a^{n+i}-b^{n+i}}{2\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a^{n}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{4N-1}a^{i}-\frac{b^{n}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{4N-1}b^{i}

(1.9)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a^{n}}{4}(a^{4N}-1)-\frac{b^{n}}{4}(1-b^{4N})

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a^{n+2N}}{4}(a^{2N}-b^{2N})-\frac{b^{n+2N}}{4}(a^{2N}-b^{2N})

\displaystyle=\ \frac{(a^{2N}-b^{2N})}{4}(a^{n+2N}-b^{n+2N})

\displaystyle=\ \frac{(a^{2N}-b^{2N})}{\sqrt{2}}P(n+2N).

∎

The above result motivates the question:
For which numbers
n\in\mathbb{N}
does the sum of
n
consecutive Pell numbers equal a fixed integer multiple of another Pell number?

We answer this question for the Pell, Fibonacci and other related sequences. In particular, for the Pell sequence we observe that multiples of
4
(see Theorem
1.6
) and the trivial case of
N=1
are the only values of
N
that work. We then extend our methods to generalized Pell numbers and present a conjecture regarding when the sum of consecutive generalized Pell numbers equals a fixed integer multiple of another generalized Pell number. Additionally, we describe several interesting properties of Pell numbers using tilings of an
n\times 1
board with polyominoes.

Lemma 2.3
.

For the Fibonacci numbers, we have for all positive integers

\varphi^{n}\ =\ F(n)\varphi+F(n-1)\quad\text{ where }\ \quad\varphi\ =\ \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}.

3
Some General Results

One of our main goals is to determine not only when the sum of consecutive terms in a recurrence is a fixed multiple of a term of the recurrence, but further to determine which term. In this section we consider the following sequence.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
r
be a non-negative integer. Consider a sequence
\{f(n)\}
of non-negative integers recursively defined by

f(n)\ :=\ rf(n-1)+f(n-2)

with initial conditions so that it is not identically zero (we call this a non-degenerate sequence).

Not only is this a natural generalization of the Fibonacci numbers (which are just the
r=1
case), but similar to how the Fibonacci numbers count various objects, this sequence as well has a combinatorial interpretation. In
[
DHW
]
the authors show that
f(n)
is the number of
r
-regular words over
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
avoiding the patterns
122
and
213
(this means we cannot form a sub-word with three objects with this relative ordering). Additionally,
\left\{f(n)\right\}
also makes a surprising appearance in elliptic curve research. Recent work in
[
PiWa
]
shows under certain circumstances, there exists a bijection between the set of integral points on elliptic curves of the form
y^{2}=(r^{2}+4)x^{4}-4
and the set of squares in
\left\{f(n)\right\}
with odd indices.

If we set

\alpha\ :=\ \frac{r+\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}{2}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\beta\ :=\ \frac{r-\sqrt{r^{2}+4}}{2}

then the generalized Binet formula (see
[
BBILMT
,
Le
]
)) yields that there exist complex numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
f(n)\ =\ a\alpha^{n}+b\beta^{n}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix any integer
N>0
. If there is an integer
C(N)
such that for every sufficiently large
n
there exists an integer index
\,j(n;N)\,
such that the following equation holds:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}f(n+i)\ =\ C(N)\cdot f(j(n;N)),

then there is an integer
k(N)
such that

j(n;N)\ =\ n+k(N)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad k(N)\in\left[\frac{N}{2},N\right].

Proof.

Define

b\ :=\ \frac{\alpha^{N}-1}{C(N)(\alpha-1)}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad k(N)\ :=\ \log\limits_{\alpha}b,

with
\alpha,\ \beta
and
f
as above.

Note that
|\alpha|\ >\ |\beta|
and
|\beta|\ <\ 1
. Then by the generalized Binet’s formula

f(n)\ =\ a\alpha^{n}+b\beta^{n}

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}|f(n)-a\alpha^{n}|\ =\ 0.

This implies that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a natural number
M
such that for all
n>M
,

\displaystyle|f(n)-a\alpha^{n}|\ <\ \frac{C(N)\cdot\varepsilon}{2N}.

(3.1)

We choose
M
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle|f(j(n;N))-a\alpha^{j(n;N)}|\ <\ \frac{\varepsilon}{2}

(3.2)

for all
n>M
.

Then

\displaystyle\left|a\alpha^{j(n;N)}-\frac{1}{C(N)}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}a\alpha^{n+i}\right|

(3.3)

\displaystyle<\ \left|a\alpha^{j(n;N)}-f(j(n;N))\right|+\frac{1}{C(N)}\left|C(N)\cdot f(j(n;N))-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}a\alpha^{n+i}\right|

\displaystyle<\ \frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{1}{C(N)}\left|\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}f(n+i)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}a\alpha^{n+i}\right|

\displaystyle<\ \frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\ =\ \varepsilon.

We now have

\frac{1}{C(N)}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\alpha^{n+i}\ =\ \alpha^{n+\log_{\alpha}b}.

If
k(N)\not\in\mathbb{N}
then consider
m=\min\{n+k(N),j(n;N)\}
. The conditions on
f
imply that it is an increasing sequence, therefore
j(n;N)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Hence
m\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. We also note that

\displaystyle\left|a\alpha^{j(n;N)}-\frac{1}{C(N)}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}a\alpha^{n+i}\right|

\displaystyle\ =\ \left|a\right|\alpha^{m}\left|\alpha^{\left|j(n;N)-n-k(N)\right|}-1\right|

\displaystyle\ \geq\ \left|a\right|\alpha^{m}\left(\alpha^{\left|j(n;N)-n-k(N)\right|}-1\right),

(3.4)

and since
j(n;N)\in\mathbb{N}
we have
j(n;N)-n-k(N)\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. Similarly, since
\alpha>1
we have
\alpha^{\left|j(n;N)-n-k(N)\right|}-1>0
. Lastly,
m\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and thus for large enough
n
, the left hand side of (
3.4
) tends to infinity:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|a\alpha^{j(n;N)}-\frac{1}{C(N)}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}a\alpha^{n+i}\right|\ \to\ \infty,

which contradicts (
3.3
), implying that
k(N)\in\mathbb{N}
, and
j(n;N)=n+k(N)
.

We now prove that

k(N)\in\left[\frac{N}{2},N\right].

We begin by noting that

\frac{\alpha^{N}-1}{C(N)(\alpha-1)}\ =\ \alpha^{k(N)}\ \implies\ C(N)\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}\alpha^{i-k(N)}.

(3.5)

Let
k(N)<\frac{N}{2}
, then

C(N)\ =\ 1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k(N)}\left(\alpha^{i}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{i}}\right)+\sum\limits_{i=2k(N)+1}^{N-1}\alpha^{i-k(N)}.

Note that the coefficient of the irrational part of
\sum\limits_{i=2k(N)+1}^{N-1}\alpha^{i-k(N)}
is a positive integer. We now have

\alpha^{i}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{i}}\ =\ \alpha^{i}+(-\beta)^{i}.

(3.6)

Applying the binomial theorem to the above-mentioned equation gives the coefficient of the irrational part in
\alpha^{i}+(-\beta)^{i}
for
i>1
to be

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{i-1}{2}\rfloor}(r^{2}+4)(r^{i-2j+1}+(-r)^{i-2j+1})\ \geq\ 0,

which implies that
C(N)
is irrational, resulting in a contradiction. Therefore
k(N)\geq N/2
.

Now, by induction we get the following inequality:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n+N-1}f(i)\ <\ f(n+N+1),

which proves
k(N)\leq N
.

This implies that

j(n;N)\in\left[n+\frac{N}{2},n+N\right].

∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Given a non-degenerate sequence of non-negative integers recursively defined by

f(n)\ :=\ rf(n-1)+f(n-2),

where
r\in\mathbb{N}
, if

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{3}f(n+i)\ =\ C(N)\cdot f(j(n;N))

then
r=2
.

Proof.

For a proof, see Appendix
A.3
.
∎

Clearly, only looking at rational multiples of terms in the sequence is sufficient, because the desired multiple can be written as a ratio of integers. The following theorem proves why only looking at
integer
multiples of terms in the sequence is sufficient.

Theorem 3.4
.

Define
\{f(n)\}
by the recurrence relation

f(n)\ :=\ rf(n-1)+f(n-2)

where
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and choose initial conditions so that
f
is not identically zero. Then if the sum of
N>1
consecutive terms of
\{f(n)\}
is a fixed rational constant times another term in the sequence, then the rational constant is an integer.

Proof.

Let
d=\gcd(f(0),f(1))
. We notice that
d\mid f(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and therefore consider the equivalent sequence
h(n):=f(n)/d
instead.
Then

\gcd(h(n),h(n+1))\ =\ 1

for all
n\geq 1
since

\displaystyle\gcd(h(n),h(n+1))\

\displaystyle=\ \gcd(h(n),rh(n)+h(n-1))

\displaystyle=\ \gcd(h(n),h(n-1))

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle=\ \gcd(h(0),h(1)).

Now suppose

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}f(n+i)\ =\ \frac{a}{b}f(j(n;N)),

(3.7)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\gcd(a,b)=1
. Dividing both sides by
d
, we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}h(n+i)\ =\ \frac{a}{b}h(j(n;N)).

(3.8)

From Theorem
3.2
we know that there exists
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that
j(n;N)=n+k(N)
for
n>M
. Applying (
3.7
) tells us that
b\mid h(n+k(N))\ \mbox{for all }n>M
. However, if
b>1
we reach the contradiction that
\gcd(f(n),f(n+1))\neq 1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore
b=1
, which completes our proof.
∎

4
Pell numbers

In Theorem
1.6
, we proved that the sum of
4N
consecutive Pell numbers is a constant integer multiple of the
(2N+1)
st
term. We generalize to other related partial sums.

4.1
Sum of
4N+2
Consecutive Terms

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P(n)
denote the
n
{\rm th}
Pell number. Fix any integer
N>0
. There is no integer
C(N)
such that for every
n
there exists an integer index
j(n;N)
such that the following equation holds:

\sum_{i=0}^{2N}P(n+i)\ =\ C(N)P(j(n;N)).

We prove this theorem in greater generality. Consider sequences
f(n)
satisfying the recurrence relation

f(n)\ :=\ rf(n-1)+f(n-2),

such that
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
and
r\geq 2
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f
be as above, and fix any integer
N>0
. There is no integer
C(N)
such that for every
n
there exists an integer index
\,j(n;N)\,
such that the following equation holds:

\sum_{i=0}^{4N+1}f(n+i)\ =\ C(N)f(\,j(n;N)\,).

Proof.

Define the sequence
g(n)
by
g(0)=2
,
g(1)=r
and

g(n)\ :=\ rg(n-1)+g(n-2).

(4.1)

Using induction on
k
, we find

f(n+k)+(-1)^{k}f(n-k)\ =\ g(k)f(n).

(4.2)

Using (
7.10
) we get

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}f(n)\ =\ \frac{1}{r}\Big(f(n)+f(n+1)-1\Big).

(4.3)

Therefore, we have

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{4N+1}f(n+k)\ &=\ \sum\limits_{k=0}^{n+4N+1}f(k)-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(k)\\
&=\ \frac{1}{r}\Big[f(n+4N+1)+f(n+4N+2)-f(n-1)+f(n)\Big]\\
&=\ \frac{1}{r}\Big[g(2N+1)f(n+2N+1)+g(2N+1)f(n+2N)\Big]\\
&=\ g(2N+1)\frac{f(n+2N+1)+f(n+2N)}{r}.\end{split}

Further by induction on
N
we know that
\dfrac{g(2N+1)}{r}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now suppose the sum of
4N+2
terms is a fixed multiple of another term. Then for some
t_{1}\geq t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, the following equations hold:

\displaystyle sf(t_{1})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle g(2N+1)\frac{f(n+2N+2)+f(n+2N+1)}{r}

\displaystyle sf(t_{2})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle g(2N+1)\frac{f(n+2N+1)+f(n+2N)}{r}.

(4.4)

Dividing both sides yields

\frac{f(n+2N+1)+f(n+2N+2)}{f(n+2N)+f(n+2N+1)}\ =\ \frac{f(t_{1})}{f(t_{2})}.

(4.5)

Now for
m
a positive integer let

T_{m}\ :=\ f(m)+f(m-1).

Then
T_{m}
satisfies the following recurrence:

\begin{split}rT_{m-1}+T_{m-2}\ &=\ rf(m-1)+rf(m-2)+f(m-2)+f(m-3)\\
&=\ f(m)+f(m-1)\\
&=\ T_{m}.\end{split}

Using this recursion and induction it follows that

r\ <\ \frac{T_{m+1}}{T_{m}}\ \leq\ \frac{3r}{2},

(4.6)

which implies that
t_{1}>t_{2}
. If
t_{1}\geq t_{2}+2
then

\begin{split}\frac{f(t_{2}+2)}{f(t_{2})}\ &=\ \frac{rf(t_{2}+1)+f(t_{2})}{f(t_{2})}\\
&=\ \frac{r^{2}f(t_{2})+rf(t_{2}-1)}{f(t_{2})}+1\\
&>\ r^{2}+1,\end{split}

(4.7)

which leads to a contradiction as
r\geq 2\implies r^{2}>3r/2
. Thus we must have
t_{1}=t_{2}+1
.

Now we know that

\begin{split}f(n+2N+2)\ &<\ f_{n+2N+1}+f_{n+2N+2}\\
&=\ \frac{rsf_{t_{1}}}{g_{2N+1}}\ <\ f_{n+2N+3},\end{split}

(4.8)

and therefore
c=rs/g_{2N+1}
cannot possibly equal
1
. Lastly, we note that

\begin{split}\gcd(T_{m+1},T_{m})\ &=\ \gcd(rT_{m}+T_{m-1},T_{m})\\
&=\ \gcd(T_{m},T_{m-1}).\end{split}

(4.9)

Applying induction proves that this
\gcd
is 1. The same argument shows that
\gcd(f_{m+1},f_{m})=1
, but this contradicts the following statements:

\displaystyle sf(t_{2}+1)\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{g(2N+1)}{r}T_{n+2N+2},

(4.10)

\displaystyle sf(t_{2})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{g(2N+1)}{r}T_{n+2N+1}

(4.11)

\displaystyle\text{ and }c>1,

(4.12)

which completes our proof.
∎

Note that in the above proof the result does not hold for
r=1
, which is the Fibonacci sequence. We will prove that the sum of
4N+2
consecutive Fibonacci numbers is a fixed integer multiple of another Fibonacci number (see Theorem
5.1
).

4.2
Sums of Odd Numbers of Consecutive Terms

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P(n)
denote the
n
{\rm th}
Pell number. Fix any integer
N>0
. There is no integer
C(N)
such that for every
n
there exists an integer index
j(n;N)
such that the following equation holds:

\sum_{i=0}^{2N}P(n+i)\ =\ C(N)P(j(n;N)).

Proof.

Suppose that we can write the sum of any
N
consecutive Pell numbers as
C(N)
times a Pell number for some positive integer
C(N)
where
N
is odd. Consider the Pell sequence modulo
C(N)
. By using the Pigeonhole Principle and the fact that two consecutive Pell numbers uniquely determine the terms before and after them we see that
\{P_{n,C(N)}\}_{n\geq 0}:=\{P(n)\ \mbox{mod }C(N)\}_{n\geq 0}
is periodic. The period is called the
Pisano Period
and is denoted by
\pi(C(N))
.

Notice that
\pi(1)=1
and
\pi(2)=2
. Now consider
C(N)>2
. Since
\{P_{n,C(N)}\}_{n\geq 0}
is not a constant sequence, therefore
\pi(C(N))\geq 2
, which implies that

P_{c+\pi(C(N)),C(N)}=P_{c,C(N)}.

Now since

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\pi(C(N))+1}\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}P(\pi(C(N))+2)&P(\pi(C(N))+1)\\
P(\pi(C(N))+1)&P(\pi(C(N)))\end{pmatrix},

(4.13)

this implies that, with
I_{2}
the
2\times 2
identity matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}^{\pi(C(N))}\ =\ I_{2}\in GL_{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}/\,C(N)\,\mathbb{Z}\right).

(4.14)

Taking the determinant, we get
(-1)^{\pi(C(N))}=1,
which implies that
\pi(C(N))
is even for all
C(N)>2
.

Now let
N
be an odd number, to emphasize this we change notation and write it as
2N+1
. Suppose the sum of any
2N+1
consecutive Pell numbers is
C(N)
times another Pell Number. Then

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N+1-1}P(n+i)\ \equiv\ 0\pmod{C(N)},\mbox{ for }n\geq 0.

Replacing
n
by
n+1
we get

P(n+2N+1)\ \equiv\ P(n)\pmod{C(N)}\;\mbox{ for all }n\geq 0,

which implies that
\pi(C(N))\mid N
. However, since
\pi(C(N))
is even when
C(N)\geq 2
, this implies that
C(N)=1
. Thus the sum of any
2N+1
consecutive Pell numbers must be equal to a Pell number. In other words,

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N+1-1}P(n+i)\ =\ P(j(n;N))

for some integer
j(n;N)
and
n\geq 0
. Notice that when
N=0
, we obtain

. Now suppose
2N+1
is an odd integer greater than
1
. Then, we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N+1-1}P(n+i)\ >\ P(n+2N+1-1).

However,

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N+1-1}P(n+i)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n+2N+1-1}P(i)\ <\ P(n+2N+1)

where the last inequality can be proven by using induction based on the value of
n+2N+1
. We conclude that

P(n+2N+1-1)<\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N+1-1}P(n+i)<P(n+2N+1),

and hence the sum of
2N+1
consecutive Pell numbers is a fixed integer multiple of a Pell Number if and only if

.
∎

We use a proof of a similar flavor in Section

.

6
A result about general Lucas sequences

Theorem
1.6
shows that

\sum_{i=0}^{4N-1}P(n+i)\ =\ 2P(2N)P(n+2N).

(6.1)

This can be interpreted as saying that for all positive integers
M\equiv-1\pmod{4}
, and all positive integers
n
, there is a positive integer
m
such that

P(m)\mid\sum_{i=0}^{M}P(n+i)\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\frac{1}{P(m)}\sum_{i=0}^{M}P(n+i)\ =\ O(1).

Indeed, to see this write
N=(M+1)/4
, and take
m=n+2N
. Then the amount
O(1)
above is in fact the constant
2P(2N)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. A similar formula holds for Fibonacci numbers by Theorem
5.1
. Namely,

\sum_{i=0}^{4N+1}F(n+i)\ =\ L(2N+1)F(n+2N+2).

(6.2)

This again implies that for all
M\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and all positive integers
n
there is a positive integer

m
such that

F(m)\mid\sum_{i=0}^{M}F(n+i)\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\frac{1}{F(m)}\sum_{i=0}^{M}F(n+i)\ =\ O(1).

Indeed, for this just write
M=4N+1
and take
m=n+2N+2
.
Other results obtained so far show that such formulas do not exist for even
M
’s (see Theorems
4.1
and
5.3
).

We take a Lucas sequence
\{U(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
of recurrence
U(n+2)\ =\ rU(n+1)+sU(n)
, where
r,~s
are nonzero coprime integers. We assume that the characteristic equation
X^{2}-rX-s=0
has two distinct roots
\alpha,~\beta
such that
\alpha/\beta
is not a root of
1
. In particular,

\Delta\ :=\ r^{2}+4s\neq 0
. Then

U(n)\ =\ a\alpha^{n}+b\beta^{n}\qquad{\text{\rm holds~for~all}}\qquad n\geq 0.

The above formula is called the Binet formula and it applies to any nondegenerate linearly recurrent sequence having the characteristic equation
X^{2}-rX-s=0
, and the coefficients
a,~b
above can be calculated in terms of
U(0),~U(1)
.
Since we restrict our investigation to Lucas sequences, for us
(U(0),U(1))=(0,1),~(2,r)
depending on whether we look at Lucas sequences of first or second kind, respectively. So, our Binet formula has

(a,b)\ :=\ \left(\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta},\frac{-1}{\alpha-\beta}\right),\qquad{\text{\rm or}}\qquad(a,b)\ :=\ (1,1).

With this formalism we state the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that there is an infinite sequence of positive integers
\{N_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
such that for each
N:=N_{j}
, there is a constant
K:=K_{j}
and infinitely many positive integers
n
such that
for each one of them there exists a positive integer
m
with

U(m)\mid\sum_{i=0}^{N}U(n+i)\quad{\text{\rm and}}\quad\left|\frac{1}{U(m)}\sum_{i=0}^{N}U(n+i)\right|\ \leq\ K.

Then either
s=-1
or all three conditions
s=1
,
r\in\{\pm 1,\pm 2\}
and
N
is odd hold. Conversely, if either
s=-1
or both
s=1
and
r\in\{\pm 1,\pm 2\}
, then the above sequences
\{N_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
and constants
K_{j}
exist.

Proof.

For a proof, see Appendix
A.4
.
∎

7

We adapt our previous results to a generalization of the Pell numbers that satisfies a
(k+1)
st

. We also conjecture that for

7.1
Definition

In

Generalized Pell

\displaystyle P_{k}^{i}(n)\ =\ 2P_{k}^{i}(n-1)+P_{k}^{i}(n-k-1)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad P_{k}^{i}(0)\ =\ P_{k}^{i}(1)\ =\ \cdots\ =\ P_{k}^{i}(i)\ =\ 0

2k+2

Applying the following formula from

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\left(-1+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-i+1)\right)

For

.

Now, setting

The sum of any four consecutive Pell numbers is four times the third of the Pell numbers:

(7.4)

We also obtain the following result.

For

The partial sum formula of Pell numbers, along with the identity

P(n+k)+(-1)^{k}P(n-k)\ =\ Q(k)P(n),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

proven in Lemma

7.3
Sum of Odd Number of Consecutive Terms

The same argument given for the classical Pell numbers (
4.3
) can be generalized for the sequence
\{P_{k}^{k-1}(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
where

is an odd natural number. This is because from
[

, §2,Theorem 2]
we have the following equality:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}2&0&\cdots&0&1\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&0\end{pmatrix}^{n}\ =\

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p+1)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+1)&\cdots&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-1)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p)\\
P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n)&\cdots&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-2)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-1)\\
P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-1)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)&\cdots&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-3)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n+p-2)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
P_{k}^{k-1}(n+1)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n-p+1)&\cdots&P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)&P_{k}^{k-1}(n)\end{pmatrix}.

Since

\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0&\dots&0&1\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\dots&\vdots&\vdots\\

we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 7.5
.

Fix any integer
N>0
. There is no integer
C(N)
such that for every
n
there exists an integer index
j(n;N)
such that the following equation holds:

\sum_{i=0}^{2N}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)\ =\ C(N)\cdot P_{k}^{k-1}\,(j(n;N)).

Proof.

The proof follows from the discussion above and from the proof of the odd case for the classical Pell numbers.
∎

We now prove the following stronger theorem for the generalized Pell sequence.

Theorem 7.6
.

Fix any odd integer
N>0
, and even integer
k\geq 0
. Suppose that there exists an integer
C(N)
such that for every
n
there exists an integer index
\,j(n;N;k)\,
such that the following equation holds:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)\ =\ C(N)P_{k}^{k-1}(j(n;N;k)).

Then

i)

2\nmid C(N)
and

ii)

N>2k+2
.

Proof.

i)

By induction we know that
a_{n}:\ =\ P_{k}^{k-1}(n)\pmod{2}
is of the form

a_{n}\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,\mbox{ if }(k+1)\mid(n+1)\\
0,\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Let
N=q(2k+2)+r
. Since
N
is odd, we have
1\leq r\leq 2k+1
. Now take any
n
such that
n\geq k+1
and
(k+1)\mid n
. We now prove that
2\nmid C(N)
. Define

\begin{cases}S_{n,N}\ :=\ \sum\limits_{i=-r+1}^{q(2k+2)}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)&\text{ if $r\leq k+1$},\\
\\
S_{n,N}\ :=\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{N-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)&\text{ if $r>k+1$}.\end{cases}

Using the explicit form of
a_{n}
we conclude that

is odd in both cases, and therefore

must be odd.

ii)

By the same argument, we know that
\pi(p)\mid N
where
p
is any prime dividing

. Similarly, using the previous argument, we also know that
p>3
. Now since

P_{k}^{k-1}(0)\ =\ P_{k}^{k-1}(1)\ =\ \cdots\ =\ P_{k}^{k-1}(k-1)\ =\ 0,

and
P_{k}^{k-1}(k)=1
, we must have

P_{k}^{k-1}(\pi(p))\ \equiv\ P_{k}^{k-1}(\pi(p)+1)\ \equiv\ \cdots\ \equiv\ P_{k}^{k-1}(k-1)\ \equiv\ 0\pmod{p}.

However, we know that

P_{k}^{k-1}(k+i)\ =\ 2^{i}\mbox{ for }1\leq i\leq k,

and since

, we have

\displaystyle P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+1)\ =\ 2^{k+1}+1

\displaystyle P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+2)\ =\ 2^{k+2}+4

\displaystyle P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+3)\ =\ 2^{k+3}+12.

Now as

, we know that

. We notice that if

, then

and

and

Therefore,

which implies

.

∎

7.4

In

Let us first define a sequence

(7.8)

It is not difficult to see that

.

We have

p_{k,(k+1)n+r+1}\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n}p_{k,m(k+1)+r},&0\leq r\ <\ k\\
\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n}p_{k,m(k+1)+r}+1,&r\ =\ k.\end{array}\right.

(7.9)

Proof.

Firstly, assume that
r<k
. Now, consider the tiling of a
[(k+1)n+r+1]\times 1
board, with the cells on the board numbered from left to right
1
to
(k+1)n+r+1
. Let
t
be the location of the last
1\times 1
cell in the tiling. Black or white squares cannot cover the cells to the right of
t
, so they must be covered by
(k+1)\times 1
polyominoes. Therefore,
t
is of the form
(k+1)m+r+1
. In this case, the number of tilings of the board is
2p_{k,mk}
(accounting for the fact that cell

can be covered by either black or white
1\times 1
squares), proving the identity.

Now, let us assume that
r=k
. We can still cover the board with black and white squares as well as grey polyominoes as we discussed in the previous case, yielding

tilings of the board. However, since the length of the board is now

, it is possible the board can be covered without black and white squares altogether. We add this new case to the total number of tilings, proving the second identity.
∎

, the congruences become

\left(\frac{\alpha^{N+1}-1}{\alpha-1}\right)\alpha^{n}\ \equiv\ \eta(N+1)\pmod{D},\qquad\alpha^{m}\ \equiv\ \eta\pmod{D}.

The number
\gamma
now becomes

\gamma\ :=\ \left(\frac{(N+1)(\alpha-1)}{\alpha^{N+1}-1}\right)^{2}.

We aim to determine when this is multiplicatively dependent on
\alpha
. This occurs only for finitely many values of
N
, because for
N>5
, the term
\alpha^{N+1}-1
possesses a primitive prime factor
p
such that
p\equiv 1\pmod{N+1}
, which does not simplify with the numerator
N+1
. Consequently, if
N>\max\{5,|\alpha|\}
, the numbers
\gamma
and
\alpha
are multiplicatively independent. The case where
\beta=-1
follows similarly and is left as an exercise for the reader.

A.5
Proof of Theorem
7.3

Proof.

Let

S_{n}\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}P_{k}^{k-1}(i).

(A.17)

Note that the first
k-1
terms in this sum are 0.

We proceed by induction on
n
, starting at
n=k
for the base case. Shifting indices on (
A.17
) for
n=k
gives

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k+1}P_{k}^{k-1}(k+i)\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=k}^{3k+1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)\ =\ S_{3k+1}.

Noting that the first
k-1
terms of
S_{n}
are all zero, we find

\displaystyle S_{3k+1}\

\displaystyle=\ S_{3k}+2P_{k}^{k-1}(3k)+P_{k}^{k-1}(2k)

\displaystyle=\ \underbrace{S_{2k-1}+2P_{k}^{k-1}(2k)+\sum\limits_{i=2k+1}^{3k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)}_{\eqref{eqn-1:gen-pell-1}}+3P_{k}^{k-1}(3k).

Now, we consider

S\ :=\ S_{2k-1}+2P_{k}^{k-1}(2k)+\sum\limits_{i=2k+1}^{3k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i).

(A.18)

Since the first
k-1
terms of the sum in (
A.17
) are zero,

\displaystyle S_{2k-1}\

\displaystyle=\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle=\ \sum\limits_{i=k}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle=\ P_{k}^{k-1}(k)+\sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i).

Then, applying recursion (
7.1
) to (
A.18
), we find

\displaystyle S\

\displaystyle=\ \sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)+P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+1)+\sum\limits_{i=2k+1}^{3k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=k+1}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)+2P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+1)+\sum\limits_{i=2k+2}^{3k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=k+2}^{2k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)+2P_{k}^{k-1}(2k+1)+P_{k}^{k-1}(k+1)+\sum\limits_{i=2k+2}^{3k-1}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle\ \ \vdots

\displaystyle\ =\ 2P_{k}^{k-1}(3k-1)+P_{k}^{k-1}(2k-1)\ =\ P_{k}^{k-1}(3k),

(A.19)

where the final reduction of
S
results from alternatively removing terms indexed by the lower bounds of each of the summations and then applying recursion (
7.1
). Thus we have
S_{3k+1}=S+3P_{k}^{k-1}(3k)=4P_{k}^{k-1}(3k)
, proving the base case.

Now, by the induction hypothesis we have

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k+1}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1+i)\ =\ 4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1),

(A.20)

and by (
7.2
) we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k+1}

\displaystyle P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1+i)\ =\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n+2k}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-2}P_{k}^{k-1}(i)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1-i)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1-i)\right).

(A.21)

Combining (
A.20
) and (
A.21
) we get

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1-i)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1-i)\ =\ 8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1).

(A.22)

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k+1}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+2-i)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-i)\right)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\bigl(P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+2)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k+1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\ P_{k}^{k-1}(n-k-1)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n)\bigl)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)-2P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\bigl(2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1)-2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k)+2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k)

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\ 2P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k)+4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k-1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ 4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k).

(A.23)

We obtain the last equation by using

P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k-1)+2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)\ =\ P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k).

(A.24)

Now, consider

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2k+1}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+i)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+2-i)}_{s_{1}\ \eqref{gen-pell-1:s1}}-\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-i)}_{s_{2}\ \eqref{gen-pell-1:s2}}\right).

(A.25)

We have the following explicit formulas for
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
:

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+2)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k+1)+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1-i),

(A.26)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{k}^{k-1}(n)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n-k-1)+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}P_{k}^{k-1}(n-i-1).

(A.27)

We now note that the RHS of
A.26
and
A.27
are particularly amenable to manipulation, and therefore turn our attention towards
\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{1}-s_{2}\right)
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}-s_{2})\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\bigl(P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+2)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k+1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\ P_{k}^{k-1}(n-k-1)-P_{k}^{k-1}(n)\bigl)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)-2P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\bigl(2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k+1)-2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k)+2P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k)

\displaystyle\ -2P_{k}^{k-1}(n-1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{2}\left(4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k)+4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+k-1)+8P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\ 4P_{k}^{k-1}(n+2k).

(A.28)

∎

A.6
Proof of Theorem
7.11

Proof.

Let the sum of any
2N+1
consecutive terms in the
kth
Fibonacci sequence be
C(N)
times another integer in the Fibonacci Sequence.
An argument similar to Theorem
4.3
rules out the cases
C(N)>2
. Therefore, we just need to take care of the cases when
C(N)=1,2
.

Case 1:

C(N)=1
.

By induction on
r>2k+1
, we have

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{r}f_{k}(n)\ <\ f_{k}(r+2),

(A.29)

which implies

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N}f_{k}(n+i)\ \leq\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n+2N}f_{k}(i)\ <\ f_{k}(n+2N+2).

From the definition of the order-
k
generalized Fibonacci sequence, for
2N>k+1
we have

f_{k}(n+2N+1)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N}f_{k}(n+i),

and thus
C(N)\neq 1
.

Case 2:

C(N)=2
.

Define

\lambda_{k}\ :=\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f_{k}(n+1)}{f_{k}(n)}.

We now note that
[
KuSi
, §11, Theorem 9]
states
\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{-k}=2
, which implies that
\lambda_{k}<2
and hence for
n>2k+1
we have
f_{k}(n+1)/f_{k}(n)<2
. Applying (
A.29
) thus implies

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{r}f_{k}(n)\ <\ f_{k}(r+2)\ <\ 2f_{k}(r+1).

(A.30)

Now since,
2N>k+1
, from the definition of the order-
k
generalized Fibonacci sequence we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(n+2N)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N-1}f_{k}(n+i)\ \ \implies\ \ 2f_{k}(n+2N)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N}f_{k}(n+i).

Lastly, we have

\displaystyle 2f_{k}(n+2N)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{2N}f_{k}(n+i)\ <\ \sum\limits_{i=0}^{n+2N}f_{k}(i)\ <\ 2f_{k}(n+2N+1),

(A.31)

which implies
C(N)\neq 2
, completing the proof.
∎
