Asymptotics for Hankel Determinants Associated to a Hermite Weight with a Varying Discontinuity This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications (OPSFA14). The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/OPSFA2017.html
Christophe CHARLIER † and Alfredo DEAÑO ‡

Abstract
We study n × n n\times n Hankel determinants constructed with moments of a Hermite weight with a Fisher–Hartwig singularity on the real line. We consider the case when the singularity is in the bulk and is both of root-type and jump-type. We obtain large n n asymptotics for these Hankel determinants, and we observe a critical transition when the size of the jumps varies with n n . These determinants arise in the thinning of the generalised Gaussian unitary ensembles and in the construction of special function solutions of the Painlevé IV equation.

Introduction and motivation

We consider the Hankel determinant

\displaystyle H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\det\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{j+k}w(x;v,s,\alpha){\rm d}x\right)_{j,k=0}^{n-1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

(1.1)

with a Gaussian weight on the real line of the form

\displaystyle w(x;v,s,\alpha)=e^{-x^{2}}|x-v|^{\alpha}\begin{cases}s,&\mbox{if }x<v,\\
1,&\mbox{if }x>v,\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
v\in\mathbb{R}
,
s\in[0,1]
and
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)
. There is a root-type Fisher–Hartwig (FH) singularity if
\alpha\neq 0
. The piecewise constant factor in (
1.2
) is a jump-type FH singularity only if
s\neq 0
and
s\neq 1
. If
s=1
there is no jump, and if
s=0
the weight is supported on the interval
[v,\infty)
. By Heine’s formula,
H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
admits the following
n
-fold integral representation:

\displaystyle H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i};v,s,\alpha){\rm d}x_{i},\qquad\Delta(x)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{j}-x_{i}).

(1.3)

In this paper we are interested in large
n
asymptotics for
H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
, uniformly in
s\in[0,1]
. An analogous case was analysed in
[
6
]
for Fredholm determinant associated with the sine kernel and in
[
10
,
11
]
for Toeplitz determinants with a weight defined on the unit circle. We briefly summarize here some known results for particular values of the parameters and we present some applications.

The Hankel determinant
H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
arises in random matrix theory. Consider the set of
n\times n
Hermitian matrices
M
endowed with the probability measure

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widehat{Z}_{n}(v,\alpha)}|\det(M)-vI|^{\alpha}e^{-\operatorname{Tr}M^{2}}{\rm d}M,\qquad{\rm d}M=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\rm d}M_{ii}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}{\rm d}\Re M_{ij}{\rm d}\Im M_{ij},

where
\widehat{Z}_{n}(v,\alpha)
is the normalisation constant. We will refer to this random matrix ensemble as the
generalised Gaussian unitary ensemble
, which we denote by
{\rm GUE}(v,\alpha)
. Such a measure of matrices
M
induces a probability measure on the eigenvalues
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of
M
which is of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!Z_{n}(v,\alpha)}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i};v,1,\alpha){\rm d}x_{i},

(1.4)

where
Z_{n}(v,\alpha)
is called the partition function of
{\rm GUE}(v,\alpha)
. From (
1.4
) and the integral representation for Hankel determinants given by (
1.3
), we have the relation

\displaystyle Z_{n}(v,\alpha)=H_{n}(v,1,\alpha).

The special case of
{\rm GUE}(0,0)
(note that if
\alpha=0
, the parameter
v
is irrelevant) is called the Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE), and has already been widely studied (see, e.g.,
[
32
]
). The partition function of the GUE is a Selberg integral and is explicitly known (see, e.g.,
[
32
]
). Its exact expression and its large
n
asymptotics are given by

\displaystyle\log H_{n}(0,1,0)=-\frac{n^{2}}{2}\log 2+\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\log(j!)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\log H_{n}(0,1,0)}{}=\frac{n^{2}}{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)-\frac{3}{4}n^{2}+n\log(2\pi)-\frac{\log n}{12}+\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1}\big),\quad\mbox{as}\ n\to\infty,

where
\zeta
is Riemann’s zeta-function.

When
\alpha\neq 0
but
v=0
, the partition function of
{\rm GUE}(0,\alpha)
is again a Selberg integral and is also known exactly for finite
n
, see
[
33
]
or
[
15
, equation (A.8)]
. This is not true for
v\neq 0
. In
[
29
]
, Krasovsky obtained via the Riemann–Hilbert method that large
n
asymptotics of
H_{n}(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha)
, when
t
is in a compact subset of
(-1,1)
, are given by

\displaystyle\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha\big)}{H_{n}(0,1,0)}=\frac{\alpha}{2}n\log n-\frac{\alpha}{2}\big(1-2t^{2}+\log 2\big)n+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\log n+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\log\big(2\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha\big)}{H_{n}(0,1,0)} =}{}+\log\frac{G\big(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\big)^{2}}{G(1+\alpha)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right),

(1.6)

where
G
is Barnes’
G
-function.

Let us denote by
x_{\min}^{(v,\alpha)}
and
x_{\max}^{(v,\alpha)}
the smallest and largest eigenvalue in
{\rm GUE}(v,\alpha)
, respectively. The probability of observing no eigenvalues in
(-\infty,v)
, denoted by
\mathbb{P}\big(x_{\min}^{(v,\alpha)}\geq v\big)
, can be expressed as a ratio of Hankel determinants given by (
1.1
) with
s=0
and
s=1
. From a direct integration of (
1.4
), and from the symmetry
w(x;v,1,\alpha)=w(-x;-v,1,\alpha)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(x_{\min}^{(v,\alpha)}\geq v\big)=\frac{H_{n}(v,0,\alpha)}{H_{n}(v,1,\alpha)}=\mathbb{P}\big(x_{\max}^{(-v,\alpha)}\leq-v\big).

(1.7)

It is well-known that the empirical spectral distribution of the eigenvalues (after proper rescaling) in the GUE converges weakly almost surely to the Wigner semi-circle distribution, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{2n}}}\rightarrow\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}{\rm d}x,

see for instance
[
1
, Chapter 2]
. It is also known that the smallest eigenvalue
x_{\min}^{(0,0)}
is usually located near
-\sqrt{2n}
and the properly rescaled fluctuations of
x_{\min}^{(0,0)}
around
-\sqrt{2n}
follow the Tracy–Widom distribution. The ratio (
1.7
) with
v=-\sqrt{2n}\big(1+\frac{w}{2n^{2/3}}\big)
and
w
in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., when
v
is near the edge of the spectrum, has been recently studied for
\alpha\neq 0
in
[
41
]
(including general
s>0
).

In this paper we investigate the probability of a large deviation of
x_{\min}^{(v,\alpha)}
, i.e., the probability (
1.7
) when
v
is sufficiently far from
-\sqrt{2n}
. In the particular case of
v=0
, (
1.7
) is the probability that a matrix
M
drawn from the
{\rm GUE}(0,\alpha)
is positive definite. Large
n
asymptotics for this probability have been obtained in
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle\log\frac{H_{n}(0,0,\alpha)}{H_{n}(0,1,\alpha)}=-\frac{\log 3}{2}n^{2}-\frac{\alpha\log 3}{2}n+\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{12}\right)\log n+c_{0}+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1}\big),

(1.8)

where

\displaystyle c_{0}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\log(2\pi)+\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{6}\right)\log 2+\left(\frac{1}{8}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)\log 3+\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\log\left[G\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}\right].

Note that the denominator
H_{n}(0,1,\alpha)
can be obtained from (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) with
t=0
. One of the goals of the present paper is to generalize this result for
v\neq 0
, i.e., to obtain strong large
n
asymptotics of
H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)
, when
t
is in a compact subset of
(-1,\infty)
. In particular, it is possible to deduce from our result the probability (
1.7
).

Assume we thin the eigenvalues
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
from (
1.4
) by removing each of them independently with a certain probability
s\in[0,1]
. The resulting point process is called the
thinned
{\rm GUE}(v,\alpha)
, whose spectrum is denoted by
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
, where
m
is itself a random variable following the Binomial distribution Bin
(n,1-s)
. Thinning was introduced in random matrix theory by Bohigas and Pato
[
5
]
, and we refer to
[
9
,
11
]
for analogous situations and an overview of the theory of thinning. Let us denote by
y_{\min}^{(v,s,\alpha)}
for the smallest thinned eigenvalue (note that we always have
y_{\min}^{(v,s,\alpha)}\geq x_{\min}^{(v,\alpha)}=y_{\min}^{(v,0,\alpha)}
). The ratio
\frac{H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}{H_{n}(v,1,\alpha)}
is a one-parameter generalisation of (
1.7
) and represents the probability of observing no eigenvalue of the thinned spectrum in
(-\infty,v)
, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(y_{\min}^{(v,s,\alpha)}\geq v\big)=\frac{H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}{H_{n}(v,1,\alpha)}.

(1.9)

In the regime when
s
is in a compact subset of
(0,1]
and
v=\sqrt{2n}t
with
t
in a compact subset of
(-1,1)
, asymptotics of this probability have been obtained rigorously in
[
27
]
for
\alpha=0
and in
[
9
]
for
\alpha\neq 0
. Note that asymptotics for integer
\alpha
were obtained previously in
[
25
]
, based on the work
[
7
]
. As
n\to\infty
, they are given by

\displaystyle\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,s,\alpha\big)}{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha\big)}=n\int_{-1}^{t}\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}{\rm d}x\log s+\frac{(\log s)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\log n+\tilde{c}_{0}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\left[2\arcsin t+2t\sqrt{1-t^{2}}+\pi\right]\frac{\log s}{2\pi}\,n+\frac{(\log s)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\log n+\tilde{c}_{0}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right),

(1.10)

where the constant
\tilde{c}_{0}=\tilde{c}_{0}(t,s,\alpha)
is explicit:

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{0}=\frac{3(\log s)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\log\big(2\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\big)+\alpha\frac{\log s}{2\pi}\arcsin t+\log\frac{G\big(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{\log s}{2\pi i}\big)G\big(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}-\frac{\log s}{2\pi i}\big)}{G\big(1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\big)^{2}}.

The second goal of the present paper is to obtain large
n
asymptotics of
H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,s,\alpha\big)
when
s=s(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and to observe a transition in the large
n
asymptotics between (
1.10
), where
s
is bounded away from
0
, and the case
H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)
.

Another motivation for the study of the Hankel determinant (
1.1
) comes from solutions of the Painlevé IV differential equation (
\textrm{P}_{\rm IV}
). The
\mathrm{P_{IV}}
equation finds interesting applications in many different areas of physics, such as non-linear optics, dispersive long-wave equations, fluid dynamics and plasma physics (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Section 10.1]
,
[
40
]
). This equation depends on two parameters
A,B\in\mathbb{C}
and is given by

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{2q(z)}q^{\prime}(z)^{2}+\frac{3}{2}q(z)^{3}+4zq(z)^{2}+2\big(z^{2}-A\big)q(z)+\frac{B}{q(z)}.

In this paper, we focus on special function solutions of
\mathrm{P_{IV}}
when the parameters
A
and
B
satisfy suitable constraints. It is known (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
26
, Theorem 25.2]
) that
\mathrm{P_{IV}}
has solutions expressible in terms of parabolic cylinder functions
U(a,z)
(these functions are defined in
[
36
, Chapter 12]
) if and only if either

\displaystyle B=-2(2n+1+\varepsilon A)^{2}\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad B=-2n^{2},

(1.11)

with
\varepsilon=\pm 1
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In this situation, we are considering the so-called special function solutions of
\mathrm{P_{IV}}
.

In general, the standard method to derive special function solutions is by considering associated Riccati equations. We refer the reader to
[
13
, Section 7]
for the general theory or
[
36
, Section 32.10]
for a summary of special function solutions. For
n=0
in (
1.11
), the Riccati equation of
\textrm{P}_{\rm IV}
is

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(z)=\varepsilon q(z)^{2}+2\varepsilon zq(z)+2\nu,

with
\nu=-(1+\varepsilon A)
.
If we set
q(z)=-\varepsilon\varphi_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)/\varphi_{\nu}(z)
to linearise this equation, then
\varphi_{\nu}
satisfies

\displaystyle\varphi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)-2\varepsilon z\varphi_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)+2\varepsilon\nu\varphi_{\nu}(z)=0,

(1.12)

whose general solutions can be written in terms of the parabolic cylinder function
U(a,z)
, see
[
36
, Section 12.2]
. If
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
, then
\varphi_{\nu}(z)=\varphi_{\nu}(z;\varepsilon)
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{\nu}(z;\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\big\{C_{1}U\big({-}\nu-\tfrac{1}{2},\sqrt{2}z\big)+C_{2}U\big({-}\nu-\tfrac{1}{2},-\sqrt{2}z\big)\big\}\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2}\big),&\mbox{if }\varepsilon=1,\\
\big\{C_{1}U\big(\nu+\tfrac{1}{2},\sqrt{2}z\big)+C_{2}U\big(\nu+\tfrac{1}{2},-\sqrt{2}z\big)\big\}\exp\big({-}\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2}\big),&\mbox{if }\varepsilon=-1,\end{cases}\!\!\!\!

(1.13)

where
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
. We comment on the case
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
at the end of this section. For general
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is a remarkable fact that the special function solutions can be constructed explicitly in terms of the following Wronskian determinants (see
[
23
,
35
]
and
[
14
, Theorem 3.5]
):

\displaystyle\tau_{n,\nu}(z;\varepsilon)=\mathcal{W}\left(\varphi_{\nu}(z;\varepsilon),\frac{{\rm d}\varphi_{\nu}(z;\varepsilon)}{{\rm d}z},\ldots,\frac{{\rm d}^{n-1}\varphi_{\nu}(z;\varepsilon)}{{\rm d}z^{n-1}}\right),\qquad n\geq 1,

with
\tau_{0,\nu}(z;\varepsilon)=1
. This Wronskian determinant can in turn be written in terms of the Hankel determinant
H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
, we obtain for
n\geq 0

\displaystyle H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\Gamma(1+\alpha)^{n}2^{-n^{2}-\frac{n(\alpha-1)}{2}}\tau_{n,\alpha}(v,-1)

\displaystyle\hphantom{H_n(v,s,\alpha)}{}=e^{-nv^{2}}\Gamma(1+\alpha)^{n}2^{-n^{2}-\frac{n(\alpha-1)}{2}}\tau_{n,-\alpha-1}(v,1),

(1.14)

where
C_{1}=1
and
C_{2}=s
. This relation relies on the integral representation for the parabolic cylinder function
[
36
, equation (12.5.1)]
:

\displaystyle U(a,z)=\frac{e^{-\tfrac{z^{2}}{4}}}{\Gamma(a+\frac{1}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{a-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}-zx}{\rm d}x,\qquad\Re\,a>-\frac{1}{2}.

(1.15)

By a change of variables, we can write the moment of order
0
of the weight function
w(x)=w(x;v,s,\alpha)
given by (
1.2
) in terms of
U
as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(x){\rm d}x=\displaystyle 2^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\left[s\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\sqrt{2}vx}{\rm d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\sqrt{2}vx}{\rm d}x\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w(x){\rm d}x}{}=2^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2}}\Gamma(1+\alpha)\left[sU\big(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2},-\sqrt{2}v\big)+U\big(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2},\sqrt{2}v\big)\right].

Therefore, from (
1.13
) with
C_{1}=1
and
C_{2}=s
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(x){\rm d}x=2^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\Gamma(1+\alpha)\varphi_{\alpha}(v;-1)=2^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}e^{-v^{2}}\Gamma(\alpha+1)\varphi_{-\alpha-1}(v;1).

(1.16)

Differentiating
j
times (
1.16
) with respect to
v
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{v}^{j}\varphi_{\alpha}(v;-1)=\frac{2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\partial_{v}^{j}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(x){\rm d}x\right)=\frac{(-1)^{j}2^{j}2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{j}w(x){\rm d}x,

\displaystyle\partial_{v}^{j}\varphi_{-\alpha-1}(v;1)=\frac{2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\partial_{v}^{j}\left(e^{v^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(x){\rm d}x\right)=\frac{(-1)^{j}2^{j}2^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}e^{v^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-v)^{j}w(x){\rm d}x.

After taking the determinant, this establishes the formula (
1.14
). Thus, the results presented in Section
2
imply large
n
asymptotics for special solutions of
\textrm{P}_{\rm IV}
expressed in terms of
\tau_{n,\nu}
, uniformly for
C_{2}
small.

As explained in
[
14
]
, when the parameter
\nu=m
is an integer, we need to take a different combination of independent solutions of equation (
1.12
), since both parabolic cylinder functions in the seed function become Hermite polynomials. This leads to the so-called generalised Hermite polynomials, which are rational solutions of
\rm{P}_{\rm IV}
, obtained as particular cases of the special function solutions, see the parameter plane of
\rm{P}_{\rm IV}
in
[
13
, Section 5.5]
or
[
26
]
. We refer the reader to the work of Kawijara and Ohta
[
28
, Definition 3.1]
for more information on the rational solutions of
\rm{P}_{\rm IV}
.

We also note that in the previous discussion, since
\nu=\alpha
, the integral representation (
1.15
) imposes the restriction
\alpha>-1
. This restriction can be lifted at the price of taking complex integration and a complex weight function. This defines an associated family of orthogonal polynomials only formally. We do not pursue this route in this paper, but we note that it has been used in the literature, for example in the analysis of the asymptotic behavior and pole structure of rational solutions of
\rm{P}_{\rm II}
by Bertola and Bothner
[
3
]
and more recently of rational solutions of
\rm{P}_{\rm IV}
by Buckingham
[
8
]
.

2
Main results

Observe that if we naively take the limit
s\to 0
in (
1.10
), the asymptotics on the right-hand side blow up, due to the presence of
\log s
terms. Therefore, a critical transition is expected when
n\to\infty
and simultaneously
s\to 0
in a suitable double scaling limit. As mentioned earlier, the contribution of this paper is to analyse 1) the case
s=0
(which is known only for
v=0
, see (
1.8
)), and 2) the transition between the situation when
s=0
and the situation when
s
is in a compact subset of
(0,1]
, given by (
1.10
). We obtain the following results.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)
and
t\in(-1,\infty)
. As
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)}{H_{n}(0,0,\alpha)}=C_{1}(t)n^{2}+C_{2}(t,\alpha)n+C_{3}(t,\alpha)+\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1}\big),

(2.1)

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)=-\frac{2t^{3}}{27}\big(\sqrt{3+t^{2}}-t\big)-\left(\frac{4}{3}t^{2}+\frac{5}{9}t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{t+\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{\sqrt{3}}\right),

(2.2)

\displaystyle C_{2}(t,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha t}{3}\big(t-\sqrt{3+t^{2}}\big)-\alpha\log\left(\frac{t+\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{\sqrt{3}}\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle C_{3}(t,\alpha)=\frac{1-3\alpha^{2}}{6}\log\left(\frac{t+\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)-\frac{1}{48}\log\left(\frac{3+t^{2}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{C_{3}(t,\alpha) =}{}-\frac{1}{16}\big(1-4\alpha^{2}\big)\log\left(\frac{3+5t^{2}+4t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{3}\right).

(2.4)

Furthermore, the error term
\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1}\big)
is uniform for
t
in a compact subset of
(-1,\infty)
.

Note that there is no critical transition in (
2.1
) as
t\to 1
.

Remark 2.2
.

The probability (
1.7
) with
v=\sqrt{2n}t
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(x_{\min}^{(\sqrt{2n}t,\alpha)}\geq\sqrt{2n}t\big)=\frac{H_{n}(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha)}{H_{n}(0,0,\alpha)}\frac{H_{n}(0,0,\alpha)}{H_{n}(0,1,\alpha)}\frac{H_{n}(0,1,\alpha)}{H_{n}(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha)}.

Large
n
asymptotics of these three ratios are given up to the constant term by Theorem
2.1
(for
t>-1
), (
1.8
) and (
1.6
) (for
t\in(-1,1)
), respectively. Putting these asymptotics together, we obtain as
n\to\infty
and for
t\in(-1,1)
,

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\big(x_{\min}^{(\sqrt{2n}t,\alpha)}\geq\sqrt{2n}t\big)=\left(C_{1}(t)-\frac{\log 3}{2}\right)n^{2}+\left(C_{2}(t,\alpha)-\frac{\alpha\log 3}{2}-\alpha t^{2}\right)n

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{12}\right)\log n+C_{3}(t,\alpha)+c_{0}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}\log\big(1-t^{2}\big)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).

(2.5)

It can be checked from (
2.2
) that
C_{1}(-1)=\frac{\log 3}{2}
and

\displaystyle C_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{8}{27}\big(\sqrt{3+t^{2}}-t\big)\big(3+5t^{2}+4t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}\big)<0,\qquad\mbox{for}\quad t>-1,

which shows that the leading term in (
2.5
) is negative. This implies that the above probability decays super exponentially fast as
n\to\infty
for
t\in(-1,1)
.

To observe a transition in the large
n
asymptotics of
H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,s,\alpha\big)
when
s=0
and when
s
is in a compact subset of
(0,1]
, we couple the parameter
s
with
n
in the form

\displaystyle s=e^{-\lambda n},\qquad\lambda\geq 0.

Large

asymptotics of

will depend on whether
\lambda
is greater or smaller than a critical value
\lambda_{c}(t)
, which is explicit and given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(t)=\frac{2t}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{3+t^{2}+2t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\lambda_{c}(t) =}{}+2\log\left(2+t^{2}+t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{3+t^{2}}+t}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{3+t^{2}+2t\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}\right).

(2.6)

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
s=e^{-\lambda n}
with
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, we have the following asymptotic results.

(1)

If
t\in(-1,1)
and
\lambda\geq\lambda_{c}(t)
, then

\displaystyle\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,e^{-\lambda n},\alpha\big)}{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)}=\mathcal{O}\big(n^{-1/2}e^{-n(\lambda-\lambda_{c}(t))}\big),\qquad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty,

(2.7)

and large
n
asymptotics for
\log H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)
are given by Theorem
2.1
. Furthermore, the
\mathcal{O}
term in (

) is uniform for
t
in a compact subset of
(-1,1)
and for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{c}(t)
.

(2)

If
t\in(-1,1)
and
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}(t)
are fixed, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\log\frac{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,e^{-\lambda n},\alpha\big)}{H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,1,\alpha\big)}=-\int_{0}^{\lambda}\Omega\big(t,\tilde{\lambda}\big){\rm d}\tilde{\lambda},

(2.8)

where

\displaystyle\Omega(t,\lambda)=\int_{a}^{b}\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x,\qquad\rho(x;t,\lambda)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{c-x}\sqrt{\frac{x-b}{x-t}}\sqrt{x-a},

and
a<b<t<c
, with
a
,
b
and
c
depending on
\lambda
and

, are uniquely determined by the following equations:

\displaystyle t=a+b+c,

\displaystyle 2=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}-t^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda=4\int_{b}^{t}\frac{\sqrt{c-x}}{\sqrt{t-x}}\sqrt{x-b}\sqrt{x-a}{\rm d}x.

Remark 2.4
.

In Theorem

Remark 2.5

Note that the denominators on the left hand sides of (

3
Orthogonal polynomials and differential identities

3.1
Orthogonal polynomials

We consider the family of orthonormal polynomials
p_{j}
of degree
j
with respect to
w
defined in (
1.2
), characterized by the orthogonality conditions

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{j}(x)p_{k}(x)w(x){\rm d}x=\delta_{jk},\qquad j,k=0,1,2,\dots,

(3.1)

and
\kappa_{j}>0
is the leading coefficient of
p_{j}
, that is

\pi_{j}(x)=\kappa_{j}^{-1}p_{j}(x)
is the monic orthogonal polynomial of degree
j
which satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\pi_{j}(x)\pi_{k}(x)w(x){\rm d}x=h_{j}\delta_{jk},\qquad j,k=0,1,2,\dots,

(3.2)

where
h_{j}
is the squared norm of
\pi_{j}
. From (
3.1
), we have
h_{j}=\kappa_{j}^{-2}
. It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
) that these OPs satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle x\pi_{j}(x)=\pi_{j+1}(x)+\beta_{j}\pi_{j}(x)+\gamma_{j}^{2}\pi_{j-1}(x),\qquad j\geq 0,

(3.3)

with
\pi_{-1}(x):=0
. Note that if we write
\pi_{j}(x)=x^{j}+\sigma_{j}x^{j-1}+\cdots
, then from (
3.3
) we get the relation

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j+1}=\beta_{j},\qquad j\geq 0,\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\sigma_{0}:=0.

(3.4)

3.2
Differential identity in
\boldsymbol{v}
for
\boldsymbol{s=0}

From the determinantal representation for OPs (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
) and (
1.3
), the Hankel determinant
H_{n}(v,0,\alpha)
can be written in terms of the norms of the OPs, one has

\displaystyle H_{n}(v,0,\alpha)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}h_{j}.

(3.5)

If we differentiate with respect to
v
the relation (
3.2
) with
k=j
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{v}h_{j}=\partial_{v}\left(\int_{v}^{\infty}\pi_{j}^{2}(x)w(x){\rm d}x\right)=\partial_{v}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\pi_{j}^{2}(x+v)w(x+v){\rm d}x\right).

Since
w(x+v)=x^{\alpha}e^{-(x+v)^{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{v}h_{j}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\pi_{j}(x+v)\partial_{v}(\pi_{j}(x+v))w(x+v){\rm d}x-2\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+v)\pi_{j}^{2}(x+v)w(x+v){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_{v}h_{j}}{}=-2\int_{v}^{\infty}x\pi_{j}^{2}(x)w(x){\rm d}x,

where we have used the orthogonality (
3.2
) and the fact that
\partial_{v}(\pi_{j}(x+v))
is a polynomial of degree at most
j-1
. From the recurrence relation (
3.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{v}h_{j}=-2\beta_{j}h_{j}.

As a consequence of this, by taking the
\log
in (
3.5
) and differentiating it with respect to
v
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\log H_{n}(v,0,\alpha)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\partial_{v}\log h_{j}=-2\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\beta_{j},

This can be simplified by using (
3.4
), and gives

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\log H_{n}(v,0,\alpha)=2\sigma_{n}(v),

(3.6)

where
\sigma_{n}
is the subleading coefficient of the polynomial
\pi_{n}(x)
, defined after (
3.3
), and we have explicitly written the dependence of
\sigma_{n}
on
v
.

3.3
Differential identity in
\boldsymbol{s}

Suppose that the thinned eigenvalues
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
are observed and that
\sharp\{y_{i}\colon y_{i}<v\}=0
. From Bayes’ formula, using (
1.4
) and (
1.9
), the distribution of the whole spectrum
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
conditionally on this event is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i};v,s,\alpha){\rm d}x_{i}.

Such point processes are called conditional, and were first considered in
[
11
]
on the unit circle and then on the real line in
[
9
]
. This point process is determinantal
[
16
]
, and its correlation kernel is given by

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sqrt{w(x)w(y)}\frac{\kappa_{n-1}}{\kappa_{n}}\frac{p_{n-1}(y)p_{n}(x)-p_{n-1}(x)p_{n}(y)}{x-y},&\mbox{if }x\neq y,\\
\displaystyle w(x)\frac{\kappa_{n-1}}{\kappa_{n}}\big(p_{n}^{\prime}(x)p_{n-1}(x)-p_{n}(x)p_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)\big),&\mbox{if }x=y,\end{cases}

(3.7)

where the OPs
p_{j}
are orthonormal with respect to
w(x;v,s,\alpha)
and are defined in (
3.1
). The expected number of points on
(-\infty,v)
in this point process is denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
. It is also known
[
16
]
that
\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
can be expressed in terms of the one-point correlation function
K_{n}(x,x)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{v}K_{n}(x,x){\rm d}x.

(3.8)

The quantity
\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
can also be expressed in terms of the logarithmic derivative of
H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
with respect to
s
. Consider the following partition of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle A_{k}=\big\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\sharp\{x_{i}\colon x_{i}<v\}=k\big\},\qquad\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{n}A_{k}=\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By definition of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{k}{n!H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}\int_{A_{k}}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i};v,s,\alpha){\rm d}x_{i}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}{}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{ks^{k}}{n!H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)}\int_{A_{k}}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}-v|^{\alpha}e^{-x_{i}^{2}}{\rm d}x_{i}.

Note that the
n
-fold integral (
1.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{s^{k}}{n!}\int_{A_{k}}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}-v|^{\alpha}e^{-x_{i}^{2}}{\rm d}x_{i},

and thus we have
\mathcal{E}_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=s\partial_{s}\log H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)
. Putting this together with (
3.8
), we obtain the differential identity

\displaystyle s\partial_{s}\log H_{n}(v,s,\alpha)=\int_{-\infty}^{v}K_{n}(x,x){\rm d}x.

(3.9)

We will also use later the well-known (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
) formula for reproducing kernels

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{n}(x,x){\rm d}x=n.

(3.10)

4
A Riemann–Hilbert problem and renormalization

of the problem

We will perform the Deift–Zhou
[
20
,
21
]
steepest descent method on a Riemann–Hilbert problem to get the large
n
asymptotics for
p_{n}
. Consider the matrix valued function
Y
, defined by

\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa_{n}^{-1}p_{n}(z)&\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{n}^{-1}}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{p_{n}(x)w(x)}{x-z}{\rm d}x\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-2\pi i\kappa_{n-1}p_{n-1}(z)&\displaystyle-\kappa_{n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{p_{n-1}(x)w(x)}{x-z}{\rm d}x\end{pmatrix}.

(4.1)

It is well-known
[
22
]
that
Y
is the unique solution of the following RH problem.

RH problem for
\boldsymbol{Y}

(a)

Y\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
is analytic.

(b)

The limits of
Y(x\pm i\epsilon)
as
\epsilon>0
approaches
0
exist, are continuous on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{v\}
and are denoted by
Y_{+}
and
Y_{-}
respectively. Furthermore they are related by

\displaystyle Y_{+}(x)=Y_{-}(x)\begin{pmatrix}1&w(x)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{v\}.

(4.2)

(c)

As
z\to\infty
, we have
Y(z)=\big(I+Y_{1}z^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-2}\big)\big)z^{n\sigma_{3}}
, where
\sigma_{3}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

(d)

As
z
tends to
v
, the behaviour of
Y
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(\log(z-v))\\
\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(\log(z-v))\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{if}\ \ \alpha=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle Y(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}((z-v)^{\alpha})\\
\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}((z-v)^{\alpha})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{if}\ \ \alpha\neq 0.

If
s=0
, from condition (b)
Y
has no jump along
(-\infty,v)
and thus
Y
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[v,\infty)
. Note also that
Y_{11}(z)=\kappa_{n}^{-1}p_{n}(z)=\pi_{n}(z)
, and thus

\displaystyle Y_{1,11}=\sigma_{n}(v),

(4.3)

where
Y_{1,11}
denotes the
(1,1)
entry of the matrix
Y_{1}
.

4.1
Normalization of the RH problem

We define
t=\frac{v}{\sqrt{2n}}
, and we normalize the RH problem for
Y
with the following transformation

\displaystyle U(z)=(2n)^{-(\frac{\alpha}{4}+\frac{n}{2})\sigma_{3}}Y\big(\sqrt{2n}z\big)(2n)^{\frac{\alpha}{4}\sigma_{3}}.

(4.4)

The matrix
U
satisfies the following RH problem.

RH problem for
\boldsymbol{U}

(a)

U\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
is analytic.

(b)

U
has the following jumps:

\displaystyle U_{+}(x)=U_{-}(x)\begin{pmatrix}1&\widetilde{w}(x)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{t\},

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}(x)=(2n)^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}w(\sqrt{2n}x)=|x-t|^{\alpha}e^{-2nx^{2}}\begin{cases}s,&\mbox{if}\ x<t,\\
1,&\mbox{if}\ x>t.\end{cases}

(c)

As

U(z)=\big(I+U_{1}z^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-2}\big)\big)z^{n\sigma_{3}}
.

(d)

As
z
tends to
t
, the behaviour of
U
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle U(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(\log(z-t))\\
\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(\log(z-t))\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{if}\quad\alpha=0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle U(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}((z-t)^{\alpha})\\
\mathcal{O}(1)&\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}((z-t)^{\alpha})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{if}\quad\alpha\neq 0.

The following lemma translates the differential identities (
3.6
) and (

) in terms of

Lemma 4.1
.

We have the following differential identities

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\log H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,0,\alpha\big)=4nU_{1,11},

(4.5)

\displaystyle s\partial_{s}\log H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,s,\alpha\big)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{\widetilde{w}(x)}{2\pi i}\big[U^{-1}(x)U^{\prime}(x)\big]_{21}{\rm d}x.

(4.6)

Proof.

The differential identity (

) is obtained by substituting (

) and (

) into (

). Similarly, using (

) and (

\displaystyle s\partial_{s}\log H_{n}\big(\sqrt{2n}t,s,\alpha\big)=\int_{-\infty}^{\sqrt{2n}t}\frac{w(x)}{2\pi i}\big[Y^{-1}(x)Y^{\prime}(x)\big]_{21}{\rm d}x,

which gives (

Remark 4.2
.

Note that

only involves the first column of

) or equivalently (

4.2
Equilibrium measure

We introduce a new parameter
\lambda\in[0,+\infty]
, defined through
s=e^{-\lambda n}
, which characterizes the speed of convergence of
s
to
0
as
n\to\infty
. An essential tool in the RH analysis is the so-called equilibrium measure. In our case, the equilibrium measure
\mu_{V}
is the unique minimizer of the functional

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|^{-1}{\rm d}\mu(x){\rm d}\mu(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x){\rm d}\mu(x),

among all Borel probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, where the potential
V
is defined by

\displaystyle V(x)=\begin{cases}2x^{2}+\lambda,&\mbox{if}\ x<t,\\
2x^{2},&\mbox{if}\ x\geq t,\end{cases}

and where the parameter
t
has been defined above (
4.4
). The equilibrium measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and its density will be denoted by
\rho(x)
. The equilibrium measure and its support, denoted by
\mathcal{S}
, are completely determined by the following Euler–Lagrange variational conditions
[
37
]
:

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\rho(y){\rm d}y=V(x)-\ell,\qquad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathcal{S},

(4.7)

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\rho(y){\rm d}y\leq V(x)-\ell,\qquad\mbox{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{S},

(4.8)

where
\ell
is a constant. Proposition
4.3
below shows that the equilibrium measure depends crucially on whether
\lambda\geq\lambda_{c}(t)
or
0<\lambda<\lambda_{c}(t)
. If
\lambda=0
, the potential is simply
V(x)=2x^{2}
and the equilibrium measure is the semicircle law supported on
(-1,1)
(see, e.g.,
[
37
]
). For convenience we also include it in Proposition
4.3
(see case (3)), but without giving a proof of it.

Proposition 4.3
.

(1)

If
t\in(-1,\infty)
and
\lambda\geq\lambda_{c}(t)
, the density of the equilibrium measure
\rho(x)=\rho(x;t)
is independent of
\lambda
and is given by

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}(x-\overline{b})\frac{\sqrt{\overline{c}-x}}{\sqrt{x-t}},

(4.9)

supported on
\mathcal{S}=[t,\overline{c}]
, with

\displaystyle\overline{b}=\overline{b}(t)=\frac{t-\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{3},\qquad\overline{c}=\overline{c}(t)=\frac{t+2\sqrt{3+t^{2}}}{3}.

The constant
\ell=\ell(t)
in the variational conditions (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) is given by

\displaystyle\ell=1+\frac{2}{3}t\big(\sqrt{3+t^{2}}+2t\big)+2\log\big(2\big(t+\sqrt{3+t^{2}}\big)\big).

(4.10)

Furthermore, the variational inequality (
4.8
) is strict for all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{S}
if
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
, and if
\lambda=\lambda_{c}
then (
4.8
) is strict for all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(\mathcal{S}\cup\{\overline{b}\})
and (
4.8
) is an equality at
x=\overline{b}
.

(2)

If
t\in(-1,1)
and
0<\lambda<\lambda_{c}(t)
, the density of the equilibrium measure
\rho(x)=\rho(x;t,\lambda)
is given by

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{c-x}\sqrt{\frac{x-b}{x-t}}\sqrt{x-a},

(4.11)

supported on two disjoint intervals

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=[a,b]\cup[t,c],\qquad a<b<t<c,

and
a
,
b
,
c
, depending on
\lambda
and
t
, are uniquely determined by the following equations:

\displaystyle t=a+b+c,

(4.12)

\displaystyle 2=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}-t^{2},

Proof.

We will start by proving case (2). From (

\displaystyle a=-\frac{b}{2}+\frac{t}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{4-3b^{2}+2tb+t^{2}}}{2},\qquad c=-\frac{b}{2}+\frac{t}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{4-3b^{2}+2tb+t^{2}}}{2}.

For

fixed and

,

If

. On the other hand, if

where

and

\displaystyle f(x)=2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\rho(y){\rm d}y-2x^{2},

\displaystyle-4\frac{\sqrt{c-x}}{\sqrt{t-x}}\sqrt{x-b}\sqrt{x-a},&b<x<t,\\

RH problem for
\boldsymbol{P^{(\infty)}}

(a)

P^{(\infty)}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,c]\to\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
is analytic.

(b)

P^{(\infty)}
has the following jumps:

\displaystyle P^{(\infty)}_{+}(z)=P^{(\infty)}_{-}(z)\begin{pmatrix}0&|z-t|^{\alpha}\\

\displaystyle P^{(\infty)}_{+}(z)=P^{(\infty)}_{-}(z)e^{2\pi i\Omega n\sigma_{3}},\qquad\mbox{if}\quad z\in(b,t).

As

As

As

The construction of

has been done in similar situations in
[
19
]
for

and in
[
31
]
for
\alpha\neq 0

\theta

X
be the two sheeted Riemann surface of genus one associated to
\sqrt{R(z)}
, with

\displaystyle R(z)=(z-c)(z-t)(z-b)(z-a),

and we let
\sqrt{R(z)}\sim z^{2}
as
z\to\infty
on the first sheet. We also define cycles
A
and
B
such that they form a canonical homology basis of
X
. The upper part of the cycle
A
(the dashed line in Fig.
4
) lies on the second sheet, and the lower part lies on the first sheet. The cycle
B
surrounds
(a,b)
in the clockwise direction, and lie in the first sheet.

Figure 4:
The cycles
A
and
B
. The solid line of
A
is in the first sheet and the dashed line is in the second sheet. The cycle
B
lies on the first sheet.

The unique
A

\omega
on
X
is given by

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{c_{0}{\rm d}z}{\sqrt{R(z)}},\qquad c_{0}=\left(\int_{A}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R(z)}}{\rm d}z\right)^{-1}.

By construction
\int_{A}\omega=1
and the lattice parameter is given by
\tau=\int_{B}\omega
. A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle c_{0}=\left(\int_{b}^{t}\frac{2}{\sqrt{|R(x)|}}{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\qquad\tau=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{2ic_{0}}{\sqrt{|R(x)|}}{\rm d}x\in i\mathbb{R}^{+}.

The associated
\theta
-function of the third kind
\theta(z)=\theta(z;\tau)
is given by

\displaystyle\theta(z)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2\pi imz}e^{\pi im^{2}\tau}.

It is an entire function which satisfies

\displaystyle\theta(z+1)=\theta(z),\qquad\theta(z+\tau)=e^{-2\pi iz}e^{-\pi i\tau}\theta(z),\qquad\mbox{for all}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(6.10)

We also need the function

\displaystyle u(z)=\int_{c}^{z}\omega,

where the path of integration lies in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,c)
. Since
\int_{C}\omega=0
for any circle
C
winding around
\mathcal{S}
,

is a single valued function for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,c)
. For
z
on the first sheet, it satisfies

\displaystyle u_{+}(z)+u_{-}(z)=0,\qquad z\in(t,c),

\displaystyle u_{+}(z)+u_{-}(z)=1,\qquad z\in(a,b),

\displaystyle u_{+}(z)-u_{-}(z)=\tau,\qquad z\in(b,t),

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}u(z)=u_{\infty}\in\mathbb{C}.

We define
\beta(z)=\sqrt[4]{\frac{z-a}{z-b}\frac{z-t}{z-c}}
, such that
\beta(z)\sim 1
as
z\to\infty
on the first sheet. On this first sheet, it can be verified that the function
\beta(z)+\beta^{-1}(z)
never vanishes, while
\beta(z)-\beta^{-1}(z)
vanishes at a single point
z_{\star}
, given by

\displaystyle z_{\star}=\frac{c-t}{c-t+b-a}b+\frac{b-a}{c-t+b-a}t\in(b,t).

This observation will be useful later, because the solution
P^{(\infty)}
will involve functions of the form
1/\theta(u(z)\pm d)
, where
d=-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\tau}{2}+\int_{c}^{z_{\star}}\omega
. The function
1/\theta(u(z)+d)
has no pole on the first sheet, while the function
1/\theta(u(z)-d)
has a pole at
z_{\star}
, see
[
19
]
. Therefore, the functions
\frac{\beta(z)\pm\beta^{-1}(z)}{\theta(u(z)\pm d)}
are analytic on the first sheet.

Before stating the solution
P^{(\infty)}
, we follow
[
31
]
and introduce a scalar Szegő function

which satisfies

(a)

D\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus[a,c]\to\mathbb{C}

(b)

has the following jumps:

\displaystyle D_{+}(z)D_{-}(z)=|z-t|^{\alpha},\qquad\mbox{if}\quad z\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle D_{+}(z)=D_{-}(z)e^{2\pi i\tilde{\alpha}},\qquad\mbox{if}\quad z\in(b,t).

(c)

As

,

.

(d)

As

,

,

.

As

tends to
\tilde{z}\in\{a,b,c\}
,

is bounded.

The new parameter

is not arbitrary and is completely determined to ensure the existence of

. The solution is given by

\displaystyle D(z)=\exp\left(\sqrt{R(z)}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\alpha\log|x-t|}{\sqrt{R(x)}_{+}}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x-z}+\tilde{\alpha}\int_{b}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R(x)}}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x-z}\right]\right),

see
[

]
for a detailed proof of it, and

is chosen such that

remains bounded as

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=-\left(\int_{b}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R(x)}}{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\alpha\log|x-t|}{\sqrt{R(x)}_{+}}{\rm d}x.

From (

as

\displaystyle D_{\infty}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\frac{\alpha x\log|x-t|}{\sqrt{R(x)}_{+}}{\rm d}x-\tilde{\alpha}\int_{b}^{t}\frac{x}{\sqrt{R(x)}}{\rm d}x\right).

.

Lemma 7.4
.

For
t\in(-1,1)
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{c}(t))
, there holds a relation between
\Omega(t,\lambda)
, the density
\rho(x;t,\lambda)
given by (
4.11
), and the Euler–Lagrange constant
\ell(t,\lambda)
given by (
4.15
):

\displaystyle\Omega(t,\lambda)=\partial_{\lambda}\ell(t,\lambda)+\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}2x^{2}\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x+\lambda\partial_{\lambda}\Omega(t,\lambda).

(7.21)

Proof.

Consider the function

\displaystyle H(x;t,\lambda)=-2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\rho(y;t,\lambda){\rm d}y.

By the Euler–Lagrange equality (
4.7
), we have

\displaystyle H(x;t,\lambda)=\ell(t,\lambda)-2x^{2},\qquad x\in(t,c),

(7.22)

\displaystyle H(x;t,\lambda)=\ell(t,\lambda)-2x^{2}-\lambda,\qquad x\in(a,b).

(7.23)

Thus, by integrating it with respect to
\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}}H(x;t,\lambda)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x=\ell(t,\lambda)-\lambda\Omega(t,\lambda)-\int_{\mathcal{S}}2x^{2}\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x.

We will evaluate
\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}H(x;t,\lambda)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x
in two different ways. From the above expression, it gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}H(x;t,\lambda)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x=\partial_{\lambda}\ell(t,\lambda)-\Omega(t,\lambda)-\lambda\partial_{\lambda}\Omega(t,\lambda)-\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}2x^{2}\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x.\!\!\!\!\!\!

(7.24)

On the other hand, by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, and by the symmetry in
x
and
y
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\rho(y;t,\lambda)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}y{\rm d}x=2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\log|x-y|\partial_{\lambda}(\rho(y;t,\lambda))\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}y{\rm d}x.

Therefore, by differentiating (
7.22
) and (
7.23
) with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}\int_{\mathcal{S}}H(x;t,\lambda)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x=2\int_{\mathcal{S}}\partial_{\lambda}\left(H(x;t,\lambda)\right)\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x=2\partial_{\lambda}\ell(t,\lambda)-2\Omega(t,\lambda).\!\!\!\!

(7.25)

Putting (
7.24
) and (
7.25
) together, we obtain (
7.21
).
∎

Let us consider the function

\displaystyle F(t,\lambda)=\ell(t,\lambda)+\int_{\mathcal{S}}2x^{2}\rho(x;t,\lambda){\rm d}x,

Lemma 7.5
.

For
t\in(-1,1)
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{c}(t))
, we have the following relation

\displaystyle\Omega(t,\lambda)=\partial_{\lambda}\left[\frac{F(t,\lambda_{c}(t))}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{c}(t)}\right)^{2}+\int_{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{c}(t)}}^{1}\xi F\big(t,\tfrac{\lambda}{\xi}\big){\rm d}\xi\right].

Proof.

From a direct calculation and a change of variables, the right-hand side of (

) is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{c}(t)}}^{1}\xi\partial_{\lambda}F\big(t,\tfrac{\lambda}{\xi}\big){\rm d}\xi=\int_{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{c}(t)}}^{1}\partial_{u}F(t,u)\big|_{u=\frac{\lambda}{\xi}}{\rm d}\xi=\lambda\int_{\lambda}^{\lambda_{c}(t)}\frac{\partial_{u}F(t,u)}{u^{2}}{\rm d}u.

(7.27)

By using (
7.21
), which can be rewritten as
\partial_{\lambda}F(t,\lambda)=\Omega(t,\lambda)-\lambda\partial_{\lambda}\Omega(t,\lambda)
, the right-hand side of (

) becomes

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\lambda}^{\lambda_{c}(t)}\frac{\Omega(t,u)-u\partial_{u}\Omega(t,u)}{u^{2}}{\rm d}u=\Omega(t,\lambda),

where the last equality is obtained via an integration by parts, and using the identity
\Omega(t,\lambda_{c}(t))

=0
.
∎

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\lambda_{c}(t)}\Omega(t,\lambda){\rm d}\lambda=C_{1}(t)-\frac{\log 3}{2}.
