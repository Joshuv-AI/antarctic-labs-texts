Abstract
. In the literature on Kleene algebra, a number of variants have been proposed which impose additional structure specified by a theory, such as Kleene algebra with tests (KAT) and the recent Kleene algebra with observations (KAO), or make specific assumptions about certain constants, as for instance in NetKAT. Many of these variants fit within the unifying perspective offered by Kleene algebra with hypotheses , which comes with a canonical language model constructed from a given set of hypotheses. For the case of KAT, this model corresponds to the familiar interpretation of expressions as languages of guarded strings. A relevant question therefore is whether Kleene algebra together with a given set of hypotheses is complete with respect to its canonical language model. In this paper, we revisit, combine and extend existing results on this question to obtain tools for proving completeness in a modular way. We showcase these tools by giving new and modular proofs of completeness for KAT, KAO and NetKAT, and we prove completeness for new variants of KAT: KAT extended with a constant for the full relation, KAT extended with a converse operation, and a version of KAT where the collection of tests only forms a distributive lattice.

Introduction

Kleene algebras (KA)
[
Kle56
,
Con71
]
are algebraic structures involving an iteration operation, Kleene star, corresponding to reflexive-transitive closure in relational models and to language iteration in language models.
Its axioms are complete w.r.t. relational models and language models
[
Koz91
,
Kro91
,
Bof90
]
, and the resulting equational theory is decidable via automata algorithms (in fact,
PSpace
-complete
[
MS72
,
HRS76
]
).

These structures were later extended in order to deal with common programming constructs. For instance, Kleene algebras with tests (KAT)
[
Koz97
]
, which combine Kleene algebra and Boolean algebra, make it possible to represent the control flow of while programs. Kleene star is used for while loops, and Boolean tests are used for the conditions of such loops, as well as the conditions in if-then-else statements.
Again, the axioms of KAT are complete w.r.t. appropriate classes of models, and its equational theory remains in
PSpace
. Proving so is non-trivial: Kozen’s proof reduces completeness of KAT to completeness of KA, via a direct syntactic transformation on terms.

Another extension is Concurrent Kleene algebra (CKA)
[
HMSW11
]
, where a binary operator for parallelism is added. The resulting theory is characterised by languages of pomsets rather than languages of words, and is
ExpSpace
-complete
[
BPS17
]
. Trying to have both tests and concurrency turned out to be non-trivial, and called for yet another notion: Kleene algebras with observations (KAO)
[
KBR+19
]
, which are again complete w.r.t. appropriate models, and decidable.

When used in the context of program verification, e.g., in a proof assistant, such structures make it possible to write algebraic proofs of correctness, and to mechanise some of the steps: when two expressions
e
and
f
representing two programs happen to be provably equivalent in KA, KAT, or KAO, one does not need to provide a proof, one can simply call a certified decision procedure
[
BP10
,
KN12
,
Pou13
]
.
However, this is often not enough
[
KP00
,
AK01
,
HK02
]
: most of the time, the expressions
e
and
f
are provably equal only under certain assumptions on their constituents. For instance, to prove that
(a+b)^{*}
and
a^{*}b^{*}
are equal, one may have to use the additional assumption
ba=ab
.
In other words, one would like to prove equations under some assumptions, to have algorithms for the Horn theory of Kleene algebra and its extensions rather than just their equational theories.

Unfortunately, those Horn theories are typically undecidable
[
Koz96
,
Koz02
]
, even with rather restricted forms of hypotheses (e.g., commutation of specific pairs of letters, as in the above example).
Nevertheless, important and useful classes of hypotheses can be ‘eliminated’, by reducing to the plain and decidable case of the equational theory. This is, for instance, the case for
Hoare hypotheses

[
Koz00
]
, of the shape
e=0
, which allow to encode Hoare triples for partial correctness in KAT.

In some cases, one wants to exploit hypotheses about specific constituents (e.g,
a
and
b
in the above example). In other situations, one wants to exploit assumptions on the whole structure. For instance, in commutative Kleene algebra
[
Red64
,
Con71
,
Bru19
]
, one assumes that the product is commutative everywhere.

Many of these extensions of Kleene algebra (KAT, KAO, commutative KA, specific hypotheses) fit into the generic framework of Kleene algebra with hypotheses
[
DKPP19
]
, providing in each case a canonical model in terms of closed languages.

We show that we recover standard models in this way, and we provide tools to establish completeness and decidability of such extensions, in a modular way. The key notion is that of
reduction
from one set of hypotheses to another. We summarise existing reductions, provide new ones, and design a toolbox for combining those reductions together. We use this toolbox in order to obtain new and modular proofs of completeness for KAT, KAO and NetKAT
[
AFG+14
]
; to prove completeness of KAT with a full element and KAT with a converse operation, two extensions that were formerly proposed for KA alone, respectively in
[
PW22
]
and
[
BÉS95
,
ÉB95
]
; and to prove completeness for the variant of KAT where tests are only assumed to form a distributive lattice.

We write

Given two functions

. When

, and for all

. These notions are generalised to arbitrary complete lattices in

2.
Kleene algebra with hypotheses, closures, reductions

A
Kleene algebra

[
Con71
,
Koz91
]
is a tuple
(K,+,\cdot,^{*},0,1)
such that
(K,+,\cdot,0,1)
is an idempotent semiring, and
∗
is a unary operator on
K
such that for all
x,y\in K
the following axioms are satisfied:

1+x\cdot x^{*}\leq x^{*}x+y\cdot z\leq z\Rightarrow y^{*}\cdot x\leq zx+y\cdot z\leq y\Rightarrow x\cdot z^{*}\leq y

Here, as later in the paper, we write
x\leq y
as a shorthand for
x+y=y
. Given the idempotent semiring axioms,
\leq
is a partial order in every Kleene algebra, and all operations are monotone w.r.t. that order. The seemingly missing law
1+x^{*}\cdot x\leq x^{*}
is derivable from these axioms.

Languages over some alphabet, as well as binary relations on a given set, are standard examples of Kleene algebras.

We let
e,f
range over regular expressions over an alphabet
\Sigma
, defined by:

\displaystyle e,f::=e+f\;\;|\;\;e\cdot f\;\;|\;\;e^{*}\;\;|\;\;0\;\;|\;\;1\;\;|\;\;a\in\Sigma

We write
{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
for the set of such expressions, or simply
{\mathcal{T}}
when the alphabet is clear from the context.
We usually write
ef
for
e\cdot f
, and
e^{+}
for
e\cdot e^{*}
.
Given alphabets
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
, a function
h\colon\Sigma\to{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
extends
uniquely to a homomorphism
h\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\to{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
, referred to as the
homomorphism generated by
h
.
As usual, every regular expression
e
gives rise to a language
\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})
.
Given regular expressions
e,f
, we write
\mathsf{KA}\vdash e=f
when
e=f
is derivable from the axioms of Kleene algebra. (Equivalently, when the equation
e=f
holds universally, in all Kleene algebras.)

The central theorem of Kleene algebra is the following:

Theorem 2.1

(Soundness and Completeness of KA
[
Koz91
,
Kro91
,
Bof90
]
)
.

For all regular expressions
e,f\in{\mathcal{T}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}\vdash e=f\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket\,.

As a consequence, the equational theory of Kleene algebras is decidable.

2.1.
Hypotheses

Our goal is to extend this result to the case where we have additional hypotheses on some of the letters of the alphabet, or axioms restricting the behaviour of certain operations.
Those are represented by sets of
inequations
, i.e., pairs
(e,f)
of regular expressions written
e\leq f
for the sake of clarity.
Given a set
H
of such inequations, we write
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e\leq f
when the inequation
e\leq f
is derivable from the axioms of Kleene algebra and the hypotheses in
H
(similarly for equations).
By extension, we write
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash H^{\prime}
when
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e\leq f
for all
e\leq f
in
H^{\prime}
.
Since the ambient theory will systematically be KA, we will sometimes abbreviate
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash H^{\prime}
as
H\vdash H^{\prime}
, to alleviate notation.

Note that we consider letters of the alphabet as constants rather than variables.
In particular, while we have
\mathsf{KA}_{ba\leq ab}\vdash(a+b)^{*}\leq a^{*}b^{*}
, we do not have
\mathsf{KA}_{ba\leq ab}\vdash(a+c)^{*}\leq a^{*}c^{*}
.
Formally, we use a notion of derivation where there is no substitution rule, and where we have all instances of Kleene algebra axioms as axioms.
When we want to consider hypotheses that are universally valid, it suffices to use all their instances. For example, to define commutative Kleene algebra, we simply use the infinite set
\left\{ef\leq fe\mid e,f\in{\mathcal{T}}\right\}
.

2.3.
Reductions

As illustrated above, the overall strategy is to reduce completeness of
\mathsf{KA}_{H}
, for a given set of hypotheses
H
,
to completeness of Kleene algebra without hypotheses. The core idea is to provide a map
r
from expressions to expressions,
which incorporates the hypotheses
H
in the sense that
\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket={H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket
,
and such that
r(e)
is provably equivalent to
e
under the hypotheses
H
.
This idea leads to the unifying notion of
reduction
, developed in
[
KM14
,
DKPP19
,
KBS+20
]
.

Definition 2.9

(Reduction)
.

Assume
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and let
H
,
H^{\prime}
be sets of
hypotheses over
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
respectively. We say that
H

reduces to

H^{\prime}
if

(1)

\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash H^{\prime}
, and

there exists a map

r\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
such that for all

e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
,

(2)

\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e=r(e)
, and

(3)

{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Gamma^{*}\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.

We often refer to the witnessing map
r
itself as a reduction.
Note that condition (
1
) vanishes when
H^{\prime}=\emptyset
is empty.
Thanks to the first two conditions, the third condition can be strengthened into an equality:

Lemma 2.10
.

If
r
is a reduction from
H
to
H^{\prime}
as in the above definition,
then for all expressions
e
we have
{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket={H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Gamma^{*}
.

Proof.

By item
3
, it suffices to show
{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket\subseteq{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket
. We have

\displaystyle{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\right\rrbracket

(item
2
with
2.4
)

\displaystyle\subseteq{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\right\rrbracket={H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket

(item
1
with
2.6
) ∎

Generalising
2.8
, we get the following key property of reductions:

Theorem 2.11
.

Suppose
H
reduces to
H^{\prime}
. If
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}
is complete, then so is
\mathsf{KA}_{H}
.

Proof.

Let
r
be the map for the reduction from
H
to
H^{\prime}
.
For all
e,f\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
,

\displaystyle{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle={H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(f)\right\rrbracket

(
2.10
)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}\vdash r(e)

\displaystyle=r(f)

(completeness of
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}
)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash r(e)

\displaystyle=r(f)

(
r
a reduction, item
1
)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e

\displaystyle=f

(
r
a reduction, item
2
)∎

An important case is when
H^{\prime}=\emptyset
: given a reduction from
H
to
\emptyset
,

2.11
gives completeness
of
\mathsf{KA}_{H}
, by completeness of
\mathsf{KA}
. Such reductions to the empty set are what we ultimately aim for.
However, in order to decompose reductions into small and elementary pieces, we need
the extra generality allowed by our definition (e.g., so that reductions can be composed—
3.11
).

While we focus on completeness in this paper, note that reductions can also be used to
prove decidability:

Theorem 2.12
.

If
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}
is complete and decidable, and
H
reduces
to
H^{\prime}
via a computable function
r
, then
\mathsf{KA}_{H}
is decidable.

Proof.

By soundness of
\mathsf{KA}_{H}
(
2.4
) and by inspecting the above proof of
2.11
, we see that
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e=f
iff
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}\vdash r(e)=r(f)
.
∎

Example 2.13
.

We consider KA together with a global “top element”
\mathbb{T}
and the axiom
e\leq\mathbb{T}
. To make this precise in Kleene algebra with hypotheses, we assume an alphabet
\Sigma
with
\mathbb{T}\in\Sigma
, and take the set of hypotheses
H_{\mathbb{T}}=\left\{e\leq\mathbb{T}\mid e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\right\}
.

We claim that
H
reduces to
\emptyset
. The first condition is void since we target the empty set.
For the two other conditions, define the unique homomorphism

r\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)

such that
r(\mathbb{T})=\Sigma^{*}
(where we view
\Sigma
as the sum
a_{1}+...+a_{n}
of all the letters in
\Sigma
) and
r(a)=a
for
a\in\Sigma\setminus\left\{\mathbb{T}\right\}
.

Thanks to the hypotheses in
H
, we have
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash\mathbb{T}=\Sigma^{*}
, from which we easily deduce the second condition (
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e=r(e)
for all expressions
e
), by induction.
Concerning the third condition, notice that the restriction to words over the smaller alphabet vanishes since the alphabet remains the same in that example.
Then it suffices to observe that
H^{\star}_{\mathbb{T}}(L)
contains those words obtained from a word
w\in L
by replacing occurrences of
\mathbb{T}
in
w
by arbitrary words in
\Sigma^{*}
.

This gives a first proof that
\mathsf{KA}_{H_{\mathbb{T}}}
is complete, which we will revisit in
3.3
.
Note that this implies completeness w.r.t. validity of equations in all language models,
where
\mathbb{T}
is interpreted as the largest language: indeed, the
closed semantics
{H_{\mathbb{T}}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket-\right\rrbracket
is generated by such a model.

Remark 2.14
.

Reductions do not always exist.
In
2.7
, we discussed commutative KA as an instance of Kleene algebra with hypotheses
H
.
While
\mathsf{KA}_{C}
is complete in that case, there is no reduction from
C
to
\emptyset
, as

{C}^{\star}
does not preserve regularity. Indeed,
{C}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket(ab)^{*}\right\rrbracket=\left\{w\mid|w|_{a}=|w|_{b}\right\}
which is
not regular. The completeness proof in
[
Con71
,
Bru19
]
is self-contained, and does not rely
on completeness of KA.

3.
Tools for analysing closures and constructing reductions

To deal with concrete examples we still need tools to construct reductions.
We provide such tools in this section.
First, we give simpler conditions to obtain reductions in the special case where the underlying function is a homomorphism (
section

3.2
).
Second, we give a general technique to construct reductions via finite automata (
section

3.3
).
Third, we establish a few basic reductions for commonly encountered sets of hypotheses (
section

3.4
).
And fourth, we provide lemmas to compose reductions (
section

3.5
).

Before doing so, we mention elementary properties of the functions
{H}^{\star}
associated to sets
H
of hypotheses. Those properties will be useful first to obtain the aforementioned tools, and then later to deal with concrete examples.

3.1.
On closures w.r.t. sets of hypotheses

Given a set
H
of hypotheses, the function
{H}^{\star}
(
2.2
) actually is the least closure containing the following function:

\displaystyle H\colon\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigcup\left\{u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v\mid e\leq f\in H,~u\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket v\subseteq L\right\}

This function intuitively computes the words which are reachable from
L
in exactly one step. In particular, when
H
consists of inequations between words, we have
u\in H(L)
iff
u\mathrel{\mathchoice{\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\textstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\rightsquigarrow$}}}_{H}v
for some
v\in L
. (Note that we slightly abuse notation by writing
H
both for a set of hypotheses and for the associated function.)

Thanks to the above characterisation, many properties of
{H}^{\star}
can be obtained by analysing the simpler function
H
. To this end, we actually develop a general theory of least closures in
appendix

A
. We will state the relevant lemmas in the main text, delegating the corresponding proofs to that appendix.

Let us call a function between complete lattices
linear
when it preserves all joins, and
affine
when it preserves all non-empty joins (see
appendix

A
).
Not all sets of hypotheses yield linear (or even affine) functions: consider for instance
\left\{1\leq a+b\right\}
.
However, an important class of hypotheses do:

Lemma 3.1
.

If
H
is a set of hypotheses whose right-hand sides are words, then the functions
H
and
{H}^{\star}
are linear.

Proof.

When
H
consists of hypotheses of the shape
e\leq w
with
w
being a word, the definition of the function
H
simplifies to
H(L)=\bigcup\left\{u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v\mid e\leq w\in H,~uwv\in L\right\}
.
We deduce

\displaystyle H\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}L_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v\mid e\leq w\in H,~uwv\in\bigcup_{i\in I}L_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v\mid e\leq w\in H,~i\in I,~uwv\in L_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in I}\bigcup\left\{u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v\mid e\leq w\in H,~uwv\in L_{i}\right\}=\bigcup_{i\in I}H(L_{i})

Thus
H
is linear, and we deduce that so is
{H}^{\star}
by
A.10
.
∎

3.2.
Homomorphic reductions

The following result from
[
KBS+20
]
(cf. Remark
3.5
)
simplifies the conditions of a reduction, assuming the underlying map
r

is a homomorphism.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and let
H
,
H^{\prime}
be sets of
hypotheses over
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
respectively.
If

(1)

\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash H^{\prime}
,

and there exists a homomorphism

r\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
such that:

(2)

for all
a\in\Sigma
, we have
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash a=r(a)
,

(3)

for all
a\in\Gamma
, we have
\mathsf{KA}\vdash a\leq r(a)
, and

(4)

for all
e\leq f\in H
, we have
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}\vdash r(e)\leq r(f)
,

then
H

reduces to

H^{\prime}
, with
r
being the witness of the reduction.

Conditions (
3
) and (
4
) are stated as derivability conditions, but this is only a matter of convenience: all we need are the corresponding inclusions of languages—closed by
H^{\prime}
for (
4
).

We prove this proposition below; first we illustrate its usage by revisiting
2.13
.

Example 3.3
.

The reduction we defined to deal with
2.13
is a homomorphism.
Accordingly, we can use
3.2
to prove more easily that it is indeed a reduction from
H_{\mathbb{T}}
to the empty set, without ever having to understand the closure
H^{\star}_{\mathbb{T}}
:

•

the first condition is void since
H^{\prime}=\emptyset
;

•

the second and third condition hold trivially for all letters different from
\mathbb{T}
, on which
r
is the identity. For the letter
\mathbb{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}\vdash\mathbb{T}\leq\Sigma^{*}=r(\mathbb{T})

(because
\mathbb{T}\in\Sigma
)

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}_{H_{\mathbb{T}}}\vdash r(\mathbb{T})\leq\mathbb{T}

(by a single application of
H_{\mathbb{T}}
)

•

the fourth condition holds by completeness of KA:

\mathsf{KA}\vdash r(e)\leq\Sigma^{*}=r(\mathbb{T})
for all expressions
e
since any language is contained in
\Sigma^{*}
, by definition.
(Note that using completeness of KA is an overkill here.)∎

We now turn to proving
3.2
. We need the following auxiliary definition:
for a map
r\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
, define

\displaystyle\dot{r}\colon\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{*})

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigcup_{w\in L}\left\llbracket r(w)\right\rrbracket\,.

(Here words
w
are seen as regular expressions when fed to
r
.)
The function
\dot{r}
is linear. When
r
is a homomorphism, we have:

Fact 3.4
.

If
r
is a homomorphism then
\dot{r}
is a homomorphism and for all expressions
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
we have
\dot{r}(\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket)=\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.

Proof of
3.2
.

First, using that
r
is a homomorphism, we extend by induction the assumptions (2) and (3) into:

(2’)

for all
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
,
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash e=r(e)

(3’)

for all
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
,
\mathsf{KA}\vdash e\leq r(e)

From (3’) we deduce

(3”)

for all
L\subseteq\Gamma^{*}
,
L\subseteq\dot{r}(L)

(Note the restriction to words in
\Gamma^{*}
.) Indeed, by soundness of KA and (3’), we have

\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\subseteq\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
for all
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Gamma)
, and in particular

w\in\left\llbracket r(w)\right\rrbracket
for all words
w\in\Gamma^{*}
.

At this point we only have the third condition of a reduction left to show, i.e., that for all
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)
,
{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Gamma^{*}\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.
We proceed as follows:

\displaystyle{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Gamma^{*}

\displaystyle\subseteq\dot{r}({H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Gamma^{*})

(by (3”))

\displaystyle\subseteq\dot{r}({H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket)

(
\dot{r}
monotone)

\displaystyle\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}(\dot{r}(\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket))

(
\dagger
)

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket\,.

(by
3.4
)

It remains to show
(\dagger)~\dot{r}\circ{H}^{\star}\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\circ\dot{r}
.
Since
\dot{r}
is linear,
A.8
applies and it suffices to prove

(\ddagger)~\dot{r}\circ H\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\circ\dot{r}
.
Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
. Again since
\dot{r}
is linear, we have

\displaystyle\dot{r}(H(L))=\bigcup_{\begin{array}[]{c}e\leq f\in H\\
u\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket v\subseteq L\end{array}}\dot{r}(u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v)\,.

Accordingly, let
e\leq f\in H
and let
u,v
be words such that

u\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket v\subseteq L
.
By assumption (
4
), we have a derivation
\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}\vdash r(e)\leq r(f)
, whence, by monotonicity, another derivation

\mathsf{KA}_{H^{\prime}}\vdash r(u)r(e)r(v)\leq r(u)r(f)r(v)\,.

(1)

We deduce:

\displaystyle\dot{r}(u\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket v)

\displaystyle=\dot{r}(\left\llbracket uev\right\rrbracket)

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket r(uev)\right\rrbracket

(by
3.4
)

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket r(u)r(e)r(v))\right\rrbracket

(
r
homomorphism)

\displaystyle\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(u)r(f)r(v))\right\rrbracket

(by
2.4
applied to (
1
))

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(ufv)\right\rrbracket

(
r
homomorphism)

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}(\dot{r}(\left\llbracket ufv\right\rrbracket))

(by
3.4
)

\displaystyle={H^{\prime}}^{\star}(\dot{r}(u\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket v))\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}(\dot{r}(L))\,.

(by monotonicity)

Therefore, we get
\dot{r}(H(L))\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}(\dot{r}(L))
: we have proved
(\ddagger)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The idea to use two sets of hypotheses in Definition
2.9

is from
[
KBS+20
]
, where reductions are defined slightly differently: the alphabet is fixed (that is,
\Sigma=\Gamma
),
and the last condition is instead defined as
{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket={H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket\Rightarrow{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket={H^{\prime}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(f)\right\rrbracket
. An extra notion of
strong
reduction is then introduced,
which coincides with our definition if
\Sigma=\Gamma
. By allowing a change of alphabet, we do not need
to distinguish reductions and strong reductions.

3.2
is in
[
KBS+20
, Lemma 4.23]
, adapted here
to the case with two alphabets (this is taken care of in
loc. cit.
by assuming

{H^{\prime}}^{\star}
preserves languages over
\Gamma
).

3.3.
Automata-based reductions

Another recipe to construct reductions consists in using finite automata (see, e.g.,
[
ÉB95
,
KM14
,
DKPP19
]
).

Suppose, for instance, that we want to build a reduction from
H\triangleq\left\{a\leq bc\right\}
to the empty set.
Given an automaton for the language
L
, an automaton for the language
{H}^{\star}(L)
is easily obtained by adding “shortcuts”: we add an
a
-transition from state
i
to state
j
whenever there is a
bc
-labelled path from
i
to
j
.
Using Kleene’s theorem—the languages denoted by regular expressions are the languages accepted by finite automata—we define a function
r
on regular expressions accordingly: given an expression
e
, compute an automaton for its language, add shortcuts as above, and define
r(e)
to be any expression for the resulting automaton.
The first requirement of a reduction holds trivially since we target the empty set; the third one holds by construction; and since
\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\subseteq\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
(also by construction), we have
\mathsf{KA}\vdash e\leq r(e)
by completeness of KA (
2.1
). Therefore, it only remains to prove
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash r(e)\leq e
. This last step is the most delicate one; it can be obtained using the following technique, inspired from
[
DDP18
, Lemma 32]
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
H
be a set of hypotheses.
Given a (non-deterministic, finite) automaton
\mathcal{A}
, a labelling function
\mathbf{f}
from states to regular expressions is
compatible (with
\mathcal{A}
)
if
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash 1\leq\mathbf{f}(i)
whenever
i
is an accepting state, and
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash a\cdot\mathbf{f}(j)\leq\mathbf{f}(i)
whenever there is an
a
-transition from
i
to
j
(for
a
a letter, or
1
in case of an epsilon transition).

Any function associating to each state a regular expression for its language in the automaton is compatible (by completeness of KA); we call such labellings
canonical
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
H
be a set of hypotheses, let
\mathcal{A}
be an automaton, let
\mathbf{r}
be a canonical labelling and let
\mathbf{f}
be a compatible labelling. For all states
i
, we have
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash\mathbf{r}(i)\leq\mathbf{f}(i)
.

Proof.

Thanks to completeness of KA, this is a direct consequence of
[
DDP18
, Lemma 32]
(this lemma only gives the statement for a specific canonical labelling).

A more direct proof can be obtained as follows, using the matricial representation of automata
[
Koz91
]
.
Write the automaton
\mathcal{A}
matricially as
\left\langle u,M,v\right\rangle
, where
u
is the row vector of initial states,
M
is the square matrix of transitions, and
v
is the column vector of accepting states. Accordingly, see the labellings
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{f}
as column vectors.
Recall that an expression for the language of
\mathcal{A}
can be obtained by computing the product
u\cdot M^{*}\cdot v
in the Kleene algebra of matrices.
We have
\mathsf{KA}\vdash\mathbf{r}=M^{*}\cdot v
by completeness of KA, and the compatibility of
\mathbf{f}
can be restated as
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash v\leq\mathbf{f}
and
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash M\cdot\mathbf{f}\leq\mathbf{f}
.
By star induction on the left, we deduce
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash\mathbf{r}=M^{*}\cdot v\leq\mathbf{f}
.
∎

This proposition makes it possible to finish our example with
H\triangleq\left\{a\leq bc\right\}
.
Let
e
be a regular expression, let
\mathcal{A}
be an automaton for its language, and let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be the automaton obtained after adding shortcuts. Let
\mathbf{e}
and
\mathbf{r}
be canonical labellings of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, respectively. We have that
\mathbf{e}
is compatible with
\mathcal{A}
, but also with
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. Indeed, each new transition
i\xrightarrow{a}j
in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
comes from two transitions
i\xrightarrow{b}k\xrightarrow{c}j
in
\mathcal{A}
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash a\cdot\mathbf{e}(j)

\displaystyle\leq b\cdot c\cdot\mathbf{e}(j)

(hypothesis in
H
)

\displaystyle\leq b\cdot\mathbf{e}(k)

(
\mathbf{e}
is compatible with
\mathcal{A}
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{e}(i)

(
\mathbf{e}
is compatible with
\mathcal{A}
)

Therefore, by
3.7
,
\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash\mathbf{r}\leq\mathbf{e}
. It follows that

\mathsf{KA}_{H}\vdash r(e)=u\cdot\mathbf{r}\leq u\cdot\mathbf{e}=e
(using the row vector
u
of initial states of
\mathcal{A}
).

3.4.
Basic reductions

The following result collects several sets of hypotheses for which we have reductions to the empty set.
These mostly come from the literature; we establish them using the tools from the previous two sections. They form basic building blocks used in the more complex reductions that we present in the examples below.

Lemma 3.8
.

The following sets of hypotheses over
\Sigma
reduce to the empty set.

(1)

\left\{u_{i}\leq w_{i}\mid i\in I\right\}

with
u_{i},w_{i}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
|u_{i}|\leq 1
for all
i\in I

(2)

\left\{1=\sum_{a\in S_{i}}a\mid i\in I\right\}
with each
S_{i}\subseteq\Sigma
finite

(3)

\left\{e\leq 0\right\}
for
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)

(4)

\left\{ea\leq a\right\}
and
\left\{ae\leq a\right\}
for
a\in\Sigma
,
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma\setminus\left\{a\right\})

(5)

\left\{aa\leq a\right\}
for
a\in\Sigma

Proof.

(1)

This is
[
KM14
, Theorem 2]
. (The result mentions equations, but in the proof
only the relevant inequations are used.)
Intuitively, the example from Section
3.3
can easily be generalised: starting from an automaton
\mathcal{A}
, add
u_{i}
-labelled shortcuts for all
w_{i}
-labelled paths in
\mathcal{A}
to obtain an automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. There is a catch, however: if some of the
u_{i}
are letters appearing in some of the
w_{i}
, then
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is not necessarily closed: we may have to add shortcuts to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

We conclude this subsection with a lemma allowing one to construct reductions for hypotheses of the shape

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
f
be a regular expression and let

be a set of hypotheses.
Set
H_{f}\triangleq\left\{f\leq 1\right\}
.
If

(1)

{(H\cup H_{f})}^{\star}={H}^{\star}\circ H^{\star}_{f}

and there is a regular expression
f^{\prime}
such that:

(2)

H,f\leq 1\vdash f^{\prime}\leq 1

(3)

\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket\cup H_{f}\left\llbracket f^{\prime}\right\rrbracket\subseteq\left\llbracket f^{\prime}\right\rrbracket

then
H\cup H_{f}
reduces to
H
.

Proof.

Let
H_{1}\triangleq H\cup H_{f}
. We trivially have
H_{1}\vdash H
.
We use the automata technique from
section

3.3
to build the reduction:
given a regular expression
e
, compute an automaton for its language, add
f^{\prime}
-labelled self-loops to all states, and read back a regular expression
r(e)
.

By assumption 2, and using
3.7
like in
section

3.3
, we get
H_{1}\vdash r(e)\leq e
. It follows that
H_{1}\vdash r(e)=e
by KA completeness, since
\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\subseteq\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.
Therefore, it only remains to show the third condition of a reduction.
By construction, we have

\displaystyle\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket=\left\{u_{0}v_{0}\cdots v_{n-1}u_{n}\mid u_{0}\cdots u_{n}\in\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\text{ and }\forall i,v_{i}\in\left\llbracket f^{\prime}\right\rrbracket\right\}

We prove that this language is
H_{f}
-closed
(H_{f}\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket\subseteq\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket)
. Indeed, if we insert a word of
\left\llbracket f\right\rrbracket
into a word of
\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
, then it has to be either into one of the

in the above formula, or into one of the

. In both cases, we deduce by assumption 3 that the resulting word belongs to
\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.

The third condition of a reduction is
{H_{1}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\subseteq{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
.
By general closure properties, it suffices to show that
{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket
is
H_{f}
-closed
(H_{f}({H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket)\subseteq{H}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r(e)\right\rrbracket)
. In turn, since
H_{f}\circ{H}^{\star}\subseteq{H}^{\star}\circ{H_{f}}^{\star}
by assumption 1, this follows from the fact that

is

-closed, which we have just proved.
∎

3.5.
Composing reductions

The previous subsection gives reductions for uniform sets of equations. However,
in the examples we often start with a
collection
of hypotheses of different shapes,
which we wish to reduce to the empty set.
Therefore, we now discuss a few techniques for combining reductions.

First, reductions can be composed:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
and
H_{3}
be sets of hypotheses.
If
H_{1}
reduces to
H_{2}
and
H_{2}
reduces to
H_{3}
then
H_{1}
reduces to
H_{3}
.

Proof.

Let
r_{1}\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{1})\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{2})
and
r_{2}\colon{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{2})\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{3})
be reductions from
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
and from
H_{2}
to
H_{3}
, respectively.
We show that
r_{2}\circ r_{1}
is a reduction from
H_{1}
to
H_{3}
.

(1)

We have
H_{1}\vdash H_{2}
and
H_{2}\vdash H_{3}
, and therefore
H_{1}\vdash H_{3}
.

(2)

For
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{1})
, we have
H_{1}\vdash e=r_{1}(e)
.
Since
r_{2}(e)\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{2})
, we also have
H_{2}\vdash r_{1}(e)=r_{2}(r_{1}(e))
.
Since
H_{1}\vdash H_{2}
, we deduce
H_{1}\vdash e=r_{1}(e)=r_{2}(r_{1}(e))
.

(3)

For
e\in{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma_{1})
, we have:

\displaystyle{H_{1}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Sigma_{3}^{*}=({H_{1}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\cap\Sigma_{2}^{*})\cap\Sigma_{3}^{*}\subseteq{H_{2}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r_{1}(e)\right\rrbracket\cap\Sigma_{3}^{*}\subseteq{H_{3}}^{\star}\!\left\llbracket r_{2}(r_{1}(e))\right\rrbracket

using that
\Sigma_{3}\subseteq\Sigma_{2}
for the first equality, and then that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are reductions.
∎

Second, the identity function gives non-trivial reductions under certain assumptions:

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
H,H^{\prime}
be sets of hypotheses over a common alphabet.
If
H\vdash H^{\prime}
and
H\subseteq{H^{\prime}}^{\star}
, then

reduces to
H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We have
{H^{\prime}}^{\star}\subseteq{H}^{\star}
by
2.6
, whence
{H}^{\star}={H^{\prime}}^{\star}
via the second assumption.
Therefore, the identity map on terms fulfils the requirements of
2.9
.
∎

In particular, equiderivable sets of hypotheses reduce to each other:

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
H,H^{\prime}
be sets of hypotheses over a common alphabet.
If
H\vdash H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}\vdash H
then
H
and
H^{\prime}
reduce to each other.

Proof.

Thanks to
2.6
,
3.12
applies in both directions.
∎

Third, the following lemma makes it possible to obtain reductions for unions of hypotheses by considering those hypotheses separately.

.

Let

If

4.
Kleene algebra with tests

In this section we apply the machinery from the previous sections to obtain a modular completeness proof for Kleene algebra with tests
[
KS96
]
.

A
Kleene algebra with tests (KAT)
is a
Kleene algebra
K
containing a Boolean algebra
L
such that the meet of
L

coincides with the product of
K
, the join of
L
coincides with the
sum of
K
, the top element of
L
is the multiplicative identity of
K
, and the bottom elements of
K
and
L
coincide.

Syntactically, we fix two finite sets
\Sigma
and
\Omega
of primitive
actions and primitive tests.
We denote the set of Boolean expressions over alphabet
\Omega
by
{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}}
:

\phi,\psi::=\phi\vee\psi\;\;|\;\;\phi\wedge\psi\;\;|\;\;\lnot\phi\;\;|\;\;\bot\;\;|\;\;\top\;\;|\;\;o\in\Omega

We write
\vdash_{\mathsf{BA}}\phi=\phi^{\prime}
when this equation is derivable from Boolean algebra axioms
[
BB70
,
DP90
]
, and similarly for inequations.

We let
\alpha,\beta
range over
atoms
: elements of the finite set
\mathsf{At}\triangleq 2^{\Omega}
. Those may be seen as valuations for Boolean expressions, or as complete conjunctions of literals:
\alpha
is implicitly seen as the Boolean formula
\bigwedge_{\alpha(o)=1}o\wedge\bigwedge_{\alpha(o)=0}\lnot o
. They form the atoms of the Boolean algebra generated by
\Omega
.
We write
\alpha\models\phi
when
\phi
holds under the valuation
\alpha
. A key property of Boolean algebras is that for all atoms
\alpha
and formulas
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\models\phi~\Leftrightarrow~\vdash_{\mathsf{BA}}\alpha\leq\phi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\vdash_{\mathsf{BA}}\phi=\bigvee_{\alpha\models\phi}\alpha

The KAT terms over alphabets
\Sigma
and
\Omega
are the regular expressions over the alphabet
\Sigma+{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}}
:
{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{KAT}}}\triangleq{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}})
.
We write
\mathsf{KAT}\vdash e=f
when this equation is derivable from the axioms of KAT, and similarly for inequations.

The standard interpretation of KAT associates to each term a language of guarded strings.
A
guarded string
is a sequence of the form
\alpha_{0}a_{0}\alpha_{1}a_{1}\ldots a_{n-1}\alpha_{n}

with
a_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
i<n
, and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathsf{At}
for all
i\leq n
.
We write
\mathcal{GS}
for the set
\mathsf{At}\times(\Sigma\times\mathsf{At})^{*}

6.
Kleene Algebra with Observations

A
Kleene algebra with Observations (KAO)
is a Kleene algebra which also contains a Boolean algebra,
but the connection between the Boolean
algebra and the Kleene algebra is different than for KAT: instead of
having the axioms
\top=1
and
\phi\wedge\psi=\phi\cdot\psi
for all

\phi,\psi\in{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}}
, we only have

\phi\wedge\psi\leq\phi\cdot\psi

[
KBR+19
]
. This system was
introduced to allow for concurrency and tests in a Kleene algebra
framework, because associating
\phi\cdot\psi
and
\phi\wedge\psi

in a concurrent setting is no longer appropriate:
\phi\wedge\psi
is
one event, where we instantaneously test whether both
\phi
and
\psi
are
true, while
\phi\cdot\psi
performs first the test
\phi
, and then

\psi
, and possibly other things can happen between those tests in
another parallel thread. Hence, the behaviour of
\phi\wedge\psi

should be included in
\phi\cdot\psi
, but they are no longer
equivalent.

Algebraically this constitutes a small change, and an ad-hoc completeness proof is in
[
KBR+19
]

, which is not present in
[
KBR+19
]

emphasise the modular aspect of the approach.

Note that even if we add the axiom

and

need not be an element of the Boolean algebra. In particular, in contrast to KAT, we do not have

when

are distinct atoms.

Similar to KAT, we add the additional axioms of KAO to KA as hypotheses. The additional axioms of KAO are the axioms of Boolean algebra and the axioms specifying the interaction between the two algebras. The KAO-terms are the same as the KAT-terms: regular expression over the alphabet
\Sigma+{\mathcal{T}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{BA}}}

Zhang et al. observed in

8.

One obtains a complete axiomatisation w.r.t. language models fairly easily: it suffices to state that converse is an involution that distribute over sums and products, reversing the arguments of the latter:

\displaystyle x^{\circ\circ}=x

\displaystyle(x+y)^{\circ}=x^{\circ}+y^{\circ}

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)^{\circ}=y^{\circ}\cdot x^{\circ}

(I)

Like with the constant

\displaystyle x\leq x\cdot x^{\circ}\cdot x

\left\langle i,j\right\rangle

Bloom, Bernátsky, Ésik, and Stefanescu have shown that adding this axiom actually yields a complete axiomatisation w.r.t. relational models

ÉB95
]

We show in this section that we can integrate these axioms for converse together with the axioms for KAT, and prove completeness of KAT with converse (KAT

12.
Conclusions and future work

We presented a general toolbox for proving completeness of Kleene algebra with hypotheses.
While our examples demonstrate the rather wide applicability of our techniques, there are natural extensions that we have not covered here. For instance KAT+B!
[
GKM14
]
and KAO with a full element and/or converse seem to fit our framework. We hope we have provided enough tools and examples such that these theories can be investigated easily and in a principled way in the future.

Of course, there are Kleene algebra extensions that we cannot cover, a priori. For instance, for
action algebras

[
Pra90
]
and
action lattices

[
Koz94
]
, it is not clear how to interpret the new operations as letters with additional structure.
Finding reference models and proving completeness for such theories remains an important challenge.

There are also variations of Kleene algebra where some of the axioms weakened or removed, such as left-handed Kleene algebra
[
KS12
,
DDP18
]
(where one of the star-induction rules is removed) or Kleene algebra with abnormal termination
[
Mam17
]
(where the axiom
x0=0
is no longer valid). In the former case, the equational theory remains the same, so that except for some of the basic reductions (e.g.,
3.8
4
), most of the framework we developed here can be reused. In the latter case instead, it is not clear how to proceed since the remaining axioms are no longer complete w.r.t. the language interpretation.

The general theory proposed here results in completeness with respect to a canonical
language model, defined via language closure. In several instances however, there are other
reference models for which we would like to obtain completeness. For instance, in the case of KAT,
we provided a separate argument to relate the canonical model defined via closure to the model
of guarded strings, which is a standard model of KAT. For NetKAT, there is a similar model of guarded
strings, which we have not considered in our treatment. It would be interesting to identify a
common pattern, and to try and develop generic tools that help transporting our results
to such reference models.

Another direction of future work is that of decidability and complexity. Whenever our reduction technique yields a proof of completeness, and the reduction itself is computable (as it is in all examples), this immediately entails a decidability result, via decidability of KA (
2.12
). However, in general this is far from an efficient procedure: computing the reduction itself can lead to a blow-up. Developing tools that help to obtain more efficient decision procedures is left for future work. This problem may well be related to the problem discussed in the previous paragraph, on identifying suitable reference models.

A.2.

Let us assume that

Definition A.14

A monotone function

Lemma A.15
.

Let

be a closure. The function

is contextual iff for all

c(x)\cdot c(y)\leq c(x\cdot y)

Proof.

If

Conversely, we have

using extensivity (i.e.,
