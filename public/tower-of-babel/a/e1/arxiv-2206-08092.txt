Abstract
We consider the robust linear regression model 𝒚 = X ​ β ∗ + 𝜼 \bm{y}=X\beta^{*}+\bm{\eta} , where an adversary oblivious to the design X ∈ ℝ n × d X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} may choose 𝜼 \bm{\eta} to corrupt all but a (possibly vanishing) fraction of the observations 𝒚 \bm{y} in an arbitrary way. Recent work [ dLN+21 , dNS21 ] has introduced efficient algorithms for consistent recovery of the parameter vector. These algorithms crucially rely on the design matrix being well-spread (a matrix is well-spread if its column span is far from any sparse vector). In this paper, we show that there exists a family of design matrices lacking well-spreadness such that consistent recovery of the parameter vector in the above robust linear regression model is information-theoretically impossible. We further investigate the average-case time complexity of certifying well-spreadness of random matrices. We show that it is possible to efficiently certify whether a given n n -by- d d Gaussian matrix is well-spread if the number of observations is quadratic in the ambient dimension. We complement this result by showing rigorous evidence —in the form of a lower bound against low-degree polynomials— of the computational hardness of this same certification problem when the number of observations is o ⁡ ( d 2 ) o(d^{2}) .

In this paper we provide, to a large extent, answers to these and related questions.

2.1
Statistical lower bounds for regression

Recall the linear model in
1.2
,

\bm{y}=X\beta^{*}+\bm{\eta},

where we observe (a realization of) the random vector
\bm{y}
, the matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is a known fixed design, the vector
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the unknown parameter of interest, and the noise vector
\bm{\eta}
has independent, symmetrically distributed coordinates with
\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\bm{\eta}_{i}}}\right\rvert\leqslant 1}}\right\}=\alpha
. We will restrict our discussion to matrices satisfying
{X}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}X=n\cdot\mathrm{Id}
, so that —up to scaling— there is no difference between prediction and parameter error.

To obtain an information-theoretic lower bound, we cast the problem as a distinguishing problem among
\ell
hypotheses of the form:

\displaystyle H_{i}:\quad\bm{y}=X\beta_{i}+\bm{\eta}\,,

(2.1)

where we ought to make the vectors
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as far as possible from each other.
It is remarkably easy to see that a small degree of spreadness is necessary to obtain any error guarantee in oblivious regression
[
dNS21
]
. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be
o(1/\alpha)
-spread, then there exists a vector
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a set
S
of cardinality
n-O(1/\alpha)
such that
\lVert X_{S}\beta\rVert_{2}\leqslant o(1)\cdot\lVert X\beta\rVert_{2}
.
8
8

8

X_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is the matrix obtained from
X
by zeroing rows with index in
[n]\setminus S
.

The problem with such a design matrix is that with probability
\Omega(1)
all nonzero entries in
(X-X_{S})\beta
will be corrupted by (possibly unbounded) noise. As a result no estimator can provide guarantees of the form
\E\lVert\hat{\beta}(\bm{y})-\beta^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant\gamma
for any
\gamma>0
. In other words, approximate recovery of the parameter vector is impossible.

Going beyond this
o(1/\alpha)
barrier, however, turns out to be non-trivial.
One issue with the above reasoning is that
even if we knew
the uncorrupted entries, we would not be able to recover the hidden vector (with any bounded error), as no such entry contains information over
\beta^{*}
. In contrast, for any
X
that is
m
-spread, with
m\geqslant\Omega(1/\alpha)
, if we knew the uncorrupted entries, after filtering out the corrupted ones, the classical least squares estimator would yield error guarantees

\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\hat{\beta}(\bm{y})-\beta^{*}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{m\alpha}}}\right)\,.

That is, knowing the uncorrupted entries one could achieve constant error for
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d/\alpha}}\right)
-spread design matrices. Notice
Theorem

1.3
implies that, there exist oblivious regression instances where no estimator can achieve these guarantees.

We overcome this barrier with a construction consisting of two main ingredients:

1.

An
m
-spread matrix
X
and a set of vectors
\beta
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert X_{S}\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant o(1)\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert X\beta}}\right\rVert_{2}

(2.2)

for some
S\subseteq[n]
with
\lvert S\rvert\geqslant n-m
, and the subspace spanned by these vectors is high dimensional.
That is, a matrix whose column span contains many nearly orthogonal sparse vectors.

2.

A distribution

over
\mathbb{R}
, for the entries of
\bm{\eta}

Low shift-sensitivity:

Insensitivity to scaling:
the Kullback-Leibler divergence does not change significantly upon scaling in the sense that

Sparsity of the noiseless observation vectors

Noise distributions with low sensitivity

It turns out that constructing a distribution with low sensitivity is straightforward. We consider the symmetric geometric distribution
\mathcal{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,\lambda}}\right)
with probability mass function

p(k)=\begin{cases}\alpha,&k=c\\
\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot\lambda(1-\lambda)^{|k|-1},&k=c\pm 1,c\pm 2,c\pm 3,...\end{cases}

Clearly,
\rho\cdot\mathcal{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\lambda}}\right)
is symmetric and satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{z}\sim\rho\cdot\mathcal{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\lambda}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lvert\bm{z}\rvert\leqslant 1}}\right)=\alpha
for any
\rho>1
and any

we can now blow up the error by scaling up
\sigma\cdot\bm{y}=\bm{X}(\sigma\cdot\beta)+\sigma\cdot\bm{\eta}
and
\sigma\cdot\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{X}(\sigma\cdot\beta^{\prime})+\sigma\cdot\bm{\eta}
, for any
\sigma>0
, without making the distinguishing problem easier.

2.2
Differences between well-spreadness and RIP, RE properties

In the context of compressed sensing we are given
n\leqslant d
observations of the form
\bm{y}_{i}=\langle M_{i},\beta\rangle+\bm{\eta}_{i}
with
M_{i},\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\eta}
being additive noise. In order to guarantee recovery of the compressed vector
\beta
, RIP
[
CT05
,
Don06
,
CRT06
,
KT07
]
is arguably the most popular condition to enforce on the sensing matrix:

Definition 2.1

(Restricted isometry property)
.

We say a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfies the
(k,\delta)
-restricted isometry property (RIP) if

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\lVert v\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant\lVert Mv\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant(1+\delta)\lVert v\rVert_{2}^{2}

for every vector
v
with at most
k
nonzero entries.

We argue here that the relation between well-spreadness and oblivious regression fundamentally differs from that of RIP and compressed sensing in two ways.

First, while state-of-the-art algorithms for compressed sensing rely on RIP in order to filter out the noise in the observations and recover the hidden vector, it is known that small prediction error can be achieved in exponential time
without
any constraint on the sensing matrix
[
BTW07
]
.
In contrast,
Theorem

1.3
shows that in the context of oblivious regression, no algorithm can achieve even small prediction error for a family of design matrices that are not sufficiently well-spread.

Second, RIP is not purely a condition of the column span of the sensing matrix. In particular, if
M
satisfies
(k,\delta)
-RIP, then the kernel of
M
must be
\Omega(k)
-spread
[
GMM21
]
.
10
10

10

The careful reader may have noticed that
\delta
seems to play no role in this implication. In fact, the relation is more general than what we consider here. See
[
GMM21
]
.
Conversely, it is easy to construct matrices with well-spread column span and kernel containing sparse vectors.

As the following examples show, it is easy to construct matrices that satisfy RIP but are not well-spread and vice versa.

Example 2.2

(RIP but not even
1
-spread)
.

Let
\bm{W}\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)^{(n-1)\times(d-1)}
and consider the following
n
-by-
d
matrix (we do not fix the relation between
n
and
d
),

\displaystyle\bm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1}}\bm{W}\end{bmatrix}.

If
n\gtrsim\delta^{-2}k\log{d}
, then with high probability,
\bm{M}
satisfies
(k,\delta)
-RIP.
However,
\bm{M}
is not even
1
-spread, since its column span contains the canonical basis vector
e_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Example 2.3

(Well-spread but not satisfying RIP)
.

Let
\bm{W}\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)^{n\times(d-1)}
and consider the following
n
-by-
d
matrix,

\displaystyle\bm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}v&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{W}\end{bmatrix},

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a unit vector parallel or highly correlated to the first column of
\bm{W}
.
Then
\bm{M}
cannot satisfy RIP or RE.
However, if
n\geqslant Cd
for some sufficiently large absolute constant
C
, then it is easy to verify that,
\bm{M}
is
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
-spread with high probability.

3.3
Spreadness and distortion

Definition 3.2

(
\ell_{p}
-spreadness)
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
,
\delta\in[0,1]
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
m\leqslant n
.
A vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread if for every subset
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leqslant m
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}\leqslant\delta\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{p}.

A subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread if every vector
v\in V
is
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread.
A matrix is said to be
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread if its column span
\mathrm{cspan}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
is
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread.

When the ambient dimension
n
is clear from the context, there are three parameters, i.e.
p,m,\delta
, to be specified in
Definition

3.2
.
If the value of
p
is not specified, set
p=2
by default. That is,
(m,\delta)
-spreadness means
(m,\delta)
-
\ell_{2}
-spreadness.
In certain cases (e.g. oblivious linear regression), we are more interested in capturing the dependence of
m
than the dependence of
\delta
on other paramters (e.g. the ambient dimension
n
).
Then it is more convenient to hide
\delta
as long as it is
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.
Concretly, we say a vector, or a subspace, or a matrix is
m
-
\ell_{p}
-spread if there exists a absolute constant
c\in(0,1)
such that it is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,c}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread.

We introduce the following definition that is closely related to spreadness and has important algorithmic implications in
Section

5.1
.

Definition 3.3

(
\ell_{p}
-vs-
\ell_{q}
distortion)
.

Given
1\leqslant p<q
, the
\ell_{p}
-vs-
\ell_{q}
distortion of a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\Delta_{p,q}(v):=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{q}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{p}}\cdot n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

The
\ell_{p}
-vs-
\ell_{q}
distortion of a subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\Delta_{p,q}(V):=\max_{v\in V,v\neq 0}\Delta_{p,q}(v).

The
\ell_{p}
-vs-
\ell_{q}
distortion of a matrix
X
is defined by

\Delta_{p,q}(X):=\Delta_{p,q}(\mathrm{cspan}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)).

By Hölder’s inequality and monotonicity of
\ell_{p}
norm, it is easy to check
1\leqslant\Delta_{p,q}(v)\leqslant n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}
for any nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Note that for a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Delta_{p,q}(v)=1
if and only if
|v_{1}|=\cdots=|v_{n}|
;
\Delta_{p,q}(v)=n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}
if and only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{0}=1
.
Intuitively, low distortion implies well-spreadness, which is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
1\leqslant p<q
and
V
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.

If
\Delta_{p,q}(V)\leqslant\Delta
, then
V
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,\delta_{p}}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread with

\delta_{p}=(m/n)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\Delta.

2.

If
V
is not
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,\delta}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread, then

\Delta_{p,q}(V)>\delta(n/m)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

3.

If
\Delta_{p,q}(V)\leqslant\Delta
, then
V
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,\delta_{q}}}\right)
-
\ell_{q}
-spread with

\delta_{q}^{q}=1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{-p}-(m/n)^{p}}}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}.

Proof.

1.

By Hölder’s inequality, for any nonzero vector
x
,

\lvert\supp{x}\rvert^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}}\leqslant 1.

For any nonzero vector
x\in V
and any subset
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leqslant m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}\leqslant|S|^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{q}\leqslant|S|^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}\leqslant\Delta(m/n)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}.

2.

Since
V
is not
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,\delta}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread, then by definition there exist
x\in V
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}=1
and
S\subset[n]
with
|S|\leqslant m
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}>\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}=\delta.

Applying Hölder’s inequality,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{q}\geqslant|S|^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}>\delta|S|^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}.

Then,

\Delta_{p,q}(V)\geqslant\Delta_{p,q}(x)=n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}}>\delta(n/m)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

3.

Fix an arbitrary vector
x\in V
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}=1
.
Then for any subset
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leqslant m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}\leqslant|S|^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{q}\leqslant m^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

As
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}\geqslant\Delta^{-1}n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{p}^{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{-p}-(m/n)^{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}n^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality again,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{q}\geqslant\lvert\bar{S}\rvert^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{p}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{-p}-(m/n)^{p}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{q}^{q}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{q}^{q}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{q}^{q}\leqslant 1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta^{-p}-(m/n)^{p}}}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}.

∎

In particular, given

and a subspace

, then
V
is both
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread and
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
-
\ell_{q}
-spread.
On the other hand, if
V
is not
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)
-
\ell_{p}
-spread, then
\Delta_{p,q}(V)\geqslant\omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.

To better understand the hardness result in

Definition 5.4

Given

, if there exists
i\in[n]
such that
\alpha_{i}
is odd or
\alpha_{i}=2
, then
\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})=0
by
Lemma

5.11
.
Thus, we define the following set

S(k,m):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:|\alpha|=k,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\alpha}}\right\rVert_{0}=m,\alpha_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0}}\right\}\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{4,6,8,...}}\right\}\text{ for all }i\in[n]}}\right\}.

As
\sigma^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)^{-C}
and
D\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)^{C}
, we have
\sigma^{2}\leqslant 1/(k-1)
for any
k\leqslant D
.
Using
Lemma

5.11
, for
\alpha\in S(k,m)
, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\prod_{\alpha_{i}\neq 0}8^{\alpha_{i}}\rho^{2-\alpha_{i}}\leqslant 8^{k}\rho^{2m-k}.

Note that
S(k,m)
is empty if
m>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k/4}}\right\rfloor
.
And it is easy to see

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert S(k,m)}}\right\rvert\leqslant{n\choose m}m^{k/2}\leqslant n^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k/4}}\right)^{k/2}.

Then, for
k\geqslant 4
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
|\alpha|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k/4}}\right\rfloor}\sum_{\alpha\in S(k,m)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\sum_{m=1}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k/4}}\right\rfloor}n^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k/4}}\right)^{k/2}8^{k}\rho^{2m-k}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k/4}}\right)^{k/2}8^{k}\rho^{-k}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\rho^{2}}}\right)^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor k/4}}\right\rfloor+1}-n\rho^{2}}{n\rho^{2}-1}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k/4}}\right)^{k/2}8^{k}\rho^{-k}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\rho^{2}}}\right)^{k/4+1}}{n\rho^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot k^{k/2}n^{k/4}\rho^{-k/2}4^{k}.

Let
\bm{u}
and
\bm{u}^{\prime}
be independent uniformly random vectors on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Using
Lemma

5.10
, for
k\geqslant 4
, we have

\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
|\alpha|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}\leqslant(k/d)^{k/2}\cdot 2\cdot k^{k/2}n^{k/4}\rho^{-k/2}4^{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{512k^{4}n}{d^{2}\rho^{2}}}}\right)^{k/4}.

Finally, by
Lemma

5.9
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L^{\leqslant D}(\bm{A})^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{D}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
|\alpha|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}=1+\sum_{k\geqslant 4}^{D}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
|\alpha|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E h_{\alpha_{i}}(\bm{x})}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\sum_{k\geqslant 4}^{D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{512k^{4}n}{d^{2}\rho^{2}}}}\right)^{k/4}\leqslant 1+\sum_{k\geqslant 4}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{512n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)^{4C}}{d^{2}\rho^{2}}}}\right)^{k/4}.

If there exists a constant
c\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{512n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)^{4C}}{d^{2}\rho^{2}}\leqslant c
, i.e.
d\geqslant\sqrt{512/c}\sqrt{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log n}}\right)^{2C}
,
then we have

\E_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L^{\leqslant D}(\bm{A})^{2}}}\right]\leqslant O(1).

∎

Appendix A
Concentration bounds

Theorem A.1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},...,\bm{X}_{n}
be independent Bernoulli ranodm variables with parameter

.
Then for any

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}-np}}\right\rvert\geqslant t}}\right)\leqslant 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{3np}}}\right).

Let

.

Let

.

Definition A.4

(Sub-Gaussian norm)
.

The sub-Gaussian norm of a
d
-dimensional random vector

is defined by

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\lVert v\rVert_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t>0:\E\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\bm{x},v}}\right\rangle^{2}}{t^{2}}}}\right)\leqslant 2}}\right\}.

Theorem A.5

(
[
Ver18
]
, Theorem 4.6.1)
.

Let
\bm{A}
be an
n\times d
random matrix with independent rows

Suppose

Theorem A.6

(Well-spreadness of sub-Gaussian matrices)
.

Let
\bm{A}
be an
n\times d
random matrix with independent rows
\bm{A}_{1},...,\bm{A}_{n}
.
Suppose
\bm{A}_{i}
’s have zero mean, identity covariance, and

Then there exist absolute constants

such that
\bm{A}
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c_{1}n,c_{2}}}\right)
-spread with probability at least
1-\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}\right)

.

Proof.

In the following,
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5}\in(0,1)
are sufficiently small constants that only depend on
K
and the absolute constant
C
in
Theorem

A.5
.
Suppose
d\leqslant c_{3}n
and let
k=c_{1}n
.
We will show that, with high probability, for any nonzero
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=k
, one has
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\bm{A}_{S}v}}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant c_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\bm{A}v}}\right\rVert_{2}

\bm{A}_{S}
is a

submatrix of
\bm{A}
with rows indexed by

.

Fix a set

with
|S|=k
. By

, with probability at least

\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A}_{S})\leqslant\sqrt{k}+C^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{c_{4}n}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{c_{1}}+C^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{c_{3}}+\sqrt{c_{4}}}}\right)}}\right)\sqrt{n},

Using

\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A}_{S})\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{c_{1}}+C^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{c_{3}}+\sqrt{c_{4}}}}\right)}}\right)\sqrt{n}

.
Using

\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})\geqslant\sqrt{n}-C^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{c_{5}n}}}\right)\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-C^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{c_{3}}+\sqrt{c_{5}}}}\right)}}\right)\sqrt{n}

Appendix B
NP-hardness of deciding well-spreadness

We prove
Theorem

B.2
that shows deciding whether a matrix satisfies a given well-spreadness condition is NP-hard.
To cope with computational complexity issues with numbers, we will assume all input numbers to be rational.
For a rational number
r\in\mathbb{Q}
, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle r}}\right\rangle
denote its encoding length, i.e. the length of its representation.
For a rational matrix
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times d}
, let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle A}}\right\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle A_{ij}}}\right\rangle
denote its encoding length.

Problem B.1
.

Given as input
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times d}
,
m\in[n]
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{Q}
, decide whether
A
is
(m,\delta)
-spread.

Theorem B.2
.

B.1
is NP-hard.

To prove
Theorem

B.2
, we will show the following problem is NP-hard and there exists a polynomial-time reduction from this problem to
B.1
.

Problem B.3
.

Given as input
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{p\times n}
,
m\in[n]
, and
\delta\in\mathbb{Q}
, decide whether
\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
is
(m,\delta)
-spread.

Following
[
BDMS13
,
TP14
]
, our proof of the NP-hardness of
B.3
is based on a reduction from the problem of deciding
matrix spark
(
B.5
).

Definition B.4

(Matrix spark)
.

The spark of a matrix
A
is the smallest number
k
such that there exists a set of
k
columns of
A
that are linearly dependent.
Equivalently,

\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right):=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{0}:Ax=0,x\neq 0}}\right\}.

Problem B.5
.

Given as input
A\in\mathbb{Q}^{p\times n}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, decide whether
\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)>m
.

By a reduction from the NP-complete
k
-clique problem, i.e. deciding whether a given simple graph has a clique of size
k
,
B.5
is proven to be NP-hard in
[
TP14
]
.
Moreover, the matrices in the hard instances of
B.5
are integer matrices whose entry-wise encoding length is bounded by a polynomial in
p
and
n
.

Theorem B.6
.

B.3
is NP-hard.

Proof.

Let
(A,m)
be a hard instance of
B.5
given by
[
TP14
]
.
Let
P=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
. It is known that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P}}\right\rangle
is bounded by some polynomial in
p
and
n
.
Our strategy is to choose an appropriate rational number
\delta\in(0,1)
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\delta}}\right\rangle
bounded by some polynomial in
p
and
n
such that the following is true.
When we give the instance (
A,m,\delta
) to an oracle of
B.3
,
if the answer is YES, then
\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)>m
;
if the answer is NO, then
\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)\leqslant m
.
If such a
\delta
exists, then we have a polynomial-time reduction from
B.5
to
B.3
, and as a result,
B.3
is NP-hard.

In the following, we show how to construct such a
\delta\in(0,1)
.
For the case when the oracle answers YES, it is straightforward to see
\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)>m
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
.
For the case when the oracle answers NO, we consider the contrapositive.
Assume
\mathrm{spark}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)>m
.
We want a
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v_{S}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert v}}\right\rVert_{2}
for any nonzero
v\in\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
and any
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|\leqslant m
.

Take an arbitrary nonzero vector
x\in\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
. Without loss of generality, assume
\lvert x_{1}\rvert\geqslant\lvert x_{2}\rvert\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lvert x_{n}\rvert
. Let
S=[m]
and
\bar{S}=[n]\setminus S
. Then it suffices to upper bound
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{2}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{2}
by
\delta
.
Let
A_{S}
be the
m\times k
submatrix of
A
with columns indexed by
S
and define
A_{\bar{S}}
likewise.
Since
x\in\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle Ax=0

\displaystyle\iff A_{S}x_{S}+A_{\bar{S}}x_{\bar{S}}=0

\displaystyle\implies\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A_{S}x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A_{\bar{S}}x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{2}}\leqslant\frac{\sigma_{\max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\bar{S}}}}\right)}{\sigma_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{S}}}\right)}.

It is easy to see

\sigma_{\max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\bar{S}}}}\right)\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A_{\bar{S}}}}\right\rVert_{F}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert A}}\right\rVert_{F}\leqslant\sqrt{pn}\cdot P.

From the proof of
[
BDMS13
, Theorem 4]
, we know

\sigma_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{S}}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(pmP^{2}}}\right)^{1-m}\geqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(pnP^{2}}}\right)^{1-p}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x_{S}}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert x}}\right\rVert_{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{\min}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{S}}}\right)^{2}/\sigma_{\max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\bar{S}}}}\right)^{2}}}\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(pnP^{2}}}\right)^{-p}}}\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(pnP^{2}}}\right)^{p}+1}}\right)}.

Set
\delta=1-\frac{1}{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(pnP^{2}}}\right)^{p}+1}}\right)}
.
Then
\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
is
(m,\delta)
-spread.
Moreover,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\delta}}\right\rangle\leqslant f(p,n)
for some polynomial
f
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Theorem

B.2
.

Proof.

Given
Theorem

B.6
, it only remains to show there exists a polynomial-time reduction from
B.3
to
B.1
.
It is well-known that, given as input a matrix
X\in\mathbb{Q}^{p\times n}
, Gaussian elimination is able to produce in polynomial time a matrix

such that
\mathrm{ker}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)=\mathrm{cspan}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle Y}}\right\rangle
is polynomial in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle X}}\right\rangle
.
∎

Theorem

B.2
establishes the NP-hardness of deciding whether a given matrix is

-spreadness when

Nevertheless, this result has a major limitation in the conext of oblivious regression.
That is, the parameter

1-o(1)
that this result reveals almost nothing about the hardness of the more interesting case when
