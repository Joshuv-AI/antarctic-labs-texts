Pop, Crackle, Snap (and Pow): Some Facets of Shards
Colin Defant, Nathan Williams

Abstract
. Reading cut the hyperplanes in a real central arrangement ℋ \mathcal{H} into pieces called shards , which reflect order-theoretic properties of the arrangement. We show that shards have a natural interpretation as certain generators of the fundamental group of the complement of the complexification of ℋ \mathcal{H} . Taking only positive expressions in these generators yields a new poset that we call the pure shard monoid . When ℋ \mathcal{H} is simplicial, its poset of regions is a lattice, so it comes equipped with a pop-stack sorting operator 𝖯𝗈𝗉 \mathsf{Pop} . In this case, we use 𝖯𝗈𝗉 \mathsf{Pop} to define an embedding 𝖢𝗋𝖺𝖼𝗄𝗅𝖾 \mathsf{Crackle} of Reading’s shard intersection order into the pure shard monoid. When ℋ \mathcal{H} is the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group, we also define a poset embedding 𝖲𝗇𝖺𝗉 \mathsf{Snap} of the shard intersection order into the positive braid monoid; in this case, our three maps are related by 𝖲𝗇𝖺𝗉 = 𝖢𝗋𝖺𝖼𝗄𝗅𝖾 ⋅ 𝖯𝗈𝗉 \mathsf{Snap}=\mathsf{Crackle}\cdot\mathsf{Pop} .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Introduction (
Section

1
)

Throughout this paper, we let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite central irreducible real hyperplane arrangement. Salvetti introduced a certain CW complex associated to
\mathcal{H}
and used it to provide a presentation of the fundamental group of the complement of the complexification of
\mathcal{H}
. We prove that Salvetti’s generating set is parameterized by shards, which Reading introduced and used to define his shard intersection order in the case when
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial. We introduce the
pure shard monoid
, which is the monoid generated by Salvetti’s generators; it comes equipped with a natural partial order that we believe deserves further attention. In the case when
\mathcal{H}
is an arrangement of rank
2
with
m
hyperplanes, we prove that the interval from the identity element to the
full twist
in the pure shard monoid is a planar lattice with rank generating function
1+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(2\binom{m}{k}-2\right)q^{k}\right)+q^{m}
and with
m2^{m-2}
maximal chains.

We then assume
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial. In this case, there is a known characterization of the shard intersection order involving the pop-stack sorting operator
\mathsf{Pop}
on the poset of regions of
\mathcal{H}
. We introduce a new map
\mathsf{Crackle}
, and we prove that it is a poset embedding of the shard intersection order into the pure shard monoid.

Next, we specialize further to the case when
\mathcal{H}
is the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group
W
. We introduce another map
\mathsf{Snap}
, and we prove that it is a poset embedding of the shard intersection order on
W
into the weak order on the positive braid monoid. The restriction of
\mathsf{Snap}
to the set
\mathrm{Sort}(W,c)
of
c
-sortable elements of
W
originally arose in connection with Deodhar decompositions of noncrossing Catalan varieties. In this setting, we obtain as a corollary that
\mathsf{Snap}
restricts to a poset embedding of the shard intersection order on
\mathrm{Sort}(W,c)
—which is isomorphic to the noncrossing partition lattice of
W
—into the weak order on the positive braid monoid.

Finally, we turn back to arbitrary finite central irreducible real arrangements and define a fourth map
\mathsf{Pow}
. We prove that
\mathsf{Pow}
is a poset embedding of the poset of regions of
\mathcal{H}
into the pure shard monoid.

1.3.
Shards (
Section

3
)

Shards
are certain closed polyhedral subsets of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
that were introduced by Reading in
[
Rea03
]
; we write
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
for the set of shards. Each cover relation
C^{\prime}\lessdot C
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
can be labeled by a shard
\Sigma(C^{\prime}\lessdot C)
, which is the unique shard separating the region
C^{\prime}
from the region
C
; in this case, we call
\Sigma(C^{\prime}\lessdot C)
a
lower shard
of
C
. Let
\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)
be the set of lower shards of
C
.

Now assume
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial. Then
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
is a semidistributive lattice
[
BEZ90
]
, and the set of shards forms an elegant geometric realization of the set of join-irreducible elements. Furthermore, shard intersections encode the canonical join representations of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
. The map
C\mapsto\bigcap\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)
defines a bijection from
\mathcal{R}
to the set of arbitrary intersections of shards. In
[
Rea11
]
, Reading introduced another poset
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B):=(\mathcal{R},\preceq)
called the
shard intersection order
, which is defined by

C\preceq D\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\bigcap\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)\supseteq\bigcap\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(D).

As with
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, the poset
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
is a lattice—but while
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
is “tall and slender” (with height equal to the number of hyperplanes and with the number of atoms equal to the dimension),
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
is “short and wide” (with height equal to dimension and with the number of atoms equal to the number of shards). When
\mathcal{H}
is the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group
W
, the relationship between noncrossing partitions and sortable elements allowed Reading to embed the
W
-noncrossing partition lattice into the shard intersection order, thereby giving a uniform proof that the noncrossing partition lattice is indeed a lattice. An example is illustrated in
Figure

2
.

1.4.
Shards and the Salvetti Complex (
Section

4
)

Whenever we have an edge
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
, we will write
\Sigma(e)
for the associated shard
\Sigma(C^{\prime}\lessdot C)
. Although the generators in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
are a priori indexed by cover relations in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, our first theorem says that they are really indexed by the much smaller set of shards.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a central real hyperplane arrangement. Given edges
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
and
D^{\prime}\xrightarrow{f}D
in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
\ell_{e}\simeq\ell_{f}
if and only if
\Sigma(e)=\Sigma(f)
.

By
Theorem

1.1
, it makes sense write
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
, indexing the loops in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
by shards. Thus, for any shard
\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
, we define the
shard loop

\ell_{\Sigma}=\ell_{e}
, where
e
is any edge such that
\Sigma(e)=\Sigma
.

We define the
pure shard monoid
, denoted
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
, to be the submonoid of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
generated by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
. That is, an element of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
is in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
if and only if it can be represented by a word in the alphabet
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
. This allows us to endow
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
with a partial order
\leq
by declaring that
p\leq p^{\prime}
if there is a word over
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
representing
p^{\prime}
that contains a prefix representing
p
.
Figure

3
shows the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
between the identity element and the
full twist

\Delta^{2}
(see
Section

4.2
) in the pure shard monoid of the arrangement from
Figure

1
. This interval is “tall and wide,” but it is
not
a lattice in general (see
Figure

7
), preventing the use of Garside theory to study
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
.

Our first main result about the pure shard monoid is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a central arrangement. The interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
in the pure shard monoid is self-dual.

In general, the combinatorics of the pure shard monoid can be quite involved. However, for a rank-2 arrangement with
m
hyperplanes, we will prove the following precise theorem, which tells us that there are exactly
m2^{m-2}
words over the alphabet
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
representing
\Delta^{2}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be hyperplane arrangement of rank
2
with
m
hyperplanes. The interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
is a planar lattice with rank generating function
1+\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(2\binom{m}{k}-2\right)q^{k}\right)+q^{m}
and with
m2^{m-2}
maximal chains.

1.5.
Pop (
Section

5
)

In the next three subsections, we discuss three incarnations of the shard intersection order given by three maps
\mathsf{Pop}
,
\mathsf{Crackle}
, and
\mathsf{Snap}
. The first one, defined using the map
\mathsf{Pop}
, is not new, but it inspired our terminology for the other two. For these three subsections, we assume that
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial so that
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
is a semidistributive lattice.

Let
L
be a locally finite meet-semilattice with meet operation denoted by
\wedge
. Motivated by work on
pop-stacks
from enumerative combinatorics and theoretical computer science
[
Ung82
,
CG19
,
AV21
]
, the first author defined the
pop-stack sorting operator

\mathsf{Pop}\colon L\to L
in
[
Def22a
]
(see also
[
Def22b
]
) by

(2)

\mathsf{Pop}(x):=x\wedge\bigwedge\{y\in L:y\lessdot x\}.

In the case when
L=\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, we can use
\mathsf{Pop}
to characterize
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
. For
C\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(C),C])
be the set of shards that label the cover relations in the interval
[\mathsf{Pop}(C),C]
; that is,

\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(C),C]):=\left\{\Sigma(D^{\prime}\lessdot D):\mathsf{Pop}(C)\leq D^{\prime}\lessdot D\leq C\right\}.

By
[
Rea11
, Proposition 5.7]
, we have

(3)

C^{\prime}\preceq C\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(C^{\prime}),C^{\prime}])\subseteq\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(C),C]).

1.6.
Crackle (
Section

6
)

We define the
crackle map

\mathsf{Crackle}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
by

(4)

\mathsf{Crackle}(C):=\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))\cdot\mathrm{gal}(\mathsf{Pop}(C),C)\cdot\mathrm{gal}(C,\mathsf{Pop}(C))\cdot\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))^{-1}.

This map generalizes the shard loops
\ell_{\Sigma}
of
Equation

1
and
Theorem

1.1
: if
J
is a join-irreducible region of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, then
\mathsf{Pop}(J)
is the unique region covered by
J
, so
\mathsf{Crackle}(J)=\ell_{\Sigma(\mathsf{Pop}(J)\lessdot J)}
.

Just as
Equation

3
characterized
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
using
\mathsf{Pop}
, we can characterize
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
using
\mathsf{Crackle}
. As above, we write
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
for the interval between the identity element
\mathbbm{1}
and the full twist
\Delta^{2}
in the pure shard monoid
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Recall that if
P
and
Q
are posets, then a map
\psi\colon P\to Q
is called a
poset embedding
if it is a poset isomorphism from
P
to its image
\psi(P)\subseteq Q
.

Theorem 1.4
.

The map
\mathsf{Crackle}
is a poset embedding from
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
into the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
.

1.7.
Snap (
Section

7
)

We now specialize to the case when
\mathcal{H}
is the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group
W
. We identify the base region
B
with the identity element of
W
; the free transitive action of
W
on
\mathcal{R}
then allows us to identity regions of
\mathcal{H}
with elements of
W
. We write
{\mathrm{Shard}(W):=\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)}
,
\mathrm{Weak}(W):=\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
,
\mathbf{P}^{+}(W)=\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
, etc.

In this setting, the group
\mathbf{P}(W):=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}},x_{B})
is called the
pure braid group
of
W
, while the group
\mathbf{B}(W):=\pi_{1}((\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}})/W,x_{B})
is called the
braid group
of
W
. The Coxeter group
W
fits into the following well-known exact sequence with its braid and pure braid groups:

1\to\mathbf{P}(W)\to\mathbf{B}(W)\xrightarrow{\varphi}W\to 1.

Let
{\bf S}
be the set of simple generators of
\mathbf{B}(W)
obtained by lifting the set
S
of simple reflections of
W
. The generators in
\bf S
satisfy the same braid relations as the corresponding simple reflections of
W
; the difference is that
W
also includes the relations stating that the simple reflections are involutions. Thus, the projection
\varphi\colon\mathbf{B}(W)\to W
is the quotient map that sends each generator
{\bf s}\in{\bf S}
to the corresponding
s\in S
and imposes these additional relations. The submonoid of
\mathbf{B}(W)
generated by
{\bf S}
is called the
positive braid monoid
of
W
and is denoted by
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
. The weak order
(W,\leq)
is defined by saying
u\leq v
if and only if any reduced word for
u
appears as a prefix of some reduced word for
v
. Analogously, the weak order
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)):=(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W),\leq)
is defined by saying
{\bf u}\leq\mathbf{v}
if and only if any word over
\bf S
representing
\bf u
appears as a prefix of some word over
\bf S
representing
\mathbf{v}
. In this setting, the full twist
\Delta^{2}
is equal to the lift
\mathbf{w}_{\circ}^{2}
of the long element
w_{\circ}
of
W
; we will also write
\Delta=\mathbf{w}_{\circ}
.

We can rephrase
Theorem

1.1
when
\mathcal{H}
is the reflection arrangement of
W
as follows.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
u,v\in W
and
s,t\in S
are such that
u\lessdot us
and
v\lessdot vt
. Let
{\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf s},{\bf t}
be the lifts of
u,v,s,t
, respectively, to
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
. We have
{\bf u}\mathbf{s}{\bf u}^{-1}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{t}\mathbf{v}^{-1}
if and only if
\Sigma(u\lessdot us)=\Sigma(v\lessdot vt)
.

For
u,v\in W
, we let
u^{v}=v^{-1}uv
. Write
\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{n})
for an ordering of the elements of
\bf S
. For
\mathbf{s}\in{\bf S}
and a positive braid
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{s}_{i_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{s}_{i_{k}}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
with projection
w=\varphi(\mathbf{w})\in W
, let
\mathbf{s}^{\mathbf{w}}=(t,j)
, where
t=s^{w}
and
j
counts the number of times
t
appears in the sequence
s^{s_{i_{k}}},s^{s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k-1}}},\ldots,s^{s_{i_{k}}s_{i_{k-1}}\cdots s_{1}}
.

In
[
GLTW22
]
, a new set of noncrossing
W
-Catalan objects was introduced as the set of subwords of
\mathbf{c}^{h+1}
that represent the full twist
\Delta^{2}=\mathbf{w}_{\circ}^{2}
and satisfy an additional
Deodhar condition
. When interpreted in the positive braid monoid
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
, this Deodhar condition is equivalent to restricting to those
c
-sortable elements
\mathbf{w}
in the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\mathbf{w}_{\circ}^{2}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
with the property that for each descent
\mathbf{s}
of
\mathbf{w}
, we have
\mathbf{s}^{\mathbf{w}}=(t,j)
with
j
even—this is a nonstandard
Deodhar embedding
of the
c
-sortable elements into the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\mathbf{w}_{\circ}^{2}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
(different from the usual lift of
W
into
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
). The second author speculated that restricting the
2
nd
c
-Fuss–Cambrian lattice to the image of this Deodhar embedding would recover the noncrossing partition lattice
\mathrm{NC}(W,c)
. As the
2
nd
c
-Fuss–Cambrian lattice is a subposet of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
, it makes sense to generalize this Deodhar embedding of
c
-sortable elements to all elements of
W
.

The
snap map

\mathsf{Snap}\colon W\to\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
is our generalization of the Deodhar embedding. We write
\mathsf{Pop}
for the pop-stack sorting operators on the lattice
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
and the meet-semilattice
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
, relying on the argument of the operator to indicate the context. For
w\in W
, let
\mathrm{des}(w)
denote the right descent set of
w
, let
w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
be the longest element of the parabolic subgroup of
W
generated by
\mathrm{des}(w)
, and write
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
for the usual lifts of
w
and
w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
to
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
. Define

(5)

\mathsf{Snap}(w):=\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})\cdot(\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))^{2}.

Since
\mathsf{Crackle}(w)=\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})\cdot(\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))^{2}\cdot\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})^{-1}
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(W)\subseteq\mathbf{B}(W)
, it follows that

\mathsf{Snap}(w)=\mathsf{Crackle}(w)\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w}).

2.
The Salvetti Complex

2.1.
Real Hyperplane Arrangements

We recall some definitions regarding real hyperplane arrangements, referring the reader to
[
Sta04
]
for additional details.

A real
hyperplane
is an affine subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of codimension 1, and a
hyperplane arrangement
(or just
arrangement
, for short) is a set of distinct hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A hyperplane is
linear
if it contains the origin, and an arrangement is
central
if all of its hyperplanes are linear. A
subarrangement
of an arrangement
\mathcal{H}
is simply a subset of
\mathcal{H}
. We say
\mathcal{H}
is
irreducible
if there do not exist a linear automorphism
\theta
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a partition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{H}_{2}
with
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\neq\emptyset
such that all of the normal vectors of hyperplanes in
\theta(\mathcal{H}_{1})
are orthogonal to all of the normal vectors of hyperplanes in
\theta(\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Throughout this paper, we always assume that
\mathcal{H}
is a finite central irreducible real hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; note that this forces
\mathcal{H}
to contain at least
3
hyperplanes.

The
rank
of an arrangement (or subarrangement) is the codimension of the intersection of its hyperplanes. We call a subarrangement
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

full
if it contains all hyperplanes of
\mathcal{H}
containing a particular subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A
region
(or
chamber
) of
\mathcal{H}
is a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{H}
; we write
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}
for the set of regions of
\mathcal{H}
. We will fix a choice of a
base region

B
. A
bounding hyperplane
of a region
C\in\mathcal{R}
is a hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
whose intersection with the boundary of
C
is
(n-1)
-dimensional. We say
\mathcal{H}
is
simplicial
if every region has exactly
n
bounding hyperplanes.

2.2.
The Poset of Regions

Let
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
be two regions of
\mathcal{H}
, and choose points
x\in C
and
x^{\prime}\in C^{\prime}
. The hyperplanes intersecting the line segment with endpoints
x
and
x^{\prime}
are said to
separate

C
from
C^{\prime}
, and we write
\mathrm{inv}(C,C^{\prime})
for the set of all hyperplanes separating
C
from
C^{\prime}
. We write
-C
for the unique region such that
\mathrm{inv}(C,-C)=\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathrm{inv}(C)=\mathrm{inv}(B,C)
, where
B
is our fixed base region. The
poset of regions
of
\mathcal{H}
is the poset
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B):=(\mathcal{R},\leq)
, where we write
C\leq C^{\prime}
if
\mathrm{inv}(C)\subseteq\mathrm{inv}(C^{\prime})
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
BEZ90
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a simplicial hyperplane arrangement, then the poset of regions
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
is a lattice.

We write
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
when
e
is the edge in the Hasse diagram of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
corresponding to a cover relation
C^{\prime}\lessdot C
. In this case, we write
H_{e}
for the unique hyperplane in
\mathrm{inv}(C)\setminus\mathrm{inv}(C^{\prime})
, and we call
H_{e}
a
lower cover
of
C
. Let
\mathrm{cov}(C)
be the set of lower covers of
C
.

2.3.
The Salvetti Complex

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The
Salvetti complex
is a combinatorial model for the complement in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the complexified hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}

[
Sal87
]
. Choose
\alpha_{i}
to be a normal vector to
H_{i}
. We write
Z(\mathcal{H})
for the
dual zonotope
of
\mathcal{H}
, defined as the Minkowski sum
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}
. As a polytope, the zonotope
Z(\mathcal{H})
comes equipped with the structure of a CW complex. For
C\in\mathcal{R}
, write
Z(\mathcal{H},C)
for an oriented copy of
Z(\mathcal{H})
, where each face of dimension at least
1
is given an orientation pointing from
C
to
-C
. The Salvetti complex is then the CW complex defined as the union of the CW complexes
Z(\mathcal{H},C)
over all
C\in\mathcal{R}
:

(6)

\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}):=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{R}}Z(\mathcal{H},C).

This is not a disjoint union: for any
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, we identify faces in
Z(\mathcal{H},C)
and
Z(\mathcal{H},C^{\prime})
that have equivalent orientations. Vertices of
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
correspond to regions of
\mathcal{H}
, so it makes sense to think of our fixed base region
B
as a point in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
Sal87
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a central real arrangement, then
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
embeds in
\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
and is a deformation retract of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Furthermore,

\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}},x_{B}),

where
B\in\mathcal{R}
and
x_{B}\in B
.

Salvetti further described the abelianization
H_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
of the group
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
(see also
Theorem

2.5
below).

Theorem 2.3

(
[
Sal87
]
)
.

The abelianization
H_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
is a free abelian group with generating set
\{\overline{\ell}_{H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}}
indexed by the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. The natural homomorphism
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)\to H_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
sends
\ell_{e}
to
\overline{\ell}_{H_{e}}
.

A direct description of the restriction of
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
to its 1-skeleton can be built directly from the Hasse diagram of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
(see
[
Sal87
, Part Two]
). The vertices are given by the regions of
\mathcal{H}
. The edges are given by the cover relations of the Hasse diagram of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
as follows. For each cover relation
C^{\prime}\lessdot C
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, introduce an oriented edge
e
from
C^{\prime}
to
C
and an oriented edge
e^{*}
from
C
to
C^{\prime}
. We extend the notation
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
to accommodate these extra (oriented) edges using
C\xrightarrow{e^{*}}C^{\prime}
. Note that
e^{*}
is not the same as
e^{-1}
. We will always use un-starred letters for edges oriented away from
B
and starred letter for edges oriented toward
B
.

Given arbitrary regions
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, we define a
gallery
from
C^{\prime}
to
C
to be a sequence of edges (of the form
e
,
e^{*}
,
e^{-1}
, and
(e^{*})^{-1}
) that starts at
C^{\prime}
and ends at
C
. A gallery from
C^{\prime}
to
C
is
positive
if it only uses edges of the form
e
and
e^{*}
(not
e^{-1}
or
(e^{*})^{-1}
). The gallery is
minimal
if its length is equal to
|\mathrm{inv}(C^{\prime},C)|
, and it is called a
loop
if
C=C^{\prime}
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are galleries such that the ending point of
\alpha
is the starting point of
\beta
, then
\alpha\cdot\beta
is the gallery that we traverse by first traversing
\alpha
and then traversing
\beta
.
We say two galleries
\alpha,\beta
in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
are
homotopic
and write
\alpha\cong\beta
if
\alpha
can be obtained from
\beta
by repeated insertion or deletion of boundary paths of 1-cells and 2-cells in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
. A gallery is minimal if and only if it has the shortest length among all galleries in its homotopy class. Any two positive minimal galleries from a region
C^{\prime}
to a region
C
are homotopic, and we write
\mathrm{gal}(C^{\prime},C)
for a choice of one such gallery. We abbreviate the concatenation of a sequence of positive minimal galleries with the notation
\mathrm{gal}(C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\ldots,C_{k-1},C_{k})=\mathrm{gal}(C_{1},C_{2})\cdot\mathrm{gal}(C_{2},C_{3})\cdot\cdots\cdot\mathrm{gal}(C_{k-1},C_{k})
.

3.
Shards

We recall some constructions and results from
[
Rea03
,
Rea11
]
. As before, fix a base region
B\in\mathcal{R}
. For a full rank-2 subarrangement
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{H}
, let
B_{\mathcal{A}}
be the region of
\mathcal{A}
containing
B
. We say a hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{A}
is
basic
if its intersection with the boundary of
B_{\mathcal{A}}
has dimension
n-1
. For
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
, we say
H

cuts

H^{\prime}
if
H
is basic in
\mathcal{A}
but
H^{\prime}
is not. Each hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{H}
is broken into a number of connected pieces if we remove all points in
H
contained the the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{H}
that cut
H
—a
shard
of
H
is then the closure of one of these connected pieces. We write
H_{\Sigma}
for the unique hyperplane containing a shard
\Sigma
. Let
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
denote the set of all shards.

Extending the notation for hyperplanes, when
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
, we write
\Sigma(e)
for the shard crossed by
e
and call
\Sigma(e)
a
lower shard
of
C
. Let
\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)
be the set of lower shards of
C
. The
shard intersection order
is the partial order
\preceq
on
\mathcal{R}
defined by saying

C^{\prime}\preceq C\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\bigcap\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C^{\prime})\supseteq\bigcap\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C).

For each shard
\Sigma
, we fix a minimal element
J_{\Sigma}
of
\{C\in\mathcal{R}:\Sigma\in\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)\}
in the poset
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Recall that an element in a finite lattice is called
join-irreducible
if it covers exactly one element. When
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial, we know by
Theorem

2.1
that
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
is a lattice, and the following results of Reading show that shards give a geometric interpretation of this lattice structure.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Rea03
, Proposition 2.2]
,
[
Rea11
, Proposition 3.3]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial, then there is a bijection between
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
and the set of join-irreducible regions in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
. The shard
\Sigma
corresponds to the join-irreducible region
J_{\Sigma}
, which is the unique minimal element of
\{C\in\mathcal{R}:\Sigma\in\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)\}
. The shard
corresponding to a join-irreducible region
J
is the unique element of
\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(J)
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
Rea11
, Theorem 3.6]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial and
C\in\mathcal{R}
, then

C=\bigvee_{\Sigma\in\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)}J_{\Sigma}.

(There is actually a stronger version of
Theorem

3.2
, which says that
\{J_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\in\mathrm{cov}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(C)\}
is the
canonical join representation
of
C
in the semidistributive lattice
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
—the lattice property is strongly tied to the simpliciality of
\mathcal{H}
; see
[
Rea11
]
.)

4.
Shards and the Salvetti Complex

Our goal in this section is to establish
Theorem

1.1
, which tells us that two loops
\ell_{e}
and
\ell_{f}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
are homotopic if and only if they go around the same shard (that is,
\Sigma(e)=\Sigma(f)
). Thus, while
Theorems

2.5
and
2.3
tell us that the elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
up to conjugation are indexed by the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
,
Theorem

1.1
tells us that the elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
(not up to conjugation) are indexed by the shards of
\mathcal{H}
.

4.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

We work toward understanding general arrangements by first understanding rank-2 arrangements. We need the following general lemma, which will also be useful elsewhere in the paper. Given a subarrangement
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{H}
, we let

\iota_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{A}}

be the map that sends a region
D\in\mathcal{R}
to the unique region of
\mathcal{A}
containing
D
, and we let
B_{\mathcal{A}}=\iota_{\mathcal{A}}(B)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subarrangement of a central arrangement
\mathcal{H}
. The map
\iota_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{A}}
induces a quotient map

\overline{\iota}_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)\to\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}),B_{\mathcal{A}})

that sends the generating set
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\mathcal{H},B)
to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\mathcal{A},B_{\mathcal{A}})\cup\{\mathbbm{1}\}
, where
\mathbbm{1}
denotes the identity element of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}),B_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Proof.

By identifying regions via
\iota_{\mathcal{A}}
, we collapse all edges
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
and
C\xrightarrow{e^{*}}C^{\prime}
with
\iota_{\mathcal{A}}(C^{\prime})=\iota_{\mathcal{A}}(C)
and identify all edges
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
and
D^{\prime}\xrightarrow{f}D
with
\iota_{\mathcal{A}}(C^{\prime})=\iota_{A}(D^{\prime})
and
\iota_{A}(C)=\iota_{A}(D)
. The quotient therefore introduces some relations of the form
\ell_{e}=\mathbbm{1}
(when
H_{e}\not\in\mathcal{A}
) and some relations of the form
\ell_{e}=\ell_{e^{\prime}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}\}
is a rank-2 hyperplane arrangement with three hyperplanes that has base region
B
and edges named
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
and
e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}
(in the same manner as in
Figure

). Then
\ell_{e_{2}}\neq\ell_{e_{5}}
.

Proof.

If follows from
Theorem

2.5
that
\ell_{e_{5}}=\ell_{e_{1}}^{-1}\ell_{e_{2}}\ell_{e_{1}}
, so we just need to show that
\ell_{e_{1}}
and
\ell_{e_{2}}
do not commute in
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
. By
Theorem

2.7
, this group has presentation
\langle\ell_{e_{1}},\ell_{e_{2}},\ell_{e_{3}}:\ell_{e_{1}}\ell_{e_{2}}\ell_{e_{3}}=\ell_{e_{3}}\ell_{e_{1}}\ell_{e_{2}}=\ell_{e_{2}}\ell_{e_{3}}\ell_{e_{1}}\rangle
. Consider the free group
F_{2}=\langle x,y\rangle
. The map
\{\ell_{e_{1}},\ell_{e_{2}},\ell_{e_{3}}\}\to F_{2}
given by
\ell_{e_{1}}\mapsto y^{-1}x^{-1}
,
\ell_{e_{2}}\mapsto x

\ell_{e_{3}}\mapsto y
extends to a homomorphism from
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
to

; this implies that
\ell_{e_{2}}
and
\ell_{e_{3}}
do not commute.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a rank-2 hyperplane arrangement with base region
B
and with edges named
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}
and
e_{m+1},\ldots,e_{2m}
as in
Figure

. For all distinct
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,2m\}
such that
\{i,j\}
is not
\{m,2m\}
or

, we have
\ell_{e_{i}}\neq\ell_{e_{j}}
.

Proof.

We may assume
i<j
. If
j\neq i+m
, then the hyperplanes
H_{e_{i}}
and
H_{e_{j}}
are distinct. In this case,
Theorem

2.3
tells us that the natural homomorphism
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)\to H_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}))
sends
\ell_{e_{i}}
and
\ell_{e_{j}}
to different elements, so
\ell_{e_{i}}\neq\ell_{e_{j}}
. Now suppose

so that
H_{e_{i}}=H_{e_{j}}
. By hypothesis, we have
1<i<m
. Consider the subarrangement
\mathcal{A}=\{H_{e_{1}},H_{e_{i}},H_{e_{m}}\}
. It follows from
Lemma

4.1
(and its proof) and
Lemma

4.2
that the quotient map
\overline{\iota}_{\mathcal{A}}
sends
\ell_{e_{i}}
and
\ell_{e_{j}}
to distinct elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{A}),B_{\mathcal{A}})
. Hence,
\ell_{e_{i}}\neq\ell_{e_{j}}
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a central arrangement. If
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
and
D^{\prime}\xrightarrow{f}D
are edges in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Sigma(e)=\Sigma(f)
, then
\ell_{e}=\ell_{f}
.

Proof.

Write
\Sigma:=\Sigma(e)=\Sigma(f)
. Choose a positive gallery
g
from
C
to
D
, and let
C=E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{r}=D
be the regions through which
g
passes. Let
1=k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{s}=r
be the indices such that
\Sigma\in\mathrm{cov}(E_{k_{i}})
. We may assume
g
is chosen so that for each
1\leq i\leq s-1
, the hyperplanes that
g
crosses when passing from
E_{k_{i}}
to
E_{k_{i+1}}
all belong to a single full rank-2 subarrangement
\mathcal{A}_{i}
with
H_{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
. For
1\leq i\leq s-1
, there is an edge
E_{k_{i}}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{d_{i}}E_{k_{i}}
with
\Sigma(d_{i})=\Sigma
. It suffices to prove that
\ell_{d_{i}}=\ell_{d_{i+1}}
for each such
i
.

Fix
1\leq i\leq s-1
, and let
B_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}=\iota_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(B)
. Let
m=|\mathcal{A}_{i}|
. The hyperplane
H_{\Sigma}
must be basic in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
(otherwise, the basic hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
would cut
\Sigma
), so either
E_{k_{i}}
or
E_{k_{i+1}}
is contained in
-B_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}
. For simplicity, let us assume
E_{k_{i+1}}\subseteq-B_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}
; the other case is virtually the same. Then
E_{k_{i}}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}
. We can find a positive minimal gallery
E_{k_{i}}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{m-1}}E_{k_{i+1}}^{\prime}
that only passes through hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Let
E_{k_{i}}\xrightarrow{e_{2m-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{m+1}}E_{k_{i+1}}
be the part of the gallery
g
from
E_{k_{i}}
to
E_{k_{i+1}}
, and note that this is a positive minimal gallery from
E_{k_{i}}
to
E_{k_{i+1}}
. Let
e_{2m}=d_{i}
and
e_{m}=d_{i+1}
. The collection of edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m},e_{m+1},\ldots,e_{2m}
forms a
2
-cell in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
, so we have the homotopy

e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{m}\cong e_{2m}e_{2m-1}\cdots e_{m+1}.

Similarly, the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m-1},e_{m}^{*},e_{m+1},\ldots,e_{2m-1},e_{2m}^{*}
form a
2
-cell in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
, so we have the homotopy

e_{2m-1}\cdots e_{m+1}e_{m}^{*}\cong e_{2m}^{*}e_{1}\cdots e_{m-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\ell_{d_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})(e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{m})e_{m}^{*}e_{m-1}^{-1}\cdots e_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})e_{2m}(e_{2m-1}\cdots e_{m+1}e_{m}^{*})e_{m-1}^{-1}\cdots e_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})e_{2m}(e_{2m}^{*}e_{1}\cdots e_{m-1})e_{m-1}^{-1}\cdots e_{1}^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(B,E_{k_{i}}^{\prime})^{-1}

Thus, the generators
\ell_{d_{i}}
and
\ell_{d_{i+1}}
are the same.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a central arrangement. If
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
and

are edges in
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})

4.2.
The Pure Shard Monoid

We now know that the generators in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
are indexed by shards, so we may write
\ell_{\Sigma}
to refer to the
shard loop
indexed by the shard
\Sigma
and write
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}
.

Definition 4.6
.

The
pure shard monoid

\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
is the submonoid of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
generated by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
.

Our name for
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
comes from the fact that the generating set
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
is indexed by shards and the fact that if
\mathcal{H}
is the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group
W
, then
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
is isomorphic to the pure braid group of
W
.

An
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-word
is a word over the alphabet
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
. By definition,
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
is the set of elements of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
represented by
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-words. We view
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
as a poset, where the partial order
\leq
is defined by saying
p\leq p^{\prime}
if there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-word representing
p^{\prime}
that contains an
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-word representing
p
as a prefix.

The
full twist
is the element
\Delta^{2}
of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
defined by

\Delta^{2}:=\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)\cdot\mathrm{gal}(-B,B).

For
\mathcal{H}
a finite irreducible simplicial arrangement, it is known that the center of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
is an infinite cyclic group generated by
\Delta^{2}

[
Cor94
]
. We are especially interested in
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
, the interval between the identity element
\mathbbm{1}
and the full twist
\Delta^{2}
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
. This interval is “tall and wide,” but it is
not
a lattice in general, preventing the use of Garside theory to study
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
(see
Figure

10
). Note that if

B=C_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}}C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{e_{k}}C_{k}=-B

is a positive minimal gallery from
B
to
-B
, then

(7)

\Delta^{2}=\ell_{e_{k}}\ell_{e_{k-1}}\cdots\ell_{e_{2}}\ell_{e_{1}}.

4.3.
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

To initiate the study of the pure shard monoid, we consider an arbitrary central arrangement
\mathcal{H}
and prove that the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
is self-dual.

Observe that the set
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
of shards defined with respect to the base region
B
is equal to the set
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},-B)
of shards defined with respect to the base region
-B
. Moreover, for each shard
\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
, the polyhedral cone
-\Sigma
is also a shard in
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Let us write
\widehat{\ell}_{e}
for the loop in
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),-B)
corresponding to an edge
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
; that is,

\widehat{\ell}_{\Sigma}=\mathrm{gal}(C,-B)^{-1}e^{-1}(e^{*})^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(C,-B).

It follows from
Theorem

1.1
that
\widehat{\ell}_{e}
only depends on the shard
\Sigma(e)
. Using Salvetti’s presentation in
Theorems

2.5
and
2.7
, we find that there is an isomorphism
\Psi\colon\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)\to\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),-B)
satisfying
\Psi(\ell_{\Sigma})=\widehat{\ell}_{-\Sigma}
for all
\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
.

For
\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
, we have

(8)

\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)\,\widehat{\ell}_{\Sigma}\,\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)^{-1}\cong\ell_{\Sigma}^{-1}.

Indeed, if
C^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e}C
is an edge such that
\Sigma=\Sigma(e)
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)\,\widehat{\ell}_{\Sigma}\,\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)^{-1}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)\mathrm{gal}(C,-B)^{-1}e^{-1}(e^{*})^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(C,B)\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)^{-1}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,C)e^{-1}(e^{*})^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(B,C^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{gal}(B,C^{\prime})(e^{*})^{-1}e^{-1}\mathrm{gal}(B,C^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{\Sigma}^{-1}.

Define a map
\kappa\colon\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)\to\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
by
\kappa(\ell_{\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma_{k}})=\ell_{-\Sigma_{k}}\cdots\ell_{-\Sigma_{1}}
. Let us check that this map is well defined. Suppose
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{k},\Sigma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
are such that
{\ell_{\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma_{k}}=\ell_{\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}}}
. Applying the isomorphism
\Psi
, we find that

\widehat{\ell}_{-\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\widehat{\ell}_{-\Sigma_{k}}=\widehat{\ell}_{-\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\widehat{\ell}_{-\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}}.

If we conjugate each side of this equation by
\mathrm{gal}(B,-B)
and use the identity (
8
), we obtain

\ell_{-\Sigma_{1}}^{-1}\cdots\ell_{-\Sigma_{k}}^{-1}\cong\ell_{-\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}}^{-1}\cdots\ell_{-\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}}^{-1}.

This shows that
\kappa(\ell_{\Sigma_{1}}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma_{k}})^{-1}\cong\kappa(\ell_{\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}})^{-1}
. Since elements of
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
are defined up to homotopy, it follows that
\kappa
is well defined. It is also clear that
\kappa
is an antihomomorphism (meaning
\kappa(xy)=\kappa(y)\kappa(x)
for all
x,y\in\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
) and an involution.

Let

B=C_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}}C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{e_{k}}C_{k}=-B

be a positive minimal gallery from
B
to
-B
. For
1\leq i\leq k
, there is an edge
-C_{i}\xrightarrow{-e_{i}}-C_{i-1}
, and we have
\Sigma(-e_{i})=-\Sigma(e_{i})
. Note that

B=-C_{k}\xrightarrow{-e_{k}}-C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{-e_{k-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{-e_{2}}-C_{1}\xrightarrow{-e_{1}}-C_{0}=-B

is a positive minimal gallery from
B
to
-B
. It follows from (
7
) that

\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e_{1})}=\Delta^{2}=\ell_{-\Sigma(e_{1})}\cdots\ell_{-\Sigma(e_{k})}.

Hence,

(9)

\kappa(\Delta^{2})=\Delta^{2}.

If
x\in[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
, then

(10)

\Delta^{2}=\kappa(xx^{-1}\Delta^{2})=\kappa(x^{-1}\Delta^{2})\kappa(x),

so
\kappa(x^{-1}\Delta^{2})\in[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
. Define
\Omega\colon[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}\to[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
by
\Omega(x)=\kappa(x^{-1}\Delta^{2})
. The following proposition implies
Theorem

1.2
.

Proposition 4.7
.

The map
\Omega
is an antiautomorphism of the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
.

Proof.

For
x\in[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
, it follows from (
10
) that

\Omega^{2}(x)=\kappa(\kappa(x^{-1}\Delta^{2})^{-1}\Delta^{2})=\kappa(\kappa(x))=x.

This shows that
\Omega
is an involution; in particular, it is a bijection.

To see that
\Omega
is order-reversing, suppose

are such that
x\leq y
, and let
z\in\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
be such that
y=xz
. We have

\Omega(x)=\kappa(x^{-1}\Delta^{2})=\kappa(y^{-1}\Delta^{2})\kappa(z)=\Omega(y)\kappa(z),

so
\Omega(y)\leq\Omega(x)
.
∎

∎

6.

We assume throughout this section that

6.1.
Full Twists, Shard Generators, and Crackle

We have
[\mathsf{Pop}(-B),-B]=\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
since
\mathsf{Pop}(-B)=B
by
[
Ede84
, Corollary 1.6]
. Given a region

, we can think of the interval

in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
as a “nonstandard parabolic subarrangement” of
\mathcal{H}
. This motivates us to define

to be the “parabolic full twist” of this subarrangement.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{R}
. We define

\mathsf{Crackle}(C):=\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))\cdot\mathrm{gal}(\mathsf{Pop}(C),C,\mathsf{Pop}(C))\cdot\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))^{-1}\in\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B).

Definition

6.1
generalizes both the full twist and the shard loops of
Section

4

\mathsf{Crackle}(-B)=\Delta^{2}
by construction, so

generalizes the full twist; on the other hand, if

is a join-irreducible region of
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
, then
\mathsf{Pop}(J)
is the unique region covered by

, so
\mathsf{Crackle}(J)=\ell_{\Sigma(\mathsf{Pop}(J)\lessdot J)}
.

Our primary goal in this section is to prove
Theorem

1.4
, which uses
\mathsf{Crackle}
to characterize the shard intersection order. Recall that this theorem states that
\mathsf{Crackle}
is a poset embedding from
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
into
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
and is illustrated in
Figures

3
and
7
.

\ell_{e_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{e_{k}},\ell_{e_{k+1}},\ldots,\ell_{e_{m}}
in

\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{C},B_{C})
such that

\ell_{e_{1}}\cdots\ell_{e_{k}}=\mathsf{Crackle}_{C}(\iota_{C}(D))\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{e_{1}}\cdots\ell_{e_{k}}\ell_{e_{k+1}}\cdots\ell_{e_{m}}=\mathsf{Crackle}_{C}(\iota_{C}(C)).

For

1\leq i\leq m
, note that
\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{i}})=\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))\cdot(\iota_{C}^{\mathsf{Pop}})^{-1}(\ell_{e_{i}})\cdot\mathrm{gal}(B,\mathsf{Pop}(C))^{-1}
is one of the shard loops in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Because
D\preceq C
,
Lemma

6.7
tells us that

\mathsf{Crackle}(D)=\Phi_{C}(\mathsf{Crackle}_{C}(\iota_{C}(D)))=\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{1}})\cdots\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{k}})

and

\mathsf{Crackle}(C)=\Phi_{C}(\mathsf{Crackle}_{C}(\iota_{C}(C)))=\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{1}})\cdots\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{k}})\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{k+1}})\cdots\Phi_{C}(\ell_{e_{m}}).

This proves that
\mathsf{Crackle}(D)\leq\mathsf{Crackle}(C)
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
.

To prove the converse, assume
\mathsf{Crackle}(D)\leq\mathsf{Crackle}(C)
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Then every
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-word representing
\mathsf{Crackle}(D)
can be extended to an
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-word representing
\mathsf{Crackle}(C)
. According to
Proposition

6.8
, we have

\{\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}:J_{\Sigma}\preceq D\}\subseteq\{\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}:J_{\Sigma}\preceq C\}.

It follows from
Proposition

5.1
that

\{\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}:J_{\Sigma}\preceq D\}=\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(D),D])\quad\text{and}\quad\{\Sigma\in{\mbox{{Sh}}}:J_{\Sigma}\preceq C\}=\Sigma([\mathsf{Pop}(C),C]).

Therefore,
Proposition

5.1
tells us that
D\preceq C
, as desired.

and

. We will also collect corollaries specializing these results to sortable elements and noncrossing partitions.

7.1.
Coxeter Groups and Braid Groups

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the reflection arrangement of a finite Coxeter group
W
. Then
\mathcal{H}
is simplicial, and
W
can be seen as the group of orthogonal transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
generated by the reflections through the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. There is bijection between
W
and
\mathcal{R}
that maps an element
w\in W
to the region
w(B)
, where
B
is the fixed base region of
\mathcal{H}
. We use this bijection to identify regions of
\mathcal{H}
with elements of
W
.

Let
S
be the set of simple reflections of
W
. Then
W
has a presentation of the form

\langle S:(ss^{\prime})^{m(s,s^{\prime})}=\mathbbm{1}\text{ for all }s,s^{\prime}\in S\rangle,

where
\mathbbm{1}
is the identity element of
W
,
m(s,s)=1
for all
s\in S
, and
m(s,s^{\prime})=m(s^{\prime},s)\in\{2,3,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}
for all distinct
s,s^{\prime}\in S
. Given symbols
\alpha,\beta
and a nonnegative integer
r
, we write
[\alpha|\beta]_{r}
for the word
\alpha\beta\alpha\cdots
of length
r
that starts with
\alpha
and alternates between
\alpha
and
\beta
. Thus, the braid relations of
W
can be written as
[s|s^{\prime}]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}=[s^{\prime}|s]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}
. For each
s\in S
. let
{\bf s}
be a formal copy of
s
. The braid group
\mathbf{B}(W)
has a generating set
{\bf S}=\{{\bf s}:s\in S\}
and presentation

\langle{\bf S}:[\mathbf{s}|\mathbf{s}^{\prime}]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}=[\mathbf{s}^{\prime}|\mathbf{s}]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}\text{ for all distinct }\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in{\bf S}\rangle.

Thus, the generators in
\bf S
have infinite order in
\mathbf{B}(W)
, while the elements of
S
are involutions in
W
. The
positive braid monoid
of
W
is the monoid
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
generated by
{\bf S}
. There is a natural homomorphic quotient map
\varphi\colon\mathbf{B}(W)\to W
defined by
\varphi({\bf s})=s
for all
s\in S
. The
pure braid group
of
W
, denoted
\mathbf{P}(W)
, is the kernel of this map:
\mathbf{P}(W)=\ker(\varphi)
.

A
reduced word
for an element
w\in W
is a word in the alphabet
S
that represents
w
and has minimum length among all such words. The length of a reduced word for
w
is called the
length
of
w
and is denoted by
\mathrm{len}(w)
. The (right)
weak order
on
W
is defined by saying
u\leq v
if there is a reduced word for
v
that contains a reduced word for
u
as a prefix. This defines a poset
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
, which coincides with
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
under the identification of
W
with
\mathcal{R}
. For each
w\in W
, there is a natural
lift

{\bf w}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
obtained by taking a reduced word for
w
and replacing each simple reflection
s
with the corresponding generator
{\bf s}
. We often denote the lift of an element of
W
using bold, but we will also sometimes write
\mathrm{lift}(w)
for the lift of
w
when we are wary about the possibility of confusion. An
{\bf S}
-word
for an element
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
is a word in the alphabet
{\bf S}
that represents
\mathbf{w}
. We can also define the
weak order
on
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
by saying
{\bf u}\leq{\bf v}
if there is an
{\bf S}
-word for
{\bf v}
that contains an
{\bf S}
-word for
{\bf u}
as a prefix. We write
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
for the poset
(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W),\leq)
. For
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
with
{\bf u}\leq{\bf v}
, we write
[{\bf u},{\bf v}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
for the interval between
\bf u
and
\bf v
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
.

A
reflection
in
W
is an element of the form
usu^{-1}
, where
u\in W
and
s\in S
. Reflections are precisely the elements that, when viewed as orthogonal transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, are reflections through hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
. An
inversion
of an element
w\in W
is a reflection
t
in
W
such that
\mathrm{len}(tw)<\mathrm{len}(w)
; such an inversion is called a
cover reflection
of
w
if
tw
is covered by
w
in the weak order. Let
\mathrm{inv}(w)
and
\mathrm{cov}(w)
be the set of inversions and the set of cover reflections of
w
, respectively. Write
\langle\mathrm{cov}(w)\rangle
for the parabolic subgroup of
W
generated by
\mathrm{cov}(w)
.

For
u,v\in W
, we have
u\leq v
in the weak order if and only if
\mathrm{inv}(u)\subseteq\mathrm{inv}(v)
. Furthermore, a reformulation of
Theorem

5.2
in this context states that

(13)

u\preceq v\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathrm{inv}(u)\subseteq\mathrm{inv}(v)\text{ and }\langle\mathrm{cov}(u)\rangle\subseteq\langle\mathrm{cov}(v)\rangle.

7.2.
Pop and Crackle for Coxeter Groups

A
descent
of an element
w\in W
is a simple reflection
s
such that there is a reduced word for
w
that ends with
s
. Similarly, a descent of an element
{\bf w}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
is a generator
{\bf s}\in{\bf S}
such that there is an
{\bf S}
-word for
{\bf w}
that ends with
{\bf s}
. We write
\mathrm{des}(w)
and
\mathrm{des}({\bf w})
for the set of descents of
w
and the set of descents of
{\bf w}
, respectively. Given a subset
J\subseteq S
, we write
w_{\circ}(J)
for the longest element of the parabolic subgroup of
W
generated by
J
. Since
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
and
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
are locally finite meet-semilattices, they come equipped with pop-stack sorting operators; we denote both of these operators by
\mathsf{Pop}
. If
w\in W
and
{\bf w}=\mathrm{lift}(w)
, then
\mathrm{des}({\bf w})
is the set of lifts of descents of
w
. We have
\mathsf{Pop}(w)=ww_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
, so it follows that

\mathsf{Pop}({\bf w})={\bf w}\cdot({\bf w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))^{-1}=\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(w)),

where
{\bf w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))
.

There is a natural action of
W
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and the braid group
\mathbf{B}(W)
is isomorphic to the fundamental group
\pi_{1}((\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}})/W,x_{B})
. The pure braid group
\mathbf{P}(W)
is isomorphic to
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{C}},x_{B})
. The identification of
W
with
\mathcal{R}
allows us to naturally label the edges of
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})
by the generators in
\mathbf{S}
. More precisely, if
w\in W
and
s\in S\setminus\mathrm{des}(w)
, then we label the edges
w\xrightarrow{e}ws
and
ws\xrightarrow{e^{*}}w
with the generator
{\bf s}\in{\bf S}
. This labeling allows us to rephrase notions and results about crackle maps from
Section

6
in the language of braid groups. For example, if
w\in W
and
\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{lift}(w)
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{Crackle}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})\cdot\left(\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))\right)^{2}\cdot\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})^{-1}\in\mathbf{P}^{+}(W)\subseteq\mathbf{B}(W),

where

\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))
.

Proposition 7.1
.

The pure shard monoid
\mathbf{P}^{+}(W)
is the submonoid of
\mathbf{P}(W)
generated by

\{\mathsf{Crackle}(j):j\in W,|\mathrm{des}(j)|=1\}.

Proof.

An element
j\in W
has exactly 1 descent if and only if it is join-irreducible in
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
.
Theorem

3.1
tells us that there is bijection
\Sigma\mapsto J_{\Sigma}
from
{\mbox{{Sh}}}(\mathcal{H},B)
to the set of join-irreducible elements of
W
(which we identify with regions of
\mathcal{H}
). Moreover,
\mathsf{Crackle}(J_{\Sigma})=\ell_{\Sigma}
. Hence, the result follows from the original definition of the pure shard monoid.
∎

By
Theorem

1.4
,
\mathsf{Crackle}
is a poset embedding from
\mathrm{Shard}(W)
into
\mathbf{P}^{+}(W)
.

7.3.
Snap

Let
w_{\circ}
be the long element of
W
. The standard lift map
\mathrm{lift}\colon W\to\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
is a poset isomorphism from
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
to the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\mathbf{w}_{\circ}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))
, where
\mathbf{w}_{\circ}=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ})
. We now define a nonstandard lift from
W
to
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
.

Definition 7.2
.

The
snap map

\mathsf{Snap}\colon W\to\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathsf{Snap}(w)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Pop}(\mathbf{w})\cdot(\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))^{2},

where
\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{lift}(w)
and
\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w)))
.

Observe that
\mathsf{Snap}(w)=\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
and that
\varphi(\mathsf{Snap}(w))=\mathsf{Pop}(w)
. Our aim in this section is to prove
Theorem

1.6
, which states that
\mathsf{Snap}
is a poset embedding from
\mathrm{Shard}(W)
into
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
. This is illustrated in
Figure

4
, which shows
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{B}^{+}}
when
W=I_{2}(4)
is the dihedral group of order
8
.

The first step in this endeavor is the following lemma, which will allow us to consider inversion multisets of elements of
\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
{\bf w}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
, and let
{\bf s}_{1}\cdots{\bf s}_{r}
and
{\bf s}_{1}^{\prime}\cdots{\bf s}_{r}^{\prime}
be two
{\bf S}
-words for
\mathbf{w}
. The multisets

\{s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\cdots s_{1}:1\leq k\leq r\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{s_{1}^{\prime}\cdots s_{k-1}^{\prime}s_{k}^{\prime}s_{k-1}^{\prime}\cdots s_{1}^{\prime}:1\leq k\leq r\}

are equal.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result when the two
{\bf S}
-words differ by a braid move. In fact, it suffices to prove the result when the braid move changes the entire first word into the entire second word. Thus, we can assume the first word is
[{\bf s}|{\bf s}^{\prime}]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}
and the second word is
[{\bf s}^{\prime}|{\bf s}]_{m(s,s^{\prime})}
. In this case, the result follows from the observation that
[s|s^{\prime}]_{k}([s|s^{\prime}]_{k-1})^{-1}=[s^{\prime}|s]_{m(s,s^{\prime})+1-k}([s^{\prime}|s]_{m(s,s^{\prime})-k})^{-1}
for all
1\leq k\leq m(s,s^{\prime})
.
∎

For each
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
,
Lemma

7.3
allows us to define the multiset

\mathrm{Inv}(\mathbf{w})=\{s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\cdots s_{1}:1\leq k\leq r\},

where
{\bf s}_{1}\cdots{\bf s}_{r}
is an
{\bf S}
-word for
\mathbf{w}
. If
\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{lift}(w)
for some
w\in W
, then
s_{1}\cdots s_{r}
is a reduced word for
w
; in this case, the multiset
\mathrm{Inv}(\mathbf{w})
is actually a set, and it is equal to the inversion set
\mathrm{inv}(w)
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
w\in W
. Let
s_{1}\cdots s_{m}
be a reduced word for
\mathsf{Pop}(w)
, and let
s_{m+1}\cdots s_{r}
be a reduced word for
w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
so that
s_{1}\cdots s_{r}
is a reduced word for
w
. Let
t_{k}=s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}s_{k}s_{k-1}\cdots s_{1}
. Then
\mathrm{Inv}(\mathsf{Snap}(w))=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m},t_{m+1},\ldots,t_{r},t_{r},\ldots,t_{m+1}\}
. Furthermore,
\mathrm{inv}(w)=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\}
, and
t_{m+1},\ldots,t_{r}
are precisely the reflections appearing in the parabolic subgroup
\langle\mathrm{cov}(w)\rangle
.

Proof.

Since
w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(w))
is an involution, it also has
s_{r}\cdots s_{m+1}
as a reduced word, so

\mathbf{s}_{1}\cdots\mathbf{s}_{m}\mathbf{s}_{m+1}\cdots\mathbf{s}_{r}\mathbf{s}_{r}\cdots\mathbf{s}_{m+1}

is an
{\bf S}
-word for
\mathsf{Snap}(w)
. Therefore, to prove the first statement, we just need to show that for each
m+1\leq k\leq r
, the reflection
t_{k}
is equal to
s_{1}\cdots s_{r}s_{r}\cdots s_{k+1}s_{k}s_{k+1}\cdots s_{r}s_{r}\cdots s_{1}
. We can group the terms in this expression as

s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}(s_{k}\cdots s_{r})(s_{r}\cdots s_{k})(s_{k+1}\cdots s_{r})(s_{r}\cdots s_{k+1})s_{k}\cdots s_{1}

to make it clear that it is indeed equal to
t_{k}
.

It is well known that
\mathrm{inv}(w)=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\}
. For the last statement, note that, in the language of hyperplanes, the reflections
t_{m+1},\ldots,t_{r}
correspond to the hyperplanes that separate the region
\mathsf{Pop}(w)
from the region
w
; these are the reflections in
\langle\mathrm{cov}(w)\rangle
.
∎

1.6

\mathsf{Snap}
is a poset embedding of
\mathrm{Weak}(W)
into
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathbf{B}^{+}(W))

\mathsf{Snap}(w_{\circ})=\mathbf{w}_{\circ}^{2}=\Delta^{2}
, where

is the long element of

\mathbf{w}_{\circ}
is the lift of

Suppose first that

\mathsf{Snap}(u)\leq\mathsf{Snap}(v)
. Then there is an
{\bf S}
-word for
\mathsf{Snap}(v)

{\bf S}

\mathsf{Pop}(u)=u_{0}\lessdot u_{1}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot u_{k}=u

\mathrm{Weak}(W)

\mathsf{Pop}(u)
to

. Identifying

with a region of
\mathcal{H}

from

(14)

\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\leq\omega_{v}(u)
. This means there exists

\mathsf{Pop}(u)\leq\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))

(16)

\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u)))=\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\,{\bf y}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pop}(u)yxy^{-1}\mathsf{Pop}(u)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))x(\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=\omega_{v}(u)(\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=u\,\mathsf{Pop}(u)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pop}(u)w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))\mathsf{Pop}(u)^{-1},

where we have used

14

{\bf yx}=\mathrm{lift}(yx)=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))y)={\bf w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u)){\bf y},

where
\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))=\mathrm{lift}(w_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u)))

\displaystyle\mathsf{Snap}(u)

\displaystyle={\bf u}\,{\bf w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))^{2}\,{\bf y}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(u))\,{\bf yx}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\,{\bf yx}^{2}.

Invoking

15
and
16
, we find that
\mathrm{lift}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\,{\bf yx}^{2}=\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u)))\,{\bf x}^{2}=\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,{\bf x}
. Therefore, to prove that
\mathsf{Snap}(u)\leq\mathsf{Snap}(v)
, we just need to show that
\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,{\bf x}\leq\mathsf{Snap}(v)
.

We defined
x
via the equation
\omega_{v}(u)=\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))\,x
; since
\omega_{v}(\mathsf{Pop}(u))
and
\omega_{v}(u)
are both in the interval
[\mathsf{Pop}(v),v]
(by the definition of
\omega_{v}
in
Theorem

6.5
), this implies that
x
is in the parabolic subgroup
\langle\mathrm{des}(v)\rangle
. It follows that
{\bf x}\leq{\bf w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))
, so
\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,{\bf x}\leq\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))
. Therefore, the proof will be complete if we can demonstrate that
\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))\leq\mathsf{Snap}(v)
.

Because
\omega_{v}(u)\leq v
, there exists
z\in\langle\mathrm{des}(v)\rangle
such that
v=\omega_{v}(u)\,z
and
{\bf v}=\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,{\bf z}
. It is well known that for every
s\in\mathrm{des}(v)
, there exists
s^{\prime}\in\mathrm{des}(v)
such that
\mathbf{s}\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))=\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))\,\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
. It follows that there exists
{\bf z}^{\prime}\in\mathbf{B}^{+}(W)
such that
{\bf z}\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))=\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))\,{\bf z}^{\prime}
. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))\,{\bf z}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lift}(\omega_{v}(u))\,{\bf z}\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))

\displaystyle={\bf v}\,\mathbf{w}_{\circ}(\mathrm{des}(v))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Snap}(v),

as desired.

7.5.
Catalan Combinatorics

A
standard Coxeter element

c
of
W
is a product of the simple reflections in some order; fix a reduced expression for
c
as
s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}
. The
c
-sorting word

\mathbf{w}(c)

[
Rea07b
,
STW22
]

\mathbf{w}_{i}
a subword of
\mathbf{s}_{1}\mathbf{s}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{s}_{n}

\mathbf{w}
is

\mathbf{w}_{i}
contains the set of letters appearing in
\mathbf{w}_{i+1}

be a positive integer. Following
[
Rea07b

STW22
]

is the
m

c
-Fuss–Cambrian lattice
of type
W
—in particular
[
GLTW22
]
,

where
h
is the
Coxeter number
of
W
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
are the

of

Continuing to identify

-B

8.
Pow

In this section,
\mathcal{H}
is an arbitrary finite central irreducible real hyperplane arrangement. We will introduce a map
\mathsf{Pow}
from the set
\mathcal{R}
of regions to the pure shard monoid
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Just as
\mathsf{Crackle}
embeds the “short and wide” poset
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
into the “tall and wide” interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
,
Theorem

1.7
states that
\mathsf{Pow}
embeds the “tall and slender” poset
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
inside
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
; this theorem is illustrated in
Figure

5
. Thus, the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
simultaneously contains
\mathrm{Shard}(\mathcal{H},B)
and
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
.

We require a short argument to show that
\mathsf{Pow}
will be well-defined.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let

B=C_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}}C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{e_{k}}C_{k}=C\quad\text{and}\quad B=C_{0}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e_{1}^{\prime}}C_{1}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}^{\prime}}C_{k-1}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{e_{k}^{\prime}}C_{k}^{\prime}=C

be two positive minimal galleries from the base region
B
to a region
C\in\mathcal{R}
. Then

\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k})}\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k-1})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e_{1})}=\ell_{\Sigma(e^{\prime}_{k})}\ell_{\Sigma(e^{\prime}_{k-1})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e^{\prime}_{1})}.

Proof.

Any two minimal galleries from
B
to
C
are homotopic by a succession of homotopies across
2
-cells of
\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H})

[
Sal87
, Lemma 11]
. Hence, it suffices to prove the proposition when
\mathcal{H}
has rank
2
and
C=-B
. In this case, the result is immediate from
Corollary

2.9
.
∎

Definition 8.2
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{R}
, and let

B=C_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}}C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{e_{k}}C_{k}=C

be a positive minimal gallery from
B
to
C
. Define

\mathsf{Pow}(C):=\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k})}\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k-1})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e_{1})}.

8.1.
Proof of
Theorem

1.7

Suppose
C,D\in\mathcal{R}
are such that
D\leq C
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
. Let

B=C_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}C_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k-1}}C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{e_{k}}C_{k}=C

be a positive minimal gallery from
B
to
C
, where
D=C_{m}
. Let

-C=-C_{k}\xrightarrow{f_{k}}-C_{k-1}\xrightarrow{f_{k-1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{2}}-C_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}-C_{0}=-B

be the corresponding positive minimal gallery from
-C
to
-B
(with
-D=-C_{m}
). Using
Corollary

2.9
, we find that

\mathsf{Pow}(C)=\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k})}\ell_{\Sigma(e_{k-1})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e_{1})}=\ell_{\Sigma(f_{1})}\ell_{\Sigma(f_{2})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(f_{k})}

and

\mathsf{Pow}(D)=\ell_{\Sigma(e_{m})}\ell_{\Sigma(e_{m-1})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(e_{1})}=\ell_{\Sigma(f_{1})}\ell_{\Sigma(f_{2})}\cdots\ell_{\Sigma(f_{m})}.

This demonstrates that
\mathsf{Pow}(D)\leq\mathsf{Pow}(C)
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
.

Now consider the natural homomorphism from
\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
to its abelianization
H_{1}(\mathrm{Sal}(\mathcal{H}),B)
. If
C\in\mathcal{R}
and we apply this homomorphism to
\mathsf{Pow}(C)
, then, by
Theorem

2.3
, we obtain
\prod_{H\in\mathrm{inv}(C)}\overline{\ell}_{H}
. It follows that if
C,D\in\mathcal{R}
are such that
\mathsf{Pow}(D)\leq\mathsf{Pow}(C)
in
\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathcal{H},B)
, then
\mathrm{inv}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{inv}(C)
, so
D\leq C
in
\mathrm{Weak}(\mathcal{H},B)
.

9.
Future Work

9.1.
Noncrossing Pure Braid Presentations

In future work, we will combine our
Theorem

1.1
with Salvetti’s
Theorem

2.7
, Coxeter–Catalan combinatorics, Cambrian lattices, and noncrossing shards to write explicit presentations of the pure braid groups of finite Coxeter groups. In the special case of the symmetric group and the Tamari lattice, our method will recover Artin’s original presentation of the pure braid group
[
Art25
,
Art47
]
.

9.2.
The Pure Shard Monoid

The pure shard monoid is an interesting algebraic and order-theoretic structure that deserves further study—in particular, we would like to better understand the elements and the maximal chains in the interval
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
. These maximal chains correspond to
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-words representing the full twist
\Delta^{2}
. When
\mathcal{H}
is an arrangement of rank 2, we characterized these words for
\Delta^{2}
as the cyclic rotations of unimodal
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-words (
Proposition

4.8
). It would already be interesting to better understand
[\mathbbm{1},\Delta^{2}]_{\mathbf{P}^{+}}
for special cases, such as rank-3 arrangements or reflection arrangements of type-
A
Coxeter groups.

9.3.
Infinite Arrangements

Throughout this paper, we have assumed that
\mathcal{H}
is finite. It is natural to ask what aspects of the above theory generalize to arrangements with infinitely many hyperplanes.

9.4.
Bubbles, Blossom, Buttercup (and Bliss)

We view the bubble sort operator
\mathsf{Bubbles}
as the 0-Hecke action of any reduced word for the long element
w_{\circ}
in the symmetric group. The
higher Bruhat order
is a partial order defined on these reduced words
[
MS89
]
. We wonder if there are similar “higher Bruhat orders” built from the
\mathcal{L}_{\text{{\mbox{{Sh}}}}}
-words for
\Delta^{2}
. One might expect to find relevant maps
\mathsf{Blossom}
and
\mathsf{Buttercup}
in this theory. We recommend this subsection’s title as the logical name for this proposed work.
