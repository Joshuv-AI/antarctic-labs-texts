An Integer Linear Programming Model for Earth Observation Missions
Vincenzo Basco

Abstract
This paper addresses an optimization problem in satellite observation mission planning, focusing on the challenges of decentralized decision-making among satellites, which is crucial for optimizing strategies in dynamic observation environments. The method integrates mathematical modeling using integer programming and time-varying communication graphs, which are essential for efficient task scheduling. Specifically, the approach utilizes distributed Lagrangian relaxation techniques to manage the complexity of the problem. Numerical simulations are conducted to explore the feasibility of the proposed approach for handling complex satellite operations under evolving communication dynamics.

Introduction

In the last decade, the topic of autonomous space missions has gained significant attention. Notable examples include the interest in Mars missions and the upcoming plans for lunar colonization. Similarly, Earth observation missions have become increasingly important, with a particular focus on making these systems intelligent, autonomous, and resilient. This work centers on autonomous task planning in observation missions using a large network of satellites, addressing this challenge through a Multi-Agent Constrained Optimization (MACO) approach (cfr.
[
10
,
15
,
1
,
12
]
). The proposed method involves a ground station transmitting a list of observation targets to multiple satellites, which then coordinate via an inter-satellite communication network to formulate an optimal scheduling strategy. The communication network between satellites is represented as an undirected, unconnected, and time-varying graph, further complicating the problem.
Centralized algorithms do not align well with the resolution logic in an autonomous multi-agent environment. In space operations, centralized techniques typically rely on a ground-based station that sends a pre-computed plan, based on the information available at a specific time, to the satellite constellation. Here, we propose breaking this operational logic by assigning each satellite the task of organizing its own scheduling based on the information it receives from satellites within its cluster, thereby making the constellation autonomous. However, feasibility issues must be addressed in a coordinated manner.

MACO problems necessitate collaboration among multiple agents to optimize a global objective while balancing individual goals and overall system performance (cfr.
[
17
,
3
,
14
,
7
,
9
]
). Each agent has a localized view of the problem, including its own variables, constraints, and cost functions, and must exchange information to find a solution that satisfies global constraints and enhances the collective objective. MACO approach is pertinent in various fields, including the autonomous planning of multi-agent systems, where their decentralized nature is especially beneficial for large-scale and dynamic applications. Distributed optimization has explored techniques such as primal-dual reduction and transition matrix methods to address MACO problems. Primal-dual methods optimize the dual problem using Lagrangian relaxation but may lose the integer structure of the original problem. Here, to tackle the scheduling task, we model the problem as a mixed-integer optimization problem with binary variables and constraints that can be depicted as polygons. The geometric structure of the problem is crucial for determining the optimal solution, with each satellite having a time-varying communication graph (cfr.
[
16
,
8
,
6
,
2
]
).

The solution process consists of three primary stages:
(i) End users submit observation targets to the ground station;
(ii) The ground station transmits these targets to visible satellites, which then distribute the information through inter-satellite links;
(iii) Each satellite resolves a local optimization problem and communicates a scalar parameter (the Lagrange multiplier) to other visible satellites, leading to a series of solutions that converge to the optimal one. This model, examined mathematically through
M
ATLAB
©
simulations, provides a decentralized solution for the scheduling of satellite tasks, capable of handling the complexity and dynamics of evolving communication networks.

A.3
Primal Decomposition by Resource Allocation

The Primal Decomposition method for the constrained-coupled problem with real data is presented here. Let us consider a network of
n
agents (satellites). We address a problem of the form:

\begin{split}\min\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq 0\\
&{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\Omega_{i},\quad i=1,...,n\end{split}

(21)

where
f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})
is the objective function for subproblem
i
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})
represents the set of constraints on the amount of resources consumed as a result of the action
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}
, and
\Omega_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
is the feasible region for subproblem
i
. The problem (
21
) represents the
primal problem
. It is convenient to think at (
21
) in the context of allocating scarce resources in order to have a better overview of primal decomposition. In this context, multi-level decomposition methods divide the problem into a sequence of smaller problems or subproblems, each of which deals only with a vector
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}
. The subproblems must be then coordinated in order to solve the primal problem.

To determine such allocation of resources we introduce the following
master problem
in the variables
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}

\begin{split}\min_{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}}&\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(y_{i})\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq 0\end{split}

(22)

and the function
\phi_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi(y_{i})\coloneqq\min_{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}}&\quad f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq y_{i},\\
&\quad{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\Omega_{i}.\end{split}

(23)

Such a master problem corresponds to first allocating a vector of resources
y_{i}
to the objective function
f_{i}
of each subproblem, and then solving for the optimal solution. The set of allocation vectors
(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{n}^{*})
that minimizes the sum of all
\phi_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,n
) gives a solution to the primal problem. The next proposition formalizes the previous explanation.

Proposition A.3.1
.

The primal problem and the master problem are equivalent, in the sense that:

(
i
)

if a finite optimal solution to either problem exists, then the optimal values of the objectives
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(y_{i})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})
are equal;

(
ii
)

if
\{{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{i}^{*}}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
solves the primal and
\{y_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1,\dots,n}
solves the master problem, then
{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{i}^{*}}
solves (
23
) with
y_{i}=y_{i}^{*}
.

Constrained-Coupled Mixed-Integer Linear Program.

Let us consider a network of
n
agents aiming to solve the optimization problem

\begin{split}\min\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq 0\\
&{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\Omega_{i},\quad i=1,...,n\end{split}

(24)

where the variable
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}
has
p_{i}+q_{i}
components and the mixed-integer constraint set is of the form
\Omega_{i}=P_{i}\cup(\mathbb{Z}^{p_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}})
, for some non-empty polyhedron
P_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}+q_{i}}
. From this point forward, we will be dealing with a
Linear Programming
problem, as both the objective functions and constraints are expressed linearly.

We now proceed with an approximation of problem (
24
): the right-hand side of the coupling constraint is relaxed by a vector
\zeta>0
and each mixed-integer set
\Omega_{i}
is replaced by its convex hull, denoted by
\text{Co}(\Omega_{i})
. That is, the relaxed problem is given by

\begin{split}\min\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq\zeta\\
&{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\text{Co}(\Omega_{i}),\quad i=1,...,n.\end{split}

(25)

Proposition A.3.2
.

Let
\{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{1}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{n}}\}
be any vertex of the feasible set of problem (
25
). Then, there exists an index set
I_{Z}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, with cardinality
|I_{Z}|\geq n-S
, where
S=d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}
, such that
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\Omega_{i}
for all
i\in I_{Z}
.

As for the previous section, we introduce the master problem associated with (
24
). Such a problem is defined as

\begin{split}\min\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i})\\
\textnormal{subject to}\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}=\zeta\\
&{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}\in\hat{\Omega}_{i},\quad i=1,...,n\end{split}

(26)

where
\psi_{i}
is defined as the optimal cost of the
i
-th subproblem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}):=\min_{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}}&\quad f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}\\
&\quad{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\text{Co}(\Omega_{i}).\end{split}

(27)

Note that
\hat{\Omega}_{i}
is the set of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
for which problem (
27
) is feasible, i.e., such that there exists
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\text{Co}(\Omega_{i})
satisfying the local constraint
g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
.

A.4
Distributed approach for convex problems

In addition to their partial knowledge of the problem, we assume that the agents communicate according to an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}^{t}=(\{1,\dots,n\},\mathcal{E}^{t})
satisfying (
14
), where
\mathcal{E}^{t}\subset\{1,\dots,n\}^{2}
is the set of edges. The set of neighbours of
i
in
\mathcal{G}^{t}
at time
t
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{t}=\{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}:(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}^{t},\,i\neq j\}
. The agents can independently solve problem (
27
) to find the solution to (
25
), provided that an optimal allocation
({{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{1}^{*},\dots,{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}_{n}^{*})
is available. To simplify the notation, let
t
denote the iteration index. At each iteration
t\geq 0
, each agent
i
maintains
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
and computes
\lambda^{t}_{i}
as a Lagrange multiplier of:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}},\,\rho_{i}}&\quad f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})+M\rho_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}+\rho_{i}{\bf 1},\\
&\quad{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\text{Co}(\Omega_{i}),\\
&\quad\rho_{i}\geq 0\end{split}

(28)

where
M>0
is a real constant. Then each agent
i
receives
\lambda_{j}^{t}
from its neighbours
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{t}
and updates
{\sigma_{i}}^{t}
with:

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}+\alpha^{t}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{t}}(\lambda_{i}^{t}-\lambda_{j}^{t})

(29)

where step-size
\alpha^{t}
satisfies
\sum_{t>0}(\alpha^{t})^{2}<+\infty
and
\sum_{t>0}\alpha^{t}=+\infty
.

Theorem A.4.3

(
Convergence to minimizer).

For all large
M>0
, the algorithm (
28
)-(
29
) generates a vector
\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}^{t},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and a sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{1}^{t},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{n}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
such that,

(
i
)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}=\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, for all
t\geq 0
.

(
ii
)

\lim_{t\to\infty}|{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{*}|=0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, where
({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}^{*},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{*})
is optimal for (
27
).

(
iii
)

Every limit point of
\{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{1}^{t},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{n}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is an optimal (feasible) solution of problem (
25
).

Hence, we get the following algorithm for distributed Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problems. First, we provide a heuristic procedure for the algorithm; then we formally present the one applied to our case in Section 4 below.

Step 1
. Set
T_{f}>0
and initialize
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{0}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{0}=\zeta
.

Step 2
. For
t=0,\dots,T_{f}
compute
\lambda_{i}^{t}
as Lagrange multipier of (
28
), receive
\lambda_{j}^{t}
from any
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{t}
and update
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}
by using (
29
).

Step 3
. Return
{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}^{T_{f}}
as optimal solution of

\displaystyle\underset{\rho_{i},{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}},\Theta_{i}}{\operatorname{lex-min}}

\displaystyle\quad\rho_{i}

subject to

\displaystyle\quad f_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle\quad g_{i}({\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}})\leq{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{T_{f}}+\rho_{i}{\bf 1}

\displaystyle\quad{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}_{i}}\in\Omega_{i},

\displaystyle\quad\rho_{i}\geq 0.

Theorem A.4.4

(
Sub-optimal estimate).

For a sufficiently large
M>0
, the algorithm (
28
)-(
29
) generates a vector

\{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{1}^{t},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{n}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\quad\&\quad\{{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{1}^{t},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\operatorname{x}}}_{n}^{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

such that there exists a family of constants
C_{i}=C_{i}\left(\rho_{i},M\right)
such that

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}^{*}\leq C_{i}.

The algorithm we use to solve the scheduling problem in a distributed way is a variant of the classical Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) algorithm, where each agent
i
maintains its own Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{i}^{t}
associated with the constraints. At each iteration
t
, each agent
i
solves the problem (
28
) independently, using its own
\lambda_{i}^{t}
, and updates its estimate of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
according to (
29
), where
\alpha^{t}
is a step-size sequence. Note that in this algorithm, the agents do not have access to the entire state of the system, but only to their local state
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
and to the local constraints
\Omega_{i}
. However, by exchanging their Lagrange multipliers with their neighbours, the agents can collaboratively determine the values of
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
that satisfy the global constraints. In fact, it can be shown that under some assumptions, the ADMM algorithm converges to the optimal solution of the problem even in a distributed setting, where each agent only has access to its own local information. The convergence of the ADMM algorithm can be guaranteed under some assumptions on the problem data, such as the convexity and continuity of the objective and the constraints, and the boundedness of the feasible set. In general, the convergence of the ADMM algorithm can be slow, and the choice of the step-size sequence can have a significant impact on the convergence rate. However, the ADMM algorithm has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems in various fields, including machine learning, signal processing, and control, and it is a popular choice for solving distributed optimization problems.

A.5
Solving the relaxed problem

Consider the following optimization problem

\begin{split}v:=\min&\quad f^{\top}x\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad Ax\leq b\\
&\quad Cx\leq d\\
&\quad x\in X\end{split}

(30)

where
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is non-empty,
f\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
d\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Assume that (
30
) has a solution. The constraints
Cx\leq d
will be kept, together with
X
, to form the Lagrangian relaxation of (
30
) as follows. Let
\lambda\geq 0
.
The Lagrangian relaxation of (
30
) relative to the constraints
Ax\leq b
, with nonnegative Lagrangian multipliers
\lambda
, is the problem

\begin{split}v(\lambda):=\min&\quad f^{\top}x+\lambda^{\top}(Ax-b)\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad Cx\leq d\\
&\quad x\in X.\end{split}

(31)

In (
31
), the slack variable of the constraints
Ax\leq b
have been added to the objective function with weight
\lambda
and the constraints
Ax\leq b
have been dropped. One says that the constraints
Ax\leq b
have been dualized. It follows that
v(\lambda)\leq v
, for all
\lambda\geq 0
, i.e., the optimal value
v(\lambda)
is a lower bound on the optimal value of (
30
).
The problem of finding the tightest Lagrangian lower bound on
v
is

\begin{split}\max&\quad v(\lambda)\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\quad\lambda\geq 0.\end{split}

(32)

It is called the Lagrangian dual of (
30
) relative to the constraints
Ax\leq b
.
The problem (
32
) is formulated in the dual space of the Lagrangian multipliers, while (
31
) is a problem formulated in terms of
x
. Now, the Lagrangian function
v(\lambda)
is an implicit function of
\lambda
. Suppose that the set
\textnormal{Co\;}\{x\in X\mid Cx\leq d\}
is a polytope, i.e., a bounded polyhedron, then there exists a finite family
\left\{x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{K}\right\}
of extreme points of
\textnormal{Co\;}\{x\in X\mid Cx\leq d\}
, i.e., of points of
\{x\in X\mid Cx\leq d\}
, such that

\textnormal{Co\;}\{x\in X\mid Cx\leq d\}=\textnormal{Co}\left\{x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{K}\right\}.

Then it follows that

\min\;\{f^{\top}x+\lambda^{\top}(Ax-b)\mid Cx\leq d,x\in X\}=\min_{k=1,\ldots,K}\left\{f^{\top}x^{k}+\lambda^{\top}\left(Ax^{k}-b\right)\right\}

and
v(\lambda)
is the lower envelope of a family of linear functions of
\lambda,f^{\top}x^{k}+\lambda^{\top}\left(Ax^{k}-b\right),k=1,\ldots,K
, and a concave function of
\lambda
, with breakpoints where it is not differentiable (i.e., where the optimal solution of (
31
) is not unique).
The dual problem (
32
) is then

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\;v(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\;\min_{k=1,\ldots,K}\;\left\{f^{\top}x^{k}+\lambda^{\top}\left(Ax^{k}-b\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0,\eta}\left\{\eta\mid\eta\leq f^{\top}x^{k}+\lambda^{\top}\left(Ax^{k}-b\right),k=1,\ldots,K\right\}.
