Abstract
. We present MLTCP , a technique to augment today’s congestion control algorithms to accelerate DNN training jobs in shared GPU clusters. MLTCP enables the communication phases of jobs that compete for network bandwidth to interleave with each other, thereby utilizing the network efficiently. At the heart of MLTCP lies a very simple principle based on a key conceptual insight: DNN training flows should scale their congestion window size based on the number of bytes sent at each training iteration. We show that integrating this principle into today’s congestion control protocols is straightforward: by adding 30–60 lines of code to Reno, CUBIC, or DCQCN, MLTCP stabilizes flows of different jobs into an interleaved state within a few training iterations, regardless of the number of competing flows or the start time of each flow. Our experiments with popular DNN training jobs demonstrate that enabling MLTCP accelerates the average and 99 th percentile training iteration time by up to 2 × \times and 4 × \times , respectively.



1.
Introduction

The relentless pursuit of training ever-larger DNN models on shared GPU clusters makes efficient network congestion management crucial in sustaining high GPU utilization. Prior papers demonstrated that network congestion often results in longer training time
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Mudigere et al. 2022
;
Cho et al. 2019
)
. The state-of-the-art approaches to minimize the impact of network congestion include (
i
) reducing the size of parameters to transmit through the network using quantization or compression techniques
(
Watcharapichat et al. 2016
;
Alistarh et al. 2017
;
Lin et al. 2017
;
Jayarajan et al. 2019
)
; (
ii
) using pipelining to reduce the exposed communication time by overlapping the communication period of a job with its computation phase
(
Narayanan et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2020a
;
Mohan et al. 2022
;
Peng et al. 2019
)
; (
iii
) employing job scheduling and worker placement techniques that minimize network sharing by placing workers of the same job as close to each other as possible
(
Xiao et al. 2018
;
Mahajan et al. 2020
;
Qiao et al. 2021
;
Gu et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2018
;
Hashemi et al. 2019
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Jayaram Subramanya et al. 2023
)
.

Although these techniques reduce the chances of network congestion, cross-job network contention is inevitable in large-scale shared GPU clusters. Recent work
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
)
demonstrated that introducing
unfairness
in congestion control has a surprising payoff that accelerates the training time of DNN jobs in shared GPU clusters. The authors manually adjusted DCQCN
(
Zhu et al. 2015a
)
congestion control parameters to force a static unfair bandwidth sharing (60%-40% instead of 50%-50%) between competing DNN training jobs. After a few training iterations, this static unfairness created a desirable side-effect that nudged the communication phase of one job to
interleave
with the other, resulting in better overall network utilization and accelerating the training time of both jobs.

In contrast to pipelining
(
Jiang et al. 2020a
;
Mohan et al. 2022
;
Peng et al. 2019
;
Narayanan et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
)
, which overlaps the compute and communication phases of the
same training job
, the goal of inter-job communication interleaving is to fit the communication phases of
different jobs
to share the network more efficiently (§
2.1
). We show that robust communication interleaving, while promising, has several unsolved challenges (§
2.2
): (
i
) robustness to stragglers; (
ii
) robustness to partially compatible jobs; (
iii
) robustness to circular dependencies between jobs and links.

This paper asks the following question: “
Is it possible to achieve robust inter-job communication interleaving in a completely distributed manner?
” At first blush, it appears that achieving this goal is impossible because communication interleaving is dependent on the exact traffic pattern of the jobs. How can two (or more) jobs interleave their communication patterns if they are unaware of each other’s existence? Without a centralized controller, end-hosts do not have any information about which other jobs are in the network, making it seemingly impossible to self-adjust their communication time to achieve interleaving.

We propose
MLTCP
, a technique to augment today’s congestion control algorithms to enable DNN training jobs to converge automatically into an interleaved state.
An attractive feature of
MLTCP
is its simple principle based on a key conceptual insight: DNN training flows should
dynamically adjust
their aggressiveness based on the number of bytes sent.
This principle applies to window-based and rate-based congestion control protocols.
For instance, upon the receipt of an acknowledgment packet, window-based congestion control protocols update the congestion window,
cwnd
, as:

(1)

cwnd\leftarrow cwnd+additive\_increase

where
additive\_increase
indicates the magnitude of congestion window increase based on the specific goals of each congestion control variant.

MLTCP
updates Equation
1
to:

(2)

cwnd\leftarrow cwnd+\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)\times additive\_increase

where
\mathcal{F}
is a linear increasing function and
bytes\_ratio
is the number of bytes successfully sent normalized by the total number of bytes during a training iteration (§
3
). Unlike traditional datacenter traffic, where accurately estimating the size of every flow is intractable
(
Alizadeh et al. 2013
;
Ðukić et al. 2019
;
Gao et al. 2015
)
, the total number of bytes in a training iteration is a predictable and consistent value throughout the lifetime of most DNN training jobs as long as the hyper-parameters remain unchanged
(
Jiang et al. 2020b
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Unger et al. 2022
)
.

The intuition behind this simple technique is that
MLTCP
adjusts the aggressiveness of competing flows based on the remaining bytes of the current iteration, thereby approximating the Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) policy. Given that DNN training workloads are periodic,
MLTCP
creates a positive
cascading effect
that separates the communication phases of competing jobs into an interleaved state after a few iterations.

To evaluate
MLTCP
, we build a testbed with 12 servers, each equipped with one NVIDIA A100 GPU
(
a10 2021
)
and one 50 Gbps RDMA NIC. We then augment TCP Reno
(
Jacobson 1988a
)
, TCP CUBIC
(
Ha et al. 2008
)
, and RoCE DCQCN
(
Zhu et al. 2015a
)
algorithms with
MLTCP
by adding 30–60 lines of code for each variant. Our experiments with 7 representative DNN models
show that
MLTCP
stabilizes the flows into an interleaved state after
\approx
10 training iterations while improving the average and 99
th
percentile tail training iteration times of jobs by up to 2
\times
and 4
\times
, respectively, compared to default congestion control algorithms. We also show that
MLTCP
reduces the average number of dropped or ECN marked packets by up to 14.59
\times
. Finally, we demonstrate that
MLTCP
flows are robust to stragglers, partially compatible jobs, and circular dependencies between jobs and flows. Our kernel patch and evaluation scripts will be available online.

Communication Interleaving

In this section, we first provide a background on inter-job communication interleaving (§
2.1

2.1.
Background

Muri
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
introduced the concept of inter-job multi-resource interleaving for DNN training jobs. Muri scheduled jobs such that different resources (GPU, CPU, network, and storage) of different jobs are interleaved across the time dimension. To do so, Muri formulated the problem of resource interleaving as a k-dimensional maximum weighted matching problem and demonstrated significant improvements in job completion times. However, given the complexity of the matching problem, Muri could only consider jobs sharing the same set of GPUs, CPUs, memory, and network resources
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
.

Rajasekaran et al.
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
;
Rajasekaran et al. 2024
)
extended Muri’s footprint by focusing on the
network
as the only bottlenecked resource. They proposed interleaving the communication phases of jobs running on different GPU servers. Figure
1
(a) illustrates their approach with two jobs: Job
1
(training on GPU
1
and GPU
2
) and Job
2
(training on GPU
3
and GPU
4
), where each GPU is located in a different server in the cluster. Figures
1
(b) and (c) show the compute and communication patterns of Job
1
and Job
2

(d), without explicit inter-job communication interleaving, the communication phases of jobs sharing a network link will likely collide, causing congestion. Figure
1

1
and Job
2
are interleaved, allowing the network link to be shared more efficiently. The compute phases are not interleaved because they are on different GPUs.

To achieve inter-job communication interleaving,
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
)
used an unfair congestion control algorithm by
manually
changing the parameters of DCQCN
(
Zhu et al. 2015a
)
. For instance, the paper showed that when two VGG19
(
Simonyan and Zisserman 2015
)
training jobs share a network link, forcing a 60%-40% bandwidth share (instead of the default 50%-50%) results in 1.23

speed up for

compared to the default case
(

1
(d). In contrast, unfair bandwidth-sharing nudges the competing jobs from synchronization and pushes them toward an interleaved state, similar to Figure

In follow-up work, Cassini
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2024
)
used a centralized scheduler that shifts the start time of jobs to fit the compute phase of one job into the communication phase of another. Cassini consists of two parts. First, a network-aware scheduler considers job compatibility when placing jobs on the network. Second, an agent on each server continuously monitors these time shifts to ensure jobs do not deviate from their intended start times.

The following section explains that both
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
)
and
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2024
)
considered relatively stable conditions in testbed experiments and ignored several practical challenges to achieve robust inter-job communication interleaving. We then propose
MLTCP
, a straightforward change to congestion control algorithms that automatically achieves robust interleaving without manually changing the congestion control parameters or using a centralized scheduler (§
3
).

Challenge 1: Robustness to partially compatible jobs.
Interleaving the communication phases of jobs that share a link requires that the compute phase of one job fit into the communication phase of the other. In other words, the jobs must be
compatible

(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
)
. However, given the plethora of DNN models, it is nearly impossible for all jobs sharing a network link to be perfectly compatible. Our experiments show that prior approaches
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022
;
Rajasekaran et al. 2024
)
cannot achieve speedups when the compatibility ratio is below 70% (§
4.6
). In particular, when partially compatible jobs share a link, prior approaches to achieve interleaving oscillate between interleaved, partially interleaved, and not interleaved states. Since these partially interleaved and not interleaved states repeat throughout the training, some of the jobs’ training times become significantly worse than fair-sharing, defeating the purpose of interleaving to accelerate all jobs.

Challenge 2: Robustness to stragglers.
Although several testbed experiments
(

have shown that the communication pattern of DNN training jobs is predictable, servers in shared clusters often experience uncorrelated latency jitters due to the presence of stragglers

Cortez et al. 2017
;
Ananthanarayanan et al. 2012

Robustness to noise becomes crucial to ensure the network maintains an interleaved state while gracefully handling these unpredictable factors without compromising the overall training time. Our experiments show that when the straggler probability is above 10%, prior approaches
(
Rajasekaran et al. 2022

are unable to maintain interleaving and end up slowing down the 99

3.
MLTCP
Design

This section describes
MLTCP
, a congestion control feature to automatically interleave the communication phases of DNN jobs that share a network. First, we describe
MLTCP
’s goals and high-level design principles (§
3.1
). Then, we describe
MLTCP
’s core techniques: the job favoritism policy (§
3.2
) and the bandwidth aggressiveness function (§
3.3
).
Next, we show how these techniques apply to various congestion control implementations, including window-based algorithms, such as Reno and CUBIC, as well as rate-based ones, such as DCQCN (§
3.4
). Finally, we discuss how
MLTCP
updates its parameters during the training (§
3.5
).

3.1.
Goals and High-Level Concept

MLTCP
’s goal is to stabilize flows belonging to different DNN training jobs into an interleaved state without using a centralized controller, regardless of job start times, DNN model size, parallelization strategy, or number of jobs competing for bandwidth. At the same time,
MLTCP
should maintain robustness to stragglers, partially compatible jobs, and circular dependencies across jobs and links.

Figure
3
illustrates
MLTCP
’s high-level concept when two jobs Job
1
and Job
2
share a bottleneck. Intuitively, transforming their communication patterns from a non-interleaved state to a fully interleaved state means the communication phase of one job should
slide
to the left of the figure, while the other simultaneously slides to the right until they are fully interleaved.
MLTCP
uses two techniques to achieve this goal. First, it selects which job should slide left by allowing it to occupy a higher portion of the link bandwidth at every iteration until the communication patterns are interleaved. This technique is called the job favoritism policy (§
3.2
). Second, to achieve the effect of favoritism in a distributed manner,
MLTCP
introduces a novel feature that dynamically adjusts the congestion window (or rate) of flows that belong to each competing job using a scaling function called the bandwidth aggressiveness function (§
3.3
).

3.2.
Job Favoritism Policy

DNN training jobs are periodic; hence, when two or more jobs compete for network, allocating more bandwidth to the flows that belong to one of the jobs accelerates that job’s current training iteration and, more importantly, affects the start times of its subsequent training iterations. In other words, giving more bandwidth to one of the jobs slides this job’s communication pattern to the left of the others. Eventually, this process nudges the communication phases of the jobs to separate. The question is, which job should be given higher bandwidth? In fact, the choice of the job matters!

For example, consider two DNN training jobs sharing a bottleneck link with 50 Gbps capacity, as shown in Figure
4
(a). At time
t=0
, Job
1
has a slight head start over Job
2
. As a result, favoring Job
1
over Job
2
accelerates its training iterations even more and causes the communication phases to further separate in the subsequent iterations. In contrast, favoring Job
2
hurts the eventual interleaving state and leads the communication pattern of the two jobs to overlap even more, as shown in Figure
4
(b).

In principle, any favoritism ordering that follows the Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) policy is valid. For instance, policies that favor jobs based on earlier iteration start time, earlier iteration finish time, smallest data remaining, or largest data sent all stabilize into an interleaved state. The intuition is that the favoritism policies that
reinforce
SRPT eventually achieve an interleaving state. However, a policy that cancels out the effect of SRPT, such as favoring jobs based on the latest iteration start time, latest iteration finish time, largest data remaining, or smallest data sent, cannot achieve interleaving. In
MLTCP
, we use the percentage of bytes sent as our favoritism policy because it enables us to implement
MLTCP
in a fully distributed manner. Time-based policies require a centralized controller to compare jobs’ start or finish times. An important property of
MLTCP
’s job favoritism technique is that it provides native robustness to stragglers and partially compatible jobs because it fundamentally emulates an SRPT policy. As a result, any disturbance in communication patterns automatically triggers the sliding mechanism toward stabilizing into an interleaved state.

3.3.
Bandwidth Aggressiveness Function

To realize
MLTCP
’s favoritism policy in a distributed manner, the flow closest to completing its iteration should automatically grab a larger bandwidth share than others, helping
MLTCP
achieve the desired sliding effect.
MLTCP
achieves this goal by dynamically adjusting the congestion window (cwnd) or sending rate of flows based on a
bandwidth aggressiveness function

\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)
, where
bytes\_ratio
is the number of bytes sent in the current iteration normalized by the total bytes (
\frac{bytes\_sent}{total\_bytes}
).
MLTCP
obtains these parameters from the TCP socket (details in §
3.5
).

Figure
5
provides a conceptual illustration of several potential aggressiveness functions. The x-axis represents
bytes\_ratio
, and the y-axis determines the range of aggressiveness. The figure shows several function shapes. Our evaluations (§
4.8
) demonstrate that many aggressiveness functions can achieve
MLTCP
’s interleaving goals as long as three requirements are satisfied: (
i
) the range is large enough to absorb the noise (e.g., slight variations in round-trip time (RTT) or iteration times) in the network; (
ii
) the derivative of the function is non-negative; (
iii
) all flows employ the same bandwidth aggressiveness function. Hence, to simplify the implementation, we propose a linear increasing function:

(3)

\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)=S\times bytes\_ratio+I

Where
S
and
I
represent the linear function’s slope and intercept, respectively. These are constant parameters tuned based on the congestion control algorithm, link rate, and noise in the system. Our evaluations suggest guidelines to select these parameters (§
4.8
).

MLTCP
has two mechanisms for using
\mathcal{F}
to augment default congestion control algorithms. The first mechanism uses
\mathcal{F}
as a scaling factor for the cwnd increment made during the window (or rate) increase step. As a result, the flow with the most bytes sent increases its window (or rate) more aggressively, triggering
MLTCP
’s favoritism policy and eventually interleaving the communication phases of different jobs. The second mechanism uses
\mathcal{F}
as a scaling factor during the multiplicative decrease step. This mechanism has a similar effect: the flow with the most bytes sent reduces its window (or rate) less aggressively, enabling it to claim a bigger share of the bandwidth even after packet loss (or ECN) events. This again follows the job favoritism principle and leads to interleaving. Note that
\mathcal{F}
’s output is unitless.

Reno additive increase.
The TCP Reno algorithm uses a cumulative ack mechanism to acknowledge multiple in-flight packets with a single ack. Upon the receipt of an ack, Reno increases its congestion window as follows:

(4)

cwnd\leftarrow cwnd+\frac{\#num\_acks}{cwnd}

MLTCP
updates the above equation to:

(5)

cwnd\leftarrow cwnd+{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)}}\times\cfrac{\#num\_acks}{cwnd}

Reno multiplicative decrease.

With every dropped packet, TCP Reno updates its cwnd as follows:

(6)

cwnd\leftarrow 0.5\times cwnd

CUBIC’s window growth algorithm computes the target cwnd using a cubic function based on the time gap from the last multiplicative decrease event, as shown below:

(8)

cwnd\leftarrow CUBIC(time)

(9)

cwnd\leftarrow CUBIC({\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)}}\times time)

When different

The multiplicative decrease step of CUBIC is similar to Reno, but instead of having a fixed value of 0.5, CUBIC specifies the multiplicative decrease factor as a parameter called

(10)

cwnd\leftarrow\beta\times cwnd

cwnd\leftarrow{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)}}\times\beta\times cwnd

DCQCN rate increase.
DCQCN is a rate-based congestion control algorithm commonly used in lossless RoCE networks
(
Zhu et al. 2015a
)
.
It periodically increases the target rate by fixed steps
R_{AI}
during the additive increase stage:

(12)

target\_rate\leftarrow target\_rate+R_{AI}

MLTCP
updates the above equation to:

(13)

target\_rate\leftarrow target\_rate+{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)}}\times R_{AI}

DCQCN rate decrease.
DCQCN uses ECN-marked packets to detect congestion. Upon the receipt of a Congestion Notification Packet (CNP) from the receiver, the sender reduces its current rate using a multiplicative decrease factor:

(14)

curr\_rate\leftarrow(1-\cfrac{\alpha}{2})\times curr\_rate

where
\alpha
is the rate reduction factor of the DCQCN algorithm.
MLTCP
updates the above equation to:

(15)

curr\_rate\leftarrow{\color[rgb]{0,1,1}{\mathcal{F}(bytes\_ratio)}}\times(1-\cfrac{\alpha}{2})\times curr\_rate

MLTCP
does not make any changes to any other parts of the congestion control algorithm (e.g., slow start, initial congestion window). Initially, we assumed that
MLTCP
must update both increase and decrease phases of congestion control algorithms to achieve interleaving. However, our evaluations show that updating only the increase or decrease phase is enough to achieve interleaving, and they both have similar gains (§
4.8
).

3.5.
Updating
MLTCP
Parameters

An essential detail in
MLTCP
is correctly updating
bytes\_ratio=\frac{bytes\_sent}{total\_bytes}
during each iteration. There are many ways to achieve this goal. This section describes our approach.

Updating
bytes_sent
.

MLTCP
uses the pluggable congestion module
(
plu 2005
;
Hemminger 2005
)
to insert the
Update_MLTCP_Params
procedure, shown in Algorithm
1
, as a hook into the TCP stack.
This function has two essential goals: the first is to update the number of successfully sent bytes based on the acked packets, and the second is to identify iteration boundaries to reset
bytes\_sent
at the start of each training iteration.
Update_MLTCP_Params
is called by the TCP stack whenever an ack packet is received. It uses the number of acks to estimate the bytes that the sender sent successfully (line
15
). For training jobs with a simple on-off communication pattern (i.e., data parallel jobs), detecting the start of the communication phase is relatively straightforward: wait for a gap in the ack arrivals that is longer than a typical RTT. However, to support training jobs with more complex communication patterns with multiple peaks appearing during the same communication phase (i.e., pipeline/tensor/model parallel jobs), we use a heuristic that estimates the gaps between the communication phase of different iterations, called
iter\_gap
(line
7
).
Our heuristic begins by keeping track of the current gap and the maximum gap between subsequent acks (lines
17
and
18
, respectively). If the current gap is within an acceptable threshold of
iter\_gap
, it considers the current ack as the start of a new iteration’s communication phase (line
19
), updates
iter\_gap
using an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) function (line
22
), and resets
MLTCP
’s parameters (lines
24
–
25
). Otherwise, it computes
bytes\_ratio
based on the current

(line
28
).

Obtaining
total_bytes
.

The total bytes in each iteration (
total\_bytes
) is a constant variable that depends on the size of the DNN model, the parallelization strategy, and the communication collective. In our implementation, we pre-calculate this number and store it in a file, but an optimization of our implementation would be to automatically learn this value by measuring the total amount of data during the first few iterations. We leave this optimization to future work.

4.5.
Impact of Stragglers

To demonstrate
MLTCP
’s robustness to stragglers, we train two GPT-2 jobs sharing a bottleneck link and synthetically insert sleep commands in each server’s training code based on a uniform straggling probability. If a server decides to lag at a particular iteration, it sleeps for a period uniformly randomly chosen between 5% and 10% of the isolation iteration time, where isolation iteration time refers to the iteration time when the job is running in isolation.

In the case of Cassini, frequent straggling events break the interleaving for a few iterations, forcing the jobs to compete for bandwidth.
Cassini’s end-host agent tries to overcome this by forcing the straggling server to skip an iteration, hoping that the noise in the system will be resolved in the next iteration and the jobs will return to an interleaved state. In contrast, MLQCN’s bandwidth adjustment function dynamically reacts to the straggling server to maintain the interleaving. Figure
13
shows the training iteration time speedups (normalized to default DCQCN) as the straggler probability increases. There are two takeaways from this figure. First, MLQCN’s speedup does not change dramatically in the presence of stragglers because both MLQCN and DCQCN experience the same straggler event. As a result, the relative speedup remains more or less constant. Second, Cassini’s tail iteration time speedup drops sharply as the straggler probably goes beyond 10%.

We now study the impact of partial compatibility on

5.
Discussion

Complex communication patterns.

Algorithm
1
uses a heuristic of detecting the longest gap in communication peaks to identify iteration boundaries.
MLTCP
’s mechanism is not tied to detecting the exact iteration boundary of the training model; in fact,
MLTCP
’s approach works well even if the parameter update happens in the middle of a training iteration, as long as its close to a large compute gap and the the parameter updates take place consistently at every training iteration.

Fairness goals.

Traditionally, congestion control algorithms converge to fair bandwidth sharing among competing flows. With
MLTCP
, flows may start with unfair bandwidth sharing and slowly converge to an interleaved state. But
MLTCP
is not an unfair congestion control algorithm for the entire duration of a training job. It
shifts
the granularity of fairness from sub-RTT time scales into training iteration (many RTTs) time scales.

Priority vs. favoritism.
We intentionally avoid calling
MLTCP
’s favoritism policy as
a priority function
to avoid confusion with conventional packet priority concept. Packet priorities require switch support, and have limited priority levels. In contrast,
MLTCP
’s favoritism is a completely distributed function implemented in congestion control with fine-grained granularity through adjusting the congestion window (or sending rate).

Interactions between
MLTCP
’s flows and legacy flows.
In our implementation, we enable
MLTCP
in the congestion control parameter of NCCL’s FAST socket plugin
(
ncc [n. d.]
)
. Hence, only DNN flows using the NCCL library change cwnd based on
MLTCP
, and non-DNN training flows use the default congestion algorithm. Since
MLTCP
changes the aggressiveness of the congestion control, DNN jobs running
MLTCP
could potentially be unfair towards non-DNN flows. But
MLTCP
’s technique can be used to address this challenge by assigning a large
\mathcal{F}
function (larger than DNN flows) to latency-sensitive legacy flows. As a result, flows at higher priorities can be safeguarded from the DNN traffic.

6.
Related Work

Our work builds on several lines of related research.

Congestion control.
There is vast literature on congestion control. Many rely on feedback signals indicating congestion in the network, and reduce their sending cwnd or rate. Such protocols include drop-based
(
Jacobson 1988b
;
Ha et al. 2008
;
Brakmo and Peterson 1995
;
Xu et al. 2004
;
Gerla et al. 2001
)
, delay-based
(
Kumar et al. 2020
;
Mittal et al. 2015
;
Lee et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2008
;
Baiocchi et al. [n. d.]
)
, and ECN-based
(
Alizadeh et al. 2010
;
Zhu et al. 2015a
)
. We adapt TCP Reno because it is the canonical congestion control algorithm, and CUBIC because it is the default in Linux. Further, we augment DCQCN because it is commonly used in machine learning training today. We believe that
MLTCP
can augment feedback-based protocols without significant change.
Other types of congestion control include deadline-aware
(
Wilson et al. 2011
;
Vamanan et al. 2012
)
, router-based
(
Katabi et al. 2002
;
Dukkipati et al. 2005
;
Taheri et al. 2020
)
, and receiver-based
(
Handley et al. 2017
;
Montazeri et al. 2018
)
. For deadline-based, inter-job interleaving could be implemented by adjusting the deadlines of training jobs competing on the same link. However, this requires redefining the concept of deadlines before flows converge to an interleaved state. Router-assisted approaches could also implement inter-job interleaving by enabling the router to determine the unfairness, based on when the flows started. Similarly, receiver-based protocols may delegate unfairness control to the receiver, which knows exactly how many bytes have been sent. We leave the implementation and analysis of these algorithms to future work.

Flow scheduling.

There has been many workload-specific dynamic flow scheduling techniques designed for optimizing datacenter networks. Many of these focus on efficient utilization of network resources, achieving high bisection bandwidths
(
Al-Fares et al. 2010
;
Chowdhury and Stoica 2015
;
Chowdhury et al. 2014
;
Jajoo et al. 2019
;
Perry et al. 2014
)
and minimizing the flow completion times
(
Hong et al. 2012
;
Alizadeh et al. 2013
;
Bai et al. 2017
)
. These proposals tend to assume the scheduler has extensive knowledge about active flows in the network. In contrast,
MLTCP
achieves interleaving in a distributed manner without any information about flow arrivals/departures.

DNN job placement schedulers.
Prior work demonstrated that generic flow schedulers are not optimal for DNN training jobs
(
Pan et al. 2022
;
Mai et al. 2015
)
. As a result, there has been much work in creating schedulers for DNN training jobs. Many focused on compute-optimization in how they assign workers to jobs, and only considered the network so far as to try and schedule workers for a job close together
(
Xiao et al. 2018
;
Mahajan et al. 2020
;
Qiao et al. 2021
;
Gu et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2018
;
Jayaram Subramanya et al. 2023
)
.
MLTCP
complements these schedulers by taking advantage of incidental compatibility between jobs and interleaving them.

Network-aware DNN schedulers.
There are also schedulers for the network and communication strategy of the jobs. TACCL
(
Shah et al. 2023
)
and BytePS
(
Jiang et al. 2020b
)
develop frameworks for optimizing the collective communication algorithm. TicTac
(
Hashemi et al. 2019
)
optimally interleaves a job’s computation with its own communication. ByteScheduler
(
Peng et al. 2019
)
optimizes the partitioning and order of tensor transmissions. Syndicate
(
Mahajan et al. 2023
)
proposes breaking down communication to smaller pieces to perform joint optimization of scheduling and execution plan of collective communications. All of these approaches focus on optimizing a single job, while
MLTCP
further enhances these strategies by optimizing the communication interleaving over all jobs sharing links.

Multi-resource sharing.
Muri
(
Zhao et al. 2022
)
introduced the idea of multi-resource interleaving for DNN training. It interleaves a variety of resources together to optimize cluster utilization. However, Muri requires all jobs to share the same set of GPUs. In practice, GPU-resource sharing is not possible in large DNN training cluster. In contrast,
MLTCP
only considers communication interleaving and is applicable to all jobs in the cluster regardless of computation resources being used.

Benefits of unfairness in the network.
Recently, there have been calls to consider new notions of fairness in the network. Zaplatel et al.
(
Zaplatel and Kuipers 2023
)
argue that flow completion time rather than bandwidth fairness should be the metric for fairness. Arslan et al.
(
Arslan et al. 2023
)
follow a similar line of reasoning to conclude that unfairness can also lead to higher energy efficiency.

7.
Conclusion

MLTCP
is a technique to augment a family of congestion control algorithms for shared DNN training clusters. The key idea in
MLTCP
is to reinforce a shortest remaining processing time first policy by dynamically adjusting the congestion window (or sending rate) of flows based on the number of successfully sent bytes. We show that this straightforward change in congestion control algorithms results in automatic inter-job communication interleaving and accelerates the average and 99
th
percentile of training iteration times of jobs by up to 2
\times
and 4
\times
, respectively. This work does not raise any ethical issues.
