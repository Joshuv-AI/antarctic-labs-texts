Polynomial Bogolyubov for special linear groups via tensor rank
Shai Evra, Guy Kindler, and Noam Lifshitz

Abstract
. We prove a polynomial Bogolyubov type lemma for the special linear group over finite fields. Specifically, we show that there exists an absolute constant C > 0 , C>0, such that if A A is a density α \alpha subset of the special linear group, then the set A ​ A − 1 ​ A ​ A − 1 AA^{-1}AA^{-1} contains a subgroup H H of density α C \alpha^{C} . Moreover, this subgroup is isomorphic to a special linear group of a smaller rank. We also show that if A A is an approximate subgroups then it can be covered by the union of few cosets of H H . Our proof makes use of the Gurevich–Howe notion of tensor rank, and of a strengthened Bonami type Lemma for global functions on the bilinear scheme. We also present applications to spectral bounds for global convolution operators, global product free sets, and covering numbers corresponding to global sets.



1.
Introduction

Bogolyubov’s lemma for finite fields
[
Bog39
]
states that for a dense-enough set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, the set
2A-2A
contains a large subspace.
The state-of-the-art in this direction was proven by Sanders
[
San12
]
who showed that if
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
has density
\alpha
then
2A-2A
contains a subspace of co-dimension
O_{q}(\log^{4}(\frac{1}{\alpha}))
.
This is refered to as a quasi-polynomial Bogolyubov result, as the density of the subspace is quasi-polynomial in the density of
A
.
It is a major open problem in additive combinatorics to obtain a polynomial version of the Bogolyugov lemma.

In this work we prove an analogue result in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, showing that for a subset
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, of density
\mu(A)=|A|/|\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
, the set
AA^{-1}AA^{-1}
contains a subgroup

whose density is polynomial in the density of
A
, thereby showing a polynomial Bogolyubov type result for
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Moreover, we show that
L
can be taken to be of a certain ‘dictatorial’ structure.
Following Friedgut
[

]
we call the set of matrices of the form
\mathcal{D}_{v,u}:=\{g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}):gv=u\}
and of the form
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{v,u}:=\{g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}):g^{t}v=u\}

dictators
.
If
k

dictators, then we call their intersection a

-

.
Our polynomial Bogolyubov lemma gaurantees that

contains a subgroup that is also an umvirate – we call these

.
A particularly nice class of groumvirates are the following subgroups.

Definition 1.1
.

A

-groumvirate
in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a conjugate of the subgroup of matrices of the form

L_{k}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&0\\
0&X\end{pmatrix}\,:\,X\in\mathrm{SL}_{n-k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\right\},

We call a coset of a good

-umvirate

Our polynomial variant of the Bogolyubov lemma takes the following form.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists

, every prime power

We prove Theorem

by first finding a good

-umvirate

satisfies a certain pseudorandomness notion called globalness.
We then prove that global sets have good growth properties by showing that if

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Hence for a global set

, the density of

1.1.
Global sets and mixing

We actually prove a stronger statement that implies growth, namely we show that the convolution of the indicators of global sets (defined below) is very close to constant.

Let

1.2.
Product mixing

Using similar methods to Theorem
1.3
, we also prove a three-function version.
Let
\langle,\rangle
be the standard inner product on
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
A,B,C\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be global sets.
Then

\left|\;\langle 1_{A}*1_{B},1_{C}\rangle-\mu(A)\mu(B)\mu(C)\;\right|\leq q^{-n/5}\mu(A)\mu(B)\mu(C).

Using an observation by Nikolov and Pyber
[
NP11
]
, this yields the following corrolary of Theorem
1.6
.

Corollary 1.7
.

If
A,B,C\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are global sets, then
ABC=\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

To put our result in context we note that Gowers
[
Gow08
]
proved an analogue of Theorem
1.6
where the globalness hypothesis is replaced by the hypothesis that the sets
A,B,C
all have density at least
\left(\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}\right)^{-1/3}
.
Gowers was motivated by the problem of finding the largest product free set in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, where
A
is said to be

if
A^{2}\cap A=\varnothing
.
He managed to prove that such sets must have density
\leq\left(\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}\right)^{-1/3}
.
This bound is polynomial in the size of the group when
n
is a constant, but the dependency on the density deteriorates as the rank
n
increases.
Gowers’ result has found various applications in theoretical computer science, e.g. to communication complexity
[
GV15
]
and to questions related to matrix multiplication
[
BCG+23
]
.

As a further corollary of Theorem
1.6
, we obtain a structural/stability version for Gowers’ problem.

Corollary 1.8
.

There exists
c>0
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any prime power
q
, the following holds.
If
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a product free set of density
\mu(A)\geq q^{-cn^{2}}
, then there exists a
t
-umvirate

, such that
\frac{|A\cap U|}{|U|}\geq q^{ctn}\mu(A)
.

1.3.
Approximate subgroups

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
.
A set
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is said to be a
K
-
approximate subgroup
if
A=A^{-1}
and
|A^{2}|\leq K|A|
.
The structure of approximate subgroups is well understood in the bounded rank regime (see e.g.
[
BGT11
,
BGT12
,
PS16
,
EMPS21
]
), however the case where the rank
n
is allowed to grow to infinity is completely open.
We make the following step towards understanding the high rank regime.

Theorem 1.9
.

There exists
C>0
, such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every prime power
q
, the following holds.
If
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a
K
-approximate subgroup, then there exists a good groumvirate
H
of density
\mu(H)\geq\mu(A)^{C}
, such that
A
is contained in the union of
\frac{K^{5}|A|}{|H|}
-cosets of
H
.

We note that an analogue result for
A_{n}
appears in Keevash and Lifshitz
[
KL
, Thm. 1.2]
.
Additionaly, note that when the factor
K
in Theorem
1.9
is not too large, then the union of cosets that is claimed to contain
A
is not much larger than
A
.
Intuitively, this means that whenever a large set

is an approximate group there must be an underlying groumvirate that explains this.

1.4.
Methods

Our work relies on ideas of Sarnak and Xue
[
SX91
]
, which were later also used by Gowers
[
Gow08
]
in study of product free sets, and on some refinments by Keevash, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
KLM22
]
.

Write
L_{0}^{2}(G)
for the space of functions on
G
with
\mathbb{E}[f]=0
.
A key idea in
[
SX91
]
is that if
G
is a group and
T
is a
G
-morphism on
L_{0}^{2}(G)
, then one can upper bound the eigenvalues of
T
by combining an upper bound on the trace of
T^{*}T
with a lower bound on the minimal dimension of an eigenspace.
The latter is always lower bounded by the minimal dimension of a nontrivial representations of
G
.
Gowers called this minimal dimension, which we denote by
D(G)
, the
Quasirandomness
of
G
, and proved that product free sets have density
\leq D(G)^{-1/3}
.
By
[
LS74
]
for finite simple groups of Lie type of bounded rank,
D(G)
is polynomial in the size of
|G|
.
However, in the unbounded rank case, or for
G=A_{n}
, it is significantly weaker.

In
A_{n}
, the above approach was refined by Eberhard
[
Ebe16
]
and Keevash–Lifshitz
[
KL
]
.
The latter paper obtained improved bounds by showing that for indicators of global sets, almost all of the Fourier mass is concentrated on the high dimensional representations.
This was used to substantially improve Gowers’ bound for global product free sets by Keevash and Lifshitz
[
KL
]
to
e^{-O(D(A_{n})^{1/3})}
.

In order to show that global product free sets have their mass concentrated on the high dimensional representations,
[
KL
]
followed Ellis, Friedgut, and Pilpel
[
EFP11
]
and decomposed the space
L^{2}(A_{n})
into levels
1
1

1

[
EFP11
]
actually worked with

, but the difference is insignificant.

L^{2}(A_{n})=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{n}V_{=d}
, where each space

is an

and the minimal dimensions of subrepresentation of

increases rapidly with

They then used ideas from the theory of Boolean functions to show that when

is small, the projection of indicators of global set onto

In this paper we develop an analogue theory for

into a direct sum of

Additionally, we show a ‘level

1.5.
Levels and tensor rank on
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))

Our partition of

uses the idea of

of representations, first defined by Gurevich and Howe
[
GH21
]
.
Their approach was a departure from the Harish-Chandra philosophy of cusp forms which, roughly speaking, classifies the set of irreducible representations in terms of the cuspidal representations, that are in a sense the largest ones.

Consider the permutation representation
\omega
of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
, given by
\omega(g)f(x)=f(g^{-1}x)
for any

and

, which decomposes as
\omega=\mathbf{1}\oplus\omega_{0}
, where
\mathbf{1}
and
\omega_{0}
are the trivial and smallest dimensional non-trivial irreducible representations of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
By
[
GH21
, Def. 3.1.1]
an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is said to be of tensor rank

if it appears in
\omega^{\otimes k}
, the
k
-fold tensor product of

, but not in
\omega^{\otimes(k-1)}
.
Denote by
(\hbox{\hskip 17.98543pt\hskip-17.98543pt\hbox{$\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})$}\hskip-17.98543pt\hskip-4.16667pt\raisebox{18.50012pt}{\hbox{$\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}$}}\hskip-4.16667pt\hskip 17.98543pt})_{\otimes,k}

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of tensor rank
k
.
By
[
GH21
, Prop. 1.2.1]
, the
(\hbox{\hskip 17.98543pt\hskip-17.98543pt\hbox{$\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})$}\hskip-17.98543pt\hskip-4.16667pt\raisebox{18.50012pt}{\hbox{$\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}$}}\hskip-4.16667pt\hskip 17.98543pt})_{\otimes,k}

, form a partition of the irreducible representation of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Recall that an irreducible representation
\rho

\rho_{v,u}\in L^{2}(G)

\rho_{v,u}(g)=\langle\rho(g)v,u\rangle

Denote by

By the Peter-Weyl Theorem,

L^{2}(G)_{\rho}\cong\rho\otimes\rho^{*},\qquad\forall\rho\in\widehat{G},

L^{2}(G)=\bigoplus_{\rho}L^{2}(G)_{\rho},\qquad\rho\in\widehat{G}.

a decomposition into irreducible

Definition 1.10
.

For

L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}=\bigoplus_{\rho}L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{\rho},\qquad\rho\in(\hbox{\hskip 17.98543pt\hskip-17.98543pt\hbox{$\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})$}\hskip-17.98543pt\hskip-4.16667pt\raisebox{18.50012pt}{\hbox{$\hbox{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\lx@scalerel@obj{\kern-0.6pt\bigwedge\kern-0.6pt}}}}$}}\hskip-4.16667pt\hskip 17.98543pt})_{\otimes,d}

The partition of

1.6.
Levels and degrees on
L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,V))

In order to obtain the level
d
inequality we first prove a corresponding Theorem in the bilinear scheme
\mathcal{L}(V,V)
.
That space is also equipped with a natural decomposition into levels but it does not seem, at first look, to be related to the spaces
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}(V))_{=d}
.
However we manage to bridge the two notions by using another deep Theorem of Gurevich and Howe that turns out to releate the representation theoretic notion of tensor rank with the Boolean theoretic notion of a

On the Boolean cube, a function

More generally, if

-

whose elements point-wise stabilize

Gurevich and Howe showed that the tensor rank of an irreducible representation

We thus connect between the notions of level in the nonabelian

As mentioned above, we obtain a level

To state the connection between the two let us first briefly recall the abelian Fourier analysis on

For a prime power

The characters

Denote

j\colon L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}(V))\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,V)),\qquad j(f)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x)&x\in\mathrm{SL}(V)\\
0&x\not\in\mathrm{SL}(V)\end{array}\right..

We find the following remarkable connection between the nonabelian notion of tensor rank/level in
\mathrm{SL}(V)
(Definition
1.10
) and the abelian notion of level/degree in
\mathcal{L}(V,V)
(Definition
1.11
).

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}(V))_{=d}
, i.e.
f
is of (non-Abelian) level
d
.
Then

\|j(f)^{\leq d}\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{4q}\|f\|_{2}.

1.7.
Bonami type and level inequalities

We make use of the following Bonami type inequality for functions on
\mathcal{L}(V,W)
, which generalizes the Bonami type result of Ellis, Kindler, and Lifshitz
[
EKL22
]
from
4
-norms to
\ell
-norms, where
\ell
is any power of
2
.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
be
(d,\epsilon)
-global of degree
d
, and let
\ell
be a power of
2
.
Then

\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}.

From Theorem
1.13
we obtain the following level
d
inequality.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{L}(V,W)\to\{0,1\}
be
(d,\epsilon)
-global, and let
\ell
be a power of
2
.
Then

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{460d^{2}\ell}\mathbb{E}[f]\epsilon^{1-2/\ell}.

Theorem
1.14
gives a level inequality on
\mathcal{L}(V,W)
w.r.t. the Abelian level notion (Definition
1.11
).
We also prove in Theorem
6.12
below a level inequality on
\mathrm{SL}(V)
w.r.t. the non-Abelian level notion (Definition
1.10
).
More precisely, we give a bound for the weight that functions over
\mathrm{SL}(V)
have on spaces of low non-Abelian level (i.e. over representations of low tensor rank).
Theorem
6.12
is obtained from Theorem
1.14
, combined with the relation expressed in Lemma
1.12
between the Abelian and non-Abelian notions of level.

1.8.
From level inequalities to growth

As mentioned above, the convolution estimate of Theorem
1.3
is the main component in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Let us explain how it is obtained from our level
d
inequality (Theorem
1.14
).
Let
A,B\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be two global subsets, let
f=\frac{1_{A}}{\mu(A)},g=\frac{1_{B}}{\mu(B)}\in L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
be their normaised indicators, and by abuse of notation, we identify them with
j(f),j(g)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}))
.
Consider the decomposition of
g
into its (non-Abelian) level componenets, as defined in Definition
1.10
,

g=\sum_{d=0}^{n}g_{=d}=1+\sum_{d=1}^{n}g_{=d}.

Let
T_{f}
denote the operator on
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}(V))
defined for any
h\in L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}(V))
by

T_{f}h=f*h

Expanding the convolution we thus get

T_{f}g=1+\sum_{d=1}^{n}T_{f}g_{=d},

and our goal reduces to showing that
\|T_{f}g_{=d}\|_{2}
is small for each
d\geq 1
.
This is acheived by both bounding the norm of
g_{=d}
using the (non-Abelian) level
d
inequality (Theorem
6.12
) and bounding the norm of the operator
T_{f}
when restricted to functions of (non-Abelian) level
d
.
The latter bound is stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.15
.

There exists
c>0
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any prime power
q
, the following holds.
Let
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be a global set with
\mu(A)>q^{-c^{2}n^{2}}
,
f=\frac{1_{A}}{\mu(A)}
and
1\leq d\leq n
.
Then for every
h\in L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}
,

\|T_{f}h\|_{2}\leq q^{-cdn}\|h\|_{2}.

A key observation for the proof of Theorem
1.15
is when restricted to functions of level
d
, the operator
T_{f}
is equal to the opertor
T_{f^{=d}}
which applies convolution with the
d
-level part of
f
.
The bound on the norm of this operator is then obtained by applying two inequalities:
The first is the level
d
inequality, again, that bounds the norm of
f^{=d}
for small values of
d
, and the other is a result of Guralnick, Larsen, and Tiep
[
GLT20
]
, which gives a lower bound showing that every subrepresentation of
V_{=d}=L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}
has dimension
q^{\Theta(nd)}
.
The lower bound on the dimension is useful to bound the norm of
T_{f^{=d}}
for larger values of
d
.

Let us explain in more detail how Theorem
1.15
is obtained, following a similar framework as is used by Keevash, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
KLM22
]
.
In order to show that
f*g
is close to
1
in
L_{2}
norm let us write

\|T_{f}\|_{V_{=d}}=\sup_{0\neq h\in L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}}\frac{\|T_{f}h\|_{2}}{\|h\|_{2}}.

In other words
\|T_{f}\|_{V_{=d}}
is the operator norm of the restriction of
T_{f}
to
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}
.
As mentioned above,
\|T_{f}\|_{V_{=d}}=\|T_{f_{=d}}\|_{V_{=d}}
, and thus it is easy to see that
\|T_{f}\|_{V_{=d}}^{2}
is equal to the maximal eigenvalue of the self adjoint operator
S:=T_{f_{=d}}^{*}T_{f_{=d}}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}
.
On the other hand, it turns out that the trace of
S
is equal to the
L^{2}
-norm of
f_{=d}
.
Moreover, since the operator
S
commutes with the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
from the right its eigenspaces are subrepresentations of
L^{2}(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))_{=d}
and therefore the maximal eigenvalue of
S
can be upper bounded by the ratio between its trace, namely
\|f_{=d}\|_{2}^{2}
, and the minimal dimension of a irreducible representation of tensor rank
d
.
Combining this with the level
d
inequality for
f
and the dimension lower bound for tensor rank
d
irreducible representations yields an upper bound on
\|T_{f}\|_{V_{=d}}
.
We note that the idea of combining the trace method with representation theoretic data appeared first in the works of Sarnak and Xue
[
SX91
]
.

1.9.
Relations with previous works

This paper can be considered as a continuation of a recent line of works that extend results about Boolean valued functions over the Boolean cube to non abelian settings.
The first such result of this kind, as far as is known to the authors, is that of Ellis, Filmus and Friedgut
[
EFF15
]
, who studied stability versions of bounds by Ellis, Friedgut and Pilpel
[
EFP11
]
on intersecting families of permutations.

This line of study received further motivation from computer science, specifically from the study of the so-called
2
to
2
conjecture
[
DKK+18
,
KMS18
]
.
These works focused on function over
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
They defined a notion of pseudornadomness, which is analogous to our notion of globalness, and showed that the 4-norm of global sets is small compared to their 2-norm.
The original proof that appears
[
KMS18
]
is a breakthrough, but it is complicated and quantitatively far from optimal.

Keevash, Long, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
KLLM21b
]
then showed how to deduce level
d
inequalities for global functions from a Theorem called ‘hypercontractivity for global functions’ in the setting of the
p
-biased cube. One of their ideas is to use iterated Laplacians and derivatives to measure the globalness of a functions in an analytic way.
Ellis, Kindler, and Lifshitz
[
EKL22
]
then imported this idea and applied it to the bilinear scheme.
They defined analogue notions of Laplacians and derivatives, and used these to give a much simpler proof of the level
d
-inequality for global sets of Khot, Minzer and Safra
[
KMS18
]
.
Moreover, their proof has two advatages that are crucial for the applications of this paper:
Their result is quantitatively sharp, and their notions of Laplacians and derivatives can be used to obtain a Bonami type inequality for global sets from
L_{2}
to
L_{2^{i}}
for any
i
, unlike the earlier more direct approach that seems to only work when
i=2
.

The ideas of reducing Bonami type inequalities in the non-Abelian setting from the Abelian setting is due to Filmus, Kindler, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
FKLM20
]
.
In Ellis, Kindler, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
EKLM24
]
this idea was used for proving a hypercontractive estimate for all compact Lie group of sufficiently high rank.

Future work

We hope that our results find future applications.
Indeed, the preprint of our work was already found useful for applications in extremal combinatorics.
Kelman, Lindzey, and Sheinfeld
[
KLS

applied our Bonami type Theorem

to obtain new bound for Erdős–Ko–Rado type theorems for matrices.
It is worth mentioning that a weaker variant of the Bogolyubov lemma appeared in the Helfgott–Seress
[
HS14

and perhaps Theorem

will play a similar role in the future for the analogue problem for
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

2.1.
Iterated Laplacians and derivatives

The iterated Laplacians in
\mathcal{L}(V,W)
(or simply Laplacians) were defined in the context of product spaces by Keevash, Long, Lifshitz, and Minzer
[
KLLM21a
]
.
They were then extended by
[
EKL22
]
to
\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
The rough idea is as follows.

The discrete derivatives of a function on the Boolean cube
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are given by
D_{i}(f)=\frac{f_{i\to 1}-f_{i\to-1}}{2}
.
One can then form the iterated derivatives by setting
D_{\{i,j\}}(f)=D_{i}D_{j}(f)
and the derivative
D_{S}(f)
is then defined by repeatedly applying the operators
D_{i}
over all
i\in S
.
This notion of a discrete derivative does not extend immediately even to other product spaces, such as
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
.
The idea of Keevash, Lifshitz, Long, and Minzer
[
KLLM21a
]
was to define the derivatives as the restrictions of the Laplacians.
In the case of the Boolean cube a function
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be expanded in terms of its
Fourier
expansion

f=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\chi_{S},

where
\chi_{S}(x)=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
.
The
iterated
Laplacians of
f
are then given by

L_{S}(f)=\sum_{T\supseteq S}f^{=T}.

It was then observed in
[
KLLM21a
]
that the derivatives can be recovered from the Laplacians by plugging in an arbitrary
x\in\{-1,1\}^{S}
in the
S
coordinates and looking at the restricted function
L_{S}(f)_{S\to x}\colon\{-1,1\}^{S^{c}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The function
L_{S}(f)_{S\to x}
is equal to
\chi_{S}(x)D_{S}(f)
. While the notion of discrete derivative does not extend well to other product spaces, the notion of Laplacian is much more flexible and can be defined in various settings.
In
[
KLLM21a
]
Keevash, Lifshtz, Long, and Minzer managed to extend the Laplacians to arbitrary product spaces and defined the derivatives as their restrictions.
In
[
EKL22
]
, Ellis, Kindler and Lifshtz followed a similar route by giving the following definition for the Laplacians and then defining their derivatives as their restrictions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
V_{1}\leq V
,
W_{1}\leq W
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
The
Laplacian

L_{V_{1},W_{1}}
and the
derivative

D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}
are the operators on
L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
, defined for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
by

L_{V_{1},W_{1}}(f):=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}(W,V):\,\mathrm{im}(X)\supseteq V_{1},\,X^{-1}(V_{1})\subseteq W_{1}}\hat{f}(X)u_{X},

D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f):=(L_{V_{1},W_{1}}(f))_{(V_{1},W_{1})\to T}.

Call
i=\dim(V_{1})+\mathrm{codim}(W_{1})
the order of
L_{V_{1},W_{1}}
and
D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}
.
For brevity, we write
D_{V_{1},W_{1}}:=D_{V_{1},W_{1},0}
.

In
[
EKL22
]
the name ‘derivatives of order
i
’ for the operators
D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}
was justified.
They showed that it sends functions of pure degree
d
to functions of pure degree
d-i
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
EKL22
, Lem. 35]

Let
V_{1}\leq V
,
W_{1}\leq W
,
i=\dim(V_{1})+\mathrm{codim}(W_{1})
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
.
Then

D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f^{=d})=\left(D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f)\right)^{=d-i}.

They also showed that derivatives behave well with respect to compositions and the composition of a derivative of order
i
with a derivative of order
j
is a derivative of order
i+j
.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
EKL22
, Prop. 38]

Let
V_{2}\leq V_{1}\leq V
,
W_{1}\leq W_{2}\leq W
,
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
and
S\in\mathcal{L}(V/V_{2},W_{2})
.
Then

D_{V_{1}/V_{2},W_{1},S}\circ D_{V_{2},W_{2},T}=D_{V_{1},W_{1},T+S}.

2.2.
Influences

Recall from Definition
1.5
that we say that
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global if
\|f_{(V_{1},W_{1})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon
for each
V_{1}\leq V
and
W_{1}\leq W
with
\dim(V_{1})+\text{codim}(W_{1})=d
, and each
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
V_{1}\leq V
,
W_{1}\leq W
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
The
influence of
(V_{1},W_{1})
at
T
, is the functional on
L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
, defined for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
by

I_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f):=\|\,D_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f)\|_{2}^{2}.

We say that
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
has
(d,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences
if
I_{V_{1},W_{1},T}(f)\leq\epsilon
for each
V_{1}\leq V
and
W_{1}\leq W
with
\dim(V_{1})+\mathrm{codim}(W_{1})\leq d
, and each
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.

Ellis, Kindler, and Lifshitz
[
EKL22
]
showed that globalness implies that
f^{=d}
has small
generalized influences.

Proposition 2.5
.

[
EKL22
, Prop. 63]

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon>0
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
.
If
f
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global, then
f^{=d}
has
(d,q^{10d^{2}}\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.

They then proved the following hypercontractive inequality.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
EKL22
, Cor. 65]

Suppose that
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
is a function of degree at most
d
that has
(d,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.
Then

\|f\|_{4}^{4}\leq q^{103d^{2}}\epsilon\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

2.3.
The averaging operator
\mathcal{E}_{v}

In the Boolean cube, the Laplacian
L_{i}(f)
has a combinatorial interpretation.
Let
E_{i}(f)(x)
be the expectation of
f(x^{\prime})
, where
x^{\prime}
is obtained from
x
by resampling its
i
th coordinate from
\{-1,1\}
uniformly at random.
Then
L_{i}(f)=f-E_{i}(f)
.
There is no completely straightforward way to generalize the averaging operator
E_{i}(f)
.
Indeed, given a linear map
A
, one cannot simply change its value on a vector
v
without affecting its values on other vectors.
A possible attempt to generalize the Laplacian is to complete
v
to a basis
v=v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
of
V
, leaving the value of
v_{i}
as it is for all
i\geq 2
, while resampling the value of
v
.
The problem with this approach is that different choices of the vectors
v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
yield different operators.

[
EKL22
]
gave the following combinatorial version of the Laplacian by setting it to be the average of all such operators.

Definition 2.7
.

Given a subspace
V^{\prime}\leq V
, we define the linear operator
\mathfrak{e}_{V/V^{\prime}}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
by

(\mathfrak{e}_{V/V^{\prime}}(f))(A):=\underset{B\in\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W)}{\mathbb{E}}f(A+B)\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{L}(V,W),

where the expectation is (as the notation suggests) over a uniform random element of
\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W)
.

Given
0\neq v\in V
, we define the linear operator
\mathcal{E}_{v}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
by

\mathcal{E}_{v}(f):=\underset{V^{\prime}\notni v}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathfrak{e}_{V/V^{\prime}}(f)],

where the expectation is over a uniformly random subspace
v\notin V^{\prime}\subseteq V
of codimension one.

Given
0\neq v\in V
, we define the
combinatorial Laplacian

\mathfrak{L}_{v}\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
by

\mathfrak{L}_{v}(f):=f-\mathcal{E}_{v}(f)\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W)).

If
U
is the one-dimensional subspace spanned by
v
, then we may write
\mathcal{E}_{U}
and
\mathfrak{L}_{U}
instead of
\mathcal{E}_{v}
and
\mathfrak{L}_{v}
, respectively.
(The operator
\mathcal{E}_{U}
is easily seen to be independent of the choice of the generator
v
.)

We note that the combinatorial Laplacian
\mathfrak{L}_{v}
is the Laplacian of the Markov chain on
\mathcal{L}(V,W)
where at each step, we replace a matrix
A
with
A+B
, where
B
is a uniform random element of
\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W)
and
V^{\prime}
is a uniform random codimension-one subspace of
V
that does not contain
v
(the random choices being independent of all previous steps).
The following formulas for the Fourier expansion were obtained by
[
EKL22
]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

[
EKL22
, Lem. 42]

For any
X\in\mathcal{L}(W,V)
, we have

\mathfrak{e}_{V/V^{\prime}}(f)=\sum_{X:\,\mathrm{Im}(X)\subseteq V^{\prime}}\hat{f}(X)u_{X}.

By averaging the above, they obtained the following result.

Lemma 2.9
.

[
EKL22
, Lem. 43]

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(W,V))
and
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{v}(f)=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}(W,V):\ v\notin\mathrm{Im}(X)}q^{-\mathrm{rank}(X)}\hat{f}(X)u_{X}.

2.4.
The dual operators
\mathcal{E}_{W^{\prime}}

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
, define
f^{*}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V^{*},W^{*}))
by
f^{*}(A)=f(A^{*})
for each
A\in\mathcal{L}(W^{*},V^{*})
.
All of the above notions for
f^{*}
correspond to dual notions for the function
f
.

Given a subspace
W^{\prime}\leq W
of codimension
1
, we define the linear operator

\mathcal{E}_{W^{\prime}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))

as follows.
We let
\varphi\in W^{*}
with
\varphi\neq 0
and
\varphi\left(W^{\prime}\right)=0
, and set

\mathcal{E}_{W^{\prime}}(f)=(\mathcal{E}_{\varphi}[f^{*}])^{*}\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W)).

Dually to Lemma
2.9
, we obtain

Lemma 2.10
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
and any codimension-one subspace
W^{\prime}
of
W
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{W^{\prime}}(f)=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}(W,V):\,\mathrm{Ker}(X)+W^{\prime}=W}q^{-\mathrm{rank}(X)}\hat{f}(X)u_{X}.

Combinatorial interpretation for the Laplacian of functions of pure degree

While the Laplacian does not have a nice combinatorial interpretation in terms of averaging operators for general functions, it does have one when

Lemma 2.11

Lemma 2.12

3.
Small generalized influences imply globalness

Proposition
2.5
above, which was proved in
[
EKL22
]
, shows that globalness implies small generalized influences.
In this section we show that the converse also holds, namely that if a function
f
of degree
d
has
(d,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences, it must also be
(r,\epsilon^{\prime})
-global for some
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon^{\prime}(r,d,\epsilon)
(see Proposition
3.6
).

Our idea is to argue inductively that each derivate of
f
is global, and then to apply Lemma
2.9
to express the restriction of
f
as a linear combination of a restriction of a derivative of
f
and a restriction of
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)
.
We then argue via Jensen’s inequality that the corresponding restriction of
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)
is small by induction on
r
. For that purpose we need some observations about the averaging operator, which we make below.

Definition 3.1
.

For
v\in V
, we write
\mathcal{B}_{v}\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
for the uniform distribution over
w\otimes\varphi
, where
w,\varphi
are chosen independently and
w
is uniformly random in
W
and
\varphi
is uniformly random among the functionals in
V^{*}
that send
v
to 1.
Here we use the identification between
W\otimes V^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}(W,V)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
and
A\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
Then

\mathcal{E}_{v}(f)(A)=\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\mathcal{B}_{v}}[f(A+B)].

Proof.

We wish to show that the following two distributions are the same.
One is
\mathcal{B}_{v}
and the other distribution is obtained by choosing a random
V^{\prime}
with
V^{\prime}+\mathrm{Span}(v)=V
, and then setting
B:V\to W
by letting
B(V^{\prime})=0
, and defining
Bv
to be a uniformly random vector
w\in W
.
Indeed, if in the process for choosing
B
we let
\varphi
be the functional sending
V^{\prime}
to 0 and
v
to
1
and use the same
w
, then
B=\varphi\otimes w
.
This completes the proof as
\varphi
is in bijection with its kernel
V^{\prime}
.
∎

We will need to understand the distribution of
A+B,
where
A\sim\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W^{\prime})
and
B\sim\mathcal{B}_{v}
for
v\in V^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
v\in V^{\prime}
,
A\sim\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W^{\prime})
and
w\otimes\varphi\sim\mathcal{B}_{v}.
Condition on the kernel
V^{\prime\prime}
of
\varphi|_{V^{\prime}}
and on
W^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}+\mathrm{Span}\{w\}
.
Then under the conditioning on
V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}
the matrix
A+\varphi\otimes w
is uniformly distributed in
\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime})
in the case where
W^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}
, and the conditioning where
W^{\prime\prime}\neq W^{\prime}
it is uniformly distributed inside the set of all maps in
\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime})
that send
v
outside of
W^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Condition on
V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}
.
We consider first the case where
W^{\prime\prime}\neq W^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
be an ordered basis for
V/V^{\prime\prime}
containing
v
as its first vector.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be an order basis of
W^{\prime\prime}
containing a basis of
W^{\prime}
as its last vectors.
When writing
A
as a matrix with respect to the bases
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
we get a matrix of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&\tilde{A}\end{array}\right)

whose first row and column is zero and
\tilde{A}
is a uniformly random matrix.
Now
B=w\otimes\varphi
and the conditioning implies that
w
is uniformly random on
W^{\prime\prime}\setminus W^{\prime}
and
\varphi
is a uniformly random functional that sends
v
to
1
.
With respect to our bases we obtain that
w
is a random vector under the conditioning
w_{1}\neq 0
and
v
is a random vector under the conditioning
v_{1}=1
.
This easily implies that the first row and column of their tensor product are uniformly random under the conditioning that
(A+B)_{11}=v_{1}w_{1}\neq 0
.
This completes the proof of this case as the condition
a_{11}\neq 0
is equivalent to
(A+B)v\notin W^{\prime}
.
In the case where
W^{\prime\prime}=W^{\prime}
we can define a basis
\mathcal{B}_{1}
similarly and we obtain that
A
is random on
\mathcal{B}_{1}\setminus\{v\}
and sends
v
to 0, while
B
, which is independent of
A
, sends
v
to a uniformly random vector in
W^{\prime}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
Let
U
be either a 1-dimensional subspace of
V
or a subspace of
W
of codimension 1, and let
f
be of degree
d
.
Suppose that
f
is
(r,\epsilon)
-global, then
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{U\to T}
is
(r,2\epsilon)
-global.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
U\leq V
(Otherwise if
U\leq W
we can view
f
as a function on
\mathcal{L}(W^{*},V^{*})
).
Let
V^{\prime}\leq V,W^{\prime}\leq W
be with
V^{\prime}\geq U
and such that
r=\dim(V^{\prime}/U)+\mathrm{codim}(W^{\prime}

(V^{\prime}/U,W^{\prime})
. Using translation, we may assume that
T=0
, and thus upper bound the
2
-norm of
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})\to S}
only when
S=0
.
We now apply Cauchy–Schwarz to have

\mathbb{E}_{A\in\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime},W^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\mathcal{B}}^{2}[f(A+B)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{A,B}[f(A+B)^{2}].

Let
V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}
be as in Lemma
3.3
.
Then when conditioning on
V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}
we obtain that either
A+B
is uniformly distributed in
\mathcal{L}(V/V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime})
or
A+B
is uniformly distributed in a subset of density
1-\frac{1}{q}
of all elements sending
v
to
W^{\prime\prime}\setminus W^{\prime}
.
In the former case we have

\mathbb{E}[f(A+B)^{2}|V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}]=\|f_{(V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime})\to 0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon.

In the latter case we have

\mathbb{E}[f(A+B)^{2}|V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}]=\frac{q}{q-1}\|1_{Av\notin W^{\prime}}f_{(V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime})\to 0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{q}{q-1}\epsilon.

The Theorem follows by averaging over
V^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f
be a function of pure degree
d
.
Suppose that
D_{U,T}(f)
is
(r-1,\epsilon_{1})
-global for all
U\subseteq V
of dimension 1 and for all
U\subseteq W
of codimension 1.
Suppose additionally that
f
is
(r-1,\epsilon_{2})
-global.
Then
f
is
(r,2\epsilon_{1}+4\cdot q^{2d}\epsilon_{2})
-global.

Proof.

Let
V^{\prime}\leq V
and
W^{\prime}\leq W
be such that
r=\dim(V^{\prime})+\mathrm{codim}(W^{\prime})
, and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(V,W)
.
We show that
\|f_{(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}q^{10dr}\epsilon
.
The case where
r=0
follows from the fact that
f
is the only
0
derivative of
f
and the only
0
-restriction of
f
.
It therefore remains to consider the case where either
V^{\prime}\neq 0
or
W^{\prime}\neq W
.
We suppose without loss of generality that
V^{\prime}\neq 0
.
(Otherwise we can switch to the dual function on
\mathcal{L}(W^{*},V^{*})
).
Let
U\leq V^{\prime}
be of dimension 1.
By Lemma
2.11
, we may write

f=L_{U}(f)+q^{d}\mathcal{E}_{U}f.

We now upper bound

\|f_{(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}\leq\|(L_{U}(f))_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}+q^{d}\|\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2},

which yields

(3.1)

\|f_{(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|(L_{U}(f))_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}+2\cdot q^{2d}\|\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}.

The first
r
-restriction above,
(L_{U}(f))_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}
, is an
(r-1)
-restriction of
D_{U,T}
.
This implies that

\|(L_{U}(f))_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{1}.

The second
r
-restriction
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}
is an
(r-1)
restriction of
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{U\to T}
.
By Lemma
3.4
, the function
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{U\to T}
is
(r-1,2\epsilon_{2})
-global.
This shows that

\|\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)_{(V^{\prime},U^{\prime})\to T}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\epsilon_{2}.

Plugging our upper bounds in (
3.1
) completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that
f
is of degree
d
and has
(d,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences, then it is
(r,q^{10dr}\epsilon)
-global for any
r\geq d
.

Proof.

Our proof is by nested induction.
The primary assumption is on
d
, and simultaneously for all
r
.
The inner induction is on
r
, and is applied when
d
is viewed as fixed.
As the base of the induction, we note that the lemma is trivial when either
r
or
d
is
0
.

By Lemma
2.2
for each
i\leq d
the function
f^{=i}
has
(d,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences, and therefore also
(i,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.
We then get by the outer inductive hypothesis that for all
i<d
the function
f^{=i}
is
(r,q^{10ri}\epsilon)
-global.
Below we show that
f^{=d}
is
(r,\epsilon_{d})
-global for
\epsilon_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{4}q^{10rd}\epsilon
.
This will allow us to use the fact that each restriction of
f
is the sum of the corresponding restrictions of the pure degree parts
f^{=i}
.
This in turn will allow us to apply the triangle inequality to obtain that
f
is
(r,\epsilon^{\prime})
-global for

\epsilon^{\prime}:=\left(\sqrt{\epsilon}+\sqrt{q^{10r}\epsilon}+\ldots+\sqrt{q^{10r(d-1)}\epsilon}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{d}}\right)^{2}\leq 2\epsilon_{d}+4q^{10r(d-1)}\epsilon\leq 2\epsilon_{d}+\frac{1}{2}q^{10rd}\epsilon.

Hence, once we show that the function
f^{=d}
is
(r,\epsilon_{d})
-global our proof will be completed.
For simplicity of notation we now assume that
f
is of pure degree
d
namely
f=f^{=d}
) that has
(r,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences and show that it is
(r,\epsilon_{d})
-global.

By the inner induction hypothesis, the function
f
is
(r-1,q^{10d(r-1)}\epsilon)
-global.
Moreover the function
D_{U,T}(f)
has
(d-1,\epsilon)
-small generalized influences as each derivative of
D_{U,T}(f)
is also a derivative of
f
by Proposition
2.3
.
This allows us to apply the outer induction hypothesis for
D_{U,T}(f)
and obtain that
D_{U,T}(f)
is
(r-1,q^{10(r-1)(d-1)}\epsilon)
-global.

We therefore obtain by Lemma
3.5
that
f
is
(r,\epsilon^{\prime\prime})
-global for

\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=2\cdot q^{10(r-1)(d-1)}\epsilon+4\cdot q^{2d}\cdot q^{10d(r-1)}\epsilon.

This completes the proof as

\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\leq\frac{1}{4}q^{10dr}\epsilon=\epsilon_{d}.

∎

4.
Bonami type inequalities

In this section our goal is to prove Theorem
1.13
.
We first use our Bonami-type lemma to show that if a function
f
of degree
d
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global, then its square
f^{2}
is
(2d,\epsilon^{\prime})
-global for an appropriate value of
\epsilon^{\prime}
.
This allows us to iteratively use the
4
vs.
2
Bonami type inequalities from Corollary
2.6
to upper bound the
\ell
-norm of a
d
-degree function
f
, by inductively upper bounding the
\ell/2
-norm of
f^{2}
.
Equipped with this
\ell
-norm Bonami type inequality, we then obtain a level
d
inequality that bounds the level
d
weight of Boolean valued functions.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global of degree
d
, then
f^{2}
is
(2d,q^{144d^{2}}\epsilon^{2})
-global.

Proof.

If
f
is a
(d,\epsilon)
-global function, then by Proposition
2.5
each
f^{=i}
has
(d,q^{10i^{2}}\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.
By Lemma
2.2
we obtain that
f
has
(d,\sum_{i=0}^{d}q^{10i^{2}}\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.
This shows that
f
has
(d,q^{11d^{2}}\epsilon)
-small generalized influences.
By Proposition
3.6
the function
f
is
(3d,q^{41d^{2}}\epsilon)
-global.
This implies that for each
i\leq 2d
each
i
-restriction of
f
is a
(d,q^{41d^{2}}\epsilon)
-global function of degree
\leq d
.
By Theorem
2.6
we obtain that for each
i
-restriction of
f
the fourth power of its
4
-norm is at most
q^{144d^{2}}\epsilon^{2}
, where we upper bounded the square of the 2-norm of the
i
-restriction by
\epsilon
.
This shows that
f^{2}
is
(2d,q^{144d^{2}}\epsilon^{2}
)-global.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Recall that the theorem states that for every
\ell
a power of 2 and a
(d,\epsilon)
-global function
f
of degree
d
we have

\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}.

We prove the statement by induction on
\log_{2}(\ell)
.
For
g=f^{2},
Lemma
4.1
implies that
g
is
(2d,q^{144d^{2}}\epsilon^{2})
-global.
By the induction hypothesis

\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}=\|g\|_{\ell/2}^{\ell/2}\leq q^{50d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|g\|_{2}^{2}(q^{144d^{2}}\epsilon^{2})^{\ell/4-1}\leq q^{86d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|g\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-2}\leq q^{86d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{4}^{4}\epsilon^{\ell/2-2}.

We now apply Theorem
2.6
to have

\|f\|_{4}^{4}\leq q^{103d^{2}}\epsilon\|f\|_{2}^{2},

which complete the proof.
∎

Theorem
1.13
yields the following upper bound on the level
d
weight of general and Boolean functions.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
such that
f^{=d}
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global.
Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of
2
and let
\ell^{\prime}=\frac{1}{1-1/\ell}
be its Hölder conjugate.
Then

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{300d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{2\ell-2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

In particular for
f\colon\mathcal{L}(V,W)\to\{0,1\}
, since
\mathbb{E}[f]=\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}
, we get

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{300d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{2\ell-2}}\mathbb{E}[f].

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}=\langle f^{=d},f\rangle\leq\|f^{=d}\|_{\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}.

We can now apply Theorem
1.13
to obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{\ell}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2/\ell}.

Combining the inequalities, we obtain

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}.

Hence, after Rearranging

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2/\ell^{\prime}}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}.

Raising everyhting to the power
\ell^{\prime}
we obtain

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{300d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{2\ell-2}}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}},

as
200\ell^{\prime}\leq 4/3\cdot 200\leq 300,
and
\ell^{\prime}=\frac{\ell}{\ell-1}.

∎

5.
Level inequalities

In this section we prove Theorem
1.14
and give a slightly more analytic version of it for functions that are not necessarily Boolean (see Theorem
5.6
below).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
, and assume that
f^{=d}
has
(d,\beta\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2})
-small generalized influences.
Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of
2
,
\ell^{\prime}
its Hölder conjugate, and
\beta\geq 1
.
Then

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{420d^{2}\ell}\beta^{1-2/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}.

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality we have

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}=\langle f^{=d},f\rangle\leq\|f^{=d}\|_{\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}.

By Proposition
3.6
the function
f^{=d}
is
(d,\beta q^{10d^{2}}\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2})
-global.
By Theorem
1.13
we obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{\ell}\leq q^{200d^{2}\ell}\left(\beta q^{10d^{2}}\right)^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f^{=d}\|_{2}.

Hence,

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{210d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}\beta^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f^{=d}\|_{2}.

Rearranging yields

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}\leq q^{210d^{2}\ell}\beta^{1/2-1/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}.

The lemma now follows by squaring.
∎

Recall from the introduction that
\zeta
is a sufficiently small absolute constant.

Definition 5.2
.

For
\ell^{\prime}\geq 1
, say that

(r,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-

-restriction of

We say that

is

-

if it is
(r,q^{\zeta rn}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}})
-global for all

.

Note that this is slightly inconcistent with definition

, as for the case
\ell^{\prime}=2
the norm is squared. So a function is
(d,\epsilon)
-global if and only if it is
(d,\epsilon^{1/2},L^{2})
-global.
However working with the
\ell^{\prime}
-power of the norm for

is inconvenient.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
.
If
f
is
(r,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global, then
\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)
is
(r,2\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global.

Proof.

The same proof of Lemma
3.4
works for general
\ell^{\prime}
-norms.
∎

Recall the operator
\mathcal{T}_{d,U}(f)
from Lemma
2.12
.
Let us show that it preserves globality.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
, and let
U
and
\mathcal{T}_{d,U}(f)
as in Lemma
2.12
.
If
f
is
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global, then
\mathcal{T}_{d,U}(f)
is
(d,q^{3d}\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mathcal{T}_{d,U}f=f-q^{d-1}\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)-q^{d}\mathcal{E}_{U}(f)+q^{2d-1}\mathcal{E}_{U}^{2}(f),
Lemma
5.3
and the triangle inequality.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of
2
and
\ell^{\prime}
its Hölder conjugate.
Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))
be
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global, and set

\epsilon^{\prime}=q^{500d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}
.
Then the function
f^{=d}
has
(d,\epsilon^{\prime})
-small generalized influences.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
d
. The statement is trivial for

, and by Lemma
5.4
, the function
\mathcal{T}_{d,U}(f)
is
(d,q^{3d}\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global. This implies that the function
(\mathcal{T}_{d,U}(f))_{U\to T}
is
(d-1,q^{3d}\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global. We can now apply the induction hypothesis and Lemma

to obtain that

has
(d-1,q^{6d}q^{500(d-1)^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2})
-small generalized influences. Let
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\max(q^{6d+500(d-1)^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2},\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}).
Then the function
f^{=d}
has
(d,\epsilon^{\prime\prime})
-small generalized influences. Indeed,
\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{\prime\prime}
and all the

-derivatives of

are

-derivatives of

-derivatives of

by Proposition

, so the desired upper bound on the

-norm of the

-derivatives. Now if
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=q^{6d+500(d-1)^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}
, then
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\leq q^{500d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}
and then we are done. Otherwise,

we may now apply Lemma

to obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{420d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}.

This completes the proof as the

-globalness of

implies that
\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}\leq\epsilon,
which in turn implies that
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\leq q^{420d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}\leq q^{500d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}.

∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of 2 and let
\ell^{\prime}
be its Hölder conjugate.
Then for all
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global functions

we have

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{460d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.5
the function
f^{=d}
has
q^{500d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}
-small generalized influences. By Lemma
5.1
we then obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{420d^{2}\ell}\left(\frac{q^{500d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2}}{\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1-2/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}.

Rearranging, we obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{4-4/\ell}\leq q^{(420+500(1-2/\ell))d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{2-4/\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{2}.

Rearranging, and raising everything to the power
\ell^{\prime}/2=\frac{1}{2-2/\ell}
we obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{\frac{420+500(1-2/\ell)}{2-2/\ell}d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\leq q^{460d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.14
.

Suppose that
f\colon\mathcal{L}(V,W)\to\{0,1\}
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global. Then it is
(d,\epsilon^{1/\ell^{\prime}},L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global. The statement now follows from Theorem
5.6
while plugging in
\epsilon^{1/\ell^{\prime}}=\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}},\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}[f]
to obtain that

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{460d^{2}\ell}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell}}\mathbb{E}[f].

∎

The following is an immediate corollary.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
f\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,W))\to\{0,1\}
be
(d,\epsilon)
-global.
Let
t>0
be such that
\epsilon\geq q^{-t^{2}}
.
Then

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{922dt}\epsilon\mathbb{E}[f],

Proof.

We may upper bound

\|f^{=d}\|_{2}\leq\|f^{\leq d}\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|f|]}.

This shows that the corollary holds trivally when
d>t/10
. Suppose otherwise,
noting that
t\leq n
we obtain that
d<n/10
, and let
\ell=2^{\lceil\log_{2}(t/d)\rceil}
.
Note that the proof of Theorem
1.14
follows through when the hypothesis that
f
takes values in
\{0,1\}

The corollary now follows by plugging in the value of
\ell
in Theorem
1.14
while noting that
\epsilon^{-2/\ell}\leq q^{2dt}
and
q^{460d^{2}\ell}\leq q^{920dt}
.
∎

6.
Levels on
\mathrm{SL}(V)
,
\mathrm{GL}(V)
and
\mathcal{L}(V,V)

In this section we Prove Lemma
1.12
. We then deduce from the lemma hypercontractive and level inequalities in the nonabelian setting from the ones in the abelian setting.

Throughout this section we set
G
to be either
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{SL}(V)
or
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{GL}(V)
, where
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
Since
G\subset\mathcal{L}(V,V)
, we have the following two
G\times G
-equivariant linear maps

i\colon L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,V))\to L^{2}(G),\qquad i(f)=f|_{G},

j\colon L^{2}(G)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,V)),\qquad j(f)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x)&x\in G\\
0&x\not\in G\end{array}\right..

Definition 6.1

(Globalness for functions over
G
)
.

We say that
f\in L^{2}(G)
is
(r,\epsilon)
-global (resp.
(r,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global) if
j(f)
is
(r,\epsilon)
-global (resp.
(r,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global) as in Definition
1.5
(resp. Definition
5.2
).

In
[
GH21
]
, Gurevich and Howe introduced the following notions:
Let
\omega
be the permutation representation of
G
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
, given by
\omega(g)f(x)=f(g^{-1}x)
for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
and
g,x\in G
, and let
\omega^{\otimes d}
be the
d
-fold tensor product of
\omega
, for any
0\leq d\leq n
.
Let
\rho
be irreducible representation of
G
.
By
[
GH21
, Def. 1.2.2]
, we say that
\rho
is of
strict tensor rank

d
, if it is a subrepresentation of
\omega^{\otimes d}
, but not of
\omega^{\otimes(d-1)}
.

[
GH21
, Def. 1.2.3 and 3.1.1]
, we say that
\rho
is of
tensor rank

d
, if it can be written as
\rho^{\prime}\otimes\chi
for
\rho^{\prime}
of strict tensor rank
d
and a multiplicative character
\chi
of
G
, i.e. a complex group homomorphism on
G
.
By
[
GH21
, Rem. 3.1.2]
, for
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
the notions of strict tensor rank and tensor rank coincide.

Recall from the introduction that the level of a function
f
on
\mathrm{SL}(V)
is the minimal
d
for which
f
is spanned by the matrix coeffecients of representations of tensor rank
\leq d
. For
\mathrm{GL}(V)
we set the level of a function in a similar fashion. We also set the
strict level
of a function
f
on
G
to be the minimal
d
for which
f
is a linear combination of matrix coefficients of representations of strict tensor rank
\leq d.
We set
L^{2}(G)_{\leq d}
be the space of complex valued functions on
G
of level
\leq d
. Finally, we write
L^{2}(G)_{=d}
for the space of functions of level
\leq d
on
G
that are orthogonal to all the functions of degree
\leq d-1
with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product given by
\langle f,g\rangle:=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim G}[f(A)\overline{g}(A)].
We write
f_{\leq d}
for the projection of
f
onto
L^{2}(G)_{\leq d}
and
f_{=d}
for the projection of
f
onto
L^{2}(G)_{=d}.

The following proposition is due to Gurevich and Howe.

Proposition 6.2

(
[
GH20
, Proposition 9.1.3]
)
.

Let
H\leq G
be the subgroup of matrices fixing a subspace of dimension
d
.
Then an irreducible representation of
G
is of strict tensor rank
d
if and only if it contains a non-zero
H
-invariant vector.

Functions on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
that depend on
d
-coordinates are termed
d
-
juntas
in the Boolean functions jargon. The following is an analogue notion for functions on
G
.

Definition 6.3

(Juntas over
G
)
.

We say that
f\in L^{2}(G)
is a
d
-
junta
if there exist a pair
(U,g)
, of a subspace
U\leq V
of dimension
d
and a complex valued function
g
on
\{A\in\mathcal{L}(U,V)\,:\,\mathrm{rank}(A)=d\}
, such that
f(A)=g(A|_{U})
.

We now show that
H
-invariance for a function can be equivalently stated as the condition that
f
is a junta.

Lemma 6.4
.

A function in
L^{2}(G)
is a
d
-junta if and only if there exists
U\leq V
of dimension
d
, such that
f
is invariant under the right action of
H=\{A\in G\,|\,A|_{U}=I_{U}\}
, the subgroup of matrices fixing
U
.

Proof.

If
f
is a
d
-junta, let
(U,g)
be as Definition
6.3
, then for any
h\in H
we get that for any
A\in G
,

h.f(A)=f(Ah)=g((Ah)|_{U})=g(A|_{U})=f(A),

therefore
f
is
H
-invariant.
If
f
is
H
-invariant, then for
A,B\in G
with
A|_{U}=B|_{U}
, we may choose
h\in H
with
Ah=B
, namely
h=A^{-1}B
and therefore
f(A)=f(B)
.
∎

Recall that for
\rho
an irreducible representation of
G
, we denote by
L^{2}(G)_{\rho}
the space of matrix coefficients of
\rho
, which by the Peter-Weyl Theorem is the isotypic component of
\rho
in
L^{2}(G)
and it is isomorphic to
\rho\otimes\rho^{*}
as a
G\times G
-representation.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\rho
be an irreducible representation of
G
of strict tensor rank
d
.
Then there exists a
d
-junta in the isotypic component of
\rho
. Moreover, every function in the isotypic component of
\rho
is a linear combination of
d
-juntas.

Proof.

First note that since
\omega
is a real representation it is self dual. Hence, the strict tensor rank of
\rho^{*}
is the same as the strict tensor rank of
\rho
.
By Proposition
6.2
,
\rho^{*}
contains a non-zero
H
-invariant vector
v
, where
H\leq G
is the subgroup of matrices fixing a vector space of dimension
d
. This shows that for every
u\in\rho
the vector
u\otimes v
is
H
-invariant with respect to the right-action of
G
. By Lemma
6.4
each such function is a
d
-junta.
Since,
\rho^{*}
is irreducible we get that it is equal to the span of the
G
-translations of
v
, and therefore every function in
\rho\otimes\rho^{*}
is a linear combination of right-translates of
d
-juntas of the form
u\otimes v.

It follows from Lemma
6.4
that the translation of a
d
-junta is again a
d
-junta, which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Recall that
f^{=d}=j(f)^{=d}
and
f^{\leq d}=j(f)^{\leq d}
, for any
d\leq n
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(G)
be a
d
-junta.
Then

\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|f\|_{2}\leq\|f^{\leq d}\|_{2}.

Proof.

Since
f
is a
d
-junta, let
(U,g)
be as in Definition
6.3
.
Define
\tilde{f}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(V,V))
by setting
\tilde{f}(A)=g(A|_{U})
if
\mathrm{rank}(A_{U})=d
, and
\tilde{f}(A)=0
otherwise, and note that
\tilde{f}(A)=f(A)
for any
A\in G
.
Since
j(f)(A)=0
for
A\not\in G
, and
j(f)(A)=\tilde{f}(A)=f(A)
for
A\in G
, we have

\frac{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}{|G|}\langle j(f),\tilde{f}\rangle=\frac{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}{|G|}\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\mathcal{L}(V,V)}j(f)(A)\overline{\tilde{f}(A)}=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim G}j(f)(A)\overline{\tilde{f}(A)}=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim G}|f(A)|^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

We also have

\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\Pr_{A\sim\mathcal{L}(V,V)}[\mathrm{rank}(A|_{U})=\dim(U)]\leq\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\tilde{f}
is a
d
-junta it is of degree
d
and we have

\langle j(f),\tilde{f}\rangle=\langle f^{\leq d},\tilde{f}\rangle
. Combining all of the above, together with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get

\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\langle j(f),\tilde{f}\rangle=\langle f^{\leq d},\tilde{f}\rangle\leq\|f^{\leq d}\|_{2}\|\tilde{f}\|_{2}\leq\|f^{\leq d}\|_{2}\|f\|_{2}.

After dividing by
\|f\|_{2}
we get the claim.
∎

We now introduce the operator

T_{d}\colon L^{2}(G)\to L^{2}(G),\qquad T_{d}(f)=i(j(f)^{\leq d}).

It is easy to observe that the operator
T_{d}
is a
G\times G
-morphism. We also note that the adjoint operator
j^{*}
is equal to
\frac{|G|}{|L(V,V)|}i
and therefore the operator
T_{d}
is self adjoint since it takes the form
\frac{|G|}{|L(V,V)|}j^{*}P_{d}^{*}P_{d}j
, where
P_{d}\colon L^{2}(V,W)\to L^{2}(V,W)
is the self adjoint operator given by
P_{d}(f)=f^{\leq d}
.
This shows that the isotypic decomposition of
L^{2}(G)
into irreducible
G\times G
-modules of the form
\rho\otimes\rho^{*}
is a refinement of the spectral decomposition of the self adjoint operator
T_{d}
. The following lemma is an operator theoretic version of Lemma
1.12
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(G)
be of strict level
d
.
Then

\|T_{d}f\|_{2}\geq\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|f\|_{2}.

Proof.

Since
T_{d}
commutes with the action of
G\times G
on
L^{2}(G)
and each isotypic space
L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
is an irreducible
G\times G
-representation appearing with multiplicity one inside
L^{2}(G)
, it follows that the eigenspace of
T_{d}
are
G\times G
-invariant and can be decomposed as direct sums of isotypic components. Moreover,
T_{d}
is self adjoint and positive semi-definite (because as mentioned it is a positive multiple of
j^{*}P_{d}P_{d}j
.)

Therefore, in order to prove the lemma it suffices to show the for each
\rho
of strict tensor rank
\leq d
there exists some nonzero function
h_{\rho}\in(L^{2}(G))_{=\rho}
with
\|T_{d}h_{\rho}\|_{2}\geq\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|h_{\rho}\|_{2}
. Indeed, the lemma would then follows by decomposing
f
orthogonally as a sum of eigenfunctions
f_{\rho}\in L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
, where the eigenfunctions
f_{\rho}
correspond to the eigenvalue
\frac{\|T_{d}h_{\rho}\|_{2}}{\|h_{\rho}\|_{2}}.

By Proposition
6.5
there exists an eigenfunction
h_{\rho}\in L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
that is a
d
-junta.
By Proposition
6.6
,

\lambda\|h_{\rho}\|_{2}^{2}=\langle T_{d}h_{\rho},h_{\rho}\rangle=\langle j(h_{\rho})^{\leq d},i^{*}h_{\rho}\rangle=\frac{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}{|G|}\|j(h_{\rho})^{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|h_{\rho}\|_{2}^{2}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
1.12
.

For every
f
we have
\langle T_{d}f,f\rangle=\langle f^{\leq d},i^{*}f\rangle=\frac{|L(V,V)|}{|G|}\|j(f)^{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}.
The statement now follows from Lemma
6.7
.
∎

6.1.
Bonami and level inequalities for strict tensor rank

We may now capitalize on Lemma
6.7
, which is an operator theoretic version of Lemma
1.12
, to deduce Bonami-type lemmas and level-
d
inequalities on
L^{2}(G)
from their
\mathcal{L}(V,V)
variants. We begin with a variant of Bonami’s lemma.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\ell\geq 4
be a power of
2,
and
\rho
be a representation of
G
of strict tensor rank
d
. If a function
f\in L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global, then

\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq q^{1212d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}.

Proof.

Since
T_{d}
is a
G\times G
-morphism, every function
f\in L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
is an eigenfunction of
T_{d}
and by Lemma
6.7
the corresponding eigenvalue is at least
\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}
in absolute value.
We therefore have

\frac{|G|}{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq\|T_{d}f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}=\|i(f^{\leq d})\|_{\ell}^{\ell}=\frac{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}{|G|}\|f^{\leq d}1_{G}\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{L}(V,V)|}{|G|}\|f^{\leq d}\|_{\ell}^{\ell}.

Rearranging yields

\|f\|_{\ell}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}(V,V)}{|G|}\right)^{2/\ell}\|f^{\leq d}\|_{\ell}\leq(4q)^{2/\ell}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\|f^{=i}\|_{\ell}.

By definition
j(f)
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global or equivalently
(d,\sqrt{\epsilon},L^{2})
-global. We may therefore apply Theorem
5.5
to obtain that the function
j(f)^{=i}
has
(i,q^{1000i^{2}}\epsilon)
-small generalized influences for each
i\leq d
. By Proposition
3.6
each such
f^{=i}
is
(i,q^{1010i^{2}}\epsilon)
-global. Therefore, by Theorem
1.13
we have

\|f^{=i}\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq q^{1210i^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f^{=i}\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}\leq q^{1210i^{2}\ell^{2}}\|j(f)\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}\leq q^{1210i^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}.

The statement follows by summing the above upper bounding for
\|f^{=i}\|_{\ell}
over all
i
.
∎

We now move on to deducing our strict level
d
-inequality.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of
2
and
\ell^{\prime}
its Hölder conjugate.
Let
f\in L^{2}(G)
be
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global. Let
g
be the projection of
f
onto the space of functions of strict level
\leq d
.
Then

\|g\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{461d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon.

Proof.

We have

\|T_{d}(f)\|_{2}^{2}=\|i(j(f)^{\leq d})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{|G|}{|L(V,V)|}\|j(f)^{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}.

As
j(f)
is
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global, we may apply Theorem
5.6
to obtain that

\|j(f)^{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\|j(f)^{=k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|j(f)\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{d}q^{460d^{2}\ell}\leq q^{461d^{2}\ell}\frac{|G|}{|L(V,V)|}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}.

Rearranging, yields that

\|T_{d}(f)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{|G|}{|L(V,V)|}\right)^{2}q^{461d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}.

Since
T_{d}
is a
G\times G
-morphism it follows that each space
L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
is
T_{d}
-invariant and therefore
T_{d}g
and
T_{d}(f-g)
are orthogonal, being Fourier supported on a disjoint set of isotypic components. Lemma
6.7
now implies that

\|g\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}(V,V)}{|G|}\right)^{2}\|T_{d}g\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}(V,V)}{|G|}\right)^{2}\|T_{d}f\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{461d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell-1}}.

∎

6.2.
Bonami and level inequalities for tensor rank

For a function
f\in L^{2}(G)
we denote by
f_{\leq d}
the projection of
f
onto the space
L^{2}(G)_{\leq d}
of functions of level
d
. We also write
f_{=d}=f_{\leq d}-f_{\leq d-1}.
We start by quickly deducing our Bonami Lemma with tensor rank replacing strict tensor rank.

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\ell\geq 4
be a power of
2,
and
\rho
be a representation of
G
of tensor rank
d
. If a function
f\in L^{2}(G)_{=\rho}
is
(d,\epsilon)
-global, then

\|f\|_{\ell}^{\ell}\leq q^{1212d^{2}\ell^{2}}\|f\|_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{\ell/2-1}.

Proof.

Each such function
f
can be multiplied by a multiplicative character
\chi,
such that
\chi\cdot f
is of strict level
d
. Since multiplicative characters have absolute value always equal to 1, the statement follows immediately from Theorem
6.8
.
∎

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
\ell\geq 2
be a power of
2
and
\ell^{\prime}
its Hölder conjugate.
Let
f\in L^{2}(G)
be
(d,\epsilon,L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global.
Then

\|f_{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{462d^{2}\ell}\|f\|_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime}}\epsilon.

Proof.

For each multiplicative character
\chi
, let
g_{\chi}
be the projection of
f\cdot\chi
onto the functions of strict level
d
. Then we may upper bound
\|f_{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{\chi}\|g_{\chi}\|_{2}^{2}.
Since multiplicative characters have absolute value 1, they do not affect globalness or norms and therefore the statement follows from Theorem
6.9
in conjunction with the fact that there are at most
q-1
multiplicative characters in
G
.
∎

We may now plug in an optimized value of
\ell
to obtain the following.

Theorem 6.12
.

Let
f\colon G\to\{0,1\}
be
(d,\epsilon)
-global with
\epsilon\geq q^{-t^{2}}
, for
t>0
.
Then

\|f_{\leq d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{926dt}\mathbb{E}[f]\epsilon.

Proof.

We may assume that
d\leq t/10
for otherwise the statement holds trivally as
\|f_{\leq d}\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{E}[f].

Now the function
f
is
(d,\epsilon^{1/\ell^{\prime}},L^{\ell^{\prime}})
-global for every
\ell\geq 1
. We apply Theorem
6.11
with
\ell=2^{\lceil\log_{2}(t/d)\rceil}
to obtain the statement, while noting that
\epsilon^{\frac{\ell-2}{\ell^{\prime}(\ell-1)}}=\epsilon\cdot\epsilon^{-2/\ell}\leq q^{2dt}\epsilon.

∎

We will also make use of the following level
d
-inequalities for global functions.

Theorem 6.13
.

Let
f\colon G\to\{0,1\}
be global with
\mathbb{E}[f]\geq q^{-t^{2}}
, for some
t>0
.
Let
\delta>926\frac{t}{n}+\zeta
.
Then

\|f_{=d}\|_{2}^{2}\leq q^{\delta dn}\mathbb{E}[f]^{2}.

Proof.

Follows immediately from Theorem
6.12
with
\epsilon=q^{\zeta dn}\mathbb{E}[f].

∎

7.
Spectral decomposition of global functions

Let
G
be either
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
or
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
In this section, we decompose the space
V=L^{2}(G)
as an orthogonal direct sum of the
G
-invariant subspaces
V_{=d}
, using the tensor rank notion of Gurevich and Howe
[
GH21
]
.
We give upper bound
\|f*g\|_{2}
whenever
g\in V_{=d}
and
f
is global, and prove Theorem
1.15
.

We recall Definition
1.10
from the introduction and extend it also to
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
Let
V=L^{2}(G)
, and by the Peter-Weyl Theorem,
V=\bigoplus_{\rho}L^{2}(G)_{\rho}
, where
\rho
runs over all irreducible representations of
G
.
For any
d\leq n
, define
V_{=d}=\bigoplus_{\rho}L^{2}(G)_{\rho}
, where
\rho
runs over the irreducible representations of
G
of tensor rank
d
.
Then
V=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{n}V_{d}
is a descomposition of subrepresentations of
G\times G
, where no two summands contain a common factor, hence the decomoposition is orthogonal, and is preserved by convolution from either side.
Denote
V_{\leq d}=\bigoplus_{i\leq d}V_{=i}
, and
V_{<d}
and
V_{>d}
are defined similarly.
For
f\in V
, define by
f_{=i}
and
f_{\leq d}
the projections of
f
onto
V_{=i}
and
V_{\leq d}
, respectively.

The following bound was proved in
[
GLT20
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3]
(see also
[
GH21
, Theorem 2.2.1 and Corollary 3.2.7]
).

Theorem 7.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
, such that for any
q
,
n
and
d\leq n
, every irreducible representation of
G
of tensor rank
d
has dimension at least
q^{c^{\prime}dn}
.

We also make use of the following lemma, which relies on a crucial idea that first appeared in the work of Sarnak and Xue
[
SX91
]
.
They were interested in the operator norm of a self adjoint
G
-endomorphism
T\colon V\to V
, where
V
is a unitary representation of
G
.
They then used representation theory to upper bound its operator norm, which is the same as its maximal eigenvalue.

For

For

Lemma 7.2

For any

-invariant and

Proof.

The subspace

This shows that if

∎

Claim 7.3
.

Let

be a finite group, let

and let

Proof.

For

∎

\nu

\frac{1}{2}\|f*g-1\|_{1}\leq q^{-n/4}.
This shows that
AB
, which is the support of
\nu
, has uniform measure
\geq 1-0.01=0.99
.
∎

Recall that a good umvirate is a set of the form
U=U_{k}^{g,h}=gL_{k}h\subset\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, and in the case where

, then
U
is a good
groumvirate
, as defined in Definition

.

Theorem 9.9
.

There exists absolute constants
c,C>0
, such that the following holds.
Let
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be a set of density
\geq q^{-cn^{2}}
.
Then there exists
k
and a good groumvirate
U_{k}^{g,g^{-1}}
of density
\geq\mu(A)^{C}
, in which
AA^{-1}
has density
\geq 0.99
.

Proof.

Let
t
be the smallest such that the density of
A
is at least
q^{-t^{2}}
.
By Lemma
9.7
, there exists
k<\frac{4t^{2}}{n}
and a good umvirate
U^{g,h}_{k^{\prime}}
where
A
is
q^{\zeta n/2}
-global relative to
U^{g,h}_{k}k
and has density
\geq q^{\zeta nk/4}\cdot\mu(A)\geq\mu(A)
there.
Let
U^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}U^{g,h}_{k}\left(U^{g,h}_{k}\right)^{-1}=U^{g,g^{-1}}
.
Then
U^{\prime}
is a good
k
-groumvirate, and by Corollary
9.8
we have that
AA^{-1}\cap U^{\prime}
has density
0.99
in
U^{\prime}
.
The density of
U^{\prime}
is at least
q^{-2kn}\geq q^{-2t^{2}}\geq\mu(A)^{c}
as desired.
∎

Theorem 9.10

(Bogolyubov Ruzsa analogue)
.

There exists absolute constants
c,C>0
, such that the following holds.
Let
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be of density
\geq q^{-cn^{2}}
.
Then
AA^{-1}AA^{-1}
contains a good groumvirate of density
\geq\mu(A)^{C}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.9
there exists a good groumvirate
U=U_{k}^{g,g^{-1}}
of density
\geq\mu(A)^{C}
, in which
AA^{-1}
has density
\geq 0.99
.
Assume in contradiction that
x\in U\setminus AA^{-1}AA^{-1}
.
Then
AA^{-1}\cap U
and
xA^{-1}A\cap U
are two disjoint sets of density
0.99
inside
U
, which is absurd.
Hence
AA^{-1}AA^{-1}
contains
U
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

9.10
.

c_{1},C_{1}
be the constants

respectively of Theorem

.
Let

Then if

9.3.
Approximate groups

Let
G
be a group.
Recall that a set
A\subseteq G
is said to be a
K
-approximate subgroup if
A=A^{-1}
and there exists a set
X
of size
K
, such that
A^{2}\subseteq X\cdot A
.
In this subsection we show that approximate subgroups are contained in the union of a few cosets of a large good umvirate.
Results of a similar spirit were obtained by Breulard, Green, and Tao
[
BGT11
]
in the case where
n
is
O(1)
.

For
\alpha<\beta
, we say that
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is an
(\alpha,\beta)
-
easy
set if there exists a good groumvirates
U=U_{k}^{g,g^{-1}}
of density
\geq\alpha
, such that
A
is a union of
s
left cosets of
U
for some
s\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
.

Theorem 9.11
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C>0
, such that the following holds.
Let
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
have density
\alpha\geq q^{-cn^{2}}
and
A^{2}A^{-1}AA^{-1}
have density
\leq\beta
.
Then there exists an
(\alpha^{C},\beta)
-easy set
J
, such that
A\subseteq J\subseteq A^{5}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
9.10
there exist a good groumvirate
U
of density
\geq\alpha^{C}
, such that
U\subseteq A^{-1}AA^{-1}A
.
Let
X\subseteq A
be the subset of
A
obtained by choosing a representative of each left coset of
U
that
A
intersects.
Then
A\subseteq XU\subseteq A^{5}
.
Moreover
|XU|=|X||U|
, hence
|X|\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
.
Setting
J=XU
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

The Theorem is an immediate corollary of Theorem
9.11
.
∎

Theorem 9.12
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C>0
, such that the following holds.
Let
A
have density
\alpha\geq q^{-cn^{2}}
.
Suppose that
A
is a
K
-approximate subgroup.
Then
A
is contained in an
(\alpha^{C},K^{4}\alpha)
-easy set.

Proof.

If
A
is a
K
-aproximate subgroup, then
A=A^{-1}
and
|A^{5}|\leq K^{4}|A|
.
The Theorem now follows from Theorem
9.11
.
∎
