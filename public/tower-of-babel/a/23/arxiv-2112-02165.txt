Abstract

We allow time-varying matroid constraints to be placed on the feasible sets. Assuming access to an online regression oracle with regret 𝖱𝖾𝗀 𝗌𝗊 ​ ( ℱ , n ) \mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n) , our algorithm efficiently randomizes around local optima of estimated functions according to the Inverse Gap Weighting strategy [ AL99 , FR20 ] . We show that cumulative regret of this procedure with time horizon n n scales as 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ 𝖱𝖾𝗀 𝗌𝗊 ​ ( ℱ , n ) ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)}) against a benchmark with a multiplicative factor 1 / 2 1/2 . On the other hand, using the techniques of [ FW14 ] we show that an ϵ \epsilon -Greedy procedure with local randomization attains regret of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ​ 𝖱𝖾𝗀 𝗌𝗊 ​ ( ℱ , n ) 1 / 3 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2/3}\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)^{1/3}) against a stronger ( 1 − e − 1 ) (1-e^{-1}) benchmark.

In this short note, we consider the problem of contextual bandits with submodular rewards. On round
t=1,\ldots,n
, the learner observes context
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, chooses (in a randomized fashion) a subset
S_{t}\subseteq[A]\triangleq\{1,\ldots,A\}
of size at most
k
, and observes a random reward
r_{t}
with mean
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})
, for some unknown
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\in\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\textsf{nms}}
. Here
\mathcal{F}_{\textsf{nms}}
is the set of all functions
\boldsymbol{u}:2^{[A]}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
such that
\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,x)
is nonnegative monotone submodular for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and the model class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\textsf{nms}}
is known to the learner. We assume for simplicity that
r_{t}\in[0,1]
almost surely. In addition, we posit time-varying matroid constraints
\mathcal{M}_{t}
on the feasible sets
\mathsf{S}_{t}
, and allow these constraints to be revealed to the decision-maker at the beginning of each round.
The goal of the learner is to minimize (with high probability) the regret

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}c\cdot\max_{\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t}),

(1.1)

the difference between a multiple of cumulative mean rewards of choosing the best subsets
\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}
of size
k
(subject to matroid constraints omitted here for simplicity) and the mean utilities of the subsets chosen by the decision-maker, for a constant
c\leq 1
. We assume that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is an arbitrary sequence of contexts that may be chosen by Nature adaptively. Furthermore, as in
[
FR20
]
, we assume access to an online regression oracle, as specified in the next section.

The setting can naturally model situations such as product selection at a fulfillment center, product search, news recommendation, or advertisement. In product search, there is a diminishing utility of adding another item to the list of responses to the search query, naturally modeled by the notion of submodularity. Similarly, adding an item to a stock at a fulfillment center has diminishing utility when similar items are already present.

In our formulation, the decision-maker does not have access to the function
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
and needs to balance learning this function in the presence of a potentially high-dimensional context together with maximizing it. This exploration-exploitation tradeoff is well-understood for contextual bandits, but the exploration step is somewhat more delicate when actions are subsets of a ground set. Indeed, a naïve application of algorithms for contextual bandits incurs a computational and statistical cost of
\mathcal{O}\left(A^{k}\right)
. We show that this prohibitive cost can be avoided by an argument that leverages the structure of submodular functions.

The widely-studied problem of contextual bandits corresponds to
k=1
, while the top-
k
problem studied in
[
SRY+21
]
corresponds to a linear (or, modular)
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
; both of these minimize regret for
c=1
. For
c<1
, the objective (
1.1
) studied in this paper is significantly less ambitious as compared to that of
[
RS15
]
; the latter paper exhibited a relaxation technique (in a non-bandit setting) for efficiently achieving—under certain assumptions—vanishing regret with
c=1
despite NP hardness of the offline problem.

The first method in the present paper is an application of the
SquareCB
algorithm
[
FR20
]
, with a few twists that exploit the structure of submodular functions. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}:2^{A}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
denote our estimate of the utility at time
t
, constructed in an online manner, as described below. Given
x_{t}
, we compute a local maximum
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}
of this function, in the sense that no swap of elements increases the (estimated) utility. We then randomize around this local maximum with the Inverse Gap Weighting strategy
[
AL99
,
FR20
]
, with actions being the
local swap operations
. The regret guarantee of
SquareCB
with time-varying action sets then implies an approximate local optimum of the true utility
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
, in a cumulative sense; submodularity of the aggregate utility then guarantees an approximation ratio for this solution. Our method naturally generalizes that of
[
SRY+21
]
for linear utility, where the
k-1
actions are chosen greedily and the last action is randomized among the remaining
A-(k-1)
choices.

To make the setting more widely applicable, we allow matroid constraints: for each time step
t
, we insist that the allowed sets
\mathsf{S}_{t}
are independent sets of a matroid
\mathcal{M}_{t}=([A],\mathcal{I}_{t})
defined over the common ground set
[A]
. The matroid
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is revealed together with context
x_{t}
(and can be thought of as part of the context), and can be chosen arbitrarily and adaptively by Nature. As a simple example, consider the
partition matroid

\mathcal{M}_{t}
defined as follows. For a collection
\{P_{i}\subseteq[A]\}_{i=1}^{m}
of disjoint sets and capacities
c_{i}\in[0,|P_{i}|]
, independent sets are those with intersection of size no more than
c_{i}
with each set
P_{i}
; that is,
\mathcal{I}_{t}=\{I\subseteq\cup_{i}P_{i}:|I\cap P_{i}|\leq c_{i},i\in[m]\}
. The partition matroid can model, for instance, constraints on choosing multiple items from the same category for product search, and the sets
P_{i}
and the capacities can themselves be chosen adaptively by Nature. We remark that our results can be further generalized to time-varying action sets and multiple matroid constraints per time step, but we leave these generalization out for simplicity of presentation.

Prior work

In the past two decades, submodularity has been successfully employed in a great number of machine learning domains
[
KG07
,
YJ08
,
LKG+07
,
LB12
,
KG14
]
. Linear submodular bandits have been studied in
[
YG11
]
in a different feedback model where the set is built progressively with noisy feedback at each stage (see also the generalization to RKHS in
[
CKK17
]
). Adaptive submodular bandits have been studied in
[
GKW+13
,
EKM21
]
, yet the setting is different from the one studied in this paper. In contrast to the aforementioned papers, in the present formulation the decision-maker selects a set and receives noisy feedback for this single choice. In particular, this means that the estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}
of the submodular function at smaller subsets may be unreliable. Additional difficulty stems from the fact that UCB-style methods cannot be employed for contextual bandits with general function approximation
[
FR20
]
, and closeness of the regression estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}
and the unknown model
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
cannot be ensured uniformly for all sets even with the method of
[
FR20
]
.

To this end, we would like to highlight again our proof technique, motivated by no-internal-regret statements in online learning: by randomizing around a local optimum of our estimated function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}
according to the IGW strategy, we obtain a regret guarantee which itself can be interpreted as a statement about the degree to which the chosen sets are, on average, local maxima of
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
. We remark that this simple approach can be used for nonconvex contextual bandit problems beyond submodularity, as long as local maxima are guaranteed to be within a multiplicative factor from the optimum.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we state the main structural results that imply global approximation ratios for local optima. In Section
3
, we present the two contextual bandit algorithms. Finally, in Section
4
, we develop efficient online regression oracles for several models.

2. Structure of Local Optima

Recall that a matroid
\mathcal{M}=([A],\mathcal{I})
is defined via a collection
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{M})
of independent sets. Given
\mathsf{S}\in\mathcal{I}
, we denote by
\mathcal{I}(\mathsf{S})\subseteq\mathcal{I}
the set of independent sets obtained by swapping one element out and a new element in, together with the set
\mathsf{S}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathsf{S})=\left\{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}:|\mathsf{S}^{\prime}|=|\mathsf{S}|,|\mathsf{S}\setminus\mathsf{S}^{\prime}|=|\mathsf{S}^{\prime}\setminus\mathsf{S}|=1\right\}\cup\{\mathsf{S}\}.

(2.5)

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
be the set of bases (maximal independent sets) of
\mathcal{M}
. Similarly to (
2.5
), define
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{S})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M})
to be the collection of bases of the matroid that differ from
\mathsf{S}
in at most one element. Recall that all the bases have the same cardinality, known as the rank of the matroid.

We remark that in the case of no matroid constraint, we take
\mathcal{I}
as the set of all sets and
\mathcal{I}(\mathsf{S})
as the set of all sets of the same cardinality as
\mathsf{S}
that differ in at most one element.

The following Lemma follows from the classical results on monotone submodular optimization with matroid constraints (
[

]

[
LMNS09

Lemma 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
be submodular nonnegative monotone functions and
\mathcal{M}_{j}=([A],\mathcal{I}_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, be matroids on
[A]

\varepsilon
-optimum, in the sense that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{T}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{T}_{j})-n\varepsilon.

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{T}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{T}_{j})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{T}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{T}_{j})-nk\varepsilon\right).

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S})=\sum_{\mathsf{T}\subseteq\mathsf{S}}w_{|\mathsf{S}|,|\mathsf{T}|}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{T}),~~~w_{s,t}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{p}}{e-1}p^{s-1}(1-p)^{s-t}dp,~~~s\geq t\geq 1.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}}\left(\mathsf{T}\right)=\frac{w_{|\mathsf{S}|,|\mathsf{T}|}}{\tau(\mathsf{S})}\cdot\mathsf{1}\{\mathsf{T}\subseteq\mathsf{S}\}.

(2.9)

Then, trivially,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S})=\tau(\mathsf{S})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{T}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}}}\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{T}).

3. Contextual Bandit Algorithms

In this section, we present two algorithms that attain sublinear regret in (
1.1
) whenever the associated online regression problem has sublinear regret. The first algorithm, based on
SquareCB
, achieves regret with rate
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)})
and constant
c=1/2
, while the second method,
\epsilon
-Greedy, attains the slower
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/3}\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)^{1/3})
regret rate but against a stronger benchmark with
c=1-e^{-1}
. Whether the first or the second result is stronger overall depends on the relative scales of the benchmark growth and
n
.
1
1

1

In practice one may interpolate between
SquareCB
and
\epsilon
-Greedy with a single extra parameter multiplying
2K
in the definition of the Inverse Gap Weighting strategy in (
3.15
).

Let us now explain the difference in the two approaches and the reason for the differing rates. The first method uses a regression oracle that can predict rewards for sets of size
k
(see Assumption
1
below). Since the algorithm randomizes around a local optimum of the estimated function, it cannot distinguish good local optima (those with a higher approximation ratio of
1-e^{-1}
) from those with a
1/2
approximation ratio. The second algorithm, on the other hand, explores subsets of a local optimum and thus obtains more information about the quality of the set. To be more precise, we follow
[
FW14
,
SVW17
]
and define a surrogate potential function which eliminates the worse local optima. Computing this surrogate, however, requires a stronger oracle (Assumption
2
) that can estimate rewards for sets of size less than or equal to
k
. Furthermore, this extra exploration step comes at a cost of a worse rate of
n^{2/3}
.

3.1 SquareCB for submodular optimization

Following

Assumption 1
.

There is an online regression algorithm for sequentially choosing
\widehat{r}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{r}_{n}
with
\widehat{r}_{t}=\widehat{r}_{t}(x_{1},\mathsf{S}_{1},r_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1},\mathsf{S}_{t-1},r_{t-1},x_{t},\mathsf{S}_{t})

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widehat{r}_{t}-r_{t})^{2}\leq\min_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{u}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})-r_{t})^{2}+\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n).

(3.13)

Alternatively, a sufficient (weaker) assumption is that for any adaptively chosen sequence

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widehat{r}_{t}-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t}))^{2}\leq\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n).

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right)^{2}\leq 2\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})-r_{t})^{2}-(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})-r_{t})^{2}\right]+16\log(2/\delta)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right]+\sqrt{2n\log(2/\delta)}.

Let us denote the above event by
\mathcal{E}
. Under this event, in view of (
3.19
), (
3.20
), (
3.21
), and by the oracle assumption,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\left(\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})\right)\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{2|\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})|}{\gamma}+16\log(2/\delta).

We now overbound

and conclude that under the event
\mathcal{E}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})\leq n\varepsilon

for

Hence, the tuple
(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{n})
is a joint local
\varepsilon
-optimum, in the sense of (

), with respect to the sum of
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\cdot,x_{t})
for
t=1,\ldots,n
. By Lemma
1
, under the event
\mathcal{E}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-nk\varepsilon\right)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t}).

(3.25)

Combining with (
3.22
), under the event
\mathcal{E}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right]+\sqrt{2n\log(2/\delta)}+\frac{1}{2}nk\varepsilon.

Hence, once again using (
3.19
), (
3.21
), and the oracle assumption, with probability at least

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\max_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{t}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t}^{*},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}n(k+1)\varepsilon+\sqrt{2n\log(2/\delta)}

(3.26)

\displaystyle=(k+1)\left[\frac{1}{2}\gamma\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)+\frac{2n(k\cdot(A-k)+1)}{\gamma}+16\log(2/\delta)\right]

(3.27)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2n\log(2/\delta)}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(k\sqrt{nk(A-k)\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)}\right)

(3.29)

with the choice of

.

∎

4. Examples

In this section, our goal is to provide a few simple examples of online regression oracles that could be used in practice together with the algorithms from previous sections. We start by noting that for any finite collection
\mathcal{F}
, one can simply use Vovk’s aggregating forecaster (see e.g.
[
CBL06
, Section 3.5]
) and obtain
\mathsf{Reg_{sq}}(\mathcal{F},n)=\mathcal{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
(see
[
FR20
]
for further discussion). Looking back at the example in the introduction, such an aggregating forecaster would be applicable if in (
1.4
) we choose
\lambda_{i}(x)
and
f_{i}(\mathsf{T},x)
from a finite collection. The techniques developed in the online learning literature, however, allow one to go beyond finite classes.

4.1 Measuring diversity of a set

We start by proposing a nonnegative monotone submodular function for a set of elements represented by
d
-dimensional vectors. With a slight abuse of notation, we identify elements
[A]
with their vector representations. Given a set
\mathsf{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of vectors, the measure
F(\mathsf{S})
should indicate whether the vectors span a sufficiently diverse set of directions. A possible approach that received attention in the machine learning community is the submodular function

F(\mathsf{S})=\log\text{det}(L_{\mathsf{S}})

where
L
is a positive definite matrix with rows indexed by elements of the ground set and
L_{\mathsf{S}}
denoting the
|\mathsf{S}|\times|\mathsf{S}|
submatrix corresponding to
\mathsf{S}
. As a concrete example, one may take
L
to be the Gram matrix
L_{i,j}=\left\langle s_{i},s_{j}\right\rangle
for
s_{i},s_{j}\in\mathsf{S}
. In this case, the determinant can be viewed as measuring the volume of the set. Unfortunately, this function is not monotone in general. To address this shortcoming, we show that the notions of Gaussian width or Rademacher averages of the set are submodular and monotone. Furthermore, thanks to standard concentration-of-measure arguments, they are easy to estimate to accuracy
\epsilon
in time
\mathcal{O}(d|\mathsf{S}|\epsilon^{-2})
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathsf{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\eta
be a random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbb{E}\eta=0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\mathsf{S})=\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle

(4.33)

is monotone submodular and nonnegative. Furthermore, if, for instance,
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
independent,

\left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta_{j}\right\rangle-\mathcal{W}(\mathsf{S})\right|\leq\text{diam}(\mathsf{S})\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{k}}.

We can extend definition (
4.33
) in a number of ways. In particular, we can define

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\mathsf{S},\Sigma)=\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\Sigma\eta\right\rangle

(4.34)

for a positive semidefinite matrix
\Sigma
. When thought of as part of a context,
\Sigma
can emphasize important features by rescaling.

4.2 A simple model

In this section we specify the online regression oracle for the following model. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{F}_{\textsf{nms}}
, and fix a nondecreasing
1
-Lipschitz function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{(\mathsf{S},x)\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S},x)\sigma(\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle):\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1\right\}.

(4.35)

Functions in this class are parametrized by a vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathcal{X}
. While this class is relatively simple, the square loss

\theta\mapsto(\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S},x)\sigma(\left\langle\theta,x\right\rangle)-r)^{2}

is not necessarily convex in the parameter
\theta
. However, a computationally-efficient GLMtron-style algorithm guarantees small regret in the well-specified setting. The proof of the following result is essentially from
[
FR20
]
, while the technique for learning GLMs with a ‘modified gradient’ is from
[
KS09
,
KKKS11
]
.

4.3 Sum of GLMs

We would like to extend the model (
4.35
) to a sum of several submodular functions weighted by monotonically increasing functions, as to cover the example in the Introduction. To be precise, let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and fix
k

P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Fix functions
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{\textsf{nms}}
and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\left\{(\mathsf{S},x)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(\mathsf{S},x)\sigma(\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{\,T}}P_{i}x):\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert\leq 1\right\}.

(4.37)

For instance, for product recommendation examples, we may posit a model of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(\mathsf{S},x)=\sum_{\mathsf{C}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{W}(\mathsf{S}\cap\mathsf{C})\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{\mathsf{C}},x\right\rangle)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a collection of categories (specified by sets

measures the quality of diverse coverage of each category

, weighted by the user-specific function
\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{\mathsf{C}},x\right\rangle)
that measures general interest in the category. The model is a natural ‘contextualized’ generalization of the model in
[

,
YJ08
]
, and can be viewed as an instance of (
4.37
) with block-diagonal indicators

.

While it is not difficult to certify existence of an online regression algorithm for the class in (

) (using the techniques in
[

RS14

), a general form of a computationally efficient algorithm in the spirit of Algorithm

is unclear. Instead, we proceed as in
[

]
and make a further simplifying assumption that

is a ReLU function. In addition, we assume that

are i.i.d. draws from a symmetric distribution with
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}x_{t}x_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{\,T}}
, and assume a uniform lower bound on the eigenvalues of the following matrices:

\displaystyle\forall t=1,\ldots,n,~~~~\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x)\boldsymbol{v}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x)P_{i}xx^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{\,T}}P_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{\,T}}\right]\succeq\lambda_{\textsf{min}}I,~~~~\lambda_{\textsf{min}}>0

\displaystyle-(1-e^{-1})\sum_{i=1}^{k}[\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j}-\pi(t_{i})+t_{i})]

(A.52)

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-1})k\left(\max_{i}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j}-\pi(t_{i})+t_{i})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})\right)

(A.53)

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-1})k\left(\max_{\mathsf{T}_{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{T}_{j})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}(\mathsf{S}_{j})\right)

(A.54)

Rearranging and summing over
j=1,\ldots,n
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

To simplify the proof, we assume a uniform upper bound

on the size
\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})
of any local neighborhood of the set
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}
. Furthermore, note that
\tau(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})
only depends on the cardinality of
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}
, and thus we abuse the notation and write

for
\tau(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})
.

Now, conditionally on
\mathcal{H}_{t-1},x_{t},\mathcal{M}_{t}
, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{\prime},\mathsf{S}_{t}}\left[(1-e^{-1})\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right]

(A.55)

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-1})\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{\prime},\mathsf{S}_{t}}\left[\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right]

(A.56)

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-1})\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})+\rho,

(A.57)

where the last step follows from the assumption that
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
takes values in
[0,1]
. On the other hand, by Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle(1-e^{-1})\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})+(1-e^{-1})nk\varepsilon

(A.58)

whenever

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\max_{\mathsf{S}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})\leq n\varepsilon.

(A.59)

We now quantify
\varepsilon
in (
A.59
). It holds that

\displaystyle\max_{\mathsf{S}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})

(A.60)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathsf{S}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})+\mathcal{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})

(A.61)

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{\mathsf{S}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})}|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}^{*},x_{t})|

(A.62)

since
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}
, by definition, is a local maximum of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(x_{t},\cdot)
in
\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})
. The last expression is at most

\displaystyle 2B\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}))}|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t},x_{t})-\mathcal{T}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t},x_{t})|

(A.63)

\displaystyle\leq 2B\tau(k)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})),\mathsf{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}}}|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})|,

(A.64)

using the definition in (
2.10
).

Summing over
t=1,\ldots,n
, assuming
\rho\in(0,1/2)
, and defining the shorthand

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{\prime},\mathsf{S}_{t}}\left[(1-e^{-1})\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}^{*}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right]

(A.65)

\displaystyle\leq\rho n+\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{\rho}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})),\mathsf{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}}}\sqrt{\rho}|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})|+\sqrt{1-\rho}|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})|\right).

(A.66)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})),\mathsf{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}}}\sqrt{\rho}|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})|+\sqrt{1-\rho}|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})|

(A.67)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})),\mathsf{S}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}}}\rho\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S},x_{t})\right)^{2}+(1-\rho)(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t})-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t},x_{t}))^{2}}

(A.68)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{S}^{\prime}_{t}\sim\text{unif}(\mathcal{B}(\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t})),\mathsf{S}_{t}\sim(1-\rho)\delta_{\widehat{\mathsf{S}}_{t}}+\rho\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{S}_{t}^{\prime}}}\left(\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{t}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\right)^{2}}.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

For every
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}\cup\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle=\max\left\{\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle,\max_{s\in\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle\right\}~~~~\text{and}~~~~\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}\cap\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle\leq\min\left\{\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle,\max_{s\in\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle\right\}.

(A.73)

Hence,

\displaystyle\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}\cup\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle+\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}\cap\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle\leq\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle+\max_{s\in\mathsf{T}}\left\langle s,\eta\right\rangle.

(A.74)

Taking expectation on both sides establishes the submodularity claim. Monotonicity is trivial while nonnegativity follows by Jensen’s inequality and the assumption of
\mathbb{E}\eta=0
.
Finally,
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})\mapsto\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta_{j}\right\rangle
is Lipschitz:

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta_{j}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta_{j}-\eta^{\prime}_{j}\right\rangle\leq\text{diam}(\mathsf{S})\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert\eta_{j}-\eta^{\prime}_{j}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\text{diam}(\mathsf{S})}{\sqrt{k}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}-\boldsymbol{\eta^{\prime}}\right\rVert.

Hence, Gaussian concentration yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max_{s\in\mathsf{S}}\left\langle s,\eta_{j}\right\rangle-\mathcal{W}(\mathsf{S})\right|\geq u\right)\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{ku^{2}}{2\text{diam}(\mathsf{S})^{2}}\right\}.

(A.75)

∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
be parametrized by
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\lVert\theta^{*}\right\rVert\leq 1
. First, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}g_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})(\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{t},x_{t}\right\rangle)-\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t}])x_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})^{2}\left(\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{t},x_{t}\right\rangle)-\sigma(\left\langle\theta^{*},x_{t}\right\rangle)\right)x_{t}.

(A.77)

We have

\displaystyle(\widehat{r}_{t}-\boldsymbol{u}^{*}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{t},x_{t}\right\rangle)-\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})\sigma(\left\langle\theta^{*},x_{t}\right\rangle)\right)^{2}

(A.78)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})^{2}\left(\sigma(\left\langle\theta_{t},x_{t}\right\rangle)-\sigma(\left\langle\theta^{*},x_{t}\right\rangle)\right)^{2}

(A.79)

by rearranging and summing the terms in

Proof of Proposition
4
.

For brevity, let us write
\boldsymbol{v}_{i,t}=\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(\mathsf{S}_{t},x_{t})
. Observe that
