Abstract
Based on a recent work on traveling waves in spatially nonlocal reaction-diffusion equations, we investigate the existence of traveling fronts in reaction-diffusion equations with a memory term. We will explain how such memory terms can arise from reduction of reaction-diffusion systems if the diffusion constants of the other species can be neglected. In particular, we show that two-scale homogenization of spatially periodic systems can induce spatially homogeneous systems with temporal memory. The existence of fronts is proved using comparison principles as well as a reformulation trick involving an auxiliary speed that allows us to transform memory terms into spatially nonlocal terms. Deriving explicit bounds and monotonicity properties of the wave speed of the arising traveling front, we are able to establish the existence of true traveling fronts for the original problem with memory. Our results are supplemented by numerical simulations.

Introduction

The study of traveling fronts in scalar reaction-diffusion equations with
a bistable nonlinearity is a classical topic and there is a rich literature
concerning the existence, uniqueness, and asymptotic stability of such fronts,
see e.g.
[
FiM77
]
and
[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
and the
references therein. The last two references are actually devoted to scalar,
spatially nonlocal, parabolic equations of the form

\dot{u}=Lu+F(u)+G\big(u,{\mathbb{J}}S(u)\big),\hskip 10.00002ptt>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1.1)

where traveling fronts of the form
u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}ct)\in{\mathbb{R}}
are
investigated. In particular,
[
Che97
]
studies the case
Lu=Du_{xx}
,
assumes that
G(u,\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
S:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are monotonously increasing
and that
{\mathbb{J}}
is a convolution with a smooth, nonnegative kernel
J\geq 0
,
i.e.
({\mathbb{J}}f)(x)=(J{*}f)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}J(x{-}y)f(y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
. The paper
[
AcK15
]

considers the case
G\equiv 0
and
Lu={\mathrm{D}}^{\alpha}_{\theta}u
, which is a
Riesz-Feller operator with
\alpha\in{]1,2]}
and

|\theta|\leq\min\{\alpha,2{-}\alpha\}
. In both cases, the nonlinearity
F

is of bistable type such that (
1.1
) has exactly three
homogeneous steady states
u\equiv u_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\{-,{\mathrm{m}},+\}
, where

u_{\pm}
are stable while the middle state
u_{\mathrm{m}}
is unstable.

In
[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
it is shown that, under suitable technical
assumptions, equation (
1.1
) admits traveling fronts

u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}ct)
with
{\mathcal{U}}(\xi)\to u_{\pm}
for
\xi\to\pm\infty
,
and that these solutions are even stable up to translation. The crucial tool for
the analysis in these two papers are comparison principles that are flexible
enough to survive nonlocal operators with positive kernels and monotone
operators. The only non-monotonicity occurs in the local function

u\mapsto F(u)
.

Here we treat a similar type of equation but allow for memory terms
(nonlocality in time), namely the equation

\dot{u}(t,x)=Du_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\,u(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptt>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1.2)

with an integrable, nonnegative memory kernel
\widehat{\Gamma}
. A more general,
nonlinear memory kernel of the type
G\big(u,\widehat{\Gamma}{*}S(u(\tau,x))\big)

could be treated as well (cf. Section
3.5
),
but we avoid this complexity to keep the main
arguments simple. Of course, memory terms of this type destroy any kind of
comparison principles, so the ideas in
[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
cannot
be applied directly. Traveling fronts in parabolic equations with discrete time
delay are treated in
[
WuZ01
]
, but not including the bistable case
considered here.

However, introducing the auxiliary wave speed
\mathtt{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we look at a
corresponding equation with spatial nonlocality, namely

\dot{u}(t,x)=Du_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\,u(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau,\hskip 10.00002ptt>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.3)

This nonlocal equation has now traveling fronts if the function

\widehat{F}:u\mapsto F(u)+\gamma u
with

\gamma=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau
is a bistable nonlinearity and the
associated fronts will have a wave speed
c=C(\mathtt{v})
. The main observation is
now that in the special case
\mathtt{v}=C(\mathtt{v})
the traveling wave

u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}\mathtt{v}t)
of (
1.3
) also solves the original memory
equation (
1.2
), see Proposition
2.1
.

Hence, existence of traveling fronts for the memory equation (
1.2
)
will be established by providing conditions that guarantee that the function

\mathtt{v}\mapsto C(\mathtt{v})
has a fixed point. Indeed, we again use the comparison
principle for the spatially nonlocal equation (
1.3
) to derive
continuity of
C
and certain monotonicities. In Theorem
2.8
we can
conclude that the memory equation (
1.2
) has always a traveling
front if
\widehat{F}:u\mapsto F(u)+\gamma u
is a bistable nonlinearity.
In Corollary
2.9
we provide bounds on the associated wave
speed in terms of the wave speed of the local equation with nonlinearity
\widehat{F}
.

In Section
3
we discuss several derivations of the memory
equation from classical reaction-diffusion systems. In all cases, the memory
appears by a coupling to ODEs or PDEs acting locally in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
but induce
memory through its internal dynamics. The simplest case occurs in the coupled
PDE-ODE system considered extensively in
[
GuR18
]
:

\dot{u}=Du_{xx}+F(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{m}}}a_{i}w_{i},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{w}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}w_{i}+b_{i}u\ \text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m_{\mathrm{m}},

(1.4)

where
a_{i},\ b_{i}
, and
\lambda_{i}
are fixed real parameters with

\lambda_{i}>0
. Clearly, the linear ODEs can be solved by
w_{i}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}\tau}b_{i}u(t{-}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau
, and we obtain
(
1.2
) with

\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{m}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}\tau}a_{i}b_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ giving }\gamma=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{m}}}\frac{a_{i}b_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}.

We emphasize that our theory is applicable also in cases where some of the
products
a_{i}b_{i}
are negative, as long as
\widehat{\Gamma}
is nonnegative.
We also discuss possible nonlinear couplings that still allow for the
application of
[
Che97
]
.

A second motivation for deriving memory equations is the study of traveling
pulses and fronts in situations where the coefficients in the system are
rapidly oscillating, thus modeling
a periodic heterostructure of the
medium on a microscopic scale. In the following, the homogenization parameter

\varepsilon>0
denotes the ratio between the characteristic length scales of the
microscopic and macroscopic structure. A typical system considered in
Section
3.2
is the heterogeneous FitzHugh-Nagumo system

\displaystyle\dot{u}

\displaystyle=\big(D_{\mathrm{u}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x)u_{x}\big)_{x}+F(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x,u)-\alpha\big(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x\big)w,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\dot{w}

\displaystyle=\big(\varepsilon^{2}D_{\mathrm{w}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x)w_{x}\big)_{x}-\lambda\big(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x\big)w+\beta\big(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x\big)u,

where all dependence on
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x
is assumed to be 1-periodic. Of
course, exact traveling waves cannot exist, but one still expects periodically
modulated pulses and fronts traveling through the heterogeneous medium. Indeed,
there exists a vast literature on the study of traveling fronts for
reaction-diffusion equations in continuous periodic
media, e.g.
[
HuZ95
,
BeH02
]
, fronts in discrete periodic media

[
GuH06
,
CGW08
]
, and fronts in perforated domains
[
Hei01
]
. We
refer to
[
Xin00
]
for a review including references to earlier
works. Most of the latter results share a common approach based on comparison
principles, and so do we. However, our approach also allows for systems of
reaction-diffusion equations.

In
[
MSU07
]
reaction-diffusion systems are studied and exponential
averaging is used to show that traveling wave solutions can be described by a
spatially homogeneous equation and exponentially small remainders. The approach
based on center-manifold reduction in
[
BoM14
]
applies to traveling
waves in parabolic equations and, moreover, the authors prove the existence of
a generalized oscillating wave that converges to a limiting wave. We point out
that all previously mentioned articles study limit problems of “one-scale”
nature, in contrast to
[
GuR18
]
and the present work where
traveling pulses in “two-scale FitzHugh–Nagumo systems” are
investigated, see Section
3.3
. According to Theorem

3.1
, the solutions
(u_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
of (
1.5
)
converge to the solution
(U,W)
of the two-scale system

\displaystyle\dot{U}(t,x)

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{eff}}U_{xx}(t,x)+\int_{\mathbb{T}}F(y,U(t,x))\;\!\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\alpha(y)W(t,x,y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\dot{W}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\big(D_{\mathrm{w}}(y)W_{y}(t,x,y)\big)_{y}-\lambda(y)W(t,x,y)+\beta(y)U(t,x),

where the microscopic variable
y
lies in the circle
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{\!/{\mathbb{Z}}}
. The
importance of the two-scale system (
1.6
) is that the microscopic
variable
y
decouples from the macroscopic space variable
x
such that this
system admits exact traveling waves of the form

(U(x{-}ct),W(x{-}ct,y))
. Moreover, the coupling from
U
to
W
and its
feedback are local in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and hence can be captured in spatially local
memory terms as in (
1.3
).

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we develop
our existence result for traveling fronts for the memory equation
(
1.2
), see Theorem
2.8
which relies on the
comparison principle in the spirit of
[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
and on new a
priori bounds for the front speed
c
in Theorem
2.5
. In
Section
3
we first present the derivation of memory equations
from coupled, but homogeneous systems, and secondly we show that two-scale
homogenization can be used to derive effective two-scale limits, which again lead to
the same memory equation. Finally, Section
4
compares the
abstract theory with numerical results in the special case that
F
is the
classical bistable cubic polynomial
-u(u{-}a)(u{-}1)
and the memory
kernel is simply given by
\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)=-\beta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}
.

2
Existence of traveling fronts

We first describe the setup and the assumptions for our theory in Section

2.1
. In Section
2.2
we introduce the spatially
nonlocal equation with the auxiliary speed
\mathtt{v}
and show that for traveling
fronts these equations are related. The main technical part are Sections

2.3
and
2.4
where we exploit the comparison
principles developed in
[
Che97
]
. The main results are presented in
Section
2.5
, where we also discuss potential generalizations.

2.1
Setup of the memory equation and assumptions

In all of Section
2
we study the Nagumo-type
reaction-diffusion equation (cf.
[
NAY62
,
McK70
]
)
with linear memory term in the form

\dot{u}(t,x)=Du_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma(\tau)u(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.1)

Here, the nonnegative memory kernel
\Gamma
is normalized, and hence satisfies

\Gamma\in{\mathrm{C}}^{0}({[0,\infty[}),\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma(\tau)\geq 0\text{ for all
}\tau\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma(\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau=1.

(2.2)

To formulate the precise conditions on the bistable nonlinearity
F
we
introduce the tilted function

F_{\gamma}(u)=F(u)+\gamma u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }u\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Hypotheses (H):

(H1)

We have
F\in{\mathrm{C}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and there exists
\gamma_{*}>0

such that for all
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
the function
F_{\gamma}
has
exactly three zeros, which we assume to be

u_{-}^{\gamma}<u_{\mathrm{m}}^{\gamma}<u_{+}^{\gamma}
and which satisfy

F_{\gamma}^{\prime}(u_{-}^{\gamma})<0
,
F^{\prime}(u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{m}})>0
, and
F_{\gamma}^{\prime}(u^{\gamma}_{+})<0
.

(H2)

The memory kernel
\Gamma
satisfies (
2.2
)
and
\widehat{g}_{1}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma(\tau)\tau\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau<\infty
.

The assumptions imply that the three constant functions
u(t,x)=u^{\gamma}_{\alpha}

with
\alpha\in\{-,{\mathrm{m}},+\}
are indeed trivial solutions for
(
2.1
). The solutions
u_{\pm}^{\gamma}
will be stable, while the middle
solution
u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{m}}
is unstable. Our aim is to show the existence of nontrivial
traveling fronts
u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}ct)
connecting the two stable levels

u^{\gamma}_{\pm}
, namely
{\mathcal{U}}(\xi)\to u^{\gamma}_{\pm}
for
\xi\to\pm\infty
. Of course,
then also a reflected traveling front
u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}^{\text{refl}}(x{-}c^{\text{refl}}t)
exists satisfying
{\mathcal{U}}^{\text{refl}}(\xi)\to u^{\gamma}_{\mp}

for
\xi\to\pm\infty
. Indeed, using the reflection symmetry
x\to-x
of
(
2.1
) gives
{\mathcal{U}}^{\text{refl}}(\xi)={\mathcal{U}}({-}\xi)
and

c^{\text{refl}}=-c
.

2.2
Traveling waves for the auxiliary equation

The works in
[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
allow for nonlocal terms
in the reaction-diffusion equation, but only for spatial nonlocality
and not for temporal one. However, for traveling waves space and time
coincide up to a scaling, so we look at an auxiliary problem, where we
choose a corresponding spatial nonlocality. For this we have to
choose an
auxiliary wave speed

\mathtt{v}
and arrive at the

auxiliary problem with spatial nonlocality
:

\dot{\widetilde{u}}(t,x)=D\widetilde{u}_{xx}(t,x)+F(\widetilde{u}(t,x))+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,\widetilde{u}(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.3)

The basis of our theory is the following simple proposition that
connects the existence of traveling waves for the original problem with
that of the auxiliary one.

Proposition 2.1

If (
2.3
) has a bounded traveling-wave solution

\widetilde{u}(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}\mathtt{c}t)
and the wave speed
\mathtt{c}
matches the
auxiliary speed
\mathtt{v}
occurring as parameter in (
2.3
), then

u(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}(x{-}\mathtt{v}t)
is also a solution of (
2.1
).

Proof.
Obviously, the partial derivatives and the local function
F(u)

in (
2.3
) and (
2.1
) coincide,
so it remains to match the integral terms. The straightforward calculation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,\widetilde{u}(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,{\mathcal{U}}(x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau-\mathtt{c}t)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\overset{\mathtt{v}=\mathtt{c}}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,{\mathcal{U}}(x+\mathtt{v}(t{-}\tau))\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,u(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

relying strongly on
\mathtt{c}=\mathtt{v}
, turns the spatial nonlinearity into a temporal
memory. This gives the desired result.

The above result does not specify the form of the traveling wave, hence it is
applicable to traveling pulse, traveling fronts, or to (quasi-)periodic wave
trains.

2.3
Comparison principles for the auxiliary equation

We are now in a position to apply the general theory for nonlocal
parabolic equations as developed in

[
Che97
,
AcK15
]
to our auxiliary problem (
2.3
)
for
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
. For this, we define the nonlinear operator

u\mapsto{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u]
via

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u](x)

\displaystyle:=Du_{xx}(x)+F(u(x))+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,u(x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(2.4)

in the notation of
[
Che97
]
. We first observe the relation

{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[\lambda\bm{1}]=F_{\gamma}(\lambda)\bm{1}
for all

\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\bm{1}
denotes the constant function
u(x)=1
. Moreover, the Fréchet derivative reads

\big({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u](\varphi)\big)(x)=D\varphi_{xx}(x)+F^{\prime}(u(x))\varphi(x)+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,\varphi(x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Hence, we obtain

\big({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u{+}v](\bm{1})\big)(x)-\big({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u](\bm{1})\big)(x)=F^{\prime}(u(x){+}v(x))-F^{\prime}(u(x))
. With
this and the assumption
F\in{\mathrm{C}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
from (H1), the two assumptions (A1)
and (A3) in
[
Che97
, Sect. 2]
are satisfied.

The crucial and nontrivial assumption (A2) in
[
Che97
]
concerns the
strong comparison principle and is the content of the following
proposition. To formulate it we introduce the notion of super- and
subsolutions for (
2.3
): We call
u^{*}
a supersolution and
u_{*}
a
subsolution if the relations
\dot{u}^{*}-{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u^{*}]\geq 0
and

\dot{u}_{*}-{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u_{*}]\leq 0
hold, respectively.

Proposition 2.2
(Strong comparison principle)

Let the Hypotheses (H1)–(H2) and
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
hold. If
u^{*}
is a
supersolution and
u_{*}
is a subsolution of (
2.3
) such that

u^{*}(0,\cdot)\geq u_{*}(0,\cdot)
and
u^{*}(0,\cdot){-}u_{*}(0,\cdot)\not\equiv 0
, then we have

u^{*}(t,\cdot)\gneqq u_{*}(t,\cdot)
for all
t>0
.

Proof.
We follow some ideas in the proof of

[
Che97
, Thm. 5.1]
for establishing condition (C2). By
assumption the difference
w(t,x):=u^{*}(t,x)-u_{*}(t,x)
satisfies

w(0,\cdot)\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\dot{w}\geq Dw_{xx}+F(u^{*})-F(u_{*})+{\mathbb{J}}w\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt({\mathbb{J}}w)(t,x):=\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)w(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Step 1.
We show by contradiction that the inequality

\dot{w}\geq Dw_{xx}+{\mathbb{J}}w
implies
w\geq 0
. We set
K:=4\gamma_{*}{+}4D
with
\gamma_{*}
from (H1). By assuming
w\ngeq 0
,
there exist
\delta>0
and
T>0
such that
w(t,x)>-\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt}
for

t\in\left[0,T\right[
and
\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}w(T,x)=-\delta\mathrm{e}^{KT}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume

w(T,0)<-\frac{3}{4}\delta\mathrm{e}^{KT}
.

Next we define the comparison function

\varphi_{\sigma}(t,x):=-(\frac{1}{2}+\sigma z(x))\,\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt}
with

z(x)=\frac{1+3x^{2}}{1+x^{2}}
, where the parameter
\sigma>0
will be
fixed later. On the one hand, using
z\geq 1
we have, for

\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2}
, the estimate

\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{\sigma}(t,x)\leq-\big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,1\big)\,\delta\,\mathrm{e}^{Kt}=-\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt}\leq w(t,x)

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. On the other hand, using
z(0)=1
for

\sigma=1/4
we find

\displaystyle\textstyle\varphi_{1/4}(T,0)=-\big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}1\big)\,\delta\,\mathrm{e}^{KT}=-\frac{3}{4}\delta\mathrm{e}^{KT}>w(T,0).

We define
\sigma_{*}
as follows:

\sigma_{*}:=\inf\Sigma\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\Sigma:=\big\{\,\sigma>0\>\big|\>\forall\,(t,x)\in[0,T]{\times}{\mathbb{R}}:\ \varphi_{\sigma}(t,x)\leq w(t,x)\,\big\}

and observe that the above estimates imply
\sigma_{*}\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]
. By
continuity, we see that
\Sigma
is closed, hence
\sigma_{*}\in\Sigma
, which
implies
\sigma_{*}>\frac{1}{4}
. Using
z(x)>2
for
|x|>2
for all

\sigma\geq\frac{1}{4}
, we have

\varphi_{\sigma}(t,x)<-(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}2)\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt}\leq w(t,x)
. Hence,
for
\sigma\in{[\frac{1}{4},\sigma_{*}[}\subset{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\Sigma
there exists

(t_{\sigma},x_{\sigma})\in[0,T]{\times}[-1,1]
such that

\varphi_{\sigma}(t_{\sigma},x_{\sigma})\geq w(t_{\sigma},x_{\sigma})
.

By the continuity of
w
and
(\sigma,t,x)\mapsto\varphi_{\sigma}(t,x)
and by
compactness of
[0,T]{\times}[-1,1]
, we hence
find
(t_{*},x_{*})\in[0,T]{\times}[-1,1]
such that
\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})\geq w(t_{*},x_{*})
. Obviously, we have
t_{*}>0
, but
t=T
may be possible.
However
\sigma_{*}\in\Sigma
means
\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t,x)\leq w(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]{\times}{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus, we conclude
w(t_{*},x_{*})=\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})
,
w_{x}(t_{*},x_{*})=\partial_{x}\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})
,

w_{xx}(t_{*},x_{*})\geq\partial^{2}_{x}\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\dot{w}(t_{*},x_{*})=\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})&\text{for }t_{*}\in{]0,T[},\\
\dot{w}(t_{*},x_{*})\leq\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})&\text{for }t_{*}=T.\end{array}\right.

Using (H2) (nonnegativity of
\Gamma
) and
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
together
with the inequalities (i)
\frac{3}{4}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{*}z(x_{*})
and
(ii)
\frac{1}{2}+\sigma_{*}z(x)\leq 2
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
the following chain of inequalities for the
particular point
(t_{*},x_{*})
holds:

\displaystyle-\tfrac{3}{4}\delta K\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{*}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{\geq}-\big(\tfrac{1}{2}+\sigma_{*}z(x_{*})\big)\delta K\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{*}}=\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})

\displaystyle\geq\dot{w}(t_{*},x_{*})\geq Dw_{xx}(t_{*},x_{*})+{\mathbb{J}}w(t_{*},x_{*})

\displaystyle\!\!\!\overset{w\geq\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}}{\geq}D\partial_{x}^{2}\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*})+\textstyle\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\,\varphi_{\sigma_{*}}(t_{*},x_{*}{+}\mathtt{v}\tau))\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(ii)}}{\geq}-D\sigma_{*}z^{\prime\prime}(x_{*})\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{0}}-2\gamma\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{*}}\geq-\big(2D+2\gamma_{*})\delta\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{*}}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta K\mathrm{e}^{Kt_{*}},

where we used
z^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 4
and
\sigma_{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and the definition of

K=4\gamma_{*}{+}4D
. Thus, we have reached a contradiction and the assertion
w\geq 0
is proven.

Step 2.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
u_{*}(t,x)
and

This is the desired strong comparison principle.

2.5
Traveling fronts for the memory equation

According to Proposition
2.1
, we obtain a traveling front for
the memory equation (
2.1
) by finding
\mathtt{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\mathtt{v}=\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})
. To guarantee existence of such solutions, we derive
suitable bounds and monotonicity properties for the wave speed function
\mathtt{C}
,
where again the comparison principle for sub- and supersolutions is useful, see
Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
. We recall our
choice that all traveling fronts
{\mathcal{U}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}
satisfy

{\mathcal{U}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}(-\infty)=u^{\gamma}_{-}\lneqq u^{\gamma}_{+}={\mathcal{U}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}(\infty)
.

Lemma 2.7
(Monotonicity of
\mathtt{C}
)

For all
\gamma,\widetilde{\gamma}\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
and all
\mathtt{v},\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we
have the following implications:

\displaystyle(a)\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\mathtt{v}\leq\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}

\displaystyle\hskip-55.00008pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})

\displaystyle\geq\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}});

(2.15)

\displaystyle(b)\ \gamma\leq\widetilde{\gamma}\text{ and }\max\{u^{\gamma}_{-},u^{\widetilde{\gamma}}_{-}\}\geq 0

\displaystyle\hskip-55.00008pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})

\displaystyle\geq\mathtt{C}(\widetilde{\gamma},\mathtt{v}).

(2.16)

Proof.
Throughout the proof we set

u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}(t,x)={\mathcal{U}}\big(x{-}\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})t\big)
and insert one
traveling front into the parabolic equation for the other front, thus
obtaining a super or a subsolution. Then, Proposition
2.3

provides a comparison of the wave speeds.

Ad (a).
We insert
u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}
into the equation for

u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}
and obtain

\dot{u}_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}{-}{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}]=\underbrace{\dot{u}_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}{-}{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}[u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}]}_{=0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\;\!\big(u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}(t,x{+}\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}\tau){-}u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.17)

Using
\Gamma(\tau)\geq 0
,
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}(\xi)\geq 0
, and
\mathtt{v}\leq\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}
, we see that the integral is nonnegative. Together
with
\gamma\geq 0
, we find
\dot{u}_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}-{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}]\geq 0
, i.e.
u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}
is
a supersolution of
\dot{u}={\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u]
, whereas
u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}

is a (sub)solution. Hence, Proposition
2.3
gives (
2.15
).

Ad (b).
Assume
u^{\gamma}_{-}\geq 0
such that
u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}(t,x)\geq u^{\gamma}_{-}\geq 0
. We insert this solution into the equation for

u_{\widetilde{\gamma},\mathtt{v}}
and obtain

\dot{u}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}{-}{\mathcal{A}}_{\widetilde{\gamma},\mathtt{v}}[u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}]=\underbrace{\dot{u}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}{-}{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}]}_{=0}+\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\gamma{-}\widetilde{\gamma})\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Gamma(\tau)\;\!u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}(t,x{+}\mathtt{v}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.18)

Since the integral is nonnegative, the assumption
\gamma\leq\widetilde{\gamma}

implies that
u_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}
is a subsolution whereas
u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}

is a (super)solution. Hence, (
2.16
) follows via Proposition

2.3
.

If
u^{\gamma}_{-}<0
we can rely on
u^{\gamma}_{-}\geq 0
. Interchanging the role
of
\gamma
and
\widetilde{\gamma}
in (
2.18
), we see that

u_{\gamma,\widetilde{\mathtt{v}}}
is a supersolution of
\dot{u}={\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma,\mathtt{v}}[u]

and (
2.15
) follows again.

Having collected all preliminary estimates, we can prove the existence of
traveling fronts for the original memory equation
(

Theorem 2.8
(Main result)

Let the Hypotheses (H1) and (H2) hold. Then, for all
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
the
equation
\mathtt{c}=\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{c})
has a unique solution
\mathtt{c}_{\gamma}
, and
thus there exists a unique (up to translation), asymptotically stable
traveling-front solution

for the
memory equation (
2.1
), where
\overline{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\gamma}
satisfies the
properties (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).

Proof.
For a fixed
\gamma\in[0,\gamma_{*}]
the function

\mathtt{v}\mapsto\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})
is continuous and non-increasing according
to Lemma
2.6
and relation (
2.15
), respectively.
Thus, there is a unique solution to
\mathtt{v}=\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})
, which we call

\mathtt{c}_{\gamma}
. According to Proposition
2.4
there is a unique
(up to translation) traveling front

for the auxiliary equation
(
2.3
)with
\mathtt{v}=\mathtt{c}_{\gamma}
.

Because of
\mathtt{v}=\mathtt{c}_{\gamma}=\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{c}_{\gamma})
we can return to the
memory equation using Proposition
2.1
, and the result is
established.

Next we combine the available estimates and monotonicity of the wave speed

\mathtt{C}(\gamma,\mathtt{v})
to give bounds on
\mathtt{c}_{\gamma}
. Before doing so, we
recall the classical result that for the local equation, i.e.
\mathtt{v}=0

sign of the wave speed

is opposite to the sign of

Indeed, to see this, we multiply (

) by
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}
and

leading to

-\mathtt{C}(\gamma,0)\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,0}(\xi)^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}\xi=\lim_{R\to\infty}\Big(\frac{D}{2}{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,0}(\xi)^{2}\Big)\Big|_{-R}^{R}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{\gamma}({\mathcal{U}}_{\gamma,0}(\xi)){\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{\gamma,0}(\xi)\;\!\mathrm{d}\xi={\mathcal{F}}(\gamma).

In particular, for

, which means
that a standing wave exists. Obviously this implies

, i.e. also the

2.1

.

(Bounds on the wave speed)

Let the assumptions (H1) and (H2) hold and let

satisfy (

(2.19)

3
Derivation of memory equations from local PDEs

Equations with memory can be derived from local models if so-called internal
variables
w
are eliminated. Changes in the main variable
u
induce
instantaneous changes in
w
, but the internal dynamics of the
w
system
leads to the delayed back-coupling from
w
. This effect stays local in space,
if the diffusion in the variable
w
can be neglected.

3.1
Parabolic equation coupled to linear ODEs

For the modeling of pulse propagation in nerves, one often uses the coupling
between a parabolic equation and ODEs, see e.g.
[
Car77
, Eqn. (0.1)]
for the
Hodgkin-Huxley equation and
[
NAY62
,
Den91
]
for the FitzHugh–Nagumo-like
equations, which corresponds to the case
m_{\mathrm{w}}=1
in the following model:

\dot{u}=Du_{xx}+F(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}a_{i}w_{i},\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{w}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}w_{i}+b_{i}u\ \text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m_{\mathrm{w}},

(3.1)

where
a_{i},\ b_{i}
, and
\lambda_{i}
are fixed real parameters with

\lambda_{i}>0
. Clearly, the linear ODEs can be solved locally in space by

w_{i}(t,x)=w_{i}(0,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}t}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}(t{-}\tau)}b_{i}u(\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau
. Assuming that there is
infinite history (which is compatible with our search for traveling fronts that
exist for all time) we may also write
w_{i}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}\tau}b_{i}u(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau
. With this we obtain the
memory equation (
2.1
) with the specific kernel

\Gamma(\tau)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}\tau}a_{i}b_{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ with }\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}\frac{a_{i}b_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}.

(3.2)

Clearly, our theory is applicable also in cases where some of the
products
a_{i}b_{i}
are negative, as long as
\Gamma
is nonnegative, e.g.
\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau}-2\mathrm{e}^{-3\tau}+\mathrm{e}^{-4\tau}=(\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}{-}\mathrm{e}^{-2\tau})^{2}
. Thus, we need a positive feedback

a_{i}b_{i}
for several species
i
, but we may also allow for a negative feedback

a_{j}b_{j}
for some components if they are not too big.

For systems of the type (
3.1
) the existence of traveling
waves, in particular pulses, is studied in
[
GuR18
]
in detail, even
in cases where Hypothesis (H2) is violated, i.e.
\Gamma
may change sign.
The latter is necessary to handle
non-monotone fronts and pulses. Therefore, the assumptions in

[
GuR18
]
are more general with respect to the types of traveling
waves (e.g. pulses, non-monotone fronts) under consideration.

However, when studying only traveling fronts, the assumptions of the present
article are much more general, since (H2) does not require any assumptions on
the (exponential) structure of
\Gamma
. In particular, we
believe that our approach may be generalized to nonlinear coupling terms as in
Section
3.5
, whereas the approach in

[
GuR18
]
relies on the linear structure of the
equations for
w_{i}
. Moreover, the present approach allows to calculate
bounds on the wave speed, see Theorem
2.5
and Corollary

2.9
.

3.2
The two-scale homogenization model

A significant body of work (cf.
[
HuZ95
,
Xin00
,
Hei01
,
BeH02
,
MSU07
,
CGW08
,
BoM14
]
)
considers the propagation in periodic media. If the period of the oscillating
coefficient is very small one can perform a homogenization and consider
traveling waves in the homogenized system. However, often the diffusion
coefficients of some of the species are very small as well, which leads to a coupled system of the form

\left.\begin{aligned} \dot{v}&=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big({\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x)\nabla v\big)+f_{\mathrm{v}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x,v,w)\\
\dot{w}&=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\varepsilon^{2}{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x)\nabla w\big)+f_{\mathrm{w}}(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}x,v,w)\end{aligned}\right\}\hskip 10.00002ptt>0,\ x\in\Omega.

(3.3)

Here the functions
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}
,
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}
,
f_{\mathrm{v}}(\cdot,v,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{v}}}
, and

f_{\mathrm{w}}(\cdot,v,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}
are assumed to be 1-periodic in each component of

y=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

It is shown in
[
MRT14
]
that under suitable conditions on the diffusion
matrices
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}
and
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}
and the reaction terms
f_{\mathrm{v}}
and

f_{\mathrm{w}}
the solutions
(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
to the initial value problem converge
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
to solutions
(V,W)
of the following two-scale
model. For this we denote by
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional torus
obtained by identifying the opposite sides of the unit cube. While the fast
diffusion of the variable
v_{\varepsilon}
guarantees that the limit
V
only depends
on the macroscopic variable
x\in\Omega
, the limit
W
of the solutions

w_{\varepsilon}
is a two-scale function depending also on the microscopic
variable
y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{x}\big({\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}^{\text{eff}}\nabla_{x}V(t,x)\big)+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f_{\mathrm{v}}(y,V(t,x),W(t,x,y))\;\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\dot{W}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{y}\big({\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}(y)\nabla_{y}W(t,x,y)\big)+f_{\mathrm{w}}(y,V(t,x),W(t,x,y))

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d}.

(3.4b)

Here
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}^{\text{eff}}
is the effective diffusion tensor
obtained by classical homogenization, see e.g.
[
BLP78
]
.
The main point in this theory is that it is not possible to replace
the slowly diffusing component
w^{\varepsilon}
by its macroscopic average. We
rather need to keep track of the microscopic distribution of the

w^{\varepsilon}
relative to the underlying periodic microstructure. This is
exactly done by the function
W
depending on
x
and
y
.

The original theory in
[
MRT14
]
and
[
Rei15
]
was
developed for bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We show in
Appendix
A
how the result can be generalized to equations
posed on the full space
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which is needed to treat traveling
waves. For this one introduces the weighted Lebesgue spaces

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\>u\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\;\Big|\;\|u\|^{2}_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varrho(x)|u(x)|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty\>\Big\},

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\>U\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})\;\Big|\;\|U\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}^{2}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varrho(x)|U(x,y)|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}y<\infty\>\Big\},

where for a radius
R>0
we set
\varrho(x)=1/\cosh(|x|/R)
. With this and
the natural conditions on
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{v}}
,
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}
,
f_{\mathrm{v}}
, and
f_{\mathrm{w}}
we
derive the following result in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.1
(Two-scale homogenization)

Let
(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
denote a sequence of solutions to the initial
value problem (
3.3
) on
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with initial conditions

(v_{\varepsilon}^{0},w_{\varepsilon}^{0})=(v_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),w_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
that are bounded in

{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{m_{\mathrm{v}}+m_{\mathrm{w}}}
and converge as follows:

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to V^{0}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptw_{\varepsilon}^{0}\stc\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W^{0}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d}),

then the solution
(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
converges to the solution
(V,W)
of the
two-scale system (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup V\ \text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})),&\ \nabla v_{\varepsilon}\wtc\nabla V{+}\nabla_{y}\widetilde{V}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})),\\
w_{\varepsilon}\stc W\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})),&\varepsilon\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\wtc\nabla_{y}W\qquad\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})),\end{array}

where
\widetilde{V}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega;{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
with

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\widetilde{V}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=0
denotes the corrector function.

The major advantage of the two-scale model (
3.4
) is that it is again
homogeneous in the macroscopic spatial variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, while the periodic
structure is restricted to the microscopic variable
y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Thus, we
have a coupling that is local in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
from
V(s,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{v}}}
to

W(s,x,\cdot)\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}
. At later times
t>s
the internal parabolic
evolution of
W(t,x,\cdot)
via (
3.4b
) leads to a delayed
feedback of
V(s,x)
for all
s<t
of memory type.

In particular, it is possible to look for exact traveling
waves for (
3.4
) in the form

(V(t,x),W(t,x,y))=({\mathcal{V}}(x{-}\mathtt{c}t),{\mathcal{W}}(x{-}\mathtt{c}t,y))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{v}}+m_{\mathrm{w}}}.

Transforming these two-scale solutions back into the one-scale form (called
folding in
[
MiT07
,
MRT14
]
) one obtains periodically
oscillating traveling
waves, namely

(\widetilde{v}_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\widetilde{w}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))=({\mathcal{V}}(x{-}\mathtt{c}t),{\mathcal{W}}(x{-}\mathtt{c}t,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x))

that provide the correct first-order approximation of the true solutions

(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
, see Figure
3.2
for a plot of such solutions.

3.3
Reduction to a scalar equation

Here we give an explicit example for deriving a scalar memory equation
from a two-scale system with
(v,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1}{\times}{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
. We start by looking at a
specific case for the full time-dependent system (
3.4
), namely
the FitzHugh-Nagumo case where
V
and
W
are scalar and are defined on
the real line
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, we assume linear couplings with

f_{\mathrm{v}}(y,V,W)=\Phi(y,V)+\alpha(y)W
and

f_{\mathrm{w}}(y,V,W)=-b(y)W+\beta(y)V
:

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t,x)\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{v}}V_{xx}(t,x)+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\Phi(y,V(t,x))\;\!\mathrm{d}y+\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\alpha(y)W(t,x,y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y,

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\dot{W}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbb{D}}_{w}(y)W_{y}(t,x,y)\right)_{y}-b(y)W(t,x,y)+\beta(y)V(t,x),

(3.6b)

where
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{T}}^{1}={\mathbb{R}}_{\!/{\mathbb{Z}}}
. All functions
\Phi
,
\alpha
,
\beta

and
b
are assumed to be continuous. In general, the coupling parameters

\alpha(y)
and
\beta(y)
may change sign, while the microscopic diffusion
coefficient
{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}(y)
and the damping factor
b
are assumed to be
strictly positive. The solution
\psi(t,y)
of the linear equation

\dot{\psi}=({\mathbb{D}}(y)\psi_{y})_{y}-b(y)\psi
,
\psi(0,\cdot)=\psi^{0}
has the
semigroup representation

\psi(t,y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}H(t,y,\widetilde{y})\psi^{0}(\widetilde{y})\;\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}
, where the Greens
function
H(t,y,\widetilde{y})
is strictly positive for
t>0
by the maximum principle
for linear parabolic equations, see e.g.
[
Eva98
, Thm. 12, p. 376]
.

By introducing the effective nonlinearity
F(V):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Phi(y,V)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
and
expressing
W
as a linear functional over the history of
V
via
(
3.6b
), we obtain a Nagumo equation with memory kernel:

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t,x)=V_{xx}(t,x)+F(V(t,x))+\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)V(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.7)

\displaystyle\text{with }\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}H(\tau,y,\widetilde{y})\alpha(\widetilde{y})\beta(y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y\;\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{y}.

Thus, using
H>0

a sufficient condition for
\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\geq 0
in our Hypothesis (H2)
is given by
\alpha(y),\beta(y)\geq 0
for all
y\in{\mathbb{T}}
or
vice versa
\alpha(y),\beta(y)\leq 0
.

Remark 3.2
(On the positivity of
\widehat{\Gamma}
)

The given conditions on
\alpha
and
\beta
are far from optimal.
Indeed, writing
{\mathbb{L}}\psi=-({\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{w}}\psi_{y})_{y}+b\psi
we find a complete
orthonormal set
(\psi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
of injunctions, i.e.
{\mathbb{L}}\psi_{n}=\lambda_{n}\psi_{n}
with
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}\to\infty
.

Expanding the coupling coefficients
\alpha(y)=\sum_{\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\psi_{n}(y)
and

\beta(y)=\sum_{\mathbb{N}}b_{n}\psi_{n}(y)
, we find
\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}a_{n}b_{n}
, and conclude
that the property
a_{n}b_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is sufficient. Hence,
setting
\alpha(y)=\beta(y)=\psi_{n}(y)
and noticing that, by the Sturm-Liouville
property, the function
\psi_{n}
has
\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
zeros, we
have constructed an example for
\alpha
and
\beta
that change sign and still
satisfy
\widehat{\Gamma}(\tau)\geq 0
.

However, it is not even necessary that all of the products
a_{n}b_{n}
are
nonnegative, see the arguments after (
3.2
).
In
[
GuR18
]
the explicit choices
\alpha(y)=-\sum_{n=1}^{m_{*}}\psi_{n}(y)
and
\beta(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{m_{*}}\sigma_{n}\psi_{n}(y)
were made, and
different signs for
\sigma_{n}
are explicitly allowed.

3.5
Possible generalizations

For mathematical conciseness we have decided to restrict ourselves to memory terms with
a simple linear structure. However, as our theory is many based on the quite
general work in
[
Che97
]
it is clear that the results can be
generalized in several ways.

First, the integral memory can be replaced by discrete time delays in the form
such that the equation reads

\dot{u}(t,x)=Du_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+\sum_{j=1}^{j_{*}}\gamma_{j}u(t{-}\tau_{j},x).

(3.8)

Assuming
\gamma_{j}>0
and setting
\gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{*}}\gamma_{j}
, we obtain
the memory equation (
2.1
) with a
\Gamma(\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{*}}\frac{\gamma_{j}}{\gamma}\,\delta_{\tau_{j}}(\tau)
. We refer to
[
WuZ01
]

for a general approach to traveling waves in reaction-diffusion equations
involving linear and nonlinear delay terms.

Secondly, further generalizations can be obtained by looking at nonlinear
couplings to ODEs, thus generalizing the coupled system
(
3.1
):

\dot{u}=Du_{xx}+F(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}A_{i}(u,w_{i}),\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{w}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}w_{i}+B_{i}(u),

(3.9)

where the coupling functions
A_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
B_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are assumed to be smooth
functions with derivatives satisfying
\partial_{u}A(u,w_{i})\leq 0
,
\partial_{w_{i}}A_{i}(u,w_{i})\geq 0
, and
B^{\prime}_{i}(u)\geq 0
. Again we can express
w_{i}(t,x)
as memory
functional depending on
u(s,x)
for
s\in{]{-}\infty,t[}
. Hence,
(
3.9
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t,x)=Du_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}A_{i}\big(u(t,x),{\mathrm{I}}\hskip-1.30005pt\Gamma_{i}{*}B_{i}(u(\cdot,x))(t)\big),

\displaystyle\text{where }{\mathrm{I}}\hskip-1.30005pt\Gamma_{i}{*}h(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}\tau}h(t{-}\tau)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Hence we have a memory in time, which is local in the space variable

x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. As in Section
2.2
we can introduce an auxiliary wave
speed
\mathtt{v}
and turn the memory terms into spatially nonlocal terms. Because
of our assumptions on
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
we are exactly in the setting of

[
Che97
, Eqn. (1.14)]
. Thus, it is expected that the methods
developed here can be extended to such nonlinear couplings.

Thirdly, it seems possible to generalize the theory to handle multidimensional
traveling waves in a cylindrical domain
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}{\times}\Sigma
as in

[
Gar86
]
, but now with memory terms.

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=D\big(u_{xx}(t,x,y)+\Delta_{y}u(t,x,y)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+F(y,u(t,x,y))+\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma(\tau)u(t{-}\tau,x,y)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\text{for }t>0,\ (x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}{\times}\Sigma,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=u(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\text{for }t>0,\ (x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}{\times}\partial\Sigma.

However, then the “bistability” Hypothesis (H1) has to be formulated in terms of the
elliptic problem
u\mapsto D\Delta_{y}u+F(u)+\gamma u
in
\Sigma
, which
should have exactly three solutions
u^{\gamma}_{\alpha}\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{0}(\Sigma)
with
appropriate properties.

4
Applications to the cubic case

In this section we study the classical FitzHugh-Nagumo system with a bistable
cubic nonlinearity, namely

\dot{u}=u_{xx}+F(u)-\beta v,\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{v}=-v+u,\hskip 10.00002ptF(u)=-u(u{-}a)(u{-}1),

(4.1)

where
a\in{]0,1[}
. Concerning traveling fronts, this system is equivalent to
the memory equation

\dot{u}(t,x)=u_{xx}(t,x)+F(u(t,x))-\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau}u(t{-}\tau,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

(4.2)

In particular, we see that the previous parameter
\gamma
is given by

\gamma=-\beta
, such that our theory developed above only applies for

\beta\leq 0
. However, in the numerical simulations documented below, we are
free to choose
\beta>0
as well.

We first observe that the nonlinearity

F_{-\beta}(u)=-u(u{-}a)(u{-}1)-\beta u

has a bistable structure if and only if
\beta<(1{-}a)^{2}/4
and then

[

]

is calculated explicitly,

\beta=0

Appendix A
Two-scale convergence in weighted Sobolev spaces

The aim of this appendix is to explain how the result in Theorem

3.1
for
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be derived by generalizing the
theory for bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as developed in

[
MRT14
,
Rei15
]
. To this end we introduce a few notations from
two-scale convergence and two-scale homogenization as developed in

[
Ngu89
,
All92
,
MiT07
,
CDG08
]
. For arbitrary
(bounded or unbounded) Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}:{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\to{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d});\hskip 10.00002pt({\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}u)(x,y):=u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\varepsilon\left[\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right]+\varepsilon y\right),

where
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is obtained by extension with
0
on

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash\Omega
and
[z]\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
denotes the integer part for
any
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We define
weak and strong two-scale convergence
via

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\wtc U\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt:\overset{\text{Def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup U_{\mathrm{ex}}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d}),

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\stc U\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt:\overset{\text{Def.}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}\to U_{\mathrm{ex}}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d}).

We briefly recall the mathematical setting for the
\varepsilon
-problem
(
3.3
) such that the two-scale system
(
3.4
) is rigorously justified. The fourth order diffusion tensors

{\mathbb{D}}_{i}\in\mathrm{Lin}({\mathbb{R}}^{d{\times}m_{i}};{\mathbb{R}}^{d{\times}m_{i}})
are uniformly
elliptic and bounded, i.e. there exist constants
M,\mu>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y)\xi:\xi\geq\mu|\xi|^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt|{\mathbb{D}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y)\xi|\leq M|\xi|\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d{\times}m_{\mathrm{i}}}\text{ and }{\mathrm{i}}\in\{{\mathrm{v}},{\mathrm{w}}\},

and they are not necessarily symmetric. For the reaction terms we assume boundedness

f_{\mathrm{i}}(\cdot,v,w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and global Lipschitz continuity, i.e. there exists
L>0
such that for
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{v}}}
and

w_{1},w_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\mathrm{w}}}

\displaystyle|f_{\mathrm{i}}(y,v_{1},w_{1})-f_{\mathrm{i}}(y,v_{2},w_{2})|\leq L(|v_{1}{-}v_{2}|+|w_{1}{-}w_{2}|)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }{\mathrm{i}}\in\{{\mathrm{v}},{\mathrm{w}}\}.

In addition to the weighted Lebesgue spaces
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and

{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
from (
3.5
), we also
define the weighted Sobolev spaces
{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and

{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
via

\displaystyle{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\>u\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\;\Big|\;\|u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}+\|\nabla u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\infty\>\Big\},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\>U\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\;\Big|\;\|U\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}+\|\nabla_{y}U\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}<\infty\>\Big\}.

Our choice
\varrho(x)=1/\cosh(|x|/R)
gives
\varrho,\varrho^{-1}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which implies that the
weighted Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces are Banach spaces, see e.g.
[
GoU09
, Thm. 1]
. Moreover, introducing the scalar product

(u,v)_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}:=(\sqrt{\varrho}u,\sqrt{\varrho}v)_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
yields that they are also separable Hilbert spaces.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
follows along the lines of

[
MRT14
, Thm. 4.1]
and
[
Rei15
, Thm. 2.1.1, Thm. 2.2.1]

with the following modifications.

•

The definition of the periodic unfolding operator

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}:{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d});\hskip 10.00002pt({\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}u)(x,y):=u\left(\varepsilon\left[\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right]+\varepsilon y\right)

is immediate and the notion of two-scale convergence follows
analogously. Notice that the assumptions on the weight function imply the
unfolding estimate

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}\varrho-\varrho\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{d}\|\nabla\varrho\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{d}/R.

Hence, we obtain the upper bound for unfolded functions

u\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}_{\varepsilon}u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}{\times}{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\leq U_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptU_{\varepsilon}=\big(1-\varepsilon\sqrt{d}/R\big)^{-1/2}.

•

The standard compactness results for two-scale convergence

[
Ngu89
,
All92
,
MiT07
]
also hold for weighted spaces,
since they are, in particular, separable Banach spaces and the
Banach–Alaoglu Theorem is applicable.

•

The definition of the gradient folding operator

\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{slow}}_{\varepsilon}
(cf.
[
MRT14
, Def. 3.5]
) needs
to be adapted as follows: In the case of slow diffusion of order
O(\varepsilon^{2})
,
we define for
W\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};{\mathrm{H}}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
the one-scale
function
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{slow}}_{\varepsilon}W:=\hat{w}_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which is given by the Lax–Milgram lemma as the unique solution of the elliptic problem

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big[\sqrt{\varrho}\hat{w}_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\varrho}W\right)\big]\cdot\sqrt{\varrho}\varphi+\big[\varepsilon\sqrt{\varrho}\nabla\hat{w}_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{\varrho}\nabla_{y}W\right)\big]:\varepsilon\sqrt{\varrho}\nabla\varphi\;\!\mathrm{d}x=0

for all
\varphi\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}_{\varrho}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

•

For the slowly diffusing species
w_{\varepsilon}
, we choose the test function

\varphi^{\mathrm{slow}}_{\varepsilon}=\varrho\left(w_{\varepsilon}{-}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{slow}}_{\varepsilon}W\right)
and continue as in

[
MRT14
,
Rei15
]
. For species
v_{\varepsilon}
undergoing diffusion of
order
O(1)
, we proceed analogously (cf.
[
Rei15
, Def. 1.2.7]
) and
choose the test function

\varphi^{\mathrm{stand}}_{\varepsilon}=\varrho\left(v_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{stand}}_{\varepsilon}V\right)
.

With these modifications for
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the proofs in

[
MRT14
, Thm. 4.1]
and
[
Rei15
, Thm. 2.1.1, Thm. 2.2.1]

can be easily adapted, and the proof of Theorem
3.1
is
complete.
