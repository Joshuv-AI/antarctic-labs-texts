Converging Periodic Boundary Conditions and Detection of Topological Gaps on Regular Hyperbolic Tessellations
Fabian R. Lux, Emil Prodan

Abstract
Tessellations of the hyperbolic spaces by regular polygons are becoming popular because they support discrete quantum and classical models displaying unique spectral and topological characteristics. Resolving the true bulk spectra and the thermodynamic response functions of these models requires converging periodic boundary conditions and our work delivers a practical solution for this open problem on generic { p , q } \{p,q\} -tessellations. This enables us to identify the true spectral gaps of bulk Hamiltonians and, as an application, we construct all but one topological models that deliver the topological gaps predicted by the K K -theory of the lattices. We demonstrate the emergence of the expected topological spectral flows whenever two such bulk models are deformed into each other and, additionally, we prove the emergence of topological channels whenever a soft physical interface is created between different topological classes of Hamiltonians.

In these new endeavors, scientists are facing challenges that requires entirely new tools of analysis, both theoretical and computational, and lack of such tools can hold a field hostage for years. For example, the lack of a systematic way to impose periodic boundary conditions (PBC) on hyperbolic lattices prevents us from resolving the true bulk spectra of the Hamiltonians, computing thermodynamic coefficients, correlations functions, bulk topological invariants and identifying topological gaps. We recall that the hyperbolic space-groups do not have finite-index abelian subgroups, as their Euclidean counterparts do, and they are non-amenable, hence the ratio between the numbers of boundary and bulk sites converges to a strictly positive value. Thus, suppressing the boundary states in finite-size samples is necessary but, unfortunately, not sufficient because convergence with the sample size cannot be taken for granted. For these reasons, resolving the true bulk characteristics of hyperbolic crystals remained an open problem.

If one is interested only in the bulk spectra, then a universal solution is to evaluate the local density of states at or near the center of the finite-size crystal with open boundary conditions

On the other hand, when PBCs can be systematically defined, they supply extremely fast convergences (typically exponential) with the size of the crystals, for both spectra and thermodynamic coefficients

Since the angles are rational, one can see how this can lead to torsion elements
(xy)^{p}
,
(yz)^{q}
and
(zx)^{2}
.
The triangle group above can be seen as the full space group symmetry of a potential hyperbolic crystal that we would like to construct.

The triangle group
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
is a special case of a Coxeter group, i.e., a group with presentation

\displaystyle\braket{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}|(g_{i}g_{j})^{m_{ij}}},

(15)

with the requirement that
m_{ii}=1
and
m_{ij}\geq 2
if
i\neq j
.
Which means that in our case, we have

\displaystyle m=\begin{pmatrix}1&p&2\\
p&1&q\\
2&q&1\end{pmatrix},

(16)

with respect to the generators
x
,
y
, and
z
.
One can summarize the structure of a Coxeter group with the help of its Coxeter graph, where
m
is interepreted as a weighted adjacency matrix and an edge is drawn between vertices if the weights is at least three.
This means in our case, we have

\Delta_{\{p,q\}}=\circ\overset{p}{-}\circ\overset{q}{-}\circ.

(17)

The goal is to find a faithful representation of
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
which allows us to introduce the concept of generalized periodic boundary conditions.
Define the
\mathbb{R}
vector space
V
with its basis vectors labelled by the generators of
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
, i.e.,
S=\{x,y,z\}\subset\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
.
A typical element
v
of
V
thus has the form
\bm{v}=a\bm{e}_{x}+b\bm{e}_{y}+c\bm{e}_{z}
.
Introduce the symmetric bilinear form
B\colon V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
by its action on the basis vectors

B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{e}_{s^{\prime}})\equiv-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{m_{ss^{\prime}}}\right).

(18)

It has the property that
B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{e}_{s})=1
and
B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{e}_{s^{\prime}})\leq 0
for
s\neq s^{\prime}
.
Define the subspaces

\displaystyle H_{s}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{v}\in V~|~B(\bm{v},\bm{e}_{s})=0\}\cong V\setminus\mathbb{R}\bm{e}_{s},

(19)

\displaystyle V_{ss^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}\bm{e}_{s}\oplus\mathbb{R}\bm{e}_{s^{\prime}}.

(20)

Each point of
H_{s}
is invariant under the action of
\sigma_{s}
, which can be seen directly from the definition of
\sigma_{s}
.
We can now attempt to construct a group action on
V
. Define
\sigma_{s}\colon V\to V
by setting

\sigma_{s}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\lambda}-B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s}.

(21)

In the following we want to sketch the proof of the following proposition

Proposition I.1
.

The assignment
s\mapsto\sigma_{s}
extends to a faithful representation
\sigma\colon\Delta_{\{p,q\}}\to\mathrm{GL}(V)
.

First of all, the group multiplication of
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
corresponds to the composition of operations acting on
V
, i.e., we interpret a word
s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\cdots s_{n}
as the composition
\sigma_{s_{1}}\circ\sigma_{s_{2}}\circ\cdots\sigma_{s_{n}}
.
This means we adhere to the convention that we “read” a word from right to left.
It is then sufficient to verify the relations
(\sigma_{s_{i}}\sigma_{s_{j}})^{m_{ij}}
in order to show that we indeed have a homomorphism onto
\mathrm{GL}(V)
.

By observing the action of
\sigma
on the basis vectors, we can identify the matrix representation.
Take
\bm{v}=\sum_{i=x,y,z}v_{i}\bm{e}_{i}
.
The application of
\sigma_{s}
yields

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}\bm{v}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=x,y,z}v_{j}(\sigma_{s}\bm{e}_{j})=\sum_{i=x,y,z}\left(\sum_{j=x,y,z}(\sigma_{s})_{ij}v_{j}\right)\bm{e}_{i},

(22)

where we can identify

\displaystyle(\sigma_{s})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}-2B(\bm{e}_{i},\bm{e}_{j})\delta_{si}

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+2\cos(\pi/m_{ij})\delta_{si}.

(23)

Note that this construction underlies the equivalence of categories between the category of group modules and the category of group representations over a given field
\mathbb{K}
:

\mathbf{Rep(G)}_{\mathbb{K}}\rightleftarrows\mathbf{GMod}_{\mathbb{K}}.

(24)

In retrospect, we therefore have indeed constructed a suitable
\mathbb{R}\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
-module before extracting the corresponding representation.
In matrix notation, we find

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2\cos(\alpha)&2\cos(\gamma)\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(25)

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
2\cos(\alpha)&-1&2\cos(\beta)\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
2\cos(\gamma)&2\cos(\beta)&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

To get Mennicke’s original result
Mennicke1967
, replace
\sigma_{s}\to-\sigma_{s}^{T}
.
By construction, one has
\sigma_{s}\bm{e}_{s}=-\bm{e}_{s}
, which we expect from a reflection.
Note also that
\det\sigma_{s}=-1
.
Further, one finds

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(\sigma_{s}\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{s}(\bm{\lambda}-2B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s})

\displaystyle=\bm{\lambda}-4B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s}+4B(\bm{e}_{s},B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s})

\displaystyle=\bm{\lambda}-4B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s}+4B(\bm{e}_{s},\alpha_{s})B(\bm{e}_{s},\bm{\lambda})\bm{e}_{s}

\displaystyle=\bm{\lambda},

(28)

and therefore
\sigma_{s}^{2}=1
, verifying the diagonal Coxeter relations.
Further, one finds that
\sigma_{xy}=\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}

has eigenvalues
1,e^{\pm 2i\alpha}
and is therefore of order
p
.
Similarly,
\sigma_{yz}
has eigenvalues
1,e^{\pm 2i\beta}
and is of order
q
, while
\sigma_{xz}
has eigenvalues
1,e^{\pm 2i\gamma}
and therefore has order

.
Therefore
\sigma
respects to relations of
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
.
For a proof of the faithfulness of the representation
\sigma
, we refer to the book Humphreys (
HumphreysBook1990
Section 5.4 p. 113).

Representation of proper triangle groups

The proper triangle group is defined as

I.5
Understanding the number field which underlies the geometric representation

Our goal is to find the minimal field extension of the rationals which is capable of describing the generators of the geometric representation of
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
.
To do so, we first need to introduce some notation.
Let
T_{n}\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
represent the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind, i.e.,

T_{n}(\cos\theta)=\cos n\theta
for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
and introduce the re-scaled polynomials
P_{n}(x)=2T_{n}(x/2)
.
Since
T_{n}
fulfills the recursion relations
T_{0}(x)=1
,
T_{1}(x)=x
and
T_{n+1}(x)=2xT_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x)
,
P_{n}
obeys

\displaystyle P_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=2

(48)

\displaystyle P_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x

(49)

\displaystyle P_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=xP_{n}(x)-P_{n-1}(x).

(50)

In particular, this implies that
P_{n}(x)
(for
n>0
) is a polynomial of degree
n
with leading term
1x^{n}
. All coefficients from
P_{n}(x)
are integer such that
P_{n}(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
.
Since the product of Chebyshev polynomials is given by
T_{n}(x)T_{m}(x)=(T_{n+m}(x)-T_{|n-m|}(x))/2
, the corresponding rule
for
P_{n}
reads

P_{n}(x)P_{m}(x)=P_{n+m}(x)-P_{|n-m|}(x).

(51)

We can now make the following observation:

Proposition I.3
.

\xi_{n}=2\cos(2\pi/n)
is an algebraic number.

Proof.

Using the properties of the Chebyshev polynomial, one has

T_{n}(\xi_{n}/2)=\cos(n2\pi/n)=1.

(52)

Rearranging this result, we find that
h(\xi)=2T_{n}(\xi_{n}/2)-2=P_{n}(\xi_{n})-2=0
.
Therefore
\xi
is a root of the polynomial
h(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
and is therefore algebraic.
∎

It is now tempting to assume that
{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{n}]
is a free module with basis
\xi_{n}^{0},\xi_{n}^{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{n-1}
.
However, there are in general more relations among the
\xi_{n}
since the polynomial
h(x)
is in fact not irreducible over the integers.
For example, if
n=2s
, we can directly find another relation.
This is because
T_{s}(\xi_{2s}/2)=\cos(s\pi/s)=-1
or equivalently
P_{s}(\xi)+2=0
.
By the multiplication rule, and using
P_{0}(x)=2
, one finds

\displaystyle P_{s}(x)P_{s}(x)=P_{2s}(x)-2=h(x),

(53)

which shows quite explicitly that it can be possible to split up the polynomial further over the integers.

A full reduction of the polynomial can be accomplished which follows from the properties of cyclotomic fields which we will very briefly introduce.
Denote the
n
-th root of unity by
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
.
Then
\xi_{n}=\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1}
.
The field extension
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n})
is known as the cyclotomic field.
The algebraic number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1})
is the maximal real subfield of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{n})
.
It contains
{\mathbb{Z}}[\zeta_{n}+\zeta_{n}^{-1}]
as its ring of integers and it is this ring that we would like to understand better.

By definition,
\zeta_{n}
is a root of the polynomial
x^{n}-1
.
If
n
is divisible by
d
, then
\zeta_{d}
is another root of
x^{n}-1
but also of
x^{d}-1
.
An
n
-th root of unity is primitive if it is not also an
m
-th root of unity for
m<n
.
The primitive
n
-th roots of unity are therefore given by

\mathcal{R}_{n}=\{\zeta_{n}^{k}~|~1\leq k\leq n,\mathrm{gcd}(k,n)=1\}.

(54)

One has
|\mathcal{R}_{n}|=\varphi(n)
, where
\varphi(n)
is Euler’s totient function, counting the positive integers smaller or equal to
n
which are coprime to
n
.
Define the polynomial

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(x)=\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(x-\zeta).

(55)

This polynomial is also known as the cyclotomic polynomial.
The roots of
\Phi_{n}(x)
are given by the primitive
n
-th roots of unity and
\Phi_{n}(x)
has degree
\varphi(n)
.

\Phi_{n}(x)
is known to be irreducible in both
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

A polynomial
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
is called palindromic if
f(x)^{\ast}\equiv x^{n}f(x^{-1})=f(x)
, where
n
is the degree of
f
and it turns out that

Proposition I.4
.

\Phi_{n}(x)
is palindromic for
n>2
.

Proof.

We check that the condition for palindromic polynomials is fulfilled:

\displaystyle x^{\varphi(n)}\Phi_{n}(x^{-1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(1-\zeta x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}\zeta\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(\zeta^{-1}-x).

(56)

For
n>2
, the product
\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}\zeta
evaluates to
1
.
This is because for
\xi_{n}^{k}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
one also has
\xi^{n-k}_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
which cancel each other mutually in the product.
Since
\varphi(n)\mod 2=0
for
n>2
, one can then write

\displaystyle x^{\varphi(n)}\Phi_{n}(x^{-1})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\varphi(n)}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(x-\zeta^{-1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}(x-\zeta)=\Phi_{n}(x),

(57)

and
\Phi_{n}(x)
is palindromic.
∎

Proposition I.5
.

Let
f(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
be palindromic, i.e,
f(x)^{\ast}\equiv x^{2n}f(x^{-1})=f(x)
, where
2n
is the degree of
f
.
Then there exists a
g(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
such that
x^{-n}f(x)=g(x+x^{-1})
and where
g
is of degree
n
.

Proof.

Write
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}a_{k}x^{k}
with
a_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Since
f
is palindromic, one has
a_{2n-k}=a_{k}
.
Then we give an explicit construction for

, by

\displaystyle x^{-n}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}a_{k}x^{k-n}

\displaystyle=a_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k+n}(x^{k}+x^{-k})

\displaystyle=a_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k+n}P_{k}(x+x^{-1}).

(58)

The last step follows from the identity
P_{m}(x+x^{-1})=x^{m}+x^{-m}
.
Therefore, the right-hand side is a polynomial in
x+x^{-1}
with integer coefficients and it is of degree
n
.
∎

, in order to introduce a ring-structure
\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2}
and therefore obtain a ring-isomorphism
(\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}],\cdot,+)\cong(\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2},\star,+)
, where
\cdot
and
+
denote the product and addition of polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}]
.
While the addition on
\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2}
is given by the natural element-wise addition of the individual integer coefficients (which we denote by the same symbol for convenience), the induced
\star
-product on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\varphi(n)/2}
is nontrivial due to the intricate back-folding.
We want the
\star
-product to be consistent with the ring-structure of
\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}]
and therefore require that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(n)/2}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}]\times\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{Z}[\xi_{n}]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi\times\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\star}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi^{-1}}

(68)

This means that for
\bm{c},\bm{c}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\varphi(n)/2}
we define

\displaystyle\bm{c}\star\bm{c}^{\prime}=\psi^{-1}(\psi(\bm{c})\cdot\psi(\bm{c}^{\prime})).

(69)

To conclude the discussion, we can define the general linear group as

\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])=\{g\in\mathcal{M}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])~|~g~\text{invertible}\}.

(70)

and the special linear group by

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])~|~\det g=1\}.

(71)

Note that invertibility in
\mathcal{M}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])
is nontrivial since
{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}]
is not a field: for
g\in\mathcal{M}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])
to be invertible, one needs
\det g
to be invertible in
{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}]
.
Given these definitions, we reach the following conclusion:

Proposition I.11
.

The image of the geometric representation
\sigma
lands in
\mathrm{GL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])
.
We therefore realize
\Delta_{\{p,q\}}
as a subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])
and

\Delta_{\{p,q\}}^{+}
as a subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(3,{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}])
.

Proof.

Due to the identities

\displaystyle P_{p}(\xi_{2pq})

\displaystyle=2T_{p}(\xi_{2pq}/2)=2\cos(\beta),

(72)

\displaystyle P_{q}(\xi_{2pq})

\displaystyle=2T_{q}(\xi_{2pq}/2)=2\cos(\alpha),

(73)

the matrix elements of the generators in the geometric representation take values in the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[\xi_{2pq}]\subset{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi_{2pq})
.
∎

Using these two identities, we can re-interpret the previously obtained matrix matrix representation in the following way:

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-1&P_{q}(\xi_{2pq})&0\\
0&1&0\\

(74)

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
P_{q}(\xi_{2pq})&-1&P_{p}(\xi_{2pq})\\

(75)

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\

(76)

And we again set

and

.

I.6
Definition of the Y-junction

In this section, we explain the details on how the Y-junction in the manuscript is constructed.
The goal is to separate the hyperbolic disk into three distinct regions with different topological phases, by assigning the three Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(1-2P_{A})+(1-\epsilon)\Delta

(83)

\displaystyle H_{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(1-2P_{B})+(1-\epsilon)\Delta

(84)

\displaystyle H_{3}

\displaystyle=\epsilon(1-2P_{C})+(1-\epsilon)\Delta,

(85)

where
\epsilon=0.8
and where
\Delta=(A+B)/4+\mathrm{h.c.}
, is the Laplace operator.
As a first step, we define three regions
Y_{i}
bounded by three geodesics
\gamma_{i}
which originate at
z=0
and asymptotically meet the following points at infinity:

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi_{Y}}

(86)

\displaystyle Y_{2}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i/3}Y_{1}^{\infty}

(87)

\displaystyle Y_{3}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i/3}Y_{2}^{\infty},

(88)

where
\phi_{Y}
determines the alignment of the junction.
We consider only the case
\phi_{Y}=\alpha/2
in the manuscript.
Let
\arg z
be the argument of the complex number
z
.
We take
\arg
to be the principal branch
-\pi<\arg z\leq\pi
.
We can use
\arg z
, to assign the desired local phase of our Y-junction.
First, we define the relative angles

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}(z)=\arg z^{\ast}Y_{i}^{\infty},

(89)

and then we make the assignment

\displaystyle p(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{for}~\kappa_{1}(z)\leq 0~\text{and}~\kappa_{2}(z)>0\\
2,&\text{for}~\kappa_{2}(z)\leq 0~\text{and}~\kappa_{3}(z)>0\\
3,&\text{for}~\kappa_{3}(z)\leq 0~\text{and}~\kappa_{1}(z)>0\end{array}\right.

The discontinuous function
p(z)
therefore assigns a label to each of three sectors which we want to equip with the respective Hamiltonians.
In other words, if the
z
-coordinate is deep into region
p(z)=i
, we want the local Hamiltonian to resemble our previously defined
H_{i}
.
However, instead of an abrupt junction of the different phases, we rather want to consider a smooth transition from one phase to another with a controllable parameter
\ell
for the width of the domain wall region.
To accomplish this, we define the Hamiltonian of the resulting Y-junction by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{Y}}(z,z^{\prime})=\sum_{l=1}^{3}\chi_{l}(\mu(z,z^{\prime}))H_{i}(z,z^{\prime}),

(93)

where
\chi_{l}
is a smooth partition of unity:

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{3}\chi_{l}(z)=1.

(94)

Note that we now passed to a representation of the Hamiltonian which refers to coordinates in the hyperbolic disk.
This is possible, by assigning to each group element
g
of
\Delta^{+}_{\{p,q\}}
(or one of its finite subgroups) a position
gz_{0}
, where
g
acts via Moebius transformation on the seed coordinate
z_{0}
.
The function
\mu(z,z^{\prime})
assigns the geodesic midpoint of
z
and
z
’, were we refer to the following theorem:

Theorem I.12

(Wang et al.
Wang2019
)
.

Let
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}
, then the hyperbolic midpoint
\mu(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{D}
along the geodesic from
z
to
z
’ with the property
d(z,\mu)=d(z^{\prime},\mu)=d(z,z^{\prime})/2
is given by

\mu(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{z^{\prime}(1-|z|^{2})+z(1-|z^{\prime}|^{2})}{1-|z|^{2}|z^{\prime}|^{2}+A[z,z^{\prime}]\sqrt{(1-|z|^{2})(1-|z^{\prime}|^{2})}},

(95)

where
A[z,z^{\prime}]
is the Ahlfors bracket

A[z,z^{\prime}]=(1-|z|^{2})(1-|z^{\prime}|^{2})+|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}.

(96)

A natural way to construct the partition
\chi_{l}
explicitly can be obtained by constructing the shortest distance into and out-of a given phase region.
Whenever
-\pi/2<\kappa_{i}(z)<\pi/2
we can construct a right triangle in the hyperbolic plane to calculate the shortest geodesic distance to the phase boundary
Y_{i}
, which we denote by
\delta_{i}(z)
.
If
\kappa_{i}(z)
is outside of this region, the shortest distance
\delta_{i}(z)
to the phase boundary is a straight line from
z
to 0.
In combination with the law of hyperbolic sines it then follows that

\displaystyle\sinh\delta_{i}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\sin\varphi_{i}|\sinh d(0,|z|)&,|\kappa_{i}(z)|<\frac{\pi}{2}\\
\sinh d(0,|z|)&,\text{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.

Using these tools, we can define
D_{i}(z)
as the shortest, signed, distance out-of, or into phase region
i
depending on the desired region.
Recall that the label of desired local phase is given by
p(z)\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Now we can define

\displaystyle D_{i}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\min(\delta_{i+1}(z),\delta_{i+2}(z)),&i=p(z)\\
\delta_{i}(z).\end{array}\right.

where it is understood that
\delta_{3+k}(z)=\delta_{k}(z)
, for
0\leq k\leq 2
.
Then we define sigmoids

\sigma_{i}(z)=(1+e^{D_{i}(z)/\ell})^{-1},

(101)

where
\ell
controls the width of the transition region.
If
z
is contained deep in phase region
k
we are guaranteed that
\sigma_{k}(z)\approx 1
, while
\sigma_{i}(z)\approx 0
for the other phases
i\neq k
.
We are finally ready to define

\displaystyle\chi_{i}(z)=\frac{\sigma_{i}(z)}{\sigma_{1}(z)+\sigma_{2}(z)+\sigma_{3}(z)}.

(102)

In figure Fig.(
6
), we give an example of how the partitions look like for
\ell=0.1,0.25,0.5
. The partition used in the main text is generated with
\ell=0.1
.
