Neural Causal Models for Counterfactual Identification and Estimation
Kevin Xia and Yushu Pan and Elias Bareinboim

Abstract
Evaluating hypothetical statements about how the world would be had a different course of action been taken is arguably one key capability expected from modern AI systems. Counterfactual reasoning underpins discussions in fairness, the determination of blame and responsibility, credit assignment, and regret. In this paper, we study the evaluation of counterfactual statements through neural models. Specifically, we tackle two causal problems required to make such evaluations, i.e., counterfactual identification and estimation from an arbitrary combination of observational and experimental data. First, we show that neural causal models (NCMs) are expressive enough and encode the structural constraints necessary for performing counterfactual reasoning. Second, we develop an algorithm for simultaneously identifying and estimating counterfactual distributions. We show that this algorithm is sound and complete for deciding counterfactual identification in general settings. Third, considering the practical implications of these results, we introduce a new strategy for modeling NCMs using generative adversarial networks. Simulations corroborate with the proposed methodology.

Introduction

Counterfactual reasoning is one of human’s high-level cognitive capabilities, used across a wide range of affairs, including determining how objects interact, assigning responsibility, credit and blame, and articulating explanations. Counterfactual statements underpin prototypical questions of the form "what if–" and "why–", which inquire about hypothetical worlds that have not necessarily been realized
(
Pearl & Mackenzie 2018
)
.
If a patient Alice had taken a drug and died, one may wonder, "why did Alice die?"; "was it the drug that killed her?"; "would she be alive had she not taken the drug?".
In the context of fairness, why did an applicant, Joe, not get the job offer? Would the outcome have changed had Joe been a Ph.D.? Or perhaps of a different race? These are examples of fundamental questions about
attribution
and
explanation
, which evoke hypothetical scenarios that disagree with the current reality and which not even experimental studies can reconstruct.

We build on the semantics of counterfactuals based on a generative process called
structural causal model
(SCM)
(
Pearl 2000
)
.
A fully instantiated SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
describes a collection of causal mechanisms and distribution over exogenous conditions. Each
\mathcal{M}^{*}
induces families of qualitatively different distributions
related to the activities of seeing (called observational), doing (interventional), and imagining (counterfactual), which together are known as the
ladder of causation

(
Pearl & Mackenzie 2018
;
Bareinboim et al. 2022
)
; also called the
Pearl Causal Hierarchy
(PCH).
The PCH is a containment hierarchy in which distributions can be put in increasingly refined layers: observational content goes into layer 1 (
\mathcal{L}_{1}
); experimental to layer 2 (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
); counterfactual to layer 3 (
\mathcal{L}_{3}
).
It is understood that there are questions about layers 2 and 3 that cannot be answered (i.e. are underdetermined), even given all information in the world about layer 1; further, layer 3 questions are still underdetermined given data from layers 1 and 2

(
Bareinboim et al. 2022
;
Ibeling & Icard 2020
)
.

Counterfactuals represent the more detailed, finest type of knowledge encoded in the PCH, so naturally, having the ability to evaluate counterfactual distributions is an attractive proposition. In practice, a fully specified model
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is almost never observable, which leads to the question – how can a counterfactual statement, from
\mathcal{L}_{3}^{*}
, be evaluated using a combination of observational and experimental data (from
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}
)? This question embodies the challenge of cross-layer inferences, which entail solving two challenging causal problems in tandem, identification and estimation.

This work introduced an object we leverage throughout this paper called
Neural Causal Model
(NCM, for short), which is a class of SCMs constrained to neural network functions and fixed distributions over the exogenous variables. While NCMs have been shown to be able to solve the identification and estimation tasks for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries, their potential for counterfactual inferences is still largely unexplored, and existing implementations have been constrained to low-dimensional settings.

Despite all the progress achieved so far, no practical methods exist for estimating counterfactual (
\mathcal{L}_{3}
) distributions in the general setting where an arbitrary combination of observational (
\mathcal{L}_{1}
) and experimental (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) distributions is available, and unobserved confounders exist (i.e. Markovianity does not hold). Hence, in addition to providing the first neural method of counterfactual identification, this paper establishes the first general counterfactual estimation technique even among non-neural methods, leveraging the neural toolkit for scalable inferences.
Specifically, our contributions are:
1. We prove that when fitted with a graphical inductive bias, the NCM encodes the
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-constraints necessary for performing counterfactual inference (Thm.
1
), and that they are still expressive enough to model the underlying data-generating model, which is not necessarily a neural network (Thm.
2
).

2. We show that counterfactual identification within a neural proxy model setting is equivalent to established symbolic approaches (Thm.
3
). We leverage this duality to develop an optimization procedure (Alg.
1
) for counterfactual identification and estimation that is both sound and complete (Corol.
2
). The approach is general in that it accepts any combination of inputs from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, it works in any causal diagram setting, and it does not require the Markovianity assumption to hold.

3. We develop a new approach to modeling the NCM using generative adversarial networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, capable of robustly scaling inferences to high dimensions (Alg.
3
). We then show how GAN-NCMs can solve the challenging optimization problems in identifying and estimating counterfactuals in practice.
Experiments are provided in Sec. 5 and proofs in Appendix
A
.

Preliminaries.

We now introduce the notation and definitions used throughout the paper. We use uppercase letters (
X
) to denote random variables and lowercase letters (

) to denote corresponding values. Similarly, bold uppercase (

) and lower case (

\mathcal{D}_{X}

for the domain of
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\}

P(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})

We utilize the basic semantic framework of structural causal models (SCMs), as defined in
(
Pearl 2000
, Ch. 7)
.
An SCM

consists of endogenous variables
\mathbf{V}
, exogenous variables
\mathbf{U}
with distribution
P(\mathbf{U})
, and mechanisms
\mathcal{F}
.
\mathcal{F}
contains a function
f_{V_{i}}
for each variable
V_{i}
that maps endogenous parents
\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}
and exogenous parents
\mathbf{U}_{V_{i}}
to
V_{i}

\mathcal{M}
induces a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}

V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}
is a vertex, there is a directed arrow
(V_{j}\rightarrow V_{i})
for every
V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}
and
V_{j}\in\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}

(V_{j}\dashleftarrow\dashrightarrow V_{i})
for every pair
V_{i},V_{j}\in\mathbf{V}
such that
\mathbf{U}_{V_{i}}
and
\mathbf{U}_{V_{j}}

(
Bareinboim et al. 2022
, Def. 13/16, Thm. 4)

\mathbf{U}_{V_{i}}

\mathbf{V}

For instance, if an SCM

There also exist constraints of layer 3 that are important for counterfactual inferences.

To motivate the use of such constraints, consider an example inspired by the multi-armed bandit problem. A casino has 3 slot machines, labeled “0", “1", and “2". Every day, the casino assigns one machine a good payout, one a bad payout, and one an average payout, with chances of winning represented by exogenous variables

U_{-}

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}&=\{U_{M},U_{+},U_{=},U_{-}\},U_{M}\in\{0,1,2\},U_{+},U_{=},U_{-}\in\{0,1\}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},X\in\{0,1,2\},Y\in\{0,1\}\\
\mathcal{F}&=\begin{cases}f_{X}(u_{M})&=u_{M}\\
f_{Y}(x,u_{M},u_{+},u_{=},u_{-})&=\begin{cases}u_{=}&x=u_{M}\\
u_{-}&x=(u_{M}-1)\%3\\
u_{+}&x=(u_{M}+1)\%3\end{cases}\end{cases}\\

Neural Counterfactual Identification

The problem of identification is concerned with determining whether a certain quantity is computable from a combination of assumptions, usually encoded in the form of a causal diagram, and a collection of distributions
(
Pearl 2000
, p. 77)
.
This challenge stems from the fact that even though the space of SCMs (or NCMs) is constrained upon assuming a certain causal diagram, the quantity of interest may still be underdetermined.
In words, there are many SCMs compatible with the same diagram
\mathcal{G}
but generate different answers for the target distribution.
In this section, we investigate the problem of identification and decide whether counterfactual quantities (from
\mathcal{L}_{3}

\mathcal{L}_{2}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
datasets together with
\mathcal{G}
, as formally defined next.

NeuralID
– Identifying/estimating counterfactual queries with NCMs.

Building on these results, we demonstrate through the procedure
NeuralID
(Alg.
1
) how to decide the identifiability of counterfactual quantities. The specific optimization procedure searches explicitly in the space of NCMs for two models that respectively minimize and maximize the target query while maintaining consistency with the provided data distributions in
\mathbb{Z}
. If the two models match in the target query
Q
, then the effect is identifiable, and the value is returned; otherwise, the effect is non-identifiable.

The implementation of how to enforce these consistency constraints in practice is somewhat challenging. We note two nontrivial details that are abstracted away in the description of Alg.
1
. First, although training to fit a single, observational dataset is straightforward, it is not as clear how to simultaneously maintain consistency with the multiple datasets in
\mathbb{Z}
. Second, unlike with simpler interventional queries, it is not clear how to search the parameter space in a way that maximizes or minimizes a counterfactual query, which may be more involved due to nesting (e.g.
P(Y_{Z_{X=0}})
) or evaluating the same variable in multiple worlds (e.g.
P(Y_{X=0},Y_{X=1})
). The details of how to solve these issues are discussed in Sec.
4
.

Interestingly, this approach is qualitatively different than the case of classical, symbolic methods that avoid operating in the space of SCMs directly. Still, in principle, this alternative approach does not imply any loss in functionality, as evident from the next result.

Conclusions

We developed in this work a neural approach to the problems of counterfactual identification and estimation using neural causal models (NCMs).
Specifically, we first showed that with the graphical inductive bias, NCMs are capable of encoding counterfactual (
\mathcal{L}_{3}
) constraints and are still expressive so as to represent any generating SCM (Thms.
1
,
2
).
We then showed that NCMs have the ability of solving any counterfactual identification instance (Thm.
3
, Corol
1
).
Given these theoretical properties, we introduced a sound and complete algorithm (Alg.
1
, Corol.
2
) for identifying and estimating counterfactuals in general non-Markovian settings given arbitrary datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. We developed an approach
based on GANs
to implement this algorithm in practice
(Alg.

P^{\widehat{M}_{1}}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})=P^{\widehat{M}_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
. This holds if and only if
P^{\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min})}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})=P^{\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\max})}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
. If they are not equal, then
\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min})
and
\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\max})
are examples of two NCMs that match in
\mathcal{G}
and the correct
\mathbb{Z}
but not in
Q
. Otherwise, all
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs that match in
\mathbb{Z}
must also match in
Q
, since any NCM that induces a result for
Q
that is less than
P^{\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min})}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
or greater than
P^{\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min})}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
would contradict the optimality of
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\max}
.

If
Q
is identifiable, then Corol.
1
guarantees that any
\mathcal{G}
-NCM
\widehat{M}
that induces the correct
\mathbb{Z}
will match
\mathcal{M}^{*}
in
Q
(i.e.
P^{\widehat{M}}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})=P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
).

B.2
Nested Counterfactuals

A more general definition of counterfactuals can be defined as shown below (see
(
Correa et al. 2021
)
for a more detailed discussion).

10
Layer 3 Valuation (General).

An SCM
\mathcal{M}
induces layer
\mathcal{L}_{3}(\mathcal{M})
, a set of distributions over
\mathbf{V}
, each with the form
P(\mathbf{Y}_{*})=P(\mathbf{Y}_{1[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{1}]},\mathbf{Y}_{2[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{2}],\dots})
such that

\displaystyle P^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{y}_{1[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{1}]},\mathbf{y}_{2[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{2}]},\dots)=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}}\mathbbm{1}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{1[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{1}]}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{2}]}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{y}_{2},\dots\right]dP(\mathbf{u}),

(6)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
is a collection of functions defined over variables
\mathbf{X}_{i}
(such that we have
\tilde{f}_{X}
mapping from some
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{X}\subseteq\mathbf{U}
to
\mathcal{D}_{X}
for each
\tilde{f}_{X}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
), and
{\mathbf{Y}}_{i[\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}]}(\mathbf{u})
(also called
potential response

(
Pearl 2000
, Def. 7.4.1)
) is the solution for
\mathbf{Y}
after evaluating

\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}}:=\{f_{V_{j}}:V_{j}\in\mathbf{V}\setminus\mathbf{X}\}\cup\{f_{X}\leftarrow\tilde{f}_{X}:\tilde{f}_{X}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}\}
with
\mathbf{u}
.

\blacksquare

\mathbf{Y}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}
is considered a natural intervention if each
\tilde{f}_{X}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
is a potential response for
X
in
\mathcal{M}
. If
\tilde{f}_{X}
for variable
X
is set to some fixed value
x
, then that corresponds to an atomic intervention, which is what is considered in Def.
10
. However, one could also have a natural intervention where
\tilde{f}_{X}:=X_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}}(\mathbf{u})
for some
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
, defined recursively. In other words, the counterfactual terms may be
nested
.

For example, consider the term
P(Y_{X=1,W_{X=0}}=1)
in the natural direct effect (NDE) query. This would be evaluated as

\displaystyle P(Y_{X=1,W_{X=0}}=1)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}}\mathbbm{1}\left[Y_{X=1,W_{X=0}}(\mathbf{u})=1\right]dP(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}}\mathbbm{1}\left[Y_{X=1,W=W_{X=0}(\mathbf{u})}(\mathbf{u})=1\right]dP(\mathbf{u}).

Intuitively, we are aggregating all settings of
\mathbf{u}
in which
Y=1
under the intervention where
X
is fixed to
1
and
W
is fixed to the value that it would take under the same setting of
\mathbf{u}
under the intervention where
X
is fixed to
0
.

It turns out that all nested counterfactuals can be unrolled into an unnested form via the Counterfactual Unnesting Theorem (CUT).

5
Counterfactual Unnesting Theorem,
(
Correa et al. 2021
, Thm. 1)
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
be any natural interventions on disjoint sets
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
. Then for
\mathbf{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
disjoint from
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Z}
, we have

P(\mathbf{Y}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}}=\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{X}}}P(\mathbf{Y}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}},\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{y},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{x})

(7)

This theorem simplifies the scope of theoretical studies on counterfactual identification, and indeed, one could perform inferences on such queries using the NCM by performing it on the terms of the unnested version. However, the proxy model nature of the NCM allows it to directly evaluate nested counterfactuals according to the definition. In the case of
P(Y_{X=1,W_{X=0}}=1)
, for example, this can be estimated by sampling several instances of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
, evaluating
W
in those instances under the intervention
X=0
, and then subsequently evaluating
Y
under the intervention
X=1
and with
W
set to the corresponding values obtained previously. This dramatically simplifies the evaluation of nested counterfactuals, which otherwise would have required an analytical understanding to unnest the query manually, followed by requiring an evaluation on all terms in the sum in the r.h.s. of Eq.
7
. In practice, this sum takes a long time (possibly intractable in continuous cases) to compute, with compounding error in each term. A visualization of the improvement is shown in Fig.

B.3
Data Generation

To generate data for our experiments, we use an implementation of what we call a
regional canonical model
, inspired by canonical SCMs
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

11
Regional Canonical Model (RCM).

Given a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
, a
regional canonical model

\mathcal{M}_{\rcm}
associated with
\mathcal{G}
is an SCM
\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U}_{\rcm},\mathcal{F}_{\rcm},P(\mathbf{U}_{\rcm})\rangle
where:

1.

\mathbf{U}_{\rcm}=\{R_{\mathbf{C}}:\forall\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{G})\}
, where
\mathbb{C}(\mathcal{G})
is the set of maximally confounded cliques of
\mathcal{G}
and
R_{\mathbf{C}}\sim\unif(0,1)
for all
R_{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbf{U}_{\rcm}
.

2.

For each
V\in\mathbf{V}
, denote
\mathbf{R}_{V}=\{R_{\mathbf{C}}:\forall\mathbf{C}\text{ s.t. }V\in\mathbf{C}\}
. Then, each
V
is associated with a set of regions
\mathbf{I}_{V}=\{\mathbf{I}_{V,i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
such that each
\mathbf{I}_{V,i}=\{[a,b]_{\mathbf{C},i}:R_{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbf{R}_{V}\}
with
a,b\in[0,1]
,
a<b
. In words, each
\mathbf{I}_{V,i}
is a collection of intervals within 0 to 1 for each of the exogenous parents in
\mathbf{R}_{V}
. Furthermore, each
\mathbf{I}_{V,i}
is associated with a corresponding function
h_{V,i}:\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{Pa}_{V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{V}
mapping to values of
V
from its parents (in
\mathcal{G}
). Here,
r
is a hyperparameter which decides the complexity of the model.

3.

Each
f^{\rcm}_{V}\in\mathcal{F}_{\rcm}
is defined as

f^{\rcm}_{V}(\mathbf{pa}_{V},\mathbf{r}_{V})=\begin{cases}h_{V,i^{*}}(\mathbf{pa}_{V})&i^{*}\text{ is the largest }i\text{ s.t. }r_{\mathbf{C}}\in[a,b]_{\mathbf{C},i}\quad\forall r_{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbf{r}_{V}\\
h_{V,\textsc{default}}(\mathbf{pa}_{V})&\text{ if no such }i^{*}\text{ exists.}\end{cases}

Explaining this model in words, a uniformly random exogenous variable is assigned to each confounded clique (
R_{\mathbf{C}}
), and all variables within that clique will take that variable as an exogenous input to its functions. Each variable
V
is then associated with a set of exogenous variables
\mathbf{R}_{V}
depending on the cliques of which it is a member. The space of the domains of
\mathbf{R}_{V}
form a unit hypercube with dimension
|\mathbf{R}_{V}|
, and each
V
is assigned
r
rectangular “regions” in this hypercube (
\mathbf{I}_{V}
). Each region is associated with a function for
V
(
h_{V,i}
) given its parents, which is the behavior that describes
f^{\rcm}_{V}
when the inputted
\mathbf{r}_{V}
falls within that region. If
\mathbf{r}_{V}
does not fall into any of the
r
regions, then
f^{\rcm}_{V}
will take a default behavior described by
h_{V,\textsc{default}}
instead.

For our experimental settings, we randomly sample an RCM given the graph
\mathcal{G}
to be the ground truth,
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, with
r=20
. Each interval
[a,b]_{\mathbf{C},i}
is sampled by sampling two numbers from
\unif(0,1)
and sorting them. Since all variables are binary vectors,
h_{V,\textsc{default}}
and each
h_{V,i}
map each possible input of
\mathbf{pa}_{V}
to a randomly sampled binary vector (each bit distributed
\textsf{Bernoulli}(0.5)
) corresponding to a value of
V
. All variables in the simulations are single-dimensional binary variables except for
Z
, which is a
d
-dimensional binary vector depending on the setting of
d
in the experiment. To simulate the ground truth, samples can be drawn from the canonical SCM using the same procedure from Def.
1
.

Similar to the canonical SCM in
Zhang et al. 2022
, the RCM assigns different functional behavior to each variable given its endogenous parents depending on the value of their exogenous parents. As implicated in the proof of Thm. 2.3 in
Zhang et al. 2022
, such a model is very expressive with sufficiently large variation in the space of exogenous parents, eventually being able to express any SCM on all three layers. In our case, this can be achieved with a large enough choice of
r
. This provides a richer experimental environment when compared to choosing a fixed ground truth
\mathcal{M}^{*}
or sampling
\mathcal{M}^{*}
from a more restricted parametric family of models (which may produce biased results depending on the family).

B.4
Hypothesis Testing

Alg.
1
is a search within the
\Omega(\mathcal{G})
space for two
NCM
parameterizations,
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\min}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\max}
, that minimizes/maximizes the desired query, respectively. After optimization is finished, the corresponding
P(\mathbf{y}_{*}\mid\mathbf{x}_{*})
of the two models can be compared to determine whether an effect is identifiable. With perfect optimization and unbounded resources, these two quantities will be equal in identifiable cases. In practice, the two quantities are unlikely to be exactly equal due to noise in the training procedure, so we rely on a hypothesis testing step such as

|f(\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{max}}))-f(\widehat{M}(\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{min}}))|<\tau

(8)

for quantity of interest
f
and a certain threshold
\tau
. This threshold is similar to a significance level in statistics and can be adjusted depending on the severity of implications of false positives or negatives. In general,
\tau
can be determined by analyzing empirical results in which the identification ground truth is known, and we choose
\tau=0.05
in our experiments for all cases.

GAN-NCM Experiments

In this section, we describe the architecture of the implicit GAN-NCM as described in Sec.
4
.

B.5.1
Model Architecture

The implementation of the GAN-NCM used in the experiments specifically utilizes Wasserstein GANs with gradient penalty
(
Gulrajani et al. 2017
)
. This choice was made to avoid the problem of mode collapse, a frequent problem that arises when training GANs. Wasserstein GANs (WGANs)
(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
minimize Earth-Mover distance via the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
(
Villani 2009
)
, producing the objective:

\min_{G}\max_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{r}}[D(\mathbf{x})]-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim P_{g}}[D(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})],

(9)

where
G
is a generating model,
D
is a discriminatory model (called a critic in this instance),
\mathcal{D}_{D}
is the set of 1-Lipshitz functions,
P_{r}
is a real distribution (i.e. from
\mathcal{M}^{*}
), and
P_{g}
is the distribution induced by
G
.

Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) removes the hard 1-Lipschitz constraint over
\mathcal{D}_{D}
and enforces this constraint softly by using an additional penalty term on the gradient of the critic in the objective, namely

L=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim P_{g}}[D(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{r}}[D(\mathbf{x})]+\lambda_{\gp}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\sim P_{\gp}}\left[\left(\left\lVert\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}D(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\right\rVert_{2}-1\right)^{2}\right],

(10)

where data points from
P_{\gp}
is sampled uniformly along straight lines between pairs of points sampled from
P_{g}
and
P_{r}
, and
\lambda_{\gp}
is a hyperparameter regulating the strength of this penalty. This objective is minimized in the optimization of the critic model. In our case, we use a generalized version of this objective:

L=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim P_{g}}[D(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{r}}[D(\mathbf{x})]+\lambda_{\gp}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\sim P_{\gp}}\left[\left(\left\lVert\max\left(0,\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}D(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\right)\right\rVert_{2}-c\right)^{2}\right],

(11)

where the desired Lipschitz constant
c
can be adjusted, and only gradients larger than
c
are penalized. For our experiments, we use
c=0.01
and
\lambda_{\gp}=10
, which produced the best results while hyperparameter tuning.

In a GAN-NCM
\widehat{M}=\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{V},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},P(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\rangle
, the noise
P(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})
and functions
\mathcal{F}
collectively form a generative model over
\mathbf{V}
. With the mutilation procedure, the same noise and functions form a generative model over
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}
, where the functions for variables in
\mathbf{X}
are set to fixed values. Hence, we treat the parameters of
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
as the parameters of the generator
G
in the GAN objective. Another fully-connected multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is used as the discriminator/critic,
D
.

When fitting a specific data distribution
P(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}})
, the generator (
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathbf{x}}
) attempts to maximize
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim P^{\widehat{M}}(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}})}[D(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})]
, the expected score assigned by the critic for generated samples. The critic (
D
) attempts to minimize the objective in Eq.
11
.

In cases where multiple datasets are fitted (
|\mathbb{Z}|>1
), one could create a critic network for each dataset, but for the sake of computational efficiency, we use the same critic network
D
for all datasets, with an additional input of an index clarifying which dataset is being evaluated. We did not find this to have any significant impact to performance.

In our experiments, each function
\hat{f}_{V_{i}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
is a fully connected multi-layer-perceptron (MLP) with 2 hidden layers of size 64, layer normalization
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
applied after each layer, and ReLU activations
(
Nair & Hinton 2010
)
after each hidden layer. These choices were made as best practices for implementing WGAN-GP. More layers of larger size can be used for more complicated data. Since we work with binary data, a sigmoid activation function is placed at the end to constrain values between 0 and 1. Values are rounded at evaluation time. To simplify training, we increase the dimensionality of each
\widehat{U}\in\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
to 2. We recommend using larger dimensions for cases where bidirected cliques are very large, and a single
\widehat{U}
variable is inputted to several functions.

For the critic
D
, we use an MLP with 2 hidden layers of size
64\times|\mathbf{V}|
, which scales depending on the number of variables in the dataset. We also apply layer normalization after each hidden layer and use ReLU activations. We do not use an activation after the last layer.

Weights of all networks are initialized using Glorot initialization
(
Glorot & Bengio 2010
)
. The models are trained using the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
.

B.5.2
Query Loss

Since variables in our experiments are binary vectors, we compute
\mathbb{D}_{Q}
as in Eq.
5
by using log loss. For example, if
Q=P(Y=1)
, let
\widehat{Q}=\{\hat{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be
m
samples of
Y
from the NCM
\widehat{M}
. Note that all outputs from
\widehat{M}
are real numbers between 0 and 1 as a result of the sigmoid activation. If the goal was to maximize the query, we would compute

\mathbb{D}_{Q}(\widehat{Q},Q)=\mathbb{D}_{Q}(\{\hat{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m},1)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}-\log(\hat{y}_{j})

which would approach 0 as
\hat{y}_{j}\rightarrow 1
and approach
+\infty
as
\hat{y}_{j}\rightarrow 0
. Penalizing this term would encourage the optimization process to generate samples of
\hat{y}_{j}
closer to 1. In the case of minimization, we would replace
-\log(\hat{y}_{j})
with
-\log(1-\hat{y}_{j})
.

More generally, denote
\hat{y}_{X=x\mid Z=z}
as a sample from
Y^{\widehat{M}}_{X=x}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})
under the condition that
Z^{\widehat{M}}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})=z
. In other words,

B.6.1

The GAN-NCM is an

Appendix C
Connection to Related Works

This paper evaluates causal queries directly through a parameterized model in the form of an SCM trained on available data from the true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, which we call a proxy model-based approach. In this section, we discuss the differences between the NCM presented in this paper and the models used in other works that also implement a proxy approach.

First, we note that many of these works implicitly benefited from the constraints of enforcing a causal diagram into the model structure without proving any properties about these constraints. One contribution of our work is to provide some explicit results about these constraints via Thms.
1
and
2
. Second, and more importantly, most of these works operated under different assumptions and problem settings, to be discussed in individual sections. When applied to our problem setting, these methods may not be as general as the method discussed in this paper. Some of these losses of generality are highlighted in the following propositions and illustrated through the further examples provided in App.
D
.

1
.

Any model in which the Markovianity assumption (also known as causal sufficiency) is enforced is not
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressive (i.e. an equivalent statement of Thm.
2
cannot be proven about such a model class).

\blacksquare

Proof.

See counterexample provided in Example
6
in Sec.
D.3
.
∎

2
.

There exist settings in which an
\mathcal{L}_{3}
query that is not identifiable from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data (
P(\mathbf{V})
) alone may be identifiable given data sets from
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

\blacksquare

Proof.

Examples include ID settings 9, 10, 11, and 12 from Fig.
4
, in which their counterparts without the
P(\mathbf{V}_{x})
datasets are not identifiable. Another example is shown in Example

.

.

We discuss more specifically the differences with each paper in the following subsections. Whenever notation differs between the two works, we use our notation to maintain consistency and make the comparison more transparent.

C.1
Kocaoglu et al. 2018

Similar to our work,
Kocaoglu et al. 2018
used an implicit approach to fit a proxy SCM on the data via generative adversarial networks. The method was specifically trained on observational (
\mathcal{L}_{1})
data and used to evaluate interventional (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) queries in the Markovian setting. Like for a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM, their approach used a proxy model
\widehat{M}=\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{V},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},P(\widehat{\mathbf{U}})\rangle
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}
consists of independent fixed noise variables and each
\hat{f}_{V_{i}}\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
is defined such that
\hat{f}_{V_{i}}
takes
\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{V_{i}}
as input. Here,
\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}
is the set of parents of
V_{i}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{V_{i}}
is a set of noise variables specific to variable
V_{i}
.

This work introduced two novel GAN architectures for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
inference, CausalGAN and CausalBEGAN, focused on applications in image generation. For this challenging task, the CausalGAN architecture was designed in a unique fashion in which the model was separated into two parts: one for generating the labels, and another for generating the image conditioned on these labels. This was in contrast to having one generator learning the entire joint distribution of labels and images. The approach for learning the image generator is similar to a conditional GAN in that the objective of the generator includes an additional term to maximize the prediction accuracy of a labeling network. However, this often results in a problem they described as
label-conditioned mode collapse
, where the generator may have been complacent in generating a small number of fixed samples that are easy to label. Hence, in addition to this labeler, CausalGAN also introduced an anti-labeler network. The anti-labeler also attempts to label the generated images, but the objective of CausalGAN simultaneously maximizes the accuracy of the labeler while minimizing the accuracy of the anti-labeler. This prevents label-conditioned mode collapse because images that are too easily labeled will fail to minimize the accuracy of the anti-labeler. More formally, for a binary label
l
with probability
\rho=P(l=1)
, the objective of the CausalGAN generator is:

\displaystyle\min_{G}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}(x)}\left[\log\left(\frac{1-D(x)}{D(x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\rho\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}^{1}(x)}[\log(D_{LR}(x)]-(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}^{0}(x)}[log(1-D_{LR}(x))]

\displaystyle+\rho\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}^{1}(x)}[\log(D_{LG}(x)]+(1-\rho)\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{g}^{0}(x)}[log(1-D_{LG}(x))],

(17)

where
G
is the generator,
D
is the discriminator,
p_{g}(x)
is the distribution induced by the generator,
p_{g}^{i}(x)
is the distribution conditioned on
l=i
,
D_{LR}
is the labeler, and
D_{LG}
is the anti-labeler. The loss is composed of the loss from the discriminator subtracted with the loss from the labeler and adding the loss from the anti-labeler. The paper proved that the generator successfully matched the true conditional image distribution when
G
was the optimum of this objective
(
Kocaoglu et al. 2018
, Thm. 2)
. CausalBEGAN was another architecture introduced where optimization considered image quality margin and label margin. In both cases, interventions were computed using the mutilation procedure on the generating model
\widehat{M}
.

CausalGAN and CausalBEGAN were empirically shown to successfully learn the data distribution in several image generation tasks. Experiments demonstrated several settings in which intervening on a label resulted in a different image compared to conditioning on the label.

The reference produced impressive results in generating images under causal interventions using novel implicit GAN architectures. While the approach is similar to our work in that a GAN proxy model is used, there are a number of differences with our work.

1.

The paper worked in the space of inferring
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities given observational data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Our work generalizes this setting to inferring any counterfactual query from
\mathcal{L}_{3}
given any combination of datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

2.

Unlike this work, we do not make the Markovianity assumption, meaning we allow unobserved confounding between variables. Many real world settings often have unobserved confounding, so the Markovianity assumption can be limiting. In a situation where the true SCM has unobserved confounding, a model class which enforces Markovianity will not be expressive enough to model the true SCM (Prop.
1
).

3.

By
(
Kocaoglu et al. 2018
, Def. 1)
, the architecture of CausalGAN took the form of a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
, similar to how the
\mathcal{G}
-constrained NCM is constructed in Def.
2
. In our work, we discuss the theoretical properties of the implied constraints via Thms.
1
and
2
. While
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{2})}
-consistency likely held for CausalGAN due to the nature of its construction, this property was not explicitly proven or discussed.

4.

The fact that all
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities are identifiable from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data
was acknowledged
(
Kocaoglu et al. 2018
, Prop. 1)
. The identification question constitutes an additional challenge in our paper since we consider layer 3 queries and allow for unobserved confounding. It’s understood that without Markovianity, even inferences about
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries are not always identifiable from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data.

C.2
Pawlowski et al. 2020

Pawlowski et al. 2020
implemented a deep proxy SCM approach in which each mechanism
\hat{f}_{V_{i}}
models a conditional distribution
P(V_{i}\mid\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}})
under the Markovianity assumption (lack of unobserved confounders). Under this assumption, the joint observational distribution
P(\mathbf{V})
can be factorized as

P(\mathbf{V})=\prod_{V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}}P(V_{i}\mid\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}),

(18)

so each mechanism represents one conditional distribution in this factorization. They proposed three novel approaches to modeling this conditional distribution.

1.

The first approach involved modeling
P(X\mid\mathbf{Pa}_{X})
using normalizing flows.
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{X}
consists of a noise variable
\epsilon
, and
\hat{f}_{X}
is an invertible function such that
x=\hat{f}_{X}(\epsilon;\mathbf{pa}_{X})
. Then the explicit likelihood could be computed as
p(x\mid\mathbf{pa}_{x})=p(\epsilon)\cdot|\det\nabla_{\epsilon}\hat{f}_{X}(\epsilon;\mathbf{pa}_{x})|^{-1}
, where
\epsilon
could be computed as
\hat{f}_{X}^{-1}(x;\mathbf{pa}_{x})
.

2.

The second approach extended the first approach to high dimensional settings by splitting
\epsilon
into two independent noise variables,
z
and
u
, and splitting
\hat{f}_{X}
into two components: a non-invertible function
g
and an invertible function
h
. Then
\hat{f}_{X}(\epsilon,\mathbf{pa}_{x})=h(u;g(z;\mathbf{pa}_{x}),\mathbf{pa}_{x})
, where
g
is expected to capture the high-level aspects of the data. This is helpful in high dimensional settings as the size of the invertible component
h
can be reduced because working with the outputs of
g
is easier than directly working with the data. Since
p(x\mid\mathbf{pa}_{x})
is no longer tractable, the model is instead trained using variational inference by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) with variational distribution
Q(z\mid x,\mathbf{pa}_{x})
,

\log p(x\mid\mathbf{pa}_{x})\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}(z\mid x,\mathbf{pa}_{x})[\log p(x\mid z,\mathbf{pa}_{x})]-D_{\kl}[Q(z\mid x,\mathbf{pa}_{x})||P(z)].

(19)

3.

Although not demonstrated in the paper, a third approach was presented suggesting the use of a GAN-based approach to implicitly model the likelihood.

The method primarily used in the paper was the 2nd approach. They evaluated counterfactual quantities using the 3-step procedure from
Pearl 2000
:

1.

Abduction
– The observations
\mathbf{v}
of all variables
\mathbf{V}
are used to predict
P(\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{v})
, where
P(\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{v})
decomposes as
\prod_{V_{i}\in V}P(\epsilon_{V_{i}}\mid v_{i},\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})
due to the Markovianity assumption. This is then computed as

\displaystyle P(\epsilon_{V_{i}}\mid v_{i},\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})

\displaystyle=P(z_{V_{i}}\mid v_{i},\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})P(u_{V_{i}}\mid z_{V_{i}},v_{i},\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})

\displaystyle\approx Q(z_{V_{i}}\mid e_{V_{i}}(v_{i};\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}}))\delta(u_{V_{i}})

(20)

where
e(v_{i};\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})
is a deterministic encoder mapping
v_{i}
and
\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}}
to
z_{V_{i}}
, and
\delta
is a Dirac delta distribution centered at
h_{V_{i}}^{-1}(v_{i};g_{V_{i}}(z_{V_{i}};\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}}),\mathbf{pa}_{V_{i}})
.

2.

Action
– For an intervention over sets of variables
\mathbf{X}
, each mechanism
\hat{f}_{X}
for
X\in\mathbf{X}
is replaced either with a fixed value
x
(atomic) or a surrogate mechanism
\tilde{f}_{X}(\epsilon_{X}\mid\widetilde{\mathbf{pa}}_{X})
(stochastic).

3.

Prediction
– Under this distribution of
P(\mathbf{U}\mid\mathbf{v})
, and with the modified mechanisms, the deep SCM is evaluated to produce a result for the counterfactual quantity
P(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{v})
.

The approach was demonstrated empirically on a synthetic MNIST dataset and on a real world brain imaging dataset.

The paper presented a powerful approach to modeling a deep SCM using normalizing flows and variational inference, allowing for efficient and precise training even in high-dimensional cases. Still, there are a number of differences between this paper and our work.

1.

Unlike this work, we do not make the Markovianity assumption, meaning we allow unobserved confounding between variables. Many real world settings often have unobserved confounding, so the Markovianity assumption can be limiting. In a situation where the true SCM has unobserved confounding, a model class which enforces Markovianity will not be expressive enough to model the true SCM (Prop.
1
). Moreover, it is not a trivial task to extend the normalizing flow approach in this paper to a case with unobserved confounders. In such a case, Eq.
18
would no longer be valid, and it is not immediately clear how to train invertible mechanisms that can share noise.

2.

Like our work, this paper assumed that the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
is provided. However, in our work, we discuss the theoretical properties of the implied constraints via Thms.
1
and
2
, while this paper implicitly benefited from these constraints without discussing these properties.

3.

The identification problem is not considered in this work while it is a major focus in our work. As shown in Prop.
3
, there exist counterfactual quantities that cannot be identified given the graph
\mathcal{G}
, and all available data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, even under the Markovianity assumption. If these quantities were to be estimated from a model trained on such data, the result will likely be incorrect and/or misleading. For that reason, we make sure to identify all queries before attempting to estimate them.

4.

While this paper only considered input in the form of observational data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, our work considers cases where additional experimental datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are provided (even cases where perhaps the observational distribution
P(\mathbf{V})
is not provided). As shown in Prop.
2
, there do exist counterfactual queries that are not identifiable given observational data but are identifiable given datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

5.

This paper evaluated counterfactual quantities using the 3-step procedure of abduction, action, and prediction. While this is a sound algorithm for evaluating applicable quantities, it does not apply generally to all counterfactual quantities. Specifically, it only applies in quantities of the form
P(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{z})
, in which only one intervention is applied before the conditioning bar, and observations are present after the conditioning bar. This excludes quantities such as
P(y_{x},y^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}})
, which is a joint probability of two counterfactual terms in different worlds (one in
X=x
and another in
X=x^{\prime}
), as well as quantities such as
P(y_{x}\mid y^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}})
, in which a term in an intervened world
(X=x^{\prime})
is after the conditioning bar.

6.

Finally, although an implicit approach via GANs was discussed in
(
Pawlowski et al. 2020
)
, it was not explored. Our paper realizes an implementation of GAN approach in the non-Markovian setting.

C.3
Witty et al. 2021

Witty et al. 2021
introduced simulation-based identifiability (SBI), which uses a Bayesian approach to solve the identification problem for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
and some
\mathcal{L}_{3}
queries given
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data. In their approach, the goal was to identify a particular query
Q
given a fixed observational distribution
P(\mathbf{V})
and a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
. An SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}=\langle\mathbf{U}^{*},\mathbf{V},\mathcal{F}^{*},P(\mathbf{U}^{*})\rangle
is sampled from a prior over SCMs (specifically their functions and exogenous variables),
P(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{U})
. In each iteration,
n
samples are drawn from
P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{V})
, the observational dataset of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Similar to our work, two parameterized SCMs,
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
are optimized to simultaneously fit
P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{V})
while maximizing the difference in the induced queries of the two SCMs,
\widehat{Q}_{1}
and
\widehat{Q}_{2}
. This is done by maximizing the objective

L(\widehat{M}_{1},\widehat{M}_{2},\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log P^{\widehat{M}_{1}}(\mathbf{v}_{i})+\log P^{\widehat{M}_{2}}(\mathbf{v}_{i})\right)+\lambda|\widehat{Q}_{1}-\widehat{Q}_{2}|,

(21)

where
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are a collection of
n
samples from
P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{V})
. Maximizing this objective is equivalent to jointly maximizing the likelihood of the data for both
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
as well as maximizing the gap between
\widehat{Q}_{1}
and
\widehat{Q}_{2}
. Identifiability is concluded if, following a hypothesis testing procedure,
|\widehat{Q}_{1}-\widehat{Q}_{2}|
is under a certain threshold. Non-identifiability is concluded otherwise. They proved that
Q
is identifiable w.r.t.
\mathcal{M}^{*}
if and only if there does not exist any parameterized SCM
\widehat{M}
fitted to the data from
P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{V})
such that
P^{\widehat{M}}(\mathbf{V})
converges to
P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{V})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
Q^{\widehat{M}}\neq Q^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
, and
\widehat{M}
is in the support of the prior
(
Witty et al. 2021
, Thm. 3.2)
.

In non-parametric settings, there exist sound and complete symbolic methods such as do-calculus that could efficiently and correctly identify any
\mathcal{L}_{2}
query
Pearl 2000
. Hence, as we understand, the paper primarily focused its attention on parametric settings, where the model class of SCMs was further restricted to specific function classes (e.g. linear), and symbolic approaches to identification under this constrained class were not as well-studied.

For the identification task, SBI shares many similarities with our approach in Alg.
1
, notably that they also trained two proxy SCMs to minimize/maximize the query while simultaneously fitting the data. Still, there are many differences between the two works.

1.

The paper considered a general form of the query
Q(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})
, which was a function of the counterfactual terms of
\mathbf{V}
in the two worlds of
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. For example, average treatment effect (ATE) given a binary treatment
X
could be computed as
Q(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{X=1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{X=0}]
, which was what was used in their experiments. While in theory, this may have included certain counterfactual queries, these queries were not explored, so the work largely focused on the space of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries. Moreover, they only considered data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Our work generalizes this setting to inferring any counterfactual query from
\mathcal{L}_{3}
given any combination of datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

2.

Like our work, this paper assumed that the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
is provided. However, in our work, we discuss the theoretical properties of the implied constraints via Thms.
1
and
2
, while this paper implicitly benefited from these constraints without discussing these properties.

3.

The paper was largely concerned with identifying quantities in parametric cases (and functions of SCMs were fixed to certain parametric families), while our work is largely focused on the non-parametric case. We make no claims about expressivity or
\mathcal{G}
-consistency in parametric settings.

4.

Our work assumes that there exists a true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, which generates a specific dataset from which we aim to identify and estimate our query. In contrast,
(
Witty et al. 2021
)
specifically aimed to identify a query without a true SCM in mind and instead matched the distribution with an SCM sampled from a prior distribution over the space of SCMs. While estimation could theoretically be performed after concluding identifiability with SBI, this was not a focus of
(
Witty et al. 2021
)
, and the estimation would not be meaningful unless
\mathcal{M}^{*}
corresponded to some true SCM in a real application.

5.

In terms of the approach,
(
Witty et al. 2021
)
differs from our work in that the two parametrized models,
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
are optimized jointly, while in our case, the two models are optimized separately. The goal in SBI is to maximize the gap between the two models in the query, while Alg.
1
does this implicitly when maximizing or minimizing the query in the two models separately. Further, our implementation in practice utilizes GANs, which is not explored in this paper.

C.4
Xia et al. 2021

Xia et al. 2021
introduced the NCM, which is the model class considered in our paper. They proved that NCMs are expressive on all three layers
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Thm. 1)
but then subsequently proved the Neural Causal Hierarchy Theorem
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 1)
which states that despite this expressivity, an NCM trained only on lower layer data would almost never match the true SCM on higher layers. To proceed, they introduced a graphical inductive bias in the form of a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
derived from the true SCM. They defined a
\mathcal{G}
-constrained version of the NCM (Def.
2
in our paper).

They proved that the class of
\mathcal{G}
-constrained NCMs is both
\mathcal{G}
-consistent
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Thm. 2)
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressive
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Thm. 3)
.
\mathcal{G}
-consistency (
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{2})}
-consistency in the context of our work) constrained the class to a smaller set of models which may have potentially agreed on the causal query, while
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressiveness guaranteed that
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs could still model any SCM within this constrained space. Still, these results only considered up to layer 2 of the causal hierarchy, and made no claims about layer 3. In contrast, Thms.
1
and
2
in our work are much stronger claims, proving these results on layer 3. That is,
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs have all of the constraints implied on the third layer while still being able to express any true SCM on all three layers within this constrained setting.

The paper then defined the neural identification problem specifically for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries. They proved a duality stating that neural identification for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities is equivalent to graphical identification
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Thm. 4)
and showed that the mutilation procedure applied to a proxy
\mathcal{G}
-NCM can be used to estimate identifiable queries
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 2)
. This definition of neural identification did not consider
\mathcal{L}_{3}
quantities or
\mathcal{L}_{2}
input data. In our case, we define neural counterfactual identification (Def.
4
) generalizing the neural identification problem to handle
\mathcal{L}_{3}
queries given arbitrary datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. We prove a stronger duality in this setting (Thm.
3
) and that counterfactual quantities can be evaluated directly from the proxy
\mathcal{G}
-NCM, akin to the mutilation procedure for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities (Corol.
1
).

Following these identification results,
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
introduced an algorithm
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Alg. 1)
which is both sound and complete for identifying and estimating
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 4)
. The algorithm was implemented in practice through a maximum likelihood approach (MLE-NCM) through
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Alg. 2)
. This involved training two
\mathcal{G}
-NCM parameterizations, where one was trained to maximize the query, while the other was trained to minimize it, and both models were trained to fit the observational data
P(\mathbf{V})
. The algorithm in our counterfactual setting (Alg.
1
) is similar on the surface, but there are many additional challenges in solving this more general problem. First, while an interventional query might be easy to compute and optimize, it is not as clear how to compute a counterfactual quantity with gradients such that it can be maximized or minimized. After all, counterfactual quantities often have complicated forms such as nesting (e.g.
P(Y_{Z_{X}})
) or having the same variable in multiple worlds (e.g.
P(Y_{X=0},Y_{X=1})
). Second, fitting a single dataset is standard practice, which is what needs to be done when only observational data is available. It is not as clear how to simultaneously fit several datasets. Our work solves both of these problems in practice using the GAN-NCM approach in Sec.
4
, but also explains how one might solve this problem using the MLE-NCM in Sec.
B.6.1
.

To demonstrate the ability of the MLE-NCM to identify and estimate
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries in practice, the paper showed results from identifying average treatment effect (ATE) from 8 typical graphs encountered in the literature. They also provided estimation results for the identifiable cases. While our experiments mainly focused on identifying
\mathcal{L}_{3}
queries, we reproduced the experiments of
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
in Sec.
B.1
using the GAN-NCM to demonstrate that our approach is general and can be applied to the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
case as well. Further, although the reason why one might prefer to use an NCM for causal inference is due to the ability of neural models to scale inferences to high dimensions, this ability was not emphasized in the experimental results of this paper. We explicitly showed how the GAN-NCM can perform nearly as well even in 16-dimensional cases while showing that the MLE-NCM scales poorly due to tractability issues (see Fig.
5
in Sec.
5
). One additional issue of the MLE-NCM is its bias towards returning ID results even in non-ID cases. This is evident in Fig.
4
and can be potentially problematic if used in practice since attempting to estimate a non-ID query will likely produce an incorrect and misleading result. For more details, see the discussion surrounding Fig.
14
in Sec.
B.1
.

To summarize, this paper introduced the NCM and proved several properties about it, including results on expressiveness, constraints, and identification.
Our work also uses the NCM and proves many analogous results, but there are still several differences:

1.

The paper primarily worked in the space of inferring
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities given observational data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Our work generalizes this setting to inferring any counterfactual query from
\mathcal{L}_{3}
given any combination of datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. This is reflected in the theoretical results, the algorithms, and the practical implementation. We show that the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM has all
\mathcal{L}_{3}
constraints (Thm.
1
) but can still express any model on all three layers in this constrained setting (Thm.
2
). We show that the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM can be used to solve the identification and estimation problem in the more general counterfactual setting (Thm.
3
, Corol.
1
, Alg.
1
, Corol.
2
). These results drastically generalize the capabilities of the NCM to
\mathcal{L}_{3}
.

2.

While the MLE-NCM implemented in this paper is an option for counterfactual identification and estimation (as shown in Sec.
B.6.1
), we develop a new type of architecture for the NCM using a generative adversarial approach. Our approach, detailed in Sec.
4
, has many advantages over the MLE-NCM, notably its design simplicity, its ability to scale to high dimensions, and its robustness in solving the ID problem. We even show that the GAN-NCM can solve all of the experimental settings originally presented in
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
in Sec.
B.1
.

From all of these results, both theoretical and empirical, it is evident that our approach, in fact, subsumes the work in
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
.

C.5
Zecevic et al. 2021

Zecevic et al. 2021
related the class of graph neural networks (GNNs) to the class of Markovian SCMs. Given a DAG
\mathcal{G}
with variables
\mathbf{V}
, they defined GNN layers using the functions

h_{V_{i}}=\phi\left(d_{V_{i}},\bigoplus_{V_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(V_{i})}\psi(d_{V_{i}},d_{V_{j}})\right),

(22)

where
d_{V_{i}}
is either data associated with
V_{i}
or a feature computed from a previous layer,
\mathcal{N}(V_{i})
is the set of neighbors of
V_{i}
in
\mathcal{G}
,
\bigoplus
refers to an aggregating function such as sum, and
h_{V_{i}}
is a feature of
V_{i}
for the current layer. They proved
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
, Thm. 1)
, which states that for any Markovian SCM
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V},\mathcal{F},P(\mathbf{U})\rangle
, there exists a GNN construction such that
h_{V_{i}}=f_{V_{i}}
for all
f_{V_{i}}\in\mathcal{F}
, and such a construction (but without shared
\phi
and
\psi
) is therefore a special case of the NCM introduced in
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
(
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
, Corol. 1)
). Using a GNN interpretation of an SCM, one could compute quantities from an intervention
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
through the mutilation approach by evaluating Eq.
22
but with edges into variables of
\mathbf{X}
removed from
\mathcal{N}
.

Leveraging results from variational graph autoencoders (VGAEs),
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
)
demonstrated an approach to learning the observational distribution with a GNN architecture. They introduced interventional VGAEs (iVGAEs) which defined inferences from VGAEs on layer 2 quantities via the mutilation procedure on the GNN layers. They proved a wealth of results related to this class of models following similar results proven in
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
:
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-expressivity
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
, Thm. 3)
, a causal hierarchy theorem result between layers 1 and 2
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
, Corol. 2)
, and equivalence between neural-identification from iVGAEs and classical identification of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
, Thm. 4)
.

(
Zecevic et al. 2021
)
introduced an interesting discussion on the relationship between GNNs, SCMs, and NCMs. The work differs from our work in a variety of ways.

1.

Like
(
Xia et al. 2021
)
,
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
)
primarily worked in the space of inferring
\mathcal{L}_{2}
quantities given observational data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Our work generalizes this setting to inferring any counterfactual query from
\mathcal{L}_{3}
given any combination of datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

2.

Unlike this work, we do not make the Markovianity assumption, meaning we allow unobserved confounding between variables. Many real world settings often have unobserved confounding, so the Markovianity assumption can be limiting. In a situation where the true SCM has unobserved confounding, a model class which enforces Markovianity will not be expressive enough to model the true SCM (Prop.
1
). The results proven in this paper do not extend trivially to cases with unobserved confounders. Traditional GNNs are typically defined on adjacency matrices, which cannot describe bidirected edges in causal diagrams.

3.

Like our work, this paper assumed that the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
was provided. However, in our work, we discuss the theoretical properties of the implied constraints via Thms.
1
and

-GNNs or iVGAEs are

\mathcal{G}
-constrained GNNs have all of the
\mathcal{L}_{2}

\mathcal{G}

4.

The identification problem is one of the main focuses of our work. While the paper discussed the identification problem of
\mathcal{L}_{2}

\mathcal{L}_{1}

5.

Finally, we note that while this paper explored some novel aspects of using GNN and VGAE architectures for causal inference, our paper focuses on GANs, an entirely different class of models.

C.6
Sanchez-Martin et al. 2021

Sanchez-Martin et al. 2021
introduced Variational Causal Autoencoders (VACA), a model for estimating interventional and counterfactual quantities under the Markovianity assumption using a variational graph autoencoder architecture (VGAE) and graph neural network (GNN) architecture like
(
Zecevic et al. 2021
)
. VACA is defined as a tuple
\langle A,p(\mathbf{Z}),p_{\theta},q_{\phi}\rangle
, where

•

A
is an adjacency matrix representing the causal DAG
\mathcal{G}
;

•

p(\mathbf{Z})=\prod_{V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}}p(Z_{V_{i}})
is a prior distribution over independent latent variables
\mathbf{Z}=\{Z_{V_{i}}:V_{i}\in\mathbf{V}\}
;

•

The decoder
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{Z},A)
is a GNN parameterized by
\theta
mapping
\mathbf{Z}
and
A
to the likelihood of
\mathbf{V}
given these values;

•

The encoder
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{V},A)
is a GNN parameterized by
\phi
mapping
\mathbf{V}
and
A
to the posterior approximation of
\mathbf{Z}
given these values.

The model was trained to maximize the likelihood of observational data
P(\mathbf{V})
, which can be expressed as

P(\mathbf{V})\approx\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{V}\mid\mathbf{Z},A)p(\mathbf{Z})d\mathbf{Z}.

(23)

Here,
\mathbf{Z}
is similar to the role of
\mathbf{U}
in an SCM. Although
\mathbf{Z}
does not have to be equal to
\mathbf{U}
from the true SCM, each
Z_{V_{i}}
provides a source of noise for variable
V_{i}
that captures the same information that
\mathbf{U}_{V_{i}}
contributes to
V_{i}
in the true SCM. The difference between
\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{U}
does not matter as long as the VACA model can match the true observational distribution.

During evaluation, interventional (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) quantities can be estimated via

p(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}})\approx\int\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{V}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Z}},\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}},A^{\mathcal{I}})p(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}})q_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}\mid A^{\mathcal{I}},\mathbf{X})d\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}d\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}},

(24)

where
A^{\mathcal{I}}
is the adjacency matrix with edges into variables of
\mathbf{X}
removed,
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}
is the set of representations of
\mathbf{X}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}
is the subset of
\mathbf{Z}
corresponding to
\mathbf{V}\setminus\mathbf{X}
. In words, intervened variables are assigned a representation from the encoder under a graph with the edges into
\mathbf{X}

Further, the following counterfactual (
\mathcal{L}_{3}
) quantity can also be estimated:

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{v}^{CF}\mid\mathbf{v}^{F})

\displaystyle\approx\int\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{v}^{CF}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{F},\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}},A^{\mathcal{I}})q_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}\mid\mathbf{x},A^{\mathcal{I}})q_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{F}\mid\mathbf{v}^{F},A)d\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}d\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{F},

where
\mathbf{v}^{F}
is the observed factual value of
\mathbf{V}
,
\mathbf{v}^{CF}
is the counterfactual value of
\mathbf{V}

\mathbf{v}^{F}

\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}
as discussed in Eq.
24

\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathcal{I}}

\mathbf{X}

A^{\mathcal{I}}
to produce a counterfactual outcome
\mathbf{v}^{CF}
for
\mathbf{V}
in this setting.

VACA presented interesting ideas for evaluating interventional and counterfactual quantities in the Markovian case using GNNs and VGAEs. Still, they differ from our work in several ways.

1.

Unlike this work, we do not make the Markovianity assumption, meaning we allow unobserved confounding between variables. Many real world settings often have unobserved confounding, so the Markovianity assumption can be limiting. In a situation where the true SCM has unobserved confounding, a model class which enforces Markovianity will not be expressive enough to model the true SCM (Prop.
1
). VACA does not extend trivially to cases with unobserved confounders. Traditional GNNs are typically defined on adjacency matrices, which cannot describe bidirected edges in causal diagrams.

2.

Like our work, this paper assumed that the causal graph
\mathcal{G}
was provided. However, in our work, we discuss the theoretical properties of the implied constraints via Thms.
1
and
2
. This paper possibly benefited from these constraints implicitly without discussing these properties, but it is not actually clear if VACA is a
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent model.

3.

The identification problem was not considered in this work while it is a major focus in our work. As shown in Prop.
3
above, there exist counterfactual quantities that cannot be identified given the graph
\mathcal{G}
, and all available data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, even under the Markovianity assumption. If these quantities were to be estimated from a model trained on such data, the result will likely be incorrect and misleading. For that reason, we make sure to identify all queries before attempting to estimate them.

4.

While this paper only considered input in the form of observational data from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, our work considers cases where additional experimental datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are provided (even cases where perhaps the observational distribution
P(\mathbf{V})
is not provided). As shown in Prop.
2
, there do exist counterfactual queries that are not identifiable given observational data but are identifiable given datasets from
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

5.

This paper evaluated counterfactual quantities using the 3-step procedure of abduction, action, and prediction. While this is a sound algorithm for evaluating applicable quantities, it does not apply generally to all counterfactual quantities. Specifically, it only applies in quantities of the form
P(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{z})
, in which only one intervention is applied before the conditioning bar, and observations are present after the conditioning bar. This excludes quantities such as
P(y_{x},y^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}})
, which is a joint probability of two counterfactual terms in different worlds (one in
X=x
and another in
X=x^{\prime}
), as well as quantities such as
P(y_{x}\mid y^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}})
, in which a term in an intervened world
(X=x^{\prime})
is after the conditioning bar.

6.

Finally, we note that while this paper explored some novel aspects of using GNN and VGAE architectures for causal inference, our paper focuses on GANs, a different class of models.

D.1
Necessity of Causal Assumptions

As emphasized at the beginning of Sec.
2

(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 1)
, following the Causal Hierarchy Theorem from
Bareinboim et al. 2022
that states that distributions from lower layers of the hierarchy almost surely underdetermine higher layers in a measure-theoretic sense. In other words, it is not possible to make a statement about a higher layer (e.g. interventions, counterfactuals) with only knowledge from lower layers (e.g. observations).
This result is well-understood in the causal inference field, and Pearl eloquently states in the Book of Why that
“without the causal model, we could not go from rung [layer] one to rung [layer] two. This is why deep-learning systems (as long as they only use rung-one data and do not have a causal model) will never be able to answer questions about interventions (…)”
(
Pearl & Mackenzie 2018
, p. 32)
.

Given this impossibility, the natural course of action to proceed in problems of cross-layer inference is to study under what assumptions inferences could potentially be made. Obviously, the problem could be solved if the mechanisms and noise of the true generating SCM are known, but this is almost never the case in practice. In this work, we assume that we are given partial knowledge of the true generating SCM in the form of a causal diagram, a qualitative description of the underlying mechanisms and noise. This assumption is commonly used in the causal inference literature starting from
Pearl 1995
, where the field started in its modern form, and later shown in several classic causal inference applications such as in the identification of causal effects with do-calculus
(
Pearl 2000
, Ch. 4)
or counterfactual identification and their axioms
(
Pearl 2000
, Ch. 7)
. There are compelling cases in which a data analyst may have a qualitative understanding of the causal relationships of the environment to form a causal graph
(
Bottou et al. 2013
;
Pearl 2000
)
. These relationships are explored in several previous counterfactual studies using symbolic approaches such as
Galles & Pearl 1998
,
Shpitser & Pearl 2007
, and
Correa et al. 2021
, to cite a few.

The reason why the causal diagram assumption can be utilized to solve counterfactual inference problems is due to the constraints implied by it. While many SCMs may agree on lower layer quantities but disagree on higher layer quantities, the pool of plausible models shrinks dramatically after falsifying all SCMs that do not match the given constraints. Specifically these constraints are the
\mathcal{L}_{i}
equality constraints discussed in Appendix

, which led to the notion of
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{i})}
-consistency discussed in Sec.
2
. Still, even with these assumptions, inferring counterfactual quantities is a non-trivial task. Despite having a constrained setting, it is still not guaranteed that a counterfactual quantity is computable from observational and interventional data, nor is it obvious how to compute such a quantity even if it is computable. This results in the counterfactual identification and estimation problems discussed in Secs.
3
and
4
.

Such works are complementary to this work since the data scientist can choose for themselves whether they would prefer to make more assumptions for stronger results.

Ultimately, the goal of this work is not to promote one type of assumption over another but to offer a toolbox to solve counterfactual inference problems once the assumptions are made. The results of this paper are simply the first steps for solving counterfactual inferences in a well-understood setting. A promising direction for future work is to further relax and generalize these approaches when the diagram is not fully known.

be partitioned into two sets of confounded and unconfounded parents,
\mathbf{Pa}_{i}^{c}
and
\mathbf{Pa}_{i}^{u}
in
\mathcal{G}_{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}
. Then

\displaystyle P(v_{i}\mid do(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{pa}_{i}^{c},do(\mathbf{pa}_{i}^{u}))

\displaystyle=P(v_{i}\mid do(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{pa}_{i}^{c},\mathbf{pa}_{i}^{u})

Here,
\mathbf{Pa}_{V_{i}}^{\mathbf{x}+}=\mathbf{Pa}(\{V\in\mathbf{C}_{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}(V_{i}):V\leq V_{i}\})
, with
\mathbf{C}_{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}
referring to the corresponding C-component in
\mathcal{G}_{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}
and
\leq
referring to the topological ordering.

\blacksquare

To clarify notation used in this definition,
P(\mathbf{y}\mid do(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{z})=P(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{x}})
for any
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\mathbf{V}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\overline{\mathbf{X}}}
refers to the graph
\mathcal{G}
with the edges into
\mathbf{X}
removed.

In words, a causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
is a CBN for a collection of distributions
\mathbf{P}^{*}
if
\mathbf{P}^{*}
satisfies the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
equality constraints induced by
\mathcal{G}
. The CBN explicitly states these constraints, and more details can be found in
Bareinboim et al. 2022
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be some causal diagram induced by true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Let
M_{\cbn}=\langle\mathcal{G},\mathbf{P}^{*}\rangle
be some CBN defined over distributions
\mathbf{P}^{*}
. Then, by definition,
M_{\cbn}
is
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{2})}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. However,
M_{\cbn}
would not be
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
since the constraints in the definition do not include any distributions outside of layer 2. In fact,
\mathbf{P}^{*}
does not even have to be defined with layer 3 distributions.

As an example, suppose
\mathcal{G}
is the graph in Fig.
18
, and that
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{M}^{*})=\mathcal{L}_{1}(M_{\cbn})
, that is,
\mathbf{P}^{*}
matches the layer 1 distribution of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. Then, since
M_{\cbn}
is
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{2})}
-consistent, one can derive
P(y_{x})=P(y\mid x)
and conclude that
P^{M_{\cbn}}(y_{x})=P^{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(y_{x})
, successfully identifying a layer 2 quantity. However, if a model were
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, one could derive quantities such as
P(y_{x},z_{x})=P(y_{xz},z_{x})=P(y_{z},z_{x})=P(y_{z})P(z_{x})=P(y\mid z)P(z\mid x)
, also computable from observational quantities. This is not guaranteed to be true with the CBN, since counterfactual quantities like
P(y_{z},z_{x})
are not guaranteed to be defined in
\mathbf{P}^{*}
, and even if they were, they could take any values since no part of the definition of CBNs constrains them.

\blacksquare

\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent but are not
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressive. Any model class in which Markovianity is enforced is an example of such a case, as shown in the following.

Given the causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
from the true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, consider
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
, the set of all Markovian SCMs that induce causal diagrams with a subset of the edges of
\mathcal{G}
. By construction,
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
is
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
since no constraint can be removed by removing edges, so any SCM in
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
has at least as many
\mathcal{L}_{3}
constraints as those induced by
\mathcal{G}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is the bow graph as shown in Fig.
19
, and the true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is defined as

\mathcal{M}^{*}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}&=\{U_{XY},U_{Y}\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathcal{F}&=\begin{cases}f_{X}(u_{XY})&=u_{XY}\\
f_{Y}(x,u_{XY},u_{Y})&=u_{XY}\oplus u_{Y}\end{cases}\\
P(\mathbf{U})&\text{ defined such that }U_{XY}\sim\bern(0.5),U_{Y}\sim\bern(0.1)\end{cases}

Evidently,
X
and
Y
are highly correlated, as
P(Y=1\mid X=1)=0.9
, but
P(Y=1)=0.5
. However, this correlation is not causal, as the input
X
remains unused by
f_{Y}
, implying that
P(Y_{X=1}=1)=P(Y=1)=0.5
. It is clear that no model in
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
will be able to represent
\mathcal{M}^{*}
on all three layers. For example, one may construct
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
as follows

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}&=\{U_{X},U_{Y}\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}&=\begin{cases}f^{\prime}_{X}(u_{X})&=u_{X}\\
f^{\prime}_{Y}(x,u_{Y})&=x\oplus u_{Y}\end{cases}\\
P(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})&\text{ defined such that }U_{X}\sim\bern(0.5),U_{Y}\sim\bern(0.1)\end{cases}

This model induces the graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
as shown in Fig.
19(b)
, so it is a valid model from
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, it can even reproduce
\mathcal{L}_{1}
quantities from
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, as it is clear that
P^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(Y=1\mid X=1)=0.9
, and
P^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(Y=1)=0.5
. This makes
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}

\mathcal{L}_{1}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
. However, on layer 2, the similarities break down. While in the true model,
X
has no effect on
Y
, in this case, the Markovian nature of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
means that
P^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(Y_{X=1}=1)=P^{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(Y=1\mid X=1)=0.9
, resulting in a different value.

Clearly
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is not
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistent with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, let alone
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-consistent. However, no other model from
\widetilde{\Omega}(\mathcal{G})
would be able to achieve
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistency either. In a Markovian model, the correlation between
X
and
Y
must be modeled through a causal effect from either
X
to
Y
or from
Y
to
X
, and both of these options would violate
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-consistency with
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

\blacksquare

Other examples of model classes that are
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistent but not
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressive include parametric SCMs in which the parameterization is not fully expressive, such as linear SCMs.

As shown in Thm.
2
, the class of
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs is
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressive. It may not be immediately clear why this expressivity holds on all three layers instead of only on layers 1 or 2. The intuition is that with the expressivity of neural networks, a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM can directly model the functions of the true SCM
\mathcal{M}^{*}
instead of indirectly matching its distributions. By matching the functions and utilizing the probability integral transform on uniform random variables to match
P(\mathbf{U})
, it is guaranteed that one can construct a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM to directly mimic the behavior of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
on the functional level, implying equivalence on all three layers. Consider the following example.

Suppose we are studying the effects of a drug (
X
) on the recovery of a disease (
Y
). For simplicity,
X
and
Y
are both binary,
X=1
represents the drug being taken, and
Y=1
represents a full recovery. There are two hypothesized models. In
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, taking the drug helps people without a certain gene (
U_{Y}=1
) but actively harms people with the gene (
U_{Y}=0
). In
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the drug has no effect. The two SCMs are clarified as follows:

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}_{1}&=\{U_{X},U_{Y}\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathcal{F}_{1}&=\begin{cases}f^{1}_{X}(u_{X})&=u_{X}\\
f^{1}_{Y}(x,u_{Y})&=x\oplus u_{Y}\end{cases}\\
P(\mathbf{U}_{1})&\text{ defined such that }U_{X},U_{Y}\sim\bern(0.5)\end{cases}

\mathcal{M}_{2}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}_{2}&=\{U_{X},U_{Y}\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}&=\begin{cases}f^{2}_{X}(u_{X})&=u_{X}\\
f^{2}_{Y}(x,u_{Y})&=u_{Y}\end{cases}\\
P(\mathbf{U}_{2})&\text{ defined such that }U_{X},U_{Y}\sim\bern(0.5)\end{cases}

It turns out that these two hypothesized models match in both
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. For example,
P^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}(Y_{X=1}=1)=P^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}(Y_{X=1}=1)=0.5
. The difference lies in their induced distributions for
\mathcal{L}_{3}
. For example,
P^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}(Y_{X=1}=1\mid X=0,Y=1)=0
. In words, in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, if it is observed that someone did not take the drug and recovered from the disease, then it is guaranteed that they would fail to recover if they had taken the drug. On the other hand,
P^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}(Y_{X=1}=1\mid X=0,Y=1)=P^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}(Y=1\mid Y=1)=1
since
X
simply has no effect on the disease.

The reasoning for this difference at the functional level is that both
f^{1}_{Y}
and
f^{2}_{Y}
have two different behaviors with respect to
X
depending on the setting of
U_{Y}
. When
U_{Y}=0
,
f^{1}_{Y}(x)=x
, while
f^{2}_{Y}(x)=0
. When
U_{Y}=1
,
f^{1}_{Y}(x)=1-x
, while
f^{2}_{Y}(x)=1
. In both cases, the functions are different between
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Notably, the output of
f^{1}_{Y}
depends on
X
while the output of
f^{2}_{Y}
does not.

We can reproduce this difference in behavior when constructing
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs to represent these SCMs.

Recall the binary step activation function,

\sigma(z)=\begin{cases}0&z<0\\
1&z\geq 0.\end{cases}

(36)

Using this activation function, we can create various useful neural network functions like the following:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{OR}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}-1\right)

bitwise OR

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{AND}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}-n\right)

bitwise AND

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{NOT}(z)

\displaystyle=\sigma(-z)

bitwise NOT

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\geq p}(z)

\displaystyle=\sigma(z-p)

\displaystyle\text{1 if }z\geq p

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{<p}(z)

\displaystyle=\hat{f}_{NOT}(\hat{f}_{\geq p}(z))

\displaystyle\text{1 if }z<p

With these functions, consider the following
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs,
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
that replicate the behavior of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
respectively.

\widehat{M}_{1}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1}&=\{\widehat{U}_{X},\widehat{U}_{Y}\}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{1}&=\begin{cases}\hat{f}^{1}_{X}(\widehat{u}_{X})&=\hat{f}_{<0.5}(\widehat{u}_{X})\\
\hat{f}^{1}_{Y}(x,\widehat{u}_{Y})&=\hat{f}_{OR}\begin{cases}\hat{f}_{AND}(x,\hat{f}_{NOT}(\hat{f}_{<0.5}(\widehat{u}_{Y})))\\
\hat{f}_{AND}(\hat{f}_{NOT}(x),\hat{f}_{<0.5}(\widehat{u}_{Y}))\end{cases}\end{cases}\\
P(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1})&\text{ defined such that }\widehat{U}_{X},\widehat{U}_{Y}\sim\unif(0,1)\end{cases}

\widehat{M}_{2}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{2}&=\{\widehat{U}_{X},\widehat{U}_{Y}\}\\
\mathbf{V}&=\{X,Y\},\text{ both binary}\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}&=\begin{cases}\hat{f}^{2}_{X}(\widehat{u}_{X})&=\hat{f}_{<0.5}(\widehat{u}_{X})\\
\hat{f}^{2}_{Y}(x,\widehat{u}_{Y})&=\hat{f}_{<0.5}(\widehat{u}_{Y})\end{cases}\\
P(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{2})&\text{ defined such that }\widehat{U}_{X},\widehat{U}_{Y}\sim\unif(0,1)\end{cases}

Note that
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
match
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, respectively, on all three layers. For example,
P^{\widehat{M}_{1}}(Y_{X=1}=1\mid X=0,Y=1)=0
and
P^{\widehat{M}_{2}}(Y_{X=1}=1\mid X=0,Y=1)=1
. This is because the construction of
\widehat{M}_{1}
and
\widehat{M}_{2}
were designed to directly match the functions of
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

\blacksquare

As a disclaimer, note that
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressiveness means the existence of a model that matches on all three layers, but it does not necessarily mean that such a model can be found. In the case of Example
7
, we could create
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs to match the hypothesized SCMs because the full specifications of the SCMs were provided. In practice, this is rarely the case, and it may not be possible to determine the correct NCM from training on data alone, which is why the identification problem is important to determine when inferences can be made from a trained NCM.

One of the major contributions emphasized in this work is the generality of the approach. In particular, Alg.
1
solves the counterfactual identification and estimation problems for any counterfactual query with any given input in any causal graph setting. Notably, we do not make the
Markovianity
assumption about the generating SCM.
Markovianity restricts the set of unobserved variables
\mathbf{U}
, requiring them to be independent and having each
U\in\mathbf{U}
as an input to at most one function in
\mathcal{F}
. This translates to the lack of bidirected edges in any causal diagram generated by a Markovian SCM (Fig.
23(a)
, for example). In other words, since no unobserved variable can be a common cause of two observed variables, Markovianity encodes the fact that there is no unobserved confounding.

Markovianity is frequently assumed in many works (including several related works in Appendix
C
) because it drastically simplifies the problem. When attempting to infer
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data and the causal diagram
\mathcal{G}
, it turns out that any interventional query
P(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{x}})
can trivially be identified as

P(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})P(\mathbf{z}),

(38)

where
\mathbf{Z}
is the set of non-descendants of
\mathbf{X}
; for details, see
(
Bareinboim et al. 2022
, Corol. 2)
.

Several observations follow from this observation. First, this implies that works that attempt to infer
\mathcal{L}_{2}
queries from
\mathcal{L}_{1}
data under the Markovianity assumption do not even need to consider the identification problem, as they are all identifiable given the graph.
Kocaoglu et al. 2018
is an example of a work that can directly estimate the query without having to be concerned about the identification problem due to the Markovianity assumption.

Second, when attempting to estimate the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
query, the estimand in Eq.
38
is the same for every query, so only one form of the query needs to be considered when designing an estimator. For example, although not explicitly assuming Markovianity, the works in Footnote
1
assume conditional ignorability, which implies the lack of unobserved confounding between the treatment
X
and the outcome
Y
. This allows the causal effect
P(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{x}})
to be identified as stated in Eq.
38
, so these works can focus directly on estimating the specific equation in Eq.
38
. On the counterfactual level, the identification problem may still need to be considered as there are non-identifiable
\mathcal{L}_{3}
queries even in the Markovian case. Still, the problem is drastically simplified as confounding is no longer an issue. In fact, works like
Pawlowski et al. 2020
and
Sanchez-Martin et al. 2021
rely on this simplification for counterfactual estimation in the Markovian case and do not consider the identification problem despite potential the lack of identifiability of various queries.

Third, another notable simplification introduced by Markovianity is that distributions from the SCM can often be factorized into a convenient form. For example, the observational distribution
P(\mathbf{v})
can be factorized as the product:

P(\mathbf{v})=\prod_{V\in\mathbf{V}}P(v\mid\mathbf{pa}_{V}),

where
\mathbf{Pa}_{V}
refer to the set of variables with an edge into
V
in
\mathcal{G}
, and
v
and
\mathbf{pa}_{V}
are consistent with
\mathbf{v}
. Similarly, an interventional distribution
P(\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{x}})
can be factorized as

P(\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{V\in\mathbf{V}\setminus\mathbf{X}}P(v\mid\mathbf{pa}_{V}),

where
v
and
\mathbf{pa}_{V}
are consistent with
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{x}
. Counterfactual distributions follow a similar pattern. For example, when considering the graph
\mathcal{G}
in Fig.
23(a)
,
P(y_{x},x^{\prime}_{z})
factorizes as

P(y_{x},x^{\prime}_{z})=\sum_{z^{\prime}}P(y\mid x,z^{\prime})P(x^{\prime}\mid z)P(z^{\prime}),

where
x\neq x^{\prime}
. The pattern is that in every case, each term in the estimand can be written as the probability of a variable given its parents. This allows for models to encode the mechanism for each variable
V
as simply the conditional distribution
P(V\mid\mathbf{Pa}_{V})
, abstracting away the functions
\mathcal{F}
and exogenous noise
P(\mathbf{U})
from the SCM. Architectures that benefit from this include DeepSCM from
Pawlowski et al. 2020
, which utilizes normalizing flows to model each conditional probability
P(V\mid\mathbf{Pa}_{V})
, and VACA from
Sanchez-Martin et al. 2021
, which fits a variational graph autoencoder to the factorized distributions. In contrast, in non-Markovian cases, these factorizations typically do not hold. For example, if the true SCM induces the graph in Fig.
23(c)
, then
P(y_{x})\neq P(y\mid x)
despite
X
being the only observed parent of
Y
. Works that rely on Markovian factorizations often do not have a straightforward generalization to non-Markovian cases.

Fourth, the works cited in this section that leverage the Markovianity assumption have still provided important contributions to the understanding and practical approaches to causal and counterfactual inference, and they are widely praised for their successes. However, Markovianity is a very restricting assumption and is unrealistic in many practical settings. For example, consider a simple study on the effects of exercise
X
on cholesterol
Y
, with age
Z
as a confounder. The relationship between variables can be modeled as shown in Fig.
23(a)
. If, in the study, data on age is simply not collected, then
Z
becomes an unobserved confounder (see Fig.
23(b)
), and Markovianity is immediately violated. The corresponding graph would look like Fig.
23(c)
with
Z
omitted. In many cases, collecting the data may not even be an option. For example, if
X
is smoking,
Y
is cancer, and
Z
is the expressiveness of a gene, perhaps collecting data on gene expressivity is too difficult or even impossible. In general, one cannot expect to be able to collect data on every relevant variable in the study, so unobserved confounding is to be expected in realistic settings.

The solution presented in this paper is therefore important to understand the more complex, realistic, and general setting of potentially having unobserved confounding. Since the lack of the Markovianity assumption greatly complicates the problem of counterfactual inference, many of the theoretical results in this work (e.g. Thms.
2
and
3
) required more involved proofs. The result is that using Alg.
1
,
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs can solve any instance of counterfactual identification and estimation even under unobserved confounding. This is demonstrated in the experiments, which consider several settings in which Markovianity is violated (e.g. the second, third, and fourth rows in Fig.
4
, where the unobserved confounding is shown visually in the causal diagrams as red dashed bidirected arrows).

Appendix E
Frequently Asked Questions

Q1.

Why do you assume that the causal diagram exists instead of learning it? Is it reasonable to expect that the causal diagram is available?

Answer
. For a detailed discussion on this topic, see Sec.
D.1
. In summary, the assumption of the causal diagram is made out of necessity. The Neural Causal Hierarchy Theorem (N-CHT)
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 1)
states that distributions from lower layers almost always underdetermine higher layers in a measure-theoretic sense
(
Bareinboim et al. 2022
, Thm. 1)
. This means that despite its universal expressivity, an NCM trained only on lower layer data (e.g., observational) cannot be reliably used for inferences on higher layers (e.g., interventional or counterfactual). Therefore, assumptions are necessary to make inferences across the PCH’s layers. The causal diagram is a flexible assumption that is used throughout the literature and encodes a qualitative description of the generating model, which is often much easier to obtain than the actual mechanisms of the generating SCM
(
Pearl 2000
;
Spirtes et al. 2000
;
Peters et al. 2017
)
. Given the N-CHT, any attempts to learn the causal diagram will be incorrect or not meaningful unless other assumptions are made in this process (e.g., faithfulness). While other types of assumptions can be made, all such assumptions have their own benefits and drawbacks. The goal of this paper is not to decide which set of assumptions is the best, but rather to provide the toolkit for data analysts to perform the inferences once the assumptions have already been made.

Q2.

How could the graphical assumption be relaxed?

Answer
. Relaxing the graphical assumption is out of the scope of this work since the goal of this paper is first to establish a principled approach of using neural models to perform counterfactual inferences under general well-understood assumptions. Still, finding a solution to the problem when only partial information about the graph is available is an interesting direction for future work. One such possible approach, discussed at the end of Sec.
D.1
, is to utilize equivalence classes of causal diagrams, learnable from data, to perform inferences
(
Jaber et al. 2018
;
Jaber et al. 2019
;
Jaber et al. 2022, forthcoming
)
. However, this approach has its own drawbacks, notably, that equivalence classes are not as descriptive as the true causal diagram, and therefore fewer queries will be identifiable. Still, this is one approach that can be considered in cases where assuming the availability of the full causal diagram is too stringent of a condition.

Q3.

Why do you assume non-Markovianity? Is this more general than solutions that assume Markovianity?

Answer
.
That is an interesting question. We do not assume “non-Markovianity”, but rather we do not make any assumption on Markovianity at all. Typically, Markovianity is the assumption and not the other way around. Markovianity requires that there exists no unobserved confounding, but works that do not assume Markovianity do not enforce such a requirement and are therefore more general because they work in both Markovian and non-Markovian cases. As mentioned earlier in the manuscript, it is hard to ascertain Markovianity in many practical settings, so not making the assumption is more reasonable in many natural settings.
Unlike many related works like those in Appendix
C
, this paper considers cases with unobserved confounding, which is represented graphically through dashed bidirected edges. This approach is more general in the sense that Alg.
1
can be used even in the presence of unobserved confounders, which is one of the major contributions of this paper. Markovianity is often assumed to simplify the problem setting, but it is often unrealistic to expect that data on all potential confounding variables are collected in a study. For a detailed discussion on this topic, see Appendix
D.4
.

Q4.

Why do you use the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM definition in Def.
2
with feedforward neural networks and uniform noise? Could the results in this paper also hold for other function classes and noise?

Answer
. The architectural choices made in Def.
2
are standard choices used to ground the discussion to a more concrete model. The same architecture is also used in the experiments. Still, the specific choice of architecture is not important to the theoretical results as long as key properties persist (e.g.
\mathcal{G}^{(\mathcal{L}_{3})}
-consistency,
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-
\mathcal{G}
expressiveness), which will often be the case, since the arguments to prove the theoretical results in this work are mostly independent of the architecture. In particular, Sec. D.1 of
Xia et al. 2021
, which introduces the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM, discusses generalizations of NCMs in terms of functions and noise which still guarantee the relevant theoretical results, so long as the functions are sufficiently expressive and the noise variables have well-defined probability density functions.

Q5.

Given that neural networks are universal approximators, it seems unsurprising that
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs can perform counterfactual inference. What makes
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs so special compared to other model classes?

Answer
. The structure of this paper follows the delicate balance between expressiveness and constraint. As emphasized by the Causal Hierarchy Theorem
(
Bareinboim et al. 2022
, Thm. 1)
and
(
Xia et al. 2021
, Corol. 1)
, expressivity alone is not enough to infer higher layer quantities from lower layer data. Without assumptions, any model, including NCMs, trained only on
\mathcal{L}_{1}
or
\mathcal{L}_{2}
data will not contain any meaningful information about
\mathcal{L}_{3}
quantities. Hence, assumptions in the form of constraints are discussed in Sec.
2
. One important question is how to properly incorporate such constraints into a model. Constraining a model too much may hinder its expressiveness, and it may no longer be able to express the true model. Therefore, a proper model class for counterfactual inference must be able to incorporate the constraints without losing expressivity within the constrained class.

The
\mathcal{G}
-NCM model class has both the critical properties of expressiveness and constraint (Thms.
1
and
2
) that are not enjoyed by many model classes. Examples of these properties are shown in Appendix
D.3
. Example
4
shows an example of a model class that, although expressive on all three layers, fails to properly incorporate the constraints of the graph,
\mathcal{G}
. Examples
5
and
6
show examples of model classes that incorporate the constraints of
\mathcal{G}
but fail to achieve expressiveness within the constrained class. In contrast to these examples, the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM model class has a natural and intuitive approach to incorporating the constraints of
\mathcal{G}
without damaging its expressiveness. These properties empower the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM the capability of solving the problems of counterfactual identification and estimation (see Thm.
3
, Corols.
1
and
2
, and Alg.
1
). Examples
8
and
9
concretely demonstrate this ability.

When compared to similar works, like the ones in Appendix
C
, these properties are not as clearly established. While many of the works do incorporate graphical constraints, these constraints are often taken for granted as they are not formally discussed. In contrast, the ideas of graphical constraints are developed formally in this work in Sec.
2
and Appendix
A.1
. Many models in the related works assume Markovianity, which fails in terms of expressiveness for the same reason as Example
6
. Some of the works only focus on the interventional (
\mathcal{L}_{2}
) level rather than the counterfactual (
\mathcal{L}_{3}
) level.

Q6.

Why would one prefer to identify counterfactual quantities using NCMs? Would it not be easier first to identify the query using symbolic approaches, then use the NCM for estimation?

Answer
. For a detailed discussion on the difference between symbolic and optimization approaches, we encourage the reader to read Appendix C.4 of
Xia et al. 2021
. In summary, the approach in this paper utilizes
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs to serve as a proxy model for the true generating SCM. In contrast, existing symbolic approaches like
Correa et al. 2021
work directly with the graph and data, which is on a level of abstraction without the generating SCM. These works typically derive an exact answer for the target query by directly using the constraints of the graph. Indeed, one could use a hybrid approach of using a symbolic algorithm to identify a counterfactual query followed by training a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM to obtain an estimate. This is certainly an option, and the goal of this paper is to provide another solution route, not to decide for the data analyst which approach is better.

The proxy model approach presented in this paper is insightful to the problem of counterfactual inference because it shows that
\mathcal{G}
-NCMs are capable of jointly solving both counterfactual identification and estimation, which typically appear together in practice. Solving both problems together indicates that the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM is capable of both reasoning whether the query has any counterfactual meaning through identification and providing the practical benefits of statistical modeling through estimation. There are many reasons why one may prefer a proxy-based approach. First, the generative properties of the learned proxy model allow it to serve as an infinite source of samples for the user. Second, the trained model could provide quick estimation results for multiple queries without the need for retraining, provided the queries are identifiable. Third, the idea of minimizing and maximizing the query in Alg.
1
is central to identification problems. Hence, a similar procedure can be used in similar identification problem settings without an existing symbolic solution. Fourth, this idea can potentially be used to bound non-identifiable queries, which is an exciting avenue for future work.

Q7.

Why would one want to learn an entire SCM to solve the estimation problem for a specific query instead of just directly estimating the query?

Answer
.
Indeed, if the user simply wants to estimate one query and is knowledgeable about the estimand, it may be more efficient, statistically or computationally, to focus efforts on just estimating that query. However, there are many reasons why the proxy model approach used in this work may be beneficial. First, by learning an entire SCM and estimating queries using Eq.
4
, one can apply the same procedure to any potential query, reducing the complexity of the solution. This relieves the user of the need to derive an estimand. Even if the estimand is known, developing a specific approach to estimating this estimand can be difficult depending on the form of the estimand. Second, if the user wants to estimate multiple queries, they could potentially reuse the same
\mathcal{G}
-NCM without retraining, provided that the queries are identifiable. Third, as a generative model, the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM can be used as an infinite source of samples should the user desire this in addition to having an estimate for the query.

Q8.

Why is the GAN architecture used for the experiments?

Answer
.
We found that using a GAN approach was the most straightforward way to implement the
\mathcal{G}
-NCM in practice. When using an implicit generative model approach, one can construct a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM exactly as described in Def.
2
and train it as specified in Alg.
3
. Using a discriminator network as the divergence function (
\mathbb{D}_{P}
) provides a flexible and practical approach to generating a loss for optimization, as understood in GAN literature. Alternative approaches to modeling the divergence functions all have practical drawbacks. For example, KL divergence is typically intractable to compute directly. Appendix
B.6.1
explains why an explicit generative model approach like the MLE-NCM (used in
Xia et al. 2021
) is significantly more complicated. We also include experiments comparing against the MLE-NCM, which demonstrated some key flaws of the MLE approach, such as poor runtime scalability (see Fig.
5
) or having a bias to output “ID” even in non-ID settings (see Fig.
4
). For further results, see Appendix
B.1
. Still, the key theoretical results in this work do not depend on the specific architecture used in practice, and the choice of using GANs is unimportant in this discussion. Users can use any architecture they see fit while still applying the results in this work.

Q9.

Given the training instability of GANs, is the GAN-NCM approach guaranteed to work?

Answer
. Indeed, the theoretical results described in Sec.
3
assume that infinite data is provided and optimization is perfect, which is unrealistic in practice. Hence, a hypothesis testing procedure is required to decide identifiability, described in Appendix
B.4
. This means that the results from solving the identification and estimation problems will have some degree of error in practice. While deriving bounds on the error could be an interesting direction for future work, the error is shown to be quite manageable empirically using GANs, as shown in the experiments in Sec.
5
. In the estimation experiments, the error is shown to decrease as samples increase, demonstrating a level of statistical consistency.

Q10.

Does the work apply to cases with continuous variables?

Answer
. The theoretical results in this work assume that the variable domains are finite, and no claims are made about continuous variables. If extending to the continuous case, on the theoretical side, counterfactual distributions and queries would have to be properly defined and new machinery based on measure theory evoked. It is non-trivial to prove some of the theoretical results of this paper in continuous settings.
While the exogenous variables and structural functions of the true model can take any form, this paper relies on the discreteness of the endogenous variables to ensure that there exists a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM that can exactly match the true model (see also
Zhang et al. 2022
). It is currently an open problem to generalize these results to the continuous case and in generality.

On the experimental side, sampling or maximizing/minimizing the query from a
\mathcal{G}
-NCM may have complications depending on the definition of a counterfactual query/distribution in a continuous setting. Intuitively, however, if the query can be sampled via Alg.
2
, then similar techniques applied in our paper can be extended without much change (although currently without theoretical guarantees). For example, if the query is
E[Y_{X=x}|X\geq 0]
with both continuous
X
and
Y
, one could (1) sample several instances of
X
, (2) filter out negative samples, (3) sample
Y
using the values of
\mathbf{U}
from the remaining instances, and (4) compute the average out of these values of
Y
. Then, this Monte Carlo estimate can be used in Alg.
3
for maximizing/minimizing the query and estimating the final result if identifiable. If the query is not so easily compatible with Alg.
2
, then the approach may need to be adjusted to accommodate continuous definitions.

Why are there no empirical studies comparing other neural estimation methods (such as the ones in Appendix

Answer
. Despite their notable contributions, as discussed earlier, related works like the one in Appendix

are not as general as the approach described in this work. For example, some works assume Markovianity, some only solve queries from

or specific queries from
\mathcal{L}_{3}

Q12.

How does the proposed approach scale to higher dimensional variables or with a greater number of variables?

Answer
. The training of the GAN-NCM scales tractably with higher dimensionality or variables, as shown in Fig.
5
. While having higher dimensions or more variables may require a larger architecture, and therefore more training time, typically this also comes with a higher data requirement. Hence, the architecture can be scaled with the complexity of the problem, and although complex problems will require more training time, the user may be willing to spend more computational resources on a more complex problem. We hope this work can be a baseline and act as a catalyst to the study of different neural architectures with different computational and statistical properties.
