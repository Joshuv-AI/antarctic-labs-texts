Hyperbolic 3-manifolds of low cusp volume
David Gabai, Robert Haraway, Robert Meyerhoff, Nathaniel Thurston, Andrew Yarmola

Abstract
We classify the complete hyperbolic 3-manifolds admitting a maximal cusp of volume at most 2.62. We use this to show that the figure-8 knot complement is the unique 1-cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold with nine or more non-hyperbolic fillings; to show that the figure-8 knot complement and its sister are the unique hyperbolic 3-manifolds with minimal volume maximal cusps; and to extend results on determining low volume closed and cusped hyperbolic 3-manifolds.

Introduction

A central goal in low dimensional topology is to relate various topological, combinatorial, geometric and algebraic structures associated to manifolds.
For hyperbolic 3-manifolds, there is the long-standing Thurston, Hodgson-Weeks, Matveev-Fomenko Hyperbolic Complexity Conjecture:
The complete low-volume hyperbolic 3 -manifolds can be obtained by filling cusped hyperbolic 3 -manifolds of small topological complexity.

One of the challenges of this open-ended conjecture is to identify the right notion of complexity, especially when restricting this conjecture to a particular class of manifolds.
This paper addresses the version of that conjecture for complete hyperbolic 3-manifolds with maximal cusps of low volume.
All manifolds in this paper are orientable.

In his revolutionary work in the 1970’s on the geometry of the figure-8 knot complement, Bill Thurston showed that it has exactly 10 non-hyperbolic Dehn fillings.
Thurston’s result spawned a tremendous amount of work towards understanding hyperbolic and non-hyperbolic fillings of hyperbolic 3-manifolds, e.g. see the surveys in

In particular, there are infinitely many 1-cusped hyperbolic manifolds with six non-hyperbolic fillings.
By 1998, there were (resp. eight, two, zero, one)) known 1-cusped manifolds with (resp. seven, eight, nine, ten) exceptional surgeries.
This led Cameron Gordon to conjecture in

The Gromov-Thurston

But coupling a length lower bound with a maximal horocusp area lower bound can lead to an improvement, and this was carried out by Bleiler and Hodgson ([BH92]) — using Adams’s area lower bound — to get a bound of 24 on the number of non-hyperbolic fillings.
Had the Cao-Meyerhoff area bound been available at that time, the non-hyperbolic-filling bound would have been reduced to 14.

In 2000, Agol and Lackenby independently improved

has word hyperbolic fundamental group (again Perelman implies that

Coupling the 6-theorem with the Cao-Meyerhoff bound led to a non-hyperbolic filling bound of at most 12 — tantalizingly close to the figure-eight case bound of 10. As the 6-theorem is sharp, attention was focused on area bound improvements.
However, a new area bound of 3.7 in the “non-Mom” case in

The

But Lackenby and Meyerhoff were able to prove in

In this section, we explain the computer-assisted proof of Theorem

The goal is to analyze low-cusp-volume hyperbolic 3-manifolds by studying the marked 3-generator bicuspid subgroups associated to their cusps. The space of all possible such marked groups with

We will use the upper-half-space model

.
Identifying
\Isom^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{3})\cong\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
, the action on
q=x+\mathfrak{i}y+\mathfrak{j}t
is given by

\pm\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot q=(aq+b)(cq+d)^{-1}.

Notice that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have that
z\,\mathfrak{j}=\mathfrak{j}\,\overline{z}
, so one computes

\pm\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\cdot t\,\mathfrak{j}=(a\,t\,\mathfrak{j}+b)(c\,t\,\mathfrak{j}+d)^{-1}=\frac{t^{2}\,a\,\overline{c}+b\,\overline{d}}{t^{2}|c|^{2}+|d|^{2}}+\frac{t\,\mathfrak{j}}{t^{2}|c|^{2}+|d|^{2}}.

(2.1)

In particular,
\mathfrak{j}
is mapped to a point of Euclidean height
1/(|c|^{2}+|d|^{2})
, which we will use later.

2.2
The compact parameter space

Let
Y
be an oriented cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold.
We identify
\Gamma=\pi_{1}(Y)
with a discrete torsion-free group of orientation-preserving Möbius transformations acting on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
Fix a maximal parabolic subgroup
\Pi\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}<\Gamma
and recall that the conjugacy class of
\Pi
corresponds to a cusp of
Y
.
Fix a maximal horoball
H
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with the property that
\Pi=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:int(\gamma\cdot H)\cap int(H)\neq\emptyset\}
. We say
\kappa=H/\Pi
is the
maximal horocusp
of the chosen cusp in
Y
.
It follows that there is an element
G\in\Gamma\smallsetminus\Pi
such that
G\cdot H\cap H=p
is a point.
We call the subgroup
\mathfrak{B}=\langle\Pi,G\rangle
a
bicuspid subgroup
of
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
bicuspid marking
is the tuple
(\Pi,H,G)
, where
\Pi
is a rank-two parabolic subgroup of
PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
,
H
is a horoball preserved by
\Pi
, and
G\cdot H
is a horoball tangent to
H
.
The associated bicuspid group is
\mathfrak{B}=\langle\Pi,G\rangle
.
The area of a bicuspid marking is the area of the (Euclidean) torus
\partial\kappa
.
Two bicuspid markings
(\Pi_{1},H_{1},G_{1})
and
(\Pi_{2},H_{2},G_{2})
are isomorphic if there is a group isomorphism
\psi:\mathfrak{B}_{1}\to\mathfrak{B}_{2}
with
\psi(\Pi_{1})=\Pi_{2}
,
\psi(G_{1})\cdot H_{2}
tangent to
H_{2}
, and
\psi^{-1}(G_{2})\cdot H_{1}
tangent to
H_{1}
.

A
geometric bicuspid marking
is one that arises from an oriented cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold. In particular,
\mathfrak{B}
is discrete, torsion-free, and
\inte(H/\Pi)
embeds and is maximal in
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\mathfrak{B}
.

Remarks 2.2
.

(i)

While
\Pi
is always discrete and isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}
, we don’t assume that
\mathfrak{B}
is discrete in a general bicuspid marking.

(ii)

An isomorphism between geometric bicuspid markings gives rise to an isometry between
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\mathfrak{B}_{2}
and vice versa.

Suppose we have a bicuspid marking
(\Pi,H,G)
. For computational reasons, we use a slightly different normalization than described in the introduction.
Working in the upper-half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, there is some isometry
\phi
taking
H
to a horoball at infinity.
Replacing
(\Pi,H,G)
with
(\phi\Pi\phi^{-1},\phi\cdot H,\phi G\phi^{-1})
, we may assume that every element of
\Pi
is of the form
z\mapsto z+c
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
, identifying
\Pi
as an additive subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
.
We can thus assign the length
|c|
to elements
z\mapsto z+c
of
\Pi
.
Let
M(z)=z+\lambda
be a shortest nontrivial element of
\Pi
.
Conjugating by
z\mapsto\lambda^{-1}z
, we may assume the shortest-length nontrivial translation
M
in
\Pi
is
z\mapsto z+1
. We may choose
N(z)=z+L
to be a shortest element of
\Pi
linearly independent of
M
(i.e.
next-shortest
).
With
g
the inverse of
G
, we have that
g\cdot H
is also a horoball tangent to
H
, centered at some point
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
Conjugating by
z\mapsto z-c
, we may assume
g\cdot H
is centered at 0.
The horoball
G\cdot H
, called the
Adams horoball
, has center
P=G(\infty)\in\mathbb{C}
.
These normalizations imply that
G(z)=P+1/(S^{2}z)
for some complex number
S
, which controls the height and rotation of the Adams horoball.

Definition 2.3
.

A
bicuspid triple
is a triple
\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
with
S\neq 0
. It gives rise to Möbius transformations
M_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)=z+1
,
N_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)=z+L
, and
G_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)=P+1/(S^{2}z)
. We name these parameters:

P
is the parabolic parameter;
S
is the (loxodromic) square-root parameter; and
L
is the lattice parameter.

We represent the associated transformations in
\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
by matrices up to sign as follows:

G_{\mathfrak{p}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}PS\,\mathfrak{i}&\mathfrak{i}/S\\
S\,\mathfrak{i}&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad g_{\mathfrak{p}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathfrak{i}/S\\
-S\,\mathfrak{i}&PS\,\mathfrak{i}\end{pmatrix}

M_{\mathfrak{p}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad N_{\mathfrak{p}}=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

To associate a bicuspid marking to
\mathfrak{p}
, we need to choose a horoball
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
centered at
\infty
. Since we want
G_{\mathfrak{p}}\cdot H_{\mathfrak{p}}
to be tangent to
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
, by equation (
2.1
), we must have

H_{\mathfrak{p}}=\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{3}:t>1/|S|\}.

In our parametrization, we have
\area(\partial\kappa_{\mathfrak{p}})=2\vol(\kappa_{\mathfrak{p}})=|S^{2}\,\imp(L)|
. To
\mathfrak{p}
, we associate the bicuspid marking
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}},H_{\mathfrak{p}},G_{\mathfrak{p}})
, where
\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle M_{\mathfrak{p}},N_{\mathfrak{p}}\rangle
. Note, we often drop the subscript when it is clear from context.

Given a geometric bicuspid marking
(\Pi,H,G)
of area
\leq 5.24
, we now show that there is a compact set
\mathscr{P}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
such that
(\Pi,H,G)
is isometric to
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}},H_{\mathfrak{p}},G_{\mathfrak{p}})
for some
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be the compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by the following conditions:

0.

|S|\geq 1

1.

\imp(S)\geq 0
,
\imp(L)\geq 0
,
\imp(P)\geq 0
,
\rep(P)\geq 0

2.

-1/2\leq\rep(L)\leq 1/2

3.

|L|\geq 1

4.

\imp(P)\leq\imp(L)/2

5.

\rep(P)\leq 1/2

6.

|S^{2}\,\imp(L)|\leq 5.24

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
(\Pi,H,G)
is a geometric bicuspid marking of area at most
5.24
. Then, there is
\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathscr{P}
such that
(\Pi,H,G)
is isometric to
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}},H_{\mathfrak{p}},G_{\mathfrak{p}})
.

Proof.

As above, we may assume
H
is centered at
\infty
and
\Pi=\langle M,N\rangle
, where
M(z)=z+1
is a shortest-length generator and
N(z)=z+L
is the next-shortest.
Since
N
is next-shortest, we have
|L|\geq 1
and
-1/2\leq\rep(L)\leq 1/2
.
If
\imp(L)<0
, then we replace
N
with
z\mapsto z-L
, which is also next-shortest and has
\imp(-L)>0
.

Next, let
P\in\mathbb{C}
be the center
G(\infty)
of the
Adams horoball

G\cdot H
.
By post-composing
G
with elements of
\Pi
, we may choose
G
such that
-\imp(L)/2\leq\imp(P)\leq\imp(L)/2
and
-1/2\leq\rep(P)\leq 1/2
.
Reflecting across the
x
-axis creates isomorphic groups with isometric quotients.
Thus we may assume
0\leq\imp(P)\leq\imp(L)/2
.
Reflecting across the
y
-axis allows us to assume likewise that
0\leq\rep(P)\leq 1/2
.
Finally, changing
S
to
-S
leaves
G
invariant, so we may assume
\imp(S)\geq 0
.
This determines
P
,
S
, and
L
and a bicuspid marking isomorphism. Properties (1), (2), (4), and (5) clearly hold and (3) holds since
N
is next-shortest. Further, since
\kappa_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a maximal horocusp in
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, we know that
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
has Euclidean height
\leq 1
. Thus,
1/|S|\leq 1
and
(0)
holds.

Finally, the area of
\partial\kappa_{\mathfrak{p}}
is
|S^{2}\im(L)|
, and this is at most
5.24
, so (6) holds. Since all the inequalities are satisfied,
(P,S,L)\in\mathscr{P}
as desired.

Note that the positive lower bounds for
|S|
and
|\imp(L)|
impose upper bounds for
|S|
and
|\imp(L)|
by using
|S^{2}\,\imp(L)|\leq 5.24
.
Thus
\mathscr{P}
is compact.
∎

2.3
Discrete points

Naturally, we are interested in points
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
, where
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is discrete, torsion-free, and geometric.
To investigate these points it is convenient to introduce the following notions:

Definition 2.6
.

The
free bicuspid group
is
\mathfrak{F}=\langle M,N,G\ \mid\ [M,N]\rangle\simeq(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\ast\mathbb{Z}.

For all
\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
with
S\neq 0
, the associated
bicuspid representation

\rho_{\mathfrak{p}}:\mathfrak{F}\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
is given by Definition
2.3
and has image
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
Let

\mathscr{D}=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}:im(\rho_{\mathfrak{p}})\text{ is discrete and torsion-free}\}.

Note that
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is automatically nonelementary for
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}
.
Let

\mathfrak{D}=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}:\mathfrak{p}\text{ corresponds to a geometric bicuspid marking}\}

The delicate distinction between
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathscr{D}
is that, in general, even if
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is discrete and torsion-free, there could be an element
h\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
such that
h\cdot H_{\mathfrak{p}}\cap H_{\mathfrak{p}}
has non-empty interior. Recall that
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
is explicitly defined using the
S
parameter. This means that
\mathfrak{p}
does not arise in the image of the map in Proposition
2.5
. We say that
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}\setminus\mathfrak{D}
are
incorrectly marked
(i.e. non-geometric since
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
is larger than the true maximal horoball of the group
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
).

Definition 2.7
.

For a word
w\in\mathfrak{F}
and
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}
, let

w(\mathfrak{p})=\rho_{\mathfrak{p}}(w)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}a_{w}(\mathfrak{p})&b_{w}(\mathfrak{p})\\
c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})&d_{w}(\mathfrak{p})\end{pmatrix}\in\PSL(2,\mathbb{C}).

Define

\mathcal{U}_{w}=\{\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathscr{P}:|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})/S|<1\}

Lemma 2.8

(Large horoball)
.

If
\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathcal{U}_{w}
for some
w\in\mathfrak{F}
of nonzero
g
-length, then
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
contains a relator of
g
-length at most the
g
-length of
w
.

Proof.

Let
H_{w}({\mathfrak{p}})
be the image of
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
under
w(\mathfrak{p})
. Recall that
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
has Euclidean height
1/|S|
, so by
2.1

H_{w}({\mathfrak{p}})
has Euclidean height
|S|/|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}
. Thus,
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
H_{w}({\mathfrak{p}})
intersect precisely when
|S|/|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}>1/|S|
, or equivalently
|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})/S|<1
.

Assume
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathcal{U}_{w}
, then
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is geometric and
H_{w}({\mathfrak{p}})
intersects
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
. But
H_{\mathfrak{p}}
is maximal in the geometric
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, so we must have
H_{\mathfrak{p}}=H_{w}({\mathfrak{p}})
and
w(\mathfrak{p})\in\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
. In particular,
m_{\mathfrak{p}}^{p}n_{\mathfrak{p}}^{q}w(\mathfrak{p})
is the identity for some
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
and has the same
g
-length as
w
.
∎

Definition 2.9
.

Let

\mathcal{K}_{w}=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{U}_{w}:|c(\mathfrak{p})|>0\text{ or }a(\mathfrak{p})\neq\pm 1\text{ or }d(\mathfrak{p})\neq\pm 1\}\subset\mathcal{U}_{w}

Corollary 2.10

(Killer word)
.

\mathfrak{D}\cap\mathcal{K}_{w}=\emptyset
for all
w\in\mathfrak{F}
.

Proof.

If
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{w}\subset\mathcal{U}_{w}
, then
w(\mathfrak{p})
is not a parabolic fixing
\infty
. In particular,
w(\mathfrak{p})\notin\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
, so by the proof of Lemma
2.8
we know that
\mathfrak{p}\notin\mathfrak{D}
.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

It is possible that
\mathcal{K}_{w}\cap\mathscr{D}
is non-empty. However, all such points would be incorrectly marked and can be ignored because every geometric bicuspid group of interest will appear in
\mathfrak{D}
by Proposition
2.5
.

The task of proving Theorem
1.8
boils down to proving that there is a finite cover of
\mathfrak{D}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{U}_{w_{i}}
, where each
w_{i}
is a word of
g
-length at most
7
. We describe the computational proof of this in the next section.

Before we move on, we need to define a few more notions that will be necessary for the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Since Theorem
1.2
uniquely identifies two manifolds, we need tools to find exact relators in bicuspid groups.

Definition 2.12
.

Define the variety of
w
by
\mathcal{V}_{w}=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}:w(\mathfrak{p})=\pm I\}

and consider the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{w}
of
\mathcal{V}_{w}
by

\mathcal{N}_{w}=\{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{P}:|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|<1\text{ and }|b_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|<1\}.

Lemma 2.13

(Variety neighborhood)
.

For all nontrivial
w\in\mathfrak{F}
, if
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}\cap\mathcal{N}_{w}
then
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{V}_{w}
. In particular,
w
is a relator in
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Proof.

The Shimizu-Leutbecher Theorem states that if

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

generate a discrete subgroup of
\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
and
|c|<1
, then we must have
c=0
.
For
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}
,
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is discrete and torsion-free, in particular,
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
w(\mathfrak{p})
generate a discrete subgroup of
\PSL(2,\mathbb{C})
.
Suppose
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}\cap\mathcal{N}_{w}
.
Dy definition
|c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|<1
, but discreteness implies
c_{w}(\mathfrak{p})=0
, so
w(\mathfrak{p})\in\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Further, since
|b_{w}(\mathfrak{p})|<1
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a shortest-length generator of
\Pi_{\mathfrak{p}}
, we must have
b_{w}(\mathfrak{p})=0
. Thus,
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{V}_{w}
.
∎

Lastly, we remark that nontrivial
w\in\mathfrak{F}
of

-length
\leq 3
are never the identity in geometric bicuspid groups.

Lemma 2.14

(
g
-length 3)

If

It is clear that any word of

off of itself, so

. For

2.4
Computational setup

To prove Theorem
1.8
, we take the same approach as
[
GMT03
]
.
In fact, all of our arithmetic code, as described in Appendix
D
, is the
exact same code
used in
[
GMT03
]
. This arithmetic relies on using 1-jets approximations with error and round-off error for computations, which often outperforms interval arithmetic. We briefly describe this arithmetic in Appendix
D.1
.

To start, we place the compact parameters space
\mathscr{P}
into a large box

\mathscr{B}=\{(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}:|x_{i}|\leq 2^{(19-i)/6}\},

where each subsequent side of the box is
1/\sqrt[6]{2}
times the size of the previous.
The embedding is given by
L=x_{3}+\mathfrak{i}x_{0}
,
S=x_{4}+\mathfrak{i}x_{1}
, and
P=x_{5}+\mathfrak{i}x_{2}
.
Since
|x_{1}|\leq 8
and
|x_{6}|\leq 2^{13/6}\approx 4.49
, we see that
\mathscr{B}
is large enough to contain all of
\mathscr{P}
.

The constant side ratio of
1/\sqrt[6]{2}
for
\mathscr{B}
is chosen specifically such that if we cut along the
1^{\text{st}}
dimension, then the two resulting boxes have the same constant side ratio as
\mathscr{B}
.
Cutting those along the
2^{\text{nd}}
dimension yields 4 boxes that still have the same constant side ratio.
This is reminiscent of the
1/\sqrt{2}
side ratio of A-series printer paper, except for
6
-dimensional boxes instead of rectangles in the plane.

This behavior yields two advantages.
First, the (sub-)boxes of
\mathscr{B}
obtained by cutting in this manner stay relatively “round,” making computational corrections for rounding error less dramatic.
Second, this subdivision allows us to encode these special (sub-)boxes in binary as
boxcodes
.
For example, a boxcode
0
corresponds to the box
\mathscr{B}_{0}
obtained by cutting
\mathscr{B}
in half along the
1^{\text{st}}
dimension and taking the resulting box on the “left.”
We fix some preferred orientation on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
to define “right” and “left.”
The box
\mathscr{B}_{01}
corresponds to cutting
\mathscr{B}_{0}
in half along the
2^{\text{nd}}
dimension and taking the piece on the “right.”
For deeper boxes, we keep cutting along the next dimension and after cutting along the
6^{\text{th}}
dimension, we start again with the
1^{\text{st}}
.
For a boxcode
\mathfrak{b}
, we let
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
denote the corresponding box.

To analyze all of
\mathscr{B}
, we build a binary tree
\mathscr{T}
corresponding to the boxcodes.
Each terminal node
\mathfrak{b}
of this tree will correspond to a box with an associated killer word or a necklace word (see the next paragraph).
The terminal nodes of the tree give a subdivision of
\mathscr{B}
into boxes of different sizes.
The reason for this approach is that the number of boxes needed is quite large
3
3

3

1,394,524,064 terminal boxes to be precise.
, if we were to divide
\mathscr{B}
into boxes all of the same size, our computation and verification time would exponentially increase.

A killer or necklace word at a terminal node
\mathfrak{b}
defines a collection of inequalities that must be checked to hold at every point of
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
.
These inequalities are only composed of basic algebraic operations
\pm,\times,/,\sqrt{\,}
, and absolute values.
They could in theory be checked by hand given an unreasonably large amount of time.
A word
w
is called a
killer word
for a box
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
if the encoded inequalities prove that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{K}_{w}
. By Corollary
2.10
, this condition corresponds either to indiscreetness or an incorrect marking of the bicuspid subgroup.

A
necklace word

w
at a terminal box
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
, corresponds to a word of
g
-length
\leq 7
and inequalities proving that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{U}_{w}
. In particular, if
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{U}_{w}
, then any geometric bicuspid triples in
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
must have a relator of the same
g
-length by Lemma
2.8
. In summary,

Proposition 2.15
.

An evaluation of the inequalities encoded in the binary tree
\mathscr{T}
proves that there is a finite collection of terminal boxes
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}
with associated necklace words
w_{i}
, such that
\mathfrak{D}\subset\bigcup_{i}\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}\subset\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{U}_{w_{i}}
. Further, each
w_{i}
has
g
-length at most
7
.

Proof.

The proof is contained in the data and code available at
[
Gitb
]
and can be checked using the
verify
program. The tree encodes a cover
\mathscr{B}=\bigcup_{i}\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}\cup\bigcup_{j}\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}\cup\bigcup_{k}\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{c}_{k}}
, where to each boxcode
b_{i}
, we associate a necklace word
w_{i}
, to each boxcode
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
a killer word
w_{j}^{\prime}
, and to each boxcode
\mathfrak{c}_{k}
a failed boundary condition. A failed boundary condition verification shows that one of the inequalities of Proposition
2.5
fails over the
entire
box
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{c}_{k}}
, showing that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{c}_{k}}\cap\mathscr{P}=\emptyset
for each
k
. The program also verifies killer word inequalities for
w_{j}^{\prime}
over
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}
for each
j
, which proves that
\mathfrak{D}\subset\bigcup_{i}\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}
. Finally, we also check necklace word inequalities for
w_{i}
over
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}
and the
g
-length of
w_{i}
for each
i
, which shows that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}_{i}}\subset\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{U}_{w_{i}}
where
g
-length of
w_{i}\leq 7
(and is nonzero) for all
i
.

The conditions are checked at all terminal nodes by traversing the binary tree from the root node (in depth-first order). A successful traversal of the tree guarantees that we have obtained a cover of
\mathscr{B}
, which corresponds to the root node. See the README of
[
Gitb
]
on how to use
verify
. ∎

The first result only relies on the structure of the parameter space itself, while the second result relies on the ability of the parameter space decomposition to rigorously find relators in Kleinian groups and being able to use those relators to identify specific hyperbolic 3-manifolds. 3.1
Cusp volume change under filling

By studying specific examples of
g
-length
\leq 7
relators, we can find some interesting patterns. In particular, if a cusped manifold has a relator
w
in its fundamental group then so do all of its Dehn filings. In the case of m129, the Whitehead link complement, such a relator defines a variety where the cusp volume is forced to be maximized at the discrete point corresponding to m129 and is strictly smaller for all discrete points on that variety.

First, we look at a special variety in our parameter space, which we will then show contains a geometric bicuspid group corresponding to a cusp of m129.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
gMGGMgN
is a relator in a bicuspid group
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
where
\mathfrak{p}=(P,S,L)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
with
S\neq 0
, then
P=L/2
,
S^{2}=-4/L
and, if
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is geometric, the maximal cusp area of
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is
4|\imp(L)/L|
.

Proof.

Using the parametrization

G=\pm\begin{pmatrix}PS\,\mathfrak{i}&\mathfrak{i}/S\\
S\,\mathfrak{i}&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad g=\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathfrak{i}/S\\
-S\,\mathfrak{i}&PS\,\mathfrak{i}\end{pmatrix}

M=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad N=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

We get that

gMGGMgN=\pm\begin{pmatrix}PS^{2}+1&PS^{2}(L-P)+L\\
S^{2}\left(PS^{2}+2\right)&PS^{4}(L-P)+S^{2}(2L-P)+1\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

Recall that the cusp area is given by
|S^{2}\,\imp(L)|
if
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is geometric. Since
S\neq 0
, the lower left entry tells us that
PS^{2}+2=0
. The top right entry then gives
P=L/2
. Since the top left entry is now
-1
, we must have

-1=PS^{4}(L-P)+S^{2}(2L-P)+1=1+(LS^{2})/2.

So
S^{2}=-4/L
and the area is
|S^{2}\,\imp(L)|=4|\imp(L)/L|\leq 4
when
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is geometric.
∎

4.1
Introduction to necklaces and horoball systems

Theorem
1.8
gives a nontrivial word that is the identity in the fundamental group of a complete hyperbolic 3-manifold with a cusp of low volume.
The next lemma shows that this algebraic condition corresponds geometrically to the existence of a

Definition 4.1
.

A
(k-)necklace

\eta=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{k})
is a cyclically ordered set of horoballs such that
N_{i}
is has disjoint interior from and is tangent to
N_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
.
Further, any two horoballs have disjoint interiors or coincide.
We also require that there are at least 3 distinct horoballs in
\eta
.
In what follows, the indices for a
k
-necklace are always taken cyclically with
N_{k+1}=N_{1}.

The
N_{i}
’s are called the
beads
, and
k
is called the
necklace
or
bead number
of
\eta.

We often abuse notation by denoting
\bigcup N_{i}
by
\eta.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
Y
is a complete hyperbolic 3-manifold
\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma
with a maximal cusp
\kappa.

Fix a bicuspid subgroup
\langle m,n,g\rangle\leq\Gamma
corresponding to
\kappa
.
Suppose
w
is a word in
m,

n,
and
g
that is the identity in
\Gamma
and has
g
-length
k.

Then there is a
k
-necklace in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
consisting only of lifts of
\kappa.

Proof.

See the proof of
[
Ago10
, Lemma 5.3]
.
Writing
w=\prod_{i=1}^{\deg_{g}(w)}\lambda_{i}g^{\pm}
where
\lambda_{i}\in\langle m,n\rangle
and letting
w_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}g^{\pm},
the desired necklace is
\{w_{i}(\mathcal{H}):1\leq i\leq k\}
. Note that in such a necklace a horoball could be visited multiple times, i.e. we could have
N_{i}=N_{i+j}
for some
j
with
|j|>1
.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

A
horoball system

(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
is a collection of horoballs
\{C_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
with pairwise disjoint interiors and
\mathcal{T}
is a subset of the tangency points between these horoballs.
We require that no triple
(C_{i},C_{j},C_{k})
of horoballs is pairwise tangent
via
tangencies in
\mathcal{T}.

We call
\mathcal{T}
the (true)
tangency set
.
We often abuse notation by denoting
\bigcup C_{i}
by
\mathcal{H}
and refer to a horoball system as
\mathcal{H}
when
\mathcal{T}
is clear from context.
A tangency

in
\mathcal{H}
is called

if
s\notin\mathcal{T}.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the set of
false tangencies
.

We say that
\eta
is a
necklace in

(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
if each

and

lies in
\mathcal{T}.

A necklace
\eta
is

if

implies
|i-j|=1.

It is

if it minimizes bead number among all necklaces in
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T}).

Since
\mathcal{H}
is locally finite, removing balls of radius

For necklaces in
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
,

is inherited from the system. For a necklace
\eta=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{k})

.

If

Then

Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.9
.
The proof breaks down into two main steps. First, using the terminology of this section, we prove that every geometric horoball system that contains some
\leq 7
-necklace must contain a (possibly different) globally minimal simple
\leq 7
-necklace
\eta

\leq 7

To attack step one, it is enough to find an unlinked
\leq 7

\leq 8

\leq 7

Theorem 4.15

(Unknotting Criterion)
.

A minimal necklace
\eta=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{k})

The backward direction of the second implication follows from the fact that if each

then, since ties can only meet at endpoints, the usual cut and paste argument produces a collection of such discs

is a simple closed curve in

\fram(\eta)
is unknotted.
The forward direction follows from the next topological lemma.
∎

Suppose

is

is

The four vertices of

Proof of Prop.
4.20
.

Suppose

away from small neighborhoods of the false tangencies, and at most one arc per side of such a tangency.

\mathscr{K}
and to all

We will prove the proposition by a base case and reduction to the base case.
It is a proof by induction on a notion of complexity that we leave implicit.

The base case is as follows.
Suppose that

and that for all

Then in particular,

Claim:

\leq 7
-necklace in an oriented geometric horoball system
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T}),
then
\eta
is unknotted, unblocked, and unlinked.

The proof will require a lot of technical geometric control of
\leq 7
-necklaces. Our goal is to prove that
\leq 7
-necklaces cannot wind around a “blocking” tie
\gamma_{b}
in a geometric horoball system. Note, this will require a good deal of work as this is false in the non-orientable case even for
6
-necklaces, see
[
AK13
]
.

We start with a few basic facts about horoball geometry.

Lemma 4.28

(Horoball distance)
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
be two horoballs with disjoint interiors in the upper-half-space model with
b_{i}=\partial_{\infty}B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and of Euclidean heights
h_{i}.

Then the hyperbolic distance
d_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1},B_{2})
between
B_{1},B_{2}
is given by

d_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1},B_{2})=\log\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}{h_{1}h_{2}}\right).

Proof.

Consider the point
b_{2}^{\prime}=b_{1}-(b_{2}-b_{1})
and let
\gamma
be the geodesic between
b_{2},b_{2}^{\prime}.

Note that
\gamma
has Euclidean radius
d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2}).

The highest point
P
of
\gamma
lies directly above (or below) the highest point of
B_{1}
in the upper-half-space model.
In particular, we have the distance
d_{\mathbb{H}}(P,B_{1})=\log\left(d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})/h_{1}\right).

Let
R_{\gamma}
be
180^{\circ}
rotation around
\gamma
.
In the plane through
b_{1},

b_{2},
and
\infty,

R_{\gamma}
acts as reflection in
\gamma,
or in Euclidean terms, inversion in
\gamma
’s associated circle.
Thus under
R_{\gamma},

B_{1}
maps to a horoball at infinity of Euclidean height
d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}/h_{1}.

Since
B_{1},B_{2}
had disjoint interiors, it follows that
h_{2}\leq d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}/h_{1}
and

d_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1},B_{2})=\log\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}{h_{1}h_{2}}\right).

∎

Lemma 4.29

(
\pi/3
-angle)
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
be two horoballs with disjoint interiors in the upper-half-space model with
b_{i}=\partial_{\infty}B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and of Euclidean heights
h_{i}.

Then the hyperbolic distance
d_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1},B_{2})
between
B_{1},B_{2}
is given by

d_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1},B_{2})=\log\left(\frac{d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{1},b_{2})^{2}}{h_{1}h_{2}}\right).

Definition 4.30
.

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with endpoints
b_{1},b_{2},
let
\eta
be a
k
-necklace with
\fram(\eta)\cap\gamma=\emptyset,
and let
\rho_{2}\in Isom(\mathbb{H}^{3})
map
b_{2}
to
\infty.

The
winding count of
\eta
around

is the absolute value of the winding number of
\pi(\rho_{2}(\fram(\eta)))
around
\rho_{2}(b_{1}).

Remark 4.31
.

This definition is independent of the orientation of
\eta,
exchanging endpoints of
\gamma,
and the choice of

Also, this definition is equivalent to the winding count around

(resp.
b_{2}
) of the piecewise circular arc given by circles through

(resp.

) and consecutive points of

at infinity.

Lemma 4.32

(Two eyes)
.

Let

be two disjoint full-sized horoballs and
\eta
a
k
-necklace of at most full-sized horoballs.
Assume that
\{B_{j},N_{i}\}
have disjoint interiors for all
i,j
and that
\eta
has positive winding count around both
\gamma(B_{1},H_{\infty})
and
\gamma(B_{2},H_{\infty}).

Then,

Proof.

We will use a visual angle argument to understand the positions of
\partial_{\infty}N_{i}.

Assume that

We can translate and rotate so that

(Three eyes)

a
k
-necklace of at most full-sized horoballs.

\{B_{j},N_{i}\}

\fram(\eta)
has positive winding count around both
\gamma(B_{1},H_{\infty})
and
\gamma(B_{2},B_{3}).

Let
d_{i\,j}=d_{\mathbb{E}}(b_{i},b_{j})

h_{j}

d_{1\,2}\leq d_{1\,3}.

D_{2}

Lemma 4.34

(Basic blocking properties)
.

Let
\eta
be a globally minimal
\leq 7
-necklace in a geometric horoball system
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
and assume that
\eta
is blocked by
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime},
where
B_{2}=H_{\infty}
of height 1.

Define the following as in Figure
15
.
Let
n_{i}=\partial_{\infty}N_{i}\in\mathbb{C},

d_{i}=\ell_{\mathbb{E}}(\pi(\gamma_{i})),

x_{i}=|n_{i}-b_{1}|,

\alpha_{i}=\angle\left(n_{i}-b_{1},n_{i+1}-b_{1}\right),
and
h_{i}=\text{height}_{\mathbb{E}}\left(N_{i}\right).
Then for all
i
one has

1.

\alpha_{i}\geq 0

2.

\pi/3\leq\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i+1}\leq 2\pi/3

3.

2\pi/3\leq\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i+1}+\alpha_{i+2}\leq\pi

4.

d_{i}^{2}=h_{i}\,h_{i+1}

5.

h_{i}\leq 1
and
d_{i}\leq 1

6.

\sqrt{h_{i}}\leq x_{i}

7.

x_{i}\leq 2\sqrt{h_{i}}

8.

1/4\leq h_{i}

Proof.

(4), (5), and (6) follow from Lemma
4.28
and disjointness.
For (1), we use the fact that
\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{6}=2\pi
and that
\alpha_{i}\leq\pi/3
by horoball packing.
For (2) and (3), we again use
\alpha_{j}\leq\pi/3
for the complementary set of angles.

Results (7) and (8) require a bit of calculus and geometry.
Without loss of generality, let
i=1
.
The law of cosines gives
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2\cos(\alpha_{1})x_{1}x_{2}=d_{1}^{2}=h_{1}h_{0}
and similarly for the triangle bounded by
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
.
Adding the two equations and solving for
x_{1}
gives:

2x_{1}=x_{0}\cos\alpha_{0}+x_{2}\cos\alpha_{1}\pm\sqrt{2h_{1}(h_{0}+h_{2})-2(x_{0}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})+(x_{0}\cos\alpha_{0}+x_{2}\cos\alpha_{1})^{2}}.

Since we want an upper bound, we take the
+
term. Notice that this equation is symmetric in the pairs
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})
and
(x_{2},\alpha_{1})
.
Additionally, it is easy to see that one can always flow towards the diagonal
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})=(x_{2},\alpha_{1})
while increasing the value of
x_{1}
, so there are no critical points on the interior of the realizable parameters.
Further, this can be done while enforcing
\pi/3\leq\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}

Note, flowing to the diagonal may take us through non-realizable parameters, but this doesn’t matter as we need an upper bound.
On the diagonal
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}\geq\pi/6
and basic calculus shows that
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\pi/6
maximizes the value of
x_{1}

This gives us
2x_{1}=\sqrt{3}x+\sqrt{2h_{1}(h_{0}+h_{2})-x^{2}}
where
x=x_{0}=x_{2}
.
Taking
h_{0}=h_{2}=1
further maximizes the value, with the final equation
2x_{1}=\sqrt{3}x+\sqrt{4h_{1}-x^{2}}
maximized at
x=\sqrt{3h_{1}}
and value
x_{1}=2\sqrt{h_{1}}
, giving us (7).

For (8), we solve the same sum of equations for
h_{1}
instead of
x_{1}
, giving

h_{1}=\frac{2x_{1}^{2}+x_{0}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-2x_{1}(x_{0}\cos\alpha_{0}+x_{2}\cos\alpha_{1})}{h_{0}+h_{2}}.

A similars observation show that it is possible to flow to the diagonal
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})=(x_{2},\alpha_{1})
while keeping
\pi/3\leq\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}
and decreasing
h_{1}
.
On the diagonal,
h_{1}
is minimized when
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\pi/6
.
Notice that there are parameters with
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\pi/6
that a realized by necklaces, so we can restrict to this case.
At such a realizing parameter, the horoballs
N_{0}
and
N_{2}
are visually
\pi/3
apart.
This forces all angles
\alpha_{i}
for
i\neq 0,1
to be
\pi/3
, which in turn forces the horoballs
N_{i}
for
i\neq 1
to be full-sized and form the hexagonal packing.
It is then easy to see that the smallest Euclidean height for
N_{1}
is
1/4
, giving us (8). ∎

Lemma 4.35

(Blocking edge)
.

Let
\eta
be a globally minimal
7
-necklace in a geometric horoball system
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T}).

If
\eta
is blocked by
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
and
\zeta=(N_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,N_{7}^{\prime})
is a conjugate of
\eta
containing
B_{1}
and
B_{2},
then
\{B_{1},B_{2}\}=\{N_{i}^{\prime},N_{i+1}^{\prime}\}
for some
i.

Proof.

The distance in
\zeta
between
B_{1},B_{2}
is at most 3.
If
B_{1}\cap B_{2}
is not part of
\zeta,
then adding this tangency to the shortest path between
B_{1},B_{2}
makes a
\leq 4
-necklace.
This contradicts global minimality of
\eta.

∎

For the remainder of this section,
\eta
will be a globally minimal
7
-necklace in a geometric horoball system
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
and will be blocked by
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}.

Let
\gamma_{b}=\gamma(B_{1},B_{2})
and for each tie
\gamma_{i}
for
\eta,
let
f_{i}
be the element acting on
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
that takes
\gamma_{b}
to
\gamma_{i}.

Set
\eta_{i}=f_{i}(\eta)
and notice that
\eta_{i}
is blocked by
\gamma_{i}
by construction.

Notation 4.36
.

For two necklaces
\eta
and
\zeta
in a horoball system, we use
\eta\Cap\zeta
to denote the set of shared horoballs.
This is their intersection as finite sets of horoballs, not their intersection as subspaces of hyperbolic space.
The latter may include tangency points; the former does not.

Lemma 4.37

(Forced share)
.

If
\eta_{i}
does not contain
B_{1}
or
B_{2},
then
\eta_{i}\Cap\eta\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
\eta_{i}
does not contain
B_{2}
and conjugate the picture so that
B_{2}=H_{\infty}.

Let
\pi_{i}=f_{i}\circ\pi\circ f_{i}^{-1}
be the “projection” from
n_{i}
or
n_{i+1},
with notation as in Lemma
4.34
.
By construction, the winding count of
\pi_{i}(\fram(\eta_{i}))
is nonzero around either
n_{i}
or
n_{i+1}.

As
\eta_{i}
is blocked by
\gamma_{i},

\pi_{i}(\fram(\eta_{i}))\cap\pi(\fram(\eta))
must contain a component lying entirely in
\pi(\gamma_{i}).

Positive winding count implies that
\pi_{i}(\fram(\eta_{i}))\cap\pi(\fram(\eta))
has at least two connected components.
A second component either contains
n_{j}
for some
j
or, for some
k\neq i,

\gamma_{k}
passes under or over some tie
\kappa
of
\eta_{i}.

However, the latter case violates Lemma
4.33
as either
\eta
is blocked by
\gamma_{b}
and
\kappa
or
\eta_{i}
is blocked by
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{k}.

Thus,
\eta\Cap\eta_{i}
is non-empty and contains some horoball of
\eta.

∎

Lemma 4.38

(No accidental true tangencies)
.

B_{k}\cap N_{j}\notin\mathcal{T}
for all
k=1,2
and
j=1,\ldots,7.

Proof.

Assume otherwise. Without loss of generality, let
B_{k}=B_{2}
and
N_{j}=N_{1}
with
B_{2}\cap N_{1}\in\mathcal{T}
.
Send
B_{2}
to
H_{\infty}
and let
f
acting on
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{T})
take
\gamma(B_{1},H_{\infty})
to
\gamma(N_{1},H_{\infty}).

Then
f(\eta)
is a necklace of at most full-sized horoballs that winds around
\gamma(N_{1},H_{\infty}).

Projecting to the plane, we obtain piecewise linear curves
\mu=\pi(\fram(\eta))
and
\mu_{f}=\pi(\fram(f(\eta)))
, which are embedded by Lemma
4.34
.
Each curve then divides the plane into two open regions: the “inside” containing
b_{1}
or
n_{1}
accordingly, and the “outside”.

Notice that if
\gamma
is a tie of
\fram(f(\eta))
with one endpoint inside
\mu
, the other outside
\mu
, and passes over
\fram(\eta)
, then
\eta
has non-trivial winding count around
\gamma
.
However, this would contradict the Three Eyes Lemma
4.33
as
\eta
also has non-trivial winding around
\gamma(B_{1},H_{\infty})
.
By symmetry, the same applies to any tie of
\fram(\eta)
that has endpoints on either side of
\mu_{f}
and goes over
\fram(f(\eta))
.

Since
\mu
and
\mu_{f}
are both simple, there are three possible isotopy types, which we now consider.

Case 1:

\mu_{f}
winds around
b_{1}
.
This is impossible by the the Two Eyes Lemma
4.32
.

Case 2:

\mu_{f}
does not wind around
b_{1}
or
\mu_{f}
does goes through
b_{1}
.
For this to hold,
\mu_{f}
must intersect the half-open line segment
[b_{1},n_{1})
between
b_{1}
and
n_{1}
.
In particular,
\mu_{f}
must cross from outside of
\mu
to the inside, and then again from inside to outside.
Looking at the ties, we have seen that these crossing cannot arise from
\fram(f(\eta))
passing over
\fram(\eta)
.
Every underpass of
\fram(f(\eta))
under
\fram(\eta)
must be cancelled out by another underpass, because a tie of
\fram(\eta)
lying over such an underpass must have endpoints on the same side of
\mu_{f}
.
Thus, by parity, we must have at least two crossings via shared horoballs
A_{1}

5.2
Sanity checks

The previous section technically completes our solution to problem NE.
However, a few additional considerations speed up our computations.
We turn to these now.

First, all necklace gluings are orientable.
Likewise, they are all connected.
If we were testing arbitrary MOM gluings, then we would add a connectedness test.
One other requirement, though, is that the links of all the vertices must be tori.
Many necklace gluings are not generalized link exteriors, and hence are of no concern to us in our enumeration.
So, we must implement a test for being a generalized link exterior.

Another fast, extremely useful check is a fundamental group check.

Regina
has routines for calculating and simplifying presentations of fundamental groups of triangulations (and ideal triangulations).
A significant plurality of necklace gluings of bead number at most 7 admit fundamental group presentations of one of the following forms, and these presentations admit no nonelementary embeddings into

Problem Bead

We now enumerate all necklace manifolds with a given number of beads, solving problem Bead.
The representations of the manifolds are the same as in

The actual enumeration is also done similarly, but with an appeal to isomorphism signatures instead of to symmetries of dipyramids.

We review the representations briefly.
A full handle structure without 3-handles deformation retracts to a “core” 2-skeleton of the handle structure.
This 2-skeleton is a spine for the 3-manifold.
Taking the dual of this complex gives one an ideal cellulation

We triangulate an

6.1.2
Heuristics for determining hyperbolicity of closed 3-manifolds

We now turn to the question of determining the hyperbolicity of closed 3-manifolds.
With Regina we can at least determine whether or not a closed 3-manifold admits any faults, using the algorithms from section
5.1.2
.
If a closed, orientable 3-manifold admits a fault, then it is not hyperbolic.
If, on the other hand, it admits no fault, then all we can conclude is that it is geometric.
If it is not hyperbolic, then the 3-manifold is small-Seifert-fibered.
There are algorithms to recognized small-Seifert-fibered manifolds, given by Tao Li in
[
Li06
]
and Hyam Rubinstein in
[
Rub04
]
.
Unfortunately their algorithms are not yet feasible to implement in code.
Fortunately, Regina is able to recognize some triangulations of many such 3-manifolds.
In particular, it recognizes all the nonhyperbolic faultless closed triangulations we encountered as small-Seifert-fibered spaces, after some randomizations and simplifications.

Thus we turn to figuring out how one can verify the hyperbolicity of a closed 3-manifold.
In many cases SnapPy in SAGE can already do so, when the given triangulation is a geometric triangulation—that is, when the triangulation supports a solution to Thurston’s gluing equations each tetrahedron of which is positively oriented.
Our methods then amounted to coaxing out various different triangulations of the same 3-manifold, until finding one that SnapPy can prove geometric.
Previous methods incorporated the taking of covers.
But we found this did not help much.
The techniques we found most useful for proving hyperbolicity were partial filling and dual curve drilling.

We first remind the reader briefly of how SnapPy works with closed 3-manifolds.
At the core of SnapPy is a modification of Newton’s method, applied to Thurston’s gluing equations for an ideal triangulation.
If the triangulation is not ideal in the usual sense, then the solution space for the equations might not be 0-dimensional, regardless of the conditions one places on the cusps.
SnapPy’s Newton’s method is not designed for this situation.
Instead, one represents a closed 3-manifold as a Dehn filling of a generalized link exterior.
With an ideal triangulation of the generalized link exterior, one gets a 0-dimensional solution space after imposing the completeness equations as usual.
But also, one gets a 0-dimensional solution space by imposing, instead of the completeness conditions, a modified condition on the cusp depending on the choice of Dehn filling coefficient.
If SnapPy’s Newton’s method settles on a putative solution to these modified equations, and SAGE verifies this solution, and if, of course, the tetrahedra are positively oriented, then the incompleteness in the structure amounts to a missing geodesic.
Filling in this geodesic recovers the original closed 3-manifold, and proves it admits a hyperbolic structure.

In the course of our work, we are given a generalized link exterior
M
and a Dehn filling coefficient
c
on its boundary, and we wish to determine whether or not
M(c)
is hyperbolic.
One has several options at this point.
The most obvious option is to attach to Thurston’s gluing equations the cusp conditions corresponding to
c
, and hope SnapPy finds a geometric solution to those equations.
If this does not work, one has the following two other options.
If
M
is multiply-cusped, then one may pick a cusp
\tau
, take the slope
s
of
c
on
\tau
, construct an ideal triangulation of
M(s)
, and consider the generalized link exterior
M(s)
with the Dehn filling coefficient
c-s
.

M(s)(c-s)
is homeomorphic to
M(c)
.
But SnapPy very well may be able to find a positive solution for the equations coming from
M(s)(c-s)
and not on those coming from
M(c)
.
This was a useful technique, which decreased the number of cusps.
The opposite technique, that of drilling out curves instead of filling them in, also proved useful.
SnapPy can drill out a curve in the dual 1-skeleton of a triangulation, putting an ideal triangulation on its complement, and remembering which slope on the new boundary component is the meridian
\mu
.
(We thank Neil Hoffman for pointing out this feature to us.)
Again, SnapPy might be able to find a positive solution for the equations on
(M-\gamma)(c+\mu)
and not on
M(c)
.
These heuristics sufficed for us to verify the hyperbolicity of the hyperbolic closed 3-manifolds we encountered.

6.1.3
Resolving Gordon’s conjecture

To resolve Gordon’s conjecture, we now only require an accounting of exceptional slopes.
Suppose
M
is a hyperbolic 3-manifold with 2 cusps.
We wish to determine the superexceptional Dehn fillings of
M
.
To that end, recall that for us a slope
s
is
short
when it is of length at most 6 on a maximal torus boundary of its associated cusp.
We may first classify the short slopes of
M
on each cusp into hyperbolic and exceptional.
It is routine to accomplish such a classification.
To enumerate short slopes on
\tau
, just have SnapPy determine an area form estimate on
\tau
, then use the methods of section
6.1.1
.
To determine which among them are hyperbolic and which are exceptional, use the methods of sections
5.1.2
and
6.1.2
.
For each cusp
\tau
, let
hyp(\tau)
be the hyperbolic short slopes on
\tau
and
exc(\tau)
the exceptional slopes, and let
short(\tau)
be all the short slopes.

Pick a cusp
\tau
.
For each hyperbolic short slope
s
on
\tau
, we may determine the exceptional fillings of
M(s)
as above.
This directly decides whether or not
M(s)
is superexceptional.
So our problem comes down to determining whether or not there are any superexceptional long fillings on
\tau
.
There are infinitely many such slopes, so a simple enumeration is out of the question.
We use the following lemma to treat these slopes.
Here, we let
hyp(s)
and
exc(s)
be the short hyperbolic and exceptional slopes on the unique cusp of
M(s)
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
M
is an orientable hyperbolic 2-cusped 3-manifold.
Suppose
\tau
is a cusp of
M
and
s
is a long slope on
\tau
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}
be the other cusp of
M
.
Then

|exc(s)|\leq|exc(\tau^{\prime})|+|\{s^{\prime}\in hyp(\tau^{\prime})\ |\ s\in exc(s^{\prime})\}|.

(6.1)

Proof.

Suppose that
s
is a long slope on
\tau
.
Then by the 6-Theorem,
M(s)
is hyperbolic.
Suppose
s^{\prime}
is an exceptional filling of
M(s)
.
We may interpret this as the exceptional filling
M(s,s^{\prime})
of
M
.
Since this is an exceptional filling and
s
is long, by the 6-Theorem,
s^{\prime}
must be short on its cusp
\tau^{\prime}
in
M
.
So
s^{\prime}\in short(\tau^{\prime})
.
This already gives us the bound
|exc(s)|\leq|short(\tau^{\prime})|=|hyp(\tau^{\prime})|+|exc(\tau^{\prime})|
.
However, we may do even better.
Suppose
s^{\prime}\in hyp(\tau^{\prime})
and
M(s,s^{\prime})
is exceptional.
Then
s
is an exceptional slope on
M(s^{\prime})
.
So
s\in exc(s^{\prime})
.
We thereby get the better bound

|exc(s)|\leq|exc(\tau^{\prime})|+|\{s^{\prime}\in hyp(\tau^{\prime})\ |\ s\in exc(s^{\prime})\}|.

∎

and second, that, of course, we assume

Appendix D
Computational aspects

In this appendix, we will go over the technical details needed to validate Propositions
2.15
and
3.5
. We organize the discussion around the theoretical aspects and the arithmetic of complex affine 1-jets, followed by a description of the files, code, and data provided.

As described in Section
2
, the proofs are provided as binary trees where each terminal node corresponds a (sub)box of the parameter space
\mathscr{B}
with a label that encodes an inequality which holds for all points in that box. This inequality is rigorously verified by the programs
verify
and
identify
to hold over the box. Each inequality is encoded either as a boundary condition or a word. In the following sections, we prove the validity of how each inequality is constructed and the validity of the arithmetic used by
verify
.

Recall that the main parameter space objects are the parent box
\mathscr{B}
, the compact subset
\mathscr{P}
that is guaranteed to contain a bicuspid triple for every cusped hyperbolic 3-manifolds with cusp volume
\leq 2.62
, and
\mathfrak{D}
, the set of all geometric bicuspid triples in
\mathscr{P}
.

We have four types of terminal conditions at a (sub)box
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
of
\mathscr{B}
. These are:

•

Boundary conditions — these prove that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
lies outside of
\mathscr{P}
by showing that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
that the inverse of one of the inequalities in Proposition
2.5
holds over the entire box.

•

Killed words — a killer word
w
associated to
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
is used to prove that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{K}_{w}
, which by Corollary
2.10
means that
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\cap\mathfrak{D}=\emptyset
. This is equivalent to saying that the bicuspid triples in
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
are either indiscrete or are incorrectly marked, meaning that a correct marking is found elsewhere in
\mathscr{P}\mathscr{B}
.

•

Necklace words — a necklace word
w
associated to
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
is used to prove
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{U}_{w}
, which by Lemma
2.8
means that any triple in
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\cap\mathfrak{D}
is a relator of
g
-length at most that of
w
. In
verify
, we allow this
g
-length to be at most
7
, while in
identify
we limit this to
3
.

•

Variety words — a variety word
w
associated to
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
is used to prove
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\subset\mathcal{V}_{w}
, which by Lemma
2.13
means that
w
is a relator for any bicuspid group associated to a triple in
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}\cap\mathfrak{D}
. This condition is only used in
identify
.

The inequalities in the definitions of
\mathcal{K}_{w}
,
\mathcal{U}_{w}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{w}
are straightforward to check after constructing the matrix corresponding to
w
at points of the box
\mathscr{B}_{\mathfrak{b}}
. To prove that these conditions hold over the entire box, we use 1-jets with error and round-off error for computations as is done in
[
GMT03
]
. If one wanted to use interval arithmetic instead, we expect that further subdivision would be necessary.

D.1
1-jets and error control

Our computational tool for verifying the above mentioned inequalities are 1-jets. Let

\mathscr{A}=\{(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:|z_{i}|\leq 1\}

and consider a (holomorphic) function
g:\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{C}.
A
1
-jet approximation with error
\epsilon
of
g
is a linear map
(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto c+a_{0}z_{0}+a_{1}z_{1}+a_{0}z_{2}
such that

|g(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})-c+a_{0}z_{0}+a_{1}z_{1}+a_{0}z_{2})|\leq\epsilon.

Considering all maps approximated by a given 1-jet, one defines the jet-set

S(c,a_{0},a_{1},a_{2};\epsilon)=\{g:\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{C}:|g(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})-c+a_{0}z_{0}+a_{1}z_{1}+a_{0}z_{2})|\leq\epsilon\}.

In
[
GMT03
]
, the authors derive the arithmetic for jets. For example, given jet-sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, they show to to compute the parameters for a jet-set
S_{3}
where
f\cdot g\in S_{3}
for all
f\in S_{1}
and
g\in S_{2}
. They do this for all basic arithmetic operations
\pm,\times,/,
and
\sqrt{\,}
. In code, we call these
ACJ
s for “affine complex jets.”

For our computations, we use this exact same arithmetic. Note, to make this arithmetic fast, the parameters of
S(c,a_{0},a_{1},a_{2};\epsilon)
are all given as floating-point doubles. Recall that floating-point numbers are a finite subset of the reals represented via a list of bits on a computer. As such, any arithmetic operation between two floating-point numbers must make a choice about which floating-point result to give, as the real value of this result may not be representable as floating-point number. On a machine conforming to the IEEE-754 standard
[
iee85
]
, all operations performed with floating points numbers are guaranteed to round in a consistent way to a closest floating-point representative, as long as overflow and underflow have not occurred. The code supplied as part of
verify
and
identify
checks that underflow and overflow does not happen during validation.

Just as in Section 7 of
[
GMT03
]
, each boxcode corresponds to a (sub)box of our parameter space that has a floating-point center
(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5})
and a floating-point size
(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5})
.
Using the IEEE-754 standard, the floating-point size is large enough so that the floating-point version of the box,
i.e.
\{(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}:|x_{i}-c_{i}|\leq s_{i}\}
,
contains the true box as a proper subset.
Recall that over a box we associate the coordinate functions
L=x_{3}+\mathfrak{i}x_{0}
,
S=x_{4}+\mathfrak{i}x_{1}
, and
P=x_{5}+\mathfrak{i}x_{2}
for our parameters.
We can replace these coordinate functions with linear maps
g_{L},g_{L},g_{P}:\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{C}
given by:

\hskip-8.61108ptg_{L}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=c_{3}+\mathfrak{i}c_{0}+(s_{3}+\mathfrak{i}s_{0})z_{0},\quad g_{S}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=c_{4}+\mathfrak{i}c_{1}+(s_{4}+\mathfrak{i}s_{1})z_{1},\quad g_{L}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})=c_{5}+\mathfrak{i}c_{2}+(s_{5}+\mathfrak{i}s_{2})z_{2}.

Notice that, by construction, the linear maps see all the
L,S,P
values over the given box.
We can now think of
g_{L},g_{S},g_{P}
as living in jet-sets.
For example,
g_{P}\in S(c_{5}+\mathfrak{i}c_{2},0,0,s_{5}+\mathfrak{i}s_{2};0)
and similarly for others.
It follows that any evaluations with jet-arithmetic will include the true values over the box up to the error that accumulates.
See
[
GMT03
]
, Sections 7 and 8 for concrete details.

D.2
Elimination conditions

Recall that the computational proofs of our results are encoded by binary trees where each terminal leaf contains a string that encodes the necessary list of inequalities that we must hold true over the box. The code in
verify.c
and
identify.c
is responsible for reading the tree starting from the root node, building the box

parameters for each boxcode, and checking each condition. The evaluations of each condition occurs in

README.md
.

We want to stress a few main points. First, the jet arithmetic code is entirely borrowed from
[
GMT03
]
, aside from a tiny patch of a clear typo in the published code, see the patch file in the scripts folder of
[
Gitb
]
. In addition, we make use of the floating-point rounding estimates from
[
GMT03
]
in a few places in our code. In particular, we apply this error control to evaluate boundary conditions such as cusp area and bounds on real and imaginary parts of parameters. Since most of these quantities are monotonic in the parameters, we simply construct validated over and under estimates at the vertices of our box. These are the
nearer
,

, and

points, which are nearer than all box points to
\vec{0}
, further than all box points for

. For a detailed explanation of how these points are chosen, see the comments in
