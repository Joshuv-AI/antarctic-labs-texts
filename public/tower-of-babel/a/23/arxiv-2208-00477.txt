Abstract
. We consider discrete (time and space) random walks confined to the quarter plane, with jumps only in directions ( i , j ) (i,j) with i + j ⩾ 0 i+j\geqslant 0 and small negative jumps, i.e., i , j ⩾ − 1 i,j\geqslant-1 . These walks are called singular, and were recently intensively studied from a combinatorial point of view. In this paper, we show how the compensation approach introduced in the 90ies by Adan, Wessels and Zijm may be applied to compute positive harmonic functions with Dirichlet boundary conditions. In particular, in case the random walks have a drift with positive coordinates, we derive an explicit formula for the escape probability, which is the probability to tend to infinity without reaching the boundary axes. These formulas typically involve famous recurrent sequences, such as the Fibonacci numbers. As a second step, we propose a probabilistic interpretation of the previously constructed harmonic functions and prove that they allow to compute all positive harmonic functions of these singular walks. To that purpose, we derive the asymptotics of the Green functions in all directions of the quarter plane and use Martin boundary theory.

1.
Introduction

Espace probability for random walks in cones

Consider a multidimensional lattice random walk
\{S(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
, i.e.,
S(n)=X(1)+\cdots+X(n)
for all
n\geqslant 1
. Given a cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 1
, introduce the associated first exit time

\tau_{x}=\inf\{n\geqslant 0:x+S(n)\notin C\}\leqslant\infty.

If the drift of the increment distribution belongs to the cone, then (ignoring pathological behaviours) for
x
interior to
C
, the escape probability (also called survival probability)

(1)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x}=\infty)=\mathbb{P}(\forall n\geqslant 0,x+S(n)\in C)

is strictly positive, and defines a discrete harmonic function with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The question at the origin of the present work is the following: does this natural harmonic function admit an expression in closed form? The harmonicity property is equivalent to a recurrence relation, which in dimension
1
may be easily solved, at least in the bounded jump case. On the other hand, it is known that the behaviour of solutions to multivariate recurrences is much harder and vast
[
5
,
6
]
, a fortiori with boundary conditions depending on a cone; there is no hope, in general, to compute explicitly the escape probability (
1
).

A glimpse of our results (Part
I
)

We may now state the contributions of our paper, which consists of two parts. We will introduce a class of singular random walks in dimension
2
(see the next subsection for a precise definition, see also Figure
1
), and look at the case of the cone
C
being the positive quarter plane. In Part
I
, we will produce explicit expressions for the escape probability (
1
). To that purpose, we will use the compensation approach, as introduced in
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
by Adan, Wessels and Zijm. Before giving more details both on the random walks considered and on the techniques used, let us present an explicit example.

Figure 1.
Various step sets. From left to right: the simplest singular random walk; an arbitrary small step singular random walk; an example of singular walk with bigger jumps.

Consider in this paragraph the model
p_{-1,1}=p_{1,1}=p_{1,-1}=\frac{1}{3}
, as on Figure
1
(left). To that example, our main result will entail that the escape probability starting at
x=(i,j)
equals

(2)

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{(i,j)}=\infty)=1-\frac{1}{2^{i}}-\frac{1}{2^{j}}+\frac{1}{2^{i}5^{j}}+\frac{1}{2^{j}5^{i}}-\frac{1}{5^{i}13^{j}}-\frac{1}{5^{j}13^{i}}+\frac{1}{13^{i}34^{j}}+\frac{1}{13^{j}34^{i}}+\cdots.

The integers appearing in the denominators in (
2
), namely

1,2,5,13,34,89,233,610,\ldots

are directly related to Fibonacci sequence (
A000045
), as the
n
-th term in the sequence is equal to
F_{2n-1}
. For
i=j=1
, this result is derived in
[
24

, using a functional equation approach.

In this paper, we will prove similar formulas for other singular random walks; in particular, we will see how to define the sequence of denominators in general (providing a nice interplay between probabilistic and arithmetic properties of these singular random walks). As a second step, we will prove that similar expressions hold for infinitely many positive harmonic functions, of the form

(3)

h(i,j)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}c_{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\beta_{n}^{j},

where the
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n},c_{n}
are real constants (to be specified).

A glimpse of our results (Part
II
)

The main objective of our second part is to propose a probabilistic interpretation of the harmonic functions (
3
) constructed via the compensation approach. Our central result is to prove that the previous harmonic functions actually allow to construct all positive harmonic functions. In other words, the compensation approach yields an exhaustive description of positive harmonic functions for singular random walks. In concrete terms, this means that there is a correspondence between minimal positive harmonic functions and the yellow domain on the left display on Figure

[

. This result is properly stated in Corollary

Among all positive harmonic functions, there is a unique bounded positive harmonic function, which is the escape probability. It corresponds asymptotically by looking at the Green function

(4)

G(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}(x+S(n)=y,\tau_{x}>n)

along the drift direction.

To prove these results, we use Martin boundary theory, and we believe that several intermediate results are of independent interest. The key idea is to compute the asymptotics of the Green function (
4
)
as
x
is fixed and
y
goes to infinity in any direction of the cone. See Theorem
11
for the main statement. While such results have been recently derived in a close context (zero drift random walks
[
15
]
, irreducible non-zero drift random walks
[
20
]
), there was no version in the literature applying to our context. The case of a boundary direction needs a particular attention, due to the interaction with the axes.

From a technical point of view, Part
II
is inspired by the work
[
20
]
,
[

]
and
[

]
.

Singular random walks in the quadrant

Throughout the paper, we assume that the distribution of the increments
X(i)
of the random walk have transition probabilities
p_{i,j}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that (see Figure
1
):

(i)

\sum_{i,j}p_{i,j}=1

(normalization)
;

(ii)

p_{i,j}=0
for all
i\leqslant-2
or
j\leqslant-2

(small negative jumps)
;

(iii)

p_{-1,-1}=p_{-1,0}=p_{0,-1}=0

(singular walks)
;

(iv)

p_{-1,1}p_{1,-1}\not=0
;

(v)

There exists
(i,j)
with
i+j>0
such that
p_{i,j}>0

(non-degeneracy)
;

(vi)

The
p_{i,j}
admit exponential moments in the following sense: the Laplace transform
\sum_{i,j}p_{i,j}e^{ix+jy}
is finite in a neighborhood of any point of the curve (
11
)
(moment assumption)
.

By definition, the kernel of the model is the bivariate polynomial

(5)

K(\alpha,\beta)=\alpha\beta\left(\sum_{i,j\geqslant-1}p_{i,j}\alpha^{i}\beta^{j}-1\right).

Compensation approach

This technique has been developed in the probabilistic context of stationary distributions for random walks, see
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
. It does not aim directly at obtaining a solution for a generating function (as it is usual for quadrant walk problems), but rather tries to find a solution for its coefficients, in our case the escape probability
h(i,j)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{(i,j)}=\infty)
starting from
(i,j)
.

As a discrete harmonic function,
h(i,j)
satisfies certain recursion relations (coming from harmonicity in our case), which differ depending on whether the state
(i,j)
lies on the boundary or not:

(6)

\displaystyle h(i,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geqslant-1}p_{k,\ell}h(i+k,j+\ell),\qquad\forall i,j\geqslant 1,

(7)

\displaystyle h(i,0)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\forall i\geqslant 0,

(8)

\displaystyle h(0,j)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\forall j\geqslant 0.

The idea is then to express
h(i,j)
as a linear combination of products
\alpha^{i}\beta^{j}
, for pairs
(\alpha,\beta)
such that the recursion relations (
6
) in the interior of the quarter plane hold. This is equivalent to choosing the parameters such that
K(\alpha,\beta)=0
, with
K
as in (
5
). The products have to be chosen such that the recursion relations on the boundaries (
7
) and (
8
) are satisfied as well. As it turns out, this can be done by alternatingly compensating for the errors on the two boundaries, which eventually leads to an infinite series of product forms. The typical outcome of the compensation approach is an expression of the form (
3
). This clearly formally contains the series presented in (
2
).

As a side note, our work proposes a new example of applicability of the compensation approach, in relation with potential theory and discrete harmonic functions.

Related literature on explicit formulas for escape probabilities

In the quarter plane, the few known formulas concern non-singular walks with certain finite reflection groups (for which an algebraic version of the reflection principle applies); see Figure
2
for three examples. For instance, for the simple random walk (leftmost display on the figure), the escape probability equals
[
21
, Cor. 8]

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{(i,j)}=\infty)=\left(1-\Bigl(\frac{p_{-1,0}}{p_{1,0}}\Bigr)^{i}\right)\left(1-\Bigl(\frac{p_{0,-1}}{p_{0,1}}\Bigr)^{j}\right),

which interestingly corresponds to a finite (four terms) sum in (
3
). Similar expressions hold for the other two models on Figure
2
, with a sum involving six and eight terms, respectively.

Figure 2.
Three non-singular step sets with finite reflection groups. The rightmost example is assumed to satisfy
p_{-1,1}p_{1,-1}=p_{-1,0}p_{1,0}
.

Still in the non-singular case, other approaches based on complex analysis techniques (boundary value problems) allow to compute the generating functions

\sum_{i,j\geqslant 1}h(i,j)x^{i}y^{j}

of harmonic functions in terms of certain conformal mappings, see
[
19
]
(zero drift case) and
[
22
]
(non-zero drift case).

In the continuous setting (Brownian motion in cones), there is a unified formula
[
18
, Thm C]
for the probability of escape from arbitrary cones
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 1
. More precisely, if the drift
\vec{d}
is interior to the cone, then

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x}=\infty)=(2\pi)^{d/2}\exp\bigl(\bigl|x-\vec{d}\bigr|^{2}\bigr)p^{C}\bigl(1,x,\vec{d}\bigr),

where
p^{C}
denotes the heat kernel of the cone. The question of finding explicit expressions is then reduced to computing in closed-form the heat kernel, which is more classical. For example, if the cone is a Weyl chamber of type
A
, the determinantal Karlin-McGregor formula holds, and the survival probability is a finite sum of product forms, as in (
3
).

Applications and refinements of our results

We conclude the introduction by mentioning related open questions and potential applications of our results.

•

One advantage of the compensation approach is that it is not based on generating functions. However, it would be interesting to understand how the compensation approach is related to Cohen and Boxma techniques
[
9
,
8
]
, which we developed in the paper
[
19
]
to study harmonic functions for non-singular random walks.

•

In principle, the compensation approach could be applied as well to harmonic functions for reflected random walks in the quarter plane (with Neumann boundary conditions). It should also provide explicit expressions for
t
-harmonic functions (Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions).

•

Formulas of the type of (
3
) hold for singular random walks (our main result) and for a few other (finite group) models, as those represented on Figure
2
. This clearly suggests the question of determining the class of models for which harmonic functions may be expressed as in (
3
), via the pattern:

\text{model}\to\text{curve}\to\text{sequence of numbers}\to\text{harmonic function (via Ansatz \eqref{eq:f(i,j)_Ansatz})}.

•

Is it possible to study more generally the Martin boundary and the Green functions asymptotics of singular random walks in arbitrary cones? What is the exact applicability of the compensation approach? For instance, can it be applied to harmonic functions of higher dimensional models?

•

The compensation formula (
15
) that we find in the context of harmonic functions for singular random walks in the quarter plane is reminiscent of the alternating formula for the harmonic function of a space-time Brownian motion
(t,B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
conditioned to stay in the cone
\{(t,y),0<y<t\}
, see
[
10
]
. This suggests a relation between the singular walks we are considering and stochastic processes conditioned to stay in affine Weyl chambers.

Properties of the Laplace transform and convergence of the series

such that
\alpha^{i}\beta^{j}
is a solution of (

, see Section

2.1.
Level sets of the Laplace transform

We first observe that the product form
\alpha^{i}\beta^{j}
is solution to (
6
) if and only if

(9)

\alpha\beta=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}\alpha^{k+1}\beta^{\ell+1},

or equivalently, using the kernel notation (
5
), if and only if
K(\alpha,\beta)=0
. We therefore introduce the algebraic curve

(10)

\mathcal{K}:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{2}:K(\alpha,\beta)=0\}.

See Figure
3
for an example.

To take advantage of some convexity properties, we mainly investigate
\mathcal{K}
through an alternative exponential scaling as follows:

(11)

\mathcal{G}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:K(e^{x},e^{y})=0\}.

See Figures
3
and
4
for examples of curves
\mathcal{G}
. Let us further denote

(12)

\mathcal{G}^{+}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:K(e^{x},e^{y})<0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}^{-}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:K(e^{x},e^{y})>0\}.

The following lemma presents some crucial properties of the curve
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumptions
(i)
–
(vi)
, we have the following assertions:

(i)

\mathcal{G}^{+}
is an unbounded convex domain and includes the ray
\{t(1,1):t<0\}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{G}
passes through
(0,0)
and the two tails of
\mathcal{G}
lie in the third quadrant
\mathbb{R}_{-}^{2}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{G}
admits a tangent at
(0,0)
satisfying the equation

\left(\sum_{k,\ell}kp_{k,\ell}\right)x+\left(\sum_{k,\ell}\ell p_{k,\ell}\right)y=0.

Proof.

We first prove
(i)
.
K(e^{x},e^{y})
can be factorized as

K(e^{x},e^{y})=e^{x+y}G(x,y),

where
G(x,y)
is defined as

(13)

G(x,y):=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}e^{kx+\ell y}-1.

Since
\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}e^{kx+\ell y}
is a moment-generating function of a random variable in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, it is convex in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The domain
\mathcal{G}^{+}
is thus convex. By letting
x=y<0
, we then have:

G(x,x)=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}e^{(k+\ell)x}-1<\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}-1=0,

since
p_{k,\ell}=0
if
k+\ell<0
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}^{+}
is unbounded and includes the ray
\{t(1,1):t<0\}
.

We now prove
(ii)
. Since
G(0,0)=0
, then
(0,0)\in\mathcal{G}
. By letting
x=0>y
with
|y|
large enough, we have

G(0,y)=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}e^{\ell y}-1>p_{1,-1}e^{-y}>0.

Similarly, for all
y=0>x
with
|x|
large enough, we have

G(x,0)=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}e^{kx}-1>p_{-1,1}e^{-x}>0.

It implies that both tails of
\mathcal{G}
lie in
\mathbb{R}_{-}^{2}
.

Finally,
(iii)
is easily seen from the equation of the tangent at
(0,0)
, which is

\partial_{x}K(e^{0},e^{0})x+\partial_{y}K(e^{0},e^{0})y=0.\qed

Although
\mathcal{G}^{+}
is always convex, the bounded domain delimited by
\mathcal{K}
is not necessarily convex (see Figure
3
). This is the main reason for us to use the curve
\mathcal{G}
rather than
\mathcal{K}
.

Figure 3.
The model
p_{1,1}=5/6
,
p_{1,-1}=p_{-1,1}=1/12
: the interior of
\mathcal{K}
(left) is not convex, while the interior of
\mathcal{G}
(right) is convex.

We now parametrize the curve
\mathcal{G}
by functions. Define
f:\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\leqslant 0
,
K(e^{x},e^{f(x)})=0
and
f(x)\geqslant x
. Similarly, we define
g:\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
K(e^{g(y)},e^{y})=0
and
g(y)\geqslant y
for all

. The following lemma describes key properties of

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumptions
(i)
–
(vi)
, we have the following assertions:

(i)

f
and
g
are well defined, concave, and infinitely differentiable on
\mathbb{R}_{-}

(ii)

\max_{x\leqslant 0}f(x)>f(0)=0
and
\arg\max_{x\leqslant 0}f(x)
includes a unique point, denoted by

. Further,

is strictly increasing on

, strictly decreasing on
(x_{0},0)
, and
\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=-\infty
;

(iii)

\max_{y\leqslant 0}g(y)>g(0)=0
and
\arg\max_{y\leqslant 0}g(y)
includes a unique point, denoted by
y_{0}
. Further,
g(y)
is strictly increasing on
(-\infty,y_{0})
, strictly decreasing on
(y_{0},0)
, and
\lim_{y\to-\infty}g(y)=-\infty

Proof.

We first prove Item
(i)
. By the convexity of
\mathcal{G}^{+}
, the slope of
\mathcal{G}
at
(0,0)
, and the tails’ position of
\mathcal{G}
(see Lemma
1
), it is easily seen that
f
and
g
are well defined and concave on
\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}
. Since any point
(x,y)\in\mathcal{G}
is not the minimiser of the convex function
G
defined in (

), then
\partial_{x}G
and
\partial_{y}G
cannot vanish simultaneously for any
x,y\in\mathcal{G}
. By the construction of
f
and
g
, we then have

\partial_{x}G(x,f(x))\not=0\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{y}G(g(y),y)\not=0

for all
x,y\leqslant 0
. Thus,

f^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{\partial_{x}G(x,f(x))}{\partial_{y}G(x,f(x))}\quad\text{and}\quad g^{\prime}(y)=-\frac{\partial_{y}G(g(y),y)}{\partial_{x}G(g(y),y)}

and

We now prove Item
(ii)
. Since

then

includes at least two distinct points, then it also contains the segment between these points by the concavity of

. This implies that

is a constant function of

, which does not hold true. Hence,

includes a unique point

. The strict monotonicity of

on

and
(x_{0},0)
then follows. By letting

such that

, we have for all

,

f(x)<\frac{f(x_{0})}{x_{0}-x_{1}}(x-x_{1})\overset{x\to-\infty}{\longrightarrow}-\infty.

Thus,

as
x\to-\infty
.

Item
(iii)
is proven similarly to
(ii)
. The proof is then complete.

. This implies that

\widehat{f}(y)\leqslant y-c_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{g}(x)\leqslant x-c_{2},

for all
y\leqslant f(x_{0})
and
x\leqslant g(y_{0})
. Thanks to the above inequalities and the monotonicity of
\widehat{g}
, we first have

a_{1}\leqslant\widehat{a}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{1}=\widehat{g}(a_{1})\leqslant\widehat{g}(\widehat{a}_{1})\leqslant\widehat{b}_{1}.

By induction argument, we then have

a_{n}=\widehat{f}(b_{n-1})\leqslant\widehat{f}(\widehat{b}_{n-1})\leqslant\widehat{a}_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{n}=\widehat{g}(a_{n-1})\leqslant\widehat{g}(\widehat{a}_{n-1})\leqslant\widehat{b}_{n},

for any
n\geqslant 2
. Similarly,
a_{-n}\leqslant\widehat{a}_{-n}
and
b_{-n}\leqslant\widehat{b}_{-n}
for any
n\geqslant 1
. We now have

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(\alpha_{n}^{i}\beta_{n}^{j}+\alpha_{n+1}^{i}\beta_{n}^{j})\leqslant\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(\widehat{\alpha}_{n}^{i}\widehat{\beta}_{n}^{j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{n+1}^{i}\widehat{\beta}_{n}^{j})=\widehat{\alpha}_{0}^{i}\widehat{\beta}_{-1}^{j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{0}^{i}\widehat{\beta}_{0}^{j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{1}^{i}\widehat{\beta}_{0}^{j}+\\
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}(e^{-jc_{2}}+e^{jc_{1}})\alpha_{0}^{i+j}\left(e^{-(c_{1}+c_{2})(i+j)}\right)^{n}+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}(e^{ic_{2}}+e^{-ic_{1}})\beta_{0}^{i+j}\left(e^{-(c_{1}+c_{2})(i+j)}\right)^{n}.

The last sums are finite for any
i,j\geqslant 1
. Hence
h(i,j)
converges absolutely for any
i,j\geqslant 1
.

We now prove that the series
h(i,j)
satisfies Eq. (
6
)–(
8
). Firstly, all the terms
\alpha_{n}^{i}\beta_{n}^{j}
and
\alpha_{n+1}^{i}\beta_{n}^{j}
satisfy Eq. (
6
) (since
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})
and
(\alpha_{n+1},\beta_{n})\in\mathcal{K}
), then
h(i,j)
should also satisfy Eq. (
6
). Secondly, since

h(i,0)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-\alpha_{n}^{i}+\alpha_{n}^{i})=0\quad\text{and}\quad h(0,j)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(\beta_{n}^{j}-\beta_{n}^{j})=0,

then
h(i,j)
satisfies Eq. (
7
) and (
8
).

We now prove that
h(i,j)\geqslant 0
for all values of
i,j\geqslant 1
, by rewriting

h(i,j)=\alpha_{0}^{i}\beta_{0}^{j}\left(1-\left(\frac{\beta_{-1}}{\beta_{0}}\right)^{j}-\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)^{i}\right)+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\alpha_{-n}^{i}\bigl(\beta_{-n}^{j}-\beta_{-(n+1)}^{j}\bigr)+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\bigl(\alpha_{n}^{i}-\alpha_{n+1}^{i}\bigr)\beta_{n}^{j}.

The first term is positive for any
i,j\geqslant 1
such that
i+j
is large enough, whereas the second and third terms are positive since
\alpha_{-n}>\beta_{-(n+1)}>\alpha_{-(n+1)}
and
\beta_{n}>\alpha_{n+1}>\beta_{n+1}
for any
n\geqslant 0
. Hence, there exists
n_{0}>0
such that
h(i,j)>0
for all
i,j\geqslant 1
and
i+j\geqslant n_{0}
. Thus,

h(i,j)=\sum_{k,\ell}p_{k,\ell}h(i+k,j+\ell)\geqslant 0,

for any
i,j\geqslant 1
and
i+j=n_{0}-1
. By induction argument, we have
h(i,j)\geqslant 0
for all
i,j\geqslant 1
. The proof is then complete.
∎

The sign of harmonic funtions

can be asserted more specifically.

Remark 4
.

If the walk has jumps inside the positive quadrant, i.e., there exist
k,\ell\geqslant 0
such that
k+\ell>0
and

, then

for all
i,j\geqslant 1
. On the other hand, if the walk does not have any jumps inside the positive quadrant, i.e.,
p_{k,\ell}=0
for all

, then

for

and
i+j
large enough, but

can vanish for some small

.

Lemma 7

We have:

(i)

\lim_{y\to y_{0}^{+}}b_{-1}(y)=y_{0}

(ii)

We first prove Item

We move to the proof of Item

3.
Explicit expression for walks with small steps

This section aims at giving an explicit expression for all terms appearing in the harmonic functions (

), in the case where the random walks only have small jumps, that is, the positive transition probabilities can only be in the set
\{p_{-1,1},p_{1,-1},p_{1,0},p_{0,1},p_{1,1}\}

The following lemma presents a uniformization of the zero set of
K(\alpha,\beta)
.

Lemma 9
.

One has

\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:K(\alpha,\beta)=0\}=\left\{(\alpha(s),\beta(s)):s\in\mathbb{C}\right\},

where

\alpha(s)^{-1}:=\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}-ac}}{2a}\left(s+\frac{1}{s}\right)+\frac{b}{a}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(s)^{-1}:=\frac{\sqrt{{\widehat{b}^{2}}-a\widehat{c}}}{2a}\left(\rho s+\frac{1}{\rho s}\right)+\frac{\widehat{b}}{a},

and
a:=1-4p_{-1,1}p_{1,-1}
,
\rho:=\sqrt{\frac{1+\sqrt{a}}{1-\sqrt{a}}}
, and finally

\displaystyle b:=p_{0,1}+2p_{-1,1}p_{1,0},

\displaystyle c:=p_{0,1}^{2}-4p_{-1,1}p_{1,1},

\displaystyle\widehat{b}:=p_{1,0}+2p_{1,-1}p_{0,1},

\displaystyle\widehat{c}:=p_{1,0}^{2}-4p_{1,-1}p_{1,1}.

We further have the involutions
\alpha(s)=\alpha(1/s)
and
\beta(s)=\beta(1/(\rho^{2}s))
.

Proof.

We will find a rational uniformization of the algebraic curve

(26)

\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}K\left(\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\beta}\right)=0.

We first rewrite

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}K\left(\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{1,1}+p_{1,0}\beta+p_{0,1}\alpha+p_{1,-1}\beta^{2}+p_{-1,1}\alpha^{2}-\alpha\beta

\displaystyle=\left(p_{1,-1}\right)\beta^{2}+\left(-\alpha+p_{1,0}\right)\beta+\left(p_{-1,1}\alpha^{2}+p_{0,1}\alpha+p_{1,1}\right).

The above polynomial admits the discriminant

\displaystyle\delta(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\left(-\alpha+p_{1,0}\right)^{2}-4p_{1,-1}\left(p_{-1,1}\alpha^{2}+p_{0,1}\alpha+p_{1,1}\right)

\displaystyle=a\alpha^{2}-2b\alpha+c=\frac{b^{2}-ac}{4a}\left[\left(2\frac{a\alpha-b}{\sqrt{b^{2}-ac}}\right)^{2}-4\right],

where
a,b,c
are defined in Lemma
9
, and

b^{2}-ac=4p_{1,-1}\bigl((1-4p_{-1,1}p_{1,-1})p_{1,1}+p_{1,0}^{2}p_{-1,1}+p_{0,1}^{2}p_{1,-1}+p_{0,1}p_{1,0}\bigr)>0.

By setting

2\frac{a\alpha-b}{\sqrt{b^{2}-ac}}=s+\frac{1}{s},\quad\text{that is,}\quad\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}-ac}}{2a}\left(s+\frac{1}{s}\right)+\frac{b}{a},\quad s\in\mathbb{C},

one can easily find
\beta
satisfying Eq. (
26
) as follows:

\beta=\frac{\alpha(s)-p_{0,1}+\sqrt{\delta\circ\alpha(s)}}{2p_{1,-1}}=\frac{\sqrt{\widehat{b}^{2}-a\widehat{c}}}{2a}\left(\rho s+\frac{1}{\rho s}\right)+\frac{\widehat{b}}{a},

where
\widehat{b},\widehat{c},\rho
are defined in Lemma
9
. The proof is then complete.
∎

By the above lemma, one can describe the curve
\mathcal{K}
in (
10
) as

\mathcal{K}=\{(\alpha(s),\beta(s)):s>0\}.

We remark that at
s=1
, the discriminant
\delta\circ\alpha(1)=0
, then
\beta(1)
is the double root of Eq. (
26
) as an equation of
\beta
. Similarly, at
s=1/\rho
,
\alpha(1/\rho)
is a double root of Eq. (
26
) as an equation of
\alpha
. Thus, defining
\mathcal{K}_{0}
as the analogue of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
(see (
16
)) before the exponential change of variable, one easily deduces the description

\mathcal{K}_{0}=\{(\alpha(s),\beta(s)):s\in(1/\rho,1)\}.

Proposition 10
.

Any sequence
\{(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying (
21
)–(
23
) and (
14
), with
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
, can be described explicitly as

\{(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}=\left\{\left(\alpha(\rho^{2n}s),\beta(\rho^{2n}s)\right)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},

with
s\in(1/\rho,1)
. As a consequence, the harmonic function formed by
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in (
15
) becomes

h(i,j)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(\rho^{2n}s)^{i}\beta(\rho^{2n}s)^{j}-\alpha(\rho^{2n+2}s)^{i}\beta(\rho^{2n}s)^{j},\qquad i,j\geqslant 1.

Proof.

We will prove the proposition by induction argument. Since
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
, then

(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})=(\alpha(s),\beta(s)),

with
s\in(1/\rho,1)
. Now assume that
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})=(\alpha(\rho^{2n}s),\beta(\rho^{2n}s))
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\beta_{n}=\beta(\rho^{2n}s)=\beta\left(1/(\rho^{2n}s)\right)

by the involution and
(\alpha_{n+1},\beta_{n})
is a root of
K(\alpha,\beta)
, then

\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{2n+2}s}\right)=\alpha(\rho^{2n+2}s).

Since
(\alpha_{n+1},\beta_{n+1})
is also a root of
K(\alpha,\beta)
, then

\beta_{n+1}=\beta(\rho^{2n+2}s)
.
Similarly, the involutions of Lemma
9
also imply that

\alpha_{n-1}=\alpha(\rho^{2n-2}s)
and
\beta_{n-1}=\beta(\rho^{2n-2}s)
.
The proof is then complete.
∎

A concrete example

We now return to our simplest walk with the transition probabilities
p_{1,1}=p_{1,-1}=p_{-1,1}=1/3
and provide a full proof of the formula (
2
). The zero set of the associated kernel

K(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{3}(x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}+y^{2})-xy

admits the uniformization presented in Lemma
9
, with

\alpha(s)=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{s+1/s},\quad\beta(s)=\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\rho s+1/(\rho s)}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}.

The sequence
\{(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in Proposition
10
can be expressed as functions of
s\in(1/\rho,1)
:

(\alpha_{n}(s),\beta_{n}(s))=\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\rho^{2n}s+1/(\rho^{2n}s)},\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\rho^{2n+1}s+1/(\rho^{2n+1}s)}\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Consequently, this leads to harmonic functions depending on
s
, denoted by

h_{s}(i,j):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{n}(s)^{i}\beta_{n}(s)^{j}-\alpha_{n+1}(s)^{i}\beta_{n}(s)^{j}.

Let us look at two specific examples. Firstly, the survival probability (
2
) is the harmonic function associated to the initial point

\left(\alpha_{0}\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\right),\beta_{0}\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\right)\right)=(1,1)\in\mathcal{K}_{0},

and can be expressed explicitly as

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{(i,j)}=\infty)=h_{\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}}(i,j)=\cdots-\frac{1}{5^{i}13^{j}}+\frac{1}{5^{i}2^{j}}-\frac{1}{1^{i}2^{j}}+\frac{1}{1^{i}1^{j}}-\frac{1}{2^{i}1^{j}}+\frac{1}{2^{i}5^{j}}-\frac{1}{13^{i}5^{j}}+\cdots

In this formula, the sequence

\left\{\frac{\rho^{n}s+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}s}}{\sqrt{5}}\right\}_{n\geqslant 0}=\{1,1,2,5,13,34,89,233,610,1597,\ldots\}

is a bisection of Fibonacci numbers.

We now give the expression for the normalized positive harmonic function constructed in Proposition
6
. Recall that such a function is obtained by differentiating the harmonic function
h_{s}(i,j)
with respect to the variable
\log\beta_{0}(s)
and evaluating as
b_{0}\to\log\beta_{0}(1)
. (The logarithms appear in our computation because Proposition
6
was stated under
(\log\alpha,\log\beta)
-coordinates instead of
(\alpha,\beta)
-coordinates.) We have:

\frac{h_{s}(i,j)}{\log\beta_{0}(s)-\log\beta_{0}(1)}\overset{s\to 1^{-}}{\longrightarrow}\left.\frac{dh_{s}(i,j)}{ds}\frac{ds}{d\left(\log\beta_{0}(s)\right)}\right|_{s=1},

where

\displaystyle\left.\frac{ds}{d\left(\log\beta_{0}(s)\right)}\right|_{s=1}=\frac{\beta_{0}(1)}{\beta_{0}^{\prime}(1)}=-\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}},

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dh_{s}(i,j)}{ds}\right|_{s=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}i\left(\alpha_{n}^{\prime}(1)\alpha_{n}(1)^{i-1}-\alpha_{n+1}^{\prime}(1)\alpha_{n+1}(1)^{i-1}\right)\beta_{n}(1)^{j}+j\left(\alpha_{n}(1)^{i}-\alpha_{n+1}(1)^{i}\right)\beta_{n}^{\prime}(1)\beta_{n}(1)^{j-1},

\displaystyle(\alpha_{n}(1),\beta_{n}(1))=\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\rho^{2n}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2n}}},\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\rho^{2n+1}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2n+1}}}\right),

\displaystyle(\alpha_{n}^{\prime}(1),\beta_{n}^{\prime}(1))=\left(-\frac{\sqrt{5}\left(\rho^{2n}-\frac{1}{\rho^{2n}}\right)}{\left(\rho^{2n}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2n}}\right)^{2}},-\frac{\sqrt{5}\left(\rho^{2n+1}-\frac{1}{\rho^{2n+1}}\right)}{\left(\rho^{2n+1}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2n+1}}\right)^{2}}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

In these formulas, we want to point out that the sequence

\left\{\rho^{n}+\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\right\}_{n\geqslant 0}=\{2,3,7,18,47,123,322,843,2207,\ldots\}=\{L_{2n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}

is a bisection of Lucas numbers
\{L_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
:

L_{0}=2,\quad L_{1}=1,\quad L_{n}=L_{n-1}+L_{n-2},\quad n\geqslant 2,

see
A005248
. Moreover, the sequence

\{u_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}=\left\{\sqrt{5}\left(\rho^{n}-\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\right)\right\}_{n\geqslant 0}=\{0,5,15,40,105,275,720,1885,4935,\ldots\}

satisfies the recurrence relation (see
A201157
):

u_{0}=0,\quad u_{1}=5,\quad u_{n}=3u_{n-1}-u_{n-2},\quad n\geqslant 2.

Part II
Green functions and Martin boundary for singular random walks in the quadrant

In this part, we describe the Martin boundary inside the cone and prove that it coincides with the set of harmonic functions constructed in Part
I
.

4.
Presentation of the results

4.1.
A brief account on Green functions and Martin boundary theory

Let us first recall the definition of the Green function of the random walk inside the cone
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
, which plays an important role in the construction of the Martin boundary. For
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
, set

G(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}(x+S(n)=y,\tau_{x}>n)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{G}(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}(x+S(n)=y),

where
\tau_{x}=\inf\{n\geqslant 0:x+S(n)\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}\}\leqslant\infty
, and set
x_{0}=(1,1)
. The Martin kernel associated to the reference point
x_{0}
is then defined as

(27)

K_{M}(x,y)=\frac{G(x,y)}{G(x_{0},y)}.

The Martin boundary
\partial_{M}^{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}}S
of the random walk
S
killed outside of
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
is the boundary in the topological space
\{f:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\}
(with the topology of point-wise convergence) of the set of maps
\left\{K_{M}(\,\cdot\,,y):y\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}\right\}
. The space
\partial_{M}^{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}}S
is a compact measurable subspace of the set of real functions on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
, see
[
13
]
, and for any non-negative function
h
on
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
which is harmonic with respect to
S
killed outside
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
, there exists a positive measure
\mu_{h}
on
\partial_{M}^{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}}S
such that

h(\,\cdot\,)=\int_{\partial_{M}^{\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}}S}K_{M}(\,\cdot\,,\omega)d\mu_{h}(\omega).

One sees from (
27
) that a convenient way to compute the asymptotics of
K_{M}(\,\cdot\,,y)
as
y
goes to infinity is to first study the behavior of
G(x,y)
when
y
goes to infinity in the cone.

We set
\Sigma=\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{2}\cap\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and for
u\in\Sigma
, denote by
\phi(u)
the unique solution of

(28)

\mathbb{E}\bigl(\exp\langle\phi(u),S(1)\rangle\bigr)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\bigl(S(1)\exp\langle\phi(u),S(1)\rangle\bigr)=r_{u}u:=\mu^{u}

for some
r_{u}>0
. Remark that
\phi
is bijective from
\Sigma
to
\overline{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
(whose definition may be found in (
16
)), with

\phi^{-1}(a,b)=\frac{\nabla K(a,b)}{\|\nabla K(a,b)\|}

for
(a,b)\in\overline{\mathcal{G}_{0}}
. For
u\in\Sigma
, we then write
\mathbb{P}_{u}
for the probability measure on
\{S(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
given by the transition probabilities

\mathbb{P}_{u}(S(1)\in A)=\mathbb{E}\bigl(\exp\langle\phi(u),S(1)\rangle\mathbf{1}_{S(1)\in A}\bigr)

for
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{u}
the corresponding expectation. We then write
\Sigma^{u}
for the covariance matrix of
S(1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{u}
(recall that we denote by
\mu^{u}
the drift of
S(1)
under
\mathbb{P}_{u}
, see (
28
)).

4.2.
Statement of the main result

The main result of this section is the following theorem, which gives an asymptotics of
G(x,y)
when
y
goes to infinity along any direction in the quarter plane. Denote by
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
the two standard basis vectors.

Theorem 11
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\{y(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
with
|y(n)|\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\infty
,

•

if
\frac{y(n)}{|y(n)|}=u_{n}
with
\lim u_{n}:=u_{0}\in\Sigma\setminus\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{|y(n)|}e^{-\langle\phi(u_{n}(y(n))),x-y(n)\rangle}G(x,y(n))=A(u_{0})\mathbb{P}_{u_{0}}(\tau_{x}=\infty);

•

if
\frac{y(n)}{|y(n)|}=u_{n}
with
\lim u_{n}=e_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, then, with
\bar{\imath}=3-i
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|y(n)|^{3/2}e^{-\langle\phi(u(y(n))),x-y(n)\rangle}}{V(y_{i}(n))}G(x,y(n))=A(e_{i})\bigl(x_{\bar{\imath}}-\mathbb{E}_{e_{i}}(x_{\bar{\imath}}+S_{\bar{\imath}}(\tau_{x}))\bigr),

with
u\mapsto A(u)
a continuous, positive function on
\Sigma
and
V
introduced in Proposition
15
.

The proof of this theorem is postponed to Section
6
. The main difficulty concerns the case of an asymptotic direction along the boundary axes, where the survival probability vanishes. The latter case is solved by first studying the Green function of the random walk in the half-planes
\mathbb{H}_{1}:=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}:=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}
. We achieve the latter in Section
5
. To conclude this section, we collect a few useful estimates on the classical random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

4.3.
Preliminary estimates, and the Ney and Spitzer theorem

For all
u\in\Sigma
, we introduce the modified Green kernels

G_{u}(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}_{u}(x+S(n)=y,\tau_{x}>n)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{G}_{u}(x,y)=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathbb{P}_{u}(x+S(n)=y).

For all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
, we have

(29)

G_{u}(x,y)=e^{\langle\phi(u),y-x\rangle}G(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{G}_{u}(x,y)=e^{\langle\phi(u),y-x\rangle}\widetilde{G}(x,y).

We recall the following result from Ney and Spitzer
[
25
, Thm 2]
, using our notation
\mu^{u}=\mathbb{E}_{u}(S(1))
.

Theorem 12
.

There exists a continuous function
A:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that, as
t\to\infty
, uniformly on
u\in\Sigma
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
|x|=o(t^{1/2})
,

\sqrt{t}\widetilde{G}_{u}(x,\lfloor t\mu^{u}\rfloor)\rightarrow A(u).

We should emphasize that the initial proof of Ney and Spitzer requires the random walk
\{S(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
to be irreducible, and is valid only for fixed
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. However, the only reason for the first requirement is the use of the local large deviation limit theorem
[
25
, Thm 2.1]
, which has since been proven for any random walk with finite generating function and whose support generates
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, see
[
27
, Ch. 7, P10]
. Likewise, the hypothesis of a fixed
x
can be relaxed to the condition
|x|=o(t^{1/2})
(see the proof of Proposition
19
for a similar computation).

We will apply several times the following lemma, which gives a large deviation bound which is uniform on
\mathbb{P}_{u}
,
u\in\Sigma
.

Lemma 13
.

There exist constants
\eta,\eta^{\prime}>0
such that for all
u\in\Sigma
,
n\geqslant 1
,

•

for all
t\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}_{u}\bigl(|S(n)-n\mu^{u}|>tn\bigr)\leqslant 4\exp(-\eta t^{2}n);

•

for all
t\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{u}\bigl(|S(n)-n\mu^{u}|>tn\bigr)\leqslant 4\exp(-\eta^{\prime}tn).

Proof.

Using Markov inequality, we get (denoting
S(n)=(S_{1}(n),S_{2}(n))
)

\mathbb{P}_{u}\bigl(|S_{1}(n)-n\mu_{1}^{u}|>tn\bigr)\leqslant 2\exp(-n\Lambda^{*}_{u,1}(t)),

where

\Lambda^{*}_{u,1}
is the Legendre transform of the function

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\log\mathbb{E}_{u}\bigl(e^{\alpha(S_{1}(1)-\mu_{1}^{u})}\bigr)=\log\mathbb{E}\bigl(e^{(\alpha+\phi(u)_{1})S_{1}(1)}\bigr)-\alpha\mu_{1}^{u}.

An easy computation yields that

\Lambda^{*}_{u,1}(t)=\Lambda_{1}(t+\mu_{1}^{u})-\phi(u)_{1}(t+\mu_{1}^{u}),

where
\Lambda_{1}
is the Legendre transform of
\alpha\mapsto\log\mathbb{E}\bigl(e^{\alpha S_{1}(1)}\bigr)
. Since
u\mapsto\mu_{1}^{u}
and
u\mapsto\phi(u)_{1}
are
\mathcal{C}^{2}
,
u\mapsto\Lambda_{u,1}^{*}(t)
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
. Since for all
u\in\Sigma
, we have
\Lambda_{u,1}^{*}(0)=(\Lambda_{u,1}^{*})^{\prime}(0)=0
, and by convexity
(\Lambda_{u,1}^{*})^{\prime}(t)>0
for all
(u,t)\in\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
, we deduce the existence of
\eta>0
such that for all
u\in\Sigma
and all
t\leqslant 1
,

\Lambda_{u,1}^{*}(t)>\eta t^{2}.

Hence, uniformly on
u\in\Sigma
, for
t\leqslant 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{u}\bigl(|S_{1}(n)-n\mu_{1}^{u}|>tn\bigr)\leqslant 2\exp(-n\eta t^{2}).

Doing the same for
S_{2}(n)
yields the result.

Likewise, by strict convexity there exists
\eta^{\prime}>0
such that

\Lambda_{u,1}^{*}(t)>\eta^{\prime}t

for
t\geqslant 1
, independently of
u
. The second result is then deduced as the first one.
∎

Finally, we will use a uniform bound on the Green function
\widetilde{G}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, namely, there exists
C>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

(30)

\widetilde{G}(x,y)\leqslant C.

The existence of such a bound is just a consequence of the transience of the walk
\{S(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
.

5.1.
Asymptotics at mesoscopic distance

Since the support of the random walk

and has interesting properties allowing to explicitly compute the survival probability.

For
z>0
, denote by
\tau^{\prime}_{z}=\inf\{n\geqslant 0:z+S_{2}(n)=0\}

\tau^{1}_{x}=\tau^{\prime}_{x_{2}}
for

(
\textrm{SF}1
)

\mathbb{P}_{u}(\tau_{x_{2}}^{\prime}<\infty)=\mathbb{P}_{u}(\tau_{1}^{\prime}<\infty)^{x_{2}}=c_{u}^{x_{2}}
, where

(
\textrm{SF}2
)

as

goes to

We have the following first asymptotic result for the Green function on

Lemma 17

Let
\varepsilon>0

Proof.

For
x\in\mathbb{H}_{1}

\tau^{1}_{x}=\inf\{n\geqslant 0:x_{2}+S_{2}(n)\leqslant 0\}

t_{u}=\lfloor t\mu^{u}\rfloor
