A non-backtracking method for long matrix and tensor completion
Ludovic Stephan, Yizhe Zhu

Abstract
We consider the problem of low-rank rectangular matrix completion in the regime where the matrix M M of size n × m n\times m is “long”, i.e., the aspect ratio m / n m/n diverges to infinity. Such matrices are of particular interest in the study of tensor completion, where they arise from the unfolding of a low-rank tensor. In the case where the sampling probability is d m ​ n \frac{d}{\sqrt{mn}} , we propose a new spectral algorithm for recovering the singular values and left singular vectors of the original matrix M M based on a variant of the standard non-backtracking operator of a suitably defined bipartite weighted random graph, which we call a non-backtracking wedge operator . When d d is above a Kesten-Stigum-type sampling threshold, our algorithm recovers a correlated version of the singular value decomposition of M M with quantifiable error bounds. This is the first result in the regime of bounded d d for weak recovery and the first for weak consistency when d → ∞ d\to\infty arbitrarily slowly without any polylog factors. As an application, for low-CP-rank orthogonal k k -tensor completion, we efficiently achieve weak recovery with sample size O ⁡ ( n k / 2 ) O(n^{k/2}) and weak consistency with sample size ω ⁡ ( n k / 2 ) \omega(n^{k/2}) . A similar result is obtained for low-multilinear-rank tensor completion with O ⁡ ( n k / 2 ) O(n^{k/2}) many samples.

Introduction

Matrix completion is the problem of reconstructing a matrix from a (usually random) subset of entries, leveraging prior structural knowledge such as rank and incoherence. When the two dimensions
n,m
of the matrix
M
are comparable, this problem has been widely studied in the past decades, with a fairly general pattern emerging. Roughly speaking, if the ground truth matrix is low-rank and eigenvectors are sufficiently incoherent (or delocalized), sampling
\Omega(n\log n)
many entries uniformly at random with a certain efficient optimization procedure, one can exactly recover the ground truth matrix
[
52
,
53
,
25
,
27
,
71
,
26
,
50
]
. This is not the case when the sample size is only
O(n)
, where full completion is provably impossible under uniform sampling. However, an approximation of the ground truth matrix is still possible
[
47
,
41
,
21
]
with efficient algorithms, and more refined estimates of the eigenvectors were studied in
[
17
]
.

In recent years, attention has shifted to “long” matrix models where the row and column sizes of the matrix are not proportional. This is the case, for example, in the spiked long matrix and tensor models
[

,

,
10
,

. This situation also occurs in bipartite graph clustering and community detection, where the goal is to recover the community structure in the smaller vertex set
[

,

,

,

for a bipartite stochastic block model, and in subspace recovery problems
[

,

]
.

Our work is closely related to tensor completion, which is the higher-order analog of matrix completion. Indeed, tensor problems are highly related to their long matrix counterparts through the unfolding operation for an order-

tensor of size

\mathrm{unfold}_{a,b}:\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n^{a}\times n^{b}},

where

yields an unfolded matrix of size

In those tensor problems, the ground truth is assumed to have a low CP-rank, which implies that the unfolded matrix is low-rank. In low-rank

-tensor completion, the best known polynomial-time algorithms require

For exact recovery results on tensor completion, all the methods in the literature are either convex optimization or spectral initialization with iterative optimization steps. Theorem 1

(long matrix completion, informal)
.

With
O(\sqrt{mn})
many samples, if the left singular vectors of a long
n\times m
matrix
(m\gg n)
are sufficiently delocalized, and the singular values are above a specific Kesten-Stigum threshold, then the non-backtracking wedge operator can recover the ground truth singular values and find correlated estimates of the left singular vectors.

The
Kesten-Stigum threshold
in Theorem
1
is analogous to a similar threshold for community detection in the stochastic block model, which characterizes a threshold of the signal-to-noise ratio for the existence of polynomial time algorithms to achieve weak recovery; see
[
1
]
for more details. We discuss the Kesten-Stigum bound in matrix completion in Section
2.4
.

We then apply our main results to the low-rank tensor completion problem under an orthogonality assumption on the
r
components in a tensor decomposition. This is the first efficient algorithm to achieve weak recovery of a low-rank tensor with
\Omega(n^{k/2})
samples without any extra
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
factors. In particular, an explicit
n^{k/2}
threshold is obtained for rank-1 tensor completion when the entries are
\pm 1
. The weak recovery results can serve as a good spectral initialization step for other optimization procedures
[
63
,
51
,
24
]
. Unlike the common approach of unfolding a tensor to a matrix as square as possible ( i.e., applying
\mathrm{unfold}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil}
to

[

,
63
]
, we unfold

to an
n\times n^{k-1}
matrix in the most unbalanced way, and this allows us to directly estimate individual components of the tensor decomposition with sample complexity
O(n^{k/2})

[
17
]
to
\mathrm{unfold}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil}(T)
will have sample complexity
O(n^{\lceil k/2\rceil})
, and one cannot directly estimate any individual component in
T
. Therefore, our algorithm has a better computational complexity for tensor completion for all integer values

. The long matrix completion result also has a direct application in low-multilinear-rank tensor completion. Detailed statements and discussion on tensor completion are given in Sections
2.5
and
2.6

Although there is a strong connection between the detection problem for matrix completion and community detection in the stochastic block model

In hypergraph community detection, efficient spectral methods can achieve weak recovery with the sample complexity down to the information-theoretical threshold up to a constant factor in all cases. However, in tensor completion, there is a conjectured diverging gap between the information-theoretical sample complexity and sample complexity for efficient algorithms

The non-backtracking wedge operator we defined in Section

is tailored for the situation where the underlying sparse bipartite graph has a diverging aspect ratio. It is similar in spirit to the non-backtracking operator for hypergraphs
[

[

,
61
]
are only about extreme eigenvalues. In terms of tensor estimation with path-counting arguments, another relevant work is the estimation of a spiked tensor model under heavy-tail noise
[

n\times n
symmetric
matrices to count the number of self-avoiding walks of length
c\log n

to compute and require a new
parameter (the length of the paths considered) that needs tuning in practice.

2
Setting and main results

2.1
Detailed setting for long matrix completion

We consider a rectangular matrix
M
of size
n\times m
, with aspect ratio
\alpha=m/n
. We write the singular value decomposition of
M
as

M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\phi_{i}\psi_{i}^{\top},

where the
\nu_{i}
are the singular values of
M
, and the
(\phi_{i})_{i\in[r]}
(resp. the
(\psi_{i})_{i\in[r]}
) are an orthonormal family of its left (resp. right) singular vectors. We order the singular values of
M
in decreasing order, that is

\nu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\nu_{n}.

Our observed matrix is of the form

A=\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{d}(X\circ M),

(1)

where
X
is an i.i.d Bernoulli matrix:

X_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\ \mathrm{Ber}\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{mn}}\right).

We will use the shortcut
p=d/\sqrt{mn}
as the sampling probability. The scaling of
A
is chosen so that the expected value of
A
is exactly
M
. Our goal is to weakly recover the eigendecomposition:

MM^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}^{2}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{\top}.

The non-backtracking wedge matrix

We can view the matrix
A
as the weighted biadjacency matrix of a bipartite graph
G=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E)
with
|V_{1}|=n
,
|V_{2}|=m
. We call
V_{1}
a
left
vertex set and
V_{2}
a
right
vertex set. A path of length 2 in
G
denoted by
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
is called a
wedge
if
e_{1},e_{3}\in V_{1},e_{2}\in V_{2},
and
e_{1}\not=e_{3}
. Define the set of
oriented wedges

\vec{E}
as

\vec{E}=\left\{(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})\in V_{1}\times V_{2}\times V_{1}:\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\in E,\{e_{2},e_{3}\}\in E,e_{1}\neq e_{3}\right\}.

(2)

The
non-backtracking wedge
matrix
B
associated to
G
is defined on
\vec{E}\times\vec{E}
by

B_{ef}=\mathbf{1}_{e\to f}A_{f_{1}f_{2}}A_{f_{3}f_{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{1}_{e\to f}=\mathbf{1}_{e_{3}=f_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{e_{2}\neq f_{2}}.

(3)

Equivalently,
\mathbf{1}_{e\to f}
is one if and only if
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},f_{2},f_{3})
is a non-backtracking walk of length 4 in
G
. See Figure
1
for an example. Note that the non-backtracking wedge matrix
B
is not symmetric: its index set
is the oriented wedge set defined in (
2
), and
B_{ef}\not=B_{fe}
in (
3
).

For comparison, we recall the definition of the
non-backtracking operator
for weighted graphs as follows. Let
A
be a weighted adjacency matrix of a graph
G=(V,E)
. Define the oriented edge set
\vec{E}
for
G
as
\vec{E}=\{(i,j):\{i,j\}\in E\}
.
The
non-backtracking operator

B
of associated with
A
is a non-Hermitian operator of size
|\vec{E}|\times|\vec{E}|
. For any
(u,v),(x,y)\in\vec{E}
,
B
is defined as

\displaystyle B_{(u,v),(x,y)}=\begin{cases}A_{xy}&\text{$v=x$, $u\neq y$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4)

In the case of an
n\times m
bipartite weighted graph defined in (
1
), the non-backtracking wedge operator only counts non-backtracking walks starting from a vertex in
V_{1}
while the non-backtracking operator defined in (
4
) counts non-backtracking walks from both
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Intuition for the non-backtracking wedge matrix

It has been shown that the non-backtracking operator, as a non-hermitian matrix, outperforms the standard (weighted) adjacency matrix when the sample size is very small in many graph clustering and matrix completion problems
[
18
,
17
,
73
,
74
]
. The key difference is that the spectrum of the non-backtracking operator is more informative and stable when the underlying graph is very sparse with a constant average degree. It is also a general phenomenon that the spectrum of non-hermitian random matrices is less sensitive to a row or column with a large
\ell_{2}
-norm
[
29
,
15
]
.

In terms of the sample size, the non-backtracking matrix in (
4
) was used in
[
17
,
73
]
to achieve the optimal scale
O(n)
for square matrix completion. However, for a low-rank tensor in a matrix form of size
n\times n^{2}
, the work
[
17
]
only achieves weak recovery with sample size
O(n^{2})
, while with the non-backtracking wedge operator, we only need
O(n^{1.5})
. The standard way in
[
63
,
24
]
to obtain
\tilde{O}(n^{1.5})
sample size is to apply a spectral method on the hollowed Gram matrix
Z=AA^{T}-\text{diag}(AA^{T})
. For
i\not=j
, we have

Z_{ij}=\sum_{\text{wedge }i\to k\to j}A_{ik}A_{kj}.

Hence, the spectrum of
Z
depends on wedge walk counts. Our new non-backtracking operator only counts the wedge walks that are
non-backtracking
, which avoids the localization effect from high-degree vertices
[
11
]
in the weighted graph corresponding to
Z
. This new operator introduces a tool to “regularize” the spectrum of a heavy-tailed long random matrix without any tuning parameter, which might be useful for other applications.

2.2
Quantifying the convergence

The alignment between the spectral decomposition of
B
and the SVD of
A
will be governed by a wealth of different quantities. For ease of understanding, we divide those parameters into two categories, depending on their role in the convergence.

Threshold parameters

These parameters describe which parts of the SVD of
M
will be reflected in the spectral decomposition of
B
.

(i)

Variance parameter
:

\rho=\lVert Q\rVert,\quad\text{where}\quad Q=\sqrt{mn}(M\circ M).

(5)

(ii)

Amplitude parameter
:

L=\sqrt{mn}\max_{x\in[n],y\in[m]}|M_{xy}|.

(iii)

Detection thresholds

\theta=\max(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),

where the two parameters
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are defined as

\theta_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{d}}\quad\ \ {}\quad\theta_{2}=\frac{L}{d}.

(iv)

Detection rank
:

r_{0}=\max\{i\in[n]:\nu_{i}>\theta\}.

Equivalently, it is the number of singular values of
M
above the detection threshold
\theta
.

(v)

Inverse signal-to-noise ratios (inverse SNRs)
: for
i\in[r_{0}]
,

\tau_{i}=\frac{\theta}{\nu_{i}}\in(0,1).

The largest of those inverse SNRs,
\tau_{r_{0}}
, will be denoted by
\tau
.

Complexity parameters

Those parameters quantify how intrinsically “easy” the matrix
M
is to recover. In particular, contrary to the previous ones, they are all invariant under any rescaling of
M
.

(i)

Rescaled amplitude parameter
:

K=\frac{L^{2}}{\rho}.

(6)

(ii)

Aspect log-ratio
:

\eta=\frac{\log(m)}{\log(n)}-1.

(7)

Equivalently, it is the solution of the equation
m=n^{1+\eta}
. When
M
is obtained from the
(a,b)
unfolding of a tensor, we get
\eta=\frac{b-a}{a}
.

(iii)

Rank
:

r=\mathrm{rank}(M)=\max\{i\in[n]:\nu_{i}\neq 0\}.

(iv)

Delocalization parameter
:

\kappa=\sqrt{n}\max_{i}\lVert\phi_{i}\rVert_{\infty}.

Note that
\kappa\geq 1
by definition. We require no such conditions on the right singular vectors
\psi_{i}
.

Simplifying assumptions

We also make a few simplifying assumptions throughout the paper; although our results are still valid when those assumptions aren’t satisfied, the proofs become much messier.
Recall the aspect ratio is defined by
\alpha=\frac{m}{n}\geq 1
. We first assume that

\liminf_{n}d>1\quad\ \ {}\quad d\leq n.

(8)

We also assume that

\eta\geq\frac{2\log(d)+2\log\log_{d}(n)}{\log(n)},\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\alpha\geq d^{2}(\log_{d}n)^{2}.

(9)

We note that if
d>n
, our results become trivially true (see the definition of
\ell
in Theorem
2
, and the bounds thereafter).

2.3
Main results for long matrix completion

Our first result shows that the eigenvalues of
B
exhibit a structure similar to those observed in
[
73
,
17
]
: namely, most eigenvalues of
B
lie in a circle of known radius, with a few outlier eigenvalues correlated with the singular values of
M
.

Theorem 2

(Singular value estimation)
.

Let

\ell=\lfloor\varepsilon\log_{d}(n)\rfloor,\quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon=\frac{(\eta/2)\wedge 1}{25}.

There exist constants
C_{0},n_{0}\geq 1
that depend polynomially on
r,K,\kappa,d,\log n
such that, if
n\geq n_{0}
and
C_{0}\tau^{2\ell}\leq 1
, the following holds on an event with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
:

(i)

There exists an ordering
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r_{0}}
of the top
r_{0}
eigenvalues of
B
such that for any
i\in[r_{0}]
,

|\lambda_{i}-\nu_{i}^{2}|\leq C_{0}\nu_{i}^{2}\tau_{i}^{2\ell}.

(ii)

All other eigenvalues of
B
have modulus at most
C_{0}^{1/\ell}\theta^{2}
.

Our second result concerns quantifying the overlaps between the top eigenvectors of
B
and the singular vectors of
M
. For
i\in[r_{0}]
, we define the relative eigengap

\delta_{i,\ell}=\min_{\nu_{j}\neq\nu_{i}}\left|1-\left(\frac{\nu_{j}}{\nu_{i}}\right)^{2\ell}\right|.

(10)

Let
\xi_{i}^{R},\xi_{i}^{L}
a right (resp. left) unit eigenvector of
B
associated with
\lambda_{i}
, and define

\zeta_{i}^{R}(x):=\sum_{e\in\vec{E}:e_{1}=x}A_{e_{1}e_{2}}A_{e_{3}e_{2}}\xi_{i}^{R}(e)\quad\ \ {}\quad\zeta_{i}^{L}(x):=\sum_{e\in\vec{E}:e_{1}=x}\xi_{i}^{L}(e).

(11)

Theorem 3

(Left singular vector estimation)
.

Under the same setting and conditions as Theorem
2
, with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
, for
i\in[r_{0}]
, there exists a unit left singular vector
\phi_{i}^{\prime}
of
M
associated with
\nu_{i}
such that

\left|\langle\zeta_{i}^{R},\phi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}\tau_{i}^{2\ell}}{\delta_{i,\ell}}\quad\ \ {}\quad\left|\langle\zeta_{i}^{L},\phi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}\tau_{i}^{2\ell}}{\delta_{i,\ell}},

(12)

where
\gamma_{i}
is defined as

\gamma_{i}=\left\langle\mathbf{1},\left(I-\frac{QQ^{*}}{\nu_{i}^{4}d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(\phi_{i}\circ\phi_{i}\right)\right\rangle.

(13)

2.4
Discussion

Comparison to
[
17
]

In the seminal work of
[
17
]
, the authors provide a spectral algorithm to weakly recover a low-rank rectangular matrix of size
m\times n
with
O(m+n)
many samples, where a Kesten-Stigum bound for estimation singular values and both left and right eigenvectors are obtained. In our low-rank long matrix setting
(m\gg n)
, their sample complexity scales as
O(m)
. In contrast, our non-backtracking wedge operator is able to estimate singular values and left singular vectors of a long matrix with sample size
O(\sqrt{mn})
. This improvement in sample complexity to recover one-sided structure is useful in tensor completion; see Section
2.5
for more details.

Overlap convergence and weak consistency

In full generality, the overlaps
\gamma_{i}
in Theorem
3
have a complicated expression. however, this can be simplified under a homogeneity assumption, namely

D_{x}:=\sum_{y\in V_{1},z\in V_{2}}Q_{xz}Q_{yz}

is independent of
x
. In this case, the Perron-Frobenius theorem implies that
D_{x}=\rho^{2}
, and hence

\langle\zeta_{i}^{R},\phi_{i}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\tau_{i}^{4}}+o(1),\quad\langle\zeta_{i}^{L},\phi_{i}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\tau_{i}^{4}}+o(1).

This implies a
weak recovery
of the singular vectors
\phi_{i}
. In general, as
d\to\infty
, we have
(\gamma_{i}-1)\sim C/d^{2}
, and hence (
12
) implies the eigenvectors of
B
are asymptotically aligned with the left singular vectors of
M
. Under the additional assumption that all singular values of
M
are distinct, the top
r
eigenvectors of
B
are asymptotically aligned with all left singular vectors of
M
, and eigenvalues are consistent estimators for
\nu_{i},i\in[r]
: this is a form of
weak consistency
for matrix completion.

Optimality of the bounds and thresholds

We have made no effort towards getting closed-form exponents in the polynomials
C_{0}
and
n_{0}
, which would probably not be sharp. An explicit bound on
C_{0},n_{0}
in the proportional case for matrix completion is worked out in
[
17
, Theorem 5.4]
. Similarly, although we do expect (when
d,\eta
are fixed) that the rate of convergence is indeed of the form
n^{-c}
, we didn’t try to optimize the value of such
c
as much as possible.

Our work shows a threshold behavior for long matrix completion: one can weakly recover the components in
MM^{\top}
whose signal-to-noise ratio is above the threshold.
On the other hand, the optimality of
\theta
is still an open and interesting question since if this threshold can be lowered, more information about
M
can be recovered. The first threshold
\theta_{1}
is inherent to the problem and can be found in many similar problems (e.g., the
Kesten-Stigum threshold
in community detection
[
57
,
18
,
65
,
66
]
): it is related to an intrinsic property on the existence of a pseudo-eigenvector in a Galton-Watson tree with Poisson offspring distribution of mean
d
, see Section
E
for details. But whether
\theta_{2}
is needed or is an artifact of proof is still unclear. We note that in a similar problem regarding the configuration model
[
28
]
, such a seemingly spurious threshold turns out to be the sharp one (albeit only for one eigenvalue). Note that as
d
grows,
\theta_{2}
decays faster than
\theta_{1}
, therefore
\theta=\theta_{1}
for sufficiently large
d
.

Recovering right singular vectors

We draw attention to the fact that our result is almost completely independent of the right singular vectors
\psi_{i}
of
M
, both on the level of assumptions (except for their influence on
K
) and results. We believe this is not an artifact of proof: indeed, most of the right vertices (a proportion
1-\alpha^{-1}
of them) are isolated, and it is impossible to recover any signal from those. This is a completely different behavior compared with
[
17
]
, where the authors show that when
\alpha
is bounded, both left and right singular vectors of
M
can be recovered. However, it matches recent results from
[
64
]
in the framework of long matrix reconstruction showing different reconstruction thresholds for the left and right singular vectors.
Our work also shows that one can effectively estimate the rank of a long matrix of size
n\times m
with
O(\sqrt{mn})
random samples when the rank
r=O(1)
. When
m,n
are proportional, a similar result was obtained in
[
72
]
based on the Bethe-Hessian matrix.

Parameter scaling

All of the results in Theorems
2
and
3
are non-asymptotic, and with more care in the proof, we would be able to give explicit expressions for
C_{0},n_{0}
(see, e.g.,
[
17
]
). However, the given bounds are meaningful only when
\ell\gg 1
, and
C_{0}\tau^{2\ell}\ll 1
. These can easily be seen to be satisfied whenever

K,\kappa,r=n^{o(1)},\quad\ \ {}\quad\log(d)^{2}=o(\log(n)).

2.5
Application in low-CP-rank tensor completion

Throughout this part, our goal will be to recover a tensor
T
of order
k\geq 3
, which has a CP decomposition (see, e.g.,
[
54
]
) with
r
components:

T=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\nu_{i}\,w_{i}^{(1)}\otimes w_{i}^{(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{i}^{(k)},

(14)

where
\|w_{i}^{(j)}\|_{2}=1
for all
i\in[r],j\in[k]
. The smallest integer
r
for the existence of the decomposition (
14
) is called the
CP-rank
of
T
, and we will call it the rank of
T
for simplicity in this section.

It is known that for a general low-rank tensor
T
, finding its CP-decomposition is NP-hard
[
48
]
. Even with the complete information of the ground truth
T
, identifying each component in
\{w_{i}^{(j)},i\in[r],j\in[k]\}
is not feasible. Since we consider asymmetric tensor completion, it will be useful to consider a general version of unfolding:

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{T}
be a partition of
[k]
in two subsets. The
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
-unfolding of
T
; denoted as
\mathrm{unfold}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}(T)
, is a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\mathcal{S}}\times n^{\mathcal{T}}}
defined as follows: for any multi-index
(i_{s})_{s\in[k]}
,

M_{(i_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{S}},(i_{s})_{s\in\mathcal{T}}}=T_{(i_{s})_{s\in[k]}}.

In particular, when
T
is as in (
14
), we have

\mathrm{unfold}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\nu_{i}\left(\bigotimes_{j\in\mathcal{S}}w_{j}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{j\in\mathcal{T}}w_{j}\right)^{*}.

(15)

For simplicity, we define the mode-
i
unfolding of
T
as
\mathrm{unfold}_{i}(T):=\mathrm{unfold}_{\{i\},[k]\setminus\{i\}}(T)
.

However, we make an important remark: Equation (
15
) is not necessarily the SVD of the tensor unfolding. Indeed, the CP decomposition does not always have orthogonal components, and hence, the vectors in (
15
) are not necessarily orthogonal.
Generally, for a given tensor
T
, the spectra of the unfolded matrices are not the same for different unfoldings, and there is a hierarchy of unfolded matrix norms
[
77
]
. In the following, we will denote by
\tilde{T}
an observed tensor from
T
with sampling probability
p=\frac{d}{n^{k/2}}
. For an unfolding mode
i
, we let
M^{(i)}=\mathrm{unfold}_{i}(\tilde{T})
, and
B^{(i)}
be its associated non-backtracking wedge matrix.

2.5.1
The rank-1 case

When
r=1
, we write

\displaystyle T=w^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w^{(k)},

(16)

where we choose
\nu_{1}=1
in (
14
) for convenience. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{unfold}_{i}(T):=\mathrm{unfold}_{\{i\},[k]\setminus\{i\}}(T)=w^{(i)}(w^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w^{(i-1)}\otimes w^{(i+1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w^{(k)})^{\top}.

We now write down the corresponding model parameters for
M^{(i)}
. It can be checked that they are the same for all
i
, independent of the choice for unfolding, except for
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n^{k/2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert w^{(j)}\right\rVert_{4}^{2}}{d}},

\displaystyle\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{k/2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert w^{(j)}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{d}

(17)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=n^{k/2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\lVert w^{(j)}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}}{\lVert w^{(j)}\rVert_{4}^{2}},

\displaystyle\kappa^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\|w^{(i)}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{n}\max_{i}\|w^{(i)}\|_{\infty}\eqqcolon\kappa_{\mathrm{max}}.

Note that we always have
\theta_{1}^{2}\leq\theta_{2}
. Applying Theorems
2
and
3
to
\mathrm{unfold}_{i}(T)
, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 1

(Rank-1 tensor completion)
.

Let
k\geq 3
, and define
\ell
as

\ell=\left\lfloor c_{1}\log_{d}(n)\right\rfloor,\quad c_{1}=\frac{(k/2-1)\wedge 1}{25}.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
,
assume that

d>(1+\varepsilon)n^{k/2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\lVert w^{(j)}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

(18)

Then there exists
C_{0},n_{0}\geq 1
that depend polynomially on
K,\kappa_{\mathrm{max}},d,\log n
such that for any
i\in[k]
,
n\geq n_{0}
and
C_{0}\theta^{2\ell}\leq 1
, the following holds on an event with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
:

(i)

The top eigenvalue
\lambda^{(i)}
of
B^{(i)}
satisfies

|\lambda^{(i)}-1|\leq C_{0}\theta^{2\ell},

where
\theta=\max(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
with
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
in (
17
).

(ii)

All other eigenvalues of
B^{(i)}
have modulus at most
C_{0}^{1/\ell}\theta^{2}
.

(iii)

There exist associated left and right eigenvectors
\xi^{(i),L},\xi^{(i),R}
of
B^{(i)}
such that

\left|\langle\zeta^{(i),L},w^{(i)}\rangle-\sqrt{1-\theta^{2}}\right|\leq C_{0}\theta^{2\ell}\quad\ \ {}\quad\left|\langle\zeta^{(i),R},w^{(i)}\rangle-\sqrt{1-\theta^{2}}\right|\leq C_{0}\theta^{2\ell},

(19)

where
\zeta^{(i),L},\zeta^{(i),R}
are defined in (
11
).

Hence, successively applying tensor unfolding
k
times to the observed tensor is enough to estimate all of the components of
T
.
When all components
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
are vectors in
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, with
(1+\varepsilon)n^{k/2}
samples, we have

\langle\zeta^{(i),L},w^{(i)}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}-1}}{1+\varepsilon}+O(n^{-c}).

2.5.2
Low-rank orthogonal components estimation

Our long matrix completion results aim to estimate individual left singular vectors above a Kesten-Stigum-type threshold. However, in the tensor case, without orthogonal assumption on the vectors
[w_{i}^{(1)},\dots,w_{i}^{(k)}]
, such a reduction to the estimation of left singular vectors in long matrix completion is impossible.
Therefore, we consider the same setting as in
[
51
,
70
]
, and assume that
T
can be written as

T=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\nu_{j}w_{j}^{(1)}\otimes w_{j}^{(2)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{j}^{(k)},

(20)

where for each
i\in[k]
,

\left\{w_{1}^{(i)},\dots,w_{r}^{(i)}\right\}
forms an orthonormal basis, and
\nu_{1}\geq\nu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\nu_{r}>0
. In contrast with the rank-one case, we don’t have access to an orthogonal CP-decomposition of
T\circ T
, and hence most parameters used in Theorem
2
depend on the unfolding mode
i
; we shall denote them by
\rho^{(i)},Q^{(i)}
, and so on. We can still provide the following estimates of
\rho^{(i)}
and
L^{(i)}
:

n^{k/2}\lVert T\rVert_{2}\leq\rho^{(i)}\leq n^{k/2}\lVert T\rVert_{F},\quad\ \ {}\quad L^{(i)}=L\coloneqq n^{k/2}\lVert T\rVert_{\infty}.

Corollary 2

(Low-rank orthogonal tensor completion)
.

Let
T
be a
k
-tensor with an orthogonal decomposition (
20
) and
k\geq 3
, and define
\ell
as

\ell=\left\lfloor c_{1}\log_{d}(n)\right\rfloor,\quad c_{1}=\frac{(k/2-1)\wedge 1}{25}.

For any
i\in[k]
, there exists
C_{0}^{(i)},n_{0}^{(i)}
, that depend polynomially on
K^{(i)},\kappa^{(i)},r^{(i)},d^{(i)},\log(n)
, such that, if
n\geq n_{0}
and
C_{0}^{(i)}\left(\tau_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{2\ell}\leq 1
, the following holds with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
:

(i)

There exists an ordering
\lambda_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\lambda_{r_{0}}^{(i)}
of the top
r_{0}
eigenvalues of
B^{(i)}
such that for any
j\in[r_{0}^{(i)}]
,

|\lambda_{j}-\nu_{j}^{2}|\leq C_{0}^{(i)}\nu_{j}^{2}\left(\tau_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{2\ell}.

(ii)

All other eigenvalues of
B^{(i)}
have modulus at most
\left(C_{0}^{(i)}\right)^{1/\ell}(\theta^{(i)})^{2}
.

(iii)

For
j\in[r_{0}^{(i)}]
, there exist associated left and right eigenvectors
\xi_{j}^{L},\xi_{j}^{R}
of
B^{(i)}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\zeta_{j}^{(i),R},w_{j}^{(i)}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{j}^{(i)}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}^{(i)}\left(\tau_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{2\ell}}{\delta_{j,\ell}},\quad\left|\langle\zeta_{j}^{(i),L},w_{j}^{(i)}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{j}^{(i)}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}^{(i)}\left(\tau_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{2\ell}}{\delta_{j,\ell}},

where
\gamma_{j}
is defined as

\gamma_{j}^{(i)}=\left\langle\mathbf{1},\left(I-\frac{Q^{(i)}Q^{(i)*}}{\nu_{j}^{4}d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(w_{j}^{(i)}\circ w_{j}^{(i)}\right)\right\rangle,

\delta_{j,\ell}
is defined in (
10
), and
\zeta_{j}^{(i),R},\zeta_{j}^{(i),L}
are defined in (
11
).

We obtain a weak recovery of an orthogonal tensor decomposition with sample size
O(n^{k/2})
, which removes the
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
factors in
[
51
,
70
]
. Same as in the discussion for long matrix completion in Section
2.4
, under the additional assumption that
\{\nu_{i},i\in[r]\}
are separated, with sample size
\omega(n^{k/2})
, weak consistency for
T
can be achieved:

Theorem 4
.

Let
T
have an orthogonal decomposition as in (
20
), such that
\delta_{i,\ell}\geq c
for some constant
c>0
. For
i\in[k],j\in[r]
, define the estimator

\hat{T}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\hat{\nu}_{j}\,\hat{w}_{j}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{w}_{j}^{(k)}\quad\text{for}\quad\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}=\zeta_{j}^{(i),R}\quad\ \ {}\quad\hat{\nu}_{j}=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}^{(1)}}.

Then, with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
, for large enough
d
,

\frac{\lVert T-\hat{T}\rVert_{F}}{\lVert T\rVert_{F}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{kr}K\tau}{d}+C_{1}\left(\frac{\tau}{d}\right)^{\ell-1},\quad\text{where}\quad\tau=\nu_{r}^{-2}\max_{i}\rho^{(i)}.

Theorem
4
is proved in Appendix
I
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result that shows weak recovery (in the sense
\lVert T-\hat{T}\rVert_{F}=o(1)
) in the regime
d\to\infty
, as opposed to
d\gtrsim\log(n)^{c}
.

2.5.3
Discussion on tensor completion

Comparison to nearly square unfolding

Corollaries
1
and
2
provide an efficient algorithm that outputs an estimate for each component
w^{(i)}
. This directly implies that one can have a correlated estimation of
T
with sample size
O(n^{k/2})
. This improves the sample complexity
O(n^{\lceil k/2\rceil})
when directly applying the matrix completion algorithm in
[
17
]
to
\mathrm{unfold}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil}(T)
when
k
is odd.
This mode-
i
unfolding approach is more computationally efficient than the nearly square unfolding
[
63
]
for component-wise estimation. For example, in the rank-1 case, applying the matrix completion algorithm to
\mathrm{unfold}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor,\lceil k/2\rceil}(T)
, one can find a correlated estimation of
w^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes w^{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor)}
. However, a further recursive unfolding is needed to recover individual components. The advantage of single-mode unfolding was also discussed in
[
10
]
for the spiked tensor model.

Optimal sample complexity for rank-one completion

We note that although our method does reach the
O(n^{k/2})
scaling for rank-one tensor completion, this is not the optimal sample complexity. Indeed, for a boolean rank-one tensor, completion is equivalent to
k
-XORSAT, and hence
[
31
]
implies the following theorem:

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
T=x^{\otimes k}
for a given vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\lVert x\rVert_{2}=1
. Then there exists a constant
c_{k}<1
such that if
p\geq c_{k}n^{-(k-1)}
, there exists an algorithm that outputs an estimator
\hat{x}
for
x
satisfying

\frac{\langle\hat{x},x\rangle}{\lVert\hat{x}\rVert_{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}}.

Hence, for the specific case of rank-one tensors with bounded entries, a near-optimal algorithm can reach a sample complexity of
O(n)
. However, the mapping to a XORSAT problem disappears for tensors of rank
r>1
, as well as with the addition of any type of noise (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
). Therefore, the conjectured
n^{k/2}
threshold in
[
7
]
for general low-rank tensor completion does not apply to this specific noiseless example. The proof of Theorem
5
is given in Appendix
J
.

Subspace recovery for general low-rank tensors

Without the orthogonal decomposition assumption of the tensor
T
, one cannot directly associate the singular vectors of the unfolded matrix to the components of
T
. In this general case, the best one can hope for is to recover the subspace spanned by the spikes from
T
. Although our result is of independent interest in long matrix completion, it can also be used directly in non-orthogonal tensor completion. First, when
d\to\infty
, the results of
[
63
]
imply that a good reconstruction of the left singular space of
M
translates directly to weak consistency for tensor reconstruction. On the other hand, several algorithms such as the ones in
[
63
,
59
,
24
]
make use of a first spectral estimate of the CP factors of the tensor
T
before a refined optimization step; a popular choice for such an estimate is usually a truncated SVD of
A
. We conjecture that even for fixed
d
when the SVD of
A
contains no information about the one of
M
, the embedded eigenvectors of
B
can be used to perform an informed initialization of those algorithms.

2.6
Application in low-multilinear-rank tensor completion

Besides CP-decomposition, another popular choice for tensor decomposition is the Tucker decomposition
[
76
]
, which is also called higher-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD)
[
32
]
. For an order
k
-tensor
T
, we write its HOSVD as

\displaystyle T=\sum_{q_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\sum_{q_{2}=1}^{r_{2}}\cdots\sum_{q_{k}=1}^{r_{k}}s_{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}}\phi_{q_{1}}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{q_{k}}^{(k)},

(21)

where
(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})
is called the
multilinear rank
of
T
,
U_{j}=[\phi_{1}^{(j)},\dots,\phi_{r_{j}}^{(j)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{j}}
is a matrix with orthonormal columns, and
S\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{k}}
is the core tensor. One can show that the columns of
U^{(j)}
are the left singular vectors of the mode-
j
unfolding of
T
denoted by
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n^{k-1}}
. See
[
54
]
for more details.
Tensor completion with low-multilinear-rank was studied in
[
55
,
81
,
63
]
. Recovering an HOSVD under additive noise was considered in
[
58
]
.

We can apply the result for long matrix completion in Section
2.3
and to
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)
to recover the mode-
j
components of the HOSVD. We denote the SVD of
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}\nu_{i}^{(j)}\phi_{i}^{(j)}\psi_{i}^{(j)}.

Let
B^{(j)}
be the non-backtracking wedge operator for the observed matrix of
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)
after sampling each entry with probability
p=\frac{d}{n^{k/2}}
. The following corollary follows directly from Theorem
3
.

Corollary 3

(Low-multilinear-rank tensor completion)
.

Let
T
be a tensor defined in (
21
). With the same notations as in Section
2.2
and Theorem
3
applied to
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)
and
B^{(j)}
, there exist constants
C_{0},n_{0}\geq 1
that depend polynomially on
r,K,\kappa,d,\log n
such that, if
n\geq n_{0}
and
C_{0}\tau^{2\ell}\leq 1
, the following holds on an event with probability at least
1-cd^{4}n^{-1/4}
: there exists a unit left singular vector
{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}^{(j)}
of
\mathrm{unfold}_{j}(T)
associated with
\nu_{i}^{(j)}
such that

\left|\langle\zeta_{i}^{R},\phi_{i}^{\prime(j)}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}\tau_{i}^{2\ell}}{\delta_{i,\ell}}\quad\ \ {}\quad\left|\langle\zeta_{i}^{L},{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}^{(j)}\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}\tau_{i}^{2\ell}}{\delta_{i,\ell}}.

By applying Corollary
3
to different unfoldings, we can achieve weak recovery for the orthonormal components
U^{(j)},1\leq j\leq k
, with
O(n^{k/2})
many samples. Our method, however, does not provide estimations for the core tensor
S
, the same as the work on low-multilinear-rank tensor approximation
[
58
]
.

Appendix B
Spectral structure of the matrix
B

In this section, we state the main intermediary result used in our paper, which characterizes almost fully the structure of the top eigenvectors of
B
. Theorems
2
and
3
then stem from a perturbation analysis already done in
[
73
,
17
,
74
]
, which we will sketch here.

B.1
Preliminaries

We begin with a few important definitions and properties. Define the
start
and
terminal
matrices
S\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{1}\times\vec{E}}
and
T\in\mathbb{R}^{\vec{E}\times V_{1}}
by

S_{xe}=\mathbf{1}_{e_{1}=x}\qquad\ \ {}\qquad T_{ex}=\mathbf{1}_{e_{3}=x}.

This implies that for any vector
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{1}}
,
[S^{*}\phi](e)=\phi_{e_{1}}
and
[T\phi](e)=\phi(e_{3})
. We shall also need the so-called
edge inversion operator

J
and a diagonal weight operator
\Delta
, defined as

J_{e,f}=\mathbf{1}\{f=e^{-1}\}\qquad\ \ {}\qquad\Delta_{e,e}=A_{e_{1}e_{2}}A_{e_{3}e_{2}},

where
e^{-1}=(e_{3},e_{2},e_{1})

B.2
Main technical results

We are now in a position to state our main technical result. Let

\ell=\left\lfloor\varepsilon\log_{d}(n)\right\rfloor\quad\text{with}\quad\varepsilon=\frac{(\eta/2)\wedge 1}{25}.

The candidate right (resp. left) eigenvectors of

are the following: for

Then our results are as follows:

.

There exists an absolute constants

such that for

\displaystyle\left\lVert U^{*}U-d^{2}\Gamma^{(\ell)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{U}^{*}\hat{U}-\left(\Gamma^{(\ell+1)}-I_{r_{0}}\right)\right\rVert

(32)

\displaystyle\left\lVert U^{*}\hat{U}-I_{r_{0}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{-\varepsilon/2}\tau^{4\ell},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{U}^{*}B^{\ell}U-\Sigma^{\ell}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{-\varepsilon/2}\tau^{4\ell}\theta^{2\ell},

\displaystyle\left\lVert B^{\ell}P_{\im(\hat{U})^{\bot}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\theta^{2\ell},

\displaystyle\left\lVert P_{\im(U)^{\bot}}B^{\ell}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\theta^{2\ell},

with

Appendix C
Parameter inequalities

In this section, we collect a few simple inequalities that will be useful in the proof.
First, since

\rho=\|Q\|\leq\sqrt{nm}\max_{xy}|Q_{xy}|,

we have
K\geq 1
.
Further, using the variational formula for singular values,

\rho\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{mn}}\langle\mathbf{1},Q\mathbf{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nm}}\sum_{xy}Q_{xy}=\sum_{x,y}M_{xy}^{2}=\|M\|_{F}^{2}\geq\nu_{1}^{2}.

Hence

1\leq K=\frac{L^{2}}{\rho}\leq\frac{L^{2}}{\nu_{1}^{2}}.

Next, we derive some entrywise bound on
\Phi:=QQ^{*}
. For any
x,y
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{xy}=(QQ^{*})_{xy}=\sum_{z}Q_{xz}Q_{yz}\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2}}{n}.

(35)

Let
(v_{k})
be a set of orthonormal left singular vectors for
Q
with singular values
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}
. For
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle(\Phi^{t})_{xy}

\displaystyle=[(QQ^{*})^{t}]_{xy}=\sum_{k}\mu_{k}^{2t}v_{k}(x)v_{k}(y)

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2t-2}\sum_{k}\mu_{k}^{2}|v_{k}(x)||v_{k}(y)|

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2t-2}\sqrt{(\Phi)_{xx}}\sqrt{(\Phi)_{yy}}\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2t}}{n},

(38)

where the last line follows from Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and (
35
). Similarly, let
\tilde{\Phi}=Q^{*}Q
, we have

\displaystyle(\tilde{\Phi}^{t})_{xy}\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2t}}{m}.

(39)

And

\displaystyle(\Phi^{t}Q)_{xy}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2t+1}}{\sqrt{nm}},\quad(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}Q^{*})_{xy}\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2t+1}}{\sqrt{nm}}.

(40)

Moreover, for any
t\geq 1
and
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\langle v,\Phi^{t}w\rangle=\sum_{xy}(\Phi^{t})_{xy}v(x)w(y)\leq\frac{K^{2}\rho^{2t}}{n}\|v\|_{1}\|w\|_{1}.

(41)

D.1
Preliminaries

Rooted graphs and trees

We begin with a rigorous definition for the objects considered in this section:

Definition 2

(Labeled rooted graphs)
.

A labeled rooted graph is a triplet
g_{\star}=(g,o,\iota)
that consists in:

•

a graph
g=(V,E)
,

•

a distinguished vertex (or

)

,

•

\mathcal{G}_{\star}
.

When the labeling function

for

. In particular, this will be the case whenever

and
\iota(x)=x
. The notion of induced subgraph extends naturally to rooted and/or labeled graphs, provided that the induced subgraph does contain the root

An important object of study will be the bipartite Galton-Watson tree defined as follows:

Definition 3

(Bipartite Galton-Watson tree)
.

The bipartite Galton-Watson tree
(T,\rho)
is a random tree
(T,o,\iota)
defined as follows:

•

the root of the tree (depth 0) has label
\iota(o)=\rho
,

•

the number of children of each vertex

is independent, and is defined as follows:

–

if
x
has even depth
2h
,

has
\Poi(d^{2})

,

–

if
x

x
has exactly one child at depth

.

•

each vertex

\iota(x)\sim\Unif([n])
, and each vertex of odd depth has a label

.

For a rooted graph
(g,o)
, we denote by

the subgraph induced by the vertices within distance

Definition 4

(Tangle-freeness)

A rooted graph

D.2
Bounding the neighborhood sizes

The main issue in bounding the neighborhood sizes is that a typical left vertex
x
has approximately
mp=d\sqrt{\alpha}
neighbors, which diverges as
\alpha\to\infty
. However, most of those neighbors have degree one and thus do not matter in the spectrum of
B
! We formalize this by defining the bipartite graph
\bar{G}=(V_{1},\bar{V}_{2},\bar{E})
induced by
V_{1}
and the non-leaf vertices in
V_{2}
:

\bar{V}_{2}:=\left\{y\in V_{2}:\deg(y)\geq 2\right\}.

We are then able to show the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

There exists constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that for all
x\in[n]
and
s>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\forall t\geq 0,\quad|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|\leq sd^{2t}\text{ and }|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\leq sd^{2t+2}\right\rparen\geq 1-c_{0}e^{-c_{1}s}

Proof.

We define

\tilde{p}=\mathbb{P}\lparen\Bin(n,p)\geq 1\rparen=1-(1-p)^{n},\quad\tilde{d}_{1}=mp\tilde{p},\quad\ \ {}\quad\tilde{d}_{2}=\tilde{d}_{1}(1+np).

We shall actually show the following bound: for some constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
, and
s>1
,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\forall\ t\geq 0,\quad|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|\leq c_{1}s\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\text{ and }|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\leq c_{2}s\tilde{d}_{1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\right\rparen\geq 1-c_{3}e^{-c_{4}s}

(42)

We first show how this implies Proposition
1
: for
s>1
the bound is vacuous as soon as
t>c\log_{d}(n)
for some constant
c
, so we can always assume otherwise. Second, we have

\tilde{d}_{1}=d^{2}+O\left(d^{3}/\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\quad\ \ {}\quad\tilde{d}_{2}=d^{2}+O\left(d^{3}/\sqrt{\alpha}\right),

and hence for the timescale considered for
t
we have
\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\leq cd^{2t}
under the assumption (
9
). Finally, we can adjust
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
such that
c_{0}e^{-c_{1}}\geq 1
and hence the bound also becomes vacuous when
s\leq 1
.

We now move to prove (
42
). Define the following sequences:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2t}

\displaystyle=\max\left(\tilde{d}_{1}^{-1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{-t+1}(2t),\sqrt{np}\tilde{d}_{1}^{-1/2}\tilde{d}_{2}^{-(t-1)/2}\sqrt{2t}\right),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2t+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{d}_{2}^{-t/2}\sqrt{2t+1}\quad\ \ {}\quad f_{t}=\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}(1+\varepsilon_{t^{\prime}}).

Due to Assumption (
8
), by definition of
\varepsilon_{t},f_{t}
, there exists some constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
such that

c_{0}\leq f_{t}\leq c_{1}\quad\ \ {}\quad\varepsilon_{t}\leq c_{1}.

We also define the following upper-bound sequence:

S_{2t}=f_{2t}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\quad\ \ {}\quad S_{2t+1}=f_{2t+1}\tilde{d}_{1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t},

which satisfies the following recursion equation:

S_{2t+1}=(1+\varepsilon_{2t+1})\tilde{d}_{1}S_{2t}\quad\ \ {}\quad S_{2t+2}=(1+\varepsilon_{2t+2})(1+np)S_{2t+1}.

We will also need the following lemma, which stems from a classical Chernoff bound:

Lemma 1
.

Let
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{T})
be i.i.d binomial random variables, with mean
\mu
. Then, for any
u\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{T}Y_{i}\geq(1+u)\mu T\right\rparen\leq e^{-T\mu\gamma(u)},

where
\gamma(u)=(1+u)\log(1+u)-u
. The function
\gamma(u)
is strictly increasing, and satisfies the inequality

\gamma(u)\geq\min\left(\frac{u^{2}}{4},u\right)

(43)

For a given vertex
x\in\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t}
, the number of neighbors of
x
in
\bar{V}_{2}\setminus(\bar{G},x)_{2t}
is dominated by
\Bin(m,p\tilde{p})
. For any
t\geq 0
, we can apply Lemma
1
with
u=\varepsilon_{2t+1}
to get

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\geq sS_{2t+1}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|\leq sS_{2t}\right\rparen\leq\exp\left(-sf_{2t}\tilde{d}_{1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\gamma(\varepsilon_{2t+1})\right).

Equation (
43
) implies that when
u\leq c_{1}
,
\gamma(u)\geq c_{2}u^{2}
for some constant
u^{2}
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\geq sS_{2t+1}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|\leq sS_{2t}\right\rparen\leq\exp\left(-cs(2t+1))\right).

Now, the number of neighbors of a vertex in
\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}
in
V_{1}
is dominated by a
X\sim\Bin(n,p)
random variable, conditioned on
X\geq 1
, which is itself dominated by a
1+\Bin(n,p)
variable (see, e.g.,
[
22
, Proposition 1.1]
). This time, we apply Lemma
1
by noticing that

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{T}(1+Y_{i})\geq(1+u)(1+\mu)T\right\rparen=\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{T}Y_{i}\geq\left(1+u+\frac{u}{\mu}\right)\mu T\right\rparen,

so that

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+2}|\geq sS_{2t+2}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\leq sS_{2t+1}\right\rparen\leq\exp\left(-snpf_{2t+1}\tilde{d}_{1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\gamma(\varepsilon_{2t+2}/np)\right).

We can now notice that by the bound on
\gamma
in (
43
) and definition of
\varepsilon_{2t+2}
, we have

np\tilde{d}_{1}\tilde{d}_{2}^{t}\gamma(\varepsilon_{2t+2}/np)\geq c(2t+2).

Hence, we have shown that for any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{t+1}|\geq sS_{t+1}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{t}|\leq sS_{t}\right\rparen\leq\exp(-cs(t+1)).

This implies that

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\exists t\geq 0,|\partial(\bar{G},x)_{t}|\geq S_{t}\right\rparen\leq\sum_{t\geq 0}\exp(-cs(t+1))=\frac{e^{-cs}}{1-e^{-cs}},

from which (
42
) ensues since
s>1
.
∎

With the same arguments as in
[
18
, Lemma 29]
, this tail bound implies the following proposition:

Proposition 2
.

With probability at least
1-1/n
, we have for all
x\in[n]
,

|(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|\leq c\log(n)d^{2t}\quad\ \ {}\quad|(\bar{G},x)_{2t+1}|\leq c\log(n)d^{2t+2}.

Further, we have for all
x\in V_{1}
,
p\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|^{p}\right]^{1/p}\leq cpd^{2t},

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{x\in[n],t\geq 0}|(\bar{G},x)_{2t}|^{p}\right]\leq(cp)^{p}+(c\log(n))^{p}.

D.3
Tree approximation

We now couple the neighborhoods of
G
with an appropriately defined tree process. Central to this section is the following breadth-first exploration process of a vertex
x\in[n]
:

•

start with
A_{0}=\{x\}

•

at step

x_{t}\in A_{t}
among those closest to

–

if
x_{t}\in V_{2}
, we let

be the set of all neighbors of
x_{t}
in
V_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{s\leq t}A_{s}

–

if

,

is the set of neighbors of
x_{t}
in
V_{2}\setminus\bigcup_{s\leq t}A_{s}

that have a neighbour in
V_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{s\leq t}A_{s}
.

•

A_{t+1}=A_{t}\cup N_{t}\setminus\{x_{t}\}
and continue.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{t}
the filtration adapted to this process and call an edge
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
a

if for some
t\geq 0
we have

and
e_{2}\in N_{t}
.
We begin with a result about the tree-like properties of neighborhoods in

:

Proposition 3
.

For a given vertex

, and

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen(G,x)_{2h}\text{ contains a cycle}\right\rparen\leq\frac{cd^{2h+2}}{n}.

Further, with probability at least
1-cd^{4h+4}/n
, the graph
G
is

,
(G,x)_{2h}
contains at most one cycle.

Proof.

Consider the exploration process outlined above, and let
\tau
be the first time at which all the vertices in
(\bar{G},x)_{h}
have been revealed. By construction,
\tau
is a stopping time for the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, and given

, the discovered edges of

form a spanning tree. Hence, there are two possibilities for a cycle in
(G,x)_{h}
:

•

there is an edge between a vertex of
(G,x)_{h}\cap V_{1}
and
(G,x)_{h}\cap V_{2}
,

•

and
x_{2}
.

The number of such events of the first type is stochastically dominated by a
\Bin(|(G,x)_{h}|^{2},p)
variable and the second by a
\Bin(|(G,x)_{h}|^{2},mp^{2})
. Further, given
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
, those two types of events are independent.
Recall the following simple bounds:

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\Bin(N,q)\geq 1\right\rparen\leq Nq\quad\ \ {}\quad\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\Bin(N,q)\geq 2\right\rparen\leq N^{2}q^{2}.

Then,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen(G,x)_{h}\text{ contains a cycle}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\right\rparen\leq|(\bar{G},x)_{h}|^{2}(mp^{2}+p).

and similarly

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen(G,x)_{h}\text{ is tangled}\nonscript{}\>\middle|\nonscript{}\>\mathopen{}\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\right\rparen\leq|(\bar{G},x)_{h}|^{4}(m^{2}p^{4}+p^{2}).

Taking expectations in both cases and using Proposition
2
,

\mathbb{P}\left\lparen(G,x)_{2h}\text{ contains a cycle}\right\rparen\leq\frac{cd^{2h+2}}{n}\quad\ \ {}\quad\mathbb{P}\left\lparen(G,x)_{2h}\text{ is tangled}\right\rparen\leq\frac{cd^{4h+4}}{n^{2}}.

The second statement of the proposition easily ensues from a union bound.
∎

We now prove a coupling result between the neighborhoods of
G
and the Galton-Watson trees introduced in Definition
3
. Recall that the total variation between two probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}
is defined as

\dtv(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})=\min_{\mathbb{P}\in\pi(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})}\mathbb{P}\lparen X_{1}\neq X_{2}\rparen,

where
\pi(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})
is the set of all
couplings
of
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{2}
, i.e. joint distributions of random variables
(X_{1},X_{2})
such that
X_{1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
X_{2}\sim\mathbb{P}_{2}
.

Proposition 4
.

There exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that for any
h\geq 0
,
x\in V_{1}
, we have

\dtv\left(\mathcal{L}((G,x)_{h}),\mathcal{L}((T,x)_{h})\right)\leq c\log(n)^{2}\frac{d^{4h+3}}{n\wedge\sqrt{\alpha}}.

Proof.

We proceed in two steps: we first couple the unlabeled versions of the graphs and then move on to the labels. We shall use the following classical bounds for total variation distances:

\dtv(\Bin(n,p),\Poi(np))\leq p\quad\ \ {}\quad\dtv(\Poi(\lambda),\Poi(\lambda^{\prime}))\leq|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|.

(44)

Firstly, from Proposition
3
,
(G,x)_{h}
is a tree with probability
1-cd^{2h+2}/n
, so we only need to couple the offspring distributions. Consider the exploration process
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined above; at each step
t
, we let
n_{t}
(resp.
m_{t}
) be the number of vertices in
V_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{t^{\prime}\leq t}A_{t^{\prime}}
(resp.
V_{2}\setminus\bigcup_{t^{\prime}\leq t}A_{t^{\prime}}
). We aim to couple it with the same exploration process
(x^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
(T,x)
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{t}
(resp.
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
) be the offspring distribution of
x_{t}
(resp.
x^{\prime}_{t}
). By definition, we have an explicit definition for
\mathbb{P}_{t}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
:

•

if
x_{t}
has even depth,
\mathbb{P}_{t}=\Bin(m_{t},p\tilde{p}_{t})
where
\tilde{p}_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\Bin(n_{t},p)\geq 1)
, and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}=\Poi(d^{2})
;

•

if
x_{t}
has odd depth,
\mathbb{P}_{t}=\Bin(n_{t},p)
conditioned on being at least 1, and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}=1
a.s.

We begin with the second case, as it is the simplest. As before,
\mathbb{P}_{t}
is dominated by a distribution equal to
1+\Bin(n,p)
, and hence

\mathbb{P}_{t}(|N_{t}|>1)\leq\mathbb{P}(\Bin(n,p)\geq 1)\leq np=d\alpha^{-1/2}.

We now move to the first case. We have

\tilde{p}_{t}=1-(1-p)^{n_{t}}=pn_{t}+O(p^{2}n^{2})=O(np).

Using the bounds in eq. (
44
),

\dtv(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t})\leq p\tilde{p}_{t}+|m_{t}p\tilde{p}_{t}-d^{2}|\leq\frac{cd^{2}}{m}+|m_{t}n_{t}p^{2}-d^{2}|+\frac{cd^{3}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}.

The middle term can be further decomposed by noticing that
nmp^{2}=d^{2}
:

|m_{t}n_{t}p^{2}-d^{2}|\leq|m_{t}-m|np^{2}+m|n_{t}-n|p^{2}\leq\frac{2d^{2}|(G,x)_{2h}|}{n}

All in all, we showed that for any
t\geq 0
,

\dtv(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t})\leq cd^{2}\left(\frac{|(G,x)_{2h}|}{n}\vee\frac{d}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right).

(45)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
denote the unlabeled distribution of the graphs.
By the high-probability bounds of Proposition
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dtv\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}((G,x)_{2h}),\tilde{\mathcal{L}}((T,x)_{2h})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{cd^{2h+2}}{n}}_{\text{$(G,x)_{h}$ is not a tree}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}}_{\text{Prop. \ref{prop:neighbourhood_size_bounds} does not hold}}

\displaystyle+c\log(n)^{2}d^{2h+2}\left(\frac{d^{2h}}{n}\vee d\alpha^{-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\log(n)^{2}\frac{d^{4h+3}}{n\wedge\sqrt{\alpha}},

where in the third part of the first inequality, we apply (
45
) with a union bound over all vertices in
(G,x)_{2h}
. This bound is lower than the one in Proposition
4
since
d>1
.

Consider now the distribution of the labels. Given a coupling between the unlabeled versions of
(G,x)_{2h}
and
(T,x)_{2h}
, the labels of the even (resp. odd) depth vertices of
(G,x)_{2h}
are obtained by sampling without replacement from
[n]
(resp.
[m]
), whereas those of
(T,x)_{2h}
are sampled with replacement. From
[
39
]
, the total variation distance between sampling with and without replacement
k
elements from a population of
N
elements is bounded above by
k^{2}/N
, and hence

\dtv(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\leq 2\frac{|(G,x)_{2h}|^{2}}{n},

where
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}
are the labels distributions for
(G,x)_{2h},(T,x)_{2h}
, respectively. A final application of Proposition
2
completes the proof.
∎

D.4
Quantitative bounds for local functionals

The bounds obtained in Proposition
4
can be viewed as a quantitative version of the local convergence of Benjamini and Schramm
[
13
]
, for possibly diverging depth. It is known that this coupling also implies weak convergence in the probability measure sense, i.e., the convergence of graph functionals. We now provide a quantitative version of this weak convergence, but only for a specific class of functionals, called
local
functionals:

Definition 5

(Local functionals)
.

A functional
f:\mathcal{G}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
t
-local if
f(G,o)
is only a function of
(G,o)_{t}
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
f:\mathcal{G}_{\star}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
2h
-local function for some
h\geq 0
, such that
f(g,o)\leq a|(g,o)_{2h}|^{b}
for some
a,b
. Then with probability at least
1-n^{-1}
,

\left|\sum_{x\in V_{1}}f(G,x)-\sum_{x\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[f(T,x)\right]\right|\leq ca\log(n)^{3/2+b}\frac{d^{2h(1+b)}}{\sqrt{n}\wedge\alpha^{1/4}}.

Proof.

Notice that the bounds on the neighborhood sizes match exactly the ones of
[
17
]
, and hence we can borrow the following concentration result from it (see the proof of Theorem 12.5 in
[
17
]
): with probability at least
1-n^{-1}
,

\left|\sum_{x\in V_{1}}f(G,x)-\sum_{x\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[f(G,x)\right]\right|\leq ca\log(n)^{3/2+b}d^{2h(1+b)}\sqrt{n}.

(46)

On the other hand, we have for any
x\in V_{1}

\left|\mathbb{E}f(T,x)-\mathbb{E}f(G,x)\right|=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|f(T,x)-f(G,x)\right|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}(x)}\right],

where
\mathcal{E}(x)
denotes the event of the coupling between
(G,x)_{2h}
and
(T,x)_{2h}
fails. From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}f(T,x)-\mathbb{E}f(G,x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left\lparen\mathcal{E}(x)\right\rparen}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(f(T,x)-f(G,x))^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\dtv\left(\mathcal{L}((G,x)_{h}),\mathcal{L}((T,x)_{h})\right)}\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[|f(G,x)|^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[|f(T,x)|^{2}\right]}\right).

Proposition
4
bounds the first factor.
It is easy to check that the bounds for
|(G,x)_{2h}|
in Proposition
2
also apply to
|(T,x)_{2h}|
, which yields

\left|\mathbb{E}f(T,x)-\mathbb{E}f(G,x)\right|\leq ca\log(n)d^{2bh+1}\frac{d^{2h}}{\sqrt{n}\wedge\alpha^{1/4}}.

(47)

Combining (
46
) and (
47
) yields the desired bound.
∎

Appendix E
From tree functionals to pseudo-eigenvectors

Since we now have a way to translate quantities from the Galton-Watson tree
T
to the graph
G
, we aim to apply these results to specific functionals. We begin with a few preliminaries: given a functional
f:\mathcal{G}_{\star}\to\mathbb{R}
, define its

\overline{f}
as

\overline{f}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[f(T,x)\right].

This definition is consistent since from Definition
3
, the distribution of a Galton-Watson tree only depends on its root label

. We shall also need the following definition: given a matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and a functional

\partial_{W}f(g,o)=\sum_{o^{\prime},o^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(o)}W_{\iota(o)\iota(o^{\prime})}W_{\iota(o^{\prime\prime})\iota(o^{\prime})}f(g\setminus\{o,o^{\prime}\},o^{\prime\prime})

where
o^{\prime},o^{\prime\prime}
runs over the set of non-backtracking wedges starting from

. In particular, when
g
is a rooted tree,
o^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by
o^{\prime\prime}
, and the graph
(g\setminus\{o,o^{\prime}\},o^{\prime\prime})
is the subtree rooted at
o^{\prime\prime}

The functionals we will consider are of the following form: for a vector
\phi

f_{\phi,t}(g,o)=\left(\frac{mn}{d^{2}}\right)^{t}\sum_{o_{1},\dots,o_{2t}}\left(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}M_{\iota(o_{2s}),\iota(o_{2s+1})}M_{\iota(o_{2s+2}),\iota(o_{2s+1})}\right)\phi_{\iota(o_{2t})}

(48)

where the sum runs over all non-backtracking paths of length
2t
starting from
o
. Then, the following proposition holds:

Proposition 6
.

For any
t\geq 0
, and
i,j\in[r]
,

\displaystyle\overline{f_{\phi_{i},t}}

\displaystyle=\nu_{i}^{2t}\phi_{i},

\displaystyle\overline{f_{\phi_{i},t}f_{\phi_{j},t}}

\displaystyle=(\nu_{i}\nu_{j})^{2t}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t}\frac{\Phi^{s}}{(\nu_{i}\nu_{j}d)^{2s}}\right)(\phi_{i}\circ\phi_{j})

and if
F_{\phi_{i},t}=(f_{\phi_{i},t+1}-\nu_{i}^{2}f_{\phi_{i},t})^{2}
, then

\overline{F_{\phi_{i},t}}=\frac{\Phi^{t}(\phi_{i}\circ\phi_{i})}{d^{2t}}

Additionally, for any functional
f:\mathcal{G}_{\star}\to\mathbb{R}
, and any weight matrix
W
,

\overline{\partial_{W}f}=\frac{d^{2}(WW^{*})}{mn}\,\overline{f}.

Proof.

We proceed by reduction to
[
73
]
. Let
\tilde{T}
be an ordinary Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
\Poi(d^{2})
, such that each vertex has a random label
\Unif([n])
, and define the random weights

\tilde{W}_{xy}=\frac{mn}{d^{2}}M_{xZ}M_{yZ},\quad Z\sim\Unif([m])

The equivalent tree functional
\tilde{f}_{\phi,t}
is defined as

\tilde{f}_{\phi,t}(g,o)=\sum_{o_{1},\dots,o_{t}}\left(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\tilde{W}_{\iota(o_{s}),\iota(o_{s+1})}\right)\phi_{\iota(o_{t})}.

It is easy to see that

f_{\phi,t}(T,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{f}_{\phi,t}(\tilde{T},x).

On the other hand, the process
\tilde{f}
on
(\tilde{T},x)
was already studied in depth in
[
73
]
. It corresponds to the signal matrix

\tilde{Q}=\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{n}\circ\left(\frac{mn}{d^{2}}\cdot\frac{MM^{*}}{m}\right)=P

\tilde{K}=\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}}{n}\circ\left(\frac{m^{2}n^{2}}{d^{4}}\frac{(M\circ M)(M\circ M)^{*}}{m}\right)=\frac{\Phi}{d^{2}}.

Proposition
6
then ensues from Propositions 6 and 7 in
[
73
]
.

Appendix I
Proof of Theorem

For simplicity, in this section, we assume that

defined in (

) for each

satisfies
\delta_{j,\ell}^{(i)}>c>0
for all

, and we place ourselves under the high-probability event of Corollary
2
. Note that as
d\to\infty
,
L/d\ll\rho^{(i)}/\sqrt{d}
for all
i
, and as such

\tau^{(i)}\leq\tau/\sqrt{d}
.

By a simple application of the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\lVert T-\hat{T}\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\lVert\nu_{j}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}w_{j}^{(i)}-\hat{\nu}_{j}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}|\nu_{j}-\hat{\nu}_{j}|+\nu_{j}\left\lVert\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}w_{j}^{(i)}-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\right\rVert_{F}

The first term is easily bounded through Corollary
2
:

\displaystyle|\nu_{j}-\hat{\nu}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu_{j}^{2}-\lambda_{j}^{(1)}}{2\min(\nu_{j},\hat{\nu}_{j})}\leq C_{1}\nu_{j}\left(\frac{\tau}{d}\right)^{\ell},

and hence by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\sum_{j=1}^{r}|\nu_{j}-\hat{\nu}_{j}|\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{\tau}{d}\right)^{\ell}\lVert T\rVert_{F}.

For the second term, we also apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to find

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\nu_{j}\left\lVert\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}w_{j}^{(i)}-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert T\rVert_{F}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\lVert\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}w_{j}^{(i)}-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lVert T\rVert_{F}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(2-2\prod_{i=1}^{k}\langle w_{j}^{(i)},\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\rangle\right)}

From Corollary
2
, we get

\langle w_{j}^{(i)},\hat{w}_{j}^{(i)}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{j}^{(i)}}}-C_{3}\left(\frac{\tau}{d}\right)^{\ell},

where

\gamma_{j}^{(i)}=\left\langle\mathbf{1},\left(I-\frac{Q^{(i)}Q^{(i)*}}{\nu_{j}^{4}d^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(w_{j}^{(i)}\circ w_{j}^{(i)}\right)\right\rangle\leq 1+K^{2}\frac{(\tau/d)^{2}}{1-(\tau/d)^{2}}

where the last inequality comes from Equation (

.
Now, it is possible to check that for

and applying this to the above bound yields

Appendix J
Proof of Theorem
5

We first treat the case where
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. By the natural homeomorphism between
(\{\pm 1\},\times)
and
(\mathbb{F}_{2},\oplus)
, each equation of the form
\hat{x}_{i_{1}}\dots\hat{x}_{i_{k}}=\tilde{T}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}
can be mapped to a linear equation in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. This is known as the
k
-XORSAT problem, and the optimal sample complexity can be found in
[
30
]
:

Theorem 7

(Proposition 4.1 from
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a random
k
-XORSAT problem, where each clause of the form
y_{i_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus y_{i_{k}}=0
is selected with probability
p
. Then, for
k\geq 3
, there exists a constant
c_{k}
such that if
p>c_{k}n^{1-k}
, then with high probability, the only solution to
\mathcal{S}
is
x=0
.

By considering
y_{i}=\hat{x}_{i}x_{i}
, when
p\geq c_{k}n^{1-k}
, the only solution to
\hat{x}_{i_{1}}\dots\hat{x}_{i_{k}}=\tilde{T}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}
for all
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
is
\hat{x}=x
. Note that this system can be solved through Gaussian elimination in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, which is a polynomial algorithm. Hence, the following holds:

Proposition 8
.

If
p\geq c_{k}n^{1-k}
, and
T=x^{\otimes k}
with
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, then there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that outputs an estimator
\hat{x}
such that
\hat{x}=x
with high probability.

Now, if
T=x^{\otimes k}
for an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\sign(T)=\sign(x)^{\otimes k}
. Hence, applying Proposition
8
to
\sign(T)
, we obtain an estimator
\hat{x}=\sign(x)
. Therefore, we get

\langle\hat{x},x\rangle=\langle\sign(x),x\rangle=\lVert x\rVert_{1}.

Theorem
5
then ensues from the simple inequality

1=\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum{x_{i}^{2}}\leq\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\sum|x_{i}|=\lVert x\rVert_{\infty}\lVert x\rVert_{1}.
