Algorithm to Produce a Density Field with Given Two,Three, and Four-Point Correlation Functions Preprint: APS/123-QED
Zachary Slepian

Abstract
Here we show how to produce a 3D density field with a given set of higher-order correlation functions. Our algorithm enables producing any desired two-point, three-point, and four-point functions, including odd-parity for the latter. We also generalize the algorithm to i) N-point correlations with N > 4 N>4 , ii) dimensions other than 3, and iii) beyond scalar quantities. This algorithm should find use in verifying analysis pipelines for higher-order statistics in upcoming galaxy redshift surveys such as DESI, Euclid, Roman, and Spherex, as well as intensity mapping.



I
Introduction

For a long time, it has been known how to realize a density field with a given pair correlation, either power spectrum or 2PCF
Hoffman and Ribak 1991
. This has been useful in 3D large-scale structure (LSS) analyses as well as in CMB analyses. In the CMB domain, it has also been known how to realize a given bispectrum (triple correlator of CMB temperature anisotropies)
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
. Furthermore, for LSS, it has been known how to realize certain specific bispectra, such as those produced by foorms of primordial non-Gaussianity during inflation.

Yet, a general solution to the problem of realizing arbitrary NPCFs for a 3D density field has yet to be presented. This, if done, would be quite useful in testing higher-order correlation analysis pipelines, as is indeed desirable given the wealth of upcoming 3D LSS data.

It is not quite so problematic to realize a density with a given 2PCF and 3PCF, as this can simply be done using N-body simulations. However, even here, the simulations are expensive, and each gives only a realization of the true desired NPCF. Furthermore, non-standard physics, such as parity violation in the 4PCF, is extremely expensive to simulate, and since it is very model-dependent, one must simulate many models, each at high expense.

On the observational side, surveys such as DESI have a density-dependent fiber-assignment effect. This could adversely effect studies of higher-order correlations. While some of this can be solved by running fiber assignment on N-body simulations, the currently-standard scheme for fixing fiber assignment errors (probability inverse pair weighting) may require a prohibitively large number of simulations if it is to be extended to the 3PCF and beyond. Thus, producing simulations with a given 3PCF,
etc.
without the high expense of performing N-body runs would perhaps bring within reach the number of realizations needed for this.

In this work, we present a method to realize any desired set of NPCFs about a given primary galaxy at
\vec{x}
. This may be done about many primaries so that the spheres of galaxies around each out to the maximal radius to which one wishes to measure the NPCF do not overlap. One could then simply run an NPCF algorithm such as
Philcox et al. 2022
by flagging only the primaries desired as those to be used. This would be a suitable setup for testing the effects outlined above. The problem of optimal sphere-packing in 3D has also been solved, so one could optimally pack as many primaries as possible into a given volume.

This work is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
we review the CMB bispectrum approach; in Sec.
III
we extend it to the 3D LSS 4PCF; in Sec.
IV
to the 3PCF, and in Sec.
V
we include 2PCF. We discuss generalization to higher-order correlators in Sec.
VI
, higher dimensions of space and arbitrary spatial geometry in Sec.
VII
, and conclude in Sec.
VIII
.

II
CMB Maps with a Given Power Spectrum and Bispectrum

II.1
CMB correlators

In the CMB context, one decomposes the temperature fluctuations around the mean as

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta(T)}{\bar{T}}=\sum_{\ell m}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n}),

(1)

with
\Delta T=T(\hat{n})-\bar{T}
,
\hat{n}
the line of sight, and
\bar{T}
the mean CMB temperature. One may then compute correlators of the
a_{\ell m}
. The CMB fluctuations have statistically isotropic correlations; that is, the pair correlation, triple correlations,
etc.
, are the same, on average, in any direction one looks, depending only on the relative direction vectors between the pair or triple,
etc.
of CMB points. This statistically isotropy means that the correlators must be invariant under rotations about the observer.

We have the CMB power spectrum

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell m}a_{\ell m}^{*}\right>=C_{\ell}.

(2)

There are only correlations at equal
\ell
because of rotation-invariance about the observer; unequal
\ell
correlators vanish under rotation-averaging due to orthogonality of the spherical harmonics. The power spectrum must be
m
-independent since
m
is the
z
-component of
\ell
, and hence dependent on choice of
z
axis, which would not be rotation-invariant.

The CMB bispectrum is

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\right>=B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right),

Spergel and Goldberg 1999
;
Goldberg and Spergel 1999

where the matrix is a Wigner 3-
j
symbol. Given rotation-invariance about the observer, the Wigner-Eckart theorem implies that the CMB bispectrum must be proportional to a 3-
j
symbol.

One conventionally defines the reduced CMB bispectrum via

\displaystyle B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}=\mathcal{C}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{0}&{0}&{0}\\
\end{array}\right)b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}},

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{(2\ell_{1}+1)(2\ell_{2}+1)(2\ell_{3}+1)}{4\pi}}.

(7)

Combining Eqs.
II.1
and
II.1
shows that

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\right>=\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}},

(8)

with the Gaunt integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}\equiv\int d\Omega\,Y_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(\hat{n})Y_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(\hat{n})Y_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(\hat{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{0}&{0}&{0}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right).

Reading Eq.
8
and Eq.
II.1
together highlights two symmetries: the 3-
j
symbol with
m
’s, as previously discussed, enforces rotation invariance about the observer. The 3-
j
symbol with zeros enforces that
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
is even; this means the CMB bispectrum is parity-even. These symmetries are reviewed in
Hu 2001
Sec. IIA. and B.

The CMB trispectrum
Hu 2001

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a_{\ell_{4}m_{4}}\right>=\sum_{\ell_{12},m_{12}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{12}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{-m_{12}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{12}}&{\ell_{3}}&{\ell_{4}}\\
{m_{12}}&{m_{3}}&{m_{4}}\\
\end{array}\right)Q_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}(\ell_{12})}^{\ell_{3}\ell_{4}},

where relative to
Hu 2001
we have switched the upper and lower indices of
Q
, rewritten
LM\to\ell_{12}m_{12}
to highlight the coupling scheme used, and subscripted
\ell_{12}
where
Hu 2001
would have it appear like an argument of
Q
. As discussed in
Hu 2001
, there are several choices for how to couple the “external”
\ell m
’s, which make no physical difference and are related by angular momentum recoupling with Wigner 6-
j
symbols (discussion below his Eq. 16 and in Appendix A). Here we adopt the coupling scheme of
Cahn and Slepian 2020
, which matches that used in the body of
Hu 2001
. We refer to
\ell_{12}
as an “intermediate” and parentheses will distinguish intermediates throughout.

II.2
Smith-Zaldarriaga Algorithm

It has for some years been of interest to produce a CMB map that has a given pair (power spectrum) and triple (bispectrum) correlation function. The problem of realizing a given power spectrum is simple; one just draws
a_{\ell m}
as an appropriate Gaussian Random Field (GRF). This problem was also solved in 3D, as appropriate for large-scale structure, with the well-known constrained realization algoorithm of
Hoffman and Ribak 1991
. For the CMB bispectrum
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
gives an algorithm, which we recap below.

Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
defines the functional

\displaystyle T[a]\equiv\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i}}B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}

(19)

(their eq. 55), where
a=\{a_{\ell_{i}m_{i}}\}
is shorthand for the set of harmonic coefficients appearing on the righthand side. We note that since the sum is over all
\ell_{i}m_{i}
, a particular given
\ell m
pair may appear three times in the sum; we may have
\ell_{1}m_{1}=\ell m
,
\ell_{2}m_{2}=\ell m
, or
\ell_{3}m_{3}=\ell m
.

They also define the operator

\displaystyle\nabla_{\ell m}T[a]\equiv\frac{\partial T[a]}{\partial a_{\ell m}^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i}}B_{\ell\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{*}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{*}

(22)

(their eq. 56). The factor of
1/2
comes from accounting for the fact that in the sum in Eq.
II.2
, a particular pair
\ell m
can appear three times, and the derivative with respect to this
\ell m
will pick up each of these, yielding
3\times 1/6=1/2
. We note that their Eq. 56 seems to have a missing
\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i}}
on its righthand side, but this error does not affect their Eq. 84 because the expectation values they have there (
<a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}>
and same for the pair with subscripts
\ell_{3}m_{3}
and
\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}
) would eliminate the sum in any case. We can be sure this sum is missing from the righthand side because without it, the number of free indices does not match between the lefthand and righthand sides.

They then show that if one takes a GRF
a_{\ell m}
and perturbs it by the gradient above evaluated at the inverse power spectrum acting on the
a_{\ell m}
, at leading order in the perturbation, the resulting bispectrum will be
B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
. Writing the perturbed
a_{\ell m}
as

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}^{\prime}\equiv a_{\ell m}+\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell m}T[C^{-1}_{\ell^{\prime}}a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}],

(23)

we may compute

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}\right>=\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}T[C^{-1}_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}]\right>

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+5\;{\rm perms.}

(24)

Terms involving three unperturbed
a_{\ell m}
vanish because
a_{\ell m}
is a GRF and so has vanishing bispectrum. we have dropped terms involving two or three
\nabla_{\ell m}
as being sub-leading. The five permutations are the five other possible orderings of
\ell_{i}m_{i}
.

Applying the definition Eq.
22
, the first term of Eq.
II.2
becomes

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}T[C^{-1}_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}]\right>=

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\ell_{i}^{\prime}}B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{3}^{\prime}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}&{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}^{\prime}}&{m_{3}^{\prime}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left<C_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}C_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}^{*}\right>.

Since
a_{\ell m}
is a GRF, the expectation value may be pulled apart into two pairwise expectations:

\displaystyle\left<C_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}C_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}^{*}\right>=C_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}\left<a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}\right>

\displaystyle\times C_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}^{-1}\left<a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}^{*}\right>=\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell_{2}\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{m_{2}m_{2}^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell_{3}\ell_{3}^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{m_{3}m_{3}^{\prime}},

(27)

with
\delta^{\rm K}
the Kronecker delta, unity if its subscripts are equal and zero otherwise. Inserting this result in Eq.
II.2
we have

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}T[C^{-1}_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}]\right>=

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{6}B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right).

The same process may be applied to the other five permutations in Eq.
II.2
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}\right>=B_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right),

i.e.
Eq. 84 of
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
. Recalling that the CMB power spectrum
C_{\ell}
used here was arbitrary (and cancelled out of the CMB bispectrum obtained above), we see that this algorithm enables realizing a set of CMB
a_{\ell m}
with any given power spectrum and bispectrum.

We now seek to generalize this algorithm to 3D LSS. Let us notice that the CMB bispectrum actually involves four points: the observer, and the three points on the CMB spherical shell. This geometry is exactly the same as that used in the spherical-harmonic decomposition NPCF algorithms
Slepian and Eisenstein 2015a
;
Slepian and Eisenstein 2016
;
Philcox et al. 2022
;
Sunseri et al. 2023
, but in that case, the “primary” galaxy is set to be at the origin and the “secondaries” are on spherical shells with different radii. In particular, the geometry for the CMB bispectrum is the same as that for the 4PCF around a given primary.

Let us briefly review this. The NPCF algorithms of the series of papers noted above expands the density on each shell about a primary galaxy (at
\vec{x}
) as

\displaystyle\delta(r,\hat{r};\vec{x})=\sum_{\ell m}a_{\ell m}(r;\vec{x})_{\ell m}(\hat{r}).

(32)

The NPCFs are then expanded in the isotropic basis of
Cahn and Slepian 2020
. In particular, the 4PCF
\zeta
is

\displaystyle\zeta(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3};\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\hat{r}_{3})=

\displaystyle\qquad\left<\hat{\zeta}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3};\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\hat{r}_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{i}}\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2},\hat{r}_{3}).

(33)

Hat denotes the estimate of
\zeta
about a specific primary galaxy at
\vec{x}
, and
\left<\cdots\right>_{\vec{x}}
the average over all primaries, and in the third line, we have performed the expansion into the isotropic basis
\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}

Cahn and Slepian 2020
. The expansion coefficients
\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
are then

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})=\left<\hat{\zeta}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{i}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}.

(36)

The similarity of the last line of Eq.
II.2
to the CMB bispectrum is notable. It leads us to ask if the
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
bispectrum algorithm, if we generalize it so that each
a_{\ell m}
may lie on a different spherical shell, could offer a means of realizing an arbitrary 4PCF about a given primary.

III
Realizing a Given 4PCF

We first make a comment about permutations. For the CMB, there is no way to distinguish the
\ell_{i}m_{i}
, so we end up with the sum over permutations of them. Once we are working on spherical shells of different radii
r_{i}
, we may let the
r_{i}
give a unique ordering to the
\ell_{i}
. Indeed, this is needed to uniquely define the coupling scheme used for the isotropic basis functions in which the harmonic-based algorithms measure NPCFs, as detailed in
Cahn and Slepian 2020
.

For the 4PCF, let us choose the convention that we consider only configurations of three galaxies around a given primary where the relative distances
r_{i}
satisfy
r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq r_{3}
. This then gives unique meaning to
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
,
etc.

\ell_{1}
is the
\ell
associated with the harmonic decomposition of the pattern of secondaries on the smallest shell of a set of three,
\ell_{2}
of the second-smallest,
etc.
This convention eliminates the need for permutations over the
\ell_{i}
, rendering the LSS problem actually simpler than the CMB problem.

By analogy with Sec.
II
, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}[a]\equiv\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i},r_{i}}\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(r_{3}).

(39)

We note that any particular given value of
\ell m
may appear in three different positions:
\ell_{1}m_{1}=\ell m
,
\ell_{2}m_{2}=\ell m
, and
\ell_{3}m_{3}=\ell m
, just as in the CMB algorithm. However, the ordering by
r_{i}
means there is no need to consider permutations.

We also define

\displaystyle\nabla_{\ell m}\mathcal{T}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}[a]}{\partial(a_{\ell m}^{*}(r))}=\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i},r_{i}}\zeta_{\ell\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r,r_{2},r_{3})\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{*}(r_{2})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{*}(r_{3}).

(42)

The derivative is intended to be for a specific
\ell m
and shell
r
.

Let us now consider perturbing the
a_{\ell m}
on each shell (denoted by
r
) about a primary galaxy at
\vec{x}
as

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}^{\prime}(r;\vec{x})=a_{\ell m}(r;\vec{x})+\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell m}\mathcal{T}[C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(r;\vec{x})].

(43)

Again, by assumption,
a_{\ell m}(r)
is a GRF with power spectrum on that shell
C_{\ell}(r)
. We note that the
r
inside
a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(r)
is indeed intended to be unprimed. Importantly, the perturbation on a given shell depends only on that shell’s
r
and
\ell m
. Going back to the definition Eq.
III
we see the perturbation is a sum over all
r_{2},r_{3}
and
\ell_{2}m_{2},\ell_{3}m_{3}
.

We now compute the leading-order 4PCF coefficient about the primary, that is, Eq.
II.2
prior to averaging over
\vec{x}
. Let us first focus on the last line of Eq.
II.2
, and write it prior to averaging over
\vec{x}
. We have, dropping terms with three unperturbed
a_{\ell m}
(as they will vanish after averaging), and dropping sub-leading terms, that

\displaystyle a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})=

\displaystyle a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\mathcal{T}[C_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell_{3}^{\prime}m_{3}^{\prime}}(r_{3};\vec{x})]

\displaystyle+2\;{\rm perms.}

(44)

Using the definition Eq.
III
we find

\displaystyle a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}(r_{2};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}(r_{3};\vec{x})=

\displaystyle a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})\,\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell_{i}^{\prime}m_{i}^{\prime},r_{i}^{\prime}}\zeta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{3}}(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime},r_{3})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}&{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}^{\prime}}&{m_{2}^{\prime}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)C_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}^{-1}(r_{1}^{\prime})C_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}(r_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\times a_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}m_{1}^{\prime}}^{*}(r_{1}^{\prime};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}(r_{2}^{\prime};\vec{x})+2\;{\rm perms.}

(47)

Now, inserting this in Eq.
II.2

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}(r_{2};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}(r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\ell_{i}^{\prime}m_{i}^{\prime},r_{i}^{\prime}}\zeta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{3}}(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime},r_{3})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}&{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}^{\prime}}&{m_{2}^{\prime}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)C_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}^{-1}(r_{1}^{\prime})C_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}(r_{2}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}m_{1}^{\prime}}^{*}(r_{1}^{\prime};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}m_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}(r_{2}^{\prime};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\;{\rm perms.}

(50)

Now, the
a_{\ell m}
are, by construction, not correlated across shells, so we must have
r_{1}=r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}=r_{2}^{\prime}
, and since on each shell, the field is a GRF, we have Kronecker deltas setting

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}^{\prime}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{\prime}(r_{2};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{\prime}(r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}=

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{1}{3}\,\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\;{\rm perms.}

\displaystyle\qquad=\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right).

Inserting this result for the average of the three
a_{\ell m}^{\prime}
in Eq.
II.2
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\sum_{m_{i}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{3}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{m_{3}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})

(59)

using the orthogonality identity 34.3.18 in
Olver, F and Clark,
C 2010
for the 3-
j
symbols.

Thus, we see that producing a GRF
a_{\ell m}(r)
on each shell (independent on each shell), and perturbing on each shell as in Eq.
43
, will produce the desired 4PCF, with coefficients
\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
when expanded in the isotropic basis of
Cahn and Slepian 2020
. We emphasize that the perturbation on a given shell
r
depends only on that shell’s
r
; it is a sum over all the other shells’
r_{i}
. Thus, it can give the correct desired 4PCF at all combinations of
r_{i}
shells; it is not just tuned to give the right 4PCF at only one combination.

Finally, we emphasize that no 3-
j
symbol with all zero
m
ever appeared; thus there is no restriction on the sum of the
\ell_{i}
, and so we can realize a parity-odd 4PCF of the kind considered in
Cahn et al. 2023
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Philcox 2022
.

IV
Realizing a Given 3PCF

The harmonic-based 3PCF algorithm
Slepian and Eisenstein 2015a
;
Slepian and Eisenstein 2016
;
Sunseri et al. 2023
essentially sits around a primary galaxy at
\vec{x}
and computes angular cross power spectra of two different spherical shells,
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
. Thus, it is exactly like the CMB angular power spectrum problem with the observer being analogous to the primary galaxy, and if we generalize to two shells rather than just one. This provokes wondering whether simply promoting the unperturbed
a_{\ell m}
of Sec.
III
to have a non-vanishing power spectrum across shells would enable realizing a desired 3PCF,
i.e.
allowing
<a_{\ell m(r_{1})a_{\ell m}^{*}(r_{2})}>\neq 0
. However, this will cause the 4PCF algorithm of Sec.
III
to break down, as there we needed to assume that the
a_{\ell m}
are independent on each shell so that only the desired
r_{i}
shells get picked out from the sum in Eq.
III
. We might consider removing this sum from the definition
III
and simply defining that quantity at a desired set of three shells, but then our 4PCF algorithm would only reproduce the desired 4PCF at one set of shells at a time. So we need a different strategy.

Let us consider adding a sum of independent GRFs
b^{(j)}
to the perturbed
a^{\prime}_{\ell m}
of Eq.
43
. Let us define them so that at fixed
j
, the GRF
b^{(j)}
on each shell is simply a scaled version of some global, shell-independent GRF
\mathcal{B}^{(j)}
, scaled by a universal function of radius
f^{(j})(r)
:

\displaystyle b_{\ell m}^{(j)}(r;\vec{x})\equiv\mathcal{B}_{\ell m}^{(j)}f^{(j)}(r),

(60)

where the power spectrum of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell m}^{(j)}
is
C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j)}
. We require that the field has mean zero,
i.e.

<b_{\ell m}^{(j)}>=0
.

We have

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}^{\prime}(r;\vec{x})=a_{\ell m}(r;\vec{x})+\frac{1}{3}\nabla_{\ell m}\mathcal{T}[C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{-1}a_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(r;\vec{x})]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j}(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j)})^{-1/2}b_{\ell m}^{(j)}(r;\vec{x}).

(61)

In the isotropic basis of two-argument functions
\mathcal{P}_{\ell\ell}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2})

Cahn and Slepian 2020
(or, up to a scaling and phase, the Legendre basis
Slepian and Eisenstein 2015a
;
Slepian and Eisenstein 2016
), the 3PCF is

\displaystyle\zeta(r_{1},r_{2};\hat{r}_{1}\cdot\hat{r}_{2})=\sum_{\ell}\zeta_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2})\mathcal{P}_{\ell\ell}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2}).

(62)

The expansion coefficient is

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2})=\left<\hat{\zeta}_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\left<a_{\ell m}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell m}^{*}(r_{2};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}.

(63)

We now compute

\displaystyle\left<a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{1};\vec{x})(a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{2};\vec{x}))^{*}\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\left<\sum_{j_{1}j_{2}}b^{(j_{1})}(r_{1};\vec{x})b^{(j_{2})}(r_{2};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}j_{2}}f^{(j_{1})}(r_{1})f^{(j_{2})}(r_{2})(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})})^{-1/2}(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{2})})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left<\mathcal{B}^{(j_{1})}(\vec{x})\mathcal{B}^{(j_{2})}(\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}.

(64)

Several terms vanished under the averaging. First, the unperturbed
a_{\ell m}
is by assumption an independent GRF on each shell, so cross-shell correlators of it vanish:
<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{*}(r_{2};\vec{x})>=0
for
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
.
a_{\ell m}
is also uncorrelated with
b_{\ell m}
, so any terms
<ab>
vanish. At leading order, we can only have terms with one factor of
\nabla_{\ell m}
in
a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{1};\vec{x})(a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{2};\vec{x}))^{*}
. While each
\nabla_{\ell m}
contains two
a_{\ell m}
, which can have a non-zero expectation since they are on the same shell, these factors of
\nabla_{\ell m}
will also be multiplied by either an
a_{\ell m}
or a
b_{\ell m}
. In the former case, we then have an expectation value of three
a_{\ell m}
, which vanishes because it is an odd correlator of a GRF. In the latter case,
b_{\ell m}
is independent of
a_{\ell m}
and we recall that
b_{\ell m}
has zero mean on each shell. Thus
<b_{\ell m}><\nabla_{\ell m}>=0
.

We now further simplify Eq.
64
. By assumption
\mathcal{B}^{(j_{1})}
and
\mathcal{B}^{(j_{2})}
are uncorrelated unless
j_{1}=j_{2}
. Focusing on the last line of Eq.
64
and setting
j_{1}=j_{2}
we have

\displaystyle(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})})^{-1/2}(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{2})})^{-1/2}\left<\mathcal{B}^{(j_{1})}(\vec{x})\mathcal{B}^{(j_{2})}(\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}=

\displaystyle(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})})^{-1/2}(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})})^{-1/2}\left<\mathcal{B}^{(j_{1})}(\vec{x})\mathcal{B}^{(j_{1})}(\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=(C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})})^{-1}C_{\mathcal{B},\ell}^{(j_{1})}=1.

(65)

Inserting this result in Eq.
64
, we find

\displaystyle\left<a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{1};\vec{x})(a^{\prime}_{\ell m}(r_{2};\vec{x}))^{*}\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\left<\sum_{j_{1}j_{2}}b^{(j_{1})}(r_{1};\vec{x})b^{(j_{2})}(r_{2};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}}f^{(j_{1})}(r_{1})f^{(j_{1})}(r_{2}).

(66)

Inserting this into Eq.
IV
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2})=\sum_{m}\sum_{j_{1}}f^{(j_{1})}(r_{1})f^{(j_{1})}(r_{2})

\displaystyle=(2\ell+1)\sum_{j_{1}}f^{(j_{1})}(r_{1})f^{(j_{1})}(r_{2}).

(67)

Thus, we see that, if the 3PCF is factorizable, it may be obtained in this way if we simply set

\displaystyle f^{(j_{1})}(r_{1})f^{(j_{1})}(r_{2})=(2\ell+1)^{-1}\zeta_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2}).

(68)

We do notice that the functional form of the dependence on
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
must be the same. Even for the real-space tree level 3PCF (
e.g.

Slepian and Eisenstein 2015b
), this gives an issue as even prior to cyclic summing, the dipole term (with respect to the angle between
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
in the triangle) has the form

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\rm pc},1}(r_{1},r_{2})=

\displaystyle\qquad\xi^{[1+]}(r_{1})\xi^{[1-]}(r_{2})+\xi^{[1+]}(r_{2})\xi^{[1-]}(r_{1}),

(69)

with subscript “pc” denoting “pre-cyclic” and where

\displaystyle\xi^{[1\pm]}(r)\equiv\int\frac{k^{2}dk}{2\pi^{2}}\;j_{1}(kr)k^{\pm 1}P(k)

(70)

and
P(k)
the power spectrum. Moreover, once redshift-space distortions (RSD) are included, the pre-cyclic bispectrum begins to include not just wave-numbers
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, but also
k_{3}

Scoccimarro et al. 1999
, leading to
r_{3}=[r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}-2r_{1}r_{2}(\hat{r}_{1}\cdot\hat{r}_{2})]^{1/2}
dependence in the pre-cyclic 3PCF
Slepian and Eisenstein 2017
. However, this is suppressed by three orders of magnitude relative to the
r_{1},r_{2}
-only terms, as shown in
Slepian and Eisenstein 2017
(compare Figs. 1 and 2 there). Thus it is not important to model in a realization of the 3PCF to match that in tree-level standard perturbation theory.

Nonetheless, cyclically summing the pre-cyclic 3PCF model and then re-projecting onto the isotropic basis in
\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2}
still leads to non-factorizable behavior. We use a trick similar to that of
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
to address this. There,
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
writes any non-factorizable CMB bispectrum as an integral over a factorizable integrand.
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
then notes that if the integral is performed numerically with quadrature, that integral becomes a sum over the integrand evaluated at the quadrature points, and each term in that sum is factorizable. This then gives the form required for their algorithm, where the CMB bispectrum is represented as a sum of factorizable pieces.

For us, we observe that, if the post-cyclic 3PCF is written in the form Eq.
62
, each of the coefficients
\zeta_{\ell}(r_{1},r_{2})
will be a sum of terms derived from projecting the pre-cyclic 3PCF at
\vec{r}_{2},\vec{r}_{3}
and
\vec{r}_{3},\vec{r}_{1}
back onto the isotropic basis at
\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2}
(
e.g.
discussed in Sec. 3 of
Slepian and Eisenstein 2017
). Each such term will look like

\displaystyle\xi^{[L]}(r_{1})\xi^{[L]}(r_{3})\mathcal{P}_{L}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{3})=

\displaystyle\sum_{J}\zeta_{J}(r_{1},r_{2})\mathcal{P}_{J}(\hat{r}_{1},\hat{r}_{2})

(71)

with coefficients

\displaystyle\zeta_{J}(r_{1},r_{2})=\xi^{[L]}(r_{1})\sum_{L_{1}}\mathcal{C}_{LJL_{1}}^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{L}&{J}&{L_{1}}\\
{0}&{0}&{0}\\
\end{array}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int\frac{k^{2}dk}{2\pi^{2}}\;P(k)j_{L_{1}}(kr_{1})j_{J}(kr_{2})

(74)

(
Slepian and Eisenstein 2017
Eq. 30); we recall that
\mathcal{C}_{LJL_{1}}
is defined in Eq.
7
.

If this integral is performed by numerical quadrature, then we will have a sum over the quadrature points in
k
, and at each such point, the integrand is factorizable.

The spherical Bessel functions, at a given
r
, are band-limited functions of
k
, since they are simply the projection of a plane wave along a line that is not necessarily perpendicular to the wave-fronts; thus their frequencies are set by that of the underlying plane wave. Put mathematically, at each
r
, they are just sums of cosines and sines of
kr
weighted by rational functions of
kr
. Thus, given some
r_{\rm max}
representing the maximal distance out to which we wish to measure our 3PCF, we may set an exact sampling scheme for them. Standard tricks like the Poisson summation formula may be employed to accelerate the convergence of the sum by which we numerically evaluate the integral.

The power spectrum also should be considered when choosing quadrature points; it is a smooth envelope, with a small additional “rider” of BAO wiggles beginning at
k\simeq 0.01\;h/{\rm Mpc}
. Since for numerical integrations,
P(k)
is always smoothed by a function such as
\exp[-k^{2}\sigma^{2}]
with
\sigma\simeq 1\;{\rm Mpc}/h
to avoid Gibbs’ phenomenon ringing,
P(k)
effectively has roughly compact support, further reducing the number of quadrature points needed. Often a logarithmic grid is employed. Typically of order a hundred or so points is sufficient.

Conveniently,
r_{\rm max}
we usually employ for 3PCF measurements (
e.g.

Slepian et al. 2017a
;
Slepian et al. 2017b
;
Moresco et al. 2021
is roughly twice the BAO scale (
i.e.

\sim 200\;{\rm Mpc}/h
), meaning that the frequency of the sBFs will at most be twice or so the BAO wiggles’ frequency. Now, the two sBFs can resonate (consider multiplying out the sines and cosine and reducing the products to sums using the double-angle formulae), giving another factor of two. Thus, a sampling four times as fine as that needed for the BAO should suffice.

Typically a numerical integration might need to range from
k_{\rm min}\simeq 10^{-4}\;h/{\rm Mpc}
to
k_{\rm max}\sim 3\;h/{\rm Mpc}
, and sample logarithmically. The BAO in the transfer function has of order 10 peaks from
0.01\;h/{\rm Mpc}
to
0.1\;h/{\rm Mpc}
(
Eisenstein and Hu 1998
Fig. 3); the power spectrum is the transfer function’s square and so with resonance of the oscillations, can have twice that. We then need to sample at roughly four times the frequency of power spectrum peak for the reasons in the prior paragraph. Thus, we should have roughly 80 points from
0.01\;h/{\rm Mpc}
to
0.1\;h/{\rm Mpc}
. If we sample uniformly per dex, and have about 5 dex from
k_{\rm min}
to
k_{\rm max}
, this implies 400 quadrature points. Thus, the integral required by Eq.
IV
should be well-approximated by a sum over 400 terms, each of which is factorizable. This is comparable in order of magnitude to the number of points used for the
Planck
forecast in
Smith and Zaldarriaga 2011
(discussion following their Eq. 44), and likely can be improved upon significantly using
e.g.
Poisson summation.

IV.1
3PCF Realization Does Not Affect 4PCF

We now show that our addition of terms in
b_{\ell m}
to
a_{\ell m}^{\prime}
as in Eq.
61
does not alter our 4PCF realization, and so one can produce a single density field with both a desired 3PCF and a desired 4PCF.

We simply need to compute Eq.
III
again, but using Eq.
61
for
a_{\ell m}^{\prime}
(and working at leading order in
b_{\ell m}
and
\nabla_{\ell m}
, as always). Only correlators involving at least one
b_{\ell m}
will be “new” terms, and any with more than one
b_{\ell m}
we drop as sub-leading (
b_{\ell m}
is perturbative). For the same reason, we drop any terms with more than one factor of
\nabla
. We can have terms as
baa
and
ba\nabla
. However, the latter is also sub-leading (because both
b
and
\nabla
are perturbative), so it should also be dropped. Thus, we are left just with a term:

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})b_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}=

\displaystyle\left<a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1};\vec{x})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}\left<b_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(r_{3};\vec{x})\right>_{\vec{x}}=0

(75)

since the mean of
b_{\ell m}
is zero (noted below Eq.
60
). Thus, the perturbation needed to produce a desired 3PCF will not impact our production of a desired 4PCF.

V
Including the 2PCF

We now discuss how to include a desired 2PCF about the primary galaxy at
\vec{x}
. For a GRF on each shell
a_{\ell m}(r)
, the 2PCF between the primary and that shell will simply be

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(r;\vec{x})=\delta(\vec{x})a_{00}(r;\vec{x}),

(76)

where
\delta(\vec{x})
is the density fluctuation associated with the primary and only
a_{00}
contributes because the 2PCF is an isotropic average.

We recall that the 4PCF is actually independent of the angular power spectrum
C_{\ell}(r)
of the
a_{\ell m}(r)
. Thus we may give
a_{00}
some desired “global” radial dependence without affecting the 4PCF, as the inverse power spectrum there will cancel it out. Thus, we may realize any desired 2PCF along with any 3PCF and 4PCF. We do note that there is an effect on the 3PCF monopole when
r_{1}=r_{2}
, but fortunately that is, up to scaling, the 2PCF squared (at tree-level in PT), and so modifying
a_{00}
as outlined above is acceptable.

VI
Extending to NPCFs,
N>4

The fundamental requirements of the algorithm of this work are simply that the NPCFs be estimated around a primary galaxy by decomposing the density field on spherical shells in spherical harmonics. Thus, the algorithm trivially extends to NPCFs for
N>4
. We note this is useful because
Philcox et al. 2022
indeed can measure the 5PCF and 6PCF in the isotropic basis of
Cahn and Slepian 2020
.

Here we write down the analog of
\mathcal{T}
(Eq.
39
) required for realizing a given 5PCF as well; going to
N>5
is straightforward but the expressions are lengthy.

For extending to a 5PCF, we would have four
a_{\ell m}
about the primary. Denoting the 5PCF analog of
\mathcal{T}
as
\mathcal{T}^{(5)}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(5)}[a]\equiv\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i},r_{i}}\sum_{\ell_{12},m_{12}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{1}}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{12}}\\
{m_{1}}&{m_{2}}&{-m_{12}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{12}}&{\ell_{3}}&{\ell_{4}}\\
{m_{12}}&{m_{3}}&{m_{4}}\\
\end{array}\right)\zeta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}(\ell_{12})\ell_{3}\ell_{4}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(r_{1})a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(r_{2})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(r_{3})a_{\ell_{4}m_{4}}(r_{4}).

We recall that
\ell_{12}
is an “intermediate” (discussion in Sec.
II
and in
Cahn and Slepian 2020
).

Defining
\nabla_{\ell m}
analogously to Eq.
III
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\ell m}\mathcal{T}^{(5)}\equiv\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}[a]}{\partial(a_{\ell m}^{*}(r))}=\sum_{\ell_{i}m_{i},r_{i}}\sum_{\ell_{12},m_{12}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell}&{\ell_{2}}&{\ell_{12}}\\
{m}&{m_{2}}&{-m_{12}}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\ell_{12}}&{\ell_{3}}&{\ell_{4}}\\
{m_{12}}&{m_{3}}&{m_{4}}\\
\end{array}\right)\zeta_{\ell\ell_{2}(\ell_{12})\ell_{3}\ell_{4}}(r,r_{2},r_{3},r_{4})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}^{*}(r_{2})a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}^{*}(r_{3})a_{\ell_{4}m_{4}}^{*}(r_{4}).

(85)

The computation of the 5PCF about a primary proceeds analogously to that in Sec.
III
. We note that at leading order, adding a term like this (with a pre-factor of
1/4
to account for the four different times one factor of
\nabla_{\ell m}

VII
Extending to Dimensions Greater than 3 and non-scalar fields

Philcox and Slepian 2022
shows how to extend the harmonic-based algorithm to arbitrary spatial dimension and arbitrary spatial geometry, via hyperspherical harmonics and generalizations of the Wigner 3-
j
symbols. Since this algorithm requires only the same factorization properties as the 3D, flat space version, extension of the approach in this paper for realizing NPCFs is straightforward. Indeed, formally, it will look the same, but the 3-
j
symbols should be rewritten as Clebsch-Gordan (CG) coefficients, in bra-ket notation; these are then agnostic as to dimension. In 2D, the CG coefficient becomes a Kronecker delta; in 3D, it is proportional to a 3-
j
symbol; in 4D, it involves a Wigner 9-
j
symbol (discussed below Eq. 17 of
Philcox and Slepian 2022
). Then the coupling coefficients may be built up as in Eq. 22 of
Philcox and Slepian 2022
; these should replace the 3-
j
symbols in our expressions for the 3PCF, 4PCF,
etc.
when they are expanded in the isotropic basis.

The algorithm here could also straightforwardly be expanded to non-scalar fields, such as fields with spin, using the spin-weighted spherical harmonics. Since these have the same factorization properties as the harmonics used here, the extension will be straightforward.

Finally, we note that optimal sphere packing has also been solved in higher dimensions, so one can pack as many primaries as possible in a given volume in these extensions as well as in the 3D case.

VIII
Conclusions

We have presented an algorithm that enables realization, about a given primary galaxy, of a density field with any desired 2PCF, 3PCF, 4PCF, and even 5PCF. This should be very useful in the context of testing pipelines for upcoming NPCF analyses in large-scale structure surveys such as DESI, Euclid, Roman, Spherex,
etc
.

We note that the 4PCF algorithm in particular would be enabling for testing the parity-odd analysis pipeline to search for parity-violation using the galaxy 4PCF, as initially described in
Cahn et al. 2023
and carried out in
Hou et al. 2023
;
Philcox 2022
using
Hou et al. 2022
for the covariance matrix.
