Robust Estimation of Sparse, High Dimensional Time Series with Polynomial Tails.
Sagnik Halder, George Michailidis

Abstract
High dimensional Vector Autoregressions (VAR) have received a lot of interest recently due to novel applications in health, engineering, finance and the social sciences. Three issues arise when analyzing VAR s s : (a) The high dimensional nature of the model in the presence of many time series that poses challenges for consistent estimation of its parameters; (b) the presence of temporal dependence introduces additional challenges for theoretical analysis of various estimation procedures; (b) the presence of heavy tails in a number of applications. Recent work, e.g. [ Basu and Michailidis 2015 ] , [ Kock and Callot 2015 ] , has addressed consistent estimation of sparse high dimensional stable Gaussian VAR models based on an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} LASSO procedure. Further, the rates obtained are optimal, in the sense that they match those for iid data, plus a multiplicative factor (which is the "price" paid) for temporal dependence. However, the third issue remains unaddressed in extant literature. This paper extends existing results in the following important direction: it considers consistent estimation of the parameters of sparse high dimensional VAR models driven by heavy tailed homoskedastic or heteroskedastic noise processes (that do not possess all moments). A robust penalized approach (e.g., LASSO) is adopted for which optimal consistency rates and corresponding finite sample bounds for the underlying model parameters are obtain that match those for iid data, albeit paying a price for temporal dependence. The theoretical results are illustrated on VAR models and also on other popular time series models. Notably, the key technical tool used, is a single concentration bound for heavy tailed dependent processes.



Keywords:
Huber Loss, Heavy Tails, High Dimensional Time Series

Introduction

A number of statistical models are routinely used in modeling and analysis of temporally dependent high dimensional data encountered in neuroscience, genomics, economics, finance and signal processing applications. To obtain consistent estimates of the underlying model parameters and thus overcome the limited availability of samples (time points) — the true, underlying parameter space is assumed to be low-dimensional or "sparse". This sparsity, in turn, is recovered by penalized ("regularized") methods, with popular methods being the LASSO or the "Dantzig" selector
[
Wainwright 2019
]
. To set the stage, consider a stochastic regression model
[
Hamilton 2020
]
given by

\displaystyle y_{t}=x_{t}^{\top}\beta^{*}+\epsilon_{t},\quad t=1,\dots,n,

(1)

where
\{y_{t}\}
and
\{x_{t}\}
are generated from stationary stochastic processes in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, respectively, and
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
is the noise process. Writing
Y^{\top}=[y_{1}\dots y_{n}],X^{\top}=[x_{1}\dots x_{n}]
and
\mathcal{E}^{\top}=[\epsilon_{1}\dots\epsilon_{n}]
, the model can be expressed in matrix form as

\displaystyle Y=X\beta^{*}+\mathcal{E}.

(2)

The goal is to consistently estimate the
p
-dimensional parameter
\beta^{*}
from data pairs
(y_{t},x_{t})
, where
1\leq t\leq n
, when the dimension
p\gg n
. To this end, the standard assumption is that
\beta^{*}
has an underlying low-dimensional structure, e.g. being sparse or group sparse
[
Wainwright 2019
]
, which can be induced using a penalized estimation approach.

The main application of this basic setup will be a VAR model of lag
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Formally, a VAR(
d
) process (
d\geq 1
represents a
fixed

Z_{t}=B_{1}^{\top}Z_{t-1}+\dots+B_{d}^{\top}Z_{t-d}+\epsilon_{t}
, where each
B_{k},k=1,\dots,d
is a fixed coefficient matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and innovations
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are
p
-dimensional random vectors whose components
\{\epsilon_{tj}\}
are independent random variables. The parameter of interest is the matrix
B^{\top}=[B_{1}^{\top},\dots,B_{d}^{\top}]
with
dp^{2}
entries. It can be seen that even for small values of
p=20
,
d=5
, there are 2000 parameters and in many applications the available number of time points is significantly smaller. Note that every VAR(d) process has an equivalent VAR(1) representation
Lütkepohl 2005

\tilde{Z}_{t}=\tilde{B}^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{t-1}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}
where

\displaystyle\tilde{Z_{t}}:=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{t}\\
Z_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{t-d+1}\end{bmatrix}_{(pd\times 1)},\quad\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}_{(pd\times 1)},\quad\tilde{B}^{\top}:=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}^{\top}&B_{2}^{\top}&\cdots&B_{d-1}^{\top}&B_{d}^{\top}\\
I_{p}&\boldsymbol{0}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&I_{p}&&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\cdots&I_{p}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}_{(dp\times dp)}.

(3)

Suppose that observations for
T\geq d

\{Z_{0},\dots,Z_{T}\}
are available; we can then write the original VAR(d) process as
Z_{t}=B^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t},d\leq t\leq T

parallel regressions, the

regression is given by

\displaystyle Z_{tj}=B_{j:}^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{t-1}+\epsilon_{tj},\quad d\leq t\leq T,

(4)

wherein
B_{j:}
represents the
j^{th}
row of
B^{\top}
. Fix
1\leq j\leq p
and rewrite
Z_{tj}=y_{t-d+1}
,
B_{j:}=\beta^{*}
,
\tilde{Z}_{t-1}=x_{t-d+1}
and
\epsilon_{tj}=\eta_{t-d+1}
(we suppress dependence on
j
for now). Hence, the
j^{th}
regression is just a stochastic regression
y_{i}=x_{i}^{\top}\beta^{*}+\eta_{i},1\leq i\leq n
, where
n=T-d+1
, or

\displaystyle Y=X\beta^{*}+\eta

(5)

in matrix form, and the parameter
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}
.
Clearly, this is an example of the generic stochastic regression framework (
1
). Hence, we can carry out
p
penalized regressions
simultaneously
with the
same
penalty
\mathcal{R}
and some tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
(as given by (
6
)). The only difference will be the error probability (regret bound) for simultaneous estimation: if the regret bound for each regression is
\mathfrak{p}_{err}
(which will be the same for every regression as it only depends on
\mathcal{R}
), then the consistency result holds with probability at least
p(1-\mathfrak{p}_{err})-(p-1)=1-p\mathfrak{p}_{err}
(simple Bonferroni bound).

One key feature of this model is that, it is an endogenous process—the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
and output
\{y_{t}\}
are both driven by the noise
\{\eta_{t}\}
. As a result two things happen: (a) if the noise
\{\eta_{t}\}
is non-Gaussian/heavy tailed, this causes both
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{y_{t}\}
to be non-Gaussian/heavy tailed, and (b) the temporal dependence in the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
is due to noise
\{\eta_{t}\}
and further, is a function of the regression parameter
\beta^{*}
.

Two popular penalized methods have been extensively studied to date — the
Dantzig
estimator and
LASSO
. Further, in order to induce a general sparsity pattern, penalized estimation using different choices of penalty (usually a norm), have been extensively studied. However, their properties have been usually studied under the fixed design setup (
\{x_{t}\}
is deterministic) or the iid case, where the
\{x_{t}\}
typically has iid Gaussian or sub-Gaussian entries/rows, but correlated columns to signify cross-sectional dependence between the
p
components of each
\{x_{t}\}
.

In time series data, a key challenge is to handle
temporal
dependence, in addition to cross sectional dependence. Hence, the methods used in extant literature are not directly applicable in our case and require a natural extension. For a comprehensive overview on recent contributions in high dimensional time series, see
[
Wong et al. 2020
, section 1.1]
. The main difficulty, specifically, is to apply concentration bounds for sums of dependent random variables, and get (a) tight bounds, i.e, those that match the iid bounds, e.g., those of Hoeffding or Bernstein inequalities, and (b) interpretable or explicit constants that clearly display the temporal dependence. In our case, such a concentration bound, is given by Proposition (
2.1
). This is a concentration bound for bounded functions of sums of random variables generated from a stationary Markov Chain. Note that there exists a substantial body of literature on concentration bounds of this type. However, the majority of the results are devoted to (a)
finite
state space, (b)
uniformly
mixing/ergodic or (c)
reversible
Markov chains
[
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
]
. However, none of those apply to the heavy tailed VAR model under consideration — e.g. see
[
Athreya and Pantula 1986
, Theorem 2]
for uniformly mixing autoregressions, and
[
Adamczak et al. 2008
, Section 3.5]
or
[
Adamczak and Bednorz 2015
, Remark 4]
. Moreover, when those conditions are applicable to other time series models of interest, they do not usually give rise to easily interpretable constants, or are not tight
[
Adamczak and Bednorz 2015
, Remark 2]
. Therein, lies the main difficulty and challenge that the current paper addresses.

An additional complication arises when the noise
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
is heavy tailed (having only finitely many moments), which in turn, induces noisiness in
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{y_{t}\}
as well (e.g. in autoregressions). In this case, a robust regression framework with a general penalty, is indispensable. A gold standard in classical robust statistics is Huber regression (or M-estimation in general), which has recently been extended to the high-dimensional setting
[
Loh 2017
,
Loh 2018
]
. We leverage this framework for the stochastic regression model (
1
), in order to consistently estimate the regression parameter
\beta^{*}
at optimal rates. Finally, we apply our results to a large class of high dimensional, heavy tailed, VAR models (with or without
heteroskedastic
noise), under general sparsity, for which few theoretical results exist thus far. The main contributions of this work are summarized next:

1.

We extend theoretical consistency results in high-dimensional literature from the iid setup to the case of temporal dependence under a general sparsity pattern.

2.

Provide non-asymptotic consistency rates and finite sample bounds for a robust approach to heavy tailed, temporally processes. These rates match the optimal rates obtained in the iid case, in many time series models.

3.

Apply our theoretical results to several interesting examples of both linear and non-linear high dimensional time series.

2
Setting Up the Problem: Preliminaries.

We apply the following penalized estimation procedure (a variant of LASSO) to the model (
1
):

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}\in\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta)+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\beta)\right\},

(6)

wherein
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta)=\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{w}(x_{i})\ell[\mathfrak{w}(x_{i})(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)]
denotes a "robust" objective function, and
\mathfrak{w}()
is a weight function which dampens the noisiness of the predictor observations
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. In case
\ell(\cdot)
is the least squares function, and the weight function
\mathfrak{w}\equiv 1
, the procedure (
6
) is just the usual LASSO. Here
\mathcal{R}()
is the penalty function (usually a norm) and
\lambda_{n}
is the tuning parameter. Before proceeding further, we need to unpack some notions on (a) the function
\ell()
, (b) the penalty
\mathcal{R}()
and (c) the idea of temporal dependence.

2.1
Some Notions on the Loss Function.

We make the following mild assumptions about the function
\ell()
:

•

\ell:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable, convex, even function (so
\ell^{\prime}
is odd).

•

\ell()
has a bounded derivative —
||\ell^{\prime}||_{\infty}\leq\tau
for some
0\leq\tau<\infty
.

•

\ell()
has positive curvature on a small interval, i.e.,
\ell^{\prime\prime}
exists and satisfies
\ell^{\prime\prime}>0
in a small neighbourhood around the origin.

If
\ell()
is non-convex, then local/global minima may not exist and additional constraints are required (usually these constraints "cheat" by "convexifying" the problem – the penalized procedure (
1
) is now done over some local convex region in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
so that local minima are guaranteed at least). A classical choice of a robust loss function is the Huber loss (and its many variants), given by

\displaystyle\ell(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{u^{2}}{2}\quad\quad\quad\quad:|u|\leq\tau\\
\tau|u|-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\quad:|u|>\tau,\end{cases}

(7)

where the "cut-off point"
\tau>0
controls the level of robustification. Small values of
\tau
restrict the influence of the quadratic least squares loss, hence its susceptibility to noisiness, while large values of
\tau
do the opposite – in the limit
\tau=\infty
,
\ell()
is just the least squares loss. Note that the Huber loss is a convex, even function,
||\ell^{\prime}||_{\infty}\leq\tau
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}=1
on
(-\tau,\tau)
. In addition,
\ell^{\prime\prime}
exists everywhere except
u=\pm\tau
.

The reason for including a weight function
\mathfrak{w}():\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is that, often in time series, the covariate process
\{x_{t}\}
is driven by heavy shocks
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
, which makes
\{x_{t}\}
heavy tailed also (e.g. autoregressions). A classical choice of such a weight was proposed by
[
Mallows 1975
]
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}(x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{b}{||B_{M}x||}\right\},\quad\text{or}\quad\mathfrak{w}(x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{b^{2}}{||B_{M}x||^{2}}\right\},

(8)

where the (positive definite) shrinkage matrix
B_{M}
and
b>0
are fixed parameters. These weights essentially shrink data points
x
for which
||x||
is large, toward an elliptical shell defined by
B_{M}
; specifically
||\mathfrak{w}(x)x||\leq b/\Lambda_{\min}(B_{M}):=b_{M}
. Such estimators are called
bounded influence
estimators
[
Rousseeuw and Leroy 2005
]
. From here on, we will assume the weights
\mathfrak{w}()
bound the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
in the following way:
||\mathfrak{w}(x_{t})x_{t}||\leq b_{M}
, for
1\leq t\leq n
. We will call the term "
b_{M}
" — Mallow’s parameter (similar to Huber’s truncation parameter "
\tau
"). In simulations, we take the first choice of the weight function
\mathfrak{w}()
and let the shrinkage matrix
B_{M}=I
so that the Mallow’s parameter
b_{M}=b
.

2.2
Some Notions on the Penalty.

As mentioned, the standard assumption behind consistently estimating a parameter living in a high dimensional space, is that, the true parameter space is actually a low-dimensional subspace or "sparse". To induce this sparsity pattern in the corresponding estimator, we use a generic penalty
\mathcal{R}()
in (
1
). As such, some generic quantities related to
\mathcal{R}()
, will pop up in our main consistency result. To that end, we require the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Gaussian Width:
Let G be a
p\times d
random matrix with iid
N(0,1)
entries. For a a set
{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, the Gaussian width of
{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle w({T})=\underset{W\in{T}}{\sup}\text{tr}(W^{\top}G).

(9)

It measures the size of a (usually convex) set in the Euclidean space. A key challenge is to evaluate Gaussian widths of sets related to the penalty
\mathcal{R}()
.

Definition 2.2
.

Subspace Compatibility:
Given a set
{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, and a generic norm
\mathcal{R}()
on
\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, the subspace norm compatibility constant is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})=\underset{W\in{C}-\{0\}}{\sup}\frac{\mathcal{R}(W)}{||W||_{F}}.

(10)

When
d=1
, the definition reduces to the one given in
[
Negahban et al. 2012
]
. It measures the relative price paid for switching between a generic norm
\mathcal{R}()
and the usual Euclidean norm
||\cdot||_{F}
. Also, the reverse norm compatibility is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}({C})=\underset{W\in{C}-\{0\}}{\sup}\frac{||W||_{F}}{\mathcal{R}(W)}.

(11)

In particular when
{C}=\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
, we write
\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}({C})
as simply
\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
. For example, we note that
||v||\leq\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
for all
v\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)
. We further assume that
\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
is bounded above by an absolute constant (this holds in most cases of interest). For more on norms that we can choose as our penalty, see Appendix (
A
).

2.3
Some Notions on Temporal Dependence: Concentration Inequalities.

From a technical standpoint, temporal dependence factors arise while using concentration inequalities, in course of proving consistency of our penalized estimates. Our goal is to recover
optimal
consistency rates that have already been derived in the independence setting, the only difference being a dependence factor (i.e., the "price paid" for temporal dependence), which appears as a multiplicative constant in our results. Unfortunately, there is no single, master framework for temporal dependence that gives us tight concentration inequalities in every example. Hence we require different, albeit related, notions of dependence that suit specific examples. Several such notions exist, but we won’t go into all of them — for a comprehensive overview, see
[
Basu and Michailidis 2015
, Supplementary, Appendix E]
. However, these different notions of dependence generally do not imply one another, and may instead, be seen as complementing each other. In this paper, we’ll mainly focus on two notion of dependence — (a) Markov Chains and (b) "mixing" (specifically
\beta
-mixing).

Definition 2.3
.

Stationarity:
A process
\{x_{t}\}
is
strictly
stationary if for all
m,n,r\in\mathbb{N}
, the vector
(x_{m},...,x_{m+n})
has the same distribution as
(x_{m+r},...,x_{m+n+r})
. It is
weakly
(or covariance) stationary if the autocorrelations
Corr(x_{t},x_{t+l})
do not depend on
t
for all
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. For Gaussian processes, the two notions coincide. However, this fails to hold in general, e.g. heavy tailed processes.

As noted, from a technical standpoint, temporal dependence is only relevant while using concentration inequalities — as such, it is
latter
that is the main tool of interest. Since the notions of dependence require a bit of lengthy exposition, hence, to reduce clutter, this exposition itself is relegated to the Appendix, while the concentration inequalities connected to said notions, are given here.

The main tool we’ll use is a concentration inequality connected to bounded functions of stationary Markov chains, satisfying some technical requirements (see Appendix (
B
)), given by Proposition (
2.1
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f
be a bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
|f|\leq L
. Let us suppose
\{x_{t}\}
is a stationary Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying a minorization (
B.4
) and drift (
B.5
) condition. Further, let us suppose the random variables
T_{1},T_{2}
defined in the Appendix (
B
), have finite exponential moments. Then, for all
t>0
, and some absolute constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\biggl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})\bigr]\biggr|\geq Ln\mathsf{C}_{MC}t\right]\leq 2\exp[-cnt^{2}].

(12)

This result is just Hoeffding inequality
[
Vershynin 2018
, section 2.2]
,
modulo
a temporal dependence factor given by the quantity
\mathsf{C}_{MC}
, which measures the exponential tails of the random lengths
T_{1},T_{2}
of the independent “blocks” defined in the Appendix (
B
).

Next, a concentration inequality that is connected to the notion of "mixing" is given in Proposition (
2.2
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\{x_{t}\}
be a (sub)-geometrically
\beta
-mixing process in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\beta_{mix}>0
and geometric index
\gamma_{1}\in(0,2)
, and let
\gamma:=[1/\gamma_{1}+1/2]^{-1}
— note that
\gamma\in(0,1)
(the terms
\beta_{mix}
,
\gamma_{1}
are defined in Appendix (
B
)). Let
f
be a bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying the bound
|f|\leq L
. There exists a constant
\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}>0
that depends on
\beta_{mix}
and
\gamma_{1}
such that, for
n>4,t>1/n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\biggl[\biggl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})\bigr]\biggr|>Lnt\biggr]\leq n\exp[-\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{-2}\min\left\{(nt)^{\gamma},nt^{2}\right\}]

(13)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp[-\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{-2}n^{\gamma}\min\left\{1,t^{2}\right\}].

(14)

This follows from
[
Wong et al. 2020
, Lemma 13]
and the fact that any function of a mixing process is also mixing with the same mixing coefficients. This result is very close to the classical Hanson-Wright inequality
[
Vershynin 2018
, section 6.2]
, again, modulo a temporal dependence factor.

Remark 2.1
.

Proposition (
2.1
) is a clear improvement over Proposition (
2.2
), since the former holds for all
t>0
and matches Hoeffding’s inequality for bounded, independent random variables. However, to use the former, two technical conditions (
B.5
) and (
B.4
) need to be verified, which can be challenging in practice. We should mention that there remains a gap in the literature, with regard to concentration inequalities for mixing processes vs Markov chains — this is addressed in
[
Merlevède et al. 2011
]
. For more general, Bernstein bounds corresponding to Propositions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), see
[
Adamczak and Bednorz 2015
, Theorem 1.1, 1.2]
and
[
Merlevède et al. 2011
, Theorem 1]
respectively. For a detailed comparison between the generalized versions, see
[
Adamczak and Bednorz 2015
, Remark 4]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In Proposition (
2.2
), the precondition
t>1/n
is extremely mild and boils down to the sample size
n
being bigger than a finite constant (that does not scale with dimension) in applications. Also, when the geometric index
\gamma_{1}\rightarrow 2
, we have
\gamma\rightarrow 1
, and then the exponential term in the right hand side matches the Hanson-Wright bound for the independent case. However, a factor of
n
still remains on the right hand side, which is sub-optimal. However, this is
not
a big price to pay — in fact, the exponential term usually dominates.

3
Why is a Robust Procedure Necessary ?

To motivate this section, let us briefly recall the main ideas behind proving the consistency of LASSO. In low dimensions when
n\gg p
, the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator
\hat{\beta}_{OLS}
consistently estimates
\beta^{*}
in (
1
). The proof is standard — the following is shown:

•

The cross-product or deviation term
X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n
in (
2
) converges to 0 (in probability), or, in other words, concentrates around 0 "with high probability".  (Deviation Condition)

•

The sample gram matrix
X^{\top}X/n
is positive definite with high probability, i.e. its variational form
v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv/n
is lower bounded by an
unrestricted
constant
\alpha_{URE}>0
over
all

v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.  (Unrestricted Eigenvalue Condition)

In high dimensions when
p\gg n
, and the usual LASSO (with least squares loss) is used, the Deviation Condition remains the same, but, since the sample gram matrix is now singular, the Unrestricted Eigenvalue condition can’t hold. It turns out that only a slight modification is enough here — the variational form
v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv/n
is now lower bounded, only over a
restricted
set of vectors
v
living in a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, by a constant
\alpha_{RE}>0
. This is the so-called "Restricted Eigenvalue" (RE) condition.

There is a balance to be struck between proving that
both
the Deviation
and
the RE conditions hold simultaneously with high probability. Roughly speaking, it is easy to show that the deviation term
X^{\top}\mathcal{E}/n
(which is already centered) concentrates around 0 (its mean) w.h.p., when the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
and noise
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are not
too
noisy or heavy tailed (since that would worsen the concentration bound, hence the final consistency rates). By contrast, the opposite tendency holds while proving the RE condition — heavier tails of
\{x_{t}\}
implies the gram matrix, or its variational form
v^{\top}X^{\top}Xv/n
concentrates
away from
0 w.h.p. Therefore, proving both simultaneously means that the random processes can’t be too light tailed or too heavy tailed. This issue is usually resolved in the classical iid literature either by assuming
\{x_{t}\}
is deterministic (fixed design setup), or by assuming
\{x_{t}\}
is exogenous, i.e. completely independent of the errors
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
. As such, the two conditions are usually verified separately
[
Negahban et al. 2012
]
. However, in the case of time series, especially in the case of endogenous processes, e.g. VAR
s
, this is not allowed. Both the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
and errors
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are random (random design), but crucially the errors/noise
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
drive both the predictors
\{x_{t}\}
and responses
\{y_{t}\}
(endogeneity). Hence, if the errors are heavy tailed (e.g. polynomial tails/only finitely many moments exist), then so are the predictors/responses. Therefore, proving both Deviation and RE w.h.p becomes challenging.

In particular, under the LASSO with least squares loss, the Deviation condition becomes hard to verify in the presence of heavy tails. To simplify, suppose
\{x_{t}\}
are iid subgaussian and the
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are iid from an
\alpha
-stable distribution
[
Loh 2017
]
with
\alpha<2
, and that
\{x_{t}\}
is independent of
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
. Then, assuming
\beta^{*}
is
s
-sparse, the least squares LASSO with the usual penalty
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)=||\cdot||_{1}
, fails to achieve the optimal
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{n}}\right)
rate. The reason is that the Deviation condition now
fails
with high probability
[
Loh 2017
, Lemma 2]
. Hence, we can’t do without a robust procedure.

4
The Main Result.

Next, we make the following probabilistic assumptions regarding the joint process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
given in the model (
1
):

1.

Stationarity.
The joint process
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is strictly stationary.

2.

Distributional Requirement of Noise Process.
For each
t
, the noise
\epsilon_{t}
has a
conditionally symmetric
density, given the
\sigma
-field
\mathfrak{F}_{i-1}
, where
\mathfrak{F}_{i}=\sigma\{x_{i+1},x_{i},x_{i-1},\dots\}
. This density is positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
. Assuming
\mathbb{E}|\epsilon_{1}|<\infty
, it follows that the noise
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
forms a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathfrak{F}_{t}\}
.

3.

Distributional Requirement of Predictor Process
: The predictor process
\{x_{t}\}
satisfies either one of the following:

(a)

Markov Chain Requirement.
The predictor process
\{x_{t}\}
is a Markov chain satisfying a minorization (
B.4
) and drift (
B.5
) condition.

(b)

Mixing Requirement.
The predictor process
\{x_{t}\}
is (sub)geometrically
\beta
-mixing with rate
\beta_{mix}
, and geometric index
\gamma_{1}
. We define
\gamma:=\left[1/\gamma_{1}+1/2\right]^{-1}
and assume
\gamma<1
(equivalently
\gamma_{1}<2
). The terms
\beta_{mix}
and
\gamma_{1}
are defined in Appendix (
B
).

Remark 4.1
.

All our examples satisfy Assumption (
2
). From a technical standpoint, this assumption is used to verify a first-order "deviation" condition, which in turn, gives us the size of the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
. Actually, rather than the full strength of Assumption (
2
), in practice, we only require that (a) each
\epsilon_{t}
given
x_{t}
has a conditionally symmetric density, positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
and that (b) for any fixed vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the sequence of random variables

\displaystyle w^{2}(x_{t})\ell^{\prime}[\epsilon_{t}w(x_{t})]u^{\top}x_{t},

(15)

forms a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathfrak{F}_{t}\}
. This in turn allows us to exploit Azuma’s inequality (i.e. Hoeffding inequality for martingale difference sequences) in order to get a deviation bound with high probability.

Remark 4.2
.

All our examples satisfy Assumption (
3
). If our example satisfies (
3a
), then we can leverage Proposition (
2.1
), and if it satisfies (
3b
), then we can leverage Proposition (
2.2
), to obtain appropriate consistency rates. In principle, we do not assume

the existence of second or even first moments of the noise
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
; nevertheless, in many examples, finiteness of these moments are used for easy-to-verify sufficient conditions to ensure (a) strict stationarity of the corresponding predictor and noise processes, and (b) the drift condition (
B.5
), which guarantees
\beta
-mixing. Instead, we consider the tail probability of the error
\mathbb{P}[|\epsilon|>\mathsf{T}/2]
, for
\mathsf{T}>0
small enough so that
\alpha_{T}:=\underset{|u|\leq\mathsf{T}}{\min}\ell^{\prime\prime}(u)>0
. For example, we may fix
\mathsf{T}=\tau
, so that
\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}=\alpha_{\tau}=1
for the Huber loss with threshold
\tau
.

We are now ready to present the theoretical consistency results of our sparse Huber estimator (

) and (

Remark 4.3
.

The issue with using a weight function
\mathfrak{w}(\cdot)
to effectively bound the predictors
\{x_{i}\}
, is that the restricted eigenvalue
\alpha_{RE}
(which measures the “curvature” of the loss function) becomes smaller. In the case of robust regression,
\alpha_{RE}\propto\Lambda_{\min}\left[\mathbb{E}(w^{3}(x_{1})x_{1}x_{1}^{\top}\mathds{1}(|\epsilon_{1}\leq\mathsf{T}/2|)\right])\ll\Lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}(x_{1}x_{1}^{\top}))
, if
\{x_{t}\}
has finite second moment. However ,the estimation error
||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||
scales inversely as
\alpha_{RE}
. Thus, bounding or truncating the predictors too aggressively via the weight function
\mathfrak{w}(\cdot)
is not recommended. On the other hand, instead of bounding the
x^{\prime}s
, the weights could be chosen judiciously so that
\mathfrak{w}(x)x
becomes Subweibull i.e. having a finite exponential moment, instead of subgaussian/bounded. In that case our results would still go through. If we assume the predictors
\{x_{i}\}
are Subweibull to begin with, then nothing is lost by taking
\mathfrak{w}\equiv 1
.

Remark 4.4
.

The exponent

that appears in the finite sample bounds in Proposition (

) under the Mixing requirement (

(by assumption), where

(since robustification basically truncates the corresponding random processes, we cross back to the subgaussian world). These exponents appear due to applying the concentration inequality (

), and make the finite sample bounds sub-optimal. For example, suppose that

\beta
-mixing, with geometric exponent

. However, under the mixing framework, the finite sample bound takes a hit. This drawback is inherent in the concentration bound given by Proposition (

Examples.

Example 5.1
.

VAR models generated by weakly heteroskedastic error processes.

Following
[
Liebscher 2005
, section 4]
, we consider the process
\{Z_{t}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
given by a vector Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedastic (ARCH) model with 1-lag:

\displaystyle Z_{t}=B^{\top}Z_{t-1}+\Sigma(Z_{t-1})\eta_{t},\quad t=1,\dots,n

(25)

where the random vectors
\{\eta_{t}\}
are iid with symmetric continuous densities and positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(e.g., the family of continuous scale mixtures of Gaussian distribution). Suppose the noise
\{\eta_{t}\}
have finite second moment, and that the noise variance is the Identity matrix. For ease of presentation, denote
\Sigma():\mathbb{R}^{p}:\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as the conditional variance that satisfies:
||\Sigma(\cdot)||_{2}
,
||\Sigma^{-1}(\cdot)||_{2}
and
\det(\Sigma(\cdot))
is bounded on compact sets in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
In this setting, given
Z_{t-1}

Z_{t}
is conditionally distributed with mean
B^{\top}Z_{t-1}
and variance
\Sigma(Z_{t-1})\Sigma(Z_{t-1})^{\top}
. As a special case, when
\Sigma(\cdot)
is a constant, say
\Sigma

0<\Lambda_{\min}(\Sigma)\leq\Lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)<\infty
. We also assume that the spectral radius
\rho(B)=\rho(B^{\top})<1
. We examine the following two regimes:

(i)

||\Sigma(z)||_{2}=o(||z||)
(
weak
conditional
heteroskedasticity
)

(ii)

||\Sigma(z)||_{2}=\mathcal{O}(||z||)
(
strong
conditional
heteroskedasticity
).

Then, assuming
\{Z_{t}\}
is (strictly) stationary and
weakly
heteroskedastic, by
[
Liebscher 2005
, Thoerem 2]
,
\{Z_{t}\}
is geometrically
\beta
-mixing (
\gamma_{1}=1
, so that
\gamma=[1/\gamma_{1}+1/2]^{-1}=2/3
). Hence, assuming each row of
B^{\top}
is
s
-sparse and splitting the model into
p
parallel regressions, e.g. fixing a
1\leq j\leq p
, the
j^{th}
regression is then given by
y_{t}=x_{t}^{\top}\beta^{*}+\epsilon_{t}
,
1\leq t\leq n
where
y_{t}=Z_{tj}
,
x_{t}=Z_{t-1}
,
\beta^{*}=B_{j:}
and
\epsilon_{t}=\Sigma_{j}^{\top}(Z_{t-1})\eta_{t}
. From
[
Liebscher 2005
]
, it is then easy to check that
\{(x_{t},\epsilon_{t})\}
is a stationary, geometrically
\beta
-mixing sequence where
\epsilon_{t}
is conditionally symmetric given
x_{t}
(by assumption on
\eta_{t}
). Hence, we can carry out the
p
regressions in parallel under the robust, mixing framework. It is important to note that, a robust framework is essential in this case, as ARCH models are well known to have polynomial tails (only finitely many moments) (cf.
[
Wong et al. 2020
, Example 5]
). However, while the “mixing” framework applies to this case, the same drawback remains for finite sample bounds: e.g. when
\mathcal{R}
is the
\ell_{1}
penalty, ignoring dependence factors and noting that
\gamma_{1}=1
and
\gamma=2/3
, the sample size
n
scales as
\max\left\{(\log p)^{2},(s\log p)^{3/2}\right\}
, which is sub-optimal. Finally, in practice, stationarity is not guaranteed. However, since
\{Z_{t}\}
is a Markov chain in this case, we can exploit the equivalence between ergodic Markov chains and
\beta
-mixing (
[
Liebscher 2005
, Proposition 2, Theorem 2(i)]
), to simulate the chain until it is close to stationarity after a sufficient “burn-in” period (this is standard practice for Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods, for example).

Moreover, under
weak heteroskedasticity
, we can actually derive

is a geometrically ergodic Markov chain

which satisfies a “drift” and “minorization” condition (as explained in section (

(in the proof, the Euclidean norm

) instead of (

), and get the following:

.

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=2\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}},

Example 5.2
.

Univariate AR models with strongly heteroskedastic noise.

We consider a univariate ARCH(p) model given by

\displaystyle z_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_{j}z_{t-j}+\sigma(z_{t-1},z_{t-2},\dots,z_{t-p})\cdot\eta_{t},\quad t=p,p+1,p+2,\dots

\{\eta_{t}\}

\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}=1

\tilde{Z}_{t}=(z_{t},\dots,z_{t-p+1})^{\top}

\tilde{Z}_{t}=B^{\top}\tilde{Z}_{t-1}+\Sigma(\tilde{Z}_{t-1})\tilde{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle B^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots b_{p-1}&b_{p}\\
1&0&\cdots 1&0\\
0&1&\cdots 0&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots 1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Sigma(z)=\text{diag}[\sigma(z),0,\dots,0]\quad\tilde{\eta}_{t}=(\eta_{t},0,\dots,0).

\epsilon_{t}:=\sigma(x_{t})\eta_{t}

, where

\tilde{B}=BB^{\top}+\text{diag}[d_{1},\dots,d_{p}]
. Note that, under “homoskedasticity”:

\rho(B)<1
. Then, by
[
Liebscher 2005

\{\tilde{Z}_{t}\}
is Geometrically ergodic, so that

is geometrically
\beta
-mixing with geometric index

. Thus, the problem falls under our robust regression framework and Proposition (

. However, by Proposition (

), the finite sample bounds suffer by an exponent term

. To get rid of the exponents, we can exploit the fact that

is a Markov chain where a “drift” and “minorization” condition applies (from section (

)). In fact, the only change from example (

), is the choice of the drift function; the latter can be set to

and then it falls under the framework posited for example (

). Hence, we can use the tighter concentration inequality (

) instead of (

), and obtain:

Proposition 5.2
.

For the ARCH(p) model posited in (

let the sample size

and tuning parameter

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=2\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}},

(34)

\displaystyle n\succsim\max\left\{1,\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}\right\}\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}b_{M}^{2}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})}{\alpha_{RE}^{2}},

(35)

\displaystyle\alpha_{RE}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{\min}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{w}^{3}(\tilde{Z_{p}})\tilde{Z_{p}}\tilde{Z_{p}}^{\top}\mathds{1}(||\Sigma(\tilde{Z_{p}})||_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{T}/2})\mathds{1}(|\eta_{p}|\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{T}/2})\right]\right],

(36)

wherein

\displaystyle||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}]\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}^{2}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},

(38)

with probability at least

Threshold VAR models.

Univariate threshold autoregressive models belongs to the class of nonlinear time series models, first proposed by Tong (1978). The major features of this class of models are limit cycles, amplitude dependent frequencies, and jump phenomena

Example 5.5

Random coefficient VAR models.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we consider the theoretical properties of
penalized estimates in high-dimensional time series models when the data are generated from a multivariate stationary process, under structured sparsity constraints. We subsequently relaxed the assumption of Gaussian tails and replaced it with heavier tails, and thereby considered a robust approach. However, the robust approach introduces potential efficiency issues due to aggressive scaling by weight functions. Without the weight functions the finite sample bound will take a huge hit, even though we can recover optimal consistency rates. In case of heavy tails, we adopted the “mixing” framework to quantify temporal dependence. However this results in sub-optimal finite sample bounds, even though we recover optimal consistency rates This may be unavoidable based on the cleanest results (as far as we know) on concentration inequalities involving heavy tailed distributions under temporal dependence. It would be interesting to derive tight Bernstein or Hoeffding inequalities for mixing processes — the one we used (Proposition (
2.2
)) is the best we are aware of. However, in some cases, we showed how to re-write the time series as Markov chains and get optimal consistency rates
and
optimal finite sample bounds. Still, there may be interesting examples of mixing processes that cannot be written in simple Markov form, or even if that were possible, it may be difficult to verify the “drift” and “minorization” conditions required to apply the tighter concentration inequality (
2.1
) (e.g. multivariate GARCH models). Finally, one potential future question which we have omitted here, is (non-asymptotic) inference (based on one-step or de-sparsified estimators) for high dimensional time series under general sparsity.

Definition A.1
.

The dual of a norm
\mathcal{R}()

\mathcal{R}^{*}(z):=\sup\{w^{\top}z,\quad\mathcal{R}(w)\leq 1\}
. It can be shown that
\mathcal{R}^{*}()

Definition A.2
.

Decomposable Norms:
A norm

is called

, for all

, we have
\mathcal{R}(v)=\mathcal{R}(v_{S})+\mathcal{R}(v_{S^{c}})
.

Definition A.3
.

Atomic Norms: Since many interesting norms are

decomposable, the notion of atomic norms is a useful generalization. Following
[
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
]
, let
\mathcal{A}
be a collection of

\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Suppose no element

lies in the convex hull of the other elements

(45)

The usual practice is to define the mixing coefficients for lags

B.2
Markov Chains and Their Connection to Mixing.

It is a common strategy to prove mixing properties of time series by first rewriting them as Markov chains, then exploiting the equivalence between ergodic Markov chains and mixing. One equivalence is the following: any stationary Markov chain is geometrically ergodic (defined below),
iff
it is geometrically
\beta
-mixing (see
[
Bradley 2005
, Section 3]
for more). With that said, let
\{x_{t}\}
(starting from an initial time point
t=0
), be a discrete time homogeneous Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with Markov transition kernel
\mathbb{P}(x,A)=\mathbb{P}[x_{t}\in A\mid x_{t-1}=x]
(which does not depend on
t
by homogeneity). The “
n
-step” transition probability is denoted by
\mathbb{P}^{n}(x,A)=\mathbb{P}[x_{t+n}\in A\mid x_{t-1}=x]
. Then we have the following:

Definition B.3
.

The Markov chain
\{x_{t}\}
is called “geometrically ergodic” if, starting from some initial point
x_{0}=x
, it converges to a stationary distribution
\pi
at a geometric rate
\rho<1
. Specifically, for some function
M:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\underset{A}{\sup}|\mathbb{P}^{n}(x,A)-\pi(A)|\leq M(x)\rho^{n},\quad n=1,2,3,\dots

(50)

Geometric ergodicity is intimately connected to the notion of
\beta
-mixing— a stationary Markov chain is geometrically ergodic
iff
, it is geometrically
\beta
-mixing
[
Liebscher 2005
, Proposition 2]
. Two important, classical tools used to establish geometric ergodicity are the so called “drift” and ‘minorization” conditions. We define the concept of “drift” and “minorization” as which is now well known in literature
[
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
]
.

Definition B.4
.

Minorization condition.

The Markov transition kernel
\mathbb{P}(x,A)
is said to be minorized by a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, if there is a Borel set
{C}_{small}
(called a “small” set in literature) such that, for some
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x,A)\geq\delta\nu(A),\quad\text{for all x}\in{C}_{small},\quad\text{and Borel sets A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x,{C}_{small})>0\quad\text{for all x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(52)

One can show that in such a situation, if the chain is stationary, with an invariant/stationary measure
\pi
, then this measure is unique and satisfies
\pi({C}_{small})>0

[
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
]
.

Definition B.5
.

Drift condition.

The Markov chain
\{x_{t}\}
satisfies the ”drift”
condition, i.e. if there is a measurable function
V:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
, together with a constant
\lambda<1
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t})\mid x_{t-1}=x]\leq\lambda V(x)\quad\forall x\notin{C}_{small},

(53)

\displaystyle\underset{x\in{C}_{small}}{\sup}\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t})\mid x_{t-1}=x]<\infty.

(54)

The above expectation is taken with respect to the Markov transition kernel
\mathbb{P}(x,A)
. We can think of the “drift” function
V
as a “potential energy” surface. If the “drift” condition holds, the chain tends to“drift” toward states of “lower energy” in expectation. The function
V
is also called the “Lyanpunov” function in some texts. In examples we will see, the drift function is usually taken to be
V(x)=1+||x||^{s}
for some real
s>0
(the Euclidean norm
||\cdot||
may be replaced by an equivalent norm if necessary). Usually
s=1,2
depending on a finite first or second moment assumption. Also, the “small” set
{C}_{small}
is usually compact, so the second condition follows whenever the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[V(x_{t})|x_{t-1}=x]
is continuous (again, this usually holds in examples).

Minorization is used to build a “split chain”
\{(\tilde{x}_{t},R_{t})\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{0,1\}
satisfying the following properties:

(a)
\{\tilde{x}_{t}\}
is again a Markov chain with transition kernel
\mathbb{P}
(hence for our purposes of estimating the tail probabilities we may identify
\{x_{t}\}
with
\{\tilde{x}_{t}\}
).

(b) If we define
T_{1}:=\inf\{n>0:R_{n}=1\}
,

T_{i+1}:=\inf\{n>0:R_{T_{1}+\dots+T_{i+n}}=1\}
,
then
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
, form a well defined, independent random sequence.

(c) If we define
S_{i}:=T_{1}+\dots+T_{i}
, then the “blocks”
y_{0}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{T_{1}})
,
y_{i}:=(x_{S_{i+1}},\dots,x_{S_{i+1}})
,
i>0
, form an independent sequence. Thus, for any bounded function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the random variables
z_{i}(f):=\sum_{j=S_{i+1}}^{S_{i}+1}f(x_{i})
,
i\geq 1
, form an iid sequence.

This construction is now well known in literature
[
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
]
. For our purposes, we do not need details of this construction in its full generality. We only require that the the sequence
T_{1},T_{2},\dots
(the random lengths of the independent “blocks”) have a finite exponential moment, i.e. a moment generating function which exists finitely at least at some point. In other words, we suppose that the Subweibull norms of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are finite, i.e.
||T_{1}||_{\psi_{1}},||T_{2}||_{\psi_{1}}<\mathsf{C}_{MC}<\infty
(for a definition of Subweibull norms, see
[
Wong et al. 2020
, section 4]
). This assumption follows from the drift condition (see
[
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
, Theorem 15.0.2]
and
[
Adamczak et al. 2008
, section 3.5]
). Hence, for studying time series as Markov chains, our main goal will be to verify the drift and minorization conditions. If these conditions are satisfied, we can use the construction of the “split” chain and the iid “blocks” and get the following concentration inequality for bounded functions of geometrically ergodic Markov chains
[
Adamczak et al. 2008
, Theorem 8]
:

Appendix C
Proofs of propositions.

C.1
Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Here, we closely follow the proof technique in
[
Loh 2017
,
Loh 2018
]
. We first minimize over a local region
\{\beta:||\beta-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}\}
, i.e. we have the following (constrained) optimization problem:

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}\in\underset{||\beta-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}}{\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta)+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\beta)\right\}.

(55)

We will show that this solution
\tilde{\beta}
lies in the interior of the constraint set, hence agree with the global optima
\hat{\beta}
of the unconstrained problem. We will prove the "algebraic" and "probabilistic" parts separately. In other words, we will assume a "deviation" and a "Restricted Eigenvalue" or "RE" condition and prove the error bounds in a deterministic fashion. Then, we will show that the "deviation" and "RE" conditions hold with high probability.

Step 1.
Since
\tilde{\beta}
is optimal and
\beta^{*}
is feasible, the basic inequality follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(\tilde{\beta})+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\beta})\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})

(56)

Hence, by convexity of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}(\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(\tilde{\beta})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\beta})].

(57)

Next, by Holder’s inequality

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(\tilde{\beta})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}(\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*})

(58)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\beta})]-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}(\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*})

(59)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\beta})]+\mathcal{R}^{*}[\nabla_{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})]\mathcal{R}(\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}).

(60)

Step 2.
Assume a deviation condition. : Here we suppose the following bound holds with high probability:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{*}[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})]\leq\lambda_{n}/2.

(61)

Then, denoting the estimate error vector by
v=\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*}+v)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}v\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)]+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}\mathcal{R}(v).

(62)

This means
v\in{C}={C}(\beta^{*})=\mathrm{cone}\{u:\mathcal{R}(u)/2+\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)\geq 0\}
. Also,
\tilde{\beta}
lies in the feasible region, so
||v||=||\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\mathsf{T}/2b_{M}
.

Step 3.
Assume an RE condition. Here we suppose a (local) RE condition holds: there exists
\alpha=\alpha_{RE}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*}+u)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}u\geq\alpha_{RE}||u||^{2}

(63)

for every
u\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})\cap{C}
where
\mathbb{B}_{2}(r^{\prime})
is the Euclidean ball of radius
r^{\prime}
around the origin. Hence,
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})\cap{C}
, and

\displaystyle\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}[\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*})-\mathcal{R}(\beta^{*}+v)]+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}\mathcal{R}(v)\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\mathcal{R}(v)}{2}.

(64)

where the last inequality is due to the triangle inequality. This finally gives

\displaystyle||v||=||\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}}.

(65)

Combined with feasibility of
\tilde{\beta}
, we have, thus,

\displaystyle||\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\min\left\{\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}\right\}.

(66)

We will prove shortly that the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
scales as
\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}
. Thus when
n\rightarrow\infty
or
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
, i.e., for
n
large enough, or equivalently for
\lambda_{n}
small enough, we see from the last inequality that the minimum of the two terms in the right hand side, just reduces to the first term:

\displaystyle||\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}}.

(67)

Now we verify that the deviation and RE conditions hold with high probability, and hence, obtain lower bounds on the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
and sample size
n
. To this end, we will use the concentration inequalities (
2.1
) or (
2.2
), according to whether we are following the Markovian regime (
3a
) or the Mixing regime (
3b
), respectively.

Verifying the Deviation and RE condition under the Mixing Regime.

We verify the deviation condition using the following steps.

Step 1.
Write the deviation term as an average of a martingale difference sequence.

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})=-\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{\prime}\left[\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})\right]w^{2}(x_{i})x_{i}.

(68)

Fixing a vector
u
so that
\mathcal{R}(u)\leq 1
(which means
||u||\leq\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}
), we have that the random variables

\displaystyle w^{2}(x_{i})\ell^{\prime}(\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i}))u^{\top}x_{i}:\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(69)

are stationary, each satisfying the bound

\displaystyle|w^{2}(x_{i})\ell^{\prime}(\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i}))u^{\top}x_{i}|\leq 1\cdot\tau\cdot\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}||w(x_{i})x_{i}||\leq\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau.

(70)

Moreover, by Assumption (
2
), they form a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathfrak{F}_{t}\}
, where
\mathfrak{F}_{t}=\sigma\{x_{t+1},x_{t},x_{t-1},\cdots\}
, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell^{\prime}[\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})]w^{2}(x_{i})x_{i}\mid\mathfrak{F}_{i-1}]

(71)

\displaystyle=w^{2}(x_{i})x_{i}\mathbb{E}[\ell^{\prime}[\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})]\mid\mathfrak{F}_{i-1}]=0,

(72)

using the assumptions that
\ell
is an even function (so
\ell^{\prime}
is an odd or symmetric function, and bounded by
\tau
) and
\epsilon_{i}
is conditionally symmetric given
\mathfrak{F}_{i-1}
. Hence, by Azuma’s inequality, for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|u^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})|>t]\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{2nt^{2}}{(\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau)^{2}}\right\}.

(73)

This leads to the deviation bound, for some absolute constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{R}^{*}[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})]>2\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}\right]\leq\exp[-w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]].

(74)

Step 2.
Choose an appropriate value of the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
. Hence, to satisfy the deviation condition, we can take

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=2\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}}.

(75)

We verify the RE condition in the following steps.

Step 1.
Start with the Taylor remainder

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\beta,\beta^{*}):=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}(\beta-\beta^{*})

(76)

over the set
\{||\beta-\beta^{*}||\leq\mathsf{T}/2b_{M}\}\cap{C}
. We will show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\beta,\beta^{*})\geq\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{3}(x_{I})(x_{i}^{\top}(\beta-\beta^{*}))^{2}\mathds{1}(A_{i})

(77)

where the sets
A_{i}:=\{|\epsilon_{i}|\leq\mathsf{T}/2\}
. First, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\beta,\beta^{*})=\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x_{i})\left\{\ell\left[(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)w(x_{i})\right]-\ell\left[\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})\right]-\ell^{\prime}\left[\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})\right]w(x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\beta)\right\}.

(78)

Fix an
1\leq i\leq n
, and let
u_{1i}=(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)w(x_{i})
and
u_{2i}=\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})
. Then
u_{1i}-u_{2i}=w(x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}(\beta^{*}-\beta)
the
i^{th}
term of the last sum is just
w(x_{i})
times
\ell(u_{1i})-\ell(u_{2i})-\ell^{\prime}(u_{2i})(u_{1i}-u_{2i})
, which is non-negative, since
\ell
is convex. Next,
|u_{1i}|,|u_{2i}|\leq\mathsf{T}
on the event
A_{i}=\{|\epsilon_{i}|\leq\mathsf{T}/2\}
.
This is because

\displaystyle|u_{2i}|=|\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})|\leq|\epsilon_{i}|\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2},

(79)

which means

\displaystyle|u_{1i}|=|(y_{i}-x_{i}^{\top}\beta)w(x_{i})|\leq|\epsilon_{i}w(x_{i})|+|w(x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}(\beta-\beta^{*})|

(80)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2}+||w(x_{i})x_{i}||||\beta-\beta^{*}||

(81)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2}+b_{M}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}\leq\mathsf{T}.

(82)

Further, for
|u_{1i}|,|u_{2i}|\leq\mathsf{T}
, we have,

\displaystyle\ell(u_{1i})-\ell(u_{2i})-\ell^{\prime}(u_{2i})(u_{1i}-u_{2i})=\ell^{\prime\prime}(u_{i}^{*})(u_{1i}-u_{2i})^{2}

(83)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}(u_{1i}-u_{2i})^{2},

(84)

where
\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}=\underset{|u|\leq\mathsf{T}}{\min}\ell^{\prime\prime}(u)
. Thus, the claim follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\beta,\beta^{*})\geq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(w(x_{i})x_{i}^{\top}(\beta-\beta^{*})\right]^{2}w(x_{i})\mathds{1}(A_{i}).

(85)

Step 2.
Lower bound the Taylor remainder by a quadratic form of (a scaled version of) the sample gram matrix
X^{\top}X/n
. Rewriting
v=\beta-\beta^{*}
and
\mathcal{T}(\beta,\beta^{*})=\mathcal{T}(v)
, we have, for
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})\cap{C}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(v)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*}+v)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}v\geq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[x_{i}^{\top}v\right]^{2}w^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(A_{i})=v^{\top}\Gamma v,

(86)

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(A_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top},

(87)

where
\Gamma
is a scaled version of the sample Gram matrix
X^{\top}X/n
.

Step 3.
Apply Proposition (
2.2
) under Assumption (
3b
). Now, fix a vector
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})\cap{C}
. Since,

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\left[x_{i}^{\top}v\right]^{2}w^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(A_{i})|\leq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot 1\cdot||w(x_{i})x_{i}||^{2}||v||^{2}\leq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot b_{M}^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}\leq\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2}/4=K_{0},\quad\text{say},

(88)

therefore, the term
v^{\top}\Gamma v
is an average of bounded, stationary, geometrically mixing random variables (with geometric index
\gamma_{1}
) so that, by Proposition (
2.2
), for
n\geq 4,t>1/n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|v^{\top}\Gamma v-\mathbb{E}(v^{\top}\Gamma v)|>K_{0}t]\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{2}}\min\left\{(nt)^{\gamma},nt^{2}\right\}\right]

(89)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{2}}n^{\gamma}\min[1,t^{2}]\right]

(90)

Step 4.
Discretize the spherical cap
{C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})
and take union bounds.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in{C},||v||=\mathsf{T}/2b_{M}}{\sup}|v^{\top}\Gamma v-\mathbb{E}(v^{\top}\Gamma v)|>2K_{0}t\right]

(91)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M})\cap{C}}{\sup}|v^{\top}\Gamma v-\mathbb{E}(v^{\top}\Gamma v)|>2K_{0}t\right]

(92)

\displaystyle\leq n\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{2}}n^{\gamma}\min(1,t^{2})+c^{\prime}\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}\cap{C})\right],

(93)

where
c^{\prime}>0
is an absolute constant (Gaussian width respects scaling).

Step 5.
Choose an appropriate finite sample bound. We choose
n
,
t
so that

\displaystyle t=\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{16b_{M}^{2}K_{0}}\Lambda_{\min}[\mathbb{E}(\Gamma)]=\frac{1}{4b_{M}^{2}\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}}\Lambda_{\min}[\mathbb{E}(\Gamma)],

(94)

\displaystyle n\geq n_{RE}:=\left[\mathsf{C}_{\mathrm{Mix}}^{2}(c^{\prime}+1)\max(1,t^{-2})\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}\cap{C})\right]^{1/\gamma}.

(95)

Note that
\mathbb{E}(\Gamma)=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[w^{3}(x_{1})x_{1}x_{1}^{\top}\mathds{1}(|\epsilon_{1}|\leq\mathsf{T}/2)\right]
. We take
\alpha_{RE}=\Lambda_{\min}[\mathbb{E}(\Gamma)]/2
and assume
\alpha_{RE}>0
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(v)=\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*}+v)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{n}(\beta^{*})^{\top}v\geq v^{\top}\Gamma v\geq\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2},\quad\forall v\in{C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}(\mathsf{T}/2b_{M}),

(96)

with probability at least
1-n\exp\left(\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}\cap{C})\right)
. Also, the choice of
t
is a constant in this case, so the precondition
t\geq 1/n
is easily satisfied for moderate values of
n
.

Putting the pieces together, we have, for

\displaystyle n\geq n_{RE},\quad\lambda_{n}=2\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{cw^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}},

(97)

with probability at least
1-n\exp(-w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)])-n\exp\left(\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}\cap{C})\right)
,

\displaystyle||\tilde{\beta}-\beta^{*}||_{2}\leq\min\left\{\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}\right\}=\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},

(98)

since
\lambda_{n}
scales as
\sqrt{w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]/n}
, so the rate
w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]=o(n)
ensures the last equality. This implies that
\tilde{\beta}
lies strictly in the interior of the local ball
\{\beta:||\beta-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}\}
which means that
\tilde{\beta}
is actually a global minimizer
\hat{\beta}
of the unconstrained problem. Furthermore, any optima of the unconstrained problem must also lie in the interior of the constraint set. This ends the proof.

Verifying the Deviation and RE condition under Markovian Regime.

(109)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathds{1}(A_{i})\geq\mathds{1}(B_{i})\mathds{1}(C_{i}),

(110)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad B_{i}:=\{||\Sigma(Z_{i-1})||_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{T}/2}\},\quad C_{i}=\{||\eta_{i}||\leq\sqrt{\mathsf{T}/2}\}.

(111)

So we have that, for a fixed
v\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}})\cap{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(v)\geq v^{\top}\Gamma v\geq v^{\top}\Gamma_{1}v,

(112)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}:=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{w}^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(B_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\mathds{1}(C_{i}).

(113)

Now,

\displaystyle v^{\top}[\Gamma_{1}-\mathbb{E}(\Gamma_{1})]v=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{w}^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(B_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v)^{2}\left\{\mathds{1}(C_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}(C_{i})]\right\}

(114)

\displaystyle+\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\frac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}(C_{i})]\left\{\mathfrak{w}^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(B_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v)^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{w}^{3}(x_{i})\mathds{1}(B_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v)^{2}\right]\right\}

(115)

\displaystyle:=I+II.

(116)

Here we crucially exploit the fact that
\eta_{i}
is independent of
x_{i}=Z_{i-1}
for each
i
. To bound
I
, we note that it is a sum of terms that form a Martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\mathfrak{F}_{i}=\{x_{i+1},x_{i},x_{i-1},\dots\}
. Moreover each term is bounded by
2\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}b_{M}^{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}/4b_{M}^{2}n
=
\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2}/2n
. Hence we use Azuma’s inequality. To bound
II
, we note that it is also a sum of bounded functions of a Markov chain, each term satisfying the same bound
\mathsf{T}^{2}/2n
. So we use Proposition (
2.1
). First,
\{x_{t}\}=\{Z_{t-1}\}
is a geometrically ergodic Markov chain in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, by
[
Liebscher 2005
, Theorem 2(i)]
. Second, we need a drift and minorization condition for
\{Z_{t}\}
.

For now, let
||B||_{2}<1
, which is stricter than assuming the spectral radius
\rho(B)<1
. We first verify the drift condition with drift function
V(z)=1+||z||
. By assumption, there is an
R>1
such that, for
||z||>R>1
(or
V(z)>R+1>2
), we have
||\Sigma(z)||_{2}\mathbb{E}[||\eta_{0}||\leq(1-||B||_{2})||z||/2
. Let
{C}_{small}=\{z:V(z)\leq R\}
. Then for any
z\notin{C}_{small}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{t+1})\mid Z_{t}=z]

\displaystyle\leq 1+||B||_{2}||z||+||\Sigma(z)||_{2}\mathbb{E}[||\eta_{0}||]

(117)

\displaystyle=||B||_{2}V(z)+1-||B||_{2}+||\Sigma(z)||_{2}\mathbb{E}[||\eta_{0}||]

(118)

\displaystyle\leq||B||_{2}V(z)+1-||B||_{2}+\frac{1-||B||_{2}}{2}||z||

(119)

\displaystyle=||B||_{2}V(z)+1-||B||_{2}+\frac{1-||B||_{2}}{2}(V(z)-1)

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+||B||_{2}}{2}V(z)+\frac{1-||B||_{2}}{2}

(121)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+||B||_{2}}{2}V(z)+\frac{1-||B||_{2}}{2}\frac{V(z)}{2}

(122)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{3+||B||_{2}}{4}}_{<1}V(z).

(123)

Similarly it is easy to see that, by assumption,
\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{t+1})\mid Z_{t}=z]
, as a function of
z
, is bounded on the compact set
{C}_{small}
. For the minorization condition, note that it is enough to verify that the Markov transition density is minorized by a density on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(equivalently a positive, Lebesgue integrable function that can be normalized to a density) over the small set
{C}_{small}
. The Markov transition density is given by

\displaystyle k(t|z)=f_{\eta}\left[\Sigma^{-1}(z)(t-B^{\top}z)\right]\det(\Sigma(z))^{-1},\quad\forall t,z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(124)

Here
f_{\eta}
stands for the joint density of the iid random vectors
\{\eta_{t}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. By assumption,
f_{\eta}
is continuous and positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which means
k(\cdot|z)
is also continuous and positive everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Fixing
t\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and taking an infimum over
z\in{C}_{small}
, we have, by assumptions on
\Sigma(\cdot)
, and compactness of
{C}_{small}
,

\displaystyle k(t|z)\geq\left\{\underset{z\in{C}_{small}}{\sup}\det[\Sigma(z)]\right\}^{-1}\left(\underset{z\in{C}_{small}}{\inf}f_{\eta}\left[\Sigma^{-1}(z)(t-B^{\top}z)\right]\right):=\delta\tilde{k}(t),

(125)

where
\tilde{k}
is a positive density on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\delta>0
is a (proportionality) constant. This gives, for any Borel set
A\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
z\in{C}_{small}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[z,A]=\int_{A}k(t|z)\,dt\geq\delta\int_{A}\tilde{k}(t)\,dt:=\delta\nu(A),

(126)

where
\nu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with density
\tilde{k}
. Also,
\mathbb{P}[z,{C}_{small}]>0
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Hence, we have shown that a drift and minorization condition holds with the assumption
||B||_{2}<1
. In the weaker case when the spectral radius
\rho(B)<1
, we can still guarantee the existence of a matrix norm
||\cdot||^{\diamond}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
(depending on B), induced by a vector norm on

\mathbb{R}^{p}
, such that
\rho(B)\leq||B||^{\diamond}\leq\rho(B)+\frac{1-\rho(B)}{2}=\frac{1+\rho(B)}{2}<1
(e.g.
[
Horn and Johnson 2012
, p347,348]
). Finally exploiting the fact that all induced matrix norms on finite dimensional spaces are equivalent, we can replace the spectral norm
||\cdot||_{2}
by
||\cdot||^{\diamond}
and do the same calculations as before.

We can now finally apply Proposition (
2.1
) to the second term
II
and overall, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|v^{\top}[\Gamma_{1}-\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}]]v|\geq t]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[|I|\geq t/2]+\mathbb{P}[|II|\geq t/2]

(127)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-\frac{nt^{2}}{2(\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2}/2)^{2}}\right]+2\exp\left[-\frac{cnt^{2}}{(\mathsf{C}_{MC}\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2})^{2}}\right]

(128)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}}\right\}\frac{nt^{2}}{\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}\mathsf{T}^{4}}\right],

(129)

where
c>0
is an absolute constant. Thus, we have as before, denoting
K_{0}=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\underset{v\in{C},||v||=\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}}}{\sup}|v^{\top}[\Gamma_{1}-\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}]]v|>2K_{0}t\right]

(130)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}}\right\}\cdot nt^{2}+c^{\prime}\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}(\mathbb{B}_{2}\cap{C})\right].

(131)

We choose
t
such that

\displaystyle 2K_{0}t=\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}])||v||^{2}

(132)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow t=\frac{1}{16\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}b_{M}^{2}}\Lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}])\quad\text{since}\quad||v||=\mathsf{T}/2b_{M},K_{0}=\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}\mathsf{T}^{2}.

(133)

With this value of
t
, we choose the sample size
n
such that

\displaystyle n\geq\frac{c^{\prime}+1}{c}\max\left\{1,\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}\right\}\cdot t^{-2}\cdot\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})

(134)

\displaystyle=\frac{64(c^{\prime}+1)}{c}\max\left\{1,\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}\right\}\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}b_{M}^{2}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})}{\Lambda_{\min}^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}])}.

(135)

Also let the restricted eigenvalue
\alpha_{RE}=\Lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}])/2
. Then, we have that the RE condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(v)\geq\alpha_{RE}||v||^{2}\quad\forall\,v\in{C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2}(\frac{\mathsf{T}}{2b_{M}})

(136)

holds, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left\{-\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right\}
.

Thus, when the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
and sample size
n

satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\asymp\overline{\Phi}_{\mathcal{R}}b_{M}\tau\sqrt{\frac{w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]}{n}},

(137)

\displaystyle n\succsim\max\left\{1,\mathsf{C}_{MC}^{2}\right\}\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{T}}^{2}b_{M}^{2}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})}{\Lambda_{\min}^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{1}])},

(138)

we have, arguing as before,

\displaystyle||\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}||\leq\frac{3\lambda_{n}\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}({C})}{2\alpha_{RE}},

(139)

with probability at least
1-2\exp[-w^{2}[\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{R}}(0,1)]]-2\exp\left[-\frac{\mathsf{T}^{2}}{4b_{M}^{2}}w^{2}({C}\cap\mathbb{B}_{2})\right]
.

C.3
Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

It is enough to show that the drift condition holds in this case. In fact, we will consider the more general case from example (
5.3
), that is, the process
\{Z_{t}\}
with conditional variance
\Sigma(z)=\text{diag}[(f_{1}+z^{\top}F_{1}z)^{1/2},\dots,(f_{p}+z^{\top}F_{p}z)^{1/2}]
, where
F_{1},\dots,F_{p}
are non-negative definite matrices and
f_{1},\dots,f_{p}>0
. We choose the drift function
V(z)=1+||z||^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{t+1})|Z_{t}=z]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\mathbb{E}||B^{\top}z+\Sigma(z)\eta_{0}||^{2}

(140)

\displaystyle\leq 1+z^{\top}BB^{\top}z+||\Sigma(z)||_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}||\eta_{0}||_{2}^{2}

(141)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\rho^{2}(B)||z||^{2}+\left(\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}f_{j}+\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}\rho(F_{j})||z||^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}||\eta_{0}||^{2}

(142)

\displaystyle=1+\left(\rho^{2}(B)+\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}\rho(F_{j})\right)\left(V(z)-1\right)+\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}f_{j}\mathbb{E}||\eta_{0}||^{2}

(143)

\displaystyle:=\lambda V(z)+K,

(144)

where
\lambda=\rho^{2}(B)+\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}\rho(F_{j})<1
by assumption and
K=1-\lambda+\underset{1\leq j\leq p}{\max}f_{j}\mathbb{E}||\eta_{0}||^{2}\geq 0
. Thus, defining the (compact) set
{C}_{small}=\{z:V(z)\leq\frac{2K}{1-\lambda}\}
, we get that, for
z\notin{C}_{small}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(Z_{t+1})|Z_{t}=z]\leq\frac{1+\lambda}{2}V(z),

(145)

where
0<\frac{1+\lambda}{2}<1
.
