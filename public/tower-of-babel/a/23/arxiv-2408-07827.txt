Twisted Hilbert spaces defined by bi-Lipschitz maps
Willian Corrêa, Sheldon Dantas, Daniel L. Rodríguez-Vidanes

Abstract
. We obtain an infinite-dimensional cone of singular twisted Hilbert spaces Z ⁡ ( φ ) Z(\varphi) which are isomorphic to their duals but not to their conjugate duals. We do that by showing that the subset of all bi-Lipschitz maps from [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty) to ℝ \mathbb{R} is coneable. We also provide a characterization of the Kalton-Peck space among all twisted Hilbert spaces of the form Z ⁡ ( φ ) Z(\varphi) , which gives a partial answer to a conjecture of F. Cabello Sánchez and J. Castillo.

46B70, 46B87, 46M18

1.
Introduction

The Kalton-Peck space is a remarkable example of a Banach space with exotic properties. It was the second example of a nontrivial (separable) twisted Hilbert space, i.e., a Banach space
X
containing an isomorphic copy of
\ell_{2}
such that
X/\ell_{2}
is also isomorphic to
\ell_{2}
and
X
non-Hilbertian. The first example was given by P. Enflo, J. Lindenstrauss and G. Pisier in
[
38
]
and we will denote it by
ELP
, for short. The construction of
ELP
is local in nature, in the sense that it is of the form
\ell_{2}(F_{n})
, where each
F_{n}
is a finite dimensional Banach space that are less and less Hilbertian. One of the consequences of
ELP
’s local nature is that, even though it is a nontrivial twisted Hilbert space,
ELP
is full of complemented copies of
\ell_{2}
(
[
25
, Section 4]
, or
[
11
, Section 10.9]
; see also
[
14
]
for a relevant reference to the general theory and open problems). On the other hand, the quotient map
q:Z_{2}\rightarrow\ell_{2}
is strictly singular and
Z_{2}^{*}\cong Z_{2}
, and therefore, the inclusion
i:\ell_{2}\rightarrow Z_{2}
is strictly cosingular. The space
Z_{2}
is then an extreme example of a twisted Hilbert space.

More generally, a
twisted sum
of Banach spaces
Y
and
Z
is a Banach space
X
containing an isomorphic copy of
Y
such that the corresponding quotient is isomorphic to
Z
. We represent this by using a short exact sequence

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{Z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

The twisted sum
X
is said to be
nontrivial
if the copy of
Y
is not complemented in
X
. If
q
is strictly singular, then
X
is said to be a
singular twisted sum
.

A great deal of work has been done in trying to determine the singularity of twisted sums (see, for instance,
[
10
,
19
,
25
,
27
,
39
,
9
,
12
]
). The present paper is a contribution to the family of examples of singular twisted Hilbert spaces. Actually, the spaces we present will have the following stronger property: let
\ell_{\Phi}
be the Orlicz sequence space corresponding to a convex function which is equivalent to
t^{2}\log^{2}t
near
0
. Then any normalized basic sequence in
Z_{2}
has a subsequence equivalent to the canonical basis of either
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{\Phi}
. We call that property
\Phi
-stability

In
[
35
]
, Kalton and Peck show that, given an unbounded Lipschitz map
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, one can obtain a twisted Hilbert space
Z(\varphi)
and the Kalton-Peck space
Z_{2}
corresponds to the choice
\varphi(t)=ct
for any
c\neq 0
. Showing that there is an infinite-dimensional cone of bi-Lipschitz functions from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathbb{R}
, we obtain an “infinite-dimensional cone” of singular twisted Hilbert spaces all of which are
\Phi
-stable. We conclude the paper by providing a characterization of the Kalton-Peck space among all spaces
Z(\varphi)
similar to the the characterization of
c_{0}
and
\ell_{p}
(
1\leq p<\infty
) as the only Banach spaces with a basis which is equivalent to all of its normalized block bases. This partially answers a conjecture by Cabello Sánchez and Castillo in
[
11
, page 517]
.

2.
Twisted sums induced by Lipschitz maps

Let
\mathbb{K}
be either the field of real or complex numbers. Recall that a
quasi-norm
on a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space
X
is a function
\|\cdot\|:X\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\|x\|=0\iff x=0
,

(2)

\|\lambda x\|=\lvert\lambda\rvert\|x\|
for every
x\in X
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
and

(3)

there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that for every
x,y\in X
, we have
\|x+y\|\leq C(\|x\|+\|y\|)
.

The space
X
endowed with a quasi-norm is called a
quasi-normed space
. The Aoki-Rolewicz theorem says that there are
p\in(0,1]
and an equivalent quasi-norm
|||\cdot|||
on
X
such that
d(x,y)=|||x-y|||^{p}
defines a distance on
X
. If that distance is complete, we say that
X
is a
quasi-Banach space
and that the original quasi-norm is complete. A twisted sum of
Y
and
Z
is a quasi-normed space
X
such that it contains an isomorphic copy of
Y
and the respective quotient is isomorphic to
Z
. It is possible to prove that
X
is a Banach space. If
i(Y)\subset X
is not complemented, we say that
X
is a
nontrivial twisted sum
. If
Y=Z=\ell_{2}
, then we say that
X
is a (separable)
twisted Hilbert space
. If
i
is strictly cosingular, we say that
X
is a
cosingular twisted sum
. If
q
is strictly singular, then
X
is said to be a
singular twisted sum
.

In
[
35
]
, Kalton and Peck provide a whole factory of twisted Hilbert spaces by making use of Lipschitz maps. If
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
is a Lipschitz map with
\varphi(0)=0
, we define
\Omega_{\varphi}:c_{00}\rightarrow\ell_{2}
by

\Omega_{\varphi}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}\varphi\left(-\log\frac{\lvert x_{n}\rvert}{\|x\|_{2}}\right)e_{n}

where
(e_{n})
is the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}
, with the understanding that
\Omega_{\varphi}(0)=0
. In this case, we have that
\Omega_{\varphi}
is a
quasi-linear map
. This means that it is homogeneous and there is
C>0
such that

\|\Omega_{\varphi}(x+y)-\Omega_{\varphi}(x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(y)\|_{2}\leq C(\|x\|_{2}+\|y\|_{2}).

This implies that if we define
\|\cdot\|_{\varphi}:\ell_{2}\times c_{00}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

\|(y,x)\|_{\varphi}:=\|y-\Omega_{\varphi}(x)\|_{2}+\|x\|_{2}

then we have that
\|\cdot\|_{\varphi}
is a quasi-norm. If we denote by
Z(\varphi)
the completion of
(\ell_{2}\times c_{00},\|\cdot\|_{\varphi})
, then we obtain a short exact sequence

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{Z(\varphi)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

where
i(y)=(y,0)
and
q(y,x)=x
. If
\varphi
is unbounded, then
Z(\varphi)
is a nontrivial twisted Hilbert space, i.e.,
i(\ell_{2})
is not complemented in
Z(\varphi)
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two twisted sums of
Y
and
Z
. We say that
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are
equivalent
if there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T}
\textstyle{Z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

where
T
is an isomorphism. The exact sequences at
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are said to be
projectively equivalent
if there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha Id_{Y}}
\textstyle{X_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T}
\textstyle{Z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta Id_{Z}}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Z\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

where
T
is an isomorphism and
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
. The Lipschitz maps
\varphi,\psi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
with
\varphi(0)=\psi(0)=0
are said to be
equivalent
whenever

\sup_{t>0}\left|\varphi(t)-\psi(t)\right|<\infty.

The maps
\varphi
and
\psi
are
projectively equivalent
(or, which is the same, that
\Omega_{\varphi}
and
\Omega_{\psi}
are projectively equivalent) if
\varphi
is equivalent to
c\psi
, for some
c\neq 0
. The twisted sums
Z(\varphi)
and
Z(\psi)
are (projectively) equivalent if and only if
\varphi
and
\psi
are (projectively) equivalent. If we have a twisted Hilbert space

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

then its dual is also a twisted Hilbert space with

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q^{*}}
\textstyle{X^{*}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i^{*}}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

In the case of twisted Hilbert spaces induced by Lipschitz maps, the dual of
Z(\varphi)
is equivalent to
Z(-\varphi)
and the duality action is given by
\langle(x,y),(w,z)\rangle=\langle y,w\rangle+\langle x,z\rangle
(for a more general result regarding twisted sums induced by interpolation, see
[
8
, Section 5]
). In particular,
Z(\varphi)
is isomorphic to
Z(\varphi)^{*}
. The sequence

\mathcal{E}:=\{(e_{1},0),(0,e_{1}),(e_{2},0),(0,e_{2}),\ldots\}

is a normalized basis for
Z(\varphi)
, which is unconditional if and only if
Z(\varphi)
is trivial (that is, Hilbertian)
[
34
]
. On the other hand, if we let
E_{n}=[(e_{n},0),(0,e_{n})]
, then
(E_{n})_{n}
defines a
2
-dimensional UFDD of
Z(\varphi)
(as in
[
35
, page 30]
). Another fact (that follows immediately from the boundedness of the function
t\mapsto t\log t
on
(0,1)
) that we will use in the paper is that
\Omega_{\varphi}:\ell_{2}\rightarrow c_{00}
is bounded.

3.
Twisted sums induced by bi-Lipschitz maps

In this section, we show that whenever
\varphi
is a bi-Lipschitz map, then
Z(\varphi)
is very similar to the Kalton-Peck space
Z_{2}
. In order to prove the results in this section, we use similar arguments to the ones from
[
35
]
. Nevertheless, we present all the details for the sake of completeness.

Let us recall that, given two metric spaces
X
and
Y
, a map
f:X\rightarrow Y
is said to be
Lipschitz
if there exists a constant
a\geq 0
such that
d(f(x),f(y))\leq a\cdot d(x,y)
for every
x,y\in X
. If there is also
b>0
such that
d(f(x),f(y))\geq b\cdot d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
, then we will call
f
a
bi-Lipschitz
map. We denote by
\mathcal{L}
the class of all Lipschitz maps
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
with
\varphi(0)=0
such that
\varphi(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
and by
\mathcal{L}_{bi}
the subclass of
\mathcal{L}
consisting of all bi-Lipschitz maps.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
. Consider
(v_{n})
to be a normalized block sequence in
\ell_{2}
and let
w_{n}=(\Omega_{\varphi}(v_{n}),v_{n})\in Z(\varphi)
. Then, we have that
(w_{n})
is a basic sequence equivalent to the basis of the Orlicz sequence space
\ell_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

Let
(w_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a block sequence of
\mathcal{E}
. Write
v_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=l_{n-1}+1}^{l_{n}}v_{n}(k)e_{k}
and let
t\in c_{00}
. We have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}w_{n}\right\rVert_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{k=l_{n-1}+1}^{l_{n}}t_{n}v_{n}(k)\left(\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\lvert v_{n}(k)\rvert}\right)-\varphi\left(\log\frac{\|t\|_{2}}{\lvert t_{n}v_{n}(k)\rvert}\right)\right)\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{2}.

Being a twisted sum of
\ell_{2}
, we have that
Z(\varphi)
is also reflexive (since being reflexive is a three space property
[
21
]
) and then
(w_{n})
is boundedly complete. Therefore, given a sequence of scalars
(t_{n})
, we have that
\sum t_{n}w_{n}
converges in
Z(\varphi)
if and only if

\sup_{N}\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{k=l_{n-1}+1}^{l_{n}}t_{n}v_{n}(k)\left(\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\lvert v_{n}(k)\rvert}\right)-\varphi\left(\log\frac{\|t\|_{2}}{\lvert t_{n}v_{n}(k)\rvert}\right)\right)\right\rVert_{2}+\|t\|_{2}<\infty.

Since
\varphi
is bi-Lipschitz, we have that

\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lvert t_{n}\rvert^{2}\left|\log\frac{\|t\|_{2}}{\lvert t_{n}\rvert}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\|t\|_{2}<\infty,

which means in turn that
(w_{n})
is equivalent to the canonical basis of
\ell_{\Phi}
.
∎

Recall from the Introduction that the Kalton-Peck space is
\Phi
-stable.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
. Then
\ell_{\varphi}
is
\Phi
-stable. More precisely, every normalized basic sequence
(w_{n})
in
Z(\varphi)
has a subsequence equivalent to the canonical basis of either
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{\Phi}
. In particular, if
w_{n}=(u_{n},v_{n})
and
\inf\|v_{n}\|>0
, then
(w_{n})
has a subsequence equivalent to the canonical basis of
\ell_{\Phi}
.

By using
[
19
, Proposition 6.9]
, we have that whenever
\varphi
is expansive,
Z(\varphi)
is singular. Theorem
3.2
gives us an alternative proof.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
. Then,
Z(\varphi)
is a singular and cosingular twisted Hilbert space.

Proof.

Let
q:Z(\varphi)\rightarrow\ell_{2}
be the quotient map and assume that it is not strictly singular. So there is a subspace
W\subset Z(\varphi)
such that
q|_{W}
is an isomorphism onto its image
q(W)
. Since
q(W)\subset\ell_{2}
, it has an orthonormal basis
f_{n}
. It follows
w_{n}=(q|_{W})^{-1}(f_{n})
is a basic sequence in
Z(\varphi)
equivalent to the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}
but
\inf\|q(w_{n})\|>0
, which contradicts Theorem
3.2
. The cosingularity now follows from the duality.
∎

To finish this section, it is worth recalling the alternative representation of
Z_{2}
as a twisted sum

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{\Phi}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Z_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{\Phi}^{*}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0.}

For this, we refer to
[
16
, Section 5.3]
,
[
15
, Proposition 3.6]
and
[
23
, Facts 1.1]
. See also
[
22
]
. Similarly, we have:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
. Then, we have the short exact sequence

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ell_{\Phi}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{j}
\textstyle{Z(\varphi)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p}
\textstyle{\ell_{\Phi}^{*}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

where

and

.

4.
Distinguishing twisted Hilbert spaces

The motivation for this section is to establish a simple criterion to guarantee that
Z(\varphi)
is non-isomorphic to
Z(\psi)
. We will follow the proof that
Z(\varphi_{\alpha})
is not isomorphic to a subspace of
Z(\varphi_{\beta})
presented in
[
3
, Chapter 16]
, which is a simplification of the proof of
[
33
]
. We present the proof in full detail for the sake of completeness.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
. Let us consider the following classes of functions. We write

(1)

\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
if the second derivative of
\varphi
exists and goes to
0
as
t
goes to
\infty
.

(2)

\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bid}
if

\limsup_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{\varphi(\log n)-\varphi(\log n^{1/2})}{\log n^{1/2}}-\frac{\varphi(\log m)-\varphi(\log m^{1/2})}{\log m^{1/2}}\right|>0.

We then set
\mathcal{L}_{bids}:=\mathcal{L}_{bis}\cap\mathcal{L}_{bid}
.

Our main goal here is to prove the following result. Recall that two Banach spaces
X
and
Y
are said to
incomparable
if no infinite dimensional subspace of
X
is isomorphic to a subspace of
Y
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bid}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
. If
\Omega_{\varphi}
and
\Omega_{\psi}
are not projectively equivalent, then we have that
Z(\varphi)
is incomparable to
Z(\psi)
.

Recall that
\Omega_{\varphi}
and
\Omega_{\psi}
are projectively equivalent if and only if

\sup_{t>0}\;\lvert\varphi(t)-a\psi(t)\rvert<\infty

for some
a\neq 0

[
35
]
.
Therefore, we have a simple test for projective equivalence.
Each element of
Z(\varphi)
is given by a pair
(x,y)
of sequences. Therefore, it is also natural to consider
2\times 2
matrices as acting on
Z(\varphi)
(see also
[
23
]
). More precisely, we write

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\mu\\
\eta&\sigma\end{pmatrix}(x,y)=(\lambda x+\mu y,\eta x+\sigma y).

For every
n\geq 1
, we set
f_{n}:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\in\ell_{2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\mu\\
\eta&\sigma\end{pmatrix}:Z(\varphi)\rightarrow Z(\psi)
be bounded. If
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
are not projectively equivalent, then
\lambda=\eta=\sigma=0
.

Proof.

Let
u_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(f_{n},0)
. Then,
Tu_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda f_{n},\eta f_{n})
, and therefore

\displaystyle\|T\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|Tu_{n}\|_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\|\lambda f_{n}-\eta\Omega_{\psi}f_{n}\|_{2}+\|\eta f_{n}\|_{2})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\lvert\eta\rvert\|\Omega_{\psi}f_{n}\|_{2}+(\lvert\eta\rvert-\lvert\lambda\rvert)\|f_{n}\|_{2})\rightarrow\infty

if
\eta\neq 0
, since
Z(\psi)
is nontrivial.

Now take
u_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Omega_{\varphi}f_{n},f_{n})
. Then,
Tu_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda\Omega_{\varphi}e_{n}+\mu f_{n},\sigma f_{n})
(since
\eta=0
) and

\displaystyle\|T\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|Tu_{n}\|_{\psi}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\lambda\Omega_{\varphi}f_{n}+\mu f_{n}-\sigma\Omega_{\psi}f_{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\|\lambda\Omega_{\varphi}f_{n}-\sigma\Omega_{\psi}f_{n}\|_{2}-\|\mu f_{n}\|_{2})\rightarrow\infty

if
\Omega_{\varphi}
and
\Omega_{\psi}
are not projectively equivalent and
\lambda\neq 0
or
\sigma\neq 0
. Indeed, we have that

\displaystyle\|\lambda\Omega_{\varphi}(f_{n})-\sigma\Omega_{\psi}(f_{n})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left|\lambda\varphi(\log\sqrt{n})-\mu\psi(\log\sqrt{n})\right|.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be given. If we go far enough down in the real line, then we may approximate any real number by one of the form
\log\sqrt{n}
up to
\varepsilon
,
Since
\varphi
and
\psi
are bi-Lispchitz, we obtain

\sup_{x>0}\left|\lambda\varphi(\log x)-\mu\psi(\log x)\right|<\infty\iff\sup_{n}\left|\lambda\varphi(\log\sqrt{n})-\mu\psi(\log\sqrt{n})\right|<\infty

as we wanted to prove.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{bid}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
. If
Z(\varphi)
is isomorphic to a subspace of
Z(\psi)
, then there is a bounded
T=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\mu\\
\eta&\sigma\end{pmatrix}:Z(\varphi)\rightarrow Z(\psi)
with
\sigma\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
S:Z(\varphi)\rightarrow Z(\psi)
with
\|S\|\leq 1
and
\|S(e_{n},0)\|_{\psi}\geq c>0
for every
n
. By taking a subsequence and perturbing
S
, we may suppose that the images of
(e_{n},0)
and
(0,e_{n})
are blocks of
\mathcal{E}
, that is, that there are indexes
p_{1}<p_{2}<p_{3}<\cdots
such that if
I_{n}=\{k:p_{n}\leq k<p_{n+1}\}
, then
S(e_{n},0)=(u_{n},y_{n})
and
S(0,e_{n})=(v_{n},w_{n})
, with
u_{n},y_{n},v_{n},w_{n}\in[e_{k}]_{k\in I_{n}}
.

Claim 1
:

\|y_{n}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0
.

Intuitively, this means that the image by
S
of the canonical copy of
\ell_{2}
in
Z(\varphi)
is essentially contained in the canonical copy of

5.
A large family of singular twisted Hilbert spaces

The results of the previous sections imply the existence of an infinite-dimensional cone of singular twisted Hilbert spaces by proving that the set of bi-Lipschitz maps from
[0,\infty)
into
\mathbb{K}
is coneable.
Following
[
1
]
, we say that a set
F
in a vector space is
coneable
(or
convex-coneable
, see, for instance,
[
6
]
) if it contains a convex cone generated by an infinite linearly independent set.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
be a differentiable function and
\beta>0
.
If
|g|
and
|g^{\prime}|
are bounded by
M>0
and
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{M(1+\beta)}
, then
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
, where

g_{\alpha,\beta}(x):=x+\alpha xg(\beta\log x).

If
g
is twice differentiable and
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\beta\log x)}{x}=0
, then
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
.
Moreover, if

\limsup_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}\left|\left(2g\left(\beta\log\log n\right)-g\left(\beta\log\log n^{1/2}\right)\right)-\left(2g\left(\beta\log\log m\right)-g\left(\beta\log\log m^{1/2}\right)\right)\right|>0,

(5.1)

then
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bids}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}
for any
\alpha>0
. We also have that

\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\lvert x+\alpha xg(\beta\log(x))-y-\alpha yg(\beta\log(y))\rvert+\alpha\lvert xg(\beta\log(x))-yg(\beta\log(y))\rvert.

As the derivative of
f(x)=xg(\beta\log(x))
is
f^{\prime}(x)=g(\beta\log(x))+\beta g^{\prime}(\beta\log(x))
, we see that

\lvert xg(\beta\log(x))-yg(\beta\log(y))\rvert\leq M(1+\beta)\lvert x-y\rvert.

Therefore, it immediately follows that
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
as long as
\alpha<\frac{1}{M(1+\beta)}
.

For the second part, as
g
is twice differentiable, we have that

g^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{x}(g^{\prime}(\beta\log x)+g^{\prime\prime}(\beta\log x))

so that
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
by the hypothesis
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\beta\log x)}{x}=0
. Condition (
5.1
) implies that
g_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}_{bids}
.
∎

In order to present an example of a function satisfying the conditions of Proposition
5.1
, we will use Kronecker’s Density Theorem (see, for instance,
[
30
,
36
]
).
In
[
29
]
, the latter theorem was first used in Lineability (the search for algebraic structures inside non-linear sets
[
2
]
). See also
[
28
]
and
[
5
]
for a better understanding on how this theorem is applied in this context.

Theorem 5.2

(Kronecker’s Density Theorem)
.

If
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent, then

\mathcal{D}:=\{(k\beta_{1}-[k\beta_{1}],\ldots,k\beta_{n}-[k\beta_{n}]):k\in\mathbb{N}\}

is dense in
[0,1]^{n}
(here,
[x]
stands for the integer part of
x
).

Example 5.3
.

Take
g(x)=\sin x
and
\beta^{\prime}>0
.
Let
\gamma=\log\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=-2\pi\beta^{\prime}\gamma
.
Of course,
g
is bounded with bounded derivative and
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\beta\log x)}{x}=0
.
Let us check (
5.1
).
By letting
y=\log\log n
and
z=\log\log m
, we see that we want to estimate

\left|(2g(\beta y)-g(\beta(\gamma+y)))-(2g(\beta z)-g(\beta(\gamma+z)))\right|.

Given
\varepsilon>0
and going down enough in the real line, we may approximate any real number by one of the form
\log\log n
up to
\varepsilon
. Thus, since
g
is Lipschitz, it is enough to show that

\limsup_{y,z\rightarrow\infty}\left|(2g(\beta y)-g(\beta(\gamma+y)))-(2g(\beta z)-g(\beta(\gamma+z)))\right|>0.

Let
y=-n\gamma
for some
n
. Then

\displaystyle 2g(\beta y)-g(\beta(\gamma+y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sin(-\beta n\gamma)-\sin(-\beta n\gamma+\beta\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sin(-\beta n\gamma)-(\sin(-\beta n\gamma)\cos(\beta\gamma)+\sin(\beta\gamma)\cos(-\beta n\gamma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin(-\beta n\gamma)(2-\cos(\beta\gamma))-\sin(\beta\gamma)\cos(-\beta n\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\sin(-\beta n\gamma)+b\cos(-\beta n\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\sin\left(2\pi\beta^{\prime}\gamma^{2}n\right)+b\cos\left(2\pi\beta^{\prime}\gamma^{2}n\right)

with
a=2-\cos(\beta\gamma)\geq 1
and
b=-\sin(\beta\gamma)
.
(Note that
a\neq b
.)
Let us define on
\mathbb{R}
the continuous function
L(t)=a\sin(2\pi t)+b\cos(2\pi t)
.
Since the sine and cosine functions are
2\pi
-periodic, Theorem
5.2
yields that

L(\mathcal{D})=\left\{a\sin\left(2\pi k\beta^{\prime}\gamma^{2}\right)+b\cos\left(2\pi k\beta^{\prime}\gamma^{2}\right):k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

is dense in
L([0,1])
.
Thus, we deduce that

\limsup_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}\left|(a\sin(-\beta n\gamma)+b\cos(-\beta n\gamma))-(a\sin(-\beta m\gamma)+b\cos(-\beta m\gamma))\right|>0,

which is equivalent to

\limsup_{y,z\rightarrow\infty}\left|(2g(\beta y)-g(\beta(\gamma+y)))-(2g(\beta z)-g(\beta(\gamma+z)))\right|>0.

Therefore,
g=\sin
satisfies (
5.1
) with
\beta=-2\pi\beta^{\prime}\log\frac{1}{2}
for any
\beta^{\prime}>0
.

We also need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n},\ \alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0
,
\alpha=\max\{\alpha_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
.
If
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}>0
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent and
\alpha\left(1+2\pi\beta\log 2\right)<1
with
\beta=\max\{\beta_{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}
, then
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}g_{\alpha_{j},-2\pi\beta_{j}\log\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathcal{L}_{bids}
for
g=\sin
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}
,
\gamma=\log\frac{1}{2}
and
h:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}g_{\alpha_{j},-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma}
.
It is obvious that
h\in\mathcal{L}
, so we will first prove that
h\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
.
By the reverse triangular inequality, we have that

|\lambda x-\lambda y|-\left|x\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\sin(-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma\log x)-y\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\sin(-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma\log x)\right|\leq|h(x)-h(y)|

Set
\ell(x):=xf(x)
with
f(x):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\sin(-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma\log x)
. We have that

\ell^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\sin(-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma\log x)-2\pi\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}\cos(-2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma\log x).

Then,

|\ell^{\prime}(x)|\leq\alpha\lambda-2\pi\alpha\beta\gamma\lambda=\alpha\lambda(1-2\pi\beta\gamma).

Therefore,

\lambda(1-\alpha(1-2\pi\beta\gamma))|x-y|\leq|h(x)-h(y)|,

which implies that
h\in\mathcal{L}_{bi}
.
Now it is straightforward that
h\in\mathcal{L}_{bis}
.
It remains to show that
h\in\mathcal{L}_{bids}
.
Similarly to Example
5.3
, by letting
y=\log m
and
z=\log p
, it is enough to show that

\limsup_{y,z\rightarrow\infty}\left|(2h(y)-h(\gamma+y))-(2h(z)-h(\gamma+z))\right|>0.

(5.2)

By letting
y=-m\gamma
for some
m
, we have

\displaystyle 2h(y)-h(\gamma+y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(a_{j}\sin\left(2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma^{2}m\right)+b_{j}\cos\left(2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma^{2}m\right)\right),

where
a_{j}=\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}(2-\cos(2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma))>0
and
b_{j}=\lambda_{j}\alpha_{j}\sin(2\pi\beta_{j}\gamma)
for every
1\leq j\leq n
.
Observe that
a_{j}\neq b_{j}
for any
1\leq j\leq n
.
As above, define the continuous function
L
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

L(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}):=(a_{1}\sin(2\pi t_{1})+b_{1}\cos(2\pi t_{1}),\ldots,a_{n}\sin(2\pi t_{n})+b_{n}\cos(2\pi t_{n})).

Then, the set

L(\mathcal{D})=\left\{\left(a_{1}\sin\left(2\pi k\beta_{1}\gamma^{2}\right)+b_{1}\cos\left(2\pi k\beta_{1}\gamma^{2}\right),\ldots,a_{n}\sin\left(2\pi k\beta_{n}\gamma^{2}\right)+b_{n}\cos\left(2\pi k\beta_{n}\gamma^{2}\right)\right):k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

is dense in
L([0,1]^{n})
by Theorem
5.2
.
As an immediate consequence, we get inequality (
5.2
), which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

The set
\mathcal{L}_{bids}
is coneable.

Proof.

Take
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\left(0,1\right)
an infinite linearly independent subset of
\mathbb{R}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Observe that, by Lemma
5.4
, it is enough to show that the family
\left\{g_{10^{-1},2\pi\beta\log 2}:\beta\in\mathcal{B}\right\}
with
g=\sin
is linearly independent.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in\mathcal{B}
be distinct. By contradiction, assume that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}g_{10^{-1},2\pi\beta_{j}\log 2}=0.

We have that, for every
x>0
,

0=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}g_{10^{-1},2\pi\beta_{j}\log 2}=\lambda x+\frac{x}{10}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\sin(2\pi\beta_{j}\log 2\log x).

For
x=1
we obtain
\lambda=0
, so that we have, for all
x>0
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\sin(2\pi\beta_{j}\log 2\log x)=0.

(5.3)

Consider now the function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]^{n}
given by

L(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=(\sin(2\pi t_{1}),\ldots,\sin(2\pi t_{n}))

which is continuous. We have that the set

L(\mathcal{D})=\{(\sin(2\pi k\beta_{1}\log 2),\ldots,\sin(2\pi k\beta_{n}\log 2)):k\in\mathbb{N}\}

is dense in
[-1,1]^{n}=L([0,1]^{n})
by Theorem
5.2
.
So, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sin(2\pi k\beta_{i}\log 2)>\frac{1}{2}\ \mbox{if}\ \lambda_{i}>0\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \sin(2\pi k\beta_{i}\log 2)<-\frac{1}{2}\ \mbox{if}\ \lambda_{i}<0.

Take now
x=e^{k}
. Then, from (
5.3
), we have that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\sin(2\pi k\beta_{j}\log 2)=0.

Denote by
\mathcal{J}^{+}:=\{j:\lambda_{j}>0\}
and
\mathcal{J}^{-}:=\{j:\lambda_{j}<0\}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\sin(2\pi k\beta_{j}\log 2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{+}}\lambda_{j}\sin(2\pi k\beta_{j}\log 2)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{-}}|\lambda_{j}|(-\sin(2\pi k\beta_{j}\log 2))

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{+}}\lambda_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{-}}|\lambda_{j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|>0

which is a contradiction.
∎

We single out a consequence of the last result.

Corollary 5.6
.

The set of bi-Lipschitz maps from
[0,\infty)
into
\mathbb{K}
is coneable.

Back to twisted Hilbert spaces, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\beta_{1}\neq\beta_{2}
and assume that
g_{\alpha,\beta_{1}},g_{\alpha,\beta_{2}}\in\mathcal{L}_{bids}
for
g=\sin
. Then
\Omega_{g_{\alpha,\beta_{1}}}
is not projectively equivalent to
\Omega_{g_{\alpha,\beta_{2}}}
.

Proof.

We need to show that for any
\beta\neq 0

\sup_{x>0}\left|x\right|\left|\sin(\beta_{1}\log x)-\beta\sin(\beta_{2}\log x)\right|=\infty.

But that is equivalent to

\limsup_{y>0}\left|\sin(\beta_{1}y)-\beta\sin(\beta_{2}y)\right|>0.

Take
y=\frac{2\pi n}{\beta_{2}}
so that the second term vanishes. Apply Kronecker’s Density Theorem to the first and we are done.
∎

As a consequence of the previous results, we obtain the following.

Theorem 5.8
.

There is an infinite-dimensional cone
\mathcal{C}
of bi-Lipschitz maps such that for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
,
Z(\varphi)
is a singular twisted Hilbert space. Furthermore, if
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{C}
and
\varphi
is not a multiple of
\psi
, then
Z(\varphi)
is incomparable to

.

Proof.

Take
\mathcal{B}\subseteq(0,1)
an infinite linearly independent subset of
\mathbb{R}
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}>0
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m}\in\mathcal{B}
distinct, and take
g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}g_{10^{-1},2\pi\beta_{i}\log 2}
and

We want to show that if

By absorbing

We have

6.
Banach spaces not isomorphic to their conjugate dual and interpolation

Let us recall that, for

We recall that if

The first example of a Banach space that is

\overline{Z(\varphi_{\alpha})^{*}}\simeq Z(\varphi_{-\alpha})

Z(\varphi_{\alpha})
is isomorphic to its dual but not to its conjugate dual.

Suppose that
\varphi,\psi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are in the class
\mathcal{L}_{bids}
. Then so are

and

Theorem 6.1
.

There is an infinite-dimensional cone

of bi-Lipschitz maps from

into

such that for every

\zeta_{2}
then

Z(\zeta_{2})
. Each space

To explain the final part of Theorem

7.
A characterization of the Kalton-Peck space

As we have seen throughout the paper, we have built a large family of spaces
Z(\varphi)
sharing the properties of
Z_{2}
, but not isomorphic to
Z_{2}
. In this section, we present a characterization of the Kalton-Peck space among all twisted Hilbert spaces of the form
Z(\varphi)
. This provides a partial answer to a conjecture by Cabello and Castillo (see
[
11
, page 517]
).

Recall that if
X
is a Banach space with normalized basis
(x_{n})
and
(x_{n})
is equivalent to all of its normalized block bases, then,
(x_{n})
is equivalent to the canonical basis of either
c_{0}
or of some
\ell_{p}
space (
1\leq p<\infty)
(see
[
40
]
).

The Kalton-Peck space satisfies the following similarity property (see
[
11
, page 517]
). Let
X
be a twisted Hilbert space. Given a normalized block basis
(u_{n})
of the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}
, let
U=[u_{n}]_{n}=\overline{\mbox{span}}\{u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and let
T_{U}:\ell_{2}\rightarrow U
be given by
T_{U}(x)=\sum x_{n}u_{n}
. Let

U(X)=\overline{\mbox{span}}\{(y,x)\in X:y,x\in\mbox{span}\{u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\}

We say that
X
is
self-similar with respect to
(u_{n})
if there is an isomorphism
T:X\rightarrow U(X)
making the following diagram commutative

\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T_{U}}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T}
\textstyle{\ell_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{0}

\textstyle{0}

We say that
X
is
self-similar
if it is self-similar with respect to every normalized block basis
(u_{n})
of the canonical basis of
\ell_{2}
.

It is clear that
X=\ell_{2}\oplus\ell_{2}
is self-similar. As mentioned,
Z_{2}
is self-similar as well. In the language of
[
17
,
20
]
, the operator
T
appearing above for
X=Z_{2}
is a
block operator
(see also
[
18
]
). Our goal here is to prove that the Kalton-Peck space
Z_{2}
is the unique nontrivial self-similar twisted Hilbert space of the form
Z(\varphi)
. The proof starts by essentially showing that if
X=Z(\varphi)
is self-similar, then we can take the operator
T
to be a block operator.

Lemma 7.1
.

The twisted Hilbert space
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar with respect to
(u_{n})
if and only if there is a linear map
L:\ell_{2}\rightarrow U
and
C>0
such that

\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+Lx\|_{2}\leq C\|x\|_{2}

for every
x\in c_{00}
. Furthermore,
\|T\|\leq 1+C
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar with respect to
(u_{n})
and let
T
be a witness for
(u_{n})
. It follows that
T
necessarily has the form
T(y,x)=(T_{U}y+Lx,T_{U}x)
. Let
x\in c_{00}
. We have that

\displaystyle\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+Lx\|_{2}\leq\|T(\Omega_{\varphi}x,x)\|_{Z(\varphi)}\leq\|T\|\|x\|_{2}.

On the other hand, if

\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+Lx\|_{2}\leq C\|x\|_{2}

for every
x\in c_{00}
, define
T(y,x)=(T_{U}y+Lx,T_{U}x)
for
x,y\in c_{00}
. We have

\displaystyle\|T(y,x)\|_{Z(\varphi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|T_{U}(y)+Lx-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)\|_{2}+\|T_{U}x\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(y,x)\|_{Z(\varphi)}+\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+Lx\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+C)\|(y,x)\|_{Z(\varphi)}

as we wanted to prove.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
u_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)e_{k}
. If
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar with respect to
(u_{n})
we may replace the map
L
from Lemma
7.1
by
L^{\prime}
given by

L^{\prime}(e_{n})=\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)u_{n}(k)e_{k}

replacing
C
by
2C
.

Proof.

Let
L
be given by Lemma
7.1
and let
\mathcal{U}=\{+1,-1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the group of units of real valued
\ell_{\infty}
. Following
[
19
, Lemma 3.15]
, define

\Lambda(x)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}S_{U}(v)L(vx)dm

for
x\in\ell_{2}
, where
S_{U}(e_{n})=\sum\limits_{j=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}e_{j}
and
m
is a left invariant finitely additive mean on
\mathcal{U}
. Notice that
\Lambda
is linear and for
x\in c_{00}

\displaystyle\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+\Lambda(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathcal{U}}S_{U}(v)[T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}(vx))-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}(vx))+L(vx)]dm\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+L(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|x\|_{2}.

Also, if
w\in\mathcal{U}
, then

\Lambda(wx)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}S_{U}(v)L(vwx)dm(v)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}S_{U}(w)S_{U}(vw)L(vwx)dm(v)=S_{U}(w)\Lambda(x).

Thus
\Lambda(e_{n})=\Lambda(e_{n}e_{n})=S_{U}(e_{n})\Lambda(e_{n})
for every
n
. It follows that the support of
\Lambda(e_{n})
is contained in the support of
u_{n}
, so that we may write

\Lambda(e_{n})=\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}\lambda_{n}(k)u_{n}(k)e_{k}.

Since

T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}e_{n})-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}e_{n})=-\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)e_{k}

we obtain

\left\|\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)\left(\lambda_{n}(k)-\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)\right)e_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq C

for every
n
. Finally, let
x\in c_{00}
and define

L^{\prime}(x)=\sum\limits_{n}x_{n}\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)e_{k}.

Let us see that we may replace
L
by
L^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\|T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+L^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|x\|_{2}+\|\Lambda(x)-L^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\|x\|_{2}+\left\|\sum\limits_{n}x_{n}\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)\left(\lambda_{n}(k)-\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)\right)e_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C\|x\|_{2}

since the
u_{n}
’s are disjointly supported.
∎

According to Lemma
7.1
, associated to
L^{\prime}
there is a witness that
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar with respect to
(u_{n})
. Let us denote that witness by
T_{U,U}
and call it
the canonical witness for
(u_{n})
.

Lemma 7.3
.

If
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar, then there is a constant
C
independent of the normalized block basis
(u_{n})
such that for every
x\in c_{00}
we have

\left\|\sum\limits_{n}x_{n}\sum\limits_{k=p_{n}+1}^{p_{n+1}}u_{n}(k)\left[\varphi\left(\log\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{\left|x_{n}\right|}\right)+\varphi\left(\log\frac{1}{\left|u_{n}(k)\right|}\right)-\varphi\left(\log\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{\left|u_{n}(k)x_{n}\right|}\right)\right]e_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq C\|x\|_{2}.

Proof.

Notice that the expression inside the norm is simply
T_{U}(\Omega_{\varphi}x)-\Omega_{\varphi}(T_{U}x)+L^{\prime}(x)
, where
L^{\prime}
is given by the previous lemma. Suppose the conclusion is false. That implies that for each
N
we are able to find a normalized block basis
(u_{n}^{N})_{n}
of
\ell_{2}
for which
\|T_{U^{N},U^{N}}\|>N
, where
U^{N}=[u_{n}^{N}]_{n}
.

We may see
(u_{n}^{N})_{n,N}
as a normalized block basis for
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})
. Use a bijection between
\mathbb{N}
and

to transport this block basis to
\ell_{2}
, call it

and let

. Notice that

is an amalgamation of the maps
T_{U^{N},U^{N}}
, and therefore should be unbounded, contradicting the fact that
Z(\varphi)
is self-similar.
∎

Theorem 7.4
.

If
Z(\varphi)
is nontrivial and self-similar, then it is projectively equivalent (and therefore isomorphic) to the Kalton-Peck space
Z_{2}
.

Proof.

Given
N\geq 1
, let
u_{n}^{N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum\limits_{j=(n-1)N+1}^{nN}e_{j}

and notice that
(u_{n}^{N})_{n}
is a normalized block basis for
(\ell_{2})
. Take
M\geq 1
and
x=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}e_{j}
in the previous lemma to get that

\left|\varphi\left(\log\sqrt{M}\right)+\varphi\left(\log\sqrt{N}\right)-\varphi\left(\log\sqrt{NM}\right)\right|\leq C

for every

. Since
\varphi
is Lipschitz, we obtain that

\sup_{a,b>0}\left|\varphi(a+b)-\varphi(a)-\varphi(b)\right|<\infty.

Following
[

, there exists

and

\sup_{t>0}\left|\varphi(t)-ct\right|<\infty

and therefore
Z(\varphi)
is projectively equivalent to
Z_{2}
.
∎

As two-thirds of the authors of

]
, we conjecture that the Kalton-Peck space
Z_{2}
is, up to isomorphism, the only nontrivial self-similar twisted Hilbert space.
