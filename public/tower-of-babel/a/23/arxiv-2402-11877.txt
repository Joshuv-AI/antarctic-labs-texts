Finite-Time Error Analysis of Online Model-Based Q-Learning with a Relaxed Sampling Model
Han-Dong Lim, HyeAnn Lee, Donghwan Lee

Abstract
Reinforcement learning has witnessed significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of model-based approaches. Among these, Q Q -learning has proven to be a powerful algorithm in model-free settings. However, the extension of Q Q -learning to a model-based framework remains relatively unexplored. In this paper, we delve into the sample complexity of Q Q -learning when integrated with a model-based approach. Through theoretical analyses and empirical evaluations, we seek to elucidate the conditions under which model-based Q Q -learning excels in terms of sample efficiency compared to its model-free counterpart.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) aims to solve a sequential decision making problem. In a broad perspective, RL algorithms can be categorized into two classes: model-free and model-based approach. Both methods have shown great success in various scenarios
[
Silver et al. 2017
,
Fawzi et al. 2022
]
. The model-free approach tries to solve the sequential decision making process without any knowledge of a model. In contrast, the model-based approach leverages a known or estimated model during the learning process.

Q
-learning, developed by
Watkins and Dayan 1992
, is one of the most widely used model-free RL algorithms. A rich body of literature has tried to understand the nature of
Q
-learning, and its non-asymptotic behavior has been recently understood in detail
[
Qu and Wierman 2020
,
Chen et al. 2019
,
Lee 2022
,
Li et al. 2023
]
. In particular,
Li et al. 2023
proved a tight sample complexity bound of
Q
-learning, which matches the lower bound on the sample complexity of
Q
-learning.

Meanwhile, a natural method to improve the sample efficiency of RL algorithms is to incorporate the model into the learning phase. Both in theoretical and experimental sense, leveraging the knowledge of learned or known model has been shown to improve over the model-free methods. For instance,
Nagabandi et al. 2018
,
Chua et al. 2018
,
Kidambi et al. 2020
experimentally verified that approximating a model improves the sample efficiency of model-free algorithms. The sample efficiency of model-based algorithms has been studied in
Kearns and Singh 2002
,
Azar et al. 2012
under various settings in theoretical manners.

An intuitive method to extend
Q
-learning to a model-based approach, is to replace the stochastic components in the
Q
-learning update with estimators calculated via previous samples. In this paper, we study to what extent, we can improve the
Q
-learning with a model-based approach. We consider a natural extension of
Q
-learning with estimated model, which we call synchronous model-based
Q
-learning (SyncMBQ). In this respect, our main contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

We consider a new model-based RL algorithm, called SyncMBQ, which is an extension of
Q
-learning to model-based approaches, and prove its near-optimal sample complexity under a general step-size regime,
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

2.

SyncMBQ can be seen as an online model-based
Q
-learning, where the model estimate and
Q
-estimate are learned simultaneously in an online manner. It is online because the model and
Q
-estimate are updated in real-time using a single transition of the system at each iteration.

3.

Moreover, SyncMBQ uses a more relaxed sampling model compared to the idealistic generative models (sampling oracle) used in some of previous model-based RLs. In particular, SyncMBQ uses a single transition at each iteration, where only one state-action pair is sampled from a fixed distribution under an exploratory behavior policy, and then the corresponding next state is sampled. This scenario is obviously weaker than the generative model cases.

4.

To the authors’ knowledge, SyncMBQ considered in this paper has not been considered and thoroughly investigated so far in the literature. In this paper, we prove that SyncMBQ can achieve
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity, which is compatible with the optimal sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
achievable by existing model-based RLs with generative models except for the order of the effective horizon
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
. Moreover, this result improves the sample complexities achievable by existing model-based RLs without relying on generative models.
Note also that this sample complexity is identical to the optimal sample complexity (not improvable) reported in
Li et al. 2023
for a model-free
Q
-learning with a restrictive step-size regime. SyncMBQ can achieve an identical efficiency under more general step-size regimes.

5.

For the analysis, the recenty developed switching system model framework in
Lee 2022
is adopted in this paper. However, we develop a new switching system model for SyncMBQ and use it for our finite-time analysis. Finally, the performance of SyncMBQ is demonstrated via simulations, which empirically verify that SyncMBQ outperforms
Q
-learning under various scenarios.

Related Works on model-free Q-learning:

Recently, some advances have been made on the non-asymptotic behavior of model-free
Q
-learning algorithms
[
Chen et al. 2019
,
Qu and Wierman 2020
,
Lee 2022
,
Li et al. 2023
]
.
For example,
Chen et al. 2019
,
Qu and Wierman 2020
,
Lee 2022
studied asynchronous
Q
-learning, where only the
Q
-function estimate corresponding to a single state-action pair is updated at each iteration. Moreover,
Li et al. 2023
proved that synchronous
Q
-learning, which updates the
Q
-function estimate for every state-action pairs each iteration, requires
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
number of samples, which matches the known lower bound of the sample complexity of
Q
-learning.

Related works on model-based Q-learning:

Model-based RL algorithms have been studied in
Azar et al. 2012
,
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
under the assumption that generative models are available, where a generative model means a sampling oracle where we can access any state-action pair in the environment on our choice and get the next state. Roughly speaking, the main idea of these works is to run a
Q
-value iteration with the transition matrix and reward function replaced with the corresponding estimated models learned through the generative models. In particular,
Azar et al. 2012
,
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
proposed model-based algorithms which consist of the two phases: in the first phase, the model is learned through samples from generative models; in the second phase, dynamic programmings are applied using the learned model. The authors of
Azar et al. 2012
established a lower bound of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
on the sample complexity inherent to all RL algorithms and demonstrated that the sample complexity of their algorithm aligns with this established lower bound when a sufficient number of samples are collected in the first phase. More recently,
Li et al. 2020
derived a similar sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
, and
Agarwal et al. 2020
theoretically studied the optimality of these model-based
Q
-learning algorithms based on generative models.

Batch
Q
-learning algorithms, which use a number of samples collected at each iteration to construct an approximate Bellman operator, can be also interpreted as a non-parametric model-based
Q
-learning. Relying on an empirical mean estimator of the Bellman operator makes similar effect as leveraging a model estimate while reducing the space complexity from
{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathcal{S}}|^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}|)
to
{\mathcal{O}}\left(|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|\right)
. For example,
Kearns and Singh 1998
,
Kalathil et al. 2021
,
Wainwright 2019
studied empirical
Q
-value iteration algorithms, which estimate empirical Bellman operator with newly collected samples from a generative model at every iteration.
In particular,
Kearns and Singh 1998
investigated the so-called phased
Q
-learning that uses estimated transition matrix estimated with newly collected samples at each iteration using the generative model. Phased
Q
-learning has been proven to achieve
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity. This algorithm includes both a non-parametric batch version of
Q
-learning and a parametric model-based
Q
-learning version. In
Kalathil et al. 2021
, the authors proved that their empirical
Q
-value iteration can have
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity to achieve desired level of accuracy. Moreover,
Sidford et al. 2018
proposed a slightly different variance-reduced
Q
-value iteration algorithm and obtained
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexities. Afterwards,
Wainwright 2019
developed a variance-reduced
Q
-learning (a batch
Q
-learning), and proved that it can also achieve the optimal sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
. Note that all the aforementioned approaches require an access to a generative model, which is one of the most idealistic scenarios. The so-called delayed
Q
-learning, introduced by
Strehl et al. 2006
, is also a batch
Q
-learning algorithm, which can be seen as a non-parametric model-based
Q
-learning. It has been proved that the delayed
Q
-learning can achieve
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{7}{\epsilon}^{4}}\right)
sample complexity, while it does not require a generative model, and uses samples from a single trajectory.

Without relying on generative models, more practical model-based RL algorithms have been studied in
Kearns and Singh 2002
,
Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002
,
Strehl and Littman 2005
,
Szita and Szepesvári 2010
with corresponding sample complexities, where explorations and single trajectory-based samples are used.
In particular,
Kearns and Singh 2002
proposed the so-called
E^{3}
algorithm which decides to explore or exploit relying on the estimated model, and
Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002
introduced the so-called R-max algorithm wherein an agent acts by an optimal policy derived from the estimated model, and requires
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}|^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{6}{\epsilon}^{3}}\right)
number of samples. In a subsequent work,
Szita and Szepesvári 2010
proposed a modified version of R-max algorithm that achieves
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{6}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity. Moreover,
Strehl and Littman 2005
used a model based interval estimation method
[
Wiering and Schmidhuber 1998
]
to deal with the exploration-exploitation dilemma and proved that it can also achieve
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}|^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{6}{\epsilon}^{3}}\right)
sample complexity. In
Lattimore and Hutter 2014
, the authors studied the so-called UCRL
\gamma
algorithm that achieves
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}|^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
sample complexity, which is optimal in terms of the effective horizon
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
, while sub-optimal in terms of the state size
|{\cal S}|
.

Lastly, we note that
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Sidford et al. 2018
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
studied sample complexities to obtain
{\epsilon}
-optimal policy.
In this work, we focus on the sample complexity to obtain the
{\epsilon}
-optimal value function rather than
{\epsilon}
-optimal policy.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Markov decision process

A Markov decision process (MDP) consists of five tuples
({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},\gamma,{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{R}})
where
{\mathcal{S}}:=\{1,2,\dots,|{\mathcal{S}}|\}
is the collection of states,
{\mathcal{A}}:=\{1,2,\dots,|{\mathcal{A}}|\}
is the collection of actions,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor,
{\mathcal{P}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}\to[0,1]
is the transition kernel, and
{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function. Upon taking action
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
at state
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, transition to
s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
occurs with probability
{\mathcal{P}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
and reward
{\mathcal{R}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
is incurred. For simplicity of the proof, we assume that the reward function is bounded, i.e.,
|{\mathcal{R}}(s,a,s^{\prime})|\leq 1
for all
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
.

A deterministic policy
\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{A}}
maps a state
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
to an action
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We study finding an optimal deterministic policy,
\pi^{*}
, that maximizes the expected sum of discounted rewards under an infinite horizon MDP setting, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi^{*}:=\argmax_{\pi\in\Omega}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma^{k}r_{k}\middle|\pi\right],

where
\Omega
is the set of all admissible deterministic policies,
r_{k}:={\mathcal{R}}(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k+1})
,
\{(s_{k},a_{k})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence of state-action trajectory generated by a Markov decision process under policy
\pi
, and
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\middle|\pi\right]
denotes the expected value conditioned on policy
\pi
. The
Q
-function under policy
\pi
,
Q^{\pi}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
, denotes expected cumulative discounted reward starting at
(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
and following policy
\pi
afterwards:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a):=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}\mathbb{E}\left[\gamma^{k}r_{k}\middle|(s_{0},a_{0})=(s,a),\;\pi\right].

The optimal
Q
-function, which is a
Q
-function induced by the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, is denoted as
Q^{*}(s,a):=Q^{\pi^{*}}(s,a)
for all
s,a\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
. The optimal policy
\pi^{*}
can be recovered once
Q^{*}
is known, i.e.,
\pi^{*}(s)=\argmax_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}Q^{*}(s,a)
. It is well known that the optimal
Q
-function,
Q^{*}
, satisfies the following equation, the so-called optimal Bellman equation
[
Bellman 1966
]
, for all
s\in{\mathcal{S}},a\in{\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle Q^{*}(s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{P}}(s,a,s^{\prime})\left({\mathcal{R}}(s,a,s^{\prime})+\gamma\max_{u\in{\mathcal{A}}}Q^{*}(s,u)\right).

2.2
Overview of Q-learning

In this section, we briefly illustrate the
Q
-learning algorithm
[
Watkins and Dayan 1992
]
. The
Q
-learning algorithm is one of the well-known model-free algorithms. The update of
Q
-learning can be written as, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
{\bm{Q}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha){\bm{Q}}_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})

\displaystyle+\alpha\left(r_{k}+\gamma\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{Q}}_{k}(s_{k+1},a)\right),

(1)

where
{\bm{Q}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
,
{\bm{Q}}_{k}(s,a)=({\bm{e}}_{s}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{a})^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{k}
for
(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the constant step-size, and
{\bm{e}}_{s}
and
{\bm{e}}_{a}
denote
s
-th and
a
-th canonical basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
and
\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{A}}|}
, respectively.

To proceed, we further assume that at time step
k
, the state-action pair
(s_{k},a_{k})
is sampled from a fixed probability
d:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]
. We will assume that
d(s,a)>0
for all
s,a\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
d_{\min}:=\min_{s,a\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}d(s,a)
. Furthermore, we introduce a set of matrix notations:

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{p}}^{1,1}&{\bm{p}}^{1,2}&\cdots&{\bm{p}}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|,|{\mathcal{A}}|}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|\times|{\mathcal{S}}|},

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{R}}^{1,1}&{\bm{R}}^{1,2}&\cdots&{\bm{R}}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|,|{\mathcal{A}}|}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|},

\displaystyle{\bm{D}}:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}d(1,1)&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&d(|{\mathcal{S}}|,|{\mathcal{A}}|)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|\times|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|},

where
{\bm{p}}^{s,a}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
is a column vector whose
s^{\prime}
-th element corresponds to
{\mathcal{P}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
for
s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and
{\bm{R}}^{s,a}:=\mathbb{E}\left[r(s,a,s^{\prime})\middle|s,a\right]
. Moreover, for any deterministic policy
\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{A}}
, define,

\displaystyle{\bm{\Pi}}^{\pi}:=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{e}}_{1}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{\pi}}(1)^{\top}\\
{\bm{e}}_{2}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{\pi}}(2)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{e}}_{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{\pi}}(|{\mathcal{S}}|)^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|\times|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|},

where
{\bm{e}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
for
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
whose
s
-th element is one and others are zero, and
{\bm{\pi}}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{A}}|}
is a column vector whose
\pi(s)
-th element is one and others are all zero. We will denote the greedy policy induced by
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
, as
\pi_{{\bm{Q}}}(s):=\argmax_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{Q}}(s,a)
where
{\bm{Q}}(s,a)=({\bm{e}}_{s}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{a})^{\top}{\bm{Q}}
, for
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and let
{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}}:={\bm{\Pi}}^{\pi_{{\bm{Q}}}}
.

2.3
Switched system theory

In this section, a brief overview of the switching system
[
Liberzon 2005
]
is given. A switched system viewpoint has been a useful tool to analyze the behavior of
Q
-learning
[
Lee and He 2019
]
. The state variable
{\bm{x}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},k\in{\mathbb{N}}
of a switched affine system evolves via the following equation:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{A}}_{\sigma(k)}{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{b}}_{\sigma(k)},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}:\{1,2,\dots,|{\mathcal{M}}|\}
is called the mode,
\sigma(k)\in{\mathcal{M}}
is the switching signal,
\{{\bm{A}}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n};\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
and
\{{\bm{b}}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
are the subsystem matrices and vectors, respectively. The switching signal can be either determined arbitrary or controlled by a particular logic. When
{\bm{b}}_{\sigma}
, the so-called affine term, is zero vector for any
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}
, the above system is called the switched linear system.

3.1
Model-based approach

The model-based approach maintains statistical estimators of the transition probability
{\mathcal{P}}(s,a,s^{\prime})
, denoted by
\hat{p}^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}
, and expected reward
\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{R}}(s,a,s^{\prime})\mid s,a]
, denoted by
\hat{r}^{s,a}_{k}
, at time step
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for all
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
. We can naturally incorporate these estimators into the
Q
-learning update in (
1
), which can be written as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{k+1}(s_{k},a_{k})=(1-\alpha){\bm{Q}}_{k}(s_{k},a_{k})

\displaystyle+\alpha\left(\hat{r}^{s_{k},a_{k}}_{k}+\gamma\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\hat{p}^{s_{k},a_{k},s}_{k}\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{Q}}_{k}(s,a)\right).

At time step
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a simple choice for the estimators
\hat{p}^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}
and
\hat{r}^{s,a}_{k}
is by a simple averaging rule over the past observations up to iteration
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}=\frac{N^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}}{N^{s,a}_{k}},\quad\hat{r}^{s,a}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{k}r_{i}\bm{1}(\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\})}{N^{s,a}_{k}},

(3)

where
N^{s,a}_{k}
denotes number of visits to state-action pair
(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
and
N^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}
is the number of visits to
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
up to time
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\bm{1}(A)
is an indicator function such that returns one if event

is true and otherwise zero.

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{k+1}(s,a)=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha){\bm{Q}}_{k}(s,a)

\displaystyle+\alpha\left(\hat{r}^{s,a}_{k}+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\hat{p}^{s,a,s^{\prime}}_{k}\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{Q}}_{k}(s^{\prime},a)\right),

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{k}^{s_{k},a_{k}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N^{s_{k},a_{k}}_{k-1}}{N_{k-1}^{s_{k},a_{k}}+1}\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{k-1}^{s_{k},a_{k}}+\frac{1}{N_{k-1}^{s_{k},a_{k}}+1}{\bm{e}}_{s^{\prime}_{k}},

(4)

\displaystyle[\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}]_{s_{k},a_{k}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N^{s_{k}a_{k}}_{k-1}}{N^{s_{k}a_{k}}_{k-1}+1}[{\bm{R}}_{k-1}]_{s_{k},a_{k}}+\frac{1}{N^{s_{k},a_{k}}_{k-1}+1}r_{k},

(5)

where
[{\bm{v}}]_{s,a}:=({\bm{e}}_{s}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{a})^{\top}{\bm{v}}
for
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
.

.

At this point, we make some remarks on the existing model-based RL methods. A line of researches
[
Azar et al. 2012
,
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
]
focuses on off-line model-based approaches, where parametric models are learned first from a generative model (or a sampling oracle), and then dynamic programming algorithms are performed using the estimated models. Moreover, batch
Q
-learning algorithms
[
Kearns and Singh 1998
,
Kalathil et al. 2021
,
Wainwright 2019
,
Strehl et al. 2006
,
Sidford et al. 2018
]
use empirical Bellman operators based on a number of new samples collected from a generative model at each iteration. The aforementioned algorithms assume that an access to a generative model is possible, under which the optimal
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
or near optimal sample complexities can be achieved. On the other hand, the proposed algorithm relaxes the ideal sampling model to the cases which cover the scenario that a state-action pair is sampled by a fixed stationary state-action distribution under an exploratory behavior policy, and then the corresponding next state is sampled. This sampling model obviously uses a weaker assumption than the generative model. Moreover, the proposed algorithm is an online algorithm, where a single transition is sampled at each iteration and is used to update the
Q
-estimate, which is different from the two-steps off-line approaches in
[
Azar et al. 2012
,
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
]
and the on-line batch approaches
[
Kearns and Singh 1998
,
Kalathil et al. 2021
,
Wainwright 2019
,
Strehl et al. 2006
,
Sidford et al. 2018
]
.
Still, the proposed algorithm can achieve the sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
, which is optimal except for the order of the effective horizon
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
.

Besides, it is also worth noting model-based RLs with more relaxed sampling scenarios (without a generative model)
[
Kearns and Singh 2002
,
Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002
,
Szita and Szepesvári 2010
,
Strehl and Littman 2005
,
Lattimore and Hutter 2014
]
. In particular,
Szita and Szepesvári 2010
and
Lattimore and Hutter 2014
obtained the sample complexities,
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{6}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}|^{2}|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
, respectively. Even though their algorithms do not rely on a generative model similar to ours and use slightly different sample complexity measures, they exhibit worse sample complexities than ours in Theorem
3.7
.

Now, we provide some details of Algorithm
1
, which mainly consists of the two stages:

1.

Data collection stage : For the first several updates, we only update the estimators
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}
and
\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}
. This guarantees that every state-action pair will be updated. Considering that we are interested in the
l_{\infty}
-norm error, we can expect that the
l_{\infty}
-norm error will decrease after such event. We will denote the number of iterations of the first stage as
m:=\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\ln\left(\frac{2|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{\delta}\right)
. Under uniform random sampling setting, i.e.,
d_{\min}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
, the first stage only requires
{\mathcal{O}}(|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|)
number of samples. That is, we require only one transition for each state-action pair.

2.

Learning stage: During this stage, we exploit the model to update the iterate and the model. We observe a sample
(s_{k},a_{k},s_{k}^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
from an i.i.d. distribution and update
{\bm{Q}}_{k}
,
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}
, and
\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}
.

Remark 3.1
.

Our purpose of data collection stage is different from that of existing model-based RL algorithms.
Q
-value iteration algorithms in in
Gheshlaghi Azar et al. 2013
,
Li et al. 2020
,
Agarwal et al. 2020
, collect all the samples at once before updating the
Q
-function estimator. Likewise, batch
Q
-learning algorithms in
[
Kearns and Singh 1998
,
Kalathil et al. 2021
,
Sidford et al. 2018
,
Wainwright 2019
]
collect sufficient number of fresh samples each iteration before updating the
Q
-function estimator. It is meant to collect sufficient number of samples to guarantee an accurate model before the update of
Q
-function estimator. In contrast, we only require one observation for each state-action pair to construct a stochastic matrix.

The following lemma guarantees that every state-action pair is visited at least one time after
m
-steps.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
m=\frac{1}{d_{\min}}\ln\frac{2|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{\delta}
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, every state-action pairs are visited, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[N^{s,a}_{m}\geq 1,\;\forall(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\right]\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}.

The proof is given in Appendix Section
C.1
. To proceed, we will denote the event that every state-action pair is visited as
{\mathcal{E}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}:=\{N^{s,a}_{m}\geq 1,\forall(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\}.

3.2
Switched system perspective on SyncMBQ

This section provides switched system perspective on SyncMBQ to study its non-asymptotic behavior. Let us first re-write the update of SyncMBQ in (
2
) with coordinate transform
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}:={\bm{Q}}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}
, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}+\alpha(\gamma\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}-\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k})

\displaystyle+\alpha(\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}+\gamma\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}-{\bm{Q}}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{k}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}+\alpha{\bm{w}}_{k}+\alpha\gamma\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}({\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}){\bm{Q}}^{*},

(7)

where,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}^{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}

\displaystyle:={\bm{I}}+\alpha(\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}}-{\bm{I}}),\quad{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|},

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}-{\bm{R}}+\gamma(\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}-{\bm{P}}){\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}

(8)

and
{\bm{w}}_{k}
corresponds to the stochastic noise term. Note that we have used the Bellman optimal equation
{\bm{Q}}^{*}={\bm{R}}+\gamma{\bm{P}}{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}
in the definition of
{\bm{w}}_{k}
. The affine term,
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}({\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}-{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}){\bm{Q}}^{*}
in (
2
), causes significant challenge in the analysis of the algorithm. To avoid such difficulty, we will adopt the switched system perspective of
Q
-learning in
Lee and He 2019
. In particular, we can construct a sequence of iterates
\{{\bm{Q}}_{k}^{U}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\{{\bm{Q}}_{k}^{L}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, which upper and lower bounds
{\bm{Q}}_{k}
, respectively, i.e.,
{\bm{Q}}^{U}_{k}\geq{\bm{Q}}_{k}\geq{\bm{Q}}^{L}_{k}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, from the following relation

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max\left\{\left\|{\bm{Q}}^{U}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{\bm{Q}}^{L}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\right\},

(9)

we obtain desired error bound upon bounding the error of the upper comparison and lower comparison system. Letting
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k}={\bm{Q}}^{U}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}
, and
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k}={\bm{Q}}^{L}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}
, the following updates yields the upper and lower bounded iterates for
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{k}^{{\bm{Q}}_{k}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k+1}+\alpha{\bm{w}}_{k},\quad{\bm{Q}}^{U}_{0}={\bm{Q}}_{0},

(10)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}_{k}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k+1}+\alpha{\bm{w}}_{k},\quad{\bm{Q}}^{L}_{0}={\bm{Q}}_{0}.

(11)

The governing dynamics of
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k}
and
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k}
are switched linear system, which does not include any affine terms.

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider
{\bm{Q}}^{U}_{k}
and
{\bm{Q}}^{L}_{k}
in (
10
) and (
11
), respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}^{L}_{k}\leq{\bm{Q}}_{k}\leq{\bm{Q}}^{U}_{k}.

The detailed derivations are given in Appendix Section
D
. Note that our construction of the upper and lower comparison systems differs from that of
Lee and He 2019
such that the noise term
{\bm{w}}_{k}
does not include the current iterate
{\bm{Q}}_{k}
. This enables us to apply i.i.d. concentration bound on
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}{\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}
, which will be clear in the subsequent subsection.

Note that under the event
{\mathcal{E}}
, for
k\geq m
,
\left\|{\bm{A}}^{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1-(1-\gamma)\alpha
for all
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that the event
{\mathcal{E}}
holds. Then,
\left\|{\bm{A}}^{{\bm{Q}}}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1-(1-\gamma)\alpha
for
k\geq m
and
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}
.

The proof is given in Appendix Section
C.2
. Note that if
{\bm{w}}_{k}
is a zero vector for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then from (
10
) and (
11
), we will have
\left\|\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\left\|\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}
converging to a zero vector at geometric rate of
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\exp(-1-(1-\gamma)\alpha k)\right)
from Lemma
3.4
. However,
{\bm{w}}_{k}
is not a zero vector, and we will show that
{\bm{w}}_{k}
can be controlled by the concentration inequality given in the subsequent section.

3.3
Concentration inequality for
{\bm{w}}_{k}

In this section, we provide a concentration inequality for
{\bm{w}}_{k}
in (
8
). The concentration inequality for
{\bm{w}}_{k}
will play an important role in deriving the sample complexity result. In particular, from the law of large numbers, we would expect
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}\to{\bm{P}}
and
\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}\to{\bm{R}}
as the number of samples increases, yielding
{\bm{w}}_{k}
asymptotically converging to a zero vector. Concentration inequalities can characterize how many samples are required for achieving desired level of an accuracy. The following lemma describes a concentration inequality for
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}
and
\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}
:

Lemma 3.5
.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|(\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{k}-{\bm{P}}){\bm{\Pi}}^{{\bm{Q}}^{*}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq{\epsilon}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{\exp(kd_{\min}(1-\gamma)^{2}{\epsilon}^{2}/4)},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|\hat{{\bm{R}}}_{k}-{\bm{R}}\right\|_{\infty}\geq{\epsilon}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 3|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|\exp(-kd_{\min}{\epsilon}^{2}/4),

for
{\epsilon}^{2}\in\left[0,\min\left\{3,\frac{3}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right\}\right]
.

The proof follows from applying standard concentration inequalities for i.i.d. random variables and is given in Appendix Section
C.3
. Similar concentration ienqualities have been also used in
Azar et al. 2012
.

Now, applying the union bound to the above result yields concentration inequality for
{\bm{w}}_{k}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\|{\bm{w}}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\geq{\epsilon}\right]\leq 6|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|\exp(-kd_{\min}(1-\gamma)^{2}{\epsilon}^{2}/16),

for
{\epsilon}^{2}\in\left[0,\min\left\{12,\frac{12\gamma^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right\}\right]
.

The proof is given in Appendix Section
C.4
.

3.4
Sample complexity of Algorihtm
1

In this section, we provide a sample complexity to achieve
{\epsilon}
-accurate estimate of the optimal
Q
-function, i.e.,
\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq{\epsilon}
. Noting that the upper and lower comparison systems in (
10
) and (
11
), respectively, share the same noise term
{\bm{w}}_{k}
, both systems can be viewed as particular case of the following recursion:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{A}}^{{\bm{y}}_{k}}_{k}{\bm{x}}_{k}+\alpha{\bm{w}}_{k},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|},

(12)

where
\{{\bm{y}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is arbitrary sequence of vectors. If
{\bm{x}}_{0}=\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{0}
and
{\bm{y}}_{k}={\bm{Q}}_{k},\;k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
{\bm{x}}_{k}
coincides with
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{U}_{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Likewise, when
{\bm{x}}_{0}=\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{0}
and
{\bm{y}}_{k}={\bm{Q}}^{*}
, then
{\bm{x}}_{k}
coincides with
\tilde{{\bm{Q}}}^{L}_{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore, it suffices to bound
\left\|{\bm{x}}_{k}\right\|_{\infty}
from the relation (
9
). Recursively expanding (
12
) and applying concentration inequality on
{\bm{w}}_{k}
from Lemma
3.6
would yield the following result:

Theorem 3.7
.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\left\|{\bm{Q}}_{k}-{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq{\epsilon}
, with at most following number of samples:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}d_{\min}}+\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)\alpha}\right),

for
{\epsilon}^{2}\in\left[0,\frac{36}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\min\left\{12,3\frac{\gamma^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right\}\right]
and
\delta\in(0,1)
.

The proof is given in Appendix Section
C.5
. As in the literature of non-asymptotic analysis of RL algorithms, our sample complexity bound depends on the so-called effective horizon
\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
and minimum value of the sampling distribution
d_{\min}
. We improve over the sample complexity result of
Lee 2022
, which provided
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{(|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|)^{4}}{(1-\gamma)^{6}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
under the step-size
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Our bound implies sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
(with
d_{\min}=1/(|{\cal S}||{\cal A}|)
) which matches the lower bound on the sample complexity of
Q
-learning in
Li et al. 2023
. Compared to
Li et al. 2023
, which restricts step-size to
(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}
, we allow the general step-size
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Now, we provide comparison with existing works that matches the lower bound on the sample complexity of RL algorithms,
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
.
Azar et al. 2012
has proved lower bound of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
to all RL algorithms and
Q
-value iteration achieves this sample complexity bound. It requires a generative model (or a sampling oracle), which is a sampling model that we can access any state-action pair in the environment on our choice and query the next state following the transition probability. Therefore, the sampling method in
Azar et al. 2012
is more idealistic compared to ours. Moreover, as mentioned in Remark
3.1
, it is not an online algorithm, where a single sample is observed every iteration.
Wainwright 2019
applied a variance-reduction technique
[
Johnson and Zhang 2013
]
to the
Q
-learning with diminishing step-size, and proved that it can achieve sample complexity
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{|{\mathcal{S}}||{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}{\epsilon}^{2}}\right)
, which is the optimal sample complexity of RL algorithms. The variance reduction technique can also be considered as implicitly estimating the model because the parameter, that the variance reduction technique tries to approximate, includes the transition matrix and reward function. As previously mentioned,
Wainwright 2019
relies on generative model and uses diminishing step-size, whereas we focus on online sampling setting and constant step-size.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the non-asymptotic behavior and empirical performance of SyncMBQ, which is direct extension of
Q
-learning to the model-based setting. We have considered online learning setting and relaxed sampling model which is weaker assumption than the generative model assumption. Furthermore, we proved that SyncMBQ can achieve the optimal sample complexity of
Q
-learning with general step-size. Moreover, we developed a new switched system model for
Q
-learning, and experimentally verified the superiority of SyncMBQ over
Q
-learning. Future studies include considering the setting where we collect samples following a single trajectory and dealing with the exploration-exploitation dilemma.
