Existence and partial regularity of Legendrian area-minimizing currents
Gerard Orriols

Abstract
. We show that Legendrian integral currents in a contact manifold that locally minimize the mass among Legendrian competitors have a regular set which is open and dense in their support. We apply this to show existence and partial regularity of solutions of the Legendrian Plateau problem in the n n th Heisenberg group for an arbitrary horizontal ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -cycle as prescribed boundary, and of mass-minimizing Legendrian integral currents in any n n -dimensional homology class of a closed contact ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) (2n+1) -manifold. In the case of the Heisenberg group, our result applies to Ambrosio–Kirchheim metric currents with respect to the Carnot–Carathéodory distance. Our results do not assume any compatibility between the subriemannian metric and the symplectic form.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to lay the foundations of an existence and regularity theory of area-minimizing currents among Legendrian integral currents in contact manifolds of arbitrary dimension. The main motivation for applying the methods of classical Geometric Measure Theory to Legendrian submanifolds comes from the project initiated by Schoen and Wolfson to develop a variational theory for the area of Lagrangian submanifolds in symplectic manifolds
[
SW99
,
SW01
,
SW03
]
.

There are many geometric reasons to study minimizers of the area among Lagrangian submanifolds. It is natural to ask whether a homology class in a symplectic manifold which admits a Lagrangian representative contains one of minimal area which enjoys some partial regularity, as in the unconstrained case. Moreover, a good understanding of the homology minimization problem for Lagrangian currents seems to be fundamental before exploring the much more delicate topic of regularity of Hamiltonian-stationary Lagrangian surfaces and of existence of global area-minimizers in Hamiltonian isotopy classes, of which very little is known. See
[
Oh93
]
for an introduction to the problem and some motivating conjectures and
[
Vit00
]
for a lower bound on the area for Hamiltonian isotopy classes in some ambient spaces.

A deeper motivation to study this minimization problem comes from mirror symmetry and the SYZ conjecture: when the symplectic manifold is Calabi–Yau, hypothetically there should exist many special Lagrangian submanifolds, which are calibrated and hence absolute homology minimizers. Therefore one expects to be able to find them by minimizing area among Lagrangian integral currents in homology classes which admit them. A very short and elegant computation of Schoen and Wolfson
[
SW01
]
shows that this strategy can be realized, not only in the Calabi–Yau setting but more generally in Kähler–Einstein manifolds, and indeed Lagrangian-stationary closed surfaces are automatically minimal (if the ambient manifold is Calabi–Yau then they must be special Lagrangian). However, for their argument to work, it is fundamental to know that this object is regular enough, and in fact Micallef and Wolfson later found examples of homology classes where singularities cannot be avoided
[
MW06
,
Wol05
]
.

It was observed by Schoen and Wolfson, building on an example of Minicozzi
[
Min95
]
, that Lagrangian area-minimizing surfaces can be very badly behaved unless they satisfy an exactness condition. More precisely, Minicozzi showed that cylinders of the form
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}^{4}
are area-minimizing among Lagrangian surfaces and, unlike classical area minimizers, they do not satisfy a monotonicity formula for the area (not even a universal lower bound for the mass ratio in a ball). Unless one can somehow control the presence of regions of this type, developing a regularity theory in this generality seems far out of reach
1

which do not assume exactness of the surface but rely on the a-priori square integrability of the mean curvature, which already excludes many interesting singularities like the Schoen–Wolfson cones.
.

However, assuming that a smooth Lagrangian surface
\Sigma^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
is exact (that is, the Liouville form
\lambda
, which satisfies
\mathrm{d}\lambda=\omega
, restricts to zero on
\Sigma

\tilde{\Sigma}
as a surface in the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{5}
, and the key insight of
[
SW01
]
was to show that this lift satisfies a monotonicity formula assuming just that
\Sigma
is stationary under Hamiltonian deformations. This is due to the larger richness of variations and competitors available in the Legendrian setting. By extensively using this monotonicity formula and techniques exclusive of dimension two (such as a conformal parametrization, energy methods and holomorphic maps), Schoen and Wolfson succeeded in developing an optimal and rather complete regularity theory for minimizers of the mapping problem among Legendrian surfaces, and applied it to the Lagrangian setting. Their main result states that such minimizers are smooth surfaces except at a finite number of points, which are either of branch type (around which the parametrization is smooth) or of cone type (around which the parametrization is Lipschitz). The Hamiltonian-stationary two-dimensional cones are classified, and among them also the stable ones, but to the best of our knowledge it is still an open question to determine which of them are minimizing.

The extension to higher dimensions of the monotonicity formula for Hamiltonian-stationary Legendrian submanifolds was announced in
[
SW99
, Theorem 3]
. We provide a counterexample in
Appendix

B
, showing that a different approach is needed and that the regularity of Hamiltonian-stationary surfaces in higher dimensions is more delicate than the regularity of Legendrian minimizing currents carried out here, where the lower bound in the mass density will come from an isoperimetric inequality.

Even in dimension two, the monotonicity formula of
[
SW01
]
is not explicit, and its construction goes through the solution of an auxiliary hyperbolic PDE. Note however that a newer simplified almost monotonicity formula has been recently found by Rivière
[
Riv24
]
, still in dimension two, and extended to parametrized Hamiltonian-stationary varifolds by Rivière and Pigati
[
PR24
]
. This monotonicity formula has been applied by Rivière to solve min-max problems for Legendrian surfaces in Sasakian
5
-manifolds
[
Riv23
]
, thus generalizing the pure minimization problem.

In addition to that, the proof of the decay of the excess in
[
SW01
]
has the added difficulty of controlling the parametrization of the surface at each step, which makes it quite technical. In fact, an attempt to understand and simplify their arguments with the help of newly available tools was the starting point of this work.

In contrast to the slower progress in the GMT problem in arbitrary dimensions, the last few years have seen many new results about the PDE that governs the local picture for graphs. Since Legendrian graphs are locally lifts of Lagrangian graphs, and Lagrangian variations agree locally with Hamiltonian variations, the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equation is precisely the Hamiltonian-stationary equation. This equation has a very special structure when the ambient manifold is
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and has been studied by Bhattacharya, Chen and Warren
[
CW19
,
BW19
]
. More recently, these authors have noticed that partial regularity results can be obtained without relying on this special structure, and therefore hold on arbitrary symplectic manifolds
[
BCW23
]
.

Even more recently, Bhattacharya and Skorobogatova
[
BS23
]
have shown, still in the graphical setting, that smallness of the average oscillation of the Hessian of the potential
u
in a ball, which corresponds to smallness of the excess of the graph in a cylinder, is enough to deduce regularity. Nevertheless they need to know a priori that
\|\mathrm{D}^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1-\eta
to guarantee that the area functional is convex, and their required smallness of the excess depends on this
\eta
. Inspired by their theorem, in our main result,
Theorem

4.7
, we remove the Lipschitz graph assumption and, working with currents, we prove an
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem for Legendrian area-minimizers analogous to the classical ones of De Giorgi
[
De 61
]
and Almgren
[
Alm68
]
for minimal surfaces.

At the same time, as with high-codimension minimal surfaces, the Euler–Lagrange PDE for graphs lacks an existence theory of weak solutions and a satisfactory functional space to search for minimizers. The framework of Legendrian currents developed here provides a solution to these issues and furthermore allows for boundary data which cannot be expressed as a graph.

1.1.
Main results

We state here our main existence and regularity results. The relevant notions will be introduced in the next two sections. For now, note that
\mathbb{H}^{n}
denotes the Heisenberg group of topological dimension
2n+1
with its standard contact structure and metric on the horizontal planes; a horizontal integral current is just an integral current almost all of whose tangent planes are isotropic, and a Legendrian current is a horizontal current of dimension
n
.

Theorem 1.1

(Legendrian Plateau problem)
.

Let
S
be a compactly supported
(n-1)
-dimensional horizontal integral current in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
\partial S=0
. Then there exists a compactly supported Legendrian integral current
T
with
\partial T=S
such that
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(T^{\prime})
for any other Legendrian integral current
T^{\prime}
with
\partial T^{\prime}=S
.

Moreover there exists an open set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
\spt T\cap\mathcal{U}
is a real-analytic embedded
n
-dimensional Legendrian submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\spt T\cap\mathcal{U}
is dense in
\spt T\setminus\spt S
.

This theorem can also be stated in terms of Ambrosio–Kirchheim metric currents with respect to the intrinsic Carnot–Carathéodory distance of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, but we have preferred to work with Legendrian Federer–Fleming currents to take advantage of the homological theory which is available for them, of the larger literature that uses them, and of their better compatibility with Lagrangian currents in a symplectic manifold. Nonetheless we have to use metric currents crucially in a portion of our arguments, and we have recast our results into that language in
Remark

A.4
.

We also remark that the Legendrian Plateau problem had already been studied by Minicozzi in his PhD thesis
[
Min94
]
and a penalization approach to solve it is explained in
[
SW03
]
.

The assumption that the ambient manifold is the Heisenberg group is not essential here, and the Plateau problem can be solved as well in compact manifolds or in noncompact manifolds with contolled geometry at infinity. For closed manifolds we have the following result
2

2

Here we assume that the ambient manifold is smooth—see
Section

4
for more precise versions of the regularity statements under weaker assumptions.
:

Theorem 1.2

(Legendrian homology minimizers)
.

Let
(M^{2n+1},\Xi,g)
be a smooth closed contact manifold with contact distribution
\Xi
and a smooth subriemannian metric
g
defined on
\Xi
. Let
\mathfrak{a}\in H_{n}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be a homology class. Then there exists a Legendrian integral current
T
representing
\mathfrak{a}
such that
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(T^{\prime})
for any other Legendrian integral current
T^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{a}
.

Moreover there exists an open set
U\subset M
such that
\spt T\cap U
is a smooth embedded
n
-dimensional Legendrian submanifold of
M
and
\spt T\cap U
is dense in
\spt T
.

The proof, both of the existence and (mainly) of the partial regularity, makes use of the isoperimetric and coning inequalities for horizontal currents in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which have been recently established by Basso, Wenger and Young
[
BWY23
]
in the context of metric currents. These results give us the basic ingredients to build up a regularity theory in the absence of a monotonicity formula, as pioneered by Almgren for currents which minimize an elliptic functional
[
Alm68
]
. In fact, our regularity theorem also works in great generality, without assuming any compatibility condition between the metric and the symplectic form (in particular, an almost complex structure may not exist anywhere), and with minor modifications can be extended to functionals more general than an anisotropic area.

Although the ideas of the regularity proof were pioneered by Almgren and De Giorgi, we follow more closely the simplifications introduced by Schoen–Simon
[
SS82
]
and Ambrosio–De Lellis–Schmidt
[
ADS18
]
in the proof of the decay of the excess, approximating our current by (roughly) the graph of the gradient of the solution of a fourth order linear PDE and using classical
L^{p}
estimates to control the error terms.

We remark that, whereas the existence theory works as well for horizontal currents of any dimension
k<n
(see Theorems
3.12
and
3.15
), our regularity theory relies crucially on the representation of our current as the graph of a gradient over a Legendrian plane, which is only available when
k=n
. Even if the surface is given as a graph over a horizontal
k
-plane, we are not aware of any results in the literature that deal with the corresponding system of PDE, in contrast with the larger literature on the hamiltonian stationary equation. In the parametrized case, however, Qiu
[
Qiu03
]
successfully extended the theory of Schoen and Wolfson to isotropic surfaces in any dimension. In our opinion, the extension of these results to higher ambient dimensions is an interesting problem.

We believe that a more precise description of the singular set should be possible, at least in two dimensions and in the Heisenberg group. The key difficulty here would be to develop a blowup analysis around branch points. It is natural to conjecture that one would recover the result obtained by Schoen and Wolfson in the parametrized setting: a locally finite set of singular points consisting of branch points and Schoen–Wolfson conical singularities.

We also hope to be able to apply our results soon to variational problems for Lagrangian surfaces; in particular there are some issues to be addressed regarding the notions of exactness and Legendrian lift for integral currents. But beyond this application, we believe that our result has many other points of interest. On one hand, contact geometry has become a subject on its own and the study of canonical representatives of their homology is a natural and basic question to study. On the other hand, from the perspective of metric geometry, we provide one of the very few available partial regularity results for high codimension minimal surfaces in non-smooth metric spaces (compare with
[
ADS18
]
for Hilbert spaces and with
[
Sta21
]
for parametrized two-dimensional surfaces in
\operatorname{CAT}(0)
spaces). In addition to that, the techniques developed here may be applied to other geometric variational problems in which Legendrian currents appear as unit normal bundles—see the survey
[
Ber03
]
and the references therein for more details.

Preliminaries and notation

2.1.
The Heisenberg group

Most of our analysis will take place in the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, because thanks to the Darboux theorem (
Proposition

2.1
), it is a local model for the geometry of a contact manifold with good homogeneity properties. Here we summarize the main geometric and analytical facts that we will use in the sequel. For the notation, we use mostly the conventions of
[
Cap+07
]
; we also refer to this book for further information and references.

We will often identify
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
by using the globally defined exponential coordinates
(\mathbf{z},\varphi)=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)
. Here
\mathbf{z}
denotes an element of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which we will identify with
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
denote
n
-tuples of real numbers corresponding to the real and imaginary part of
\mathbf{z}
, respectively.

In these coordinates, the group operations are

(2.1)

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)\cdot(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\left(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\varphi+\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}^{\prime}-\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)\right)

and

(2.2)

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)^{-1}=(-\mathbf{x},-\mathbf{y},-\varphi).

The Heisenberg group is naturally a contact manifold with global contact form

(2.3)

\theta:=\mathrm{d}\varphi-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}),

which defines a hyperplane distribution
\Xi=\Xi_{\mathbb{H}}
by
\Xi_{\xi}:=\ker\theta(\xi)
. As all Lie groups, the Heisenberg group is homogeneous and parallelizable. In particular, left translations allow us to identify all tangent spaces. Moreover, it can be checked that
\theta
is left-invariant and therefore the horizontal bundle
\Xi_{\mathbb{H}}
can also be trivialized by using left translations. In what follows, we will use this observation extensively and identify the horizontal spaces at all points with
\Xi_{0}
.

We endow
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with a subriemannian metric
g_{\mathbb{H}}
, which we will also write as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, defined as follows: take
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\Xi_{0}
to be the standard metric of
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n}\simeq\Xi_{0}\subset T_{0}\mathbb{H}^{n}
, and extend it to the whole
\Xi_{\mathbb{H}}
in a left-invariant way.

For any horizontal
k
-plane
\pi
(
1\leq k\leq 2n
) and any smooth function
f
, we denote by
\nabla^{\pi}f
the unique vector in
\pi
such that
\langle v,\nabla^{\pi}f\rangle=\mathrm{d}f(v)
for any vector
v\in\pi
. In the case that
\pi
is the whole horizontal
2n
-space
\Xi
, we denote
\nabla^{\pi}f
as
\nabla^{H}f
. Then
\nabla^{\pi}f
is the (Riemannian) orthogonal projection of
\nabla^{H}f
onto
\pi
with respect to the Riemannian metric on
\Xi
.

For the coordinate functions, it is easy to check that

(2.4)

\nabla^{H}x^{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}-\frac{1}{2}y^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^{H}y^{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}+\frac{1}{2}x^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi},

since all vector fields involved are left-invariant and agree at the origin, where they form the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Thus, by definition,
\{\nabla^{H}x^{i},\nabla^{H}y^{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n}
form an orthonormal basis of
\Xi
for the standard subriemannian metric.

As is customary, we will work with the Folland–Korányi gauge

(2.5)

\tau(\mathbf{z},\varphi):=\|(\mathbf{z},\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{FK}}:=\left(|\mathbf{z}|^{4}+16\varphi^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Cygan
[
Cyg81
]
proved that
\|\xi\xi^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{FK}}\leq\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{FK}}+\|\xi^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{FK}}
and therefore it defines a left-invariant distance

(2.6)

\dist_{\mathrm{FK}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime}):=\|\xi^{-1}\xi^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{FK}}

whose balls will be denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{r}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{FK}}<r\}
. This distance is (bilipschitz) equivalent to the Carnot–Carathéodory distance, but for convenience we will use the former most of the time. In particular, it is smooth away from the origin and enjoys the following useful property: the function
\tau:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\tau(\xi)=\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{FK}},
satisfies

(2.7)

|\nabla^{\pi}\tau|\leq|\nabla^{H}\tau|=\frac{|\mathbf{z}|}{\tau}\leq 1

for every horizontal
k
-plane
\pi
. This follows from a computation:

\displaystyle(4\tau^{3}|\nabla^{H}\tau|)^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla^{H}\tau^{4}|^{2}=|\nabla^{H}|\mathbf{z}|^{4}+16\nabla^{H}\varphi^{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla^{H}|\mathbf{z}|^{4}|^{2}+2^{8}|\nabla^{H}\varphi^{2}|^{2}+32\langle\nabla^{H}|\mathbf{z}|^{4},\nabla^{H}\varphi^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=4|\mathbf{z}|^{4}|\nabla^{H}|\mathbf{z}|^{2}|^{2}+2^{10}\varphi^{2}|\nabla^{H}\varphi|^{2}+2^{7}|\mathbf{z}|^{2}\varphi\langle\nabla^{H}|\mathbf{z}|^{2},\nabla^{H}\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=16|\mathbf{z}|^{4}|\mathbf{z}|^{2}+2^{8}\varphi^{2}|\mathbf{z}|^{2}=16|\mathbf{z}|^{2}\tau^{4}.

2.2.
Contact manifolds

For simplicity, we will follow
[
Bla10
]
and will consider only contact manifolds admitting a global contact form. All our arguments generalize easily to manifolds that are not co-orientable. Hence a
contact manifold
is a smooth manifold
M^{2n+1}
equipped with a
1
-form
\theta
such that
\theta\wedge(\mathrm{d}\theta)^{n}\neq 0
everywhere. In fact, we will only be interested in the
2n
-dimensional plane distribution
\Xi
given by
\ker\theta
, so all of the geometric notions discussed below will be invariant under scaling
\theta
by a nonvanishing function.

It is clear that the
1
-form
\theta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
from (
2.3
) defines a global contact form on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, since

\theta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}\theta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\right)^{n}=\theta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\wedge\left(\sum_{k}\mathrm{d}x^{k}\wedge\mathrm{d}y^{k}\right)^{n}=n!\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}y^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{n}\wedge\mathrm{d}y^{n}\neq 0.

To study the volume of submanifolds, we will need a Riemannian metric
g
defined on the planes
\Xi
, a so-called subriemannian structure on
M
.
Then we can state a subriemannian version of Darboux’s theorem as follows:

Proposition 2.1
.

For every point
\xi
of a contact manifold
(M^{2n+1},\theta)
there is a neighborhood
U\ni\xi
, an open set
0\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a diffeomorphism
\phi:U\to\mathcal{U}
such that
\theta=\phi^{*}\theta_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
.

Moreover, if
M
is compact and
g
is a Riemannian metric on the contact distribution
\Xi=\ker\theta
, there exist constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda<\infty
such that

(2.8)

\lambda\phi^{*}g_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\leq g\leq\Lambda\phi^{*}g_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}.

Proof.

The first part is just a restatement of the classical Darboux theorem for contact manifolds, see for example
[
Bla10
]
. The second part just follows from the compactness of
M
by covering it with finitely many Darboux charts.

2.3.
Biharmonic functions

Here we collect some of the classical estimates for solutions to elliptic fourth order equations with constant coefficients:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(a_{ik}^{jl})_{i,j,k,l=1}^{n}
be real numbers satisfying the boundedness condition
|a_{ik}^{jl}|\leq\Lambda_{0}
and the Legendre ellipticity condition,
a_{ik}^{jl}\sigma_{ij}\sigma_{kl}\geq\lambda_{0}|\sigma|^{2}
for every
n\times n
symmetric matrix
(\sigma_{ij})
.

Then for any
f\in W^{2,\infty}(B_{R})
there exists a unique function
u:B_{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which is in
W^{2,p}(B_{R})
for every
1\leq p<\infty
and solves the Dirichlet problem

(2.9)

\begin{cases}a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ijkl}u=0&\text{in }B_{R}\\
u=f&\text{on }\partial B_{R}\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\partial_{\nu}f&\text{on }\partial B_{R}.\end{cases}

Moreover,
u
is smooth inside
B_{R}
and satisfies the interior estimates

(2.10)

\sup_{B_{r}}|\mathrm{D}^{2+k}u|^{2}\leq C_{k}\frac{1}{(R-r)^{n+2k}}\int_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\qquad\text{for any }0<r<R\text{ and any integer }k\geq 0,

(2.11)

\sup_{B_{r}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u-\mathrm{D}^{2}u(0)|^{2}\leq C\frac{r^{2}}{R^{n}}\int_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\qquad\text{for any }0<r<\frac{R}{2},

the global
L^{p}
estimate

(2.12)

\int_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{p}\leq C_{p}\int_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}f|^{p}\qquad\text{for any }1<p<\infty

and Agmon’s maximum estimate for the derivative:

(2.13)

\sup_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}u|\leq C\sup_{\partial B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}u|\leq C\sup_{B_{R}}|\mathrm{D}f|.

Here the constants depend only on
n,\Lambda_{0}/\lambda_{0}
and their subindices.

Proof.

The existence, uniqueness and interior estimates are classical, see for example
[
Agm10
]
. The estimates up to the boundary follow from
[
ADN59
]
, and Agmon’s maximum principle originally appeared in
[
Agm60
]
. See also the book
[
GGS10
]
and the references therein.

3.
Horizontal currents and existence of minimizers

In order to do calculus of variations with Legendrian submanifolds, as in the classical Plateau problem, we need to enlarge our class of objects to a space that includes weaker objects but enjoys better compactness properties. Based on the success of the Federer–Fleming theory of integral currents
[
FF60
]
, which is based on the De Rham complex of differential forms, Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano introduced in
[
FSS07
]
a class of objects in the Heisenberg group that, in low dimensions, generalizes horizontal submanifolds. As with Federer–Fleming currents, these objects are defined in duality with a differential complex introduced by Rumin
[
Rum94
]
in any contact manifold.

Here we work in an arbitrary contact manifold
M^{2n+1}
and take our class of horizontal currents to be the Rumin currents of dimension at most
n
. In these dimensions, these currents can be characterized as Federer–Fleming currents in which the contact form and its exterior derivative restrict to zero. In the case of the Heisenberg group, integral Rumin currents correspond to integral metric currents, in the sense of Ambrosio–Kirchheim
[
AK00
]
, with respect to the Carnot–Carathéodory metric (see
Appendix

A
for more details).

For Federer–Fleming currents, we follow the conventions of
[
Sim83
]
; in particular we allow our integral currents to have globally infinite mass.

3.1.
The Rumin complex and horizontal currents

We begin by recalling the Rumin complex in low dimensions. Let
U\subset M
be an open set in which
\Xi
is determined by the
1
-form
\theta
, and let
0\leq k\leq n
. We define the differential ideal generated by
\Xi
as

(3.1)

\mathscr{I}^{k}(U):=\{\theta\wedge\alpha+\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta:\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(U),\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{k-2}(U)\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{k}(U).

Notice that
\mathscr{I}^{k}
only depends on
\Xi
and not on
\theta
, and thus makes sense even if
\Xi
is not the kernel of a single
1
-form in
U
.
The (lower half of the) Rumin complex is the chain complex

(3.2)

\mathcal{D}^{0}_{\mathrm{R}}(U)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{D}^{1}_{\mathrm{R}}(U)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{d}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{D}^{n-1}_{\mathrm{R}}(U)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{D}^{n}_{\mathrm{R}}(U)

where

(3.3)

\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{R}}(U):=\mathcal{D}^{k}(U)/\mathscr{I}^{k}(U)

and the differential
\mathrm{d}
clearly descends to the quotients.

Notice that a linear map
T:\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathcal{R}}(U)\to\mathbb{R}
can be identified with a map
T:\mathcal{D}^{k}(U)\to\mathbb{R}
which vanishes on
\mathscr{I}^{k}
. Thus it is natural to define

Definition 3.1

(Horizontal current)
.

A De Rham current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(U)
is called
horizontal
if

(3.4)

T(\theta\wedge\alpha+\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta)=0

for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(U)
and
\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{k-2}(U)
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
the set of horizontal currents in
U
, and we call them
Legendrian
when
k=n
.

Horizontal submanifolds (or currents) are sometimes called
isotropic
in the literature.
It is immediate from the definition that if
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
, then
\partial T\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k-1}(U)
. It is also clear that
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
is closed in
\mathcal{D}_{k}(U)
with respect to weak convergence. Next we need a subriemannian notion of mass. We start with the following definition:

Definition 3.2

(Horizontal mass and comass)
.

Let
\omega\in\bigwedge^{k}T^{*}_{\xi}M
. We define its
horizontal comass
as

(3.5)

\|\omega\|^{\mathrm{hor}}_{*}:=\sup\{\omega(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}):v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\Xi_{\xi}\subset T_{\xi}M,|v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}|_{g}\leq 1\}.

For a current
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
, we let its
horizontal mass
be

(3.6)

\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T):=\sup\{T(\omega):\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(U),\|\omega\|^{\mathrm{hor}}_{*}\leq 1\text{ in }U\}.

In order to make use of the Federer–Fleming theory it will be convenient to extend the metric
g
, only defined on the distribution
\Xi
, to a Riemannian metric
g_{0}
on the whole
TM
and then to embed
(M,g_{0})
into some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{L}
using Nash’s theorem. The following proposition shows that the relevant notions are independent of this extension.

Proposition 3.3
.

The horizontal mass enjoys the following properties:

(1)

For any current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
,
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)
holds.

(2)

Given
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
with
\mathbf{M}(T)<\infty
, we have
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)<\infty
if and only if
T(\theta\wedge\alpha)=0
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}
. Moreover, in that case
\mathbf{M}(T)=\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)
.

(3)

A current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
satisfies
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)+\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(\partial T)<\infty
if and only if
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}
and
\mathbf{M}(T)+\mathbf{M}(\partial T)<\infty
.

In particular,
\mathbf{M}(T)
and
\mathbf{M}(\partial T)
do not depend on the extension
g_{0}
of
g
for
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}
.

Proof.

Clearly
\|\omega\|^{\mathrm{hor}}_{*}\leq\|\omega\|_{*}
, so
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)
for any current
T
, which proves (i).

We now show (ii): suppose first that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)<\infty
. Since
\|\theta\wedge\alpha\|^{\mathrm{hor}}_{*}=0<1
, we have that
|T(s\theta\wedge\alpha)|\leq\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)
for any
s>0
, so by letting
s\to\infty
it follows that
T(\theta\wedge\alpha)=0
.

To prove the opposite implication we will show that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(T)
, which together with (i) yields the equality. First let
X
be the smooth vector field everywhere
g_{0}
-orthogonal to
\Xi
such that
\theta(X)\equiv 1
. Given
\omega\in\mathcal{D}^{k}
with
\|\omega\|^{\mathrm{hor}}_{*}\leq 1
, consider the
k
-form
\omega^{\prime}:=X\slot(\theta\wedge\omega)
.

We claim that
\|\omega^{\prime}\|_{*}\leq 1
everywhere: indeed, fix
\xi\in M
and let
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}\in\bigwedge^{k}T_{\xi}M
span the
k
-plane
V
with
|v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}|_{g_{0}}\leq 1
. If
V
is not horizontal, by choosing an orthonormal basis of the
(k-1)
-plane
V\cap\Xi_{\xi}
and completing it to an orthonormal basis of
V
, we can suppose that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1}\in\Xi_{\xi}
and write
v_{k}=v_{k}^{\prime}+sX
for some
v_{k}^{\prime}\in\Xi_{\xi}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, since
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k-1}\wedge v_{k}^{\prime}
is
g_{0}
-orthogonal to
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k-1}\wedge sX
, it holds that

|v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k-1}\wedge v_{k}^{\prime}|_{g}\leq|v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}|_{g_{0}}\leq 1,

which implies that

(X\slot\theta\wedge\omega)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=(\theta\wedge\omega)(X,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=(\theta\wedge\omega)(X,v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime})=\theta(X)\omega(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime}),

hence

\omega^{\prime}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\leq|\omega(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime})|\leq|v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}^{\prime}|_{g}\leq 1

and the claim is proven. Now observe that

\omega^{\prime}=X\slot(\theta\wedge\omega)=(X\slot\theta)\wedge\omega-\theta\wedge(X\slot\omega),

which implies that for any
T
satisfying
T(\theta\wedge\cdot)\equiv 0
,

T(\omega)=\theta(X)T(\omega)-T(\theta\wedge(X\slot\omega))=T(\omega^{\prime})\leq\mathbf{M}(T)

and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{hor}}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(T)
follows. Finally, (iii) is immediate from (ii) and the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}
.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

The groups of normal and integral horizontal currents in
U\subset M
are

(3.7)

\mathbf{N}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U):=\mathbf{N}_{k}(U)\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U):=\mathbf{I}_{k}(U)\cap\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U).

The Federer–Fleming compactness theorem
[
Sim83
, Theorem 27.3]
immediately gives:

Proposition 3.5
.

If
\{T_{j}\}\subset\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
is a sequence with
\|T_{j}\|(W)+\|\partial T_{j}\|(W)\leq C(W)
for every
W\Subset U
, then a subsequence converges weakly to a current
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
.

It is well known that for a rectifiable current
T
and
\|T\|
-almost every point
p
, if
\eta_{p,\rho}
denotes the map
q\mapsto\frac{q-p}{\rho}
in any coordinates around
p
, then
(\eta_{p,\rho})_{\#}T\rightharpoonup\Theta(\|T\|,p)T_{p}
as
\rho\searrow 0
, where
T_{p}
is the current induced by a plane called the tangent plane at
p
. For horizontal currents we have the following characterization:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
T
be a rectifiable
k
-current in
U
. Then
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(U)
if and only if for
\|T\|
-almost every
p\in U
, the approximate tangent plane
T_{p}
is isotropic, which means that both
\theta
and
\mathrm{d}\theta
vanish on it.

Proof.

If all tangent planes are isotropic, then (
3.4
) is immediate by the integral representation formula for
T
. For the reciprocal, we just show that
\theta_{p}
vanishes on
T_{p}
(the computation for
(\mathrm{d}\theta)_{p}
is similar). Since the statement is local and invariant by diffeomorphisms, we may suppose that
T
is a current in a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
p=0
(in our case,
m=2n+1
, and clearly the statement is much more general).
Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and write, for short,
\eta_{\rho}:=\eta_{0,\rho}
. Suppose that
\alpha
is supported in
B_{R}
. Then

T_{p}(\theta_{p}\wedge\alpha)=\lim_{\rho\searrow 0}(\eta_{\rho})_{\#}T(\theta_{p}\wedge\alpha)=\lim_{\rho\searrow 0}T(\eta_{\rho}^{*}\theta_{p}\wedge\eta_{\rho}^{*}\alpha)=\lim_{\rho\searrow 0}T((\eta_{\rho}^{*}\theta_{p}-\theta)\wedge\eta_{\rho}^{*}\alpha)

and thus

\displaystyle\left|T_{p}(\theta_{p}\wedge\alpha)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\rho\searrow 0}\|T\|(B_{\rho R})\sup_{B_{\rho R}}|\eta_{\rho}^{*}\theta_{p}-\theta|\sup_{\rho R}|\eta_{\rho}^{*}\alpha|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\rho\searrow 0}\|T\|(B_{\rho R})C\rho^{1-k}\leq\limsup_{\rho\searrow 0}C\rho=0.\qed

As a consequence, in the coarea formula for horizontal currents we can replace the gradient of the slicing function by its horizontal gradient. Not only that, but thanks to the work of Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
AK00
,
AK00a
]
and the discussion in
Appendix

A
, we can slice by any function which is Lipschitz with respect to the intrinsic Carnot–Carathéodory distance. In order to make our presentation more self-contained, however, we will just state the following result, which follows from the Euclidean coarea formula and will suffice for us.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
T
be a horizontal rectifiable current in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with finite mass. Then

(3.8)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\mathbf{M}(T)

where
\tau:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the Folland–Korányi gauge
\tau(\xi)=\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{FK}}
.

Proof.

Since
\tau
is smooth and Lipschitz outside of
\mathcal{B}_{r}
for any
r>0
, it follows from (
2.7
) and the Euclidean coarea formula that

\int_{r}^{\infty}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{r}}|\nabla^{\vec{T}}\tau|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|=\mathbf{M}(T).

The proposition follows by just letting
r\searrow 0
.
∎

We finally notice that horizontal currents indeed generalize isotropic submanifolds:

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\Sigma^{k}\subset M^{2n+1}
be a
C^{1}
embedded isotropic submanifold with (possibly empty)
C^{1}
boundary (recall that this means that
T_{\xi}\Sigma\subset\Xi_{\xi}
for every
\xi\in\Sigma
). Then
\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(M)
.

Proof.

We have that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(M)
,

\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket(\theta\wedge\alpha)=\int_{\Sigma}\langle\theta\wedge\alpha,\vec{\tau}_{\Sigma}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{k}=0

because
\vec{\tau}_{\Sigma}
is a wedge product of vectors in
\Xi
. Since
T_{\xi}(\partial\Sigma)\subset T_{\xi}\Sigma
, it follows that
\partial\Sigma
is isotropic too and thus for any
\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{k-2}(M)
,

\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket(\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta)=\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket(\mathrm{d}(\theta\wedge\beta)+\theta\wedge\mathrm{d}\beta)=\partial\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket(\theta\wedge\beta)+\llbracket\Sigma\rrbracket(\theta\wedge\mathrm{d}\beta)=\llbracket\partial\Sigma\rrbracket(\theta\wedge\beta)=0.\qed

Remark 3.9
.

In light of this example, a natural question is whether any integer-rectifiable current
T
which annihilates
\theta
is automatically horizontal, that is, also annihilates
\mathrm{d}\theta
. This is in general false, and a counterexample can be found in
[
Fu04
]
. However, as shown in the same erratum, answer becomes positive if
T
is an integral current.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
T\in\mathbf{I}_{k}(M)
and suppose that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(M)
,
T(\theta\wedge\alpha)=0
. Then
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(M)
.

Proof.

This was already proved by Fu in
[
Fu04
]
, but we prefer to give the short proof here for the convenience of the reader and to fix some typos. Let
T
be such a current and let
E:=\spt(\partial T)
. Then
E
has locally finite
\mathcal{H}^{k-1}
measure, hence
\mathcal{H}^{k}(E)=0
which implies that
\|T\|(E)=0
.

Let
\rho:M\to\mathbb{R}
be the function
\dist(\cdot,E)
with respect to any Riemannian metric. Clearly
\rho
is Lipschitz, so the slices
\langle T,\rho,r\rangle
are well-defined integral currents for almost every
r>0
, and they are oriented by
\vec{T}\res\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{|\mathrm{d}\rho|}
. This implies that
\langle T,\rho,r\rangle(\theta\wedge\beta)=0
for each
\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{k-2}(M)
. We compute, for any such
\beta
and almost every
r>0
,

\displaystyle(T\res\{\rho>r\})(\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta)

\displaystyle=(T\res\{\rho>r\})(\mathrm{d}(\theta\wedge\beta)+\theta\wedge\mathrm{d}\beta)

\displaystyle=\partial(T\res\{\rho>r\})(\theta\wedge\beta)+(T\res\{\rho>r\})(\theta\wedge\mathrm{d}\beta)

\displaystyle=((\partial T)\res\{\rho>r\})(\theta\wedge\beta)-\langle T,\rho,r\rangle(\theta\wedge\beta)=0.

Hence, using the fact that
\|T\|(E)=0
and
\|T\|
is a Radon measure,

|T(\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta)|\leq\limsup_{r\searrow 0}|(T\res\{\rho\leq r\})(\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\beta)|\leq\sup_{M}(|\mathrm{d}\theta||\beta|)\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\|T\|\left(\{\rho\leq r\}\cap\spt\beta\right)=0.\qed

3.2.
Existence for the Plateau problem in the Heisenberg group

We first need a lemma to control the supports of a minimizing sequence.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
,
r>0
and
m>0
. Then for any current
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\spt\partial T\subset\mathcal{B}_{r}
and
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq m
we can find another current
\hat{T}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\partial\hat{T}=\partial T
,
\mathbf{M}(\hat{T})\leq\mathbf{M}(T)
and
\spt\hat{T}\subset\mathcal{B}_{R}
, where
R=r+Cm^{\frac{1}{k}}
for a constant
C=C(n,k)>0
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Let
R_{1}=r+\lambda m^{1/k}
, for a dimensional constant
\lambda>0
to be determined later, and consider the set

G=\left\{t\in[r,R_{1}]:\langle T,\tau,t\rangle\text{ exists and }\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)\leq\frac{2m}{R_{1}-r}\right\},

where
\tau
is the Folland–Korányi gauge (
2.5
). Then by (
2.7
) and the coarea formula we have that

\mathcal{L}^{1}([r,R_{1}]\setminus G)\leq\frac{R_{1}-r}{2m}\int_{[r,R_{1}]}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{R_{1}-r}{2m}\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\frac{1}{2}(R_{1}-r),

so that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(G)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(R_{1}-r)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda m^{1/k}
. Now for every
t\in G
, consider the current
Q_{t}
given by the isoperimetric inequality, which satisfies

\partial Q_{t}=\langle T,\tau,t\rangle,\qquad\mathbf{M}(Q_{t})\leq C_{1}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\spt Q_{t}\subset\mathcal{B}_{R},

where

R:=R_{1}+C\left(\frac{m}{R_{1}-r}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}=r+\lambda m^{1/k}+C\lambda^{-\frac{1}{k-1}}m^{1/k}.

This exists because
\partial\langle T,\tau,t\rangle=-\langle\partial T,\tau,t\rangle=0
. Then let
\hat{T}_{t}:=T\res\mathcal{B}_{t}-Q_{t}
, also supported in
\mathcal{B}_{R}
and with boundary

\partial\hat{T}_{t}=\partial(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t})-\partial Q_{t}=\langle T,\tau,t\rangle+(\partial T)\res\mathcal{B}_{t}-\langle T,\tau,t\rangle=\partial T.

Now if the lemma were false we must have
\mathbf{M}(T)<\mathbf{M}(\hat{T}_{t})
. Hence

\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t})+\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t}^{c})=\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(\hat{T}_{t})\leq\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t})+\mathbf{M}(Q_{t})\leq\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t})+C_{1}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}.

Letting
g(t):=\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t}^{c})
and using the coarea formula with (
2.7
), for almost every
t\in G
we obtain that

g(t)=\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{t}^{c})\leq C_{1}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,t\rangle)^{\frac{k}{k-1}}\leq C_{1}(-g^{\prime}(t))^{\frac{k}{k-1}}.

or

\left(-g^{1/k}\right)^{\prime}\geq C^{-1}.

Since
g^{1/k}
is monotonically decreasing, we can integrate over all of
[r,R_{1}]
and find that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda m^{1/k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}^{1}(G)\leq C\int_{[r,R_{1}]}\left(-g(t)^{1/k}\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(g(r)^{1/k}-g(R)^{1/k})

\displaystyle\leq Cg(r)^{1/k}\leq C\mathbf{M}(T)^{1/k}\leq Cm^{1/k},

which is a contradiction if
\lambda
is chosen large enough.
∎

Using this lemma the existence part of
Theorem

1.1
follows directly.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
and consider a current
S\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\spt S
compact. Then there exists a current
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
also with
\spt T
compact such that
\mathbf{M}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}(T^{\prime})
for any other current
T^{\prime}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\partial T^{\prime}=S
.

Proof.

Let
(T_{j})
be a minimizing sequence, that is,
T_{j}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
,
\partial T_{j}=S
and

\mathbf{M}(T_{j})\longrightarrow\inf\{\mathbf{M}(T^{\prime}):T^{\prime}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),\partial T^{\prime}=S\}.

Let
r>0
be such that
\spt S\subset\mathcal{B}_{r}
. By
Lemma

3.11
, since
\mathbf{M}(T_{j})
are bounded, we can improve our sequence to another minimizing sequence
(\hat{T}_{j})
with
\spt\hat{T}_{j}\subset\overline{\mathcal{B}_{R}}
for some
R>0
. Then by the compactness theorem and the lower semicontinuity of the mass, a subsequence converges to a current
T
belonging to the same class and supported in
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{R}}
, thus attaining the minimum.
∎

3.3.
Existence of Legendrian minimizers in a homology class

The existence part of
Theorem

1.2
follows easily using the compactness theorem once we can show that horizontal currents exist in any homology class. This is the content of the following proposition:

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
(M^{2n+1},\Xi)
be a closed contact manifold and
1\leq k\leq n
. Then any homology class
\mathfrak{a}\in H_{k}(M,\mathbb{Z})
contains a horizontal integral cycle.

Proof.

Let
g
be any subriemannian metric on
\Xi
. Since
M
is compact, we can cover it with finitely many Carnot–Carathéodory geodesic balls
B_{R}(\xi_{1}),\ldots B_{R}(\xi_{N})
such that
B_{2R}(\xi_{i})
admits a Darboux chart
\phi_{i}:B_{2R}(\xi_{i})\to\mathcal{U}_{i}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
for each
i
. Let
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda<\infty
be such that

\lambda g_{\mathbb{H}}\leq(\phi_{i}^{-1})^{*}g\leq\Lambda g_{\mathbb{H}},

so that in particular the maps
\phi_{i}
preserve distances up to a constant.

Given
\mathfrak{a}\in H_{k}(M,\mathbb{Z})
, represent it by a Lipschitz polyhedral chain
\bm{\sigma}=\sum_{j}m_{j}\sigma_{j}
, where
m_{j}
are positive integers and
\sigma_{j}
are Lipschitz simplices each contained in some ball
B_{r}(\xi)
, where
r\leq R/C_{2}
and
C_{2}
is a constant to be determined below (depending only on
n
and
\Lambda/\lambda
).

We will deform
\bm{\sigma}
into a horizontal integral current
S
by inductively replacing its skeleton using the isoperimetric inequality (
Theorem

A.5
). We first construct, for each
1
-simplex
\tau
in the
1
-skeleton of
\bm{\sigma}
, a horizontal integral
1
-current
S_{\tau}
with the same boundary
\partial S_{\tau}=\partial\llbracket\tau\rrbracket
, with mass at most
C^{(1)}r
, and with support still contained in a ball of radius
C^{(1)}r
(for example we can take a minimizing Carnot–Carathéodory geodesic). Moreover we record the existence of a (not necessarily horizontal) filling, that is, an integral
2
-current
V_{\tau}
with
\partial V_{\tau}=S_{\tau}-\llbracket\tau\rrbracket
and
\diam(\spt V_{\tau})\leq C^{(1)}r
, which exists simply because these
1
-currents are supported in a contractible ball.

Then, for each
2
-simplex
\tau
in the
2
-skeleton of
\bm{\sigma}
, we construct a horizontal integral
2
-current
S_{\tau}
whose boundary is
\partial S_{\tau}=S_{\tau_{0}}-S_{\tau_{1}}+S_{\tau_{2}}
, where the
1
-simplices
\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
are defined by
\partial\tau=\tau_{0}-\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}
. We construct this horizontal filling by applying
Theorem

A.5
on

Remark 3.14

3.4.
General properties of local minimizers

In this section we define local minimizers and prove a strong convergence theorem. Since these concepts are local, we work with a subriemannian metric
h
defined on an open subset
\mathcal{U}
of the Heisenberg group.

Denote by
\mathscr{L}_{0}^{k}
the set of oriented isotropic
k
-planes in
\Xi_{0}
, and set
\mathscr{L}_{0}:=\mathscr{L}_{0}^{n}
, the so-called oriented Lagrangian Grassmannian. We can identify
\mathscr{L}_{0}^{k}
with a subset of
\bigwedge^{k}\Xi_{0}
; note that any
\vec{\pi}\in\mathscr{L}_{0}^{k}
has
|\vec{\pi}|=1
. After identifying all horizontal spaces via left translations, any Lipschitz metric
h
on
\Xi|_{\mathcal{U}}
induces a norm on
\mathscr{L}_{0}^{k}
for each
\xi\in\mathcal{U}
in the usual way:

|\vec{\pi}|_{h_{\xi}}=\sqrt{h_{\xi}(\vec{\pi},\vec{\pi})}.

The mass with respect to the metric
h
then takes the form

(3.9)

\mathbf{M}^{h}_{\mathcal{U}}(T)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}|\vec{T}(\xi)|_{h_{\xi}}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|(\xi).

Definition 3.16
.

We will say that a current
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is
\mathbf{M}^{h}
-minimizing in
\mathcal{U}
if
\mathbf{M}^{h}_{\mathcal{U}}(T)\leq\mathbf{M}^{h}_{\mathcal{U}}(T^{\prime})
whenever
T^{\prime}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
has
\spt(T^{\prime}-T)\Subset\mathcal{U}
and
T-T^{\prime}
is the boundary of a integral
(k+1)
-current supported in
\mathcal{U}
.

Note that the current whose boundary must be
T-T^{\prime}
is not required to be horizontal; in particular, if
k=n
, it will never be so. We add this condition just to enforce that homology minimizers are mass-minimizing on the whole manifold. Of course, if
\mathcal{U}
is contractible, the condition reduces to
\partial(T-T^{\prime})=0
.

As in the classical setting, sequences of mass minimizing currents have improved convergence properties. In particular, we will need the following proposition in the regularity theory. The proof relies crucially on a theorem Wenger
[
Wen07
]
about flat convergence of integral currents in metric spaces that we adapt in the Appendix.

as Radon measures in
\mathcal{U}

and exhaust

Moreover we may assume that

and

4.
Main regularity theorems

In this section we state the
\varepsilon
-regularity theorem for Legendrian local area minimizers (
Theorem

4.7
) and derive its main consequences for the partial regularity of area-minimizing Legendrian currents. The proof of
Theorem

4.7
will be carried out in the following two sections. Note that from here onwards we consider only Legendrian currents, that is,
n
-dimensional horizontal currents.

4.1.
Geometric considerations

Thanks to the homogeneity of the Heisenberg group, we can develop our regularity theory around the origin. Fix a Legendrian plane
\pi
through
0\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, namely a horizontal plane
\pi\subset\Xi_{0}
such that
\omega|_{\pi}=0
. It will be important to keep in mind that there is a canonical smooth action of the unitary group
\mathsf{U}(n)
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
via

(4.1)

U\cdot(\mathbf{z},\varphi)=(U\mathbf{z},\varphi),\qquad U\in\mathsf{U}(n)

that preserves all the structures of the Heisenberg group, that is: acts as group automorphisms, pulls back
\theta
to itself, and is compatible with the metric
g_{\mathbb{H}}
, the almost complex structure
J
and the symplectic form
\omega
on
\Xi
. This group action acts transitively on the set of all Legendrian planes in
\Xi_{0}
. Therefore, up to applying such an automorphism, there is no loss of generality in supposing that our plane
\pi
is the plane
\pi_{0}:=\Span\{\partial_{x^{1}},\ldots,\partial_{x^{n}}\}
.

Let
\vec{\pi}\in\bigwedge^{n}\Xi_{0}
be an
n
-vector orienting
\pi
. By a slight abuse of notation, we will denote also by
\vec{\pi}
the left-invariant
n
-vector field that extends
\vec{\pi}
at the origin. We thus have the explicit form

(4.2)

\vec{\pi}_{0}=\vec{\nabla}^{H}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\vec{\nabla}^{H}x^{n}

for the plane
\pi_{0}
with its natural orientation.

There is a canonical projection map
\Pi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}\cong\Xi_{0}
that “forgets” the last coordinate and is a group homomorphism:
\Pi(\mathbf{z},\varphi)=\mathbf{z}
. Then for any Lagrangian plane
\pi\subset\Xi_{0}
with respect to
\omega
(we will call such planes
Legendrian
) we can consider the projections
\mathbf{p}^{\pi}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\pi\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined as the composition of
\Pi
and then the
g_{\mathbb{H}}
-orthogonal projection onto
\pi
. We will just write
\mathbf{p}
when the plane
\pi
is clear. We will also consider the projection
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}^{\pi}
onto the orthogonal of
\pi
(after applying
\Pi
). For the plane
\pi_{0}
this has the coordinate expression

\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)=\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)=\mathbf{y}.

It is clear from the expression for the multiplication in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
that
\Pi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a group homomorphism. Hence the same is true about
\mathbf{p}^{\pi}
and
\mathbf{q}^{\pi}
for any Legendrian plane
\pi
.
For
r>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})
denote the Euclidean open ball
B_{r}^{\pi}:=\{x\in\pi:|x-x_{0}|<r\}\subset\pi
and define the cylinder
\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0}):=(\mathbf{p}^{\pi})^{-1}(B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0}))
. We will omit the superscript
\pi
when the plane is clear (most of the time it will be
\pi_{0}
), and also set
B_{r}:=B_{r}(0)
and
\mathbf{C}_{r}:=\mathbf{C}_{r}(0)
. We will also denote for short
\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(\xi):=\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(\mathbf{p}^{\pi}(\xi))
for
\xi\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

As in
Section

3
it will be convenient to endow
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with a left-invariant Riemannian metric
g_{0}
such that
g_{0}|_{\Xi}=g_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
; this is not absolutely necessary but it will allow us to recycle standard arguments from the Riemannian setting. Nevertheless none of our hypotheses or conclusions will involve this auxiliary metric. In particular, since all the currents that we consider are horizontal, their mass is independent of
g_{0}
by
Proposition

3.3
.

Observe that with respect to such a metric
g_{0}
, any of the projections
\mathbf{p}
satisfies

(4.3)

|\nabla\mathbf{p}|\leq 1.

Indeed, it is enough to check this at
0\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
thanks to the left-invariance of
g_{0}
and to the fact that
\mathbf{p}
is a group homomorphism. But at
\nabla\mathbf{p}(0)
is just the projection onto the
x^{i}
-coordinates, which clearly has norm at most
1
. A similar argument gives the following: for any oriented horizontal
n
-plane
\vec{\pi}^{\prime}
at any point
\xi\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, its push-forward by
\mathbf{p}
is

(4.4)

\mathbf{p}_{\#}(\vec{\pi}^{\prime})=\langle\vec{\pi}^{\prime},\vec{\pi}(\xi)\rangle\vec{\pi}.

As a consequence we get the classical formula for the excess of mass:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a measurable set. Then

(4.5)

\mathbf{M}(T\res\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K))-(\mathbf{p}_{\#}T\res K)(\vec{\pi})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|.

Proof.

Notice that
|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}|^{2}=2-2\langle\vec{T},\vec{\pi}\rangle
. Thus, using (
4.4
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle=\|T\|(\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K))-\int_{\mathbf{p}^{-1}(B_{r})}\langle\vec{T},\vec{\pi}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle=\|T\|(\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K))-(\mathbf{p}_{\#}T\res K)(\vec{\pi}).\qed

This leads us to a natural notion of (cylindrical) excess, the quantity that will lead the regularity theory. Suppose that
T
is a horizontal integral current in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
(\partial T)\res\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})=0
. Since
(\partial\mathbf{p}^{\pi}_{\#}T)\res B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})=\mathbf{p}^{\pi}_{\#}((\partial T)\res\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0}))=0
, by the constancy theorem we have that
(\mathbf{p}^{\pi}_{\#}T)\res B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})=Q\llbracket B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})\rrbracket
for some integer

. By orienting
\vec{\pi}
appropriately we can and will suppose that

, thus
\|\mathbf{p}^{\pi}_{\#}T\|(B_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0}))=Q\omega_{n}r^{n}
, where
\omega_{n}=\mathcal{L}^{n}(B_{1})
. Thus we can define:

Definition 4.2
.

The
excess
of
T
in the cylinder
\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})
is the quantity

(4.6)

\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})):=r^{-n}\left(\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0}))-Q\omega_{n}r^{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2}r^{-n}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{r}^{\pi}(x_{0})}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|.

If we require that a graph of the form

By considering instead

\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{g})-f\right)=\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

Note that, in the symplectic

Motivated by this observation, for a

2C_{1}^{n-1}m<\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n},

where
C_{1}
denotes the constant from the isoperimetric inequality (
Theorem

A.5
), otherwise we are done. By the coarea formula (
Proposition

3.7
),

\mathcal{L}^{2}([0,\tfrac{r}{2}]\setminus G)\leq\frac{r}{4m}\int_{0}^{\frac{r}{2}}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,s\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{r}{4m}\|T\|(\mathcal{B}_{r/2})=\frac{1}{4}r,

hence
\mathcal{L}^{1}(G)\geq\tfrac{1}{4}r
. For each
s\in G
we may apply
Theorem

A.5
and obtain a current
Q_{s}
with

\partial Q_{s}=\langle T,\tau,s\rangle,\qquad\mathbf{M}(Q_{s})\leq C_{1}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,s\rangle)^{\frac{n}{n-1}}

and additionally
\spt Q_{s}\subset\mathcal{B}_{r}\subset\mathcal{U}
thanks to (
5.2
). Now for
T_{s}=Q_{s}+T\res\mathcal{B}_{s}^{c}
we have that

\partial T_{s}=\langle T,\tau,s\rangle+\partial(T\res\mathcal{B}_{s}^{c})=\partial(T\res\mathcal{B}_{s})-(\partial T)\res\mathcal{B}_{s}+\partial(T\res\mathcal{B}_{s}^{c}),=\partial T

so
T_{s}
is a valid competitor for
\mathbf{M}^{h}
and we get

\|T\|(\mathcal{B}_{s})\leq\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{h}(T\res\mathcal{B}_{s})\leq\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{h}(Q_{s})\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}\mathbf{M}(Q_{s})\leq\frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}C_{1}\mathbf{M}(\langle T,\tau,s\rangle)^{\frac{n}{n-1}}.

Letting
g(s):=\|T\|(\mathcal{B}_{s})
and using again the coarea formula, we see that

g(s)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq Cg^{\prime}(s).

Thus, since
g(s)>0
for every
s>0
, we have that
g^{\frac{1}{n}}(s)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{n}g^{\prime}(s)g(s)^{\frac{1-n}{n}}\geq c
for every
s\in G
. Thanks to the monotonicity of
g(s)^{1/n}
we may integrate and get

g^{\frac{1}{n}}(r)\geq g^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\tfrac{r}{2}\right)\geq\int_{G}\left(g^{\frac{1}{n}}(s)\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}s\geq c\mathcal{L}^{1}(G)\geq cr.\qed

As a consequence we obtain a bound on the vertical oscillation of the current, as in
[
Fed69
, Lemma 5.3.4]
or in the Appendix of
[
SS82
]
. The bound for the
\mathbf{q}
components is standard, but since the proof of the bound for
\varphi
uses the same strategy, we have decided to present them together for completeness.

Lemma 5.2

(height bounds)
.

There are constants
\varepsilon_{0},C>0
depending only on
n,\Lambda/\lambda
and
Q
such that, if
T
satisfies
Assumption

4.6
at scale
R=1
with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then

(5.3)

\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/2}}|\mathbf{q}(\xi_{1})-\mathbf{q}(\xi_{2})|\leq C\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{1})^{\frac{1}{2n}}.

Moreover, if
\mathbf{q}(\xi_{0})=0
for some
\xi_{0}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/2}
, then also

(5.4)

\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/4}}|\varphi(\xi_{1})-\varphi(\xi_{2})|\leq C\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}.

Proof.

Denote
E:=\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{1})
and choose
\varepsilon_{0}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}Q\omega_{n}
so that

(5.5)

\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{p}_{\#}(T\res\mathbf{C}_{1}))+E\leq\frac{3}{2}Q\omega_{n}.

Let
f:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and for
-\infty\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq+\infty
define

W(t_{1},t_{2}):=\{\xi\in\mathbf{C}_{1}:t_{1}<f(\xi)<t_{2}\}

and
T_{t}:=T\res W(t,\infty)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
t_{0}
be a median of
f
, in the sense that

\max\{\|T\|(W(-\infty,t_{0})),\|T\|(W(t_{0},\infty))\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1}).

We proceed in three steps.

Step 1.

We show that whenever
t_{1}>t_{0}
is such that
\mathbf{M}(T_{t_{1}})\geq 2E
,

(5.6)

(t_{1}-t_{0})\mathbf{M}(T_{t_{1}})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\int_{W(t_{0},t_{1})}\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}f\right|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

for a constant
C=C(n,\Lambda/\lambda,Q)>0
. First observe that
\mathbf{M}(T_{t_{0}})\leq\tfrac{3}{4}Q\omega_{n}
. By the standard coarea formula and (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{n}\{x\in B_{1}:\Theta^{n}(\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|,x)>0\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n}\{\xi\in\mathbf{C}_{1}:\Theta^{n}(\|T_{t}\|,\xi)>0\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{n}\{\xi\in\mathbf{C}_{1}:\Theta^{n}(\|T_{t}\|,\xi)\geq Q\}\leq\frac{1}{Q}\mathbf{M}(T_{t})\leq\frac{1}{Q}\mathbf{M}(T_{t_{0}})\leq\frac{3}{4}\omega_{n}.

Notice that here is the only place where we use the assumption (
4.14
). Thus, thanks to the fact that
\mathcal{L}^{n}\{x\in B_{1}:\Theta^{n}(\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|,x)=0\}\geq\tfrac{1}{4}\omega_{n}
, we may apply the Poincaré–Sobolev inequality (see
[
Fed69
, 5.3.2]
) to the integer-valued BV functions corresponding to (the signed multiplicity of)
\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}
:

\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\|\partial\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})=C\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}\partial T_{t}\|(B_{1})=C\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}\partial T_{t}\|(B_{1}).

Now for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-almost every
t>t_{0}
we may apply (
4.3
) and use slicing to deduce

\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}\partial T_{t}\|(B_{1})\leq C\|\partial T_{t}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})=C\|\langle T,f,t\rangle\|(\mathbf{C}_{1}).

On the other hand, by
Proposition

4.1
,

\|T_{t}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})\leq\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{1}}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T_{t}\|\leq\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})+E.

Assuming also that
t<t_{1}
, we have that
\|T_{t}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})\geq\|T_{t_{1}}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})\geq 2E
, thus
E\leq\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})
and we get

\|T_{t}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq\left(2\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T_{t}\|(B_{1})\right)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\|\langle T,f,t\rangle\|(\mathbf{C}_{1}).

Integrating gives finally the desired estimate:

(t_{1}-t_{0})\|T_{t_{1}}\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq C\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\|\langle T,f,t\rangle\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t=C\int_{W(t_{0},t_{1})}\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}f(\xi)\right|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|(\xi).

Step 2.

We now show the classical height bound (
5.3
). Choose
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)=y^{k}
for a fixed
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We claim that there exists
\overline{t}\geq t_{0}
such that
\|T\|(W(\overline{t},\infty))\leq\sqrt{E}
and
\overline{t}-t_{0}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}
. If
\|T\|(W(t_{0},\infty))\leq\sqrt{E}
we may just choose
\overline{t}=t_{0}
, otherwise define
\overline{t}
to be the supremum of all
t_{1}>t_{0}
such that
\|T\|(W(t_{1},\infty))\geq\sqrt{E}
. Then it is easy to see that
\|T\|(W(\overline{t},\infty))\leq\sqrt{E}
. To get the bound on
\overline{t}-t_{0}
, we apply (
5.6
) for all such
t_{1}
and pass to the limit
t_{1}\nearrow\overline{t}
:

(5.7)

(\overline{t}-t_{0})E^{\frac{n-1}{2n}}\leq C\int_{W(t_{0},\overline{t})}\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

(note that this is justified since
\varepsilon_{0}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
guarantees that
\sqrt{E}\geq 2E
). We now claim that

(5.8)

\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}\right|^{2}\leq 1-|\langle\vec{T},\vec{\pi}_{0}\rangle|^{2}.

Recall that
\vec{\pi}_{0}=\nabla^{H}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\nabla^{H}x^{n}
and
\{\nabla^{H}x^{i},\nabla^{H}y^{i}\}
are a global orthonormal frame of
\Xi
. Thus

1=|\vec{T}|^{2}\geq|\langle\vec{T},\nabla^{H}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\nabla^{H}x^{n}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{e}}\left|\left\langle\vec{T},\nabla^{H}y^{k}\wedge\mathbf{e}\right\rangle\right|^{2}

where the sum is over the orthonormal basis of
\bigwedge^{n-1}(\Xi)
induced by our orthonormal frame. On the other hand,

|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}|^{2}=|\vec{T}\res\mathrm{d}y^{k}|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{e}}|\langle\vec{T}\res\mathrm{d}y^{k},\mathbf{e}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{e}}|\langle\vec{T},\nabla^{H}y^{k}\wedge\mathbf{e}\rangle|^{2}

and (
5.8
) follows. Putting this together with the standard inequality
1-\langle\vec{T},\vec{\pi}_{0}\rangle^{2}\leq|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}
we have

\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}\right|^{2}\leq|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}.

Using this after applying Cauchy–Schwarz and the bound (
5.5
) on (
5.7
), we get

(\overline{t}-t_{0})E^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2n}}\leq C\left(\int_{W(t_{0},\overline{t})}\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\right)^{1/2}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2}},

from which the estimate
\overline{t}-t_{0}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}
follows. Finally let
\xi\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/2}
, suppose that
y^{k}(\xi)>\overline{t}
and let
r=\min\left\{\tfrac{1}{2},y^{k}(\xi)-\overline{t}\right\}
. Since
\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi)\subset W(\overline{t},\infty)
, the lower bound (
5.1
) shows that

cr^{n}\leq\|T\|(W(\overline{t},\infty))\leq\sqrt{E}\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}.

By choosing
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough we can guarantee that
r<\tfrac{1}{2}
; in particular,
r=y^{k}(\xi)-\overline{t}
and it follows that
y^{k}(\xi)\leq\overline{t}+CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}\leq t_{0}+CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}
. Repeating the same argument for the level sets of
f
below
t_{0}
the oscillation bound for
y^{k}
follows.

Step 3.

We finally show the bound for
\varphi
. In order to use the bound from Step 2 we will have to apply Step 1 in the smaller cylinder
\mathbf{C}_{1/2}
; we can do this thanks to the scale invariance of the hypotheses (see
Corollary

5.3
below). In this case we choose
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi):=\varphi
and, arguing as before, we will find
\overline{t}\geq t_{0}
such that

\overline{t}-t_{0}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1/2}\cap\{\varphi>\overline{t}\})\leq E^{\frac{1}{2(n+1)}}.

To see this, we argue as above and define
\overline{t}
as the supremum of those
t_{1}
for which the second inequality does not hold. Then using (
5.6
) (after making
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough) and passing to the limit
t_{1}\nearrow\overline{t}
we get

(\overline{t}-t_{0})E^{\frac{n-1}{2n(n+1)}}\leq C\int_{\mathbf{C}_{1/2}\cap\{t_{0}<\varphi<\overline{t}\}}\left|\nabla^{\vec{T}}\varphi\right|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|.

Since
T
is horizontal,
\|T\|
-almost everywhere it holds that

\nabla^{\vec{T}}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{x}\cdot\nabla^{\vec{T}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}\cdot\nabla^{\vec{T}}\mathbf{x}\right),

hence
|\nabla^{\vec{T}}\varphi|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}|x^{k}||\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}|+|y^{k}||\nabla^{\vec{T}}x^{k}|
. Now (
5.3
) together with the additional hypothesis on
\spt T
implies that
|y^{k}|\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}
for every point in
\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/2}
, and moreover we have the trivial bounds
|x^{k}|\leq 1
and
|\nabla^{\vec{T}}x^{k}|\leq 1
. Therefore

\displaystyle(\overline{t}-t_{0})E^{\frac{n-1}{2n(n+1)}}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbf{C}_{1/2}\cap\{t_{0}<\varphi<\overline{t}\}}\left(E^{\frac{1}{2n}}+\sum_{k}|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}|\right)\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}+C\sum_{k}\left(\int_{\mathbf{C}_{1/2}}|\nabla^{\vec{T}}y^{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}+CE^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n}}

and the bound
\overline{t}-t_{0}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}
follows. Finally consider a point
\xi_{0}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{1/4}\cap\{\varphi>\overline{t}\}
with coordinates
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{0},\varphi_{0})
and let
0<r\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
. Given a point
\xi=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\varphi)\in\spt T\cap\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi_{0})
, we have that

\xi_{0}^{-1}\xi=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})=\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{0},\varphi-\varphi_{0}+\frac{1}{2}(-\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x})\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(0),

so it is clear that
|\mathbf{x}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and, again thanks to (
5.3
),

\varphi>\varphi_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(-\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x})-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\geq\varphi_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(|\mathbf{x}_{0}||\mathbf{y}|+|\mathbf{y}_{0}||\mathbf{x}|)-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\geq\varphi_{0}-\overline{C}E^{\frac{1}{2n}}-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\geq\overline{t}

whenever
0<r\leq r_{0}
, where
r_{0}
is the solution of
\tfrac{1}{4}r_{0}^{2}+\overline{C}E^{\frac{1}{2n}}=\varphi_{0}-\overline{t}
. Arguing as in the end of Step 2, let
r=\min\left\{\tfrac{1}{4},r_{0}\right\}
and apply (
5.1
) to
\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi_{0})
, noting that
\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi_{0})\cap\spt T\subset\mathbf{C}_{1/2}\cap\{\varphi>\overline{t}\}
:

cr^{n}\leq\|T\|(\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi_{0}))=\|T\|(\mathcal{B}_{r}(\xi_{0})\cap\spt T)\leq\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1/2}\cap\{\varphi>\overline{t}\})\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2(n+1)}}.

After making
\varepsilon_{0}
smaller if necessary, it follows that
r<\tfrac{1}{4}
, so
r_{0}=r\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n(n+1)}}
and finally

\varphi_{0}-t_{0}\leq(\varphi_{0}-\overline{t})+(\overline{t}-t_{0})\leq\frac{1}{4}r_{0}^{2}+\overline{C}E^{\frac{1}{2n}}+CE^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}\leq CE^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}

The oscillation bound now follows by arguing analogously below
t_{0}
. ∎

By using the scaling automorphisms of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
we get with little effort the following scaled version of the height estimates:

Corollary 5.3
.

For the same constants
\varepsilon_{0},C>0
as in
Lemma

5.2
the following holds: suppose that
T
satisfies
Assumption

4.6
for some
R>0
and some
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, and that
\{\mathbf{q}=0\}\cap\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{R/2}\neq\varnothing
. Then

(5.9)

\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{R/2}}|\mathbf{q}(\xi_{1})-\mathbf{q}(\xi_{2})|\leq C\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R})^{\frac{1}{2n}}R

and

(5.10)

\sup_{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{C}_{R/4}}|\varphi(\xi_{1})-\varphi(\xi_{2})|\leq C\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R})^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}R^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{R}:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}^{n}
be the automorphism
\delta_{R}(\mathbf{z},\varphi)=(R\mathbf{z},R^{2}\varphi)
. If
T
satisfies
Assumption

4.6
for some
R>0
, then
\tilde{T}:=(\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}T
is also in
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and satisfies the same assumptions with
R=1
, except possibly for (
4.11
). This is clear for (
4.12
), (
4.13
) and for (
4.14
) (since
\delta_{R}
is a diffeomorphism). To get (
4.15
) we use the definition

\mathbf{E}((\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}T,\mathbf{C}_{1})=\|(\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})-Q\omega_{n}=R^{-n}\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{R})-Q\omega_{n}=\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R});

here we have made use of the formula for the push-forward of currents,

\|(\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1})=\int_{\mathbf{C}_{R}}\left|\left.{\wedge}^{n}(\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}\right|_{\xi}(\vec{T})\right|\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|(\xi)=R^{-n}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{R}}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|=R^{-n}\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{R}),

since the tangent map
(\delta_{R^{-1}})_{\#}
restricts to
\Xi_{\xi}\longrightarrow\Xi_{R^{-1}\xi}
as multiplication by
R^{-1}
(under the identification of all tangent planes to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
via left translations).

The scaled back version of (
4.11
) asserts that
\tilde{T}
is
\mathbf{M}^{\tilde{h}}
-minimizing in
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}:=\delta_{R^{-1}}(\mathcal{U})
for the metric
\tilde{h}:=\delta_{R}^{*}h
. This follows from a similar computation:
Since the quotient
\Lambda/\lambda
is unaltered for
\tilde{h}
, we may apply the estimates (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) to
\tilde{T}
. Then (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) follow by simply scaling back.
∎

5.2.
Approximation by the graph of a Lipschitz gradient

In order to embed most of the support of our minimizing current into such a graph, we will need an appropriate version of the Whitney–Glaeser
C^{1,1}
extension theorem:

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
G\subset\overline{B_{R}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any set,
L_{0},L_{1},L_{2}>0
given constants and
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{g}:G\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given functions with
|f|\leq L_{0}
and
|\mathbf{g}|\leq L_{1}
on
G
. Suppose that the following Whitney conditions are satisfied: for any
x,y\in G
,

(5.11)

|\mathbf{g}(x)-\mathbf{g}(y)|\leq L_{2}|x-y|\quad\text{and}\quad|f(x)-f(y)-\mathbf{g}(y)\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})|\leq L_{2}|x-y|^{2}.

Then there exists a function
\tilde{f}\in C^{1,1}(\overline{B_{R}})
such that
\tilde{f}=f
and
\mathrm{D}\tilde{f}=\mathbf{g}
on
G
, and which satisfies the following bounds:

(5.12)

\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq CL_{0},\qquad\|\mathrm{D}\tilde{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq CL_{0}+CRL_{2},\qquad\|\mathrm{D}^{2}\tilde{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq CL_{2}

for a constant
C=C(n)>0
.

Proof.

This follows from the proof of Theorem VI.2.4 from
[
Ste70
]
. Notice that, in the definition of the extension through a sum on Whitney cubes, we do not need to restrict ourselves to cubes near
G
since we are working on a bounded domain. Moreover, we may assume that
G
is closed if we extend
f
and
\mathbf{g}
by continuity. The more precise, scale-invariant estimates given here follow by closely examining the proof.
∎

Now the approximation of
T
by a Lipschitz graph follows the strategy of
[
SS82
]
.

Lemma 5.5

(Lipschitz approximation)
.

Suppose that a current
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
satisfies
Assumption

4.6
with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and has
0\in\spt T
. Then for every
0<\gamma\leq 1
there exists a
C^{1,1}
function
v:B_{R/9}\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties:

(5.13)

\sup_{B_{R/9}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}v|\leq\gamma,

(5.14)

\sup_{B_{R/9}}|\mathrm{D}v|\leq C\left(\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R})^{\frac{1}{2n}}+\gamma\right)R,\qquad\sup_{B_{R/9}}|v|\leq C\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R})^{\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}R^{2},

(5.15)

T\res\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)=T^{v}\res\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)\text{ for a set }K\subset B_{R/9}\text{ with }\mathcal{L}^{n}(B_{R/9}\setminus K)\leq C\gamma^{-n(n+1)}R^{n}\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R})

and

(5.16)

\|T-T^{v}\|(\mathbf{C}_{R/9})\leq C\gamma^{-n(n+1)}R^{n}\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R}),

where
T^{v}
is the current
T^{v}:=Q(\Phi^{v})_{\#}\llbracket B_{R/9}\rrbracket
and the constant
C
depends only on
n
,
\Lambda/\lambda
and
Q
.

Moreover we have the following estimate for the Dirichlet energy of
v
:

(5.17)

\int_{B_{R/9}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C\gamma^{-n(n+1)}R^{n}\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R}).

Proof.

Fix
0<\eta<\varepsilon_{0}
to be determined later and consider

(5.18)

G_{\gamma}:=\left\{x\in B_{R/9}:\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{r}(x))\leq\eta\;\forall r\in(0,8R/9)\right\}.

For each
x\in B_{R/9}\setminus G_{\gamma}
we can find
0<r<8R/9
such that
\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{r}(x))>\eta
. Vitali’s covering Lemma gives us a countable disjoint subcollection of such balls
B_{r_{1}}(x_{1}),B_{r_{2}}(x_{2}),\ldots\subset B_{R}
such that
\{B_{5r_{i}}(x_{i})\}
cover
B_{R/9}\setminus G_{\gamma}
. Then

(5.19)

\mathcal{L}^{n}(B_{R/9}\setminus G_{\gamma})\leq\sum_{i}\omega_{n}(5r_{i})^{n}\leq\frac{\omega_{n}5^{n}}{\eta}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{r_{i}}(x_{i})}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq\frac{\omega_{n}5^{n}}{\eta}R^{n}\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{R}).

On the other hand, let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\spt T\cap\mathbf{p}^{-1}(G_{\gamma})
and denote
\xi_{i}=(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},\varphi_{i})
. Consider the current
\tilde{T}:=(\ell_{\xi_{1}^{-1}})_{\#}T

\displaystyle\leq 2\|L-T\|(\mathbf{C}_{r_{i+1}}\setminus\mathbf{C}_{r_{i}})+C\|L-T\|(\mathbf{C}_{1/9})\cdot\sup_{B_{r_{i}}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\eta}+CE\cdot E^{\frac{1}{2}(1-n(n+1)\delta-n\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\eta}+CE^{1+\frac{1}{2}(1-n(n+1)\eta-n\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\eta}

thanks to the upper bound on
\eta
.

Step 4.

Using the minimality of
T
.

Once we have chosen a good radius, our goal is to show that the tangent planes to
S
approximate those of
T
very closely. More precisely, in this step we exploit the
\mathbf{M}^{h}
-minimality of
T
to prove the following preliminary estimate:

(6.18)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq CE^{1+\eta}+C\mu+C\left|(S-L_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})\right|

Here the constant
C
depends only on
n,\Lambda,\lambda
and
Q
, and
\eta
should be smaller than a dimensional constant.
Notice that the remaining term involves only the graphical currents corresponding to the functions
u
and
v_{0}
. This will allow us to exploit the PDE satisfied by
u
in the next step.

To prove this, we begin with using the isoperimetric inequality (
Theorem

A.5
) and (
6.15
) to produce a current
V\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\partial V=\partial(T_{\sigma}-S)
and

\mathbf{M}(V)\leq C\mathbf{M}(\partial(T_{\sigma}-S))^{\frac{n}{n-1}}\leq CE^{\frac{n}{n-1}(1-n(n+1)\delta)}\leq CE^{1+\eta}

(the last inequality uses that
\eta
is small enough). Moreover, by making
\varepsilon
smaller if necessary we may assume that
\spt V\subset\mathcal{B}_{1/2}\subset\mathcal{U}
. Furthermore
\spt T_{\sigma}\subset\mathcal{B}_{1/2}
, and thanks to Agmon’s estimate (
2.13
), also
\spt S\subset\mathcal{B}_{1/2}
. We may now fill
S+V-T_{\sigma}
and use the minimality of
T
to get

\mathbf{M}^{h}(T_{\sigma})\leq\mathbf{M}^{h}(S+V)\leq\mathbf{M}^{h}(S)+\mathbf{M}^{h}(V),

which implies (since the total masses of
T
and
S
are bounded by a constant)

\mathbf{M}^{0}(T_{\sigma})\leq\mathbf{M}^{h}(T_{\sigma})+C\mu\leq\mathbf{M}^{h}(S)+\mathbf{M}^{h}(V)+C\mu\leq\mathbf{M}^{0}(S)+C\mathbf{M}(V)+C\mu.

Here we are denoting
\mathbf{M}^{0}:=\mathbf{M}^{h_{0}}
, with
h_{0}
extended to
\Xi
by left translations. Assumption (
4.9
) now gives

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}\left(|\vec{T}|_{h_{0}}-\frac{h_{0}(\vec{T},\vec{S})}{|\vec{S}|_{h_{0}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|=\mathbf{M}^{0}(T_{\sigma})-T_{\sigma}(\vec{S}^{h_{0}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{0}(T_{\sigma})-\mathbf{M}^{0}(S)+S(\vec{S}^{h_{0}})-T_{\sigma}(\vec{S}^{h_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbf{M}(V)+C\mu+(S-T_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}).

Notice that
(S+V-T_{\sigma})(\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})=0
since
\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}}
is constant. Thus

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbf{M}(V)+C\mu+(S-T_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})-V(\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbf{M}(V)+C\mu+(S-T_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}}).

We finally estimate the last term by (
6.16
) and use the bound on
\mathbf{M}(V)
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\eta}+C\mu+C|(S-T_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})|

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\eta}+C\mu+C|(S-L_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})|.

Step 5.

Linearization and
L^{p}
estimates.

We proceed to estimate the last term in (
6.18
). In the proof we will need to estimate simultaneously the energy of
u
, so we record it here as well. More precisely, we claim that if the
\varepsilon
in (
4.15
) is small enough, then

(6.19)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq CE^{1+\delta/2}+C\mu

and

(6.20)

\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq CE+C\mu

for a constant
C=C(n,\Lambda,\lambda,Q)
. To prove this, let us examine the last term in (
6.18
):

\displaystyle(S-L_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})

\displaystyle=Q\int_{B_{\sigma}}(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})(\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}-\Phi^{v_{0}}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0})

\displaystyle=Q\int_{B_{\sigma}}h_{0}\left(\frac{\vec{S}}{|\vec{S}|_{h_{0}}}-\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}},\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}-\Phi^{v_{0}}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=Q\int_{B_{\sigma}}h_{0}\left(\frac{\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}-\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}},\partial_{kl}(u-v_{0})\vec{\pi}_{k}^{l}+\mathcal{O}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{n}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2})\right).

We expand the first factor using (
6.8
):

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\Phi^{u}_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}-\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}+u_{ij}\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}+\mathcal{O}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{n})}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}\left(1-\frac{u_{ij}b_{i}^{j}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2})\right)-\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}\left(u_{ij}\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}-u_{ij}b_{i}^{j}\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{n+2}).

Putting all together and analyizing the error terms (in particular we use that
2|\mathrm{D}^{2}u||\mathrm{D}^{2}v|\leq|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}v|^{2}
) we get:

\displaystyle|(S-L_{\sigma})(\vec{S}^{h_{0}}-\vec{\pi}_{0}^{h_{0}})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Q}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}}\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}h_{0}\left(u_{ij}\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}-u_{ij}b_{i}^{j}\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}^{2}},\partial_{kl}(u-v_{0})\vec{\pi}_{k}^{l}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle(=:I)

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{3}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2n+2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u||\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle(=:II)

We first examine the term
(I)
. To begin with, we identify the coefficients
a_{ij}^{kl}
in this expression:

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\sigma}}h_{0}\left(u_{ij}\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}-u_{ij}b_{i}^{j}\frac{\vec{\pi}_{0}}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}^{2}},\partial_{kl}(u-v_{0})\vec{\pi}_{k}^{l}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\sigma}}\left(\tilde{a}_{ik}^{jl}-\frac{1}{|\vec{\pi}_{0}|_{h_{0}}^{2}}b_{i}^{j}b_{k}^{l}\right)u_{ij}\partial_{kl}(u-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\sigma}}a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(u-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Integrating by parts twice on (
6.13
) against
u-v_{\delta}
and using the fact that
u=v_{\delta}
and
\mathrm{D}u=\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}
on
\partial B_{\sigma}
, we obtain that

\int_{B_{\sigma}}a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(u-v_{\delta})\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Therefore our task is to estimate

(I)\leq C\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|.

Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{\sigma})
be a smooth radial cutoff function such that
\chi\equiv 1
in
B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}}
,
\chi\equiv 0
outside
B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}/2}
and
|\mathrm{D}\chi|\leq CE^{-\eta}
. We have

(6.21)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}(1-\chi)a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|&\leq C\int_{B_{\sigma}\setminus B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|(|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{\delta}|+|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq C\int_{B_{\sigma}\setminus B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}}}E^{\eta/2}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+E^{-\eta/2}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{\delta}|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq CE^{\eta/2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+CE^{1-n(n+1)\delta+\eta/2}\end{split}

using Young’s inequality and (
6.17
). For the remainder, we integrate by parts and use the interior estimates of (
2.10
) and Cauchy–Schwarz:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}\chi a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-\int_{B_{\sigma}}\partial_{k}\left(\chi\,a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\right)\partial_{l}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-\int_{B_{\sigma}}\left(\partial_{k}\chi\,a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)+\chi\,a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ijk}(u)\right)\partial_{l}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}/2}}\left(E^{-\eta}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|+|\mathrm{D}^{3}u|\right)|\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}-\mathrm{D}v_{0}|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{B_{\sigma-E^{\eta}/2}}\left(E^{-\eta}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|+|\mathrm{D}^{3}u|\right)\left(\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}-\mathrm{D}v_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{-\eta(n+2)/2}\left(\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}-\mathrm{D}v_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

Now recall that in a set
K=K_{0}\cap K_{\delta}\cap B_{\sigma}
such that
\mathcal{L}^{n}(B_{\sigma}\setminus K)\leq CE^{1-n(n+1)\delta}
we have that
T^{v_{0}}\res\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)=T^{v_{\delta}}\res\mathbf{p}^{-1}(K)
, so in particular
\mathrm{D}v_{0}=\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}
there, and everywhere we have the height bound
|\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}-\mathrm{D}v_{0}|\leq CE^{\frac{1}{2n(n+1)}}
from (
5.20
). As a result,

\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}v_{\delta}-\mathrm{D}v_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq CE^{1-n(n+1)\delta+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}}

and

(6.22)

\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}\chi a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\left(\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(E^{1-n(n+1)\delta+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}-(n+2)\eta}\right)^{1/2}.

Now fix
\delta=\tfrac{\eta}{4n(n+1)}
so that the error term in (
6.21
) is at most
CE^{1+\eta/4}
, and then choose
\eta
small enough so that the exponent in the second factor of (
6.22
) is at least
1+\eta
. Note that we can do this thanks to the summand
\tfrac{1}{n(n+1)}
which is independent of
\eta
. This will be our choice of parameters for the rest of the proof. Adding up (
6.21
) and (
6.22
) and applying Young’s inequality we get

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}\chi a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+C\left|\int_{B_{\sigma}}(1-\chi)a_{ik}^{jl}\partial_{ij}(u)\partial_{kl}(v_{\delta}-v_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\eta/2}\left(\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}E^{1/2}+CE^{\eta/2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+CE^{1+\eta/4}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{\eta/2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+CE^{1+\eta/4}.

Now we have to estimate the integrals that appear in
(I)+(II)
. Following
[
ADS18
]
, we split the integrals according to whether
|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|
is small or not. On one hand, choose
p=1+\tfrac{2}{\delta}=p(n)>2n+2
and compute

(6.23)

\begin{split}&\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|>E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{3}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2n+2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u||\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2}+CE^{\eta/2}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq C\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|>E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2n+2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq C\int_{B_{\sigma}}(E^{-(p-1)\delta/2}+E^{-(p-2n-2)\delta/2})|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq CE^{-(p-1)\delta/2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{p}E^{-(p-1)\delta/2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{\delta}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq C_{p}E^{p\delta-(p-1)\delta/2}\leq CE^{1+\delta}.\end{split}

On the other hand, by (
6.11
),

(6.24)

\begin{split}&\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{3}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2n+2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u||\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2}+CE^{\eta/2}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq C\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{3}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u||\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2}+E^{\eta/2}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad\leq CE^{\delta/2}\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+|\mathrm{D}^{2}v_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq CE^{\delta/2}\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+CE^{1+\delta/2}.\end{split}

Thanks to (
6.7
), by assuming that
\varepsilon
is small enough, we can estimate the first summand by

\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}2|\vec{T}^{u}(x)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}(x)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Now recall that
\mathbf{p}_{\#}(T\res\mathbf{C}_{\sigma})=Q\llbracket B_{\sigma}\rrbracket
. Therefore the coarea formula and (
4.3
) give

\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}(x)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{Q}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}(x)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|\mathbf{p}_{\#}T\|(x)\leq\frac{1}{Q}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}(\xi)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|(\xi),

since we defined
\vec{S}(\xi)=\vec{S}(\mathbf{p}(\xi))
. Furthermore,

\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq 2\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}-\vec{T}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+2\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq 2\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}-\vec{T}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+2\mathbf{E}(T,\mathbf{C}_{1})

and as a result,

(6.25)

\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\leq C\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}-\vec{T}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+CE.

Putting this together with (
6.24
), (
6.23
) and (
6.18
) we get

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq CE^{\delta/2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+CE^{1+\delta/2}+C\mu

and, by choosing
\varepsilon
small enough, we can absorb the first term and (
6.19
) is proven. Finally (
6.20
) follows easily from (
6.25
), (
6.23
) and (
6.19
):

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|\leq E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}+\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|>E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{S}-\vec{T}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+CE+C\int_{\{|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|>E^{\delta/2}\}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|+|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2n+2}

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\delta/2}+C\mu+CE+CE^{1+\delta}\leq CE+C\mu.

Step 6.

Excess improvement by tilting.

We finally show the bounds (
6.1
) and (
6.2
). We start by showing that

(6.26)

\sup_{B_{2\rho}}|\vec{S}-\vec{S}(0)|^{2}\leq C\rho^{2}(E+\mu)

holds for any
0<\rho<\tfrac{\sigma}{4}
, provided that
E,\mu\leq\varepsilon_{\rho}
, with
C
independent of
\rho
.
Recall the estimates (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) in the ball of radius
2\rho<\tfrac{\sigma}{2}
in light of the bound (
6.20
):

\sup_{B_{2\rho}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\leq C\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\leq C(E+\mu)

\sup_{B_{2\rho}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u-\mathrm{D}^{2}u(0)|^{2}\leq C\rho^{2}\int_{B_{\sigma}}|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\leq C\rho^{2}(E+\mu).

After making
\varepsilon_{\rho}
smaller if necessary, we may assume that
|\mathrm{D}^{2}u|^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
in
B_{2\rho}
. Hence (
6.6
) implies that

\mathcal{J}(\Phi^{u}(x))=1+\mathcal{O}(|\mathrm{D}^{2}u(x)|^{2})=1+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu)

in
B_{2\rho}
. Since
\vec{S}(x)=\vec{T}^{u}(x)
where
\vec{T}^{u}
is as in (
6.3
), we have that

\vec{S}(x)=\frac{(\Phi^{u})_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}(x)}{\mathcal{J}(\Phi^{u})(x)}=\frac{(\Phi^{u})_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}(x)}{1+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu)}=\left((\Phi^{u})_{\#}\vec{\pi}_{0}(x)\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu)),

so by (
6.5
) this is

\vec{S}(x)=\left(\vec{\pi}_{0}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{ij}u(x)\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu)\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu)).

Hence

\vec{S}(x)-\vec{S}(0)=(\vec{\pi}_{0}-\vec{\pi}_{0})+\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\partial_{ij}u(x)-\partial_{ij}u(0))\vec{\pi}_{i}^{j}\right)+\mathcal{O}(E+\mu),

which, after choosing
\varepsilon_{\rho}
small enough, gives

|\vec{S}(x)-\vec{S}(0)|\leq C\rho\sqrt{E+\mu}+C(E+\mu)\leq C\rho\sqrt{E+\mu}

and (
6.26
) immediately follows. Next we estimate, using (
6.19
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}(0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+\int_{\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}}|\vec{S}-\vec{S}(0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\sigma}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|+\sup_{B_{2\rho}}|\vec{S}-\vec{S}(0)|^{2}\|T\|(\mathbf{C}_{2\rho})

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\delta/2}+C\mu+C\rho^{2}(E+\mu)(\rho^{n}+E)

\displaystyle\leq CE^{1+\delta/2}+C\mu+C\rho^{2+n}E.

Thus

(6.27)

\rho^{-n}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}}|\vec{T}-\vec{S}(0)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T\|\leq C(\rho^{2}+\rho^{-n}E^{\delta/2})E+C\rho^{-n}\mu.

On the other hand, thanks to (
6.7
), if we define
\vec{\pi}_{1}:=\vec{S}(0)
, we have

|\vec{\pi}_{1}-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}=|\vec{S}(0)-\vec{\pi}_{0}|^{2}\leq C|\mathrm{D}^{2}u(0)|^{2}\leq C(E+\mu),

which is (
6.1
). Next we need to show that
\spt T_{1/2}\cap\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{\pi_{1}}\subset\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}^{\pi_{0}}
. If
\xi\in\spt T_{1/2}
, by the height bound (
5.3
) it holds that
|\mathbf{q}^{\pi_{0}}(\xi)|\leq\rho
as long as we take
\varepsilon_{\rho}
small enough. Then
Lemma

6.2
below shows that
|\Pi(\xi)|\leq 2\rho
, so in particular
\xi\in\mathbf{C}_{2\rho}^{\pi_{0}}
. Combining this with (
6.27
) we get

\rho^{-n}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{C}_{\rho}^{\pi_{1}}}|\vec{T}-\vec{\pi}_{1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\|T_{1/2}\|\leq C(\rho^{2}+\rho^{-n}E^{\delta/2})E+C\rho^{-n}\mu.

From this, (
6.2
) finally follows by choosing
\varepsilon_{\rho}\leq\rho^{2(n+2)/\delta}
. ∎

We still need to prove:

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\vec{\pi}_{0},\vec{\pi}_{1}
be two oriented Legendrian
n
-planes such that
|\mathbf{p}^{\pi_{1}}-\mathbf{p}^{\pi_{0}}|^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{8}
and let
r>0
. Suppose that
\xi\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
satisfies
|\mathbf{p}^{\pi_{1}}(\xi)|\leq r
and
|\mathbf{q}^{\pi_{0}}(\xi)|\leq r
. Then
|\Pi(\xi)|\leq 2r
.

Proof.

Write
z=\Pi(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and compute

Appendix A
Metric geometry of the Heisenberg space

Here we recall some facts about the intrinsic metric geometry of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with respect to its Carnot–Carathéodory distance
d_{\mathrm{CC}}
. This distance is defined as the infimum of the lengths, computed with the standard subriemannian metric, of smooth horizontal curves joining the two extrema. The infimum is always attained and the resulting distance has an explicit expression, although less convenient than the one induced by the Folland–Korányi norm (
2.5
), which is bilipschitz equivalent to it. In this section we work with the former distance because we need some equalities with precise constants that we have only been able to find in the literature with
d_{\mathrm{CC}}
.

A lot of progress has been made in recent years in understanding the space
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
since the influential book of Gromov
[
Gro96
]
, and a rather satisfactory theory has been built, which encompasses results about Lipschitz extensions, fillings and rectifiable horizontal subsets. In particular, at the time when
[
SW01
]
was published, the theory of integral currents in metric spaces had just been born
[
AK00
]
, and only the isoperimetric inequality for surfaces of Allcock
[
All98
]
was known.

A.1.
Comparison between horizontal currents and metric currents

The following theorem, relating metric currents in
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
and horizontal currents in the sense of
Definition

3.1
, follows from the work of Ambrosio–Kirchheim
[
AK00
,
AK00a
]
and Williams
[
Wil12
]
. Note that an analogous result also applies to more general Carnot groups.

We need to endow
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with a left-invariant Riemannian metric
g_{0}
which agrees with the standard subriemannian metric
g_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
on the contact distribution; let
d_{0}
denote its induced distance. Note that any such distance is locally bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean distance coming from the identification
\mathbb{H}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
using exponential coordinates. Since the lengths of horizontal curves computed with respect to
g_{0}
and
g_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}
agree, the identity map
I:(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})\to(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{0})
is
1
-Lipschitz.

Let
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{met}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
denote the space of
k
-dimensional metric integral currents in the sense of Ambrosio–Kirchheim
[
AK00
]
, and let
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
denote the space of Rumin horizontal currents
Definition

3.1
. This is a subset of the space of Federer–Fleming (locally) integral currents
\mathbf{I}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, which can be identified with Ambrosio–Kirchheim currents in
\mathbf{I}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{0})
.

.

Moreover, given a metric current

Proposition A.3
.

Let

(\delta_{1/\rho}\circ\ell_{\xi^{-1}})_{\#}T\rightharpoonup\Theta^{k}_{d_{\mathrm{CC}}}(\|T\|,\xi)\llbracket\vec{T}(\xi)\rrbracket\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\text{ as }\rho\searrow 0,

Proof.

This follows from

, for
\mathcal{H}^{k}

through

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{k}(S\cap\mathscr{B}_{r}(\xi)\setminus X(\xi,\pi_{\xi},s))}{r^{k}}=0

Moreover, the plane

If

\mathcal{H}^{k}\res S

(A.7)

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\|T\|(\mathscr{B}_{r}(\xi)\setminus X(\xi,\pi_{\xi},s))}{r^{k}}=0

-almost every

\|(\eta_{\xi,\rho})_{\#}T\|(\mathscr{B}_{R})=\frac{\|T\|(\mathscr{B}_{\rho R}(\xi))}{\rho^{k}}\xlongrightarrow{\rho\searrow 0}\omega_{k}R^{n}\Theta^{k}_{d_{\mathrm{CC}}}(\|T\|,\xi)<\infty\qquad\forall R>0

and (

\lim_{r\to 0}\|(\eta_{\xi,\rho})_{\#}T\|(\mathscr{B}_{R}(0)\setminus X(0,\pi,s))=0

and every

. Now, given any sequence

) and the lower semicontinuity of the mass implies that

\vec{T}\theta

\mathcal{H}^{k}_{d_{\mathrm{CC}}}\res S

\|(\eta_{\xi,\rho_{i^{\prime}}})_{\#}T\|\rightharpoonup Q\mathcal{H}^{k}\res\pi

\displaystyle Q\int_{\pi}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{k}

A.2.
Results from metric geometry

Here we state two results for horizontal Federer–Fleming currents (or Rumin currents) that were first obtained in several works by Basso, Wenger and Young by working intrinsically in
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
.

Theorem A.5

(isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
,
r>0
and
S\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\spt S\subset\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{r}(0)
. Then there exists
T\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
with
\spt T\subset\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{r+C_{1}\mathbf{M}(S)^{1/(k-1)}}(0)
such that
\partial T=S
and

(A.10)

\mathbf{M}(T)\leq C_{1}\mathbf{M}(S)^{\frac{k}{k-1}},

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(n,k)
.

Proof.

This follows from the discussion in
[
BWY23
, pp. 5-6]
for Ambrosio–Kirchheim metric currents in
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
and hence for horizontal currents thanks to
Theorem

A.1
. The bound in the support is given in
[
Wen05
, Lemma 3.4]
.

As explained in the cited paper, the result that we need (for compactly supported currents) actually follows from the earlier work of Young
[
You13
]
and Wenger
[
Wen11
]
.
∎

Theorem A.6

(flat convergence)
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n
and
R_{j}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
be a sequence of horizontal currents with
\partial R_{j}=0
and
\spt R_{j}\subset K
for some compact set
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Suppose that
R_{j}
have uniformly bounded masses and converge weakly (in the Federer–Fleming sense) to a current
R
. Then there exist currents
S_{j}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{hor}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and positive real numbers
s_{j}\to 0
such that

\partial S_{j}=R_{j}-R,\qquad\mathbf{M}(S_{j})\to 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\spt S_{j}\subset\mathcal{B}_{s_{j}}(K).

Proof.

We will deduce this from the main theorem in
[
Wen07
]
. It is clear that
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
is complete, quasiconvex (in fact it is a geodesic space), and again by
[
BWY23
, pp. 5-6]
,
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
enjoys coning inequalities for
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{met}}_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
for each
0\leq k^{\prime}\leq n-1
.

Let
\tilde{R},\tilde{R}_{j}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{met}}_{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
be the metric currents corresponding to
R,R_{j}
via
Theorem

A.1
. If we can show that
\tilde{R}_{j}\rightharpoonup\tilde{R}
, then
[
Wen07
, Theorem 1.4]
will give us metric currents
\tilde{S}_{j}\in\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{met}}_{k}(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
with
\partial\tilde{S}_{j}=\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R}
and
\mathbf{M}(\tilde{S}_{j})\to 0
. These facts translate automatically into the corresponding ones for
S_{j}:=I_{\#}\tilde{S}_{j}
and
R_{j}=I_{\#}\tilde{R}_{j}
, and the control on the support comes once more from
[
Wen05
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Thus we just need to show that, given
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k}\in\Lip(\mathbb{H}^{n},d_{\mathrm{CC}})
with
f_{1}
bounded,

(A.11)

\tilde{R}_{j}(f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}\tilde{R}(f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}).

Following
[
Wil12
, Lemma 7.4]
, we can approximate the functions
f_{i}
by smooth functions
f_{i}^{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
by convolution (the support of
f_{i}^{\epsilon}
may be made compact by cutting off the functions away from
K
). In particular,

\Lip_{d_{\mathrm{CC}}}(f_{i}^{\epsilon})\leq C\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{i}^{\epsilon}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\to 0}f_{i}\text{ uniformly in a neighborhood of }K.

Now we write

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{1}-f_{1}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}(f_{2}-f_{2}^{\epsilon})\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})+\cdots

\displaystyle\quad+(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}(f_{k}-f_{k}^{\epsilon}))

\displaystyle\quad+(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}^{\epsilon})

and observe that, since
\partial(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})=0
,

\displaystyle(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}(f_{i}-f_{i}^{\epsilon})\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})

\displaystyle=(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}(f_{1}^{\epsilon}(f_{i}-f_{i}^{\epsilon}))\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})-(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{i}-f_{i}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{1}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})

\displaystyle=-(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{i}-f_{i}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{1}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}).

Therefore

\displaystyle|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{1}-f_{1}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})|

\displaystyle\quad+|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{2}-f_{2}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{1}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k})|+\cdots

\displaystyle\quad+|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})((f_{k}-f_{k}^{\epsilon})\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{1}^{\epsilon})|

\displaystyle\quad+|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}^{\epsilon})|

\displaystyle\leq C(\mathbf{M}(\tilde{R}_{j})+\mathbf{M}(\tilde{R}))\left(\sup_{K}|f_{1}-f_{1}^{\epsilon}|+\cdots+\sup_{K}|f_{k}-f_{k}^{\epsilon}|\right)

\displaystyle\quad+|(\tilde{R}_{j}-\tilde{R})(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}^{\epsilon})|.

Finally, since
f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{D}^{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is a smooth compactly supported
(k-1)
-form, we may replace the last term by
|(R_{j}-R)(f_{1}^{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{d}f_{2}^{\epsilon}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}f_{k}^{\epsilon})|
, which converges to zero when we send
j\to\infty
by our hypothesis. Then (
A.11
) follows after letting
\epsilon\searrow 0
.
∎

Appendix B
Absence of monotonicity formula in higher dimensions

In this section we show that the phenomenon of monotonicity of the area density of a
Hamiltonian-stationary
smooth
n
-dimensional Legendrian submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is special of dimension
n=2
. In fact, the surface that we produce does not have a density lower bound on large scales and thus cannot be globally area-minimizing as those considered in this paper.

Let
\mathbb{T}^{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{n}
and
\phi:\mathbb{T}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the standard parametrization of the generalized Clifford torus
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
(translated by
(-1,\ldots,-1)
for convenience), which is an isometry:

\phi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=(\cos t_{1}-1,\ldots,\cos t_{n}-1,\sin t_{1},\ldots,\sin t_{n}).

It is well known that this surface is Hamiltonian-stationary (see
[
Oh93
]
) but not exact. However, it has an exact covering which lifts to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{C}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\langle 2\pi(e_{1}-e_{2}),\ldots 2\pi(e_{1}-e_{n})\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}

\displaystyle(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\longmapsto(\cos t_{1}-1,\ldots,\cos t_{n}-1,\sin t_{1},\ldots,\sin t_{n},\varphi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))

where
\varphi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\tfrac{1}{2}(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}-\sin t_{1}-\cdots-\sin t_{n})
is well defined and satisfies

\mathrm{d}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{d}t_{1}+\cdots+\mathrm{d}t_{n}-\cos t_{1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}-\cdots-\cos t_{n}\mathrm{d}t_{n}\right)=\phi^{*}\lambda

for the Liouville form
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})
. It is clear that
\tilde{\phi}
is a Legendrian embedding, which is still an isometry and Hamiltonian-stationary because these two properties are local and preserved by Legendrian lifts.

For
r
small,
\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r})\subset\{t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{n}^{2}<r^{2}\}\subset\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r+o(r)})
, hence
\mathrm{vol}(\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{C})\cap\mathcal{B}_{r})=\omega_{n}r^{n}+o(r^{n})
.

To understand the large scale behavior, first observe that since
\phi
is bounded,
\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r})\subset\{4|\varphi|<r^{2}\}\subset\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r+o(r)})
and as a result
\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r-o(r)})\subset\left\{|t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}|<\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}\right\}\subset\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{r+o(r)})
for
r
large. To compute the volume of this region, we use the fundamental domain
\mathcal{C}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}\times[0,2\pi)\times\cdots\times[0,2\pi)
of
\mathcal{C}
. Then

\mathrm{vol}(\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{C})\cap\mathcal{B}_{r})\sim\mathrm{vol}\left(\left\{(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})\in\mathcal{C}_{0}:|t_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}|<\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\right\}\right)\sim(2\pi)^{n-1}r^{2},

which shows that the quotient
(\omega_{n}r^{n})^{-1}\mathrm{vol}(\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{C})\cap\mathcal{B}_{r})
actually tends to zero for large
r
when
n>2
.
