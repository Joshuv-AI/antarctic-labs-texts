Abstract
. We study nearest-neighbour correlation functions for the ground state of the supersymmetric XYZ spin chain with odd length and periodic boundary conditions. Under a technical assumption related to the Q Q -operator of the corresponding eight-vertex model, we show that they can be expressed exactly in terms of the Painlevé VI tau functions s n s_{n} and s ¯ n \bar{s}_{n} introduced by Bazhanov and Mangazeev. Furthermore, we give an interpretation of the correlation functions in terms of the Painlevé VI Hamiltonian.



[2]

1.
Introduction

In the theory of lattice models, solvability usually refers to situations when physically relevant quantities can be computed exactly in the infinite-lattice limit. A famous example is Baxter’s computation of the ground-state energy per site for the XYZ spin chain
[
B2
]
. By contrast, it is much more unusual to encounter exact results for finite-size systems. In the context of the XYZ chain, one instance of this phenomenon was discovered by Stroganov
[
St1
]
. Baxter had already observed that, if the model’s parameters satisfy

J_{x}J_{y}+J_{x}J_{z}+J_{y}J_{z}=0

(1.1a)

and

J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}>0,

(1.1b)

then the ground-state energy per site of the infinite chain takes the remarkably simple form

-\frac{J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}}{2}.

(1.2)

Stroganov found empirically that (
1.2
) seems to hold exactly also for finite chains of odd length with periodic boundary conditions.

One conceptual explanation of why the condition (
1.1
) is special was given by Fendley and the first author
[
FH
]
. They proved that in this case the XYZ chain is supersymmetric in the sense that, roughly speaking, the Hamiltonian can be expressed as an anticommutator of nilpotent operators. This property was used in
[
HL
]
to give a rigorous proof of Stroganov’s observation.

When
J_{x}=J_{y}
, the XYZ chain reduces to the XXZ chain. It is customary to take
J_{x}=J_{y}=1
and write
J_{z}=\Delta
. The condition (
1.1
) is then
\Delta=-1/2
. There is a large literature on the XXZ chain with
\Delta=-1/2
and its relations to combinatorial objects such as loop configurations, plane partitions and alternating sign matrices. We mention here only the Razumov–Stroganov conjecture
[
RS1
]
, which was eventually proved by Cantini and Sportiello
[
CS
]
. Generalizing this work to the XYZ chain is difficult and relations to combinatorics are still not well understood. However, it seems that three-colourings should play a role
[
H1
,
H2
,
R1
]
.

An intriguing aspect of the supersymmetric XYZ chain is its relations to the Painlevé VI equation, which are present even for finite chains. Bazhanov and Mangazeev studied the ground-state eigenvalue of the
Q
-operator, in Stroganov’s setting of odd length
L=2n+1
and periodic boundary conditions
[
BM1
,
BM2
,
BM4
]
. They found that, at special values of the spectral parameter, this eigenvalue can be expressed in terms of certain polynomials denoted
s_{n}
and
\bar{s}_{n}
. It was observed that these polynomials satisfy recursions that can be used to identify them with tau functions of Painlevé VI. These recursions were proved in
[
R4
]
. It also appears that the same polynomials are directly related to the eigenvector. In an appropriate normalization and ignoring elementary factors, its square norm is conjecturally given by
s_{n}s_{-n-1}
, its component with all spins equal given by
s_{n}
and its component with all but one spin equal given by
\bar{s}_{n}
. Partial proofs of these results have been given in
[
BH
,
ZJ
]
.

In the present work, we study nearest-neighbour correlation functions for the ground state of the supersymmetric XYZ chain, still in Stroganov’s setting. We present an overview of our results on these correlation functions in §2. We find that they can be expressed in terms of a single quantity that we denote
f_{n}
, see Proposition
2.2
. In our main result, Theorem
2.3
, we express
f_{n}
in terms of the combination
\bar{s}_{n}\bar{s}_{-n-1}/s_{n}s_{-n-1}
of Painlevé tau functions. Our proof is incomplete, as it is based on a technical assumption related to the eigenvalue of the
Q
-operator. In Theorem
2.4
, we give a more direct interpretation of
f_{n}
as a Painlevé VI Hamiltonian, evaluated at a solution of another instance of Painlevé VI, with shifted parameters.

Our methods are similar to those of Stroganov
[
St1
,
St2
]
, who computed the correlation functions in the XXZ limit case, but the details are more involved. We present the details of the computation in §3. The XYZ chain can be parametrized by the crossing parameter
\eta
and the elliptic nome
\tau
, where the supersymmetric case is
\eta=\pi/3
. Using the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, we can express the quantity
f_{n}
in terms of the ground-state energy per site
\varepsilon
, see (
3.11
). We then exploit the relation between the XYZ spin chain and the eight-vertex model to deduce an expression for
f_{n}
in terms of the transfer-matrix eigenvalue
\lambda(u)
, see Proposition
3.3
. Next, we apply Baxter’s
TQ
-relation, which relates the transfer matrix and the
Q
-operator. It is at this point that we need to make an assumption, Assumption
3.4
. Although
Q
-operators are known both for generic
\eta\neq\pi/3
and for
\eta=\pi/3
, we do not know any
Q
-operator that is analytic in a neighbourhood of
\eta=\pi/3
. Thus, it is not clear that we can differentiate the
TQ
-relation with respect to
\eta
. However, assuming that this is allowed leads to an expression for
f_{n}
in terms of the
Q
-operator eigenvalue
q(u)
, see Corollary
3.8
. In the case of the XXZ chain, one can write down
q(u)
explicitly and easily finish the computation
[
St1
,
St2
]
. For the XYZ chain, most of the work remains. The key fact is a new differential-difference equation relating
q(u)
and
q(u+\pi)
, see Theorem
3.9
. We also need some properties of the polynomials
s_{n}
and
\bar{s}_{n}
proved in
[
R4
]
.

After having proved Theorem
2.3
, we turn to Theorem
2.4
in §4. Here, the key fact is the factorization (
4.2
) of the Painlevé VI Hamiltonian for special parameter values, which is also related to classical solutions of Painlevé VI.

We relegate several technical points to the appendices. In Appendix
A
, we collect a number of theta function identities that we use throughout the main text. In Appendix
B
, we present several properties of the polynomials
s_{n},\bar{s}_{n}
. Finally, we discuss a derivation of the nearest-neighbour correlation functions in the infinite-lattice limit, starting from Baxter’s formula for the ground-state energy per site, in Appendix
C
. It is interesting to note that our expression for
f_{n}
, given in Theorem
2.3
, splits naturally as an elementary term corresponding to the infinite-lattice limit and a term involving Painlevé tau functions that gives the finite-length correction.

We conclude this introduction by mentioning several open problems. Clearly, it would be interesting to find explicit expressions for other correlation functions. For instance, the one-point correlation functions are known in the infinite-lattice limit
[
BK
,
JMN
]
, but an exact finite-size result for the supersymmetric case remains to be found. Another natural candidate to investigate is the finite-size emptiness formation probability, whose expression is explicitly known for the limit of the XXZ chain at
\Delta=-1/2

[
C
]
.

One major obstruction to studying the supersymmetric XYZ chain seems to be that its ground-state eigenvectors are not known explicitly. By contrast, in the XXZ case one can write down integral formulas for all their components
[
RSZ
]
. Importantly for many applications, these formulas extend to the inhomogeneous six-vertex model.
If one had similar expressions for the eight-vertex model, one could presumably complete the proof of many properties of the ground state described in
[
BM4
,
BH
,
RS2
,
ZJ
]
. They might also be useful for proving our Assumption
3.4
and for computing more general correlation functions. We mention in this context the paper
[
FWZ
]
, which contains integral formulas of the desired type for the eight-vertex-solid-on-solid model.

One important limit of the supersymmetric XYZ chain is obtained by letting
n
and
\tau/\mathrm{i}
tend to infinity with
e^{\pi\mathrm{i}\tau}\sim n^{-2/3}

[
BM1
]
. This limit is believed to be related to the sine-Gordon model at its supersymmetric point and to polymers on a cylinder
[
L
,
FS
]
. In this limit, Painlevé VI should degenerate to Painlevé III. It would be interesting to compute the corresponding limit of the correlation functions.

The present work provides further examples of the relation between the supersymmetric XYZ spin chain and the Painlevé VI equation discovered in
[
BM2
]
. We stress that we have no conceptual explanation for this relation. Both our incomplete proof that the polynomials
s_{n}
and
\bar{s}_{n}
appear in the correlation functions and the proof that they are Painlevé tau functions
[
R4
]
emerge like miracles at the end of long computations.

Another phenomenon that calls for further explanation is the relation between the classical and quantum versions of Painlevé VI. To discuss this, let us first recall the elliptic form of Painlevé VI, given in
[
Ma
]
as

\frac{d^{2}q}{d\tau^{2}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}\beta_{j}\wp^{\prime}(q+\omega_{j}|1,\tau),

(1.3)

where
\wp
is the Weierstrass elliptic function,
\beta_{j}
are parameters and

(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})=\left(0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{\tau}{2},\frac{\tau+1}{2}\right).

Writing
p=dq/d\tau
, it is equivalent to the Hamiltonian system

\frac{dq}{d\tau}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\hskip 23.49976pt\frac{dp}{d\tau}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},

where

H=\frac{p^{2}}{2}+V(q,\tau)

and

V(q,\tau)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{3}\beta_{j}\wp(q+\omega_{j}|1,\tau)

(1.4)

is the Darboux–Treibich–Verdier potential
[
D
,
TV
,
V
]
.

The corresponding Schrödinger equation

2\pi\mathrm{i}\psi_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{xx}-V(x,\tau)\psi

(1.5)

has been called the quantum Painlevé VI equation (if
\tau\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
it is a Schrödinger equation in imaginary time, that is, a heat equation). There is a direct link between classical and quantum Painlevé VI based on Lax pairs, the so called quantum Painlevé–Calogero correspondence
[
ZZ
]
, see also
[
CD
,
N
,
Su1
,
Su2
]
.

In
[
BM1
]
, it was found that the
Q
-operator eigenvalue satisfies the non-stationary Lamé equation, which is the special case of (
1.5
) where
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=n(n+1)/2
and
\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=0
. More generally, in
[
R4
]
the second author constructed solutions to (
1.5
) with any values
\beta_{j}=k_{j}(k_{j}+1)/2
,
k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. For such parameters, the potential (
1.4
) has the so-called finite-gap property
[
TV
,
V
]
. Specializing the variable
x
to a half-period, the resulting functions of
\tau
are tau functions of classical Painlevé VI in the Picard class, that is,
\beta_{j}=l_{j}^{2}/2
,
l_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This link between quantum and classical Painlevé VI seems different from the quantum Painlevé–Calogero correspondence of
[
ZZ
]
. It would be interesting to know if there is a hidden connection.

2.
Statement of results

2.1.
Nearest-neighbour correlation functions

We write
V=\mathbb{C}|{\uparrow}\rangle\oplus\mathbb{C}|{\downarrow}\rangle
.
The Pauli matrices acting on
V
are

\sigma^{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 23.49976pt\sigma^{y}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 23.49976pt\sigma^{z}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{matrix}\right].

The XYZ spin chain is defined by the Hamiltonian

\mathbf{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(J_{x}\,\sigma_{j}^{x}\sigma_{j+1}^{x}+J_{y}\,\sigma_{j}^{y}\sigma_{j+1}^{y}+J_{z}\,\sigma_{j}^{z}\sigma_{j+1}^{z}\right)

(2.1)

acting on
V^{\otimes L}
.
Here,
J_{x}
,
J_{y}
and
J_{z}
are real anisotropy parameters and the lower indices of the Pauli matrices indicate on which tensor factor they act. We will only consider periodic boundary conditions, that is, the index
L+1
should be understood as
1
. Moreover, we will always assume that
L
is odd and write
L=2n+1
.

We are interested in the supersymmetric case (
1.1
). We write the lowest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
as
L\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon
is the ground-state energy per site. As was mentioned in the introduction, it is known that

\varepsilon=-\frac{J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}}{2}.

(2.2)

Let
\Psi
be an eigenvector of
\mathbf{H}
with eigenvalue
L\varepsilon
.
We write

\langle\mathbf{A}\rangle=\frac{\langle\Psi|\mathbf{A}|\Psi\rangle}{\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle}

for the expectation value of an observable
\mathbf{A}
with respect to the corresponding ground-state. Here,
\langle\Phi|\Phi^{\prime}\rangle
denotes the standard Hermitian scalar product of two vectors
\Phi,\Phi^{\prime}\in V^{\otimes L}
. We are interested in the nearest-neighbour correlation functions

C^{x}=\langle\sigma_{j}^{x}\sigma_{j+1}^{x}\rangle,\hskip 23.49976ptC^{y}=\langle\sigma_{j}^{y}\sigma_{j+1}^{y}\rangle,\hskip 23.49976ptC^{z}=\langle\sigma_{j}^{z}\sigma_{j+1}^{z}\rangle.

(2.3)

Lemma 2.1
.

The correlation functions (
2.3
) are independent of the choice of ground-state vector
\Psi
, as well as of the index
j
.

Proof.

Write
V^{\otimes L}=W^{+}\oplus W^{-}
, where
W^{+}
and
W^{-}
are spanned by states with, respectively, an even and an odd number of down spins.
This decomposition is preserved by
\mathbf{H}
. It is proved in
[
HL
]
that the ground-state eigenspace of
\mathbf{H}\big|_{W^{+}}
is one-dimensional. Let
\Psi^{+}
be a vector in this space, normalized so that
\langle\Psi^{+}|\Psi^{+}\rangle=1
. Then, the whole eigenspace is spanned by
\Psi^{+}
and

\Psi^{-}=\mathbf{F}\Psi^{+}\in W^{-}
, where
\mathbf{F}=\prod_{j=1}^{L}\sigma^{x}_{j}
is the spin-flip operator.
Let
\Psi=\alpha\Psi^{+}+\beta\Psi^{-}
be an arbitrary ground-state vector. By orthogonality,

\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}\langle\Psi^{+}|\Psi^{+}\rangle+|\beta|^{2}\langle\Psi^{-}|\Psi^{-}\rangle=|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}.

Moreover, for
a\in\{x,y,z\}
,

\displaystyle\langle\Psi|\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|\alpha|^{2}\langle\Psi^{+}|\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}|\Psi^{+}\rangle+|\beta|^{2}\langle\Psi^{-}|\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}|\Psi^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=(|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2})\langle\Psi^{+}|\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}|\Psi^{+}\rangle,

where we used in the first step that the operator
\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}
preserves the spaces
W^{\pm}
and in the second step that it commutes with
\mathbf{F}
. This shows that the correlation functions (
2.3
) do not change if we replace
\Psi
by
\Psi^{+}
. The final statement follows since
\Psi^{+}
is translation invariant
[
HL
]
(or, alternatively, since by the first statement we could replace
\Psi^{+}
by any one of its translates).
∎

Our goal is to compute the correlation functions (
2.3
) exactly.
As a first step, we express them in terms of a single quantity that we denote
f_{n}
.
It will be convenient to introduce the normalized discriminant

Z=\frac{(J_{x}-J_{y})^{2}(J_{y}-J_{z})^{2}(J_{x}-J_{z})^{2}}{J_{x}^{2}J_{y}^{2}J_{z}^{2}}=-4\frac{(J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z})^{3}}{J_{x}J_{y}J_{z}}-27,

(2.4)

where the second equality depends on (
1.1
).

Proposition 2.2
.

For the supersymmetric periodic XYZ spin chain of odd length
L=2n+1
,
we can write

C^{a}=1+\frac{J_{x}J_{y}J_{z}}{J_{a}^{2}(J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z})}\,f_{n},\hskip 23.49976pta\in\{x,y,z\},

(2.5)

where

f_{n}
is a rational function of
Z
.

For small
n
, it is straightforward to compute the ground-state eigenvectors of the supersymmetric XYZ Hamiltonian and infer
f_{n}
. To give some examples, we find for
n=0,\dots,5
the expressions

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z+27}{Z+25},

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{(Z+24)(Z+27)}{(Z+21)(Z+28)},

\displaystyle f_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{Z^{3}+74Z^{2}+1807Z+14520}{Z^{3}+72Z^{2}+1701Z+13068},

\displaystyle f_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{(Z+27)(Z^{4}+96Z^{3}+3420Z^{2}+53404Z+306735)}{(Z^{2}+44Z+429)(Z^{3}+77Z^{2}+1991Z+17303)}.

Since the correlation functions only depend on the Hamiltonian up to normalization,
we may use the parametrization

J_{x}=1+\zeta,\hskip 23.49976ptJ_{y}=1-\zeta,\hskip 23.49976ptJ_{z}=\frac{\zeta^{2}-1}{2}.

(2.6)

Then, (
2.5
) takes the form

C^{x}=1-\frac{(1-\zeta)^{2}f_{n}}{\zeta^{2}+3},\hskip 11.74988ptC^{y}=1-\frac{(1+\zeta)^{2}f_{n}}{\zeta^{2}+3},\hskip 11.74988ptC^{z}=1-\frac{4f_{n}}{\zeta^{2}+3},

(2.7)

where
f_{n}
is a function of

Z=\frac{\zeta^{2}(\zeta^{2}-9)^{2}}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{2}}.

(2.8)

The symmetry of
Z
under permutations of the anisotropy parameters corresponds to
the fact that
Z(\zeta)=Z(\zeta^{\prime})
if and only if

\zeta^{\prime}\in\{\pm\zeta,\pm\gamma,\pm\delta\}
, where

\gamma=\frac{\zeta+3}{\zeta-1},\hskip 23.49976pt\delta=\frac{\zeta-3}{\zeta+1}.

That is,

f_{n}(\pm\zeta)=f_{n}(\pm\gamma)=f_{n}(\pm\delta).

(2.9)

Indeed, it is easy to check that the transformations
\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{\prime}
permute
the parameters (
2.6
) up to normalization.

2.2.
Correlation functions and tau functions

Our main result gives an explicit formula for
f_{n}
in terms of the polynomials
s_{n}
and
\bar{s}_{n}
introduced by Bazhanov and Mangazeev
[
BM1
]
. As was conjectured in
[
BM2
]
and proved in
[
R4
]
, they can be identified with tau functions of Painlevé VI and, hence, satisfy a Toda-type recursion. For
s_{n}=s_{n}(z)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, this recursion takes the form

8(2n+1)^{2}s_{n+1}s_{n-1}+2z(z-1)(9z-1)^{2}(s_{n}^{\prime\prime}s_{n}-(s_{n}^{\prime})^{2})+2(3z-1)^{2}(9z-1)s_{n}s_{n}^{\prime}\\
-\big(4(3n+1)(3n+2)+n(5n+3)(9z-1)\big)s_{n}^{2}=0

(2.10)

with starting values
s_{0}=s_{1}=1
. To obtain
\bar{s}_{n}
, one should replace
n(5n+3)
by
(n-1)(5n+4)
and the starting values by
\bar{s}_{0}=1
,
\bar{s}_{1}=3
. It was conjectured in
[
MB
]
that, in a certain natural normalization of the eigenvector
\Psi
, its square norm is essentially given by
s_{n}(\zeta^{-2})s_{-n-1}(\zeta^{-2})
. A partial proof of this conjecture is given in
[
ZJ
]
. Thus, it is natural to expect this product in the denominator of
f_{n}
. More surprisingly, it appears that the numerator can be expressed in terms of
\bar{s}_{n}\bar{s}_{-n-1}
. Although we have checked the following result for chains up to length
L=11
, we have only proved it under a technical assumption that will be explained below.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
Assumption
3.4
holds, then

f_{n}=\frac{(\zeta^{2}+3)(\zeta^{2}-3)}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{2}}-\frac{2\zeta^{2}(\zeta^{2}+3)}{(2n+1)^{2}(\zeta^{2}-1)^{2}}\frac{\bar{s}_{n}(\zeta^{-2})\bar{s}_{-n-1}(\zeta^{-2})}{s_{n}(\zeta^{-2})s_{-n-1}(\zeta^{-2})}.

(2.11)

By (
2.9
), one can obtain seemingly different expressions for
f_{n}
by replacing
\zeta
in (
2.11
) with
\gamma
or
\delta
.

The decomposition of
f_{n}
into two terms has a natural interpretation. Namely, in Appendix
C
we
argue that
f_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}
is given by

f_{\infty}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(\zeta^{2}+3)(\zeta^{2}-3)}{(\zeta^{2}-1)^{2}},&|\zeta|\geq 3,\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(\delta^{2}+3)(\delta^{2}-3)}{(\delta^{2}-1)^{2}}=-\frac{(\zeta^{2}+3)(\zeta^{2}+6\zeta-3)}{8(\zeta-1)^{2}},&-3\leq\zeta\leq 0,\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma^{2}+3)(\gamma^{2}-3)}{(\gamma^{2}-1)^{2}}=-\frac{(\zeta^{2}+3)(\zeta^{2}-6\zeta-3)}{8(\zeta+1)^{2}},&0\leq\zeta\leq 3.\\
\end{cases}

(2.12)

Hence, when
|\zeta|\geq 3
,
the first term in (
2.11
) gives the infinite-lattice limit and the second term the finite length correction. The variants of (
2.11
) with
\zeta
replaced by
\gamma
and
\delta
have a similar interpretation in the other parameter regimes.
In Appendix
C
, we deduce (
2.12
) from Baxter’s formula for the ground-state energy per site in the infinite-lattice limit. We do not know how to prove (
2.12
) directly from (
2.11
). In Figure
1
, we illustrate the convergence of
f_{n}
to
f_{\infty}
.

f_{n}
for
n=1,\dots,5
(dotted lines) and

.

To see that Theorem
2.3
gives the correct result for the XXZ chain, we replace
\zeta
by
\gamma
in (

) and let
\zeta=0
,
\gamma=-3
. Then, the parameters (
2.6
) reduce to

,

. At the corresponding value

, the recursion (

) for

and the recursion for

. This leads to

and we recover Stroganov’s results

in the limit

, which corresponds to

and

(after rescaling the Hamiltonian appropriately). Indeed, it follows from
[

, Thm. 4.1]
that
s_{n}(0)\neq 0
for all
n
,
\bar{s}_{n}(0)\neq 0
for
n\geq 0
and that
\bar{s}_{-n-1}(z)
is divisible by

for

. Hence, the second term in (

) tends to zero (except when

) and we obtain

,

. This is trivial to check directly since the ground-state vector can be chosen as

.

2.3.
The Painlevé Hamiltonian

The expression (
2.11
) can be interpreted as the Painlevé VI Hamiltonian, evaluated at a solution to the same equation with different parameters. To explain this, we recall the algebraic form of Painlevé VI,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}q}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q-1}+\frac{1}{q-t}\right)\left(\frac{dq}{dt}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{t-1}+\frac{1}{q-t}\right)\frac{dq}{dt}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.74988pt+\frac{q(q-1)(q-t)}{t^{2}(t-1)^{2}}\left(\alpha+\beta\frac{t}{q^{2}}+\gamma\frac{t-1}{(q-1)^{2}}+\delta\frac{t(t-1)}{(q-t)^{2}}\right).

(2.13)

As in
[
NY

3.
Computation of the correlation functions

3.1.
The eight-vertex model

To compute
f_{n}
, we will exploit the relation between the XYZ spin chain and the eight-vertex model
[
B2
]
. With respect to the basis

|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle
,
|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle
,
|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle
,
|{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle
of
V^{\otimes 2}
, the
R
-matrix of
the eight-vertex model is given by

R=\left[\begin{matrix}a&0&0&d\\
0&b&c&0\\
0&c&b&0\\
d&0&0&a\end{matrix}\right],

where
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
are Boltzmann weights. The transfer matrix is the operator on
V^{\otimes L}
defined by

\mathbf{T}=\operatorname{Tr}_{0}(R_{01}R_{02}\dotsm R_{0L}),

where the index
0
refers to an additional auxiliary copy of
V
. The parameter combinations

\zeta=\frac{cd}{ab},\hskip 23.49976pt\Gamma=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2}-d^{2}}{2ab}

(3.1)

play a special role. Indeed, if
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
is another set of parameters such that
\zeta^{\prime}=\zeta
and
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma
, then the corresponding transfer matrices
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{T}^{\prime}
commute.

An important step in Baxter’s solution of the eight-vertex model is to parametrize the Boltzmann weights by theta functions. In our conventions,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\rho\,\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u-\eta|2\tau)\theta_{1}(u+\eta|2\tau),

(3.2a)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\rho\,\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{1}(u-\eta|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u+\eta|2\tau),

(3.2b)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\rho\,\theta_{1}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u-\eta|2\tau)\theta_{4}(u+\eta|2\tau),

(3.2c)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\rho\,\theta_{1}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{1}(u-\eta|2\tau)\theta_{1}(u+\eta|2\tau);

(3.2d)

see (
A.1
) for the definition of Jacobi theta functions. The spectral parameter
u
and the crossing parameter
\eta
can be taken as complex, and the modular parameter
\tau
as complex in the upper half-plane. Without loss of generality, we fix the normalization
\rho
as

\rho=\frac{2}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)},

which implies

a+b=\frac{\theta_{1}(2\eta|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(\eta|\tau)}\,\theta_{1}(u|\tau).

(3.3)

As a function of
u
, the transfer matrix satisfies the quasi-periodicity and reflection symmetry

\mathbf{T}(u+\pi)=\mathbf{T}(-u)=(-1)^{L}\mathbf{T}(u),\hskip 23.49976pt\mathbf{T}(u+\pi\tau)=(-1)^{L}e^{-\mathrm{i}L(2u+\pi\tau)}\mathbf{T}(u).

(3.4)

In the parametrization (
3.2
), the quantities (
3.1
) are independent of
u
. More precisely
[
RS2
, Eqs. (2.6)–(2.7)]
,

\zeta=\frac{\theta_{1}(2\eta|2\tau)^{2}}{\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)^{2}},\hskip 23.49976pt\Gamma=\frac{\theta_{2}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{3}(2\eta|2\tau)\theta_{4}(0|2\tau)^{2}}{\theta_{2}(0|2\tau)\theta_{3}(0|2\tau)\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)^{2}}.

(3.5)

Hence, the transfer matrices
\mathbf{T}(u)
form a commuting family for fixed
\eta
and
\tau
. Moreover, the XYZ Hamiltonian is contained within this family in the sense that

\mathbf{H}=\frac{L(a_{u}+c_{u})}{2b_{u}}\,\operatorname{Id}-\frac{a}{b_{u}}\,\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_{u}\bigg|_{u=\eta},

(3.6)

where the subscripts denote
u
-derivatives and the parameters are related through

J_{x}=1+\zeta,\hskip 23.49976ptJ_{y}=1-\zeta,\hskip 23.49976ptJ_{z}=\Gamma.

(3.7)

As was noted by Baxter
[
B2
]
, when
\eta=\pi/3
the supersymmetry condition (
1.1
) holds. That is,
\Gamma=(\zeta^{2}-1)/2
and we recover (
2.6
).

Let us choose
\Psi=\Psi^{+}
as in the proof of Lemma
2.1
. It belongs to the sector
W^{+}
spanned by states with an even number of down spins. Since
W^{+}
is preserved by
\mathbf{H}
, the ground-state eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}\big|_{W^{+}}
remains single on
W^{+}
for
\eta
near
\pi/3
. We will still denote this eigenvalue by
L\varepsilon
, where we know from (
2.2
) that

\varepsilon\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=-\frac{(\zeta^{2}+3)}{4}.

(3.8)

We extend
\Psi
to an eigenvector that depends analytically on
\eta
. It is also an eigenvector of the transfer matrices
\mathbf{T}(u)
; the eigenvalue will be denoted
\lambda
. It was conjectured in
[
St1
]
and proved in
[
HL
]
that

\lambda\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=\theta_{1}(u|\tau)^{L}.

(3.9)

For generic
\eta
, there are no simple finite-size expressions for neither
\varepsilon
nor
\lambda
, but (
3.6
) gives the relation

\varepsilon=\frac{a_{u}+c_{u}}{2b_{u}}-\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\Bigg|_{u=\eta}.

(3.10)

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
2.2

We will now prove that Proposition
2.2
holds, with

f_{n}=\frac{\varepsilon(2\varepsilon_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta})}{\zeta\zeta_{\eta}-\Gamma_{\eta}}\Bigg|_{\eta=\pi/3},

(3.11)

where the subscripts on the right-hand side denote
\eta
-derivatives.

We first note that (

) implies

L\varepsilon=\langle\mathbf{H}\rangle=-\frac{L}{2}\left(J_{x}C^{x}+J_{y}C^{y}+J_{z}C^{z}\right).

(3.12)

The Hellmann–Feynman theorem asserts that, if
\mathbf{H}
depends smoothly on a parameter, then

\langle\mathbf{H}\rangle^{\prime}=\langle\mathbf{H}^{\prime}\rangle,

where the prime denotes the derivative with respect to the parameter. Thus, we can write

\varepsilon^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(J_{x}^{\prime}C^{x}+J_{y}^{\prime}C^{y}+J_{z}^{\prime}C^{z}\right).

The derivative may be taken with respect to either
\eta
or
\tau
. Together with (
3.12
), we thus obtain a system of three linear equations for
C^{x}
,
C^{y}
and
C^{z}
. Consider this system at the point
\eta=\pi/3
. Using (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), it can be written

\left[\begin{matrix}1+\zeta&1-\zeta&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{2}-1)\\
\displaystyle\zeta_{\eta}&\displaystyle-\zeta_{\eta}&\Gamma_{\eta}\\
1&-1&\zeta\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}C^{x}\\
C^{y}\\
C^{z}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{2}+3)\\
\displaystyle-2\varepsilon_{\eta}\\
\zeta\end{matrix}\right].

(3.13)

The determinant of the coefficient matrix in (

) is

2(\Gamma_{\eta}-\zeta\zeta_{\eta})
. By Lemma

,

\zeta\zeta_{\eta}-\Gamma_{\eta}\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=6\chi\frac{a}{b_{u}}\Big|_{u=\eta=\pi/3},

(3.14)

where

\chi=\frac{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{2}(\pi/3|\tau)^{2}}{\theta_{1}(\pi/3|\tau)^{2}\theta_{2}(0|\tau)^{2}}.

(3.15)

This expression implies, in particular, that the determinant is non-zero for generic
\tau
. It is then easy to see that (

) has the unique solution (

), where

is given by (
3.11
). As we have noted, (

) can equivalently be written as (
2.5
).

It remains to show that

is a rational function of
Z
. Note that the eigenvalue equation for
\Psi
is a linear system whose coefficients are linear in
(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})
. Hence, we may normalize
\Psi
so that its coefficients are homogeneous polynomials in the anisotropy parameters. Then, (
2.5
)
defines

as a homogeneous rational function of
J_{x}
,
J_{y}
and
J_{z}
.
To complete the proof we need the following fact.

Lemma 3.1
.

Permuting the anisotropy parameters entails a corresponding permutation of the correlation functions. That is, if
(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z})
is any permutation of the symbols
(x,y,z)
and we consider the correlations as functions of the anisotropy parameters, then

C^{a}(J_{\tilde{x}},J_{\tilde{y}},J_{\tilde{z}})=C^{\tilde{a}}(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z}),\hskip 23.49976pta\in\{x,y,z\}.

(3.16)

Proof.

Let

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
be the operator obtained from the Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}
after replacing the parameters

(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})
with
(J_{\tilde{x}},J_{\tilde{y}},J_{\tilde{z}})
.
It is well-known that there exists
U\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
such that
\sigma^{a}=\pm U\sigma^{\tilde{a}}U^{-1}
,
a\in\{x,y,z\}
. The signs involved in these formulas will be irrelevant.
Writing
\mathbf{U}=U^{\otimes L}
, we have

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{U}^{-1}
. Given a ground-state eigenvector
\Psi
of
\mathbf{H}
, we can obtain a ground-state eigenvector of
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
as
\mathbf{U}\Psi
. By Lemma
2.1
, we may use
\mathbf{U}\Psi
to compute the left-hand side of (
3.16
). That is, we are reduced to the identity

\frac{\langle\mathbf{U}\Psi|\sigma_{j}^{a}\sigma_{j+1}^{a}|\mathbf{U}\Psi\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{U}\Psi|\mathbf{U}\Psi\rangle}=\frac{\langle\Psi|\sigma_{j}^{\tilde{a}}\sigma_{j+1}^{\tilde{a}}|\Psi\rangle}{\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle},

which is clear from the properties of
U
.
∎

It is easy to see that
the expressions (
2.5
) are consistent with Lemma

only if

is invariant under permutation of the anisotropy parameters. It must then be
a rational expression in

e_{1}=J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}
,
e_{2}=J_{x}J_{y}+J_{x}J_{z}+J_{y}J_{z}
and
e_{3}=J_{x}J_{y}J_{z}
.
Since
e_{2}=0
and
f_{n}
is homogeneous,
it is in fact a function of
e_{1}^{3}/e_{3}
or, equivalently, of
Z
.
This completes the proof of Proposition
2.2
.

3.3.
Expressions in terms of the transfer-matrix eigenvalue

The next step is to express (

) in terms of the transfer matrix eigenvalue

.

Lemma 3.2
.

One has

\varepsilon_{\eta}+\frac{\Gamma_{\eta}}{2}\Bigg|_{\eta=\pi/3}=-\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}\frac{\lambda_{u\eta}\lambda-\lambda_{u}\lambda_{\eta}}{\lambda^{2}}\Bigg|_{u=\eta=\pi/3}.

(3.17)

Proof.

Since
a=c
and
b=d=0
when
u=\eta
, the case
u\rightarrow\eta
of (
3.1
) is

\Gamma=\frac{a_{u}-c_{u}}{b_{u}}\Bigg|_{u=\eta}.

Combining this with (
3.10
) gives

\varepsilon+\frac{\Gamma}{2}=\frac{a_{u}}{b_{u}}-\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\Bigg|_{u=\eta}.

Writing the variables as
(u,\eta)
, we split this expression as
T_{1}+T_{2}
, where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{u}}{b_{u}}(\eta,\eta)-\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}(\eta,\eta)\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\left(\eta,\pi/3\right),

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}(\eta,\eta)\left(\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\left(\eta,\pi/3\right)-\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\left(\eta,\eta\right)\right).

By (
3.3
) and (
3.9
),

\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}(u,\pi/3)=L\frac{a_{u}+b_{u}}{a+b}(u,\eta),

where
\eta
on the right-hand side is arbitrary. In particular,
taking
u=\eta
and using
that
b(\eta,\eta)=0
gives

\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}(\eta,\pi/3)=L\frac{a_{u}+b_{u}}{a}(\eta,\eta).

It follows that
T_{1}=-1
. Hence, the only contribution to (
3.17
) comes from
T_{2}
.
To give a non-zero contribution, the derivative must hit the second factor
and we obtain

\frac{\partial T_{2}}{\partial\eta}\Bigg|_{\eta=\pi/3}=-\frac{a}{Lb_{u}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\frac{\lambda_{u}}{\lambda}\Bigg|_{u=\eta=\pi/3},

which equals the right-hand side of (
3.17
).
∎

Using also (
3.8
) and (
3.14
), we may now rewrite (
3.11
) as follows.

Proposition 3.3
.

The quantity
f_{n}
appearing in (
2.7
) can be expressed as

f_{n}=\frac{\zeta^{2}+3}{12L\chi}\frac{\lambda_{u\eta}\lambda-\lambda_{u}\lambda_{\eta}}{\lambda^{2}}\Bigg|_{u=\eta=\pi/3}.

3.4.
The
TQ
-relation

Let

\phi(u)=\lambda(u)\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=\theta_{1}(u|\tau)^{L}.

We have reduced the computation of the correlation functions to the evaluation of

\lambda_{u\eta}\lambda-\lambda_{u}\lambda_{\eta}\Big|_{u=\eta=\pi/3}=\lambda_{u\eta}\phi-\phi_{u}\lambda_{\eta}\Big|_{u=\eta=\pi/3}.

To handle the
\eta
-derivatives, we will exploit Baxter’s
TQ
-relation.

A
Q
-operator is a family of operators
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}(u)
(depending also on
\eta
and
\tau
) acting on
V^{\otimes L}
such that
\mathbf{Q}(u)
and
\mathbf{T}(v)
always commute, and

\mathbf{T}(u)\mathbf{Q}(u)=\phi(u-\eta)\mathbf{Q}(u+2\eta)+\phi(u+\eta)\mathbf{Q}(u-2\eta).

It is also natural to assume the quasi-periodicity and reflection symmetry

\mathbf{Q}(u+2\pi)=\mathbf{Q}(-u)=\mathbf{Q}(u),\hskip 23.49976pt\mathbf{Q}(u+2\pi\tau)=e^{-2\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau)}\mathbf{Q}(u),

which are consistent with (
3.4
).

Baxter gave two different constructions of
Q
-operators for the eight-vertex model. Unfortunately, neither of these work in our situation, that is, for odd
L
and
\eta=\pi/3
. Baxter’s first
Q
-operator
[
B1
]
is defined in terms of the inverse of an auxiliary operator
\mathbf{Q}_{R}
, which is not invertible for
\eta=\pi/3
. His second definition
[
B3
]
only works for even
L
. Different constructions due to Fabricius
[
F
]
and Roan
[
Ro
]
, give solutions defined at
\eta=\pi/3
, but as we also need the
\eta
-derivative at
\eta=\pi/3
, that is not enough for our purposes. One possible way out of this problem was suggested by Bazhanov and Mangazeev
[
BM1
]
. They propose that the ground-state eigenvector
\Psi
belongs to a subspace of
V^{\otimes L}
where
\mathbf{Q}_{R}
is invertible. Restricting Baxter’s first
Q
-operator
\mathbf{Q}
to that space, one would then have
\mathbf{Q}\Psi=Q\Psi
, where the eigenvalue
Q=Q(u)
satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda(u)Q(u)=\phi(u-\eta)Q(u+2\eta)+\phi(u+\eta)Q(u-2\eta),

(3.18a)

\displaystyle Q(u+2\pi)=Q(-u)=Q(u),\hskip 23.49976ptQ(u+2\pi\tau)=e^{-2\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau)}Q(u).

(3.18b)

Our results will be derived from these identities, which still lack a rigorous proof. More formally, we make the following assumption:

Assumption 3.4
.

There exists a non-zero function
Q
that satisfies (
3.18
) and is
analytic for all

u\in\mathbb{C}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\tau)>0
and
\eta
in some neighbourhood of
\pi/3
(that may depend on
u
and
\tau
).

Lemma 3.5
.

If
Assumption
3.4
holds, then
the space of functions satisfying the conditions described there is two-dimensional. It is spanned by
two functions
Q^{\pm}
that satisfy

Q^{\pm}(u+\pi)=Q^{\mp}(u),\hskip 23.49976ptQ^{\pm}(u+\pi\tau)=\pm e^{-\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau/2)}Q^{\pm}(u).

(3.19)

Moreover, writing
Q^{\pm}=Q^{\pm}(u|\tau)
, these functions can be normalized so that

Q^{\pm}(u|\tau+2)=Q^{\mp}(u|\tau).

(3.20)

Proof.

By (
3.4
), the transfer-matrix eigenvalue
\lambda(u)
obeys

\lambda(u+\pi)=-\lambda(u),\hskip 23.49976pt\lambda(u+\pi\tau)=-e^{-\mathrm{i}L(2u+\pi\tau)}\lambda(u),

and we also have

\phi(u+\pi)=-\phi(u),\hskip 23.49976pt\phi(u+\pi\tau)=-e^{-\mathrm{i}L(2u+\pi\tau)}\phi(u).

Using these relations, it is easy to check that if
Q(u)
solves (
3.18
), then so do
Q(u+\pi)
and
e^{\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau/2)}Q(u+\pi\tau)
.
We can then write any solution as
Q=\tilde{Q}_{+}+\tilde{Q}^{-}
, where

\tilde{Q}^{\pm}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left(Q(u)\pm e^{\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau/2)}Q(u+\pi\tau)\right).

If
Q
is non-zero, the functions
\tilde{Q}^{\pm}
cannot both vanish identically.
If
\tilde{Q}^{+}\not\equiv 0
, we let
Q^{+}=\tilde{Q}^{+}
and define

Q^{-}(u)=Q^{+}(u+\pi)
. Otherwise, we let
Q^{-}=\tilde{Q}^{-}
and
Q^{+}(u)=Q^{-}(u+\pi)
.
It is then clear that
Q^{\pm}
satisfy (
3.18
) and (
3.19
).

We will write
q(u)=Q^{+}(u)\big|_{\eta=\pi/3}
. This function satisfies

\displaystyle\phi(u)q(u)+\phi\left(u+\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)q\left(u+\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)+\phi\left(u-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)q\left(u-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=0,

(3.21a)

\displaystyle q(u+2\pi)=q(-u)=q(u),\hskip 23.49976ptq(u+\pi\tau)=e^{-\mathrm{i}L(u+\pi\tau/2)}q(u).

(3.21b)

It is proved in
[
R2
]
that
the space of entire functions subject to these conditions is one-dimensional.
(More precisely, it follows from the discussion in

[
R2
, §5.4]
that this is a consequence of

[
R2
, Thm. 2.4]
.)
Since a small perturbation cannot increase the dimension of the solution space,
our assumption that a non-zero
Q
exists implies that
Q^{+}
remains unique for
\eta
near
\pi/3
.
In particular, the function
\tilde{Q}^{+}
is proportional to
Q^{+}
and similarly
\tilde{Q}^{-}
is proportional to
Q^{-}
.
Hence, any solution
Q
is in the span of
Q^{+}
and
Q^{-}
.

For the final statement, we first note that
Q^{-}(u|\tau+2)=Q^{+}(\pi-u|\tau+2)
satisfies the properties defining
Q^{+}
, so there exists

f(\tau)
(depending also on
\eta
) such that

Q^{+}(\pi-u|\tau+2)=f(\tau)Q^{+}(u|\tau).

In particular,
u=\pi/2
has the same multiplicity as a zero of both
Q^{+}(u|\tau+2)
and
Q^{+}(u|\tau)
. If
k
is this multiplicity and
a(\tau)
is the leading Taylor coefficient of
Q^{+}(u|\tau)
at
u=\pi/2
, then

f(\tau)=\lim_{u\rightarrow\pi/2}\frac{Q^{+}(\pi-u|\tau+2)}{Q^{+}(u|\tau)}=(-1)^{k}\frac{a(\tau+2)}{a(\tau)}.

It is now easy to check that, if
k
is even, then the renormalized functions
\tilde{Q}^{\pm}=Q^{\pm}/a
satisfy
\tilde{Q}^{\pm}(u|\tau+2)=\tilde{Q}^{\mp}(u|\tau)
. If
k
is odd, one can instead take
\tilde{Q}^{\pm}=e^{\mathrm{i}\pi\tau/2}Q^{\pm}/a
.
∎

From now on, we fix two functions
Q^{\pm}
satisfying all conditions in
Lemma
3.5
. Writing, as in the proof,
q=Q^{+}|_{\eta=\pi/3}
,
we have in particular

q(u+\pi|\tau)=q(u|\tau+2).

(3.22)

The functions
Q^{\pm}
satisfy the Wronskian relations
[

Appendix C
Infinite-lattice limit

Correlation functions for the infinite-length XYZ spin chain have been studied by various methods, see e.g.
[
B4
,
BJ
,
LP1
,
LP2
,
Q
,
S
]
. In this appendix, we will sketch how such correlation functions can be computed in the special case of interest to us. We have not made a detailed comparison with more general results available in the literature, but the remark at the end of
[
BJ
, §4.1]
indicates that our expression should agree with
[
BJ
, Eq. (4.2)]
, after a suitable identification of parameters.

In this appendix, we write the lowest eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian for a chain of length
L
as
L\varepsilon_{L}
and use
\varepsilon
for the infinite-lattice limit
\varepsilon=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{L}
(assuming convergence). We have shown that when
L=2n+1
, our correlations can be expressed in terms of the quantity (
3.11
),

f_{n}=\frac{\varepsilon_{L}(2(\varepsilon_{L})_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta})}{\zeta\zeta_{\eta}-\Gamma_{\eta}}\Bigg|_{\eta=\pi/3}.

If we let
n\rightarrow\infty
, we find that identical expressions (
2.5
) hold for the correlations, but with
f_{n}
replaced by

f_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}=\frac{\varepsilon(2\varepsilon_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta})}{\zeta\zeta_{\eta}-\Gamma_{\eta}}\Bigg|_{\eta=\pi/3}.

(C.1)

We will compute
f_{\infty}
directly using Baxter’s explicit expression for
\varepsilon
. We note that Baxter obtained this expression, and several of its properties, by considering the infinite-lattice limit along chains of even length. In contrast, we focus on odd lengths. Hence, we need to assume that the limits along chains of even and odd lengths lead to the same result. Although this assumption seems plausible, we have no proof that it holds.

We start from
[
B5
, (10.14.30)]
, written in Baxter’s notation as

\varepsilon=\frac{-J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}}{2}\\
-\frac{\pi(J_{x}^{2}-J_{y}^{2})^{1/2}}{2I\sqrt{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{-m}(x^{3m}-q^{m/2})(1-q^{m/2}x^{-m})(1-x^{2m})}{(1-q^{m})(1+x^{2m})}.

(C.2)

Here,
I,k,q,x
are functions of the spin chain’s anisotropy parameters. The expression for
\varepsilon
holds if these parameters are in the so-called principal regime, defined through the inequalities

|J_{y}|<J_{x}<-J_{z}.

The values in other regimes follow from the fact that
\varepsilon
is invariant if the parameters
(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})
are permuted or if any two of them are multiplied by
-1
.

We parametrize the chain as in (
3.5
), (
3.7
), where we now take
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. One can show that
0<\zeta<1
and hence

|J_{z}|<J_{y}<J_{x}
. Thus, we should apply (
C.2
) with the replacements
(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})\mapsto(J_{y},-J_{z},-J_{x})
. The parameters in (
C.2
) are related to ours by

q^{1/2}=e^{-\pi\mathrm{i}/\tau},\hskip 23.49976ptx=e^{\mathrm{i}(2\eta-\pi)/\tau}

and one can compute

\frac{\pi}{2I\sqrt{k}}=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\tau\theta_{4}(0|\tau)^{2}},\hskip 23.49976pt(J_{y}^{2}-J_{z}^{2})^{1/2}=\frac{2\theta_{4}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{1}(2\eta|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)}.

The replacements, together with these expressions, lead us to the identity

\varepsilon=-\frac{J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}}{2}\\
-\frac{4\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(2\eta|2\tau)}{\tau\theta_{2}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{4}(2\eta|2\tau)}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{-m}(x^{3m}-q^{m/2})(1-q^{m/2}x^{-m})(1-x^{2m})}{(1-q^{m})(1+x^{2m})}.

(C.3)

The supersymmetric case
\eta=\pi/3
corresponds to

x^{3}=q^{1/2}
. In this case, all terms in the infinite series vanish and we recover (
1.2
), that is,

\varepsilon\big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=-\frac{J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}}{2}=-\frac{\zeta^{2}+3}{4}.

(C.4)

Let us now consider the quantity
2\varepsilon_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta}\big|_{\eta=\pi/3}
. Since
\Gamma=J_{x}+J_{y}+J_{z}-2
, the contribution from the first term on the right-hand side of (
C.3
) vanishes. In the remaining terms, the derivative with respect to
\eta
must hit the factor
x^{3m}-q^{m/2}
to yield a non-zero contribution when
\eta=\pi/3
. It follows that

2\varepsilon_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta}\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=\frac{48\,\theta_{1}(2\pi/3|2\tau)}{\tau^{2}\theta_{2}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{4}(2\pi/3|2\tau)}\,S,

(C.5)

where

S=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{mq^{\frac{m}{3}}(1-q^{\frac{m}{3}})^{2}}{(1-q^{m})(1+q^{\frac{m}{3}})}.

To compute
S
we will use the identity
[
WW
, §21, Ex. 11]

\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\log\theta_{4}(u|\tau)=4\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\pi\tau m}\sin(2mu)}{1-e^{2\mathrm{i}\pi\tau m}},\hskip 23.49976pt|\operatorname{Im}(u)|<\frac{\pi}{2}\,\operatorname{Im}(\tau),

which implies

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log\theta_{4}(u|\tau)=8\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{me^{\mathrm{i}\pi\tau m}\cos(2mu)}{1-e^{2\mathrm{i}\pi\tau m}}.

Rewriting (
C.5
) with the help of the identity

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\frac{m}{3}}(1-q^{\frac{m}{3}})^{2}}{(1-q^{m})(1+q^{\frac{m}{3}})}

\displaystyle=q^{m}\frac{4-3(q^{\frac{m}{3}}+q^{-\frac{m}{3}})+(q^{\frac{2m}{3}}+q^{-\frac{2m}{3}})}{1-q^{2m}}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}m/\tau}\frac{4-6\cos\left(\frac{2\pi m}{3\tau}\right)+2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi m}{3\tau}\right)}{1-e^{-4\pi\mathrm{i}m/\tau}},

S=\frac{1}{4}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log\frac{\theta_{4}\big(u|-\frac{2}{\tau}\big)^{2}\theta_{4}\big(u+\frac{2\pi}{3\tau}|-\frac{2}{\tau}\big)}{\theta_{4}\big(u+\frac{\pi}{3\tau}|-\frac{2}{\tau}\big)^{3}}\right|_{u=0}.

Applying (
A.3
) gives

S=\frac{\tau^{2}}{16}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log g(u)\right|_{u=0},

where

2\varepsilon_{\eta}+\Gamma_{\eta}\Big|_{\eta=\pi/3}=\frac{3\theta_{1}(2\pi/3|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)^{2}\theta_{4}(2\pi/3|2\tau)}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log g(u)\right|_{u=0}.

(C.6)

To proceed, we use some theta function identities. On the one hand, we have

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log g(u)\right|_{u=0}=\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(0)g(0)-g^{\prime}(0)^{2}}{g(0)^{2}}=\left.\frac{1}{2g(0)^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}g(u)g(-u)\right|_{u=0}.

On the other hand, using (
A.12
