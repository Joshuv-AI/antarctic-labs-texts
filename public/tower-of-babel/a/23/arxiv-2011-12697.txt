A Lepskiĭ-type stopping rule for the covariance estimation of multi-dimensional Lévy processes
Katerina Papagiannoulilabel=e1]papagiai@hu-berlin.de

Abstract
We suppose that a Lévy process is observed at discrete time points. Starting from an asymptotically minimax family of estimators for the continuous part of the Lévy Khinchine characteristics, i.e., the covariance, we derive a data-driven parameter choice for the frequency of estimating the covariance. We investigate a Lepskiĭ-type stopping rule for the adaptive procedure. Consequently, we use a balancing principle for the best possible data-driven parameter. The adaptive estimator achieves almost the optimal rate. Numerical experiments with the proposed selection rule are also presented.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of multi-dimensional Lévy processes for modeling purposes has become very popular in many areas, especially in the field of finance (e.g.
Cont and Tankov 2004
; see also
Sato 1999
for a comprehensive study). The distribution of a Lévy process is usually specified by its characteristic triplet (drift, Gaussian component, and Lévy measure) rather than by the distribution of its independent increments. Indeed, the exact distribution of these increments is most often intractable or without closed formula. For this reason, an important task is to provide estimation methods for the characteristic triplet.

Such estimation methods depend on the way observations are performed. In our model, two-dimensional Lévy process
\textbf{X}_{t}
is observed at high frequency, i.e., the time between two consecutive observations is
\frac{1}{n}
. The characteristic function of such a two-dimensional Lévy process is given by

\phi_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n}):=\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\langle\textbf{u}_{n},X_{t}\rangle)]=\exp\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\Psi(\textbf{u}_{n};\textbf{b},C,F)\bigg\},\quad\mbox{$\textbf{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

(1.1)

where

\displaystyle\Psi(\textbf{u}_{n})=\Psi(\textbf{u}_{n};\textbf{b},C,F)=

\displaystyle i\left\langle\textbf{u}_{n},\textbf{b}\right\rangle-\frac{\left\langle C\textbf{u}_{n},\textbf{u}_{n}\right\rangle}{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big(\exp(i\left\langle\textbf{u}_{n},\textbf{x}\right\rangle)

(1.2)

\displaystyle-1-i\left\langle\textbf{u}_{n},\textbf{x}\right\rangle\mathds{1}_{\left\{||\textbf{x}||_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\leq 1\right\}}\big)F(d\textbf{x}),

\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the drift,
C=\begin{pmatrix}C^{11}&C^{12}\\
C^{21}&C^{22}\end{pmatrix}
is the covariance matrix, and
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the jump measure. The triplet
(\textbf{b},C,F)
is called Lévy Khinchine characteristic. By virtue of simplicity, we consider the characteristic function on the diagonal and concentrate primarily on a two-dimensional regime, but extensions to the general multi-dimensional setting are straightforward to obtain as well.

Nonparametric inference from high-frequency data on the triplet of a Lévy process has been considered by
Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard 2002
,
Aït-Sahalia et al. 2010
,
Jacod and Reiß 2014
,
Bibinger et al. 2014
,
Mancini 2017
,
Belomestny and Trabs 2018
, and the references therein. In addition, minimax estimation of the covariance has been the subject of
Papagiannouli 2020
. In this work, the author develops a family of covariance estimators
\widehat{C}_{n}^{12}(U_{n})
to infer
C^{12}
. Although this contribution proves that
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})
achieves minimax rates for the estimation of
C^{12}
, this approach nevertheless presents a drawback insofar as
U_{n}
depends on a number of unknown parameters, such as the co-jump activity index
r\in(0,2]
. Co-jumps refer to the case when the underlying processes jump at the same time with the same direction.
r
refers to the Blumenthal-Getoor index for co-jumps. To overcome this shortcoming, a data-driven choice
\widehat{U}
is needed which ensures near-minimax rates for the estimation error.

A natural way to extend minimax theory to an adaptation theory is to construct estimators which simultaneously achieve near-minimax rates over many subsets of parameter space. Starting with the work of
Lepskiĭ 1991
, the design of minimax-adaptive estimators for linear functionals has been widely covered in the literature, e.g.
Efromovich and Low 1994
and
Birgé 2001
. Lepskiĭ designed a strategy for choosing a data-dependent parameter which uses only differences between estimators. His stopping rule considered only the monotonicity of the deterministic and stochastic errors. This method is widely applied in learning theory, where supervised learning algorithms depend on some tuning parameter, correct choice of which is crucial to ensure optimal performance.

Although it is no easy task, the implementation of Lepskiĭ-type stopping rule has been used in the literature as a recipe for adaptive procedures, e.g.
De Vito et al. 2010
and the references therein. What interests us particularly in the present context is the fact that we have to deal with the problem of adaptation to the unknown characteristic function appearing in the denominator of the stochastic error. A behavior which also occurs in the deconvolution problem, e.g.
Neumann and Hössjer 1997
,
Comte and Lacour 2011
,
Dattner et al. 2016
In our case, the unknown characteristic function in the denominator leads to the stochastic error behaving irregularly. In order to apply Lepskiĭ’s rule, it is crucial to overcome this irregular behavior.

The main contribution of the present work is to construct adaptive estimators and extend the minimax result obtained in
Papagiannouli 2020
. We provide a remedy for the irregular behavior of the stochastic error. The unknown characteristic function in the denominator leads to a U-shaped stochastic error. This behavior prevents us from applying Lepskiĭ’s rule. So it is crucial to find an index for the oracle start of our parameter. As a result, a monotonically increasing bound for the stochastic error is constructed. Finally, the convergence rate of the adaptive estimator is proven to be near-minimax.

.

2
Estimating the characteristic function

Here, we discuss technical tools which provide a uniform control of the deviations of the empirical characteristic function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The interesting point here is that the decay of the characteristic function is not assumed to be explicitly known but comes in by implication. To keep the exposition intuitive and free from technicalities, the proofs of lemmas have been postponed to
Section

8
. Throughout this section, we use the letter
C
to denote a constant that may change from line to line.

For the sake of keeping the calculations simple, we will restrict ourselves to estimating the characteristic function on the diagonal. For this purpose, let us introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

We define the subsets of the diagonal as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=\{\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\textbf{u}=(U,U),U\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tilde{\textbf{u}}=(U,-U),U\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Let a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left(\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be given. We assume that
\textbf{X}_{t}=(X^{(1)},X^{(2)})
is a bivariate Lévy process observed at
n
equidistant time points
\Delta,\ldots,n\Delta=T
, where
\Delta=\frac{i}{n}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
T=1
. We denote by

C_{n}(\textbf{u}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{i\langle\textbf{u},\Delta_{j}^{n}\textbf{X}\rangle}-\mathbb{E}[e^{i\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{X}_{1/n}\rangle}]\bigg)

(2.1)

the normalized empirical characteristic function process, where
\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}
. For an appropriate weight function
w:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1]
, we consider

\mathbb{E}\|C_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}(w)}:=\mathbb{E}\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\big\{|C_{n}(\textbf{u})|w(U)\big\}.

(2.2)

Recall that
C_{n}(\textbf{u})
converges weakly to a Gaussian process if and only if
\big\{\textbf{x }\to e^{i\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle},\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}\big\}
is a functional Donsker class for
\mathbb{P}
.

We start by defining a weight function that was introduced in
Neumann and Reiß 2009
and is the key for the uniform convergence of the empirical characteristic function.

Definition 2.2
.

For some
\delta>0
, let the weight function
w
be defined as

w(U):=\big(\log(e+|U|)\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\delta}.

The above definition is meaningful under the following, rather general assumption concerning the characteristic function.

Assumption 1
.

There is a function
g
which is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}^{-}
and non-increasing on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. There exist positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
, such that

\displaystyle\forall\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:Cg(U)\leq|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq C^{\prime}g(U)

\displaystyle\forall\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\mathcal{\tilde{A}}:Cg(U)\leq|\phi_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}})|\leq C^{\prime}g(U).

Some remarks are in order here: The following cases may be considered for the characteristic function.

(
a
)

Gaussian decay.
Under some boundedness condition for the covariance matrix and the activity of jumps, we can prove that

|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq e^{-\frac{CU^{2}}{2n}},\qquad\mbox{{$\forall\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}$}}.

(
b
)

Exponential decay.
Here,the characteristic function
\phi_{n}
decays at most exponentially, that is, for some
a>0
,
C>0
,

|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq Ce^{-a|U|/n},\qquad\mbox{{$\forall\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}$}}.

Examples of distributions with this property include normal inverse Gaussian and generalized tempered stable distributions.

(
c
)

Polynomial decay.
In this case the characteristic function satisfies for some
\beta\geq 0
,
C>0
,

|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq C(1+|U|)^{-\beta/n},\qquad\mbox{$\forall\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}$}.

Typical examples for this property are the compound Poisson distribution, gamma distribution, and variance gamma distribution. Contrary to the properties formulated above, our reasoning does not rely on any semiparametric assumption about the shape of the characteristic function. The only thing needed is the quasi-monotonicity of Assumption
1
which is fairly general.

We receive the following result, extending Theorem 4.1 of
Neumann and Reiß 2009
in two dimensions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}<\infty
for some
\gamma>0
, and let the weight function
w
be defined as in Definition
2.2
. Then

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\|C_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}(w)}<\infty.

Let us mention that the logarithmic decay of the weight function
w
is in accordance with the well-known results of
Csörgő and Totik 1983
, where

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}C_{n}\left((T_{n},T_{n})\right)=0

almost surely on intervals
[-T_{n},T_{n}]
whenever
\log T_{n}/n\to\infty
. We are now ready to prove a uniform bound for the deviation of the empirical characteristic function from the true one. First, we establish a Talagrand inequality using Lemma
A.2
from Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be some countable index set. Then for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, there are positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}=c_{2}(\epsilon)
, such that for every
\kappa>0
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\sup_{j\in\mathcal{I}}|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{j})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u}_{j})|

\displaystyle\geq(1+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{j\in\mathcal{I}}|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{j})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u}_{j})|\big]+\kappa\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\bigg(-n\bigg(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{c_{1}}\wedge\frac{\kappa}{c_{2}}\bigg)\bigg).

Now we introduce a logarithmic factor which is essential to proving uniformness on the diagonal. This comes at the cost of losing a logarithmic factor.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
t>0
be given, and
\mathcal{A}
defined as in Definition
2.1
. Then, for arbitrary
\beta>0
, there exists a constant
C
, such that we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\exists\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq t\bigg(\frac{\log{n}}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg]\leq Cn^{-\frac{(t-\beta)^{2}}{c_{1}}},

where the constant
C
depends on
\delta
appearing in Definition
2.2
and
c_{1}
is the constant in Talagrand’s inequality from Lemma
2.4
.

The statement of
Lemma

2.5
holds for
\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\tilde{A}
. A direct consequence of Lemma
2.5
is that we can consider a favorable set for the deviation of the empirical characteristic function from the true one.

Lemma 2.6
.

For some
p\geq 1/2
and
\kappa\geq 4(\sqrt{pc_{1}}+\beta)
, let us consider the event

\mathcal{E}:=\bigg\{\forall\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi(\textbf{u})|\leq\frac{\kappa}{4}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}.

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\bigg\{\forall\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}})-\phi(\tilde{\textbf{u}})|\leq\frac{\kappa}{4}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}.

Thus, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\mathcal{E}^{\complement}\bigg]\leq Cn^{-p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\bigg[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\complement}\bigg]\leq Cn^{-p}.

Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
hold for
\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\mathcal{\tilde{A}}
as well.

2.1
Truncated characteristic function

Here we present an extension of Lemma 2.1 in
Neumann and Hössjer 1997
, which renders the point-wise control of the characteristic function in the denominator uniform on sets
\mathcal{A}
. Now, we briefly discuss the idea of a truncated characteristic function presented in detail in
Neumann and Hössjer 1997
. It is clear that the characteristic function
\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})
can be estimated at each point
\textbf{u}=(U,U)
with the rate
n^{-1/2}
. Hence,
\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})
is a reasonable estimator of
\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})
, if
|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\gg n^{-1/2}
. The idea is to cut off the frequencies
u
, for which
|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq n^{-1/2}
.

First, we recall the key Lemma 2.1 from
Neumann and Hössjer 1997
:

Lemma 2.7
.

It holds that, for any
p\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(u)}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(u)}\bigg|^{2p}\bigg)\leq C\bigg(\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(u)|^{2p}}\wedge\frac{n^{-p}}{|\phi_{n}(u)|^{4p}}\bigg),

where
\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(u)}:=\frac{\mathds{1}\big(|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(u)|\geq n^{-1/2}\big)}{\widehat{\phi}_{n}(u)}
.

Neumann’s result is for
p=1
, but the extension to any
p
is straightforward. See also
Neumann and Reiß 2009
. The global threshold must be formulated in terms of
\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})
, so that the compact set is in fact random. The main difference with Neumann’s truncated estimator lies in the fact that we introduce an additional logarithmic factor in the thresholding scheme. This logarithmic factor allows us to derive exponential inequalities, as we saw in Lemma
2.5
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let the weight function
w
be given like in Definition
2.2
. For some positive constant
\kappa
, set

\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})},&\text{if $|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq\kappa_{n}n^{-1/2},$}\\
\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}n^{-1/2}},&\textit{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2.3)

where
\kappa_{n}:=\frac{\kappa}{2}(\log n)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}
.

We can now use Lemma
2.5
to assess the deviation of
\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}
from
\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that for some
p\geq 1/2
and
\beta>0
, we have
\kappa\geq 2(\sqrt{pc_{1}}+\beta)
, where
c_{1}
is the constant in Talagrand’s inequality. Then, for
n>0
and a positive constant
C
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\exists\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}>\bigg(\frac{9\kappa^{2}}{16}\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\leq Cn^{-p}.

We are now in position to formulate a uniform bound on the diagonal, which is an immediate result of Lemma
2.9
.

Lemma 2.10
.

If the assumptions of Lemma
2.9
hold, then there is a constant
C>0
depending on
\kappa
, such that for
n\geq 1

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\bigg)^{-1}\bigg]\leq C.

Also, Lemma
2.10
can be extended to powers different from
2
. We just need to substitute
2
with
2q
.

Note that an intermediate consequence of the preceding Lemma
2.9
is the following important corollary, which allows us to interchange between the empirical characteristic function and the true one with high probability.

Corollary 2.11
.

In the situation of the preceding statement, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\exists\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|>\frac{1}{2}\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|\bigg]\leq Cn^{-p}.

It is in fact this version of the statement which will play an important role below. On the complement of the preceding event, we have with high probability

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq-\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|+\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|\leq\frac{3}{2}\bigg|\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|.

The statement of
Corollary

2.11
and the above inequality hold for
\tilde{\textbf{u}}\in\tilde{A}
.

3
Adaptive parameter estimation

After recalling the statistical model, in this section we discuss the goal of this study. We aim to extend the minimax theory, from
Papagiannouli 2020
, to an adaptation theory for the covariance estimator.

3.1
Statistical model

We observe a two-dimensional Lévy process
(\textbf{X}_{t_{i}})_{t_{i}\geq 0}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
at equidistant time points
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n},
where
t_{i}=\frac{i}{n}
. We consider the characteristic function (
1.1
) on the diagonal, i.e.,
\textbf{u}_{n}=(U_{n},U_{n})
, with characteristic triplet
(\textbf{b},C,F)
with drift part
\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, covariance matrix
C=\begin{pmatrix}C^{11}&C^{12}\\
C^{21}&C^{22}\end{pmatrix}
, and jump measure
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

In what follows, we are in a nonparametric setting in which the process
\textbf{X}_{t_{i}}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{L}^{r}_{M}
. Let us now recall this class.

Definition 3.1
.

For
M>0
and
r\in[0,2)
, we define the class
\mathcal{L}^{r}_{M}
, the set of all Lévy processes, satisfying

\|C\|_{\infty}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(1\wedge|x_{1}x_{2}|^{r/2}\right)F(dx_{1},dx_{2})<M,

(3.1)

where
\|C\|_{\infty}=\max(|C^{11}+C^{12}|,|C^{21}+C^{22}|)
is the maximum of the row sums. In the second term
r
refers to the co-jump activity index of the jump components.

For details and examples concerning this class we refer to Section 3 in
Papagiannouli 2020
, where a minimax estimator for the covariance
C^{12}
is available. In addition,
Jacod and Reiß 2014
provide a minimax estimator for the marginals, i.e.
C^{11},C^{22}
.
Given the empirical characteristic function of the increments
\Delta\textbf{X}_{j}=\textbf{X}_{j/n}-\textbf{X}_{(j-1)/n}

\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{i\langle\textbf{u}_{n},\Delta_{j}^{n}\textbf{X}\rangle},\quad\mbox{$\textbf{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$}

a spectral estimator is used:

\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})=\frac{n}{2U_{n}^{2}}\left(\log|\hat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})|\mathds{1}(\hat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})\neq 0)-\log|\hat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})|\mathds{1}(\hat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})\neq 0)\right),

where
\textbf{u}_{n}=(U_{n},U_{n})
,
\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n}=(U_{n},-U_{n})
.

A bias-variance type decomposition for the estimation error is available by Lemma 6.1 in
Papagiannouli 2020
. We recall the Lemma without the proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

The error bound for the estimation satisfies

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})-C^{12}|\leq|H_{n}(U_{n})|+|D(U_{n})|,

(3.2)

where

D(U_{n})=\frac{n}{2U^{2}_{n}}\bigg(\log|\phi_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})|-\log|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})|\bigg)-C_{12},

(3.3)

and

H_{n}(U_{n})=-\frac{n}{2U^{2}_{n}}\bigg(\log\Bigl|\frac{\phi_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})}\Bigr|-\bigg(\log\Bigl|\frac{\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})}{\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})}\Bigr|\bigg)\mathds{1}\left(\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}}_{n})\neq 0,\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u}_{n})\neq 0\right)\bigg).

(3.4)

H_{n}(\cdot),D(\cdot)
are the corresponding stochastic and deterministic errors.

The spectral estimator
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})
achieves minimax rates for the optimal parameter
U_{n}
. For
r\in[0,2)
,
M
defined as in Definition
3.1
and for every
0<\eta\leq 1
, there is a constant
A_{\eta}>0
, and
N_{\eta}
such that for every
n\geq N_{\eta}

\mathbb{P}\Big[|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})-C^{12}|\leq w_{n}A_{\eta}\Big]\geq 1-\eta,

(3.5)

where

w_{n}=\begin{cases}n^{-1/2}&\text{if $r\leq 1$}\\
(n\log n)^{\frac{r-2}{2}}&\text{if $r>1$}\end{cases}

(3.6)

are the minimax rates for the optimal parameter

U_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{n}&\text{$r\leq 1$}\\
\frac{\sqrt{(r-1)n\log n}}{\sqrt{M}}&\text{$r>1.$}\end{cases}

(3.7)

The error bound incurred by the spectral estimator in
Lemma

3.2
is the sum of two terms, i.e., the deterministic and stochastic error, with respect to the tuning parameter
U_{n}
. The stochastic error displays behavior opposite to the deterministic error. The stochastic error tends to explode, however the deterministic error tends to zero as
U_{n}
grows. This observation and the fact that
U_{n}
depends on unknown parameters (
r,M
) impose the need for a-posteriori choices of the parameter
U_{n}
, which ideally are optimal in a well-defined sense. The goal is to derive a theoretical error bound for the adaptive estimator achieving almost the optimal rates.

3.2
Lepskiĭ’s stopping rule

In this section, we establish an adaptive choice for the parameter
U_{n}
, as this is achieved by Lepskiĭ’s principle. Following Lepskiĭ’s principle, a “stopping” rule is designed to achieve adaptation for a class of minimax estimators. We use the following conventions for the notations. We denote by
\mathcal{U}
the parameter space. We consider a suitable finite discretization
U_{0}<\ldots<U_{K}
for our parameter. We set
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}:=\widehat{C}^{12}(U_{j})
, i.e., we assign an estimator
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}
for each
U_{j}
. For each estimator
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}
we set
s_{n}(U_{j})
to be the upper bound of the stochastic error
\mathbb{E}|H_{n}(U_{j})|
for
j=0,1,\ldots,K
.

Starting from a family of rate asymptotically minimax estimators
\big\{\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U_{n})\big\}
, how can one get adaptation over the parameter space
\mathcal{U}
, to find an optimal tuning parameter
U_{j}
, which provides simultaneously minimax rates for the covariance over the sets
[U_{0},U_{K}]\subset\mathcal{U}
?

Remark 3.3
.

In this paper we refer to the value
U_{n}
as the best choice and to the corresponding rate as the best possible rate. The rate will be optimal in a minimax sense since the bound we started from is tight (

Let us first give a brief and simplified account of the classical Lepskiĭ method adjusted to our problem. We use the results in Section 5.4 of

. The key idea is to test real-valued estimators

, whose stochastic errors are increasing as the index is increasing and the bias is decreasing, for the hypotheses

. If we accept

, this means that
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j+1}
differs significantly from
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,1},\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,2},\dots,\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}
so we reject

. Further, we set

. We summarize the above discussion in the following definition.

Definition 3.4

We choose a suitable finite discretization

and take

we define the Lepskiĭ principle as

\hat{j}=\inf\Big\{j=0,1,\dots,K-1|\exists k\leq j:d\left(\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j+1},\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,k}\right)\leq Cs_{n}(U_{j+1})\Big\}\wedge K.

(3.8)

Analysis of the stochastic error

The main objective of the present section is to prove a high probability bound for the stochastic error. Observing the form of the stochastic error

4.1
The oracle start of the parameter
U

In order to apply a Lepskiĭ-type stopping rule, we need to ensure that the bound for the stochastic error is monotonically increasing. First we introduce some further notation.

4.1.1
Further notation

We write
U_{start}^{oracle}
as the staring point for the Lepskiĭ principle. By (
3.7
), we denote the optimal choice for the parameter, as
U_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{r-1}{M}n\log n}
.
We also denote as
C_{sum}=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}
, i.e. the sum of all elements of the covariance matrix.

We allow the bound for the stochastic error to depend either on the (possibly) unknown characteristic function or on the truncated empirical characteristic function. Since we can interchange w.h.p. between the true and empirical characteristic function, we use two different notations for the corresponding bounds of the stochastic error:

s_{n}(U):=CU^{-2}(n\log n)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|}

(4.6)

\tilde{s}_{n}(U):=CU^{-2}(n\log n)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\frac{1}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|}.

(4.7)

We use these bounds for the stochastic error because it easy to check that
\frac{1}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|}\vee\frac{1}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\tilde{\textbf{u}})|}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|}
.
In what follows, we occasionally use
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U)
instead of
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})
because we are estimating the characteristic function on the diagonal. Same rule applies for the function
h(\textbf{u}):=h(U,U)=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1-\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)F(dx)
.

and the stochastic error

We define the oracle start of

as follows:

U^{oracle}_{start}=\inf\bigg\{U>0:|\phi_{n}(U)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg\}.

Let us highlight the strategy of constructing a monotonically increasing bound for the stochastic error. Finding the oracle start of

, we show that

. Then, we prove that

Lemma 4.3
.

For big

, and

, the interval for

is

\bigg[\frac{\sqrt{2\log 2}}{\sqrt{C_{sum}+K}}\cdot\sqrt{n},\quad\frac{\sqrt{2\log 2}}{\sqrt{C_{sum}}}\cdot\sqrt{n}\bigg]

and for

, we get that

U_{start}^{oracle}<U_{n},

where
U_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{r-1}{M}n\log n}

Proof.

The absolute value of the characteristic function is given by

|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|=\exp\bigg\{-\frac{1}{2n}\bigg(\langle C\textbf{u},\textbf{u}\rangle+h(\textbf{u})\bigg)\bigg\},

(4.9)

where
\textbf{u}=(U,U)
. We define

h(\textbf{u})=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1-\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)F(d\textbf{x}),

where

is the Lévy measure in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for
|\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle|^{2}\leq\|\textbf{u}\|^{2}\|\textbf{x}\|^{2}
, a positive constant
K
and
v_{0}=(0,1)^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle h(\textbf{u})=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Big(1-\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)\Big)F(d\textbf{x})

\displaystyle=2\int_{v_{0}}\Big(1-\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)\Big)F(d\textbf{x})

(4.10)

\displaystyle+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus v_{0}}\Big(1-\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle\Big)F(d\textbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{v_{0}}|\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle|^{2}F(d\textbf{x})+4\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus v_{0}}dF(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq 4U^{2}\int_{v_{0}}\|x\|^{2}dF(\textbf{x})+4F(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus v_{0})

\displaystyle\leq KU^{2}

The last inequality derives from the fact that we always have
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(1\wedge\|\textbf{x}\|^{2})F(d\textbf{x})<\infty
. So we can obtain the following inequality

0\leq h(\textbf{u})\leq KU^{2}.

(4.11)

It is easy to check that
\langle C\textbf{u},\textbf{u}\rangle=C_{sum}U^{2}
. Inserting this fact and (

) into (

) we get the following inequality for the absolute value of the characteristic function

\exp\bigg\{-\frac{(C_{sum}+K)U^{2}}{2n}\bigg\}\leq|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq\exp\bigg\{-\frac{C_{sum}U^{2}}{2n}\bigg\}

Inserting the above inequality into (

, which ensures that

. This implies that
U_{start}^{oracle}<U_{n}
for big

. This concludes the proof.

Lemma 4.4
.

For

, the stochastic error satisfies

s_{n}(U_{start}^{oracle})\leq s_{n}(U_{n}).

Proof.

It suffices to show that

\frac{s_{n}(U_{start}^{oracle}}{s_{n}(U_{n})}\leq 1.

(4.12)

By the form of

in (

\frac{s_{n}(U_{start}^{oracle}}{s_{n}(U_{n})}=\bigg(\frac{U_{n}}{U_{start}^{oracle}}\bigg)^{2}\frac{w(U_{n})}{w(U_{start}^{oracle})}\bigg|\frac{\phi_{n}(U_{n})}{\phi_{n}(U_{start}^{oracle})}\bigg|.

(4.13)

By

4.3
, it yields
\bigg(\frac{U_{n}}{U_{start}^{oracle}}\bigg)^{2}\leq 1
. By

, we also know that

is a decreasing function, which means that
\frac{w(U_{n})}{w(U_{start}^{oracle})}>1

For the third term of (

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\phi_{n}(U_{n})}{\phi_{n}(U_{start}^{oracle})}\bigg|=\exp\bigg\{

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\big(C_{sum}\left((U_{start}^{oracle})^{2}-U_{n}^{2}\right)\big)

(4.14)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2n}\big(C_{sum}\left(h(U_{start}^{oracle})-h(U_{n})\right)\big)\bigg\}.

By (

Lemma 4.7

For

Inserting Definition

to the empirical characteristic function, the statement is proven.

5
Balancing principle when the stochastic error is known

In this section, we prove an upper bound for the best possible adaptive parameter using a balancing principle inspired by the work of
De Vito et al. 2010
for adaptive kernel methods. The optimal choice
U_{n}
crucially depends on the unknown parameters
r,M
. Here, we construct a completely data-driven estimation procedure adapted to
U\in\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}=[U_{start}^{oracle},U_{end}]
. Our main result for the adaptive estimation shows that the Lepskiĭ estimator achieves almost the optimal rates.

In the following we denote by
a(n):=\exp(-2n(c+1)^{2})
. By (
4.7
), w.h.p. at least
1-a(n)
the upper bound for the stochastic error will be of the form

\tilde{s}_{n}(U)=\frac{2C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U)}\frac{(w(U))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U)|}.

(5.1)

where
\theta(U)=U^{2}
,
\gamma(n)=(n\log n)^{1/2}
. Further, the term
d(U)
is the deterministic error bound, which does not depend on data and is of the form

d(U)=\frac{M2^{r/2}}{U^{2-r}},

(5.2)

where
r\in(1,2]
is the co-jump activity index and
M
is from the
3.1
. Consequently, The estimation error bound is given by the sum of two competing terms with probability at least
1-\exp(-2n(c+1)^{2})
i.e.,

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U)-C^{12}|\leq\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U).

(5.3)

The upper bound of (
5.3
) is the sum of a bias term which decreases in
U
and a stochastic error which increases in
U
, for
U\in\mathcal{U}
. According to the balancing principle, the best possible adaptive parameter choice is found by solving the bias-variance-type decomposition (
5.3
), which implies that we have to balance the deterministic and the stochastic error. We consider that
U_{bal}
makes the contribution of two terms equal, i.e.
d(U_{bal})=\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})
. We observe that the corresponding error estimate is, with probability at least
1-a(n)
,

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}-C^{12}|\leq 2d(U_{bal})=2\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal}),

(5.4)

where
0<a(n)<1
and
U_{bal}
is the best possible parameter. Using the equation in (
5.4
) and the knowing the form of the stochastic error and the deterministic error by (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), the theoretical best parameter according to balancing principle will satisfy

U_{bal}=\bigg(\frac{4C}{\kappa 2^{r/2}M}\bigg)^{1/r}n^{1/r}.

(5.5)

Let us now highlight the idea behind the balancing principle. It is clear, by the monotonicity of the stochastic and deterministic error, that

\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})+d(U_{bal})\leq 2\min_{U}\{\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U_{bal})\}.

If we choose
U_{*}\leq U_{bal}
:

\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})+d(U_{bal})\leq 2d(U_{bal})\leq 2d(U_{*})\leq 2\min_{U}\{\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U)\}.

On the other hand, if we choose
U_{*}\geq U_{bal}
:

\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})+d(U_{bal})\leq 2\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})\leq 2\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{*})\leq 2\min_{U}\{\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U)\}.

Since
C>0
, we can rewrite the decomposition error bound as

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}-C^{12}|\leq 2C\bigg(\frac{\gamma(n)}{\theta(U)}\frac{(w(U))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U)|}+d(U)\bigg),

(5.6)

where
d(\cdot)
is assumed to be a continuous, monotonically non-increasing function. As a result, the corresponding best parameter choice
U_{bal}
gives, with probability
1-a(n)
, the rate

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,bal}-C^{12}|\leq 2\frac{2C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{bal})}\frac{(w(U_{bal}))^{-1}}{{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{bal})|}}=2d\left(U_{bal}\right).

(5.7)

The aim is to choose
U_{bal}
from the set:

\mathcal{U}:=[U_{start}^{oracle},U_{end}]\quad\text{with}\quad U_{start}^{oracle}\sim\sqrt{n}.

(5.8)

To define a parameter strategy, we first consider a discretization for the possible values of
U_{j}
, that is, an ordered sequence
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the best value
U_{bal}
falls within the considered grid
\mathcal{U}
. The
balancing principle
estimate for
U_{bal}
is defined via

U_{\widehat{j}}=\max\Bigg\{U_{i}:\forall U_{j}\leq U_{i},U_{i}\in\mathcal{U},\big|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,j}-\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,i}\big|\leq\frac{8C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{j})}\frac{w(U_{j})}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{j})|}\Bigg\}.

(5.9)

The reasons why we expect this estimate to be sufficiently close to
U_{bal}
and why this estimate does not depend on
d
are better explained with the following argument.
Observe that if we choose two indices
\alpha,\beta
such that
U_{\alpha}\geq U_{\beta}\geq U_{bal}
, then with probability at least
1-a(n)
,

\displaystyle|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\alpha}-\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\beta}|

\displaystyle\leq|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\alpha}-C^{12}|+|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\beta}-C^{12}|

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq 2C\Bigg(\frac{\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{\alpha})}\frac{(w(U_{\alpha}))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{\alpha})|}+d(U_{\alpha})\Bigg)

\displaystyle+2C\Bigg(\frac{\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{\beta})}\frac{(w(U_{\beta}))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{\beta})|}+d(U_{\beta})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{\alpha})}\frac{(w(U))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{\alpha})|}.

The intuition is that when such a condition is violated, we are close to the value which contributes equal to the deterministic and stochastic errors, which is
U_{bal}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For a positive constant
C
, the estimation error bound, with high probability, is written like

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U)-C^{12}|\leq 2C\bigg(\frac{\gamma(n)}{\theta(U)}\frac{(w(U))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U)|}+d(U)\bigg),

(5.11)

where

•

d(U)
is a continuous, non-increasing function,

•

\theta(U)=U^{2}
,

•

w(U)
is a non-increasing function such
0<w(U)\leq 1
,

•

|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U)|
is a continuous, non-increasing function for
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Equation

4.2
gives us an upper bound for the stochastic error
H_{n}(U)
. Similar
d(U)
is an upper bound for the deterministic error
D(U)
. By
Lemma

3.2
, we can decompose the estimation error as a sum of stochastic error
H_{n}(U)
and a deterministic error
D(U)
. Consequently, it yields, with high probability,

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n}(U)-C^{12}|\leq|H_{n}(U)+D(U)|\leq s_{n}(U)+d(U).

Recall that the inequality (
2.4
) allows us to interchange with high probability between the (perhaps) unknown characteristic function and the empirical characteristic function. A direct consequence of the above observation is that we can interchange with high probability between the population bound
\tilde{s}_{n}(U)
and the theoretical bound
s_{n}(U)
for the stochastic error

\frac{1}{2}s_{n}(U)\leq\tilde{s}_{n}(U)\leq 3s_{n}(U).

(5.12)

This leads to
s_{n}+d(U)\leq 2\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U)
. Now taking into consideration the form of
\tilde{s}_{n}(U)
in (
5.1
), the proof is complete.
∎

5.1
requires the bound to be uniform with respect to
U_{j}
, since the parameter choice is data-dependent. The uniform condition is satisfied due to the Lemma
2.5
.
Driven by the inequality (
5.12
), the strategy for the balancing principle will give us with high probability

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})

\displaystyle\leq 2\min_{U}\{\tilde{s}_{n}(U)+d(U)\}

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq 2\min_{U}\{3s_{n}(U)+d(U)\}

\displaystyle\leq 6\min_{U}\{s_{n}(U)+d(U)\}

\displaystyle\leq 6s_{n}(U_{bal}).

The following theorem shows that the value
U_{\widehat{j}}
, given by the balancing principle (

), provides the same estimation error of
U_{bal}
up to a constant. Note that all the inequalities in the following proofs are to be interpreted as holding with high probability. We can now prove the convergence rate for the adaptive estimator
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\widehat{j}}

Theorem 5.2
.

Using
5.1
for a sequence of parameters
U_{j}
which satisfies
U_{j}\in[U_{start}^{oracle},U_{end}]
,
then there is a constant
C
such that the adaptive estimator satisfies with high probability

\mathbb{P}\big[|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\widehat{j}}-C^{12}|\leq 5\tilde{s}_{n}(U_{bal})\big]\geq 1-a(n).

Proof.

Recall that by (
5.10

\alpha,\beta
such that

we have

|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\alpha}-\widehat{C}_{n,\beta}^{12}|\leq\frac{8C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{\alpha})}\frac{(w(U_{\alpha}))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{\alpha})|}.

It is easy to see that

U_{bal}
and the triangle inequality, it yields

\displaystyle|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\widehat{j}}-C^{12}|

\displaystyle\leq|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\widehat{j}}-\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,bal}|+|\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,bal}-C^{12}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8C\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{bal})}\frac{(w(U_{bal}))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{bal})|}+2C\bigg(d(U_{bal})+\frac{\gamma(n)}{\theta(U_{bal})}\frac{(w(U_{bal}))^{-1}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(U_{bal})|}\bigg)

6
Discussion

Comments on the stochastic error.
The bound (
4.4
) ensures that the stochastic error of the estimator is upper-bounded by the truncated characteristic function up to a logarithmic factor and a multiplicative constant
C
. This means that the bound depends on the random quantity
|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|
but not on the unknown characteristic function
\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})
. With high probability, the inequality (
2.4
) allows us to interchange between the unknown characteristic function and the truncated empirical characteristic function, which is data-dependent. A direct consequence of the above observation is that we can interchange with high probability between the population bound
\tilde{s}_{n}(U)
and the theoretical bound
s_{n}(U)
for the stochastic error.

In Figure
1
, an irregular behavior of the bound for the stochastic error is observed for small values of
U
, because of the empirical characteristic function in the denominator. To overcome this obstacle, we find an oracle start for
U
, so as to ensure a monotonically increasing bound for the stochastic error.

Comments on the balancing principle.
The construction of a monotonically increasing bound for the stochastic error allows us to apply Lepskiĭ’s principle for the adaptive estimator
\widehat{C}^{12}_{n,\hat{j}}
. Theorem
5.2
shows us that the balancing principle can adaptively achieve the best possible rate, which is near-optimal in a minimax sense.

Proofs for

In this section, we provide proofs of the results which are presented in Section
2
.
The proof of Theorem
2.3
follows a chaining argument for the empirical processes. Thus,
we recall the following definitions from empirical process theory.

Definition 8.1
.

We consider measurable functions
f,g:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
. For two such functions
f,g
we introduce the “bracket” notation:

[f,g]:=\big\{h:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{such that}\quad f\leq h\leq g\big\}.

(8.1)

Definition 8.2
.

By the bracketing entropy number
N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G})
of a class
\mathbb{G}
we mean the minimal number
N
for which there exist functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
such that

\mathbb{G}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}[f_{i},g_{i}]\qquad\mbox{and}\quad\int|f_{i}-g_{i}|^{2}d\mathbb{P}\leq\epsilon^{2},\quad i=1,2,\dots,N.

N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G})
is the minimal number of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-balls of radius
\epsilon
which are needed to cover
\mathbb{G}
. The class
\mathbb{G}
is called bracketing compact if
N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G})<\infty
for any
\epsilon>0
. The entropy integral is defined by

J_{[\cdot]}(\delta,\mathbb{G}):=\int_{0}^{\delta}\big(\log(N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G}))\big)^{1/2}d\epsilon.

The convergence of the integral depends on the size of the bracketing numbers for
\epsilon\to 0
. Finally, a function
F\geq 0
is called an envelope function for
\mathbb{G}
, if

\forall f\in\mathbb{G}:|f|\leq F.

Proof of Theorem
2.3

We decompose
C_{n}
in its real and imaginary parts,

\displaystyle\textsf{Re}(C_{n}(\textbf{u})):=n^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(\cos(\langle\textbf{u},X_{t}\rangle)-\mathbb{E}\cos(\langle\textbf{u},X_{1}\rangle)\big)

\displaystyle\textsf{Im}(C_{n})(\textbf{u}):=n^{-1/2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(\cos(\langle\textbf{u},X_{t}\rangle)-\mathbb{E}\cos(\langle\textbf{u},X_{1}\rangle)\big)

We consider the class
\mathbb{G}
, which consists of complexed valued functions,

\mathbb{G}=\Big\{\textbf{x}\to w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\Big\}\cup\Big\{\textbf{x}\to w(U)\sin(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\Big\}.

An application of Corollary 19.35 in
Van der Vaart 2000
gives

\mathbb{E}\|C_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}(w)}\leq CJ_{[\cdot]}\big(\mathbb{E}[F^{2}(X_{1})],\mathbb{G},L^{2}(\mathbb{P})\big),

(8.2)

where
F=1
is an envelope function in
\mathbb{G}
. It remains to prove that the bracketing integral on the right-hand side of (
8.2
) is bounded. We need to cover
\mathbb{G}
with functions such that

\mathbb{G}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\Big\{[g_{i}^{-},g_{i}^{+}]:\int|g_{i}^{+}-g_{i}^{-}|^{2}d\mathbb{P}\leq\epsilon^{2}\Big\}

and find
N
, the minimum number to cover
\mathbb{G}
. Inspired by
Yukich 1985
, we characterize the convergence of
C_{n}(\textbf{u})
in terms of the tail behavior of
\mathbb{P}
. For every
\epsilon>0
, we set

M:=M(\epsilon):=\inf\{m>0:\mathbb{P}(|X_{1}|>m)\leq\epsilon^{2}\}.

(8.3)

Furthermore, for all
j
, define the bracket functions for
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle g_{j}^{\pm}(\textbf{x})=\big(w(U_{j})\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)\pm\epsilon\big)\mathds{1}_{[-M,M]}(\textbf{x})\pm\|w\|_{\infty}\mathds{1}_{[-M,M]^{\complement}}(\textbf{x}),

\displaystyle h_{j}^{\pm}(\textbf{x})=\big(w(U_{j})\sin(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)\pm\epsilon\big)\mathds{1}_{[-M,M]}(\textbf{x})\pm\|w\|_{\infty}\mathds{1}_{[-M,M]^{\complement}}(\textbf{x}),

where
\textbf{u}_{j}=(U_{j},U_{j})
and
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
. We obtain for the size of the brackets that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|g_{i}^{+}(X_{1})-g_{i}^{-}(X_{1})|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|2\epsilon\mathds{1}_{[M,M]^{2}}(\textbf{x})+2\|w\|_{\infty}\mathds{1}_{[-M,M]^{\complement}}(\textbf{x})|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\epsilon^{2}(1+\|w\|_{\infty}^{2}).

An analagous argument gives

\mathbb{E}\big[|h_{i}^{+}(X_{1})-h_{i}^{-}(X_{1})|^{2}\big]\leq 4\epsilon^{2}(1+\|w\|_{\infty}^{2}).

It remains to choose
U_{j}
in such a way that the brackets cover
\mathbb{G}
. We consider an arbitrary
U\in\mathbb{R}
and any grid point
U_{j}
. For a function
g_{U}(\cdot):=w(U)\cos(U(x_{1}+x_{2}))\in\mathbb{G}
to be contained in the bracket
[g^{-}_{j},g_{j}^{+}]
, we have to ensure

|w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)-w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\leq\epsilon,\quad\mbox{$\forall\textbf{x}\in[-M,M]^{2}$}.

(8.4)

With the estimate

\displaystyle|w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)-w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(w(U)+w(U_{j})\big)\wedge

\displaystyle\big(|w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)-w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\mathds{1}_{[M,M]^{2}}(\textbf{x})

\displaystyle+|w(U)\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)-w(U_{j})\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\mathds{1}_{[M,M]^{2}}(\textbf{x})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(w(U)+w(U_{j})\big)\wedge\big(2M\|w\|_{\infty}|U-U_{j}|+Lip(w)|U-U_{j}|\big),

where
Lip(w)
is the Lipschitz constant of the weight function
w
. In the last inequality, we used the estimate
|\cos(\langle\textbf{u},\textbf{x}\rangle)-\cos(\langle\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{x}\rangle)|\leq 2\big|\sin((x_{1}+x_{2})(U-U_{j})/2)\big|\leq 2M|U-U_{j}|
. (
8.4
) is seen to hold for any
\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, when

\min\big\{w(U)+w(U_{j}),|U-U_{j}|\big(Lip(w)+2M\|w\|_{\infty}\big)\big\}\leq\epsilon.

Consequently, we choose the grid points
U_{j}
such as

\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}|U_{j}-U_{j-1}|\leq\frac{2\epsilon}{Lip(w)+2M\|w\|_{\infty}}

(8.5)

and

U_{j}=\frac{j\epsilon}{Lip(w)+2M\|w\|_{\infty}}

(8.6)

for
|j|\leq J(\epsilon)
, where
J(\epsilon)
is the smallest integer such that
w(U_{1})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},\cdots,\\
w(U_{J(\epsilon)})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. We need to find the smallest integer
J(\epsilon)
in order to cover
\mathbb{G}
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-balls of radius
\epsilon
. This yields

J(\epsilon)\leq\frac{2U(\epsilon)(Lip(w)+2M\|w\|_{\infty})}{\epsilon},

with
U(\epsilon)\leq U_{J(\epsilon)}
, where

U(\epsilon):=\inf\Big\{U>0:w(U)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big\}=O(\exp(\epsilon^{-(1+1/2)^{-1}})).

Therefore, the minimal number of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-balls of radius
\epsilon
satisfies
N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G})\leq 2(2J(\epsilon)+1)
. The generalized Markov inequality yields that

M(\epsilon)\leq\bigg(\frac{\mathbb{E}|X_{1}|^{2+\gamma}}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg)^{1/\gamma}=O(\epsilon^{-2/\gamma}).

The entropy number bracketing satisfies

\displaystyle\log(N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G}))

\displaystyle\leq\log(U(\epsilon))+\log\bigg(\frac{Lip(w)+2M\|w\|_{\infty}}{\epsilon}\bigg)

(8.7)

\displaystyle=O(\epsilon^{-(1+1/2)^{-1}}+\log(\epsilon^{-1-2/\gamma}))=O(\epsilon^{-(1+1/2)^{-1}}).

Thus, we have shown that

J_{[\cdot]}(\mathbb{E}[F^{2}(X_{1})],\mathbb{G},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}))=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log(N_{[\cdot]}(\epsilon,\mathbb{G}))}d\epsilon<\infty.

This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma

We consider the following partition in the diagonal of the characteristic function
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}
with

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\bigg\{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq\frac{3\kappa}{4}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\},

(8.12)

\mathcal{A}_{2}=\bigg\{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}:|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|>\frac{3\kappa}{4}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\},

(8.13)

We analyze the deviation of the truncated estimator from the true one on each aforementioned sets and the event
\mathcal{E}
. The event
\mathcal{E}^{\complement}
is negligible by Lemma
2.6
, so it is enough to take into consideration only the event
\mathcal{E}
. First, we consider
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. For arbitrary
\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, we get

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}

(8.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\mathds{1}\bigg(\bigg\{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|>\frac{\kappa}{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\mathds{1}\bigg(\bigg\{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle:=A_{1}+A_{2}.

We will bound the quantities
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
separately. We start with the first term of the sum (
8.14
), which is
A_{1}
. On the set
\bigg\{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|>\frac{\kappa}{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}
, using the Definition
2.8
we get that
|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|=|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|
. On the one hand, by Lemma
2.6
, we have

\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\leq\frac{\frac{k^{2}}{4^{2}}\frac{\log n}{n}(w(U))^{-2}}{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}.

(8.15)

We observe that using (
8.12
), we get

\frac{1}{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|}\leq\frac{2}{\kappa\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|}.

(8.16)

On the other hand, inserting (
8.16
) into
A_{1}
, it yields that

\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}=\frac{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}\leq\frac{9k^{2}}{16}\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}.

(8.17)

Combining (
8.15
) with (
8.17
), we get

A_{1}\leq\frac{9k^{2}}{16}\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}.

(8.18)

Next, we study the second term of the sum (
8.14
), which is
A_{2}
. On the set
\bigg\{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\bigg\}
, along with the Definition
2.8
, we have that
|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|=\frac{\kappa}{2}(\frac{\log n}{n})^{1/2}w(U)^{-1}
. It yields that

\displaystyle A_{2}\leq\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\big|\frac{3\kappa}{4}\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}-\frac{\kappa}{2}\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\big|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}

(8.19)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}.

Then, (
8.18
) and (
8.19
) imply that

A_{1}+A_{2}\leq\frac{9k^{2}}{16}\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}.

(8.20)

The last ingredient is to consider the set
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. Using (
8.13
), it holds that

|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|\geq\big||\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|-|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\big|\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}.

(8.21)

By Definition
2.8
, the above inequality implies that
|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|=|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|
. On the one hand we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}

(8.22)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}.

On the other hand, Lemma
2.6
and (
8.13
) give

\displaystyle|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|

\displaystyle\geq\big||\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|-|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|\big|

(8.23)

\displaystyle\geq\big||\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|-\frac{\kappa}{4}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{1/2}(w(U))^{-1}\big|

\displaystyle\geq 2|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|.

Consequently, by (
8.23
)

\displaystyle\frac{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})-\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}{|\widehat{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4^{2}}\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)(w(U))^{-2}}{4|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}

(8.24)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4^{3}}\frac{\big(\frac{\log n}{n}\big)(w(U))^{-2}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}},

which concludes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
2.10
.

To derive the desired upper bound we distinguish between two events,
E
and
E^{\complement}
, which are defined as in Lemma
2.6
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}\mathds{1}(E)\bigg]

(8.25)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}\mathds{1}(E^{\complement})\bigg].

First, we establish an upper bound for the first part of the right hand side sum.
Lemma
2.9
on the event
E
yields that,

\displaystyle E\bigg[\sup_{\textbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\bigg|\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{n}(\textbf{u})}-\frac{1}{\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})}\bigg|^{2}}{\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(U)|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}}\mathds{1}(E)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\frac{9\kappa^{2}}{16}\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}}{\frac{\log n(w(U))^{-2}n^{-1}}{|\phi_{n}(U)|^{4}}\wedge\frac{1}{|\phi_{n}(\textbf{u})|^{2}}}\mathds{1}(E)\bigg]\leq\frac{9\kappa^{2}}{16}.

On the contrary, the event
E^{\complement}
is negligible, using Lemma
2.6
and this concludes the proof.

Appendix A
Concentration inequalities

We restate, for the reader’s convenience, the concentration inequalities which will be essential for our reasoning. The following lemmas are classical. Proofs can be found for example in
Reiß 2012
or
Dudley 2014
. First, we present the Bernstein inequality.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be complex valued i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
and
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\big)
. Suppose that
\|X_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq R
for some
R<\infty
. Then, the following holds true for arbitrary
k>0

\mathbb{P}\big(|S_{n}|\geq k\big)\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{k^{2}}{4(\textsf{Var}(S_{n})+kR)}\bigg).

Finally, we need the Talagrand inequality, which strengthens the classical Bernstein to countable sets of random variables.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be some countable index set. For each
u\in\mathcal{U}
, let
X_{1}^{(u)},\dots,X_{n}^{(u)}
be i.i.d. complex valued random variables, defined on the same probability space, with
\|X_{1}^{(u)}\|_{\infty}\leq R
for some
R<\infty
. Let
v^{2}:=\textsf{Var}X_{1}^{(u)}
. Then, for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, there are positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}=c_{2}(\epsilon)
depending only on
\epsilon
such than for any
k>0
:

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|S_{n}^{(u)}|\geq(1+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}|S_{n}^{(u)}|\big]+k\bigg]\leq 2\exp\bigg(-n\bigg(\frac{k^{2}}{c_{1}v^{2}}\wedge\frac{k}{c_{2}R}\bigg)\bigg).

(A.1)

Lemma
A.2
is taken from
Massart 2007
, see formula (5.50) on page 170. From the arguments given therein, we derive that for
\eta\in(0,1)
, we can take
c_{1}=4/(1-\eta^{2})
and
c_{2}=4\sqrt{2}(1/3+\epsilon^{-1})/\eta
.
