Bryce Joseph Orloski and Naser Talebizadeh Sardari

Abstract
. Given a compact subset Σ ⊂ ℝ \Sigma\subset\mathbb{R} (or ℂ \mathbb{C} ) with logarithmic capacity greater than zero, we construct an explicit family of probability measures supported on Σ \Sigma such that their closure is all the possible weak limit measures of complete sets of conjugate algebraic integers lying inside Σ \Sigma . We give an asymptotic formula for the number of algebraic integers with given degree and prescribed distribution. We exploit the algorithmic nature of our approach to give a family of upper bounds that converges to the smallest limiting trace-to-degree ratio of totally positive algebraic integers and improve the best previously known upper bound on the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem to 1.8216.



To Peter Sarnak on the occasion of his seventieth birthday.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Fekete’s theorem

A celebrated theorem of Fekete states that if a fixed compact set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
contains an infinite collection of complete sets of conjugate algebraic integers then the logarithmic capacity of
\Sigma
is greater than or equal to zero.
Fekete and Szegö
[
FS55
]
proved a partial converse to this theorem.
Smyth showed that for any given probability distribution
\mu
which is the weak limit of a sequence of distinct uniform probability measures on the complex roots of irreducible monic integral polynomials all of whose roots lie in
\Sigma
, then

(1)

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu(x)\geq 0

for every non-zero
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Serre
[
Ser19
, Lemma 1.3.7]
showed more generally that

(2)

\int\log|Q(x,y)|d\mu(x)d\nu(y)\geq 0

for every two variable integral polynomial
Q(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
and every pair of limiting measures
\mu,\nu
. Serre
[
Ser19
]
also commented that the
n
-dimensional version of (
2
) holds and in
[
Ser19
, Corollary 1.3.10]
considered the special case when
Q(x,y)=x-y
in (
2
) which implies

I(\mu,\nu):=\int\log|x-y|d\mu(x)d\nu(y)\geq 0.

When
\mu=\nu
, let
I(\mu):=\int\log|x-y|d\mu(x)d\mu(y)
be the logarithmic energy of
\mu
.

In a recent breakthrough, Smith
[
Smi21
]
proved that the inequalities in (
1
) are sufficient for
\mu
to be a limiting measure when
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
.
The authors
[
OT23
]
recently established this result for any symmetric compact subset
\Sigma
of the complex plane with a new proof of Smith’s result.
In doing so, we found new applications of the following inequality in
[
OT23
]
and
[
OT24
]

(3)

\int\log(|Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})|)d\mu(x_{1})\dots d\mu(x_{n})\geq 0

for every limiting measure
\mu
and every non-zero
Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{C}
with the assumption that it is invariant under complex conjugation) is a compact subset with logarithmic capacity greater than zero and let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
that satisfies (
3
). In this paper, we prove two basic results about the set of algebraic integers inside
\Sigma
that are equidistributed according to
\mu
.

First, we prove in Theorem
1.12
that the number of algebraic integers of a given degree
n
and approximate distribution
\mu
is about

e^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}I(\mu)+o(n^{2-\delta})}.

Theorem
1.12
gives a purely arithmetic definition of the logarithmic energy of
\mu
in terms of the growth of the number of algebraic integers modelling
\mu
as a function of their degree. Our proof of Theorem
1.12
is based on
the geometry of numbers and a discrete version of John’s theorem
[
TV08
, Theorem 1.6]
for convex bodies inside Euclidean spaces. We also use (
3
) in order to bound Minkowski’s successive minima.

Second, we construct an explicit family of probability measures supported on small neighborhoods of
\Sigma
in Theorem
1.4
that satisfy (
3
) and are dense with respect to the weak topology among all possible probability measures that satisfy (
3
). Our construction in Theorem
1.4
uses the counting formula in Theorem
1.12
. As an application, we give a family of upper bounds in Corollary
that converges to the smallest limiting trace-to-degree ratio of totally positive algebraic integers and improve the previously best-known upper bound on the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem to 1.8216 in Corollary
1.10
.
Theorem
1.4
addresses the following question of Sarnak:

“What are the measures with compact support
\Sigma
that satisfy the infinite number of inequalities in (
1
) or equivalently in (
3
)?”

Before this work, the only known measures satisfying (
3
) were the equilibrium measure of
\Sigma
and an explicit construction by Serre
[
AP08
]
when
\Sigma
is a specific closed interval discussed in further detail in the next subsection.

1.2.
The Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem.

In this subsection, we discuss the application of our main theorems to the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem.
For an algebraic integer
\alpha
with
\deg(\alpha)=n
and Galois conjugates
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
, define

\text{Tr}(\alpha):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}.

We call
\alpha
totally positive if all of its conjugates
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are real and non-negative. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of totally positive algebraic integers.
Note that for a given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there are only finitely many
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
of a given degree such that
\text{Tr}(\alpha)=m
.
The Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem asks for the value of the lowest limiting trace-to-degree ratio. More specifically,
define

\overline{\textrm{Tr}}(\alpha)=\frac{\text{Tr}(\alpha)}{\deg(\alpha)}.

The Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem seeks the value of

\lambda^{SSS}=\liminf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{\textrm{Tr}}(\alpha).

Much work has been done computing lower bounds of
\lambda
initiated by Schur
[
Sch18
]
, Siegel
[
Sie45
]
, and Smyth
[
Smy84
]
who gave a lower bound on the trace problem by minimizing
\int xd\mu(x)
subject to (
1
). In
[
OT24
]
, the authors used (
3
) to improve the best known lower bound on the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem to
1.79824\leq\lambda^{SSS}.
For providing upper bounds, one needs to construct probability measures with small trace that satisfy (
1
) which is the main goal of this paper.

1.2.1.
History of Upper Bounds

The first known upper bound on
\lambda^{SSS}
was given by Schur
[
Sch18
]
who proved that
\lambda^{SSS}\leq 2
by giving an infinite family of totally positive algebraic integers of average conjugate value exactly 2.
Later, Siegel
[
Sie45
]
gave an example of an infinite family of totally positive algebraic integers
\{p_{n}\}
with
p_{n}
being degree
n
and the trace of
p_{n}
being
2n-1
. For a long time, 2 was the only known upper bound for
\lambda^{SSS}
. For this reason, the problem was usually phrased as finding the smallest
\epsilon>0
so that there are only finitely many totally positive algebraic integers
\alpha
for which
\overline{\textrm{Tr}}(\alpha)<2-\epsilon
. Work by Serre
[
AP08
]
and Smith
[
Smi21
]
showed that
\lambda^{SSS}<1.898304
. We discuss this upper bound in the following details.

Let

\mu
be a measure and define

(4)

U_{\mu}(x):=\int\log|x-y|d\mu(y)

to be the (logarithmic) potential of
\mu
. Note that

I(\mu)=\int U_{\mu}(x)d\mu(x),

and for two measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we define

I(\mu,\nu):=\int U_{\mu}(x)d\nu(x)=\int U_{\nu}(x)d\mu(x).

In his letter to Smyth in February 1998, Serre showed that if
Q
is a polynomial with real coefficients with leading and constant coefficient of modulus at least 1, and if
y,z>0
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
are constants such that

(5)

x-z\log(x)-y\log|Q(x)|\geq c

for all
x>0
, then
c\leq 1.898303
. In his second letter to Smyth in March 1998, Serre constructed a measure
\nu_{S}
supported on an interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(6)

x\geq c+\lambda_{x}\log|x|+\lambda_{\nu_{S}}U_{\nu_{S}}(x)

U_{\nu_{S}}(0)=\int\log(x)d\nu_{S}(x)\geq 0

for all
x>0
and some
c>1.898302
,
\lambda_{x},\lambda_{\nu_{S}}\geq 0.
By taking roots of
Q
according to the distribution
\nu_{S}
, it follows that for every
c<1.898302
, there exists some
Q
and
y,z>0
such that (
5
)
holds. Let
c_{S}:=\sup c
, where
c
satisfies (
6
) for some choice of
\nu_{S}
,
\lambda_{x},\lambda_{\nu_{S}}\geq 0
. The above implies that
c_{S}\in(1.898302,1.898303)
.

Using Serre’s construction, Smith showed that there exists another measure
\mu_{S}
supported on the same interval
[a,b]
satisfying the conditions

(7)

U_{\mu_{S}}(x)\geq\gamma\log|x|

and

(8)

U_{\mu_{S}}(0)=\int\log(x)d\mu(x)\geq 0

with

\int xd\mu_{S}(x)\leq 1.898304

for a fixed
\gamma\geq 0
. Note that
\mu_{S}
satisfies Smyth’s necessary conditions (
1
) for
Q(x)=x
by definition and for every
Q(x)
coprime to
x
, we have

(9)

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu_{S}(x)=\sum_{Q(\alpha)=0}U_{\mu_{S}}(\alpha)\geq\gamma\sum_{Q(\alpha)=0}\log|\alpha|\geq 0.

By Smith’s theorem, we have

\lambda^{SSS}\leq 1.898304.

This was the best-known bound on
\lambda_{SSS}
prior to this work. Let
C_{s}=\inf\int xd\mu(x)
where
\mu
satisfies (
7
) and (
8
) for some
\gamma>0
. Next, we show that
c_{c}\leq C_{s}
which is known as weak duality in the context of linear programming.
In fact, suppose that a given probability measure
\mu
supported on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfies the inequalities (
7
) and (
8
). We take the average of (
6
) with respect to
\mu
and obtain

(10)

\int xd\mu(x)\geq c+\lambda_{x}\int\log|x|d\mu(x)+\lambda_{\nu}\int U_{\nu}(x)d\mu(x).

By inequality (
8
) and
\lambda_{x}\geq 0
, we have

\lambda_{x}\int\log|x|d\mu(x)\geq 0.

Moreover, by (
7
),

\int U_{\nu}(x)d\mu(x)=\int U_{\mu}(x)d\nu(x)\geq\int\gamma\log(x)d\nu(x)\geq 0.

By (
10
) and the above inequalities, it follows that

C_{S}\geq c_{S}.

1.3.
Main results

Before this work, it was not known whether
c_{s}=C_{S}
which is known as strong duality. As a consequence of Theorem
1.5
, we show that
c_{s}=C_{S}
and we express this optimal value in the following corollary. This answers a question that was raised by Serre in his letter
[
AP08
]
about finding the optimal value for the optimization problem in (
5
).

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
c_{S}
and
C_{S}
be as above and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
be the unique solution to

\begin{cases}y\log y=(y-x)\log(y-x)+x\\
\log(x)\log(y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x).\end{cases}

Then

c_{S}=C_{S}=\hat{y}-\hat{y}\log\hat{y}+\hat{x}-\hat{x}\log\hat{x}.

In Theorem
1.4
, we show that every limiting measure
\mu
of uniform distributions on roots of monic integral polynomials is well-approximated by probability measures
\eta
for which there exists an integral polynomial
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that

U_{\eta}(x)\geq\gamma\log|Q(x)|

for some constant
\gamma\geq 0
and

\int\log|Q(x)|d\eta(x)\geq 0.

Therefore, Theorem
1.4
reduces the upper bound problem to measures
\eta
satisfying the above conditions.

Definition 1.2
.

A Borel probability measure is
arithmetic
if

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu\geq 0

for all
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\setminus\{0\}
.

Definition 1.3
.

The
root distribution
of a polynomial
P
is defined by
\mu_{P}:=\frac{1}{\deg(P)}\sum_{P(\alpha)=0}\delta_{\alpha}
where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac-
\delta
mass at
x
and where the sum counts roots with multiplicity. For a monic polynomial
P
, we write

\overline{\textrm{Tr}}(P):=\int xd\mu_{P}(x)

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose
\mu
is a compactly supported arithmetic probability measure. For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there is some

for which there exists a monic irreducible integral polynomial
p_{n}
of degree

which satisfy

\int\log|p_{n}(x)|d\mu_{n}(x)\geq 0,

for every

and some constants

where
\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}=1
, and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}=\mu

in the weak-* topology.
Furthermore, we can take
\mu_{n}
so that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}

\text{supp}(\mu_{n})\supseteq\text{supp}(\mu_{n+1})
and
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{supp}(\mu_{n})=\Sigma
. If the support of

1.3.1.
Primal problem

Fix a finite subset of integer polynomials
A=\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}\}
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{+}
be the space of all non-negative Borel measures with compact support and finite mass on the non-negative real numbers.
Define

\mathcal{C}(A):=\{(\mu,b_{Q}):\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+},b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for every }Q\in A\}.

We call the following linear constraints for
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)
the
primal constraints

(11)

\begin{split}U_{\mu}(x)\geq\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\log|Q(x)|,\\
\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu(x)\geq 0,\\
\int d\mu(x)\geq 1,\\
b_{Q}\geq 0\text{ for every }Q\in A,\end{split}

where the first inequality holds for every
x\in\mathbb{C}
and the second holds for every
Q\in A
. We say
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)
is a feasible point if
(\mu,b_{Q})
satisfies all inequalities in (
11
). We denote the subset of feasible points by
\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
. Note that
(0,\dots,0)\in\mathcal{C}(A)
, but
(0,\dots,0)
is not a feasible point.

The primal problem is to minimize the trace
\int xd\mu(x)
for
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
. Let

(12)

\Lambda_{A}:=\inf_{(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}}\int xd\mu(x).

Suppose that
B\subset A
and
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(B)^{+}
. It is easy to check that
(\mu,b^{\prime}_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
, where
b^{\prime}_{Q}:=b_{Q}
if
Q\in B
and
b^{\prime}_{Q}:=0
if
Q\in A\setminus B.
This implies that

\Lambda_{A}\leq\Lambda_{B}

We say a set
A
is a minimal primal set if

\Lambda_{A}<\Lambda_{B}

for every subset
B\subset A
, where
B\neq A
.
We say there is a solution
\mu
to the primal problem associated to
A
if there exists some coefficients
b_{Q}\geq 0
such that
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
and

\int xd\mu(x)=\Lambda_{A}.

Suppose that
(\mu,b_{Q})
is a solution to primal problem and that
b_{Q}=0
for some
Q\in A
. It follows that
\Lambda_{A\setminus\{Q\}}=\Lambda_{A}
which implies
A
is not a minimal primal set.

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix a finite subset of integral polynomials
A\subset\mathbb{Z}[x]
with all real roots. There is a feasible dual measure
\nu_{A}
with
\lambda_{0}(\nu_{A})=\lambda_{A}
, and we have

\lambda_{A}=\Lambda_{A}.

Let
\mu_{A}
be any solution in Theorem
with support
\Sigma_{A}
and expectation
\int xd\mu_{A}(x)=\Lambda_{A}
. Moreover,
\nu_{A}
has the same support as
\mu_{A}
with

\begin{array}[]{cc}x\geq\Lambda_{A}+\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}\log|Q(x)|+U_{\nu_{A}}(x)\text{ and}\\
I(\mu_{A},\nu_{A})=0\end{array}

where in the first line the inequality holds for non-negative
x
and equality holds for every
x\in\Sigma_{A}
with some scalars
\lambda_{Q}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
. Moreover if
\Sigma_{A}:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{l}[a_{2i},a_{2i+1}]
and

\nu_{A}
has the following density function

\frac{|p(x)|\sqrt{|H(x)|}}{\pi\prod_{\lambda_{Q}\neq 0}|Q(x)|},

where
H(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{2l+1}(x-a_{i})
and

is a monic polynomial with degree
\sum_{Q\in A}\deg(Q)-l-1

, and

\Lambda_{A}\geq\lambda^{SSS}\geq\Lambda_{A}+\frac{I(\nu_{A})}{2\nu_{A}(\mathbb{R})}.

1.5.
Counting algebraic integers of given degree and distribution

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is compact and symmetric about the real axis with logarithmic capacity greater than or equal to zero. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}
be the space of probability measures supported on
\Sigma
equipped with the weak-* topology. Let

\Sigma(\rho):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-\sigma|<\rho\text{ for some }\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

If
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
, let

\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|x-\sigma|<\rho\text{ for some }\sigma\in\Sigma\}.

Note that
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Next, we introduce some new notation to state our main theorem under some assumptions. Let

[a,b]\times[c,d]:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textup{Re}(z)\in[a,b]\text{ and }\textup{Im}(z)\in[c,d]\right\}\subset\mathbb{C}.

We call
[a,b]\times[c,d]
a rectangle, and we call it a real interval if
c=d=0
. We denote the boundary of a rectangle or an interval by
\partial R
and
\partial I
.
A measure
\mu
is a Hölder probability measure if there exists
\delta>0
and
A>0
such that

\mu([a,b]\times[c,d])\leq A\max(|b-a|,|d-c|)^{\delta}

for every
a<b,c<d.

Here
\delta
is called a Hölder exponent of
\mu
.

1.5.1.
Generic symmetric sampling

Suppose that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{C}
is a finite union of some rectangles and real intervals and
\Sigma
is symmetric about the real axis. We write

\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{1}}(R_{i}\cup\bar{R_{i}})\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{2}}I_{j}

where
R_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}
are rectangles,
\bar{R_{i}}
is the reflection of
R_{i}
by the
x
-axis and
I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}
are intervals. We define

\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{1}}(R_{i}(\rho)\cup\bar{R_{i}}(\rho))\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{2}}{I_{j}}_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)

for any
\rho>0
. Note that if
\bigcup_{j}{I_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, then
\Sigma_{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)\subsetneq\Sigma(\rho)
. We define the boundary of
\Sigma
to be

is arithmetic.

which is

as needed.
Indeed,

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{n}([r_{i}-c2^{1-i},r_{i}-c2^{-i}])+\tilde{\mu}_{n}([r_{i}+c2^{-i},r_{i}+c2^{1-i}])\right)\left|\log|c2^{-i}|\right|\ll_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-i\delta_{n}}\left|\log(c2^{-i})\right|\ll_{n}1

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{m_{n},n}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\mu.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int fd\mu_{m_{n},n}-\int fd\tilde{\mu}_{n}=0

Fix a continuous function
f
. Since
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\text{supp}(\tilde{\mu}_{n})\cup\text{supp}(\mu_{m_{n},n}))
is bounded, we have that
f
is uniformly continuous on this set. Fix
\epsilon>0
. Suppose that for any

and

implies

. Also let the supremum of

on these supports be denoted by

such that for all

, we have that
\text{diam}(B_{ij})<\epsilon^{\prime}
for all

. Then

\displaystyle\left|\int fd\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\int fd\mu_{m_{n},n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{ij}\left|\int_{B_{ij}}f(x)d\tilde{\mu}_{n}(x)-\sum_{z\in B_{ij}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{m_{n},n}}m_{n}\int_{\frac{-1}{2m_{n}^{2}}}^{\frac{1}{2m_{n}^{2}}}f(z+x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{ij}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{n}(B_{ij})\frac{\epsilon}{2}+m_{n}^{-\frac{2}{3}-\delta_{n}}\left(M+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+m_{n}^{-\min(\frac{1}{2},\delta)}\left(M+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle<\epsilon

This completes the proof of the theorem.

Lemma 5.3

\begin{split}\int_{x\in\Sigma_{A}}f(x)d\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}(x)&=\int_{y\in\Sigma_{A}}\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\log|Q|(y)g(y)dy\\
&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i}\sum_{Q}b_{Q}\log|Q(\xi_{i,N})|g(\xi_{i,N})\text{len}(O_{i})\\
&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i}U_{\mu_{A}}(\xi_{i,N})g(\xi_{i,N})\text{len}(O_{i})\\
&=\int_{x\in\Sigma_{A}}\int_{y\in\Sigma_{A}}\log|x-y|g(y)dyd\mu_{A}(x)\\
&=\int f(x)d\mu_{A}(x).\end{split}

By taking
f(x)=1
and
g(y)dy=\frac{1}{C_{\Sigma_{A}}}d\mu_{eq,\Sigma_{A}}(y)
, we have

\int d\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}(x)=\int d\mu_{A}(x)=1.

Similarly, by taking
f(x)=\frac{\log|Q(x)|}{\deg(Q)}
and
g(y)dy=d\mu_{Q,\Sigma_{A}}+\frac{C_{Q,\Sigma_{A}}}{C_{\Sigma_{A}}}
, we have

\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}(x)=\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu_{A}(x)\geq 0.

By
[
OT24
, Lemma 3.3]
, there exists
g(y)dy
such that

x=\int_{\Sigma_{A}}\log|x-y|g(y)dy.

This implies that

\int xd\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}(x)=\int xd\mu_{A}(x)=\Lambda_{A}.

Finally, let
\eta:=\sum_{Q}b_{Q}\deg(Q)\mu_{Q}
. Note that
|\eta|=\sum b_{Q}\deg(Q)\leq 1
.
By Theorem
5.1
, since
\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}
is a probability measure and
U_{\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}}(x)=\sum b_{Q}\log|Q|(x)=U_{\eta}(x)
for every
x\in\Sigma_{A}
,
we have

U_{\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}}}(x)\geq\sum b_{Q}\log|Q|(x)=U_{\eta}(x)

for every
x\in\mathbb{C}
.
This implies that
(\mu_{A,\Sigma_{A}},b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
and concludes our proposition.
∎

5.1.
Dual problem

In this subsection, we define the associated dual optimization problem.
Let

\mathcal{D}(A):=\{(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0}):\nu\in\mathcal{M}^{+},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{Q}\geq 0\text{ for every }Q\in A,\text{ and }\lambda_{0}\geq 0\}.

For
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)
and
(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\in\mathcal{D}(A)
, we define the Lagrangian function as

(25)

L\left((\mu,b_{Q}),(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\right):=\int xd\mu(x)-\int\left(U_{\mu}(x)-\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\log|Q(x)|\right)d\nu(x)\\
-\sum_{Q\in A}\left(\lambda_{Q}\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu(x)+b_{Q}\beta_{Q}\right)-\lambda_{0}\left(\int d\mu-1\right).

We note that if
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)^{+}
is a feasible point then

(26)

L\left((\mu,b_{Q}),(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\right)\leq\int xd\mu(x)

for every
(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\in\mathcal{D}(A).
We may rewrite the Lagrangian function as

(27)

L\left((\mu,b_{Q}),(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\right)=\lambda_{0}+\int\left(x-U_{\nu}(x)-\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}\log|Q(x)|-\lambda_{0}\right)d\mu(x)\\
+\sum_{Q\in A}\left(\int\log|Q(x)|d\nu(x)-\beta_{Q}\right)b_{Q}.

We define

g((\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})):=\inf_{(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)}L\left((\mu,b_{Q}),(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\right).

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that
g((\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0}))>-\infty
. Then

(28)

\begin{split}\int\log|Q(x)|d\nu(x)=\beta_{Q}\geq 0,\\
x-U_{\nu}(x)-\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}\log|Q(x)|\geq\lambda_{0}\geq 0\end{split}

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We denote the above by the dual constraints. Moreover, we have

g((\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0}))=\lambda_{0}.

Proof.

Since the minimum of a non-zero linear function is
-\infty
, by (
27
)
g((\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0}))=-\infty
unless
\int\log|Q(x)|d\nu(x)=\beta_{Q}
. Suppose to the contrary that for some
x_{0}>0
,

x_{0}-U_{\nu}(x_{0})-\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}\log|Q(x_{0})|<0.

By taking
(\mu,b_{Q})=(a\delta_{x_{0}},0,\dots,0)
for arbitrary large
a>0
, it follows that

g((\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0}))=-\infty.

Finally, by the above inequalities and (
27
), we have

L\left((\mu,b_{Q}),(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\right)\geq\lambda_{0}

for every
(\mu,b_{Q})\in\mathcal{C}(A)
with equality for
(\mu,b_{Q})=(0,\dots,0)
.
∎

We say
(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})
is a feasible dual point if
(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})
satisfies all the dual constraints. We denote the set of dual feasible points by
\mathcal{D}(A)^{+}.

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose that
(\nu,\lambda_{Q},\beta_{Q},\lambda_{0})\in\mathcal{D}(A)^{+}
is a feasible point. We have

2e\geq\lambda_{0}+\sum_{Q}\deg(Q)\lambda_{Q}+\nu(\mathbb{R}).

Note that

(29)

x\geq\lambda_{0}+\sum_{Q}\lambda_{Q}\log|Q(x)|+U_{\nu}(x)

. We integrate the above inequality with respect to
\mu_{[0,4e]}
, the equilibrium measure of the interval

2e\geq\lambda_{0}+\sum_{Q}\deg(Q)\lambda_{Q}+\nu(\mathbb{R}).

Let
w_{\varepsilon}
be the compactly supported probability measure supported on

with logarithmic potential

(30)

for every

(31)

\varepsilon^{1/2}\gg C_{\varepsilon}=\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}+2\varepsilon^{3/2}+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}}\right)>0.

be the root distribution of

On the other hand, average the inequality (

This completes the proof of our proposition.
∎

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
be the unique solution to

\begin{cases}y\log y=(y-x)\log(y-x)+x\\
\log(x)\log(y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x).\end{cases}

Then
\Lambda_{A}=\hat{y}-\hat{y}\log\hat{y}+\hat{x}-\hat{x}\log\hat{x}
.

Here if the support is
\Sigma_{A}=[a,b]
,
\hat{x}
represents
\frac{a+b-2\sqrt{ab}}{4}
and
\hat{y}
represents
\frac{a+b+2\sqrt{ab}}{4}
. Note that the approximating support given by Serre as given in Corollary
1.7
satisfies the system of equations with error at most
10^{-10}
and giving
\Lambda_{\{x\}}\in(1.8983020088,1.8983020091)
.

Proof.

As seen by equation (33) in
[
OT24
]
, the fact that
\int\log|x|d\nu_{A}=0
is equivalent to

(50)

y\log y-y=(y-x)\log(y-x)-(y-x)

where
y=\int zd\nu_{A}(z)=\frac{a+b+2\sqrt{ab}}{4}
and
x=\frac{a+b-2\sqrt{ab}}{4}
for
0<a<b
. For the other equation, we look at the primal problem.

By Theorem
, we have that
U_{\mu_{A}}(x)=c\log(x)
on
[a,b]
for some positive constant
c
. The following is equivalent to the constant term of
U_{\mu_{A}}(x)
being zero:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(1-c)\log\left(\frac{b-a}{4}\right)-c\log\frac{a+b+2\sqrt{ab}}{b-a}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{b-a}{4}\right)-c\log\frac{a+b+2\sqrt{ab}}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(xy)-c\log(y).

So that
c=\frac{\log(xy)}{2\log(y)}
.
We are asked to minimize expectation of
\mu
which is

(51)

(y-x)+2(1-c)x=y+x-\frac{x\log(xy)}{\log(y)}=y-\frac{x\log(x)}{\log(y)}.

The following is equivalent to
\int\log|x|d\mu_{A}(x)=0
:

0=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(xy\right)-2c^{2}\log\left(\frac{y}{y-x}\right).

Since
c=\frac{\log(xy)}{2\log(y)}
, we have

0=\frac{1}{2}\log(xy)-\frac{\log(xy)^{2}}{2\log(y)^{2}}\left(\log(y)-\log(y-x)\right).

Dividing both sides by
\frac{\log(xy)}{2\log(y)}
, we can rearrange to get that

(52)

\log(x)\log(y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x).

Thus for some solution
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
to the simultaneous system (
50
) and (
52
),
\Lambda_{A}
is given by
\hat{y}-\frac{\hat{x}\log\hat{x}}{\log\hat{y}}
as stated in (
51
).
From (
50
), we can multiply by
\log(x)
to get

x\log x=y\log(y)\log(x)-(y-x)\log(y-x)\log(x).

Using (
52
) and simplifying, we get

x\log x=(y\log(y)+x\log(x))\log(y-x).

Thus

\Lambda_{A}=\hat{y}-\frac{\hat{x}\log\hat{x}}{\log\hat{y}}=\hat{y}-\frac{(\hat{y}\log\hat{y}+\hat{x}\log\hat{x})\log(\hat{y}-\hat{x})}{\log\hat{y}}=\hat{y}-(\hat{y}-\hat{x})\log(\hat{y}-\hat{x})-\frac{\hat{x}\log(\hat{x}\hat{y})\log(\hat{y}-\hat{x})}{\log\hat{y}}.

Applying (
50
) and (
52
) one last time, we have

\Lambda_{A}=\hat{y}-\hat{y}\log\hat{y}+\hat{x}-\hat{x}\log\hat{x}
.
It remains to show that the system of equations given by (
50
) and (
52
) has a unique solution.

The fact that the system has a solution is a consequence of Theorem
. For uniqueness, it suffices to show by uniqueness of the optimal dual measure per Theorem
1.5
that
\lambda_{A}
given by the dual measure is also
\hat{y}-\hat{y}\log\hat{y}+\hat{x}-\hat{x}\log\hat{x}
and that any solutions to the system of equations correspond to positive measures.
The value of
\lambda_{A}
is
\int zd\nu_{A}(z)-2xI_{\nu}
assuming the density function of
\nu_{A}
vanishes at
a
and
b
per Theorem
1.5
. This is

y-2x\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(xy)-\frac{2g^{2}}{(m-g)^{2}}\log\frac{m+g}{2g}+\frac{3g-m}{2(m-g)}\right)

where
m=x+y
and
g=y-x
. This simplifies to

2x-x\log(xy)+\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{x}\log\frac{y}{y-x}.

Since
(y-x)\log(y-x)=y\log y-x
, after simplifying we get

x-x\log(x)+y-y\log(y).

Thus the lower bound on
\Lambda_{A}
given by the dual measure matches the upper bound on
\Lambda_{A}
given by the primal solution.
To complete the proof of uniqueness, we need to show that solutions to

\begin{cases}y\log y=(y-x)\log(y-x)+x\\
\log(x)\log(y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x)\end{cases}

only correspond to
positive
measures in the primal and dual problems. For the dual problem, the density functions are always of the form
\hat{c}\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}
for some
\hat{c}>0
and
0<a<b
. Hence these measures are positive. For the primal problem, it suffices to show
c=\frac{\log(\hat{x}\hat{y})}{2\log(\hat{y})}>0
. We show the more general statement that
xy>1
and
y>1
for any point
(x,y)
on
\log(x)\log(y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x)

Let
L(x,y)=\log(x)\log(y)
and
R(x,y)=\log(xy)\log(y-x)
. Suppose
L(x,y)=R(x,y)
. Firstly,
y>x>0
in order for
L
and
R
to be defined.
Furthermore, for the sake of contradiction assume
y\leq 1
. Then we have
\log(xy)\leq\log(x)<0
and
\log(y-x)<\log(y)\leq 0
which is a contradiction because then
R(x,y)>L(x,y)
. So
y>1
.
Now assume
xy\leq 1
so that
0<x<1<y
and

. Firstly,
xy\neq 1
because then
R(x,y)=0
and
L(x,y)<0
. If

, then

because otherwise
L(x,y)<0
and
R(x,y)\geq 0
. Therefore,

0<-\log(xy)<-\log(x)

and

0<\log(y-x)<\log(y).

Multiplying, we have that
R(x,y)>L(x,y)
which is again a contradiction. Therefore,

and

for any solution to the system of equations. This completes the proof.

, one can write

(54)

[b_{Q}]_{Q\in A}=[W(Q,Q^{\prime})]_{Q,Q^{\prime}\in A}^{-1}\times[W(Q^{\prime})]_{Q^{\prime}\in A}.

Note
\overline{\textrm{Tr}}(Q)<\lambda_{SSS}
for every
Q\in\{x,x-1,x^{2}-3x+1,x^{3}-5x^{2}+6x-1\}
. By Proposition
5.6
,
\lambda_{Q}\neq 0
for every
Q\in\{x,x-1,x^{2}-3x+1,x^{3}-5x^{2}+6x-1\}
. Our numerical calculation also shows that
\det(W(Q,Q^{\prime}))\neq 0
for

Q,Q^{\prime}\in\{x,x-1,x^{2}-3x+1,x^{3}-5x^{2}+6x-1\}.

8.3.
Gradient descent algorithm

We estimate
\Lambda_{A}
by adapting the gradient descent algorithm developed in
[
OT24
]
. Given
\Sigma=\bigcup_{i}[a_{2i},a_{2i+1}]
, we are able to compute
X_{lin}
,
X_{eq}
and
X_{Q}
for each
Q\in A
as in section
8.1
.
We then compute
\mu_{eq,\Sigma}
,
\mu_{lin,\Sigma}
, and
\mu_{Q,\Sigma}
for each
Q\in A
as shown in section
8.1
. Define

\nu_{\Sigma}:=X_{eq}\mu_{eq,\Sigma}+X_{lin,\Sigma}\mu_{lin,\Sigma}+\sum_{Q\in A}X_{Q}\mu_{Q,\Sigma}.

We then compute the matrix
[W(Q^{\prime},Q)]
and compute
b_{Q}
coefficients as in (
54
), and define

\mu_{\Sigma}:=\left(1-\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\right)\mu_{eq}+\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\mu_{Q}.

With these density function, we can compute all of the required quantities of a particular measure. So the high-level algorithm is to perform gradient descent on
\Sigma
numerically (where
a_{i}
’s are the parameters) minimizing the sum of squares of
I(\nu_{\Sigma},\mu_{\Sigma})
, the value of the density function of
\nu_{\Sigma}
at the
a_{i}
’s, the values of
\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu_{\Sigma}
and
\int\log|Q(x)|d\nu_{\Sigma}
for
Q\in A
, and finally
\int xd\mu_{\Sigma}-\Lambda_{\Sigma}
because these values are 0 from Theorem
and Theorem
1.5
.

We use the same numerical methods that we introduce in
[
OT24
]
. We refer the reader to
[
OT24
, Section 6.2]
for further details of the numerical computations of singular integrals and the optimization methods that we introduce to increase the speed of the computations.

8.4.
Verification of Results

In this section, we verify that given numerical approximations to
\nu_{A}
and
\mu_{A}
, we get valid lower and upper bounds on
\Lambda_{A}
, respectively.
Fix a finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{Z}[x]
and
\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=0}^{l}[a_{2i},a_{2i+1}]
. Suppose
\mu
and
\nu
are Borel probability measures with support
\Sigma
.
Let
b_{Q}^{\prime}\geq 0
for each
Q\in A
so that
U_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|
for each
x\in\Sigma
and let
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}>0
and
\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\geq 0
for all
Q\in A
such that
x=\lambda_{0}^{\prime}+\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|+U_{\nu}(x)
for each
x\in\Sigma
.
Further assume that
\int\log|Q(x)|d\mu\geq 0
and
\int\log|Q(x)|d\nu\geq 0
for
Q\in A
.
In the rest of this section, we prove that
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)\leq\Lambda_{A}\leq\int xd\mu
.
In particular, we must also show that

(55)

U_{\mu}(x)\geq\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

and

(56)

x\geq\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)+\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|+U_{\nu}(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

given equality on
\Sigma
. We do this for special classes of
A
and
\mu
which are of interest to us and which are satisfied by Corollaries
1.8
through
1.10
. The following proposition gives a lower bound estimate on
\Lambda_{A}
. The proof is based off of the proof of Proposition 6.1 in
[
OT24
]
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
A,\Sigma,\lambda_{0}^{\prime},
and
\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}
for all
Q\in A
be defined as above. Suppose
\Sigma\subset[0,18]
and
\mu_{eq}
is the equilibrium measure of
\Sigma
. Define
\mathcal{R}
to be
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}:\exists Q\in A,Q(\alpha)=0\}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{R}\cap(a_{2i+1},a_{2(i+1)})=\{r_{i}\}
for each
i=0,\dots,l-1
and
\mathcal{R}\cap\big([0,a_{0})\cup(a_{2l+1},\infty)\big)=\{0\}
. Fix
\delta>0
so that for each
a_{i}
,

\delta_{i}:=\lim_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to a_{i}}}{{x\in\Sigma}}}\frac{d\nu(x)}{d\mu_{eq}(x)}\leq\delta,

for any
x\in(a_{2i+1},r_{i})
,

\sum_{Q\in A}\sum_{Q(\alpha)=0}\frac{\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}}{(x-\alpha)^{2}}+\int\frac{f_{2i+1}(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}dy>0

where
f_{i}(y)
is the density function of
\nu-\delta_{i}\mu_{eq}
, and for any
x\in(r_{i},a_{2(i+1)})
,

\sum_{Q\in A}\sum_{Q(\alpha)=0}\frac{\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}}{(x-\alpha)^{2}}+\int\frac{f_{2(i+1)}(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}dy>0.

Then
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)\leq\Lambda_{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{A}
is the measure given by Theorem
with support
\Sigma_{A}\subset[0,18]
.

It suffices to prove

x\geq\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)+\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|+U_{\nu}(x)\text{ for all }x\in[0,18].

Indeed, integrating both sides with respect to
d\mu_{A}(x)
, we conclude

\Lambda_{A}\geq\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)+I(\mu_{A},\nu).

Note that
I(\mu_{A},\nu)=\int U_{\mu_{A}}(x)d\nu\geq\int\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}\log(x)d\nu\geq 0
so that
\Lambda_{A}\geq\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)
.
Let

g(x):=x-\lambda_{0}^{\prime}+\delta\log(18)-\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|-U_{\nu}(x).

It is sufficient to show positivity of
g(x)\geq 0
on
[0,18]
. By assumption,
g(x)=\delta\log(18)>0
on
\Sigma
so we must show that this holds on the complement. We start by showing
g(x)\geq 0
for
x\in(a_{2i+1},r_{i})
. The proof for the remaining intervals is similar.

By the assumptions of the proposition, the density of
\nu-\delta_{2i+1}\mu_{eq}
vanishes at
a_{2i+1}
for some
\delta_{2i+1}<\delta
. Let

f(x):=x-\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\sum_{Q\in A}\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|-U_{\nu-\delta_{2i+1}\mu_{eq}}(x).

Note that
f(x)=\delta_{2i+1}C_{\Sigma}\geq 0
on
\Sigma
, where
C_{\Sigma}>0
is the logarithmic capacity of
\Sigma
and

g(x)-f(x)=\delta\log(18)-\delta_{2i+1}U_{\mu_{eq}}(x)\geq 0

for
0\leq x\leq 18
.
It is enough to show that
f(x)\geq 0
for any
x\in(a_{2i+1},r_{i}).
This is deduced from the fact that for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\Sigma
,

f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\sum_{Q\in A}\sum_{Q(\alpha)=0}\frac{\lambda_{Q}^{\prime}}{(x-\alpha)^{2}}+\int\frac{f_{2i+1}(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}dy>0,

where
f_{2i+1}(y)
is the density function of
\nu-\delta_{2i+1}\mu_{eq}
, which is positive by our assumption. We get this by Liebnitz’s rule since
\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\Sigma
is open.
By Lemma 4.2 of
[
OT24
]
,
f^{\prime}(x)=0
at
a_{2i+1}
. Since we assumed that there is only one root between any two intervals in
\Sigma
,
g(x)\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
∎

Now let
\mu_{Q}
denote the unique probability measure with potential
U_{\mu_{Q}}(x)\geq\frac{\log|Q(x)|}{\deg(Q)}-C_{Q,\epsilon}
with equality precisely on
\Sigma
for some constant
C_{Q,\epsilon}>0
. Since in the gradient descent algorithm, we choose
\mu=c\mu_{eq}+\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}^{\prime}\deg(Q)\mu_{Q}

for suitable
c>0
, it follows that
U_{\mu}(x)\geq\sum_{Q\in A}b_{Q}^{\prime}\log|Q(x)|
with equality precisely on
\Sigma
.
Therefore, it simply remains to check that the density function of
\nu
is positive which is possible because the numerator of its density function is a polynomial combination of absolute values of polynomials.
Once this is established,
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}-\delta\log(18)\leq\Lambda_{A}\leq\int xd\mu
.

be a probability measure, we let

However, since this is only an approximation of the goal measure, this does not have all of the positive values we require (including positivity of the density function). To account for this, we take

9.
Open Questions

The method provided herein helps us to compute better upper bounds on
\lambda^{SSS}
from the Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem; however, many questions about the problem remain open.
Some such questions about the general Schur-Siegel-Smyth trace problem are on the uniqueness of an optimal distribution and properties of the optimal distribution.

Conjecture 9.1
.

There is a unique arithmetic probability distribution
\mu_{SSS}
compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
so that
\int xd\mu_{SSS}=\lambda^{SSS}
.

Conjecture 9.2
.

If
\mu
is an arithmetic probability distribution compactly supported in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\int xd\mu=\lambda^{SSS}
, then
I(\mu)=0
.

Note that Conjecture
9.2
implies Conjecture
9.1
by strict concavity of the energy functional.
Note that Theorem
proves a finite version of Conjecture
9.2
.
Of course there is also future work in improving the bounds on
\lambda^{SSS}
to a more precise value, but it is currently thought to be out of reach to compute an explicit form for
\lambda^{SSS}
.

With the creation of a new method, other open questions arise about the method itself. In particular, we are interested in properties of the measures provided by the method for fixed
A\subset\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Let
\eta_{A}
denote the lower bound measure given in
[
OT24
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Conjecture 9.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be finite sets of integer polynomials with all of their roots being real. If
A\subset B
, then

\text{supp}(\eta_{B})\subset\text{supp}(\eta_{A})

and

\text{supp}(\mu_{B})\subset\text{supp}(\mu_{A})\subset\text{supp}(\eta_{A})

where
\text{supp}(\mu)
for a measure
\mu
denotes the support of
\mu
.

These containment proposed in Conjecture
9.3
have been observed by the authors in the cases which are computed and given herein and in
[
OT24
]
.
Another property studied of these measures is their logarithmic energies. We know that
I(\eta_{A})=I(\mu_{A})=0
, but
I(\nu_{A})<0
.

Conjecture 9.4
.

Let
A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset\dots
be an increasing chain of finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}[x]
so that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{A_{n}}\to\lambda^{SSS}
. Then

\frac{I(\nu_{A_{n}})}{\nu_{A_{n}}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{I(\nu_{A_{n+1}})}{\nu_{A_{n+1}}(\mathbb{R})}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{I(\nu_{A_{n}})}{\nu_{A_{n}}(\mathbb{R})}=0
.

Another open problem is in regards to the polynomial
p(x)
in Theorem
1.5
. This polynomial appears when multiple roots from the polynomials in
A
occur in a single gap of the measure’s support
\Sigma_{A}
.

Conjecture 9.5
.

Suppose that for a finite set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}[x]
containing only polynomials with all real roots. Let
\Sigma_{A}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{l}[a_{2i},a_{2i+1}]
. Enumerate the roots of the polynomials in
A
inside
(a_{2j+1},a_{2j+2})
by
r_{1}<\dots<r_{k}
for some
0\leq j<l
and
k>1
. Then the polynomial
p(x)
from Theorem
1.5
has a unique root in each of the intervals
(r_{s},r_{s+1})
for
1\leq s<k
.

Note that this accounts for all of the roots of
p
. The motivation behind this conjecture is that there “ought to be” an interval between the roots of the
Q
’s; however, these intervals are degenerate, one-point, closed intervals. Allowing such
\Sigma
s in Theorem
1.5
, we get this result. This conjecture is also supported by our numerical experiments.
