Consensus-Based Optimization for Saddle Point Problems
Hui Huang, Jinniao Qiu, Konstantin Riedl

Abstract
In this paper we propose consensus-based optimization for saddle point problems (CBO-SP), a novel multi-particle metaheuristic derivative-free optimization method capable of provably finding global Nash equilibria. Following the idea of swarm intelligence, the method employs two groups of interacting particles, one of which performs a minimization over one variable while the other performs a maximization over the other variable. The two groups constantly exchange information through a suitably weighted average. This paradigm permits for a passage to the mean-field limit, which makes the method amenable to theoretical analysis and it allows to obtain rigorous convergence guarantees under reasonable assumptions about the initialization and the objective function, which most notably include nonconvex-nonconcave objectives. We further provide numerical evidence for the success of the algorithm.



Keywords:
saddle point problems, Nash equilibria, nonconvex-nonconcave, derivative-free optimization, metaheuristics, consensus-based optimization, Fokker-Planck equations

Introduction

Optimization problems where the goal is to find the best possible objective value for the worst-case scenario can be formulated as minimax optimization problems of the form

\min_{x\in{\cal X}}\max_{y\in{\cal Y}}{\cal E}(x,y).

To be more specific, given a class of objective functions
\{{\cal E}(\,\cdot\,,y),y\in{\cal Y}\}
, the aim is to determine the argument
x^{*}\in{\cal X}
that leads to the smallest objective value even for the worst-case function parametrized by
y^{*}\in{\cal Y}
.
Such type of problems were originally formulated in two-player zero-sum game theory
[
47
]
but now arise in many areas in mathematics, biology, the social sciences and especially in economics
[
34
]
. Diverse applications may be found in engineering, operational research, biology, ecology, finance, economics, energy industry, environmental sciences and so on. In the last few years, minimax optimization has also experienced substantial attention from the signal processing community, due to its connection to distributed processing
[
8
]
, robust transceiver design
[
26
]
, and communication in the presence of jammers
[
16
]
.
Moreover, in modern machine learning, several problems are formulated as minimax optimization, such as the training of generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
17
]
, multi-agent reinforcement learning
[
38
]
, fair machine learning
[
29
]
, and adversarial training
[
30
]
.
For example, when training GANs,
x
models the parameter of a generator, usually a neural network, whose aim is to generate synthetic data with the same statistics as of a given training set, while
y
represents the parameters of a competing discriminator, who has to distinguish generated data by the generator from data of the true distribution.
Relatedly, in adversarial machine learning, one aims at learning the parameters
x
of a model in a robust manner by exposing it during training to possible adversarial attacks modeled by
y
.
Both examples can be interpreted as a game between two neural networks trained in an adversarial manner until some kind of equilibrium is reached.

In a two-player zero-sum game, the joint payoff function
{\cal E}(x,y)
encodes the gain of the maximization player whose action is to choose
y\in{\cal Y}
, as well as the loss of the minimization player controlling the action
x\in{\cal X}
.
In simultaneous games, each player chooses its action without the knowledge of the action chosen by the other player, so both players act simultaneously.
Conversely, in sequential games there is an intrinsic order according to which the players take their actions, meaning that the ordering of the minimization and maximization matters, i.e., it plays a priorly a role whether is
\min_{x}\max_{y}
or
\max_{y}\min_{x}
.
GANs and adversarial training, for instance, are in fact sequential games in their standard formulations.
In the classical case, where the payoff function
{\cal E}
is
convex-concave
(i.e.,
{\cal E}(\,\cdot\,,y)
is convex for all
y\in{\cal Y}
and
{\cal E}(x,\,\cdot\,)
is concave for all
x\in{\cal X}
), the intrinsic order of sequential games does not matter under an additional compactness assumption on either
{\cal X}
or
{\cal Y}
by the well-known minimax theorems of von Neumann and Sion
[
46
,
45
]
.
However, nowadays, most modern applications in signal processing and machine learning entail the setting of
nonconvex-nonconcave
minimax problems, where the minimization and maximization problems are potentially nonconvex and nonconcave.
This is significantly more complicated and available tool sets and theories are very limited; see the review paper
[
42
]
.

A well-known notion of optimality originating from game theory is the one of Nash equilibria (also referred to as saddle points)
[
35
]
, where neither of the players has anything to gain by changing only his own strategy.
This concept is formalized within the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
is called Nash equilibrium or saddle point of a function
{\cal E}
if it holds

{\cal E}(x^{*},y)\leq{\cal E}(x^{*},y^{*})\leq{\cal E}(x,y^{*})\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}

or, equivalently, if

\min_{x\in{\cal X}}\max_{y\in{\cal Y}}{\cal E}(x,y)={\cal E}(x^{*},y^{*})=\max_{y\in{\cal Y}}\min_{x\in{\cal X}}{\cal E}(x,y).

To keep the notation concise we write
{\cal E}^{*}
for
{\cal E}(x^{*},y^{*})
in what follows.

In the
convex-concave
setting an approximate Nash equilibrium can be found efficiently by variants of gradient descent-ascent (GDA) algorithms
[
4
,
18
]
, which alternate between one or more gradient decent steps in the
x
variable and gradient ascent steps in the
y
coordinate. Indeed, even if
{\cal E}(x,y)
is either concave in
y
or convex in
x
, there are available some multi-step GDA algorithms; see
[
36
,
42
]
for instance. However, as soon as the payoff function becomes
nonconvex-nonconcave
, finding a global equilibrium is in general an NP-hard problem
[
33
]
.
For this reason, some recent works such as
[
31
,
9
]
consider a local version of equilibria.
More precisely, a point
(x^{*},y^{*})\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
is called local Nash equilibrium if there exists some
\delta>0
such that
(x^{*},y^{*})
satisfies Definition
1
in a
\delta
-neighborhood of
(x^{*},y^{*})
. Local Nash equilibria can be characterized in terms of the so-called quasi-Nash equilibrium condition
[
39
]
or the first-order Nash equilibrium condition
[
36
]
. Even so, we mention two recent works where special classes of nonconvex-nonconcave payoff functions are concerned. When
{\cal E}(x,y)
is weakly convex in
x
and weakly concave in
y
and the associated Minty variational inequality admits a solution, Liu et al.
[
25
]
employ the inexact proximal point method and prove the first-order convergence, while under the so-called “sufficiently bilinear” condition, the stochastic Hamiltonian method is investigated by Loizou et al.
[
28
]
. In this work, we shall drop such restrictions and the gradient-dependence in the algorithms and consider a
zero-order
(derivative-free) method with rigorous convergence guarantees.
Note that the family of population-based algorithms, such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
[
22
]
, has been adapted to solve min-max problems as done for instance in
[
44
,
23
,
24
]
. One straightforward approach is to treat the min-max problem as a minimization problem and embed the maximization part in the calculation of the objective values
[
24
]
.
Alternatively, a multi-PSO strategy
[
44
,
23
]
may be employed, where the min-max problem is converted into two optimization problems,
one being a maximization problem, and the other a minimization problem.
Two PSO algorithms are then used to solve these two optimization problems, respectively, and they are run independently.
Each PSO is treated as a changing environment of the other PSO, allowing them to cooperate through the calculation of the objective.
Both approaches cannot avoid the necessity of nested loops/circles of optimization algorithms, which significantly increases the time complexity.

In the present paper we propose a zero-order consensus-based optimization method for finding the global Nash equilibrium
(x^{*},y^{*})
of a smooth objective function
{\cal E}:{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
{\cal X}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
{\cal Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, which is designed to be amenable to a rigorous theoretical convergence analysis, missing so far in the literature on population-based methods for min-max problems.
The dynamics of the algorithm is inspired by consensus-based optimization, a paradigm for global nonconvex minimizations, which was introduced by the authors of
[
40
]
.
Their method employs a system of interacting particles which explore the energy landscape in order to form a global consensus about the global minimizer of the objective function as time passes.
Taking inspiration from this concept, let us consider two sets of particles
(X^{i})_{i=1}^{N_{1}}
and
(Y^{i})_{i=1}^{N_{2}}
of potentially different size, one for minimization, the other for maximization.
Each individual particle of either set is formally described by a stochastic process.
In order to achieve consensus about the equilibrium point of
{\cal E}
, the particles interact through a system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of the form

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}\left(X_{t}^{i}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}})\right)dt+\sigma_{1}D\!\left(X_{t}^{i}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}})\right)dB_{t}^{X,i},

\displaystyle\textstyle\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}=\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}},

(1a)

\displaystyle dY_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{2}\left(Y_{t}^{i}-y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})\right)dt+\sigma_{2}D\!\left(Y_{t}^{i}-y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})\right)dB_{t}^{Y,i},

\displaystyle\textstyle\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\delta_{Y_{t}^{i}},

(1b)

which is complemented by suitable initial conditions
X_{0}^{i}\sim\rho_{X,0}\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
for
i=1,\dots,N_{1}
and
Y_{0}^{i}\sim\rho_{Y,0}\in{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
for
i=1,\dots,N_{2}
and where
\big(\big(B_{t}^{X,i}\big)_{t\geq 0}\big)_{i=1,\dots,N_{1}}
and
\big(\big(B_{t}^{Y,i}\big)_{t\geq 0}\big)_{i=1,\dots,N_{2}}
are independent standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, respectively.
Moreover,
\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}
and
\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}
denote the empirical measures of the particles’
x
- and
y
-positions, respectively.
While the dynamics (
1a
) performs minimization in the
x
-variable, (
1b
) performs maximization in the
y
-coordinate.
This is encoded in the computation of the so-called consensus point
\big(x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}}),y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})\big)
, whose components are given by

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}})

\displaystyle=\!\int\!x\,\frac{\omega_{\alpha}\big(x,\int\!y\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y)\big)}{\big\|{\omega_{\alpha}\big(\,\cdot\,,\int\!y\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y)\big)}\big\|_{L_{1}(\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}})}}\,d\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}(x),

\displaystyle\textrm{with}\ \,\omega_{\alpha}(x,y)\!:=\!\exp(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)),

(2a)

\displaystyle y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})

\displaystyle=\!\int\!y\,\frac{\omega_{-\beta}\big(\int\!x\,d\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}(x),y\big)}{\big\|{\omega_{-\beta}\big(\int\!x\,d\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}(x),\,\cdot\,\big)}\big\|_{L_{1}(\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}})}}\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y),

\displaystyle\textrm{with}\ \,\omega_{-\beta}(x,y)\!:=\!\exp(\beta{\cal E}(x,y)).

(2b)

Attributed to the Laplace principle
[
32
]
,
x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}})
can be interpreted as an approximation of
\operatorname*{\arg\min}_{i=1,\dots,N_{1}}{\cal E}(X_{t}^{i},\int\!y\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y))
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
while
y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})\approx\operatorname*{argmax}_{i=1,\dots,N_{2}}{\cal E}(\int\!x\,d\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}(x),Y_{t}^{i})
as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, see, e.g.,
[
14
, Equation (7)]
.
The dynamics of each of the particles in (
1
) is governed by two terms.
A drift term drags the particles towards the respective component of the instantaneous consensus point
\big(x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\widehat{\rho}_{X,t}^{N_{1}}}),y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})\big)
and thereby expectedly improves the position of the particles.
The second term injects stochasticity into the dynamics by diffusing the particles according to a scaled Brownian motion, which features the exploration of the landscape of the objective.
In what follows we use anisotropic noise, i.e.,
D(\,\cdot\,)=\operatorname{diag}(\,\cdot\,)
, which is typically more competitive in high dimensions compared to isotropic noise
D(\,\cdot\,)=\left\|{\,\cdot\,}\right\|_{2}
, see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
]
.
The theoretical results of this paper, however, can be obtained mutatis mutandis also in the isotropic setting.

An implementable scheme for a numerical algorithm can be obtained from (
1
) by a simple Euler-Maruyama time discretization
[
19
,
41
]
.
For details about the implementation we refer to Algorithm
1
in Section
5.1
.

Remark 2
.

While the definition of the consensus point in (
2
) is a natural option, there are two equally reasonable alternatives.
The first possibility is to replace the mean
\int\!y\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y)
in (

and integrate w.r.t. the joint measure
\widehat{\rho}_{t}^{N}

Analogously,

is substituted by

).
The second option is to use the other component of the consensus point instead of the respective mean, i.e.,
y_{\beta}^{X}({\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}})

\int\!y\,d\widehat{\rho}_{Y,t}^{N_{2}}(y)

) and

The main motivation for using the variant as in (

Using either of the other two alternatives significantly complicates the convergence analysis in Sections

Contributions.

Motivated by the fundamental importance of
nonconvex-nonconcave
saddle point problems in various applicational areas and the desire for having numerical algorithms with rigorous global convergence guarantees, we theoretically analyze in this work a novel consensus-based optimization method (CBO-SP) capable of tackling saddle point problems.
Using mean-field analysis techniques, we rigorously prove that CBO-SP converges to saddle points as the number of interacting particles goes to infinity.
Our results hold under reasonable assumptions about the objective function and under certain conditions of well-preparation of the hyperparameters and the initial data.

Well-Posedness of CBO-SP and its Mean-Field Dynamics

In the first part of this section we provide a well-posedness result about the interacting particle system (
1
) of CBO-SP, i.e., we show that a process obeying (
1

Afterwards we also prove the well-posedness of the nonlinear macroscopic SDE (
3
).

2.1
Well-Posedness of the Interacting Particle System

To keep the notation concise in what follows, let us denote the state vector of the entire particle system (
1
) by
\mathbf{Z}\in{\cal C}([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}d_{1}+N_{2}d_{2}})
with
\mathbf{Z}(t)=\mathbf{Z}_{t}=\left((X_{t}^{1})^{T},\dots,(X_{t}^{N_{1}})^{T},(Y_{t}^{1})^{T},\dots,(Y_{t}^{N_{2}})^{T}\right)^{T}
for every
t\geq 0
.
Equation (
1
) can then be reformulated as

d\mathbf{Z}_{t}=-\bm{\lambda}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})\,dt+\bm{\sigma}\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})\,d\mathbf{B}_{t}

(5)

with
(\mathbf{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
being a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}d_{1}+N_{2}d_{2}}
and definitions

\begin{split}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}):=\,&\left({F}^{1,X}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})^{T},\dots,{F}^{N_{1},X}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})^{T},{F}^{1,Y}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})^{T},\dots,{F}^{N_{2},Y}(\mathbf{Z}_{t})^{T}\right)^{T}\\

Having fixed the notation, we have the following well-posedness result for the SDE system (

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\cal E}\in{\cal C}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}})
be locally Lipschitz continuous.
Then, for
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, the system of SDEs (
1
) admits a unique strong solution
\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
for any initial condition
\mathbf{Z}_{0}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\|{\mathbf{Z}_{0}}\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
.

In order to employ the standard result
[
10
, Chapter 5, Theorem 3.1]
about the existence and uniqueness of solutions to SDEs, we need to verify that the coefficients of the SDE are locally Lipschitz continuous and of at most linear growth.
This is inherited from the assumed local Lipschitz continuity of
{\cal E}
as we make explicit in the subsequent lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha,\beta>0
and

be arbitrary.
Let
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{\widehat{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}d_{1}+N_{2}d_{2}}
be of the form
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{y}^{T})^{T}=\big((x^{1})^{T},\dots,(x^{N_{1}})^{T},(y^{1})^{T},\dots,(y^{N_{2}})^{T}\big)^{T}
and analogously for
\mathbf{\widehat{z}}
.
Then, for any
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{\widehat{z}}
with
\|{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}\leq R
and
\|{\mathbf{\widehat{z}}}\|_{2}\leq R
it holds for any

the bounds

\big\|{{F}^{i,X}(\mathbf{z})}\big\|_{2}\leq\|{x^{i}}\|_{2}+\|{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\big\|{{F}^{i,Y}(\mathbf{z})}\big\|_{2}\leq\|{y^{i}}\|_{2}+\|{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2},

and, abbreviating
c_{R}(\gamma):=4\gamma e^{2\gamma\Delta_{R}{\cal E}}\big\|{\left\|{\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}{\cal E}}\right\|_{2}}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}

with
\Delta_{R}{\cal E}:=\sup_{\mathbf{z}\in B_{R}}{\cal E}(\mathbf{z})-\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in B_{R}}{\cal E}(\mathbf{z})

3
Convergence to Saddle Points

Inspired by the theories of mean-field limits for consensus-based optimization methods (see
[
14
,
20
,
11
]
for instance), the convergence of particles systems (
1
) to the mean-field dynamics (
3
) follows in a similar way and thus the associated argument is omitted. In this section we present the main theoretical result of our paper concerned with the convergence of the macroscopic dynamics (

) towards saddle points of objective functions

that satisfy the following conditions.

Definition 9

(Assumptions)
.

Throughout this section we are interested in objective functions
{\cal E}\in{\cal C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}})
, for which

A1

there exist two functions
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in{\cal C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in{\cal C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(y)\leq{\cal E}(x,y)\leq\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(x)

for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
.
The functions
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muC\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{\nabla{\cal E}}>0

A2

there exist constants

\begin{split}&\max\left\{\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\left\|{\nabla_{x}{\cal E}(x,y)}\right\|_{2},\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\left\|{\nabla_{y}{\cal E}(x,y)}\right\|_{2}\right\}\leq C_{\nabla{\cal E}},\text{ and}\\

denotes the
L^{\infty}
norm on
{\cal C}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\right)
and
\rho
denotes the spectral radius.

A3

there exist constants
\epsilon_{0},\eta,\nu>0
such that for each
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
satisfying

{\cal E}^{*}-{\cal E}(x^{*},{y})\leq\epsilon_{0}

and

for some

of

, we have

\left\|{x-x^{*}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\big(|{{\cal E}(x,y^{*})-{\cal E}^{*}}|\big)^{\nu}\mbox{ and }\left\|{y-y^{*}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\big(|{{\cal E}(x^{*},y)-{\cal E}^{*})}|\big)^{\nu}.

Assumption
A1
requires that the twice continuously differentiable objective function
{\cal E}
is bounded from below by a function
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, which depends only on the
y
coordinate, and from above by a function
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, which depends only on the
x
coordinate.
Moreover, the first order derivatives of
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

is a mere technical regularity assumption about
{\cal E}
in terms of the first and second derivatives.
In particular, it requires that the gradients as well as second order derivatives of

[

5

21

However, as a purely zero-order derivative-free method, our CBO-SP algorithm only uses point-wise values of the objective function

{\cal E}
.
It imposes coercivity of
{\cal E}
around saddle points, which relates the distance from

We refer to the discussion after
[

and

In order to ensure consensus formation of the mean-field dynamics (
3
) we show that the variances
\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathrm{Var}^{Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t}}\|_{2}^{2}
of the particle distribution decay to
0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
For this we need to analyze their time-evolutions, which is the content of the following statement.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathrm{Var}^{X}
and
\mathrm{Var}^{Y}
be as defined in (
12
) and let us recall the definitions of
{\cal M}^{X}
and
{\cal M}^{Y}
from (
13b
).
Then, under Assumption
A1
, it holds

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)\leq-2\bigg(\lambda_{1}\!-\!\frac{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}{{\cal M}^{X}(t)}\bigg)\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Var}^{Y}(t)\leq-2\bigg(\lambda_{2}\!-\!\frac{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}{{\cal M}^{Y}(t)}\bigg)\mathrm{Var}^{Y}(t).\end{split}

(18)

Proof.

By means of Itô’s calculus we have

d\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=2\left(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}\right)\cdot d\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}dt.

Since the appearing stochastic integral vanishes when taking the expectation as a consequence of the regularity established in Theorem
6
and Assumption
A1
, we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)&=-2\lambda_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})\right)\right]+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=-2\lambda_{1}\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)+\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2},\end{split}

(19)

where we used that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})\right)\right]=0
.
Analogously, we derive

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Var}^{Y}(t)&=-2\lambda_{2}\mathrm{Var}^{Y}(t)+\sigma_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t}-y_{\beta}^{X}({\rho_{Y,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(20)

In order to control the terms
\mathbb{E}\big\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\big\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\big\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t}-y_{\beta}^{X}({\rho_{Y,t}})}\big\|_{2}^{2}
appearing in (
19
) and (
20
), let us first observe that, for any
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
\widehat{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\|{\widehat{x}-x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\omega_{\alpha}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t},\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})}\int\!\left\|{\widehat{x}-x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})\,d\rho_{X,t}(x),

(21)

\displaystyle\left\|{\widehat{y}-y_{\beta}^{X}({\rho_{Y,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\omega_{-\beta}(\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})}\int\!\left\|{\widehat{y}-y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\omega_{-\beta}(\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t},y)\,d\rho_{Y,t}(y).

Exploiting the boundedness of
{\cal E}
as of Assumption
A1
, the two latter bounds in particular imply

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}\!-\!x_{\alpha}^{Y}({\rho_{X,t}})}\right\|_{2}^{2}&\!\leq\!\frac{2}{\mathbb{E}\omega_{\alpha}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t},\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})}\int\!\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|{\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}-\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\!+\!\left\|{\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t}\!-\!x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\omega_{\alpha}(x,\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})\,d\rho_{X,t}(x)\\
&\!\leq\!2\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)\!+\!\frac{2}{\mathbb{E}\omega_{\alpha}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{X}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}X_{t},\mathbb{E}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{Y}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\phantom{Y}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}Y_{t})}e^{-\alpha\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu{\cal E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu(\mathbb{E}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muY\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 0.0mu_{t})}\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)\!\leq\!4\frac{\mathrm{Var}^{X}(t)}{{\cal M}^{X}(t)}\end{split}

(23)

and analogously

6
Conclusions

In this paper we propose consensus-based optimization for saddle point problems (CBO-SP) and analyze its global convergence behavior to global Nash equilibria.
As apparent from the proof, our technique requires the equilibrium to satisfy the saddle point property, i.e., that
\min_{x}\max_{y}
and
\max_{y}\min_{x}
coincide.
We leave to further research the extension of the results to sequential games, where the latter condition does not hold.
This is in particular relevant in, for instance, the training of GANs, which are formulated as non-simultaneous games.

A.2
The Laplace Principle

Lemma A.3
.

For any fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and any
\delta>0
, define the set

\displaystyle S_{y,\delta}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}:\exp\left(-{\cal E}(x,y)\right)>\exp\left(-\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta\right\}.

Let
\{\mu^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq 1}
be a family of measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and assume that there exists some constant
C_{\delta}>0
depending only on
\delta
such that
\mu^{\alpha}(S_{y,\delta})\geq C_{\delta}
for all
\alpha\geq 1
.
Then it holds

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\exp\left(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)\right)d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y).

Proof.

We first notice that by definition of the set
S_{y,\delta}
it holds

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\exp\left(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)\right)d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int_{S_{y,\delta}}\left(\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta\right)^{\alpha}d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left(\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta\right)\mu^{\alpha}(S_{y,\delta})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta\right)C_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta\quad\text{ as }\quad\alpha\to\infty.

Thus, for any
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\exp\left(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)\right)d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\geq\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right)-\delta.

On the other hand, clearly

\displaystyle\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\exp\left(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)\right)d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\leq\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right).

Since
\delta
was arbitrary, this implies

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\exp\left(-\alpha{\cal E}(x,y)\right)d\mu^{\alpha}(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}=\exp\left(-\textstyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}{\cal E}(x,y)\right),

giving the result after taking the logarithm on both sides.
∎
