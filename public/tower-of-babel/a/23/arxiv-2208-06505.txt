Bound States of Central Force System in Special Relativity
Iwan Setiawan, Ryan Sugihakim, Bobby Eka Gunara

Abstract
In this paper we consider the central force problem in the special theory of relativity. We derive the special relativistic version of the Binet equation describing the orbit of a body. Then, the motion of a planet in a solar-like system where the gravitational potential is modified by adding a term that is proportional to the inverse of the square of the radial coordinate is discussed. Using perturbative method, we obtain the explicit orbital function. At the end, the orbital precession of the planet and its relation to the result from general relativity are also discussed.

INTRODUCTION

One of the common situations in physics is the motion of a body under influence of the central force in which the magnitude of the force depends only on the radial coordinate. The problem is relevant to realistic conditions such as the motion under either gravitational force or electrostatic force. Many standard textbooks in classical mechanics discuss the central force problem such as two-body system to solve the motion of interacting objects, see for example,
[
1
,
2
]
. We can say that this problem is essential to physics students improving their competence.

In the non-relativistic mechanics, the central force problem can be reduced to second order ordinary differential equation called Binet equation whose solutions are called orbit in which the position can be viewed as a function of angle in the polar coordinate. Several papers have been published to study this equation. For example, in
[
3
,
4
]
some authors considered a macroscopic case where the potential energy has polynomial form which covers the standard Newton gravitational potential energy that is proportional to the inverse of the radial coordinate. They find that the orbit is an implicit function related to a hypergeometric function. At the microscopic level, the authors in
[
5
]
have studied the Sommerfeld atomic model of Hydrogen-like model. However, we know that this is unrealistic since according to the astronomical observation the relativistic effect cannot be ignored especially in the motion of a planet near a massive object. Therefore, the Binet equation has to be extended to the relativistic domain.

In the extension to the relativistic mechanics, although general relativity is the most favourable at the level of macroscopic system, it is still of interest to consider special relativity since it is much simpler than the general theory. In other words, we can study the dynamics of a particle without curving the spacetime. There are several attempt in this direction. For example, in
[
6
]
the author extend the concepts the central and conservative forces to special relativity. The other authors in
[
7
]
have tried to extend Bertrand’s theorem to the central force problem in special relativity. They also find that there is a special form of potentials which admits a stable circular orbit. There are also other authors that have considered this problem at the microscopic level to produce Sommerfeld-Dirac fine structure formula
[
8
,
9
]
.

According to the above developments, we also attempt to consider the central force problem in special relativity. First, we review the derivation of the relativistic Binet equation describing the orbit of a body
[
7
,
10
]
. We shortly discuss the stability criterion of the circular orbit. Then, a simple case is considered where the potential is proportional to the inverse of the radial coordinate. We obtain an explicit orbital function in which the orbital precession of a body appears naturally for elliptical orbits which can be regraded as the relativistic effect
[
10
]
, see also the discussion in
[
11
]
. This precession vanishes if the orbit is circular.

Next, at the microscopic level the above results are applied to the case of the hydrogen-like atom. Using the Wilson-Sommerfeld quantization rule, we obtain the energy level of the system so called Sommerfeld-Dirac fine structure formula
[
8
,
9
]
which can also be derived from Dirac equation in relativistic quantum mechanics
[
12
,
13
]
.

At the final part, we discuss a planetary motion in which the potential energy is fine-tuned by adding the term which is proportional to the cubic of of the radial coordinate with arbitrary pre-coefficient. This potential energy has been appeared in the case of a free falling body in Schwarzschild spacetime, see for example,
[
14
,
15
]
and Randall-Sundrum model of gravity
[
16
]
. Using perturbative method, we get an explicit orbital function that admits the orbital precession of the planet and then, we compare it with the result from general relativity. It is worth mentioning that this explicit orbital function has never been considered in the literature above. Also, we pointed out the second Kepler’s laws which differs to the case of Newtonian mechanics.

In this paper, it is convenient to use Lagrangian formulation as a starting point. We use the relativistic Lagrangian that can be found from textbooks such as by Landau and Lifshitz

2
Special Relativistic Central Force Problem

In this section we discuss our general setup of a central force system in special theory of relativity. Then, our discussion will be focus on an attractive force whose scalar potential is proportional to the inverse of the distance.

Let us first consider a relativistic particle with the rest mass
m_{0}
moving with speed
v
under the influence of a central force that depends only on the radial coordinate
r
whose potential energy function is given by
V\equiv V(r)
. This implies that we could reduce the case to two dimensional problem such that using the polar coordinate
(r,\theta)
for space with coordinate time
t
. We define

\displaystyle\gamma(v)\equiv\quantity(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2})^{-1/2}~.

(1)

The Lagrangian of this system is

\displaystyle L(r,\theta,\dot{r},\dot{\theta})=-\frac{m_{0}c^{2}}{\gamma(v)}-V(r)~.

(2)

The dot over a quantity denotes the derivative with respect to
t
. After some computation we could show that the constants of motion are the angular momentum
p_{\theta}
and the total ’mechanical’ energy
E
(see
A
for details)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=\gamma m_{0}r^{2}\dot{\theta}~,

(3)

\displaystyle E=\gamma m_{0}c^{2}+V(r)~,

(4)

whereas the radial momentum

\displaystyle p_{r}=\gamma m_{0}\dot{r}=-p_{\theta}u^{\prime}~,

(5)

is not constant where
u\equiv 1/r,u^{\prime}\equiv\derivative*{u}{\theta}
. We note that

\displaystyle\gamma=\quantity[1 + \frac{p_\theta^2}{m_0^2 c^2}\qty(u'^2 + u^2)]^{1/2}=\frac{E-V}{m_{0}c^{2}}~.

(6)

From (
4
) and (
6
), we obtain then

\displaystyle m_{0}^{2}c^{4}=(E-V)^{2}-u^{\prime 2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}-u^{2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}~,

(7)

such that if we differentiate (
7
) with respect to
\theta
, it leads to the relativistic Binet equation

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{u}{\theta}+u+\frac{(E-V)}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\derivative{V}{u}=0~.

(8)

Some comments are in order. If
u
is a positive constant, then we may have a circular orbit. This is the simplest closed orbit which is possible if
the following condition fulfills

\displaystyle u_{0}=\frac{(V(u_{0})-E)}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\frac{dV}{du}(u_{0})~,

(9)

where
r_{0}=1/u_{0}
is the radius of the circular orbit implying
V(u_{0})\neq E
and
u_{0}
is not the extremal point of
V(u)
, namely,
\frac{dV}{du}(u_{0})\neq 0
. These features distinguish to the case in Newtonian mechanics as discussed in
[
1
]
. Moreover, this orbit is stable under small perturbations if it satisfies
[
7
]

\displaystyle\xi\equiv 1-\frac{dJ}{du}(u_{0})>0~,

(10)

that should be a rational number where

\displaystyle J\equiv\frac{(V-E)}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\frac{dV}{du}~.

(11)

In the case of attractive potentials such as Newtonian gravitational potential and Coulomb potential, the condition (
10
) follows

\displaystyle K^{2}/p_{\theta}^{2}<c^{2}~.

(12)

Orbital Motion in a Special Class of Central Force

Now, we particularly discuss the potential energy function whose form is given by

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{K}{r},\quad K>0~,

(13)

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{u}{\theta}+\quantity(1 - \frac{K^2}{p_\theta^2 c^2})u=\frac{KE}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}~.

\displaystyle u(\theta)=\frac{1+e\cos(\kappa\theta)}{a(1-e^{2})}~,

(15)

\displaystyle e=\sqrt{\beta}~,

(16)

\displaystyle a=\frac{K}{E}\quantity[\qty(\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^2 - 1]\frac{1}{1-e^{2}}~,

(17)

\displaystyle\kappa\equiv\quantity[1 - \qty(\frac{K}{p_\theta c})^2]^{1/2}~,

(18)

\displaystyle\beta\equiv\quantity(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E})^{2}+\quantity(\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^{2}-\quantity(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E}\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^{2}~.

(19)

We could express the angular momentum and the energy in terms of

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}{K^{2}}=\frac{1+e^{2}}{2}\quantity[1 + \frac{1 - e^2}{1 + e^2}\sqrt{1 + 4\eta^2}]~,

(20)

\displaystyle E=\frac{K}{a(1-e^{2})}\quantity[\frac{p^2_\theta c^2}{K^2} - 1]~,

(21)

respectively, with
\eta\equiv\frac{m_{0}c^{2}a}{K}
. It is also important to state that in this relativistic case we have the orbital precession

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta=\frac{2\pi}{1-\frac{K^{2}}{2p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}}-2\pi~.

(22)

3
Hydrogen-like Atomic Model

In this section we apply our study in the preceding case to the model of hydrogen-like atom where an electron with mass
m_{e}
orbits a proton as nucleus with mass
m_{N}
. They interact by the Coulomb force with
K=q^{2}/4\pi\epsilon_{0}
, where
q
is the magnitude of electron and proton charges. This case at hand can be viewed as a single particle model moving under the influence of the Coulomb force with reduced mass
\mu=\quantity(1/m_e + 1/m_N)^{-1}
. Using Wilson-Sommerfeld rule of quantization

\displaystyle\oint p_{\theta}\differential\theta=n_{\theta}h,\rightarrow p_{\theta}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta=n_{\theta}h~,

(24)

\displaystyle\oint p_{r}\differential r=n_{r}h\rightarrow p_{\theta}\oint\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{u^{2}}\differential\theta=p_{\theta}\oint\frac{\kappa^{2}\sin[2](\kappa\theta)}{\quantity(1/e + \cos(\kappa\theta))^{2}}\differential\theta=n_{r}h~,

(25)

with
n_{\theta},n_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
. From the first integral, we have

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=n_{\theta}\hbar~,

(26)

whereas for the second integral, it gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin[2](\tilde\theta)}{\quantity(1/e + \cos(\tilde\theta))^{2}}\differential\tilde{\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}+2\pi=\frac{2\pi n_{r}}{\sqrt{n_{\theta}^{2}-\alpha^{2}}}~,

(27)

where
\tilde{\theta}=\kappa\theta
,
\kappa=\sqrt{1-(\alpha^{2}/n_{\theta}^{2})}
, and
\alpha=q^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon_{0}\hbar c)
implying

\displaystyle e=\quantity[1 - \qty(\frac{\sqrt{n_\theta^2 - \alpha^2}}{n_r + \sqrt{n_\theta^2 - \alpha^2}})^2]^{1/2}.

(28)

Since
e\in[0,1]
,
n_{\theta}
cannot be zero since it produces complex number for
e
, then
n_{\theta}\geq 1
. While
n_{r}
can be chosen
n_{r}\geq 0
. Defining
\tilde{n}\equiv\sqrt{n_{\theta}^{2}-\alpha^{2}}
and
n^{\prime}\equiv n_{r}+\sqrt{n_{\theta}^{2}-\alpha^{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle e=\sqrt{1-\frac{\tilde{n}^{2}}{n^{\prime 2}}}~.

(29)

The values of
n^{\prime}
is given in Figure

.

\displaystyle e=\pm\quantity[\qty(\frac{\mu c^2}{E})^2 + \qty(\frac{n_\theta}{\alpha})^2 - \qty(\frac{n_\theta}{\alpha}\frac{\mu c^2}{E})^2]^{1/2}~.

(30)

The energy of the system is

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\mu c^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n_{\theta}^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{n_{\theta}^{2}-e^{2}\alpha^{2}}}=\frac{\mu c^{2}}{\sqrt{1+\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{\prime 2}}}}=\mu c^{2}\quantity(1 + \dfrac{\alpha^2}{\qty(n_r + \sqrt{n_\theta^2 - \alpha^2})^2})^{-1/2}.

\displaystyle n=1,2,3,\dots,\quad j=\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2},\dots

(32)

where

4
The Orbital Precession in Modified Gravity

This section is devoted to consider a solar system where a planet with mass
m
moves around a massive star with mass
M
. We assume that the orbit is closed. In this section, we take
K=GMm
where
G
is the universal gravitational constant.

First, let us focus on a modified gravitational theory whose potential energy has the form

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{K}{r}+\frac{\epsilon K_{1}}{r^{3}}~,

(33)

where
K_{1}
is a real constant and
\epsilon
is a small dimensionless quantity. The above form of potential energy is motivated by generalized Binet equation derived using Schwarzschild geometry, see for example
[
15
]
and a modified gravitational theory in the context of Randall-Sundrum Model
[
16
]
. It is important to notice that since
K_{1}
is an arbitrary real constant in (
33
), it also covers the case in
[
10
]
where they have fixed the constant coming from perturbative modified gravitational force.

Setting
\epsilon\ll 1
, the Binet equation (
8
) simplifies to

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}+\kappa^{2}u-\frac{EK}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}=-\epsilon K_{1}\quantity(\frac{4 K}{p_\theta^2 c^2}u^3 + \frac{3 E }{p_\theta^2 c^2}u^2)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})~,

(34)

such that we can write the solution of (
34
) as

\displaystyle u(\theta)=u_{0}(\theta)+\epsilon u_{1}(\theta)~.

(35)

The function
u_{0}
is the unperturbed solution given in (
15
), whereas the function
u_{1}
is the first order of perturbation with the boundary value
u_{1}(0)=u_{1}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Inserting the ansatz (
35
) into (
34
), it results

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon u_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\kappa^{2}(u_{0}+\epsilon u_{1})-\frac{EK}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}=-\epsilon K_{1}\quantity(\frac{4 K}{p_\theta^2 c^2}u_0^3 + \frac{3 E}{p_\theta^2 c^2}u_0^2)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})~.

(36)

Then, equalizing the first order terms, we have

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\kappa^{2}u_{1}=-u_{0}^{2}\quantity(\frac{4 KK_1}{p_\theta^2 c^2}u_0 + \frac{3 EK_1}{p_\theta^2 c^2})~,

(37)

whose solution has the form

\displaystyle u_{1}=c_{1}\cos(\kappa\theta)+c_{2}\sin(\kappa\theta)+v_{1}(\theta)\cos(\kappa\theta)+v_{2}(\theta)\sin(\kappa\theta)~,

(38)

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are real constants, and

\displaystyle v_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{1}}{\kappa p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\theta}\differential\theta^{\prime}\ u_{0}^{2}\quantity(4Ku_0 + 3E)\sin(\kappa\theta')

\displaystyle=-\frac{K_{1}}{ea\kappa^{2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}\quantity(1 - e^2)^{2}}\bigg[\frac{K\quantity[1 + e\cos(\kappa\theta)]^{4}}{ea\quantity(1 - e^2)^{3}}+E\quantity[1 + e\cos(\kappa\theta)]^{3}\bigg]~,

(39)

\displaystyle v_{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{K_{1}}{\kappa p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\theta}\differential\theta^{\prime}\ u_{0}^{2}\quantity(4Ku_0 + 3E)\cos(\kappa\theta')

\displaystyle=-\frac{K_{1}}{8a\kappa^{2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}\quantity(1 - e^2)^{3}}\bigg[Ke^{3}\sin(4\kappa\theta)+\quantity{12Eea(1-e^2) + 8Ke^3 + 24 Ke}\sin(2\kappa\theta)

\displaystyle\quad+\quantity{-8Ee^2a(1-e^2) - 32Ke^2}\sin[3](\kappa\theta)+\quantity{(24Ee^2 + 24E)a(1-e^2) + 96Ke^2 + 32K}\sin(\kappa\theta)

\displaystyle\quad+\quantity{24Eea(1-e^2) + 12Ke^3 + 48 Ke}\kappa\theta\bigg],

(40)

where
e
and
a
are given in (
16
) and (
17
) respectively. According to the initial value,
u_{1}(0)=u_{1}^{\prime}(0)=0
, we should have
c_{1}=-v_{1}(0)
and
c_{2}=-v_{2}(0)=0
. Therefore, the complete orbit function is

\displaystyle u(\theta)=\frac{1+e\cos(\kappa\theta)}{a(1-e^{2})}-v_{1}(0)\epsilon\cos(\kappa\theta)+\epsilon v_{1}(\theta)\cos(\kappa\theta)+\epsilon v_{2}(\theta)\sin(\kappa\theta)~.

(41)

From the equations (
41
), (
4
), and (
4
), it is easy to see that
v_{1}
is bounded from above and below since it consists of cosinus functions, thus we can ignore the term
\epsilon v_{1}\cos(\kappa\theta)
. We can also ignore the term
v_{1}(\theta)\epsilon\cos(\kappa\theta)
by the same reason. Meanwhile,
v_{2}
, is a non bounded function on
\theta
, so that we can approximate the last term in (
41
) to

-\frac{\epsilon K_{1}\quantity{24Eea(1-e^2) + 12Ke^3 + 48 Ke}\theta\sin(\kappa\theta)}{8a\kappa p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}(1-e^{2})^{3}}~.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle u(\theta)\approx\frac{1+e\cos(\kappa\theta)}{a(1-e^{2})}-\frac{\epsilon K_{1}}{\kappa p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\frac{6Eea(1-e^{2})+3Ke^{3}+12Ke}{2a(1-e^{2})^{3}}\theta\sin(\kappa\theta)~.

(42)

Let us define

\displaystyle\sigma\equiv-\frac{\epsilon K_{1}}{\kappa p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\frac{6Eea(1-e^{2})+3Ke^{3}+12Ke}{2a(1-e^{2})^{3}}~.

(43)

Since
\sigma
is small, then

\displaystyle\alpha\cos(\kappa\theta)+\sigma\theta\sin(\kappa\theta)\approx\alpha\cos(\qty(\kappa- \frac{\sigma}{\alpha})\theta)~,

(44)

where

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{e}{a(1-e^{2})}~.

(45)

So we can simplify (
42

\displaystyle u(\theta)=\frac{1+e\cos(\qty[\kappa- \frac{\sigma a(1 - e^2)}{e}]\theta)}{a(1-e^{2})}~.

(46)

The orbital precession when the body revolves in one period

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta=\frac{2\pi}{\kappa-\frac{\sigma a(1-e^{2})}{e}}-2\pi~.

(47)

Taking the approximation

\displaystyle\frac{K^{2}}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\ll 1~,

(48)

we finally have the orbital precession

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{K^{2}}{2p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}\quantity[1 - \frac{3 \epsilon K_1}{K(1-e^2)^2} \qty( \frac{2 p_\theta^2 c^2 }{K^2} + e^2 + 2)]~.

(49)

4.1
Comparison with Other Theories

We consider an example of Mercury’s orbit. In this case
K=GMm
, where
G
is Newton gravity constant,
M=1.989\times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}
is mass of Sun,
m=3.301\times 10^{23}\ \mathrm{kg}
is mass of Mercury. Its semi-major axis
a=57.91\times 10^{9}\mathrm{m}
and eccentricity
e=0.2056
. Since

\displaystyle\frac{mc^{2}}{GMm}\approx 10^{-3}>\frac{e}{a(1-e^{2})}\approx 10^{-8}~,

(50)

and
\eta\gg e
, then eq. (
20
) becomes approximately

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}{(GMm)^{2}}=\frac{c^{2}a(1-e^{2})}{GM}~.

(51)

Inserting (
51
) into (
49
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{GM}{2c^{2}a(1-e^{2})}-\frac{3\epsilon K_{1}}{2mc^{2}a(1-e^{2})^{3}}\quantity( \frac{2 c^2 a(1-e^2)}{GM} + e^2 +2)~.

(52)

Meanwhile, in general relativity the orbit precession of Mercury is given by
[
15
]

\displaystyle\delta_{GR}=\frac{3GM}{c^{2}a(1-e^{2})}=\epsilon\approx 10^{-7}.

(53)

In order to make a contact with observation, we have to set
\delta=\delta_{GR}
implying

\displaystyle K_{1}=-\frac{5a(1-e^{2})^{3}GMmc^{2}}{9\quantity(GM(2 + e^2) + 2 a c^2 (1-e^2))}~.

(54)

Another interesting gravitational theory is Randall-Sundrum model
[
16
]
. In this theory we have a five dimensional bulk spacetime with negative cosmological constant in which we embed two 3-branes (3+1 dimensional spacetimes) lying on the fifth coordinate with distance
r_{c}
where
r_{c}
is called compactification radius. After integrating out all massive Kaluza-Klein particles by taking
r_{c}
to be very large (compared to Planck length
\sim 10^{-35}
m), we finally obtain the modified Newtonian gravitational potential energy

\displaystyle V(r)=-\frac{K}{r}-\frac{\epsilon_{RS}K}{r^{3}}~,

(55)

where
\epsilon_{RS}\propto 1/k^{2}
is a dimensionless quantity and
k
is a warped factor on the bulk spacetime. After employing similar computation as above, we obtain the orbit precession of Mercury

\displaystyle\delta_{RS}

\displaystyle=\frac{GM}{2c^{2}a(1-e^{2})}+\frac{3\epsilon_{RS}GM}{2c^{2}a(1-e^{2})^{3}}\quantity( \frac{2 c^2 a(1-e^2)}{GM} + e^2 +2)~.

(56)

However, since the warped factor
k
is propotional to Planck energy in SI unit, that is,
k\approx 10^{8}
, so the last term in (
56
) is suppressed to zero. Thus, this Randall-Sundrum model is ruled out in this case.

At the end, it is worth mentioning that in Newtonian theory we could also use the potential energy (
33
). Using the same reason as above, we have to take

\displaystyle K_{1}=-\frac{a^{2}}{3}(1-e^{2})^{2}GMm~,

(57)

in order to have consistent model with observation.

4.2
Relevance with second Kepler’s law

It is well known that in the problem of planetary orbit, the motion should satisfy the second Kepler’s law. Let us consider a section area in the orbit plane
\differential A=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}\differential\theta
. Then, it can be shown that the rate of swept area is constant since the
\gamma
factor and the angular momentum
p_{\theta}
are constant. In other words,
\dot{A}
is constant, namely,

\displaystyle\dot{A}=\frac{p_{\theta}}{2\gamma m_{0}}=\frac{a(1-e)}{2m_{0}\sqrt{m_{0}^{2}c^{2}+p_{\theta}^{2}a^{2}(1-e)^{2}}}~.

(58)

The area of ellipse is
\pi\sqrt{1-e^{2}}a^{2}
. From (
58
), the Kepler’s law in this case has the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\differential t\ \dot{A}=\frac{p_{\theta}T}{2\gamma m_{0}}=n\pi\sqrt{1-e^{2}}a^{2}~,

(59)

where
T
is the period such that the orbit of a planet is closed and
n
is the number revolution of a planet forming closed orbit which has to be a positive integer as illustrated by the Figure
5
.

5
Conclusion

We have discussed the motion of a body under the influence of central force in the special theory of relativity. We particularly reviewed the motion under the force that is proportional to
1/r^{2}
where
r
is the radial coordinate in which it admits the precessing elliptical orbit. This orbital precession depends on the eccentricity and semi-major axis of the orbit. If the orbit is circular, then the orbital precession does not appear.

Then, we applied the above results to the case of the hydrogen-like atom. We used the Wilson-Sommerfeld quantization rule to obtain the energy level of the system so called Sommerfeld-Dirac fine structure formula which can also be derived from Dirac equation in relativistic quantum mechanics.

At the end, we have considered the planetary motion in which the potential energy is fine-tuned by adding the term which is proportional to
1/r^{3}
. We obtained the explicit orbital function and then, derived the precession of the planetary orbit. Since the pre-coefficient of the additional term in the potential energy is arbitrary, we could make a contact with the result from general relativity. However, in the Randall-Sundrum model this pre-coefficient is suppressed to zero implying that the model can be ruled out. Also, we pointed out the second Kepler’s laws which differs to the case of Newtonian mechanics.

Appendix A
Derivation of Constant of Motion

For a relativistic particle motion under the influence of central potential, the Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle L=-\frac{m_{0}c^{2}}{\gamma(v)}-V(r),\quad\gamma=\quantity(1 - \frac{\dot r^2}{c^2} - \frac{r^2 \dot\theta^2}{c^2})^{-1/2}.

(60)

The conjugate momenta correspond to
r,\theta
are

\displaystyle p_{r}=\gamma m_{0}\dot{r}~,

(61)

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=\gamma m_{0}r^{2}\dot{\theta}~,

(62)

respectively. From Euler-Lagrange equation of motions for
\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(\gamma m_0 r^2 \dot\theta)=0~,

(63)

where the quantity
\gamma m_{0}r^{2}\dot{\theta}
is the angular momentum
p_{\theta}
. From the above equation, it implies that
p_{\theta}
is constant.

Now we consider the total time derivative of Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\dot{L}=\partialderivative{L}{r}\dot{r}+\partialderivative{L}{\theta}\dot{\theta}+\partialderivative{L}{\dot r}\ddot{r}+\partialderivative{L}{\dot\theta}\ddot{\theta}+\partialderivative{L}{t}.

(64)

The last term vanish since the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on time. Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{L}{\dot q_i}\ddot{q}_{i}=\derivative{t}\quantity(\pdv{L}{\dot q_i}\dot q_i)-\derivative{t}\quantity(\pdv{L}{\dot q_i})\dot{q}_{i},\quad\text{where}\quad q_{i}=r,\theta~.

(65)

Then,

\displaystyle\dot{L}=\quantity[\pdv{L}{r} - \dv{t}\qty(\pdv{L}{\dot r})]\dot{r}+\quantity[\pdv{L}{\theta} - \dv{t}\qty(\pdv{L}{\dot\theta})]\dot{\theta}+\derivative{t}\quantity(\pdv{L}{\dot r}\dot r)+\derivative{t}\quantity(\pdv{L}{\dot\theta}\dot\theta)~.

(66)

From Euler-Lagrange equation of motions, the first and second terms vanish implying

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(\pdv{L}{\dot r}\dot r + \pdv{L}{\dot\theta}\dot\theta- L)=0~,

(67)

which is nothing but the energy function of the system. Thus, we conclude that the total energy of the system

\displaystyle E=\partialderivative{L}{\dot r}\dot{r}+\partialderivative{L}{\dot\theta}\dot{\theta}-L=\gamma(v)m_{0}c^{2}+V(r)~,

(68)

is also the constant of motion.

Appendix B
Orbit Equation

We begin by writing the Binet equation

\displaystyle\quantity(\dv[2]{\theta} + 1 - \frac{K^2}{p_\theta^2 c^2})u=\frac{KE}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}~.

(69)

The characteristic equation of the above differential equation for homogeneous case is

\displaystyle\rho^{2}+1-\frac{K^{2}}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}=0~,

(70)

whose solution is given by

\displaystyle\rho=\pm i\Omega,\quad\Omega=\sqrt{1-\frac{K^{2}}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}}}~.

(71)

Let
u_{0}
be the solution to the homogeneous equation

\displaystyle u_{0}=A\exp(i\Omega\theta)+B\exp(-i\Omega\theta)~,

(72)

whereas
u_{1}
is the particular solution. Since the rhs of the Binet equation is constant, then
u_{1}^{\prime\prime}=0
such that we have

\displaystyle u_{1}=\frac{KE}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}-K^{2}}~.

(73)

Finally, we can write the solution as

\displaystyle u(\theta)=u_{0}(\theta)+u_{1}=A\exp(i\Omega\theta)+B\exp(-i\Omega\theta)+\frac{KE}{p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}-K^{2}}~.

(74)

Appendix C
Derivation of Eccentricity

Let us first consider

\displaystyle\quantity(m_0 c^2)^{2}=\quantity(E + Ku)^{2}-u^{\prime 2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}-u^{2}p_{\theta}^{2}c^{2}~,

(75)

for
\theta=0
which implies

\displaystyle\quantity(m_0 c^2 a(1-e))^{2}=\quantity(Ea(1-e) + K)^{2}-\quantity(p_\theta c)^{2}~.

(76)

Defining
\alpha=a(1-e)
, we get

\displaystyle\quantity(m_0^2 c^4 - E^2)\alpha^{2}-2KE\alpha-\quantity(K^2 - p_\theta^2 c^2)=0~,

(77)

resulting

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{KE}{m_{0}^{2}c^{4}-E^{2}}\quantity[1 \pm\sqrt{\qty(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E})^2 + \qty(\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^2 - \qty(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E}\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^2}]~.

(78)

Since

\displaystyle a=\frac{K}{E}\quantity(\frac{p_\theta^2 c^2}{K^2} - 1)\frac{1}{1-e^{2}}~,

(79)

it gives then

\displaystyle\frac{K}{E}\quantity(\frac{p_\theta^2 c^2}{K^2} - 1)\frac{1}{1+e}=\frac{KE}{m_{0}^{2}c^{4}-E^{2}}\quantity[1 \pm\sqrt{\qty(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E})^2 + \qty(\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^2 - \qty(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E}\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^2}]~,

(80)

whose solution is

\displaystyle e=\sqrt{\beta}~,

(81)

where

\displaystyle\beta=\quantity(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E})^{2}+\quantity(\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^{2}-\quantity(\frac{m_0 c^2}{E}\frac{p_\theta c}{K})^{2}~.

(82)

Appendix D
Solving the angular momentum

Let
x=p_{\theta}c/K
, then we substitute (
79
) into (
82
) we have

\displaystyle(x^{2}-1)\quantity[(x^2 - e^2)(x^2 - 1) - \eta^2(1 - e^2)^2]=0~,

(83)

whose solutions are given by

\displaystyle x^{2}\in\quantity{1, \frac{1+e^2}{2}\qty[1 + \frac{1 - e^2}{1 + e^2}\sqrt{1 + 4\eta^2}], \frac{1+e^2}{2}\qty[1 - \frac{1 - e^2}{1 + e^2}\sqrt{1 + 4\eta^2}]}~,

Appendix E
Solving The Radial Momentum Quantization

We start from

\displaystyle n\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{u^{2}}\differential\theta=2\pi k~,

(85)

such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\quantity(n\frac{u'^2}{u^2} - k)\differential\theta=0~.

(86)

u^{\prime 2}/u^{2}=\kappa^{2}e\sin[2](\kappa\theta)/(1+e\cos(\kappa\theta))^{2}

\kappa^{2}=1-(\alpha^{2}/n^{2})

\displaystyle\frac{\quantity(n - (\alpha^2/n))e\sin[2](\kappa\theta)}{(1+e\cos(\kappa\theta))^{2}}=k~.

(87)

We use the above equation to solve the relation between
e
,
k
, and
n

\theta=\pi/2
, we have

\displaystyle\quantity(n - \frac{\alpha^2}{n})e=k~,

(88)

and for
\theta=\pi/4
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\quantity(n - \frac{\alpha^2}{n})e}{2(1+\quantity(e/\sqrt{2}))^{2}}=k~.

(89)
