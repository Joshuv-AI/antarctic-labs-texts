On vertices of frontals in the Euclidean plane
Nozomi Nakatsuyama, Masatomo Takahashi

Abstract
We investigate vertices for plane curves with singular points. As plane curves with singular points, we consider Legendre curves (respectively, Legendre immersions) in the unit tangent bundle over the Euclidean plane and frontals (respectively, fronts) in the Euclidean plane. We define a vertex using evolutes of frontals. After that we define a vertex of a frontal in the general case. It is also known that the four vertex theorem does not hold for simple closed fronts. We give conditions under which a frontal has a vertex and the four vertex theorem holds for closed frontals. We also give examples and counter examples of the four vertex theorem.

Introduction

The four vertex theorem is a classical result in differential geometry.
It states that there are four vertices for simple closed regular convex plane curves (cf.
[
20
]
).
In general, there are four vertices for simple closed regular plane curves (cf.
[
19
]
).
There are counter examples for non-simple closed regular plane curves.
See also
[
6
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

We investigate vertices for plane curves with singular points.
As smooth curves with singular points, we consider Legendre curves (respectively, Legendre immersions) in the unit tangent bundle over the Euclidean plane and frontals (respectively, fronts) in the Euclidean plane.
For basic results on singularity theory see
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
14
,
17
]
.
It is also known that the four vertex theorem does not hold for simple closed fronts (see, Example
2.19
).
In
[
8
]
, we gave conditions for a front to have at least four vertices.
In this paper, we give conditions under which a frontal has a vertex and the four vertex theorem holds for closed frontals.

In §2, we review the theories of regular curves, Legendre curves on the unit tangent bundle over the Euclidean plane and the evolutes of fronts.
In §3, using evolutes of frontals, we define the vertex as a singular point of the evolute and investigate the four vertex theorem of frontals.
We give conditions that frontal has at least four vertices using the curvature of the Legendre curve and the type of singular points of the Legendre curve.
Moreover, for a closed Legendre curve if inflection and singular points of the frontal are isolated points, and the frontal is a simple convex frontal, then the frontal has at least four vertices (Theorem
3.11
).
In §4 we define a vertex of a frontal in the general case.
We investigate properties of vertices of frontals.
For a closed Legendre immersion, if the front is a simple convex with at least two singular and two inflection points, then the front has at least four vertices (Theorem
4.12
).

We shall assume throughout the whole paper that all maps and manifolds are
C^{\infty}
unless the contrary is explicitly stated.

Preliminaries

We quickly review the theories of regular curves, Legendre curves on the unit tangent bundle over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the evolutes of fronts.

Let
I
be an interval or
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the Euclidean plane with the inner product
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=a_{1}b_{1}+a_{2}b_{2}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}=(a_{1},a_{2})
and
\mbox{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

2.1
Regular plane curves

Let
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a regular plane curve, that is,
\dot{\gamma}(t)=(d\gamma/dt)(t)\not=0
for all
t\in I
.
We have the unit tangent vector
\mbox{\boldmath$t$}(t)=\dot{\gamma}(t)/|\dot{\gamma}(t)|
and the unit normal vector
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}(t)=J(\mbox{\boldmath$t$}(t))
, where
J
is the anti-clockwise rotation by
\pi/2
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then we have the Frenet formula

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}(t)\\
\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$n$}}(t)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\kappa(t)\\
-|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\kappa(t)&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{\boldmath$t$}(t)\\
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}(t)\end{array}\right),

where the curvature
\kappa:I\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\kappa(t)=\frac{\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}(t)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$n$}(t)}{|\dot{\gamma}(t)|}=\frac{{\rm det}\left(\dot{\gamma}(t),\ddot{\gamma}(t)\right)}{|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{3}}.

Note that the curvature
\kappa(t)
is independent of the choice of a parametrization up to sign.

We say that
t_{0}\in I
is an
inflection point
of
\gamma
if
\kappa(t_{0})=0
and
a
vertex
of
\gamma
if
\dot{\kappa}(t_{0})=0
.
Then the classical four vertex theorem is as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(The four vertex theorem of regular curves,
[
19
,
20
]
)

If
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple convex regular curve, then
\gamma
has at least four vertices.
In general, if
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a simple closed regular curve, then
\gamma
has at least four vertices.

Suppose that
\gamma
does not have inflection points.
We define an
evolute

Ev(\gamma):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
\gamma
by

Ev(\gamma)(t)=\gamma(t)+\frac{1}{\kappa(t)}\mbox{\boldmath$n$}(t).

Then
t_{0}
is a singular point of
Ev(\gamma)
if and only if
t_{0}
is a vertex of
\gamma
.
It follows that
Ev(\gamma)
has at least four singular points for simple closed regular curves
\gamma
without inflection points (see Example
2.18
).
We investigate this phenomena for curves with singular points by using evolutes.

Remark 2.2

Suppose that
t_{0}
is a regular point and not an inflection point of
\gamma
.
It is well-known that if
t_{0}
is a vertex of
\gamma
, then
\gamma
and the osculating circle have at least third order contact at
t_{0}
(cf.
[
5
,
11
,
25
]
).

2.2
Legendre curves in the unit tangent bundle over the Euclidean plane

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a smooth mapping, where
S^{1}
is the unit circle.
We say that
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is
a Legendre curve
if

(\gamma,\nu)^{*}\theta=0
, where
\theta
is the canonical contact
1
-form on the unit tangent bundle
T_{1}\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
(cf.
[
1
,
2
]
).
This condition is equivalent to
\dot{\gamma}(t)\cdot\nu(t)=0
for all
t\in I
.
Moreover, if
(\gamma,\nu)
is an immersion, we call
(\gamma,\nu)

a Legendre immersion
.
We say that
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
a frontal
(respectively,
a front
or
a wave front
) if there exists a smooth mapping
\nu:I\to S^{1}
such that

(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre curve (respectively, a Legendre immersion).

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a Legendre curve.
We put on
\mu(t)=J(\nu(t))
and call the pair
\{\nu(t),\mu(t)\}
a
moving frame along the frontal
\gamma(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then we have the Frenet type formula of the Legendre curve which is given by

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\nu}(t)\\
\dot{\mu}(t)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\ell(t)\\
-\ell(t)&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\nu(t)\\
\mu(t)\end{array}\right),\ \dot{\gamma}(t)=\beta(t)\mu(t),

where
\ell(t)=\dot{\nu}(t)\cdot\mu(t),\beta(t)=\dot{\gamma}(t)\cdot\mu(t)
.
The pair
(\ell,\beta)
is an important invariant of Legendre curves.
We call the pair
(\ell,\beta)

the (Legendre) curvature of the Legendre curve
(cf.
[
7
]
).
Then
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is a Legendre immersion if and only if
(\ell(t),\beta(t))\not=(0,0)
for all
t\in I
.

We have the existence and the uniqueness for Legendre curves in the unit tangent bundle like as regular plane curves, see
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(The Existence Theorem for Legendre curves
[
7
]
)

Let
(\ell,\beta):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth mapping.
There exists a Legendre curve
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
whose associated curvature of the Legendre curve is
(\ell,\beta)
.

In fact, the Legendre curve whose associated curvature of the Legendre curve is
(\ell,\beta)
, is given by the form

\displaystyle\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\int\beta(t)\sin\left(\int\ell(t)dt\right)dt,\ \int\beta(t)\cos\left(\int\ell(t)dt\right)dt\right),

\displaystyle\nu(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\cos\left(\int\ell(t)dt\right),\ \sin\left(\int\ell(t)dt\right)\right).

Definition 2.4

Let
(\gamma,\nu)
and
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\nu}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be Legendre curves.
We say that
(\gamma,\nu)
and
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\nu})
are
congruent as Legendre curves
if there exist a constant rotation
A\in SO(2)
and a translation
a
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\widetilde{\gamma}(t)=A(\gamma(t))+\mbox{\boldmath$a$}
and
\widetilde{\nu}(t)=A(\nu(t))
for all
t\in I
.

Theorem 2.5

(The Uniqueness Theorem for Legendre curves
[
7
]
)

Let
(\gamma,\nu)
and
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\nu}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be Legendre curves with curvatures of Legendre curves
(\ell,\beta)
and
(\widetilde{\ell},\widetilde{\beta})
.
Then
(\gamma,\nu)
and
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\nu})
are congruent as Legendre curves if and only if
(\ell,\beta)
and
(\widetilde{\ell},\widetilde{\beta})
coincide.

Example 2.6

(Regular curves)

Let
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a regular curve.
If we consider
n
as a unit normal vector, then
(\gamma,\mbox{\boldmath$n$}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is a Legendre immersion with the curvature
(\ell,\beta)=(|\dot{\gamma}|\kappa,-|\dot{\gamma}|)
.
It follows that
\kappa(t)=-\ell(t)/\beta(t)
.
If we consider
-\mbox{\boldmath$n$}
as a unit normal vector, then
(\gamma,-\mbox{\boldmath$n$}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is also a Legendre immersion with the curvature
(\ell,\beta)=(|\dot{\gamma}|\kappa,|\dot{\gamma}|)
.
It follows that
\kappa(t)=\ell(t)/\beta(t)
(cf. Proposition
2.12
).

Example 2.7

(Type
(n,m)
)

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m=n+k
.
We consider a smooth map germ
(\gamma,\nu):(\mathbb{R},0)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
,

\displaystyle\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=(\pm t^{n},t^{m}f(t)),

\displaystyle\nu(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(mt^{k}f(t)+t^{k+1}\dot{f}(t))^{2}+n^{2}}}(-mt^{k}f(t)-t^{k+1}\dot{f}(t),\pm n),

where
f:(\mathbb{R},0)\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function germ with
f(0)\not=0
.
Note that
0
is a singular point of
\gamma
when
n>1
.
Then
(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre curve with curvature

\displaystyle\ell(t)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{nt^{k-1}(mkf(t)+(m+k+1)t\dot{f}(t)+t^{2}\ddot{f}(t))}{(mt^{k}f(t)+t^{k+1}\dot{f}(t))^{2}+n^{2}},

\displaystyle\beta(t)

\displaystyle=-t^{n-1}\sqrt{(mt^{k}f(t)+t^{k+1}\dot{f}(t))^{2}+n^{2}}.

We say
\gamma
is of type
(n,m)
.
If
n=1
, then
\gamma
is regular (a front) and
(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre immersion.
If
k=1
, then
\gamma
is a front and
(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre immersion.
Otherwise, that is, if
n,k>1
, then
\gamma
is a frontal and
(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre curve.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
7
]
)

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a Legendre curve with curvature
(\ell,\beta)
and

be a smooth function, where
\widetilde{I}
is an interval. Then
(\gamma\circ t,\nu\circ t):\widetilde{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}

We say that
t:\widetilde{I}\to I
is a parameter change if
t^{\prime}(u)\not=0
for all
u\in\widetilde{I}
.

Two map germs
f,g\colon(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{p},0)
are said to be
\mathcal{A}
-equivalent
if there exist diffeomorphism germs

\phi\colon(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)

and

\Phi\colon(\mathbb{R}^{p},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{p},0)

such that

f=\Phi\circ g\circ\phi
.
When
\Phi
is the identity in the above,

and

are said to be
\mathcal{R}
-equivalent.
A lot of works on the classification of germs with the above equivalences have been done in the context of singularity theory.
See
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
17
]
for basic idea of singularity theory.
The
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{R}
-equivalent class of mappings or functions
are worth studying from the viewpoint of differential topology.

A one-variable function
f:(I,t_{0})\to(\mathbb{R},0)
has type
A_{k}
at
t_{0}\in I
if
f^{(i)}(t_{0})=0
for
i=1,\dots,k
and
f^{(k+1)}(t_{0})\not=0
.
Then

is
\mathcal{R}
-equivalent to
\pm t^{k+1}
(cf.

]
).

Proposition 2.10

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a Legendre curve with curvature
(\ell,\beta)
and

\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\Phi(x,y)=(\phi_{1}(x,y),\phi_{2}(x,y))
be a diffeomorphism.
We denote
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t)),\nu(t)=(a(t),b(t))
and
\widetilde{\gamma}=\Phi\circ\gamma
.
Then
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\nu}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is a Legendre curve, where
\widetilde{\nu}=\overline{\nu}/|\overline{\nu}|
,

\overline{\nu}(t)=(\phi_{2y}(\gamma(t))a(t)-\phi_{2x}(\gamma(t))b(t),-\phi_{1y}(\gamma(t))a(t)+\phi_{1x}(\gamma(t))b(t)).

The curvature
(\widetilde{\ell},\widetilde{\beta})
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\overline{\nu}(t)|^{2}}\Bigl(\bigl((\phi_{2xx}(\gamma(t))b^{2}(t)-2\phi_{2xy}(\gamma(t))a(t)b(t)+\phi_{2yy}(\gamma(t))a^{2}(t))

\displaystyle\qquad(-\phi_{1x}(\gamma(t))b(t)+\phi_{1y}(\gamma(t))a(t))

\displaystyle\quad-(\phi_{1xx}(\gamma(t))b^{2}(t)-2\phi_{1xy}(\gamma(t))a(t)b(t)+\phi_{1yy}(\gamma(t))a^{2}(t))

\displaystyle\qquad(-\phi_{2x}(\gamma(t))b(t)+\phi_{2y}(\gamma(t))a(t))\bigr)\beta(t)

\displaystyle\quad+(\phi_{1x}(\gamma(t))\phi_{2y}(\gamma(t))-\phi_{2x}(\gamma(t))\phi_{1y}(\gamma(t)))\ell(t)\Bigr),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=|\overline{\nu}(t)|\beta(t).

Proof.
Since
\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t)=(\phi_{1x}(\gamma(t))\dot{x}(t)+\phi_{1y}(\gamma(t))\dot{y}(t),\phi_{2x}(\gamma(t))\dot{x}(t)+\phi_{2y}(\gamma(t))\dot{y}(t))
,
we have
\dot{\widetilde{\gamma}}(t)\cdot\overline{\nu}(t)=0
for all

, where

\overline{\nu}(t)=(\phi_{2y}(\gamma(t))a(t)-\phi_{2x}(\gamma(t))b(t),-\phi_{1y}(\gamma(t))a(t)+\phi_{1x}(\gamma(t))b(t)).

Moreover, since

is a diffeomorphism,

for all

.
It follows that

is a Legendre curve, where
\widetilde{\nu}=\overline{\nu}/|\overline{\nu}|
.
By a direct calculation, we have the curvature

of

Proposition 2.12

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}

Then
(\gamma,-\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a Legendre curve with curvature
(\ell,\beta)
.
We say that a point

is an

of the frontal
\gamma
(or, the Legendre curve

) if

.

We define a

\gamma^{\lambda}:I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

Proposition 2.13

(
[
9
]
)

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}

, we say that a Legendre curve

.

We define a convex frontal in the Euclidean plane.
From now on,
I
is a closed interval. Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a Legendre curve.
We denote the tangent line at

of
\gamma
by

, that is,
L_{t}=\{\lambda\mu(t)+\gamma(t)\ |\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
Any tangent line

divides
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into two half-planes
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
such
that
H_{+}\cup H_{-}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
H_{+}\cap H_{-}=L_{t}
.
By using
\nu
, the half-planes
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
are presented by

H_{+}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ (\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\gamma(t))\cdot\nu(t)\geq 0\}
and

H_{-}=\{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ (\mbox{\boldmath$x$}-\gamma(t))\cdot\nu(t)\leq 0\}
.
For a Legendre curve
(\gamma,\nu):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
,
we say that
(\gamma,\nu)
is a

(or,
\gamma
is a

) if
\gamma(I)\subset H_{+}
for any tangent line of
\gamma
or
\gamma(I)\subset H_{-}
for any tangent line of
\gamma
.
Note that if
\gamma
is a regular curve, then
\mu(t)
is equal to the unit
tangent vector of
\gamma
at
\gamma(t)
up to sign. Therefore,
\gamma
is a convex curve as a frontal if and only if

is a convex curve as the usual mean when

is a regular curve (cf.
[
13
]
).

By definition, convexity of a Legendre curve is preserved under a congruent as Legendre curves. Moreover, if
(\gamma,\nu):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
is a convex Legendre curve, then

is also

and
any smooth function

Example 4.10

Let
(\gamma,\nu):[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be

\displaystyle\gamma(t)=\left(\frac{1}{5}\cos^{5}t,\frac{1}{5}\sin^{5}t\right),\ \nu(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t}}\left(-\sin^{3}t,-\cos^{3}t\right).

By a direct calculation,
(\gamma,\nu)
is a closed Legendre curve with the curvature

\displaystyle\ell(t)=-\frac{3\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t}{\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t},\ \beta(t)=-\cos t\sin t\sqrt{\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t}.

If
k_{1}(t)=-(\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t)^{\frac{3}{2}},\ k_{2}(t)=3\cos t\sin t
, then
(k_{1},k_{2})
is a non-zero smooth function and
k_{1}(t)\ell(t)+k_{2}(t)\beta(t)=0
for all
t\in I
. By a direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{1}(t)=9\cos 2t\cos t\sin t\sqrt{\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t},\ \dot{k}_{2}(t)=3\cos 2t.

Since

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{1}(t)k_{2}(t)-k_{1}(t)\dot{k}_{2}(t)=3\cos 2t\sqrt{\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t}(9\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+\cos^{6}t+\sin^{6}t),

\dot{k}_{1}(t)k_{2}(t)-k_{1}(t)\dot{k}_{2}(t)=0
if and only if
\cos 2t=0
.
It follows that
t=\pi/4,3\pi/4,5\pi/4,7\pi/4
are vertices of
\gamma
.
Therefore,
\gamma
has four vertices.
Note that the singular points of
\gamma
are
5/2
-cusps at
t=0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2
.

Example 4.11

Let
(\gamma,\nu):[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be

\displaystyle\gamma(t)=\left(\cos t,\frac{1}{3}\sin^{3}t\right),\ \nu(t)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1}}\left(\cos t\sin t,1\right).

By a direct calculation,
(\gamma,\nu)
is a closed Legendre curve with the curvature

\displaystyle\ell(t)=-\frac{\cos 2t}{\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1},\ \beta(t)=-\sin t\sqrt{\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1}.

Note that
(\gamma,\nu)
is a closed Legendre immersion, since
(\ell(t),\beta(t))\neq(0,0)
for all
t\in[0,2\pi]
.
By Theorem
2.14
,
\gamma
is not a convex front.
Moreover,
t=\pi/4,3\pi/4,5\pi/4,7\pi/4
are inflection points of
\gamma
.
If
k_{1}(t)=-\sin t(\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1)^{\frac{3}{2}},\ k_{2}(t)=\cos 2t
, then
(k_{1},k_{2})
is a non-zero smooth function and
k_{1}(t)\ell(t)+k_{2}(t)\beta(t)=0
for all
t\in I
. By a direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{1}(t)=-\cos t(\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1+3\sin^{2}t\cos 2t)\sqrt{\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1},\dot{k}_{2}(t)=-2\sin 2t.

Since

\displaystyle\dot{k}_{1}(t)k_{2}(t)-k_{1}(t)\dot{k}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cos t\sqrt{\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1}(\cos 2t(\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1+3\sin^{2}t\cos 2t)+4\sin^{2}t(\cos^{2}t\sin^{2}t+1)),

\dot{k}_{1}(t)k_{2}(t)-k_{1}(t)\dot{k}_{2}(t)=0
if and only if
\cos t=0
.
It follows that
\gamma
has only two vertices.
Therefore, it does not satisfy on the four vertex theorem.
Note that the singular points of
\gamma
are
3/2
-cusps at
t=0,\pi
.

Theorem 4.12

Let
(\gamma,\nu):I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}
be a closed Legendre immersion with curvature
(\ell,\beta)
.
If
\gamma
is a simple convex front with at least two singular and two inflection points, then
\gamma
has at least four vertices.

Proof.
Since
(\gamma,\nu)
is a Legendre immersion,
(\ell(t),\beta(t))\not=(0,0)
for all
t\in I
.
If
k_{1}(t)=-\beta(t),k_{2}(t)=\ell(t)
, then
(k_{1},k_{2})
is a non-zero smooth function and
k_{1}(t)\ell(t)+k_{2}(t)\beta(t)=0
for all
t\in I
.
Then we have
\dot{k}_{1}(t)k_{2}(t)-k_{1}(t)\dot{k}_{2}(t)=-\dot{\beta}(t)\ell(t)+\beta(t)\dot{\ell}(t)
.
If
\ell(t_{0})=0
and
\dot{\ell}(t_{0})\not=0
, then
\beta(t_{0})\not=0
and hence
t_{0}
is an ordinary inflection point (cf.
[
4
,
11
]
).
However, it contradicts the fact that
\gamma
is convex.
Hence, if
\ell(t_{0})=0
, then
\dot{\ell}(t_{0})=0
.
It follows that
t_{0}
is a vertex of
\gamma
.
Moreover, if
\beta(t_{0})=0
and
\dot{\beta}(t_{0})\not=0
, then
\ell(t_{0})\not=0
and hence
\gamma
has the point
t_{0}
of type
(2,3)
.
That is, an ordinary cusp (
3/2
-cusp) at
t_{0}
(cf.
[
8
]
).
However, it also contradicts the fact that
\gamma
is convex.
Hence, if
\beta(t_{0})=0
, then
\dot{\beta}(t_{0})=0
.
It follows that
t_{0}
is a vertex of
\gamma
.
Therefore, if
\gamma
is a simple convex front with at least two singular and two inflection points, then
\gamma
has at least four vertices.

\Box

We give a problem asking how about simple closed frontals with inflection points.
That is, does the four vertex theorem hold for a simple closed (convex) frontal with inflection points?
