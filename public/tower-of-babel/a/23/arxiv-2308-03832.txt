EFT corrections to scalar and vector quasinormal modes of rapidly rotating black holes
Filipe S. Miguel

Abstract
Quasinormal modes characterize the final stage of a black hole merger. In this regime, spacetime curvature is high, these modes can be used to probe potential corrections to general relativity. In this paper, we utilize the effective field theory framework to compute the leading order correction to massless scalar and electromagnetic quasinormal modes. Proceeding perturbatively in the size of the effective field theory length scale, we describe a general method to compute the frequencies for Kerr black holes of any spin. In the electromagnetic case, we study both parity even and parity odd effective field theory corrections, and, surprisingly, prove that the two have the same spectrum. Furthermore, we find that, the corrected frequencies separate into two families, corresponding to the two polarizations of light. The corrections pertaining to each family are equal and opposite. Our results are validated through several consistency checks.

Introduction

Studying
BH
(
BH
) spacetimes has proven to be a very fruitful endeavor in understanding the merits and limitations of Einstein’s theory of
GR
(
GR
). These powerful astrophysical objects cause spacetime to heavily curve around them, and thus, we expect possible deviations from
GR
to be noticeable there. The no-hair theorems imply that the mathematical description of these objects is remarkably simple. In a vacuum, asymptotically flat spacetime, all stationary
BH
solutions are described by the two-parameter Kerr family of metrics. The mass and angular momentum
\left(M,a\right)
of the
BH
are sufficient to specify these highly symmetric solutions of the Einstein equations. In the limit
a\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the only static, spherically symmetric solution of the
EOM
, known as the Schwarzschild solution. However, real-world scenarios are dynamic, and thus, solutions will necessarily differ from the stationary ones discussed above.

The observation of
GW
by the LIGO/VIRGO experiment offers a key opportunity to experimentally test the dynamical evolution of
BH
spacetimes. In particular, by observing the collision between two
BH
, we can understand how generic
BH
solutions evolve into the stationary Kerr spacetime. The collision of two
BH
is characterized by three phases. Initially, during the inspiral phase, the
BH
orbit each other, with motion that can be described using Newtonian physics. Then, during the merger phase, the
BH
effectively collide and form a single
BH
. This phase is challenging to study analytically, and a numerical relativity approach must be adopted. Finally, during the
BH
ringdown, the final
BH
decays into stationarity, ’vibrating’ around a Kerr solution. This phase can be modelled by understanding linear perturbations of the
EOM
.

The study of the linear perturbations of Schwarzschild was pioneered in
Regge and Wheeler 1957
, where the authors derived the Regge-Wheeler equation, which describes odd parity (axial) perturbations of a Schwarzschild spacetime. This work was complemented in
Zerilli 1970
with the Zerilli equation, describing even parity (polar) perturbations of Schwarzschild. Concurrently, Teukolsky demonstrated that in a Kerr background, most fields with linear
EOM
are described by two coupled wave-like
ODE

Teukolsky 1973
. The Schwarzschild limit of this is known as the Bardeen-Press equation
Bardeen and Press 1973
, which does not coincide with the Zerilli or Regge-Wheeler equation. It was later proved that all three equations are related to each other by ordinary and generalized Darboux transformations
Chandrasekhar and Detweiler 1975
;
Glampedakis et al. 2017
. This implies that the polar and axial
EOM
have the same spectrum, a conclusion that does not necessarily generalize to all physical systems, as we will discuss later. Therefore, conveniently, characterising solutions to the Teukolsky equation is sufficient to understand the behaviour of most linear fields in a Kerr/Schwarzschild background.

Solving these equations in general is no simple task, however, similar to quantum mechanical systems, we can decompose general solutions into a set of normal modes and frequencies - the eigenvector/eigenvalue pairs of the system.
BH
are, by definition, absorbent objects: the energy of a field in a
BH
background is gradually dissipated into its interior. Vishveshwara
Vishveshwara 1970
was the first to understand that this implies the frequencies must be complex, with the imaginary part controlling the damping of the fields. Thus, these are
QNM
as was coined in
Press 1971
. See
Nollert 1999
;
Kokkotas and Schmidt 1999
;
Berti et al. 2009
;
Konoplya and Zhidenko 2011
for a review.

Obtaining the values of the
QNM
frequencies is a non-trivial problem. Analytically, we can use a WKB approach to approximate the values of large
QNM
frequencies. In fact, it can be demonstrated that the frequency and damping of these
QNM
are related to the frequency and Lyapunov exponent of circular null geodesics around the
BH
, as seen in
Mashhoon 1985
;
Dolan 2010
;
Yang et al. 2012
. This connection will be vital to make progress in section
IV.2.1
. However, to achieve accurate values, we must resort to a numerical approach. Following a strategy to compute the spectrum of the molecular hydrogen ion, Leaver devised the first method that yields accurate results across most of the Kerr parameter space
Leaver 1985
. However, the approach loses accuracy in the extremal limit (
a\rightarrow M
). Modern approaches project the
EOM
onto a discrete grid, finding the
QNM
spectrum with a pseudospectral approach, as detailed in
Dias et al. 2010
;
Miguel 2021
;
Dias et al. 2016
;
Trefethen 2000
;
Boyd 2000
.

Since
QNM
frequencies are complex-valued, the
EOM
cannot be self-adjoint. Additional complexity arises from the fact that, in general, the
QNM
are not a complete basis for the solutions of the
EOM
; for an arbitrary linear perturbation, there will be a residual in the form of a polynomial tail. Nevertheless, we expect that most of the ringdown waveform can be described with
QNM
contributions, see
Baibhav et al. 2023
for details. By fitting the data to a finite sum of the slowest decaying modes, we can obtain the values of the first few
QNM
frequencies. This is known in the literature as
BH
spectroscopy. In a Kerr background, the frequencies are fully specified by the mass and spin of the
BH
. Theoretically, by extracting the real and imaginary parts of a single
QNM
with sufficient precision, we have enough data to fully specify the parameters of the
BH
. Conversely, by detecting two or more modes, we can test
GR
as a theory
Dreyer et al. 2004
. If the detected spectrum is inconsistent with the predictions from
GR
, we need to consider corrections to
GR
. Modern approaches use the
GW
from the inspiral and merger phases to determine the
BH
parameters, and then check their consistency with the ringdown spectrum
Brito et al. 2018
;
Ghosh et al. 2021
;
Silva et al. 2022
.

To validate
GR
as a theory, we need a concrete framework to parametrize possible deviations. If we knew the full form of the true theory of quantum gravity, we could make physical predictions at low energy scales by
integrating out
the high energy degrees of freedom. This process maps the Lagrangian into an infinite sum of low-energy interactions. Using dimensional analysis, we know that each spacetime derivative must be preceded by some length scale
L
. Thus, we can organize the sum in ascending order of powers of
L
, its scaling dimension, see
Weinberg 2013
;
Burgess 2004
;
Burgess 2007
. Truncating the sum at some order we obtain an
EFT
(
EFT
) description of the system. The approximation is valid as long as
L
is the smallest length scale of the theory. To consider values of
L
closer to the spacetime length scale, we must include more terms in the sum.
Reall and Warnick 2022
provides compelling evidence that at a classical level, the difference between the solutions to
EFT
and UV
EOM
is dominated by the scaling dimension of the next term in the series. Remarkably, making simple assumptions about the theory (e.g., gauge invariance and locality), the form of the interactions becomes heavily constrained. Thus, up to a given accuracy, the space of
EFT
is fully parametrized by a set of finitely many coupling constants. This suggests a bottom-up approach. We include in our Lagrangian all the physically reasonable terms up to a given order, extract physical predictions, and use experiments to constrain the coupling constant values. Then, we eliminate all UV theories that lead to coupling parameters that violate these constraints. Thus, even if we do not observe any deviations from
GR
, we can constrain the UV theory space.

Calculating the impact of gravitational
EFT
corrections on

BH
solution of the uncorrected
GR

EOM
.
QNM
are encoded in dynamical perturbations of this background, i.e.

will correct both the stationary background metric and the

EFT
correction to the background metric and pure corrections to the linearised

equations. In the Schwarzschild limit,
Cardoso et al. 2018
computed the correction to the background spacetime, and corresponding correction to gravitational
QNM
. More recently, Cano et al. studied the corrections to Kerr
QNM
. In
Cano et al. 2020
, they obtained the correction to the background metric for
BH
with
a\lesssim 0.7M
, and used the result to obtain the shifts in the

of a massless scalar field. Then, in
Cano et al. 2022
;
Cano et al. 2023a
;
Cano et al. 2023b
, the same group obtained the corrections to gravitational
QNM
of
not
rapidly rotating Kerr
BH
, under parity even and parity odd
EFT
terms. Crucially, all of the results are perturbative in
a/M
(where
a
is the
BH
spin parameter), with the method breaking down for rapidly rotating
BH
. In fact, in
Cano et al. 2023a
, the authors argue that the method breaks down when considering

with
a\gtrsim 0.7M
. Astrophysical

can have

much closer to
1
, thus, it is essential to develop an approach that is non-perturbative in

. This is fundamentally more difficult, because the

cannot be separated into radial and angular

, the

are eigenvalues of two dimensional

. Throughout this paper, we develop a framework to perform this calculation by studying similar, albeit simpler, theories. This is in preparation for future work that will generalize the method to the gravitational case. We will study leading order

II.2
The GHP formalism

The
GHP
(
GHP
) formalism
Geroch et al. 1973
is a more covariant version of the
NP
(
NP
) approach
Newman and Penrose 1962
;

As in the
NP

II.4
Quasinormal Modes

QNM
are the natural basis to encode the late time behaviour of linear fields in a
BH
spacetime. Because the system is naturally dissipative, the corresponding frequencies are complex valued. To formally define these modes, the standard approach is to introduce hyperboloidal coordinates in a conformally compactified version of spacetime.
QNM
are solutions of a given wave equation, with defined frequency, that are regular at
\mathcal{H}^{+}
and
\mathcal{J}^{+}
, see
Ansorg and Panosso Macedo 2016
;
Panosso Macedo 2020
;
Ripley 2022
. Physically, this is the statement that
QNM
are waves that are ingoing through
\mathcal{H}^{+}
and outgoing at
\mathcal{J}^{+}
. For simplicity, in this paper, we will work with a constant
t

BC
for the radial function that encode the appropriate ingoing / outgoing behaviour of the full solution. We will specialize in
QNM
solutions of the Teukolsky equation, (see (
43
)).

A Frobenius analysis of the radial
EOM
near
r=r_{+}
shows that the radial function can be decomposed as the sum of an ingoing and an outgoing mode

;
Leaver 1985
:

R_{s}(r)=\mathcal{A}(\omega)R_{\text{out},r_{+}}(r)+\mathcal{B}(\omega)R_{\text{in},r_{+}}(r)\,,

(52)

where:

\displaystyle R_{\text{out},r_{+}}

\displaystyle=\left(r-r_{+}\right)^{i\rho}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(r-r_{+})^{-1}\right)\,,

(53)

\displaystyle R_{\text{in},r_{+}}

\displaystyle=\left(r-r_{+}\right)^{-s-i\rho}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(r-r_{+})^{-1}\right)\,,

with:

\rho:=\frac{\omega-m\,\Omega_{+}}{2\,\kappa}\,.

(54)

Similarly, an asymptotic expansion near
r\rightarrow\infty
yields a decomposition into an ingoing and an outgoing mode:

R_{s}(r)=\mathcal{C}(\omega)R_{\text{out},+\infty}(r)+\mathcal{D}(\omega)R_{\text{in},+\infty}(r)\,,

(55)

where:

\displaystyle R_{\text{out},+\infty}(r)

\displaystyle=r^{-1-2s+2iM\omega}e^{i\omega r}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{-1}\right)\right)\,,

(56)

\displaystyle R_{\text{in},+\infty}(r)

\displaystyle=r^{-1-2iM\omega}e^{-i\omega r}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{-1}\right)\right)\,.

are defined as solutions of the Teukolsky equation with
\mathcal{A}(\omega)=\mathcal{D}(\omega)=0
. This defines a
BVP
(
BVP
) with eigenfunction

and eigenvalue
\omega
.

(57)

where

\alpha_{y,s}(y(r)):=e^{ir\omega}r^{4iM\omega}\Delta_{r}^{-s-iM\omega}\left(\frac{r-r_{-}}{r-r_{+}}\right)^{\frac{i\left(\frac{M}{r_{+}}\omega-m\Omega\right)}{2\kappa}}\,.

50

Denote

the parity transformation around

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}l^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=l^{a}\partial_{a},

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}n^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=n^{a}\partial_{a},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}m^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=-\bar{m}^{a}\partial_{a},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bar{m}^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle=-m^{a}\partial_{a}.

The vector potential

and
\bar{m}

and
\bar{m}\rightarrow m

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}\eta:=\overline{\mathbb{P}\eta}\,.

(66)

As an example, consider the action in the Teukolsky field
\psi^{(}s)
. Using equation (
45

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\psi^{(s)}(t,r,x,\phi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}S(t,\phi)\,\Psi_{s}(r,x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\bar{S}(t,\phi)\bar{\Psi}_{s}(r,-x)\,,

where the
(-1)^{m}

.

The transformation rule in equation (

\eta
is constructed by taking covariant derivatives and tensor products of tetrad quantities, we have:

\mathcal{P}\eta=(-1)^{s}\eta\,,

(68)

where
s
is the number of
m^{a}
and
\bar{m}^{a}
instances in the definition of
\eta
. As an example, take
\rho
and
\tau
. Using equation (
25
), we have:

\mathcal{P}\rho=(-1)^{2}\rho\qquad\mathcal{P}\tau=(-1)^{1}\tau\,.

(69)

By definition,
\eta
is a
GHP
covariant quantity. Thus,
s
is simply its spin weight:
s=(p-q)/2
.

Now, consider
\mathcal{G}
, a differential operator constructed with tensor products and covariant derivatives of tetrad quantities, acting on some scalar
f
. Following the same reasoning, we have:

\mathcal{P}\,\left(\mathcal{G}\,f\right)=(-1)^{s}\mathcal{G}\,\mathcal{P}\,f\,.

(70)

where
s
is the spin-weight of
\mathcal{G}
. Because Kerr is invariant under parity, this rule also extends to the case where
\mathcal{G}
contains pure gravitational scalars, e.g.
\psi_{2}
.

In particular, the Teukolsky operator
\mathcal{O}_{s}
has spin-weight
0
, thus:

\left[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O}_{s}\right]=0,

(71)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the commutator operator. Standard results tell us that if
\psi^{(s)}
is in the kernel of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
, then so is
\mathcal{P}\psi^{(s)}
. Using equation (
45
), we have that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{s}\,S\,\Psi_{s}=0\implies

(72)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{s}\,(-1)^{m}\bar{S}\,\mathbb{P}\bar{\Psi}_{s}=0\,.

This proves that if
\left(m,\omega\right)
is in the
QNM
spectrum of
\mathcal{O}_{s}
, then so is
\left(-m,-\bar{\omega}\right)
. The parity invariance of the Kerr metric leads to a
degeneracy
between these two families of
QNM
.

The action of
\mathbb{P}
on the Kinnersley tetrad, see (
65
), does not generalize to all the equivalent tetrads. In general, if we generate a new null tetrad with a
GHP
transformation, see (
21
), the identities in (
65
) are no longer respected. To circumvent this, we can restrict the allowed
GHP
transformations, such that the resulting tetrads obey (
65
). Under a generic
GHP
transformation, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}l^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda\bar{\lambda}\right)\mathbb{P}l^{a}\partial_{a},

(73)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}n^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda\bar{\lambda}}\right)\mathbb{P}n^{a}\partial_{a},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}m^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)\mathbb{P}m^{a}\partial_{a},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bar{m}^{a}\partial_{a}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{\lambda}\right)\mathbb{P}\bar{m}^{a}\partial_{a}.

Thus, to preserve (
65
), we must have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\lambda\bar{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\lambda\bar{\lambda}\,,

(74)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{\lambda}\,.

The absolute value and phase of
\lambda
must be parity even and odd respectively. Take
\eta
to be a
GHP
scalar with boost weight
r
and spin weight
s
. Restricting to
GHP
transformations that respect (
74
), we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\eta

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\lambda\bar{\lambda}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)^{-s}\mathbb{P}\eta\,,

(75)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\eta

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left(\lambda\bar{\lambda}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)^{s}\mathcal{P}\eta\,.

The action
\mathbb{P}
is akin to complex conjugation. It takes a
\{p,q\}

GHP
scalar to a
\{q,p\}
scalar. Consequently,
\mathcal{P}
preserves the
GHP
weight. This fact is particularly useful, as it allows us to take linear combinations of
\eta
and
\mathcal{P}\eta
without breaking
GHP
covariance.

II.6
Hodge duals and p forms

In this section, we will review some key concepts regarding
p
forms, and their hodge duals. The hodge dual operator,
\star
, is a linear map from the space
p
forms to the space of
d-p
forms. We will follow the convention:

\left(\star\omega\right)_{a_{1}\cdots a_{d-p}}=\frac{1}{p!}\varepsilon_{a_{1}\cdots a_{d-p}b_{1}\cdots b_{p}}\omega^{b_{1}\cdots b_{p}}\,,

(76)

where
\varepsilon
is the Levi-Civita tensor. We have
\varepsilon_{a_{1}\cdots a_{d}}=\varepsilon_{[a_{1}\cdots a_{d}]}
and
\varepsilon_{123\cdots d}=\sqrt{-g}
. For a Lorentzian metric, we have:

\star\star\omega=-(-1)^{p\left(d-p\right)}\omega\,.

(77)

Finally, we can define the differential and co-differential operators:

\displaystyle\left(\differential\omega\right)_{a_{1}\cdots a_{p+1}}

\displaystyle:=(p+1)\nabla_{[a_{1}}\omega_{a_{2}\cdots a_{p+1}]}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle\left(\differential^{\dagger}\omega\right)_{a_{1}\cdots a_{p-1}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\star\differential\star\omega\right)_{a_{1}\cdots a_{p-1}}

\displaystyle=-(-1)^{p\left(d-p\right)}\nabla^{b}\omega_{a_{1}\cdots a_{p-1}b}\,.

The Weyl tensor is not fully antisymmetric, and thus cannot be regarded as a
4
form. However, it is antisymmetric in the first two and the last two indices, and so, we can take their dual. In 4-dimensions, we define the left and right dual of
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\left(\star\mathcal{C}\right)_{abcd}=\frac{1}{2!}\varepsilon_{abef}\mathcal{C}^{ef}{}_{cd}\,,

(79)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}\star\right)_{abcd}=\frac{1}{2!}\mathcal{C}_{ab}{}^{ef}\varepsilon_{efcd}\,.

Now, taking the left and right dual at the same time, we get:

\displaystyle\left(\star\mathcal{C}\star\right)^{ab}{}_{cd}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{abef}\varepsilon_{cdgh}\mathcal{C}_{ef}{}^{gh}

(80)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4!}{4}\delta^{a}_{[c}\delta^{b}_{d}\delta^{e}_{g}\delta^{f}_{h]}\,\mathcal{C}_{ef}{}^{gh}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4!}{4\cdot 3!}\delta^{e}_{[c}\delta^{f}_{d]}\delta^{a}_{[g}\delta^{b}_{h]}\,\mathcal{C}_{ef}{}^{gh}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{C}^{ab}{}_{cd}\,.

In the second step we used the standard result for the contraction of two instances of
\varepsilon
, and in the the third step, we used the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is traceless, i.e.
\mathcal{C}^{c}{}_{acb}=0
. Using
\star^{2}=-1
(for a 2 form in 4 dimensions), we get:

\star\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\star\,,

(81)

The left and right duals are equivalent.

There are multiple ways to fix the degeneracy between the two electromagnetic / gravitational degrees of freedom. Usually, it is helpful to decompose the relevant quantities in a basis of eigenstates of a given operator. If we pick that operator to be
\star
, we decompose the electromagnetic and Weyl tensor into their
self-dual
and
anti-self-dual
parts:

\displaystyle F^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(F\mp i\star F\right)\,,

(82)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{C}\mp i\star\mathcal{C}\right)\,,

As intended,
F^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
are
\star
eigenstates:

\star F^{\pm}=\pm iF^{\pm}\,,\qquad\star\mathcal{C}^{\pm}=\pm i\mathcal{C}^{\pm}\,.

(83)

When projected to the Kinnersley tetrad (
40
), the Levi-Civita tensor takes a simple form:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{(\alpha)(\beta)(\gamma)(\delta)}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{abcd}e^{a}_{(\alpha)}e^{b}_{(\beta)}e^{c}_{(\gamma)}e^{d}_{(\delta)}

(84)

\displaystyle=-i\,E_{(\alpha)(\beta)(\gamma)(\delta)}

where
E
is fully antisymmetric and
E_{1234}
. Thus, we get
\varepsilon_{(1)(2)(3)(4)}=-i
. Dualized quantities, carry an extra factor of
i
, and thus, complex conjugation will add an extra
-
contribution. Concretely, consider the
dualized
GB
term
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=\left(\star\mathcal{C}\right)^{abcd}\mathcal{C}_{abcd}

(85)

\displaystyle=24i\left(\bar{\psi}_{2}^{2}-\psi_{2}^{2}\right)\,.

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
can be understood as a product of
i
and a spin weight
0

GHP
scalar that obeys the transformation rules detailed in section
II.5
. Thus, under the
conjugate-parity-transform
, we have:

\mathcal{P}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\,.

(86)

As a rule of thumb, quantities that are dualized an odd amount of times are parity odd.

II.7
The vacuum Maxwell equations

In the
GHP
formalism, we represent the electromagnetic field with 3 complex scalar fields. However, we know that electromagnetism is fully specified by two independent polarizations. Thus, two of the
GHP
scalars must be redundant. In
Wald 1978
, Wald derived an approach to fully reconstruct the electromagnetic tensor
F_{ab}
from a single complex scalar. This is commonly known in the literature as the Hertz potential approach. In this section, we will start by deriving the Teukolsky equation for
\phi_{0}
, and then explain how to generate
F_{ab}
from it.

The vacuum Maxwell equations take the form:

\displaystyle\nabla^{a}F_{ab}=0,

(87)

\displaystyle\differential F=0.

Here, the second equation, commonly known as the Bianchi identity, implies that
F_{ab}
must be a closed
2
form. Because
F
is a real field, we can use the results in section
II.6
, to combine the two equations into a single complex equation:

M_{a}:=i\left(\star\differential F^{-}\right)_{a}=0\,,

(88)

where
F^{-}
was defined in (
82
). The, first and second equations in (
87
), are encoded by the real and imaginary parts of (
88
) respectively. In the tetrad basis, using the
GHP
formalism,
M
takes a simple form
Geroch et al. 1973
:

M_{(\alpha)}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\eth^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime}\right)\phi_{0}-\left(\text{\thorn}-2\rho\right)\phi_{1}\\
\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}-2\rho^{\prime}\right)\phi_{1}-\left(\eth-\tau\right)\phi_{2}\\
\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime}\right)\phi_{0}-\left(\eth-2\tau\right)\phi_{1}\\
\left(\eth^{\prime}-2\tau^{\prime}\right)\phi_{1}-\left(\text{\thorn}-\rho\right)\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}_{(\alpha)}\,.

(89)

The corresponding
GHP
weights are:

\{1,1\},\quad\{-1,-1\},\quad\{1,-1\},\quad\{-1,1\}\,.

(90)

If we increase the spin-weight of
M_{l}
and the boost-weight of
M_{m}
by 1, the two components will have the same
GHP
weights and can be combined. That is precisely the role of the operator
\zeta
introduced by Teukolsky in
Teukolsky 1973
:

\zeta^{a}:=\left(\eth-2\tau+\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\right)l^{a}-\left(\text{\thorn}-2\rho+\bar{\rho}\right)m^{a}\,.

(91)

Using the
GHP
relations in
Geroch et al. 1973
, we can obtain the Teukolsky equation for
s=1
(see equation (
44
)):

\zeta^{a}M_{a}=\mathcal{O}_{1}\phi_{0}=0\,.

(92)

F
is a closed form, thus, locally, there is a form
A
, such that
F=\differential A
. This is known as the vector potential. We can express the components of
F
directly in terms of the
A
. Using the definition of
\phi_{i}
(equation (
37
)), and treating
A^{a}
as a
\{0,0\}

GHP
covariant vector field, we have:

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\tau_{0}{}^{a}A_{a}:=\left(m^{a}\text{\thorn}-l^{a}\eth\right)A_{a}\,,

(93)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}

\displaystyle=\tau_{1}{}^{a}A_{a}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(n^{a}\text{\thorn}-l^{a}\text{\thorn}^{\prime}+m^{a}\eth^{\prime}-\bar{m}^{a}\eth\right)A_{a}\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\tau_{2}{}^{a}A_{a}:=\left(n^{a}\eth^{\prime}-\bar{m}^{a}\text{\thorn}^{\prime}\right)A_{a}\,.

Thus, in terms of
A
, the Teukolsky equation is:

\mathcal{O}_{1}\,\tau_{0}\,A=0\,.

(94)

On the other hand, working directly in an abstract basis, we can obtain
M_{a}
in terms of
A_{a}
. We have:

M_{a}=\left(\mathcal{E}A\right)_{a}:=\nabla_{b}\nabla^{b}A_{a}-\nabla_{b}\nabla_{a}A^{b}\\

(95)

Acting with
\zeta^{a}
on the left, we must recover the Teukolsky equation:

\mathcal{O}_{1}\phi_{0}=\zeta\,\mathcal{E}\,A\,.

(96)

Thus, we have the operator identity:

\mathcal{O}_{1}\tau_{0}=\zeta\mathcal{E}\,.

(97)

The key insight comes from taking the transpose
4
4

4

Here the transpose refers to the classical transpose of a differential operator, no complex conjugation is needed. See
Wald 1978
for details.
of this identity. We have that:

\tau_{0}{}^{T}\mathcal{O}_{1}{}^{T}=\mathcal{E}^{T}\zeta^{T}\,.

(98)

Now, we can prove that
\mathcal{E}^{T}=\mathcal{E}
, thus, for a mode
\psi_{H}
such that:

\mathcal{O}_{1}{}^{T}\psi_{H}=0\,,

(99)

we have:

\mathcal{E}\left(\zeta^{T}\psi_{H}\right)=0\,.

(100)

We proved that
\hat{A}_{a}=\zeta_{a}{}^{T}\psi_{H}
, solves the vacuum Maxwell equations. Thus, by solving equation (
99
), we can generate all the components of
F_{ab}
. This is not yet the complete story. In general,
\psi_{H}
is a complex scalar, thus,
\hat{A}_{a}
is a complex vector. Nevertheless, the reality of
A_{a}
and consequently
F_{ab}
is a crucial assumption in the derivation of equation (
88
).
\mathcal{E}
is a real linear operator, thus if
\hat{A}
is in its kernel, then so must be
\overline{\hat{A}}
. Hence, for any mode
\psi_{H}
that solves (
99
), we can construct a real solution to Maxwell’s equation given by:

A_{a}:=\mathcal{R}e(\zeta_{a}{}^{T}\psi_{H}).

(101)

In the literature,
\psi_{H}
is usually known as the Hertz potential.

From equation (
43
), we can deduce that
\mathcal{O}_{s}{}^{T}=\mathcal{O}_{-s}
. Thus,
\psi_{H}
is a solution to the master Teukolsky equation with
s=-1
. In fact, this is a re-parametrization of the
EOM
for
\phi_{2}

Aksteiner and Andersson 2011
:

\mathcal{O}_{-1}\left(\psi_{2}^{-2/3}\phi_{2}\right)=0

(102)

Thus, the Hertz potential is proportional to the
\phi_{2}
component of a solution to the Maxwell equations, and we can deduce
\psi_{H}
has GHP weight
\{-2,0\}
. We can prove that any
QNM
of
\phi_{2}
will generate a
QNM
for
A^{a}
with the same frequency, and consequently, all

\phi_{2}

QNM
of
\phi_{0}
. The Hertz potential derivation can be reversed, and we can generate a vector potential
A^{a}

\phi_{0}
. Thus, all
\phi_{0}

QNM
must also be
QNM
of
\phi_{2}
. Hence,
\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{2}
must be isospectral, as seen in
Chandrasekhar 1985
.

Notwithstanding, the
\phi_{2}
proportional to the Hertz potential, is not the same as the component of the electromagnetic tensor generated by (
101

\tau_{i}
defined in (
93

GHP
identities in
Geroch et al. 1973
we get
Wald 1978

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\tau_{0}{}^{a}A_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\text{\thorn}\text{\thorn}\bar{\psi}_{H}

(103)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}

\displaystyle=\tau_{1}{}^{a}A_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{\thorn}\,\eth^{\prime}+\tau^{\prime}\,\text{\thorn}\right)\bar{\psi}_{H}

\displaystyle\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\tau_{2}{}^{a}A_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\eth^{\prime}\eth^{\prime}\bar{\psi}_{H}

Because we required
A
to be a real vector,
\bar{\phi}_{i}
can be obtained by simply taking the complex conjugate of these expressions. If we worked with complex
A

\phi_{i}
and
\bar{\phi}_{i}
would be independent variables. Conveniently,
\phi_{i}
depends exclusively on
\bar{\psi}_{H}
.

\phi_{i}

F

\psi_{H}
and
\bar{\psi}_{H}
.
Thus, we can encode equation (

\mathcal{T}_{i}

\mathcal{T}_{i}\bar{\psi}_{H}:=\phi_{i}

(104)

In this section, we will compute the

III.1
Parity even equations of motion

The
EOM
considering parity even

4

\left(\mathcal{O}_{0}-L^{2}\lambda\,\mathcal{G}\right)\Phi=0\,,

(105)

\mathcal{O}_{0}

s=0

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}=24\left(\psi_{2}^{2}+\bar{\psi}_{2}^{2}\right)\,.

[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O}_{0}]=[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{G}]=0\,,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}(y,x)

III.2
On parity odd EFT corrections

To achieve full generality, we must consider parity odd
EFT
corrections. For a vacuum spacetime, under the assumptions we made when deriving the parity even correction, the most general odd correction can be obtained by replacing
\mathcal{G}
with:

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}:=(\star\mathcal{C})_{abcd}\mathcal{C}^{abcd}\,.

(117)

As argued at the end of section
II.6
,
\mathcal{P}
anticommutes with
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
:

\mathcal{P}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}+\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\mathcal{P}\,=0,

(118)

thus, the action of
\mathcal{P}
in the
EOM
will be:

\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{O}_{0}-L^{2}\lambda\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\right)\Phi=\left(\mathcal{O}_{0}+L^{2}\lambda\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\right)\mathcal{P}\Phi\,.

(119)

\mathcal{P}
flips the sign of
\lambda
. Thus, again, we can relate
QNM
whose frequency has positive real part with the ones with negative real part. If
(m,\omega(\lambda))
defines a
QNM
frequency, then so does
(-m,-\bar{\omega}(-\lambda))
. Bearing this in mind, and following the approach in section
III.1
, we could obtain the parity odd
EFT
corrections to scalar
QNM
numerically.

IV
Corrections to electromagnetic modes

Drawing from the methods and intuition developed in section
III
, we will obtain the corrections to electromagnetic
QNM
, under the parity even / odd terms introduced in the action (
6
). Contrary to a scalar field, electromagnetism has two degrees of freedom, encoded in the two polarizations of light. In a vacuum, both follow null geodesics of the background spacetime, propagating at the speed of light. However, when passing through certain media, the propagation speed can be polarization dependent, leading to birefringent behaviour. In
Balakin and Zayats 2018
, considering the parity even part of
6
, the authors proved that the
EFT
correction, effectively turns vacuum into a birefringent medium. For a type-D spacetime, in the Eikonal limit, they found that light propagates along the null geodesics of two different effective metrics, depending on its polarization. There is a known correspondence between null geodesics and the large
\ell
limit of
QNM
frequencies, see
Mashhoon 1985
;
Dolan 2010
;
Yang et al. 2012
. Hence, the birefringent behaviour of light should lead to a splitting of the
QNM
frequencies into two families. In section
IV.2
, we will set
\lambda_{(o)}=0
in the action (
6
), and obtain the corrections to the two families under parity even
EFT
corrections. Then, in section
IV.3
, we will keep
\lambda_{(o)}\neq 0
and generalize the result to mixed parity
EFT
corrections. Surprisingly, we find that the correction to
QNM
frequencies due to parity odd
EFT
terms coincides with the parity even case. We find that, for each background
QNM
frequency there are two possible corresponding corrections with opposing signs:

\omega^{\pm}=\omega_{0}+\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{2}\absolutevalue{\lambda}\,\omega_{1}^{\pm}\,.

(120)

where:

\omega_{1}^{\pm}=\pm(-1)^{\ell+m+1}\,\omega_{1}

(121)

and:

\absolutevalue{\lambda}=\sqrt{\lambda_{(e)}^{2}+\lambda_{(o)}^{2}}\,.

(122)

The factor of
(-1)^{\ell+m+1}
was introduced to ensure that, in all cases studied, the Schwarzschild limit of
\omega_{1}
has positive real and imaginary parts.

6

6

In section
IV.2.1
, we will show that, for parity even corrections, in the Schwarzschild limit, the imaginary part of
\omega_{1}
vanishes in the limit
\ell\rightarrow\infty
. As will be clear in section

, this feature does not generalize for finite
\ell
, thus we can, in general, enforce the positivity of
\mathcal{I}m\,\omega_{1}
.
Because
\omega_{1}^{+}=-\omega_{1}^{-}
, this sign carries no physical meaning. This is in close analogy to the results for the gravitational case, see
Cano et al. 2022
;

. Finally, in section

, obtaining the corrections to the

IV.1
Equations of motion

In this section, we will derive the corrected
EOM
for electromagnetic waves in a Kerr background. Using
p
forms and hodge duals, we will cast them into a form that can be easily tackled in later sections. Varying action (
6
) with respect to the vector potential, and using equation (
81
), we get:

\nabla^{b}\left(F_{ab}-\frac{1}{3}L^{2}\left(\lambda_{(e)}\mathcal{C}+\lambda_{(o)}\star\mathcal{C}\right)_{ab}{}^{cd}F_{cd}\right)=0\,.

(123)

We want to solve this equation directly in terms of the components of
F_{ab}
, thus, we must also enforce the electromagnetic Bianchi identity,
\differential F=0
, guaranteeing
F
is a closed form. Following the approach in section
II.7
, the two conditions can be encoded by the real and imaginary part of a single complex equation. We get:

i\star\differential\left(F^{-}+i\frac{1}{3}L^{2}\left(\lambda_{(e)}\star\mathcal{C}-\lambda_{(o)}\mathcal{C}\right)\,:F\right)=0\,,

(124)

where,
F^{-}
was defined in equation (
82
), and we define “
:
” as a double contraction. For some 4 tensor
\mathcal{A}_{ab}{}^{cd}
and 2 tensor
\mathcal{B}_{ab}
we have:

\left(\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{B}\right)_{ab}:=\mathcal{A}_{ab}{}^{cd}\mathcal{B}_{cd}\,.

(125)

Assuming
\mathcal{C}
and
F
are real, the real part of (
124
) reduces to equation (
123
), whereas the imaginary part encodes the Bianchi identity. By solving the equation for a general
F
, we guarantee that both conditions are satisfied. This equation takes a simpler form, if we aggregate
\lambda_{(e)}
and
\lambda_{(o)}
into a single complex coupling constant:

\lambda=\lambda_{(e)}+i\lambda_{(o)}\,.

(126)

Using the identities in section
II.6
, we get:

i\star\differential\left(F^{-}-L^{2}\frac{\bar{\lambda}}{6}\,\mathcal{C}^{-}:F^{-}+L^{2}\frac{\lambda}{6}\,\mathcal{C}^{+}:F^{+}\right)=0\,.

(127)

By definition,
F^{+}=\bar{F}^{-}
, thus the
EOM
depend on both,
F^{-}
and its complex conjugate. Consequently, the equation is not holomorphic in
F^{\pm}
, and we must independently solve its real and imaginary parts.

To make progress, define
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
, such that:

\mathcal{F}^{\pm}=F^{\pm}-\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{2}\left(\lambda_{(e)}\pm i\lambda_{(o)}\right)M^{2}\mathcal{C}^{\pm}:F^{\pm}\,.

(128)

Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
obeys the same properties as
F^{\pm}
. We have:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{\mp}\,,

(129)

\displaystyle\star\mathcal{F}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\pm i\mathcal{F}^{\pm}\,.

Thus, we can define:

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{+}+\mathcal{F}^{-}\,,

(130)

as a
2
form that reduces to
F
in the
L\rightarrow 0
limit. Working perturbatively in
L/M
, in terms of
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
, the
EOM
are:

\star\differential\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}+L^{2}\frac{\lambda}{6}\,\mathcal{C}^{+}:\mathcal{F}^{+}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{4}\,.

(131)

To project this into a null tetrad basis, we must first define
NP
scalars to encode
\mathcal{F}
. Following the convention in equation (
37
), we get:

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle:=l^{a}m^{b}\mathcal{F}_{ab}=\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\bar{\lambda}\,L^{2}\,\psi_{2}\right)\phi_{0}\,,

(132)

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(l^{a}n^{b}-m^{a}\bar{m}^{b}\right)\mathcal{F}_{ab}=\left(1+\frac{2}{3}\bar{\lambda}\,L^{2}\,\psi_{2}\right)\phi_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle:=\bar{m}^{a}n^{b}\mathcal{F}_{ab}=\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\bar{\lambda}\,L^{2}\,\psi_{2}\right)\phi_{2}\,.

In the limit
L\rightarrow 0
,
\Phi_{i}\rightarrow\phi_{i}
. The Weyl tensor also obeys a Bianchi identity
\nabla_{[e}\mathcal{C}_{ab]cd}=0
. In a vacuum, this implies that
\nabla^{a}\mathcal{C}_{abcd}=0
. Putting the two identities together, we can prove:

\left(\star\differential\,\mathcal{C}^{+}:\mathcal{F}^{+}\right)_{a}=\mathcal{C}^{+}_{ab}{}^{cd}\nabla^{b}\mathcal{F}_{cd}^{+}\,.

(133)

Using this, we can prove that in the null tetrad basis, (
131
) reduces to:

\mathcal{M}=\frac{1}{3}\bar{\psi}_{2}L^{2}\lambda\,J+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{4}\,,

(134)

where:

\mathcal{M}_{(\alpha)}:=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\eth^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime}\right)\Phi_{0}-\left(\text{\thorn}-2\rho\right)\Phi_{1}\\
\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}-2\rho^{\prime}\right)\Phi_{1}-\left(\eth-\tau\right)\Phi_{2}\\
\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}-\rho^{\prime}\right)\Phi_{0}-\left(\eth-2\tau\right)\Phi_{1}\\
\left(\eth^{\prime}-2\tau^{\prime}\right)\Phi_{1}-\left(\text{\thorn}-\rho\right)\Phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}_{(\alpha)}\,,

(135)

and:

J_{(\alpha)}:=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\eth+2\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{0}+2\left(\text{\thorn}+\bar{\rho}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{1}\\
-\left(\eth^{\prime}+2\bar{\tau}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{2}-2\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}+\bar{\rho}^{\prime}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{1}\\
-\left(\text{\thorn}+2\bar{\rho}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{2}-2\left(\eth+\bar{\tau}^{\prime}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{1}\\
\left(\text{\thorn}^{\prime}+2\bar{\rho}^{\prime}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{0}+2\left(\eth^{\prime}+\bar{\tau}\right)\bar{\Phi}_{1}\end{pmatrix}_{(\alpha)}\,.

(136)

As expected from the identities
n^{a}=\left(l^{a}\right)^{\prime}
and
\bar{m}^{a}=\left(m^{a}\right)^{\prime}
, the
2^{nd}
and
4^{th}
line can be obtained from the
1^{st}
and
3^{rd}
by using the prime operation.

While
M_{(\alpha)}
depends exclusively on
\Phi_{i}
,
J_{(\alpha)}
, is a function of
\bar{\Phi}_{i}
. Thus, if we try to factor out
\exp(- i \omega t)
from each
\Phi_{i}
, we will get a term proportional to
\exp(- i \omega t)
on the
LHS
and a term proportional to
\exp(i \bar\omega t)
on the
RHS
. The standard approach to define
QNM
cannot be applied directly. As remarked before, this is a consequence of the non holomorphic character of equation (
127
). Physically, this happens because the
EFT
correction breaks the degeneracy between the two polarizations of light. To tackle this issue, we must combine (
134
) with its complex conjugate, and choose a basis for
\Phi_{i}
and
\bar{\Phi}_{i}
, that leads to decoupled
EOM
. This is analogous to finding a basis for the polarizations of light that propagate along the two effective light cones of the corrected theory. If we consider exclusively parity even corrections, we expect that this basis will be related with parity eigenstates. In section
IV.2
, we will make this statement more concrete. When considering mixed parity corrections, the answer is not as obvious. We will explore this case in section
IV.3
.

In this section, we set

IV.2.1
Eikonal limit

The Eikonal approximation, also known as the geometric optics limit, is valid when the wavelength of light, dubbed
\varepsilon
, is much smaller than the characteristic length scale of the background spacetime. For a Kerr background, this is the statement that
\alpha\ll 1
, with:

\alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\,.

(137)

In
Baibhav et al. 2023
, the authors explored this limit for the parity even part of (
6
), considering several algebraically special
BH
spacetimes. In the beginning of this section, we will outline their approach, and obtain the effective metrics that encode the propagation of light on a Kerr background. Then, we will exploit the connection with the large
\ell
limit of
QNM
to derive analytic expressions for the frequencies in this limit.

We start with a plane wave
Ansatz
:

A^{a}=\mathcal{A}^{a}e^{i\frac{\Theta}{\alpha}}\,.

(138)

We assume that the characteristic length scale of all variables is
M
, with the factor of
1/\alpha
in the exponential ensuring that the signal has a short wavelength. Plugging this into equation (
123
), and setting
\lambda_{(o)}
we get:

C_{abcd}K^{b}K^{c}\mathcal{A}^{d}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\right)=0\,.

(139)

where:

C_{ab}{}^{cd}=\delta_{a}^{[c}\,\delta_{b}^{d]}-\frac{1}{3}\,\lambda_{(e)}L^{2}\,\mathcal{C}_{ab}{}^{cd}\,,

(140)

K_{a}:=\nabla_{a}\Theta
is the wavevector of (
138
), and parametrizes the propagation direction.

As pointed out in
Reall 2021
, there is an issue with this approach. By dimensional analysis, we have
\mathcal{C}\sim 1/M^{2}
, thud, the size of the correction in (
140
) is:

\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{2}\lambda_{(e)}=\left(\frac{L}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\right)^{2}\lambda_{(e)}\,.

(141)

Now, the
EFT
approximation is only valid, if
L
is much smaller than any other length scale in the theory. In particular,
L/\varepsilon\ll 1
, or else, higher order derivative corrections become relevant. Thus:

\left(\frac{L}{M}\right)^{2}\ll\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\right)^{2}

(142)

In the Eikonal approximation, we neglected all terms that are
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/M)
, thus we must also neglect the contribution of the
EFT
correction. The
EFT
correction is invisible in the Eikonal limit.

The same argument can be made on the
QNM
side. Bellow, we will obtain an asymptotic expansion of the
QNM
frequencies in the large
\ell
limit. Roughly, we have:

\omega=\ell\,\omega_{(1)}+\omega_{(0)}+\frac{1}{\ell}\omega_{(-1)}+\cdots

(143)

The angular part of a
QNM
with azimuthal number
\ell
has
\ell
nodes, thus, the length-scale of this mode is at least
M/\ell
. Hence, the validity of the
EFT
approximation requires
L/M\ll 1/\ell
. We expect the correction to the
QNM

C_{ab}{}^{cd}

IV.3.2
Generalization to Kerr

We can now generalize our conclusions to Kerr
BH

\lambda_{(e)}

\absolutevalue{\lambda}=\sqrt{\lambda_{(e)}^{2}+\lambda_{(o)}^{2}}

V
Discussion

In this paper, we computed the correction to
BH

QNM
, under scalar and electromagnetic, leading order
EFT
corrections. In the electromagnetic case, we proved that the leading order parity even and parity odd
EFT
corrections have the same effect on
QNM
frequencies. Taking
\lambda{(e)/(o)}
to be the coupling parameter that control parity even / odd corrections, the combined effect of the two is controlled by
\sqrt{\lambda_{(e)}^{2}+\lambda_{(o)}^{2}}
. Furthermore, we found that the degeneracy between the two polarizations of light is broken, splitting the
QNM
into two families. At leading order, the corrections to the corresponding frequencies were shown to be equal and opposite.

Electromagnetic
QNM
from a
BH
merger have not yet been detected. Thus, from an observational perspective, our results are not very relevant. However, as seen in
Cano et al. 2022
;
Cano et al. 2023a
;
Cano et al. 2023b
, the main features generalize to pure gravity
EFT
corrections. There, the degeneracy breaking between the two possible polarizations is particularly relevant. In theory, if we know the precise direction of a
GW
source, combining the signal from several
GW
detectors, we may disentangle the two
GW
tensor polarizations, see
Will 2014
. Thus, if both families of
QNM
are excited in a
BH
merger, we may be able to disentangle the
QNM
spectrum into contributions corresponding to each polarization, and use the difference to infer an upper bound for the size of the
EFT
correction.

We used, scalar, and electromagnetic
QNM
as toy models to learn how to, in general, compute the
EFT
corrections to gravitational
QNM
of Kerr
BH
. There, the challenge is that not only the
EOM
are corrected, but the
background
stationary metric is also corrected.
Cano et al. 2022
;
Cano et al. 2023a
;
Cano et al. 2023b
have overcome this challenge, by working perturbatively in the
BH
spin. In future work, we expect that it is possible to extend our method to compute the gravitational
QNM
of
BH
with arbitrary spin. The correction to the background metric can be determined numerically and then, the resulting
EOM
can be decoupled and solved with an extension of both our methods.
