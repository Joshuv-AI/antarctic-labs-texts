Abstract
. We study the distribution of the Hodge spectral exponents of an irreducible plane curve by comparing it with a continuous distribution. We provide a closed formula for this difference in terms of numerical invariants of the curve. We characterize those families of irreducible plane curves whose limit distribution of Hodge spectral exponents is the continuous distribution and we provide intervals of dominating values.



1.
Introduction

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
be a germ of a holomorphic function (or equivalently a convergent power series
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
) with an isolated singularity at the origin.
Using the canonical mixed Hodge structure of the cohomology groups of the Milnor fiber of
f
, Steenbrink
[
Ste77
]
defined the
Hodge spectrum
of
f
as the generating function

\operatorname{Sp}_{f}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}T^{\alpha_{i}},

where
\mu=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}}{\left(\frac{df}{dx_{0}},\dots,\frac{df}{dx_{n}}\right)}
is the
Milnor number
and the positive rational numbers

0<\alpha_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\alpha_{\mu}<n+1

form a discrete set of invariants of the singularity
f
called
Hodge spectral exponents
(or simply
spectral numbers
).
They are symmetric with respect to
(n+1)/2
, i.e. for every
j=1,\dots,\mu
, we have
\alpha_{\mu+1-j}=(n+1)-\alpha_{j}
and thus it is
enough to study them in the interval
\bigl(0,(n+1)/2\bigr]
. Another interesting feature proved by Varchenko
[
Var82
]
is that
the Hodge spectral exponents of
f
are stable under deformations with constant Milnor number
\mu
.

Recall that a deformation of a hypersurface
f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
is a family of hypersurfaces
f_{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})

for some set of parameters
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in S\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
, satisfying
f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=f_{0,\dots,0}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
. Then, in Varchenko’s result we are asking that
the Milnor number of
f_{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is the same for all
(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in S
.

K. Saito
[
Sai83a
]
considered the normalized spectrum which he denoted as
the
characteristic function

\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}(T)=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}T^{\alpha_{i}}.

We may also display the Hodge spectral exponents as a discrete (probability) distribution on
\mathbb{R}
. Namely, The
distribution of the Hodge spectral exponents
is

D_{f}(s)=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\delta(s-\alpha_{i}),

where
\delta(s)
is Dirac’s delta distribution.
Indeed considering either
D_{f}(s)
or
\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}(T)
is equivalent because the characteristic function is the Fourier transform of the distribution of Hodge spectral exponents, i.e.,

\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}(T)=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{D_{f}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau).

Considering the change of variables
T=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\tau}

we will treat the dependence of
\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}
on
T
and on
\tau
interchangeably throughout this paper.

Remark 1.0.1
.

Because of the symmetry of the Hodge spectrum, we will be interested in the truncations

\mbox{\Large$\chi$}^{<1}_{f}(T)=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{\alpha_{i}<1}T^{\alpha_{i}}\hskip 5.69054pt,\hskip 28.45274ptD^{<1}_{f}(s)=\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{\alpha_{i}<1}\delta(s-\alpha_{i}).

Definition 1.0.2
.

The
continuous distribution
function is
N_{n+1}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as:

\displaystyle N_{n+1}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{0}+\dots+x_{n}=s}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,1)$}}(x_{0})\cdots\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,1)$}}(x_{n})\differential{x_0}\dots\differential{x_n}

\displaystyle=\left(\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,1)$}}\ast\overset{n+1}{\dots}\ast\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,1)$}}\right)(s),

where
\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,1)$}}(s)
is the indicator function and
\ast
denotes the convolution product. One may check that the Fourier transform of
N_{n+1}(s)
is

\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{n+1}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau)=\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{n+1}.

N_{n+1}(s)

Definition 1.0.3
.

We define

\phi_{f}\colon\left[0,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

as the
cumulative difference function
between
N_{n+1}(s)
and
D_{f}(s)
, that is

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}N_{n+1}(s)-D_{f}(s)\differential{s}=\int_{0}^{r}N_{n+1}(s)-\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\delta(s-\alpha_{i})\differential{s}=\int_{0}^{r}N_{n+1}(s)\differential{s}-\frac{1}{\mu}\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}.

Definition 1.0.4
.

We say that
r\in\left[0,\frac{n+1}{2}\right)
is a
dominating value
if
\phi_{f}(r)>0
, or equivalently if

\frac{1}{\mu}\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}<\int_{0}^{r}N_{n+1}(s)\differential{s}.

K. Saito
[
Sai83a
]
introduced these notions of cumulative difference function and dominating values. Moreover he formulated the following questions:

{QUESTION}

For which limits of sequences of hypersurfaces
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
does the distribution of Hodge spectral exponents
D_{f^{(i)}}(s)
converge to
N_{n+1}(s)
?
Equivalently, for which limits of
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
does the characteristic function
\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f^{(i)}}(T)
converge to
\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{n+1}\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau)=\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{n+1}
?

{QUESTION}

Given
f
, what is the set of all dominating values?

The limit of
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
in Question
1
has to be specified, since it is not clear a priori which kind of limit one should consider. The few results we may find in the literature all consider different types of limits. K. Saito already calculated the following two limits, both of which converge to
N_{n+1}(s)
:

Proposition 1.0.5

(
[
Sai83a
, (3.7)]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
be a quasi-homogeneous polynomial of degree 1 with respect to the weights
r_{0},\dots,r_{n}
, i.e., satisfying
f(\lambda^{r_{0}}x_{0},\dots,\lambda^{r_{n}}x_{n})=\lambda f(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
. Then, taking a sequence of such functions with the limit
r_{i}\rightarrow 0
for all
i=0,\dots,n
, one has

\lim_{r_{0},\dots,r_{n}\rightarrow 0}\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}(T)=\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{n+1}=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{n+1}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau).

Proposition 1.0.6

(
[
Sai83a
, (3.9)]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
be an irreducible plane curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
. Then, taking a sequence of such functions with the limit
n_{g}\rightarrow+\infty
(keeping all other
n_{j}
and
l_{j}
fixed), one has

\lim_{n_{g}\rightarrow+\infty}\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f}(T)=\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{2}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau).

The Puiseux pairs are defined in Section
2
.

More recently, Almirón and Schulze gave another example for which the distribution of Hodge spectral exponents also converges to the continuous distribution
N_{n+1}(s)
:

Proposition 1.0.7

(
[
AS22
]
)
.

Consider a fixed Newton diagram
\Gamma
. Let
f_{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a Newton non-degenerate function with Newton diagram
\omega\Gamma
(the rescaling of
\Gamma
by a factor
\omega\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
). Then, taking a sequence of such functions with limit
\omega\rightarrow+\infty
, one has

\lim_{\omega\rightarrow+\infty}\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f_{\omega}}(T)=\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{n+1}=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{n+1}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau).

We point out that in all of the above examples, the log-canonical threshold of the sequence of hypersurfaces has limit 0.
Thus one is tempted to consider this condition in the definition of limit of Question
1
.
In Section
5
we will prove that this is in fact a necessary condition but it is not sufficient.

Regarding Question
1
on the set of dominating values, Tomari proved the following result which, in terms of dominating values, states the following:

Theorem 1.0.8

(
[
Tom93
]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
be a plane curve and
0<\alpha_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\alpha_{\mu}<2
its corresponding Hodge spectral numbers. Then
\frac{1}{2}
is a dominating value, i.e.,

\#\left\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\right\}<\frac{\mu}{8}.

In Section
6
we will give an alternative proof of this theorem when restricted to the case where
f
is irreducible. K. Saito asked whether
\frac{1}{2}
is a dominating value for any

f\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
, that is

\#\left\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\right\}<\frac{\mu}{(n+1)!\,2^{n+1}}.

A conjecture posed by Durfee states:

Conjecture 1.0.9

(
[
Dur78
]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y,z\}
be surface with a singularity at the origin. Then

p_{g}<\frac{\mu}{6}.

Here,
p_{g}
denotes the
geometric genus
defined as

p_{g}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(R^{n-1}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\right)_{0}\hskip 11.38109pt\text{for }n\geqslant 2,\hskip 28.45274pt(\;p_{g}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}/\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\right)_{0}\hskip 11.38109pt\text{for }n=1\;),

with
\pi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
being a resolution of the singularity. M. Saito
[
Sai83b
]
proved a relation between this invariant and the Hodge spectral exponents.
Namely,
p_{g}=\#\{i\,|\,\alpha_{i}\leqslant 1\}
, and thus Durfee’s conjecture predicts that
1
a dominating value when
n=2
.
K. Saito asked whether one can generalize this statement to:

Question:

Is
1
a dominating value for all
n\geqslant 2
? That is, is it true that

p_{g}=\#\left\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant 1\right\}<\frac{\mu}{(n+1)!}

for any
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
?

The aim of this paper is to study Questions
1
and
1
for the case of irreducible plane curves.
More precisely, we will give a closed formula for
\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}
in Theorem
3.0.1
and
\phi_{f}(r)
in Theorem
3.0.2
in terms of
numerical invariants of the irreducible plane curve. With these tools in hand, we will give in Theorem
4.0.1
a characterisation of those sequences of irreducible plane curves whose
discrete distribution of Hodge spectral exponents converge to the continuous distribution. This would give a complete answer to Question
1
in this case. In Section
5
we will show that
the log-canonical threshold converging to zero is a necessary but not sufficient condition for Question
1
. Regarding Question
1
, we provide in Theorem
6.0.1
intervals
of dominating values for an irreducible plane curve. These intervals contain the value
\frac{1}{2}
and thus we reprove Tomari’s result in this particular case (see Corollary
6.0.4
).

2.
Irreducible plane curve singularities

In this section we will briefly present the necessary background on irreducible plane curves that we will use in this paper and we refer to Casas-Alvero’s book
[
CA00
]
for unexplained terminology.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)
be a germ of a holomorphic function, or equivalently a convergent power series
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
. The equation
f=0
defines locally a (complex) plane curve around the origin. We will only consider irreducible plane curves
f
, i.e., irreducible elements of the unique factorization domain
\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
.

Theorem 2.0.1

(Puiseux)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
define an irreducible plane curve that is not tangent to the
y
-axis (i.e.,
\partialderivative{f}{x}\neq 0
). Then there is a Puiseux series
s(x)=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}a_{i}x^{i/m}
such that
f(x,s(x))=0
. Moreover, all such series are conjugates
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(s)=\sum_{i\geqslant 0}a_{i}\varepsilon^{i}x^{i/m}
with
\varepsilon^{m}=1
. The curve can be parameterized by
t\mapsto\left(t^{m},\sum_{i\geqslant 0}a_{i}t^{i}\right)
.

A Puiseux series of
f
has the form

s(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in(e_{0})\\
0\leqslant j<\beta_{1}\end{subarray}}a_{j}x^{j/m}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in(e_{1})\\
\beta_{1}\leqslant j<\beta_{2}\end{subarray}}a_{j}x^{j/m}+\dots+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in(e_{g-1})\\
\beta_{g-1}\leqslant j<\beta_{g}\end{subarray}}a_{j}x^{j/m}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in(e_{g})\\
\beta_{g}\leqslant j\end{subarray}}a_{j}x^{j/m}

with

\displaystyle e_{0}=m

\displaystyle\beta_{i}=\min\{j\,|\,a_{j}\neq 0,\ j\notin(e_{i-1})\}\quad(i=1,\dots,g)

\displaystyle e_{i}=\gcd(e_{i-1},\beta_{i})\quad(i=1,\dots,g)

where
m
is chosen as small as possible, such that
e_{g}=1
. Since
e_{i}|e_{i-1}
, we can define
n_{i}=e_{i-1}/e_{i}
and thus

(2.0.1)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle e_{i}=n_{g}\cdots n_{i+1}\quad(i=0,\dots,g-1)

\displaystyle e_{g}=1

\displaystyle n_{i}\geqslant 2\quad(i=0,\dots,g-1).

For the interested reader, these numerical invariants have a geometric meaning:
e_{0}
is the multiplicity of
f
at the origin and
e_{i}\,(i=1,\dots,g)
is the multiplicity of
f
at the
i
-th rupture divisor of its minimal embedded resolution, or equivalently the last infinitely near point of the
i
-th cluster of consecutive satellite points. These concepts are explained in
[
CA00
]
.

We present three equivalent sets of complete topological invariants of the singularity of
f
:

•

the
characteristic exponents

\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{m},\dots,\frac{\beta_{g}}{m}\right)
,

•

the
Puiseux pairs

(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
,

•

the
semigroup

\langle\bar{\beta}_{0},\dots,\bar{\beta}_{g}\rangle
.

They determine, and are determined by, the homeomorphism class of
f^{-1}(0)\cap U
for a small enough neighbourhood
U
of the origin.

From the information given by the Puiseux series we get the first set of invariants.

Definition 2.0.2
.

The
characteristic exponents
of an irreducible plane curve
f
are the rational numbers
\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{m},\dots,\frac{\beta_{g}}{m}\right)
.

M. Saito
[
Sai00
]
used the following notation for the characteristic exponents:

\frac{\beta_{i}}{m}=\frac{k_{1}}{n_{1}}+\dots+\frac{k_{i}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{i}}\quad(i=1,\dots,g)

with
k_{1}>n_{1}
. Additionally, they satisfy
n_{j}\geqslant 2
,
k_{j}\geqslant 1
, and
\gcd(k_{j},n_{j})=1
.

For our purposes it will be more convenient to avoid having to work with the condition
k_{1}>n_{1}
. Therefore we
introduce the following change of variables:

\displaystyle k_{1}=n_{1}+l_{1}\quad\text{with}\quad l_{1}\geqslant 1,

\displaystyle k_{j}=l_{j}\quad(j=2,\dots,g).

With this slightly different notation, the characteristic exponents are

\frac{\beta_{i}}{m}=1+\frac{l_{1}}{n_{1}}+\dots+\frac{l_{i}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{i}}\quad(i=1,\dots,g)

with
n_{j}\geqslant 2
,
l_{j}\geqslant 1
,
\gcd(l_{j},n_{j})=1
.

Definition 2.0.3
.

The
Puiseux pairs
of an irreducible plane curve
f
are
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
.

Remark 2.0.4
.

The name Puiseux pairs appear in various slightly different ways in the literature.
We based our definition on the one given by M. Saito
[
Sai00
]
, who used this name for the pairs
(k_{1},n_{1}),\dots,(k_{g},n_{g})
.
Casas-Alvero
[
CA00
]
used the similar term characteristic pairs to refer to
(\beta_{1},m),\dots,(\beta_{g},m)
.

Definition 2.0.5
.

The semigroup of an irreducible plane curve
f
is

\Gamma_{f}=\{o_{f}(g)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\,|\,g\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}\setminus(f)\}

where the vanishing order of
g
along
f
is
o_{f}(g)=o_{t}\Bigl(g\bigl(t^{m},s(t^{m})\bigr)\Bigr)

with
o_{t}
the order of a power series in
t
, and
s
a Puiseux series of
f
.

The semigroup of
f
is a finitely generated (additive) semigroup, where the number of the minimal generators is one more than the number of characteristic exponents.
They are denoted by
\Gamma_{f}=\langle\bar{\beta}_{0},\dots,\bar{\beta}_{g}\rangle
. The relation with the characteristic exponents and thus the Puiseux pairs is given by the following:

Proposition 2.0.6
.

The minimal generators of the semigroup
\Gamma_{f}=\langle\bar{\beta}_{0},\dots,\bar{\beta}_{g}\rangle
satisfy:

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}_{0}=m,\hskip 14.22636pt\bar{\beta}_{1}=\beta_{1},

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\bar{\beta}_{i}=n_{i-1}\bar{\beta}_{i-1}-\beta_{i-1}+\beta_{i}\quad(i=2,\dots,g),\hskip 14.22636pte_{i}=\gcd(e_{i-1},\bar{\beta}_{i}).

2.1.
Some juggling with numerical invariants

M. Saito
[
Sai00
]
considered the following numerical invariants
in order to obtain a formula for the characteristic function of the Hodge spectral exponents of an irreducible plane curve.

\displaystyle w_{1}=n_{1}+l_{1},

\displaystyle w_{j}=n_{j}n_{j-1}w_{j-1}+l_{j}\quad(j=2,\dots,g).

We can actually extend his definition by letting
n_{0}=1,\;w_{0}=1,

from which
w_{1}=n_{1}+l_{1}=n_{1}n_{0}w_{0}+l_{1}

also satisfies the recurrence relation. Thus, we can write

Definition 2.1.1
.

We define the following numerical invariants:

\displaystyle w_{0}=1,

\displaystyle w_{j}=n_{j}n_{j-1}w_{j-1}+l_{j}\quad(j=1,\dots,g).

This means that we can use the recursive definition also in the case
j=1
, which will simplify things later. Checking the recurrence relations, one finds that

Proposition 2.1.2
.

The numerical invariants
w_{j}
can be interpreted in terms of the semigroup of
f
as follows:

w_{j}=\frac{\bar{\beta}_{j}}{e_{j}}\quad(j=0,\dots,g).

Definition 2.1.3
.

We define the following numerical invariants:

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=0,

\displaystyle\mu_{j}=(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)+n_{j}\mu_{j-1}\quad(j=1,\dots,g).

Proposition 2.1.4

(
[
Sai83a
]
)
.

The Milnor number of
f
is
\mu=\mu_{g}
. More generally, the Milnor number of a curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{j},l_{j})
is
\mu_{j}
, for any
j\in\{1,\dots,g\}
.

The following technical lemmas where we express
w_{j}
and
n_{g}\mu_{g-1}
in terms of
l_{j},n_{j}
(and
e_{j}
) will be useful in Section
3
.

Lemma 2.1.5
.

The numerical invariant
w_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,g
can be written as

w_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}},

and in particular

\displaystyle w_{g}=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{n_{g}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{n_{g}}.

Proof.

We prove it by induction:

•

For
j=1
,

\displaystyle w_{1}=l_{1}\frac{e_{1}^{2}n_{1}}{e_{1}^{2}n_{1}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{1}^{2}n_{1}}=l_{1}+n_{1}.

•

The induction step:

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=n_{j}n_{j-1}w_{j-1}+l_{j}=n_{j}n_{j-1}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j-1}^{2}n_{j-1}}+n_{j}n_{j-1}\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j-1}^{2}n_{j-1}}+l_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+l_{j}\frac{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}.

∎

Lemma 2.1.6
.

The product of the numerical invariants
n_{g}\mu_{g-1}
can be written as

\displaystyle n_{g}\mu_{g-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}\left(\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{n_{g}}-\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{k-1}}\right)+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{n_{g}}+n_{g}-2e_{0}.

Proof.

Using the definition of
\mu_{j}
and the previous lemma:

\displaystyle n_{g}\mu_{g-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(w_{j}-1)(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}-1\right)(n_{j}-1)e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}.

We can simplify this expression as follows:

1)

The first term:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}\sum_{j=k}^{g-1}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\sum_{j=k}^{g-1}\frac{1}{e_{j}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\sum_{j=k}^{g-1}\left(\frac{1}{e_{j}}-\frac{1}{e_{j-1}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\left(\frac{1}{e_{g-1}}-\frac{1}{e_{k-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}\left(\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{n_{g}}-\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{k-1}}\right).

2)

The second term:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}e_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{e_{j}}-\frac{1}{e_{j-1}}\right)=e_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{e_{g-1}}-\frac{1}{e_{0}}\right)=\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{n_{g}}-e_{0}.

3)

The third term:

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}e_{j}-e_{j-1}=e_{g-1}-e_{0}=n_{g}-e_{0}.

Putting it together:

\displaystyle n_{g}\mu_{g-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g-1}l_{k}\left(\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{n_{g}}-\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{k-1}}\right)+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{n_{g}}+n_{g}-2e_{0}.

∎

Lemma 2.1.7
.

The Milnor number of an irreducible plane curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
is

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.1.5
we have:

w_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}+\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}.

Then, the Milnor number can be written as

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mu_{g}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}(w_{j}-1)(n_{j}-1)e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}.

We can simplify this expression as follows.

1)

The first term:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{k=1}^{j}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g}\sum_{j=k}^{g}l_{k}\frac{e_{k}^{2}n_{k}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\sum_{j=k}^{g}\frac{1}{e_{j}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\sum_{j=k}^{g}\left(\frac{1}{e_{j}}-\frac{1}{e_{j-1}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}^{2}n_{k}\left(\frac{1}{e_{g}}-\frac{1}{e_{k-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}e_{k-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{e_{k-1}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}(e_{k-1}-1).

2)

The second term:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{e_{0}^{2}}{e_{j}^{2}n_{j}}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=e_{0}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\left(\frac{1}{e_{j}}-\frac{1}{e_{j-1}}\right)=e_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{e_{g}}-\frac{1}{e_{0}}\right)=e_{0}^{2}-e_{0}.

3)

The third term:

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}e_{j}-e_{j-1}=e_{g}-e_{0}=1-e_{0}.

Putting it together:

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{g}l_{k}e_{k}(e_{k-1}-1)+e_{0}^{2}-e_{0}+1-e_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}.

∎

2.2.
Hodge spectral exponents of irreducible plane curves

Thàn and Steenbrink
[
TS89
]
already described the Hodge spectrum of any plane curve in terms its topological invariants, but in this work we will use a closed formula
given by M. Saito:

Theorem 2.2.1

(
[
Sai00
, Th. 1.5]
)
.

The Hodge spectral exponents in the interval
(0,1)
are:

\left\{\frac{1}{e_{j}}\left(\frac{b}{n_{j}}+\frac{a}{w_{j}}\right)+\frac{c}{e_{j}}\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}0<a<w_{j}\\
0<b<n_{j}\\
0\leqslant c<n_{j+1}\cdots n_{g}\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant g\end{array},\frac{b}{n_{j}}+\frac{a}{w_{j}}<1\right\}.

Notice that this formula gives us a set of
\mu/2
Hodge spectral exponents and thus, by symmetry, it characterizes all the Hodge spectral exponents of
f
.

Remark 2.2.2
.

If we use

\displaystyle 1\leqslant a\leqslant w_{j}-1\text{ and }\frac{b}{n_{j}}+\frac{a}{w_{j}}<1

\displaystyle\iff 1\leqslant a\leqslant w_{j}-1\text{ and }a<w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\iff 1\leqslant a\leqslant\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor,

the set of Hodge spectral exponents in
(0,1)
can be written as

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{g}\bigcup_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\bigcup_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{\frac{c}{e_{j}}+\frac{b}{e_{j}n_{j}}+\frac{a}{e_{j}w_{j}}\ \middle|\ 1\leqslant a\leqslant\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\right\}.

In terms of the distribution,

\displaystyle\mu\,D_{f}^{<1}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\sum_{a=1}^{\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor}\delta\left(s-\left(\frac{c}{e_{j}}+\frac{b}{e_{j}n_{j}}+\frac{a}{e_{j}w_{j}}\right)\right).

Corollary 2.2.3
.

The smallest Hodge spectral exponent is the log-canonical threshold

\alpha_{1}=\lct(f)=\frac{1}{e_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}+l_{1}}\right)=\frac{1}{n_{g}\cdots n_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}+l_{1}}\right)=\frac{1}{e_{0}}\left(1+\frac{1}{1+\frac{l_{1}}{n_{1}}}\right).

The largest Hodge spectral exponent in the interval
(0,1)
is

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu/2}=1-\frac{1}{e_{g-1}w_{g}}=1-\frac{1}{n_{g}w_{g}}.

3.0.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.0.1

The number of Hodge spectral exponents less or equal to
r

for
r\in[0,1)
is given by the cumulative distribution, that is

\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}=\int_{0}^{r}\mu\,D_{f}^{<1}(s)\differential{s}.

To calculate this integral, we start with the following observation.
Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then

\displaystyle\#\{i\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,1\leqslant i\leqslant m,\ i\leqslant x\}=\lfloor x\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,m)$}}(x)+m\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[m,+\infty)$}}(x),

and since
\lfloor x\rfloor=m
in the interval
x\in[m,m+1)
, we also have

\displaystyle\#\{i\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,1\leqslant i\leqslant m,\ i\leqslant x\}=\lfloor x\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,\lambda)$}}(x)+m\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[\lambda,+\infty)$}}(x)

for any
\lambda\in[m,m+1]
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\mu\,D_{f}^{<1}(s)\differential{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\sum_{a=1}^{\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor}\delta\left(s-\left(\frac{c}{e_{j}}+\frac{b}{e_{j}n_{j}}+\frac{a}{e_{j}w_{j}}\right)\right)\differential{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\#\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}\,\middle|\,1\leqslant a\leqslant\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor,\ \frac{c}{e_{j}}+\frac{b}{e_{j}n_{j}}+\frac{a}{e_{j}w_{j}}\leqslant r\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\#\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}\,\middle|\,1\leqslant a\leqslant\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor,\ a\leqslant w_{j}\left(e_{j}r-c-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(e_{j}r-c-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[0,w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right)$}}\!\left(w_{j}\left(e_{j}r-c-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right),+\infty\right)$}}\!\left(w_{j}\left(e_{j}r-c-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{c=0}^{e_{j}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(e_{j}r-c-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(e_{j}r-c\right)+\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[1,+\infty)$}}\!\left(e_{j}r-c\right).

Now we need the following facts:

1)

For the first term, let
f(x)
be a function with support in
[0,1)
. Since

\displaystyle x-c\in[0,1)\iff x\in[c,c+1)\iff c=\lfloor x\rfloor,

\displaystyle 0\leqslant c\leqslant e-1\iff x\in[0,e),

then we have

\sum_{c=0}^{e-1}f(x-c)=f(x-\lfloor x\rfloor)\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,e)$}}(x)=f(\{x\})\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,e)$}}(x)

2)

For the second term, we calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{c=0}^{e-1}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[1,+\infty)$}}(x-c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{e-1}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[c,+\infty)$}}(x-1)=\sum_{c=1}^{e}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[c,+\infty)$}}(x)=\#\{c\in\mathbb{Z}\,|\,1\leqslant i\leqslant e,\ c\leqslant x\}

\displaystyle=\lfloor x\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,e)$}}(x)+e\,\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[e,+\infty)$}}(x).

3)

Also for the second term, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\sum_{a=1}^{\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor}1=\#\left\{\frac{b}{n_{j}}+\frac{a}{w_{j}}<1\,\middle|\,1\leq b\leq n_{j}-1,\,1\leq a\leq w_{j}-1\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}.

Using these calculations, we continue:

\displaystyle\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\left(\lfloor e_{j}r\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[0,e_{j})$}}(e_{j}r)+e_{j}\,\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[e_{j},+\infty)$}}(e_{j}r)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\lfloor w_{j}\left(1-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\rfloor\lfloor e_{j}r\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left(w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)-\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\right)\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}(e_{j}r-\{e_{j}r\}).

Now, we use the following facts:

1)

For the first term, we calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{n-1}\left(x-\frac{b}{n}\right)\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n},1\right)$}}(x)

\displaystyle=\lfloor nx\rfloor x-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{b=1}^{n-1}b\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$[b,n)$}}(nx)=\lfloor nx\rfloor x-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{b=0}^{\lfloor nx\rfloor}b

\displaystyle=\lfloor nx\rfloor x-\frac{1}{n}\frac{(\lfloor nx\rfloor+1)\lfloor nx\rfloor}{2}=\lfloor nx\rfloor\left(x-\frac{\lfloor nx\rfloor+1}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=(nx-\{nx\})\left(\frac{x}{2}-\frac{1-\{nx\}}{2n}\right)=\frac{n}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{2n}\{nx\}(1-\{nx\}).

2)

Also for the first term, we use

\displaystyle\{n_{j}\{e_{j}r\}\}=n_{j}\{e_{j}r\}-\lfloor n_{j}e_{j}r-n_{j}\lfloor e_{j}r\rfloor\rfloor=n_{j}e_{j}r-\lfloor n_{j}e_{j}r\rfloor=\{n_{j}e_{j}r\}=\{e_{j-1}r\}.

3)

For the second term,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}e_{j}=\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}.

With this, we finish our calculations:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}w_{j}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}(e_{j}r-\{e_{j}r\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}n_{j}}{2}\{e_{j}r\}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}}{2}\{e_{j}r\}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}}{2n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}(e_{j}r-\{e_{j}r\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{(n_{j}-1)(w_{j}-1)}{2}e_{j}r-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{n_{j}w_{j}-n_{j}-w_{j}+1}{2}\{e_{j}r\}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}n_{j}}{2}\{e_{j}r\}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}}{2}\{e_{j}r\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{w_{j}}{2n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}r+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{n_{j}-1}{2}\{e_{j}r\}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{n_{j}w_{j}}{2}\{e_{j}r\}(1-\{e_{j}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\left(\frac{n_{j-1}w_{j-1}}{2}+\frac{l_{j}}{2n_{j}}\right)\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}r+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{n_{j}-1}{2}\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{n_{g}w_{g}}{2}\{e_{g}r\}(1-\{e_{g}r\})+\frac{n_{0}w_{0}}{2}\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{2n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{g}-n_{g}w_{g}}{2}r+\frac{n_{g}w_{g}}{2}r^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{n_{j}-1}{2}\{e_{j}r\}+\frac{1}{2}\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{2n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right).

This finishes the proof of Theorem
3.0.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.0.2

We want to calculate

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\frac{\mu}{2}r^{2}-\#\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\}\right).

\displaystyle 2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right).

This finishes the proof of Theorem
3.0.2
.

4.
Characterization of the limit distribution for irreducible plane curves

In this section we will prove the main result of the paper which is the following:

Theorem 4.0.1
.

Let
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
be a sequence of irreducible plane curves. Then, the distribution of Hodge spectral exponents of
f^{(i)}
has limit

\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i)}}(s)=N_{2}(s)

if, and only if,

\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\beta_{g}^{(i)}}{\mu^{(i)}}=0,

where
\frac{\beta_{g}^{(i)}}{e_{0}^{(i)}}

is the last characteristic exponent of
f^{(i)}
and
\mu^{(i)}
is the Milnor number of
f^{(i)}
.

Proof.

Let
f
define an irreducible plane curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
.
We want to characterize all sequences of irreducible plane curves such that

\lim D_{f}(s)=N_{2}(s)
or equivalently

\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0
. Recall that Theorem
3.0.2
states:

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\biggl(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\biggr)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 42.67912pt-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)\right)

with

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}.

We will see in forthcoming Theorem
5.0.1
that
\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0
implies
\lct(f)\rightarrow 0
, so in the following we will assume this to be the case.
This is equivalent, by Corollary
2.2.3
, to assuming
e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty
. By Lemma
2.1.7
, we have
\frac{1}{\mu}\xrightarrow{e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}0
and we also have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\right)\xrightarrow{e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}0\;,\hskip 28.45274pt\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}1\right)\xrightarrow{e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}0

and

\frac{1}{\mu}\left(2e_{0}-1\right)=\frac{2e_{0}-1}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}}\xrightarrow{e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}0.

These limits imply

\displaystyle\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0\iff e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty,\quad\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\biggr)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})\right)\rightarrow 0

since all functions of
r
are bounded between 0 and 1 (excluding signs and coefficients).
We want the limit of this expression to be 0 for
r\in[0,1)
. In particular it must be 0 for
r\in\left[\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}\right]
and, in this interval,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\frac{1}{4}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}\frac{1}{8}\leqslant\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)\geqslant\frac{3}{16}\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\biggr)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{16}\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0\implies\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0.

Furthermore, if we assume
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0
, then for all
r\in[0,1)
we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\absolutevalue{
\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^g l_j e_j\biggr) r(1-r)
-
\sum_{j=1}^g \frac{l_j}{n_j} \fract{e_{j-1} r}(1-\fract{e_{j-1} r})
}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\max\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j},\ \sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\right\}\leqslant\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\rightarrow 0.

In conclusion, we have proved

\displaystyle\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0\iff e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty,\quad\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0.

We can rewrite this expression using the definitions
e_{0}=n_{g}\cdots n_{1},\quad e_{i}=n_{g}\cdots n_{i+1}
which imply
\frac{e_{0}}{e_{i}}=n_{i}\cdots n_{1}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{g}}{e_{0}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{l_{1}}{n_{1}}+\dots+\frac{l_{i}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{i}}+\dots+\frac{l_{g}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{g}}=1+\frac{l_{1}}{\frac{e_{0}}{e_{1}}}+\dots+\frac{l_{i}}{\frac{e_{0}}{e_{i}}}+\dots+\frac{l_{g}}{\frac{e_{0}}{e_{g}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e_{0}}(e_{0}+l_{1}e_{1}+\dots+l_{i}e_{i}+\dots+l_{g}e_{g})=\frac{1}{e_{0}}\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right),

which implies
\beta_{g}=e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}.

Since we are in the case
e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty
and
\frac{e_{0}}{\mu}\xrightarrow{e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}0
, the previous equivalence can be written as

\displaystyle\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0\iff e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty,\quad\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0.

Now assume that we have a sequence
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
with

e_{0}^{(i)}\not\rightarrow+\infty
. Then

\displaystyle e_{0}^{(i)}\not\rightarrow+\infty

\displaystyle\implies\exists\;\text{subsequence with $e_{0}^{(i)}$ constant}

\displaystyle\implies\exists\;\text{subsequence $\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\in I}$ with $g^{(i)},e_{0}^{(i)},\dots,e_{g}^{(i)}$ constant}.

For this subsequence,

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{g}^{(i)}}{\mu^{(i)}}=\frac{e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}}\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)}\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}\rightarrow+\infty

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists k\in\{1,\dots,g\}\quad\frac{l_{k}^{(i)}e_{k}(e_{k-1}-1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}\rightarrow+\infty

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists\;k\in\{1,\dots,g\}\quad\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}{l_{k}^{(i)}}\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\exists\;k\in\{1,\dots,g\}\colon\forall j\in\{1,\dots,g\}\quad\frac{l_{j}^{(i)}e_{j}}{l_{k}^{(i)}}\rightarrow 0\,.

But since
\frac{l_{k}^{(i)}e_{k}}{l_{k}^{(i)}}\not\rightarrow 0
, we can not have
\frac{\beta_{g}^{(i)}}{\mu^{(i)}}\rightarrow 0
for the subsequence and thus it can not be true for the full sequence either. This proves

\displaystyle e_{0}\not\rightarrow+\infty\implies\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}\not\rightarrow 0,\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm and\;thus}\hskip 28.45274pt\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0\implies e_{0}\rightarrow+\infty.

In conclusion,

\displaystyle\lim\phi_{f}(r)=0\iff\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0.

∎

The following corollary is a generalization of the result of K. Saito presented in Proposition
1.0.6
.

Corollary 4.0.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}
be an irreducible plane curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
. Then, taking a sequence of such functions with the limit
n_{k}\rightarrow+\infty
(keeping all other
n_{j}
and
l_{j}
fixed), one has

\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i)}}(s)=N_{2}(s).

Proof.

Consider
n_{k}\rightarrow+\infty
,
g
fixed,
n_{j}
fixed for
j\neq k
and
l_{j}
fixed
\forall j
. Then,

\displaystyle\beta_{g}=e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}=O(1)n_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}O(1)n_{k}+\sum_{j=k}^{g}O(1)=O(n_{k}),

where we use the usual asymptotic
big O notation
, and

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}(e_{j-1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}O(n_{k}^{2})+O(n_{k})+\sum_{j=k+1}^{g}O(1)+O(n_{k}^{2})=O(n_{k}^{2}).

Here we used
e_{i}=n_{g}\cdots n_{i+1}
and thus the assymptotic behaviour when
n_{k}\rightarrow+\infty
is

O(1)n_{k}
for
i<k
and
O(1)
otherwise. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}=O\left(\frac{n_{k}}{n_{k}^{2}}\right)\implies\frac{\beta_{g}}{\mu}\rightarrow 0.

This satisfies the hypothesis for Theorem
4.0.1
, so
we get the desired result.
∎

5.
On the condition
\lct\rightarrow 0

The examples provided by K. Saito
[
Sai83a
]
and Almirón and Schulze
[
AS22
]
satisfy that the log-canonical thresholds of the sequence of hypersurfaces tend to zero.
In this section we will prove that this is a necessary condition but it is not sufficient for the discrete distribution converging to the continuous distribution.

Theorem 5.0.1
.

Let
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
be a sequence of hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with an isolated singular point at the origin. Then

\displaystyle\lct\left(f^{(i)}\right)\not\rightarrow 0\implies\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i)}}(s)\neq N_{n+1}(s).

Proof.

We consider a sequence of hypersurfaces
\left(f^{(i)}\right)_{i\geqslant 0}
with
\lct\left(f^{(i)}\right)\not\rightarrow 0
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
\left(f^{(i_{j})}\right)_{j\geqslant 0}
for some
(i_{j})_{j\geqslant 0}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\varepsilon\leqslant\lct\left(f^{(i_{j})}\right)\ \forall j
. Thus,

0<\varepsilon\leqslant\lct\left(f^{(i_{j})}\right)\leqslant\alpha_{k}^{(i_{j})}\quad\forall j,k,

so there are no Hodge spectral exponents of any
f^{(i_{j})}
in the interval
(0,\varepsilon)
. In terms of the distribution,

\lim_{j\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i_{j})}}(s)=0\quad\text{for }s\in(0,\varepsilon).

However, the usual limit distribution satisfies
N_{n+1}(s)>0\quad\text{for }s\in(0,\varepsilon).

Therefore,

\lim_{j\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i_{j})}}(s)\neq N_{n+1}(s)

and thus

\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i)}}(s)\neq N_{n+1}(s).

or equivalently,

\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}\mbox{\Large$\chi$}_{f^{(i)}}(T)\neq\left(\frac{T-1}{\log{T}}\right)^{n+1}=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\left\{N_{n+1}(s)\right\}\mathclose{}(\tau).

∎

We will also prove

\lct\!\left(f^{(i)}\right)\rightarrow 0\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\implies}}}\lim_{i\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f^{(i)}}(s)=N_{n+1}(s).

by means of the following counterexample:

Lemma 5.0.2
.

Consider a sequence of irreducible plane curves with Puiseux pairs
(2k,1),(2,2k^{3}+1)
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then

\lct(f)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2k}+\frac{1}{2k+1}\right)\rightarrow 0.

However, the limit of the distribution of Hodge spectral exponents is

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f}(s)

\displaystyle=\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right]$}}(s)\neq N_{2}(s).

Proof.

Consider a sequence of irreducible plane curves with Puiseux pairs
(2k,1),\,(2,2k^{3}+1)
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
(
k\rightarrow+\infty
). We have the following numerical invariants:

\displaystyle e_{2}=1,\qquad e_{1}=2,\qquad e_{0}=4k,

and from Lemma
2.1.7

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=l_{1}e_{1}(e_{0}-1)+l_{2}e_{2}(e_{1}-1)+(e_{0}-1)^{2}=2k^{3}+16k^{2}.

Now, recall the expression of
\phi_{f}(r)
from Theorem
3.0.2
:

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\biggl(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\biggr)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip 42.67912pt-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)\right).

Then:

1)

The limit of the first term is
r(1-r)
times

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\biggl(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu}\biggl(8k-1+2+(2k^{3}+1)\biggr)=\frac{2k^{3}+8k+2}{4k^{3}+32k^{2}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{2}.

2)

The limit of the second term is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{- \sum_{j=1}^g (n_j -1) \fract{e_j r}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^g (n_j -1)}=\frac{\absolutevalue{2k-1+2-1}}{4k^{3}+32k^{2}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}0.

3)

The limit of the third term is

\displaystyle\frac{\absolutevalue{- \fract{e_0 r} (1 - \fract{e_0 r})}}{2\mu}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\absolutevalue{1}}{2\mu}=\frac{1}{4k^{3}+32k^{2}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}0.

4)

For the limit of the fourth term we separate each summand:

•

For
j=1
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\frac{l_1}{n_1} \fract{e_0 r}(1-\fract{e_0 r}) }

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\frac{l_1}{n_1}}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2k}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}0.

•

For
j=2
the coefficient is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\frac{l_{2}}{n_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\frac{2k^{3}+1}{2}\right)=\frac{2k^{3}+1}{8k^{3}+64k^{2}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{4}.

Thus the fourth term has limit

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})\right)\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{4}\{e_{1}r\}(1-\{e_{1}r\})=\frac{1}{4}\{2r\}(1-\{2r\}).

5)

The limit of the fifth term is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^g \sum_{b=1}^{n_j-1} 2 \fract*{w_j\left(\fract{e_j r} - \frac{b}{n_j}\right)} \I{\left[\frac{b}{n_j},1\right)}\!\left(\fract{e_j r}\right)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\mu}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^g \sum_{b=1}^{n_j-1} 2}=\frac{1}{\mu}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^g (n_j -1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\absolutevalue{2k-1+2-1}}{2k^{3}+16k^{2}}\xrightarrow{k\rightarrow+\infty}0.

In total, for
r\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r(1-r)-\frac{1}{4}\{2r\}(1-\{2r\}).

By the definition of
\phi_{f}(r)
,

\displaystyle\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}N_{2}(s)-D_{f}^{<1}(s)\differential{s},

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}N_{2}(s)-\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f}^{<1}(s)\right)\differential{s}.

Since in this case
\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{f}(r)
is a continuous function and differentiable almost everywhere, we can recover the corresponding distribution as follows. Let
s\in[0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\derivative{s}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{f}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=N_{2}(s)-\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f}^{<1}(s)\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f}^{<1}(s)

\displaystyle=N_{2}(s)-\derivative{s}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{f}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=s-\derivative{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}s(1-s)-\frac{1}{4}\{2s\}(1-\{2s\})\right)

\displaystyle=s-\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-2s)-\frac{1}{4}(2-4\{2s\})\right)

\displaystyle=2s-\{2s\}=\lfloor 2s\rfloor=\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right)$}}(s).

By the symmetry of the Hodge spectral exponents, for any
s

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}D_{f}(s)=\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right]$}}(s).

∎

6.
Dominating values for irreducible plane curves

In this section we will give partial answers to K. Saito’s Question
1
on the dominating values for the case of irreducible plane curves. To such purpose we will use Theorem
3.0.2
which states

\displaystyle 2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right).

We will give two lower bounds to this function and calculate the sets where the bound is positive. This way we obtain two intervals where
\phi_{f}
is positive.
The following picture illustrates the idea.

Figure 6.0.1.
Example of the cumulative difference function

is positive and negative respectively.
These intervals are obtained directly from the smallest and largest Hodge spectral exponents.

Theorem 6.0.1
.

Let
f
be an irreducible plane curve with Puiseux pairs
(n_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(n_{g},l_{g})
. Then,

1)

A set of dominating values is given by the interval

r\in\left(\frac{\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)-\sqrt{D_{1}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)},\ \frac{\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)+\sqrt{D_{1}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)}\right)

with

D_{1}=\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)^{2}-4\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)>0.

2)

A set of dominating values is given by the interval

r\in\left(\frac{\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)-\sqrt{D_{2}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)},\ \frac{\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)+\sqrt{D_{2}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)}\right)

with

3)

We have that the leftmost interval of
(0,1)

\displaystyle r\in\left(0,\lct(f)\right)=\left(0,\ \frac{1}{e_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}+l_{1}}\right)\right)

is a set of dominating values, whereas the rightmost interval

\displaystyle r\in\left[1-\frac{1}{n_{g}w_{g}},1\right)

satisfies
\phi_{f}(r)<0
.

Remark 6.0.2
.

proved that the log-canonical threshold of an irreducible plane curve is a dominating value except for the cases where the curve has semigroup
(2,3)
or
(2,5)
.

An immediate consequence is the following:

Corollary 6.0.3
.

For any
r
in the intervals described in parts
1)
and

of Theorem

r^{2}>\frac{2}{\mu}\#\left\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant r\right\}.

In the course of the proof of Theorem

we will obtain an alternative proof of the result Tomari
[

]
but only for irreducible curves.

Corollary 6.0.4
.

Let
f
define an irreducible plane curve. Then
\frac{1}{2}
is a dominating value and thus

\#\left\{\alpha_{i}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\right\}<\frac{\mu}{8}.

6.0.1.
Interval of dominating values using the first lower bound of
\phi_{f}

We will now give a lower bound of
\phi_{f}(r)
, from which we will obtain an interval of dominating values. We use the following bounds:
\{e_{g}r\}=r
,
\{e_{j}r\}\leqslant 1
,
\{e_{j}r\}(1-\{e_{j}r\})\leqslant\frac{1}{4}
and

\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)\geqslant 0.

Applying these bounds to the previous expression of
\phi_{f}(r)
we get

\displaystyle 2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-(n_{g}-1)r-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)-\frac{1}{4}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r^{2}+\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon p_{1}(r).

Therefore,
2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)\geqslant p_{1}(r).

We will now find the interval where
p_{1}(r)>0
:

Evaluating
p_{1}(r)
at
r=0
,

\displaystyle p_{1}(0)=-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)<-\left(0+\frac{1}{4}+0\right)<0.

Evaluating at
r=1
,

\displaystyle p_{1}(1)

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)+\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-n_{g})-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)<0.

Evaluating at
r=\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle p_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\frac{1}{4}+\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\frac{1}{2}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(-2e_{0}+1-\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}+4e_{0}-2n_{g}+2\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}4(n_{j}-1)-1-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(2e_{0}-2n_{g}-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}4(n_{j}-1)+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}\left(e_{j}-\frac{1}{n_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{0}-n_{g}-\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}2n_{j}+2(g-1)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{g}\cdots n_{1}-n_{g}-2n_{g-1}-\dots-2n_{1}+2(g-1)\right).

This last term is greater or equal than zero since:

•

If
g>1
, then

\displaystyle n_{g}\cdots n_{1}-n_{g}-2n_{g-1}-\dots-2n_{1}+2(g-1)

\displaystyle\geqslant(n_{g}-2)n_{g-1}\cdots n_{1}-n_{g}+2n_{g-1}\cdots n_{1}-2n_{g-1}-\dots-2n_{1}+2

\displaystyle\geqslant(n_{g}-2)\cdot 2-n_{g}+2(n_{g-1}\cdots n_{1}-n_{g-1}-\dots-n_{1})+2

\displaystyle\geqslant 2n_{g}-4-n_{g}+0+2=n_{g}-2\geqslant 0.

•

If
g=1
, then

\displaystyle n_{g}\cdots n_{1}-n_{g}-2n_{g-1}-\dots-2n_{1}+2(g-1)=n_{1}-n_{1}+0=0.

Therefore,

p_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)>0.

Remark 6.0.5
.

This proves Corollary
6.0.4
.

Since
p_{1}(r)
is a quadratic function,
p_{1}(0)<0
,
p_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)>0
and
p_{1}(1)<0
, we conclude that the graph of
p_{1}(r)
is an inverted parabola with a root in
\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and a root in
\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. More concretely,
p_{1}(r)>0
and therefore
\phi_{f}(r)>0
in the interval

r\in\left(\frac{\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)-\sqrt{D_{1}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)},\frac{\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)+\sqrt{D_{1}}}{2\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)}\right)

with

D_{1}=\left(2e_{0}-n_{g}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)^{2}-4\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}(n_{j}-1)+\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)>0.

6.0.2.
Interval of dominating values using the second lower bound of
\phi_{f}

We will now give a different lower bound of
\phi_{f}(r)
, from which we will obtain another interval of dominating values.
If instead of bounding
\{e_{j}r\}\leqslant 1
we bound it by
\{e_{j}r\}\leqslant e_{j}r
, we will get a better bound for small values of
r
(at least for
r<\frac{1}{e_{1}}

\displaystyle\{e_{j}r\}

\displaystyle\leqslant e_{j}r,

\displaystyle\{e_{j}r\}(1-\{e_{j}r\})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4},

\displaystyle\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

Then we get

\displaystyle 2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)\{e_{j}r\}-\{e_{0}r\}(1-\{e_{0}r\})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\{e_{j-1}r\}(1-\{e_{j-1}r\})+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\sum_{b=1}^{n_{j}-1}2\left\{w_{j}\left(\{e_{j}r\}-\frac{b}{n_{j}}\right)\right\}\mbox{1}_{\mbox{$\left[\frac{b}{n_{j}},1\right)$}}\!\left(\{e_{j}r\}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r(1-r)-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}r-\frac{1}{4}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r^{2}+\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{g}(n_{j}-1)e_{j}\right)r-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r^{2}+\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)r-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon p_{2}(r).

Therefore,
2\mu\,\phi_{f}(r)\geqslant p_{2}(r).

We will now find the interval where
p_{2}(r)>0
:

Evaluating
p_{2}(r)
at
r=0
,

\displaystyle p_{2}(0)

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)<0.

Evaluating at
r=1

\displaystyle p_{2}(1)

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)+\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=-(e_{0}-1)-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)<0.

Evaluating at
r=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle p_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(2e_{0}-1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\frac{1}{4}+\left(e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}\right)\frac{1}{2}-\left(\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{4n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(-2e_{0}+1-\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}+2e_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}e_{j}-1-\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{l_{j}}{n_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}l_{j}\left(e_{j}-\frac{1}{n_{j}}\right)\right)>0.

6.0.3.
Intervals of constant sign at both ends of
[0,1)

These intervals come directly from the first and the last Hodge spectral exponents
in the set given in Corollary
2.2.3
and will not use the explicit expression for
\phi_{f}(r)
.
For the leftmost interval, the log-canonical threshold is

\alpha_{1}=\lct(f)=\frac{1}{e_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}+l_{1}}\right).

Thus,
\#\{\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{1}\}=0
and
\phi_{f}(r)>0

For the rightmost interval, the largest Hodge spectral exponent is

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu/2}=1-\frac{1}{e_{g-1}w_{g}}=1-\frac{1}{n_{g}w_{g}}

and thus we have
\phi_{f}(r)<0
in the interval
