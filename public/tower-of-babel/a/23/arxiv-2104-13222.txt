Universality vs Genericity and C 4 C_{4} -free graphs Thanks: Funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) under Germany’s Excellence Strategy EXC 2044–390685587, Mathematics Münster: Dynamics–Geometry–Structure and by CRC 1442 Geometry: Deformations and Rigidity.
Aristotelis Panagiotopoulos, Katrin Tent

Abstract
. We show that the existence of a universal structure implies the existence of a generic structure for any approximable class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of countable structures. We also show that the converse is not true. As a consequence, we provide several new examples of weak Fraïssé classes of finite graphs. Finally, we show that the class of all countable C 4 C_{4} -free graphs does not contain a generic structure, strengthening a result of A. Hajnal and J. Pach.

1.
Introduction

An
approximating class
is any family
\mathcal{K}
of finitely generated
\mathcal{L}
-structures which is countable up to isomorphism and which has the
Hereditary Property
(HP) and the
Extension Property
(EP):

•

(HP) if
B\in\mathcal{K}
,
A
embeds in
B
, and
A
is finitely generated, then
A\in\mathcal{K}
;

•

(EP) every
A\in\mathcal{K}
admits a non-surjective embedding to some
B\in\mathcal{K}
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an approximating class. A class
\mathcal{C}
of countable
\mathcal{L}
-structures is
approximable
by
\mathcal{K}
if
\mathcal{C}
consists precisely of all
\mathcal{L}
-structures
N
on universe
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
that satisfy the following properties:

•

every finitely generated substructure
A
of
N
is in
\mathcal{K}
;

•

N
is not finitely generated.

In this case, we write
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{Age}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathcal{C}=\lim(\mathcal{K})
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
approximable
if
\mathcal{C}=\lim(\mathcal{K})
for some approximating class
\mathcal{K}
.
Standard examples of approximable classes are classes consisting of all countably infinite graphs that omit all graphs which are included in some fixed countable collection
\mathcal{F}
of “forbidden” finite graphs.

In what follows, we will always assume that
\mathcal{C}
is approximable and hence each structure in
\mathcal{C}
has universe
\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
has a
universal element

U\in\mathcal{C}
, if every
N\in\mathcal{C}
embeds in
U
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
has a
generic element

G\in\mathcal{C}
, if in the Baire space
\mathcal{C}
(see Section
2
) the following set is comeager:

\{N\in\mathcal{C}\;\colon N\text{ is isomorphic to }G\}.

Several approximable classes
\mathcal{C}
admit a structure
M\in\mathcal{C}
that is both universal and generic. For example, the Rado graph is both a universal and a generic element of the class of all countable graphs; see
[
24
,
25
]
.
More generally, if
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{Age}(\mathcal{C})
is a Fraïssé class (see Section
2
), then
\mathcal{C}
contains a canonical structure
M\in\mathcal{C}
—known as the Fraïssé limit of
\mathcal{K}
—which is both universal and generic. However, it is possible for an approximable class
\mathcal{C}
to admit a universal
U\in\mathcal{C}
that is not generic and a generic
G\in\mathcal{C}
that is not universal; see Example
4
. It is therefore natural to ask whether there is an example of an approximable class which admits a universal element but does not admit a generic element and similarly whether there is an approximable class which admits a generic element but does not admit a universal element. We settle this with the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an approximable class of
\mathcal{L}
-structures. If

\mathcal{C}
admits a universal element, then
\mathcal{C}
admits a generic element. The converse is not true.

As a consequence of Theorem
1
, we get several new examples of approximable classes of countable graphs which admit a generic element. Notice that, by the Lachlan-Woodrow classification theorem
[
18
]
, the Fraïssé construction—in its original form—provides very few examples of approximable classes of graphs which admit a generic element. While we now know that a certain weak version of the Fraïssé construction is essentially the only way to produce generic elements
[
15
,
17
,
19
,
21
]
, very few “weak Fraïssé classes” of graphs have been explicitly constructed
[
16
]
. On the other hand,
there is a vast literature on approximable classes
\mathcal{C}
of graphs which admit a universal element
U\in\mathcal{C}
; e.g.,
[
14
,
12
,
13
,
7
,
5
,
4
]
. By Theorem
1
, these classes also admit a generic element
G\in\mathcal{C}
. We collect some examples in Section

.

Finally, there are several approximable classes
\mathcal{C}
for which it has been established that they do not admit a universal element. A natural question stemming from Theorem
1
is whether results about non-existence of universal elements can be strengthened to non-existence of generic elements. Here we strengthen a result of Hajnal and Pach on the non-existence of a universal countable

-free graph

. Recall that a graph

is

The class of all countable

-free graphs admits no generic element.

By Theorem

does not admit a generic element is a priori more difficult than establishing that

does not admit a universal element. The proof of Theorem

). Whenever

of

and

over

. We say that

and

amalgamate over

. We say that

. We say that

there is an embedding

so that for every two embeddings

and

.
Notice that:

\mathcal{K}\text{ has AP}\implies\mathcal{K}\text{ has CAP}\implies\mathcal{K}\text{ has WAP}

Assume that
\mathcal{K}
is an approximating class, as defined in the introduction.
Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{lim}(\mathcal{K})
be the associated approximable class and notice that, by EP,

. As usual, we view

as a topological space with basic open sets of the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{A}

\displaystyle=\{N\in\mathcal{C}\colon\text{ the restriction of }N\text{ to }\mathrm{dom}(A)\text{ is equal to }A\}

\displaystyle=\{N\in\mathcal{C}\colon A\leq N\},

where

ranges over all elements of
\mathcal{K}
with

; see e.g.,
[

While

fails in general to be a Polish space (see
[

is always a Baire space. That is, the intersection of countably many comeager subsets of

; see

Proposition 3
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an approximable class, then
\mathcal{C}
is a Baire space.

Proof.

By
[
20
, Theorem 8.4]
is suffices to show that
\mathcal{C}
admits a complete metric compatible with the topology on
\mathcal{C}
. For any
N,M\in\mathcal{C}
let
\delta(N,M)\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be the set of all
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that the identity map
\mathrm{id}_{k}\colon\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\to\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
extends to an isomorphism between the substructures
\langle\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\rangle_{M}
of
M
and
\langle\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}\rangle_{N}
of
N
generated by
\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
. We set

d_{\mathcal{C}}(M,N):=\mathrm{inf}\{1/2^{k}\colon k\in\delta(N,M)\}.

Here we adopt the convention that the empty structure is an
\mathcal{L}
-structure and hence
0\in\delta(N,M)
for all
M,N\in\mathcal{C}
.
It is easy to see that
d_{\mathcal{C}}
is a metric on
\mathcal{C}
that is compatible with the topology on
\mathcal{C}
. To see that
d_{\mathcal{C}}
is complete let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
d_{\mathcal{C}}
-Cauchy sequence. By passing to a subsequence we may assume that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
m\geq n
, the substructure of
M_{n}
generated by
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
is equal to the substructure of
M_{m}
generated by
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. Call this common substructure
A_{n}
and let
M
be the
\mathcal{L}
-structure that is the union of the increasing sequence

A_{0}\leq A_{1}\leq A_{2}\leq\cdots\leq A_{n}\leq\cdots

Then
M\in\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, it is clear that
\mathrm{dom}(M)=\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, if
A
is some finitely generated structure with
A\leq M
then there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\mathrm{dom}(A_{n})
contains the generators of

and hence
A\leq A_{n}
. By HP we have that
A\in\mathcal{K}
. Moreover, since
M_{n}
is not finitely generated we have that

. Hence

is not finitely generated either. It is finally clear that
M_{n}\to M
.
∎

Since
\mathcal{C}
is a non-empty Baire space, the
\sigma

\mathcal{C}
is non-trivial, i.e., the empty set is not comeager in
\mathcal{C}
; see
[

\mathcal{C}
carries a useful intrinsic notion of “largeness” which allows one to talk about properties of the “generic” structure in the same fashion that a probability measure on
\mathcal{C}
would allow one to talk about properties of the“random” structure of
\mathcal{C}
. The following definitions make this precise.
A property
\mathcal{P}
of structures of
\mathcal{C}
is a
generic property
if the set of all
N\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying
\mathcal{P}
forms a comeager set. We say that
\mathcal{C}

has a generic element
if there is
G\in\mathcal{C}
so that

\{N\in\mathcal{C}\;\colon N\text{ is isomorphic to }G\}

is a comeager subset of
\mathcal{C}
. In this case, we say that
G

is a generic element of

\mathcal{C}
.

Fact 1
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is approximable then there exists at most one generic element of
\mathcal{C}
up to isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
both be generic elements of
\mathcal{C}
.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is a Baire space, the set

\mathcal{D}:=\{N\in\mathcal{C}\colon N\text{ is isomorphic to }G_{1}\}\cap\{N\in\mathcal{C}\colon N\text{ is isomorphic to }G_{2}\}

is dense in
\mathcal{C}
and hence non-empty—recall that by EP we have that
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
. Hence,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic, since they are both isomorphic to a structure in
\mathcal{D}
.
∎

3.
Universality vs Genericity

We first show that the existence of a universal
U\in\mathcal{C}
implies the existence of a generic element
G\in\mathcal{C}
. By Fact
2
it suffices to show the following:

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an approximable class of
\mathcal{L}
-structures and let
\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{Age}(\mathcal{C})
. If
\mathcal{C}
admits a universal element then
\mathcal{K}
has JEP and WAP.

Proof.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
. Since
\mathcal{K}
has EP one can easily build
M,N\in\mathcal{C}
with
A\leq M
and
B\leq N
. Indeed using EP we can inductively define strictly increasing sequences:

\displaystyle A=A_{0}\lneq A_{1}\lneq A_{2}\lneq\cdots

\displaystyle B=B_{0}\lneq B_{1}\lneq B_{2}\lneq\cdots

of structures in
\mathcal{K}
. Let
M
and
N
be the union of the sequences
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
respectively. But then
M
and
N
are not finitely generated since if, say
F\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(M)
is finite, then
F\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(A_{n})
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and hence
\langle F\rangle_{M}\leq A_{n}
.
By renaming the domains of
M,N
if necessary, we may assume that
M,N\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
M,N
embed in the universal element
U
we may assume that
A,B\leq U
. Consider the substructure
C:=\langle\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B)\rangle_{U}
of
U
that is generated by
\mathrm{dom}(A)\cup\mathrm{dom}(B)
. Notice that
C\in\mathcal{K}
and
A,B\leq C
. Hence,
\mathcal{K}
has JEP.

We now recall some standard pieces of notation which will be used for showing WAP. As usual we identify
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. Let
2^{\mathbb{N}}
and

2^{<\mathbb{N}}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{n}
be the sets of all infinite and finite sequences, respectively, with values in
\{0,1\}
