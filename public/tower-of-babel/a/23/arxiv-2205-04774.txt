Automorphism Shuffles for Graphs and Hypergraphs and Its Applications
Kazumasa Shinagawa and Kengo Miyamoto

Abstract
. In card-based cryptography, a deck of physical cards is used to achieve secure computation. A shuffle, which randomly permutes a card-sequence along with some probability distribution, ensures the security of a card-based protocol. The authors proposed a new class of shuffles called graph shuffles, which randomly permutes a card-sequence by an automorphism of a directed graph (New Generation Computing 2022). For a directed graph G G with n n vertices and m m edges, such a shuffle could be implemented with pile-scramble shuffles with 2 ​ ( n + m ) 2(n+m) cards. In this paper, we study graph shuffles and give an implementation, an application, and a slight generalization of them. First, we propose a new protocol for graph shuffles with 2 ​ n + m 2n+m cards. Second, as a new application of graph shuffles, we show that any cyclic group shuffle, which is a shuffle over a cyclic group, is a graph shuffle associated with some graph. Third, we define a hypergraph shuffle, which is a shuffle by an automorphism of a hypergraph, and show that any hypergraph shuffle can also be implemented with pile-scramble shuffles.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Card-based cryptography
is one of active research areas in cryptography.
It enables secure computation by using a deck of physical cards.
In card-based protocols, a deck of physical cards is used to achieve secure computation by hands.
Thus, it is easy to understand the correctness and the security of protocols, even for non-experts who are unfamiliar with cryptography.
In fact, there are some reports on an educational application of card-based cryptography for teaching cryptography (e.g., Cornell University
[
7
]
, University of Waterloo
[
2
]
, Tohoku University
[
9

[
13

In 2014, Mizuki and Shizuya
[
10

defined a mathematical model of card-based cryptography.
On the one hand, Mizuki–Shizuya model helps to find some new protocols and to prove some impossibility results (e.g.,
[
6

On the other hand, it allows some operations which are not clear how to implement physically.
In particular, the Mizuki–Shizuya model allows the use of shuffles where it is non-trivial to implement physically.
It is undesirable since card-based cryptography is easy to perform and easy to understand visually.

Let

A

n

When we consider

It is said to be

The uniform closed shuffle over

for the uniform distribution
\mathcal{F}
.

Given a shuffle
(\mathsf{shuffle},\Pi,\mathcal{F})

In this paper, we study the class of
graph shuffles
(see Subsection
2.3

[
3
]
, random bisection cuts
[

]
, pile-shifting shuffles
[

[

1.2.
Related Work

Koch and Walzer
[
5
]
showed that any uniform closed shuffle
(\mathsf{shuffle},\Pi)
for any group
\Pi

random cuts
.
Although it is worthwhile to show how to implement an arbitrary uniform closed shuffle, it requires many operations (at least the size of the group
\Pi

On the other hand, our protocol requires a small number of operations.

Saito, Miyahara, Abe, Mizuki, and Shizuya

Although it is worthwhile to show that every shuffle can be implemented theoretically, it requires many cards (at least

On the other hand, our protocol requires a relatively small number of cards.

2.1.
Pile-scramble shuffles

A
pile-scramble shuffle
is a uniform closed shuffle that randomly permutes
n
piles of
m

Suppose that we have the following card-sequence:

\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{1,1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{1,m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[1]}\,\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{2,1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{2,m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[2]}\,\cdots\,\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{n,1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{n,m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[n]}~.

(n,m)
-pile-scramble shuffle

\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{1},1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{1},m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[\alpha_{1}]}\,\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{2},1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{2},m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[\alpha_{2}]}\,\cdots\,\underbrace{\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{n},1}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}\,\cdots\,\underset{\mathsf{x}_{\alpha_{n},m}}{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}}_{\mathsf{pile}[\alpha_{n}]}~,

where
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is
\pi(1,2,\ldots,n)
for some
\pi\in S_{n}
chosen uniformly at random.

2.3.
Graph Shuffles

First, we recall some fundamentals from graph theory; for details, see

]
.

A directed graph
is a quadruple
G=(V_{G},E_{G},s_{G},t_{G})
consisting of two sets:
V_{G}
(whose elements are called vertices) and
E_{G}
(whose elements are called directed edges), and two maps

which associates to its

s_{G}(\alpha)\in V_{G}
and its

t(\alpha)\in V_{G}

A directed edge
\alpha

A directed graph

and

For a vertex

and

and

.
The cardinality of

and

, denoted by
\mathsf{out}(v)
and

, are called

and

, respectively.
We remark that any undirected graph is regarded as a directed graph by changing each undirected edge to a 2-cycle

Let
G

be two directed graphs.
A pair of maps

consisting of
f_{0}:V_{G}\to V_{G^{\prime}}
and

is a

and

are bijective,

.
In particular, an isomorphism between

of

We denote by

the set of all isomorphisms between

G^{\prime}
, and we write
\mathsf{Iso}_{0}(G,G^{\prime})
for all bijection

such that

.
When

The set

It is easy to check that the group structure of

Then, we regard

Graph Shuffle Protocol

Let
G
be a directed graph with

A

for

is a card-based protocol that implements a graph shuffle associated with

Given a card-sequence

\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}_{\mathsf{x}}~\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$h_{1}$}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$h_{k}$}}}_{\mathsf{h}}~\longrightarrow~\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){{\large{?}}}}}_{\mathsf{y}}~\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$h_{1}$}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$h_{k}$}}}_{\mathsf{h}}~,

3.
Our Graph Shuffle Protocol

In this section, we propose a new graph shuffle protocol for an arbitrary directed graph, which is a more efficient protocol than that of
[
8
]
in terms of the number of cards.

3.1.
Our Idea

The protocol in
[
8
]
needs
n+m
piles of two cards:
n
piles for vertices and
m
piles for edges.
Each pile
\mathsf{vertex}[i]
corresponding to a vertex
i
consists of a card representing a (randomized) vertex
\overline{\alpha_{i}}
and the
i
-th input card
\mathsf{x}_{i}
.
Each pile
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}edge}}[i\rightarrow j]
corresponding to an edge consists of cards representing a randomized edge
(\overline{\alpha_{i}},\overline{\alpha_{j}})
.
Thus, the number of cards is
2(n+m)
.

Our idea to reduce the number of cards is that we make
n
piles only
.
For each vertex, we assign a pile as follows.
A pile associated with the
i
-th vertex consists of the
i
-th input card
\mathsf{x}_{i}
, a card representing a (randomized) vertex
\overline{\alpha_{i}}
, and
\mathsf{out}(i)
cards representing (randomized) outgoing edges from
i
.
Thus, the number of cards is
2n+\sum_{i\in V_{G}}\mathsf{out}(i)=2n+m
.

.

3.3.
Correctness and Security

For an arbitrary directed graph
G
with
V_{G}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we set

\displaystyle H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid\mathsf{in}(i)=\mathsf{in}(\pi(i))~\text{for all $1\leq i\leq n$}\},

\displaystyle H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid\mathsf{out}(i)=\mathsf{out}(\pi(i))~\text{for all $1\leq i\leq n$}\}.

Then
H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}
and
H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}
are subgroups of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
is a subgroup of
H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}
and
H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}
since every automorphism preserves the indegree and outdegree of each vertex.

Let
\mathsf{x}=(\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{n})
be an input card-sequence,
\mathsf{y}=(\mathsf{y}_{1},\mathsf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{y}_{n})
the card-sequence described in Step (8), and
\mathsf{z}=(\mathsf{z}_{1},\mathsf{z}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{z}_{n})
the corresponding output card-sequence.
We take permutations
\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma^{-1}(i)=\alpha_{i}
in Step (2) and
\tau^{-1}(i)=\beta_{i}
in Step (5), respectively.
One can easily check that
\sigma\in H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}
and
\tau\in H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}
.
Let
G^{\prime}
be the directed graph defined at Step (7).
Let
\chi=(\chi_{0},\chi_{1})\in\mathsf{Iso}(G,G^{\prime})
be an isomorphism such that
\chi_{0}=\sigma^{-1}
and
\psi=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1})\in\mathsf{Iso}(G^{\prime},G)
an isomorphism chosen at Step (8).

Since
\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
is a subgroup of
H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}\cap H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}
, we have
\sigma=\pi\sigma^{\prime}
and
\tau=\tau^{\prime}\pi^{\prime}
for some
\sigma^{\prime}\in H_{G}^{\mathsf{in}}
,
\tau^{\prime}\in H_{G}^{\mathsf{out}}
, and
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
.
Then we have

\tau\sigma=\tau^{\prime}\pi^{\prime}\pi\sigma^{\prime}=\tau^{\prime}\pi^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{\prime}

(1)

for
\pi^{\prime\prime}=\pi^{\prime}\pi\in\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
.
It implies from
\psi_{0}=\widetilde{\pi}\sigma^{\prime}
for some
\widetilde{\pi}\in\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
that

\mathsf{z}=\psi_{0}\sigma^{-1}(\mathsf{x})=\widetilde{\pi}\sigma^{\prime}(\pi\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathsf{x})=\widetilde{\pi}\pi^{-1}(\mathsf{x}).

(2)

As the distribution of
\pi^{\prime}
is uniform over
\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
, we conclude that the distributions of
\widetilde{\pi}
and
\pi^{\prime\prime}
are independent by the above equations (
1
) and (
2
).
It yields that
\widetilde{\pi}\pi^{-1}
is uniform over
\mathsf{Aut}_{0}(G)
.
Therefore, our protocol is secure and correct.

5.
Hypergraph Shuffle Protocol

In this section, we define a “hypergraph shuffle”, which is a generalization of (undirected-)graph shuffles, and propose a hypergraph shuffle protocol for an arbitrary hypergraph.

5.1.
Hypergraph Shuffles

A hypergraph
is a pair
H=(V_{H},\mathcal{E}_{H})
consisting of a set of
vertices

V_{H}
and a family of subsets of
V_{H}
.
Each element
e^{H}\in\mathcal{E}_{H}
is called
a hyperedge
.
Note that every undirected graph is a hypergraph such that any hyperedges has exactly two vertices.
In this section, we deal with finite hypergraphs, i.e., the numbers of vertices and hyperedges are finite.
For two hypergraphs
H=(V_{H},\mathcal{E}_{H})
and
H^{\prime}=(V_{H^{\prime}},\mathcal{E}_{H^{\prime}})
, where
\mathcal{E}_{H}=\{e^{H}_{1},\ldots,e^{H}_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{H^{\prime}}=\{e^{H^{\prime}}_{1},\ldots,e^{H^{\prime}}_{n}\}
, we say that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are
isomorphic
if there exist a bijective map
f:V_{H}\to V_{H^{\prime}}
and a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{|E_{H}|}
such that, for any
e^{H}_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{H}
,
f(e^{H}_{i})=e^{H^{\prime}}_{\sigma(i)}
holds.
Such a map
f
is called
an isomorphism
of hypergraphs between
H
and
H^{\prime}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{Iso}(H,H^{\prime})
the set of all isomorphisms of hypergraphs between
H
and
H^{\prime}
.
We set
\mathsf{Aut}(H)=\mathsf{Iso}(H,H^{\prime})
whose elements are called
automorphisms
of
H
.
By the definition,
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{|V_{H}|}
.

Now we define a class of hypergraph shuffles.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
H
be a hypergraph.
The
hypergraph shuffle
associated with
H
is the uniform closed shuffle over
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
.

\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$1$}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$1$}}}_{|e_{1}|}\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$2$}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$2$}}}_{|e_{2}|}\,\cdots\,\underbrace{\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$m$}}\,\cdots\,\raisebox{-3.44444pt}{\pic@makebox@{\framebox}{framed=true}(11.0,12.0){$m$}}}_{|e_{m}|}\,.

\vec{G}
implement the same shuffle.
We observe that the hypergraph shuffle protocol requires
n+2m
cards while the graph shuffle protocol requires
2(n+m)
cards.
(Note that
\vec{G}
has
2m
directed edges.)
Thus, the hypergraph shuffle protocol is more efficient than the graph shuffle protocol for undirected graphs in terms of the number of cards.

5.3.
Correctness and Security

For an arbitrary hypergraph
H=(V_{H},\mathcal{E}_{H})
with
V_{H}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{H}=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{m}\}
, we set

S_{H}=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid|E_{H}^{(i)}|=|E_{H}^{(\pi(i))}|~\text{for all $1\leq i\leq n$}\}.

Then
S_{H}
is a subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
is a subgroup of
P_{H}
since every automorphism preserves the number of adjacent edges of each vertex.

Let
\mathsf{x}=(\mathsf{x}_{1},\mathsf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{x}_{n})
be an input card-sequence,
\mathsf{y}=(\mathsf{y}_{1},\mathsf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{y}_{n})
the card-sequence described in Step (8), and
\mathsf{z}=(\mathsf{z}_{1},\mathsf{z}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{z}_{n})
the corresponding output card-sequence.
We take permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
and
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma^{-1}(i)=\alpha_{i}
in Step (2) and
\tau^{-1}(i)=\beta_{i}
in Step (5), respectively.
One can easily check that
\tau\in S_{H}
.
Let
H^{\prime}
be the hypergraph defined at Step (7).
Let
\psi\in\mathsf{Iso}(H,H^{\prime})
by an isomorphism chosen at Step (8).

Since
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
is a subgroup of
P_{H}
, we have
\tau=\tau^{\prime}\pi
for some
\tau^{\prime}\in S_{H}
and
\pi\in\mathsf{Aut}(H)
.
It implies from
\psi=\tau^{\prime}\pi^{\prime}
for some
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Aut}(H)
that

\mathsf{z}=\psi^{-1}\tau(\mathsf{x})=(\tau^{\prime}\pi^{\prime})^{-1}\tau^{\prime}\pi(\mathsf{x})=(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}\pi(\mathsf{x}).

(3)

Thanks to the randomization by
\sigma
, the opened symbols in Step (6) reveal nothing about the permutation
\pi
.
Thus, the choice of
\pi^{\prime}
is independent from
\pi
.
Since the distribution of
\pi
is uniform over
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
, it yields that
(\pi^{\prime})^{-1}\pi
is uniform over
\mathsf{Aut}(H)
.
Therefore, our protocol is secure and correct.

We remark that
