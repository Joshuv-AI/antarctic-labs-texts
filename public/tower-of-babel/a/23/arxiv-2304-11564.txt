Technical-Report: Automating Recoverability Proofs for Cyber-Physical Systems with Runtime Assurance Architectures
Vivek Nigam and Carolyn Talcott

Abstract
Cyber-physical systems (CPSes), such as autonomous vehicles, use sophisticated components like ML-based controllers. It is difficult to provide evidence about the safe functioning of such components. To overcome this problem, Runtime Assurance Architecture (RTA) solutions have been proposed. The RTA’s decision component evaluates the system’s safety risk and whenever the risk is higher than acceptable the RTA switches to a safety mode that, for example, activates a controller with strong evidence for its safe functioning. In this way, RTAs increase CPS runtime safety and resilience by recovering the system from higher to lower risk levels. The goal of this paper is to automate recovery proofs of CPSes using RTAs. We first formalize the key verification problems, namely, the decision sampling-time adequacy problem and the time-bounded recoverability problem. We then demonstrate how to automatically generate proofs for the proposed verification problems using symbolic rewriting modulo SMT. Automation is enabled by integrating the rewriting logic tool (Maude), which generates sets of non-linear constraints, with an SMT-solver (Z3) to produce proofs

Introduction

Cyber-physical systems (CPSes) are increasingly performing complex safety-critical missions in an autonomous fashion,
autonomous vehicles (AVs) being a current prime example.
Given the complexity of the environment in which such CPSes operate,
they often rely on highly complex machine learning (ML) based controllers
[
1

It has been notably hard, however, to provide safety arguments using only such ML-based components due to their functional insufficiency
[

An RTA contains a decision module that evaluates the system’s safety risk formalized as a collection of safety properties. Whenever a safety risk is higher than acceptable, the RTA moves the system to a safe state.

As illustrated by Figure

These levels of risks are formalized as properties tailored according to the operational domain of the system

Safety Properties and Levels of Risk

A key aspect of RTA mechanisms is the ability to check for the level of risk of the system, e.g., whether it is safe or not.
We formalize the notion of level of risk as a partial order on safety properties as follows:

Definition 1

An RTA safety property specification for a logical specification
{\mathsf{LS}}
is a tuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\prec_{1},{\mathsf{bad}},\vDash\rangle
where

•

\mathcal{S}=\{{\mathsf{SP}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{SP}}_{n}\}
is a finite set of safety properties;

•

\prec_{1}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
is an asymmetric binary relation over
\mathcal{S}
, where
{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}\prec_{1}{\mathsf{SP}}_{2}
denotes that the safety property
{\mathsf{SP}}_{2}
specifies a less risky condition than the safety property
{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
. Let
\prec
be the order obtained from
\prec_{1}
by applying transitivity. We assume that
\prec
is a strict pre-order
(no cycles).

•

the safety property
{\mathsf{bad}}\in\mathcal{S}
is the least element of
\prec
, specifying the condition that shall be avoided, i.e., the highest risk

•

\vDash
specifies when an instance
\mathsf{conf}
of
{\mathsf{LS}}
satisfies a property
{\mathsf{SP}}\in\mathcal{S}
, written
{\mathsf{conf}}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
.
Moreover, we assume that if
{\mathsf{conf}}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
and
{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}_{2}
or
{\mathsf{SP}}_{2}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
, then
{\mathsf{conf}}\nvDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{2}
. That is, any instance of a logical scenario can only satisfy one level of risk. We also assume that any instance of a logical scenario is at some level of risk, that is, for all instances
{\mathsf{conf}}
of
\mathsf{LS}
, there is at least one
{\mathsf{SP}}
such that
{\mathsf{conf}}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
.

Sampling Time (
\mathsf{dt}
) Adequacy

The RTA monitor has to detect when the system risk changes, and even more so when risk increases, that is, when systems satisfy properties
{\mathsf{SP}}
that are closer to
{\mathsf{bad}}
, i.e., move lower in the order
\prec
. This means that the sampling time
\mathsf{dt}
plays an important role in the correctness of a RTA system.
For example, if the sampling time is
4\times\mathsf{dt}
in Figure
1
, the RTA monitor may fail to detect elevation of risk from
\mathsf{safer}
to
\mathsf{safe}
thus not activating the trusted controller soon enough to avoid further escalation of risk.

There is a trade-off between the ability of the system to detect changes of risk and therefore its ability to quickly react to changes, and the performance requirements of monitor system in determining risk, i.e.,
\mathsf{dt}
time.
The lower the
\mathsf{dt}

Moreover, a key challenge is that
\mathsf{dt}
shall be appropriate in detecting risk changes for all instances of the ODD, i.e., all possible instances of speeds and accelerations.
Our approach is to use SMT-solvers to generate
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy proofs automatically building on ideas in
[
23
]
. Depending on the definition of
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy, the complexity of the problem can increase substantially, making automation difficult or not feasible.

We propose three alternative definitions of requirements on
\mathsf{dt}

Figure
3
illustrates these definitions.
The first definition, called

3

Intuitively, this definition states that the

As an example, the dotted evolution of the system passing through

5
RTA-based Recoverability Properties

There are many informal definitions of resilience
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
18
]
.
In the broadest sense, resilience is
“the ability of a system to adapt and respond to changes (both in the environment and internal)”
[
6
]
.
NIST
[
28
]
provides a more precise, but still informal definition of resilience and more focused on attacks: “The ability to anticipate, withstand, recover, and adapt to adverse conditions, stresses, attacks or compromises on systems that use or are enabled by cyber resources.”

Intuitively, systems, such as an autonomous vehicle in an
\mathsf{LS}
instance, implementing RTA can be shown to
exhibit a basic form of resilience we refer to as
recoverability: they detect when a specified risk level
is reached and adapt to reduce the risk.
Our goal is to formalize this intuition of RTA recoverability with precise definitions.

To accomplish this, we must model the control aspects
of the system as well as the change in physical state.
Thus we augment the semantic relation
\to_{\mathsf{dt}}

(called
\longrightarrow_{\Delta}
above)
which models the
physical aspect of behavior
with a relation
\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}
that
models the control aspect, typically sensing, analyzing, and deciding/planning. Formally, the system behavior is a set of (possibly infinite) execution traces:

{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{2}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}\cdots

where
\mathsf{dt}
is the system’s sampling time,

{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{i+1}
is a function,
and
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}^{\prime}
is an internal transition specifying the behavior of the agents in
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}
, e.g., sensing, updating local knowledge bases, and deciding which actions to take. The exact definition of this transition depends on system specification.
Since safety properties are related to the physical attributes of the system, e.g., speed, location, we normally assume that if
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
, then also
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}^{\prime}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
. For example, this is the case with the safety properties in Example
1
.
This assumption is not strictly necessary as the definitions below can be extended to cover cases when this assumption does not hold.

Definition 5

Let
Spec=\langle\mathcal{S},\prec_{1},{\mathsf{bad}},\vDash\rangle
be a safety property specification for a logical scenario
\mathsf{LS}
and
\mathsf{dt}
a sampling time, where
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\in\mathcal{S}
is the minimal acceptable safe property and
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}\in\mathcal{S}
is the acceptable safer property where
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
.
Let
t
be a positive natural number.
A system
S
is
\langle{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}},{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}},t\rangle
-recoverable if for all instances
{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}
of
{\mathsf{LS}}
and traces
\tau={\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}\cdots
such that
{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
with
{\mathsf{SP}}={\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
or
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}
:

•

For all
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{i+1}
in
\tau
, there is no decomposition
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}_{1}}{\mathsf{conf}}\to_{\mathsf{dt}-\mathsf{dt}_{1}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{i+1}
, with
0\leq\mathsf{dt}_{1}\leq\mathsf{dt}
, such that
{\mathsf{conf}}\vDash{\mathsf{bad}}
. That is, the system never reaches a configuration that satisfies
{\mathsf{bad}}
.

•

For all
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}
in
\tau
, such that
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
, then
{\mathsf{conf}}_{i+t}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}
with
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}
or
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}={\mathsf{SP}}
. That is, if the system reaches the minimal safety property, it necessarily returns to the acceptable safer property.

This definition formalizes the ability of the system to recover from a higher level of risk as illustrated by Figure
1
.
Intuitively, the property
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
specifies the highest acceptable risk before the system shall react to reduce risk, i.e., when the RTA instance triggers the safe controller, while
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
specifies the risk that shall be achieved within
t
logical ticks of the system, i.e.,
t\times\mathsf{dt}
, that is when the RTA instance resumes using the output of the primary controller.

There are some subtleties in this definition that are worth pointing out:

•

Recovery Period: The time
t
in Definition
5
specifies the time that the system has to recover. On the one hand, it avoids that the system stays in a higher risk situation, albeit still safe, for a long period of time, thus reducing the chance of safety accidents. On the other hand, if
t
is too small, it will require a stricter safe controller or not be realizable given the vehicle’s capabilities, e.g., maximum deceleration.
Therefore, the value of
t
will depend on situation under consideration. To mitigate this problem, we propose automated ways to prove recoverability in Section
6
.

•

Recoverability Smoothness: Notice that we require that
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
and not
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\prec_{1}{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
, i.e.,
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
can be multiple levels of risk safer than
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
.
By selecting appropriately these properties, e.g., setting
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
with a much lower risk than
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
, one can avoid the oscillation of the system between normal operation (using the primary controller) and recovery operation (using the safe controller).

6.1
Automating Recoverability Proofs using Symbolic Soft-Agents

Figure 4:
Key libraries and tools used for automating recoverability proofs.

Figure
4
depicts the main machinery that has been implemented and used.
It is based on the soft-agents framework
[
33
]

and the general symbolic libraries described in
[
23
]
. The general symbolic soft-agents libraries specify the executable semantics of CPS based on rewriting rules. The symbolic soft-agents rewrite rules correspond directly to the two
\mathsf{LS}
relations
\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}
and
\to_{\mathsf{dt}}
.
We implemented the vehicle-specific libraries for specifying vehicle scenarios.
We have also implemented the machinery for checking for
\mathsf{dt}
-adequacy (
{\mathsf{bad}}
-adequacy and
\prec_{1}
-adequacy) and Timed Recoverability.

The symbolic soft-agents are executable specifications. In particular, the execution traces are enumerated by Maude
[
10
]
search.
The constraints in the traces (non-linear arithmetic formulas) are solved by the SMT-solver (Z3
[
13
]
).
We implemented the connection between the symbolic soft-agents libraries and SMT solvers using the Python Binders described in
[
29
]
, thus enabling easy extensions to additional solvers and other tools in the future.

The basic idea
is to search for a counter-example to the property of interest. Because the symbolic search is complete, failure to find
a counter-example means that the property holds for all
instances of the
{\mathsf{LS}}
under consideration.
4
4

4

In our scenarios, we consider traces of finite length. There may be infinitely may state, but search terminates as the states finitely represented using
symbolic terms.

As an example, to check bad-adequacy, the algorithm follows Proposition
2
by searching for a
counter example, i.e. properties
{\mathsf{SP}}_{0}\prec_{1}{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
(not
{\mathsf{bad}}
) and

{\mathsf{LS}}
instances
{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}
,
{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}
such that
{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}

satisfies
{\mathsf{SP}}_{0}
,
{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}
satisfies
{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
,

{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}
,
and there is
\mathsf{dt}_{0}
with
0<\mathsf{dt}_{0}<\mathsf{dt}
,
{\mathsf{conf}}_{2}
such that
{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{dt}_{0}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{2}\to_{\mathsf{dt}-\mathsf{dt}_{0}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}
,
where
{\mathsf{conf}}_{2}
satisfies
{\mathsf{bad}}
.
If no counterexample is found then bad-adequacy holds for the
given
\mathsf{LS}
,
\mathsf{dt}
, and property specification.

Using symbolic rewriting, an arbitrary instance of
\mathsf{LS}
is
represented by a term,
\mathsf{asys}
, consisting of a
symbolic agent configuration and a symbolic environment. The
environment contains knowledge of the physical state and the
constraint on symbol values. The assertion that a property
\mathsf{SP}
holds
for a configuration is represented by the term

{\mathsf{enforce}}({\mathsf{asys}},{\mathsf{SP}})

that conjoins the boolean term specifying
\mathsf{SP}
in terms of the
symbols of
\mathsf{asys}
to the constraint in the environment.

{\mathsf{cond}}({\mathsf{asys}})
is the constraint in the environment part of
\mathsf{asys}
.

The base case is adequacy for a pair of properties,

{\mathsf{SP}}_{0}
,
{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
.
The algorithm for this case does the following.
First, use symbolic search from
{\mathsf{asys}}_{0}={\mathsf{enforce}}({\mathsf{asys}},{\mathsf{SP}}_{0})
for some
{\mathsf{asys}}_{1}
such that
{\mathsf{asys}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{asys}}_{1}
and

{\mathsf{cond}}({\mathsf{enforce}}({\mathsf{asys}}_{1},{\mathsf{SP}}_{1})))
is satisfiable.
If no such
{\mathsf{asys}}_{1}
is found, dt-adequacy holds for the
given
{\mathsf{SP}}_{0},{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
.
Otherwise, for some found
{\mathsf{asys}}_{1}
do a symbolic search from (a copy of)

{\mathsf{asys}}_{0}
for
some
{\mathsf{asys}}_{2},\mathsf{dt}_{0}
, where
\mathsf{dt}_{0}
is symbolic,
such that
{\mathsf{asys}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{dt}_{0}}{\mathsf{asys}}_{2}
and

{\mathsf{cond}}({\mathsf{enforce}}({\mathsf{asys}}_{1},{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}))\wedge{\mathsf{cond}}({\mathsf{enforce}}({\mathsf{asys}}_{2},{\mathsf{bad}}))\wedge 0<\mathsf{dt}_{0}<\mathsf{dt}

is satisfiable.
If such
{\mathsf{asys}}_{1},{\mathsf{asys}}_{2},\mathsf{dt}_{0}
are found we have a counter-example,
otherwise bad-adequacy holds for
{\mathsf{SP}}_{0},{\mathsf{SP}}_{1}
.

The remaining algorithms for
\prec_{1}
-adequacy, noSkip property, and
t
-recoverability follow the same pattern as for bad-adequacy.

Related Work

RTAs.

Since the first proposal of RTAs, called Simplex Architecture
[
30
]
, there has been several recent proposals of RTA variants
[
25
,
20
,
11
]
(to name a few).
While there are some differences on their architectures and functions, they all contain a decision module that evaluates the system risk level to decide which controller to use (the safe or the advanced controller).
Therefore, all the requirements formalized in this paper, namely, the time sampling adequacy and recoverability are still relevant and applicable.
Indeed, we advance the state of the art by providing suitable definitions that are amenable to automated verification.

We have been inspired by
[
14
]
that proposes high-level requirements on the recoverability of RTAs based on the level of risk of the system.
In particular, the methods for checking adequacy
of the sampling and for checking t-recoverability correspond
to the safety and liveness requirements of RTA wellformedness. The third condition concerns the minimum
time to become unsafe (non-safe) with any controller in charge, needed to ensure that the monitor can switch controllers and the safe controller can react before reaching
an unsafe condition. This can be shown using
<_{1}
-adequacy
and continuity of properties in a
<_{1}
-chain.
Summarizing, symbolic rewriting combined with SMT solving provides automated methods to verify correctness of time sampling mechanisms and safety requirements such as those of the RTA framework of
[
14
]
.

In a similar direction,
[
20
]
proposes high-level requirements for the correctness of the decision module based on the definition of what is safe and existence of “permanently safe command sequences”, which seems related to our time recoverability property.
They do not investigate, however, the effect of the time sampling and the correctness of the decision module.

CPS Verification and Validation

Much of the literature in CPS verification, e.g.
[
16
]
to name one, including some of the previous work on RTA
[
25
,
11
,
20
]
, rely on simulation-based methods.
These approaches are complementary to the one introduced in this paper. While this paper’s approach targets more early phase development by providing proofs that RTA specifications are suitable for all instances of a logical scenario, simulation-based approaches focus on later approaches for validating and testing implementations of RTA systems on particular instances of logical scenarios.

dL, KeYmaera X, and VeriPhy.

The KeYmaera X prover

[
34
,
24
]
uses
differential dynamic logic (dL) to specify and
verify CPS controller designs.
It is the starting
point of the VeriPhy pipeline

[
7
,
8
]

for producing code from logical specifications.
dL specifications and logical scenarios have in
common that they are given by terms with
constrained variables representing all instances
where values of variables satisfy the given
constraints.
Our methods differ in that dL specifications are not directly
executable and therefore, one uses interactive theorem proving
methods to verify dl specifications, whereas logical scenarios
are executable thus enabling further automation of
verification proofs using rewriting modulo SMT.

Formal Definitions of Resilience:

Alturki
et al.

[
4
]
propose formal definitions for resilience and show them to be undecidable in general and PSPACE-complete for some cases.
While formal connections are left to future work, our definition of timed recoverability seem to specialize their definition so to be applicable for RTA architectures, e.g., considering
\mathsf{dt}
-adequacy.

8
Conclusions

In this paper we present methods to automate proving safety properties using abstract logical scenarios (
{\mathsf{LS}}
). An
{\mathsf{LS}}

consists of instances of a pattern satisfying given ODD
constraints, together with a two-step transition relation giving
the semantics. The first step corresponds to reading sensors,
analyzing and deciding on actions (setting control parameters). The
second step evolves the system for the sampling time between
observations. Towards a formal foundation we introduce a notion of
Safety Property Specification for an
{\mathsf{LS}}
as a set of property
(names) with a risk level ordering relation, a unique least (most
risky) element,
{\mathsf{bad}}
, and a satisfaction relation. An adequate
sampling time should ensure that nothing important is missed.
We define three notions of
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy and show that they
are distinct and totally ordered. A system may be allowed to
enter a situation that is safe but risky, but a resilient system
will recover to an acceptably safe situation. This is formalized
in a definition of
t
-recoverability. A notion of
one-period-recovery
t
-recoverability is defined that is amenable to verification, and
shown to be equivalent to
t
-recoverability for adequate
\mathsf{dt}

using an inductive argument.

Towards automation of proofs, we use symbolic rewriting modulo SMT
as the execution and search engine
[
23
]
. Algorithms
were developed to prove all (infinitely many) instances of an
{\mathsf{LS}}
satisfy different notions of
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy or
t
-recoverability
(or to provide counter example instances). We report a set of
experiments checking
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy and
t
-recoverability properties
for
{\mathsf{LS}}
s and safety property specifications related to vehicle automation: vehicle following and pedestrian crossing. The experiments
show that it possible to find values of
\mathsf{dt}
and safety parameters
where adequacy holds and very simple controllers satisfy
t
-recoverability.
They also highlight corner cases where things go awry.

One direction of future work is to investigate a wider range of
case studies to better understand how the different design
parameters interact. Another important direction is to develop
methods to compose Logical Scenarios and proofs, thus scaling
analysis of complex systems.

{\mathsf{SP}}={\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
or
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}

{\mathsf{conf}}_{0}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}\to_{\mathsf{tasks}}{\mathsf{conf}}_{1}^{\prime}\to_{\mathsf{dt}}\to{\mathsf{conf}}_{2}\cdots
as part of a normal operation mode and part of a recovery period mode.

{\mathsf{conf}}_{k}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}

{\mathsf{conf}}_{l}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safer}}
with

is guaranteed to exist due to the resilience property. At this point we classify again configurations as normal operation.
The segment from
{\mathsf{conf}}_{k}
to
{\mathsf{conf}}_{l}
defines a recovery period. The remaining segments are normal operation periods.

Now, the proof goes by induction on the number of recovery and normal periods.
All configurations in a normal period satisfy a property
{\mathsf{SP}}
with
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}\prec{\mathsf{SP}}
, thus not satisfying
{\mathsf{bad}}
. Moreover, from
\mathsf{dt}
adequacy, no decomposition satisfies
{\mathsf{bad}}
either.
On a recovery period, no decomposition satisfies
{\mathsf{bad}}
from the definition of one-period-recovery resilience. (In fact no decompositions satisfy
{\mathsf{bad}}
.)
Moreover, from the fact that
\mathsf{dt}
does not skip
{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
, it is not possible not to activate a recovery period.

For the other direction, assume that the system is resilient.
This means in particular that all traces starting with a
{\mathsf{conf}}
such that
{\mathsf{conf}}\vDash{\mathsf{SP}}_{\mathsf{safe}}
will recover within
t
time units and no decomposition will pass through a configuration satisfying
{\mathsf{bad}}
. That is, the first piece of the trace is already a one-period recovery.

Specification of Pedestrian Crossing in Soft-Agents

Following the methodology described in
[
23
]
for automatically generating safety proofs for CPSes, we have implemented the machinery necessary for specifying logical scenarios such as the pedestrian crossing described above.

Specifications are written in Maude
[
10
]
, which implements rewriting logic
[
22
]
.
While familiarity with Maude is helpful, we believe that the specifications are understandable without it.

Figure 5:
Soft Agent (
\mathsf{SA}
) architecture.

Figure
5
depicts the overall architecture of a Soft Agent (
\mathsf{SA}
). It adopts a traditional sense-understand-decide-act architecture. In particular, a
\mathsf{SA}
has a local knowledge (
\mathsf{lkb}

\mathsf{SA}

0.D.1
Symbolic Knowledge Specifications

The main innovation of our previous work
[
23
]
is that instead of using concrete values for, e.g., location and speeds, as done previously
[
12
]
, we now represent these values by constrained symbols of sort
\mathtt{RealSym}
.
Elements of
\mathtt{RealSym}
are either real number constants, or symbols of the form
vv(i)
or
vv(i,str)
where
i
is a
Nat
uniquely identifying a symbol and
str
is a string describing the intuitive meaning of the symbol, used for improved readability.

The following symbols represent the initial conditions for
\mathtt{vh}
, namely, its position, speed, maximum acceleration, maximum deceleration, and initial acceleration.

eq vposx = vv(2,"vh-posX") . eq vposy = vv(3,"vh-posY") .
eq vvel = vv(5,"vh-speed") . eq maxacc = vv(9,"vh-maxAcc") .
eq maxdec = vv(10,"vh-maxDec") . eq acc = vv(32,"vh-acc") .

The values that these symbols may represent are specified by a set of (non-linear) arithmetic constraints.

Example 3

The following constraints specify the values for the vehicle’s initial speed and acceleration as defined in the pedestrian crossing ODD:

baseCond = {2 <= vvel and vvel <= 10
and acc >= maxdec and acc <= maxacc,
maxacc == 2 and maxdec == -8}

These constraints specify, e.g., that the vehicle speed can be any value between 2m/s and 10m/s exactly as specified in the pedestrian crossing logical scenario.

The initial knowledge base of the vehicle,
\mathtt{vh}
, at logical tick 0, contains the following terms, specifying its initial position, speed, acceleration, and direction:

lkbI = (atloc(vh,loc(vposx,vposy)) @ 0) (speed(vh,vvel) @ 0)
(accel(vh,acc1) @ 0) (dir(vh,loc(0,0),loc(0,1),1) @ 0)

The vehicle’s direction is a vector specified by two points and a magnitude. Initially, the vehicle is moving on the Y-axis.
Moreover,
\mathtt{vh}
has not yet detected the pedestrian.

An
\mathsf{SA}
updates its local knowledge base by observing the environment through its sensors. For example,
\mathtt{vh}
has the set of sensors
sset
:

eq sset = (locS speedS pedS(pedSDist,errPedS)) .

where
locS
is a location sensor, i.e., determines
\mathtt{vh}
’s position;
speedS
its speed; and
pedS
detects pedestrians.
pedS
has two parameters. The first,
pedSDist
, specifies the distance in which a pedestrian can be detected. The second parameter,
errPedS
, specifies the maximum relative error on the position of the pedestrian. These parameters abstracts issues such as camera resolution, that may lead to error or non detection of objects.

During execution at logical time
t
, sensors are used to update the information in
\mathsf{SA}
’s local knowledge base. For example, if the pedestrian
\mathtt{p1}
is within sensing range, the
pedS
includes a knowledge item of the form:

(ped(p1,loc(px,py),spd1,loc(px1,py1),loc(px2,py2) @ t)

where

is the sensed
\mathtt{p1}
location, which may be erroneous;
spd1
its speed; and the pair

specifies its direction.
Since the pedestrian sensor may be erroneous, as specified by the parameter
errorPedS
, the sensed pedestrian location
loc(px,py)
may not correspond to the pedestrian actual position.
This is also specified symbolically by means of constraints.

Example 4

The constraint below, called
\mathsf{ErrSensor}
, specifies the sensed pedestrian X-axis position, where
loc(vx,vy)
and
loc(px0,py0)
are, respectively, the actual positions of the vehicle and of the pedestrian:

(vv(i,"errY") <= (vy - py0) * errPedS) and

The symbol

is a fresh symbol specifying the maximum error on the Y-axis. A similar constraint can be used for specifying an error for the X-axis. However, since the logical scenario, the vehicle is moving on the Y-axis, this is not needed.

Agent, Environment, System Configurations and Executable Semantics for Pedestrian Crossing

An agent configuration has the form
[id : class | attrs ]
, where
id
is the agent’s unique identifier,
class
is its class, e.g., vehicle (
veh
), and
attrs
are its attributes which include its local knowledge base written
lkb : kb
, where
lkb
is a label and
kb
is the local knowledge base contents, and include sensors
sensors : sset
, where
sset
is a collection of sensors including
pedS(pedSDist,errPedS)
for sensing pedestrians.

An environment configuration has the form
[eId | ekb]
where
ekb
is the environment knowledge base which specifies state of the world.
The environment knowledge base contains the knowledge item
constraints(i,cond)
where
i
is the current index of fresh variables, and
cond
is the constraints (accumulated) on the existing symbols.

A system configuration is then a collection of agent configurations and an environment configuration. For example, a configuration specifying the pedestrian crossing logical scenario is as follows:

asysI = { [eid | (kb constraint(i,condI))]
[vh : veh | lkb : kb0 ; sensors : sset]
[p1 : ped | lkb : kb1 ] }

It contains the agent configuration for the vehicle
\mathtt{vh}
and for the pedestrian
\mathtt{p1}
.

The set of (initial) constraints
condI
specify the instances of a logical scenarios.
The following are some examples of how constraints can be used to specify assumptions about the logical scenario.

Example 5

The following set of initial constraints
condI
specifies a logical scenario with exactly one instance.

vposx == 5 and vposy == 0 and vvel == 5 and acc == 1
st == 30 and fn == 45 and pvel == 1

where the vehicle’s initial position is (0,5), with a speed of
5m/s
, acceleration of
1m/s^{2}
, and the pedestrian is walking from the position 30 of the road edge to to the position 45 of the other side of the road with constant speed of
1m/s
.

More interesting is when the logical scenario denotes an uncountable number of instances.
This is the case with the following two examples which also assumes
baseCond
in Example
3
.

Example 6

Together with
baseCond
, the following constraints

vposy >= 0 and vposy < cr1 and
st == fn and st >= cr1 and sn <= cr2

specify that the vehicle is facing the pedestrian crossing and from
st == fn
, the pedestrian is moving on the X-axis within the pedestrian crossing.

By removing the constraint
st == fn
and adding
fn >= cr1
and
fn <= cr2
, the resulting constraints specifies instances where the pedestrian is crossing (in straightline) from any point of the crossing at one side of the road to any point of the crossing on the other side of the road.

Example 7

Finally, we can also specify scenarios with different assumptions on the error of pedestrian detection sensors. For example, if one adds the constraint
errPedS == 0
, one assume a perfect pedestrian sensor. The constraint
errPedS <= 0.5
, on the other hand, specifies on the other hand a maximum error of 50% on the relative distance to the pedestrian (as described above).

Executable Semantics

A key feature of the framework is that such symbolic system configurations are executable.
The semantics is specified by two steps, formalized as rewriting rules over system configurations.

•

In the first phase, agents update their local knowledge bases with the information obtained by the their sensors, and decide which actions to take, e.g., accelerate or decelerate.

•

Then the second phase executes the actions decided by the agents.
This means generating fresh symbols for the positions, speeds and other physical attributes of agents, and adding new constraints for these fresh symbols according to the expected physics.

For example, consider
\mathtt{vh}
’s state as specified by the constrained symbols shown in Example
3
. Moreover, let
\mathtt{vh}
decide to maintain a constant speed, i.e., zero acceleration and not change its direction. Moreover, let
\mathsf{dt}
be the time corresponding to a logical tick.
The time tick rule will result in the following new knowledge base:

(atloc(vh,loc(vxnu,vynu)) @ 1)
(speed(vh,velnu) @ 1) (accel(vh,accnu) @ 1)

and adds the followin constraints specifying
\mathtt{vh}
’s possible state at logical time 1:

vynu == vposy + (vvel + velnu) * dt / 2 and
velnu == vvel + accnu * dt and vxnu == vposx and accnu == 0

Notice that the new configuration is also symbolic, i.e., correspond to a possibly uncountable number of concrete configurations.
