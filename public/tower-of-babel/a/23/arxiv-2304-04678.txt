SCATTERING BY A PERFORATED SANDWICH PANEL: METHOD OF RIEMANN SURFACES
Y.A. Antipov

Abstract
The model problem of scattering of a sound wave by an infinite plane structure formed by a semi-infinite acoustically hard screen and a semi-infinite sandwich panel perforated from one side and covered by a membrane from the other is exactly solved. The model is governed by two Helmholtz equations for the velocity potentials in the upper and lower half-planes coupled by the Leppington effective boundary condition and the equation of vibration of a membrane in a fluid. Two methods of solution are proposed and discussed. Both methods reduce the problem to an order-2 vector Riemann-Hilbert problem. The matrix coefficients have different entries, have the Chebotarev-Khrapkov structure and share the same order-4 characteristic polynomial. Exact Wiener-Hopf matrix factorization requires solving a scalar Riemann-Hilbert on an elliptic surface and the associated genus-1 Jacobi inversion problem solved in terms of the associated Riemann θ \theta -function. Numerical results for the absolute value of the total velocity potentials are reported and discussed.

The function
\chi(s,w)

is bounded at infinity if and only if
w\chi_{2}(s)=O(1)
,
s\to\infty
, or, equivalently,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\log\epsilon(t)dt}{f^{1/2}(t)}+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{dt}{\xi}+m_{a}\int_{{\bf a}}\frac{dt}{\xi}+m_{b}\int_{{\bf b}}\frac{dt}{\xi}=0.

(3.27)

If the function
\chi_{2}(s)
satisfies this condition, then because of the identity

\frac{1}{t-s}=-\frac{1}{s}+\frac{t}{s(t-s)},

(3.28)

it admits an alternative representation

\chi_{2}(s)=\frac{1}{4\pi is}\int_{L}\frac{\log\epsilon(t)tdt}{(t-s)f^{1/2}(t)}+\frac{1}{2s}\left(\int_{\Gamma}+m_{a}\int_{{\bf a}}+m_{b}\int_{{\bf b}}\right)\frac{tdt}{(t-s)\xi}.

(3.29)

This formula is valid for all
s\neq 0
and can be used for numerical calculations when
|s|>1

instead of formula (
3.26
)
that is more convenient when
|s|\leq 1
.

3.3
Jacobi inversion problem

The condition (
3.27
) is equivalent to a genus-1 Jacobi inversion problem. To show this, consider
the abelian (elliptic) integral

\omega(q)=\int_{-s_{2}}^{q}\frac{ds}{w(s)},\quad q=(s,w(s)).

(3.30)

Then the third and fourth integrals in equation (
3.27
),

{\cal A}=\int_{{\bf a}}\frac{ds}{w(s)}=2\int_{{\bf a}^{+}}\frac{ds}{f^{1/2}(s)},

{\cal B}=\int_{{\bf b}}\frac{ds}{w(s)}=2\int_{s_{2}}^{-s_{1}}\frac{ds}{f^{1/2}(s)},

(3.31)

are the
A
- and
B
-periods of the integral
\omega(q)
.
Thus the condition (
3.27
) constitute the following Jacobi inversion problem.

Find a point
q_{1}=(\zeta_{1},w_{1})\in{\cal R}
and two integers
m_{a}
and
m_{b}
such that

\omega(q_{1})+m_{a}{\cal A}+m_{b}{\cal B}=d_{0},

(3.32)

where

d_{0}=\omega(q_{0})-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\log\epsilon(t)dt}{f^{1/2}(t)}.

(3.33)

By dividing equation (
3.32
) by
{\cal A}
we arrive at the canonical form of the Jacobi problem

\hat{\omega}(q_{1})=e_{1}-k_{1}-m_{a}-m_{b}\hat{\cal B}\equiv e_{1}-k_{1}\quad(\rm{modulo\;the\;periods})

(3.34)

for the canonical abelian integral
\hat{\omega}(q)=\frac{\omega(q)}{{\cal A}}
. It has the unit
A
-period, while its
B
-period

\hat{\cal B}=\frac{{\cal B}}{{\cal A}}
has a
positive imaginary part,
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\hat{\cal B}>0
. Here,

e_{1}=\frac{d_{0}}{{\cal A}}+k_{1}
and

k_{1}
is the Riemann constant of the surface
\tilde{\cal R}
cut along the loops
\rm{\bf a}
and
\rm{\bf b}
. It is
computed in
(
17
)
,
k_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{\cal B}
.

The unknown point
q_{1}=(\zeta_{1},w_{1})
is the single zero of the genus-1 Riemann
\theta
-function

{\cal F}(q)=\theta(\hat{\omega}(q)-e_{1})=\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\{\pi i\hat{\cal B}\nu^{2}+2\pi i\nu[\hat{\omega}(q)-e_{1}]\}.

(3.35)

To find
\zeta_{1}
, consider the integral

M=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\hat{\cal R}}\frac{d\log{\cal F}(q)}{s-i},

(3.36)

where
\partial\hat{\cal R}
is the boundary of the surface
{\cal R}
cut along the cross-section
{{\bf a}}
only.
The procedure we are going to apply is a modification of the method
(
9
)
that instead of the poles
at the two points
i_{1}=(i,f^{1/2}(i))\in{{C}_{1}}
and
i_{2}=(i,-f^{1/2}(i))\in{{C}_{2}}
in (
3.36
)
uses an integrand with two poles at the infinite points of the surface
{\cal R}
.
Without loss we assume that
{\cal F}(i_{n})\neq 0
,
n=1,2
, and compute the integral
M
by applying the theory
of residues. We have

M=\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}-i}+\frac{{\cal F}^{\prime}(i_{1})}{{\cal F}(i_{1})}+\frac{{\cal F}^{\prime}(i_{2})}{{\cal F}(i_{2})}.

(3.37)

where the derivative of the Riemann
\theta
-function is a rapidly convergent series

{\cal F}^{\prime}(q)=\frac{2\pi i(-1)^{n-1}}{{\cal A}f^{1/2}(s)}\sum_{\nu=-\infty}^{\infty}\nu\exp\{\pi i\hat{\cal B}\nu^{2}+2\pi i\nu[\hat{\omega}(q)-e_{1}]\},\quad q\in{{C}_{n}},\quad n=1,2.

(3.38)

The integral (
3.36
) can also be represented as a contour integral

M=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\int_{{\bf a}^{+}}+\int_{{\bf a}^{-}}\right)\frac{d\log{\cal F}(q)}{s-i}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\bf a}}\frac{1}{s-i}[d\log{\cal F}^{+}(q)-d\log{\cal F}^{-}(q)],

(3.39)

where
{\bf a}^{+}
and
{\bf a}^{-}
have opposite directions and pointwise coincide with the loop
{\bf a}
from the side of the sheets
{C}_{1}
and
{C}_{2}
, respectively, while the positive direction of the loops
{\bf a}^{+}
and
{\bf a}

are chosen such that the exterior of the cut
[s_{1},s_{2}]
on the first sheet is on the left.
Using the relation between the boundary values of the Riemann
\theta
-function
on the loop
{\bf a}

{\cal F}^{+}(p)={\cal F}^{-}(p)\exp\{\pi i\hat{\cal B}-2\pi ie_{1}+2\pi i\hat{\omega}^{+}(p)\},\quad p\in{\rm{\bf a}},

(3.40)

where

\hat{\omega}^{+}(p)=\lim_{q\to p,q\in{{C}_{1}}}\hat{\omega}(q),

(3.41)

we derive another formula for the integral
M

M=\int_{{\bf a}}\frac{d\hat{\omega}^{+}(q)}{s-i}=\frac{2}{{\cal A}}\int_{[s_{1},s_{2}]^{+}}\frac{ds}{(s-i)f^{1/2}(s)}.

(3.42)

Combining formulas (
3.37
) and (
3.42
) and introducing the quantity

{\cal J}=\frac{2}{{\cal A}}\int_{[s_{1},s_{2}]^{+}}\frac{ds}{(s-i)f^{1/2}(s)}-\frac{{\cal F}^{\prime}(i_{1})}{{\cal F}(i_{1})}-\frac{{\cal F}^{\prime}(i_{2})}{{\cal F}(i_{2})}

(3.43)

we find the parameter
\zeta_{1}

\zeta_{1}=i+\frac{1}{{\cal J}}.

(3.44)

Evaluate now the abelian integral at the point
\zeta_{1}
lying on the first and second sheets,

\hat{\omega}(\zeta_{1},\pm f^{1/2}(\zeta_{1}))=\pm\frac{1}{{\cal A}}\int_{-s_{2}}^{\zeta_{1}}\frac{ds}{f^{1/2}(s)}.

(3.45)

and denote

d_{\pm}=\frac{d_{0}}{{\cal A}}-\hat{\omega}(\zeta_{1},\pm f^{1/2}(\zeta_{1})).

(3.46)

Taking the imaginary and real parts of the complex equation (
3.40
) we determine
the constants
m^{\pm}_{b}
and
m^{\pm}_{a}

m^{\pm}_{b}=\frac{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits d_{\pm}}{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\hat{\cal B}},\quad m^{\pm}_{a}=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits d_{\pm}-\frac{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\hat{\cal B}}{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\hat{\cal B}}\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits d_{\pm}.

(3.47)

If it turns out that both of the integers
m^{+}_{a}
and
m_{b}^{+}
are integers, then
m_{a}=m_{a}^{+}
,

m_{b}=m_{b}^{+}
, and the point
q_{1}\in{{C}_{1}}
. Otherwise,
m_{a}=m_{a}^{-}
,

m_{b}=m_{b}^{-}
are integers, and
q_{1}\in{{C}_{2}}
.

3.4
Solution of the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem

On having factorized the matrix
G(t)=X^{+}(t)[X^{-}(t)]^{-1}=[X^{-}(t)]^{-1}X^{+}(t)
,
t\in L
, and eliminated the essential singularity
of the factors
X^{\pm}(s)
at infinity, we proceed with the solution of the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem (
3.14
)
by rewriting the boundary relation as

X^{+}(t)\Phi^{+}(t)-N\Psi_{+}^{(1)}(t)-\Psi_{+}^{(2)}(t)=-X^{-}(t)\Phi^{-}(t)-N\Psi_{-}^{(1)}(t)-\Psi_{-}^{(2)}(t),\quad t\in L.

(3.48)

Here,
\Psi^{(n)}_{\pm}(t)
are the limit values of the Cauchy integrals

\Psi^{(1)}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{X^{-}(t)J}{\gamma(t)(t^{2}-\mu^{2})}\frac{dt}{t-s},\quad J=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-1\\
1\end{array}\right),

\Psi^{(2)}(s)=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{L}\frac{X^{-}(t)J}{\gamma(t)(t^{2}-\mu^{2})(t+k\sin\theta_{0})}\frac{dt}{t-s},

(3.49)

defined by the Sokhotski-Plemelj formulas

\Psi_{\pm}^{(1)}(t)=\pm\frac{X^{-}(t)J}{2\gamma(t)(t^{2}-\mu^{2})}+P.V.\Psi^{(1)}(t),

\Psi_{\pm}^{(2)}(t)=\mp\frac{\alpha iX^{-}(t)J}{\gamma(t)(t^{2}-\mu^{2})(t+k\sin\theta_{0})}+P.V.\Psi^{(2)}(t),\quad t\in L.

(3.50)

By the continuity principle and the generalized Liouville’s theorem of the theory of analytic functions,

\pm X^{\pm}(s)\Phi^{\pm}(s)-N\Psi_{\pm}^{(1)}(s)-\Psi_{\pm}^{(2)}(s)=R(s),\quad s\in{{C}^{\pm}},

(3.51)

where
R(s)
is a rational vector-function to be determined.
Since the matrices
X^{\pm}(s)
are bounded at infinity, while the vectors
\Phi^{\pm}(s)
,
\Psi_{\pm}^{(1)}(s)
and

\Psi_{\pm}^{(2)}(s)
behave as
s^{-1}\mbox{const}
for large
s
, we have
R(s)=O(s^{-1})
,
s\to\infty
.

Show next that the rational vector-function
R(s)
has a pole at the point
s=\zeta_{0}
.
Indeed, due to the logarithmic singularity of the function
\chi(s,w)

at the starting point
(\zeta_{0},f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0}))\in{{C}_{1}}
of the contour
\Gamma
, the function
F(s,w)
has a simple pole at this point
and is bounded at the point
(\zeta_{0},-f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0}))\in{{C}_{2}}
,

F(s,w)\sim D_{0}(s-\zeta_{0})^{-1},\quad F(s,-w)=O(1),\quad s\to\zeta_{0},\quad(s,w)\in{{C}_{1}}.

(3.52)

Employing the first formula in (
3.19
)
for the matrix
X(s)
we obtain

X(s)\Phi(s)\sim\frac{D_{0}}{2(s-\zeta_{0})}\left[\left(1+\frac{l(\zeta_{0})}{f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0})}\right)\Phi_{0}(\zeta_{0})+\frac{m}{f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0})}\Phi_{1}(\zeta_{0})\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\eta_{0}\end{array}\right),

(3.53)

where

\eta_{0}=\frac{m}{l(\zeta_{0})+f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0})}.

(3.54)

This enables us to find the vector
R(s)
. We have

R(s)=\frac{C}{s-\zeta_{0}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\eta_{0}\end{array}\right),

(3.55)

where
C
is a free constant. By substituting this expression into equation (
3.51
) we get the solution
of the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem (
3.14
)

\Phi^{\pm}(s)=\pm[X^{\pm}(s)]^{-1}\left[\frac{C}{s-\zeta_{0}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\eta_{0}\end{array}\right)+N\Psi_{\pm}^{(1)}(s)+\Psi_{\pm}^{(2)}(s)\right],\quad s\in{{C}^{\pm}}.

(3.56)

Now, the function
F(s,w)
has a simple zero at the point
q_{1}\in{\cal R}
caused by the logarithmic singularity
of the function
\chi(s,w)
at the terminal point of the contour
\Gamma
. If
q_{1}=(\zeta_{1},w_{1})\in{{C}_{1}}
,
then
w_{1}=f^{1/2}(\zeta_{1})
and

F(s,w)\sim D_{1}(s-\zeta_{1}),\quad F(s,-w)\sim D_{2},\quad(s,w)\in{{C}_{1}},\quad s\to\zeta_{1}.

(3.57)

Otherwise, is
q_{1}=(\zeta_{1},w_{1})\in{{C}_{2}}
,
then
w_{1}=-f^{1/2}(\zeta_{1})
and

F(s,w)\sim D_{2},\quad F(s,-w)\sim D_{1}(s-\zeta_{1}),\quad(s,w)\in{{C}_{1}},\quad s\to\zeta_{1}.

(3.58)

Here,
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are nonzero constants. From the second formula in (
3.19
)
for the inverse matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}
, it becomes evident that the matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}
has a pole at the point

s=\zeta_{1}
. Since
{\rm rank}\,B(s,w)=1
, the vector-function
F(s,w)
has a removable singularity at the point
s=\zeta_{1}

if and only if

\Pi_{0}C+\Pi_{1}N=-\Pi_{2},

(3.59)

where

\Pi_{0}=\left(\frac{w_{1}+l(\zeta_{1})}{m}+\eta_{0}\right)\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0}},\quad\Pi_{1}=\frac{w_{1}+l(\zeta_{1})}{m}\Psi_{0}^{(1)}(\zeta_{1})+\Psi_{1}^{(1)}(\zeta_{1}),

\Pi_{2}=\frac{w_{1}+l(\zeta_{1})}{m}\Psi_{0}^{(2)}(\zeta_{1})+\Psi_{1}^{(2)}(\zeta_{1}),

(3.60)

and
\Psi_{0}^{(n)}(s)
and
\Psi_{1}^{(n)}(s)
(
n=1,2
) are the two components of the vectors
\Psi^{(n)}(s)

given by (
3.49
). Equation (
3.59
) constitutes the first equation for the unknown constants
C
and
N
.

The solution derived has to be restricted to the class of functions satisfying the conditions (
3.15
).
To verify these conditions, we examine the asymptotics of the vectors
\Phi^{+}(t)
and
\Phi^{-}(t)
as
|t|\to\infty
,
t\in L
.
We start with the analysis of the functions
\Delta(t)
and
\epsilon(t)

(3.61)

By applying the Sokhotski-Plemelj formulas to the integral representations of the functions

and

We focus our attention on the principal terms of the asymptotic expansions at infinity of the two functions in (
3.62
)
and observe that

\chi_{1}^{\pm}(t)=\mp\frac{k\tau}{4|t|}+\frac{k\tau}{2\pi it}\log|t|+\frac{h_{1}}{t}+O(t^{-2}),\quad|t|\to\infty,\quad t\in L,

(3.63)

where

(3.64)

and
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are some constants. Their values do not affect the asymptotics we aim to derive.

Next, by virtue of the relation (
3.25
) we can derive the asymptotics of the solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem

{\cal R}
as follows:

F^{\pm}(t,f^{1/2}(t))=e^{h_{0}}\left[1+\frac{h_{+}}{t}+O(t^{-2})\right],\quad|t|\to\infty,\quad t\in L,

F^{\pm}(t,-f^{1/2}(t))=e^{-h_{0}}\left[1\mp\frac{k\tau}{2|t|}+\frac{k\tau}{\pi it}\log|t|+\frac{h_{-}}{t}+O\left(\frac{\log^{2}|t|}{t^{2}}\right)\right],\quad|t|\to\infty,\quad t\in L,

where

. Substituting these expressions into formula (

) for the inverse matrix

gives

[X^{\pm}(t)]^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-h_{0}}(1-h_{+}t^{-1})&0\\

0&1\end{array}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right),\quad|t|\to\infty,\quad t\in L,

where

is a constant. From here we immediately get

and

,

|t|\to\infty
,

, and the conditions (

3.5
Partial indices of factorization

In this section we wish to determine the partial indices of factorization defined as
the orders of the columns of the canonical matrix of factorization
(
18
)
,
(
19
)
,
(
20
)

by the method
(
18
)
applied in the genus 3 case
(
5
)
.
The canonical matrix is a matrix
X(s)
that solves the factorization problem
X^{+}(t)=G(t)X^{-}(t)
,
t\in L
, and satisfies the conditions

(i) at any finite point
s\in{{C}}
,
X(s)
is in normal form,

(ii)
\det X(s)
does not have zeros at any finite point in the complex plane, and

(iii) the matrix
X(s)

We recall that a matrix
Y(s)
is in normal form at a point (finite or infinite) if the order of the determinant of the matrix
at this point is equal to the sum of the orders of the matrix columns.

Assume

,
s\to s_{0}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, where
\alpha_{j}
is real,
Y_{j}^{*}(s)
is bounded at

and
Y_{j}^{*}(s_{0})\neq 0
. Then
\alpha_{j}
is called the order of the function
Y_{j}(s)
at
s=s_{0}
and

\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
is called the order of the vector
Y(s)=(Y_{1}(s),\ldots,Y_{n}(s))
at the point
s=s_{0}
.

Let
Y_{j}(s)=Y_{j}^{*}(s)s^{-\alpha_{j}}
,
s\to\infty
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, where
\alpha_{j}
is real,
Y_{j}^{*}(s)
is bounded at infinity and
Y_{j}^{*}(\infty)\neq 0
. Then
\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
are called the order at infinity of the function
Y_{j}(s)
and vector
Y(s)
,
respectively.

Since the properties of the inverse matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}
have been studied in Section 3.4, we shall convert the
matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}
, not the matrix
X(s)
itself, into a canonical matrix.
The matrix

has three singular points,
\zeta_{0}
,
\zeta_{1}
, and
\infty

At the point

[X(s)]^{-1}\sim F_{0}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\frac{l_{0}}{w_{0}}&-\frac{m}{w_{0}}\\
-\frac{m}{w_{0}}&1+\frac{l_{0}}{w_{0}}\end{array}\right)+(s-\zeta_{0})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}F_{11}&F_{12}\\
F_{21}&F_{22}\end{array}\right),\quad s\to\zeta_{0},

(3.74)

where
l_{0}=l(\zeta_{0})
,
w_{0}=f^{1/2}(\zeta_{0})
, and
F_{0}
and
F_{nj}
are nonzero constants. It is clear that
\det\{[X(s)]^{-1}\}\sim 0
as

s\to\zeta_{0}
, and the order of the determinant at the point
\zeta_{0}
is equal to 1, while both columns have zero-orders.
To transform the matrix into normal form, we multiply it from the right by the matrix

T_{0}(s)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{s-\zeta_{0}}&0\\
\frac{\nu_{0}}{s-\zeta_{0}}&1\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\nu_{0}=\frac{w_{0}-l_{0}}{m}.

(3.75)

The new matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}T_{0}(s)
is in normal form at the point
s=\zeta_{0}
,

[X(s)]^{-1}T_{0}(s)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}F_{11}+\nu_{0}F_{12}&-\frac{m}{w_{0}}F_{0}\\
F_{21}+\nu_{0}F_{22}&\left(1+\frac{l_{0}}{w_{0}}\right)F_{0}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad s\to\zeta_{0}.

(3.76)

Proceed now with converting the new matrix into normal form at the point
\zeta=\zeta_{1}
. The original matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}

behaves at the point
(\zeta_{1},w_{1})
as

[X(s)]^{-1}\sim\frac{F_{1}}{s-\zeta_{1}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1+\frac{l_{1}}{w_{1}}&\frac{m}{w_{1}}\\
\frac{m}{w_{1}}&1-\frac{l_{1}}{w_{1}}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{F}_{11}&\hat{F}_{12}\\
\hat{F}_{21}&\hat{F}_{22}\end{array}\right),\quad(s,w)\to(\zeta_{1},w_{1}),

(3.77)

where
w_{1}=(-1)^{n-1}f^{1/2}(\zeta_{1})
,
n=1
if the point
(\zeta_{1},w_{1})
lies on the first sheet
{{C}_{1}}
and
n=2
otherwise,
l_{1}=l(\zeta_{1})
, and

F_{1}
and
\hat{F}_{nj}
are nonzero constants. By multiplying the new matrix

from the right by the matrix

\nu_{1}-\nu_{0}&s-\zeta_{1}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\nu_{1}=-\frac{w_{1}+l_{1}}{m},

(3.78)

we obtain the matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)
, where

\hat{T}(s)=T_{0}(s)T_{1}(s)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0}}{s-\zeta_{0}}&0\\
\nu_{1}-\nu_{0}+\frac{\nu_{0}(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})}{s-\zeta_{0}}&s-\zeta_{1}\\
\end{array}\right).

(3.79)

It is directly verified that the matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)
is in normal form at both points,
s=\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{1}
.
At the point
(s,w)=(\zeta_{1},w_{1})
, we have

[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{F}_{11}+\nu_{1}\hat{F}_{12}&\frac{m}{w_{1}}F_{1}\\
\hat{F}_{21}+\nu_{1}\hat{F}_{22}&\left(1-\frac{l_{1}}{w_{1}}\right)F_{1}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad s\to\zeta_{1}.

(3.80)

A similar remedy is to be used to make the matrix
[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)
in normal form at infinity. Analysis of the matrix

[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)
at infinity shows

[X(s)]^{-1}\hat{T}(s)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-h_{0}}(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})s^{-1}&h_{12}s^{-1}\\
e^{h_{0}}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})&e^{h_{0}}s\\
\end{array}\right),\quad s\to\infty,

where
h_{0}
is given by (
3.64
) and
h_{12}
is a nonzero constant.
The orders of the columns of the matrix

at infinity are equal to 0 and
-1
, and their sum does not equal
the order 0 of the determinant of this matrix at infinity. We multiply it from the right by the matrix

U(s)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\nu s\\
0&1\\
\end{array}\right),

(3.82)

and arrive at the matrix
\tilde{X}(s)=[X(s)]^{-1}T(s)
that is in normal form at the infinite point if the parameter
\nu
is chosen to be

\nu=(\nu_{0}-\nu_{1})^{-1}
. Then

\tilde{X}(s)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{-d_{0}}(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})s^{-1}&e^{-d_{0}}\frac{\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0}}{\nu_{0}-\nu_{1}}\\
e^{d_{0}}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{0})&d_{22}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad s\to\infty,

(3.83)

where
d_{22}
is a constant. The transformation matrix
T(s)=\hat{T}(s)U(s)
is

T(s)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0}}{s-\zeta_{0}}&\frac{(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})s}{(s-\zeta_{0})(\nu_{0}-\nu_{1})}\\
\nu_{1}-\nu_{0}+\frac{\nu_{0}(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})}{s-\zeta_{0}}&-\zeta_{1}+\frac{\nu_{0}(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{0})s}{(\nu_{0}-\nu_{1})(s-\zeta_{0})}\\
\end{array}\right).

(3.84)

The orders of the columns of the matrix
\tilde{X}(s)
and its determinant at infinity are equal to
0. The determinant of the matrix
\tilde{X}(s)
does not have zeros in any finite complex plane. Therefore
the matrix
\tilde{X}(s)
is the canonical matrix of factorization
and the partial indices
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=0
.

Notice that the original vector Riemann-Hilbert problem can be rewritten as

(3.85)

Since

(3.86)

and

(3.88)

Therefore
\tilde{X}(s)
is the canonical matrix of factorization of the coefficient
[G(t)]^{-1}
of the Riemann-Hilbert
problem (
3.85
).
We may conclude now that the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem (

) has zero partial indices and
according to the stability criterion
(
21
)
,
(
20
)
they are stable.

4
Vector Riemann-Hilbert problem associated with the direct extension of the boundary conditions

In the preceding section we solved the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem derived by employing the Laplace transforms
of the velocity potentials.
In this section we wish to apply a different approach for its derivation that employs the Fourier transform
and another way of extension of the boundary conditions on the whole real axis. We aim to understand whether one method has the advantage of the other.
For simplicity, we confine ourselves to the case
0<\theta_{0}<\pi/2
and take
L
as the real axis.
We start with writing the general integral representation of the scattering potentials

\phi_{0}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A_{0}(s)e^{-isx+\gamma y}ds,\quad y<0,

\phi_{1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A_{1}(s)e^{-isx-\gamma y}ds,\quad y>0,

(4.1)

where
\gamma=\gamma(s)
is the branch fixed by (
3.3
). The first derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\phi_{0}(x,0)

is continuous at the point
x=0
and equals 0, while the mixed derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\phi_{0}(x,0)
is bounded
at the point
x=0
and discontinuous,

\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\phi_{0}(x,0)=0,\quad\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\partial y}\phi_{0}(x,0)=N,

(4.2)

where
N
is a nonzero constant.
Extend now the boundary conditions (
2.13
) onto the whole real axis
L=\{-\infty<x<\infty,y=0\}
except for the point

x=0

\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial y}=\phi_{0}^{+}(x),\quad\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial y}=\phi_{1}^{+}(x),

\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{0}}{\partial x^{2}\partial y}+\mu^{2}\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial y}-\alpha(\phi_{1}-\phi_{0})=\beta^{+}(x)+\phi_{0}^{-}(x),

\frac{\partial\phi_{1}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial y}+k\tau\phi_{1}=\phi_{1}^{-}(x),

(4.3)

where

\phi_{j}^{+}(x)=0,\quad x<0;\quad\phi_{j}^{-}(x)=0,\quad x>0,\quad j=0,1.

\beta^{+}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-2\alpha e^{ikx\sin\theta_{0}},&x>0,\\
0,&x<0.\\
\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

To derive the associated vector Riemann-Hilbert problem, we introduce the Laplace transforms (one-sided Fourier integrals)

\hat{\Phi}_{j}^{-}(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\phi_{j}^{-}(x)e^{isx}dx,\quad\Phi_{j}^{+}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{j}^{+}(x)e^{isx}dx,\quad j=0,1,

(4.5)

apply the Fourier integral transform to the boundary conditions (
4.3
) and observe that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{0}}{\partial x^{2}\partial y}(x,0)e^{isx}dx=-N-s^{2}\gamma(s)A_{0}(s).

Then the boundary condition (

and

\gamma(s)A_{0}(s)=\Phi_{0}^{+}(s),\quad\gamma(s)A_{1}(s)=-\Phi_{1}^{+}(s),

A_{0}(s)[\gamma(s)(\mu^{2}-s^{2})+\alpha]-\alpha A_{1}(s)=\hat{\Phi}_{0}^{-}(s)-\frac{2i\alpha}{s+k\sin\theta_{0}}+N,

[-\gamma(s)+k\tau]A_{1}(s)-\gamma(s)A_{0}(s)=\hat{\Phi}_{1}^{-}(s).

(4.7)

We now eliminate the functions
A_{0}(s)
and
A_{1}(s)
to have the vector Riemann-Hilbert problem

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\Phi}_{0}^{-}(s)\\
\hat{\Phi}_{1}^{-}(s)\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu^{2}-s^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\gamma(s)}&\frac{\alpha}{\gamma(s)}\\
-1&1-\frac{k\tau}{\gamma(s)}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi_{0}^{+}(s)\\
\Phi_{1}^{+}(s)\\
\end{array}\right)+\frac{1}{2}g(s),\quad s\in L,

(4.8)

where

g(s)=\left(\frac{4i\alpha}{s+k\sin\theta_{0}}-2N\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\\
\end{array}\right).

(4.9)

To transform the matrix coefficient of the problem to the form (

\Phi_{0}^{-}(s)=2\hat{\Phi}_{0}^{-}(s)+\frac{2\alpha}{k\tau}\hat{\Phi}_{1}^{-}(s),\quad\Phi_{1}^{-}(s)=-\frac{2\alpha}{k\tau}\hat{\Phi}_{1}^{-}(s).

In terms of the vector-functions

with the matrix coefficient

and

) on the same genus-1 Riemann surface

The solution of the problem (

), the matrix factorization and Jacobi inversion problems are given by the same formulas as in Section 3 if

Consider first the limit values

6
Conclusions

A closed-form solution has been given for the model problem of the scattering
of a plane sound wave by an infinite thin structure formed by a semi-infinite
acoustically hard screen attached to a sandwich panel with acoustically hard walls.
The upper side of the sandwich panel is perforated, while the lower side is an unperforated
membrane. We have applied two methods of extension of the boundary conditions to the whole
real axis and deduce two order-2 vector Riemann-Hilbert problems. The matrix coefficients
of both problems have
the Chebotarev-Khrapkov structure with the same order-4 characteristic polynomial but
with distinct entries.
Wiener-Hopf matrix factors for both problems have been derived by quadratures by solving a
scalar Riemann-Hilbert problem on the same elliptic surface. The coefficient
of the scalar problem is equal to the first eigenvalue of the matrix on the upper sheet of the surface and
the second eigenvalue on the lower sheet. We have eliminated the essential singularity
caused by simple poles of the Cauchy analogue at the two infinite points of the surface
by solving a genus-1 Jacobi inversion problems in terms of the Riemann
\theta
-function.

We have found that the analysis of the Wiener-Hopf matrix factors at infinity is
simpler for the first method that sets the Riemann-Hilbert problem for the one-sided Fourier transforms
of the velocity potentials on the upper and lower sides of the infinite structure. The second method
extends the four boundary conditions to the whole real axis by means of unknown functions and
employ the one-sided Fourier transforms of these functions.
The advantage of the first method over the second one is explained by the presence of the logarithmic growth at infinity
of the densities of the singular integrals involved in the solution obtained by the second
method. Both methods lead to the solution having two arbitrary constants. The constants have been
fixed by additional conditions of the problem.
For the first method, in addition to the meromorphic Wiener-Hopf factors, we constructed the canonical matrix
of factorization and computed the
partial indices of factorization. It turns out that they both are equal to zero and therefore stable.

Numerical tests have been implemented for the solution derived by the first method.
The integrals involved are rapidly convergent for all values of the parameters tested except for the case when

|\tau|\to\infty
, when the method is not numerically efficient.
We have computed the absolute values of the full velocity potentials, the function
P(r,\theta)=|\phi_{1}|
,

0<\theta<\pi
, and
P(r,\theta)=|\phi_{inc}+\phi_{ref}+\phi_{0}|
,
\pi<\theta<2\pi
. We have found that the presence
of the sandwich panel perforated from the upper side reduces the transmission of sound, and when the membrane surface density
m_{0}
is growing
the function
P(r,\theta)
(
0<\theta<\pi)
decreases.
We have also discovered that when the absolute value
|k|
of the complex wave number approaches
the resonance value
k_{res}
, then
|\tau|\to\infty
and the magnitude of the function
P(r,\theta)
tends to infinity.
