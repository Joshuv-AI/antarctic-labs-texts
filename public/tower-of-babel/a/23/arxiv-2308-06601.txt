Spectral smooth tests for goodness-of-fit
Victor Cândido Reis, Rafael Izbicki

Abstract
Goodness-of-fit tests are crucial tools for assessing the validity of statistical models. In this paper, we introduce a novel approach, the Spectral Smooth Test (SST), that generalizes Neyman’s smooth test to high-dimensional data settings. While conventional goodness-of-fit tests for univariate data are well-established, extending them to high dimensions, such as images, trajectories, and SNPs, poses significant challenges. Our proposed SST leverages spectral bases, which adapt naturally to the geometry of feature spaces, to model multivariate distributions. Unlike traditional orthogonal bases, these spectral bases are tailored to the data distribution, enabling more effective function modeling. The SST framework offers a principled way to estimate the underlying model, thereby providing actionable insights even when the null hypothesis is rejected. We present experimental results demonstrating the robustness of SST across various tuning parameter choices and compare its performance against other goodness-of-fit tests. Furthermore, we apply SST to the MNIST dataset as a real-world example, showcasing its effectiveness in high-dimensional scenarios.

Introduction

Hypothesis tests are widely used by scientists. In particular, goodness-of-fit tests are important because they enable practitioners to assess statistical models, and thus are a key tool for creating accurate descriptions of the world.
In its simplest formulation, the goal of a goodness-of-fit test is to test the null hypothesis that an i.i.d. sample,
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
, comes from a postulated distribution
F_{0}
.

Although there exists a large body of work that develops goodness-of-fit tests for univariate settings
(
Mann and Whitney 1947
;
Kolmogorov 1933
;
Smirnov 1948
;
Algeri and Zhang 2020
)
, it was only recently that nonparametric tests designed for high dimensional data have gained interest
(
Gretton et al. 2012
;
Ramdas et al. 2017
;
Pfister et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Inacio et al. 2021
;
Polo et al. 2022
;
McNeely et al. 2023
)
. This is because such tests are essential to deal with complex objects such as images, trajectories and SNPs.
These tests typically have good statistical power, but they are often hard to interpret: The output of such tests are just
reject/don’t reject decisions, that do not offer insights on
how
a given model could be improved.

In the univariate context,
an alternative approach to hypothesis testing is Neyman’s smooth test
(
Neyman 1937
)
, which has a key advantage: when the null hypothesis is rejected, it automatically yields an estimate for the correct model
(
Shalizi 2013
)
. This is because Neyman’s approach builds on a density estimator of the distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}
. More specifically, in the special case where
{\mathbf{X}}\in(0,1)
and
H_{0}:X\sim U(0,1)
,
the key idea of Neyman’s test is to represent the density of
{\mathbf{X}}
as

f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})\propto\exp{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}\phi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})\right)},

where
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis with
\phi_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\equiv 1

(such as the Fourier basis). Because the distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}
is uniform (that is,
f_{\theta}({\mathbf{x}})=1
) if, and only if,
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\ldots=0,

the idea is then to test
H_{0}

by first estimating
\theta_{i}
’s and then computing
the test statistic

T=\sum_{i=1}^{I}(\widehat{\theta}_{i})^{2},

where
I
is a tuning parameter that needs to be chosen. The test rejects
H_{0}
if
T
is large enough. If the null hypothesis is rejected,

\widehat{f}({\mathbf{x}})\propto\exp{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{I}\widehat{\theta}_{i}\phi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})\right)}
can be used as an alternative model. The estimate
\widehat{f}({\mathbf{x}})
can also be used to quantify
how

f
differs from the null model (see
Algeri and Zhang 2020
for ways in which this insights can be used).

Several extensions of Neyman’s original proposal exist. For instance,
Thomas and Pierce 1979
work with cases where the null hypothesis is composite, and

Xiao et al. 2004
propose a version of the test that can be used for two-sample comparisons. See
Rayner and Best 1990
;
Ghosh and Bera 2002
;
Algeri and Zhang 2020
and references therein for other extensions. To the best of our knowledge, none of such extensions is able to deal with high-dimensional data.
In this work, we propose a generalization that smooth tests that can be used for high dimensional data, the
Spectral Smooth Test
(SST). This setting is a challenge for the original formulation because
it is computationally expensive to extend
Fourier and other data-independent unidimensional bases to higher dimensions. Indeed, this extension is typically done by using tensor products
(
Efromovich 2008
)
, which quickly become computationally intractable even for as few as five
variables. In order to overcome this issue, we instead use
spectral bases

(
Lee and Izbicki 2016
;
Izbicki and Lee 2016
)
to model multivariate distributions.
Spectral bases depend on the distribution of the data and naturally adapt to the geometry of the the feature space, and thus they can do a better job in modeling functions defined over the data domain. However, because spectral basis are orthogonal with respect to the data distribution (rather than to the Lebesgue measure), Neyman’s approach needs to be adapted.

Section
2
introduces our approach, the
spectral smooth test
.
Section
3
shows experiments that evaluate its robustness to the choice of tuning parameters. It also presents comparisons to other goodness-of-fit tests.
Section
4
presents an application of SST to the MNIST dataset
(
Deng 2012
)
.
Section
5
concludes the paper.

2
Methodology

2.1
Diffusion basis

We start by reviewing the diffusion basis

Lee and Wasserman 2010a
, the basis functions we use in SST.
Let
F_{0}
be a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})
be a Mercer Kernel defined over

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The diffusion operator
Lee and Wasserman 2010a
is defined as

A(h)({\mathbf{z}})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}a({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})h({\mathbf{y}})dF_{0}({\mathbf{y}}),

where

a({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})=\dfrac{k({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})}{p({\mathbf{z}})}

and

p({\mathbf{z}})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})dF_{0}({\mathbf{y}}).

The diffusion basis
\{\psi_{i}({\mathbf{z}})\}_{i\geq 0}
is composed of the eigenfunctions of this operator. We sort them according to their eigenvalues,
1=\lambda_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq 0
. These eigenfunctions are orthonormal with respect to the measure
S(\mathcal{C})\propto\int_{\mathcal{C}}p({\mathbf{z}})dP({\mathbf{z}})
, that is,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{i}({\mathbf{z}})\psi_{j}({\mathbf{z}})dS({\mathbf{z}})=\delta_{i,j}.

The diffusion basis
\{\psi_{i}({\mathbf{z}})\}_{i\geq 0}
adapts to the intrinsic geometry of the data, forming an efficient Fourier-like
orthogonal basis for smooth functions on the data
(
Lee and Izbicki 2016
)
. More precisely, they are concentrated around high-density regions, where
lower-order terms are smoother than higher-order terms.
Thus, this basis can efficiently model smooth functions in
\mathcal{X}
, meaning that only the first few terms of the series are needed. Moreover, they have the property that

\psi_{0}({\mathbf{z}})\equiv 1
.
As we will see in the next section, this property is key to develop our test statistic.

2.2
Rewriting the null hypothesis

We now show how diffusion basis can be used to model the distribution of the data and how it can be used to test goodness-of-fit.
Let
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be independent and identically distributed to

F
. Our goal is to test the null hypothesis

H_{0}:F=F_{0}
. We assume that
F_{0}
dominates
F
.
The null hypothesis is therefore equivalent to

H_{0}:h({\mathbf{x}})\equiv 1,

where

h({\mathbf{x}}):=\dfrac{dF}{dF_{0}}

is the Radon–Nikodym density of
F

with respect to
F_{0}
.

Next, let
\{\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})\}_{i\geq 0}
be the diffusion basis with respect to
F_{0}
. Notice that as long as
h\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{X},S)
, this density admits the representation

\displaystyle h({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{i\geq 0}\theta_{i}\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}}),

(1)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h({\mathbf{x}})\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})dS({\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\dfrac{dF}{dF_{0}}({\mathbf{x}})\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})s({\mathbf{x}})dF_{0}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})s({\mathbf{x}})dF({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{F}[\psi_{i}({\mathbf{X}})s({\mathbf{X}})].

(2)

Now, Equation
2.2
implies that the projection of
any density into
\psi_{0}
is 1, and thus
\theta_{0}=1
. Moreover, the projection of
the function
g({\mathbf{x}})\equiv 1
on the basis
is given by
and
\theta_{i}=0
,
i>0
. Thus, the null hypothesis may be written as

\displaystyle H_{0}:\theta_{0}=1\text{ and }\theta_{i}=0\text{ for every }i>0.

(3)

It follows that we can test the null hypothesis by testing whether the expansion coefficients are zero.

2.3
Estimating the expansion coefficient

The expansion coefficients in Equation
3
are parameters. In this section we discuss how to estimate them, and in the next section we show how to use such estimates to test
H_{0}
.

First we will estimate the diffusion basis by using the approach from
Lee and Wasserman 2010b
.
We start by generating an i.i.d. sample from
F_{0}
with size
m
,
{\mathbf{Y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{Y}}_{m}
. Next, let
\mathbb{K}
be the
m\times m
Gram matrix whose
(i,j)
entrance is given by
k({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})
. For simplicity, we use the gaussian kernel,

k({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})=\exp{\left(-\frac{d^{2}({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})}{\epsilon}\right)},

(4)

although other kernels could be used as well. Next, we renormalize each row of the matrix according to
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{D}^{-1}\mathbb{K},
where
\mathbb{D}
is the a diagonal matrix where the element
(i,i)
is
\sum_{l=1}^{m}K({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{l})
.
Finally, we compute the
I
first right eigenvectors of
\mathbb{A}
following Appendix
A
,
\widehat{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{y}}_{1}),\ldots,\widehat{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{y}}_{m}),

i=0,\ldots,I
(we discuss the choice of
I
in Section
2.4
).
Our estimate of
\psi_{i}({\mathbf{x}})
is given by the Nyström extension of such eigenvectors:

\widehat{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{k({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})}{\sum_{l=1}^{n}k({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}_{l})}\widehat{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{y}}_{j}),

where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
.

Equation
2.2
suggests the following estimator to the expansion coefficients:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{\psi}_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})\mathbf{s}({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),

where

\mathbf{s}({\mathbf{x}}_{k})=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})}{\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}k_{\epsilon}({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{l})}=\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}k({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})}{\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}k({\mathbf{y}}_{i},{\mathbf{y}}_{l})}.

2.4
Test statistic

Inspired by Neyman’s test statistic, we can test
H_{0}
by using the statistic

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}=(\widehat{\theta}_{0}-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\widehat{\theta}_{i}^{2},

where
\lambda
denotes the dependence of the test statistic on tuning parameters associated to the kernel (such as the bandwidth
\epsilon
in the case of a Gaussian kernel) as well as on the cutoff
I
. The p-value against
H_{0}
,
p_{\lambda}:=P_{0}(T_{\lambda}^{*}>t_{\lambda}^{*(obs)})
, can be estimated via Monte Carlo sampling at
F_{0}
. Let
\widehat{p}_{\lambda}
be such estimate.

Instead of relying solely on a single choice
\lambda
, we combine several of these choices into a new test statistic. As we will show in Section
3.1
, this will lead to robust results.
In order to do so, let
{\mathbb{E}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})
and
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})
denote the mean and variance of
T_{\lambda}
under the null hypothesis, and let
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})
and
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})
be estimates of these quantities obtained by Monte Carlo sampling at
F_{0}
. First, we define
T_{\lambda}^{*}
, a re-scaled version of
T_{\lambda}
, according to:

\displaystyle T^{*}_{\lambda}=\frac{T_{\lambda}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{0}(T_{\lambda})}}.

The SST combined test statistic is given by

\displaystyle T_{SST}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(1-\widehat{p}_{\lambda})|T_{\lambda}^{*}|,

where
\Lambda
denotes a set of predefined choices
for
\lambda
. The idea is that if
T_{\lambda}
is close to what is expected under the null, then the p-value associated to
\lambda
should not receive a large weight relative to the other ones. Thus, the combined statistic weights p-values according to
|T^{*}_{\lambda}|
.

Again, p-values for
T_{SST}
can be approximated by Monte Carlo sampling at
F_{0}
.

Application to MNIST dataset

In this section we apply SST to the MNIST dataset
(
Deng 2012
)
and show that how our test can be used to interpret the difference between the null hypothesis and the true distribution of the data.

MNIST is a dataset composed by handwritten digits of two groups, students and employees. It is a modification of NIST (
https://www.nist.gov/srd/nist-special-database-19
) that attempted to minimize the differences between train and test data observed in that dataset.

First, we applied our test to detect potential differences between the distribution of train and test dataset. That is, the null hypothesis establishes that the train and test data have the same distribution over the images. We sample from
F_{0}
by sampling with replacement from the train set.
Our test yields a
p-value smaller than 0.001, and thus we conclude there exists a difference between the training and testing data. Next, for each

, we test:

H^{d}_{0}:F_{\text{train}}({\mathbf{x}}|d)=F_{\text{test}}({\mathbf{x}}|d),

where

is the distribution over an image conditional on the digit being of type
d
. Table
1

shows that only digits
4
and

have p-values above
0.05

5
Final remarks

We have shown how smooth tests can be used on high-dimensional data. The key idea is to use spectral bases, which automatically adapt to the geometry of feature space.
We also created a version of our test that is robust to the selection of tuning parameters.

We have shown evidence that SST has high power, and also that it offers useful insights on how the distributions being tested differ. Moreover, it naturally yields an estimate of the density of the data if the null hypothesis is rejected.
This paper therefore solidifies the standing of smooth tests as a versatile tool in the analysis of high-dimensional data.
