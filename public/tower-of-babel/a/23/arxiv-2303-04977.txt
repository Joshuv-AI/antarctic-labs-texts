Quantum Ergotropy and Quantum Feedback Control
Kenta Koshihara, Kazuya Yuasa

Abstract
We study the energy extraction from and charging to a finite-dimensional quantum system by general quantum operations. We prove that the changes in energy induced by unital quantum operations are limited by the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary quantum operations. This implies that, in order to break the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary quantum operations, one needs to perform a quantum operation with feedback control. We also show that the ergotropy/charging bound for unital quantum operations, applied to initial thermal equilibrium states, is tighter than the inequality representing the standard second law of thermodynamics without feedback control.

I
Introduction

The energy extraction and charging are fundamental tasks in thermodynamics.
Pursuing the limitations on them leads us to the basic principles of thermodynamics.
For instance, the Gibbs states are understood to be characterized by their complete passivity (the impossibility of energy extraction from an arbitrary number of copies of them)
Pusz1978
;
Lenard1978
;
ThirringBook2002
, and the second law of thermodynamics can be phrased in terms of extractable work.
The maximum extractable work from a general nonpassive state of a quantum system via cyclic unitary operation is called ergotropy
Allahverdyan2004
and has been under intense study these years.
An interesting research direction related to the quantum ergotropy is the exploration for the enhancement of the ergotropy and the charging capacity by making use of various quantum features, such as entangling operations
Alicki2013
;
Hovhannisyan2013
;
Giorgi2015
;
Llobet2015PRE
, quantum correlations
Llobet2015PRX
;
Binder2015NJP
;
Salvia2022
;
Touli2022
;
Francica2022
, and quantum coherences
Francica2020
;
Cakmak2020
.

Another interesting feature to explore in quantum thermodynamics is quantum measurement.
The role of quantum measurement in quantum thermodynamics is not simply to acquire some information on quantum state.
Quantum measurement disturbs the state of the measured system, and thus changes the energy of the system.
In other words, quantum measurement can be used to extract/inject energy from/to a quantum working substance
Solfanelli2019
.
An extreme idea exploring this feature leads to the measurement-driven quantum heat engines
Elouard2017a
;
Elouard2017b
;
YiTalkerKim2017
;
Anders2017
;
Yi2017
;
Elouard2018
;
YiTalkerKim2018
;
Buffoni2019
;
Seah2020
;
Behzadi2021
;
Bresque2021
;
Anka2021
;
Lin2021
;
Manikandan2022
;
Lisboa2022
, where quantum measurement acts as a heat bath, fueling energy to the working substance.
Moreover, if one exploits the information acquired by a measurement, applying an additional feedback control depending on the outcome of the measurement, one would be able to extract more energy than the ergotropy and to break the bound given by the change in free energy representing the standard second law of thermodynamics without feedback control
Sagawa2008
;
Maruyama2009
;
Jacobs2009
;
MorikuniTasaki2011
;
Esposito2011
;
Funo2013
;
ParrondoHorowitzSagawa2015
;
Goold2016
;
Francica2017
;
Esposito2017
;
Manzano2018
;
Bernards2019
;
Landi2021
;
Koshihara2022
.

In this paper, we discuss energy extraction and charging beyond unitary operations, in particular focussing on the roles of quantum measurement and quantum feedback control.
We wish to clarify which kind of quantum operations can break the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations.
We give a clear answer to this question: any quantum operations without feedback control cannot break the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations.
This clarifies that
the feedback control is necessary to break the bound for unitary operations
.
This is the main message of the paper.

There are studies on feedback strategies for the energy extraction and charging via continuous measurements in open-system scenarios, e.g. to counteract the effects of noise
Mitchison2021
and to recover the information leaking to the environment
Morrone2023
.
Our objective is not to counteract the environmental effects, and our problem is much simpler:
we just study the change in the energy of a
d
-dimensional quantum system by a general quantum operation.
The setup of the problem is presented in Sec.
II
.
The key observation is that generic quantum operations can be interpreted as quantum feedback processes.
They are indistinguishable from processes consisting of bare quantum measurements
Jacobs2009
;
JacobsBook2014
(or minimally disturbing quantum measurements
WisemanMilburnBook2010
) followed by quantum feedback operations.
This point is recalled in Sec.
III
.
We also point out that quantum operations without feedback structure are unital.
Conversely, we show that the output state
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
of a unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
for a given initial state
\rho
can be yielded by a quantum map without feedback structure.
The unitality is thus characterized by the unnecessity of feedback control.
Then, in Sec.
IV
, we prove that the energy gain by any unital quantum operation is bounded by the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations.
This implies
the necessity of the feedback structure in quantum operation to break the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations
.
As an application, in Sec.
V
, we apply our bound to initial thermal equilibrium states to see an implication of our result for the second law of thermodynamics.
We show that our ergotropy/charging bound for unital operations provides a tighter bound than the standard second law of thermodynamics without feedback control.
These results are based on the mathematical theorems proved in Appendices
A
–
C
.

II
setup

We are going to study how much energy one can extract from or charge to a
d
-dimensional quantum system by a general quantum operation
\Phi
.
Here, we focus on quantum operations
\Phi
represented by completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps
Nielsen2010
;
Hayashi2015
;
ref:DynamicalMap-Alicki
; we do not consider trace-decreasing maps, such as quantum operations with postselections.
Suppose that before a quantum operation
\Phi
the system is in a state
\rho
with a Hamiltonian
H
.
By the quantum operation
\Phi
, the state of the system is changed from
\rho
to
\rho^{\prime}
,

\rho\to\rho^{\prime}=\Phi(\rho),

(1)

during which the Hamiltonian may be steered from
H
to
H^{\prime}
.
The gain of the energy by the quantum operation
\Phi
is given by

\Delta E(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}\rho^{\prime})-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho).

(2)

We wish to clarify the bound on
\Delta E(\rho)
, i.e., the maximum extractable/chargeable energy, by general quantum operations
\Phi
, in particular focusing on the roles of quantum measurement and quantum feedback control.

III
Structure of Quantum Operation

III.1
General Quantum Operations and Feedback Control

Before starting to investigate the energy extraction and charging, let us recall the structure of general quantum operation.
The quantum operations of the most basic kind are the unitary operations,

\Phi_{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger},

(3)

represented by unitary operators
U
, satisfying
U^{\dagger}U=\openone
.
In quantum mechanics, however, one can think of more general quantum operations.
General quantum operation
\Phi
(without postselection) is described by a CPTP map, and is given by the Kraus representation
ref:DynamicalMap-Alicki
;
Nielsen2010
;
Hayashi2015

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger},

(4)

with
\{K_{i}\}
a set of Kraus operators satisfying
\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=\openone
.
Interesting and relevant examples of nonunitary operation include quantum measurements.
They provoke nonunitary changes in quantum state and are regarded as quantum operations
Nielsen2010
;
Hayashi2015
.
If one accepts any outcomes of a quantum measurement without postselection (nonselective measurement), the net effect of the quantum measurement on quantum state is described by a CPTP map (
4
).
Feedback control, where an additional unitary operation is applied depending on the outcome of the measurement, also results in a nonunitary transformation on quantum state.

Generic quantum operations represented by the Kraus representation (
4
) are actually regarded as (or indistinguishable from) quantum feedback controls.
To see this, consider the polar decomposition of each Kraus operator
K_{i}
of the general quantum operation
\Phi
in (
4
),

K_{i}=U_{i}M_{i},\quad M_{i}\geq 0,

(5)

where
M_{i}
is a positive-semidefinite operator and
U_{i}
is a unitary
Nielsen2010
;
WisemanMilburnBook2010
;
HornJohnson2012
;
JacobsBook2014
;
Hayashi2015
.
Then, the map (
4
) is written as

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i}U_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}U_{i}^{\dagger},

(6)

and can be interpreted as a quantum feedback process, where a measurement represented by the set of measurement operators
\{M_{i}\}
is performed and then a unitary control
U_{i}
is applied depending on the outcome
i
of the measurement
Jacobs2009
;
JacobsBook2014
;
Koshihara2022
.
See Fig.
1
.
Note that the normalization condition on the Kraus operators
\{K_{i}\}
is translated to
\sum_{i}M_{i}^{2}=\openone
, and the operators
\{M_{i}\}
are valid measurement operators.
We will see that the presence of such a feedback structure in a quantum operation
\Phi
, whether or not it is intentionally implemented by an experimenter, makes a difference in the maximum extractable or chargeable energy on a quantum system:
the feedback structure is necessary to go beyond the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations
.

Quantum operation
\Phi
as a quantum feedback control.

III.2
Quantum Operations without Feedback Control

The quantum measurement process

\Phi_{\mathrm{ms}}(\rho)=\sum_{i}M_{i}\rho M_{i}

(7)

represented by the positive-semidefinite measurement operators
\{M_{i}\}
, with unitaries removed from the Kraus operators, is called “bare” quantum measurement
Jacobs2009
;
JacobsBook2014
or “minimally disturbing” quantum measurement
WisemanMilburnBook2010
.
It is considered as a “pure” quantum measurement, without any feedback operations involved.
Projective measurements, with the measurement operators
\{M_{i}\}
given by orthogonal projections
\{P_{i}\}
satisfying
P_{i}=P_{i}^{\dagger}
,
P_{i}P_{j}=P_{i}\delta_{ij}
, and
\sum_{i}P_{i}=\openone
, are the canonical examples of the bare quantum measurement.

Even if an additional unitary operation
U
is applied after such a bare quantum measurement as

(\Phi_{U}\circ\Phi_{\mathrm{ms}})(\rho)=\sum_{i}UM_{i}\rho M_{i}U^{\dagger},

(8)

it is not considered as a feedback control, since the unitary
U
is just applied irrespective of the outcome
i
of the measurement.
We point out that the absence of the feedback structure in quantum operation is closely related to the unitality of operation.
A quantum operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
is called unital if it preserves the identity,

\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\openone)=\openone.

(9)

Any quantum operations of the type (
8
), without feedback structure, are unital, satisfying the unitality condition (
9
).
Its contrapositive says that, if a quantum operation
\Phi
is not unital,
\Phi(\openone)\neq\openone
, then it is for sure endowed with a feedback structure as (
6
), involving different unitaries
\{U_{i}\}
in the Kraus operators.
The converse is not necessarily true: there exist unital maps
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
that are endowed with feedback structures.
However, the following variant of the converse is true: given a unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
and an input state
\rho
, there exist a set of orthogonal rank-1 projections
\{P_{i}\}
and a unitary
U
such that
footnote:UnitalFeedback

\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}UP_{i}\rho P_{i}U^{\dagger}.

(10)

We prove it in Appendix
A
.
This means that, for each input state
\rho
, the same effect as the one induced by a unital operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
can be achieved without feedback control.
In this way, the unitality of an operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
is characterized by the unnecessity of feedback control.

IV
Necessity of Feedback Control to Break the Ergotropy Bound

Let us now investigate the energy extraction and charging by general quantum operations.

IV.1
Generalized Ergotropy/Charging Bound

We first review the ergotropy bound and provide a generalization of it.
In its original formulation
Allahverdyan2004
, ergotropy refers to the maximum extractable energy via unitary operation
\Phi_{U}
realized by driving the Hamiltonian of the working substance in a cyclic manner.
Let
\Delta E_{U}(\rho)
be the energy gain (
2
) obtained by performing a unitary operation
\Phi_{U}
on the working substance in the state
\rho
.
After the operation, the driven Hamiltonian is brought back to the initial one
H
.
Then, one can prove that the following lower bound on
\Delta E_{U}(\rho)
holds,

\Delta E_{U}(\rho)\geq-\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{-}\quad\mathrm{if}\quad H\to H,

(11)

with

\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{-}=\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow},

(12)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}
is the
d
-dimensional vector consisting of the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian
H
arranged in the increasing order
\varepsilon_{1}^{\uparrow}\leq\varepsilon_{2}^{\uparrow}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{d}^{\uparrow}
, and
\bm{r}^{\downarrow}
is the
d
-dimensional vector consisting of the eigenvalues of the initial density operator
\rho
arranged in the decreasing order
r_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq r_{2}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq r_{d}^{\downarrow}
.
In the following,
\bm{v}^{{\uparrow}(\downarrow)}
of a vector
\bm{v}
means the vector obtained by rearranging the elements of
\bm{v}
in the increasing (decreasing) order.
The quantity
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{-}\,(\geq 0)
in (
12
) is always nonnegative (see Appendix
B
), giving the maximum extractable energy from the system, and it is called ergotropy.
It depends on the initial state
\rho
and the initial/final Hamiltonian
H

footnote:Unknown
.
This bound is achievable by a suitable unitary operation
\Phi_{U}
.

As noted in Ref.
Binder2015NJP
, one can also derive the upper bound on
\Delta E_{U}(\rho)
, i.e., the maximum chargeable energy on the system.
Moreover, one can generalize the bound to accommodate the situations where the driven Hamiltonian is not brought back to the initial one
H
but is left
H^{\prime}
at the end of the unitary operation
\Phi_{U}
.
The bound valid for such a generalized situation reads

\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}-\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\rho}\leq\Delta E_{U}(\rho)\leq\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}+\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\rho},

(13)

with

\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{+}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho),

(14)

where
\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}
, with
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}
consisting of the eigenvalues of the final Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
in the increasing order.
The quantity
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{+}\,(\geq 0)
is also always nonnegative (see Appendix
B
), and gives the upper bound on
\Delta E_{U}(\rho)
, i.e., the maximum chargeable energy on the system, when the Hamiltonian is brought back to the initial one
H^{\prime}=H
.
We provide the proof of the generalized bound (
13
) in Appendix
B
.
Both lower and upper bounds of (
13
) are tight, and can be reached by suitable unitary operations
\Phi_{U}
.

IV.2
Ergotropy/Charging Bound for Unital Quantum Operations

We now present the key result of this paper.
Let
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
be the energy gain (
2
) obtained by performing a unital quantum operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
on the working substance in the state
\rho
.
The initial Hamiltonian of the working substance is
H
, which may be changed to
H^{\prime}
at the end of the unital operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
.
Then, the following bound on
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
holds,

\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}-\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\rho}\leq\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)\leq\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}+\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\rho}.

(15)

This is exactly the same as the ergotropy/charging bound presented in (
13
) for the energy gain
\Delta E_{U}(\rho)
obtained by a unitary operation
\Phi_{U}
.
Note that both lower and upper bounds of (
15
) are tight.
In fact, unitary operations
\Phi_{U}
are special instances of unital operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
, and the lower and upper bounds of (
15
) are achieved by the unitary operations
\Phi_{U}
saturating the lower and upper bounds of (
13
).

The bound (
15
) shows that any unital operations
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
cannot extract or charge energy beyond the ergotropy/charging bound (
13
) for unitary operations
\Phi_{U}
.
This implies, for instance, that the energy change induced by the backaction of a bare quantum measurement
\Phi_{\mathrm{ms}}
is limited by the ergotropy/charging bound (
13
) (cf., the energy extraction by projective measurement is studied in Ref.
Solfanelli2019
).
More interestingly, since the nonunitality requires some feedback structure in the operation, as clarified in Sec.
III.2
, the bound (
15
) reveals that,
in order to extract or charge energy beyond the ergotropy/charging bound (
13
), a feedback control is necessary
.
This is the main message of this paper.
It is indeed possible to break the ergotropy/charging bound (
13
) via a feedback control, as we will see later with some simple examples.

Let us prove the bound (
15
).
We just have to recall Uhlmann’s representation theorem for unital maps
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
: given a unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
and an input state
\rho
, there exist a probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}
and a set of unitaries
\{U_{i}\}
such that

\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d!}p_{i}U_{i}\rho U_{i}^{\dagger}.

(16)

See, e.g., Refs.
Nielsen2001
;
Nielsen2010
;
Sagawa2022
.
This means that, for each input state
\rho
, the same output state
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
as the one yielded by the unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
can be obtained by a random unitary operation as (
16
)
footnote:RandomUnitary
;
footnote:MarcusReeTheorem
.
This provides another characterization of the unitality of an operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
, alternative to the characterization presented in (
10
).
Using this representation (
16
), one can bound the energy gain
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
obtained by a unital quantum operation
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
as

\displaystyle\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)={}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[H^{\prime}\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)]-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)\vphantom{\sum_{i}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U_{i}\rho U_{i}^{\dagger})-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\max_{U}\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U\rho U^{\dagger})-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max_{U}\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U\rho U^{\dagger})-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)\vphantom{\sum_{i}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\max_{U}\Delta E_{U}(\rho)\vphantom{\sum_{i}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}+\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\rho}.

(17)

This proves the upper bound of (
15
).
The last equality is due to the upper bound of (
13
) for unitary operations
\Phi_{U}
.
The lower bound of (
15
) is proven in the same way, but
\max_{U}
in (
17
) is replaced by
\min_{U}
and the lower bound of (
13
) is used instead of the upper bound.

V
Application to Equilibrium Initial State

Let us focus on systems initially in thermal equilibrium, to see how our main result (
15
) is relevant to the second law of thermodynamics.

V.1
Second Law of Thermodynamics

Let us first recall a generalized version of the second law of thermodynamics
Sagawa2008
;
Esposito2011
.
Consider the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}/Z_{\beta}
at an inverse temperature
\beta\,(>0)
.
After some operation, the state is changed from
\rho_{\beta}
to
\rho^{\prime}
, and the Hamiltonian is changed from
H
to
H^{\prime}
.
A generalized version of the second law of thermodynamics
Sagawa2008
;
Esposito2011
says that the energy gain
\Delta E(\rho_{\beta})=\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}\rho^{\prime})-\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho_{\beta})
from the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
is bounded by

\Delta E(\rho_{\beta})\geq\Delta F_{\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta S(\rho_{\beta}),

(19)

where
\Delta S(\rho_{\beta})=S(\rho^{\prime})-S(\rho_{\beta})
is the change in the von Neumann entropy from the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
, and
\Delta F_{\beta}=F_{\beta}^{\prime}-F_{\beta}
is the difference between the free energies
F_{\beta}=-\beta^{-1}\log Z_{\beta}
and
F^{\prime}_{\beta}=-\beta^{-1}\log Z^{\prime}_{\beta}
defined through the partition functions
Z_{\beta}=\operatorname{Tr}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}
and
Z_{\beta}^{\prime}=\operatorname{Tr}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H^{\prime}}
based on the initial and final Hamiltonians
H
and
H^{\prime}
, respectively.
The inequality (
19
) is a direct consequence of the inequality between the equilibrium and nonequilibrium free energies [Eq. (11) of Ref.
Esposito2011
, Eq. (42) of Ref.
Landi2021
, and Eq. (8.5) of Ref.
Sagawa2022
],

f_{\beta}(\rho,H)\geq F_{\beta},

(20)

valid for any density operator
\rho
, any Hamiltonian
H
, and any positive inverse temperature
\beta\,(>0)
.
While the equilibrium free energy
F_{\beta}
is defined through the partition function
Z_{\beta}
as above, the nonequilibrium free energy
f_{\beta}(\rho,H)
is defined for an arbitrary (generally nonequilibrium) state
\rho
by
f_{\beta}(\rho,H)=\operatorname{Tr}(H\rho)-S(\rho)/\beta
.
The equality
f_{\beta}(\rho,H)=F_{\beta}
holds if and only if
\rho=\rho_{\beta}=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}/Z_{\beta}
.
The inequality (
19
) is obtained immediately from the inequality
f_{\beta}(\rho^{\prime},H^{\prime})\geq F_{\beta}^{\prime}
applied to the final state
\rho^{\prime}
and the final Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
.

In particular, if the operation performed on the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
is unital, it induces a positive entropy change
\Delta S_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})\geq 0

HolevoBook2019
;
Sagawa2022
, as noted in Sec.
IV.3
, and the generalized second law of thermodynamics (
19
) is reduced to

\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})\geq\Delta F_{\beta}.

(21)

This reproduces a version of the standard second law of thermodynamics without feedback control.
Note that the bound (
21
) can also be derived from fluctuation relations for unitary operations
Tasaki2000
;
Talkner2007
;
Campisi2011
and for unital operations
MorikuniTasaki2011
;
Rastegin2013
;
Goold2021
.
In the following, we will call the bound (
21
) free-energy bound.
If one performs a nonunital operation, endowed with a feedback structure, on the other hand, it can decrease the entropy,
\Delta S(\rho_{\beta})<0
, and the free-energy bound (
21
) can be broken
MorikuniTasaki2011
.
In other words, feedback control is necessary to break the free-energy bound (
21
).

V.2
Tightness of the Ergotropy Bound

The energy gain
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})
by unital quantum operations from the initial thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
at a positive inverse temperature
\beta\,(>0)
is bounded from below by the free-energy bound (
21
).
On the other hand, the same quantity
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})
is also bounded from below by the ergotropy bound (
15
).
One can show that the latter is always tighter than the former.

Notice first that the thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
is a passive state with respect to the initial Hamiltonian
H
, and hence
\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{-}=0
.
Then, the ergotropy bound of (
15
) on
\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})
is reduced to

\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})\geq\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}_{\beta},

(22)

where
\bm{r}_{\beta}^{\downarrow}
is the
d
-dimensional vector consisting of the eigenvalues of the density operator
\rho_{\beta}
of the initial thermal equilibrium state, arranged in the decreasing order.
This lower bound
\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}_{\beta}
is further bounded from below by
\Delta F_{\beta}
.
In fact, recalling again
\mathcal{E}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{-}=0
and letting
U^{-}
be the optimal unitary achieving
\Delta E_{U^{-}}(\rho_{\beta})=\min_{U}\Delta E_{U}(\rho_{\beta})
, the ergotropy bound in (
13
) on the energy gain
\Delta E_{U}(\rho_{\beta})
by unitary operations yields

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}_{\beta}={}

\displaystyle\min_{U}\Delta E_{U}(\rho_{\beta})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Delta E_{U^{-}}(\rho_{\beta})-\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta S_{U^{-}}(\rho_{\beta})

\displaystyle\geq{}

\displaystyle\Delta F_{\beta},

(23)

where the second equality is due to the invariance of the von Neumann entropy under a unitary operation,
\Delta S_{U}(\rho)=S\bm{(}\Phi_{U}(\rho)\bm{)}-S(\rho)=0
, and the last inequality is due to the generalized second law of thermodynamics (
19
), which is based on the inequality (
20
), namely, in the present case,
f_{\beta}\bm{(}\Phi_{U^{-}}(\rho_{\beta}),H^{\prime}\bm{)}\geq F_{\beta}^{\prime}
.
The equality hence holds if and only if
\Phi_{U^{-}}(\rho_{\beta})=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/Z^{\prime}_{\beta}
.
We have thus proven that

\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{\beta})\geq\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}_{\beta}\geq\Delta F_{\beta},

(24)

that is, the ergotropy bound of (
15
) for the initial thermal equilibrium state
\rho_{\beta}
is tighter than the free-energy bound (
21
).
The two bounds coincide if and only if the initial Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
can be transformed into the Gibbs state
\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H^{\prime}}/Z^{\prime}_{\beta}
of the final Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
at the same inverse temperature
\beta
by the unitary operation
\Phi_{U^{-}}
saturating the ergotropy bound for the initial Gibbs state.

Similar inequalities hold for initial Gibbs states
\rho_{-\beta}=\mathrm{e}^{\beta H}/Z_{-\beta}
at negative inverse temperatures
-\beta\,(<0)
,

\Delta E_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho_{-\beta})\leq\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}_{-\beta}\leq\Delta F_{-\beta}.

(25)

This can be proven by using, instead of the inequality (
20
),

f_{-\beta}(\rho,H)\leq F_{-\beta},

(26)

valid for any density operator
\rho
, any Hamiltonian
H
, and any negative inverse temperature
-\beta\,(<0)
.
The flip of the inequality is simply due to the negative sign of the inverse temperature
-\beta\,(<0)
.
See Appendix
C
for its proof.

VI
conclusions

We have studied the energy extraction and charging by general quantum operations, in particular focusing on the roles of quantum measurement and quantum feedback control.
We have shown that the energy gain by a unital quantum operation is bounded by the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations.
This implies that, in order to extract/charge energy beyond the ergotropy/charging bound for unitary operations, feedback control is necessary.
We have also shown that the ergotropy/charging bound proven here for unital operations is tighter than the standard second law of thermodynamics.

In Ref.
Solfanelli2019
, the maximum extractable energy by projective measurement is studied, and it is shown to be strictly smaller than the ergotropy.
It appears that, if one performs quantum measurement but does not apply any feedback control, the ergotropy/charging bound (
15
) for unital quantum operations is not saturable.
It would be intriguing to explore the tight bound on the energy gain by general quantum measurement, to acquire better understanding on the possible roles and the limitations of quantum measurement in quantum thermodynamics.

Appendix A
Proof of the Representation (
10
) of Unital Map

We here provide the proof of the decomposition of unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}
in (
10
).
Let
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}|r_{i}\rangle\langle r_{i}|
be a density operator in the diagonalized form, and
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}^{\prime}|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\prime}|
be the eigenvalue decomposition of the output state of a unital map
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
.
It is known that the output state
\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
of a unital map is majorized by the input state
\rho
, i.e.,
\rho\succ\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho)
, or equivalently
\bm{r}\succ\bm{r}^{\prime}
, which means
\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}^{\downarrow}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}^{{\prime}{\downarrow}}
, for all
k=1,\ldots,d
(Theorem 6.1 of Ref.
Sagawa2022
).
Then, the Schur-Horn theorem ensures that there exists a Hermitian matrix
X
whose eigenvalues and diagonal elements are given by the elements of
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
, respectively (Theorem 4.3.48 of Ref.
HornJohnson2012
and Theorem 9.B.2 of Ref.
Marshall2011
).
Since
X
is diagonalizable with a unitary matrix
V
as
X=V\operatorname{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})V^{\dagger}
, the diagonal elements
X_{ii}=r_{i}^{\prime}
satisfy

r_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|V_{ij}|^{2}r_{j}.

(27)

Now, let us take a complete set of orthonormal basis vectors
\{|{\psi_{i}}\rangle\}
fulfilling
V_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i}|r_{j}\rangle
, and construct orthogonal projections
P_{i}=|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|
.
In addition, let
U
be the unitary connecting the basis vectors as
U|\psi_{i}\rangle=|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle
.
Then, these elements yield

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}UP_{i}\rho P_{i}U^{\dagger}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}U|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|r_{j}\rangle r_{j}\langle r_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|V_{ij}|^{2}r_{j}|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}^{\prime}|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathrm{unital}}(\rho),\vphantom{\sum_{i=1}^{d}}

(28)

which proves the representation (
10
).
Note that the projectors
\{P_{i}\}
and the unitary
U
depend on the input state
\rho
.

Appendix B
Proof of the Generalized Ergotropy Bound (
13
)

In this appendix, we prove the generalized ergotropy bound (

\Phi_{U}
.
It suffices to prove

\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\downarrow}\leq\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U\rho U^{\dagger})\leq\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}.

(29)

We prove it in basically the same way as the one used for the proof of passivity on

-dimensional quantum systems
Lenard1978
;
ThirringBook2002
;
Koukoulekidis2021
.
See also Theorem 4.3.53 of Ref.
HornJohnson2012

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U\rho U^{\dagger})={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}|\langle\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}|U|r_{j}\rangle|^{2}r_{j}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\cdot B\bm{r},

(30)

where

and

\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
and
r_{j}
of
H^{\prime}
and
\rho

(31)

d

Marshall2011

Zhang2011

\bm{x}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{y}^{\downarrow}\leq\bm{x}\cdot\Pi_{k}\bm{y}\leq\bm{x}^{\uparrow}\cdot\bm{y}^{\uparrow},

(32)

for any pair of
d
-dimensional real vectors
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
, and for all permutations

.
Then, using its upper bound, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(H^{\prime}U\rho U^{\dagger})={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d!}p_{k}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\cdot\Pi_{k}\bm{r}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d!}p_{k}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}^{\prime\uparrow}\cdot\bm{r}^{\uparrow},

(33)

which is the upper bound of (
29
).
The lower bound of (
29

and

U^{-(+)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\uparrow}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\downarrow(\uparrow)}|

\rho

;
Lenard1978
(maximally active
Binder2015NJP

\rho_{P(A)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}^{\downarrow(\uparrow)}|\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\uparrow}\rangle\langle\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime\uparrow}|
with respect to the final Hamiltonian

Note that the bound (

.
By using (

Appendix C
Proof of the Inequality (

) for the Nonequilibrium Free Energy at a Negative Temperature

The inequality (
20
) at a positive inverse temperature
\beta>0
is a fundamental relation between the equilibrium and nonequilibrium free energies,
F_{\beta}
and
f_{\beta}(\rho,H)

;
Esposito2011
