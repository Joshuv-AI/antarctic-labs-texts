Proportions of Incommensurate, Resonant, and Chaotic Orbits for Torus Maps
E. Sander, J.D. Meiss

Abstract
This paper focuses on distinguishing classes of dynamical behavior for one- and two-dimensional torus maps, in particular between orbits that are incommensurate, resonant, periodic, or chaotic. We first consider Arnold’s circle map, for which there is a universal power law for the fraction of nonresonant orbits as a function of the amplitude of the nonlinearity. Our methods give a more precise calculation of the coefficients for this power law. For two-dimensional torus maps, we show that there is no such universal law for any of the classes of orbits. However, we find different categories of maps with qualitatively similar behavior. Our results are obtained using three fast and high precision numerical methods: weighted Birkhoff averages, Farey trees, and resonance orders.



We study nonlinear one- and two-dimensional torus maps, starting with Arnold’s circle map.

IV.1
Resonance and Incommensurability

Perhaps the most natural generalization of the rational verses irrational
dichotomy for rotation numbers to higher dimensions is to ask whether
a vector has rational components, i.e.,
\omega=\tfrac{p}{q}

for some
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
,
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
A more general concept, that of
commensurability
,
resonance
,
or
mode-locking
, corresponds to the existence of
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

such that

\omega\in{\cal R}_{m,n}=\left\{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:m\cdot\alpha=n\right\},

(9)

a codimension-one plane. Such an
\omega
has
resonance order

M=\|m\|_{1}
if
this is the smallest length of a (nonzero) vector
m

for which
\omega\in{\cal R}_{m,n}
.
The set of vectors that do not lie in any resonant plane are
incommensurate
, or
nonresonant
.
An example is
\omega=(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{5})
. When
f
is conjugate
to a rigid rotation (
4
), these orbits are dense on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

For
d=2
, the sets
{\cal R}_{m,n}
are lines; these are shown in Fig.
4

up to order
M=7
.
The
rank
of a resonance for a given
\omega
is the number of independent commensurability vectors
m
;
i.e., the dimension of the module of resonance vectors.
In the figure, the rank-two frequency vectors are the points at which nonparallel lines intersect.
Note that
\omega
is rational only if it has rank two: these correspond to eventually periodic orbits.

Critical Parameter

In this section, we establish the existence of a critical amplitude,
\varepsilon_{\rm crit}
, so that
the map (
1

1

The experience with circle maps indicates that local invertibility
should be important dynamically.
Note however that even when

this lemma does not guarantee the existence of a conjugacy nor the absence of chaotic orbits.

Recall from Lem.
1

\displaystyle\det(Df)

\displaystyle=\det\left(I+\varepsilon H\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\det H+\varepsilon\;{\rm tr}H+1,

\displaystyle\varepsilon H\equiv Dg

V
Conclusions and Future Directions

In this paper, we have used efficient techniques to characterize the dynamics of orbits of
one- and two-dimensional torus maps.
We used the convergence rate of the WBA to distinguish between chaotic and regular
orbits, defining a threshold for the precision,
\mathop{\mathrm{dig}}\nolimits_{T}
(
17
), after a fixed number of iterates
T
.
Given an accurate value for rotation vector
\omega_{T}
, we determined if the vector is nearly resonant by finding the smallest order of a resonance vector within a distance
\delta
. This allows use
to characterize regular orbits as incommensurate (nonresonant), rank-one (resonant), or rank-two (periodic).
This computation is especially efficient for the 1D case,
where we can use the Farey tree to compute the minimum denominator (
23
).
[
9
]

We hope in the future that an efficient algorithm to compute (
31
) for the 2D case can be found.
Meanwhile, we used a brute force method, following our previous work on volume-preserving maps.
[
10
]

Our methods naturally extend to higher dimensional maps.
However, there are some significant computational challenges for
d>2
.
The WBA method extends without any difficulty to three and higher dimensions;
[
10
]

other than the standard problem of needing more initial conditions and iterates to get a sense of the full dynamics,
there are no difficulties to distinguish between chaotic and regular orbits.
Finding periodic (ie. rank-
d
resonant) orbits in dimension
d
is also just a matter of
checking that the rotation vector is rational in each component.

However, there are computational issues that may limit our ability to distinguish lower rank resonances.
In App.
A
, we observed that for random values in
{\mathbb{T}}^{1}
the typical denominator of
\delta
-close rational approximation to an irrational scales as
q_{min}\sim\delta^{-1/2}
, recall (
24
).
Of course
q_{min}
is the 1D version of the resonance order
M
, (
31
).
In dimension two, for random values in
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
, we observed that
M
scales as

M\sim\delta^{-1/3}
, recall (
32
).
There is theoretical support for the
d=1
result
[
43
]
and
Marklof [44]
,
Marklof [45]
has shown that
for the general
d
-dimensional case (
32
) becomes

\langle\log_{10}(M(\omega,\delta))\rangle\approx-\tfrac{1}{d+1}\log_{10}\delta+c_{d}\;.

(13)

This is a new torment to add to the usual “curse of dimensionality.”
As
d
grows, the typical resonance order of a theoretically incommensurate vector will be pushed closer to zero
for a given precision
\delta
.
For example, if for
d=4
we were to use the same precision,
\delta=10^{-9}
,
that we used in the current paper, then typical resonance order would be
M\sim 10^{9/5}\sim 63
.
Therefore the computed order of an incommensurate
\omega
would
be small enough that it would be quite hard to distinguish it from a vector that is actually resonant
with even a modest resonance order.
In order to be able to distinguish these values, one would need the added computational expense of extended precision;
moreover, the computation of a frequency
\omega
to higher accuracy would also require increasing the
number of iterates
T
. The calculations would quickly become very slow.
We hope that further research in this area will prove us to be overly pessimistic.

A.1
Regularity vs. Chaos: Weighted Birkhoff Averages

We briefly review here the weighted Birkhoff average
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
and how it
distinguishes between regular and chaotic orbits.
[
9
]
Given a map
f:M\to M
, recall that the time average of a function
h:M\to{\mathbb{R}}
along an orbit of
f
is simply

B(h)(z)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}h\circ f^{t}(z),

(14)

if this limit exists. The classic theorem of Birkhoff implies that if the orbit of
f
is ergodic on a
set with invariant measure
\mu
, and if
h\in L^{1}(M,{\mathbb{R}})
, then

B(h)(z)=\langle h\rangle=\int_{M}h\,d\mu

for
\mu
-almost every
z
. However, the convergence to this limit is at best as
1/T

and can be arbitrarily slow.
[
46
,
47
]
.

To compute the average efficiently and accurately for a length-
T
segment of an orbit,
we modify (
14
) using the
C^{\infty}
weight function

\Psi(s)\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{-[s(1-s)]^{-1}}&s\in(0,1)\\
0&s\leq 0\mbox{ or }s\geq 1\end{array}\right.\;.

This exponential bump function limits to zero with infinite smoothness
at
0
and
1
, i.e.,
\Psi^{(k)}(0)=\Psi^{(k)}(1)=0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The finite-time weighted Birkhoff average (WBA) is then defined by

\mathit{WB}_{T}(h)(z)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Psi\left(\tfrac{t}{T}\right)h\circ f^{t}(z)\;,

(15)

with the normalization constant

S\equiv\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Psi\left(\tfrac{t}{T}\right)\;.

(16)

As shown by
Das et al. [6]
, this gives the same answer as
T\to\infty

as (
14
); however, for regular orbits (
15
) can converge much more quickly.
In particular, if the orbit is conjugate to a rigid rotation
(
4
) with a Diophantine rotation vector
\omega

and the map
f
and function
h
are
C^{\infty}
, then (
15
) converges faster than any power,
[
12
]

|\mathit{WB}_{T}(h)-\langle h\rangle|<\frac{C_{k}}{T^{k}},\quad\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Note that the function
\Psi
is not the only
C^{\infty}
weighting function with these excellent convergence properties. Recent work of
Ruth and Bindel [48]
uses reduced rank extrapolation to optimize the
choice of weighting function.

We estimate the error of the WBA for a given function
h
and a given time
T
by
computing the effective number of digits of accuracy:

\mathop{\mathrm{dig}}\nolimits_{T}=-\log_{10}\left|\mathit{WB}_{T}(h)(z)-\mathit{WB}_{T}(h)(f^{T}(z))\right|,

(17)

i.e., comparing the result for the first
T
iterates with that for the next
T
iterates.
[
9
,
10
]

To obtain a criterion distinguishing chaotic and regular orbits,
we need to make a choice of cutoff value for
\mathop{\mathrm{dig}}\nolimits_{T}
, declaring that orbits with

\mathop{\mathrm{dig}}\nolimits_{T}<D_{T}\Rightarrow\mbox{ ``chaotic'' };

(18)

conversely, all orbits with
\mathop{\mathrm{dig}}\nolimits_{T}\geq D_{T}
are “nonchaotic”.
Based on Fig.
1
for the 1D case, we choose the cutoff:

T=10^{5},\quad D_{T}=9\quad\mbox{(Circle Maps)}.

(19)

This guarantees at
least nine digits of accuracy in the computed rotation number.
For the Arnold circle map in §
III
, we know that there are no
chaotic orbits for
0<a\leq 1
; with the adopted criterion, our computations falsely identify only

0.07\%
(
1145
out of
1.6
million) of the orbits in this range to be chaotic.

For the 2D maps in §
IV
, we use the criterion

T=10^{6},\quad D_{T}=9\quad\mbox{(Two-Torus Maps)}.

(20)

Like the one-dimensional case, this is conservative in that chaotic orbits are quite unlikely to be identified as regular.

A.2
Rational vs. Irrational: Farey Trees

In addition to providing the distinction between regular and chaotic orbits,
the WBA can be used to compute an accurate value of the time average of a function
h
.
In particular, we can compute the rotation vector (
3
) of an orbit for a torus
map of the form (
1
) using

\omega_{T}=\mathit{WB}_{T}(F(x)-x)=\Omega+\mathit{WB}_{T}(g(x;a)).

(21)

If
T
is large enough and the rotation vector exists, we expect

\omega_{T}\approx\omega(x,f)
(
3
). We note that this is by no means the only possible
useful
h
to choose. For example, for quasiperiodic orbits,
another choice of
h
allow one to compute the conjugacy between
the map and a rigid rotation.
[
23
,
6
]

In this section, we focus on circle maps,
d=1
, and review the Farey
tree method of
Sander and Meiss [9]
, which when combined with (
21
)
allows us to distinguish orbits that are periodic—those with rational

\omega
—from those that are dense on a circle with irrational
\omega
.

Even though a numerical determination of the irrationality of
\omega_{T}
is impossible, we
will declare it to be “effectively rational” if it is sufficiently close to a
low-order

\tfrac{p}{q}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
,
i.e., one with a “small” denominator
q
.
A rational is
\delta
-close to
x
if it lies in the interval

B_{\delta}(x)\equiv(x-\delta,x+\delta).

(22)

The smallest denominator of a rational approximation within
\delta
of
\omega
is then

q_{min}(\omega,\delta)\equiv\min\{q\in{\mathbb{N}}:\tfrac{p}{q}\in B_{\delta}(\omega),p\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

(23)

We previously discussed an efficient method to compute (
23
)
that uses the Farey tree expansion of
\omega
.
[
9
]
The Farey (or Stern-Brocot) tree computes a sequence of
rational approximations that converge to
\omega
.
We proved that the first such rational on the tree
that falls in
B_{\delta}(\omega)
gives
q_{min}
.
[
9
]

For example,

q_{min}\left(\sqrt{2},10^{-9}\right)=33,461

(with corresponding numerator
p=47,321
). Note that
q_{min}\sim 10^{4.52}
,
which is close to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}=10^{4.5}
—as we see next, this is not unusual.

To decide what it means for a denominator to be “small”, we computed

q_{min}
for a uniform distribution
\omega\in(0,1)
; since irrationals
have measure one, we take the computed distribution to be that of irrationals.
Numerically we found that the resulting log-denominators have a distribution
that is nearly symmetric about the mean

\langle\log_{10}q_{min}\rangle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\log_{10}\delta+\alpha,

(24)

where
\alpha=-0.05\pm 0.001
,
with standard deviation

\sigma=0.2935\pm 0.0006

(25)

which is independent of
\delta
.
In support of this, in a recent paper
Chen and Haynes [43]
proved that as
\delta\to 0
,

\log_{10}\langle q_{min}\rangle
has the form (
24
) with a different
\alpha
.
However, this result is for the log of the mean, whereas our numerics were for the mean of the log.
More recently,
Marklof [45]
has fully verified (
24
), showing that
\alpha=-0.0502959\dots
and

\sigma=0.293336\dots
, consistent with our numerical results.

It is important to note that it is not just small
q_{min}
that correspond to a nearby rational:
if an interval
B_{\delta}(\omega)
is close to a low-order rational, but does not include this point, then
q_{min}

can be much larger than the mean indicated by (
24
). For example, if

\omega
is very close to
\tfrac{0}{1}
, but
\delta
is so small
that
0\notin B_{\delta}(\omega)
, then the denominator can be unusually large; for example,

q_{min}(\sqrt{2}\times 10^{-8},10^{-9})=66,040,883\sim 10^{7.8},

which is
11\sigma
above the mean (
24
).
Thus we will declare a number effectively irrational only if
q_{min}

is close to the mean (
24
) in the sense of the standard deviation
\sigma
.
Such an
\omega
is “typical” in the sense of the uniform distribution and thus is “irrational”.

For a given accuracy
\delta
, we declare that
\omega
is an approximation of an irrational if

\left|\log_{10}(q_{min}(\omega,\delta))+\tfrac{1}{2}\log_{10}(\delta)\right|<s,

(26)

for a given tolerance
s
. We typically choose

\delta=10^{-9},\quad\mbox{and}\quad s=1.6875\approx 5.75\sigma.

(27)

This means that we declare that
\omega
approximates an irrational for periods

649<q_{min}<1.54(10)^{6}\Rightarrow\mbox{``irrational''}.

(28)

This choice of tolerance
s
means we are quite conservative in designating a rotation number as rational.
Using Criterion (
27
) we incorrectly identify
0.05\%
of the random

\omega
as rational (since rationals have measure zero, the result should be zero).
Similarly, we see in §
III
that (
27
)
erroneously identifies only
0.08\%
of the nonchaotic orbits of the Arnold circle map as having irrational rotation number when
a\in(1,2.5)
, where it is known that there are no such orbits.

A.3
Resonant vs. Incommensurate: Resonance Orders

Here we recall a numerical method that generalizes the Farey tree method of App.
A.2
to higher dimensions.
In particular given
\omega_{T}
we wish to compute the rank and resonance order, recall §
IV.1
.

A vector
\omega
is approximately commensurate if
\left|m\cdot\omega-n\right|
is
small. In
Meiss and Sander [10]
we developed a method for computing such commensurabilities.
We say that a vector
\omega
is
(m,n)
-resonant
to precision
\delta
if
the resonant plane (
9
) intersects the ball (
22
) about
\omega
,

{\cal R}_{m,n}\cap B_{\delta}(\omega)\neq\emptyset.

(29)

Using the Euclidean norm, the minimum distance between the plane and the point
\omega
is

\Delta_{m,n}(\omega)=\min_{\alpha\in{\cal R}_{m.n}}\|\alpha-\omega\|_{2}=\frac{|m\cdot\omega-n|}{\|m\|_{2}}.

(30)

Thus
\omega
is
(m,n)
resonant to precision
\delta
, whenever
\Delta_{m,n}(\omega)<\delta
, and
we call the value

M(\omega,\delta)=\min\{\|m\|_{1}:\Delta_{m,n}(\omega)<\delta,\,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\},

(31)

the
resonance order
of
\omega
.

As far as we know, there is no generalization of the
d=1
Farey tree result of App.
A.2

to compute (
31
) efficiently.
†
†

†

One could use the Kim-Ostlund tree to get resonance relations;
[
49
]
however, it is not clear that this algorithm returns a minimal
\|m\|
.

Nevertheless, since there are finitely many
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
such that
\|m\|_{1}\leq M
, a brute force computation
is possible for modest values of
M
.
[
10
]

To understand what resonance orders are “typical,” we computed the minimal resonance order
(
31
) for a set of equi-distributed, random
\omega\in[0,1)^{2}
as a function of the precision
\delta
.

[
10
]
The resulting distribution of
\log(M)
has a mean
[
10
]

\langle\log_{10}M(\omega,\delta)\rangle=-0.334\log_{10}(\delta)-0.091.

(32)

Marklof [44]
,
Marklof [45]
has recently obtained theoretical results that are consistent with (
32
).
We find a standard deviation of

\sigma=0.171.

As in the one-dimensional case, the standard deviation seems to be essentially independent of
\delta
.

Since the cutoff (
20
) gives rotation number calculations
accurate to within
10^{-9}
, we choose
\delta=10^{-9}
. For this case, (
32
) implies
that
\langle\log_{10}M\rangle=2.915
. We declare that a vector is nonresonant if

256\leq M\leq 2673\Rightarrow\mbox{``nonresonant''},

(33)

corresponding to
2.407<\log_{10}(M)<3.427
,
which is a range of approximately
\pm 3\sigma
about the mean (
32
).
To test this criterion we selected
10^{4}
randomly distributed values
uniformly in
[0,1)^{2}
, and found that
1.36\%
were incorrectly identified as resonant.
Note that the distribution of log-orders for random vectors is not symmetric around the mean;
in particular,
M<256
occurred
1.32\%
of the time, and
M>2673
occurred
0.04\%
of the time.

We can further categorize the orbits that are determined to be resonant (those
that fail Criterion (
33
)) by the rank of the resonance.
Rank-two resonant orbits have frequencies on the intersection of a pair of different resonance lines,
recall Fig.
4
. That is, both of the components of the rotation vector are “rational”.
These can be identified using the criterion

M\mbox{ fails (\ref{eq:Incommensurate}) and }\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\mbox{ fail (\ref{eq:IrrationalCriterion}) }\Rightarrow\mbox{``periodic''}.

(34)

If a resonant orbit is not periodic, then it lies on a single resonance line and so has rank one.
We will simply refer to such orbits as “resonant”; they typically are dense on topological circles.
Thus the criterion for a (rank-one) resonant orbit is

M\mbox{ fails (\ref{eq:Incommensurate}) but at least one of }\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\mbox{ satisfy (\ref{eq:IrrationalCriterion})}\Rightarrow\mbox{``resonant''}.

(35)

The criteria (
34
) and (
35
) are used to distinguish the orbit types for the 2D maps in §
IV
.

Appendix B
Critical
\varepsilon

In this appendix, we explain why
\varepsilon_{\rm crit}
(
11
) almost always
exists for most choices of amplitudes
a_{i}
and phases
\phi_{i}
.
We first argue that typically there is some point
(x_{1},x_{2})
for which
\det(H)<0
for
the matrix
H
in (
10
). First, if we choose
x_{2}=-\phi_{2}
, then

\det(H(x_{1},-\phi_{2}))=a_{1}a_{4}\sin(2\pi(x_{1}+\phi_{1}))\sin(2\pi(\phi_{4}-\phi_{2})).

Therefore if
a_{1}a_{4}\neq 0
and
\phi_{4}-\phi_{2}\neq n\pi
for some integer
n
,
this determinant is nonzero and odd about
x_{1}=-\phi_{1}
.
Thus
\det(H)<0
at some point.
If
\phi_{4}-\phi_{2}=n\pi
, we can consider a similar argument upon choosing
x_{1}=-\phi_{3}
as long as
\phi_{3}-\phi_{1}\neq m\pi
for some integer
m
. Equivalent arguments apply if
a_{2}a_{3}\neq 0
, as long as
\phi_{4}-\phi_{2}\neq n\pi
or

\phi_{3}-\phi_{1}\neq m\pi
.

An exceptional case would be if both
\phi_{4}-\phi_{2}=n\pi
,
\phi_{3}-\phi_{1}=m\pi
.
Now choose
x_{1}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\phi_{1}
and
x_{2}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\phi_{4}
. Then

\displaystyle\det H

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{4}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}-a_{2}a_{3}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\phi_{3}-\phi_{1}\right)\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\phi_{2}-\phi_{4}\right)

(36)

\displaystyle=a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+n\pi\right)\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-m\pi\right)

Note that if
m
and
n
are both even or both odd (i.e., have the same parity), then at this point
H=a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}
.
Thus long as
a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}<0
,
\det(H)<0
at this point. On the other hand if
a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}>0
, then use
x_{2}=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-\phi_{2}
, which flips the signs of both terms, again giving
\det(H)<0
.
If however,
m
and
n
have the opposite parity, then
at the point (
36
)
\det(H)=a_{1}a_{4}+a_{2}a_{3}
. If this is negative, we are done. Otherwise
use
x_{2}=-\phi_{2}+\tfrac{3\pi}{2}
, which flips the signs of both terms.

If none of the above cases hold, then
a_{1}a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3}=a_{1}a_{4}+a_{2}a_{3}=0
,
\phi_{4}-\phi_{2}=n\pi
,
\phi_{3}-\phi_{1}=m\pi
, implying

\det(H)
is identically zero. But as long as the trace is nonzero, we can use a similar argument for the term that is linear in
\varepsilon

in (
10
) to show that
\varepsilon_{\rm crit}
exists.

Therefore the only exception to the existence of
\varepsilon_{\rm crit}
is the case
a_{1}=a_{4}=0
and
a_{2}a_{3}=0
,
which gives trivial dynamics.
