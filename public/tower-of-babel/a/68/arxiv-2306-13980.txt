Regularized Multivariate Functional Principal Component Analysis
Hossein Haghbin , Yue Zhao † , and Mehdi Maadooliat

Abstract
Multivariate Functional Principal Component Analysis (MFPCA) is a valuable tool for exploring relationships and identifying shared patterns of variation in multivariate functional data. However, controlling the roughness of the extracted Principal Components (PCs) can be challenging. This paper introduces a novel approach called regularized MFPCA (ReMFPCA) to address this issue and enhance the smoothness and interpretability of the multivariate functional PCs. ReMFPCA incorporates a roughness penalty within a penalized framework, using a parameter vector to regulate the smoothness of each functional variable. The proposed method generates smoothed multivariate functional PCs, providing a concise and interpretable representation of the data. Extensive simulations and real data examples demonstrate the effectiveness of ReMFPCA and its superiority over alternative methods. The proposed approach opens new avenues for analyzing and uncovering relationships in complex multivariate functional datasets.



Keywords:
Hilbert space, Regularization, Multivariate functional data, functional principal component analysis

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a notable surge in interest towards functional data analysis (FDA) owing to its remarkable capability to effectively handle intricate data structures, including longitudinal data, image data, and time series data. Multivariate functional data, characterized by observations composed of vectors of functions, holds significant relevance across diverse applications such as biomedical imaging, environmental science, and finance.

Functional Principal Component Analysis (FPCA) is a key technique in FDA for dimension reduction by capturing the primary modes of variation in functional variables, known as functional principal components (PCs). The foundational contributions to FPCA for univariate functional data can be attributed to the works of
Rice and Silverman, 1991
and
Pezzulli and Silverman, 1993
. For a comprehensive survey of the topic, see
Shang, 2014
.

To mitigate the issue of undesirable fluctuations in functional PCs extracted from (univariate) functional data, various regularization techniques have been proposed
(
Silverman, 1996
;
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
;
Huang et al., 2008
;
Qi and Zhao, 2011
;
Lakraj and Ruymgaart, 2017
)
. These methods aim to enhance the interpretability and stability of the PCs, ultimately improving the accuracy of the results. Throughout this paper, we refer to
Silverman, 1996
’s
Silverman, 1996
approach as regularized FPCA (ReFPCA).

Multivariate FPCA (MFPCA) extends the concept of FPCA to the multivariate setting, where each observation comprises a vector of functions. MFPCA enables the exploration of relationships between multiple functional variables and the identification of shared patterns of variation. Numerous approaches have been proposed for MFPCA in the literature.
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
provides a concise overview of MFPCA for bivariate functional data, while
Jacques and Preda, 2014
apply MFPCA in the context of clustering.
Chiou et al., 2014
introduce a normalized MFPCA method that yields a normalized Karhunen-Loeve representation of the data.
Happ and Greven, 2018
propose a generalized MFPCA framework applicable to functional data across various domains with possibly different dimensions.

Despite the recognized advantages of ReFPCA for univariate functional data, there is a research gap regarding the definition of an appropriate smoothness level for MFPCA. To address this gap, we propose a novel approach called regularized MFPCA (ReMFPCA), which employs a parameter vector to control the smoothness of each functional variable. By incorporating smoothness constraints as penalty terms within a regularized optimization framework, ReMFPCA generates smoothed multivariate functional PCs, leading to a concise and interpretable representation of the multivariate functional data. We validate the effectiveness of ReMFPCA through simulations and real data examples, comparing its performance with that of MFPCA, marginal FPCA and ReFPCA methods.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the preliminary notations, while Section
3
offers a concise review of the relevant literature. The proposed ReMFPCA method and its properties are presented in Section
4
, followed by implementation details in Section
5
. To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method, Sections
6
and
7
provide simulation results and a real data example, respectively. Lastly, Section
8
concludes the paper, highlighting key findings, and suggests potential avenues for future research.

Preliminary Notations

We consider
\mathcal{T}_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,p,
as a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, where
p
denotes the number of variables. Let’s define
H_{j}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{j})
, consisting of measurable functions
x(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{T}_{j}
with
\int_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}|x(t)|^{2}dt<\infty
.
H_{j}
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
\langle x,y\rangle_{H_{j}}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}x(t)y(t)dt
and the norm
\|x\|_{H_{j}}=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{H_{j}}}
. The tensor (outer) product
x\otimes y
corresponds to the operator
x\otimes y:{H}_{i}\rightarrow{H}_{j}
, defined as (
x\otimes y)h:=\langle x,h\rangle y
, where
h\in{H}_{i}
.

Functional random variables is represented by univariate random processes
X_{j}=\{X_{j}(t):\ t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}\}
, with mean function
\mu_{j}(\cdot)
and finite covariance function
c_{j}(s,t)=\text{cov}\left(X_{j}(s),X_{j}(t)\right)
. The space
L^{2}_{H_{j}}
refers to the space of
H_{j}
-valued random variables
X_{j}
satisfying
E(\|X_{j}\|_{H_{j}}^{2})<\infty
. This space is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^{2}_{H_{j}}}=E\langle X,Y\rangle_{{H}_{j}},\ X,Y\in L^{2}_{H_{j}}
. Let
c_{i,j}(s,t)=\text{cov}\left(X_{i}(s),X_{j}(t)\right)
for
X_{i}\in L^{2}_{H_{i}}
and
X_{j}\in L^{2}_{H_{j}}
, the associated cross-covariance operator is an integral operator with respect to the kernel function
c_{i,j}(\cdot,\cdot)
i.e.,

\mathcal{C}_{i,j}(f):=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}c_{i,j}(t,\cdot)f(t)dt,\ f\in H_{i}.

The cartesian product space
\mathbb{H}=H_{1}\times H_{2}\times\cdots\times H_{p}
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
\langle{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle x_{j},y_{j}\rangle_{H_{j}}
. The data of interest in this paper consists of multivariate functional random variables
{\boldsymbol{X}}=\left(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{p}\right)
, belonging to
L^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}
, with a domain
\mathbfcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{T}_{p}
. The covariance operator of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
will de defined by
\mathbfcal{C}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
as

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{C}(\boldsymbol{f})(\boldsymbol{t}):=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}c_{1,j}(s_{j},t_{1})f_{j}(s_{j})ds_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}c_{p,j}(s_{j},t_{p})f_{j}(s_{j})ds_{j}\end{bmatrix},

where
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\in\mathbfcal{T}
and
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{p})\in\mathbb{H}.
This definition implies that
\mathbfcal{C}
is linear, self-adjoint and positive operator
(
Happ and Greven, 2018
)
.

The Sobolev space
W^{2}_{j}
is defined as

W^{2}_{j}:=\left\{x(.):x\ and\ x^{\prime}\ are\ absolutely\ continuous\ on\ \mathcal{T}_{j}\ and\ x^{\prime\prime}\in H_{j}\right\},

where
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
denote the first and second derivatives of
x
, respectively. We introduce the bilinear form
[x,y]_{H_{j}}:=\langle x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\rangle_{H_{j}}
for elements
x,y\in W^{2}_{j}
. When
g^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\in H_{j}
, it can be demonstrated that
[x,y]_{H_{j}}=\langle x,y^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}\rangle_{H_{j}}
(
Silverman, 1996
)
. Given a smoothing parameter
\alpha_{j}>0
, we define the inner product
\langle x,y\rangle_{\alpha_{j}}
as

\langle{x,y}\rangle_{\alpha_{j}}:=\langle{x,y}\rangle_{H_{j}}+\alpha_{j}[{x,y}]_{H_{j}}.

The corresponding norm associated with this inner product is
\|x\|_{\alpha_{j}}:=\sqrt{\langle x,y\rangle_{\alpha_{j}}}
. The cartesian product of Sobolev spaces is denoted as
\mathbb{W}^{2}=W^{2}_{1}\times W^{2}_{2}\times\cdots\times W^{2}_{p}
. Given a smoothing parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+}
, the inner product
\langle{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is defined as

\langle{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle{x_{j},y_{j}}\rangle_{\alpha_{j}}=\langle{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}[{x_{j},y_{j}}]_{H_{j}},

where
{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2}.
The corresponding norm is denoted by
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|{x_{j}}\|_{\alpha_{j}}^{2}}.

3
Multivariate functional PCA

In this paper we consider
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}
are independent and identically distributed (iid) observations defined on the set
\mathbfcal{T}
. We aim to address the standard FPCA problem, which seeks a linear functional
\ell
by maximizing the variance of
\ell(\boldsymbol{X})
, subject to
\|\ell\|_{\mathbb{H}^{*}}=1
, where
\mathbb{H}^{*}
denotes the dual space of bounded linear functionals on
\mathbb{H}
. Applying the Riesz representation theorem, there is a unique
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in\mathbb{H}
associated with any
\ell\in\mathbb{H}^{*}
, such that
\ell(\boldsymbol{X})=\langle\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
and
\|\ell\|_{\mathbb{H}^{*}}=\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbb{H}}
. Consequently, the variance of
\ell(\boldsymbol{X})
can be expressed as
\mathbb{E}\left(\langle\boldsymbol{X-\mu},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right)=\langle\mathbfcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.

The MFPCA problem involves finding the first multivariate functional PC, denoted as
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}\in{\mathbb{H}}
, which solves the optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}{\dfrac{\langle\mathbfcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}}.

(1)

Furthermore for
j>1
, the
j
-th multivariate functional PC, denoted as
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\in{\mathbb{H}}
, is obtained by solving the following optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1,\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0,\ i=1,\ldots,j-1}{\dfrac{\langle\mathbfcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}}.

(2)

The solutions to the successive optimization problems (
1
) and (
2
) correspond to the leading solutions of the eigenfunction equation,
{\mathbfcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\varphi}
. Since the covariance operator
\mathbfcal{C}
is typically unknown, direct estimation of the population multivariate functional PCs is not feasible. Instead, researchers rely on the sample covariance function
{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}
to estimate
\mathbfcal{C}
and use the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
{{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}}
as estimates of those of
\mathbfcal{C}
. Specifically consider a sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
of observations from
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,p})^{\top}
, without loss of generality we assume that the
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are centered (i.e., their sample mean is zero). The sample estimator of the covariance operator
\mathbfcal{C}
is given by:

{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\otimes}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}.

It has been demonstrated that the number of sample PCs with positive eigenvalues, denoted as
k
, satisfies
k\leq n
, where
n
is the sample size. The collection of sample multivariate functional PCs is denoted as
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
, and the corresponding estimated eigenvalues is denoted as
\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
.

4
Regularized Formulation

The ReFPCA method, proposed by
Silverman, 1996
(see
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
Chapter 9 for more details), introduces smoothing by modifying the norm used in traditional FPCA. To develop a similar approach for the multivariate case, we define the regularized multivariate functional PC as the solution to the following optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=1}\dfrac{\langle\mathbfcal{C}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\|{\varphi_{j}}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}+\alpha_{j}[\varphi_{j}]_{H_{j}}^{2}\right)},

(3)

where
\mathcal{D}^{2}
is the second-derivative operator, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a positive vector of smoothing parameters.

Let
{\lambda}_{1}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
and
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
represent the maximum value and solution, respectively, obtained from the optimization problem (
3
). Now, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, assuming we have obtained
\{({\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}):i=1,\ldots,k-1\}
, the solution
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
is found by maximizing the following optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=1,\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=0}\dfrac{\langle{{\mathbfcal{C}}}\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}},\ \ i=1,\ldots,k-1.

(4)

We denote the maximum value of (
4
) as
{\lambda}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
. Replacing
{\mathbfcal{{C}}}
with
{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}
, for
k\leq n
, the set of sample regularized multivariate functional PCs is defined as
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
, and the corresponding estimated eigenvalues (which are positive) are denoted as
\{\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
.

4.1
Existence of Solutions

We investigate the existence of solutions to the optimization problem (
4
) based on the following assumptions:

A1.

The functions
c_{i,j}
satisfy the following conditions for all
i,j=1,\ldots,p
:

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}c_{i,j}(s_{i},t_{j})ds_{i}<K_{ij},\quad\forall\ t_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{j},

where
K_{ij}
is a finite constant. Additionally, the function
c_{i,j}
is uniformly continuous:

\forall\ \varepsilon>0,\ \exists\ \delta_{ij}>0:|t_{j}-t_{j}^{*}|<\delta_{ij}\Rightarrow|c_{i,j}(s_{i},t_{j})-c_{i,j}(s_{i},t^{*}_{j})|<\varepsilon,\ \forall\ s_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}.

These assumptions ensure that the covariance operator
\mathbfcal C
is a compact positive operator on
\mathbb{H}
, allowing the existence of a complete sequence of eigenfunctions
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
s with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>0
.
(
Happ and Greven, 2018
, See Proposition 2 in)
.

A2.

Each eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
belongs to the space
\mathbb{W}
, implying finite roughness (i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}
is finite).

A3.

All eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
have a multiplicity of 1, yielding an ordering of
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under assumptions A1-A3, the optimization problems (
3
) and (
4
) have almost sure solutions
\{({\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p})
, where
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
.

We provide the proofs of all the theorems and lemmas in the appendix for the sake of brevity and to maintain the flow of the main text. To facilitate the understanding of the subsequent discussion, we introduce the second-derivative operator
\mathcal{R}
and the fourth-derivative operator
\mathcal{Q}:=\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathcal{R}
. In particular, we define the operator
S_{\alpha_{j}}
as follows:

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}=\left(\mathcal{I}+\alpha_{j}\mathcal{Q}\right)^{-1/2},\quad\alpha_{j}>0,\ j=1,\ldots,p.

The operator
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}
is a bounded linear self-adjoint operator mapping on
{H}_{j}
, with its range contained in
W_{j}^{2}
. Notably, the linear norm of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}
does not exceed 1. This operator is commonly referred to as the “half-smoothing” operator and plays a crucial role in the context of this study. For more comprehensive insights, please refer to
Silverman, 1996
and
Qi and Zhao, 2011
. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that the inverse of
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}
exists and is self-adjoint.

Note that
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}^{2}f
solves the differential equation

g(x)+\alpha_{j}g^{{}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}}(x)=f(x),\quad\alpha_{j}>0,

and
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{j}}^{2}
is a bounded and positive self-adjoint operator. To introduce the multivariate counterpart of the half-smoothing operator, we define the operator
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
as

\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{1}}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{2}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\ldots&0&\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{p}}\end{bmatrix}.

It is worth noting that
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{k}=\textrm{diag}\left(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{1}}^{k},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{\alpha_{p}}^{k}\right)
for
k=-2,-1,2
. The following lemma provides justification for referring to
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as half-smoothing operators.

Lemma 4.1
.

The operator
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a bounded linear self-adjoint operator with a linear norm of at most 1. Its range, or the domain of
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}
, is
\mathbb{W}^{2}
. Additionally, for any
{\boldsymbol{x,y}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2}
, we have:

\langle\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\langle{\boldsymbol{x,y}}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
4.1
, we have
\|\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{W}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\{({\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the solution of the successive optimization problems (
3
) and (
4
). Then, the pairs
\{({\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}
represent the leading eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the compact operator
\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{{\mathbfcal{C}}}\mathbfcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

Consistency and Asymptotic Results

This subsection establishes the consistency of the proposed method in an asymptotic framework as the number of observations, denoted as

, increases. Let us define
{\mathbfcal P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
as the projection onto the subspace generated by
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathbb{H}
:

{\mathbfcal P}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\boldsymbol{\rho}}{\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\rho}\otimes\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\|\boldsymbol{\rho}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}},\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{H}.

.

Let
\{(\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}):i\in\mathbb{N}\}

) and (

5
Implementation Strategy

In the context of functional data analysis, we assume that the underlying sample functions, denoted as
x_{i,j}(\cdot)
, are smooth for each sample
i
and variable
j
, where
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,p
. However, in practical scenarios, observations are often obtained discretely on a set of grid points and contaminated by random noise. This can be represented as:

Y_{i,j,k}=x_{i,j}(t_{k})+\varepsilon_{i,j,k},\quad k=1,\ldots,K_{j}.

Here,
t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{j}
and
K_{j}
represents the number of discrete grid points for all samples from variable
j
. The
\varepsilon_{i,j,k}
terms are iid random noises. To preprocess the raw data, smoothing techniques are commonly employed to convert the discrete observations into a continuous form, typically done separately for each variable and sample. One commonly used approach is the finite basis function expansion
(
Ramsay and Silverman, 2005
, see)
.

We consider a basis system
\{\nu_{j}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for the space
H_{j}
, where
j=1,\ldots,p
. By projecting each element
x_{i,j}(t)
onto a finite-dimensional subspace
H_{j}^{d_{j}}=sp\{\nu_{j}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{d_{j}}\subseteq H_{j}
, we can represent
x_{i,j}(t)
uniquely as:

x_{i,j}(t)=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(t)^{\top}\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}=(c_{i,j,1},\ldots,c_{i,j,d_{j}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}=(\nu_{j}^{1},\ldots,\nu_{j}^{d_{j}})^{\top}
,
t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}
, and
i=1,\ldots,n
. Similarly, we define
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}=(\psi_{1}^{(\ell)},\ldots,\psi_{p}^{(\ell)})^{\top}
with components represented as
\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}(t)^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}_{j}
, where
\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
. Using these notations, we derive the following results. First, we have:

\langle x_{i,j},\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{H_{j}}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{j}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}_{j},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{j}
represents the
Gram matrix
of the space
H_{j}^{d_{j}}
. Based on this, we obtain:

\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}_{j}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}_{j}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}.

Here,
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}=(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,1}^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{i,p}^{\top}),

\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}=({\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}_{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}_{p}})
, and
\boldsymbol{G}=\textrm{diag}\{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{G}_{p}\}.

Furthermore, we have:

\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\dfrac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)},

where
\boldsymbol{B}=\left[\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\right]^{\top}.
This leads to:

\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}-\dfrac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)},

and subsequently:

\displaystyle\langle{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}-\dfrac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)},

where
\boldsymbol{V}=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\left({\bf I}_{n}-\dfrac{1}{n}{\bf J}_{n}\right)\boldsymbol{B}
. In addition, we have:

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}\|_{\alpha_{j}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|{\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|{\mathcal{D}^{2}\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}.

(6)

Using (
5
), the first term of (
6
) can be expressed as:

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\|{\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}.

(7)

Furthermore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j}}b^{(\ell)}_{j,k}\nu_{j}^{k}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{j}}b^{(\ell)}_{j,k}\mathcal{D}^{2}\nu_{j}^{k}(t).

From this, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}_{j}}\boldsymbol{D}_{j}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}_{j},

where
\boldsymbol{D}_{j}
is the penalty matrix of the
j
-th term. Using this, the second term of (
6
) becomes:

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|{\mathcal{D}^{2}\psi_{j}^{(\ell)}}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\textrm{diag}\{\alpha_{1}\boldsymbol{D}_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}\boldsymbol{D}_{p}\}
. By substituting (
7
) and (
8
) into (
6
), we obtain:

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}.

Finally, equation (
3
) can be rewritten as:

\dfrac{\langle{\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}=\dfrac{{\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}}{{\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}}.

(9)

The eigenequation corresponding to (
9
) can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}=\lambda\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}.

By considering a factorization
\boldsymbol{LL}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and defining
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{L}^{-1}
, we obtain:

\left(\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}\right)=\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}.

(10)

To solve (
10
), we utilize the eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{u}^{(\ell)}
and their corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda^{(\ell)}
of
\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}
. Note that any scalar multiple of
\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{(\ell)}
is a valid solution to (
10
) with the same eigenvalue
\lambda^{(\ell)}
. However, in order to satisfy the constraint
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(\ell)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)\top}}\boldsymbol{LL}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}=1
, we normalize
\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}
by setting
\boldsymbol{b}^{(\ell)}=\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}^{(\ell)}.

We note that the proposed method is implemented in an
R
package
ReMFPCA
(
https://github.com/haghbinh/ReMFPCA
).

\boldsymbol{t}

\boldsymbol{0}

8
Conclusion

In summary, this paper introduced ReMFPCA to address the challenge of defining an appropriate smoothness level for multivariate functional data. By incorporating smoothness constraints as penalty terms within a regularized optimization framework, ReMFPCA generates smoothed multivariate functional PCs that offer improved interpretability and stability. Through simulations and real data examples, we demonstrated the effectiveness of ReMFPCA compared to other methods such as MFPCA, marginal FPCA, and regularized FPCA (ReFPCA). ReMFPCA showcased superior performance in terms of interpretability, stability, and accuracy of results. Its ability to capture shared patterns of variation provides valuable insights into complex multivariate functional datasets across various domains.

A limitation of the proposed technique lies in the computational cost associated with determining the tuning parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, which has a length of
p
. In this study, cross-validation was employed to select the tuning parameter, which proved to be computationally expensive within the current framework. Future research efforts may focus on devising more efficient approaches for obtaining the smoothing parameters in order to address this issue.

Overall, ReMFPCA contributes to the growing field of functional data analysis, empowering researchers to gain deeper insights from complex multivariate functional datasets in domains such as biomedical imaging, environmental science, and finance.

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\to 1.

(A.5)

Furthermore, the limit (
A.4
) can be equivalently expressed as:

\|{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}}-{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H})}\to 0.

(A.6)

Let us consider a fixed
j\geq 1
and assume, by induction, that
\mathfrak{A}_{k}
holds for all
k<j
(For
j=1
, there is no assumption to be made). Now, we introduce the projection
{\mathbfcal P}
, which is perpendicular to
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j-1}
:

{\mathbfcal P}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},

and the projection
{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
, which is perpendicular to
\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j-1}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
with respect to the inner product
\langle.,.\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
:

{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}.

The following lemma is essential to finalize the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Lemma A.1
.

For any
j\geq 1
, assuming
\mathfrak{A}k
holds for all
k<j
, let
\mathbfcal P
and
\mathbfcal P^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
be defined as above. Then, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\left|\langle{\mathbfcal P}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\langle{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\boldsymbol{x},\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right|\to 0.

(A.7)

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

We consider
j\geq 2
. Throughout the proof, we focus on
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
. For each
k<j
, applying the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, and utilizing the fact that
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, we obtain

\begin{aligned} \sup&{}_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\langle\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathcal{D}^{2}{x_{j}}\rangle_{H_{j}}\\
&\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1}\|\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&+\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}{x_{j}}\|_{H_{j}}\\
&=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1}\|{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}-{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1}\|\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\\
&+\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}{x_{j}}\|_{H_{j}}\\
&=\|{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}}-{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H})}+\|{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H})}\left(1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\right)\qquad\qquad\quad\ \ \ \ \\
&\qquad+\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}{x_{j}}\|_{H_{j}}.\end{aligned}

In the last term since for each
i
,
\|x_{i}\|_{H_{i}}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},
using (
A.3
) we get

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}{x_{j}}\|_{H_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}\to 0.

Using this and Equations (
A.5
) and (
A.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}

\displaystyle\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0\ \text{for all }k<j.

(A.8)

By summing over
k<j
we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}}\dfrac{\|({\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}-{\mathbfcal P})\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1}\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0.

(A.9)

In a seminal work,
Dauxois et al., 1982
demonstrated the convergence of
\|\hat{\mathbfcal{C}}-\mathbfcal{C}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H})}
by utilizing the strong law of large numbers in Hilbert space. Building upon this finding and leveraging the uniform convergence result given by (
A.9
), we establish (
A.7
) and successfully conclude the proof of the lemma. ∎

We can now prove the three parts of
\mathfrak{A}_{j}
successively.

Proof of (
A.2
) for
k=j
. Please note that the maximum value of
\langle{\mathbfcal P}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
over the set
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1
is
\lambda_{j}
, and it is achieved at
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}
. Similarly, the maximum value of
\langle{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\boldsymbol{x},\widehat{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
over the set
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq 1
is
\lambda_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
, attained at
\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
. Using property (
A.7
) we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbfcal P}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\geq\langle{\mathbfcal P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\frac{\langle{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}

(A.10)

\displaystyle\geq\langle{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\langle{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\hat{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+o(1)=\lambda_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}+o(1).

Also, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\hat{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\geq\langle{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}}/{\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},\hat{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}}/{\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbfcal P}{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}}/{\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},{\mathbfcal{C}}{\mathbfcal P}{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}}/{\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}+o(1)={\lambda_{j}}/{\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}}+o(1)=\lambda_{j}+o(1),

and the fact that
\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\to 1
, since
\alpha_{i}\to 0.
The proof of (
A.2
) is completed for
k=j.

Proof of (
A.3
) for
k=j
.
It immediately follows from

\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{j}\|\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{kj}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{H_{j}}^{2}=1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=1-\dfrac{\langle{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\langle{\mathbfcal P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal{C}{\mathbfcal P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}\to 0.

Proof of (
A.4
) for
k=j
.
We begin by considering the case when
j\geq 2
and substitute
x=\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
into equation (
A.8
). Given that
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{2}=1
, and for each
k<j
,

\displaystyle\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}-\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=\|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=|\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}|,

we can deduce from this and equation (
A.5
) for
k=j
that, for each
k<j
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=0,

implying that

\displaystyle\sum_{k<j}\langle{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{k},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0.

(A.11)

Next, we expand
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}
in terms of the complete orthonormal sequence
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}=\mathbfcal{P}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbfcal{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i},

(A.12)

where we utilize the fact that
\mathbfcal{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=0
for
i<j
and
\mathbfcal{P}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=1
for
i\geq j
. By employing the property
\mathbfcal C\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbfcal C\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbfcal C\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}.

(A.13)

Combining equations (
A.12
) and (
A.13
) and utilizing the orthonormality of
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal C\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

(A.14)

Considering that
\mathbfcal{P}
is a projection, we have
\|\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=1
. Substituting this into equation (
A.14
), we find:

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle-\langle\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal C\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\geq\lambda_{j}\|\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}-\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}-\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

(A.15)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=j}^{\infty}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j+1})\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq 0,

since
\lambda_{j}
is a decreasing sequence. As the inequalities in (
A.10
) tend to equalities, we have
\lambda_{j}-\langle\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},\mathbfcal C\mathbfcal{P}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0
. Consequently, all the inequalities in (
A
) tend to equalities. Since
\lambda_{j}\neq\lambda_{j+1}
, it follows that:

\sum_{i>j}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\to 0.

(A.16)

Combining equations (
A.11
) and (
A.16
) with the property
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=1
, we can conclude that
\langle{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}{j}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}},\boldsymbol{\psi}{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\to 1
. This completes the proof of equation (
A.4
) for
k=j
. By following the inductive argument outlined above, we have now completed the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
∎

.

Using the Theorem
4.2
we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}-{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}-2\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=2-2\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0,

Proof of Corollary
4.2
.

Using the Theorem
4.2
we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}-{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}\|{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\boldsymbol{x}-{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1}\left(\|{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\|{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}-2\langle{\mathbfcal P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}}\boldsymbol{x},{\mathbfcal P}_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2-2\dfrac{\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}=2-2\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0.
